Sparsest cut and eigenvalue multiplicities on low degree Abelian Cayley graphs
Tommaso d’Orsi, Chris Jones, Jake Ruotolo, Salil Vadhan, Jiyu Zhang

Abstract
Whether or not the Sparsest Cut problem admits an efficient O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) -approximation algorithm is a fundamental algorithmic question with connections to geometry and the Unique Games Conjecture. Revisiting spectral algorithms for Sparsest Cut, we present a novel, simple algorithm that combines eigenspace enumeration with a new algorithm for the Cut Improvement problem. The runtime of our algorithm is parametrized by a quantity that we call the solution dimension SD ε ​ ( G ) \textup{{SD}}_{\varepsilon}(G) : the smallest k k such that the subspace spanned by the first k k Laplacian eigenvectors contains all but ε \varepsilon fraction of a sparsest cut. Our algorithm matches the guarantees of prior methods based on the threshold-rank paradigm, while also extending beyond them. To illustrate this, we study its performance on low degree Cayley graphs over Abelian groups—canonical examples of graphs with poor expansion properties. We prove that low degree Abelian Cayley graphs have small solution dimension, yielding an algorithm that computes a ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximation to the uniform Sparsest Cut of a degree- d d Cayley graph over an Abelian group of size n n in time n O ⁡ ( 1 ) ⋅ exp ⁡ { ( d / ε ) O ⁡ ( d ) } n^{O(1)}\cdot\exp\{(d/\varepsilon)^{O(d)}\} . Along the way to bounding the solution dimension of Abelian Cayley graphs, we analyze their sparse cuts and spectra, proving that the collection of O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) -approximate sparsest cuts has an ε \varepsilon -net of size exp ⁡ { ( d / ε ) O ⁡ ( d ) } \exp\{(d/\varepsilon)^{O(d)}\} and that the multiplicity of λ 2 \lambda_{2} is bounded by 2 O ⁡ ( d ) 2^{O(d)} . The latter bound is tight and improves on a previous bound of 2 O ⁡ ( d 2 ) 2^{O(d^{2})} by Lee and Makarychev.

Introduction

For an undirected graph
G
, the sparsest cut measures how poor of an expander the graph is,
characterizing how slowly random walks on
G
mix. For simplicity in this introduction, we will state our definitions just for the case of regular graphs:

Definition 1.1

(Sparsest Cut)
.

For a
d
-regular graph
G
on
n
vertices and a partition
(Q,\bar{Q})
of its vertex set
V(G)
, the

(normalized) density

of the cut
(Q,\bar{Q})
is defined as:

\displaystyle\psi_{G}(Q):=\frac{|E(Q,\bar{Q})|}{|Q|\cdot|\bar{Q}|}\cdot\frac{n}{d}\,.

The
sparsest cut
problem is, given
G
, to find
Q\subseteq V(G)
that minimizes
\psi_{G}(Q).
We write
\psi(G)
to denote the minimum value of
\psi_{G}(Q)
over all cuts
(Q,\bar{Q})
.

The normalization factor of
n/d
ensures that the largest possible value of
\psi(G)
is
1
, because for a randomly chosen cut
Q
, the expectation of
|E(Q,\bar{Q})|
is
nd/4
, and the expectation of
|Q|\cdot|\bar{Q}|
is
n^{2}/4
.
We’ll often switch back and forth between
\psi(G)
and the closely related quantity of
conductance
, defined as

\phi(G)=\min_{|Q|\leqslant n/2}\phi_{G}(Q),\quad\text{ where }\quad\phi_{G}(Q)=\frac{|E(Q,\bar{Q})|}{d\cdot|Q|}.

We have
\phi(G)\leqslant\psi(G)\leqslant 2\cdot\phi(G)
, so the problem of approximating
conductance
is equivalent to the problem of approximating
sparsest cut
, up to multiplying the approximation factor by 2.
Via known reductions, up to a constant factor of approximation,

sparsest cut
is also equivalent to approximating several other graph parameters such as
edge expansion
and
balanced separator

[
ARV09
]
.

Because of its centrality to algorithms, computational complexity, combinatorics, and geometry, efficient algorithms for
sparsest cut
have been the main focus of a long line of work.
A polynomial-time
O(\log n)
-approximation algorithm was obtained via a linear programming relaxation by Leighton and Rao
[
LR99
]
, and later improved to
O(\sqrt{\log n})
by Arora, Rao, and Vazirani (ARV)
[
ARV09
]
via semidefinite programming. To this day, the ARV algorithm remains the state-of-the-art for general graphs.

A central challenge to resolving the approximability of
sparsest cut
is its intricate relationship with the
Unique Games Conjecture
(UGC) and the
Small Set Expansion Hypothesis
(SSEH), themselves outstanding open problems with a close connection
[
Kho02
,
RS10
,
RST12
]
.
Assuming the SSEH,
sparsest cut
does not have a polynomial-time
O(1)
-approximation algorithm
[
RST12
]
(the same holds for “non-uniform”
sparsest cut
assuming the UGC
[
CKK+06
,
KV15
,
AKK+08
]
).
Thus we have some evidence that beating the
O(\sqrt{\log n})
approximation factor of the ARV algorithm may be hard.
On the other side of the coin, searching for better approximation algorithms for
sparsest cut
is an ostensible approach to developing algorithms for related problems, including
small-set expansion
and
unique games
.

Given the above situation, researchers have attempted to pair interesting instances with interesting algorithms to determine when constant-factor approximation to
sparsest cut
is possible.
Below we summarize the most relevant algorithmic approaches and what is known about them.

Fiedler’s Algorithm.

The simplest algorithm for
sparsest cut
is Fiedler’s Algorithm, which is just to threshold the second eigenvector
[
Fie73
]
.
Fiedler’s Algorithm always finds a cut
Q
such that
\phi(G)\leqslant\sqrt{2\lambda_{2}}
, where
\lambda_{2}
is the second-smallest eigenvalue of the normalized Laplacian of
G
. Indeed, this performance guarantee for Fiedler’s Algorithm is the proof of the right-hand side of Cheeger’s Inequality, which says:

\lambda_{2}/2\leqslant\phi(G)\leqslant\sqrt{2\lambda_{2}}\,.

This analysis of Fiedler’s algorithm shows that it gives an
O(\sqrt{\lambda_{2}}/\phi(G))
worst-case approximation ratio, which can be quite large. Indeed,
examples show that the approximation factor achieved by Fiedler’s Algorithm can be as bad as
\Omega(n^{1/3})
in general
[
GM98
]
.

Expander-like graphs.

Starting around 15 years ago, a series of works gave approximation algorithms for

sparsest cut
and
unique games
on graphs that are similar to expanders. A constant-factor approximation to
sparsest cut
is trivial on actual expanders (every cut is a constant-factor approximation since
\psi(G)=\Omega(1)
), but it took a nontrivial work of Arora et al.
[
AKK+08
]
to approximate
unique games
on expanders. This in turn led to efficient algorithms for
sparsest cut
on graphs that are “expander-like” in the sense of having few small eigenvalues, as captured by the following definition:

Definition 1.2

(
\tau
-threshold-rank)
.

For a graph
G
and
2\geqslant\tau\geqslant 0\,,
the
\tau
-
threshold-rank

\textnormal{{mul}}_{\tau}(G)
is the number of eigenvalues of the normalized Laplacian with value at most
\tau
.

Recall that
G
is an expander if and only if
\lambda_{2}
is bounded away from 0. Equivalently, there is a constant
\tau>0
(independent of
n
) such that
\textnormal{{mul}}_{\tau}(G)=1
. For every constant
\tau>0
, the works
[
ABS15
,
BRS11
]
give a constant-factor approximation algorithm for
sparsest cut
that runs in time polynomial in
n
but exponential in
\textnormal{{mul}}_{\tau}(G)
.

The algorithm of Barak, Raghavendra, and Steurer
[
BRS11
]
is based on a rounding of a semidefinite programming relaxation, the
O(\textnormal{{mul}}_{\tau}(G))
’th level of the Sum-of-Squares hierarchy.
Recent work has extended the Sum-of-Squares technique based on looser expansion properties, including being a small-set expander
[
BBK+21
]
, coming from a high-dimensional expander
[
BHKL22
]
, or having a “succinct characterization” of non-expanding sets
[
BM23
]
.

The algorithm of Arora, Barak, and Steurer
[
ABS15
]
is based on enumerating the subspace spanned by the first
\textnormal{{mul}}_{\tau}(G)
eigenvectors to find a vector that is very close to a sparse cut.
Approaches like this, which utilize multiple low eigenvectors of the Laplacian matrix, are natural generalizations of Fiedler’s Algorithm, and we collectively refer to them as
higher-order spectral algorithms
.

Cheeger-lower-bound graphs.

With a more careful use of the Sum-of-Squares hierarchy,
Guruswami and Sinop
[
GS13
]
showed that we can replace
\textnormal{{mul}}_{\Omega(1)}
in the aforementioned results with
\textnormal{{mul}}_{O(\phi(G))}
.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
GS13
]
)
.

For all constants
\varepsilon>0\,
,

sparsest cut
admits a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm in time
n^{O(1)}\cdot\exp\{O(r)\}\,,
where
r
is the
O(\phi(G)/\varepsilon)
-threshold-rank.

When
\phi(G)
is small, it is not clear that graphs with small
O(\phi(G))
-threshold-rank are “expander-like” anymore. Instead, we can think of them as graphs where the lower bound of Cheeger’s Inequality (
\lambda_{2}/2\leqslant\phi(G)
) is nearly tight in the sense that
G
has few eigenvalues between
\lambda_{2}/2
and
\phi(G)
.

The crucial feature of this class of graphs is that they have relatively few distinct near-sparsest cuts.
Indeed, the indicator vector of a set
Q
with conductance
O(\phi(G))
must be close to the subspace spanned by the eigenvalues of magnitude
O(\phi(G))
.
Thus if the latter subspace has dimension
r
, all of the near-sparsest cuts can be covered by a net of size exponential in
r
.

A similar consequence of small
O(\phi(G))
-threshold-rank is that these graphs can be decomposed into a small number
of pieces with conductance at most
O(\phi(G))
, such
as depicted in
Fig.

1
.
See
Appendix

A
for a simple statement and proof of this decomposition.

Cut improvement.

As noted by Guruswami and Sinop
[
GS13
]
,

Theorem

1.3
can also be proven via a higher-order spectral algorithm, based on eigenspace enumeration plus an algorithm for the
cut improvement
problem.

non-uniform

Informally,
cut improvement
is the problem of: given a cut
Q\subseteq V(G)
, compute the sparsest cut that has large intersection with
Q
.
This is a problem of independent interest, with practical applications
[
AL08
,
LMO09
,
FLGM23

.

Andersen and Lang
[
AL08
]
gave a flow-based approximation algorithm for

which for our purposes can be summarized as follows.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
AL08
]
)
.

Let
G
be an
n
-vertex graph, let
0<\varepsilon<1/2
, and let
Q^{*}\subseteq V(G)
.
There is a polynomial-time algorithm that, given
G
and
Q\subseteq V(G),|Q|\leqslant n/2

such that
|Q\cap Q^{*}|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)|Q^{*}|
,
returns
\hat{Q}\subseteq V(G)
with
0<\lvert\hat{Q}\rvert\leqslant n/2
and
\phi(\hat{Q})\leqslant\frac{1}{1-2\varepsilon}\cdot\phi(Q^{*})
.

With this
cut improvement
algorithm in hand, we can derive
Theorem

1.3
(with a factor 2 loss in approximation factor) as follows: by
enumerating the eigenspace up to eigenvalue
O(\phi(G)/\varepsilon)
, we can obtain a cut
Q
that is
\varepsilon
-close to a sparsest cut

, and
then we use
Theorem

1.4
to find
\hat{Q}
such that

\phi(\hat{Q})\leqslant(1+O(\varepsilon))\cdot\phi(Q^{*})\leqslant(1+O(\varepsilon))\cdot 2\psi(G),

where the factor 2 loss comes from moving between conductance and density.

Buser graphs.

A class of graphs which is in a sense complementary to the class of Cheeger-lower-bound graphs consists of graphs that satisfy a
Buser Inequality

\phi=\Theta(\sqrt{\lambda_{2}})
.
Examples of graphs with bounded degree
d
which satisfy this inequality are: graphs with non-negative discrete curvature
[
KKRT16
,
Mün23
]
, Cayley graphs over Abelian groups
[
KKRT16
,
OT21
]
, or more generally Cayley graphs over nilpotent
groups
[
BT16
]
, or graphs satisfying strong bounds on their volume growth
[
BRT21
]
.
All these graphs satisfy
\phi(G)\geqslant\Omega(\sqrt{\lambda_{2}/d})
where
d
is the degree of the graph.

For solving these instances, it has been observed experimentally that spectral algorithms are less successful than flow-based algorithms
based on the Leighton–Rao LP and its successors
[
LR99
,
FLGM23
]
.
For example, the computations of
[

demonstrate that: for low-dimensional mesh graphs, Leighton–Rao succeeds while simple spectral thresholding algorithms are suboptimal, whereas the opposite is true for an “expander with a planted cut” similar to the graph in

Note that the Buser Inequality
\phi(G)\geqslant\Omega(\sqrt{\lambda_{2}/d})
implies that Fiedler’s Algorithm achieves
O(\sqrt{d})
-approximation to
sparsest cut
since the conductance of the Fiedler cut is
O(\sqrt{\lambda_{2}})
.
Thus, Fiedler’s Algorithm can still achieve constant approximation ratio
for constant
d
, although the approximation ratio decays with
d
.
The approximation ratio of Fiedler’s Algorithm grows to
\Theta(\sqrt{\log n})
for the hypercube graph (when it is perturbed slightly so that the
\{\pm 1\}
-indicator of the Majority function becomes the unique second eigenvector).

Results

In this work:

1.

We give a simple algorithm for the
cut improvement
problem using a modification of the Leighton–Rao linear program
[
LR99
]
.
This extends into a simple eigenspace enumeration algorithm for

in the same way as
[

2.

We define a new complexity measure, the

Comparing to

We demonstrate that this is the case for Cayley graphs over Abelian groups, which are a specific family of graphs satisfying the previously mentioned Buser Inequality. This gives us a PTAS for

To prove our upper bound on the solution dimension,

We bound the number of sparse cuts in

-approximate sparse cuts are

We also establish a new and tight bound of

1.1.1
Cut Improvement

Our first result is a new algorithm for the
cut improvement
problem.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
G
be a regular
n
-vertex graph, let
0<\varepsilon<1/3
and let
Q^{*}\subseteq V(G)
,
\vol(Q^{*})\leqslant\vol(G)/2

There is a polynomial time algorithm that, given
G

Q\subseteq[n]
such that
\vol(Q\triangle Q^{*})\leqslant\varepsilon^{3}\vol(Q^{*})\,,

returns
\hat{Q}\subseteq[n]
with
\psi(\hat{Q})\leqslant(1+O(\varepsilon))\cdot\psi(Q^{*})\,.

Compared to
Theorem

1.4
of Andersen and Lang
[
AL08
]
, this theorem offers a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation to the density
\psi(Q^{*})
rather than to the conductance
\phi(Q^{*})
, which will save us a factor of 2 in moving between the two.
The algorithm of Andersen and Lang is based on iterative max flow computations on an augmented graph. In comparison, our algorithm seems conceptually simpler in that it uses the Leighton–Rao linear program with one extra constraint, followed by the simplest ball rounding scheme.
While other algorithms have been proposed for the
cut improvement
problem, such as a spectral algorithm by Mahoney et al
[
MOV09
]
,
we are unaware of other algorithms besides Theorems

and

1.1.2
Solution Dimension

Our second contribution is a new characterization of graphs for which a combination of eigenspace enumeration with an algorithm for
cut improvement
is an effective approach to
sparsest cut
.
We name the parameter controlling the runtime of our spectral enumeration algorithm
the
solution dimension
of the graph.
For a subspace
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\,,
let
\mathbf{\Pi}_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be the projection onto
S\,,
let
C_{\varepsilon}(S):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\,\lVert x\rVert=1,\lVert\mathbf{\Pi}_{S}{x}\rVert^{2}\geqslant 1-\varepsilon\}
be the set of unit vectors near
S\,.

Definition 1.6

(Solution Dimension)
.

Let
0\leqslant\varepsilon\leqslant 1\,,c\geqslant 1\,,
let
G
be an

n
-vertex graph.
Let
\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be the diagonal matrix whose
i
th entry is
\deg_{G}(i)
.
Let
\lambda_{1}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant\lambda_{n}
be the sorted eigenvalues of the normalized Laplacian of
G
and let
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the associated eigenvectors.
For
Q\subseteq[n]
, let
\bar{\mathbf{1}}_{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the projection of
\mathbf{1}_{Q}
orthogonal to the trivial eigenvector
v_{1}
.

The
(\varepsilon,c)
-solution-dimension
of
G
, denoted by
\textup{{SD}}_{\varepsilon,c}(G)\,,
is the smallest
k\in[n]
such that
there exists
Q\subseteq[n]
with:

(i)

\psi_{G}(Q)\leqslant c\cdot\psi(G)\,.

(ii)

\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\bar{\mathbf{1}}_{Q}/\lVert\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\bar{\mathbf{1}}_{Q}\rVert\in C_{\varepsilon}(\textnormal{span}(v_{2},\ldots,v_{k+1}))
.
2
2

2

Because of the centering, it does not matter whether the indicator of
Q
is represented as a 0/1 vector or a
\pm 1

When

\textup{{SD}}_{\varepsilon}(G)\,.

It can be shown that
\textup{{SD}}_{\varepsilon}(G)\leqslant\textnormal{{mul}}_{\psi(G)/\varepsilon}(G)\leqslant\textnormal{{mul}}_{2\phi(G)/\varepsilon}(G)
; indeed
every
sparse cut

can be approximated in the span of eigenvectors of eigenvalue at most
\psi(G)/\varepsilon
.
(See

Surprisingly, the

-threshold-rank does
not

match the solution dimension, allowing for potentially large speedups over

The cycle graph is perhaps the most basic example with solution dimension
O(1)

\textnormal{{mul}}_{\phi(G)}=\Theta(\sqrt{n})

Put another way, it suffices for eigenspace enumeration to find a
(1-\varepsilon)
fraction of a sparse cut, instead of the entire cut,
which can dramatically reduce the runtime of the enumeration.

Furthermore, while
\textnormal{{mul}}_{\psi(G)/\varepsilon}(G)
being small implies that there are few “distinct” sparsest cuts (since all of them
are
\varepsilon

From this perspective, understanding whether graphs with small solution dimension yet many distinct sparsest cuts exist—and what their structure might be—appears to be a potentially valuable direction for developing new algorithms for

1.1.3
Abelian Cayley Graphs

Our next contribution is to show that using the solution dimension framework gives a dramatic speedup generically for low-degree Cayley graphs over Abelian groups.

Definition 1.8

(Cayley Graph)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a group and let
S
be a multiset (called the set of
generators
) from
\Gamma
such that the multiplicity of
x\in S
and
-x\in S
is the same for all
x\in\Gamma
. The
Cayley graph
of
\Gamma
generated by
S
, denoted
{\textup{{Cay}}}(\Gamma,S)
, is the graph with vertex set
\Gamma
and edge set
\{(v,v\cdot s):v\in\Gamma,s\in S\}
.

Even when restricted to constant degree,
Cayley graphs provide a simple way to construct graphs with interesting algebraic and geometric properties.
For example, the first expander construction by Margulis
is a 12-regular Cayley graph over the group
SL_{3}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})

[
Mar73
]
.

Constant-degree Cayley graphs over Abelian groups, and more generally nilpotent groups, are well-known in computer science and geometric group theory as
non-expanding
graphs which have slow mixing properties.
Degree-
d
Abelian Cayley graphs satisfy
\phi(G)\leqslant O(n^{-2/d})

[
Kla81
,
FMT06
]
,
the Cheeger upper bound is nearly tight due to the Buser inequality
\phi(G)\geqslant\Omega(\sqrt{\lambda_{2}/d})

[
KKRT16
,
OT21
]
, the volume of a radius-
r
ball is at most
O(r^{d/2})
,
and they are flat or positively curved using any of several definitions of discrete curvature
[
CKK+21
,
KKRT16
]
.

Trevisan posed the question of whether
sparsest cut
admits an
O(1)
-approximation in polynomial time on Abelian Cayley graphs
[
OT21
]
.
We prove that higher-order spectral algorithms can
efficiently solve
sparsest cut
on low-degree Abelian Cayley graphs using the solution dimension framework.

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
G={\textup{{Cay}}}(\Gamma,S)
be a Cayley graph over an Abelian group
\Gamma,|\Gamma|=n
with generating set
S\subseteq\Gamma,|S|=d
. There is an algorithm that finds a set
Q\subseteq[n],|Q|\leqslant n/2
satisfying
\psi_{G}(Q)\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\psi(G)
in time
n^{O(1)}\cdot\exp\{(d/\varepsilon)^{O(d)}\}\,.

Recall that, because of the aforementioned Buser Inequality, Fiedler’s algorithm gives an
O(\sqrt{d})
approximation algorithm on degree
d
Abelian Cayley graphs. In contrast,
Theorem

1.9
gives a
(1+\varepsilon)
approximation ratio. The price is that runtime is doubly exponential in the degree
d
. Indeed, we obtain a PTAS for
degree
d=o(\log\log n/\log\log\log n)
and a subexponential-time approximation scheme for
d=o(\log n/\log\log n)
.

Every finite abelian group
\Gamma
is isomorphic to a product of cyclic groups
\Gamma\cong\mathbb{Z}_{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}_{n_{k}}
. The minimum number
k
of cyclic factors in such a decomposition is also the minimum size of a generating set
S
for
\Gamma
. Thus our algorithm is useful for groups
\Gamma
that can be decomposed into
o(\log n/\log\log n)
cyclic factors (such as cyclic groups). It is not applicable to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
, since that requires
d\geqslant k=\log n
generators.

To prove
Theorem

1.9
from
Theorem

1.7
, we bound the solution dimension of Abelian Cayley graphs by analyzing both their sparse cuts
and their low eigenvectors.
First we prove a generic bound on the multiplicity of eigenvalues near
\lambda_{2}
in terms of a notion of volume growth.

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
G
be a regular graph. For every
\lambda_{2}\leqslant\tau\leqslant\tfrac{3}{2}
,

\displaystyle\textnormal{{mul}}_{\tau}(G)\leqslant O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\tau}{\lambda_{2}}}}\right)^{\log\gamma^{\textup{{cp}}}_{G}}

where
\gamma_{G}^{\textup{{cp}}}=\max_{t\geqslant 0}\frac{\textup{{cp}}_{t}}{\textup{{cp}}_{2t}}
and
\textup{{cp}}_{t}
is the return probability of a
2t
-step lazy random walk (
Definition

5.1
).

The quantity
\gamma_{G}^{\textup{{cp}}}
is bounded by
2^{O(d)}
for degree-
d
Abelian Cayley graphs, as we prove, which yields a bound of
2^{O(d)}
on the multiplicity of the
\lambda_{2}
eigenvalue itself (by plugging in
\tau=\lambda_{2}
). This
improves a previous bound of
2^{O(d^{2})}
proven by Lee and Makaryachev
[
LM08
]
.
Moreover, our bound is robust, in that we also bound the number of eigenvalues that are at most

.

The quantity
\log\gamma_{G}^{\textup{{cp}}}

Techniques

We present here the main ideas behind our results.

Cut improvement (Theorem
1.5
) and eigenspace enumeration (Theorem
1.7
)

The starting point for
Theorem

1.5
is the natural LP relaxation for
sparsest cut
studied in
[
LR99
]
(see
Section

4
for the precise definition of the program). This LP relaxation assigns lengths to edges such that the average distance between all pairs of vertices is fixed, while the average edge length is minimized.
The pairwise distances are constrained to form an
n
-point semi-metric
d(\cdot,\cdot)\,:V(G)\times V(G)\to\mathbb{R}\,
.
The Leighton-Rao rounding procedure then computes a randomized line embedding
f:V(G)\to\mathbb{R}
that is
1
-Lipschitz and has small average distortion with respect to this metric:

\displaystyle\Omega(1/\log n)\sum_{{ij}\in V(G)}d(i,j)\leqslant\sum_{{ij}\in V(G)}\lvert f(i)-f(j)\rvert\leqslant\sum_{{ij}\in V(G)}d(i,j)\,.

Finally, the line embedding can be easily converted into a cut, yielding a cut with sparsity at most
O(\log n)\cdot\psi(G)\,.

The improvement of
[
ARV09
]
comes from the fact that their program returns a metric of negative type and, for such metrics, one can efficiently construct a
1
-Lipschitz line embedding with average distortion at most
O(\sqrt{\log n})\,.

Hence, natural paths to improve over these results may require better constructions of the metric and the line embedding,
which appears challenging and may be computationally hard in general
[
RST12
]
.

Our key insight is that, if we enforce solutions to be close to a given partition
(Q,V(G)\setminus Q)
in the sense that:

\displaystyle\sum_{{ij}\in Q}d(i,j)+\sum_{{ij}\notin Q}d(i,j)\leqslant\varepsilon|Q|\cdot|V(G)\setminus Q|\,,

(2.1)

then any feasible metric must have two “well-separated” sets of size at least
(1-\varepsilon^{\Omega(1)})|Q|
and
(1-\varepsilon^{\Omega(1)})|V(G)\setminus Q|
which are at distance at least
1-\varepsilon^{\Omega(1)}\,.

Any such feasible metric admits an efficiently computable line embedding with distortion at most
1+\varepsilon^{\Omega(1)}
which can then be easily rounded into an integral solution.
In this approach, all feasible solutions must have large overlap with
(Q,V(G)\setminus Q)
and hence the overall quality of the output depends on the starting point
Q
.

Finally, to obtain
Theorem

1.7
, we simply initialize the program at a good
Q
, by finding in time
\exp\{\textup{{SD}}_{\varepsilon}(G)\}
a partition
(Q,V(G)\setminus Q)
that differs from a sparsest cut in at most an
\varepsilon
-fraction of the vertices. For this choice of the constraint
Eq.

2.1
we can simultaneously ensure that the returned metric is easily roundable
and
that there is a nearby optimal integral solution which we
(1+\varepsilon^{\Omega(1)})
-approximate.

Eigenvalue multiplicity (Theorem
1.10
) and slow decay of collision probability

The common approach to bounding the eigenvalue multiplicity of graph Laplacians boils down to relating the local volume growth of induced subgraphs with the spectrum of the whole graph
[
JTY+21
,
LM08
,
MRS21
]
. We remark that weaker bounds are also immediate consequences of higher order Cheeger inequalities
[
LRTV12
,
LOGT14
]
.

Limiting our discussion to
\textnormal{{mul}}_{\lambda_{2}}(G)\,,
in the context of Abelian Cayley graphs the most relevant work is by Lee and Makarychev
[
LM08
]
. Their notion of volume growth is the
doubling constant
:
\gamma_{G}:=\max_{t\geqslant 0}|B(2t)|/|B(t)|
, where
B(t)
is the ball of radius
t
about the identity element of
\Gamma
. By vertex transitivity the choice of the center of the ball is inconsequential.
The importance of the doubling constant stems from the observation that it can be used to bound the packing number
\mathcal{N}(t)
of the graph
G\,.

Building on a series of existing results
[
CM97
,
Kle10
,
GKL03
]
, Lee and Makarychev use this observation to establish

\displaystyle\textnormal{{mul}}_{\lambda_{2}}(G)\leqslant\mathcal{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1/(\gamma_{G}^{O(1)}\cdot\sqrt{\lambda_{2}})}}\right)\leqslant\gamma_{G}^{O(\log\gamma_{G})}\,,

where the second step leveraged the inequality
\lambda_{2}\leqslant O(\log(\gamma_{G})/\diam(G))^{2}\,.

Because Abelian Cayley graphs are known to have polynomial growth
\gamma_{G}\leqslant 2^{O(d)}

[
DSC94
]
, this allows them to conclude
\textnormal{{mul}}_{\lambda_{2}}(G)\leqslant 2^{O(d^{2})}\,.

We take a much more direct (and arguably significantly simpler) approach which directly relates spectral and combinatorial quantities.
The starting point is the notion of
t
-step collision probability
\textup{{cp}}_{t}\,.

The
t
-step collision probability equals the probability that two independent walks of length
t
have the same endpoint when starting from the same vertex, sampled from the stationary distribution of
G
.
4
4

4

A technical detail is that our analysis uses
lazy
random walks.
Thus
1/\textup{{cp}}_{t}
serves as a probabilistic analog of the size of the ball
B(t)
(a similar notion was also used in
[
MRS21
]
).
This motivates us to consider the following “smooth” version of the doubling constant:

\gamma^{CP}_{G}=\max_{t\geqslant 0}\frac{\textup{{cp}}_{t}}{\textup{{cp}}_{2t}}.

An important fact is that the collision probability equals the average of the eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix of
G^{2t}\,,
the
{2t}
-th power of our starting graph. That is, we have for
t\geqslant 0\,,

\displaystyle\textup{{cp}}_{t}=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-\lambda_{i})^{2t}\,.

(2.2)

This is the
2t
-th norm of the eigenvalues, also called the
2t
-th Schatten norm of the adjacency matrix of
G\,.

The crucial consequence of the expressiveness of
Eq.

2.2
is that the collision probability ratio is then entirely captured by the spectrum of
G\,:

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{{cp}}_{t}}{\textup{{cp}}_{2t}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-\lambda_{i})^{2t}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-\lambda_{i})^{4t}}\,.

(2.3)

And now the key insight is that for an appropriate choice of
t=\Theta(\log\textnormal{{mul}}_{\lambda_{2}}(G)/\lambda_{2})\,,

Eq.

2.3
relates the collision probability ratio, the multiplicity of
\lambda_{2}
and the eigenvalues of
G\,:

\displaystyle\textnormal{{mul}}_{\lambda_{2}}(G)^{\Omega(1)}\leqslant\frac{\textup{{cp}}_{t}}{\textup{{cp}}_{2t}}\leqslant\gamma^{CP}_{G}\,.

The observation that, by taking longer random walks, we can learn critical information about the spectrum and avoid constructing a large packing, is the fundamental improvement over
[
LM08
]
.
Our inequality is valid for all regular graphs and hence immediately implies a bound on the eigenvalue multiplicities for all graphs that satisfy a bound on the collision probability ratio.

Our second key contribution is an upper bound on
\gamma^{CP}_{G}
for all Abelian Cayley graphs. To prove this statement, our challenge is to relate walks (not balls) of length
t
to those of length
2t
in an Abelian Cayley graph.
First, we can see that in an Abelian Cayley graph, the endpoint of a walk is completely determined by how many times each generator is chosen, and not on their order.
Therefore, we may replace the random walk by a random draw from a multinomial distribution, using
t
samples of
d
items each occurring with probability
1/d
.

From here we can directly relate the multinomial density for
t
to that of
2t
.

The binomial densities for
t
and
2t
are approximately Gaussians
X
and
Y,
where
Y
has twice variance of
X
,
leading to a direct comparison inequality on the probability density functions,
p_{Y}(x)\leqslant 2p_{X}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
For
d
generators, this idea can be extended (with additional arguments) to obtain a comparison equality with factor
2^{O(d)}
which then implies

\displaystyle\textnormal{{mul}}_{\lambda_{2}}(G)^{\Omega(1)}\leqslant\gamma^{CP}_{G}\leqslant 2^{O(d)}\,.

(2.4)

The bound in
Eq.

2.4
is best possible up to the constant in the exponent: there exist Abelian Cayley graphs
{\textup{{Cay}}}(\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{2},S)
with
\textnormal{{mul}}_{\lambda_{2}}\geqslant 2^{\Omega(d)}
. The construction of such graphs comes from a characterization of eigenvalue multiplicity in Cayley graphs over
\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{2}
in terms of binary linear codes. Every Cayley graph
{\textup{{Cay}}}(\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{2},S)
corresponds to a binary linear code of dimension
k
and block length
|S|=d
with the multiplicity of
\lambda_{2}
corresponding to the number of code words of minimum weight. Thus, the problem of constructing Abelian Cayley graphs with large eigenvalue multiplicity is equivalent to constructing binary linear codes with many code words of minimum weight. Using algebraic geometry codes, Ashikhmin, Barg, Vlădut̨
[
ABV01
]
constructed a family of binary linear codes with linear rate and distance, and with an exponential number of minimum-weight codewords, which furnishes the desired construction.

Sparse cuts are approximately low-dimensional (Theorem
1.11
)

To show that a partition
(Q,V(G)\setminus Q)
in a graph
G
approximately lives in the span of the first few eigenvectors, we observe that it suffices to relate its conductance in
G
, with its conductance in
G^{2t}
in the sense:

\displaystyle\phi_{G^{2t}}(Q)\leqslant\sqrt{t}\cdot\ell\cdot\phi_{G}(Q),

(2.5)

for some sufficiently large
t>0
and small
\ell>0\,.

Recall now that the expansion can be expressed spectrally as the Rayleigh quotient of the vector
\mathbf{1}_{Q}
. Let
\mathbf{1}_{Q}=\sum_{i\geqslant 1}q_{i}v_{i}
be the representation of the indicator vector of the set
Q
in the eigenbasis
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
of the graph. Then the above argument implies

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}^{2}\cdot(1-(1-\lambda_{i})^{2t})\leqslant\sqrt{t}\cdot\ell\cdot\phi_{G}(Q)\,.

From this inequality, simple manipulations show that with the choice
t=\Theta(\varepsilon^{2}\ell^{-1}\phi^{-2})
all but
\varepsilon
of the spectral mass of
\bar{\mathbf{1}}_{Q}
must be contained on eigenvalues up to
1/t
.

To establish a version of
Eq.

2.5
for Abelian Cayley graphs,
we leverage the analysis of Buser inequality
[
KKRT16
,
OT21
]
.
The proof by Oveis Gharan and Trevisan
[
OT21
]
studies the probability of a length-
2t
random walk
X_{0},\ldots,X_{2t}
crossing an arbitrary cut
Q
.
This measures the expansion of
Q
in the graph
G^{2t}
and their combinatorial analysis proves that
Eq.

2.5
holds for
\ell=\Theta(\sqrt{d})\,.

As before, the crucial property of Abelian Cayley graphs being used is that it suffices to count how many times each generator is used by the walk.
In the proof of this inequality, it is also important that generators
g
and
-g
cancel out, which allows to bound the expected number of steps in the direction of generator
g
by only
O(\sqrt{t/d})
.
Finally, because this argument does not rely on the specific cut
Q\,,
we immediately obtain that for Abelian Cayley graphs all sparsest cut approximately live in the span of the first
O(\phi^{2}\cdot d/\varepsilon^{2})
eigenvectors.

4.1
Eigenspace enumeration for Sparsest Cut

Next, we show how to derive an eigenspace enumeration algorithm for

using the algorithm for

Recalling the definition of solution dimension

from the introduction, we will prove:

Theorem

(Restatement of

admits a

Algorithm 4.6

Input:

(1)

(2)

(3)

For each enumerated vector

(4)

There are at most

We use the following Lemma.

Remark 4.9
.

Eigenspace enumeration can also be viewed as searching for a hyperplane cut in the spectral embedding.
That is, letting

be the sorted eigenvalues of the normalized Laplacian and

be the associated eigenvectors,
the

Then,

\text{embedding with normal vector }\vec{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\,.\end{array}

In this way, eigenspace enumeration is equivalent to brute-force searching over all possible hyperplane cuts in a certain low-dimensional embedding of the graph. It is interesting to compare this with the Goemans–Williamson hyperplane rounding technique which computes a random hyperplane cut in the SDP embedding of the graph

5
The low eigenspace of Abelian Cayley graphs

In this section, we study the low eigenspace of Abelian Cayley graphs and prove
Theorem

1.10
. We do so by analyzing the collision probability
of a random walk in
G
. For a probability distribution
\pi
, the
collision probability
of
\pi
is defined by
\textup{{cp}}(\pi)=\|\pi\|^{2}_{2}=\mathbb{P}_{x,x^{\prime}\sim\pi}(x=x^{\prime})\,.

Definition 5.1

(
t
-step lazy collision probability)
.

Let
G
be a graph and
\pi
be the stationary distribution for
\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{I}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{W}
.
The
t
-step lazy collision probability
is defined by
\textup{{cp}}_{t}=\E_{x\sim\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\textup{{cp}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{I}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{W}}}\right)^{t}\mathbf{1}_{x}}}\right)}}\right]
.

Recall that the stationary distribution is proportional to the degree,
and is the uniform distribution when the graph is regular. Note that for vertex-transitive graphs such as Abelian Cayley graphs, the collision probability satisfies
\textup{{cp}}_{t}=\textup{{cp}}((\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{I}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{W})^{t}\mathbf{1}_{x})
for all vertices
x
.
The next Lemma gives a spectral interpretation of
\textup{{cp}}_{t}
as the power sums of the eigenvalues of the normalized adjacency matrix (a.k.a the moments of the empirical spectral distribution).

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
G
be a regular graph. Then

\textup{{cp}}_{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{i}}{2}}}\right)^{2t}.

Proof.

Let
X_{0},X_{1},\dots,X_{2t}
be a simple random walk initialized at
X_{0}
drawn from the stationary distribution.
Let
\tilde{X}_{0},\dots,\tilde{X}_{t}
be an independent simple random walk initialized at
X_{0}
.
Since the simple random walk is a reversible Markov chain,

\textup{{cp}}_{t}=\ProbOp(X_{t}=\tilde{X}_{t})=\ProbOp(X_{2t}=X_{0})
.

On the other hand, the diagonal elements of the transition matrix equal the returning probabilities of a random walk. Therefore

\displaystyle\textup{{cp}}_{t}=\E_{x\sim\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{I}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{W}}}\right)^{2t}_{x,x}}}\right]=\frac{1}{n}\Tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{I}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{W}}}\right)^{2t}}}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{i}}{2}}}\right)^{2t},

as desired. The second equality uses that
G
is regular, and the last equality uses that
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{A}
have the same spectrum.
∎

To bound the multiplicity of eigenvalues close to
\lambda_{2}
, we analyze the ratio
\textup{{cp}}_{t}/\textup{{cp}}_{t(\kappa+1)}
where
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
\kappa\geqslant 1
are parameters.
Interestingly, the argument works for all regular graphs and not only for Abelian Cayley graphs.
We will derive
Theorem

1.10
as a consequence of the next Lemma.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
G
be a connected regular graph on
n
vertices. Suppose
\lambda_{2}\leqslant\tau\leqslant\frac{3}{2}
and let
\kappa=\lceil\tau/\lambda_{2}\rceil
. Then for

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{{cp}}_{t}}{\textup{{cp}}_{t(\kappa+1)}}\geqslant\textnormal{{mul}}_{\tau}^{1/3}/(2e^{3/2})\,.

First notice that for any

it holds

since
\lambda_{2}\leqslant\lambda_{i}\leqslant 2\,.
By applying

\frac{\textup{{cp}}_{t}}{\textup{{cp}}_{t(\kappa+1)}}=\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}(1-\lambda_{i}/2)^{2t}}{\sum_{i\in[n]}(1-\lambda_{i}/2)^{2t(\kappa+1)}}=\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}(1-\lambda_{i}/2)^{2t}}{1+\sum_{i\geqslant 2}(1-\lambda_{i}/2)^{2t(\kappa+1)}}.

We show the following lower bound,

\sum_{i\in[n]}(1-\lambda_{i}/2)^{2t}\geqslant\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\textnormal{{mul}}_{\tau}\cdot e^{-2t\tau},e^{t\tau}\sum_{i\geqslant 2}(1-\lambda_{i}/2)^{2t(\kappa+1)}}}\right).

(5.1)

First observe that if
\lambda_{i}\leqslant\tau

(1-\lambda_{i}/2)^{2t}\geqslant(1-\tau/2)^{2t}

8
Sparsest cut in
\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p}

sparsest cut
over the Boolean hypercube
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k}
can be solved exactly by a different type of spectral algorithm. The eigenvectors of
\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{2}
are
\pm 1
-valued (the Boolean Fourier characters) which implies that (1) the lower bound in the Cheeger inequality is exact
\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_{2}=\phi(G)\,
, (2) there is an eigenvector which sign-indicates a sparsest cut.

In this section, we show that there is a simple polynomial time algorithm that computes a
O(p)
-approximation for sparsest cut on undirected Cayley graphs over
\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p}
, where
p
is prime.

Proposition 8.1
.

There exists a polynomial time algorithm that computes an
O(p)
-approximation on undirected Cayley graphs over
\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p}
.

The idea is given
G={\textup{{Cay}}}(\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p},S)
, we define a related graph
G^{\prime}={\textup{{Cay}}}(\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p},S^{\prime})
such that

1.

\phi(G^{\prime})
is a
O(p)
-approximation of
\phi(G)
, and

2.

\lambda_{2}(G^{\prime})
is a
O(1)
-approximation of
\phi(G^{\prime})
.

Combining both
(1)
and
(2)
implies
\lambda_{2}(G^{\prime})
is an
O(p)
-approximation of
\phi(G)
. Hence, the polynomial time
O(p)
-approximation algorithm for sparsest cut on
\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p}
is computing
\lambda_{2}(G^{\prime})
. In fact, our proof shows that the subspace orthogonal to
g
where
\chi_{g}
is an eigenvector corresponding to
\lambda_{2}(G^{\prime})
provides an
O(p)
-approximation to
\phi(G)
.

Let
G={\textup{{Cay}}}(\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p},S)
. Consider
G^{\prime}={\textup{{Cay}}}(\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p},S^{\prime})
, where
S^{\prime}
is the multiset of size
\frac{p-1}{2}|S|
containing
\cup_{\ell\in[(p-1)/2]}\ell S
, where
\ell S=\{\ell s:s\in S\}
. The set
S^{\prime}
is obtained from
S
by taking all non-zero multiples of
S
up to
(p-1)/2
. By symmetry of
S
, the multiset
S^{\prime}
contains all non-zero multiples of each
x\in S
. For each
\ell\neq 0
, the graph
G_{\ell}={\textup{{Cay}}}(\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p},\ell S)
is isomorphic to
{\textup{{Cay}}}(\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p},S)
. In this section we use the fact that the eigenvectors of Cayley graphs over
\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p}
are given by
\chi_{g}(x)=e^{2\pi i\langle x,g\rangle/p}
, where
g\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p}
(see chapter
16
of
[
Tre17
]
).

The lemma below shows that
\phi(G)
and
\phi(G^{\prime})
differ by at most a factor of
(p+1)/4
.

Lemma 8.2
.

The graphs
G
and
G^{\prime}
satisfy that,

\phi(G)\leqslant\phi(G^{\prime})\leqslant\frac{p+1}{4}\phi(G).

Proof.

We begin with the lower bound
\phi(G)\leqslant\phi(G^{\prime})
. Let
Q\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p}
such that
|Q|\leqslant p^{k}/2
. Observe that
E_{G^{\prime}}(Q,\bar{Q})=\sum_{\ell\in[(p-1)/2]}E_{G_{\ell}}(Q,\bar{Q})
. Since
G_{\ell}
is isomorphic to
G
for all
\ell\in[(p-1)/2]
, we have
E_{G_{\ell}}(Q,\bar{Q})\geqslant\phi(G)|S||Q|
. This implies

\displaystyle\phi_{G^{\prime}}(Q)

\displaystyle=\frac{E_{G^{\prime}}(Q,\bar{Q})}{|S^{\prime}||Q|}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\ell\in[(p-1)/2]}E_{G_{\ell}}(Q,\bar{Q})}{|S^{\prime}||Q|}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\frac{p-1}{2}|S||Q|\phi(G)}{|S^{\prime}||Q|}

\displaystyle=\phi(G).

Hence,
\phi(G^{\prime})\geqslant\phi(G)
.

Now, we prove the upper bound
\phi(G^{\prime})\leqslant\frac{p+1}{4}\phi(G)
. Observe that we can write the number of edges cut by
Q
as
E_{G}(Q,\bar{Q})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s\in S}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p}}(\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x)-\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x+s))^{2}
and
E_{G^{\prime}}(Q,\bar{Q})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell\in[(p-1)/2]}\sum_{s\in S}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p}}(\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x)-\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x+\ell s))^{2}
. The terms
(\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x)-\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x+\ell s))^{2}
satisfy a “triangle inequality”

(\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x)-\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x+\ell s))^{2}\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}(\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x+(i-1)s)-\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x+is))^{2}.

Note that for each fixed
i\in[\ell]
,
\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p}}(\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x+(i-1)s)-\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x+is))^{2}=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p}}(\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x)-\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x+s))^{2}
. This implies,

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p}}(\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x)-\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x+\ell s))^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}(\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x+(i-1)s)-\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x+is))^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\ell\sum_{x}(\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x)-\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x+s))^{2}.

Using this we obtain the following bound on
E_{G^{\prime}}(Q,\bar{Q})
in terms of
E_{G}(Q,\bar{Q})

\displaystyle E_{G^{\prime}}(Q,\bar{Q})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell\in[(p-1)/2]}\sum_{s\in S}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{p}_{n}}(\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x)-\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x+\ell s))^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell\in[(p-1)/2]}\sum_{s\in S}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p}}(\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x)-\mathbf{1}_{Q}(x+s))^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(p-1)(p+1)}{8}E_{G}(Q,\bar{Q}).

Dividing by
|S^{\prime}||Q|
, we obtain
\phi_{G^{\prime}}(Q)\leqslant\frac{p+1}{4}\phi(Q)
. Thus,
\phi(G^{\prime})\leqslant\frac{p+1}{4}\phi(G)
, as desired.
∎

To conclude the analysis, we show that
\lambda_{2}(G^{\prime})
and
\phi(G^{\prime})
differ by a factor of at most
1/2
. Our proof makes use of a well-known lemma which can be found in
[
HLW06
]
.

Lemma 8.3
.

[
HLW06
]

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph,
\mathbf{L}
the normalized Laplacian of
G
, and
\gamma
an eigenvector corresponding to
\lambda_{2}(G)
. Define
\gamma_{+}
by
\gamma_{+}(x)=\max\{f(x),0\}
and
\gamma_{-}
by
\gamma_{-}(x)=\min\{\gamma(x),0\}
. Then
\gamma_{+},\gamma_{-}
are disjointly supported and satisfy

\frac{\gamma^{T}_{-}\mathbf{L}_{G^{\prime}}\gamma_{-}}{\gamma^{T}_{-}\gamma_{-}}\leqslant\lambda_{2}(G),\qquad\frac{\gamma^{T}_{+}\mathbf{L}_{G^{\prime}}\gamma_{+}}{\gamma^{T}_{+}\gamma_{+}}\leqslant\lambda_{2}(G).

Lemma 8.4
.

The graph
G^{\prime}
satisfies

\frac{\lambda_{2}(G^{\prime})}{2}\leqslant\phi(G^{\prime})\leqslant\lambda_{2}(G^{\prime}).

Proof.

The first inequality is a direct application of Cheeger’s inequality. It remains to prove the second inequality
\phi(G^{\prime})\leqslant\lambda_{2}(G^{\prime})
.
Let
\ell\neq 0
and
g\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p}
and
\chi_{g}
be the corresponding eigenvector. We claim that if
\chi_{g}
is an eigenvector corresponding to
\lambda_{2}(G^{\prime})
, then so is
\chi_{\ell g}
. By definition, for every
s\in S^{\prime}
the element
\ell s\in S^{\prime}
occurs with the same multiplicity. This implies
\sum_{s\in S^{\prime}}\chi_{g}(s)=\sum_{s\in S^{\prime}}\chi_{\ell g}(s)
. Hence,
\chi_{\ell g}
is also an eigenvector corresponding to
\lambda_{2}(G^{\prime})
.

Consider the symmetrized eigenvector
\gamma=\sum_{\ell\in[p-1]}\chi_{\ell g}(s)
corresponding to
\lambda_{2}(G^{\prime})
. One can see that for each
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p}
, the vector
\gamma
satisfies

\gamma(x)=\begin{cases}p-1&\text{if }\langle g,x\rangle=0\\
-1&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Define
\gamma_{+}
by
\gamma_{+}(x)=\max\{\gamma(x),0\}
. Observe that
\gamma_{+}=(p-1)\mathbf{1}_{Q}
, where
Q=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{p}:\langle g,x\rangle=0\}
is the subspace orthogonal to
g
. Applying Lemma

, we obtain

\phi(G^{\prime})\leqslant\phi_{G^{\prime}}(Q)=\frac{\mathbf{1}^{T}_{Q}\mathbf{L}_{G^{\prime}}\mathbf{1}_{Q}}{\mathbf{1}^{T}_{Q}\mathbf{1}_{Q}}=\frac{\gamma^{T}_{+}\mathbf{L}_{G^{\prime}}\gamma_{+}}{\gamma^{T}_{+}\gamma_{+}}\leqslant\lambda_{2}(G^{\prime}).

∎

Let

Low threshold rank implies graph decomposition

Proposition A.1
.

Let
G
be a graph and
2\geqslant\tau\geqslant 0
.
If
\textnormal{{mul}}_{\tau}(G)=r\,,
then
V(G)
has a partition into
H_{1},\dots,H_{r^{\prime}}
with
r^{\prime}\leqslant r+1
such that:

(i)

\phi_{G}(H_{i})\leqslant\tau
for all
i=1,\dots,r^{\prime}
except possibly
i=r^{\prime}

(ii)

\phi_{G}(K)>\tau
for all
i=1,\dots,r^{\prime}
and subgraphs
K\subset H_{i}\,.

Proof.

Maintain a set
V
which is initially equal to
V(G)\,
.
Select the smallest subgraph
H\subseteq V
such that
\phi_{G}(H)\leqslant\tau\,,
if any. We output
H
as a piece of the partition, then remove it and recursively proceed on
V\leftarrow V\setminus H
.
We take the final piece of the partition to be
V
once the process terminates.

It is clear that this generates a partition of
V(G)\,,
and that conditions (i) and (ii) are satisfied.
It remains to show
r^{\prime}\leqslant r+1
.

Each of the subgraphs
H_{i}
except for the last one has expansion at most
\tau\,.
Therefore, by
Fact

3.7
, we have a collection of
r^{\prime}-1
disjointly-supported (thus orthogonal) vectors
\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{1}_{H_{i}}
whose Rayleigh quotients are at most
\tau
.
This implies
\lambda_{r^{\prime}-1}(G)\leqslant\tau\,,

as can be proven using the variational formula for the eigenvalues:

\lambda_{k}(G)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\\
v_{i}\perp v_{j}\end{subarray}}\;\max_{v\in\linspan(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})}\frac{v^{T}\mathbf{L}(G)v}{v^{T}v}\,.

We conclude

.
∎

Appendix B
Solution dimension and threshold rank

B.1
Solution dimension of the cycle graph

The fact that the solution dimension can be smaller than the
\phi
-threshold-rank is unexpected because every cut must have some component in the eigenspace to at least
\phi
.
This can be seen using the spectral interpretation of the conductance in
Fact

3.7
, which says that for all
Q\subseteq[n]
, assuming
G
is regular,

\displaystyle\phi_{G}(Q)=\frac{\mathbf{1}_{Q}^{T}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{1}_{Q}}{\mathbf{1}_{Q}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{Q}}

\displaystyle=\E_{i\sim\mathcal{S}(Q)}[\lambda_{i}]

(B.1)

where
\mathcal{S}(Q)
is the distribution on
[n]
proportional to
\langle\mathbf{1}_{Q},v_{i}\rangle^{2}
and
v_{i}
is an eigenbasis for
G
.

Eq.

B.1
is at least the expansion parameter
\phi
, implying that every Boolean vector
\mathbf{1}_{Q}

must have some component in the eigenspaces to at least
\phi
.
In other words,
\textup{{SD}}_{0}(G)\geqslant\textnormal{{mul}}_{\phi}(G)\,,

and in order to exactly recover a cut indicator, it is necessary to explore
the entire eigenspace up to at least
\phi
.
Conversely, Markov’s inequality on
Eq.

B.1
shows that every cut satisfying
\phi_{G}(Q)\leqslant 2\phi(G)
must have all but
\varepsilon
fraction of its weight on eigenvalues up to
2\varepsilon^{-1}\phi
.

Surprisingly, it may still be that only a tiny fraction of
\mathbf{1}_{Q}
lies in the eigenspaces above
\phi
, i.e.,
\textup{{SD}}_{\varepsilon}(G)\ll\textnormal{{mul}}_{\phi}(G)
.

For example, the cycle graph
G={\textup{{Cay}}}(\mathbb{Z}_{n},\{\pm 1\})
has
\phi=\Theta(\frac{1}{n})

and the sparsest cuts are the bisections of the cycle into two halves.
An eigenbasis for the cycle consists of the Fourier characters,

\displaystyle v_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:\mathbb{Z}_{n}\to\mathbb{C}\,,

\displaystyle v_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle=\exp(2\pi i\cdot\alpha x/n)

for
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}
with normalized Laplacian eigenvalue
\lambda_{\alpha}=1-\cos(2\pi\alpha/n)
.
Estimating
1-\cos(2\pi\alpha/n)=\Theta(\alpha^{2}/n^{2})
for
\alpha=o(n)

we obtain
\textnormal{{mul}}_{\phi}=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
.

In contrast we compute that
\textup{{SD}}_{\varepsilon}=\Theta(1)
for the cycle graph, which may be predicted since the eigenvector to
\lambda_{2}
already sign-indicates a sparsest cut.
For simplicity, assume
4|n
and let
Q^{*}=\{-\frac{n}{4},-\frac{n}{4}+1,\dots,\frac{n}{4}-1\}
be an optimal cut.
Let the eigenbasis representation of
Q^{*}
be
\mathbf{1}_{Q^{*}}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}q_{\alpha}v_{\alpha}
.
We compute,

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in Q^{*}}v_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\Theta(\frac{n}{\alpha})&\alpha\text{ odd}\\
0&\alpha\text{ even}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\implies q_{\alpha}=\frac{\sum_{x\in Q^{*}}v_{\alpha}(x)}{\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}|v_{\alpha}(x)|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\Theta(\frac{1}{\alpha})&\alpha\text{ odd}\\
0&\alpha\text{ even}\end{cases}

Therefore, the squared weight on eigenspace
\alpha
decreases at the rate
\Theta(\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}})
.
Summing this up, the total squared weight beyond the
1/\varepsilon
-th eigenvalue is
\Theta(\varepsilon)
.
At the same time, in the equation,

\displaystyle\phi(Q^{*})

\displaystyle=\E_{\alpha\sim\mathcal{S}(Q^{*})}[\lambda_{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}}\Theta(\tfrac{1}{\alpha^{2}})\cdot\Theta(\alpha^{2}/n^{2})

\displaystyle=\Theta(\tfrac{1}{n})

each Fourier level is contributing the same amount
\Theta(\frac{1}{n^{2}})
towards the expansion of
Q^{*}
.

B.2
Threshold rank upper bounds solution dimension

We formally show here that the solution dimension is upper bounded by the
\psi
-threshold rank.

Fact B.1
.

Let
G
be a graph and let
0<\varepsilon<1\,.

Then
\textup{{SD}}_{\varepsilon}(G)\leqslant\textnormal{{mul}}_{\psi(G)/\varepsilon}(G)\,.

Proof.

Let
Q\subseteq V(G)
be any sparsest cut. Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
be the eigenbasis for
G
, and expand
\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{1}_{Q}
in the eigenbasis as,

\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{1}_{Q}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}v_{i}\,.

The normalized density is, using
Fact

3.7
,

\psi_{G}(Q)=\psi(G)=\frac{\bar{\mathbf{1}}_{Q}^{T}\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\bar{\mathbf{1}}_{Q}}{\bar{\mathbf{1}}_{Q}^{T}\mathbf{D}\bar{\mathbf{1}}_{Q}}=\frac{\sum_{i=2}^{n}q_{i}^{2}\lambda_{i}}{\sum_{i=2}^{n}q_{i}^{2}}\,.

The last quantity can be interpreted as the expected value of
\lambda_{i}
for
i\in[n]\setminus\{1\}
drawn with probability proportional to
q_{i}^{2}
.
Since
\lambda_{i}\geqslant 0
we can apply Markov’s inequality.
For all
\tau>0
we have:

\frac{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[n]:\\
\lambda_{i}\geqslant\tau\end{subarray}}q_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{i=2}^{n}q_{i}^{2}}\leqslant\frac{\psi(G)}{\tau}\,.

Taking
\tau=\psi(G)/\varepsilon
, the right-hand side becomes
\varepsilon
.
Hence
\mathbf{D}^{1/2}\bar{\mathbf{1}}_{Q}
has all but
\varepsilon
fraction of its spectral mass on eigenvalues up to
\tau=\psi(G)/\varepsilon
which implies
\textup{{SD}}_{\varepsilon}(G)\leqslant\textnormal{{mul}}_{\psi(G)/\varepsilon}
.
∎
