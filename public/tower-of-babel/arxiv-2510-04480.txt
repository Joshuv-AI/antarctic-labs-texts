On Continuous Optimization for Constraint Satisfaction Problems
Yunuo Cen, Zixuan Wang, Jintao Zhang, Zhiwei Zhang, Xuanyao Fong

Abstract
Constraint satisfaction problems (CSPs) are fundamental in mathematics, physics, and theoretical computer science. While conflict-driven clause learning Boolean Satisfiability (SAT) solvers have achieved remarkable success and become the mainstream approach for Boolean satisfiability, recent advances show that modern continuous local search (CLS) solvers can achieve highly competitive results on certain classes of SAT problems. Motivated by these advances, we extend the CLS framework from Boolean SAT to general CSP with finite-domain variables and expressive constraints. We present FourierCSP, a continuous optimization framework that generalizes the Walsh-Fourier transform to CSP, allowing for transforming versatile constraints to compact multilinear polynomials, thereby avoiding the need for auxiliary variables and memory-intensive encodings. Our approach leverages efficient evaluation and differentiation of the objective via circuit-output probability and employs a projected gradient optimization method with theoretical guarantees. Empirical results on benchmark suites demonstrate that FourierCSP is scalable and competitive, significantly broadening the class of problems that can be efficiently solved by CLS techniques.



Introduction

Constraint Satisfaction Problems (CSPs) are foundational in mathematics, physics, and theoretical computer science.
Given a set of variables with domains taken from finite sets, and a set of constraints on the variables, i.e., a CSP instance, solving the instance is to find an assignment of variables that satisfies all the constraints.
Applications of CSP solving are in artificial intelligence
(
Baluta et al. 2019
)
, database query
(
Kolaitis and Vardi 1998
)
, operational research
(
Brailsford, Potts, and Smith 1999
)
, statistical physics
(
Nagy et al. 2024
)
, information theory
(
Golia, Juba, and Meel 2022
)
, quantum computing
(
Vardi and Zhang 2022
)
, and so on.

Most CSP solvers can be categorized into two types: systematic search and local search.
Systematic methods are often built upon the Branch-and-Bound (BnB) framework, which systematically explores the solution space while pruning infeasible branches.
Notably, SAT solving, driven by Conflict-Driven Clause Learning (CDCL),
i.e.
, BnB with clause learning
(
Davis and Putnam 1960
;
Davis, Logemann, and Loveland 1962
;
Marques-Silva and Sakallah 1999
)
, has shown remarkable engineering success, routinely handling industrial-scale problems involving millions of variables
(
Vardi 2014
)
.
Nevertheless, SAT solvers primarily handle Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF) formulas, necessitating Booleanizations
(
Huang 2008
)
and subsequent CNF encodings
(
Prestwich 2021
)
, potentially leading to substantial increases in formula size and inefficiency.

Integer Linear Programming (ILP) provides a more general formulation than CNF clauses, permitting more compact CSP representations through linearization, and utilizes linear programming relaxations to prune the search space efficiently.
However, ILP solvers generally exhibit weaker Boolean reasoning capabilities than SAT solvers
(
Elffers and Nordström 2018
)
.
Constraint programming (CP) offers a native approach, directly accepting constraints expressed via a broad range of predicates over diverse variable types
(
Krupke et al. 2024
)
.
The research community of CP has developed multiple open-source solvers
(
Schulte, Lagerkvist, and Tack 2006
;
Prud’homme and Fages 2022
;
Hebrard 2008
;
Kuchcinski and Szymanek 2013
)
.
However, they typically do not scale as efficiently as ILP solvers and are less effective at constrained optimization problems.
The OR-Tools CP-SAT solver, a portfolio solver with SAT, CP, has effectively addressed many limitations of traditional CP solvers and emerged as a robust alternative to ILP solvers, demonstrating competitive performance across a wide range of problems
(
Perron, Didier, and Gay 2023
)
.

Systematic search solvers are complete; they can prove unsatisfiability or optimality if provided sufficient computation time.
In contrast, local search methods aim to discover feasible solutions efficiently without proving unsatisfiability.
Local search methods can be categorized into discrete local search (DLS) and continuous local search (CLS).
DLS iteratively changes the value of variables to maximize the number of satisfied constraints
(
Mitchell, Selman, and Leveque 1992
;
Selman et al. 1993
)
.
CLS, is initiated by UniSAT
(
Gu 1994
)
and followed by FourierSAT
(
Kyrillidis et al. 2020
)
, GradSAT
(
Kyrillidis, Vardi, and Zhang 2021
)
, and FastFourierSAT
(
Cen, Zhang, and Fong 2025
)
.
CLS-based SAT solvers have been recently actively studied as a novel line of SAT frameworks.
These solvers transform SAT into a polynomial optimization problem over the real domain and apply gradient-based optimizers.
The latest work on this line, named FastFourierSAT, reveals that CLS solvers can be highly parallelizable and amenable to GPU accelerators.
Recent theoretical and engineering efforts help CLS solvers achieve state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance on certain benchmarks in SAT solving,
e.g.
, parity learning with error problems
(
Crawford, Kearns, and Schapire 1994
)
.

Contributions

Despite promising advancements, current CLS methods are restricted to Boolean domains, limiting applicability to problems with inherently finite, multi-valued variables.
In this work, we extend the FourierSAT framework to general CSPs, overcoming the significant challenge of directly mapping finite-domain variable domains to continuous optimization domains.
We introduce an orthonormal product Fourier basis, allowing each constraint to be represented as a linear combination of these bases.
The constraints are then reduced to a continuous polynomial with randomized rounding, where the input represents the probability of an assignment, and the output corresponds to the expectation of the constraint being satisfied.
The polynomial representation of a constraint,
i.e.
, the Walsh-Fourier expansion (WFE), can have an exponential number of terms, and a naive implementation is memory- and time-inefficient.
In this work, we show that the WFE can be represented by the circuit-output probability (COP) of a decision diagram, which can be efficiently evaluated and differentiated.

We implemented our approach as FourierCSP with projected gradient ascent,
The benchmark instances include both constraint satisfaction and optimization problems, including task scheduling and graph coloring with hashing query problems.
Our approach is accelerated with GPU parallelization and compared with SOTA SAT-based, ILP-based, and CP-based complete solvers.
Despite the incompleteness, our solver obtained encouraging results on CSP solving.
To the best of our knowledge, this paper is the first algorithmic work to introduce continuous optimization to CSP.
This opens a new research direction, enabling the integration of future continuous optimization techniques and advanced parallel hardware.

Limitations of Previous CLS Solvers

The entire lineage of CLS solvers, from the 1990s approaches
(
Gu 1994
)
to modern variants like FourierSAT, FastFourierSAT, and GradSAT, is exclusively designed for Boolean constraints.
While they perform well on certain problem classes, these solvers often fail to scale effectively to large or complex CSP instances.
This limitation becomes particularly acute for finite-domain problems, which require conversion to potentially larger Boolean formulas.
This limitation fundamentally restricts their practical application for real-world problems that naturally require finite-domain representations.

FourierCSP

To address the above limitation, we propose FourierCSP, a CLS framework that directly supports finite-domain CSPs without requiring Booleanization.
We start by applying randomized rounding to the generalized form of WFE in Theorem
1
.
With the randomized rounding, we can reduce a constraint satisfaction problem to a continuous optimization problem.
Subsequently, we reveal that the objective function is equivalent to the circuit-output probability of a decision diagram.
Hence, the computational complexity of objective evaluation,
i.e.
, one of the key bottlenecks for continuous optimizers, scales with the size of the decision diagram.
Moreover, we derive practical convergence bounds that gradient-based methods can achieve within our framework.

Reduction by Randomized Rounding

Theorem
1
illustrates how to expand discrete functions to multilinear polynomials through Walsh-Fourier expansion.
The resulting Fourier coefficients
\hat{f}(\alpha)
provide valuable insights into analyzing the discrete function properties.
However, the expanded function
f
remains discrete and non-differentiable given the Fourier Bases in Definition
1
, limiting its utility for gradient-based optimization.

To enable continuous optimization, we introduce a randomized rounding
\mathcal{R}
that relaxes discrete variable assignments into a continuous domain.
Consider a set of finite domain variables
X=\{x_{1},...,x_{n}\}
.
The vector probability space of
x_{i}
is a simplex denoted by

\tilde{D}_{i}:=\left\{p_{i,j}\Bigg|\min_{j\in\mathrm{dom}(x_{i})}p_{i,j}\geq{}0,\sum_{j\in\mathrm{dom}(x_{i})}p_{i,j}=1\right\}.

The collection of all simplexes forms a continuous search space
\tilde{D}=\left(\tilde{D}_{1}\times\cdots\times\tilde{D}_{n}\right)
.

Definition 3

(Randomized Rounding)
.

Given a finite domain
D
.
The randomized rounding function, denoted by
\mathcal{R}:\tilde{D}\to{}D
is defined by

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{R}(P)_{i}=j]=p_{i,j}

for all
j\in\mathrm{dom}(x_{i})
,
p_{i}\in\tilde{D}_{i}
and
i\in[n]
.

Intuitively, if
p_{i,j}
is closer to
1
, then
\mathcal{R}(a)_{i}
is more likely to be rounded to
j
.
Using this randomized interpretation, we can define a probability distribution over full assignments by rounding
\mathcal{R}
of each variable independently.

Definition 4

(Probability Space)
.

We define a probability space on a discrete matrix w.r.t. to real point
P\in\tilde{D}
, denoted by
\mathcal{S}:D\to\tilde{D}
, as:

\mathcal{S}_{P}(X)=\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{R}(P)=X]=\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{i,x_{i}},

for all
X\in{}D
, with respect to the randomized-rounding function
\mathcal{R}
.
We use
X\sim\mathcal{S}_{P}
to denote that
X
is sampled from the probability space
\mathcal{S}_{P}

This probability space enables us to define a continuous extension of any discrete function by taking its expectation over a randomized assignment.

Theorem 2

(Expectation)
.

Given a Walsh-Fourier expansion
\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{X\sim\mathcal{S}_{P}}[\mathcal{F}(X)]=\sum_{c\in{}C}\sum_{\alpha}\left(\hat{f}_{c}(\alpha)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{i,\alpha_{i}}\right),

(1)

where the expectation is a multilinear polynomial.

Hence, the satisfiability problem of a CSP can be reduced to a continuous optimization problem by Theorem
3

Theorem 3

(Reduction)
.

Given a constraint satisfaction problem of formula
F

c\in{}C
.

\max_{P\in\tilde{D}}\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{X\sim\mathcal{S}_{P}}[\mathcal{F}(X)]\right)=|C|.

(2)

Circuit-Output Probability

Theorem
3

The performance of gradient-based optimization crucially depends on the efficiency of evaluating and differentiating the objective function.

Implementation Techniques

The primary computational workload of FourierCSP consists of repeatedly executing Algorithm
2
and Algorithm
3
for each constraint during optimization.
Since the COP of individual constraints is independent of one another, these computations can be parallelized.
Specifically, both algorithms can be compiled into kernel functions and executed concurrently, thereby accelerating the projected gradient step via hardware-level parallelism.
Additionally, we leverage autodiff to compute gradients.
As a result, it suffices to construct only the top-down evaluation trace.
The top-down messages at the terminal nodes of the decision diagram are complementary,
i.e.
,
m_{td}[\texttt{true}]+m_{td}[\texttt{false}]=1
.
This allows us to compute the top-down message for only one terminal node while skipping all nodes that do not contribute to its computation.
When the COP expression is sufficiently compact, it can be evaluated in closed form without traversing the decision diagram, thereby further reducing computational overhead.

Experimental Results

In this section, we compare our tool, FourierCSP, with systematic and local search solvers.
The objective of our experimental evaluation is to answer three research questions:

RQ1.

Benchmarks and Encodings

To fully exploit the potential of Fourier expansion-based CLS, benchmark instances should ideally be symmetric,
i.e.
, their constraints should exhibit similar structural patterns.
This symmetry enables efficient GPU acceleration by allowing the solver to fully utilize parallelism, achieving up to 100+ times speedup in gradient computation compared to CPU
(

)
.
The benchmarks include constraint satisfaction and optimization problems.
The first benchmark is

For each combination of

The second benchmark,

Specifically, we use graph coloring as the underlying problem and add parity (modulo 2) constraints to the formula to serve as hash functions.

and

Additionally,

Benchmark instances are linearized for ILP solvers and Booleanized for Boolean SAT solvers.
Further details on the benchmarks and the encodings used for different solvers can be found in the appendix, where we show that the Booleanization will naturally lead to pseudo-Boolean and XOR constraints.

Although modern SAT solvers are highly optimized and capable of solving large benchmarks, such encodings are extremely memory-inefficient; for instance, the corresponding CNF text file is around 16 GB in size.

Specifically, FourierCSP and ParaILP represent two parallel local search solvers based on continuous and discrete optimization methods, respectively.
Notably, under a timeout limit of 1000 seconds, FourierCSP consistently solves all 150 instances, substantially surpassing ParaILP, which solves only 60.
This significant performance gap can be attributed to the differences in the search methods.
Discrete local search methods require that each assignment strictly increase the number of satisfied constraints, often leading to plateaus, particularly for constraints that are long or structurally complex, where small perturbations to discrete variable assignments may not immediately yield progress.
In contrast, CLS methods can exploit fine-grained improvements: the optimizer advances as long as the continuous objective function increases, even marginally.

Among the complete solvers, CP-SAT, Gurobi-ILP, and LinPB solve 89, 60, and 30 instances, respectively.
Although CP-SAT supports channeling constraints, it requires auxiliary variables for modeling conditional logic (

A key advantage of FourierCSP is, that it evaluates the circuit-output probability directly, without introducing auxiliary variables, thus maintaining a lower-dimensional search space and preserving problem structure.
As a result, FourierCSP achieves a 13.88

Answer to RQ3.

We evaluate this benchmark using two metrics: the relative score and the number of instances for which a solver attains the best objective value (#Win).
The relative score is defined as follows.

\text{score}(s,i)=\frac{1+c_{i}^{s}}{1+c_{i}^{*}}

where
c_{i}^{s}
denotes the result obtained for instance
i
by algorithm
s
, and
c_{i}^{*}
denotes the best-known result.

Notably, this benchmark includes a substantial number of modulo constraints, which are known to be challenging for CP-SAT
(
Krupke et al. 2024
)
.
While modulo constraints remain beyond the expressive or algorithmic reach of most conventional solvers,
FourierCSP
can natively accommodate them through its uniform Fourier expansion framework.
As in Figure
2
, FourierCSP outperforms all competing methods on this benchmark, achieving a relative score of 0.98 and attaining the best solution for 84 out of 160 instances
In contrast to its competitive performance on the previous benchmark, CP-SAT’s relative score is 0.18 lower than that of Gurobi-ILP for these instances.
This relative underperformance can likely be attributed to CP-SAT’s inefficiency in processing modulo constraints.

Under binary logarithm encoding, a modulo 2 constraint can be easily Booleanized to an XOR constraint.
Although LinPB can natively solve XOR constraints using Gaussian elimination, it achieves a relative score of 0.16 on this benchmark.
Nevertheless, LinPB contributes more to the virtual best solver than CP-SAT, as it attains the best solution for 17 instances.
Notably, formulas with parity constraints are known to be challenging for DLS solvers
(
Crawford, Kearns, and Schapire 1994
)
.
It is unsurprising that ParaILP fails to provide valid solutions for any instance in this benchmark.

Previous work demonstrated that FourierSAT performs competitively on graph problems when the encoded formulas comprise exclusively symmetric Boolean constraints
(
Cen, Zhang, and Fong 2025
)
.
In contrast, the Booleanized formulas used in this benchmark consist primarily of pseudo-Boolean constraints, which would require further symmetrization into disjunctive clauses to fit the FourierSAT framework.
This additional encoding step increases the complexity, making the problem significantly more challenging for FourierSAT, which does not provide valid solutions for any instance in this benchmark.
While FourierSAT encounters difficulties when addressing formulas containing a large number of clauses, FourierMaxSAT adopts a hybrid approach.
It leverages an SAT solver to solve part of the formula,
i.e.
, the clauses, and subsequently uses CLS to improve the solution,
i.e.
, by attempting to satisfy more XOR constraints.
Eventually, FourierMaxSAT achieved a relative score of 0.94.

Moreover, we observe that the instances for which FourierCSP attains the best solutions are typically those with larger problem sizes. This observation suggests a qualitative distinction in scalability: while systematic search solvers tend to be effective for smaller instances, FourierCSP exhibits improved scalability and maintains solution quality as instance size increases.

Conclusion and Future Directions

In this paper, we introduced FourierCSP, a novel continuous optimization framework for constraint satisfaction problems.
By extending the Walsh-Fourier expansion to finite-domain variables, our approach can handle versatile constraints without the need for memory-intensive encodings or auxiliary variables.
The equivalence between circuit-output probability and Walsh-Fourier expansion allows us to evaluate and differentiate the objective function efficiently, enabling gradient-based optimization.
We adopted a projected gradient approach for our optimization framework, which is supported by theoretical guarantees on convergence.
Empirical evaluations on benchmark suites demonstrated that FourierCSP is scalable and performs competitively.
The experimental results indicate that FourierCSP is a substantial extension of FourierSAT, broadening the class of problems that can be efficiently solved using CLS techniques.

The performance of Fourier transform-based CLS relies heavily on GPU parallelism.
An important direction for future work is to investigate how this parallelism can be effectively leveraged when constraints exhibit non-structural patterns.
This would further generalize FourierCSP, enabling its application to a broader range of CSP problems.

{P}_{t+1}={P}_{t}+\eta g({P}_{t})
.
Due to Lemma
3
we have
\mathcal{C}({P}_{(t+1)})-\mathcal{C}({P}_{t})\geq\frac{\eta}{2}\|g({P}_{t})\|^{2}
.
Given any initial point
a_{(0)}
, it will converge to
{P}^{*}
.
After
T
gradient step, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}({P}^{*})-\mathcal{C}({P}_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2L}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\|g(a_{(t)})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{T+1}{2L}\min_{t}\|g(a_{(t)})\|^{2},

which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\min_{t}\|g(a_{(t)})\|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2L}{T+1}}\sqrt{\mathcal{C}({P}^{*})-\mathcal{C}({P}_{0})}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2L}{T+1}}\sqrt{|C|}

To achieve a point
{P}_{T}
such that
\|g({P}_{t})\|\leq\epsilon
, we require
\sqrt{\frac{2|C|L}{T+1}}\leq\epsilon
, which implies
T\sim\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{|C|L}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)
.

Proof of Lemma
4
and Theorem
6

Since
\mathcal{C}
is a multilinear function, it can be decomposed as

\mathcal{C}(A)=\sum_{j\in\mathrm{dom}(x_{1})}a_{1,j}{}g_{j}(A_{\backslash{}1})+h(A_{\backslash{}1}),

where
g_{j}
’s and
h
are multilinear polynomials not depending on
j
.
Let

\displaystyle\eta q^{+}_{i}=\begin{cases}{{proj}_{\tilde{D}_{i}}(a_{1}+\eta\nabla\mathcal{C}(a_{1}))-a_{1}},&i=1,\\
\vec{0},&otherwise,\end{cases}

and
\epsilon
be an arbitrary positive number.
For all
\eta
such that
\eta\in(0,\epsilon)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(A+\eta{}q^{+})-\mathcal{C}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathrm{dom}(x_{1})}\eta q^{+}_{1,j}\cdot g_{j}(A_{\backslash{}1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathrm{dom}(x_{1})}\left({proj}_{\tilde{D}}(a_{1}+\eta\nabla\mathcal{C}(a_{1}))_{j}-a_{1,j}\right)\cdot g_{j}(A_{\backslash{}1}).

If
{proj}_{\Delta_{1}}(a_{1}+\eta{}q_{1}^{+})
is on
\Delta\backslash{\delta\Delta}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\eta}\left(\mathcal{C}(A+\eta{}q^{+})-\mathcal{C}(A)\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{j\in\mathrm{dom}(x_{1})}\left(\eta g_{j}(A_{\backslash{}1})-\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathrm{dom}(x_{1})}\left(a_{1,j}+\eta g_{j}(a_{\backslash{}1})\right)-1}{|\mathrm{dom}(x_{1})|}\right)g_{j}(A_{\backslash{}1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathrm{dom}(x_{1})}\left(g_{j}(A_{\backslash{}1})-\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathrm{dom}(x_{1})}g_{j}(A_{\backslash{}1})}{|\mathrm{dom}(x_{1})|}\right)\cdot g_{j}(A_{\backslash{}1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathrm{dom}(x_{1})}g_{j}(A_{\backslash{}1})^{2}-\frac{1}{|\mathrm{dom}(x_{1})|}\left(\sum_{j\in\mathrm{dom}(x_{1})}g_{j}(A_{\backslash{}1})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j\in\mathrm{dom}(x_{1})}g_{j}(A_{\backslash{}1})^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{j\in\mathrm{dom}(x_{1})}\frac{1}{|\mathrm{dom}(x_{1})|}\right)-\left(\sum_{j\in\mathrm{dom}(x_{1})}\frac{g_{j}(A_{\backslash{}1})}{\sqrt{|\mathrm{dom}(x_{1})|}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 0

The last inequality is the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, where the equality holds when
\mathcal{C}(a)
is a constant.

If
{proj}_{\tilde{D}}(a\pm\eta{}q^{\pm})
is on
{\delta\tilde{D}}
, we can always find an
\epsilon^{\prime}\in(0,\epsilon)
such that for all
\eta^{\prime}\in(0,\epsilon^{\prime})
,
{proj}_{\tilde{D}}(a\pm\eta^{\prime}q^{\pm})
is on
{\tilde{D}\backslash\delta\tilde{D}}
.

Lemma
4
states that, any interior point
a\in\tilde{D}\backslash{}\delta\tilde{D}
cannot be a local maximum of
\mathcal{C}(a)
.
In contrast, all local maxima must lie on the boundary
\delta\tilde{D}
.

Appendix B
Benchmarks and Encodings

Task Scheduling Problem

Task scheduling problems can be useful in compilers for multi-core CPUs.
Given a task graph
G(V,E)
, where
V\ni{}v
is a set tasks,
E\ni(u\to{}v)
is a set of task dependency.
Given
T
clock cycles and
S
workers, we define variables
t_{v}\in\{0,\cdots,T-1\}
and
s_{v}\in\{0,\cdots,S-1\}
to denote the task
v
is execute at
t_{v}
by worker
s_{v}
.
Then, the scheduling problem can be formulated as

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=F_{1}\wedge F_{2},

\displaystyle F_{1}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{(u\to{}v)\in{}E}(t_{u}<t_{v}),

\displaystyle F_{2}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{(u\to{}v)\notin{}E}\left((t_{u}\neq{}t_{v})\vee(s_{u}\neq{}s_{v})\right).

For each
T\in\{32,64,128,256,512\}
,
S\in\{4,8,16\}
, we choose
|V|={T\cdot S}/{2}
to generate 10 directed graph
G(V,E)
.
Every variable
v\in{}V
has a variable index
idx(v)
.
Between any two variable
u,v\in{}V
and
idx(v)>idx(u)
, we add an edge
u\to{}v
with probability
5^{idx(u)-idx(v)}
.

Circuit-Output Probability

For any
(u\to{}v)\in{}E
, the corresponding Fourier expansion,
i.e.
, circuit-output probability of the constraint
c\in{}F_{1}
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[t_{u}<t_{v}]

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}[t_{u}\geq{}t_{v}]

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{T-1}\mathbb{P}[t_{u}\geq{}i]\cdot\mathbb{P}[t_{v}=i],

where
\mathbb{P}[t_{u}\geq{}i]
can be efficiently computed by cumulative sum.

For any
(u\to{}v)\notin{}E
, the corresponding Fourier expansion,
i.e.
, circuit-output probability of the constraint
c\in{}F_{2}
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[(t_{u}\neq{}t_{v})\vee(s_{u}\neq{}s_{v})]=

\displaystyle 1-\mathbb{P}[(t_{u}=t_{v})\wedge(s_{u}=s_{v})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\mathbb{P}[(t_{u}=t_{v})]\cdot\mathbb{P}[(s_{u}=s_{v})].

Linearization

While encoding
F_{1}
into ILP is straightforward, encoding
F_{2}
requires auxiliary variables
q_{u,v,i}\in\{0,1\}
.

\displaystyle F^{int}

\displaystyle=F_{1}^{int}\wedge F_{2}^{int}\wedge F_{3}^{int}\wedge F_{4}^{int}\wedge F_{4}^{int}\wedge F_{6}^{int},

\displaystyle F_{1}^{int}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{(u\to{}v)\in{}E}(t_{v}-t_{u}\geq{}1),

\displaystyle F_{2}^{int}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{(u\to{}v)\notin{}E}(t_{u}-t_{v}+Tq_{u,v,1}\geq 1),

\displaystyle F_{3}^{int}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{(u\to{}v)\notin{}E}(t_{v}-t_{u}+Tq_{u,v,2}\geq 1),

\displaystyle F_{4}^{int}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{(u\to{}v)\notin{}E}(s_{u}-s_{v}+Sq_{u,v,3}\geq 1),

\displaystyle F_{5}^{int}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{(u\to{}v)\notin{}E}(s_{v}-s_{u}+Sq_{u,v,4}\geq 1),

\displaystyle F_{6}^{int}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{(u\to{}v)\notin{}E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{4}q_{u,v,i}\leq 3\right).

For
F_{2}^{int}
to
F_{6}^{int}
, the interpretation is as follows: given
(u\to{}v)\notin{}E
, when
(t_{u}=t_{v})\wedge(s_{u}=s_{v})
, all
q_{u,v,i}
should be 1 to satisfy the constraints in
F_{2}^{int}
to
F_{5}^{int}
.
However, the constraint in
F_{6}^{int}
is violated.
Only when
(t_{u}\neq{}t_{v})\vee(s_{u}\neq{}s_{v})
, at least one of the
q_{u,v,i}
can be 0, then the constraint in
F_{6}^{int}
can be satisfied.

Booleanization

To further Booleanize this problem into Boolean constraints, we use a log-2 encoding.

\displaystyle t_{v}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\log_{2}(T)-1}2^{i}t_{v,i},

(11)

\displaystyle s_{v}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\log_{2}(S)-1}2^{i}s_{v,i},

(12)

where
t_{v,i}\in\{0,1\}
and
s_{v,i}\in\{0,1\}
.
Similarly, substituting
F_{1}^{int}
with Eq. (
11
) directly leads to
F_{1}^{bool}
.
And for encoding
F_{2}
, we introduce
q_{u,v,i}^{(t)}\in\{0,1\}
and
q_{u,v,i}^{(s)}\in\{0,1\}
.

\displaystyle F^{bool}

\displaystyle=F_{1}^{bool}\wedge F_{2}^{bool}\wedge F_{3}^{bool},

\displaystyle F_{1}^{bool}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{(u\to{}v)\in{}E}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\log_{2}(T)-1}2^{i}t_{v,i}-\sum_{i=0}^{\log_{2}(T)-1}2^{i}t_{u,i}\geq{}1\right),

\displaystyle F_{2}^{bool}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{(u\to{}v)\notin{}E}\bigwedge_{i=0}^{\log_{2}(T)-1}(t_{u,i}\oplus t_{v,i}\oplus\neg{}q_{u,v,i}^{(t)}),

\displaystyle F_{3}^{bool}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{(u\to{}v)\notin{}E}\bigwedge_{i=0}^{\log_{2}(S)-1}(s_{u,i}\oplus s_{v,i}\oplus\neg{}q_{u,v,i}^{(s)}),

\displaystyle F_{4}^{bool}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{(u\to{}v)\notin{}E}\left(\bigvee_{i=0}^{\log_{2}(T)-1}q_{u,v,i}^{(t)}\vee\bigvee_{i=0}^{\log_{2}(S)-1}q_{u,v,i}^{(s)}\right).

F_{2}^{bool}
and
F_{3}^{bool}
(resp.
F_{4}^{bool}
) should be interpreted similarly to
F_{2}^{int}
to
F_{5}^{int}
(resp.
F_{6}^{int}
).
Any
t_{u,i}\neq t_{v,i}
will lead to
q_{u,v,i}^{(t)}=1
(or
s
equivalently), the constraint in
F_{4}^{bool}
will be satisfied.

Graph Coloring Problem with Hashing Queries

Given a graph
G(V,E)
, where
V\ni{}v
is a vertex set,
E\ni(u,v)
is an edge set.
Given
C
colors, we define variables
x_{v}\in\{0,\cdots,C-1\}
to denote the vertex
v
is colored by
x_{v}
.

Then, the graph coloring problem can be formulated as a constraint set

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{(u,v)\in{}E}(x_{u}\neq{}x_{v}).

In this problem, we additionally use a hash function to generate soft modulo constraints.
The hash code
h\in{}H
randomly samples half of the variables from the vertex set.
The hash function is defined by
\sum_{i\in h}x_{i}\mod 2
.
Then, the soft constraint set is:

\displaystyle C_{2}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{h\in{}H}\left(\sum_{i\in h}x_{i}\mod 2=1\right).

The constraint optimization problem is

\displaystyle\min_{a}|H|\cdot\text{cost}(C_{1},a)+\text{cost}(C_{2},a),

where
\text{cost}(C,a)
is the number of unsatisfied constraints in
C
when given an assignment
a
.

For each
|V|\in\{512,1024,2048,4096\}
,
C\in\{8,16,32,64\}
, we generate 10 random regular graphs with degree
2C
.
We add
|V|
modulo constraints as soft constraints.

Circuit-Output Probability

For any
(u,v)\in{}E
, the corresponding Fourier expansion,
i.e.
, circuit-output probability of the constraint
c\in{}C_{1}
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[x_{u}\neq{}x_{v}]

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}[x_{u}=x_{v}]

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}[x_{u}=i]\cdot\mathbb{P}[x_{v}=i].

For any
h\in{}H
, as inspired by the Fourier expansion of XOR constraints
(
Kyrillidis et al. 2020
)
, the circuit-output probability of the constraint
c\in{}C_{2}
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{i\in{}h}x_{i}\mod{2}=1\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[x_{j}\mod{2}=0]\cdot\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{i\in{}h\backslash{j}}x_{i}\mod{2}=1\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[x_{j}\mod{2}=1]\cdot\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{i\in{}h\backslash{j}}x_{i}\mod{2}=0\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\left(1-2\mathbb{P}[x_{j}\mod{2}=1]\right)\cdot\left(1-2\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{i\in{}h\backslash{j}}x_{i}\mod{2}=1\right]\right)}{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\prod_{i\in{}h}\left(1-2\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{C}{2}-1}\mathbb{P}[x_{i}=2j+1]\right)}{2},

which can be handled by slicing.

After the above relaxations, the main computation of
FourierCSP
can be amenable for parallelization using Jax
(
Bradbury et al. 2018
)
.

Linearization

When encoding
C_{1}
for ILP, we introduce two auxiliary variables
q_{u,v},q_{v,u}\in\{0,1\}
for every
(u,v)\in{}E
.

\displaystyle C_{1}^{int}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{(u,v)\in{}E}\left(x_{u}-x_{v}+Cq_{u,v}\geq{}1\right),

\displaystyle C_{2}^{int}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{(u,v)\in{}E}\left(x_{v}-x_{u}+Cq_{v,u}\geq{}1\right),

\displaystyle C_{3}^{int}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{(u,v)\in{}E}\left(q_{u,v}+q_{v,u}\leq{}1\right).

When encoding
C_{2}
for ILP, we introduce two auxiliary variables
q_{h}\in\left\{0,...,\frac{|h|(C-1)}{2}\right\}
, and
p_{h}\in\{0,1\}
for every
h\in{}H
.

\displaystyle C_{4}^{int}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{h\in{}H}\left(\sum_{i\in h}x_{i}-2q_{h}+p_{h}=0\right),

where
p_{h}
should be 1 (resp. 0) when
\sum_{i\in h}x_{i}
is odd (resp. even).
Then ILP should be formulated as:

\displaystyle\min|H|-\sum_{h\in{}H}p_{h}

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }C_{1}^{int}\cup C_{2}^{int}\cup C_{3}^{int}\cup C_{4}^{int}

Booleanization

The Booleanization for PB-XOR is similar to the previous section.

\displaystyle x_{v}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\log_{2}(C)-1}2^{i}x_{v,i},

(13)

where
x_{v,i}\in\{0,1\}
.

For every
(u,v)\in{}E
, and
i\in\{0,...,\log_{2}(C)-1\}
, we introduce an auxiliary variable
q_{u,v,i}\in\{0,1\}
.
Consequently,
C_{1}
can be encoded by

\displaystyle C_{1}^{bool}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{(u,v)\in{}E}(x_{u,i}\oplus{}x_{v,i}\oplus{}\neg{}q_{u,v,i}),

\displaystyle C_{2}^{bool}

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{(u,v)\in{}E}\left(\bigvee_{i=0}^{\log_{2}(C)-1}q_{u,v,i}\right).

Note that the parity of
x_{u}
only depends on
x_{u,0}
.
Hence, the soft constraint set
C_{2}
can be encoded by

C_{3}^{bool}=\bigwedge_{h\in{}H}\left(\bigoplus_{i\in{}h}x_{i,0}\right)

Eventually, the constrained optimization problem becomes

\min_{a}|H|\cdot\left(\text{cost}(C_{1}^{bool},a)+\text{cost}(C_{2}^{bool},a)\right)+\text{cost}(C_{3}^{bool},a).
