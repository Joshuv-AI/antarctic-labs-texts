Christian Reiher, Vojtěch Rödl, Mathias Schacht

Abstract
. We study subgraphs that appear in large Ramsey graphs for a given graph F F . The recent girth Ramsey theorem of the first two authors asserts that there are Ramsey graphs such that all small subgraphs are ‘forests of copies of F F ’ amalgamated on vertices and edges. We derive a few further consequences from this structural result and investigate to which extent such forests of copies must be present in Ramsey graphs.

1.
Introduction

Some of the most fundamental and general types of questions in combinatorics
aim for understanding the interplay of
local
and
global
properties in discrete structures.
As an insightful example for our discussion serve those local properties that are captured by the appearance of
subgraphs of fixed isomorphism type in large graphs with high chromatic number. We consider having a high chromatic number to be
a global property of graphs, since in general it cannot be bounded by studying subgraphs of given size.
In fact, this is a consequence of the work of Erdős
[
4
]
, which asserts the existence of
graphs with arbitrarily high chromatic number all of whose ‘small’ subgraphs are forests.
This result is qualitatively complemented by the simple graph theoretic fact, that any graph of sufficiently high chromatic number
must contain all forests of fixed size.

Natural generalisations of these results, in the context of Ramsey theory for
vertex colourings
,
were obtained by Nešetřil and Rödl
[
7
]
and by Diskin et al.
[
3
]
. Here we investigate to which extent
these results generalise to
edge colourings
. A central tool for our investigation is the
recent girth Ramsey theorem of the first two authors and we start with its formulation.

1.1.
Girth Ramsey theorem

Given two graphs
F
and
G
and a number of colours
r\geq 2
we write
G\longrightarrow(F)_{r}
for the statement that every
r
-colouring
of the edge set of
G
yields a monochromatic copy of
F
which is induced in
G
. In the situation, when we do not require the monochromatic copy to be necessarily induced, we write
G\xrightarrow{\text{\,n.n.i.\,}}(F)_{r}
. We may also say
G
is a
Ramsey graph
for
F
and
r
colours in these situations.
If a graph
G
fails to be such a Ramsey graph, then we write
G\mathrel{\mkern 5.5mu\arrownot\mkern-5.5mu}\longrightarrow(F)_{r}
and
G\mathrel{\mkern 10.5mu\arrownot\mkern-10.5mu}\xrightarrow{\text{\,n.n.i.\,}}(F)_{r}
, respectively.
It is easy to see that Ramsey graphs for a given graph
F
must contain copies of
F
sharing vertices and edges.
Roughly speaking, the girth Ramsey theorem (see Theorem
1.2
below) asserts, that there are Ramsey graphs with the property that locally the copies
of
F
have a forest-like structure. In order to make this precise we define forests in this context.

Definition 1.1

(Forests of copies)
.

A set of graphs
\mathscr{F}
is called a
forest of copies
if there
exists an enumeration
\mathscr{F}=\{F_{1},\dots,F_{|\mathscr{F}|}\}
such that
for every
j\in[2,|\mathscr{F}|]
the set

V(F_{j})\cap\bigcup_{i<j}V(F_{i})

is either empty or a single vertex, or an edge belonging both to
E(F_{j})

and to
\bigcup_{i<j}E(F_{i})
.

We remark that for a forest of copies
\mathscr{F}
of a graph
F
the graph
\mathfrak{F}=\bigcup\mathscr{F}
may contain additional
copies of
F
, which are not present in
\mathscr{F}
itself. It is also important to note that contrary to forests in graphs,
subsets of forests of copies might not be such forests themselves, as the example in Figure
1.1
illustrates.
On the left hand side we see a ‘cycle’ of triangles, i.e., a collection of
five triangles not forming a forest of copies. By adding two further edges, however,
we can hide this configuration inside a forest of eight triangles (see Figure
1.1(b)
).

We denote by

the set of all induced copies
of
F
in
G

F

For a subsystem

and a number of colours

indicates that for every
r

of

one
of the copies in

is monochromatic.
The rôle of
\mathscr{X}
in the following theorem is due to the fact that, for the above
reason,

can only be demanded to be a subset of a forest copies rather than an actual forest of copies.
With this notation at hand we can state the following version of the girth Ramsey theorem of the first two authors
[
9
]
.

Theorem 1.2

(Girth Ramsey theorem)
.

Given a graph
F
and
r,\ell\in\mathds{N}
there exists a graph
G

together with a system of copies
\mathscr{G}\subseteq\binom{G}{F}

satisfying not only
\mathscr{G}\longrightarrow(F)_{r}
, but also the following statement:
For every
\mathscr{F}\subseteq\mathscr{G}
with
|\mathscr{F}|\in[2,\ell]
there exists
a set
\mathscr{X}\subseteq\mathscr{G}
such that
|\mathscr{X}|\leq|\mathscr{F}|-2
and
\mathscr{F}\cup\mathscr{X}

is a forest of copies. ∎

Similarly, as Erdős’ theorem concerns graphs

yielding monochromatic copies of
K_{2}
for vertex colourings,
Theorem
1.2
concerns graphs
G
yielding monochromatic copies of
F
for edge colourings. Both theorems then
assert that there are such graphs

with the additional property of containing locally only forests of the target graph
F
.

1.2.
New results

Our first two results address the question to which extent the converse of Theorem
1.2
holds.
More precisely, for which graphs
F
every forest of copies necessarily occurs in every Ramsey graph for sufficiently many colours.
In the positive direction we show that this is indeed true for cycles and cliques.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
F
be a cycle or a balanced, complete, multipartite graph with at least two edges. For every forest
\mathscr{F}
of copies of
F
,
there exists some
r\geq 2
such that every Ramsey graph
G\xrightarrow{\text{\,n.n.i.\,}}(F)_{r}
contains a subgraph isomorphic
to
\bigcup\mathscr{F}
.

The proof of Theorem
1.3
will be presented in §
2
.
It seems plausible that Theorem
1.3
extends to a larger family of graphs.
However, our next result shows that there are graphs for which such a statement fails.

Theorem 1.4
.

There exist infinitely many graphs
F
such that for every integer
r\geq 2

there exists a Ramsey graph
G\longrightarrow(F)_{r}
with the following properties.

(
)

All copies of
F
in
G
are induced.

(
)

Any two induced copies of
F
in
G
,
sharing an edge
xz
, also share a vertex
y
that is neither
adjacent to
x
nor adjacent to
z
in
G
.

At first sight, property
()
‣
1.4
seems quite orthogonal to anything the girth
Ramsey theorem might be capable of doing for us. Nevertheless, the proof of
Theorem
1.4
presented in §
4
relies on a
version of the girth Ramsey theorem for ordered hypergraphs, which we discuss
in §
3
. It might also be interesting to point out that the
graphs
F
constructed to this end are dense and highly connected, so the phenomenon
described here is not just some pathologic degenerate case.

The two remaining results concern other local structures in Ramsey graphs. The next theorem
establishes the existence of Ramsey graphs for many colours,
which locally fail to have the Ramsey property for two colours. In that sense it parallels the simple consequence of Erdős’ theorem
that there are high chromatic graphs, which are locally bipartite.

Theorem 1.5
.

For every graph
F
containing a cycle and all integers
r\geq 2
and
n\in\mathds{N}
there exists a Ramsey graph
G\longrightarrow(F)_{r}
, such that for every subgraph
H\subseteq G
on
n
vertices we have
H\mathrel{\mkern 10.5mu\arrownot\mkern-10.5mu}\xrightarrow{\text{\,n.n.i.\,}}(F)_{2}
.

We remark that Theorem
1.5
does not hold for all graphs
F
. For example, it obviously fails for matchings and stars.
We deduce Theorem
1.5
from the girth Ramsey theorem and we present the details of that proof in §
5
.

Our last result shows that for bipartite graphs
F

there exist Ramsey graphs containing
F
-free subgraphs with positive density.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
F
be a bipartite graph that cannot be disconnected by deleting either
one vertex or two vertices forming an edge,
and let
\ell(F)
be the number of edges of a longest path in
F
.

For every integer
r\geq 2

there exists a Ramsey graph
G\longrightarrow(F)_{r}
with the property that every subgraph
H\subseteq G
contains
an
F
-free subgraph
H^{\prime}\subseteq H
with
|E(H^{\prime})|\geq\frac{\ell(F)-1}{2\ell(F)}|E(H)|
.

Theorem
1.6
is remotely related to Pisier’s problem and we refer to the survey
[
5
]
,
where the result for
F=C_{4}
was stated.
We remark that, due to limitations of our approach,
the promised subgraph

has less than half of the edges of

and, hence, the corresponding statement trivially holds for graphs with
chromatic number at least three. In view of that we put forward the following question.

Question 1.7
.

Does there exist an
\alpha>1/2
such that for every integer
r\geq 2
there is a Ramsey graph

G\longrightarrow(K_{3})_{r}
with the property that every
H\subseteq G
contains a triangle-free subgraph
H^{\prime}\subseteq H

with
|E(H^{\prime})|\geq\alpha|E(H)|
.

The proof of Theorem
1.6
presented in §
6
also relies on the girth Ramsey theorem for ordered graphs
stated in §
3
.

1.3.
Ramsey infinite graphs

It turns out that the proof of Theorem
1.5
also shows, that graphs containing a cycle have infinitely many minimal
Ramsey graphs. We recall that a graph
F
is
Ramsey infinite
,
if there are infinitely many minimal graphs
G
satisfying the partition
relation
G\xrightarrow{\text{\,n.n.i.\,}}(F)_{2}
and otherwise we say
F
is
Ramsey finite
.

Burr, Erdős, and Lovász
[
1
]
expressed the belief that with a few exceptions most graphs are Ramsey infinite
and this was confirmed through the work of Murty (see, e.g.,
[
1
]
), of Burr, Erdős, and Lovász
[
1
]
,
of Nešetřil and Rödl
[
8
]
, of Faudree
[
6
]
, and of Rödl and Ruciński (see, e.g., Łuczak
[
12
]
).
As it turned out the only Ramsey finite graphs are certain types of star forests, among which are
matchings of any size and stars with an odd number of edges (see the work of Faudree
[
6
]
for a full classification).
The complementing case, that all graphs containing a cycle are Ramsey infinite, is a
consequence of the work of Rödl and Ruciński
[
10
,
11
]
on thresholds for Ramsey properties in random graphs.

Theorem 1.8
.

Every graph containing a cycle is Ramsey infinite. ∎

In §
5
we see that this follows easily from Theorem
1.5
.

2.
Forcing forests of cliques and cycles

In this section we derive Theorem
1.3
. The proof for cycles and the proof for
balanced, complete, multipartite graphs are different and we start the discussion with the latter.

2.1.
Forests of copies of balanced, complete, multipartite graphs

The proof relies on a theorem of de Bruijn and Erdős on set mappings.
A
set mapping
over a set
X
is defined to be a map
f\colon X\longrightarrow\powerset(X)

with
x\not\in f(x)
for every
x\in X
. Subsets
Y\subseteq X
such
that
y\not\in f(y^{\prime})
holds for all
y,y^{\prime}\in Y
are called
free
.
Let us quote
[
2
]
*Theorem 3.

Theorem 2.1

(de Bruijn and Erdős)
.

Let

be a set mapping over a (finite or infinite) set

, and let
k
be a
positive integer. If
|f(x)|\leq k
for every
x\in X
, then
X
can be partitioned
into
2k+1
free sets. ∎

We call a graph

strongly edge transitive
if for all ordered pairs

(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in V(F)^{2}
with
xy,x^{\prime}y^{\prime}\in E(F)
there exists an automorphism
of
F
sending
x
to
x^{\prime}
and
y
to
y^{\prime}
. For instance, all balanced, complete
multipartite graphs, Kneser graphs, and cycles are strongly edge-transitive. Given
a strongly edge-transitive graph

with at least one edge and a positive
integer

we denote the graph obtained by gluing

copies of
F
along an edge
by
(m,F)
. For clarity we point out that this graph has
m\cdot(v(F)-2)+2

and
m\cdot(e(F)-1)+1
edges. The edge where the gluing occurs is called the
kernel

of
(m,F)
. In the special case
{m=1}
every edge can be viewed as a kernel
of

.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let

If for some positive integer

Proof.

We define a set mapping

over

as follows.
Given

if

Since

.
For every vertex

We note

Let

It suffices to show that no n.n.i. copy of

Assume for the sake of contradiction that

∎

We handle this case by induction on

Forests of copies of cycles

The proof of Theorem
1.3
for cycles
follows the one for balanced, complete, multipartite graphs with the key lemma, Lemma

,
replaced by the following statement.

Lemma 2.3
.

For all integers
\ell\geq 3
,

Now suppose that for some positive

inner vertex in

. Consequently, there is a path

such that

is a cycle in

Clearly this cycle contradicts the minimal choice of

∎

It remains to observe that the case

in particular, that no

∎

Imitating the proof of Theorem

for cycles and we omit the details.

3.
Ordered hypergraphs

We shall need the following version of Theorem
1.2
for ordered, linear hypergraphs.
Recall that a hypergraph is

if any pair of distinct edges shares at most one vertex.
An
ordered hypergraph

is a hypergraph
(V,E)
equipped with a total
order
<
of its vertex set; all subgraphs of
H
inherit the ordering induced by

For two ordered hypergraphs

and

we require for copies of
F
in

to display the same ordering as
F
itself.
Moreover, in this context

\binom{H}{F}_{\!<}
stands for the set of all ordered, induced copies
of

in

. We shall also consider unordered, induced copies of

in the unordered hypergraph

and for the set
\binom{(V(H),E(H))}{(V(F),E(F))}
of those copies we shall simply write
\binom{H}{F}
.
In the context of linear hypergraphs, forests of copies can be defined in the same
way as for graphs, i.e., one just replaces the word ‘graphs’ in the first
line of Definition
1.1
by ‘linear hypergraphs’.
The variant of the girth Ramsey theorem for ordered linear hypergraphs can be stated as follows.

Theorem 3.1

(Girth Ramsey theorem for ordered hypergraphs)
.

Given an ordered linear
k
-uniform hypergraph
F
and
r,n\in\mathds{N}
there exists an ordered
linear
k
-uniform hypergraph
H
together with a system of ordered copies
\mathscr{H}\subseteq\binom{H}{F}_{\!<}

satisfying not only
\mathscr{H}\longrightarrow(F)_{r}
, but also the following statement:
For every set of ordered copies
\mathscr{N}\subseteq\mathscr{H}
with
|\mathscr{N}|\in[2,n]
there exists
a set
\mathscr{X}\subseteq\mathscr{H}
such that
|\mathscr{X}|\leq|\mathscr{N}|-2
and
\mathscr{N}\cup\mathscr{X}

is a forest of copies. ∎

We remark that the system
\mathscr{H}
in Theorem
3.1
is necessary for linear hypergraphs

that are not sufficiently connected,
since in this case the hypergraph

may contain some ‘unwanted’ copies spread over
several copies from
\mathscr{H}
. Roughly speaking, this situation can be excluded
if

cannot be disconnected by removing the vertex set of an edge or fewer vertices.
Let us recall here that a hypergraph
F
is said to be

if for
every partition
V(F)=X\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\displaystyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\textstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptscriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}}Y

such that

. The stronger notion we require is
rendered by the following definition.

Definition 3.2
.

We say a linear
k
-uniform hypergraph

is
(e,v)
-inseparable

(
)

is connected and contains at least two edges,

(

(

and for every edge

This property is precisely what we need for proving the following result.

Lemma 3.3

Let

Proof.

A straightforward argument by induction on

for two linear

. Clearly if

and

.
Similarly if

and we are done again.

Thus it suffices to derive a contradiction from the assumption

It remains to consider the case

∎

4.

In this section we establish Theorem

4.1.
Graphs derived from ordered linear hypergraphs

The graphs

For that, for an ordered hypergraph

we define the (unordered) graph

on the same vertex set

(4.1)

In other words, the edge set of the graph

For the proof of Theorem

we require that

-inseparable and

is

Proposition 4.1
.

There are infinitely many ordered,
(e,v)
-inseparable, linear

-uniform hypergraphs

such that the

is

-connected.

Proof.

For any sufficiently large integer

divisible by

, consider the ordered, linear

-uniform hypergraph

defined on

with its natural ordering and

Clearly,

is linear. Since for edges of

the vertices

,

, and

must be distinct, one may observe that
every fixed vertex

forms an edge in

with all other vertices except one of

or

and
with vertices of the form

or

and

there are at least

Since any vertex can play each of the rôles

can destroy at most three such

cannot be separated in

We prepare the proof of the

by analysing its minimum degree.
This will later exclude the existence of ‘small’ components.

Claim 4.2
.

We have

.

Proof.

We show that for every vertex

there are at least

pairs

such that

and either

or

. In fact,
one can check that depending on the choice of

those pairs

can be
found with at least one of the vertices

and

from the interval

defined by

[3n/4,n]\,,&\text{if}\ x\in[n/4,n/2-1]\\
[1,n/4]\,,&\text{if}\ x\in[n/2,3n/4-1]\\
[n/2+1,3n/4-1]\,,&\text{if}\ x\in[3n/4,n]\,.\end{cases}

This proves the claim.
∎

The proof of the fact that

is sufficiently connected is rendered by the following claim.

Claim 4.3
.

The derived graph

is

-connected.

Proof.

Let

be an arbitrary set of at most

We plan to show that these vertices are in the same component of

Clearly, we have

.
Thus for every positive integer

\{x_{\star},n/8+k-x_{\star},7n/8-k\}\,,\,\,\,\{n/2-k,5n/8+2k,7n/8-k\}\,,\,\,\,\{n/2-k,3n/2+k-x^{\star},x^{\star}\}

of

yield a path

-
(7n/8-k)

in

.
As these paths are internally vertex disjoint, at least one of them is

and

Due to

Altogether, we obtain

∎

This concludes the proof of Proposition

∎

5.
Globality of Ramsey properties and Ramsey infinite graphs

In this section we shall prove Theorem
1.5
and Theorem
1.8
(see §
5.2
).
In those proofs the
2
-density of a graph will play an important rôle and we discuss this parameter first.

5.1.
The 2-density of a graph

For every graph
F
with at least one edge we define its
2
-density

d_{2}(F)=\begin{cases}\frac{|E(F)|-1}{|V(F)|-2}\,,&\text{if}\ |V(F)|\geq 3\,,\\
1\,,&\text{if}\ F=K_{2}\,,\end{cases}

and denote by
m_{2}(F)
the
maximum
2
-density

m_{2}(F)=\max\big\{d_{2}(F^{\prime})\colon F^{\prime}\subseteq F\ \text{and}\ e(F^{\prime})\geq 1\big\}\,.

We say a graph
F
with at least two edges is
strictly
2
-balanced
if
d_{2}(F)>d_{2}(F^{\prime})

for every proper subgraph
F^{\prime}\subsetneq F
with at least one edge. In particular, for any strictly
2
-balanced graph
F
we have

d_{2}(F)>1
and, consequently,
|E(F)|\geq|V(F)|
.

The
2
-density is a well-known parameter in random graph theory, as
p=n^{-1/d_{2}(F)}

marks the edge probability in
G(n,p)
where the expected number of copies of
F

is as large as the expected number of edges.

We first observe that building forests of copies does not increase the maximum
2
-density.

Proposition 5.1
.

For every nonempty forest of copies
\mathscr{F}
of a graph
F
with at least one edge we have

m_{2}(\mathfrak{F})=m_{2}(F)
for the graph
\mathfrak{F}=\bigcup\mathscr{F}
.

Proof.

The statement is obvious if
\mathscr{F}
contains only one copy of
F
and the general case
follows from a simple inductive argument on the number of copies in
\mathscr{F}
.

For some
\ell>1
let
\mathscr{F}=\{F_{1},\dots,F_{\ell}\}
be a forest of copies of
F
and set

q=V(F_{\ell})\cap\bigcup_{i<\ell}V(F_{i})\,.

Recall that the set
q
contains at most two vertices and if
|q|=2
, then it spans an edge from the set
E(F_{\ell})\cap\bigcup_{i<\ell}E(F_{i})
.

We shall show that
d_{2}(J)\leq m_{2}(F)
for every subgraph
J\subseteq\bigcup\mathscr{F}
.
Owing to the induction hypothesis, it suffices to consider such a graph
J
with both subgraphs

J_{1}=J\cap\bigcup_{i<\ell}F_{i}
and
J_{2}=J\cap F_{\ell}
containing at least one edge.
We set
q_{J}=q\cap V(J)
. Note that without loss of generality we may assume that
J
is an induced subgraph and, hence, if
|q_{J}|=|q|=2
, then
the edge spanned by
q_{J}
is also contained in
J
. Let
\zeta\in\{0,1\}
indicate if
q_{J}
spans an edge. We have

1-\zeta\leq(2-|q_{J}|)\cdot m_{2}(F)

because if
\zeta=0
, then
|q_{J}|\leq 1\leq m_{2}(F)
.
For
j\in\{1,2\}
the estimate
d_{2}(J_{j})\leq m_{2}(F)

yields

|E(J_{j})|-1\leq\big(|V(J_{j})|-2\big)\cdot m_{2}(F)\,.

By adding all three inequalities we infer

|E(J_{1})|+|E(J_{2})|-1-\zeta\leq\bigl(|V(J_{1})|+|V(J_{2})|-2-|q_{J}|\bigr)\cdot m_{2}(F)\,,

i.e.,
|E(J)|-1\leq\bigl(|V(J)|-2\bigr)\cdot m_{2}(F)
, whence
d_{2}(J)\leq m_{2}(F)
.
∎

For strictly
2
-balanced graphs similar calculations yield the following relation.

Proposition 5.2
.

For any union
H
of two distinct copies of a strictly
2
-balanced graph
F
, which intersect in at least two edges,
we have
d_{2}(H)>d_{2}(F)
.

Proof.

Consider a graph
H=F_{1}\cup F_{2}
being the union of two copies
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
of
F
.
We denote by
I=\big(V(F_{1})\cap V(F_{2}),E(F_{1})\cap E(F_{2})\big)
the intersection of both copies and our assumption
yields
|E(I)|\geq 2
and
|V(I)|\geq 3
.
Since
F
is strictly
2
-balanced, we have
d_{2}(I)<d_{2}(F)
, whence

|E(I)|-1<\bigl(|V(I)|-2\bigr)\cdot d_{2}(F)\,.

By subtracting this from
2\bigr(|E(F)|-1\bigr)=2\bigl(|V(F)|-2\bigr)\cdot d_{2}(F)

we obtain

\displaystyle|E(H)|-1

\displaystyle=2|E(F)|-|E(I)|-1>\bigr(2|V(F)|-|V(I)|-2\bigr)\cdot d_{2}(F)

\displaystyle=\bigl(|V(H)|-2\bigr)\cdot d_{2}(F)\,,

which implies
d_{2}(H)>d_{2}(F)
.
∎

We complement Proposition
5.1
with the observation that certain
‘cycles of copies’
have larger
2
-density.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
F
be a graph with
d_{2}(F)>1
. Suppose that for some
\ell\geq 3

we have a cyclic sequence
F_{1},\dots,F_{\ell}
of copies of
F
such that
no edge belongs to three of these copies and for all distinct
i,j\in\mathds{Z}/\ell\mathds{Z}

we have

|E(F_{i})\cap E(F_{j})|=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }j=i\pm 1\\
0&\text{ else}\,.\end{cases}

Then the union
\mathfrak{C}=\bigcup_{i\in\mathds{Z}/\ell\mathds{Z}}F_{i}
satisfies
d_{2}(\mathfrak{C})>d_{2}(F)
.

Proof.

Notice that
|V(\mathfrak{C})|\leq\ell(|V(F)|-2)+1

and
|E(\mathfrak{C})|=\ell(|E(F)|-1)
. So by subtracting
d_{2}(F)>1
from

\ell(|V(F)|-2)\cdot d_{2}(F)=\ell(|E(F)|-1)

we obtain

(|V(\mathfrak{C})|-2)\cdot d_{2}(F)<|E(\mathfrak{C})|-1\,,

whence
d_{2}(F)<d_{2}(\mathfrak{C})
.
∎

The following observation appeared in slightly stronger form in the work of Rödl and Ruciński
[
10
]

and it establishes a link between the
2
-density of
F
and the
2
-density of its Ramsey graphs.

Proposition 5.4
.

For every graph
F
containing a cycle and every graph
G
satisfying the Ramsey property
G\xrightarrow{\text{\,n.n.i.\,}}(F)_{2}

we have
m_{2}(G)>m_{2}(F)
.

Proof.

Since any given graph
F
containing a cycle satisfies
m_{2}(F)>1
, there exists a strictly
2
-balanced subgraph
F_{\star}\subseteq F

with
d_{2}(F_{\star})=m_{2}(F)
and we fix such an
F_{\star}
.

Assume for the sake of contradiction that
m_{2}(G)\leq m_{2}(F)=d_{2}(F_{\star})
.
This implies that all copies of
F_{\star}
in
G
are induced.
We consider the auxiliary

|E(F_{\star})|
-uniform hypergraph
A
with vertex set
V(A)=E(G)
whose hyperedges
correspond to the copies of
F_{\star}
in
G
.
In view of Propositions
5.2
and
5.3
,
the hypergraph
A
is linear and contains no cycles.
Consequently,
A
admits a
2
-colouring of its vertices without monochromatic
hyperedges. However, this colouring signifies
G\mathrel{\mkern 10.5mu\arrownot\mkern-10.5mu}\xrightarrow{\text{\,n.n.i.\,}}(F_{\star})_{2}
, which is absurd.
∎

Together with Proposition
5.1
this has the
following
m_{2}
-free consequence.

Corollary 5.5
.

For every graph
F
containing a cycle every forest
\mathscr{F}
of copies
of
F
satisfies
\bigcup\mathscr{F}\mathrel{\mkern 10.5mu\arrownot\mkern-10.5mu}\xrightarrow{\text{\,n.n.i.\,}}(F)_{2}
. ∎

5.2.
Proofs of the theorems

In this section we deduce Theorem
1.5
and Theorem
1.8
. Both
results are consequences of the girth Ramsey theorem
combined with
Corollary
5.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Given a graph
F
that is not a forest and integers
r\geq 2
and
n\geq 1

let the graph
G
and a system of copies
\mathscr{G}
be provided by Theorem
1.2

applied with
F
,
r
, and
\ell=\binom{n}{2}
. Without loss of generality we may assume
G=\bigcup\mathscr{G}

and obviously
G
enjoys the Ramsey property
G\longrightarrow(F)_{r}
.

Let
H\subseteq G
be a subgraph on
n
vertices and for every edge
e
of
H
fix a copy
F_{e}
of
F
from the system

\mathscr{G}

containing
e
. Theorem
1.2
tells us that the set
\{F_{e}\colon e\in E(H)\}
is contained in a forest

\mathscr{F}
of copies of
F
and by definition we have
H\subseteq\bigcup\mathscr{F}
. On the other hand,
Corollary
5.5

implies
\bigcup\mathscr{F}\mathrel{\mkern 10.5mu\arrownot\mkern-10.5mu}\xrightarrow{\text{\,n.n.i.\,}}(F)_{2}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

Suppose there are only finitely many minimal Ramsey graphs for a given graph
F

containing a cycle and that
n\in\mathds{N}
exceeds the number of vertices of each
such graph. By Theorem
1.5
applied to
F
,
r=2
, and
n
there
is a Ramsey graph
G\longrightarrow(F)_{2}
containing none of the minimal Ramsey graphs
for
F
, which is absurd.
∎
