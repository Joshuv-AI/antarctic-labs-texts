Introduction and main results

Let
d\geq 2
be a fixed integer. Throughout the paper, the space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is equipped with an orthonormal basis
e_{1},\cdots,e_{d}
, and its associated coordinate system.

For
n\geq 1
, we say that an
n
-tuple of points
u[n]=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
is in convex position if
\{u_{1},\cdots,u_{n}\}
forms the vertex set of a convex polytope. For any compact convex set
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with non-empty interior and for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we let
\mathbb{U}_{K}
be the uniform distribution on
K
, and let
\mathbb{U}_{K}(n)
be the product measure of
n
iid random variables
U[n]=(U_{1},\cdots,U_{n})
with common distribution
\mathbb{U}_{K}
.

Finally, we let
P^{(d)}_{K}(n)
be the probability that an
n
-tuple of points
U[n]
with distribution
\mathbb{U}_{K}(n)
is in convex position.

The very first problem in the study of
P^{(d)}_{K}(n)
was posed by Sylvester
[
23
]
in 1864 (see Pfiefer
[
20
]
for a history of this problem) and consisted in determining the probability that 4 random points in the plane, are in convex position. This problem evolved over time into computing the convex sets
K
that minimize or maximize the probability
P^{(2)}_{K}(4)
. Blaschke
[
4
]
provided the answer to this question in 1917, by proving that the lower bound is reached when
K=\triangle^{2}
is a triangle, and the upper bound when
K=\bigcirc^{2}
is a disk or an ellipse, namely

\frac{2}{3}=P^{(2)}_{\triangle^{2}}(4)\leq P^{(2)}_{K}(4)\leq P^{(2)}_{\bigcirc^{2}}(4)=1-\frac{35}{12\pi^{2}}.

In larger dimensions, an analogous problem exists. Since
d+1
random points are in convex position with probability 1,
the question is whether we may optimize the quantity
P^{(d)}_{K}(d+2)
?
As a generalization of Blaschke’s proof, Groemer
[
11
]
proved that the upper bound
P^{(d)}_{K}(d+2)\leq P^{(d)}_{\bigcirc^{d}}(d+2)
holds true whereas the following
d
-Sylvester’s question

P^{(d)}_{\triangle^{d}}(d+2)\leq P^{(d)}_{K}(d+2)\leq P^{(d)}_{\bigcirc^{d}}(d+2),

(
\triangle^{d}
and
\bigcirc^{d}
denote respectively a simplex and an ellipsoid of volume 1) remains a conjecture due to the lower bound. To prove the lower bound, Blaschke uses the fact that if
K
is a convex set, and
S(K)
is obtained from
K
by a “shaking” with respect to a given line, then
P^{(2)}_{S(K)}\leq P^{(2)}_{K}
(this eventually allows to conclude, because, for each
K
, there exists a sequence of shakings
(S_{i})
such that
S_{i}(...S_{1}(K))
converges to a triangle). A shaking with respect to an hyperplane is well defined in higher dimension, but the property
P^{(d)}_{S(K)}\leq P^{(d)}_{K}
does not hold in all generality.

The quantity
P^{(d)}_{K}(d+2)
satisfies

\displaystyle P^{(d)}_{K}(d+2)=1-(d+2)\mathbb{E}\left({\sf Vol}_{d}({\sf CH}(\{U_{1},\cdots,U_{d+1}\}))\right),

(1.1)

(where
{\sf CH}(\{U_{1},\cdots,U_{d+1}\})
is the convex hull of
\{U_{1},\cdots,U_{d+1}\}
)
and this formula may be understood as the fact that
U_{1},\cdots,U_{d+2}
are not in convex position if and only if one of the
U_{i}
is in the convex hull of the others.
Hence, the problem of optimizing
K\mapsto P^{(d)}_{K}(d+2)
is equivalent to the problem of optimizing
K\mapsto-\mathbb{E}({\sf Vol}_{d}({\sf CH}(\{U_{1},\cdots,U_{d+1}\})))
.

This problem seems to be out of reach for the moment, and then, we will turn our attention to another problem, which has a common flavor for many reasons, which should become clear gradually.

.

Let

\mathbb{R}^{d}

, and

Hence, the floor of

\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times\{0\}

For the sequel, we will need to distinguish

(see Fig.

\blacksquare

1.1
Related results in 2D

Under the quantity
P^{(d)}_{K}(n)
lie many other questions. Can we compute
P^{(d)}_{K}(n)
for some specific sets
K
? Can we optimize
P^{(d)}_{K}(n)
on
K
for other values of
n
? Is there a limit shape theorem for a
n
-tuple of points
U[n]
that are
\mathbb{U}_{K}(n)
-distributed and conditioned to be in convex position ?

To date, the overwhelming majority of known answers to these problems have been given when
d=2
, but even there the questions are still largely open.

For example, a natural generalization of Sylvester’s problem when
d=2
would be

P^{(2)}_{\triangle}(n)\leq P^{(2)}_{K}(n)\leq P^{(2)}_{\bigcirc}(n),

for any compact convex set
K
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The case
n=5
was proved by Marckert and Rahmani
[
16
]
, but
n\geq 6
is still a conjecture (but one finds in
[
16
]
an explicit formula to compute
P^{(2)}_{K}(n)
).

As for the computation of
P^{(2)}_{K}(n)
, several results are known. If
K
is a parallelogram or a triangle, Valtr
[
24
,
25
]
computed two exact formulas for all
n\geq 3
:

{P}^{(2)}_{\Box}(n)=\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}{2n-2\choose n-1}^{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad{P}^{(2)}_{\triangle}(n)=\frac{2^{n}(3n-3)!}{(2n)!((n-1)!)^{3}}.

In dimension
d=2
, a fixed floor
F

can only be a nonzero segment. Thus,
Q_{K}(n)
is the probability that
n
points in
K
are in convex position, together with both extremities of
F
. In fact,

Figure 3
:
Buchta’s method
[
7
]
to compute the probability that the
n
points
U_{1},\cdots,U_{n}
are in convex position in a parallelogram. Push the sides of the initial parallelogram inside, and stop them when they hit a point. In the end, each of the four sides contains a point (the 4 points are not necessarily different). This forms 2, 3 or 4 triangles, all of which having 2 marked points at their boundaries. The
U_{i}
are in convex position, if and only if each of the other points belong to a triangle, and if all the points inside a triangle together with the two marked points are in convex position.

an important tool for these results relates to the
2D
-floor case. Indeed, a classical method to compute
P_{K}(n)
consists ”in shrinking
K
”, e.g., by pushing the boundaries inwards (as in Buchta
[
6
]

[
15
]

[

18
]

are ”hit”. This defines several regions, which either must not contain any points, or must bound a compact convex set containing a subset of the

that must be in convex position together with the two extremities of a segment (these extremities being some

discovered when pushing the boundaries). Up to an affine map, this latter set is a compact convex set with a floor.
In

18

Some important asymptotic results also involve this type of tool, such as Bárány’s formula
[

\lim_{n\to+\infty}n^{2}\left({P}^{(2)}_{K}(n)\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}=\frac{1}{4}e^{2}{\sf AP}^{*}(K)^{3},

where
{\sf AP}^{*}(K)
is the supremum of the affine perimeters of all convex sets

In the case where

is a polygon, this logarithmic equivalent was refined into a true equivalent by Morin
[
19
,
18
]
.
Both authors used the well-known case when

is a triangle, due to Bárány, Röte, Steiger and Zhang

3

\displaystyle t_{n}:=Q_{\triangle}(n)=\frac{2^{n}}{n!(n+1)!}.

(1.7)

Again, the notation ”
Q
” refers to the floor case when

refers to the classical one.

Other questions then arise. Do we know other values of
Q_{K}(n)
? Can we optimize
Q_{K}(n)
for values of

? In this paper, we give a recursive formula for

in the

), to compute exact values in the case where

Of course, a rewording of Theorem
1.5
in the
2D
-case gives immediately that

t_{2}=\frac{1}{3}\leq Q_{K}(2)\leq q_{2}=\frac{1}{2}.

However, an additional independent elementary proof of this result will be given in Section

.
More generally, we believe that,

.

For all

, and all

Our belief in this conjecture relies on several facts, but the main one is the following: we made a random generator of convex sets

1.3
Motivation

There are several motivations for studying these ”floor case questions”.

\blacksquare

As already mentioned, in
2D
, “the floor case” is already present in the literature (but the terminology “floor case” is not), since convex chains in a triangle (with two vertices of the triangle being elements of this chain) appear under some forms in many papers devoted to points in convex position: Bárány & al.
[
3
]
, Valtr
[
24
,
25
]
, Buchta
[
6
]
, Morin
[
18
,
19
]
.

The computation of
P_{K}^{(d)}(d+2)
becomes much more difficult as soon as
d\geq 3
, and this is most likely due to the fact that there is no convenient decomposition suitable for a combinatorial approach. For example, if one draws
n
iid uniform points
U_{1},\cdots,U_{n}
in the 3D cube, and if one pushes the faces of the cube inward until they hit a point in
U[n]
, then one gets a parallelepiped with marked points, and this structure does not seem to be helpful for further decomposition of the problem! (For example, if each of the 6 faces contains a distinct point, then, the complement of the convex hull of these points is still connected, and it seems difficult to make any progress from there).

However, because of the success and simplicity of the study of the ”floor case in 2D”, it is natural to try to understand the floor case in 3D, and in the following dimensions.

\blacksquare

Take a compact convex set
K
of dimension
d
, and let
d+2
iid uniform points
U_{1},\cdots,U_{d+2}
into
K
.
For
1\leq m\leq d,
let
S_{m}
be the convex hull of
U_{1},\cdots,U_{m}
, and
H_{m}
be the affine space of dimension
m-1
containing
S_{m}
. We have

\displaystyle P_{K}^{(d)}(d+2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-(d+2)\mathbb{E}\Big({\sf Vol}_{d}(S_{d+1})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{d+2}{d}\mathbb{E}\Big({\sf Vol}_{d-1}(S_{d})\times D(U_{d+1},H_{d})\Big)

where
D(U_{d+1},H_{d})
is the Euclidean distance of
U_{d+1}
from the hyperplane
H_{d}
(for more information, see e.g. Kingman
[
14
]
, who computed
P_{K}^{(d)}(d+2)
, where
K
is the unit Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
).

To solve the Sylvester problem, it is natural to try to understand the typical (joint) behavior of the two random variables
{\sf Vol}_{d-1}(S_{d})
and
D(U_{d+1},H_{d})
, since the probability that
U_{d+2}
belongs to
{\sf CH}(U[d+1])
is
{\sf Vol}_{d-1}(S_{d})\times D(U_{d+1},H_{d})/d
.
A small picture shows that
H_{d}
cuts
K
into two parts
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
, and
U_{d+1}
will fall into one of these parts, proportional to its
d
-volume. Hence,
H_{d}
plays the role of a floor, and conditional on
H_{d}
(and of the part
P_{j}
in which
U_{d+1}
is)
D(U_{d+1},H_{d})
has the same distribution ”as the height of a random point in
P_{j}
”. Trying to understand what kind of part maximizes the height expectation – and we proved that they are mountains – can be seen as a step towards solving the Sylvester problem.

Lemma 2.1

\displaystyle Q_{K}(2)=1-2\int_{0}^{+\infty}L_{K}(t)\frac{t}{d}dt.

Proof.

By (

\displaystyle Q_{K}(2)=1-2\mathbb{E}\left({\sf Vol}_{d}({\sf Mountain}_{F}(Z,H))\right)=1-2\,\mathbb{E}({H}/d)=1-2\int_{0}^{+\infty}L_{K}(t)\frac{t}{d}dt.

(2.10)

∎

Hence, in the case of unitary mountains,

as announced. In the case of

2.1.1
Proof of Theorem

Each element

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rccl}G_{K}:&F&\longrightarrow&[0,+\infty)\\
&z=(z_{1},\cdots,z_{d-1})&\longmapsto&G_{K}(z):=\max\{y\in\mathbb{R},(z_{1},\cdots,z_{d-1},y)\in K\}\end{array}

\displaystyle K=\bigcup_{z\in F}\Big[\big(z_{0},\cdots,z_{d-1},0\big),\big(z_{0},\cdots,z_{d-1},G_{K}(z)\big)\Big].

(2.15)

Thus, if

and

characterize each other.
Moreover, a function

is the top function of a compact convex set

in

iff it is non-negative, concave, and satisfies

.

Recall that for

uniform in

The density of

\displaystyle Q_{K}(2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-2\int_{F}G_{K}(z)\frac{G_{K}(z)}{2d}dz=1-\int_{F}\frac{G_{K}^{2}(z)}{d}dz.

\displaystyle Q_{{\sf UnitPrism}}(2)-Q_{K}(2)=\int_{F}\frac{G_{K}^{2}(z)-1}{d}dz=\int_{F}\frac{(G_{K}(z)-1)^{2}}{d}dz+\underbrace{\int_{F}\frac{(2G_{K}(z)-2)}{d}dz}_{=0}\geq 0.

Proof of Lemma

.

There is nothing to prove if

is a unit mountain: we will assume that

is not a unit mountain, so that its maximum height satisfies

.

Consider the map
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}

We claim that there exists

such that

is non-negative on

, non-positive on
(t^{\star},m_{K})
.

The main argument will be the concavity of

and of

(notice the sign): there is only a tiny complication that comes from the fact that the first one is concave on
[0,m_{K}]
(and null thereafter), while the second is concave on the larger interval
[0,d]
(and null thereafter).

To prove the claim we need three facts:

\bullet
By (
2.20
),
\Psi
is concave on
[0,m_{K}]
(and negative on
(m_{K},d)
, and null thereafter).

\bullet
We have
\int_{0}^{m_{K}}L_{K}(t)dt=1>\int_{0}^{m_{K}}L_{M}(t)dt

in
(0,m_{K})
, so that
\Psi
must be positive somewhere on
(0,m_{K})
.

\bullet
In the extremities, we have

– either
\Psi(m_{K})\geq 0
, and since
\Psi(t)<0

– or
\Psi(m_{K})<0

on

, and

is non-positive on

.

This proves the claim.

Since the map

is non-decreasing (on

), the claim is also valid for the map
\Phi(t):=L_{K}(t)-L_{M}(t)
.

Therefore the map

and non-increasing on

(and null after that).

2D - considerations

In the 2D case, we will make an extensive use of top functions, which are simple concave functions. We focus on convex sets having floor
F:=[0,1]\times\{0\}
, belonging to
{\sf SubPrism}(F),
which prosaically means that they are contained in the rectangle

{\sf Rect}:=[0,1]\times[0,2].

We denote by
{\sf Conca}
the set of concave functions

such that
\int_{0}^{1}G(x)\,dx=1
. The set
{\sf Conca}
and
{\sf SubPrism}(F)
can be identified by associating with each

its top function and reciprocally, to a function

, the set below its graph

D_{G}:=\{(x,y)~:x\in[0,1],y\leq G(x)\}\cap{\sf Rect}

(see if needed the beginning of Section

).
Since it is more convenient to work with functions in the sequel, we will use the notation

3.1
General decomposition formula for
Q_{K}(n)

The main message of this section is that there exists a formula that allows to compute
Q^{G}_{n}
: the formula is recursive and uses some elements
Q^{H}_{n}
where
H
are defined using a part of the graph of
G
. Let us introduce the material devoted to the expression of this decomposition.

Take

and consider the two segments
S_{1}:=[(0,0),(t,G(t))]
and
S_{2}:=[(t,G(t)),(1,0)]
. Consider
L(t)
, the points that are in
D_{G}
and above the segment

; similarly, let

those that are in

and above

. Formally,

\displaystyle L(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{(x,y)~:0\leq x\leq t,y\geq\frac{x}{t}G(t)\right\}\cap D_{G},

(3.23)

\displaystyle R(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{(x,y)~:t\leq x\leq 1,y\geq G(t)+\frac{x-t}{1-t}(G(1)-G(t))\right\}\cap D_{G}.

(3.24)

In what follows, we will sometimes write

and

to clarify the dependence of these sets on

. For any

, we will also use the notation

standing for the Lebesgue measure of the set

; hence

|L(t)|+|R(t)|=1-G(t)/2.

and

We would rather work with the fixed floor

\bullet
a unique affine map

The sequence

.

For any function

, any

\displaystyle Q^{G}_{n}=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{m}\int_{0}^{1}G(t)Q^{NL(t)}_{m}Q^{NR(t)}_{n-1-m}|L(t)|^{m}|R(t)|^{n-1-m}dt.

(3.25)

Remark 3.2
.

Theorem
3.1
allows to express

in terms of
Q^{NR(t)}_{m}
and
Q^{NL(t)}_{n-1-m}
where

and

are relatively simple functions of the initial function

. In order to derive a closed formula for

, it is morally necessary that the class of domains

and

appearing in the decomposition remains under control. This is the case if the class of functions is stable by composition by affine functions (as constant, linear, quadratic functions are).

Proof.

The proof is inspired by the comb formula in
[
16
]
.

Consider the family of maps
G_{\lambda}:x\mapsto(1-\lambda)G(x)
. For
\lambda=0
the graph of

is the same as that of

. As
\lambda
moves from 0 to

, the family of domains

decreases for the inclusion partial order, and eventually, at time

, becomes flat.

Let

be iid uniform in

. Let

. Almost surely

(computed in

We claim that, for

\displaystyle Q_{n}^{G}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\binom{n-1}{m}|L(t)|^{m}|R(t)|^{n-m-1}Q^{NL(t)}_{m}Q^{NR(t)}_{n-m-1}\mathbb{P}(i^{\star}=k,X_{i^{\star}}\in dt)d\mathbb{P}_{\lambda^{\star}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(i^{\star}=k,X_{i^{\star}}\in dt)=(1/n)G(t)dt,

Lemma 3.6
.

Take
N>0
,
S>0
.
The number of paths in

with step

or
-1
, starting at 0, of length

, that hits
-S
for the first time at time

is counted by
\binom{3N+S}{N}\frac{S}{3N+S}={\sf Q}[3N,S]
. Hence , for any
S>0
,

,
S_{2}>0
such that

,

{\sf Q}[3N,S]=\sum_{k=0}^{N}{\sf Q}[3k,S_{1}]{\sf Q}[3(N-k),S_{2}].

Proof.

This Lemma is a consequence of a classical result in combinatorics which appears in the literature under many names: the cycle lemma, Dvoretzy-Motzkin’s lemma
[
9
]
, Otter formula, Kemperman’ s formula, and so on (see Pitman
[
21
, Section 6.1]
for historical notes and discussion).

It possesses a probabilistic and a combinatorial formulation, as follows:
Consider
AI
a set of “allowed increments” included in
\{-1,0,1,2,...\}
containing at least
-1
and a positive element. Denote by
{\sf Paths}[n,f]=\{w[n]=(w_{0},\cdots,w_{n}):~:~w_{i}-w_{i-1}\in AI,w_{0}=0,w_{n}=-f\}
the set of paths of length
n
whose increments are allowed, starting at zero, ending at
-f
, for some
f>0
. For a path
w[n]
, denote by
\tau_{f}(w[n])
the first index
k
such that
w_{k}=-f
(the hitting time of
-f
).
Let

{\sf Path}^{\star}[n,f]=\{w[n]\in{\sf Paths}[n,f],\tau_{f}(w[n])=n\}

that is, the elements of
{\sf Paths}[n,f]
hitting
-f
at the end, at time
n
. The cycle lemma asserts that if
n\geq 1
and
f>0
,

|{\sf Path}^{\star}[n,f]|=(f/n)\,\,|{\sf Path}[n,f]|.

The probabilistic version (proved exactly the same way) is valid when the increments are iid, with a distribution having supports in
AI
. We then sample a random walk
W=(W_{0},\cdots,W_{n})
with these increments, and denoting by
\tau_{f}
the hitting time by the random walk of
-f
, the formula goes as
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{f}(W[n])=n)=(f/n)\mathbb{P}(W_{n}=-f)
.

Here, we take
AI=\{2,-1\}
. In this case, observe that if
w
is in
{\sf Paths}[n,f]
for a fixed
f=S
, then, if
n_{2}
and
n_{-1}
are respectively its numbers of
+2
and
-1
steps, then

(n,-S)=(n_{2}+n_{-1},2n_{2}-n_{-1}),~~\Rightarrow(n_{2},n_{-1})=(\frac{n-S}{3},\frac{2n+S}{3}).

In particular,
n
has the form
3N+S
and
(n_{2},n_{-1})=(N,2N+S)
(for
N=(n-S)/3
).
It follows that
{\sf Path}[3N+S,S]=\binom{3N+S}{N}
, and by the cyclic lemma
{\sf Path}^{\star}[3N+S,S]=(S/(3N+S))\binom{3N+S}{N}
, which proves then the first statement of the Lemma.

The second statement is easier: we will associate bijectively, to each element
w
in
{\sf Path}^{\star}[3N+S,S]
, a pair of paths
(w^{(1)},w^{(2)})
, where
w^{(1)}
is in
{\sf Path}^{\star}[3k+S_{1},S_{1}]
and
w^{(2)}
is in
{\sf Path}^{\star}[3(N-k)+S_{2},S_{2}]
, for some
k
. This association is a bijection from
{\sf Path}^{\star}[3N+S,S]
onto
\cup_{k=0}^{N}{\sf Path}^{\star}[3k+S_{1},S_{1}]\times{\sf Path}^{\star}[3(N-k)+S_{2},S_{2}]
.

Take a path
w
in
{\sf Path}^{\star}[3N+S,S]
. It hits level
-S
at time
3N+S
for the first time. Since the only negative steps in
w
are
-1
, this path hits level
-S_{1}
for the first time at some time
\tau_{S_{1}}
of the form
3k+S_{1}
, and the increments of the rest of the paths correspond to a path with length
3N+S-(3k+S_{1})=3(N-k)+S_{2}
hitting
-S_{2}
at the end. Technically:

\displaystyle w^{(1)}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w_{j},\textrm{ for }0\leq j\leq\tau_{S_{1}},

\displaystyle w^{(2)}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w_{\tau_{S_{1}}+j}-w_{\tau_{S_{1}}},0\leq j\leq 3N+S-\tau_{S_{1}}=3(N-\tau_{S_{1}})+S_{2}.

The inverse of this bijection consists just to take a pair of paths
(w^{(1)},w^{(2)})
in
{\sf Path}^{\star}[3k+S_{1},S_{1}]\times{\sf Path}^{\star}[3(N-k)+S_{2},S_{2}]
and to concatenate their increments in order to construct a path in
{\sf Path}^{\star}[3N+S,S]
.
∎

3.3
Optimization of
\texorpdfstring

In 2D, since we want to prove

which is then equivalent to

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}G^{2}(t)dt\leq\int_{0}^{1}(2t)^{2}dt=\frac{4}{3}.

The idea of this second argument relies on a “representation formula”: every function

can be written as a mixture of mountains – that is a weighted sums of mountains – that is, it can be written as

\displaystyle G=\int_{0}^{1}M_{s}d\mu(s)

(3.39)

where
\mu
is a positive Borelian measure with total mass 1 on
[0,1]
. Such a
\mu
is a probability distribution on
[0,1]
, which means that for
S
a random variable with law
\mu
,

G(x)=\mathbb{E}(M_{S}(x)),~~~\textrm{ for all }x\in[0,1].

Assume for a moment that such a representation exists and observe that for any mountain

,

\int_{0}^{1}M_{s}^{2}(x)dx=\int_{0}^{1}(2t)^{2}\,dt=4/3.

Now, by Fubini

\int_{0}^{1}G(x)^{2}dx=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}(M_{S}(x))^{2}dx\leq\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}(M_{S}(x)^{2})dx=\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{1}M_{S}(x)^{2}dx\right)\leq\int_{0}^{1}\frac{4}{3}dx=\frac{4}{3}.

It remains to establish the representation formula, and we will do so by proving it first in the case where

is piecewise linear, with a finite number of pieces.
In this case
G^{\prime}(a^{+})-G^{\prime}(a^{-})
is zero except at

for a finite set

G
, corresponding to discontinuities in the derivative of

.
Consider a sum of mountains with same set of vertices:
H:=\sum_{s\in I\cup\{0,1\}}p_{s}M_{s}
where the

are non negative (and positive if
s\in S
). Since for the mountains
M_{s}
and
s\in(0,1)
,

M^{\prime}_{s}(s^{+})-M^{\prime}_{s}(s^{-})=-2/(1-s)-2/s

it is possible to fix the
p_{s}
so that

G=\sum_{s\in I\cup\{0,1\}}p_{s}M_{s}

by taking
p_{s}=\frac{G^{\prime}(s^{+})-G^{\prime}(s^{-})}{-2/(1-s)-2/s}
for
s\in I
, and
p_{0}=G(0)/2
,
p_{1}=G(1)/2
.
The fact that
\sum_{s\in I\cup\{0,1\}}p_{s}=1
is just a consequence of the fact that

, and
\int M_{s}=1
for all
s
.
This provides a representation formula for these types of functions

, which are dense in

, for the uniform topology. For a general

in

, take a sequence

(\mu_{n})
possesses an accumulation point

in the set of probability measures on

(which is compact) and then there exists a converging subsequence
(\mu_{n_{k}})
, that converges to a probability measure
\mu
on
[0,1]
. Let

be
\mu

G(x)=\lim G_{n}(x)=\lim_{k}G_{n_{k}}(x)=\lim_{k}\mathbb{E}\left(M_{S_{n_{k}}}(x)\right).

Since the map

, is bounded and continuous on

, we get from

G(x)=\mathbb{E}(S_{X}(x))=\int_{0}^{1}M_{s}(x)d\mu(s),

which is the wanted representation formula.

does not depend on the side lengths of the tetrahedron (as long as
T
is taken non trivial), but note that here the floor area is
1/2
: the simplicity of the floor will help to get a tractable formula in the sequel. Note that for this reason, ”the apex” of
T
, seen as a mountain, is
D
and has a height of 6.

The aim of this section is to prove Theorems
4.5
and
4.6
, that give two (non trivial) sequences
(\ell_{n})
and
(u_{n})
such that

\displaystyle\ell_{n}\leq Q_{T}(n)\leq u_{n},\textrm{~~for~~}n\geq 0.

(4.44)

4.2.1
Computation of an upper bound.

Theorem 4.5
.

For any
n
, we have
Q_{T}(n)\leq u_{n}
, where
(u_{n})
satisfies the following recursion:
u_{0}=1
, and for
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle u_{n}=\frac{6}{(n+2)(n+1)n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}u_{k}u_{n-1-k},

(4.45)

(so that, in particular
u_{1}=Q_{T}(1)=1
,
u_{2}=Q_{T}(2)=1/2
).
The first terms from
u_{0}
to
u_{9}
are:

\left[1,1,{\frac{1}{2}},{\frac{1}{5}},{\frac{7}{100}},{\frac{79}{3500}},{\frac{337}{49000}},{\frac{2069}{1029000}},{\frac{7033}{12348000}}\right]

Proof.

Let us call top of
T
, the triangle
BCD
.
We define a floor to top projection,
\pi_{t}:ABC\to BCD
parallel to the normal to the floor. Hence,
for
q=(x,y,0)
,

\pi_{t}(q):=\pi_{t}(x,y,0)=(x,y,h(x,y)),

where
h(x,y)=6\,(1-(x+y))
is the height of the top, above
q
.

Figure 8
:
Representation of
q
, and
\pi_{t}(q)
. The plane incident to
A,D,q,\pi_{t}(q)
cuts the initial tetrahedron into two parts.

The vertical plane incident to
A,D,q,\pi_{t}(q)
intersects the segment
(B,C)
at the point

p=\left(\frac{x}{x+y},\frac{y}{x+y},0\right),

and this plane cuts the initial tetrahedron in two parts. In one of the two half-spaces, one finds the tetrahedron

T_{1}(q)=A,\pi_{t}(q),B,D
, and in the other side
T_{2}(q)=A,\pi_{t}(q),C,D
.
We have

\displaystyle{\sf Vol}_{2}(ABC)=\frac{1}{2},~~{\sf Vol}_{2}(ABp)=\frac{y}{2(x+y)},~~{\sf Vol}_{2}(ACq)=\frac{x}{2(x+y)}.

Hence

{\sf Vol}_{3}(ADBp)=\frac{y}{x+y},~~~{\sf Vol}_{3}(ADCp)=\frac{x}{x+y},

and then

{\sf Vol}_{3}(ABp\pi_{t}(q))=yh(x,y)/(6(x+y)),~~{\sf Vol}_{3}(ACp\pi_{t}(q))=xh(x,y)/(6(x+y)).

Finally set

\displaystyle V_{1}(x,y):={\sf Vol}_{3}(T_{1}(q))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\sf Vol}_{3}(ADBp)-{\sf Vol}_{3}(ABp\pi_{t}(q))=\frac{y}{x+y}-y\frac{(1-(x+y))}{x+y}=y

\displaystyle V_{2}(x,y):={\sf Vol}_{3}(T_{2}(q))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\sf Vol}_{3}(ADCp)-{\sf Vol}_{3}(ACp\pi_{t}(q))=\frac{x}{x+y}-x\frac{(1-(x+y))}{x+y}=x

The way of reasoning from here is very similar to that which led to ”the comb formulas” in
[
16
]
.
We will need two main principles:

(1) If
(X,Y,Z)
is a uniform point in the tetrahedron, then conditional on
(X,Y)
, the variable
Z
is uniform in
[0,h(X,Y)]
. To prove this, take a bounded continuous test function
f:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
, and write
\mathbb{E}(f(Z/h(X,Y),X,Y))=\int_{x+y\leq 1}\int_{z=0}^{h(x,y)}f(z/h(x,y),x,y){\mathrm{d}}z{\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}y
, then proceed to the change of variables
(u,x,y)=(z/h(x,y),x,y)
, so that
{\mathrm{d}}z{\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}y=h(x,y){\mathrm{d}}u{\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}y
, and get
\mathbb{E}(f(Z/h(X,Y),X,Y))=\int_{x+y\leq 1}\int_{u=0}^{1}f(u,x,y){\mathrm{d}}u(h(x,y){\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}y)
, from what we see that the marginal
Z/h(X,Y)
is uniform and independent of
(X,Y)
, whose distribution has density
h(x,y)\mathds{1}_{x+y\leq 1}{\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}y
.

(2) Take
(U_{i},1\leq i\leq n)
some iid uniform random variables taken in a set
D
(with positive Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
), and
D^{\prime}\subset D
, also with positive Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, if we condition by the event
E=\{U_{i}\in D^{\prime},1\leq i\leq n\}
, then the
U_{i}
are independent, and uniform in
D^{\prime}
.

Now, consider the coordinates of the points
U_{i}=(X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i})
for the independent uniform random points taken in
T
. Conditional on
(X_{i},Y_{i})
,
Z_{i}
being uniform on
[0,h(X_{i},Y_{i})]
can be written as
Z_{i}=V_{i}h(X_{i},Y_{i})
with
V_{i}
uniform on
[0,1]
, independent of
X_{i},Y_{i}
. Now, consider
i^{\star}
the special index
i
of the maximal
V_{i}
. Conditional on
(i^{\star},V_{i^{\star}})
, the other
V_{i}
are uniform on
[0,V_{i^{\star}}]
(and independent).
In other words, the other variables
U_{i}
are iid uniform below the plane
(x,y,h(x,y)V_{i\star})
while
U_{i^{\star}}
is on that plane. It means that the other variables can be written under the form
(X_{i},Y_{i},h(X_{i},Y_{i})V_{i\star}W_{i})
where the
W_{i}
are uniform on
[0,1]
, and the
(X_{i},Y_{i})
are distributed according to the density
h(x,y){\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}y
as usual (and all these variables are independent).

In words, the points
(X_{j},Y_{j},Z_{j})
are now written under the form
(X_{j},Y_{j},h(X_{j},Y_{j})W_{j}V_{i^{\star}})
where the
W_{j}
are uniform on
[0,1]
, except for
W_{i^{\star}}
which is equal to 1.
The fact that they are in convex position with the floor is equivalent to the problem where we remove
V_{i^{\star}}
.

From here we can deduce that

Q_{T}(n)=Q^{\prime}_{T}(1,n-1):=\mathbb{P}\left(\{U_{0},\cdots,U_{n-1}\}\subset\partial CH(F\cup\{U_{0},\cdots,U_{n-1}\})\right)

where:

– the point
U_{0}
is placed at position
(X,Y,h(X,Y))
on the surface of the top triangle, where the density of the variable
(X,Y)
is
h(x,y){\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}y
(notice that
(X,Y)
is not biased because of the independence between the
(V_{i})
and the
(X_{i},Y_{i})
),

– the points
U_{1},\cdots,U_{n-1}
are inside
T
uniformly at random, independently from
U_{0}
.

Conditionally on
U_{0}=(x,y,h(x,y))
(which is
\pi_{t}(q)
in the previous discussion), consider the subsets of the indices
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
of the
U_{i}
belonging to
T_{1}(q)
and
T_{2}(q)
. If the points
U_{0},U_{1},\cdots,U_{n-1}\in\partial CH(F\cup\{U_{0},\cdots,U_{n-1}\})
, then
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
must form a partition of
\{1,\cdots,n-1\}
. In particular, we must have

\displaystyle\{U_{i},i\in J_{1}\}

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle\partial{\sf CH}(\{U_{i},i\in J_{1}\}\cup\{A,B,\pi_{t}(q)\},

(4.46)

\displaystyle\{U_{i},i\in J_{2}\}

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle\partial{\sf CH}(\{U_{i},i\in J_{2}\}\cup\{A,C,\pi_{t}(q)\}

(4.47)

so that, we are back in the initial floor problem settings.
We then deduce that

\displaystyle Q_{T}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q_{T}^{\prime}(1,n-1)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{(x,y)\in ABC}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{k}h(x,y){\sf Vol}(T_{1})^{k}{\sf Vol}(T_{2})^{n-k-1}Q_{T}(k)Q_{T}(n-k-1)dxdy.

The symbol ”
\leq
” comes from the fact that the union over
(J_{1},J_{2})
of the events (
4.46
) contains positions of points that are not globally in convex position.
Observe that

\displaystyle\alpha(n,k)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\binom{n-1}{k}\int_{(x,y)\in ABC}V_{1}(x,y)^{k}V_{2}(x,y)^{n-1-k}h(x,y)dxdy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{n-1}{k}\int_{x+y\leq 1\atop x,y\geq 0}y^{k}x^{n-1-k}6(1-(x+y))dxdy

Using again (
3.30
),

\displaystyle D[k,j]:=\int_{x=0}^{1}x^{k}\int_{y=0}^{1-x}y^{j}dydx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{x=0}^{1}x^{k}(1-x)^{j+1}/(j+1)dx=\frac{k!j!}{(k+j+2)!}

and then

\alpha(n,k)=6\binom{n-1}{k}(D[k,n-1-k]-D[k,n-k]-D[k+1,n-1-k])=6\frac{(n-1)!}{(n+2)!}.

In fine, putting all together,

Q_{T}(n)\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{6(n-1)!}{(n+2)!}Q_{T}(k)Q_{T}(n-1-k)
from what the result follows.
∎

4.2.2
Lower bound

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
(\ell_{n},n\geq 0)
be the sequence defined by
\ell_{0}=1
, for

\displaystyle\ell_{n}=6\frac{(n-1)!\;n!}{(2n+1)!}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\ell_{k}\;\ell_{n-1-k},~~~\textrm{ for }~~n\geq 1,

(4.48)

then
Q_{T}(n)\geq\ell_{n}
for all
n
.

The first terms of the sequence
(\ell_{n})
, for
n
from 0 to 6 are

\displaystyle\left[1,1,{\frac{1}{5}},{\frac{1}{50}},{\frac{11}{10500}},{\frac{431}{12127500}},{\frac{2371}{2801452500}}\right].

This bound is better than the general bound given in Theorem
4.1
, meaning
Y_{n}\leq\ell_{n}
for all
n\geq 0
(we prove this statement at the end of this proof).

Proof.

We proceed in the beginning as in the proof of Theorem
4.5
until the addition of the point
U_{0}
, and again use that
Q_{T}(n)=Q^{\prime}_{T}(1,n-1)
.

Consider
B^{\prime}C^{\prime}
the segment obtained as the intersection of the top triangle
BCD
with the line parallel to
BC
containing
\pi_{t}(q)
, oriented as in Figure
8
.

Now, take the indices
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
of the
U_{i}
(with
1\leq i\leq n-1
), such that
i\in J_{1}\iff U_{i}\in T^{\prime}_{1}:=AB\pi_{t}(q)B^{\prime}
and
i\in J_{2}\iff U_{i}\in T^{\prime}_{2}:=AC\pi_{t}(q)C^{\prime}
.

We claim that if
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
form a partition of
\{1,\cdots,n-1\}
and if

\displaystyle\{U_{i},i\in J_{1}\}

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle\partial{\sf CH}(\{U_{i},i\in J_{1}\}\cup AB\pi_{t}(q))

\displaystyle\{U_{i},i\in J_{2}\}

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle\partial{\sf CH}(\{U_{i},i\in J_{2}\}\cup AC\pi_{t}(q))

then, the points
U_{0},\cdots,U_{n-1}\in{\sf CH}(\{U_{0},\cdots,U_{n-1}\}\cup ABC)
. Indeed, the plane
AB^{\prime}C^{\prime}
is a supporting plane of the
{\sf CH}(\{U_{0},\cdots,U_{n-1}\}\cup\{A,B,C\})
which, once more, splits the points
U_{i}
in two independent regions.

Conditional on
U_{0}=\pi_{t}(q)=(x,y,h(x,y))
,
B^{\prime}=(x,0,h(x,y))
and
C^{\prime}=(0,y,h(x,y))

we get (using the determinant formula for the computation of the volume),

\displaystyle V^{\prime}_{1}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{\sf Vol}(T_{1}^{\prime})=y(1-(x+y)),

(4.49)

\displaystyle V^{\prime}_{2}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{\sf Vol}(T_{2}^{\prime})=x(1-(x+y)).

(4.50)

We then have that a lower bound is given by

\ell_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\ell_{k}\;\ell_{n-1-k}\;g(n,k)

with

\displaystyle g(n,k)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\binom{n-1}{k}\int_{ABC}V_{1}^{\prime k}(x,y)V_{2}^{\prime n-1-k}(x,y)h(x,y)dxdy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 6\binom{n-1}{k}\int_{ABC}y^{k}x^{n-1-k}(1-(x+y))^{n}dxdy

Set
t=x+y
, and since
x+y\leq 1
we get by expanding
y^{k}=\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{k}\binom{k}{k_{1}}t^{k_{1}}(-x)^{k-k_{1}}

\displaystyle g(n,k)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle 6\binom{n-1}{k}\int\int_{0\leq x\leq t\leq 1}\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{k}\binom{k}{k_{1}}(-1)^{k-k_{1}}t^{k_{1}}x^{n-1-k_{1}}(1-t)^{n}dxdt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 6\binom{n-1}{k}\int_{0\leq t\leq 1}\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{k}\binom{k}{k_{1}}(-1)^{k-k_{1}}\frac{1}{n-k_{1}}t^{n}(1-t)^{n}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 6\binom{n-1}{k}\frac{(n!)^{2}}{(2n+1)!}\underbrace{\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{k}\binom{k}{k_{1}}(-1)^{k-k_{1}}\frac{1}{n-k_{1}}}_{S_{n}}

we claim that this last sum
S_{n}
equals
(n\binom{n-1}{k})^{-1}
so that we get
g(n,k):=6n!(n-1)!/(2n+1)!
in the end, which is the theorem statement.

To prove the claim, set
\psi(x):=\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{k}\binom{k}{k_{1}}(-1)^{k-k_{1}}\frac{x^{n-k_{1}}}{n-k_{1}}
and observe that

\psi^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{k}\binom{k}{k_{1}}(-1)^{k-k_{1}}x^{(n-1)-k_{1}}=x^{n-1}(-1+1/x)^{k}=x^{n-1-k}(1-x)^{k}.

Since
\psi
is the primitive of
\psi^{\prime}
which is null at zero, we have
S_{n}=\psi(1)=\int_{0}^{1}\psi^{\prime}(x)dx=\int_{0}^{1}x^{n-1-k}(1-x)^{k}dx
, so that with (
3.30
),
S_{n}=(n\binom{n-1}{k})^{-1}
.

To prove that
Y_{n}\leq\ell_{n}
where
Y_{n}
was introduced in Theorem
4.1
, we proceed by induction and assume that
\ell_{k}\geq Y_{k}
for all
1\leq k\leq n-1
(one can check that this property is indeed true for the first values of
k
).
Now, by definition of
\ell_{n}
and
Y_{n}
, and by hypothesis, we have the inequality

\displaystyle\ell_{n}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{6}{n(2n+1)}{2n\choose n}^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}Y_{k}Y_{n-1-k}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{6}{n(2n+1)}{2n\choose n}^{-1}\frac{2^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}{n-1\choose k}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{3j-1}\right)\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n-1-k}\frac{1}{3j-1}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{6}{n(2n+1)}{2n\choose n}^{-1}Y_{n-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}{n-1\choose k}\frac{\prod_{j=k+1}^{n-1}{(3j-1)}}{\prod_{j=1}^{n-1-k}(3j-1)}

By factor comparisons, one can check that
\frac{\prod_{j=k+1}^{n-1}{(3j-1)}}{\prod_{j=1}^{n-1-k}(3j-1)}\geq{n-1\choose k}
, so that, since
\binom{2(n-1)}{n-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{k}^{2}
, we reach

\ell_{n}\geq\frac{6}{n(2n+1)}\frac{{2(n-1)\choose n-1}}{{2n\choose n}}Y_{n-1},

and it then suffices to see that
\displaystyle\frac{6}{n(2n+1)}\frac{{2(n-1)\choose n-1}}{{2n\choose n}}\geq\frac{2}{n(3n-1)}
to complete the claim.

∎
