Evaluation of Virulence Profiles and Molecular Typing of ESBL-Producing Uropathogenic Escherichia coli: Dominance of ST131 and Emergence of ST421 in Iran
Authors: Zahra Asadi, Mehrdad Halaji, Mehdi Rajabnia, Zahra Ghasemian, Abazar Pournajaf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeGlobally, the clonal lineages ST69, ST73, ST95, ST127, and ST131 account for more than half of Uropathogenic Escherichia coli (UPEC) infections. Given the global dissemination and clinical importance of ST131 as a high-risk multidrug-resistant clone, the present study aimed to characterize and compare antimicrobial susceptibility profiles, virulence genes, and resistance determinants of ST131 and non-ST131 UPEC isolates collected from hospitalized patients in Iran over three years (2021–2024).Patients and MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, 179 confirmed UPEC isolates were examined. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method according to CLSI guidelines, and ESBL production was phenotypically confirmed using the combined disk test (CDT). PCR was performed to detect the blaCTX-M-15 and blaCTX-M-14 genes as well as virulence factors. Common STs were identified via multiplex PCR and MLST, and a clonal relationship was drawn.ResultsOverall, 75.4% of the isolates were MDR and 52.5% were ESBL producers, predominantly harboring blaCTX-M-15 (52.5%). The most frequent virulence genes were sat (44.7%), kpsM II-K2 (41.3%), and iroN (40.2%). ST131 was the dominant lineage (63.1%), followed by ST69 (8.4%), ST95 (7.3%), and ST73 (3.9%); notably, ST421 was detected for the first time in Iran. ST131 isolates presented significantly greater resistance to cephalosporins and a stronger association with ESBL production and blaCTX-M-15. Virulence profiles varied across STs, with iroN enriched in ST131.ConclusionThis study highlights the dominance of ST131 and the first detection of ST421 among UPEC strains in Iran, with blaCTX-M-15 as the principal ESBL determinant. The heterogeneous virulence landscape underscores the clinical threat posed by MDR clones, emphasizing the need for molecular surveillance and strengthened infection control strategies in hospital settings.

Introduction

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) represent one of the most common and recurrent infectious diseases in humans, affecting millions of people worldwide each year. A wide range of pathogens can cause UTIs, including gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria as well as fungi; however, Escherichia coli is by far the predominant causative agent.1,2 Strains of E. coli capable of causing UTIs are termed uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC), a subgroup within the broader category of extraintestinal pathogenic E. coli (ExPEC). UPEC accounts for approximately 75–95% of UTI cases and has been associated with prolonged hospital stays, increased healthcare costs, mortality rates, and elevated morbidity.3

Antimicrobial therapy remains the primary treatment strategy for UTIs. The widespread administration of antibiotics has led to a marked rise in antimicrobial resistance, particularly against fluoroquinolones and third-generation cephalosporins.4 The increasing prevalence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) among UPEC strains has become a major clinical and public health concern.5 Many UPEC isolates are now classified as multidrug-resistant (MDR), extensively drug-resistant (XDR), or even pandrug-resistant (PDR), which complicates treatment and reduces therapeutic options. Consequently, continuous monitoring of AMR emergence and dissemination is essential for updating and optimizing empiric treatment guidelines.6 β-lactam antibiotics have long been the treatment of choice for UPEC infections. Unfortunately, their effectiveness has been increasingly compromised by the emergence of extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs).7,8

UPEC pathogenicity is largely attributed to virulence-associated genes (VAGs), including adhesins, toxins, siderophores, capsules, and protectins, that enable colonization, immune evasion, and host tissue damage.9 Notably, in the globally disseminated ST131 lineage, four major virotypes (A–D) have been defined according to distinct virulence gene profiles. Previous reports indicate that most UPEC isolates arise from a few dominant clonal lineages and exhibit diverse virulence traits and resistance profiles. Molecular epidemiological studies have demonstrated that UPEC strains responsible for UTIs often arise from a few globally disseminated clonal lineages. Multiple-locus sequence typing (MLST) has revealed that only a small subset of pandemic lineages, including ST10, ST69, ST73, ST95, ST127, and ST131, accounts for most ExPEC infections worldwide. Among these, ST131 has emerged as the most clinically important high-risk clone because of its global dissemination, multidrug-resistant phenotype, and frequent association with ESBL production, particularly CTX-M enzymes. In contrast, non-ST131 clones exhibit considerable diversity in their antimicrobial resistance and virulence profiles. Therefore, comparing ST131 with non-ST131 isolates provides valuable insights into the molecular epidemiology, resistance patterns, and pathogenic potential of circulating UPEC strains.10,11 Furthermore, the rapid identification of predominant sequence types (STs) through molecular techniques can support clinicians in selecting appropriate antimicrobial therapies prior to the availability of conventional susceptibility test results.

In recent years, a new family of plasmid-mediated ESBLs, termed cefotaxime-hydrolyzing β-lactamases (CTX-M), has become widespread globally.12 Based on amino acid sequence identity, CTX-M enzymes are grouped into five clusters: CTX-M-1, CTX-M-2, CTX-M-8, CTX-M-9, and CTX-M-25/26. Among these variants, the CTX-M-1 group is the most prevalent worldwide, with CTX-M-15 being the dominant variant. In the CTX-M-9 group, CTX-M-9 and CTX-M-14 are the most frequently reported enzymes.13–15

Currently, limited information is available on the molecular epidemiology of UTIs caused by ST131 and non-ST131 ESBL-producing UPEC clones, especially in northern Iran. Thus, the present study aimed to determine the differences in antibiotic susceptibility patterns, virulence, and resistance markers between ST131 and non-ST131 clones in Iranian hospitals over three years (2021–2024). The findings are expected to enhance the understanding of the molecular epidemiology of UPEC isolates and provide regional data that may support antimicrobial surveillance and guide empirical treatment and infection control strategies.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Bacterial Isolation

In this cross-sectional observational study, 179 nonduplicate Escherichia coli strains were isolated from patients diagnosed with UTIs. Clinical urine samples were obtained from hospitalized patients admitted to hospitals affiliated with Babol University of Medical Sciences, located in northern Iran, from 2021–2024. Isolates were included based on the following criteria: I. Significant bacteriuria (≥105 CFU/mL), II. Clinical diagnosis of UTI by an infectious disease specialist, III. Pure culture of E. coli without mixed growth. In brief, isolates were cultured on eosin methylene blue (EMB) agar and blood agar, and E. coli isolates were confirmed via standard biochemical tests, including colony morphology, Gram staining, Methyl Red-Voges Proskauer (MR-VP), Simmons citrate, urease, nitrate reduction, motility at 37 °C, indole production, and gas formation. To confirm that the isolates were E. coli, the presence of the uidA gene was examined via PCR. This gene encodes the β-glucuronidase enzyme, which is considered highly specific for E. coli.16 The 179 UPEC isolates from our established collection were re‑identified after recovery and then used for all subsequent analyses.17

Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (AST)

The antimicrobial susceptibility of the isolates was determined via the Kirby–Bauer disc diffusion method on Mueller–Hinton agar (Merck, Germany) following the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. Thirteen antimicrobial agents, including amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (AMC, 20/10 μg), ceftazidime (CAZ, 30 μg), cefotaxime (CTX, 30 μg), cefixime (CFM, 5 μg), nalidixic acid (NA, 30 μg), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (SXT, 25 μg), meropenem (MEM, 10 μg), gentamicin (GM, 10 μg), amikacin (AMK, 30 μg), ciprofloxacin (CIP, 5 μg), ceftriaxone (CRO, 30 μg), nitrofurantoin (FM, 300 μg), and ampicillin-sulbactam (SAM, 20 μg) (Padtan Teb, Iran), were tested. E. coli ATCC 25922 was used as the quality control strain.18 The resistance score (RS) was calculated as the number of antibiotics to which each isolate exhibited resistance. Isolates resistant to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial classes were defined as MDR.19

Screening for the ESBL Phenotype

ESBL production was phenotypically assessed via the combined disk test (CDT) in accordance with CLSI guidelines.18

Genomic DNA Extraction and Molecular Detection of Antimicrobial Resistance Genes

Genomic DNA was extracted from freshly cultured colonies as previously described.20 ESBL genes, including blaCTX-M-15 and blaCTX-M-14, were detected via PCR using the primers listed in Supplementary Table 1. The PCR cycling protocol included an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30s, annealing at gene-specific temperatures (Supplementary Table 1) for 30s, and extension at 72 °C for 30s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were separated on a 1.5% agarose gel and visualized under UV illumination.21

Molecular Typing of Isolates

All 179 isolates were initially screened via PCR to determine whether they belonged to the main E. coli sequence types ST69, ST73, ST95, ST121, and ST131 using methods described by Doumith et al.22 Afterward, four ESBL-producing isolates harboring the blaCTX-M-15 gene that could not be assigned to any of the targeted sequence types by PCR-based screening were further characterized using sequence-based MLST analysis based on sequencing of housekeeping genes according to the Achtman scheme. Seven housekeeping genes (adk, fumC, gyrB, icd, mdh, purA, and recA) were amplified, and STs were assigned via the MLST database (https://pubmlst.org/bigsdb?db=pubmlst_escherichia_seqdef).23 To identify CCs, single-locus variants (SLVs), double-locus variants (DLVs), and singletons, MLST profiles from PubMLST and the present study were analyzed using the goeBURST algorithm within PHYLOViZ 2.0 software (https://online.phyloviz.net/index).24

Virulence Gene Profiling

The presence of key UPEC virulence genes was assessed via PCR using previously described specific primers (Supplementary Table 1). The targeted genes included adhesins (afa/draBC, afa operon FM955459), iron uptake (iroN), capsules (kpsM II), miscellaneous (ibeA), and toxins (hlyA, sat). The combination of these virulence genes was then used to classify the isolates into different virotypes on the basis of their virulence profiles.25,26

GenBank Accession Numbers

Sequence data corresponding to the MLST housekeeping genes obtained from four ESBL-producing E. coli isolates were deposited in the GenBank database under the listed accession numbers and sample IDs: PQ656111 (ZA-1), PQ656112 (ZA-2), PQ656113 (ZA-3), PQ656114 (ZA-4), PQ656115 (ZA-5), PQ656116 (ZA-6), PQ656117 (ZA-7), PQ656118 (ZA-8), PQ656119 (ZA-9), PQ656120 (ZA-10), PQ656121 (ZA-11), PQ656122 (ZA-12), PQ656123 (ZA-13), PQ656124 (ZA-14), PQ656125 (ZA-15), PQ656126 (ZA-16), PQ656127 (ZA-17), PQ656128 (ZA-18), PQ656129 (ZA-19), PQ656130 (ZA-20), PQ656131 (ZA-21), PQ656132 (ZA-22), PQ656133 (ZA-23), and PQ656134 (ZA-24).

Statistical Analysis

A chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were used to analyze group differences in frequency distributions in SPSS version 26.0. Comparisons of score distributions were performed with the Mann–Whitney U-test. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value of <0.05.

Results

Study Population

In this study, a total of 179 confirmed UPEC isolates were analyzed. Among these, 57.5% (n=103) were isolated from children, and 42.5% (n=76) were from adults. A majority of the isolates, 71.5% (n=128), were obtained from female patients, whereas 28.5% (n=51) were from male patients.

Phenotypic Antimicrobial Resistance of UPEC Isolates

The antimicrobial susceptibility patterns of 179 UPEC isolates were determined against the tested antibiotics. The highest resistance rate was observed against nalidixic acid at 83.8% (150/179), followed by ampicillin-sulbactam (73.2%; 131/179). In contrast, the lowest resistance rates were detected against nitrofurantoin (10.6%; 19/179) and meropenem (15.1%; 27/179). Overall, 135 isolates (75.4%) were classified as MDR, showing resistance to three or more antimicrobial agents. Furthermore, 94 isolates (52.5%) were phenotypically confirmed as ESBL producers. Presence of blaCTX-M-15 and blaCTX-M-14

PCR amplification of the blaCTX-M-15 and blaCTX-M-14 genes in 179 UPEC strains revealed that 52.5% (94/179) of the strains harbored the blaCTX-M-15 gene, whereas 15.6% (28/179) carried the blaCTX-M-14 gene.

Antibiotic Susceptibility in Relation to blaCTX-M-15 and blaCTX-M-14

The antimicrobial susceptibility pattern demonstrated that isolates harboring blaCTX-M-15 and blaCTX-M-14 presented the highest resistance to NA, with rates of 87.2% and 94.6%, respectively. In contrast, the lowest resistance rates were observed for AMK and FM, at 11.7% among blaCTX-M-15-positive isolates and 14.3% among blaCTX-M-14-positive isolates.

Prevalence of VF Genes and Distribution of Virotypes

PCR analysis revealed that the prevalence of individual VF genes ranged from 5.6% for ibeA to 44.7% for sat. The most common VF gene was sat (44.7%), followed by kpsM II-K2 (41.3%), iroN (40.2%), afa/draBC (19.6%), and hlyA (19%), whereas the afa operons FM955459 (8.4%) and ibeA (5.6%) were the least common. Among the 179 isolates, A (10%), B (19%), C (12%), D3 (4%), D5 (2%), and E (4%) were distributed across virotypes; notably, 50% of the isolates could not be assigned to any virotype because of the absence of virotype-defining genes.

ST Distribution and the Clonal Relationship

Analysis of the major E. coli STs revealed ST131 was the most prevalent, accounting for 63.1% (113/179) of the isolates. Other detected STs included ST69 (8.4%), ST95 (7.3%), ST73 (3.9%), and ST127 (1.1%). Overall, 63.1% of the isolates belonged to ST131, whereas 36.9% were classified as non-ST131 strains. Analysis of the remaining four ESBL-producing isolates carrying the blaCTX-M-15 gene revealed they belonged to four distinct STs: ST744 (one isolate), ST421 (one isolate), and ST1193 (two isolates). Notably, ST421 was identified for the first time in Iran, although only in a small number of isolates. Approximately 14% of isolates remained untyped. These isolates did not cluster within the five major STs and were generally non-ESBL producers.

Clonal relationship analysis revealed that ST131 clustered within CC131, ST95 and ST421 within CC95, ST69 within CC69, ST73 within CC73, ST744 within CC10, and ST1193 within CC14. A phylogenetic analysis of the sequence types within each clonal complex is presented in Figures 2 and 3, demonstrating both genetic relatedness and diversity among the isolates. Antibiotic Susceptibility in Relation to That of ST131 and Non-ST131 Strains

As shown in Figure 4, NA presented the highest resistance rates in both the ST131 and non-ST131 isolates, at 86.7% and 78.8%, respectively. Notably, compared with non-ST131 isolates, ST131 isolates presented significantly greater resistance to AMC (P = 0.027), MEM (P = 0.048), CTX (P = 0.010), CRO (P = 0.001), CAZ (P = 0.005), and CFM (P < 0.001). Accordingly, resistance to third- and fourth-generation cephalosporins was significantly more common among the ST131 isolates (P < 0.05). A comparison of ESBL production and the presence of blaCTX-M-15 and blaCTX-M-14 resistance genes between the ST131 and non-ST131 isolates revealed a statistically significant association between ST131 and both ESBL production and blaCTX-M-15 carriage (P < 0.001). Discussion

This cross-sectional investigation provides a comprehensive characterization of UPEC isolates recovered from hospitalized patients in northern Iran, offering critical insights into their epidemiology, antimicrobial resistance profiles, and virulence determinants.27

The management of UTIs caused by E. coli has been increasingly challenged by the emergence of antimicrobial resistance, particularly in recent years. In the present study, UPEC isolates presented high resistance rates to nalidixic acid, ampicillin-sulbactam, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, whereas nitrofurantoin and meropenem remained the most effective agents. Overall, 75.4% were classified as MDR, reflecting the growing therapeutic challenge posed by these pathogens. These findings are consistent with previous studies in Iran. Hemmati et al28 reported elevated resistance to ampicillin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, ciprofloxacin, cefazolin, and ceftriaxone, with 75% and 5% of the isolates classified as MDR and XDR, respectively. Similarly, Memar et al found high resistance to piperacillin, aztreonam, ciprofloxacin, ampicillin-sulbactam, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and nalidixic acid, with MDR rates of 62–77%; piperacillin/tazobactam (12%) and meropenem (9%) remained effective, and ESBL production was common.29

In contrast, MDR prevalence in European countries is considerably lower, ranging from 1.7% in Latvia to 26.9% in Russia (Ny et al), reflecting geographic variation.30 These differences result from widespread antibiotic misuse in Iran, whereas developed countries have strict stewardship policies. Overall, these findings emphasize the need for continuous surveillance to guide empiric therapy, inform infection control, and limit the spread of MDR strains.31

Given the high prevalence of MDR isolates, we next examined the major blaCTX-M ESBLs. The global emergence of blaCTX-M ESBLs is closely linked to the widespread use of β-lactam antibiotics in both human and veterinary medicine, making them the most prevalent plasmid-mediated ESBL enzymes worldwide.32 In line with previous studies, our findings demonstrate that blaCTX-M-15 was considerably more common than blaCTX-M-14 among UPEC isolates. Specifically, blaCTX-M-15 was detected in 52.5% of the isolates, whereas blaCTX-M-14 was detected in 15.6%.33–35 These results are consistent with Abbasi et al, who reported a strikingly high prevalence of blaCTX-M-15 (91.6%) among urinary E. coli isolates in Iran,36 as well as studies from Tehran and Kerman37,38 documenting similarly elevated frequencies. Halaji et al reported that among 35 ESBL-producing E. coli isolates, 82.9% harbored blaCTX-M alleles, with blaCTX-M-15 representing the overwhelming majority (27/29) alongside sporadic detection of blaCTX-M-55.25 Collectively, these data suggest that blaCTX-M-15 has become the dominant ESBL allele across Iran, reinforcing the notion that CTX-M-type ESBLs are endemic in the region.

Comparable trends have been observed in other countries. Similarly, in a multicenter survey spanning six Latin American countries, Caballero et al identified blaCTX-M-15 as the most common β-lactamase, followed by blaCTX-M-27, highlighting its global predominance as a major driver of ESBL-mediated resistance.39 Overall, the predominance of blaCTX-M-15 in our study, in agreement with national and international reports, emphasizes the need for continuous molecular surveillance to guide antimicrobial stewardship and infection control strategies.40

In the present study, PCR-based analysis of major E. coli STs revealed ST131 was the predominant clonal lineage, accounting for 63.1% of the isolates, followed by ST69 (8.4%), ST95 (7.3%), ST73 (3.9%), and ST127 (1.1%). Less frequent STs included ST744, ST421, and ST1193. The predominance of ST131 in our cohort is consistent with the findings of numerous regional and international reports, underscoring its critical role in UTIs.41,42

In Iran, several investigations have reported similar trends. For example, Hojabri et al43 reported that 29.8% of urinary E. coli isolates in Semnan belong to ST131, whereas Hemmati et al28 reported that ST131 (28%) is the leading clone among bloodstream infections in Tehran. Halaji et al identified ST131 as the most prevalent lineage among 18 unique STs recovered from UPEC isolates in Isfahan.25 Similarly, Neamati et al reported that more than half (54.5%) of UPEC isolates in Tehran were ST131.44 In line with these studies, Heidari et al detected ST131, ST95, ST73, and ST1193 in colorectal biopsy isolates, further confirming the broad dissemination of these lineages across different clinical niches in Iran.45

In addition to Iran, variations in dominant STs have been reported across different regions. In Pakistan, ST405 was the most prevalent clone among UPEC isolates, while studies from South Korea demonstrated that most major STs identified in our study were also present in their bloodstream and urinary isolates, except for ST1193 and ST421.46,47

A major finding of this study was the identification of ST421, a member of CC95 that is classified as virotype B, as an MDR sequence type with considerable pathogenic potential. To our knowledge, this represents the first report of ST421 among UPEC isolates in Iran. Although ST421 has previously been described in E. coli isolates associated with neonatal meningitis in France, its occurrence within UPEC populations has rarely been documented.48,49 The detection of this sequence type in our collection broadens the epidemiological landscape of UPEC in Iran and suggests its potential emergence as a clonal lineage of clinical relevance within extraintestinal pathogenic E. coli. The presence of an ESBL-producing ST421 in urinary isolates underscores the evolving population dynamics of UPEC and raises concerns regarding its contribution to the regional burden of antimicrobial resistance. These observations highlight the importance of continuous molecular surveillance and robust infection control strategies to mitigate the dissemination of such high-risk clones.

Importantly, the emergence of ST1193 as a novel MDR lineage has been documented in Europe. Flament-Simon et al reported that ST1193 is an emerging global clonal group in France and Spain, reinforcing its clinical significance.50 Over the past decade, ST1193 has increasingly been recognized as a rapidly expanding fluoroquinolone-resistant pandemic clone, with reports of its dissemination across diverse geographic regions. Importantly, ST1193 originates from the ST14 clonal complex and has recently emerged as a notable cause of extraintestinal infections in humans.25 Consistent with our findings, similar observations have been reported in distinct patient populations, including kidney transplant recipients in Iran, where UPEC isolates harboring this lineage were identified. Furthermore, globally disseminated clones such as ST73, ST141, ST131, ST69, and ST1193 are now recognized as being collectively responsible for nearly half of all ExPEC infections worldwide, particularly UTIs, bloodstream infections, and healthcare-associated infections.25 Kim et al, in their analysis of 122 E. coli isolates from blood and urine in South Korea, also reported the presence of ST744 alongside other globally relevant clones, highlighting its sporadic yet noteworthy distribution.46 Taken together, our findings reinforce the global epidemiological significance of ST131 while also underscoring the emergence of additional MDR lineages such as ST1193 within the UPEC population.

With the rising global burden of MDR E. coli, especially ST131, continuous molecular surveillance is essential to guide infection control, empirical therapy, and public health strategies against UPEC infections.

To further assess the clinical relevance of the predominant lineages, we compared their antimicrobial resistance patterns and ESBL production. We observed that the highest RS was associated with the ST1193 isolates, a finding that may pose significant challenges for therapeutic management. Furthermore, a substantial proportion of the ST131 (61.9%) and ST95 (61.5%) isolates produced ESBL. These frequencies are markedly higher than those reported in other regions, including Brazil (21%)51 and France (17.5%),52 suggesting a regional burden of ESBL-producing lineages in Iran.

In particular, all the isolates belonging to the less frequently detected sequence types ST1193, ST421, ST744, and ST127 were ESBL producers, highlighting their potential role as emerging contributors to the dissemination of antimicrobial resistance. The elevated prevalence of ESBL-positive strains among both dominant and minor STs in our study may reflect a growing trend of resistance in UPEC populations within this geographical setting. The detection of ESBL production in globally emergent lineages such as ST1193, alongside previously under-reported clones, including ST421 and ST744, underscores the dynamic and evolving nature of UPEC epidemiology. Comparative statistical analysis of antibiotic resistance profiles across STs further demonstrated significant heterogeneity, suggesting that specific clonal lineages may serve as reservoirs for resistance determinants.

One of the key findings of this study was the higher prevalence of the blaCTX-M-15 gene among the ST131 isolates, confirming that this clone is the major reservoir of ESBL determinants within the UPEC population. This observation is consistent with global reports identifying ST131 as the dominant lineage driving the persistence and dissemination of blaCTX-M genes, highlighting its critical role in shaping the clinical burden of resistance.

This study provides a detailed characterization of VFs and virotypes among UPEC isolates. The toxin-associated gene sat (44.7%), capsule biosynthesis gene kpsM (40%), and siderophore receptor gene iroN were the most prevalent, whereas ibeA and afa were rare. The high prevalence of sat and kpsM aligns with their established roles in cytotoxicity and protection against host defenses.37,53,54 The prevalence of IroN, which is critical for iron acquisition, was comparable to that reported in previous studies in China but higher than that reported in prior studies in Iran, reflecting regional variation.29,55, Adhesin genes were detected in only 21% of the isolates, which is lower than reports from Russia, Egypt, and Iran, highlighting geographic and source-dependent differences in UPEC virulence.56–58

Virotype analysis revealed that virotype B was generally predominant, whereas ST127 exclusively carried virotype A. Among the ST131 and non-ST131 isolates, virotype B was predominant (17.7% and 21.2%, respectively), and the ST1193, ST744, and ST421 isolates harbored only virotypes B and C. Notably, virotype E was significantly enriched in the ST131 isolates compared with the non-ST131 isolates (p = 0.01). These patterns are consistent with global trends, in which subtypes B and C dominate ST131 and contribute to increased virulence, whereas regional, host, and isolate-specific factors influence virotype distribution.2,59

Collectively, these findings underscore the heterogeneity of UPEC virulence profiles across STs and geographic regions. The observed distribution of VFs and virotypes highlights the importance of molecular surveillance for understanding pathogenic potential, guiding clinical management, and informing infection control strategies. In contrast, Mamani et al reported a temporal shift in ESBL-producing E. coli from bloodstream infections, where virotypes E and F emerged and replaced virotype A over 12 years.60 Similarly, Alonso et al reported that virotype E was the most prevalent type among ST131 isolates from spinach.61 Flament et al identified virotype D as predominant in porcine isolates.50 Such variations in virotype prevalence may reflect host genetic factors, infection site, geographic origin, isolation source, and sample size differences.

It should be noted that virotyping was possible for only approximately 50% of isolates due to the limited panel of virulence genes included in the study. This restricted set of targets may have underestimated the true diversity of virotypes among the UPEC population. Future studies should incorporate a broader range of virulence determinants to achieve more comprehensive virotyping. Additionally, because all isolates were collected from a single region in northern Iran, the generalizability of our findings to other areas of the country may be limited.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive characterization of UPEC isolates from hospitalized patients in northern Iran, revealing a high prevalence of ST131 as the dominant clonal lineage. To our knowledge, this represents the first report of ST421 among UPEC isolates in Iran, while confirming the widespread distribution of blaCTX-M-15 among ESBL-producing isolates. The virulence gene profile revealed heterogeneous distributions, with sat, kpsM, and iroN as the most frequent genes and virotype B as the leading pattern, while virotype E was significantly enriched among the ST131 isolates. These findings improve our understanding of the molecular epidemiology of UPEC and emphasize the urgent need for continuous molecular surveillance and targeted infection control strategies to limit the spread of MDR UPEC and to refine empirical therapy in hospital settings. Furthermore, adopting a One Health approach that integrates human, animal, and environmental surveillance may contribute to more effective monitoring and prevention of antimicrobial resistance at both regional and global levels.
