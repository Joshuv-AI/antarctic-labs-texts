Management of Adverse Events Associated with MET TKIs in Patients with Advanced NSCLC: A Podcast Discussion
Authors: Wade T Iams, Narjust Florez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMET exon 14 (METex14) skipping is observed in approximately 3–4% of patients with non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC). These patients are typically older and may or may not have a history of tobacco use. Several small molecule MET tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have been developed, showing clinical efficacy in trials targeting the MET pathway in these patients. Among these MET TKIs, tepotinib and capmatinib are globally approved and have demonstrated robust and durable efficacy in patients with METex14 skipping NSCLC in the VISION and GEOMETRY mono-1 trials. While MET TKIs have provided an important therapeutic option for patients with METex14 skipping NSCLC, they are associated with some challenging treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs). Across studies investigating MET TKIs, peripheral edema (49–68%) was reported as the most frequently observed TRAE and is widely recognized as a class effect of MET TKIs in patients with METex14 skipping NSCLC. Nausea (23–44%) and blood creatinine increase (22–34%) were other commonly reported TRAEs observed with treatments in this class. Effective treatment with MET TKIs relies on early recognition of AEs, regular monitoring, patient and caregiver education, and tailored supportive care interventions to ensure treatment continuity and optimal outcomes. Dose reductions have proven effective in mitigating AEs while preserving efficacy, by allowing patients to continue to receive efficacious targeted therapy. In this podcast article, the authors will discuss safety considerations with MET TKI treatment for patients with METex14 skipping NSCLC. They will also share their perspectives on the current landscape of MET TKIs in METex14 skipping NSCLC, commonly reported TRAEs in clinical practice, management strategies for these TRAEs, and safety considerations when switching one MET TKI to another. In addition, authors will discuss two patient cases to illustrate effective management of TRAEs associated with MET TKIs in patients with METex14 skipping NSCLC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11523-026-01242-z.

Key Points

Targeted treatment with MET TKIs such as tepotinib and capmatinib has shown benefit in patients with METex14 skipping NSCLCAdverse events are common with these treatments, with swelling/edema being the most frequent, along with nausea and changes in creatinine levels; these adverse events are usually manageable with close monitoring and supportive careOptimal management in these patients’ cases depends not only on choosing the appropriate targeted therapy but also on identifying side effects early, educating patients and caregivers, and adjusting the dose when needed so treatment can continue without compromising benefit.

So, switching may be a better option, and patients will always prefer to be on oral therapy [more] than on chemo-immunotherapy, which gets them closer or tied up to their geographic location. And a lot of these patients want to have a life and travel. I encourage my patients to travel, so oral treatments allow that.

There is a case report describing a patient receiving a standard dose of tepotinib following advanced capmatinib-induced pneumonitis without recurrence of pneumonitis, but it’s one case report [46]. And with pneumonitis, I like to be very careful. Similarly, there are some cases of tepotinib treatment after capmatinib-induced ILD was reported in one patient [47]. These outcomes—I would take them with a grain of salt because they’re limited to case reports, but it’s a possibility. In a few cases, I have switched patients. So, I did switch a patient from capmatinib to tepotinib, in part because his diabetes was very challenging. He was gaining a lot of weight, a lot of edema. It was becoming very challenging. So, I was able to switch the patient from capmatinib to tepotinib, and the patient was able to ride the tepotinib for 8 more months. So that’s 8 more months not getting infusions, which is very invaluable. However, I did have some issues trying to get it approved—the reality of oncology with these very expensive TKIs—but eventually with good discussion and peer-to-peer, I was able to get it approved.

Another thing that has allowed me to do more than switching is holding, [which] allows the patients to improve a little bit; especially if you have control of disease, just to take a treatment holiday has been successful. Have you ever been able to swap a patient from one TKI to another?

Wade Iams: Yeah, I have switched. I’ve had resolution of toxicity with some other TKI classes. I have tried switching between capmatinib and tepotinib; one particular case with severe edema comes to mind where they had tripling [of] the size of their lower extremities on capmatinib—maybe pushed too hard there. [We] switched over to tepotinib. And then within a few months, they ran into similar issues with tepotinib as well. So, I think it’s worth a try to switch TKIs. And I think of that broadly for all of our targeted therapies. In the case of MET TKIs, it may or may not be successful. Just from the anecdote, I wouldn’t generalize. I would still try switching, see if you can have resolution of the toxicity, and keep our patients on these drugs for longer.

So, we’re now going to discuss two anonymized cases, just illustrating in specific terms management of the treatment-related adverse events that we’ve been discussing so far. So, I’ll jump right in with the first case.

Let’s talk about a 65-year-old woman who worked in a plastics factory, actually right at retirement at 65 [years]. She has Stage 4 MET exon 14 skipping non-small-cell lung cancer, adenocarcinoma histology. She started on tepotinib [in the] upfront setting. We know the upfront data with response; durability of response is best upfront for both tepotinib and capmatinib. But let’s say 3 weeks after initiating, she develops trace to 1+ bilateral lower extremity edema. She’s having a harder time putting on her shoes. Labs showed no kidney or liver dysfunction, and her albumin is already mildly reduced. And so, we advise her to use compression stockings and elevate her legs. As you and I have said from the beginning, I think we should be proactive and advise that right from the start. And maximal—while being safe, I always add—but maximal physical activity is also the best thing we know.

But for the sake of this case, let’s say we’re a little bit reactive. We waited that few weeks. She’s already developing some edema. But she comes in 1 week later, [and] says her lower extremity edema is worsening. She’s re-evaluated. Let’s say she does say that she is using compression stockings for most of the day. And she’s still trying to walk as much as possible, although it’s still difficult to put on her shoes. She’s now having mild pain. You can have stress on the nerves in the lower extremities. Edema is variably painful but can be painful for some patients. And you can develop that neuropathy as well from just loss of the interstitial functioning as normal. And then that can result in loss of balance. So, she’s starting to lose her balance. Edema is now classified as 1+ to 2+. It is bilateral and symmetric. Nonetheless, we have a patient with advanced cancer. We should always rule out thrombosis. So, lower extremity ultrasound shows no [deep vein thrombosis] DVT. And let’s say, at that point, it’s clearly worsening. She’s compliant with her conservative measures, exercise, compression, [and] elevation. So, we hold the drug for 2 weeks and the lower extremity edema improves—not right away, certainly not within a day or 2, but within a couple of weeks, it improves. Her balance comes back to normal. Her foot and leg pain is better. Her shoes are fitting like normal again. And let’s say we reduce her dose down to 250 mg daily, and she’s advised to continuing doing that compression stockings and leg elevation and exercise. That’s key. And then fortunately, after 8 weeks, we’re starting to see an excellent response on treatment.

So, with close monitoring and guidance like this, this is a good example where, if we have a patient who’s having worsening edema and we saw it was impairing balance, risk for falls, obviously uncomfortable for the patient, [and] causing pain despite those conservative measures, you may need to just interrupt the dose and reduce the dose, and patients can still be on the drug for a long period of time with good clinical benefit and quality of life.

Narjust Florez: I think this is a very good example of how many things can be done to allow these patients to remain in effective therapy, in my opinion.

Alright, so let’s talk about our next case, which is a 69-year-old man, a former nurse, with Stage 4 MET exon 14 skipping mutation non-small-cell lung cancer, adenocarcinoma. Four weeks after initiating tepotinib, the patient develops persistent nausea, reduced appetite, and weight loss of 3 kg. He describes feeling full after a few bites and notes a metallic taste that makes food unappealing. No vomiting is reported. Lab tests show no hepatic or renal abnormalities, [and] no evidence of disease progression. And he also notes mild diarrhea, 3 to 4 stools per day, particularly in the morning, and intermediate loss of appetite.

He has been using [an] antidiarrheal, like loperamide, with some benefit. Treatment with tepotinib is briefly interrupted for 7 days, followed by a dose reduction to 250 mg daily [225 mg active moiety] [and] continued use of loperamide as needed for diarrhea. Close follow-up every 2 weeks is arranged for weight, symptom diary, and quality of life assessment. I think close follow-up is very important, and [with] these patients, having this every 2 weeks, we are sure that the patient does not continue to lose weight and also to troubleshoot with the patient. And the patient always knows he has a follow-up in 2 weeks. So, that also allows with some of the mental health issues that come with side effects. The patient meets with nutritionists for small, frequent, high-protein meals and hydration guidance. Everything is written down for the patient for nutrition, and he follows the diet that he eats. That is also key. Don’t bring the patient a diet that doesn’t work. Antiemetic therapy, ondansetron, is used as needed before meals because that’s when the nausea is more prominent for this patient. He’s encouraged to increase his physical activity and do some short post-meal walks to stimulate the appetite. And they can change the taste issues by using more cold foods, citrus food, and some flavor enhancers. The gastrointestinal effects in this patient include nausea, anorexia, [and] changes in taste. They’re common for MET TKIs and can affect adherence and nutrition. Nutrition or eating is one of the pleasures of our life. So, early intervention is key. Finding many things—antiemetics, nutritional support, and individualized dose adjustments—often allow[s] for these patients to manage these side effects while maintaining quality of life.

Wade, what do you think about this patient, and what would you do in your practice?

Wade Iams: Yeah, I think the keys, I think, that you’ve articulated very well are close follow-up and then patient diaries. I love the use of patient diaries, and I need to have my patients do it more. It helps patients to give insight and really empower them to take action around their diet and those aspects that may contribute to GI toxicity and managing loss of taste. As you’ve noted, it’s a very disruptive side effect and certainly would impair my quality of life severely if I had a loss of taste. So, I think those are really good gems that you’ve described in helping manage GI toxicity that can occur with MET TKIs. And I’m definitely going to take some to my practice as well.

Narjust Florez: Well, we’re coming to the end of this wonderful conversation. I think you and I can talk for days about how to manage these patients. But let’s just summarize for our audience. We want to thank everybody for listening. Targeted treatment with MET TKIs such as tepotinib and capmatinib has shown benefits in patients with MET exon 14 skipping mutations.

Wade Iams: Absolutely. Thank you all for listening.

And we also know that adverse events are common—peripheral edema far and away the most important—and be very proactive in managing that, followed closely by GI toxicities like nausea, and then other class effects like elevated creatinine. But overall, these toxicities with MET TKIs are certainly manageable with close monitoring and supportive care.

Narjust Florez: Like we discussed, the optimal management of these patients depends not only on choosing the appropriate targeted agent but also identifying side effects early, providing a lot of patient education early and continuously, not only once, to the patient and their caregivers, and adjusting the dose when needed, and also maximizing supportive care for these patients.
