ST6GAL1-mediated sialylation inhibits the antitumor immune response in colorectal cancer
Authors: Yuanchao Shi, Zhihong Peng, Zhenzhong Pan, Jingwei Duan, Zexing Wang, Quanlin Guan, Yiliang Fang, Bo Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeInterferon gamma (IFNG) directly affects the antitumor immune response. ST6-β-galactoside α-2,6-sialyltransferase 1 (ST6GAL1) is also positively correlated with poor prognosis for colorectal cancer (CRC). we performed some works to exploreexplored the underlying mechanism to further understand immune escape in colorectal cancer (CRC).MethodsFirst, we used clinical samples to confirm the relationship between ST6GAL1 and CRC. Afterward, we constructed overexpression/knockdown cell lines and performed bulk RNA sequencing, kinase phosphorylation chip, mass spectrometry, and in vitro and in vivo assays to explore the mechanism regulated by ST6GAL1. Finally, we verified the mechanism by performing immunoprecipitation and immunofluorescence (IF) staining.ResultsST6-β-galactoside α-2,6-sialyltransferase 1 (ST6GAL1) expression was negatively correlated with the response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in patients with CRC and negatively correlated with the sensitivity to IFNG in CRC cell lines. We confirmed that ST6GAL1 overexpression inhibited the infiltration of effector T cells, the levels of IFNG produced by CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells decreased, and the levels of granzyme B produced by effector cells decreased in animal models. The results of in vitro assays and phosphorylation chip detection revealed that ST6GAL1 inhibited IFNG receptor 1 (IFNGR1) phosphorylation, thus decreasing the activation of the JAK1/STAT1 signaling pathway. Through immunoprecipitation assays and mass spectrometry, we found that ST6GAL1 can sialylate BICD cargo adaptor 2 (BICD2), thereby affecting the interaction between BICD2 and IFNGR1 to ultimately inhibit the phosphorylation of IFNGR1 and promote immune escape.ConclusionsOur results confirmed that ST6GAL1 decreases the sensitivity of tumor cells to IFNG. This study describes a novel mechanism by which ST6GAL1 promotes the immune escape and malignant progression of CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13402-025-01118-w.Keywords: Colorectal cancer (CRC); Immune evasion; Interferon gamma (IFNG); ST6 beta-galactoside alpha-2,6-sialyltransferase 1 (ST6GAL1); BICD cargo adaptor 2 (BICD2)

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third-most-prevalent malignant tumor worldwide [1]. Although several clinical trials have confirmed the great benefit of neoadjuvant therapy in CRC patients [2, 3], some patients fail to achieve tumor shrinkage or stage reduction after neoadjuvant therapy; namely, they are not sensitive to neoadjuvant therapy. Importantly, the response rate of CRC patients, especially those with advanced-stage disease, to immunotherapy is very low in clinical practice, and immune escape and immune tolerance are widespread [4]. These findings prompt the question of whether insensitivity to neoadjuvant therapy is due to the presence of a cold tumor with immune escape.

Interferon gamma (IFNG) is a key antitumor effector molecule produced primarily by cytotoxic T cells and natural killer (NK) cells [5]. In the tumor microenvironment, IFNG recruits and activates the downstream Janus kinase (JAK) 1 and JAK2 proteins by binding to the subunits of the receptors IFNG receptor (IFNGR) 1 and IFNGR2 and subsequently activating signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) [6]. Activated STAT1 is transported to the nucleus, where it subsequently regulates the expression of IFN-stimulating genes (ISGs), thus playing a role in antitumor immunity [7]. The expression of various ISGs, such as guanylate binding protein 1 (GBP1) [8], which can suppress the proliferation of CRC cells, and the interferon regulatory factor (IRF) family, which can recruit immune cells to tumor sites [9], is associated with a less malignant phenotype. Interestingly, early research on human CRC cell lines revealed the direct antiproliferative activity of IFNG in five of seven cell lines [10]. Some cell lines respond well to IFNG treatment, whereas others do not; most importantly, the level of sensitivity is related to the posttranslational modification (PTM) of the protein [11]. However, reports on the specific regulatory mechanisms involved are lacking.

ST6 β‑galactoside α‑2,6‑sialyltransferase 1 (ST6GAL1) is an enzyme that modifies various proteins in vivo by adding sialic acid to terminal N‑glycans, thereby altering the structure and function of the proteins and affecting the biological behavior of tumor cells [12]. Previous studies have confirmed that ST6GAL1 is involved in the progression of malignant tumors. Our previous research and omics data have also revealed the tumor-promoting effect of ST6GAL1 on CRC [13]. More importantly, in our previous studies, we confirmed the regulatory effect of ST6GAL1 on tumors by constructing cell lines with ST6GAL1 overexpression and knockdown [13]. Therefore, in this study, based on previous research and bioinformatics data, we further clarified the regulatory mechanism by which ST6GAL1 affects immune escape in colorectal cancer. Many previous studies have confirmed that sialylation is the most important function of ST6GAL1, and this modification is widely present in tumors and affects their biological behavior [12, 14, 15]. Additionally, based on the results of staining for sialylated proteins in clinical samples in our previously published study, we showed that ST6GAL1 is sialylated and significantly highly expressed in tumor tissues in CRC; moreover, through specific staining for sialic acid in the ST6GAL1-overexpressing (SW48) and knockdown (Caco2) cell lines, we fully confirmed that ST6GAL1 can directly promote sialylation in tumor cells [13]. Most importantly, some studies have shown that ST6GAL1 can affect the function of T cells [16], B cells [17], NK cells [18] and other immune cells, but these studies focused on the mechanism by which ST6GAL1 regulates immune cells to alter the antitumor immune response and did not explore the regulatory mechanism of the sensitivity of ST6GAL1 to IFNG as a direct antitumor effector molecule in depth. CRC has substantial internal differences in sensitivity to IFNG [19]. We explored the relationship between ST6GAL1-mediated sialylation and IFNG sensitivity in detail by performing in vivo and in vitro experiments to investigate its mechanism of action in CRC and further understand the factors that influence IFNG sensitivity in the antitumor immune response in CRC.

Bicaudal D (BICD), which includes BICD1 and BICD2, is a family of motor adaptor proteins [20]. Phosphorylation of the BICD2 N-terminus is involved in the regulation of the cell cycle, suggesting that the PTM of the BICD2 protein is essential for regulating the biological function of cells [21]. Some studies have reported the phosphorylation [22], ubiquitination [22] and methylation [23] of BICD2. For example, the tumor suppressor von Hippel‒Lindau (VHL) interacts with the cargo adaptor BICD2 and promotes the K48-linked polyubiquitination of ITSD, consequently resulting in the proteasomal degradation of BICD2 [22]. Moreover, by altering STAT1 nuclear translocation, BICD2 regulates IFNγ signaling transduction and enhanced the IFNγ-mediated suppression of the cystine/glutamate antiporter system Xc-, ultimately affecting cellular sensitivity to ferroptosis [22]. Moreover, a BRAF inhibitor impairs the interaction of VHL with BICD2 by inducing BICD2 phosphorylation, ultimately causing severe ferroptosis [22]. Additionally, a mass spectrometry analysis revealed that BICD2 is potentially phosphorylated at multiple serine/threonine sites and is involved in the regulation of the cell cycle; moreover, BICD2 has been reported to be phosphorylated on S615 in response to vemurafenib [24], and vemurafenib was first developed against lung cancers with the mutated form of the serine/threonine kinase BRAFV600E [25]. With respect to other modifications, including methylation, one investigation revealed that PCIF1 regulates BICD2 protein levels through its m6Am catalytic activity, which in turn reduces the stability and translation efficiency of the BICD2 mRNA [23]. Therefore, the PTM of BICD2 is involved in the occurrence and progression of tumor biological processes, but the specific mechanism still requires further verification and exploration. Additionally, one study revealed that BICD2 influences IFNG signal transduction and ultimately increases cellular sensitivity to ferroptosis by promoting STAT1 nuclear translocation [22]. If BICD2 affects the STAT1 pathway, does it affect the IFNG response further upstream? Is this effect related to the presence of other posttranslational modifications? Because very few studies have investigated the specific mechanisms involved, we attempted to clarify these interactions and explore the corresponding mechanisms more accurately at the molecular and omics levels.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human Caco-2, SW480, RKO, HCT-116, SW1116, HT29, SW48 and mouse MC38 cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Rockville, MD, USA). All the cells used in this research were identified via short tandem repeat (STR). The cells were monitored for mycoplasma contamination using the MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza, Rockland, MA, USA). The cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 6123081) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Invitrogen, 2535493P) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 2441886).

Lentiviral transduction of cell lines

SW48 and HT29 cells were transduced with lentiviruses expressing an EV (empty vector) or the human ST6GAL1 gene (NM_003032.3). MC38 cells were transduced with lentiviruses expressing an EV or the mouse ST6GAL1 gene (NM_001252506). Caco2 cells were transduced with lentiviruses expressing a control sequence (hshC) or shRNA targeting ST6GAL1 (sequences: F, CCCAGAAGAGATTCAGCCAAA and R, CCCAGAAGAGATTCAGCCAAA). Stable shRNA-expressing polyclonal populations and monoclonal cell lines were selected via flow cytometry. The expression of ST6GAL1 was confirmed by reverse transcription‒quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‒qPCR) and Western blotting (WB).

Clinical sample collection

A total of 14 patients participated in this study. Patients were pathologically diagnosed with colorectal cancer at The First Affiliated Hospital of the Third Military Medical University of the PLA. All patients signed informed consent forms to participate in the study and for the publication of their data. This study was approved by the Research and Ethical Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of the Third Military Medical University of the PLA and complied with all relevant ethical regulations. The detailed clinical characteristics of the patients are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Animal model

Eight mice (6–8-week-old female C57BL/6 immunocompetent mice) were randomly allocated to two groups. ST6GAL1-EV (MC38, n = 4) and ST6GAL1-OE (MC38, n = 4) were subcutaneously injected into mice. The diameters of the tumors were monitored daily. Ethical approval for the animal experiments was granted by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the Army Medical University.

Mouse tissue preparation and disposal

On day 21 after tumor cell implantation, tumor tissues and the ipsilateral draining lymph nodes were collected. The tumor tissues were quickly minced and digested with digestive enzymes (1.5 mg/ml collagenase IV (Roche, 11088866001), 1 mg/ml DNase I (Roche, 10104159001) and 0.2 mg/ml dispase II (Roche, 4942078001)) in basic DMEM and passed through a 70-µm cell strainer. The cells were resuspended in PBS supplemented with 1% FBS and 0.1% EDTA (Invitrogen, AM9260G). The lymph nodes were ground and then suspended in medium.

Isolation of mouse peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs)

Mouse PBMCs were isolated by Ficoll-Paque PLUS (GE Healthcare) density gradient centrifugation, washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended at 106 cells/mL in RPMI 1640 medium.

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

The samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h. Dehydration and paraffin embedding were performed using routine methods. Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were stained with antibodies against ST6GAL1 (AF5924; R&D Systems) and visualized using the Dako REAL™ EnVision™ detection system (Dako, K5007). The cell nuclei were restained with hematoxylin (Beyotime, C0105S) and differentiated with an acid‒alcohol fast differentiation solution (Beyotime, C0163S). The sections were directly stained with hematoxylin, hydrochloric alcohol, and eosin.

Western blotting

The cell pellet was lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Beyotime, P0013C) supplemented with PMSF (Beyotime, ST507) and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, P1081). The total protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Beyotime, P0012S). The following antibodies were used to detect different proteins: anti-ST6GAL1 (RD, AF5924, 1:200), anti-IFNGR1 (RD, MAB6731-SP, 1:1000), anti-p-IFNGR1 (Tyr457, Thermo Fisher, PA5-105503, 1:1000), anti-STAT1 (CST, 9172 S, 1:1000), anti-p-STAT1 (Tyr701, CST, 9167 S, 1:1000), anti-IRF-1 (CST, 8478 S, 1:1000), anti-GBP1 (Affinity, DF3598, 1:1000), anti-GAPDH (CST, 2118 S, 1:2000), and anti-BICD2 (Abcam, ab237616, 1:1000). A donkey anti-goat IgG antibody coupled to horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Beyotime, A0181, 1:2000), a goat anti-rabbit-HRP antibody (Beyotime, A0208, 1:2000), and a goat anti-mouse-HRP antibody (Beyotime, A0216, 1:2000) were used as secondary antibodies. The blots were developed with BeyoECL (Beyotime, P0018S). High expression of ST6GAL1 directly leads to increased sialic acid levels in cells. Previous studies have also confirmed a positive correlation between ST6GAL1 levels and sialacidification [14]. Sambucus nigra lectin (SNA) is a plant lectin that specifically recognizes the sialic acid structure. SNA mainly recognizes α-2,6-linked sialic acid, which is also a characteristic sialic acid modification mediated by ST6GAL1 [26]. Therefore, through SNA staining, we determined that ST6GAL1 mediated sialic acid modifications. For SNA staining, the membranes were incubated with antibodies against SNA (1:400; VectorLabs; B-1305-2) diluted in SABC (A: B = 1:1; Beyotime, 7074). The signal intensity was determined using Fiji software and normalized as indicated.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100. The fixed cells were blocked with 5% BSA and then incubated with primary antibodies (SNA-FITC, 1:200; ADI, SNA15-FITC; anti-BICD2, 1:400; Abcam, ab237616) for 12 h at 4 °C. Secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1,000, Abcam, ab150079)) were applied and incubated for 40 min at RT. Finally, the sections were sealed with antifade mounting medium supplemented with DAPI (Beyotime, P0131).

Immunoprecipitation (IP) and immunoblot analysis

Cell lysates were incubated with a primary antibody and Protein A/G agarose beads (Thermo Fisher, 88802). The resulting immunocomplexes were subsequently washed three times with PAST (PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20), subjected to polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis according to the manufacturer’s protocol, transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (PVDF, T830.1, 0.45 mm, Roth) and detected as described previously. Anti-BICD2 (Abcam, ab237616, 1:1000) was used as the primary antibody, and a goat anti-rabbit-HRP antibody (Beyotime, A0208, 1:2000) was used as the secondary antibody. For SNA staining, the membranes were incubated with antibodies against SNA (1:400; VectorLabs; B-1305-2) diluted in SABC (A: B = 1:1; Beyotime, 7074) for 30 min. The blots were developed with BeyoECL (Beyotime, P0018S). The signal intensity was quantified using Fiji software and normalized as indicated.

RNA isolation and real-time quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT‒qPCR)

RNA was extracted from cells using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme, RC112-01) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration of RNA was determined with a Nanodrop 2000c (PeqLab). The RNA was subsequently reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, RR047A). RT‒qPCR was performed using a QuantiTect SYBR Green PCR Kit (Bio-Rad, 1725121) and primers designed by Beijing Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd. The 2−ΔΔCt method was used to quantify gene expression levels. The relative expression levels of genes were obtained through sequential normalization of the values to those of GAPDH and the experimental controls.

Flow cytometry

The cells were incubated with specific antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C to analyze cell surface marker expression. A flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) was used to acquire the cells. The FACS data were analyzed using FlowJo v10 software. The following antibodies were used: Zombie NIR (BioLegend, 423105, 1:400), anti-CD45 BV510 (BioLegend, 368525, 1:200), anti-NK1.1 FITC (eBioscience, 11–5941–82, 1:200), anti-CD3 PE (BioLegend, 980008, 1:200), anti-CD8 BUV737 (Bioscience, 741850, 1:200), anti-CD4 AF700 (BioLegend, 344621, 1:250), anti-CD25 PerCP-Cy5.5 (BioLegend, 302625, 1:200), anti-Foxp3 APC (eBioscience, 17–578573–82, 1:200), anti-granzyme B (GZMB) PE-Cy7 (BioLegend, 372213, 1:200), anti-CD8 BV421 (BioLegend, 344748, 1:200), and anti-IFNG APC (BioLegend, 505859, 1:200).

Kinase phosphorylation array

Approximately 4 million SW48 cells transfected with ST6GAL1-overexpressing (OE) or ST6GAL1-EV strains were separated using flow cytometry, and site-specific tyrosine phosphorylation in these cells was measured using a Tyrosine Phosphorylation ProArray (Full Moon BioSystems, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) from the Antibody Array Assay Kit (Full Moon BioSystems). In total, 50 µg of cell lysate in 60 µl of labeling buffer was incubated with 3 ml of biotin/DMF at room temperature for 2 h with shaking; 30 ml of stop reagent was then added, and the mixture was incubated for an additional 30 min at room temperature. The antibody microarray was blocked with blocking solution at room temperature for 45 min, rinsed 10 times with Milli-Q water, and incubated with the biotin-labeled cell lysates in protein coupling mix for 2 h at room temperature with slow rotation. It was then rinsed three times (10 min each) with wash solution and 10 times with Milli-Q water. Finally, the microarray was incubated with Cy3-streptavidin (0.5 mg/mL) for 20 min at room temperature in the dark, followed by rinses with wash solution and Milli-Q water. After the surface was dried, the array was scanned on a GenePix 4000B (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). Relative protein expression is presented as the fluorescence of the OE group divided by the fluorescence of the EV group (OE/EV). Three independent experiments were conducted, and the average value obtained for each group was calculated. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) dataset was used to calculate the significance of the enrichment of differentially expressed proteins in each pathway [27, 28].

Bulk RNA isolation, sequencing and analysis

The cells were removed from the incubator and washed twice with ice-cold PBS. Total RNA was extracted from the cells as described above. Libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Poly (A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module Kit (NEB) and the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina Paired-End Multiplexed Sequencing Library (NEB). The samples were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 sequencer as 150-bp paired-end reads. FastQC version v0.11.5 was used to check the quality of the sequencing reads, and low-quality fragments (Phred score < 30) and adapters were removed using Trim-Galore (version 0.4.5). The remaining qualified reads were aligned to the GRCm38 genome assembly using STAR (version 2.7.10a). RSEM was used as the quantitative tool to generate a count table. The differential expression analysis was performed using the R 4.2 Bioconductor package DESeq2 (version 1.36.0). Principal component analysis was performed by constructing a data matrix normalized by the rlog function in DESeq2. Normalization was performed using the default parameters of DESeq2. The DESeq2 results were transformed using the lfcShrink function. Volcano plots were generated using EnhancedVolcano (version 1.8.0). Heatmaps were generated with pheatmap (version 1.0.12) and RColorBrewer (version 1.1-2) using the normalized count data generated with DESeq2. For the generation of bigWig files, bam files were indexed using SAMtools (version 1.12) with the default parameters. The bigWig files were created using the deepTools (version 3.5.0) function bamCoverage with the default bin size and the forward RNA strand. For pathway analyses, genes with P-adjusted < 0.05 and log2-fold change (FC) > 1 or < -1 were selected. The upregulated and downregulated genes were separately input into Enrichr (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/).

Analysis of publicly available CRC scRNA-seq datasets

The single-cell data used in this study were from the study by Markus Morkel et al. The datasets are available in GEO with accession number GSE166555 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE166555). A total of 366,650 cells from tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues from CRC patients were obtained. We utilized Seurat (v4.4.0) [29] for data normalization (NormalizeData, LogNormalize method, scaling factor 10,000), scaling of data features (ScaleData), and detection of variable genes (FindVariableGenes with vst method). Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on these variable genes with RunPCA. The statistically significant principal components (PCs) were used to generate a two-dimensional UMAP. Cell clustering was conducted using the original Louvain algorithm (FindClusters). The expression of the ST6GAL1 gene in each of the cell subsets is visualized in UMAP plots. Cell types were determined using published sets of characteristic genes from the study by Markus Morkel et al. GSEA was performed using the gseGO function from the clusterProfiler package (v4.4.4) [30].

Statistical analysis

The Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test was performed to determine whether the continuous variables were normally distributed (P > 0.10 was considered to indicate a normal distribution). Student’s t test was used to compare continuous variables with an abnormal distribution, and the Mann‒Whitney U test was used to compare variables with an abnormal distribution. The χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables. A P value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All the statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

High expression of ST6GAL1 negatively correlates with sensitivity to neoadjuvant therapy in CRC

We investigated the relationship between ST6GAL1 expression and CRC by obtaining 14 samples of surgically resected tumor tissues and paired adjacent normal tissues from patients with CRC to ST6GAL1 expression in these samples. First, we found that the level of the ST6GAL1 mRNA in tumor tissues was higher than that in adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1A); the corresponding statistical results are shown in Fig. 1B (tumor vs. normal tissues: P = 0.002). The protein expression level also showed a consistent difference (Fig. 1C); the corresponding statistical results are shown in Fig. 1D (tumor vs. normal tissues: P < 0.001). In addition, IHC revealed that ST6GAL1 was more highly expressed in tumor tissues than in adjacent normal tissues from CRC patients (Fig. 1E), and the corresponding statistical results are shown in Fig. 1F (tumor vs. normal tissues: P < 0.001). Interestingly, the analysis of the clinical information of the 14 included patients revealed that patients with high ST6GAL1 expression were not sensitive to neoadjuvant therapy, whereas patients with low ST6GAL1 expression had significantly increased sensitivity to neoadjuvant therapy (tumor vs. normal tissues: P < 0.001; Fig. 1G). Similarly, high ST6GAL1 expression was detected in tumor samples from patients who were insensitive to neoadjuvant therapy using IHC, and a significantly greater proportion of patients who were sensitive to neoadjuvant therapy presented low ST6GAL1 expression (Fig. 1H); the corresponding statistical results are shown in Fig. 1I (tumor vs. normal tissues: P = 0.001). The patients’ clinical information is shown in Supplementary Table 1. These results showed that ST6GAL1 expression correlated negatively with the sensitivity to neoadjuvant therapy in CRC patients.

ST6GAL1 expression is negatively correlated with sensitivity to the IFNG response in CRC

We used single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) results derived from published data to investigate ST6GAL1 expression in different cell clusters in CRC patients. Although ST6GAL1 was expressed in tumor cells, stem cells, enterocytes, endothelial cells and goblet cells present in the tumor microenvironment, it was expressed mainly in tumor cells and stem cells in CRC patients (Fig. 2A, B and Fig. S1A, B). The marker genes of the corresponding subclusters were shown in the heatmap in Fig. S1 C. We subsequently conducted gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in tumor cells and stem cells with high and low ST6GAL1 expression and found that cells with high ST6GAL1 expression exhibited greater downregulation of immune response-related signaling pathways, such as antigen processing and presentation of endogenous antigens, positive regulation of the antigen receptor-mediated signaling pathway, positive regulation of lymphocyte-mediated immunity, and positive regulation of interleukin-2 production and the T-cell response-associated signaling pathway (Fig. 2C). These results further show that ST6GAL1 expression is closely related to the immune response.

IFNG is among the most important cytokines involved in the tumor immune response. CRC cell lines were reported to have different sensitivities to IFNG. Britzen-Laurent et al. reported that Caco2, HCT116 and RKO cells did not respond to IFNG treatment, whereas SW48 and HT29 cells respond to IFNG [11]. Therefore, we first measured ST6GAL1 expression in different CRC cell lines to explore whether it is related to the IFNG response. We indeed found that ST6GAL1 was differentially expressed in these cell lines (Fig. 2C, D, E and Fig. S1D). Moreover, we treated SW48, HT29, HCT116, RKO and Caco2 cells with IFNG (100 ng/ml) for 24 h and measured the expression levels of key molecules in the signaling pathway downstream of IFNG, including caspase-1, GBP1, IRF1, indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) and STAT1 (the sequences of the primers are shown in Supplementary Table 2). SW48 cells responded well to IFNG, followed by HT29 cells, whereas Caco2 cells hardly responded to IFNG (Fig. 2F). These findings are consistent with the sensitivity of CRC cell lines to IFNG reported in previous studies. Importantly, SW48 and HT29 cells with relatively high ST6GAL1 expression presented resistance to IFNG, whereas cell lines with relatively low ST6GAL1 expression, such as HCT116, RKO and Caco2 cells, presented better responses to IFNG.

ST6GAL1 expression decreases the sensitivity to the IFNG response

We constructed ST6GAL1-OE SW48 and HT29 cells and ST6GAL1-knockdown (KD) Caco2 and HT29 cells to further determine the correlation between ST6GAL1 and the IFNG response. We verified the overexpression and knockdown efficiency via RT‒qPCR and WB (Fig. S2A and B) and isolated stable ST6GAL1-OE and ST6GAL1-KD cell lines. The expression levels of key molecules in the signaling pathway downstream of the IFNG response were measured after these cell lines were treated with IFNG for 12, 24 and 48 h in vitro. SW48 and HT29 cells presented significantly decreased IFNG sensitivity after ST6GAL1 overexpression (Fig. 3A and B). Conversely, the IFNG sensitivity of Caco2 and HT29 cells increased after ST6GAL1 knockdown (Fig. 3C and D).

We also examined the changes in the GBP1 and IRF1 protein levels after ST6GAL1 overexpression and knockdown in cells treated with 100 ng/ml IFNG for 12 and 24 h. We found that the levels of GBP1 and IRF1, which are molecules associated with the IFNG response, decreased after ST6GAL1 overexpression in the SW48 (Fig. 3E and F) and HT29 (Fig. 3I and J) cell lines; conversely, the levels of these proteins increased in Caco2 (Fig. 3G and H) and HT29 (Fig. 3I and J) cells after ST6GAL1 knockdown. The results obtained with both the original and ST6GAL1-OE/KD cell lines confirmed that ST6GAL1 regulates the sensitivity of CRC cells to IFNG.

ST6GAL1 overexpression in CRC tumor cells promotes immune escape

Our results suggest that ST6GAL1 may play an important role in tumor immunity. We genetically overexpressed ST6GAL1 in MC38 colon cancer cells to explore its effects on the immune system. We then performed RT‒qPCR to verify its overexpression efficiency (Fig. S3A). We inoculated ST6GAL1-OE cells into wild-type syngeneic (immunocompetent) C57/BL6 mice and plotted tumor growth curves for mice bearing ST6GAL1-OE (n = 4) and scramble MC38 tumor cells (n = 4). We found that ST6GAL1-OE resulted in rapid tumor growth (Fig. S3B). After 20 days, when the tumors had increased to 1200 mm3 in size, we sampled the tumor tissue, abdomen-draining lymph nodes and peripheral blood and prepared suspensions of single cells from these tissues. We found that in mice bearing ST6GAL1-OE tumors, the total numbers of cells in tumor tissues (ST6AGL1-EV vs. ST6GAL1-OE: P = 0.019), abdomen-draining lymph nodes (ST6AGL1-EV vs. ST6GAL1-OE: P = 0.035) and peripheral blood (ST6AGL1-EV vs. ST6GAL1-OE: P < 0.001) significantly increased (Fig. 4A).

We explored the causality of the association between ST6GAL1 and T-cell counts using flow cytometry to detect changes in the numbers of cells from different T-cell subsets, including CD3+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, Treg cells, and GZMK, and the production of cytokines by CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells after the overexpression of ST6AGL1. The detailed results for representative T-cell subpopulations and effector molecules are shown in Fig. S3C and D. In tumor tissues from the ST6GAL1-OE group, the numbers of CD3+ (ST6AGL1-EV vs. ST6GAL1-OE: P = 0.005) and CD8+ T (ST6AGL1-EV vs. ST6GAL1-OE: P = 0.001) cells were significantly decreased, whereas the numbers of Treg (ST6AGL1-EV vs. ST6GAL1-OE: P = 0.04) cells was significantly increased, and the number of CD4+ T cells did not change significantly (ST6AGL1-EV vs. ST6GAL1-OE: P = 0.279) (Fig. 4B, C, D and E). However, the numbers of T-cell subsets in the lymph nodes did not change significantly (Fig. S3E and G), and the numbers of T-cell subsets in the peripheral blood showed different trends (Fig. S3F and G). This difference may be due to the completely different immune microenvironments of peripheral blood and tumor tissue. Importantly, we found that the production of GZMB by CD8+ T cells (ST6AGL1-EV vs. ST6GAL1-OE: P < 0.001; Fig. 4F and G) and IFNG by CD4+ T (ST6AGL1-EV vs. ST6GAL1-OE: P = 0.002; Fig. 4H and I) and CD8+ T (ST6AGL1-EV vs. ST6GAL1-OE: P = 0.001; Fig. 4J and K) cells was significantly reduced after ST6GAL1 overexpression in tumor tissues. However, we did not observe consistent differences in the levels of effector molecules between the ST6GAL1-EV and ST6GAL1-OE groups in the peripheral blood or draining lymph nodes (Fig. S4A, B, C and D). These data further indicate that the differential expression of ST6GAL1 leads to significant changes and affects the immune response in the tumor microenvironment.

ST6GAL1 expression decreases IFNG signaling pathway activity

Next, we investigated how changes in ST6GAL1 affect the IFNG response. First, we performed bulk RNA sequencing of SW48-EV and SW48-OE cells after they were treated with 100 ng/µl IFNG for 24 h (original data in Supplementary excel 1). The analysis of the data revealed that many relevant molecules in the IFNG signaling pathway, including STAT1, JAK2, IRF1, IRF9, IDO1 and GBP1, were significantly downregulated in the SW48-OE group compared with the SW48-EV group (Fig. 5A and B). We performed GSEA on all DEGs in SW48-OE cells compared with SW48-EV cells to further clarify the functional changes in DEGs after ST6GAL1 overexpression and found that many immune response-associated pathways, including those related to T-cell-mediated immunity, the defense response to bacteria, cytokine-mediated signaling pathways and the regulation of the adaptive immune response, were downregulated in cells with ST6GAL1 overexpression (Fig. 5C). Most importantly, the interferon signaling pathway and the interferon production signaling pathway were also downregulated in the SW48-OE group (Fig. 5C). These bulk RNA sequencing data further indicate that ST6GAL1 regulates the IFNG response in CRC cells.

Previous studies have confirmed that ST6GAL1 is an N-glycosylating enzyme [31]; thus, it usually affects the activity of signaling pathways through the phosphorylation of downstream molecules that normally exercise further biological functions [32]. Given that lentiviral transfection was used to construct overexpression and knockdown cell lines and that the lentivirus itself is key for the activation of the IFNG signaling pathway, we performed a kinase phosphorylation array in SW48-EV and SW48-OE cell lines to explore how the phosphorylation of these molecules is directly affected by ST6GAL1 (original data in Supplementary excel 2). Data analysis revealed that the phosphorylation of IFNGR1 at Tyr457 was significantly inhibited after ST6GAL1 overexpression (Fig. 5D). Next, we explored the KEGG signaling pathways enriched in proteins whose phosphorylation was inhibited after ST6GAL1 overexpression and found that signaling pathways closely related to the antitumor immune response, including the B-cell receptor signaling pathway, natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, the JAK/STAT signaling pathway and Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis, were significantly upregulated (Fig. 5E). These data clearly demonstrate that ST6GAL1 overexpression inhibits IFNGR1 phosphorylation and significantly inhibits the antitumor immune response of CRC cells.

ST6GAL1 regulates the IFNGR1/JAK1/STAT1 signaling pathway by altering IFNGR1 and BICD2 binding

We treated SW48-OE, HT29-OE, HT29-KD, Caco2-KD and corresponding control empty virus-expressing cell lines with 100 ng/µl IFNG for 15–30 min and then measured changes in the phosphorylation of key molecules in the IFNG signaling pathway to further clarify the mechanism by which ST6GAL1 regulates IFNG signaling. Interestingly, in SW48 cells, the phosphorylation of IFNGR1 at Tyr457 was inhibited after ST6GAL1 overexpression and IFNG treatment (Fig. 6A and B), and the phosphorylation of STAT1 at Tyr750 was also significantly inhibited after ST6AGL1 overexpression and IFNG treatment (Fig. 6A and B); these results were consistent with the results obtained using the kinase phosphorylation array. Similarly, the IFNGR1/JAK1/STAT1 signaling pathway was upregulated after ST6GAL1 knockdown in Caco2 (Fig. 6C and D) and HT29 (Fig. 6E and F) cells, and the IFNGR1/JAK1/STAT1 signaling pathway was downregulated after ST6GAL1 overexpression in HT29 cells (Fig. 6E and F). These data further confirm that ST6GAL1 expression inhibits activation of the IFNG signaling pathway, thus inhibiting the IFNG-induced death of tumor cells.

We subsequently wished to determine how ST6GAL1 overexpression inhibits IFNGR1 phosphorylation and whether this occurs because overexpressed ST6GAL1 affects the binding of IFNGR1 to key transcription factors. Therefore, we conducted immunoprecipitation (IP) experiments using SW48-EV and SW48-OE cells, performed mass spectrometry on proteins precipitated with an antibody against IFNGR1, and screened sialylated substrate molecules after ST6GAL1 overexpression (original data shown in Supplementary excel 3). The results revealed significant sialylation of BICD2 after ST6GAL1 overexpression. We conducted an anti-BICD2 IP experiment using HT29 cells after ST6GAL1 overexpression to verify our findings and observed that BICD2 was sialylated (Fig. 6G). Most importantly, the sialylation of BICD2 blocked its binding to IFNGR1 (Fig. 6G). Finally, we measured the sialylation of BICD2 in Caco2-EV and Caco2-KD cells by IFC and found that sialylated BICD2 was not detectable after ST6GAL1 knockdown (Fig. 6H). Thus, through mass spectrometry and in vitro experiments, we showed that the sialylation of BICD2 inhibits its binding to IFNGR1, further inhibiting the phosphorylation of IFNGR1 at Tyr457 and the activation of the downstream signaling pathway after ST6GAL1 overexpression (Fig. 7). This process ultimately suppresses the antitumor immune response of CRC cells and leads to immune escape and the malignant progression of CRC.

Discussion

The immune system is activated when tumor-associated or tumor-specific antigens are recognized and captured by antigen-presenting cells of the immune system; when this process occurs, immune cells release effector molecules that kill tumor cells through a series of cascade reactions [33]. However, strong immune escape occurs in the tumor microenvironment, and immune cells are unable to kill tumor cells; thus, the treatment of cancer patients is often very difficult in clinical practice. IFN signaling is required to trigger antitumorigenic immunity [34]. IFNG, the most important cytokine that leads to tumor cell death, is also involved in tumor cell resistance in CRC [11]. Most importantly, the clinical benefits of IFNG treatment or immunotherapies such as immune checkpoint inhibitors for CRC patients remain limited, suggesting that primary immune escape is a common event [35]. Immune escape is the greatest problem facing tumor therapy, and the resistance of tumor cells to IFNG in CRC further accelerates immune escape. In this work, we used transcriptome sequencing, kinase chip detection and scRNA-seq to explore new mechanisms affecting the immune escape of tumor cells.

Increasing evidence suggests a correlation between ST6GAL1 expression and immune cells [36, 37]. As a sialyltransferase, ST6GAL1 performs a variety of functions that relate to the different modified molecules; thus, its function is very powerful and complex [14]. ST6GAL1 overexpression in malignant cells has been reported to promote tumor progression and lead to poor disease outcomes [38]. One study proposed that effector T cells initiate the sialylation of IgG via an interferon (IFN)-γ–ST6GAL1-dependent pathway; meanwhile, upon interacting with sialylated IgG, the cGAS–STING pathway is inactivated and subsequent type-I-IFN-triggered antitumorigenic immunity is eliminated [17]. Another study revealed that ST6GAL1 overexpression promoted hepatocarcinoma cell proliferation, migration, and immune escape by increasing the levels of CD147, MMP9, MMP2, and MMP7. Some studies in which CD8+ T cells were cocultured with cell lines expressing different levels of ST6Gal1 revealed that ST6GAL1 upregulation inhibited T-cell proliferation and increased the secretion of IL-10 and TGF-β1, while the secretion of IFN-γ and TNF-α decreased [16]. In addition, a study in which the ST6GAL1 gene was knocked out in a cell line showed increased expression of important antigen-presenting and costimulatory surface receptors and specifically increased activation of allogenic human CD4+ T cells [39]. Therefore, the results of previous studies and our data show that ST6GAL1 not only affects the function of tumor cells themselves but also strongly affects the immune microenvironment of tumors [40]. Through in vitro experiments and scRNA-seq, we found that high ST6GAL1 expression led to a decrease in the number of effector cell subsets, an increase in the number of Treg subsets, and a decrease in the production of effector molecules such as IFNG and GZMB by effector cells. Through these changes, the powerful tumor-promoting and immunosuppressive functions of ST6GAL1 further promote the malignant progression of CRC.

In vitro experiments and bulk transcriptome result clearly demonstrated that ST6GAL1 inhibits the IFNG-mediated immune response, while the results of CRC scRNA sequencing based on the information available in public databases clearly showed that ST6GAL1 promoted immune escape in the TME. In contrast to the previously reported findings that ST6GAL1 promotes the proliferation, invasion and metastasis of tumor cells, we found that ST6GAL1 directly regulates the activity of the IFNG signaling pathway. Our kinase phosphorylation chip data indicated that ST6GAL1 overexpression inhibited the phosphorylation of IFNGR1 at Tyr457. Most importantly, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the Jak/STAT signaling pathway was significantly downregulated after ST6GAL1 overexpression. B-cell receptor signaling, natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, and Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis were also downregulated after ST6GAL1 overexpression. Moreover, the expression of IFNG response-related genes such as STAT1, IRF1, GBP1, JAK2, IDO1 and ISGs was downregulated in SW48-OE cells after treatment with IFNG [41, 42]. GSEA of bulk RNA-seq data also revealed that interferon production, T-cell-mediated immunity and regulation of the immune response signaling pathway were downregulated. Therefore, we believe that ST6GAL1 regulates the activity of the IFNG signaling pathway and subsequently affects the immune escape of tumor cells.

Through IP and mass spectrometry analyses of IFNGR1, we determined that BICD2 plays a key role in this mechanism. Studies have shown that phosphorylated CTD-interacting factor 1 regulates BICD2 protein levels through its m6Am catalytic activity and thereby reduces the stability of BICD2 [23]. These findings suggest that BICD2, a protein substrate, can be modified to affect its function. Here, we explored a previously unreported PTM of BICD2, the addition of sialic acid, and showed that this modification inhibits its binding to IFNGR1 and thereby decreases the activity of the IFNG signaling pathway. The inhibition of IFNG signaling further promotes the immune escape of tumor cells, preventing tumor cells from being killed by the immune system [43, 44]. We found that ST6GAL1 can induce sialylation at amino acid 691 of BICD2 and that when sialylation of this site cannot occur because of a mutation, IFNGR1 phosphorylation is activated. In summary, based on the results of previous studies and our experimental and omics data, the PTM of BICD2 can significantly affect the activity of signaling pathways and the stability of proteins, ultimately altering the biological characteristics of cells. Therefore, we believe that BICD2 is a new sialylation substrate. When tumor cells overexpress ST6GAL1, it can effectively target BICD2 and transfer sialic acid groups to BICD2. BICD2 proteins carrying sialic acid groups in the tumor microenvironment cannot bind to IFNGR1 and activate the IFNGR1 signaling pathway outside cells because of changes in their molecular structure and chemical properties. Our experiments revealed that this regulatory mechanism is attributed mainly to the inhibition of IFNGR1 phosphorylation. We hypothesize that as a cargo adapter, sialylated BICD2 interferes with the stability and activation of the phosphorylated protein, ultimately leading to the transportation and recruitment of IFNGR1. However, more experiments are needed to verify the effects of changes in the chemical structure on the molecular functions.

A previous study showed that ST6GAL1 upregulation inhibited T-cell proliferation and increased the secretion of IL-10 and TGF-β1, whereas the secretion of IFN-γ and TNF-α decreased. In their tumor transplant model, ST6GAL1 upregulation resulted in the formation of significantly larger tumors and suppressed intratumoral CD8+ T-cell infiltration. In our animal model of colorectal cancer, we also discovered a similar pattern [16]. We wondered whether sialylation increases the sialylation of the tumor microenvironment in which ST6GAL1 is highly expressed, which hinders the aggregation of T cells around tumor cells, whether tumor cells with stronger sialylation are more likely to escape T-cell recognition, or whether sialylation promotes the loss and downregulation of surface antigens on tumor cells. In the human body, the intestinal tract is a slightly acidic environment. This environment maintains the mucosal barrier in a balanced state. High expression of ST6GAL1 increases the level of sialic acid, disrupts the balance of the intestinal microenvironment, and may affect the state of immune cells in the microenvironment [28]. However, this mechanism is only a conjecture. How ST6GAL1-mediated sialylation regulates the infiltration of immune cells in the microenvironment remains a difficult problem. We believe that the solution to this issue will provide a new entry point for targeting immune escape in CRC.

Taken together, the results of our study provide evidence for a novel pathway of CRC immune escape that involves the inhibition of ST6GAL1 expression and activation of the IFNG signaling pathway. Knowledge of this novel mechanism can be used to effectively solve the problem of drug resistance in CRC patients who are resistant to some degree to IFNG and immunotherapy, identify a new target and suggest a new direction for the future treatment of CRC [45, 46].
