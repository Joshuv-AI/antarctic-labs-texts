Temporal trends in outcomes for infants with neonatal opioid withdrawal managed with the Finnegan scoring tool: Insights from the INFORM NOW Study
Authors: Lori A Devlin, Walter K Kraft, Denise C Babineau, Evan Rhodes, Abhik Das, Margaret Crawford, Stephanie Merhar, Sucheta Telang, Lawrence Leeman, Gregory M Sokol, Namasivayam Ambalavanan, Sara B DeMauro, Richard W Hall, Scott A Lorch, for the HEAL Evaluation of Limited Pharmacotherapies for Neonatal Opioid Withdrawal Syndrome (HELP for NOWS) Consortium
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOBJECTIVE:To evaluate changes in hospital outcomes for infants with neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome (NOWS) assessed by the Finnegan Tool before (10/1/2019 to 12/31/2019;pre-epoch) and after (7/1/2021 to 3/31/2022;post-epoch) publication of the 2020 AAP NOWS clinical report.STUDY DESIGN:This subgroup analysis included infants with NOWS assessed with the Finnegan Tool and enrolled in the INFORM NOW study (pre- and post-epoch 183 and 267 infants respectively).RESULTS:No differences were found between epochs in initiation or length of pharmacologic treatment(LOT), or length of stay(LOS). Outcomes were modified by opioid type: LOT was 20% shorter (aMR=0.80; 95%CI:[0.69,0.93]) and LOS was 16% shorter (aMR=0.84; 95%CI:[0.73,0.96]) for buprenorphine treated infants post- compared to pre-epoch, while LOT and LOS were longer in the post-epoch for those treated with methadone.CONCLUSION:There was no difference in outcomes before and after publication of the AAP report. However, the type of postnatal opioid did modify study results.

INTRODUCTION

Neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome (NOWS), an anticipated consequence of antenatal opioid exposure, remains a significant public health concern and continues to impact tens of thousands of infants across the United States (US) annually [1]. Infants with NOWS often have prolonged initial hospital stays to monitor for and treat the signs of opioid withdrawal after birth. Treatment of NOWS is composed of nonpharmacologic interventions (i.e., holding, swaddling, and skin-to-skin care with parents/caregivers in a low stimulation environment and on demand breastfeeding as appropriate) with the addition of pharmacologic agents if nonpharmacologic measures alone do not control withdrawal severity.

In November of 2020, the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) published an updated clinical report on NOWS [2]. This report synthesized current evidence across the field and provided clinical recommendations for the management of infants with antenatal opioid exposure, including guidance on best practices for the assessment and treatment of infants with NOWS. Recommendations in this report supported the use of (1) a standardized assessment approach with a tool, such as the Finnegan Neonatal Abstinence Scoring Tool, which was designed to measure the presence and severity of opioid withdrawal and to assess the response to treatment; and (2) a protocolized treatment approach founded on non-pharmacologic care with the use of opioid medications to treat severe signs of withdrawal that were not responsive to non-pharmacologic care alone. The 2020 AAP report did not strongly endorse longer acting opioids (i.e., methadone and buprenorphine) due in part to the high alcohol content in commonly used formulations.

To better understand if clinical practices before and after the 2020 AAP report were associated with differences in hospital-based outcomes, we utilized data from infants included in a multicenter retrospective study Informing NOWS Research through Retrospective Observational Data Collection (INFORM NOW) which was developed through the HEAL Evaluation of Limited Pharmacotherapies for Neonatal Opioid Withdrawal Syndrome (HELP for NOWS) Consortium. As part of this study, we aimed to evaluate differences in hospital outcomes for infants who were assessed with the Finnegan Tool before and after publication of the 2020 AAP report. We hypothesized that clinical treatment after the publication of the updated AAP guidelines would be associated with a decreased length of pharmacologic treatment (LOT) and length of stay (LOS) for infants assessed and managed for NOWS with the Finnegan Tool.

MATERIALS/SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Data sources

In this subgroup analysis of infants with NOWS, all data were abstracted from medical records of opioid-exposed infants who were at least 36 weeks’ gestation at the time of birth and their mothers. All infants were cared for at one of 13 hospitals affiliated with HELP for NOWS Consortium sites (supplemental Table 1). Infants were divided into a pre-epoch (October 1, 2019 to December 31, 2019) and a post-epoch (July 1, 2021 to March 31, 2022) group. The time period from January 1, 2020 to June 30, 2021 was excluded to minimize the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on hospital outcomes. Pharmacologic and nonpharmacologic treatment regimens were determined by local practice. The primary opioid used for pharmacologic treatment at each study hospital was consistent throughout the study. The proportion of hospitals using each primary opioid is detailed in Supplemental Table 2. Some sites transitioned from Finnegan to ESC during the time between the pre- and post-epochs, leading to a difference in the number of sites included in each epochs. Secondary medications for all but one study hospital were consistent between epochs.

A quality assurance and control framework to improve data abstraction and accuracy was used throughout this study [3]. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines were followed [4].

Participants

Infants included in this study were at least 36 weeks’ gestation, exposed to opioids during the second and/or third trimester of pregnancy and assessed and managed for NOWS with the Finnegan Tool. Infants were excluded if they were discharged on opioid treatment for NOWS or co-enrolled in the Advancing Clinical Trials in Neonatal Opioid Withdrawal Weaning trial (NCT04214834) as the duration of pharmacologic treatment in this clinical trial was blinded [5].

Outcomes

Study outcomes included: (1) initiation of pharmacologic treatment (yes/no), defined by initiation of either morphine, methadone, or buprenorphine for the treatment of NOWS; (2) LOT, defined by the number of days between the first and last dose of pharmacologic treatment; and (3) LOS, defined by the number of days between birth and hospital discharge. We chose these outcomes as they are clinically meaningful hospital-based outcomes that are commonly reported across the literature [6–9].

Statistical analyses

Demographic characteristics and outcomes in each epoch were summarized using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and median (interquartile range) for continuous variables and compared using Pearson’s chi-squared tests for categorical variables and Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables.

To account for correlation amongst infants treated at the same site, a generalized estimating equations approach using an independent or compound symmetric correlation matrix was used to estimate the association between epoch and each outcome; the selected correlation matrix was based on model fit using the QICu criteria [10]. The model for initiation of pharmacologic treatment assumed a binomial distribution and logit link while the models for LOT and LOS assumed a negative binomial distribution and log link. Variables included as covariates in each model were selected prior to the analysis due to their known association with hospital-based outcomes for infants with NOWS. Aside from epoch, covariates included infant sex [11], maternal race and ethnicity [12], categorized site volume, social holds (i.e. prolonged hospitalization due to child protective system and/or foster care placement), other medical issues not related to NOWS that prolong the newborn hospitalization, highest level of care [13], gestational age of the infant at birth [14], medication for opioid use disorder (MOUD) use during pregnancy and type of MOUD used [15] and polysubstance exposure [16]. Polysubstance exposure was defined by positive maternal history and/or positive maternal and/or neonatal toxicology screen for opioids and at least one of the following substances: amphetamines, barbiturates, benzodiazepines, cocaine, gabapentin, marijuana (tetrahydrocannabinol), methamphetamines, phenylcyclohexyl piperidine (PCP), selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, or xylazine. In subgroup analyses of infants who were pharmacologically treated for NOWS, a two-way interaction between epoch and primary opioid type was included in the models of LOT and LOS to examine whether the association between epoch and each outcome varied by primary opioid type.

Based on the fitted models, unadjusted and adjusted model-based estimates of association (as well as corresponding 95% CIs and p-values) included: (1) the odds of initiation of pharmacologic treatment in each epoch and odds ratio of initiation of pharmacologic treatment comparing the post-epoch to the pre-epoch; and (2) the mean LOS in each epoch and the mean ratio of LOS comparing the post-epoch to the pre-epoch. In all infants with NOWS who received pharmacologic treatment, estimates of association (as well as corresponding 95% CIs and p-values) included: (1) the mean LOS in each epoch, the mean ratio of LOS comparing the post-epoch to the pre-epoch (overall and by primary opioid type), the mean ratio of LOS through pairwise comparisons of the three primary opioid types by epoch; and (2) the mean LOT in each epoch, the mean ratio of LOT comparing the post-epoch to the pre-epoch (overall and by primary opioid type), and the mean ratio of LOT through pairwise comparison of the three primary opioid types by epoch. Unadjusted and adjusted odds, probabilities, and means as well as corresponding 95% CIs in each group of interest were also estimated (as appropriate). Mean unadjusted and adjusted differences between groups (and corresponding 95% confidence intervals) were estimated using 5000 bootstrap samples that were sampled with replacement within sites.

All tests of association were performed using a two-sided significance level of 0.05. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was made for this descriptive study as supported by Altman [17]. Any missing data were not imputed. All analyses were completed using SAS version 9.4 software (The SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina).

In the group of infants who received pharmacologic treatment, the association between epoch and LOT was modified by the postnatal opioid used to treat NOWS (p-value for two-way statistical interaction (on the multiplicative scale) between epoch and opioid type <0.001; Table 3). While there was no difference in the adjusted mean LOT between epochs for infants treated with morphine (aMR: 1.04; 95%CI: [0.85,1.26]), the adjusted mean LOT was 20% shorter in the post-epoch than in the pre-epoch for infants treated with buprenorphine (aMR: 0.80; 95%CI: [0.69,0.93]) and 30% longer in the post-epoch than in the pre-epoch for infants treated with methadone (aMR: 1.30; 95%CI: [1.17,1.46]). In this same analysis, no difference was noted in the adjusted mean LOT for infants treated with methadone when compared to infants treated with buprenorphine in the pre-epoch (Supplemental Table 5; aMR: 1.10; 95%CI [0.68, 1.78]). However, the adjusted mean LOT was 80% longer in infants treated with methadone than in infants treated with buprenorphine in the post-epoch (aMR: 1.80; 95%CI [1.20, 2.70]). Additionally, there was no statistically significant difference in the adjusted mean LOT between infants treated with methadone and those treated with morphine in the pre-epoch (aMR: 1.00; 95%CI [0.68, 1.48]) or post-epoch (aMR: 1.26; 95%CI [0.98, 1.61]). Lastly, there was no difference in the adjusted mean LOT between infants treated with buprenorphine and infants treated with morphine in the pre-epoch or in the post-epoch (Supplemental Table 5).

Among pharmacologically treated infants, we also found that the association between epoch and LOS was modified by opioid type (p < 0.001; Table 3). While there was no difference in the adjusted mean LOS between epochs for infants treated with morphine (aMR: 1.08; 95%CI: [0.92, 1.27]), the adjusted mean LOS was 21% longer in the post-epoch than in the pre-epoch for infants treated with methadone (aMR 1.21; 95%CI: [1.11,1.32]) and 16% shorter in the post-epoch than in the pre-epoch in infants treated with buprenorphine (aMR: 0.84; 95%CI: [0.73,0.96]). In the same analysis, there was no difference in the adjusted mean LOS between infants treated with methadone and infants treated with buprenorphine in the pre-epoch (aMR: 1.08; 95%CI [0.72, 1.61]), although the adjusted mean LOS was 55% longer in infants treated with methadone than in infants treated with buprenorphine in the post-epoch (aMR: 1.55; 95%CI [1.15, 2.10]), see supplemental Table 6. Additionally, there was no difference in the adjusted mean LOS between infants treated with methadone versus those treated with morphine. Among infants treated with buprenorphine versus those treated with morphine, there was no difference in LOS in the pre-epoch, but the adjusted mean LOS was 34% shorter in the post-epoch (aMR: 0.66; 95%CI [0.44, 0.98]) (Supplemental Table 6).

To address that only one of the three buprenorphine sites contributed to both the pre- and post-epochs (Supplemental Table 2), a sensitivity analysis excluding the two sites that only contributed to the pre-epoch was completed; results were fundamentally unchanged (Supplemental Tables 7–13).

DISCUSSION

Publication of the 2020 AAP clinical report on NOWS provided an opportunity to evaluate outcomes before and after updates to the clinical guidelines. In this observational multicenter study of infants with NOWS who were managed with the Finnegan Tool, there were no differences in the initiation of pharmacologic treatment, LOT, or LOS between the pre- and post-epochs (Table 2). However, the type of opioid used for postnatal pharmacologic treatment did modify the associations between epoch and hospital-based outcomes (Table 3).

Infants treated with buprenorphine in the post-epoch had a shorter LOT and LOS when compared to the pre-epoch, while those treated with methadone had a longer LOT and LOS in the post-epoch. Although, as buprenorphine and methadone treated infants in the post-epoch were each enrolled by a single site (Supplemental Table 1), the generalizability of these outcomes are limited. Study sites that contributed to the pre- and post-epochs did not report changes to their preferred opioid or report substantial changes to their pharmacologic treatment protocols between epochs. Which suggests that the etiology of these findings may be multifactorial, nuanced, and difficult to account for in retrospective research. Enhancements in non-pharmacologic care, adherence to pharmacologic treatment algorithms, and variation in drug exposure during pregnancy have all been noted to modify outcomes for infants with NOWS and may also have modified the outcomes of this study. In addition, other site characteristics not captured in treatment algorithms may have also modified study outcomes.

The use of nonpharmacologic care for the treatment of NOWS, specifically rooming in and breastfeeding, has been associated with a reduction in LOT and LOS [9, 18–20]. Non-pharmacologic care is recommended as first-line treatment for NOWS in the updated 2020 AAP clinical guidelines [2], yet, data to specifically inform the implementation of non-pharmacologic interventions in clinical practice are lacking [21]. To date, there is no standardized definition for a “dose” of a non-pharmacologic intervention and quantifying the amount and duration of such care is challenging. As we found in this study, many electronic medical record (EMR) systems are still not designed to readily capture nonpharmacologic interventions and surrogate measures are being used to approximate desired outcomes (e.g., level of care and inborn verses outborn for rooming-in). Assessment tools, such as the Eat, Sleep and Console Care Tool, have integrate nonpharmacologic care into the bedside assessment which improves the ability to record and thus identify non-pharmacologic interventions in the EMR. However, the checklists included in many tools still lack the detail needed to truly quantify nonpharmacologic care so that its effects can be adequately measured.

Adherence to a standardized treatment algorithm has been associated with improved outcomes for infants with NOWS [22] and all study hospitals reported use of a protocolized approach to pharmacologic treatment throughout the study. Yet, adherence to such algorithms varies between medical providers and across patients and is often not captured in the EMR and if it is captured, it is difficult to abstract for research purposes. Therefore, confounding due to the degree of algorithm adherence is not accounted for in most retrospective studies of infants with NOWS, including this study.

In addition, quantifying total maternal substance use and timing of such use during pregnancy has always been problematic in this field of research as such details are often not readily recalled by parents and/or not reported to medical providers and recorded in the EMR. If recorded, the dose and timing of exposure, especially for non-prescribed substances, is not accurate or reliable. Variation in the unprescribed US drug supply was expected across the observational period for this study. During this period, fentanyl and fentanyl analogs inundated the unprescribed US drug supply and consequently, fentanyl use during pregnancy increased [23, 24]. In addition, xylazine, an alpha2-adrenergic agonist used in veterinary medicine as a sedative, analgesic, and muscle relaxant, has also infiltrated the unprescribed US drug supply and is most commonly mixed with opioids such as fentanyl and heroin [25]. Such changes in exposure during pregnancy may have also contributed to any differences found in study outcomes.

In the group of infants who received pharmacologic treatment, we also found that opioid type modified outcomes by epoch, with statistically significant differences noted in the post- but not the pre-epoch. The improvement in hospital-based outcomes associated with the use of buprenorphine in the post-epoch should be viewed with some caution, as the sample size was small, and all infants treated for NOWS with buprenorphine were enrolled at a single study site. However, these findings do support the need for continued research to determine the effectiveness of buprenorphine in the treatment of NOWS. Such research will benefit from the development of an alcohol free buprenorphine solution [26], which was developed in part due to concerns raised by the 2020 AAP clinical report.

Additional limitations of this study include the retrospective nature of medical record review, and unaccounted for site-to-site variation that is common in clinical practice and is difficult to fully account for in any analysis. To optimize data quality for this study, a data quality framework which has been previously shown to decrease data abstraction error was used throughout this study [3, 27]. We did not correct for multiple comparisons in the study analyses. This decision although supported by the literature, may have increased the chances for false positive results [17]. In addition, neonatal exposure was defined by toxicology screening and/or history of drug use during pregnancy, which both have limitations, including the inability to accurately identify and quantify all opioid and polysubstance exposures during pregnancy, although this limitation is common to NOWS studies.

Publication of the 2020 update to the AAP clinical report on NOWS provided a natural experiment to evaluate if hospital-based outcomes differed between infants assessed and managed for NOWS with the Finnegan Tool in a pre- versus post-epoch. In this study we found no difference in hospital-based outcomes before and after publication of the AAP clinical report. However, the use of buprenorphine for the pharmacologic treatment of NOWS during a time of high exposure to non-prescribed fentanyl may be associated with a shorter LOT and LOS. Further study is needed to clearly understand the implications of these findings.
