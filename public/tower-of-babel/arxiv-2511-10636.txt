Abstract
We study the spectral problems associated with the finite-difference operators H N = 2 ​ cosh ⁡ ( p ) + V N ​ ( x ) \mathrm{H}_{N}=2\cosh\!{\left(p\right)}+V_{N}{\left(x\right)} , where V N ​ ( x ) V_{N}{\left(x\right)} is an arbitrary polynomial potential of degree N N . These systems can be regarded as a solvable deformation of the standard Schrödinger operators p 2 + V N ​ ( x ) p^{2}+V_{N}{\left(x\right)} , and they arise naturally from the quantization of the Seiberg-Witten curve of four-dimensional, 𝒩 = 2 \mathcal{N}=2 , SU( N N ) supersymmetric Yang–Mills theory. Using the open topological string/spectral theory correspondence, we construct exact, analytic eigenfunctions of H N \mathrm{H}_{N} , valid for arbitrary polynomial potentials and describing both bound and resonant states. Our solutions are entire in x x for generic values of the energy, and become L 2 L^{2} -normalizable only at a discrete set of energies. An interesting feature of these Hamiltonians is the existence of special loci in the parameter space of the potential, the so-called Toda points. The eigenfunctions exhibit enhanced decay at these points, leading to spectral degeneracies for confining potentials and to a real energy spectrum for unbounded ones. Our results provide a rare example of a quantum-mechanical spectral problem that is exactly solvable, admitting explicit, analytic eigenfunctions for both bound and resonant states.

Introduction

Exactly solvable models have been playing a central role in quantum mechanics, providing profound insights into its mathematical structure and phenomenology. Over the past decade, advances in topological string theory and supersymmetric gauge theory have uncovered many new examples of solvable quantum spectral problems, often arising from the quantization of mirror curves; see
Marino:2015nla
;
Marino:2024tbx
for a review and a list of references. Regarding quantities that encode the energy spectrum (such as exact quantization conditions, Fredholm determinants and spectral traces) the situation is now well understood for operators associated with mirror curves of arbitrary genus. In contrast, much less is known about the corresponding eigenfunctions, particularly for mirror curves of genus greater than one, which are related to gauge theories with higher-rank gauge groups. In this work, we fill this gap for a specific class of geometries. More precisely, we analyse the spectral problem associated with the difference operator

\mathrm{H}_{N}=2\Lambda^{N}\cosh\!{\left({\rm p}\right)}+V_{N}{\left({\rm x}\right)}\,,\qquad\qquad\left[{\rm x},{\rm p}\right]={\rm i}\hbar\,,

(1.1)

where

is a generic polynomial potential of degree
N

{\rm p}^{2}+V_{N}{\left({\rm x}\right)}\,,\qquad\qquad\left[{\rm x},{\rm p}\right]={\rm i}\hbar\,.

(1.2)

As we will show, the deformation (
1.1
) has a remarkable property: it defines a spectral problem that is exactly solvable in terms of explicit analytic functions. This stands in sharp contrast to the quantum anharmonic oscillator (
1.2
), for which no explicit, closed-form analytic expressions for the eigenfunctions are known

N odd: unbounded potential.

When

Let us briefly recall the main idea.
For

Therefore, to improve convergence, we perform a small complex rotation of the position and momentum operators by an angle

\psi_{n}(x,{\boldsymbol{h}})=\psi^{(\theta)}_{n}({\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\theta}x,{\boldsymbol{h}})\,.

3
Comments on the Toda points

So far, we have discussed the spectral problem (
4.63
) from the standpoint of standard quantum mechanics, wherein one first constructs entire off-shell solutions and then imposes square-integrability as a boundary condition.

However, there is another way of to view (
2.6
), namely from the perspective of integrability. Indeed, the difference equation (
2.6
) is closely related to the Baxter equation for the quantum
\mathrm{SU}(N)
Toda lattice
PasquierGaudin1992
;
Gutzwiller1981
;
Gutzwiller1980
;
Sklyanin1985
, namely

({\rm i}\Lambda)^{N}Q(x+{\rm i}\hbar)+(-{\rm i}\Lambda)^{N}Q(x-{\rm i}\hbar)-V_{N}(x)Q(x)=EQ(x)\,.

(3.49)

In this setting, one looks for entire solutions to (
3.49
) such that:

Q(x)\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})\quad\text{with}\quad Q(x)\simeq{\rm e}^{-\tfrac{N\pi}{2\hbar}|x|},\qquad x\to\pm\infty\,.

(3.50)

By defining:

Q(x)=\left(\sum_{n_{i}\in S}d_{i}{\rm e}^{-\frac{2\pi xn_{i}}{\hbar}}\right){\rm e}^{-\frac{\pi(N+2)}{2\hbar}x}\psi(x)\,,\quad S\subset{\mathbb{Z}},\quad d_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}\,,

(3.51)

it is easy to show that
Q(x)
solves (
3.49
) for any choice of
S\subset{\mathbb{Z}}
. However, the mapping between the Baxter solution (
3.50
) and our solutions (
2.28
), (
2.34
) requires a particular choice of
S
. We will address this question in a separate work
wip
.

Nevertheless, even after taking (
3.51
) into account, one can see that for generic values of the potential parameters
h_{k}
(
k=1,\ldots,N-1
), the on-shell eigenfunctions (
2.28
) and (
2.34
) do not reproduce (
3.50
).
For odd
N
, this follows directly from the fact that the asymptotics of (
2.34
) are outgoing with power-law decay on one side and exponentially decaying on the other, with a rate which is independent on
N
.
Likewise, for
N
even, this follows from the fact that the decay behaviour of (
2.28
) as
x\to\pm\infty
is the same for both plus and minus infinity, and it is independent of
N
. This is in line with the works of
PasquierGaudin1992
;
Gutzwiller1981
;
Gutzwiller1980
;
Sklyanin1985
, where they found that eigenfunctions satisfying (
3.50
) exist only for special values of the moduli
h_{k}
,
k=2,\dots,N
. We refer to these distinguished values as
Toda points
and denote them by
h_{k}^{\rm T}
,
k=2,\dots,N
. This, in turn, means that for (
3.50
) to hold, one must impose simultaneously
N-1
quantization conditions, thereby quantizing all the
h_{k}
,
k=2,\dots,N
. This was nicely illustrated in
PasquierGaudin1992
. From the gauge theory perspective, it was found in
ns
;
Kozlowski:2010tv
;
Mironov:2009dv
that the quantization conditions determining the Toda points are (following the notation of
Grassi:2018bci
):

\frac{{\rm i}}{\hbar}\frac{\partial F_{\rm NS}}{\partial\boldsymbol{a}}={\rm i}2\pi\boldsymbol{\ell}+{\rm i}\pi\boldsymbol{\rho}\,,

(3.52)

where
\boldsymbol{\ell}
encodes the quantum numbers,

\boldsymbol{\ell}=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\ell_{k}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}\,,\qquad\qquad\ell_{k}\in\mathbb{N}\,,\qquad\qquad\boldsymbol{\rho}=\sum_{I=1}^{N}\left(N-I\right)\boldsymbol{e}_{I}\,,

(3.53)

and
\boldsymbol{\rho}
is a constant vector. It is important to note that (
3.52

N-1
quantization conditions for the
a_{I}
, the corresponding solutions being denoted by
\boldsymbol{a}{\left(\boldsymbol{\ell}\right)}

2.15

C.129

N-1

h_{k}
,
k=2,\dots,N

This stands in sharp contrast with standard quantum mechanics, where square-integrability imposes only a single quantization condition, namely (
2.32
) for
N
even and (
2.37
) for
N

2.32

2.37

5
Outlook

In this work, we investigated the open TS/ST correspondence for the family of
Y^{N,0}
toric Calabi–Yau geometries. Following the general framework of
Marino:2016rsq
;
Marino:2017gyg
;
Francois:2025wwd
, we constructed a background-independent quantity, given in equation (
4.76
), which plays the role of the non-perturbative open topological string partition function. This object is entire in both the open and closed string moduli and provides an exact analytic solution to the difference equation obtained by quantizing the mirror curve of the
Y^{N,0}
geometry. The resulting wavefunction becomes square-integrable only at discrete energy values, where it reproduces the physical eigenfunctions of the associated operator. We then analysed the four-dimensional limit of this construction. In this limit, the quantum mirror curve (
4.63
) reduces to the deformed quantum-mechanical Hamiltonian (
1.1
), which can be regarded as a deformation of the standard Schrödinger operator with an arbitrary polynomial potential. Through the open TS/ST correspondence, we derived explicit analytic expressions for the corresponding eigenfunctions, given in equations (
2.28
) and (
2.34
). These functions are entire in
x
for arbitrary values of the moduli
h_{k}
and become
L^{2}
-normalizable only for a discrete set of values of the energy, in perfect agreement with the quantization conditions previously derived in
Grassi:2018bci
.

Our analysis opens up several directions for future investigation, some of which we briefly outline below.

-

Within the open TS/ST correspondence, the structure of the non-perturbative open partition function still poses some interesting challenges. As shown in
Marino:2016rsq
;
Marino:2017gyg
;
Francois:2025wwd
and summarized in equation (
4.76
), this function can be expressed as a sum of two contributions, or saddles. Although in some cases we have a technical prescription to construct the second saddle from the first, its geometrical and physical meaning remains elusive and calls for a deeper understanding.

-

It would be interesting to investigate how our solutions (
4.76
), (
2.28
) and (
2.34
) emerge from a resurgent analysis of the open-string wavefunction; see for instance
Grassi:2022zuk
;
Alim:2022oll
;
Hao:2025azt
.

-

The deformed Hamiltonian (
1.1
) exhibits several novel spectral features absent in standard Schrödinger operators. Due to its difference-equation nature, the usual oscillation theorem
(
Berezin1991
, thm. 3.5)
does not apply. Moreover, the system can display ground-state degeneracies and, remarkably, bound states with real energies even in potentials unbounded from below. These phenomena reveal a rich and unconventional spectral structure whose physical interpretation deserves further study. A detailed analysis of these aspects will be presented in a forthcoming work
wip
;
wip2
.

-

It is important to undertake a mathematical study of the spectral properties of (
1.1
) for odd
N
. Indeed, when the potential is confining (
N
even), the spectral properties of the corresponding difference operators have been analysed rigorously in
Laptev:2015loa
, where it was shown that such operators are self-adjoint on a suitable domain and possess a purely discrete spectrum. See also
Ilyin2025
for extensions to certain complex potentials. It would be meaningful to extend this type of rigorous analysis to the case of potentials unbounded from below. See also
Caliceti
;
Caliceti1983
for some discussion on the case of the standard Schrödinger operator.

-

It was pointed out in
bgt
;
bgt2
that there exists another four-dimensional limit, the so-called dual 4d limit, which can be implemented on the mirror curve (
4.59
) and leads to a family of integral operators. The corresponding spectral problems make contact with the 4D gauge theory in the self-dual phase of the
\Omega
-background, rather than the NS-phase, as is the case for the spectral problems studied in this paper. For
N=2
, this limit on the eigenfunctions was analysed in
Francois:2023trm
;
Francois:2025wwd
, where a new functional relation between the modified Mathieu and the McCoy-Tracy-Wu operators was found. It would be interesting to extend this analysis to
N>2
in light of our new results on the eigenfunction.

-

Finally, it would be interesting to generalize our construction of the eigenfunctions of the deformed Hamiltonian (
1.1
) to other four-dimensional quantum mirror curves, including those associated with SW theories with matter or with different gauge groups. Such extensions could reveal new classes of solvable quantum systems and provide further insights into the open TS/ST correspondence.

We hope to report on some of these problems in the future.

C.2
The NS partition function in presence of a surface defect

In the presence of a type-II chiral defect, the partition function was computed in
Gaiotto:2014ina
;
Bullimore:2014awa
and is nicely summarized in
(
Sciarappa:2017hds
, app. A)
. By evaluating the residues in these expressions explicitly, the partition function can be written as

Z^{(c),\mathrm{inst}}_{2d/4d}(x,{\boldsymbol{a}},\Lambda,\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}\big((-1)^{N}\Lambda^{2N}\big)^{\ell({\boldsymbol{Y}})}\,\mathcal{Z}_{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}({\boldsymbol{a}},\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\,\mathcal{Z}_{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}^{(c)}(x,{\boldsymbol{a}},\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\,,

(C.119)

where the defect factor is

\mathcal{Z}_{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}^{(c)}(x,{\boldsymbol{a}},\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\prod_{I=1}^{N}\prod_{(i,j)\in Y_{I}}\frac{a_{I}-x+i\,\epsilon_{1}+j\,\epsilon_{2}}{a_{I}-x+i\,\epsilon_{1}+(j-1)\epsilon_{2}}.

(C.120)

Here
x
denotes the two-dimensional twisted mass parameter. We then define the full instanton defect partition function in the NS limit as

Z_{\rm D}^{\rm inst}(x,{\boldsymbol{a}},\Lambda,\hbar)=\lim_{\epsilon_{2}\to 0}\frac{Z^{(c),\mathrm{inst}}_{2d/4d}(x,{\boldsymbol{a}},\Lambda,{\rm i}\hbar,\epsilon_{2})}{Z^{\rm inst}({\boldsymbol{a}},\Lambda,{\rm i}\hbar,\epsilon_{2})}\,.

(C.121)

C.3
The Wilson loops

Another ingredient we need is the four-dimensional limit of Wilson-loop expectation values
Gaiotto:2014ina
;
Bullimore:2014awa
; here we closely follow
Grassi:2018bci
. As before, let
{\boldsymbol{Y}}=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{N})
denote an
N
-tuple of Young diagrams. We define the equivariant Chern character as

{\rm Ch}_{\boldsymbol{Y}}({\boldsymbol{a}},\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})={\cal W}-(1-{\rm e}^{R\epsilon_{1}})(1-{\rm e}^{R\epsilon_{2}}){\cal V}_{\boldsymbol{Y}}\,,

(C.122)

where

\displaystyle{\cal W}

\displaystyle=\sum_{I=1}^{N}{\rm e}^{Ra_{I}},

(C.123)

\displaystyle{\cal V}_{\boldsymbol{Y}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{I=1}^{N}{\rm e}^{R\alpha_{I}}\,\sum_{(k,l)\in Y_{I}}{\rm e}^{(k-1)R\epsilon_{1}+(l-1)R\epsilon_{2}}\,.

In the
R\to 0
limit,
{\cal W}
behaves as

{\rm Ch}_{\boldsymbol{Y}}({\boldsymbol{a}},\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=N+\sum_{k\geqslant 2}\mathcal{C}^{(k)}_{\boldsymbol{Y}}({\boldsymbol{a}},\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\,R^{k},

(C.124)

where, for example:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{(2)}_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{a},\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{I=1}^{N}a_{I}^{2}-\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}\ell\left(\boldsymbol{Y}\right),

(C.125)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{(3)}_{\boldsymbol{Y}}(\boldsymbol{a},\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{I=1}^{N}a_{I}^{3}-\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}\left(\frac{\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}}{2}\ell\left(\boldsymbol{Y}\right)+\epsilon_{1}\sum_{I=1}^{N}c_{2}\left(Y_{I}^{T}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\quad+\epsilon_{2}\sum_{I=1}^{N}c_{2}\left(Y_{I}\right)+\sum_{I=1}^{N}a_{I}\left|Y_{I}\right|\right),

while

c_{2}\left(Y\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\geqslant 1}y_{i}\left(y_{i}-1\right).

(C.126)

We define the “4d Wilson loops” as

{\rm W}^{(k)}({\boldsymbol{a}},\Lambda,\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\frac{1}{Z^{\rm inst}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{Y}}((-1)^{N}\Lambda^{2N})^{\ell(\boldsymbol{Y})}\,\mathcal{C}^{(k)}_{\boldsymbol{Y}}({\boldsymbol{a}},\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\,Z_{\boldsymbol{Y}}({\boldsymbol{a}},\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\,.

(C.127)

Here we are interested in their NS limit, which we denote by

{\rm W}^{(k)}_{\rm NS}({\boldsymbol{a}},\Lambda,\hbar)=\lim_{\epsilon_{2}\to 0}{\rm W}^{(k)}({\boldsymbol{a}},\Lambda,{\rm i}\hbar,\epsilon_{2}),\quad k=1,\dots N-1.

(C.128)

These observables are directly related to the complex moduli parameters
h_{k}
appearing in the mirror curve, and they generalise the Matone relation to higher rank gauge theories. More precisely, we have

\boxed{h_{j}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\frac{(-1)^{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|-\ell({\boldsymbol{k}})}}{z_{\boldsymbol{k}}}{\rm W}_{\rm NS}^{\boldsymbol{k}}({\boldsymbol{a}},\Lambda,\hbar),\qquad j\in\left\{2,\cdots,N\right\}}

(C.129)

where
\boldsymbol{k}=(0,k_{2},\cdots)
satisfies

\ell(\boldsymbol{k})=\sum_{m\geqslant 2}mk_{m}=j\,,

(C.130)

and we used

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle W}_{\rm NS}^{\boldsymbol{k}}({\boldsymbol{a}},\Lambda,\hbar)=\prod_{j\geqslant 1}(j!{\rm W}_{\rm NS}^{(j)}({\boldsymbol{a}},\Lambda,\hbar))^{k_{j}}\,,\quad z_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\prod_{j\geqslant 1}k_{j}!j^{k_{j}}\,,\quad|\boldsymbol{k}|=\sum_{m}k_{m}\,.

(C.131)

Appendix D
Figures and numerical evidence

In this appendix, we present additional numerical evidence supporting our results. We focus on the cubic and quartic potentials, displaying, for various choices of parameters, the ground, first, and second excited states for the cubic case, the first and second excited states for the quartic case.

In
figure 6
we show the ground-state wavefunction for the cubic potential for
h_{2}=-5
. Due to the deep local minimum of the potential, the wavefunction closely resembles that of a bound state. In
figure 7
we show the first excited state for
h_{2}=-3
, together with the two singular saddles, making the cancellation of poles in their sum evident.
Finally, for the cubic case,
figure 8
shows the wavefunction corresponding to the second excited state for the cubic potential with
h_{2}=2
. In this case, the potential has no real stationary points, yet resonant eigenfunctions still exist. Moving on to the quartic case, in
figure 9
we show the wavefunction of the first excited state for the symmetric (
h_{3}=0
) single well potential with
h_{2}=3.5
, together with the two singular saddle contributions. The on-shell solution is antisymmetric and normalized to
1
at
x=1/2
. Finally,
figure 10
displays the wavefunction corresponding to the second excited state of the quartic potential with
h_{2}=1.2
and
h_{3}=-4.7
. The corresponding potential is an asymmetric quartic single well with a unique minimum.
