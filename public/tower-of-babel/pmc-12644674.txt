Practical considerations for clinical translation of PET imaging of adoptive cell therapies
Authors: Alessia Volpe, Jonathan Pham, Mark A. Sellmyer, Vladimir Ponomarev
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Research and development programs for adoptive cell therapies continue to expand, but few products make it to late-phase clinical trials, and even fewer receive FDA approval. Despite undergoing extensive validation before entering the trial phase, variable results may be observed in patients due to inherent differences between preclinical models and human subjects as well as heterogeneity between tumors. Moreover, the current clinical evaluation of cell therapies, including CAR-T cells, relies on limited or inconclusive approaches – usually blood sampling or tissue biopsies – lacking spatial and temporal information about their fate in the human body. Here we offer our perspective on how the application of PET imaging to track cell therapies in clinical studies could address these shortcomings and enhance our understanding of cell therapy biodistribution, patients and trial-level therapeutic success or failure, and safety considerations. We further address key challenges, from probe development to methodological, technical, and regulatory, and financial hurdles for integrating PET imaging of cell therapies into clinical studies.

Introduction

Rationale for monitoring cell therapies in human subjects

Cell therapies have demonstrated the ability to offer a transformative therapeutic opportunity for patients with advanced malignancies1. However, treatment failure or relapse may occur due to antigen escape or limited cellular expansion and persistence, often driven by the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment2,3. That said, these contributing factors remain challenging to identify and distinguish on an individual basis. As technologies and tools used in academia and industry to assess cell therapy efficacy and safety concerns in the trial phase (including quantitative PCR, tissue biopsies, and FDG-PET) have various limitations, molecular imaging (MI) of therapeutic cells may offer complementary quantitative information, which may ultimately prove to be important to our understanding of cell therapies. The imaging of a cell therapy could enable a feedback loop between clinical trials and translational science. This could be especially relevant for drug developers interested in iterative product design and optimization. Once integrated as part of the cell therapy regimen in clinical trials, imaging can provide information about distribution, on-target on-tumor specificity, on-target off-tumor toxicity, tumor infiltration, expansion, persistence, and associated therapeutic response (Fig. 1). The set of information acquired will be essential for data-driven decision making in the drug development phase. This includes addressing unanticipated safety issues, toxicity management, dosing strategy, and product fine-tuning to improve treatment efficacy. In the era of precision medicine, MI can provide powerful data to support the development and approval of therapeutic products. MI of T cell-based immunotherapy

Approaches for imaging and tracking CAR-T cells

Our goal in this perspective is not to provide a comprehensive review of these strategies for imaging cell therapies, but rather to discuss considerations impacting their translation to clinical study. Given their more advanced clinical development, this perspective will focus on considerations affecting clinical imaging of CAR-T cells. Nonetheless, scientific and regulatory factors relevant to imaging of CAR-based T cell immunotherapies may also apply to other cell therapy products. Several reviews have been written on the development of techniques for nuclear imaging CAR-T cells4–7, and we would point readers to those for a more comprehensive summary of the field. Briefly, direct and indirect labeling strategies allow for specific spatiotemporal visualization of CAR-T cell therapies. The direct approach enables ex vivo labeling of cells with positron emission tomography (PET) or single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) radioactive tracers prior to administration. This strategy may be particularly useful when the cells of interest are not expected to expand. For an indefinite imaging window and to capture cellular kinetics, expansion, contraction, etc., indirect labeling based on the genetic engineering of adoptively transferred cells with imageable reporter systems (gene/probe) is likely to provide the most useful information. The imaging target (a cell or a biological process), imaging intervals, modality or probe of choice, are only some of the parameters dictating the selection of the appropriate approach7.

The optimal reporter candidate for imaging cell-based immunotherapies is selected based on the pattern of endogenous expression, target-to-background ratio, detection sensitivity, paired radiotracer background, and clearance. Short-lived radiotracers can be used and are, in fact, preferred to limit dose to healthy tissues and/or associated toxicity to T cells8.

Consideration for developing probes for imaging CAR-T cells

There are three major clinical areas for nuclear imaging probes - oncology, neurology, and cardiology - with many reviews describing their development and clinical application9–11. In the early phases of probe development, demonstrating that the addition of a radioisotope does not perturb or impair the biological or binding properties of the probe is of great importance, as it could hinder its potential for clinical translation. The radioisotope should be selected to match the half-life of the radiochemical probe to allow the probe to accumulate enough signal intarget tissues and remain detectable by imaging.11. If a probe is used to directly or indirectly track a cell-based product, efforts should also be made to optimize the dosing and minimize potential toxicity and radio-damage of the therapeutic cells by selecting the radioisotope with the most favorable half-life and physical decay properties. We now know that not all radiolabeling approaches are suitable for cell-based immunotherapies due to radiation dose and toxicity. One example is that of regulatory T cells (Tregs), which show impaired proliferation and survival upon direct labeling with 89Zr-oxine8. Reporter-expressing CAR-T cells and gamma delta T cells also present a dose-dependent loss of viability when labeled with [99mTc]TcO4-, [18F]BF4-12, or 89Zr-oxine13. If an imaging probe is used in combination with cell therapies, assessing the consequences of labeling should be implemented at the probe development stage as a standard of practice14. In the early phases of development, this includes extensive in vitro and preclinical testing proving that the addition of a probe/reporter pair does not perturb or impair the biological and functional properties of the cell product, as this could hinder its potential for clinical translation.

The scientific community should be aware of the existence of the Molecular Imaging and Contrast Agent Database (MICAD), a collaborative platform between the NCBI at the National Library of Medicine and the Cancer Imaging Program of the National Institute of Health (NIH), providing a readily available and up-to-date list of >5000 probes at the preclinical investigation stage, in in-human clinical trials and those approved by the FDA. The information available through the database may be used to promote collaborations between academic institutions and the pharmaceutical industry to catalyze research efforts towards the imaging probes for clinical application.

The role of MI in drug development

The roadmap leading to the regulatory approval of a CAR-T product consists of different phases, including target assessment, lead product optimization, preclinical assessment, and phase I-III clinical trials. MI could have a prominent role throughout the entire cellular immunotherapy development pipeline. Target assessment and drug product optimization are typically evaluated heavily in tissue culture; however, in the preclinical assessment phase, imaging can assist in optimizing the lead cell therapy candidates and help streamline the clinical translation of novel CAR-T strategies. Whilst non-imaging preclinical studies have survival as the primary assessment parameter, MI can provide insight into the mechanisms of therapy action and failure and help overcome product limitations by better tailoring the treatment, avoiding unneeded and costly optimization steps. For example, bioluminescence imaging may show better trafficking of an engineered, temporally regulated CAR-T cell compared to constitutively active CAR-T cells, or even off-tumor accumulation of the T cells15. Adapting nuclear techniques to evaluate CAR trafficking of different products, especially temporally regulated CARs, is an important future direction16. In the exploratory and safety clinical development stage (phase 0/1), imaging of cell therapies could allow better assessment of pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, delivery, and dosing, providing the necessary information for planning the later phase I-III studies. Similarly, if an imaging arm were added to clinical trials, it could allow an adaptive clinical trial design by enabling the investigator to change or add therapies based on cellular trafficking, moving toward a tailored and likely-to-succeed clinical trial.

Addressing challenges and barriers

Despite substantial progress being made in developing imaging tools capable of sensitive tracking of CAR-T cells, their integration in clinical trials has been extremely limited to date. This underutilization likely reflects a combination of methodological, regulatory, and funding barriers. Overcoming these challenges will be essential to enable a broader clinical applicability of this approach.

Overcoming methodological and technological considerations

As with any technique, MI has its own pitfalls and limitations. Perhaps the most directly relevant of these relates to CAR-T cell detection sensitivity, which is defined as the number of labeled cells needed in a specific volume for their non-invasive detection. The sensitivity of CAR-T and other therapeutic cell tracking using nuclear imaging is highly dependent on the specific approach used due to differences in labeling methodology, inherent limitations of the selected radioisotope, its half-life, radiotracer dosimetry, and instrumentation sensitivity. Together, these parameters can also greatly influence the potential tracking interval (from days to weeks to indefinitely). While PET scanner sensitivity and, therefore, the tracking time could be improved with the recent technological development of total-body PET17,18, other limitations could be overcome by rationally matching the investigational question to the most appropriate imaging approach available.

Immunogenicity is a key concern when using PET reporter genes of non-human origin. While important, it does not represent an absolute barrier to clinical translation. Notably, many approved CAR-T cell products contain non-human sequences, which themselves carry a potential risk of immunogenicity and may contribute to premature CAR-T cell clearance19. Moreover, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) previously approved the use of a highly immunogenic herpes simplex virus type 1 thymidine kinase (hsv-tk) reporter gene to visualize adoptively transferred cells in patients with advanced cancers20,21 These studies demonstrated increased radiotracer uptake in targeted lesions following cell therapy administration. A number of non-immunogenic human-derived reporter systems have been proposed to overcome the intrinsic immunogenicity of xenogenic reporters 22. More recently, the immunogenicity of bacterial enzyme E. coli dihydrofolate reductase (eDHFR), used as a PET reporter, has been investigated in human cell cultures, mice, and non-human primates. These studies showed minimal immune reactivity, suggesting that immunogenicity of non-human proteins should be assessed empirically rather than assumed16,23.

Approval processes and regulatory hurdles

Regulatory requirements and approval pathways governing clinical imaging of cell therapies may vary depending on the specific agencies overseeing studies involving investigational products. In this review, we will outline key translational considerations for clinical imaging of cell therapies in the United States, noting that these considerations are likely applicable to studies conducted in other regions of the world—though specific regulatory pathways and requirements may vary. In the United States, radiopharmaceuticals intended for clinical use require extensive testing and are regulated by the FDA24. The clinical approval process requires extensive and repeated quality control testing, stringent Good Manufacturing Practice (cGMP) requirements24,25, and a lengthy and costly application process11, which is why only a few probes make it to the trial phases and final FDA approval. The path towards clinical testing frequently starts with a Radioactive Drug Research Committee (RDRC)26,27 application, which allows for initiation of human subject research to understand the biodistribution of radiopharmaceuticals that are often based on existing drugs or natural substrates28. To develop a radiopharmaceutical approved for diagnostic and/or therapeutic purposes, an exploratory Investigational New Drug (eIND)29 or an Investigational New Drug (IND)30,31 application will be required. The eIND is relevant for conducting small, first-in-human phase 0 studies and is usually used to select the leading candidate for further clinical development. The IND is pursued to introduce a promising imaging candidate into phase I-III trials for clinical development, where the radiopharmaceutical will be assessed for toxicity and efficacy to support a future New Drug Application (NDA). This path is more stringent, requiring at least Good Laboratory Practice (GLP)-compliant and sometimes cGMP preclinical data on pharmacokinetics and safety/toxicity32.

For imaging strategies involving direct modification of cellular therapy products into imageable ones, additional regulatory hurdles will likely have to be overcome. In Fig. 2 we outline the regulatory path to incorporate monitoring of cell therapies in clinical studies. Since the cellular therapy product itself is intended to have a therapeutic effect, direct modification to facilitate imaging would likely entail the submission of a new IND application, as cells modified for imaging result from a different manufacturing process and thus represent a different cellular product compared to cells not modified for imaging. A recent FDA guideline for CAR-T cell products development (FDA-2021-D-0404)33 opens to the possibility of including with the CAR transgene also additional components, including “detection genes” which could also be interpreted as imaging reporters. However, MI scientists should be aware that any novel accessory component (e.g., an imaging probe) and further genetic modification (e.g., an inserted reporter gene) would require additional non-clinical testing to evaluate their safety and efficacy. Another FDA guideline on studying cell and gene therapy products in the early clinical trial phases (FDA-2021-D-0776)34 may also be of interest for those seeking to incorporate MI into clinical cell therapy investigation. This document presents the possibility of having a “therapeutic-only” arm and a “therapy and imaging” arm, where the cell therapy product is also rendered imageable. For cell therapy developers, this strategy provides flexibility for imaging to be included in the evaluation of an investigational cellular product while presenting a de-risked approach should the imaging component negatively affect its safety and therapeutic efficacy. Funding and resource allocation

Clinical trials start in a number of ways, including as investigator initiated trials (IITs) at individual academic hospitals/institutions. The pharmaceutical industry can also play a key role in initiating clinical trials conducted by investigators by providing funding, drug products, or both. Investigators may also access the funding opportunity announcement (FOA) for the Exploratory/Developmental Phased Cooperative Agreement (UG3/UH3) from the National Cancer Institute (NCI), a bi-phasic grant intended to accelerate development, innovation, and establishment of adoptive cell therapy clinical trials, specifically supporting preclinical, translational, and IND-enabling studies constituting the bedrock for the UH3 early-stage clinical trials. The NCI also offers financial support for the translation of imaging technologies (including molecular agents, companion diagnostics, and prognostic technologies) into clinical trials through Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) and Small Business Technology Transfer (STTR) grant funding opportunities35. While SBIR requires the Principal Investigator to be employed by the small business, the STTR scheme does not have primary employment stipulations, but requires a formal sponsored research agreement (SRA) between the small business and other research partners in universities and/or non-profit research institutions. As such, the STTR represents an attractive route to financially support the translation of imaging strategies into the clinical trial stage for the monitoring of cell-based immunotherapies, noting that there needs to be a clear commercial opportunity for the imaging strategy—an often challenging requirement given the low reimbursement rates for diagnostics and the relatively limited patient population eligible for cell therapy products.

Future directions and opportunities for collaboration

Key studies enabling clinical translation

As imaging technologies and modalities continue to advance, the imaging community can facilitate the clinical translation of cell therapy imaging by expanding the focus beyond the temporal and spatial biodistribution of these “living drugs,” as the ability to track cellular immunotherapies alone is only one reason to advance this platform into clinical studies. MI scientists should also demonstrate that imaging can provide a unique set of information to guide treatments. Efforts from this community should also be made to rigorously address the safety concerns encompassing the immunogenicity of non-human reporters and rodent dosimetry of the imaging probe (required for the IND application), while also preserving the functionality of the investigational product.

Establishing long-term collaborative partnerships for translation into clinical practice

Given the significant expertise and resources required for the development and evaluation of cell therapies, it is critical that MI scientists establish collaborative partnerships with stakeholders across the public and private sectors throughout the translational pipeline. Stakeholders include cell therapy manufacturers, cyclotrons, radiochemistry and nuclear imaging facilities, clinical teams overseeing cell therapy trials, federal regulatory agencies, academic/hospital/non-profit institutions, contractrResearch organizations (CROs), industry, and commercial partners.

The Clinical Trials Network (CTN) of the Society of Nuclear Medicine and Molecular Imaging (SNMMI) is an example of collaboration with pharmaceutical manufacturers to provide oversight, strategic guidance, database management, scanner validation, technologist training, trial design, and high-quality imaging at multiple clinical trial sites36. The ultimate scope is to closely work with manufacturers interested in acquiring knowledge of the safety and efficacy of their product and speeding up their approval. Similarly, the American College of Radiology Imaging Network (ACRIN) and the Cancer Imaging Program of the NCI bring together stakeholders to advance MI techniques into the clinics. The Health and Environmental Sciences Institute (HESI) is a non-profit scientific organization formed by the first Cell and Gene Therapy-TRAcking, Circulation and Safety (CGT-TRACS) Committee, bringing together different stakeholders to improve the safety of cell therapies using in vivo imaging, and help advance them into the clinic37.

Conclusions

The limited understanding of the in vivo fate and safety profile of cell therapies in patients is one reason preventing many products from entering the approval phase and becoming a standard therapeutic option. The ability to track cell therapies from the point of administration can go beyond addressing relevant clinical questions about distribution, targeting, and persistence, may also provide valuable information to improve clinical decisions, including addressing and mitigating risk, dosing, and change of therapy. While this may lead to undesired findings that could be detrimental to the product progression through the trial phase, it could enhance confidence in the efficacy and safe use of other, more promising candidates throughout the translational process and accelerate their clinical authorization. Given the in-depth expertise and financial resources required for the development and evaluation of imageable cell therapeutics, combining efforts from all relevant stakeholders in the imaging and cellular immunotherapy communities across private and public sectors of academia and industry is expected to be the most effective strategy to increase the likelihood of success and overcome regulatory hurdles. Importantly, some cell-based products have already incorporated a reporter gene20, and others show promise12,38. Recent advancements, from total-body PET17, which allows imaging with greater sensitivity and lower radioactivity doses, to multiplexed PET39, are also significant technological steps facilitating the integration of imaging into cell therapy trials. Our steady investment into developing imaging approaches for cell therapies should ultimately yield clinically applicable tools for monitoring these living medicines with spatiotemporal resolution.
