Advancing Regulatory Adoption of New Approach Methodologies
Authors: Jeffry Florian, Kimberly Chiu, Rebecca Racz, C. Lee Elmore, Shirley K. Seo, Ksenia Blinova, Wendy W. Wu, Rodney Rouse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) has a long history advancing new approach methodologies (NAMs). In collaboration with external organizations and other regulatory agencies, FDA supports continued development of NAMs for regulatory use. Within CDER, qualification programs and interdisciplinary review teams provide opportunities for increased regulatory impact of NAMs. CDER also continues to provide regulatory input to NAMs developers and end users to advance regulatory acceptance of NAMs.

The Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) of the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has been at the forefront of new approach methodologies (NAMs) development for nearly two decades, to improve toxicology testing and strengthen its human‐relevance and predictive value for clinical outcomes. NAMs are defined here as innovative testing methods and strategies used to assess the safety, efficacy, and quality of drugs, biologics, and other CDER‐regulated products and include in vitro human‐based systems, in silico modeling, and other innovative platforms. 1 The rapid evolution of NAMs science and technology is driven in part by CDER's increased engagement in guiding the science toward regulatory applications. With passage of the FDA Modernization Act 2.0, which encouraged the use of NAMs in regulatory submissions, and the April 2025 FDA Roadmap to Reducing Animal Studies in Preclinical Safety Studies, 1 , 2 CDER's efforts to bring NAMs into regulatory use have intensified. However, realizing the opportunities that NAMs can provide requires evidentiary standards demonstrating the validity and risk associated with emerging tools and recognition that increased integration of NAMs must not come at the expense of safety.

It is critical that regulators better understand how these approaches are already being used by sponsors to support decision making that moves candidate drugs into nonclinical submissions. Increasing regulatory comfort with NAMs and identifying the potential and limitations of these approaches will help drive regulatory adoption. Achieving the full potential of NAMs requires navigating significant scientific, technical, and regulatory challenges. Establishing evidentiary frameworks that demonstrate the validity and predictive value of emerging tools is an ongoing and complex endeavor. Despite these challenges, CDER remains committed to active leadership in this space, including advancing regulatory science, refining internal practices, and engaging stakeholders. The following sections describe CDER efforts to support the progressive and responsible integration of NAMs into nonclinical drug development.

CDER: BUILDING ORGANIZATIONAL INFRASTRUCTURE AND INTERNAL EXPERTISE

CDER has taken multiple steps to support regulatory adoption of NAMs, including publishing articles on opportunities and challenges for regulatory application of NAMs 3 and the optimization and characterization of NAMs. 1 Additionally, CDER recently published a comprehensive NAM submissions analysis, providing strategic insights on opportunities and needs 4 as well as the year one progress report Reducing Animal Testing in Nonclinical Studies summarizing progress and next steps to realize the 3Rs principles of animal research to replace, reduce, or refine in animal testing. 1 In October 2025, CDER also published an inventory of specific drug development contexts where streamlined nonclinical programs are acceptable. 1 In 2026, FDA formed the NAMs Program Working Group comprised of leading scientific NAMs experts within FDA to advance regulatory use of NAMs.

CDER formed the CDER NAMs Coordinating Committee (CDER NAMs CC) to coordinate NAMs implementation strategy and stakeholder engagement. In May 2025, the CDER NAMs CC launched a pilot Integrated Review Team (NAM‐IRT) to support consistent, expert‐led review of NAMs submissions and to further encourage sponsors to include NAMs in their submissions. The committee continues to coordinate CDER activities with industry, international drug regulators, and other stakeholders to harmonize on regulatory acceptance of NAMs. Some of these efforts are described in the next section.

ALIGNMENT OF INTERNATIONAL GUIDANCE AND REGULATORY POLICY

CDER is working with global regulators and drug developers to integrate or expand upon NAMs approaches within existing International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) guidelines. This includes updating ICH E14 and S7B Q&As, the clinical and nonclinical testing paradigms for assessing proarrhythmic risk in drug development, to expand the incorporation of NAMs within a totality of evidence approach. 5 CDER is also involved in planning meetings to align revision proposals for ICH S7A. 6 Additionally, FDA is a charter member of the International Medicines Regulators' Working Group on 3Rs, with CDER currently serving as co‐chair to advance harmonization across regulatory bodies. Member agencies share existing guidance and policies on NAMs for regulatory use while working collaboratively to coordinate future approaches.

In addition to alignment efforts for international guidelines, CDER has issued multiple guidance documents and other resources to facilitate the incorporation of NAMs in drug development. 1 This includes draft guidances General Considerations for the Use of New Approach Methodologies in Drug Development, outlining expectations for regulatory acceptance, 1 Monoclonal Antibodies: Streamlined Nonclinical Safety Studies, indicating that some nonhuman primate studies could be replaced using a weight‐of‐evidence approach, 1 and Quantitative Systems Pharmacology‐Based Dose Selection for Minimum Anticipated Biological Effect Level in First‐in‐Human Trials, encouraging use of quantitative modeling and simulation to inform drug development and regulatory decisions. Multiple sponsors have already applied the concepts outlined in these guidance documents in their regulatory submissions. These efforts reflect both CDER's commitment to advancing the regulatory use of NAMs as well as a willingness of sponsors to apply these methods.

ENGAGING EXTERNAL STAKEHOLDERS

FDA collaborates with National Institutes of Health (NIH) programs to advance the development, standardization, validation, and regulatory integration of NAMs. 1 These partnerships help NAM developers identify regulatory context for their methods and provide evidentiary frameworks that will demonstrate fit‐for‐purpose validation to regulators who must assess their methods. CDER plays a central leadership role in these initiatives to build the shared infrastructure needed to move NAMs toward wider regulatory acceptance on an international level.

CDER recognizes the importance of engaging both drug developers and NAMs technology developers simultaneously. To foster such discussions, CDER held workshops in 2023 and 2024 to discuss evidentiary considerations for regulatory assessment and qualification of complex in vitro NAMs. This workshop series continued in 2026 through the combined efforts of NIH and the Critical Path Institute. Moreover, in 2025, FDA and NIH co‐convened a workshop on Reducing Animal Testing, bringing together international regulators from Europe, Japan, and Australia to work toward global alignment on NAMs strategies.

CDER is aware that substantial nonclinical work, including NAMs, is conducted but never submitted for CDER review. Unlike clinical development, CDER has limited visibility into the decision making that occurs within a nonclinical development program. CDER also recognizes that sponsors may be hesitant to submit data generated by NAMs due to proprietary considerations, lack of guidelines for submissions, and uncertainty in how NAMs results may be interpreted. These challenges constrain regulators' ability to understand and assess NAMs when they are submitted and to provide guidance to industry on NAMs submissions and explain why regulatory adoption of novel approaches lags behind scientific and technical advances. CDER has engaged with industry to address some of these barriers through potential data sharing outside of regulatory submissions.

PATHWAYS FOR INTERACTING WITH FDA

With recent draft guidance releases and enhanced stakeholder engagement, CDER expects to see progressively more NAMs included in regulatory submissions. While regulatory submissions can include NAMs at any stage in a product life cycle, discussions may be most timely when a sponsor is planning their nonclinical development program or seeking feedback prior to initiating first‐in‐human studies. The FDA provides several pathways (e.g., Pre‐Investigational New Drug Meetings, Initial Targeted Engagement for Regulatory Advice on CBER/CDER ProducTs [INTERACT] meetings) for such discussions. 7

Outside of individual product submissions, CDER also implemented qualification programs to obtain regulatory acceptance of NAMs for a specific context‐of‐use in drug development. 1 , 8 The benefit of these programs is transparency of the process, and upon regulatory acceptance, the availability of the tool for use by others within the same context‐of‐use without additional validation. With increased attention to and evolution of NAMs, CDER has seen qualification submissions for NAMs nearly double from 2024 (9 submissions) to 2025 (16 submissions) within the Innovative Science and Technology Approaches for New Drugs (ISTAND) program. As of August 2026, NAMs submissions are on track to increase again in 2026.

In parallel, CDER is evaluating submissions where sponsors have incorporated NAMs into their nonclinical development program to better understand uptake and utilization to support regulatory decisions. With publication of recent guidance, sponsors have begun including weight‐of‐evidence justifications in their submissions to address questions around long‐term safety for investigational monoclonal antibody products. CDER has also initiated an internal review of cardiac and neural NAMs that have been included in regulatory submissions to identify the frequency with which NAMs are included in submissions and the proposed regulatory contexts of use in these submissions. These findings will identify knowledge gaps and limitations of these approaches that will need to be filled to build confidence for their use in regulatory decision making.

CONCLUSION

There are meaningful opportunities to better leverage existing and emerging information to support safe use in humans, whether by incorporating new tools and technologies into nonclinical development, or by identifying how existing information can be more effectively applied to decision making. CDER views progress in this area as an evidence‐driven expansion of nonclinical tools that must meet or exceed existing safety standards that protect public health.

As reflected in this article, CDER recognizes that existing NAMs platforms have limitations that may constrain their application but remains committed to an active role in shaping the science and policy of NAMs toward regulatory acceptance. With recent draft guidance releases, improved internal practices, and enhanced stakeholder engagement, CDER continues to support progressively greater inclusion of valid NAMs in regulatory decision making.
