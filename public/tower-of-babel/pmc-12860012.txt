Gait analysis in bladder exstrophy patients in late follow-up period
Authors: Ayten Ceren Bakır, Ozan Ali Erdal, Mehmet Eliçevik, Haluk Emir, Yunus Söylet, Nur Danişmend, Cenk Büyükünal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBladder exstrophy (BE) is a rare congenital anomaly associated with complex urogenital malformations and musculoskeletal sequelae. Despite surgical advances, the long-term biomechanical implications of reconstructive procedures, particularly on gait and pelvic kinematics, remain underexplored. Previous studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding hip motion, pelvic tilt, and gait symmetry, and the influence of osteotomy on locomotor function is not well defined. This study aimed to investigate long-term gait and pelvic biomechanics in patients with BE following childhood surgical repair, to evaluate the effects of pelvic osteotomy, and to determine correlations between radiographic pubic diastasis and quantitative gait parameters.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study included 27 patients (20 males, 7 females; age 12–33 years) treated for BE at a tertiary pediatric surgery center. All participants underwent comprehensive evaluation including clinical history, physical examination, radiological assessment, and three-dimensional gait analysis using a motion capture system with reflective markers. Parameters assessed included trunk and pelvic rotation, pelvic obliquity, hip abduction, and electromyographic muscle activation. Subgroup analyses compared patients with and without osteotomy, and correlations between gait parameters and radiographic pubic diastasis were examined. Data were analyzed using Student’s t-test, Mann–Whitney U test, Chi-square/Fisher’s exact test, and Spearman correlation, with p < 0.05 considered significant.ResultsQuantitative gait analysis demonstrated significant deviations compared with normative values, including a 5° increase in trunk obliquity at initial contact, a 3° decrease during pre-swing, and a 10° increase in bilateral hip abduction during the swing phase. Patients with a history of osteotomy exhibited greater posterior pelvic tilt and increased pelvic obliquity during swing (p < 0.01). Pubic diastasis correlated with trunk and pelvic rotation parameters (rho values ranging from − 0.585 to 0.552, p < 0.05). Despite these measurable deviations, none of the participants reported difficulties in daily activities, school attendance, or recreational physical activity, indicating effective neuromuscular compensation.ConclusionsBladder exstrophy patients demonstrate persistent alterations in pelvic biomechanics and gait kinematics in long-term follow-up, particularly after pelvic osteotomy. However, these changes did not translate into clinically limiting impairments, highlighting the adaptive capacity of the neuromuscular system. Quantitative gait analysis provides objective insights into musculoskeletal sequelae and underscores the importance of tailored long-term surveillance strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06584-4.

Introduction

Bladder exstrophy (BE) is a rare congenital anomaly within the bladder exstrophy–epispadias complex (BEEC), representing a spectrum of midline closure defects involving the genitourinary tract and musculoskeletal system. The incidence of BE is approximately 1 in 50,000 live births, with a male predominance of 2:1 [1]. Surgical management traditionally focuses on early bladder closure and urinary continence, yet the long-term musculoskeletal and functional implications of this complex condition are increasingly recognized.

Despite advances in reconstructive techniques, patients with BE frequently demonstrate altered locomotor function, including gait and pelvic deviations, which may persist into adolescence and adulthood. While some studies have reported near-normal functional outcomes, others describe abnormal pelvic kinematics, reduced hip motion, or altered foot progression angles compared with healthy controls [2–4]. These conflicting results suggest that long-term biomechanical adaptations are complex and multifactorial.

Pelvic anatomy, particularly pubic diastasis, is a critical determinant of stability and alignment in BE. Residual separation of the pubic bones after closure has been associated with compensatory changes in gait mechanics and posture. Similarly, pelvic osteotomy is often performed to facilitate closure and reduce pubic diastasis, yet its long-term biomechanical consequences remain debated. While some authors argue that osteotomy provides structural benefits, others suggest that it may induce compensatory gait adaptations [5–7].

In addition to physical outcomes, quality of life and psychosocial well-being are important dimensions of long-term follow-up in BEEC patients. Previous research has highlighted that functional independence and psychosocial adaptation often coexist with measurable kinematic deviations [8].

The present study aimed to evaluate long-term gait and pelvic biomechanics in patients with BE who underwent childhood surgical repair. Specifically, we sought to compare gait parameters between patients with and without a history of osteotomy and to assess the correlation between pubic diastasis and biomechanical outcomes. We hypothesized that individuals who underwent pelvic osteotomy would demonstrate distinct gait alterations, and that greater pubic diastasis would be associated with significant deviations in trunk and pelvic kinematics.

Materials and methods

Patients and study design

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted between 2017 and 2018 at a single tertiary pediatric surgery center. Out of approximately 190 patients treated for bladder exstrophy, 80 were over the age of 10 at the time of review. All patients with a diagnosis of BE who were ≥ 12 years at the time of gait analysis and had complete clinical and radiographic data were included. Among these, 27 patients (20 males, 7 females) aged 12–33 years were included in the final analysis. The study design is summarized in Fig. 1. The included patients had undergone surgical repair between 1985 and 2017. Children over the age of ten were selected to ensure adequate musculoskeletal maturity and gait coordination. Patients were grouped based on multiple factors, including the presence or absence of pelvic osteotomy, whether augmentation had been performed, and radiographic pubic diastasis measurements. Osteotomy was performed in 5 patients (18.5%) using an anterior iliac approach. Fixation methods varied across cases and were based on individual surgeon preference at the time of surgery.Fig. 1Study design. Surgical management

Pelvic osteotomy was considered for patients with a wide pubic diastasis (greater than 5 cm) or when delayed bladder closure was expected to cause excessive tension on the anterior abdominal wall. Among the 27 patients, five (18.5%) underwent bilateral anterior iliac osteotomy during primary closure in infancy. The procedure was performed through an oblique iliac approach, allowing medial rotation of each hemipelvis to facilitate pubic approximation. After closure, the pubic symphysis was secured using non-absorbable sutures or tension bands, according to the surgeon’s preference at the time of surgery. Pelvic stabilization was maintained with a pelvipedal cast for three weeks, followed by elastic bandage support for an additional three weeks.

In patients who did not undergo osteotomy, bladder closure was achieved through wide soft-tissue mobilization and direct pubic approximation without bone division.

Radiographic pubic diastasis was defined as the minimal interpubic distance (mm) on anteroposterior pelvic radiographs and was assessed at three time points: preoperatively, immediately after closure, and at the time of gait analysis.

All procedures were performed at the same tertiary pediatric surgery center by different surgeons over several decades, reflecting the evolution of institutional practice during this period.

Data collection

Demographic data, surgical history, and long-term outcomes were collected through chart review and direct patient interviews. Physical examinations, gait analysis, and musculoskeletal assessments were performed. Body mass index (BMI), serum calcium, and bone mineral density (BMD) were recorded. Urological and nephrological assessments included renal function tests, ultrasonography, and urinalysis. Because recordings of pubic diastasis were variable, proportional indices were used to standardize the analysis.

Laboratory and radiologic assessments

Calcium levels and BMD were assessed to determine systemic skeletal effects post-surgery. Serum urea and creatinine levels, urinalysis, and renal ultrasonography were used to evaluate renal health.

Gait and orthopedic assessment

Gait assessment involved three stages:

1. Musculoskeletal examination (range of motion, joint mobility, muscle strength).

2. Observational gait assessment by a specialist.

3. Quantitative 3D gait analysis using a Motion Analysis system (Motion Analysis Corp., Santa Rosa, CA, USA), which included eight infrared cameras, two video cameras, and 28 reflective markers. Patients walked barefoot on a 10-m walkway.

Time-distance parameters and joint positions (hip, knee, ankle) were analyzed across sagittal, frontal, and transverse planes. Specific gait phases like Initial Contact and Pre-Swing were evaluated. Electromyographic (EMG) data were used to assess muscle dynamics.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 25.0. Descriptive statistics were presented as means ± SD for continuous variables and frequencies for categorical data. Group comparisons were made using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, and Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Spearman correlation was used to explore associations between gait metrics and pubic diastasis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Pelvic kinematics

Pelvic tilt was characterized by an increased posterior orientation, particularly evident during the stance phase. Pelvic obliquity demonstrated asymmetry between sides, with an average difference of 4°–6° compared with normative values. Pelvic rotation patterns also showed increased variability across the gait cycle, with asymmetrical peak values in swing phase.

Lower limb parameters

Hip abduction was significantly greater in both limbs during pre-swing and swing phases (mean 10° vs. normal reference 2°–3°). Knee and ankle kinematics were largely preserved, though mild reductions in ankle dorsiflexion at terminal stance were observed in some patients.

Subgroup analysis: osteotomy versus non-osteotomy

Patients with a history of pelvic osteotomy (n = 5) exhibited more pronounced posterior pelvic tilt (mean ± SD: − 12.1 ± 3.5° vs. − 7.8 ± 2.9°, p = 0.01) and greater pelvic obliquity during swing (5.4 ± 2.1° vs. 2.7 ± 1.4°, p = 0.02) compared with the non-osteotomy group. Pelvic diastasis and femoral rotation

At the time of gait assessment, the mean pubic diastasis measured 68.6 ± 24.4 mm in the osteotomy group and 81.4 ± 20.9 mm in patients without osteotomy. Although the difference did not reach statistical significance due to the limited number of cases, patients who had undergone osteotomy in infancy tended to have smaller residual separation between the pubic bones.

Comparison of femoral rotation angles showed a mean right femoral rotation (RTAz0) of − 2.09 ± 3.61° in the osteotomy group and − 2.20 ± 4.64° in the non-osteotomy group, while the mean left femoral rotation (LTAz0) was − 1.19 ± 3.46° and − 2.69 ± 4.69°, respectively. Although no significant difference was detected, greater variability within the osteotomy group suggests subtle skeletal asymmetry—possibly reflecting early bony remodeling rather than ongoing functional limitation.

Correlation with pubic diastasis

Radiographic pubic diastasis was significantly correlated with trunk and pelvic kinematic parameters. Larger pubic separation was associated with increased trunk obliquity (rho = 0.552, p = 0.01) and greater pelvic rotation asymmetry (rho = − 0.585, p = 0.02). Pubic diastasis also correlated with lower extremity angular deviations, particularly hip abduction during swing (rho = 0.431, p = 0.04). Clinical outcomes

Despite objective deviations in gait and pelvic kinematics, none of the patients reported limitations in daily activities, school attendance, or recreational physical activity. All were ambulatory without assistive devices, and no significant musculoskeletal pain or orthopedic interventions were required during follow-up.

Discussion

This study provides new insights into the long-term musculoskeletal consequences of bladder exstrophy (BE) repair by combining quantitative gait analysis with radiographic pubic diastasis measurements. While urinary continence and upper tract preservation are the primary goals of surgical management, the musculoskeletal sequelae of BE have received less attention. Our results demonstrate that patients continue to exhibit measurable gait and pelvic kinematic deviations into adolescence and adulthood, particularly in the presence of prior pelvic osteotomy and increased pubic diastasis. Importantly, these objective findings did not translate into clinically limiting impairments, emphasizing the adaptive capacity of the neuromuscular system.

Comparison with existing literature

Previous studies of gait in BE patients have produced inconsistent findings. Satisfactory long-term functional results have been observed in a low-volume center, whereas persistent gait deviations, particularly in pelvic motion, have been reported in other studies [3, 4]. Functional and psychosocial challenges have been observed in adolescents with BEEC, suggesting that musculoskeletal adaptation is not uniform across patients [8]. Our findings align with those studies reporting residual deviations, as we observed increased trunk obliquity, greater hip abduction during swing, and asymmetry in pelvic rotation. The variability across published reports may reflect differences in surgical protocols, patient selection, and follow-up methodology.

Role of pelvic osteotomy

The use of pelvic osteotomy in BE repair remains controversial. Historically, osteotomy has been advocated to facilitate bladder closure, decrease pubic diastasis, and improve pelvic stability [5]. Pelvic osteotomies were originally described to facilitate primary closure and reduce the tension across the symphysis and anterior abdominal wall, thereby improving the likelihood of successful repair [9]. Despite this anatomic advantage, our findings indicate that long-term pelvic alignment and gait symmetry may remain altered in some patients following osteotomy [6]. In our study, patients with osteotomy demonstrated more pronounced posterior pelvic tilt and increased pelvic obliquity, consistent with altered biomechanics. Although the osteotomy group was small (n = 5), these consistent patterns suggest that skeletal modifications made during early childhood may have long-lasting biomechanical consequences. Larger multicenter cohorts are needed to validate these observations and determine whether osteotomy should be tailored more selectively. Our findings are consistent with previous long-term series describing functional outcomes and musculoskeletal adaptation in exstrophy patients [10–14].

Impact of pubic diastasis

Residual pubic diastasis is one of the most distinctive musculoskeletal features of BE, persisting even after successful bladder closure. Pubic separation alters pelvic anatomy and contributes to instability, which in turn may affect gait [15]. In our cohort, pubic diastasis correlated significantly with trunk obliquity and pelvic rotation, suggesting that greater separation predisposes to compensatory gait strategies. This structural determinant likely explains part of the variability in functional outcomes and supports the inclusion of pubic diastasis as a key parameter in long-term follow-up protocols.

Functional and psychosocial implications

An important aspect of our findings is the dissociation between objective gait alterations and subjective patient experience. None of the participants reported limitations in daily activities, school attendance, or recreational physical activity. Previous work by Ebert et al. and Diseth et al. has emphasized that BE patients often achieve satisfactory psychosocial outcomes and independence, even in the presence of measurable physical differences [16, 17]. This resilience underscores the importance of considering both objective and subjective dimensions of outcome assessment. Nevertheless, gait deviations may contribute to subtle issues such as fatigue, postural discomfort, or altered athletic participation, which may not be readily captured by routine clinical questioning.

Clinical significance and applications

The use of quantitative gait analysis in this context provides objective, reproducible data that can enhance long-term follow-up of BE patients. Identifying deviations in trunk and pelvic kinematics may inform targeted interventions, such as physiotherapy programs, postural training, or orthopaedic monitoring. In particular, patients with larger pubic diastasis or prior osteotomy may benefit from proactive musculoskeletal evaluation, even in the absence of overt symptoms. Multidisciplinary collaboration between pediatric urologists, orthopedic surgeons, and rehabilitation specialists is essential for comprehensive care.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include its focus on long-term outcomes, integration of gait analysis with radiographic parameters, and systematic subgroup evaluation. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective design limits causal inference, and the osteotomy subgroup was small, reducing statistical power. No age- and sex-matched healthy control group was available; instead, comparisons relied on laboratory normative data, which may not fully capture individual variability. Additionally, functional outcomes were assessed based on self-reported activity rather than standardized quality-of-life instruments. Future prospective, multicenter studies with larger samples and validated functional outcome measures are needed to build on these findings.

In summary, patients with bladder exstrophy demonstrate persistent alterations in gait and pelvic biomechanics in long-term follow-up. These deviations are more pronounced in patients with prior pelvic osteotomy and greater pubic diastasis, yet they do not appear to compromise everyday functioning. Quantitative gait analysis provides valuable insights into musculoskeletal sequelae and should be incorporated into long-term surveillance strategies. By integrating radiological, biomechanical, and functional outcomes, clinicians can better understand the adaptive processes in BE and design more tailored interventions for affected individuals.

Conclusion

Patients with bladder exstrophy exhibit persistent alterations in pelvic and gait kinematics in long-term follow-up, particularly in association with pelvic osteotomy and increased pubic diastasis. Despite these measurable deviations, patients maintained functional independence and reported no limitations in daily life, underscoring the adaptive capacity of the neuromuscular system. Quantitative gait analysis provides an objective means of detecting musculoskeletal sequelae and highlights the need for multidisciplinary, long-term surveillance strategies. Future prospective, multicenter studies are warranted to further clarify the biomechanical impact of osteotomy and pubic diastasis and to guide the development of tailored rehabilitation approaches.
