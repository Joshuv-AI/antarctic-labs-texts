Communication skills of residents: are they as good as they think?
Authors: Namra Qadeer Shaikh, Ali Aahil Noorali, Asma Altaf Hussain Merchant, Noreen Afzal, Maryam Pyar Ali Lakhdir, Komal Abdul Rahim, Syeda Fatima Shariq, Rida Ahmad, Saqib Kamran Bakhshi, Saad Bin Zafar Mahmood, Shayan Shah, Muhammad Rizwan Khan, Muhammad Tariq, Adil H Haider
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeTo evaluate the current communication skills of resident physicians and identify areas for improvement.Study designUsing a cross-sectional design, data from medical students, residents, fellows, and faculty were assimilated at one of the largest academic medical centers in a low-and-middle-income country via a self-administered, validated survey with a 5-point LIKERT scale. One-way ANOVA was used to compare intra-group means, followed by the Bonferroni test, with a p-value <0.05 considered significant. Heat maps using means were generated and color-coded to signify the level of communication skills expertise.ResultsA total of 320 participants (119 residents, 34 fellows, 78 medical students and 89 attendings) completed the survey. Most residents (58%) reported not having received formal communication skills training. Major barriers impeding effective patient-resident communication were long working hours, inadequate time, and difficulty understanding patients’ language (p < 0.001). Residents’ self-evaluation of communication skills was significantly higher than observer evaluations from faculty, fellows, and students, reflecting a lack of self-awareness and overestimation of communication expertise. Observer cohorts rated residents moderately on para-verbal communication skills while giving the lowest ratings for breaking bad news (mean 2.8, p-value <0.001) and residents’ experience in dealing with workplace conflicts (mean 2.8, p-value = 0.023).ConclusionsOur study identified a clear and critical need for a structured, longitudinal, competency-based communication skills program for residents. Targeted efforts towards bridging the gaps in communication skills identified in this study can improve trainee communication skills expertise and augment patient satisfaction rates to improve the quality of care in developing countries.KEYWORDS: Communication skills, curriculum, residents, fellows, graduate medical education

Introduction

Clear and effective patient-physician communication is a crucial component of patient-centric healthcare. A satisfying patient-physician encounter can translate to efficient delivery of better quality medical care and improved patient outcomes [1,2]. It can also augment overall patient satisfaction, facilitate efficacious and prompt diagnoses, and improve treatment compliance and continuity of care while reducing disparities and inequity in healthcare [1,3–5]. On the contrary, inadequate communication skills lead to a plethora of challenges and subpar clinical care in the form of increased psychological stress to the patient, avoidable medical errors, aggravated burden on emergency and inpatient services, and misunderstandings of potential health risks [6–9].

Training and evaluation of competencies are considered indispensable, especially during cross-cultural encounters in heterogeneous societies like the United States (US). Evidence-based communication skills training interventions in developed countries for trainee and practicing physicians have substantially improved patient satisfaction rates and healthcare outcomes [10,11] The paramount importance of these skills is strongly reflected in the competency criteria of national medical training certification boards across many countries. These include the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) model of the United States and Japan’s Primary Care Association Board Certification Examination. Curricula based on these models have shown consistent improvement in communication skills of trainees across the entire spectrum of medical specialties in a variety of settings.

In the context of Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs), dissimilarities in socioeconomic status and cultural backgrounds between physicians and patients inherent to the region have been shown to affect communication and clinical decision-making [12,13], advocating for the need to develop cultural competency among health care professionals. Additionally, in a study conducted on health workers’ knowledge and communication skills needs assessment in a developing country, 80% of the respondents described their communication skills as moderately sufficient and expressed a need for improvement [14]. However, there is a dearth of data from this region where little is known about patient-physician communication practices.

While there is adequate insight into the crucial role of communication skills training, little has been done to go beyond this perception. There is a troubling lack of formal communication training programs, and key areas of improvement that must be incorporated into tailored curricula have not been systematically identified yet. In order to develop similar but contextually relevant and culturally appropriate national education models for such regions, first an in-depth assessment of communication skills expertise, and the unique socio-cultural challenges of trainees is needed. Hence, this study aimed to acquire a holistic understanding of the communication practices of residents in a developing country.

Methods

Study design

To evaluate the communication skills capabilities of residents, we explored multidisciplinary perspectives from residents and other key informants. This was achieved by collating two types of data: residents’ self-evaluations and observer evaluations from three cohorts (medical students, fellows and attendings) from five specialties with the highest clinical volume and number of trainees in our institution. We employed a cross-sectional survey study design. Perceptions centered around the residents’ communication skills training and current proficiencies. Furthermore, the study also aimed to identify specific communication and interpersonal skills gaps that required the highest degree of reform. A detailed description of the methodology employed in this study has already been published [15].

Study setting

The study was conducted at the Aga Khan University Hospital Pakistan, which is one of the largest Academic Medical Centers (AMC) in a LMIC. This center is a 650-bed tertiary-care university hospital that became the first center in Pakistan to have received the Joint Commission International (JCI) accreditation for its globally recognized best practices in patient care and safety [16,17].

Data sources, participant enrollment and data collection

This study spanned 3 months, from June to September 2022. Data were collected using a three-section validated questionnaire that focused on the frequency of conflicts with patients and attendants, self-assessment of communication skills and perceived barriers to practicing good communication skills [18]. The questionnaire was slightly modified, and additional questions querying residents on prior communication skills training were also added. Similarly, for the observer cohorts (faculty, fellows, and medical students), questions from the same tool were modified to reflect the perceptions of individuals who routinely observed residents’ communication skills. The survey was disseminated from institutional login IDs using REDCap software for efficient and systematic data collection.

The survey was administered through a centralized email address designated for the study [15]. Participants were recruited from five specialties with the highest clinical volume at our institution via purposive sampling. These included Surgery, Obstetrics and Gynecology, Family Medicine, Internal Medicine and Pediatrics. Attendings at a position of Senior Instructor or above affiliated with these departments for at least 3 months were included. Similarly, residents and fellows from the aforementioned departments with a minimum of 3 months of training were included. Additionally, medical students who rotated with the respective departments for a minimum of 1 month, and nurses practicing within the same departments for at least 6 months were also included. Additionally, faculty members working as lecturers or part-time and medical students with 3 years of undergraduate education who had yet to complete their rotations in any of the specialties in our inclusion criteria were also excluded.

Statistical analysis

The survey leveraged a standard LIKERT scale, which converted descriptive qualifiers to a score, where strongly disagree = 1, disagree = 2, neutral = 3, agree = 4 and strongly agree = 5. Residents from years 1 and 2 were categorized as junior residents and years 3 and beyond were categorized as senior residents. Categorical variables have been reported as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables are reported as means and standard deviations. Means were first compared amongst all cohorts using a one-way ANOVA test, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Following that, the Bonferroni test was conducted to make a pair-wise comparison by practicing level, with residents kept as the reference cohort and a p-value of <0.05 considered significant. Means of each component of the tool for each cohort (medical students, residents, fellows and attendings) were then coded to construct a heatmap graded from red to green, denoting the spectrum of communication skills expertise from poor to excellent, respectively. The key for interpreting the heatmap varied for different components of the tool, subject to the overall theme being assessed. For components focusing on the frequency of conflicts and violence, green denoted minimum/complete absence, while red denoted frequent/near-daily occurrence. In contrast, for components exploring the practice of various verbal and paraverbal aspects of effective communication, green denoted the presence of such practices, while red denoted the absence of relevant aspects from residents’ daily communication practices. Cronbach's alpha was 0.95 for items pertaining to workplace conflict and 0.72 for all other items related to communication skills assessment, indicating high reliability and validity of the evaluated components.

Ethical approval and bias reduction

The study protocol underwent full review and received approval from the Ethical Review Committee at the Aga Khan University (ERC # 2021–6041-17126). Participants signed a digital informed consent form before filling out the online survey. Due diligence was paid to minimize risk of biases and protect patient privacy. An anonymous, validated, standardized questionnaire was used to minimize data collection bias [19,20], and participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any point without any adverse consequences. To protect participant privacy, all data were collected and stored securely in a password-protected database with access limited to the research team. Additionally, responses were de-identified to blind the research team and reduce analysis bias.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

In this study, on self and external assessment of communication skills of residents at a large urban AMC, 320 participants, including residents, medical students, fellows and attendings, responded to our survey. This included 36 surgical residents (hailing from various sub-specialties, including General Surgery, Cardiothoracic Surgery, Plastic Surgery, OBGYN, Neurosurgery, Urology, Orthopedics and Otolaryngology), 83 medical residents (from Internal Medicine, Cardiology, Neurology, Gastroenterology, Nephrology, Family Medicine, and Pediatrics), 34 fellows, 78 medical students and 89 attendings. Majority of the residents were year 1 trainees (30.25%), had a mean age of 28.55 ± 2.40 years, with a slight female predominance (53.78%) and belonged to the department of surgery (29.41%). The residents’ median weekly working hours ranged from 80 to 89. More than half of all residents (58%) had not received any prior formal communication skills training. Informal sources of learning these skills varied from observation of consultants (41%) and senior colleagues (32%), trial and error method (29%), self-study from videos (13%) or books and journals (15%). Pairwise comparisons using the Bonferroni test showed that residents rated themselves the highest on all survey items, while the observer cohorts ranked them lower overall, with the highest rating by fellows and the lowest by faculty among observer cohorts.

Section A of Table 2 shows a heatmap of residents’ workplace conflicts with patients and attendants. While residents reported experiencing an overall low frequency of conflicts (with minor conflicts occurring more frequently than major conflicts), the observer cohorts significantly disagreed and reported witnessing a higher frequency of conflicts between residents and patients\attendants (overall p-value was 0.023 and 0.009 for minor and major conflicts, respectively). The highest degree of disagreement was found between faculty and residents.

Among all domains in the section on self-assessment of communication skills, residents rated themselves higher than all observer cohorts, except on items related to communicating with patients and their attendants in in-door/high dependency units. Residents believed they had difficulty in communicating the course and prognosis of the disease multiple times for critically ill patients (mean score: 3.85, p < 0.05), answering the queries of the patients/attendants (mean score: 4.04, p < 0.05) and talking to attendants in detail after visiting the patient’s bed (mean score: 4.04, p < 0.05). Faculty and medical students had the highest differences in their ratings of residents’ communication skills competency compared to residents’ self-reported scores, with the latter reporting higher scores denoting self-confidence in their communication skills expertise. Medical students rated residents the lowest on breaking bad news to the patients and their families. Table 2 also compares residents’ self-reported scores and those of the evaluating cohorts on all items about various components of communication skills.

When analyzed by specialty, residents in surgical specialties (Surgery and OBGYN) rated themselves highest compared to the observing cohorts, with the most significant disagreement in ratings among faculty and residents. Similarly, medical residents also rated themselves highest compared to observer cohorts, with their self-reported rating significantly differing from those of faculty and fellows. In contrast, pediatric residents rated themselves lower compared to fellows who gave the highest rating, with significant disagreements between the scoring of faculty and fellows, and faculty and residents. When compared by training level of residents, junior residents (Year 1 and 2) were found to rate themselves significantly higher on their communication skills compared to senior residents (Year 3 and above) across most domains.

The last part of our survey inquired about the barriers impeding effective communication between residents and patients. Long working hours were the most significant contributing factor (mean score: 4.27, p < 0.001). This was followed by a lack of time (mean score: 4.02, p < 0.001) and difficulty in understanding the patient’s language (mean score: 3.70, p < 0.001). Another significant finding of our study was that residents believed they did not lack insight into the importance of patient-physician communication (mean score: 2.14, p < 0.001). In contrast, their evaluating cohorts thought that they did, as indicated by higher mean scores, which were statistically significant. All other barriers were also significant contributors, as shown in Table 2 (section C).

Overall, the results revealed a lack of self-awareness on the part of the residents, as they had marked themselves highest in all aspects of communication skills when their evaluators had not. As shown in Table 3, an overwhelming majority of 71% residents identified a strong need for a formal communication skills curriculum for residents with a focus on overall communication skills (60%) followed by dealing with angry patients and their attendants (48%), targeted training on breaking bad news (35%), and disclosing a diagnosis for the first time (27%).

Discussion

In this study exploring communication skills expertise amongst residents at one of the largest AMCs in our region, we found that an alarming majority had received no prior formal communication skills training, with a significantly higher rating in the residents’ self-assessment of communication skills as compared to the observations of medical students, fellows, and faculty. The study participants further identified a well-demonstrated need for a comprehensive, longitudinal, and structured curriculum around communication skills and provided recommendations for including suitable content and pedagogies within it.

Most residents denied receiving formal communication skills training programs before starting their residency. Instead, they relied on secondary sources such as observation, trial-and-error methods, and self-study. These findings are consistent with a similar study done in Peshawar, Pakistan, which showed that 95% of residents had no communication skills training at the undergraduate level [21]. Increased patient care responsibilities without any foundation in communication skills and the absence of formal training lead residents to learn these skills through observing their seniors or trial and error [22], as highlighted in our study. However, there was an improvement in the residents’ communication skills over the duration of their residency, which is consistent with other studies from developed countries showing senior residents to be more prepared in communication skills [23], owing to the development of their leadership and nontechnical skills over the years [24].

Residents’ self-evaluation revealed a lack of self-awareness, as they had marked themselves highest in all aspects of communication skills when their observers had not. This shows an overestimation of their skills. In contrast, participating residents from a similar study conducted in 2014 underestimated their capabilities compared to their faculty’s responses [25]. Similarly, self-assessment of communicative competence by residents in Russia revealed statistically significant lower scores compared to their expert evaluation [26]. A cross-sectional study in a Nigerian tertiary care hospital concluded that the residents had little or no knowledge of the protocol for breaking bad news. However, the self-rated competence was still high [26], resembling our results. This remarkable difference in residents’ self-perception and observation from key informants can be attributed to the Dunning-Kruger effect. As evidenced by our findings, residents lack the baseline knowledge and skill in communication due to a lack of formal national training, which might have led them to overestimate their own competence [27].

The fellows, faculty and medical students marked residents moderately in paraverbal communication skills, with the lowest ratings by students for breaking bad news and by faculty for residents’ experience in dealing with workplace conflicts. Sarwar et al. emphasized the lack of training in communication skills in their study from Lahore, Pakistan, which concluded that 56% of residents from the surgery and allied departments had not received any prior training on breaking bad news [22]. The ability to deal with workplace conflicts affects the quality of timely and effective patient care [28]. High incidents of conflicts highlight a lack of emotional intelligence among the residents. The ability to understand and manage patients’ emotions is crucial in the high-stress environment of healthcare [29]. A deficiency in these skills can lead to misunderstandings, frustration, and dissatisfaction, negatively impacting physician–patient relationships, thereby leading to conflicts. Poor performance in these domains reiterates the need to integrate breaking bad news and conflict management strategies within residents’ communication skills training.

Responses from the residents on the survey highlight a strong consensus on the need for a formal communication skills curriculum. This existing gap encourages residents to participate in communication skills programs, as shown by Sarwar et al. [22], where 65% of residents were eager to get training for breaking bad news. Furthermore, Friendly et al. showed interpersonal communication paramount for 100% of its respondent residents [30]. Such training can also benefit faculty who could practically reinforce their teachings after the curricular programs’ conclusion [31].

Our study shows integrated results of wide-ranging domains of in residents’ communication skills, ranging from paraverbal communication, understanding patient-doctor dialogues, interactions with colleagues, breaking bad news and dealing with workplace conflicts. Additionally, views regarding residents’ communication skills were obtained using the same survey from diverse cohorts to compare the introspective perspectives of residents and the observational perspectives of faculty, fellows and medical students rotating within the same specialty. This provided a holistic overview of the communication skills capabilities of residents and identified areas of improvement required to refine the quality of communication. Another strength of our study is the use of a quantitative survey developed and validated in a developing country with similar cultural practices as Pakistan [18].

However, this study has some limitations as well. Firstly, we report findings from a single-center cross-sectional study, which may have limited the generalizability of our findings. Moreover, as a private institution, our communication skills practices may differ from other public AMCs in Pakistan. As such, our findings may not align with institutions within those settings. However, given that residents training at the post-graduate level at this AMC comprise of doctors graduating from various medical schools across the country, an appalling percentage of whom have received no formal communication skills training, a partial insight into this nationwide deficit can be extrapolated. Moreover, as an equal-opportunity employer, medical graduates from all over the country join the training programs at our center, which adds some diversity to our study sample. The response rate to our survey was slightly less than optimal (42%, expected rate − 50%), which could be linked to the survey being disseminated online during the COVID-19 pandemic. Multiple potential causes, including the unprecedented surge in COVID-19-related research, policy updates, and institutional alert communications, may have led to our survey being overlooked amidst the influx of information. Additionally, access restrictions within hospital settings during the pandemic significantly hindered our ability to engage with participants directly. Healthcare workers were primarily focused on meeting clinical demands, leaving limited capacity for engagement in research. Personalized reminder emails and in-person follow-up of study participants could help improve response rates in future studies. Additionally, some of the components of the tool assessed internal behaviors of the residents (e.g., paying attention to non-verbal cues like gestures and facial expressions of the patient), making it difficult to detect by an external observer. As such, the differences in scoring of these components might not be reflective of the true behavior of the trainees.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first observational study from a developing country that uses a concomitant self and observer evaluation of residents’ communication skills. This study establishes a clear and critical need for a structured, longitudinal, competency-based communication skills program for residents to deliver higher-quality patient care and augment intra-team rapport. Gap assessment from this study will guide us in developing an integrated and targeted communication skills curriculum based on a modified Delphi consensus, as elaborated in the published protocol of this study [15]. This curriculum will offer a flexible model that training institutions in analogous environments can readily adopt. In collaboration with accreditation bodies, it can be seamlessly integrated into the national residency training curriculum, thereby uniformly enhancing and standardizing trainees’ communication skills across a broader spectrum. Additionally, our findings support the development of ongoing professional development opportunities focused on advanced communication techniques. Such initiatives could address the evolving challenges in patient care and adapt to the diverse needs of the healthcare workforce. Our study can serve as the foundation for more research on communication skills at other centers towards eliminating major barriers to practicing effective patient-physician communication. For LMICs, where distrust in healthcare systems alongside deficits in resources and policy is rampant, this will have ripple effects downstream on improving patient-provider relationships, patient outcomes, and health-seeking behaviors.
