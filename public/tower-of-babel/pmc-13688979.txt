Management of severe postpancreatectomy hemorrhage requiring emergency intervention: treatment pathways and surgical salvage in a single-center case series

Introduction

Postpancreatectomy hemorrhage (PPH) is one of the most serious and potentially life-threatening complications after pancreatic resection. Although relatively uncommon, PPH is associated with substantial morbidity and may result in increased mortality [1, 2]. To standardize its diagnosis and reporting, the International Study Group of Pancreatic Surgery (ISGPS) proposed a definition and grading system based on the timing of onset, location, and severity of bleeding [1]. This classification has since provided a common framework for the clinical evaluation and reporting of PPH [1, 2].

However, PPH is not a uniform clinical entity. According to the original ISGPS concept, early PPH is most commonly related to technical failure of hemostasis during the index operation or perioperative coagulopathy, whereas late PPH usually develops secondary to postoperative local complications, including postoperative pancreatic fistula, intra-abdominal infection, vascular erosion, and pseudoaneurysm formation [1]. Because of these differences in pathophysiology, the diagnostic process, therapeutic priority, and timing of intervention differ substantially between early and late hemorrhage [3, 4].

Recent advances in interventional radiology have markedly changed the management of severe PPH, particularly delayed arterial hemorrhage [4, 5]. In many contemporary series, interventional radiology has become the preferred first-line treatment for late PPH when endovascular access is feasible [3–5]. Endovascular embolization and stent-graft placement can provide rapid hemorrhage control while avoiding the technical difficulty of reoperation in a hostile postoperative field [4, 5]. Nevertheless, surgery remains indispensable in selected patients, particularly when hemodynamic instability, abdominal compartment syndrome, or the need for definitive source control precludes successful nonoperative management [3, 4].

Despite these advances, several important clinical issues remain unresolved. Severe PPH remains clinically diverse, and treatment decisions often require individualized judgment beyond simple application of the ISGPS classification [1, 6]. In addition, even when interventional radiology achieves technical hemostasis, definitive clinical recovery is not always assured, and some patients ultimately require surgical intervention [3, 4]. Furthermore, the current ISGPS grading system does not fully capture the therapeutic diversity of PPH, particularly within grade B hemorrhage, where clinically distinct situations may be grouped together despite marked differences in timing, mechanism, and management complexity [6]. Accordingly, further characterization of the clinical features, treatment pathways, and outcomes of severe PPH remains clinically relevant. In particular, the distinction between technical hemostasis and definitive clinical rescue may have important practical implications in patients requiring emergency intervention. The present study aimed to describe our single-center experience with severe PPH requiring emergency intervention, with particular focus on its timing, etiology, treatment pathway, and outcomes.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This was a retrospective single-center observational study conducted at our institution. Consecutive patients who underwent pancreatectomy between April 2009 and October 2025 were reviewed. The included procedures comprised pancreatic head resections, including pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD), subtotal stomach-preserving pancreaticoduodenectomy (SSPPD), pylorus-preserving pancreaticoduodenectomy (PPPD), and duodenum-preserving pancreatic head resection (DPPHR), as well as pancreatic body/tail resections, including distal pancreatectomy (DP) and spleen-preserving distal pancreatectomy (SPDP). Total pancreatectomy was not included in the present study. Surgical approach was categorized as open, laparoscopic, or robotic. During index pancreatectomy, relevant vessels were managed using ligation and/or hemo-clips according to the intraoperative situation. Stapling was not used for vascular ligation in these cases. Hemostatic agents and pedicled omental or falciform flaps were not routinely used for vessel stumps. Among the entire cohort, patients who developed severe PPH requiring emergency intervention were identified and included in the present analysis.

Definitions

PPH was defined and graded according to the criteria proposed by the International Study Group of Pancreatic Surgery (ISGPS) [1]. Hemorrhage was classified according to the time of onset, location, and severity. Early PPH was defined as hemorrhage occurring within 24 h after completion of the index operation, whereas late PPH was defined as hemorrhage occurring thereafter. According to the ISGPS classification, early severe hemorrhage was classified as grade B and late severe hemorrhage as grade C [1]. In this study, early hemorrhage categorized as technical failure referred to bleeding considered attributable to incomplete hemostasis at the index operation rather than to delayed vascular complications associated with postoperative local morbidity. Postoperative pancreatic fistula was defined according to the 2016 update of the ISGPS definition [7]. Bile leakage was defined according to the criteria of the International Study Group of Liver Surgery (ISGLS) [8].

Data collection

Clinical data were collected retrospectively from electronic medical records, operative reports, anesthetic records, radiologic findings, and postoperative charts. The following variables were reviewed: age, sex, underlying disease, type of index operation, surgical approach, timing of hemorrhage onset, clinical presentation, associated postoperative complications, bleeding source, treatment modality, and clinical outcome. Associated postoperative complications of interest included postoperative pancreatic fistula, bile leakage, pseudoaneurysm formation, and other intra-abdominal infectious complications.

Management strategy

Management of severe PPH was determined according to the timing of onset, hemodynamic status, imaging findings, and associated postoperative conditions. All 16 patients in this series underwent contrast-enhanced CT evaluation before intervention. In principle, interventional radiology was prioritized for delayed arterial hemorrhage when arterial bleeding was radiologically identifiable and endovascular access was feasible. Primary surgery was selected in patients with early hemorrhage suspected to be related to technical failure of hemostasis or in those requiring immediate operative intervention because of hemodynamic instability or overall clinical deterioration. Surgical salvage after initial interventional radiology was considered when endovascular hemostasis alone was insufficient to achieve decompression, source control, or physiological stabilization. Interventional radiologic procedures were performed by interventional radiologists at our institution.

Outcomes

The variables of interest included the timing of hemorrhage onset, associated postoperative complications, bleeding sources, treatment pathways, and clinical outcomes, with particular attention to patients requiring surgical salvage after initial interventional radiology. Continuous variables are presented as median (range), and categorical variables are presented as number and percentage.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Nagasaki University Hospital (approval number: 25121806). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Results

Incidence and overall characteristics of severe postpancreatectomy hemorrhage

During the study period, 750 pancreatectomies were performed at our institution, including 521 pancreaticoduodenectomies and 229 distal pancreatectomies. Minimally invasive approaches were used in 180 pancreaticoduodenectomies and 137 distal pancreatectomies. Among the 16 patients with severe postpancreatectomy hemorrhage, 4 pancreaticoduodenectomy cases and 2 distal pancreatectomy cases occurred after minimally invasive procedures. Among the entire cohort, 16 patients developed severe postpancreatectomy hemorrhage requiring emergency intervention, yielding an overall incidence of 2.1%. The incidence was 2.7% after pancreaticoduodenectomy and 0.9% after distal pancreatectomy. The clinical characteristics of the 16 patients are summarized in Table 1. According to the ISGPS classification, 4 patients had early severe grade B hemorrhage and 12 had late severe grade C hemorrhage.

Timing and clinical presentation

Early severe PPH occurred within 24 h after surgery, whereas late severe PPH developed after 24 h. In this cohort, most cases were classified as late severe PPH. The initial manifestations included bleeding from the drain, hemodynamic deterioration, and radiological findings suggestive of active bleeding or pseudoaneurysm formation. Several patients had concomitant postoperative complications, including bile leakage and postoperative pancreatic fistula.

Etiology and bleeding sources

The etiology of hemorrhage differed according to the timing of onset. Among the 4 patients with early severe PPH, the cause of hemorrhage was categorized as technical failure. Among the 12 patients with late severe PPH, the causes included postoperative pancreatic fistula, bile leakage, pseudoaneurysm, and other local postoperative complications, as summarized in Tables 1 and 2.

The bleeding sources included the gastroduodenal artery stump, branches of the hepatic artery, and other visceral arterial branches. Pseudoaneurysm was identified in 7 patients.

Initial management and treatment pathways

Initial treatment consisted of surgery first in 3 patients, interventional radiology only in 10 patients, and interventional radiology followed by surgery in 3 patients. Interventional radiology was selected in patients with radiologically identifiable arterial bleeding and feasible endovascular access, whereas surgery was selected as the initial treatment in selected patients according to the clinical condition.

Interventional radiology achieved hemostasis in several patients; however, subsequent surgery was required in 3 patients. No patient required repeat interventional radiology after the initial endovascular procedure. In the three early hemorrhage cases requiring reoperation, bleeding control was achieved by relaparotomy with direct ligation of the bleeding point.

Ninety-day mortality occurred in 1 patient. No patient required re-intervention after definitive bleeding control. No patient developed hepatic ischemia or liver failure after hepatic artery embolization or covered stent placement. Hospital length of stay is summarized in Table 2. All patients who survived to discharge were discharged home.

Discussion

The present study describes the clinical characteristics, treatment pathways, and outcomes of severe PPH requiring emergency intervention in a single high-volume center. Three findings are particularly relevant. First, most cases were delayed hemorrhages associated with postoperative local complications, whereas early severe hemorrhage was relatively uncommon. Second, the underlying mechanisms and treatment requirements varied substantially across patients, indicating that severe PPH should not be regarded as a single uniform entity. Third, although interventional radiology played a central role in the management of delayed arterial bleeding, additional surgery remained necessary in selected patients to achieve definitive clinical rescue.

The predominance of late severe hemorrhage in our series is consistent with the conceptual framework proposed by the International Study Group of Pancreatic Surgery (ISGPS) [1]. Early hemorrhage is generally considered to reflect technical failure of hemostasis during the index operation or perioperative coagulopathy, whereas late hemorrhage usually develops in association with postoperative local complications such as postoperative pancreatic fistula, intra-abdominal infection, vascular erosion, and pseudoaneurysm formation [1]. Our findings aligned with this framework. In the present cohort, early severe hemorrhage accounted for only a minority of cases and was typically attributed to technical failure, whereas late severe hemorrhage represented the majority and was frequently accompanied by postoperative pancreatic fistula, bile leakage, or pseudoaneurysm.

Interventional radiology has become the preferred first-line treatment for many cases of delayed arterial PPH [3–5, 9]. This shift reflects both advances in endovascular techniques and the recognized difficulty of relaparotomy in the postoperative pancreatic field [4, 5]. The representative patient shown in Fig. 1 illustrates this principle: a pseudoaneurysm at the gastroduodenal artery stump associated with bile leakage was successfully excluded by covered stent placement in the common hepatic artery. This case illustrates the value of interventional radiology as a minimally invasive and anatomically targeted treatment modality for delayed arterial hemorrhage.

The treatment pathways observed in this series represented clinically distinct scenarios rather than a single uniform management sequence. One of the central practical observations of the present study was that technical hemostasis did not necessarily translate into definitive clinical rescue. In several patients, active arterial bleeding was successfully controlled by interventional radiology, yet further surgical management was still required. The representative patient shown in Figs. 2 and 3 illustrates this issue clearly. Although embolization successfully eliminated contrast extravasation from a branch of the replaced right hepatic artery, the patient subsequently required laparotomy for abdominal compartment syndrome caused by massive intra-abdominal hemorrhage and was ultimately treated with open abdomen management. In this situation, the clinical challenge extended beyond control of arterial bleeding and required decompression and staged abdominal management. This distinction between “hemostatic success” and “clinical rescue” may be particularly relevant in severe PPH and should be considered when evaluating the apparent effectiveness of interventional treatment.

This concept also has practical implications. In severe PPH, particularly delayed hemorrhage related to fistula-associated vascular complications, direct reoperation alone may not always provide the fastest or most definitive solution. The postoperative abdominal cavity is often a hostile operative field because of inflammation, adhesions, hematoma, and ongoing contamination. As shown in Fig. 3 and Supplementary Video 1, even after laparotomy, direct identification of the bleeding source may be technically difficult. Severe PPH should therefore not be framed simply as a binary choice between interventional radiology and surgery, but rather as a staged rescue strategy in which each modality serves a complementary role. In many patients, interventional radiology offers the most effective initial approach for rapid hemorrhage control, whereas surgery becomes necessary when decompression, source control, or physiological rescue cannot be achieved by interventional radiology alone [3, 4].

The present findings also underscore both the value and the limitations of the current ISGPS classification. The ISGPS definition has provided a widely accepted framework for standardizing the diagnosis and reporting of PPH [1, 2]. Nevertheless, several authors have noted that its grading system does not fully reflect the heterogeneity of clinical severity, particularly within grade B hemorrhage [6, 10]. Our findings support this concern. In practice, management decisions were determined not only by ISGPS grade, but also by hemodynamic instability, associated postoperative complications, bleeding mechanism, and the need for decompression or source control. Thus, although the ISGPS classification remains highly valuable as a common reporting framework, it should not be used as a substitute for individualized clinical judgment.

Beyond the technical management of hemorrhage itself, these findings also have implications for the organization of pancreatic surgical care. Successful rescue from severe PPH requires immediate access to multiple therapeutic resources, including interventional radiology, emergency reoperation, intensive care, and multidisciplinary postoperative support [11–14]. Severe PPH is a representative example of a complication in which outcome depends not only on the bleeding event itself, but also on the institutional ability to recognize deterioration and provide timely rescue. Our experience underscores the importance of multidisciplinary rescue capability, including both advanced interventional radiology and timely surgical salvage, in centers performing complex pancreatic surgery.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective analysis conducted at a single center, which may limit generalizability. Second, the number of severe PPH cases was relatively small. Third, because the study specifically focused on severe hemorrhage requiring emergency intervention, it does not reflect the entire clinical spectrum of PPH. Finally, treatment selection was not fully protocolized throughout the study period and may have been influenced by temporal changes in institutional strategy, technical experience, and resource availability. In addition, endovascular techniques, covered stent availability, minimally invasive pancreatectomy, and postoperative fistula management likely evolved substantially between 2009 and 2025, which may have affected both treatment selection and clinical outcomes. Precise time intervals from hemorrhage recognition to intervention could not be analyzed uniformly because the exact recognition time was not consistently documented in all cases.

Conclusion

Severe PPH is a heterogeneous clinical entity requiring individualized management. Although interventional radiology is the principal first-line treatment for delayed arterial hemorrhage, technical hemostasis alone may not always achieve definitive clinical rescue, and selected patients still require prompt surgical salvage. These findings underscore the importance of multidisciplinary rescue capability in high-volume pancreatic centers.
