Patient and health care practitioner experiences and perspectives on expanded carrier screening: A scoping review
Authors: Sakina Walji, Sahana Kukan, Amanda L Terry, Judith Belle Brown, June C Carroll
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarrier screening identifies individuals/couples at risk for autosomal recessive conditions, and expanded or universal approaches are increasingly being recommended. Implementation poses clinical, ethical, and logistical challenges that remain underexplored. This scoping review synthesized patient/public and health care practitioner experiences and perspectives on expanded carrier screening (ECS) and universal carrier screening. A search of 4 databases identified 1433 records, plus 19 additional studies. After screening and full-text review (112 articles), 45 studies were included in this review. Content analysis was conducted, and findings were mapped to the theoretical domains framework to guide implementation. Six major categories were identified: (1) attitudes and awareness of screening; (2) barriers and concerns regarding ECS decision-making; (3) personal and broader public-health benefits; (4) ethical, psychological, and equity considerations; (5) infrastructure, clinical, and policy considerations; and (6) research gaps and future directions. Key theoretical domains framework domains included beliefs about consequences, environmental context and resources, social/professional role and identity, and emotion. Engagement with ECS/ universal carrier screening is shaped by intersecting emotional, ethical, and systemic factors. The findings support behaviorally informed strategies and identify critical priorities for future research, education, and policy development.Keywords: Carrier Screening, Ethical legal and social implications, Reproductive screening, Scoping review

Introduction

Some genetic diseases, such as cystic fibrosis, can be caused by a single gene variant. Carrier screening refers to genetic testing of genes to assess for such variants. In autosomal recessive conditions, a person typically shows no symptoms unless they inherit 2 copies of the faulty gene, 1 from each parent. For couples who are both heterozygotes for autosomal recessive conditions, there is a 25% chance their offspring will be affected by the condition. Health care practitioners (HCPs) may offer carrier screening to individuals contemplating pregnancy or to those who are pregnant. Carrier screening can strengthen reproductive autonomy and support reproductive choices aligned with the values of an individual or couple. Reproductive choices available when both partners are identified as heterozygotes include prenatal diagnosis, in vitro fertilization/intracytoplasmic sperm injection combined with preimplantation genetic testing, gamete donation, adoption, or refraining from having children together.1

“Expanded carrier screening” (ECS) refers to testing a large panel of genes associated with autosomal recessive or X-linked recessive conditions. In the United States, carrier screening may be offered in clinical practice, and coverage varies by insurer and state policy; while not publicly funded in Canada, this screening can be requested privately. “Universal carrier screening” (UCS) refers to carrier screening that is made available to all individuals regardless of ancestry and may include screening for only a limited number of diseases or an expanded panel, as with ECS.

The estimated risk of a couple having a child affected by an autosomal recessive or X-linked condition ranges from 1.14% to 6.11%, depending on genetic ancestry.2 In Canada, limited screening typically occurs for individuals identified by their HCP as being at high risk for genetic conditions in the peripartum period because of family history, ethnicity, or consanguinity. Although recent U.S. data are limited, a 2018 survey found that only 27% of U.S. obstetric providers offered ECS universally, whereas most offered screening based on ethnicity, reflecting ongoing variability in practice.3

Several professional committees have recommended UCS for certain genetic conditions, such as cystic fibrosis.4,5 Between 2015 and 2016, major U.S. and Canadian professional bodies recommended offering carrier screening to all reproductive-age individuals, regardless of ethnicity or family history, enabled by advances in genetic testing that allow screening for many conditions from a single sample and driven by the difficulty in obtaining accurate ethnicity and family history.6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Similarly, in 2016, the European Society of Human Genetics recommended offering ECS to couples or individuals without increased a priori risk but emphasized responsible implementation through preconception counseling, informed reproductive choice, and careful consideration of clinical validity, utility, and ethical implications.1

The recent release of the Canadian College of Medical Geneticists’ position statement strongly supporting the introduction of provincial screening programs for reproductive carrier screening and expanded access makes this a critical moment to understand the current landscape of UCS (expanded and limited).11

Before UCS can be widely implemented, clinical and ethical questions remain, including which conditions to include because some are rare, have variable outcomes, or have a delayed onset.7 Current guidelines do not identify which conditions should be screened for, but most recommend screening for certain conditions, such as cystic fibrosis and spinal muscular atrophy.6,10

Harm may arise from testing, declining testing, or a lack of counseling. Although UCS promotes reproductive autonomy, it also raises ethical concerns regarding the termination law and may be perceived as disability-based discrimination.12

There are implementation considerations, such as when and how screening should be offered. Pre- and post-test counseling must be considered, along with exploring patient expectations regarding UCS implementation. Additionally, it is important to assess HCP comfort and competence and the potential impact on the workload of genetic specialists.1

In 2020, Steijvoort et al13 completed a systematic review involving 12 studies on ECS uptake, primarily focused on utilization rates with only limited exploration of patient experiences. Best et al14 completed a systematic review to assess HCP-identified barriers and enablers in implementing reproductive UCS/ECS. Three main themes transpired: the equity of service and uptake, practitioner beliefs and knowledge, and resources available. Since this review, new studies have been published assessing both patient and HCP perceptions of UCS and ECS.15, 16, 17 Additionally, a systematic review was done in 2023 to assess the attitudes of professional stakeholders towards the implementation of reproductive carrier screening.18 They focused on identifying key clinical and programmatic considerations relevant to reproductive carrier screening implementation and policy development. However, its scope was limited to specific stakeholder groups and did not examine the perspectives of patients/the public. To address this gap, we conducted a scoping review to synthesize evidence on patients/the public’s and HCPs perspectives on UCS and ECS. This work is also timely because of the continued growth of research and policy interest in this area.

Objective

Because UCS and ECS are increasingly considered in diverse health care systems, we must understand the experiences and perspectives of patients/the public and HCPs regarding UCS and/or ECS to make suggestions for implementation into practice. Our objective was to complete a scoping review to identify, review, and summarize the literature regarding HCP and patient/public experiences and their perspectives on UCS and ECS in the perinatal population.19 Our secondary objective was to identify areas for future research.

Materials and Methods

We used the Arksey and O’Malley methodological framework.20,21 We performed a scoping review to survey the existing literature on patient and HCP experiences and perspectives related to UCS (both expanded and limited). Given the wide scope of this topic, we did not restrict the review to a specific study design.20 Our goal was to summarize and disseminate the findings related to patient and HCP perspectives on UCS, to aid in the implementation of carrier screening.20 We followed six steps: identifying the research question, identifying relevant studies, selecting studies to be included, charting the data, and collating and summarizing the results.22 We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses for Scoping Reviews checklist.23

Search strategy and identifying relevant studies

An initial search was conducted on Medline, PubMed, Embase, and Scopus in January 2025. Search terms related to carrier screening and patient and HCP experiences and perspectives were used and developed with librarian input (see Supplemental file 1). Articles were reviewed to identify additional terminology or keywords.

No limits were applied to our search. Once the search was completed, filters were applied to include only English studies published after 2001. We chose 2001 as a starting date because this marked the time when professional committees recommended UCS for select genetic conditions, likely prompting increased research activity.5 To assess applicability, 20 abstracts were randomly selected for review, and we cross-checked the full list of retrieved articles to ensure known studies were captured. A gray literature search was done manually before title and abstract screening by reviewing reports and publications of organizations, such as the Society of Obstetricians and Gynecologists of Canada and the Canadian College of Medical Geneticists. Retrieved references were uploaded into Covidence, an online platform for managing systematic and scoping review workflows, and duplicate articles were removed.24

Study selection

Title and abstract screening were conducted by 2 reviewers (S.W., S.K.). Eligibility criteria included: (1) English language, (2) focus on UCS and/or ECS, (3) related to the experiences, perspectives, or attitudes of patients/public or HCPs, (4) related to carrier screening in the perinatal period, and (5) published after 2001. The reviewers pilot-screened 15 abstracts together to ensure consistency, then screened the remaining records independently. Disagreements were resolved through discussion. Titles without abstracts were excluded, and reviewer decisions were blinded throughout.

Articles meeting the eligibility criteria underwent full-text screening by 2 independent reviewers (S.W., S.K.). Opinion pieces, case studies, editorials, commentaries, annual reports, systematic or scoping reviews, and newspaper articles were excluded. Studies focusing on individuals affected by a specific disease were also eliminated. We also screened the reference lists of all articles eligible for full data extraction to identify additional relevant articles. The additional articles identified (n = 19) underwent the same screening and extraction process.

Data extraction and charting

A form for data collection was developed by S.W. and reviewed by S.K., A.L.T., and J.C.C. (Supplemental File 2). It was pilot-tested by S.W. and S.K. using 3 selected articles and then revised to ensure consistency and capture of relevant data. The remaining articles were randomly assigned to the 2 reviewers for full data extraction. The categories included author, title, year, study design, country, participant numbers, UCS/ECS focus, outcome of interest, population studied, key findings, limitations, and reviewer-identified gaps.22

Data synthesis

Content analysis focused on extracted data on study findings, gaps, and opportunities. (See Supplemental file 2). Data were analyzed inductively by the principal investigator (S.W.), with categories generated directly from the extracted data. After analysis of the first 3 studies, the research team met to review and define the initial categories. These categories were iteratively refined during the analysis of subsequent studies. Following analysis of an additional subset (n = 5), the team reconvened to consolidate the categories. Analysis of the remaining studies was completed using the refined categories, with ongoing team discussions to ensure consistency. Categories were developed by identifying patterns across the data.25

The team engaged in reflexive discussions to consider how their professional backgrounds might influence the review. S.W., a family physician with genomics research experience, and S.K., a non-clinical research associate, contributed complementary clinical and non-clinical perspectives across all study stages; S.W. conducted the thematic analysis. Their differing viewpoints supported balanced screening and data extraction. S.W. and J.C.C., both family physician researchers, contributed insights into feasibility and workflow, whereas A.L.T, an epidemiologist and mixed-methods researcher, provided methodological and system-level perspectives that challenged clinical assumptions and grounded interpretations in the data. This interdisciplinary approach helped mitigate discipline-specific bias and supported a balanced interpretation of the findings.

We registered our protocol with the Open Science Framework on 17 January, 2025 (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/5AYN7).

The Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF)26 guided the synthesis, offering a structured approach to understanding behavioral, cognitive, and contextual influences on health decisions. Organizing findings across 14 domains (eg, knowledge, skills, social influences, environmental context), the TDF allowed for a structured interpretation of patient and HCP experiences with carrier screening.

This framework was especially relevant given the review’s behavioral focus, enabling the identification of screening barriers and facilitators. Applying the TDF lays the groundwork for targeted, evidence-based implementation strategies.11

Results

The combined searches yielded 1433 articles, with 19 additional articles identified through prior knowledge and reference checks. After excluding non-English and pre-2001 publications, 1426 articles remained. After removal of duplicates (n = 904), 522 articles were left for screening. Title and abstract review excluded 410 articles, leaving 112 articles for full-text review. Of these, 45 articles met the eligibility criteria and underwent data extraction (see Figure 1).

Our study identified 6 key categories related to ECS/UCS (Table 3). The 6 major categories were: (1) attitudes and awareness of screening; (2) barriers and concerns regarding ECS decision-making; (3) personal and broader public-health benefits; (4) ethical, psychological, and equity considerations; (5) infrastructure, clinical, and policy considerations; and (6) research gaps and future directions. Attitudes and awareness of carrier screening

Across studies, awareness of ECS among the public remains low.28, 29, 30, 31, 32 For example, only 4.7% of women attending an obstetric clinic,29 and less than half of surveyed reproductive-aged women in the U.S. were aware of ECS30 (The term “women” is used in this paper when reflecting the terminology employed by the original studies. We recognize that some individuals discussed may not identify as women, and we acknowledge the importance of gender-inclusive language). Similar gaps were reported among pregnant Latina individuals17 and pregnant women in countries such as China, France, and the Netherlands,31,33,34 which were raised as barriers to implementation.34, 35, 36 HCPs and health policy professionals emphasized that adequate genetic literacy before conception is essential for informed decision-making and effective counseling.34, 35, 36, 37

Awareness among HCPs was variable,3,15,35,38 but 1 study reported that reproductive endocrinologists were more likely to offer ECS than maternal-fetal medicine specialists,3 and about half of obstetricians felt comfortable discussing positive results.3 A Dutch qualitative study highlighted that many reproductive HCPs lacked familiarity with ECS initiatives and were not offering such services.15

Attitudes toward ECS/UCS were generally positive among patients and HCPs.17,30,38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43 A majority of patients supported ECS in principle and expressed interest in learning about ECS from HCPs.31 In a Belgian population study, 86% of men and women viewed ECS as acceptable when planning children,44 whereas a French study showed that 91% of respondents supported ECS access, albeit with varied views on regulation.33

Among HCPs, there were varying opinions on the composition of screening panels45, 46, 47, 48; in 1 study it was found that genetic counselors favored broad ECS panels,49 whereas in another study it was found that obstetricians tended to favor ethnicity-based screening and expressed hesitancy pending further evidence of clinical utility.3 HCPs expressed concerns about time constraints, lack of specialist support, and potential patient anxiety.3,40

Support for ECS was primarily driven by informational and reproductive motives.17,30,43,48,50,51 Individuals valued gaining genetic risk information for planning and preparation. Patients viewed ECS as offering reassurance and greater control over family planning,17,43,52 whereas HCPs highlighted its role in providing meaningful information for informed reproductive decisions.41,42 Concerns about the limitations of ancestry-based screening also motivated support for a universal, publicly delivered approach to ensure equitable access and to maintain trust in test reliability.15,16,48

Barriers and concerns regarding ECS decision-making

A consistent determinant of ECS uptake was perceived personal risk.31,41,44,51,53, 54, 55, 56 Participants across studies equated the absence of a family history of genetic conditions with low risk, resulting in lower uptake of ECS,31,41,44,53, 54, 55 and studies assessing experiences of those identified as heterozygotes found that participants were surprised at the results.51,56 Those who perceived an elevated risk based on family history, ethnicity, or past genetic consultations were more inclined to consider ECS.41,44,57,58 A lack of familiarity with genetic conditions was cited as a barrier, leading participants to view ECS as unnecessary.42,59

Religious and ideological beliefs also influenced ECS decision-making, albeit less frequently. Some participants expressed that ECS conflicted with their faith or moral values, and a small proportion rejected testing on these grounds,54,55,59 whereas 1 study assessing HCP perspectives reported that religion would not affect their views about ECS or abortion.38 For others, the belief that ECS results would not impact their reproductive decisions reduced the perceived utility.52,53

Cost and logistical factors consistently limited ECS uptake. Concerns included out-of-pocket expenses, insurance gaps, and perceived inequities in access.29,31,53,60 Some participants explicitly favored sequential testing (testing the second partner only if the first partner is identified as a heterozygote) to reduce the cost burden, especially in resource-limited settings.60

Patients often declined ECS cause of logistical challenges, including appointment scheduling, travel, or the perceived burden of participation.52,58

Additionally, there were concerns that patients may not fully understand the results, such as if both partners are heterozygotes for different conditions.35,61, 62, 63 HCPs shared concerns that counseling and follow-up added strain to their existing workloads.35,36,49 HCPs also raised concerns about downstream costs and equity issues, particularly for those who might not be able to afford reproductive interventions, such as in vitro-fertilization with preimplantation genetic diagnosis, following a positive result.15,64

Several studies found that both patients and HCPs viewed potential anxiety related to results as a barrier to participation.49,53,54,57 Among general population respondents, fear of knowing one’s carrier status led to test refusal in a notable minority.33,41 Anxiety was not exclusive to patients; genetic counselors themselves cited potential emotional distress as a reason they might decline ECS.49

Personal and broader public health benefits

Patients and HCPs recognized ECS as enhancing reproductive autonomy by providing actionable information to guide decisions regarding prenatal diagnosis, preimplantation testing, gamete donation, or pregnancy continuation.16,29,31,37,57,62,64,65 Patients and the public valued ECS for enabling informed, individualized reproductive choices and for helping manage genetic risks.30,31,54,59,66

ECS was valued for its potential to prevent the birth of children with severe genetic conditions and to prepare families emotionally and practically.15,16,29,31,32,39,55,60,63,66 Patients viewed ECS as offering reassurance and facilitating early planning, whereas HCPs emphasized the reduced emotional and financial burden on families.17,41,43,53,54,59

HCPs, policy makers, and patients noted that the preventative approach of ECS aligns with public health goals.17,37,48,52,59,61 ECS was seen by geneticists as a fair alternative to individualized screening that could reach high-risk individuals regardless of background.37,61 Non-pregnant couples also highlighted potential reductions in long-term health care costs and a sense of societal or parental responsibility in promoting genetic literacy and responsible reproductive planning.52,59

Ethical, psychological, and equity considerations

ECS raises ethical questions about reproductive autonomy and potential social pressure to undergo testing or avoid having children with disabilities.16,32,33,43,54,59,65,66 Among HCPs, policymakers, and ethico-legal experts, the voluntary and non-directive nature of ECS and the need for well-informed consent were repeatedly emphasized.34,63,65

Participants from the general population and health care workers feared that ECS could foster “perfect society” ideals or stigmatize disability and influence partner choice or pregnancy termination.47,59,66 General practitioners cautioned that ECS might indirectly shift moral responsibility onto individuals who forgo testing and subsequently have children with genetic conditions.37,63 A French study assessing the public’s perceptions of ECS demonstrated that a minority (9%) of the public expressed concerns about the implications for pregnancy decisions and societal attitudes towards disability.33 Participants, including general practitioners, stressed the need for neutral, balanced information to support reflection and informed choice.59,63

Psychological consequences of ECS, particularly anxiety and decisional conflict, were frequently mentioned by patients and the public, particularly following positive or uncertain results.42,54,59,66 Concerns about misinterpreting carrier status as a diagnosis or equating a negative result with a healthy child were reported by both patients and HCPs.59,66

Some HCPs warned of the increased medicalization of pregnancy and the emotional burden placed on prospective parents.63,67 Studies have reported increased perceived and actual patient anxiety associated with waiting for results, receiving positive results, or discordant decisions between partners in the context of complex results, and, in some instances, this anxiety may lead patients to decline testing,49,51,54,56, 57, 58,62 whereas other studies have reported reduced anxiety after receiving results.68 HCPs consistently underscored the need for neutral counseling and psychological support.15,16,49,64,66

Concerns about stigmatization and the reinforcement of ableist attitudes were frequently raised by patients, HCPs, and ethico-legal experts.16,31,37,65 HCPs cautioned that ECS might extend selective choices beyond severe conditions, raising fears of eugenics and genetic partner selection over emotional compatibility.16,65

HCPs, policy makers, and ethico-legal experts noted the potential risk of stigma towards those declining testing or identified as heterozygotes. Ethical experts advocated for the inclusion of disability perspectives in defining ECS panel content to balance prevention goals with respect for autonomy.37,46

General practitioners, midwives, policy makers, and patients warned that language barriers, cost, and low health literacy could limit access among disadvantaged groups.16,34,46,53

ECS uptake was higher among individuals with higher education, raising concerns about unequal reproductive choice.53 HCPs also noted that preconception-only models risk excluding those with unplanned pregnancies, which are more common in populations with fewer resources.16

Current ECS panels may inadequately represent diverse genetic backgrounds, prompting calls for greater inclusivity.35,64 Experts have called for parallel investment in disability support and maternal-child health to ensure equitable benefits.46

Infrastructure, clinical, and policy considerations

HCPs cautioned that ECS may create unrealistic expectations, with some individuals assuming that all genetic conditions are included and that it can yield uncertain or low-relevance results, causing anxiety without clear benefit.35,64 Limited genetic literacy among patients and professionals was viewed as an obstacle to large-scale implementation.34,59

HCPs and policymakers highlighted insufficient infrastructure, workforce shortages, funding limitations, and the absence of standardized clinical pathways.15,34,48

Lack of standardized, evidence-based panel criteria remains a major barrier. HCPs and experts recommended limited inclusion for severe, prevalent conditions with major reproductive implications.45,47,49 However, defining “severity” and determining whether treatability or age of onset should influence the inclusion criteria remain controversial.48,67

Although some professionals and patients supported broad panels, others preferred a stepwise approach or smaller panels to preserve informed consent and prevent overwhelming patients.35,45,51 HCPs, patients, and policymakers agreed that panel content should be set by multidisciplinary expert groups under national guidance.48

Patients, HCPs, and policymakers consistently underscored the need for accessible, nondirective pre- and post-test counseling.16,35,36,48,54,56,60,64,67 Genetic counselors were considered ideal providers, though task-sharing with trained general practitioners or nurses could improve scalability.36,49 Among HCPs, there was no consensus as to who would be best suited to provide ECS; general practitioners were viewed as well positioned to deliver ECS given their accessibility and knowledge of patients, but they cited limited time and genetic expertise as barriers,36 whereas genetic counselors were viewed as knowledgeable in this topic area.49,62 Despite these challenges, most couples favored in-person pre-test counseling.60

Patients frequently requested clearer explanations of inheritance, disease severity, test accuracy, and the implications of carrier status.28,54 HCPs expressed concern about the complexity of conveying information for expanded panels.67 Geneticists recommended layered educational materials, including audiovisual or digital tools, to support comprehension.35

Both patients and HCPs preferred preconception testing to allow broader reproductive options.16,31,35,62 General practitioners warned that restricting ECS to the preconception stage could worsen inequities, given the prevalence of unplanned pregnancies in disadvantaged groups.16

Although some couples supported joint testing,28,32 others, particularly those from the general population, preferred individual results.55,59 Simultaneous testing was viewed as more efficient, whereas sequential testing could reduce costs by limiting follow-up when the first partner tested negative.46,60

Cultural factors, such as the limited normalization of preconception care in some countries (eg, the Netherlands), continued to constrain uptake.34 HCPs concluded that flexible delivery models integrating the preconception and prenatal stages were widely recommended to enhance equity.35

Research gaps and future directions

The studies included in this scoping review reported research gaps and identified future directions regarding ECS.

Four key areas for future patient-centered research were identified. First, understanding patient attitudes, preferences, and decision-making in response to ECS, especially in diverse and underrepresented populations41,55,57; second, exploring nuanced parent and couple perspectives, including emotional responses to carrier status and potential misunderstandings regarding personal health risks68; third, assessing barriers between intention and actual ECS uptake,31 and the impact of early education on awareness and decision-making30,31; and fourth, examining how prior genetic testing influences interpretation of results43,59 and preferences for education and counseling to inform scalable interventions.

Studies have indicated that more research is needed to explore the perspectives and training needs of HCPs involved in ECS. Although much of the literature has focused on geneticists, several studies have highlighted a need to capture the views of general practitioners, midwives, obstetricians, and other frontline HCPs, who may be responsible for offering ECS.3,35,48

Future studies should assess comfort levels of HCPs, resource needs, and readiness to deliver ECS, including how best to train nonspecialist HCPs and incorporate ECS into routine care pathways.3,33,52 Exploration of HCP opinions toward commercial ECS products is also warranted, particularly regarding test quality, panel composition, and ethical standards.64

Finally, international comparative studies could offer insight into how health care context shapes HCP perspectives and influences ECS delivery.49

Operationalizing ECS requires research to guide best practices. Key priorities identified by participants in these studies include evaluating the composition of commercial ECS panels and establishing clear, evidence-based inclusion criteria.45,64 Future studies should involve prospective parents in panel design, ensuring transparency and responsiveness to public values.45,64

There was no consensus on the optimal time to offer UCS/ECS, balancing the benefits of preconception testing with the prevalence of unplanned pregnancies. Studies highlighted the potential of alternative models for offering UCS/ECS, such as digital or community-based approaches.29,30

The need for proactive, multidisciplinary research to address ethical and social issues, such as stigmatization, misinterpretation of carrier status, and the societal effects of reducing certain conditions’ birth prevalence, was emphasized.47,61

It was recommended that future studies explore how prevention-oriented ECS aligns with or challenges its’ goal of supporting reproductive choice over population-level outcomes.16

The inclusion of diverse perspectives from patients, HCPs, ethicists, and policymakers was deemed essential for building consensus on acceptable ECS models.61

Mapping findings to the TDF

Applying the TDF enabled the identification of psychological, social, and environmental factors that can shape ECS and UCS implementation strategies. Findings were most frequently mapped to 4 domains: Beliefs about consequences, environmental context and resources, social/professional role and identity, and emotion. Beliefs about consequences were central because both patients and HCPs balanced perceived benefits (eg, prevention and preparedness) against potential harms (eg, anxiety and uncertain results). Environmental factors such as cost, time, counseling access, and system capacity were major determinants of uptake. The influence of social and professional identity was also evident. Differences across HCP groups reflected variations in role identity and comfort with genetic testing. Lastly, emotional responses including, anxiety, reassurance, and perceived ethical responsibility, further influenced both patient decisions and professional recommendations (See Table 4 for detailed mapping).

Discussion

This scoping review explored the experiences and perspectives of patients and HCPs regarding ECS and/or UCS. ECS presents promising opportunities to enhance reproductive autonomy, enable the prevention of serious genetic conditions, and contribute to broader public health goals. Across diverse populations and among HCPs, ECS is valued as a tool for informed reproductive decision-making, psychological preparedness, and reducing the burden of genetic disease. Yet, despite widespread interest and generally positive attitudes, the effective and equitable implementation of ECS faces multiple challenges. Technical and operational barriers, including limited health care infrastructure, a lack of standardized guidelines, and insufficient genetic literacy, constrain the scalability and responsible integration of ECS into routine care. The complexity of ECS results demands comprehensive pre- and post-test counseling tailored to varied literacy levels and cultural backgrounds, further highlighting resource needs. Moreover, ethical concerns, such as potential social pressure, ableism, stigma, and balancing autonomy, require sensitive navigation to avoid unintended harms. Equity issues persist around awareness, access, and inclusivity, especially for marginalized and underserved groups, raising questions about how ECS can be delivered fairly across diverse populations.

To support a structured analysis of the behavioral factors influencing engagement with screening, we applied TDF. This framework clarified how individual, interpersonal, and system-level factors intersect to shape engagement with carrier screening. Key domains converged in predictable ways. For example, beliefs about consequences were closely connected with emotional responses and decision-making. Additionally, environmental factors such as cost, access to counseling, and time constraints influenced the feasibility of acting on intentions. From an HCP perspective, professional role identity shaped how HCPs interpreted their responsibility in offering and supporting screening, which may contribute to the variation in attitudes and practice. These findings suggest that effective implementation of UCS programs will require coordinated strategies that address multiple domains simultaneously. Isolated interventions targeting knowledge or attitude are unlikely to be sufficient without addressing system barriers. Overall, applying the TDF highlights several priority targets for implementation, including managing uncertainty and emotional responses, clarifying professional roles, and ensuring that appropriate system-level resources are available to support equitable access.

Ramdaney et al69 found that there was a desire among individuals to undergo ECS in the U.S. but this was not reflected in the clinical uptake of ECS. Consistent with our findings, they reported that genetic counselors were comfortable with UCS, whereas other nongenetic reproductive care HCPs preferred ancestry-based approaches and cited limited time as 1 barrier. This underscores the importance of clear workflows to support UCS implementation. Their review, however, was limited to U.S. studies and offered less insight into system-level and emotional influences, highlighting the added value of our broader analysis.

Our findings align with those of Edwards et al,6 which emphasized the value of offering screening preconceptionally, the need for HCP education, and the impact of systemic and informational barriers. Although both reviews highlighted the importance of aligning counseling with patient values and context, Edward et al6 placed greater focus on public education and alternative counseling delivery formats (eg, telehealth, videos). In contrast, our review assessed both patient and HCP perspectives (beyond genetic counselors) and gave more attention to emotional responses, which emerged as a key factor influencing both patient and HCP engagement with screening.

Best et al14 explored HCPs’ perceptions of the barriers and enablers to implement reproductive genetic carrier screening. Consistent with our findings, knowledge gaps, system constraints, and role ambiguity among HCPs were identified. Our inclusion of patient studies reflects a shift in literature toward broader population screening and highlights evolving concerns, such as emotional impact and ethical tensions. Although the systematic review by Pasquier18 identified key clinical and programmatic challenges to reproductive carrier screening implementation from the perspective of professional stakeholders, our findings are broadly aligned in highlighting issues related to access, informed consent, and the complexity of delivering genetic information. However, by incorporating patient and public perspectives alongside HCPs, our review additionally captures experiential and contextual factors, such as perceived utility, acceptability, and decision-making preferences. These complementary insights provide a more comprehensive understanding of the considerations relevant to implementing UCS.

It is important to acknowledge that most of the existing evidence comes from high-income, Western health care contexts. These geographic and cultural concentrations may shape the themes identified in this review and limit the transferability of findings to health care settings with different resources, cultural values, and health care structures.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of our review include its comprehensive inclusion of both patient and HCP perspectives, the application of a validated behavioral framework (ie, TDF), and its relevance to current debates about UCS and ECS. Screening demonstrated substantial agreement between reviewers, supporting the consistent application of eligibility criteria and the reproducibility of the screening process.

Restricting inclusion to English-language studies published after 2001 may have introduced language and publication date bias. Although multiple major databases were searched with input from a research librarian, it is possible that relevant studies were missed, affecting comprehensiveness. Titles without abstracts were excluded during screening, which might have led to the omission of pertinent articles. Data extraction was divided between reviewers, with only partial double extraction, potentially introducing variability despite efforts to ensure consistency. The analysis and application of the TDF were primarily conducted by S.W., which may introduce interpretive bias despite the analysis being reviewed by the research team.

Additionally, we did not assess the quality of the included studies, in keeping with scoping review methodology, which focuses on mapping the breadth and nature of existing evidence rather than evaluating study rigor. Our aim of exploring experiences and perspectives across diverse contexts, including all relevant studies regardless of quality, allowed for a more comprehensive synthesis.

Implications for future practice and research

Policy makers may use the findings of this review to assess how best to implement or augment population-wide ECS/UCS within their context. Prenatal screening programs should address emotional and informational needs by ensuring personalized, culturally sensitive counseling that clearly communicates the benefits, limitations, and potential emotional impacts. HCP education and support, especially for nongenetic health care professionals, are essential to enhance confidence and knowledge.

Future research is needed to explore how implementation strategies can be tailored using dominant TDF domains. The exploration and assessment of innovative service delivery models may help overcome systemic barriers, such as limited time and access to genetics services, facilitating more equitable and informed patient decision-making.

There is a paucity of research on patient and HCP perspectives, particularly primary care HCPs. Longitudinal studies following patients and HCPs through the screening process are required. Finally, evaluating the implementation of UCS and ECS across different health care systems will support the development of scalable, effective, and patient-centered models of care.

Conclusion

This scoping review highlights the complex interplay of cognitive, emotional, ethical, and systemic factors that shape how patients and HCPs perceive and engage with ECS. Key influences include perceived benefits and risks, anxiety surrounding results, variable HCP roles and confidence, and access to information and support. By mapping findings to the TDF, we identified dominant behavioral drivers, such as beliefs about consequences, environmental context, professional identity, and emotion. The review also revealed important gaps in the literature, particularly the underrepresentation of primary care perspectives, and underscored the need for more personalized, accessible, and emotionally supportive screening pathways. These insights can inform the development of targeted interventions and policies that address both structural barriers and individual needs, ultimately supporting more informed, values-aligned reproductive decision-making.
