New Diagnosis of Atrial Fibrillation in Pregnancy
Authors: Manasa Jasti, Cornelia R Graves, Stephen R Clendenin, Hannah L Fish-Trotter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtrial fibrillation (AF) during pregnancy is associated with increased maternal risk. New-diagnosis AF in pregnancy presents several complex clinical dilemmas, including decisions regarding stroke prophylaxis, the risk of AF recurrence, and the need for long-term anticoagulation beyond pregnancy. Evidence to inform these decisions is sparse, and existing risk-stratification tools have not been validated in pregnant populations. In this case-based series, we present 2 illustrative cases of new-diagnosis AF during pregnancy that highlight real-world management challenges and evidence gaps. These cases underscore the need for individualized clinical decision-making and multidisciplinary care in this patient population and the importance of further research/data to guide optimal management of AF in pregnancy. We review available literature, discuss practical considerations for anticoagulation and follow-up, and further highlight future research directions.Key words: anticoagulation, atrial fibrillation, cardio-obstetrics, pregnancy, rhythm control

Patient 1

A 27-year-old-female G3P0111 with no significant medical history was referred to cardiology for new diagnosis of atrial fibrillation (AF) with rapid ventricular response (RVR) at outside emergency department at 13-week gestation. Transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE)-guided direct current cardioversion (DCCV) was planned, but she spontaneously converted to sinus rhythm within <24 hours from AF onset. Transthoracic echo demonstrated normal biventricular size and systolic function, normal diastolic function, normal biatrial size, and no significant valvular pathology. Laboratory results were unremarkable, including thyroid function test, brain natriuretic peptide hormone. She was discharged the following day with metoprolol succinate 50 mg daily, therapeutic anticoagulation with subcutaneous enoxaparin 1 mg/kg twice daily, and an ambulatory heart monitor for 5 days, which showed no AF recurrence. She was referred to outpatient cardiology and maternal fetal medicine. Her obstetric history included a prior pregnancy complicated by premature rupture of membranes at 36-week gestation and subsequent pregnancy ending in early miscarriage.

At 15 weeks of gestation, she presented to the emergency department with recurrent AF, which again spontaneously converted to normal sinus rhythm within 24 hours. She was started on flecainide 50 mg twice daily, while continuing metoprolol succinate and enoxaparin. She had no further recurrence of AF throughout pregnancy and uncomplicated vaginal delivery at 37 weeks and 5 days of gestation, with telemetry demonstrating no AF recurrence. She requested to discontinue flecainide and metoprolol in the postpartum period. She was advised to continue therapeutic anticoagulation until 12 weeks postpartum, at which time, through shared decision-making and acknowledgment of a CHADS2VASc score of 1 (female sex), the patient opted to discontinue enoxaparin. A repeat ambulatory monitor is pending.

Patient 2

A 39-year-old female G3P1011 with a history of obesity status post gastric sleeve and hypertension was admitted at 31 weeks of gestation for new-onset AF with RVR. Initially, rate control with metoprolol was attempted, but with continued symptomatic AF lasting 14 hours with intermittent RVR, TEE-guided DCCV performed with conversion to sinus rhythm. Transthoracic echo showed normal biventricular size and systolic function, normal diastolic function, normal biatrial size, no significant valvular pathology, and small pericardial effusion without hemodynamic compromise. She was initiated on subcutaneous enoxaparin for therapeutic anticoagulation at 1 mg/kg twice daily, with metoprolol tartrate 12.5 mg twice daily for rate control. She was discharged with cardiology, maternal fetal medicine, and obstetrician follow-up. Due to her prior history of cesarean section (CS) delivery, a repeat CS delivery was planned at 37 weeks of gestation, with recommendation to discontinue enoxaparin 24 hours earlier.

One day prior to her scheduled delivery, she presented with palpitations, in recurrent AF with RVR. She was treated with intravenous (IV) metoprolol, and ventricular rates decreased, though rhythm remained AF. Enoxaparin was held for CS delivery the following day. She underwent CS delivery, and due to 36 hours of persistent AF, she underwent TEE-guided DCCV, 24 hours following CS delivery. Therapeutic anticoagulation with enoxaparin was resumed. TEE confirmed normal left ventricular size and function, and she was started on flecainide 50 mg twice daily. She was monitored for 2 days, remaining in sinus rhythm. She was discharged on postpartum day 3. Given her plans not to breastfeed, and long-acting reversible contraception, she opted to transition to direct factor Xa inhibitor for therapeutic anticoagulation with apixaban 5 mg twice daily. She remained on flecainide and metoprolol tartrate. An ambulatory heart monitor was placed at discharge, which showed brief paroxysms of supraventricular tachycardia lasting up to 8.5 seconds and no AF. Her calculated CHADS2VASc score was 2 (female sex, hypertension). She was recommended to continue anticoagulation for at least 12 weeks postpartum.

She was seen for follow-up at 6 weeks postpartum without a report of any sustained episode of palpitations or elevated heart rate. Electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus bradycardia with a rate of 58 beats/min. She was continued on flecainide, metoprolol, and apixaban. At 16-week postpartum follow-up, flecainide was stopped, and a repeat 14-day ambulatory heart monitor was placed, which showed no AF recurrence. This was complemented by a lack of recurrence of symptomatic palpitations or Apple watch alerts, both of which had previously accompanied the patient's prior episodes of AF. At this time, through shared decision-making, the patient elected to remain off therapeutic anticoagulation and flecainide. Metoprolol tartrate was continued at a low dose of 12.5 mg twice daily according to the patient's preference. She was educated to call for recurrence of AF as evidenced by symptoms, wearable watch alerts, or other medical evaluations, at which point, discussion for re-initiation of therapeutic anticoagulation and referral to electrophysiology for possible ablation would be made.

For both patients above, multidisciplinary team discussions included obstetrics, maternal fetal medicine, cardiology, and electrophysiology, which guided intrapartum management and delivery timing.

Discussion

Supraventricular arrhythmias, including AF, fall under modified World Health Organization 2.0 Class II, with small increased risk of maternal morbidity and mortality. Pregnancy heart team management is recommended for these patients, with cardiology follow-up once every trimester during pregnancy, and delivery is recommended at a referral center.1,2

AF in pregnant women is more frequent with increasing age, obesity, congenital heart disease, or comorbidities such as hypertension and heart failure.3 Other risk factors for AF in pregnancy include diabetes, illicit drug use, tocolytics, magnesium sulfate, hyperthyroidism, and electrolyte imbalances.4 From the ROPAC (Registry on Pregnancy and Cardiac Disease) data, in patients with structural heart disease, the prepregnancy risk factors for developing AF included mitral valve disease, left-sided lesions, and prior history of AF. In these patients with structural heart disease, maternal mortality was noted to be higher in patients with AF/atrial flutter when compared to those without (11.8% vs 0.9%; P = 0.01). Low birth weight (<2.5 kg) occurred more in women with AF/atrial flutter than in those without (35% vs 14%; P < 0.02).5

Rhythm control is the preferred strategy for AF in pregnancy.3 The 2025 European Society of Cardiology guidelines for the management of cardiovascular disease and pregnancy recommend flecainide, in addition to beta-blockers, for long-term AF rhythm control in pregnancy (Class IIa). DCCV is a class I recommendation for hemodynamically unstable AF or AF with pre-excitation and high ventricular rates and for patients with severe structural heart disease.3 When DCCV is required in pregnancy, it should be performed with fetal heart rate monitoring during and immediately after DCCV.6 Electrodes should be placed avoiding breast tissue to optimize delivery of the shock. Pads positioning is antero-posterior for atrial arrhythmias and sterno-apical for ventricular tachycardias.7 The decision of anticoagulation use prior to DCCV or need for TEE guidance prior to DCCV should be made as in a nonpregnant female. After DCCV, uninterrupted anticoagulation should be maintained for at least 4 weeks.3

Per European Society of Cardiology guidelines, in pregnant patients with no structural heart disease, pharmacologic cardioversion can be considered with IV flecainide or ibutilide (class IIb). Sotalol can also be considered as an alternative for rhythm control though it requires monitoring fetal growth.3 IV flecainide is not available in the United States, and experience with IV sotalol in pregnancy is limited. The commonly used antiarrhythmic in the nonpregnant population amiodarone has a category-D Food and Drug Administration labeling for pregnant patients and should be avoided during pregnancy and lactation. Significant adverse effects of amiodarone include fetal hypothyroidism, bradycardia, and growth restriction.8 It is preferable to defer ablation to the postpartum period. However, if ablation is deemed necessary during pregnancy, this can be achieved through minimal or zero fluoroscopy techniques using 3-dimensional mapping systems.6,7

For rate control, metoprolol is recommended (class I). Alternatively, verapamil in preserved systolic function or digoxin can be used (class IIa).3 Atenolol is contraindicated in pregnancy.7

There are several clinical conundrums when managing an AF first diagnosed in pregnancy. The first is determining thromboembolic risk from AF in a pregnant patient. Calculating the CHADS2VASc score provides a starting point to determine the thromboembolic risk associated with AF in this population; however this scoring system does not account for the hypercoagulable state of pregnancy. In addition, the CHADS2VASc score thresholds to decide anticoagulation have not been validated in the pregnant population. Currently, the guidelines recommend approaching thromboembolic risk as in nonpregnant patients. Shared decision-making is important in deciding on anticoagulation initiation, as well as in determining the choice of agent.

Therapeutic anticoagulation with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) is recommended for pregnant women with persistent or permanent AF at elevated thromboembolic risk (Class I).3 Warfarin is preferably avoided in the first trimester and is contraindicated in the first trimester if daily dose is >5 mg. Warfarin can be used in the second and third trimesters, with shared decision-making regarding the risks, and is often the anticoagulation of choice in pregnant patients with mechanical valves or moderate to severe mitral stenosis. In such patients, if warfarin dose cannot be <5 mg to maintain a required international normalized ratio in the first trimester, factor Xa level-guided LMWH can be used. Direct oral anticoagulants are contraindicated in pregnancy and lactation as they can cross the placenta and can be excreted in breast milk, respectively, and there is lack of data of the effect of these drugs in these patients.3,6 One week before the planned delivery date, anticoagulation should be switched to either IV heparin or dose-adjusted LMWH. If transitioned to IV heparin, this should be discontinued 4 to 6 hours before planned delivery. With LMWH, it must be switched to IV heparin 36 hours before planned delivery, with the plan to stop it 4 to 6 hours prior to planned delivery.9 When on therapeutic anticoagulation, it is recommended to discontinue anticoagulation before neuraxial procedure (4-6 hours for IV heparin and 24 hours for LMWH). Anticoagulation is recommended to be resumed no sooner than 1 hour with IV heparin after neuraxial catheter removal and 4 hours with LMWH.10

The second challenge faced in caring for these patients is determining future risk of AF recurrence, in the setting of new-onset diagnosis during pregnancy. There is data on recurrence risk in patients with a pre-existing history of AF: the recurrence of AF in pregnancy in patients with known prepregnancy AF has been reported to be 39.2%.11 However, we lack data in those with newly diagnosed AF in pregnancy. Based on existing guidelines, in patients with AF diagnosis in the setting of acute medical illness or surgery, counseling about the significant risk of recurrent AF after acute illness resolution and outpatient follow-up for thromboembolic risk stratification and AF surveillance is recommended.9 Although pregnancy by itself is not a medical illness, since we have paucity of data in this patient population, we could possibly apply the same principles here. The use of consumer wearable devices or external heart monitors may be of particular value in recurrence surveillance. Postpartum counseling should include lifestyle and risk-factor modification, particularly in patients with obesity, hypertension, or other cardiometabolic risk factors, which influence future AF burden and long-term cardiovascular risk. In deciding long-term anticoagulation needs for these patients, we need to consider underlying risk factors, such as cardiac structural abnormalities, and have shared decision-making discussions.

A better understanding of AF recurrence risk following an index event that has occurred in the setting of pregnancy, in the absence of prepregnancy arrhythmia history, can help to guide counseling and long-term management for these patients. This includes decisions regarding the risks and benefits of discontinuing anticoagulation following the postpartum period, considerations on referral for ablation, and expectations for likelihood of recurrence in subsequent pregnancies. These decisions can significantly affect the quality of life of reproductive-aged women and necessitates a call for future registries to understand these questions. The ongoing HOPE (Heart Outcomes in Pregnancy Expectations) registry may provide insight into postpartum and lifetime AF recurrence risk among patients first diagnosed during pregnancy. While the HOPE study will provide this data in pregnant women with known cardiovascular disease, it is important to develop registries for pregnant women without cardiovascular disease, with and without baseline cardiometabolic risk factors.

Conclusions

New-diagnosis AF in pregnancy presents several clinical dilemmas, including decisions regarding stroke prophylaxis, AF recurrence risk, and long-term anticoagulation needs. Individualized shared clinical decision-making and multidisciplinary care is important in this patient population. Future research, including registries, is pivotal for understanding these clinical conundrums.
