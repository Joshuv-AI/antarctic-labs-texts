Tunable acoustic rotation for deep biophysical phenotyping of preclinical Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Yuxin Wang, Yue Quan, Shizheng Zhou, Yinning Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite advances in imaging and biomarker-based approaches, early diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease (AD) at single-cell resolution remains challenging. Biophysical signals at single-cell scale such as calcium dysregulation and reduced membrane fluidity may serve as early markers of AD, yet these weak signals are difficult to capture. Here, we characterized both phenomena using sensitive optical readouts that were enhanced by acoustic-induced cell rotation. The photobleaching half-life of the calcium indicator fluorescence signal and the half-recovery time of the readout used to characterize membrane fluidity both varied with the acoustic frequency used to induce cell rotation. Signal enhancement peaked within an intermediate acoustic-frequency window and decreased at both lower and higher frequencies, producing a bandpass-like response. This frequency-dependent behavior guided the selection of optimal operating frequencies for subsequent measurements. We then established an Amyloid-β (Aβ)-induced in vitro cellular model and used multidimensional spatial, temporal, and frequency-domain features to evaluate Aβ-induced cellular stress states. The acoustic-rotation approach distinguished early Aβ-induced cellular stress from untreated controls with 99.0% accuracy and classified five cellular states defined by Aβ exposure duration with 94.8% accuracy. By actively enhancing weak single-cell functional signals, this frequency-dependent acoustic strategy provides a generalizable approach for live-cell optical phenotyping and may support future preclinical studies of AD-related cellular stress.

Introduction

Preclinical Alzheimer's disease (AD) highlights a methodological bottleneck: the lack of practical tools to quantify ultra-weak and rapid functional transitions at single-cell resolution [[1], [2], [3]]. Existing diagnostic modalities typically seek a balance between scale and sensitivity [4,5]. Tissue-level imaging (MRI, PET) [6,7] provides macroscopic context, but large-field-of-view imaging results in lower spatial resolution [8]. Fluid biomarkers can be sensitive, but they average across heterogeneous cell states, obscuring the pathways where dysfunction first emerges [[9], [10], [11]]. Meanwhile, many high-resolution cellular assays are destructive or endpoint-based, limiting longitudinal measurements in living cells [12,13]. Consequently, transient and heterogeneous single-cell functional information at the early disease stages remains difficult to resolve robustly with current instrumentation [[14], [15], [16], [17]].

To bridge this measurement gap, we focus on readouts that are mechanistically proximal to early dysfunction in living single cells. In AD models, intracellular calcium homeostasis [18,19] and membrane biophysics [20,21] are tightly coupled. Changes in membrane receptor diffusivity and clustering can transduce extracellular Amyloid-β (Aβ) exposure to intracellular Ca2+ dysregulation [22,23]. These early phenotypes are subtle and heterogeneous, motivating time-series recording rather than static snapshots [15,16]. Optical methods (e.g., Ca2+ indicators [24] and fluorescence recovery after photobleaching, FRAP [25]) enable the tracking of such dynamics in living single cells with high spatiotemporal resolution. However, phototoxicity remains inherent to fluorescence imaging [26].

Current strategies to mitigate photobleaching and phototoxicity mainly rely on passive control of illumination. These typically involve minimizing excitation (e.g., reducing power, exposure time, sampling frequency, or restricting ROIs) [27,28], enhancing fluorophore stability (e.g., using red-shifted variants, oxygen scavengers) [29,30], or concentrating photon flux via optical sectioning (e.g., light-sheet, two-photon) [31,32] coupled with denoising algorithms [33]. The common denominator of these approaches is the reduction of bleaching rates by limiting photon absorption or delaying photochemistry. Consequently, when weak single-cell signals are close to the noise floor, reducing illumination can also reduce detected photons, image contrast, and the reliability of downstream feature extraction.

Here, we use a gentle acoustic field below 2000 Hz to drive controlled 3D rotation of single cells and reduce local overexposure during live-cell imaging. Acoustic rotation has been established as a contactless tool for orientation control and 3D visualization in prior acoustofluidic studies, including microbubble-driven rotation of microparticles, single cells, and C. elegans, as well as surface acoustic wave based rotation for multi-orientation imaging [34,35]. Building on this foundation, our study applies acoustic rotation to molecular optical readouts. By imposing predictable modulation on FRAP and calcium fluorescence signals, acoustic rotation helps separate weak functional dynamics from drift-related noise. Acoustic frequency tuning further showed a bell-shaped response: signal enhancement was strongest at intermediate frequencies and weaker at lower or higher frequencies. These optimized readouts were then analyzed using spatial, kinetic, and spectral features for machine-learning classification of Aβ exposure-duration cellular states. The approach distinguished Aβ 2 h cells from controls with 99.0% accuracy and classified five experimentally defined Aβ-induced cellular stress states with 94.8% accuracy. This strategy provides a general framework for enhancing weak live-cell optical signals and may support future preclinical studies of AD-related cellular stress.

Materials and methods

Chip fabrication

The mold for the four-pointed-star pillar array was fabricated on a silicon wafer using standard SU-8 photolithography. Replica chips were produced in polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) by mixing Sylgard 184 base and curing agent (Dow Corning, Midland, MI, USA) at a 10:1 (w/w) ratio, degassing the mixture under vacuum, and casting it onto the master. The cast PDMS was cured at 80 °C for 2 h and then gently peeled from the mold. The PDMS replica and a glass coverslip were activated by oxygen plasma treatment (PDC-002, Harrick Plasma, USA) for 3 min to generate surface hydroxyl groups, and were immediately brought into conformal contact to form sealed microfluidic channels.

Geometrically, each four-pointed star pillar was designed as two identical ellipses intersecting orthogonally with a common center. Each ellipse had a major axis of 60 μm and a minor axis of 20 μm. In the array layout, neighboring pillar centers were spaced 180 μm apart in both the horizontal and vertical directions, and the pillar height was 60 μm.

Cell culture and sample preparation

HEK293T (Human Embryonic Kidney 293 T) and SH-SY5Y (Human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cell line) were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA), fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (15,140-122, Gibco, USA) at 37 °C in an incubator (BPN-40RH, Bluepard, China) with 5% CO2. For experiments, cells were detached with TrypLE, resuspended in fresh medium, and adjusted to a final concentration of 2 × 105 cells/mL. Suspended cells were loaded into a custom microfluidic chamber mounted on the piezoelectric actuator platform. Unless otherwise specified, all measurements were performed within 1 h after loading to minimize confounding effects of cell settling or viability loss.

We chose Aβ aggregates for this model because Aβ accumulation is an early event in AD, while tau pathology typically emerges later in the disease process, as shown by in vivo and modeling studies where Aβ predominates in the early stages of AD and tau accumulates subsequently [41]. In addition, Aβ aggregation is more easily replicated in vitro, whereas tau aggregation often requires complex conditions, making Aβ a more practical and effective choice for simulating early AD pathology in cell-based models [42].

Preparation of Aβ1-42 oligomers

Synthetic human amyloid-β (1–42) peptide (Aβ1-42) was prepared as soluble oligomers using a HFIP/DMSO pre-treatment followed by controlled oligomerization, adapting established workflows. Briefly, lyophilized Aβ1-42 was dissolved in hexafluoro-2-propanol (HFIP), evaporated to a thin film, and re-dissolved in DMSO to generate a concentrated stock. The stock was then diluted into phenol-red-free culture medium to 100 μM and incubated for 24 h at 4 °C to enrich soluble oligomeric species prior to use. Aliquots were stored at −20 °C and freeze-thaw cycles were minimized to reduce shifts in aggregate distribution [43,44].

Dose-fixed, time-graded Aβ exposure model

Time points were chosen to reflect the non-linear kinetics of Aβ toxicity: early functional deviations emerge within hours, whereas overt cell death is typically detected later. For example, a SH-SY5Y Aβ1-42 time-course defined an early pre-death window (∼5 h at 0.5 μM) and reported significant death starting ∼18 h with maximal effect ∼24 h, motivating dense sampling in the first hours while extending to a late boundary condition [43]. Consistently, differentiated SH-SY5Y models explicitly used 2-6 h exposures to capture changes before the initiation of cell death, with death first detectable at ∼24 h, supporting the 2 h to 6 h Aβ exposure groups, as well as the 24 h boundary condition [45].

To discretize a continuous Aβ-driven stress trajectory into supervised labels, we used a dose-fixed, time-graded exposure paradigm. Cells were treated with 1 μM Aβ1-42 oligomers (monomer equivalents) and assigned to five classes based on exposure duration: Control (0 h; vehicle only), cells exposed to Aβ for 2 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h. Vehicle controls matched the final DMSO content of the Aβ1-42 condition [44,46].

Acoustic-induced single-cell rotation setup

Acoustic rotation was generated using an ultralow-frequency function generator (AFG 31102, Tektronix, USA) connected to a voltage amplifier (E00.A4, COREMORROW, China) that drove a piezoelectric actuator plate (P16.XY20Z7K, COREMORROW, China) underneath the microfluidic chip. The driving signal was sinusoidal with frequencies in the range 500-2000 Hz. The driving voltage was adjusted (0-5 V pp) to achieve stable rotation without detaching cells from the imaging plane. For no-rotation controls, the piezoelectric actuator was left unpowered while all other optical settings were kept identical. Synchronous rotation of individual cells was confirmed by time-lapse fluorescent imaging and trajectory inspection.

Confocal microscopy for imaging subtle membrane fluidity

To accurately quantify the subtle millisecond-scale recovery kinetics of membrane lipids, Fluorescence Recovery After Photobleaching (FRAP) assays were performed on a laser-scanning confocal microscope (LSM710, Carl Zeiss, Germany) equipped with a high-NA 50× objective. The confocal modality was strictly selected over conventional wide-field fluorescence microscopy for this assay. Given that biophysical perturbations in the Aβ 2 h exposure window stage are inherently weak, the optical sectioning capability of the confocal system is essential to eliminate out-of-focus background noise and ensure a high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).

For sample preparation, cell membranes were labeled with DiI (1 μM) for 30 min at 37 °C. DiI was excited with a 561-nm laser line; emitted fluorescence was collected through the corresponding emission filter set. For each cell, a single optical plane intersecting the plasma membrane was selected while the microfluidic chip was mounted on the piezoelectric actuator. Under the rotation condition, the X axis acoustic driver was turned on to maintain continuous in plane rotation for 15 s during photobleaching. The acoustic driver was then turned off before recovery imaging. For no rotation controls, the acoustic driver remained off while all confocal settings were kept identical.

Because only in-plane rotation was applied, the bleached region was confined to the upper portion of the plasma membrane. The laser was switched to high power (80%) for 15 s to induce photobleaching, and fluorescence recovery was then monitored for 15 s at low laser power (5%). Fluorescence intensities within the bleached ROI were extracted from the confocal image series after background subtraction. For each cell, the recovery trace I(t) was normalized to the mean pre-bleach intensity and corrected for overall imaging-induced photobleaching using a non-bleached reference region acquired in the same field of view.

Wide-field fluorescence microscopy for imaging genetically encoded calcium indicators

Calcium Ion Fluorescence Photobleaching (CFPB) assays were conducted on an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX73) equipped with a DP74 camera. Wide-field illumination was employed to ensure uniform volumetric excitation during 3D rotation, utilizing a 488-nm LED source and a 525/50 nm emission filter. Cells stably expressing jGCaMP8s were loaded into the device, with the acoustic frequency tuned to size-specific resonances (1500 Hz for HEK293T; 1300 Hz for SH-SY5Y) for rotation-induced photostability enhancement (RIPE). Photobleaching kinetics were recorded under continuous excitation for 100 s.

Performance was quantified by comparing the RIPE group (active rotation) against static controls. Fluorescence decay kinetics were analyzed by extracting the whole-cell mean intensity to calculate the decay time constant (τ) and the Area Under the Curve (AUC) of normalized intensity traces.

Quantification of FRAP kinetics and statistical analysis

Half-recovery time and recovery rate

Fluorescence recovery trajectories were processed using custom algorithms developed in MATLAB (MathWorks). To mitigate stochastic noise while preserving kinetic features, raw intensity curves were smoothed using a Savitzky-Golay filter (3rd order polynomial, 11-point frame length). The smoothed trajectories were normalized to the full dynamic range of recovery, defined as:(1)Inorm(t)=I(t)−Ipost‐bleachIplateau−Ipost‐bleachwhere Ipost‐bleach is the intensity immediately after the photobleaching pulse and Iplateau is the maximal recovered intensity. The normalized data were fitted to a mono-exponential recovery model I(t)=1−e−(t−t0)/τ using an unconstrained non-linear minimization algorithm (Nelder-Mead simplex method, implemented via fminsearch). The half-recovery time (τ1/2) was derived from the fitted characteristic time constant (τ) as follows:(2)τ1/2=τ×ln2

To explicitly characterize the turnover speed of fluorescent molecules, the fluorescence recovery rate (v) was defined as the reciprocal of the characteristic recovery time constant:(3)v=1τwhere τ represents the fundamental time scale of diffusion-limited recovery. To quantify the magnitude of the rotation-induced effect, the percentage change (δ) in membrane mobility between the control (ctrl) and rotated (rot) groups was calculated as δ=[(τctrl‾−τrot‾)/τctrl‾]×100. The associated uncertainty was determined using standard error propagation rules for arithmetic operations, as follows:(4)σδ=δ·(σctrlτctrl‾)2+(σrotτrot‾)2where τ‾ and σ denote the mean and standard deviation of the characteristic time constants, respectively. Statistical significance of the difference between the means of the two independent groups was assessed using a two-tailed independent samples t-test, with P<0.05 considered statistically significant.

Calculation of apparent FRAP recovery coefficient

To provide a comparative descriptor of FRAP recovery kinetics, the apparent diffusion-like coefficient (Dapp) was derived from the characteristic recovery time (τ) assuming a uniform circular bleach profile, and was calculated as follows [47]:(5)Dapp=w24τwhere w represents the radius of the photobleached region, calibrated to 10 μm in our experimental setup. More rigorous FRAP fitting depends on bleach geometry and the illumination profile [48]. Statistical significance of the difference in Dapp was assessed using a two-tailed independent samples t-test (P < 0.05).

Area under the curve (AUC) integration

To capture the aggregate recovery performance over the entire acquisition window, the AUC was calculated for each normalized recovery trajectory. The cumulative fluorescence recovery was integrated numerically using the trapezoidal rule:(6)AUC=∫tstarttendInorm(t)d≈∑k=1N−1Inorm(tk+1)+Inorm(tk)2·Δtkwhere Inorm(t) represents the normalized fluorescence intensity at time t, and the integration interval [tstart,tend] corresponds to the fixed acquisition period (15-30 s). The relative enhancement in total recovery was quantified as the fold change of the mean AUC in the rotated group relative to the control. Statistical significance was assessed using a two-tailed independent samples t-test.

Spatial morphology analysis of control and Aβ 2 h-treated cells

For geometric characterization, single cells were segmented from video frames using an automated Otsu's thresholding method followed by morphological opening to remove noise. We extracted six key morphological descriptors for each cell trajectory: Area, Perimeter, Eccentricity, Major/Minor Axis Length, and Circularity (Fig. 4C and D). To visualize the separation between functional states and reduce feature redundancy, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) [49] was employed to project the high-dimensional feature space into principal components (PC1 and PC2). Subsequently, unsupervised k-means clustering was applied to the PCA-reduced data to identify cell-state groups (Control versus Aβ 2 h-treated cells) without prior labeling (Fig. 4B), assessing the intrinsic separability of the phenotypes under rotated versus static conditions.

Image-based classification of control and Aβ-treated cells

To stratify discrete AD progression stages, we implemented a machine learning classification module based on ResNet (Residual Network) [50] architectures. We evaluated three variants of increasing depth: ResNet-18, ResNet-50, and ResNet-101 (Fig. 4E). The networks utilize skip connections (identity mappings, F(x)+x) to mitigate the vanishing gradient problem, allowing for the training of deep models on kinetic video data.

The models were trained to classify single cells into treatment-duration groups based on input image sequences. We performed a comparative analysis between rotating-cell and static-cell datasets. Performance was evaluated using multi-class accuracy, visualized via radar charts. The ResNet-18 model typically achieved the optimal balance between computational efficiency and classification accuracy (as indicated by the radar plots showing >90% accuracy for rotation-enhanced data across models).

Time- and frequency-domain machine learning for binary and five-class Aβ stress state classification

Image processing and 3D reconstruction

To visualize the spatial distribution of fluorophores, we performed single-cell 3D reconstruction by stacking 2D optical sections obtained during the controlled rotation. This yielded volumetric projections (X-Y, X-Z, Y-Z views) that revealed the spatial differences among Aβ treatment-duration groups (Control, Aβ 2 h, Aβ 6 h, Aβ 12 h, and Aβ 24 h). Morphological features, including Area, Perimeter, Eccentricity, Major/Minor Axis Length, and Circularity, were extracted to construct a high-dimensional feature vector for each cell.

Train/test splits and cross-validation

To minimize data leakage, all machine-learning analyses were performed at the single-cell level. Each fluorescence trajectory was summarized into one feature vector before splitting, and frames, time points, or FFT bins were not treated as independent samples. We used stratified five-fold cross-validation, with preprocessing fitted only on training folds through a scikit-learn Pipeline. Fold audits confirmed zero train-test overlap in row indices, sample IDs, and cell IDs. Overfitting was evaluated using held-out performance, train-test gaps, and label-permutation controls (Fig. S45–S47).

Machine learning benchmarking and classification

We benchmarked acoustic-rotation and no-rotation conditions using the available FRAP and CFPB datasets (Fig. S45). To identify the optimal classifier for distinguishing subtle functional states, we used seven distinct machine learning algorithms: Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) [51], Logistic Regression [52], Naive Bayes [53], Support Vector Machine (SVM) [54], Neural Network (Multilayer Perceptron) [55], Decision Tree [56], and Random Forest [57]. Models were trained on the extracted morphological and kinetic feature sets.

(G) Performance metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score) for different algorithms in binary classification using frequency-domain features; logistic regression attains the highest accuracy (∼0.990). This transformation revealed latent periodicities and amplitude modulations that were otherwise obscured in the time domain. We compared the discriminative power of time-domain features (raw intensity points) versus frequency-domain features (spectral amplitudes) by training classifiers on both datasets. The spectral features demonstrated superior separability, forming distinct, compact clusters in the feature space compared to the overlapping distributions observed in the time domain.

Performance evaluation

Classification performance was rigorously evaluated using confusion matrices, quantifying True Positives, False Positives, and class-specific accuracy. Model robustness was assessed via standard metrics including Precision, Recall, and F1-score. Comparative analysis revealed that models trained on rotation-enhanced data consistently outperformed those trained on static data.

FlowMind: An integrated computational pipeline for automated high-throughput single-cell analysis

To translate the raw, high-speed imaging data from the acoustic rotation experiments into quantitative analysis outputs, we developed FlowMind (version 2) (building upon our previous work [58], FlowMind version 1), a custom-built, Python-based software suite with a modular graphical user interface (GUI) (Fig. S31). This integrated pipeline automates the entire analytical workflow, from real-time motion tracking to machine learning-based classification, ensuring high throughput and reproducibility.

The core functionality of FlowMind (version 2) begins with the “Motion Tracker” module (Fig. S36), designed to handle the dynamic nature of rotating cells. Unlike standard static cell segmentation tools, this module employs an adaptive tracking algorithm to lock onto moving cells in real-time video streams. It automatically identifies and isolates single cells within the microfluidic channel, extracting Regions of Interest (ROIs) for both calcium (green channel) and FRAP (red channel) imaging (Fig. S32–35). This capability is critical for maintaining stable signal extraction despite the active mechanical actuation.

Following tracking, the “Data Processing” and “Curve Visualization” modules perform automated signal extraction and quality control. For calcium imaging, the software extracts mean fluorescence intensity over time to generate photobleaching decay curves (Fig. S38), while for FRAP, it quantifies the fluorescence recovery kinetics post-bleaching (Fig. S39). Crucially, the software integrates automated edge detection and contour fitting algorithms to compute morphological parameters such as cell diameter, area, and circularity (Fig. S37). This feature extraction step transforms raw video data into quantitative biophysical descriptors, such as equivalent diameter and PDI, providing the necessary geometric constraints for acoustic parameter optimization (as shown in Fig. 3E).

Finally, the processed data feeds directly into the “Binary/Multi-class Classification” modules, which house the pre-trained machine learning models (Figs. S40 and S41). Users can select from a library of seven algorithms (including Random Forest, SVM, and Neural Networks) to perform instant classification (Control vs. early Aβ-induced cellular stress state).

Results

Acoustic rotation improves optical readouts in an Aβ-treated cell model

To quantify subtle biophysical changes associated with the AD-like state, we developed an integrated platform combining acoustic microfluidics with machine learning analysis (Fig. 1). We modeled the time-graded Aβ exposure response with jGCaMP8s-expressing SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 1A and B). This treatment was intended to mimic early amyloid stress, inducing measurable perturbations in Ca2+ signaling and membrane fluidity.

To capture these weak signatures, we coupled the optical path to the acoustic-induced vibration (AIV) system (Fig. 1C; Fig. S1) that rotates suspended cells with ultralow-frequency acoustic waves (<2000 Hz). Acoustic rotation improved both FRAP signal quality and the photostability of the calcium indicator. The optimal frequency was defined operationally as the frequency that maximized the relevant optical readout. For RIAE (Rotation-induced afterglow enhancement), the optimal frequency was defined as the acoustic frequency that maximized the FRAP (Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching) recovery readout. For RIPE (Rotation-induced photostability enhancement), the optimal frequency was defined as the acoustic frequency that minimized calcium ion fluorescence photobleaching (CFPB). In addition, we found that both the CFPB half-life and the FRAP-derived half-recovery time exhibit a rise-then-fall dependence on increasing rotation frequency, which we refer to as an acousto-bandpass effect. The frequency at which the response peaks was defined as the optimal frequency and was subsequently used to set the acoustic stimulation frequency for downstream experiments (Fig. 1D; Fig. S2).

After identifying the optimal frequency windows by acoustic frequency scanning, we extracted time-frequency-space features and integrated them with machine learning analysis (Fig. 1E). Using cross-validation, these features discriminated control cells from early Aβ-induced stress state with 99.0% accuracy and classified five Aβ exposure-defined cellular states with 94.8% accuracy.

Decoding weak membrane fluidity signals via rotation-induced afterglow enhancement

As shown in our previous work, connecting the piezoelectric transducer to X/Y-axis vibration can induce the in-plane rotation [59]. To overcome the low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) often encountered in static FRAP measurements of membrane fluidity, we evaluated the impact of active in-plane rotation on fluorescence recovery kinetics (Fig. 2A and B). Conventional static FRAP is often limited by passive diffusion kinetics, yielding faint recovery signals that are difficult to quantify in fragile cellular models. In contrast, applying stable in-plane acoustic rotation produced a distinct spatiotemporal signature: unlike the localized and slow recovery observed in static controls, rotating cells exhibited a rapid and homogeneous recovery of fluorescence across the cell perimeter following photobleaching (Fig. 2C–F). We termed this physical amplification rotation-induced afterglow enhancement (RIAE). Time-lapse contour mapping further showed that in-plane rotation effectively redistributed the recovery region from a confined focal spot to the entire membrane interface within 30 s (Figs. S3 and S4).

We benchmarked the quantitative performance of RIAE using both HEK293T cells and neuron-like SH-SY5Y models. In HEK293T cells (Fig. 2G; Supplementary Table 1), acoustic rotation triggered an 18.86 ± 12.84-fold amplification in recovery amplitude compared to static controls. This signal enhancement was accompanied by faster kinetics, evidenced by a 77.7% increase in recovery rate and a 79.9% rise in the Dapp, while the half-recovery time decreased by 31.6%.

Crucially, this amplification performance was conserved in the SH-SY5Y AD model (Fig. 2H; Supplementary Table 2), which is characterized by subtler membrane dynamics. Rotating SH-SY5Y cells displayed a comparable 19.56 ± 10.58-fold increase in recovery amplitude relative to static baseline. Kinetic parameters were similarly enhanced, showing an 18.3% increase in recovery rate, an 18.5% reduction in half-recovery time, and a 17.8% increase in the Dapp.

By transforming faint, diffusion-limited traces into high-amplitude kinetic readouts, RIAE provides a robust quantitative basis for resolving Aβ-associated membrane changes in this cell model.

Acoustic frequency acting as a bandpass filter for selective kinetic modulation

To systematically exploit these rotation-mediated signal enhancements, we first established stable reporter lines by transducing HEK293T and SH-SY5Y cells with lentiviral jGCaMP8s (Fig. 3A; Fig. S42–44). This genetic encoding enabled the simultaneous monitoring of intracellular Ca2+ kinetics and membrane fluidity. Because the acoustic frequency governs the sample-side rotational dynamics, it determines how rotation couples to illumination and imaging acquisition. Therefore, the magnitude of enhancement and its spatiotemporal pattern should vary with frequency, giving rise to an optimizable operating point (an optimal frequency). The above reasoning suggests a frequency-division multiplexing paradigm, where distinct acoustic frequencies can be selected to preferentially modulate either membrane diffusion kinetics or calcium photostability (Fig. 3B).

We first investigated the rotation-induced photostability enhancement (RIPE) effect against the rapid photobleaching typical of static wide-field microscopy. As shown in our previous work, connecting the piezoelectric transducer to Z-axis vibration can induce the out-of-plane rotation [59]. In situ time-averaged imaging confirmed that tracked regions of interest remained well registered with minimal motion blur, indicating that the imposed rotation is compatible with stable kinetic measurements (Fig. S7). In both HEK293T and SH-SY5Y cells, continuous out-of-plane rotation exposed different cell regions to illumination over time. This strategy markedly preserved jGCaMP8s fluorescence intensity over prolonged acquisition, contrasting sharply with the exponential decay observed in static controls (Fig. 3C and D; Figs. S5 and S6).

To define the frequency regime in which RIAE and RIPE are most effective, we next considered the physical constraints imposed by cell size. We performed automated edge detection to characterize the equivalent diameter distributions of our suspended cells (Figs. S8 and S9, Supplementary Text 2). HEK293T cells exhibited a mean diameter of ∼14.95 μm, while SH-SY5Y cells were smaller at ∼13.05 μm (Fig. 3E). Simulations revealed that both the photobleaching half-life of calcium indicators and FRAP half-recovery time readouts exhibit a frequency-dependent bandpass effect. Notably, the frequency corresponding to the bandpass peak is correlated with cell diameter (Fig. 3F, Supplementary Text 1). This is important for translating the platform to other cell types or organoid-scale specimens, where active frequency tuning may be required.

Experimental data further supported the simulations and confirmed that the peak frequency of the bandpass response depended on cell diameter. The resulting time-frequency maps and 3D response surfaces revealed distinct, bell-shaped spectral profiles (Fig. 3G–J). For RIAE (membrane fluidity), the optimal response window centered at ∼1100 Hz for HEK293T cells and ∼1000 Hz for SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 3G–I). RIPE (calcium indicator photostability) required higher driving frequencies, peaking at ∼1500 Hz for HEK293T cells and ∼1300 Hz for SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 3H–J). These data demonstrate that optical readouts acquired during acoustic rotation can be modulated by the acoustic frequency, enabling frequency-division-multiplexed biophysical control.

Morphology-based binary classification of Aβ 2 h-treated cells versus control

To translate the physical signal enhancement of RIAE and RIPE into classification performance, we developed FlowMind, a computational pipeline integrating automated object tracking, morphological feature extraction, and deep-learning classification (Fig. 4A). We hypothesized that, by improving the signal-to-noise ratio, acoustic rotation can not only generate high-SNR signals but also reveal subtle morphological changes in Aβ 2 h-treated cells.

We first analyzed the distribution of six key morphological parameters, area, perimeter, eccentricity, major and minor axis lengths, and circularity. Under static conditions, distributions for control and early Aβ-induced stress state largely overlapped for both CFPB (Fig. S10) and FRAP (Fig. S11) datasets, indicating that passive observation provides limited discriminative power. In contrast, under acoustic rotation (RIPE/RIAE), these distributions exhibited distinct systematic shifts (Fig. 4C and D; Fig. S12).

This improved feature separability was quantitatively validated using unsupervised learning. Principal component analysis (PCA) followed by k-means clustering revealed that while static cells formed diffuse, overlapping clusters, rotation enhanced signals (both CFPB and FRAP) yielded tighter, well-separated clusters corresponding to control and early Aβ-induced stress state (Fig. 4B, Fig. S13). These results suggest that physical signal amplification increases the statistical separability of cellular states in feature space.

To quantify the classification performance of these enhanced signals, we evaluated them using standard supervised learning models (ResNet-18, ResNet-50 and ResNet-101) on imaging datasets (Fig. 4E). For FRAP kinetics, acoustic rotation yielded a consistent accuracy gain, peaking at ∼94% with ResNet-101 compared to ∼87% for static controls. Notably, acoustic amplification yielded the largest gain in classification accuracy for calcium fluorescence. While static datasets suffered from low signal-to-noise ratios due to photobleaching, limiting classification accuracy to ∼60% (ResNet-18), rotation-stabilized datasets improved accuracy by over 27 percentage points, reaching ∼87%. These benchmarks confirm that the acousto-bandpass stabilization improves data quality sufficiently to enable high-performance classification using standard architectures.

Seven classifiers distinguish Aβ-treated cellular states using temporal features

To further support the improvement in signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), we performed an additional static-control benchmark focused on signal quality and downstream classification robustness. Specifically, we quantified the plateau cross-curve SNR and compared static control and acoustic-rotation datasets using seven standard machine-learning algorithms across both CFPB and FRAP readouts. The detailed analysis is provided in Fig. S45. Briefly, acoustic rotation increased the plateau SNR from 1.87 in the static control group to 5.67, corresponding to an approximately 3.03-fold enhancement. In CFPB-based classification, acoustic rotation improved the accuracy for six of seven classifiers, with only SVM showing a slight decrease. In FRAP-based classification, acoustic rotation improved the accuracy for all seven classifiers. These results indicate that acoustic rotation improves not only optical signal quality but also downstream classification robustness.

To determine whether rotation-enhanced optical readouts improve the separability of experimentally defined Aβ-induced cellular states, we established a continuous exposure-duration trajectory comprising five states: the normal state and four early Aβ-induced cellular stress states. We used a schematic to summarize the major AD-associated stressors (Fig. 5A) and selected Aβ oligomers as the protein species with the earliest and strongest impact. Using time-sequenced exposure, we established an in vitro cellular AD model (see Methods 2.3 for details). While representative single-cell 3D reconstructions revealed subtle time-dependent shifts (Fig. 5B), manual quantification remained challenging. To rigorously classify these cellular states, we collected the time-series curves for RIPE (shown in Fig. S14 and S15) and RIAE (shown in Fig. S16 and S17), and extracted key time-domain features. For RIPE, the features include half-life, maximum decay slope, early decay rate, tail slope and minimal intensity. For RIAE, the features include recovery rate, maximum recovery slope, early recovery rate, tail slope and maximum intensity. Feature heatmaps visualized a progressive drift in the multi-dimensional feature space, validating the continuity of the biological model (Fig. 5C).

We benchmarked the classification performance of seven machine learning classifiers for binary discrimination (control vs. Aβ 2 h-treated cells) using RIPE-stabilized calcium features (Fig. 5F and G; Fig. S24–S30). The Random Forest model achieved superior performance, with accuracy, precision, and recall all exceeding 97.5% (Fig. 5E; Fig. S18). This significantly outperformed linear models (LDA, Logistic Regression), which plateaued at ∼67% accuracy (Fig. S18). Comparative analysis revealed a critical modality dependence: while RIPE features drove high-precision classification, RIAE features alone yielded only ∼66% binary accuracy (Fig. S20), suggesting that calcium-related kinetics provide more informative early signals in the time domain.

Finally, we evaluated the platform's capacity for five-class classification. We trained neural networks to classify single cells into the five specific progression states. The resulting confusion matrix exhibited high diagonal dominance, indicating the model's ability to resolve even adjacent stages (e.g., Aβ 2 h vs. Aβ 6 h) with high fidelity (Fig. 5D; Fig. S19). Consistent with binary results, RIPE-based networks achieved robust separation, whereas RIAE-based staging failed to resolve stage overlap (Fig. S21). These results confirm that time-resolved, feature-based machine learning effectively distinguishes cells across different Aβ exposure-defined cellular states.

High-accuracy classification from frequency-domain features

Although RIPE-stabilized calcium features proved effective for classification, time-domain analysis of RIAE (membrane fluidity) was limited by the subtlety of diffusion changes, yielding only moderate discriminative power (Figs. S20 and S21). To resolve these complex recovery patterns, we applied a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) to convert FRAP signals into frequency-domain features (Fig. 6B). We used the magnitude spectrum as features.

This spectral transformation revealed stage-specific phenotypic features invisible in the time domain. A comparative heatmap analysis confirmed that while time-domain features exhibited class overlap, frequency magnitude features displayed clear stratification across the five Aβ exposure-duration groups (Fig. 6A and B). These observations suggest that acoustic rotation redistributes information about fine-grained membrane biophysical alterations into frequency-dependent patterns.

We quantified this advantage by benchmarking machine learning performance on time-domain versus frequency-domain features. For binary classification (control vs. Aβ 2 h-treated cells) based on RIAE, applying the FFT led to an increase in accuracy. While time-domain models plateaued at ∼66% accuracy (Fig. 6C), frequency-domain Logistic Regression achieved 99.0% accuracy (cross-validation) (Fig. 6D–G; Fig. S22). This ∼37 percentage-point gain indicates that frequency features of membrane recovery provide a more robust discriminant for identifying the Aβ 2 h cellular state than conventional diffusion-derived metrics.

Furthermore, this FFT-enhanced accuracy extended to five-class classification. Unlike time-domain models, which failed to resolve intermediate stages (Fig. 6E), frequency-domain models successfully stratified the entire continuous trajectory with an overall accuracy of 94.8% (cross-validation) (Fig. 6F–H; Fig. S23). The resulting confusion matrix confirmed that FFT-derived features can discriminate subtle phenotypic differences between neighboring Aβ exposure-duration groups.

Because the FFT transformation produced a large gain in classification accuracy, we performed additional validation audits to test whether this improvement could arise from feature leakage or frequency artifacts. Label-permutation controls showed that shuffled-label performance returned to the five-class chance level, whereas the observed balanced accuracy remained high at 0.948 (permutation p = 0.020; Fig. S49A). Training and held-out accuracies were similar for logistic regression and linear SVM, suggesting that the model is not merely relying on trivial memorization (Fig. S49B). Single-bin diagnostics showed that the best individual FFT bin did not reproduce the full-spectrum performance, and the median single-bin accuracy returned near chance level (Fig. S49C). Frequency-band sensitivity and top-bin removal analyses further showed that discriminative information was distributed across multiple spectral regions rather than being driven by one isolated frequency bin (Fig. S49D and E). Finally, bin-marginal shuffling reduced performance to chance level, and the maximum train-test row overlap was zero (Fig. S49F).

Discussion

Most existing optical approaches mitigate photobleaching or phototoxicity through defensive (light-side control), reducing the number of photons absorbed per unit time and volume. However, when signals are noise-limited, temporal resolution is inevitably sacrificed. Our acoustically driven sample-manipulation approach proactively reshapes the illuminated portion of the specimen, enhancing image quality and reducing photobleaching without increasing excitation dose. After selecting the optimized acoustic parameters, we achieved 99.0% classification accuracy at the single cell level. This performance supports computational stratification of a continuous preclinical trajectory in the Aβ treated cellular model and may complement imaging and fluid biomarkers that primarily report tissue-level or bulk changes.

Our work builds on, but is distinct from, prior acoustic rotation platforms. Earlier acoustofluidic studies established that surface acoustic waves can rotate cells and small organisms in a contactless manner, primarily for orientation control, three-dimensional visualization, and morphological inspection. Here, we use ultra-low-frequency acoustic waves for controlled rotation to reduce localized over-exposure and amplify weak fluorescence kinetics. For CFPB and FRAP applications, we designed tailored rotation protocols. In our previous work [59], we found that acoustic-induced X/Y-axis vibration can induce in-plane rotation, while Z-axis can induce out-of-plane rotation. For CFPB, out-of-plane rotation periodically exposes all regions to the illumination and distributes excitation dose across the cell surface, reducing local hot spots and slowing photobleaching, thereby improving photostability and measurement fidelity.

Conversely, in-plane rotation optimizes FRAP readouts. We designed the FRAP protocol as in-plane rotation during photobleaching, followed by stopping rotation for recovery recording to simultaneously achieve uniform bleaching and high-contrast recovery readout. During in-plane rotation, the cell remains coplanar with the imaging plane, avoiding the global bleaching associated with out-of-plane rotation and preserving the side facing away from illumination as an intact fluorophore reservoir. Meanwhile, rotation effectively sweeps a fixed bleaching field across the cell surface, reducing local overexposure and bleaching nonuniformity. Once entering the recovery phase, stopping rotation keeps the bleached region spatially stable and prevents continuous mixing of unbleached fluorophores into the ROI, an effect that would otherwise produce an apparent dilution-driven pseudo-recovery. This improves the contrast and signal-to-noise ratio between bleached and unbleached regions, yields fluorescence recovery curves with higher contrast and greater fitting stability.

We further found that the half-life of CFPB and the half-recovery time of FRAP exhibit a rise-then-fall trend as the frequency parameter of acoustic-driven rotation increases. We term this phenomenon acousto-bandpass amplification. This bandpass behavior provides a practical rule for selecting operating parameters. At the optimal acoustic frequency, rotation enhances optical kinetics while suppressing noise, thereby improving measurement fidelity. The distinct optimal bands for FRAP and calcium stability could also support frequency-division multiplexing for pathway-selective decoding. The dependence of the bandpass peak on cellular properties (e.g., size-dependent shifts in the frequency-response peak) offers a route to personalize rotational parameters across cell types and potentially organoids, reducing reliance on case-specific calibration for a given biological target. This standardization strengthens downstream computational decoding, rendering ultra-weak functional transitions analytically tractable for staging and mechanism-guided screening.

We coupled this physical amplification with a machine learning framework integrating space-time-frequency domain features across both FRAP and CFPB modalities. Following a systematic feature screening, we found that frequency-domain features extracted solely from FRAP achieved 99.0% binary classification accuracy, indicating that CFPB signals are dispensable in this experimental setting. Unlike calcium imaging requiring viral transduction (e.g., GCaMP), FRAP utilizes generic membrane dyes without complex gene engineering. This suggests that the method may be adaptable to primary samples, pending further validation. Nevertheless, we retained a modular architecture, preserving spatial and temporal modalities to ensure interpretability and robustness across experimental conditions where clean rotational frequency responses may be difficult to obtain.

Rather than treating classification as a single task, we adopt a hierarchical decision strategy that reflects practical screening workflows, a high-sensitivity binary gate followed by fine-grained multiclass staging. The binary model aims to maximize sensitivity and reduce decision complexity when separating clearly normal cells from those that deviate from the physiological baseline. However, once a sample is deemed abnormal, a binary label obscures the biologically meaningful heterogeneity of continuous changes. We implemented a five-stage classification scheme (control, Aβ exposure-duration groups) to refine binary diagnostic outcomes into a continuous trajectory of subtle early-phase alterations. In future studies, this framework could support longitudinal tracking and provide quantitative pharmacodynamic readouts [37,60]. Fine-grained classification also improves mechanistic interpretability by resolving whether the dominant deviation is membrane-centered (FRAP/RIAE-driven) or calcium-centered (CFPB/RIPE-driven). The five-class task kept adjacent groups sufficiently separable for model training and cross-validation.

Despite improved sensitivity and phenotyping depth, the current implementation has several limitations. Although the platform processes 150-200 cells s−1, exceeding manual patch-clamp or confocal workflows, it remains below the throughput of commercial flow cytometry (>104 cells s−1) [61,62]. Accordingly, we position the method as a deep-phenotyping modality for profiling rare subpopulations and the secondary validation of candidates from high-throughput screens, rather than for primary library screening [63]. In addition, because the acousto-bandpass amplification is size-dependent, highly size-heterogeneous samples currently require active frequency tuning or pre-enrichment. A closed-loop implementation with real-time feedback to adjust the drive frequency on a per-cell basis would mitigate this constraint in future versions [64,65].

In parallel, future work must address biological generalization. Our current study focuses on live single-cell optical phenotyping, whereas recent advanced models address complementary molecular or tissue-scale modalities. DrugFormer [66] predicts drug sensitivity from single-cell molecular profiles using graph-enhanced language modeling, while HECLIP [67] infers spatial transcriptomic profiles from H&E histology images using contrastive learning. These frameworks provide useful directions for future multimodal validation. Future work will require independent experimental batches, primary neurons, iPSC-derived neurons, organoids, patient-derived cells, and orthogonal molecular readouts, including single-cell transcriptomics, spatial transcriptomics, proteomics, and drug-response profiling, to determine whether the FFT-derived spectral structure corresponds to broader AD-relevant biological states.

Looking ahead, the bandpass nature of our acoustic amplification provides a basis for future translational validation. By defining an optimal frequency window that maximizes the signal-to-noise ratio for specific cell sizes, we will standardize the detection of subtle membrane defects associated with early Aβ toxicity. In principle, this physics-driven sensitivity could support minimally invasive workflows in which peripheral cells report AD-relevant biophysical alterations, but this will require validation in primary samples and clinical cohorts. Beyond neurodegeneration, this active biophysical strategy will be broadly applicable to other mechanosensitive pathologies, such as screening for membrane softening in metastatic cancer cells or characterizing cytoskeletal rigidification during immune synapse formation. By combining physical signal amplification with machine-learning inference, this work provides a general approach for quantifying transient functional states in living cells.
