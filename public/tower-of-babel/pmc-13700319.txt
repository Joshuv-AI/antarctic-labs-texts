Participatory action research in rural and vulnerable settings for community health: a systematic literature review
Authors: Miriam Carmena del Viso, Umayma Beni-Abdellah, Ana Moradell, Carlos Navas-Ferrer, Isabel Blázquez-Ornat, Armando Raimundo, Indrani Kalkan, Isabel Antón-Solanas, Isabel Iguacel, David Navarrete-Villanueva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCommunity participation is a key strategy to reduce health inequities, particularly in rural or socially vulnerable settings. Participatory Action Research (PAR) provides methodological frameworks to actively engage communities across all phases of health interventions. However, systematic guidance on the concrete implementation of participatory action research is limited, and few studies explicitly evaluate the levels of community participation reached.ObjectiveTo critically analyze qualitative studies implementing PAR in rural and vulnerable contexts by describing the participatory techniques employed, assessing the levels of community engagement achieved (based on the AdaptaGPS adaptation of the NICE NG44 guideline), and examining the utility and outcomes of these participatory approaches.MethodsA qualitative systematic review was conducted following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Six databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, SciELO, IBECS, and CUIDEN Plus) and additional sources were searched up to April 2025. Eligible studies were qualitative and demonstrated a community participation level of ≥ 3. Methodological quality was assessed using the CASP tool, and findings were thematically synthesized.ResultsA total of 43 studies were included, employing a range of PAR techniques such as Photovoice, Community Advisory Boards, intersectoral coalitions, co-creation methods, and Indigenous-led approaches. Techniques embedded within existing community structures were more effective in fostering network-building and sustainability. Expressive methods promoted critical reflection and political awareness.ConclusionsPAR techniques applied in rural and socially vulnerable contexts show strong potential for promoting empowerment and community-led transformation. Improving methodological rigor, systematizing participatory practices, and evaluating their structural impact on health equity are essential for advancing knowledge, informing policy, and strengthening practice in this field.RegistrationPROSPERO 2025 CRD420251059871 (May 27, 2025).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28651-z.Keywords: Participatory action research, Community participation, Rural health, Health equity, Vulnerable populations, Qualitative systematic review

Introduction

Population health is influenced by a complex interplay of factors, with social, economic, political, and environmental determinants playing a central role that goes beyond individual behaviors or genetic factors [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the social determinants of health include income, education, employment, physical and social environments, access to healthcare services, civic participation, and community cohesion [2]. Inequities in access to and control over these resources lead to avoidable and systematic health disparities.

Vulnerable contexts are characterized by the accumulation of structural conditions that systematically disadvantage access to the resources needed to maintain or improve health. From the WHO’s social determinants of health framework, these inequities are articulated across intersecting axes such as gender, social class, ethnicity, and geography [3]. The territorial axis becomes particularly salient in rural areas, where geographic isolation, low population density, and lack of infrastructure limit equitable access to services, rights, and conditions for healthy living [1, 2].

Rural and socially vulnerable populations often face structural barriers that negatively affect their physical, mental, and social well-being. These include distance from healthcare facilities, scarcity of material and human resources in public services, limited availability of healthy food and safe spaces for physical exercise, and weaker community support networks [4–6]. These challenges are compounded by institutional invisibility, political underrepresentation, and the absence of participatory mechanisms that amplify the voices of these populations in decision-making processes [7].

However, rural communities should not be viewed solely through a deficit lens. In many contexts, limited access to services has also fostered strong forms of community solidarity, local leadership, and endogenous participation, enabling communities to identify priorities, mobilize local resources, and develop context-specific responses to health challenges. Recent participatory studies have shown that rural communities can successfully lead health initiatives by building on local knowledge, existing social networks, and community assets rather than relying exclusively on external resources [8, 9].

For decades, international organizations such as the WHO, the European Commission, and public health agencies have advocated for the active involvement of communities in the design, implementation, and evaluation of health interventions [10–12]. Community participation is now recognized not only as an ethical principle, linked to self-determination and equity, but also as an effective strategy to improve cultural relevance, effectiveness, and sustainability of public health policies [13, 14]. Participatory and deliberative processes contribute to the generation of situated knowledge, the strengthening of social capital, and the fostering of ownership over change processes [15]. Within this framework, Participatory Action Research (PAR) has been recognized as a valuable methodological approach for facilitating transformative community participation, although its effectiveness depends on the sociocultural context, the quality of implementation, and the degree of community engagement.

PAR is based on collaboration between community members, professionals, and academics to investigate issues grounded in lived experience, with the explicit aim of promoting collective action [15, 16]. Techniques within the PAR framework are diverse and heterogeneous, involving different levels of engagement and interaction formats [17]. Their effectiveness varies according to the local context, target population, project objectives, and available resources. However, many studies agree that the deeper and more authentic the participation, the greater the sustainability and relevance of the solutions generated [18]. Genuine participation is therefore not only an ethical imperative, but also a critical condition for the success of community-based health interventions [19]. This perspective also resonates with feminist approaches to research as a practice committed to social transformation. Feminist scholarship challenges positivist and androcentric paradigms that have historically excluded subjectivity, lived experience, and the voices of marginalized communities [18].

Thus, unlike traditional methodologies that view people as passive subjects of study, PAR acknowledges their capacity to generate knowledge and act upon their realities. It is guided by principles of horizontality, knowledge co-construction, reciprocity, social justice, and collective action [19, 20]. From this perspective, collaborative knowledge production emerges from concrete and collective lived experiences, positioning PAR not merely as a participatory method, but as a critical praxis capable of democratizing the research process and fostering socially meaningful change [20].

Community participation in health encompasses a broad range of approaches through which communities are involved in health promotion, planning, implementation, and evaluation [21, 22]. Within this broader framework, PAR represents a specific research methodology that seeks to generate knowledge through collaborative action while promoting social transformation. Specifically, participation in the context of PAR is understood as a relational, situated, and transformative process in which individuals are not passive recipients of interventions but active agents in the collective construction of knowledge and social change [15, 19]. It must go beyond consultative or symbolic mechanisms and foster genuine involvement grounded in horizontality, trust, and mutual respect [15, 19, 22]. Furthermore, for participation to be considered meaningful, it must allow communities to influence decisions, share power, and take ownership of the outcomes [22, 23].

Several theoretical frameworks have been developed to assess the quality and scope of community participation, although they were designed for different contexts and purposes. Notable among them are Arnstein’s Ladder of Participation, which conceptualizes participation as a progression toward citizen power [23]; Rifkin’s framework for community participation in health programmes [24], further operationalized through the indicators proposed by Draper et al. [25]; Pretty’s typology, originally developed in the context of rural development [26]; and, more recently, the NICE guideline NG44 (Community engagement: improving health and wellbeing and reducing health inequalities), which classifies participation into five levels: informing, consulting, involving, collaborating, and empowering [27]. Given the objective of this review, the AdaptaGPS framework was considered the most appropriate tool for evaluating participation in PAR studies, as it integrates health participation frameworks while specifically assessing participatory processes in health interventions [27, 28].

Previous reviews [15, 18, 29] have shown that participatory action research can strengthen social cohesion, improve health outcomes, foster community empowerment, and produce more effective and sustainable interventions in a range of contexts, including mental health promotion, chronic disease prevention, school health programs, and public health emergency responses. More recent systematic reviews have confirmed the continued expansion of participatory approaches in health research while also highlighting persistent methodological challenges, including heterogeneous terminology, variability in partnership evaluation, and inconsistent reporting of participatory processes. These ongoing gaps underscore the need for reviews that examine not only the outcomes of PAR but also the participatory techniques and methodological characteristics underpinning their implementation [30–32]. However, its implementation still presents methodological challenges, such as building trust, redistributing decision-making power, ensuring broad and equitable representation, and aligning institutional timelines with community processes [33, 34]. Furthermore, despite the growing interest in participatory action research, there remains a significant gap in the literature regarding how these techniques are being used in rural and vulnerable contexts, what levels of community engagement are actually achieved, how interventions are methodologically structured, and what impacts they generate. While some reviews have addressed community participation in health, none have systematically synthesized qualitative evidence focused on specific participatory techniques in these settings, with rigorous analysis of their methodological specificities and participatory depth.

This systematic review aims to identify and analyze qualitative studies using PAR techniques in community health interventions conducted in rural or socially vulnerable contexts, describing their methodological features, the levels of participation achieved, and the outcomes reported. Furthermore, it offers a synthesis of how these techniques have been applied in practice, highlighting the methodological strategies, actors involved, and transformations generated to inform future participatory health initiatives.

Materials and methods

Study design

A systematic review of the literature was conducted to identify and analyze research studies employing PAR techniques in rural and vulnerable settings. Special attention was paid to the degree of community participation, the stages in which the population was involved, and the effects on health, equity, and collective action.

The review followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) [35] and combined systematic analysis with a critical appraisal of the level of participation and social transformation within PAR processes. The review protocol was registered in the PROSPERO database (registration number: CRD420251059871).

Eligibility criteria

Qualitative studies evaluating PAR-based interventions in rural or vulnerable settings were included, regardless of population group or specific community health context. For the purpose of this review, “vulnerable settings” were defined as environments characterized by social, economic, or territorial conditions that restrict equitable access to health-related resources such as healthcare services, healthy food, or safe spaces for physical exercise. This definition encompasses areas affected by structural poverty, Indigenous communities, sparsely populated regions, forced migration, structural discrimination, or social exclusion, in line with the social determinants of health framework proposed by the WHO [36] and the NICE NG44 guideline on community engagement in health [27].

Studies were included if they involved community members (citizens), as well as professionals from healthcare, education, or social work sectors, and other key stakeholders such as institutional representatives, members of community-based organizations, local leaders, or policy-makers. This decision aligns with the PAR approach, which conceives participation as a collective process involving multiple actors committed to improving community health and well-being [37, 38].

Studies were defined as PAR-based if they described collaborative processes between professionals and communities aimed at improving social or health-related conditions, and involved community participation in at least two key stages of the process (e.g., needs assessment, intervention design, implementation, or evaluation) [15, 21].

Only studies documenting a community participation level of 3 or higher were selected. This corresponds to active community involvement in at least one phase of intervention design, implementation, or evaluation, according to the AdaptaGPS adaptation of the NICE NG44 framework [27, 28]. This threshold was chosen because, from level 3 onward, participation goes beyond mere consultation or information-sharing and becomes a structural component of the process, enabling greater community ownership, agency, and alignment with core PAR principles. These levels entail a redistribution of decision-making power, which is considered a minimum requirement for ensuring a genuinely transformative and equity-oriented participatory approach.

Quantitative studies were excluded because they generally do not provide sufficient detail regarding participatory processes, decision-making dynamics, or community involvement to allow assessment using the AdaptaGPS framework. Narrative reviews, systematic reviews, and scoping reviews were also excluded.

Sources of information and search strategy

The literature search was conducted in the following electronic databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, SciELO, IBECS, and CUIDEN Plus. The search covered all records from the inception of each database through April 2025. No formal restrictions were applied regarding language or publication date in order to maximize sensitivity and capture the widest possible range of relevant studies. However, only articles published in English or Spanish were ultimately included, reflecting the language competencies of the review team.

The search strategy combined MeSH descriptors and free-text terms related to PAR, community participation, public health, social inequities, and rural contexts. Search strategies were tailored to the syntax and indexing systems of each database and were fully documented to ensure reproducibility. The complete search strategy is available as supplementary material (Appendix A).

Study selection process

The selection process was conducted in two consecutive phases. First, titles and abstracts were screened to identify potentially eligible articles. Then, full-text assessments were performed on the preselected studies. This process was carried out independently by two reviewers (MC and UBA) using a structured screening form developed in Microsoft Excel. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, or when necessary, by consultation with a third reviewer (DN), who acted as an adjudicator. The overall selection process is summarized in the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram [35], presented in the Results section.

Variables of interest

From each included study, the following variables were extracted: authorship, year of publication, country or region, study context, participant population, PAR technique used, stages of the participatory process in which the community was involved, and the level of community participation achieved.

In order to deepen the qualitative analysis of the identified PAR techniques, additional information was collected on their methodological features, including mode of implementation, actors involved, tools used, phases of participation, duration, and contextual conditions.

The level of community participation was assessed using the AdaptaGPS framework, which adapts the NICE NG44 guideline to the Spanish public health context by operationalizing its five levels of community participation through context-specific criteria while preserving the original conceptual framework [27, 28]. Because it is based on the internationally recognized NICE NG44 guideline, its underlying principles can be applied across different settings; however, the interpretation of participation levels should take into account cultural, organizational, and health system differences. This model defines five progressive levels of community participation, ranging from information to community empowerment, as summarized in (Table 1).

Quality appraisal

The methodological quality of the included qualitative studies was assessed using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) tool [39]. This instrument evaluates the rigor of qualitative research design through 10 items covering aspects such as clarity of objectives, methodological appropriateness, transparency in data collection and analysis, ethical considerations, and the relevance of findings. It enables the identification of common strengths and limitations across the body of literature reviewed.

Two researchers (MC and UBA) independently conducted the appraisal, applying a qualitative scoring approach based on full, partial, or non-compliance with each item. This critical appraisal was used to deepen the interpretation of findings and to weigh the contribution of each study within the overall synthesis.

Results

Search results

A total of 1,607 records were identified through six electronic databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, SciELO, IBECS, and CUIDEN Plus). An additional five articles were retrieved through other sources, including reference list screening of the included studies. After removing duplicates (n = 583), 1,029 titles and abstracts were screened. Of these, 84 articles were selected for full-text review, and ultimately, 43 studies met the selection criteria. The main reasons for exclusion were: failure to reach the minimum required level of community participation (level 3 according to the AdaptaGPS framework) (n = 35), absence of a participatory intervention (n = 3), use of a quantitative design (n = 1), and inclusion of narrative, scoping, or theoretical reviews (n = 2). Characteristics of the studies

The 43 studies included in the qualitative synthesis were published between 2002 and 2025. Most were conducted in the United States (n = 29) [40–69], followed by Canada (n = 4) [70–73], Spain (n = 2) [74, 75], Brazil (n = 1) [76], Malaysia (n = 1) [8], India (n = 1) [77], Pakistan (n = 1) [78], Ghana (n = 1) [79], South Africa (n = 1) [80], and the United Kingdom (n = 1) [9]. One multicountry study was conducted across Denmark, the Netherlands, Nigeria, and South Africa [80]. All studies were implemented in rural settings or among socially vulnerable populations.

Regarding the PAR techniques used, the most common approaches included Photovoice [8, 41–43, 50–52, 59, 61, 71, 76, 77]; Community Advisory Boards (CABs) [44, 45, 55, 56, 60, 64–66, 69], community or intersectoral coalitions [9, 46, 48, 54, 62, 64, 68, 69] co-creation methodologies [9, 40, 60 ,63, 71, 72, 78], and Indigenous or community-led participatory frameworks [44, 57, 70, 72, 73, 79]. In some cases, multiple participatory techniques were combined to strengthen community engagement throughout the process. For a detailed comparative description, see Appendix B.

Participant populations were diverse and included rural residents [40, 42, 45, 46, 52, 54, 60, 61], Indigenous communities [51, 57, 58, 66], youth [41, 52, 53, 60, 65], women [44, 49, 50], older adults [70, 75], and other socially vulnerable groups [8, 9, 40, 43, 46, 48, 54, 60, 62, 67, 68]. Most interventions involved active collaboration with local organizations, healthcare professionals, community health workers, and social leaders [40, 75, 45, 54, 60, 61, 70, 75].

In terms of the level of community participation, according to the AdaptaGPS adaptation of the NICE NG44 guideline, 42 out of 43 studies (97.7%) achieved high levels of engagement, corresponding to levels 4 (collaboration) and 5 (empowerment), which involve shared decision-making or community leadership in the participatory process: 15 studies (34.9%) reached level 4 (collaboration) [8, 41–43, 47, 50, 56–60, 62, 65, 67, 77, 78], and 27 studies (62.8%) reached level 5 (empowerment) [40, 44–46, 48, 49, 53–55, 58, 61, 63, 64, 66, 68, 69–72, 75, 76, 77, 79, 80]. Only one study (2.3%) achieved level 3 (involvement) [51]. Community involvement was particularly prominent during the needs assessment and priority-setting phases [8, 40–62, 75, 75, 58], though in several studies, it also extended to planning, implementation, and evaluation stages [9, 41–74, 76, 79, 80].

Key outcomes reported across the studies included the strengthening of community networks [40, 46, 49, 54, 61, 62, 64] increased individual and collective empowerment [41, 43, 47, 50, 61, 63, 73], identification of barriers and facilitators for health promotion in rural settings [8, 40, 41, 43, 48, 49, 51, 55, 61, 78] and the development and, in several studies, implementation of community-driven proposals and interventions to improve healthy eating, physical activity, health equity, and access to health services [41, 42, 45–47, 50, 51, 58, 63, 66, 68]. A detailed description of the characteristics of the included studies is available in Appendix C.

The following sections present the findings organized by PAR technique, including a concise methodological description and a summary of the main results and impacts observed.

Photovoice

Photovoice was employed in twelve of the studies analyzed, primarily involving youth, women in rural areas, or Indigenous communities. This visual participatory technique enabled not only the identification of social and health-related issues, but also the expression of emotions, the recovery of collective memory, and the formulation of contextually grounded proposals for change. Studies such as those by Helm et al. [52], Costa et al. [76], and Folkerth et al. [49] documented how active participation in photo-based reflection sessions fostered processes of empowerment and ownership over the community narrative. In Costa et al. [76], one participant stated: “I never thought my photos would speak so loudly; now I feel like my community is listening to me.”

Regarding the level of participation achieved, over 60% of the studies using Photovoice reached levels 4 or 5 (co-production and empowerment), as observed in the work of Costa et al. [76], Helm et al. [52], and Folkerth et al. [49]. In these cases, photographs were not only used for diagnostic purposes, but also played a direct role in shaping local policy or practice, with community representatives actively involved in public exhibitions and advocacy. The duration of interventions ranged from 4 weeks to 6 months, depending on the degree of community engagement and institutional support.

Community Advisory Boards (CAB)

CABs were documented in nine studies, particularly in settings with established institutional frameworks such as universities, health centers, and publicly funded programs. Aronoff-Spencer et al. [40], Davis et al. [47], and Parker et al. [62] highlighted that CABs functioned as mechanisms for validation, feedback, and shared decision-making throughout the interventions. Their primary role was to ensure cultural relevance of materials and to promote continuous communication between communities and researchers.

While most CABs were established during the early design phases of the interventions, in studies such as those by Rhodes et al. [63] and Chinapaw et al. [80], their involvement extended through to the final evaluation, contributing to the redefinition of success indicators based on community-driven criteria. The level of participation ranged from level 3 (active collaboration) to level 4 (co-design), depending on the degree of autonomy granted to the board.

Community or intersectoral coalitions

This participatory strategy was documented in eight studies, including those by Cameron et al. [70], Ko et al. [56], and Jäderholm et al. [53]. These coalitions involved representatives from neighborhood associations, local institutions, NGOs, and public services, articulating a horizontal governance model that enabled the development of community health policies. Notably, the studies by Skewes et al. [66] and Gandy et al. [50] demonstrated how multisectoral engagement sustained actions over time and amplified their impact through co-created strategic plans.

In terms of participation, most of these experiences reached level 4 (co-production), and some achieved level 5 (empowerment), as illustrated in the work of Barnidge et al. [42], where participatory budgeting enabled community members to directly decide how health resources would be allocated.

Co-creation / co-design

Co-creation methodologies were applied in seven studies, with a particular focus on youth, migrants, and sexual minorities, as seen in the work of Chinapaw et al. [80], Markham et al. [72], and Rhodes et al. [63]. This technique stood out for its capacity to adapt content to the cultural and generational specificities of the participating groups. In Chinapaw’s study, for instance, adolescents co-designed physical activity interventions and urban improvements, stating: “They let us decide what to say and how to say it, so we trust the message more.”

The methodology employed included iterative workshops, empathy mapping, creative dynamics, and validation sessions. The recorded level of participation was predominantly level 4 (co-production), with some cases reaching level 5 (empowerment) when the community-led proposals were implemented with sustained local leadership.

Community leadership / Indigenous approaches

Finally, approaches grounded in Indigenous or community leadership were identified in six studies. In the works of Jernigan et al. [54], Dakubo [79], and Mahmood et al. [78], traditional knowledge systems and local social organization frameworks guided the entire participatory research process, from diagnosis to evaluation. In these cases, the community was not merely a participant but the driving force behind the process. Techniques included dialogue circles, collective rituals, and oral forms of knowledge transmission, all aligned with the local cosmovision. A detailed description of these culturally embedded methods can be found in Appendix D.

Participation levels in these cases consistently corresponded to level 5 (empowerment). As noted in Dakubo’s study [79]: “Self-determination was a precondition for participation; we did not come to collaborate, we came to lead.”

Results of the methodological quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the CASP tool for qualitative research. For descriptive purposes, each CASP item was scored as follows: “Yes” = 1 point, “Partial” = 0.5 points, and “No” = 0 points, yielding a maximum score of 10. Studies scoring 9–10 were classified as high quality, whereas those scoring 7–8 were considered moderate quality. A detailed table summarizing the assessment is available in Appendix E. This matrix systematically outlines the methodological strengths and limitations of each study, contributing to transparency in the critical appraisal process.

Overall, the quality of the studies ranged from moderate to high, based on the CASP checklist. Scores ranged from 7 to 10 out of 10, with studies scoring 7–8 considered moderate quality and those scoring 9–10 considered high quality. 95% of the studies clearly stated a research objective and provided an appropriate rationale for the use of qualitative methods. 87% described an adequate participant selection strategy, and in 84% of the cases, data collection procedures were aligned with the research aims.

Among the main methodological limitations identified, only 56% of the studies explicitly addressed researcher reflexivity and its potential influence on the research process. Ethical issues were adequately reported in 68% of the articles. Additionally, approximately 30% of the studies lacked sufficient detail and depth in the description and analysis of qualitative data.

Most studies (92%) supported their findings with clear and relevant evidence, and 88% presented results with practical applicability for community health interventions. These findings suggest that, although there is room for improvement, the overall methodological quality of the included studies is sufficient to support the conclusions of this systematic review.

Discussion

This systematic review identified and analyzed 43 qualitative studies that implemented PAR techniques in community health interventions carried out in rural or socially vulnerable settings. The findings show that PAR is increasingly applied in community health and that its use has intensified over the past decade, coinciding with greater institutional and academic attention to participation, equity, and territorial justice [60, 65, 67, 69].

PAR offers not only an ethical and epistemological framework for community work, but also adaptable tools tailored to specific social, cultural, and territorial realities. The review identified multiple PAR techniques implemented in these contexts, each with distinct characteristics, advantages, and degrees of participation, reinforcing the need for flexible and context-sensitive planning.

Photovoice was among the most widely used techniques, particularly in interventions targeting youth, rural women, and Indigenous communities. Its implementation showed high potential to foster empowerment, critical reflection, and the visibility of health and social issues from the participants’ own perspectives [49, 67]. A key finding of this review is that studies [49, 67] employing the SHOWED method during group reflection sessions reported deeper levels of collective analysis and significant community ownership of the findings, which translated into active participation even during dissemination. The SHOWED method is a structured reflexive tool used in Photovoice research based on the sequence: What do you See? (S), What is Happening? (H), How does this relate to Our lives? (O), Why does this problem exist? (W), How can we Educate others? (E), and What can we Do about it? (D). This structure promotes critical thinking, personal connection, and action-oriented outcomes. This methodological feature may explain why studies using SHOWED reached level 5 participation (empowerment), whereas more descriptive studies lacking this structured reflection remained at levels 3 or 4. For instance, in the study by Zoellner et al. [69], adolescent participants expressed: “We took the photos, we talked about them, and then we showed them to the whole town… I felt like it was our voices being heard.” These narratives contrast with other studies [41, 43] which, although using Photovoice, did not include feedback or community leadership in dissemination. This was the case of Behringer et al. [43] and Balvanz et al. [41], where images were limited to diagnostic purposes without prompting collective action. This suggests that the methodological structure, especially the use of reflexive cycles and feedback mechanisms, is critical to the level of participation and empowerment achieved.

Several studies noted important limitations in the implementation of Photovoice, particularly regarding technological access and skills. For example, in rural settings or among older populations [42, 49], difficulties in operating cameras or smartphones required simplification of the technique or reliance on external facilitators, risking the mediation of participants’ voices. Similarly, in youth populations, a lack of familiarity with the reflexive use of images sometimes limited critical analysis and turned the exercise into a more descriptive than transformative activity [52, 65]. These methodological limitations should be addressed in the design of participatory processes by adapting resources, timelines, and technical support to the participants’ capacities and contexts.

Regarding CABs, there was variability in their structure and level of institutionalization. Dailey et al. [46] and Jäderholm et al. [53] described CABs with real consultative capacity and regular meetings, where methodological decisions were validated and adapted by community members, thus achieving level 4 participation. Conversely, in other studies such as Davis et al. [47], CABs were formal but minimally active, with limited influence on decision-making, thereby reducing their participatory impact. The lack of documentation about CABs’ internal dynamics or their role in decision-making was a recurring methodological shortcoming.

Community and intersectoral coalitions appeared in larger-scale studies, such as Rhodes et al. [58] and Adsul et al. [77]. Both project-specific and pre-existing community or institutional coalitions were identified across the included studies. Regardless of their origin, coalitions that reported sustained collaboration, clearly defined roles, and opportunities for shared decision-making appeared to facilitate higher levels of community participation. These networks have the potential to mobilize diverse actors and foster community health governance, but their success depended on factors like the duration of the process, stable funding, and pre-existing structures. In Rhodes et al. [63], for instance, the coalition developed community indicators and participatory budgets, reaching level 5 participation. In contrast, in Yeary et al. [68], the coalition served only an instrumental role in recruitment, leading to lower community ownership. This comparison highlights the importance of stable structures with real decision-making power to achieve meaningful outcomes. The included studies also suggested that contextual factors such as governance arrangements, institutional capacity, existing community networks, and Indigenous or local leadership may have influenced how participatory approaches were implemented and the levels of participation achieved. However, these factors were reported inconsistently across studies, limiting more detailed cross-context comparisons.

Studies using co-creation or co-design approaches were characterized by cultural adaptability and methodological creativity. In the work of Miyamoto et al. [61] and Montesanti et al. [73], prototyping activities, empathy mapping, and ideation workshops helped generate innovative solutions aligned with the needs of migrant and LGBTQ+ populations. In both cases, participation was sustained throughout the intervention cycle, including the final evaluation, which was associated with high satisfaction and sustainability. These methodological features suggest that co-creation reaches its full potential when articulated as an iterative and participatory process embedded across all phases of the intervention.

Lastly, experiences based on community or Indigenous leadership, such as those undertaken by Dakubo [9], Serafica et al. [64], and Mahmood et al. [78], stood out for their cultural coherence, decolonial orientation, and integration of local knowledge systems. In these cases, participation extended beyond methodology to become a political and epistemological practice. Key decisions were made by community actors, and the processes were guided by horizontal, relational logics. Nevertheless, these studies often lacked structured documentation, complicating comparison with more conventional approaches. Despite this, their transformative impact on communities was evident and should be valued using contextually appropriate criteria. Accordingly, the application of participation frameworks should consider the cultural, organizational, and health system context in which PAR is conducted, as these factors may influence both participatory processes and their interpretation.

Beyond the participatory techniques used, the included studies displayed wide heterogeneity in data collection and qualitative analysis. Most studies relied on audio or video recordings of participatory sessions (focus groups, workshops, collective interviews), which were later transcribed and analyzed using thematic approaches, content analysis, or narrative techniques. However, important differences were found in the depth of analysis and methodological transparency. Whilst some studies [39, 80] provided detailed coding and triangulation procedures, including community involvement in analysis, strengthening the validity of the findings, others [10, 41, 54, 56, 60, 72] merely presented findings without clarifying their analytical process, weakening the traceability of knowledge production.

Participant observation was rarely used explicitly, despite its potential to enrich contextual analysis. Only Dakubo [79] and Serafica et al. [64] mentioned observation as an additional data source, which enhanced holistic understanding of community processes. Similarly, discourse analysis or critical language approaches were applied in very few cases, representing a missed methodological opportunity, especially in studies addressing power dynamics or social exclusion.

These methodological limitations also extend to the reporting of participatory processes. Although the studies included in this review consistently described participatory approaches as collaborative and inclusive, some studies implicitly addressed issues related to power, culture, or vulnerable populations; however, relatively few explicitly reported reflexive considerations regarding researchers’ positionality, intersectional social factors, or existing power relations that may have influenced participation, decision-making, and knowledge co-production. Consequently, these dimensions could not be systematically assessed or compared across studies. Future PAR studies would benefit from more explicit reporting of these dimensions, as this would strengthen methodological transparency, facilitate cross-context comparisons, and support a more nuanced interpretation of participatory processes across diverse cultural and community settings [19, 22, 37].

Beyond these considerations, the quality of qualitative analysis and methodological reporting also remain areas for improvement. Future research should not only describe participatory phases, but also document the processes of data analysis and interpretation more thoroughly, while critically reflecting on their epistemological and ethical implications. Acknowledging these methodological differences is essential for advancing more rigorous, context-sensitive, and socially transformative PAR [19, 37].

Strengths and limitations of the review

This systematic review presents several noteworthy strengths. First, it is focused exclusively on qualitative studies that applied PAR techniques in health interventions conducted in rural or socially vulnerable settings. This focus allowed for an in-depth exploration of methodological aspects that have been underexamined in previous reviews. Furthermore, the review employed a broad, multilingual, and multidisciplinary search strategy that included regional databases such as SciELO, CUIDEN, and IBECS, thereby enhancing the visibility of studies frequently excluded from global reviews.

Second, the use of the AdaptaGPS framework [28], based on the NICE NG44 guideline [27], enabled the rigorous and context-sensitive systematization and comparison of community participation levels. This tool provided analytical clarity to distinguish between consultative experiences and truly collaborative or empowering processes.

Third, the review followed the methodological recommendations of the PRISMA 2020 statement [35], including a complete flow diagram, peer-reviewed study selection, and quality appraisal using the CASP tool [39]. This adherence strengthens the transparency, traceability, and validity of the findings.

Finally, the review incorporated a complementary narrative analysis of the methodological characteristics of each identified PAR technique, offering operational insights for professionals and researchers. This approach allowed for the identification of patterns related to implementation, duration, stages of participation, and stakeholder engagement.

The included studies exhibit several common limitations. First, there was substantial heterogeneity in the quality of methodological reporting, with frequent omissions regarding the actual role of participants, decision-making mechanisms, and the criteria used to define the impact of the interventions. This lack of detail limits the ability to comparatively assess the participatory quality across studies. Moreover, there is a marked overrepresentation of Anglophone experiences, particularly from the United States and Canada. Although studies from Latin America and Europe were included, the geographical distribution of the identified evidence may have been influenced by the use of search terms primarily developed in English and by the inclusion of studies published only in English or Spanish. Although regional databases such as SciELO, CUIDEN, and IBECS were searched to enhance the identification of literature published outside the Anglosphere, some participatory research published under alternative traditions or terminology (e.g., Investigación-Acción Participativa or Pesquisa-Ação), particularly if not indexed with English-language metadata, may not have been retrieved. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the geographical distribution and transferability of the findings [15, 19].

A probable publication bias was also identified, as nearly all studies reported successful experiences or positive outcomes. The lack of published reports on failed initiatives, institutional resistance, or internal conflicts within participatory processes may result in an overly idealized image of PAR and hinder honest learning about its weaknesses and challenges [29].

Regarding the methodological limitations of this review, although a comprehensive search was conducted without applying formal language or publication date restrictions, grey literature and local institutional sources were not included. This may have led to the exclusion of relevant community experiences not indexed in academic databases. Additionally, while study selection was carried out independently by two reviewers with arbitration in case of disagreement, the initial data extraction was conducted by a single reviewer, which may introduce unintended bias.

In addition, although no language restrictions were applied during the database searches, the search strategy was primarily developed using English-language descriptors, which may have reduced the retrieval of studies published under different participatory traditions or terminology in other languages.

Implications for practice, policy, and research

The findings of this review carry significant implications for professional practice, public policy development, and future research agendas. From a practical standpoint, the results help identify PAR techniques that are particularly effective in specific contexts or with certain populations. This analysis may serve as an operational guide for health, education, and social work professionals seeking to implement participatory processes that are rigorous, sustainable, and adapted to the realities of their communities.

At the policy level, the review underscores the need to incorporate systematic and evaluable mechanisms of community participation into public health strategies, particularly in rural, impoverished, or marginalized areas that are often overlooked by dominant urban and institutional health models. The AdaptaGPS framework [28] emerges as a valuable tool for assessing such participation and could be integrated into regional and national community health plans.

Finally, from a research perspective, this review highlights the need to generate longitudinal studies, to document failed or interrupted experiences, and to explore new inclusive and digital participatory formats, especially with young people, migrant populations, and individuals affected by medicalization and social exclusion. Further integration of qualitative and quantitative methodologies is also needed to evaluate not only participatory processes themselves, but also their medium- and long-term impacts on health and wellbeing.

Conclusions

This systematic review consolidates qualitative evidence on the application of PAR techniques in community health interventions conducted in rural and socially vulnerable settings. Rather than constituting a homogeneous set of methodologies, PAR techniques display considerable diversity in terms of approach, depth of participation, and community impact.

The PAR techniques that demonstrated the highest levels of empowerment and community ownership were Photovoice using the SHOWED methodology, intersectoral coalitions with horizontal governance structures, and approaches led by Indigenous communities or established community actors. These strategies not only facilitated greater involvement in the diagnostic phases but also promoted active participation in decision-making, implementation, and dissemination of results, frequently reaching levels 4 and 5 of participation (co-production and empowerment). In contrast, techniques such as CABs and isolated co-creation initiatives showed greater variability in the degree of community engagement. Their success was strongly influenced by factors such as the duration of implementation, availability of funding, and the extent to which decision-making power was shared with the community.

Regarding transferability, while positive outcomes such as higher levels of community participation, empowerment, and community ownership were observed across diverse geographical regions, our synthesis suggests that sociocultural context, institutional frameworks, and the community’s prior experience with collective organization may influence the implementation and impact of PAR techniques. Not all techniques are equally effective in all settings, and their impact depends on context-sensitive planning, shared leadership, and sustained institutional commitment.

In conclusion, the findings of this review underscore the need to strengthen methodological rigor in PAR studies, promote authentic participation strategies, and document more thoroughly the mechanisms that enable transformative processes. Only through such efforts can we move toward more equitable, democratic, and contextually grounded approaches to community health.
