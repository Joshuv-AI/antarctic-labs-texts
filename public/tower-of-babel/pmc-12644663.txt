Trajectory planning for drone landing, incorporating wind-sensing capabilities, operational and safety objectives, and reinforcement learning
Authors: Hao Xiong, Long Li, Weifeng Zeng, Daiying Li, Yutong Liu, Franz Raps, Yunjiang Lou, Bernd R Noack
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrone flight safety and operational efficiency are challenged in the landing phase, especially in windy conditions. While flight control should be a key focus, flight trajectory also plays a critical role in landing. Inspired by the multiple objectives, wind sensory capability, and skill learning of avian species, this study proposes a reinforcement learning-based trajectory planner for a drone to perform trajectory planning in the wind, aiming to balance multiple landing-related objectives based on onboard wind sensory capability and address the safety-operational efficiency dilemma of a landing site. Through four key experiments, this study demonstrates successful training, balanced landing performance, and strong generalization capability of the trajectory planner. The experiments highlight the importance of velocity sensory capability while indicating that wind sensory capability is less critical to the trajectory planner. The proposed framework with multiple objectives, wind sensory capability, and skill learning can benefit applications such as improving drone performance.Subject terms: Aerospace engineering, Fluid dynamics, Biomimetics

Introduction

Drones are increasingly utilized across diverse fields, including delivery services1, agriculture2, urban management3, and military operations4. Among the various stages of drone operation, the landing phase (illustrated in Fig. 1a) poses the greatest challenge due to its implications for safety and operational efficiency, particularly under windy conditions5. Beyond safety considerations, landing also requires balancing other objectives, including minimizing time consumption6 and energy consumption7, while ensuring a smooth descent8. Achieving a safe and efficient landing demands a carefully planned trajectory that integrates multiple objectives, accounts for polymorphic modal sensory inputs (e.g., the position and velocity of a drone as well as wind information) during planning, and employs an effective trajectory planning strategy.

Scholars acknowledge that drone landings are inherently risky and demand precisely engineered controllers along with high-accuracy sensors5. Scholars and engineers have developed sophisticated flight controllers based on, for instance, proportional integral derivative (PID) control9, model predictive control (MPC)10, and robust control11 to achieve the flight control of drones and address disturbances (e.g., wind). Despite these advancements, flight controllers rely on well-defined target trajectories to function effectively. While flight control receives great emphasis, flight trajectory is also critical for landing.

Delivery drones are usually required to cruise to a region above a landing site and then land vertically and slowly (Supplementary Video 1 and Supplementary Fig. 4). In this study, a trajectory planner that designs a direct vertical landing trajectory is termed the conventional trajectory planner. According to the conventional trajectory planner, the vertical approach path overlaps with the vertical departure path of a landing site. As the number of drones increases around a landing site-common in urban environments-this overlapping design can cause spacing conflicts and reduce operational efficiency. Furthermore, the performance balance of the conventional trajectory planner remains uncertain. An investigation of drone landing trajectories is necessary and may provide interesting insights.

Scholars have proposed several trajectory planning approaches for drones12,13. Many of these approaches prioritize obstacle avoidance to ensure safety14. Several existing trajectory planning approaches focus on improving a certain performance of a drone, such as time consumption15 or energy consumption16,17. However, landing-phase trajectory planning must address safety concerns, including wind effects, tracking errors, and control authority, alongside objectives like smoothness, time efficiency, and energy conservation. Enhancing drone landing performance under windy conditions requires advanced trajectory planning approaches that balance safety, efficiency, smoothness, and control authority.

The trajectory planning of a drone in the wind by taking multiple objectives (e.g., safety, time, energy, and smoothness) and multiple sensory capabilities (e.g., wind sensory capability) into account can be challenging in three main aspects. (1) Objectives - the objectives beyond achieving low velocity after being close to a landing position, along with their appropriate quantitative representations, remain unclear. (2) sensory capabilities - beyond position sensory capability, which is essential for reaching a landing position, it is uncertain whether additional sensory inputs, such as wind sensory capability, are critical for trajectory planning in windy conditions. (3) Integration of objectives and sensory capabilities for planning - developing an effective trajectory planner becomes more challenging when accounting for complex objectives and sensory inputs.

Drone control and landing control: The landing phase is the most critical phase of drone operations due to challenges posed by wind disturbances and proximity to obstacles and the ground5. Scholars have developed different control approaches for drones to improve flight control from the sophisticated PID control approach18 and MPC control approach19 to advanced control approaches for addressing fault-tolerant control20, anti-disturbance control9, and anti-wind control21. Several methods specifically targeting landing control22, including landing in windy conditions, have also been proposed5,23,24. However, most of these approaches emphasize landing control without exploring how trajectory planning can enhance landing performance. Studies on landing control have not clarified the influence of flight trajectory on landing performance. The importance of a flight trajectory to the landing performance of a drone remains unexplored.

Trajectory planning approaches: Scholars have studied drone trajectory planning for years, proposing numerous approaches13,25. The trajectory planning approaches can be based on continuous-time polynomial representation or discrete-time state space representation. Continuous-time polynomial representation-based trajectory planning approaches use polynomial formulation26,27 to exploit the differentially flat output states of a drone with high computational efficiency. Discrete-time state space representation-based trajectory planning approaches plan time-discretized trajectories and include search and sampling-based methods28–30 or optimization-based methods31,32. In addition, scholars have proposed learning-based approaches33–35 for the navigation36–38 and trajectory planning39,40 of drones. Moreover, trajectory planning approaches have been proposed to achieve different objectives in addition to reaching a target position, including improving time consumption15,41–43 and energy consumption16,44. Specifically, Scholars proposed a multi-phase optimal control framework to address the trajectory planning of drones for increasing field area coverage and reducing energy45. The drone fleet landing planning problem has been solved by using mixed integer programming techniques46. A swarm optimization algorithm combining the dragonfly optimization method and the differential evolution method has been proposed to address the landing planning of a drone47. Dynamic programming techniques have been used to investigate drone landing planning in three scenarios, including minimum-time landing, minimum-energy landing, and planned landing48. Scholars have investigated the optimal trajectory planning of a drone acting as a communication base station and applied RL to maximize the sum rate of the transmission49. However, most existing trajectory planning approaches have not addressed wind effects and prioritize specific objectives, such as time efficiency, rather than emphasizing a balance among multiple objectives for drone landing.

Wind sensory capability for trajectory planning: Wind can affect the future velocity and position of a drone. The perception of wind could be critical for the trajectory planning of a drone landing in the wind. The Federal Aviation Administration mandates that the approach path of vertical take-off and landing vehicles align with prevailing wind directions to minimize downwind and crosswind operations, highlighting wind’s critical role in landings22,50. Scholars also have paid attention to and disclosed the importance of the interaction between wind and a drone in areas such as flight control and global path planning in a large-scale environment, as shown in Fig. 2a. For global path planning, path planning approaches have been proposed to utilize tailwind wind to improve energy consumption based on a large-scale wind field map17,51–53. For flight control, scholars have proposed to sense and compensate for wind effects21,24. With an assumption of a uniform flow field around a drone in small time scales and spatial scales, wind sensory capability achieved based on onboard sensors has been used to improve flight control performance21. However, it is not yet sure if wind sensory capability is crucial for local trajectory planning (Fig. 2a) in a uniform flow field in small time scales and spatial scales (e.g., trajectory planning for an approach to land in seconds), as much as wind sensory capability that has been proved crucial for global path planning and flight control.

Applying biomimicry principles to equip machines and robots with abilities akin to natural organisms is becoming a prominent focus in the artificial intelligence and robotics fields54–59. Scholars frequently derive behavioral features from natural biological entities and integrate these features into designs of machines and robots60,61. Avian species use different mechanisms to respond to winds aloft and land62,63, as shown in Fig. 1b. These processes involve complex sensing and neural computations that enable adaptive flight adjustments to meet multiple objectives64. Experience also plays a critical role, with young birds gradually learning and perfecting wind-optimized flight techniques64. For the landing of a drone, the perching maneuvers of avian species provide innovative perspectives for trajectory planning on objectives, sensory capabilities, and skill learning. Fig. 1c demonstrates the similar mechanisms of the perching of a bird and the autonomous landing of a drone in objectives, sensory capabilities, and skill learning.

Multiple objectives: To perform a safe and performance-balanced landing, the trajectory planning of a drone has to address objectives in addition to the fundamental objective - achieving low velocity after being close to a landing position. Avian species can exploit wind conditions for energy-saving flight maneuvers65. Birds of prey use orographic lift and updrafts along hilly terrain and ridgelines to gain altitude with minimal flapping. However, scholars have shown that the perching maneuvers of avian species do not necessarily only minimize energy consumption or time consumption64. The perching maneuvers reduce aggressive wing movement to avoid hazardous loss of control authority and perform a smooth approach by avoiding wingbeat perturbations to aid visual fixation64. Moreover, avian species learn to minimize stall distance (i.e., the distance between a stall position and a landing position) through perching maneuvers. The perching maneuvers of avian species are determined by taking into account control authority, smoothness, and stall distance66. The objectives of improving energy consumption, avoiding loss of control authority, enhancing smoothness, and minimizing stall distance are inspiring for the objectives of the trajectory planning of a drone to enhance landing performance. As shown in Fig. 2d, the objective of improving energy consumption of birds can be an objective of a drone. Although the optimal energy consumption for a drone is not necessarily the optimal time consumption, the energy consumption is positively correlated with the flight time. Thus, improving the energy consumption of a drone suggests reducing unnecessary flight time and improving time consumption by the way. To align with the objective of avoiding loss of control authority of birds achieved by “avoiding wingbeat perturbations on approach"64, the trajectory planner can guide a drone to avoid aggressive attitudes and thus leave the majority of control authority for the flight controller to enhance control authority67. Avoiding aggressive attitudes also leads to smoothness. It should be noted that although a drone doesn’t stall in the manner of a bird, the stall state of a bird achieved by minimizing the stall distance64 (i.e., a state with minimized distance to a landing position when the velocity is reduced to considerably low for perching) is similar to the state to be achieved by a drone (i.e., a state with minimized distance to a landing position and magnitude of velocity) from an optimization perspective. This observation inspires us to correspond the objective of minimizing stall distance of birds to the drone’s fundamental objective - achieving low velocity after being close to a landing position.

Sensory capabilities: Avian species can perform “fly-by-feel" and perch onto branches in different wind conditions64. To offset wind effects and eventually move in the target direction, homing seabirds may head in a direction different from that leading to the colony68. This suggests that avian species can optimize their flight trajectories by sensing and adapting to their own state and surrounding wind fields. To minimize stall distance in landing, avian species need to collect combined motion information (e.g., from static visual69 or optic flow70 cues) and aerodynamic information (e.g., from feather71 or muscle72 proprioceptors)64, through their perception systems including vision, feathers64, and inner ear65, as shown in Fig. 1c. With the combined information obtained by position sensory capability, velocity sensory capability, and wind sensory capability, “fly-by-feel" concepts73–75 may prove critical to the trajectory planning of an autonomous drone in wind conditions.

Skill learning: Avian species learn and improve perching trajectories that enable them to reach the perch in a glide64. This suggests a heuristic for achieving the trajectory planning of a drone in landing based on reinforcement learning (RL)76,77. RL is a rapidly evolving approach, successfully applied in diverse contexts, including humanoid robots78, legged robots79,80, medical robots81, drone control82–84, and investigation of biological systems85,86. RL allows the integration of sensory inputs inspired by birds into observations, while multiple objectives from birds’ perching and flight can inform the design of reward functions. The inference of RL also offers high computational efficiency, beneficial for dynamic environments87. For the real-time trajectory planning of a drone taking into account wind effects, on one hand, conventional non-learning planning approaches, such as sampling-based, searching-based, and optimization-based approaches88, are less suitable, since it is hard to achieve a computation-efficient wind effects representation model applicable to these planning approaches. On the other hand, common learning approaches such as supervised learning (including imitation learning) and unsupervised learning cannot directly address multiple trajectory planning objectives. Compared to the alternatives, RL provides an effective and more suitable skill acquisition method for achieving a trajectory planner to address multiple objectives and sensory capabilities, including wind sensory capability.

This study focuses on trajectory planning, the preceding step to flight control, for drone landing. We assume a trajectory planner on top of an effective flight controller, considering that different sophisticated flight control approaches have been developed for drones already. A trajectory planner with multiple objectives and wind sensory capability is proposed (Fig. 2b) to address the local trajectory planning issue (Fig. 2a) for drone landing. The trajectory planner is achieved through a skill learning method - RL, inspired by the observation that avian species can learn and improve flight and perching skills. It should be emphasized that this study doesn’t directly mimic avian species’ designs according to the idea of biomimicry. The trajectory planner is just inspired by the avian perching processes64 and the objectives, sensory capabilities, and acquisition method of the trajectory planner are inspired by the corresponding functions or mechanisms of avian species mentioned in references such as64–66. The implementation of multiple objectives, wind sensory capability, and skill learning is presented in the Methods Section. The trajectory planner assumes a uniform flow field in small time scales and spatial scales, according to the observation that a bird can sense the nearby wind field with biological sensors, such as feathers. It should be noted that the uniform flow field aims to represent a local flow field in a short period of time and the uniform flow field cannot represent a large-scale atmospheric boundary layer. The trajectory planner also assumes the wind-effect force acting on the translational dynamics of a drone according to89.

The trajectory planner determines the next few waypoints of the reference position and velocity (i.e., a short target trajectory) for a drone by iteratively implementing an RL agent to determine the reference acceleration. This is inspired by the observation that a flight trajectory of a short period in the future can lead to a better flight performance of a drone than a single waypoint in the future39. The next waypoints are sent to a low-level flight controller. The flight controller controls the drone to track the waypoints and provides robustness to disturbances. The trajectory planner is supposed to replan a flight trajectory dynamically like a bird and then navigate a drone from an initial position around a landing site (e.g., the final approach fix) and eventually to a landing position with a quasi-static state. The trajectory planner aims to (1) achieve a balance of control authority, smoothness, time consumption, and energy consumption for the landing of a drone, except for achieving low velocity after being close to a landing position, and (2) prevent drones from crowding on top of a landing site and thus improve the operational efficiency of a landing site. The former goal is achieved by the multiple objectives of the trajectory planner. The latter goal is achieved by a planned trajectory that guides every drone from an initial position around a landing site to swoop to the landing position, avoiding every drone moving to the same region above a landing site. Although the trajectory planner in this study focuses on multiple objectives and doesn’t involve the obstacle avoidance objective, one can add the obstacle avoidance to the trajectory planner if necessary.

This study formalizes the steps to achieve a proposed trajectory planner and perform a drone landing with wind sensory capability and multiple objectives. We have investigated the training, performance, and generalization capability of the trajectory planner and the importance of wind sensory capability and velocity sensory capability to the trajectory planner. The Supplementary Video 2 summarizes the methods and results of this study.

This study draws inspiration from avian species in flight and perching and develops a unified real-time trajectory planner for drones, according to multiple objectives, wind sensory capability, and skill learning. With the trajectory planner, the importance of a flight trajectory to the landing performance of a drone and the importance of wind sensory capability and velocity sensory capability to the trajectory planner are disclosed. Simulation and experiments are conducted to demonstrate the implementation, performance, and generalization capability of the trajectory planner and the importance of wind sensory capability and velocity sensory capability to the trajectory planner. This study contributes in the following ways. (1) This study develops a trajectory planner that can account for wind effects of nearby local wind fields based on onboard sensors and balance the landing performance in control authority, smoothness, time consumption, and energy consumption, except for achieving low velocity after being close to a landing position. (2) This study highlights the critical role of flight trajectories in drone landings under windy conditions and demonstrates the advantages of the proposed trajectory planner over conventional and time-optimal planners. (3) This study discloses the importance of velocity sensory capability while the relative unimportance of wind sensory capability to the proposed trajectory planner and provides a guideline for the selection of sensors for drones. (4) This study proposes a simulation framework that can simulate the wind-effect force according to aerodynamic data on a real drone for learning in wind conditions.

It should be noted that although this study adopts the neural wind sensor proposed in21 to enable a drone to sense wind, the contributions mentioned above are different from the contributions of21. The contributions of21 include the development of the neural wind sensor and a wind-informed flight control method for a drone. The contributions mentioned above lie in the development of a performance-balanced trajectory planning method for a drone in landing and the disclosure of the importance of different sensory capabilities to trajectory planning. As shown in Fig. 2a, the flight control and trajectory planning of a drone are two problems at different levels.

Results

This study proposes a trajectory planner that can be combined with a sophisticated flight controller and balance the landing performance of a drone in control authority, smoothness, time consumption, and energy consumption, except for achieving low velocity after being close to a landing position. With the trajectory planner, this study investigates the importance of a flight trajectory to the landing performance of a drone and the importance of wind sensory capability and velocity sensory capability to the trajectory planner. With quantitative performance metrics proposed for the evaluation of landing performance, we investigate the trajectory planner from four perspectives - (1) the training of a proposed trajectory planner; (2) the performance of the proposed trajectory planner compared to baselines - a conventional trajectory planner and a time-optimal trajectory planner; (3) the generalization capability of the proposed trajectory planner; and (4) the importance of wind sensory capability and velocity sensory capability to the proposed trajectory planner. The results of this study are achieved based on a self-made standard drone, a large-scale wind generator, a motion capture system, and a simulation environment that can simulate the wind-effect force on a drone according to aerodynamic data on the real drone. The wind speed of experiments is up to 5 m s−1, since it is shown that 5 m s−1 is close to the performance limit of the drone and is appropriate to distinguish the performance of different trajectory planners.

Trajectory planner learning

This study demonstrates and evaluates the proposed trajectory planner by implementing the planner on a self-made drone. This study trains a neural wind sensor to acquire wind sensory capability for the trajectory planner and then trains a trajectory planner through RL, according to the steps presented in Fig. 3a. First, a dataset of 16,511 samples was collected for training the neural wind sensor, with 2,628 samples reserved for validation -comparable to prior studies21. The training was conducted according to ref.21 based on aerodynamic data from the self-made drone under windy conditions. The validation results, shown in Fig. 3d, demonstrate that the well-trained neural wind sensor accurately estimates the wind-effect force on the drone following an adaptation process as outlined in ref.21. Details of the implementation of the neural wind sensor are presented in Section. It should be noted that the neural wind sensor is not only used to estimate the wind-effect force for the real drone but also to reflect the wind-effect force on the drone for the simulation environment. Then, with the wind-effect force represented by the neural wind sensor, a simulation environment is established for the training and evaluation of a proposed trajectory planner based on MuJoCo, a widely used physics simulator, as shown in Fig. 3e. Next, the proximal policy optimization (PPO) algorithm is used for the RL agent and a proposed trajectory planner is established (Fig. 3f). Finally, the proposed trajectory planner is trained through two stages and then evaluated based on the simulation environment, as shown in Fig. 3g, h, respectively. The results show that the training of the proposed trajectory planner converges in the second stage eventually. The well-trained trajectory planner can successfully guide a drone to land in 5 m s−1 wind.

Quantitative performance metrics for landing performance evaluation

This study proposes a trajectory planner to improve the performance of a drone in control authority, smoothness, time consumption, and energy consumption, except for achieving low velocity after being close to a landing position. To quantitatively evaluate the performance of trajectory planners in safety, energy consumption, time consumption, smoothness, and control authority, a set of performance metrics is designed, inspired by90. Note that the performance metrics are used for the evaluation of trajectory planners and are not the same as the reward terms designed for the training of a proposed trajectory planner, in terms of formulations and weights. This is because the reward terms are designed to make the trajectory planner more trainable while achieving the objectives, but performance metrics aim to comprehensively reflect the performance of the trajectory planner. The performance metrics for safety, energy consumption, time consumption, smoothness, and control authority are illustrated as follows.

Safety

For safety, a performance metric is defined based on the trajectory tracking error of a drone. A large trajectory tracking error suggests that a drone deviates from a safe trajectory to a greater extent, exposing it to greater risks.

Energy consumption

For energy consumption, a performance metric is defined based on the accumulation of thrust. The accumulation of thrust can be calculated by summing up the thrust command determined by the flight controller of a drone. We use the accumulation of thrust to represent the energy consumption because the energy consumption of a rotor is approximately linearly related to the 1.5 power of thrust91 and it is difficult to directly accurately calculate the power consumption of a drone in high-frequency variations in simulation or for a real drone based on the common sensors. A smaller value of the accumulation of thrust indicates a lower energy consumption in performing a landing.

Time consumption

For time consumption, a performance metric is defined based on the time consumption of a drone in performing a landing.

Smoothness

For smoothness, performance metrics are defined based on the average acceleration and maximum acceleration of a drone. Smaller values of average acceleration and maximum acceleration suggest smoother changes in velocity and a smoother landing. Also, a smaller value of maximum acceleration prevents a drone from performing aggressive attitudes and leaves the majority of control authority of a drone to flight control67.

Control authority

For control authority, a performance metric is defined based on the maximum attitude deviation of a drone from the hovering attitude. A smaller value of maximum attitude deviation suggests the majority of control authority of a drone is left for flight control67. The drone tends to achieve a better performance in flight control with greater control authority.

Comparison of the performance of different trajectory planners

To evaluate the proposed trajectory planner, we compare the performance of the trajectory planner to baselines (i.e., a time-optimal trajectory planner and a conventional trajectory planner) in navigating a drone to land on a vertiport with a high of 1.5 m (Fig. 4a). The details of the time-optimal trajectory planner and a conventional trajectory planner are introduced in Section 3. We randomly select an initial position within every grid, nine initial positions in total (Fig. 4c), for the real drone to perform a landing and investigate the performance of trajectory planners. For each initial position, the drone controlled by an MPC-based flight controller makes three attempts according to the three different trajectory planners, respectively, as shown in Fig. 4b. Given a landing position, the proposed trajectory planner provides dynamic short target trajectories for the drone from the initial position and guides the drone to the landing position, as shown in Fig. 4d. Landing tests are conducted in the wind condition of 5 m s−1. A total of 27 landing tests have been performed based on the real drone (Supplementary Video 3 and Supplementary Fig. 5). The trajectories of the drone following the three trajectory planners are shown in Fig. 4e. The proposed trajectory planner and conventional trajectory planner can navigate the drone to land in all test scenarios. With the same flight controller, the time-optimal trajectory planner leads to failures in two test scenarios. Performance metrics of the three trajectory planners are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1. The proposed trajectory planner shows a balanced performance in safety, energy consumption, time consumption, smoothness, and control authority. The time-optimal trajectory planner shows a relatively poor performance in smoothness and control authority. The conventional trajectory planner shows a relatively poor performance in energy consumption and time consumption.

Generalization capability of the proposed trajectory planner

The same type of drones may be equipped with different flight controllers and carry different payloads, leading to different trajectory tracking performances. To explore the generalization capability of the proposed trajectory planner to different trajectory tracking performances, we have combined the proposed trajectory planner with not only the default MPC-based flight controller but also a PID-based flight controller, respectively (Fig. 5b). We conduct landing tests in downwind (Fig. 5a) from the same randomly selected initial position, utilizing the proposed trajectory planner with these two flight controllers, respectively (Supplementary Video 4 and Supplementary Fig. 6). The wind speed of the landing tests has been set to 5 m s−1. The trajectories of the drone following the proposed trajectory planner combined with different flight controllers are presented in Fig. 5c. The normalized performance metrics of the proposed trajectory planner combined with different flight controllers are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2. The results of landing tests show that with different trajectory tracking performances, the proposed trajectory planner can provide similar trajectories and guide the drone to achieve similar performances. The results verify the generalization capability of the proposed trajectory planner to the same type of drones with different trajectory tracking performances.

However, a trajectory planner obtained for a model of drones that have certain dynamic and aerodynamic characteristics may not be applicable to a different model of drones that have different dynamic and aerodynamic characteristics, since the trajectory planner has adapted to the former type of drone by learning.

Importance of wind sensory capability and velocity sensory capability to the proposed trajectory planner

To discover the importance of wind sensory capability and velocity sensory capability to the proposed trajectory planner, we conduct ablation experiments using three variants - the proposed trajectory planner, the proposed trajectory planner without wind sensory capability, and the proposed trajectory planner without velocity sensory capability (Fig. 6c). Landing tests are first conducted in simulation, as shown in Fig. 6b. 50 initial positions (Fig. 6a) are randomly selected for 50 landing tests conducted in wind conditions of 4 m s−1 and 5 m s−1, respectively. Namely, 100 landing tests in total have been conducted. The performance metrics of the drone in the 100 landing tests are presented in Supplementary Fig. 3a. To better investigate the performance of the trajectory planners, we also calculate the rewards achieved by these three trajectory planners (Supplementary Fig. 3b), according to the reward function used for the training of the proposed trajectory planner. It is shown that the proposed trajectory planner without velocity sensory capability fails to guide the drone to land in 94 tests and shows poor performance metrics and low achieved rewards. The performance difference between the proposed trajectory planner and the version without wind sensory capability is minimal. The results suggest that velocity sensory capability is important while wind sensory capability is relatively unimportant to the proposed trajectory planner.

Discussion

The performance of trajectory planners and the importance of a flight trajectory

This study has shown that the proposed trajectory planner can lead to a balanced landing performance for a drone (Supplementary Fig. 1) and has the advantage of avoiding drones clustering above a landing site (Fig. 4e). According to the performance metrics of acceleration and attitude deviation, the proposed trajectory planner has learned to avoid aggressive maneuvers and thus provides the majority of control authority for a flight controller to address disturbances (e.g., turbulence), leading to a decent performance in smoothness and control authority. The proposed trajectory planner also has learned to save energy and, by the way, save time, leading to performance in time consumption and energy consumption comparable to those of the time-optimal trajectory planner. By dynamically replanning short target trajectories, the planner minimizes the accumulation of trajectory tracking errors, resulting in consistently low tracking errors.

(1) Compared to the conventional trajectory planner, the trajectory is more energy-efficient and time-efficient (Supplementary Fig. 1). It should be noted that according to the performance metrics of acceleration and attitude deviation, the proposed trajectory planner achieves a performance slightly worse but comparable to the conventional trajectory planner in smoothness and control authority. (2) Unlike the time-optimal trajectory planner, the proposed trajectory planner avoids landing failures (Fig. 4e). The proposed trajectory planner considerably outperforms the time-optimal trajectory planner in safety, smoothness, and control authority while achieving comparable time and energy efficiency, as shown by metrics of tracking error, acceleration, attitude deviation, time consumption, and energy consumption (Supplementary Fig. 1). (3) Unlike state-of-the-art trajectory planners that rely on predefined reference trajectories or prioritize a single objective with precise constraints, the proposed trajectory planner excels in managing multiple sensory inputs and balancing diverse objectives. (4) The different performances achieved by different trajectory planners show that a flight trajectory is crucial for the landing performance of a drone in the wind.

Importance of wind sensory capability and velocity sensory capability

The performance metrics presented in Supplementary Fig. 3 show that (1) the performance of the proposed trajectory planner without wind sensory capability is comparable to the performance of the proposed trajectory planner, suggesting that wind sensory capability is not critical for the local trajectory planning of a drone with objectives that do not include a wind-related term. The fact that a wind-related term is not included in the objectives of the proposed trajectory planner could be a reason for this result. This result suggests that to utilize wind information to enhance the performance of drones, one should focus on global path planning and flight control, rather than local trajectory planning. It should be emphasized that this result doesn’t conflict with the importance of wind sensory capability to global path planning51,52 and flight control21 of drones. The performance metrics shown in Supplementary Fig. 3a also show that (2) the proposed trajectory planner without velocity sensory capability fails to guide the drone to land and the performance metrics of the proposed trajectory planner without velocity sensory capability are much worse than those of the proposed trajectory planner. It is suggested that velocity sensory capability is crucial for the local trajectory planning of a drone for the multiple objectives discussed in this study. The fact that a velocity-related term (i.e., the penalty defined based on the acceleration of a drone, denoted as a(k) for smoothness and control authority objectives) is included in the reward function could be a reason for this result.

Benefits for drones and landing sites

This study benefits drones and landing site design in several aspects. (1) Verifies the importance of a flight trajectory to the landing performance of a drone. Provides a trajectory planner to determine a flight trajectory with a balanced landing performance. (2) Enables real-time trajectory planning for drones by leveraging wind sensory capability, laying the foundation for “fly-by-feel" drone operations. (3) Discloses the unimportance of wind to the local trajectory planning of a drone with objectives that do not include a wind-related objective (e.g., wind offset compensation). Provides prioritization of sensor selection for drones. (4) Avoids drones clustering above a landing site and the conflict between the approach path and departure path of a landing site. Provides an effective way to optimize the traffic pattern and glide path of a landing site.

Conclusions

In this paper, we have proposed a trajectory planner for drone landing achieved by RL and based on wind sensory capability and multiple objectives. We have shown that the proposed trajectory planner has the advantage of avoiding drones clustering over a landing site and balancing the landing performance of a drone in safety, time consumption, energy consumption, smoothness, and control authority. The proposed trajectory planner also shows a decent generalization capability to different trajectory tracking performances. Through investigating the proposed trajectory planner, the importance of a flight trajectory to landing performance and the importance of wind sensory capability and velocity sensory capability to the proposed trajectory planner have been discovered. We disclosed that velocity sensory capability is important while wind sensory capability is relatively unimportant to the proposed trajectory planner. We hope this study will contribute to the improvement of the landing performance of drones, the optimization of the approach paths of landing sites, and explanations of the trajectories and behaviors of avian species.

This study is not without limitations. The trajectory planner assumes a uniform flow field in small time scales and spatial scales within a local environment of the atmospheric boundary layer. Thus, the conclusions related to the flow field are representative for the laminar atmospheric situation only and not for the turbulent situation.

As for future work, further investigation of additional objectives and the weight of objectives to improve the performance of drones in landing would be an interesting direction. In the future, applying the proposed trajectory planner to find approach paths for a landing site could be a valuable application. The framework of multiple objectives, wind sensory capability, and skill learning can be used to explore and explain the trajectory planning and behaviors of flying creatures and aerial robots.

Implementation of multiple objectives, wind sensory capability, and skill learning

The proposed trajectory planner is developed according to multiple objectives, wind sensory capability, and skill learning. Inspired by the objectives of the perching maneuvers of avian species, we propose multiple objectives for the proposed trajectory planner to achieve a balance of control authority, smoothness, energy consumption, and time consumption, except for achieving low velocity after being close to a landing position. Objectives are represented by the quantitative reward terms of RL, including terminal state, acceleration, and thrust terms, to address achieving low velocity after being close to a landing position, control authority and smoothness, energy consumption and time consumption, respectively, as shown in Fig. 2d. The terminal state term is designed inspired by the observation that avian species minimize the stall distance in perching64. The acceleration term is designed inspired by the observation that avian species reduce aggressive wing movement to avoid hazardous loss of control authority and avoid wingbeat perturbations to perform a smooth approach to aid visual fixation64. The thrust term is designed to reduce energy consumption and, by the way, time consumption, inspired by the observation that avian species can save energy through maneuvers, such as static soaring to exploit updrafts and dynamic soaring to exploit gradients and gusts65.

Inspired by the sensory capabilities of avian species, the proposed trajectory planner is designed to be able to sense the position of a landing position with respect to the drone through position sensory capability, the velocity of the drone through velocity sensory capability, and the wind-effect force through wind sensory capability, as shown in Fig. 2b. Thus, the inputs of the proposed trajectory planner involve the wind-effect force (represented by wind-effect acceleration), the velocity of the drone, and the position of a landing position with respect to the drone. To sense the wind-effect force in real time, this study adopts a neural wind sensor that can estimate the wind-effect force for a real drone according to21, as shown in Fig. 2c.

Inspired by the observation that avian species can improve their flight skills with the accumulation of experience, the proposed trajectory planner is designed to be achieved based on accumulated data through RL, as shown in Fig. 2e. To this end, this study proposes a simulation framework that can simulate the wind-effect force according to the data on a real drone for studies of the trajectory planning of a drone in wind conditions and uses the simulation framework to train a proposed trajectory planner.

Drone platform

This study uses a self-made drone with an X configuration, 25 cm in width and 23 cm in length. The drone has a mass of 0.96 kg and uses four standard motors with a KV rating of 2250. The drone is equipped with a Pixhawk flight control device with PX4, a widely used open-source autopilot platform, and a Jetson Orin Nano onboard computer that can interact with the flight control device via the robot operating system (ROS). The onboard computer is used to perform wind estimation, trajectory planning, and the position-velocity control loop of the MPC-based flight controller at a frequency of 50 Hz. The Pixhawk flight control device is used to perform the position-velocity control loop of the PID-based flight controller at a frequency of 50 Hz based on the position-velocity control loop of PX4. The Pixhawk flight control device also receives thrust and attitude commands from a position-velocity control loop and performs the PID-based attitude-angular rate control loop of PX4 at a frequency of 400 Hz. We employ the extended Kalman filter of PX4 for the state estimation of the drone. The extended Kalman filter fuses the acceleration, angular velocity, and attitude of the drone measured by the onboard inertial measurement unit with the position of the drone measured by a motion capture system. The hardware setup of the drone is standard and similar to general drone platforms21.

Experimental platform

To obtain the aerodynamic data on a drone for a neural wind sensor and investigate the performance of the proposed trajectory planner in wind conditions, a large-scale wind generator and a motion capture system are used in this study. The wind generator has a size of 4 m x 4 m and consists of 1296 individually controllable fans. The wind generator can generate wind at a speed of up to 12 m s−1. The motion capture system consists of 16 infrared cameras (Nokov Mars2H) and can measure the pose of a drone at a frequency of 50 Hz.

Neural wind sensor for the real drone and simulation

The effectiveness of a neural wind sensor in real-time prediction of the wind-effect force on a drone has been verified in ref.21. Thus, we achieve wind sensory capability for the proposed trajectory planner based on a neural wind sensor developed according to21. The neural wind sensor is a key contribution of21 and the foundational information of the neural wind sensor is demonstrated in its main text, supplementary material, and codes. Details of the implementation of the neural wind sensor are introduced in this section. It should be noted that the neural wind sensor is used for not only the wind-effect force estimation of the real drone according to21 but also the wind-effect force simulation of the simulation environment.

Architecture

A neural wind sensor uses a neural network for the wind-invariant representation, denoted as ϕINV, and an adaptive parameter αAD=[αADx,αADy,αADz]T to represent wind-specific coefficients. The wind-invariant representation refers to a common representation of a drone shared in different wind conditions. Scholars have shown that the three components of the wind-effect force are highly correlated and share common features, so that we can use ϕINV as the basis function for all the components21. The wind-effect acceleration of the drone, represented by aw, can be calculated based on multiplying the wind-invariant representation with wind-specific coefficients.

The wind-effect force can be calculated based on the wind-effect acceleration and the mass of the drone.

Wind-invariant representation

For the neural network ϕINV utilized for the wind-invariant representation, the inputs of the neural network include the velocity of the drone (three elements), the attitude of the drone represented by a quaternion (four elements), and the thrust commands of the drone represented by pulse width modulation values (four elements). The inputs of the neural network ϕINV are an 11-dimensional vector. The neural network ϕINV outputs the wind-invariant representation, represented by a four-dimensional vector. The neural network ϕINV includes four fully-connected hidden layers and has an architecture of 11 → 50 → 60 → 50 → 4 with the rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation. The batch size of the training of the neural network ϕINV is 256. A well-trained neural network ϕINV is used to represent the wind-invariant representation for both the wind-effect force estimation of the real drone and the wind-effect force simulation of the simulation environment.

Wind-specific coefficients

For the adaptive parameter αAD reflecting wind-specific coefficients, we utilize the least squares fitting method to identify the adaptive parameter that can minimize the wind-effect acceleration prediction error. In the training of a neural network ϕINV, the adaptive parameter αAD is updated depending on a batch of 128 data samples disjoint with the batch of data samples used for the training of the neural network ϕINV. Except for updating the adaptive parameter αAD for the training of the neural network ϕINV, the adaptive parameter αAD is updated to represent wind-specific coefficients under corresponding wind conditions for both the real drone and the simulation environment. For the simulation environment, the adaptive parameter αAD corresponding to a certain wind condition is calculated based on all training data of the corresponding wind condition and a well-trained neural network ϕINV. For the real drone, the adaptive parameter αAD is updated depending on the recent prediction error of wind-effect acceleration. A composite adaptation rate based on rapid estimation using Kalman filtering is designed according to21. We remove the position-velocity tracking error term in updating αAD.

Data collection and processing

To learn a neural wind sensor, we collect data samples on the position, velocity, attitude, and thrust (represented by pulse width modulation values) of the self-made drone. The velocity, attitude, and thrust of the drone are the inputs of the neural wind sensor. The acceleration of the drone is calculated based on the position and velocity of the drone through a 5th-order smooth curve. The wind-effect acceleration of the drone, which is the ground truth of the output of the neural wind sensor, is calculated based on the acceleration of the drone, thrust-effect acceleration, and gravity of the drone.

Simulation framework

In this study, we propose a simulation framework that can simulate the wind-effect force on a drone according to aerodynamic data on the real drone. The simulation framework combines MuJoCo, a widely used physics simulator, and the neural wind sensor obtained based on data samples on the interaction between the real drone and wind to establish a simulation environment. The simulation environment includes the dynamic model of a drone established according to the real drone. The simulation environment has a simulation frequency of 1 KHz. To simulate the interaction between the real drone and wind, we propose to apply the wind-effect force calculated based on the multiplication of wind-specific coefficients αAD and the wind-invariant representation ϕINV to the drone. The wind-invariant representation used for the simulation environment is the same as the well-trained wind-invariant representation used for the real drone. Wind-specific coefficients αAD corresponding to a certain wind condition are calculated based on all training data of the corresponding wind condition and a well-trained neural network ϕINV. In this study, wind-specific coefficients αAD corresponding to wind speeds of 2 m s−1, 3 m s−1, 4 m s−1, and 5 m s−1 have been achieved and used for the simulation of the interaction between the real drone and wind.

Flight controllers

This study utilizes the widely used flight control framework that includes a cascaded position-velocity control loop and an attitude-angular rate control loop19 to establish an MPC-based flight controller and a PID-based flight controller. The MPC-based flight controller is the default flight controller used in the training of a proposed trajectory planner and most experiments, except for the evaluation of the generalization capability of the proposed trajectory planner based on the real drone with a PID-based flight controller. For the position-velocity control loop, the MPC-based flight controller utilizes nonlinear MPC92. The PID-based flight controller uses the position-velocity control loop of PX4. The position-velocity control loop runs at a frequency of 50 Hz to determine target attitude and thrust. The attitude-angular rate control loop is established based on PID both for the MPC-based flight controller and the PID-based flight controller. The attitude-angular rate control loop, operating at a frequency of 400 Hz, executes target attitude and thrust commands to regulate the rotors of the drone.

Training process

To train a proposed trajectory planner, repetitive training episodes are conducted. The training of a proposed trajectory planner is based on the parameters listed in Supplementary Table 4. For an episode, a drone is initialized at a random location in the environment to track a random landing position in wind conditions at a random speed of 0 m s−1, 2 m s−1, 3 m s−1, 4 m s−1, and 5 m s−1. If the drone reaches the landing position or flies outside the boundary of the defined region, the episode terminates. If the episode reaches the maximum step, the episode terminates also. The landing position is considered to be reached by the drone if the distance between the drone and the landing position is less than 0.15 m and the magnitude of the velocity of the drone is less than 0.1 m s−1.

Baseline design

Two baseline trajectory planners are used for the evaluation of the proposed trajectory planner, including a conventional trajectory planner and a time-optimal trajectory planner.

Conventional trajectory planner

The first baseline trajectory planner is designed according to the conventional trajectory planner widely used in practice. According to the conventional trajectory planner, a drone should fly to a region above a landing site and then descend vertically to the landing site. In this study, the drone is required to fly to a region above a landing site at a speed of 0.3 m s−1 and then descend vertically at a speed of 0.3 m s−1, according to the flight performance of the real drone.
