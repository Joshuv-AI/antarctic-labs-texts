Bayesian copula-based spatial random effects models for inference with complex spatial data
Alan R. Pearse, David Gunawan, Noel Cressie

Abstract
In this article, we develop fully Bayesian, copula-based, spatial-statistical models for large, noisy, incomplete, and non-Gaussian spatial data. Our approach includes novel constructions of copulas that accommodate a spatial-random-effects structure, enabling low-rank representations and computationally efficient Bayesian inference. The spatial copula is used in a latent process model of the Bayesian hierarchical spatial-statistical model, and, conditional on the latent copula-based spatial process, the data model handles measurement errors and missing data. Our simulation studies show that a fully Bayesian approach delivers accurate and fast inference for both parameter estimation and spatial-process prediction, outperforming several benchmark methods, including fixed rank kriging (FRK). The new class of copula-based models is used to map atmospheric methane in the Bowen Basin, Queensland, Australia, from Sentinel 5P satellite data.



Keywords:
Basic Areal Unit; Markov chain Monte Carlo; measurement error; fixed rank kriging; Sentinel 5P satellite; t spatial process

1
Introduction

Non-Gaussian spatial processes where the marginal distributions are non-Gaussian (e.g., skewed, strictly positive, or multimodal), are commonly used in environmental-science models. Even after transformation of the marginals, the spatial-dependence structure of the process may be unlike that of a Gaussian process. Trans-Gaussian models
(
Cressie, 1993
, e.g., )
and Spatial Generalized Linear Mixed Models
(
Diggle et al., 1998
, SGLMMs;)
use marginal transformations of a Gaussian spatial process; however, the spatial dependence in these models is fundamentally Gaussian-like, with spatial correlation that tends to zero as the values become more extreme.

Spatial copulas enable modeling with non-Gaussian marginals and non-Gaussian spatial dependence that can, for example, capture substantial correlation among extreme values of the process.
Bardossy, 2006
first used copulas to model a non-Gaussian random field in hydrology, developing the chi-squared copula for spatial processes exhibiting asymmetric dependence between small and large values. Subsequently, many copulas that generate non-Gaussian spatial processes have been developed for environmental applications, including the Fisher copula
(
Favre et al., 2018
)
, the conditional-normal extreme-value copulas
(
Krupskii and Genton, 2021
)
, and a family of “Clayton-like” spatial copulas
(
Bevilacqua et al., 2024
)
. The Gaussian copula
(
Song, 2000
, e.g.,)
and the t copula
(
Demarta and McNeil, 2005
, e.g.,)
can also be used for spatial-statistical inference
(
Kazianka and Pilz, 2011
;
Erhardt et al., 2015
, e.g.,)
. In fact, the aforementioned trans-Gaussian models are special cases of Gaussian-copula models
(
Kazianka and Pilz, 2010
)
, and we expand on this result for ‘transformed t’ spatial processes in Section
2.5
.

In this article, we not only develop new dimension-reduced spatial copulas, but we also show how they should be used in a Bayesian hierarchical statistical model. That is, we show how to capture the large-, small-, and micro-scale variability of the spatial process and separate them from measurement errors in noisy and incomplete spatial data. Not distinguishing between the data and the process has led in the past to the unrealistic assumption of zero measurement error or to assuming an overly smooth process with no micro-scale variation
(
Cressie and Wikle, 2011
, pp. 120-123)
. Previous attempts at hierarchical spatial-copula models include
Ghosh and Mallick, 2011
,
Beck et al., 2020
, and
Garcia et al., 2021
. In these models, a copula is inserted at the data-model level of the hierarchy, so measurement errors are still confounded with the variability of the scientific process. On the other hand,
Ghosh, 2009
,
Prates et al., 2015
and
Musgrove et al., 2016
defined copula-based SGLMMs for count and binary data, featuring a data model that deals with the discreteness of the observed data. However, the general notion of measurement errors and missingness is absent from these works.

The past literature on spatial-copula modeling has not adequately considered inference that scales to very large datasets. In today’s data-rich world of large remote-sensing and sensor-network datasets, spatial-copula models that can efficiently handle tens of thousands of observations or more are needed. Fixed-rank kriging (FRK) and, more broadly, spatial random effects (SRE) models
(
Cressie and Johannesson, 2008
;
Zammit-Mangion and Cressie, 2021
;
Sainsbury-Dale et al., 2024
)
use basis functions for dimension reduction, which we generalize here to the spatial-copula model.

In this article, our contributions are as follows. We develop novel Gaussian copulas and t copulas with embedded SREs that enable fast Bayesian computation for large spatial datasets, based on Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC). The Gaussian-copula SRE models generalize existing trans-Gaussian SRE models and SGLMMs
(
Sengupta and Cressie, 2013
;
Sainsbury-Dale et al., 2024
)
. The t-copula models with embedded SREs result in non-Gaussian spatial processes generated from a multivariate t distribution. Importantly, these models are developed in a Bayesian hierarchical spatial-statistical-modeling framework
(
Cressie and Wikle, 2011
, e.g.,)
that enables inference on a latent scientific process from large, noisy, incomplete spatial data. Simulation studies and an application to a satellite-remote-sensing dataset of atmospheric methane concentrations demonstrate the inferential and computational advantages of our hierarchical spatial-copula modeling of noisy, incomplete, non-Gaussian spatial data.

The rest of this article is organized as follows. Section
2
defines the general copula-based Bayesian hierarchical spatial-statistical model, which includes our copula-based SRE models. Section
3
discusses computationally efficient Bayesian inference, and Section
4
presents simulation studies that demonstrate the validity of our Bayesian inferences. In Section
5
, we present an application to predicting atmospheric methane concentrations from satellite data taken in and around the Bowen Basin, Queensland, Australia, which is a region of intense coal-mining activity. Conclusions and a discussion are given in Section
6
. This article has a Supplement containing proofs for all propositions as well as additional technical details and empirical results.

2
Copula-based spatial random effects model

In this section, we define a general Bayesian hierarchical spatial-statistical model (BHM; Section
2.1
), and then we particularize it to incorporate spatial copulas (Section
2.2
). Sections
2.3
–
2.5
present Gaussian copulas and t copulas that accommodate a spatial random effects (SRE) structure for efficient computations.

2.1
Bayesian hierarchical spatial-statistical models

Let
Y(\cdot)\equiv\{Y(\mathbf{s}):\mathbf{s}\in D\}
be a spatial stochastic process defined over points
\mathbf{s}
in spatial domain
D
, usually a
d
-dimensional subset of Euclidean space. Define a set of polygons
\mathcal{A}\equiv\{A_{j}:j=1,...,N\}
with areas
|A_{j}|>0
,
j=1,...,N
, that tile
D
. The polygons in
\mathcal{A}
are called Basic Areal Units
(
Nguyen et al., 2012
, BAUs;)
, and they are constructed to represent the smallest areal regions over which spatial-statistical inference is meaningful. The BAUs need not be the same size and shape, although they often are
(
Zammit-Mangion and Cressie, 2021
)
. For each
j=1,...,N
, define aggregation over
A_{j}
as
Y(A_{j})\equiv|A_{j}|^{-1}\int_{\mathbf{u}\in A_{j}}Y(\mathbf{u})~\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}
, which is the areal average of
\{Y(\mathbf{u}):\mathbf{u}\in A_{j}\}
, for
j=1,...,N
. The random vector
\mathbf{Y}\equiv(Y(A_{1}),...,Y(A_{N}))^{\top}
is called the
BAU-level process
and fully describes the (discretized) spatial process over
D

(
Zammit-Mangion and Cressie, 2021
)
. Explicit process models for the marginal behavior and dependence structure of the spatial process are provided by the BAU-level process. The advantage of this approach is that it simplifies
change-of-support
problems
(e.g.,
Cressie, 1993
, Sec 5.2)
, where predictions are made at geographic ‘blocks’ made up of several BAUs
(
Sainsbury-Dale et al., 2024
, e.g., see the examples in)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\equiv\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{O}}\cup\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{M}}
, where
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{O}}\equiv\{A_{\mathrm{O}k}:k=1,...,K\}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{M}}\equiv\{A_{\mathrm{M}l}:l=1,...,L\}
are two mutually exclusive sets of BAUs (i.e.,
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{O}}\cap\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{M}}=\emptyset
) with
K\leq N
and
L=N-K
. We call
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{O}}
the set of ‘observed BAUs’ (with subscript ‘O’) and
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{M}}
the set of ‘missing BAUs’ (with subscript ‘M’). The corresponding latent-process vectors are
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\equiv(Y(A_{\mathrm{O}1}),...,Y(A_{\mathrm{O}K}))^{\top}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\equiv(Y(A_{\mathrm{M}1}),...,Y(A_{\mathrm{M}L}))^{\top}
, and we can write
\mathbf{Y}\equiv(\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top},\mathbf{Y}^{\top}_{\mathrm{M}})^{\top}
. At the ‘observed BAUs’, we observe the noisy, incomplete spatial data,
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}\equiv(Z(A_{\mathrm{O}1}),...,Z(A_{\mathrm{O}K}))^{\top}
. Spatial data are observed at or aggregated to the BAU resolution. The ‘missing BAUs’ are so called, because data are considered missing there.

We now define the Bayesian hierarchical spatial-statistical model, comprising a
data model
, a
process model
, and a
parameter model
. Let
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}
be a vector of parameters for the measurement error associated with the data
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}
. The data model specifies the distribution of
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}
given
\mathbf{Y}
, written as
[\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\mathbf{Y},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}]
. With very few exceptions
(
Wikle and Berliner, 2005
, e.g.,)
, conditional independence of
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}
given
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}
is assumed, resulting in
[\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\mathbf{Y},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}]=[\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}]=\prod_{k=1}^{K}[Z(A_{\mathrm{O}k})\mid Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k}),\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}]
.

The
process model
describes the conditional distribution of the latent spatial process
\mathbf{Y}\equiv(\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top},\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}^{\top})^{\top}
, usually denoted as
[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
but equivalently as
[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
, where
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
is a vector of parameters that govern the latent spatial process. From this point,
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}
is assumed known (e.g., from independent calibration experiments of the scientific instrument), so only
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
is given a distribution. That is, the
parameter model
is
[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
, which is often referred to as the prior distributions of the parameters.

The goal of Bayesian inference is to infer the ‘unknowns’, namely the latent process
\mathbf{Y}
and the parameters
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
, given the noisy, incomplete spatial data
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}
. Inference proceeds from the joint posterior distribution,
[\mathbf{Y},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\mid\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}]
. Since
\mathbf{Y}\equiv(\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top},\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}^{\top})^{\top}
, we use the decomposition,
[\mathbf{Y},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\mid\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}]=[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\mid\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}]
, since conditional on
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}
,
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}
is independent of
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}
. This enables us to address inference on the parameters and
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}
separately from inference on
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}
. By Bayes’ rule, the joint posterior of
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}
and
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
is given by
[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\mid\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}]\propto[\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}]\times[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
. The predictive distribution,
[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}]
can be obtained by integrating out
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
. Similarly, predictions of
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}
alone are obtained via the integral
[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}]=\int\int[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\mid\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}]~\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}~\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}
, using the decomposition introduced above. Practical aspects are discussed in Section
3
.

2.2
Copula-based spatial models

An
N
-variate copula function,
\mathfrak{C}_{1:N}(u_{1},...,u_{N})
,
u_{1},...,u_{N}\in[0,1]
, is a joint cumulative distribution function (CDF) for a random vector
\mathbf{U}\equiv(U_{1},...,U_{N})^{\top}
, where marginally each
U_{j}
,
j=1,...,N
, is uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
. Let
\mathbf{Y}\equiv(Y_{1},...,Y_{N})^{\top}
be an
N
-variate random vector. The notation here is not necessarily spatial, but it can be made so by letting
Y_{j}
be
Y(A_{j})
, for
j=1,...,N
.

Now write the joint CDF of
\mathbf{Y}
as
F_{1:N}(y_{1},...,y_{N})
and the joint PDF as
f_{1:N}(y_{1},...,y_{N})
for
y_{1},...,y_{N}\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
F_{j}(y_{j})
denote the marginal CDF of
Y_{j}
, for
j=1,...,N
. We link
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{Y}
by transforming
U_{j}\equiv F_{j}(Y_{j})
or, conversely,
Y_{j}\equiv F_{j}^{-1}(U_{j})
, where
F_{j}^{-1}(\cdot)
is the quantile function, for
j=1,...,N
. For realizations
y_{j}
and
u_{j}
, the same relationship holds:
u_{j}\equiv F_{j}(y_{j})
for
u_{j}\in[0,1]
and
y_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
. By Sklar’s theorem
(
Sklar, 1959
)
, combining the marginal CDFs
\{F_{j}(y_{j}):y_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\}_{j=1}^{N}
with a copula function,
\mathfrak{C}_{1:N}(\cdot)
, results in the joint CDF of
\mathbf{Y}
. That is, for
y_{1},...,y_{N}\in\mathbb{R}
,

F_{1:N}(y_{1},...,y_{N})\equiv\mathfrak{C}_{1:N}(F_{1}(y_{1}),...,F_{N}(y_{N})).

(1)

When the marginal distributions are all continuous, (
1
) is unique. Assuming that
\mathfrak{C}_{1:N}(\cdot)
and all marginal CDFs are absolutely continuous, we obtain the joint density of
\mathbf{Y}\equiv(Y_{1},...,Y_{N})^{\top}
from (
1
). That is, for
y_{1},...,y_{N}\in\mathbb{R}
,

f_{1:N}(y_{1},...,y_{N})\equiv\left(\prod_{j=1}^{N}f_{j}(y_{j})\right)\times\mathfrak{c}_{1:N}(F_{1}(y_{1}),...,F_{N}(y_{N})),

(2)

where
f_{j}(y_{j})
is the marginal PDF of
Y_{j}
, for
j=1,...,N
; and
\mathfrak{c}_{1:N}(\cdot)
is the joint copula density,
\mathfrak{c}_{1:N}(u_{1},...,u_{N})\equiv\partial^{N}\mathfrak{C}(u_{1},...,u_{N})/\partial u_{1}...\partial u_{N}
, where
u_{1},...,u_{N}\in[0,1]
.

In a copula-based hierarchical spatial-statistical model, (
2
) defines the process model. We set
[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]=(\prod_{j=1}^{N}f_{j}(y_{j}))\times\mathfrak{c}_{1:N}(F_{1}(y_{1}),...,F_{N}(y_{N}))
, where the right-hand side implicitly depends on
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
through the marginal CDFs and PDFs,
\{F_{j}(\cdot):j=1,...,N\}
and
\{f_{j}(\cdot):j=1,...,N\}
, respectively, and through the joint copula density
\mathfrak{c}_{1:N}(\cdot)
. The vector
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
typically includes parameters such as linear-model coefficients
\bm{\beta}
that parameterize the marginal expectations (e.g.,
E(Y(A_{j})\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}})=\mathbf{x}(A_{j})^{\top}\bm{\beta}
, where
\mathbf{x}(A_{j})^{\top}
denotes a vector of covariates defined on BAU
A_{j}
), and a marginal variance parameter
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{P}}\equiv\mathrm{var}(Y(A_{j})\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}})
, for
j=1,...,N
. Next, we develop spatial copulas to insert into the process model,
[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
.

2.3
The Gaussian spatial random effects (Gau-SRE) copula

In order to construct the Gaussian SRE (Gau-SRE) copula, we first introduce the Gaussian SRE model. Let the process
Y(\cdot)
have an SRE component, which is a linear combination of the
b
-dimensional vector of basis functions
\mathbf{S}(\cdot)
, where the coefficients are random
(
Cressie and Johannesson, 2008
)
. Then, let
\mathbf{S}(A_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{b}
be a vector of
b
spatial basis functions
(
Cressie and Johannesson, 2008
)
integrated over the BAU
A_{j}
or perhaps evaluated at its centroid, for
j=1,...,N
. Let
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times b}
be a matrix where the
j
-th row is
\mathbf{S}(A_{j})^{\top}
, for
j=1,...,N
. We can also write
\mathbf{S}\equiv(\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top},\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{M}}^{\top})^{\top}
, where
\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}
and
\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{M}}
are the
K\times b
and
L\times b
matrices of spatial basis functions for the observed and missing BAUs, respectively.

Let the basis-function coefficients
\bm{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{b}
be a
b
-variate Gaussian random vector with mean vector
\mathbf{0}
and positive-definite covariance matrix
\mathbf{E}
, and let
\bm{\xi}
be an
N
-variate Gaussian random vector, independent of
\bm{\eta}
, with mean vector
\mathbf{0}
and covariance matrix
\sigma^{2}_{\xi}\mathbf{I}_{N}
, where
\mathbf{I}_{N}
is the
N
-dimensional identity matrix. For reasons explained in Section
2.4
, we fix
\sigma^{2}_{\xi}=1
. Then, define the Gaussian random vector
\mathbf{W}\equiv(W(A_{1}),...,W(A_{N}))^{\top}
as,

\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{S}\bm{\eta}+\bm{\xi}.

(3)

Now
\mathbf{W}
in (
3
) can also be represented hierarchically as follows
(
Kang and Cressie, 2011
)
:
\mathbf{W}\mid\bm{\eta}\sim\mathrm{MVG}(\mathbf{S}\bm{\eta},\mathbf{I}_{N})
and
\bm{\eta}\mid\mathbf{E}\sim\mathrm{MVG}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{E})
, where
\mathrm{MVG}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})
denotes a multivariate Gaussian distribution with mean vector
\bm{\mu}
and covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
. Integrating out
\bm{\eta}
gives the unconditional distribution of
\mathbf{W}
, which is multivariate Gaussian with mean vector
\mathbf{0}
and covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}\equiv\mathbf{S}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{S}^{\top}+\mathbf{I}_{N}
. The form of
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}
enables us to leverage the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury identities to efficiently compute its inverse and determinant
(
Henderson and Searle, 1981
)
; that is,
(\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}})^{-1}=\mathbf{I}_{N}-\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{E}^{-1})^{-1}\mathbf{S}^{\top}
and
\det(\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}})=\det(\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{E}^{-1})\det(\mathbf{E})
.

In Proposition
2.1
, we show how to obtain the Gau-SRE copula, where we use the notation
\Phi
and
\phi
to denote the CDF and PDF, respectively, of a univariate standard Gaussian random variable, and hence
\Phi^{-1}
is its quantile function.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
F_{\mathcal{G},1:N}(\cdot;\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}})
denote an
N
-variate Gaussian CDF with mean vector
\mathbf{0}
and covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}\equiv\mathbf{S}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{S}^{\top}+\mathbf{I}_{N}
. For
0\leq u_{1},...,u_{N}\leq 1
, a Gaussian copula with parameter given by an SRE covariance-matrix is,

\mathfrak{G}_{1:N}^{\mathrm{SRE}}(u_{1},...,u_{N};\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}})=F_{\mathcal{G},1:N}(\sigma_{1}^{\mathrm{SRE}}\Phi^{-1}(u_{1}),...,\sigma_{N}^{\mathrm{SRE}}\Phi^{-1}(u_{N});\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}),

(4)

where
\sigma_{j}^{\mathrm{SRE}}
is the square-root of the
j
-th diagonal element of
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}
. The associated joint copula density is,

\mathfrak{g}_{1:N}^{\mathrm{SRE}}(u_{1},...,u_{N};\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}})=\frac{f_{\mathcal{G},1:N}(\sigma_{1}^{\mathrm{SRE}}\Phi^{-1}(u_{1}),...,\sigma_{N}^{\mathrm{SRE}}\Phi^{-1}(u_{N});\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}})}{\prod_{j=1}^{N}\phi(\Phi^{-1}(u_{j}))/\sigma_{j}^{\mathrm{SRE}}}.

(5)

The proof (and all other proofs) is given in Supplement
S1
.

Copulas are invariant to monotonic transformations of the marginal variables (e.g., scaling operations). If the covariance matrix’s diagonals
\sigma_{1}^{2},...,\sigma_{N}^{2}
are free parameters only constrained to be positive, the invariance to scaling operations on the marginals makes them non-identifiable. However, the diagonal elements of
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}
, namely
(\sigma^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{1})^{2},...,(\sigma^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{N})^{2}
, are constrained by the SRE parameterization. Provided that the constraints imposed in Section
2.4
are followed, all parameters remain identifiable. The purpose of the parameterization given in (
4
) in Proposition
2.1
is that the diagonal elements of
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}
, which depend on
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{E}
, can be computed quickly and directly without computing and storing the large
N\times N
matrix prior to extracting the diagonals. Traditionally, copulas have relied on the correlation matrix for their parameterization, but our new approach using (
4
) avoids this.

The proposition below establishes the connection between the process
\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{W}
given in (
3
), through a special trans-Gaussian model.

Proposition 2.2
.

Define the (Gaussian) anamorphosis
(
Matheron, 1976
)
: Set
W(A_{j})=\sigma_{j}^{\mathrm{SRE}}\Phi^{-1}(F_{j}(Y(A_{j})))
, for
j=1,...,N
, where
\mathbf{W}\equiv(W(A_{1}),...,W(A_{N}))^{\top}
jointly follows a multivariate Gaussian distribution with mean vector
\mathbf{0}
and covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}
. Then, a transformation of variables shows that, for
y_{1},...,y_{N}\in\mathbb{R}
, the joint PDF of
\mathbf{Y}
is,

\displaystyle[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{N}f_{j}(y_{j})\right)\mathfrak{g}_{1:N}^{\mathrm{SRE}}(F_{1}(y_{1}),...,F_{N}(y_{N});\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}),

(6)

where
\mathfrak{g}_{1:N}^{\mathrm{SRE}}
is given by (
5
).

Proposition
2.2
allows us to obtain the Gau-SRE copula model for any subvector of
\mathbf{Y}
. Let
\{F_{\mathrm{O}k}:k=1,...,K\}
and
\{f_{\mathrm{O}k}:k=1,...,K\}
denote the marginal CDFs and PDFs of
\{Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k}):k=1,...,K\}
, respectively. Also let
\bm{\xi}_{\mathrm{O}}
be the
K
-variate sub-vector of
\bm{\xi}
in (
3
), with mean vector
\mathbf{0}
and covariance matrix
\mathbf{I}_{K}
. Hence,
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{O}}\equiv(W(A_{\mathrm{O}1}),...,W(A_{\mathrm{O}K}))^{\top}\equiv\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}\bm{\eta}+\bm{\xi}_{\mathrm{O}}
, where
\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}
is the corresponding
K\times b
submatrix of
\mathbf{S}
. Unconditional on
\bm{\eta}
,
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{O}}
follows a multivariate Gaussian distribution with mean vector
\mathbf{0}
and covariance matrix,
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{O}\mathrm{O}}^{\mathrm{SRE}}\equiv\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}+\mathbf{I}_{K}
. Applying Proposition
2.2
, we see that, for
y_{\mathrm{O}1},...,y_{\mathrm{O}K}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]=\left(\prod_{k=1}^{K}f_{\mathrm{O}k}(y_{\mathrm{O}k})\right)\mathfrak{g}_{1:K}^{\mathrm{SRE}}\left(F_{\mathrm{O}1}(y_{\mathrm{O}1}),...,F_{\mathrm{O}K}(y_{\mathrm{O}K});\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{\mathrm{O}\mathrm{O}}\right).

(7)

This property, that the copula representation is conserved under marginalization, is an attractive feature of our parameterization.

2.4
Parameter identifiability in
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}

The construction of (
4
) finesses the normalization of
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}
into its correlation matrix,
\mathbf{R}^{\mathrm{SRE}}
, so avoiding unnecessary and inefficient matrix operations. However, care is needed when parameterizing
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}
. Suppose
\mathbf{E}\equiv\sigma^{2}_{E}\times\mathbf{R}
, where
\sigma^{2}_{E}>0
and
\mathbf{R}
is a
b\times b
correlation matrix; and suppose
\bm{\xi}
has covariance matrix
\sigma^{2}_{\xi}\mathbf{I}_{N}
. Then,
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}=\sigma^{2}_{E}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}^{\top}+\sigma^{2}_{\xi}\mathbf{I}_{N}
,
which is over-parameterized. The parameters
\sigma^{2}_{E}
and
\sigma^{2}_{\xi}
cannot be identified together, but their ratio can be. Fixing
\sigma_{\xi}^{2}=1
, we have
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}=\theta_{s}\left(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}^{\top}\right)+\mathbf{I}_{N}
, where
\theta_{s}
is interpreted as the relative importance of the spatially dependent
\mathbf{S}\bm{\eta}
, relative to the vector of independent standard Gaussian vector,
\bm{\xi}
. In Supplement
S2
, we extend this parameterization to multiple resolutions of spatial basis functions.

2.5
The t spatial random effects (t-SRE) copula

Here, we define a novel t spatial process with an SRE component. Let
\gamma
follow a Gamma distribution with shape and rate parameters both equal to
\nu/2
, where
\nu>2
. That is,
\gamma\mid\nu\sim\mathrm{Gam}(\nu/2,\nu/2)
. Then, define the
N
-variate random vector,

\mathbf{V}\equiv\gamma^{-1/2}\times\mathbf{W}=\gamma^{-1/2}\times\left(\mathbf{S}\bm{\eta}+\bm{\xi}\right).

(8)

As with the Gaussian SRE model,
\mathbf{V}
in (
8
) also has a hierarchical representation. Write (
8
) as
\mathbf{V}\equiv\mathbf{S}\bar{\bm{\eta}}+\bar{\bm{\xi}}
, where
\bar{\bm{\eta}}\equiv\gamma^{-1/2}\bm{\eta}
and
\bar{\bm{\xi}}\equiv\gamma^{-1/2}\bm{\xi}
. Then,
\mathbf{V}\mid\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma\sim\mathrm{MVG}(\mathbf{S}\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma^{-1}\mathbf{I}_{N})
and
\bar{\bm{\eta}}\mid\gamma,\mathbf{E}\sim\mathrm{MVG}(\mathbf{0},\gamma^{-1}\mathbf{E})
. This uses a well known representation of the multivariate t distribution as a scale-mixture of Gaussian distributions
(e.g.,
Koop et al., 2007
, pp. 256–258)
. From this hierarchical representation, integrating out
\bar{\bm{\eta}}
and
\gamma
yields the unconditional distribution of
\mathbf{V}
, namely a multivariate t distribution on
\nu>2
degrees of freedom with mean vector
\mathbf{0}
and positive-definite scale matrix
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}
.

In Proposition
2.3
that follows, we show how to obtain the t-SRE copula. Let
F_{t,1:N}(\cdot;\bm{\Sigma},\nu)
and
f_{t,1:N}(\cdot;\bm{\Sigma},\nu)
denote the CDF and PDF of the
N
-variate t distribution with
\nu>2
degrees of freedom, respectively, with mean vector
\mathbf{0}
and positive-definite scale matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
. For
\mathbf{v}\equiv(v_{1},...,v_{N})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

f_{t,1:N}(v_{1},...,v_{N};\bm{\Sigma},\nu)=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu+N}{2}\right)\det(\bm{\Sigma})^{-\frac{1}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)\left(\nu\pi\right)^{\frac{N}{2}}}\left(1+\frac{1}{\nu}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{v}\right)^{\left(-\frac{\nu+N}{2}\right)},

(9)

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the Gamma function. Then, the t copula with parameter given by an SRE covariance matrix, can be derived as follows.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
F_{t,1:N}(\cdot;\bm{\Sigma},\nu)
and
f_{t,1:N}(\cdot;\bm{\Sigma},\nu)
be the CDF and PDF, respectively, of an
N
-variate t distribution on
\nu>2
degrees of freedom with mean vector
\mathbf{0}
and positive-definite scale matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
. For
0\leq u_{1},...,u_{N}\leq 1
, the
N
-variate t copula with an SRE scale matrix and its joint copula density are, respectively,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}_{1:N}^{\mathrm{SRE}}(u_{1},...,u_{N};\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}},\nu)

\displaystyle=F_{t,1:N}(\sigma_{1}^{\mathrm{SRE}}T^{-1}_{\nu}(u_{1}),...,\sigma_{N}^{\mathrm{SRE}}T^{-1}_{\nu}(u_{N});\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}},\nu)

(10)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{t}_{1:N}^{\mathrm{SRE}}(u_{1},...,u_{N};\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}},\nu)

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{t,1:N}(\sigma_{1}^{\mathrm{SRE}}T^{-1}_{\nu}(u_{1}),...,\sigma_{N}^{\mathrm{SRE}}T^{-1}_{\nu}(u_{N});\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}},\nu)}{\prod_{j=1}^{N}t_{\nu}(T^{-1}_{\nu}(u_{j}))/\sigma_{j}^{\mathrm{SRE}}},

(11)

where
t_{\nu}(\cdot)
,
T_{\nu}(\cdot)
, and
T_{\nu}^{-1}(\cdot)
are the PDF, CDF, and quantile function of a standardized t distribution on
\nu>2
degrees of freedom, respectively, and recall that
\sigma_{1}^{\mathrm{SRE}},...,\sigma_{N}^{\mathrm{SRE}}
are the square-roots of the diagonal elements of
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}
.

Similar to the Gaussian-SRE copula, the proposition below shows how a particular elementwise transformation of
\mathbf{Y}
to
\mathbf{V}
induces the t-SRE copula model for
[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Define the t anamorphosis function as
V(A_{j})\equiv\sigma^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{j}T_{\nu}^{-1}(F_{j}(Y(A_{j})))
,
j=1,...,N
. Let
\mathbf{V}\equiv(V(A_{1}),...,V(A_{N}))^{\top}
follow a multivariate t distribution on
\nu>2
degrees of freedom with mean vector
\mathbf{0}
and positive-definite scale matrix
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}
. Then, by a transformation of variables, and for
y_{1},...,y_{N}\in\mathbb{R}
, it follows that,

\displaystyle[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{N}f_{j}(y_{j})\right)\mathfrak{t}_{1:N}^{\mathrm{SRE}}(F_{1}(y_{1}),...,F_{N}(y_{N});\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}},\nu),

(12)

where
\mathfrak{t}_{1:N}^{\mathrm{SRE}}
is given by (
11
)

Proposition
2.4
provides a straightforward method for deriving
[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
under the t-SRE copula model. Define
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{O}}\equiv(V(A_{\mathrm{O}1}),...,V(A_{\mathrm{O}K}))^{\top}\equiv\gamma^{-1/2}\times\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{O}}
, which unconditionally follows a
K
-variate t distribution on
\nu>2
degrees of freedom with mean vector
\mathbf{0}
and positive-definite scale matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{O}\mathrm{O}}^{\mathrm{SRE}}
. By a transformation of variables associated with the t anamorphosis in Proposition
2.4
, we obtain,

\displaystyle[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]=\left(\prod_{k=1}^{K}f_{\mathrm{O}k}(y_{\mathrm{O}k})\right)\mathfrak{t}_{1:K}^{\mathrm{SRE}}(F_{\mathrm{O}1}(y_{\mathrm{O}1}),...,F_{\mathrm{O}K}(y_{\mathrm{O}K});\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{\mathrm{O}\mathrm{O}},\nu).

(13)

Hence, the representation of a t-SRE copula is conserved under marginalization.

3
Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling

Our copula-based SRE models enable fast Bayesian inference for large spatial datasets. In this section, we show how the hierarchical representation of the copula-based model for
[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
enables a computationally efficient Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm to obtain the posterior distributions of
\mathbf{Y}
and
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
given the data
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}
. Section
3.1
sets out the type of algorithm we use. Sections
3.2
and
3.3
particularize this to, respectively, the Gau-SRE copula model and the t-SRE copula model.

3.1
Gibbs sampler with Metropolis-Hastings steps

We use a Gibbs sampler
(
Gelfand and Smith, 1990
)
with some Metropolis-Hastings steps
(
Metropolis et al., 1953
;
Hastings, 1970
)
, which we refer to as Metropolis-within-Gibbs (MwG) steps. We require the full-conditional distributions of all parameters and latent variables in the model. We denote the full-conditional density for
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
(say) as
[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\mid~\mathrm{rest}]
, where “rest” is used to represent all remaining variables. In the example, “rest” stands for
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}
. We write
[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\mid~\mathrm{rest}]\propto[\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}]\times[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
, which simplifies to
[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\mid~\mathrm{rest}]\propto[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
, because the term with
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}
does not depend on
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
.

3.2
MCMC for the Gau-SRE copula model

The key to computationally efficient Bayesian inference in our copula-based SRE models is to leverage the fact that, due to (
3
), the elements of
\mathbf{W}
(and therefore
\mathbf{Y}
) are conditionally independent of each other given
\bm{\eta}
. Therefore, by Proposition
2.2
, which connects
\mathbf{W}
to
\mathbf{Y}
in the Gau-SRE copula model, we can represent the process model in terms of
[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]=[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
and
[\bm{\eta}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
. Using (
3
), we can see that
\mathbf{W}\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\sim\mathrm{MVG}(\mathbf{S}\bm{\eta},\mathbf{I}_{N})
; it also easily follows that
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\sim\mathrm{MVG}(\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}\bm{\eta},\mathbf{I}_{N})
and
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\sim\mathrm{MVG}(\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{M}}\bm{\eta},\mathbf{I}_{N})
. Then by applying the same transformation of variables used in Proposition
2.2
, we have, for
y_{\mathrm{O}1},...,y_{\mathrm{O}K},y_{\mathrm{M}1},...,y_{\mathrm{M}L}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{K}\left(\frac{\sigma^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{\mathrm{O}k}f_{\mathrm{O}k}(y_{\mathrm{O}k})}{\phi(\Phi^{-1}(F_{\mathrm{O}k}(y_{\mathrm{O}k})))}\frac{\exp\{-0.5(w_{\mathrm{O}k}-\mathbf{S}(A_{\mathrm{O}k})^{\top}\bm{\eta})^{2}\}}{(2\pi)^{1/2}}\right),

(14)

\displaystyle[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]

\displaystyle=\prod_{l=1}^{L}\left(\frac{\sigma^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{\mathrm{M}l}f_{\mathrm{M}l}(y_{\mathrm{M}l})}{\phi(\Phi^{-1}(F_{\mathrm{M}l}(y_{\mathrm{M}l})))}\frac{\exp\{-0.5(w_{\mathrm{M}l}-\mathbf{S}(A_{\mathrm{M}l})^{\top}\bm{\eta})^{2}\}}{(2\pi)^{1/2}}\right),

(15)

where
\sigma^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{\mathrm{O}k}
is the square-root of the
k
-th diagonal element of
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{\mathrm{O}\mathrm{O}}\equiv\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}+\mathbf{I}_{K}
;
\sigma_{\mathrm{M}l}^{\mathrm{SRE}}
is the square-root of the
l
-th diagonal element of
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{\mathrm{M}\mathrm{M}}\equiv\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{M}}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{M}}^{\top}+\mathbf{I}_{L}
;
w_{\mathrm{O}k}\equiv\sigma^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{\mathrm{O}k}\Phi^{-1}(F_{\mathrm{O}k}(y_{\mathrm{O}k}))
for
k=1,...,K
; and
w_{\mathrm{M}l}\equiv\sigma^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{\mathrm{M}l}\Phi^{-1}(F_{\mathrm{M}l}(y_{\mathrm{M}l}))
for
l=1,...,L
.

Under the hierarchical representation of the Gau-SRE copula model, the full joint posterior can be written as,

\displaystyle[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}},

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\mid\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}]=[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}][\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\mid\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}]

\displaystyle\propto[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\Big\{[\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}][\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}][\bm{\eta}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}][\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\Big\}.

(16)

To sample from the full joint posterior on the left-hand side (LHS) of (
16
), we sample from the posterior distribution
[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\mid\mathbf{Z}]
and then sample from the conditional distribution
[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]=[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
in (
15
). Hence, we need the full-conditional distributions of
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}
,
\bm{\eta}
, and
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
. In Supplement
S3.1
, we show the full conditionals and
[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
are either available in closed form or can be sampled efficiently using MwG steps by leveraging the conditional independence of the elements of
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}
given
\bm{\eta}
.

3.3
MCMC for the t-SRE copula model

Efficient MCMC for the t-SRE copula model works similarly. From (
8
), the elements of
\mathbf{V}
are conditionally independent given
\bar{\bm{\eta}}
,
\gamma
, and
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
. Then we can exploit the fact that Proposition
2.4
links
\mathbf{V}
to
\mathbf{Y}
via a transformation of random variables to rewrite the process model of the t-SRE copula models in terms of
[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]=[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
,
[\bar{\bm{\eta}}\mid\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
, and
[\gamma\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
. In particular, we have
\mathbf{V}\mid\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\sim\mathrm{MVG}(\mathbf{S}\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma^{-1}\mathbf{I}_{N})
. It also follows that
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\sim\mathrm{MVG}(\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma^{-1}\mathbf{I}_{K})
and
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\sim\mathrm{MVG}(\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{M}}\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma^{-1}\mathbf{I}_{L})
. By applying the same transformation of variables used in Proposition
2.4
, we obtain,
for
y_{\mathrm{O}1},...,y_{\mathrm{O}K},y_{\mathrm{M}1},...,y_{\mathrm{M}L}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{K}\left(\frac{\sigma^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{\mathrm{O}k}f_{\mathrm{O}k}(y_{\mathrm{O}k})}{t_{\nu}(T_{\nu}^{-1}(F_{\mathrm{O}k}(y_{\mathrm{O}k})))}\frac{\exp\{-0.5\gamma(v_{\mathrm{O}k}-\mathbf{S}(A_{\mathrm{O}k})^{\top}\bar{\bm{\eta}})^{2}\}}{(2\pi\gamma^{-1})^{1/2}}\right),

(17)

\displaystyle[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]

\displaystyle=\prod_{l=1}^{L}\left(\frac{\sigma^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{\mathrm{M}l}f_{\mathrm{M}l}(y_{\mathrm{M}l})}{t_{\nu}(T_{\nu}^{-1}(F_{\mathrm{M}l}(y_{\mathrm{M}l})))}\frac{\exp\{-0.5\gamma(v_{\mathrm{M}l}-\mathbf{S}(A_{\mathrm{M}l})^{\top}\bar{\bm{\eta}})^{2}\}}{(2\pi\gamma^{-1})^{1/2}}\right),

(18)

where
v_{\mathrm{O}k}\equiv\sigma^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{\mathrm{O}k}T_{\nu}^{-1}(F_{\mathrm{O}k}(y_{\mathrm{O}k}))
,
k=1,...,K
, and
v_{\mathrm{M}l}\equiv\sigma^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{\mathrm{M}l}T_{\nu}^{-1}(F_{\mathrm{M}l}(y_{\mathrm{M}l}))
,
l=1,...,L
.

The conditional distributions (
17
) and (
18
) are needed to write the full joint posterior distribution under the hierarchical representation of the t-SRE copula model. This is,

\displaystyle[

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}},\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\mid\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}]=[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}][\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\mid\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}]

\displaystyle\propto[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\Big\{[\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}][\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}][\bar{\bm{\eta}}\mid\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}][\gamma\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}][\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\Big\},

(19)

where
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
includes the degrees-of-freedom parameter
\nu>2
, in addition to all those present for the Gau-SRE model. To sample from the full joint posterior on the LHS of (
19
), we sample from the joint posterior
[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\mid\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}]
and also the conditional distribution,
[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]=[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
in (
18
). Like in the Gaussian case, Supplement
S3.2
shows that the full-conditional distributions of
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}
,
\bar{\bm{\eta}}
,
\gamma
, and
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
, as well as (
18
), are either available in closed form or can be sampled efficiently with MwG steps as a consequence of our SRE representation of the t-SRE copula model.

4
Simulation studies

The simulation studies given in this section demonstrate parameter recovery, prediction, uncertainty quantification, and computational efficiency for the copula-based hierarchical spatial-statistical models developed in Section
2
. Section
4.1
outlines four copula-based spatial-statistical models. Section
4.2
outlines the structure of the simulation experiments. Section
4.3
shows selected results and interprets them, with the remaining results given in Supplement
S4
.

4.1
Models

Four basic models are used in this simulation study. They are obtained by using log-Gaussian (LG) and skew-Gaussian (SG)
(
Azzalini, 1985
)
marginals with joint dependence captured by the Gau-SRE and t-SRE copula models. Hence, there are four models (
2\times 2
), which are labelled LG-Gau-SRE, SG-Gau-SRE, LG-t-SRE, and SG-t-SRE models, whose names indicate the marginals and the copula used. For both sets of marginals, we set
E(Y(A_{j})\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}})=\exp\{\beta_{0}\}
(a log-linear model with only an intercept term) and
\mathrm{var}(Y(A_{j})\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}})=\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{P}}
, for
j=1,...,N
. Here,
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
depends on the copula model. For the LG-Gau-SRE model,
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}=(\beta_{0},\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{P}},\theta_{s},\theta_{r})^{\top}
, with an extra degrees-of-freedom parameter
\nu>2
for the t-SRE copula models, and a skewness parameter
\lambda
for the SG-Gau-SRE and SG-t-SRE models. The parameters
\theta_{s}
and
\theta_{r}
(explained below) control spatial dependence in the copula. The log-Gaussian CDF for
Y(A_{j})
is written as
F_{LG,j}(y_{j};\beta_{0},\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{P}})
,
y_{j}\in(0,\infty)
, and the skew-Gaussian CDF is written as
F_{SG,j}(y_{j};\beta_{0},\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{P}},\lambda)
,
y_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is the skewness parameter of the skew-Gaussian distribution. Note that the LG-Gau-SRE model is equivalent to a log-Gaussian SRE model, where a version is implemented in the R package FRK
(
Sainsbury-Dale et al., 2024
)
. See Supplement
S4.1
–
S4.8
for more details.

4.2
Structure of the simulation study

The spatial domain for the simulation study was the unit square,
D\equiv[0,1]^{2}
, split into 10,000 equal-sized BAUs. Of these, 5,000 were ‘observed BAUs’,
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{O}}
, and 5,000 were ‘missing BAUs’,
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{M}}
. The choice of
K=5,000
observed BAUs was motivated by the size of the real datasets analyzed in Section
5
. The missing BAUs were either randomly selected, hence ‘missing at random’ (MAR), or systematically missing in blocks, hence ‘missing by design’ (MBD). The MBD case was designed to provide a true test of the predictive performance of our models, since most missing BAUs are far from any observations. For the MBD case, the 2,500 BAUs in the top-left and bottom-right corners of the unit square were all designated as members of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{M}}
. Once chosen, the same
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{O}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{M}}
were used for each model. See Supplement
S4.9
for maps of the BAUs in the MAR and MBD configurations.

Then,
R=100
realizations of
\mathbf{Y}
and
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}
were simulated. Details are given below for a t-SRE copula model, and Supplement
S4.10
shows how datasets were simulated for models with Gau-SRE copulas. First, the true scale matrix for the random-effects vector was calculated as
\mathbf{E}^{*}(\theta_{s},\theta_{r})\equiv\theta_{s}\exp\{-\mathbf{D}/\theta_{r}\}
, elementwise, where
\mathbf{D}
is a matrix whose
(i,j)
-th entry is the distance between the
i
-th and
j
-th spatial basis functions. The true values of the parameters were set as
\beta_{0}^{*}=\log(1000)
,
\lambda^{*}=-5
,
\theta_{s}^{*}=10
,
\theta_{r}^{*}=\sqrt{2}/4
, and
\nu^{*}=4
for all models. For the models with log-Gaussian marginals, we set
(\sigma^{*}_{\mathrm{P}})^{2}=(0.1)^{2}
. For the models with skew-Gaussian marginals, we set
(\sigma^{*}_{\mathrm{P}})^{2}=(100)^{2}
. In both cases, we set
(\sigma^{*}_{\mathrm{O}})^{2}=0.05(\sigma^{*}_{\mathrm{P}})^{2}
. The FRK package
(
Zammit-Mangion and Cressie, 2021
;
Sainsbury-Dale et al., 2024
)
in R was used to generate 36 bisquare spatial basis functions
(
Cressie and Johannesson, 2008
)
with apertures of
0.375
units on a regular grid across
D
(see Supplement
S4.9
). To generate the simulated datasets, let
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}^{*}
be a vector of the true parameter values for the model. For
r=1,...,R
, we simulated
\gamma^{(r)}\sim[\gamma\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}^{*}]
; then we simulated
\bar{\bm{\eta}}^{(r)}\sim[\bar{\bm{\eta}}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}^{*},\gamma^{(r)}]
; then we simulated
\mathbf{Y}^{(r)}\sim[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bar{\bm{\eta}}^{(r)},\gamma^{(r)},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}^{*}]
, where recall
\mathbf{Y}^{(r)}\equiv((\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}^{(r)})^{\top},(\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}^{(r)})^{\top})^{\top}
; and finally, we simulated the observed spatial data
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}^{(r)}\sim[\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}^{(r)},(\sigma^{*}_{\mathrm{O}})^{2}]
.

For each of
R=100
simulated datasets and for each model, fully Bayesian inference was carried out via MCMC. The weakly informative priors for the parameters are shown in Supplement
S4.11
, and the initial values are described in Supplement
S4.12

For the LG-Gau-SRE and LG-t-SRE models, it took, respectively, 7-8 minutes and 18-20 minutes to run the MCMC for 45,000 iterations. For the SG-Gau-SRE and SG-t-SRE models, it took, respectively, 16-18 minutes and 27-28 minutes. To speed up computations, the skew-Gaussian CDF and quantile function were evaluated at 101 points and interpolated using splines. We ran the MCMC for each dataset in parallel on Australia’s National Computing Infrastructure’s GADI high-performance computing cluster.

6
Discussion and conclusion

In this article, we develop copula-based hierarchical spatial-statistical models for non-Gaussian spatial processes that can be fitted to very large spatial datasets while properly accounting for measurement errors in the spatial data. We show how embedding spatial random effects into Gaussian copulas and t copulas, enables fast Bayesian spatial prediction from large, incomplete, noisy, and non-Gaussian spatial datasets. Simulation studies demonstrate reliable parameter recovery, superior predictive performance, and improved uncertainty quantification, compared to several benchmark spatial-statistical models. To illustrate the practical utility of our approach, we apply it to mapping atmospheric methane over a coal-producing region in Queensland, Australia. Future research could investigate copula-based hierarchical spatio-temporal modeling with spatio-temporal random effects, which would allow smoothing, filtering, and forecasting of non-Gaussian spatio-temporal random fields from large amounts of noisy, incomplete, non-Gaussian spatio-temporal data.

U_{j}\equiv T_{\nu}(V(A_{j})/\sigma^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{j})
, where
U_{j}
is uniform on
[0,1]
and
V(A_{j})/\sigma^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{j}
follows a standardized t distribution on
\nu
degrees of freedom; conversely,
V(A_{j})=\sigma_{j}^{\mathrm{SRE}}T_{\nu}^{-1}(U_{j})
. In obvious notation, we write the same transformations for realizations

and
v_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
, as
u_{j}=T_{\nu}(v_{j}/\sigma^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{j})
and
v_{j}=\sigma^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{j}T_{\nu}^{-1}(u_{j})
. Then the t copula with SRE covariance-matrix parameter can be written as follows: For
u_{1},...,u_{N}\in[0,1]
,

\mathfrak{T}_{1:N}^{\mathrm{SRE}}\!\left(u_{1},...,u_{N};\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}},\nu\right)=F_{t,1:N}\!\left(\sigma_{1}^{\mathrm{SRE}}T_{\nu}^{-1}(u_{1}),...,\sigma_{N}^{\mathrm{SRE}}T_{\nu}^{-1}(u_{N});\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}},\nu\right),

which is (
10
). For the copula density, simply take partial derivatives with respect to all
u_{1},...,u_{N}
and use the chain rule to arrive at (
11
).
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.4
.

By construction,
\mathbf{V}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
follows an
N
-variate t distribution on
\nu
degrees of freedom with mean vector
\mathbf{0}
and positive-definite scale matrix
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}
. Therefore, its PDF can be written as follows: For
\mathbf{v}\equiv(v_{1},...,v_{N})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

[\mathbf{V}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]=f_{t,1:N}\left(v_{1},...,v_{N};\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}},\nu\right)=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu+N}{2}\right)|\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}|^{-\frac{1}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)\left(\nu\pi\right)^{\frac{N}{2}}}\left(1+\frac{1}{\nu}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\bm{\left}(\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{v}\right)^{\left(-\frac{\nu+N}{2}\right)}.

For

[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]=\left|\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\partial v_{j}}{\partial y_{j}}\right|\times f_{t,1:N}(\sigma_{1}^{\mathrm{SRE}}T_{\nu}^{-1}(F_{1}(y_{1})),...,\sigma_{N}^{\mathrm{SRE}}T_{\nu}^{-1}(F_{N}(y_{N}));\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}},\nu).

The
j

\frac{\partial v_{j}}{\partial y_{j}}=\frac{\sigma_{j}^{\mathrm{SRE}}f_{j}(y_{j})}{t_{\nu}(T_{\nu}^{-1}(F_{j}(y_{j})))},~y_{j}\in\mathbb{R},

where recall that

\displaystyle[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\sigma_{j}^{\mathrm{SRE}}f_{j}(y_{j})}{t_{\nu}(T_{\nu}^{-1}(F_{j}(y_{j})))}\right)\times f_{t,1:N}(\sigma_{1}^{\mathrm{SRE}}T_{\nu}^{-1}(F_{1}(y_{1})),...,\sigma_{N}^{\mathrm{SRE}}T_{\nu}^{-1}(F_{N}(y_{N}));\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}},\nu)

Appendix S2
Parameterization of
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}
with multiple resolutions of basis functions

Recall from Section
2.4
that
\mathbf{S}
is an
N\times b
matrix of spatial basis functions, and
\bm{\eta}\sim\mathrm{MVG}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{E})
is a
b
-variate random vector of basis-function coefficients with mean vector
\mathbf{0}
and
b\times b
covariance matrix
\mathbf{E}
. Also recall that
\bm{\xi}\sim\mathrm{MVG}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{N})
, where
\mathbf{I}_{N}
is the
N
-dimensional identity matrix, is an
N
-variate standard Gaussian random vector that is independent of
\bm{\eta}
. Then we have
\mathbf{W}\equiv\mathbf{S}\bm{\eta}+\bm{\xi}
per (
3
), where the unconditional distribution of
\mathbf{W}
is an
N
-variate Gaussian distribution with mean vector
\mathbf{0}
and covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}\equiv\mathbf{S}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{S}^{\top}+\mathbf{I}_{N}
. In this section, we show how to validly parameterize
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}
when
\mathbf{S}
contains multiple resolutions of spatial basis functions. This is a common strategy to capture spatial dependence at various spatial length scales.

Let
P\geq 2
, and define
\{\mathbf{S}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times b_{p}}:p=1,...,P\}
to be a set of spatial-basis-function matrices for
P
different resolutions of basis functions, with a total of
b=\sum_{p=1}^{P}b_{p}
basis functions. In (
3
), the matrix
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times b}
can be partitioned as
\mathbf{S}\equiv(\mathbf{S}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{S}_{P})
. The corresponding
b
-variate vector of random effects can also be decomposed as
\bm{\eta}\equiv(\bm{\eta}_{1}^{\top},...,\bm{\eta}_{P}^{\top})^{\top}
, where
\bm{\eta}_{p}
is a
b_{p}
-variate Gaussian random vector with mean vector
\mathbf{0}
and positive-definite covariance matrix
\mathbf{E}_{p}
for
p=1,...,P
. Parameterize the covariance matrix as
\mathbf{E}_{p}\equiv\theta_{s,p}\times\mathbf{R}_{p}
, where
\mathbf{R}_{p}
is an
b_{p}\times b_{p}
correlation matrix. Finally, assume
\{\bm{\eta}_{p}:p=1,...,P\}
are mutually independent. Therefore, after defining
\bm{\eta}\equiv(\bm{\eta}_{1}^{\top},...,\bm{\eta}_{P}^{\top})^{\top}
, we have
\bm{\eta}\sim\mathrm{MVG}(\mathbf{0},\mathrm{diag}\{\mathbf{E}_{1},...,\mathbf{E}_{P}\})
or, equivalently,
\bm{\eta}\sim\mathrm{MVG}(\mathbf{0},\mathrm{diag}\{\theta_{s,1}\mathbf{R}_{1},\cdots,\theta_{s,P}\mathbf{R}_{P}\})
. Each of the parameters
\{\theta_{s,p}:p=1,...,P\}
represents the relative importance of
\{\mathbf{S}_{p}\bm{\eta}_{p}:p=1,...,P\}
relative to
\bm{\xi}
. The parameters
\{\theta_{s,p}\}
and any parameters of the correlation matrices
\{\mathbf{R}_{p}:p=1,...,P\}
remain identifiable in
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{S}^{\top}+\mathbf{I}_{N}
because they do not merely scale the marginal random variables and the covariance matrix; see Section
2.4
of the main article for further details on parameter identification.

~\mathrm{rest}

S3.1
Derivations of the full-conditional densities for the Gau-SRE copula model

In this section, we derive the full-conditional distributions needed for MCMC with the Gau-SRE copula model (Section
2.3
and Section
3.2
). We also give information for sampling from these full-conditional distributions, if they are not available in closed form.

S3.1.1
Derivation of
[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid~\mathrm{rest}]
and sampling from it

From (
16
), we see that the joint posterior of
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}
,
\bm{\eta}
, and
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
is given by,

[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\mid\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}]\propto[\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}]\times[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bm{\eta}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}].

(S1)

To obtain the full-conditional distribution for
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}
, we write
[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid~\mathrm{rest}]\propto[\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}]\times[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bm{\eta}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
, which simplifies to
[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid~\mathrm{rest}]\propto[\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}]\times[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
, where recall that the data-model parameters
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}
are assumed known. Conditional independence in the data model and the conditional independence of the elements of
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}
given
\bm{\eta}
(see (
14
)) mean that we can write,

[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid~\mathrm{rest}]\propto\prod_{k=1}^{K}[Z(A_{\mathrm{O}k})\mid Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k}),\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}]\times[Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k})\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}].

The
k
-th term (
k=1,...,K
) in the product (i.e.,
[Z(A_{\mathrm{O}k})\mid Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k}),\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}]\times[Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k})\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
) is in fact equivalent to
[Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k})\mid~\mathrm{rest}]
, which is the full-conditional density of
Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k})
. This means the latent values
\{Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k}):k=1,...,K\}
can be sampled independently and in parallel in
K
separate Metropolis-within-Gibbs (MwG) steps with random-walk proposals. Each step only involves evaluating two univariate conditional densities, which makes the computations very fast.

S3.1.2
Derivation of
[\bm{\eta}\mid~\mathrm{rest}]
and sampling from it

Recall from Section
2.3
that
\bm{\eta}\sim\mathrm{MVG}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{E})
is a random vector of spatial-basis function coefficients from (
3
), where
\mathbf{E}
is a
b\times b
covariance matrix. Also recall that
\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}
is a
K\times b
dimensional matrix of spatial basis functions at the observed BAUs, and let
\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{O}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
be a realization of
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{O}}
(see Section
2.3
).

From (
S1
), we have
[\bm{\eta}\mid~\mathrm{rest}]\propto[\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}]\times[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bm{\eta}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
. This simplifies to
[\bm{\eta}\mid~\mathrm{rest}]\propto[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bm{\eta}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
. The following algebra then shows that the full conditional distribution
[\bm{\eta}\mid~\mathrm{rest}]
has a recognizable form:

\displaystyle[\bm{\eta}\mid~\mathrm{rest}]

\displaystyle\propto\frac{\exp\{-0.5(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{O}}-\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}\bm{\eta})^{\top}(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{O}}-\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}\bm{\eta})\}}{(2\pi)^{K/2}}\times\frac{\exp\{-0.5\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{E}^{-1}\bm{\eta}\}}{(2\pi)^{b/2}\det(\mathbf{E})^{1/2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\{-0.5(\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{O}}-2\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{O}}+\bm{\eta}^{\top}(\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}+\mathbf{E}^{-1})\bm{\eta})\}}{(2\pi)^{(K+b)/2}\det(\mathbf{E})^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\propto\exp\{-0.5(\bm{\eta}^{\top}(\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}+\mathbf{E}^{-1})\bm{\eta}-2\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{O}})\}

\displaystyle\propto\exp\{-0.5(\bm{\eta}^{\top}(\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}+\mathbf{E}^{-1})\bm{\eta}-2\bm{\eta}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{O}}+\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}(\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}+\mathbf{E}^{-1})^{-1}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{O}})\}

\displaystyle\propto\exp\{-0.5(\bm{\eta}-(\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}+\mathbf{E}^{-1})^{-1}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{O}})^{\top}(\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}+\mathbf{E}^{-1})(\bm{\eta}-(\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}+\mathbf{E}^{-1})^{-1}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{O}})\}.

The right-hand side of the final line is proportional to the density of a
b
-variate Gaussian distribution with mean vector,
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{\eta}\mid~\mathrm{rest}}\equiv(\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}+\mathbf{E}^{-1})^{-1}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{O}}
, and covariance matrix,
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{\eta}\mid~\mathrm{rest}}\equiv(\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}+\mathbf{E}^{-1})^{-1}
. This completes the derivation. Sampling from this low-dimensional Gaussian distribution is straightforward.

S3.1.3
Derivation of
[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\mid~\mathrm{rest}]
and sampling from it

From (
S1
), we have
[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\mid~\mathrm{rest}]\propto[\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}]\times[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bm{\eta}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
, which simplifies to,

[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\mid~\mathrm{rest}]\propto[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bm{\eta}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}].

However, integrating out the random effects
\bm{\eta}
results in better mixing of the Markov chain. Hence, we use the full-conditional density,

\displaystyle[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\mid~\mathrm{rest}]

\displaystyle\propto\left\{\int_{\bm{\eta}}[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bm{\eta}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]~\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta}\right\}\times[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]

\displaystyle\propto[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{k=1}^{K}f_{\mathrm{O}k}(y_{\mathrm{O}k})\right)\times\mathfrak{g}_{1:K}^{\mathrm{SRE}}(F_{\mathrm{O}1}(y_{\mathrm{O}1}),...,F_{\mathrm{O}K}(y_{\mathrm{O}K});\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{\mathrm{O}\mathrm{O}})\times[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}].

The parameters can be sampled using an adaptive random-walk Metropolis-Hastings (MH) algorithm. Any strictly positive elements of
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
(e.g.,
\theta_{s}
) are log-transformed; the resulting vector of parameters, where some elements have been transformed, is written as
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{P}}
. The proposal distribution for MH sampling of
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{P}}
is taken to be a multivariate Gaussian distribution. We follow
Garthwaite et al., 2016
to adapt a scaling factor for the covariance matrix of the proposal distribution to achieve an acceptance rate of approximately 24%. The covariance matrix itself is adapted at each iteration using all previous samples.

S3.1.4
Sampling from
[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]

As for sampling from the conditional distribution
[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
, this is simplified by the fact that
[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]=[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]=\prod_{l=1}^{L}[Y(A_{\mathrm{M}l})\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
(see (
15
)), since
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}
(and all of their elements) are conditionally independent given
\bm{\eta}
.

Now, recall that
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\equiv(Y(A_{\mathrm{M}1}),...,Y(A_{\mathrm{M}L}))^{\top}
. For
l=1,...,L
and given posterior samples of
\bm{\eta}
and
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
, we can simulate
Y(A_{\mathrm{M}l})
from
[Y(A_{\mathrm{M}l})\mid\bm{\eta},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
by the following procedure:

1.

Simulate
W(A_{\mathrm{M}l})\sim\mathrm{Gau}(\mathbf{S}(A_{\mathrm{M}l})^{\top}\bm{\eta},1)
, and

2.

Compute
Y(A_{\mathrm{M}l})=F_{\mathrm{M}l}^{-1}\big(\Phi\big(W(A_{\mathrm{M}l})/\sigma^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{\mathrm{M}l}\big)\big)
, where recall that
\sigma^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{\mathrm{M}l}
is the square root of the
l
-th diagonal element of
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{M}\mathrm{M}}^{\mathrm{SRE}}\equiv\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{M}}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{M}}^{\top}+\mathbf{I}_{L}
.

b
-variate Gaussian distribution with mean vector,
\bm{\mu}_{\bar{\bm{\eta}}\mid~\mathrm{rest}}\equiv(\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}+\mathbf{E}^{-1})^{-1}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{O}}
, and covariance matrix,
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bar{\bm{\eta}}\mid~\mathrm{rest}}\equiv\gamma^{-1}(\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}+\mathbf{E}^{-1})^{-1}
. This completes the derivation of
[\bar{\bm{\eta}}\mid~\mathrm{rest}]
. Sampling from this low-dimensional multivariate Gaussian distribution is straightforward.

S3.2.3
Derivation of
[\gamma\mid~\mathrm{rest}]

From (
S2
), the full-conditional density of
\gamma
can be written as
[\gamma\mid~\mathrm{rest}]\propto[\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}]\times[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bar{\bm{\eta}}\mid\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\gamma\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
. This can be simplified to,

[\gamma\mid~\mathrm{rest}]\propto[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bar{\bm{\eta}}\mid\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\gamma\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}].

In the full-conditional distribution, we condition on the parameters
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
. The transformation that links
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}
with
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{O}}
(see Proposition
2.4
) is
V(A_{\mathrm{O}k})\equiv\sigma_{\mathrm{O}k}^{\mathrm{SRE}}T_{\nu}^{-1}(F_{\mathrm{O}k}(Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k})))
, for
k=1,...,K
, and this transformation is deterministic when
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
is fixed. Therefore, we can write,

[\gamma\mid~\mathrm{rest}]\propto[\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bar{\bm{\eta}}\mid\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\gamma\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}].

We can achieve better mixing of the MCMC sampler by integrating out the random effects
\bar{\bm{\eta}}
in the equation above. Therefore, we use,

[\gamma\mid~\mathrm{rest}]\propto[\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\gamma\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}],

where
[\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]=\int_{\bar{\bm{\eta}}}[\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{O}},\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bar{\bm{\eta}}\mid\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]~\mathrm{d}\bar{\bm{\eta}}
. From (
8
), we know that
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\sim\mathrm{MVG}(\mathbf{0},\gamma^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathrm{O}\mathrm{O}}^{\mathrm{SRE}})
. Therefore, it follows that

\displaystyle[\gamma\mid~\mathrm{rest}]

\displaystyle\propto[\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\gamma\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]

\displaystyle\propto\gamma^{0.5K}\exp\{-0.5\gamma\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}(\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{\mathrm{O}\mathrm{O}})^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{O}}\}\gamma^{0.5\nu-1}\exp\{-0.5\gamma\nu\}

\displaystyle\propto\gamma^{0.5(K+\nu)-1}\exp\{-0.5\gamma(\nu+\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}(\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{\mathrm{O}\mathrm{O}})^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{O}})\}.

The right-hand side of the last line is proportional to the density of a Gamma-distributed random variable with shape parameter
0.5(K+\nu)
and rate parameter
0.5(\nu+\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\top}(\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{\mathrm{O}\mathrm{O}})^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{O}})
, which completes the derivation. Sampling from this Gamma distribution is straightforward.

S3.2.4
Derivation of
[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\mid~\mathrm{rest}]
and sampling from it

From (
S2
), the naïve construction of the full-conditional density for
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
is,
[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\mid~\mathrm{rest}]\propto[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bar{\bm{\eta}}\mid\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\gamma\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
. As in the Gaussian-copula case, integrating out the random effects
\bar{\bm{\eta}}
, results in better mixing of the Markov chain for the parameters. Additionally, integrating out
\gamma
further improves the mixing properties. Therefore, for sampling the process-model parameters, we use the following full-conditional density:

\displaystyle[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}\mid~\mathrm{rest}]

\displaystyle\propto\left\{\int_{\gamma}\left\{\int_{\bar{\bm{\eta}}}[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bar{\bm{\eta}}\mid\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]~\mathrm{d}{\bar{\bm{\eta}}}\right\}\times[\gamma\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]~\mathrm{d}\gamma\right\}\times[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]

\displaystyle\propto[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]\times[\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}],

where
[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
is given by (
13
).

The parameters can be sampled using an adaptive random-walk Metropolis-Hastings algorithm. Any strictly positive elements of
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
(e.g.,
\theta_{s}
) are log-transformed; the resulting vector of parameters, where some elements have been transformed, is written as
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{P}}
. The proposal distribution for
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{P}}
is taken to be a multivariate Gaussian distribution. We follow
Garthwaite et al., 2016
to adapt the scale factor of the proposal distribution’s covariance matrix to achieve an acceptance rate of approximately 24%. The covariance matrix itself is adapted using at each iteration all previous samples.

S3.2.5
Sampling from
[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]

Recognizing that
[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]=[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
and using (
18
), it is straightforward to sample from
[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma,\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]
: For
l=1,...,L
and given posterior samples of
\bar{\bm{\eta}}
,
\gamma
, and
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
,

1.

Generate
V(A_{\mathrm{M}l})
from
\mathrm{Gau}(\mathbf{S}(A_{\mathrm{M}l})^{\top}\bar{\bm{\eta}},\gamma^{-1})
, and

2.

Compute
Y(A_{\mathrm{M}l})=F_{\mathrm{M}l}^{-1}(T_{\nu}(V(A_{\mathrm{M}l})/\sigma^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{\mathrm{M}l}))
.

S4.1
LG-Gau-SRE model

The LG-Gau-SRE model is specified as follows. Let
Y\sim\mathrm{LG}(m,s^{2})
denote a (generic) random variable
Y
that follows a log-Gaussian distribution with log-scale mean
m
and log-scale variance
s^{2}
. Now, for the data model, the
k
-th observation follows the conditional distribution,

Z(A_{\mathrm{O}k})\mid Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k}),\sigma_{\mathrm{O}}^{2}\sim\mathrm{LG}(\log(Y(A_{j}))-0.5\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{O}},\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{O}}),

(S3)

and we also assume that the spatial data are conditionally independent given the underlying latent-process values. The data model in (
S3
) has been parameterized to guarantee that
E(Z(A_{\mathrm{O}k})\mid Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k}),\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{O}})=Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k})
.

For the marginals of the copula-based process, suppose
Y(A_{j})\mid\beta_{0},\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}\sim\mathrm{LG}(\beta_{0}-0.5\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{P}},\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{P}})
for
j=1,...,N
, which means that
E(Y(A_{j})\mid\beta_{0},\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{P}})=\exp\{\beta_{0}\}
and
\mathrm{var}(Y(A_{j})\mid\beta_{0},\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{P}})=\exp\{2\beta_{0}\}\times(\exp\{\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{P}}\}-1)
. In general, it is not necessary to restrict ourselves to a constant mean for the marginals. We can easily replace
\beta_{0}
with the linear model,
\mathbf{x}(A_{j})^{\top}\bm{\beta}
, where
\mathbf{x}(A_{j})^{\top}
is a vector of covariates at BAU
A_{j}
, for
j=1,...,N
. The dependence structure of
\mathbf{Y}
is given by the Gau-SRE copula in (
6
), which has covariance-matrix parameter
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}\equiv\mathbf{S}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{S}^{\top}+\mathbf{I}_{N}
. Here,
\mathbf{E}
is an exponential covariance matrix; as explained in Section
4.1
. Letting
\theta_{s},\theta_{r}>0
be parameters, we write the matrix as
\mathbf{E}(\theta_{s},\theta_{r})
, where the
(i,j)
element of
\mathbf{E}
is given by
E_{i,j}=\theta_{s}\exp\{-d_{ij}/\theta_{r}\}
, and
d_{ij}
is the Euclidean distance (or, depending on the application, great-circle distance) between the
i
-th and
j
-th basis function centre. Hence, the joint PDF of
\mathbf{Y}
is defined as follows: For
y_{1},...,y_{N}>0
,

\displaystyle[\mathbf{Y}\mid\beta_{0},\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{P}},\theta_{s},\theta_{r}]

\displaystyle~~~~~~~=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{N}f_{LG,j}(y_{j};\beta_{0},\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}^{2})\right)\times\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{1:N}(F_{LG,1}(y_{1};\beta_{0},\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}^{2})),...,F_{LG,N}(y_{N};\beta_{0},\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}));\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}),

where recall that
\{f_{LG,j}(y_{j};\beta_{0},\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}):j=1,...,N\}
and
\{F_{LG,j}(y_{j};\beta_{0},\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}):j=1,...,N\}
denote, respectively, the PDFs and CDFs of the log-Gaussian variables
\{Y(A_{j}):j=1,...,N\}
, as defined in Section
4.1
. The priors are given in Section
S4.11
.

S4.2
LG-t-SRE model

The LG-t-SRE model is specified as follows. The LG-t-SRE model uses the same data model as the LG-Gau-SRE model. The marginal distributions of the latent process are also set to be log-Gaussian, such that

Y(A_{j})\mid\beta_{0},\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{P}}\sim\mathrm{LG}(\beta_{0}-0.5\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{P}},\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{P}}),~j=1,...,N.

The marginal variables
\{Y(A_{j}):j=1,...,N\}
have been constructed to have
E(Y(A_{j})\mid\beta_{0},\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{P}})=\exp\{\beta_{0}\}
and
\mathrm{var}(Y(A_{j})\mid\beta_{0},\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}^{2})=\exp\{2\beta_{0}\}\times(\exp\{\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{P}}\}-1)
. The marginals are used in the t-SRE copula (
12
), which has the parameters
\theta_{s}
,
\theta_{r}
, and
\nu>2
. Hence, for
y_{1},...,y_{N}>0
,

\displaystyle[\mathbf{Y}\mid\beta_{0},\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{P}},\theta_{s},\theta_{r},\nu]

\displaystyle~~~~~~=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{N}f_{LG,j}(y_{j};\beta_{0},\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}^{2})\right)\times\mathfrak{t}^{\mathrm{SRE}}_{1:N}(F_{LG,1}(y_{1};\beta_{0},\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}^{2})),...,F_{LG,N}(y_{N};\beta_{0},\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}^{2}));\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}},\nu).

The priors are given in Section
S4.11
.

S4.3
SG-Gau-SRE model

The SG-Gau-SRE model is specified as follows. The SG-Gau-SRE model uses a Gaussian data model. Let
\sigma_{\mathrm{O}}^{2}
be a known constant measurement-error variance. Then, for
k=1,...,K
,
Z(A_{\mathrm{O}k})\mid Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k}),\sigma_{\mathrm{O}}^{2}\sim\mathrm{Gau}(Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k}),\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{O}})
, and the spatial data are assumed to be conditionally independent given the underlying latent-process values. Clearly,
E(Z(A_{\mathrm{O}k})\mid Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k}),\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{O}})=Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k})
for
k=1,...,K
.

For the process model, the marginal variables
\{Y(A_{j}):j=1,...,N\}
are assumed to follow skew-Gaussian (SG) distributions
(
Azzalini, 1985
)
. Let
Y(A_{j})\sim\mathrm{SG}(\psi,\omega,\lambda)
denote that the
j
-th latent process value,
j=1,...,N
, follows a skew-Gaussian distribution variable with location parameter
\psi\in\mathbb{R}
, scale parameter
\omega>0
, and shape/skewness parameter
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. The PDF of
Y(A_{j})
is given by,

\displaystyle f_{SG,j}(y_{j})=\frac{2}{\omega}\phi\left(\frac{y_{j}-\psi}{\omega}\right)\Phi\left(\frac{\lambda\times(y_{j}-\psi)}{\omega}\right);~y_{j}\in\mathbb{R}.

The subscript
SG
denotes that the PDF
f_{SG,j}
is for a skew-Gaussian distribution. The CDF of
Y
is denoted by
F_{SG,j}(y_{j}),~y_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
. The parameters relate to the mean and variance of
Y(A_{j})
as follows:

\displaystyle E(Y(A_{j})\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}})

\displaystyle=\psi+\omega\times\zeta\times\sqrt{2\pi^{-1}}

(S4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}(Y(A_{j})\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}})

\displaystyle=\omega^{2}\times\left(1-\frac{2\zeta^{2}}{\pi}\right),

(S5)

where
\zeta=\lambda/\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}
. Conversely, the skew-Gaussian parameters
\psi
,
\omega
, and
\lambda
can be expressed in terms of
E(Y(A_{j})\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}})
,
\mathrm{var}(Y(A_{j})\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}})
, and
-1<\zeta<1
, as follows:

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mathrm{var}(Y(A_{j})\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}})\Big/\left(1-\frac{2\zeta^{2}}{\pi}\right)}

(S6)

\displaystyle\psi(A_{j})

\displaystyle=E(Y(A_{j})\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}})-\sqrt{\mathrm{var}(Y(A_{j})\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}})\Big/\left(1-\frac{2\zeta^{2}}{\pi}\right)}\times\zeta\times\sqrt{2\pi^{-1}}

(S7)

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{\zeta}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^{2}}}.

(S8)

Therefore, for
j=1,...,N
,

[Y(A_{j})\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}]=f_{SG,j}(y_{j};\bm{\beta},\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{P}},\lambda),~y_{j}\in\mathbb{R},

(S9)

which we parameterize in terms of a log-linear model, with BAU-level marginal variance
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{P}}
, and skewness parameter
\lambda
, as follows:

\displaystyle E(Y(A_{j})\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}})

\displaystyle=\exp\{\mathbf{x}(A_{j})^{\top}\bm{\beta}\}=\psi(A_{j})+\omega\times\zeta\times\sqrt{2\pi^{-1}}

(S10)

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}(Y(A_{j})\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}})

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{P}}=\omega^{2}\times\left(1-\frac{2\zeta^{2}}{\pi}\right).

(S11)

Now
\psi(A_{j})
depends on the BAU,
A_{j}
, but
\omega
does not. The skew-Gaussian parameters
\psi(A_{j})
and
\omega
are uniquely determined by (
S6
)–(
S7
). For the simulation study, we do not have covariates, so the log-linear model is simply
\exp\{\beta_{0}\}
. The specification of the SG-Gau-SRE model is completed by using these marginals in the Gau-SRE copula (
6
). This means that the density for the process model can be written as,

[\mathbf{Y}\mid\beta_{0},\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{P}},\lambda,\theta_{s},\theta_{r}]=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{N}f_{SG,j}(y_{j})\right)\times\mathfrak{g}_{1:N}^{\mathrm{SRE}}(F_{SG,1}(y_{1}),...,F_{SG,N}(y_{N});\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}),

where it is understood that
F_{SG,j}
and
f_{SG,j}
,
j=1,...,N
, are functions of
\beta_{0}
,
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{P}}
, and
\lambda
, and
\bm{\Sigma}^{\mathrm{SRE}}
is a function of
\theta_{s}
and
\theta_{r}
. The priors for the parameters are given in Section
S4.11
.

S4.5
No-measurement-error (NME) versions of the copula-based models

This section briefly describes the no-measurement-error (NME) versions of the LG-Gau-SRE, SG-Gau-SRE, LG-t-SRE, and SG-t-SRE models presented in Section
4.1
of the main article.

In the NME versions of the models, it is assumed that the spatial process
Y(\cdot)
can be observed directly at the observed BAUs, and there is no distinction between the process and the data. Hence models without measurement errors can be viewed as special cases of the hierarchical models of Section
4.1
, where the measurement-error variance is zero. Consequently, the previously ‘latent’ spatial-process vector
\mathbf{Y}\equiv(\mathbf{Y}^{\top}_{\mathrm{O}},\mathbf{Y}^{\top}_{\mathrm{M}})^{\top}
has first sub-vector that is no longer latent; the vector
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}
denotes observed spatial data. However, the vector
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}
is latent in the sense that it denotes the remaining ‘missing’ values of the spatial process.

Adapting the MCMC algorithms for the LG-Gau-SRE, SG-Gau-SRE, LG-t-SRE, and SG-t-SRE models to their NME versions is straightforward. The MCMC samplers for the process-model parameters
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}
and the random effects
\bm{\eta}
(or
\bar{\bm{\eta}}
and scale factor
\gamma
for the t-SRE copula models) remain unchanged. However, there are some meaningful changes elsewhere in the MCMC algorithm. First, we do not need to sample
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}
since we treat these as being directly observed. Second, it is meaningless to ‘predict’
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}
conditional on itself. The ‘predictions’ at the observed BAUs are simply the observed values at those BAUs; hence they are excluded from any prediction-based comparison metrics (see Section
4.3
in the main article). Third, for the prediction of the ‘missing’ latent-process vector,
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}
, we generate predictions through the predictive distribution
[\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}]
. It is straightforward to generate samples from the predictive distribution by adapting results of Section
S3.1.4
or
S3.2.5
as appropriate.

S4.6
Fixed rank kriging (FRK)

Here, we give a brief description of fixed rank kriging (FRK) based on the Spatial Random Effects (SRE) model implemented in the FRK R package
(
Zammit-Mangion and Cressie, 2021
;
Sainsbury-Dale et al., 2024
)
. It is based on a (hierarchical) Spatial Generalized Linear Mixed Model
(
Diggle et al., 1998
;
Sengupta and Cressie, 2013
, SGLMM;)
as follows. The data model is the usual one that assumes conditional independence of the spatial data given the underlying latent-process values: Letting
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}
be a vector of known parameters for the data model, we write,

[\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}]=\prod_{k=1}^{K}[Z(A_{\mathrm{O}k})\mid Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k}),\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}].

Each term in the data model follows an exponential-family distribution (e.g., Bernoulli, Gamma, Gaussian, or Poisson). There may or may not be parameters in the data model. For example, the Poisson data model depends on
Y(\cdot)
but has no parameters, while the Gamma and Gaussian data models do. In the case of a Gaussian data model, each of
k=1,...,K
terms in the data model may be parameterized by a BAU-specific measurement-error variance,
\sigma_{\mathrm{O}}(A_{\mathrm{O}k})^{2}
, in which case we define
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{O}}\equiv(\sigma_{\mathrm{O}}(A_{\mathrm{O}1})^{2},...,\sigma_{\mathrm{O}}(A_{\mathrm{O}K})^{2})^{\top}
. The measurement-error variances may be the same at all observed BAUs, in which case
\sigma_{\mathrm{O}}(A_{\mathrm{O}1})^{2}=\dots=\sigma_{\mathrm{O}}(A_{\mathrm{O}K})^{2}=\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{O}}>0
.

The process model specifies the joint distribution of the latent spatial process
\mathbf{Y}
to be a trans-Gaussian spatial random effects (SRE) model. Let
\mathbf{S}(A_{j})
be a
b
-variate vector of spatial basis functions at BAU
A_{j}
, and let
\mathbf{S}
be an
N\times b
matrix of spatial basis functions, whose
j
-th row is given by
\mathbf{S}(A_{j})^{\top}
, with
j=1,...,N
. Define
\bm{\eta}
to be a
b
-variate vector of Gaussian spatial random effects with mean vector
\mathbf{0}
and covariance matrix
\mathbf{E}
. Also let
\xi(A_{j})
,
j=1,...,N
, be i.i.d Gaussian random variables, independent of
\bm{\eta}
, that have mean
0
and variance
\sigma^{2}_{\xi}
. Because FRK is not designed to work with general copula-based models, the parameterization of
\mathbf{E}
and
\sigma^{2}_{\xi}
is different to the parameterization discussed in Section
2.4
of the main article. Let
g(\cdot)
be a link function. The FRK R package implements common options including the identity function, the logarithmic function, the inverse function, and the complementary log-log function, among others. Now, define
\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}\equiv(g(Y(A_{1})),...,g(Y(A_{N})))^{\top}
, which we model as,

\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}=\bm{\mu}+\mathbf{S}\bm{\eta}+\bm{\xi},

where
\bm{\mu}
is mean vector, usually given by the linear model
\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}
, where
\mathbf{X}
is matrix of BAU-level covariates. We refer readers to
Sainsbury-Dale et al., 2024
for more details on Bayesian inference, including parameter estimation and spatial prediction, under this model.

S4.7
FRK for the LG-Gau-SRE and LG-t-SRE data

For the datasets simulated from the LG-Gau-SRE and LG-t-SRE models, we first log-transformed the data and then fitted a model with a Gaussian data model and Gaussian process model (i.e.,
g(\cdot)
is the identity function) with no covariates. In the data model, we set the log-scale variance to be
(\sigma_{\mathrm{O}}^{*})^{2}
(i.e., the true log-scale measurement-error variance, which was assumed known). After samples of the predictive distribution were obtained on the log-scale, they were back-transformed to the original scale (i.e., by exponentiation), and the mean of the back-transformed samples was taken as the value of the predictor. The default settings in the FRK R package were used to fit the model.

S4.8
FRK for the SG-Gau-SRE and SG-t-SRE data

For the datasets simulated from the SG-Gau-SRE and SG-t-SRE models, we simply used a Gaussian data model and Gaussian process model with no covariates in the FRK R package. The default settings in the FRK package were used to fit the model.

S4.10
Simulating data from a Gau-SRE copula model

Section
4.2
of the main article describes how data were simulated from the t-SRE copula model. The steps for simulating data from the Gau-SRE copula model are simpler, and they are as follows. Let
\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}^{*}
be a vector of all true parameter values. Then, for
r=1,...,R
, we simulated
\bm{\eta}^{(r)}\sim[\bm{\eta}\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}^{*}]
; then
\mathbf{Y}^{(r)}\sim[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{\eta}^{(r)},\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}}^{*}]
, where recall
\mathbf{Y}^{(r)}\equiv((\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}^{(r)})^{\top},(\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}^{(r)})^{\top})^{\top}
; and finally, the observed spatial data
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}^{(r)}\sim[\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}^{(r)},(\sigma^{*}_{\mathrm{O}})^{2}]
.

S4.12
Initial values

For
r=1,...,R
, let
\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}^{(0,r)}
,
\beta_{0}^{(0,r)}
,
\theta_{s}^{(0,r)}
,
\theta_{r}^{(0,r)}
, and
\nu^{(0,r)}
be the initial values, respectively, of the parameters
\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}
,
\beta_{0}
,
\theta_{s}
,
\theta_{r}
, and
\nu
in the MCMC algorithm for the
r
-th simulated dataset. These were set as follows. For the LG-Gau-SRE and LG-t-SRE models, let
\log(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}^{(r)})
denote the elementwise logarithm of the
r
-th spatial dataset, for
r=1,....,R
. Then, for the
r
-th dataset,
\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}^{(0,r)}
was set to the sample standard deviation of
\{\log(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}^{(r)}):r=1,...,R\}
(elementwise). Then,
\beta_{0}^{(0,r)}
was set to the sample mean of
\{\log(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}^{(r)}):r=1,...,R\}
. An empirical semivariogram was computed using
\log(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}})
detrended with a linear model involving the
x
and
y
coordinates of the data. The robust Cressie-Hawkins semivariogram estimator
(
Cressie and Hawkins, 1980
)
was used, and all variogram calculations were done using the R package gstat
(
Pebesma, 2004
)
. Subsequently, a spherical semivariogram model was fitted to the empirical semivariogram. If this failed, an exponential semivariogram model was fitted. If this failed, a wave semivariogram model was fitted. See
Cressie, 1993
for details about these models. The estimated range parameter from the fitted semivariogram model was used as
\theta_{r}^{(0,r)}
, and the ratio of the estimated partial sill to the estimated nugget effect was used as
\theta_{s}^{(0,r)}
. If the estimated nugget effect was zero,
\theta_{s}^{(0,r)}=1
was used instead. For the LG-t-SRE model,
\nu^{(0,r)}=10
was always used.

For
r=1,...,R
, let
\lambda^{(0,r)}
denote the initial value of the skewness parameter
\lambda
in the MCMC for the
r
-th dataset. For the SG-Gau-SRE and SG-t-SRE models, and for simulated datasets indexed by
r=1,...,R
,
\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}^{(0,r)}
was set to the sample standard deviation of
\{\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}^{(r)}:r=1,...,R\}
. For
\beta_{0}^{(0,r)}
, we used the sample mean of
\{\log(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}^{(r)}):r=1,...,R\}
. The log-scale data are used because, per (
S10
), we have
E(Y(A_{j})\mid\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{P}})=\exp\{\beta_{0}\}
. Therefore,
\exp\{\beta_{0}\}
is on the same scale as the spatial data
\{\mathbf{Z}^{(r)}:r=1,...,R\}
, and
\beta_{0}
is on the same scale as
\{\log(\mathbf{Z}^{(r)}):r=1,...,R\}
. The sample skewness of
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}^{(r)}
, calculated via the ‘moments’ R package
(
Komsta and Novomestky, 2022
)
, provided an initial value of
\lambda^{(0,r)}
. The same semivariogram-based procedure outlined above was used to initialize
\theta_{s}^{(0,r)}
and
\theta_{r}^{(0,r)}
. Again, for the SG-t-SRE model, we set
\nu^{(0,r)}=10
for all datasets.

S4.14
Additional results for the predictive performance of the LG-Gau-SRE and SG-Gau-SRE models

Here we show that, when data are simulated from the LG-Gau-SRE and SG-Gau-SRE models, the respective model accurately predicts the latent spatial process, achieves adequate coverage of the 90% prediction intervals, and outperforms the no-measurement-error versions of these models (NME LG-Gau-SRE and NME SG-Gau-SRE) and FRK.

Fig.

S6
shows the same for the SG-Gau-SRE model compared to the NME SG-Gau-SRE model, and FRK when the data are simulated from the SG-Gau-SRE model. The plots show that, when the missing BAUs are MAR, the SG-Gau-SRE and LG-Gau-SRE models show, at most, marginal improvements in terms of predictive ability compared to the reference models, namely their no-measurement-error counterparts and FRK. However, the LG-Gau-SRE and SG-Gau-SRE models decisively outperform the reference models when the missing BAUs are MBD. The latter case is a better test of the models’ predictive capabilities, since the former case (MAR) represents a scenario where accurate prediction of the process at the missing BAUs is easy since every missing BAU is virtually guaranteed to be close to several observed BAUs.

S4.15
Results for inference on the parameters

Recall the parameters of the models and their true values. The true values of the parameters were set as
\beta_{0}^{*}=\log(1000)
,
\lambda^{*}=-5
,
\theta_{s}^{*}=10
,
\theta_{r}^{*}=\sqrt{2}/4
, and
\nu^{*}=4
for all models. For the LG-Gau-SRE and LG-t-SRE models, we have
(\sigma^{*}_{\mathrm{P}})^{2}=(0.1)^{2}
. For the SG-Gau-SRE and SG-t-SRE models, we have
(\sigma^{*}_{\mathrm{P}})^{2}=(100)^{2}
.

For every combination of our models (LG-Gau-SRE, SG-Gau-SRE, LG-t-SRE, and SG-t-SRE) and each arrangement of the missing BAUs (MAR and MBD), we examined the sample mean and sample standard error (SE) over the
R=100
simulated datasets of the posterior means of the parameters. The bias of the estimates was also computed. For generic parameter
\theta
, and estimator
\hat{\theta}=E(\theta\mid\mathbf{Z})
, the bias is approximated as
\mathrm{Bias}(\hat{\theta})\approx R^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{R}(\hat{\theta}^{(r)}-\theta),
where
\hat{\theta}^{(r)}=E(\theta\mid\mathbf{Z}^{(r)})
from the
r
-th simulated dataset. Similarly, the sample SE is calculated as
\mathrm{SE}(\hat{\theta})\approx\sqrt{(R-1)^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{R}(\hat{\theta}^{(r)}-\bar{\theta})^{2}}
, where
\bar{\theta}=R^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\hat{\theta}^{(r)}
Finally, the proportion of datasets for which the 95% credible interval for each parameter contained the true value of the parameter was computed.

S4.16
Timing of computations

For the LG-Gau-SRE and SG-Gau-SRE models, for each configuration of missing BAUs (MAR and MBD), and for each of
r=1,...,100
simulated datasets, a total of 45,000 MCMC iterations (including burn-in) were run concurrently on Australia’s National Computing Infrastructure’s GADI high-performance computing cluster, using R version 4.2.2. Each set of 45,000 MCMC iterations took, on average, approximately 8 minutes for the LG-Gau-SRE model and 16-17 minutes for the SG-Gau-SRE model. The NME versions of the LG-Gau-SRE and SG-Gau-SRE models each took approximately 6-7 minutes and 11-12 minutes, respectively, for 45,000 MCMC iterations.

S5.4
Measurement errors of the XCH
4
retrievals

On any given day, let
\{\mathbf{s}_{i}\in D:i=1,...,n\}
denote the set of
n
spatial locations (reported by Sentinel 5P as a longitude-latitude coordinate pair) where XCH
4
has been retrieved. The retrievals are written as
\{Z(\mathbf{s}_{i}):i=1,...,n\}
. Individual measurement-error quantifications associated with the XCH
4
retrievals are provided at every location by the TROPOMI instrument onboard the Sentinel 5P satellite
(
Hu et al., 2016
)
. Hence write the retrieval-specific measurement-error standard deviations as
\{\sigma_{\mathrm{TROP}}(\mathbf{s}_{i}):i=1,...,n\}
(in ppb). The subscript ‘
\mathrm{TROP}
’ is intended to recall ‘TROPOMI’. The notation here is deliberately different from the ‘
\sigma_{\mathrm{O}}
’ used to denote the (BAU-level) measurement-error standard-deviation parameter in various data models described elsewhere in the main article and in this Supplement. The reasons for this are made clear in Section
S5.6
, where we describe the process of aggregating the point-level XCH
4
retrievals to the BAU-level.

Fig.
S10
plots
\{\sigma_{\mathrm{TROP}}(\mathbf{s}_{i}):i=1,...,n\}
against
\{Z(\mathbf{s}_{i}):i=1,...,n\}
. It reveals that the behavior of the measurement errors and their relationship to the magnitude of the underlying measurements vary by day. There are some days where there appears to be no relationship. On other days, the measurement error increases or decreases with the magnitude of the retrieval.

\sigma_{\mathrm{TROP}}(\cdot)
and magnitude of the XCH

retrieval, both in parts per billion (ppb), by day in August 2020, in and around the Bowen Basin in the state of Queensland, Australia.

S5.6
Defining BAU-level data and averaging retrievals within the BAUs

On any given day, the retrievals
\{Z(\mathbf{s}_{i}):i=1,...,n\}
may occur anywhere in the domain
D
that falls along the track of the Sentinel 5P satellite. Hence the retrieval locations
\{\mathbf{s}_{i}:i=1,...,n\}
do not form a regular grid, and they are not necessarily aligned with the BAUs shown in Fig.
S8
. Since our copula-based models (Sections
2.1
,
S4.1
–
S4.4
) are defined for BAU-level spatial data, we need to aggregate here the point-level retrievals
\{Z(\mathbf{s}_{i}):i=1,...,n\}
to produce a dataset of BAU-level observations,
\{Z(A_{\mathrm{O}k}):k=1,...,K\}
, where
K\leq n
and, obviously, each location
\{\mathbf{s}_{i}:i=1,...,n\}
falls into one and only one BAU.

For a given BAU,
A_{\mathrm{O}k}
(
k=1,...,K
), suppose that there are retrieval locations
\{\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{1},...,s^{\prime}_{n_{k}}\}
that fall into
A_{\mathrm{O}k}
. Then
A_{\mathrm{O}k}
contains the retrievals
\{Z(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{1}),...,Z(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{n_{k}})\}
with measurement-error standard deviations
\{\sigma_{\mathrm{TROP}}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{1}),...,\sigma_{\mathrm{TROP}}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{n_{k}})\}
, provided by the Sentinel 5P database. Assume all point-level retrievals are conditionally independent given
Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k})
, and let
\{[Z(\mathbf{s}_{h}^{\prime})\mid Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k})]:h=1,...,n_{k}]\}
denote the conditional distributions of the point-level retrievals given
Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k})
, with conditional mean
E(Z(s_{h}^{\prime})\mid Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k}))=Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k})
and conditional variance
\mathrm{var}(Z(s_{h}^{\prime})\mid Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k}))=\sigma_{\mathrm{TROP}}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{h})^{2}
, for
h=1,...,n_{k}
. Note that, unconditionally, the retrievals are spatially dependent.

For BAUs that contain at least one observation, we define the BAU-level observation as the average of the point-level retrievals: That is,

Z(A_{\mathrm{O}k})=\frac{1}{n_{k}}\left(Z(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{1})+\cdots+Z(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{n_{k}})\right).

The consequences of this averaging for the measurement-error standard deviations are as follows: Due to conditional independence, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}(Z(A_{\mathrm{O}k})\mid Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k}))

\displaystyle\equiv\mathrm{var}\!\left(\frac{1}{n_{k}}(Z(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{1})+\cdots+Z(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{n_{k}}))\middle|~Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{k}^{2}}\!\Big(\mathrm{var}\!\left(Z(\mathbf{s}_{1}^{\prime})\mid Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k})\right)+\cdots+\mathrm{var}\!\left(Z(\mathbf{s}_{n_{k}}^{\prime})\mid Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k})\right)\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{k}^{2}}\Big(\sigma_{\mathrm{TROP}}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{1})^{2}+\cdots+\sigma_{\mathrm{TROP}}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{n_{k}})^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\equiv\sigma_{\mathrm{O}}(A_{\mathrm{O}k})^{2}.

It follows that the BAU-level measurement-error standard deviations are given by,

\sigma_{\mathrm{O}}(A_{\mathrm{O}k})\equiv\frac{1}{n_{k}}\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{TROP}}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{1})^{2}+\cdots+\sigma_{\mathrm{TROP}}(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}_{n_{k}})^{2}}.

(S12)

Hence, we use
\sigma_{\mathrm{O}}(A_{\mathrm{O}k})
as the measurement-error standard deviation in the data model of the copula-based hierarchical spatial statistical model for XCH
4
(see next section). BAUs that contain no data define which part of the latent vector
\mathbf{Y}
are written as
\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{M}}
.

For the XCH
4
dataset analyzed in Section
5
, there are a small number of BAUs containing two retrievals, and no BAUs contain more than two retrievals. On 23 August (Day 23) and 28 August (Day 28), 2020, there are 33 and 31 BAUs containing two retrievals, respectively. The pairs of retrievals were averaged to define the BAU-level XCH
4
value, and the measurement-error standard deviations of the BAU-level XCH
4
values were calculated using (
S12
).

S5.7
The SG-Gau-SRE model for XCH
4

The SG-Gau-SRE model described in Section
S4.3
was used to analyze the XCH
4
data, with the following modifications. For the data model, we modelled
Z(A_{\mathrm{O}k})\mid Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k}),\sigma_{\mathrm{O}}(A_{\mathrm{O}k})^{2}\sim\mathrm{Gau}(Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k}),\sigma_{\mathrm{O}}(A_{\mathrm{O}k})^{2})
, and
[\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{O}}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\mathrm{O}},\{\sigma_{\mathrm{O}}(A_{\mathrm{O}k})^{2}:k=1,...,K\}]=\prod_{k=1}^{K}[Z(A_{\mathrm{O}k})\mid Y(A_{\mathrm{O}k}),\sigma_{\mathrm{O}}(A_{\mathrm{O}k})^{2}]
, where
\sigma_{\mathrm{O}}(A_{\mathrm{O}k})
is given by (
S12
) for all
k=1,...,K
. This differs slightly from the model described in Section
S4.3
in that the measurement-error variances are now allowed to differ for every observation. In the process model, the matrix of spatial basis functions
\mathbf{S}
is
7,370\times 101
, where there are
7,370
BAUs and
101
bisquare basis functions (Sections
S5.1
and
S5.5
). The values of the basis functions for the
j
-th BAU,
j=1,...,7,370
, were calculated by evaluating each spatial basis function at the centroid of the
j
-th BAU. The number of rows in
\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{O}}
and
\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{M}}
differed by day. Basis functions were used to capture the principal spatial variability, and no covariates were used.

S5.8
FRK for XCH
4

For fixed-rank kriging (FRK) of the XCH
4
dataset, the following SRE model was used. A Gaussian data model with observation-specific measurement-error variances described in Sections
S5.6
and
S5.7
was assumed, and a Gaussian process model with no covariates and a
7,370\times 101
matrix of spatial basis functions was used (Section
S5.7
). Note that our approach to averaging multiple observations within a BAU, as described in Section
S5.6
, differs from the default behavior of the R package FRK
(
Zammit-Mangion and Cressie, 2021
)
. Therefore, the averaging of pairs of retrievals within some BAUs was handled outside of the R package, but the model was otherwise fitted using the default arguments in the R package FRK
(
Sainsbury-Dale et al., 2024
)
.

S5.10
Initial values of the parameters in MCMC

Recall that the parameters of the SG-Gau-SRE model are
\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}
,
\beta_{0}
,
\lambda
,
\theta_{s}
, and
\theta_{r}
. Let
\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}^{(0,t)}
,
\beta_{0}^{(0,t)}
,
\lambda^{(0,t)}
,
\theta_{s}^{(0,t)}
, and
\theta_{r}^{(0,t)}
denote the initial values of the parameters in the MCMC algorithm for
t\in\{23,28\}
(i.e., Days 23 and 28). The initial values were set as follows: The initial value
\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}^{(0,t)}
was set to the standard deviation of the respective day’s XCH
4
values. The mean parameter,
\beta_{0}^{(0,t)}
, was set to be the mean of the logarithm of the respective day’s XCH
4
values. The reasons for using the logarithm of the data are the same as those articulated in Section
S4.12
. The initial value of the skewness parameter,
\lambda^{(0,t)}
, was set to the maximum likelihood estimate of the skewness parameter of a skew-Gaussian distribution fitted directly to the respective day’s XCH
4
values (assuming independence of the observations). The initial values
\theta_{s}^{(0,t)}
and
\theta_{r}^{(0,t)}
(corresponding to parameters
\theta_{s}
and
\theta_{r}
in the spherical covariance function for
\mathbf{E}(\theta_{s},\theta_{r})
; see Section
5.2
) were set equal to 1 and
0.5
, respectively.
