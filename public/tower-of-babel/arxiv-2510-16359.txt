Utilising Large Language Models for Generating Effective Counter Arguments to Anti-Vaccine Tweets
Utsav Dhanuka, Soham Poddar, Saptarshi Ghosh

Abstract
In an era where public health is increasingly influenced by information shared on social media, combatting vaccine skepticism and misinformation has become a critical societal goal. Misleading narratives around vaccination have spread widely, creating barriers to achieving high immunization rates and undermining trust in health recommendations. While efforts to detect misinformation have made significant progress, the generation of real-time counter-arguments tailored to debunk such claims remains an underexplored area. In this work, we explore the capabilities of LLMs to generate sound counter-argument rebuttals to vaccine misinformation. Building on prior research in misinformation debunking, we experiment with various prompting strategies and fine-tuning approaches to optimize counter-argument generation. Additionally, we train classifiers to categorize anti-vaccine tweets into multi-labeled categories—such as concerns about vaccine efficacy, side effects, and political influences—allowing for more context-aware rebuttals. Our evaluation, conducted through human judgment, LLM-based assessments, and automatic metrics, reveals strong alignment across these methods. Our findings demonstrate that integrating label descriptions and structured fine-tuning enhances counter-argument effectiveness, offering a promising approach for mitigating vaccine misinformation at scale.



1
Introduction

The rapid proliferation of social media platforms such as Twitter and Reddit has
significantly transformed the way information is disseminated and consumed. However,
this transformation has also facilitated the spread of misinformation, with
anti-vaccine rhetoric becoming a prominent example. Such misinformation can erode
public trust in vaccines, impede public health efforts, and contribute to the resurgence
of preventable diseases. Addressing these challenges requires innovative solutions
that go beyond manual moderation and fact-checking due to the sheer scale of
content generated daily.
The COVID-19 pandemic, which began in 2020, has underscored the critical importance
of mass vaccination as the most effective means to achieve herd immunity and
mitigate the virus’s impact. Despite the availability of safe and effective vaccines,
misinformation campaigns have fueled skepticism and resistance, endangering public
health initiatives worldwide. To counter this, there is an urgent need for automated
systems capable of responding to anti-vaccine claims with persuasive and factual
counter-arguments in real time.

While efforts to detect misinformation have made significant progress, the generation
of real-time counter-arguments tailored to debunk such claims remains an
underexplored area. Our works seeks to bridge that gap by focusing on the automatic
generation of counter-arguments for anti-vaccine tweets. By providing timely,
informative, and convincing rebuttals, we can reduce the spread of misinformation
and promote trust in vaccination efforts. Prior studies have explored the
theoretical underpinnings of countering anti-vaccination attitudes, highlighting both
cognitive mechanisms and dual-process theories to address resistance to vaccination
messages
(
Okuhara et al. 2020
;
Horne et al. 2015
)
. However, these works largely focus on the psychology of persuasion
and broad message framing rather than on the generation of targeted counterarguments
addressing specific user concerns. To the best of our knowledge, our work
is the first to systematically tackle the problem of generating fine-grained, concernspecific
counter-arguments for anti-vaccine content using large language models.

In this work we focus on developing a comprehensive approach to generate
counter-arguments for anti-vaccine tweets by leveraging recent advancements in
artificial intelligence and natural language processing. Key contributions of our work include:

•

First
, we establish an effective strategy for addressing anti-vaccine rhetoric through prompt engineering techniques applied to GPT-4o-Mini. To ensure the generated counter-arguments are both relevant and impactful, we employ a rigorous evaluation framework that incorporates human judgment, LLM-based assessments, and automatic metrics to assess dimensions such as factual accuracy, clarity, coverage, and persuasiveness.

•

Second
, we develop classifiers capable of generating label descriptions for anti-vaccine tweets, which are then used to prompt smaller language models (SLMs) for producing effective and context-aware rebuttals. We further enhance this process by designing a Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning approach, where the model is guided to first infer relevant concerns or themes before generating the counter-argument, leading to more coherent and targeted responses.

•

Finally
, we fine-tune small language models (SLMs) on high-quality counter-arguments generated by GPT-4o to enable them to operate independently. These models are evaluated using standard automatic metrics such as ROUGE and BERTScore. Our approach highlights the practicality of using SLMs, which offer a favorable trade-off between performance and computational efficiency, making them suitable for deployment in real-world, resource-constrained settings.

2
Related Work

In this section, we review prior research on counter-argument generation, highlighting its significance in online discourse and exploring how effective counter-arguments should be constructed. We also discuss related efforts in countering misinformation, hate speech, and other adversarial content using language models and prompting strategies.

Approaches to Counter-Narratives:

Recent work has shown that techniques developed for political argumentation and counterspeech can inform strategies to counter vaccine misinformation effectively.

Palmer and Spirling 2023
demonstrate that Large Language Models (LLMs) are capable of producing persuasive arguments on politically polarizing topics that are often as convincing as human-authored ones. However, they also find that human evaluators display bias against LLM-generated arguments when informed of their origin, highlighting the challenges of trust in AI-generated persuasion. Extending LLM capabilities to toxic online discourse,
Saha et al. 2022
propose COUNTERGEDI, a controllable generation framework that produces counterspeech with specific attributes such as politeness, emotional tone, and detoxification. Their model significantly improves the quality and acceptability of counterspeech, offering a promising avenue for generating nuanced responses to online hate, which is often closely intertwined with misinformation. Similarly,
Saha and Srihari 2024
propose a hybrid framework that adapts counter-hate strategies to misinformation domains, combining annotated response strategy classifiers with an argument-graph-guided LLM generator. Their approach demonstrates improvements in factual accuracy, naturalness, and persuasiveness.

Alshomary et al. 2021
proposed argument undermining, a novel approach that identifies weak premises in arguments and generates responses that directly challenge those specific points, demonstrating improved quality in generated counter-arguments.
Saha et al. 2024
investigated zero-shot counterspeech generation using models like GPT-2, DialoGPT, ChatGPT, and Flan-T5, highlighting the efficacy of strategic prompting and model scaling while raising concerns around toxicity. A complementary study by
Islam and Goldwasser 2024
introduced an LLMs-in-the-loop framework to uncover latent arguments from social media discourse, which supports dynamic argument discovery in evolving domains such as COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy and climate change.
These works collectively provide useful methods that inform our LLM-based counter-argument generation pipeline for addressing anti-vaccine discourse, ranging from premise analysis and dataset curation to prompting strategies and model-assisted argument extraction.

Studies on Vaccine-related Arguments:

Recent studies provide crucial insights into the psychological and communicative strategies needed to construct effective counter-arguments against vaccine misinformation.
Okuhara et al. 2020
emphasize that anti-vaccine rhetoric often appeals to fast, emotional reasoning (System 1), while pro-vaccine messages typically rely on slower, analytical thinking (System 2). They argue that persuasive messages should engage both systems—combining emotional appeal with factual evidence. Complementing this,
Germani and Biller-Andorno 2022
stress the importance of filling “information voids” where misinformation thrives, advocating for proactive public health messaging and increased digital literacy to build long-term resilience.
Horne et al. 2015
offer another important perspective, focusing on how the framing of counter-arguments influences their effectiveness. Their work reveals that direct myth-debunking strategies—such as presenting scientific facts that contradict anti-vaccine claims—often fail to change minds and may even backfire. Instead, they demonstrate that highlighting the real-world consequences of vaccine refusal, especially through moral and empathetic narratives (e.g., risks to children and communities), can significantly shift attitudes.
Inspired by these results, our work uses large language models (LLMs) to produce factually sound, emotionally compelling, and context-sensitive counterarguments. In order to align with these evidence-based strategies—which include emotional framing, relevance, and clarity to enhance the persuasive quality of automated counter-narratives—we plan to condition the models on descriptive labels that capture misinformation themes.

Studies on Vaccine misinformation:

Poddar et al. 2022b
introduced the CAVES dataset, a multi-label annotated collection of anti-vaccine tweets, offering rich explanations and summaries to facilitate explainable classification and summarization—laying the groundwork for targeted counter-argument tasks. Another work by
(
Poddar et al. 2022a
)
explored how vaccine-related stances on Twitter evolved over time, particularly across the pre-COVID and COVID periods. They developed a high-precision classifier (97%) to categorize users based on their vaccine stance, and investigated shifts in discourse and topical trends within and across different user groups. Notably, their work also identified users who changed stance during the pandemic and analyzed potential drivers behind such shifts—offering valuable sociolinguistic context to vaccine discourse.

(
Loomba et al. 2021
)
also showed how exposure to false information about the COVID-19 vaccine can drastically lower vaccination intent in the United Kingdom and the United States, highlighting the practical repercussions of such content and the pressing need for efficient counter-misinformation tactics like focused counterargument creation.
However, to the best of our knowledge, no prior work has tried to automatically generate counter arguments to vaccine misinformation.

We investigated two prompting strategies for generating counter argument to anti-vaccine tweets as described below.

(a) Without Label Information:

One method was to use standard prompts like “

The produced counterarguments were of good quality (as discussed later in this section), offering reasoned and cogent rebuttals that successfully countered the tweets’ anti-vaccine content.

These counter-arguments often lacked the contextual awareness and specificity needed to directly engage with and effectively refute the concerns expressed in the original tweets.

(b) With Label Information:

Numerous tweets addressed specific topics, including politics, pharmaceutical companies, side effects, and conspiracy theories.

4
Evaluation of the counter arguments

To ensure the counter-arguments by GPT-4o-mini are impactful and relevant, we employ a comprehensive evaluation framework, combining human judgment and LLM-based assessment.

4.1
Human Evaluation

In this subsection we explain our methodology to evaluate our generated arguments (by GPT-4o-mini) by humans through a survey.

4.2
Data Selection for Survey

For the survey, we sampled 100 data points from the dataset, each consisting of an anti-vaccine tweet paired with two corresponding counter-arguments generated by GPT-4o-mini: one without label descriptions and the other with label descriptions. Given the multi-label nature of the dataset, we employed stratified sampling to ensure balanced representation across all label categories. The final sample comprised 60 multi-labelled tweets and 40 single-labelled tweets.

4.3
Survey Details

To assess the effectiveness of counter-arguments generated by Large Language Models (LLMs) in addressing vaccine-related misinformation, we conducted a structured human evaluation survey. The study was hosted on the Prolific platform, which is widely used for collecting reliable and diverse human judgments. We recruited four participants—two who self-identified as vaccine-hesitant and two who were pro-vaccine—to ensure a balanced perspective. Participants were selected based on their consistently high approval ratings (99–100%) and strong track record on the platform, which ensured data quality and reliability. Each participant was fairly compensated at a rate consistent with Prolific’s ethical pay guidelines.

4.4
Survey Objective

The primary objective of this survey was to assess whether incorporating label descriptions—which highlight key concerns (e.g., vaccine safety, efficacy, political motives)—into prompts improves the quality of counter-arguments generated by LLMs in response to anti-vaccine tweets.

•

Prompt A

(Baseline)
: The LLM was prompted using only the anti-vaccine tweet.

•

Prompt B (Label Aware)
: The LLM was prompted with both the tweet and corresponding label descriptions that explain the main objections raised.

We conducted a two-phase user study to evaluate the impact of incorporating explicit contextual information—specifically, label descriptions—on the quality of counter-arguments generated by Large Language Models (LLMs). We first conducted a preliminary pilot study on 20 tweets to identify and resolve potential issues related to survey design, question framing, and interface usability. Following this, a main evaluation was carried out on 80 anti-vaccine tweets.

Each participant was presented with 100 tweets, each accompanied by the tweet text, a set of assigned labels (capturing key concerns), and their corresponding descriptions. For each tweet, participants were shown two counter-arguments: one generated using a baseline prompt (Prompt A) without label descriptions, and another generated using an enhanced prompt (Prompt B) that included the label descriptions. To mitigate positional bias, the order of the two responses was randomized.

Participants were asked to choose the counter-argument they found more effective in addressing the concerns raised by the tweet and to briefly justify their selection. Evaluation criteria included comprehensiveness (i.e., whether all key concerns were addressed), clarity, factual accuracy, and overall persuasiveness. To ensure traceability and accurate analysis, structured metadata was embedded in the Google Forms used for data collection. This metadata enabled us to de-randomize the responses during post-survey analysis and accurately attribute each counter-argument to its corresponding prompting strategy.

4.8
Motivation

In real-world social media environments, tweets containing misinformation rarely come with predefined labels or explanations of underlying concerns, making it challenging for automated systems to generate precise counter-arguments. Relying solely on such labels limits the practicality and scalability of counter-misinformation efforts. To address this, we introduce a two-step pipeline that mimics human reasoning: first, identifying the core concerns embedded in the tweet, and then generating a contextually grounded counter-argument. This approach not only eliminates dependency on manually annotated labels but also improves robustness in unseen scenarios. Furthermore, by incorporating Chain-of-Thought prompting, we encourage the model to explicitly reason through the content of the tweet before crafting a response. This structured reasoning improves interpretability and leads to more nuanced, targeted rebuttals—critical for effectively addressing complex and multi-faceted vaccine skepticism.

Generating Label Descriptions.

Following
(
Poddar et al. 2024
)

We instruction fine-tune these models on 7,000 anti-vaccine tweets paired with their corresponding label descriptions from the CAVES train split and evaluate them on 2,000 tweets from the test split.
We also instruction fine-tune the decoder-only models using the same setup.

Matching Label Descriptions.

Since the generated label descriptions may not exactly match the gold standard in the CAVES dataset, we adopt the mapping strategy proposed by
(
Poddar et al. 2024
)
.
We split each generated description into sentences (if multiple), encode both generated and ground-truth descriptions using a pre-trained Sentence-BERT encoder
(
Reimers and Gurevych 2019
)
, compute cosine similarity scores with all available ground-truth label descriptions, and assign the label corresponding to the highest similarity.
This ensures that the final assigned labels are semantically consistent and aligned with the CAVES taxonomy.

We fine-tune the encoder-only models (
RoBERTa-Large
,
Covid-Twitter-Bert

This enables them to predict label categories directly without explicit prompt-based instruction tuning.
These models can infer descriptive label phrases or raw identifiers, making them suitable for downstream tasks such as counter-argument generation when explicit label metadata is unavailable.

4.11
Knowledge Distillation and Model Training

Recent advancements in large language models (LLMs), such as GPT-4 and LLama, have demonstrated remarkable performance across a wide spectrum of natural language processing tasks. However, deploying these models in real-world applications particularly within resource constrained environments remains challenging due to their substantial computational and memory demands.

To mitigate these limitations, we adopt a knowledge distillation framework
(
Xu et al. 2024
)
, where in a compact
student
model is trained to emulate the behavior of a larger, pre-trained
teacher
model. This strategy allows us to preserve much of the performance of the larger model while significantly reducing inference latency and memory footprint, making the deployment of LLMs more practical and scalable in constrained settings.

Applying knowledge distillation to small language models (SLMs) such as Gemma, Phi-3, and LLaMA offers several practical advantages that enhance their real-world utility. It improves computational efficiency by enabling faster inference and reducing resource usage, which makes these models suitable for deployment in resource-constrained environments such as edge devices. The technique also supports task-specific adaptation by allowing models to be fine-tuned on domain relevant tasks using fewer resources. Through soft-label supervision and representation alignment, knowledge distillation helps smaller models retain much of the reasoning ability and accuracy of larger counterparts. This makes them capable of delivering strong performance without the high computational cost. It also enables scalability, making it feasible to deploy language models in settings that demand real-time responses or operate under limited hardware constraints, such as mobile platforms or IoT systems.
Thus, knowledge distillation bridges the gap between state-of-the-art model performance and practical deployment requirements, making it a highly effective strategy for enhancing the utility of models like Gemma and LLaMA.

1. ROUGE-2 Score.

ROUGE-2 evaluates the bigram overlap between generated and reference text:

\text{ROUGE-2}=\frac{\sum_{n=2}^{N}\text{count}_{\text{match}}(n)}{\sum_{n=2}^{N}\text{count}_{\text{reference}}(n)}.

2. ROUGE-L Score.

ROUGE-L measures the longest common subsequence (LCS)-based F-measure:

\text{ROUGE-L}=\frac{F_{1}}{F_{1}+\lambda\cdot\text{missed}},

where
F_{1}
is the F-measure and
\lambda
controls the importance of recall relative to precision.

3. BERT Precision.

BERT Precision computes the average cosine similarity between each token embedding in the generated text and its best match in the reference text:

\text{BERT Precision}=\frac{1}{|T|}\sum_{t\in T}\max_{r\in R}\text{CosineSim}(E_{t},E_{r}),

(1)

where
T
is the set of generated tokens and
R
is the set of reference tokens.

4. BERT Recall.

Similarly, BERT Recall measures how well the reference tokens are covered by the generated tokens:

\text{BERT Recall}=\frac{1}{|R|}\sum_{r\in R}\max_{t\in T}\text{CosineSim}(E_{r},E_{t}).

(2)

5. BERT F1.

Finally, BERT F1 is computed as the harmonic mean of BERT Precision and Recall:

\text{BERT F1}=2\times\frac{\text{BERT Precision}\times\text{BERT Recall}}{\text{BERT Precision}+\text{BERT Recall}}.

(3)

6
Conclusion

This research addresses the challenge of generating effective counter-arguments to anti-vaccine tweets using both Large Language Models (LLMs) and Small Language Models (SLMs). To facilitate this task, we first curated a high-quality dataset by prompting GPT-4o Turbo to generate counter-arguments for real-world anti-vaccine tweets, which were then evaluated and refined through human review for clarity, accuracy, and persuasiveness. This dataset served as the foundation for all subsequent experiments

We conducted three main experiments to assess how different prompt configurations affect the quality of generated counter-arguments. In Experiment 1, models were prompted with the raw tweet alone, resulting in lower coherence and weak performance across standard metrics. Experiment 2 added stance labels, which slightly improved contextual understanding. The most notable gains were observed in Experiment 3, where label descriptions were included in the prompt. This addition consistently led to higher scores in metrics, confirming that detailed contextual cues enable models to generate more precise and persuasive counter-arguments.

Further, we evaluated reasoning strategies by comparing Two-Step prompting and Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning on Gemma and LLaMA 3.2 3B models. Both strategies improved performance over the baseline, with the Two-Step approach slightly outperforming CoT in most metrics. However, neither surpassed the results of Experiment 3, reinforcing the effectiveness of descriptive prompting over pure reasoning techniques in this context.

Our research demonstrates that prompt engineering especially through the use of descriptive label information and reasoning strategies can significantly influence the quality of counter-arguments generated by LLMs and SLMs. It also highlights that smaller models, when guided effectively, can generate credible and coherent arguments, thereby offering a cost-effective alternative to larger models for specific tasks.

Overall, this research provides a foundational framework for enhancing automated argumentation, contributing to the broader goal of combating misinformation with informed, diverse, and accurate counterpoints.
