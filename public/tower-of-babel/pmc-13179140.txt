Crafty Tuberculous Pleurisy Presented With Hydropneumothorax in a 49‐Year‐Old Male: A Case Report
Authors: Marah Mansour, Tala Dakkak, Bassam Alwaraa, Rama Aboudan, Mohamad Salah Mahhouk, Mohammad Yousuf Hasan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 49‐year‐old male presented with acute dyspnea and hydropneumothorax. Imaging and biopsy confirmed pleural tuberculosis. Despite atypical features and no risk factors, anti‐tuberculosis therapy led to rapid improvement. This case highlights the diagnostic challenge of distinguishing tuberculosis‐related hydropneumothorax from necrotizing bacterial pneumonia and empyema in the presence of complex radiological findings.Keywords: case report, hydropneumothorax, pleura, tuberculosis

Introduction

When infection by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (TB) involves the pleura, it may result in tuberculous pleural effusion (TPE), characterized by the accumulation of inflammatory fluid within the pleural space [1]. Although its incidence varies geographically, TPE represents one of the most common forms of extrapulmonary tuberculosis [1]. Tuberculous pleurisy typically manifests with a nonproductive cough and pleuritic chest pain, while other common symptoms include fever, night sweats, weight loss, malaise, and dyspnea [2]. Hydropneumothorax, defined as the presence of both air and fluid in the pleural cavity, is an uncommon presentation of pleural tuberculosis and may create diagnostic challenges [3, 4].

In this report, we describe a case of pleural tuberculosis presenting with extensive hydropneumothorax and necrotizing pulmonary consolidation.

Case History

A 49‐year‐old male presented to the pulmonology department with an acute onset of dyspnea, fever, and mild left‐sided pleuritic chest pain. The patient reported a three‐month history of asthenia, anorexia, unintentional weight loss, and intermittent night sweats. There was no known risk factor for tuberculosis such as diabetes mellitus, immunosuppressive conditions, or prior tuberculosis. The patient denied tobacco or alcohol use and reported no regular medication use.

Methods

The vital signs were stable except for hypoxemia with oxygen saturation (SpO2) of 89% on room air. Laboratory tests showed elevated neutrophil count and C‐reactive protein (CRP) (Table 1). Physical examination revealed dyspnea and respiratory distress, dullness to percussion over the lower left hemithorax, a tympanitic percussion note on the upper left hemithorax, and diminished breath sounds on the left side. CXR revealed left‐sided hydropneumothorax (Figure 1A). The patient developed worsening dyspnea. A chest tube was inserted, draining approximately 3 Liters of serosanguinous pleural fluid, resulting in lung re‐expansion (Figure 1B). No clear evidence of bronchopleural fistula was identified. The drained pleural fluid was serosanguinous and not frankly purulent, arguing against classical empyema. Cytological analysis did not demonstrate malignant cells. Pleural fluid analysis revealed neutrophils 53%, lymphocytes 47%, glucose 118 mg/dL, protein 3.9 g/dL, and LDH 1327 U/L (Table 2). Additional microbiological investigations for tuberculosis, including pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA), Xpert MTB/RIF assay, and mycobacterial culture, were not available at the time of evaluation. Sputum samples and bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage were not performed due to the patient's clinical condition and the presence of a large hydropneumothorax requiring urgent management. Dyspnea improved, and SpO2 increased to 94%. A CT scan demonstrated apical pleural thickening, loculated pleural effusion, and parenchymal consolidation predominantly involving the left lower lobe, with air bronchograms and areas suggestive of necrotizing inflammation (Figures 2 and 3). Persistent pneumothorax was noted despite chest tube drainage, along with marked pleural thickening. The parietal pleura measured more than 1 cm in thickness (Figure 4). Because pleural fluid cultures for common bacteria were negative, and necrotizing parenchymal changes were noted on imaging, further diagnostic evaluation was performed. Given the presence of hydropneumothorax and necrotizing parenchymal changes on imaging, several differential diagnoses were considered, including bacterial necrotizing pneumonia with bronchopleural fistula and empyema with air leakage. However, pleural fluid culture for common bacteria was negative, and the radiological findings, together with persistent pneumothorax, raised suspicion of an alternative etiology. Therefore, further diagnostic evaluation was pursued. A CT‐guided pleural biopsy was performed. Histopathological examination revealed chronic granulomatous inflammation with caseous necrosis, findings consistent with tuberculous pleuritis. Cytological examination did not reveal malignant cells. The diagnosis was therefore established by CT‐guided pleural biopsy demonstrating histopathological features consistent with tuberculous pleuritis. The chest tube remained in place until adequate drainage was achieved and radiological improvement was observed. Follow‐up imaging demonstrated satisfactory lung re‐expansion without evidence of non‐expandable lung. A standard anti‐tuberculous therapy was initiated, consisting of isoniazid (5 mg/kg), rifampicin (10 mg/kg), pyrazinamide (25 mg/kg), and ethambutol (20 mg/kg) for 2 months, followed by isoniazid (5 mg/kg) and rifampicin (10 mg/kg) for 4 months. Antibiotics were not initiated as there was no evidence of bacterial infection.

Conclusion and Results

Clinical improvement was observed after 3 weeks of anti‐tuberculous therapy (Figure 5A,B). Fever and night sweats resolved, and appetite improved. A follow‐up CT scan performed after 6 weeks of treatment demonstrated radiological improvement (Figure 6). After 18 weeks of treatment, the patient showed further clinical and radiological improvement on CXR (Figure 5C).

Discussion

Extrapulmonary tuberculosis occurs in approximately 25% of patients, with pleural tuberculosis representing one of the most frequent forms in adults [1, 2, 5, 6]. TB is one of the most common causes of pleural exudates globally [1, 7]. Primary pleural tuberculosis typically arises from the rupture of subpleural caseous foci into the pleural space [2].

In some patients, pleural tuberculosis may coexist with pulmonary tuberculosis when a subpleural caseous lesion ruptures into the pleural cavity. This mechanism can result in simultaneous pleural effusion, pneumothorax, and parenchymal necrotizing changes. This mechanism likely explains the clinical presentation in our patient. The CT findings demonstrated necrotizing parenchymal consolidation associated with hydropneumothorax, suggesting pleuropulmonary tuberculosis rather than isolated pleural involvement. Rupture of a subpleural caseous focus may allow both air and inflammatory fluid to enter the pleural space, leading to hydropneumothorax and empyema‐like features. Such combined pleural and pulmonary involvement has been described in advanced tuberculous disease [5, 6].

Although hydropneumothorax is a recognized complication of tuberculosis, extensive hydropneumothorax associated with necrotizing pulmonary consolidation can closely mimic bacterial necrotizing pneumonia and empyema, creating significant diagnostic challenges [3, 4]. In the present case, hydropneumothorax was extensive and led to significant respiratory compromise, necessitating urgent drainage. The differential of hydropneumothorax includes thoracic trauma, necrotizing bacterial pneumonia, bronchopleural fistula, and malignancy [3]. Patients with pleural tuberculosis commonly present with fever, pleuritic chest pain, cough, and constitutional symptoms such as weight loss and night sweats [1, 5]. Our patient reported constitutional symptoms, including fatigue, weight loss, and intermittent night sweats for 3 months before presentation. These constitutional symptoms raised suspicion for underlying pulmonary tuberculosis in addition to pleural involvement, particularly in the presence of necrotizing parenchymal consolidation on CT imaging.

Chest CT plays an important role in identifying pleural thickening, loculated effusions, and associated parenchymal involvement in tuberculous pleurisy complications [2, 5]. In our case, CT imaging demonstrated early complex radiological findings, including necrotizing parenchymal consolidation and hydropneumothorax, which suggested advanced pleuropulmonary involvement. CT imaging demonstrated parenchymal consolidation with necrotizing features, which raised suspicion for necrotizing pneumonia. However, the absence of frank purulent pleural drainage, negative bacterial cultures, and the histopathological confirmation of tuberculosis supported tuberculous pleuro‐pulmonary involvement rather than bacterial necrotizing pneumonia. The absence of frank purulent pleural drainage and the subsequent clinical response to anti‐tuberculous therapy supported tuberculosis as the underlying etiology rather than bacterial necrotizing pneumonia.

Pleural fibrosis is a known complication of tuberculous pleurisy, and residual pleural thickening greater than 10 mm may lead to persistent chest pain and impaired pulmonary function. Pneumothorax associated with tuberculosis often reflects severe pulmonary involvement, including the formation of a bronchopleural fistula [6].

The necrotizing parenchymal consolidation observed in our patient further supported the presence of active pulmonary involvement accompanying pleural tuberculosis. Hydropneumothorax in tuberculosis may occur due to rupture of a subpleural caseous focus or the formation of a bronchopleural fistula, allowing air to enter the pleural space.

In our patient, CT imaging demonstrated necrotizing parenchymal consolidation predominantly in the left lower lobe associated with hydropneumothorax (Figures 2 and 3). These radiological findings created a diagnostic challenge, as necrotizing bacterial pneumonia with bronchopleural fistula and empyema was initially considered among the main differential diagnoses.

Tuberculous pleural effusions typically present as exudative fluid with elevated protein and lactate dehydrogenase levels [8], as presented in our patient (Table 2). Although markedly elevated LDH levels may suggest empyema, tuberculous pleural effusion may also demonstrate high LDH values due to intense pleural inflammation and cellular breakdown [1, 5, 8]. The serosanguinous appearance of the pleural fluid in our patient, together with the absence of frank pus and negative bacterial culture, argued against classical bacterial empyema despite the elevated LDH level. The differential diagnosis of exudative pleural effusion includes malignancy, pulmonary embolism, and infectious causes such as bacterial pneumonia [9]. Although tuberculous pleural effusion usually demonstrates lymphocyte predominance, early disease may present with neutrophil‐predominant pleural fluid. Pleural fluid glucose levels in tuberculous effusion are generally normal or mildly reduced [8]. In our patient, pleural fluid analysis demonstrated neutrophil predominance with relatively preserved glucose levels (Table 2). The definitive diagnosis of tuberculous pleurisy relies on microbiological or histopathological confirmation from sputum, pleural fluid, or pleural biopsy specimens [1, 6]. In our case, pleural fluid culture was negative, and the diagnosis was ultimately established by CT‐guided pleural biopsy demonstrating tuberculous pleuritis. Microbiological confirmation was not available, and the diagnosis relied on histopathological findings from the pleural biopsy.

Pleural tuberculosis is treated using the same standard anti‐tuberculosis regimen recommended for pulmonary tuberculosis. Standard therapy consists of a two‐month intensive phase with isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol, followed by a continuation phase with isoniazid and rifampicin. Therapeutic drainage may be required in patients with large effusions causing significant dyspnea [1, 5]. In our patient, severe dyspnea and hypoxemia necessitated chest tube drainage, with approximately three liters of pleural fluid evacuated.

Early drainage was required to relieve respiratory compromise and to prevent further complications associated with large hydropneumothorax.

Anti‐tuberculosis therapy was subsequently initiated. Although corticosteroids may accelerate symptom resolution in selected patients, their use remains controversial and lacks official recommendations for inclusion in the treatment of tuberculous pleurisy [6]. Therefore, corticosteroids were not used in this case. The main goals of treatment are symptom relief, prevention of active disease progression, and avoidance of complications such as fibrothorax [1]. Clinical improvement was observed after initiation of anti‐tuberculosis therapy. Despite drainage, complete lung re‐expansion was limited by marked pleural thickening. An 18‐week follow‐up revealed continued clinical improvement. Follow‐up chest radiography showed a gradual reduction in pleural thickening and progressive lung re‐expansion (Figure 5C).

This report has several limitations: microbiological confirmation from sputum or pleural fluid cultures for Mycobacterium tuberculosis was not available. Consequently, drug susceptibility testing could not be performed. In addition, some diagnostic parameters, such as serum LDH, were unavailable, which limited the formal application of Light's criteria.

This case highlights several diagnostic challenges. The presence of hydropneumothorax, together with necrotizing parenchymal changes, initially raised suspicion for bacterial necrotizing pneumonia or empyema with possible bronchopleural fistula. However, negative bacterial cultures and the absence of purulent pleural fluid prompted further investigation. In the absence of confirmatory microbiological tests, CT‐guided pleural biopsy played a crucial role in establishing the diagnosis of tuberculous pleuritis. Early chest tube drainage combined with prompt anti‐tuberculous therapy resulted in significant clinical and radiological improvement.

In conclusion, pleural tuberculosis may present with unusual manifestations such as hydropneumothorax, emphasizing the need for heightened clinical suspicion in endemic regions. This case highlights that tuberculosis may rarely present with hydropneumothorax associated with necrotizing parenchymal involvement, which can mimic other infectious or malignant conditions. Awareness of this atypical presentation may facilitate earlier diagnosis and help differentiate tuberculosis from other causes of hydropneumothorax.
