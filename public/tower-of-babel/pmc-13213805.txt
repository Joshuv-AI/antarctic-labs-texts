Synergistic role of naringin and pectin in microwave-assisted green synthesis of stable silver nanoparticles with potent antibacterial activity
Authors: Jinyuan Cai, Yongjin Sun, Shuyun Liang, Hock Eng Khoo, Jianyi Wang, Mian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The objective of this study was to develop a green, microwave-assisted method for synthesizing stable silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) with potent antibacterial activity, using naringin and pectin as dual reducing and stabilizing agents. Four AgNP formulations were prepared with different naringin-to-pectin ratios: N-AgNPs (naringin only), P-AgNPs (pectin only), NP-AgNPs1 (3:1 ratio), and NP-AgNPs2 (1:3 ratio). NP-AgNPs1 displayed the largest TEM particle size but the lowest polydispersity index, the highest thermal stability, and the highest Ag+ release. Antibacterial assays demonstrated that NP-AgNPs1 exhibited the strongest activity against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus. Mechanistic studies revealed that NP-AgNPs1 induced significant reactive oxygen species generation, protein leakage, reducing sugar leakage, and lipid peroxidation, indicating that the antibacterial mechanism involves oxidative stress-mediated membrane damage. In summary, the synergistic combination of naringin and pectin at a 3:1 ratio facilitates the green synthesis of stable, antibacterial AgNPs, providing a promising platform for food packaging and biomedical applications.

Introduction

In recent years, the development of nanotechnology has led to the widespread application of metal nanoparticles in various fields, including biomedicine, food packaging, and catalytic materials (Chen et al., 2024; Trotta et al., 2024; Wang, Xuan, et al., 2024). Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) are defined as particles with a diameter of less than 100 nm (Nie et al., 2023). AgNPs have been demonstrated to exhibit outstanding performance, boasting unique surface enhancement properties and specialized physical and chemical properties (Chandak & Nagime, 2025). The substance exhibits not only heat resistance and drug resistance, but also antibacterial properties and broad-spectrum bactericidal activity (Khatoon et al., 2017; Velidandi et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023). The synthesis of AgNPs encompasses physical, chemical, and biological (green) approaches. Each of these approaches offers distinct advantages and limitations, which are based on application demands and environmental considerations (Lakshmanan et al., 2021). Chemical reduction remains one of the most prevalent methods, where silver salts (e.g., AgNO3) are reduced using agents such as sodium borohydride or trisodium citrate (Beyene et al., 2017). This approach has been demonstrated to yield high-quality results and allow relative control over the size of the resulting particles. However, it is crucial to note that this method often involves the use of toxic reagents and the generation of hazardous by-products, which can raise environmental and safety concerns (Vreeland et al., 2015). The evolution of green chemistry has prompted scientists to devise novel methodologies to minimize the release of hazardous waste into the environment (Chen et al., 2020). It is imperative to identify eco-friendly methodologies for synthesizing silver nanoparticles.

Biosynthesis is a green synthesis method (Li et al., 2020), primarily utilizing microorganisms, fungi, and plant extracts for the synthesis of AgNPs (Shah et al., 2021). The synthesis effect is influenced by multiple factors, including the type of plant extract, reaction pH, reaction temperature, and Ag+ concentration (Restrepo & Villa, 2021). Green synthesis is a biologically harmless and inexpensive method for preparing nanoparticles using plant extracts (Hano & Abbasi, 2022). The process is characterized by its simplicity, the absence of any significant environmental impact, the generation of substantial amounts, and the utilization of widely available raw materials. These attributes contribute to its high feasibility for industrial implementation (Ying et al., 2022). The Teucrium parvifolium extract acts as a reducing agent and stabilizer in the green synthesis of silver nanoparticles, with a particle size of approximately 14 nm. This synthesis exhibits a notable antibacterial effect (Soltani et al., 2024). The walnut green shell acidic polysaccharide WGHP2 was utilized as a reducing agent, dispersant, and stabilizer to successfully synthesize silver nanoparticles (WGHP2-AgNPs). These nanoparticles have been shown to possess notable efficacy in inhibiting bacterial proliferation, specifically that of Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) at a concentration of 50 μg/mL (Wang, Yang, et al., 2024). The microwave-assisted method is a sustainable and environmentally friendly approach that utilizes a microwave extraction instrument (Albuquerque et al., 2021; Li et al., 2025). The method offers several advantages, including ease of operation, high yield, and rapid reaction kinetics. Its versatility extends to a range of chemical synthesis research endeavors and large-scale industrial production (Manklinniam et al., 2025; Rehman et al., 2025; Strapasson et al., 2021). The biosynthesis method can be integrated with microwave radiation techniques to improve the synthesis efficiency of AgNPs.

Naringin, a flavonoid compound found, has been found in the peels and flesh of pomelo, grapefruit, and lime fruits. Previous studies have demonstrated its capacity to manifest a variety of biological functions, including antioxidative properties, anti-inflammatory effects, tumor suppression, and enhancement of glucose and lipid metabolism (Alam et al., 2020; Bai et al., 2020). The presence of flavonoids, alkaloids, terpenes, and tannins plays a significant role in the bioremediation of ions. Furthermore, their amalgamation with silver nanoparticles has been shown to yield a synergistic effect against pathogenic microorganisms (Mutha et al., 2021; Ullah et al., 2020). Pectin, a natural polysaccharide polymer, has been demonstrated to be an excellent biological macromolecular material for synthesizing metal nanoparticles. The substance exhibits low toxicity, favorable ecological compatibility, high biodegradability, and the capacity to readily form solid materials (Roman-Benn et al., 2023). Consequently, naringin and pectin have been shown to function as reducing agents and stabilizers during the green synthesis of AgNPs. It is imperative to investigate the potential of naringin and pectin as reducing agents and stabilizers in the green synthesis of silver nanoparticles. The process conditions for the microwave-assisted synthesis of silver nanoparticles should be optimized. The structural and other physicochemical characteristics of the synthesized silver nanoparticles suggest their potential applications in various fields, including their use as a functional material in food processing and medical supplies.

It is noteworthy that the silver nanoparticles exhibit significant antibacterial properties. Consequently, this study proposes a novel methodology for the environmentally friendly synthesis of silver nanoparticles, employing naringin and pectin as essential constituents. The microwave-assisted green synthesis method of AgNPs from these natural compounds exhibits several advantages, including cost-effectiveness, environmental sustainability, and the absence of toxic chemicals. This work establishes a foundation for the promotion and application of biosynthetic AgNPs, offering novel perspectives on the potential applications of NP-AgNPs1 and NP-AgNPs2 in food packaging and biomedicine.

Materials and methods

Materials

Naringin was procured from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Pectin was procured from Shanghai Yien Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The silver nitrate was obtained from Xilong Science Co., Ltd. (Guangdong, China). The strains of E. coli and S.aureus were obtained from the laboratory.

Exploration of green synthesis process of silver nanoparticles

The preparation method of AgNPs was based on the published literature (Zhao et al., 2022). A 0.2-g mixture of naringin and pectin, with a ratio of 3:1, was weighed. The substance was dissolved in 100 mL of distilled water. Subsequently, 100 mg of NaOH was added and evenly dispersed (final concentration 25 mM). Subsequently, 2 mL of silver nitrate solutions with specific concentrations (0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 1.0, and 1.3 mol/L) were added separately. The experiment was conducted under specific conditions of microwave power (250, 410, 570, 730, and 890 W) and microwave temperature (30, 40, 50, 60, and 70 °C). The reaction was executed for designated durations (1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 min) employing a microwave extraction instrument (UWave-2000, China). After the reaction process, a 10-min period was allocated for the reaction solution to stand. The reaction solution was dissolved in 49 mL of distilled water. The UV-2600 Ultraviolet Spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Osaka, Japan) was utilized to perform spectral scanning within the range from 300 to 600 nm. The maximum absorption value of the silver nanoparticles solution at 410 nm was observed, and the optimal preparation process of AgNPs was explored.

The synthesis of AgNPs was optimized to ensure the most effective process. A precise quantity of 0.2 g of a mixture containing naringin and pectin was weighed, with the ratios of naringin to pectin being 1:0, 0:1, 3:1, and 1:3, respectively. The reaction solution was subjected to a centrifugal process at 4000 rpm for 30 min. Subsequently, the upper layer, or superior portion of the mixture, was discarded, and 100 mL of distilled water was added. Substances that had not undergone reaction were removed through a series of repetitive centrifugation procedures. Subsequently, the precipitate was subjected to drying in an oven at 60 °C for 5 h, resulting in the formation of AgNPs powder. The silver nanoparticles prepared with different naringin-to-pectin ratios were designated as follows: N-AgNPs (naringin-only silver nanoparticles, 1:0), P-AgNPs (pectin-only silver nanoparticles, 0:1), NP-AgNPs1 (naringin:pectin = 3:1 silver nanoparticles), and NP-AgNPs2 (naringin:pectin = 1:3 silver nanoparticles).

Structural characterization of AgNPs

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis

The Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopic analysis was conducted in accordance with the method previously documented in the existing literature (Wang et al., 2025). In summary, a 1 mg freeze-dried sample was meticulously combined with 100 mg of potassium bromide, pulverized, and subsequently compressed into a disk. The absorption spectra of the compounds were obtained using an FTIR spectrometer (Iraflinty-1S, Shimadzu Corporation, Osaka, Japan). The wavelengths were set at a range from 4000 to 400 cm−1.

Scanning electron microscopic (SEM) and transmission electron microscopic (TEM) analyses

Before observing the microstructures of the AgNP samples using a scanning electron microscope (SEM), a gold coating was applied through sputtering (Singh et al., 2025). A SU5000 field emission SEM (Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was employed to observe the structural morphology of the silver nanoparticle samples at 20 °C. The acceleration voltage was set to 5 kV, and the magnification was 30,000 × .

The silver nanoparticle samples were subsequently observed under a JEM-2000EXII Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM, JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The measurement of the particle size of the AgNP samples was conducted using a lightweight image analysis tool, “Nano Measurer”.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurement

The hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index (PDI) of the AgNP samples were measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Panalytical, UK). The samples were diluted to 0.1 mg/mL with deionized water and sonicated for 5 min before measurement. All measurements were performed at 25 °C with a scattering angle of 173°. Each sample was measured in triplicate (n = 3), and data are presented as mean ± SD.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) and size analysis based on Zeta potential

The silver nanoparticle samples were analyzed using a D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer (Bruker Inc., Karlsruhe, Germany) operating at 40 kV and 40 mA with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5420 Å). XRD data were collected over a 2θ range of 5.00° to 50.00°, employing sequential scanning with a step width of 0.02° and a scanning speed of 10°/min (Sun et al., 2022).

The silver nanoparticle samples were dissolved in 2 mL of deionized water for particle size measurement using a Zeta potential analyzer (Zetasizer Nano ZS, Malvern Instruments Ltd., Worcestershire, UK) (Shah et al., 2019).

Thermogravimetric (TGA) analysis

The thermal stability of the silver nanoparticle samples was determined using a thermogravimetric analyzer (SDT Q600 TA Instrument, USA). In summary, a 10 mg sample was weighed in the crucible and inserted into the furnace chamber of the thermogravimetric analyzer. The TGA was conducted at a combusting temperature of 400 °C, with an initial temperature of 25 °C at ambient temperature. The heating rate was 20 °C /min. High-purity argon (99%) was used as both protection gas (20 mL/min) and reaction gas (50 mL/min). This phenomenon was expressed as a weight loss rate.

Stability analysis of silver nanoparticles solution

The AgNPs solution was serially diluted 100-fold and stored in a light-proof environment at 4 °C. The absorbance value of the AgNPs diluted solution was measured at 410 nm using a UV-2600 Ultraviolet Spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Osaka, Japan). The stability of the silver nanoparticles in solution was analyzed by measuring the shift in absorbance.

Preparation of bacterial suspension

The Lennox broth (LB) liquid medium was sterilized at 121 °C for 20 min. E. coli and S. aureus were inoculated, and the activated bacterial solution was agitated in an oscillating incubator at 37 °C for 18–24 h.

Determination of the diameter of the bacteriostatic zone

The 6-mm filter paper discs were immersed in AgNP solutions of varying concentrations (0.2, 0.4, 0.8, 1.6, and 3.2 mg/mL) for 24 h. Thereafter, the agar medium was prepared, sterilized, and subsequently poured into plates to solidify. Subsequently, 0.1 mL of diluted solutions of E. coli and S. aureus were separately taken, and the bacteria were evenly spread using a sterile swab. After a 10-min interval, the 6-mm filter paper discs saturated with AgNPs solutions were positioned on the surface of the medium. Subsequently, the plates were then inverted and subjected to an incubation temperature of 37 °C for 12 h. The antibacterial activities of the silver nanoparticle samples were evaluated based on the measurement of the diameters of the inhibition zones using a vernier caliper (Li et al., 2022). The same experiments were conducted using naringin and pectin, and the influence of the natural production itself on the inhibition zone was excluded. Negative controls were prepared using sterile distilled water. Tests were performed under the same conditions to exclude any potential antibacterial effects from the solvent.

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of AgNPs

The MIC of each AgNP sample against E. coli and S. aureus was determined using the broth microdilution method according to the CLSI guideline. Briefly, bacterial suspensions were adjusted to 1 × 106 CFU/mL in Mueller-Hinton broth. The AgNP powder was dispersed in LB broth to prepare an AgNP solution at a specific concentration. Subsequently, a mixture comprising 0.2 mL of LB broth, 0.05 mL of the AgNPs broth, and 0.05 mL of the bacterial suspension should be prepared and subsequently added to a 96-well plate. The experimental design involved measuring the absorbance values at 600 nm at 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, and 24 h, respectively (Ma, Guo, et al., 2022). MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of AgNPs that completely inhibited visible bacterial growth, which corresponded to an OD600 value ≤0.05.

Reactive oxygen species detection

The generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) was measured and verified using a 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate assay (Mutalik et al., 2020). The excitation wavelength was set at 488 nm, and the emission wavelength was scanned at 525 nm. E. coli and S. aureus were treated with N-AgNPs, P-AgNPs, NP-AgNPs1, and NP-AgNPs2 at a concentration of 100 μg/mL. The samples were incubated at 37 °C for 0 h and 12 h, respectively. Subsequently, the samples were subjected to a centrifugal process at a temperature of 4 °C and a rotational speed of 3000 rpm for 15 min. Following centrifugation, 2 mL of the supernatant was transferred to a sealed culture tube. Subsequently, 3 mL of the diluted DCFH-DA reagent (the DCFH-DA reagent was prepared in LB broth at a ratio of 1:1000, v/v) was added and thoroughly mixed. The mixture was then incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged at 3000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min. The fluorescence intensity of the resulting supernatant was measured at 0 h and 12 h using an F96S Fluorescence Spectrometer (Lengguang Instruments, Shanghai, China).

Determination of protein leakage

The protein content was determined using the Bradford method (Bradford, 1976). Specifically, 3 mL of N-AgNPs, P-AgNPs, NP-AgNPs1, and NP-AgNPs2 solutions, each with a concentration of 3.2 mg/mL, were measured separately. Subsequently, 12 mL of sterile water and 3 mL of bacterial suspensions of E. coli and S. aureus were added. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of the solution was subjected to a centrifugal process at 4000 rpm for 30 min. A 20-μL sample was then collected and permitted to stand for 5 min. The baseline absorbances of these samples were measured at 600 nm using a K6600-A Microplate Reader (Kaiao Technology, Beijing, China). The five sampled centrifuge tubes were subjected to a thorough and uniform shaking process before being placed in a constant-temperature incubator shaker maintained at 37 °C for 12 h. After this, the samples were removed, and the absorbances of E. coli and S. aureus treated with AgNPs were measured, in accordance with the aforementioned protocol.

Determination of released reducing sugars

The leakage of reducing sugar in E. coli and S. aureus under stress by AgNPs was determined by the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method. A 3-mL solution of N-AgNPs, P-AgNPs, NP-AgNPs1, and NP-AgNPs2 at a concentration of 3.2 mg/mL was prepared for each sample, and 12 mL of sterile water and three mL of bacterial suspensions of E. coli and S. aureus were added. A quantity of 200 μL of AgNPs was deposited into a 96-well plate. The measurement of the absorbances of E. coli and S. aureus under AgNP samples subjected to stress for 0 h was conducted at a wavelength of 540 nm. This procedure is to be carried out using a microplate reader. After culturing in centrifuge tubes for 12 h, the aforementioned steps were repeated to measure the absorbance for 12 h (Liu et al., 2025).

Determination of malondialdehyde

The concentration of malondialdehyde (MDA) was measured to assess the degree of bacterial lipid peroxidation. The intracellular MDA levels were detected using the cell pellets of E. coli and S. aureus, according to the manufacturer's instructions from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Quantification of Ag+ release by ICP-MS

The release of silver ions (Ag+) from the four AgNP samples (N-AgNPs, P-AgNPs, NP-AgNPs1, and NP-AgNPs2) was quantified using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). Each sample was dispersed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) at a concentration of 100 μg/mL and incubated at 37 °C in the dark. At predetermined time points (1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h), 1 mL aliquots were collected and immediately centrifuged through 3 kDa ultrafiltration tubes (Amicon Ultra, Millipore) at 10,000 g for 30 min at 4 °C to separate free Ag+ from AgNPs. The filtrates were collected, acidified with 2% HNO₃, and analyzed using an ICP-MS instrument (Agilent 7900, USA). Silver standard solutions (0–100 μg/L) were used for calibration. All measurements were performed in triplicate (n = 3), and data are expressed as mean ± SD.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in three independent replicates (n = 3). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 27 software, and figures were prepared using Origin 2024 software. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Duncan's multiple range test. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels are indicated in the figures as *P < 0.05 and **P < 0.01.

Results and discussion

Analysis of the preparation process of silver nanoparticles

Ultraviolet spectroscopy scanning is a pivotal method for confirming the synthesis of silver nanoparticles. This is due to the fact that surface plasmon resonance occurs subsequent to the synthesis of silver nanoparticles, causing an absorption peak in the silver nanoparticles sample near 410 nm (Jing et al., 2021). Conventionally, the higher the intensity of the ultraviolet spectrum, the greater the efficiency with which AgNPs are synthesized. Consequently, peak intensity levels can be utilized to ascertain the optimal AgNP synthesis process.

As illustrated in Fig. 1A, with an increase in microwave temperature, the absorbance value of the AgNPs solution initially rises, followed by a subsequent decline. It was observed that the microwave temperature of 60 °C resulted in the highest absorbance value. However, when the microwave temperature rose to 70 °C, the opposite effect was observed, with a decrease in the absorbance value. This phenomenon can be attributed to the instability of the properties of naringin and pectin caused by high temperatures (Casagrande & de Lima, 2019).Fig. 1The influence of different reaction conditions on the absorbance of silver nanoparticles at a 410 nm wavelength. (A) Microwave temperature, (B) microwave power, (C) silver nitrate concentration, and (D) microwave time.Fig. 1

The influence of different reaction conditions on the absorbance of silver nanoparticles at a 410 nm wavelength. (A) Microwave temperature, (B) microwave power, (C) silver nitrate concentration, and (D) microwave time.

Research has demonstrated that elevated temperatures can modify the interaction between biomolecules and the silver surface, thereby impeding the aggregation of nanoparticles in solution. This effect is unfavorable for the formation of silver nanoparticles (Lin et al., 2010). The influence of microwave power on the synthesis of silver nanoparticles is demonstrated in Fig. 1B. The maximum value was attained at an input microwave power of 410 W, and it underwent a decrease as the microwave power rose to 570 W. This phenomenon may be attributed to the fact that an excessively high microwave power accelerates the intermolecular forces, resulting in the aggregation of the generated nanoparticles (Hosny et al., 2024).

As demonstrated in Fig. 1C, with an increase in the concentration of silver nitrate, the absorption peak of the AgNPs solution exhibits an initial increase, followed by a subsequent decrease. When the concentration of AgNO3 is 1.0 mol/L, the maximum absorption peak appears at 410 nm. When the concentration of AgNO3 is 1.3 mol/L, the absorption peak decreases at 410 nm. It has been posited that the elevated concentration of AgNO3 may result in the accumulation of unreacted Ag+ on the surface of AgNPs, leading to a reduction reaction. This, in turn, may cause the agglomeration of AgNPs particles. Research has demonstrated a direct correlation between the initial concentration of silver nitrate and the size of the resulting silver nanoparticles (Nguyen et al., 2023).

As demonstrated in Fig. 1D, microwave time exerts a negligible influence on the absorption peak of silver nanoparticles. The maximum values of the absorbances at microwave-assisted synthesis times of 4 min and 1 min, respectively, achieved through microwave-assisted synthesis, were not significantly different (P > 0.05). After a thorough review of the relevant economic benefits and existing research that has been conducted on the subject, it is concluded that the synthesis should be conducted at a microwave time of 1 min. This has been identified as the optimal synthesis time. The findings of the single-factor experiment demonstrate that the process conditions for microwave-assisted green synthesis of AgNPs are as follows: microwave temperature of 60 °C, a microwave power of 410 W, a silver nitrate concentration of 1.0 mol/L, and microwave time of 1 min.

In the current study, the primary objective was not to maximize synthesis efficiency, but to obtain stable, well-characterized naringin-pectin silver nanoparticles with consistent physicochemical properties for antibacterial evaluation. Nevertheless, we recognize the value of RSM for future investigations. Follow-up studies will employ a central composite design or Box-Behnken design to systematically evaluate the interactive effects of synthesis parameters (temperature, microwave power, AgNO₃ concentration, and reaction time) on nanoparticle properties such as particle size, PDI, yield, and antibacterial activity. Such an approach would further optimize the synthesis protocol and may reveal nonlinear relationships and parameter interactions that single-factor experiments cannot detect. We have noted this as an important direction for future work.

To elucidate the roles of functional groups in the reduction and stabilization of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs), this study comparatively analyzed the FTIR spectral characteristics of naringin, pectin, and their derived AgNPs. The results showed that naringin exhibited characteristic absorption peaks at 3418 cm−1 (–OH stretching vibration), 1635 cm−1 (CC framework vibration), and 1050 cm−1 (C—O stretching vibration). Pectin displayed typical signals at 3428 cm−1 (–OH stretching vibration), 1625 cm−1 (asymmetric stretching vibration of carboxyl groups), and 1099 cm−1 (C–O–C stretching vibration). Compared with the raw materials, the –OH characteristic peaks of N-AgNPs, P-AgNPs, NP-AgNPs1, and NP-AgNPs2 all showed significant redshifts (e.g., P-AgNPs shifted to 3458 cm−1). The peak positions in the 1600–1650 cm−1 region corresponding to C=C/C=O also shifted (e.g., the 1606 cm−1 peak of P-AgNPs), and the signal intensity of the C—O bond in the 1050–1100 cm−1 region changed markedly. These results indicate that the hydroxyl and conjugated double-bond functional groups in naringin and pectin not only participated in the reduction of Ag+ to Ag0 as electron donors but also contributed to the surface modification and stable dispersion of AgNPs through coordination or hydrogen bonding interactions with the nanoparticle surface.

TEM analysis of AgNP samples

TEM has been demonstrated to provide the most accurate and high-resolution imaging information about the size, shape, morphology, state of aggregation, and distribution of nanoparticles at nanometer resolution (Lin et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2016). The results of transmission electron microscopy are shown in Fig. 3E-H, further confirming the hypothesis that the silver nanoparticles are spherical in shape. However, no discernible particle agglomeration was observed during TEM observation, and the particle distribution was uniform. This phenomenon may be attributed to the fact that the particle size of silver nanoparticles using microwave-assisted synthesis is typically in the range of 20 to 50 nm (Cai et al., 2019). Research has demonstrated that the plant components of green synthetic silver nanoparticles can adsorb onto the surface of AgNPs, thereby reducing their surface activity. Furthermore, these components acted as stabilizers, preventing the agglomeration of silver nanoparticles (Wang et al., 2015).

Energy-dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) analysis of AgNP samples

EDS analysis is a rapid quantitative and qualitative technique for detecting the elemental composition of nanocomposites (Shah et al., 2020). As demonstrated in Fig. 3I-L, the EDS of silver nanoparticles exhibits a uniform distribution of silver elements, accompanied by trace amounts of carbon (C) and oxygen (O) elements. The normalized masses of Ag, C, and O elements in N-AgNPs were 86.97%, 9.29%, and 1.56%, respectively. The normalized masses of Ag, C, and O elements of P-AgNPs were 86.97%, 4.40%, and 1.00%, respectively. The normalized masses of Ag, C, and O elements in NP-AgNPs1 were 83.65%, 4.55%, and 1.78%, respectively. The normalized masses of Ag, C, and O elements in NP-AgNPs2 were 86.77%, 2.17%, and 0.60%, respectively. In addition, the Ag element in the silver nanoparticles synthesized from naringin and pectin in varying proportions consistently exceeded 80%.

Particle size analysis of AgNP samples

As demonstrated in Fig. 4, the particle size of silver nanoparticles is analyzed. The mean diameter of the N-AgNPs was 19.59 nm, with a minimum diameter of 7.34 nm and a maximum diameter of 38.89 nm. The mean value of P-AgNPs was determined to be 21.42 nm, with a minimum value of 7.18 nm, and a maximum value of 38.43 nm. The mean value of NP-AgNPs1 was 27.06 nm, the minimum value was 10.15 nm, and the maximum value was 44.00 nm. The mean value of NP-AgNPs2 was 20.51 nm, with a minimum value of 7.07 nm. This finding suggests that the particle size of AgNPs is concentrated within the range of 10–35 nm. A comparison of the mean particle size values revealed the following sequence: N-AgNPs < NP-AgNPs2 < P-AgNPs < NP-AgNPs1. A comparison of NP-AgNP1 and NP-AgNPs2 revealed a direct correlation between the amount of naringin added and the resulting change in the average particle size. The polydispersity index (PDI) of the four AgNP samples was measured by DLS. The experimental results are shown in Table S1. NP-AgNPs1 exhibited the lowest PDI (0.186 ± 0.021), indicating the narrowest size distribution and highest uniformity. XRD analysis of AgNP samples

The crystal structure of AgNPs was analyzed using an XRD analyzer. The results of this study are presented in Fig. 4E-H. The analysis revealed the presence of sharp absorption peaks at the 2θ values of N-AgNPs, P-AgNPs, NP-AgNPs1, and NP-AgNPs2 at approximately 38°, 44°, 64°, 77°, and 81°, respectively. A comparison of the standard data of Ag (JCPDS, Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards, NO:04–0783) with the reflections corresponding to the crystal planes of (111), (200), (220), (311), and (222) indicated that the green-synthesized AgNPs have a face-centered cubic structure. Furthermore, the intensity of the diffraction peaks was relatively large, suggesting that the prepared silver nanoparticles have good crystallinity and a complete structure (Binsalah et al., 2022; Ma, Jiang, et al., 2022). The diffraction characteristic peaks of the Ag element confirmed that the microwave-assisted green synthesis method for silver nanoparticles is feasible (Kuang et al., 2023).

The average crystallite sizes were 15.5 nm (N-AgNPs), 18.0 nm (P-AgNPs), 25.5 nm (NP-AgNPs1), and 19.0 nm (NP-AgNPs2). These values are consistent with the particle sizes observed by TEM. The crystallite sizes of N-AgNPs (15.5 nm) and P-AgNPs (18.0 nm) were smaller than their respective TEM particle sizes (19.59 nm and 21.42 nm), indicating that these nanoparticles are polycrystalline in nature. In contrast, the crystallite sizes of NP-AgNPs1 (25.5 nm) and NP-AgNPs2 (19.0 nm) were close to their TEM particle sizes (27.06 nm and 20.51 nm), suggesting a near-single-crystalline structure with high uniformity.

The relatively high Zeta potential value of silver nanoparticles indicates their high stability. Stability analysis of AgNP solutions

Based on the change in the absorbance value of the silver nanoparticle solution at 410 nm, the stability of silver nanoparticles in the solution was detected. The initial values of N-AgNPs, P-AgNPs, NP-AgNPs1, and NP-AgNPs2 were recorded as approximately 0.883. On the 14th day, they all showed different degrees of changes, namely 0.855, 0.835, 0.894, and 0.868. The degree of fluctuation of the absorbance values of N-AgNPs, P-AgNPs, NP-AgNPs1, and NP-AgNPs2 was recorded as 0.028, 0.048, 0.011, and 0.015, respectively. This finding indicates that the stability order in the solution is: P-AgNPs < N-AgNPs < NP-AgNPs2 < NP-AgNPs1. Therefore, NP-AgNPs1 exhibits extremely high stability in the nanoparticle solution.

Zeta potential measures the electrostatic repulsion force between particles under highly diluted conditions, which indicates the potential for colloidal stability. In contrast, the time-dependent absorbance change reflects the actual sedimentation and aggregation behavior in the working solution, which is governed by a combination of factors, including particle size, size distribution, degree of initial agglomeration, and density, in addition to surface charge.

In this study, although N-AgNPs exhibited the highest Zeta potential (−41.7 mV), suggesting strong electrostatic repulsion, SEM imaging (Fig. 3A) revealed that N-AgNPs were heavily agglomerated even before the stability test. These pre-existing large aggregates, despite carrying a high surface charge, are subject to rapid gravitational sedimentation, leading to a more pronounced decrease in absorbance over time. Conversely, NP-AgNPs1, although having a moderately lower Zeta potential (−38.8 mV), displayed uniform particles with minimal agglomeration (Fig. 3C). Their superior initial dispersion and smaller hydrodynamic size resulted in slower sedimentation and hence the smallest fluctuation in absorbance over 14 days.

Thermal stability analysis of AgNP samples

The thermal stability of the silver nanoparticles was determined by thermogravimetry, and the results are shown in Fig. 5E. At a temperature of 100 °C, the observed mass loss was primarily attributed to the release of water molecules that were weakly bound to the surface (Akl et al., 2025). As the temperature increased to 200 °C, a substantial decrease in mass was observed. At a temperature of 400 °C, the mass loss of N-AgNPs-1 was approximately 6%, and the sample exhibited notable thermal stability. The XRD analysis shows that NP-AgNPs1 exhibit characteristic peaks at approximately 38°, 44°, 64°, 77°, and 81°, indicating that the greenly synthesized silver nanoparticles (NP-AgNPs1) have a face-centered cubic structure. The EDS analysis indicates that the main elements of NP-AgNPs1 are Ag, along with small amounts of C and O. The high residual mass suggests that the organic components (naringin and pectin) undergo carbonization during heating, forming a protective carbon shell that suppresses the volatilization of Ag. This thermal protection is consistent with the excellent colloidal stability of NP-AgNPs1 observed in solution (Fig. 5F). The mass percentages of N-AgNPs, P-AgNPs, NP-AgNPs1, and NP-AgNPs2 at 400 °C were 82.65%, 82.88%, 93.97%, and 84.45%, respectively (Gopalakrishnan et al., 2023).

Analysis of the antibacterial mechanism of silver nanoparticles samples

Determination of ROS level

The results of the ROS detection process are presented in Fig. 7A and E. At 0 h, the fluorescence intensity of the CK group and the E. coli and S. aureus treated with silver nanoparticles was approximately 100. At 12 h, the fluorescence intensity of the silver nanoparticles group increased significantly, indicating that the ROS content in the bacteria increased and that the bacteria were under oxidative stress conditions. A statistically significant disparity was observed between N-AgNPs and P-AgNPs; however, a significant discrepancy was identified among the other silver nanoparticle samples (P < 0.05). The fluorescence intensity of NP-AgNPs1 was found to be the most pronounced. In instances where bacterial cells are unable to eliminate the excessive ROS that accumulates within them, an occurrence known as oxidative stress ensues. Oxidative stress is regarded as one of the important antibacterial mechanisms of AgNPs. ROS has a strong oxidative potential and induces severe damage to nucleic acids, lipids, and proteins (Li et al., 2012). The overproduction of ROS triggered by nanoparticles has been shown to result in the degradation of biomolecular and organelle structures. This process has been observed to induce protein oxidative carbonylation, lipid peroxidation, DNA/RNA cleavage, and membrane structural destruction. Determination of protein leakage

Proteins are considered to be pivotal biological macromolecules that are present in the cell membranes and cytoplasm of bacteria. These macromolecules play a substantial role in life activities. Damage to the cell membrane can result in the leakage of nucleic acids and proteins. Consequently, the extent of nucleic acid and protein leakage can serve as a marker of the cell membrane's integrity (Zyoud et al., 2019). As demonstrated in Fig. 7B and F, after the administration of silver nanoparticles, the protein concentrations in the bacterial liquid increased to varying degrees at 12 h. The protein concentration was the highest after treatment with NP-AgNPs1. The substantial buildup of intracellular ROS resulting from AgNP exposure has been demonstrated to induce oxidative damage to proteins, compromise total bacterial proteins, and expedite or augment apoptosis.

Determination of released reducing sugars

To verify that an excessive accumulation of ROS leads to damage to the cell membrane of E. coli, resulting in the leakage of reducing sugars. As demonstrated in Fig. 7C and G, upon detecting the bacterial liquid treated with silver nanoparticles at 0 h, the content of reducing sugar was found to be approximately 5 μg/mL, exhibiting a comparable level to that observed in the silver nanoparticles group and the control group. At 12 h, the content of reducing sugar in the blank group increased to 6.33 ± 0.32 μg/mL. The leakage of reducing sugar in E. coli treated with N-AgNPs, P-AgNPs, NP-AgNPs1, and NP-AgNPs2 was 7.5 μg/mL, 8.5, 10.07, and 9.37 μg/mL, respectively. The leakage amounts of reducing sugars from S. aureus treated with N-AgNPs, P-AgNPs, NP-AgNPs1, and NP-AgNPs were 7.92, 8.86, 10.36, and 9.32 μg/mL, respectively. A statistically significant variation in the content of reducing sugar was observed among the various silver nanoparticle treatments (P < 0.05). The content of reducing sugar in the bacterial liquid treated with NP-AgNPs1 and NP-AgNPs2 was the greatest, indicating that the oxidative stress caused by the increase of ROS led to the leakage of reducing sugar. AgNP has been demonstrated to expedite the leakage of reducing sugars from the cytoplasm by means of damage and destruction of the cell membrane (Wang et al., 2018).

Lipid peroxidation determination

MDA is the final product of lipid peroxidation, and its content can serve as a metric for evaluating the extent of lipid peroxidation and cell membrane damage in bacteria (Wang et al., 2023). The results are displayed in Fig. 7D and H. At 12 h post-treatment, the MDA content of E. coli and S. aureus in the silver nanoparticles group exhibited a certain degree of increase compared with the content at 0 h. In the blank group, N-AgNPs, P-AgNPs, NP-AgNPs1, and NP-AgNPs2 increased by 96.12%, 177.29%, 322.33%, 804.64%, and 529.41%, respectively. The MDA content of S. aureus treated with the silver nanoparticles group showed an increase compared with that at 0 h, with values of 41.766%, 166.08%, 404.10%, 737.20%, and 582.18%, respectively. As the duration of AgNPs treatment increased, a gradual increase in the MDA content was observed. The underlying cause of this phenomenon is the occurrence of oxidative stress within the bacterial cells. This results in an increase in intracellular ROS, which, in turn, leads to an increase in lipid metabolites and, consequently, an increase in MDA content.

Silver ion (Ag+) release from AgNPs

To investigate whether Ag+ release contributes to the antibacterial activity of the synthesized AgNPs, we quantified the time-dependent release of Ag+ from N-AgNPs, P-AgNPs, NP-AgNPs1, and NP-AgNPs2 using ICP-MS. The samples were incubated in PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C for 24 h, and the concentration of free Ag+ in the filtrate (separated by 3 kDa ultrafiltration) was measured at 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h. As shown in Fig. 8, NP-AgNPs1 exhibited the highest cumulative Ag+ release, reaching 8.5 ± 0.60 μg/mL after 24 h. This was significantly higher than that of NP-AgNPs2 (4.0 ± 0.35 μg/mL), N-AgNPs (1.0 ± 0.08 μg/mL), and P-AgNPs (0.8 ± 0.07 μg/mL). For NP-AgNPs1, the release of Ag+ was rapid during the first 6 h, accounting for approximately 76% of the total 24 h release (6.5 μg/mL at 6 h vs. 8.5 μg/mL at 24 h), followed by a slower sustained release phase. This initial burst is typical for AgNPs and is attributed to the rapid oxidation of surface silver atoms. Importantly, the 24 h Ag+ release showed a strong positive correlation with the antibacterial activity of the samples. The sample with the highest Ag+ release (NP-AgNPs1) also exhibited the largest inhibition zones and the lowest MIC values, while the samples with lower Ag+ release (N-AgNPs and P-AgNPs) showed negligible or moderate antibacterial activity. These results demonstrate that Ag+ release is a major contributor to the antibacterial activity of the synthesized AgNPs, particularly for NP-AgNPs1. Potential applications in food packaging

The potent antibacterial activity, good colloidal stability, and green synthesis route of the naringin-pectin silver nanoparticles (particularly NP-AgNPs1) make them promising candidates for food packaging applications. In this section, we discuss the feasibility, challenges, and future research directions for translating our findings into practical applications.

Food spoilage caused by bacteria is a major concern in the food industry. It leads to economic losses and health risks. Incorporating antibacterial nanoparticles into food packaging materials can extend shelf life and improve food safety. NP-AgNPs1 exhibit several properties that are desirable for food packaging.

NP-AgNPs1 effectively inhibited both E. coli and S. aureus, with MIC values of 50 μg/mL and 6.25 μg/mL, respectively. The monodisperse nature (PDI = 0.186) and long-term stability (minimal absorbance change over 14 days) of NP-AgNPs1 ensure consistent antibacterial performance during storage and use. The use of naringin and pectin as reducing and stabilizing agents eliminates the use of potentially harmful chemicals, making the nanoparticles more acceptable for food-contact applications compared to chemically synthesized AgNPs. Previous studies have successfully incorporated AgNPs into biodegradable polymers such as chitosan, cellulose, and polylactic acid (PLA) to produce antibacterial films or coatings. Based on these reports, our NP-AgNPs1 could be similarly embedded into edible films or coatings for applications such as fruit and vegetable preservation, meat packaging, or dairy product containers.

To translate our findings into practical applications, we propose the following future studies: food preservation trials to evaluate NP-AgNPs-incorporated films or coatings in real food products by monitoring bacterial counts and sensory properties; and foodborne pathogen testing to assess antibacterial activity against a broader panel of pathogens. In summary, NP-AgNPs1 exhibit strong antibacterial activity, good stability, and a green synthesis route, making them promising candidates for food packaging applications. However, significant challenges remain, including cytotoxicity issues. Addressing these challenges through the proposed future studies will help bridge the gap between in vitro findings and practical applications. We believe that with further development, naringin-pectin stabilized silver nanoparticles could contribute to safer food preservation and more effective antibacterial therapies.

Conclusion

In this study, a novel microwave-assisted green synthesis method was successfully developed for the preparation of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) using naringin and pectin as reducing and stabilizing agents. Four formulations—N-AgNPs, P-AgNPs, NP-AgNPs1, and NP-AgNPs2—were prepared and comprehensively characterized. The optimal synthesis conditions were determined as follows: a microwave temperature of 60 °C, power of 410 W, AgNO₃ concentration of 1.0 mol/L, and reaction time of 1 min. While the single-factor approach provided reliable working conditions for producing reproducible AgNPs, future optimization using response surface methodology (RSM) is recommended to capture parameter interactions.

Structural characterization confirmed that all AgNPs were spherical with a face-centered cubic crystal structure. Among the four formulations, NP-AgNPs1 (naringin-to-pectin ratio of 3:1) exhibited the largest TEM particle size (27.06 nm) but the lowest polydispersity index (0.186), the highest thermal stability (93.97% mass retention at 400 °C), and the best long-term solution stability (smallest absorbance fluctuation of 0.011 over 14 days). Antibacterial assays demonstrated that NP-AgNPs1 possessed the strongest activity against both Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus, with inhibition zones of 1.118 cm and 1.550 cm at 3.2 mg/mL, and MIC values of 50 μg/mL and 6.25 μg/mL, respectively, confirming a synergistic antibacterial effect between naringin and pectin at the optimal ratio.

Mechanistic studies revealed that NP-AgNPs1 induces excessive ROS generation, leading to oxidative stress, which subsequently causes membrane damage, protein leakage, reducing sugar leakage, and increased malondialdehyde content. Additionally, NP-AgNPs1 released the highest amount of Ag+ (8.5 μg/mL over 24 h). The green synthesis route, excellent stability, and potent antibacterial activity of NP-AgNPs1 make them promising candidates for food packaging (e.g., antibacterial films or coatings). However, challenges such as cytotoxicity, regulatory compliance, real-environment stability, scalability, and potential bacterial resistance remain to be addressed in future studies. Collectively, this work provides a theoretical and experimental foundation for the development of next-generation silver-based antibacterial materials.
