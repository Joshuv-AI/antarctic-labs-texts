Abstract
. For many common height functions, the explicit determination of the essential minimum is an open problem. We consider a classical method to obtain lower bounds that goes back at least to C.J. Smyth, and a method to obtain upper bounds based on the knowledge of the limit distribution of integral points. We use an infinite dimensional linear programming scheme to show that that both methods agree in the limit, by showing that the principle of strong duality holds in our situation. As a corollary we prove that the essential minimum can be attained by sequences of algebraic integers. Recent results by A. Smith and B. Orloski–N. Sardari, furnish a characterization of compactly supported measures that can be approximated by complete sets of conjugates of algebraic integers, in terms of infinitely many nonnegativity conditions. We establish an extension of this characterization to measures with non necessarily compact support. As an application of this result and of strong duality, we show that the essential minimum is a computable real number when the Green function used to define the height is computable. We systematically use potential theory for measures that can integrate functions with logarithmic growth.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
The essential minimum

For many common height functions, the explicit determination of the
essential minimum
(see (
1.3
)) is an open problem. We consider a classical method to obtain lower
bounds, that goes back at least to C.J. Smyth, and a method to obtain
upper bounds based on the knowledge of the limit distribution of
integral points used in

[
BGMRL18
]
. In this
work we use an infinite dimensional linear programming scheme to show
that both methods agree in the limit, by showing that the principle of strong duality holds in our situation. This allows us to prove that the essential minimum
can be attained by sequences of algebraic integers.

We also establish a characterization, in terms of infinitely many
nonnegativity conditions, of probability measures on
\mathbb{C}
with non
necessarily compact support that can be approximated, in a suitable
sense, by sequences of complete sets of conjugates of distinct
algebraic integers. This characterization, in the case of measures
with compact support, was previously established by A. Smith and
B. Orloski–N. Sardari. As an application of this result and of strong duality, we
show that the essential minimum is a computable real number when the Green function used to define the height is computable. These issues can be considered on any variety
X

defined over a number field, but in this work we will focus in the
cases
X=\mathbb{P}^{1}
or
X=\mathbb{A}^{1}
.

We proceed to state our results precisely. We denote by

\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
the set of all algebraic numbers. Given
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, we denote by
P_{\alpha}(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
a primitive
irreducible polynomial having
\alpha
as a root. The polynomial

P_{\alpha}
is determined by
\alpha
up to sign. Its set of roots

O(\alpha)
is called the
complete set of conjugates
of

\alpha
.

We say that
\mathrm{g}\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a
Green function
, if it is continuous, invariant under complex conjugation and obeys the asymptotic

(1.1)

\mathrm{g}(z)=\log|z|+o(\log|z|),\textrm{ as }|z|\longrightarrow\infty.

Such functions correspond to metrics on the line bundle

\mathcal{O}(1)
on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
with certain singularities at
infinity (see Section
6.1
). We define the
height function
\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}\colon\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

associated to such
\mathrm{g}
by the rule

(1.2)

\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\#O(\alpha)}\left(\log|a_{\alpha}|+\sum_{\beta\in O(\alpha)}\mathrm{g}(\beta)\right).

Here,
a_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}
is the leading coefficient of
P_{\alpha}
.

Height functions measure the arithmetic complexity of algebraic
numbers, due to two key properties: the function
\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}
is
bounded from below and for any
A,B>0
the set

\big\{\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\mid\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\alpha)<A,\;\#O(\alpha)<B\big\}\quad\textrm{ is finite.}

The
essential minimum
of
\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}
is defined as

(1.3)

\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})\coloneqq\inf\left\{\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\alpha_{n})\,\middle|\,(\alpha_{n})\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\textrm{ is a sequence of distinct algebraic numbers}\right\}.

It follows directly from the definitions that

\inf(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})\leq\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})
. Also, it can be shown that the image of
\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}
is dense in
[\essh,\infty)
(see Remark
6.8
), so
\essh
coincides with the smallest limit point in
\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
.

When
\mathrm{g}(z)=\log^{+}|z|
, we obtain the Weil (or naïve) height
\Ht_{W}=\Ht_{\log^{+}}
. Clearly
\Ht_{W}\geq 0
and for any root of unity
\zeta
, it holds
\Ht_{W}(\zeta)=0
. This shows that
\inf(\Ht_{W})=\ess(\Ht_{W})=0
and the closure of the image of
\Ht_{W}
is
[0,\infty)
.

The essential minimum of a height function plays a role in the subject of equidistribution of small points. Indeed, a generic sequence of algebraic points is
small
if its height converges to the essential minimum. A theorem of Yuan
[
Yua08
, Theorem 3.1]
, subsuming previous results by many mathematicians, ensures that under an appropriate hypothesis on the essential minimum, small points are equidistributed with respect to a measure depending only on the height under consideration. There are many situations where the hypothesis in Yuan’s theorem does not hold, and hence the asymptotic distribution of small points is not known. Very few situations beyond Yuan’s theorem are well understood, see
[
cBS25
]
,
[
BGPRLS19
]
and
[
Küh22
]
.

In the case of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
considered here, the hypothesis in Yuan’s theorem boils down to
\inf(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})=\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})
. However, for many common heights the strict inequality
\inf(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})<\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})
holds and both the explicit determination of
\essh
and the asymptotic distribution of small points, are open problems. An
illustrative example is the Zhang-Zagier height, defined as
follows. Extend the Weil height to

\mathbb{A}^{2}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})=\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{2}
by

\Ht_{W}^{2}(\alpha,\beta)=\Ht_{W}(\alpha)+\Ht_{W}(\beta)
. If
X\subsetneq\mathbb{A}^{2}
is any subvariety, we define a height
\Ht_{X}
on
X
by

\Ht_{X}\coloneqq(\Ht_{W}^{2})_{|X}
. Then, a theorem of Zhang

[
Zha92
]
ensures that
\ess(\Ht_{X})>0
if and
only if
X
does not contain a torsion translate of a toric
subvariety. For concreteness, Zagier took
X=\{x+y=1\}\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}
and studied in
[
Zag93
]
the resulting height, that we denote by
\Ht_{\operatorname{ZZ}}
. In our framework, identifying
X
with
\mathbb{A}^{1}
by projection on one coordinate, the choice

\mathrm{g}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\log^{+}|z|+\log^{+}|1-z|\Big)

leads to a height
\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}
such that
\Ht_{\operatorname{ZZ}}=2\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}
. In
loc. cit.
, it is obtained a
lower bound for
\ess(\Ht_{\operatorname{ZZ}})
.
These results were improved by Doche (
[
Doc01a
]
,
[
Doc01b
]
), who also gives upper bounds for
\ess(\Ht_{\operatorname{ZZ}})
, obtaining

(1.4)

\log(1.2817770214)\leq\ess(\Ht_{\operatorname{ZZ}})\leq\log(1.289735).

An important height function is the stable Faltings height on elliptic curves. Namely,
\Ht_{\operatorname{F}}\colon\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is defined as

\Ht_{\operatorname{F}}(\alpha)=\Ht_{\operatorname{Fal}}(E_{\alpha}),

where
E_{\alpha}
is an elliptic curve with
j
-invariant
j(E_{\alpha})=\alpha
and

\Ht_{\operatorname{Fal}}(E_{\alpha})
is the stable Faltings height of the elliptic curve

E_{\alpha}
. This height is also an explicit multiple of
\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}
for an appropriate
\mathrm{g}
, see section
6.5
. In
[
Löb17
]
is obtained a lower bound that shows
\inf(\Ht_{\operatorname{F}})<\ess(\Ht_{\operatorname{F}})
and both lower and upper bounds for
\ess(\Ht_{\operatorname{F}})
are obtained in
[
BGMRL18
]
. In particular, it follows from
loc. cit.
that

(1.5)

-0.748629\leq\ess(\Ht_{\operatorname{F}})\leq-0.748622.

We denote by
\overline{\mathbb{Z}}
the set of all algebraic integers (recall that
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
is an
algebraic integer
if the polynomial
P_{\alpha}
can be taken to be monic). In both estimations (
1.4
) and (
1.5
), only algebraic integers were used. A surprising consequence of our main result (Theorem
B
below) and our study of limit distributions of algebraic integers (Theorem
C
below) is that, even though
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}-\overline{\mathbb{Z}}
is an infinite set, sequences of algebraic integers are already enough to reach
\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})
from above.

Theorem A

(Corollary
6.7
and Corollary
6.9
)
.

Let
\mathrm{g}\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a Green function. Then,
\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\overline{\mathbb{Z}})
is dense in
[\essh,\infty)
. In particular, there exists a sequence
(\alpha_{n})\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{Z}}
of distinct algebraic integers such that
\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\alpha_{n})
is monotonically decreasing and
\inf_{n}\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\alpha_{n})=\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}).

A direct consequence of this statement is that the essential minimun of the Faltings height
\Ht_{\operatorname{Fal}}
can be attained by a sequence of elliptic curves having good reduction everywhere.

1.2.
Strong duality

The explicit determination of
\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})
from the definition requires to exhibit a
sequence of small points
. That is, a sequence of distinct algebraic numbers
(\alpha_{n})
such that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\alpha_{n})=\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})
. For the Weil height, roots of unity do the job. When
\mathrm{g}
is a radial function, an useful characterization is obtained in
[
BGPS15
]
, in terms of convex analysis. But for many common heights (such as
\Ht_{\operatorname{ZZ}}
and
\Ht_{\operatorname{F}}
) no explicit sequence is known and the essential minimum remains unknown.

We propose in this article a characterization of the essential minimum in terms of the common optimal value of two linear optimization problems which are in duality.

We start by reviewing a method to obtain upper bounds used in
[
BGMRL18
]
. The basic idea is to shift from the search of sequences of small points to the determination of measures describing the expected asymptotic distribution of such sequences. In order to make this idea rigorous we introduce a class of measures and a notion of convergence adapted to this situation.

Let
\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
be the space of probability measures
\mu
on
\mathbb{C}
such that

\int\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu<\infty.

We say a function
f\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\penalty\mathbb{R}
has
logarithmic growth
if there exist constants

A,B
such that for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
, we have
|f(z)|\leq A+B\log^{+}|z|
. We say that a sequence of measures
(\mu_{n})\subset\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
converges
\log
-weakly
to
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
if for any continuous
function
f
with logarithmic growth, it holds that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}=\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

To any
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Z}}
we attach the probability measure

\delta_{O(\alpha)}=\delta_{P_{\alpha}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\#O(\alpha)}\sum_{\beta\in O(\alpha)}\delta_{\beta}.

We denote by
\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
the subset of
\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
consisting of the limit points of
\{\delta_{O(\alpha)}\mid\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Z}}\}
in the
\log
-weak topology. Below we give structural information on the space
\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
, but for the moment let us focus on the upper bounds. The basic observation is that for
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Z}}
, the term
\log|a_{\alpha}|
in (
1.2
) vanishes and so
\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\alpha)
is just the average value of
\mathrm{g}
over
O(\alpha)
. Then, for any measure
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
and sequence
(\alpha_{n})
of distinct algebraic integers such that
\delta_{O(\alpha_{n})}

\log
-weakly converges to
\mu
, in view of (
1.1
) we can conclude

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\alpha_{n})=\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Hence,

(1.6)

\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})\leq\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\textrm{ and }\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\textrm{ is in the real closure of }\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\overline{\mathbb{Z}}).

The best upper bound we can obtain this way is

\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g})\coloneqq\inf\left\{\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\middle|\,\mu\in\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})\right\}.

Now we describe the method to obtain lower bounds. Let
P_{1},\dots,P_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
be polynomials with integer coefficients and let

a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
be non negative real numbers. Set

f(z)\coloneqq\mathrm{g}(z)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\log|P_{i}(z)|.

The basic observation in Smyth’s method
[
Smy81
]
is that for every
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
that is not a zero of any
of the polynomials
P_{i}
, it holds

\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\alpha)\geq\inf_{z\in\mathbb{C}}f(z).

Hence,
\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})\geq\inf f
. The best lower bound that we can obtain in this way is

\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})\coloneqq\sup\left\{\inf_{z\in\mathbb{C}}\Big(\mathrm{g}(z)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\log|P_{i}(z)|\Big)\,\middle|\,k\geq 0,a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},P_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]\right\}.

Our next result is that both
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})
and
\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g})
attain the essential minimum.

Theorem B

(Strong duality, Theorem
6.6
)
.

Let
\mathrm{g}\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a Green function. Then

\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})=\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})=\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g}).

Assuming
\mathrm{g}
is subharmonic and
\mathrm{g}(z)=\log|z|+a+o(1)
at
infinity, the equality
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})=\ess(\mathrm{g})
is the
specialization to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
of a theorem of Ballaÿ
[
Bal21
, Theorem
1.2]
. Our result gives a new proof of this special case.

Our method of proof of Theorem
B
is based on the
interpretation of
\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g})
and
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})
as the optimal
values of two linear programming problems which are in duality. They
are akin to those
considered by Smyth
[
Smy84
]
, Smith
[
Smi24
]
and
Orloski–Sardari–Smith
[
OSS24
]
in
their works on totally real algebraic integers of small trace, which
were indeed a major source of inspiration for this part of our work.
The
weak duality

\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})\leq\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g})
trivially
holds. The content of Theorem
B
is the statement that
the
strong duality

\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})=\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g})
holds.

1.3.
Approximation of measures by algebraic integers

Smyth in
[
Smy81
]
shows that the smallest limit point of the image of the Weil height restricted to totally real algebraic integers is upper bounded by an integral similar to (
1.6
), for a certain explicit measure
\mu
on the real line. He constructs his
\mu
by first constructing an explicit sequence of points with small asymptotic height and then computing their limit distribution. In this work, in contrast, we require to be able to decide whether a given measure
\mu
belongs to
\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
without necessarily constructing the sequence of small points.
In a recent breakthrough, Smith and Orloski–Sardari obtain an useful characterization of this kind for measures of compact support. We extend their characterization to the case of non necessarily compact support, which allows us to obtain structural information on the space
\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
. We proceed to review the compact support setting and then present our results.

We first remark that for our method on upper bounds to work, the approximation of measures by algebraic integers in the traditional weak-* topology is unsuitable. Indeed, weak convergence allows only for test functions which are continuous and bounded, and our
\mathrm{g}
does not belong to such space. We are thus led to strengthen the required notion of approximation. A first possibility is to consider the
proper convergence
: a sequence of measures
(\mu_{n})
properly converges to a measure
\mu
if
(\mu_{n})
weakly converges to
\mu
and moreover there exists a compact set
K
such that
\operatorname{supp}(\mu_{n})\subseteq K
for all
n
. Also, we denote by
\mathscr{P}_{c}(\mathbb{C})
the space of probability measures on
\mathbb{C}
with compact support, endowed with the topology of the proper convergence. We denote by
\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{c}(\mathbb{C})
the subset of
\mathscr{P}_{c}(\mathbb{C})
consisting of the limit points of
\big\{\delta_{O(\alpha)}\mid\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Z}}\big\}
in the topology of the proper convergence.

A plethora of measures in
\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{c}(\mathbb{C})
can be constructed using potential theory. Indeed, let
K\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be a compact set which is conjugation invariant and assume that the capacity of
K
satisfies
\operatorname{cap}(K)=1
. We denote by
\mu_{K}
the equilibrium measure of
K
. Then, combining a landmark result by Fekete and Szegő
[
FS55
]
and a theorem of Rumely in
[
Rum99
]
, it can be shown that
\mu_{K}\in\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{c}(\mathbb{C})
(see Section
2.1
). Moreover, among the
measures with support in a compact set
K
with
\operatorname{cap}(K)=1
and
invariant under complex conjugation, only the
equilibrium measure
\mu_{K}
belongs to
\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{c}(\mathbb{C})
.

Smith and
Orloski–Sardari provide an elegant characterization of the set
\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{c}(\mathbb{C})
. The following statement is a weak version of
[
Smi24
, Theorem 1.5]
and
[
OS24a
, Theorem 1.2]
(in fact, they prove stronger statements in the Fekete–Szegő vein, see Remark
2.9
).

Theorem 1.1

(Smith, Orloski–Sardari)
.

Assume
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{c}(\mathbb{C})
is invariant under complex conjugation. Then,
\mu\in\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{c}(\mathbb{C})
if and only if

\int\log|Q|\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geq 0,\textrm{ for any }Q\in\mathbb{Z}[x].

This characterization directly implies that
\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{c}(\mathbb{C})
is a convex set and that
\mu_{K}\in\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{c}(\mathbb{C})
for every compact conjugation invariant
K
with
\operatorname{cap}(K)\geq 1
. On the other hand, Fekete
[
Fek23
]
showed that whenever
K
has
\operatorname{cap}(K)<1
, there is an open neighborhood
U
of
K
such that only finitely many complete sets of conjugates of algebraic integers lie inside
U
. In particular,
\mu_{K}\notin\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{c}(\mathbb{C})
for such
K
.

Recall that for any
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{c}(\mathbb{C})
, the potential
U^{\mu}\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is defined as

(1.7)

U^{\mu}(z)=\int\log\frac{1}{|w-z|}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(w).

Our next result is an extension of Theorem
1.1
beyond measures of compact support. We focus on the space
\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
, which is the largest class of probability measures
\mu
on
\mathbb{C}
, with support non necessarily compact such that (
1.7
) is still a well defined function. Aspects of potential theory in
\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
have been developed for some time, see for example
[
BLW15
]
,
[
ORSLW19
]
. We show that
U^{\mu_{n}}\longrightarrow U^{\mu}
as distributions if and only if
\mu_{n}\longrightarrow\mu

\log
-weakly (combine Proposition

2.14
, Lemma
2.4
and Lemma
2.1
in the text), so arguably the
\log
-weak convergence is the right topology on
\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
to do potential theory.

We prove that

Theorem C

(Theorem
4.1
)
.

Assume
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
is invariant
under complex conjugation. Then,
\mu\in\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
if and only if

(1.8)

\int\log|Q|\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geq 0\textrm{ for any }Q\in\mathbb{Z}[x].

As before, we deduce from Theorem
C
that
\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
is a convex set.

Even though (
1.8
) may seem daunting because it refers to infinitely many inequalities, we can construct a countable (
\log
-weakly) dense set of elements in
\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
as follows. Let
P\neq Q\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
be two monic and irreducible polynomials. We denote by
\mu_{P,Q}
the probability measure such that

U^{\mu_{P,Q}}(z)=-\log\max\left\{\frac{|P(z)|}{\deg P},\frac{|Q(z)|}{{\deg Q}+1}\right\}.

It is supported on the compact lemniscate
\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,\middle|\,|P(z)|^{\deg Q+1}=|Q(z)|^{\deg P}\right\}
and is nothing but the pullback of the Haar measure on the unit circle by the rational map

P^{\deg Q+1}/Q^{\deg P}\colon\mathbb{P}^{1}\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}.

We show that
\mu_{P,Q}
belongs to
\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
and the set of all such measures is dense in
\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
with respect to the
\log
-weak topology (Proposition
7.9
). This gives a partial answer to (the analogue in our context of) a question of Sarnak alluded to in
[
OS24b
, Section 1.1]
.

Also, we establish a characterization of the
\log
-weak closure of the set of equilibrium measures of capacity one compact sets as the set of measures with negative potential.

Theorem D

(Theorem
4.4
)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
. Then, the followings are
equivalent

(1)

U^{\mu}\leq 0
,

(2)

there exists a sequence of compact subsets
K_{n}

of capacity one, such that
\mu_{K_{n}}\longrightarrow\mu

\log
-weakly.

We remark that not all measures satisfying the equivalent
conditions in Theorem
D
have compact
support. A first example would be the measure

\mu_{\mathrm{FS}}
induced by the Fubini-Study form

\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}=\frac{i}{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}\bar{z}}{(|z|^{2}+1)^{2}}

that is clearly not compactly supported. On the other hand,

\mu_{\mathrm{FS}}
has potential

U^{\mu_{\mathrm{FS}}}(z)=-\frac{1}{2}\log(1+|z|^{2})\leq 0,

so
\mu_{\mathrm{FS}}\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}(\mathbb{C})
by Corollary
4.3
.

Also, using Theorem
D
, we can deduce that the measures obtained from the Fekete–Szegő construction and Rumely’s theorem do not exhaust all the elements in
\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
. Indeed, consider the measure
\mu_{P_{0},Q_{0}}
for the choice
P_{0}(x)=x-1,Q_{0}(x)=x^{2}-x+1
. Since
P_{0}(1/2)=-1/2,Q_{0}(1/2)=3/4
, we see that
U^{\mu_{P_{0},Q_{0}}}(1/2)>0
. By Theorem
D
, this measure cannot be
\log
-weakly approximated by equilibrium measures of compact sets of capacity one.

In
[
BGMRL18
]
, it was used the upper bound

(1.9)

\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})\leq\inf\left\{\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{K}\,\middle|\,K\textrm{ is a conjugation invariant compact set with }\operatorname{cap}(K)=1\right\}

In view of Theorem
D
and the previous discussion, the closure of the space of conjugation invariant measures of the form
\mu_{K}
with
\operatorname{cap}(K)=1
is strictly contained in
\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
, so for many functions
\mathrm{g}
the bound (
1.9
) will not reach
\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})
. See Section
6.4
for an example.

1.4.
Computability of the essential minimum

With regards to the Zhang-Zagier height and the Faltings height discussed at the beginning, a practical algorithm to obtain, in a reasonable time frame, higher accuracy in the estimations (
1.4
) or (
1.5
), is not currently known. In practice, after a few iterations, both Smyth’s lower bound procedure and the upper bounds procedure described here reach a point where it is unclear how to continue, due to the extremely big size of the search space and the lack of an efficient criterion to find the optimal direction (e.g. see the discussions in
[
Zag93
, Section 3]
,
[
Doc01a
, Section 5]
,
[
Doc01b
, p.110]
and
[
BGMRL18
, Ancillary file
1
1

1

arXiv:1609.00071
]
).

Loosely speaking, a real number
\lambda
is
computable
if there exist an algorithm that, for any given precision, after a finite number of steps gives a rational approximation of
\lambda
within the required precision. Here, the algorithm is not constrained to run in a reasonable time frame, in any sense. Even so, the set of computable real numbers is countable, so most real numbers are not computable. In view of the previous discussion, this raises the following question: is the essential minimum a computable real number? That is, does there exists an algorithm that, in a finite amount of
time, gives us the essential minimum with arbitrary precision?

As a consequence of Theorems
B
and
C
, we can show the following computability result.

Theorem E

(Theorem
7.6
)
.

Let
\mathrm{g}\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a computable Green function. Then
\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})
is a computable real number.

We refer the reader to Section
7
for the
precise definitions of computability we use here. The idea is to show
that both
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})
and
\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g})
can be taken inside a
computable countable set (Proposition
6.10
and Proposition

7.9
). Hence, one can set up an algorithm
producing an increasing sequence of lower bounds by day and a
decreasing sequence of upper bounds by night. When the bounds are
less-than-epsilon apart, the procedure has found an approximation of
the required precision.

We remark that both upper and lower bounds are required in order to have a halting criterion. On the other hand, the algorithm in our proof depends on a choice of enumeration of the aforementioned countable sets, so it is hopelessly ineffective.

2.
Potential theory and limit distribution of algebraic integers

2.1.
Potential theory on the complex plane and the Fekete–Szegő
theorem

Let
\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{C})
be the set of all probability Borel
measures on
\mathbb{C}
and let
\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
be
the subspace of those that can integrate
\log^{+}|z|
, i.e.

\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})\coloneqq\left\{\mu\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{C})\,\middle|\,\int\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu<+\infty\right\}.

Let
\mathscr{P}_{c}(\mathbb{C})
be the set of compactly supported
measures. Then
\mathscr{P}_{c}(\mathbb{C})\subseteq\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
.

Potential theory of measures with non-necessarily compact support
has been developed for some time, see for example

[
BLW15
]
,
[
ORSLW19
]
. We recall the basic facts.
For any
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
, we define its
potential function
as

U^{\mu}\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\},\quad U^{\mu}(z)\coloneqq\int-\log|w-z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(w).

We justify that
U^{\mu}
is well defined as follows: since
\mu
can
integrate
\log^{+}|z|
, the integral
\int_{|w-z|\geq 1}-\log|w-z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(w)
is absolutely convergent. On the other hand, the
monotone convergence theorem ensures that

\int_{|w-z|\leq 1}-\log|w-z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z)=\lim_{M\rightarrow\infty}\int_{|w-z|\leq 1}\min\left\{M,-\log|w-z|\right\}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(w)

is a well
defined element in
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
. Then,
-U^{\mu}
is
subharmonic. Hence it is locally integrable.

Proof.

The first statement is
[
BLW15
, Lemma 3.2]
and the second is a
classical result on subharmonic functions.
∎

To be on the safe side, we next check that Fubini’s theorem can be
applied to potentials of measures in
\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
and let
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{C})
. Then

\iint\varphi(z)\log|w-z|^{-1}\mathrm{d}\mu(w)\mathrm{d}\lambda(z)=\iint\varphi(z)\log|w-z|^{-1}\mathrm{d}\lambda(z)\mathrm{d}\mu(w).

Proof.

It is enough to check that

(2.10)

\iint\left|\varphi(z)\log|w-z|^{-1}\right|\mathrm{d}\lambda(z)\mathrm{d}\mu(w)<+\infty

Since
\log|z|
is locally integrable, the function
\int|\varphi(z)\log|w-z|^{-1}|\mathrm{d}\lambda(z)
is a continuous function
on
\mathbb{C}
. Clearly it has logarithmic growth. Since
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
the condition (
2.10
) is satisfied.
∎

As in the case of compactly supported measures, we recover the measure
from the potential.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
. Then
-\Delta U^{\mu}=2\pi\mu
as distributions.

Proof.

Let
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{C})
. Then

\displaystyle\Delta(-U^{\mu})(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\int-U^{\mu}(z)\Delta(\varphi)(z)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda(z)

\displaystyle=\iint\log|z-w|\Delta(\varphi)(z)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(w)\mathrm{d}\lambda(z)

\displaystyle=\iint\log|z-w|\Delta(\varphi)(z)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda(z)\mathrm{d}\mu(w)

\displaystyle=\int 2\pi\varphi(w)\mathrm{d}\mu(w)

\displaystyle=2\pi\mu(\varphi).

Here the third equality is Lemma
2.2
and the fourth equality
is the identity
U^{\Delta(\varphi)\lambda}=-2\pi\varphi
.
∎

We quote a result that will be useful latter.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
f_{n},f\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
,

n\geq 0
, be
subharmonic functions. Then
f_{n}
converges to
f
in

L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
if and only if
f_{n}
converges to

f
as distributions.

Proof.

[
Hör07
, Theorem 3.2.13]
.
∎

We next recall the definition of capacity and the Fekete–Szegő
theorem.
The
energy
of a measure
\mu
as is defined as

I(\mu)\coloneqq\int U^{\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\iint-\log|w-z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(w)\mathrm{d}\mu(z)\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be a compact subset, and let
\mathscr{P}(K)
be
the space of all probability measures supported on
K
. We say
K
is

polar
if
I(\mu)=+\infty
for all
\mu\in\mathscr{P}(K)
.

Now assume
K
is non-polar, then there exists uniquely a measure

\mu_{K}\in\mathscr{P}(K)
such that
I(\mu_{K})=\inf_{\mu\in\mathscr{P}(K)}I(\mu)

[
Ran95
, Theorem 3.7.6]
. This
\mu_{K}

is called the
equilibrium measure
of
K
, and the

capacity
of
K
is defined as
\operatorname{cap}(K)\coloneqq e^{-I(\mu_{K})}
.

Let
\alpha
be an algebraic number and
O(\alpha)\coloneqq\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q})\cdot\alpha
be the Galois
orbit of
\alpha
, that is the complete set of conjugates of
\alpha
. Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be a subset, we say
\alpha
is
totally in

S
if
O(\alpha)\subseteq S
.

Theorem 2.5

(Fekete–Szegő
[
Fek23
,
FS55
]
)
.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be a compact subset. Then

•

if
\operatorname{cap}(K)<1
, then there exists an open
neighborhood
U
of
K
, such that there are only finitely many
algebraic integers totally in
U
.

•

if
\operatorname{cap}(K)\geq 1
and
K
is invariant under
complex conjugation, then for any open neighborhood
U
of
K
,
there are infinitely many algebraic integers totally in
U
.

Remark 2.6
.

In the critical case
\operatorname{cap}(K)=1
, let
(\alpha_{n})
be a sequence of algebraic integers such that

O(\alpha_{n})\subseteq K_{1/n}\coloneqq\big\{z\mid|z-w|<1/n\text{ for
some }w\in K\big\}
. Then the sequence of measures
\delta_{O(\alpha_{n})}

converges properly to the equilibrium measure
\mu_{K}

[
Rum99
, Theorem 1]
.

2.2.
Smith and Orloski–Sardari theorems

When
\operatorname{cap}(K)\geq 1
, by Fekete–Szegő there will
be infinitely many algebraic integers totally contained in any open
neighborhood of
K
and we would like to know what are the possible accumulation measures describing their asymptotic distribution. For
\capac(K)=1
this is answered by a result of
Rumely as in Remark
2.6
, while for
\capac(K)>1
, this is answered in the recent breakthrough by
Smith and Orloski–Sardari.

We say a sequence
(\mu_{n})\subseteq\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{C})

converges weakly
to
\mu
, if we have for any continuous and compactly supported function
f\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}=\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

In this notion of convergence we can replace “compactly supported” by “bounded”
[
Bil97
, Lemma 2.2]
.

For compactly supported measures there is a stronger notion of convergence. Let
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{c}(\mathbb{C})
. We say a sequence
(\mu_{n})\subseteq\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{C})

converges properly
to
\mu
, if
\mu_{n}\longrightarrow\mu
weakly and there exists a compact subset
K\subseteq\mathbb{C}
such that for all
n
, the support of
\mu_{n}
is contained in
K
. Note that this implies that we have for all continuous function
f\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

(2.11)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}=\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Let
(\alpha_{n})\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
be a sequence
of algebraic numbers. We say
O(\alpha_{n})
converges to
\mu
weakly
(resp. properly) if the associated sequence of normalized discrete
measures

\delta_{O(\alpha_{n})}=\frac{1}{\deg(\alpha_{n})}\sum_{\beta\in O(\alpha_{n})}\delta_{\beta}

converges to
\mu
weakly (resp. properly).

A reformulation of the result by Smith
[
Smi24
, Theorem 1.5]
and
Orloski–Sardari
[
OS24a
, Theorem 1.2]
is

Theorem 2.7

(Smith, Orloski–Sardari)
.

Assume
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{c}(\mathbb{C})
is invariant under complex
conjugation. Then the following are equivalent

(1)

there exists a sequence of
distinct algebraic integers
(\alpha_{n})\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
O(\alpha_{n})
converges
to
\mu
properly,

(2)

\displaystyle\int\log|Q|\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geq 0
for any
Q\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
.

Remark 2.8
.

Let
\Ht\colon\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be
the standard Weil height function. Suppose
(\alpha_{n})\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{Z}}
is a sequence of distinct algebraic
integers such that
O(\alpha_{n})
converges
to
\mu
properly, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\Ht(\alpha_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\delta_{O(\alpha_{n})}=\int\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu<\infty.

This forces
\deg(\alpha_{n})
to converge to
\infty
because
otherwise it would contradict the Northcott property.

Remark 2.9
.

The original results of Smith and Orloski–Sardari are in fact
stronger and are more of Fekete–Szegő style, in the sense that
they prove that for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a sequence of
algebraic integers
(\alpha_{n})
that are totally contained in

\supp(\mu)_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\big\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid|z-w|<\varepsilon\text{ for some }w\in\supp(\mu)\big\}.

and such that
O(\alpha_{n})
converges weakly to
\mu
. This is
much stronger that just proper convergence.
Besides, when
\mu
is supported on
\mathbb{R}
, they prove that
the approximating algebraic integers
\alpha_{n}
can be chosen
to be totally real.

2.3.
\log
-weak convergence of measures

We say a function
f\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\penalty\mathbb{R}
has
logarithmic growth
, if there exist
constants

A,B
such that for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
, we have
|f(z)|\leq A+B\log^{+}|z|
.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
\{\mu,(\mu_{n})\}\subset\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
. We say
\mu_{n}

converges
\log
-weakly
to
\mu
, if for any continuous
function
f
with logarithmic growth, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}=\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

On the other hand, we say that the sequence
(\mu_{n})
is

log-tight
if for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a compact
set
K
such that

\int_{K^{c}}\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(z)<\varepsilon,\qquad\text{for all }n.

The following statement follows readily from the definitions.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
\{\mu,(\mu_{n})\}\subset\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
such that

\mu_{n}
converges weakly to
\mu
. Then, the following assertions are equivalent:

i)

The sequence
(\mu_{n})
converges
\log
-weakly to
\mu

ii)

The sequence
(\mu_{n})
is
\log
-tight

iii)

We have that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(z)=\int\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z).

We record here the following fact for future use.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
\big\{\mu_{n}\big\}\subseteq\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{C})
be a sequence
that
\log
-weakly converges to
\mu
. Then, for all polynomials

Q(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x]
, we have that

\int\log|Q|\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geq\liminf_{n}\int\log|Q|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}.

Proof.

Using the monotone convergence theorem and (
2.11
), we have that

\displaystyle\int\log|Q|\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\lim_{M\rightarrow\infty}\int\max\{-M,\log|Q|\}\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\lim_{M\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\max\{-M,\log|Q|\}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n},

the last equality follows from the fact that the function

h^{M}(z)=\max\{-M,\log|Q(z)|\}
is continuous of logarithmic
growth. We conclude by
h^{M}(z)\geq\log|Q(z)|
.
∎

It will be useful in what follows to be able to do diagonal arguments when dealing with weak convergence and

\log
-weak convergence.

Lemma 2.13
.

Consider
\{\mu,(\mu_{n}),(\mu_{n,m})\}\subset\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{C})
. Assume that

•

\mu_{n}
converges weakly to
\mu
as
n\longrightarrow\infty
,

•

for
each
n
,
\mu_{n,m}
converges weakly to
\mu_{n}
as
m\longrightarrow\infty
.

Then, there is a diagonal subsequence
\mu_{n_{i},m_{i}}
that
converges weakly to
\mu
as
i\longrightarrow\infty
.

Moreover, the same holds if we consider
\mu,\mu_{n}
and
\mu_{n,m}
in

\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
and replace weak convergence
with
\log
-weak convergence.

Proof.

Weak convergence on
\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{C})
is metrizable (e.g. see
[
Bil99
, Theorem 6.8]
,
[
Pol84
, Example
IV.22-23]
), so the first assertion follows formally from a diagonal argument in a metric space.

Now we prove the second assertion. Using that for all
n
, the sequence
(\mu_{n,m})_{m}

\log
-weakly converges to
\mu_{n}
, we have that there exists
k(n)
such that for all
m\geq k(n)
, it holds

\left|\int\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(z)-\int\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n,m}(z)\right|<\frac{1}{n}.

Using the first assertion, we extract from
\{\mu_{n,m}\mid m\geq k(n)\}
a subsequence
\nu_{i}=\mu_{n_{i},m_{i}}
that weakly converges
to
\mu
. We remark that we can assume that
n_{i}<n_{i+1}
for all

i
. We will show that the sequence
(\nu_{i})
converges
\log
-weakly
to
\mu
. By Lemma
2.11
, it is enough to show that
(\nu_{i})

is
\log
-tight.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Since
(\mu_{n})
is log-tight, there exists a compact set

K
such that for all
n
, we have that
\int_{K^{c}}\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(z)<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}
. On the other hand, since

(\nu_{i})
and
(\mu_{n_{i}})
have the same weak limit, there is
i_{0}

such that for all
i\geq i_{0}
, we have that

\left|\int_{K}\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n_{i}}(z)-\int_{K}\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{i}(z)\right|<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}

Then, for all
i\geq i_{0}
,

\displaystyle\int_{K^{c}}\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{i}(z)=

\displaystyle\left(\int\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{i}(z)-\int\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n_{i}}(z)\right)+\int_{K^{c}}\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n_{i}}(z)

\displaystyle+\left(\int_{K}\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n_{i}}(z)-\int_{K}\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{i}(z)\right)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{i}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}

Since
n_{i}\geq i
, we conclude that for all
i\geq\max\left\{i_{0},\frac{3}{\varepsilon}\right\}
, we have that

\int_{K^{c}}\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{i}(z)<\varepsilon,

as desired.
∎

2.4.
Subharmonic functions and
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
convergence

Let
f_{n},f\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
n\geq 0
, be functions that are
locally integrable. We say that the sequence
(f_{n})
converges to
f

in

L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
, if for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
, there exists an
open neighborhood
U
of
x
such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{U}|f_{n}-f|\,\mathrm{d}\lambda=0,

where
\lambda
is the usual Lebesgue measure on the complex plane. In
this section we show that the
\log
-weak convergence of measures
corresponds exactly to
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
convergence of
potentials.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
and let
(\mu_{n})\subset\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
be a sequence. The sequence

(\mu_{n})
converges
\log
-weakly to
\mu
if and only if
the sequence
(U^{\mu_{n}})
converges to
U^{\mu}
in

L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
.

Proof.

We start by the direct implication. By Lemmas
2.1
and
2.4
we
need only to show that
U^{\mu_{n}}\longrightarrow U^{\mu}
as distributions. Taking
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{C})
and using Lemma
2.2
, we have that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\varphi(z)U^{\mu_{n}}(z)\mathrm{d}\lambda(z)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\iint\varphi(z)\log|z-w|^{-1}\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(w)\mathrm{d}\lambda(z)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\iint\varphi(z)\log|z-w|^{-1}\mathrm{d}\lambda(z)\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(w)

\displaystyle=\iint\varphi(z)\log|z-w|^{-1}\mathrm{d}\lambda(z)\mathrm{d}\mu(w)

\displaystyle=\iint\varphi(z)\log|z-w|^{-1}\mathrm{d}\mu(w)\mathrm{d}\lambda(z)

\displaystyle=\int\varphi(z)U^{\mu}(z)\mathrm{d}\lambda(z).

The third equality follows from the fact that the function

w\longmapsto\int_{\mathbb{C}}\varphi(z)\log|z-w|^{-1}\mathrm{d}\lambda(z)

is continuous of logarithmic growth and the
hypothesis of
\log
-weak convergence.

Now we prove the reverse implication. By Lemma
2.3
we
have that
\Delta(-U^{\mu})=2\pi\mu
as distributions. Now
assume
U^{\mu_{n}}\longrightarrow U^{\mu}
in

L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
. It follows that
\Delta U^{\mu_{n}}\longrightarrow\Delta U^{\mu}
as distributions. We
conclude that
\mu_{n}\longrightarrow\mu
as distributions. Since

C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{C})
is uniformly dense in the space of
compactly supported continuous functions, this implies that

\mu_{n}\longrightarrow\mu
weakly. By Lemma
2.11
it
only remains to be proven that

(2.12)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(z)=\int\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(z).

Let
f\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a smooth
subharmonic function such that
|\log^{+}-f|
is bounded and

\frac{1}{2\pi}\Delta f=h\mathrm{d}\lambda,

where
\mathrm{d}\lambda
is the Lebesgue measure and
h
is a smooth
function with compact support. Using Lemma

2.2
and the convergence in
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
, we
have that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int f(z)\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\iint\log|z-w|h(w)\mathrm{d}\lambda(w)\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\iint\log|z-w|\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(z)h(w)\mathrm{d}\lambda(w)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}-\int U^{\mu_{n}}(w)h(w)\mathrm{d}\lambda(w)

\displaystyle=-\int U^{\mu}(w)h(w)\mathrm{d}\lambda(w)

By a similar reasoning, the last quantity is
\int f(z)\mathrm{d}\mu(z)
. Since
f(z)-\log^{+}|z|
is continuous and bounded, by weak
convergence we deduce (
2.12
).
∎

3.
The sweetened truncation

The goal of this section is to define the
sweetened truncation
and prove technical lemmas and propositions that are related to it. They will be used the the next sections.

3.1.
Sweetened truncation

Let
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
be a probability measure. For any
R>1
, we want to produce a new probability measure
\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{R}
, whose support is contained in the ball

\{|z|\leq R\}
and such that
\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{R}
converges to
\mu

\log
-weakly as

R
goes to
\infty
. The naïve candidate for such
a truncation is

\mu_{R}^{\prime}\coloneqq\mu|_{\{|z|\leq R\}}+(1-m_{R})\lambda_{S_{R}},

where
m_{R}\coloneqq\mu\left(\big\{|z|\leq R\big\}\right)
and

\lambda_{S_{R}}
is the equilibrium measure on
\big\{|z|=R\big\}
.
We want to refine the truncation is such a way that, if
\mu

satisfies condition (2) in Theorem

4.1
, then the measure
\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{R}
still
satisfies the same condition. The following definition is inspired in
Smith’s concept of
sweetened measure
, see
[
Smi24
, Definition
5.5]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
be a probability measure. Let
R>1
and set

T_{R}\coloneqq\int_{|z|\geq R}\log^{+}|z|\mathrm{d}\mu,\ L_{R}\coloneqq 2(1-m_{R})\log 2+T_{R},\ \eta_{R}\coloneqq\frac{\log R}{\log R+L_{R}}.

We define the sweetened truncation
\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{R}
as

(3.13)

\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{R}\coloneqq\eta_{R}\mu|_{\{|z|\leq R\}}+(1-m_{R}\eta_{R})\lambda_{S_{R}}.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
. Then,

(1)

\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{R}
converges to
\mu

\log
-weakly as
R
goes to

\infty
.

(2)

It holds
U^{\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{R}}\leq\eta_{R}U^{\mu}
. In particular, if
\int\log|Q|\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geq\penalty 0
for some
Q\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
, then we also have
\int\log|Q|\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{R}\geq\penalty 0
.

(3)

If
\mu
is conjugation invariant, then
\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{R}
is conjugation invariant as well.

We will prove this statement after the following Lemma.

Lemma 3.3
.

The naïve truncation
\mu_{R}^{\prime}
satisfies
U^{\mu_{R}^{\prime}}\leq U^{\mu}+L_{R}
.

Proof.

Assume
R>1
. Note that

U^{\mu_{R}^{\prime}}(z)-U^{\mu}(z)=\int_{|w|>R}\log|z-w|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(w)-(1-m_{R})\log\max\big\{|z|,R\big\}.

Assume
|z|<R
. Then for all
w
with
|w|>R
, we have
|z-w|\leq|z|+|w|\leq 2|w|
and hence

\displaystyle U^{\mu^{\prime}_{R}}(z)-U^{\mu}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{|w|>R}\log|z-w|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(w)-(1-m_{R})\log R

\displaystyle\leq\int_{|w|>R}\log|2w|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(w)

\displaystyle\leq L_{R}.

Assume now
|z|\geq R
. Then,

\displaystyle U^{\mu_{R}^{\prime}}(z)-U^{\mu}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{|w|>R}\log|z-w|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(w)-(1-m_{R})\log|z|

\displaystyle=\int_{|w|>R}\log\left|1-\frac{w}{z}\right|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(w)

\displaystyle=\int_{|w|>|z|\geq R}\log\left|1-\frac{w}{z}\right|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(w)+\int_{|z|\geq|w|>R}\log\left|1-\frac{w}{z}\right|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(w)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{|w|>|z|\geq R}\log\left|\frac{2w}{R}\right|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(w)+\int_{|z|\geq|w|>R}\log 2\,\mathrm{d}\mu(w)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{|w|>R}\log|2w|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(w)+\int_{|w|>R}\log 2\,\mathrm{d}\mu(w)

\displaystyle=L_{R}.

∎

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Item
(
3
) follows readily from the
definitions. Now we show (
1
). It is
easy to see from (
3.13
) that
\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{R}

converges to
\mu
weakly as
R
goes to
\infty
. So it
remains to test against
\log^{+}|z|
:

\displaystyle\int

\displaystyle\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{R}-\int\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=(1-m_{R}\eta_{R})\log R+(\eta_{R}-1)\int_{|z|\leq R}\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu-\int_{|z|>R}\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Clearly the last two terms vanish when

R
goes to
\infty
. With regards to the first term, we
have that

(3.14)

(1-m_{R}\eta_{R})\log R=\big((1-m_{R})\log R+L_{R}\big)\frac{\log R}{\log R+L_{R}}.

Then, the estimate

0\leq(1-m_{R})\log R\leq T_{R}
shows that (
3.14
) also vanishes as
R\rightarrow\infty
.

Now we prove (
2
). For the first assertion, we use Lemma
3.3

\displaystyle U^{\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{R}}

\displaystyle=\eta_{R}U^{\mu_{R}^{\prime}}+(1-\eta_{R})U^{\lambda_{S_{R}}}

\displaystyle\leq\eta_{R}U^{\mu}+\eta_{R}L_{R}-(1-\eta_{R})\log R

\displaystyle=\eta_{R}U^{\mu}.

For the second one, let
a\in\mathbb{Z}
be the leading coefficient of
Q

and
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
be the roots of
Q
. Then

\displaystyle\int\log|Q|\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{R}

\displaystyle=\log|a|-\sum U^{\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{R}}(\alpha_{i})

\displaystyle\geq\eta_{R}\log|a|-\eta_{R}\sum U^{\mu}(\alpha_{i})

\displaystyle\geq\eta_{R}\int\log|Q|\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle\geq 0.

∎

3.2.
Sweetened truncation and asymptotically logarithmic functions

Definition 3.4
.

A function
\mathrm{g}\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called
asymptotically logarithmic at infinity
, if it obeys the asymptotic

\mathrm{g}(z)=\log|z|+o(\log|z|),\quad\text{as }|z|\longrightarrow\infty.

A
Green function
is a function
\mathrm{g}\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
which is continuous, invariant under complex conjugation and asymptotically logarithmic at infinity.

Such Green functions arise when considering singular metrics on

\mathcal{O}(1)
(see Remark
6.1
).
The purpose of this section is to prove the following statement.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\mathrm{g}\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function that
is asymptotically logarithmic. Let
(\mu_{n})
be a sequence of
probability measures on
\mathbb{C}
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}<\infty.

Then, for all
\varepsilon>0
there exists
R>1

such that

(3.15)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{n,R}\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}+\varepsilon.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be a continuous function. We
say that a sequence
(\mu_{n})
is
f
-tight
, if

\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{|z|\geq R}|f|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}=0.

If we set
f=\log^{+}|z|
, we recover
\log
-tightness as in
Definition
3.16
. We say a sequence
(\mu_{n})
is

tight
, if it is
1
-tight.

The following elementary statement follows directly from the definitions.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
f,h\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be continuous functions
with
h=o(f)
as
|z|\longrightarrow\infty
. Suppose we have
a sequence
(\mu_{n})\subseteq\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{C})
such that

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\int|f|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}<+\infty.

Then
(\mu_{n})
is
h
-tight.

Proof of Proposition
3.5
.

The hypotheses imply

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}<+\infty,

so we may assume that

(3.16)

{}\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\int\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}<+\infty.

Since
1=o(\log|z|)
when
|z|\longrightarrow\infty
, we have

(\mu_{n})
is tight by Lemma
3.6
. Using
Prohorov’s theorem
[
Bil99
, Theorem 5.1]
, we can assume,
after taking a subsequence if necessary, that there is a probability measure

\mu_{\infty}
on
\mathbb{C}
such that
\mu_{n}
converges weakly to

\mu_{\infty}
. In particular, we know that
\mu_{\infty}
can integrate

\log^{+}|z|
, because

(3.17)

\int\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\infty}\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}<\infty.

Set
E_{n,R}\coloneqq\int\mathrm{g}d\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{n,R}-\int\mathrm{g}d\mu_{n}
. In order to prove (
3.15
) it is enough to show that

(3.18)

\limsup_{R\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}E_{n,R}\leq 0

We break the proof of (
3.18
) in several steps.

Claim 1
. We have that

\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{n}\{|z|\geq R\}\log R=0.

Indeed,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{n}\big\{|z|\geq R\big\}\log R

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{\infty}\big\{|z|\geq R\big\}\log R

\displaystyle\leq\int_{|z|\geq R}\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\infty}.

Here the second inequality follows from the Portmanteau theorem

[
Bil99
, Theorem 2.1 (iii)]
, because
\mu_{n}
converges
weakly to
\mu_{\infty}
and the set
\big\{|z|\geq R\big\}
is
closed. Since (
3.17
) implies that the last term
vanishes when
R\longrightarrow\infty
, this proves the claim.

Claim 2
. There exists
M>0
such that

\{L_{n,R},T_{n,R}\mid n\geq 1,R>1\}\subseteq[0,M].

We see directly from the definitions that
L_{n,R},T_{n,R}\geq 0
. Also,
T_{n,R}
is bounded because of (
3.16
). On the
other hand,

\displaystyle L_{n,R}

\displaystyle=2(1-m_{n,R})\log 2+\int_{|z|\geq R}\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}

\displaystyle\leq 2\log 2+\int\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}

\displaystyle\leq 2\log 2+\sup_{k}\int\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{k}<\infty,

by (
3.16
).

Claim 3
. We have that

\limsup_{R\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left((1-m_{n,R}\eta_{n,R})\log R-T_{n,R}\right)\leq 0.

Indeed, using Claim 2 we see that

\displaystyle(1-m_{n,R}\eta_{n,R})\log R-T_{n,R}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log R}{\log R+L_{n,R}}\cdot\left((1-m_{n,R})\log(4R)+T_{n,R}\right)-T_{n,R}

\displaystyle\leq\big((1-m_{n,R})\log(4R)+T_{n,R}\big)-T_{n,R}

\displaystyle=(1-m_{n,R})\log(4R)

We conclude by Claim 1.

Claim 4
. There exists
M^{\prime}>0
such that for all
n\geq 1
and
R>0
it holds

(1-m_{n,R}\eta_{n,R})\log R\leq M^{\prime}.

This follows from Claim 2 and Claim 3.

Claim 5.
We have that
0\leq 1-\eta_{n,R}\leq\frac{M}{\log R}
.

This follows from
1-\eta_{n,R}=\frac{L_{n,R}}{\log R+L_{n,R}}
and Claim 2.

Write
\mathrm{g}=\log^{+}|z|+h
, with
h=o(\log|z|)
at infinity.

Claim 6.
The sequence
(\int h\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is bounded.

This follows from (
3.16
) and the fact that there exists a constant
A>0
such that
|h(z)|\leq A(1+\log^{+}|z|)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Since
h=o(\log|z|)
, there exists
R_{0}>1
such that for all
z
with
|z|\geq R_{0}
, we have that
h(z)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{M^{\prime}}\log|z|
. For
R\geq R_{0}
and using Claim 4, we have

\displaystyle E_{n,R}=

\displaystyle(1-m_{n,R}\eta_{n,R})\int\mathrm{g}d\lambda_{S_{R}}-\int_{|z|\geq R}\mathrm{g}d\mu_{n}-(1-\eta_{n,R})\int_{|z|\leq R}\mathrm{g}d\mu_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big((1-m_{n,R}\eta_{n,R})\log R-T_{n,R}\big)-(1-\eta_{n,R})\int_{|z|\leq R}\log^{+}|z|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+(1-m_{n,R}\eta_{n,R})\int h\,\mathrm{d}\lambda_{S_{R}}-\int_{|z|\geq R}h\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}-(1-\eta_{n,R})\int_{|z|\leq R}h\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}.

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big((1-m_{n,R}\eta_{n,R})\log R-T_{n,R}\big)+\varepsilon-\int_{|z|\geq R}h\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}-(1-\eta_{n,R})\int_{|z|\leq R}h\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big((1-m_{n,R}\eta_{n,R})\log R-T_{n,R}\big)+\varepsilon+\eta_{n,R}\int_{|z|\geq R}h\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}-(1-\eta_{n,R})\int h\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}

By Lemma
3.6
we know that
(\mu_{n})
is
h
-tight. Combining this information with Claim 3, Claim 4 and Claim 5, we obtain

\limsup_{R\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}E_{n,R}\leq\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, this shows (
3.18
).
∎

4.
Approximating measures by algebraic integers and by capacity one compact sets

4.1.
Approximating measures by algebraic integers in the non-compact setting

The aim of this section is to prove

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
is invariant
under complex conjugation. Then,
\mu\in\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
if and
only if

(4.19)

\int\log|Q|\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geq 0\textrm{ for any }Q\in\mathbb{Z}[x].

It extends the Smith–Orloski–Sardari Theorem

2.7
to non-compactly supported measures that
can integrate functions of logarithmic growth. In the setting of
non-compactly supported measures, proper convergence does not make
sense. Also weak convergence is not appropriate because any
probability measure can be written as the weak limit of a sequence of
Galois orbits of integers. In particular a weak limit of measures
satisfying condition (
4.19
) does not need to satisfy the
same condition (e.g. see the example at the end of Section

6.2
). The main result of this section shows
that the notion of
\log
-weak convergence is the right one for
non-compactly supported measures.

Remark 4.2
.

Suppose
(\alpha_{n})\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{Z}}
is a
sequence of distinct algebraic integers such that
\delta_{O(\alpha_{n})}
converges
\log
-weakly to

\mu
, then
\deg(\alpha_{n})
converges to
\infty
by the
same reason as Remark
2.8
.

Before proving Theorem
4.1
we state a direct consequence.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
be a conjugation invariant measure such that
U^{\mu}\leq 0
. Then,
\mu
belongs to
\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}(\mathbb{C})
.

Proof.

The hypothesis
U^{\mu}\leq 0
readily implies (
4.19
) in Theorem
4.1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Assume that
\mu\in\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
. We prove that it satisfies
condition (
4.19
) by an adaptation of a classical
argument to our setting (e.g. see
[
Ser19
, Lemma 1.3.4]
). Let

(\alpha_{n})
be a sequence of algebraic integers such that
(\delta_{O(\alpha_{n})})
converges
\log
-weakly to
\mu
. We denote by
P_{n}(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
the minimal polynomial of
\alpha_{n}
. Let
Q\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
a non
zero
polynomial. Using Lemma
2.12
, we have that

\int\log|Q|\mathrm{d}\mu\geq\liminf_{n}\frac{1}{\deg(\alpha_{n})}\sum_{\beta\in O(\alpha_{n})}\log|Q(\beta)|.

We denote by
\Res(P_{n},Q)
the resultant of
P_{n}
and
Q
. Since
P_{n}

is monic, we have that
|\Res(P_{n},Q)|=\prod_{\beta\in O(\alpha_{n})}|Q(\beta)|
(e.g. see

[
BG06
, B.1.13]
). Hence,

\sum_{\beta\in O(\alpha_{n})}\log|Q(\beta)|=\log|\Res(P_{n},Q)|\geq 0,

the last inequality being due to the fact that

\operatorname{Res}(P_{n},Q)
is a non zero integer when
n
is big
enough.

Let now
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
that is invariant
under complex conjugation, and that satisfies condition (
4.19
). For
R>1
, consider the sweetened truncation
\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{R}

as in Definition
3.1
. By Proposition
3.2
(
2
) and
(
3
), we can apply Theorem

2.7
to
\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{R}
in order to conclude that it
can be approximated properly (in particular
\log
-weakly) by Galois
orbits of algebraic integers. Using Proposition
3.2
(
1
) and Lemma

2.13
, we can find a diagonal subsequence that
converges to
\mu

\log
-weakly, as desired.
∎

The classical Fekete–Szegő theorem (Theorem
2.5
), plus
Rumely’s remark on Bilu’s equidistribution (Remark

2.6
), imply that if
K\subseteq\mathbb{C}

is a compact subset that is invariant under complex conjugation and
has capacity one, then
\mu_{K}\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}(\mathbb{C})
. The
Smith–Orloski–Sardari theorem (Theorem

2.7
) characterizes all compactly supported ones.

There do exist measures in

\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}(\mathbb{C})
that are not
compactly supported. For instance, the Fubini-Study form

\omega_{\mathrm{FS}}=\frac{i}{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}\bar{z}}{(|z|^{2}+1)^{2}}

induces a conjugation invariant measure
\mu_{\mathrm{FS}}\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
with
potential

U^{\mu_{\mathrm{FS}}}(z)=-\frac{1}{2}\log(|z|^{2}+1)\leq 0.

We deduce
\mu_{\mathrm{FS}}\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}(\mathbb{C})
by Corollary
4.3
.

4.2.
Approximating measures by equilibrium measures of compact sets of capacity one

Here, we prove

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
. Then, the followings are
equivalent

(1)

U^{\mu}\leq 0
,

(2)

there exists a sequence of compact subsets
K_{n}

of capacity one, such that
\mu_{K_{n}}
converges

\log
-weakly to
\mu
.

This theorem characterizes measures with negative potential as the

\log
-weak closure of the set of equilibrium measures of capacity one
compact sets.

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

We show first that (
1
) implies (
2
). Using the
monotone convergence theorem,

\displaystyle U^{\mu}(z)

\displaystyle=\int-\log|z-w|\mathrm{d}\mu(w)

\displaystyle=\lim_{M\rightarrow\infty}\int\min\left\{M,-\log|z-w|\right\}\mathrm{d}\mu(w)

\displaystyle=\lim_{M\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\min\left\{M,-\log|z-w|\right\}\mathrm{d}\mu_{K_{n}}(w).

In the last step we use that the function
w\mapsto\min\left\{M,-\log|z-w|\right\}
is continuous and of logarithmic growth. We deduce
U^{\mu}(z)\leq 0
from

\int\min\left\{M,-\log|z-w|\right\}\mathrm{d}\mu_{K_{n}}(w)\leq U^{\mu_{K_{n}}}(z)\leq 0,

the last inequality being due to Frostman’s
theorem
[
Ran95
, Theorem 3.3.4(a)]
.

Now we prove that (
2
) implies (
1
). First, we
claim that there exists a sequence of monic polynomials
P_{n}
such
that
\delta_{O(P_{n})}
converges to
\mu

\log
-weakly.

Indeed, by Proposition
3.2
(
1
) and Lemma
2.13
, we are reduced to
prove this assertion replacing
\mu
by a measure
\mu^{\prime}
having
compact support
K
. In that case, we can weakly approximate
\mu^{\prime}
by
a sequence of measures
(\mu_{n})
with discrete support
S_{n}
contained
in
K
and of the form

\mu_{n}=\sum_{s\in S_{n}}\frac{a_{s}}{b_{n}}\delta_{s},\quad a_{s},b_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}.

But then

(\mu_{n})
converges properly and we can take
P_{n}(x)\coloneqq\prod_{s\in S_{n}}(x-s)^{a_{s}}
, thus proving our claim.

We denote by
U_{n}\coloneqq-\frac{\log|P_{n}|}{\deg(P_{n})}
the
potential of
\delta_{O(P_{n})}
. Using
Proposition
2.14
, we have that

U_{n}
converges tto
U^{\mu}
in
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
. Let

U_{n}^{-}\coloneqq\min\{U_{n},0\}
be the negative part of
U_{n}
. Since

U^{\mu}\leq 0
, we have that
|U_{n}^{-}-U^{\mu}|\leq|U_{n}-U^{\mu}|
, so

U_{n}^{-}
converges to
U^{\mu}
in
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
as well. But

U_{n}^{-}=-\frac{\log^{+}|P_{n}|}{\deg(P_{n})}
is the potential of the
equilibrium measure of the compact set
K_{n}\coloneqq\big\{z\mid|P_{n}(z)|\leq 1\big\}
, which is of capacity one because
P_{n}
is
monic. We conclude by another application of Proposition

2.14
.
∎

5.
Strong duality

Here we present two optimization problems that will be crucial for our
study of heights of algebraic points. They are akin to those
considered by Smyth
[
Smy84
]
and Smith
[
Smi24
]
in
their works on totally real algebraic integers of small trace, which
were indeed a major source of inspiration for the material in this
section.

Let
\mathrm{g}\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a continuous
function that is asymptotically logarithmic at
\infty
in the sense of
Definition
3.4
, and for simplicity fix an enumeration

Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3},\dots
of all nonconstant primitive irreducible
polynomials with integer coefficients.
We consider the
primal problem
and the
dual problem

respectively defined as

(5.20)

\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g})=\inf\left\{\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\ \,\middle|\,\ \mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C}),\,\int\log|Q_{n}|\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geq 0\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

and

(5.21)

\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})=\sup_{a}\inf_{z\in\mathbb{C}}\left(\mathrm{g}(z)-\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n}\log|Q_{n}(z)|\right),

the supremum being over the sequences
a=(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with
a_{n}=0
for all
but a finite number of
n
’s. We refer to the quantities

\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g})
and
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})
as the

optimal values
for these problems.

Remark 5.1
.

If
\mathrm{g}
is invariant under the complex conjugation then

\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g})=\inf\Big\{\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\Big|\ \mu\in\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})\Big\}.

Indeed, in this case the primal problem can be computed over the
measures
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
that are invariant under
the complex conjugation and satisfy

\int\log|Q_{n}|\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geq 0
for all
n
, which by
Theorem
4.1
coincide with those in

\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
.

As explained in Appendix
A
, this pair of
problems is an instance of primal and dual problems in linear
optimization (Example
A.4
). In particular, they satisfy the
weak duality property.

Proposition 5.2
.

We have

\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g})\geq\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})
.

Proof.

This is a particular case of Proposition
A.5
but can
also be checked directly: for all

\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
such that

\int\log|Q_{n}|\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geq 0
for all
n
and

a=(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
as in (
5.21
) we have

\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geq\int\Big(\mathrm{g}-\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n}\log|Q_{n}|\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geq\inf_{z\in\mathbb{C}}\Big(\mathrm{g}(z)-\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n}\log|Q_{n}(z)|\Big),

which gives the inequality.
∎

Our main result in this section shows that these problems
satisfy the strong duality property, which consists in the equality
between their optimal values. Its proof is modelled in that of
Theorem
A.7
for the finite dimensional case.

Theorem 5.3
.

We have

\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})=\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g})\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\prime}(\mathbb{C})
be the convex set of probability
measures on
\mathbb{C}
that integrate all the functions of the
form
\log|Q_{i}|
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}

consider the convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
defined as

V_{n}=\Big\{\Big(\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu,\int\log|Q_{1}|\,\mathrm{d}\mu,\dots,\int\log|Q_{n}|\,\mathrm{d}\mu\Big)\,\Big|\ \mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\prime}(\mathbb{C})\Big\},

and for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
consider also the convex subset
of
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
defined as

W_{n,\lambda}=\{(t,x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\,|\ t\leq\lambda,\ x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\geq 0\}.

We have that

V_{n}\cap W_{n,\lambda}\neq\emptyset
if and only if there exists

\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\prime}(\mathbb{C})
such that

\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\lambda
and

\int\log|Q_{i}|\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geq 0
,
i=1,\dots,n
.
Now set

\lambda_{n}=\inf\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\,|\ V_{n}\cap W_{n,\lambda}\neq\emptyset\}.

Since
\mathrm{g}
is bounded from below the values

\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu
for

\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
are also bounded from below,
and so
\lambda_{n}>-\infty
.
On the other hand, the Dirac delta
measure
\mu=\delta_{z}
for
z\gg 0
satisfies

(5.22)

\int\log|Q_{i}|\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\log|Q_{i}(z)|>0,\quad i=1,\dots,n.

In particular
\lambda_{n}\leq\mathrm{g}(z)<+\infty
. Hence
\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Since
\lambda_{n}-1/n<\lambda_{n}
the convex subsets
V
and

W_{n,\lambda_{n}-1/n}
are disjoint, and so by the hyperplane
separation theorem there exists

h\in(\mathbb{R}^{n+2})^{\vee}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
with

h\neq 0
such that
h(p)\geq h(p^{\prime})
for all
p\in V_{n}
and

p^{\prime}\in W_{n,\lambda_{n}-1/n}
. Writing

h=(b,-a_{1},\dots,-a_{n})
with
b,a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}

these conditions amount to

(5.23)

b\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\int\log|Q_{i}|\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geq b\,t-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}x_{i}

for all
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\prime}(\mathbb{C})
,

t\leq\lambda_{n}-1/n
and
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\geq 0
.

Since
x_{i}
can be arbitrarily large this inequality implies that

a_{i}\geq 0
,
i=1,\dots,n
, and since
t
can also
be arbitrarily negative we similarly deduce that
b\geq 0
. To exclude
the possibility that
b=0
, consider the case
x_{i}=0
,

i=1,\dots,n
, note that in this situation
(
5.23
) gives

-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\int\log|Q_{i}|\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geq 0\quad\text{ for all }\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\prime}(\mathbb{C}).

Since
h\not=0
, we have
a_{i}>0
for some
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, in which case
the inequality does not hold for the measure in (
5.22
). We
conclude that
b>0
, and so we can assume without loss of generality
that
b=1
.

Setting
t=\lambda_{n}-1/n
and
x_{1}=\cdots=x_{n}=0
in (
5.23
) we get

(5.24)

\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\int\log|Q_{i}|\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geq\lambda_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\quad\text{ for all }\mu\in\mathscr{P}^{\prime}_{\log}(\mathbb{C}).

Considering this inequality for

\mu=\delta_{z}
with

z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
we get

\mathrm{g}(z)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\log|Q_{i}(z)|\geq\lambda_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\quad\text{ for all }z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{Q}},

which extends by density to all
z\in\mathbb{C}
. Hence

\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})\geq\lambda_{n}-1/n
. Therefore

(5.25)

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lambda_{n}\leq\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g}).

On the other hand we can choose

\mu_{n}\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\prime}(\mathbb{C})
satisfying

(5.26)

\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}\leq\lambda_{n}+\frac{1}{n}\quad\text{ and }\quad\int\log|Q_{i}|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}\geq 0,\quad i=1,\dots,n.

By (
5.25
)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}\leq\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g}).

By Proposition
3.5
, for any

\varepsilon>0
there exists
R>1
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{n,R}\leq\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})+\varepsilon,

where
\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{n,R}
denotes the sweetened truncation of
\mu_{n}
to
the ball
B_{R}=\big\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,|\ |z|\leq R\big\}

(Definition
3.1
). Since all the probability measures

\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{n,R}
are supported on
B_{R}
, up to taking a subsequence we
can assume that they converge properly to a probability
measure
\mu_{R}
with support contained in
B_{R}
. Then

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{R}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{n,R}\leq\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})+\varepsilon,

and by Lemma
2.12
and
Proposition
3.2
(
2
) we also have

\displaystyle\int\log|Q_{n}|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{R}\geq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\log|Q|\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\mathrm{sw}}_{n,R}\geq 0\quad\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Hence
\mu_{R}\in
is a candidate for the primal problem and we
deduce
\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g})\leq\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})+\varepsilon
. Taking

\varepsilon>0
arbitrarily small we get

\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g})\leq\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})
, and we obtain the equality by combining this
with the weak duality property (Proposition
5.2
).

To see that

\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g})
is a real number we use the estimates

-\infty<\inf_{z\in\mathbb{C}}\mathrm{g}(z)\leq\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g})\leq\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{R}<\infty.

∎

Remark 5.4
.

A related result was proved by Smith for functions on subsets of the real line with super-logarithm behavior around the point at infinity
[
Smi24
, Theorem 5.11]
. The proof is also similar to
loc. cit.
and consists of two ingredients: the finite dimensional strong duality and the passage from finite dimensional to infinite dimensional by a limiting process.

The finite dimensional strong duality part shares the same spirit of the proof of Theorem
A.7
in the appendix, which is the standard proof of strong duality for finite dimensional linear programming. But here we also need some suitable modifications due to the fact that here we deal with specific infinite dimensional vectors spaces and cones that are not necessarily closed.

The passage to the limit part is carried out by the technique of sweetened truncation and related estimations, especially Proposition
3.5
.

Remarks 5.5
.

i)

As we will see in Section
6.4
, there may not exist
a measure realizing the optimal value
\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g})
. On the
other hand, when such measure does exist, it needs not to be

\mathrm{g}(z)=\log^{+}|z/2|
. In fact, let

\lambda_{r}
be the equilibrium measure on the circle
\big\{|z|=r\big\}
. Then for any
r\in[1,2]
we have that
\lambda_{r}\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}(\mathbb{C})
and it attains the

\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g})
.

ii)

More generally, when
\mathrm{g}(z)
is radial
(i.e.
\mathrm{g}(z)=\mathrm{g}(|z|)
for all
z
) and
\mathrm{g}
is also
subharmonic, the measure
\lambda_{1}
attains the optimal value of

\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g})
. If we assume further that
\mathrm{g}
is smooth and

[

,
BGPS15
,
BGPRLS19
]
.

6.
On the essential minimum of heights

In this section we recall the Arakelov point of view of the height
functions on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
and use the strong duality theorem to extract
some properties for the essential minimum of such heights.

6.1.
Arakelov theory on
\mathbb{P}^{1}

Let
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{Z}}
denote the projective line over
\Spec(\mathbb{Z})
and
let
\overline{\mathcal{L}}=(\mathcal{O}(1),\|\cdot\|)
be the
metrized line bundle on
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{1}
consisting of the
line bundle
\mathcal{O}(1)
plus the extra data of a continuous
norm on the holomorphic line bundle
\mathcal{O}(1)(\mathbb{C})
over
the Riemann sphere
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{C})
, that is invariant under
complex conjugation. To such metrized line bundle one associates a
height function
\Ht_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}}

[
BGS94
]
, see also
[
Yua12
, Section 8,9]
for a survey.

Let
(x_{0}\colon x_{1})
be homogeneous coordinates of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Then
x_{1}
is
a global section of
\mathcal{O}(1)
.
Using the identification
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{C})\backslash(1:0)\cong\mathbb{C}
, given
by
(x_{0}\colon x_{1})\mapsto x=x_{0}/x_{1}
, the data of the metric

\|\cdot\|
is encoded in the so called Green function (of continuous type)

(6.27)

\mathrm{g}\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad x\longmapsto-\log\|x_{1}(x\colon 1)\|.

This Green function is continuous, invariant under complex
conjugation, and there is a neighborhood
U
of the point
(1\colon 0)\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{C})
such that
\mathrm{g}(x)-\log(x)
extends continuously
to the point
(1\colon 0)
. Clearly this condition is stronger than
the condition in Definition
3.4
. In fact any Green
function of continuous type defines a
continuous norm on
\mathcal{O}(1)
, thus the data of a continuous metric and
the data of a Green function of continuous type are equivalent.

Remark 6.1
.

Already in the work of Faltings leading to the proof of Mordell’s
conjecture
[
Fal83
]
, it
became apparent that for many arithmetic applications one should
not restrict one self to continuous metrics but also allow for
some singularities. In the recent work
[
YZ26
]
, Yuan and
Zhang have introduced adelic line bundles on
quasi-projective vartieties extending the notion of heights of
points to a very wide class of singular metrics. If we assume that
the metric
\|\cdot\|
is singular at the point
(1\colon 0)
in such a
way that
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
defines an adelic line bundle
over the quasi-projective variety
\mathbb{A}^{1}\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}
then the
associated Green function
\mathrm{g}
satisfies Definition
3.4
. The converse is also true, if
\mathrm{g}
is a Green
function as in Definition
3.4
, then the associated
metrized line bundle is an adelic metrized line bundle
over
\mathbb{A}^{1}
in the sense of
[
YZ26
]
.

Following Remark
6.1
, from now on we will assume that the
metrized line bundle
\overline{\mathcal{L}}=(\mathcal{O}(1),\|\cdot\|)

defines an adelic line bundle over
\mathbb{A}^{1}
and that
\mathrm{g}
is the associated
Green function given by (
6.27
). Hence it is a Green function
as in Definition
3.4
. The height function

\Ht_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}}
only depends on
\mathrm{g}
and will be denoted as

\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}
unless we want to stress the Arakelov point of
view.

This height function can be described in
very concrete terms. Let
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
and let
P_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
be a primitive irreducible polynomial with
P_{\alpha}(\alpha)=0
. The polynomial
P_{\alpha}
is unique up to a sign.
Denote by
O(\alpha)\subset\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
the set of zeros of
P_{\alpha}
and
let
a_{\alpha}
be the leading coefficient of
P_{\alpha}
. The
height of
\alpha
is given by

(6.28)

\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\deg(\alpha)}\left(\log|a_{\alpha}|+\sum_{\beta\in O(\alpha)}\mathrm{g}(\beta)\right).

If
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Z}}
then, we can choose
P_{\alpha}

monic. Therefore

(6.29)

\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\deg(\alpha)}\sum_{\beta\in O(\alpha)}\mathrm{g}(\beta),\qquad\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Z}}.

While for an arbitrary algebraic number we only have the inequality

\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\alpha)\geq\frac{1}{\deg(\alpha)}\sum_{\beta\in O(x)}\mathrm{g}(\beta),\qquad\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}.

There is another description of the height of an algebraic number as a
sum of local contributions that will be useful latter.
Let
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Q}}
be the set of places of
\mathbb{Q}
, that is

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Q}}=\{p\in\mathbb{Z}\mid p>0\ \text{prime}\}\cup\{\infty\}.

For each
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Q}}
let
|\cdot|_{\nu}
denote either the usual
absolute value of
\mathbb{Q}
if
\nu=\infty
or the
p
-adic absolute value
if
\nu\not=\infty
. The absolute value
|\cdot|_{\nu}
extends uniquely
to the field
\mathbb{C}_{\nu}
. For each
\nu
fix an embedding

j_{\nu}\colon\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\to\mathbb{C}_{\nu}
and write

|\alpha|_{\nu}=|j_{\nu}(\alpha)|_{\nu},\quad\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}

for a choice of absolute value in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
that extends
|\cdot|_{\nu}
.

The alternative description of the height of
\alpha
is
[
BG06
, Section 1.5.7]

(6.30)

\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\deg(\alpha)}\left(\left(\sum_{p\textrm{ prime}}\sum_{\beta\in O(\alpha)}\log^{+}|\beta|_{p}\right)+\sum_{\beta\in O(\alpha)}\mathrm{g}(\beta)\right).

Definition 6.2
.

Let
\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}
be the height function associated with a Green
function
\mathrm{g}
. The
essential minimum
of
\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}
is

\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})\coloneqq\inf\left\{\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\alpha_{n})\,\middle|\,(\alpha_{n})\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\textrm{ is a sequence of distinct algebraic numbers}\right\}.

Remark 6.3
.

To be as elementary as possible, from now on we will forget about
the Arakelov point of view and focus on the Green function
\mathrm{g}

as the source for the height function. Nevertheless the Arakelov
point of view already points out towards many generalizations of the
present work. First, instead of considering the canonical model

\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{Z}}
of
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{Q}}
one can consider
arbitrary models of
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{Q}}
over
\Spec(\mathbb{Z})
or
even more generally one can consider general adelic line bundles,
with different metrics over each place of
\mathbb{Q}
. Second,
instead of the open set
\mathbb{A}^{1}\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}
and the divisor

[(1:0)]
of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
with respect to which the Green function is
defined, one can consider arbitrary divisors of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Third,
instead of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
one can consider arbitrary curves or even
higher dimensional varieties.

6.2.
The essential minimum and linear programming

In this section we relate the essential minimum of the height function
with the linear programming problems
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})
and
\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g})
.

Proposition 6.4
.

The relation

\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})\geq\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})

holds true.

Proof.

Recall the product formula

\prod_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Q}}}|\alpha|_{\nu}=1,\qquad\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times},

wich implies that, for
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\times}
,

(6.31)

\sum_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Q}}}\sum_{\beta\in O(\alpha)}\log|\beta|_{\nu}=0,

because
\prod_{\beta\in O(\alpha)}\beta\in\mathbb{Q}
.
Let
Q_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
be polynomials with integer coefficients
and
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. If we show that

(6.32)

\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})\geq\inf_{z\in\mathbb{C}}\left(\mathrm{g}(z)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\log|Q_{i}(z)|\right),

then the proposition will be proved.

If
\sum a_{i}\deg(Q_{i})>1
, then

\inf_{z\in\mathbb{C}}\left(\mathrm{g}(z)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\log|Q_{i}(z)|\right)=-\infty

and equation (
6.32
) is trivially true. So we can assume that

(6.33)

\sum a_{i}\deg(Q_{i})\leq 1.

Write
Z
for the set of zeroes of the polynomials
Q_{i}
. This is a
finite set. Then,
by the product formula, for every
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\setminus Z
,

\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\deg(\alpha)}\sum_{p\textrm{ prime}}\sum_{\beta\in O(\alpha)}\left(\log^{+}|\beta|_{p}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\log|Q_{i}(\beta)|_{p}\right)\\
+\frac{1}{\deg(\alpha)}\sum_{\beta\in O(\alpha)}\left(\mathrm{g}(\beta)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\log|Q_{i}(\beta)|\right).

By (
6.33
), and the fact that the polynomials
Q_{i}
have
integer coefficients, we obtain, for every prime
p
,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\log|Q_{i}(\beta)|_{p}\leq\log^{+}|\beta|_{p}.

Therefore

\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\alpha)\geq\frac{1}{\deg(\alpha)}\sum_{\beta\in O(\alpha)}\left(\mathrm{g}(\beta)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\log|Q_{i}(\beta)|\right),\quad\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}\setminus Z.

This implies that equation (
6.32
) holds, proving the proposition.
∎

Proposition 6.5
.

The relation
\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})\leq\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g})

holds true.

Proof.

Let
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}
and take a
sequence of distinct algebraic integers
(\alpha_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
\delta_{O(\alpha_{n})}
converges in the
log-weak topology to
\mu
. Using formula (
6.29
) and the fact that

\mathrm{g}
is a continuous function of logarithmic growth, we have that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\alpha_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\deg(\alpha_{n})}\sum_{p\in O(\alpha_{n})}\mathrm{g}(p)=\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Hence,

\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})\leq\inf_{\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}(\mathbb{C})}\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

∎

Putting together Propositions
6.4
and
6.5
with
Theorem
5.3
we obtain the main theorem of this
section.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
\mathrm{g}
be a Green function. Then,

\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})=\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})=\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g}).

A direct consequence of Theorem
6.6

is that the essential minimum can be reached by a sequence of
algebraic integers.

Corollary 6.7
.

There exists a sequence of distinct algebraic integers

(\alpha_{n})\subseteq\overline{\mathbb{Z}}
such that

\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\alpha_{n})
is monotonically decreasing and
\inf_{n}\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\alpha_{n})=\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}).

Remark 6.8
.

The fact that
\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
is dense in
[\essh,\infty)
can be obtained as a
consequence of Szachniewicz

[
Sza23
, Theorem A]
that uses the theory of globally valued
fields. As another consequence of our previous results, here we can refine this statement to
\overline{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Corollary 6.9
.

Let
\mathrm{g}\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a Green function. Then,

\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\overline{\mathbb{Z}})
is dense in
[\essh,\infty)
.

Proof.

By (
1.6
), the set

M=\left\{\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\middle|\,\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}(\mathbb{C})\right\}

is contained in the closure of
\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\overline{\mathbb{Z}})
. From Theorem
4.1
we deduce that
M
is a convex subset of
\mathbb{R}
, hence an interval. In view of Theorem
6.6
, such interval contains
(\essh,\infty)
, as desired.
∎

6.3.
Asymptotic maximal slope

In this section we prove that in the dual problem we can reduce to
linear combinations with rational coefficients. This has
two applications. The first one is to relate the essential minimum with another
Arakelov theory invariant called the assymptotic maximal slope and the
second one is the left computability of the essential minimum as we
will see in section
7
. Consider
the rational dual problems

\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathrm{g})\coloneqq\sup\left\{\inf_{z\in\mathbb{C}}\Big(\mathrm{g}(z)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\log|Q_{i}(z)|\Big)\,\middle|\,a_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0},Q_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[x],\sum a_{i}\deg(Q_{i})\leq 1\right\}.

and

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathrm{g})\coloneqq\sup\left\{\inf_{z\in\mathbb{C}}\Big(\mathrm{g}(z)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\log|Q_{i}(z)|\Big)\,\middle|\,a_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0},Q_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[x],\sum a_{i}\deg(Q_{i})<1\right\}.

Clearly
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})\geq\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathrm{g})\geq\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathrm{g})
.

Proposition 6.10
.

The equalities
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})=\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathrm{g})=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathrm{g})
hold.

Proof.

Let
n\geq 1
and take
{\bf a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
and

(Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{n})\in(\mathbb{Z}[x])^{n}
. Let

\varphi_{\bf a}(z)\coloneqq\mathrm{g}(z)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\log|Q_{i}(z)|.

Then, it is enough to show that for any
\varepsilon>0
and any
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
, there exists
{\bf b}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}^{n}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}\deg Q_{i}<1
such that

(6.34)

\inf\varphi_{\bf a}\leq\inf\varphi_{\bf b}+\varepsilon.

Relation (
6.34
) holds trivially if
\inf\varphi_{\bf a}=-\infty
or
{\bf a}={\bf 0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, so we can assume
\inf\varphi_{\bf a}>-\infty
and
a_{i}\neq 0
for all
i
. Then, necessarily

(6.35)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\deg Q_{i}\leq 1.

Let
U
be an open and bounded neighborhood of the set of zeroes of

\prod_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}(x)
such that for all
z\in U
, it holds

(6.36)

\inf\varphi_{\bf a}\leq\mathrm{g}(z)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}\log|Q_{i}(z)|,\log|Q_{i}(z)|\right\}

Choose
R\geq 1
big enough so that
\log|Q_{i}(z)|\geq 0
for all

|z|\geq R
and
i=1,\ldots,n.
Set
K\coloneqq U^{c}\cap\{|z|\leq R\}
. Since
K
is a compact set, there exist
{\bf b}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}^{n}
such that

\frac{a_{i}}{2}\leq b_{i}<a_{i}\textrm{ for all }i\textrm{ and }\inf_{x\in K}\varphi_{\bf b}(x)\geq\inf_{x\in K}\varphi_{\bf a}(x)-\varepsilon.

In particular, in view of (
6.35
) we have that

(6.37)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}\deg Q_{i}<1\textrm{ and }\inf_{z\in K}\varphi_{\bf b}(z)\geq\inf\varphi_{\bf a}-\varepsilon.

On the other hand, we claim that

(6.38)

\inf_{z\in U}\varphi_{\bf b}(z)\geq\inf\varphi_{\bf a}.

Indeed, due to the choice of the
b_{i}
we have

\varphi_{\bf b}(z)\geq\mathrm{g}(z)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}\log|Q_{i}(z)|,\log|Q_{i}(z)|\right\},

so the claim follows from (
6.36
). Finally, for
z
such that
|z|\geq R
we have

\varphi_{\bf b}(z)-\varphi_{\bf a}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i}-b_{i})\log|Q_{i}(z)|\geq 0,

thus
\inf_{|z|\geq R}\varphi_{\bf b}(z)\geq\inf\varphi_{\bf a}.
This estimate, together with (
6.37
) and
(
6.38
), imply (
6.34
).
∎

We now consider on
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{Z}}
the line bundles
\mathcal{O}(n)=\mathcal{O}(1)^{\otimes n}
. The metric on
\mathcal{O}(1)
induces metrics on each
\mathcal{O}(n)
. For each
global section
s\in\rH^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{Z}},\mathcal{O}(n))
the sup norm is defined
as

\|s\|_{\infty}\coloneqq\sup_{z\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{C})}\|s(z)\|.

The
asymptotic maximal slope
of
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
is defined as
by

\hat{\mu}(\overline{\mathcal{L}})\coloneqq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\geq 1\\
s\in\rH^{0}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{Z}}^{1},\mathcal{O}(n))\end{subarray}}-\frac{\log\|s\|_{\infty}}{n}.

By unfolding the definitions, one can see that

\hat{\mu}(\overline{\mathcal{L}})=\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathrm{g}),

which implies the next result.

Corollary 6.11
.

The equality

\hat{\mu}(\overline{\mathcal{L}})=\ess(\Ht_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}})
is satisfied.

Remark 6.12
.

When
\mathrm{g}
is subharmonic and
\mathrm{g}(z)=\log|z|+a+o(1)
at

\mathbb{P}^{1}
of a result of Ballaÿ
[
Bal21

. Yuan conjectured

. This conjecture has been

6.4.
An example with no minimizer

In this subsection we present an example due to Nuno Hultberg, where the infimum

\inf_{\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}(\mathbb{C})}\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu

is not attained by any measure
\mu_{0}\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}(\mathbb{C})
.
Consider the automorphism
f\colon\mathbb{P}^{1}\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}
,
x\longmapsto x^{-1}+2
. In homogeneous coordinates is
given by
(x_{0}\colon x_{1})\longmapsto(x_{1}+2x_{0}\colon x_{0})
under
the identification
(x:1)=(x_{0}\colon x_{1})
. Let

\overline{\mathcal{L}}=\big(\mathcal{O}(1),\|\cdot\|_{\operatorname{can}}\big)
, where the
canonical metric
\|\cdot\|_{\operatorname{can}}
is the metric whose
Green function is
\log^{+}
.
Consider the hermitian line bundle

f^{*}\overline{\mathcal{L}}
. By the projection formula

\Ht_{f^{*}\overline{\mathcal{L}}}=\Ht_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}}\circ f
, we see that

\ess(\Ht_{f^{*}\overline{\mathcal{L}}})=\ess\Ht_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}}=\penalty 0.

The Green function associated with
f^{\ast}\overline{\mathcal{L}}
is

\mathrm{g}(z)=\log^{+}|z^{-1}+2|+\log|z|
. In particular, Theorem

6.6
applies to

\Ht_{f^{*}\overline{\mathcal{L}}}
.

Assume there exists
\mu_{0}\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}(\mathbb{C})
such that
\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}=\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})=\penalty 0
. Then
there exists a sequence of distinct algebraic integers
(\alpha_{n})
such that

\delta_{O(\alpha_{n})}
converges to
\mu_{0}
in the
\log
-weak sense. Therefore
\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(\alpha_{n})
converges to
\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}=0
. Using the projection formula and Bilu’s
equidistribution theorem
[
Bil97
, Theorem 1.1]
, we see that

\delta_{O(\alpha_{n})}
must converge weakly to
f^{*}\mu_{S^{1}}
, which forces

\mu_{0}=f^{*}\mu_{S^{1}}
. However,

\displaystyle\int\log^{+}|z^{-1}+2|+\log|z|\,\mathrm{d}f^{*}\mu_{S^{1}}(z)

\displaystyle=\int\log^{+}|t|+\log|t-2|^{-1}\mathrm{d}\mu_{S^{1}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\int\log|t-2|\mathrm{d}\mu_{S^{1}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\log 2\not=0,

which is a contradiction.

The preceeding argument also shows that
f^{*}\mu_{S^{1}}\notin\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}(\mathbb{C})
, even though

\mu_{S^{1}}
does belong to
\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}(\mathbb{C})
. To see this
more concretely, consider the polynomial

P_{n}(x)=\frac{x^{n+1}-2x^{n}+1}{x-1}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]

and let
\alpha_{n}
be a root of
P_{n}
. Then
f^{-1}(\alpha_{n})

is an algebraic integer and the sequence
\Ht_{\mathrm{g}}(f^{-1}(\alpha_{n}))=\Ht_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}}(\alpha_{n})
,
n\geq 1
, converges to zero when
n
goes
to
\infty
. If we look at
the Galois orbit of
f^{-1}(\alpha_{n})
, there are
n-1
conjugates of

f^{-1}(\alpha_{n})
that are close to the support of
f^{*}\mu_{S^{1}}
, yet the
remaining conjugate goes to infinity at the speed of
2^{n}
. Therefore

\delta_{O(f^{-1}(\alpha_{n}))}
converges weakly to
f^{*}\mu_{S^{1}}
but
does not converge
\log
-weakly.
The situation is similar to Autissier’s counterexample

[
Aut06
]
showing that logarithmic equidistribution of small
points is not true in general.

In this example, it also happens that the essential minimum is strictly smaller than

(6.39)

\inf\left\{\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{K}\,\middle|\,K\textrm{ is a conjugation invariant compact set of capacity one}\right\}.

Indeed,

\displaystyle\mathrm{g}(z)=\log^{+}|z^{-1}+2|+\log|z|=\log|z|+\log 2+o(1)\quad\text{as
$z\longrightarrow\infty$}.

Let
\mu_{\infty}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\pi}\Delta\mathrm{g}=f^{*}\mu_{S^{1}}
. Then

\mathrm{g}(z)=-U^{\mu_{\infty}}+\log(2)
and for any probability measure

\mu
,

\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\log 2+\int-U^{\mu_{\infty}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\log 2+\int-U^{\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\infty}.

If
\mu
is equilibrium measure of a capacity one set, then

-U^{\mu}\geq 0
, and we will have
\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geq\log 2
. So (
6.39
) cannot be the true essential minimum
(which is
0
).

6.5.
The essential minimum of Faltings’ height

Let
\Gamma=\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and consider the
associated modular curve
Y
, which is defined over
\mathbb{Q}
. Note
that
Y(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
is in bijection with the set of
isomorphism classes of elliptic curves over

\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Let
X
be the compactification of
Y
. There is a line bundle

M_{12}
on
X
such that
\rH^{0}(X,M_{12}^{\otimes n})\simeq M_{12n}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Q})
as Hecke modules, where the latter is the

\mathbb{Q}
-space of modular forms of
weight
12n
and level
1
with rational Fourier coefficients.

We have a canonical integral model
\big(\mathcal{X}/\mathbb{Z},\mathcal{M}_{12}\big)
of
\big(X/\mathbb{Q}
,
M_{12}\big)
. Note that

\rH^{0}(\mathcal{X},n\mathcal{M}_{12})\simeq\mathcal{M}_{12n}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})

as Hecke modules, where the latter is the abelian group of modular
forms of weight
12n
and level
1
with integral Fourier
coefficients.

The Petersson metric
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{Pet}}
on
M_{12}(\mathbb{C})
is
defined as
\|f\|_{\mathrm{Pet}}(\tau)\coloneqq|f(\tau)|\big(4\pi\operatorname{Im}\tau\big)^{6}
, i.e. taking
\big(4\pi\operatorname{Im}\tau\big)^{6}
as metric weight, where
f
is any modular
form of weight
12
and level
1
and
\tau\in\mathbb{H}\coloneqq\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid\Img(z)>0\}
is a point in the Poincaré upper half
plane. The function
\|f\|_{\mathrm{Pet}}(\tau)
is invariant under the
action of
\Gamma
and descents to a function on
Y(\mathbb{C})
.
We
also define the
L_{2}
-norm on

M_{12}(\mathbb{C})
by

\|f\|_{L^{2}}\coloneqq\int_{Y(\mathbb{C})}\|f\|_{\mathrm{Pet}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\mathrm{hyp}}.

Here,
\mu_{\mathrm{hyp}}
is the hyperbolic measure on
Y(\mathbb{C})
normalized
such that it is a probability measure.

Let
\overline{\mathcal{M}_{12}}\coloneqq(\mathcal{M}_{12},\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{Pet}})
. Then,
the Faltings height can be brought to our framework by the relation

[
BGMRL18
, Section 2.1]

\Ht_{\rm F}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{12}\Ht_{\overline{\mathcal{M}_{12}}}(\alpha).

Remark 6.13
.

This height is in fact the
stable
Faltings height of the elliptic curve of
j
-invariant
\alpha
, and can also be defined using the Hodge bundle equipped a canonical metric
[
YZ26
, Theorem 5.5.1]
.

The
j
-function induces an isomorphism
j\colon\mathcal{X}\cong\penalty\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{Z}}
over
\mathbb{Z}
, where

•

the cusp point corresponds to
\infty
,

•

the automorphic bundle
\mathcal{M}_{12}
corresponds to
\mathcal{O}(1)
,

•

the modular discriminant
\Delta\in\rH^{0}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{M}_{12})
corresponds to the
section
x_{1}\in\rH^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{Z}},\mathcal{O}(1))
.

Let
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{hyp}}
be the metric on

\mathcal{O}(1)(\mathbb{C})
corresponding to the Petersson metric on

M_{12}(\mathbb{C})
and let
\mathrm{g}_{\mathrm{hyp}}\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the induced Green function. By the previous
identifications it is given by

\mathrm{g}_{\mathrm{hyp}}(z)=-\log\|\Delta(\tau_{z})\|_{\mathrm{Pet}},

where
\tau_{z}\in\mathbb{H}
is sent to
z
by

.
Note that
we have the asymptotic estimate
[
BGMRL18
, Section 3.2]

\mathrm{g}_{\mathrm{hyp}}(z)=\log|z|-6\log\log|z|+\penalty O(1),\quad\text{as $|z|\longrightarrow\infty$},

so

is a Green function in the sense of Definition

3.4
. Thus Theorem

6.6
and Corollary
6.11
apply and we get

Theorem 6.14
.

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\geq 1\\
s\in\rH^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\mathbb{Z}},\mathcal{O}(n))\end{subarray}}-\frac{\log\|s\|_{\mathrm{hyp},\infty}}{n}=12\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{F}})=\inf_{\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}(\mathbb{C})}\int\mathrm{g}_{\mathrm{hyp}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Theorem 6.15
.

Let
\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}\big(\mathcal{Y}(\mathbb{C})\big)
be the set of probability measures that
can be approximated
\log
-weakly by Galois orbits in

\mathcal{Y}(\overline{\mathbb{Z}})
. Then

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\geq 1\\
f\in\mathcal{M}_{12n}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})\end{subarray}}-\frac{\log\|f\|_{\mathrm{Pet},\infty}}{n}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\geq 1\\
f\in\mathcal{M}_{12n}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})\end{subarray}}-\frac{\log\|f\|_{L^{2}}}{n}

\displaystyle=12\ess(\Ht_{\operatorname{F}})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}\big(\mathcal{Y}(\mathbb{C})\big)}\int-\log\|\Delta\|_{\operatorname{Pet}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Proof.

The first equality follows from
[
CGS21
, Lemma
3.4.5]
, where it is proved that the

-norm is subexponential. The

.
∎

Remark 6.16
.

In

7.
The computability of the essential minimum

For a particular height function, it is not obvious how to compute its essential minimum.
As a consequence of the strong duality Theorem
6.6
, we can construct both a decreasing sequence and an increasing sequence converging to the essential minimum,
thus showing that this invariant is a computable real number.
The obtained algorithm is far from being practical, but might be a
first step in the search of an effective procedure for this problem.

To make the ideas precise, we will use the theory of computability and we refer the reader to
[
PER89
]
for preliminaries on computability.

7.1.
Computability

We first recall the notion of computable number, in one of its
equivalent definitions.

Definition 7.1
.

A real number
r
is
computable
if there exists a Turing
machine that given any rational number
\varepsilon>0
produces a rational number
q
with

|r-q|<\varepsilon
. A complex number is
computable
if both its
real and imaginary part are computable.

The set of computable numbers is a countable subfield of
\mathbb{C}
that
contains all algebraic numbers and as a consequence, most real numbers
are non-computable. Nevertheless it is very difficult to give a
concrete non-computable number. Examples of such numbers are Chaitin’s
constants, which are associated to Turing’s halting problem.

There are weaker notions of computability.

Definition 7.2
.

A real number
r
is
left computable
if there is a Turing machine
that given a natural number
n
produces a rational number
x_{n}
such that

\sup_{n}x_{n}=r
. Similarly
r
is
right computable
if there
is a Turing machine that given
n
produces a rational number
y_{n}
such that

\inf_{n}x_{n}=r
.

Lemma 7.3
.

A real number is computable if and only if it is both left and right computable.

Proof.

Assume that
r
is computable. Then there is a Turing machine that
given
n
produces a rational number
z_{n}
with
|r-z_{n}|<1/n
. The
sequences
x_{n}=z_{n}-1/n
and
y_{n}=z_{n}+1/n
show that
r
is left and
right computable.

Now assume that
r
is both right and left computable and

0<\varepsilon\in\mathbb{Q}
. Then there is a Turing machine that given a
natural number
N
produces
\alpha_{N}=\max_{n\leq N}x_{n}
and

\beta_{N}=\min_{n\leq N}y_{n}
. When

\beta_{N}-\alpha_{N}<\varepsilon
we have reached the desired
precision and the algorithm stops.
∎

Definition 7.4
.

A function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}
is
computable
if there is a
Turing machine that given input
n
produces output
f(n)
.

The Green functions that will be well suited for our computations are those computable in appropriate compact sets and having effective asymptotics.

Definition 7.5
.

Let
\mathrm{g}\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a Green
function as in
3.4
. It is said to be

computable
if it satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

for any rational rectangle
E
(i.e.
E=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\colon a_{1}\leq\operatorname{Re}(z)\leq b_{1},a_{2}\leq\operatorname{Im}(z)\leq b_{2}\}
for rational numbers
a_{1}<b_{1},a_{2}<b_{2}
), the restriction
\mathrm{g}\colon E\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is computable in the sense of
[
PER89
, Section
0.3, Definition A]
,

(2)

there is a computable function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}

such that for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
with
|z|>f(n)

\big|\mathrm{g}(z)-\log|z|\big|\leq\frac{1}{n}\log|z|.

The following is our main result in this section.

Theorem 7.6
.

Let
\mathrm{g}
be a computable
Green function. Then
\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})
is a computable real number.

The proof will be carried out in the next two sections, where we will
see that the essential minimum is both left and right computable.

7.2.
Left computability

By Proposition
6.10
we have

\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})=\sup_{\Lambda}\inf_{z\in\mathbb{C}}\Big(\mathrm{g}(z)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\log|Q_{i}(z)|\Big),

with

\Lambda=\Big\{(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{k},a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\,\Big|\ k\in\mathbb{N},Q_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[x],a_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}\text{ and }\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\deg(Q_{i})<1\Big\}.

Since the index set
\Lambda
can be effectively enumerated, the left
computability of
\ess(\Ht_{g})
is a direct consequence of the next
statement.

Proposition 7.7
.

Assume that
\mathrm{g}
is a computable Green function. Let
(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{k},a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in\Lambda
and set

\varphi=\mathrm{g}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\log|Q_{i}|
. Then,

\inf_{z\in\mathbb{C}}\varphi(z)
is a computable real number.

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{g}
is a computable Green function and

\sum a_{i}\deg(Q_{i})<1
, we can determine a rational rectangle
E
such that the
infimum of
\varphi
is attained in
E
. Similarly we
can determine
M\in\mathbb{N}
such that that there is

z\in E
with
\varphi(z)\leq M
. Then

\inf_{z\in\mathbb{C}}\varphi(z)=\inf_{z\in E}\min\{\varphi(z),M\}.

Then this infimum is computable
[
PER89
, Section 0.6, Theorem 7]
, because the restriction of

\min\{\varphi,M\}
to
E
is a computable function.
∎

7.3.
Right computability

For the right computability, we look at the other side of the strong
duality property, namely

\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})=\inf\Big\{\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\Big|\ \mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}(\mathbb{C})\Big\}.

Since the set
\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
is not
countable, our first step is to show that it admits a countable subset
that is dense with respect to the
\log
-weak topology.

Consider the index set

\Theta=\big\{(P,Q)\,\big|\ P,Q\in\mathbb{Z}[x]\text{ with }P\neq Q\text{ monic irreducible
polynomials}\big\}.

For
(P,Q)\in\Theta
we denote by

\varphi_{P,Q}\colon\mathbb{P}^{1}\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}
the map
induced by the rational function
P^{\deg(Q)+1}/Q^{\deg(P)}
. This
is a finite and proper map of degree
\deg(P)(\deg(Q)+1)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a function. We denote by
(\varphi_{P,Q})_{*}f:\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
the

push-forward
of
f
by
\varphi_{P,Q}
, defined as

(\varphi_{P,Q})_{*}f(z)=\sum_{w|\varphi_{P,Q}(w)=z}e_{w}f(w),

where
e_{w}
is the ramification index of
\varphi_{P,Q}
at
w
.

We remark that due the properness of
\varphi_{P,Q}
, the push-forward operation preserves the space of continuous and compactly supported functions. Hence, if
\mu
is a measure on
\mathbb{C}
, we can define the pullback measure
\varphi_{P,Q}^{*}\mu
by the rule

\int f\mathrm{d}(\varphi_{P,Q}^{*}\mu):=\int(\varphi_{P,Q})_{*}f\mathrm{d}\mu,

where
f:\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is continuous and compactly supported.

Finally, we consider
the probability measure
\mu_{P,Q}
defined as

\mu_{P,Q}=\frac{(\varphi_{P,Q})^{*}\mu_{S^{1}}}{\deg(P)(\deg(Q)+1)},

with
\mu_{S^{1}}
the Haar measure on the unit circle.

Proposition 7.8
.

Let
(P,Q)\in\Theta
. Then
\mu_{P,Q}
is a probability measure supported
on the compact set

(7.40)

\left\{x\,\middle|\,|P(x)|^{\deg(Q)+1}=|Q(x)|^{\deg(P)}\right\}

with potential function
\displaystyle{U^{\mu_{P,Q}}=\min\left\{-\frac{\log|P|}{\deg(P)},-\frac{\log|Q|}{\deg(Q)+1}\right\}}
.

Proof.

The support of
\mu_{P,Q}
is the preimage of
S^{1}
with respect
to the map
\varphi_{P,Q}
, which coincides with (
7.40
). On the other hand, set
d=\deg(P)
and
e=\deg(Q)
. Then for
z\in\mathbb{C}
we have

(7.41)

U^{\mu_{P,Q}}(z)=-\int\log|z-w|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{P,Q}(w)=\frac{-1}{d\,(e+1)}\int\sum_{\varphi_{P,Q}(w)=y}e_{w}\log|z-w|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{S^{1}}(y)

We have

\sum_{\varphi_{P,Q}(w)=y}e_{w}\log|z-w|=\log\Big|\prod_{\varphi_{P,Q}(w)=y}(z-w)^{e_{w}}\Big|=\log|P(z)^{e+1}-yQ(z)^{d}|

and the expression formula for the potential follows from
(
7.41
) applying Jensen’s formula.
∎

Proposition 7.9
.

The family of probability measures

\{\mu_{P,Q}\,|\ (P,Q)\in\Theta\}
is a countable dense subset
of
\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
.

Proof.

Let
(P,Q)\in\Theta
and take an irreducible primitive polynomial

F\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
with leading coefficient
a\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and
roots
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
. Using Proposition
7.8
we get

\displaystyle\int\log|F|\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{P,Q}

\displaystyle=\log|a|-\sum_{i}U^{\mu_{P,Q}}(\alpha_{i})

\displaystyle=\log|a|-\sum_{i}\max\left\{\frac{\log|P(\alpha_{i})|}{\deg(P)},\frac{\log|Q(\alpha_{i})|}{\deg(Q)+1}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\max\left\{\sum_{i}\frac{\log|P(\alpha_{i})|}{\deg(P)},\sum_{i}\frac{\log|Q(\alpha_{i})|}{\deg(Q)+1}\right\}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\frac{\log|\operatorname{Res}(P,F)|}{\deg(P)},\frac{\log|\operatorname{Res}(Q,F)|}{\deg(Q)+1}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq 0

where the last inequality follows from the fact that

\operatorname{Res}(P,F)
and
\operatorname{Res}(Q,F)
are integers
that cannot be both zero because
F
,
P
,
Q
are irreducible and

P
,
Q
are coprime. Hence

\mu_{P,Q}\in\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
.

Now let
\mu\in\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
, and let

(\alpha_{n})
be a sequence of distinct algebraic integers with

\delta_{O(\alpha_{n})}\longrightarrow\mu

\log
-weakly, and
denote by
P_{n}
the minimal polynomial of
\alpha_{n}
. Then by
Proposition
2.14
, we have

-\frac{\log|P_{n}|}{\deg(P_{n})}\longrightarrow U^{\mu}\quad\text{in $L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}$}.

Hence

\min\left\{-\frac{\log|P_{n}|}{\deg(P_{n})},-\frac{\log|P_{n+1}|}{\deg(P_{n+1})+1}\right\}\longrightarrow U^{\mu}\quad\text{in $L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}$},

which gives
\mu_{P_{n},P_{n+1}}\longrightarrow\mu

\log
-weakly by Proposition

2.14
again. This proves
that the family
\{\mu_{P,Q}\}
is dense under the
\log
-weak
topology.
∎

Remark 7.10
.

One could alternatively consider for each
(P,Q)\in\Theta
the
probability measure
\mu^{\prime}_{P,Q}
similarly induced by the
rational function
P^{\deg(Q)}/Q^{\deg(P)}
. This gives another
countable dense subset of
\mathscr{P}^{\overline{\mathbb{Z}}}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})

and might look more natural than
\{\mu_{P,Q}\}
. The advantage
of the measures
\mu_{P,Q}
is that they are compactly supported,
which is important in the proof of Proposition
7.11
.

By Proposition
7.9
, we have

\ess(\Ht_{\mathrm{g}})=\inf_{(P,Q)\in\Theta}\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{P,Q}.

Hence the
right computability of the essential minimum follows by the next result.

Proposition 7.11
.

Let
(P,Q)\in\Theta
. Assume that
\mathrm{g}
is a computable Green function. Then, the integral

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{P,Q}
is computable.

Proof.

Set for short
d=\deg(P)
and
e=\deg(Q)
and consider the function

\rho\colon[0,1]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad\rho(\theta)=\frac{((\varphi_{P,Q})_{*}\mathrm{g})(e^{2\pi i\theta})}{d\,(e+1)}.

For each
\theta\in[0,1]
consider also the polynomial

S_{\theta}=P^{\deg(Q)+1}-e^{2\pi i\theta}Q^{\deg(P)}\in\mathbb{C}[x]
. Then

(7.42)

\rho(\theta)=\frac{1}{d\,(e+1)}\sum_{S_{\theta}(w)=0}e_{w}\mathrm{g}(w).

There exists an effective
0<R\in\mathbb{Q}
such that

\varphi_{P,Q}^{-1}(S^{1})\subseteq[-R,R]\times i[-R,R]
. Since

\mathrm{g}
restricted to
[-R,R]\times i[-R,R]
is computable by
definition, and the process of finding the complete set of roots of
polynomials is computable by
[
Spe69
]
,
\rho
is a
computable function and hence the Riemann integral

\int_{0}^{1}\Big((\varphi_{P,Q})_{*}\mathrm{g}\Big)(e^{2\pi i\theta})\,\mathrm{d}\theta

is computable
[
PER89
, Section 0.5, Theorem 5]
.
∎

Appendix A
Duality in linear
programming

In this appendix we present a coordinate-free formulation of duality in linear
optimization and recall the proof of the strong duality property in
the finite dimensional case. This allows to place the optimization
problems from Section
5
within a general
framework and to give a more conceptual approach to their duality
properties.

Let
E,F
be two real vector spaces equipped with a pairing

\langle-,-\rangle\colon E\times F\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Let

E\longrightarrow F^{\vee}
and
F\longrightarrow E^{\vee}
be the induced linear maps between
these spaces and their duals, that we respectively denote by

x\longmapsto x^{{\dagger}}
and
y\longmapsto y^{{\dagger}}
. They are defined by
setting

x^{{\dagger}}(y)=y^{{\dagger}}(x)=\langle x,y\rangle\quad\text{ for all }x\in E\text{ and }y\in F.

Consider two convex cones
\sigma\subset E
and
\tau\subset F
. Their duals are the convex cones

\sigma^{\vee}=\{u\in E^{\vee}\mid u(x)\geq 0\text{ for all }x\in\sigma\},\quad\tau^{\vee}=\{v\in F^{\vee}\mid v(y)\geq 0\text{ for all }y\in\tau\}.

Let also
u_{0}\in E^{\vee}
and
v_{0}\in F^{\vee}
.

Definition A.1
.

The
primal problem
and the
dual problem
for this
datum are the optimization problems respectively given by

\mathcal{P}=\inf\{u_{0}(x)\mid x\in\sigma,\ x^{{\dagger}}-v_{0}\in\tau^{\vee}\},\quad\mathcal{D}=\sup\{v_{0}(y)\mid y\in\tau,\ u_{0}-y^{{\dagger}}\in\sigma^{\vee}\}.

We refer to the quantities
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{D}
as the
optimal
values
of these problems.

Remark A.2
.

The role of the primal and the dual problems can be exchanged: denote by
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{op}}
and

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{op}}
the primal and dual problems that arise when
swapping the vector spaces
E
and
F
and considering the cones

-\tau\subset F
and
-\sigma\subset E
together with the functionals

-v_{0}\in F^{\vee}
and
-u_{0}\in E^{\vee}
. Then it can be easily
verified that
\mathcal{P}=-\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{op}}
and

\mathcal{D}=-\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{op}}
.

The classical problems in linear programming are a particular case of
this framework.

Example A.3
.

Set
E=\mathbb{R}^{m}
and

F=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and given
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,

b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
consider the pairing
and functionals defined by

(x,y)\longmapsto\langle x,y\rangle=x^{\mathrm{T}}A\,y,\quad x\longmapsto u_{0}(x)=b^{\mathrm{T}}x,\quad y\longmapsto v_{0}(y)=c^{\mathrm{T}}y

together with the cones
\sigma=\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
and

\tau=\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
. Then the associated primal and dual problems
boil down to the usual forms

\mathcal{P}=\inf\{b^{\mathrm{T}}x\mid x\geq 0,\ A^{\mathrm{T}}x\geq c\},\quad\mathcal{D}=\sup\{c^{\mathrm{T}}y\mid y\geq 0,\ A\,y\leq b\},

where
\leq
and
\geq
on these real vector spaces means
that these inequalities hold coordinate-wise.

The problems from Section
5
also fit within
this framework. Denote by
\mathscr{M}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
and

\mathscr{S}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
the cone and the vector space generated by

\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
. The elements of
\mathscr{M}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
are the measures
on
\mathbb{C}
that integrate the function
\log^{+}|z|
, whereas
those of
\mathscr{S}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
are the differences of these
measures.

We also let
\mathscr{P}^{\prime}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
be the set of probability measures

\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
that integrate the functions
\log|Q|
for
all
Q\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
, and we respectively denote by
\mathscr{M}^{\prime}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})

and
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
the cone and vector space
generated by this set of probability measures.

Example A.4
.

Set

E=\mathscr{S}^{\prime}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})\quad\text{ and }\quad F=\mathbb{R}\oplus\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{R}.

Fix an enumeration
Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3},\dots
of all nonconstant
integer polynomials and recall that the elements of
F
are the
tuples
a=(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\dots)
with
a_{n}=0
for all but
a finite number of
n
’s. We then consider the pairing

E\times F\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

(\mu,a)\longmapsto a_{0}\int\mathrm{d}\mu+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n}\int\log|Q_{n}|\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

We also consider the convex cones defined as

\sigma=\mathscr{M}_{\log}^{\prime}(\mathbb{C})\subset E\quad\text{ and }\quad\tau=\{(a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{n},\dots)\mid a_{n}\geq 0\text{ for all
}n>0\}\subset F.

Let
\mathrm{g}\colon\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a
continuous function that is asymptotically logarithmic at
\infty
in the sense of
Definition
3.4
, and define the functionals

u_{0}\in E^{\vee}
and
v_{0}\in F^{\vee}
as

u_{0}(\mu)=\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\quad\text{ and }\quad v_{0}(a)=a_{0}.

The associated primal problem amounts to the minimization

\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g})=\inf_{\mu}\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu

over the measures
\mu\in\mathscr{M}_{\log}^{\prime}(\mathbb{C})
such
that

a_{0}\left(\int\mathrm{d}\mu-1\right)+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n}\int\log|Q_{n}|\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geq 0

for all
a_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
a_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,

n\in\mathbb{N}
, with
a_{n}=0
for all but a finite number of

n
’s. Since
a_{0}
is arbitrary, this forces
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\prime}(\mathbb{C})
. Hence, this minimization is over the probability
measures
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}^{\prime}(\mathbb{C})
such that

\int\log|Q_{n}|\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geq 0
for all
n
, which coincide with
those in
\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C})
satisfying the same
condition. Hence

\mathcal{P}(\mathrm{g})=\inf\Big\{\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,\Big|\ \mu\in\mathscr{P}_{\log}(\mathbb{C}),\,\int\log|Q_{n}|\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geq 0\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\Big\}

as in (
5.20
). Similarly, the associated dual problem is the
maximization
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})=\sup_{a}a_{0}
over

a\in\tau
such that

(A.43)

\int\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{d}\mu-a_{0}\int\mathrm{d}\mu-\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n}\int\log|Q_{n}|\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geq 0\quad\text{
for all }\mu\in\mathscr{M}_{\log}^{\prime}(\mathbb{C}).

This is equivalent to the inequality

\mathrm{g}(z)-\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n}\log|Q_{n}(z)|\geq a_{0}

for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
, as it can be seen by considering
(
A.43
) for the Dirac delta measures
\mu=\delta_{z}
for all

z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
. Hence

\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{g})=\sup_{a\in\tau}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{C}}\Big(\mathrm{g}(x)-\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n}\log|Q_{n}(x)|\Big),

in agreement with (
5.21
).

The weak duality property is the fact that the optimal value of the
primal problem bounds above that of the dual, and follows readily from
the definitions.

Proposition A.5
.

We have

\mathcal{P}\geq\mathcal{D}
.

Proof.

For all
x\in\sigma
and
y\in\tau
such that
x^{{\dagger}}-v_{0}\in\tau^{\vee}
and
u_{0}-y^{{\dagger}}\in\sigma^{\vee}
we have

u_{0}(x)\geq\langle x,y\rangle\geq v_{0}(y)
, which gives the
inequality.
∎

The strong duality property is the equality between the optimal values
of the primal and the dual problem. We end this appendix by recalling
the proof of this property in the finite dimensional situation.

Definition A.6
.

The
feasibility set
of the primal problem
\mathcal{P}

and the dual problem
\mathcal{D}
are

S_{\mathcal{P}}=\{x\in\sigma\mid x^{{\dagger}}-v_{0}\in\tau^{\vee}\}\subset E\quad\text{ and }\quad S_{\mathcal{D}}=\{y\in\tau\mid u_{0}-y^{{\dagger}}\in\sigma^{\vee}\}\subset F.

We say that
\mathcal{P}
(respectively
\mathcal{D}
) is

feasible
if
S_{\mathcal{P}}\not=\emptyset
(respectively if

S_{\mathcal{D}}\neq\emptyset
), and we say that
\mathcal{P}

(respectively
\mathcal{D}
) is
bounded
if the set

\{u_{0}(x)\colon x\in S_{\mathcal{P}}\}
is bounded below
(respectively if the set
\{v_{0}(y)\colon y\in S_{\mathcal{D}}\}
is
bounded above).

We also say that
\mathcal{P}
(respectively
\mathcal{D}
) is

attained
if there exists
x\in S_{\mathcal{P}}
such that

\mathcal{P}=u_{0}(x)
(respectively if there exists

y\in S_{\mathcal{D}}
such that
\mathcal{D}=v_{0}(y)
).

The primal problem is feasible and bounded if and only if
\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{R}
,
and similarly for the dual problem. Moreover, the weak duality
property shows that if one of these problems is feasible then the
other is bounded.

Theorem A.7
.

Assume that
E
and
F
are finite
dimensional vector spaces and that
\sigma
and
\tau
are closed convex
cones. The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

the primal problem
\mathcal{P}
is feasible
and bounded,

(2)

the dual problem
\mathcal{D}
is feasible and bounded.

If any of these conditions holds then
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{D}\in\mathbb{R}
and
both
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{D}
are attained.

Proof.

First assume that (
1
) holds and consider the closed
convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}\times F^{\vee}
defined as

V=\{(u_{0}(x),x^{{\dagger}})\,|\ x\in\sigma\}\quad\text{ and }\quad W_{\lambda}=\{(t,v+v_{0})\mid t\leq\lambda,\ v\in\tau^{\vee}\},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

For each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
we have that

V\cap W_{\lambda}\neq\emptyset
if and only if there exists

x\in S_{\mathcal{P}}
such that
u_{0}(x)\leq\lambda
.

We have the decomposition

W_{\lambda}=\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}\times\tau^{\vee}+(p,v_{0})
. Hence
considering the closed convex cone and the point respectively defined as

C=V-\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}\times\tau=\{(u_{0}(x)-t,x^{{\dagger}}-v):t\in\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0},\ x\in\sigma,\ v\in\tau^{\vee}\}\quad\text{ and }\quad p_{\lambda}=(\lambda,v_{0}),

the condition
V\cap W_{\lambda}\neq\emptyset
turns out to be
equivalent to
p_{\lambda}\in C
. Since
C
is a closed cone, this
condition on
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is closed, and it is also
nonempty and bounded below because
\mathcal{P}
is feasible and
bounded. Thus setting

\lambda_{0}=\inf\{\lambda\,|\ p_{\lambda}\in C\}
we have

\mathcal{P}=\lambda_{0}=\min\{\lambda\,|\ p_{\lambda}\in C\}\in\mathbb{R}.

In particular
\mathcal{P}
is attained.

Now let
\lambda<\lambda_{0}
. By the point-cone separation theorem
there exists

h\in(\mathbb{R}\times F^{\vee})^{\vee}=\mathbb{R}\oplus F
such
that
h|_{C}\geq 0
and
h(p_{\lambda})<0
, which implies that

h|_{V}>h|_{W_{\lambda}}
. Hence
writing
h=(b,-y)
with
b\in\mathbb{R}
and
y\in F
we
have

(A.44)

b\,u_{0}(x)-\langle x,y\rangle>b\,t-v(y)-v_{0}(y)\quad\text{ for all }t\leq\lambda,x\in\sigma,v\in\tau^{\vee}.

Specializing (
A.44
) to

x=x_{1}\in S_{\mathcal{P}}\subset\sigma
and

v=v_{1}=x_{1}^{{\dagger}}-v_{0}\in\tau^{\vee}
we get

b\,u_{0}(x_{1})>b\,t
, which implies that
b>0
because
t

can be an arbitrarily large negative number.

We assume without loss of generality that
b=1
. Then
(
A.44
) specialized to
t=\lambda
becomes

(A.45)

u_{0}(x)-\langle x,y\rangle>\lambda-v(y)-v_{0}(y)\quad\text{ for all }x\in\sigma,v\in\tau^{\vee}.

Specializing this inequality to
x=0
gives
v(y)\geq 0
for all

v\in\tau^{\vee}
, whereas taking instead
v=0
gives

(u_{0}-y^{{\dagger}})(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\sigma
. Since
\tau
is
assumed to be closed,
y\in\tau

and
u_{0}-y^{{\dagger}}\in\sigma^{\vee}
, and so the dual problem

\mathcal{D}
is feasible. We also have that
\mathcal{D}
is
bounded because
\mathcal{P}
is feasible, thus proving the
condition (
2
). Moreover (
A.45
) specialized to
x=0

and
v=0
gives

\mathcal{D}\geq v_{0}(y)>\lambda,

and since
\lambda
can be arbitrarily close to
\mathcal{P}
we
obtain
\mathcal{P}\leq\mathcal{D}
. Combining with the weak duality
property (Proposition
A.5
) we conclude that

\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{D}
, as stated.

Finally, the case when (
2
) holds reduces to the previous
one using Remark
A.2
.
∎

Remark A.8
.

The proof of Theorem
5.3
follows this approach,
