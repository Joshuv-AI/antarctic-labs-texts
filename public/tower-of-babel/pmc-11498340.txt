A 33-year-old woman presented to our outpatient endocrinology clinic with worsening hypertension (clinic blood pressure reading 169/112 mm Hg) despite treatment with perindopril 4 mg daily. She reported a history of easy bruising without other symptoms of endocrine hormone excess, such as palpitation, anxiety, flushing, sweating, and headache. She had no history of fragility fractures or diabetes, and no family history of hypertension. The patient had generalized adiposity, with a body mass index (BMI) score of 30.9, but no other overt clinical features of Cushing syndrome such as facial plethora, striae, or proximal myopathy.

Several years prior, from the age of 26 years, the patient had undergone investigations for secondary hypertension in a hypertension clinic in Canada. Further investigations and management were not undertaken because she relocated to England. Clinical correspondence supplied to the patient reported that renal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) excluded renal artery stenosis and fibromuscular dysplasia, but a leftsided adrenal nodule measuring 0.9 × 0.5 cm was discovered, aldosterone-to-renin ratio was normal, 24-hour urinary free cortisol was elevated on 2 occasions, and a 1-mg dexamethasone suppression test was “positive.”

Given the patient’s history of young-onset hypertension and adrenal nodule, we performed laboratory studies to assess for adrenal hyperfunction. Morning serum cortisol levels were 517 and 673 (normal 200–500) nmol/L with corresponding adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) levels of 20 and 17 (normal 0–46) ng/L (4.4 and 3.74 [normal 0–10.13] pmol/L). Overnight and 48-hour low-dose dexamethasone suppression tests showed nonsuppressed cortisol levels at 318 (normal < 50) nmol/L and 285 (normal < 50) nmol/L, respectively, indicating pathologic hyper-cortisolism. Late-night salivary cortisol readings were high, indicating loss of normal circadian rhythm. Serum dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEAS) was 7.2 (normal 1–12) μmol/L.

We completed further investigations to exclude pheochromocytoma and primary aldosteronism. Plasma metadrenaline at 0.28 (normal < 0.33) nmol/L, normetadrenaline at 0.48 (normal < 1.07) nmol/L, and aldosterone-to-renin ratio (after replacing perindopril with doxazosin) at 563 (normal < 800) were normal. Hemoglobin A1c was 31 (normal ≤ 41) mmol/mol (5.0% [normal ≤ 5.9%]). The pituitary hormone profile, including gonadotrophins, thyrotropin, prolactin, and growth hormone levels, was unremarkable. The investigations so far indicated hypercortisolism, caused by either autonomous adrenal secretion (adrenal Cushing syndrome) from the patient’s known unilateral adrenal nodule, ACTH-dependent Cushing disease from an undiagnosed pituitary lesion, or ectopic ACTH-secreting neuroendocrine tumours (a rare cause of hypercortisolism).

Magnetic resonance imaging confirmed a left adrenal nodule (Figure 1A), raising suspicion of adrenal Cushing syndrome. However, we noted that ACTH and DHEAS levels were not suppressed as would be typically expected from negative feedback inhibition in adrenal Cushing syndrome. We therefore investigated for pituitary ACTH-dependent Cushing disease. Magnetic resonance imaging of the pituitary gland showed a right-sided 4-mm microadenoma (Figure 1B). Inferior petrosal sinus sampling (IPSS) confirmed pituitary ACTH hypersecretion with right-sided lateralization (Figure 2). Genetic testing for endocrine neoplasms including MEN1, RET, CDC73, CDKN1B, and AIP genes did not identify any pathogenic variants.

(A) Magnetic resonance image of the abdomen of a 33-year-old woman with hypertension and hypercortisolism showing an incidental non-functioning left adrenal adenoma 29 mm in maximum diameter (arrowhead). The contralateral adrenal gland was not atrophied (not shown). (B) Magnetic resonance image of the pituitary gland with contrast showing a right-sided microadenoma 4 mm in maximum diameter (arrowhead).

Results from simultaneous bilateral inferior petrosal sinus sampling (IPSS). The bars depict serial measurements of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) taken from peripheral vein (peripheral, black bars), left inferior petrosal sinus (left sinus, blue bars), and right inferior petrosal sinus (right sinus, orange bars) by radiologically guided catheter insertion. Unstimulated baseline ACTH concentrations were higher in the right IPSS and increased sharply after intravenous corticotropin-releasing hormone stimulation. A stimulated right IPSS–to–peripheral ACTH ratio greater than 3 confirms a diagnosis of Cushing disease and excludes ectopic ACTH secretion. The ACTH gradient between the right and left IPSS indicates tumour lateralization to the right, which corresponded with the imaging evidence of a right-sided pituitary microadenoma.

The patient underwent transsphenoidal endoscopic excision of the right-sided pituitary microadenoma. Histology confirmed corticotroph adenoma. Postoperative morning ACTH of 9 ng/L (1.98 pmol/L) and cortisol of 43 nmol/L confirmed biochemical remission. No pituitary tumour remnant was seen on postoperative MRI. As blood pressure normalized to 115/57 mm Hg, antihypertensive treatment (doxazosin) was stopped on the third postoperative day. The patient’s hypertension has remained in remission at a follow-up appointment 21 months after surgery. Her body weight has reduced by 20 kg (current BMI 24.7).

Discussion

Hypercortisolism, known eponymously as Cushing syndrome, is characterized by clinical manifestations of glucocorticoid excess. Patients can present with a spectrum of symptoms and signs, including central obesity, facial rounding and plethora, supraclavicular and dorsocervical fat pads, easy bruising, purple striae, proximal muscle weakness, emotional changes, hirsutism, osteoporosis, and metabolic syndrome including hypertension, hyperglycemia or type 2 diabetes, and hyperlipidemia. Many of these features are common in the general population, none are pathognomonic of Cushing syndrome, and the features may develop slowly over time.1 Thus, delayed diagnosis of early or mild Cushing syndrome is not uncommon. 2 Recognition becomes clinically less difficult when hypercortisolism is severe.

Screening tests for endogenous Cushing syndrome can include a 1-mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test, or measuring late-night salivary cortisol or 24-hour urinary free cortisol. 1,3 If any one test is positive, confirmatory testing is by a 48-hour low-dose dexamethasone suppression test.3

Cushing disease, defined as hypercortisolism due to excessive ACTH secretion from a pituitary tumour, is the most common cause (accounts for > 80% of cases) of endogenous Cushing syndrome.4 Other causes include ACTH-independent Cushing syndrome from an autonomous adrenal lesion, and very rarely ectopic ACTH secretion from neuroendocrine tumours such as pulmonary carcinoid tumours, islet cell pancreatic tumours, medullary thyroid cancer, thymic tumours, and small cell bronchogenic tumours, which present with unmistakable, aggressive, rapidly progressive disease. Rarely, cyclical Cushing syndrome due to intermittent hypercortisolism can occur. Iatrogenic causes and pseudo-Cushing states driven by alcohol excess, obesity, or other chronic diseases are important differential diagnoses.4

The cause of hypercortisolism is classified by ACTH dependence, making ACTH a key marker for localization. However, the interpretation of ACTH levels can be nuanced. Patients with Cushing disease do not always exhibit levels above the reference range. Levels of ACTH can vary, and while they are often high-normal, midrange readings can cause diagnostic uncertainty. 3,5 Similarly, ACTH is not always suppressed in adrenal hypercortisolism and can present with levels in the low-normal range.6 Therefore, in our patient, ACTH levels did not clearly differentiate between a pituitary and adrenal cause of hypercortisolism. The secretion of DHEAS, a steroid hormone precursor to androgens and estrogens synthesized by the adrenal cortex, is regulated by ACTH. Thus, DHEAS is typically suppressed in adrenal hypercortisolism. Normal DHEAS levels in a patient with hypercortisolism should prompt investigation for a pituitary cause.5 In our patient, the presence of an adrenal nodule initially suggested adrenal Cushing syndrome. However, non-suppressed ACTH and high-normal DHEAS levels prompted further evaluation for a pituitary cause.

Identifying the underlying cause of Cushing syndrome can be challenging, yet important, as there is a distinct therapeutic approach to each. Adrenocorticotropic hormone–independent Cushing syndrome from an adrenal lesion is treated with adrenalectomy. Unilateral adrenalectomy provides a cure rate of nearly 100% when performed by experienced adrenal surgeons.6 Transsphenoidal surgery is the first-line treatment for Cushing disease, which induces remission in most cases.4,6

Magnetic resonance imaging is the preferred imaging modality to identify Cushing disease, typically caused by pituitary microadenomas.4,6 However, standard MRI can fail to identify smaller lesions. Higher resolution MRI with dynamic sequences can help to identify and localize pituitary tumours.4 In our patient, a right-sided 4-mm pituitary microadenoma was clearly seen on MRI. However, incidental, nonfunctioning pituitary adenomas are not uncommon in the population. Thus, current guidance on diagnosis of Cushing disease recommends IPSS as further confirmatory testing in the presence of pituitary microadenoma (< 10 mm).4

Simultaneous bilateral IPSS, performed in high-volume centres owing to complexity and intricacy, is the most accurate procedure in the differential diagnosis of ACTH-dependent hypercortisolism compared with clinical, biochemical, and imaging analyses, with a sensitivity and specificity of 88%–100% and 67%–100%, respectively.7 It relies on the anatomically ipsilateral venous drainage such that the side of the pituitary tumour has high concentration of ACTH compared with the contralateral side where ACTH secretion is low due to chronic suppression. Intravenous corticotrophin-releasing hormone is used to stimulate ACTH release, causing a surge in ACTH level on the affected side, as illustrated in our patient. This also helps to exclude ectopic ACTH secretion wherein levels are high in peripheral blood samples and typically suppressed in inferior petrosal sinus samples.4

Most adrenal nodules are incidental findings on imaging investigations.8 Adrenal tumours less than 4 cm in maximum diameter and with a density less than 20 Hounsfield units on dedicated noncontrast computed tomography are considered benign10 and have slow rate of growth over a long period of follow-up.11 Magnetic resonance imaging is favoured in younger patients to avoid exposure to ionizing radiation. Our patient’s MRI scans showed features indicating the presence of fat and a benign rate of tumour growth over a 6-year period, confirming its benign nature.

Hypertension can be the first presenting feature in endocrine disease.1 Whereas screening for acromegaly, thyroid dysfunction, or hyperparathyroidism should be considered with clinical context,1 the Endocrine Society recommends screening for primary aldosteronism, Cushing syndrome, and pheochromocytoma in patients most at risk of these conditions or in the presence of adrenal tumours.8 About 15% of people with hypertension have secondary hypertension, which is defined as hypertension due to an underlying cause (Appendix 1, Supplementary Table 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.240341/tab-related-content).1,9 More than 50% of children and 30% of young adults (< 40 yr) presenting with hypertension have a secondary cause1 and should be targeted for screening. Other features that warrant screening include acute worsening of stable hypertension, resistant hypertension, or presence of severe hypertension-mediated organ damage such as cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease, nephropathy, and retinopathy.9 Although our patient had neither resistant hypertension nor organ damage, she warranted investigations for secondary causes owing to young age at onset and presence of an adrenal lesion.

Hypertension, present in 75%–80% of patients with Cushing syndrome, can resolve with successful treatment of hypercortisolism. 1 Our patient had definitive treatment performed 8 years after her initial presentation with hypertension and remains in remission and off antihypertensive treatment. Substantial weight loss is frequently observed after surgical remission of hypercortisolism, as observed in this patient, which underscores the effectiveness of definitive treatment in normalizing cortisol levels and reversing the metabolic abnormalities associated with Cushing syndrome.12

Delayed diagnosis and treatment of Cushing syndrome and other forms of secondary hypertension are not uncommon. This case highlights the approach to obtaining the correct diagnosis and the impact of treatment on weight and metabolic health in patients with hypercortisolism.
