Agenesis of the left pulmonary artery and cardiac hydatid cysts: A dual rarity
Authors: Soufiane Benaazza, Lina Belkouchi, Siham Elhaddad, Nazik Allali, Latifa Chat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Unilateral pulmonary artery agenesis is a rare congenital anomaly with variable clinical presentations, most often diagnosed by CT angiography. Cardiac hydatid disease is also an uncommon entity, with manifestations depending on cyst location, and imaging plays a pivotal role in diagnosis and follow-up. We report the case of a 45-year-old woman admitted for acute-onset dyspnea in whom thoracic CT angiography revealed agenesis of the left pulmonary artery and cardiac hydatid cysts. Additional findings included mosaic perfusion suggestive of pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), and infected bronchiectasis, likely explaining the acute respiratory deterioration. The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit for further management. This case highlights an exceptional association of 2 rare entities and emphasizes the importance of multimodality imaging for accurate diagnosis, assessment of complications, and therapeutic planning. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of such an association, which appears to be coincidental rather than pathophysiologically linked.

Introduction

Unilateral pulmonary artery agenesis is a rare congenital anomaly, estimated to occur in approximately 1 in 200,000 individuals, with a clinical spectrum ranging from incidental discovery on imaging to recurrent respiratory infections, hemoptysis, or pulmonary hypertension; its diagnosis relies primarily on imaging, particularly chest CT angiography [1]. Cardiac hydatid disease, caused by Echinococcus granulosus, is an uncommon condition that accounts for fewer than 2% of all reported hydatid disease cases and remains more prevalent in regions where livestock farming is common, notably around the Mediterranean basin; it may remain asymptomatic or present with variable symptoms depending on the location of the cysts, with diagnosis and follow-up guided mainly by CT and MRI [2]. Awareness of these rare entities is important for accurate diagnosis and optimal management.

Case presentation

We report the case of a 45-year-old woman, with no significant medical history, who presented to the emergency department with acute-onset dyspnea. On physical examination, the patient was dyspneic (respiratory rate: 25/min) and tachycardic (heart rate: 130 beats/min), with stable blood pressure (110/70 mmHg). Chest radiography revealed a mild cardiomegaly, associated with small, partially calcified masses at the level of the left ventricle and the homolateral subdiaphragmatic region. The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit for hemodynamic monitoring and supportive care. Hydatid serology (ELISA) was subsequently performed and returned positive, supporting the diagnosis of hydatid disease, so Albendazole (200 mg twice daily) was started promptly as antiparasitic treatment and maintained pre-operatively, with the dual aim of sterilizing the cysts and minimizing the risk of parasitic dissemination during the procedure. An abdominal CT performed as part of the extension workup revealed no extracardiac localization. The patient was subsequently scheduled for open cardiac surgical resection of the cysts.

Discussion

Unilateral agenesis of a pulmonary artery (UAPA) is a rare condition, thought to result from an embryological abnormality caused by failure of migration and rotation of the primitive sixth aortic arch. Absence of the right pulmonary artery is twice as common as the absence of the left pulmonary artery, but the latter is more frequently associated with other malformations, particularly cardiac anomalies such as Tetralogy of Fallot or aortic coarctation [1,3]. The vascular supply of the affected lung relies on bronchial arteries, large aortopulmonary collateral arteries, and other systemic arteries [4].

Most cases are diagnosed during childhood, but in some asymptomatic patients, the condition is only discovered in adulthood [1,3]. The most frequently observed symptoms are recurrent pulmonary infections and dyspnea. More rarely, cases of hemoptysis or signs of pulmonary hypertension may be observed [4].

Chest radiography is the recommended initial examination for the diagnosis of unilateral pulmonary artery agenesis. Typical radiological signs include a reduced volume of the affected hemithorax, mediastinal shift, and elevation of the ipsilateral diaphragm, along with absence of visualization of the corresponding pulmonary artery [5]. Echocardiography helps confirm the diagnosis by detecting pulmonary hypertension, and ruling out associated cardiac anomalies [3,5].

CT angiography remains the reference technique for confirming the diagnosis, as it visualizes the absence of the pulmonary artery and depicts the collateral vessels arising from the descending thoracic aorta, as well as any pulmonary parenchymal abnormalities [1,3].

Management of isolated UAPA depends on the clinical presentation and associated conditions and may be medical or surgical. Forms complicated by persistent pulmonary infections or recurrent hemoptysis may sometimes require pulmonary resection, such as lobectomy or pneumonectomy. In cases of massive hemoptysis, bronchial artery embolization is generally preferred as the treatment of choice [3].

Differentiation of pulmonary artery agenesis from its radiological mimics relies primarily on assessment of vessel continuity at the expected arterial origin. True agenesis is defined by complete congenital absence of the pulmonary artery from its origin, without a proximal stump, and is typically accompanied by compensatory systemic collateral circulation. Chronic thromboembolic occlusion, by contrast, characteristically demonstrates a residual arterial stump or organized intraluminal thrombus, while postsurgical loss is inferred from a documented history of prior intervention with corresponding operative changes. Pulmonary artery hypoplasia represents a distinct entity in which the vessel remains patent and in continuity with the pulmonary trunk, albeit with significantly reduced caliber [6].

Cardiac Echinococcosis is an uncommon manifestation of hydatid disease, accounting for fewer than 2% of reported cases. It is caused by a parasitic infection with Echinococcus granulosus, which can involve any organ, although the liver and lungs are most frequently affected [7,8]. Cardiac involvement results from myocardial invasion, most commonly via the coronary arterial circulation. A secondary pathway of infection involves the pulmonary veins, following rupture of pulmonary hydatid cysts into the pulmonary venous system [9].

The left ventricular wall is the most common location for cardiac hydatid cyst development. Less frequently, cysts may also occur in the right ventricular wall, left atrial wall, interventricular septum, pericardium, and pulmonary trunk [8].

The clinical manifestations of cardiac hydatid cysts are highly variable and largely determined by their anatomical location. Many patients remain asymptomatic because of the slow growth of the cysts, while others may experience nonspecific precordial discomfort [2,7]. Moreover, hydatid disease affecting the interventricular septum may lead to conduction abnormalities. Those within the left ventricular myocardium are often subpericardial and therefore have a tendency to rupture into the pericardial space, potentially causing inflammatory pericarditis and, in severe cases, cardiac tamponade. In contrast, right ventricular hydatid cysts typically grow in a subendocardial and intracavitary pattern and are more prone to rupture, which may result in anaphylactic shock or sudden death. Additionally, dissemination of cyst contents can lead to life-threatening pulmonary or aortic embolism [8,9].

Imaging is essential to the diagnosis of cardiac hydatid cysts. Transthoracic echocardiography typically shows a well-defined cystic, spherical mass, depending on its type, sometimes simple or containing detached membranes, daughter cysts or a calcified wall [8]. CT confirms the cystic nature, its relation to cardiac chambers, and any wall calcifications, as was the case in our patient. MRI was not performed in the present case; when available, it can further delineate the anatomical location and internal cyst structure and is useful for post-treatment follow-up, typically depicting oval lesions that are hypointense on T1weighted and hyperintense on T2-weighted images, with a characteristic hypointense peripheral rim corresponding to the pericyst [9].

Several etiologies should be considered in the differential diagnosis, including left ventricular aneurysm, myxoma, fibroma, rhabdomyoma, and cardiac sarcoma. Intracardiac thrombus and pericardial calcifications from other causes should also be considered [9,10].

Surgical intervention remains the treatment of choice for hydatid disease. In addition to antihelminthic therapy administered before and after surgery, complete removal of the lesion under cardiopulmonary bypass is recommended [9].

The acute dyspnea observed in this patient was primarily attributed to superimposed infection of pre-existing bronchiectasis, occurring against a background of chronically reduced cardiopulmonary reserve secondary to long-standing UAPA-associated pulmonary arterial hypertension. The cardiac hydatid cysts may have constituted an additional contributing factor. This presentation is therefore considered multifactorial in etiology.

To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first reported case of this specific association. A systematic literature search was performed in the PubMed/MEDLINE database on (January 12, 2026), using the terms “pulmonary artery agenesis,” “cardiac hydatid cyst,” “cardiac echinococcosis,” and “echinococcosis,” without restriction on publication date or language. No previously published case reporting the coexistence of unilateral pulmonary artery agenesis and cardiac hydatid disease was identified, suggesting that this association is exceptional and most likely coincidental, with no established pathophysiological link between the 2 entities.

Conclusion

Unilateral pulmonary artery agenesis and cardiac hydatid disease are both rare entities that may remain asymptomatic and be incidentally discovered in adulthood. While pulmonary artery agenesis is a congenital vascular anomaly best diagnosed and evaluated using chest CT angiography, cardiac hydatid disease is a parasitic condition whose clinical manifestations vary with cyst location and whose diagnosis and follow-up rely primarily on CT and MRI. Awareness of these rare conditions is essential to ensure accurate diagnosis and to guide appropriate management.

Teaching points

•Unilateral pulmonary artery agenesis may remain clinically silent into adulthood and can mimic pulmonary embolism at presentation; absence of the pulmonary artery on CT angiography, rather than an intraluminal filling defect, should prompt consideration of this diagnosis.•Cardiac hydatid disease should be included in the differential diagnosis of calcified intracardiac masses, particularly in endemic regions, even when serology has not yet been obtained—imaging findings alone are often sufficient to guide initial management.•The coexistence of rare entities does not imply a pathophysiological link; a systematic, imaging-driven approach to each abnormality remains essential even when their association appears exceptional.
