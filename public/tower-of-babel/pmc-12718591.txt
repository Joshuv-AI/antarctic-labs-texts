Non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) and COPD: a multicentre prospective study
Authors: Eva Tabernero, Amaia Aramburu, Raquel Sanchez, Borja Santos, Nerea Ortiz, Ines Lopez de Calle, Larraitz Garcia, Elena Urra, Patricia Sobradillo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAn increase in airway isolates of non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) has been observed, particularly in patients with previous lung damage. Inhaled corticosteroids may increase the risk of NTM lung disease. NTM isolation is of therapeutic importance, especially when macrolides are used. There are few data on the actual prevalence of NTM isolation in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).ObjectiveTo determine the prevalence of NTM isolation and NTM pulmonary disease according to the ATS/ERS/IDSA 2020 criteria in patients with high-risk COPD. As a secondary objective, we sought to identify risk factors for NTM isolation and developing NTM pulmonary disease in patients with COPD.MethodsProspective multicentre observational study based on the collection of three sputum samples in a year, for standard, mycobacteria and fungi cultures, in patients with high-risk COPD (postbronchodilator forced expiratory volume in 1 s<50% and/or ≥2 exacerbations in the previous year), with a 12-month follow-up. Patients with at least two good-quality samples were included.Results305 patients were initially selected, of which only 258 had at least two valid samples. NTM was isolated in 15% of patients (n=39), though only 8 (3%) met the ATS 2020 criteria for NTM disease. The most commonly isolated species was mycobacterium avium complex (MAC). Multivariate analysis identified the following risk factors for NTM isolation: low body weight, alpha-1 antitrypsin (AAT) deficiency, inhaled corticosteroids and cancer. NTM disease was only associated with body mass index <21.ConclusionsNTM isolation is more common than expected in patients with COPD and may have implications for treatment. It is associated with low body weight, AAT deficiency, inhaled corticosteroid use and cancer.Keywords: COPD epidemiology, Alpha1 Antitrypsin Deficiency, Atypical Mycobacterial Infection, Clinical Epidemiology

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a major global cause of morbidity and mortality, with rising incidence due to ageing populations and persistent risk factors like smoking and biomass exposure. Inhaled corticosteroids (ICSs) are known to reduce exacerbations and improve symptoms and quality of life in patients with COPD and are recommended by guidelines, although their use varies considerably in clinical practice.1,3 On the other hand, they can impair immune responses, increasing the risk of respiratory infections, including pneumonia. These risks are higher in patients with chronic bronchial infection and low eosinophil counts (<100 cells/µL).4

Non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) are widely disseminated in nature and show low-to-moderate pathogenicity depending on host factors. Nevertheless, the incidence of isolation in respiratory samples and NTM pulmonary disease (NTM-PD) is increasing worldwide, by up to 4% annually, according to a recent meta-analysis, although with significant variability across different geographical areas.5 Structural lung diseases such as COPD and bronchiectasis are well-established risk factors for NTM-PD.6 A UK study found NTM isolation was significantly linked to COPD (OR=7).7 The prevalence of patients with COPD diagnosed with NTM-PD varies from 68% in US veterans to 40% of patients hospitalised with NTM in Germany and 23% in northern Spain.8,10 Factors linking COPD with NTM-PD include structural changes (eg, mucus plugging, impaired mucociliary clearance), immune dysfunction, especially involving alveolar macrophages and Toll-like receptors, and disruption of T-cell regulation, including PD-1 pathways.

Despite the clinical implications of NTM isolation in patients with COPD, since ICS may increase the risk of NTM-PD, and macrolide use should be stopped, clinical guidelines pay little or no attention to them and no prospective studies have evaluated the prevalence of NTM isolation in patients with stable COPD. For this reason, we designed this study to determine the prevalence of NTM isolation in sputum samples and NTM-PD defined according to the ATS/ERS/IDSA 2020 criteria11: (1) presence of compatible symptoms (cough, fever, weight loss) not attributable to other diseases; (2) characteristic findings on chest radiography (infiltrate, nodules and cavitation) or high-resolution CT scan (multifocal bronchiectasis with multiple nodules) and (3) positive culture results from at least two separate expectorated sputum samples or one bronchial wash or lavage or lung biopsy sample, in patients with high-risk COPD (defined according to the Spanish COPD Guidelines (GesEPOC)2 as forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) <50% and/or at least two moderate or severe exacerbations per year). As a secondary objective, we sought to identify risk factors for respiratory NTM isolation in patients with COPD and associated pathogens, especially gram-negative bacilli and Aspergillus, and their interaction with NTM.

Methods

Design: Prospective observational multicentre study with collection of demographic, clinical, comorbidity and microbiological data, and a minimum follow-up period of 12 months.

Intervention: Collection of three spontaneous sputum samples from these patients within 1 year, each separated by at least 1 month. Each sample underwent standard, NTM and fungal culture. For inclusion, it was required that at least two samples representative of the lower respiratory tract were valid, based on the Murray and Washington criteria.12

Study population

Patients with COPD from Basurto, Galdakao and Cruces University Hospitals, meeting the following inclusion criteria: aged 40–80 years, diagnosed with high-risk COPD according to GesEPOC criteria (postbronchodilator FEV1/forced vital capacity ratio <0.7 and postbronchodilator FEV1 <50% and/or ≥2 exacerbations in the previous year), and able to expectorate, as well as provision of consent to participate. The study was conducted between 2020 and 2024.

Exclusion criteria: Patient refusal or inability to provide sputum samples, and pre-existing diagnosis of PD due to NTM.

Data collection

We recorded data on demographic characteristics, medication and baseline comorbidities. Heart disease included ischaemic disease, congestive heart failure and arrhythmia. Cancer was defined as malignancy with treatment still ongoing or within the previous 5 years. Patients were considered smokers if they had cumulative tobacco exposure >10 pack-years. Low weight was defined as a body mass index (BMI) <21 kg/m2. We classified patients as immunocompromised if they were transplant recipients, had haematological malignancies or used systemic steroids, specifically, ≥7.5 mg/day of prednisone for >3 months or other immunosuppressive treatments, including anti-TNF treatments.

We also collected data on pulmonary function and the Body-Mass Index, Degree of Airflow Obstruction and Dyspnea, and Exercise (BODE) index, COPD impact on quality of life as measured using the COPD Assessment Test (CAT), dyspnoea level as measured using the modified Medical Research Council scale, blood test results, including absolute eosinophil count and measurement of serum alpha-1 antitrypsin (AAT) levels in mg/dL, thoracic CT, identifying the presence of bronchiectasis and/or pulmonary emphysema, and any moderate or severe exacerbations in the previous 2 years.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct or dissemination plan of the study.

Microbiological analysis

Spontaneous sputum was obtained during the stable phase. For the digestion and decontamination of respiratory samples, NaOH and N-acetyl-L-cysteine were used (BD BBL MycoPrep system; Becton Dickinson, Maryland, USA).

All samples were cultured in liquid medium using the Bactec MGIT960 system (Becton Dickinson) and on Löwenstein-Jensen solid medium (ThermoFisher, Lenexa, Kansas). For the identification of NTM, we used matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionisation time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS; MALDI Biotyper, Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). The GenoType NTM-DR test (HAIN Lifescience, Nehren, Germany) was used for subspecies differentiation and detection of macrolide and aminoglycoside resistance. For isolates that could not be identified using the above methods, the 16S rRNA, hsp-65 and rpoβ genes were sequenced.

Phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed by broth microdilution using Mueller-Hinton culture medium. RAPMYCOI AST and SLOWMYCOI AST panels (Sensititre, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) were used for rapidly and slowly growing mycobacteria, respectively.

Samples were also routinely cultured for bacterial and fungal isolation according to standard methodology using blood, chocolate, MacConkey and Sabouraud-chloramphenicol agar (bioMérieux, Marcy l’Etoile, France).

Statistical analysis

A sample size calculation was performed for the catchment population of the three hospitals aged 40–80 years in Bizkaia, approximately 500 000 people according to Basque Statistics Institute data.13 We assumed a prevalence rate of COPD with FEV1 >50% of 0.4%, based on the latest COPD prevalence studies,14 and of NTM isolates of 22%, based on data from a small study in COPD with exacerbations.15 Sample size calculations estimated 299 patients would be required to detect significant differences, assuming a 20% error (epsilon) and considering a 95% confidence level.

Qualitative variables were represented as absolute and relative frequencies, normally distributed quantitative variables as mean and SD, and non-normally distributed variables as median and quartiles 1 and 3. Comparisons between groups with and without NTM isolation were performed with the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test for qualitative variables as appropriate. For quantitative variables, analysis of variance was performed if the data were normally distributed, and otherwise, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used.

In the multivariate logistic regression model, predictors were selected with univariate models (p≤0.15). Correlation and collinearity analysis was performed (Spearman’s, Cramer’s V and variance inflation factor coefficients) and the final model was constructed using significant variables. Goodness-of-fit was tested with the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. The significance threshold was set at p<0.05. R statistical software V.4.0.1 was used.

Results

A total of 397 patients were evaluated. Three sputum samples were successfully collected in only 305 cases, and further, 30% of the samples collected were of poor quality. The flow of patients is shown in figure 1. Only 258 patients were finally included in the study. Their mean age was 70.4±9 years and 179 (69.4%) were male. Overall, 22% of the patients were active smokers, 51% met criteria for chronic bronchitis and 25% also met criteria for asthma. 25 patients were diagnosed with severe AAT deficiency with AAT levels below 60 mg/L. The main baseline data are summarised in table 1. Comparing patients in whom valid samples were and were not obtained, we did not observe significant differences except in the rates of chronic bronchitis and bronchiectasis.

Patients with NTM isolation

NTM were isolated in 15% of patients (n=39). We have not taken into account Mycobacterium gordonae isolates since they rarely cause disease and most authors do not include them as pathogens.16,18 The species isolated were mostly slow-growing: Mycobacterium avium complex (23 patients), Mycobacterium lentiflavum (3), Mycobacterium kansasii (2), Mycobacterium paragordonae (2), Mycobacterium simiae. The fast-growing species isolated were: Mycobacterium fortuitum (2 cases), Mycobacterium peregrinum (2), Mycobacterium septicum, Mycobacterium abscessus (sp masiliense), Mycobacterium chelonae and Mycobacterium senegalense.

Table 2 summarises the differences between patients with and without NTM isolation. No statistically significant differences were found in age or sex; however, a lower BMI (<21 kg/m2) was significantly more common in patients with NTM isolation. Notably, NTM isolation was significantly more likely in patients receiving ICS, with no significant differences related to other treatments. Regarding comorbidities, there were no significant between-group differences except a higher rate of NTM isolation in patients with cancer. This difference persisted when analysing only patients with lung cancer, although it lost statistical significance. Fungal and bacterial coinfection rates did not differ between the groups.

In the multivariate analysis, four independent factors were found to be associated with NTM isolation: diagnosis of severe AAT deficiency (OR 5.95; 95% CI 1.61 to 15.61; p=0.004), BMI <21 (OR 3.07; 95% CI 1.09 to 8.21; p=0.027), use of ICS (OR 8.45; 95% CI 1.59 to 158.1; p=0.045) and coexistence of solid tumour in the last 5 years (OR 2.71; 95% CI 1.22 to 6.02; p=0.013). The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test for logistic regression gave a good p value of 0.86353. In the ROC curve analysis, the area under the curve was 0.71 (poor discrimination) (see figure 2).

Patients with NTM-PD

Eight patients (3.1%) were diagnosed with NTM-PD, with an average age of 66.6 years, and half of them were women. The mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) was the most commonly isolated species, found in 75% of cases, while one patient had M. kansasii, and another had M. simiae. A bronchiectasis-nodular pattern was detected in 62% of patients, and a cavitary pattern in the others. No resistance to macrolides or aminoglycosides was detected (table 2). The sample of patients with NTM-PD was too small for statistical analysis and the only factor that was significantly more common in patients with NTM-PD was low weight.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first prospective multicentre study that analyses the frequency of NTM isolation in patients with stable COPD. Regarding the main variable, prevalence of NTM in sputum of patients with high-risk COPD, at least one NTM was isolated from sputum in 15.1% of patients (n=39), a sufficiently high rate to be taken into account in the routine clinical management of patients with COPD of these characteristics. This figure is slightly lower than the 22% found in a study by Hoefsloot in 73 patients with COPD with an exacerbation,15 although 66% of isolated NTM in that study were M. gordonae, which is rarely pathogenic and almost always contaminating, and hence, has not been taken into account in our study. In a previous publication in 2007 in patients with COPD exacerbation using only solid culture medium, Ko et al found a much lower rate of 1.4%.19

In a small retrospective study of 44 patients with severe COPD who underwent endoscopic volume reduction, 18% were found to have NTM during the procedure.20 Similarly, in a study of surgical specimens from severe emphysema volume reduction surgery in 126 patients, 10% of histologic specimens had evidence of unsuspected NTM infection.21

Many more studies analyse the existence of COPD as a risk factor starting from populations of patients with disease or isolation of NTM. Previous studies in Spain have described COPD in 31%–35% of patients with NTM isolation.9 22 A recent meta-analysis grouping 49 studies and 117 000 patients with NTM-PD shows a COPD prevalence of 28%.23 According to this meta-analysis, patients with NTM-PD were six times more likely to have COPD than patients without NTM disease, which is consistent with the data published by Loebinger et al in their systematic review of risk factors for NTM-PD (OR 6.6).6

The most commonly isolated species was M. avium complex, accounting for 58% of cases. This finding is consistent with data from other countries and has been linked to the global rise in NTM isolates.24 25

There were no significant differences between patients with COPD with and without NTM isolation regarding clinical characteristics, or symptoms such as dyspnoea, CAT score or functional severity (BODE index, diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide test (DLCO)% or FEV1%). On the other hand, a BMI <21 kg/m2 was strongly associated with NTM isolation in severe patients with COPD and remained significant in multivariate analysis. This relationship is well-documented as a risk factor in the general population for both NTM isolation and NTM-PD.726,29

One proposed mechanism for the association between low body fat and NTM-PD is deficiency of leptin, a fat-derived satiety hormone. Leptin is known to skew undifferentiated T cells toward the interferon-gamma-producing Th1 phenotype. Patients with NTM-PD have reduced serum leptin levels and elevated adiponectin levels. Leptin-deficient mice are also more susceptible to experimental M. abscessus lung infection.27 30 Moreover, malnutrition affects innate and adaptive immunity, worsening this vulnerability as seen in patients with tuberculosis.

Another factor significantly associated with NTM isolation in the multivariate analysis was AAT deficiency. All patients included in the study classified as having AAT deficiency were severely deficient, with baseline AAT levels <60 mg/dL, and all patients with NTM isolation were receiving augmentative treatment. Beyond its known role as a protease inhibitor, AAT exhibits several non-proteinase-mediated effects that contribute to its immunomodulatory functions. These include inhibition of neutrophil recruitment by interleukin 8 and leukotriene B4, reducing chemotaxis with effects on T and B cell function, and decreasing TNF activity. It appears that AAT plays a protective role against mycobacteria by binding to the glucocorticoid receptor, stimulating the production of cytokines (IL-1β, IL-23) and granulocyte-monocyte colony-stimulating factor. It also reduces the load of M. intracellulare inside macrophages, favouring phagosome-lysosome binding and the induction of autophagy.31

Although AAT is mentioned in multiple reviews as a risk factor for NTM infection, there are hardly any original studies that support this association. There are in vitro studies in which the AAT administration improves the elimination of M. abscessus in macrophages by up to 65%. In another investigation, macrophages from PiZZ subjects incubated in autologous plasma after AAT infusion were better able to control M. intracellulare infection than those obtained before infusion.32

Regarding human studies, we have only identified a cohort of 100 patients with rapidly growing NTM disease, mostly M. abscessus, in which 27% of patients had abnormalities in AAT levels.32 Our results provide novel clinical data in these patients on the increased risk of NTM isolation in patients with AAT deficiency.

We also identified the diagnosis of active cancer within the last 5 years as a significant risk factor for NTM isolation in multivariate analysis. Some previous studies have reported this relationship and there are multiple hypotheses to explain it. On the one hand, a possible carcinogenic role of low-grade inflammation caused by NTM infection has been suggested, as is the case for M. tuberculosis. Nonetheless, a recent genome-wide study failed to confirm this hypothesis and found no significant causal association between NTM infection and cancer risk. Another explanation for this cancer-NTM association could be the immunosuppression via neutrophilic pathway associated with conventional chemotherapy that could justify the lower clearance of NTM once inhaled in these patients.33

Our study observed that more than 50% of patients had bacterial or fungal coinfection, but no link was found between positive bacterial cultures and NTM isolation, either generally or by specific germs. Previous literature includes one cystic fibrosis study associating Strenotrophomonas maltophilia29 with NTM isolation, and multiple studies on NTM and aspergillosis coinfection in bronchiectasis patients, a phenomenon that we have not been able to confirm with our data.34

When analysing treatments and the risk of developing NTM isolation, we observed a clear increase in risk associated with the use of ICS, which remained significant in the multivariate analysis. This risk has been extensively discussed in the literature since the early observations by Hojo in 2012, in asthma patients in Japan that patients with NTM infections were more likely to have used higher doses of fluticasone (>800 µg) than those used in asthma patients without NTM isolation.34 Andrejak’s population-based case-control study in Denmark (1997–2008) found a 16-fold higher NTM-PD risk in patients with chronic respiratory disease using ICS.35 Similar studies using administrative databases have confirmed this association, particularly with fluticasone, but not with budesonide.7 35 A meta-analysis of four studies found that ICS use increased NTM risk (OR 3.93; 95% CI: 2.12 to 7.27), especially at high doses (OR=2.27) and with fluticasone (OR=2.42).36 In our cohort, both fluticasone and budesonide were linked to increased NTM risk in univariate analysis, though this was not significant in multivariate models.

ICS are known to increase pneumonia and NTM risk through various mechanisms. They can alter gamma-interferon production, suppress the epithelial secretion of antimicrobial peptide cathelicidin, induce epithelial apoptosis and disrupt airway microbiota via dysbiosis.36 Our findings underscore the need to carefully evaluate the risk–benefit ratio of using inhaled steroids, particularly in patients with AAT deficiency and low body weight.

We did not find significant differences regarding macrolide use between patients with and without NTM isolation. Although early studies suggested that macrolide use could increase the risk of M. abscessus infection in patients with cystic fibrosis, due to its effect on phagolysosomal degradation and suppression of IL-12, IFN-γ and TNF-α production, a recent meta-analysis points to a slight protective effect, although without statistical significance.6

Long-term use of macrolides is indicated in patients with COPD who have had at least three exacerbations in the previous year despite optimal inhaled therapy, due to their effect in reducing exacerbations.2 There is consensus in the guidelines that, following an NTM isolation, it is prudent to discontinue this treatment until NTM-PD can be confirmed or ruled out, as macrolides are an essential component of the treatment for this disease. Their use as monotherapy and at low doses may promote the development of resistance.17 In a recent international survey, only 15% of physicians reported requesting an NTM culture before initiating macrolide therapy, and fewer than 10% did so before prescribing ICS.37

This study has some limitations. First, a significant proportion—15% of the patients initially included in the study—failed to provide valid high-quality samples for analysis. This reduced the sample size and may have introduced selection bias. Nonetheless, in the comparative analysis of these patients versus those ultimately included, we found no significant differences in baseline severity, comorbidities or treatments, except for a lower prevalence of bronchiectasis and chronic bronchitis.

Regarding the number of NTM isolations, given that these are ubiquitous microorganisms, some of the isolations could be due to contamination during processing. This might lead to overestimation of the prevalence of NTM isolation, although not that of NTM-PD.

Another limitation is that, although this is a multicentre study, it was conducted in geographically close areas. Considering that the distribution of mycobacteria varies by region and depends on local temperature and humidity conditions, the prevalence results in other COPD populations may differ.

We did not collect data on ICS dosage or treatment duration, which would have allowed us to estimate cumulative dose and potentially stratify risk more effectively. It should be noted that, in our study, 16% of patients switched corticosteroid type during follow-up.

Although we have excluded patients with previously diagnosed NTM-PD, we could only calculate prevalence because NTM was isolated from the first sample in some patients and we cannot rule out previous NTM colonisation, given the absence of previous cultures and the insidious clinical manifestations, similar to the underlying pathology.

Finally, we also did not collect data on prior tuberculosis, which is a known risk factor described in several previous studies, including among patients with COPD, and which forms part of the predictive model developed by Wang et al to identify NTM-PD in patients with COPD,28 that also includes variables such as emergency visits, haemoptysis, cough, cancer and low body weight, achieving a sensitivity and specificity of around 80%.

Conclusions

The isolation of NTM and presence of NTM-PD are more frequent than previously suspected in patients with severe COPD or frequent exacerbations. NTM isolation was significantly associated with low body weight, severe AAT deficiency, use of ICSs and a diagnosis of cancer. These findings underline the need to periodically screen for NTM in such patients during follow-up—especially in those treated with macrolides or who have the aforementioned risk factors.

Early detection of NTM isolation in patients with COPD requires CT imaging and repeated cultures, along with reinforcing physiotherapy and bronchial hygiene measures to facilitate NTM clearance. It also implies discontinuation of macrolide therapy if present, and reassessment and minimisation of ICS dosing by evaluating the individual risk–benefit ratio.
