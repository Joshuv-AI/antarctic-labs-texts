Cognivue Clarity characterizes mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer’s disease in biomarker confirmed cohorts in the Bio-Hermes Study
Authors: James E Galvin, Michael J Kleiman, Paul W Estes, Heather M Harris, Ernest Fung
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Bio-Hermes Study was a cross-sectional observational study designed to develop a database of blood-based and digital biomarkers to improve detection of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and mild cognitive impairment (MCI). We examined the ability of Cognivue Clarity to (a) detect MCI and AD in clinical diagnostics groups, (b) determine the presence of amyloid, and (c) distinguish between biomarker-confirmed groups. Bio-Hermes enrolled 887 participants who completed both Cognivue Clarity and amyloid PET scans (388 Cognitively Normal, 282 MCI, 217 Probable AD). Cognivue Clarity differentiated between Cognitively Normal, MCI, and probable AD in clinical cohorts, amyloid positive from amyloid negative individuals, and True Controls from MCI due to AD and AD in biomarker-confirmed cohorts (all p < 0.001) with large effect sizes. Cognivue Clarity correlated with amyloid PET and plasma amyloid and pTau (all p < 0.001). In biomarker confirmed groups, Cognivue Clarity had a positive likelihood ratio of 2.17, a negative likelihood ratio of 0.29, and a diagnostic odds ratio of 7.48. Cognivue Clarity detected cognitive impairment and differentiated between both clinically and biomarker defined MCI and AD groups. The use of Cognivue Clarity could assist with identification of MCI-AD or AD for inclusion into current treatment protocols or for enriching recruitment into clinical trials. Introduction

Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) and related dementias affect nearly 7 million people in the US and more than 55 million people worldwide1. Detection of the early stages of AD in the community is a clinical challenge with many people coming to medical attention and diagnosis at the moderate stage2,3. Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) represents a prodromal state of AD, and recent reports suggest that nearly 80% of people living with MCI are not diagnosed until progression to dementia2. A robust screening process that can detect individuals with cognitive impairment in clinical practice, especially in primary care settings, at the earliest stages could either direct people into appropriate current treatment protocols and/or increase the pipeline of individuals eligible to enroll in AD clinical trials4.

As its primary objective, the Bio-Hermes Study5 tested the relationship between emerging blood-based and digital biomarkers and the presence of amyloid measured by Positron Emission Tomography (PET) scan. Participants were recruited from sites experienced in AD clinical trials using recruitment procedures common to those used in standard AD clinical trials. The participants enrolled in Bio-Hermes were hypothesized to be similar in most clinical characteristics to patients who had been enrolled in disease-modifying therapeutic AD trials. As multiple digital, fluid, and PET biomarkers were obtained from each participant, the Bio-Hermes Study provided an opportunity to evaluate the predictive value of biomarkers alone or in combination. One such digital marker included was Cognivue Clarity6,7.

Cognivue Clarity is the first FDA-cleared computerized cognitive test that uses adaptive psychophysics to detect the risk of cognitive impairment assessing baseline motor skills and visual salience to test information processing and memory, thus minimizing many of the biases found in common cognitive testing mechanisms and brief screening tests. We examined the ability of Cognivue Clarity to (a) detect MCI and AD in clinical diagnostics groups, (b) determine the presence of amyloid, and (c) distinguish between biomarker-confirmed groups.

Methods

Study design and participants

The Bio-Hermes Study was a cross-sectional observational study and has been described elsewhere5. The primary objective of the Bio-Hermes Study was to evaluate the ability of promising blood-based and digital biomarkers to reflect the presence of brain amyloid in participants enrolled at clinical trial sites using recruitment procedures similar to those commonly used in AD therapeutic drug studies. Participants in the Bio-Hermes Study had clinical characteristics similar to those enrolled in clinical trials of disease-modifying treatments and, because multiple biomarkers were obtained, the predictive value of biomarkers alone or in combination could be evaluated. The study also aimed to include a significant representation of racial and ethnic minority participants. Successful enrollment of Bio-Hermes could facilitate analyses and selection of digital and plasma biomarkers as prescreening tools to identify participants who were more likely to have amyloid, and thus avoid time consuming and expensive screen failures.

Between April 2021 and November 2022, 17 research sites with expertise in AD clinical trials recruited 1296 from their community-based populations. After screening, 1001 participants were enrolled of which 960 had valid and complete Cognivue Clarity, 920 completed amyloid PET scans, and 887 completed both Cognivue Clarity and amyloid PET scans. Resources provided by the Global Alzheimer’s Platform Foundation helped facilitate recruitment and representation from different ethnoracial groups. Eligibility criteria included being age 60–85, fluency in English or Spanish, and a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE)8 score 20–30 inclusive. Sites recruited individuals based on standardized diagnostic criteria9 used in their activities to recruit for AD clinical trials. Cognitively normal individuals had no impairment. Participants with probable AD met published criteria10 with progressive cognitive impairment that interfered with activities of daily living. Individuals with MCI met criteria for MCI due to AD11 with cognitive impairment without significant interference in everyday activities. Exclusion criteria included no known depression, no known negative amyloid PET scan in the past 12 months, no history of strokes or seizures in the past 12 months, and no cancer in the past 5 years. No magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was included in the Bio-Hermes study, and no neuropsychological assessment or neurological exam findings were available other than what was collected as part of the Bio-Hermes protocol. Prior to any study procedures, written informed consent was obtained from all study participants and research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol was reviewed and approved by Advarra, a central institutional review board (Reference Number Pro00046018). The study was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04733989) on 2/2/2021.

Clinical diagnoses

Bio-Hermes participants stratified into three clinical cohorts (Cognitively Normal, MCI, probable AD) representing the range of cognitive functioning included in therapeutic or prevention trials. Participants were classified based scores on the MMSE, Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT) delayed recall score12 and Functional Activities Questionnaire (FAQ)13. Cognitively normal controls had no self-reported or informant-reported memory loss, MMSE scores 26–30 inclusive, RAVLT scores in the normal range based on age- and race-adjusted norms, and no evidence of functional decline in the FAQ. MCI individuals met NIA-AA criteria for MCI and verified through medical records OR had MMSE scores 24–30 inclusive, RAVLT delayed recall score at least 1 standard deviation below age- and race-adjusted norms, and preservation of functional independence based on FAQ scores. Mild AD individuals met NIA-AA criteria for AD and verified through medical records OR had MMSE scores 20–24 inclusive, RAVLT delayed recall score ≥ 1 standard deviation below age- and race-adjusted norms, and dependence in functional abilities based on FAQ scores. As the MMSE was used for eligibility and combined with RAVLT and FAQ for defining clinical diagnostic groups, in this report we avoided circular logic of using classifiers as predictors or outcomes by only comparing Cognivue Clarity to the Bio-Hermes defined clinical diagnoses of Cognitively Normal, MCI, or probable AD.

AD biomarkers

Plasma biomarkers were collected and analyzed by C2N Diagnostics laboratories for Aβ40, Aβ42, Aβ42/Aβ40, and Apolipoprotein E epsilon (ε) proteotype (ApoE) (PrecivityAD)14 assays including the Amyloid Probability Score (APS), Quanterix laboratories for pTau18115, and Eli Lilly and Company for pTau21716. Participants also had amyloid PET using 18 F-Florbetapir. All images were uploaded into an electronic imaging portal that was accessible by a network of specialists trained to interpret Florbetapir PET images that included each participant’s standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR)17,18 value. The central reader made the final determination according to manufacturer standards. In this report, amyloid status (present vs. absent) was established using a SUVR of 1.1 which is equivalent to a Centiloid Level of 24.1 consistent with thresholds used in recent AD clinical trials19,20.

Biomarker-confirmed diagnoses

Based on amyloid PET status, clinical diagnoses were further refined by their biomarker status into True Controls (cognitively normal/negative amyloid), MCI due to AD (cognitively impaired/positive amyloid), AD dementia (cognitively impaired/positive amyloid), non-AD MCI (cognitively impaired/negative amyloid), and non-AD dementia (cognitively impaired/negative amyloid). In the absence of other clinical data, MRI, or full neuropsychological characterization, non-AD etiologies could not be determined and co-pathologies for AD-etiologies are unknown.

Cognivue Clarity

Cognivue Clarity is an FDA-cleared Class II exempt medical device for computerized, self-administered cognitive testing that takes 10 min to complete. It is indicated for use as an adjunctive tool for evaluating perceptual and memory function in older adults. Automated Cognivue technology utilizes adaptive psychophysics to assess baseline motor skills and visual acuity and uses the stimuli and the responses to the stimuli that occurred in the previous trial for determine the level of difficulty in subsequent trials6. Cognivue Clarity is a modified laptop computer with only a screen and the CogniWheel, an easy-to-maneuver wheel that provides a single point of patient interaction to select the answer. Cognivue Clarity collects 130,000 data points per administration, and provides a customized assessment based on each individual’s visual and motor skills. A video demonstrating the Cognivue Clarity and the tests can be found at https://cognivue.com/videos/.

Cognivue Clarity is comprised of 10 subtests that include two validity performance measures (Adaptive Motor Control, Visual Salience) that are used to set the adaptive psychophysics profile. There are four discrimination tests (Letter, Word, Shape, Motion), and four memory tests (Letter, Word, Shape, Motion). The Adaptive Motor Control subtest measures the speed at which the participant is able to manipulate the flywheel on the device. The Visual Salience subtest measures the threshold of the participant to determine visual contrast of the target stimuli. The inability to effectively manipulate the flywheel or visually detect the stimuli ends the session – this assures that the patient has sufficient motor and visual ability to complete the cognitive tests. It also permits setting the baseline ability skill for individuals with motor impairments (i.e. arthritis) or visual impairments (e.g., glaucoma, macular degeneration) so that valid cognitive testing can occur. The Discrimination tests present Letter, Word, Shape or Motion stimuli that the participant must match compared with non-target stimuli manipulating the flywheel. The Memory tests present Letter, Word, Shape, or Motion stimuli the participant must first immediately recall, followed by a delayed recall and a series of n-back paradigms.

After completion, the Cognivue Clarity device provides immediate results as a report and/or in a comma-separated values file. Results include an overall average score, four domain specific scores (Memory, Executive-Attention, Perceptual, Visuomotor), 10 subtest scores, motor reaction time and processing speed [9]. Response tracings for each of the subtests to assess performance validity plotted as time (seconds) x test difficulty (percent of maximum score). Delayed or irregular response tracings reflect lapses in performance, fluctuating attention, or difficulty in comprehension. Global, domain and subtest scores are scaled 0-100 with higher scores reflecting better performance. Score interpretations are provided as normal, borderline, or impaired performance based on prior testing consistent with the FDA-filing30,31 and a recent report with age-normed ranges of performance, validated in 18–85-year-olds against the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS). Individuals age 60+ perform similarly with a cut-off score of 69 for normal performance6.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS v29 (Armonk, NY). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize overall sample characteristics. Internal consistency was examined as the proportion of the variability in the responses that is the result of differences in the respondents, reported as the Cronbach alpha reliability coefficient. Coefficients greater than 0.7 are good measures of internal consistency. Hypothesis testing was conducted to assess the ability of Cognivue Clarity to differentiate between (a) clinical diagnoses (Cognitively Normal, MCI, probable AD), (b) amyloid presence by PET scan, and (c) biomarker-defined groups (True Controls, MCI due to AD, AD, non-AD MCI, non-AD dementia). Student t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey-Kramer post-hoc tests were used for continuous data and Chi-square analyses were used for categorical data. Stratified analyses were conducted to study subgroup differences in Cognivue Clarity performance. As ApoE4 genotype plays an increasingly important role in treatment decisions and the risk of side effects with anti-amyloid antibody treatments19,20, we also conducted a stratified analysis examining Cognivue Clarity performance in non-impaired vs. impaired individuals by ApoE genotype and ApoE4 carrier status. In addition to statistical significance, effect size was calculated for ability to differentiate between clinical diagnostic groups, amyloid status, and biomarker-confirmed groups. Effect size is the magnitude of difference between groups and is less dependent on sample size. Effect size for ANOVA is reported as Eta squared with values between 0.01 and 0.06 representing a small effect, values between 0.06 and 0.14 representing a medium effect and values ≥ 0.14 representing a large effect. Effect size for t-tests are reported as Cohen’s d with values of 0.2 representing a small effect, values of 0.5 representing a medium effect, and values of 0.8 representing a large effect. Pearson correlation coefficients were used to assess the strength of association between Cognivue average scores and subtests and AD biomarkers. Multiple comparisons were addressed using the Bonferroni correction.

Receiver operator characteristics (ROC) curves, area under the curve (AUC) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used to test discriminative properties of Cognivue Clarity and optimal cut-points (by using closest top-left criteria and Youden index). Psychometric properties of Cognivue Clarity were then determined using the same framework as hypothesis testing - (a) clinical diagnoses, (b) amyloid status, and (c) biomarker-defined groups - including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, positive likelihood ratio, negative likelihood ratio, and diagnostic odds ratio21. The diagnostic odds ratio represents the odds of positivity in individuals with disease relative to the odds of individuals without the disease providing a clinician or researcher with a measure of the effectiveness of a test. It provides a single indicator of test performance but unlike positive and negative predictive values, is independent of disease prevalence in a sample.

Results

Bio-Hermes sample characteristics

From April 2021 through November 2022, 887 individuals who completed both Cognivue Clarity testing and amyloid PET scan were evaluated (89% of the total sample). The clinical diagnoses of these participants included 388 Cognitively Normal, 282 MCI, and 217 Probable AD cases. The mean age of the sample was 71.8 ± 6.7y (range 59–85) with 15.5 ± 2.7y of education (range 2–24). The sample was 56.4% female, 37.3% were ApoE ε4 carriers, and the ethnoracial self-identification included 78.8% non-Hispanic White, 9.6% Black, 9.8% Hispanic, and 1.8% Asian. The mean MMSE score was 26.9 ± 2.7 (range 17–30), the mean FAQ score was 3.6 ± 5.2 (range 0–30), and the mean total score for the RAVLT was 40.6 ± 14.1 (range 0–67). The mean Cognivue Clarity score was 63.8 ± 16.7 (range 0–93).

Cognivue Clarity performance by clinical diagnosis

Table 1 shows the performance on Cognivue Clarity across Bio-Hermes clinical diagnostic groups. The Cognivue Clarity global score and each of the 10 subtests were able to discriminate cognitively normal individuals from those with clinically defined MCI and from those with Probable AD (all p-values < 0.001). Further, Cognivue Clarity was able to differentiate MCI from Probable AD (all p-values < 0.001). Cognivue Clarity scores were lower in ApoE4 carriers than non-carriers (63.9 ± 16.5 vs. 61.2 ± 18.4, p = 0.021, Cohen’s d = 0.155). Cognivue Clarity had strong internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.879 (95%CI: 0.87–0.89).

While p-values support statistical differences, they are unable to provide estimates of effect size. With a large sample size, p-values may demonstrate differences even if the clinical effect size is small. The Cognivue Clarity global score had a very large effect size (Eta squared = 0.214; 95% CI: 0.168–0.258) while each of the Cognivue Clarity subtests had medium to large effect sizes (Table 1). The effect size to differentiate Cognitively Normal individuals from MCI was medium to large (Cohen’s d = 0.545; 95% CI: 0.388–0.701).

We next conducted stratified analyses comparing Cognivue Clarity global scores in impaired vs. non-impaired individuals by ethnoracial identity (non-Hispanic White, Black, Hispanic), education, age, sex, and ApoE4 carrier status (Table 2). There were too few individuals identifying themselves as members of other ethnoracial groups to make meaningful comparisons. Cognivue Clarity global scores differentiated individuals with cognitive impairment across all sociodemographic variables. Non-impaired African Americans had lower Cognivue Clarity global scores compared with non-Hispanic Whites and Hispanics, consistent with prior reports6. Performance in impaired individuals was not different by ethnoracial identity. Among the African Americans, subtest performance suggested visual discrimination tests (visual salience, shape discrimination, and motion discrimination) best defined the presence of cognitive impairment. The reasons for this are unknown and warrant further investigation. Consistent with previous reports6, differences in Cognivue Clarity global scores were seen by age and education but not by sex.

The ROC analyses demonstrated that the Cognivue Clarity global score had an AUC of 0.720 (95%CI: 0.69–0.75) to detect the presence of cognitive impairment with the optimal cut-off of 66 which closely aligns with established age-normed cut-offs of Cognivue Clarity to determine the presence of cognitive impairment6. Using the clinical diagnoses as the gold standard, Cognivue Clarity has 73.9% sensitivity, 58.6% specificity, a positive predictive value of 71.7%, a negative predictive value of 62.7%, a positive likelihood ratio of 1.78, a negative likelihood ratio of 0.45, and a diagnostic odds ratio of 3.95.

Cognivue Clarity performance by amyloid status

Participants were divided by amyloid based on 18 F-Florbetapir PET scans into amyloid negative (n = 555) and amyloid positive groups (n = 332). Table 3 shows the performance on Cognivue Clarity by amyloid status. The Cognivue Clarity global score and each of the 10 subtests were able to discriminate individuals with negative amyloid PET scans from those with positive amyloid PET scans (all p-values < 0.001). The effect size of the Cognivue Clarity global score (Table 3) to differentiate amyloid negative individuals from amyloid positive individuals was medium to large (Cohen’s d = 0.568; 95% CI: 0.430–0.707).

There were notable differences in amyloid SUVR values between Non-Hispanic White, African American and Hispanic participants with African Americans trending to have the lowest SUVR, although not reaching statistical significance (p = 0.07). To explore this further we conducted a stratified analysis of amyloid PET SUVR by ethnoracial identity and cognitive status. While there were robust differences between the amyloid PET scans of non-impaired and impaired individuals in non-Hispanic Whites (1.04 ± 0.18 vs. 1.19 ± 0.28, p < 0.001) and Hispanics (1.00 ± 0.14 vs. 1.18 ± 0.27, p = 0.002), there was no difference in SUVR in African Americans by cognitive status (1.04 ± 0.16 vs. 1.10 ± 0.21, p = 0.15). This is consistent with other recent reports of lower amyloid levels in African Americans diagnosed with AD22,23.

We conducted stratified analyses comparing Cognivue Clarity global scores in amyloid negative vs. amyloid positive individuals by ethnoracial identity (non-Hispanic Whites, Black, Hispanic), education, age, sex, and ApoE4 carriers (Table 4). Cognivue Clarity global scores differentiated individuals by amyloid status across all sociodemographic variables except in African Americans. A smaller effect was also seen in participants over age 80 as there is more likely to be accumulations of other pathologies (e.g., vascular lesions, Lewy bodies) in these individuals.

The ROC analyses demonstrated that the Cognivue Clarity global score had an AUC of 0.714 (95%CI: 0.68–0.75) to detect the presence of amyloid with the optimal cut-off of 65, similar to established cut-off of Cognivue Clarity to determine the presence of cognitive impairment6. Table 5 demonstrates the association between Cognivue Clarity global score and 10 subtests and available AD biomarkers – SUVR from the amyloid PET and plasma pTau217 from Eli Lilly, plasma Aβ42/40 ratio and APS from C2N, and plasma pTau181 from Quanterix. In addition, two amyloid/tau ratios were created comparing Aβ42/40 ratios to pTau217 or pTau181 that may more closely align with amyloid PET than plasma Aβ or pTau measurements alone and increasingly used in commercial platforms24–27. Lower Cognivue Clarity global scores and each of the 10 subtests were strongly associated with higher amyloid PET SUVR and pTau217 measurements as well as the Aβ42/pTau217 ratios (all p < 0.001). Moderate correlation was seen between Cognivue Clarity global scores and each of the 10 subtests and Aβ42/40 ratios, APS, pTau181 and Aβ42/pTau181 ratios. Interestingly, the correlation between Cognivue Clarity global scores with AD biomarkers was similar to the strength of association of the AD biomarkers with each other, with pTau217 having the strongest association with amyloid PET (Table 6).

Cognivue Clarity performance by ApoE genotype

With the development and subsequent approval in the United States and other countries of anti-amyloid monoclonal antibody therapies for MCI due to AD and mild AD, the importance of ApoE genotypes has expanded. Individuals who are ApoE4 carriers are at higher risk of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities with homozygotes at the highest risk19,20. Using an omnibus ANOVA approach, there was no difference in Cognivue Clarity global scores by ApoE genotype (F = 1.795, p = 0.111). In a stratified analysis approach by different ApoE genotypes and by ApoE carrier status comparing non-impaired individuals to impaired individuals (Table 6), we found that Cognivue Clarity global scores were different regardless of genotype or carrier status. In non-impaired individuals, there was no difference in Cognivue Clarity performance, but ApoE4 homozygotes tended to have lowest Cognivue Clarity scores and ApoE2 homozygotes tended to have the highest Cognivue Clarity scores. In impaired individuals, there was no difference in Cognivue Clarity performance, but ApoE4 carriers tended to have lower Cognivue Clarity scores than non-carriers.

Cognivue Clarity performance by biomarker confirmed diagnoses

Based on the 18 F-Florbetapir PET scan findings, 66% of the clinically diagnosed MCI individuals and 38% of the clinically diagnosed probable AD individuals had an incorrect clinical diagnosis with an etiology for their cognitive symptoms being a condition other than AD5. As there is not a detailed neuropsychological test battery, neurologic examination, or MRI completed in Bio-Hermes, it is impossible to determine what is the cause of their cognitive impairment. By biomarker confirmation, the Bio-Hermes Study further breaks down into 297 True Controls (cognitively normal/negative amyloid), 111 MCI due to AD (cognitively impaired/positive amyloid), 130 AD dementia (cognitively impaired/positive amyloid), 171 non-AD MCI (cognitively impaired/negative amyloid), and 87 non-AD dementia (cognitively impaired/negative amyloid). In Table 7, we compared the two cognitive tests used for clinical diagnosis (MMSE, RAVLT) and Cognivue Clarity in their ability to differentiate between groups. Cognivue Clarity differentiated between True Controls, MCI, and dementia groups regardless of amyloid status. All 3 tests differentiated True Controls from individuals with any form of cognitive impairment (all p < 0.001). All three tests differentiated AD dementia from non-AD dementia. However, only Cognivue Clarity was able to differentiate MCI due to AD from non-AD MCI (p = 0.003) supporting its ability to discern not only the early stages of cognitive impairment but also the presence of amyloid. Whether Cognivue Clarity can differentiate between other pathologies cannot be tested in Bio-Hermes and warrants further investigation.

The ROC analyses on the biomarker-confirmed sample demonstrated that the Cognivue Clarity global score had an improved AUC of 0.793 (95%CI: 0.75–0.83) to detect the presence of cognitive impairment. Using the biomarker-confirmed clinical diagnoses as the gold standard, Cognivue Clarity had improved psychometric properties with 81.7% sensitivity, 62.3% specificity, a positive predictive value of 63.8%, a negative predictive value of 80.8%, a positive likelihood ratio of 2.17, a negative likelihood ratio of 0.29, and a diagnostic odds ratio 7.48.

Discussion

The Bio-Hermes Study provided an ideal opportunity to explore the value of digital biomarkers ability to differentiate (a) cognitively impaired individuals from cognitively normal individuals, (b) individuals with amyloid from individuals without amyloid, and (c) between biomarker-confirmed diagnostic groups. Cognivue Clarity was able to differentiate cognitively normal older adults from clinically diagnosed MCI and probable AD, and individuals with amyloid from individuals without amyloid. Cognivue Clarity performed well across different ethnoracial groups. Compared with the MMSE and RAVLT, only Cognivue Clarity could differentiate MCI due to AD from non-AD causes of MCI. Cognivue Clarity performed equally well across all ApoE genotypes in both impaired and non-impaired participants. In a biomarker-confirmed subsample, Cognivue Clarity was also able to differentiate True Controls from MCI due to AD dementia, and both True Controls and MCI due to AD from AD dementia. Cognivue Clarity had medium to large effect sizes and moderate correlations with imaging and plasma AD biomarkers. This strength of association was similar to the correlations the different AD biomarkers had with each other and suggests plasma ptau217 could potentially be a good choice to use as screen. As not all individuals with cognitive impairment have amyloid, we also demonstrated that Cognivue Clarity can detect cognitive impairment in non-AD etiologies.

The psychometric properties of Cognivue Clarity supported good sensitivity reliably detecting the presence of MCI and AD (clinical diagnosis sample) or MCI due to AD and AD dementia (biomarker-confirmed sample). Cognivue Clarity also had good negative predictive value reliably identifying Cognitively Normal individuals (clinical diagnosis sample) and True Controls (biomarker-confirmed sample). This suggests that Cognivue Clarity captures most individuals with cognitive impairment and excludes most individuals who are cognitively normal21. These individuals could then be moved forward for further evaluation with biomarkers and more detailed neuropsychological evaluation. Impaired performance on Cognivue Clarity gave a 3.9-fold increased likelihood of identifying cognitive impairment in a clinical sample, a 4.6-fold increase likelihood of detecting amyloid, and a 7.5-fold increase likelihood of identifying MCI due to AD or AD compared with True Controls in a biomarker confirmed sample. The ability to differentiate MCI due to AD from AD, and between AD and non-AD forms of impairment has value to both real-world clinical practice and clinical research as AD treatments move to a more precision-based approach where differential treatment options, both pharmacologic and non-pharmacologic may require such distinction.

Alzheimer’s disease is often under-recognized and under-diagnosed in the community. This may be due, in part, to the lack of brief screening measures that can discriminate normal ageing from MCI and very mild AD, and at the present time, the US Preventive Services Task Force does not make any recommendations regarding dementia screening28. One of most commonly used screening instruments in the clinic or for clinical trial inclusion is the MMSE8. The MMSE, and other similar tests, may be insensitive to subtle changes in high functioning individuals (i.e. ceiling effects) who may score well within the normal range in the early stages of impairment29,30. These same measures also may prevent detection of dementia in individuals with poorer lifelong abilities31. The MMSE also has well recognized education32 and ethnoracial biases33–35 including the serial subtraction task.

Brief cognitive tests may lead to misclassification associated with factors such as older age, lower education, illiteracy, visual impairment, and being from a minoritized group36. This could have implications on determining eligibility for treatment protocols or enrollment into clinical trials37. Gold Standard tests, such as neuropsychological test batteries, provide expert patient characterization but are lengthy and require specialized staff to administer, score and interpret, making them impractical in the clinic4. Cognivue Clarity offers a global assessment of cognitive functioning for brief, easy screening while also providing domain and subtest scores, processing speed, and reaction times all validated against the RBANS6 for more detailed characterization of patients. The adaptive psychophysics of Cognivue Clarity also provides the potential for use in clinical research with age-normed global scores, good test-retest reliability, internal consistency, and small practice effects for repeated testing in the context of a clinical trial6,37.

Study limitations

The Bio-Hermes Study5 was a cross-sectional study so that longitudinal changes in Cognivue Clarity or other biomarkers could not be determined. There are plans for a Bio-Hermes 2 study that will recruit a new cohort and include MRI and longitudinal follow-up. The cohorts used in the Bio-Hermes Study were based on clinical diagnoses using limited testing (MMSE, RAVLT, FAQ). No detailed neuropsychological evaluation, neurological examination, Clinical Dementia Rating, or MRI was performed. The absence of these standards in clinical practice limits the full characterization of the Bio-Hermes cohort and has the potential to miss important contributors to the cognitive profile such as non-memory domains, small vessel disease, extrapyramidal symptoms, and other features that present in AD and non-AD dementias. There are ongoing studies with Cognivue Clarity that include detailed neuropsychological evaluation, neurological examination, Clinical Dementia Rating, or MRI that can address these issues.

A substantial proportion of clinically diagnosed MCI and probable AD cases did not have amyloid, however there is insufficient information to discern what was the underlying etiology of cognitive impairment in those participants. This uncertainty likely contributed to the lower specificity and positive predictive values of Cognivue Clarity in this study. Cognivue Clarity tasks include n-back tests assessing working memory, a frontal executive skill that would potentially be sensitive to conditions with frontal lobe involvement (e.g. frontotemporal degeneration, vascular dementia). Cognivue Clarity also assesses attention, reaction time, and visual processing, domains that are typically affected in Lewy body diseases. Evidence supporting this possibility includes a recent publication on the performance of the digital clock draw and recall, another digital biomarker included in Bio-Hermes with a sensitivity of 0.67, a specificity of 0.61, a positive predictive value of 0.66, and a negative predictive value of 0.62 when compared with the MMSE38. In aforementioned report, the authors created their own rules-based classification of cases that was always not consistent with Bio-Hermes consensus diagnoses and demonstrates the difficulty in attempting to use the MMSE as a comparator or explaining clinical diagnoses that do not match biomarker findings. Any screening test can only perform as well as the Gold Standard it is compared against, and predictive values are highly dependent on the prevalence of disease in the sample4,21. Rather than relying solely on negative and positive predictive values, likelihood ratios may provide more convincing evidence on the utility of screening tests with higher diagnostic odds ratios supporting their use to effectively detect disease21.

We described differences in Cognivue Clarity scores by age, ethnoracial identify, and education. Cognivue Clarity reports are age-normed so that interpretation of cognitive performance for clinical use eliminates this bias, particularly in older adults as performance on Cognivue Clarity is similar in individuals aged 60 years or older with the same cut-off points6. Like many brief screening tests, individuals with 12 or less years of education score lower than individuals with higher educational attainment. Future corrections for education should be considered. African Americans without cognitive impairment, on average, score lower on Cognivue Clarity compared with non-Hispanic White and Hispanic participants. This is consistent with prior reports6. The reasons for this are unknown, but subtest performance on visual discrimination tests (visual salience, shape discrimination, and motion discrimination) were lower suggesting that co-pathologies might be involved. This is an area for future research.

There were only 19 individuals enrolled who self-identified as Asian, American Indian, Alaska Native, Native Hawaiian, or Pacific Islander. This unfortunately replicates the recruitment pattern of many AD clinical trials in the United States. Better outreach and inclusion of underrepresented minorities should be a major focus for future studies. This limitation also restricts conclusions that could be made regarding performance of Cognivue Clarity in these ethnoracial groups. Bio-Hermes was only offered in English and Spanish and while ~ 20% of the sample was from an underrepresented group, this is less that the ~ 40% representation in the US population. There are several independent studies of Cognivue Clarity in diverse populations (e.g., rural, American Indian, Pacific Islander) that are ongoing and may address our understanding of the utility of Cognivue Clarity in these populations in the future. The strengths of this study include a large sample of older adults similar to cohorts recruited for AD prevention and treatment trials with ~ 20% representation of African American and Hispanic individuals and extensive biomarker characterization. The biomarker characterization used in Bio-Hermes is consistent with the National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer’s Association research framework9 for biological definitions of AD and non-AD causes of cognitive impairment.

Conclusions

Detection of early-stage AD is challenging in the primary care setting with many cases being diagnosed in the United States at the moderate stage – too late for treatment with emerging disease-modifying medications. The delays in diagnosis may be particularly relevant to individuals from racial and ethnic minorities, socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, and geographic locales that lack specialty memory care settings (i.e., rural areas) and who are at higher risk for AD1. There are likely many causes contributing to low rates of diagnosis including access to care, access to specialists, under- or non-insurance, disease stigma, and health literacy4. Dementia screening could capture cases at the earliest possible stage in at-risk populations permitting early intervention and enrollment into trials. Although the Medicare Annual Wellness Visit (AWV) includes assessing for cognitive impairment as part of the required elements, there is no agreement on how this could be done and less than 25% of Medicare beneficiaries receive AWV as part of the medical care39,40. One potentially addressable solution for low rates of screening would be to use Cognivue Clarity that provides a valid and sensitive measure that performs well across different sociodemographic and ethnoracial groups and does not require extensive staff or physician time.

Screening for MCI and AD requires consideration of the population at risk and the psychometric properties of the instruments used36,41. A large number of false positive individuals might expend limited health care funds. Conversely, a large number of false negative individuals would be denied treatment with emerging disease-modifying therapies and might miss opportunities to participate in clinical research studies. Cognivue Clarity was able to differentiate both clinically defined groups and biomarker defined groups enhancing its utility to screen for cognitive impairment. To further increase the efficiency and cost-effectiveness of screening, a staged screening approach for MCI and AD likely makes the most sense42. Cognivue Clarity, a 10-minute self-administered cognitive test can be used to define presence of impairment. This is low cost and requires little physician or staff time. If the screen is suggestive of cognitive impairment, this can be followed by a readily accessible AD biomarker such as plasma pTau217 that is strongly correlated with an abnormal amyloid PET scan. This staged approach may increase the likelihood of identifying a case of MCI-AD or AD for inclusion into current treatment protocols or for enriching recruitment into planned clinical trials.
