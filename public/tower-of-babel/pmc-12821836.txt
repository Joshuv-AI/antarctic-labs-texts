Reinterpretation of the clinical and biological behaviors of papillary thyroid carcinoma subtypes within the 2022 WHO classification: a cross-sectional study of 768 cases
Authors: Ying-Jie Xu, Chen Qian, Wei-Qi Kong, Dong-Lai Fan, Wei-Wei Liu, Yue-Mei Hu, Cheng-Guang Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLimited comprehensive evaluations have been conducted to date regarding the clinical behavior of papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) subtypes based on the 2022 World Health Organization (WHO) classification of thyroid neoplasms.MethodsA total of 768 patients who underwent surgical treatment for PTC were retrospectively categorized into 12 subtypes according to the 2022 WHO classification system. Clinical and pathological variables including incidence rate, age at onset, sex, anatomical location of lesions, lesion size, presence of lymph node metastasis, capsular invasion, tumor microenvironmental features, familial predisposition, and gene mutations were comparatively analyzed across these subtypes.ResultsAmong the 12 identified subtypes, the diffuse sclerosing subtype (DSS) demonstrated the highest incidence, followed by the encapsulated subtype (ES) and the subtype with fibromatosis/fasciitis-like stroma (WF/FLS). The spindle cell subtype (SCS) exhibited the lowest incidence. An annual increase in the incidence of WF/FLS subtypes was observed. Multifocal lesion occurrence was significantly more frequent in individuals with the DSS subtype compared to the overall PTC cohort (p < 0.05). Lymph node metastasis rates were notably elevated in the hobnail subtype (HS), tall cell subtype (TCS), DSS, and Warthin-like subtype (WLS). The prevalence of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis was significantly lower in the ES subtype (p < 0.05) and significantly higher in the WLS subtype (p < 0.001), relative to the broader PTC population. Subtypes including Solid subtype (SS), WF/FLS, HS, and WLS demonstrated higher frequencies of familial tumor history. Although gene mutation rates were elevated across all subtypes, no statistically significant differences were identified among them.ConclusionPapillary thyroid carcinoma subtypes defined by the 2022 WHO classification exhibit distinct demographic, pathological, and molecular profiles. Subtypes such as hobnail, tall cell, diffuse sclerosing, and Warthin-like show aggressive features, while encapsulated, infiltrative follicular, and oncocytic types follow indolent courses.The high familial rate and increasing trend of the WF/FLS subtype provide novel insight into its possible genetic and microenvironmental influences. Recognizing these subtype-based differences may refine surgical decision-making, risk stratification, and postoperative surveillance strategies.Keywords: Cell subtype, Familial inheritance, Gene mutation, Papillary thyroid carcinoma, Tumor microenvironment

Background

The incidence of thyroid cancer has demonstrated a consistent upward trend in recent years [1]. In 2022, thyroid cancer ranked as the third most frequently diagnosed malignancy in China [2]. Differentiated thyroid carcinomas represent the predominant category of thyroid cancer, with papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) accounting for the majority of malignant cases within this group [3]. The 2022 fifth edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Endocrine and Neuroendocrine Tumors revised the categorization of PTC subtypes, reducing the number from 14 (as defined in the fourth edition) to 12. This updated framework places greater emphasis on the pathological and molecular features of thyroid neoplasms, forming the basis for subtype delineation [4]. The implementation of this unified classification approach enhances diagnostic precision, particularly for papillary microcarcinomas measuring less than 1 cm in diameter. It also facilitates the early detection of subtypes with aggressive biological behavior, providing relevant criteria for evaluating the risk of microcarcinoma recurrence. Each PTC subtype displays distinct patterns in incidence and clinicopathological behavior. Parameters such as lesion size and multiplicity, gene mutation profiles, family history of neoplastic disease, and the tumor microenvironment influence the metastatic potential to lymph nodes across subtypes. Subtypes such as tall cell (TCS), columnar cell (CoCS), hobnail (HS), solid (SS), and diffuse sclerosing (DSS) are characterized by aggressive clinical behavior and a high propensity for lymph node metastasis. In contrast, subtypes including encapsulated (ES), invasive follicular (IFS), and oncocytic (OS) typically exhibit lower invasive potential and are associated with more favorable prognostic outcomes [3–5]. Due to their low prevalence, limited data are available on certain rare subtypes, such as clear cell (CCS), spindle cell (SCS), and PTC with fibromatosis/fasciitis-like stroma (WF/FLS) [3, 4, 6]. Although some studies examined the clinicopathological features of individual PTC subtypes using the 2022 WHO classification, comprehensive comparative analyses addressing the incidence and clinical risk across all 12 recognized subtypes, particularly the rarer forms, remain scarce.

Patients and methods

A retrospective analysis was conducted on clinical records from 768 patients diagnosed with PTC, including 10 with undefined subtypes and 145 with benign thyroid nodules. These patients underwent surgical management for thyroid carcinoma or nodular thyroid disease between January 2017 and June 2023. The process of patient selection is detailed in Fig. 1. Of these, 758 individuals with clearly defined PTC subtypes were categorized into 12 distinct cellular subtypes according to the criteria established in the fifth edition of the WHO Classification of Endocrine and Neuroendocrine Tumors (2022) [4]. Comparative analyses were performed between the 12 PTC subtypes and the overall PTC cohort (n = 768), as well as between individual subtypes. Variables included age at onset, sex, lesion size and multiplicity, lymph node metastasis, capsular invasion, characteristics of the tumor microenvironment, gene mutation profiles associated with thyroid carcinoma, and familial predisposition.

Flowchart of patient screening and inclusion

All patients provided written informed consent after being apprised of the surgical risks. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Ethical approval (number: K2024-063-01) was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Tongren Hospital (Shanghai, China). The study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening The Reporting Of Cohort Studies In Surgery (STROCSS) guidelines and the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [7]. Surgical procedures were performed by board-certified surgeons with more than ten years of clinical experience. Depending on individual clinical presentation and surgeon judgment, patients underwent open, endoscopic assisted, or total endoscopic thyroid lobectomy with isthmus resection. Total thyroidectomy was performed for bilateral malignancies, and central neck dissection, either prophylactic or therapeutic, was conducted as indicated. Histopathological classification was independently verified by two pathologists, each with more than 10 years of diagnostic experience, All paraffin sections of patients previously diagnosed with thyroid cancer were re-evaluated by pathologists according to the 2022 WHO diagnostic criteria, rather than merely reviewing the original reports. Molecular profiling was conducted using an 8-gene, polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based Thyroid Cancer Detection Kit (Shanghai Rightongene Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded specimens from both malignant and benign thyroid tissues. Targeted genes included BRAF, NRAS, KRAS, HRAS, TERT, CCDC6-RET, PAX8-PPARγ, and ETV6-NTRK3.

Classification of cellular pathological subtypes

Subtype classification for PTC followed the 2022 WHO Classification of Endocrine and Neuroendocrine Tumors, supported by detailed morphological descriptions from existing literature [4, 8–11]. The diagnostic criteria for PTC included hallmark histological features such as papillary architecture, infiltrative growth patterns, and distinctive nuclear alterations. These nuclear features comprised irregular membrane contours, grooves, pseudoinclusions, nuclear enlargement and elongation, overlapping and crowding, clearing of chromatin, margination, and a ground-glass appearance. Further refinement of subtype classification incorporated immunohistochemical and molecular marker profiles, including thyroglobulin (Tg), thyroid peroxidase (TPO), cytokeratin 19, Galectin-3, thyroid transcription factor-1 (TTF-1), HBME-1, β-catenin, CDX2, and Ki-67. Based on these criteria, PTC was categorized into 12 subtypes: ES, IFS, OS, DSS, TCS, HS, CoCS, SS, SCS, Warthin-like subtype (WLS), CCS, and WF/FLS.

Familial thyroid cancer

Familial thyroid cancer was defined to include both same-generation and intergenerational hereditary cases. Same-generation familial cancer referred to two or more individuals within the same generation of a family (e.g., siblings or cousins) who shared common ancestry (parents or grandparents) and were affected by thyroid cancer. Intergenerational familial cancer involved affected individuals from different generations, such as parent-child or grandparent-grandchild relationships.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Origin 8.0 software (Origin Lab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA). Continuous variables were analyzed using Student’s t-test, and categorical variables were assessed using the chi-squared (χ²) test or Fisher’s exact test in cases involving small sample sizes. A two-tailed p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sex distribution and age at onset

The overall male-to-female incidence ratio of PTC was 1:3.04 (190:578). The ES demonstrated a significantly lower male-to-female incidence ratio than that of overall PTC (p < 0.01), while the WLS exhibited a significantly higher ratio (p < 0.05). No significant differences were observed in sex distribution between the remaining ten subtypes and overall PTC (p > 0.05). Pairwise comparisons revealed significant differences in sex distribution between the ES subgroup and the WF/FLS, DSS, OS, IFS, and WLS subgroups (p < 0.01, p < 0.05, p < 0.005, p < 0.05, and p < 0.01, respectively). Additional significant differences were identified between the CoCS and the OS and WLS subtypes (p < 0.05 and p < 0.01, respectively), and between the HS and the WLS subtype (p < 0.05). No other pairwise comparisons demonstrated significant differences (p > 0.05).

The mean age at diagnosis for individuals with PTC was 48.3 ± 13.5 years. The youngest mean age was observed in the SS (43.5 ± 20.6 years), and the oldest in the SCS (56.7 ± 17.5 years). There were no statistically significant differences in mean age at onset between the 12 PTC subtypes and the overall PTC group (p > 0.05). However, pairwise comparisons indicated significant differences between the WLS subtype and the TCS, OS, and IFS subtypes (p < 0.05 for all), as well as between the CoCS and IFS subtypes (p < 0.05). No other pairwise comparisons yielded significant differences (p > 0.05).

Anatomical distribution

Among the total PTC cases, the unilateral-to-bilateral carcinoma ratio was 721:96, indicating that bilateral lesions were present in 11.75% of cases. The left-to-right distribution ratio was 407:497. No statistically significant differences in unilateral-to-bilateral incidence ratios were identified between any of the 12 subtypes and the overall PTC cohort (p > 0.05).

Lesion multiplicity

Multifocal disease was identified in 25.32% of PTC cases. Among the 12 PTC subtypes, only the DSS exhibited a significantly higher incidence of multifocal lesions compared to the overall PTC group (p < 0.05). No other subtypes demonstrated significant differences in lesion multiplicity when compared to the overall PTC group (p > 0.05). Pairwise comparisons further confirmed that the DSS subgroup had a significantly higher proportion of multifocal tumors (33.75%) than the OS subgroup (p < 0.05). No other significant differences in multifocality were observed among the subtypes (p > 0.05). Additionally, analysis of the ratio of disseminated to non-disseminated lesions across all subtypes revealed no statistically significant differences compared to the overall PTC group, nor among the subtypes themselves (p > 0.05).

Tumor size distribution

Tumor size was classified according to the 2022 edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) guidelines and the 2022 Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Thyroid Cancer. Lesions smaller than 1 cm were further stratified into three categories based on visibility under gross and microscopic examination (≤ 1 cm, ≤ 0.5 cm, and ≤ 0.2 cm). This classification aimed to ensure balanced comparison across subgroups with adequate sample sizes and to facilitate a more detailed analysis of the association between tumor size and lymph node metastasis. Significant differences in tumor sizes were observed in the CoCS, ES, and IFS subgroups compared to the overall PTC cohort (p < 0.05, p < 0.005, and p < 0.001, respectively), while no significant differences were identified in the remaining subtypes (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Thyroid capsule invasion rates across subtypes

In this cohort, the overall capsular invasion rate among individuals with PTC was 9.22% (78/846). A significantly higher rate of capsular invasion was identified in the WF/FLS subtype (16.28%) compared with the overall PTC group (p < 0.05). No significant differences in capsular invasion rates were observed between the remaining PTC subtypes and the overall PTC group (p > 0.05). Pairwise comparisons revealed that the WF/FLS subtype had significantly higher capsular invasion rates than the HS, DSS, and ES subtypes (p < 0.001, p < 0.001, and p < 0.005, respectively).

The ES subtype exhibited a significantly lower incidence of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis compared to the overall PTC group (p < 0.05), whereas a significantly higher incidence was observed in the WLS subtype (p < 0.001). No statistically significant differences in Hashimoto’s thyroiditis rates were identified between the remaining subtypes and the overall PTC group (p > 0.05). The occurrence of follicular hyperplastic nodules was significantly more frequent in the TCS and IFS subtypes than in the overall PTC group (p < 0.05 and p < 0.001, respectively), while it was significantly less frequent in the CoCS subtype (p < 0.05).

When comparing PTC with benign nodules, the incidence of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis was significantly higher in PTC (47.39%, p < 0.001). Additionally, microcalcifications were slightly more common in PTC (14.61%), though this difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). In contrast, the incidence of other tissue morphologies (adenoma, follicular hyperplastic nodules, colloid hyperplastic nodules, fibrosis/collagenization, papillary hyperplasia, and atypical epithelial hyperplasia) was significantly lower in PTC than in benign nodules (p < 0.001 for the first three categories; p < 0.05 for the last three). Other tissue types, including colloid nodules, atypical epithelial hyperplasia, and subacute thyroiditis, also had lower frequencies in PTC than in benign nodules, although these differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

Family history of thyroid cancer across subtypes

The overall incidence of familial PTC in the study cohort was 5.01%. The highest familial occurrence was found in the SS subtype (25.00%), followed by the WLS (11.11%), WF/FLS (9.24%), HS (6.49%), and CoCS (5.45%) subtypes. No cases of familial PTC were observed in the TCS, SCS, and CCS subtypes. The incidence rates in the WF/FLS, HS, CoCS, SS, and WLS subtypes were all higher than the overall familial rate, while the remaining seven subtypes exhibited lower rates; however, none of these differences reached statistical significance (p > 0.05). A significantly higher familial occurrence was observed in the WF/FLS subtype compared to the ES subtype (p < 0.05). No other pairwise comparisons yielded statistically significant differences (p > 0.05).

Gene mutations in PTC subtypes and benign thyroid nodules

Among those with PTC who underwent molecular testing, the overall gene mutation rate was 87.82% (137/156). The highest mutation rate was observed in the TCS subtype (100%, 3/3), followed by the HS (94.44%), DSS (93.75%), WF/FLS (90.62%), and CCS (88.89%) subtypes. The SS and SCS subtypes demonstrated the lowest mutation rates (66.67%). Although mutation rates across subtypes ranged from 66.67% to 100%, these differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). However, the mutation rate in PTC was significantly higher than that in benign thyroid nodules (p < 0.001). The frequencies of specific gene alterations in PTC were as follows: single BRAF mutation (81.69%), co-mutation of BRAF and TERT (2.60%), NRAS mutation (1.30%), CCDC6–RET rearrangement (1.30%), and KRAS mutation (1.30%). Notably, in 19 cases (12.46%) of PTC, no mutations were detected in any of the eight genes examined.

Discussion

PTC is generally classified as a low-grade malignant tumor with a favorable prognosis. Accordingly, some researchers have indicated that surgical intervention may not be necessary, advocating instead for active surveillance and regular follow-up for individuals diagnosed with thyroid cancer [12, 13]. The favorable clinical outcomes associated with PTC are attributed to several factors: a high proportion of low-invasive cell subtypes; a predominance of tumors with either no gene mutations or single-site mutations rather than combined mutations [14]; advancements in health screening enabling early detection of small tumors; and the inhibitory influence of the tumor immune microenvironment on invasion and metastasis [15]. Despite these favorable characteristics, increasing clinical knowledge of PTC has led to ongoing debates concerning the need for extended follow-up or regional lymph node dissection based solely on tumor size [3, 16–18].

In the present study, it was observed that certain subtypes exhibited regional lymph node metastasis, intraglandular dissemination, or extracapsular tissue invasion, even when the lesions were small and not highly invasive [16]. These findings indicate that in such cases, delayed surgical treatment may increase the risk of disease progression or recurrence. Furthermore, omitting lymph node dissection may permit residual tumor cells to metastasize to distant regions or result in local recurrence. Therefore, for individuals with confirmed diagnoses of specific aggressive PTC subtypes, prompt surgical intervention is recommended upon subtype identification. Previous literature has reported relatively high rates of lymph node metastasis in region VI among individuals with PTC [3, 16]. In this study, the incidence of region VI metastasis was 29.25%, predominantly involving T1 and T2 stage tumors, thus indicating a high rate of early lymphatic involvement. Based on these findings, central neck dissection of region VI is recommended to minimize recurrence risk while preserving critical anatomical structures such as the recurrent laryngeal nerve and parathyroid glands.

The histopathological subtype of thyroid cancer plays a critical role in predicting its invasive potential and metastatic behavior. Each subtype presents distinct clinical and biological profiles, and histological classification is essential when assessing the risk of postoperative recurrence [4]. Additional factors influencing recurrence risk include tumor size, lymph node status, capsular invasion, and mutation type [3–6, 16]. The pathological subtype also informs decisions regarding the application of adjuvant thyroid hormone suppression therapy following surgery.

Data on the clinical and biological characteristics of rarer PTC subtypes, including CCS, SCS, WLS, and WF/FLS, remain limited due to the lack of large-scale studies [3, 4, 16]. In the present cohort, a relatively high number of individuals were diagnosed with the WF/FLS subtype (n = 119), with findings indicating an annual increase in its incidence. Notably, this subtype exhibited a higher frequency of familial inheritance and capsular invasion and was more frequently associated with lymph node metastasis. These features indicate that the WF/FLS subtype demonstrates more aggressive biological behavior, thus warranting increased clinical attention. In our research report, the incidence rate of PTC with fibromatosis/fasciitis-like stroma (WF/FLS) was 15.69%, while the literature reports it as 0.03%–0.5% [19]. The significant deviation could be attributed to several factors. Firstly, it might be due to the selection bias of the single-center study population, including differences in ethnicity, social structure, or geographical location. Secondly, the new classification and diagnostic criteria in the 2022 WHO version might have an impact on the diagnosis of this small subtype of papillary carcinoma. Additionally, it could be due to excessive exposure to certain risk factors during a specific time period. Our study shows that the WF/FLS subtype not only has a 9.2% familial inheritance tendency but also a 16.3% rate of capsule invasion. Takada et al. [10] also reported that this subtype is prone to thyroid capsule invasion (28.6%), suggesting that it is prone to local invasion and metastasis. The lower rate of capsule invasion we observed in this subtype might be related to the tumor size, age distribution, local microenvironment, and immune status of the observed population. Takada et al. reported that the WF/FLS subtype is a rare tumor consisting of two components: fibromatosis with a BRAF mutation and a CTNNB1 mutation accompanied by β-catenin nuclear expression [20].

In the current cohort, the overall male-to-female incidence ratio for PTC was 1:3.04, aligning with previous epidemiological findings [16]. Additionally, the mean age at diagnosis for each subtype was below the 55-year threshold outlined in contemporary clinical guidelines. The predominance of early-stage cases likely reflects improvements in diagnostic capabilities and heightened awareness regarding the benefits of early screening.

Additionally, instances of discordant subtypes between bilateral or multifocal lesions have been reported. In the present study, 5.99% (46/768) of multifocal PTC cases were composed of two or more histopathological subtypes. Multifocality is a hallmark of PTC and is recognized as a contributor to its metastatic and recurrent potential [21]. Among the 12 PTC subtypes evaluated, only the DSS exhibited a significantly higher proportion of multifocal lesions (33.75%) compared to overall PTC, indicating that the DSS subtype may represent a high-risk group in terms of tumor spread and recurrence. Among the population we studied, there were 96 cases of bilateral thyroid cancer, 662 cases of unilateral cancer, 630 cases of single-focal cancer, and 217 cases of multifocal cancer. Our statistical data shows that there were 56 cases of bilateral single-focal cancer, 23 cases of single-focal cancer on one side and multifocal cancer on the other, 15 cases of bilateral multifocal cancer, 164 cases of unilateral multifocal cancer, and 495 cases of unilateral single-focal cancer. Since the number of patients with unilateral multifocal cancer (164 cases) was significantly higher than that of patients with bilateral thyroid cancer (96 cases), the association between the multiplicity of tumor foci and the involvement of both sides of the thyroid may be insignificant.

Lymph node metastasis in thyroid cancer is generally associated with elevated risks of distant metastasis and tumor recurrence [22]. In the present study, lymph node metastasis in region VI was observed in 29.25% of cases. The subtypes with the highest rates of metastasis were primarily those classified as high-risk. Interestingly, although the WLS is rare, it demonstrated a relatively high lymph node metastasis rate (42.86%), indicating the need for heightened surgical consideration in individuals with this subtype.

A significantly increased incidence of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (47.39%, p < 0.001) was observed when compared with benign nodules, particularly among those with the WLS subtype. This finding indicates a potential positive correlation between Hashimoto’s thyroiditis and the development of thyroid carcinoma [23]. Additionally, gravel-like calcifications were observed in 14.61% of cases. Although this increase was not statistically significant, previous studies have indicated that microcalcifications may represent a risk factor for PTC [24].

The overall incidence of familial inheritance in PTC was 5.01%. A higher familial prevalence was noted in several subtypes: SS (25%), WLS (11.11%), WF/FLS (9.24%), HS (6.49%), and CoCS (5.45%). In contrast, no familial cases were identified in TCS, SCS, or CCS, possibly due to small sample sizes. Pairwise comparisons indicated that the WF/FLS subtype may be more strongly influenced by genetic factors.

The overall gene mutation rate in PTC was 87.82%, with relatively consistent mutation frequencies across different subtypes. The high prevalence of mutations at the BRAF-V600E locus (81.69%) was especially notable, indicating a strong association with the development of PTC. The significantly higher mutation rate in PTC relative to benign nodules (p < 0.001) supports the role of genetic alterations in PTC pathogenesis. Although the BRAF V600E mutation rate we reported (81.7%) is higher than that reported by The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) in 2014 (approximately 59.7%) and by Liu et al. [25] (27.7%), the BRAF V600E mutation rate of 82.0% in T1a stage papillary thyroid carcinoma reported by Zhang et al. [26] is relatively consistent with our reported incidence. The discrepancies among various reports may be related to biases caused by differences in the pathological subtypes, clinical stages of the tumors, and sample sizes of the thyroid cancer study populations focused on. Additionally, the different BRAF V600E mutation rates among different racial groups in the study populations could also be a contributing factor.

Current treatment approaches for small thyroid carcinomas, particularly tumors measuring less than 1 cm in diameter, remain a subject of debate. Some researchers have advocated for observational management. However, the present findings contribute to the clinical literature by emphasizing that even tumors within the T1 stage, especially T1a, may demonstrate significant rates of lymph node metastasis. With a 29.25% rate of region VI lymph node metastasis observed in this study, early surgical intervention may be warranted in small lesions confirmed or highly suspected to be malignant. This decision should be individualized and guided by factors such as histological subtype aggressiveness, family history of hereditary thyroid cancer, presence of multiple foci, and relevant genetic mutations.

Multifocality, a recognized feature of PTC, adds complexity to staging and prognosis.

In some individuals, coexisting multiple subtypes, ranging from low risk to high risk, were identified in separate tumor foci. These individuals frequently presented with multiple lymph node metastases, despite having relatively small tumor diameters. This multifocal and multiclonal presentation complicates the accurate determination of T stage. Current guidelines often use the maximum diameter of the largest tumor for T staging in multifocal cases. However, this method may be insufficient for accurately staging cases involving multiple foci with varied subtypes. A unified standard is not yet available, and this lack of consensus underscores the need for future refinement in T staging protocols. The monoclonal versus polyclonal cell origin theory of PTC also merits further investigation as it may provide critical insights into the development of multiple primary thyroid carcinomas.

These findings, derived from a comprehensive analysis of each histopathological subtype, enhance the understanding of the heterogeneity in PTC and clarify the complexities associated with its subtype classification. The results hold practical clinical value by informing the evaluation of prognosis and aiding in subtype-specific treatment planning. Accurate and detailed identification of cellular subtypes enables individualized assessments of recurrence and metastasis risk, as well as tailored follow-up strategies for individuals diagnosed with different forms of PTC. Future research will aim to further elucidate the associations between cellular subtypes and gene mutations, and their respective influences on lymph node metastasis, capsular invasion, and the development of multifocal disease in patients with PTC.

Of particular note, the term “microcarcinoma” was removed in the 2022 classification, and the majority of the present cohort comprised microcarcinoma cases. Previous literature has rarely addressed subtype stratification in microcarcinomas; the stratified analysis employed in this study may therefore explain discrepancies in subtype prevalence. The large sample size (n = 768) further minimized single-center selection bias.

Although the incidence of non-PTC we reported was 1.54%, which was significantly lower than the incidence rate (10%~15%) reported in the literature [4], the main reason for this deviation might be the selection bias of the single-center study sample; however, this bias does not affect our research on the clinical biological behavior of the 12 subtypes of papillary thyroid cancer, because the incidence bias of the major subtype does not affect the structural proportion of the 12 subtypes of papillary thyroid cancer.

This study found that the incidence of diffuse sclerosing type and the rate of multiple lesions were high, the incidence of the encapsulated type was high and it was prone to be combined with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, the lymph node metastasis rate of hobnail (HS), tall cell, and DSS was high, the incidence of rare subtypes WF/FLS subtypes increased significantly and had a family genetic tendency as well as a higher rate of capsule invasion. These clinical characteristics of different cell subtypes make it necessary for surgeons to formulate individualized surgical plans and follow-up strategies when dealing with thyroid cancer foci.

Limitations

Our research is a single-center retrospective cross-sectional study. Although the sample size is relatively large, it has not been verified by external units and awaits validation by more similar studies. In this experiment, no multiple regression analysis was conducted to screen for independent predictive factors of lymph node metastasis. Additionally, the genetic testing mainly analyzed the impact of the BRAF gene on thyroid cancer, while other thyroid cancer-related mutation genes (such as RAS, TP53, and PIK3CA) were not included in the statistical analysis.

Contribution to the literature

This study is one of the largest single-center series systematically evaluating all 12 PTC subtypes under the 2022 WHO classification. By correlating clinical, pathological, and microenvironmental features, it establishes updated reference values for familial occurrence, multifocality, and nodal metastasis patterns in an East Asian cohort. These findings underscore the need for subtype-oriented clinical management and suggest that current TNM or ATA risk systems might benefit from refinement based on histological subtype behavior.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this cross-sectional analysis demonstrates that papillary thyroid carcinoma subtypes defined by the 2022 WHO classification exhibit distinct demographic, pathological, and molecular profiles. Subtypes such as hobnail, tall cell, diffuse sclerosing, and Warthin-like show aggressive features, while encapsulated, infiltrative follicular, and oncocytic types follow indolent courses. The high familial rate and increasing trend of the WF/FLS subtype provide novel insight into its possible genetic and microenvironmental influences.Recognizing these subtype-based differences may refine surgical decision-making, risk stratification, and postoperative surveillance strategies. Future multicenter studies integrating comprehensive genomic profiling (such as TERT, RAS, CCDC6-RET, TP53, and PIK3CA) are warranted to clarify subtype-specific oncogenic mechanisms.
