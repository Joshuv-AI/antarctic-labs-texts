Transcription Impairment of TMEM208 by ZBTB14 Suppresses Breast cancer Radiotherapy Resistance
Authors: Yan Li, Lili Liang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Zinc finger and BTB domain-containing protein (ZBTB) proteins have been implicated in different cellular processes, including DNA damage responses and cell cycle progression. However, the mechanism by which ZBTB14 modulates radiotherapy (RT) radioresistance (RT-R) remains to be elucidated. We aimed to elucidate the regulation mechanism of ZBTB14 in breast cancer (BC) RT-R. Using integrated bioinformatics prediction, ZBTB14 was identified as a hub transcription factor related to RT-R in BC. ZBTB14 was significantly under-expressed in non-responders and RT-R/BC cells, whereas its target transmembrane protein 208 (TMEM208) was significantly overexpressed in non-responders and RT-R/BC cells. Chromatin immunoprecipitation-qPCR and luciferase reporter assays revealed that ZBTB14 downregulated TMEM208 expression through transcriptional repression. Overexpression of ZBTB14 significantly inhibited the malignant biological behavior of BC cells and tumor growth in vivo, and further upregulation of TMEM208 reversed the biological activity and radiotherapy resistance of RT-R/BC cells inhibited by overexpression of ZBTB14.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10911-024-09573-1.

Introduction

Adjuvant radiation therapy has been shown to reduce locoregional recurrences and improve survival for early-stage to locally advanced breast cancer (BC) and has been part of the standard treatment paradigm for decades [1]. Imaging plays a central role in the diagnosis and staging of BC, and computed tomography scans can not only offer structural and morphologic information for the initial evaluation of breast lesions but remain the primary modality for radiation treatment planning locally in the breast [2]. Surgical treatment, the cornerstone in the management of early-stage BC, can be combined with radiotherapy (RT) to improve local control of the disease, leading to excellent survival outcomes [3]. However, RT radioresistance (RT-R), which results in RT failure, metastasis, cancer recurrence, and a poor prognosis, continues to be a main obstacle in improving treatment outcomes [4]. The mechanisms behind RT-R involve processes like hypoxia response, cell proliferation, DNA repair, apoptosis inhibition, and autophagy [5]. RT induces various DNA damage, encompassing various forms such as base damage, single-strand breaks, double-strand breaks (DSB), and DNA interstrand crosslinks, and the inability to fully repair DSBs is believed as the primary factor behind reduced cell survival following radiation exposure [6]. On the other hand, the DNA damage response system, which involves the phosphorylation of the histone variant H2AX (γ-H2AX), is vital in maintaining genomic integrity by facilitating the repair of DNA DSBs [7]. Therefore, identifying the molecules involved in the contribution to RT-R would assist in improving the treatment outcomes for patients with BC.

By analyzing the GEO dataset (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds/) GSE40640, we obtained zinc finger and BTB domain-containing protein 14 (ZBTB14; also known as ZF5, ZFP161 or ZNF478) as a candidate transcription factor (TF) related to RT-R in BC. Approximately 60 ZBTB proteins have been identified to date, many of which have been implicated in different cellular processes including DNA damage responses and cell cycle progression [8]. ZBTB14 has been identified to be downregulated in BC, and its downregulation was associated with adverse clinical outcomes of BC patients and the luminal B molecular subtype [9]. Still, the possible contribution of ZBTB14 to RT-R in BC remains largely unknown. Transmembrane protein 208 (TMEM208), a possible regulator of autophagy and endoplasmic reticulum stress [10] has thus been identified as a target ZBTB14 in the present study. A TMEM is a protein that spans the entire width of the lipid bilayer and is permanently anchored, and TMEMs function as channels to transport specific substances across the biological membranes [11]. Among the cellular processes in which TMEM proteins have been involved in cancer are the promotion or suppression of cell proliferation, invasion, migration, metastasis, modulation of the immune response, and response to antineoplastic drugs [12]. Therefore, we assumed that the possible role ZBTB14 played in RT-R in BC is elicited through its target TMEM208. Our discoveries might offer new potential targets for BC prognosis and treatment.

Materials and Methods

Patients

The samples used in this study have received consent from the Ethics Committee of Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital and were conducted in conformity with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants in the study received written informed consent. Between October 2020 and October 2021, samples of 22 female BC patients were collected from Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital. All of these patients received postoperative RT, and there were 10 cases with local recurrence. The clinical information collected is shown in Table 1. Patients with local recurrence in the breast and/or lymph nodes after RT were defined as non-responders.

Cell Lines and Culture

Human BC cells MDA-MB-231 cells (92020424, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) were placed in L-15 medium (L5520, Merck KGaA) supplemented with 2 mM L-Glutamine (G7513, Merck KGaA) and 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS, F2442, Merck KGaA). BT-549 (HTB-122, American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA, USA) cells were placed in Roswell Park Memorial Institute-1640 (30-2001, American Type Culture Collection) medium containing 0.023 U/mL insulin (I9278, Merck KGaA) and 10% FBS. Both cell lines were grown at 37 °C in an incubator at 5% CO2.

Short Hairpin RNA (shRNA)-knockdown, Virus Packaging, and Lentiviral Infection

Human ZBTB14 shRNA and lentivirus for overexpression (oe)-ZBTB14 and oe-TMEM208 were designed and purchased from Origene (Beijing, China). MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells were lentivirally infected at a multiplicity of infection = 10. Cells were transferred to a medium containing puromycin (A1113803, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) for three weeks and screened for cells with stable knockdown or overexpression.

Establishment of MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R Cells

Referring to the common protocol for RT of BC patients, MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells were exposed to small doses of X‑ray irradiation (2 Gy) using a 6-MV photon beam generated by Varian 21EX Linear Accelerator (13818, Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The cells were irradiated 25 times at regular intervals (a total of 50 Gy). After the cells were treated with RT and grew to approximately 90% confluence, they were trypsinized and passaged into a fresh medium for further culture. Cells were irradiated when they reached 70% confluence, and thereafter, multiple irradiations were continued until the total dose reached 50 Gy [13]. Radioresistant BC cells (MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R) were developed and used for subsequent experiments.

In Vivo Tumor Experiments

All animal studies in this study were conducted following the guidelines provided by the Animal Ethics Committee of Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital. Eight-week-old male BALB/C nude mice were purchased from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Changsha, Hunan, China). MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R (2 × 106) infected with oe-NC, oe-ZBTB14, oe-ZBTB14 + oe-NC, and oe-ZBTB14 + oe-TMEM208 were injected subcutaneously into mice (n = 5/group). Tumor volumes were calculated by the formula (a × b2)/2, where a is the major tumor axis and b is the minor tumor axis. The mice were regularly treated with radiation (16 Gy once every 3 days) starting on day 15 [14]. On day 35, the mice were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg), and the tumors were harvested for subsequent experiments.

RNA Isolation and RT-qPCR

Tumor tissue from BC patients and xenograft tumor tissue from nude mice were first cut into 5 × 10 mm sections. Tumor sections and MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R with different treatments were homogenized with 1 mL of TRIzol Reagent (15596026, I-presci, Beijing, China). The homogenate was added with 200 µL of chloroform and briefly centrifuged to induce phase separation. The upper aqueous phase was transferred to a new tube, and isopropanol was added to precipitate the RNA, followed by a series of ethanol washes. After that, 1 µg of RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using the First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit for RT-PCR (11483188001, Merck KGaA). RT-qPCR analysis was performed on a CFX Opus Deepwell Real-Time Fluorescence qPCR System using Premix Taq (RR003Q, Shanghai Zhengyu Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Relative gene expression was evaluated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with GAPDH mRNA as the endogenous control. Primers used were as follows: CTGCTACCATCAGGGACGTG and ACTGTCATCCTGATCCCCGA for ZBTB14; TGGTGCTTAGTGATTGCGGT and GATGAGATGCCGTCAGGAGG for TMEM208; and AATGGGCAGCCGTTAGGAAA and GCGCCCAATACGACCAAATC for GAPDH.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tumor tissues and xenograft tumors were fixed in 10% formalin, dehydrated, cleared, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned. The sections were heat-treated for antigen retrieval, treated with hydrogen peroxide, and sealed with BSA containing 0.3% goat serum. The sections were incubated with the antibodies including ZBTB14 (1:100, PA5-62236, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.), TMEM208 (1:250, 23882-1-AP, ProteinTech Group, Chicago, IL, USA), γ-H2AX (phospho S139, 1:100, ab81299, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and Ki67 (1:1000, ab15580, Abcam) at 4 ℃ overnight, and with the horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-linked goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:100, HAF008, Novus Biological Inc., Littleton, CO, USA) for 1 h. The sections were sealed for microscopy observation after color development by diaminobenzidine and nuclei counter-staining with hematoxylin.

By recording the percentage of positive staining (0 = negative, 1 ≤ 10%, 2 = 10–50%, 3 ≥ 50%) and the intensity of staining (0 = absent, 1 = weak, 2 = moderate, 3 = strong), each sample from patients was scored by multiplying the percentage of positive staining with the intensity of staining [15]. The results were assessed by two pathologists who were unaware of the grouping information, and the final score was expressed as the average of the two experts’ scores. For IHC results of xenograft tumors, positive cell rates were counted by ImageJ.

Western Blot Assays

Tumor tissues or cells were treated with RIPA lysis and extraction buffer (89901, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). The supernatant was collected by centrifugation and detected using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (GK10009, Glpbio, Montclair, CA, USA). The protein was separated on SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)/Tween to prevent nonspecific binding and incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. All samples were incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody [HRP] (1:1000, HAF008, Novus Biological) or goat anti-mouse secondary antibody [HRP] (1:5000, ab6728, Abcam) and detected via chemiluminescence detection system (E411-04; NanJing Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd, Nanjing, China). Primary antibodies used in this study were as follows: ZBTB14 (1:1000, H00007541-M02, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.), TMEM208 (1:1000, 23882-1-AP, ProteinTech Group), and GAPDH (1:5000, NB300-327, Novus Biological).

Colony Formation Assay

MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R after different infections were seeded at 5 × 104 cells in six-well plates and incubated for 24 h under the desired culture conditions. The number of colonies formed in each well was counted under a microscope after 2 h of irradiation using different doses (0, 2, 4, 6 Gy) of γ-rays, and cell survival was calculated [16].

Wound Healing Assay

MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R after different infections were seeded at 2 × 105 cells/well in six-well plates. When the cells were growing at approximately 90% confluence, a 200 µL pipette tip was used to create a scratch on the confluent monolayer of cells. Detached cells were removed by PBS, and the cells were subjected to 8 Gy γ-irradiation [17]. The medium was replaced with DMEM containing 0.1% FBS every 12 h. Images were taken at the indicated time points (0 and 36 h) to calculate the wound healing rate.

Transwell Assay

Matrigel Basement Membrane Substrate (100 µL, CLS354234, Corning Glass Works, Corning, N.Y., USA) was applied to the apical chamber of the insert. MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R cells (2 × 105 cells/well) in serum-free medium were subsequently added to the apical chamber, and medium containing 10% FBS was loaded into the basolateral chamber for a 24-h incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2), during which RT at 8 Gy was given. Uninvaded cells remaining on the upper surface of the membrane were rinsed using PBS. Cells invading the lower chamber were stained using a 5% crystal violet stain, and the stained cells were counted by microscopy.

Terminal Deoxynucleotidyl Transferase (TdT)-Mediated dUTP Nick end Labeling (TUNEL) and Immunofluorescence

TUNEL assay was performed using Click-iT Plus TUNEL Assay Kits (C10617, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R cells were seeded into a 12-well plate and fixed for 30 min using 4% paraformaldehyde. The cells were permeabilized with PBS containing 0.2% Triton X-100 for 15 min, sealed in PBS containing 2% BSA for 1 h, and incubated with 50 µL of TUNEL staining solution for 60 min at 37 °C in the dark or anti-γ-H2AX (1:250, ab81299, Abcam) overnight at 37 °C in the dark (8 Gy γ-irradiation). Before observation, DAPI (C0065, Beijing Solarbio Life Sciences Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was used for counterstaining for 30 min (TUNEL staining), and immunofluorescence was performed with goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 594 (1:2000, R37117, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) at room temperature for 1 h. The cells were analyzed under a fluorescence microscope.

Flow Cytometry

After 8 Gy γ-irradiation, the cells were detached by trypsin and stained with 20 µg/mL of Annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) (IF0080, Solarbio) and 50 µg/mL of propidium iodide (PI, P8080, Solarbio), while flow cytometry was performed to analyze the change in apoptosis rate.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

The BC cells were cross-linked using 1% formaldehyde at room temperature using the ChIP Assay Kit with Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (YTB3092, Biolab, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the cross-linking reaction was terminated with glycine. Cells were resuspended in an SDS buffer solution and subjected to sonication to fragment the chromatin. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation and incubated with anti-ZBTB14 (1:20, sc-514298, Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc., Santa Cruz, CA, USA) or mouse IgG2a isotype control (1:50, ab18413, Abcam) at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, 80 µL of Protein A/G magnetic beads were added and slowly rotated or oscillated for 60 min at 4 °C to precipitate. The magnetic beads were separated by placing them on a magnetic rack for 10 s. The supernatant was removed, and the magnetic beads were washed. The purified DNA was subjected to PCR for amplification.

Luciferase Report Assay

For the construction of the TMEM208 luciferase reporter gene, we inserted the TMEM208 promoter sequence obtained from UCSC (https://genome.ucsc.edu/cgi-bin/hgGateway) into the pMCS-Red Firefly Luc vector (16155, Thermo Fisher). The plasmid was co-transfected with oe-ZBTB14 into BC cells and incubated for 48 h. Luciferase activity was detected using the Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (11401ES76, Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 10.2.0 software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA) and presented as mean ± SD of at least three independent experiments. Differences between any two variables were done by using the unpaired t-test. The significance of the differences among three or more groups was conducted by using one-way or two-way ANOVA with Tukey or Sidak post-test. p-values less than 0.05 were deemed statistically significant.

Results

ZBTB14 Is Significantly Under-Expressed in Tumor Tissues of BC RT Responders

We analyzed the mRNA profiles of lymphocytes from BC patients before and 6 h after in vitro irradiation with 5 Gy in the GSE40640 dataset (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE40640) (Fig. 1A). Genes associated with BC radiation therapy resistance and the human TF list were downloaded from GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) and HumanTFDB (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/HumanTFDB#!/), respectively. Finally, the differentially expressed genes (adj. p. < 0.05) in the GSE40640 dataset, BC radiation therapy resistance-related genes, and human TF were analyzed by Jvenn (https://jvenn.toulouse.inrae.fr/app/ example.html) to obtain the 23 intersections (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Table 1). ZBTB14 was significantly under-expressed in the GSE40640 dataset (LogFC = -0.3617429). Therefore, we hypothesized that the significantly low expression of ZBTB14 is one of the causes of RT in BC.

To further elucidate the correlation between ZBTB14 and RT-R in BC patients, the expression of ZBTB14 in 22 BC patients undergoing postoperative radiotherapy was examined (10 non-responders and 12 responders). The mRNA and protein expression of ZBTB14 in tumor tissues of non-responders was found to be significantly lower than that of responders by RT-qPCR and IHC (Fig. 1C, D).

First, we analyzed ZBTB14 expression levels in parental BC (MDA-MB-231, BT-549) cells and radiotherapy-resistant BC cell lines (MDA-MB-231/RT-R, BT-549/RT-R). RT-qPCR and Western blot assays revealed that compared with MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells, ZBTB14 mRNA and protein expression were significantly downregulated in MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R cells (Fig. 2A, B).

To explore the association between ZBTB14 and RT-R in BC, we performed ZBTB14 overexpression lentiviral infection on MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R cells and verified the success of overexpression infection by RT-qPCR (Fig. 2C). Colony formation assay showed that the proliferative activity of MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R cells was significantly reduced and radiosensitivity was increased after overexpression of ZBTB14 (Fig. 2D). Under the irradiation of 8 Gy, the migration (Fig. 2E) and invasion (Fig. 2F) potential of MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R cells were diminished upon ZBTB14 upregulation. The results of immunofluorescence staining revealed that overexpression of ZBTB14 resulted in elevated γ-H2AX expression in MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R cells (Fig. 2G). A significantly higher apoptotic rate was detected by flow cytometry in MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R cells overexpressing ZBTB14 (Fig. 2H).

ZBTB14 Overexpression Impairs BC Cell Growth and Radioresistance in vivo

MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R cells infected with or without overexpression of ZBTB14 were injected into mice and treated with radiation, and it was found that MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R cells overexpressing ZBTB14 grew more slowly in mice than those without (Fig. 3A). As expected, the protein expression of ZBTB14 was upregulated in the tumor tissues formed by MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R cells overexpressing ZBTB14 (Fig. 3B). Ki67 and γ-H2AX levels in tumor sections were detected using IHC. Ki67 expression was decreased in tumor tissues of the overexpression ZBTB14-treated mice (Fig. 3C), and γ-H2AX expression was elevated instead (Fig. 3D).

ZBTB14 Transcriptionally Represses TMEM208 Expression

ZBTB14 has been revealed to act as a transcription repressor gene [18]. Genes negatively correlated with ZBTB14 expression were downloaded on the University of Alabama at Birmingham Cancer Data Analysis Portal (UALCAN, https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/). The top 25 genes ranked by negative correlation coefficients are shown in Fig S1A, and four intersections were obtained with the differentially expressed genes (adj. p. < 0.05) in the GSE40640 dataset on Jvenn: U1 small nuclear ribonucleoprotein A (SNRPA), Protein transport protein Sect. 61 subunit beta (SEC61B), TMEM208, and Coiled-coil-helix-coiled-coil-helix domain-containing protein 10, mitochondrial (CHCHD10) (Fig S1B). Survival prognostic analysis by Kaplan-Meier Plotter (https://kmplot.com/analysis/index.php?p=background) revealed that SNRPA (Fig S1C), SEC61B (Fig S1D), and CHCHD10 (Fig S1E) had no significant prognostic value in BC, which were excluded. The prognostic analysis of TMEM208 in BC patients showed that BRCA patients with high expression of TMEM208 had significantly worse OS (Fig S1F). TMEM208 was significantly highly expressed in the GSE40640 dataset (LogFC = 0.33451165). The ChIP-seq results in the JASPAR website (https://jaspar.elixir.no/) indicated that ZBTB14 had a binding site in the TMEM208 promoter (chr16: 67226831–67227180) (Fig S1G).

Consistent with the prediction results, the mRNA and protein expression of TMEM208 was much higher in the non-responders relative to responders (Fig. 4A, B). Relative to the parental cells, the MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R cells exhibited upregulated expression, as revealed by RT-qPCR (Fig. 4C) and western blot analysis (Fig. 4D). In MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R cells overexpressing ZBTB14, we found that the transcriptional level of TMEM208 was suppressed (Fig. 4E). Subsequently, it was also detected by ChIP-qPCR that overexpression of ZBTB14 significantly increased the occupancy of ZBTB14 in the TMEM208 promoter region (Fig. 4F). In addition, luciferase reporter assay also showed that overexpression of ZBTB14 significantly reduced the transcriptional activity of TMEM208 (Fig. 4G).

Restoration of TMEM208 Promotes Malignant Biological Behavior in MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R Cells Overexpressing ZBTB14

To demonstrate the role of the ZBTB14/TMEM208 axis in RT-R, we combined TMEM208 overexpression lentiviral treatment with overexpression of ZBTB14 in MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R cells. Overexpression of TMEM208 reversed the downregulation of TMEM208 expression by oe-ZBTB14, yet had no significant effect on mRNA expression of ZBTB14 (Fig. 5A). As indicated by colony formation assays, the reduction in viability caused by oe-ZBTB14 was reversed by overexpression of TMEM208 (Fig. 5B). At 8 Gy γ-irradiation, the migratory and invasive ability of MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R cells treated with TMEM208 overexpression was significantly increased (Fig. 5C, D). TUNEL analysis showed that the apoptotic rate of MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R cells after irradiation with an 8 Gy dose of γ-rays was repressed by reactivation of TMEM208 (Fig. 5E). Likewise, overexpression of TMEM208 resulted in a decrease in γ-H2AX expression in MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R cells (Fig. 5F).

Overexpression of TMEM208 Eliminates ZBTB14-Induced Radiosensitization of RT-R Cells in vivo

MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R cells with different infections were delivered into mice, and after combined overexpression of TMEM208, MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R cells again showed over-proliferation (Fig. 6A). RT-qPCR detection of TMEM208 mRNA expression in tumors revealed that overexpression of TMEM208 enhanced TMEM208 expression in the presence of overexpression of ZBTB14 (Fig. 6B). The harvested tumor tissues were subjected to IHC. Combined overexpression of TMEM208 led to enhanced expression of Ki67 (Fig. 6C) and reduced expression of γ-H2AX (Fig. 6D).

Discussion

BC remains the leading malignant tumor for women, and RT is an integral part of the multidisciplinary management of BC [19]. The exact mechanisms and molecular occurrences incurred by RT have not been completely elucidated, with research on the impact of RT becoming an increasingly prominent topic in cancer therapy, including BC [20]. The current study demonstrates that the TF ZBTB14 hampers RT-R and tumor growth of BC cells through transcriptional repression of the TMEM208 gene.

Among the ZNF family, ZNF750 and ZNF224 were lowly expressed in BC, and their expressions were significantly associated with BC prognosis [21]. Moreover, ZFP proteins are involved in the development of drug resistance in colon cancer [22]. However, the role of ZNF478 (ZBTB14) in BC has not been revealed. It has been recently shown by O’Sullivan et al. that knockdown of ZNF432 inhibited phospho-DNA-PKcs and increased RAD51 foci formation following irradiation [23]. Kim et al. also reported that loss of ZBTB14 sensitized colorectal cancer HCT116 cells to DNA damage [24], indicating the possible linkage between ZBTB14 dysregulation and DNA repair-related RT-R in pan cancers. Interestingly, high H2AX-expressing tumors were associated with upregulated DNA repair gene sets [25]. In the present study, we found that the knockdown of ZBTB14 in MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R cells led to enhanced γ-H2AX not only in these cells but also in tumor tissues formed by these cells, which might explain the reduced proliferation, migration, invasion, and apoptosis resistance. ZNF575 has been revealed to impair colorectal cancer growth by targeting the promoter region and promoting the transcription of p53, a well-known tumor suppressor [26], which made us anticipate that the role of ZBTB14 in regulating RT-R in BC was related to its target. ZBTB14 was originally cloned as a transcriptional repressor on the mouse c-myc promoter and contains the Kruppel-type zinc fingers and a conserved POZ domain [27]. Therefore, we searched for the negatively correlated genes of ZBTB14 in the online algorithm.

TMEM208 has been identified to be transcriptionally regulated by HIF-1α, a critical transcription factor [28]. In this study, it was chosen for its significant upregulation after RT and outstanding prognostic value for BC patients. Likewise, 3 TMEMs (TMEM273, TMEM164, and TMEM125) were used to construct a risk model to predict the prognosis of lung adenocarcinoma, and patients with a low-risk score were characterized by increased activation of immunity, tumor cell apoptosis and had better disease-free survival and overall survival [29]. Many of its counterparts, including TMEM60, TMEM100, and TMEM229A have been revealed to engage in cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in glioma [30], prostate cancer [31], as well as non‑small cell lung cancer [32]. More relevantly, TMEM proteins have been described to be involved at the early stage of metastasis and/or before the emergence of chemotherapy and RT resistance [33]. For instance, TMEM205 induced M2 polarization of tumor-associated macrophages to promote cisplatin resistance in gastric cancer [34]. Under the condition of triple-negative BC, TMEM63A has been reported to function as a novel oncogene to promote cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and xenograft tumor growth and lung metastasis in vivo [35]. Interestingly, in a cellular model of BC, expression of TMEM97 parallels radioligand binding of 18F-ISO-1, and 18F-ISO-1 correlates with tumor proliferative status as assessed by Ki-67 [36, 37], which was in line with our in vivo evidence. It was evident that the role of TMEM proteins in radioresistance has yet to be clear. Here, we found that the overexpression of TMEM208, in the presence of ZBTB14 upregulation, enhanced the malignant phenotype and RT-R of MDA-MB-231/RT-R and BT-549/RT-R cells.

Despite the inspiring findings, there remain several limitations of the study. First, according to the bioinformatics analyses, there might be more TFs involved in the regulation of RT-R in BC. Furthermore, the detailed mechanism through which TMEM208 inhibits γ-H2AX-mediated DSB and induces RT-R remains to be explored. We will focus on these issues in our future studies.

Conclusion

In summary, we have identified a new mechanism by which ZBTB14 enhances radiosensitivity through TMEM208 transcription repression. These results expand our understanding of the role of TMEM208 in RT-R to BC. Moreover, we provide new insights into the ZBTB14/TMEM208 axis in therapeutic targets for reversing cancer radioresistance.
