Use of On-Table Cone Beam Computed Tomography–Guided Adaptive Radiation Therapy to Account for Subcutaneous Emphysema Following Placement of a Tracheostomy Tube in Head and Neck Patient
Authors: Fiona Li, Alexander R. Podgorsak, Sean M. Tanny, Dandan Zheng, Neil Joyce, Deepinder Singh
Published: 2026

Introduction

Tracheostomy is commonly performed in patients with head and neck malignancies to secure the airway in the setting of tumor burden and treatment-related edema. Subcutaneous emphysema, although unlikely, may occur following a tracheostomy placement when air leaks into the soft tissues of the neck. Extensive subcutaneous emphysema can cause rapid and marked distortion of cervical anatomy, which poses unique challenges for radiation therapy treatment planning, immobilization, and treatment delivery, including dose perturbation and inhomogeneity resulting from air pockets that may evolve daily.

The occurrence of subcutaneous emphysema following computed tomography (CT) simulation poses a substantial challenge, given that the initial anatomic data set may not accurately represent the patient’s actual treatment geometry. Despite the potential impact on radiation therapy delivery accuracy, there is limited literature addressing the management of such cases within radiation oncology. In this report, we present a patient with head and neck cancer who developed significant subcutaneous emphysema following the tracheostomy tube placement after CT simulation and prior to treatment initiation. The report highlights the role of adaptive radiation therapy in responding to this unanticipated anatomic change and discusses practical considerations for maintaining safe and effective treatment delivery.

Case report

Patient and treatment information

A 67-year-old man with a 55-pack-a-year smoking history and prior Hodgkin lymphoma treated under the Princess Margaret Hospital regimen1 in 2019 was presented in 2025 with dysphasia and thick mucus. Over the preceding month, he lost 50 pounds of weight. A positron emission tomography/CT scan revealed a large mass at the bilateral base of the tongue, extending into the right tonsillar pillar involving the vallecula, oropharynx, left hypopharynx, and supraglottic larynx. There were no suspicious pulmonary/distant metastases or any other lesions in the nasal cavity or nasopharynx. Biopsy confirmed human papillomavirus–associated palatine tonsil squamous cell carcinoma, stage III (AJCC 8th edition). Definitive chemoradiation with cisplatin and 70 Gy in 35 fractions was prescribed as first-line therapy. The radiation protocol is 60 Gy to the subclinical disease and nodal region (primary target volume 60 Gy, PTV60) with a simultaneous integrated boost to 70 Gy to the primary tumor (PTV70). Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and any accompanying images. The patient was informed about the nature and purpose of the report, and all reasonable efforts have been made to protect the patient's identity and confidentiality.

Tracheostomy tube placement and anatomic change

For radiation therapy treatment planning, the patient initially underwent a CT simulation on a SOMATOM go.Open Pro (Siemens Healthineers), and a standard image guided radiation therapy (IGRT) plan (plan 0) was performed according to our departmental protocol on an Ethos linac (Varian Medical Systems) along with concurrent chemotherapy. Unfortunately, shortly before the initiation of chemoradiation, the patient developed dyspnea with stridor. Given the enlarging airway mass, an awake tracheostomy was performed. Radiation therapy planning decision

A full replan based on the resimulation images would have required several days to complete. To avoid delaying the planned concurrent chemoradiation therapy, and rather than waiting for the subcutaneous emphysema to resolve, a clinical decision was made to initiate radiation therapy according to the original schedule using an online direct-to-unit (DTU) adaptive planning approach. For this workflow, the original plan (plan 0) was rapidly converted into a reference adaptive plan (plan 1) within the Ethos Treatment Planning System (v2.0). The initial simulation images and contours, reflecting the pretracheostomy anatomy, were retained, with minor modifications to PTV60 to account for anterior anatomic changes associated with the tracheostomy tube. The plan was subsequently reoptimized incorporating these limited modifications using the Intelligent Optimization Engine v2.0. Intelligent Optimization Engine is Ethos’ automated plan-generation algorithm, which creates treatment plans based on a user-provided, prioritized, and ordered list of clinical goals2.

First-day online adaptation

For the first fraction, the Ethos adaptive workflow was initiated with the acquisition of a HyperSight cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) image (Fig. 1c). The images demonstrated partial resolution of the subcutaneous emphysema. Automatically generated target volumes and organs-at-risk (OAR) contours were carefully reviewed by the attending physician and medical physicist. Overall, the contours were deemed acceptable; however, manual edits were performed where necessary, particularly in regions affected by the tracheostomy tube.

As anticipated, the adaptive plan demonstrated improved dosimetric fidelity to the prescription intent for the altered anatomy compared with the scheduled plan and was therefore selected for treatment delivery. Including a postadaptation verification CBCT scan, the total treatment time was approximately 43 minutes. Figure 2 compares the dose distributions for the reference (top), scheduled (middle), and adaptive (bottom) plans. The scheduled plan illustrates a suboptimal dose distribution that would have been delivered without online adaptation, potentially resulting in systematic underdosing of the target throughout the treatment course. Additionally, the dose-volume histogram for the adaptive plan demonstrated a sharper dose falloff, reflecting improved target coverage and dose conformity, and more effective sparing of adjacent normal tissues. Converting plan to IGRT with offline adaptation as needed

The Ethos platform supports 2 treatment modalities: IGRT or daily online adaptive radiation therapy. Following treatment on day 1, the patient’s anatomy was assessed by the clinical team to be reasonably stable, and the dosimetric impact of the gradually resolving subcutaneous emphysema was considered clinically insignificant. Accordingly, the day-1 adaptive plan was converted to an IGRT plan for subsequent fractions per our standard departmental head and neck treatment protocol. Offline daily image review and cumulative dose evaluation were assessed to evaluate the need for further offline adaptation.

The emphysema was assessed daily on imaging by the physicist, and the decision to replan was made by the physician. As shown in Fig. 4, the subcutaneous emphysema had completely resolved by day 9, and ongoing daily imaging and dosimetric review did not indicate a need for offline replanning at that time. Later in the treatment course, anatomic changes related to patient weight loss were deemed sufficiently significant to warrant adaptation. An off-table adaptive replan was therefore generated using the day 27 CBCT image set, implemented beginning with fraction 28, and used through treatment completion.Figure 4Coronal view of the patient demonstrating the emphysema resolution comparing (a) day 1 of treatment and (b) day 9 of treatment.Figure 4 dummy alt text

Discussion

In the absence of the Ethos 2.0 system, the management of this patient would have differed substantially. Within a conventional radiation therapy workflow, changes in body habitus—whether due to weight loss, the introduction of a foreign object within or adjacent to the target volume, or normal tumor dynamics—typically require repeat CT simulation and treatment replanning. This process can take several days and may result in clinically significant treatment delays.

Adaptive and IGRT are the 2 modes available under the Ethos Treatment Planning Management System. During adaptive, the normal tissues are automatically contoured based on the daily CBCT. The targets are deformed based on the influencing structures, which are closest to the target and have the greatest impact on the target contouring.3 On the contrary, the IGRT workflow is like a conventional workflow, in which contours are rigidly propagated onto the daily CBCT for dose calculation. For converting the plan from adaptive to IGRT or vice versa, a plan revision or a new plan based on the previous fraction needs to be made. Converting from an adaptive workflow to an IGRT workflow requires a plan revision; however, using the adaptive plan during on-couch sessions, together with Intelligent Optimization Engine, streamlines and accelerates the process. For this patient, the adaptive plan from the first session was used as the reference for subsequent fractions.

In this case, the use of the Ethos DTU adaptive workflow enabled rapid plan generation following tracheostomy placement, thereby avoiding any delay in the initiation of the planned concurrent chemoradiation therapy. The availability of high-quality HyperSight CBCT imaging supported accurate online adaptation on the first treatment day, as well as ongoing anatomic and dosimetric monitoring after subsequent conversion to an IGRT workflow. Although daily online adaptive radiation therapy could theoretically be employed following the initial DTU treatment to account for day-to-day anatomic variations, an IGRT approach was selected after weighing the clinical benefits against associated workflow and resource demands, in accordance with our head and neck protocol. Notably, the adaptive plan generated during the initial on-couch session, including updated target and OAR contours, served as a robust reference for subsequent fractions, substantially reducing workflow burden for both the patient and the treatment team. Later in the treatment course, when clinically significant anatomic changes related to weight loss emerged, this established adaptive framework facilitated efficient offline adaptation using CBCT imaging, allowing timely plan modification without disruption of treatment continuity.

Aside from a conventional replanning workflow, an alternative strategy for managing subcutaneous emphysema prior to resuming radiation therapy is the application of compression bandages. However, this intervention itself may delay treatment initiation by approximately 1 week.4 In contrast, expectant management without medical intervention may require up to 1 month for spontaneous resolution of the emphysema.

HyperSight imaging within the DTU adaptive workflow has been reported for several additional treatment sites. The approach has also been explored in palliative settings, in which conventional CT simulation is entirely omitted. In these workflows, reference plans are generated using diagnostic imaging, yielding clinically acceptable treatment plans while shortening the overall workflow—including reduced planning time—and decreasing the number of patient visits.5,6 Other unconventional applications include single-visit stereotactic body radiation therapy for lung adenocarcinoma in a patient with uncontrolled Parkinson’s disease, in which the entire treatment process was completed in just over 1 hour.6 Another potential workflow involves the use of plan libraries, whereby a reference plan is selected from a repository of anatomically similar cases rather than generated from the patient’s own simulation scan.7 Collectively, these approaches aim to eliminate the need for a separate CT simulation visit, thereby reducing overall treatment burden for patients.
