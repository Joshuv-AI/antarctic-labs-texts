Quality analysis of photometers using vertical window illuminance measurements
Authors: Przemysław Tabaka, Tomasz Ściężor
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn urban areas, one of the main forms of light pollution is the intrusion of light through windows into residential buildings. This phenomenon can harm human health, so standards have been introduced to define limits for vertical illuminance. This study aims to assess the levels of outdoor lighting on the windows of residential buildings in Poland in terms of compliance with standards for protection against intrusive light, as well as to compare the results obtained using various commercially available photometers. The paper presents the results of field measurements of vertical illuminance on the window surfaces of 17 residential buildings in several cities in Poland. The measurements were made with 22 photometers representing different metrological levels. The measuring instruments selected for the study are a sample of commercially available photometers. Despite the same lighting conditions on the surface of the individual windows, discrepancies can be seen in the measured illuminance values of the different photometers. The vertical illuminance measurements were subjected to statistical analysis. It was found that of the 22 photometers, only a few were suitable for assessing obtrusive light from outdoor lighting installations. It has also been noted that on window surfaces, in buildings located on a busy street in the city centre, the illuminance can reach up to 100 lx, many times exceeding the permissible values of 1, 2, 5, 10 and 25 lx.Keywords: Outdoor lighting, Obtrusive light, Light trespass, Vertical illumination, Lux meter, Apartment buildingSubject terms: Ecology, Environmental sciences, Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

Outdoor lighting installations have various functions. For example the lighting of roads, pavements, paths, or squares in residential areas affects the comfort and sense of security of the residents1–4. Streets play a special role in the transport system of cities. Their lighting plays an important role for drivers and pedestrians. Stationary road lighting has been reported in the literature to reduce accidents after nightfall5,6. Outdoor lighting often also fulfils an aesthetic aspect. For example, façade lighting influences the image of a building and gives it an appropriate atmosphere6–8. Outdoor lighting also has an informative function. Examples are illuminated advertisements conveying specific information about the products or services on offer. Unfortunately, the use of outdoor lighting installations often involves the emission of light flux towards objects that are not the target of the lighting. Light from outdoor lighting equipment located near residential properties often illuminates the windows of buildings, disrupting the normal resting conditions of residents. Excessive brightening at night in living spaces (particularly bedrooms), regardless of the user’s wishes, can be a source of discomfort. While local lighting installations are a significant source of light pollution, larger-scale events contribute substantially to this problem. Large outdoor events, such as music festivals, significantly increase light pollution. For instance, a study conducted during the Lollapalooza festival in Berlin demonstrated that such events can dramatically improve local light pollution9. This excessive brightening affects not only residential areas but also entire urban ecosystems. Such events pose particular challenges, as they attract thousands of attendees and generate intense lighting, resulting in a significant increase in light emission. In addition to disrupting ecosystems, excessive artificial lighting can seriously affect human health.

Humans have adapted over millions of years to the natural day-night cycle. Exposure to light at night-time adversely affects human health10–13. It disrupts the human circadian rhythm14 and can be a source of obesity in humans15, and cancers16,17. So the impact of light here has to be considered as a nuisance. There is no doubt, therefore, that excessive and nuisance light emission fits into the general definition of light pollution, which nowadays affects all areas related to the broadly understood environment. Given that artificial lighting provided by outdoor lighting installations has an adverse effect on the well-being of building occupants, requirements have been developed to reduce obtrusive light. One key objective of this study is to assess the levels of light falling on the windows of residential buildings to determine whether they meet standards for protection against intrusive light. This analysis will help answer whether the measured values comply with the requirements or may negatively impact residents’ comfort.

Any measuring transducer used to measure illumination parameters should respond to particular wavelengths similarly to the human eye. Unfortunately, the spectral sensitivities of the photovoltaic cells used in the photometric heads of illuminance meters often deviate from the standard sensitivity curve of the human eye. Therefore it is necessary to use special filters that correct the sensitivity of the photometric head to obtain results closer to reality.

The quantity characterising the nuisance consisting of illumination from outside the window is the vertical illuminance. To measure the illuminance, a photometer called an illuminance meter is used. This device is often referred to as a ‘lux meter,’ especially in the context of practical applications. The most essential component of an illuminance meter is the photometric head, which can receive visible radiation of any spectral distribution. It is in the photometric head that the energy of radiation is converted into an electrical quantity (current or voltage) proportional to its value, which is displayed in lux. For the illuminance recorded by the meter to correspond to the reference value, the meter must meet several requirements18. Among the most important of these is the spectral and spatial correction of the photometric head of the illuminance meter. The task of the photometric head is to cause the generated response to match the CIE normal observer’s assessment (spectral correction) and for the indicated illuminance value to vary with the cosine of the angle of incidence (spatial correction).

Unfortunately, the spectral sensitivity of the detectors used in the photometric heads of illuminance meters often deviates from the sensitivity curve of the human eye, requiring the use of appropriate correction filters. These filters aim to match the sensitivity characteristics of the head to the spectral curve V(λ), but the quality of this correction depends on the technological solutions and materials used. The better the materials and filters used, the higher the cost of the illuminance meter. This problem is widely described in the literature19,20.

Another important metrological requirement is spatial correction. In measurement practice, the light flux falls on the photometric head at different angles. For the same light flux falling from a direction other than perpendicular, the illuminance meter reading should change according to the cosine function. If the direction of the light beam is closer to the perpendicular, more light will enter the cell, which will be the source of measurement error. To compensate for this error, manufacturers use various solutions, most commonly an overlay made of a diffusing element.

In addition to photometers from reputable manufacturers, there are many meters available on the market that do not meet many of the requirements. Unfortunately, photometers that are characterised by poor quality in terms of spectral and spatial matching are very often used in measurement practice (due to their attractive price). The price range of the illuminance meters used in the study is considerable. The cheapest model (illuminance meter no. 21) costs around €359, while the price of the most expensive illuminance meter reaches nearly €30,000 (illuminance meter 1). An analysis of the available data on illuminance meters shows a significant relationship between the price of the instrument and its measurement accuracy. Higher-priced illuminance meters usually have a lower total error, which translates into higher measurement precision. Customers purchasing a new illuminance meter are often convinced that the measurement results obtained will correspond to the actual state of affairs. The certificate of calibration of an illuminance meter also does not guarantee the correctness of indications. The certificate does not contain information about the influence of the spectral distribution of the radiation emitted by different types of light sources on the readings of a given illuminance meter. The included data refer only to the incandescent lamp used during the calibration of the illuminance meter. This means that the user of the illuminance meter has no way of knowing what error the results obtained will have. It is worth mentioning that illuminance meters are not subject to legal metrological control. The national rules on legalisation have been replaced by Directive 2014/32/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council21. Accordingly, the second objective of this study is to evaluate the performance of measurement instruments in real field conditions, focusing on potential discrepancies between their readings. This analysis will help determine how different devices perform in practice and identify differences in their measurements, which may be important for the reliability and comparability of the data obtained.

The issue of the influence of the lux meter used on the value of the measured illuminance is not new19,20,22. However, the experimental measurements were carried out under laboratory conditions, which undoubtedly differ from those in the field. The number of papers dealing with the issue is limited23–25. In these works, only one type of illuminance meter was used for horizontal illuminance measurements. Similarly, in the publication26, which mainly focuses on luminance measurements, a single lux meter was used to assess vertical illuminance.

Studies indicate that intrusive light penetrating building interiors can affect the microclimate, disrupting conditions conducive to regeneration and rest. Even low levels of this light can disrupt the diurnal rhythm and cause chronic inflammation, which affects sleep quality and health27. In pregnant women, exposure to nocturnal light can lead to changes in the gut microflora, with health consequences for mother and child28. Intrusive light sources, especially LEDs, can disrupt occupant comfort by brightening indoor environments29,30, which affects mental and physical health31. Results suggest that uncontrolled intrusive light disrupts the indoor microclimate and may have negative health effects, including those related to diurnal rhythm and melatonin production32,33.

The aim of this study was therefore not only to determine the level of light falling on the window surfaces of residential buildings but also to compare the readings of different measuring instruments in real field conditions. The first part of the study focused on assessing the level of light falling on windows to determine compliance with requirements for protection against intrusive light. The second part of the study aimed to analyze the results obtained by various photometers, enabling their comparison and evaluation regarding their suitability for assessing intrusive light.

To achieve these objectives, the study employed a variety of measurement methods and used several models of photometers, whose performance was compared under the same lighting conditions. A detailed description of the measurement procedures, equipment used, and methods of data analysis is provided in the following section.

Materials and methods

Vertical illuminance limits

The requirements for limiting obtrusive light from outdoor lighting installations are given in the European standard EN 12464-2:2014-0534 and the International Commission on Illumination technical report CIE 150:201735. The illuminance limits depend on the environmental zone, which varies according to the brightness of the surroundings and are denoted by the symbols E0, E1, E2, E3 and E4. The brightest area is zone E4, while the darkest area is zone E0.

Values that are acceptable in the early evening are not necessarily acceptable in the later hours, which is why a distinction is additionally made between pre-curfew and post-curfew time (Table 1). The dimming time is defined as the time during which strict obtrusive light requirements apply34; however, often the conditions for the use of lighting are set and controlled by local authorities. In case no curfew regulations are available, the higher values shall not be exceeded and the lower values should be taken as preferable limits.

In Poland, the document with the status of a legal act containing guidelines for limiting obtrusive light is the Ordinance of the Minister of Infrastructure on the technical conditions to be met by buildings and their location (Dz.U. 2022, poz. 1225). Section 6, § 293 of this Ordinance36 states: “Lighting devices, including advertisements, placed on the outside of a building or in its surroundings must not cause a nuisance to its users or to passers-by and drivers. If the light is directed at the façade of a building containing windows, the illuminance at this façade must not exceed 5 lux for white light and 3 lux for coloured light or light of varying intensity, flashing light or possibly pulsating light”.

Subject, scope and method of testing

The subject of the study is vertical illuminance measurements, for which 21 illuminance meters and one compact spectroradiometer with an illuminance measurement function were used. The measurements were carried out on the surface of 17 windows of various residential buildings located in several cities in Poland (Łódź, Zgierz, Kraków, Kalisz, Warszawa). All the rooms in which light is emitted through outdoor lighting installations are intended for daily rest (bedrooms). Most properties are residential buildings, the three are student residences, and one is an assistant’s house. The choice of windows where measurements were taken was based on the possibility of access. The measuring instruments used in the study represent different levels in terms of workmanship, technical parameters and also price. Of the 21 illuminance meters, two have a resolution of 0.001 lx, six a resolution of 0.01 lx, six a resolution of 0.1 lx and seven a resolution of 1 lx, on the first (lower) measuring range. However, it is worth mentioning on this occasion, that for lux meters numbered 1 and 2, a resolution of 0.001 lx applies when measuring values below 10 lx. Once a value of 10 lx is reached (despite the same measuring range), the values are displayed with a resolution of 0.01 lx. In addition, a compact spectroradiometer was used in the measurements. This is a measuring instrument which, in addition to the absolute spectral characteristics of the radiation, additionally enables the measurement of, among other things, illuminance with a resolution of 0.01 lx. By resolution, we mean the smallest change in the measured value that can be displayed by the meter. The inclusion of illuminance meters with a resolution of 1 lx (when measuring low illuminance levels) may cause some surprises. It is worth pointing out, however, that some ‘measurers’ who do not deal with professional illuminance measurements on a daily basis believe that if the lower value of the measurement range of 0 lx is given in the specification of the illuminance meter and the illuminance meter is calibrated, the values indicated by the lux meter can be considered reliable.

Measurements were taken from May to August 2023, under good weather conditions (no precipitation, fog or wind), on moonless nights. Measurements on a given window were taken with all photometers over the course of one night, successively changing devices. When a measurement was completed with one photometer, it was put away, and the sensor of the next meter was placed in its place. In this way, one result was obtained for each photometers. Neither the prescriptive requirements34 nor the ordinance36 provide precise information on the methodology for vertical illuminance measurements. The draft Regulation of the Minister of Infrastructure and Construction of 11 May 201637 states that vertical illuminance is measured at the central point of the window plane of the room of the permanent residence. However, this regulation has not entered into force. This document does not specify on which side of the window (external or internal) the photometric head of the illuminance meter should be placed. Because Regulation36, which addresses the nuisance of obtrusive light, refers to the illuminance at the façade, it was assumed that measurements should be carried out on the external side of the window.

Illuminance measurements were taken approximately one hour after the lighting equipment was switched on. This time was considered sufficient to achieve thermal stabilisation of the light source and thus its luminous flux value38.

The recorded values are those after the meter display has stabilised. Before taking measurements, each time the photometer display was checked for a low battery indicator.

All the properties for which vertical illuminance measurements were carried out are located in urban areas, which corresponds to an urbanised urban zone (zone E4). This means that, according to CIE 150:2017, the illuminance value should not exceed 25 lx for pre-curfew and 5 lx for post-curfew. If, on the other hand, one were to refer to the provisions of Regulation36, the measured illuminance value should not exceed 5 lx.

It is worth noting that in Poland (as well as in many other European countries) when it comes to outdoor public lighting installations, night-time luminous flux reduction is not a standard solution. Typically, outdoor lighting installations emit the same luminous flux throughout the night (they are not dimmed). This means that both pre-curfew and post-curfew, the luminous flux emitted by the outdoor lighting equipment towards the windows will be at the same level. In this case, it seems reasonable to guide the values for post-curfew (Table 1). Analysing the data in Fig. 2. suggesting the obtained readings of most of the photometers, it can be concluded that the locations with the contractual numbers 1 and 2 correspond to the requirements for zone E3. The illuminance values obtained (ignoring the indication of lux meter 8) do not exceed 2 lx. Locations 3 and 4 can be included in class E4. The readings of all photometers, except for the illuminance meter 8, do not exceed 5 lx. For locations 6 to 10, the opinion on whether the illuminance limit is met depends on which photometer reads the measured value. For example, for site 6, nine photometers display a value of no more than 5 lx. For the following locations, the situation is as follows: for location 7 - seven photometers, for location 8 – eight photometers, for location 9 - six photometers and location 10 - four photometers. Only for sites 11 to 17, regardless of the type of photometer used, will it be possible to unequivocally state that the requirements for class E4 are not met, as each of the meters displayed a value exceeding 5 lx.

Due to the need to minimise the costs associated with the electricity consumption of outdoor lighting installations, it is becoming increasingly common for lighting equipment to be switched off at night. In this case, it can be assumed that the vertical intensity values should not be greater than those defined for pre-curfew (Table 1).

Despite the practically constant lighting conditions for a given location, discrepancies between the readings of the individual photometers are apparent. Depending on the photometer used during the measurements, the assessment of the fulfilment of the requirements concerning the reduction of obtrusive light can be positive or negative.

The correct assessment of the lighting conditions is important in terms of providing an opinion on whether the requirements for illuminance limits on window surfaces are met. Depending on the meter used, the conclusions of the resulting measurement can vary. Statistical analysis

The measurements taken with 22 photometers at 17 locations were subjected to statistical analysis. Considering the different values of light flux emitted towards the analysed windows, it was deemed beneficial to normalise the measurement results for each measurement location by determining the median of the illuminance values (E) from all measurements and normalising them to a specified quantity, denoted as Enorm. The median is a better measure of central tendency than the commonly used arithmetic mean of illuminance, as it is less sensitive to strongly deviating values, which occur in this case.

To analyse the studied set of illuminance meters in terms of such a normalized Enorm value, a cluster analysis was performed. Cluster analysis or clustering is the task of grouping a set of objects in such a way that objects in the same group (called a cluster) are more similar (in some specific sense defined by the analyst) to each other than to those in other groups (clusters). The statistical clustering of Enorm values in the available data set was tested using hierarchical clustering analysis with a Euclidean metric and Ward’s minimum variance clustering criterion (WardD2). A clear clustering of the illuminance meters was observed. The graphical elbow method for finding the optimal K value in a k-means clustering algorithm was used to determine the optimal number of clusters. It allowed us to conclude that the dataset can be grouped into six clusters, which are marked with coloured rectangles on the dendrogram (Fig. 4). Illuminance meter 8 clearly stands out from all the others, forming its own group (Group A: red). The other illuminance meters can be grouped into five clusters, with Groups B and C (green and blue) being similar, and D, E and F (pink, orange and purple) forming a different cluster.

Conclusions

Vertical illuminance measurements in the field, unlike in laboratory conditions, are burdened with a variety of factors influencing the obtained results. These include, among others, the type and arrangement of light sources, spectral characteristics of radiation, distance and angle of inclination of lighting fixtures and atmospheric conditions. Therefore, verification of compliance with the requirements for limiting disturbing light requires precise measurement methodology and the use of high-quality photometers.

Verification of the requirements specified in CIE 150:201735 and in the standard34 regarding limiting glare is associated with the need for conducting measurements. Compliance with the specified vertical illuminance limit values is not covered by formalized procedures. Therefore, it seems necessary to clarify the methodology for conducting measurements.

Another crucial issue is the choice of the meter. Considering the wide range of photometers available, the decision regarding its selection is not easy. Analysing the results obtained from the conducted measurements, it can be concluded that not all photometers are suitable for measuring illuminance concerning meeting the limit value requirements. Out of 22 photometers, in the authors’ opinion, only a few meters are suitable for conducting illuminance measurements for assessing light pollution. These are meters 1, 2, 4, 5 and 9 (group B). Statistical cluster analysis has shown that this group is the most homogeneous; in addition, it also includes a spectroradiometer (meter 22), which we consider to be the reference meter that gives reliable measurement results. The remaining groups are either less homogeneous (group C: meters 3, 7, 11, 15, 19) or contain too few devices for fully reliable statistical inference (groups D, E, F). Furthermore, all of these remaining groups give clearly underestimated measurement results compared to group B, which we consider to be the exemplary group.

Due to the influence of multiple factors on vertical illuminance measurements, as well as the low levels of limit values, the authors suggest recommending illuminance meters with the lowest possible values of errors f’1, f2 for assessing light pollution. The limit values of these errors should be as for Class A photometers (f’1≤3%, f2 ≤ 1.5%). Class A guarantees adequate spectral and spatial correction, which is crucial when judging whether intrusive light requirements are met. The lack of information on the error values of f’1 and f2 may suggest that these error values exceed the acceptable limits for Class A. In practice, if a manufacturer has a high-quality photometer, it will usually include error details in the technical specification. In the case of lower-quality devices, instead of specific numerical values, there are often general descriptions referring to ‘good spectral and spatial correction’. In addition, we recommend that the resolution of the illuminance meter on the lower measuring range should be no less than 0.01 lx. Although the results obtained with some lower-resolution meters (0.1 lx) were consistent with those obtained with high-end illuminance meters, a higher resolution provides greater accuracy and reliability of measurements, especially for very low illuminance values. Although there is no legal requirement to calibrate illuminance meters, we recommend periodic calibration of such devices, because their accuracy may change over time. In the current legislation, there is no provision regulating the frequency of calibration. In practice, many laboratories adopt a calibration interval for illuminance meters of 12 to 24 months. The authors recommend that this calibration frequency should also be used by those involved in illuminance measurement in the context of intrusive light assessment.

When evaluating compliance with vertical illuminance requirements, it is necessary to accept tolerance between the required value and that determined based on measurements. Small differences in illuminance values will not significantly affect how lighting is perceived, but they will facilitate assessing compliance with requirements when the measured value slightly exceeds the reference value. It seems reasonable to adopt a tolerance of ± 10% (with a tendency towards + 10%). For example, if a criterion of 5 lx is adopted, in the case of measurement point No. 5 (Fig. 1e), photometers whose readings exceeded the reference value by a few percent could be considered as not exceeding the limit value requirements for vertical illuminance. This applies to four photometers 1, 2, 5, and 22.

The results presented in the study clearly indicate that the type of photometer used significantly influences the assessment of light pollution. Photometers tend to both overestimate and underestimate the measured illuminance values. This means that for the same measurement location, conclusions drawn, for example, in a report (in the research protocol) may vary, which can lead to misunderstandings. Vertical illuminance measurements on the surfaces of residential windows are usually conducted when there are reasons to suspect that the limit values of intrusive light, originating from outdoor lighting installations, have been exceeded. Depending on the measurement result obtained, interested parties may decide to take (or refrain from taking) action to minimize the impact of outdoor lighting on the interior of bedrooms.

It is difficult to find comparable field studies in the literature that take such a wide range of measuring instruments and their influence on the measurement results in the context of obtrusive light assessment. The analyses show that cheaper photometers can differ significantly in accuracy from professional devices. Such differences may lead to erroneous conclusions regarding compliance with the obtrusive light limits set out in the regulations. The current rules in force in Poland36 and international recommendations, such as the CIE 150:2017 report35, do not contain specific requirements for illuminance meters used to measure obtrusive light. The lack of these guidelines creates a potential risk, as users may turn to cheap, readily available devices with low accuracy or even smartphone apps. In addition, illuminance meters are currently not subject to legal metrological control in Poland, which means that they can be marketed without meeting specific requirements for the accuracy and reliability of measurements. This means that both high-precision devices and cheaper, less-precise illuminance meters are available for sale, with no formal guarantee of their compliance with standards. The problem is exacerbated by the fact that Polish regulations do not require those carrying out lighting measurements to have the relevant qualifications, certificates or training. The lack of a qualification requirement means that the level of knowledge and awareness of such people may be low, increasing the risk of incorrect selection of measuring instruments and leading to erroneous results. Incorporating the recommendations made into the regulations would improve the reliability of measurements. The authors’ main contribution is to conduct field studies that consider a variety of measuring instruments in the context of obtrusive light assessment - a topic that has so far been poorly analysed. The recommendations presented address the specific requirements for illuminance meters and could form the basis for potential changes when the regulations are revised.

The measurement results presented in the paper refer to specific instances of photometers (with assigned serial numbers). It should be noted that the readings of photometers with different serial numbers may vary.
