Fathers’ Childcare Success and Maternal Parenting Stress: A Comparative Study of Families With and Without Children Having Neurodevelopmental Disabilities
Authors: Shiori Ishida, Tomone Takahashi, Aimi Kinoshita, Hisato Igarashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPaternal engagement has been described as a relational process that redistributes caregiving responsibilities and strengthens family resilience and function. This study investigated the association between fathers’ childcare success and maternal parenting stress by comparing families raising children with neurodevelopmental disabilities (ND) with those raising typically developing (TD) children. Fifty‐three fathers of children with ND and 206 fathers of TD children completed online surveys on parenting self‐efficacy, childcare knowledge, involvement, communication with their spouse, and perceived social support. In paired surveys, spouses reported on parenting stress. Structural equation modeling indicated that in both groups, higher paternal childcare success was significantly associated with lower maternal stress (ND: β = −0.42, p < 0.05; TD: β = −0.39, p < 0.001). However, the contributing factors differed; in TD families, paternal role awareness, father–child relationship, and spousal relationship were most strongly related to fathers’ childcare success, whereas these associations were less prominent in ND families. These findings highlight the importance of interventions that address fathers’ psychological traits as well as the specialized skills and knowledge required for parenting children with ND to enhance family functioning and parental well‐being.

Introduction

A growing body of research has underscored the critical role of paternal involvement in alleviating maternal parenting burden, reducing psychological distress, and fostering children's socio‐emotional and cognitive development (Cabrera et al. 2018; Pleck 2010). Active engagement by fathers has been associated with enhanced maternal mental health, improved marital quality, and favorable developmental outcomes in children (Nomaguchi and Milkie 2020; Panter‐Brick et al. 2014). From the perspective of family systems, paternal engagement is conceptualized as a relational process that redistributes caregiving responsibilities and strengthens family resilience.

Parenting a child with a neurodevelopmental disorder (ND) presents considerably greater challenges than raising a typically developing (TD) child and is frequently accompanied by elevated parental stress (Hayes and Watson 2013; Davis and Carter 2008). Children with ND often display unpredictable behaviors, strong insistence on routines, sensory sensitivities, and communication difficulties, all of which increase caregiving demands and place a disproportionate burden on mothers (McStay et al. 2014). Fathers of ND children have also reported substantial difficulties in childcare engagement and frequently experience ambiguity regarding their roles. Nonetheless, empirical evidence on fathers’ specific contributions and their potential to mitigate maternal stress remains limited (Freeman et al. 2010).

Clarifying the paternal characteristics and behaviors that facilitate effective caregiving and exploring their impact on maternal stress are therefore important areas of inquiry. Prior studies have identified parenting self‐efficacy, cooperative attitudes, caregiving skills, and marital communication as behaviors that support maternal adaptation (Pleck 2010). Recent meta‐analytic evidence further highlights the centrality of parental self‐efficacy for psychological well‐being among parents of ND children (Abdel‐Baki. 2023). However, the extent to which such paternal skills operate in the context of ND remains underexplored.

Several salient roles of fathers have been identified in ND families. First, when mothers bear primary responsibility for caregiving, their stress can escalate to clinically significant levels and heighten the risk of depression and anxiety; paternal engagement may buffer against these outcomes (Davis and Carter 2008; Nomaguchi and Milkie 2020). Second, children derive unique benefits from father–child interactions, which provide distinctive opportunities for stimulation and adaptive growth (Cabrera et al. 2018). Third, fathers’ active participation may strengthen family cohesion, reduce isolation, and counterbalance inequities in caregiving roles (Panter‐Brick et al. 2014). However, many fathers of ND children still face barriers including the complexity of caregiving, feelings of personal anxiety or helplessness, limited societal expectations, and insufficient access to parenting knowledge (Freeman et al. 2010). Moreover, it remains unclear whether paternal factors affect maternal stress differently in ND versus TD families.

Significance of the Present Study

The present study sought to elucidate how paternal involvement contributed to maternal stress reduction in families of children with ND, as well as to identify the paternal characteristics most instrumental in this process. This research addresses a persistent gap in the literature on fatherhood, which has historically emphasized maternal perspectives. Our findings are expected to enhance father‐focused parenting interventions and provide an empirical foundation for advancing clinical and policy approaches to family support beyond mother‐centric models (Pleck 2010; Panter‐Brick et al. 2014).

Objectives and Hypotheses

The primary objective was to examine how paternal factors enabling effective childcare influenced maternal parenting stress, and whether those associations differed between ND and TD families. Specifically, we focused on paternal self‐efficacy, cooperative behavior, childcare knowledge, and marital communication as potential determinants, as earlier established by Pleck's (2010) conceptualization of father involvement, Bandura's (1997) theory of self‐efficacy, and the family systems perspective (Cox and Paley 1997) as theoretical underpinnings. We hypothesized that higher levels of those paternal factors would associate with lower maternal parenting stress. Furthermore, we speculated stronger associations in ND families, particularly for marital communication and flexible responsiveness, and that the primary influential paternal factors might differ between family types.

Methods

Participants and Procedure

Online surveys were conducted between May 2023 and May 2024 targeting father–spouse pairs raising children aged 2–12 years. In the ND group, children were diagnosed with attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) or autism spectrum disorder (ASD), or were receiving developmental support services due to suspected ASD/ADHD. Eligibility required that the father co‐resided with the child and spouse. The ND group comprised 53 father–spouse pairs that were recruited mainly from rural and small‐ to mid‐sized cities in Japan via support organizations, medical institutions, and special education facilities. The TD group contained 206 father–spouse pairs recruited primarily through online parenting communities, municipal childcare mailing lists, and social media. Informed consent was obtained electronically for all participants. An explanatory statement outlined the study's objectives, procedures, risks, and benefits. Participation was confirmed by voluntary consent and full completion of the survey. Fathers and mothers were instructed to respond independently to avoid influencing the other's answers.

Measures

Maternal parenting stress was assessed using the Japanese version of the parenting stress index‐short form (PSI‐SF) (Abidin 1995; Higuchi 1994). The PSI‐SF includes 36 items across three subscales (parental distress, parent‐child dysfunctional interaction, and Difficult Child). Items were rated on a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Higher scores indicated greater stress.

Regarding paternal factors, fathers completed a questionnaire developed with reference to Pleck's (2010) framework and adapted to reflect the Japanese cultural context. Domains included stress coping, hardiness, self‐understanding, father's role, relationship with the child, and spousal and father's PSI score (parental aspect and child aspect). Items were rated on a 5‐point Likert scale, as described above. The paternal factors questionnaire comprised 30 items in total. Sample items included “I feel confident in my ability to care for my child's daily needs” (self‐efficacy) and “I actively discuss childcare responsibilities with my spouse” (marital communication). In addition to the Difficult Child subscale from the PSI‐SF, which captured the child's behavioral characteristics from a parental perspective, the childcare knowledge domain of the paternal questionnaire included items assessing fathers’ understanding of their child's developmental stage and specific care needs, such as “I understand my child's developmental characteristics” and “I know what kinds of support my child needs.”

Statistical Analysis

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was conducted to examine for associations among paternal factors, paternal childcare success, and maternal stress. Model fit was evaluated using χ 2, degrees of freedom (df), CMIN/df, and RMSEA by means of AMOS software (IBM SPSS Statistics, IBM Corp). A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Due to estimation constraints, the comparative fit index (CFI) and Tucker–Lewis index (TLI) were unavailable. Models were tested separately for the ND and TD groups.

Results

Summary of Participants

Fathers and mothers in the ND group tended to be older than in the TD group (fathers: 42.7 vs. 36.2 years; mothers: 40.8 vs. 34.0 years) (Table 1). Regarding child age, the ND group showed a higher mean age than the TD group (ND: 8.3 years; TD: 5.6 years), suggesting that within the overall age range of 2–12 years, TD families tended to include younger children while ND families tended to contain older children. This difference in age may partly explain the parental age gap and should be considered when interpreting the findings. With respect to maternal employment, a notably higher proportion of mothers in the TD group were employed full‐time, which might reflect the greater caregiving demands associated with raising a child with ND. These employment differences may have influenced maternal parenting stress levels independently of paternal factors and are discussed further below (Table 1).

Impact of Paternal Childcare Success on Maternal Parenting Stress

SEM revealed that paternal childcare success had a significant inverse effect on maternal parenting stress in both groups. For fathers of children with ND, this effect was β = −0.42 (p < 0.05) (Figure 1), while for fathers of TD children, it was β = −0.39 (p < 0.001) (Figure 2).

Comparison of Factors Contributing to Paternal Childcare Success

The strength and significance of the factors contributing to paternal childcare success differed between the ND and TD groups. In fathers of TD children, paternal role awareness (β = 0.82, p < 0.001), relationship with the child (β = 0.70, p < 0.001), and spousal relationship (β = 0.78, p < 0.001) contributed most predominantly, with most factor pathways strong and significant (Figure 2). In contrast, fathers of children with ND showed weaker contributions of these factors, with the PSI‐Parental aspect not reaching significance (β = 0.30) (Figure 1). Path coefficients ranged from 0.41 to 0.77 in this cohort, with significance levels of p < 0.05 to p < 0.01.

Model Fit

SEM demonstrated an acceptable fit of the data. In the ND group, fit indices were: χ 2 (30) = 65.32 (p < 0.001), CMIN/df = 2.18, and RMSEA = 0.102 (Figure 1). In the TD group, these results were: χ 2 (27) = 58.46 (p < 0.001), CMIN/df = 2.21, and RMSEA = 0.107 (Figure 2). Although other commonly reported fit indices, such as the CFI and TLI, were not available in the estimation output due to software and estimation constraints, model fit based on χ 2, CMIN/df, and RMSEA fell within thresholds generally considered acceptable for SEM analyses (e.g., CMIN/df < 3, RMSEA < 0.08–0.10; Kline 2016).

Discussion

The present study examined how paternal childcare success impacted maternal parenting stress in comparisons of fathers raising children with ND or TD. Our findings demonstrated that greater paternal childcare success was significantly associated with lower maternal parenting stress in both groups, with differences noted for the specific contributing factors. This result is consistent with previous studies suggesting that fathers’ active involvement in childcare alleviates mothers’ burden (Shobana and Saravanan 2014; Kiely et al. 2016).

Differences Between TD and ND Fathers

The strength and patterns of the factors contributing to paternal childcare success varied between the TD and ND groups. In fathers of TD children, paternal role awareness, a good relationship with the child, and an understanding of the spousal relationship contributed strongly to childcare success, which in turn reduced maternal parenting stress. This finding aligned with earlier reports supporting that fathers’ parenting self‐efficacy and active engagement were effective in reducing maternal stress (Freeman et al. 2010; Shave & Lashewicz et al. 2015). Fathers of TD children appear to benefit from these psychological resources and relational factors, which translate well into a sense of success in childcare and reduce their partner's stress. In contrast, the same factors showed relatively weaker contributions to paternal childcare success in fathers of ND children, with the PSI‐Parental aspect path not reaching significance. While paternal role awareness and the father–child relationship did contribute to childcare success in this group, their influence was statistically weaker compared with the TD group. Parenting a child with ND often involves unpredictable challenges and high demands (Estes et al. 2013; Hayes and Watson 2013), which may hinder the direct impact of fathers’ psychological traits and relationships on their sense of childcare success and their partners’ stress reduction. Notably, despite the weaker individual factor loadings in the ND group, the overall effect of paternal childcare success on maternal stress was slightly greater in the ND group than in the TD group (β = −0.42 vs. −0.39). This finding suggests that the buffering effect of paternal childcare success on maternal stress may be equally or even more pronounced in ND families, regardless of which specific factors underlie that success. One possible explanation is that maternal stress is already elevated in ND families (Hayes and Watson 2013), leaving greater room for stress reduction when paternal involvement is effective. Future studies should investigate what specific pathways enable fathers of ND children to attain childcare success despite these constraints. In terms of group similarities, both the TD and ND groups showed that paternal childcare success was a significant mediating construct linking paternal attributes to maternal outcomes, underscoring the cross‐group validity of this framework. The key difference lies in which attributes drive that success: psychological and relational resources appear sufficient in TD families, whereas ND families may additionally require domain‐specific knowledge and practical skills. Parental age and maternal employment status could have also contributed to these inter‐group differences; the older age and lower employment rate observed in the ND group may reflect longer caregiving tenure and greater work‐life disruption, both of which elevating maternal stress and altering the conditions under which paternal involvement proves beneficial.

Implications for Supporting Fathers in ND Families

The above findings suggest that in families raising children with ND, enhancing fathers’ psychological resources and relational qualities alone may not be sufficient. This inference is supported by the finding that in the ND group, the three factors most strongly associated with childcare success in TD families (paternal role awareness, father–child relationship, and spousal relationship) showed weaker and partially nonsignificant path coefficients. In contrast, domains related to childcare knowledge and stress coping retained significant contributions in the ND group, indicating that more concrete, skill‐based competencies may drive paternal success in this context. Thus, it may be necessary to provide fathers with more specific knowledge and practical skills tailored to the child's disability characteristics for supporting their childcare success and family involvement.

Future Directions

Future research should aim to identify the specific factors that help fathers of children with ND enhance their sense of childcare success, including parenting skills tailored to the child's condition and how to access appropriate support. Interventional studies are also needed to develop and test programs that promote effective paternal involvement. Furthermore, longitudinal investigations should explore the causal relationships and examine the long‐term effects of fathers’ involvement on maternal stress and overall family functioning.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged, including a relatively small sample size, inclusion of severe cases, possible recall bias, and variability in children's symptoms that may have impacted maternal stress. Furthermore, the diagnoses of ASD and ADHD were based on parent reports, with details on the diagnostician or diagnostic method unavailable in some cases. Given that such diagnoses in Japan are sometimes made by nonspecialists, such as child psychiatrists, pediatricians, and physicians at community support centers, this study focused more on observed traits and support needs rather than diagnostic precision. Nevertheless, inconsistent diagnostic procedures may have influenced sample selection, and future research should employ physician‐issued diagnoses and standardized diagnostic tools for ASD, ADHD, and any possible co‐occurring conditions.

Another limitation was that parental mental health was assessed only superficially in this investigation and was not treated as a key variable due to its sensitive nature and the lack of objective measures. More detailed assessments are currently being planned under appropriate ethical protocols. Additionally, the involvement of child guidance centers and welfare programs, while not explicitly excluded, was not systematically assessed and may have influenced caregiving or child development. Future studies should address this aspect as well. Similarly, the broader social context, including environmental factors such as support from maternal and paternal grandparents, should be factored more thoroughly to clarify their effects on children and caregivers.

With respect to the statistical analyses, some limitations of the SEM used in this study must also be noted. Although the available indicators (χ 2, CMIN/df, and RMSEA) suggested acceptable model fit, future studies should aim to include a wider range of fit indices for a more comprehensive evaluation and greater robustness. Given the limited sample size and diversity, the SEM model fit was modest; larger, more diverse samples will help refine the model and clarify the pathways by which paternal factors influence maternal stress and child behavior.

Additional limitations included the cross‐sectional study design, which precluded causal inferences. As such, it remains unclear whether fathers’ factors directly reduce maternal stress or whether lower maternal stress facilitates greater paternal involvement. Our findings also relied on self‐reported questionnaires from both spouses, which might be subject to social desirability bias and other response biases. Furthermore, a potential selection bias exists, as fathers who are more actively involved in childcare may have been more likely to participate, thereby limiting the generalizability of our findings to less‐involved fathers. Finally, since this study was conducted within a specific cultural and regional context, caution should be exercised when generalizing the results to other populations or settings.

As larger scale studies become feasible, stratified analyses by developmental stage should also be conducted to identify more specific needs for parenting support.

Conclusion

This study confirmed that fathers’ childcare success was associated with reduced maternal parenting stress in both TD and ND families. In TD families, psychological traits and role awareness strongly contributed to those outcomes. In ND families, these contributions tended to be weaker, suggesting that the challenges of raising a child with ND may diminish the effectiveness of fathers’ psychological and relational resources. Our findings highlight the importance of fostering not only fathers’ psychological traits and relationships, but also concrete parenting skills and knowledge tailored to ND. Such targeted interventions may enhance paternal childcare success and more effectively support mothers.
