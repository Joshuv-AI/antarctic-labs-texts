Environmentally sustainable fabrication of high-performance M-type hexaferrite BaFe11.6Mn0.4O19 using manganese residue and rare earths for microwave absorption applications
Authors: Wentao Li, Qian Zhang, Zuohua Liu, Changyuan Tao, Guocan Zheng, Nanxiong Chen, Dong Wang, Hufei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractManganese residue is a dangerous industrial by-product and has traditionally been stored. Conventional harmless treatment can stabilize Mn2+, but its resource utilization is relatively difficult due to the complex composition of manganese residue. This study proposes a sustainable development approach for synthesizing high-performance M-type ferrite BaFe11.6Mn0.4O19 from manganese residue rich in Fe and Mn for microwave absorption. By optimizing roasting conditions, we controlled oxygen vacancy concentration, enhancing the ferrite’s dielectric and magnetic properties for superior microwave absorption. By simulating the composition of rare earth solid waste, rare earth elements La2O3 and CeO2 were incorporated into ferrite. Further optimize the loss matching mechanism and shielding effect. Characterization techniques confirmed a multiphase-phase M-type ferrite structure with a minimum reflection loss of -33.42 dB. It is clarified that the main reason for the performance improvement mechanism after the incorporation of rare earths is that the formation of new phases and the appearance of solid solutions promote the synergistic effect in polarization and electromagnetic energy dissipation. This method provides a scalable model for industrial waste recycling. Introduction

Manganese sulfate serves as a pivotal raw material within the battery1–3, electronics4–6 and various other industrial sectors7–9. The byproduct generated during manganese sulfate production, known as manganese sulfate residue (MSR), poses significant environmental challenges. Currently, the predominant industrial practice for managing MSR is stockpiling. However, prolonged stockpiling results in the release of soluble Mn2+ ions, which subsequently contaminate soil, surface water, and groundwater resources10–12. Consequently, scholars have extensively investigated treatment strategies for MSR, which can be broadly classified into two categories: harmless treatment and resource utilization treatment. Harmless treatment methodologies predominantly encompass leaching13–16 and solidification17–19. Although both approaches effectively mitigate the issue of soluble Mn2+ contamination, they fall short in facilitating subsequent resource recovery from the treated residue20. This limitation invariably leads to the accumulation of residue that, despite being rendered harmless, remains underutilized. In light of the inherent constraints associated with harmless treatment and the escalating global demand for sustainable solid waste management practices that emphasize resource recovery, it becomes imperative to explore innovative and resource-efficient treatment avenues for MSR.

Current resource utilization strategies predominantly center on transforming manganese residue (MR) into construction materials such as bricks21, cement22, road bases23, adsorbents24 and catalyst25. These methods represent viable pathways for EMR valorization, offering effective means to repurpose this industrial byproduct. which appear to be effective strategies for MR valorization. Nevertheless, the production of unfired bricks, cement, and roadbed aggregates is contingent upon stringent sulfur content specifications26–28, which may limit their applicability in certain contexts. Moreover, functional materials such as adsorbents and catalysts face the recurring challenge of regeneration20, necessitating ongoing research and development efforts to enhance their sustainability and cost-effectiveness. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that manganese sulfate residue (MSR) differs significantly from EMR in its composition, particularly with respect to its relatively elevated iron and manganese content29. This distinction underscores the pressing need to investigate novel resource utilization scenarios specifically tailored to the unique properties of iron- and manganese-rich MSR, thereby unlocking its full potential as a valuable secondary resource.

With the rapid advancement of modern electronic information technology, microwave technology has exerted a profound influence on human productivity and daily life30–32. However, the concomitant issue of electromagnetic radiation pollution has escalated in severity33–35. Among various microwave-absorbing materials, M-type ferrite stands out due to its distinctive structural attributes, rendering it highly effective in mitigating electromagnetic interference36–38. Its extensive applications span across diverse electromagnetic domains, including electronics39, telecommunications40, and radar systems41. Notably, prior research has demonstrated the feasibility of utilizing solid waste as a precursor for fabricating microwave-absorbing materials42–44. In our previous endeavors, we successfully employed a specific type of iron- and manganese-rich manganese residue (MSR-F) in the synthesis of M-type ferrites, thereby establishing a viable pathway for hierarchical resource recovery from MSR45. Nevertheless, our preliminary investigations revealed that M-type ferrites synthesized solely from MSR-F exhibited subpar microwave absorption capabilities. Consequently, enhancing the microwave absorption performance of M-type ferrites derived from MSR-F remains a paramount challenge.

M-type ferrites primarily dissipate electromagnetic energy through magnetic loss mechanisms46. The optimization of this loss mechanism, achieved by attaining a superior balance between electrical and magnetic energy, is pivotal for augmenting microwave absorption performance. Literature suggests that doping composite ferrites with other elements can effectively modulate the dielectric constant or reduce the magnetic permeability of the material47,48. Furthermore, some studies have highlighted the potential of regulating oxygen vacancy concentration in ferrites to enhance their microwave absorption properties49,50. Additionally, research has demonstrated that rare-earth element doping can elevate oxygen vacancy concentration within materials51,52. Collectively, these studies underscore the potential of incorporating rare-earth elements into M-type ferrites to fine-tune their oxygen vacancy concentration, enhance the dielectric constant, and ultimately improve microwave absorption performance.

In this study, we successfully synthesized M-type ferrite (BaFe11.6Mn0.4O19) utilizing magnetically separated MSR-F as an additive, with the primary objective of enhancing microwave absorption performance through precise regulation of oxygen vacancy concentration. Oxygen vacancy levels were meticulously controlled by adjusting the roasting temperature and duration. Moreover, we investigated the impact of rare earth (RE) doping simulating compositions derived from neodymium-iron-boron (NdFeB) waste and RE polishing powders on oxygen vacancy concentrations. The underlying mechanisms through which CeO2 and La2O3 enhance oxygen vacancy density and microwave absorption performance were systematically elucidated. This research not only provides valuable insights into the optimization of M-type ferrites but also establishes a practical and sustainable framework for MSR recycling, thereby contributing to the advancement of environmentally friendly material science.

Experimental

Reagents and materials

Manganese fining residue obtained after magnetic separation of manganese sulfate residue was supplied by a manganese sulfate factory in Guizhou Province (China). Nd2O3(≥ 99%), La2O3(≥ 99.9%), CeO2(≥ 99.95%), Fe3O4(≥ 99%), BaCO3(≥ 99%), Mn3O4(≥ 99.5%) were purchased from Aladdin Biochemistry Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China).

Characterizations

Samples and precipitates were analyzed by X-ray diffractometer (XRD, D/MAX-2500, Rigaku, Japan). Microscopic surface morphology and microscale composition analyses were conducted using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM 2100 F, JEOL, Japan), scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Ultra Plus, Carl Zeiss, Germany) and X-ray energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS, Oxford X-MAX, Carl Zeiss, Germany). The composition of the sample was used to detect by X-ray fluorescence (XRF, ARL Perform’X, Thermo Fisher Scientific CDL. td, Switzerland). Sample valence change was measured by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher Sci entific, America). The absorbing performance was tested by Vector Network Analyzer (E5071C, Agilent, America).

Roasting experiment

First, 5% MSR-F were added (The composition of MSR-F is shown in Table S1), and then a certain amount of BaCO3, Fe3O4 and Mn3O4 were weighed according to the ratio of target product n(Ba2+): n(Fe3+): n(Mn2+) = 1:11.6:0.4. Subsequently, the mixtures were calcined at 1273.15 K, 1373.15 K, 1473.15 K, 1573.15 K, and 1673.15 K for 8 h each and labeled as 1#, 2#, 3#, 4#, and 5#, respectively. Additionally, based on the aforementioned raw material ratio, the mixtures were calcined at 1573.15 K for 2 h, 4 h, 6 h, and 10 h and labeled as 6#, 7#, 8#, and 9#, respectively.

Roasting experiment of doped rare earth

5% manganese residue were added, and then a certain amount of BaCO3, Fe3O4 and Mn3O4 were weighed according to the ratio of target product n(Ba2+): n(Fe3+): n(Mn2+) = 1:11.6:0.4. Subsequently, to simulate the rare earth components in rare earth solid waste (as shown in Tables S2 and S3), 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% of Nd2O3 were individually incorporated (calculated based on 20 g of BaFe11.6Mn0.4O19). After thorough mixing, the samples were heated to 1573.15 K at a rate of 10 K min−1 and maintained at this temperature for 2 h. Based on the varying proportions of Nd2O3 weighed, the samples were designated as 10#, 11#, 12#, 13#, 14#. Additionally, with a total mass (mLa₂O₃ + mCeO₂) of 5 g, the ferrite was sintered by doping with La2O3 and CeO2 in the ratios of mLa₂O₃: mtotal =0:5, 1:4, 2:3, 3:2, and 4:1, respectively. These samples were labeled as 15#, 16#, 17#, and 18#,19#. The sample doped with 5 g of La2O3 was marked as 20#. 5%, 10%, 15% and 20% of CeO2 and L2O3 were individually incorporated. the samples were designated as 21#, 22#, 23#, 24#, 25#, 26#, 27#, 28#.

Testing method of microwave absorption

The electromagnetic parameters of the materials were measured by using a vector network analyzer. The frequency range of the test was 1–18 GHz, applying a coaxial method using a ring with an outer diameter of 7.0 mm, an inner diameter of 3.0 mm, and a height of 2 mm. Results and discussion

The concentration of oxygen vacancies regulated by controlling roasting conditions

Roasting temperature

From Fig. 1a, it can be observed that only the BaFe11.6Mn0.4O19 phase is present under different synthesis temperatures. Fig. S1a,b illustrate the uniform dispersion of Mn, Fe, O, and Ba in the synthesized material. This showed the successful preparation of BaFe11.6Mn0.4O19 and no other impurities will appear after manganese residue is added at different temperatures.

Ferrite of (a) XRD, and microwave absorption performance of ferrite of (b) 1#, (c) 2#, (d) 3#, (e) 4#, (f) 5#.

Figure 1b,f show the microwave absorption performance of samples 1#–5#. A smaller reflection loss value indicates stronger microwave absorption capability. When the reflection loss (RL) reaches − 10.00 dB, the material can absorb 90% of the electromagnetic waves54,55. At − 20.00 dB, the attenuation reaches 99%56,57. The results demonstrated that the minimum RL exhibited a trend of initial decrease followed by an increase with increasing roasting temperature, as illustrated in Fig. 1b,f. Specifically, the minimum RL were − 10.49 dB, − 10.53 dB, − 10.00 dB, − 16.90 dB, and − 10.73 dB for samples 1#–5#, respectively. This performance trend can be attributed to variations in real parts of permittivity and real parts of permeability with temperature. The permittivity and permeability are key factors determining the material’s interaction with electromagnetic waves. The refractive index of the medium is directly proportional to real parts of permittivity. The increasing of real parts of permittivity will enhance the medium’s refractive index. The increasing of the mediums’ refractive index will cause electromagnetic waves to propagate at a lower speed and interact more with the interface. This increased interaction enhances the material’s ability to absorb and dissipate electromagnetic energy. Additionally, comparing the magnetic loss tangent (tanσµ = µ″/µ′) and dielectric loss tangent (tanδε = ε″/ε′) revealed that magnetic energy dissipation far exceeds electrical energy dissipation, indicating magnetic energy dissipation as the dominant loss mechanism in these ferrites, as shown in Fig. S2c,f46.

The porous structure of the material also played a crucial role in enhancing microwave absorption performance. The porous structure provides multiple internal reflections and scattering events for electromagnetic waves, increasing their path length within the material and enhancing absorption58,59. Better impedance matching might be the reason why 4# has the best microwave absorption performance among 1#–5#.

Moreover, microwave absorption performance increases with oxygen vacancy concentration, as validated by previous studies. Fig. S5 shows a positive correlation between oxygen vacancy concentration(Oβ) and microwave absorption performance60–62. This might be due to the mechanism of defect-induced dipole polarization63–65. Furthermore, oxygen vacancy concentration in ferrites can affect the dielectric constant and permeability, which improving the magnetic energy dissipation-dominated loss mechanism and enhancing microwave absorption performance Thus, increasing oxygen vacancy concentration may improve microwave absorption performance.

Roasting time

From Fig. 2a, it is evident that only the BaFe11.6Mn0.4O19 phase is present under different synthesis durations, similar to Fig. 1a. However, the minimum RL value and bandwidth exhibited significant variations with roasting duration. Specifically, the minimum RL values were − 19.21 dB, − 17.56 dB, − 16.89 dB, − 10.73 dB, and − 15.67 dB for 4# and 6#–9#, as shown in Fig. 2b–f. The reason why the reflection loss value of 6# is the smallest might be related to its smallest real part of the dielectric constant, as reflected in Fig. S6. Bandwidth, another indicator of microwave absorption capability, refers to the frequency range where the reflection loss is less than − 10.00 dB66. A wider bandwidth indicates better microwave absorption performance. Sample 6# has the widest bandwidth of 2.26 GHz at a thickness of 8 mm as can been seen in Fig. 2b,f. Similar to Figs. 1 and 4#, 6#, 7#, and 9# exhibit obvious porous structures. 6# has the highest oxygen vacancy concentration, resulting in the smallest minimum reflection loss value and the largest minimum reflection loss value for sample 8#, as can been seen in Figs. Therefore, the focus of the following discussion is on how to further increase oxygen vacancy concentration by modifying material structure to change the dielectric constant.

Doping with multiple rare earths improved the concentration of oxygen vacancies

Doping with other elements effectively improves oxygen vacancy concentration. Rare earth elements have unique electronic configurations and chemical properties that make them attractive dopants for increasing the concentration of oxygen vacancies67,68. In this study, the rare earth components Nd2O3, La2O3 and CeO2 from two typical rare earth solid wastes (neodymium iron boron waste and rare earth polishing powder) were selected as dopants.

Effect of Nd2O3 doping

Doping with Nd2O3 decreases microwave absorption performance, as shown in Fig. 3a–e. It was found that the incorporation of Nd2O3 would reduce the microwave absorption performance of BaFe11.6Mn0.4O19.

Microwave absorption performance of different samples (a) 10#, (b) 11#, (c) 12#, (d) 13#, (e) 14# and ferrite of (f) XRD and (g) SEM-EDS of 13#.

The minimum RL of 10#-14# are all higher than that of 6# (− 12.08 dB, − 12.44 dB, − 13.05 dB, − 13.11 dB, − 12.79 dB). Figure 3f shows the doped material contains phases such as BaFe11.6Mn0.4O19, Ca2Nd2Fe4O11, Ca1.5Nd0.5MnO4. Figure 3g presents uniform dispersion of Fe, Mn, O, and Ba, but Nd is not detected in some areas, further validating Fig. 3f. The decrease in microwave absorption performance upon Nd2O3 doping can be attributed to changes in the dielectric constant permittivity and permeability. Fig. S9 shows that doping with Nd2O3 increases the real part of the permittivity and decreases the real part of permeability.

Although magnetic and electrical energy dissipation become closer, the significant decrease in permeability real part leads to low permeability and degraded microwave absorption performance. This suggests that increasing the permittivity alone is not sufficient to improve microwave absorption performance, and a balance between permeability and permittivity is crucial for optimizing the loss mechanism. Thus, Nd2O3 is unsuitable for doping into BaFe11.6Mn0.4O19, reinforcing that increasing the dielectric constant is the key to improving microwave absorption performance in ferrites.

Effect of La2O3 and CeO2 doping

In contrast to Nd2O3 doping, doping with a mixture of La2O3 and CeO2 could significantly enhance microwave absorption performance. The minimum reflection loss values of the samples exceed − 20 dB, as shown in Fig. 4a–f. The minimum reflection loss values of 15#, 16#, 18#, 19# and 20# are − 21.23 dB, − 24.21 dB, − 29.39 dB, − 24.40 dB and − 30.70 dB respectively. The minimum reflection loss values of 17# achieved an impressive − 33.42 dB. Additionally, bandwidth slightly increases and thickness decreases when both La2O3 and CeO2 are doped. Existing literature indicates that the minimum reflection loss value of the composite material prepared by BaFe12O19 is − 29.17 dB69, and another literature shows that the minimum reflection loss value of the composite material prepared by BaFe12O19 is − 16.24 dB70. This paper is − 33.42 dB, which is better than the literature reports.

Thickness is another important indicator of microwave absorption performance; thinner materials indicate better performance. 19# has the widest bandwidth of 2.37 GHz at a thickness of 6.5 mm. After doping with La2O3 and CeO2, new phases such as MnO2, CeO2, (Ba0.5La0.5) FeO2.955, and (Lao0.7Ba0.3) MnO3 appear, as shown in Fig. 4g. In addition, the constituent elements of different phases show an enriched state in certain regions in 17#, as can been seen in Fig. S10. The results of Zone 1, Zone 2, Zone 3 and Zone 4 further confirm this view of XRD.

Compared to undoped materials, the doped samples exhibit significantly higher oxygen vacancy concentrations, as illustrated in Fig. 4h. The enhanced microwave absorption performance is attributed to improvements in the imaginary part of the dielectric constant and the real part of permeability, altering the magnetic energy dissipation-dominated loss mechanism due to changes in oxygen vacancy concentration caused by La2O3 and CeO2 doping. Thus, doping with La2O3 and CeO2 effectively improves the microwave absorption performance of BaFe11.6Mn0.4O19.

The formation of solid solutions and new phases improving matching concentration of oxygen vacancies

To further clarify the roles of CeO2 and La2O3 in the material, separate doping experiments with CeO2 and La2O3 were conducted.

Effect of CeO2 doping

Figure 5 illustrates the experimental results of doping with CeO2 alone. Figure 5a–e indicate a decrease in the minimum reflection loss value after CeO2 doping. The minimum reflection loss values of 21#, 22#, 23# and 24# are − 19.43 dB, − 20.54 dB, − 22.01 dB and − 20.74 dB respectively. Fig. S12 shows increases in the real part of permeability and the imaginary part of the dielectric constant. This result will lead to a decrease in the magnetic loss tangent and an increase in the dielectric loss tangent, so the magnetic energy-dominated loss mechanism could be improving. XRD results reveal that CeO2 does not form a new phase with BaFe11.6Mn0.4O19, as shown in Fig. 5f. Figure S13 shows CeO2 existing as a solid solution in the material. TEM results reveal two phases in the material, with lattice spacings d1 = 0.32 nm for BaFe11.6Mn0.4O19 and d2 = 0.28 nm for CeO2 as can been seen in Fig. 5g. This result further confirms CeO2’s existence as a solid solution. Additionally, oxygen vacancy concentration increases compared to undoped CeO2 materials, as shown in Fig. 5h. However, the bandwidth range is much narrower than that of undoped CeO2 materials. The widest bandwidth for CeO2-doped samples is 0.7225 GHz for sample 22# at a thickness of 8.0 mm.

Ferrite of microwave absorption performance of different samples (a) 21#, (b) 22#, (c) 23#, (d) 24#, (e) 15#, and (f) XRD, (g) TEM, (h) XPS.

Thus, CeO2 forms a solid solution phase in the material, increasing oxygen vacancy concentration and improving the imaginary part of the dielectric constant and the real part of permeability, enhancing the minimum reflection loss value. However, doping with CeO2 alone do not contribute to a larger bandwidth range.

Effect of La2O3 doping

Figure 6 presents the experimental results of doping with La2O3 alone. Figure 6a-e show that both the minimum reflection loss value and thickness after La2O3 doping have decreased. The minimum reflection loss values of 25#, 26#, 27# and 28# are − 20.75 dB, − 19.93 dB, − 23.89 dB and − 27.77 dB respectively. 20# achieves a minimum reflection loss value of − 30.70 dB and a maximum bandwidth of 2.18 GHz at d = 7.5 mm. The decrease in minimum reflection loss value is attributed to an increase in the dielectric constant, while the improvement in thickness is due to an increase in the real part of permeability, as illustrated in Fig. S14. Phase detection reveals new phases such as MnO2, (Ba0.5La0.5) FeO2.995, and (La0.7Ba0.3) MnO3, as shown in Fig. 6f. Zones 5, 6, and 7 in Fig. S15 further confirm this view of XRD.

Ferrite of microwave absorption performance of different samples (a) 25#, (b) 26#, (c) 27#, (d) 28#, (e) 20#, and (f,g) XRD, (h,i) TEM, (j) XPS.

Additionally, as La2O3 doping increases, the characteristic peak at 32.0°–32.5° undergoes a redshift, as can been seen in Fig. 6g. TEM before and after doping reveals single-phase regions. Before doping, d3 = 0.274 nm corresponds to the (1 0 7) crystal plane of BaFe11.6Mn0.4O19. After doping, d4 = 0.24 nm, as shown in Fig. 6h-i. Combining Figs. 6g–i and S15, it is evident that La substitutes part of Ba in the (1 0 7) crystal plane of BaFe11.6Mn0.4O19, forming a new LaxBa1−xFe11.6Mn0.4O19 phase and causing the redshift. Figure 6j shows a significant increase in oxygen vacancy concentration after forming this new phase.

Therefore, the addition of La2O3 enables the material to form a new LaxBa1−xFe11.6Mn0.4O19 phase, which not only increases the oxygen vacancy concentration but also enhances the dielectric constant and magnetic permeability, and raises the minimum reflection loss value. However, the microwave absorption performance improvement with La2O3 alone is still inferior to that with combined La2O3 and CeO2 doping.

Combining the above results, it is evident that doping with La2O3 and CeO2 alone can reduce the minimum reflection loss value but does not significantly improve bandwidth and thickness. This is because the two rare earths have different effects, as shown in Fig. 7. Although both improve the magnetic energy dissipation-dominated loss mechanism, La2O3 primarily promotes oxygen vacancy generation by substituting part of Ba2+ and forming LaxBa1−xFe11.6Mn0.4O19, increasing the dielectric constant. CeO2, existing as a solid solution, reduces permeability tangent and improves the magnetic energy dissipation-dominated loss mechanism. Thus, when both are doped together, a synergistic effect occurs, significantly increasing oxygen vacancy concentration and improving microwave absorption -related properties (minimum reflection loss value, bandwidth, and thickness). Rare earth polishing powder contains both La2O3 and CeO22, making it feasible to dope a small amount during sintering, laying a solid foundation for future research.

The improvement of microwave absorption performance is caused by the formation of solid solutions and new phases.

Conclusion

This study successfully prepared M-type ferrite BaFe11.6Mn0.4O19 using magnetically separated sulfate manganese residue fine residue as a dopant. The goal was to regulate oxygen vacancy concentration and improve the loss mechanism to enhance microwave absorption performance. Oxygen vacancy concentration was controlled by adjusting roasting time and temperature. Then, by simulating the components of two typical rare earth solid wastes (neodymium-iron-boron waste and rare earth polishing powder), different rare earths were doped to prepare composite ferrites. The results show that doping with Nd2O3 does not improve microwave absorption, while doping with a La2O3 and CeO2 mixture effectively enhances it, reaching a minimum of − 33.42 dB under optimal conditions. At a width of 6.5 mm, the bandwidth is 2.37 GHz. Finally, the mechanisms by which CeO2 and La2O3 enhance oxygen vacancy concentration and microwave absorption performance were discussed in detail. La2O3 primarily promotes oxygen vacancy generation by substituting part of Ba2+ and forming LaxBa1−xFe11.6Mn0.4O19, increasing the dielectric constant. CeO2, existing as a solid solution, reduces permeability and improves the magnetic energy dissipation-dominated loss mechanism. This study provides a practical approach for resource.
