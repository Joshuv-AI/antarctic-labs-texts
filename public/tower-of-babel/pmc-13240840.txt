Introduction

The widespread implementation of combination antiretroviral therapy (ART) has fundamentally transformed the clinical course of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, converting a once-fatal disease into a manageable chronic condition (Alum et al., 2024). The widespread implementation of combination antiretroviral therapy (ART) has fundamentally transformed the clinical course of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, converting a once-fatal disease into a manageable chronic condition. ART effectively suppresses plasma viremia to undetectable levels by targeting multiple steps of the viral life cycle; however, it does not eliminate the virus. A major barrier to cure is the persistence of replication-competent proviruses within latent reservoirs, predominantly in long-lived resting CD4⁺ T cells, which rapidly reseed systemic infection upon treatment interruption (Łupina et al., 2025; Martínez-Sanz et al., 2022; Larijani et al., 2019).

HIV latency is a multifactorial and multilayered phenomenon regulated by both transcriptional and post-transcriptional mechanisms. At the transcriptional level, latency is largely maintained by an unfavorable chromatin environment at the integrated proviral long terminal repeat (LTR), characterized by restrictive histone modifications and nucleosome positioning that limit access of transcriptional machinery (Kobayashi-Ishihara and Tsunetsugu-Yokota, 2024; Matsuda and Maeda, 2024). In addition, the absence or cytoplasmic sequestration of key transcription factors, including nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), further contributes to transcriptional silencing in resting CD4⁺ T cells (Chan and Greene, 2011).

Beyond transcriptional repression, post-transcriptional mechanisms also play a critical role in maintaining HIV latency. Even when transcriptional initiation is achieved, productive viral gene expression requires efficient transcriptional elongation, alternative RNA splicing, nuclear export of viral transcripts, RNA stability, and translation. Defects or blocks at any of these stages can significantly reduce viral protein production and infectious virion assembly, thereby sustaining a functionally latent state despite the presence of initiated transcription. This highlights that HIV latency is not solely defined at the level of transcriptional initiation but represents a continuum of regulatory blocks spanning multiple stages of gene expression (Okeoma et al., 2025; Yukl et al., 2018). Accordingly, NF-κB signaling has emerged as a central host pathway in HIV latency regulation. The HIV LTR contains multiple NF-κB binding sites, and NF-κB activation strongly promotes viral transcriptional reactivation in response to immune stimulation, inflammation, or cellular stress. In this context, pharmacological targeting of NF-κB has become a key component of latency-reversal strategies within the “shock and kill” framework, which aims to induce viral gene expression in latently infected cells and facilitate their elimination through immune-mediated clearance or viral cytopathic effects (Madapuji Srinivasan, 2024; Chandrasekar et al., 2024).

However, it is important to emphasize that NF-κB primarily regulates transcriptional initiation and is insufficient on its own to guarantee productive viral reactivation. Effective reversal of latency requires coordinated completion of downstream processes, including transcriptional elongation and post-transcriptional RNA processing (Yukl et al., 2018; Dorman et al., 2025; Lassen et al., 2006). This limitation is particularly relevant when evaluating NF-κB based latency-reversing strategies, as successful viral reactivation depends on overcoming multiple regulatory barriers beyond promoter activation.

This review critically examines the mechanistic role of NF-κB signaling in HIV latency and reactivation, with particular emphasis on its therapeutic potential, limitations, and integration within combinatorial latency-reversal strategies aimed at achieving durable HIV remission.

Accordingly, NF-κB centered interventions must be evaluated within a multilayer regulatory framework encompassing both transcriptional and post-transcriptional barriers to HIV expression.

General overview of NF-κB signaling

Nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) is a family of transcription factors that plays a central role in regulating immune responses, inflammation, cell survival, and stress adaptation. Originally identified as a regulator of immunoglobulin κ light-chain expression in B cells, NF-κB is now recognized as a master transcriptional hub that integrates signals from a wide range of extracellular and intracellular stimuli, including cytokines, pattern recognition receptors, antigen receptors, and cellular stress signals (Guo et al., 2024).

The NF-κB family consists of five structurally related proteins: RelA (p65), RelB, c-Rel, p50 (processed from p105), and p52 (processed from p100). These proteins form various homo- and heterodimers that differ in transcriptional activity and biological function. In resting cells, NF-κB dimers are retained in the cytoplasm through interaction with inhibitory IκB proteins, which mask their nuclear localization signals and prevent transcriptional activity (Msweli et al., 2024; Dhaka et al., 2024).

NF-κB signaling is classically divided into two major pathways: the canonical and the non-canonical pathways. The canonical pathway is rapidly activated by pro-inflammatory stimuli such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and Toll-like receptor ligands. Activation of this pathway involves the IκB kinase (IKK) complex composed of IKKα, IKKβ, and the regulatory subunit NEMO (IKKγ) which phosphorylates IκB proteins, targeting them for proteasomal degradation. This process releases NF-κB dimers, predominantly p65/p50, allowing their translocation into the nucleus and initiation of target gene transcription (Mukherjee et al., 2024; Ko et al., 2022).

In contrast, the non-canonical NF-κB pathway is activated by a more restricted set of stimuli, including certain members of the TNF receptor superfamily such as CD40, BAFF-R, and lymphotoxin-β receptor. This pathway depends on NF-κB-inducing kinase (NIK) mediated activation of IKKα, leading to phosphorylation and partial proteolysis of p100 into p52. The resulting p52/RelB heterodimers regulate a distinct transcriptional program involved in lymphoid organogenesis, B-cell maturation, and immune homeostasis (Chandrasekar et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025).

Beyond its role in immunity, NF-κB signaling exerts profound effects on chromatin remodeling and transcriptional elongation by recruiting co-activators such as CBP/p300, histone acetyltransferases, and components of the transcriptional elongation machinery. Consequently, NF-κB functions not only as a signal-responsive transcription factor but also as an epigenetic regulator capable of shaping long-term gene expression programs (Chen et al., 2022; Hoffmann et al., 2025).

Given its central position at the interface of immune activation, inflammation, and transcriptional control, NF-κB has been implicated in a wide spectrum of pathological conditions, including cancer, autoimmune diseases, chronic inflammation, and viral infections. This multifunctional nature renders NF-κB both an attractive and challenging therapeutic target particularly in the context of persistent viral infections where controlled transcriptional activation may be desirable. This central positioning of NF-κB at the intersection of immune activation and transcriptional control makes it a critical host determinant of both HIV latency establishment and latency reversal. (Guo et al., 2024).

Role of NF-κB in HIV latency and transcription

NF-κB signaling in the context of HIV transcription

NF-κB is a central host transcription factor that directly regulates HIV gene expression through its interaction with κB enhancer elements located within the viral long terminal repeat (LTR). While the general features of NF-κB signaling pathways are outlined in Section 2, their relevance in HIV infection is primarily defined by their ability to control proviral transcription in response to cellular activation signals.

The canonical NF-κB pathway represents the principal driver of HIV transcriptional activation. Upon stimulation of infected CD4⁺ T cells by pro-inflammatory cytokines, antigen receptor engagement, or other activating signals, NF-κB dimers predominantly p65/p50 translocate into the nucleus and bind to the HIV LTR (Trares et al., 2022). This interaction initiates transcription and promotes the recruitment of co-activators such as CBP/p300 and other chromatin-modifying enzymes, leading to histone acetylation and increased accessibility of the viral promoter. In addition, NF-κB cooperates with host transcription factors such as Sp1 and the viral protein Tat to enhance transcriptional elongation and amplify viral gene expression (Williams et al., 2006; Sandoval et al., 2024).

In contrast, the non-canonical NF-κB pathway appears to play a more limited and context-dependent role in HIV transcription. Although p52/RelB complexes regulate specific immune gene programs, their direct contribution to HIV LTR activation is considerably less pronounced than that of canonical NF-κB signaling (Sun, 2017; Chen, 2024; de Oliveira and Abreu, 2024).

Overall, NF-κB functions as a molecular link between cellular activation and HIV transcription. Signals that induce NF-κB activity can trigger rapid transcriptional activation of the provirus, positioning NF-κB as a key regulator of HIV reactivation from latency.

In the canonical pathway (left), activation is initiated by upstream receptors and stimuli such as the TNF receptor (TNFR1), T-cell receptor (TCR), and CD40, typically in response to pro-inflammatory cytokines and T-cell activation signals. The complex phosphorylates IκBα, triggering its ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, which releases the p65/p50 NF-κB heterodimer. The p50 subunit is derived from proteasomal processing of its precursor p105. The liberated p65/p50 dimer translocates to the nucleus, binds the HIV long terminal repeat (LTR), and drives robust viral transcription. This pathway represents the dominant and rapid mechanism for proviral reactivation and HIV gene expression. In the non-canonical pathway (right), signaling is activated by a distinct set of receptors, including lymphotoxin-β receptor (LTβR), BAFF-R, CD40, and RANK. These signals stabilize and activate NF-κB-inducing kinase (NIK), which in turn activates IKKα homodimers independently of NEMO. Activated IKKα phosphorylates the NF-κB precursor p100, leading to its partial proteasomal processing into p52. The resulting RelB/p52 heterodimer translocates to the nucleus and binds κB elements within the HIV LTR, contributing to transcriptional activation. Compared to the canonical pathway, this signaling axis is slower, more sustained, and context-dependent. Overall, both pathways can regulate HIV transcription; however, the canonical NF-κB pathway (p65/p50) is the primary driver of HIV LTR activation and proviral transcription, whereas the non-canonical pathway (RelB/p52) provides complementary and modulatory effects that may influence latency reversal and transcriptional fine-tuning under specific cellular conditions.

NF-κB in maintaining HIV latency

Despite its well-established role in promoting HIV transcription, NF-κB also contributes to the maintenance of viral latency under basal conditions. In resting CD4⁺ T cells, NF-κB activity is tightly suppressed, resulting in limited nuclear availability of transcriptionally active NF-κB dimers (Scaglioni, 2025). This inactive state is associated with a repressive chromatin architecture at the HIV LTR, characterized by positioned nucleosomes and restricted access of RNA polymerase II and essential transcriptional cofactors, thereby maintaining the provirus in a transcriptionally silent state (Pluta et al., 2020).

Importantly, distinct NF-κB subunits exert differential effects on HIV transcription. While p65/p50 heterodimers act as potent transcriptional activators upon cellular stimulation, p50 homodimers lack transactivation domains and instead function as transcriptional repressors. These repressive complexes bind to κB sites within the HIV LTR and recruit histone deacetylases (HDACs) and other chromatin-remodeling enzymes, reinforcing a condensed chromatin state and stabilizing latency (Pluta et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020). This dual and context-dependent behavior highlights NF-κB as both an activator and a repressor of HIV gene expression. The balance between these opposing functions is determined by cellular activation status, subunit composition, and chromatin context. Understanding this dynamic regulation is critical for the development of therapeutic strategies aimed at latency reversal, where controlled activation of NF-κB may be leveraged to induce viral transcription without triggering excessive immune activation or systemic inflammation.

PKC agonists

Protein kinase C (PKC) agonists represent one of the most extensively studied classes of compounds for NF-κB mediated HIV latency reversal. These agents activate canonical NF-κB signaling through PKC stimulation, which in turn triggers IκB kinase (IKK) activation and subsequent phosphorylation and proteasomal degradation of IκB inhibitory proteins. Release of p65/p50 heterodimers permits their nuclear translocation and binding to κB elements within the HIV- LTR, thereby initiating viral transcription (Jiang and Dandekar, 2015). Representative PKC agonists include Prostratin (Beans et al., 2013), Bryostatin-1 (Darcis et al., 2015; Hany et al., 2022), and Ingenol derivatives (Jiang et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2019), have demonstrated robust HIV reactivation in both in vitro and ex vivo models of latently infected primary CD4⁺ T cells. Collectively, these findings highlight the promise of PKC agonists as potent latency-reversing agents (LRAs), although their pronounced systemic immune-activating effects remain a significant translational challenge (Rasi et al., 2025; Cary et al., 2016).

Combination with other latency-reversing agents (LRAs)

The latency-reversing efficacy of PKC agonists can be substantially enhanced through rational combination with other classes of latency-reversing agents (LRAs), particularly those targeting complementary layers of HIV transcriptional regulation. Among these, combinations involving histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACi) and bromodomain and extra-terminal domain (BET)/BRD4 inhibitors have emerged as the most mechanistically and functionally significant (Mirza et al., 2017; Curreli et al., 2020). Co-administration of PKC agonists with HDAC inhibitors such as vorinostat promotes HIV reactivation by simultaneously relieving chromatin-mediated repression and enhancing transcription factor accessibility at the HIV long terminal repeat (LTR) (Kroon et al., 2020; Gay et al., 2024). However, beyond epigenetic modulation, a critical additional regulatory checkpoint in HIV transcription involves transcriptional elongation control mediated by BRD4 and positive transcription elongation factor b (P-TEFb).

BET/BRD4 inhibitors, such as JQ1, act by displacing BRD4 from chromatin and releasing sequestered P-TEFb complexes. This liberation of P-TEFb enables efficient phosphorylation of RNA polymerase II C-terminal domain (CTD), thereby promoting transcriptional elongation of nascent HIV transcripts. Importantly, this mechanism synergizes with PKC agonist-induced NF-κB activation, which primarily drives transcriptional initiation at the HIV LTR.

Preclinical studies have demonstrated that combinations such as bryostatin-1 + JQ1 and ingenol-B + JQ1 produce some of the most potent latency-reversing effects across multiple in vitro and ex vivo models (Darcis et al., 2015). Similarly, Bullen et al. reported that BET inhibition significantly enhances PKC-mediated HIV reactivation by overcoming transcriptional elongation blocks, highlighting a dual requirement for both initiation (NF-κB-dependent) and elongation (P-TEFb-dependent) processes in productive viral reactivation (Bullen et al., 2014).

Despite their strong synergistic activity in experimental systems, translation of these combinations into clinical settings remains limited due to concerns regarding toxicity, off-target transcriptional effects, and incomplete immune-mediated clearance of reactivated cells. These findings underscore that effective latency reversal requires coordinated targeting of both chromatin accessibility and transcriptional elongation machinery rather than activation of NF-κB signaling alone.

Collectively, PKC agonist BET inhibitor combinations represent a mechanistically rational and highly potent class of LRAs that bridge two essential barriers to HIV reactivation: transcriptional initiation and elongation. Their inclusion in combinatorial “shock-and-kill” strategies provides an important framework for future therapeutic development (Jiang and Dandekar, 2015).

These combinatorial strategies highlight that effective latency reversal requires simultaneous targeting of transcriptional initiation, elongation, and epigenetic repression.

NF-κB and interaction with nucleotides/HDACs

NF-κB functions not only as a transcriptional activator at the HIV LTR but also as a critical interface with chromatin-modifying enzymes, including histone deacetylases (HDACs). In resting CD4⁺ T cells, HDAC activity sustains a repressive chromatin configuration at the LTR, thereby restricting RNA polymerase II recruitment. Upon NF-κB activation, the coordinated recruitment of transcriptional co-activators and histone acetyltransferases antagonizes HDAC-mediated repression, enabling transcriptional initiation (Khatun et al., 2024; Tioka et al., 2025). This dynamic interplay underscores the necessity of coordinated modulation of NF-κB signaling and epigenetic regulators to achieve efficient HIV latency reversal.

Indirect targeting of NF-κB pathways

Beyond direct PKC-mediated activation, accumulating evidence suggests that the non-canonical NF-κB pathway also contributes to HIV latency reversal. For instance, SMAC mimetics, such as AZD5582, promote proteolytic processing of p100 to p52, thereby activating p52/RelB heterodimers that translocate to the nucleus and induce HIV transcription (Molyer et al., 2021). Notably, this indirect targeting strategy may confer greater selectivity, as non-canonical NF-κB activation can elicit latent HIV reactivation with reduced global T-cell activation compared with canonical PKC agonists. These approaches are currently under active investigation and represent promising avenues for the development of next-generation latency-reversing therapies (Wong and Jiang, 2021).

Recent studies have demonstrated that AZD5582 exhibits enhanced latency-reversal activity when combined with BET bromodomain inhibitors such as JQ1, highlighting the potential of combinatorial LRA strategies (Falcinelli et al., 2022). In addition to BET inhibitors, combinations of AZD5582 with other latency-reversing agents (LRAs), including NF-κB activating compounds such as prostratin, should be considered and discussed due to their potential synergistic effects on HIV latency reversal (Li et al., 2013). Recent studies have demonstrated that AZD5582 exhibits enhanced latency-reversal activity when combined with histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACis) such as vorinostat, highlighting the potential of combinatorial LRA strategies (Lilie et al., 2024). Combinations of AZD5582 with protein kinase C (PKC) agonists, including prostratin and bryostatin-1, have also been suggested to potentiate HIV latency reversal (Marsden et al., 2018) and should be discussed in the text and summarized in Table 1.

Challenges and safety concerns

Adverse effects of NF-κB activation

Although NF-κB targeted strategies show considerable promise for reversing HIV latency, their clinical application is constrained by important safety concerns. Broad or sustained NF-κB activation can provoke systemic inflammation and non-specific induction of immune-related gene expression, increasing the risk of immune hyperactivation and cytokine-mediated toxicity. Moreover, aberrant NF-κB signaling may exert off-target effects on other genomic elements, including transposable elements, leading to unintended chromatin remodeling, genomic instability, or dysregulated host gene expression (Wong and Jiang, 2021; Lv et al., 2021). These potential adverse consequences highlight the necessity for precise, temporally controlled, and selective modulation of NF-κB signaling in the development of effective and safe latency-reversing therapies.

Current clinical limitations

Despite encouraging results in vitro and ex vivo systems, many NF-κB targeting latency-reversing agents (LRAs) have demonstrated limited efficacy in vivo. Some compounds fail to elicit sufficient viral reactivation in patients, whereas others are constrained by dose-limiting toxicity or adverse immunological effects (Tioka et al., 2025). For instance, PKC agonists, while highly potent in experimental models, can induce off-target immune activation that compromises tolerability and restricts clinical applicability. These observations underscore the persistent gap between experimental latency reversal and its safe and effective translation into human therapies (Kawano et al., 2021; Irrinki et al., 2025).

Ultimately, the central challenge lies in achieving robust HIV reactivation while minimizing systemic toxicity. Addressing this balance will require the development of more selective NF-κB modulators, optimization of dosing strategies, and rational combinatorial approaches incorporating complementary LRAs or immune-based interventions. Future and combinatorial strategies

Combining LRAs with immunotherapy

One of the most promising approaches to enhance HIV reservoir clearance is the combination of NF-κB targeted latency-reversing agents (LRAs) with immunotherapeutic strategies. Following LRA-induced viral reactivation, therapeutic vaccines or bispecific T-cell engagers (BiTEs) can augment immune recognition and elimination of reactivated infected cells. This integrative approach effectively couples the “shock” phase of latency reversal with a robust “kill” phase, thereby enhancing reservoir clearance while potentially limiting off-target immune activation (Brozy et al., 2018; Debaveye, 2022).

Precision NF-κB targeting

To mitigate the systemic inflammatory effects associated with broad NF-κB activation, next-generation strategies emphasize selective and context-dependent modulation of this pathway (Mashhadi Abolghasem Shirazi et al., 2023). Targeting upstream signaling nodes or HIV-specific transcriptional cofactors may confine NF-κB activation to latently infected cells, thereby minimizing unintended global T-cell activation. Such precision approaches include small molecules designed to fine-tune NF-κB activation kinetics, as well as agents that selectively regulate nuclear translocation or cofactor recruitment at the HIV -LTR. Collectively, these strategies offer the potential for improved safety profiles for clinical application (Mashhadi Abolghasem Shirazi et al., 2025).

Genetic and RNA-based approaches

Recent advances underscore the potential of genetic and RNA-based interventions to modulate NF-κB dependent HIV transcription. Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), such as NKILA, have been shown to suppress NF-κB signaling by sequestering p65 in the cytoplasm, thereby influencing viral transcriptional activity (Ramirez et al., 2023). Exploiting lncRNAs or other RNA-based regulators could provide novel targets for latency management, either by enhancing latency reversal in a controlled manner or by maintaining latency when necessary (Wang et al., 2020; Liu, 2025). These approaches represent an emerging frontier that integrates molecular precision with therapeutic modulation of viral reservoirs. Conclusion

The NF-κB signaling pathway occupies a bi-functional and context-dependent position in HIV biology, acting both as a principal driver of viral transcription and as a regulator that contributes to the maintenance of viral latency under basal cellular conditions. This duality underscores both the therapeutic potential and the inherent risks of targeting NF-κB in HIV cure strategies.

Within the “shock-and-kill” framework, several classes of latency-reversing agents (LRAs) converge on NF-κB activation but differ substantially in their potency, safety, and translational feasibility. Protein kinase C (PKC) agonists such as bryostatin-1 and prostratin are among the most potent inducers of HIV transcription in ex vivo models, largely due to their robust activation of canonical NF-κB signaling. However, their clinical applicability has been limited by significant toxicity, narrow therapeutic windows, and challenges in dose optimization. In contrast, histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors such as vorinostat have demonstrated more favorable safety profiles in clinical settings, yet their ability to induce HIV transcription in vivo has been modest and often insufficient to meaningfully reduce the latent reservoir.

More recently, SMAC mimetics such as AZD5582 have emerged as promising agents that activate the non-canonical NF-κB pathway. Preclinical studies, including ex vivo and animal models, suggest that these compounds can induce HIV latency reversal with a potentially improved tolerability profile compared to PKC agonists. Nevertheless, clinical data remain limited, and their long-term safety and efficacy in humans have yet to be fully established.

Importantly, accumulating clinical evidence indicates that latency reversal alone, regardless of the potency of NF-κB activation, is insufficient to achieve durable reservoir reduction. Trials involving LRAs, including HDAC inhibitors, have consistently shown increases in HIV transcription without corresponding decreases in reservoir size. These findings highlight a major translational limitation: effective HIV cure strategies must integrate latency reversal with immune-mediated clearance mechanisms.

Consequently, combinatorial approaches that couple NF-κB–targeting LRAs with immune enhancing interventions such as therapeutic vaccines, bispecific T-cell engagers, or engineered cytotoxic lymphocytes are increasingly recognized as essential. At the same time, advances in the molecular understanding of NF-κB signaling, including pathway-specific activation and HIV-selective transcriptional regulation, offer opportunities for more precise therapeutic targeting aimed at minimizing systemic immune activation.

Emerging regulatory layers, including long non-coding RNAs such as NKILA, further expand the landscape of potential interventions by enabling fine-tuned modulation of NF-κB activity. Collectively, these insights support a model in which NF-κB centered strategies, when critically optimized and combined with immunological and epigenetic approaches, hold promise for overcoming HIV latency. However, achieving a functional cure will require not only potent and safe induction of viral transcription but also efficient and sustained clearance of infected cells in vivo.

Ultimately, the path toward a functional HIV cure will depend not on indiscriminate activation of NF-κB, but on the precise and context-specific orchestration of its signaling to selectively unlock proviral transcription while simultaneously enabling efficient immune-mediated clearance. Future therapeutic success will depend on integrating NF-κB modulation with control of RNA processing and immune clearance mechanisms to achieve complete viral eradication from latent reservoirs.
