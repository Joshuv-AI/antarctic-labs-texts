Endoscopic evaluation in patients with gastrointestinal bleeding receiving anticoagulation for venous thromboembolism
Authors: Nichole E. Brunton, Waldemar E. Wysokinski, Ryan T. Moerer, David O. Hodge, Andres J. Acosta, Danielle T. Vlazny, Damon E. Houghton, Ana I. Casanegra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and AimsGastrointestinal bleeding (GIB) is a common adverse event of anticoagulation in patients undergoing treatment for venous thromboembolism (VTE) and is often assessed by endoscopic evaluation. It remains unclear whether identification of the bleeding source impacts outcomes. Our aim was to describe endoscopic evaluation strategies, management, and outcomes in patients with GIB complicating anticoagulation for VTE.MethodsPatients in the Mayo Clinic VTE registry between March 2013 and October 2021 were prospectively followed. For patients with a first episode of GIB, endoscopic studies and related interventions were reviewed.ResultsAmong the 3287 patients, 109 (3%) experienced GIB (median age = 67, 52.3% female). Endoscopic evaluation occurred in 73 patients (67%): upper endoscopy (n = 53, 49%), colonoscopy (n = 41, 38%), and capsule endoscopy (n = 2, 2%). Endoscopy identified a bleeding source in 51 cases (73%), and hemostatic intervention occurred in 19 cases (26%). A bleeding source was not identified in 26 (24%) of cases. Among the 33 (30%) patients who did not undergo endoscopic evaluation, the majority (n = 29, 88%) had an identified source by either prior endoscopy or alternative imaging modality. Anticoagulation was interrupted in 90 (82%) patients for an average of 3.5 days; this was similar regardless of whether a source was identified. During follow-up (median = 103 days), 16 (14.7%) patients experienced a recurrent GIB, and 54 (50%) patients died. Rates of recurrent bleeding, VTE, and death were similar between patients with identified and unidentified bleeding sources.ConclusionsAlthough endoscopy has a high diagnostic yield in anticoagulated patients, anticoagulation management and clinical outcomes are similar regardless of GIB source identification. Further studies are needed to determine the net clinical benefit of endoscopy in this high-risk cohort.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal bleeding (GIB) is a well-described adverse event in patients receiving anticoagulation therapy for the treatment of venous thromboembolism (VTE). GIB is the most common site of bleeding regardless of anticoagulant choice (warfarin, heparin-based therapy, or direct oral anticoagulant [DOAC]). Prior studies suggest GIB events occur at a rate of 1.02% to 3.43%, depending on anticoagulant type.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 However, these trials excluded patients with a GIB history in the 6 to 12 months prior, potentially underestimating real-world risks.3

GIB events in patients with VTE carry significant implications for morbidity and mortality. Clinicians must balance the risk of bleeding with the risk of recurrent VTE, which can range from 0.7% to 19% in the early months after the VTE event, depending on clinical circumstances.3,9 Despite the frequent and serious clinical problem, few studies have evaluated treatment strategies for patients with concomitant thrombosis and GIB. Little is known about the benefit of endoscopic evaluation or the utility of endoscopic intervention in this cohort. To date, to our knowledge, only 1 study exists reviewing endoscopic findings in patients receiving anticoagulants.10

Currently, there are no clear guidelines from the American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) or the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy to assist in guiding anticoagulation continuation after GIB, even when a source can be identified and treated. If anticoagulation is restarted, the optimal timing and net benefit are unknown. Anticoagulant resumption was previously associated with reduced rates of mortality and thrombosis; however, it has also been associated with higher rates of recurrent bleeding.11

The primary aim of this study was to investigate endoscopic evaluation strategies and anticoagulation management in patients experiencing GIB while receiving anticoagulation to treat VTE. Secondary aims were to compare the clinician management and clinical outcomes between patients with an identified GIB source to those without an identifiable source.

Methods

Patients requiring anticoagulation therapy for VTE were followed in the Mayo Clinic VTE registry (Rochester, Minn, USA) from March 1, 2013 to October 1, 2021. All 3287 enrolled patients were followed prospectively for major bleeding (MB) events, clinically relevant non-MB (CRNMB) events, VTE recurrence, and all-cause mortality. The VTE registry includes all patients referred to the thrombosis, anticoagulation, and thrombophilia clinic for management of VTE regardless of the diagnostic setting (outpatient primary care, specialty care, radiology, or inpatient).12 Patients were prospectively followed face-to-face at predefined intervals (3, 6 months, and annually). If an in-person encounter was not feasible, a questionnaire was used (e-mail, letter, or scripted telephone interview).

For this study, patients who experienced MB or CRNMB from a gastrointestinal (GI) source were identified from the registry for further review of the medical records for details of the bleeding event, including endoscopic evaluation, interventions, and management of anticoagulant therapy.

The study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board.

Study definitions and outcome measures

Clinical outcomes of MB and CRNMB were defined in accordance with the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis. MB was defined as an overt bleed with a corresponding hemoglobin decrease of ≥2 g/dL, a bleed requiring 2 or greater units of packed red blood cell (PRBC) transfusion within a 24-hour timeframe, or bleeding at a critical site.13 A critical bleeding site is defined as intracranial, intraspinal, intraocular, pericardial, intra-articular, retroperitoneal, or any bleeding that results in hemodynamic compromise.13 CRNMB was defined as a bleeding episode that did not meet criteria for MB; however, it required temporary discontinuation of anticoagulation or unscheduled interaction with medical personnel. Anticoagulation included heparin-based strategies, DOACs, or warfarin.

Active malignancy for this study was defined as a patient with evidence of cancer on imaging or receiving cancer-related therapies (chemo/immunotherapy, radiation, and surgical intervention) within the prior 6 months without achievement of complete remission. The GI cancer group was comprised of patients with malignancy affecting the GI tract (esophageal, gastric, small intestine, or colonic) and pancreatic and hepatobiliary cancers. VTE recurrence was defined as a new VTE event during the follow-up timeframe while on anticoagulation.

All events were independently adjudicated using previous study criteria outlined by 4 thrombophilia clinic providers (A.I.C., D.T.V., D.E.H., and W.E.W.).12

Statistical analysis

Baseline data are reported as a percent of the total for categorical variables, or medians and interquartile ranges for continuous variables. Comparisons between groups for the categorical variables were completed using Fisher exact tests. Continuous variables were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Follow-up events were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Log-rank tests were used to compare curves between groups. Analysis was completed using R Statistical Software (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Patients with GIB were most frequently prescribed apixaban (n = 36, 33.0%), followed by rivaroxaban (n = 30, 27.5%), heparin-based medications (n = 26, 23.9%), and warfarin (n = 17, 15.6%). Underlying malignancy was present in 76 patients (69.7%), with 22 patients (20.2%) diagnosed with a primary malignancy of the GI tract. A history of prior GIB was common in the cohort (n = 42, 41.2%), with 13 patients (11.9%) having undergone an endoscopy in the 6 months preceding the index event.

Management strategies for bleeding events, including endoscopy

Endoscopic evaluation at the time of the event was completed in 73 patients (66.9%), with 64 patients (58.7%) undergoing endoscopy while hospitalized. In 9 cases (8.3%), endoscopy was completed in the outpatient setting after a bleeding event. In total, these included upper endoscopy (n = 53, 48.6%), colonoscopy (n = 41, 36.6%), and capsule endoscopy (n = 2, 1.8%). Both upper and lower endoscopy were completed in 21 patients (19.3%) (Fig. 1, Table 2). There were 3 cases (2.7%) in which endoscopic evaluation was not documented. When used during acute hospitalization, endoscopy identified a bleeding source in 51 cases (69.9%). Active bleeding was observed in 14 endoscopic evaluations (19.2%), and 28 (38.4%) demonstrated stigmata of recent bleeding. Blood transfusion occurred in 62 events (56.4%) with a mean of 1.8 units of PRBC transfused. For upper GIB sources identified on endoscopy, ulcers (n = 12, 22.6%) and varices (n = 8, 15.1%) were most frequent. Other etiologies included GI mass (n = 5, 9.4%) and angioectasia (n = 4, 7.5%). Median duration of time from bleeding event to EGD was 2 days, with 25 upper endoscopies occurring urgently in <1 day of admission. Lower GI sources consisted of diverticulosis (n = 7, 17.5%), mass (n = 5, 12.5%), ulceration (n = 4, 10.0%), and hemorrhoids (n = 2, 5.0%). Infrequent sources of bleeding included lymphocytic colitis, arteriovenous malformation, ischemic colitis, rectal varices, and radiation proctitis. Median duration of time from bleeding event to colonoscopy was 3 days, with 12 colonoscopies occurring urgently within <1 day of admission.

Patients did not undergo endoscopic evaluation at the time of GIB in 33 cases (30.3%) (Fig. 1). Of these, 12 patients (34.3%) had an endoscopy in the preceding 6 months with 4 (30.8%) having a GI malignancy and 6 (50.0%) having a nonmalignant source of bleeding identified including ulcerations, active colitis, diverticulosis, or anastomotic site. These findings were assumed to be the bleeding source for the subsequent event. Two patients (15.4%) had a prior endoscopy without an identified source of bleeding. In the remainder of patients without endoscopic evaluation, a bleeding source was identified by an alternate imaging modality (n = 10, 45.5%), such as a recent computed tomography scan. Others were attributed to recent procedures (n = 3, 13.6%), such as bowel resection, or an obvious bleeding source was externally visible (n = 3, 13.6%), such as visibly bleeding external hemorrhoids or an ostomy site. Infrequent causes of endoscopic deferral included patient declination of endoscopy (n = 3, 13.6%) and transition to hospice care (n = 2, 9.1%). One single patient was not recommended for further endoscopic evaluation and had not undergone prior evaluation (n = 1, 4.5%). This case reported an isolated minor GIB that resolved spontaneously before presentation.

Of the 38 patients (72.0%) with a bleeding source identified on upper endoscopy, 15 (28.3%) had an intervention. These included argon plasma coagulation (n = 4, 26.7%), bipolar cautery (n = 4, 26.7%), banding (n = 3, 20.0%), and clipping (n = 2, 13.3%). Endoscopic intervention was used in only 4 (10%) of the lower GIB events, including 3 treatments with argon plasma coagulation and 1 with bipolar cautery. Alternative strategies including open surgery were pursued in 4 cases, and interventional radiology–guided embolization in 1 additional case (Fig. 1, Table 2).

Management of anticoagulation and outcomes

Therapeutic anticoagulation was held in 90 patients (82%) for a median duration of 3.5 days (range, 0-116 days). For 21 patients (19.2%), anticoagulation was reduced to a prophylactic dose temporarily, and in 4 cases (3.7%), it was reduced to prophylactic dosing long-term. Anticoagulation was indefinitely discontinued in 36 cases (33.0%). Patients self-discontinued anticoagulation before presentation in 9 events (8.3%). A minority of cases continued anticoagulation despite bleeding (n = 19, 17.3%). During follow-up, 56 patients (51.0%) died. There were 20 patients (18.3%) who experienced a recurrent MB or CRNMB. Of these, 16 (80.0%) occurred from a GI source, giving a cumulative event probability of 0.24 at 2 years. Clinical outcomes between patients with and without bleeding source identified

Anticoagulation management after GIB event was not statistically different between patients with identified sources of bleeding compared with those without an identified source. For patients with an identified GIB source, anticoagulation was interrupted for a median range of 3.5 days (range, 0-116 days), similar to those without an identified source (3.5 days; range, 0-33 days; P = .9). Resumption of therapeutic anticoagulation on hospital dismissal was similar, occurring in 24 cases (29.6%) in the identified group compared with 12 (46.2%) in the unidentified group, P = .23. Discussion

Our study reveals that endoscopy is a helpful tool in establishing the etiology of GIB in most patients receiving anticoagulation for VTE treatment. Despite the identification of bleeding source and high thrombotic risk in our population that warrants resumption of anticoagulation, bleeding source control was often managed conservatively. Endoscopic intervention remains infrequent. Management of anticoagulation after GIB did not significantly differ between patients with and without an identified source. Furthermore, outcomes, including recurrent bleeding, recurrent VTE, and all-cause mortality, were similar regardless of the identification of the bleeding source.

Utility of endoscopic evaluation and intervention in anticoagulated patients

Few studies exist investigating endoscopic utilization in patients receiving anticoagulation therapy, to our knowledge. One retrospective study by Peloquin et al14 in 2016 reviewed the diagnostic and therapeutic yield of endoscopy in patients receiving warfarin with a supratherapeutic international normalized ratio (INR), concluding that diagnostic utility was inversely related to INR levels. Our study population included mostly patients within therapeutic INR levels (13 of 15 patients, 86.7%) and included patients on DOAC or heparin-based anticoagulation therapies. When compared with patients not on anticoagulation, the diagnostic yield for endoscopy in our study was similar.15, 16,17, 18, 19

Acute upper GIB carries a significant in-hospital mortality risk, approximately 10%, necessitating early identification and endoscopic intervention when applicable.17,20 Current recommendations for upper endoscopy by the ACG are to complete the EGD evaluation within 24 hours of initial presentation.21 Common findings on upper endoscopy in our study were similar to the general population; peptic ulcer disease (25.4%) followed by varices (15.7%).21, 22, 23 The third-most common etiology of upper GIB in the general population is esophagitis, typically comprising 10% of events.23 In our study, the third-most most common cause was upper GI mass (9.8%), a rare cause of GIB in the general population.21,24 A potential reason for this discrepancy is due to the nature of our patient population, being a quaternary care center with a large referral basis for those with underlying malignancy.

For presumed lower GIB events, current guidelines recommend that colonoscopy be performed during hospitalization if bowel preparation is adequate.15 Early colonoscopy, occurring within 24 hours of presentation, is not associated with decreased mortality, recurrent bleeding rates, or adverse events on meta-analysis of the 4 available randomized control studies comparing early versus delayed colonoscopy.25 Frequent sources for lower GIB in our population were diverticular bleed (18.4%), followed by colonic masses (13.2%). In the general population, diverticular bleeding accounts for approximately 40% of lower GI bleeds, followed by ischemic colitis at 20%. GI masses represent an uncommon etiology18 and were more frequent in our study than in the general population. This reflects the population served in our quaternary care center because 28.9% of patients included in our study had a known primary GI malignancy.

Anticoagulation interruption for GIB events (identified vs unidentified)

Most patients experienced anticoagulation interruption, either by direction of the medical team or by patient choice. Anticoagulation was interrupted for a median duration of 3.5 days in our cohort (range, 0-116 days). Optimal timing to resume anticoagulation after a bleeding event is unclear in patients with high thrombotic risk. In patients receiving vitamin K antagonist, the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy recommends resuming vitamin K antagonist with heparin bridge within 72 hours of bleeding in patients with high VTE risk.25 However, management of DOAC therapy is more ambiguous.

The majority of our patients were taking DOAC therapy at the time of GIB. Although guidelines recommend DOAC cessation for a minimum of 7 days after a major GIB event, thrombotic risk was not incorporated as part of this recommendation. In the 2022 ACG guidelines, a consensus could not be reached regarding optimal timing for resumption of anticoagulation after elective endoscopy, and no commentary was made regarding anticoagulation resumption after an acute lower GIB event.26 No guidelines address patients with acute VTE directly, to our knowledge.26,27

From prior cohort studies and risk analysis, anticoagulation resumption between 1 and 3 weeks after GIB is reasonable to balance between the risk of bleeding and thrombosis.28 However, most studies have included a mix of indications for anticoagulation (atrial fibrillation, valves, and VTE) with various thrombotic risks. All our patients have VTE, and most carry high-risk features for recurrence (acute thrombosis, cancer, thrombophilia, etc). Although no studies have reviewed risks and benefits in this difficult clinical scenario, to our knowledge, if therapeutic anticoagulation must be held, it may be reasonable to consider placement of an inferior vena cava filter if proximal VTE is present or to consider a trial of prophylactic dose of anticoagulation.29 No data exist to support these as effective alternative strategies, and decisions must be determined either by expert opinion or a multidisciplinary team in each clinical scenario.

On the basis of the currently available literature, it is unknown whether the identification of bleeding source alters the ideal timing of anticoagulation resumption. In our study, patients with an identified bleeding source by endoscopy had anticoagulation interrupted for the same median time (3.5 days) compared with patients without an identified source. Anticoagulation was also similarly discontinued indefinitely in patients between the identified and unidentified bleeding source groups. This suggests that even when a bleeding source is not identified, anticoagulation is still managed in a similar manner to those with a clear source. Practically, this suggests anticoagulation resumption is determined by an individualized risk/benefit analysis weighing the magnitude of the bleeding event and the thrombotic risk, and is less driven by bleeding source subtypes. The sample size of our study precludes comparisons by type of bleeding source and intervention. It is logical to think that different bleeding sources may have different recurrence risk and that timely intervention may alter the course. Until more granular data are available, it is reasonable to make clinical decisions regarding anticoagulation timing/resumption based on the risk-benefit balance and shared decision-making in this complex and tenuous patient cohort.

Further studies would be needed to better understand predictive variables for safe resumption of anticoagulation and if certain clinical thresholds (hemodynamics, laboratory values, etc) trigger a clinician's decision to resume anticoagulation independent of source.

Interestingly, although patients did undergo both upper and lower endoscopy for evaluation, only 2 patients underwent capsule study for further evaluation of the bleeding source. This is likely due to the limited availability of this test in the outpatient setting, a frequently infeasible evaluation to coordinate in this high-risk patient population with concurrent thrombosis and bleeding.

Clinical outcomes

Recurrent bleeding events were common in our population, with most being recurrent GIB episodes. Our rates of recurrent bleeding events are similar when compared with previously described rates in the general population (7%-16% in general and 19% at 1 year), despite the need for anticoagulation in our patient population.19,30 Patients without an identifiable bleeding source had a similar recurrent bleeding risk when compared with those with an identified site (Table 3). Similarly, VTE recurrence rates were comparable between the groups, although this occurred very rarely.

In the general population, mortality in upper GIB ranges from 0.3% to 25% depending on patient risk factors31 and for lower GIB, it ranges from 2.4% to 3.9% (26, 32). Our patient population had a higher mortality rate of 50%, which is likely multifactorial. First, our patient cohort includes a high proportion of patients with underlying malignancy (69.7%), which is much higher than the general population. Second, these patients have a concurrent recent VTE in addition to a GIB event, highlighting a profoundly challenging and delicate clinical scenario.

Our finding of similar mortality rates regardless of source identification reflects that even when a bleeding source can be identified, the overall tenuous balance of bleeding and thrombosis is unmitigated by endoscopic evaluation or intervention. Although the cohort is limited, this suggests that this high-risk patient group may not derive as much clinical benefit from endoscopic intervention because of their underlying comorbidities. Further studies would be helpful to understand whether endoscopic intervention, when the lesion is amenable, in this high-risk cohort, could decrease mortality risk in comparison to the general GIB population.

Study limitations

The wide variety of pathologies leading to GIB events and the heterogeneous nature of our study population resulted in a limited number of each specific GIB pathology. This makes generalized conclusions difficult because of the small sample size and has led to our study being underpowered. Furthermore, our cohort is derived from registry patients followed within a single vascular medicine group and is reflective of anticoagulation strategies primarily managed by our practitioners. Given the complexity and nuances of these clinical scenarios, there is likely a selection and survivorship bias, which could impact reported management strategies. Additional studies are needed to help draw conclusions about each unique bleeding phenotype (upper, lower, unknown source, etc) because of the limited patient number contained in our cohort.

An additional limitation of our study is that incomplete data on antiplatelet use, particularly that of over-the-counter medications, precluded assessment of their contribution to bleeding risk. It would be beneficial for future studies to ensure accurate reporting of this variable to determine whether it would alter results.

Lastly, at a quaternary referral center caring for complex patients and a referral center for advanced malignancies, our results may not reflect the experience of community health centers serving a population with a lower incidence of underlying cancer or complex diseases. In addition, as in this population, anticoagulation was prescribed for the indication of VTE; these findings may not be replicable in patients on anticoagulation for other indications (ie, nonvalvular atrial fibrillation, mechanical valves, left-ventricular thrombus, and peripheral arterial disease) because the risk/benefit of anticoagulation may be different for those individual indications. Further studies are necessary to compare these patient profiles and examine the impact of management styles at various institutions.

Conclusions

Optimal management of patients receiving anticoagulation for VTE with acute GIB is unknown despite being a frequent adverse event in this population. In this cohort, endoscopic evaluation is frequent and carries a reasonable diagnostic yield when used. Our study highlights that although endoscopy can help determine the etiology of GIB, this does not currently appear to impact clinical decisions for anticoagulation resumption. In the absence of societal recommendations, it is likely that this decision is driven by other clinical parameters such as hemodynamic stability, need for transfusion, and clinician perception of bleeding versus thrombotic risk. Further studies are needed to explore safety in anticoagulation resumption and clinical thresholds, which may be useful in complex decision-making.

Rates of recurrent bleeding, recurrent VTE, and all-cause mortality were similar regardless of GIB source identification. This is likely reflective of our high-risk patient population, of which many had underlying malignancies. Additional studies are needed to evaluate whether our findings are similar in patients without underlying malignancy, if more aggressive endoscopic intervention could be considered in this population, and the clinical factors that physicians/care teams use to determine the safety of anticoagulation resumption in this high-risk population.
