Feasibility of a new bicycle ergometer with adjustable pedaling configuration for personalized lower limb rehabilitation
Authors: Seonghyun Kang, Jaewook Kim, Yekwang Kim, Juhui Moon, Seunghan Park, Seung-Jong Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Muscle atrophy due to prolonged immobilization leads to severe dysfunction and progression of disease and injury. This highlights the necessity for early rehabilitation, even during the non-ambulatory stages. As manifestations vary among individuals, target-specific rehabilitation is crucial for achieving optimal outcomes. Conventional bicycle ergometers, despite their wide usage in rehabilitation and sports training, fail to adequately recruit the muscles that predominantly operate in the coronal or axial plane. This is thought to be due to the restriction of pedaling trajectory in the sagittal plane. This study introduces a new bicycle ergometer capable of tilting the pedaling plane and adjusting the pedal orientation, in order to alter muscle recruitment patterns and limb alignments. A biomechanical analysis of eight healthy volunteers suggests that our device can elicit dynamic valgus/varus alignment and alter the axial rotations of the lower extremities. An electromyography analysis revealed a significant increase in the activation of specific muscle groups, particularly the abductors and adductors, suggesting promising results for targeted muscle recruitment. We expect this ergometer to provide a new method for target-specific rehabilitation and the treatment of bedridden patients or those with weak muscles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-77369-8.

Introduction

Muscle atrophy due to long-term disuse is a common issue among patients that are physically confined. This condition affects various populations such as those with musculoskeletal injuries, osteoarthritis, neurological diseases, postsurgical recovery, and bedridden individuals1–3. To prevent or reduce the detrimental effects of muscle atrophy, such as residual contracture, dysfunction, and long-term sequelae, it is essential to implement an exercise and rehabilitation regimen as early and regularly as possible4–6. Although various training methods are available for individuals who can support their own body weight, a majority of these methods are not applicable for bedridden subjects. To bridge this gap, several rehabilitation devices such as continuous passive motion devices, tilt tables, walking assistance robots, and bicycle ergometers have been suggested.

Lower extremity muscles which are predominantly responsible for coronal or axial plane movement, such as the gluteus medius (Gmed), hip adductor groups, and peroneus longus (PL) mediate medial-lateral stability and dynamic balance7–9. Medial-lateral stability is crucial for patients in the process of regaining their gait ability10. Hence, for effective recovery, training such muscles is essential. When performing tasks that carry extrinsic loads, body postures, such as limb alignment and relative joint position, play a crucial role in muscle recruitment patterns, movement, and joint pressure distribution11–15. Subtle changes in posture can alter muscle activation and limb movements16–18. For instance, during the squat, the abduction of the foot coupled with a wide stance increases the medial knee joint contact force and promotes the activation of gluteus maximus11,16. Therefore, adjusting the subject’s specific postures and joint alignments can help target the recruitment of specific muscles.

However, most conventional devices widely used by non-ambulatory patients restrict the foot trajectory exclusively to the sagittal plane. This results training of the muscles such as the gluteus maximus, quadriceps, and hamstrings, which are predominately responsible for the sagittal movements19–21. Thus, muscles that elicit coronal or axial plane motions remain undertrained. This suggests a need to address this disparity and ensure the comprehensive recruitment of the muscles associated with both sagittal and coronal plane motions before entering the full weight-bearing exercise stage.

Bicycle ergometers have been used for rehabilitation for over a century. A major advantage of the ergometer is that it relieves the need for the lower extremities to support one’s full body weight, allowing the subject to control the interaction force between the pedal and foot without the risk of falling22,23. Moreover, ergometers are known to promote muscle strength and cardiovascular health19,24,25. A few prior investigations have focused on adjusting the coronal plane alignment during pedaling tasks12,15,26,27. These studies have shown that induced dynamic varus/valgus alignment via tilted pedals can alter the adduction/abduction and axial moments applied to the knee joint. It was also shown that muscle recruitment patterns could be altered, such as the activation ratio between the vastus medialis (VM) and vastus lateralis (VL), and activation of the tensor fascia latae (TFL)12,15,26. Furthermore, a study which allowed lateral pedal translation, showed improved power output and additional recruitment of the hip abductors27. However, even with these variations, the devices were still fundamentally confined by the sagittal pedaling plane. Thus, the primary mechanism for generating power remains largely unchanged and the limb movement in the coronal and axial planes is not significant.

Here, we introduce a novel tilted-plane ergometer28 for target-specific rehabilitation that emphasizes the recruitment of coronal/axial plane muscles and controls the direction of the joint reaction forces (Fig. 1). This device can tilt the pedaling plane out of the sagittal plane and adjust the orientation of the pedal relative to the crank axis. This study serves as a proof-of-concept investigation using biomechanical analysis. It aims to determine whether different configurations of the device’s angular parameters can effectively alter the relative alignment of joints, elicit specific movements, and consequently, recruit diverse muscle groups. We hypothesize that tilting the pedaling plane outward will recruit the lateral muscle groups during the push phase, while an inwardly tilted pedaling plane will predominantly recruit the medial muscle groups. Furthermore, we propose that modifying the device parameters can prompt the subjects’ lower limbs to adopt specific alignments.

Materials and methods

Tilted-plane ergometer

A tilted-plane ergometer (Fig. 1a) was designed to address the needs of target-specific rehabilitation. Unlike conventional bicycle ergometers with a fixed pedaling trajectory in the sagittal plane, the proposed ergometer can tilt the pedaling plane about the yaw axis to facilitate adduction and abduction motions (Fig. 1c). The pedals can also be tilted and rotated to induce foot eversion/inversion and internal/external rotation. The relative positions of the hip, knee, and ankle joints can be altered by adjusting the pedaling trajectory and pedal orientation which facilitates the usage of different forces and muscles.

The device comprises of four main components, an ergometer frame, a plane tilting joint, a crank, and an adjustable pedal (Fig. 1b). To create a prototype, we modified a recumbent ergometer with a magnetic flywheel (Bike 906R Egojin Ltd., South Korea). It provides 12 load levels and displays the pedaling speed in real time. We incorporated a universal joint between the ergometer frame and crank such that the pedaling plane could be tilted in the XY plane. The tilt angle of pedaling plane (ψplane), which is the angle between the pedaling plane and the sagittal (XZ) plane, can be adjusted from − 30° to 30° with 5° increments using a ball lock pin (Fig. 1c). In this paper, the terms “abduction” and “adduction” tilt refer to the positive and negative value of ψplane, respectively. The distance between the two pedals was approximately 400 mm in the neutral state, and the length of the crank was 180 mm. The pedals were attached to the crank via a ball joint and an offset bracket, allowing the pedal to rotate in all three directions (Fig. 1d). The pedal orientations were manually adjusted and fixed using locking bolts. The roll angle of the tilted pedal was defined as ϕpedal, where a positive value represents “inversion” and negative value represents “eversion” tilt. Lastly, the yaw angle of the pedal was defined as ψpedal, where a positive value represents “external” and a negative value represents “internal” rotation. These terms for the device’s tilting angles were named based on respective motion of the participants.

Participants

Eight healthy volunteers (age: 30.1 ± 5.5 years, height: 1.76 ± 0.07 m, and weight: 70.6 ± 9.9 kg) with no prior history of musculoskeletal injury (within 6 months) or major surgery of lower extremities participated in this study. All participants provided written informed consents, and the research ethics for the human experiments were ensured by conducting the sessions in accordance with the contents approved by the institutional review board of the Korea University (IRB No.: 2022-0366-01, Approval date: Nov. 10, 2022). All experiments were carried out in accordance with the approved guidelines and with the Declaration of Helsinki. An orthopedic surgeon measured the participants’ hip range of motion (ROM), leg length, and global alignment prior to the experiments (Table 1). The leg length was defined as the actual anatomical length, measured as the distance between the anterior superior iliac spine and the medial malleolus. This measurement was intended to assess whether participants could perform the experiment without any restriction of movement or pain. Although there was a considerable variation in the leg lengths and hip ROMs, no severe physical limitation that could potentially affect the performance of each task was observed.

Experimental procedures

To investigate whether the ergometer configurations could alter the pedaling biomechanics, we employed four different pedaling conditions: neutral (NEU), pushing via abduction with pedal eversion and internal rotation (ABE), pushing via abduction with pedal inversion and external rotation (ABI), and pushing via adduction with pedal inversion (ADI) (Fig. 2, Supplementary Materials Pedaling video 1–4). We excluded the combination of rotations with the ankle inversion orientation for the adduction condition, as these rotations were not expected to facilitate meaningful adjustments in limb alignment. Additionally, the everted orientation was excluded, as it led to physical discomfort. The NEU condition, where both pedaling planes are parallel and identical to those of conventional ergometers, served as the basis for evaluating the biomechanical characteristics. For the abduction conditions (ABE and ABI), we need to take in consideration of the subject’s hip joint ROM because the maximum adduction of the hip occurs when the pedal is closest to the subject’s body. An excessively large abduction tilt of the pedaling plane (ψplane > 0°) may result in compensatory motions, such as excessive ankle eversion, hip rotation, or elevation of the hip joint from the seat. Considering that the mean value of the maximal hip joint adduction angles of the participants was around 30° (Table 1), we set the positive limit of ψplane to 30°. For the adduction condition ADI, the limit of ψplane along the adduction direction (negative value) was set at -15° such that the subjects’ legs do not touch the ergometer when extending their knees.

Furthermore, we established two sets of pedal orientations to facilitate valgus and varus postures. In the ABE condition both ψpedal and ϕpedal was set to -5°, inducing internal rotation and eversion. Conversely, in the ABI condition ψpedal was set to 30° and ϕpedal to 15°, promoting external rotation and inversion. These pedal orientation angles were selected in reference to studies examining the relationship between the toe-out angle and muscle recruitment patterns during push exercises, such as bicycle pedaling, squats, and heel raises11,16,17,29.

Prior to the experiments, the distance from the pedal to the subject was set by sliding the seat such that the knee flexion angle was approximately 30° when the pedal was the farthest from the subject’s body. The pedaling intensity, in this paper, refers to an arbitrary resistance level of the magnetic flywheel within the generic Egojin ergometer. The intensity was set to level 8, for both male and female participants, which was determined by developer feedback after riding the ergometer before the commencement of the experiments with recruited subjects. The subjects were instructed to maintain a pedaling speed in the range of 15–17 km/h as consistently as possible. Ample time was provided for the participants to get acclimated to each condition.

To analyze the biomechanical effects caused by the different ergometer configurations, data was collected using an RGB-D camera (Microsoft Kinect, Microsoft Inc., USA), inertia measurement unit (IMU) sensors (Trigno wireless system, Delsys Inc., USA), and surface electromyography (EMG) sensors (Trigno wireless system, Delsys Inc., USA). Kinect, which is composed of an RGB camera in addition to an infra-red projector and camera in order obtain 3-dimensional (3D) coordinates of the body segments, was positioned on the ceiling to capture the top-down view of the lower limb kinematics, as shown in Fig. 3a. The IMU and EMG sensors, sampling rate of 100 Hz and 1777 Hz, respectively, were placed on the right lower extremity (Fig. 3b). The IMU sensors were attached to the anterior aspect of the trunk, thigh, shin, dorsum of the foot, and outer surface of the crank, and 12 EMG sensors were placed on the Gmed, TFL, adductor magnus (AM), rectus femoris (RF), VL, VM, lateral head of the biceps femoris (BF), semimembranosus (SM), PL, tibialis anterior (TA), and gastrocnemius laterals/medialis (GL/GM), based on the SENIAM protocol30. After calibration and synchronization of the sensors, 25 pedaling cycles were performed for each condition, with a 2-minute rest period between each trial to minimize fatigue. The order in which each condition was performed was randomized for each participant.

Signal processing

Skeletal tracking was performed using a Kinect RGB-D camera, and its embedded software which enabled the acquisition of the 3D coordinates at a sampling rate of 30 Hz. After acquiring the raw coordinate data, we projected the coordinates onto the XY plane, which corresponds to the coronal plane from the perspective of the lower extremities. Then, we computed the hip-knee coronal angle (θH-Kcor) by measuring the angle between the pelvic line (a virtual line that connects the bilateral hip joints) and thigh axis (which connects the ipsilateral hip and knee joint). We then estimated the hip-ankle coronal angle (θH-Acor) by measuring the angle between the pelvic line and global axis (which connects the ipsilateral hip and ankle joint) (Fig. 3a). Given our particular interest in the relative varus and valgus alignments of the joints, which result in abduction and adduction moments at the knee joint, we captured the dynamic limb alignment angle as projected in the coronal plane (Fig. 3a and 4b). The actual varus/valgus alignment of the knee joint in a standing or supine position (with the limb in full extension and neutral rotation) typically remains unchanged unless there is an issue with the stabilizing ligaments or bony structures. However, this alignment can be altered when combined with hip and knee joint motions during dynamic tasks, which may hold significant clinical relevance. Previously, dynamic knee valgus during single-leg weight-bearing tasks has been measured using coronal plane projection angles, such as the dynamic valgus index31 and Q angle32. In this study, we introduced a “projected coronal angle,” estimated by measuring the difference between θH-Kcor and θH-Acor, obtained by projecting the limbs onto the coronal plane. A negative value indicates a valgus alignment, while a positive value indicates a varus alignment (Fig. 4c). To assess the rotational angles of the femur and tibia, which were poorly captured in the marker-less motion capture system, we utilized the 3D orientation data obtained from the IMU sensors. The sagittal and axial angles of the femur and tibia were defined based on the pitch and yaw values of the IMU sensors attached to the anterior aspects of the thigh and shin, respectively. The knee sagittal angle, which represents the flexion and extension angles of the knee joint, was calculated from the difference between the femoral and tibial sagittal angles (Fig. 4a).

The skeletal tracking, IMU, and EMG data were initially synchronized and then divided into cycles (Fig. 3c). The initiation of each phase was defined when the knee was closest to the body which aligns with the start of the push phase. The data was further normalized against the cycle time, such that the x-axis could be represented as percentages of a pedaling cycle. Mean values and either 95% confidence interval (C.I.) or standard deviation (S.D.) were calculated to present the EMG and kinematic data. These data are represented by a line and shaded area, allowing further comparisons between the NEU, ABE, ABI, and ADI conditions.

Group analysis

While kinematic data can be directly compared between subjects, EMG signals should be normalized within each individual for statistical group analysis. This study primarily focuses on identifying the increase or decrease in EMG amplitude relative to the baseline NEU condition. Therefore, we normalized the EMG WL values by calculating the ratio of the mean EMG WL value for each condition against that of the NEU condition.

The normality of each dataset was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk tests. Depending on the normality, paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were performed to compare each condition with the NEU condition. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL).

Results

Kinematic data

Prior to analyzing the muscle recruitment patterns, we evaluated whether the ABE, ABI, and ADI conditions induced changes in the femoral and tibial rotation angles and projected coronal angle compared to that of the NEU condition (Fig. 4). We calculated the mean values of the angular data from all eight subjects and represented them as means ± S.D. The third and fourth rows of Fig. 4c show that the femur and knee angles in the sagittal plane had similar patterns and ranges under the four conditions. Meanwhile, in the first, second and fifth rows of Fig. 4c, changes were observed due to the ergometer conditions. In the ABE condition, we observed internal rotation of both the femur and tibia during the early push phase (defined as 0–50% of the pedaling cycle). This orientation transitioned back to a neutral position during the late push and pull phases (defined as the second half of the pedaling cycle). Furthermore, the lower limb displayed valgus alignment during the entire cycle of the ABE condition, whereas it remained almost neutral during the NEU condition (Fig. 4c). In the ABI condition, the varus alignment and externally rotated femur and tibia changes to a neutral position during the push phase. This result is different from that of the ABE condition, which shows that pedal orientation can influence the motion of the entire lower limb. Finally, the ADI condition resulted in a pattern similar to that of the NEU condition, in which both the femur and tibia initiated pedaling by external rotation. Then the lower limb internally rotates until halfway through the pull phase and then externally rotates back to the neutral position. Notably, the projected coronal angle demonstrate that the lower limb remained in a neutral state, similar to that in the NEU condition (Fig. 4c).

Statistical analysis, as presented in Supplementary Table S1, shows that the mean femur and tibial axial rotation, as well as projected coronal angle, were significantly decreased in the ABE condition and increased in the ABI condition. Additionally, in the ADI condition, the knee joint was more flexed throughout the entire cycle compared to the NEU condition.

EMG data

In addition, we analyzed the EMG WL time traces to investigate how the muscle recruitment patterns changed according to the pedal configuration. Figure 5 shows the mean values and 95% C.I. of the EMG WL data measured from the 12 lower extremity muscles while Subject 1 pedaled 25 times under each pedaling condition. This allowed us to visually compare the ABE (red), ABI (green), and ADI (blue) conditions against the NEU (gray) condition. In the ABE configuration, we observed increases in the Gmed and TFL EMG WL, characterized by a gradual rise from the middle-push phase and high peak during the middle-pull phase. Additionally, the PL showed consistently high activation throughout the cycle, whereas the TA showed minimal activation. In the ABI configuration, the activation patterns of the muscles, except for the TA and GM, were very similar to those in the NEU condition. The Gmed is well activated under the ABE configuration, but the ABI configuration does not effectively recruit hip abductors, despite providing a similar outward push. This is because the externally rotated and abducted femur in the ABI condition lowered the tension of the Gmed, which acts as a hip external rotator and abductor11,29. Finally, under the ADI condition, an increase in EMG WL was observed for AM, BF, SM, and GM during the push phase, whereas a decrease was observed for the knee extensors, including the VL, VM, and RF. This suggests that the primary muscles generating power for pedal rotation shifted from the knee extensors to the hip adductors and hamstrings (BF and SM).

Group analysis

A qualitative analysis of the results of Subject 1 showed distinct variations in the muscle recruitment patterns depending on the ergometer configuration. To ensure consistency across the participant group, these changes should be identified quantitatively. Thus, we calculated the mean EMG WL values throughout the entire pedaling cycle and obtained the ratio between each specific condition and the baseline (NEU condition) for every subject, as summarized in Table 2. These ratios are presented as box plots in Fig. 6, where red, green, and blue boxes indicate the ABE, ABI, and ADI conditions, respectively. For each box plot, the distribution of the ratios for each ergometer condition is displayed in terms of the median, 25th percentile, and 75th percentile. The group-level results demonstrate that the Gmed and TFL exhibited substantial increase exclusively in the ABE condition, which is consistent with our observations from Subject 1. Notably, decreased activation levels were observed for the monoarticular knee joint extensor muscles (VL and VM), in the ABE and ADI condition. The activation of the AM, PL, and SM primarily increased under the ADI condition. To further investigate the specific differences in the recruitment patterns during the push and pull phases, separate ratios were calculated for each phase (Supplementary Table S2). In the ABE condition, increased activation of the TFL was observed exclusively during the pull phase, whereas the Gmed exhibited increased activation in both phases. In the ADI condition, significant increases in EMG activity were observed in the AM, SM, PL, GM, and GL muscles during both push and pull phases, whereas the TA muscle showed increased activation only during the push phase.

Discussion

When performing tasks that deliver extrinsic loads to the human body, limb alignment significantly influences biomechanical factors such as muscle recruitment patterns and joint pressure distributions. Even for similar tasks, subtle changes in posture can alter muscle activity and joint moment. Therefore, proper motion generation with the correct posture is necessary to achieve desired rehabilitation effect. Stationary bicycles are commonly employed in daily fitness routines and rehabilitation, which involve repetitive motions under extrinsic loads. By modifying the trajectory and posture during pedaling, one can potentially optimize the activation of subject-specific muscles and modulate joint moments, thereby alleviating joint pain and preventing potential injury. Although previous studies implemented minor alterations in the coronal alignments using pedal tilting12,15,26 and variations in the distance between pedals27,33, there have been no attempts to substantially modify the trajectory that deviates from the traditional pedaling plane. Therefore, we propose a novel tilted-plane ergometer capable of tilting the pedaling plane and adjusting the pedal orientations.

In this study, a biomechanical analysis was performed to establish whether different pedaling plane and pedal tilt conditions lead to changes in dynamic alignment and muscle recruitment patterns prior to clinical studies. We compared four different ergonomic conditions, NEU, ABE, ABI, and ADI, for eight participants, each of whom completed 25 cycles under each condition while maintaining constant speed. Skeletal tracking was performed using Kinect motion capture system. The 3D orientation of the femur and tibia was acquired via IMU sensors, and the muscle recruitment patterns were obtained via EMG sensors. The results suggest that varying the planes of motion and pedal orientations altered dynamic varus/valgus alignment, external/internal rotation of the femur and tibia, and muscle recruitment during pedaling.

The ABE condition, which encourages outward pushing with an everted pedal orientation, caused the femur to adduct and internally rotate, leading to valgus alignment during the push phase. A transition towards abduction and external rotation is observed during the pull phase (Fig. 4). This induced posture is expected to increase the abduction and external axial moment at the knee joint and shorten the moment arm of the hip abductors with stretched muscle fibers, resulting in marked increases in the Gmed and TFL activity (Fig. 5). The ABI condition is peculiar in that a slight change in pedal orientation alters the overall movement pattern. The femur and tibia were externally rotated, and the subject was in a varus state throughout the entire pedaling cycle, except during full extension when the lower limb was in the neutral state. Lastly, in the ADI condition, the lower limb exhibited varus state, external rotation, and inward pushing during the push phase and valgus state, internal rotation, and outward pulling during the pull phase. This altered motion, which is difficult to observe in conventional rehabilitation movements, significantly elicits increased activation of the AM and SM, which is typically challenging to train, especially for non-ambulatory patients.

We would like to note that our study is not without limitations. First, in order to analyze changes in EMG levels due to different ergometer conditions, we used the ratio of mean EMG WL values of ABE, ABI, and ADI conditions against that of the NEU condition. This method may elicit misleading results especially when the EMG WL levels for the NEU condition are low34. For example, if a muscle during the NEU condition is non-active, which would result in a very low EMG WL level, a high increase in ratio would be observed with a slight increase in EMG WL levels even though there is only a small increase in maximum voluntary contraction percentage. Thus, when interpreting our results, caution is warranted especially for high EMG WL ratio increases. Second, we only recruited healthy young adults, even though the target population for our tilted-plane ergometer are patients that cannot perform full body weight support exercises but need to train and strengthen coronal plane muscles. Furthermore, we postulate that patients, that need or must avoid certain joint moments, such as knee​ ​osteoarthritis,​ ​hip​ ​osteoarthritis and ​chronic​ ​back​ ​pain could benefit from our ergometer. However, we must also consider the fact that these patients might lack the range of motion to actually perform tilted-plane pedaling and possibly be a safety risk. Thus, warranting future clinical studies with the actual target population. Third, we only acquired data from 25 cycles of pedaling with only one resistance and speed condition. It maybe possible that longer durations of training may elicit compensation patterns or even worse joint pain. Because we only tested one speed and resistance condition, whether the same increased muscles recruitment patterns will be observed even if the ergometer is configured to a smaller angle and whether ergometer intensity and speed will affect the muscle recruitment patterns are currently unknown. Fourth, because of our small sample size we were not able to conduct thorough statistical analysis such as Bonferroni correction. While the changes in muscle activation patterns and lower-limb kinematics were consistent throughout group, the results must be accepted with caution. Even though we intended this current investigation to serve as a proof-of-concept, prior to studies with actual patients, it seems that large scale clinical trials are necessary to come to a concrete conclusion.

While current bicycle training is mostly confined to the sagittal plane muscles, we believe that this in some specific cases one might benefit from deliberately inducing either varus or valgus alignments. This could strengthen specific muscles or avoid movement that can elicit joint pain. With a comprehensive understanding of how the muscle activation patterns and kinematics changes with simple configuration adjustments of an ergometer paired with physiological knowledge of the disorder, physicians and physiotherapists would be allowed to prescribe patient specific training. This targeted approach can potentially stimulate a specific muscle group, protect certain anatomical structures, and mitigate pain during repetitive tasks. For instance, patients with medial osteoarthritis or knee chondral injury could benefit from the ABE condition because valgus orientation alleviates the compression force on the medial side of the joint35. Furthermore, the increased activation of the primary hip abductors during the ABE condition could be beneficial in patients with conditions such as hip osteoarthritis, chronic lower back pain, patellofemoral pain syndrome, and iliotibial band friction syndrome36–38. Conversely, patients at the risk exacerbating one’s injury due to valgus alignment, such as recurrent patellar subluxation or knee joint medial collateral ligament injury39–41, the ABI or ADI condition could be a suitable option. In the ADI condition, the power demand for knee extensors shifts to the hamstrings and below-knee muscles, which could be beneficial for patients with anterior collateral ligament injury, where hamstrings strengthening is crucial for functional recovery and treatment outcomes42.

We believe that, while the results form our investigation need further bolstering via further studies, our ergometer can be easily utilized in clinical settings such as bedsides, rehabilitation centers, and sports treatment centers to assist with patient specific ergometer training. As previously noted, our proof-of-concept study was performed with a small group that lacks diversity, thus, we are currently in preparation for a long-term and large scale clinical study involving sedentary elderly individuals and patients with various dysfunctions.
