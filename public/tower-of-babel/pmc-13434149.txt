External validity of deep learning solution for spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage detection on head CT scans
Authors: Juuso Takala, Olivier Zanier, Valérie Blom, Vincens Kälin, Lara Maria Höbner, Chantal Marie Klesel, Isabel C Hostettler, Victor E Staartjes, Miikka Korja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe externally validated the performance of deep learning (DL) solution for detection of spontaneous intracerebral (ICH), intraventricular (IVH) and subarachnoid hemorrhages (SAH) on non-contrast enhanced head CT scans (NCCTs). We analyzed 901 NCCTs collected retrospectively from two Swiss hospitals: University Hospital Zürich (USZ) and HOCH Health Ostschweiz, Kantonsspital St. Gallen (KSSG). Of these 901 NCCTs, 81 had spontaneous ICH, IVH or SAH. The diagnostic accuracy was evaluated using the radiologist’s reports as the reference standard. The DL solution correctly identified 74 out of the 81 intracranial hemorrhages (sensitivity 91.4%). In the USZ cohort, the sensitivity was 88.5% and the specificity was 89.7%. Using the original KSSG-NCCTs, the DL solution had a sensitivity of 100.0% and a specificity of 47.5%. After adjusting the KSSG-NCCTs pixel matrix to a standardized 512 × 512 resolution the KSSG cohort sensitivity remained at 100.0%, and the specificity increased to 74.0%. The overall specificity was 78.4% when using the original imaging data. The overall specificity increased to 85.5% when using the padded KSSG-NCCTs along with original imaging data from USZ. Particularly the specificity varied substantially depending on imaging acquisition parameters. Introduction

Spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage is a critical neurological emergency that necessitates prompt and accurate diagnosis as well as rapidly initiated treatment1,2. Non-contrast head CT scan (NCCT) is the standard imaging modality used in the diagnosis of intracranial hemorrhages due to its widespread availability, fast execution time and high sensitivity3–5. Despite this, missed diagnoses still occur in real-life clinical workflow due to human errors6–8. Various deep learning (DL) systems have been developed to aid in medical imaging study interpretation, and even commercially available solutions exist9,10. Although the use of DL algorithms in medical imaging analysis is a widely studied topic, very few studies have reported the performance of developed DL algorithms in external settings11,12. Due to overfitting, DL algorithms may perform well on datasets that resemble the training dataset but poorly on external datasets originating from different countries and hospitals13,14. Thus, DL algorithm’s performance cannot be reliably assessed without a non-optimized external validation15,16.

This external validation study evaluated previously developed and internally validated a DL solution for detecting acute spontaneous intracerebral (ICH), intraventricular (IVH) and subarachnoid hemorrhages (SAH) on head NCCTs17,18. The internal validation study included a real-world dataset of 7797 consecutive and emergent head NCCTs imaged in 10 different hospitals in 3 months in Finland18. The DL solution has been designed to be used particularly for NCCTs imaged within 12 h of symptom onset, since the sensitivity of CT scans is close to 100% up to 12 h but declines with increasing imaging delays. In the internal validation, the sensitivity was 100% for NCCTs imaged within 12 h18. Overall, when NCCTs imaged even days after the bleeding (symptom onset) were included in the analyses, the sensitivity and specificity were 89.8% and 89.5%, respectively.

To assess the DL solution’s performance more reliably, we conducted an external validation study using NCCTs from two international hospitals with varying imaging acquisition parameters. Our hypothesis was that the solution’s performance does not differ significantly between external and internal datasets, despite somewhat varying imaging acquisition parameters used in different hospitals.

Materials and methods

Study approval and ethical considerations

The study was approved by the local institutional review boards (IRB) of both University Hospital Zurich (USZ) and HOCH Health Ostschweiz, Kantonsspital St. Gallen (KSSG). Due to the retrospective study design, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB in both hospitals. All experiments were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki.

Deep learning solution

The DL solution performs semantic segmentation by predicting pixel-wise probabilities of intracranial hemorrhage. The development of this DL solution has been previously described in detail18. In short, the DL solution is based on a convolutional neural network ensemble model. The DL solution comprises four base U-Nets, each trained for specific bleed type detection (one U-Net for ICH, one for IVH, and two for SAH) and a meta-model, which integrates the outputs from the base models producing the final detection result. After generating predictions from the base U-Net models and the meta-model, the system applies post-processing techniques to improve the detection accuracy. These steps include majority voting based on the base U-Nets predictions and test-time augmentation, and followingly positive prediction clusters smaller than 125 pixels are filtered out. The code used for developing, training and conducting the inference tasks for internal validation are available online (https://github.com/Juusotak/Intracranial_hemorrhage_metalearning). In the external validation, we run the DL solution locally with the full post-processing.

External datasets

The imaging data of both international hospitals was collected retrospectively. Head NCCTs with non-traumatic ICH, IVH and SAH were included as positive cases. The ground truth for the presence or absence of intracranial hemorrhage in NCCT was based on radiology reports. At USZ, the radiology reports were based on assessments by two neuroradiologists. At KSSG, the reports were initially generated by an on-call radiologist and subsequently reviewed and confirmed by a consultant radiologist. All head NCCTs without reported intracranial hemorrhages were included as negative cases. Only NCCTs of adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) were included. NCCTs of both hospitals were extracted in DICOM format from the local picture archiving and communication systems (PACS), and later saved and used in a fully anonymized NIfTI file format. We collected patient demographic data (age and sex). Confidential or personally identifiable information was neither recorded nor analyzed. Figure 1 presents an overall summary of the used datasets. We used a python script for calculating case-level performance metrics, which were based on the ground truth annotations.

Diagram of Dataset Collection. Validation data used in this study was gathered from two external centers. Spontaneous intracerebral, intraventricular and subarachnoid hemorrhages were included as positive cases. Both centers excluded traumatic hemorrhages and follow up images.

Statistical methods

The performance was measured using sensitivity, specificity, false positive rate, positive and negative predictive values and accuracy. Performance metrics were calculated using Python (version 3.9.6) and NumPy (version 2.0.2). We used the Wilson score method19, implemented with statsmodels Python package (version 0.14.5), for computing the 95% confidence intervals (CIs). JT was responsible for statistical analyses.

USZ cohort

The USZ-NCCTs were imaged between January 5, 2012, and September 18, 2020, using nine different CT scanners from Siemens, Siemens Healthineers and GE Medical Systems (Supplement 1, Supplementary Table 1). Negative USZ-NCCTs were sampled from a pool of scans without intracranial hemorrhages, whereas USZ-NCCTs showing hemorrhage constituted a consecutive series.

The mean slice thickness of reconstructed axial head CT scans was 0.76 mm (Standard Deviation [SD]: ± 0.04 mm). NCCTs in the USZ cohort had pixel matrix size of 512 × 512 (Supplement 1, Supplementary Table 1). The DL solution inference tasks and performance analyses were conducted on a workstation running Linux Ubuntu 22.04 operating system equipped with AMD Ryzen Threadripper PRO 5955WX (Advanced Micro Devices, 2485 Augustine Drive Santa Clara, California 95054 United States) central processing unit (CPU) and 256 GB of random-access memory (RAM). USZ-NCCTs with spontaneous intracranial hemorrhages were annotated by one study author (VB; Bachelor of Medicine with 2 years of experience in neuroimaging research) using 3D slicer20. The annotations were subsequently reviewed and corrected when needed by another study author from the same institution (LH; neurosurgeon in-training with 4 years of experience).

KSSG cohort

The KSSG-NCCTs were imaged between July 1 and July 31, 2023, using a single CT scanner model from Siemens Healthineers (Supplement 1, Supplementary Table 1). The KSSG-NCCTs constituted a consecutive series, with NCCTs showing traumatic hemorrhage excluded.

The mean reconstructed axial slice thickness in this cohort was 4.00 mm (SD: ± 0.00 mm). The KSSG-NCCTs had varying pixel matrix size, which ranged from 363 to 561 pixels in x-axis and from 508 to 636 pixels in y-axis. The KSSG-NCCTs included one NCCT with a pixel matrix size greater than 512 pixels along the x-axis, and 21 NCCTs with a pixel matrix size greater than 512 pixels along the y-axis. The median pixel matrix size along the x-axis was 448 pixels (interquartile range [IQR]: 429–465 pixels) and 512 pixels in y-axis (IQR: 512–512).

The DL solution inference tasks and performance analyses were run on an Apple MacBook Pro (Apple, One Apple Park Way, Cupertino, CA 95014, United States) computer (manufactured in November 2023), equipped with an Apple M3 Pro chip and 36 GB of RAM. KSSG-NCCTs with spontaneous intracranial hemorrhages were annotated by one study author (VK; board certified neurosurgeon with 6 years of experience) using ITK Snap21. The annotations were subsequently reviewed and corrected if needed by a senior study author (IH; consultant neurosurgeon with 14 years of experience). We first analyzed the KSSG-NCCTs using the original imaging data. Subsequently, NCCTs with an image matrix size smaller than 512 pixels in either x- or y-axis were zero-padded to standardize the pixel matrix size to 512 × 512.

Results

USZ cohort

The USZ consisted of 662 NCCTs of individual patients, of which 326 (49.2%) were women and 336 (50.8%) men (Supplement 1, Supplementary Table 2). The mean age of patients was 61.8 years (SD: ± 17.1 years) (Supplement 1, Supplementary Table 2). In USZ cohort, 61 NCCTs (9.2%) were positive and 601 (90.8%) were negative for spontaneous intracranial hemorrhages. The median run time for analyzing all slices of one NCCT scan (median: 248 slices; range: 111–480 slices) was 65.0 s (range: 28.9–147.1 s). In the USZ cohort, 95% of the scans were processed within 88.7 s.

At the case level, the DL solution correctly identified 54 of the 61 NCCTs with spontaneous intracranial hemorrhages (sensitivity: 88.5%, 95% CI: 78.2–94.3%) (Table 1). All 7 false negative cases were subsequently reviewed, and they included 6 SAHs and one ICH. Two of the SAH cases were imaged within 12 h of symptom onset and the remaining four SAHs later than 24 h of symptom onset. The ICH case was imaged between 12 and 24 h of symptom onset. In terms of false positives, the DL solution incorrectly identified hemorrhage in 62 (10.3%) of the 601 NCCTs negative for spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage (specificity 89.7%, 95% CI: 87.0–91.9%) (Table 1).

KSSG cohort

The KSSG cohort consisted of 239 NCCTs of individual patients, of which 106 (44.4%) were women and 133 (55.6%) were men (Supplement 1, Supplementary Table 2). The mean age of patients was 67.1 years (SD: ± 17.5 years) (Supplement 1, Supplementary Table 2). In the KSSG cohort, 20 NCCTs (8.4%) were positive and 219 (91.6%) were negative for spontaneous intracranial hemorrhages. The median run time for analyzing all slices in one NCCT scan (median: 36 slices; range: 30–47 slices) was 4.2 s (range: 2.9–13.6 s). In the KSSG cohort, 95% of scans were processed within 7.9 s.

The DL solution correctly identified hemorrhage in all 20 NCCTs with spontaneous intracranial hemorrhages (sensitivity: 100%; 95% CI: 83.9–100.0%) (Table 1). However, when applied to the original imaging data from KSSG, the DL solution incorrectly identified hemorrhage in 115 of the 219 NCCTs that were negative for spontaneous intracranial hemorrhages (specificity: 47.5%; 95% CI: 41.0–54.1%), and the false positive rate was 52.5% (95% CI: 45.9–59.0%) (Table 1). Using the zero-padded KSSG-NCCTs, the solution still correctly identified hemorrhage in all 20 scans with spontaneous intracranial hemorrhages (sensitivity: 100%; 95% CI: 83.9–100.0%) (Table 2). With zero-padded KSSG-NCCTs, the DL solution incorrectly identified hemorrhage in 57 of the 219 NCCTs that were negative for spontaneous intracranial hemorrhages, resulting in a specificity of 74.0% (95% CI: 67.8–79.3%). The false positive rate was 26.0% (95% CI: 20.7–32.2%) (Table 2) with zero-padded KSSG-NCCTs.

Original imaging data

Using the original imaging data from USZ and KSSG, the DL solution correctly identified hemorrhage in 74 of 81 NCCTs with spontaneous intracranial hemorrhages, resulting in an overall sensitivity of 91.4% (95% CI: 83.2–95.8%). The solution incorrectly identified hemorrhage in 177 of 820 NCCTs that were negative for hemorrhage, resulting in an overall specificity of 78.4% (95% CI: 75.5–81.1%) and a false positive rate of 21.6% (95% CI: 18.9–24.5%).

Zero-padded imaging data

Using the zero-padded KSSG-NCCTs and original data from USZ, the DL solution correctly identified hemorrhage in 74 of 81 NCCTs with spontaneous intracranial hemorrhages, resulting in a sensitivity of 91.4% (95% CI: 83.2–95.8%). The solution incorrectly identified hemorrhage in 119 of 820 NCCTs with spontaneous intracranial hemorrhages, resulting in an overall specificity of 85.5% (95% CI: 82.9–87.7%) and a false positive rate of 14.5% (95% CI: 12.3–17.1%). All false-positive cases in the KSSG cohort were reviewed, confirming that none represented hemorrhages missed in the radiology reports.

Discussion

The DL solution still achieved a relatively high sensitivity of 91.4% and a moderate specificity of 78.4% when tested with non-optimized imaging data from two external centers, each with acquisition parameters differing from those used in the solution’s development and internal validation. Interestingly, despite varying slice thicknesses and pixel matrix sizes, sensitivity remained (from 88.5% to 100%) similar to the internal validation results18. In the internal validation, the solution had 100% sensitivity for spontaneous intracranial hemorrhages imaged within 12 h of symptom onset, with an overall sensitivity of 89.8% for all imaging time windows. On the contrary, CT image acquisition parameters had a dramatic effect on specificity, not so much on sensitivity. In more detail, when the pixel matrix size differed from the standard of 512 × 512 pixels, the false positive rate increased to over 50%. A smaller, bigger or asymmetric pixel matrix size introduces distortion of normal anatomical structures during the preprocessing phase. When smaller pixel matrix sizes were zero-padded, i.e. resized to the standard matrix size of 512 × 512, the specificity in the KSSG cohort increased from 47.5% to 74.0% and the overall specificity increased from 78.4% to 85.5%. With regard to the slice thickness, the other external dataset had a mean slice thickness of 0.76 mm, i.e. the axial raw slices were not fused and reconstructed using the standard multi-planar reformat filter, which typically emphasizes soft tissues and reduces the granular noise of NCCT. In other words, thin slice thickness increases the image noise22. Despite the raw and granular image data, the DL solution achieved relatively high sensitivity (88.5%) and specificity (89.7%) in the USZ cohort. The development and internal validation datasets comprised fused and reconstructed NCCTs with a slice thickness of 3.00 mm and a pixel matrix size of 512 × 512. Contrary to our hypothesis, NCCT acquisition parameters, particularly the pixel matrix size, do affect the DL model’s false positive rate.

In the internal validation, the DL solution showed 100% sensitivity for the acute-phase (imaged within 12 h) hemorrhage detection18. However, in USZ cohort, two acute-phase SAHs were missed, and therefore the 100% sensitivity for acute-phase hemorrhage detection could not be extended to this cohort. Given the identical pixel matrix size of 512 × 512 used in the USZ cohort, the sensitivity of the DL solution (trained with reconstructed 3.0 mm axial reformats) appears to decrease when applied to raw (thin slices) axial imaging data. With regard to the KSSG cohort, the high false positive rate would very likely in clinical setting cause alarm fatigue, reduce end-user satisfaction, and potentially lead to overtreatment of false positive findings. The observed false positive rates — 52.5% with the original imaging data and 26.0% with the padded imaging data — would lead to false identification of hemorrhage in approximately 1 out of every 2 negative NCCTs and 1 out of every 4 negative NCCTs, respectively.

Only a minority of the developed DL solutions for intracranial hemorrhage detection have undergone external validation11,23. The lack of external validation studies can be attributed to several factors, such as the inherent difficulty in acquiring medical imaging data for research purposes, regulatory frameworks and legal barriers governing data sharing, and lack of resources needed to process and curate comprehensive real-world datasets for validation purposes24,25. The problem related to the lack of comprehensive external validation studies concerns also commercially available DL solutions11. Moreover, the existing validation studies conducted during the development of commercially available DL solutions have often included only a limited number of imaging studies and inadequate reporting of validation cohort details26,27. Thus, the responsibility for evaluating and validating the performance of such systems in clinical practice ultimately falls on the institutions and hospitals that plan to implement these software solutions. As an example, a minor modification to image acquisition parameters (i.e. padding the pixel matrix size to 512 × 512) in the KSSG cohort resulted in 50.5% improvement in the false positive rate. Therefore, when DL systems are deployed in clinical setting, it is crucial to account for varying image acquisition and reconstruction parameters. External validation studies of DL systems seldom achieve the performance level observed in internal validation28, which may be at least partially explained by differences in image acquisition and reconstruction settings. Since external validation is considered the gold standard for assessing the performance of DL solutions, the use of multi-source data with varying imaging acquisition settings could provide a more comprehensive and realistic evaluation of a DL solution’s generalizability and performance. By utilizing suboptimal real-world data, we were able to provide insights into how image acquisition parameters influence the accuracy of the DL solution.

Our study has acknowledged limitations. First, the study was conducted retrospectively. Therefore, we could not assess the DL solution’s impact on clinical workflow and end-user satisfaction. Second, the ground truth for diagnosed spontaneous intracranial hemorrhages was based on radiology reports, and the two validation centers had different processes for generating radiology reports. Therefore, we acknowledge that the validation dataset may include a very few misdiagnoses, missed findings and minor variation between the two cohorts; however, prior studies have shown that such discrepancies are rare even between less experienced on-call radiologists and expert readers in large hospitals6,29. Third, the total number of head NCCTs included in the external validation dataset was 901 out of which 81 (9.0%) had spontaneous intracranial hemorrhages. Although the number of positive NCCTs may seem relatively low, this distribution aligns with the low prevalence of abnormal findings in non-selected cohorts of emergency head NCCTs30,31. Another limitation is that variations in imaging acquisition parameters, particularly pixel matrix size, are not the only factors influencing the algorithm’s specificity. The two cohorts differed not only in matrix size but also in slice thickness, scanner types, reconstruction methods, and acquisition time periods (e.g., the KSSG dataset was acquired within a single month, whereas the USZ cohort was collected over eight years). Consequently, the observed differences in specificity cannot be attributed to pixel matrix size alone. This possibility should be considered and addressed in future in more controlled studies.

In conclusion, although the accuracy of the internally validated DL solution declined during external validation, it still demonstrated high sensitivity. The study findings indicate that minimal imaging parameters changes, such as the pixel matrix size, affect the performance of DL solutions, likely increasing the rate of false positives. The reported findings are likely to affect not only solutions in the research and development phase but also those that are already commercially available.
