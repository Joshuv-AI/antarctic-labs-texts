Associations between sulfur dioxide exposure and mortality, with a focus on cardiovascular disease: a multi-county time series analysis in central China
Authors: Ling-Shuang Lv, Xin Xia, Zhi-Wen Yang, Ning An, Min Zhang, Xiu-Ying Liu, Li-Dong Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite growing evidence on health effects associated with atmospheric pollutants, limited research has investigated the effect of ambient sulfur dioxide (SO2) on disease-specific mortality, particularly cardiovascular disease (CVD).MethodsWe evaluated the effect of SO2 on CVD mortality in 122 counties, Hunan Province, Central China, during the period 2018–2022. Daily mortality records and ambient air pollution concentrations were collected, and time-series regression models combined with multilevel meta-analyses were utilized for data analysis.ResultsA total of 2.21 million all-cause deaths were recorded, with CVD accounting for 1.15 million cases. The annual average SO2 concentration exhibited a significant decline, decreasing from 11.30 µg/m3 in 2018 to 8.08 µg/m3 in 2022, with an overall mean concentration of 9.39 µg/m3. The relative risk (RR) for per 10 µg/m3 increase in SO2 at lag03 were 1.0258 (95% CI: 1.0033, 1.0488) for CVD and 1.0346 (95% CI: 1.0092, 1.0607) for ischaemic heart disease, respectively. Ambient exposure to SO2 was linked to an excess mortality fraction of 2.52% (95% CI: 2.17%, 2.84%) for CVD, with a decline from 3.11% (95% CI: 2.66%, 3.49%) in 2018 to 2.17% (95% CI: 1.86%, 2.44%) in 2022. For ischaemic heart disease, the excess mortality fraction was 3.13% (95% CI: 2.87%, 3.37%). After adjustment for co-pollutants such as PM10, PM2.5 or NO2, the effect of SO2 on CVD mortality remained significant and was even enhanced.ConclusionsThe results highlight the importance of continued air quality improvements and targeted public health interventions to mitigate the burden of CVD mortality associated with SO2.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25483-1.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), primarily ischemic heart disease and stroke, represent the leading cause of death worldwide [1]. Between 1990 and 2021, global CVD-related deaths increased by 57.5%, rising from 12.33 million to 19.42 million. Notably, China accounted for the highest number of CVD deaths, with 5.1 million cases reported in 2021 [2].

Environmental air pollution is an important but often overlooked non-traditional risk factor for CVD [3, 4]. Among the various ambient pollutants, particulate matter (PM) emerged as the leading contributor to the global disease burden in 2021, accounting for 8.0% of total disability-adjusted life years [5]. Sulfur dioxide (SO2), another significant indicator of air pollution, has been shown to exert independent health effects [6, 7]. Previous studies have demonstrated the association between SO2 and negative health outcomes, with a predominant focus on all-cause and respiratory-related deaths [8–10]. A recent meta-analysis encompassing 67 eligible studies revealed that a 10 µg/m3 increase in SO2 (24-hour average) was associated with elevated risks of all-cause mortality and respiratory mortality [10]. A multi-country analysis demonstrated that short-term exposure to SO2 was linked to an excess mortality fraction of 0.50%, with China ranking third globally, exhibiting an attributable mortality fraction of 1.48%, trailing only Brazil and Japan [11]. Emerging evidence points to a potential link between exposure to SO2 and higher rates of CVD-related morbidity and mortality [12]. However, the relationship between SO2 exposure and CVD mortality, particularly for ischemic heart disease and stroke, remains uncertain. Some studies have proposed that SO2 may act as a surrogate for other pollutants, implying that the observed health effects could be confounded by other co-pollutants [13, 14]. Contrastingly, a number of recent studies have shown persistent associations between SO2 exposure and adverse health outcomes even after adjusting for co-pollutants such as PM and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) [15–17]. These results emphasize the need to disentangle the independent effects of SO2 from the potential synergistic effects of other pollutants on cardiovascular mortality.

In this study, we performed a comprehensive time-series analysis to evaluate both the short-term effects and long-term trends of SO2 on CVD and cause-specific mortality across 122 counties of Hunan Province, Central China, during the period 2018–2022. Additionally, we explored whether the health impacts of SO2 were modified by co-exposure to other contaminants. Furthermore, subgroup analyses were undertaken to assess these associations across different demographic subgroups, including gender and age.

Methods

Data collection

The study was conducted in Hunan Province, located in Central China, encompassing 122 districts and counties (Fig. 1A). Hunan Province is renowned as the “hometown of non-ferrous metals”. Daily mortality data were obtained from the China Center for Disease Control and Prevention, spanning the period from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2022. The mortality dataset included detailed information on sex, age, residential address, and disease classification codes. Causes of death were categorized based on the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10). Daily counts of mortality were extracted for CVDs (I00-I99), respiratory diseases (J00-J99), and neoplasms (C00-C97, D00-D48). Additionally, three specific diseases were selected for further analysis: stroke (I60-I69) and ischemic heart disease (I20-I25) from CVDs, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD, J44) from respiratory diseases.

Air pollution data, including concentrations of SO2, carbon monoxide (CO), NO2, ozone (O3), PM2.5 and PM10, were obtained from the China High Air Pollutants dataset. The spatial distribution of mean SO2 concentrations across the counties is illustrated in Fig. 1B. Meteorological data for the study period, including daily mean temperature, and precipitation, were collected from the National Centers for Environmental Information.

Statistical methods

A time series regression was conducted in this study, which has been widely used in environmental epidemiology [18]. We employed a two-stage analytical approach to evaluate the association between SO2 and CVD mortality, following the method proposed by O’Brien et al. [11]. In the first stage, county-specific time-series analyses were conducted using a quasi-Poisson generalized linear model incorporating distributed lag terms. The model is expressed as follows:

Yt and E(Yt) represent the observed and expected values of CVD deaths on day t, respectively. α denotes the intercept. β quantifies the logarithm of the change in CVD deaths associated with a unit increase in SO2 concentration. Xt−n represents the concentration of SO2 lagged by n days. Considering the short-term effects of SO₂, and in accordance with previous study [11], a cumulative lag of 0–3 days (lag03) was selected. cb models the exposure-lag-response relationship for temperature using a distributed lag nonlinear model, with a maximum lag period of 21 days. For both the exposure and lag dimensions, B-spline bases were employed, with the degrees of freedom (df) set to 2 and 4 for the exposure and lag dimensions, respectively. Long-term trends and seasonality were controlled using a natural spline function (ns) with df of 5 per year. Day of the week (Dow) was included to control for weekly variations in mortality.

In the second stage, county-specific estimates were combined using a multilevel meta-analysis. The pooled estimate reflects the overall average relationship between SO2 and mortality, whereas county-specific estimates were calculated as best linear unbiased predictions at their respective aggregation levels. RRs for a 10-ug/m3 increase in SO2 are reported, along with corresponding 95% CI. Heterogeneity across counties was assessed using the I2 statistics and tested using Cochran’s Q test. The attributable fraction (AF) was calculated using the formula: AF = (RR − 1)/RR, where RR represents the relative risk.

To evaluate the robustness of our findings, several sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, potential confounding effects of other air pollutants were assessed using bi-pollutant models. Second, alternative lag structures for SO2 exposure were explored, including moving averages of the current day and the preceding 1–5 days (lag 0–1 to lag 0–5). R software (version 4.3.3) was used for all analyses, with the “ggplot2”, “dlnm” and “mixmeta” packages.

Results

Study population and air pollutant concentration characteristics

This analysis included a total of 2.21 million deaths from all causes during the period 2018–2022. Of these, CVD, respiratory diseases and neoplasms accounted for 1.15 million, 0.23 million, 0.43 million, respectively. The average daily death for all causes, CVD, respiratory diseases and neoplasms was 10.14, 5.30, 1.06 and 1.99, respectively. The mean temperature was 17.68 °C (Table 1). The average daily concentration of SO2 was 9.39 µg/m3, with considerable variation across counties (Fig. 1B). The concentration of SO2 decreased considerably during the study period, from 11.30 µg/m3 in 2018 to 8.08 µg/m3 in 2022 (Fig. 1C). The average daily concentrations of other air pollutants were as follows: CO (0.83 mg/m3), NO2 (17.67 µg/m3), O3 (91.01 µg/m3), PM2.5 (31.73 µg/m3), and PM10 (48.67 µg/m3). Correlations between different air pollutants are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Association between SO2 and mortality

The association between SO2 and mortality was strongest 2–3 days after exposure, followed by a gradual decline (Fig. 2). On average, a per 10 µg/m3 increase in SO2 concentration was linked to a RR of 1.0227 (95% CI: 0.9984, 1.0476) for all-cause mortality. For cause-specific mortality, the RRs were 1.0258 (95% CI: 1.0033, 1.0488) for CVD, 1.0168 (95% CI: 0.9824, 1.0525) for respiratory diseases, and 1.0160 (95% CI: 0.9826, 1.0505) for neoplasms. Given the statistically significant association between SO2 and CVD mortality, stratified analyses were conducted by cause of death and demographic characteristics (Fig. 3). Individuals aged 75 years and older were more susceptible to the effects of SO2 compared to younger individuals. Specifically, SO2 exposure was linked to a higher risk of ischemic heart disease mortality, with a RR of 1.0346 (95% CI: 1.0092, 1.0607). Stratified analyses further revealed that females (RR: 1.0487, 95% CI: 1.0065, 1.0927) and the elderly (RR: 1.0374, 95% CI: 1.0085, 1.0672) were more susceptible to SO2-related ischemic heart disease mortality compared to males (RR: 1.0282, 95% CI: 0.9948, 1.0628) and younger individuals (RR: 1.0312, 95% CI: 0.9819, 1.0831). Heterogeneity among counties was significant for total mortality (I2 = 61.8%, Cochran Q = 316.52, P < 0.001), CVD (I2 = 50.6%, Cochran Q = 244.81, P < 0.001), respiratory disease (I2 = 32.7%, Cochran Q = 179.92, P < 0.001), and neoplasms (I2 = 36.8%, Cochran Q = 191.58, P < 0.001).

Excess mortality

The excess mortality fraction for CVD attributable to SO2 was 2.52% (95% CI: 2.17%, 2.84%). A notable temporal decline was observed in the excess mortality fraction, decreasing from 3.11% (95% CI: 2.66%, 3.49%) in 2018 to 2.17% (95% CI: 1.86%, 2.44%) in 2022. This downward trend aligns with the long-term reduction in ambient SO2 concentrations, as previously described. Stratified by specific CVD subtypes, the excess mortality fraction were 3.13% (95% CI: 2.87%, 3.37%) for ischaemic heart disease and 2.27% (95% CI: 1.90%, 2.59%) for stroke (Table 2). Further stratified analysis by gender revealed that females with ischemic heart disease exhibited the highest vulnerability to SO2 exposure, resulting in an excess mortality fraction of 4.06% (95% CI: 3.24%, 4.71%). Stratified analysis by gender revealed that the elder age 75 years with ischemic heart disease exhibited the highest vulnerability to SO2 exposure, resulting in an excess mortality fraction of 3.37% (95% CI: 3.07%, 3.65%). Detailed cause-specific mortality fractions, stratified by sex and age, are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Bi-Pollutant analysis and sensitivity analysis

In the bi-pollutant analysis, the association between SO2 and CVD mortality risk remained robust and statistically significant after adjusting for PM10, PM2.5 or NO2, with the RR values of 1.0459 (95% CI: 1.0161, 1.0766), 1.0357 (95% CI: 1.0082, 1.0639) and 1.0375 (95% CI: 1.0049, 1.0711). However, the inclusion of CO and O3 in the models attenuated the associations, rendering them statistically insignificant, with the RR values of 1.0201 (95% CI: 0.9974, 1.0433) and 1.0260 (95% CI: 0.9928, 1.0604), respectively. To evaluate the robustness of our findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted by varying the cumulative lag periods (lag01, lag02, lag03, lag04, and lag05). The results confirmed the stability of the observed associations across different lag structures (Table 3).

Discussion

The study explored the effect of SO2 exposure on daily CVD mortality across 122 counties in Hunan Province, Central China, during the period 2018–2022. The findings revealed the relationships between both short-term effect and long-term trend of SO2 and CVD mortality. These relationships remained robust after adjusting for PM10, PM2.5 and NO2 but were attenuated and became statistically insignificant when adjusted for CO and O3. Furthermore, the analysis demonstrated that the associations were more pronounced among elderly individuals and the female subgroup.

Our analysis revealed a significant short-term risk associated with SO2 exposure, with a RR value of 1.0258 (95% CI: 1.0092–1.0607) per 10 µg/m3 increase in SO2, although heterogeneity across counties was observed. This indicates that for every 10 µg/m3 rise in ambient SO2 levels, the risk of death increases by 2%. These pooled RR estimates align with findings from prior epidemiological studies [19–21]. For instance, the Public Health and Air Pollution in Asia Study reported a 1.09% (95% CI, 0.71–1.47) increase in CVD mortality per 10 µg/m3 rise in SO2 across four cities [19]. Similarly, in the United States, a per interquartile range increase in SO2 was associated with a RR of 1.054 for CVD mortality [20]. A meta-analysis further demonstrated a 1.019-fold increase in relative risk for stroke per 10 µg/m3 increment in SO2 concentration [21]. In addition to risk estimates, we quantified the attributable risk, finding that SO2 exposure was linked to an excess increase of 2.52% (95% CI: 2.17%, 2.84%) in CVD mortality. This suggests that eliminating SO2 exposure could potentially reduce the population-level CVD mortality rate by 2.52%. Similar results were observed in a study across 272 Chinese cities, where a 10 µg/m3 increase in SO2 was linked to a 2.23% rise in CVD mortality [22]. Furthermore, a 26-year study identified the highest SO2-attributed CVD mortality fraction as 0.080 (95% CI: 0.024–0.168) [23]. However, some studies have reported no significant association between SO2 and disease mortality [24, 25]. These discrepancies may stem from variations in exposure levels, exposure patterns, population susceptibility, study designs, and statistical methodologies. For example, Hunan Province, known as the “hometown of non-ferrous metals”, experiences elevated SO2 emissions due to metal smelting processes [26, 27]. Additionally, regional differences in climate and socioeconomic characteristics may further contribute to the variability in findings across studies.

When conducting an analysis of the long-term trend and stratifying by year, the excess mortality fraction attributable to SO2 exposure showed a notable decline. It decreased from 3.11% in 2018 to 2.17% in 2022. This trend reflects a significant reduction in the health impacts of SO2, consistent with the observed decrease in ambient SO2 concentrations. The improvement in air quality can be largely attributed to the implementation of China’s Blue Sky Defense Action Plan, which has led to substantial reductions in air pollution levels [28]. The average daily concentration declined from 11.30 µg/m3 in 2018 to 8.08 µg/m3 in 2022, a level well below the World Health Organization (WHO) guideline of 40 µg/m3 [29]. Despite this progress, SO2 exposure remained associated with a considerable number of excess deaths in recent years, underscoring the persistent health risks even at lower concentration levels. These findings highlight that even within low exposure levels, SO2 poses a significant public health threat. Our study emphasizes the need for continued vigilance regarding the health effects of low-level SO2 exposure and underscores the importance of further efforts to mitigate air pollution and protect vulnerable populations.

Our study further elucidated the associations between SO2 and daily mortality from various specific CVD, particularly ischaemic heart disease and stroke. SO2 exposure was linked to an increased risk of ischemic heart disease mortality, with a RR value of 1.0346. Several biological pathways and mechanisms may explain how SO2 contributes to elevated CVD mortality risks. For ischemic heart disease, cardiomyocytes are the primary target, while for stroke, neurons and brain tissues are the most affected. Animal studies have demonstrated that SO2 exposure induces dysfunction in myocardial mitochondria, the most sensitive organelles in the myocardium of SO2-exposed rats [30]. Additionally, SO2 has been shown to impair baroreflex sensitivity and cardiac vagal control, while increasing plasma fibrinogen levels, oxidative stress, and blood viscosity [11, 31].

We also performed subgroup analyses by sex and age. In line with previous epidemiological studies on air pollution, we observed significant effect modification by age, with older individuals exhibiting heightened vulnerability [32–34]. This increased susceptibility in older populations may be attributed to age-related declines in immune function and a higher prevalence of comorbid conditions. Furthermore, we identified stronger associations in the female population, which may be influenced by socioeconomic disparities and other gender-specific factors. The results underscore the significance of considering demographic differences in assessing the health effects of air pollution and tailoring public health interventions accordingly.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, similar to most time-series analyses, this study is inherently ecological in nature. Individual-level confounders cannot be comprehensively addressed, a limitation that may lead to overestimation or underestimation of associations due to the “ecological fallacy.” Second, this study inferred population SO2 exposure based on site-specific data, which may introduce potential measurement errors. Third, the study was conducted in 122 counties within Hunan Province, Central China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Even with these limitations, the findings deliver meaningful insights into the health impacts of SO2 exposure.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated significant associations between short-term exposure to ambient SO2 and increased CVD mortality, while long-term exposure exhibited a declining trend over time. The findings underscore the critical public health implications of SO2 exposure and highlight the importance of raising health awareness among the general population. Additionally, the results provide valuable evidence to guide health policymakers in developing targeted strategies and interventions to mitigate the adverse health effects of air pollution. In the future, further studies should elucidate the biological pathways through which SO2 affects the cardiovascular system. In-depth investigations into gene-environment interactions should also be conducted. Through these efforts, both differences in individual susceptibility to SO2-related CVD risks and their underlying mechanisms can be uncovered.
