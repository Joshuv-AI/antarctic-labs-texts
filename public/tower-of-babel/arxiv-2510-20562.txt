Exponential sums with polynomials and their applications to primes in sparse sets
Lingyu Guo, Victor Zhenyu Guo, Mengyao Jing

Abstract
. Exponential sums with monomials are highly related to many interesting problems in number theory and well studied by many literatures. In this paper, we consider the exponential sums with polynomials and prove a new upper bound. As an application, we study the Piatetski-Shapiro sequence of the form ( ⌊ n c ⌋ ) (\lfloor n^{c}\rfloor) where c > 1 c>1 is not an integer. We improve the admissible range of the asymptotic formula for primes in the intersection of Piatetski-Shapiro sequences. We also study the iterated Piatetski-Shapiro sequence and prove an asymptotic formula for the prime counting function.



MSC Numbers:
11B83; 11L20.

Keywords:
exponential sum; Piatetski-Shapiro sequence; prime; lattice.

1.
Introduction

The estimation of the exponential sum of the following form is a core part of many interesting problems in number theory:

\mathcal{S}_{0}=\sum_{m\sim M}\sum_{n\sim N}a_{m}b_{n}\mathbf{e}(f(m,n))

with the notations
\mathbf{e}(t)=e^{2\pi it}
, where
N,M
are positive numbers,
a_{m},b_{n}
are complex numbers,
f
is a smooth function,
m\sim M
means that
M<m\leqslant 2M
.

The monomial case when

f(m,n)=E\frac{m^{\beta}n^{\gamma}}{M^{\beta}N^{\gamma}}\ \ \ \ (E\in\mathbb{R})

was well studied; see Bombieri and Iwaniec
[
4
]
, Fouvry and Iwaniec
[
8
]
, and so on. We mention that a remarkable result is by Robert and Sargos
[
21
]
who proved the bound of
\mathcal{S}_{0}
via double large sieves and a spacing problem solved by an optimal bound of the number of solutions of a diophantine inequality.

In this paper, we consider a more general case when the function
f
is a polynomial, which leads the exponential sum to be

\mathcal{S}=\sum_{m\sim M}\mathop{\sum_{n\sim N}}_{mn\sim X}a_{m}b_{n}\mathbf{e}\left(\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}\frac{E_{j}m^{\beta_{j}}n^{\gamma_{j}}}{M^{\beta_{j}}N^{\gamma_{j}}}\right).

Unfortunately the method for the monomial case in
[
4
,
8
,
21
]
does not work in the polynomial case, since double large sieve provides a bound worse than the trivial bound. A traditional method is to apply the van der Corput’s inequality or a higher derivative test for exponential sums which work on any smooth function; see Chapter 2 in
[
9
]
. With this idea, Baker
[
3
]
proved a bound of a special case of
\mathcal{S}
when
\beta_{j}=\gamma_{j}
for all
j
.

We prove the bound of
\mathcal{S}
which is also better than Baker’s bound in the special case. A key idea is that we recognize the method of exponent pairs (Lemma
3.2
) can be applied to this problem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
M,N,X
be positive real numbers such that
X=MN
.
Let
E_{j},\beta_{j},\gamma_{j}\ (j=1,\cdots,d)
be real numbers.
For every exponent pair
(\kappa,\lambda)
and any sufficiently small real

\varepsilon>0
we have

\displaystyle X^{-3\varepsilon}\mathcal{S}\ll M^{1/2}X^{1/2}+\Delta^{-1/2}X

\displaystyle+M^{(\lambda-\kappa-1)/(2\kappa+2)}X

\displaystyle+\Delta^{\kappa/(2\kappa+2)}M^{(\lambda-1)/(2\kappa+2)}X^{(2+\kappa)/(2\kappa+2)},

where
|a_{m}|\ll X^{\varepsilon}
,
|b_{n}|\ll X^{\varepsilon}
and

\Delta=\Bigg|\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}\gamma_{j}\cdots(\gamma_{j}-s+1)E_{j}\Bigg|.

1.1.
Applications of the exponential sums

An application is related to the Piatetski-Shapiro sequences defined as

\mathcal{N}^{c}=(\lfloor n^{c}\rfloor)_{n=1}^{\infty},

where
\lfloor x\rfloor
is the largest integer not exceeding
x
with real
c>1
and
c\not\in\mathbb{N}
. Piatetski-Shapiro
[
18
]
gave the following prime number theorem:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\#\left\{p\leqslant X:p\in\mathcal{N}^{c}\right\}=(1+o(1))\frac{X^{1/c}}{c\log X}\ \ \ \hbox{as}\ X\rightarrow\infty

for
1<c<12/11
. This result is considered as an approximation of the well-known conjecture that there exist infinitely many primes of the form
n^{2}+1
.

To date, the best known admission range for
c
is
1<c<2817/2426
due to Rivat and Sargos
[
19
]
. If one only considers (
1.1
) with a lower bound of the right order of magnitude instead of an asymptotic formula, a large range
1<c<243/205
was proved by Rivat and Wu
[
20
]
. The readers are referred to
[
10
]
for a survey regarding the distribution of Piatetski-Shapiro primes.

An analogue problem of (
1.1
) is to count primes in the intersection of multiple Piatetski-Shapiro sequences, which is

\mathcal{N}(X,\boldsymbol{c})=\#\left\{p\leqslant X:p=\lfloor n_{1}^{c_{1}}\rfloor=\cdots=\lfloor n_{d}^{c_{d}}\rfloor\hbox{\ for some integers}\ n_{1},\cdots,n_{d}\right\},

where
c_{1},\cdots,c_{d}>1
and
\boldsymbol{c}=(c_{1},\cdots,c_{d})
. Denote

\gamma_{j}=\frac{1}{c_{j}},\ \ \delta_{j}=1-\gamma_{j},\ \ \sigma=\delta_{1}+\cdots+\delta_{d}.

We expect that for some
\sigma_{d}
such that
\sigma<\sigma_{d}
, it follows that

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(X,\boldsymbol{c})=(1+o(1))\frac{\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{d}}{1-\sigma}\frac{X^{1-\sigma}}{\log X}.

Leitmann
[
16
]
proved that
\sigma_{2}=1/28
as a first result. Sirota
[
23
]
extends this result to
\sigma_{2}=1/16
. Moreover, Sirota also proved an asymptotic formula for these primes in arithmetic progressions when
d=2
. Zhai
[
24
]
proved that
\sigma_{d}=d/(4d^{2}+2)
for
d\geqslant 3
and claimed a stronger result for
d=2
but it has not been published. Baker
[
3
]
proved the following best result with

\sigma_{2}=\sigma_{3}=\frac{1}{12},\ \ \sigma_{4}=\frac{1}{16},\ \ \sigma_{5}=\frac{1}{18},\ \ \sigma_{d}=\frac{1}{3d}\ (d\geqslant 6).

In this paper, we further improve Baker’s results as an application of our Theorem
1.1
. Our main result is as follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

Under the above notations, suppose that
\sigma<\sigma_{d}
, where

\sigma_{d}=\frac{290}{3297}\approx\frac{1}{11.36}\ (2\leqslant d\leqslant 10)\ \ \ \hbox{and}\ \ \ \sigma_{d}=\frac{1}{d+1}\ (d\geqslant 11),

we have

\mathcal{N}(X,\boldsymbol{c})=(1+o(1))\frac{\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{d}}{1-\sigma}\frac{X^{1-\sigma}}{\log X}.

Another application of Theorem
1.1
is the iterated Piatetski-Shapiro sequences of the form

\mathcal{N}^{(c_{1},c_{2})}=\big(\lfloor\lfloor n^{c_{1}}\rfloor^{c_{2}}\rfloor\big)_{n=1}^{\infty},

where
1<c_{1},c_{2}<2
are real numbers. Obviously, the elements in
\mathcal{N}^{(c_{1},c_{2})}

construct new sparser sets. It is natural to establish a prime number theorem for the iterated Piatetski-Shapiro sequences. Our result is enunciated as follows.

1<c_{1},c_{2}<2

\gamma_{1}=1/c_{1}

\gamma_{1}

\displaystyle 26\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}-2\gamma_{2}-23>0,

we have

\mathcal{P}(X;c_{1},c_{2})=(1+o(1))\frac{X^{\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}}{c_{1}c_{2}\log X}.

Notations

As is customary, we write

\mathbf{e}(x)=\exp(2\pi ix)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\{x\}=x-\left\lfloor x\right\rfloor\qquad(x\in\mathbb{R}).

We denote by

The sawtooth function is defined by

\psi(x)=x-\left\lfloor x\right\rfloor-\tfrac{1}{2}=\{x\}-\tfrac{1}{2}\qquad(x\in\mathbb{R}).

For given functions

and

C>0
.

Outlines

Our improvements on exponential sums relies on an observation that we can apply exponent pair method together with a counting problem (Lemma

). We complete the proof of Theorem

in Section

.

The applications to prime numbers are started by a lattice method investigated by Harman
[
11
]
and Baker
[
3
]
. We provide a slightly more general form here. The theorem is proved in Section
5
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\Gamma
denote a lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Pi
denote the polar lattice of
\Gamma
. Let
a_{1},\cdots,a_{N}
be complex weights, and let
\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{N}
be a sequence in

\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Define

\mathcal{N}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}\in\mathcal{B}_{d}\pmod{\Gamma}}a_{n},

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{N})
and
\mathcal{B}_{d}=[-1,1]^{d}
. Then we have

\mathcal{N}=\frac{1}{\det\Gamma}\bigg((2^{d}+O(w^{-1}))\sum_{n\leqslant N}a_{n}+O\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{q}\in\Pi\\
0<|\boldsymbol{q}|<w\end{subarray}}\bigg|\sum_{n\leqslant N}a_{n}e(\boldsymbol{q\alpha}_{n})\bigg|\bigg)\bigg),

where
w
is a parameter in
(1,\infty)
at our disposal.

2.1.
Intersection of Piatetski-Shapiro sequences

Based on the lattice method, we prove Proposition
6.2
to analyze primes in the intersection of Piatetski-Shapiro sequences, which means that it is sufficient to bound

\mathop{\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}}_{|h_{j}|<\mathcal{L}X^{\delta_{j}}}\Bigg|\sum_{X<n\leqslant aX}\Lambda(n)\mathbf{e}\left(h_{1}n^{\gamma_{1}}+\cdots+h_{d}n^{\gamma_{d}}\right)\Bigg|

with
\boldsymbol{h}=(h_{1},\cdots,h_{d})
and
\mathcal{L}=\log X
.
Now via the well-known Heath-Brown identity (Lemma
3.4
), we need to work on the following sum

\mathfrak{S}=\mathop{\sum_{0<|h_{j}|<X^{\delta_{j}}\mathcal{L}}}_{j=1,\cdots,d}\Bigg|\mathop{{\sum_{m\sim M}\sum_{n\sim N}}}_{mn\sim X}a_{m}b_{n}\mathbf{e}\left(h_{1}(mn)^{\gamma_{1}}+\cdots+h_{d}(mn)^{\gamma_{d}}\right)\Bigg|,

where
N\geqslant 1,M\geqslant 1,MN\asymp X
,
|a_{m}|\ll X^{\varepsilon}
and
|b_{n}|\ll X^{\varepsilon}

for any
\varepsilon>0
. If
b_{n}=1
or
b_{n}=\log n
, we call it a Type I sum and denote it as
\mathfrak{S}_{I}
; otherwise we call it as a Type II sum and denote it as
\mathfrak{S}_{II}
.

A normal method is to bound type I sum by the van der Corput inequality or a higher derivative test. The type II sum is estimated by the Weyl-van der Corput inequality (A-process) together with the van der Corput inequality. We mention that Zhai
[
24
]
and Baker
[
3
]
investigated the problem exactly by the normal method we mention here.

In this paper, we bound the type II sum by our Theorem
1.1
. The type I sum is bounded by an exponent pair method. Finding that how to apply an exponent pair method is one of the main reasons leading to the improvements. Section
6
gives a detailed proof how we apply the lattice method to the intersection of Piatetski-Shapiro sequences and Section
7
provides the estimation of exponential sums based on Theorem
1.1
.

2.2.
The iterated Piatetski-Shapiro sequence

We also deploy primes in the iterated Piatetski-Shapiro sequence by our lattice method (Theorem
2.1
) and arrive at a sum of the form

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(k,h)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\backslash(0,0)\\
|k|<\mathcal{L}X^{1-\gamma_{2}}\\
|h|<\mathcal{L}X^{\gamma_{2}(1-\gamma_{1})}\end{subarray}}\Bigg|\sum_{X<m\leqslant aX}\Lambda(m)\mathbf{e}(km^{\gamma_{2}}+h\lfloor(m+1)^{\gamma_{2}}\rfloor^{\gamma_{1}})\Bigg|.

The trick is that we need a Fourier analysis method to work on exponential sums with floor functions. We introduce a method due to Kolesnik
[
15
, Lemma 1]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
f(\mu,\nu)
be a real valued function such that

|f(\mu_{1},\nu)-f(\mu_{2},\nu)|\leqslant\lambda|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}|.

Then, for any real function
g(x)
, any positive integer
r
and any
W>0

we have

\displaystyle\sum_{x}a(x)

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}\left(f(g(x),\{h(x)\})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{W}\sum_{w=0}^{W-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}B_{j,w}\sum_{x}a(x)\mathbf{e}\left(f\left(g(x),\frac{w}{W}\right)+jh(x)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+O\left(\frac{\lambda r+r}{W}\sum_{x}|a(x)|\right)

\displaystyle\quad+O\left(\frac{r}{W}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(2\pi rj/W)}{\sin(\pi j/W)}A_{j}\sum_{x}|a(x)|\mathbf{e}(jh(x))\right),

where

\displaystyle A_{j}=\left(\frac{\sin(\pi j/M)}{\pi j/W}\right)^{r+1},\ \ \ A_{0}=1

\displaystyle B_{j,w}=A_{j}\mathbf{e}\left(-\frac{(2w+1)j}{2W}\right).

After many technical calculations, it is sufficient to prove

\Psi=\sum_{X<m\leqslant aX}\Lambda(m)\mathbf{e}\left(h\left((m+1)^{\gamma_{2}}-\frac{w}{W}\right)^{\gamma_{1}}+km^{\gamma_{2}}+j(m+1)^{\gamma_{2}}\right).

Now the Vaughan identity (Lemma
3.3
) also allows us to bound corresponding type I and type II sums, which are similar to the proof of Theorem
1.1
. We make the technical discussion together with the proof of Theorem
1.3
in Section
8
and finish the bounds of error terms in Section
9
.

In this article, we only consider the twice-iterated Piatetski-Shapiro sequences. We believe that our method also works for multiple iterated Piatetski-Shapiro sequences of the form

\mathcal{N}^{(\mathbf{c})}=\big(\left\lfloor\left\lfloor\cdots{\left\lfloor n^{c_{1}}\right\rfloor^{c_{2}}}\cdots\right\rfloor^{c_{n}}\right\rfloor\big)_{n=1}^{\infty}.

However, this may be done by a very complicated induction which can take very long calculations. We think this may be of independent interest.

4.
Bounds of exponential sums

In order to prove Theorem
1.1
, we first give the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
d\geqslant 1
be an integer, and let
L,L_{0},z_{1},\cdots,z_{d},\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d}
be real numbers such that
L>1
,
L_{0}\leqslant L
and

\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d}\in(0,1)
. Then we have

\sum_{L<l\leqslant L+L_{0}}\mathbf{e}\left(z_{1}l^{\alpha_{1}}+\cdots+z_{d}l^{\alpha_{d}}\right)\ll\Delta_{0}^{\kappa}L^{\lambda}+\Delta_{0}^{-1},

where

\Delta_{0}=\Bigg|\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}\alpha_{j}\cdots(\alpha_{j}-s+1)z_{j}L^{\alpha_{j}-1}\Bigg|.

Proof.

Write
f(x)=z_{1}x^{\alpha_{1}}+\cdots+z_{d}x^{\alpha_{d}}
. For any positive integer
s
,
we have

\displaystyle|f^{(s)}(l)|=\Bigg|\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}\alpha_{j}\cdots(\alpha_{j}-s+1)z_{j}l^{\alpha_{j}-s}\Bigg|\asymp\Delta_{0}L^{1-s}.

It follows from Lemma
3.2
that

\sum_{L<l\leqslant L+L_{0}}\mathbf{e}\left(f(l)\right)\ll\Delta_{0}^{\kappa}L^{\lambda}+\Delta_{0}^{-1}.

This proves Lemma
4.1
.
∎

Now we return to the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
Applying the Cauchy inequality we have

\displaystyle X^{-2\varepsilon}|\mathcal{S}|^{2}\ll M\sum_{m\sim M}\Bigg|\mathop{\sum_{n\sim N}}_{mn\sim X}b_{n}\mathbf{e}\left(\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}\frac{E_{j}m^{\beta_{j}}n^{\gamma_{j}}}{M^{\beta_{j}}N^{\gamma_{j}}}\right)\Bigg|^{2}.

Let
Q\leqslant N
be a positive real number. The Weyl-van der Corput inequality
(Lemma
3.6
) yields

\displaystyle X^{-2\varepsilon}|\mathcal{S}|^{2}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{NM}{Q}\sum_{|q|<Q}\Big(1-\frac{q}{Q}\Big)\sum_{n\sim N}b_{n+q}\overline{b_{n}}\mathop{\sum_{m\sim M}}_{mn\sim X}\mathbf{e}\Bigg(\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}\frac{E_{j}m^{\beta_{j}}((n+q)^{\gamma_{j}}-n^{\gamma_{j}})}{M^{\beta_{j}}N^{\gamma_{j}}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{X^{2+2\varepsilon}}{Q}+\frac{X^{1+2\varepsilon}}{Q}\sum_{|q|<Q}\sum_{n\sim N}\Bigg|\mathop{\sum_{m\sim M}}_{mn\sim X}\mathbf{e}\Bigg(\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}\frac{E_{j}((n+q)^{\gamma_{j}}-n^{\gamma_{j}})}{M^{\beta_{j}}N^{\gamma_{j}}}m^{\beta_{j}}\Bigg)\Bigg|.

Now we estimate the innermost sum. Recall that

\Delta=\Bigg|\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}\beta_{j}\cdots(\beta_{j}-s+1)E_{j}\Bigg|.

For a fixed integer
n\sim N
, denote

\displaystyle f(m)=\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}\frac{E_{j}((n+q)^{\gamma_{j}}-n^{\gamma_{j}})}{M^{\beta_{j}}N^{\gamma_{j}}}m^{\beta_{j}}.

Since

f^{(s)}(M)\asymp\Bigg|\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}\beta_{j}\cdots(\beta_{j}-s+1)\frac{E_{j}((n+q)^{\gamma_{j}}-n^{\gamma_{j}})}{M^{\beta_{j}}N^{\gamma_{j}}}M^{\beta_{j}-1}\Bigg|\asymp\Delta|q|X^{-1}.

In view of Lemma
3.2
we derive that

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum_{m\sim M}}_{mn\sim X}\mathbf{e}(f(m))\ll(\Delta|q|X^{-1})^{\kappa}M^{\lambda}+(\Delta|q|X^{-1})^{-1}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle X^{-5\varepsilon}|\mathcal{S}|^{2}\ll X^{2}Q^{-1}+\Delta^{-1}X^{3}M^{-1}Q^{-1}+\Delta^{\kappa}X^{2-\kappa}M^{\lambda-1}Q^{\kappa}.

This bound is trivial when
Q<1
, so we may consider the case that
Q\in(0,N]
.
Lemma
3.5
yields

\displaystyle X^{-3\varepsilon}\mathcal{S}\ll M^{1/2}X^{1/2}

\displaystyle+\Delta^{-1/2}X+M^{(\lambda-\kappa-1)/(2\kappa+2)}X

\displaystyle+\Delta^{\kappa/(2\kappa+2)}M^{(\lambda-1)/(2\kappa+2)}X^{(2+\kappa)/(2\kappa+2)}.

This completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

5.
A lattice method

We review a few terms related to lattice theory. Let
E
be a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
having dimension
t
with
1\leqslant t\leqslant n
. A lattice in
E
is a subgroup
\Lambda
of
E
of the form

\Lambda=\{m_{1}z_{1}+m_{2}z_{2}+\cdots+m_{t}z_{t}:m_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\},

where
z_{1},\cdots,z_{t}
are given linearly independent points in
E
. We also say
z_{1},\cdots,z_{t}
is a basis of
\Lambda
or generates
\Lambda
. The fundamental parallelepiped (or fundamental domain) for
\Lambda
corresponding to this basis is the set

\mathcal{F}=\{t_{1}z_{1}+t_{2}z_{2}+\cdots+t_{n}z_{n}:0\leqslant t_{i}<1\}.

The
n
-dimensional volume of
\mathcal{F}
is called the determinant of
L
, denoted by
\det(\Lambda)
. The polar lattice to
\Lambda
is the lattice
\Pi
of points
\pi
satisfying

\pi\lambda\in\mathbb{Z},\quad\forall\lambda\in\Lambda.

We also need the following lemma which is
[
2
, Lemma 2.4]
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Given any interval
I=[a,b]
, let
\chi_{I}(x)
be the indicator function of
I
. There are continuous functions
G_{1}(x),G_{2}(x)
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
such that

\displaystyle G_{1}(x)\leqslant\chi_{I}(x)\leqslant G_{2}(x),

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{1}(t)=\hat{G}_{2}.(t)=0\quad\text{for}\ |t|\geqslant 1,

and

\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}(\chi_{I}(x)-G_{1}(x))\textup{d}x=1,\quad\intop\nolimits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}(G_{2}(x)-\chi_{I}(x))\textup{d}x=1.

Now we give the proof of Theorem
2.1
. Let
\chi_{d}
denote the indicator function of
\mathcal{B}_{d}
. By lemma
5.1
, we know there exists two continuous functions
\chi^{\pm}
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying

\displaystyle\chi^{-}(x)\leqslant

\displaystyle\chi_{1}(x)\leqslant\chi^{+}(x),

\displaystyle\hat{\chi}^{\pm}(t)=

\displaystyle 0\quad\text{for}\ |t|\geqslant w

and

\intop\nolimits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}(\chi_{1}(x)-\chi^{-}(x))\textup{d}x=\intop\nolimits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}(\chi^{+}(x)-\chi_{1}(x))\textup{d}x=\frac{1}{w}.

Here and below, the symbol
\hat{g}
denotes the Fourier transform defined on

L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as

\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{t})=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbf{e}(-\boldsymbol{xt})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x},

and the parameter
w
in
(1,+\infty)
is at our disposal.

Let

\sigma(j,k)=\left\{\begin{aligned} -\quad\text{if}\ j=k,\\
+\quad\text{if}\ j\neq k,\end{aligned}\right.\quad\text{and}\quad\tau(j,k)=\left\{\begin{aligned} 0\quad\text{if}\ j=k,\\
+\quad\text{if}\ j\neq k.\end{aligned}\right.

We claim that

(5.1)

\displaystyle\chi_{d}(\boldsymbol{x})\geqslant\sum_{k=1}^{d}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\chi^{\tau(j,k)}(x_{j})-(d-1)\prod_{j=1}^{d}\chi^{+}(x_{j}),

here
\chi^{0}(x_{j})
means
\chi_{1}(x_{j})
. To see this, first note that the right-hand side of (
5.1
) is clearly non-positive if one of
\chi^{0}(x_{j})
is zero. If every
\chi^{0}(x_{j})
is
1
then the right-hand side can be shown not to exceed
1
by induction. Hence, it follows that

\chi_{d}(\boldsymbol{x})\geqslant\sum_{k=1}^{d}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\chi^{\sigma(j,k)}(x_{j})-(d-1)\prod_{j=1}^{d}\chi^{+}(x_{j}).

Put

g(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\chi^{\sigma(j,k)}(x_{j})-(d-1)\prod_{j=1}^{d}\chi^{+}(x_{j}),

It is obvious that

\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{t})=0,\quad\text{when}\ |\boldsymbol{t}|\geqslant w.

Also we obtain that

\displaystyle\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{0}),

\displaystyle=d\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{d}\chi^{\sigma(j,k)}(x_{j})-\chi_{1}(x_{j})+\chi_{1}(x_{j})\bigg)\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle\qquad-(d-1)\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{d}\chi^{+}(x_{j})-\chi_{1}(x_{j})+\chi_{1}(x_{j})\bigg)\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle=d\big(2+\frac{1}{w}\big)^{d-1}\big(2-\frac{1}{w}\big)-(d-1)\big(2+\frac{1}{w}\big)^{d}=2^{d}+O(w^{-1}).

Let
T(\boldsymbol{x})
be the function

T(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\Gamma}g(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{y}).

We have the Fourier expansion

T(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{q}\in\Pi\\
|\boldsymbol{q}|<w\end{subarray}}c_{\boldsymbol{q}}\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{qx})

with

c_{\boldsymbol{q}}=\frac{1}{\det\Gamma}\intop\nolimits_{\mathcal{F}}T(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{qx})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x},

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the fundamental domain for
\Gamma
. By the definition of
T(x)
, it follows that

\displaystyle c_{\boldsymbol{q}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\det\Gamma}\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\Gamma}\intop\nolimits_{\mathcal{F}}g(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{y})\mathbf{e}(-\boldsymbol{qx})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\det\Gamma}\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\Gamma}\intop\nolimits_{\mathcal{F}+\boldsymbol{y}}g(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbf{e}(-\boldsymbol{qx}+\boldsymbol{qy})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}.

Since
\boldsymbol{q}\in\Pi
and
\Pi
is the polar lattice of
\Gamma
, we have
\boldsymbol{qy}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Noting that
\mathcal{F}+\boldsymbol{y}
is a translation of
\mathcal{F}
, we achieve that

c_{\boldsymbol{q}}=\frac{1}{\det\Gamma}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbf{e}(-\boldsymbol{qx})\textup{d}\boldsymbol{x}=\frac{1}{\det\Gamma}\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{x}).

Next, we continue to prove this theorem for the case that
a_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
.
In this case, we can suppose that

\mathcal{A}_{1}=\{1\leqslant n\leqslant N:a_{n}\geqslant 0\},\ \ \ \mathcal{A}_{2}=\{1\leqslant n\leqslant N:a_{n}<0\}.

One knows that
\alpha_{n}\in\mathcal{B}_{d}\pmod{\Gamma}
if and only if there exists a

\boldsymbol{y}\in\Gamma
such that

\chi_{d}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}+\boldsymbol{y})=1.

Then

(5.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}a_{n}\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\Gamma}\chi_{d}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}+\boldsymbol{y})+\sum_{n\in\mathcal{A}_{2}}a_{n}\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\Gamma}\chi_{d}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}+\boldsymbol{y}).

By the definition of
T(\boldsymbol{x})
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}a_{n}\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\Gamma}\chi_{d}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}+\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{n\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}a_{n}T(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n})

\displaystyle=c_{\boldsymbol{0}}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}a_{n}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{q}\in\Pi\\
0<|\boldsymbol{q}|<w\end{subarray}}c_{\boldsymbol{q}}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}a_{n}\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{q}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}).

Since the Fourier transform of any product
\prod_{j=1}^{d}\chi^{\pm}(x_{j})
is bounded in modulus by
(2+1/w)^{d}
, we have

\frac{|c_{\boldsymbol{p}}|}{c_{\boldsymbol{0}}}=\frac{\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{q})}{\hat{g}(\boldsymbol{0})}\leqslant\frac{(2d-1)(2+1/w)^{d}}{2^{d}+O(1/w)}\ll 1

for large
w
. Thus we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}a_{n}\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\Gamma}

\displaystyle\chi_{d}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}+\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle\geqslant(2^{d}+O(w^{-1}))\bigg(\sum_{n\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}a_{n}+O\Bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{q}\in\Pi\\
0<|\boldsymbol{q}|<w\end{subarray}}\Bigg|\sum_{n\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}a_{n}\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{q}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n})\bigg|\Bigg)\Bigg).

An exactly similar upper bound follows from

\chi_{d}(x)\leqslant\prod_{j=1}^{d}\chi^{+}(x_{j}),

and we conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}a_{n}\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\Gamma}

\displaystyle\chi_{d}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}+\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=(2^{d}+O(w^{-1}))\bigg(\sum_{n\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}a_{n}+O\Bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{q}\in\Pi\\
0<|\boldsymbol{q}|<w\end{subarray}}\Bigg|\sum_{n\leqslant N}a_{n}\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{q}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n})\bigg|\Bigg)\Bigg).

For the case that
n\in\mathcal{A}_{2}
,
applying the same method we can also obtain the above asymptotic formula. Therefore it follows from (
5.2
) that

(5.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}=\frac{1}{\det\Gamma}\bigg((2^{d}+O(w^{-1}))\sum_{n\leqslant N}a_{n}+O\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{q}\in\Pi\\
0<|\boldsymbol{q}|<w\end{subarray}}\bigg|\sum_{n\leqslant N}a_{n}\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{q}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n})\bigg|\bigg)\bigg).

If
f(n)
are complex weights, we write
a_{n}=\mu_{n}+\textup{i}\nu_{n}
, where
\textup{i}^{2}=-1
. We have

\mathcal{N}=\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant N}\mu_{n}+\textup{i}\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant N}\nu_{n}.

Continuing with the above process, we also conclude (
5.3
).
This completes the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

6.
The intersection of Piatetski-Shapiro sequences

For a given real
c
with
1<c<2
, we denote by
\mathbf{1}_{c}(\cdot)
the characteristic
function of numbers in the Piatetski-Shapiro sequence
\mathcal{N}^{c}
, i.e.,

\mathbf{1}_{c}(m)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\hbox{if\ }m\in\mathcal{N}^{c},\\
0,&\hbox{if\ }m\not\in\mathcal{N}^{c}.\end{array}\right.

A series of literature (for example, see
[
1
, Lemma 2]
) provides the following result.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
c\in(1,2)
and denote
\gamma=1/c
. Assume that
f
is an arithmetic
function such that
f(m)\ll m^{\varepsilon}
. Then for any real numbers
1\leqslant M_{1}<M_{2}
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{M_{1}<m\leqslant M_{2}}f(m)\mathbf{1}_{c}(m)

\displaystyle=\gamma\sum_{M_{1}<m\leqslant M_{2}}f(m)m^{\gamma-1}

\displaystyle+\sum_{M_{1}<m\leqslant M_{2}}f(m)\left(\psi(-(m+1)^{\gamma})-\psi(-m^{\gamma})\right)+O(1).

Here we give an analogue result when the variable belongs to the intersection of
Piatetski-Shapiro sequences.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
f

be an arithmetic function, and let
1<c_{1},\cdots,c_{d}<2
be real numbers.
Denote
\gamma_{j}=1/c_{j}
and
\sigma_{j}=1-\gamma_{j}
for
j=1,\cdots,d
.
Let
x>0
be a real number,
\mathcal{L}=\log x
,
1<a<1+\mathcal{L}^{-1}
and let

\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}(c_{1},\cdots,c_{d},f,x)=\sum_{x<m\leqslant ax}f(m)\mathbf{1}_{c_{1}}(m)\cdots\mathbf{1}_{c_{d}}(m).

We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{d}

\displaystyle x^{-(\delta_{1}+\cdots+\delta_{d})}\Bigg((1+O(\mathcal{L}^{-1}))\sum_{x<m\leqslant ax}f(m)

\displaystyle+O\Bigg(\mathop{\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}}_{|h_{j}|<\mathcal{L}x^{\delta_{j}}}\Bigg|\sum_{x<m\leqslant ax}f(m)\mathbf{e}\left(h_{1}m^{\gamma_{1}}+\cdots+h_{d}m^{\gamma_{d}}\right)\Bigg|\Bigg)\Bigg),

where
\boldsymbol{h}=(h_{1},\cdots,h_{d})
.

Such an asymptotic formula is derived from Baker
[
3
, P. 351]
,
who provided the corresponding result when
f(n)=\Lambda(n)
. In this section,
our main purpose is to show that it also holds for any arithmetic
function
f(n)
. We shall show some necessary definitions and properties regarding to our lattice method
in Section
5
and prove Proposition
6.2
. We also remark that our proof methods are mainly taken from Baker
[
3
, Section 2]
.

Proof of Proposition
6.2
.

We start by finding a necessary condition and a sufficient condition of the intersection of Piatetski-Shapiro sequences. Then by Theorem
2.1
, we obtain a lower bound and a upper bound of
\mathcal{M}
. By showing that the main terms of the lower bound and upper bound are the same, we finish the proof.

We first rewrite the sum
\mathcal{M}
as

\mathcal{M}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x<m\leqslant ax\\
m=\left\lfloor m_{1}^{c_{1}}\right\rfloor=\cdots=\left\lfloor m_{d}^{c_{d}}\right\rfloor\end{subarray}}f(m).

Here
m_{1},\cdots,m_{d}
are arbitrary integers. We have
m=\left\lfloor m_{1}^{c_{1}}\right\rfloor=\cdots=\left\lfloor m_{d}^{c_{d}}\right\rfloor
if and only if

m^{\gamma_{j}}\leqslant m_{j}<(m+1)^{\gamma_{j}}.

In this way, for any
j=1,\cdots,d
, we have

(6.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x<m\leqslant ax\\
m^{\gamma_{j}}\leqslant m_{j}<(m+1)^{\gamma_{j}}\end{subarray}}f(m).

Next we try to find a necessary condition of (
6.1
). Since
\gamma_{j}<1
for any
j=1,\cdots,d
, we note that

m^{\gamma_{j}}-(m+1)^{\gamma_{j}}>-\gamma_{j}x^{\gamma_{j}-1}.

It follows that

(6.2)

\displaystyle m^{\gamma_{j}}\leqslant m_{j}<(m+1)^{\gamma_{j}}\Rightarrow-\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{j}x^{\gamma_{j}-1}\leqslant m^{\gamma_{j}}+\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{j}x^{\gamma_{j}-1}-m_{j}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{j}x^{\gamma_{j}-1}

We apply theorem
2.1
to count sums of the weighted numbers of

x<m\leqslant ax
such that

\|z_{m,j}+t_{j}\|\leqslant v_{j}\ \ \ (j=1,\cdots,d),

where
\boldsymbol{z}_{m}=(z_{m,1},\cdots,z_{m,d})
are given points,

\max\limits_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}v_{j}<1/2

and
t_{1},\cdots,t_{d}
are fixed. Let

\Gamma=\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{v_{1}}\times\cdots\times\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{v_{d}},\ \ \ \Pi=\left\{(m_{1}v_{1},\cdots,m_{d}v_{d}):m_{1},\cdots,m_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\},

and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{m}=((z_{m,1}+t_{1})/v_{1},\cdots,(z_{m,d}+t_{d})/v_{d})
. Take

\displaystyle z_{m_{j}}=m^{\gamma_{j}},\quad t_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{j}x^{\gamma_{j}-1}-m_{j},\quad v_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{j}x^{\gamma_{j}-1}.

By (
6.2
), we apply Theorem
2.1
with the parameter
\omega=\mathcal{L}/4
and

\mathcal{N}=\mathop{\sum_{x<m\leqslant ax}}_{\|z_{m,j}+t_{j}\|\leqslant v_{j}}f(m),

it follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\leqslant\mathcal{N}=v_{1}\cdots v_{d}(2^{d}

\displaystyle+O(w^{-1}))\sum_{x<m\leqslant ax}f(m)

\displaystyle+O\Bigg(v_{1}\cdots v_{d}\mathop{\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}}_{|h_{j}|<\mathcal{L}x^{1-\gamma_{j}}}\Bigg|\sum_{x<m\leqslant ax}f(m)\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{z}_{m})\Bigg|\Bigg).

Now we give a sufficient condition of (
6.1
) by the same way. Also noting that for a suitable constant
b
, we have

m^{\gamma_{j}}-(m+1)^{\gamma_{j}}<-\gamma_{j}x^{\gamma_{j}-1}+b\mathcal{L}x^{\gamma_{j}-1}.

It follows that

(6.3)

\displaystyle m^{\gamma_{j}}\leqslant m_{j}<(m+1)^{\gamma_{j}}\Leftarrow-\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{j}x^{\gamma_{j}-1}+b\mathcal{L}x^{\gamma_{j}-1}\leqslant m^{\gamma_{j}}+\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{j}x^{\gamma_{j}-1}-m_{j}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{j}x^{\gamma_{j}-1}.

Using the same definitions of
\Gamma,\Pi,
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{m}
and taking

\displaystyle z_{m_{j}}=m^{\gamma_{j}},\quad t_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{j}x^{\gamma_{j}-1}-m_{j},\quad v_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{j}x^{\gamma_{j}-1}-b\mathcal{L}x^{\gamma_{j}-1},

it follows from Theorem
2.1
that

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\geqslant\mathcal{N}=v_{1}\cdots v_{d}(2^{d}

\displaystyle+O(w^{-1}))\sum_{x<m\leqslant ax}f(m)

\displaystyle+O\Bigg(v_{1}\cdots v_{d}\mathop{\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}}_{|h_{j}|<\mathcal{L}x^{1-\gamma_{j}}}\Bigg|\sum_{x<m\leqslant ax}f(m)\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{h}\boldsymbol{z}_{m})\Bigg|\Bigg).

Combining the upper bound and the lower bound, we finish the proof.

∎

7.
Primes in the intersection of Piatetski-Shapiro sequences

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is carried out
via the the Heath-Brown identity. Here one naturally encounters some bilinear forms
of the shape

\mathfrak{S}=\mathop{\sum_{0<|h_{j}|<X^{\delta_{j}}\mathcal{L}}}_{j=1,\cdots,d}\Bigg|\mathop{{\sum_{m\sim M}\sum_{n\sim N}}}_{mn\sim X}a_{m}b_{n}\mathbf{e}\left(h_{1}(mn)^{\gamma_{1}}+\cdots+h_{d}(mn)^{\gamma_{d}}\right)\Bigg|,

where
\mathcal{L}:=\log X
,

N\geqslant 1,M\geqslant 1,MN\asymp X
,
|a_{m}|\ll X^{\varepsilon}
and
|b_{n}|\ll X^{\varepsilon}

for any
\varepsilon>0
. If
b_{n}=1
or
b_{n}=\log n
, we call it a Type I sum and denote it as
\mathfrak{S}_{I}
; otherwise we call it as a Type II sum and denote it as
\mathfrak{S}_{II}
.
Applying Theorem
1.1
and Lemma
4.1
we can prove
the following estimates.

Proposition 7.1
.

Assume that
\varepsilon>0
is a
sufficiently small real number and
M<X^{29/42-2\sigma-8\varepsilon}
. Then we have

\mathfrak{S}_{I}\ll X^{1-\varepsilon}.

Proposition 7.2
.

Assume that
\varepsilon>0
is a
sufficiently small real number and
X^{117\sigma/20+30\varepsilon}<M<X^{1-2\sigma-10\varepsilon}
.
Then we have

\mathfrak{S}_{II}\ll X^{1-\varepsilon}.

7.1.
Proof of Proposition
7.1

Denote

\mathcal{T}=\sum_{m\sim M}\mathop{\sum_{n\sim N}}_{mn\sim X}a_{m}b_{n}\mathbf{e}\left(h_{1}(mn)^{\gamma_{1}}+\cdots+h_{d}(mn)^{\gamma_{d}}\right).

We divide the range
m\sim M
into the following two integer sets:

\mathcal{I}_{1}=\left\{m\sim M:\left|h_{1}X^{\gamma_{1}}\left(\frac{m}{M}\right)^{\gamma_{1}}+\cdots+h_{d}X^{\gamma_{d}}\left(\frac{m}{M}\right)^{\gamma_{d}}\right|<X^{2\sigma+10\varepsilon}\right\}

and

\mathcal{I}_{2}=\{m\sim M:m\notin\mathcal{I}_{1}\}.

Then we have

(7.1)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{I}=\mathop{\sum_{1<|h_{j}|<X^{\delta_{j}}\mathcal{L}}}_{j=1,\cdots,d}\mathcal{T}_{1}+\mathop{\sum_{1<|h_{j}|<X^{\delta_{j}}\mathcal{L}}}_{j=1,\cdots,d}\mathcal{T}_{2},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{1}=\mathop{\sum_{m\sim M}}_{m\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}\mathop{\sum_{n\sim N}}_{mn\sim X}a_{m}b_{n}\mathbf{e}\left(h_{1}(mn)^{\gamma_{1}}+\cdots+h_{d}(mn)^{\gamma_{d}}\right),

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{2}=\mathop{\sum_{m\sim M}}_{m\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}\mathop{\sum_{n\sim N}}_{mn\sim X}a_{m}b_{n}\mathbf{e}\left(h_{1}(mn)^{\gamma_{1}}+\cdots+h_{d}(mn)^{\gamma_{d}}\right).

We begin with

. Clearly,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{1}\ll X^{2\varepsilon}N\mathop{\sum_{m\sim M}}_{m\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}1\ll X^{2\varepsilon}N\#\mathcal{I}_{1},

where
\#\mathcal{I}_{1}
is the cardinality of
\mathcal{I}_{1}
.
It follows from Lemma
3.1
that

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{I}_{1}\ll\left(\frac{X^{2\sigma+10\varepsilon}}{|h_{1}X^{\gamma_{1}}|+\cdots+|h_{d}X^{\gamma_{d}}|}\right)^{1/(d-1)}M.

Then we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum_{1<|h_{j}|<X^{\delta_{j}}\mathcal{L}}}_{j=1,\cdots,d}\mathcal{T}_{1}\ll

\displaystyle X^{1+2\sigma/(d-1)+12\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\times\mathop{\sum_{1<|h_{j}|<X^{\delta_{j}}\mathcal{L}}}_{j=1,\cdots,d}\left(\left(|h_{1}|X^{\gamma_{1}}\right)^{-1/(d-1)}+\cdots+\left(|h_{d}|X^{\gamma_{d}}\right)^{-1/(d-1)}\right).

For every integer
1\leqslant j\leqslant d
we have

\sum_{|h_{j}|<X^{\delta_{j}}\log X}\left(|h_{j}|X^{\gamma_{j}}\right)^{-1/(d-1)}\ll X^{\delta_{j}-1/(d-1)}\mathcal{L}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum_{1<|h_{j}|<X^{\delta_{j}}\mathcal{L}}}_{j=1,\cdots,d}\mathcal{T}_{1}\ll X^{(d+1)\sigma/(d-1)-1/(d-1)+1+13\varepsilon}

Furthermore, since
\varepsilon>0
is a sufficiently small real number
and

(7.2)

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum_{1<|h_{j}|<X^{\delta_{j}}\mathcal{L}}}_{j=1,\cdots,d}\mathcal{T}_{1}\ll X^{1-\varepsilon}.

Now we estimate
\mathcal{T}_{2}
. Let

\Delta_{1}=\Bigg|\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}\gamma_{j}\cdots(\gamma_{j}-s+1)h_{j}X^{\gamma_{j}}\Bigg|N^{-1}.

The condition
m\in\mathcal{I}_{2}

\Delta_{1}\gg X^{2\sigma}N^{-1}
.
Applying a partial summation and

4.1
with
z_{1}=h_{1}m^{\gamma_{1}},\cdots,z_{d}=h_{d}m^{\gamma_{d}}

\displaystyle X^{-2\varepsilon}\mathcal{T}_{2}\ll\sum_{m\sim M}\left(\Delta_{1}^{\kappa}N^{\lambda}+\Delta_{1}^{-1}\right)\ll\Delta_{1}^{\kappa}X^{\lambda}M^{1-\lambda}+\Delta_{1}^{-1}XN^{-1}.

Therefore,

(7.3)

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum_{1<|h_{j}|<X^{\delta_{j}}\mathcal{L}}}_{j=1,\cdots,d}\mathcal{T}_{2}\ll X^{\lambda+\sigma+3\varepsilon}M^{1+\kappa-\lambda}+X^{1-\sigma+3\varepsilon}.

Inserting the exponent pair

(see Theorem 6 by Bourgain
[

]
) into (

.
This proves Proposition
7.1
.

7.2.
Proof of Proposition
7.2

For convenience, we continue to use the notation

\mathcal{T}_{2}=\mathop{\sum_{m\sim M}}_{m\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}\mathop{\sum_{n\sim N}}_{mn\sim X}a_{m}b_{n}\mathbf{e}\left(h_{1}(mn)^{\gamma_{1}}+\cdots+h_{d}(mn)^{\gamma_{d}}\right).

Following the proof of Theorem
7.1

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{II}=\mathop{\sum_{1<|h_{j}|<X^{\delta_{j}}\mathcal{L}}}_{j=1,\cdots,d}\mathcal{T}_{2}+O\left(X^{1-\varepsilon}\right).

We also remark that
b_{n}\ll X^{\varepsilon}
in this case.

Denote

\Delta_{2}=\Bigg|\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant d}\gamma_{j}\cdots(\gamma_{j}-s+1)h_{j}X^{\gamma_{j}}\Bigg|.

The condition
n\in\mathcal{I}_{2}
implies that
\Delta_{2}\gg X^{2\sigma+10\varepsilon}
.
Applying Theorem
1.1
with
E_{j}=X^{\gamma_{j}}h_{j}

and

(see Page 58 in
[

]

\displaystyle X^{-3\varepsilon}\mathcal{T}_{2}\ll M^{1/2}X^{1/2}+\Delta_{2}^{-1/2}X+M^{-73/234}X+\Delta_{2}^{11/78}M^{-20/117}X^{67/78}.

Hence,

\displaystyle X^{-4\varepsilon}\mathop{\sum_{1<|h_{j}|<X^{\delta_{j}}\mathcal{L}}}_{j=1,\cdots,d}\mathcal{T}_{2}\ll M^{1/2}X^{1/2+\sigma}+X^{1-5\varepsilon}+M^{-73/234}X^{1+\sigma}+M^{-20/117}X^{1+\sigma}.

Since
M>X^{117\sigma/20+30\varepsilon}
and
