DIAPH1 regulates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway resulting in microcephaly and visual impairment
Authors: Dayan Wang, Panjian Lai, Kan Wang, Zhuanbin Wu, Xiaobing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBiallelic DIAPH1 mutations are linked to hereditary microcephaly syndrome, yet the underlying pathogenic mechanism remains unelucidated. This study aimed to clarify how DIAPH1 biallelic mutations cause microcephaly and visual impairment, focusing on the gene’s regulatory role in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway.MethodsWhole exome sequencing was performed on a patient’s peripheral blood to identify DIAPH1 mutations. A zebrafish model was established by microinjecting mutant human DIAPH1 cDNA into one-cell embryos (no zebrafish DIAPH1 homolog exists). Phenotypic analyses (morphology, neuronal axon growth, behavior) and quantitative real-time PCR for Wnt/β-catenin pathway genes were conducted. Data were mean ± SEM; statistical tests (Student’s t-test, ANOVA, χ²) used GraphPad Prism 5.0 (P < 0.05, P < 0.0001 for significance).ResultsCompound heterozygous DIAPH1 mutations (c.1051 C > T, p.R351X; c.609delA, p.E203E fs*19) were found and associated with clinical symptoms. Mutant DIAPH1 zebrafish showed abnormal eye shape, shortened body length, axis defects, impaired motor axon growth, reduced locomotor activity, upregulated WNT8A, WNT9A, LRP5, LRP6, and downregulated AXIN1, AXIN2, β-CATENIN, indicating excessive Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation.ConclusionsDIAPH1 compound heterozygous mutations may trigger microcephaly and visual impairment by abnormally activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. The zebrafish model provides a reliable in vivo system for studying DIAPH1-related microcephaly, advancing understanding of hereditary primary microcephaly pathogenesis and potential therapeutic target exploration.

Background

Microcephaly primary hereditary (MCPH) disorder is a kind of neurodevelopmental disorder displaying significantly reduced skull, occipital frontal lobe, and head circumference. The reduced brain volume was particularly pronounced in the cerebral cortex part, which is associated with mental retardation. Gulhan et al., 2014, discovered that mutation of DIAPH1 was correlated with the autosomal recessive microcephaly syndrome [1]. Five children from the intermarriage family with homozygous nonsense variation in the gene of DIAPH1 (c.2332C4, p.Q778X) developed Microcephaly, blindness, seizures, bronchiectasis, and growth retardation. Gulhan et al., 2014 studies show reduced transcription levels of DIAPH1 followed by its reduced protein levels confirmed through western blot [1]. In addition, homozygous mutations (including c.2769delT, p.F923fs and c.3145 C > T, p.R1049X) were identified in four subjects from two unrelated intermarriage families in another study. These subjects displayed microcephaly, early-onset epilepsy, severe impairment in vision and lungs infection [2].

In this study, we report on a 22-month-old girl who manifested epileptic seizures at two months after birth and was hospitalized with repeated severe lungs infection at six months of age. Furthermore, she also displayed visual impairment, microcephaly, and motor retardation. The whole exome sequencing results revealed compound heterozygous mutations in DIAPH1 (c.1051 C > T, p.R351X,922; c.609delA, p.E203E fs*19), and were correlated with the complex syndrome.

The animal model of microcephaly caused by this gene mutation has only been implemented in mice. DIAPH1 knockdown mice show only lateral ventricle enlargement but no microcephaly or agenesis of the corpus callosum, etc. Therefore, further animal experiments are needed to investigate the mechanism of microcephaly caused by DIAPH1 biallelic mutation [1]. Due to the characteristics of zebrafish, such as relatively semi-transparent embryos, rapid growth, ease of gene manipulation, and a simplified vascular development process, this model allows for convenient observation of relatively significant changes occurring in the phenotype [3–5]. In 2016, Punwani et al. successfully inserted the human gene BCL11B (p.N441K) mutant in zebrafish to study Severe Combined Immunodeficiency (SCID) disorder, and elucidated a previously unknown disorder related to the function of BCL11B related to the regulation of positioning as well as movement of hematopoietic progenitors [6]. In 2018, Wang et al. first time confirmed through in vivo zebrafish experiments that the human TIE2 gene mutation R849W would lead to tail vein fusion, maxillofacial skeletal defects, eye development defects, and other phenotypes in zebrafish [7]. Therefore, we proposed that the zebrafish model can provide a reliable and concise in vivo study system for a more direct and rapid study of the mechanism of the DIAPH1 point mutation. As there is no DIAPH1 homolog in zebrafish, we transfected the zebrafish with the mutated human DIAPH1. Mutated zebrafish exhibited abnormal eye shape (small eyes), short body length, dorsal and ventral axis development defects, body axis bending, abnormal peripheral neuronal motor axons growth, and limited behavioral activities. Furthermore, we investigated that these phenotypes might be correlated with excessive activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Therefore, based on our findings we conclude that the Wnt/β-catenin signaling upregulated by the mutated DIAPH1 gene resulted in microcephaly and visual impairment. As zebrafish model has been used for the first time to study the effect of mutated DIAPH1 gene and its relationship with the regulation of Wnt/β-catenin pathway therefore, this study will be helpful in understanding the pathogenesis of primary microcephaly hereditary (MPPCH) disorder.

Methods

Patient information and informed consent

The Ethics Committee of Jinhua Central Hospital approved the research plan (Ethics No. 2020-QT-23). Informed consent was signed by the family after the initial clinical evaluation. All the family members knew about the study including the toddler’s father and mother, and they agreed to use the data for research purposes.

The patient was a 22-month-old toddler girl born to non-consanguineous healthy parents. She had intrauterine growth retardation before the (G1P1) vaginal delivery. Her birth weight was 3.0 kg, and her length was 49 centimeters. She was hospitalized in infancy (2 months after birth) as she was reported to have more than 30 convulsions in three days. Physical examination revealed 39 cm (-1.9 SD) of head circumference and low muscle tone. The head MRI test was regular. Electroencephalogram (EEG) indicated epileptic discharge in the left occipital region, posterior temporal region, and right occipital region, which was diagnosed as “epilepsy.” However, the patient still showed recurrent seizures after oral medication with many kinds of antiepileptic drugs, including sodium valproate oral liquid, topiramate tablets, and nitrazepam. Fever usually induced convulsions.

Moreover, the patient, from infancy to toddler, that is, from the age of six to twenty months, have had seven pulmonary infections, including two times of severe pneumonia. Her eyes remained in a closed state for most of the time, besides she also showed unresponsive eye movements. Fundus examination indicated that the patient had a pale optic disc and could only recognize the light. The girl had normal hearing. She also showed growth retardation, i.e., head circumference of 40.2 cm (-4.8 SD), body length of 82 cm (-0.5 SD) and body weight of 10 kg (-0.7 SD). The patient was unable to walk and stood only with aid besides showing unconscious vocalization.

Experimental apparatus

The names and models of experimental apparatus used in this study are given here; Fluorescence microscope (SMZ-18, Nikon, Light microscope (SMZ-745, Nikon), Microinjection (MN-153 NARISHIGE, Microinjection pump (FemtoJet, Eppendorf), Behavioral traces of zebrafish (Noldus DanioVision System, Noldus), Light constant temperature incubator (GZP-250 S).

Exome sequencing

Peripheral blood samples were obtained from the subject and the parents. DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (DP348) (Qiagen) was utilized to extract the genomic DNA. The extracted genomic DNA was sonicated into fragments randomly and purified. Genome-wide exons were captured using Roche SeqCap EZ MedExome Kit, followed by library preparation. We used Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer for high-throughput sequencing (PE150). Target sequencing coverage was more extensive than 99% performed by Zhiyin Oriental Translational Medicine Research Center, China. Clean data was obtained from Raw data after removing joints, low-quality reads, and other quality controls. Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) was used to map the sequences to the reference genome (hg19) (http://hgdownload.soe.ucsc.edu/goldenPath/hg19/bigZips/)(NG_021242.2). Then the mapping results were analyzed by SAMtools (version 0.1.18) and Picard (http://picard.sourceforge.net/) (version 1.91) to classify and convert the short sequences and remove redundant information, respectively. The SNP and Indel sites were obtained by Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) software (version 2.6-4). Anavar software (version 2013, August 23) was then used to analyze the functional annotation of mutation sites. Proven was assessed to predict the protein structure for of single base variation.

Bioinformatics analysis

Quality control: Raw data were cleaned after adapters removing, low-quality reads filtering and other quality control protocol.

b.Variants calling: The clean data were aligned to the NCBI human reference genome (hg18) using BWA and variants were called using GATK. Samtools and Pindel were used to call SNPs (Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms) and indels, respectively. The clean data were than filtered, according to the quality of the sequencing, for further protocol.

c.Variants annotation and prediction: Nonsynonymous substitutions and SNPs with MAF (Minor Allele Frequency) lower than 5% were filtered using SIFT. The function of mutated genes and their pathogenicity were then analyzed referencing to dbSNP, 1000 Genomes Project, ExAC, ESP, OMIM, Swiss-var, HGMD, ClinVar and other disease databases.

d.The variants with unknown pathogenicity of single bases were screened by Provean, SIFT, Polyphen2-HVAR, Polyphen2-HDIV, Mutationtster and other protein structure prediction software. MaxEntScan were used to screen potential splice sites.

Zebrafish care and maintenance

Adult zebrafish were kept in an incubator (28.5 ℃) under the condition of 14 h light while 10 h dark cycle). Zebrafish (5–6 pairs) were set to mate naturally each time, which generated fertilized eggs. Sources were kept at 28.5℃ in fish water, which was made by dissolving Instant Ocean Salt in deionized water (0.2%). The embryos were rinsed and staged as per the previously reported method [4]. The HuC-EGFP transgenic lines were established and characterized as previously described [8]. The zebrafish facility of SMOC (Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc.) is accredited by the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC) International. (Accreditation number is required here).

Zebrafish microinjections

For over-expression assay, one-cell embryos following fertilization were injected with 100 pg pcDNA3 comprising human DIAPH1 cDNA (mutant) to achieve 50 ng/µL. Sangon Biotech performed the synthesis of the coding region of the DIAPH1. Then DNA segment was subcloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector (Invitrogen). Following these previous steps, all embryos were kept at 28.5 ℃. At 2-dpf. 0.016% MS-222 (tricaine methanesulfonate, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was utilized to anesthetize embryos. Then the zebrafish was placed on the outer side (anterior, left; posterior, right; dorsal, top), dorsal, or ventral side, and fix it on a recessed glass slide with 3% methylcellulose for visualizing using a fluorescence microscope. Phenotypes of the whole body, trunk, head, as well as brain were assessed.

Zebrafish neurons studies

To examine the formation of spinal motor neuron in zebrafish, one-cell HuC-GFP transgenic lines embryos following fertilization were injected with a control vector (empty vector) and a mutant human DIAPH1 over-expressing vector. At 2-dpf, seeds were dechorionated. 0.016% MS-222 was utilized to anesthetize embryos. Then the zebrafish was placed on the outer side and fixed the same way as embryos before visualization. The phenotypes of the brain and spinal motor neurons were determined.

Quantitative real-time PCR

As per the manufacturer’s protocols, Trizol (Roche) was utilized to extract total RNA from 30 to 50 embryos in each group. PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara) was utilized to perform reverse transcription of RNA. cDNA levels were quantitatively measured utilizing Bio-rad IQ SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-rad) through Realplex system (Eppendorf). All measurements were conducted in triplicates. Relative RNA levels were quantitively calculated using the comparative threshold cycle method (2 − ΔΔCt), while the gene of ef1α was deployed as endogenous control. Behavioral analysis

At 5-dpf, the following experiments were conducted. At 5-dpf, the larvae were allocated into WT control (empty vector), DIAPH1-MT1, and DIAPH1-MT2.Larvae were gathered, rinsed, and seeded in 96-well plates. One larva was placed in 0.2 mL of fish water per well, with ten such wells (10 larvae total) constituting one group. Then behavioral tests were carried out. At first, the larvae were allowed to have an adaptation time of 15 min before their movement was monitored [9]. Secondly, the larvae were set for free exposure to the aquarium for 30 min. Locomotion tracking was carried out utilizing a camera placed above the plate. Ethovision XT software (Noldus Information Technology, Wageningen, Netherlands) was utilized for analysis of all digital tracks with the system noise filtered out by setting a smallest value of moving distance (0.2 mm). The total length of movement, speed, mobility, and maximum acceleration were recorded.

Image acquisition

Nikon SMZ 18 Fluorescence microscope was utilized to examine embryos and larvae. Photographs were taken utilizing digital cameras. Adobe Photoshop 7.0 software (Adobe, San Jose, California) was utilized to optimize the image of the expression patterns with adjustments in levels, luminance, contrast, hue, and saturation. Image-based morphometric Analysis (NIS-Elements D4.6, Japan) and ImageJ software (US National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA; http://rsbweb.nih.gov/ij/) was were utilized for quantitative image analysis processing. Processing was performed based on inverted fluorescent images. Ten animals for each treatment were quantified, and the total signal per animal was calculated.

Statistical analysis

All data in this study is expressed as mean ± SEM. GraphPad Prism 5.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) was utilized to carry out statistical analysis and graphical representation display of the data. Student’s t-test, ANOVA, or χ2 test was utilized to evaluate the statistical significance. Statistical significance in the study is expressed as *, P < 0.05, and ***, P < 0.0001.

Results

Whole exome sequencing (WES) and sanger sequencing

The genomic DNA, extracted from the peripheral blood of the patient, was sequenced using WES. Results suggested that the patient has a compound heterozygous mutation in the DIAPH1, which is located on chromosome 5 (c.1051 C > T, exon11, p.R351X,922; c.609delA, p.E203E fs*19). The DIAPH1 (c.1051 C > T) mutation was inherited from the mother. Such variation could affect the gene product, including conservation and protein structure, which is predicted by software of both MutationTaster GERP phyloP20way and phastCons20way (MAF < 0.05). In addition, the other variation of c.609delA in the DIAPH1 was inherited from her father. However, this variation could result in Gene dysfunction (MAF < 0.05). This mutated proband was heterozygous, following the pathogenesis of complex heterozygous genetic diseases caused by autosomal recessive inheritance (AR). The phenotypes and genotypes of the proband and her family members were separated as shown in Fig. 1.

Overexpression of mutant human DIAPH1 causes microcephaly and curved body axis in Zebrafish

The fertilized single-cell embryos of zebrafish were injected with the mutated cDNA of DIAPH1. At 2-dpf, we observed the phenotypic changes of various body sections of the transfected zebrafish, including body, head, and brain, using fluorescence microscope (Fig. 2). However, a large number of transfected zebrafish either died (Fig. 2p) or showed developmental defects (Fig. 2). The transfected zebrafish mainly died at 6-hpf. The most significant death rate was discovered in the group of DIAPH1-MT2 (79.87%, 238/298), followed by the group of DIAPH1-MT1 (64.81%, 175/270), while the control group showed the lowest death rate (4.31%, 10/232).

The transfected zebrafish of the two groups were significantly more symptomatic than the control group, indicating that the mutated DIAPH1 was correlated with the high death rates. The death rate of the zebrafish at 72-hpf was similar to 24-hpf. Next, we determined the developmental defects rate of the control group and that of the experimental groups. Our findings revealed that the developmental defects rate of DIAPH1-MT1 (16.30%, 44/270) and DIAPH1-MT2 (9.40%, 28/298) were significantly higher than the WT-Control group. We also observed developmental malformation in the head and eyeball, shortened body length, and lateral bending of the body axis at 2-dpf (Fig. 2, E, I, D0, G0, blue arrow). The average cross-sectional area of the head in the WT-Control group was 18.70 ± 0.5609’ 104 µm2 (n = 10), while only 8.312 ± 1.205’ 104 µm2 (n = 10) in a group of DIAPH1-MT1 and 8.593 ± 1.253’ 104 µm2 (n = 10) in a group of DIAPH1-MT2. Therefore, the cross-sectional area of the head in the experimental group was significantly higher than the WT-Control group.

We also determined the eyeball malformation of all three groups (Fig. 2, E, I, G, K, D0, G0, black arrows). The average eyeball diameter of WT-Control was 0.2640 ± 0.0097 mm (n = 10), while the groups of DIAPH1-MT1 and DIAPH1-MT2 were 0.1560 ± 0.0135 mm (n = 10), and 0.1520 ± 0.0092 mm (n = 10) at 2-dpf, respectively. The statistical analysis indicated mutated zebrafish to have significantly reduced eyeball diameter (p < 0.0001) as compared to WT-Control.

For the evaluation of the growth development of the zebrafish, we measured the body length of all three groups. The average body length of both DIAPH1-MT1 and DIAPH1-MT2 were significantly reduced in comparison with the WT-Control, indicating the developmental retardation of the mutated zebrafish (Fig. 1, E-L). The mutated zebrafish also showed defects in the dorsal-ventral axis (Fig. 2, E-I).

Overexpression of mutant human DIAPH1 impairs neuronal outgrowth

To evaluate the development of motor neuronal cells in the spinal cord of zebrafish, we deployed the zebrafish transfected with HuC-GFP for proper visualization. Results showed the developmental defects in axons of spinal motor neurons, disordered arrangement of neurons, and shortened length of axial diagram (Fig. 3). The motor neurons length of both the DIAPH1-MT1 and DIAPH1-MT2 were significantly reduced compared with the WT-Control (Fig. 3. M, p < 0.0001).

Locomotor capacity is reduced in DIAPH1 mutated zebrafish

Next, we evaluated the intellectual and motor development status based on the behavior of the zebrafish at 5-dpf. Through the analysis of digital trajectory and heat map image, it was concluded that the locomotor capacity of the WT-Control was higher than the mutated groups (Fig. 4, A, B). The movement distance of WT-Control was significantly higher than the groups of DIAPH1-MT1 and DIAPH1-MT2, indicating the reduced movement distance of the mutated zebrafish (Fig. 4C, p < 0.0001). As expected, the velocity (Fig. 4D, p < 0.0001), mobility (Fig. 4E, p < 0.0001) and Maximum acceleration (Fig. 4F, p < 0.0001) of the groups of DIAPH1-MT1 and DIAPH1-MT2 were also significantly reduced in comparison with that of the WT-Control.

DIAPH1 mutation activated Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway

Wnt/β-catenin plays a critical role in the construction of the embryo’s somatic axis, development of the dorsal-ventral axis, and regulation of the proliferation of retinal reinserted cells in zebrafish. Wnt ligand firstly, binds to Frizzled receptor and co-receptor of lipoprotein receptor-related protein 6 (LRP6) consisting of a heterotrimeric complex and then recruits the signaling complex to activate the receptor complex. After that, the depolymerized β-catenin degrades the heterotrimeric complex, thus effectively inhibiting the degradation of β-catenin. Subsequently, the stabled β-catenin enters the nucleus to bind the transcription factor of TCF/LEF [10]. Therefore, we determined gene expression changes of the genes involved in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway among the three groups of the zebrafish. The results of RT-PCR analysis showed that the expression levels of WNT8A, WNT9A, LRP5, LRP6, MYCN, and LEF1 were significantly augmented in the group of DIAPH1-MT1 and DIAPH1-MT2 causes more potent morphological defects, more severe eye defects (Fig. 2, H0-I0) ,lower survival rate (Fig. 2,O-P) and more impact on the Wnt pathway activation, the upregulation of the LRP co-receptors (lrp5 and lrp6) and the transcription factor (lef1) and target gene( mycn) campared with DIAPH1-MT1 (Fig. 5). These results indicated that the Wnt signaling pathway was excessively activated in the mutated zebrafish.

Discussion

Since 2014, biallelic mutation in DIAPH1 has been identified in 4 families. In addition to the case of this study, the other 3 were the immediate families (Table 3). Similar to the other nine patients, the girl in our case had symptoms of microcephaly(HP:0000252), early-onset of seizures (HP:0011152), visual impairment (HP:0000505), growth retardation (HP:0001510), and repeated pulmonary infections (HP:0006532). Gülhan Ercan-Sencicek et al. [11] investigated the DIAPH1 protein distribution of the inner ear and nervous system in embryonic mice and found a large allocation of the DIAPH1 protein in peripheral nerves and auditory centers, myelin components of auditory nerves in Schwann cells, and oligodendrocytes (including the progenitor cells of the forebrain, brainstem, and inferior ventricle). The mice with defected Diaph1 only showed enlarged lateral ventricles without microcephaly or cortical dysplasia. Therefore, the defects in the myelin sheath of the nervous system might be caused by the homozygous DIAPH1 mutation.

In this study, we deployed the model animal of zebrafish to explore the gene function of human DIAPH1. Results showed that the mutated zebrafish had increased death and malformation rates. The mutated phenotypes mainly showed symptoms, such as microcephaly, abnormal ocular shape (small eyes), short body length, defective dorsal, and ventral axis development, body axis bending, and abnormal peripheral neuron motor axons growth. In addition, the zebrafish’s behavioral tracking revealed the reduced locomotor function of mutated groups. Based on our results we propose that the mutation of DIAPH1 (c.1051 C > T, p.R351X, 922; c.609delA, p.E203E fs*19) might be the cause of the syndrome of microcephaly and visual impairment. Compared to MT1, MT2 causes more severe eye defects and higher mortality rates. It is speculated that this is because the mutation type of MT2 is a frameshift mutation, which has a greater impact on the structure of functional proteins than MT1, which is the missense mutation.

The Wnt signaling pathway is a major critical machinery accounting for the regulation during the early development in zebrafish. Meanwhile, Wnt8a is one of the primary ligands of the Wnt pathway and a key candidate participating in dorsal axis specification in zebrafish. In a previous study injections containing excessive wnt8 mRNA was administrated to the embryos of zebrafish, compared to uninjected embryos, 55–79% of the embryos were not able to produce typical anterior structures, developed one or two eyes (with decreased eye cup size and/or absent lens), and lost the prominent midbrain-hindbrain boundary [12, 13]. As a terminal executant and core downstream molecule involved in the Wnt pathway, β-catenin is a cadherin-associated protein expressed maternally in zebrafish. Unphosphorylated β-catenin translocate into the nucleus, where it regulates the transcriptional activity of target genes through interactions with molecules of the TCF/LEF1 family of transcription factors [14]. The mutated zebrafish showed cerebral palsy, small eyes, and developmental defects in the dorsal-ventral axis. Such phenotypes were associated with the excessively activated Wnt-mediated signaling pathway [15–17]. Wnt plays a role in the process of cell proliferative growth of cell, cell polarity, and the determination of cell destiny during embryonic development, and homeostatic status of tissues. Wnt regulates the amount of the transcription coactivators, β-catenin, which controls the expression of critical genes responsible for the product [18]. Before or during the process of gastrula formation, the excessively activated Wnt/β-catenin signaling inhibited the appearance of the forebrain, eye, and retina [19, 20].

Consequently, we examined the mRNA levels of the genes participating in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling by RT-PCR. We found that the genes of wnt8a, wnt9a, lrp5, and lrp6 were upregulated, while axin1, axin2, and β-catenin presented with suppressed expression in the mutated zebrafish in comparison with the WT-control. The transcript levels of genes wnt8a and wnt9a were increased at the pre-embryonic stage in the mutated zebrafish, inhibiting the forebrain and eye formation. On the other hand, the increased expression levels of lrp5 and lrp6 resulted in the reduced expression of axin1, axin2, and β-catenin, which subsequently caused dysplastic forebrain, defects in ocular, retinal dysplasia, and axial in the mutated zebrafish. It has been demonstrated that DIAPH1 is involved in cell migration, which is critical for cytoskeletal dynamics, required to slow the polymerization and de-polymerization of actin, enhance microtubules stability, and facilitate the migratory behavior of cell [21, 22]. We searched for a target protein that interacts with DIAPH1 protein (Fig. 6) according to https://string-db.org/cgi/network.pl?taskId=5ptXjCF51F0K. However, further investigations are warranted to elucidate in detail that the protein DIA1 drives the Wnt signaling pathway.

We propose that the compound heterozygous mutations of DIAPH1 (c.1051 C > T, p.R351X, 922; c.609delA, p.E203E fs*19) could be the cause of the microcephaly and visual impairment. Moreover, such mutation probably activated the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, resulting in Microcephaly’s phenotypes and visual impairment. In the future, we will further dissect the apoptosis, demyelination, and optic nerve development of central neurons in the mutated zebrafish by pathology method. We will also develop an in vitro system using the human cells to illustrate the protein level changes of the genes altered by the mutated DIAPH1.

Conclusions

This study identifies compound heterozygous DIAPH1 mutations (c.1051 C > T and c.609delA) as the genetic basis for a complex neurodevelopmental syndrome featuring microcephaly, epilepsy, and visual impairment. By establishing the first zebrafish model through overexpression of mutant human DIAPH1, we successfully recapitulated core clinical phenotypes, including microcephaly and impaired locomotor capacity. Our model reveals that the c.609delA frameshift mutation induces more severe developmental defects than the c.1051 C > T nonsense mutation, suggesting a mutation-dependent impact on protein function. Mechanistically, DIAPH1 mutations trigger excessive activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Upregulation of key ligands (WNT8A, WNT9A) and co-receptors (LRP5, LRP6), coupled with the downregulation of regulators (AXIN1, AXIN2), disrupts anterior embryonic patterning and neurodevelopment. Unlike previous knockdown mouse models that failed to exhibit microcephaly, this zebrafish system provides a robust platform for studying DIAPH1-related disorders. These findings elucidate the pathogenic role of DIAPH1 in microcephaly and establish the DIAPH1-regulated Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascade as a potential therapeutic target.
