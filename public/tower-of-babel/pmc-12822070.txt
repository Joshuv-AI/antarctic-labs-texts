Establishing criteria and distributing conference grants for participants from the Global South: experiences from the Canadian Conference on Global Health 2024
Authors: Alisha Gauhar, Margaret J Mutumba-Nakalembe, Colleen M Davison, Michelle Amri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe annual Canadian Conference on Global Health (CCGH) provides a platform for global health professionals and students from various sectors and disciplines to share knowledge and innovations in research, policy, and practice. However, known barriers disproportionately faced by participants from the Global South (PGS) often result in a lower representation, attendance, and participation of PGS in conferences such as the CCGH. Recognizing these barriers and to promote greater conference equity, the Canadian Association for Global Health (CAGH) with funding from the International Development Research Centre created a scholarship programme for PGS to provide full and partial funding to attend the CCGH. This paper details the six-step process used for the creation of the PGS scholarship criteria, as well as considerations for selecting recipients, methods for scoring applications, and processes for distributing the scholarships. The criteria and rubric designed provides a foundation for a reproducible resource that can be scaled up for other international conferences and can be applied to other groups underrepresented in conference settings.

Introduction

In October 2024, the Canadian Association for Global Health (CAGH) hosted the 30th annual Canadian Conference on Global Health (CCGH) with the theme of “Poly-crisis and global health: How can we improve human health and well-being, while protecting the planet?” [1]. The conference provides a platform for global health professionals and students from a variety of sectors and disciplines to share knowledge and innovations in research, policy, and practice, and has been praised for its diverse themes, global representation of speakers, and commitment to bilingualism [2]. The CAGH convenes, partners, and champions for global health equity. It is a membership-based organization where individual and institutional members can engage, access networks and resources, and participate in discussions among those interested in improving global health, health equity, and Canada’s role in addressing critical global health priorities [3]. There are, however, known barriers disproportionately faced by participants from the Global South (PGS), which result in a lower representation of PGS in the attendance and participation of global health conferences [4]. Barriers include most conferences being hosted in the global north (i.e., high-income countries), a lack of online options for participation and associated costs for attending [5]. Irrespective of previous advocacy [6], gaps still remain.

Failure to address these gaps and barriers is inconsistent with the very definition of 'global health' as an area of study, research, and practice that prioritizes improving health and achieving health equity for all people worldwide [7]. As the global north continues exerting control and influence over health policies and interventions, research agendas, and funding priorities in the global south, greater attention is needed to “decolonize global health governance” and focus on incorporating global south knowledge systems and community priorities [8, 10, 11]. The need to address barriers is also crucial as PGS face a disproportionately higher global disease burden than the global north, and yet, are underrepresented in academic articles, programmes, and conferences [10, 12]. Efforts to decolonize global health systems, including platforms to advance knowledge translation, such as conferences, cannot be achieved without active participation from PGS whose countries continue to feel the lingering effects of colonial practices [8]. Amri et al. found that almost 75% of all included articles discussing and defining decolonizing global health are dominated by voices from or affiliated with the global north, continuing the same power imbalance that decolonization aims to dismantle [9]. In recognizing these barriers, and to promote greater conference equity, the CAGH created a scholarship programme for PGS to provide at least some additional opportunities for enhanced financial support to attend the CCGH.

Specific funding for PGS was granted in the amount of $30 000 (CAD) from the International Development Research Center (IDRC) for international travel and conference registration for participants and speakers. The planning committee aimed to design a meritocratic process for the selection and allocation of funding, undertaking a six-step process to create and distribute a scholarship for PGS applicants. This article details the CAGH’s creation and administration of a rubric to simplify the selection and allocation process for their scholarship for PGS applicants. The rubric deliverable establishes criteria that can serve as a guide for other international conference planning committees to increase PGS inclusivity and reduce conference inequity.

Methods

An approach to distributing awards was determined by the planning committee who undertook a six-step process for the creation of the PGS scholarship. The six steps were (i) determining the number of individuals who could be funded and the funding allocation process, (ii) developing the rubric criteria, (iii) consultations and revisions, (iv) a call for applications, (v) candidate scoring and (vi) allocating funding. The method’s process is visualized in a decision tree (Fig. 1) and each of these steps are detailed below. The key criteria and considerations for weighing applications are outlined in detail within the Results section of this article.

Step 1: determining the number of individuals who could be funded and the funding allocation process

An estimate for the average cost (CAD) for PGS applicants on the basis of price breakdowns from the previous CCGHs was used to set the budget for the number of individuals who could be funded with the funding provided. This included the cost of registration, average cost of airfare, visa fees, cost of accommodation at the conference hotel, and standard per diem. The estimated cost was $4300 CAD per recipient, and the breakdown is detailed in Table 1. Cost variability for each participant was expected owing to factors such as differentiated flight pricing on the basis of the participant’s geographic location, whether the participant required partial or full funding, the participant’s attendance being in-person or virtual, whether the participant was eligible for CAGH membership registration pricing, and whether visas to travel to Canada had been secured. On the basis of cost estimates, it was determined that IDRC funding would allow seven individuals at $4500 per recipient to be funded with full costs covered, 45 in-person registration fees only (25 CAGH members and 20 non-members) and 120 virtual registration fees only (80 CAGH members and 40 non-members). Although the full costs estimated were determined by previous conference price breakdowns, the number of in-person registration and virtual scholarships were allocated to maximize conference participation and flexibility of attendance. The lower virtual attendance costs and flexible nature of online attendance was prioritized to maximize the number and diversity of conference participants. Allocation breakdowns by CAGH membership preferred registered members, without exclusion of non-members, and with flexibility to change funding allocation ratios depending on the number of CAGH members that applied for funding.

Step 2: developing the rubric criteria

Once the number of scholarships that could be funded in each category were determined, a subsection of the conference planning committee was tasked with creating a proposal for the breakdown of PGS grants, grant creation, and allocation. Grant breakdowns were designed to maximize conference attendance, with sufficient virtual and in-person attendees. The proposal suggested a timeline and general methods for the call for applications and scoring via rubric criteria. This proposal was drafted by the scholarship planning committee and discussed in planning committee meetings for feedback, before developing the rubric. Considerations when selecting the members of this scholarship creation team included wide geographic representation and diversity among the professional backgrounds of members (including students) to afford more fulsome considerations for criteria development. Discussions among the scholarship creation team members led to a consensus on five key criteria and five considerations for assessing applicant eligibility and per person funding, with particular emphasis on the funding needs of the applicant and the relevance of their work in relation to the conference theme [1]. A draft rubric was designed to contain both analytic and holistic criteria, broken down into scoring levels with weightings. Considerations as to how rubric criteria questions would be formatted into the online application form were noted to ensure ease-of-use and clarity for applicants.

Step 3: consultations and revisions

After the scholarship creation team discussed key criteria and considerations, the draft rubric was presented to the full conference planning committee for consultation. During the consultations, the proposed criteria were discussed and feedback was recorded. This consultation was conducted to ensure criteria were comprehensive and incorporated the perspectives and experiences of committee members. Criteria rationale was provided and discussions about the necessity, wording, and fairness of each question was considered and discussed until consensus was reached. Time for additional feedback was given before a final vote on criteria, allowing for the revision and presentation of the final version of the rubric.

Step 4: a call for applications

Upon completing consultations with the conference planning committee, a call for applications was administered on social media platforms through official CAGH accounts on platforms such as Instagram, LinkedIn, the CAGH website, and membership newsletter. Applications were collected through a Google Form, which opened on 23 August 2024 and closed on 11 September 2024. Application questions were designed to mirror the finalized criteria and considerations of the rubric for PGS applicants. Form distribution, decisions pertaining to the platforms used and the allotted time given for application submissions were decided by the scholarship planning committee to allow for widened engagement, to occur after the completion of the abstract selection process for the conference, and to increase the likelihood of applications from a broader geographic distribution. A total of 151 applications were submitted, of which 15 were disqualified under the scholarship’s eligibility requirements. This included submissions from applicants not from PGS, those that did not require financial assistance, or incomplete applications. Thus, 136 applications were eligible and entered the next stage of the assessment.

Step 5: candidate scoring

Point allocation was conducted by two independent reviewers, with any conflict resolved through discussion. Applicants were scored on the basis of their responses to online form questions, with each individual criteria allowing for a minimum score of one point and a maximum score between two to five points depending on the question. Final scores were compiled and sorted by applicants who received the highest scores and whether they indicated interest in in-person or virtual participation. Scored applications were colour-coded by status and a legend was created to understand the rationale for why an applicant received a certain category. Applicant status was colour-coded on the basis of definitions in Table 2. A separate colour-coded column indicated whether the applicant had accepted the award or had to withdraw owing to an absence of supporting paperwork and documentation for visa approval to travel to Canada.

Step 6: allocating funding

After the independent blinded scoring progress and colour coding, initial allocation was on the basis of applications with the highest scores achieved based on the rubric criteria. However, consideration of visa requirements to attend the conference in-person resulted in a tiered approach to award allocation. Of the total 136 PGS applications that were submitted through the Google Form qualified for scoring and allocation, four awards were given to applicants falling under the full in-person (FULL IP) category. These participants were not plenary participants. An additional three FULL IP awards were assigned to PGS participants who were plenary panelists in the conference.

Regular in-person (REGULAR IP) and VIRTUAL scholarships were also awarded to applicants who requested full conference registration sponsorship (FULL IP) but on the basis of their scoring and the number of awards available, were offered either REGULAR IP or VIRTUAL awards. Overall, 7 applicants with the highest quantitative scores who applied for FULL IP awards to attend in-person were given full sponsorship, the next 45 highest scoring applicants were offered REGULAR IP if they wanted to attend in-person with only the registration fee covered, and the remaining applicants received VIRTUAL category scholarships despite some applicants expressing interest in REGULAR IP or FULL IP scholarships. Of the 45 awards given to applicants falling under the REGULAR IP category, 36 were awaiting visas to travel to Canada to attend at the time of allocation. These applicants were first granted awards to cover virtual conference registration and would have the option to have their status changed to recipients of the in-person registration award upon proof of a visa to travel to Canada by a selected deadline. In total, 84 awards were given to applicants falling under the VIRTUAL category. With the addition of the 36 REGULAR IP award recipients awaiting visas being offered virtual tickets, 120 total VIRTUAL awards were allocated. Therefore, all 136 applications received either the FULL IP, REGULAR IP, or VIRTUAL award.

Results

The resulting rubric consists of five key criteria and five considerations for weighing applications to assist in the PGS application selection process. The resulting criteria were (i) the PGS status of the applicant, (ii) applicant position/role, (iii) expression of a need of funding support, (iv) applicant’s research relevance to the conference themes, and (v) a willingness to contribute to the conference. The rubric also noted incomplete applications and applicants not classified as PGS, as metrics for application disqualification where applications would receive a score of zero and no longer be considered for the scholarship process. Once applications were collected, the rubric was used in a two-step process to distribute funding by first scoring applications, then allocating awards on the basis of the qualitative score and qualitative additional considerations. Step 1: scoring

Criterion 1: PGS/LMIC status

The first criterion was confirming the applicant’s low- or middle-income country (LMIC)/PGS status, with the World Bank’s 2022 definition of an LMIC used as the criteria for applicants [13]. Non-PGS applicants were disqualified from consideration. The online form that collected information regarding these criteria asked: “Which low- or middle-income country (LMIC) are you a citizen of?”, with the choice to note a country or statelessness. Proof of valid citizenship or residency to the LMIC country the applicant listed was required, with expectations for those who identified as stateless uploading related documentation. During the consultation stage of rubric creation, concerns regarding PGS now affiliated with institutions in the global north and the reduced barriers they may have to access the conference compared with PGS applicants currently located in the global south were addressed and considered in the weighting scheme for these criteria. The scholarship creation team clarified to PGS applicants that the scholarship’s focus was on participants outside of Canada in recognition of the burden of travel. Additional concerns regarding the difficulties stateless applicants may have in securing a visa to travel to Canada and supporting documentation were also considered in the weighting scheme.

Criterion 2: applicant role

The second criterion was the applicant being a global health professional. Applicants who were researchers, practitioners, policymakers, advocates, and/or doctoral students affiliated with global health sectors represented by government, private sector, civil society organizations, or academic institutions at the time of the conference were eligible. The online application form collected information regarding these criteria by asking “How would you describe your current role?” Proof of status, such as proof of faculty/student status or a letter of organizational support, may be required if awarded and was indicated in the Google Form.

Criterion 3: express a need for funding support

The third criterion was the applicant’s expressed need for funding support. Applicants were asked to list in the application what funding support they currently have or anticipate having (if applicable), and what are the applicant’s greatest funding barriers to attending, including but not limited to visa application fees, travel, accommodation, and conference registration fees. Applicants were disqualified for expressing no need for funding support. The online application form also asked whether applicants were seeking “full” or “partial” registration funding, where the definition of “full” and “partial” in relation to the applicant’s expression of funding needs were determined and scored holistically by independent reviewers. Another key consideration of funding needs were price differences in registration fees for registered CAGH members, with membership holders requiring a reduced registration fare. The online application form asked applicants whether they were members of the CAGH. Feedback from the consultation stage suggested creating a tiered weighting approach where point allocation differed on the basis of determination of “full” or “partial” funding needs, and whether applicants were CAGH members. Non-CAGH members were given higher weighting than CAGH members to promote individuals unfamiliar with past CCGH events to attend. Once initial scoring had been completed and applicants had been sorted by score, information on whether applicants currently held visas to travel to Canada or had no need for them was collected in a follow-up survey.

Criterion 4: research relevance

The fourth criterion was determining the relevance of the applicant’s experience to the conference’s themes. Applicants were asked to describe their research, practice, and interests and how they relate to global health and poly-crisis, broadly approached as per the theme of the conference [1]. Through committee discussions, it was agreed to keep the definition of research and practice relevance broad in the interest of inviting applicants from diverse backgrounds in global health. Considerations for point allocation raised by consultations also included whether the applicant was a panelist speaker or had an accepted abstract (i.e., oral or poster conference presentation). Applicants also taking part in plenary panels had been considered in Step 6. Point allocation was determined and scored holistically by two independent reviewers.

Criterion 5: conference contribution

The final criterion was determining what applicants would like to achieve through attending the conference. This includes how they will communicate what they have learned, how they will use the knowledge they have gained at the conference, openness to partaking in social media campaigns, and willingness to take notes. The online application asked, “Would you be willing to contribute to the conference (through social media profiles, notetaking, and/or writing etc.)?” Point allocation was determined and scored holistically by independent reviewers who determined whether applicants adequately described how they will use the knowledge they have gained. Upon committee consultation, it was decided that the draft weighting given to this criterion be increased owing to the impact of participant contribution on engagement.

Step 2: allocation

CAGH membership

To increase fairness in scoring, award allocation was equally distributed to CCGH members and non-members and was dependent on the ratio of applicants with membership that applied. During the scoring process, any point allocation for this consideration was amalgamated with the third criteria: “express a need for funding support”, as the criteria also makes a distinction in weighting depending on CAGH status.

The next four considerations were not weighted and, therefore, the applicant did not receive additional scoring regardless of the answer. The online form asked applicants whether they have received a scholarship to attend a conference in the past and whether they have ever attended a CCGH conference. The rationale for inclusion of this consideration was to ensure that scholarships are not distributed to the same recipients in repetitive years; however, this information was not ultimately used to reallocate awards. This prioritization approach was designed to maximize impact and opportunity for participants who recognized the unique allocation design of the scholarship, with one participant noting that “[the scholarship] is given with dignity, and it truly opens doors that otherwise remain closed for professionals from LMICs” [18]. The final optional consideration asked applicants to list any social media handles associated with the applicant or their organization. The rationale for inclusion of this consideration was increased opportunity to promote the conference and applicant contribution online.

Geographic representation

Geographic representation and diversity within PGS recipients were factored into the FULL IP award allocation process to avoid an overrepresentation of recipients from concentrated regions. This discussion did not affect the actual allocation of awards owing to the diversity of applicants who had the highest quantitative scores. Participants noted the importance of having platforms for knowledge exchange to discuss underrepresented regions, with one participant stating “I wouldn’t have been able to attend or bring work from underrepresented regions like Yemen into the conversation on polycrisis last year” [19]. Patterns in the list of award applicants and recipients found a lack of applicants from Southeast Asia. Engagement strategies to broaden the applicant pool will be considered for future CAGH conferences.

Discussion

Broader visa challenges

The greatest barrier to the allocation and distribution of funding for PGS participants were broader visa challenges, resulting in a tiered approach to award distribution. Consideration of which applicants had proof of a Canadian visa or eligibility to travel to Canada was required for scholarship consideration significantly affected the decision-making process. Both broader committee and planning team discussions ensured a fair distribution of awards while also encompassing the rubric scores. However, it would have been beneficial to provide additional time for applicants to apply for and receive their visa approvals. Feedback from conference participants reiterated concerns about timelines, with one participant noting that “for those considering applying, my advice would be to apply as early as possible and to ensure that all documentation is submitted promptly. I would also encourage organizers to continue the great work of supporting participants and, where possible, communicate funding decisions a bit earlier to help with travel planning” [20]. Ensuring sufficient time for applications and the communication of decisions while maintaining the thorough and rigorous review process will require continued attention in future cycles.

In addition to visa approval delays, the planning committee undertook rigorous verification methods of institutions and the legitimacy of applicant appointments owing to some applicants providing fraudulent letters of support or outdated letters (i.e., not written within 12 months of submission). Quantitative scores generated by the rubric were initially intended to act as the primary metric for award distribution. However, recognizing complexities such as capacity restrictions for each award and barriers collecting key documentation, primary reliance on this metric was overridden, distributing the REGULAR IP award equally between recipients who did and who did not have documentation at the time of distribution. Recipient spaces were held for in the REGULAR IP category for those who did not have documentation, with the understanding that those who do not submit all paperwork by a clear deadline, will have the REGULAR IP award withdrawn, and will be granted spots in the VIRTUAL category instead, where visas for travel to Canada are not required but still allow for conference participation. Visa challenges represent a commonly reported barrier to conference attendance, which disproportionately impacts applicants from different regions. In 2018, the Together Science Can campaign found 34% of academics from Africa and 40% from Asia faced visa difficulties compared with only 9% from Europe and North America [5].

Key contributions to existing research

Our results represent a novel contribution to limited literature on equity-informed scholarship selection processes for LMIC conference participation. Although barriers to PGS in health conferences, including visa restrictions, financial constraints, and limited speaking opportunities are documented in existing research, literature does not address the participant selection process itself. There is a lack of guidance on how to fairly select participants for scholarships and mentorship opportunities designed to overcome these barriers, and there are no published frameworks or rubrics that integrate equity considerations into conference scholarship selection criteria. Overcoming financial barriers for PGS through scholarships is a key tool that can be used to increase geographic representation and participation [5], as noted by one participant’s remark that, “this funding is a key pathway for LMIC researchers, practitioners, and advocates to access global platforms” [19].

Our research provides a decision-making framework that aligns with broader calls to decolonize global health by shifting from relying solely on traditional merit-based frameworks towards those that consider social justice benchmarks for conference participation [6]. Allowing voices from the global south to be heard and respected in the same way as those within the global north would reinforce the relevance and effectiveness of research solutions to local and international health challenges [9]. Supporting PGS with both funding and visa facilitation is critical to shifting power and ensuring more equitable representation in global health spaces. This was further elaborated upon by another participant who mentioned that, “funding not only eases financial barriers but also amplifies voices from underrepresented contexts. Attending CCGH can be transformative, broadening one’s perspective on equity, decolonization, and the role of inclusive leadership in global health” [21]. These reflections reiterate how funding opportunities can increase opportunities for more equitable and inclusive global health dialogue.

Limitations

In addition to the general limitations faced by award recipients of visa approval delays and verification, the planning committee had to balance the financial costs of the conference and the set funding granted for PGS scholarships when designing and distributing awards. Owing to CAGH receiving funding at a later timeline than intended, submission of supporting paperwork for the scholarship was also limited to a tighter schedule for applicants. A need to solidify the funding distribution into the greater financial calculations for the conference and time constraints affected the deadlines given to PGS award recipients to submit paperwork. Potential recipients were given 3 weeks to submit appropriate documentation. A greater timeframe between when the scholarships were announced and awarded relative to the length of time it would take applicants to obtain their visas and submit documentation may have allowed for more potential recipients to successfully submit paperwork before the deadline. Reconsidering the timeframe for each phase of the scholarship is particularly critical given that visa processing time is the most cited challenge for PGS [17] and barriers often prevent PGS from applying early [6].

Conclusions

PGS continue to be underrepresented in the attendance and participation of global health conferences. The CAGH created a rubric with both analytic and holistic criteria to score and allocate funding for PGS registration and attendance. Factors such as the status, applicant position, need for funding support, research relevance, and willingness to contribute to the conference were used to create a metric score for each applicant. These factors balanced facilitating committee decision-making on applicant needs with central tenants of the CAGH’s mission to promote equity-centred principles of global health research [14–16]. However, barriers such as visa considerations, time, and funding resulted in restructuring the PGS award distribution strategy by the committee. The method used in this conference for the scoring and weighting of criteria can be applied in the decision-making process of similar committees creating scholarship rubrics for PGS applicants. Holistically addressing fairness in conference attendance for PGS requires an intersectional perspective on the accessibility and inclusion barriers PGS face [5]. In relation to social justice ideals, examining underlying processes such as conference registration, abstract review, and attendance are essential to identify where systemic barriers to participation by certain subpopulations or groups may exist [6]. The criteria and rubric designed provides a foundation for a reproducible resource that can be scaled-up for other international conferences. We imagine there is wide utility of our rubric and weighted criteria that is not solely limited to PGS inclusion (i.e., that can be applied to other groups underrepresented in conference settings). We call for the consideration of similar funding opportunities for PGS researchers and other groups underrepresented in conferences held in the global north to allow for increased opportunities to share diverse perspectives.
