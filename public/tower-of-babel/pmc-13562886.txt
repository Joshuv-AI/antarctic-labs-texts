Anxiety disorders in China and G20 countries: A comparative burden study
Authors: Yuling Wang, Wenjun Wang, Xiao Liu, Mengqi Zhou, Rubing Gu, Fengjuan Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo compare the burden and temporal patterns of anxiety disorders in China with those in Group of Twenty (G20) countries from 1990 to 2021 and to project future disease burden.MethodsThis ecological secondary analysis used publicly available, aggregated estimates from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 for China and G20 countries. We assessed incidence, prevalence, and disability-adjusted life years using decomposition analysis, autoregressive integrated moving average models, and Bayesian age–period–cohort models.ResultsChina's age-standardized anxiety disorder burden decreased after 2000 (average annual percentage change, −1.2%). Its age profile differed from those of G20 countries, with a relatively greater disease burden among middle-aged and older adults. The prevalence of anxiety was 1.8-fold higher in females than in males. Decomposition analysis attributed 68% of the increase in anxiety prevalence in China to epidemiological change and population growth, whereas population aging accounted for 52% of the increase in G20 countries with a high sociodemographic index. The total number of anxiety cases in China were projected to rise by 22% by 2040, despite stable age-standardized rates.ConclusionsAnxiety disorder burden patterns differed between China and G20 countries and were associated with demographic and developmental contexts. These ecological findings support development-sensitive mental health planning and warrant confirmation in studies using individual-level data.

Introduction

Anxiety disorders represent a prevalent category of mental health conditions characterized by excessive and uncontrollable worry and fear that can substantially impair psychosocial functioning and pose a major challenge to global mental health systems. 1 According to recent estimates from the World Health Organization, approximately 301 million people worldwide were affected by anxiety disorders in 2022, with an annual incidence of approximately 3.7%, making anxiety disorders the leading cause of disability among mental disorders. 2 Despite increased investment in mental health services across several countries, the global number of cases is projected to rise to approximately 380 million by 2040, with the greatest increases in disease burden expected in low- and middle-income countries. 3 Regions with notably high prevalence include North America, Western Europe, Australia/New Zealand, and parts of East Asia, whereas significant gaps in diagnosis and treatment persist across the Middle East, Africa, and parts of Asia. 4 The burden within China is of particular concern. Data from the China Mental Health Survey indicated an age-standardized prevalence of approximately 4.5% in 2022, corresponding to more than 60 million individuals living with anxiety disorders. 5

Despite advances in treatments such as cognitive behavioral therapy and newer pharmacotherapies, anxiety disorders continue to impose a substantial global burden. This persistent burden is attributable to multiple factors, including the often insidious onset of symptoms, pervasive stigma, and low mental health literacy, all of which contribute to substantial underdiagnosis and delayed intervention. Even when treatment is initiated, approximately 30%–40% of patients exhibit an insufficient response to first-line therapies. Consequently, a substantial portion of cases progress to chronicity, with particularly poor outcomes in underserved and remote regions with limited access to care. Critically, however, the fundamental drivers of this burden, its epidemiological trends and their variation across different developmental contexts, remain inadequately characterized. Most existing evidence is derived from high-income settings, offering limited insight into the dynamics in rapidly transitioning economies such as China. Therefore, a systematic analysis comparing the epidemiological evolution of anxiety disorders between China and major economies is not merely descriptive but essential. Such an analysis is a prerequisite for moving beyond one-size-fits-all approaches and developing tailored, effective, and equitable mental health policies and preventive strategies.

The Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) 2021 study provides a comprehensive, standardized dataset encompassing 204 countries and territories, offering an unparalleled resource for cross-national comparisons of disease burden, including anxiety disorders. 6 Although several studies have leveraged GBD data to describe the global or regional burden of anxiety, and some have focused on individual high-income countries (e.g. the United States and Japan), a critical evidence gap persists. Systematic comparisons between China, the world's largest transitioning economy, and other major economies are notably scarce. Limited comparative data with respect to regional neighbors such as Japan and South Korea exist; however, a broader, structured analysis across different developmental contexts remains lacking. 7 This gap obscures the understanding of how socioeconomic and demographic factors differentially shape the epidemiology of anxiety disorders, thereby hindering the cross-pollination of effective policy and practice. To address this gap, we selected the Group of Twenty (G20) as the comparative framework. The G20 comprises 19 of the world's largest economies and the European Union, collectively representing approximately 85% of the global gross domestic product (GDP) and two-third of the world's population. 8 This group inclusively spans the full spectrum of national development, from high-income to low-income countries, making it an ideal platform for examining how mental health outcomes vary with developmental stage. A focused comparison between China and its G20 peers is therefore both highly policy-relevant and essential for deriving context-specific insights.

To bridge this knowledge gap, this study employed GBD 2021 data to conduct a comprehensive, comparative analysis of anxiety disorders in China and other G20 countries from 1990 to 2021. The study objectives were as follows: (a) quantify and compare trends in prevalence, incidence, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs); (b) decompose the contributions of demographic changes and epidemiological shifts to burden trends; (c) analyze age- and sex-specific patterns to identify vulnerable subpopulations; and (d) project key burden indicators to 2040. Methodologically, we employed decomposition analysis, autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models, and Bayesian age–period–cohort (BAPC) forecasting to achieve these aims.9,10 The ultimate objective was to generate actionable evidence that can inform the evaluation of current policies, guide the development of targeted prevention and care strategies, and promote the equitable allocation of mental health resources across different developmental contexts.

Methods

Data source

This ecological investigation was a secondary analysis of anonymized, aggregated estimates from the GBD 2021. We selected the period of 1990–2021 because this was the complete historical time series available in the GBD 2021 release, allowing consistent comparison across China and G20 countries. More recent GBD estimates were not available at the time of analysis. We used the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) Results Tool to extract annual estimates for anxiety disorders in China and G20 member nations, including age-standardized incidence, prevalence, and DALY rates and their 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs). In the GBD nosology, anxiety disorders are classified under mental disorders and correspond to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes F40–F49. 11 The data are publicly available and contained no personally identifiable information; therefore, ethical review was not required. The Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting were followed.

Projection modeling

To forecast the future epidemiological trajectory of anxiety disorders, we implemented two distinct but complementary statistical modeling frameworks. ARIMA modeling. We employed ARIMA models to generate short- to medium-term forecasts (2022–2040) of incidence and DALYs for anxiety disorders in China and G20 nations. 9 The ARIMA (p, d, and q) framework, where “p,” “d,” and “q” denote the orders of autoregression, integration (differencing), and moving average, respectively, is particularly suited for modeling nonstationary time series data and capturing of the latent effects of past interventions or secular trends.12,13 Model specification, including the determination of optimal (p, d, and q) parameters, was conducted in accordance with standard iterative procedures involving autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation function diagnostics.BAPC modeling. For a more granular decomposition of long-term trends and independent long-range projections, we applied a BAPC model. 10 The BAPC model provides a powerful framework for disentangling the temporal drivers of disease rates by partitioning them into three orthogonal components: (a) age effect (variation in risk attributable to physiological aging); (b) period effect (temporal shifts in risk affecting all age groups simultaneously, e.g. due to policy changes or diagnostic practices); and (c) cohort effect (variation in risk shared by individuals born in the same era, reflecting early-life exposures). The model was fitted to the historical age-stratified data from the GBD 1990–2021 using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulation to estimate posterior distributions for all parameters, with convergence assessed according to standard diagnostics. Projections for 2022–2050 were derived by holding the estimated age and cohort effects constant at their posterior means while extrapolating the period effect forward based on its estimated temporal trend.Uncertainty quantification. For both modeling approaches, prediction uncertainty was rigorously quantified. All forecasts are presented with 95% UIs, generated by propagating the full posterior uncertainty from parameter estimation through the projection process. This captured uncertainty stemming from model estimation, stochastic variation, and the extrapolation of future trends.

Decomposition analysis

To quantitatively attribute the temporal changes in anxiety disorder burden from 1990 to 2021, we performed a demographic decomposition analysis following the standard GBD framework.14,15 This method decomposes the observed change in absolute disease burden metrics (e.g. number of prevalent cases or DALYs) into independent contributions from three fundamental drivers: (a) population growth, reflecting changes due solely to increases in total population size while holding age structure and disease rates constant; (b) population aging, capturing the effect of shifts in the population's age distribution toward older groups; and (c) changes in epidemiological rates, representing the variation attributable to shifts in age-specific disease risk, thereby isolating the net impact of changes in exposure, diagnostics, treatment, and policy over time. 16 The analysis was performed by constructing counterfactual scenarios to estimate the burden change that would have occurred if each driver had evolved independently, with all other factors were held constant at their baseline values.

Statistical analyses

We extracted incidence, prevalence, and DALY estimates for anxiety disorders in China and G20 countries from GBD 2021, together with age-standardized rates (ASRs) and 95% UIs. All analyses and visualizations were performed in R version 4.2.3. 17 The workflow included extraction of GBD estimates, calculation of ASRs and average annual percentage change (AAPC) using log-linear regression, demographic decomposition analysis, ARIMA forecasting, and BAPC forecasting. This was a descriptive analysis of modeled GBD estimates; therefore, p-values were not used for inference; 95% UIs were reported where available.

Results

Comparison of overall trends of anxiety disorders between China and G20 countries

We systematically compared the distribution of anxiety disorders in China across several metrics, including DALYs, incidence, prevalence, and years lived with disability (YLDs). The results are presented in Figure 1(a). The X-axis represents age groups in 5-year intervals, from <5 years to >95 years, whereas the y-axis presents the ASR. To quantify the temporal trends in the ASR of anxiety disorder burden, we calculated the AAPC. A log-linear regression model was fitted to the natural logarithm of the ASR, with the calendar year as the independent variable: ln(ASR) = α + β × (calendar year) + ε. The AAPC was then derived using the formula (exp(β) − 1) × 100%, which represents the average annual change in the ASR over a specified period. Additionally, we found that China's anxiety disorder burden declined after 2000 (AAPC = −1.2%). As shown in Figure 1, the distributions of DALYs, prevalence, and YLDs are highly consistent, indicating a relatively stable proportion of burden from adolescence through old age. In contrast, the incidence curve suggests that the proportion of new-onset anxiety disorders begins to decline from middle age onward.

Patterns of anxiety disorder burden within China: age, sex, and temporal trends (1990–2021). (a) Age distribution of DALYs, incidence, prevalence, and YLDs across 5-year age groups, showing a higher burden in middle-aged and older adults; (b) sex-stratified distribution of the four metrics by age group, indicating consistently higher burden in females than in males; (c) overall sex-specific prevalence ratio across all ages, confirming higher prevalence in females; (d) temporal trend of age-standardized rates (ASRs) by sex since 1990, with a decline after 2000 and a recent rebound.

Figure 1(b) illustrates the distribution of these metrics stratified by sex. In the overall population, the burden of anxiety disorders in China was higher among females than among males. Interestingly, among middle-aged and older populations, the incidence of new cases appeared higher in males than in females. Although case numbers in older age groups were relatively smaller, this trend persisted across the study period, suggesting that it reflected specific cohort characteristics in China, such as cohort-specific socioeconomic stressors. This sex-specific distribution across all age groups is summarized in Figure 1(c), which confirms that the prevalence of anxiety disorders was markedly higher in females at the population level. Figure 1(d) presents the temporal trend of the ASR for anxiety disorders by sex from 1990 to the present. A declining trend was observed beginning approximately during the year 2000, with a marked, recent fluctuation or rebound.

Comparative analysis of anxiety disorder burden across age and sex between China and G20 countries

As a comparative benchmark, a descriptive analysis of G20 countries was conducted. The distribution trends of the four metrics, including DALYs, incidence, prevalence, and YLDs, across age groups were highly consistent in these nations (Figure 2(a)). In all instances, the burden of anxiety disorders rose markedly during adolescence, peaked at 20–30 years of age, and gradually declined with advancing age. This pattern contrasts with that observed in China, where a relatively high proportion of the burden was maintained among middle-aged and older adults. When stratified by sex, trends in G20 countries aligned with those in China (Figure 2(b)), indicating a globally consistent pattern of higher risk among women. The overall sex-specific proportions showed higher ASRs in female patients than in male patients in China (Figure 2(c)). Figure 2(d) illustrates the temporal trend of anxiety disorders in G20 countries, revealing that although the total number of cases continued to increase, the ASR remained stable. These comparative findings suggest that China achieved notable success in controlling the burden of anxiety disorders after 2000, resulting in a sustained decline in the proportional rates.

(a) Age distribution of DALYs, incidence, prevalence, and YLDs across 5-year age groups, showing a rise during adolescence, a peak at 20–30 years of age, and a subsequent decline with age; (b) sex-stratified distribution of the four metrics by age group, indicating a consistently higher burden in females than in males, consistent with patterns in China; (c) overall sex-specific prevalence ratio across all ages, showing higher ASRs in female patients than in male patients observed in China; (d) temporal trend of age-standardized rates (ASRs) in G20 countries, indicating stable ASR despite an increase in total case numbers.

Variations in anxiety disorder burden across G20 regions, sex, and SDI levels

Data were further stratified by geographic region to compare the proportion and number of anxiety disorder cases across G20 countries (Figure 3(a)). The highest prevalence occurred in Tropical Latin America, whereas the lowest was observed in Central Asia. When analyses were stratified by sex, the pattern remained consistent with that in China, showing a consistently higher prevalence among females than among males (Figure 3(b)). Finally, categorization by sociodemographic index (SDI) revealed a positive correlation between development level and anxiety disorder prevalence: regions with higher SDI exhibited higher prevalence rates than regions with lower SDI (Figure 3(c)). This suggests that residents of more economically developed regions are exposed to higher levels of stress and associated risk factors, contributing to an elevated burden of anxiety disorders.

(a) Prevalence of anxiety disorders by G20 region, with the highest rate in Tropical Latin America and the lowest in Central Asia; (b) sex-stratified prevalence across G20 regions, showing consistently higher rates in females than in males, a pattern consistent with observations in China; (c) association between prevalence and sociodemographic index (SDI), indicating higher prevalence in high-SDI regions than in low-SDI regions.

Geographic distribution and temporal trends of anxiety disorders in G20 countries

To visually compare the burden of anxiety disorders across G20 countries, we mapped ASRs of DALYs, incidence, prevalence, and YLDs by region (Figure 4(a) to (d)). The maps indicated generally higher prevalence in North America and Africa relative to those in Europe and Asia. We further assessed regional differences in trends using the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC), a summary measure of the average annual rate of change in ASR over time (Figure 4(e) to (h)). 18 The EAPC reveals both direction and velocity of burden change. The analysis revealed that China had the lowest EAPC, reflecting effective control of anxiety disorder growth. In contrast, regions such as Africa exhibited a more rapid upward trend.

Projection of anxiety disorders incidence in China

We performed a comprehensive analysis of the prevalence of anxiety disorders in China, examining trends longitudinally across years, age groups, and sex strata and comparing these patterns horizontally with corresponding data from G20 countries. Building on this comparative foundation, we implemented two established forecasting approaches, ARIMA and BAPC modeling, to project the incidence of anxiety disorders over the next 30 years. The resulting trajectories are presented in Figure 5(a) and (b). The model was fitted to the historical time series data of the absolute number of cases from 1990 to 2021. After identifying the optimal model parameters (p, d, and q) through diagnostics of the autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation functions, we generated projections for the period 2022–2040. The percentage change in the total number of cases by 2040 was calculated relative to the baseline number of cases in 2021, using the formula: ((projected cases2040 − baseline cases2021)/baseline cases2021) × 100%. We found that projections indicated a 22% rise in China's total cases by 2040 despite stable ASRs.

Decomposition analysis of anxiety disorders burden in China compared with that in G20 countries

Decomposition analysis based on the GBD 2021 study quantified the contributions of three drivers, including population aging, population growth, and changes in epidemiological rates, to changes in anxiety disorder burden from 1990 to 2021 in China and G20 nations (Figure 7). In China, the overall burden was substantial, with total DALYs reaching 1.2 million; number of cases, 10 million; and incidence, 1.5 million cases. The decomposition revealed a distinctive pattern; epidemiological change was the predominant driver, followed by population growth, while population aging contributed minimally.

Decomposition analysis of anxiety disorder DALY changes in China and G20 countries (1990–2021) (a) Contributions of population growth, aging, and epidemiological changes to DALY changes in China, with epidemiological change as the predominant driver; (b) corresponding decomposition for G20 countries, showing a substantially greater contribution from population aging compared with China.

In contrast, G20 countries exhibited a more balanced driver profile, with population aging playing a markedly larger role than in China, reflecting more advanced demographic transitions. The contributions of epidemiological change and population growth remained largest but were relatively balanced against aging. This comparative analysis highlights a stark divergence; China's burden change was primarily driven by epidemiological shifts and population size, whereas in G20 countries, population aging emerged as a central and powerful driver alongside other factors. These distinct profiles underscore the need for tailored policy responses, focusing on shifting risk factors and service capacity in China and integrating mental health with aging-focused initiatives in G20 nations.

Decomposition analysis of disease burden drivers stratified by SDI group in G20 countries

We further stratified G20 countries into five groups based on the SDI to examine how development level influences the drivers of anxiety disorder burden. Decomposition analysis across these strata revealed a clear and consistent gradient (Figure 8). The absolute burden was highest in high and high-middle SDI regions and declined progressively with lower SDI. Crucially, the primary driver of burden shifted systematically with SDI level; population aging was the dominant contributor in high-SDI regions, whereas population growth emerged as the primary driver in low-SDI regions. Epidemiological changes played a consistent, although nondominant, role across all SDI strata. This analysis demonstrates that the drivers of anxiety disorder burden are not uniform but are fundamentally structured by a region's level of socioeconomic development. Consequently, public health strategies must be tailored accordingly, with high-SDI regions requiring a focus on aging populations and low-SDI regions needing to address challenges amplified by population growth.

Discussion

This comparative study illustrates notable divergences in anxiety disorder epidemiology between China and other G20 economies, differences that are plausibly shaped by heterogeneous national developmental stages and demographic trajectories.

After 2000, China experienced a marked decline in age-standardized anxiety disorder burden. This temporal association should not be interpreted as evidence that specific policies, economic expansion, or health-literacy changes caused the decline because these factors cannot be separated within the ecological GBD data. Decomposition analyses defined epidemiological change as a composite residual term capturing changes unrelated to population size and age composition, including possible changes in diagnostic criteria, survey protocols, coding standards, and population-level exposures. Unlike most G20 countries, where anxiety prevalence peaked among young adults aged 20–30 years, China's anxiety burden appeared relatively concentrated in middle-aged and older populations; this pattern requires cautious interpretation.

Decomposition analyses offer tentative insights into mechanisms underlying cross-national disparities. Epidemiological change refers to the change driven by variations in age-specific disease rates, reflecting the net impact of changes in risk factors, diagnostics, and treatment. The contribution of each factor was calculated by constructing counterfactual scenarios, and its relative contribution was expressed as a percentage of the total change in disease burden. For China, the overall growth in anxiety case counts between 1990 and 2021 was predominantly driven by epidemiological changes (68%) alongside population expansion. Such epidemiological shifts may reflect modified risk exposure levels, varying accessibility of medical services, and evolving diagnostic practices, whereas population aging exerted a comparatively minor contribution to the total burden growth. By contrast, population aging constituted the leading contributor to the rising anxiety burden across high-SDI G20 nations, accounting for 52% of the total increments in the burden. This pattern is consistent with a well-documented theoretical framework positing that a country's SDI gradient may modulate dominant disease burden drivers; developing economies are more likely to face burden shifts linked to population expansion and epidemiological transitions, whereas mature high-income societies tend to confront aging-related mental health challenges.19,20 Our cross-SDI stratified analyses of G20 members further lend tentative support to this gradient hypothesis, suggesting that population growth could function as a primary burden driver in low-SDI regions, and aging could be a key contributor in high-SDI contexts (Figure 8).

Analyses stratified by sex and age revealed additional nuanced cross-group variations. Consistent with global epidemiological consensus, females exhibited a higher prevalence of anxiety than males (female-to-male ratio = 1.8), a pattern replicated in our dataset. 21 The relatively elevated anxiety burden observed among middle-aged Chinese males represents a deviation from prevailing international trends, which may be attributed to cohort-specific socioeconomic stressors arising during China's rapid socioeconomic transformation, such as fierce occupational competition and heavy family caregiving obligations. Cultural barriers to mental health help seeking could also plausibly exacerbate anxiety risks within this subgroup. Meanwhile, the persistent anxiety burden among Chinese older adults, in contrast to the age-related burden decline seen across most G20 nations, has several competing plausible explanations, including cumulative lifelong psychological risk exposure, insufficient diagnosis of mild anxiety during younger life stages, and frequent comorbidity with age-associated chronic physical illnesses.

Certain key limitations of GBD aggregated ecological data must be acknowledged when interpreting the above observational associations. First, persistent mental illness stigma and uneven diagnostic capacity, especially in rural and underserved Chinese regions, may have led to systematic underreporting and an underestimation of true anxiety prevalence. 15 Second, the GBD dataset aggregates all anxiety subtypes, masking potential divergent temporal trends for generalized anxiety disorder, specific phobias, and other anxiety subcategories. Third, our ARIMA and BAPC projection models relied solely on historical population and disease data for extrapolation; unforeseen nonlinear societal shocks, including global pandemics, severe economic recessions, and large-scale health policy reforms could alter future burden trajectories and render projections less accurate.

Despite these analytical constraints, the present observational findings generate tentative policy implications that require validation in independent study designs. For China, two sets of intervention priorities represent reasonable, unconfirmed recommendations rather than definitive conclusions: (a) scale up nationwide mental health literacy campaigns and anti-stigma initiatives with targeted outreach toward middle-aged male populations, the underrecognized high-risk subgroup identified in this study and (b) integrate routine mental health screening and early intervention protocols into primary care and chronic disease management systems to address the unmet mental health demands of aging residents. For high-SDI G20 nations, it is plausible that greater investment and integration of geriatric-adapted mental health services within care systems for older adults could alleviate aging-related anxiety burdens. Low-SDI economies may benefit from youth-centered mental prevention programs and the construction of basic grassroots mental healthcare infrastructure to mitigate burden growth linked to population expansion.

In conclusion, anxiety disorder burden patterns differed between China and G20 countries, with demographic change contributing differently across development contexts. The projected increase in absolute case numbers in China underscores the need for planning that considers age, sex, and national development. Future longitudinal, individual-level, and subtype-specific studies should test the mechanisms suggested by these ecological findings and assess interventions in clinical and community settings.
