Quantum Artificial Intelligence for Software Engineering: the Road Ahead
Xinyi Wang, Shaukat Ali, Paolo Arcaini

Abstract
Artificial Intelligence (AI) has been applied to various areas of software engineering, including requirements engineering, coding, testing, and debugging. This has led to the emergence of AI for Software Engineering as a distinct research area within software engineering. With the development of quantum computing, the field of Quantum AI (QAI) is arising, enhancing the performance of classical AI and holding significant potential for solving classical software engineering problems. Some initial applications of QAI in software engineering have already emerged, such as software test optimization. However, the path ahead remains open, offering ample opportunities to solve complex software engineering problems with QAI cost-effectively. To this end, this paper presents open research opportunities and challenges in QAI for software engineering that need to be addressed.

Introduction

Modern software systems like cyber-physical, autonomous, and LLM-based systems are becoming increasingly big and complex. These systems are affected by challenges, including uncertainty in their operating environments, lack of precise oracles, and multi-modal inputs. Consequently, all the software engineering activities associated with their development, testing, and maintenance are becoming more complex and challenging to apply. For example, test suites of large software projects can have millions of tests
[
Memon et al.(2017)
]
, significantly affecting the scalability of activities like test case minimization and prioritization.

To tackle these issues, in recent years, Artificial Intelligence (AI) has been proposed to solve some software engineering problems more efficiently and effectively, starting from the very active area of Search-Based Software Engineering (SBSE)
[

[

]
, program repair
[

[

However, software systems continue to evolve, and their complexity and size will continue to grow in the future. Such systems will be characterized by higher levels of autonomy and intelligence than the current ones. We forecast that current AI-based software engineering approaches could have problems handling the complexity of the development of these systems. So, we claim that it is time to reflect on how to handle such complexity in the future.

Quantum computing (QC) promises to solve problems that can not be solved with classical computers, not even the most complex supercomputers

•

•

Support for black box systems.

The performance of software engineering approaches that rely on prediction (e.g., regression testing) is severely affected by the scarcity of features. QML approaches are also known to be efficient in the presence of few features

General QAI Challenges

Some general challenges must be addressed when adopting a QAI approach for a software engineering problem. We discuss them in the following.

We believe that the application of QAI for software engineering should start as soon as possible to have efficient QAI approaches for software engineering when quantum computers will become widely available. Therefore, while waiting to transition to a higher number of qubits, we need to develop techniques that can operate using a limited number of them. For example, decomposition techniques could be adopted to split a problem into small sub-problems that can be solved with a few qubits. Although such decomposition-based approaches may obtain results that are not as good as those that could be obtained by treating the problem as a whole, they may still show the potentiality of QAI as well as build the competency of solving software engineering problems with QAI.

2.2
Classical vs. Quantum Dilemma

While some problems can not be solved with classical computers, others remain feasible but with a high computational cost. In these cases, when considering the adoption of QAI, one should consider two main aspects:

•

what is the
expected benefit
in terms of computational speed provided by QAI w.r.t. classical computing? Indeed, while the additional benefit will be substantial for very complex problems, it will be minimal for simpler ones.

•

what is the
economical cost
of using a quantum computer w.r.t. a classical computer? Indeed, although more quantum computers will become available at a lower price, for some years, their cost will remain higher than that of classical computers.

Therefore, one should consider the trade-off between expected benefit and economic cost when deciding whether to apply a quantum or a classical approach.

2.3

3
Quantum Optimization for Software Engineering

The software engineering domain involves numerous optimization challenges, such as test case optimization, software refactoring, resource allocation, release planning, and project management problems
[
Harman et al.(2012)
]
. Currently, classical optimization algorithms, such as search algorithms, are widely used to solve them
[
Harman et al.(2012)
]
, including simulated annealing, genetic algorithms, and NSGA-II
[
Deb et al.(2002)
]
. With the progress in QC, it holds great potential to outperform the state-of-the-art classical optimization algorithms with quantum computers
[
Abbas et al.(2024)
]
. Currently, there have been several well-known optimization algorithms emerged, such as Grover’s search
[
Wang and Krstic(2020)
]
, quantum annealing (QA)
[
Yarkoni et al.(2022)
]
, quantum approximate optimization algorithms (QAOA)
[
Blekos et al.(2024)
]
. However, to successfully apply those algorithms to the software engineering domain and demonstrate the quantum advantage, several challenges must be addressed.

3.1
Identifying Relevant Software Engineering Problems for Quantum Optimization

To effectively use quantum optimization for solving software engineering optimization, we need to identify problems that are difficult for classical computers to solve and have the potential to demonstrate quantum advantage. For example, combinatorial optimization algorithms such as test case minimization and resource allocation problems demonstrate exponential complexity. QA
[
Yarkoni et al.(2022)
]
and QAOA
[
Blekos et al.(2024)
]
are promising algorithms for tackling these challenges, suggesting potential advantages in these areas. In addition, we need to identify the scale of these problems that suit the capacity of near-term noisy quantum computers to enable practical applications with quantum computing. To this end, research is needed to map existing software engineering problems to various quantum optimization algorithms and assess their feasibility in solving small-scale problem instances that current quantum computers can handle. The next step involves researching the problem scale that would justify using quantum computers.

3.2
Adapting Quantum Optimization Algorithms for Software Engineering

Theoretically, certain quantum optimization algorithms have been proved to outperform classical optimization algorithms
[
Shaydulin et al.(2024)
,
Jiang and Chu(2023)
]
. However, practical implementations face challenges due to hardware noise (see Section
2.3
), the limited number of qubits (see Section
2.1
), and qubit connectivity limits (see Section
2.1
). Moreover, several distinct features exist in software engineering optimization problems, such as large scales, uncertainty and incomplete information, multi-objective nature, and combinatorial complexity. Thus, improving those quantum algorithms to tackle those challenges is necessary. This may involve developing hybrid algorithms and proposing specialized variants tailored to specific software engineering problems.

3.3
Encoding of Software Engineering Problems for Quantum Optimization

Different quantum optimization algorithms require specific encodings for their problems and solutions. For example, QA requires the representation of optimization problems in the QUBO model. Given an optimization problem defined in classical terms, there is no standard way to automatically transform it into a QUBO model. Therefore, developers must develop a specific QUBO model for the problem at hand. This requires deep knowledge of quantum computing and optimization. To facilitate QAI adoption, the research community must find ways to (semi-)automatically transform classical optimization problems into their quantum counterpart. LLMs are increasingly used to solve software engineering problems
[
Fan et al.(2023)
]
. To this end, there is potential to use LLMs to guide software engineers in supporting software engineering optimization problems with quantum optimization. For example, research is needed to develop customized LLMs that can assist in mapping software engineering problems to QUBO or Ising formulations and generate relevant code to embed into QA or QAOA algorithms.

3.4
Solving Large Software Engineering Problems with Small Quantum Computers

Since realistic software engineering optimization problems commonly contain large-scale data, decomposition algorithms are necessary to fit them into quantum computers with limited qubit numbers and connectivity. These algorithms must be practically efficient to ensure the speed advantage in quantum algorithms compared to classical algorithms. Effective encoding strategies to fit large-scale optimization problem data into a smaller number of qubits are also required. Furthermore, due to hardware noise mentioned in Section
2.3
, promising noise mitigation techniques tailored for software engineering optimization problems are essential to enhance the performance of solving these problems in practice.

3.5
Solving Multi-and Many-Objective Software Engineering Problems

Many software engineering optimization problems involve more than one objective (e.g., finding the minimum number of test cases that trigger the maximum number of failures). However, quantum optimization algorithms such as QA and QAOA are typically single-objective. Some strategies have been used to tackle this problem, such as weighted-sum
[
Dahi et al.(2024)
,
Wang et al.(2024b)
]
. Despite these efforts, there is a need for more sophisticated quantum optimization algorithms tailored to multi-objective optimization in software engineering. This may involve integrating classical techniques with quantum algorithms or designing specialized quantum circuits to handle it.

3.6
Supporting Constraints of Software Engineering Problems Effectively

Most software engineering optimization problems have constraints (e.g., the total execution time of the selected test cases shall not go beyond a defined threshold). In implementing QUBO, for example, these constraints are typically incorporated by adding linear inequality and equality constraints. This is often achieved by introducing penalty terms with slack variables to directly represent the constraints
[
Glover et al.(2022)
]
. However, the cost (i.e., the number of used qubits) is rather large when implemented on quantum computers. There have been several initial explorations to incorporate constraints efficiently in the optimization algorithm directly
[
Cook et al.(2020)
,
Dahi et al.(2024)
,
Bartschi and Eidenbenz(2020)
]
. Despite these advancements, more sophisticated approaches are needed to effectively implement constraints of real-world software engineering problems into quantum optimization algorithms. Such approaches shall consider the limitations of the current quantum computers (e.g., the limited number of qubits and noise).

4
Quantum Machine Learning for Software Engineering

Classical machine learning techniques (e.g., deep learning) have been widely applied to solve many software engineering problems, such as coding, test case generation, and debugging
[
Terragni et al.(2025)
]
. Furthermore, with the rise of large language models, their applications in solving software engineering problems are also rapidly expanding
[
Gao et al.(2025)
]
. This evidence suggests that classical AI provides promising solutions for software engineering challenges. Following the principles of classical machine learning, the growing field of quantum machine learning (QML)
[
Narwane et al.(2025)
,
Cerezo et al.(2022)
]
holds great potential for addressing classical software engineering problems more efficiently. Moreover, QML may enable the solution of even more complex software engineering problems that are infeasible for classical machine learning. Several research challenges need to be addressed as a first step toward applying QML to classical software engineering problems.

4.1
Applying Existing QML Algorithms for Software Engineering

Efforts have been made to port several classical machine learning algorithms to quantum domains, including quantum neural networks (QNNs), quantum kernels, quantum convolutional neural networks, quantum extreme learning machines (QELMs), and quantum clustering
[
Narwane et al.(2025)
]
. However, few studies exist on applying QML to software engineering problems
[
Murillo et al.(2025)
]
. For instance, QELMs were recently used for regression testing of an industrial elevator software
[
Wang et al.(2024a)
]
. As a first step toward applying QML to software engineering, we must identify existing software engineering problems for which QML can be readily applied. This investigation requires defining a comprehensive mapping of existing software engineering problems to various QML algorithms. Such an investigation will also provide initial evidence on the strengths and weaknesses of QML in the software engineering domain and help determine whether new classes of QML need to be tailored to software engineering problems.

4.2
Mapping Software Engineering Artefacts into Quantum Domain

A key step in solving classical software engineering problems with QML is mapping artifacts into the quantum domain. To this end, one research direction is to develop efficient mechanisms for mapping software engineering artifacts—such as code, test cases, and their execution results—into the quantum domain, enabling the application of QML. Thus, further research is needed to design efficient mapping techniques that maximize the use of available quantum hardware resources, e.g., the limited number of qubits and their connectivity (see Section
2.1
). To identify such mappings, there is potential to explore classical optimization algorithms, such as genetic algorithms and other multi-objective search algorithms (e.g., NSGA-II). Moreover, investigating the application of quantum optimization algorithms also holds great potential.

4.3
Building Quantum Machine Learning Features

Similar to classical machine learning, QML operates on features. Therefore, further research is needed to identify features that can efficiently represent software engineering artifacts, such as test cases, source code, and test logs. A systematic exploration of this area is required to investigate whether classical features can be used or if there is a need for a specific way to create features that suit QML the most. Some existing approaches can be used to map features, such as amplitude encoding, basis encoding, and angle encoding
[
Schuld and Petruccione(2018)
]
. However, those encoding strategies have trade-offs in terms of qubit number, circuit depth, and ease of implementation
Thus, further research is required to determine whether these methods are efficient for software engineering problems.

4.4
Noise-Aware QML for Software Engineering

Until fully fault-tolerant quantum computers become available, QML—whether applied to software engineering or other problems—must address noise that could otherwise affect the performance of the software engineering problem QML is trying to solve (see Section
2.3
). This also limits the application of QML on current quantum computers, which are inherently noisy. Therefore, research is needed to investigate whether existing error mitigation methods provided by vendors, such as those from IBM, work well for solving software engineering problems using QML. Moreover, it is important to explore whether specialized methods, like Q-LEAR
[
Muqeet et al.(2024a)
]
, can be integrated into QML to solve software engineering problems. In addition, this raises questions about whether different mechanisms for handling noise during training could improve QML performance or whether training a small model on a noisy simulator and later transferring learning to a quantum computer QML model would be effective.

5
Common Considerations for Quantum Optimization and Quantum Machine Learning for Software Engineering

Some considerations regarding the adoption of QAI for software engineering problems are the same, regardless we adopt quantum optimization or quantum machine learning. We discuss them in the following.

5.1
Guidance for Classical Software Engineers to Apply QAI

Given the differences between classical and quantum computing, using QAI requires a steep learning curve. This challenge is further complemented by the lack of existing guidance, making the application of QAI to software engineering problems even more difficult. To address this, research is needed to develop systematic processes and guidelines to help software engineers integrate QAI into their current workflows. To automate these processes and guidelines, investigating classical AI techniques, including LLMs, could be valuable in assisting traditional software engineers with adopting QML.

5.2
Need for Empirical Evaluations for QAI for Software Engineering

Another important research direction is to empirically evaluate whether there is a need to solve classical software engineering problems with QAI or whether classical AI is sufficient to solve these problems. To this end, there is a need for systematic empirical evaluations to compare classical and quantum AI to solve various software engineering problems. In the near term, with small-scale noisy quantum computers, it is difficult to demonstrate the clear benefits of using QAI. However, the initial empirical studies should focus on assessing the feasibility of QAI to pave the way for solving problems more efficiently on larger and fault-tolerant quantum computers when they become available. In addition, initial studies could focus on solving small-scale problems using classical and quantum QAI with a fair comparison to show whether QAI can achieve at least similar performance and potentially be faster. These new empirical evaluations of QAI for software engineering could provide evidence about the performance of a set of QAI algorithms and their performance on different software engineering artifacts.

5.3
QAI Optimization for Software Engineering

Hyperparameters of QAI need to be optimized to solve classical software engineering problems. Currently, no framework exists to support the optimization of QML hyperparameters for solving software engineering problems. To this end, automated frameworks are needed to support hyperparameter optimization. Towards this goal, classical optimization algorithms, such as genetic and quantum optimization algorithms, could be applied to guide software engineers in tuning parameters when solving software engineering problems with QAI.

5.4
QAI for Software Engineering Benchmarks

There is an open question about whether there is a need for specialized benchmarks to assess QAI for solving classical software engineering problems. Given that the input to QAI is classical software engineering artifacts, the existing software engineering artifacts, and benchmarks could be used to evaluate the performance of current and future QAI algorithms compared to classical ML algorithms. However, existing benchmarks could be not complex enough to justify using QAI; thus, we would need more complex benchmarks that would require QAI in order to be handled.

Related Work

Quantum computing holds the potential to solve software engineering problems, which researchers are recognizing. To this end, recent work discussed key quantum algorithms that could potentially be useful in solving software engineering problems
[
Miranskyy et al.(2022)
]
. For example, the study argued that using and extending Grover’s search for string comparison could provide a quadratic speedup over classical implementations
[
Miranskyy(2022)
]
. The most prominent quantum optimization algorithms, such as quantum approximate optimization algorithms for gate-based quantum computers
[
Wang et al.(2024b)
]
, as well as quantum annealers
[
Wang et al.(2024c)
,
Trovato et al.(2024)
]
for D-wave quantum computers, have been explored for test optimization of classical software. These works provide initial evidence of the feasibility of applying quantum optimization algorithms to solve software engineering problems. Quantum machine learning has also gained some interest from the classical software engineering community. For instance, recently, there has been an investigation into applying quantum extreme learning machines (QELMs) for regression testing of the industrial elevator software built by Orona
[
Wang et al.(2024a)
]
. Later, QELMs were also investigated for software testing problems in two real-world applications: the cancer registry of Norway and Oslo City’s healthcare software
[
Muqeet et al.(2024b)
]
. An important aspect of this study was that it also evaluated QELMs with and without noise, and the results indicated good performance of QELMs on ideal simulators. However, hardware noise affected their performance, calling for more research and investigation.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

Along the lines of classical artificial intelligence applied to software engineering problems, interest has risen in the classical software engineering community to apply quantum artificial intelligence (QAI) to solve software engineering problems. To this end, this paper presents open research directions along two lines. First, we discuss research challenges in applying quantum optimization algorithms to software engineering problems. Second, we discuss the challenges of applying quantum machine learning (QML) to software engineering problems. Moreover, we present challenges shared by quantum optimization and QML, such as hardware noise, which must be addressed. In future work, we aim to provide a detailed mapping of various software engineering problems that can be solved with quantum AI in general, along with research directions. Moreover, we aim to highlight specific QAI challenges that must be addressed.
