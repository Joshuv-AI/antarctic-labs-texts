Efficacy of a Nursing Checklist During Mechanical Ventilation in Patients With Severe Pneumonia
Authors: Miao Guo, Li Qiao, Xiaohui Xing, Jing Zhao, Zheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSevere pneumonia is a respiratory disease associated with high morbidity and mortality, often necessitating mechanical ventilation. However, prolonged mechanical ventilation is associated with an increased risk of complications and higher use of healthcare resources. The efficacy of a nursing checklist on airway management during mechanical ventilation in patients with severe pneumonia has not been extensively investigated.AimThis study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a nursing checklist in improving airway management during mechanical ventilation for patients with severe pneumonia.Study DesignA prospective, assessor‐blind, single‐centre, randomized controlled trial was conducted, and 100 patients diagnosed with severe pneumonia who required mechanical ventilation were enrolled in the study. Participants were randomly assigned to either the Nursing Checklist group (NC group) or the Routine Management group (RM group). The primary outcome measures included the duration of mechanical ventilation, length of stay in the intensive care unit, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score, tidal volume (TV), respiratory rate (f) and the oxygenation index (the ratio of arterial oxygen partial pressure [PaO2] to fractional inspired oxygen [FiO2] or PaO2/FiO2).ResultsThe duration of mechanical ventilation was significantly shorter among patients in the NC group compared to those in the RM group (p < 0.001), but the difference in length of stay in the intensive care unit (ICU) between the two groups was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). By Day 14, the APACHE II score in the NC group was significantly lower than that in the RM group (p < 0.001), and improvements in TV, f and PaO2/FiO2 in the NC group were also statistically significant compared to the RM group (p < 0.001).ConclusionsIn this study, it was observed that the implementation of a nursing checklist was associated with significant improvements in the quality of airway care and patient outcomes during mechanical ventilation in patients with severe pneumonia, leading to a significant reduction in the duration of mechanical ventilation and improved oxygenation status and prognosis of patients.Relevance to Clinical PracticeThese findings provide valuable insights for future research and clinical practice, and it is recommended that the use of a nursing checklist be integrated into clinical practice to improve the quality and safety of airway management for patients with severe pneumonia on mechanical ventilation.Keywords: critical care nursing, mechanical ventilation, nursing checklist, patient safety, severe pneumonia

Introduction

Severe pneumonia is a serious respiratory disease characterized by high morbidity and mortality. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), pneumonia is one of the leading causes of mortality among both children and adults worldwide [1]. Globally, approximately 14 million individuals are affected by pneumonia each year, with more than 5 million deaths, especially in low‐ and middle‐income countries. Severe pneumonia predominantly affects older individuals and those with chronic diseases, with its incidence increasing due to population ageing and the rising prevalence of chronic diseases. The high incidence and mortality rates associated with severe pneumonia make it a critical public health issue, especially among older adults and immunocompromised individuals [2].

In patients with severe pneumonia, mechanical ventilation is a widely used life‐support intervention for managing respiratory failure. There is evidence from studies that a substantial proportion of patients with severe pneumonia require mechanical ventilation, and the risk of complications increases with prolonged ventilatory support [3]. Mechanical ventilation itself may lead to a variety of complications, including ventilator‐associated pneumonia (VAP), airway injury and aspiration. These complications not only contribute to patient morbidity but also place enormous pressure on healthcare resources. VAP is one of the most prevalent complications, with an incidence rate ranging from 9% to 27% and a mortality rate between 20% and 50%. The occurrence of VAP has been linked to prolonged hospital stays as well as escalating healthcare costs and mortality rates [4]. These complications collectively contribute to longer hospitalizations, higher medical expenses and increased mortality.

In China, although research has been conducted on nursing management and airway care for patients with severe pneumonia, the use and effectiveness of a nursing checklist for structured airway care have been explored in only a limited number of studies. In a study conducted at the First Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine, it was found that airway grading management yielded improved outcomes in mechanically ventilated patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Patients were assessed using an airway grading scale, and different frequencies of chest physiotherapy were implemented based on the scores. By the 10th day of mechanical ventilation, patients in the observation group had better outcomes in tidal volume, respiratory rate, arterial blood CO2 partial pressure and other physiological indices compared to the control group. Additionally, the duration of mechanical ventilation was significantly shorter in the observation group [5].

Researchers in China have primarily focussed on improving the technical proficiency of nursing staff and standardizing nursing procedures, while systematic research and efficacy analyses of using nursing checklists remain insufficient [6, 7]. Internationally, the impact of nursing interventions on airway management during mechanical ventilation in patients with severe pneumonia has been examined in several studies [8, 9]. The use of appropriate airway management strategies for mechanically ventilated patients has been emphasized in the clinical practice guidelines of the American Association for Respiratory Care (AARC) for effectively reducing the incidence of complications and improving survival rates [10].

In addition, it has been shown in several studies that the quality of airway management has a direct impact on patient prognosis. Interventions such as airway humidification and sputum suctioning significantly enhance respiratory function. Significant differences in the effectiveness of artificial airway humidification between continuous oxygen nebulization and continuous drip humidification solutions were found in a systematic review [11]. Nurse‐led airway management strategies, such as regular turning, oral care and suctioning, to reduce the incidence of VAP have been investigated [12]. Another area of focus has been the role of nursing checklists in improving quality of care and patient safety [13]. Care Bundles, introduced by the Institute for Healthcare Improvement (IHI), are defined as the integration of 3–5 evidence‐based interventions into a cohesive unit; when all components are implemented simultaneously, they significantly improve patient outcomes compared with individual interventions [14]. The nursing checklist serves as an effective implementation tool for Care Bundles, ensuring the complete delivery of evidence‐based interventions through standardized, visualized step‐by‐step verification. However, research evidence on the specific effectiveness of using a nursing checklist for comprehensive airway and ventilator care during mechanical ventilation in patients with severe pneumonia remains limited.

The aim of this study in China was to assess the effectiveness of using a nursing checklist during mechanical ventilation in patients with severe pneumonia. The findings are intended to provide evidence‐based support for clinical nursing practice and facilitate more effective airway management to improve patient prognosis and establish a scientific basis for improving the quality of clinical care and patient safety.

Methods

Participants

This study was designed as a prospective, assessor‐blind, single‐centre randomized controlled trial. A total of 100 patients diagnosed with severe pneumonia and requiring mechanical ventilation and intensive care at the Department of Intensive Care Medicine of a hospital between December 2022 and December 2024 were included in the study.

Informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee of Peking University International Hospital (No. 2025‐KY‐0039‐01, 25‐06‐2025). This waiver was granted because all recruited patients were mechanically ventilated and unable to provide consent due to their critical condition, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable regulations for minimal‐risk research in emergency settings. As an alternative safeguard, the next‐of‐kin or legal representatives of all enrolled patients were notified of the study at the earliest opportunity following ICU admission. They were provided with written information about the study objectives, procedures and potential risks, and their verbal or written acknowledgment of the patient's participation was documented in the medical records. The Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines were adhered to in the trial.

Calculation of Sample Size

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the effectiveness of using a nursing checklist‐based structured airway care protocol in reducing the duration of mechanical ventilation in patients who were critically ill. The required sample size was estimated based on data from previous studies. For instance, in one related study, the mean duration of mechanical ventilation in the intervention group was reported to be 11.19 days (SD = 1.05 days), while the mean duration in the control group was 15.32 days (SD = 2.68 days) [17]. Assuming a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a statistical power (1‐β) of 0.80, the required sample size was calculated using G*Power, version 3.1.9.7 (University of Düsseldorf). As per the analysis, 32 patients per group (64 in total) were required for adequate statistical power. To account for a 20% attrition rate, the target sample size was adjusted to 80 patients (40 per group). In order to further enhance the statistical power and robustness of the study, a total of 100 patients (50 per group) were ultimately enrolled, which exceeds the minimum requirement.

Study Randomization

To ensure the integrity and validity of the study, a randomized controlled trial design was employed. The randomization process was conducted using a computer‐generated random sequence by an independent statistician who was not involved in participant recruitment or clinical management. Eligible patients were enrolled in the study after informed consent was obtained from them by the study coordinator. Each participant was assigned a unique identification number upon enrolment.

The randomization sequence was accessed by the study coordinator through a secure online platform or by calling a designated phone number to obtain the allocation result for the next enrolled participant. Participants were randomly assigned to either the nursing checklist group or the routine airway management group. To minimize bias, outcome assessors and participants were blinded to group assignments; however, the nurses who delivered the interventions could not be blinded due to the nature of the nursing checklist intervention. The randomization process and participant allocation results were kept confidential and were known only to the statistician responsible for generating the random sequence and the study coordinator overseeing participant recruitment.

Outcome Parameters

Duration of Mechanical Ventilation

The duration of mechanical ventilation was defined as the time from the initiation of mechanical ventilation to the successful withdrawal, recorded in hours. Data on the mechanical ventilation duration were collected through the Hospital Information System (HIS) and nursing records. Specific records were maintained from the time of tracheal intubation to the point of successful weaning from the mechanical ventilation and extubation. A standardized nursing record form was used to document the start and end times of mechanical ventilation. This record sheet included basic patient information, ventilation initiation and termination times, and the reason for withdrawal.

Length of Stay in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU)

The length of stay in the ICU was defined as the duration from ICU admission to discharge, recorded in days. Data were collected through the HIS and nursing records. The start time was recorded upon ICU admission, and the end time was documented upon transfer or discharge from the ICU. A standardized nursing record form was used to document basic patient information, admission and discharge times for each ICU stay, and the reason for transfer out of the ICU.

Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) Score

The APACHE II score is a widely used tool in critical care medicine for assessing disease severity and predicting patient prognosis [18]. This scoring system integrates the physiological parameters, age and chronic health status of the patient. Although APACHE II scores may vary across studies, their utility in evaluating critically ill patients has been well‐established. In this study, the baseline APACHE II scores of patients were assessed within the first 24 h of admission to the ICU and subsequently recorded on Days 3, 7, and 14 of the patient's stay in the ICU.

Respiratory Parameters: Tidal Volume (TV), Respiratory Rate (f), and Oxygenation Index (PaO2 /FiO2 )

Interventions

Nursing Checklist (NC) Group

In the NC group, patients were administered interventions based on a pre‐established standardized checklist. The checklist was developed based on relevant practice guidelines [19], existing hospital airway management protocols and relevant research findings. The following are the specific intervention steps for the NC group.

The checklist was developed by a multidisciplinary team of hospital personnel comprising critical care physicians, nurses, respiratory therapists and infection control specialists. The checklist covered all aspects of airway management, including regular assessment of the patient's airway status focussing on airway patency, secretion management, oxygenation status and the patient's tolerance to mechanical ventilation, and relevant airway management measures (Table 1). It also included the implementation of preventive measures to minimize airway complications, including regular oral care, elevation of the head of the bed and timely adjustments of ventilator parameters. Respiratory mechanics parameters, blood gas analysis results and infection markers were continuously monitored. Suctioning, airway humidification, adjustment of ventilator settings and administration of necessary medications were performed in a timely manner as per the patient's specific condition.

All healthcare providers involved in the intervention underwent standardized training on the use of the checklist and were required to strictly adhere to the prescribed protocol. Each participant's care process was documented in detail on the checklist to ensure compliance with all outlined measures.

Routine Airway Management Group

Patients in the routine airway management group were administered the hospital's current standard airway management practices and were cared for according to the hospital's existing airway management guidelines and procedures. Standard care included regular airway assessments, suctioning procedures, oral hygiene care and ventilator management. Airway humidification was maintained using a passive approach with a heated humidifier filter. Patient progress and any relevant clinical events were documented by the nursing staff in accordance with hospital regulations.

Mechanical Ventilation Modes and Parameters

A pressure‐controlled ventilation strategy was used, with the following initial parameters: respiratory rate (f): 10–12 breaths per minute; pressure control: 12–20 cm H2O; inspiratory duration: 0.80–1.20 s; oxygen concentration: 40%–50%; positive end‐expiratory pressure (PEEP): 3–8 cm H2O; and flow trigger sensitivity: 1.5–3 L/min.

When patients met the withdrawal criteria [20], they were transitioned to pressure‐support ventilation mode with the following parameters: pressure support: 8–15 cm H2O; oxygen concentration: 30%–40%; PEEP: 3–5 cm H2O; and flow‐triggered sensitivity: 1.5–3 L/min.

All ventilation parameters were adjusted within the specific ranges based on the patient's pulmonary function and clinical status.

Statistical Analysis

Data were processed and analysed using the SPSS 27.0 statistical software. Categorical variables were analysed using the χ2 test to assess associations. For continuous variables, the independent samples t‐test was used for group comparisons and the paired samples t‐test was used for within‐group comparisons over time. Results were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). A two‐sided test was used, with p < 0.05 as the threshold for statistical significance. A p‐value < 0.05 for differences between the two groups was considered statistically significant.

Respiratory Parameters (TV, f and PaO2/FiO2)

At baseline, no significant differences were observed between patients in the NC and RM groups in terms of TV (t = 0.186, p = 0.853), f (t = −0.798, p = 0.427) or PaO2/FiO2 (t = −0.871, p = 0.386). On Day 3, the differences in PaO2/FiO2 (t = 1.594, p = 0.114), TV (t = 1.039, p = 0.302) and f (t = 0.797, p = 0.428) between the two groups remained statistically insignificant. By Day 7, the differences in TV (t = 11.789, p < 0.001), f (t = −12.953, p < 0.001) and PaO2/FiO2 (t = 20.087, p < 0.001) between the NC group and the RM group were statistically significant. By Day 14, the differences in TV (t = 7.247, p < 0.001), f (t = −11.491, p < 0.001) and PaO2/FiO2 (t = 4.413, p < 0.001) further increased and were statistically significant.

From these results, it can be seen that the differences in respiratory parameters between the two groups of patients became more pronounced over time, favouring the NC group (Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, the duration of mechanical ventilation and length of stay in the ICU were compared between patients in the NC and RM groups. It was seen that the duration of mechanical ventilation in the NC group was significantly shorter than that in the RM group (p < 0.001), whereas the difference in length of stay in the ICU between the two groups was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). These findings suggest that the use of the nursing checklist was effective in reducing the duration of mechanical ventilation but had a limited impact on length of stay in the ICU. This result is similar to the findings of Li, who, through a meta‐analysis, demonstrated that early activity nursing could effectively shorten mechanical ventilation time but had no significant effect on the length of ICU stay [21]. Reducing mechanical ventilation duration is critical for improving prognosis in patients with severe pneumonia [22]. Prolonged mechanical ventilation not only increases the risk of complications but also extends ICU stays, leading to higher use of medical resources and greater economic burdens on patients [23].

The impact of various interventions on mechanical ventilation duration and ICU stay in critically ill patients has been investigated in several studies [24]. In a network meta‐analysis that included 37 randomized controlled trials involving 3977 critically ill patients, it was seen that both modified ventilation (MV) and combined intervention (COR) were significantly more effective in reducing both the duration of mechanical ventilation and ICU stay. Specifically, MV was found to outperform COR (SMD = −2.35, 95% CI: −4.30 to −0.39), early pulmonary rehabilitation (SMD = −2.59, 95% CI: −4.81 to −0.37) and usual care (UC) (SMD = −4.10, 95% CI: −5.71 to −2.49) in improving the duration of mechanical ventilation. Additionally, COR was found to be significantly better than UC in reducing length of stay in the ICU (SMD = −5.72, 95% CI: −10.07 to −1.37) [25].

In another study, the use of a progressive early activity program based on the International Classification of Functioning (ICF), Disability and Health concept in mechanically ventilated patients in the ICU was investigated [26]. It was seen that the duration of mechanical ventilation and ICU stay in the intervention group was significantly shorter than that of the control group (p < 0.05), and the incidence of delirium and ICU‐acquired debility was significantly lower (p < 0.05) while muscle strength was higher in the intervention group compared to the control group (p < 0.05). These findings suggest that a progressive early activity program is an effective and safe intervention that can reduce mechanical ventilation duration and ICU stays while improving overall patient outcomes. Regarding the validity and reliability of the present study, several factors merit consideration. First, the prospective randomized controlled design, standardized intervention protocol and predefined outcome measures enhance internal validity by minimizing selection bias and confounding. Second, the primary outcomes, including TV, f and the PaO2/FiO2 ratio, are objective physiological parameters measured directly from mechanical ventilation equipment and blood gas analysis. This ensures measurement reliability and reduces observer‐dependent variability. The APACHE II score is also a widely validated, standardized scoring system for assessing disease severity and prognosis, which further supports the reliability of outcome assessment. Third, to ensure intervention fidelity, all nurses in the NC group underwent standardized training on the use of the checklist and were required to strictly adhere to the prescribed protocol. Compliance was monitored through documentation review, strengthening the reliability of intervention delivery. However, as a single‐centre study, the generalizability of the findings, that is, external validity, may be limited. The patient population, healthcare setting and clinical practices in a single institution may not fully represent those in other regions or hospitals. Therefore, multi‐centre studies with larger and more diverse samples are needed to confirm the generalizability of our findings.

In the present study in China, differences in APACHE II scores and respiratory parameters such as the TV, f and PaO2/FiO2 were compared between the NC and RM groups. It was seen that, by Day 14, patients in the NC group had significantly lower APACHE II scores compared to those in the RM group (p < 0.001). In addition, outcomes in the NC group, in terms of TV, f and PaO2/FiO2, were significantly improved at both Day 7 and Day 14 compared to the RM group (p < 0.001). These results are consistent with the findings of Wanfei et al. that airway grading management combined with early‐stage planned rehabilitation exercises significantly improved the prognosis of patients with severe pneumonia [27]. TV is directly related to mechanical power and represents a critical parameter for preventing ventilator‐induced lung injury (VILI). Excessive TV may lead to barotrauma and volutrauma, whereas insufficient TV can result in CO2 retention and hypoventilation; therefore, precise adjustment based on airway grading is essential [28]. Respiratory rate serves as an important indicator for assessing ventilatory demand, respiratory muscle strength and the ability to maintain adequate ventilation [29]. The PaO2/FiO2 ratio is a core metric for grading oxygenation status and lung injury severity, and its distribution under different ventilatory strategies can predict the risk of treatment failure [30]. In addition, a cluster care strategy based on list management has been shown to enhance the quality of care and reduce complication rates [31]. These findings suggest that the implementation of a nursing checklist during mechanical ventilation for patients with severe pneumonia holds considerable clinical value.

The PaO2/FiO2 ratio is widely used as a key oxygenation index to assess the respiratory status and prognosis of critically ill patients. In a study, PaO2/FiO2 was identified as an independent risk factor for mortality in patients with COVID‐19 who were critically ill (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.928–0.994, p = 0.021) [32]. The findings of the present study, of significantly higher PaO2/FiO2 among patients in the NC group compared to the RM group at Day 14, are consistent with these results, suggesting that improved PaO2/FiO2 rates are closely associated with improved patient prognosis. Additionally, in a related study, it was reported that arterial hyperoxia (PaO2 > 300 mmHg) was correlated with increased in‐hospital mortality among patients in a cardiac intensive care unit (CICU) setting [33]. This underscores the importance of avoiding hyperoxia during mechanical ventilation to minimize potential complications. In the present study, the improved PaO2/FiO2 rates observed among patients in the NC group compared to the RM group may be attributable to the use of the nursing checklist, which facilitated more accurate oxygen management and prevented over‐oxygenation.

The findings of this study not only provide empirical evidence supporting the use of a nursing checklist during mechanical ventilation in patients with severe pneumonia but also offer valuable insights for further research. The focus of future studies can be exploring the effectiveness of using a nursing checklist in managing other critical illnesses and investigating ways to further optimize the content and implementation of such nursing checklists. In addition, integrating modern information technologies, such as electronic medical record systems, could enhance the efficiency and accuracy of checklist‐based interventions.

Limitations of the Study

Despite the promising results of this study, there are some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, as the study was conducted in a single centre, the results may not fully reflect variations in patient populations, healthcare settings or clinical practices in other regions. Third, this trial was not formally registered in a public clinical trial registry (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov) prior to participant enrolment. While this does not affect the internal validity of the findings, trial registration is increasingly recognized as a standard practice for enhancing research transparency and preventing selective reporting. Future studies should ensure prospective registration.

In addition, due to constraints in the study design, the potential influence of unmeasured confounding factors could not be entirely ruled out. To enhance the reliability and generalizability of the results, a multi‐centre design with a larger sample size and more comprehensive control of confounding variables must be adopted in future research.

Implications for Clinical Practice and Future Research

The nursing checklist developed in this study provides a standardized tool for structured airway care. It operationalizes evidence‐based interventions, including airway assessment, oral care, head‐of‐bed elevation and ventilator parameter adjustment, into actionable step‐by‐step procedures. ICU nurses can use this checklist to ensure consistency in care delivery and enhance adherence to best‐practice protocols. The checklist aligns with the care bundle philosophy and can be adapted to accommodate dynamic changes in patient condition.

For clinical practice, this checklist offers several tangible benefits for bedside ICU nurses. It reduces cognitive load by providing a structured reminder of essential interventions during high‐stress situations. It also facilitates interdisciplinary communication by standardizing documentation and ensuring that all team members are aware of completed and pending care elements.

Several areas warrant further investigation. Multi‐centre randomized controlled trials with larger and more diverse samples are needed to validate the generalizability of our findings. The long‐term sustainability of checklist effects beyond the 14‐day observation period remains uncertain, and studies with extended follow‐up are required. The effectiveness of this checklist in other mechanically ventilated populations, such as patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome or those recovering from cardiac arrest, also deserves exploration. In addition, integration of the checklist into electronic health record systems could streamline documentation, though the feasibility and cost‐effectiveness of such integration require formal evaluation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated the significant efficacy of using a nursing checklist during mechanical ventilation in patients with severe pneumonia. By optimizing nursing workflows and enhancing the quality of care, the implementation of a nursing checklist was found to significantly reduce the duration of mechanical ventilation as well as improve patients' oxygenation status and prognosis. Despite its limitations, this study provides valuable insights for both clinical practice and future research. Given its potential benefits, it is recommended that the use of a nursing checklist be widely adopted in clinical settings to improve the quality and safety of care for patients with severe pneumonia.
