Are we judicious enough regarding usage of opioids for traumatic brain injury?
Authors: Marie Hanna, Shruti Sudhakar
Published: 2025

The devastating opioid crisis in the USA has deep and multifaceted roots, closely linked to the healthcare system’s management of the “fifth vital sign”—pain. Prescribing providers have contributed to the crisis primarily through overprescribing opioids for pain management which exposed patients to addiction and caused enough overdose deaths to make it the leading cause of death in the USA. Significant attention has been brought to this issue and several national agencies, such as the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in 2022, sought to provide guidelines to reduce opioid exposure, stressing utilization of multimodal therapies to mitigate opioid dependency.1 A Johns Hopkins Expert Panel, consisting of surgeons, pain specialists, and pharmacists, created recommendations to limit the number of tablets to 0–20 tablets for common uncomplicated general surgery, orthopedic, and gynecological surgeries. Interestingly, a consistent trend observed was that many of the patients who underwent those procedures voted for lower opioid amounts than the surgeons who performed them.2 Forward-thinking strategies have been implemented by individual institutions to address this problem. One group of emergency physicians altered the default opioid dose in their electronic medical record from “every 6 hours as needed” to “1 tablet”, which led to the reduction of prescriptions greater than 50 Morphine Milligram Equivalent dose (MMED) from 46% to 1.6%.3 Another group developed an innovative perioperative pain program at Johns Hopkins with multidisciplinary teams including acute and chronic pain specialists, psychiatrists, and physical medicine services to address postoperative pain regimen with greater success than traditional approaches.4 Although there are many encouraging articles presenting small victories, there is very limited evidence regarding a national trend.

Medvecz et al seeks to answer the question of whether outpatient opioid and non-opioid analgesia prescribing trends have changed between 2016 and 2022 with increased prescriber awareness in the traumatic brain injury and long-bone fractures.5 Using Merative MarketScan commercial claims data, the authors were able to analyze nationally the prescribing trends of trauma patients. They were able to show patients with all injury types observed a decrease in opioid prescribing and conversely an increase in gabapentin, a non-opioid and notable component of multimodal analgesia. However, despite the encouraging downward trend, opioids remain a significant component of postdischarge pain management (54.6% in 2022 vs 69.9% in 2016). The strength of this article is that it is one of the largest data sets that represent opioid prescriber behavior. It also showed regional trends with lowest opioid prescribing rates in the Northeast and increased usage among those with concurrent long-bone fractures. Unfortunately, it is unable to capture uninsured populations, severity of injury, patient postoperative pain control, and how much of the prescription was used. Post-traumatic brain injury patients are already at increased risk for depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, and polysubstance use disorders; thus, they may have similar vulnerabilities to opioid abuse.6 More research is necessary to guide posttraumatic pain management that proves effective in pain control and concurrently reduces opioid-related harm among this vulnerable patient population. This article is an excellent start to understanding how effective our initial interventions have been and will help us guide future efforts.
