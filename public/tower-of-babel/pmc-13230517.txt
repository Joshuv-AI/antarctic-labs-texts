Latent profile analysis of health-related quality of life among older adults with age-related thoracic hyperkyphosis
Authors: Xinyun Wang, Qilin Sun, Lili Sun, Zhaodi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To explore the potential classes of health-related quality of life (HRQOL) among older adults with age-related thoracic hyperkyphosis (ARTH) and examine the factors associated with each profile. From April to October 2025, 350 older adults with ARTH were recruited via convenience sampling from a community health center in Shaoxing City, Zhejiang Province. Each completed questionnaires on Scoliosis Research Society-22, Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly, Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale, 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale, Social Support Rating Scale, and Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire. Latent profile analysis was conducted to categorize HRQOL patterns, and multinomial logistic regression was used to analyze intergroup differences and identify associated factors for the different profiles. Three latent HRQOL profiles were identified, with self-image serving as the core dimension that distinguished among the classes: a low HRQOL with poor function group (25.7%), a moderate HRQOL group (45.7%), and a high HRQOL group (28.6%). Significant differences were observed across these profiles in terms of age, occiput-wall distance, physical activity level, depressive symptoms, self-esteem, social support, and illness perception. The multinomial logistic regression analysis identified age, depressive symptoms, self-esteem, and illness perception as independent factors associated with membership in the HRQOL profile. The results demonstrate considerable heterogeneity in HRQOL among older adults with ARTH. Healthcare providers should pay close attention to self-image concerns in this target population, as well as in patients with low HRQOL and poor functional profiles, and design targeted intervention strategies based on the distinct characteristics of each subgroup to enhance their quality of life effectively.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47384-y.

Introduction

Thoracic hyperkyphosis is defined as an exaggerated anterior curvature of the thoracic spine, clinically evident as a hunched posture. Age-related thoracic hyperkyphosis (ARTH) refers to the progressive increase in the outward curvature of the thoracic spine beyond the normal range as individuals age. It is also considered a new geriatric syndrome1. It is the most common postural deformity in the elderly population, typically emerging around age 502. Clinically, it is characterized by an abnormally accentuated thoracic concavity exceeding 40°3. Furthermore, research has shown that approximately 62.5% to 75.2% of older adults in the community exhibit excessive thoracic curvature, with mean hyperkyphosis angles approaching or exceeding 45°4,5.

Moreover, in the elderly population, ARTH is closely associated with osteoporosis6. The condition not only exacerbates existing risks in osteoporosis patients (such as pain, height loss, respiratory dysfunction, and fractures) but also further impairs their postural stability and balance, leading to difficulties in performing activities of daily living, as well as physical impairment and psychological damage7,8. Consequently, patients with thoracic hyperkyphosis report increased difficulty with physical activities, significant adaptations in daily living, heightened apprehension about the future, and more constrained social engagement9. The condition also adversely affects physical appearance in older adults, frequently leading to feelings of inferiority and depression10, which in turn diminishes their satisfaction with health status and overall quality of life and well-being. In light of these multifaceted issues, there is a clear need for holistic long-term care strategies that address not only survival but also the enhancement of patients’ overall quality of life.

Health-related quality of life (HRQOL) refers to how people perceive and experience their health across cultures and life situations. It encompasses their goals, hopes, values, and lifestyle11. It shows their physical, mental, social, spiritual, and role well-being. Using HRQOL checks in thoracic hyperkyphosis care enables doctors to develop more effective care plans for patients12. Still, most research focuses on the frequency of thoracic hyperkyphosis and its associated problems, rather than on how patients themselves perceive their condition13–15. Even when HRQOL is studied, researchers mostly use scores from a single time point and do not examine differences between patients10,16. Latent profile analysis (LPA) is a method for grouping patients based on their characteristics and for identifying distinct HRQOL types within each group17.

In terms of influencing factors, existing research has predominantly focused on biological or clinical predictors18,19 (such as bone density and muscle mass), often failing to adequately incorporate psychological and social factors that may account for variations in HRQOL. Simultaneously, due to the absence of a systematic theoretical framework to guide research design, variable selection in current studies tends to be fragmented and inconsistent. This limitation limits the explanatory power of such research in terms of correlations and interactions among variables, as well as its ability to reflect patients’ lived experiences. The Common-Sense Model of Self-Regulation (CSM) is a self-regulation theory20. According to the CSM, when individuals perceive a threat to their health and well-being, they form cognitive and emotional representations of threat, which subsequently influence their coping strategies and health outcomes. Existing research has confirmed that the CSM is well-suited for exploring factors related to HRQOL21. Therefore, this study aims to explore the heterogeneity of HRQOL among older adults with ARTH using LPA, and based on CSM, to determine whether psychological and social factors are associated with membership in specific HRQOL profiles. This study provides a theoretical basis for clinicians to develop targeted interventions for older adults with ARTH who exhibit different HRQOL characteristics.

Methods

Participants

Convenience sampling was used in this study. From April to October 2025, older adults with ARTH who received diagnosis and treatment services at a community health center in Shaoxing City, Zhejiang Province, were selected as survey participants. As a representative coastal city in Eastern China, Shaoxing’s population of those aged 60 and over was 1.406 million, accounting for 31.76% of the total population, which was higher than the national average of 10.66%22. The inclusion criteria were as follows:(a) Age ≥ 60 years; (b) Clear consciousness and able to answer questions independently; (c) Signed informed consent; (d) occiput-to-wall distance (OWD) ≥ 6.5 cm, measured three times by healthcare staff and averaged. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) Severe cognitive or psychiatric disorders; (b) Thoracic hyperkyphosis due to pathological causes such as ankylosing spondylitis or spinal tuberculosis; (c) Comorbidities with other serious physical diseases; (d) Inability to walk independently; (e) Participation in other clinical trials.

Sample size

To determine the suitable sample size for this study, established guidelines for conducting sample size calculations in the context of LPA were followed23. Based on LPA requirements, a minimum sample size of 300 was targeted to ensure accurate profile detection. With 10% of the samples invalid, the minimum requirement is 330 cases. Moreover, the five-class model remained robust even with a sample size of 350, as evidenced by its high entropy (> 0.80) and clear differentiation among latent profiles. Therefore, a final sample size of 350 was selected for this investigation. All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and we obtained informed consent from the participants and their families. The Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine has approved this research project. (Approval No. IIT20250488B).

Measures

Measurement of thoracic hyperkyphosis

Participants assumed a standing position with their heels, sacrum, and back in contact with a wall. The head was kept neutral, and the gaze was directed forward. The occiput-to-wall distance (OWD) was measured using two rulers. One ruler was positioned horizontally against the occiput, while the other determined the perpendicular distance to the wall. Three measurements were taken for each participant. The mean value was used for analysis. Rest periods were provided as needed. Trained healthcare personnel conducted all assessments using a standardized protocol24,25.

The OWD is a non-invasive measure widely used in epidemiological studies for large-scale screening and monitoring of thoracic hyperkyphosis. It shows a significant correlation with the radiographically measured Cobb angle. The method demonstrates excellent reliability (ICC > 0.90, p < 0.001) and good validity (r = 0.683, p < 0.001). An OWD of 6.5 cm or higher, which corresponds to a Cobb angle greater than 40°, indicates thoracic hyperkyphosis26. This cut-off value demonstrates favorable diagnostic performance, with a sensitivity of 71.4%, a specificity of 76.6%, and an area under the curve of 0.84625,26.

Investigation tools

Scoliosis Research Society-22 (SRS-22)

The SRS-22 questionnaire was initially developed to evaluate quality of life in patients with adolescent idiopathic scoliosis27. Existing studies have confirmed that it is equally applicable to assessing quality of life in patients with thoracic hyperkyphosis and has been widely adopted for this purpose28–30. This scale comprises 22 items, organized into five domains: function, pain, mental health, self-image, and satisfaction with management. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Given that no treatment was administered to the patients in this study, items 21 and 22, which pertain to "satisfaction with management," were excluded from evaluation. Higher scores on the scale indicate a better quality of life. Previous studies have reported Cronbach’s α coefficients ranging from 0.85 to 0.96, demonstrating good reliability of the scale31. In the present study, Cronbach’s α for this scale was 0.867.

Physical Activity Scale for The Elderly (PASE)

The PASE was developed by Washburn and used to measure physical activity32. The scale includes 12 questions on the frequency and duration of leisure, household, and work-related activities over the last 7-day period. The total PASE score is calculated by multiplying the time spent on each activity (in hours per week) or the participation in an activity (yes/no) by the corresponding item weight, and then summing the scores across all activities. The overall PASE score ranges from 0 to 400, with higher scores indicating greater physical activity. The PASE has demonstrated good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.7333. The Cronbach’s α for the scale was 0.735 in the present study.

Rosenberg Self‐Esteem Scale (RESE)

Self-esteem was assessed using the RESE, developed by Rosenberg34. The scale consists of 10 items, five of which are positively and five negatively worded. Each item employs a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). The total score, obtained by summing all items, ranges from 10 to 40, with higher scores indicating higher levels of self-esteem. It is one of the most widely used measures of global self-esteem. The scale had a Cronbach’s α of 0.832, which in this study was 0.751, indicating high internal consistency.

Geriatric Depression Scale-15 (GDS-15)

Depression was assessed using the GDS-15, an adaptation of the original 30-item GDS by Sheikh and Yesavage35. The Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS): Recent evidence and development of a shorter version. The scale consists of 15 items, each answered with a “yes” or "no." A “yes” response earns 1 point, while a “no” response earns 0 points. A total score of 5 or higher indicates the presence of depression. Shin reported a Cronbach’s α of 0.86 for the GDS-15 in a Chinese elderly population36. In the present study, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.728).

Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS)

The Chinese version of the SSRS, developed by Xiao et al.37, was used to assess individuals’ perceived level of social support. The scale consists of 10 items measuring three dimensions: subjective support, objective support, and utilization of social support. Responses are recorded on a 4-point Likert scale, with a total score ranging from 10 to 40; higher scores indicate greater social support. The scale demonstrated good reliability, with a reported Cronbach’s α of 0.857 and, in this study, a Cronbach’s α of 0.858.

Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (BIPQ)

The BIPQ was developed by Broadbent et al.38. This instrument comprises 9 items that assess three dimensions: disease cognition, emotion, and disease understanding. Each item is rated on a 10-point Likert scale, with the ninth open-ended item excluded from the total score. Higher scores reflect more negative perceptions of and emotional responses to the illness. The scale demonstrated good reliability, with a reported Cronbach’s α of 0.770 and, in the current study, a Cronbach’s α of 0.749.

Data collection

Before the formal survey, a pre-test was conducted with five selected participants to estimate the time required to complete the questionnaires. All investigators received standardized training. Before distributing the questionnaires, the purpose, significance, and instructions of the study were explained to the patients using a prepared script, and written informed consent was obtained. A one-on-one, paper-based survey was then administered. For participants with limited literacy or visual impairment, the investigator read each question aloud and recorded the patient’s choice. Questionnaires were collected on-site and immediately checked for completeness. Any missing responses were supplemented promptly. After data collection, two researchers independently verified and entered the data. Invalid questionnaires—such as those with patterned responses or obvious logical inconsistencies—were excluded from the analysis.

Data analysis

Use Mplus 8.3 and SPSS 29.0 software for data analysis. An initial descriptive analysis of all variables was performed using SPSS 29.0. Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations, while categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and proportions. This process also served to examine the data for implausible or outlying values and to assess the extent of missing data. And then, LPA was used to explore the latent categories of patients’ HRQOL, identifying subclasses based on the four dimensions of the SRS-22. We systematically fitted a series of models, starting with a single-class model and progressively increasing the number of classes from 1 to 5. The optimal model was selected by evaluating goodness-of-fit indices and considering clinical feasibility and interpretability. The criteria for assessing model fit are outlined below. (1) Information criteria: The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (aBIC) were used to evaluate model fit. Lower values of AIC, BIC, and aBIC indicate a better-fitting model. (2) Classification accuracy: Entropy was used to assess classification accuracy. Entropy values range from 0 to 1; values greater than or equal to 0.80 indicate a good profile solution. (3) Likelihood ratio tests: The Lo-Mendell-Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMRT) and the Bootstrapped Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT) were employed to compare the fit between a model with k-1 profiles and a model with k profiles. A significant p-value (p < 0.05) suggests that the k-profile model provides a better fit than the (k-1)-profile model. Next, differences in the SRS-22 subscales across the three classes were examined using analysis of variance (ANOVA). Furthermore, demographic and psychosocial variables were compared across potential HRQOL subtypes using the chi-squared test for categorical variables and ANOVA for continuous variables. To explore the factors associated with the identified HRQOL profiles, multinomial logistic regression analysis was performed. Prior to conducting the multinomial logistic regression analysis, all continuous variables were standardized using the Z-score method, and multicollinearity diagnostics were performed for all independent variables. In all analyses, a two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Descriptive statistics and correlations

A total of 350 older adults with ARTH were analyzed in the study, with a mean (SD) age of 75.03 (7.902) years (range: 61–98 years). Among them, 161 (46.0%) were females and 189 (54.0%) were males. The majority had an education level of elementary school or below (76.3%), and 14.6% of the patients were living alone. And the mean occiput-to-wall distance (OWD) was (7.49 ± 0.66) cm. On the SRS-22 questionnaire, the scores for the function, pain, self-image, and mental health domains were (2.76 ± 0.56), (2.62 ± 0.39), (2.55 ± 0.41), and (2.60 ± 0.41), respectively. The physical activity score was (196.32 ± 41.48), and the illness perception score was (38.77 ± 10.25). In terms of psychosocial variables, the scores for depression, self-esteem, and social support were (2.67 ± 1.49), (20.02 ± 3.58), and (36.58 ± 4.74), respectively. The bivariate correlations among the study variables are presented in Fig. 1. The study found that the domains of HRQOL demonstrated significant negative correlations with the patient’s age, OWD, illness perception, and depressive symptoms, as well as significant positive correlations with self-esteem, physical activity (including leisure time and work-related activities), and social support (utilization of support and cognitive illness perception). Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first reported study to use LPA with a large sample of older adults with ARTH and to identify different HRQOL categories and their influencing factors. This cross-sectional study aimed to analyze differences in HRQOL patterns by CSM, while controlling for personal factors, physical activity, depression, self-esteem, social support, and illness perception.

Potential classes of HRQOL

This study identified three latent HRQOL classes among older adults with ARTH, with significant heterogeneity across these classes. The high HRQOL group accounted for 28.6% of participants and was characterized by greater physical activity and milder pain. However, their mental health scores were relatively lower compared to other dimensions, indicating potential psychological concerns. This may stem from a heightened focus on self-image in these patients, in whom the visible deformity of thoracic hyperkyphosis can foster stigma and impair psychological well-being39. Consequently, clinicians should prioritize mental health support for this group, providing positive reinforcement, guiding patients to recognize their strengths, and facilitating healthy emotional expression to strengthen self-identity. The moderate HRQOL group constituted the largest proportion (45.7%) and was characterized by relatively severe pain. Studies have shown that a significant number of patients with thoracic hyperkyphosis suffer from chronic pain, which is also the primary reason for seeking medical care12,40. A hyperkyphotic posture not only aggravates postural back pain in older adults with ARTH but also increases the risk of falls and subsequent fractures6. Therefore, healthcare providers should proactively offer patients pain relief strategies, such as postural training, exercise, myofascial release, massage, and cognitive-behavioral therapy41. The low HRQOL group with poor function accounted for 25.7% of the patients. Individuals in this group exhibited significantly impaired functional mobility. This may be related to severe thoracic hyperkyphosis, which can lead to an inability to straighten the lower back, reduced knee flexibility, and ankle stiffness, among other limitations15. These factors significantly hinder patients’ physical activities and, over time, create a vicious cycle that leads to a substantial decline in their quality of life. For this group of patients, it is recommended that interventions (e.g., corrective, weight-bearing, stretching, and rehabilitation exercises) be tailored to individual conditions.

Additionally, our study has revealed the importance of self-image in classifying HRQOL among older adults with ARTH. The mean self-image score among participants in this study was 2.55, indicating the lowest dimension on the SRS-22 scale. It was substantially lower than those reported by Tuba et al.30 and Jin et al.42. This may be attributed to the fact that the patients in this study were predominantly elderly farmers, whose severe kyphosis resulting from prolonged farming and heavy physical labor thus affected their perception of self-image. Unlike common chronic conditions such as hypertension, thoracic hyperkyphosis is characterized by a drooping postural deformity43. Changes in physical appearance (such as forward head posture and winged scapula) may contribute to body image disturbances; these alterations not only trigger negative cognitions and emotions, such as low self-esteem and fear44, but may also lead to social avoidance behaviors, further exacerbating social alienation. In recent years, multiple studies have demonstrated that body image is closely related to the subjective well-being of older adults, and satisfaction with body image can directly affect self-evaluation and quality of life45,46. Therefore, healthcare professionals should pay greater attention to how these patients perceive their own body image. It is essential to provide group-based support focused on self-image, develop diverse kyphosis-specific exercise programs, and strengthen the role of social support to help patients gain more emotional support and confidence.

Associated factors of potential classes of HRQOL

The results indicated that older patients were more likely to be classified into the "low HRQOL with poor function" group. Previous research has shown that age is an independent risk factor for the quality of life of patients with thoracic hyperkyphosis12. With advancing age, the severity of pain and functional impairment increases, which directly diminishes patients’ quality of life. Compared with younger older adults, older individuals exhibit more pronounced kyphotic angles, particularly among females, a difference that may be attributed to the cumulative effects of functional decline and osteoporosis47. In earlier studies, the broad age range and diverse etiologies among patients with thoracic hyperkyphosis have limited the specificity of findings. Therefore, future research incorporating longitudinal designs is recommended to clarify differences across age strata further.

It is noteworthy that the average self-esteem score of participants in this study was 20.02 ± 3.58, which is significantly lower than the score of 28.13 ± 3.57 reported by Ji among patients with chronic diseases48. This suggests that low self-esteem may be more pronounced in older adults with ARTH. Multivariate analysis confirmed that low self-esteem was significantly associated with HRQOL in the "low HRQOL with poor function group" (OR = 0.631, p = 0.008), indicating that low self-esteem is linked to worse HRQOL outcomes in older adults with ARTH. Previous studies have primarily focused on the relationship between self-esteem and quality of life in adolescent patients with spinal deformity49. However, elderly individuals with thoracic hyperkyphosis also frequently encounter social discrimination, which adversely affects their self-esteem and social relationships, leading to conflicts in body image perception. Furthermore, research by Heeß et al. confirmed that enhancing self-esteem through psychological interventions in older adults can significantly improve frailty status and reduce the prevalence of geriatric syndromes50. Therefore, greater emphasis should be placed on providing humanistic care for older adults with ARTH. Through regularly conducted professional posture training, self-image education, and group activities, healthcare professionals can affirm patients’ uniqueness and effectively enhance their self-esteem.

The study demonstrated that elevated depression scores were associated with a higher probability of older adults with ARTH being categorized into the "low HRQOL with poor function" group, indicating that depressive symptoms substantially diminish HRQOL. This finding aligns with those of Soylu et al.10. Similarly, Kim et al. observed that in patients with Parkinson’s disease and comorbid depression, the severity of depressive symptoms correlated with increased anterior head and trunk tilt51, which subsequently compromised quality of life. Further supporting this, Canales et al. noted that pharmacological amelioration of depression led to a reduction in thoracic kyphosis, underscoring a link between mood improvement and postural correction52. Depressive symptoms in this population often arise from the psychosocial stress accompanying progressive kyphosis. Chronic experience of the condition can lead to a perceived loss of control, future-related anxieties, and diminished self-worth—all of which contribute to reduced quality of life. Thus, it is essential for clinicians to routinely evaluate psychological well-being in older adults with ARTH, facilitate early detection of distress, and collaborate with psychologists to form a multidisciplinary care team. Such an approach enables a more thorough understanding of underlying psychosocial factors and provides integrated, multidimensional support to enhance quality of life. In addition, engaging family members, community resources, and other stakeholders can help ensure patients receive robust social support, dignity, and compassionate care—collectively fostering better long-term outcomes.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that HRQOL in older adults with ARTH was significantly negatively correlated with illness perception. Compared to the "low HRQOL with poor function" group, patients with lower levels of illness perception were more likely to belong to the moderate-to-high HRQOL group. According to the CSM, this finding elucidates the intrinsic link between patients’ cognitive representations and health outcomes53,54. In terms of correlates of symptom perceptions from the CSM framework, our results indicate that patients with negative perceptions of their illness typically experience greater functional and emotional difficulties and demonstrate slower recovery55, which consequently affects their health outcomes. Specifically, among patients with thoracic hyperkyphosis, those with higher levels of illness perception often develop negative emotions such as low self-esteem, anxiety, and depression due to pain, limited mobility, and postural changes56. These negative emotions may further exacerbate the kyphosis angle, leading to greater disease-related distress, severely impacting daily activities, and diminishing HRQOL57. Conversely, patients who believe they can effectively manage their condition are more likely to adopt positive coping strategies, such as actively seeking information about their disease and treatment, as well as adjusting their physical and mental state. Therefore, when aiming to improve HRQOL in this patient population, healthcare professionals should, based on the CSM, focus not only on somatic symptoms but also on patients’ cognitive levels and emotional representations. Timely implementation of interventions, including cognitive behavioral therapy and collaborative care models, is recommended to help regulate emotions and provide comprehensive physical and psychological support.

Limitations and future directions

This study is the first to use LPA to examine HRQOL among older adults with ARTH; therefore, further research is needed to support our findings. Firstly, this study has inherent limitations due to its single-center design. Given that the HRQOL and psychosocial factors of older adults are profoundly influenced by regional culture, socioeconomic status, healthcare accessibility, and even dialect, the findings cannot represent the overall status of community-dwelling older adults with ARTH in China. Therefore, future research should adopt multi-center survey methods to collect large-scale samples across multiple regions to enhance the representativeness of the research findings and supplement them with objective data, such as imaging findings and bone mineral density. Second, the cross-sectional design limits inferences about causality, allowing only for a discussion of factors that influence quality of life. Consequently, more longitudinal studies should be conducted to understand the dynamic nature of quality of life and to provide valuable insights for the timing of targeted interventions. Finally, regarding variable selection, this study was limited to common research topics such as self-esteem and depression. However, for older adults with ARTH, it is essential to incorporate and explore the relationship of critical variables such as body image, falls, frailty, and social support. Future research could include a broader range of relevant risk factors and examine intervention priorities for different combinations of these factors, providing a reference for developing targeted interventions to enhance their quality of life.

Conclusion

This study highlights the need to place greater emphasis on HRQOL among older adults with ARTH, particularly self-image. Three latent classes of HRQOL were identified within this population: the high HRQOL group, the moderate HRQOL group, and the low HRQOL with poor function group. The influencing factors associated with these profiles were examined, revealing that patient age, level of self-esteem, depressive symptoms, and illness perceptions are significantly linked to distinct HRQOL characteristics. Therefore, nursing staff should implement targeted interventions based on the patient’s classification characteristics. However, this cross-sectional study identified three distinct HRQOL classes; future longitudinal or interventional studies are needed to establish causality and identify modifiable therapeutic targets. As we move forward, future research must focus on exploring the deeper relationships between body image and self-esteem, depression, and illness perception in this population.
