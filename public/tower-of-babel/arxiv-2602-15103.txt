UV and Optical Signatures of Late-time Disk Instabilities in Tidal Disruption Events
Daichi Tsuna, V. Ashley Villar, Anthony L. Piro, Samantha C. Wu

Abstract
Tidal disruption events (TDEs) are unique probes of evolving accretion in supermassive black holes. Recent models of TDE disks show that they undergo brief thermal instabilities with temporal super-Eddington accretion at late times, which has been suggested as a possibility to explain the ubiquitous late radio emergence in TDEs. We model the ultraviolet (UV) and optical signatures of such disk instabilities, expected from the accretion power being reprocessed by the optically-thick outflow following super-Eddington accretion. Our model predicts brief UV-bright transients lasting for days, with luminosities of 10 42 10^{42} – 10 43 10^{43} erg s -1 in near-UV and 10 41 10^{41} – 10 42 10^{42} erg s -1 in optical for a typical TDE by a 10 6 ​ M ⊙ 10^{6}~M_{\odot} black hole. These could be detectable by near-future surveys such as ULTRASAT, Vera C. Rubin Observatory and Argus Array, for TDEs of redshifts out to ≈ 0.1 \approx 0.1 . We further conduct a search for these transients in existing nearby TDEs using data from the Zwicky Transient Facility, placing upper limits on the flare rate for each TDE of 1 1 – 2 2 yr -1 dependent on the outflow mass. In the era of future surveys, combined UV/optical and radio monitoring would be an important test to the disk instability phenomena, as well as its explanation for the late-time radio emission in TDEs.

I
Introduction

A star is tidally disrupted when it reaches too close to a supermassive black hole (BH), such that tidal forces overwhelm the star’s self-gravity
(
Hills 1975
;
Rees 1988
;
Phinney 1989
)
. These tidal disruption events (TDEs), observed in wavelengths spanning from radio to X-rays, are valuable probes for studying the physics of BH accretion and the environments surrounding supermassive BHs
(e.g.
Alexander et al. 2020
;
Gezari 2021
;
Saxton et al. 2021
;
Mockler et al. 2025
, for reviews)
.

About half of the disrupted stellar debris is gravitationally bound to the BH and eventually settles to form an accretion disk once its energy and angular momentum are significantly dissipated. The UV plateau observed in a number of TDEs indicates disk formation within a timescale of years after optical peak
(
van Velzen et al. 2019
;
Mummery et al. 2024
;
Guolo et al. 2025
)
.

Meanwhile, radio observations find late time brightening hundreds to thousands of days after optical peak
(
Horesh et al. 2021
;
Cendes et al. 2022
;
Cendes et al. 2024
;
Sfaradi et al. 2024
;
Golay et al. 2025
)
for a large fraction of TDEs (40% of optical TDEs;
Cendes et al. 2024
). Equipartition analysis of a large sample of TDEs favor the late radio emission in many of them being powered by a delayed launch of mildly relativistic (
\sim 0.1c
) outflows
(
Cendes et al. 2024
;
Goodwin et al. 2025
)
. The origin(s) for such delayed outflows is unclear, while recent studies indicate connections with the evolution of the accretion onto the supermassive BH
(
Alexander et al. 2025
)
.

One possibility to explain the delayed outflow is that the disk formation itself is delayed. Such delayed circularization/disk formation relative to optical peak is argued in recent simulations, while the detailed timescale is still in active debate
(
Ryu et al. 2023
;
Price et al. 2024
;
Steinberg & Stone 2024
;
Huang et al. 2024
;
Huang et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. Another possibility is thermal instabilities in TDE disks occurring at late times
(
Lu 2022
;
Piro & Mockler 2025
;
Guo & Qiao 2025
, but see also
Alush & Stone 2025
)
. Under these instabilities the disk can undergo brief, enhanced accretion onto the BH, which often exceeds the Eddington limit and could lead to mildly relativistic outflows
(
Jiang & Dai 2024
, e.g.,)
. Recently,
Wu et al. 2025
find from detailed radio modeling that the sub-relativistic outflows expected from disk instabilities are a viable possibility to explain many of the events
(
Sato et al. 2025
, see also)
.

As various scenarios of delayed radio emission exist, a key question is whether there could be other unique predictions from such disk instabilities. In this work we consider the emission from the outflow at the onset of such disk accretion flares. We find that the flares are typically accompanied by brief UV-bright transients, lasting for a few days with peak luminosities of
10^{42}
–
10^{43}
erg s
-1
in near-UV, making this scenario testable in high-cadence UV and optical surveys.

Our work is constructed as follows. In Section
II
we present our model for calculating the UV/optical emission from the accretion flares, and the key features of the light curve evolution. We present a parameter study of this in Section
III
, and discuss the prospects of UV/optical surveys to test the existence of disk instabilities using these transients. In Section
IV
we present our search for these transients in archival TDEs, using existing high-cadence optical data. We conclude in Section
V
.

II
Emission from Disk Outflows by the Accretion Flare

Our model for the accretion flare is inspired by time-dependent one-zone modeling of
Piro & Mockler 2025
. We have three parameters, the total mass ejected by the accretion flare
M_{\rm fl}
, the flare’s duration
t_{\rm fl}
, and the characteristic disk radius
r_{d}
during the flare.
Piro & Mockler 2025
find that the flare’s duration is nearly independent of both the BH’s mass
M_{\rm BH}
and time from disruption, with
t_{\rm fl}\approx 1
–
2
days. This arises from the disk radius scaling with the tidal radius

\displaystyle r_{T}\approx\left(\frac{M_{\rm BH}}{M_{*}}\right)^{\!\!1/3}R_{*}\sim 100~R_{\odot}\left(\frac{M_{\rm BH}}{10^{6}~M_{\odot}}\right)^{\!\!1/3}\left(\frac{M_{*}}{M_{\odot}}\right)^{\!\!7/15},

(1)

by a nearly constant factor (
r_{d}\sim 5r_{T}
in their model at the flaring state), and
t_{\rm fl}
being roughly the viscous time

\displaystyle t_{\rm visc}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{r_{d}^{3}}{GM_{\rm BH}}}\sim 2~{\rm day}\left(\frac{r_{d}}{5r_{T}}\right)^{\!\!3/2}\left(\frac{M_{*}}{M_{\odot}}\right)^{\!\!7/10},

(2)

where
\alpha\approx 0.1
is the viscosity parameter. In equation (
1
) we have used the mass-radius relation of main sequence stars
R_{*}/R_{\odot}\approx(M_{*}/M_{\odot})^{4/5}
. One sees that the dependence on
M_{\rm BH}
cancels out when
r_{d}/r_{T}
is fixed.

On the other hand, the value of
M_{\rm fl}
is less certain, as it depends on the accretion history (as well as
\alpha
and
M_{\rm BH}
) which is less clear especially at early times. In this work we take an empirical approach and consider masses of
M_{\rm fl}=10^{-2}
–
10^{-1}~M_{\odot}
, motivated by the results of
Wu et al. 2025
for explaining both the timescale and luminosity of the delayed radio detections in TDEs.

In this high accretion state, most of the accreted mass is ejected as a wind due to temporal super-Eddington accretion with rate

\displaystyle\dot{M}_{w}\approx\frac{M_{\rm fl}}{t_{\rm fl}}\sim 4~M_{\odot}\ {\rm yr}^{-1}\left(\frac{M_{\rm fl}}{10^{-2}M_{\odot}}\right)\left(\frac{t_{\rm fl}}{1\ {\rm day}}\right)^{-1},

(3)

that highly exceeds the Eddington-limited accretion rate
\dot{M}_{\rm Edd}\sim 10^{-2}M_{\odot}\ {\rm yr}^{-1}(M_{\rm BH}/10^{6}M_{\odot})
.

We assume the wind is launched quasi-spherically from the disk due to accretion at a characteristic radius
r_{\rm in}
with constant rate
\dot{M}\approx\dot{M}_{w}\approx M_{\rm fl}/t_{\rm fl}
, carrying an accretion power of

(

Here
R_{g}=GM_{\rm BH}/c^{2}\sim 1.5\times 10^{11}
cm

is the BH’s gravitational radius.
The launching radius
r_{\rm in}
could correspond to the “spherization radius”
(
Shakura & Sunyaev 1973

inside which the local accretion power exceeds the Eddington limit

(5)

or
r_{\rm in}\approx r_{d}
if
r_{d}<r_{\rm Sph}
and the accretion is super-Eddington everywhere in the disk. We therefore adopt

\displaystyle r_{\rm in}={\rm min}(r_{d},r_{\rm Sph}).

(6)

The opacity
\kappa
is expected to be dominated by scattering opacity for the density and temperature of our interest, and we adopt a constant
\kappa\approx 0.3\ {\rm cm^{2}\ g^{-1}}
for a solar-like composition.

At
r_{\rm in}
we expect the accretion power to be shared nearly equally by internal and kinetic energy, but the wind is highly optically thick. Due to adiabatic expansion, internal energy is degraded as it is converted to kinetic energy via PdV work, and the observed luminosity is generally reduced from
L_{\rm acc}
.

Our aim is to semi-analytically calculate the emission from such time-dependent wind outflows. We follow the spirit of
Piro & Lu 2020
(see their Figure 1 for context), but we build the model slightly differently so that it can properly solve under abrupt changes in the accretion power and outflow like considered here.

II.1
Light curve

Our approach below is to split the wind into multiple “shells” launched at times
\tilde{t}
(
0<\tilde{t}<t_{\rm fl}
) and calculate the radiative output of each shell at time
t^{\prime}
from launch, to obtain the luminosity observed at time
t=\tilde{t}+t^{\prime}
.

Let us consider a shell of wind material launched over a time
\Delta t
at radius
r_{\rm in}
and time
\tilde{t}
, with internal energy

\displaystyle E_{\rm int,0}\approx\frac{1}{2}L_{\rm acc}\Delta t

(7)

and with velocity

\displaystyle v_{w}\sim\sqrt{(L_{\rm acc}/2)/(\dot{M}_{w}/2)}\sim 0.06c\left(\frac{r_{\rm in}}{300R_{g}}\right)^{-1/2}.

(8)

At a time
t^{\prime}
from launch, the shell increases its volume as
V_{\rm sh}=4\pi[r_{\rm in}+(v_{w}t^{\prime})]^{2}(v_{w}\Delta t)
, and its internal energy
E_{\rm int}
(assumed to be radiation dominated) evolves as

\displaystyle\frac{dE_{\rm int}}{dt^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{E_{\rm int}}{3V_{\rm sh}}\frac{dV_{\rm sh}}{dt^{\prime}}-L_{\rm rad}

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{2E_{\rm int}}{3(t^{\prime}+r_{\rm in}/v_{w})}-\frac{E_{\rm int}}{t_{\rm diff}},

where the first term is adiabatic cooling by PdV work, and the second term is the radiative cooling regulated by the diffusion of photons through the wind over a timescale
(
Piro & Lu 2020
)

\displaystyle t_{\rm diff}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa\dot{M}_{w}}{4\pi v_{w}c}\times\left[\frac{r_{w}-(r_{\rm in}+v_{w}t^{\prime})}{r_{w}}\right]^{2}

(10)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa\dot{M}_{w}}{4\pi v_{w}c}\times\left[\frac{v_{w}\tilde{t}}{r_{\rm in}+v_{w}(\tilde{t}+t^{\prime})}\right]^{2}

Here
r_{w}=r_{\rm in}+v_{w}(\tilde{t}+t^{\prime})
is the outer edge of the wind. The differential equation in
t^{\prime}
has an analytical solution, with initial condition of
E_{\rm int}=E_{\rm int,0}
at
t^{\prime}=0
, of

\displaystyle E_{\rm int}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{\rm int,0}\left(\frac{r_{\rm in}}{r_{\rm in}+v_{w}t^{\prime}}\right)^{2/3}

(11)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\frac{B^{3}}{3A}\left[\left(1+\frac{1}{B}\frac{t^{\prime}}{\tilde{t}}\right)^{3}-1\right]\right\}

where
A,B
are dimensionless parameters that are independent of
t^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle A=\frac{1}{\tilde{t}}\frac{\kappa\dot{M}_{w}}{4\pi v_{w}c},\ B=1+\frac{r_{\rm in}}{v_{w}\tilde{t}}\ .

(12)

The corresponding luminosity
L_{\rm rad}=E_{\rm int}/t_{\rm diff}
is

\displaystyle L_{\rm rad}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{L_{\rm acc}\Delta t}{2A\tilde{t}}\left(B+\frac{t^{\prime}}{\tilde{t}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{r_{\rm in}}{r_{\rm in}+v_{w}t^{\prime}}\right)^{2/3}

(13)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\frac{B^{3}}{3A}\left[\left(1+\frac{1}{B}\frac{t^{\prime}}{\tilde{t}}\right)^{3}-1\right]\right\}.

We can now integrate over all the emitted shells to obtain the light curve as a function of
t
. Using the solution for
L_{\rm rad}(t^{\prime})
and substituting
t^{\prime}=t-\tilde{t}
for each wind shell launched at
\tilde{t}
, we sum up the contributions from all shells emitted within
t
and obtain

\displaystyle L_{\rm obs}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}d\tilde{t}~\frac{L_{\rm acc}(\tilde{t})}{2A\tilde{t}}\left(B+\frac{t-\tilde{t}}{\tilde{t}}\right)^{\!\!2}\left[\frac{r_{\rm in}}{r_{\rm in}+v_{w}(t-\tilde{t})}\right]^{\!2/3}

(14)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\frac{B^{3}}{3A}\left[\left(1+\frac{1}{B}\frac{t-\tilde{t}}{\tilde{t}}\right)^{3}-1\right]\right\},

, the light curve settles to a plateau-like morphology. The plateau is partly due to our assumed top-hat time-dependence of
\dot{M}

L_{\rm acc}
. The observed luminosity at this phase is a factor of several lower than the input luminosity
L_{\rm acc}
due to the adiabatic loss. Once the outflow shuts off at
t=t_{\rm fl}
, the light curve drops sharply over a timescale of a few days, which is set by the diffusion timescale in the wind.

II.2
Temperature Evolution

We calculate the temperature of the emission by approximately solving the reprocessing by the wind. Following
Piro & Lu 2020
, we obtain this by calculating the color radius
r_{c}
, the outermost radius where thermalization occurs.

At a given time
t
, the density profile of the wind varies during and after the flare as

\displaystyle\rho(r,t<t_{\rm fl})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{M}_{w}}{4\pi r^{2}v_{w}}~(r<r_{w}=r_{\rm in}+v_{w}t),

(15)

\displaystyle\rho(r,t>t_{\rm fl})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{\quad}l}\frac{\dot{M}_{w}}{4\pi r^{2}v_{w}}&(r_{\rm in}+v_{w}(t-t_{\rm fl})<r<r_{w})\\
0&({\rm otherwise}).\end{array}\right.

The diffusion time in equation (
10
) indicates that the bulk of the radiation is emitted around while the wind expands out to the trapping radius

\displaystyle r_{\rm tr}\approx r_{\rm in}+\frac{\kappa\dot{M}_{w}}{4\pi c}.

(19)

At the earliest stage when
r_{w}<r_{\rm tr}
, photons travel by advection throughout the wind, and we adopt the temperature set by adiabatic cooling out to the outermost radius of the wind, with
r_{c}\approx r_{w}
and

\displaystyle T_{\rm obs}\approx\left(\frac{L_{\rm obs}}{4\pi r_{w}^{2}v_{w}a}\right)^{1/4}.

(20)

Once
r_{w}>r_{\rm tr}
, above
r_{\rm tr}
the luminosity is roughly constant, and the temperature could be well-represented by the diffusion approximation as
(
Piro & Lu 2020
)

\displaystyle L_{\rm obs}=-\frac{4\pi r^{2}ac}{3\kappa\rho}\frac{\partial T^{4}}{\partial r}.

(21)

This leads to a temperature profile

\displaystyle T(r,t)^{4}\approx T_{\rm out}^{4}+\int_{r}^{r_{w}}\frac{3\kappa\rho L_{\rm obs}}{4\pi r^{2}ac},

(22)

where
T_{\rm out}
is the temperature at the outer edge, which we solve for by fixing the temperature at
r_{\rm tr}
as

\displaystyle T(r=r_{\rm tr},t)\approx\left(\frac{L_{\rm obs}}{4\pi r_{\rm tr}^{2}v_{w}a}\right)^{1/4}.

(23)

In the density regimes of interest the opacity is scattering-dominated, and the color radius
r_{c}(t)
that determines the temperature is interior to the photosphere. This radius is solved by

\displaystyle\int_{r_{c}}^{r_{w}}\sqrt{3\kappa_{\rm abs}\kappa}~\rho dr=1

(24)

For
\kappa_{\rm abs}
, we adopt a Kramer’s opacity law motivated for bound-free and free-free absorption, with a sharp cutoff at low temperatures due to hydrogen recombination

\displaystyle\kappa_{\rm abs}(\rho,T)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{\quad}l}C\rho T^{-3.5}&(T\geq T_{\rm rec})\\
C\rho T_{\rm rec}^{-3.5}(T/T_{\rm rec})^{\beta}&(T<T_{\rm rec}),\end{array}\right.

where the parameters are in cgs units, and
C
,
T_{\rm rec}
are dependent on the composition of the wind. Here we set
\beta=15
to mimic the sharp cutoff at
T<T_{\rm rec}
, and adopt constant values (
C
,
T_{\rm rec}
)
=
(
4\times 10^{25}
cgs,
6000
K) motivated for solar abundance gas
(
Matsumoto & Piran 2021
, e.g. Appendix of)
. The detailed choice of
\beta
does not affect our conclusions, as we later find emission temperatures to be much larger than
T_{\rm rec}
.

When
r_{c}
solved by this procedure satisfies
r_{c}>r_{\rm tr}
(“thermalization-dominated temperature” in
Piro & Lu 2020
),
T_{\rm obs}
would be the temperature at the color radius

\displaystyle T_{\rm obs}\approx\left[T_{\rm out}^{4}+\int_{r_{c}}^{r_{w}}\frac{3\kappa\rho L_{\rm obs}}{4\pi r^{2}ac}\right]^{1/4}\ (r_{c}>r_{\rm tr}).

(28)

On the other hand if
r_{c}<r_{\rm tr}
(“trapping-dominated temperature” in
Piro & Lu 2020
), the temperature of the emission is set by the trapping radius. We adopt the temperature at
r_{\rm tr}
of

\displaystyle T_{\rm obs}\approx\left(\frac{L_{\rm obs}}{4\pi r_{\rm tr}^{2}v_{w}a}\right)^{1/4}\ (r_{c}<r_{\rm tr}),

(29)

and update
r_{c}=r_{\rm tr}
.

Parameter Exploration

The most promising instruments to detect these transients are surveys with short cadences around a few days or less, such as the Zwicky Transient Facility
(
Bellm et al. 2019
, ZTF;)
, ULTRASAT
(
Shvartzvald et al. 2024
)
and Vera C. Rubin Observatory’s Legacy Survey of Space and Time
(

. The detectability of these flares in optical and UV vary by both

, as they control both the energetics and temperature of the flare. We calculate a grid of light curves varying these two parameters, and measure the peak magnitudes

and duration

1
mag from peak.

We adopt

–
0.1~M_{\odot}

(

)
, and choose a range of

around

, corresponding to the disk’s outer radius, is also roughly reproduced in multi-wavelength analyses of late-time plateau emission in TDEs
(
Guolo et al. 2025
, their Figure 7)
. We set the flare duration

as the viscous time of the disk, scaling with

as in equation

(with

).

Given the flare’s luminosity, we expect that the flares may even be detectable by existing optical surveys, especially for nearby TDEs that result in brighter flares. For example, TDEs at distances
\lesssim 100
Mpc could have flares of
\lesssim 21
mag in optical, potentially within reach of surveys like ZTF.

Motivated by this, we conduct a search of late-time flares in existing TDEs, using light curve data from the OTTER database
(
Franz et al. 2025
)
. We select a sample of 28 TDEs with (i) redshift less than
0.1
, (ii) host correction applied, and (iii) with at least one ZTF detection (flagged by
>3\sigma
confidence in OTTER) at
>1
year from discovery. We show the redshift and the epoch of the last ZTF detection (from discovery) in Figure
4
(for details of the events see Appendix
A
). About half of the samples have detections as late as 3 years after discovery, and four TDEs are at luminosity distances
D_{L}<100
Mpc.

As TDEs generally have disk-dominated plateau emission at late times
(
van Velzen et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
, we search for any short-duration excess that could be consistent with a flare on top of a longer plateau. For each TDE, we extract all host-subtracted ZTF data at
>1
yr from discovery, and compare the flux of each detection with the surrounding flux measurements in the same band at
\pm
(3–50) days. Assuming the flux uncertainties are Gaussian, we estimate the significance as
{\rm min}[(F-f)/\sqrt{dF^{2}+df^{2}}]
, evaluated using all the surrounding flux measurements
f\pm df
around the detection with flux
F\pm dF
. We exclude measurements within 3 days as they could be part of a single flare, and beyond 50 days to prevent multiple flares from blending into the analysis. Our search is hence less sensitive to flares frequently occurring with intervals
\lesssim 50
days, as the flares can blend into both
F
and
f
. However we believe these short intervals are unlikely at
\gtrsim 1
yr after the TDE, based on existing disk models
(
Lu 2022
;
Piro & Mockler 2025
)
.

Our analysis did not find any strong candidates of a disk instability flare. The excess found with high significance were all second peaks of events claimed as repeating (partial) TDEs in the literature, AT 2020vdq
(
Somalwar et al. 2025a
)
and AT 2022dbl
(
Lin et al. 2024
;
Hinkle et al. 2024
)
. These have luminous peaks (
\nu L_{\nu}\sim 10^{43}
erg s
-1
) and slow declines (
\approx 1
month) in the optical, inconsistent with the expectations from our model in Section
III
. Hence we believe these excesses are unlikely to be due to disk instabilities.

The remaining candidates are all low significance and not strong enough to claim a detection. In Figure
5
we show the two highest-significance candidates from AT2021nwa and AT2019azh, which are both flagged as an
r
-band excess. We believe these candidates are also unlikely to be disk instability flares, as the
g
-band flux in the vicinity of each candidate is not enhanced like the
r
-band flux.

We finally place rough rate constraints of these flares by artificial injections of our theoretical light curve models into ZTF data. For this purpose we focus our analysis on the three most constraining TDEs, which are nearby (
D_{L}<100
Mpc) and have long ZTF baselines of several years: AT 2019qiz, AT 2019azh, and AT 2021ehb.

Into the observed data, we add signals of flux

\displaystyle f_{\nu}=\frac{1+z}{4\pi D_{L}^{2}}L_{\nu^{\prime}}[L_{\rm obs},T_{\rm obs}],

(30)

where
\nu^{\prime}=(1+z)\nu
is the source-frame frequency and
L_{\nu^{\prime}}
is calculated assuming a blackbody (at source-frame) with bolometric luminosity
L_{\rm obs}
and temperature
T_{\rm obs}
. The observer-frame frequency
\nu
is represented with the central frequency of each filter,
6.34\times 10^{14}
Hz and
4.72\times 10^{14}
Hz for
g
and
r
band respectively. For the signal we again vary the flare mass as
M_{\rm fl}=0.01
–
0.1~M_{\odot}
, while we fix the disk radius as
r_{d}=5r_{T}
and adopt (median) supermassive BH masses inferred from host galaxy scaling relations
(
Yao et al. 2023
, see Table
1
for their values)
.

The injections are done from 1 year after discovery to the last ZTF observation sampled by 0.1 day interval, and the same search is repeated for each injection made. By requiring each signal model to not be recovered by the search with
>5\sigma
significance in all ZTF bands, we set the constraint on the rate of the signal. We assume the flares occur according to a Poisson process, a model-agnostic simplification which could be potentially improved in future works by employing detailed model predictions of the flare pattern.

\lesssim 1

V
Conclusion

We have studied the transient UV and optical emission from accretion disks formed in TDEs, under the picture suggested from recent models that they undergo late-time thermal instabilities. Such instabilities may also explain the emergence of late-time radio emission, seen in a significant fraction of TDEs
(
Wu et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
.

We find that the emission from such disk instabilities is expected to be a very hot (a few – 10
\times 10^{4}
K), luminous transient with luminosities of
10^{42}
–
10^{43}
erg s
-1
in the near-UV. The near-UV magnitude peaks between
-17
and
-19
mag, which is a promising target for ULTRASAT for TDEs out to redshift
\approx 0.1
. The optical emission is expected to be an order of magnitude fainter, but may still be well constraining in the era of future high-cadence surveys like LSST and Argus Array.

We finally conducted a search for these transient flares in the sample of 28 nearby TDEs (redshift
<0.1
) using optical data from ZTF. We did not find any promising candidates from late-time photometric detections. The non-detection led to upper limits in their event rates of
1
–
2
yr
-1
, for each of the three most constraining TDEs.

We conclude with suggestions for potential improvements to theoretical predictions and observational searches. First, the disk modeling could be improved by extending the one-zone model
(
Lu 2022
;
Piro & Mockler 2025
, e.g.,)
to a more realistic 1D/2D model, and/or including the effects of delayed circularization over a timescale of months to years as suggested in recent numerical studies. This could provide more robust predictions for both the timing and the mass of the flares. Second, the greybody assumption for the spectra could be improved by detailed radiative transfer modeling of the outflow, which could impact more on the predictions for optical that are far from the spectral peak. Finally the search can be improved in several ways as more sensitive surveys are realized, such as combining information of multiple filters and applying more physically informed priors on the flares for significance estimates and rate constraints.
