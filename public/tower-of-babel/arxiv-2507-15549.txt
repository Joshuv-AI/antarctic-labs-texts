An n O ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ n ) n^{O(\log\log n)} time approximation scheme for capacitated VRP in the Euclidean plane.
René Sitters

Abstract
We present a quasi polynomial time approximation scheme (Q-PTAS) for the capacitated vehicle routing problem (CVRP) on n n points in the Euclidean plane for arbitrary capacity c c . The running time is n f ⁡ ( ϵ ) ⋅ log ⁡ log ​ n n^{f(\epsilon)\cdot\log\log n} for any c c , and where f f is a function of ϵ \epsilon only. This is a major improvement over the so far best known running time of n log O ⁡ ( 1 / ϵ ) ⁡ n n^{\log^{O(1/\epsilon)}n} time and a big step towards a PTAS for Euclidean CVRP. In our algorithm, we first give a polynomial time reduction of the CVRP in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} (for any fixed d d ) to an uncapacitated routing problem in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} that we call the m m -paths problem. Here, one needs to find exactly m m paths between two points a a and b b , covering all the given points in the Euclidean space. We then give a Q-PTAS for the m m -paths problem in the pane. Any PTAS for the (arguably easier to handle) Euclidean m m -paths problem is most likely to imply a PTAS for the Euclidean CVRP.

Introduction

The Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP), introduced by Dantzig and Ramser in 1959
[
8
]

Here, we give an algorithm for the classic Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem (CVRP)
[
8
]

An instance consists of a capacity
c
, and a set of points in a metric space, where one point is the origin (or depot), and the others are the locations to be visited. A feasible solution is a set of tours that together cover all points, and every tour contains the origin plus at most
c
locations. The objective is to minimize the total distance. We study the version where all points are in the Euclidean plane. The CVRP is also known as the
k
-tours problem or the cycle covering problem
[
4
,
2
,
7
]
.

Haimovich and Rinnooy Kan
[
12
]
where the first to give a polynomial time approximation scheme (PTAS) for CVRP in the Euclidean plane. The running time of their algorithm is exponential in
c
, but still polynomial for
c=O(\log\log n)
. Asano et al.
[
1
]
increase this to

and also show that any PTAS for TSP in the Euclidean plane
[
2
,
19

implies a PTAS for CVRP with

.
Adamaszek et al.
[

increased the capacity, for which a PTAS exists, to

, for some
\delta
depending on
\epsilon
. In their algorithm, they first reduce the number of points substantially, by partitioning the instance and moving points to grid points, and then apply the
quasi
polynomial time approximation scheme (Q-PTAS) by Das and Mathieu
[

The Q-PTAS from
[

[

The DP-solution then is a set of tours that may violate the capacity constraint by a small factor. This is resolved by removing points from tours, which are then assigned to new tours. For higher dimensional Euclidean metrics, Khachay and Dubinin [17] gave a PTAS for fixed dimension

For arbitrary metrics, Haimovich and Rinnooy Kan

Recently, Blauth et al.

In the unsplittable demand setting of CVRP, each point is given a demand that cannot be split over different tours and the total demand served by each tour should stay within the capacity

Model and results

An instance of the Euclidean CVRP is given by an integer
c\geq 1
and
n
points (the input points) plus one point
r

c
other points. The objective is to minimize the total distance.
We shall often refer to the points to be visited as the

to distinguish them from points defined by the algorithm such as ‘portals’, ‘anchor points’, or ‘points of intersection’.

We use the notation
\tilde{O}()
to hide dependency on
\epsilon
of the hidden constant in the big-O notation. Throughout the paper we assume w.l.o.g. that
0<\varepsilon<1
and

is integer. The base of the logarithms is 2, i.e.,
\log n:=\log_{2}n

Theorem 1
.

There is an approximation scheme for CVRP in the Euclidean plane with running time
n^{\tilde{O}(\log\log n)}
, which holds for any capacity
c
.

The running time of our Q-PTAS is a major improvement over the

n^{\log^{O(1/\epsilon)}n}

]
and a big step towards a PTAS for Euclidean CVRP. Moreover, we provide a way to take the last hurdle by giving a polynomial time reduction from CVRP to what we call the

-paths problem
, which is, arguably, an easier problem to handle than the CVRP. Any PTAS for the Euclidean
m
-paths problem is most likely to imply a PTAS for the Euclidean CVRP.

Our algorithm, when implemented as described, has an absolutely impractical running time. The number

Our algorithm uses a wide variety of techniques, some easy and standard, others sophisticated and less common.
Two new ingredients are of particular interest. In Section

The difficulty lies in choosing the structure of the subproblems in the DP in such a way that the obtained pseudo-solution can be repaired at small cost. In the Q-PTAS from

1.1
Outline of the algorithm and proof

The Q-PTAS contains numerous details but the main ideas are rather simple. A high level overview is displayed in Algorithms 1,2,3 below.
First, we apply an established technique (Algorithm 1) in which we reduce the problem to
bounded instances
. A set of instances is called bounded if there is some constant number
Q
such that
d_{\max}/d_{\min}\leq Q
for every instance in the set.

For a bounded instance we find a solution by dividing the space into
\tilde{O}(1)
squares of equal size and solve many subproblems on each square. (We do not use a hierarchical DP here). We restrict the solution space by defining
\tilde{O}(1)
points on the boundary of each square and restrict any crossing of a tour with the boundary of a square to these points, which we call
anchor points
.
In total, there are only
\tilde{O}(1)
anchor points and we say that two tours are of the same
type
if their sequence of anchor points is the same. Two tours of the same type can swap their parts that fall inside a single square, which we call
segments
. (See Figures
3
and
4
.)
We show that if we have a large number
k
of tours of the same type such that their total number of points is at most
kc
, then by swapping and splitting we can reassign points to tours such that each tour has most
c
points and the total increase in length is small. This is a key observation in our proof and is rather similar to bin packing. One of the arguments used here is that, since the instance is bounded, we can choose the grid dense enough such that the size of a single grid cell is small compared to the length of any tour.
We then use the standard bin packing techniques, such as rounding the item sizes (here, the number of points on a segment), and solving a linear program to assign items to bins (here segments to tours), and then adding the fractional items (here, the segments) to tours in a greedy way. (See Algorithm 2.)

An instance of any subproblem on a square is given by the boundary information (how the tours enter and leave the square) and for each tour type, the number of points visited by all tours of that type together. This ’together’ is the key to the efficiency of the Q-PTAS. We do not need to keep track of the number of points on each tour, but only on the total for each type and we have only
\tilde{O}(1)
different types of tours. So we basically got rid of the capacity constraints since there are only
\tilde{O}(1)
of them. But, we still need to deal with a non-constant number of tours in each square. We refer to the subproblem that needs to be solved as the
general
m
-paths problem
.
The easiest version of this subproblem, which we call the
m
-paths problem
, is defined as follows. We are given a square containing a set of points, two points
a
and
b
on the boundary, and a number
m
. Goal is to find exactly
m
paths between
a
and
b
that together visit all points in the square. (See Figure
1
.) Note that the case
m=1
is similar to the TSP-path problem. In fact, for constant
m
, a PTAS follows easily using Arora’s approach
[
2
]
. But
m
is not constant in general and keeping track of all the different paths in the DP is non-trivial. To simplify analysis we assume tours are directed. Then, the
m
paths problem is to construct a directed graph with flow values on the arcs, defining an integral flow from
a
to
b
of value
m
and such that the support graph is connected and contains every input point. An important observation is that when we solve the
m
-paths problem using Arora’s approach in a pretty straight forward way, we do get an
n^{\tilde{O}(\log n)}
time approximation scheme if, in the DP, we store precise information on the flow values, i.e., the number of paths crossing each portal in either direction. The running time follows easily from the fact that there are
\tilde{O}(\log n)
portals per dissection square and each portal is crossed
O(m)=O(n)
times. We describe the details of this approach in this paper for three reasons:
First, this is already a big improvement over the running time of

n^{\log^{O(1/\epsilon)}n}
from
[
9
]
. Second, the DP and its analysis stay close to that of Arora
[
2
]
and it is relatively easy to verify the correctness of this part of the algorithm. Third, it forms the basis for the more complex analysis of the
n^{\tilde{O}(\log\log n)}
time algorithm.

To get the improved running time, we round flow values in the DP to powers of some number
\alpha
. Of course, using rounded numbers like this gives a mismatch of flow values at portals, i.e, we lose flow conservation. This error can easily be fixed by adding arcs to the solution. The details of the Q-PTAS for the
m
-paths problem are rather involved as we need to carefully keep track of the rounding error. We cannot simply apply the same DP with rounded values but need to add more structure to the configurations stored in the DP. For example, we use a sophisticated portal layout (see Figure
14
) which is more restricted than the standard layout, and provides a new lower bound on the cost of any portal respecting solution but gives no significant increase in the cost of the solution. This layout is of general interest for TSP-like problems in the Euclidean plane.

An important insight is that the rounding approach has to give, at least intuitively,
some
improvement over the
n^{\tilde{O}(\log n)}
time algorithm of Section
3.1
, which does not use rounding. Intuitively, modest rounding gives small errors that can be fixed at a small cost.
The finesse is in the details though.

After analyzing the
m
-paths problem, we discuss the complexity of the
general

m
-paths problem, which is basically a combination of
\tilde{O}(1)

m
-paths problems that have to solved simultaneously on the same square. In the DP, these different
m
-paths problems are only dealt with together at the lowest level (the grid cells), where we assign input points to paths. At all other levels in the DP, we can handle the different
m
-paths problems independently in parallel, which leads to an
\tilde{O}(1)
factor increase in the exponent of the running time. Hence, the running time remains
n^{\tilde{O}(\log\log n)}
.

2.1
Reducing to bounded instances

Bounding the ratio of maximum and minimum distance to the origin is a first step in several approximation schemes
Adamaszek et al.
[
1
]
(2010) introduce the technique to reduce the number of points in the instance to some function of

In their reduction to bounded instances, they use a Baker’s type approach

[
10

adopt the same partitioning scheme.
Mathieu and Zhou
[
16

17
]
use a similar approach for trees, splitting the instance in two types of bounded instances, where one of the type contributes only

A reduction to bounded instances has also been used for the average completion time objective
[
20

11

[

Definition 1

We say that a solution is

Lemma 2
.

Let
\text{\sc{OPT}}_{A}
be the optimal cost over all anchor point respecting solutions. Then

\mathbb{E}[\text{\sc{OPT}}_{A}]\leq(1+5\varepsilon)\text{\sc{OPT}},

where the expectation is over the random placement of the grid.

Proof.

Consider an optimal solution
\mathcal{T}
and let
T
be some tour in it. First, assume

is not directed and ignore the direction of the anchor points. We make
T
anchor point respecting, as done in the PTAS for TSP
[

\epsilon\tau

such that each anchor point is crossed at most twice. So we use this traversal of

\epsilon\tau

T
with a grid line is at most
3\epsilon\tau
.

The expected number of crossings of

with grid lines is at most

\sqrt{2}\cdot{\textit{{length}}}(T)/\tau.

(An easy way to see this is to consider an axis-aligned straight line segment: the expected number of crossings is exactly

So, the expected total extra cost for making

\sqrt{2}\cdot{\textit{{length}}}(T)/\tau\cdot(3\epsilon\tau)\leq 5\epsilon\cdot{\textit{{length}}}(T).

3.1.3
The Dynamic Program (DP)

The graph we need to construct in the DP is directed and the inflow equals the outflow on each portal, except for the two anchor points

The random grid is defined exactly the same. Note that when input points are moved to the middle of grid cells, we can no longer merge these to a single input point. So, in the base problems in the grid cells, we need to specify how many points from the grid cell go to each of the anchor pairs. Since there are

\left(n^{\tilde{O}(\log n)}\right)^{\tilde{O}(1)}=n^{\tilde{O}(\log n)}.

4
An
n^{\tilde{O}(\log\log n)}
time approximation scheme.

The approach here is exactly the same as in the previous section but now we use rounded numbers for the flow values. This, however, is easier said than done. When rounding the flow values we lose flow conservation, which then needs to be corrected at every level of the DP. We want to maintain flow conservation on each dissection square, i.e., the total flow in equals the total flow out of the square, but also we also want to maintain flow conservation in each portal. We shortly discuss these two properties here.

To maintain flow conservation for each dissection square in the DP, we will only enumerate configurations with this property. For this, we use the same procedure as was done in Section
3.1
. That means, we first generate a flow graph, and use rounded flow values on the arcs. But when we add up these numbers at the portals, we do not round them. See the
example of Figure
13
.

Maintaining flow conservation at portals is more involved. Two types of errors may occur: There can be a difference in flow value at a portal on either side of the grid line (,i.e., among two adjacent squares of the same level), or a difference in flow value at a portal among squares at the same side of the grid line but at different levels. Correcting the imbalance is easily done by adding paths to the solution. However, bounding the cost for this correction appears non-trivial.
We solve this issue by putting more restrictions on the structure of a solution. To be precise, we restrict the ways in which portals can be crossed by giving an orientation to each portal and removing some portals on the intersection of grid lines. This restricted portal layout gives for any solution a lower bound on the cost of the part within a dissection squares
S
in terms of the flow value of
S
, i.e., the total in and out flow for
S
. Actually, we already used a similar statement in Section
3.1
, when we analyzed the cost of making an optimal tour portal respecting. It said that, we can charge, for any crossing with a grid line, the
expected
inter portal distance of the grid line to the solution. (This is basically the key insight in the TSP algorithm by Arora.) However, we can’t use this expected cost argument here since we will apply the bound to solutions that we construct
after
running the DP. In stead, we will use a deterministic argument, derived from a restricted portal layout, which is described precisely in the next section.
This layout still has the property that the expected detour for moving a crossing to a portal is small (Lemma
8
), and additionally gives a lower bound on the cost of a solution inside a dissection square (Lemma
9
), and is of general interest for TSP-like problems in the Euclidean plane.

We shall go over the details for the
m
-paths problem in Section
4.1
and discuss the general form in Section

.

4.1.1
A restricted portal layout

The sole purpose of the restricted portal layout is to have a lower bound on the cost of any solution within a dissection square

in terms of the number of portal crossings of

Portal restriction procedure:

1.

Starting from the standard portal layout (as used in
[

]
and in Section

) we give an orientation to the 4 portals on each intersection of grid lines, exactly as shown in the upper left crossing of Figure

2.

For

•

Keep the 4 portals on any intersection of two level

•

For any level

This special structure increases the detour for a crossing of a path with a grid line by only a factor 4 (compared to the standard layout) but has the additional property that the minimum path length inside a dissection square is at least the inter portal distance.

Lemma 8
.

Any path can be made portal respecting such that the detour per crossing of a grid line is at most 4 times the inter portal distance of that line.

Proof.

Consider for example the horizontal level
i

. Now consider the intersection of this line with 2 vertical lines that are of level
j\leq i
and that are consecutive. In the figure, these two intersections are marked
A

and

and the 2 vertical arrows at

and

i

∎

The lemma above states that we did not lose anything substantial in the analysis by using this portal structure.
We conclude that we can, from now on, restrict to portal respecting solutions using the special portal layout and denote by

the optimal cost among the portal respecting solutions. As before (see (

\text{Expected}(\text{\sc{OPT}})\leq(1+4\varepsilon)\text{\sc{OPT}}_{\sigma}.

The next lemma gives an interesting property and may be of of general interest for network optimization problems in

. We shall use it to prove Lemma

Lemma 9

Let

Proof.

In the corners of a dissection square, a path can only leave or enter but not both. On a side between two corners, portals have alternating directions so any path must go between two different portals, and these are separated by at least the inter portal distance of a level

∎

4.1.2
The DP with rounded flow values

We apply the same DP as in Section
3.1
but now with rounded flow values. The consistency check in the DP is replaced by a ’pseudo-consistency’ check, which is formalized in Definition
6
.

We will round to powers of
\alpha
, where
\alpha
is specified later, and we define
N_{\mathcal{F}}
such that
\lfloor\alpha^{N_{\mathcal{F}}}\rfloor=n
. Let
\mathcal{F}=\{0\}\cup\{\lfloor\alpha^{i}\rfloor\mid 0\leq i\leq N_{\mathcal{F}}\}
. It turns out that we can choose
\alpha
such that

N_{\mathcal{F}}=\tilde{O}(\log^{3}n)
.

Since we gave an orientation to every portal, all paths crossing a portal
p
, do so in the same direction: The flow through
p
either goes into or out of
S
, but not both. We denote by
\textsc{Flow}(p,S)\geq 0
the total flow entering or leaving
S
through
p
. The orientation of the portal tells what the direction of the flow is.
If dissection squares
S
and
S^{\prime}
share a portal
p
, then in any feasible solution,
\textsc{Flow}(p,S)=\textsc{Flow}(p,S^{\prime})
. However, when we run the DP with rounded numbers then we cannot maintain this identity and flow values for the same portal may differ among various dissection squares. We will show that we can repair this imbalance at a cost of a factor
\alpha
at every level.

Enumeration of configurations

As before, a configuration for a dissection square is given by the flow information for the portals and the component information.
For the enumeration of configurations we start, as before, from the flow graphs. So, for a dissection square
S
, we first generate all possible flow graphs on the portals of
S
. Then, for any flow graph we enumerate over all possible flow values, where we now restrict to values from
\mathcal{F}
. Then, we add up these numbers at the portals to get the flow values for the portals. But, we
do not round
these numbers. (See Figure
13
for an example.) Doing the rounding on the flow on the arcs and not for the portals ensures flow conservation on
S
. Also, the flow graph provides the necessary component information. Note that a flow value of 1 is not rounded down to 0, since
1\in\mathcal{F}
. Hence, rounding has no effect on the component information.
Again, we do not store the flow graphs in the DP but only the configurations derived from them.

The enumeration of configurations for the grid cells and square
B_{0}
requires more attention. For the grid cells, we only enumerate configurations that are valid and optimal. This works almost the same as in Section
3.1
.
We try all possible flow graphs and use
rounded
flow values. If the cell contains part of the anchor paths
(a^{\prime},a)
and
(b,b^{\prime})
, then that part is a fixed part of the flow graph that we enumerate for that cell and we also round the flow value
m
.
The rounded values are added to obtain the flows at the portals, but these values are not rounded. If the square contains no input point then all connections are straight lines. If there is a point then the cheapest option is to make all connections straight and then bend one of the lines to serve the point. All these computed configurations are declared
valid
.

For
B_{0}
we only want configurations with an in-and outflow that is not much larger than
m
. It turns out (Lemma
10
) that a value of at most
\alpha^{\rho}m
suffices. We put that as a hard constraint in the DP.

Running time

First, lets bound the size of the DP table. The number of dissection squares is
O(4^{\rho})=O(4^{\log(n/\varepsilon)})=n^{\tilde{O}(1)}
. For each dissection square, the number of possible flow graphs is
n^{\tilde{O}(1)}
. The size of
\mathcal{F}
is
\tilde{O}((\log n)^{3})
and the number of arcs in a flow graph is
\tilde{O}(\log n)
. So, the number of possible ways to put flow values on arcs (and consequently, the portals) is

\tilde{O}((\log n)^{3})^{\tilde{O}(\log n)}=(\log n)^{\tilde{O}(\log n)}=n^{\tilde{O}(\log\log n)}.

Hence, the size of the DP table is
n^{\tilde{O}(\log\log n)}
.
Verifying validity of any entry in the table can be done in

n^{\tilde{O}(\log\log n)}
time so the total time for the DP is
n^{\tilde{O}(\log\log n)}
.
Finally, given an optimal pseudo solution by the DP, we can turn it into a real solution in

n^{\tilde{O}(1)}
time since we only need to add arcs in each dissection square in a straightforward way.

4.2
The general
m
-paths problem.

The analysis of the general
m
-paths problem from Section
3.2
applies here as well.
An instance of the general
m
-paths problem is in fact a set of
\tilde{O}(1)

m
-paths instances that need to be solved in parallel. We refer to these simply as
the instances
and label them by
i=1,2,\dots
.
The DP can be done almost independently for the instances. The only connection is in the base case where the input points are divided over the different instances.
The running time of the DP is

\left(n^{\tilde{O}(\log\log n)}\right)^{\tilde{O}(1)}=n^{\tilde{O}(\log\log n)}.

The only important difference is that the DP no longer gives an exact solution but a pseudo-solution that needs to be repaired. The repairing can be done independently for each instance and only takes
n^{\tilde{O}(1)}
time per instance as discussed above.

It remains to bound the cost for repairing. In our analysis of the cost of repairing we used that
m>1/\varepsilon
. Note however, that for an
m
-paths instance
i
with
m_{i}\leq 1/\varepsilon
, rounding of flow values is not needed. So, in the combined DP, we do not round the flow values for such an instance and, consequently, no repairing is needed.
To see that the cost for repairing is not too large, note that the pseudo solution returned by the DP is actually a pseudo solution for each of the
m
-paths instances and the cost of the pseudo solution can be divided over the different
m
-paths instances accordingly. Also, an optimal solution of cost
OPT
for the combined problem is composed of a solution for each of the
m
-paths instances. Let
\text{\sc{OPT}}_{i}
be the cost of the part for
m
-paths instance
i
. Following the analysis of the
m
-paths problem above for each of the instances separately, we find a solution for each instance
i
of cost no more than
(1+14\varepsilon)\text{\sc{OPT}}_{i}
and the total cost is no more than
(1+14\varepsilon)\text{\sc{OPT}}
.

5
Generalizations

We presented a Q-PTAS for CVRP in the Euclidean plane, but the reductions we used are more general.
The reduction to bounded instances holds for Euclidean spaces of any dimension
d
. The reduction to the
m
-paths problem is only polynomial for Euclidean spaces of a fixed dimension
d
. The Q-PTAS for the

m
-paths problem was presented for the plane only. Here, we used the fact that paths do not cross, to bound the number of possible flow graphs for any dissection square (Lemma
5
). This no longer works so easily for higher dimensions.

Although our CVRP algorithm has quasi polynomial running time and only works for the Euclidean plane, our reductions show that any PTAS for the
m
-paths problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for some fixed
d
, will most likely yield a PTAS for CVRP in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Arguably, a PTAS for the
m
-paths problem is easier to derive than a PTAS for CVRP.

Adamaszek et al.
[
1
]
showed that the reduction to bounded instances, together with the
n^{\log^{O(1/\epsilon)}n}
-time approximation scheme by Das and Mathieu
[
9
]
implies a polynomial time approximation schemes for all
c<2^{\log^{\delta}n}
, for some
\delta(\epsilon)<1
. If we now use the
n^{\tilde{O}(\log\log n)}
-time approximation scheme, we see that a PTAS for CVRP exists for
c=n^{O(1/\log\log n)}
.
