Modular differential equations and orthogonal polynomials
Khalil Besrour, Hicham Saber, Abdellah Sebbar

Abstract
. We study second-order modular differential equations whose solutions transform equivariantly under the modular group. In the reducible case, we construct all such solutions using an explicit ansatz involving Eisenstein series and the J J -invariant, reducing the problem to an algebraic system. We show that the roots of this system are captured by orthogonal polynomials satisfying a Fuchsian differential equation. Their recurrence, norms, and weight function are derived, completing the classification of equivariant solutions in this setting.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
11F03, 11F11, 34M05.

1.
Introduction

Modular differential equations (MDEs) are linear differential equations on the upper half-plane whose solution spaces are preserved under the action of the modular group. Their study goes back to the classical works of Klein and Hurwitz and plays an important role in the theory of modular forms, vector-valued modular functions, and conformal field theory. The modern framework, as developed in
[
6
]
, defines an MDE of weight
k
as a differential equation

y^{(n)}+A_{n-1}(z)y^{(n-1)}+\cdots+A_{1}(z)y^{\prime}(z)+A_{0}(z)y(z)=0,

with holomorphic coefficients that are bounded at the cusps and whose solution space is stable under the slash operator
|_{k}\gamma
for all
\gamma\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.

When
n=2
, an MDE can always be reduced to an equation of the form

y^{\prime\prime}+sE_{4}(z)y=0,

where
E_{4}
is the weight
4
Eisenstein series and
s
is a complex parameter. Solutions to this equation are related to functions whose Schwarzian derivative equals a multiple of
E_{4}
. Such functions transform equivariantly under the modular group and are governed by rich arithmetic and geometric constraints. Our aim is to explicitly construct all solutions to this equation for rational values of
s=\pi^{2}r^{2}
, particularly in the reducible case where the associated monodromy representation decomposes.

We proceed by characterizing the values of
r
for which the Schwarzian differential equation admits a meromorphic equivariant solution, and reduce the problem to the construction of modular forms with prescribed singularities and residues. For
r=m/6
with
\gcd(m,6)=1
, we introduce a modular ansatz using the Eisenstein series
E_{4}
,
E_{6}
, and the
J
-invariant. The existence of such solutions reduces to a nonlinear algebraic system on the values
J(w_{i})
, which we analyze and solve explicitly.

To complete the construction, we introduce a second-order Fuchsian differential equation whose polynomial solutions encode the roots of the algebraic system. These polynomials form a family of orthogonal polynomials with respect to a Beta-type weight. We derive their weight function, orthogonality relations, recurrence formulas, and norms. This orthogonal family provides an explicit and unified framework to describe all solutions to the modular differential equation in the reducible case, recovering and extending classical results due to Hurwitz and Klein.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews modular differential equations and the Schwarzian formalism. Section 3 focuses on the reducible case and introduces the modular ansatz. Section 4 reduces the residue vanishing conditions to an algebraic system and solves it via an auxiliary differential equation. In Section 5, we analyze the associated orthogonal polynomials in detail and complete the classification of solutions.

2.
Modular Differential Equations

Let
{\mathbb{H}}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Im z>0\}
denote the complex upper half-plane. The modular group
{\mbox{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})}
, consisting of
2\times 2
integer matrices of determinant one, acts on
{\mathbb{H}}
by Möbius transformations:

\gamma\cdot z=\frac{az+b}{cz+d},\qquad\text{for }\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in{\mbox{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})}.

For an integer
k
and
\gamma={\binom{a\,\ b}{c\,\ d}}\in{\mbox{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})}
, the slash operator of weight
k
is defined on functions
f:{\mathbb{H}}\to\mathbb{C}
by

(f|_{k}\gamma)(z):=(cz+d)^{-k}f(\gamma\cdot z).

A meromorphic function
f
on
{\mathbb{H}}
is called a modular form of weight
k
for
{\mbox{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})}
if

f|_{k}\gamma=f,\qquad\text{for all }\gamma\in{\mbox{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})}.

If in addition
f
is holomorphic on
{\mathbb{H}}
and holomorphic at the cusp
\infty
, it is called a holomorphic modular form.

For the matrix
T=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
, the condition
f(z+1)=f(z)
implies that
f
has a Fourier expansion:

f(z)=\sum_{n\geq n_{0}}a_{n}q^{n},\qquad q=e^{2\pi iz},\quad n_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Holomorphy at
\infty
requires
n_{0}\geq 0
.

The classical Eisenstein series are given by:

E_{4}(z)=1+240\sum_{n\geq 1}\sigma_{3}(n)q^{n},\qquad E_{6}(z)=1-504\sum_{n\geq 1}\sigma_{5}(n)q^{n},

where
\sigma_{k}(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}d^{k}
. Then
E_{4}
and
E_{6}
are holomorphic modular forms of weights
4
and
6
, respectively.

Let
M_{k}
denote the
\mathbb{C}
-vector space of holomorphic modular forms of weight
k
. Then
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}M_{k}<\infty
. For example:

\dim M_{2}=\dim M_{2k+1}=0,\quad M_{4}=\mathbb{C}\cdot E_{4},\quad M_{6}=\mathbb{C}\cdot E_{6}.

The Dedekind eta-function is defined by:

\eta(z)=q^{1/24}\prod_{n\geq 1}(1-q^{n}),

and is a modular form of weight
1/2
with a multiplier system. The function
\eta^{4}
is a modular form of weight
2
with a character. The modular invariant
J
is given by:

J(z)=\frac{E_{4}^{3}(z)}{\Delta(z)},\qquad\Delta(z)=\eta^{24}(z)=\frac{1}{1728}(E_{4}^{3}-E_{6}^{2}),

where
\Delta
is a weight
12
cusp form called the modular discriminant.

According to
[
6
]
, a homogeneous linear differential equation

y^{(n)}+A_{n-1}(z)y^{(n-1)}+\cdots+A_{1}(z)y^{\prime}+A_{0}(z)y=0,

with holomorphic coefficients that are bounded when
z\longrightarrow i\infty
, is called a modular differential equation (MDE) of weight
k
if its solution space is preserved under the action of the slash operator
|_{k}\gamma
for all
\gamma\in{\mbox{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})}
.

In this paper, we focus on second-order MDEs of the form:

y^{\prime\prime}+A_{1}(z)y^{\prime}+A_{0}(z)y=0,

which may be reduced to a normal form:

y^{\prime\prime}+F(z)y=0,

where
F(z)
is a holomorphic modular form of weight
4
. This differential equation is now modular of weight -1.
Since
M_{4}=\mathbb{C}\cdot E_{4}
, we are naturally led to study:

(2.1)

y^{\prime\prime}+s\,E_{4}(z)\,y=0,\qquad\text{with }s\in\mathbb{C}.

According to
[
9
]
, the Frobenius method shows that in order for solutions to (
2.1
) to exhibit either a
q
-expansion (meromorphic at the cusp) or a logarithmic singularity (
\log q
term), one must have:

s=\pi^{2}r^{2},\qquad r\in\mathbb{Q}.

The key tool to analyze such differential equations is the
Schwarzian derivative
, defined as follows. For a meromorphic function
h
on a domain
D
,

\{h,z\}=\left(\frac{h^{\prime\prime}}{h^{\prime}}\right)^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{h^{\prime\prime}}{h^{\prime}}\right)^{2}.

This operator has several fundamental properties:

(1)

It is projectively invariant:
\{f,z\}=\{g,z\}
if and only if
g=\frac{af+b}{cf+d}
for some matrix
{\binom{a\,\ b}{c\,\ d}}\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.

(2)

\{f,z\}=0
if and only if
f(z)
is a Möbius transformation.

(3)

The Schwarzian transforms under change of variable as a quadratic differential:
\{f,z\}dz^{2}=\{f,w\}dw^{2}+\{w,z\}dz^{2}
.

In particular, under a modular transformation
\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in{\mbox{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})}
,

\left\{f,\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\right\}=(cz+d)^{4}\{f,z\}.

Now, consider the equation

y^{\prime\prime}+R(z)y=0,

and let
y_{1},y_{2}
be two linearly independent meromorphic solutions. Then the ratio
h=y_{2}/y_{1}
satisfies:

\{h,z\}=2R(z).

Conversely, if
\{h,z\}=2R(z)
and
h
is locally univalent (i.e.,
h^{\prime}(z)\neq 0
), then
y_{1}=1/\sqrt{h^{\prime}}
and
y_{2}=h/\sqrt{h^{\prime}}
are two linearly independent solutions of the equation.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
9
]
)
.

Let
h
be meromorphic on
{\mathbb{H}}
. Then
\{h,z\}
is a weight
4
meromorphic modular form for a discrete group
\Gamma
if and only if there exists a two-dimensional complex representation
\varrho:\Gamma\to\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
such that

(2.2)

h(\gamma\cdot z)=\varrho(\gamma)\cdot h(z),\qquad\text{for all }\gamma\in\Gamma,\,z\in{\mathbb{H}}.

A function
h
satisfying (
2.2
) is called a
\varrho
-equivariant function. Such functions have been extensively studied; see for instance
[
3
,
4
,
13
,
14
]
. For any discrete subgroup
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and any two-dimensional complex representation
\varrho
of
\Gamma
, equivariant functions always exist
[
10
]
.

Examples include:

•

If
\varrho=\mathrm{id}
, then equivariance means
h(\gamma\cdot z)=\gamma\cdot h(z)
, and we say
h
is an equivariant function. A trivial example is
h_{\infty}(z)=z
.

•

If
F=\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}\\
f_{2}\end{pmatrix}
is a vector-valued modular form with multiplier system
\varrho
, then
h=f_{2}/f_{1}
is
\varrho
-equivariant.

•

If
f
is a holomorphic modular form of weight
k
, then

h_{f}(z)=z+k\frac{f(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}

is an equivariant function.

Remark 2.2
.

Throughout this article, we assume that all representations
\varrho
of
{\mbox{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})}
satisfy
\varrho(-I)\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}\cdot I
.

In this context, we aim to analyze the pair of equivalent equations, for
r\in\mathbb{Q}
:

(2.3)

\displaystyle y^{\prime\prime}+r^{2}\pi^{2}E_{4}(z)y=0,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\{h,z\}=2\pi^{2}r^{2}E_{4}(z),

which are equivalent in the sense that the ratio of any two linearly independent solutions of (
2.3
) solves (
2.4
), and conversely, any locally univalent solution to (
2.4
) gives rise to two independent solutions of (
2.3
).

Since a solution to (
2.4
) must be
\varrho
-equivariant for some representation
\varrho
of
{\mbox{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})}
, a thorough understanding of such representations is necessary. Write
r=m/n
in lowest terms. The nature of the solutions depends critically on the value of
n
. Specifically, there are four cases:

(1)

n=1
,

(2)

2\leq n\leq 5
,

(3)

n=6
,

(4)

n\geq 7
.

Each case exhibits distinct analytic and arithmetic behavior. These families have been systematically analyzed in
[
12
,
9
,
11
,
2
]
, each requiring different techniques.

3.
The Reducible Case

As discussed earlier, a solution to the Schwarzian differential equation

\{h,z\}=2\pi^{2}r^{2}E_{4}(z)

must be
\varrho
-equivariant for some representation
\varrho
of
{\mbox{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})}
. This section focuses on the case when the representation
\varrho
is
reducible
.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
9
]
)
.

A meromorphic function
h
on
{\mathbb{H}}
is
\varrho
-equivariant for a reducible representation
\varrho
of
{\mbox{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})}
if and only if
h^{\prime}
is a meromorphic modular form of weight
2
with a character. This situation occurs if and only if

r=\frac{m}{6},\qquad\gcd(m,6)=1.

Remark 3.2
.

Since every character of
{\mbox{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})}
is trivial on the commutator subgroup, which is a level
6
and index
12
congruence subgroup, we have:

T^{12}\in\ker\varrho,\quad\text{where }T=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

The image of
T
in
{\mbox{PSL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})}
has order 6, which justifies its appearance as a distinguished denominator.

Using the analytic properties of the Schwarzian derivative, we have the following:

Proposition 3.3

(
[
11
]
)
.

Let
h
be a rho-equivariant meromorphic function such that
h^{\prime}
is non-vanishing and holomorphic, or has only double poles with zero residues. Then
\{h,z\}
is a holomorphic modular form of weight
4
, and
h
satisfies

\{h,z\}=2\pi^{2}r^{2}E_{4}(z)\quad\text{for some }r=\frac{m}{6},\ \gcd(m,6)=1.

We begin with the simplest case, when
h^{\prime}
is holomorphic and non-vanishing on
{\mathbb{H}}
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Up to scalar multiplication,
\eta^{4}
is the only non-vanishing holomorphic modular form of weight
2
with character. Then the function

h(z)=\int_{i}^{z}\eta^{4}(\omega)\,d\omega

is
\varrho
-equivariant and satisfies the Schwarzian equation with
r=\frac{1}{6}
.

Proof.

The space of weight
2
holomorphic modular forms with character is one-dimensional and spanned by
\eta^{4}
. Since
\eta^{4}
is non-vanishing on
{\mathbb{H}}
, the integral
h(z)=\int_{i}^{z}\eta^{4}(\omega)\,d\omega
defines a holomorphic locally univalent function. Its Schwarzian derivative is holomorphic of weight
4
and, by computation of the leading term, satisfies:

\{h,z\}=2\pi^{2}\left(\frac{1}{6}\right)^{2}E_{4}(z)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{18}E_{4}(z).

∎

The corresponding second-order modular differential equation is:

(3.1)

y^{\prime\prime}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{36}E_{4}(z)y=0.

This equation appeared as early as 1877 in Klein’s work
[
7
]
. In 1889, Hurwitz showed that
\eta^{-2}
is a solution; see
[
5
, Equation 13]
. Our construction validates this result: since
h^{\prime}=\eta^{4}
, one solution is
y=1/\sqrt{h^{\prime}}=\eta^{-2}
.

To construct more general solutions of the Schwarzian equation for arbitrary values of
r=\frac{m}{6}
with
\gcd(m,6)=1
, we seek functions
h
such that
h^{\prime}
is a meromorphic modular form of weight
2
with character, and has only double poles with zero residues. The strategy is to build such
h^{\prime}
via an ansatz.

Recall that:

(1)

The modular function
J(z)-J(w)
has a simple zero for each non-elliptic point
w\in{\mathbb{H}}
.

(2)

The Eisenstein series
E_{4}
vanishes only at the orbit of the cubic root of unity
\rho
, and
E_{6}
vanishes only at the orbit of
i
.

This motivates the following ansatz:

Let
k\geq 0
be an integer,
s,t\in\{0,2\}
, and let
w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\in{\mathbb{H}}
be pairwise
{\mbox{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})}
-inequivalent and not elliptic. Define:

(3.2)

f(z):=\frac{\eta(z)^{4}}{E_{4}^{s}(z)E_{6}^{t}(z)}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{(J(z)-J(w_{i}))^{2}}.

Then
f
is a modular form of weight
2
(with character), with double poles at the points
w_{i}
, and at
\rho
and
i
if
s=2
or
t=2
, respectively. Provided all residues vanish, the function

h(z)=\int_{i}^{z}f(\omega)\,d\omega

is equivariant and satisfies the Schwarzian equation.

Theorem 3.5

(
[
8
]
)
.

Let
f
be defined as in (
3.2
) and suppose that all its residues vanish. Then
h(z)=\int_{i}^{z}f(\omega)\,d\omega
satisfies the Schwarzian equation

\{h,z\}=2\pi^{2}r^{2}E_{4}(z),

for the following values of
r
:

(1)

(s,t)=(0,0)\Rightarrow r=\dfrac{1+12k}{6}
,

(2)

(s,t)=(0,2)\Rightarrow r=\dfrac{5+12k}{6}
,

(3)

(s,t)=(2,0)\Rightarrow r=\dfrac{7+12k}{6}
,

(4)

(s,t)=(2,2)\Rightarrow r=\dfrac{11+12k}{6}
.

These account for all rational values
r=m/6
with
\gcd(m,6)=1
.

The vanishing of the residues at
i
or
\rho
in the above ansatz relies on the following identities among derivatives of classical modular forms:

Proposition 3.6

(
[
8
]
)
.

We have

12\,\frac{\eta^{\prime}(i)}{\eta(i)}=\frac{3}{7}\frac{E_{6}^{\prime\prime}(i)}{E_{6}^{\prime}(i)}=\frac{J^{\prime\prime\prime}(i)}{J^{\prime\prime}(i)}=3i,

24\,\frac{\eta^{\prime}(\rho)}{\eta(\rho)}=\frac{J^{(4)}(\rho)}{J^{\prime\prime\prime}(\rho)}=\frac{6}{5}\frac{E_{4}^{\prime\prime}(\rho)}{E_{4}^{\prime}(\rho)}=12\,\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}.

The final ingredient is the condition for the residues at the points
w_{i}
to vanish.

Theorem 3.7

(
[
8
]
)
.

Let
x_{i}=J(w_{i})
with
x_{i}\neq x_{j}
for
i\neq j
, and
x_{i}\notin\{0,1\}
. Then the function
f
has vanishing residues at each
w_{i}
if and only if the tuple
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
satisfies the algebraic system:

\frac{a}{x_{i}}+\frac{b}{x_{i}-1}+\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{c}{x_{i}-x_{j}}=0,\quad 1\leq i\leq k,

with parameters
(a,b,c)
depending on
(s,t)
as follows:

(1)

(s,t)=(0,0)\Rightarrow(a,b,c)=(4,3,12)
,

(2)

(s,t)=(0,2)\Rightarrow(a,b,c)=(4,9,12)
,

(3)

(s,t)=(2,0)\Rightarrow(a,b,c)=(8,3,12)
,

(4)

(s,t)=(2,2)\Rightarrow(a,b,c)=(8,9,12)
.

Remark 3.8
.

The ansatz construction applies uniformly to all values
r=\frac{m}{6}
with
\gcd(m,6)=1
, and reduces the problem to solving the system (
4.1
). Once a single solution
h
to the Schwarzian equation is found, all others are obtained by Möbius transformations of
h
, due to the projective invariance of the Schwarzian derivative.

4.
Algebraic Systems and an Auxiliary Differential Equation

When the coefficients
a,b,c
in the algebraic system

(4.1)

\frac{a}{x_{i}}+\frac{b}{x_{i}-1}+\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{c}{x_{i}-x_{j}}=0,\qquad 1\leq i\leq k,

share the same sign, it was shown abstractly in
[
11
]
that the system always admits a solution. In this section, we aim to characterize more precisely when the system admits a solution and to determine such solutions explicitly. This is accomplished by introducing a second-order differential equation of Fuchsian type, whose solutions encode the roots of (
4.1
).

When
k=1
, the system reduces to a single quadratic equation.
Let
k\geq 2
and fix complex parameters
a,b,c\in\mathbb{C}
. We assume
c\neq 0
; otherwise, the system becomes decoupled and trivial. Define the following second-order Fuchsian differential equation:

(4.2)

\frac{c}{2}x(x-1)y^{\prime\prime}(x)+\bigl[(a+b)x-a\bigr]y^{\prime}(x)-\lambda y(x)=0,

with

\lambda:=k(a+b)+\frac{c}{2}k(k-1).

This equation has regular singularities at
x=0,1,\infty
. We consider the formal power series solution:

S(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}s_{i}x^{i},

and derive from (
4.2
) the recurrence relation:

(4.3)

(k-i)\left(a+b+\frac{c}{2}(k+i-1)\right)s_{i}+(i+1)\left(a+\frac{c}{2}i\right)s_{i+1}=0.

This recurrence implies that the solution
S(x)
is a polynomial of degree at most
k
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
be a solution to (
4.1
), where the
x_{i}
are pairwise distinct and not equal to
0
or
1
. Define

P(x):=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(x-x_{i}).

Then
P(x)
is a solution to the differential equation (
4.2
).

Proof.

Since the roots
x_{i}
are simple, we have for each
i
,

\frac{P^{\prime\prime}(x_{i})}{P^{\prime}(x_{i})}=\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{2}{x_{i}-x_{j}}.

Substituting this into the system (
4.1
), we obtain:

\frac{a}{x_{i}}+\frac{b}{x_{i}-1}+\frac{c}{2}\frac{P^{\prime\prime}(x_{i})}{P^{\prime}(x_{i})}=0.

Therefore, the polynomial

Q(x):=\frac{c}{2}x(x-1)P^{\prime\prime}(x)+\bigl[(a+b)x-a\bigr]P^{\prime}(x),

is a degree
\leq k
polynomial that vanishes at all
x_{i}
, hence divisible by
P(x)
. Since
\deg Q(x)\leq k
, it follows that
Q(x)=\lambda P(x)
, and
P(x)
is a solution of (
4.2
).
∎

We now give a complete criterion for the existence of a solution to the algebraic system (
4.1
).

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
a,b,c\in\mathbb{C}
and
k\geq 2
. Then the system (
4.1
) admits a solution
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in(\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\})^{k}
with distinct coordinates if and only if

(4.4)

D_{k}(a,b,c):=\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\left(a+\frac{c}{2}i\right)\left(b+\frac{c}{2}i\right)\left[a+b+\frac{c}{2}(k+i-1)\right]\neq 0.

Proof.

Suppose such a solution exists. Then the polynomial

P(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(x-x_{i})

is a degree
k
solution of (
4.2
). The coefficients
s_{i}
of
P(x)
satisfy the recurrence relation (
4.3
), with
s_{k}=1
. By Bochner’s theorem
[
15
, Chapter 6]
, a linear differential equation with polynomial coefficients has at most one monic polynomial solution of each degree, so the solution
S(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}s_{i}x^{i}
is uniquely determined.

Now, the recurrence (
4.3
) gives a triangular system for
s_{0},\ldots,s_{k-1}
, whose solvability requires that the coefficients in the denominators do not vanish. Hence, all factors in the product defining
D_{k}(a,b,c)
must be nonzero.

Furthermore, for
P(x)
to have no roots at
x=0
, we must have
a+\frac{c}{2}i\neq 0
for all
i
, and similarly
b+\frac{c}{2}i\neq 0
for all
i
, to avoid roots at
x=1
since
P(1-x)
satisfies a similar Fuchsian differential equation. This shows the necessity of non-vanishing of all the terms in
D_{k}(a,b,c)
.

Conversely, assume
D_{k}(a,b,c)\neq 0
. We may take
s_{k}=1
and solve the recurrence (
4.3
) backwards to uniquely determine
s_{0},\ldots,s_{k-1}
. Then

S(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}s_{i}x^{i}

is a monic polynomial of degree
k
satisfying (
4.2
). By construction,
S(0)\neq 0
and
S(1)\neq 0
, so all roots of
S(x)
lie in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}
.

We must show that the roots of
S(x)
are distinct. If
x_{i}
were a multiple root, then all derivatives of
S(x)
would vanish at
x_{i}
, which contradicts the fact that
S
is a nonzero polynomial of degree
k
. Thus, the roots are simple.

Finally, using the identity

\frac{S^{\prime\prime}(x_{i})}{S^{\prime}(x_{i})}=\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{2}{x_{i}-x_{j}},

and evaluating the differential equation at each
x_{i}
, we deduce that
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
satisfies the system (
4.1
).
∎

5.
Orthogonal Polynomial Sequence and Structural Properties

We conclude our construction of explicit solutions to the modular differential equation by analyzing the auxiliary Fuchsian equation (
4.2
). The polynomial solutions to this equation, which encode the roots of the algebraic system (
4.1
), form a family of classical orthogonal polynomials. In this section, we describe their structural properties, orthogonality, recurrence, and norms.

We consider the second-order differential equation:

(5.1)

\frac{c}{2}x(x-1)P_{n}^{\prime\prime}(x)+\bigl[(a+b)x-a\bigr]P_{n}^{\prime}(x)-\lambda_{n}P_{n}(x)=0,

where
a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}
,
c\neq 0
, and

\lambda_{n}=n(a+b)+\frac{c}{2}n(n-1).

For each integer
n\geq 0
, equation (
5.1
) admits a unique (up to scalar) polynomial solution
P_{n}(x)
of degree
n
, which we normalize to be monic.

Orthogonality and Weight Function

To determine the orthogonality of the sequence
\{P_{n}(x)\}
, we rewrite the differential operator in Sturm–Liouville form:

(5.2)

\mathcal{D}f(x)=\frac{1}{w(x)}\frac{d}{dx}\left(-x(1-x)w(x)\frac{df}{dx}\right).

This holds if and only if the weight function
w(x)
satisfies the Pearson differential equation:

\frac{d}{dx}\left(x(1-x)w(x)\right)=\tau(x)w(x),\quad\text{where }\tau(x)=\frac{2}{c}[(a+b)x-a].

Solving yields:

w(x)=x^{\alpha}(1-x)^{\beta},\qquad\alpha=\frac{2a}{c}-1,\quad\beta=\frac{2b}{c}-1.

This function is integrable on
(0,1)
and defines a positive measure precisely when
\alpha,\beta>-1
, which corresponds to the parameter regime encountered in the construction of modular differential equation solutions in the previous sections, that is,
a
,
b
and
c
have the same sign.

The corresponding inner product is:

\langle f,g\rangle:=\int_{0}^{1}f(x)g(x)w(x)\,dx.

The operator (
5.2
) is formally self-adjoint with respect to this inner product, and thus distinct eigenfunctions
P_{n}(x)
and
P_{m}(x)
, for
n\neq m
, are orthogonal.

Three-Term Recurrence Relation

By Favard’s theorem (see
[
1
]
), the orthogonal polynomials
\{P_{n}(x)\}
satisfy a three-term recurrence relation of the form:

(5.3)

P_{n+1}(x)=\left(A_{n}x+B_{n}\right)P_{n}(x)+C_{n}P_{n-1}(x),

valid for all
n\geq 1
, with initial conditions:

P_{0}(x)=1,\qquad P_{1}(x)=x-\frac{a}{a+b}.

The recurrence coefficients are explicitly given in terms of
\alpha,\beta
by:

\displaystyle A_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(n+1)(n+\alpha+\beta+1)}{(2n+\alpha+\beta+1)(2n+\alpha+\beta+2)},

\displaystyle B_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta^{2}-\alpha^{2}}{(2n+\alpha+\beta)(2n+\alpha+\beta+2)},

\displaystyle C_{n}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2(n+\alpha)(n+\beta)(2n+\alpha+\beta+2)}{(2n+\alpha+\beta)^{2}(2n+\alpha+\beta+1)}.

In particular, if the parameters
a
,
b
and
c
are rational, then
P_{n}(x)\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
for all
n
.

Norms and Classical Properties

The squared norms of the orthogonal polynomials with respect to
w(x)
are given by the classical beta integral:

\langle P_{n},P_{n}\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}P_{n}(x)^{2}x^{\alpha}(1-x)^{\beta}\,dx=h_{n},

where

h_{n}=\frac{1}{2n+\alpha+\beta+1}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)\Gamma(n+\beta+1)}{n!\Gamma(n+\alpha+\beta+1)}.

This expression is valid for all
\alpha,\beta>-1
, ensuring that the sequence
\{P_{n}\}
forms a complete orthogonal system in
L^{2}((0,1),w(x)\,dx)
.

Under the same condition
\alpha,\beta>-1
, the roots of
P_{n}
are all real, simple, and lie in the open interval
(0,1)
. Moreover, the roots of
P_{n+1}
interlace those of
P_{n}
, a classical property of orthogonal polynomials on the real line (see
[
15
, Thm. 6.21.1]
).

In summary, the construction of orthogonal polynomials solving (
5.1
) completes the explicit resolution of the modular differential equation. These polynomials encode the Schwarzian solutions via their roots and reflect the arithmetic structure of the original equivariant functions.
