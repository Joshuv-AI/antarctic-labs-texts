q q -analogues of sums of consecutive powers of natural numbers and extended Carlitz q q -Bernoulli numbers and polynomials
Bakir FARHI

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate a specific class of q q -polynomial sequences that serve as a q q -analogue of the classical Appell sequences. This framework offers an elegant approach to revisiting classical results by Carlitz and, more interestingly, to establishing an important extension of the Carlitz q q -Bernoulli polynomials and numbers. In addition, we establish explicit series representations for our extended Carlitz q q -Bernoulli numbers and express them in terms of q q -Stirling numbers of the second kind. This leads to a novel formula that explicitly connects the Carlitz q q -Bernoulli numbers with the q q -Stirling numbers of the second kind. MSC 2020: Primary 05A30; Secondary 11B68, 11B73, 05A40. Keywords: Carlitz q q -Bernoulli numbers and polynomials, q q -analogue of Appell sequences, q q -analogues of sums of consecutive powers of natural numbers, q q -Stirling numbers of the second kind, umbral calculus.

Introduction and Notation

Throughout this paper, we let
{\mathbb{N}}
and
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
respectively denote the set of positive integers and the set of nonnegative integers. For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we let
{(X)}_{n}
denote the falling factorial of
X
to depth
n
, defined as

{(X)}_{n}:=X(X-1)\cdots(X-n+1).

The Stirling numbers of the second kind, denoted
S(n,k)
(
n,k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,
n\geq k
), are then defined through the polynomial identity:

X^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S(n,k){(X)}_{k}~~~~~~~~~~(\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}).

The Bernoulli polynomials are denoted, as usual, by
B_{n}(X)
and the Bernoulli numbers by
B_{n}
(
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
). A modern definition of the Bernoulli polynomials and numbers employs their exponential generating functions, given by

\frac{t}{e^{t}-1}e^{Xt}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B_{n}(X)\frac{t^{n}}{n!}~~,~~\frac{t}{e^{t}-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B_{n}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}

(so
B_{n}=B_{n}(0)
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
). As is well-known, the Bernoulli polynomials and numbers play a central role in various branches of mathematics, including number theory, mathematical analysis, and algebraic geometry. For a modern and exhaustive perspective, the reader is referred to the paper by Kouba
[
7
]
. An interesting and well-known formula expressing the Bernoulli numbers in terms of the Stirling numbers of the second kind is given by:

B_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\frac{k!}{k+1}S(n,k)~~~~~~~~~~(\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0})

(1.1)

(see e.g.,
[
4
, Corollary 2.8]
).

It should be noted that the Bernoulli polynomial sequence
{\left(B_{n}(X)\right)}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
belongs to the broader class of Appell sequences (see
[
3
,
9
]
). These are sequences of polynomials
{\left(P_{n}(X)\right)}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
characterized by the following properties:
P_{0}(X)
is a nonzero constant polynomial, and for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the derivatives satisfy the relation
P_{n}^{\prime}(X)=nP_{n-1}(X)
. Using the Taylor expansion, an Appell polynomial sequence
{\left(P_{n}(X)\right)}_{n}
can also be characterized by its general term, which takes the form

P_{n}(X)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}p_{k}X^{n-k}~~~~~~~~~~(\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}),

where
{(p_{k})}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is a sequence of scalars with
p_{0}\neq 0
.

Further, let
q
be a positive real parameter. The
q
-analogue of
X
, whether treated as an indeterminate or a number, is defined by:

[X]:=\frac{q^{X}-1}{q-1}.

For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, the
q
-analogue
{[X]}_{k}
of
{(X)}_{k}
is defined as

[X]_{k}:=[X]\cdot[X-1]\cdots[X-k+1].

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, the
q
-analogue of
n!
is given by

[n]!:={[n]}_{n}=[n]\cdot[n-1]\cdots[1].

Furthermore, for
n,k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
with
n\geq k
, the
q
-analogue
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}
of the binomial coefficient
\binom{n}{k}
is defined as

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}:=\frac{[n]!}{[k]![n-k]!}=\frac{{[n]}_{k}}{[k]!}.

These numbers appear, in particular, in the famous Gauss binomial formula:

\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(x+q^{k}y\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}x^{n-k}y^{k}~~~~~~~~~~(\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}},\forall x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}),

(1.2)

and, just like the classical binomial coefficients, they also have a combinatorial interpretation (see, e.g.,
[
5
]
).

On the other hand, we let
\Delta
denote the forward difference operator which acts linearly on
{\mathbb{R}}[X]
by the formula:

(\Delta P)(X):=P(X+1)-P(X)~~~~~~~~~~(\forall P\in{\mathbb{R}}[X]).

It is well known and easy to verify that the
n
-fold composition of
\Delta
is given by the formula:

(\Delta^{n}P)(X)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}P(X+k)~~~~~~~~~~(\forall P\in{\mathbb{R}}[X],\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}).

(1.3)

In this paper, we call a
q
-polynomial any polynomial in
[X]
with real coefficients. Equivalently, a
q
-polynomial is a polynomial in
q^{X}
with real coefficients. The degree of a
q
-polynomial refers to its degree as a polynomial in
[X]
(or equivalently in
q^{X}
). We denote by
\mathscr{E}
the
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space of all
q
-polynomials and by
\mathscr{E}_{d}
(
d\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
) the
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector subspace of
\mathscr{E}
consisting of
q
-polynomials of degree
\leq d
. An important example of
q
-polynomials arises naturally in the evaluation of sums of the form

S_{n,r}(N):=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{rk}{[k]}^{n}

(
N,n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
), which are
q
-analogues of the sum of powers
\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}k^{n}
. More interestingly, when expressed as a linear combination of the
q
-polynomials
q^{kN}{[N]}^{n-k}
(
0\leq k\leq n
),
S_{n,r}(N)
leads to a
q
-analogue example of Appell polynomial sequences (see Theorem
2.6
). Perhaps it was by exploring this fact that Carlitz
[
1
]
obtained important
q
-analogues of Bernoulli numbers and polynomials. By means of the symbolic calculus, Carlitz
[
1
]
defined two real sequences
{(\eta_{n})}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
and
{(\beta_{n})}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
(depending on
q
) by:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\eta_{0}=1,\eta_{1}=0,\\
(q\eta+1)^{n}=\eta^{n}~~(\forall n\geq 2)\end{array}\right.,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\beta_{0}=1,\\
q(q\beta+1)^{n}-\beta^{n}=\delta_{n,1}~~(\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}})\end{array}\right.,

where
\delta_{i,j}
denotes the Kronecker delta. Furthermore, it is noted that for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
:

\beta_{n}=\eta_{n}+(q-1)\eta_{n+1}.

(1.8)

Although both sequences are intriguing, the sequence
{(\beta_{n})}_{n}
is particularly noteworthy because its terms are well-defined at
q=1
(unlike
{(\eta_{n})}_{n}
, for which only the first two terms are defined at
q=1
). Moreover, specializing
q=1
in
\beta_{n}
recovers the
n
th
Bernoulli number
B_{n}
. These facts are far from trivial. To prove them, Carlitz was led to introduce
q
-analogues of the Stirling numbers of the second kind,
S_{q}(n,k)(n,k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},n\geq k)
, which he defined via the following identity of
q
-polynomials:

{[X]}^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{\frac{1}{2}k(k-1)}S_{q}(n,k){[X]}_{k}

(1.9)

(for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
). Specializing
q=1
in
S_{q}(n,k)
(
n,k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,
n\geq k
) recovers the classical Stirling numbers of the second kind
S(n,k)
. Using the
S_{q}(n,k)
’s, Carlitz
[
1
]
established the following formula for
\beta_{n}
(
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
):

\beta_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\frac{[k]!}{[k+1]}S_{q}(n,k),

(1.10)

which confirms that
\beta_{n}
is always well-defined at
q=1
, with value equal to the Bernoulli number
B_{n}
(according to (
1.1
)).

Carlitz also associated to the sequences
{(\eta_{n})}_{n}
and
{(\beta_{n})}_{n}
the
q
-polynomial sequences
{(\eta_{n}(X))}_{n}
and
{(\beta_{n}(X))}_{n}
, given by:

\displaystyle\eta_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\eta_{k}q^{kX}{[X]}^{n-k},

(1.11)

\displaystyle\beta_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\beta_{k}q^{kX}{[X]}^{n-k}

(1.12)

(for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
). The original sequences
{(\eta_{n})}_{n}
and
{(\beta_{n})}_{n}
are then respectively obtained as the values of
{(\eta_{n}(X))}_{n}
and
{(\beta_{n}(X))}_{n}
at
X=0
. Especially,
{(\beta_{n}(X))}_{n}
constitutes a
q
-analogue of the classical Bernoulli polynomial sequence
{(B_{n}(X))}_{n}
. That said, alternative definitions of the Carlitz
q
-Bernoulli numbers and polynomials exist, such as those based on generating functions or
p
-adic
q
-integrals on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
. For example, Koblitz
[
6
]
constructed
q
-analogues of the
p
-adic Dirichlet
L
-series that interpolate the Carlitz
q
-Bernoulli numbers.

In this work, we first incorporate the Carlitz
q
-polynomial sequences
{(\eta_{n}(X))}_{n}
and
{(\beta_{n}(X))}_{n}
into a broader class of
q
-polynomial sequences, which we call “Carlitz-type
q
-polynomial sequences”. We establish a fundamental theorem showing that this class of
q
-polynomial sequences serves as a
q
-analogue of the classical Appell polynomial sequences. Next, We verify that the closed forms of the
q
-analogues of power sums of consecutive natural numbers can essentially be expressed in terms of Carlitz-type
q
-polynomial sequences. Furthermore, we show that a specific subclass of Carlitz-type
q
-polynomial sequences (including
{(\eta_{n}(X))}_{n}
and
{(\beta_{n}(X))}_{n}
) can be generated using simple symbolic formulas, such as those established by Carlitz in
[
1
]
. We then establish several properties of Carlitz-type
q
-polynomial sequences and leverage these properties to extend
{(\eta_{n}(X))}_{n}
and
{(\beta_{n}(X))}_{n}
into
{(\beta_{n}^{(r)}(X))}_{n}
(
r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
), which provide important
q
-analogues of the classical Bernoulli polynomials for
r\geq 1
. Additionally, we represent the numbers
\beta_{n}^{(r)}:=\beta_{n}^{(r)}(0)
as series and express them in terms of the
q
-Stirling numbers of the second kind. Finally, we derive a novel formula connecting the Carlitz
q
-Bernoulli numbers with the
q
-Stirling numbers of the second kind.

The results and the proofs

2.1

Carlitz-type
q
-polynomial sequences

We first introduce the following definition:

Definition 2.1
.

Let
{(T_{n}(X))}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
be a
q
-polynomial sequence. We say that
{(T_{n}(X))}_{n}
is
Carlitz-type
if its general term
T_{n}(X)
(
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
) has the form:

T_{n}(X)=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}a_{k}q^{kX},

where
{(a_{k})}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is a real sequence. In this case,
{(a_{k})}_{k}
is called
the associated sequence
of the Carlitz-type
q
-polynomial sequence
{(T_{n}(X))}_{n}
.

An important characterization of the Carlitz-type
q
-polynomial sequences is given by the following fundamental theorem:

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
{(T_{n}(X))}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
be a
q
-polynomial sequence, and for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, set
t_{n}:=T_{n}(0)
. Then
{(T_{n}(X))}_{n}
is Carlitz-type if and only if we have for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
:

T_{n}(X)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}t_{k}q^{kX}{[X]}^{n-k},

that is (symbolically):

T_{n}(X)=\left(q^{X}t+[X]\right)^{n}.

To prove this theorem, we need to use the inversion formula from the following lemma:

Lemma 2.3

(see e.g,
[
2
,
8
]
)
.

Let
{(u_{n})}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
and
{(v_{n})}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
be two real sequences. Then the two following identities are equivalent:

\displaystyle u_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}v_{k}~~~~~~~~~~

\displaystyle(\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}),

\displaystyle v_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}u_{k}~~~~~~~~~~

\displaystyle(\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}).

∎

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Suppose that
{(T_{n}(X))}_{n}
is Carlitz-type. So there exists a real sequence
{(a_{n})}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
for which we have for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
:

T_{n}(X)=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}a_{k}q^{kX}.

In particular, we have for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
:

t_{n}:=T_{n}(0)=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}a_{k},

that is

(q-1)^{n}t_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}a_{k}.

By inverting this last formula (using Lemma
2.3
), we derive that for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
:

a_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(q-1)^{k}t_{k}.

Then, by inserting this into the expression of
T_{n}(X)
, we obtain that for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
:

\displaystyle T_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}(q-1)^{i}t_{i}\right)q^{kX}

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}\binom{k}{i}(q-1)^{i}t_{i}q^{kX}.

By inverting the summations and remarking that
\binom{n}{k}\binom{k}{i}=\binom{n}{i}\binom{n-i}{k-i}
(for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,
k\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
, and
i\in\{0,1,\dots,k\}
), we derive that for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
:

\displaystyle T_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{0\leq i\leq n}\sum_{i\leq k\leq n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{i}\binom{n-i}{k-i}(q-1)^{i}t_{i}q^{kX}

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{0\leq i\leq n}\sum_{0\leq j\leq n-i}(-1)^{n-i-j}\binom{n}{i}\binom{n-i}{j}(q-1)^{i}t_{i}q^{(i+j)X}~~~(\text{where we set $j=k-i$})

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{0\leq i\leq n}\binom{n}{i}(q-1)^{i}t_{i}q^{iX}\sum_{0\leq j\leq n-i}\binom{n-i}{j}q^{jX}(-1)^{n-i-j}.

But according to the binomial formula, we have for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and all
i\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
:

\sum_{0\leq j\leq n-i}\binom{n-i}{j}q^{jX}(-1)^{n-i-j}=\left(q^{X}-1\right)^{n-i}=(q-1)^{n-i}{[X]}^{n-i}.

By inserting this into the last obtained expression for
T_{n}(X)
, we finally derive that for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
:

T_{n}(X)=\sum_{0\leq i\leq n}\binom{n}{i}t_{i}q^{iX}{[X]}^{n-i},

as required.

Conversely, suppose that we have for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
:

T_{n}(X)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}t_{k}q^{kX}{[X]}^{n-k},

and define for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
:

\displaystyle a_{n}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(q-1)^{k}t_{k}

(2.1)

and

\displaystyle S_{n}(X)

\displaystyle:=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}a_{k}q^{kX}.

Equivalently,
{(S_{n}(X))}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is a Carlitz-type
q
-polynomial sequence, with associated sequence
{(a_{n})}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
. So, according to the first part of this proof, we have for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
:

S_{n}(X)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}s_{k}q^{kX}{[X]}^{n-k},

(2.2)

where

s_{n}:=S_{n}(0)=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}a_{k}.

(2.3)

But the inversion of Formula (
2.1
) (using Lemma
2.3
) gives for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
:

(q-1)^{n}t_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}a_{k},

that is

t_{n}=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}a_{k}=s_{n}

(according to (
2.3
)). Hence we have for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
:

\displaystyle T_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}t_{k}q^{kX}{[X]}^{n-k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}s_{k}q^{kX}{[X]}^{n-k}

\displaystyle=S_{n}(X)~~~~~~~~~~(\text{according to \eqref{eq11}}).

Consequently
{(T_{n}(X))}_{n}={(S_{n}(X))}_{n}
is Carlitz-type, as required. This complete this proof.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

From the perspective of Theorem
2.2
, the Carlitz-type
q
-polynomial sequences can be regarded as a
q
-analogue of the Appell polynomial sequences.

Examples 2.5
.

In
[
1
]
, Carlitz introduced two important
q
-polynomial sequences, which are
{(\eta_{n}(X))}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
and
{(\beta_{n}(X))}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
, defined for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
by:

\displaystyle\eta_{n}(X)

\displaystyle:=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}\frac{k}{[k]}q^{kX},

(2.4)

\displaystyle\beta_{n}(X)

\displaystyle:=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}\frac{k+1}{[k+1]}q^{kX}

(2.5)

(where we take by convention
\frac{k}{[k]}=1
for
k=0
). From their definitions, it is clear that
{(\eta_{n}(X))}_{n}
and
{(\beta_{n}(X))}_{n}
are both Carlitz-type with associated real sequences
{(\frac{k}{[k]})}_{k}
and
{(\frac{k+1}{[k+1]})}_{k}
respectively. In addition, Carlitz
[
1
]
explored the real sequences constituted of the values of
{(\eta_{n}(X))}_{n}
and
{(\beta_{n}(X))}_{n}
at
X=0
; that is the sequences
{(\eta_{n})}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
and
{(\beta_{n})}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
, defined by:

\eta_{n}:=\eta_{n}(0)~~\text{and}~~\beta_{n}:=\beta_{n}(0)~~~~~~~~~~(\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}).

So, according to Theorem
2.2
, we have important alternative expressions for
\eta_{n}(X)
and
\beta_{n}(X)
(
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
), which are

\displaystyle\eta_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\eta_{k}q^{kX}{[X]}^{n-k},

(2.6)

\displaystyle\beta_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\beta_{k}q^{kX}{[X]}^{n-k}.

(2.7)

Using the symbolic calculus, Formulas (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) can be written more simply as:

\displaystyle\eta_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=\left(q^{X}\eta+[X]\right)^{n},

(2.8)

\displaystyle\beta_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=\left(q^{X}\beta+[X]\right)^{n}

(2.9)

(for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
).

We now see that Carlitz-type
q
-polynomial sequences naturally emerge when deriving closed-form expressions for
q
-analogues of power sums of consecutive positive integers. We begin with the following theorem:

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
r
be a positive integer. For all nonnegative integer
n
and all positive integer
N
, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{rk}{[k]}^{n}=q^{rN}S_{n}(N)-S_{n}(0),

where
{(S_{k}(X))}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is the Carlitz-type
q
-polynomial sequence associated to the real sequence of general term
a_{k}=\frac{1}{q-1}\frac{1}{[k+r]}
(
\forall k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
).

Proof.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
be fixed. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{rk}{[k]}^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{rk}\left(\frac{q^{k}-1}{q-1}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{rk}\left(q^{k}-1\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{rk}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n}{i}q^{ki}(-1)^{n-i}~~~~~~~~~~(\text{by the binomial formula})

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-i}\binom{n}{i}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{(i+r)k}

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-i}\binom{n}{i}\frac{q^{(i+r)N}-1}{q^{i+r}-1}

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-i}\binom{n}{i}\frac{1}{(q-1)[i+r]}\left(q^{(i+r)N}-1\right).

Setting for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
:

S_{n}(X):=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-i}\binom{n}{i}\frac{1}{(q-1)[i+r]}q^{iX}

(so
{(S_{n}(X))}_{n}
is the Carlitz-type
q
-polynomial sequence associated to the real sequence of general term
a_{i}=\frac{1}{(q-1)[i+r]}
), we conclude that:

\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}q^{rk}{[k]}^{n}=q^{rN}S_{n}(N)-S_{n}(0),

as required.
∎

The following generalization of Theorem
2.6
aims to provide a larger class of real sequences, for which the associated Carlitz-type
q
-polynomial sequences naturally arise when evaluating
q
-analogues of power sums of consecutive positive integers.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
r
be a positive integer and
d
be a nonnegative integer such that
r\geq d
. For all integer
n\geq d
and all positive integer
N
, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{rk}{[k]}^{n-d}=\frac{q^{(r-d)N}T_{n}(N)-T_{n}(0)}{n(n-1)\cdots(n-d+1)},

where
{(T_{k}(X))}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is the Carlitz-type
q
-polynomial sequence associated to the real sequence of general term

b_{k}=(q-1)^{d-1}\frac{k(k-1)\cdots(k-d+1)}{[k+r-d]}~~~~~~~~~~(\forall k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}).

Proof.

Let
n
and
N
be fixed integers such that
n\geq d
and
N\geq 1
. According to Theorem
2.6
, we have that

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{rk}{[k]}^{n-d}=q^{rN}S_{n-d}(N)-S_{n-d}(0),

where
{(S_{k}(X))}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is the Carlitz-type
q
-polynomial sequence associated to the real sequence of general term
a_{k}=\frac{1}{(q-1)[k+r]}
(
\forall k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
). By introducing the
q
-polynomial sequence
{(T_{k}(X))}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
defined by:

T_{k}(X)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }k<d\\
k(k-1)\cdots(k-d+1)q^{dX}S_{k-d}(X)&\text{else}\end{cases}~~~~~~~~~~(\forall k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}),

the closed form of the sum of
q
-powers above is transformed to

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{rk}{[k]}^{n-d}=\frac{q^{(r-d)N}T_{n}(N)-T_{n}(0)}{n(n-1)\cdots(n-d+1)}.

So it remains to verify that
{(T_{k}(X))}_{k}
is Carlitz-type with the associated sequence that given by the theorem. For an integer
k\geq d
, we have

T_{k}(X):=k(k-1)\cdots(k-d+1)q^{dX}S_{k-d}(X)\\

\displaystyle=k(k-1)\cdots(k-d+1)q^{dX}\cdot(q-1)^{-k+d}\sum_{i=0}^{k-d}(-1)^{k-d-i}\binom{k-d}{i}\frac{1}{(q-1)[i+r]}q^{iX}

\displaystyle=k(k-1)\cdots(k-d+1)q^{dX}(q-1)^{-k+d}\sum_{j=d}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}\binom{k-d}{j-d}\frac{1}{(q-1)[j+r-d]}q^{(j-d)X}

(by setting
j=i+d
). But since
k(k-1)\cdots(k-d+1)\binom{k-d}{j-d}=j(j-1)\cdots(j-d+1)\binom{k}{j}
(for all integers
k,j\geq d
), it follows that for all integer
k\geq d
:

\displaystyle T_{k}(X)

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-k}\sum_{j=d}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j}(q-1)^{d-1}\frac{j(j-1)\cdots(j-d+1)}{[j+r-d]}q^{jX}

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-k}\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j}(q-1)^{d-1}\frac{j(j-1)\cdots(j-d+1)}{[j+r-d]}q^{jX}.

By remarking that the last formula for
T_{k}(X)
is obviously valid for
0\leq k<d
, we conclude that
{(T_{k}(X))}_{k}
is Carlitz-type and its associated real sequence has the general term
b_{j}=(q-1)^{d-1}\frac{j(j-1)\cdots(j-d+1)}{[j+r-d]}
(
\forall j\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
). The theorem is thus proved.
∎

Remark 2.8
.

It is important to note that the general term of the real sequence
{(b_{k})}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
associated to the
q
-polynomial sequence in Theorem

has the form
\frac{P(k)}{[k+s]}
, with

is a real polynomial and

is a nonnegative integer. This fact will be used below for establishing some important symbolic formulas.

In the following theorem, we will establish symbolic formulas generating the values at

of some particular Carlitz-type
q
-polynomial sequences.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
r
and
d
be two nonnegative integers and
P
be a real polynomial of degree
d
. Let also
{(T_{n}(X))}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
be the Carlitz-type
q
-polynomial sequence whose associated real sequence is
{\left(\frac{P(k)}{[k+r]}\right)}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
and
{(t_{n})}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
be the real sequence defined by
t_{n}:=T_{n}(0)
(
\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
). Then
{(t_{n})}_{n}
satisfies the symbolic formula:

q^{r}\left(qt+1\right)^{n}=t^{n}~~~~~~~~~~(\forall n>d).

Proof.

Let
n>d
be a fixed integer. From the definition of
T_{n}(X)
, we have that

T_{n}(X)=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}\frac{P(k)}{[k+r]}q^{kX}.

Thus

\displaystyle q^{r}T_{n}(1)-T_{n}(0)

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}\frac{P(k)}{[k+r]}\left(q^{k+r}-1\right)

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-n+1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}P(k)~~~~~~~~(\text{since }q^{k+r}-1=(q-1)[k+r])

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-n+1}\left(\Delta^{n}P\right)(0)~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~(\text{according to \eqref{eqn2}})

\displaystyle=0~~~~~~~~~~(\text{since }n>\mathrm{deg}{P}=d).

Consequently, we have

q^{r}T_{n}(1)=T_{n}(0)=t_{n}.

(2.10)

On the other hand, we have (according to Theorem
2.2
) the symbolic formula:

T_{n}(X)=\left(q^{X}t+[X]\right)^{n}.

In particular, we have symbolically

T_{n}(1)=\left(qt+1\right)^{n}.

By inserting this into (

), it finally follows that we have symbolically

q^{r}\left(qt+1\right)^{n}=t^{n},

as required.
∎

We now see, for a general
r\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, how the
q
-polynomials
\beta_{n}^{(r)}(X)
arise in
q
-analogues of sums of powers of consecutive positive integers. We have the following theorem providing a generalization of Corollaries
2.13
and
2.14
.

Theorem 2.18
.

Let
r
be a nonnegative integer. Then we have for all positive integers
n
and
N
:

n\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{(r+1)k}{[k]}^{n-1}+(q-1)r\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{rk}{[k]}^{n}=q^{rN}\beta_{n}^{(r)}(N)-\beta_{n}^{(r)}.

Proof.

Let
n
and
N
be fixed positive integers. By applying Theorem
2.7
for the couples
(r,0)
and
(r+1,1)
(instead of
(r,d)
), we respectively obtain the two following formulas:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{rk}{[k]}^{n}

\displaystyle=q^{rN}T_{n}(N)-T_{n}(0),

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{(r+1)k}{[k]}^{n-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{rN}S_{n}(N)-S_{n}(0)}{n},

where
{\left(T_{k}(X)\right)}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
and
{\left(S_{k}(X)\right)}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
are the Carlitz-type
q
-polynomial sequences associated respectively to the real sequences of general term:

a_{k}=\frac{1}{q-1}\frac{1}{[k+r]}~~~~\text{and}~~~~b_{k}=\frac{k}{[k+r]}~~~~~~~~~~(\forall k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}).

By combining the above formulas, we derive that:

n\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{(r+1)k}{[k]}^{n-1}+(q-1)r\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}q^{rk}{[k]}^{n}=q^{rN}U_{n}(N)-U_{n}(0),

where

U_{k}(X):=S_{k}(X)+(q-1)rT_{k}(X)~~~~~~~~~~(\forall k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}).

On the other hand, from the fact that
{\left(T_{k}(X)\right)}_{k}
and
{\left(S_{k}(X)\right)}_{k}
are Carlitz-type
q
-polynomial sequences whose associated real sequences are respectively
{\left(\frac{1}{q-1}\frac{1}{[k+r]}\right)}_{k}
and
{\left(\frac{k}{[k+r]}\right)}_{k}
, we derive that
{\left(U_{k}(X)\right)}_{k}
is also a Carlitz-type
q
-polynomial sequence, with an associated real sequence having the general term:

\frac{k}{[k+r]}+(q-1)r\left(\frac{1}{q-1}\frac{1}{[k+r]}\right)=\frac{k+r}{[k+r]}~~~~~~~~~~(\forall k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}).

Hence
{\left(U_{k}(X)\right)}_{k}
coincides with
{\left(\beta_{k}^{(r)}(X)\right)}_{k}
, concluding to the required result.
∎

2.2.2

Representation of the
\beta_{n}^{(r)}
’s as series

We now turn our attention to the representation of the extended Carlitz
q
-Bernoulli numbers
\beta_{n}^{(r)}
as numerical series. We have the following theorem:

Theorem 2.19
.

Suppose that
|{q}|<1
. Then we have for all
r\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
:

\beta_{n}^{(r)}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle-n\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}q^{k}{[k]}^{n-1}+(1-q)^{-n}&\text{if }r=0\\[8.53581pt]
\displaystyle-n\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}q^{(r+1)k}{[k]}^{n-1}+r(1-q)\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}q^{rk}{[k]}^{n}&\text{else}\end{cases}.

Proof.

The result follows by letting
N\to+\infty
in the formula of Theorem
2.18
. However, before doing so, we must evaluate
\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}q^{rN}\beta_{n}^{(r)}(N)
(for given
r\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
). To achieve this, we use Formula (
2.18
) of Proposition
2.17
. Let
r\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and
n,N\in{\mathbb{N}}
be fixed. According to Formula (
2.18
) of Proposition
2.17
, we have that:

\displaystyle\beta_{n}^{(r)}(N)

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}\frac{k+r}{[k+r]}q^{kN}

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{-n}\left[(-1)^{n}\frac{r}{[r]}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n}{k}\frac{k+r}{[k+r]}q^{kN}\right].

Since
|{q}|<1
by hypothesis, we derive that:

\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\beta_{n}^{(r)}(N)=(1-q)^{-n}\frac{r}{[r]}.

Thus

\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}q^{rN}\beta_{n}^{(r)}(N)=\begin{cases}\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\beta_{n}^{(r)}(N)=(1-q)^{-n}&\text{if }r=0\\
0&\text{else}\end{cases}.

Finally, by letting
N\rightarrow+\infty
in the formula of Theorem
2.18
and incorporating the result we just obtained, we get

n\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}q^{(r+1)k}{[k]}^{n-1}+(q-1)r\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}q^{rk}{[k]}^{n}=\begin{cases}(1-q)^{-n}-\beta_{n}^{(r)}&\text{if }r=0\\
-\beta_{n}^{(r)}&\text{else}\end{cases},

implying the desired result of the theorem and completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.20
.

For
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the formula of Theorem
2.19
can be written more simply as:

\beta_{n}^{(r)}=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\left(rq^{rk}-(n+r)q^{(r+1)k}\right){[k]}^{n-1}.

(2.23)

Taking in particular
r=1
, we derive the following important representation of the Carlitz
q
-Bernoulli numbers as series:

\beta_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\left(q^{k}-(n+1)q^{2k}\right){[k]}^{n-1}

(2.24)

(valid for
|{q}|<1
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
).

From Remark
2.20
and the fact that the extended Carlitz
q
-Bernoulli numbers of any positive order are
q
-analogues of the classical Bernoulli numbers, we immediately derive the following corollary:

Corollary 2.21
.

For all
r,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have that

B_{n}=\lim_{q\stackrel{{\scriptstyle<}}{{\to 1}}}\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\left(rq^{rk}-(n+r)q^{(r+1)k}\right){[k]}^{n-1}.

Remark 2.22
.

If, in the formula of Corollary
2.21
, we allow the limit to be taken inside the sum and also permit the manipulation of divergent series, we obtain, for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
:

B_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}-nk^{n-1}=-n\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}k^{n-1}=-n\zeta(1-n).

This provides a well-known and valid formula for the classical Bernoulli numbers, where
\zeta
denotes the Riemann zeta function.

2.2.3

Expression of the
\beta_{n}^{(r)}
’s in terms of
q
-Stirling numbers of the second kind

The following theorem provides a generalization of Formula (
1.10
) of Carlitz, which express the Carlitz
q
-Bernoulli numbers in terms of
q
-Stirling numbers of the second kind.

Theorem 2.23
.

For all
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we have

\beta_{n}^{(r)}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\varphi_{r}(q,k)S_{q}(n,k),

where

\displaystyle\varphi_{r}(q,k)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}[k]!\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}q^{ik}\frac{{[r-1]}_{i}}{{[k+r]}_{i+1}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}[k]![r-1]!q^{-r+1}\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\frac{(q-1)^{i}q^{\frac{1}{2}i(i+1)-i(r-1)}}{[r-1-i]!}\frac{1}{[k+i+1]}

(for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
).

The proof of this theorem needs some preparations which are presented below. We begin with the following proposition:

Proposition 2.24
.

For all
r,N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we have

\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}q^{ir}{[i]}_{k}=\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{r-1}q^{(r-\ell-1)N+(\ell+1)k}\frac{{[r-1]}_{\ell}}{{[k+r]}_{\ell+1}}\right){[N]}_{k+1}.

Proof.

Let
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
be fixed. For all
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta\left(q^{(r-1)X}{[X]}_{k+1}\right)

\displaystyle=q^{(r-1)(X+1)}{[X+1]}_{k+1}-q^{(r-1)X}{[X]}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=q^{(r-1)(X+1)}[X+1][X]\cdots[X-k+1]-q^{(r-1)X}[X][X-1]\cdots[X-k]

\displaystyle=\left(q^{(r-1)(X+1)}[X+1]-q^{(r-1)X}[X-k]\right){[X]}_{k}.

But since

\displaystyle q^{(r-1)(X+1)}[X+1]-q^{(r-1)X}[X-k]

\displaystyle=q^{(r-1)(X+1)}\frac{q^{X+1}-1}{q-1}-q^{(r-1)X}\frac{q^{X-k}-1}{q-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{r(X+1)}-q^{(r-1)(X+1)}-q^{rX-k}+q^{(r-1)X}}{q-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{rX-k}\left(q^{k+r}-1\right)-q^{(r-1)X}\left(q^{r-1}-1\right)}{q-1}

\displaystyle=q^{rX-k}[k+r]-q^{(r-1)X}[r-1],

it follows that:

\Delta\left(q^{(r-1)X}{[X]}_{k+1}\right)=\left(q^{rX-k}[k+r]-q^{(r-1)X}[r-1]\right){[X]}_{k}.

By multiplying both sides of this last equality by
\frac{q^{k}}{[k+r]}
and then summing both sides of the resulting equality from
X=0
to
N-1
, we get (after simplifying and rearranging)

\sum_{X=0}^{N-1}q^{rX}{[X]}_{k}=\frac{q^{(r-1)N+k}}{[k+r]}{[N]}_{k+1}+q^{k}\frac{[r-1]}{[k+r]}\sum_{X=0}^{N-1}q^{(r-1)X}{[X]}_{k}.

By reiterating this last formula
r
times, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{X=0}^{N-1}q^{rX}{[X]}_{k}

\displaystyle=q^{(r-1)N+k}\frac{1}{[k+r]}{[N]}_{k+1}+q^{(r-2)N+2k}\frac{[r-1]}{[k+r][k+r-1]}{[N]}_{k+1}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+q^{(r-3)N+3k}\frac{{[r-1]}_{2}}{{[k+r]}_{3}}{[N]}_{k+1}+\dots+q^{rk}\frac{{[r-1]}_{r-1}}{{[k+r]}_{r}}{[N]}_{k+1}+q^{rk}\frac{{[r-1]}_{r}}{{[k+r]}_{r}}\sum_{X=0}^{N-1}{[X]}_{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{r-1}q^{(r-\ell-1)N+(\ell+1)k}\frac{{[r-1]}_{\ell}}{{[k+r]}_{\ell+1}}\right){[N]}_{k+1}~~~~~~~~~~(\text{since }{[r-1]}_{r}=0).

The proposition is proved.
∎

In what follows, for
r,N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we set

f_{r}(q,k,N):=q^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}q^{ir}{[i]}_{k},

which is
q
-polynomial in
N
(according to Proposition
2.24
). We let also
f_{r}(q,k,X)
denote the unique
q
-polynomial interpolating
f_{r}(q,k,N)
at the positive integers
X=N
, and
g_{r}(q,k)
denote the limit:

g_{r}(q,k):=\lim_{X\to 0}\frac{1}{[X]}f_{r}(q,k,X).

A first useful expression for
g_{r}(q,k)
is directly derived from Proposition
2.24
. We have the following corollary:

Corollary 2.25
.

For all
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we have

g_{r}(q,k)=(-1)^{k}[k]!\left(\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}q^{ik}\frac{{[r-1]}_{i}}{{[k+r]}_{i+1}}\right).

Proof.

Let
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
be fixed. According to Proposition
2.24
, we have for all
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
:

f_{r}(q,k,N)=q^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{r-1}q^{(r-\ell-1)N+(\ell+1)k}\frac{{[r-1]}_{\ell}}{{[k+r]}_{\ell+1}}\right){[N]}_{k+1}.

Hence

f_{r}(q,k,X)=q^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{r-1}q^{(r-\ell-1)X+(\ell+1)k}\frac{{[r-1]}_{\ell}}{{[k+r]}_{\ell+1}}\right){[X]}_{k+1}.

Consequently, we have

\displaystyle g_{r}(q,k)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{X\to 0}\frac{1}{[X]}f_{r}(q,k,X)

\displaystyle=\lim_{X\to 0}q^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{r-1}q^{(r-\ell-1)X+(\ell+1)k}\frac{{[r-1]}_{\ell}}{{[k+r]}_{\ell+1}}\right){[X-1]}_{k}

\displaystyle=q^{\frac{k(k+1)}{2}}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{r-1}q^{\ell k}\frac{{[r-1]}_{\ell}}{{[k+r]}_{\ell+1}}\right){[-1]}_{k}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}[k]!\sum_{\ell=0}^{r-1}q^{\ell k}\frac{{[r-1]}_{\ell}}{{[k+r]}_{\ell+1}}

(since
{[-1]}_{k}=(-1)^{k}q^{-\frac{k(k+1)}{2}}[k]!
). The corollary is proved.
∎

An equally interesting formula for
g_{r}(q,k)
(
r\in{\mathbb{N}},k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
) can be derived by decomposing the
q
-rational function
\frac{q^{iX}}{{[X]}_{i+1}}
(
0\leq i\leq r-1
) into
q
-partial fractions. This decomposition is given by the following proposition:

Proposition 2.26
.

For all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we have

\frac{q^{kX}}{{[X]}_{k+1}}=\frac{1}{[k]!}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left((-1)^{k-i}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k}{i}q^{\frac{k^{2}+k}{2}+\frac{i^{2}-i}{2}}\frac{1}{[X-i]}\right).

Proof.

Let
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
be fixed. We will first establish the existence and uniqueness of real numbers
\alpha_{k,i}(q)
(
0\leq i\leq k
) such that the following holds:

\frac{q^{kX}}{{[X]}_{k+1}}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{\alpha_{k,i}(q)}{[X-i]}.

(2.25)

We have:

(
2.25
)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow q^{kX}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_{k,i}(q)\frac{{[X]}_{k+1}}{[X-i]}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow q^{kX}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_{k,i}(q)\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j\leq k\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}[X-j]\right).

(2.26)

Consider the family
\mathscr{B}
of
(k+1)

q
-polynomials of degree
k
given by:

\mathscr{B}:=\left\{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j\leq k\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}[X-j]\right\}_{0\leq i\leq k}.

It can be observed that any
k
arbitrary
q
-polynomials from
\mathscr{B}
always share a common root that is not a root of the remaining
q
-polynomial in
\mathscr{B}
. Therefore, no
q
-polynomial in
\mathscr{B}
can be expressed as a linear combination of the others, which shows that
\mathscr{B}
is a linearly independent family in
\mathscr{E}_{k}
. Consequently, since
\mathrm{Card}{\mathscr{B}}=\dim{\mathscr{E}_{k}}=k+1
,
\mathscr{B}
is a basis of
\mathscr{E}_{k}
. It follows that the
q
-polynomial
q^{kX}
(belonging to
\mathscr{E}_{k}
) can be uniquely expressed as a linear combination of the
q
-polynomials in
\mathscr{B}
. This establishes the existence and uniqueness of real numbers
\alpha_{k,i}(q)
(
0\leq i\leq k
) satisfying (
2.26
) (equivalently (
2.25
)).

Let us now determine the real numbers
\alpha_{k,i}(q)
(
0\leq i\leq k
). For a given
\ell\in\{0,1,\dots,k\}
, by multiplying both sides of (
2.25
) by
[X-\ell]
and then substituting
X=\ell
into the resulting identity, we obtain:

\frac{q^{k\ell}}{[\ell][\ell-1]\cdots[1]\cdot[-1][-2]\cdots[\ell-k]}=\alpha_{k,\ell}(q).

But since
[\ell][\ell-1]\cdots[1]=[\ell]!
and
[-1][-2]\cdots[\ell-k]=(-1)^{k-\ell}q^{-\frac{(k-\ell)(k-\ell+1)}{2}}[k-\ell]!
(using the property
[-X]=-q^{-X}[X]
), it follows that:

\displaystyle\alpha_{k,\ell}(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{k-\ell}q^{\frac{(k-\ell)(k-\ell+1)}{2}+k\ell}}{[\ell]![k-\ell]!}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{[k]!}(-1)^{k-\ell}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k}{\ell}q^{\frac{k^{2}+k}{2}+\frac{\ell^{2}-\ell}{2}},

as required. This completes the proof.
∎

Using the formula from Proposition
2.26
, the formula of Corollary
2.25
for
g_{r}(q,k)
can be transformed into another, more interesting form for studying the limiting case as
q\to 1
. We have the following proposition:

Proposition 2.27
.

For all
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we have

g_{r}(q,k)=(-1)^{k}[k]![r-1]!q^{-r+1}\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\dfrac{(q-1)^{i}q^{\frac{1}{2}i(i+1)-i(r-1)}}{[r-1-i]!}\frac{1}{[k+i+1]}.

Proof.

Let
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
be fixed. By successively applying Corollary
2.25
and Proposition
2.26
, we obtain

\displaystyle g_{r}(q,k)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}[k]!\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}q^{-ir}{[r-1]}_{i}\left\{\dfrac{q^{i(k+r)}}{{[k+r]}_{i+1}}\right\}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}[k]!\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}q^{-ir}{[r-1]}_{i}\frac{1}{[i]!}\sum_{j=0}^{i}(-1)^{i-j}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{i}{j}q^{\frac{i^{2}+i}{2}+\frac{j^{2}-j}{2}}\frac{1}{[k+r-j]}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}[k]!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{l}0\leq i\leq r-1\\
0\leq j\leq i\end{subarray}}q^{-ir}\frac{{[r-1]}_{i}}{[i]!}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{i}{j}(-1)^{i-j}q^{\frac{i^{2}+i}{2}+\frac{j^{2}-j}{2}}\frac{1}{[k+r-j]}.

By inverting the summations over
i
and
j
and noting that
\frac{{[r-1]}_{i}}{[i]!}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{i}{j}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r - 1}{j}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r - 1 - j}{i - j}
and that
q^{-ir}\cdot q^{\frac{i^{2}+i}{2}+\frac{j^{2}-j}{2}}=q^{\frac{(i-j)^{2}+(i-j)}{2}-i(r-j)}
(for all
0\leq i\leq r-1
and
0\leq j\leq i
), we derive that:

g_{r}(q,k)=(-1)^{k}[k]!\sum_{0\leq j\leq r-1}\left(\sum_{i=j}^{r-1}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r - 1 - j}{i - j}(-1)^{i-j}q^{\frac{(i-j)^{2}+(i-j)}{2}-i(r-j)}\right)\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r - 1}{j}\frac{1}{[k+r-j]}.

Then, performing the change of index
\ell=i-j
in the inner summation gives

g_{r}(q,k)=(-1)^{k}[k]!\sum_{0\leq j\leq r-1}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{r-1-j}q^{\frac{\ell(\ell-1)}{2}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r - 1 - j}{\ell}\left(-q^{1-r+j}\right)^{\ell}\right)q^{j^{2}-jr}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r - 1}{j}\frac{1}{[k+r-j]}.

But according to the Gauss binomial formula (
1.2
), we have for all
j\in\{0,1,\dots,r-1\}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{r-1-j}q^{\frac{\ell(\ell-1)}{2}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r - 1 - j}{\ell}\left(-q^{1-r+j}\right)^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\left(1-q^{1-r+j}\right)\left(1-q^{2-r+j}\right)\cdots\left(1-q^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=q^{1-r+j}\left(q^{r-1-j}-1\right)\cdot q^{2-r+j}\left(q^{r-2-j}-1\right)\cdots q^{-1}(q-1)

\displaystyle=q^{-\frac{(r-1-j)(r-j)}{2}}(q-1)^{r-1-j}[r-1-j]!.

Substituting this into the last obtained expression for
g_{r}(q,k)
, we get

g_{r}(q,k)=(-1)^{k}[k]!\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}q^{-\frac{(r-1-j)(r-j)}{2}}(q-1)^{r-1-j}[r-1-j]!q^{j^{2}-jr}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r - 1}{j}\frac{1}{[k+r-j]}.

Finally, changing the index
i=r-1-j
and rearranging yields

g_{r}(q,k)=(-1)^{k}[k]![r-1]!q^{-r+1}\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\dfrac{(q-1)^{i}q^{\frac{1}{2}i(i+1)-i(r-1)}}{[r-1-i]!}\frac{1}{[k+i+1]},

as required.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
2.23
.

Proof of Theorem
2.23
.

Let
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
be fixed. In view of Corollary
2.25
and Proposition
2.27
, we need to show that:

\beta_{n}^{(r)}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}g_{r}(q,k)S_{q}(n,k).

To achieve this, consider
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and evaluate the sum

\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}q^{ri}[i]^{n}

in two different ways. On the one hand, from Theorem
2.6
, we have

\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}q^{ri}{[i]}^{n}=q^{rN}S_{n}(N)-S_{n}(0),

where
{(S_{k}(X))}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is the Carlitz-type
q
-polynomial sequence associated to the real sequence of general term
a_{k}:=\frac{1}{q-1}\frac{1}{[k+r]}
(
\forall k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
). So, putting
s_{k}:=S_{k}(0)
(
\forall k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
) and using the formula of Theorem
2.2
for
S_{k}(X)
, we derive that:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}q^{ri}{[i]}^{n}

\displaystyle=q^{rN}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}s_{k}q^{kN}{[N]}^{n-k}-s_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k}s_{k}q^{(k+r)N}{[N]}^{n-k}+\left(q^{(n+r)N}-1\right)s_{n}.

(2.27)

On the other hand, by using Formula (
1.9
), we have that:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}q^{ri}{[i]}^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}q^{ri}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{\frac{1}{2}k(k-1)}S_{q}(n,k){[i]}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S_{q}(n,k)\left(q^{\frac{1}{2}k(k-1)}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}q^{ri}{[i]}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S_{q}(n,k)f_{r}(q,k,N).

(2.28)

Comparing (
2.27
) and (
2.28
), we derive the identity:

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k}s_{k}q^{(k+r)N}{[N]}^{n-k}+\left(q^{(n+r)N}-1\right)s_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S_{q}(n,k)f_{r}(q,k,N).

Since this identity consists of
q
-polynomials in
N
and it is valid for all
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
then
it extends as a
q
-polynomial identity. Namely, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k}s_{k}q^{(k+r)X}{[X]}^{n-k}+\left(q^{(n+r)X}-1\right)s_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S_{q}(n,k)f_{r}(q,k,X).

Dividing through by
[X]
and letting
X\to 0
, we derive

ns_{n-1}+(q-1)(n+r)s_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}g_{r}(q,k)S_{q}(n,k).

(2.29)

Now, setting for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
:

\displaystyle T_{k}(X)

\displaystyle:=kS_{k-1}(X)+(q-1)(k+r)S_{k}(X)

\displaystyle=(q-1)rS_{k}(X)+k\left(S_{k-1}(X)+(q-1)S_{k}(X)\right),

it is clear that
ns_{n-1}+(q-1)(n+r)s_{n}=T_{n}(0)
. Next, the
q
-polynomial sequence
{\left((q-1)rS_{k}(X)\right)}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is clearly Carlitz-type with associated real sequence
{\left((q-1)ra_{k}\right)}_{k}={\left(\frac{r}{[k+r]}\right)}_{k}
. Besides, according to Item 4 of Proposition
2.11
, the
q
-polynomial sequence
{\big(k\left(S_{k-1}(X)+(q-1)S_{k}(X)\right)\big)}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is also Carlitz-type with associated real sequence
{\left((q-1)ka_{k}\right)}_{k}={\left(\frac{k}{[k+r]}\right)}_{k}
. Consequently, the
q
-polynomial sequence
{\left(T_{k}(X)\right)}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is Carlitz-type with associated real sequence
{\left(\frac{r}{[k+r]}\right)}_{k}+{\left(\frac{k}{[k+r]}\right)}_{k}={\left(\frac{k+r}{[k+r]}\right)}_{k}
. Thus
{\left(T_{k}(X)\right)}_{k}
coincides with
{\left(\beta_{k}^{(r)}(X)\right)}_{k}
, that is
T_{k}(X)=\beta_{k}^{(r)}(X)
(
\forall k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
). In particular, we have
ns_{n-1}+(q-1)(n+r)s_{n}=T_{n}(0)=\beta_{n}^{(r)}(0)=\beta_{n}^{(r)}
. Substituting this into (
2.29
) yields the required formula. This completes the proof.
∎

From Theorem
2.23
, we derive the following curious corollary:

Corollary 2.28
.

For all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we have

\beta_{n}+q\beta_{n+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\frac{[k]!}{[k+2]}S_{q}(n,k).

Proof.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
be fixed. According to Formula (
2.17
), we have

\beta_{n}^{(2)}=\beta_{n}^{(1)}+(q-1)\beta_{n+1}^{(1)}=\beta_{n}+(q-1)\beta_{n+1},

which gives
\beta_{n+1}=\frac{\beta_{n}^{(2)}-\beta_{n}}{q-1}
, and then

\beta_{n}+q\beta_{n+1}=\beta_{n}+q\left(\frac{\beta_{n}^{(2)}-\beta_{n}}{q-1}\right)=\frac{q\beta_{n}^{(2)}-\beta_{n}^{(1)}}{q-1}.

It follows by using Theorem
2.23
that

\beta_{n}+q\beta_{n+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\dfrac{q\varphi_{2}(q,k)-\varphi_{1}(q,k)}{q-1}S_{q}(n,k).

(2.30)

Further, from the second expression of
\varphi_{r}(q,k)
in Theorem
2.23
, we have that

\varphi_{1}(q,k)=(-1)^{k}\frac{[k]!}{[k+1]}~~\text{and}~~\varphi_{2}(q,k)=(-1)^{k}[k]!q^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{[k+1]}+\frac{q-1}{[k+2]}\right).

Thus

\frac{q\varphi_{2}(q,k)-\varphi_{1}(q,k)}{q-1}=(-1)^{k}\frac{[k]!}{[k+2]}.

Substituting this into (
2.30
) yields the required formula. This achieves the proof.
∎

Letting
q\to 1
in Corollary
2.28
gives the following result on the classical Bernoulli numbers, which is very recently pointed out by the author
[
4
, Page 12, Formula (2.3)]
:

Corollary 2.29
.

For all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we have

B_{n}+B_{n+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\frac{k!}{k+2}S(n,k).

∎
