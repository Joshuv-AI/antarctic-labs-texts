Swimmer-Types of Optimum Surface-Driven Active Particles
Rafe Md Abu Zayed 1, Arezoo M. Ardekani 2, Amir Nourhani 1,3,4

Abstract
An optimal microswimmer with a given geometry has a surface velocity profile that minimizes energy dissipation for a given swimming speed. An axisymmetric swimmer can be puller-, pusher-, or neutral-type depending on the sign of the stresslet strength. We numerically investigate the type of optimal surface-driven active microswimmers using a minimum dissipation theorem for optimum microswimmers. We examine the hydrodynamic resistance and stresslet strength with nonlinear dependence on various deformation modes. The results show that microswimmers with fore-and-aft symmetry exhibit neutral-type behavior, while asymmetrical geometries exhibit pusher-, puller-type or neutral-type behavior, depending on the dominant deformation mode and the nonlinear dependence of the stresslet for an optimum microswimmer on deformation mode and amplitude.

Introduction

Microswimmers are small-scale biological or artificial self-propelled entities that navigate through their environments in a regime dominated by viscous forces, making their motion governed by low Reynolds hydrodynamics. Reciprocal motion cannot lead to net propulsion at low Reynolds numbers, due to the reversibility of governing equations as highlighted in Purcell’s scallop theorem
(
Purcell, 1977
)
. As a result, the movement of biological microswimmers at this scale, such as bacteria and spermatozoa, often relies on non-reciprocal dynamics of flagella or cilia
(
Lauga, 2016
;
Lauga & Powers, 2009
)
. Artificial microswimmers, inspired by these biological systems, similarly exploit non-reciprocal dynamics via surface activity
(
Paxton et al., 2004
;
Cordova-Figueroa et al., 2013
)
, such as in self-phoretic swimmers
(
Nourhani, 2024
;
Lammert et al., 2016
;
Yariv, 2011
;
Golestanian et al., 2007
)
.

The self-propulsion of a microswimmer is force-free and torque-free. Thus, the leading-order flow field generated by axisymmetric particle motion has the symmetry of a force dipole and is characterized by a second-rank symmetric stresslet tensor
(
Batchelor, 1970
)
. The sign of the stresslet strength determines the swimmer type: positive values correspond to pullers (similar to
C. reinhardtii
and the biflagellated algae
Chlamydomonas
), where the thrust is at the front and the resistive drag by the fluid is at the back. In contrast, negative values of the stresslet strength represent pushers with rear thrust (similar to
E. coli
and
B. subtilis
)
(
Spagnolie, 2015
;
Saintillan & Shelley, 2015
;
Wang & Ardekani, 2012
)
. A zero value corresponds to neutral swimmers, like Volvox, neither push nor pull fluid in either direction
(
Drescher et al., 2009
;
Pedley et al., 2016
)
. Understanding these classifications is crucial for predicting how different swimmers interact with their environments, including interactions with other swimmers, boundaries, or obstacles
(
Spagnolie, 2015
;
Doostmohammadi et al., 2012
)
.

The energetic efficiency of microswimmers is a topic of ongoing research. The propulsion of microswimmers arises from the interplay between thrust generation and the hydrodynamics of the particle’s interaction with its viscous environment, which results in energy dissipation. For spherical swimmers, it is well-established that neutral swimmers exhibit minimal viscous dissipation and are considered energetically optimal
(
Michelin & Lauga, 2010
)
. However, deviations from perfect spherical symmetry complicate this understanding, as more complex shapes can alter the balance between thrust generation and energy dissipation. Recent studies, including those using boundary element methods, have demonstrated that non-spherical swimmers may exhibit more efficient propulsion as pushers or pullers
(
Guo et al., 2010
)
. A minimum dissipation theorem for surface-driven active microswimmers sets a lower bound on the energy dissipation rate for rigid, surface-driven microswimmers in the regime of low Reynolds number hydrodynamics
(
Nasouri et al., 2021
)
. Using this theorem, Daddi-Moussa-Ider
et al.

(
Daddi-Moussa-Ider et al., 2021
)
demonstrated that for axisymmetric slightly deformed spheres, only the sign of the third Legendre mode of the geometry determines the type of the optimum axisymmetric swimmer for a given geometry.

Our study builds on these foundations by numerically exploring how the shape of a microswimmer influences its optimal propulsion type using the spectral formalism for Stokes flow
(
Nabil et al., 2022
)
. We show that deviations from sphericity beyond slight deformations involve additional Legendre modes of the geometry in determining the optimum swimmer type. The sign of the third Legendre mode of shape deformation plays a leading role in determining whether a swimmer type is a pusher, puller, or neutral in most of the geometrical parameter space. However, the phase diagram reveals a domain where, for a given sign of the third mode contribution, the optimal swimmer’s behavior can vary. In this domain, the swimmer can be a puller, pusher, or neutral depending on the extent of the deformation. Moreover, our analysis shows that the optimum swimmer type of axisymmetric particles with fore-and-aft symmetry is neutral. The proposed approach provides a theoretical framework for designing artificial microswimmers, allowing for the prediction and optimization of swimmer behavior in various fluid environments.

Optimal microswimmers

We study the motion of an axisymmetric particle with unit speed along the symmetry axis
\hat{\bf e}
in a fluid of viscosity
\mu
under the prescribed surface boundary condition ‘BC.’ The spherical coordinate system
(r,\theta,\phi)
is used to describe the system. Under the no-slip boundary condition, ‘NS’, the velocity field over the particle surface
{\bm{r}}_{\!S}
is

\bm{v}_{\text{NS}}({\bm{r}}_{\!S})=\hat{\bf e}.

(1)

The flow field due to the perfect-slip boundary condition, ‘PS’, satisfies

\displaystyle\text{Impermeability condition}:\ \ \hat{\bf n}\cdot\bm{v}_{\text{PS}}({\bm{r}}_{\!S})=\hat{\bf n}\cdot\hat{\bf e},\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad

(2a)

\displaystyle\text{Zero tangential stress}:\qquad\ \ \hat{\bf n}\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{\text{PS}}({\bm{r}}_{\!S})\cdot\hat{\bf t}\equiv 2\mu\,\hat{\bf n}\cdot\bm{E}_{\text{PS}}({\bm{r}}_{\!S})\cdot\hat{\bf t}=0,

(2b)

where the stress tensor

\bm{\sigma}=-{p}\bm{I}+2\mu\bm{E}

and the strain tensor

\bm{E}=\frac{1}{2}\left[(\bm{\nabla}{\bm{u}})+(\bm{\nabla}{\bm{u}})^{T}\right]

are evaluated over the particle surface,
\hat{\bf n}
and
\hat{\bf t}=\hat{\bf e}_{\phi}\times\hat{\bf n}
are the normal and tangent unit vectors on the axisymmetric particle surface, and
p
is the pressure field.

The minimum dissipation theorem
(
Nasouri et al., 2021
)
shows that the velocity field
\bm{v}_{\text{OM}}
around an
o
ptimum
m
icroswimmer, ‘OM’, moving with unit velocity along its symmetry axis, can be expressed as a linear combination of the velocity fields due to the motion of a passive particle of the same geometry moving with the same velocity under no-slip
\bm{v}_{\text{NS}}
and perfect-slip
\bm{v}_{\text{PS}}
boundary conditions

\bm{v}_{\text{OM}}({\bm{r}})=\left(\frac{R_{\text{NS}}}{R_{\text{NS}}-R_{\text{PS}}}\right)\bm{v}_{\text{PS}}({\bm{r}})-\left(\frac{R_{\text{PS}}}{R_{\text{NS}}-R_{\text{PS}}}\right)\bm{v}_{\text{NS}}({\bm{r}}),

(3)

where the resistance coefficient
R_{\text{BC}}
is numerically equal to the force
F_{\text{BC}}=\hat{\bf e}\cdot{\bm{F}}_{\text{BC}}
exerted by the particle with boundary condition BC = NS, PS on the fluid along
\hat{\bf e}
.

The multipole expansion of the flow field around a particle
\bm{v}_{\text{BC}}=\bm{v}_{\text{BC}}^{\text{pf}}+\bm{v}_{\text{BC}}^{\text{fd}}+\mathcal{O}(r^{-3})
can be written in terms of increasing powers of
r^{-1}
, where the first two terms exhibit the symmetries of a
p
oint
f
orce and a
f
orce-
d
ipole, respectively. The axisymmetric flow with force-dipole symmetry is characterized by a trace-free, second-rank stresslet tensor

\bm{S}_{\text{BC}}=S_{\text{BC}}\left(\hat{\bf e}\hat{\bf e}-\frac{1}{3}\bm{I}\right),

(4)

where
S_{\text{BC}}
is the strength of the stresslet, and the resulting flow field
\bm{v}_{\text{BC}}
is

{\bm{v}}_{\text{BC}}(r,\theta)=\frac{-3}{8\pi\mu}\frac{1}{r^{2}}(\hat{\bf e}_{r}\cdot\bm{S}_{\text{BC}}\cdot\hat{\bf e}_{r})\hat{\bf e}_{r}=\left(\frac{-S_{\text{BC}}}{4\pi\mu}\right)\frac{1}{r^{2}}\text{P}_{2}(\cos\theta)\,\hat{\bf e}_{r}.

(5)

Here,
\hat{\bf e}_{r}={\bm{r}}/r
and
\text{P}_{\ell}
is the Legendre polynomial of degree
\ell
. Using the equations for the velocity field around an optimum active particle (
3
), and the linearity of Stokes flow, we have the stresslet strength for the optimum microswimmer moving with unit velocity
\hat{\bf e}
along its symmetry axis,

\displaystyle S_{\text{OM}}

\displaystyle=\frac{R_{\text{NS}}S_{\text{PS}}-R_{\text{PS}}S_{\text{NS}}}{R_{\text{NS}}-R_{\text{PS}}}.

(6)

Positive, negative, and zero values of
S_{\text{OM}}
correspond to puller, pusher, and neutral swimming type, respectively.
Daddi-Moussa-Ider
et al.

(
Daddi-Moussa-Ider et al., 2021
)
studied the effect of geometry on the swimming mode of optimal swimmers represented by slightly deformed spheres with

r_{\!S}(\theta)=r_{0}[1+\delta\xi(\theta)],\qquad\xi(\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{n}\text{P}_{n}(\cos\theta),

(7)

where
\delta
is the deformation amplitute and the deformation function
\xi
is expanded in terms of Legendre polynomials. They showed that for slight deformations to the linear order in the deformation amplitude, only mode
n=3
of the Legendre polynomials contributes to the stresslet given by Eq. (
6
),

S_{\text{OM}}=\left[8\pi\!\left(\frac{27}{14}\right)\!\mu r_{0}^{2}V_{\!\text{A}}\right]\gamma_{3}\,\delta+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}).

(8)

Thus, optimal swimmers with spherical or slightly deformed spherical geometries, where
\gamma_{3}=0
, are neutral, and the swimming mode of other slightly deformed spheres depends on the sign of
\gamma_{3}
.

In our study, we numerically evaluate the hydrodynamic resistance and stresslet beyond the linear term in
\delta
using the spectral method
(
Nabil et al., 2022
)
and show that optimal microswimmers with fore-and-aft symmetry, corresponding to individual or combinations of even
n
geometry modes, are neutral. For individual odd values,
n=3
has the highest contribution, and the stresslet strength due to
n=5
and
n=7
is one and two orders of magnitude smaller than that of
n=3
, respectively. Their swimming modes are opposite to that of
n=3
. We also evaluate a range of geometries with non-zero
\gamma_{3}
and
\gamma_{5}
to study the phase diagram of their optimum swimmer types and neutral behavior with non-zero
\gamma_{3}
.

3
Calculating Force and Stresslet Using Spectral Method

The flow field around a particle moving at low Reynolds number in a fluid of viscosity
\mu
satisfies the Stokes and continuity equations,

\mu\nabla^{2}\bm{u}=\bm{\nabla}{p},\qquad\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{u}=0,

(9)

respectively.
The pressure field satisfies
\nabla^{2}{p}=0
and is either a harmonic function or constant. Imposing homogeneity in the radial coordinate, we have Mode-1 biharmonic velocity fields (
\ell\geq 1
) and Mode-2 harmonic velocity fields (
\ell\geq 0
),

\displaystyle\text{Mode-1}:\ \nabla^{2}\nabla^{2}{\bm{u}}_{\ell}^{[1]}=0\quad{\bm{u}}_{\ell}^{[1]}({\bm{r}})=\left(\frac{r_{0}}{r}\right)^{\ell}\!{\bm{u}}_{\ell}^{[1]}({\bm{r}}_{0}),\quad\quad\ {p}_{\ell}^{[1]}\propto r^{-(\ell+1)}\text{P}_{\ell}(\cos\theta)

\displaystyle\text{Mode-2}:\ \nabla^{2}{\bm{u}}_{\ell}^{[2]}=0\quad\quad{\bm{u}}_{\ell}^{[2]}({\bm{r}})=\left(\frac{r_{0}}{r}\right)^{\ell+2}\!{\bm{u}}_{\ell}^{[2]}({\bm{r}}_{0}),\ \ \quad{p}_{\ell}^{[2]}=\text{constant}

(10)

where
r_{0}
is the radius of an arbitrary reference sphere
\mathbb{S}_{0}
, co-centered with the particle, and
{\bm{u}}_{\ell}^{[\beta]}({\bm{r}}_{0})
’s are
\theta
-dependent vectorial basis functions defined over
\mathbb{S}_{0}
. The explicit forms of these modes over the reference sphere are
(
Nabil et al., 2022
)
:

{\bm{u}}_{\ell}^{[1]}({\bm{r}}_{0})=[\ell(\ell+1){\bf P}^{[1]}_{\ell}-(\ell-2){\bf P}^{[2]}_{\ell}]
and
{\bm{u}}_{\ell}^{[2]}({\bm{r}}_{0})=[-(\ell+1){\bf P}^{[1]}_{\ell}+{\bf P}^{[2]}_{\ell}]
where
{\bf P}^{[1]}_{\ell}=\text{P}_{\ell}(\cos\theta)\hat{\bm{e}}_{r}
and
{\bf P}^{[1]}_{\ell}=\partial_{\theta}\text{P}_{\ell}(\cos\theta)\hat{\bm{e}}_{\theta}
are vectorial basis functions.

We can expand the velocity field
{\bm{u}}({\bm{r}}_{0})
over
\mathbb{S}_{0}
in terms of
{\bm{u}}_{\ell}^{[\beta]}({\bm{r}}_{0})
. To project onto individual modes, for two vectorial functions
\bm{H}
and
\bm{G}
over
\mathbb{S}_{0}
, we define

\langle\bm{H}|\bm{G}\rangle=\int_{0}^{\pi}\bm{H}(\theta)\cdot\bm{G}(\theta)\,\sin\theta\,d\theta=(4\pi r_{0}^{2})^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{0}}\bm{H}(\theta)\cdot\bm{G}(\theta)\,dS

(11)

as the inner product.
Moreover, since the Stokes modes (
10
) are not orthogonal over the reference sphere,

\langle{\bm{u}}_{\ell}^{[1]}({\bm{r}}_{0})|{\bm{u}}_{\ell}^{[2]}({\bm{r}}_{0})\rangle\neq 0
, we define their corresponding dual vectors

\displaystyle{\bm{D}}_{\ell}^{[1]}(\cos\theta)=\frac{2\ell+1}{4\ell(\ell+1)}\left[\ell{\bf P}^{[1]}_{\ell}(\cos\theta)+{\bf P}^{[2]}_{\ell}(\cos\theta)\right]

(12)

\displaystyle{\bm{D}}_{\ell}^{[2]}(\cos\theta)=\frac{2\ell+1}{4(\ell+1)}\left[(\ell-2){\bf P}^{[1]}_{\ell}(\cos\theta)+{\bf P}^{[2]}_{\ell}(\cos\theta)\right]

(13)

that satisfy

\left\langle{\bm{D}}_{\ell_{1}}^{[\beta_{1}]}\middle|{\bm{u}}_{\ell_{2}}^{[\beta_{2}]}({\bm{r}}_{0})\right\rangle=\delta_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}\delta_{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}}

where the Kronecker delta
\delta_{ij}
equals 1 if
i=j
and 0 otherwise.

Knowing the velocity field
{\bm{u}}({\bm{r}}_{0})
over the surface of the reference sphere
{\mathbb{S}_{0}}
, we can write the velocity field in its spectral expansion

{\bm{u}}({\bm{r}})=\sum_{\alpha,\ell}\left\langle\bm{D}_{\ell}^{[\beta]}\middle|{\bm{u}}({\bm{r}}_{0})\right\rangle{\bm{u}}_{\ell}^{[\beta]}({\bm{r}}).

(14)

Due to the linearity of Stokes flow and the properties of a Newtonian fluid, a similar spectral expansion with the same expansion coefficients can be written for pressure, strain tensor, and stress tensor.
An axisymmetric particle moving with unit velocity
\hat{\bf e}
under boundary condition BC exerts a force on the fluid, given by,

F_{\text{BC}}=8\,\pi\mu r_{0}\left\langle\bm{D}_{1}^{[1]}\middle|\bm{v}_{\text{BC}}({\bm{r}}_{0})\right\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle N}}{{=}}R_{\text{BC}}

(15)

where ‘
N
’ atop the equals sign serves as a reminder that the resistance coefficient
R_{\text{BC}}
is numerically equal to the magnitude of the force exerted on the fluid by a particle moving with unit velocity. We will use expression (
15
) in Eq. (
6
) for no-slip and perfect-slip boundary conditions.
Since the velocity field due to the stresslet, according to Eq. (
5
), scales as
r^{-2}
, and the particle is rigid with no fluid source, for
\ell\geq 1
, the only Stokes mode that gives a
\sim r^{-2}
dependence is Mode-1 with
\ell=2
. Therefore, the velocity field contribution with the symmetry of a force dipole for a particle moving with unit velocity
\hat{\bf e}
under boundary condition BC is

\left\langle\bm{D}_{2}^{[1]}\middle|\bm{v}_{\text{BC}}({\bm{r}}_{0})\right\rangle{\bm{u}}_{2}^{[1]}({\bm{r}})=6r_{0}^{2}\left\langle\bm{D}_{2}^{[1]}\middle|\bm{v}_{\text{BC}}({\bm{r}}_{0})\right\rangle\,\frac{1}{r^{2}}\text{P}_{2}(\cos\theta)\,\hat{\bm{e}}_{r}.

(16)

Comparing this expression with the flow field (
5
) gives the stresslet strength,

S_{\text{BC}}=-24\,\pi\mu r_{0}^{2}\left\langle\bm{D}_{2}^{[1]}\middle|\bm{v}_{\text{BC}}({\bm{r}}_{0})\right\rangle.

(17)

After using the expression for the force exerted on the fluid (
15
) and the stresslet strength (
17
),
we can rewrite the stresslet strength (
6
) for the optimum microswimmer moving with unit velocity
\hat{\bf e}
along its symmetry axis,

\displaystyle S_{\text{OM}}

\displaystyle=-24\,\pi\mu r_{0}^{2}\left[\frac{\left\langle\bm{D}_{1}^{[1]}\middle|{\bm{v}}_{\text{NS}}({\bm{r}}_{0})\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{D}_{2}^{[1]}\middle|{\bm{v}}_{\text{PS}}({\bm{r}}_{0})\right\rangle-\left\langle\bm{D}_{1}^{[1]}\middle|{\bm{v}}_{\text{PS}}({\bm{r}}_{0})\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{D}_{2}^{[1]}\middle|{\bm{v}}_{\text{NS}}({\bm{r}}_{0})\right\rangle}{\left\langle\bm{D}_{1}^{[1]}\middle|{\bm{v}}_{\text{NS}}({\bm{r}}_{0})\right\rangle-\left\langle\bm{D}_{1}^{[1]}\middle|{\bm{v}}_{\text{PS}}({\bm{r}}_{0})\right\rangle}\right]

(18)

which can be calculated using the
\ell=1,2
coefficients of Mode-1 terms in the spectral expansion (
14
) for flow fields with no-slip (
1
) and perfect-slip (
2
) boundary conditions, as elaborated below.

To calculate the unknown expansion coefficients
\langle\bm{D}_{\ell}^{[\beta]}|\bm{v}_{\text{NS}}({\bm{r}}_{0})\rangle
for an axisymmetric particle with surface
\mathbb{S}
, which for a non-spherical particle is different from
\mathbb{S}_{0}
, we apply the no-slip boundary condition (
1
) over
\mathbb{S}
to the spectral expansion (
14
) and take the inner product with the dual vectors, yielding

\left\langle\bm{D}_{\ell_{1}}^{[\beta_{1}]}\middle|\bm{v}_{\text{NS}}({\bm{r}}_{\!S})\right\rangle=\sum_{\beta_{2},\ell_{2}}\left\langle\bm{D}_{\ell_{1}}^{[\beta_{1}]}\middle|{\bm{u}}_{\ell_{2}}^{[\beta_{2}]}({\bm{r}}_{\!S})\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{D}_{\ell_{2}}^{[\beta_{2}]}\middle|\bm{v}_{\text{NS}}({\bm{r}}_{0})\right\rangle.

(19)

Here, except for the expansion coefficients, the rest of the terms are known.

For the perfect-slip scenario, both sides of Eqns. (
2
) are scalar functions of
\theta
. We define the inner product

\langle h|g\rangle=\int_{0}^{\pi}h(\theta)\,g(\theta)\,\sin\theta\,d\theta

\displaystyle\left\langle\text{P}_{\ell_{1}}\middle|\hat{\bf n}\cdot\bm{v}_{\text{PS}}({\bm{r}}_{\!S})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta_{2},\ell_{2}}\left\langle\text{P}_{\ell_{1}}\middle|\hat{\bf n}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{\ell_{2}}^{[\beta_{2}]}({\bm{r}}_{\!S})\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{D}_{\ell_{2}}^{[\beta_{2}]}\middle|\bm{v}_{\text{PS}}({\bm{r}}_{0})\right\rangle

(20a)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta_{2},\ell_{2}}\left\langle\text{P}_{\ell_{1}}\middle|\hat{\bf n}\cdot\bm{E}_{\ell_{2}}^{[\beta_{2}]}({\bm{r}}_{\!S})\cdot\hat{\bf t}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{D}_{\ell_{2}}^{[\beta_{2}]}\middle|\bm{v}_{\text{PS}}({\bm{r}}_{0})\right\rangle,

(20b)

where the strain tensor for Stokes modes is

\bm{E}_{\ell}^{[\beta]}=\frac{1}{2}[(\bm{\nabla}{\bm{u}}_{\ell}^{[\beta]})+(\bm{\nabla}{\bm{u}}_{\ell}^{[\beta]})^{T}]

Results and Discussion

For a particle with fore-and-aft symmetry,
\bm{D}_{2}^{[1]}\cdot\bm{v}_{\text{BC}}({\bm{r}}_{0})
is odd with respect to
\cos\theta

S_{\text{BC}}\propto\left\langle\bm{D}_{2}^{[1]}\middle|\bm{v}_{\text{BC}}({\bm{r}}_{0})\right\rangle=0
. Consequently, the stresslet strength for any optimum swimmer with a geometry consisting of individual even geometrical modes
\gamma_{2k}
with

, or linear combinations of these even modes, is zero. Therefore, the optimum swimmer type for a particle with fore-and-aft symmetry is neutral.

For individual odd geometrical modes
n=3,5
, and 7, for which
\xi(\theta)=\text{P}_{n}(\cos\theta)
, we evaluated the force and stresslet strength for particles under no-slip (Fig.
1
(a)&(b)) and perfect-slip (Fig.
1
(c)&(d)) boundary conditions, and calculated the stresslet for the optimum swimmer (Fig.
1
(e)) using Eq. (

Next, we examine the deformation function
\xi_{35}(\theta;\alpha)
defined by

\xi_{nm}(\theta;\alpha)=\alpha\text{P}_{n}(\cos\theta)+(1-\alpha)\text{P}_{m}(\cos\theta),\qquad\alpha\in[0,1],

(21)

such that

5
Conclusion

Our study presents a numerical analysis of the swimmer types of optimum surface-driven active particles, focusing on the interplay between the geometrical modes. Utilizing the minimum dissipation theorem, we systematically evaluated the contributions of various geometrical modes to the swimming type of deformed spherical microswimmers. Our findings indicate that microswimmers with fore-and-aft symmetry exhibit neutral swimming type due to the cancellation of stresslet contributions. In contrast, asymmetrical geometries display distinct pusher- or puller-type behaviors depending on the dominant deformation mode and the nonlinear dependence of the stresslet on the deformation amplitude. The phase diagram constructed for small third Legendre mode of deformation reveals the sensitivity of the optimum swimmer type to geometric perturbations, providing insight into the transition between pusher and puller behavior. These results contribute to a deeper understanding of the intricate relationship between geometry and swimming efficiency in active microswimmers, which could inform the design of artificial microswimmers for practical applications.
