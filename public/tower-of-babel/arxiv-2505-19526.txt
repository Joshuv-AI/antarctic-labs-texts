Robert Fraser and Kyle Hambrook and Donggeun Ryou

Abstract
. We prove the optimality of the exponent in the Mockenhaupt-Mitsis-Bak-Seeger Fourier restriction theorem in all dimensions d d and the full parameter range 0 < a , b < d 0<a,b<d . Our construction is deterministic and also yields Salem sets.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Main Result

This article concerns the optimality of
the constant
p_{*}(a,b,d)
in
the following general Fourier restriction theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(Mockenhaupt-Mitsis-Bak-Seeger)
.

Let
0<a,b<d
, with
d
an integer.
Define
p_{*}(a,b,d)=(4d-4a+2b)/b.

Suppose
\mu
is a Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the following properties:

(1)

\mu(B(x,r))\lesssim r^{a}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and all
r>0
.

(2)

|\widehat{\mu}(\xi)|\lesssim(1+|\xi|)^{-b/2}
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Then, for each
p\geq p_{*}(a,b,d)
, the following holds:

(18)

\|\widehat{f\mu}\|_{L^{p}}\lesssim_{p}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}
for all
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
.

The notation is explained in Subsection
1.3
. The history of this theorem and of the question of the optimality of the constant
p_{*}(a,b,d)
is deferred to Section
2
.

The main result of this article is that the constant
p_{*}(a,b,d)
in Theorem
1.1
is best possible for all values of
0<a,b<d
.

We prove our main result by constructing a measure
on a set of vectors that are well-approximable by elements of lattices arising from algebraic number theory.

Let
K
be a number field (i.e., a finite extension field of
\mathbb{Q}
) of degree
d
.
Let
\mathcal{O}_{K}
be the ring of integers of
K
.
For each ideal
I\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}
, let
N(I)
denote the norm of
I
and
I^{-1}
denote the fractional ideal which is the inverse of
I
.
Let
B=\left\{{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{d}}\right\}
be an integral basis for
K
(i.e., a basis for
K
as a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space which is also a basis for
\mathcal{O}_{K}
as a
\mathbb{Z}
-module). We identify
\mathbb{Q}^{d}
with
K
by identifying

q=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n})\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
with

q=\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{i}\omega_{i}\in K
.
Since
B
is an integral basis,
this also identifies
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
Moreover, if
I\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}
is an integral ideal,
I^{-1}
is identified with a lattice containing
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
This identification also extends to lengths and distances:
If
q=\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{i}\omega_{i}\in K

and
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
then

|q|=\left({\sum_{i=1}^{d}|q_{i}|^{2}}\right)^{1/2}

and
|q-x|=\left({\sum_{i=1}^{d}|q_{i}-x_{i}|^{2}}\right)^{1/2}
.

Let
\tau>1
. Define

E(K,B,\tau)
to be the set of all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\dist(x,I^{-1})\leq|N(I)|^{-(\tau+1)/d}

for infinitely many ideals
I
of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
Here

\dist(x,I^{-1})=\min\left\{{|x-t|:t\in I^{-1}}\right\}.

Theorem 1.2

(Main Theorem)
.

Let
1\leq q<\infty
.
Let
-d<\rho<d

and define

\rho^{+}=\max\left\{{\rho,0}\right\}
and
\rho^{-}=\max\left\{{-\rho,0}\right\}
.
There exists a Borel probability measure

\mu
with the following properties:

(1)

\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\subseteq E(K,B,\tau)
;

(2)

\mu
satisfies (A) for each exponent
a<\frac{2d-\rho^{-}}{1+\tau}
;

(3)

\mu
satisfies (B) for each exponent
b<\frac{2(d-\rho^{+})}{1+\tau}
;

(4)

There exists a sequence of functions
f_{k}\in L^{q}(\mu)
such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\|\widehat{f_{k}\mu}\|_{L^{p}}}{\|f_{k}\|_{L^{q}(\mu)}}=\infty

whenever
p<p(\tau,\rho,q,d):=\frac{q(d\tau-\rho)}{(q-1)(d-\rho^{+})}
.

Subsection
2.3
describes the main ideas and novel features of the proof of this theorem.
The formal proof of
the theorem
constitutes Sections
3
to
8
.

Theorem
1.2
and the following remark
show that, for any integer
d
and any real numbers
a
and
b
satisfying
0<a,b<d
, the exponent
p_{*}(a,b,d)
in the Mockenhaupt-Mitsis-Bak-Seeger restriction theorem cannot be improved.

Remark 1.3
.

We may assume
b\leq 2a
. Indeed, Mitsis
[
24
, Proposition 3.1]

showed that if (A) and (R) hold, then

p\geq 2d/a
.
By combining this with Theorem
1.1
, we see that (A) and (B) imply
b\leq 2a
.
Let
p_{0}<p_{*}(a,b,d)
. Choose
a_{0}
and
b_{0}
such that
0<a<a_{0}<d
,
0<b<b_{0}<d
,
b_{0}\leq 2a_{0}
, and
p_{0}<p_{*}(a_{0},b_{0},d)<p_{*}(a,b,d)
.
If
b_{0}\leq a_{0}
, choose
\tau=\frac{2d}{a_{0}}-1
and
\rho=d\left(1-\frac{b_{0}}{a_{0}}\right)
.
If
a_{0}\leq b_{0}\leq 2a_{0}
, choose
\tau=\frac{2d}{b_{0}}-1
and
\rho=2d\left(\frac{a_{0}}{b_{0}}-1\right)
.
In both cases,
a_{0}=\frac{2d-\rho^{-}}{1+\tau}
,
b_{0}=\frac{2(d-\rho^{+})}{1+\tau}
, and

p_{*}(a_{0},b_{0},d)=p(\tau,\rho,2,d)
.
Then Theorem
1.2
gives a measure
\mu
satisfying (A) and (B) but failing (R) for
p=p_{0}
(and every other
p<p_{*}(a_{0},b_{0},d)
).

1.2.
Deterministic Salem Sets and a Variant of
E(K,B,\tau)
.

A set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called a Salem set if it supports a Borel measure
\mu
that satisfies (B) for every value of
b
less than the Hausdorff dimension of the set.
There are many random constructions of Salem sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(see
[
28
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
21
]
,
[
29
]
).
The first deterministic Salem sets in
\mathbb{R}
of arbitrary dimension were found by
Kaufman
[
19
]
, who proved

E(\tau)=\left\{{x\in\mathbb{R}:|x-r/q|\leq|q|^{-(1+\tau)}\text{ for infinitely many }(q,r)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}}\right\}

is a Salem set of dimension
2/(1+\tau)
when
\tau>1
.

Fraser and Hambrook
[
9
]

extended Kaufman’s result to
\mathbb{R}^{d}

by showing the set

E_{\mathrm{prin}}(K,B,\tau)=\left\{{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x-r/q|\leq|q|^{-(\tau+1)}\text{ for infinitely many }(q,r)\in\mathcal{O}_{K}\times\mathcal{O}_{K}}\right\}

is a Salem set of dimension
2d/(1+\tau)
when
\tau>1
.

The set
E_{\mathrm{prin}}(K,B,\tau)
is a variant of
E(K,B,\tau)
defined in terms of
elements
rather than
ideals
.
In the present article, we primarily considered
E(K,B,\tau)
rather than
E_{\mathrm{prin}}(K,B,\tau)
because working with
ideals
rather than
elements
simplifies many parts of the argument. However, the two sets have many of the same properties, as the next two results illustrate.

By Frostman’s lemma, the exponent
b
in (B) can never exceed the Hausdorff dimension of the support of
\mu
(see, e.g.,
[
23
, Section 3.6]
).
Thus, Theorem
1.2
combined with Proposition
10.1
(which gives an upper bound on the Hausdorff dimension of
E(K,B,\tau)
)
implies

Corollary 1.4
.

E(K,B,\tau)
is a Salem set of dimension
2d/(1+\tau)
for every
\tau>1
.

By modifying the proof of Theorem
1.2
as described in Section
11
, we can prove

Theorem 1.5
.

The statement of Theorem
1.2
also holds for
E_{\mathrm{prin}}(K,B,\tau)
.

1.3.
Notation

For a measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Fourier transform of
\nu
is

\widehat{\nu}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\xi}d\nu(x)\quad\text{for all $\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

We make the following exception to
this notation: If
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
is
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-periodic,

\widehat{F}(s)=\int_{[0,1)^{d}}e^{-2\pi ix\cdot s}F(x)dx\quad\text{for all $s\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}$}.

For a measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
L^{q}(\nu)
is the space of all measurable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\int|f|^{q}d\nu<\infty
, with functions that are equal
\nu
-a.e. identified.
If
\nu
is Lebesgue measure, we write
L^{q}
instead of
L^{q}(\nu)
.

We use
B(x,r)
to denote the closed ball with center
x
and radius
r
in the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

For any set
A
, the indicator function of
A
is the function
\mathbf{1}_{A}
defined by
\mathbf{1}_{A}(x)=1
if
x\in A
and
\mathbf{1}_{A}(x)=0
otherwise.

A statement of the form “
F(u)\lesssim G(u)
for all
u
” means “there exists a constant
C>0
such that
F(u)\leq CG(u)
for all
u
.” If the implied constant
C
depends on a relevant parameter, say
p
, we write
\lesssim_{p}
instead of
\lesssim
.
Note also

F(u)\gtrsim G(u)
means
G(u)\lesssim F(u)
,
and

F(u)\sim G(u)
means
F(u)\lesssim G(u)
and
F(u)\gtrsim G(u)
.

2.
Motivation and Discussion

2.1.
Restriction Theorems

The problem of restriction of the Fourier transform to small sets is an interesting one with a long history (which we describe only briefly). Because the Fourier transform is an isometry on
L^{2}
, there is no sensible way to restrict the Fourier transform of an arbitrary
L^{2}
function to a set of Lebesgue measure zero.
On the other hand, because the Fourier transform of a function in
L^{1}
is continuous,
the Fourier transform of an
L^{1}
function can be sensibly restricted to any measurable subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The area of
Fourier restriction
concerns ways of restricting the Fourier transform of
functions in spaces between
L^{1}
and
L^{2}

to certain sets of Lebesgue measure zero.

We say that the measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies a
Fourier extension
estimate with exponents
p
and
q
if

(2.1)

\left\|\widehat{f\mu}\right\|_{L^{p}}\lesssim_{p,q}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{q}(\mu)}\text{ for all $f\in L^{q}(\mu)$.}

It can be seen using a duality argument (see, e.g.,
[
23
]
) that the estimate (
2.1
) implies that the Fourier transform of functions in
L^{p^{\prime}}
can be meaningfully restricted to the support of the measure
\mu
. More precisely, (
2.1
) is equivalent to the statement that the Fourier transform map
f\mapsto\widehat{f}
is a bounded linear map from
L^{p^{\prime}}
to
L^{q^{\prime}}(\mu)
.
Here
p^{\prime}
and
q^{\prime}
are the conjugate exponents to
p
and
q
, respectively.

The foundational result in Fourier restriction theory is the Stein-Tomas theorem
[
31
]
,
[
32
]
, which says that (
2.1
) holds for
q=2
and
p\geq 2(d+1)/(d-1)
when
\mu
is the surface measure on a sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Notable generalizations of the Stein-Tomas theorem to the surface measure on quadratic hypersurfaces and arbitrary smooth submanifolds in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
were obtained, respectively, by Strichartz
[
30
]
and Greenleaf
[
11
]
.

All of the restriction theorems mentioned in the precious paragraph are special cases of
Theorem
1.1
, the
Mockenhaupt-Mitsis-Bak-Seeger restriction theorem
.
Mockenhaupt
[
25
]
and Mitsis
[
24
]
independently proved Theorem
1.1
with
p>p_{*}(a,b,d)
;
the endpoint case
p=p_{*}(a,b,d)
was established by Bak and Seeger
[
1
]
.

Condition (A) in Theorem
1.1
is a well-known condition in geometric measure theory sometimes called the Frostman condition or ball condition.
Mitsis
[
24
]
proved that condition (B) in Theorem
1.1
implies condition (A) with
a=b/2
.
This makes Theorem
1.1
quite remarkable — it allows one use the pointwise estimate (B) on
\widehat{\mu}
to conclude an
L^{p}
decay estimate (R) on
\widehat{f\mu}
for all functions
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
.

2.2.
Sharpness of Theorem
1.1
.

Mockenhaupt
[
25
]
and Mitsis
[
24
]

both noted the question of whether the constant
p_{*}(a,b,d)
in Theorem
1.1
is best possible was an open problem.

When
d\geq 2
and
a=b=d-1
, the constant
p_{*}(a,b,d)
in Theorem
1.1
is known to be optimal because of an example due to Knapp (according to
[
32
]
) for the surface measure on the sphere.
The original purpose of Knapp’s example was to show that the constant
2(d+1)/(d-1)
in the Stein-Tomas theorem (mentioned in Subsection
2.1
) is best possible.
The Knapp example was later extended
to smooth hypersurfaces with nonvanishing Gaussian curvature by Strichartz
[
30
]
. See also Example 1.8 of Demeter
[
7
]
for a further generalization of Knapp’s example that shows (
2.1
) cannot hold if
\mu
is a measure on a smooth hypersurface and
p<\frac{q(d+1)}{(q-1)(d-1)}.

Hambrook and Łaba
[
13
]
proved the optimality of
p_{*}(a,b,d)
in Theorem
1.1
when
d=1
and
0<b\leq a\leq d
.
(In fact, Hambrook and Łaba
[
13
]
only claimed the case
a=b
for
a,b
of a special form.
Hambrook
[
12
]

showed how optimality for all
b\leq a
could be deduced from the result of
[
13
]
.
Chen
[
5
]
modified the construction of

[
13
]
to obtain a technically stronger result that also implied optimality for
b\leq a
.)
By combining
the ideas of Knapp’s example and
[
13
]
,
Hambrook and Łaba
[
14
]
proved the optimality of
p_{*}(a,b,d)
for
d\geq 2
and
d-1\leq b\leq a\leq d
.
Note that none of the sharpness results mentioned above covered the case
b>a
. Fraser, Hambrook, and Ryou

[
10
]

built upon the idea of
[
13
]
to
prove the optimality of
p_{*}(a,b,d)
when
d=1
in the full range
0<a,b<d
(notably, the construction is deterministic).
Li and Liu
[
22
]
independently obtained the same optimality result for
d=1
by a similar construction.
The present article
fully resolves
the problem of the optimality of
p_{*}(a,b,d)
for
all

d\geq 1
and the full range
0<a,b<d
.

Remark 2.1
.

We have considered the question of the sharpness of
p_{*}(a,b,d)
in Theorem
1.1
by fixing
a,b,d
and asking whether the statement holds with
p_{*}(a,b,d)
replaced by a smaller number. An alternative way to consider the question is to look for measures which satisfy (A), (B), and (R) for
p<p_{*}(a,b,d)
. For some results in this vein, see Shmerkin and Suomala
[
29
]
, Chen and Seeger
[

]
, Łaba and Wang
[
20
]
, and Ryou
[
27
]
.

2.3.
Key Ideas in the Proof of Theorem
1.2

The key to failure of restriction estimates in Knapp’s example is
constructive interference
.
The smoothness of hypersurfaces guarantees that a
\delta
-neighborhood of a point on the hypersurface will be contained in a
\delta\times\cdots\times\delta\times\delta^{2}
-rectangle oriented along the tangent plane to the hypersurface.
The uncertainty principle (or a direct calculation) implies that
the Fourier transform of the indicator function of this
\delta
-ball should therefore be
\gtrsim\delta^{d-1}
in a dual rectangle with side lengths
\delta^{-1}\times\cdots\times\delta^{-1}\times\delta^{-2}
.

When studying restriction problems for
fractal sets
, the easiest way to achieve constructive interference is to choose the indicator function of a neighborhood of an arithmetic progression. For simplicity, we will consider the case
d=1
. In fact, the indicator function of a
\delta
-neighborhood of an arithmetic progression with common difference
z
will have large Fourier transform near frequencies of the form
\xi=nz,n\in\mathbb{Z},|n|\leq\delta^{-1}
. This observation is used by Hambrook and Łaba
[
13
]
and a later work by Chen
[
5
]
to provide a random Cantor measure for which the exponent in (R) cannot be improved beyond
p_{*}(a,b,d)
.

In order to construct deterministc examples of fractal sets allowing for constructive interference,
it is useful to have an example of a set supporting measures satisfying (A) and (B) for
prescribed
exponents
a
and
b
that also contains long arithmetic progressions.
A natural choice for this set is the set
E(\tau)
provided by Kaufman
[
19
]
. This set is a good choice for two reasons:
first, it is easy to locate a compact subset
F
of
E(\tau)
for which fattenings of
F
contain long arithmetic progressions;
second, the limsup structure of the set
E(\tau)
allows for the insertion of arithmetic progressions of different length if necessary (it turns out to be necessary in the case where
a\neq b
).
These observations are central to the previous work of Fraser, Hambrook and Ryou
[
10
]

locating measures on
E(\tau)
for which the exponent appearing in (R) cannot be improved beyond
p_{*}(a,b,d)
.

In the same spirit, it is natural to seek measures on the set
E(K,B,\tau)
as possible counterexamples to (R) for
p<p_{*}(a,b,d)
.
First, the set
E(K,B,\tau)

supports measures satisfying (A) and (B) for prescribed
a
and
b
;
second, it is easy to locate a compact subset of
E(K,B,\tau)
is naturally concentrated around a small neighborhood of a small number of lattices; third, because
E(K,B,\tau)
is a limsup set, it is easy to insert a generalized arithmetic progression of a desired length in order to obtain a counterexample if
a\neq b
.

Kaufman
[
19
]
constructed a measure on
E(\tau)
of maximal Fourier decay. In

[
10
]
, Fraser, Hambrook, and Ryou
modified Kaufman’s construction (and Papadimitropoulos’s construction
[
26
]
) so that the measure satisfied (A) and (B) for prescribed values of
a
and
b
and failed
(R)
for
p<p_{*}(a,b,d)
. As alluded to above, inserting (and modifying the weight on) a generalized arithmetic progression is the key to establishing the failure of (R).

Hambrook and Fraser
[
9
]
constructed a measure on

E_{\mathrm{prin}}(K,B,\tau)

with maximal Fourier decay.
The proof of Theorem
1.2

modifies
the Hambrook and Fraser construction
using the ideas of

[
10
]
.
The method used to establish the necessary Fourier decay estimate in Theorem
1.2
is essentially the same as the method in
[
9
]
.
However, the set
E(K,B,\tau)
in Theorem
1.2
is defined using ideals while the set
E_{\mathrm{prin}}(K,B,\tau)
in
[
9
]
is defined using elements.
This change streamlines the argument in the present paper in some ways (less explicit reliance on the basis and a simpler proof of the divisor bound).
However, it required some additional technology (the notion of the different ideal of a number field and a more abstract exponential sum calculation).
The regularity property in Theorem
1.2
was not considered in
[
9
]
.
The argument in the present paper generalizes the argument of Papadimitropoulos
[
26
]
to the number field setting.
The key ingredient is the separation lemma (Lemma
3.4
) and its application in Section
7
.
As
[
10
]
(and in contrast to
[
26
]
) we use a Fourier-analytic argument in proof of regularity (see Step 2 in the proof of Lemma
7.5
).
The failure of the estimate (R) is proved by an argument similar to that used in
[
10
]
.
However, the argument requires more advanced ideas due to the more general setting. The role of the arithmetic progression at each scale is played by the inverse of a prime ideal.
As in
[
10
]
, a Chinese remainder theorem argument is used. However, it is slightly more exotic (though not deeper) than the Chinese remainder theorem found in a typical text on ring theory. In general, the additional abstraction required by the proof in the present leads to an overall streamlining of the proof compared to the one in
[
9
]
. While the argument is more difficult (due to its generality), it is more efficient.

3.
Algebraic Number Theory

In this section, we give the definitions and facts from algebraic number theory necessary for the proof of our main theorem. As a reference for this material, see
[
15
]
.
We also prove some lemmas that we will need.

Recall that
K
is a number field of degree
d
,
\mathcal{O}_{K}
is its ring of integers, and
B=\left\{{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{d}}\right\}
is an integral basis for
K
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{K}
denote the set of all injective homomorphisms
from
K
into
\mathbb{C}
. There are precisely
d
such homomorphisms.
For each
q\in K
, the field norm of
q
is

(3.1)

\displaystyle N(q)=\prod_{\tau\in\mathcal{E}_{K}}\tau(q).

and the field trace of
q
is

(3.2)

\displaystyle\tr(q)=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{E}_{K}}\tau(q).

The field norm is multiplicative:

N(ab)=N(a)N(b)
for all
a,b\in K
.
The field trace is additive:

\tr(a+b)=\tr(a)+\tr(b)
for all
a,b\in K
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For the integral basis
B=\left\{{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{d}}\right\}
, define

\displaystyle C_{B}=\max\left\{{\textstyle\left({\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\tau(\omega_{i})|^{2}}\right)^{1/2}:\tau\in\mathcal{E}_{K}}\right\}.

For every
q\in K
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle|N(q)|\leq C_{B}^{d}|q|^{d}.

Proof.

If
q=\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{i}\omega_{i}\in K
and
\tau\in\mathcal{E}_{K}
, then

\tau(q)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}q_{i}\tau(\omega_{i})\in\mathbb{C}
.
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality implies
|\tau(q)|\leq C_{B}|q|
, so that (
3.1
) implies (
3.3
).
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

For all
a,b\in K
,
|ab|\lesssim_{B}|a||b|
.

Proof.

Write
a=\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}\omega_{i}
,

b=\sum_{j=1}^{d}b_{j}\omega_{j}
, and
\omega_{i}\omega_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}c_{ijk}\omega_{k}
, where
a_{i},b_{j},c_{ijk}\in\mathbb{Q}
.
By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

|ab|^{2}=\sum_{k}\left|{\sum_{i}\sum_{j}a_{i}b_{j}c_{ijk}}\right|^{2}\lesssim_{B}\left({\sum_{i}|a_{i}|^{2}}\right)\left({\sum_{j}|b_{j}|^{2}}\right)=|a|^{2}|b|^{2}.

∎

An ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
is an additive subgroup
I
of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
such that
rI=\left\{{ra:a\in I}\right\}\subseteq I
for all
r\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
. A fractional ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
is a subset
J\subseteq K
of the form

J=\frac{1}{r}I=\left\{{\dfrac{1}{r}a:a\in I}\right\},

where
I
is an ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
and
r
is a non-zero element of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
(neither
I
nor
r
are uniquely determined by
J
).
Every ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
is a fractional ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, and every fractional ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
is an
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-submodule of
K
.
A fractional ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
is an ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
if and only if it is contained in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
. The principal fractional ideal generated by the element
q\in K
is
\left\langle{q}\right\rangle=q\mathcal{O}_{K}
.

The product of two fractional ideals
I
and
J
is the fractional ideal

IJ=\left\{{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{i}:a_{i}\in I,b_{i}\in J,n\in\mathbb{N}}\right\}.

The non-zero fractional ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
form a commutative group under multiplication. The multiplicative identity is
\mathcal{O}_{K}
. The inverse of a non-zero fractional ideal
J
is

J^{-1}=\left\{{a\in K:aJ\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}}\right\}.

The group is partially ordered by inclusion
:
I\subseteq J
implies
HI\subseteq HJ
for all non-zero fractional ideals
H,I,J
.
Consequently, if
I\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}\subseteq J
, then
J^{-1}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}\subseteq I^{-1}
.

A non-zero proper ideal
P
of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
is called prime if

ab\in\mathcal{O}_{K}\setminus P
whenever
a,b\in\mathcal{O}_{K}\setminus P
.
It is not generally true that every non-zero, non-unit element of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
can be uniquely factored into a product of prime elements.
However, every non-zero proper ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
can be uniquely factored
as a product of prime ideals.

If
J=\frac{1}{r}I
is a fractional ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, where
I
is an ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
and
r
is a non-zero element of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, the ideal norm of
J
is defined to be

\displaystyle N(J)=\dfrac{|\mathcal{O}_{K}/I|}{|\mathcal{O}_{K}/\left\langle{r}\right\rangle|},

which is finite whenever
J
is non-zero.
It can be shown that this definition does not depend on the choice of
I
and
r
. Moreover, the ideal norm is multiplicative:
For all fractional ideals
J_{1},J_{2}
of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
,

N(J_{1}J_{2})=N(J_{1})N(J_{2}).

Lemma 3.3
.

If
I_{2}\subseteq I_{1}
are fractional ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
,
then
N(I_{2})=N(I_{1})|I_{1}/I_{2}|
.

Proof.

Choose
r\in\mathcal{O}_{K}\setminus\left\{{0}\right\}
such that
rI_{2}\subseteq rI_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
Then

\displaystyle N(\left\langle{r}\right\rangle)N(I_{2})

\displaystyle=N(rI_{2})=|\mathcal{O}_{K}/rI_{2}|=|\mathcal{O}_{K}/rI_{1}|\cdot|rI_{1}/rI_{2}|

\displaystyle=N(rI_{1})|rI_{1}/rI_{2}|=N(rI_{1})|I_{1}/I_{2}|=N(\left\langle{r}\right\rangle)N(I_{1})|I_{1}/I_{2}|.

∎

The field norm and ideal norm are related as follows:
For all
q\in K
,

(3.4)

\displaystyle|N(q)|=N(\left\langle{q}\right\rangle).

This fact combines with Lemmas
3.1
and
3.3
to give the following separation lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
be non-zero fractional ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Let
r_{1}\in I_{1}^{-1}
and
r_{2}\in I_{2}^{-1}
with
r_{1}\neq r_{2}
. Then

C_{B}|r_{1}-r_{2}|\geq(N(I_{1}\cap I_{2}))^{-1/d}\geq(N(I_{1})N(I_{2}))^{-1/d}.

Proof.

Since
I_{1}I_{2}\subseteq I_{1}\cap I_{2}
, Lemma
3.3
implies
N(I_{1}I_{2})\geq N(I_{1}\cap I_{2})
, which gives the second inequality. Now we prove the first inequality.
We have
r_{1}(I_{1}\cap I_{2})\subseteq r_{1}I_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}

and

r_{2}(I_{1}\cap I_{2})\subseteq r_{2}I_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
Thus
\left\langle{r_{1}-r_{2}}\right\rangle(I_{1}\cap I_{2})\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Now Lemma
3.3
implies

N(\left\langle{r_{1}-r_{2}}\right\rangle)N(I_{1}\cap I_{2})\geq N(\mathcal{O}_{K})=1
.
The desired result follows from Lemma
3.1
and (
3.4
).
∎

We now define the different ideal
\delta
of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
The set

\{x\in K:\tr(xy)\in\mathbb{Z}\ \text{ for all }y\in\mathcal{O}_{K}\}

is a fractional ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
The different ideal
\delta
is the
inverse
of this fractional ideal. In other words,
\delta^{-1}
is equal to the fractional ideal above. Note that
\delta\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}\subseteq\delta^{-1}
.

Recall that the integral basis is
B=\left\{{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{d}}\right\}
. Define the
d\times d
matrix

T=[\tr(\omega_{i}\omega_{j})].

Since
(x,y)\mapsto\tr(xy)
is a non-degenerate bilinear form on
\mathbb{Q}^{d}\cong K
,
T
is invertible and
the dot product is related to the trace by

\tr(r(T^{-1}s))=r\cdot s\text{ for all $r,s\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}\cong K$}.

It follows that
T^{-1}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})=\delta^{-1}
.

For
u\in\mathbb{R}
, define

e(u)=e^{-2\pi iu}.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
I
be a non-zero ideal in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
and let
R(I)\subseteq I^{-1}
be a complete set of representatives of
I^{-1}/\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Let
s\in\mathcal{O}_{K}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{r\in R(I)}e(r\cdot s)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}N(I)&\text{if $s\in T(\delta^{-1}I)$}\\
0&\text{if $s\notin T(\delta^{-1}I)$.}\end{array}\right.

Proof.

For any fractional ideal
J
, we have
J=\mathcal{O}_{K}J
, and hence (as is easily verified)
J\subseteq\delta^{-1}
if and only if
\tr(z)\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
z\in J
.
Taking

J=(T^{-1}s)I^{-1}
, it follows that
s\in T(\delta^{-1}I)
if and only if
r\cdot s=\tr(r(T^{-1}s))\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
r\in I^{-1}
.

Therefore, if
s\in T(\delta^{-1}I)
, we have

\sum_{r\in R(I)}e(r\cdot s)=\sum_{r\in R(I)}1=|R(I)|.

But, by Lemma
3.3
,

|R(I)|=|I^{-1}/\mathcal{O}_{K}|=\dfrac{N(\mathcal{O}_{K})}{N(I^{-1})}=N(I).

Now assume
s\notin T(\delta^{-1}I)
.
Then there exists an
r_{0}\in I^{-1}
such that
r_{0}\cdot s=\tr(r_{0}(T^{-1}s))\notin\mathbb{Z}
, and hence
e(r_{0}\cdot s)\neq 1
.
By pigeonholing,
r_{0}+R(I)
is also a complete representatives of
I^{-1}/\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
So there is a bijection
f:R(I)\to r_{0}+R(I)
such that
r-f(r)\in\mathcal{O}_{K}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for all
r\in R(I)
.
Since
r\mapsto e(r\cdot s)
is
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-periodic,

\begin{split}\sum_{r\in R(I)}e(r\cdot s)=\sum_{r\in R(I)}e(f(r)\cdot s)=e(r_{0}\cdot s)\sum_{r\in R(I)}e(r\cdot s).\end{split}

Since
e(r_{0}\cdot s)\neq 1
,
the sum must be zero.
∎

We shall need the following version of the Chinese remainder theorem.

Lemma 3.6
.

Suppose
I_{1}
,
I_{2}
, and
D
are fractional ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
Suppose
a_{1},a_{2}\in D
.

(1)

If
a_{1}-a_{2}\notin D(I_{1}+I_{2})
, then

(a_{1}+DI_{1})\cap(a_{2}+DI_{2})=\emptyset.

(2)

If
a_{1}-a_{2}\in D(I_{1}+I_{2})
, then there exists
a\in D
such that

(a_{1}+DI_{1})\cap(a_{2}+DI_{2})=a+(DI_{1}\cap DI_{2}).

Proof.

(1)

If
(a_{1}+DI_{1})\cap(a_{2}+DI_{2})\neq\emptyset
, then
a_{1}+d_{1}=a_{2}+d_{2}
for some
d_{1}\in DI_{1}
and
d_{2}\in DI_{2}
, and so

a_{1}-a_{2}=d_{2}-d_{1}\in DI_{1}+DI_{2}=D(I_{1}+I_{2}).

(2)

Note

(I_{1}+I_{2})^{-1}I_{1}+(I_{1}+I_{2})^{-1}I_{2}=(I_{1}+I_{2})^{-1}(I_{1}+I_{2})=\mathcal{O}_{K}.

So there exist
x_{1}\in(I_{1}+I_{2})^{-1}I_{1}
and
x_{2}\in(I_{1}+I_{2})^{-1}I_{2}
such that
x_{1}+x_{2}=1
.
Define
a=x_{1}a_{2}+x_{2}a_{1}
.
Suppose
a_{1}-a_{2}\in D(I_{1}+I_{2})
.
Then

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=x_{1}a_{2}+(1-x_{1})a_{1}=a_{1}+(a_{2}-a_{1})x_{1}

\displaystyle\in a_{1}+D(I_{1}+I_{2})(I_{1}+I_{2})^{-1}I_{1}=a_{1}+DI_{1}.

A similar calculation shows that
a\in a_{2}+DI_{2}
. The desired equality is now easily verified.

∎

4.
Intermediate Functions

In this section, we define several functions necessary for the construction of the measure
\mu
. We also prove necessary Fourier-analytic estimates on these functions.

4.1.
The Functions
\phi
and
\Phi

Fix
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\phi
is
C^{\infty}
,
\phi\geq 0
,
\mathrm{supp}(\phi)\subseteq[-1,1]^{d}
, and

\widehat{\phi}(0)=\int\phi(x)dx=1
. For each non-zero ideal
I
of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
and each
\eta>0
, define

(4.1)

\displaystyle\Phi_{I,\eta}(x)=\sum_{r\in I^{-1}}\eta^{-d}\phi((x-r)/\eta).

The sum converges uniformly on bounded subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
In fact, since
\phi
is compactly supported, for any bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, only finitely many terms of the sum are non-zero on that subset.
Note that
\eta^{-d}\phi((x-r)/\eta)
is an
L^{1}
-normalized bump function on the

\ell^{\infty}
-ball with radius
\eta
and center
r
.
Note also that
\Phi_{I,\eta}
is
C^{\infty}
, non-negative, and
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-periodic.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
I
be a non-zero ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, let
s\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cong\mathcal{O}_{K}
, and let

\eta>0
. Then

(4.2)

\widehat{\Phi_{I,\eta}}(s)=\widehat{\phi}(\eta s)N(I)\mathbf{1}_{\delta^{-1}I}(T^{-1}s).

Moreover, if
0<\eta<C_{B}^{-1}N(I)^{-1/d}
, then

(4.3)

\widehat{\Phi_{I,\eta}^{2}}(s)=\eta^{-d}\widehat{\phi^{2}}(\eta s)N(I)\mathbf{1}_{\delta^{-1}I}(T^{-1}s).

Proof.

For each prime ideal
I
, set
R(I)=I^{-1}\cap[0,1)^{d}
. Note that
R(I)
is a complete set of representatives of
I^{-1}/\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
Every element
r\in I^{-1}
can be written uniquely as
r=r^{\prime}+k
, where
r^{\prime}\in R(I)
and
k\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{O}_{K}
is identified with
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
via the integral basis
B
. Thus

\displaystyle\widehat{\Phi_{I,\eta}}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1)^{d}}\sum_{r\in I^{-1}}\eta^{-d}\phi((x-r)/\eta)e(s\cdot x)dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in I^{-1}}\int_{[0,1)^{d}}\eta^{-d}\phi((x-r)/\eta)e(s\cdot x)dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in R(I)}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\int_{[0,1)^{d}}\eta^{-d}\phi((x-r-k)/\eta)e(s\cdot x)dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in R(I)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\eta^{-d}\phi((x-r)/\eta)e(s\cdot x)dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in R(I)}e(s\cdot r)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(u)e(\eta s\cdot u)du

\displaystyle=\widehat{\phi}(\eta s)\sum_{r\in R(I)}e(s\cdot r).

Now Lemma
3.5
implies (
4.2
).
If
0<\eta<C_{B}^{-1}N(I)^{-1/d}
, then Lemma
3.4
implies
\phi((x-r)/\eta)
and
\phi((x-r^{\prime})/\eta)
have disjoint support whenever
r
and
r^{\prime}
are distinct elements of
I^{-1}
, and so

\Phi_{I,\eta}^{2}(x)=\sum_{r\in I^{-1}}\sum_{r^{\prime}\in I^{-1}}\eta^{-2d}\phi((x-r)/\eta)\phi((x-r^{\prime})/\eta)=\sum_{r\in I^{-1}}\eta^{-2d}\phi^{2}((x-r)/\eta).

Thus an argument similar to the one above gives (
4.3
).
∎

4.2.
The collection of ideals
Q(M)

For each real number
M>1
,
define
Q(M)
to be the set of prime ideals
I
of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
such that
M^{d}/2\leq|N(I)|\leq M^{d}
.
By the Landau prime ideal theorem,

(4.4)

|Q(M)|\sim\frac{M^{d}}{\log(M)}.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
J
be a non-zero integral ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Then

(4.5)

|\left\{{I\in Q(M):J\subseteq I}\right\}|\leq\frac{\log(2N(J))}{d\log M}.

Proof.

Since
J
factors uniquely as a product of prime ideals
I
that contain
J
, and since the ideal norm is multiplicative,

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in Q(M)\\
J\subseteq I\end{subarray}}N(I)\leq N(J).

The desired result follows by taking logarithms and using that each ideal
I\in Q(M)
satisfies
d\log M\leq\log(2N(I))
.
∎

4.3.
The Functions
F_{k}

Let
\{M_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}

be an increasing sequence of positive real numbers. We assume
M_{0}
is large
and the sequence
\{M_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
increases rapidly. Sufficient size and growth conditions will be noted as they arise in the argument.

Fix
-d<\rho<d
. For each odd
k
, fix an ideal
J_{k}\in Q(M_{k}^{1+\frac{\rho}{d}})
. Thus
J_{k}
is a prime ideal
such that

M_{k}^{d+\rho}/2\leq|N(J_{k})|\leq M_{k}^{d+\rho}.

Assuming
M_{1}
is large enough, (
4.4
) guarantees such an ideal exists. Note
J_{k}\in Q(M_{k})
if
\rho=0
.

Recall that
\rho^{+}=\max\left\{{\rho,0}\right\}
and

\rho^{-}=\max\left\{{-\rho,0}\right\}
.
Define

P_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}M_{k}^{\rho^{-}}&\text{if }k\text{ is odd}\\
0&\text{if }k\text{ is even}\\
\end{array}\right.

Recall that
\tau>1
. Define
\eta_{k}=M_{k}^{-(1+\tau)}
.
Define
c_{k}
by

\dfrac{1}{c_{k}}=P_{k}N(J)+\sum_{I\in Q(M_{k})}N(I).

For future reference, by (
4.4
) and assuming
M_{k}
is large enough, we have

(4.6)

\displaystyle c_{k}\sim\log(M_{k})M_{k}^{-2d}.

Define
F_{k}
by

(4.7)

\displaystyle F_{k}(x)=c_{k}\left({P_{k}\Phi_{J_{k},\eta_{k}}(x)+\sum_{I\in Q(M_{k})}\Phi_{I,\eta_{k}}(x)}\right).

Lemma 4.3
.

Define

C_{0}=2^{-1/d}C_{B}^{-1}\|T^{-1}\|^{-1}N(\delta)^{1/d}.

Let
k\geq 1
and
s\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

(4.8)

\displaystyle\widehat{F_{k}}(0)

\displaystyle=1.

(4.9)

\displaystyle|\widehat{F_{k}}(s)|

\displaystyle\leq 1,\qquad\forall s\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}

(4.10)

\displaystyle|\widehat{F_{k}}(s)|

\displaystyle=0,\qquad\text{if}\ 0<|s|\leq C_{0}M_{k}^{1-\rho^{-}/d}.

(4.11)

\displaystyle|\widehat{F_{k}}(s)|

\displaystyle\lesssim M_{k}^{-d+\rho^{+}}\log|s|\qquad\text{if}\ |s|\geq C_{0}M_{k}^{1-\rho^{-}/d}.

(4.12)

\displaystyle|\widehat{F_{k}}(s)|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{N}M_{k}^{-d+\rho^{+}}\log|s|\left(\frac{M_{k}^{1+\tau}}{|s|}\right)^{N}\qquad\text{if}\ |s|\geq M_{k}^{1+\tau}.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.1
, we obtain

\widehat{F_{k}}(s)=c_{k}\widehat{\phi}(\eta_{k}s)\left(P_{k}N(J_{k})\mathbf{1}_{\delta^{-1}J_{k}}(T^{-1}s)+\sum_{I\in Q(M_{k})}N(I)\mathbf{1}_{\delta^{-1}I}(T^{-1}s)\right).

Since
\widehat{\phi}(0)=1
, (
4.8
) follows immediately. Since
F_{k}\geq 0
, (
4.9
) follows from (
4.8
).

We prove the contrapositive of (
4.10
).
Assume
s\neq 0
and
\widehat{F_{k}}(s)\neq 0
.
So there is at least one
I\in\left\{{J_{k}}\right\}\cup Q(M_{k})
such that
T^{-1}s\in\delta^{-1}I
.
Then, by Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.3
,

N(\delta^{-1})N(I)\leq N(\left\langle{T^{-1}s}\right\rangle)=|N(T^{-1}s)|\leq C_{B}^{d}|T^{-1}s|^{d}\leq C_{B}^{d}\|T^{-1}\|^{d}|s|^{d}.

If
I\in Q(M_{k})
, then
N(I)\geq M_{k}^{d}/2
. If
I=J_{k}
, then
N(I)\geq M_{k}^{d+\rho}/2
.
Thus

|s|\geq 2^{-1/d}C_{B}^{-1}\|T^{-1}\|^{-1}N(\delta)^{1/d}\min\left\{{M_{k},M_{k}^{1+\rho/d}}\right\}.

Now we prove (
4.11
) and (
4.12
).
Recall
N(J_{k})\leq M_{k}^{d+\rho}
and
N(I)\leq M_{k}^{d}
for all
I\in Q(M_{k})
.
By (
4.6
),
c_{k}\lesssim\log(M_{k})M_{k}^{-2d}
.
We need an upper bound on the number of
I\in Q(M_{k})
such that
T^{-1}s\in\delta^{-1}I
.
Note that
T^{-1}s\in\delta^{-1}I
implies
(T^{-1}s)\delta\subseteq I
. So it suffices to find an upper bound on the number of
I\in Q(M_{k})
such that
(T^{-1}s)\delta\subseteq I
. By applying Lemma
4.2
with
J=(T^{-1}s)\delta
and then using (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), the number in question is seen to be
\lesssim\log|s|/\log M_{k}
, assuming
M_{k}
(and hence
|s|
) is large enough.
Therefore,

|\widehat{F_{k}}(s)|\lesssim M_{k}^{-d}(M_{k}^{\rho^{+}}\log M_{k}+\log|s|)|\widehat{\phi}(\eta_{k}s)|.

Since
\phi
is Schwartz, (
4.11
) and (
4.12
) follow immediately.
∎

5.
Intermediate Measures

In this section, we define and establish Fourier-analytic estimates on measures
\mu_{k}
and
\mu_{l,k}
.
These measures are used in the construction of
\mu
and in proving the required properties of
\mu
.

5.1.
The measures
\mu_{k}

Let
\phi_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a non-negative Schwartz function with support contained in
([-3/8,-1/8]\cap[1/8,3/8])^{d}
and
\widehat{\phi_{0}}(0)=\int\phi_{0}(x)dx=1
.

We define measures
\mu_{k}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

d\mu_{0}=\phi_{0}dx,\quad d\mu_{k}=\phi_{0}F_{M_{1}}\cdots F_{M_{k}}dx\quad\text{ for all }k\in\mathbb{N}.

To simplify the statement and proof of the next lemma, we define
\mu_{-1}=0
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
N
be an integer with
N\geq 100d
.
If
(M_{k})_{k=0}^{\infty}
grows sufficiently rapidly, then for all integers
k\geq 0
and all
s\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the following statements hold:

(5.1)

|\widehat{\mu}_{k}(s)|\lesssim 1,

(5.2)

|\widehat{\mu}_{k}(s)-\widehat{\mu}_{k-1}(s)|\lesssim M_{k}^{-(N-2d)}\log(M_{k})\qquad{\text{if}}\ |s|\leq\frac{1}{2}C_{0}M_{k}^{1-\rho^{-}/d},

6.
Construction and Fourier Analysis of

In this section we construct the measure

6.1.
Construction of
\mu

Lemma 6.1
.

The sequence
(\mu_{k})
converges weakly to a non-zero finite Borel measure
\mu
.

The proof of Lemma
6.1
requires the Lévy continuity theorem.

Theorem 6.2

(Lévy Continuity Theorem)
.

Let
(\nu_{k})
be a sequence of Borel measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
0<c\leq\nu_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\leq C<\infty
for all
k
.
Suppose
(\widehat{\nu_{k}}(\xi))
converges for each
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

and
suppose
(\nu_{k})
is tight (i.e., for every
\epsilon>0
there is a compact set
K_{\epsilon}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\nu_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus K_{\epsilon})<\epsilon
for all
k
). Then there exists a finite non-zero Borel measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\nu_{k}
converges to
\nu
weakly (i.e., in distribution).

For a proof of this theorem when
\nu_{k}
are probability measures, see
[
2
, Section 26]
for
d=1
and
[
2
, Section 26]
for
d\geq 1
. The general case follows by considering
a_{k}\nu_{k}
, where
1/a_{k}=\nu_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Alternatively, for a direct proof of the general case, see
[
4
, Section 8.8]
.

We also need the following lemma.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
(\nu_{k})
be a sequence of Borel measures with support contained in
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{d}
and with
\sup_{k}\nu_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})<\infty
. If
(\widehat{\nu_{k}}(s))
converges for each
s\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, then
(\widehat{\nu_{k}}(\xi))
converges for each
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

Let
\psi
be a Schwartz function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have (see
[
33
, Eq. (110)]
)

\widehat{\psi\nu_{k}}(\xi)=\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\widehat{\nu_{k}}(s)\widehat{\psi}(\xi-s).

Because
\widehat{\psi}
is a Schwartz function and because
\sup_{k}|\widehat{\nu_{k}}(\xi)|\leq\sup_{k}\nu_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})<\infty
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem lets us take the limit as
k\to\infty
. Thus, for each
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\widehat{\psi\nu_{k}}(\xi)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\widehat{\nu_{k}}(s)\widehat{\psi}(\xi-s)=\sum_{s\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\lim_{k\to\infty}\widehat{\nu_{k}}(s)\widehat{\psi}(\xi-s)

and the limit is finite. If
\psi
is equal to 1 on
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{d}
, then
\psi\nu_{k}=\nu_{k}
for all
k
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
6.1
.

By construction,
\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{k})\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(\phi_{0})\subseteq[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]^{d}
for all

(\mu_{k})
is tight.
By (

), whenever

and

we have

\left|\widehat{\mu_{k}}(s)-\widehat{\mu_{n}}(s)\right|\leq\sum_{j=n}^{\infty}M_{j}^{-100}\log(M_{j}).

In particular,

Since
\widehat{\mu_{0}}(0)=\widehat{\phi_{0}}(0)=1
, we have

for all

Furthermore, since the sum above goes to

as

Lemma

∎

6.2.
Support of
\mu

Proposition 6.4
.

\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(\phi_{0})\cap E(K,B,\tau).

Proof.

Because
\mu_{k}=\phi_{0}F_{1}\cdots F_{k}
and because

is the weak limit of

,

\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\subseteq\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{k})=\mathrm{supp}(\phi_{0})\cap\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{supp}(F_{k}).

For even values of

,

\mathrm{supp}(F_{k})=\bigcup_{I\in Q(M_{k})}\mathrm{supp}(\Phi_{I,\eta_{k}}).

If
I\in Q(M_{k})
and
\Phi_{I,\eta_{k}}(x)>0
, then

\dist(x,I^{-1})\leq\eta_{k}\leq N(I)^{-(\tau+1)/d}.

Since
I^{-1}
is discrete, it follows that

\mathrm{supp}(\Phi_{I,\eta_{k}})\subseteq\left\{{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\dist(x,I^{-1})\leq N(I)^{-(\tau+1)/d}}\right\}

.
By putting everything together and using that the sets

are disjoint, we have

\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{supp}(F_{k})\subseteq E(K,B,\tau).

∎

6.3.

We will show (

6.2
) follows

is a Schwartz function. Suppose

and suppose the statement

and

(7.4)

\mu(B)\lesssim\log^{2}(M_{k-1})M_{k-1}^{d(\tau-1)+\rho^{-}}|Q(M_{k})|^{-1}M_{k}^{-d}.

Proof.

Note (
7.3
) follows from (
7.4
), if
M_{k}
is large enough. So we only need to prove (
7.2
) and (
7.4
).

Step 1: Estimate for
\mu_{k}(2B)
.

We first prove (
7.2
) and (
7.4
) with
\mu
replaced by
\mu_{k}
and
B
replaced by
2B
.
Note

\displaystyle\mu_{k}(2B)=\int_{2B}\phi_{0}F_{1}\cdots F_{k}dx.

Since the Lebesgue measure of
2B
is
\lesssim M_{k}^{-(1+\tau)d}
, it will suffice to obtain appropriate pointwise bounds on the integrand.
If
x\in\mathrm{supp}(\phi_{0})
, then, by Lemma
7.4
(a),

\displaystyle\phi_{0}F_{1}\cdots F_{k}(x)

\displaystyle\lesssim\log(M_{k})c_{k}\eta_{k}^{-d}(1+P_{k})

\displaystyle\lesssim\log^{2}(M_{k})M_{k}^{d(\tau-1)+\rho^{-}},

assuming
M_{k}
is large enough.
This proves (
7.2
) with
\mu
replaced by
\mu_{k}
and
B
replaced by
2B
.
Now suppose
2B
does not intersect
\mathrm{supp}(\Phi_{J_{k},\eta_{k}})
.
For each
x\in\mathrm{supp}(\phi_{0})\cap 2B
, we apply Lemma
7.4
(a) to
F_{1}(x),\ldots,F_{k-1}(x)
and apply Lemma
7.4
(b) to
F_{k}(x)
to get

\displaystyle\phi_{0}F_{1}\cdots F_{k}(x)

\displaystyle\lesssim\log(M_{k-1})c_{k-1}\eta_{k-1}^{-d}(1+P_{k-1})c_{k}\eta_{k}^{-d}

\displaystyle\lesssim\log^{2}(M_{k-1})M_{k-1}^{d(\tau-1)+\rho^{-}}|Q(M_{k})|^{-1}M_{k}^{\tau d},

assuming
M_{k-1}
and
M_{k}
are large enough.
This proves (
7.4
) with
\mu
replaced by
\mu_{k}
and
B
replaced by
2B
.

Step 2: Estimate for
\mu(B)
.

We assume
B
intersects the support of
\phi_{0}
(otherwise
\mu_{l}(B)=0
and there is nothing to prove).
Suppose
B=B(x_{0},\eta_{k})
. So
2B=B(x_{0},2\eta_{k})
.
Let
\psi
be a Schwartz function with support contained in
B(0,2)
, with
\psi\geq 0
everywhere, and with
\psi\gtrsim 1
on
B(0,1)
.
Let
\psi_{B}(x)=\psi((x-x_{0})/\eta_{k})
.
For arbitrary
l\geq k
, we write

\mu_{l}(\psi_{B}):=\int\psi_{B}\mu_{l}dx.

By Plancherel’s theorem, we have

\mu_{l}(\psi_{B})-\mu_{k}(\psi_{B})=\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\widehat{F_{k}\psi_{B}}(t)(\widehat{\mu_{l,k}}(t)-\widehat{\mu}_{k-1}(t)).

Note that hypotheses of Lemma
7.3
are satisfied.
By Lemma
7.3
with
Z=10d
, the hypotheses of
Lemma
5.3
are satisfied with
C_{k}=c_{k}(1+P_{k})
,
H(u)=\widehat{F_{k}\psi_{B}}(-u)
, and
s=0
. From Lemma
5.3
, we obtain

\mu_{l}(\psi_{B})-\mu_{k}(\psi_{B})\lesssim c_{k}(1+P_{k})M_{k}^{-80(1+\tau)d}.

Since
\mathbf{1}_{B}\lesssim\psi_{B}\lesssim\mathbf{1}_{2B}
, we have
\mu_{l}(B)\lesssim\mu_{l}(\psi_{B})

and

\mu_{k}(\psi_{B})\lesssim\mu_{k}(2B)
.
Therefore

\mu_{l}(B)\lesssim\mu_{k}(2B)+c_{k}(1+P_{k})M_{k}^{-80(1+\tau)d}.

Letting
l\rightarrow\infty
, we have established
(
7.4
) and (
7.2
).
∎

Now we prove the regularity of the measure
\mu
.

Proposition 7.6
.

For each ball
B
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of radius
0<r<1
,

\mu(B)\lesssim\log^{2}(r^{-1})r^{\frac{2d-\rho^{-}}{1+\tau}}.

Proof.

Preliminaries.
Since
\mu
is a probability measure, the result holds for
r\geq M_{0}^{-(1+\tau)}
by taking a large enough implied constant.
Thus we assume that
M_{k}^{-(1+\tau)}\leq r\leq M_{k-1}^{-(1+\tau)}
for some
k\geq 1
.

We introduce some terminology.
A
k
-ball is closed ball of radius
\eta_{k}
with center
r\in I^{-1}
for some
I\in Q(M_{k})\cup\left\{{J_{k}}\right\}
.
We consider two kinds of
k
-balls.
Let
\mathcal{J}_{2}
be the set of
k
-balls
J
such that
2J
intersects
\mathrm{supp}(\Phi_{J_{k},\eta_{k}})
and let
\mathcal{J}_{1}
be the remaining
k
-balls. (Recall that
2J
is the ball with the same center as
J
but with twice the radius.) Note that if
k
is even, then
\mathcal{J}_{2}
is empty.

The support
\mu
is contained in the support of
F_{k}
, which is the union of all the
k
-balls.
Hence, we have the decomposition

\mu(B)\leq\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}_{1},J\cap B\neq\emptyset}\mu(J)+\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}_{2},J\cap B\neq\emptyset}\mu(J).

Sum over
J\in\mathcal{J}_{1}.

First we estimate the sum over
J\in\mathcal{J}_{1}
.
Since each ball in
\mathcal{J}_{1}
is centered at an element of
I^{-1}
for some
I\in Q(M_{k})
, we will use Lemma
3.4
to bound the number of terms in the sum.
We consider three cases.

Case 1:
M_{k}^{-(1+\tau)}\leq r\leq M_{k}^{-2}
.
By Lemma
3.4
, the centers of balls in
\mathcal{J}_{1}
are separated by
\gtrsim M_{k}^{-2}
.
Thus the number of terms in the sum over
\mathcal{J}_{1}
is
\lesssim 1
. By (
7.3
),

\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}_{1},J\cap B\neq\emptyset}\mu(J)\lesssim\log^{2}(M_{k})M_{k}^{-2d}\lesssim\log^{2}(r^{-1})r^{\frac{2d}{1+\tau}}.

Case 2:
M_{k}^{-2}\leq r\leq M_{k}^{-1}
. Again, by Lemma
3.4
, the centers of balls in
\mathcal{J}_{1}
are separated by
\gtrsim M_{k}^{-2}
. Now the number of terms in the sum is
\lesssim(M_{k}^{2}r)^{d}
. By (
7.3
),

\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}_{1},J\cap B\neq\emptyset}\mu(J)\lesssim\log^{2}(M_{k})r^{d}\lesssim\log^{2}(r^{-1})r^{d}\leq\log^{2}(r^{-1})r^{\frac{2d}{1+\tau}}.

Case 3:
M_{k}^{-1}\leq r\leq M_{k-1}^{-(1+\tau)}
. Fix an ideal
I\in Q(M_{k})
. By Lemma
3.4
, the elements in
I^{-1}
are at least
M_{k}^{-1}
separated.
So the number of
k
-balls in
\mathcal{J}_{1}
that intersect
B
and whose centers belong to
I^{-1}

is
\lesssim(M_{k}r)^{d}
.
Therefore the total number of
k
-balls in
\mathcal{J}_{1}
that intersect
B
is
\lesssim|Q(M_{k})|(M_{k}r)^{d}
.
Using (
7.4
) and that
r\leq M_{k-1}^{-(1+\tau)}
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}_{1},J\cap B\neq\emptyset}\mu(J)\lesssim\log^{2}(M_{k-1})M_{k-1}^{(\tau-1)d+\rho^{-}}r^{d}\lesssim\log^{2}(r^{-1})r^{\frac{2d-\rho^{-}}{1+\tau}}.

Sum over
J\in\mathcal{J}_{2}.

Now we consider the sum over
J\in\mathcal{J}_{2}
.
Let
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}_{2}
be the subcollection of
\mathcal{J}_{2}
consisting of the balls whose center is an element of
J_{k}^{-1}
.
Let
\mathcal{J}^{\prime\prime}_{2}
be the remaining balls in
\mathcal{J}_{2}
.
By Lemma
7.1
(b), each ball in
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}_{2}
intersects at most one ball in
\mathcal{J}^{\prime\prime}_{2}
.
Thus the cardinality of
\mathcal{J}_{2}
is at most twice that of
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}_{2}
.
Moreover, by Lemma
7.1
(a),
the balls in
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}_{2}
are separated by
\gtrsim M_{k}^{-1-\frac{\rho}{d}}
.
Thus the number of terms in the sum over
\mathcal{J}_{2}
is
\lesssim\max\left\{{1,M_{k}^{d+\rho}r^{d}}\right\}
.

By (
7.2
),

(7.5)

\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}_{2},J\cap B\neq\emptyset}\mu(J)\lesssim\log^{2}(M_{k})M_{k}^{-2d+\rho^{-}}\cdot\max\left\{{1,M_{k}^{d+\rho}r^{d}}\right\}.

If the maximum
in (
7.5
)
is
1
, then
M_{k}^{-(1+\tau)}\leq r\leq M_{k}^{-1-\frac{\rho}{d}}
, and so

\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}_{2},J\cap B\neq\emptyset}\mu(J)\lesssim\log^{2}(r^{-1})r^{\frac{2d+\rho^{-}}{1+\tau}}.

If the maximum
in (
7.5
)
is
M_{k}^{d+\rho}r^{d}
,
then

\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}_{2},J\cap B\neq\emptyset}\mu(J)\lesssim\log^{2}(M_{k})M_{k}^{-d+\rho^{+}}r^{d}.

Since the function

\log^{2}(x)x^{-d+\rho^{+}}

is eventually decreasing and
since
M_{k}^{-(1+\tau)}\leq r\leq M_{k-1}^{-(1+\tau)}
,
the last expression is

\displaystyle\lesssim\log^{2}(r^{-1})r^{\frac{d-\rho^{+}}{1+\tau}}r^{d}\leq\log^{2}(r^{-1})r^{\frac{2d-\rho^{-}}{1+\tau}}.

∎

8.
Failure of the Extension Estimate

In this section, we show that
\mu
satisfies (iv) of Theorem
1.2
with the functions
f_{k}=\Phi_{J_{k},\eta_{k}}
.
Explicitly, our goal is to prove the following proposition.

Proposition 8.1
.

For all
1\leq q<\infty
and all
p<p(\tau,\rho,q,d):=\frac{q(d\tau-\rho)}{(q-1)(d-\rho^{+})}
,

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\to\infty\\
k\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}\dfrac{\|\widehat{\Phi_{J_{k},\eta_{k}}\mu}\|_{L^{p}}}{\|\Phi_{J_{k},\eta_{k}}\|_{L^{q}(\mu)}}=\infty.

Our proof of this proposition requires establishing several intermediate results.

8.1.
Pointwise Lower Bound

Fix a constant
0<c<1
such that
\widehat{\phi^{2}}(\xi)\geq c
for all
|\xi|\leq c
. (Such a constant exists because
\widehat{\phi^{2}}(0)=\int\phi^{2}(x)dx>0
and
\widehat{\phi^{2}}
is continuous.)
Fix an odd positive integer
k
.
We will prove:

Lemma 8.2
.

If
T^{-1}s\in\delta^{-1}J_{k}
and
|s|\leq cM_{k}^{1+\tau}
, then

(8.1)

\left|\widehat{\Phi_{J_{k},\eta_{k}}\mu}(s)\right|\gtrsim\log(M_{k})M_{k}^{d\tau+\rho^{+}}.

The rest of the subsection is devoted to the proof of this lemma. It suffices to show that

(8.2)

|\widehat{\Phi_{J_{k},\eta_{k}}\mu_{l}}(s)|\gtrsim\log(M_{k})M_{k}^{d\tau+\rho^{+}}

for
l\geq k
.
Fix
l,k\in\mathbb{N}
with
l\geq k
.
Write
\Phi_{J_{k},\eta_{k}}F_{k}=h_{1}+h_{2}
where

h_{1}(x)=c_{k}P_{k}(\Phi_{J_{k},\eta_{k}}(x))^{2}

and

h_{2}(x)=c_{k}\Phi_{J_{k},\eta_{k}}(x)\sum_{I\in Q(M_{k})}\Phi_{I,\eta_{k}}(x).

Recall that

\mu_{l,k}=F_{l}F_{l-1}\cdots F_{k+1}\mu_{k-1}
.
Therefore
\Phi_{J_{k},\eta_{k}}\mu_{l}=h_{1}\mu_{l,k}+h_{2}\mu_{l,k}
, and hence

(8.3)

\displaystyle\widehat{\Phi_{J_{k},\eta_{k}}\mu_{l}}(s)=\widehat{h_{1}}(s)\widehat{\mu_{l,k}}(0)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\\
t\neq 0\end{subarray}}\widehat{h_{1}}(s-t)\widehat{\mu_{l,k}}(t)+\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\widehat{h_{2}}(s-t)\widehat{\mu_{l,k}}(t).

Lemma 8.3
.

If
T^{-1}s\in\delta^{-1}J
and
|s|\leq cM_{k}^{1+\tau}
, then

(8.4)

|\widehat{h_{1}}(s)\cdot\widehat{\mu_{l,k}}(0)|\gtrsim\log(M_{k})M_{k}^{d\tau+\rho^{+}}.

Proof.

By (
4.3
),
(
4.6
), and the definition of
c
,

\displaystyle|\widehat{h_{1}}(s)|=c_{k}P_{k}\eta_{k}^{-d}N(J_{k})|\widehat{\phi^{2}}(\eta_{k}s)|\gtrsim\log(M_{k})M_{k}^{d\tau+\rho^{+}}.

Since
\widehat{\mu_{0}}(0)\widehat{\phi}_{0}(0)=1
, we iterate (
5.6
) and obtain
|\widehat{{\mu}_{l,k}}(0)|\gtrsim 1
.
∎

Lemma 8.4
.

If
T^{-1}s\in\delta^{-1}J
and
|s|\leq M_{k}^{1+\tau}
, then

(8.5)

\sum_{t\neq 0}|\widehat{h_{1}}(s-t)\widehat{\mu_{l,k}}(t)|\lesssim\log(M_{k})M_{k}^{d\tau+\rho^{+}-80(d+\rho)}.

Proof.

By (
4.3
) and (
4.6
),

\displaystyle\widehat{h_{1}}(u)

\displaystyle=c_{k}P_{k}\widehat{\Phi_{J_{k},\eta_{k}}^{2}}(u)

\displaystyle\lesssim c_{k}\eta_{k}^{-d}P_{k}\widehat{\phi^{2}}(\eta_{k}u)N(J_{k})\mathbf{1}_{\delta^{-1}J_{k}}(T^{-1}u)

\displaystyle\lesssim\log(M_{k})M_{k}^{d\tau+\rho^{+}}\left(1+\frac{|u|}{M_{k}^{1+\tau}}\right)^{-10d}\mathbf{1}_{\delta^{-1}J_{k}}(T^{-1}u).

Therefore the hypotheses of Lemma
5.3
are satisfied with

C_{k}=\log(M_{k})M_{k}^{d\tau+\rho^{+}}

and
H_{k}(u)=\widehat{h_{1}}(u)\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\left\{{s}\right\}}(u)
.
Consequently, using the assumption
|s|\leq M_{k}^{1+\tau}
, we get

\sum_{t\neq 0}|\widehat{h_{1}}(s-t)\widehat{\mu_{l,k}}(t)|\lesssim C_{k}M_{k}^{-80(1+\tau)d}+\sum_{t\neq 0}|\widehat{h_{1}}(s-t)\widehat{\mu_{k-1}}(t)|.

Since
T^{-1}s\in\delta^{-1}J
and since
\widehat{h}_{1}(s-t)
is zero when
T^{-1}(s-t)\notin\delta^{-1}J
, we conclude that
T^{-1}t\in\delta^{-1}J
for those
t
such that
\widehat{h_{1}}(s-t)\neq 0
.
Thus, by (
3.3
) and Lemma
3.3
,

|t|^{d}\gtrsim N(J)\gtrsim M_{k}^{d+\rho}
,
which implies
|t|\geq 2M_{k-1}^{1+\tau}
.
Thus, by (
5.4
), we have

\displaystyle|\widehat{\mu}_{k-1}(t)|\lesssim M_{k-1}^{-d+\rho^{+}}\log(|t|)(M_{k-1}^{1+\tau}/|t|)^{99d}.

Recall that
|\widehat{h}_{1}(u)|\lesssim\log(M_{k})M_{k}^{d\tau+\rho^{+}}=C_{k}
.
Therefore

\sum_{t\neq 0}|\widehat{h_{1}}(s-t)\widehat{\mu_{k-1}}(t)|=\sum_{|t|\gtrsim M_{k}^{1+\frac{\rho}{d}}}|\widehat{h_{1}}(s-t)\widehat{\mu_{k-1}}(t)|\lesssim C_{k}M_{k}^{-97(d+\rho)}.

Since
80(1+\tau)d
and
97(d+\rho)
are both larger than
80(d+\rho)
,
we have established (
8.5
).
∎

To bound the sum involving
\widehat{h_{2}}
in (
8.3
), we first prove the following lemma.

Lemma 8.5
.

Suppose
\rho\neq 0
.
For all

s\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,

|\widehat{h_{2}}(s)|\lesssim M_{k}^{d\tau}(1+M_{k}^{\rho-d(\tau-1)})(1+\eta_{k}|s|)^{-10d}.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\widehat{h_{2}}(s)=\sum_{I\in Q(M_{k})}\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}c_{k}\widehat{\Phi_{I,\eta_{k}}}(t)\widehat{\Phi_{J_{k},\eta_{k}}}(s-t).

By Lemma
4.1
,

\widehat{h_{2}}(s)=\sum_{I\in Q(M_{k})}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}(I)}c_{k}N(I)N(J_{k})\widehat{\phi}(\eta_{k}t)\widehat{\phi}(\eta_{k}(s-t)),

where

\mathcal{T}(I)=\left\{{t\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:T^{-1}t\in\delta^{-1}I\cap(T^{-1}s+\delta^{-1}J_{k})}\right\}.

Since at least one of
|s-t|
and
|t|
will be
\geq|s|/2
,
the Schwartz bounds on
\widehat{\phi}

imply

|\widehat{\phi}(\eta_{k}t)\widehat{\phi}(\eta_{k}(s-t))|\lesssim(1+\eta_{k}|s|)^{-10d}(1+\eta_{k}|t|)^{-50d}.

Therefore,

(8.6)

|\widehat{h_{2}}(s)|\lesssim(1+\eta_{k}|s|)^{-10d}\log(M_{k})M_{k}^{\rho}\sum_{I\in Q(M_{k})}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}(I)}(1+\eta_{k}|t|)^{-50d}.

Consider a fixed
I\in Q(M_{k})
.
By Lemma
3.6
, either the set

\delta^{-1}I\cap(T^{-1}s+\delta^{-1}J_{k})
is empty or
there exists
a\in\delta^{-1}
such that

\delta^{-1}I\cap(T^{-1}s+\delta^{-1}J_{k})=a+\delta^{-1}I\cap\delta^{-1}J_{k}.

In the first case,
\mathcal{T}(I)
is empty, so the sum over
t\in\mathcal{T}(I)
in (
8.6
) is zero.
In the second case,

\delta^{-1}I\cap(T^{-1}s+\delta^{-1}J_{k})
is a translate of

\delta^{-1}I\cap\delta^{-1}J_{k}
,
so the separation between the elements of
\delta^{-1}I\cap(T^{-1}s+\delta^{-1}J_{k})

is the same as the separation between the elements of
\delta^{-1}I\cap\delta^{-1}J_{k}
.
By Lemma
3.4
(with
I_{1}=I_{2}=(\delta^{-1}I\cap\delta^{-1}J_{k})^{-1}
), the elements of
\delta^{-1}I\cap\delta^{-1}J_{k}
are separated by

\gtrsim N(\delta^{-1}I\cap\delta^{-1}J_{k})^{1/d}.

But, by Lemma
3.3
,

(8.7)

\displaystyle N(\delta^{-1}I\cap\delta^{-1}J_{k})^{1/d}\gtrsim N(I\cap J_{k})^{1/d}=(N(I)N(J_{k}))^{1/d}\gtrsim M_{k}^{2+\frac{\rho}{d}},

where the equality holds because
I
and
J_{k}
are relatively prime.
Thus the elements of

\delta^{-1}I\cap(T^{-1}s+\delta^{-1}J)

are separated by

\gtrsim M_{k}^{2+\frac{\rho}{d}}.

Since
T
is a bounded linear isomorphism,
the elements of
\mathcal{T}(I)

are also separated by
\gtrsim M_{k}^{2+\frac{\rho}{d}}
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}(I)}(1+\eta_{k}|t|)^{-50d}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(1+\eta_{k}M_{k}^{2+\frac{\rho}{d}}|t|)^{-50d}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}(M_{k}^{1+\tau}/M_{k}^{2+\frac{\rho}{d}})^{d}&\text{if }\rho\leq d(\tau-1),\\
1&\text{if }\rho>d(\tau-1).\end{array}\right.

In any case, the sum is
\lesssim M_{k}^{d(\tau-1)-\rho}+1
.
Plugging this estimate and (
4.4
) into (
8.6
)
completes the proof.
∎

Now we are ready to bound the sum involving
\widehat{h_{2}}
in (
8.3
).

Lemma 8.6
.

Suppose
\rho\neq 0
. If
T^{-1}s\in\delta^{-1}J
and
|s|\leq M_{k}^{1+\tau}
, then

(8.8)

\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|\widehat{h_{2}}(s-t)\widehat{\mu_{l,k}}(t)|\lesssim\log^{1/10}(M_{k})M_{k}^{d\tau}(1+M_{k}^{\rho-d(\tau-1)}).

Proof.

By Lemma
8.5
, the hypotheses of Lemma
5.3
are satisfied with

C_{k}=M_{k}^{d\tau}(1+M_{k}^{\rho-d(\tau-1)})
and
H_{k}=\widehat{h_{2}}
.
Consequently, using the assumption
|s|\leq M_{k}^{1+\tau}
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|\widehat{h_{2}}(s-t)\widehat{\mu_{l,k}}(t)|\lesssim C_{k}M_{k}^{-80d(1+\tau)}+\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|\widehat{h_{2}}(s-t)\widehat{\mu_{k-1}}(t)|.

To bound the sum on the right, we consider separately the ranges
|t|<\log^{1/10d}(M_{k})
and
|t|\geq\log^{1/10d}(M_{k})
.
In both cases, we shall use the bound
|\widehat{h}_{2}(u)|\lesssim M_{k}^{d\tau}(1+M_{k}^{\rho-d(\tau-1)})
furnished by Lemma
8.5
.
By (
5.1
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{|t|<\log^{1/10d}(M_{k})}|\widehat{h}_{2}(s-t)\widehat{\mu}_{k-1}(t)|\lesssim\log^{1/10}(M_{k})M_{k}^{d\tau}(1+M_{k}^{\rho-d(\tau-1)}).

We assume
M_{k}
is large enough that
\log^{1/10d}(M_{k})\geq 2M_{k-1}^{1+\tau}
.
Thus we can apply (
5.4
) (with
k-1
in place of
k
) to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{|t|\geq\log^{1/10d}(M_{k})}|\widehat{h}_{2}(s-t)\widehat{\mu}_{k-1}(t)|\lesssim M_{k}^{d\tau}(1+M_{k}^{\rho-d(\tau-1)})\log^{-100}(M_{k}).

∎

We now complete the proof of Lemma
8.2
. If
\rho\neq 0
, (
8.2
) follows by plugging the estimates (
8.4
), (
8.5
), (
8.8
) into

8.3
.
If
\rho=0
, it suffices to consider
h_{1}
only. Indeed,

\displaystyle h_{2}(x)=c_{k}(\Phi_{J_{k},\eta_{k}}(x))^{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in Q(M)\\
I\neq J_{k}\end{subarray}}c_{k}\Phi_{J_{k},\eta_{k}}(x)\Phi_{I,\eta_{k}}(x)

and the terms of the sum vanish for
x\in\mathrm{supp}(\phi_{0})
by Lemma
3.4
. Hence, if
\rho=0
, we have

h_{2}(x)=c_{k}(\Phi_{J_{k},\eta_{k}}(x))^{2}=\frac{1}{P_{k}}h_{1}(x)=h_{1}(x).

So (
8.2
) follows from (
8.4
) and (
8.5
).

8.2.
Norm Bounds

Lemma 8.7
.

For each
1\leq q<\infty
,

\left\|\Phi_{J_{k},\eta_{k}}\right\|_{L^{q}(\mu)}^{q}\lesssim\log^{2}(M_{k})M_{k}^{qd(1+\tau)-d+\rho^{+}}.

Proof.

Note
\Phi_{J_{k},\eta_{k}}(x)
is supported on balls of radius
\eta_{k}=M_{k}^{-(1+\tau)}
which
(by Lemma
7.1
) are separated by
\gtrsim M_{k}^{-1-\frac{\rho}{d}}
.
As the support of
\mu
is compact, it intersects
\lesssim M_{k}^{d+\rho}
of these balls.
So, by (
7.2
) of Lemma
7.5
,

9.
General Convolution Stability Lemma

Lemma 9.1
.

Let
G,H:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
be functions such that
|G|\leq G(0)=1
and
H
is bounded.
Let
a>0
. Let
N
be a positive integer such that

N>2d+2a
. Let
1\leq A\leq B
.
Let
D
be a number satisfying

(9.1)

B^{-(N-d)}\leq A^{-(N-d)}\leq D

Let
L:(1,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
be a function such that, for all sufficiently large
x
,

(9.2)

L(x)
is increasing

(9.3)

L(x)/x^{a}
is decreasing

Suppose the following estimates hold for all
s\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
:

(9.4)

\displaystyle G(s)=0\quad\text{if }1\leq|s|\leq A;

(9.5)

\displaystyle|G(s)|\lesssim DL(|s|)\quad\text{if }|s|\geq A;

(9.6)

\displaystyle|G(s)|\lesssim D(B/|s|)^{N}L(|s|)\quad\text{if }|s|\geq B;

(9.7)

\displaystyle|H(s)|\lesssim|s|^{-(N-d)}L(|s|)\quad\text{if }|s|\geq 1.

If
A
and
B

are sufficiently large
depending
on
the other parameters
and on the implicit constants in (
9.5
), (
9.6
), and (
9.7
), then the following estimates hold for all
s\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
:

(9.8)

\displaystyle|G\ast H(s)-H(s)|\lesssim A^{-(N-2d)}L(A/2)\quad\text{if }|s|\leq A/2;

(9.9)

\displaystyle|G\ast H(s)|\lesssim DL(2|s|)\quad\text{if }|s|\geq A/2;

(9.10)

\displaystyle|G\ast H(s)|\lesssim D(B/|s|)^{N-d}L(|s|/2)\quad\text{if }|s|\geq 2B.

Proof of (
9.8
).

Assume
|s|\leq\dfrac{1}{2}A
.
By (
9.4
) and because

|G|\leq G(0)=1
,
we have

|G\ast H(s)-H(s)|=\left|{\sum_{|t|\geq A}G(t)H(s-t)}\right|\leq\sum_{|t|\geq A}|H(s-t)|.

The assumption
|s|\leq\dfrac{1}{2}A
and the condition
|t|\geq A
on the sum imply that

|s-t|\geq A/2
.
Thus

|G\ast H(s)-H(s)|\leq\sum_{|s-t|\geq A/2}|H(s-t)|.

Now (
9.7
) implies

\displaystyle|G\ast H(s)-H(s)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{|s-t|\geq A/2}|s-t|^{-(N-a-d)}\dfrac{L(|s-t|)}{|s-t|^{a}}.

By (
9.3
), assuming
A
is sufficiently large,

L(x)/x^{a}
is decreasing for all
x\geq\dfrac{1}{2}A
.
Moreover,
N-a-d>d
by assumption.
Therefore

|G\ast H(s)-H(s)|\lesssim A^{-(N-2d)}L(A/2).

∎

Proof of (
9.9
).

Assume
|s|\geq A/2
. Assume
A
is large enough that

L(x)
is increasing
and
L(x)/x^{a}
is decreasing for all
x\geq A/2
.
By (
9.4
) and because
G(0)=1
, we have

G\ast H(s)=H(s)+S_{1}+S_{2}

where

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|s-t|\geq A\\
|t|\leq|s|\end{subarray}}G(s-t)H(t),

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|s-t|\geq A\\
|t|\geq|s|\end{subarray}}G(s-t)H(t).

By (
9.7
),

|H(s)|\lesssim|s|^{-(N-d)}L(|s|)\lesssim A^{-(N-d)}L(2|s|).

By the assumption that

A^{-(N-d)}\leq D
, we have

|H(s)|\lesssim DL(2|s|).

By (
9.5
),

|S_{1}|\lesssim DL(2|s|)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|s-t|\geq A\\
|t|\leq|s|\end{subarray}}|H(t)|\leq DL(2|s|)\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|H(t)|.

Because
H
is bounded and because of (
9.3
) and (
9.7
),
the last sum is
\lesssim 1
.
Thus

|S_{1}|\lesssim DL(2|s|).

On the other hand, by (
9.5
) and (
9.7
),

|S_{2}|\lesssim D\sum_{|t|\geq|s|}|t|^{-(N-d-2a)}\dfrac{L(2|t|)}{(2|t|)^{a}}\dfrac{L(|t|)}{|t|^{a}}.

By (
9.3
) and because
N-d-2a>d
by assumption,

|S_{2}|\lesssim D|s|^{-(N-2d)}L(2|s|)L(|s|)\lesssim D|A|^{-(N-2d)}L(2|s|)L(A).

Using (
9.3
) and
N-d-2a>d
again, if
A
is large enough, we have

|S_{2}|\leq DL(2|s|).

∎

Proof of (
9.10
).

Assume
|s|\geq 2B
. Assume
A
is large enough that
L(x)
is increasing and
L(x)/x^{a}
is decreasing for all
x\geq A
.
By (
9.4
) and because
G(0)=1
, we have

G\ast H(s)=H(s)+S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3},

where

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\leq|t|<B\\
|s-t|\geq|s|/2\end{subarray}}G(t)H(s-t),

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|t|\geq|s|/2\\
|s-t|\leq|s|/2\end{subarray}}G(t)H(s-t),

\displaystyle S_{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|t|\geq B\\
|s-t|\geq|s|/2\end{subarray}}G(t)H(s-t).

By (
9.7
),

|H(s)|\lesssim|s|^{-(N-d)}L(|s|)\leq(|s|/2)^{-(N-d)}L(|s|/2).

Because of the assumption
B^{-(N-d)}\leq D
, we have

|H(s)|\lesssim D(B/|s|)^{N-d}L(|s|/2).

By (
9.5
) and (
9.7
),

\displaystyle|S_{1}|

\displaystyle\lesssim|s|^{-(N-d)}L(|s|/2)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\leq|t|<B\\
|s-t|\geq|s|/2\end{subarray}}|G(t)|

\displaystyle\lesssim|s|^{-(N-d)}L(|s|/2)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\leq|t|<B\\
|s-t|\geq|s|/2\end{subarray}}DL(|t|)

\displaystyle\lesssim D|s|^{-(N-d)}L(|s|/2)L(B)B^{d}

Since
L(x)/x^{a}
is decreasing for
x\geq A
and since
N-d>d+a
, we have

.
Moreover, by
(

),

.
Thus

|S_{1}|\lesssim D(B/|s|)^{N-d}L(|s|/2).

By (

),

\displaystyle|S_{2}|

\displaystyle\lesssim D(B/|s|)^{N}L(|s|/2)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|t|\geq|s|/2\\
|s-t|\leq|s|/2\end{subarray}}|H(s-t)|

Because

is bounded, because

is eventually decreasing, and because of (

), the last sum is bounded by a constant independent of

.
Thus

|S_{2}|\lesssim D(B/|s|)^{N-d}L(|s|/2).

By (
9.6
) and (

),

\displaystyle|S_{3}|

\displaystyle\lesssim|s|^{-(N-d)}L(|s|/2)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|t|\geq B\\
|s-t|\geq|s|/2\end{subarray}}|G(t)|

\displaystyle\lesssim DB^{N}|s|^{-(N-d)}L(|s|/2)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|t|\geq B\\
|s-t|\geq|s|/2\end{subarray}}|t|^{-(N-a)}\dfrac{L(|t|)}{|t|^{a}}.

Since

and since

is decreasing for

, the last sum is

. Therefore

|S_{3}|\lesssim D|s|^{-(N-d)}L(|s|/2)L(B)B^{d}.

Since
L(x)/x^{a}
is decreasing for

and since

, we have

L(B)B^{d}\leq L(A)A^{-a}B^{N-d}
.
Moreover, by (
9.3
),
L(A)A^{-a}\lesssim 1
.
Thus

|S_{3}|\lesssim D(B/|s|)^{N-d}L(|s|/2).

∎

10.
Hausdorff Dimension Upper Bound

Proposition 10.1
.

\dim_{H}(E(K,B,\tau))\leq 2d/(1+\tau)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
denote the set of all ideals of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
Note
E(K,B,\tau)
is invariant under translation by elements of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
because for
\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cong\mathcal{O}_{K}\subseteq I^{-1}
for every
I\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{O}_{K})
.
Thus it suffices to show that
E(K,B,\tau)\cap[-1/2,1/2)^{d}
has Hausdorff dimension at most
2d/(1+\tau)
.
Let
B(r,M)
denote the closed ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with center
r
and radius
M
.
Note

\displaystyle E(K,B,\tau)=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{O}_{K})\\
N(I)>n\end{subarray}}\bigcup_{r\in I^{-1}}B(r,N(I)^{-(1+\tau)/d}).

So, for every
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle E(K,B,\tau)\cap[-1/2,1/2)^{d}\subseteq\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{O}_{K})\\
N(I)>n\end{subarray}}\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in I^{-1}\\
|r|<2\end{subarray}}B(r,N(I)^{-(1+\tau)/d}).

Since
\mathcal{O}_{K}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, for every
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
the set
I^{-1}\cap(a+[0,1)^{d})
is a complete set of representatives for
I^{-1}/\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Thus the set of
r\in I^{-1}
with
|r|<2
is covered by at most
4^{d}
complete sets of representatives of
I^{-1}/\mathcal{O}_{K}
. By Lemma
3.3
,

|I^{-1}/\mathcal{O}_{K}|={N(\mathcal{O}_{K})}/{N(I^{-1})}=N(I)
.
Therefore the set of
r\in I^{-1}
such that
|r|<2
has size
\lesssim N(I)
. Thus, for every
s\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{O}_{K})\\
N(I)>n\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in I^{-1}\\
|r|<2\end{subarray}}\left({\text{diam}(B(r,N(I)^{-(1+\tau)/d}))}\right)^{s}\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{O}_{K})\\
N(I)>n\end{subarray}}N(I)^{1-s(1+\tau)/d}.

If
s>2d/(1+\tau)
, the last sum goes to zero as
n\to\infty
. (This is because the sum
\zeta_{K}(z):=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{O}_{K})}N(I)^{-z}
, known as the Dedekind zeta function, converges whenever
\operatorname{Re}(z)>1
; see
[
15
, Ch.10]
for a proof.)
Therefore the Hausdorff dimension of
E(K,B,\tau)\cap[-1/2,1/2]^{d}
is at most
2d/(1+\tau)
.
∎

11.
Appendix: modifications for
E_{\mathrm{prin}}(K,B,\tau)

We will describe the modifications necessary to prove that Theorem
1.2
holds for the set
E_{\mathrm{prin}}(K,B,\tau)
. The key
is to modify the argument to apply to
principal
ideals. This does not change the argument much because of the following standard fact from algebraic number theory.

Theorem 11.1

(Finiteness of the ideal class group)
.

Let
K
be an algebraic number field. Then there exists a finite collection
\mathcal{H}
of integral ideals such that if
I
is an ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, then there exists
H_{I}\in\mathcal{H}
such that the ideal
IH_{I}
is a principal ideal.

Using Theorem
11.1
, we define the following analog of
Q(M)
consisting of principal ideals:

Q_{\mathrm{prin}}(M)=\left\{IH_{I}:I\in Q(M/R)\right\}

where

R=\max\left\{{N((H_{1}^{-1}+H_{2}^{-1})^{-1}):H_{1},H_{2}\in\mathcal{H}}\right\}.

Then
Q_{\mathrm{prin}}(M)
is a collection of
\sim{M^{d}}/{\log M}
principal ideals of norm
\sim M^{d}
that are “almost” prime ideals.

Everywhere in the original argument, we replace
Q(M)
by
Q_{\mathrm{prin}}(M)
.
In particular, this means
J_{k}\in Q_{\mathrm{prin}}(M_{k})
.

Lemma
4.2
is replaced by the following lemma (which is actually a corollary of Lemma
4.2
).

Lemma 11.2
.

Let
J
be a nonzero integral ideal of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Then

(11.1)

|\{I\in Q_{\mathrm{prin}}(M):J\subseteq I\}|\leq\frac{\log(2N(J))}{d\log(M/R)}.

Proof.

This follows from Lemma
4.2
upon observing that

\displaystyle|\left\{{I\in Q_{\text{prin}}(M):J\subseteq I}\right\}|

\displaystyle=|\left\{{IH_{I}:I\in Q(M/R),J\subseteq IH_{I}}\right\}|

\displaystyle\leq|\left\{{IH_{I}:I\in Q(M/R),J\subseteq I}\right\}|

\displaystyle\leq|\left\{{I:I\in Q(M/R),J\subseteq I}\right\}|.

∎

The proof of Lemma
7.1
(c) must be changed because distinct ideals in
Q_{\mathrm{prin}}(M_{k})
may not be relatively prime.
To deal with this, we modify the definition of
\phi_{0}
so that its support is contained in
([-3/(8R^{1/d}),-1/(8R^{1/d})]\cap[1/(8R^{1/d}),3/(8R^{1/d})])^{d}
. Here is a restatement of Lemma
7.1
(c) and its new proof.

Lemma 11.3
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose
I_{1},I_{2}\in Q_{\mathrm{prin}}(M_{k})
,
r_{1}\in I_{1}^{-1},I_{2}^{-1}
.
Then
I_{1}=I_{1}^{\prime}H_{I_{1}^{\prime}}
and

for some

If

and

, then

r_{1}=r_{2}\in H_{I_{1}^{\prime}}^{-1}+H_{I_{2}^{\prime}}^{-1}

and (consequently)

\dist(r_{1},\mathrm{supp}(\phi_{0}))=\dist(r_{2},\mathrm{supp}(\phi_{0}))>3\eta_{k}

Since
I_{1}\neq I_{2}
, we must have
I_{1}^{\prime}\neq I_{2}^{\prime}
.
Thus
I_{1}^{\prime}
and
I_{2}^{\prime}
are distinct prime ideals and (hence) are relatively prime, i.e.,
1\in I_{1}^{\prime}+I_{2}^{\prime}

Set
r=r_{1}=r_{2}

Since

and

and

Therefore

.
Thus, if

, then

|r|^{d}\gtrsim N(r)\geq N(H_{I_{1}^{\prime}}^{-1}+H_{I_{2}^{\prime}}^{-1})\geq R^{-1}.

Since

is contained in
([-3/(8R^{1/d}),-1/(8R^{1/d})]\cap[1/(8R^{1/d}),3/(8R^{1/d})])^{d}

Note that (with the modifications to
