Heuristic centrality methods challenge greedy optimization in influence maximization
Authors: Laiali Almazaydeh, Arar Al Tawil, Amneh Shaban, Rabiah Al-Qudah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Influence maximization is a process where a small number of nodes are selected from social networks to maximize the spread of information. Many of the algorithms designed are supported by strong theory, but we do not know enough about their practical performance. This paper presents an extensive empirical analysis of eight representative algorithms from three paradigms: greedy optimization (CELF++), sampling-based approximation (RIS, IMM) and structural heuristics (Degree, PageRank, Betweenness, Dangling, and GCCDC). We tested Facebook social network subgraphs containing 200, 400, 600, 800, and 1,000 nodes through 10 independent runs under the Independent Cascade model for each configuration to obtain statistically reliable results. The findings indicate a significant disparity between theoretical guarantees and actual performance: despite achieving \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1-1/e)$$\end{document} of CELF++ approximation guarantee, CELF++ only achieves 11.2% of the spread attainable using sampling-based algorithms this gap is likely due to an insufficient number of Monte Carlo simulations and implementation constraints as opposed to a fundamental algorithmic flaw. Across all network scales, the sampling-based methods showed nearly optimal performance (IMM: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$955.24 \pm 0.74$$\end{document}; RIS: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$951.27 \pm 2.22$$\end{document}). Importantly, PageRank achieved 96.3% of IMM’s spread quality while being 794 times faster than the latter, which further proves that complex algorithms aren’t necessary for competing results. Betweenness centrality was found to perform as effectively as PageRank among new centrality measures while Dangling centrality and GCCDC performed as effectively as Degree centrality. All the differences in performance were found to be significantly different. The experimental results show that certain simple heuristics can outperform highly-optimized methods in practice and that theoretical guarantees do not always correlate with reality.

Introduction

Today, everything is being digitalized. The digital age has changed everything. One such thing is the social networks. Social networks have changed the way information is being transferred in today’s world. Social media sites such as Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, etc. all facilitate quick and widespread production and dissemination of content. Marketing strategies, public health communication, political mobilization, and misinformation suppression this disrupted1,2. Organizations with limited budgets face the practical challenge of finding a small number of highly influential people whose adoption or endorsement can create information cascades across the network. Viral marketing campaigns to encourage adoption of products make use of influential people to engage customers. Vaccination awareness initiatives leverage key community actors to encourage positive health behaviours. Political campaigns mobilise opinion leaders to sway voters. And misinformation control efforts target key nodes in the network to stem the flow of false narratives3. The effectiveness or ineffectiveness of any campaign largely depends upon optimally choosing such influencers. Thus, influence maximization holds much importance.

The influence maximization problem is formally defined as choosing a subset of (k) seed nodes in a social network such that its expected influence spread is as large as possible through a stochastic diffusion process. This refers to the fact that information, behaviours or innovations are transferred from person to person in a social network with some probabilities (not certain)4–6. Practitioners must strike a balance between high quality influence spread and computational feasibility, which necessitates designing algorithms that can deliver good results in a reasonable time period7.

Efforts are being made to strike a balance between efficiency and effectiveness, as seen by the evolution of such influence maximization algorithms. At one extreme, simple heuristics, such as Degree centrality and PageRank, can be computed in seconds. However, such algorithms may not identify the best (or near-best) seed set. On the flip side, sophisticated greedy algorithms guarantee robust theoretical performance but impose extremely costly computation, making their implementation limited to small networks5. Intermediate strategies, like modern sampling-based approaches, claim to merge the benefits of both extremes by providing a nearly optimal influence spread but much better runtime efficiency. The CELF++ optimization makes use of lazy evaluation so as not to perform calculations that do not contribute to the end result. It provides speedups of several hundred times over the naive implementation while maintaining approximation guarantees8.Despite the advancements in technology today, each of these algorithms improves by itself. Comparative empirical evaluation of these different algorithms under the same settings is very limited.

This is a key research gap due to the lack of systematic, comparative evaluations. Most of the existing studies focus on algorithms that are similar to or better than the existing baselines in a one network scenario9. As a result, professionals don’t have any explicit evidence-based directions on choosing algorithms that are well-tailored to their needs and constraints. When does relative simplicity of standard algorithms make them preferable to sophisticated algorithms? When might simple heuristics lead to adequately effective solutions?10 To what extent do these algorithms scale in relation to the size and structure of the network? It is unlikely we will bridge the theory-practice gap without detailed empirical assessments at different scales and with real world datasets that enable evidence-based decision-making in application.

In order to address this gap, this research work presents a comparative analysis of eight representative influence maximization algorithms which are falling under greedy, sampling-based and heuristic categories. The study used actual Facebook social network data and systematically varied the graph size from 200 to 1000 nodes to test for robustness and scale performance7. To guarantee statistical robustness, we perform ten independent runs for every experimental configuration followed by significance testing of observed differences. The evaluation of the criteria includes quality of spread of influence, run time, scalability, and trade-off between effectiveness and efficiency11. The objective is to offer practitioners practical guidance and evidence-based advice in the selection of algorithms; furthermore to examine theoretical assumptions in a social network context.

Related work

Kempe et al. first formalized the influence maximization problem 12, Contemporary social network applications have evolved from an academic issue to a practical need. The discussion will cover the greedy and sampling-based algorithms that laid the foundations for this discipline, up until new deep learning developments emerged. Foundational work and theoretical guarantees

Domingos and Richardson13,14 were the first to propose algorithms to find influencers in viral marketing. Kempe, Kleinberg, and Tardos12 provided a rigorous theoretical framework for research on the topic in question. In their research study, the authors proved that influence maximization is NP-hard under the Independent Cascade (IC) and Linear Threshold (LT) models. However, they also showed that because of the submodular structure of influence functions, a greedy algorithm has a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1-1/e)\approx 63\%$$\end{document} approximation guarantee15. This was the first result to show that due to the hardness of the problem, close to optimal solutions are possible. This has led to the development of algorithms for two decades and more. As a strengthening of the above statement, Mossel and Roch16 established similar guarantees for general threshold models. The journal version17 contains all the proofs and experiments that establish the Independent Cascade model as the accepted model for influence diffusion.

Although Kempe’s greedy algorithm is guaranteed theoretically, its O(knm) time complexity means that we have to perform expensive Monte Carlo simulations for every candidate node. This makes it impractical for large networks. In18, Leskovec et al. propose the Cost-Effective Lazy Forward (CELF) algorithm making use of submodularity’s diminishing return to get a staggering speedup of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$700\times$$\end{document} by way of lazy evaluation. CELF postpones costly influence calculations by keeping track of an upper bound on marginal gains, only recomputing them when needed. Goyal et al.19 further improved this with CELF++, which he claimed would provide an additional improvement of 35-55% through lazy-lazy evaluation.

Chen et al. significantly advanced scalability through multiple innovations. Their NewGreedy algorithm20 reused Monte Carlo simulations across iterations, while the DegreeDiscount heuristic offered millisecond-scale approximations for time-sensitive applications.To tackle networks with a million scale, the Maximum Influence Arborescence (MIA) framework21,22 has been developed, which achieves approximation through local trees rather than using global simulation. Variants of Linear Threshold model have received focused attention through SIMPATH23 and specialized algorithms24. Even with these improvements, greedy techniques proved to be too limited in their iterative structure, leading to a change to sampling-based ones.

Borgs et al.25 introduced Reverse Influence Sampling (RIS) which revolutionized the field, transforming influence maximization into a set cover problem. The approach for Reverse reachable Influence Sets (RIS) generates random reverse-reachable (RR) sets from the target nodes chosen at random instead of estimating the influence in the forward direction from the seed nodes. Time complexity of the RIS technique is reduced from O(knm) to almost linear time. Tang and others executed this model using TIM and TIM+26, These systems achieves unprecedented scalability, processing billion-edge graphs in under an hour, with approximation guarantees. The study demonstrated that looks can be functional and performance can be aesthetic.

The influential algorithm IMM (Influence Maximization via Martingales)27 improved the solution RIS through sampling theory based on martingales, which produced tighter theoretical guarantees together with better empirical performance. The sampling-based influence maximization method IMM became a de facto standard, resulting in many extensions. Nguyen et al.28 introduced SSA with optimal sampling checkpoints, though Huang et al.29 identified and corrected approximation guarantee issues in the original formulation. Tang et al.30 developed OPIM for online processing scenarios requiring early termination capabilities. Recent improvements include HIST31, which strengthens the theoretical bounds through subset sampling strategies. Sampling-based methods preside over large-scale applications these days because of their better speed, quality and theoretical guarantees.

Heuristic approaches and centrality-based methods

Heuristic methods, while lacking theoretical guarantees, are appealing for situations requiring quick results or where they can be used as a preprocessing filter. Degree-based methods32 select high-degree nodes, which are individuals who have a lot of connections. PageRank-based methods33,34 exploit the power-law influence distributions of social networks. Signed PageRank variants34 consider networks with positive and negative relationships. The iterative voting mechanism has been proposed in VoteRank35, where the selected nodes reduce the influence scores of their neighbours to escape redundant selections which take place in local neighbourhoods of the nodes.

Community-based techniques36–39 divide networks into modules and choose influential nodes in each community. They leverage network modularity to reduce computational complexity and obtain coverage at every edge of the network. These methods leverage the fast-provisioning aspect of simple heuristics, along with the quality improvement of structural awareness, at the expense of the theoretical guarantees provided by greedy and sampling-based methods.

Comparative studies and benchmarking

The proliferation of influence maximization algorithms necessitates rigorous comparative evaluation. Arora et al.40 We performed the largest benchmark study carried out to date involving 11 algorithms. We tested these algorithms on multiple diffusion models and network datasets. The researchers found no single one of the existing state-of-the-art techniques performs best on all metrics. This finding casts doubt on the notion of “winner” algorithms and implies the importance of matching an algorithm to the requirements of specific applications.Lu et al.41 have questioned the experimental methodologies along with the discussion over evaluation metrics. The suite of Influence Maximization Benchmarker42 has standardized tools for evaluation reproducing. Jung et al.43 and Ohsaka et al.44 provide analyses of algorithms that substantiate the speed-memory-likeness trade-offs. The set of SNAP dataset collections45 provides standard benchmarks like Twitter (41.6M nodes, 1.4B edges), LiveJournal (5M nodes, 69M edges), Orkut (3M nodes, 117M edges). So that other papers can compare with their work.

Recent trends: deep learning and machine learning approaches

One of the most important developments of late concerns the application of deep reinforcement learning and graph neural networks to influence maximization. Li et al.46 developed PIANO, a system that employs reinforcement learning to establish seed selection policies, tailored to specific network characteristics without the need for hand-crafted heuristics. Ling et al.47 proposed deep graph representation learning frameworks that optimize node embeddings and influence estimation jointly. Chen and colleagues48 and Yang and colleagues49 proved deep reinforcement learning can achieve a competitive performance as theoretical algorithms while generalizing better across network types.

Graph neural network approaches50–52 learn influence and strategies directly from the structure of the network, enabling techniques like transfer learning for similar networks and amortization of training costs for multiple influence maximization instances. These methods are moving away from the direction of algorithm design towards that of learned optimization, although they presently lack the classical guarantees. Therefore, fairness-aware extensions53,54 have been suggested in order to ensure fairness under them.

Research gaps and motivation

There are still important gaps despite extensive research. Many existing researches propose a new algorithm as the sole contribution and include only fewer baselines with a direct comparison with only the immediate predecessor limited only to one network. There hasn’t been any extensive empirical study with statistical rigor to compare greedy optimization (CELF++), sampling-based methods (RIS, IMM), and simple heuristics (Degree, PageRank) over a wide range of scales. Moreover, theoretical complexity does not always predict practical performance, because hidden constants, dependencies on network structure, and implementation matter. To systematically evaluate algorithms from different paradigms, we conduct a rigorous statistical analysis over simulation and real social network data. Problem formulation

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G = (V, E)$$\end{document} be a social network such that V is the node set (users) with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|V| = n$$\end{document} and E is the edge set (social connections) with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|E| = m$$\end{document} The influence maximization problem focuses on the determination of a seed set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S \subseteq V$$\end{document} of size k that will maximize the expected influence spread \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma (S)$$\end{document} according to a diffusion model BP or some other model. In our analysis we utilize the Independent Cascade (IC) model12 which stochastically spreads information throughout the network. We associate with each edge (u,v) in E an influence probability p_uv, the probability that node u activates node v. At the start of the diffusion process, one activates all the alternate nodes in S. Upon arriving at step t, each newly activated node \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u$$\end{document} at step \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t-1$$\end{document} tries to activate each of the inactive neighbors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v$$\end{document} with the probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{uv}$$\end{document}. The process terminates when no more activations occur. The spread of influence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma (S)$$\end{document} is the expected number of activated nodes by the end of the diffusion.

Algorithms

We examine eight different algorithms that span three different paradigms and discuss their advantages. As depicted in Fig. 1, the experimental workflow of the study.

CELF++ (cost-effective lazy forward plus plus)

CELF++19 Improves the greedy algorithm using lazy evaluation. Instead of recomputing marginal gains for every candidate in every iteration, CELF++ maintains an upper bound on marginal gains and only recomputes when necessary. The algorithm maintains a priority queue of candidate nodes with estimates of their marginal gain. When the i-th seed is chosen, nodes are listed in decreasing order of upper bounds. In case the upper bound of a node was obtained for the current seed set (i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{flag}$$\end{document} = \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i-1$$\end{document}), then it gets selected immediately. Otherwise, its marginal gain gets recomputed. This was observed to achieve speedups of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$700\times$$\end{document} over naive greedy but with the same \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1-1/e)$$\end{document} guarantee18.

RIS (reverse influence sampling)

RIS25,26 takes a new approach which transforms influence maximization into set coverage problem using RR sets. For a target node v selected at random, the RR set of v comprises of every node which can reach v through reverse edges sampled by influence probabilities. The algorithm constructs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document} RR sets in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {R}$$\end{document}. The value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document} is determined through theoretical analysis to guarantee approximation. The selection of seeds thus amounts to the identification of k nodes that cover the most number of RR sets. This is carried out greedily. The reasoning behind this is that nodes covering expansive RR sets are likely to induce propagation to multiple nodes. RIS attains a near-linear time complexity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O((k+\ell )(n+m)\log n / \epsilon ^2)$$\end{document} for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1-1/e-\epsilon )$$\end{document}-approximation.

IMM (influence maximization via martingales)

IMM27Improves RIS via Martingale-Based Concentration Inequalities and Adaptive Sampling. While RIS generates a predetermined number of RR sets, IMM modifies the sample size based on its observations of the coverage pattern. The algorithm calculates:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \theta = \frac{(2 + \frac{2}{3}\epsilon )(\log n + \log 2)n}{\epsilon ^2} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon$$\end{document} controls the approximation accuracy. This adaptive approach tightens theoretical bounds while improving practical performance, making IMM the current standard for sampling-based influence maximization.

Degree centrality

The Degree heuristic chooses the most k connected nodes based on the idea that a highly connected node has a good influence. We can express degree centrality as d(v)=|N(v)|. Here, N(v) is the neighbor set. In other words, it is an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(n+m)$$\end{document} algorithm which has no theoretical guarantees, runs quickly, and gives us a faster baseline.

PageRank centrality

The way PageRank function models influence as a stationary distribution of a random walk on the network33. The PageRank score PR(v) for node v satisfies:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} PR(v) = \frac{1-\alpha }{n} + \alpha \sum _{u \in N_{in}(v)} \frac{PR(u)}{d_{out}(u)} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The damping factor of is denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha = 0.85$$\end{document} and is equal to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{1}{|N_{in}(v)|}$$\end{document}. Furthermore, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{in}(v)$$\end{document} denotes the incoming neighbours, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{out}(u)$$\end{document} is the out-degree of u. We choose nodes with the top k PageRank scores. This heuristic captures a network’s global structure, which is beyond mere connectivity. Hence it may help identify influential nodes that aren’t positioned in a very obvious way.

Using version 2.6 of the library NetworkX with 0.85 as the damping factor, we used the power iteration method to compute the PageRank scores with a convergence tolerance of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-6}$$\end{document} and maximum no. 100 iterations. The iterative method takes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(T(n+m))$$\end{document} time to execute, where T is the number of iterations until convergence (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T<50$$\end{document} for social networks). Our study confirms the applicability of PageRank computation for time-critical applications. For all considered graph sizes, PageRank computation converged within 0.5 seconds.

Betweenness centrality

Betweenness centrality refers to the bridging nodes that lie on the shortest path between other nodes. It measures the ability of nodes to connect all others55. For a node v, the betweenness centrality is defined as:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} BC(v) = \sum _{s \ne v \ne t} \frac{\sigma _{st}(v)}{\sigma _{st}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}In the above expression \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _{st}$$\end{document} denotes the total number of shortest paths from node s to node t. Whereas, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _{st}(v)$$\end{document} refers to a number of those paths that pass through v. In our implementation, we compute betweenness centrality via the NetworkX library using Brandes’ algorithm56 with time complexity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(n \cdot m)$$\end{document}. The nodes with the first k highest betweenness scores are selected as seeds.

Dangling centrality

The removal of a link from a network can affect network connectivity. It measures this effect. This measure is called dangling centrality and it is used to measure network stability55,57 For each node v, the dangling centrality DC(v) is defined as:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} DC(v) = \frac{1}{\deg (v)} \sum _{(v,u) \in E} \left[ C_{\text {before}}(u) - C_{\text {after}}(u) \right] \end{aligned}$$\end{document}In this expression, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{\text {after}}(u)$$\end{document} refers to the connectivity score once the edge (v, u) is removed, whilst \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{\text {before}}(u)$$\end{document} is the connectivity score of neighbour u with node v present. This metric identifies important nodes whose removal would greatly disrupt information flow. Our implementation using degree centrality as a proxy to assess connectivity impact runs in time of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(n \cdot \bar{d})$$\end{document} where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{d}$$\end{document} is the average degree.

Global clustering coefficient-dependent degree centrality (GCCDC)

GCCDC combines the global clustering coefficient with degree centrality to create a comprehensive measure of node importance that takes into account not just local connectivity, but also the global clustering structure of the network58. For each node v:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} GCCDC(v) = DC(v) \times (1 + GCC \times LC(v)) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$DC(v) = \deg (v)/(n-1)$$\end{document} is the degree centrality, GCC is the global clustering coefficient of the network (transitivity), and LC(v) is the local clustering coefficient of node v. The idea is that in networks with high clustering, things would be easier for the nodes that have a dense cluster community.

Experimental setup

Dataset

For the project we use Facebook social network dataset45 which has 4,039 nodes and 88,234 undirected edges which represent friendships. We sample subgraphs with the number of nodes set to 200, 500, 800, and 1000 via importance sampling for a systematic study of scalability. The significance of a node is the average of its normalized degree centrality and normalized PageRank scores. To ensure that the selected nodes are connected, the top-ranked nodes are chosen by importance and their maximum importance neighbours are included. The strategy acts to filter the networks’ influence structure, allowing for scalabilities and experiments. Parameters

All experiments utilize the Independent Cascade model, in which the propagation probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p=0.1$$\end{document} is uniform on all edges. This is standard practice (Kempe et al., 2003, Tang et al., 2015). The seed budget k = 10, which is a reasonable limitation for viral marketing applications. To balance accuracy and computational cost, any assessment of the impact would entail using 50 Monte Carlo simulations. The specific parameters for every algorithm are specified here. CELF++ performs 50 simulations to estimate the marginal gain. For architecture RIS the RR sets are given by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta = \min (10000, 50n)$$\end{document}. For adaptive sampling we set the IMM sets \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon = 0.2$$\end{document}. To ensure reliability, we run all configurations independently over 10 runs with different random seeds (42–51).

In our experiment, we will be clear about what is being repeated at two levels. To begin with, influence evaluation runs 50 Monte Carlo simulation to compute an estimate of the expected spread \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma (S)$$\end{document} for seed set S. Every simulation of Monte Carlo runs the IC diffusion process from the seed set once only, and average number of activated nodes in 50 simulations gives a good estimate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma (S)$$\end{document}. Second, 10 independent runs of each algorithm are performed using different random seeds (42–51). Every independent run generates a potentially different seed set because, due to stochasticity in algorithms, e.g. random RR set generation in RIS/IMM, Monte Carlo estimation variance in CELF++. Thus, for sake of every algorithm-graph size configuration, we obtain 10 influence spread values. Each of these 10 values is estimated from the algorithm’s 50 Monte Carlo simulations. Hence, we have robust statistical measures (mean, standard deviation, coefficient of variation) for comparison of our estimates.

All algorithms were implemented in Python within a unified experimental framework to ensure fair comparison under identical computational conditions, including shared data structures, random number generation, and Independent Cascade simulation code.

Evaluation metrics

The main performance metric we use is influence spread (i.e., the mean number of activated nodes) taken over 50 Monte Carlo simulations. The S Objective gives an overall score of how well the algorithm succeeded in locating a set of nodes without using the coverage measure. Computational time is defined as the elapsed time in seconds between when the algorithm starts until the last seed set output. In this way, we can see how efficient the algorithm is. The scalability increases the performance of the algorithm. As the graph size continues to increase, the speed of performance is capable of giving a full picture of what the algorithm does. Statistical Evaluations of the 10 Runs The mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, and coefficient of variation (CV) for these 10 runs are all calculated. To assess the significance paired t-tests (for the treatment conditions) with Bonferroni (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha =0.05$$\end{document}) correction for multiple comparisons.

Implementation details

In Python 3.8, all algorithms are implemented using NumPy 1.21 for numerical operations and NetworkX 2.6 to build a graph. To reduce run time, vectorized operations are used in the Independent Cascade simulation. To implement priority queues for greedy algorithms we use Python’s heapq module Methods such as RIS, IMM are essentially set-based sampling techniques that utilize efficient set operations and thus allow efficient RR set generation and coverage computation. The experiments are performed on a standard computer with an Intel Core i7 processor and 16G RAM that can be repeated on multiple commodity hardware. The initialization of all random number generators remains consistent across simulations to ensure replicability.

Computational efficiency

Our method achieves high efficiency at all four levels according to the results. Heuristic methods (Degree, PageRank, and Dangling) are the fastest tier and take 0.5 seconds to execute on all networks. The second ring of mid-range heuristics is Betweenness centrality (0.3-8.8 seconds)/GCCDC (0.3-2.9 seconds). These heuristics produce a reasonably better spread quality at moderate implementation cost. The third group consisted of algorithms that use sampling: for RIS the running time was 11.8–64.9 seconds while for IMM it was 11.8–323.5 seconds. The CELF++ algorithm required 24.8 seconds to 688.4 seconds of computation time, which was the highest among all tested algorithms. Moreover, spread quality was the worst among all algorithms in the case of CELF++.

The efficiency metric (spread per second, Fig. 3c) clearly indicates the quality-speed trade-off. The fastest heuristics achieved the highest efficiency (i.e., PageRank performed 2304 spread/sec at 1000 nodes, followed by Degree (2027 spread/sec) and Dangling (1994 spread/sec). Betweenness centrality attained an 104.75 spread per second while GCCDC attained a 279.86 spread per second, offering a good quality and speed trade-off. With 14.68 spread/second, RIS competently balanced quality and speed. Similarly, the absolute spread was on the maximum side for IMM but at lowest efficiency amongst the competitive algorithms (2.95 spread/second). The spread of CELF++ was 0.18 spread/second which makes it have a high runtime and low-quality spread.

Within the important configuration of 1000 nodes, PageRank rendered a spread of 921.78 in 0.4 seconds (2,304 spread/sec) while RIS achieved a spread of 952.53 in 64.9 seconds (14.68 spread/sec). This is a 3.3% quality improvement for a 162-fold increase in runtime, making PageRank very cost-effectiveness for those after near-optimal solutions. Betweenness centrality is a solution that offers an intermediate option. It achieves a spread of 921.80 in 8.8 seconds virtually the same quality as PageRank. However, the item is at a higher cost. This cost may be justified when identifying bridge-nodes is valuable in its own right.

Algorithm-specific observations

Sampling-based methods (RIS, IMM)

Sampling-based methods are supported by the theoretical predictions validated by reverse influence sampling and martingale-based concentration inequalities. The marginal superiority of IMM (0.25% on average) is consistent with its tighter theoretical bounds, derived from martingale concentration inequalities. Both methods exhibited very low variance (std < 2.72 for all configurations), an indication of algorithmic stability.

The gap in runtime between RIS and IMM increased as the network scaled up: IMM at 1000 nodes took 5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} longer than RIS (323.5s vs 64.9s). These high computational costs entail meager quality gains, suggesting RIS can be chosen when quality differences are negligible.

Global heuristic methods (PageRank, betweenness centrality)

The substantial difference between PageRank and simple Degree centrality indicates the importance of the overall network structure over its local connectivity. The performance gap grew as the size of the network grew. In a network of 1000 nodes, for example, PageRank achieved a spread of 921.78, while Degree’s spread was 810.87 PageRank scored 13.7% better overall. Although the computational cost remained the same (0.4 s), PageRank is strictly better than Degree for this problem.

Betweenness centrality55,56 emerged as a strong competitor, achieving 921.80 spread at 1000 nodes statistically indistinguishable from PageRank (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p = 0.99$$\end{document}). Betweenness identifies bridge nodes lying on shortest paths between node pairs, capturing a fundamentally different aspect of network structure compared to PageRank’s random walk model. The near-identical performance suggests that in networks with high clustering coefficients, bridge nodes and structurally central nodes overlap significantly. However, Betweenness requires 8.8 seconds compared to PageRank’s 0.4 seconds due to its \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(n \cdot m)$$\end{document} computational complexity, making PageRank the preferred choice when runtime is critical and Betweenness the preferred choice when bridge-node identification has independent value.

The performance of PageRank and Betweenness in comparison to advanced sampling methods is notable. For real-time seed selection or large networks, PageRank offers an attractive quality, speed and simplicity trade-off, while Betweenness provides equivalent spread quality at a moderate computational cost.

Local heuristic methods (degree, dangling centrality, GCCDC)

Dangling centrality, Degree centrality55,57, and GCCDC58 formed a lower-performing cluster among heuristic methods. At 1000 nodes, the spread of GCCDC (811.59) and Degree (810.87) was almost the same. Among all heuristics, Dangling centrality (797.44) was the lowest.

The evaluation of stability in a network due to link deletion is referred to as dangling centrality. The dangling centrality heuristic had the highest CV (up to 6.24% at 200 nodes). The metric focuses on the impact of removing an edge and not on the global routes through which influence takes place. As the scale increases (say, from 100 to 500 nodes), other heuristics tend to have greater performance than ours. In fact, for 500 nodes, our heuristic ranks 5th with a spread of 457.82. This suggests that link-removal-based importance has more information to offer in intermediate scale networks with moderate density.

GCCDC utilizes the degree centrality58, which is the global clustering coefficient, in an attempt to provide users with a richer alternative to pure degree because it accounts for clustering. The marginal difference in the performance of Degree centrality (811.59 as compared to 810.87 at 1000 nodes) suggests that including such a structure in the clustering format may not help much in such an IC model. This is in line with the observation that metrics that are designed to detect clustering capture mostly redundant influence paths within strong communities, which matter less to influence spread than bridges between communities.

Greedy method (CELF++)

Our findings suggest that CELF++ is significantly under-performing. Even after guaranteeing and claiming to be 700 times faster than the naive greedy, CELF++ performs much worse than all the other algorithms. There appears to be a myriad of causes.

Monte Carlo simulations are not enough

The approximation guarantee in CELF++ assumes that we can evaluate the influence function exactly, which is not the case in practice. Our marginal gain estimation uses only 50 Monte Carlo simulations which is much less than the 10,000+ used in standard practice19. This generates a huge variance which may cause the lazy evaluation to wrongly rank the nodes which selects non-optimal seeds. This can explain most effectively the observed violation of the theoretical bound.

The use of the Python 3.8 programming language imposes a significant computational overhead because the CELF++ algorithm is required to use iterative loops. A compiled implementation (C++, say) would likely reduce run time by one to two orders of magnitude and also allow for larger Monte Carlo budgets in a practical time to obtain solutions.

When only 50 simulations are performed at each evaluation stage, these marginal gains can become very variable. The lazy constraints of CELF++ can inaccurately rank nodes and select candidates with local inferiority.

The power-law degree distribution with high clustering found in the Facebook network can worsen the weakness of greedy algorithms. Choosing high degree seeds at an early stage can result in the depletion of influence potential at local nodes, which in turn means that the marginal contribution of later choice becomes trivial.

Importance-based graph sampling refers to our sampling strategy that selects top nodes by their degree and PageRank score. Thus, the sub-graph in our sampling may become denser around the high-centrality nodes. This has the potential to create a fake favour on heuristic methods while interfering with the long-path structures which greedy algorithms exploit. The relative performance may differ with the use of alternative sampling strategies, such as random walk or forest fire sampling.

The algorithm doesn’t seem to be consistently biased; rather, it appears to be unstable. The performance of CELF++ in our experimental setup (50 MC simulations, Python implementation, importance-based sampling) shall not be misinterpreted to indicate that the CELF++ algorithm is indeed fundamentally faulty. With a large enough Monte Carlo budget, optimised implementation and unbiased graph sampling, CELF++ is expected to perform with its theoretical guarantee. Our results demonstrate that greedy optimisation is sensitive to implementation parameters and experimental design choices.

Discussion

Theoretical vs. empirical performance

Our findings highlight a significant gap between theory and practice in influence maximization. The CELF++ algorithm holds a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1-1/e) \approx 63\%$$\end{document} approximation guarantee inherited from the greedy algorithm12,15, yet achieved only 12.9% of IMM’s spread in our experiments. However, this discrepancy does not necessarily invalidate the theoretical bound. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1-1/e)$$\end{document} guarantee assumes exact evaluation of the influence function, whereas our implementation uses only 50 Monte Carlo simulations per marginal gain estimation far below the 10,000+ typically required for reliable lazy evaluation. This insufficient simulation budget introduces substantial variance that can cause incorrect node rankings and suboptimal seed selection, effectively violating the preconditions under which the theoretical guarantee holds.

More broadly, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1-1/e)$$\end{document} guarantee is insensitive to (1) errors arising from Monte Carlo influence evaluation, (2) tie-breaking decisions, or (3) the way lazy evaluation heuristics interact with the network structure. When these practical factors are present as they inevitably are in real deployments the gap between theoretical bounds and observed performance can be substantial. Our findings suggest that practitioners should verify that implementation parameters satisfy the assumptions underlying theoretical guarantees before relying on them for algorithm selection.

On the other hand, sampling-based methods (RIS, IMM) have theory to support them and are also effective in practice. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1-1/e-\epsilon )$$\end{document} approximation26,27 translates into consistent high-quality solutions, validating the shift in paradigm from greedy to sampling-based methods. Crucially, sampling-based methods embed their Monte Carlo estimation directly into the algorithmic framework (via reverse-reachable sets), making them inherently more robust to estimation variance than greedy methods that rely on external influence evaluations.

The surprising effectiveness of simple heuristics

The competitiveness of PageRank questions the need for elaborate algorithms for influence maximization. Obtaining 96.3% of the spread of IMM for only \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$794\times$$\end{document} is a strong indication that the complexity of algorithms does not necessarily justified its cost. PageRank is effective because it looks globally at nodes. In contrast to Degree centrality, which merely studies direct connectivity, PageRank considers the indirect paths of influencers through a random walk. When the connectivity forms clustering in social networks, it captures more important global structure than marginal gains through netting. This finding is relevant to practitioners; in real-time marketing, emergency response, and breaking news diffusion, PageRank provides instantaneous near-optimal seed selection. The performance loss of 3.7% to IMM may not be significant in context of sub-second execution.

Scalability considerations

The near-linear growth of sampling-based methods confirms their practicality for large networks. Based on our findings, we estimate that RIS could take about 795 seconds (or 13 min) to process a 10,000 nodes network while IMM could do so in about 3970 s (or 66 min). Modern algorithms can be made tractable even for networks containing millions of nodes (such as social media networks) with enough power.

Nonetheless, as the network size continues to increase, the performance gap between RIS and IMM results becomes super-linear. The adaptive sampling method of IMM is worst-case optimal but has greater overhead than necessary. There is a point where the fixed-sampling strategy of RIS starts to make sense with a lower theoretical bound.

The runtime scaling of CELF++ was found to be prohibitive. A linear extrapolation would give 840 s for 1000 nodes and over 8,400 s (2.3 h) for 10,000 nodes. CELF++ has no advantages over sampling-based methods due to its poor solution quality.

Implications for algorithm selection

Based on our comprehensive evaluation, we provide the following evidence-based recommendations:

Quality-critical applications: use IMM for marketing campaigns with substantial budgets, public health interventions, with high stakes notwithstanding high computational cost. The algorithm boasts a mean spread of 954.90 at 1000 nodes and exceptional stability (CV < 0.1%) that warrant its use for applications where influence maximization directly impacts utility.

Balanced quality-efficiency scenarios: use RIS. Achieving 99.6% of IMM’s quality at one-fifth the runtime makes RIS the practical choice for most real-world applications.

Time-critical or large-scale applications: use PageRank or Betweenness centrality. PageRank’s minimal computational cost (under 0.5 seconds for 1000 nodes) and 96.3% spread quality relative to IMM make it the ideal choice when speed is paramount. Betweenness centrality achieves statistically equivalent spread (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p = 0.99$$\end{document}) in 8.8 seconds, and may be preferred when bridge-node identification provides independent analytical value.

Moderate-quality rapid deployment: use degree, GCCDC, or dangling centrality. These heuristics execute in under 3 seconds and achieve 80–85% of IMM’s spread at 1000 nodes, suitable for exploratory analysis or scenarios where approximate solutions are acceptable.

Exercise caution with greedy optimization: our results show that CELF++ substantially underperformed under our experimental configuration (50 Monte Carlo simulations, Python implementation, importance-based graph sampling). However, this should not be interpreted as evidence that greedy methods are fundamentally flawed. Rather, practitioners deploying CELF++ or similar greedy algorithms should ensure (1) a sufficient Monte Carlo simulation budget (10,000+ per evaluation step), (2) an optimised compiled implementation to enable higher simulation budgets within practical time constraints, and (3) unbiased graph sampling strategies. Without validated configurations meeting these requirements, greedy methods may not realise their theoretical potential and sampling-based alternatives (RIS, IMM) are recommended instead.

Limitations and future directions

The limitations must be noted. First, our experiment was performed on a single real-world social network (Facebook) but at various scales. The algorithmic performance of different types of networks is likely to vary due to the nature of their structural properties. The algorithmic performances of different network topologies will differ, for example, directed networks such as Twitter, weighted networks with heterogeneous edge strengths, or dynamic networks, where the edges change over time. For example, directed networks may favor PageRank more because it is inherently built to model directional flow of influence, while weighted networks may affect the relative effectiveness of degree-based heuristics. In the future, the comparative framework we provided should be complemented, with the use of additional network types, like directed graphs (Twitter, citation networks), weighted networks (email communication, collaboration networks), and temporal networks. Varying the propagation probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p \in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5\}$$\end{document} of edges or using edge specific probabilities based on the properties of the network (like using inverse degree weighting) will also help evaluate robustness of the algorithms better.

Second, we implemented the Independent Cascade model having uniform propagation probability (p = 0.1). Influence in the real world varies across edges, depending on co-relationship strength, content type and time. By extending this comparison to weighted cascade models or other diffusion models (Linear Threshold, triggering models), it could be more easily applied. The performance ranking from Linear Threshold (LT) model12 essentially dictates that we might arrive at different kinds of performance ranking when dealing with the use of some algorithm. In this case, we are talking about algorithms that essentially identifies nodes with some kind of different neighbourhood coverage. In other words, algorithms which identifies the nodes where the most propagated nodes are present rather than those which are highly central. Weighted cascade variants where propagation probabilities are proportionate to inverse degree \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(p_{uv} = 1/d_{in}(v))$$\end{document} better capture real-world heterogeneous influence. This may also change the effectiveness of heuristics. In the future comparison of multiple diffusion models, we will see if this performance hierarchy is model dependent or a fundamental characteristic of the algorithms. In particular, the superiority of PageRank, and inferiority of CELF++.

Also, the seed budget \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k = 10$$\end{document} refers to one operating point. When the seed set is too small \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k = 1$$\end{document}–3 or too big \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k > 50$$\end{document}, performance characteristics can differ; in particular, heuristics are sometimes more efficient than optimised algorithms and vice versa.

In addition, although our experiments using networks ranging from 200 to 1,000 nodes reached similar scalability results, real-world social network platforms operate at scales from millions to billions of nodes. RIS has an extrapolation to run 10,000-node networks in 13 minutes and run PageRank in 5 seconds according to our runtime extrapolations, but these analyses must be validated experimentally at larger scales. When dealing with such scales, constraints of memory, cache efficiency and opportunities of parallelization can change. Future work should apply these algorithms to large-scale benchmark datasets like LiveJournal (5M nodes), Orkut (3M nodes) and Twitter (41.6M nodes) from the SNAP collection45 to check whether the performance hierarchy holds for web-scale networks.

Moreover, recent advances in deep reinforcement learning (DRL) and graph neural networks (GNNs) have introduced promising alternatives for influence maximization46–48,50. These methods learn seed selection policies directly from network structure, potentially capturing complex patterns that hand-crafted algorithms may miss. PIANO46, for example, adapts seed selection for specific network characteristics without manual heuristic design using reinforcement learning. Though these methods show competitive performance, they necessitate a long training time and heavy computational power (GPU infrastructure), and cannot provide the theoretical guarantees of sampling-based methods yet. A direct performance comparison between our evaluated algorithms and DRL/GNN-based approaches would be interesting to assess the training overhead versus performance at inference time. Nevertheless, such a comparison should take into consideration the fairness in evaluation. The reason being, the learning based methods amortize the computational cost across different problem instance. Traditional algorithms on the other hand solve each instance independently. This is a key avenue for future research.

One methodological limitation that warrants a discussion concerns the reported CELF++ results. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1-1/e)$$\end{document} approximation guarantee provided by the greedy algorithm assumes an exact evaluation of the influence function, but when we implement this in practice, we obtain the marginal gain by estimating it using 50 Monte Carlo simulations. The CELF++ algorithm is commonly executed at least 10,000 times before every evaluation step to guarantee that the highest marginal gain nodes are selected correctly by the lazy evaluation mechanism. The underperformance of CELF++ is most likely a result of the lack of simulation budget, not a failure of algorithm. The implementation of all algorithms was done in Python 3.8, which imposes significant computational overhead due to the iterative loops required by CELF++. A compiled implementation (e.g., C++) would more likely give CELF++ a run-time advantage of one to two orders of magnitude, which would make run-time results language rather than algorithm dependent. Our importance-based graph sampling strategy selects nodes according to their degree and PageRank score ranking. This means there may be selection bias caused by our strategy. This bias may favor centrality-based heuristics since our sampling strategy may densify the subgraph around high-degree nodes. Our findings are robust to the manner in which the graph is extracted. Future work should evaluate alternative sampling strategies such as random walk or forest fire sampling. Future work to investigate CELF++ should involve orders of magnitude higher Monte Carlo budgets (e.g., 10,000), optimized compiled implementations, and unbiased graph sampling, to assess whether the performance mismatch persists under typical conditions. In particular, publicly available C++ implementations such as the CELF framework by Gupta et al. (https://github.com/NikhilGupta1997/Influence-Maximization-CELF) could serve as a starting point for cross-language performance comparison, which remains an important direction for future work.

Finally, we acknowledge that English is not the first language of all authors, and despite careful proofreading, some linguistic imperfections may remain. We welcome suggestions for improving the clarity and readability of the manuscript.

Future work should study the underperformance of CELF++ in detail using algorithm traces. Moreover, there may be hybrid approaches that use PageRank seed initialization and sampling for refined targeted sampling. Similarly, dynamic influence maximization with the evolving structure of the network is of interest. Finally, fairness in influence maximization to achieve similar coverage in various groups is yet another direction for future work53,54.

Broader implications

The consequences of our findings go further. A good example of this gap is the performance guarantees given by CELF for static allocations and custom author-data constraints. In complexity analysis of algorithms, one gets asymptotic guarantees, but an algorithm’s actual performance depends on the constant of proportionality and other details. These details are important because they are interacting with actual distributions of data.

The finding that simple heuristics like PageRank often work better than complex optimization algorithms like CELF++ and sometimes even sampling methods is echoed in many fields. According to40 simple heuristics outperform sophisticated models. When algorithms are prescribed, empirical evaluation as well as theoretical analysis should be taken into account.

Ultimately, the increasing domination of sampling techniques also affirms a more recent algorithmic trend in combinatorial optimization: Reformulating an optimization problem as a sampling or coverage problem often yields both theoretical guarantees and practical efficiency improvements. This model has been successfully utilized in numerous fields such as influence maximization, submodular optimization and probabilistic inference and can be effectively used in future research.

Centrality-based heuristic analysis

In addition to Degree centrality, the inclusion of Betweenness, Dangling, and GCCDC centrality will also be studied to gain more insight into the effect of different node importance metrics on their ability to propagate influence.

Betweenness centrality: the centrality-based heuristic emerged as the strongest among the ones we proposed in SATIC. It achieved 96.5% of the performance of IMM in case of 1000 nodes (921.80 vs. 954.90). It only takes 8.8 s, which is much lower than IMM’s running time of 323.5 s. The proximity of PageRank value (921.78) indicates there is considerable overlap between bridge nodes as per betweenness centrality and structurally important nodes as per PageRank in social networks with high clustering coefficient. The stability of betweenness-based seed selection is confirmed by the low coefficient of variation.

Dangling centrality: their performance was average and ranked between 5th to 7th in terms of graph sizes, when it has a spread between 75.8% to 83.5% of IMM. The greater variability (CV of 6.24% with 200 nodes) implies sensitivity to short-term network structure. The focus of this metric on edge-removal impact captures the local disruption potential rather than the global influence pathways.

GCCDC: across all scales of graphs, achieved a spread of IMM between 82.0%–93.1 %. The global clustering coefficient (GCC) was combined with local clustering to give slightly better performance than pure degree centrality at larger scales (811.59 vs. 810.87 at 1000 nodes). So, it can be said that clustering-aware metrics offer little additional gains in influence maximization under the IC model with uniform propagation probability. Conclusion

An empirical study comparing eight influence maximization algorithms at multiple scales is presented in this paper. We run each configuration 10 times independently on the Facebook subgraphs (with 200–1000 nodes) and show statistically significant differences in the performance. We also show that practical assessments often ignored in theoretical analyses matter a lot.Recent studies show that sampling-based methods (RIS, IMM) yield near-optimum performance which is stable (CV <0.25%). In our configuration, CELF++ did not succeed at all with only 12.9% of IMM’s spread at 1000 nodes. This poor performance is likely due to running too few Monte Carlo simulations (50 vs. 10,000+ recommended), Python overhead, and that lazy evaluation is sensitive to importance-based sampling. We have no reason to believe that CELF++ fails basically. This begs the question of using the greedy optimization without tuning many parameters. The PageRank heuristic achieves 96.3% accuracy with respect to IMM, while being 794\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} faster than IMM at runtime. Hence, PageRank is suitable for applications with a time-critical requirement. The Betweenness centrality compared to PageRank had a great effectiveness of 96.5% of IMM. The performance is at a moderate cost. The Dangling centrality and GCCDC had a comparable performance to the Degree centrality. The gap between theoretical guarantees and empirical performance of CELF++ stemmed from the implementation parameters used, the budget for simulation and the sampling employed for the simulation. The success of PageRank shows that simple heuristics that leverage the global structure of the network can yield competitive results if the global structure of the network provides strong signals about node importance. For practitioners, the IMM method is advised for applications where quality is critical, the RIS method where a quality-efficiency balance is desired, and the PageRank or Betweenness Centrality methods for tasks where efficiency is predominant. To realize their theoretical potential, greedy methods require careful tuning of parameters and numerous Monte Carlo simulations. Future research could analyse the algorithmic traces of CELF++, use higher Monte Carlo budgets, and implemented compilations. One promising direction involves hybrid approaches that exploit PageRank initialization followed by sampling-based refinement and extension to dynamic networks.
