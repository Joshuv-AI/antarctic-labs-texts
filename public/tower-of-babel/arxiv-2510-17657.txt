PDE-Free Mass-Constrained Learning of Complex Systems with Hidden States: The Crowd Dynamics Case
Gianmaria Viola, Alessandro Della Pia, Lucia Russo , Ioannis Kevrekidis, Constantinos Siettos, Institute of Science, Technology for Energy, Sustainable Mobility (STEMS) Consiglio Nazionale delle Ricerche (CNR) , Naples 80125, Italy, Modeling, Engineering Risk, Complexity Scuola Superiore Meridionale, School for Advanced Studies , Naples 80138, Italy, Department of Chemical, Biomolecular Engineering, Department of Applied Mathematics and Statistics & Department of Urology, Johns Hopkins University , Baltimore, MD 21218, USA, Dipartimento di Matematica e Applicazioni “Renato Caccioppoli" Università degli Studi di Napoli “Federico II" , Naples 80126, Italy

Abstract
We propose a machine learning (ML) framework based on the next-generation Equation-Free algorithm for learning the spatio-temporal dynamics of mass-constrained complex systems with unobserved (hidden) states, whose dynamics can in principle be described by partial differential equations (PDEs), but lack explicit models. In these cases, the underlying variables, closures, and potentials governing the dynamics are generally unknown or not directly observable and therefore must be inferred from data. Here, we construct reduced-order models (ROMs) within a learned latent space – obtained via manifold learning – using delayed coordinates, thus exploiting the Takens’/Whitney’s embedding theorems. In the first stage, we employ both linear (Proper Orthogonal Decomposition, POD) and nonlinear manifold learning (Diffusion Maps, DMs) to extract low-dimensional latent representations of the complex spatio-temporal evolution. In the second step, we learn predictive ROMs using delayed manifold coordinates to approximate the solution operator on the latent space. In the final step, the latent dynamics are lifted back to the original high-dimensional space by solving a pre-image problem. We demonstrate that both POD and the particular k k -nearest neighbors ( k k -NN) lifting operators preserve the mass, a crucial property in the context of many problems, including computational fluid dynamics (CFD) and crowd dynamics. Actually, the proposed framework reconstructs the solution operator of the unavailable mass-constrained PDE, bypassing the need to discover an explicit form of the PDE per se, based on a manifold-informed objective map that bridges the scales . For our illustrations, we generated detailed spatio-temporal data using the Hughes model, which couples a continuity PDE with an Eikonal equation for the potential field, modeling how individuals choose optimal paths to minimize travel time while avoiding obstacles and high-density regions. We show that DMs provide nonlinear embeddings that outperform POD in reconstruction accuracy, thus resulting in better parsimonious manifold-informed ROMs that provide a stable and accurate approximation of the solution operator both in the latent space and, via reconstruction, in the high-dimensional space, and can therefore be integrated reliably over long time horizons.

Problem statement: Learning the dynamics of complex systems and the crowd dynamics paradigm

From a modeling perspective, crowd dynamics can be approached using the conceptual tools of complex systems theory, wherein large-scale collective patterns emerge from localized interactions among individuals
[
17
,
83
,
84
,
85
]
. Depending on the scale of observation, modeling strategies are generally categorized as either microscopic or macroscopic
[
86
]
. Microscopic models in crowd dynamics are typically based on pseudo-Newtonian mechanics and rely on the mathematical framework of behavioral swarm theory
[
87
]
. In these agent-based models, individual trajectories are usually governed by systems of ordinary or stochastic differential equations (ODEs/SDEs), with the social force model (SFM) serving as a prominent example
[
88
,
89
]
. Conversely, macroscopic models conceptualize pedestrian crowds as continuum systems, drawing inspiration from fluid dynamics
[
90
,
17
,
91
,
92
]
. These approaches describe the evolution of the crowd using macroscopic fields—most notably the density
\rho
and velocity
\mathbf{u}
—that evolve in space and time according to PDEs
[
90
,
17
,
19
,
93
,
15
,
82
]
. Depending on the modeling assumptions, macroscopic frameworks can be either first-order (velocity-based) or second-order (acceleration-based) and may incorporate local or nonlocal interaction terms. Interactions can also be defined metrically—based on absolute distance—or topologically—based on a fixed number of neighbors
[
17
]
. A critical component in these models is the so-called
fundamental diagram
, which relates pedestrian density to velocity. This relationship serves as a closure term in the PDEs but remains incompletely understood and highly context-dependent
[
92
]
. Beyond localized collision-avoidance mechanisms, macroscopic models also account for higher-level decision-making processes through
optimal path planning
, which refers to how pedestrians select their routes toward a destination. These choices are influenced by factors such as prior knowledge of the environment, anticipation of other agents’ movements, line-of-sight visibility, and the presence of obstacles.

In addition to microscopic and macroscopic formulations, it is worth mentioning that an intermediate (mesoscale) modeling approach can also be adopted for the description of crowd motion. In this framework, pedestrians are treated as discrete entities, but their behavior is characterized statistically using a limited number of degrees of freedom, such as position and orientation. This hybrid perspective bridges individual-based and continuum models
[
86
,
94
]
by capturing both microscopic variability and emergent behaviors. Notably, this approach draws strong analogies with the kinetic theory of gases, where the evolution of probability distributions over microscopic states replaces deterministic trajectories
[
92
]
.

Following the continuum-mechanics-based macroscopic modeling approach, crowd flow is described by the density field
\rho(t,x,y)
and the corresponding average velocity field
\mathbf{u}(t,x,y)
, where
t\in(t_{0},t_{0}+t_{f})
denotes the temporal variable (spanning from an initial
t_{0}
to a final
t_{0}+t_{f}
time instant), and
(x,y)\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
are the spatial coordinates within the two-dimensional domain of interest. The momentum of the local flow is then defined as
\mathbf{q}(t,x,y):=\rho(t,x,y)\,\mathbf{u}(t,x,y)
. These flow variables are assumed to be differentiable with respect to both time and space. The evolution of
\rho
and
\mathbf{u}
is governed by a system of macroscopic PDEs that reflect both local dynamics—such as density-dependent velocity constraints—and nonlocal interactions within the domain
\Omega
. A general form of such a model is given by (see, e.g.,
[
90
]
):

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{f}(\rho,\mathbf{u})\right)

\displaystyle=S(\rho,\mathbf{u}),\quad\text{in }(t_{0},t_{0}+t_{f})\times\Omega,

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(\rho\mathbf{u})}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{h}(\rho,\mathbf{u})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}(\rho,\mathbf{u},\Psi),\quad\text{in }(t_{0},t_{0}+t_{f})\times\Omega,

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\rho(t,x,y),\mathbf{q}(t,x,y))

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{for }(x,y)\in\partial\Omega,\qquad\mathcal{I}(\rho(t_{0},x,y),\mathbf{q}(t_{0},x,y))=0,\quad\text{for }\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,

(3)

where
\mathbf{f}(\rho,\mathbf{u})
and
\mathbf{h}(\rho,\mathbf{u})
denote the mass and momentum flux functions, respectively;
S(\rho,\mathbf{u})
is a source term; and
\mathbf{F}(\rho,\mathbf{u},\Psi)
models a “psychomechanical” force acting on the crowd. This force term may depend not only on local variables but also on a nonlocal interaction potential
\Psi=\Psi(\rho)
, which typically incorporates spatially distributed (global) effects. The boundary operator
\mathcal{B}
reflects the imposed constraints at the domain boundary
\partial\Omega
, and the initial operator
\mathcal{I}
is defined by
\mathcal{I}(\rho,\mathbf{q}):=\left(\rho-\rho_{0}(x,y),\,\mathbf{q}-\rho_{0}(x,y)\mathbf{u}_{0}(x,y)\right)
, prescribing the initial density
\rho_{0}
and initial velocity
\mathbf{u}_{0}
at time
t_{0}
.

Eq. (
1
) is the continuity equation, expressing conservation of mass while allowing for volumetric sources and sinks through the term
S(\rho,\mathbf{u})
. Eq. (
2
) is the momentum equation, governing the evolution of the velocity field and incorporating external or internal forces through
\mathbf{F}(\rho,\mathbf{u},\Psi)
. This force may depend not only on the local state but also on global features of the density distribution via the potential
\Psi
, which models behavioral interactions such as attraction, repulsion, and alignment, as discussed in
[
20
]
. Since the exact forms of the fluxes
\mathbf{f}
and
\mathbf{h}
, as well as the force term
\mathbf{F}
, are typically not known
a priori
, they must be estimated from empirical data or inferred through appropriate modeling assumptions.

A seminal model for approximating behavioral dynamics via PDEs is the continuum Hughes model
[
79
,
80
,
81
,
95
]
, which combines a conservation law for the crowd density with an Eikonal equation to compute shortest-time paths to a common goal. Crucially, the
optimal
path selection is assumed to depend on the global distribution of pedestrians in the environment, necessitating a dynamic resolution of the Eikonal equation at every time step. Although the local density–velocity relationship appears explicitly in the conservation law, the coupling with the Eikonal equation imparts a global character to the dynamics. This allows the model to reproduce complex behavioral features such as obstacle avoidance and re-routing in congested environments
[
92
]
. Both theoretical investigations and empirical studies support the notion that pedestrian speed and direction are influenced by the full spatial distribution of the crowd, rather than just local surroundings
[
17
]
. Consequently, while such global dependencies enhance model fidelity, they also introduce substantial challenges in terms of analytical tractability and computational cost. This motivates the need for efficient and compact representations of crowd motion—specifically, ROMs accounting for the
unknown hidden behavioral
dynamics
[
90
,
20
,
17
,
1
,
96
,
20
]
—so as to retain the essential features required to reproduce its global evolution.

In recent years, advances in machine learning (ML) and deep learning have provided powerful tools for modeling and forecasting crowd dynamics. Most of this work has been conducted at the microscopic level, where individual pedestrian trajectories are analyzed to infer local interactions and collective movement patterns. Traditional machine learning models have utilized trajectory data to learn behavioral patterns and group dynamics
[
97
,
98
]
, while more recent deep learning frameworks have employed various neural architectures to improve prediction accuracy
[
99
,
100
,
101

. A common evaluation strategy for these models is to assess their ability to predict short- and long-term future trajectories. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs), particularly those designed for sequence modeling such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, have demonstrated good performance in pedestrian trajectory prediction tasks
[
102
,
103
]
. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have also been explored, where historical trajectory points are encoded as spatial features, forming an image-like input over which convolutional operations are performed to extract global motion patterns. Hybrid approaches combining CNNs and RNNs have been introduced to further enhance predictive capabilities
[
104
]
. Graph-based models incorporating attention mechanisms have been developed to capture spatial and temporal dependencies within pedestrian groups, improving multi-agent interaction modeling
[
105
,
106
]
. Generative models such as GANs have also been used
[
107
]
, while transformer-based architectures have recently emerged as a viable alternative to RNNs, offering competitive performance and parallel training advantages
[
108
]
.

In contrast, applications of ML to macroscopic crowd dynamics remain far less explored. Recent work has begun to bridge this gap by integrating deep learning with continuum modeling to describe the behavior of high-density crowds. One approach draws analogies between fluid dynamics and crowd flow, incorporating physical and social factors into hydrodynamic equations and solving them using neural network surrogates trained with one-step-ahead predictions
[
109
]
.

Based on this framework, in the case of hydrodynamic-type PDEs for crowd dynamics, such a learning-based approach can be represented by equations of the form:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}^{(\rho)}\bigg(\rho,u,v,\frac{\partial(\rho u)}{\partial x},\frac{\partial(\rho u)}{\partial y},\frac{\partial(\rho v)}{\partial x},\frac{\partial(\rho v)}{\partial y}\bigg),

(4)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(\rho u)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}^{(\rho u)}\bigg(\rho,u,v,\frac{\partial(\rho u)}{\partial x},\frac{\partial(\rho u)}{\partial y},\frac{\partial(\rho v)}{\partial x},\frac{\partial(\rho v)}{\partial y}\bigg),

(5)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(\rho v)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}^{(\rho v)}\bigg(\rho,u,v,\frac{\partial(\rho u)}{\partial x},\frac{\partial(\rho u)}{\partial y},\frac{\partial(\rho v)}{\partial x},\frac{\partial(\rho v)}{\partial y}\bigg),

(6)

where
u(t,x,y)
and
v(t,x,y)
are the components of the velocity field
\mathbf{u}(t,x,y)
along the spatial directions
x
and
y
, respectively. The functions
\mathcal{N}^{(\rho)}
,
\mathcal{N}^{(\rho u)}
, and
\mathcal{N}^{(\rho v)}
are neural networks approximating the right-hand sides (RHSs) of the PDEs. This formulation can be readily extended to another broad class of macroscopic behavioral PDE crowd models based on the Hughes-type formulation
[
79
]
, where pedestrians choose their direction of motion by minimizing an instantaneous travel-time potential (given by an Eikonal or Hamilton–Jacobi equation). Related approaches also emerge from optimal transport formulations. In particular, Hughes
[
79
]
proposed a first-order system (further details are given in Section
4
) of the form:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=0,\quad\mathbf{u}=-f(\rho)\,\frac{\nabla\phi}{\|\nabla\phi\|},\quad\|\nabla\phi\|=\frac{1}{f(\rho)},

(7)

where
f(\rho)
is a decreasing speed–density function, and
\phi
plays the role of an explicitly unobserved (hidden) variable. In this formulation, a surrogate PDE model must infer
\phi
given the instantaneous crowd distribution
\rho
(e.g., via deep neural networks or operator learning).

On the one hand, the framework described above enables flexible data-driven modeling of macroscopic dynamics. However, as discussed, learning the RHS (or solution operators) of the PDEs can be problematic due to the curse of dimensionality. High-dimensional spatio-temporal inputs require large networks, extensive training data, and careful regularization, often leading to poor generalization, instability, and accumulated errors in long-term predictions. These challenges motivate latent-space modeling, where the effective dynamics are learned in a lower-dimensional representation that captures the essential structure of the PDE without explicitly resolving its full high-dimensional operator. Toward this aim, we present the proposed framework in the following section.

The Methodology

Schematic of the PDE-free mass-preserving approach.

(3) Solution of the pre-image problem: construction of the map between the learned latent-space dynamics and the spatio-temporal dynamics (i.e., reconstruction of the solution operator of the unavailable PDE).

A schematic of the proposed framework is shown in Fig.
1
.
Assuming a given set of
M
spatio-temporal snapshots of a field, say

\{\mathbf{x}_{m}\}_{m=1,\ldots,M}
, the aim is to learn a solution operator on a low-dimensional latent space using manifold-informed ROMs, and then to reconstruct—in a fully data-driven, Equation-Free manner—an approximation of the apparent solution operator in the high-dimensional input space.

Each snapshot at time
t_{m}
may contain, for example, the density of pedestrians across the spatial domain; i.e.,
\mathbf{x}_{m}=[\rho(t_{m},x_{i},y_{j})]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where
i=1,\ldots,N_{x}
,
j=1,\ldots,N_{y}
, and
N=N_{x}\times N_{y}
is the number of points in the computational grid.
Let us represent the dataset as

\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{M}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M},

(8)

with columns
\mathbf{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
being the density fields.
The key assumption of the methodology is that these data points lie on a smooth
d
-dimensional manifold
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and we seek a data-driven mapping

\varPhi:\mathbb{R}^{N}\;\longrightarrow\;\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad d\ll N,

(9)

such that
\mathbf{y}_{m}=\varPhi(\mathbf{x}_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
captures the dominant coherent structures and emergent dynamics in the latent space, where
d
approximates the intrinsic dimension of
\mathcal{M}
. The construction of this mapping via manifold learning constitutes the first step of the proposed framework.

Once
\varPhi
is obtained, one can learn surrogate ROMs of the high-dimensional dynamics embedded in the latent space, i.e., learn the solution operator
\mathcal{S}_{T}
of the reduced-order dynamics:

\boldsymbol{y}(t+T)=\mathcal{S}_{T}[\boldsymbol{y}(t)],

(10)

via machine-learning surrogates, for example, MVARs, DNNs, neural operators, or Gaussian processes.

In the final step, we solve the so-called pre-image problem, i.e., we seek a ‘lifting operator’
\mathcal{L}
to reconstruct “on demand” the apparent solution operator
\mathcal{S}^{X}_{T}
of the high-dimensional spatio-temporal dynamics from the latent dynamics. This allows us to construct the map

\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}(t+T)=\mathcal{L}\big(\mathcal{S}_{T}[\boldsymbol{y}(t)]\big)=\mathcal{S}^{X}_{T}[\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)],\quad\mathcal{S}^{X}_{T}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N},\quad\mathcal{S}^{X}_{T}=\mathcal{L}\circ\mathcal{S}_{T},

(11)

thus bypassing the need, as in the Equation-Free framework
[
40
]
, to learn explicitly the high-dimensional map itself.

This approach yields compact, computationally efficient surrogates that are non-intrusive (they do not require any explicit knowledge of the governing PDE or parts of it) and are well suited to the Equation-Free workflow
[
40
]
: fast prediction in latent space followed by data-driven reconstruction to recover high-fidelity solutions.

Here, for manifold learning, we employ both POD and Diffusion Maps (DMs), describing them briefly below along with the corresponding pre-image problem. Importantly, we theoretically show that the linear POD lifting operators, as well as the
k
-NN algorithm used to construct the lifting operator for DMs, conserve the mass in the input/high-dimensional space. This property is crucial, as in systems governed by conservation laws—such as crowd flows—if the lifting step adds or removes mass, the downstream dynamics (even if learned correctly in the latent space) become meaningless for applications that depend on conserved quantities.

3.1
Manifold Learning and the Pre-image/Reconstruction Problem

In the first step, we determine the mapping
\varPhi

\varPhi
that preserves intrinsic geometric properties of the data. Here, we used both POD and Diffusion Maps.

When using POD, the lifting operator is trivial: the full state is reconstructed as a linear combination of POD basis modes weighted by the latent coefficients. In the case of nonlinear manifold learning algorithms, such as Diffusion Maps, we aim to learn an approximation of the inverse map (the lifting operator/decoder):

\mathcal{L}\approx\varPhi^{-1}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N},\quad\varPhi^{-1}(\mathbf{Y})\rightarrow\mathbf{X},

(12)

for new samples on the manifold, say
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}\notin\varPhi(\mathbf{X})
,
i.e.
, those produced by the MVAR models. This inverse problem is referred to as the “out-of-sample extension pre-image” problem, which is ill-posed. Compactly, this can be written as

\varPhi(\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{y}^{*}))\approx\mathbf{y}^{*}.

(13)

3.1.1
Encoding and Decoding with POD

In POD, we decompose the fluctuations of the density field
\rho(t,x,y)
with respect to the temporal mean
\overline{\rho}(x,y)
as

\rho^{\prime}(t,x,y)=\rho(t,x,y)-\overline{\rho}(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}y_{i}(t)\,\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}(x,y),

(14)

where the spatial modes
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}(x,y)
are mutually orthogonal. The discrete modes
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}
are obtained via the method of snapshots
[
112
]
, namely by solving the eigenvalue problem for the covariance matrix
\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
:

\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,M,

(15)

where
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}/\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}
is the
i
th mode, and
\lambda_{i}
,
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}
are the corresponding eigenvalue and eigenvector of the covariance matrix, sorted in descending order such that
\lambda_{1}>\ldots>\lambda_{M}
. Here,
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}
is the snapshot matrix. By retaining the leading
d\ll M
modes, we construct the reduced-order POD basis parameterizing the manifold, which is subsequently used to project the high-dimensional density field onto the temporal coordinates
y_{i}(t)
, for
i=1,\dots,d
.

Using the POD basis, we define the mapping
\varPhi
in Eq. (
9
) as
\mathbf{y}=\varPhi(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x}
, where
\mathbf{W}=[\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{d}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
, thus defining the latent variables as the leading POD coefficients
\mathbf{y}=[y_{1},\ldots,y_{d}]^{\top}
. Note that the POD basis provides a parameterization of the manifold for both seen points
\mathbf{x}_{m}
and unseen ones
\mathbf{x}^{*}_{m}
. Hence, it can be employed for any point in the high-dimensional space, providing the projection subsequently used for ROM construction via the MVAR model.

Decoding with POD conserves mass.

We now show that the reconstructed solution
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
via POD preserves the total mass in each snapshot.

Proposition 1
.

Let the dataset be

\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{M}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M},

(16)

with columns
\mathbf{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
representing normalized to sum to 1 density fields. If the total mass of the original data is preserved, i.e.,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i1}&\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i2}&\dots&\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{iM}\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{1}_{M}^{\top},

(17)

then the mass of the reconstructed/decoded field computed by POD as

\hat{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{U}_{d}\mathbf{U}_{d}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}+\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{I}_{M}-\mathbf{H}),\quad\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{I}_{M}-\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{1}_{M}\mathbf{1}_{M}^{\top},

(18)

where
\mathbf{U}_{d}=[\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{d}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
is the orthonormal basis formed by the first
d
left singular vectors of the SVD of the centered matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{H}
, is also preserved, i.e.,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{x}_{i1}&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{x}_{i2}&\dots&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{x}_{iM}\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{1}_{M}^{\top}.

(19)

Proof.

Since the sum of each column of
\mathbf{X}
is 1 by hypothesis, we have

\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{1}_{M}^{\top}.

Then, for the centered matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{H}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{1}_{M}^{\top}\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{1}_{M}^{\top}-\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{1}_{M}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{M}\mathbf{1}_{M}^{\top}=\mathbf{1}_{M}^{\top}-\mathbf{1}_{M}^{\top}=\mathbf{0}_{M}^{\top}.

(20)

This implies that
\mathbf{1}_{N}
is orthogonal to the column space of
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}
, and therefore to the left singular vectors of its SVD, which provide an orthogonal basis for that column space. Thus,

\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{d}=\boldsymbol{0}^{\top}_{d},

(21)

where
d=1,2,\dots,r
, and
r
is the rank of
\mathbf{X}
. Multiplying Eq. (
18
) by
\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}
yields

\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\mathbf{U}_{d}\mathbf{U}_{d}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}+\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{I}_{M}-\mathbf{H})=\mathbf{0}_{M}^{\top}+\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{1}_{M}\mathbf{1}_{M}^{\top}=\mathbf{1}_{M}^{\top},

(22)

since
\mathbf{1}_{M}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{M}=M
. We therefore obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{X}}=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{x}_{i1}&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{x}_{i2}&\dots&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{x}_{iM}\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{1}^{\top}_{M},

(23)

hence showing that the total mass in each snapshot is preserved under POD reconstruction.
∎

The above result follows from the well-known property of POD to preserve symmetries
[
113
]
. This property also generalizes, under certain assumptions, to nonlinear manifold learning algorithms such as Diffusion Maps, as shown next.

3.1.2
Encoding with DMs and Decoding with the k-NN Algorithm

The purpose of Diffusion Maps (DMs) is to construct a nonlinear mapping from a high-dimensional space to a low-dimensional subspace while preserving the intrinsic geometry of the underlying manifold. We follow the theoretical formulation and numerical implementation of DMs presented in earlier works
[
64
,
114
,
42
,
67
,
52
]
.

Assume that the data lie on a smooth, low-dimensional manifold
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Diffusion Maps then aim to obtain low-dimensional embeddings
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
d\ll N
, collected in the matrix
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times d}
, such that Euclidean distances between points
\mathbf{y}
approximate the diffusion distances between the original points
[
110
]
.

The algorithm begins by defining a similarity measure between pairs of data points
\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\in\mathbf{X}
,
\forall i,j=1,\ldots,M
, in the high-dimensional space. Using the Euclidean norm
d_{ij}=\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\|
, we construct a Gaussian kernel
k(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
, which defines the affinity matrix:

\mathbf{A}=[a_{ij}]=[k(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})]=\exp\left(-\frac{\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\|^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right),

(24)

where
\epsilon
controls the local neighborhood size in the high-dimensional space. In our implementation, we set
\epsilon=\mathrm{median}(d_{ij})
, which promotes a relatively large neighborhood. Other strategies for selecting
\epsilon
exist
[
115
,
116
]
.

Next, the
M\times M
Markov transition matrix
\mathbf{M}
is formed by row-normalizing the affinity matrix:

\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{A},\quad\text{with}\quad\mathbf{D}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{M}a_{ij}\right).

(25)

Each entry
\mu_{ij}
of
\mathbf{M}
represents the probability of moving from point
i
to point
j
in the high-dimensional space:

\mu_{ij}=\operatorname{Prob}\left(X_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\mid X_{t}=\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right).

(26)

Equivalently, using the kernel,

\mu_{ij}=\frac{k(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})}{\operatorname{deg}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})},\quad\text{with}\quad\operatorname{deg}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{M}k(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j}),

(27)

recovering Eq. (
25
).

The transition matrix
\mathbf{M}
is similar to the symmetric, positive-definite matrix
\hat{\mathbf{M}}=\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}
, which allows an eigendecomposition

\mathbf{M}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{w}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top},

(28)

where
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
are eigenvalues and
\mathbf{w}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
are left and right eigenvectors, satisfying
\langle\mathbf{w}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{j}\rangle=\delta_{i}^{j}
. The right eigenvectors
\mathbf{u}_{i}
span an orthonormal basis for the low-dimensional subspace
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the best
d
-dimensional approximation is obtained from the
d
largest eigenvalues.

The standard DMs embedding maps each snapshot
\boldsymbol{x}_{m}
to

\mathbf{y}_{m}=(\lambda_{1}u_{1,m},\ldots,\lambda_{d}u_{d,m}),\quad m=1,\ldots,M,

(29)

where
u_{i,m}
denotes the
m
-th component of the
i
-th right eigenvector corresponding to the
i
-th largest non-trivial eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
. This embedding approximates the diffusion distance in the high-dimensional space by Euclidean distance in the embedded space:

D_{t}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})=\left\|\mu_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\cdot)-\mu_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j},\cdot)\right\|_{L_{2},1/\operatorname{deg}}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\frac{\left(\mu_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{k})-\mu_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\right)^{2}}{\operatorname{deg}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})},

(30)

with
\mu_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\cdot)
the
i
-th row of
\mathbf{M}^{t}
. In our computations, we use
t=1
.

In practice, the embedded dimension
d
is determined by the
spectral gap of the eigenvalue ratio of the transition matrix
M
,
assuming that the first
d
leading eigenvalues are adequate to provide a good approximation of the diffusion distance between all pairs of points.
The resulting DMs embedding is constructed from the retained eigenpairs
\{\lambda_{i},\mathbf{u}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
. The restriction operator (encoder)
\varPhi
, evaluated on a data point
\mathbf{x}_{m}
, is

\varPhi(\mathbf{x}_{m})=(\lambda_{1}u_{1,m},\ldots,\lambda_{d}u_{d,m})=\mathbf{y}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad m=1,\ldots,M.

(31)

For new, unseen points, we employ the Nyström method
[
117
,
71
,
72
,
38
,
42
]
.

Decoding with k-nearest neighbors

Here, for the k-NN approach for the solution for the pre-image problem
[
67
]
, we will demonstrate the following proposition.

Proposition 2
.

Let the assumptions for the data matrix
\mathbf{X}
in Proposition
1
hold, i.e., columns
\mathbf{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
are normalized to sum to 1. Then the solution of the pre-image problem using the k-NN algorithm with convex interpolation preserves the total mass.

Proof.

Construct the lifting operator (decoder)
\mathcal{L}
using k-NN as (see in
[
67
]
):

\mathbf{x}^{*}=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{y}^{*})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}b_{k}\mathbf{x}_{S(k)},

(32)

where
\mathbf{x}_{S(k)}\in\mathbf{X}
are the nearest neighbors in the high-dimensional space corresponding to
\mathbf{y}^{*}
in latent space. The convex weights
b_{k}
satisfy
\sum_{k=1}^{K}b_{k}=1
and
b_{k}\in[0,1]
.

Since
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is a convex combination of normalized columns of
\mathbf{X}
, we have

\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{*}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}b_{k}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{S(k)}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}b_{k}=1.

(33)

Hence, the reconstructed solution preserves the total mass.
∎

3.2
Manifold-Informed Multivariate Autoregressive Modeling

We use
Multivariate Autoregressive
(MVAR) models as surrogate models for the latent dynamics. As shown in
[
37
]
, MVARs in delay-coordinate latent spaces
[
48
,
50
,
3
,
51
,
52
,
53
]
outperform nonlinear machine learning surrogates when the dynamics evolve around a single nonlinear basin of attraction. In this regime, the latent dynamics are approximately linearizable, and MVAR models capture the evolution with high fidelity. Training MVARs requires only linear regression, offering a computationally efficient alternative to deep nonlinear models such as neural networks, LSTMs, or GRUs. Furthermore, the learned MVAR coefficients provide interpretability of the mode interactions, and the framework naturally supports uncertainty quantification. For multiple nonlinear basins, nonlinear surrogates (e.g., neural networks, Gaussian Processes, or SINDy
[
78
]
) can be used without changing the proposed framework.

An MVAR model of order
l
expresses the next latent state
\mathbf{y}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as a linear combination of its previous
l
values:

\mathbf{y}(t+\Delta t)=\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}\mathbf{C}_{k}\,\mathbf{y}(t-k\Delta t),

(34)

where
\mathbf{C}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are the autoregressive coefficient matrices at lag
k
. In compact form,

\mathbf{y}(t+\Delta t)=\mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{r}(t),

(35)

with
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(d\cdot l)}
concatenating the
\mathbf{C}_{k}
, and the regressor vector

\mathbf{r}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}(t)^{\top}&\mathbf{y}(t-\Delta t)^{\top}&\cdots&\mathbf{y}(t-(l-1)\Delta t)^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\cdot l}.

(36)

To estimate the MVAR parameters, we minimize the total squared prediction error over all sequences corresponding to different initial conditions. Let
\{\mathbf{y}^{(n)}(t)\}_{t=0}^{\tau}
denote the latent sequence for the
n
-th initial condition,
n=1,\dots,N_{ic}
, and
\tau=t_{f}-t_{0}
the sequence length. The corresponding regressor is

\mathbf{r}^{(n)}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}^{(n)}(t)^{\top}&\mathbf{y}^{(n)}(t-\Delta t)^{\top}&\cdots&\mathbf{y}^{(n)}(t-(l-1)\Delta t)^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

The Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) solution
[
118
]
minimizes

\min_{\mathbf{C}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{ic}}\sum_{t=(l-1)\Delta t}^{\tau-\Delta t}\left\|\mathbf{y}^{(n)}(t+\Delta t)-\mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{r}^{(n)}(t)\right\|_{2}^{2},

(37)

yielding the closed-form solution

\mathbf{C}^{\top}=\left(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{Y},

(38)

where
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times(d\cdot l)}
stacks all regressor vectors and
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times d}
contains the target latent states. Temporal stationarity of the latent states was verified via the Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test
[
119
]
.

The optimal lag order
l
is selected using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)
[
118
]
:

\text{BIC}(l)=-2\cdot\log\!\left(\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(l)\right)+\log(N_{\tau})\cdot T_{c},

(39)

where
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{h}(l)
is the maximum likelihood for lag
l
and
T_{c}=l\cdot d^{2}
is the number of autoregressive coefficients. The optimal lag minimizes
\text{BIC}(l)
. Once trained, the MVAR model provides a compact, interpretable representation of the latent temporal dynamics, suitable for forecasting, simulation, and uncertainty quantification.

3.3
Assessing the Reconstruction Error Using the Wasserstein Distance

Once latent coordinates are obtained (via POD or DMs), a high-dimensional state
\rho(t,x,y)
can be projected into the latent space and then lifted back to the high-dimensional space to obtain a reconstructed field
\hat{\rho}(t,x,y)
. The reconstruction quality is evaluated against the ground-truth
\rho(t,x,y)
using the
Wasserstein-
p
distance
(
W_{p}
), which measures the minimal transport cost required to rearrange one mass distribution into another while preserving total mass
[
120
,
121
,
122
,
123
]
.

The Wasserstein distance is particularly suitable for crowd dynamics because it accounts for both magnitude and spatial rearrangements: small translations of a density pattern yield small
W_{p}
, unlike
L_{2}
or
L_{\infty}
norms that can overestimate error due to local misalignments.

We employ the
Wasserstein-1 distance
(
W_{1}
), defined as

W_{1}(t)=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\rho,\hat{\rho})}\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\|x-y\|_{2}\,d\gamma(x,y),

(40)

where
\Gamma(\rho,\hat{\rho})
is the set of joint distributions (couplings) with marginals
\rho
and
\hat{\rho}
, and
\|x-y\|_{2}
is the Euclidean distance. The infimum selects the transport plan minimizing the total cost. Computation is performed using MATLAB
[
124
]
.

For completeness, we also report the absolute and relative
L_{2}
errors:

\varepsilon_{2}(t)=\|\rho(t,\cdot,\cdot)-\hat{\rho}(t,\cdot,\cdot)\|_{2},\qquad\varepsilon^{r}_{2}(t)=\frac{\|\rho(t,\cdot,\cdot)-\hat{\rho}(t,\cdot,\cdot)\|_{2}}{\|\rho(t,\cdot,\cdot)\|_{2}}.

(41)

The distributions of
W_{1}(t)
,
\varepsilon_{2}(t)
, and
\varepsilon^{r}_{2}(t)
across all snapshots allow summary statistics, such as mean and 10%-90% percentiles. Together, Eqs. (
41
)–(
40
) provide a robust framework to assess manifold-informed MVAR reconstruction, capturing both amplitude deviations and structural rearrangements in space and time.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}

represents the pedestrian speed, while

(43)

where

\rho_{m}
the maximum pedestrian density. In this study, these parameters are assigned equal to

and assembled in the snapshot matrix

Numerical Results

The construction of
X_{\text{train}}
,

, and

4
.
Finally, we created an additional test set

with

\sigma_{x}

approximately
25\%
below the lower bound and above the upper bound of the training range.

In the first stage, as previously discussed, we employed both POD and DMs to learn a low-dimensional parametrization of the manifold. Given the high dimensionality of each snapshot (
N=N_{x}\times N_{y}=200\times 50
) and the large ensemble of training data (
N_{\text{train}}\times N_{t}=40\times 700
), performing manifold learning on the full dataset would be computationally prohibitive. Moreover, in the present crowd dynamics configuration, there exists a single nonlinear basin of attraction: trajectories originating from different initial conditions converge toward similar long-term behavior, with most variability concentrated in the initial transient phase and around the obstacle. During this transient—when eikonal-driven optimal paths bifurcate and spatial patterns (e.g., splitting flows and front-wave formations) emerge—the dynamics are particularly informative and of primary interest (see Fig.
3
and related discussion in Section
4
).
Consequently, we learn the restriction and lifting operators from a subset of snapshots emphasizing the transient dynamics: one-third of the selected snapshots are drawn from the first
10\,\text{s}
of each simulation, and two-thirds from the remaining time. In total,
X_{\text{train}}
contains
6{,}000
snapshots for operator construction, with approximately
4{,}000
from the initial
10\,\text{s}
and
2{,}000
from the later interval. This design keeps the computational cost manageable while enhancing the reconstruction of early, fast evolving dynamics without neglecting the slow, more homogeneous regime that follows.

6
Conclusions

The proposed PDE-free approach is broadly applicable to systems that can, in principle, be described by PDEs—including those with hidden or unobserved variables—because it avoids learning the full spatio-temporal PDE operator. Instead, we learn reduced-order models (ROMs) in a latent space using delay coordinates, thereby recovering an effective ODE/solution operator in the latent space that can be discovered with manifold learning techniques (e.g., diffusion maps, DMs), as we have demonstrated. This task is far more computationally efficient than learning the high-dimensional PDE operator directly, a task that suffers from the curse of dimensionality. After training the manifold-informed ROM, the original spatio-temporal fields can be reconstructed by solving the manifold pre-image problem, effectively reproducing
implicitly/on demand
the high-dimensional solution operator rather than learning it explicitly. We demonstrate the approach on crowd dynamics—a natural testbed because behavioral hidden variables are common in this field and PDE models (e.g., Hughes-type formulations) are well established—showing that PDE-free manifold-informed ROMs can capture decision-driven transport phenomena.

For systems with conservation laws, learning the full high-dimensional solution operator for a mass-conserving PDE is not trivial
[
127
,
128
,
129
,
130
]
: small model errors compound under long-time integration, producing significant drifts or even blow-ups. By contrast, training a reduced-order surrogate in latent spaces using delay coordinates can produce, as we have shown, a much more robust time integrator (easier to fit and simulate) and—if properly controlled/analyzed—can allow stability guarantees in certain cases (e.g., for MVARs when there is one nonlinear basin of attraction). Hence, with such ROMs, one can perform accurate long-horizon simulations.

Of course, for these ROMs to perform well, one must parametrize the latent dynamics correctly. Here and in other works
[
131
,
132
]
, we have shown that diffusion maps (DMs) outperform POD—which remains dominant in the majority of such studies—because DMs capture the nonlinear manifold geometry. Consequently, significantly fewer coordinates are required to parametrize the low-dimensional latent space where robust and accurate ROMs can be constructed with reduced complexity. This advantage of nonlinear manifold learning algorithms, however, introduces the ill-posed pre-image problem: mapping latent coordinates back to high-dimensional fields is numerically sensitive. Crucially, mass conservation must be enforced consistently when lifting back to physical space. Naïve post-hoc normalization of a reconstructed field (rescaling to match total mass) introduces bias: it alters spatial distributions and implicitly changes the projection from the latent dynamics that produced the reconstruction, rendering the ROM and decoder inconsistent. Instead, conservation should be enforced by construction using an appropriate lifting scheme, such as the k-NN algorithm with convex interpolation
[
67
]
, which, as we have proven here, preserves mass.
Future work will address systems with multiple basins of attraction— and comparing our manifold-informed ROMs against autoencoders, neural operators and other ML architectures to quantify robustness, generalization and conservation properties.
Another direction is to handle cases where high-dimensional statistics are functions of low-dimensional variables (i.e., low-to-high probability distributions)
[
133
]
, thus identifying the underlying
SDEs, that preserve distributional constraints.

Appendix A
Numerical discretization of the Hughes model

The numerical solution of the Hughes model (Eqs. (
42a
)–(
42b
)) is carried out using a finite-volume scheme combined with the Fast Sweeping Method
[
134
]
to efficiently solve the nonlinear Eikonal equation (
42b
). The approach outlined here specifies the spatial and temporal discretization of the governing equations, the evaluation of numerical fluxes via Godunov’s method, and the stability condition adopted for time-step selection.

At each time step, given the current density field
\rho_{i,j}^{n}
, the right-hand side of the Eikonal equation is evaluated, and the potential
\phi_{i,j}^{n}
is computed using the Fast Sweeping Method
[
134
]
. Once the potential is available, the system (
42a
)–(
42b
) reduces to a scalar nonlinear conservation law:

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial F_{x}(\rho,\phi)}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial F_{y}(\rho,\phi)}{\partial y}=0,

(47)

where
F_{x}(\rho,\phi)
and
F_{y}(\rho,\phi)
denote the density fluxes along the
x
- and
y
-directions:

\displaystyle F_{x}(\rho,\phi)

\displaystyle=\rho U_{x}(\rho,\phi)=\rho g(\rho)v_{f}\left(1-\frac{\rho}{\rho_{m}}\right)\frac{\phi_{x}}{\sqrt{\phi_{x}^{2}+\phi_{y}^{2}}},

(48)

\displaystyle F_{y}(\rho,\phi)

\displaystyle=\rho U_{y}(\rho,\phi)=\rho g(\rho)v_{f}\left(1-\frac{\rho}{\rho_{m}}\right)\frac{\phi_{y}}{\sqrt{\phi_{x}^{2}+\phi_{y}^{2}}},

(49)

with
\phi_{x}=\partial\phi/\partial x
and
\phi_{y}=\partial\phi/\partial y
evaluated by first-order forward finite differences of the discrete potential field.

The density field is then updated in conservative form:

\rho_{i,j}^{n+1}=\rho_{i,j}^{n}-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left(\hat{F}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}^{n}-\hat{F}_{i-\frac{1}{2},j}^{n}\right)-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta y}\left(\hat{F}_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n}-\hat{F}_{i,j-\frac{1}{2}}^{n}\right),

(50)

where
\Delta x=\Delta y
denotes the uniform grid spacing.

The intercell fluxes are computed using Godunov’s method. In the
x
-direction:

\displaystyle\hat{F}^{n}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\min\limits_{\rho^{n}_{i,j}\leq\theta\leq\rho^{n}_{i+1,j}}F_{x}(\theta)&\text{if }\rho^{n}_{i,j}\leq\rho^{n}_{i+1,j},\\
\max\limits_{\rho^{n}_{i+1,j}\leq\theta\leq\rho^{n}_{i,j}}F_{x}(\theta)&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(51)

\displaystyle\hat{F}^{n}_{i-\frac{1}{2},j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\min\limits_{\rho^{n}_{i-1,j}\leq\theta\leq\rho^{n}_{i,j}}F_{x}(\theta)&\text{if }\rho^{n}_{i-1,j}\leq\rho^{n}_{i,j},\\
\max\limits_{\rho^{n}_{i,j}\leq\theta\leq\rho^{n}_{i-1,j}}F_{x}(\theta)&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(52)

and analogously in the
y
-direction:

\displaystyle\hat{F}^{n}_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\min\limits_{\rho^{n}_{i,j}\leq\theta\leq\rho^{n}_{i,j+1}}F_{y}(\theta)&\text{if }\rho^{n}_{i,j}\leq\rho^{n}_{i,j+1},\\
\max\limits_{\rho^{n}_{i,j+1}\leq\theta\leq\rho^{n}_{i,j}}F_{y}(\theta)&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(53)

\displaystyle\hat{F}^{n}_{i,j-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\min\limits_{\rho^{n}_{i,j-1}\leq\theta\leq\rho^{n}_{i,j}}F_{y}(\theta)&\text{if }\rho^{n}_{i,j-1}\leq\rho^{n}_{i,j},\\
\max\limits_{\rho^{n}_{i,j}\leq\theta\leq\rho^{n}_{i,j-1}}F_{y}(\theta)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(54)

The fluxes
F_{x}(\theta)
and
F_{y}(\theta)
in Eqs. (
50
) are evaluated at frozen coefficients
\phi_{x},\phi_{y}
. The optimization is carried out with respect to
\theta\in[\rho_{L},\rho_{R}]
, where
\rho_{L}
and
\rho_{R}
are the left and right states at the interface.

Finally, the time step
\Delta t
is chosen according to a Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition:

\Delta t=CFL\cdot\min\left(\frac{\Delta x}{\max|U_{x}|},\frac{\Delta y}{\max|U_{y}|}\right),

(55)

where the maxima of
|U_{x}|
and
|U_{y}|
(see Eqs. (
48
)–(
49
)) are taken over all grid points. In all simulations, a Courant number of
CFL=0.25
was adopted.
