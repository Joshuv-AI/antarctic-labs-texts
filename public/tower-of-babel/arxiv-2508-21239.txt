An analogue of the Dedekind Eta function for Hecke Groups 𝑯 ⁡ ( 𝑫 ) H(\sqrt{D}) Thanks: Dorian Goldfeld is partially supported by Simons Collaboration Grant Number 567168.
Debmalya Basak, Dorian Goldfeld, Nicolas Robles, Alexandru Zaharescu

Abstract
. Let D ≡ 1 mod 4 D\equiv 1\bmod{4} be a fundamental discriminant of a real quadratic field. We construct an analogue of the classical Dedekind eta function for the Hecke group H ⁡ ( D ) H(\sqrt{D}) . This gives rise to a family of holomorphic modular functions for H ⁡ ( D ) H(\sqrt{D}) which vanish at the cusp at ∞ \infty and were not known previously. We establish results and propose some conjectures regarding the behavior of the Fourier coefficients associated to these modular forms.

Introduction

Let
\lambda
be a positive real number. Hecke (see
[
5
]
) introduced the matrix groups
H(\lambda)
generated by the two matrices

T_{\lambda}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&\lambda\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad S=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&\phantom{-}0\end{pmatrix}.

These are now called Hecke groups.
In the case that
\lambda=1
, the Hecke group
H(\lambda)
is just the modular group
\SL(2,\mathbb{Z}).
The Hecke groups act on the upper half plane

\mathfrak{h}:=\{x+iy\mid x\in\mathbb{R},\;y>0\}

in the usual manner. If
\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in H(\lambda)
and
z\in\mathfrak{h}
then the action of
\gamma
on
z
is denoted
\gamma z
and is given by

\gamma z=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}.

Hecke proved in
[
5
]
that if

\lambda=2\cos(\pi/q),\;\qquad(\text{\rm for an integer}\;q>3\;\text{\rm or}\;q=\infty),

then
H(\lambda)
is a discrete subgroup of
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
with fundamental domain
H(\lambda)\backslash\mathfrak{h}
having finite area, that is,
H(\lambda)
is a Fuchsian group of the first kind. He also proved that if
\lambda
is any real number satisfying

\lambda>2

then
H(\lambda)
is a discrete subgroup of
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
with fundamental domain
H(\lambda)\backslash\mathfrak{h}
having infinite area, that is,
H(\lambda)
is a Fuchsian group of the second kind. Furthermore he showed that these are the only discrete subgroups of
\SL(2,\mathbb{R})
. In the case of
\lambda>2
, the fundamental domain is given as in the following figure.

Non holomorphic Eisenstein series and Maass cusp forms for the Hecke groups

The non-holomorphic Maass cusp form associated to the base eigenvalue of the Laplacian for the Hecke group

[

[

[

H(\lambda)
(with
\lambda>2

More recently Karlovitz

[
6

The main goal of this paper is to construct holomorphic modular forms for the Hecke groups
H\big(\sqrt{D}\big)
where
D\equiv 1\bmod{4}
is a fundamental discriminant of a real quadratic field. Since the smallest possible such discriminant is
D=5
(which means
\sqrt{D}
is bigger than 2), it will not be possible to have holomorphic modular forms on
H\big(\sqrt{D}\big)
which are square integrable over the fundamental domain which has infinite area. Let

q=e^{2\pi iz}
with
z\in\mathfrak{h}
. Our approach is the construction of an
H\big(\sqrt{D}\big)
analogue of the classical Dedekind eta function
\eta(z)

[
1
]
defined by

(1.1)

\eta(z):=q^{\frac{1}{24}}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-q^{n}\right)

which satisfies the modular relations

(1.2)

\displaystyle\eta(z+1)=e^{\frac{\pi i}{12}}\,\eta(z),\qquad\;\;\eta(-1/z)=\sqrt{-iz}\cdot\eta(z).

Recall that

\eta(z)^{24}=\Delta(z),

the Ramanujan cusp form of weight
12

\SL(2,\mathbb{Z}).

Remark 1.11
.

Curiously, we have not been able to find an analogue of the modular forms in the case when

Remark 1.12
.

In the Hecke group
H(\sqrt{D})
with
D\equiv 1\bmod{4}
and
D\geq 5
, there is a hyperbolic element
\gamma_{D}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\sqrt{D}&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
with fixed points

\omega_{D}=\frac{\sqrt{D}+\sqrt{D-4}}{2},\quad\omega_{D}^{\prime}=\frac{\sqrt{D}-\sqrt{D-4}}{2},

that is,
\gamma_{D}\omega_{D}=\omega,\gamma_{D}\omega_{D}^{\prime}=\omega^{\prime}
. We then define the matrix
W_{D}:=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-\omega_{D}\\
1&-\omega_{D}^{\prime}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
which has the property that the conjugate of
\gamma_{D}
by
W_{D}
is a diagonal matrix. It follows that

W_{D}\cdot\gamma_{D}\cdot W_{D}^{-1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\sqrt{\kappa}&0\\
0&1/\sqrt{\kappa}\end{array}\right),

with
\sqrt{\kappa}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}=\sqrt{D}
since conjugation preserves the trace of a matrix. Then if
f(z)
is modular for the group
H(\sqrt{D})
, the function
f\left(W_{D}^{-1}z\right)
is invariant under the transformation
z\mapsto\kappa z
. Following
[
11
]
, a flare domain for the conjugated Hecke group
H(\sqrt{D})
is isometric to a domain of the form
\{z\in\mathfrak{h}:1<|z|<\kappa,\,0<\arg(\mathrm{z})<\alpha\}
for some angle
\alpha<\pi/2
. In this paper the functions
\Delta_{5}(z)
and
\eta_{D}(z)
(with
D\equiv 1\bmod{4}
and
D>5
) are holomorphic functions which are modular for
H(\sqrt{D})
, vanish at the cusp at
\infty
, but are not square integrable in the flare domain.

A natural question at this stage is to understand the behavior of the Fourier coefficients
a_{{}_{D}}(N)
. For the classical Dedekind eta function
\eta(z)
, the exponents of the factors in (
1.1
) are always
1
. The coefficients
\tau(N)
that arise from
\Delta(z)=\eta(z)^{24}
are multiplicative and satisfy the bound

\lvert\tau(p)\rvert\leq 2p^{\frac{11}{2}},\quad\textrm{for all primes }p.

On the other hand, when the exponents of the factors in (
1.1
) are all
-1
, say for instance, the function
\eta(z)^{-1}
, this gives rise to the coefficients
p(N)
, the number of partitions of
N
, which grows like

p(N)\sim\frac{1}{4N\sqrt{3}}\exp\bigg(\pi\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}N^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg),\quad\textrm{as}\quad N\to\infty.

In (
1.4
) and (
1.9
), the exponents corresponding to the factors take values both
1
and
-1
. In this context, we prove the following result regarding the Fourier coefficients
a_{{}_{D}}(N)
.

Theorem 1.14
.

Let
D\equiv 1\bmod{4}
be a fundamental discriminant of a real quadratic field. For
N\geq 0
, let
a_{{}_{D}}(N)
be defined by (
1.13
). Then
a_{{}_{D}}(N)
lies in
\mathbb{Z}[\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}]
. Furthermore, there exists a constant
C_{D}>0
depending only on
D
such that

\lvert a_{{}_{D}}(N)\rvert\ll_{{}_{D}}e^{C_{D}\sqrt{N}}\cdot N^{\varphi(D)/4+1}\cdot(\log(N+2))^{\varphi(D)/2+1},

Here
\varphi(D)
denotes the Euler totient function and the implied constant depends only on
D
.

Remark 1.15
.

For
D
prime, we can allow

C_{D}=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3D}}\left(5D^{2}+7D-10\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

For clarity, we mention that the coefficients in (
1
) are obtained by raising both sides of (
1.13
) to the fifth power and using the values of
a_{5}(N)
(see Table
1
). Based on the numerical and graphical data (see also Section
5
), we propose the following conjectures related to
a_{{}_{D}}(N)
.

Conjecture 1.16
.

Let
D\equiv 1\bmod{4}
be a fundamental discriminant of a real quadratic field. For
N\geq 0
, let
a_{{}_{D}}(N)
be defined by (
1.13
). Then there exists a constant
C_{D}>0
depending only on
D
such that

\log\,\lvert a_{{}_{D}}(N)\rvert=C_{D}\sqrt{N}(1+o(1)),\quad\textrm{as}\quad N\to\infty.

Conjecture 1.17
.

Let
D\equiv 1\bmod{4}
be a fundamental discriminant of a real quadratic field. For
N\geq 0
, the sequence
\{a_{{}_{D}}(N)\}
defined in (
1.13
) changes sign infinitely often.

The above conjectures are motivated by an interesting connection between the Fourier coefficients
a_{{}_{D}}(N)
and partition functions, which we establish in Section
4
. In particular, the twisted partition function defined by

(1.18)

\displaystyle p_{\textrm{ord}}(k,\theta)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash k}\theta^{\ell(\lambda)},\quad\theta\in\mathbb{C},\,\lvert\theta\rvert\leq 1,

plays a key role in this correspondence. In (
1.18

\lambda
denotes a partition of
k
and
\ell(\lambda)
denotes the number of parts of
\lambda
, that is, its length. When
\theta=1
, this reduces to the ordinary partition function. In our case, we shall have
\theta
to be certain
D

1.18
) yields exponential sums involving the lengths
\ell(\lambda)
. It is natural to speculate that the sign changes of
a_{{}_{D}}(N)
is related to some oscillatory behavior of these exponential sums. For more details, see Section

2.
Modular Relations between Products Twisted by Characters

Let
D\equiv 1\bmod{4}
with
D>0
be a fundamental discriminant of a real quadratic field. In order to prove our results, we need to study the following twisted product: for
z\in\mathfrak{h}
, define

(2.1)

\displaystyle\Phi_{{}_{D}}(z):=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-q^{n}\right)^{{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(n)},\quad\textrm{where}\quad q=e^{\frac{2\pi iz}{\sqrt{D}}},

and
{\chi_{{}_{D}}}
is the primitive real character with conductor
D
. In this case, the Dirichlet
L
-function

L(s,{\chi_{{}_{D}}})=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(n)n^{-s}
satisfies the functional equation

(2.2)

\displaystyle\xi(s,{\chi_{{}_{D}}}):=\left(\frac{D}{\pi}\right)^{\tfrac{s}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)L(s,{\chi_{{}_{D}}})=\xi(1-s,{\chi_{{}_{D}}}).

In this section, we prove the following theorem which gives a modular relation between
\Phi_{{{}_{D}}}
and a certain dual of
\Phi_{{{}_{D}}}
, denoted
\Phi^{\#}_{{{}_{D}}}
, which is defined as follows: for
z\in\mathfrak{h}
, let

(2.3)

\displaystyle\Phi^{\#}_{{{}_{D}}}(z):=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\prod_{a=1}^{D}\left(1-e^{\frac{2\pi ia}{D}}\cdot q^{n}\right)^{{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(a)}

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
D\equiv 1\bmod{4}
with
D>0
be a fundamental discriminant of a real quadratic field. Let
z\in\mathfrak{h}
. Define
\chi_{{}_{D}}
to be the primitive real character with conductor
D
. Then we have

(2.5)

\displaystyle\Phi^{\#}_{{{}_{D}}}(-1/z)=e^{L^{\prime}(0,{\chi_{{}_{D}}})-iz\cdot\frac{\pi\,L(-1,{\chi_{D}})}{\sqrt{D}}}\cdot\Phi_{{{}_{D}}}(z),

where
\Phi_{{{}_{D}}}(z)
and
\Phi^{\#}_{{{}_{D}}}(z)
are defined by (
2.1
) and (
2.3
) respectively.

We break the proof of Theorem
2.4
into the following subsections.

2.1.
The function
F_{{{{}_{D}}}}(z)
.

We follow Weil’s proof
[
20
]
of the modular relation (
1.2
) for the Dedekind’s eta function. The key point is to use the Taylor series given by

\log(1-x)=-\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}m^{-1}x^{m},

which is valid for
|x|<1.
For
z\in\mathfrak{h}
, define

\displaystyle F_{{{}_{D}}}(z)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(n)}{m}e^{\frac{2\pi imnz}{\sqrt{D}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(n)\bigg(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}m^{-1}\left(e^{\frac{2\pi imnz}{\sqrt{D}}}\right)^{m}\bigg)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(n)\log\left(1-e^{\frac{2\pi imnz}{\sqrt{D}}}\right).

It follows that

(2.6)

F_{{{}_{D}}}(z)=-\log\Phi_{{{}_{D}}}(z).

Let
s\in\mathbb{C}
with
{\rm Re}(s)>1
. Then for
\sigma>1,

we have the Mellin transform pair

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}_{{{}_{D}}}(s)

\displaystyle:=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}F_{{}_{D}}(iu)\,u^{s}\,\frac{du}{u},\quad\textrm{and}\quad F_{{}_{D}}(iu)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\limits_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}\widetilde{F}_{{}_{D}}(s)u^{-s}\,ds,

where
u>0
. We prove the following lemma.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{C}
with
{\rm Re}(s)>1
. The function
\widetilde{F}_{{}_{D}}(s)
satisfies the following functional equation:

\widetilde{F}_{{}_{D}}(s)=\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{D}}{2\pi}\bigg)^{s}\Gamma(s)L(s,{\chi_{{}_{D}}})\zeta(s+1)=\sqrt{D}\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{D}}{2\pi}\bigg)^{-s}\Gamma(-s)\,\zeta(-s)\,L(1-s,{\chi_{{}_{D}}}).

Proof.

We compute

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}_{{}_{D}}(s)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(n)}{m}e^{-\frac{2\pi mnu}{\sqrt{D}}}\,u^{s}\,\frac{du}{u}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(n)}{m}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{2\pi mnu}{\sqrt{D}}}\,u^{s}\,\frac{du}{u}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(n)}{m}\cdot\sqrt{D}^{s}\,(2\pi mn)^{-s}\Gamma(s)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{D}}{2\pi}\bigg)^{s}\Gamma(s)L(s,{\chi_{{}_{D}}})\zeta(s+1).

We have the functional equation of the Riemann zeta function

\xi(s):=\pi^{-\frac{s}{2}}\Gamma\left(\tfrac{s}{2}\right)\zeta(s)=\xi(1-s).

It follows from (
2.2
) and the identity
\Gamma\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{s+1}{2}\right)=2^{1-s}\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(s)
that

\xi(s+1)\xi(s,{\chi_{{}_{D}}})=2\,D^{\frac{s}{2}}(2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)\zeta(s+1)L(s,{\chi_{{}_{D}}})=\xi(-s)\xi(-s+1,{\chi_{{}_{D}}}).

Then we have the functional equation

\widetilde{F}_{{}_{D}}(s)=\sqrt{D}\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{D}}{2\pi}\bigg)^{-s}\Gamma(-s)\,\zeta(-s)\,L(1-s,{\chi_{{}_{D}}}),

which completes the proof.
∎

2.2.
The dual function
F_{{}_{D}}^{\#}(z)

We recall the identity

{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(m)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{D}}\,\sum\limits_{a=1}^{D}{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(a)e^{\frac{2\pi iam}{D}},

which gives the Fourier expansion of
{\chi_{{}_{D}}}
in terms of the additive characters. For
z\in\mathfrak{h}
, we define the dual function

\displaystyle F_{{}_{D}}^{\#}(z)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{D}\cdot\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(m)}{m}e^{\frac{2\pi imnz}{\sqrt{D}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{D}{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(a)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigg(\sum_{m=1}\frac{1}{m}\left(e^{\frac{2\pi ia}{D}}\cdot e^{\frac{2\pi inz}{\sqrt{D}}}\right)^{m}\bigg)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{a=1}^{D}{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(a)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\log\left(1-e^{\frac{2\pi ia}{D}}\cdot e^{\frac{2\pi inz}{\sqrt{D}}}\right).

It follows that

(2.8)

F_{{}_{D}}^{\#}(z)=-\log\Phi_{{}_{D}}^{\#}(z).

Let
s\in\mathbb{C}
with
{\rm Re}(s)>1
. Then for
\sigma>1,

we have the Mellin transform pair

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}^{\#}_{{}_{D}}(s)

\displaystyle:=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}F^{\#}_{{}_{D}}(iu)\,u^{s}\,\frac{du}{u},\quad\textrm{and}\quad F^{\#}_{{}_{D}}(iu)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\limits_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}\widetilde{F}^{\#}_{{}_{D}}(s)u^{-s}\,ds,

where
u>0
. We establish the following lemma.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{C}
with
{\rm Re}(s)>1
. The dual function
\widetilde{F}^{\#}_{{}_{D}}(s)
satisfies the following functional equation:

\widetilde{F}^{\#}_{{}_{D}}(s)=\widetilde{F}_{{}_{D}}(-s)=\sqrt{D}\cdot\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{D}}{2\pi}\bigg)^{s}\Gamma(s)L(1+s,{\chi_{{}_{D}}})\zeta(s).

Proof.

We compute

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}^{\#}_{{}_{D}}(s)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{D}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(m)}{m}e^{-\frac{2\pi mnu}{\sqrt{D}}}\,u^{s}\,\frac{du}{u}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{D}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(m)}{m}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{2\pi mnu}{\sqrt{D}}}\,u^{s}\,\frac{du}{u}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{D}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(m)}{m}\cdot\sqrt{D}^{s}\,(2\pi mn)^{-s}\Gamma(s)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{D}\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{D}}{2\pi}\bigg)^{s}\Gamma(s)L(s+1,{\chi_{{}_{D}}})\zeta(s).

Combining this with Lemma
2.7
completes the proof.
∎

2.3.
Proof of Theorem
2.4

We now present the proof of the modular relation (
2.5
). Since both sides of (
2.5
) are analytic functions on
\mathfrak{h}
, by the Identity Theorem, it suffices to prove (
2.5
) in the special case when
z=iy
for any
y>0
. It follows from Lemma
2.7

that
\widetilde{F}_{{}_{D}}(s)
has a double pole at
s=0
with residue
L^{\prime}(0,{\chi_{{}_{D}}})
and a pole at
s=-1
with residue
\frac{\pi\,L(-1,\,{\chi_{D}})}{\sqrt{D}}
and no other poles. Fix
\sigma>1.
Using inverse Mellin transform (after shifting the line of integration to the left), we have

\displaystyle F_{{}_{D}}(iy)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\limits_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}\widetilde{F}_{{}_{D}}(s)y^{-s}\,ds

\displaystyle=L^{\prime}(0,{\chi_{{}_{D}}})+y\cdot\frac{\pi\,L(-1,{\chi_{{}_{D}}})}{\sqrt{D}}+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\limits_{-\sigma-i\infty}^{-\sigma+i\infty}\widetilde{F}_{{}_{D}}(s)y^{-s}\,ds

\displaystyle=L^{\prime}(0,{\chi_{{}_{D}}})+y\cdot\frac{\pi\,L(-1,{\chi_{{}_{D}}})}{\sqrt{D}}+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\limits_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}\widetilde{F}_{{}_{D}}(-s)y^{s}\,ds.

Now we apply Lemma
2.9
. It follows that

\displaystyle F_{{}_{D}}(iy)

\displaystyle=L^{\prime}(0,{\chi_{{}_{D}}})+y\cdot\frac{\pi\,L(-1,{\chi_{{}_{D}}})}{\sqrt{D}}+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int\limits_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}\widetilde{F}^{\#}_{{}_{D}}(s)y^{s}\,ds

(2.10)

\displaystyle=L^{\prime}(0,{\chi_{{}_{D}}})+y\cdot\frac{\pi\,L(-1,{\chi_{{}_{D}}})}{\sqrt{D}}+F_{{}_{D}}^{\#}(i/y).

Finally, we exponentiate both sides of (
2.3
). Using the identities (
2.6
) and (
2.8
), we obtain

\Phi_{{}_{D}}(iy)^{-1}=e^{L^{\prime}(0,{\chi_{{}_{D}}})+y\cdot\frac{\pi\,L(-1,{\chi_{D}})}{\sqrt{D}}}\cdot\Phi_{{}_{D}}^{\#}(i/y)^{-1}

which is equivalent to

This completes the proof. ∎

3.
Modular Forms on
H(\sqrt{D})
: Proofs of Theorems
1.6
and
1.10

3.1.
Preliminaries

Here, we prove Theorems
1.6
and
1.10
. We begin with the following lemma regarding values of Dirichlet
L
-functions associated to quadratic characters at
s=-1
. This has been alluded to before (see Zagier
[
21
, Pg. 54]
). We provide a complete proof for the reader’s convenience.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
D\equiv 1\bmod{4}
with
D>0
be a fundamental discriminant of a real quadratic field. Define
\chi_{{}_{D}}
to be the primitive real character with conductor
D
. Then we have

L(-1,{\chi_{D}}):=\begin{cases}-\frac{2}{5},&\textrm{if }D=5\\
-2m,\quad\textrm{for some $m\in\mathbb{N}$},&\textrm{if }D>5.\end{cases}

Proof.

The case
D=5
can be verified numerically. So we may assume
D>5
. We have

\displaystyle L\left(-1,{\chi_{{}_{D}}}\right)

\displaystyle=D\sum_{n=1}^{D}{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(n)\zeta\left(-1,n/D\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{D}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{D}{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(n)\left(\frac{n^{2}}{D^{2}}-\frac{n}{D}+\frac{1}{6}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2D}\sum_{n=1}^{D}n^{2}{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(n),

where
\zeta(s,a)
denotes the Hurwitz zeta function and since
{\chi_{{}_{D}}}
is even, we have noted that

\sum_{n=1}^{D}{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(n)=\sum_{n=1}^{D}n\cdot{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(n)=0.

Writing

S({\chi_{{}_{D}}})=\sum_{n=1}^{D}n^{2}{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(n),

it suffices to show that
S({\chi_{{}_{D}}})
is divisible by
4D
. Since
{\chi_{{}_{D}}}
is primitive and real,
D
must be squarefree. We consider the following cases.

Case 1:

D
is prime. We write

S({\chi_{{}_{D}}})=\sum_{n=1}^{D}n^{2}{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq D-1\\
n\equiv\square\bmod D\end{subarray}}n^{2}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq D-1\\
n\not\equiv\square\bmod D\end{subarray}}n^{2}=S_{1}({\chi_{{}_{D}}})-S_{2}({\chi_{{}_{D}}}),

say. Let
g
be a primitive root of
D
. Then the non-residues are congruent to the odd powers of
g
. Therefore, their squares are congruent to
g^{2},g^{6},g^{10}
, and so on up to
g^{2q-4}
. Therefore, we have

(3.2)

\displaystyle\left(g^{4}-1\right)S_{2}({\chi_{{}_{D}}})\equiv g^{2}\left(g^{2(D-1)}-1\right)\bmod D.

The right hand side of (
3.2
) is congruent to
0
by Fermat’s Little Theorem. Since
D>5
, we have
g^{4}-1\not\equiv 0\bmod D
. This implies that
S_{2}({\chi_{{}_{D}}})\equiv 0\bmod D
. Since
S_{1}({\chi{{}_{D}}})+S_{2}({\chi_{{}_{D}}})
is the sum of squares up to
D-1
, we also have
S_{1}({\chi_{{}_{D}}})+S_{2}({\chi_{{}_{D}}})\equiv 0\bmod D
. It follows that
S({\chi_{{}_{D}}})\equiv 0\bmod D
.

To check divisibility by
4
, note that since
-1
is a quadratic residue modulo
D
,
S_{1}({\chi_{{}_{D}}})
is odd if
D\equiv 5\bmod 8
and even if
D\equiv 1\bmod 8
. The claim is now immediate by writing

S_{1}({\chi_{{}_{D}}})-S_{2}({\chi_{{}_{D}}})=2S_{1}({\chi_{{}_{D}}})-\frac{(D-1)D(2D-1)}{6},

and carrying out a parity check.

Case 2:

D
is squarefree of the form
D=\prod_{i=1}^{k}p_{i},
for
p_{i}
prime and
k\geq 2
. We only consider the case when
k=2
. The argument is similar for
k>2
.

We write
D=p_{1}p_{2}
. By the Chinese Remainder Theorem, we can break
S({\chi_{{}_{D}}})
as

\displaystyle S({\chi_{{}_{D}}})

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n_{1}\leq p_{1}\\
n_{1}\equiv\square\bmod p_{1}\end{subarray}}n_{1}^{2}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n_{2}\leq p_{2}\\
n_{2}\equiv\square\bmod p_{2}\end{subarray}}n_{2}^{2}\bigg)-\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n_{1}\leq p_{1}\\
n_{1}\not\equiv\square\bmod p_{1}\end{subarray}}n_{1}^{2}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n_{2}\leq p_{2}\\
n_{2}\equiv\square\bmod p_{2}\end{subarray}}n_{2}^{2}\bigg)

(3.3)

\displaystyle\quad-\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n_{1}\leq p_{1}\\
n_{1}\equiv\square\bmod p_{1}\end{subarray}}n_{1}^{2}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n_{2}\leq p_{2}\\
n_{2}\not\equiv\square\bmod p_{2}\end{subarray}}n_{2}^{2}\bigg)+\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n_{1}\leq p_{1}\\
n_{1}\not\equiv\square\bmod p_{1}\end{subarray}}n_{1}^{2}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n_{2}\leq p_{2}\\
n_{2}\not\equiv\square\bmod p_{2}\end{subarray}}n_{2}^{2}\bigg).

By Case 1, each term on the right hand side of (
3.1
) is divisible by both
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, and hence by
q
. It follows that
S({\chi_{{}_{D}}})\equiv 0\bmod D
. As for divisibility by
4
, we observe that, by Case 1, the sum of the first two terms on the right-hand side of (
3.1
) is congruent to
0\bmod 4
. Likewise, the sum of the last two terms of (
3.1
) is also congruent to
0\bmod 4
.
∎

By (
1.4
), (
1.9
) and Lemma
3.1
, we see that for
z\in\mathfrak{h}
,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\eta_{{}_{D}}(z)=e^{-\frac{\pi iL(-1,{\chi_{D}})}{\sqrt{D}}\cdot z}\cdot\Phi_{{}_{D}}(z)\Phi_{{}_{D}}^{\#}(z).

Using Theorem
2.4
, we have the relations

(3.5)

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{\pi iL(-1,{\chi_{D}})}{\sqrt{D}}\cdot z}\cdot\Phi_{{}_{D}}(z)

\displaystyle=e^{-L^{\prime}(0,{\chi_{{}_{D}}})}\cdot\Phi^{\#}_{{{}_{D}}}(-1/z)

(3.6)

\displaystyle\textrm{and}\quad\Phi_{{}_{D}}^{\#}(z)

\displaystyle=e^{L^{\prime}(0,{\chi_{{}_{D}}})}\cdot e^{-\frac{\pi iL(-1,{\chi_{D}})}{\sqrt{D}}\cdot\frac{-1}{z}}\cdot\Phi_{{}_{D}}(-1/z).

Multiplying the two relations above, we obtain

(3.7)

e^{-\frac{\pi iL(-1,{\chi_{D}})}{\sqrt{D}}\cdot z}\cdot\Phi_{{}_{D}}(z)\Phi_{{}_{D}}^{\#}(z)\;=e^{-\frac{\pi iL(-1,{\chi_{D}})}{\sqrt{D}}\cdot\frac{-1}{z}}\cdot\Phi_{{}_{D}}(-1/z)\Phi^{\#}_{{{}_{D}}}(-1/z).

3.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.6

Since
z\in\mathfrak{h}
, the products in (
1.4
) are convergent, and thus
\eta_{5}(z)
is holomorphic over
\mathfrak{h}
. As mentioned in Remark
1.7
, we will show that for every

\gamma\in H\big(\sqrt{5}\big)
we have

\eta_{5}\left(\gamma z\right)=u_{\gamma}\cdot\eta_{5}(z)

where
u_{\gamma}
is a certain fifth root of unity. In fact, in what follows, we precisely compute
u_{\gamma}
.

By (
3.4
) and (
3.7
) , we have

(3.8)

\displaystyle\eta_{5}(z+\sqrt{5})

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{5}}\eta_{5}(z)

(3.9)

\displaystyle\textrm{and}\quad\eta_{5}(-1/z)

\displaystyle=\eta_{5}(z).

Choose any element of
H(\sqrt{5})
. It can be expressed in the form

\alpha(k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{\ell}):=T_{\sqrt{5}}^{k_{1}}\cdot S\cdot T_{\sqrt{5}}^{k_{2}}\cdot S\cdots T_{\sqrt{5}}^{k_{\ell-1}}\cdot S\cdot T_{\sqrt{5}}^{k_{\ell}}

with
k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}
, where

T_{\sqrt{5}}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&\sqrt{5}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\textrm{and}\quad S=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&\phantom{-}0\end{pmatrix}.

We consider the following two cases.

Case 1 :

\ell
is even. In this case, it is straightforward to check that the matrix
\gamma
is of the form

\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a\sqrt{5}&b\\
c&d\,\sqrt{5}\end{pmatrix},

where
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}
. We claim that

(3.10)

\displaystyle u_{\gamma}=e^{\frac{2\pi ic(a+d)}{5}}.

To prove this, we proceed by induction. First, by (
3.8
) and (
3.9
), we have

(3.11)

\eta_{5}\Big(\alpha(k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell})\,z\big)=e^{{\frac{2\pi i(k_{1}+k_{2}+\cdots+k_{\ell})}{5}}}\cdot\eta_{5}(z).

For the base case, note that an element of the form
\alpha(k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell})
with
\ell
even and the smallest number of
S
-terms is

\alpha(k_{1},k_{2})=T_{\sqrt{5}}^{k_{1}}\cdot S\cdot T_{\sqrt{5}}^{k_{2}}=\begin{pmatrix}k_{1}\sqrt{5}&-1+5k_{1}k_{2}\\
1&k_{2}\sqrt{5}\end{pmatrix}.

One immediately checks using (
3.11
) that (
3.10
) holds in this case. We additionally note the congruence conditions
bc\equiv-1\bmod{5}
and
c^{2}\equiv 1\bmod 5
, which we use in our induction step.

Let

\alpha(k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{2n})=\begin{pmatrix}a\sqrt{5}&b\\
c&d\,\sqrt{5}\end{pmatrix}

where
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}
and the congruence conditions
bc\equiv-1\bmod{5}
and
c^{2}\equiv 1\bmod 5
holds. Assume that (
3.10
) holds for
\alpha(k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{2n})
. We now show that this assumption implies (
3.10
) holds for
\alpha(k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{2n+2})
. Note that

S\cdot T_{\sqrt{5}}^{k_{2n+1}}\cdot S\cdot T_{\sqrt{5}}^{k_{2n+2}}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&-k_{2n+2}\,\sqrt{5}\\
k_{2n+1}\,\sqrt{5}&\;\;-1+5k_{2n+1}k_{2n+2}\end{pmatrix}.

Then

\displaystyle\alpha(k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{2n+2})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}a\sqrt{5}&b\\
c&d\,\sqrt{5}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}-1&-k_{2n+2}\,\sqrt{5}\\
k_{2n+1}\,\sqrt{5}&\;\;-1+5k_{2n+1}k_{2n+2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a^{\prime}\sqrt{5}&b^{\prime}\\
c^{\prime}&d^{\prime}\,\sqrt{5}\end{pmatrix},

where we explicitly compute that

\displaystyle a^{\prime}\equiv 4a+bk_{2n+1}\bmod{5}

\displaystyle b^{\prime}\equiv 4b\bmod{5}

\displaystyle c^{\prime}\equiv 4c\bmod{5}

\displaystyle d^{\prime}\equiv 4d+4c\,k_{2n+2}\bmod{5}.

It follows
(
using the fact that
bc\equiv-1\bmod{5}
and
c^{2}\equiv 1\bmod{5}
)
that

\displaystyle c^{\prime}(a^{\prime}+d^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv ac+cd+4bc\,k_{2n+1}+c^{2}\,k_{2n+2}\bmod{5}

\displaystyle\equiv c(a+d)+k_{2n+1}+k_{2n+2}\bmod{5}.

It follows using (
3.11
) that (
3.10
) holds for
\alpha(k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{2n+2})
. The desired claim follows.

Case 2 :

\ell
is odd. In this case, the matrix
\gamma
is of the form

\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\sqrt{5}\\
c\sqrt{5}&d\,\end{pmatrix},

where
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then we have

S\cdot\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\,\sqrt{5}\\
c\,\sqrt{5}&d\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-c\,\sqrt{5}&-d\\
a&b\,\sqrt{5}\end{pmatrix}.

By (
3.9
) and Case 1, we have

\eta_{5}(\gamma z)=\eta_{5}(S\gamma z)=u_{S\gamma}\cdot\eta_{5}(z)=e^{\frac{2\pi ia(b-c)}{5}}\eta_{5}(z).

Therefore, in this case, one obtains

(3.12)

\displaystyle u_{\gamma}=e^{\frac{2\pi ia(b-c)}{5}}.

Combining (
3.10
) and (
3.12
), we have for any
\gamma\in H(\sqrt{5})
,

\eta_{5}(\gamma z)=u_{\gamma}\cdot\eta_{5}(z),

where
u_{\gamma}
is a certain fifth root of unity. Consequently,
\Delta_{5}(z)=\eta_{5}(z)^{5}
is invariant under the action of
H(\sqrt{5})
, thereby establishing the modularity of
\Delta_{5}
as stated in Theorem
1.6
. Moreover, from the definition in (
1.4
), it follows that
\Delta_{5}(z)
vanishes at the cusp
\infty
, that is,
\lim_{y\to\infty}\Delta_{5}(iy)=0.
∎

. For

(3.13)

\displaystyle\eta_{{}_{D}}(z+\sqrt{D})=e^{-\pi iL(-1,{\chi_{{}_{D}}})}\cdot\eta_{{}_{D}}(z)=\eta_{{}_{D}}(z).

(3.15)

4.
Fourier Coefficients and Partitions : Proof of Theorem
1.14

In this section, we aim to establish some interesting connections between the Fourier coefficients of
\eta_{{}_{D}}(z)
and the theory of partitions, which will lead to the proof of Theorem
1.14
. Let
D\equiv 1\bmod{4}
with
D>0
be a fundamental discriminant of a real quadratic field. Recall that
q=e^{\frac{2\pi iz}{\sqrt{D}}}
. Setting
\zeta_{{}_{D}}=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{D}},
we write

\displaystyle\eta_{{}_{D}}(z)

\displaystyle=q^{-\frac{L(-1,{\chi_{D}})}{2}}\cdot\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{n})^{{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(n)}\prod_{a=1}^{D}(1-\zeta_{{}_{D}}^{a}\cdot q^{n})^{{\chi_{{}_{D}}}(a)}

(4.1)

\displaystyle=q^{-\frac{L(-1,{\chi_{D}})}{2}}\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}a_{{}_{D}}(N)\,q^{N}.

Let
\theta\in\mathbb{C}
with
\lvert\theta\rvert\leq 1
. We define the following three products:

(4.2)

\displaystyle F_{\textrm{ord}}(q,\theta)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=1\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\left(1-\theta\cdot q^{n}\right)^{-1}

(4.3)

\displaystyle F_{e}(q,\theta)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=1\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\left(1-\theta\cdot q^{n}\right),

(4.4)

\displaystyle F_{\textrm{NR}}(q,D)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}n=1\\
n\not\equiv\square\bmod D\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\left(1-q^{n}\right)^{-1}.

Then we have the relation

\displaystyle\eta_{{}_{D}}(z)

\displaystyle=q^{-\frac{L(-1,{\chi_{D}})}{2}}\cdot F_{\textrm{NR}}(q,D)^{2}\cdot F_{e}(q,1)\cdot F_{\textrm{ord}}(q^{5},1)

(4.5)

\displaystyle\quad\times\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}a=1\\
a\equiv\square\bmod D\end{subarray}}^{D}F_{e}(q,\zeta_{{}_{D}}^{a})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}a=1\\
a\not\equiv\square\bmod D\end{subarray}}^{D}F_{\textrm{ord}}(q,\zeta_{{}_{D}}^{a}).

By Euler’s Pentagonal Number Theorem, we have for
\lvert q\rvert<1
,

(4.6)

\displaystyle F_{e}(q,\theta)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\left(\theta^{3k-1}q^{k(3k-1)/2}+\theta^{3k}q^{k(3k+1)/2}\right)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}p_{e}(k,\theta)\,q^{g(k)},

where we set

g(k)=\frac{k(3k-1)}{2},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z},

and

p_{e}(k,\theta)=\begin{cases}(-1)^{k}\cdot\theta^{3k-1},&\textrm{if }k>0\\

Note that the sequence
\{g(k)\}
forms the generalized pentagonal numbers. Next, we write
\lambda\vdash k
to denote that
\lambda
is a partition of
k
. Then we have

(4.7)

\displaystyle F_{\textrm{ord}}(q,\theta)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{\textrm{ord}}(k,\theta)\,q^{k},

p_{\textrm{ord}}(k,\theta)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash k}\theta^{\ell(\lambda)},

and
\ell(\lambda)
denotes the number of parts of the partition
\lambda
. In particular, when
\theta=1
, we obtain

, the ordinary partition function. Finally, we also have

(4.8)

\displaystyle F_{\textrm{NR}}(q,D)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{\textrm{NR}}(k,D)\,q^{k},\quad\lvert q\rvert<1,

where
p_{\textrm{NR}}(k,D)
denotes the number of partitions of
k
into parts which are all quadratic non-residues modulo
D
.

Putting together (
4
)–(
4.8
), we can write

(4.9)

\displaystyle a_{{}_{D}}(N)=\sum_{\vec{k}}\sum_{\vec{\ell}}\sum_{\vec{m}}p_{\textrm{NR}}(k_{1},D)p_{\textrm{NR}}(k_{2},D)\prod_{i=0}^{\varphi(D)/2}p_{e}(\ell_{i},\zeta_{{}_{D}}^{a_{i}})\prod_{j=0}^{\varphi(D)/2}p_{\textrm{ord}}(m_{j},\zeta_{{}_{D}}^{b_{j}}).

Here we set
a_{0}=b_{0}=1
and
\{a_{i}\}
and
\{b_{j}\}
denote the
i
-th quadratic residue and the
j
-th quadratic non-residue modulo
D
, respectively, in the interval
[1,D]
, where
1\leq i,j\leq\varphi(D)/2
. Also,
\varphi(n)
denotes the Euler totient function. The sum in (
4.9
) is taken subject to the following notation and conditions:

\displaystyle\vec{k}

\displaystyle=(k_{1},k_{2}),\,0\leq k_{1},k_{2}\leq N,

\displaystyle\vec{\ell}

\displaystyle=(\ell_{0},\ell_{1},\cdots,\ell_{\frac{\varphi(D)}{2}}),\,\ell_{i}\in\mathbb{Z},\,0\leq g(\ell_{i})\leq N,

\displaystyle\vec{m}

\displaystyle=(m_{0},m_{1},\cdots,m_{\frac{\varphi(D)}{2}}),\,0\leq 5m_{0},m_{j}\leq N,\textrm{ for all $j\geq 1$ },

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=k_{1}+k_{2}+\sum_{i=0}^{\varphi(D)/2}g(\ell_{i})\,+5m_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{\varphi(D)/2}m_{j}.

In particular, using (
4.9
), we can express the Fourier coefficients
a_{{}_{D}}(N)
in terms of certain classical functions related to partitions. Furthermore, by Rademacher’s work
[
17
,
18
]
, we have

(4.10)

\displaystyle p_{\textrm{ord}}(k,1)=p(k)=\frac{2\pi}{(24k-1)^{\frac{3}{4}}}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{A_{m}(k)}{m}\cdot I_{\frac{3}{2}}\left(\frac{\pi}{6m}\sqrt{24k-1}\right),

where
A_{m}(k)
is a certain Kloosterman sum and

is the modified Bessel function of first kind. Also, there are multiple results in the literature regarding exact formulas for
p_{\textrm{NR}}(k,D)
. For instance, when
D=5
, by work of Lehner
[
13
]
, we have

\displaystyle p_{\textrm{NR}}(k,5)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{(60k+11)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m>0\\
5\nmid m\end{subarray}}\frac{\widetilde{A}_{m}(k)}{m}I_{1}\bigg(\frac{\pi(60k+11)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{15m}\bigg)

(4.11)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\pi}{(60k+11)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m>0\\
5\nmid m\end{subarray}}\left|\csc\frac{\pi am}{5}\right|\frac{\widetilde{B}_{m}(k)}{m}I_{1}\bigg(\frac{\pi(60k+11)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{15m}\bigg)

where
\widetilde{A}_{m}(k)
and
\widetilde{B}_{m}(k)
are certain Dedekind sums and
I_{1}
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind. For general
D
, interested readers are referred to the works of Livingood
[
14
]
, Grosswald

3
]
, Hagis

]
and the references therein.

Remark 4.12
.

In connection with the above discussion, we observe that each factor in (
4.9
) can be written down explicitly, with the exception of the terms of the form
p_{\mathrm{ord}}(m_{j},\zeta_{{}_{D}}^{b_{j}})
. A typical such factor is of the form

(4.13)

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda\vdash k}\exp\left(\frac{2\pi ib\,\ell(\lambda)}{D}\right),

for some quadratic non-residue
b\bmod D
. Thus the problem naturally reduces to understanding exponential sums involving the number of parts
\ell(\lambda)
. Earlier works of Erdős–-Lehner
[
2
]
and Hwang
[
7
]
established limiting distribution results for
\ell(\lambda)
, including central limit theorem type phenomena. The relation (
4.9
), together with (
4.13
), suggests that Conjectures
1.16
and
1.17
may be connected to finer questions concerning the distribution of
\ell(\lambda)
in arithmetic progressions. This would be in close analogy to the study of the partition function
p(n)
in arithmetic progressions, as in the work of Ono
[
15
]
.

Proof of Theorem
1.14
.

We first show that
a_{{}_{D}}(N)\in\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
. To establish this, it suffices to study only the coefficients arising from the products over
a
in (
4
). We treat the case where the product runs over the quadratic residues modulo
D
; the argument for the quadratic non-residues is concomitant.

Since
D\equiv 1\bmod{4}
, we have
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})\subseteq\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{{}_{D}})
. Upon expanding

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}a=1\\
a\equiv\square\bmod D\end{subarray}}^{D}(1-\zeta_{{}_{D}}^{a}q^{n}),

the resulting coefficients are elementary symmetric polynomials in
\zeta_{{}_{D}}^{a}
with
a
quadratic residues modulo
D
. Consider the Galois group
\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{{}_{D}})/\mathbb{Q})
, which has order
\varphi(D)
. Let
H
denote the subgroup defined by

H=\left\{\sigma\in\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{{}_{D}})/\mathbb{Q}):\sigma(\zeta_{{}_{D}})=\zeta_{{}_{D}}^{k}\text{ for some }k\in(\mathbb{Z}/D\mathbb{Z})^{*}\text{ with }\left(\frac{k}{D}\right)=1\right\}.

This subgroup
H
has index
2
, and its fixed field is quadratic. Moreover, the classical Gauss sum

G_{D}:=\sum_{a=1}^{D}\left(\frac{a}{D}\right)\zeta_{{}_{D}}^{a}

is equal to
\sqrt{D}
and is fixed by all elements of
H
. Therefore, the fixed field of
H
is
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
. Since symmetric polynomials in
\zeta_{{}_{D}}^{a}
(with
a
ranging over quadratic residues modulo
D
) are fixed under
H
, the coefficients lie in
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
. Also, the expressions involve only roots of unity, so they are algebraic integers. Hence, the coefficients lie in the ring of integers of
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
, which is
\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}\right]
.

Now, we establish the desired bounds on
a_{{}_{D}}(N)
. For
\lvert\theta\rvert\leq 1
and
k\geq 1
, we have

(4.14)

\displaystyle\lvert p_{e}(k,\theta)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq 1,

(4.15)

\displaystyle p_{\textrm{ord}}(k,\theta)\leq p(k)

\displaystyle\ll e^{c_{0}\sqrt{k}}\cdot k^{-1},

where
c_{0}=(\pi/\sqrt{3})\cdot\sqrt{2}
, and

(4.16)

\displaystyle p_{\textrm{NR}}(k,D)\ll_{{}_{D}}e^{c_{{}_{D}}\sqrt{k}}\cdot k^{-3/4},

for some positive constant
c_{{}_{D}}
depending only on the modulus
D
. Trivially, one may take
c_{{}_{D}}=c_{0}
. When
D\equiv 1\bmod 4
is prime, we have

c_{{}_{D}}=(\pi/\sqrt{3})\cdot\sqrt{(D-1)/D},

see for instance, Grosswald
[
3
]
. Substituting the above bounds in (
4.9
), we obtain

(4.17)

\displaystyle\lvert a_{{}_{D}}(N)\rvert\ll_{{}_{D}}\sum_{\vec{k}}\sum_{\vec{\ell}}\sum_{\vec{m}}\frac{e^{c_{{}_{D}}(\sqrt{k_{1}}+\sqrt{k_{2}})}}{(1+k_{1}^{3/4})(1+k_{2}^{3/4})}\cdot\prod_{j=0}^{\varphi(D)/2}\frac{e^{c_{0}\sqrt{m_{j}}}}{1+m_{j}}.

An application of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality yields

\displaystyle c_{{}_{D}}(\sqrt{k_{1}}+\sqrt{k_{2}})+\sum_{j=0}^{\varphi(D)/2}c_{0}\sqrt{m_{j}}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(2c_{{}_{D}}^{2}+c_{0}^{2}\bigg(\frac{\varphi(D)}{2}+\frac{1}{5}\bigg)\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\quad\times\bigg(k_{1}+k_{2}+5m_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{\varphi(D)/2}m_{j}\bigg)^{1/2}

(4.18)

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{c}_{{}_{D}}\sqrt{N},

where we set

\widetilde{c}_{{}_{D}}=\bigg(2c_{{}_{D}}^{2}+c_{0}^{2}\bigg(\frac{\varphi(D)}{2}+\frac{1}{5}\bigg)\bigg)^{1/2}.

Substituting (
4
) in (
4.17
), we arrive at

(4.19)

\displaystyle\lvert a_{{}_{D}}(n)\rvert\ll_{{}_{D}}e^{\widetilde{c}_{D}\sqrt{N}}\cdot N^{\varphi(D)/4+1}\cdot(\log(N+2))^{\varphi(D)/2+1},

where we have noted that the entries
\ell_{i}
of
\vec{\ell}
is of size at most
\sqrt{N}
. This completes the proof.
∎
