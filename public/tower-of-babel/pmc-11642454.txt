Radotinib-induced Eruptive Melanocytic Naevi in a Patient with Chronic Myeloid Leukaemia
Authors: Jung Min Lee, Byeol Han, June H. Lee, Tae Young Han, Jae Eun Choi
Published: 2024

Sir,

Development of eruptive melanocytic naevi (EMN) refers to the phenomenon of the sudden development of hundreds of melanocytic naevi associated with various underlying factors.[1] These factors include trauma, pregnancy, psoralen plus ultraviolet A therapy, malignancy, severe bullous diseases, immunocompromised states due to renal transplantation or bone-marrow transplantation, and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome. In addition, the development of EMN has been associated with drugs such as immunosuppressants, chemotherapeutics, and biologics.[12] We report a case of EMN induced by radotinib, a selective BCR/ABL tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), in a patient with chronic myeloid leukaemia.

A 33-year-old male presented with a dark-brownish pigmented macular eruption over his entire body that had started to appear 5 years earlier. He had a history of chronic myeloid leukaemia and started radotinib treatment (600 mg/day) 7 years ago. He also had a history of ankylosing spondylitis and was treated with naproxen and sulfasalazine. He had noticed the number of pigmented lesions becoming significantly numerous over the past 3 years. Dermatological examination revealed approximately 400 monomorphous brownish pigmented macules distributed over the whole body: 40 on the face, 35 on the neck, 60 on the trunk, 150 on the arms, and 80 on the legs [Figure 1]. Lesions were more prominently distributed on areas prone to sun exposure such as the cheeks, neck, and forearms. Histopathological examination of one lesion from his arm revealed slightly elongated rete ridges, a lentiginous proliferation of melanocytes along the basal layer with mild-to-moderate cytologic atypia, and scattered melanophages in the superficial dermis [Figure 2]. Shouldering phenomenon was not observed. Based on the clinical history of the eruption of monomorphous brownish macules post radotinib initiation and pathological findings consistent with melanocytic naevi, a diagnosis of radotinib-induced EMN was made. Reassurance was provided, and he was treated with carbon dioxide ablative laser for cosmetic reasons. To date, he remains on radotinib treatment with no new appearance of lesions.

Despite a variety of aetiological factors, EMN can notably be divided into two groups: drug-associated EMN and skin disease-associated EMN. Drug-associated EMN has recently gained significant attention, mainly due to an increased use of immunomodulatory drugs. EMN related to medication use are characterised by the development of one or more of the following conditions within the first 6 months of initiating a new medication: the appearance of more than five palmoplantar melanocytic naevi at any age, the emergence of more than 10 melanocytic naevi outside the periods of puberty and pregnancy, or the development of more than 20 melanocytic naevi during puberty or pregnancy.[12] TKIs are a group of small molecules that disrupt signal transduction by inhibiting several protein kinases. As numerous TKIs are implemented in the treatment of various cancers nowadays, TKIs have emerged as a major trigger of drug-associated EMN.[3456] Radotinib is a second-generation TKI that selectively binds to BCR-ABL1 and inhibits cellular signal transduction.[46] Although maculopapular rashes and pruritus have been most commonly associated with TKIs, this drug class has been associated with pigmentary changes such as hypopigmentation and hyperpigmentation, with hyperpigmentation being more rarely reported.[345] Although there have been multiple reports of EMN associated with various TKIs, the number of reports induced by radotinib is relatively limited. All previously reported cases of radotinib-induced EMN were of adults between the ages of 20 and 85 years. The onset of EMN after initiation of radotinib varied from as soon as 1 month to as long as 45 months. Cases have been reported with numerous to as many as over 1000 melanocytic naevi distributed over the whole body.[4678]

The exact mechanism behind the development of EMN is not yet fully understood, but it is postulated to be the consequence of drug-induced immunosuppression that downregulates the regulators of melanocyte survival resulting in the proliferation of melanocytes.[17] Another hypothesis specific to radotinib-induced EMN is the relative activation of c-kit compared to other TKIs. TKIs inhibit not only BCR-ABL but also c-kit, which plays an important role in the proliferation of melanocytes and upregulation of pigmentation-related genes such as tyrosine.[478] Thus, the downregulation of c-kit may induce TKI-associated hypopigmentation. Radotinib, unlike other TKIs, only selectively inhibits BCR-ABL1, resulting in the reduced suppression of c-kit, which causes paradoxical activation of c-kit-related hyperpigmentation.[48] Although long-term follow-up data are not readily available, most of the previously reported cases of drug-induced EMN lesions remained stable or faded after secession of treatment.[2] However, an immunosuppressed state, an increased number of melanocytic naevi, and having more than five dysplastic naevi are risk factors for melanoma; thus, regular monitoring for new lesions and dysplastic changes is advised in these patients, especially in those with pre-existing dysplastic lesions.[1-29]

Herein, we report an unusual case of radotinib-induced EMN in a patient with chronic myeloid leukaemia. As the use of biological agents such as TKIs is increasing, dermatologists should be aware of this potential cutaneous adverse reaction for proper diagnosis and management. Further studies with long-term follow-up of patients with drug-associated EMN are needed for proper assessment of the risk of malignancy development in these patients.
