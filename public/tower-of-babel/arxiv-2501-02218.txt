Introduction

In recent years a great attention has been devoted to nonlocal functionals in the form of double integrals because of their many different applications; see, for instance
[
12
,
13
,
16
,
17
]
, and the bibliography contained therein, among a wide literature. New mathematical topics, inspired by machine
learning issues, presented models involving supremal
(or
L^{\infty}
-)functionals in the nonlocal setting, see
[
19
,
23
,
22
,

,
27
,

where energies are either of the form

{\rm ess}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sup_{(x,y)\in\Omega\times\Omega}f(u(x),u(y)),

with
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
a suitable lower semicontinuous and coercive function, and
u\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
, possibly satisfying a PDE’s constraint, or

{\rm ess}\sup_{x\in\Omega}f(x,u(x),\nabla^{\alpha}u(x)),

with
f:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{N\times m}\to\mathbb{R}
a suitable integrand and
u\in\mathcal{S}^{\alpha,\infty}_{u_{0}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
, with
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\nabla^{\alpha}u
being the Riesz weak fractional gradient and the space
S^{\alpha,\infty}_{u_{0}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})=\{u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{m}),\nabla^{\alpha}u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}),u=u_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega\}
.

The interest towards supremal functionals started with the pioneering works by Aronsson, see
[
5

,
7
,
8
]
and has been later developed by many authors see
[
1
,
11
]
, both in connection with the so-called power-law approximation (see, for instance,
[
18
]
and
[
14
]
for developments in wider contexts than classical Sobolev spaces) and in relation with the
\infty
-laplacian type equations. We refer to
[
9
,
20
,
21
]
for recent advances on this topic.

L^{\infty}
energies defined at jump points of piecewise continuous functions on the real line were first analyzed in
[
2
]
.
Indeed, the
L^{1}
-lower semicontinuity of the functional

{\rm ess}\sup_{t\in I}g([u](t)),

(1.1)

where
I
is a bounded open interval of the real line,
u
is a piecewise constant function in the sense of
[
4
]
and

[u](r):=\lim_{\varrho\to r^{+}}u(\varrho)-\lim_{\varrho\to r^{-}}u(\varrho),

(1.2)

was characterized in terms of the lower semicontinuity and the
submaximality
of the density
g
, which can be seen as the supremal counterpart of
BV
-ellpiticty introduced by
[
3
]
to characterize the lower semicontinuity of elliptic integrands defined on piecewise constant functions.

More specifically, we recall that a function
g:\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be submaximal if the following inequality
holds true for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
such that
x_{1}\not=-x_{2}
:

g(x_{1}+x_{2})\leqslant\max\{g(x_{1}),g(x_{2})\}.

It is also possible to consider
g
as defined in whole
\mathbb{R}
, even
though its value in
0
is never taken into account, by setting
g(0)=\inf g
. Note
that, in
[
2
]
,
g
is submaximal and lower semicontinuous in
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
if and only if its
extension to
0
is submaximal and lower semicontinuous.

Taking into account the models described before, we consider here the following nonlocal energy

H(u):={\rm ess}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sup_{(s,t)\in S(u)\times S(u)}h([u](s),[u](t)),

where, as in (
1.1
),
u
is a piecewise constant function on
I
and
S(u)
denotes the set of discontinuity points of
u
.

In fact, under natural assumptions on the supremand
h:(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\times(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\to\mathbb{R}
, which we refer to Definition
3.1
for, we detect necessary and sufficient conditions on
h
for the
L^{1}
-lower semicontinuity of
H
, namely, we prove the following result:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
h:(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\times(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\to\mathbb{R}
be diagonal and symmetric and let
H
be the functional defined by (
2.2
).

H
is lower semicontinuous with respect to
L^{1}(I)
convergence if and only if
h
is lower semicontinuous and Cartesian submaximal.

2.
Notation and preliminaries

For the reader’s convenience, we mainly adopt the notation in
[
2
]
.
Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be open and bounded. We recall that a function
u\in L^{1}(I)
is
a function of bounded variation if its distributional derivative
Du
is a measure
in
I
. In this case
u
is approximately differentiable almost everywhere and its
a.e. derivative is denoted by
u^{\prime}
; moreover the set of approximate discontinuity
points (or jump set) of
u
, denoted by
S(u)
, is at most countable and at each point

t\in S(u)
there exist the right hand and left hand approximate limits, denoted by

u(t^{\pm})
. We denote by
[u](t)
the difference between the two sides approximate limits, i.e.
[u](t):=u(t^{+})-u(t^{-})
,
the so called jump of
u
at
t,
as in (
1.2
). With this notation, the distributional derivative
Du
admits the
decomposition

Du=u^{\prime}\lfloor{\mathcal{L}}^{1}+D^{s}u=u^{\prime}\lfloor{\mathcal{L}}^{1}+\sum_{t\in S(u)}(u(t^{+})-u(t^{-}))\delta_{t}+D^{c}u
,
where
\mathcal{L}^{1}
stands for the one-dimensional Lebesgue measure,
\delta_{t}
is the Dirac delta
at
t
, and
D^{c}u
, the Cantor part of
Du
, is a non-atomic measure which is orthogonal
to the Lebesgue measure. The notation
D^{s}u
stands for the singular part of the
measure
Du
(with respect to the Lebesgue measure). The space of functions of
bounded variation in
I
is denoted by
BV(I).
Its subset, constituted by functions which
D^{c}u
is null, is denoted by
SBV(I)
and the ones where
u^{\prime}
is also null is constituted by functions which take countably many constant values, i.e. such that
|D^{j}u|(I)<+\infty
.
Furthermore, when the set where
u
takes constant values has finite perimeter, we say, as in
[
15
]
, that
u\in SBV_{0}(I)
, namely the singular set of
u
is a Caccioppoli’s partition, equivalently the set
S(u)
has finite perimeter, i.e.
\mathcal{H}^{0}(S(u))<+\infty
.
In the subsequent analysis, we will focus on this class of functions, which, as proven in
[
4
]
can be identified with the set of piecewise constant functions, also denoted by
PC(I)
. In the following, as in
[
2
]
, We will also adopt this notation.

For the sake of exposition,
I:=(a,b)
,
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,
a<b
,

Definition 2.1
.

A function
u:(a,b)\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be piecewise constant on
(a,b)
if there exist
points
a=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{N}<t_{N+1}=b
such that

u(t)\hbox{ is constant a.e. on }(t_{i-1},t_{i})\hbox{ for all }i=1,2,\dots,N+1.

(2.1)

Then
S(u)
can be seen as the minimal set
\{t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{N}\}\subset(a,b)
such that (
2.1
) holds. The subspace of
L^{1}(a,b)
of all such
u
is denoted by
PC(a,b)
.

We aim at studying the lower semicontinuity with respect to the
L^{1}
topology of the following nonlocal functional

H(u):PC(I)\to\mathbb{R}
of the form

H(u)=\;\;\;\;{\rm ess}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sup_{(t,s)\in S(u)\times S(u)}h([u](t),[u](s)),

(2.2)

where
h:(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\times(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}.

We observe that there is no loss of generality in assuming
h
bounded, since, as emphasized in
[
1
]
, the analysis regarding the behaviour of the functional
H
is not affected by replacing
h
by the composition
f\circ h
, with
f
strictly increasing, such as
f(t)=\arctan t
.

As mentioned earlier, the goal is to characterize the lower semicontinuity of the functional
H
.

It is also possible to define
h
at points of the type
(\zeta,0)
or
(0,\theta)
, with an abuse of notation, imposing that
h(\zeta,0)=h(0,\theta):=\inf_{(\xi,\eta)\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\times(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})}h(\xi,\eta).

Clearly, it is possible to consider
h
defined in all
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
, tacitly assuming the above values in
(\zeta,0)
and
(0,\theta)
,
\zeta,\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.
With this choice, the functional
H
in (
2.2
) can be replaced by

H(u)={\rm ess}\!\!\!\!\!\!\sup_{(t,s)\in I\times I}h([u](t),[u](s)),

without affecting the analysis that we will present below.

3.
Lower semicontinuity

We start by showing some intermediate results that are important for the proof of Theorem
1.1
below.

As first observed in the integral settind in
[
25
,
26
]
when dealing with nonlocal functionals, and later expanded in
[
24
,
22
]
in the supremal framework, some symmetry of the densities involved in the nonlocal models appear naturally.

For every
m\in\mathbb{N}
, given a set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, following
[
24
, (1.6)]
, we introduce

\displaystyle\hat{E}:=\{(\xi,\eta)\in E:(\xi,\xi),(\eta,\eta),(\eta,\xi)\in E\}.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, and
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

The
c
-(sub)level set of
f
is defined as

L_{c}(f):=\{(\xi,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}:f(\xi,\eta)\leqslant c\}.

Given
h:(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\times(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\to\mathbb{\overline{}}{\mathbb{R}}
, define

\hat{h}(\zeta,\eta):=\inf\{c\in\mathbb{R}:(\zeta,\eta)\in\widehat{L_{c}(h)},(\zeta,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\}.

(3.1)

Definition 3.1
.

A function
h:(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\times(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is said to be symmetric
if
h(\xi,\eta)=h(\eta,\xi)
for every
\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

A symmetric function
h:(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\times(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is said to be diagonal if
h(\xi,\eta)=\hat{h}(\xi,\eta)
for every
\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

Observe that, as in
[
2
]
, the functional
H
in (
2.2
) coincides with

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{0}-{\rm ess}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sup_{(s,t)\in S(u)\times S(u)}h([u](s),[u](t))\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}.

We stress that there is no loss of generality in assuming
h
diagonal and symmetric when evaluating
H
in (
2.2
), and, as observed before, that
h
is finite valued.

First, as proven in
[
22
, eq. (2.8)]
,
\hat{h}
in (
3.1
) admits the following equivalent representation

\displaystyle\hat{h}(\xi,\eta)=\max\{h(\xi,\xi),h(\eta,\eta),h(\eta,\xi),h(\xi,\eta)\}.

(3.2)

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
h:(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\times(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\to\mathbb{R}
and let
\hat{h}
be as in (
3.1
). Then

\displaystyle\hat{H}(u):=\mathcal{H}^{0}-{\rm ess}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sup_{(t,s)\in S(u)\times S(u)}\hat{h}([u](s),[u](t))=H(u).

Proof.

It can be easily verified that the symmetry assumption is not restrictive. It remains to check the diagonality. To this end, it immediately results that
h(\xi,\eta)\leqslant\hat{h}(\xi,\eta)
,
hence
H\leqslant\hat{H}
.
To prove the opposite inequality, we can observe that
{\mathcal{H}}^{0}-{\rm esssup}
in (
2.2
) is indeed a maximum and is evaluated in a finite set of values, thus it will be a value of the type
h([u](\bar{s}),[u](\bar{t}))
with
\bar{s},\bar{t}\in S(u)
, but, clearly by the very definition of (
2.2
), it is greater than
h([u](\bar{t}),[u](\bar{s})),h([u](\bar{t}),[u](\bar{t})),h([u](\bar{s}),[u](\bar{s}))
, hence
h([u](\bar{s}),[u](\bar{t}))=\hat{h}([u](\bar{s}),[u](\bar{t}))
,
hence
\hat{H}\leqslant H
.
∎

In view of the above result, in the sequel, when not explicitly mentioned, we will assume that
h
is diagonal and symmetric.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
h:(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\times(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\to\mathbb{R}
be symmetric and diagonal. We say that
h
is Cartesian submaximal if

h(w_{1}+w_{2},y)\leqslant\text{max}\{h(w_{1},y),h(w_{2},y),h(w_{1},w_{2})\},

(3.3)

for every
w_{1},w_{2},y\in\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 3.4
.

It is also possible to consider Cartesian submaximal functions
h:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
. Then a Cartesian submaximal extension
\tilde{h}
of such functions to
(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\times(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})
could be the following

\tilde{h}(\xi,\eta):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}h(\xi,\eta)&\hbox{ if }(\xi,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+},\\
{\rm ess}\sup_{(\xi,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}}h(\xi,\eta)&\hbox{ otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

Condition
3.3
characterizes the
L^{1}(I)
-lower semicontinuity of
H
in (
2.2
).
In fact, the following result holds.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
H
be
L^{1}(I)
-lower semicontinuous, then, taking two sequences
w_{n}\not=v_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, converging to
w\not=v\in\mathbb{R}
respectively and defining for every
t\in I
and
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{+\infty\}
,

u_{n}(t):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}z&\hbox{ if }t\leqslant t_{0},\\
z+w_{n}&\hbox{ if }t_{0}<t\leqslant t_{1},\\
z+w_{n}+v_{n}&\hbox{ if }t>t_{1},\end{array}\right.\hbox{ and }u_{\infty}(t):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}z&\hbox{ if }t\leqslant t_{0},\\
z+w&\hbox{ if }t_{0}<t\leqslant t_{1},\\
z+w+v&\hbox{ if }t>t_{1},\end{array}\right.

with
t_{0}<t_{1}\in I
,
and taking into account that
u_{n}\to u_{\infty}
in
L^{1}(I)
,
H(u_{n})=h(w_{n},v_{n})
, and
H(u_{\infty})=h(w,v)
, it results that

h(w,v)\leqslant\liminf_{n}h(w_{n},u_{n})
,
hence
h
is lower semicontinuous.

Moreover
h
turns out to be Cartesian submaximal, indeed, consider
y,w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that we can consider the sequence

u_{n}(t):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}z&\hbox{ if }t\leqslant t_{0},\\
z+y&\hbox{ if }t_{0}<t\leqslant t_{1},\\
z+y+w_{1}&\hbox{ if }t_{1}\leqslant t\leqslant t_{1}+\frac{1}{n},\\
z+y+w_{1}+w_{2}&\hbox{ if }t>t_{1}+\frac{1}{n}.\end{array}\right.

Clearly
u_{n}\to u_{\infty}
in
L^{1}(I)
, where

u_{\infty}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}z&\hbox{ if }t\leqslant t_{0},\\
z+y&\hbox{ if }t_{0}<t\leqslant t_{1},\\
z+y+w_{1}+w_{2}&\hbox{ if }t>t_{1},\end{array}\right.

Then, by the
L^{1}(I)
-lower semicontinuity of
H
,

\displaystyle\supess_{s,t\in S(u)}h([u_{\infty}](s),[u_{\infty}](t))=

\displaystyle h(y,w_{1}+w_{2})\leqslant

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\supess_{s,t\in S(u)}h([u_{n}](s),[u_{n}](t))=

\displaystyle\max\{h(y,w_{1}),h(y,w_{2}),h(w_{1},w_{2})\},

where in the above equalities we have exploited the symmetry and diagonality of
h
.

In order to prove the opposite implication, assume that
h
is Cartesian submaximal and lower semicontinuous.

With the same construction as in
[
2
, Proof of Theorem 3.1]
, take
u_{n}
converging to
u_{\infty}
in
L^{1}_{loc}(I)
, hence, up to a not relabelled subsequence, also converging a.e. in
I
.
Since
u_{\infty}\in PC(I)
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\varepsilon<\inf\{|t-s|:t,s\in S(u_{\infty}),t\not=s\}
. Fix
t,t^{\prime}\in S(u_{\infty})
, we can suppose that

u_{n}(t\pm\varepsilon)\to u_{\infty}(t\pm\varepsilon)=u_{\infty}(t\pm)
,

u_{n}(t^{\prime}\pm\varepsilon)\to u_{\infty}(t^{\prime}\pm\varepsilon)=u_{\infty}(t^{\prime}\pm)

and that, for all
n
,
(t\pm\varepsilon),(t^{\prime}\pm\varepsilon)\not\in S(u_{n}).

By the lower semicontinuity of
h
it results

h([u_{\infty}](t),[u_{\infty}](t^{\prime}))=h([u_{\infty}](t\pm\varepsilon),[u_{\infty}](t^{\prime}\pm\varepsilon))\leqslant\liminf_{n\to+\infty}h(u_{n}(t+\varepsilon)-u_{n}(t-\varepsilon),u_{n}(t^{\prime}+\varepsilon)-u_{n}(t^{\prime}-\varepsilon))

By the Cartesian submaximality, the right hand side can be estimated as

\displaystyle h(u_{n}(t+\varepsilon)-u_{n}(t-\varepsilon),u_{n}(t^{\prime}+\varepsilon)-u_{n}(t^{\prime}-\varepsilon))\leqslant

\displaystyle\sup_{s,s^{\prime}\in S_{u_{n},\varepsilon,t,t^{\prime}}}h([u_{n}](s),[u_{n}](s^{\prime}))\leqslant\sup_{s,s^{\prime}\in S(u_{n})}h([u_{n}](s),[u_{n}](s^{\prime})),

where the set
S_{u_{n},\varepsilon,t,t^{\prime}}:=(S(u_{n})\cap(t-\varepsilon,t+\varepsilon))\cup(S(u_{n})\cap(t^{\prime}-\varepsilon,t^{\prime}+\varepsilon))
.

Consequently, taking the supremum as
t,t^{\prime}\in S(u)
on the left hand-side, we have the desired inequality.

\sup_{t,t^{\prime}\in S(u)}h([u](t),[u](t^{\prime}))\leqslant\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\sup_{t,t^{\prime}\in S(u_{n})}h([u_{n}](t),[u_{n}](t^{\prime})).

This concludes the proof.
∎

We conclude by making some comments and presenting some examples devoted to a better understanding of the notion of Cartesian submaximality.

Remark 3.5
.

•

The composition of a submaximal function with a subadditve one is not Cartesian submaximal as the following example shows

h(x,y):=\frac{|\sin(x+y)|}{x+y}
: indeed
h=g\circ+
, with
g:\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as
g(x):=\frac{|\sin x|}{x}
and
+:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
;

It suffices to consider
x,y,w=\pi/2
to verify
1=h(x+y,w)>\max\{h(x,y),h(y,w),h(x,w)\}=0
.

•

Each function
h:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to(0,+\infty)
which is separately submaximal, i.e. such that
h(y,w_{1}+w_{2})\leqslant\max\{h(y,w_{1}),h(y,w_{2})\}
is also Cartesian submaximal.
In particular,
if a symmetric function
h:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\to(0,+\infty)
is such that
h(\cdot,y)
is decreasing for a.e.
y
and we extend it
as

h^{\star}(w,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}h(w,y),&\hbox{ if }(w,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+},\\
0&\hbox{ if }w=0\hbox{ or }y=0,\\
+\infty&\hbox{ otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

(3.4)

then
h^{\star}
is separately submaximal.
As an example one can consider
h(w,y)=\frac{1}{w+y}
for
w,y\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
h^{\star}
as in (
3.4
).

The same holds if
h
is the ratio of a subadditive and superadditive function in each variable.

•

Examples of separately submaximal functions defined in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
with values in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
(and hence Cartesian submaximal) resulting immediately from
[
2
, Example 3.3]
:

h(w,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\max\left\{\frac{|\sin w|}{w},\frac{|\sin y|}{y}\right\},&\hbox{ if }(w,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+},\\
\color[rgb]{0,1,1}0&\hbox{ if }w=0\hbox{ or }y=0,\\
\color[rgb]{0,1,1}+\infty&\hbox{ otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

and

h_{\alpha}(w,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\alpha\frac{|\sin w|}{w}+(1-\alpha)\frac{|\sin y|}{y}&\hbox{ if }(w,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+},\\
\color[rgb]{0,1,1}0&\hbox{ if }w=0\hbox{ of }y=0,\\
\color[rgb]{0,1,1}+\infty&\hbox{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

\alpha\in(0,1).
