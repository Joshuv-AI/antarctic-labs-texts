Economic impact of standardized hypertension treatment on household income in Argentina: a longitudinal analysis, 2019–2024
Authors: Santiago Torroba, Hilario Bielsa, Nasim Iusef Venturini, Manuel Requejo, Julieta Arilla, Martín Pont Verges
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective.To estimate the economic impact of standard pharmacological treatment for hypertension on a typical household in Argentina using a new indicator relating treatment costs to household income.Methods.A longitudinal study was conducted from December 2019 to December 2024. The standard Argentine antihypertensive treatment (amlodipine 5 mg and losartan 50 mg) was selected. Retail prices of these drugs, both as single-drug combinations and fixed-dose combinations, were obtained from the publicly available Kairos database. The Standard Treatment Economic Impact Index was developed to express monthly out-of-pocket expenditure as a proportion of the total basic basket published by Argentina’s National Institute of Statistics and Censuses. Analyses were stratified by household health coverage status – with or without formal coverage.Results.Marked differences were seen in household costs by coverage status. Among households with health coverage, antihypertensive treatment costs represented 2.04%–3.39% of the total basic basket, placing expenditure below catastrophic health expenditure thresholds throughout the study. For households without health coverage, the index ranged from 6.81% to 11.31% of the total basic basket, which was above at least one of the catastrophic health expenditure thresholds used, and sometimes above both. Throughout the study, single-drug combinations were more expensive than fixed-dose combinations.Conclusions.These findings indicate a substantial financial burden for families without formal health coverage when accessing essential antihypertensive therapy. By focusing on treatment affordability rather than total household expenditure, the index may help anticipate and prevent future catastrophic health spending, particularly among uninsured populations.

Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death and disability in the Region of the Americas. In 2021, cardiovascular disease caused about 2.2 million deaths and accounted for the loss of 47.3 million disability-adjusted life years, making it the region’s foremost public health challenge (1). Hypertension is the main modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease and is responsible for up to one third of cases of cardiovascular disease (2, 3). At the same time, hypertension is also a major risk factor for the development of chronic kidney disease, second only to diabetes mellitus (4). For this reason, control of hypertension is recognized as a global health priority (5).

In Argentina, cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death, accounting for 27.7% of all deaths (6). According to the Fourth National Survey of Risk Factors conducted in 2018, 46.6% of the adult Argentine population – equivalent to about 21 million people – has hypertension, yet only about one in five patients with hypertension has their blood pressure levels under control. (7). According to Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) estimates, if Argentina increased its hypertension control rate from 14.8% to 50.0%, more than 64 000 deaths from coronary heart disease and cerebrovascular disease could be prevented over the next 5 years (8, 9). This low control rate is due, in part, to underdiagnosis and the lack of appropriate treatment: one third of patients are unaware of their condition; among those who are aware, one third does not receive pharmacological treatment, and among people who do receive treatment, only half achieve adequate control (10).

In this context, Argentina has partnered with PAHO under the HEARTS in the Americas initiative to promote policies aimed at strengthening hypertension control and management of cardiovascular risk at the primary care level. One of the pillars of this initiative is the provision of standardized antihypertensive treatment that ensures efficacy, safety, affordability, and continuity of therapy throughout a person’s lifetime (11, 12).

Among the multiple factors that influence adherence to treatment, economic barriers are a key determinant (13). The inability to afford the cost of medications is a major obstacle to treatment continuity, particularly for economically vulnerable people (14–16). In Argentina, where nearly 30% of the population lives below the poverty line, this issue is especially relevant (17).

The analysis of treatment affordability can be considered within the broader discussion of financial protection in health. When the direct household expenditure on health goods and services is not reimbursed by third parties and reaches a level that seriously affects living standards, this is called catastrophic health expenditure (18). Catastrophic health expenditure is typically measured as the proportion of individuals or households whose cumulative out-of-pocket health expenditure exceeds specified thresholds relative to household income or capacity to pay.

Therefore, any policy aimed at improving access to and adherence with antihypertensive treatment and hypertension control must understand and address the financial risk faced by families when they have to pay for essential medicines. This study sought to contribute to that objective through the development of a new indicator, the Standard Treatment Economic Impact Index (STEII), which relates the average cost of a standard antihypertensive treatment to the income of a typical household in Argentina in order to identify affordability gaps in antihypertensive therapy.

METHODS

This non-experimental longitudinal study estimated the economic impact of standard pharmacological treatment for hypertension on a typical household in Argentina through the development of the STEII and its use. The analysis covered 61 months, from December 2019 to December 2024.

To estimate treatment costs, retail prices of losartan 50 mg and amlodipine 5 mg were sourced for both single-drug combinations (losartan and amlodipine taken together but as separate tablets) and fixed-dose combinations (losartan and amlodipine taken together in one tablet). Losartan and amlodipine were selected as the reference treatment based on the recommendations of the 2022 Clinical Pathway for the Management of Hypertension from the Argentine Ministry of Health, which recommends the use of these two drugs at their defined daily doses (19) as part of the hypertension treatment plan. This therapeutic combination has proven clinical efficacy, and has a favorable safety profile and broad market availability (20, 21). Moreover, this regimen meets the criterion of clinical representativeness: according to international evidence, about 60% of patients with hypertension achieve adequate blood pressure control with this pharmacological regimen (22).

All 30-tablet formulations with sustained availability in the Kairos database throughout the study period were included. Kairos is a publicly accessible database in Argentina that records the monthly retail prices of medicines available in the national market. Two reference subpopulations were considered: the population without formal health coverage, for whom 100% of the retail price was assumed to be paid out of pocket; and the population with formal health coverage (social health insurance or private health insurance), for whom a 70% discount was applied in accordance with the provisions of the Mandatory Medical Program, with beneficiaries covering 30% of the retail price.

In total, 36 commercial formulations of losartan 50 mg (corresponding to 34 manufacturers), 33 of amlodipine 5 mg (32 manufacturers), and 10 fixed-dose combinations (nine manufacturers) were analyzed. The geometric mean of prices was used to calculate the average monthly treatment cost, expressed in constant Argentine pesos ($Arg) as of December 2024 to ensure comparability with the selected income measure.

The total basic basket, published monthly by Argentina’s National Institute of Statistics and Censuses, was used as the indicator of household income. The total basic basket estimates the minimum monthly income required for a typical household – composed of a 35-year-old man, a 31-year-old woman, an 8-year-old girl, and a 6-year-old boy – to cover both food and non-food goods, excluding housing rent. This measure, which defines the threshold below which a family in Argentina is considered poor, is widely used as a reference for assessing economic sufficiency. At the same time, it provides monthly updated values in current Argentine pesos.

For the estimation of catastrophic health expenditure, the budget share approach was used, whereby health expenditure is considered catastrophic when it exceeds a predefined proportion of household income or total household expenditure. In defining the threshold for catastrophic health expenditure, two cut-offs were adopted: 10% of total household income, in accordance with the World Bank criterion and Sustainable Development Goal indicator 3.8.2, and 5%, based on previous evidence specific to the Argentine context (23). Results were analyzed and interpreted according to both thresholds in order to compare the incidence and severity of catastrophic health expenditure by type of health coverage and hypertension treatment formulation.

The STEII was developed to quantify the economic burden represented by an essential pharmacological treatment in relation to household income. This indicator was calculated using the following formula:

STEII=(g×AF)/i

where g is the monthly expenditure associated with a standard treatment (in this case, for hypertension); AF is the adjustment factor reflecting the type of health coverage (with a value of 1 for the population without formal health coverage and 0.3 for the population with formal health coverage); and i is the reference measure of household income (in this case, the total basic basket).

Using this measure across all the series made it possible to quantify trends in the economic burden of treatment in the context of changes in the population’s purchasing power.

This study was based exclusively on aggregated, publicly available data and therefore did not require ethics review or informed consent, in accordance with current Argentine regulations (Resolution No. 1480-11). Microsoft Excel was used for data processing and statistical analyses, which was done between March and May 2025.

RESULTS

Figure 1 shows the changes in the average monthly price, in constant currency (December 2024 Argentine pesos), of standard hypertension treatment in Argentina between December 2019 and December 2024. Values were compared for amlodipine 5 mg and losartan 50 mg single-drug formulations, the single-drug combination, and the losartan 50 mg + amlodipine 5 mg fixed-dose combination.

The highest recorded average monthly price for the single-drug combination was in December 2023 ($Arg39 500.1; 38.7 United States dollars (US$)), as was also the case for losartan ($Arg23 475.3; US$ 23.1) and amlodipine ($Arg16 024.8; US$ 15.6). The fixed-dose combination also reached a peak in December 2023, at $Arg33 875.7; US$ 33.3. In contrast, the lowest prices for all formulations were between May 2022 and April 2023; in particular, the fixed-dose combination had an average monthly price of $Arg24 297.0; US$ 23.7 in March 2023, a difference of 28.2% compared with its peak value in December 2023.

Throughout the study, a sustained gap was seen between the average monthly price of the single-drug combination and the fixed-dose combination. The price difference between the two formulations was narrowest in April 2020 ($Arg459.9; US$ 0.45) and progressively widened to a maximum in January 2024 ($Arg9 457.5; US$ 9.27). On average, the monthly gap between the prices of single-drug combination and the fixed-dose combination over the study period was $Arg4 887.6; US$ 4.8 (standard deviation (SD) $Arg1 473.9; US$ 1.44), indicating a stable trend with moderate fluctuations.

Figure 2 shows the change in the STEII for the selected antihypertensive treatments for individuals without formal health coverage in Argentina from December 2019 to December 2024. The index followed a general trend similar to that seen for the retail prices of the treatments analyzed in Figure 1. For the single-drug combination, the maximum index value was 11.31% in December 2023, whereas the minimum value was 8.27% in March 2023. Likewise, the fixed-dose combination also reached its maximum in December 2023, at 9.70%, while its minimum was recorded in December 2024, at 6.81%.

Over the study period, a sustained gap in the STEII was seen between the two formulations, with consistently higher values for the single-drug combination. The smallest absolute difference between the two formulation was in April 2020, at 0.13 percentage points, corresponding to a 1.4% relative difference. The largest gap was in December 2024, at 2.57 percentage points, corresponding to a 27.4% relative difference. On average, the monthly relative difference in the STEII over the study period was 15.00% (SD 4.28%), indicating a persistent affordability advantage for the fixed-dose combination.

Figure 3 shows changes in the STEII for the selected antihypertensive treatments for individuals with formal health coverage in Argentina over the study period. Since the difference between the STEII for people with and without formal health coverage lies in the application of fixed discounted retail price, the trend in the index is the same, although with lower absolute values throughout the study period.

The single-drug combination reached a maximum value in December 2023, with an STEII of 3.39%, while its minimum value was in March 2023, at 2.48%. For the fixed-dose combination, the peak value was also in December 2023, at 2.91%, while the minimum was in December 2024, at 2.04%.

Throughout the study period, the single-Drug combination consistently represented a greater relative burden on household income. The smallest difference in STEII between formulations was observed in April 2020, at only 0.04 percentage points, while the largest gap occurred in December 2024, at 0.77 percentage points.

Finally, the STEII showed substantial differences between people without formal health coverage and those covered through social health insurance or private health insurance plans (Figure 4). During the study period, a typical household without formal health coverage had to allocate a significant proportion of the total basic basket to afford standard antihypertensive treatment. In fact, for the selected therapeutic combination (losartan 50 mg + amlodipine 5 mg daily), the STEII for the population without formal health coverage ranged from 6.81% (December 2024) to 11.31% (December 2023) of the total basic basket. This value consistently exceeded the 5% total basic basket threshold and at certain points reached or even surpassed the 10% threshold.

In contrast, among the population with formal health coverage, where insurance covers 70% of the medicine cost, the economic burden was substantially lower. The STEII for households with social health insurance or private coverage ranged from 2.04% (December 2024) to a 3.39% of the total basic basket over the same period. These figures were about one third of the value for uninsured people and were below the 5% threshold throughout the study period.

DISCUSSION

These results suggest that even diseases considered to be of low complexity, such as hypertension, may impose a considerable economic burden on vulnerable populations. If, in December 2024 – the last period analyzed – one member of an average household without formal health coverage in Argentina had been diagnosed with hypertension and the household income had been just sufficient to cover basic needs, then that household would have required a 9.38% increase in income to afford basic antihypertensive treatment (single-drug combination) without falling below the poverty line. In populations with formal health coverage, the finding of an STEII below 5% throughout the study period reinforces the expected role of health insurance in preventing excessive out-of-pocket expenditure. This finding is consistent with previous studies in Argentina showing that expanded formal health coverage is associated with a lower incidence of catastrophic health expenditure (23).

From a public health policy perspective, these findings underscore the need to strengthen financial protection mechanisms for populations without formal health coverage. In Argentina, where about 35% of the population lacks formal health coverage (24), the estimate that a standard antihypertensive treatment may account for as much as 10% of the total basic basket for households without formal health coverage is particularly relevant. This finding highlights the importance of essential medicines access programs, pooled procurement strategies, and regional mechanisms such as the PAHO Strategic Fund to improve affordability and sustainable access to standardized antihypertensive therapy. In settings without formal health coverage, where out-of-pocket expenditure is high, higher rates of treatment discontinuation or non-initiation for economic reasons may be expected. By contrast, formal coverage schemes, by reducing direct patient expenditure to about 30% of the market price of the drug, are likely to promote higher levels of treatment adherence. Ensuring the financial accessibility of antihypertensive drugs is therefore not only a strategy for health equity, but also a necessary condition for optimizing clinical outcomes in hypertension management.

The finding that fixed-dose combinations were consistently less expensive than the equivalent single-drug combinations in Argentina should be interpreted in light of local market conditions. Although in many settings fixed-dose combinations are more expensive than separate single-drug formulations, the Argentine pharmaceutical market includes a relatively large number of commercial presentations and a domestic pharmaceutical industry. These factors may contribute to greater competition and could partly explain the lower observed prices of fixed-dose combinations in this analysis. Therefore, this finding should not be generalized to all countries, but it supports the need for country-specific affordability assessments when designing standardized treatment pathways.

From a theoretical and methodological standpoint, the development of the STEII provides a novel tool for measuring economic barriers to health-care access. Unlike traditional indicators of catastrophic or impoverishing health expenditure, which retrospectively assess the aggregate impact of all health-related expenses on household finances, the STEII is a disease-specific and treatment-specific indicator that allows prospective impact assessment. It estimates the expected affordability burden of a predefined standard treatment before expenditure occurs and relates it to a reference income threshold. This approach complements conventional financial protection indicators. For example, even when a household does not experience catastrophic expenditure in global terms, a high STEII for an essential medicine or health condition may indicate an affordability barrier associated with a risk of non-adherence. Thus, the index translates treatment affordability into an easily interpretable percentage value, facilitating comparisons over time and across settings. Although developed using Argentine data, the STEII could be adapted to other countries where standardized treatment protocols, reliable medicine price data, and a reference measure of household income or capacity to pay are available. This index may be particularly relevant for low- and middle-income countries, where out-of-pocket expenditure on essential medicines is still an important barrier to continuity of care. Its interpretation, however, should consider local pharmaceutical markets, coverage arrangements, and reimbursement policies. The study period included the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. No major disruption was observed in the STEII trend during the pandemic months, suggesting that the affordability pattern identified in this analysis was not substantially modified by the COVID-19 period.

As with any descriptive study, this analysis has methodological limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the use of the total basic basket as an indicator of household income involves a generalization that does not fully capture real differences across households. Households with incomes below the total basic basket would face a greater economic burden than estimated, whereas higher-income households may be better able to afford treatment costs. Therefore, depending on the socioeconomic group considered, the STEII may underestimate or overestimate the relative impact of anti-hypertensive treatment costs on household income. Second, although the budget share approach was chosen to estimate catastrophic health expenditure, this methodological decision also defines the scope of the findings. Unlike the capacity-to-pay approach, which measures the share of health expenditure relative to disposable income after basic needs have been met, the budget share approach adopted here measures treatment burden against a general income threshold (25). This choice is consistent with the use of the total basic basket as a proxy for household income, given that this measure already incorporates the basic needs of a typical household. However, it also limits the ability to capture with greater precision the difference in economic constraints faced by the most vulnerable households. Third, the analysis was restricted to the standardized losartan 50 mg and amlodipine 5 mg regimen. Higher-dose regimens and thiazide-containing combinations were not evaluated because fixed-dose presentations with sustained availability were insufficient for a robust longitudinal comparison. Estimating these alternatives by simply doubling the current regimen cost was not considered methodologically appropriate because medicine prices do not correlate linearly with dose. The STEII therefore reflects a potential economic burden rather than an average real-world situation, which limits its direct relationship with actual levels of treatment adherence. Likewise, the STEII focuses exclusively on the direct cost of medications and does not include other treatment-related expenses, which may lead to an underestimation of the total economic burden of hypertension care. Finally, the indicator does not account for the existence of current financial protection mechanisms for the population without formal health coverage, such as the Remediar program or provincial medicine provision programs, which could partially mitigate the estimated out-of-pocket expenditure.

Despite these limitations, the trends and gaps identified clearly point to the existence of an affordability problem, although the results should not be interpreted as individualized predictions. Future research could address these limitations by incorporating income distribution variables, variations in cost-of-living assumptions and alternative medication coverage scenarios in order to achieve a more precise and comprehensive characterization of the problem.

In conclusion, this study made it possible to estimate the economic impact of standardized antihypertensive treatment through the STEII. The index identified a sustained affordability gap in households without formal health coverage and showed that, in the Argentine market, fixed-dose combinations may represent both a clinically and economically attractive option within standardized hypertension treatment pathways. In the future, the index may also be useful for estimating the economic impact of other high-priority public health conditions and for supporting affordability monitoring across countries.
