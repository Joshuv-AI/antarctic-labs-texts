Intratumoral microbiota exhibiting varied responses to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in triple-negative breast cancer revealed by 2bRAD-M
Authors: Ziteng Zhang, Xindi Ma, Jing Zhang, Shang Wu, Xiangmei Zhang, Chao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) is the conventional therapy for early-stage and locally advanced triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). Pathological complete response (pCR) serves as an essential indicator for predicting the effectiveness of NACT; however, a limited percentage of patients benefit from NACT. This research examined variations in the tumor microbiota between the pCR and non-pCR groups via 2BRAD sequencing for microbiome (2bRAD-M) technology and assessed the potential of the microbiota as a biomarker for predicting the NACT response.MethodsA total of 44 TNBC patients were enrolled, of whom 25 achieved pCR and 19 were classified as non-pCR patients. Clinical data were collected, tumor tissue was biopsied as sample, and DNA was extracted. The 2bRAD-M sequencing method was used to analyse the microbial communities and community structures of tumor tissues in both groups, conduct microbial diversity and differential analyses, and perform qualitative and quantitative studies on the microbes inside the tissues.ResultsA total of 1,896 microbial species were identified. The microbial diversity in non-pCR tissue exceeded that in pCR patient tissue. The microbial composition of the tumor tissues in both groups was comparable. In the non-pCR group, there were significantly more gram-negative bacteria, such as Klebsiella, Escherichia, Acinetobacter, Vibrio, and Meiothermus, than in the pCR group. Calidithermus chliarophilus, Meiothermus sp.003226535, and Escherichia coli were identified as the three principal species distinguishing the two groups. Seven species were identified as markers to distinguish between non-pCRs and pCRs, with an AUC value of 90.3%. Validation in an independent cohort via qPCR indicated the potential predictive value of this seven-species model, yielding an AUC of 84%. Functional annotation analysis revealed 3,207 differentially expressed COGs and 230 differentially enriched signalling pathways across the non-pCR and pCR microbiomes. In vitro experiments suggested that lipopolysaccharide may contribute to doxorubicin and paclitaxel resistance in MDA-MB-231 cells, a process potentially linked to the reactivation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.ConclusionThis exploratory 2bRAD-M microbiome study of pCR and non-pCR tissues from TNBC patients receiving NACT identified significant differences in microbial environments between the two groups. Based on these findings, we developed a predictive model for chemotherapy effectiveness and highlighted the microbiome’s potential as a biomarker for the efficacy of NACT in TNBC patients, offering novel insights that may inform future clinical diagnostic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08187-x.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the predominant malignant neoplasm that jeopardizes women’s health globally [1], with both incidence and mortality rates showing a persistent increasing trend [2]. Breast cancer may be categorized into hormone receptor-positive (HR+), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-positive (HER2+), and triple-negative (TNBC) subtypes according to molecular subtyping criteria. TNBC is characterized by the absence of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression [3] and accounts for approximately 15–20% of all breast cancer cases [4]. Compared with other subtypes, TNBC exhibits significant aggressive characteristics, including elevated early recurrence rates, a heightened risk of distant metastasis, and worse overall survival outcomes [5]. Owing to the lack of definitive therapeutic targets, these patients are unlikely to benefit from endocrine therapy or HER2-targeted drugs, and the restricted clinical treatment alternatives lead to nearly fifty percent of patients experiencing disease recurrence or metastasis within 3–5 years of diagnosis [6].

Cytotoxic chemotherapy remains the primary treatment strategy for TNBC; however, the challenge of drug resistance continues to limit its clinical effectiveness. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) is the standard treatment regimen for early-stage and locally advanced TNBC [7], with pathological complete response (pCR) serving as the primary indicator of efficacy, which has been proven to be strongly correlated with a favourable prognosis [8]. Notably, the pCR rate of conventional NACT regimens is only 35–45% [9, 10]. Patients who fail to achieve pCR after surgery also have worse outcomes with capecitabine-based adjuvant chemotherapy as salvage therapy [11]. Studies indicated that combination therapy regimens involving chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy can ameliorate the tumor microenvironment. In particular, modalities combining immunotherapy with other treatments can significantly enhance antitumor efficacy [12]. The KEYNOTE-522 trial demonstrated that the combination of chemotherapy and immunotherapy (chemo-IM) significantly increased the pCR rate and progression-free survival in early-stage TNBC patients [13]. Nevertheless, the incorporation of immunotherapy hardly improved the absolute pCR benefit by 13%, and approximately 40% of patients who did not reach the pCR endpoint [14]. This significant variation in treatment response indicates that TNBC patients do not respond similarly to chemotherapy. Numerous studies are currently focused on identifying biomarkers predictive of treatment response in TNBC. One study demonstrated that TNBC tumor tissues exhibit higher expression of CD1B compared to other subtypes, which may contribute to their poorer response to therapy relative to other breast cancer subtypes [15]. Currently, there are no reliable markers available to guide the selection of the most effective treatment. Consequently, the development of particular prognostic indicators has substantial therapeutic importance for realizing precision medicine and preventing overtreatment.

Recent studies have increasingly concentrated on exploring the impact of the intratumoral microbiota on malignancy development and the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) [16–20]. The intratumoral microbiota can influence the occurrence and development of several malignancies, including breast cancer [21–23]. The intratumoral microbiota, which are bacteria that reside within the tumor, can affect tumor growth and progression through a variety of mechanisms, including DNA mutation, activation of oncogenic pathways, modulation of immune responses, alteration of cellular signalling pathways, promotion of chronic inflammation, complement system activation, and modification of the tumor microenvironment [24–27]. Various tumor types have unique tumor-associated microbial compositions, with breast cancer being characterized by a rich and diverse microbiota [28], which is dominated primarily by the Proteobacteria phylum, followed by the Bacteroidetes phylum [29, 30]. Advances in polymerase chain reaction technology and 16S rRNA sequencing have shown that alterations in the microbiome of breast tissues may serve as a mechanistic connection between modified stroma in the microenvironment and subsequent breast cancer risk factors [31]. Intratumoral microbiota can pass through the circulatory system with cancer cells and significantly contribute to metastatic colonization [32]. Fu et al. reported that Staphylococcus aureus, Lactobacillus lactis, and Streptococcus aureus are enriched in breast cancer cells and can inhibit the RhoA-ROCK signalling pathway to restructure the cytoskeleton, thereby helping tumor cells resist mechanical stress in blood vessels to prevent damage and promote tumor metastasis. Additionally, studies using germ-free mice and immunodeficient mice have shown that tumor-associated bacteria may facilitate cancer metastasis independently of the gut microbiota and immune response [32, 33]. It is hypothesized that dysbiosis may serve as a precursor to carcinogenic events, establishing a tumor-promoting microenvironment [34]. Moreover, disparities exist in the microbial distribution between tumors and adjacent tissues. A study on breast cancer demonstrated that normal and tumor tissues have different microbial populations [29]. We are still in the early stages of exploring the intratumoral microbiota in breast cancer, and we have not yet gained a comprehensive understanding of the underlying processes involved. Further exploration of the intratumoral microbiota in breast cancer could identify potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Recent studies have demonstrated that the intratumoral microbiota plays a crucial role in modulating the efficacy of chemotherapy. For instance, Chen et al. [35] utilized 16S rRNA sequencing and found that the intratumoral bacterial load could predict the response of TNBC patients to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy. Similarly, Ma et al. [36], through 16S rRNA analysis, identified Bacteroides fragilis as a factor promoting chemoresistance and stemness.

However, these pioneering studies primarily relied on 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Although 16S sequencing is highly effective for taxonomic analysis at broader levels, it has inherent limitations, particularly its inability to provide precise, species-level taxonomic resolution. While shotgun metagenomics can achieve species-level resolution, it faces challenges such as the typically low biomass of the intratumoral microbiota and excessively high levels of host DNA contamination. We employed 2bRAD-M, a high-resolution sequencing technique optimized for low-biomass samples. 2bRAD-M effectively minimizes host DNA interference and achieves species-level resolution comparable to shotgun metagenomics, enabling us to accurately identify specific microbial species and functional pathways associated with the response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy.This study systematically analysed the tumor tissue microbiome characteristics of TNBC patients receiving NACT treatment via 2bRAD-M technology, focused on the disparities in microbial communities between the pCR group and the non-pCR group, and preliminarily explored the potential of differences in microbial communities as biomarkers for predicting the efficacy of NACT.

Methods

Patient enrollment

In this study, we ultimately enrolled 44 female breast cancer patients from the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University between 2019 and 2022 who underwent 2bRAD-M profiling. Additionally, 43 patients from the Tangshan People’s Hospital between 2020 and 2023 were recruited as an independent validation cohort. All patients received a standardized NACT regimen consisting of anthracycline and taxane. All patients received EC with epirubicin 90 mg/m2 and cyclophosphamide 600 mg/m2 on day 1 once every 3 weeks for four cycles, followed by either docetaxel 75 mg/m2 on day 1 once every 3 weeks for four cycles. Dose adjustments were made by oncology specialists according to standard clinical guidelines and patient tolerance.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosed with unilateral invasive ductal carcinoma through tissue analysis, with negative results for ER, PR, and HER2; (2) tumor samples were checked and confirmed by the hospital’s Pathology Department following the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO)/College of American Pathologists (CAP) guidelines (cells with ER/PR-positive staining less than 1% were considered negative); (3) aged between 18 and 70 years; (4) no signs of cancer spread were detected by imaging at the first diagnosis; (5) had at least 1 cycle of taxane- and anthracycline-based NACT, with surgery performed within 4 weeks after finishing NACT. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) had a history of other malignant tumors; (2) received other anticancer treatments (e.g., radiotherapy, targeted therapy, etc.) prior to or during NACT; (3) antibiotic use during the preceding 4 weeks.

Sample collection and processing

The samples analysed in this study were obtained from pretreatment hollow-needle aspiration biopsy samples of patients with newly diagnosed TNBC. Fresh tissues were cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen and then transferred to an ultralow-temperature refrigerator at -80 °C for long-term freezing and storage. The entire process was conducted in accordance with aseptic techniques (sample packaging was completed in a biosafety cabinet).

DNA extraction, library construction and sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted with a TIANamp Micro DNA Kit (Tiangen Company). 2bRAD-M is a microbial diversity profiling technique that performs microbial identification and relative quantification based on unique tags derived from the digestion of microbial genomes by type IIB restriction enzymes. It combines the high resolution and accuracy of metagenomic sequencing—providing species-level resolution—with the high sensitivity and adaptability of amplicon sequencing such as 16S rRNA sequencing. This is achieved through its enzymatic digestion-based approach, which efficiently handles samples with trace biomass, degradation, or severe host DNA contamination. To ensure the reliability of the microbiome analysis, field blanks (empty cryovials) were deployed at the sampling site to monitor potential contamination introduced during the collection process. In the laboratory workflow, reagent blank controls were processed in parallel for both the nucleic acid extraction and library preparation steps. Finally, contaminant sequences identified in these blank controls were statistically recognized using the RLD (Relative Log Distance) algorithm [37] and subsequently subtracted from the data of the formal experimental samples. The 2bRAD-M library was subsequently constructed according to the established protocol [38–40]: an appropriate amount of DNA (≤ 200 ng) was digested with BcgI restriction endonuclease (NEB, USA) at 37 °C for 3 h, and the products were ligated with specific aptamers (Ada1, Ada2) via T4 DNA ligase (NEB, USA) at 4 °C overnight. The ligated products were amplified by polymerase chain reaction using Phusion High Fidelity DNA polymerase (NEB, USA), and the resulting amplicons were purified by QIAquick polymerase chain reaction purification kit (Qiagen, CA). The purified library was sequenced by Qingdao OE Biotechnology Co., Ltd. using the Illumina Nova PE150 platform, and the aptamer and primer sequences are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

2bRAD microbiological database construction

On the basis of the GTDB and Ensembl databases, 404,199 microbial genomes, including bacterial, fungal, and archaeal genomes, were acquired. By simulating the cutting action of electronic restriction enzymes for 16 types of 2b-type restriction endonucleases, we identified unique tag sequences that do not overlap with those of other species within the same group for each taxonomic unit. This process allowed us to establish a database of 2bRAD microbial genomes that is specific to each species.

Bioinformatic and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed via SPSS (version 20) and R software (version 4.1.1). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and inter-group comparisons were performed using the independent Student’s t-test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages), and group differences were analyzed using the χ2 test; a Venn diagram was used to show the unique and shared species between the low and high groups. The ‘vegan’ package was used to calculate α diversity indices, including the Chao 1, Shanno, and Simpson indices, while paired Wilcoxon tests were conducted to compare α diversity between the two groups. Additionally, the ‘vegan’ package was used to calculate the Bray-Curtis distance, Binary Jaccard distance and Euclidean distance to estimate β diversity, which was statistically analysed via permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA). Wilcoxon analysis was used to assess overall disparities between groups. Differential bacteria in each group were identified via LEfSe. A random forest model was constructed using the top 30 species in terms of relative abundance. Feature selection was performed by applying the “one standard error rule,” wherein the number of features was chosen from within a range defined by the minimum error rate plus one standard deviation. This approach prioritizes the smallest reasonable feature set to favor a more parsimonious model and avoid overfitting. To control for false positives, Recursive Feature Elimination with Stability Selection was employed. Specifically, through 10 independent rounds of cross-validation, the frequency with which each feature was selected as important was recorded. Only those features consistently identified as important across multiple cross-validation iterations were retained. Cluster of homologous groups (COG) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses were used to predict what the urobiota does. The results with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction assays

Tumor tissue samples were collected from 43 patients diagnosed with TNBC. Total DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Microbiome Kit (Qiagen, Germany). Given the typically low bacterial biomass in tissue samples, a pre-amplification step targeting the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was performed via PCR prior to species-specific detection assays. A pre-amplification PCR was performed using universal 16S rRNA primers (27 F/1492R) [41] in a total volume of 20 µL. The cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 15 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing at 55 °C for 30 s. Then, 2 µL of the product was diluted 1:50, and 2 µL of the diluted product was added to a 20 µL qPCR reaction system (with a negative control included in parallel). The reaction mixture contained specific primers for the following target microorganisms: Meiothermus sp. 003226535, Calidithermus chliarophilus, Acinetobacter baumannii, Vibrio parahaemolyticus, Phreatobacter sp.022843985, Rhabdaerophilum calidifontis, Escherichia coli. The cycling conditions were set as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing at 60 °C for 30 s. A melting curve analysis was subsequently performed to confirm the specificity of the amplified products.

Cell counting kit-8 assay

MDA-MB-231 cells (Catalog No. CL-0150B, Procell, Wuhan, China) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Catalog No. C11995, Gibco) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS; Catalog No. M1805, Beijing Xigong Biosciences Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin solution (PS; Catalog No. HCCC102, Beijing Haichengyuanhong Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). All cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with a gas mixture of 5% CO₂ and 95% air. For the cell viability assay, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2,000 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. Subsequently, the cells were treated with designated concentrations of Doxorubicin (Catalog No. HY-15142, MedChemExpress), Paclitaxel (Catalog No. HY-B0015, MedChemExpress), or Lipopolysaccharide (LPS; Catalog No. L2880, Sigma-Aldrich). After 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h of drug exposure, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (CA1210, Solarbio) was added to each well and incubated for an additional 2 h at 37 °C. Finally, the absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a Varioskan LUX microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Western blot

Cellular proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime) and quantified with a BCA assay kit (23227, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Subsequently, 20 µg of protein from each sample was subjected to SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (IPVH00010, Merck Millipore). The membrane was first blocked and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against the following proteins: p-PI3K (Abcam, ab182651), PI3K (Proteintech, 60225-1-Ig), p-AKT (CST, 4060), AKT (CST, 9272), and GAPDH (Abcam, ab181602) as a loading control. After washing, the membrane was incubated with a secondary antibody for 90 min at room temperature. Signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (MA0186, Melunbio).

We collected the clinicopathological characteristics of all patients (Table 1). The mean age of the included patients was 47.36 years. There were no important differences in age, type of surgery, or stage between the pCR and non-pCR groups (P > 0.05), indicating that the baseline characteristics were balanced and comparable between the two groups.

Diversity analysis of the intratumoral microbiota in TNBC

The quality control details throughout the detection procedure are outlined in Supplementary Table 2. This research yielded approximately 349 million clean reads from 44 samples of BC tissue, with an average of approximately 7.93 million reads per sample. The reads were compared with those of 2b-Tag-DB, resulting in the identification of 1,896 microbial taxa at the species level. The Venn diagram (Fig. 1A) illustrates that the non-pCR group and the pCR group shared 990 microbial species, with 222 species belonging exclusively to the non-pCR group and 684 species belonging to the pCR group. The α diversity analysis of the pCR and non-pCR groups revealed statistically significant differences in the Chao1 estimator, Shannon index, and Simpson index, all of which were greater in the non-pCR group than in the pCR group (P < 0.05, Fig. 1B). Similar results were observed in the β diversity analysis. The β diversity analysis (Fig. 1C) revealed similar findings, where PERMANOVA tests utilizing Bray-Curtis distance (P = 0.001), binary Jaccard distance (P = 0.003), and Euclidean distance (P = 0.001) revealed significant differences in the microbial community structure between the two groups.

Bacterial community composition in the microbiota

A total of 30 and 29 phyla were identified in the non-pCR and pCR groups, respectively (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 10). Pseudomonadota was the predominant phylum in both groups (54.69% and 33.55%), followed by Deinococcota (25.75% and 25.13%) and Actinomycetota (11.88% and 34.82%). A total of 524 genera were identified in the non-pCR group (Fig. 2B), whereas 692 genera were identified in the pCR group. The dominant genera in the non-pCR group included Nocardia (9.6%), Vibrio (9.1%), and Meiothermus (7.47%), whereas those in the pCR group included Nocardia (31.8%), Deinococcus (14.53%), and Vibrio (4.27%). The five most prevalent species in both groups at the species level were Nocardia farcinica, Deinococcus geothermalis, Calidithermus chliarophilus, Vibrio parahaemolyticus, and NICIL_2 sp.002898675 (Fig. 2C).

Differential abundance of microbial taxa between non-pCR and pCR patients

We used the paired Wilcoxon test (Table 2) to delineate the differences in taxonomy between the two groups of microorganisms. At the phylum level, Pseudomonadota and Fusobacteriota were considerably more abundant in the non-pCR group, but Actinomycetota and Chlamydiota were comparatively more abundant in the pCR group. At the genus level, Calidithermus, Meiothermus, Meiothermus (phylogroup B), and Klebsiella exhibited substantially greater abundances in the non-pCR group, whereas Nocardia and Deinococcus presented greater abundances in the pCR group. At the species level, Calidithermus chliarophilus, Meiothermus sp.003226535, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Meiothermus cerbereus were significantly enriched in the non-pCR group, whereas Nocardia farcinica and Deinococcus murrayi exhibited greater enrichment in the pCR group (Fig. 3A).

Moreover, we used LEfSe analysis to elucidate the composition of the differentially abundant species across the two microbial communities (Fig. 3B, C). LEfSe analysis identified a total of 31 taxonomic categories with differential abundance (LDA score ≥ 2.0). At the genus level, the non-pCR group was enriched in Vibrio, Meiothermus, Escherichia, Meiothermus (phylogroup B), Calidithermus, Acinetobacter, and Phreatobacter, whereas the pCR group was enriched in Nocardia and Deinococcus. Model prediction analysis between non-pCR and pCR patients

We used random forest analysis to delineate the significance of the top 30 species on the basis of relative abundance (Supplementary Fig. 1). Species importance point plots indicated that Calidithermus chliarophilus, Meiothermus sp.003226535, and Escherichia coli were the three most important species for distinguishing between the non-pCR and pCR groups.

We subsequently developed a random forest classifier and identified seven species as the candidate marker set for differentiating between non-pCR and pCR groups (Supplementary Table 3). The predictive performance of this marker set was evaluated via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, with the average area under the curve (AUC) values for non-pCR and pCR reaching 90.3% (Fig. 4). Compared with the non-pCR group, the probability of disease (POD) value in the pCR group was significantly increased (P = 0.000006), suggesting that POD derived from microbial species markers had promising diagnostic capability for differentiating between non-pCR and pCR cases.

Subsequently, by integrating patient clinical characteristics with the aforementioned seven microbial species, three potential predictors (Vibrio parahaemolyticus, Meiothermus sp.003226535, and Age) were screened using LASSO regression analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2, 3 and Supplementary Table 4). Further multivariate analysis indicated that Meiothermus sp.003226535 and Age were independent risk factors affecting chemotherapy response (Supplementary Fig. 5).

To validate the utility of the predictive model, we included 43 samples from an independent validation cohort and quantified the relative abundance of the aforementioned seven bacterial species via qPCR (Supplementary Fig. 4). This cohort consisted of 22 non-pCR and 21 pCR cases. Based on the qPCR results, a ROC curve was constructed to evaluate the model’s discriminative ability for pCR, and the AUC was calculated as an overall performance metric (Supplementary Figs. 5, 6). The achieved AUC of 0.84 suggests that the seven-species signature maintains consistent predictive value in an independent patient population.

Variations in predictive functions between non-pCR and pCR

We predicted the roles of the detected microbial communities and compared the two groups using the Wilcoxon test. A total of 3,207 COGs were identified as potentially differentially abundant between the non-pCR and pCR groups. The five most significant COGs included COG2814 (predicted arabinose efflux permease, MFS family), COG0745 (DNA-binding response regulator, OmpR family, containing REC and winged-helix (wHTH) domain), COG1028 (NAD(P)-dependent dehydrogenase, short-chain alcohol dehydrogenase family), COG0438 (glycosyltransferase involved in cell wall biosynthesis), and COG1309 (DNA-binding transcriptional regulator, AcrR family) (Fig. 5A). Analysis based on the KEGG database suggested 230 potentially differential signalling pathways between the non-pCR and pCR groups, with the five most significantly different pathways comprising metabolic pathways, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, microbial metabolism in diverse environments, and ABC transporters (Fig. 5B).

LPS may reduce the efficacy of doxorubicin and paclitaxel by activating the PI3K/AKT pathway

In vitro cell experiments were conducted to explore potential biological interactions. CCK-8 assays showed that doxorubicin (Dox)+ paclitaxel (PTX) treatment significantly inhibited the viability of MDA-MB-231 cells in a time- and concentration-dependent manner, and this inhibition was partially reversed by co-treatment with LPS. Western blot analysis observed that Dox + PTX markedly reduced the p-AKT/AKT and p-PI3K/PI3K ratios in MDA-MB-231 cells, suggesting that its anti-tumor effect may be mediated through suppression of the PI3K/AKT pathway. The addition of LPS was found to restore the phosphorylation levels of this pathway, which aligns with the recovery of cell viability observed in the CCK-8 assays. These findings suggest a potential link where LPS might alleviate the cytotoxicity induced by Dox and PTX, possibly involving the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.

Discussion

In the context of precision medicine, a growing body of research has focused on predicting treatment response in cancer patients [42, 43]. This aims to optimize therapeutic strategies for patients while also striving to provide novel therapeutic targets. NACT has emerged as a conventional therapeutic approach for early-stage TNBC, pCR is a crucial metric for assessing the effectiveness of NACT, demonstrating a substantial correlation with long-term survival in TNBC patients, and the prognosis of patients who achieve pCR is much superior to that of those who do not [44]. Numerous studies have focused on the prediction of pCR by multiple biomarkers to optimize individualized therapy [45, 46]. Previous studies have revealed a strong correlation between the diversity and load of the tumor-associated microbiota and the pCR rate of NACT in conjunction with immunotherapy (chemo-IM). Patients with high microbial burdens are more likely to benefit from treatment. Additionally, the diversity of tumor microbiota is significantly correlated with immune cell infiltration, as tumors with elevated microbial loads demonstrate increased T-cell infiltration and reduced infiltration of tumor-associated macrophages [47]. Moreover, studies have identified the presence of gut-inflammation-associated Bacteroides fragilis (ETBF) in the tumors of TNBC patients who were unresponsive to taxane-based NACT [35, 36]. Previous studies on microbiota have greatly benefited from technologies such as 16S rDNA sequencing and metagenomics. For instance, He et al. utilized 16S rDNA sequencing to demonstrate that the composition and metabolic functions of the intratumoral microbiome in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) are closely associated with tumor progression [48]. Similarly, in the field of HCC research, a study by Li et al. integrated multi-omics analyses—including the microbiome, metabolome, transcriptome, and epigenome—to systematically unravel the complex interactive network between intratumoral microbes and host metabolic reprogramming, DNA methylation, and gene expression in HCC. This work provides multidimensional evidence for understanding the functional mechanisms of microbes in the HCC tumor microenvironment and their potential as diagnostic biomarkers [49]. Previous studies using 16S rDNA sequencing for microbiome analysis in TNBC have encountered difficulties in attaining species-level identification, thereby constraining our comprehension of the tumor microbiome and obstructing the advancement of accurate microbial biomarkers. We used a new method called 2bRAD-M to study microbes in tissue samples from patients with TNBC, with the goal of clearly identifying the different types of microbes in TNBC and creating a model to predict how well NACT works. 2bRAD‑M effectively reduces host DNA interference, enabling us to accurately identify specific microbial species associated with the response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy. This species‑level discrimination is critical. Our 2bRAD‑M analysis allowed us to refine this to the species level, identifying specific pathogens within this class that may be key contributors to non‑pCR. Unlike previous 16S‑based models that relied on genus‑level biomarkers, the identification of specific species minimizes taxonomic ambiguity and enables the precise construction of predictive models, thereby guiding clinical application.

Our investigation revealed that α diversity analysis indicated a greater level in the non-pCR group than in the pCR group. The previously stated taxane-based NACT trial indicated a similar tendency, although it contradicted the findings of the chemo-IM therapy study. We found that this difference might be linked to the different treatment groups, and having more samples could help explain the differences in microbial diversity between them. The analysis of β diversity also revealed significant differences between the two treatment groups.

Our findings suggested that the predominant bacterial communities of the two populations are analogous across several taxonomic levels. Pseudomonadota, as a core bacterial community in the breast, may facilitate tumorigenesis by alterations in abundance and metabolic activity, perhaps via DNA damage, hormone activation, or immunological dysregulation [50, 51]. In the comparison of the two groups, Pseudomonadota exhibited a significantly greater abundance in the non-pCR group. Previous studies have shown that ETEC infection increases the abundance of Pseudomonadota [52], suggesting a potential association between our findings and those of the aforementioned studies of ETBF in TNBC. The abundance of Actinomycetota was greater in the pCR group, and numerous secondary metabolites derived from Actinomycetota possess antitumour properties that demonstrate inhibitory effects on breast cancer cells in vitro [53, 54], suggesting a potential association with improved treatment outcomes in the pCR group.

Notably, the Wilcoxon test and LEfSe analysis identified a higher relative abundance of gram-negative bacterial taxa, including Klebsiella, Escherichia, Acinetobacter, Vibrio, and Meiothermus, in the non-pCR group. Previous studies have also reported that Escherichia and Acinetobacter are prevalent in the breast tissue of individuals with BC [55, 56]. Although these taxa are typical inhabitants of the gut, their presence in mammary tumors may be attributed to the existence of a “gut-mammary axis” [57].

Dysregulation of the microbiota may result in the generation of certain metabolic byproducts that might significantly influence tumor formation. We observed a marked increase in the abundance of gram-negative bacterial communities in the non-pCR group, suggesting that these microbes may be the principal factor contributing to decreased chemotherapeutic effectiveness. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a key component of the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria, is a potent activator of Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) on tumor and immune cells. Gram-negative bacterial communities enrichment intrinsically elevates local LPS concentrations in the tumor microenvironment. Research indicates that LPS-mediated activation of TLR4 plays a pivotal role in driving chemotherapy resistance in TNBC [58]. TLR4 activation triggers the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. When pathologically and persistently activated, this pathway confers upon tumor cells the capacities for uncontrolled proliferation, apoptosis resistance, metastasis, and angiogenesis, and can even lead to the development of drug resistance [59, 60]. Wilkie et al. [61] conducted a metagenomic analysis using a mouse model to assess the association between the microbiota and breast tumor proliferation. The study found that compared with normal breast tissue or early-stage breast tumors, late-stage tumors presented a significant increase in the abundance of gram-negative bacteria and higher levels of LPS, demonstrating the regulatory role of LPS in the expression of the S100A7 protein, which is associated with BC progression and metastasis. Our in vitro experiments observed that the addition of LPS was associated with a restoration of the phosphorylation levels of PI3K/AKT and partially reversed the chemotherapy-induced inhibition of MDA-MB-231 cell viability. These findings suggest a potential link where LPS might mitigate Dox- and PTX-induced cytotoxicity, possibly involving the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Notably, our in vitro experiments employed LPS as a simplified mechanistic model to explore potential biological pathways. While these assays offer preliminary insights, they do not provide direct evidence that the specific bacterial species identified in our microbiome analysis are the causative agents responsible for the observed clinical outcomes. The relationship between particular processes and alterations in pathways linked to elevated LPS levels requires additional examination.

Random forest analysis identified Calidithermus chliarophilus, Meiothermus sp.003226535, and Escherichia coli as the top three contributing species for classifying non-pCR and pCR groups. The selected optimal microbiome containing seven microbial species, showed promising performance in distinguishing non-pCR and pCR tumors, achieving an average AUC value of 84.0%, suggesting that the tumor microbiota may serve as a candidate biomarker for estimating the efficacy of NACT in TNBC patients. We further predicted COG annotations and KEGG pathways, subsequently compared the two groups. Our results suggested broadly significant disparities in predicted microbiome functionality across the non-pCR and pCR groups, as well as KEGG-enriched metabolic pathways. The biosynthesis of secondary metabolites and microbial metabolism in diverse environment-associated metabolic pathways also suggest the influence of microbial metabolites on chemotherapy outcomes. Notably, we observed a significant enrichment of ABC transporters in the non-pCR group. Bacterial ABC transporters can actively efflux a wide range of cytotoxic drugs, including the taxanes and anthracyclines used in NACT, thereby reducing intracellular drug concentrations [62, 63]. The enrichment of this pathway suggests that the intratumoral microbiota in non-pCR patients may possess enhanced multidrug resistance capabilities [64]. Moreover, two-component systems and quorum sensing were upregulated in the non-pCR group. Two-component systems enable bacteria to sense environmental stresses, such as chemotherapeutic agents, and trigger adaptive responses to improve survival. Quorum sensing serves as a key regulator of biofilm formation. Biofilms act as a physical barrier that impedes drug penetration and shields bacterium-tumor cell interactions from immune attack [65].

In terms of clinical translation, intratumoral microbiota analysis could be integrated into personalized treatment frameworks for TNBC. For instance, the enrichment of specific Gram-negative bacteria may serve as a clinical indicator of relative insensitivity to standard anthracycline- and taxane-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy, prompting clinicians to consider alternative therapeutic strategies to optimize patient outcomes. Furthermore, our mechanistic insights into microbiota-induced drug resistance pave the way for developing targeted pharmacological interventions. Nevertheless, as the current study is retrospective in nature, prospective, large-scale multicenter trials are warranted to validate the predictive accuracy of the model and conduct more granular mechanistic studies to refine these microbe-based precision medicine approaches.

Our study has several limitations: Firstly, as a low-biomass microbiome study, this research is subject to certain inherent limitations; despite rigorous quality control, potential environmental contamination and batch effects cannot be entirely eliminated. Secondly, the relatively modest sample size and the imbalance between the pCR and non-pCR groups may have constrained the statistical power. Thirdly, although the external validation cohort was collected in strict accordance with the inclusion and exclusion criteria, the lack of granular clinical data precluded a comprehensive assessment of baseline comparability between the discovery and validation cohorts, which may limit the generalizability of the model. Furthermore, while our study highlights a clinical correlation between the intratumoral microbiota and treatment response, it lacks in-depth mechanistic exploration using live bacterial co-culture systems or in vivo murine models. Such functional validations are imperative to transcend associative evidence and establish definitive causality. Consequently, our findings remain exploratory. Future studies integrating these comprehensive experimental approaches within larger, prospective multicenter cohorts are warranted to fully elucidate the complex crosstalk between the microbiota and the tumor microenvironment.

Conclusion

In summary, our study is an exploratory investigation to characterize the disparities in microbial environments between the non-pCR and pCR groups via 2bRAD-M. The two tissue types have comparable microbial compositions. We found that gram-negative bacterial genera such as Klebsiella, Escherichia, Acinetobacter, Vibrio, and Meiothermus were significantly enriched in the non-pCR group. We hypothesize that elevated LPS levels induced by these bacteria may correlate with diminished chemotherapeutic effectiveness. We developed a prediction model for chemotherapy efficacy using seven microbial species, highlighting the microbiome’s potential as a candidate biomarker for NACT effectiveness in TNBC. Our work enhances the understanding of the microbiome in TNBC and presents novel opportunities for the creation of clinical diagnostic biomarkers.
