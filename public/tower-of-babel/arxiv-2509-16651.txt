Investigating the influence of hydrophobicity and electrostatics on the particle-scale dynamics and rheology of dense microgel suspensions
Sayantan Chanda, Chandeshwar Misra, Ranjini Bandyopadhyay

Abstract
Colloidal microgel particles such as poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM) undergo a reversible volume phase transition at a volume phase transition temperature, VPTT, in aqueous media. Although the particles shrink in size as the temperature is raised beyond the VPTT, Romeo et. al [Adv. Mater. 2010, 22, 3441–3445] had previously shown that dense aqueous PNIPAM suspensions transform from one viscoelastic solid-like phase to another, with an intermediate viscoelastic liquid-like phase near the VPTT, due to a change in the inter-particle interaction from hydrophilic to hydrophobic. Here, we show using a combination of experimental techniques that particle hydrophobicity can be significant even below the VPTT and can result in the emergence of attractive gel-like phases. We achieve this by incorporating polar salts such as sodium chloride and potassium chloride, or non-polar additives such as sucrose, to the aqueous medium. Above the VPTT, we observe that suspension rigidity is the highest in the presence of polar salts because of the combined effects of ionic and hydrophobic attractions. In the presence of non-dissociating sucrose, in contrast, the inter-microgel interaction remains hydrophobic across the VPTT. Such easy tunability of interactions by incorporating commonly available chemicals into the suspension medium opens up new avenues for the synthesis of novel metamaterials.

Introduction:

Colloidal microgel particles, comprising chemically cross-linked three-dimensional polymer networks, are compressible and deformable and can swell and shrink in response to external stimuli such as temperature, pressure, pH, ionic strength, solvent medium, cross-linking density, etc
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
. Poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) or PNIPAM is one such thermo-responsive microgel.
Aqueous suspensions of PNIPAM particles undergo a reversible volume phase
transition at a volume phase transition temperature, VPTT, of approximately 33
∘
C
[
6
]
.
The interaction between microgel particles is governed by a combination of steric hindrance, screened Coulombic repulsion, hydrophobicity and van der Waals interaction
[
7
,
8
]
. Below the VPTT, the solvent-polymer interaction dominates and PNIPAM microgels behave as hydrophilic particles. They remain swollen by imbibing water and interact mainly
via
steric repulsion
[
9
,
7
]
. With an increase in temperature, the particles shrink in size by expelling water and become hydrophobic above the VPTT, leading to a loss of steric repulsion. Charges on the microgel surfaces, arising from the charged initiators and polar cross-linkers used during synthesis, can still electrostatically stabilize the microgels above the VPTT
[
10
]
.

The thermo-responsive nature of PNIPAM particles has been exploited to study a wide variety of viscoelastic phases
[
9
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
. Rheological experiments have previously reported that dense PNIPAM suspensions transform from a repulsive glassy phase below the VPTT to an attractive gel phase above the VPTT
via
a viscoelastic liquid-like phase near the VPTT
[
12
,
13
]
. More recently, Misra et al.
[
10
]
have demonstrated that this phase behaviour of dense PNIPAM suspensions is influenced by the stiffness of individual microgels. Notably, an intermediate liquid-like phase was observed only for the softest PNIPAM microgels. In contrast, the rheological response of suspensions prepared with stiffer particles remained viscoelastic solid-like even near the VPTT.

Incorporating additives in the suspension medium can greatly modify inter-particle interactions among PNIPAM particles by influencing particle hydrophobicity
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
and inter-particle electrostatic interactions
[
8
]
. Osmolytes like glucose and sucrose can significantly increase the hydrophobicity of aqueous PNIPAM suspensions by displacing water molecules from the hydration shells of PNIPAM particles
[
16
]
.
Dissociating salts can also enhance PNIPAM particle hydrophobicity by either rupturing the amide-water hydrogen bonding between the PNIPAM particles and the surrounding water medium or by increasing the surface tension at the particle-water interface, inhibiting hydrophobic hydration
[
18
]
. This enhanced hydrophobicity results in the lowering of the VPTT of the PNIPAM suspensions
[
16
,
19
]
. Additionally, polar salts can also suppress electrostatic repulsion among PNIPAM particles, leading to large-scale particle flocculation
[
8
,
20
]
.

While the roles of hydrophobicity and electrostatics on PNIPAM particles and their suspensions have been studied separately
[
16
,
18
,
8
,
20
]
, their combined effect on the bulk viscoelastic properties of dense PNIPAM suspensions remains largely unexplored. In the present study, we employed an array of experimental techniques to study the impact of hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions on the bulk rheological properties of dense aqueous suspensions of PNIPAM particles. Controlled and systematic variations in particle hydrophobicity and inter-particle electrostatic interaction were achieved
via
the incorporation of dissociating (sodium chloride and potassium chloride) and non-dissociating (sucrose) additives to the suspension medium. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) experiments showed a reduction in particle sizes, while temperature-sweep oscillatory rheology displayed a loss in suspension rigidity below the VPTT due to the introduction of both dissociating and non-dissociating additives. Furthermore, the structural relaxation timescales and yield strain values also decreased with increase in concentrations of all the additives used here. Simultaneously, our cryogenic field emission scanning electron microscopy (cryo-FESEM) data displayed more porous suspension structures in the presence of additives and agreed well with the observed reduction in mechanical moduli values. The contribution of hydrophobicity in driving microgel association and in the emergent suspension properties of dense PNIPAM suspensions can therefore be appreciable even below the VPTT in the presence of suitable additives.

Near and above the VPTT, dense PNIPAM suspensions displayed colloidal gel-like behaviour. Rheological data acquired above the VPTT suggested that the suspensions containing dissociating additives formed stronger and more rigid gels. This observation was again supported by the cryo-FESEM images, which showed comparatively denser space-spanning networks with thicker gel strands in samples prepared with polar salts. Based on our findings from FTIR and zeta potential experiments, we argue that a combined effect of hydrophobic and electrostatic attraction drives stronger gelation above the VPTT in the presence of dissociating salts. In the presence of non-dissociating sucrose, however, inter-particle attraction above the VPTT is driven solely by suspension hydrophobicity, leading to the formation of comparatively weaker gels.

Our results clearly show that while only the contribution of hydrophobicity is important in determining PNIPAM microgel suspension properties in the presence of dissociating and non-dissociating additives below the VPTT, a combined effect of hydrophobicity and electrostatics dominate near and above the VPTT. The physicochemical properties of PNIPAM suspensions can, therefore, be controlled by carefully tuning the interplay between hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions.
The incorporation of dissociating and non-dissociating additives to aqueous PNIPAM suspensions is therefore a good strategy to fine-tune inter-particle interactions, particle self-assembly and the viscoelastic properties of the suspensions.

Materials and methods

2.1
Synthesis and sample preparation:

N-isopropylacrylamide (NIPAM, 99%), N, N
′
-methylenebisacrylamide (MBA, 99.5%), sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and potassium persulfate (KPS) (99.9%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Potassium chloride (KCl,
\leq
99.0%) and sucrose (
\leq
99.5% ) were also procured from Sigma-Aldrich. Sodium chloride (NaCl, purity 99%) was purchased from Labort Fine Chem Pvt. Ltd. All the chemicals were used as received without any further purification.

The synthesis process followed in this study was the same as reported in a previous work
[
21
]
. 7.0 gm NIPAM, 0.7 gm MBA and 0.03 gm SDS were dissolved in 470 ml MilliQ water (Millipore Corp.) in a three-necked round-bottom (RB) flask. The solution was then stirred at 600 RPM and purged with N
2
gas for 30 minutes to create an inert environment for subsequent polymerization. The temperature inside the RB flask was raised to 70
∘
C, after which 0.28 gm KPS dissolved in 30 ml Milli-Q water was introduced. The introduction of KPS initiated a polymerization reaction that continued for 4 hours. The suspension was then cooled down to room temperature. Four successive rounds of centrifugations at 20,000 RPM for 60 minutes each were conducted to remove unused SDS, the remaining monomers, and other impurities. After every round of centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded and replaced by fresh Milli-Q water. After the fourth round of centrifugation, the samples were completely dried. The dried particles were ground into fine powder for further use. The spherical PNIPAM particles synthesized using this process have a core-shell structure, with the inner core exhibiting high polymer density and the peripheral shell having low polymer density
[
22
]
.

Dense aqueous PNIPAM suspensions were prepared by adding dry PNIPAM powder to Milli-Q water. To prepare PNIPAM-additive mixtures, aqueous solutions of specific additive concentrations were first prepared by adding NaCl, KCl or sucrose in water. A fixed amount of dry PNIPAM powder was next added to the additive solutions. All the dense suspensions thus prepared were stirred very gently over a period of 48 to 72 hours to ensure homogeneity and stored in a refrigerator at a temperature of 4
∘
C for future use.

2.2
Dynamic light scattering (DLS):

The mean hydrodynamic diameters
<d_{h}>
of non-interacting PNIPAM particles in dilute aqueous suspensions with and without additives were evaluated as a function of temperature
via
dynamic light scattering experiments using a Brookhaven Instruments Corporation (BIC) BI- 200SM spectrometer. The temperature of the sample was controlled between 18
∘
C and 50
∘
C by a water-circulating temperature controller unit (Polyscience Digital). Intensity autocorrelation functions, acquired by an autocorrelator (BI-9000AT) over a range of delay times spanning from 1
\mu
s to 500
\mu
s for an experimental duration of 4 minutes, were acquired and analysed to compute
<d_{h}>
. The procedure adopted to estimate
<d_{h}>
is given in section ST1 of the supplementary information.
Further details about the setup are given elsewhere
[
23
,
24
]
. The mean hydrodynamic diameters,
<d_{h}>
, were further used to calculate temperature-dependent changes in the effective volume fraction,
\phi_{eff}
, of the compressible and deformable PNIPAM particles in dense suspensions. The detailed procedure used to estimate
\phi_{eff}
is given in section ST2 of the supplementary information. Fig. S3 shows the variation of
\phi_{eff}
as a function of temperature in the presence of various additives. We note a reduction in the effective volume fractions of PNIPAM suspensions upon the inclusion of additives in the medium.

2.3
Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC):

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) (Mettler Toledo, DSC 3) measurements were performed to estimate the VPTT of dense PNIPAM suspensions of concentration 15.42% w/v with and without additives. The corresponding effective volume fractions,
\phi_{eff}
, lie between 1.5 in the case of pure water to 0.95 for 0.6M NaCl at 25
∘
C. A temperature ramp of 1
∘
C/min was applied to estimate the heat flows in a temperature range 20-50
∘
C. The heat flows evaluated in DSC experiments as a function of temperature, T, are plotted in Figs. S4(a-c). The temperature corresponding to the endothermic peak was designated as the VPTT and is displayed in Fig. S5. As additive concentrations are increased, the observed decreases in the VPTTs indicate earlier onset of hydrophobicity.

2.4
Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy:

FTIR spectra were measured using a Shimadzu IR Tracer-100 Fourier Transform Infrared Spectrometer. PNIPAM suspensions of concentration 15.42% w/v, both with and without additives, were loaded in a sample cell sandwiched between two zinc sulfide (ZnS) plates separated by a Teflon spacer of thickness 1 mm. The temperature of the sample cell was controlled using a water circulation unit (Julabo 300F). Background spectra containing either pure water or additive solutions (without PNIPAM particles) were acquired and subtracted from the spectra obtained for the PNIPAM suspensions by following the protocol given in a previous work
[
17
]
. Baseline correction, which involved flattening the baseline around the peaks of interest, was carried out using the ’Peak and Baseline’ tool in Origin (version: 2023).

The FTIR spectra of the suspensions are shown in Figs. S6-S9. of the supplementary information. Two prominent peaks, the amide II and amide I bands, were observed at
{\sim}
1560 cm
-1
and
{\sim}
1625 cm
-1
, respectively. The amide II band results from N–H bending and C–N stretching vibrations in PNIPAM particles
[
25
]
, while the amide I peak results from C=O stretching and H-bonding interactions between water and the amide moieties in PNIPAM particles
[
25
]
. At temperature above the VPTT, a weak peak at
{\sim}
1652 cm
-1
represents intra or inter-molecular hydrogen bonding among the amide moieties of the PNIPAM particles
[
26
]
. An increase in hydrophobicity due to enhanced amide-amide bonding is expected to increase the area under the weak peak at
{\sim}
1652 cm
-1

[
25
,
26
]
. Simultaneously, a decrease in hydrogen bonding between the amide moiety of PNIPAM particles and water reduces the area under the
{\sim}

,
26

Following the procedure adopted in previous reports
[

26

, we deconvoluted the amide I peak obtained from FTIR data of PNIPAM suspensions with and without additives. The areas under
{\sim}
1625 cm

{\sim}

, defined as the ratio of the area under the 1652 cm

-1
and 1625 cm

[
26
]
, with a higher

2.5
Zeta potential:

Zeta potentials were measured using an electro-acoustic device (DT-100) procured from Dispersion Technology Inc.
[
27
]
. Aqueous suspensions of PNIPAM particles at a fixed particle concentration of 1% w/v with varying additive concentrations were studied. The samples were kept inside an oil bath to maintain a fixed temperature during the experiments. The zeta potential probe comprises a transducer generating ultrasound waves at a frequency of 3 MHz. The charges present in the electrical double layer of the PNIPAM particles execute oscillation in response to the ultrasound waves, thereby producing a colloidal vibration current (CVI). The probe detects the amplitude and phase of this CVI and computes zeta potential using the DT-1200 software. Further details about the instrument can be found elsewhere
[
27
]
.

2.6
Rheology:

Rheological measurements were performed using a stress-controlled MCR 702 rheometer. A cone-plate geometry with a measuring gap of 0.048 mm and a Peltier unit capable of controlling temperatures between 0
∘
C and 180
∘
C were used. Silicone oil of viscosity 5 cSt was used as a solvent trap oil to prevent solvent loss from the samples during the experiments. The viscoelastic properties of dense suspensions of PNIPAM particles of concentration 15.42% w/v, with and without additives, were investigated by performing oscillatory rheological experiments. Temperature sweep experiments were conducted by increasing the temperature from 15
∘
C to 60
∘
C at a rate of 1
∘
C/min while maintaining the peak-to-peak strain amplitude at 0.5% and the applied angular frequency at 0.5 rad/sec. For frequency sweep experiments, the peak-to-peak strain amplitude was kept constant at 0.5% while logarithmically ramping up the applied angular frequency. For amplitude sweep experiments, the applied angular frequency was kept constant at 0.5 rad/sec, while the applied strain amplitude was increased logarithmically. Frequency and amplitude sweep experiments were performed at discrete temperatures below (18
∘
C), near (27
∘
C and 34
∘
C) and above (45
∘
C) the VPTT.

The structural relaxation timescales of soft glassy systems are very slow and usually lie outside the experimental time window accessible in a regular frequency sweep test
[
12
,
9
]
. To quantify the slow glassy dynamics of the samples below the VPTT, strain rate frequency sweep (SRFS) experiments were performed. In contrast to regular frequency sweep measurements where the oscillatory strain amplitude,
{\gamma_{0}}
, is kept constant, the strain rate amplitude,
\dot{\gamma_{0}}
, is kept constant in SRFS experiments
[
28
,
29
]
.
It was shown that under an externally imposed strain, a system’s structural relaxation timescale satisfies the empirical relation 1/
\tau
(
\dot{\gamma_{0}}
) = 1/
\tau_{0}
+ K
\dot{\gamma_{0}}^{\nu}
, where
\tau_{0}
is the relaxation timescale in the absence of external strain,
\tau
(
\dot{\gamma_{0}})
is the structural relaxation timescale measured upon the application of a strain rate amplitude
\dot{\gamma_{0}}
, while K and
\nu
are positive constants
[
28
]
. By applying sufficiently large
\dot{\gamma_{0}}
values, relaxation processes can be driven to frequency windows lying within the experimentally accessible frequency range. As a result, even ultra-slow relaxation processes of soft glassy materials can be reliably determined in SRFS experiments.

2.7
Cryogenic field emission scanning electron microscopy:

Cryogenic field emission scanning electron microscopy (cryo-FESEM) was carried out using a field emission scanning
electron microscope (Ultra Plus FESEM-4098; Carl Zeiss) having an electron beam strength of 5 KeV. The samples were first equilibrated to a pre-determined temperature and subsequently loaded in a rivet fixed with a cryostub. The samples were then vitrified in liquid nitrogen slush at -190
∘
C
and transferred to a vacuum chamber maintained at -190
∘
C(PP3000T Quorum technology). The samples were then sublimated at -90
∘
C for 5 minutes and cryo-fractured using an in-built stainless steel knife. The fractured samples were further sublimated for 12 minutes at -90
∘
C to remove ice from the sample structures. A gold coating of thickness around 5 nm was then applied to the sample surfaces to ensure good image contrast. Finally, the samples were transferred to the cryo-chamber, maintained at a temperature of -190
∘
C for imaging. Back-scattered secondary electrons were used to reconstruct the surface images of the samples.

When compared to particle sizes,

We note that the effective volume fractions of the suspensions,

Simultaneously, the temperature-dependent G

Fig. S12 shows that the temperature at which

The simultaneous increase in suspension rigidity near the VPTT suggests the aggregation of PNIPAM particles into clusters. One possible mechanism for such aggregation could be the suppression of inter-particle screened Coulombic repulsions. The abrupt rise in
<d_{h}
> near the VPTT in the presence of dissociating additives and the absence of cluster formation in the presence of non-dissociating sucrose verifies our hypothesis that the screening of electrostatic attractions between PNIPAM particles plays a crucial role in the observed increase in

near the VPTT. However, the decrease in

Aqueous suspensions of PNIPAM microgels are known to form attractive gel phases above the VPTT
[
12
,
20
]
. Although the particles shrink in size, the attractive inter-particle interaction originating from growing particle hydrophobicity drives the gelation process
[
10
,
9
]
. The observed increase in moduli values above the VPTT with increase in temperature, as seen in Fig.
1
(b), is therefore not surprising. Contrary to our observations below the VPTT, we see that suspension rigidities, as estimated from G
′

1
and S10(a-c) that the G
′

This observation indicates the substantial impact of electrostatic attraction even above the VPTT in the presence of dissociating salts.

3.2
Modifying hydrophobicity and electrostatic interactions in aqueous PNIPAM suspensions by incorporating additives:

Alterations in particle hydrophobicity due to the incorporation of additives in aqueous PNIPAM suspension was quantified by performing FTIR measurements as discussed in section 2.4. Fig.
2
(a) shows that the measure of hydrophobicity,
f_{A}
, increases with both temperature and additive concentration. Experiments and MD simulations have reported that the introduction of osmolytes like sucrose in the aqueous medium increases the hydrophobicity of PNIPAM particles
[
16
]
.

Fig. 2:
(a) The relative strength of the amide-amide bonding,
f_{A}
, used here as a parameter to quantify particle hydrophobicity, is plotted as a function of additive concentration for NaCl (red), KCl (green) and sucrose (blue) at 18
∘
C (bold symbol) and 45
∘
C (hollow symbol). (b) Temperature-dependent zeta potential of 1% w/v PNIPAM suspensions with varying concentrations of additives.

Sucrose, being highly hydrophilic, displaces water molecules from the hydration shell of PNIPAM particles. The dehydration of PNIPAM particles in the presence of sucrose facilitates intramolecular hydrogen bonding in PNIPAM particles, leading to a coil to globule transition
[
16
]
.
Additionally, a minor contribution to hydrophobicity also comes from the direct interaction between the amide moieties of the particle and sucrose, which reduces the number of hydrogen bonding sites available between water molecules and PNIPAM particles
[
16
]
. This results in an increase in particle hydrophobicity with an increase in sucrose concentration as seen in Fig.
2
(a).

In contrast, the presence of ionic salts like NaCl and KCl in the aqueous medium enhance hydrophobicity mainly by increasing the surface tension at the particle-water interface
[
18
,
30
]
. Water molecules form ordered structures around the hydrophobic polymer backbone and the isopropyl groups of PNIPAM particles, a phenomenon known as hydrophobic hydration. Cl
-1
anions raise the surface tension at this interface formed by the water molecules and the hydrophobic groups in PNIPAM particles
[
18
,
30
]
. This inhibits hydrophobic hydration, leading to the shrinkage of PNIPAM particles. Previous studies have reported that surface tension increases with increase in salt concentration at the aqueous-polymer interfaces
[
31
]
.
Therefore, as shown in Figs.
1
, S3 and
2
(a), the incorporation of suitable additives in aqueous PNIPAM suspensions can lead to significant particle hydrophobicity, reductions in particle sizes
<d_{H}>
and, in turn, the particle effective volume fraction,
\phi_{eff}
, and G
′
even below the VPTT. Above the VPTT, the substantially higher values of G
′
indicates the formation of stronger gels due to increase in particle hydrophobicity in suspensions containing additives. We further note that the increase in particle hydrophobicity in the presence of additives also leads to a systematic reduction of the VPTT of the suspensions as estimated from DSC experiments shown previously in Fig. S5.

The impact of additives on inter-particle electrostatic interactions was analysed through zeta potential measurements, as described in section 2.5. Fig.
2
(b) shows temperature-dependent variations of the surface zeta potentials,
\zeta
, of PNIPAM particles in aqueous suspensions both in the presence and absence of dissociating and non-dissociating additives.
PNIPAM particles acquire surface charges due to the use of the polar cross-linker (MBA) and initiator (KPS) during particle synthesis. As the temperature rises and the particles shrink, the surface charge density increases
[
32
,
10
]
, leading to higher negative zeta potential values in in PNIPAM samples prepared in pure water and non-dissociating sucrose solutions.
Previous reports have shown that a minimum zeta potential of
\pm
30mV is required for electrostatic stabilisation of colloidal particles in suspensions
[
33
]
.
So the high negative zeta values of PNIPAM suspensions prepared in pure water and sucrose above the VPTT indicate good electrostatic stability. In the presence of NaCl and KCl, however, the Debye screening layers surrounding colloidal PNIPAM particles shrink due to the increase in the number of dissociated ions in the suspension medium
[
33
]
. Addition of dissociating salts, therefore, results in the observed reduction of
\zeta
and significantly accelerates the flocculation of PNIPAM particles
[
8
,
34
]
, leading to an abrupt jump in
<d_{H}>
near the VPTT as shown previously in Figs.
1
and S10(a,b). The additional impact of electrostatic attraction also leads to stronger gelation near and above the VPTT, as shown by the higher G
′
values in the presence of polar salts as compared to sucrose in Figs
1
.
It is also important to note that while the
\zeta
values remain low below the VPTT in all cases, flocculation is less likely in this regime due to a negligible mismatch in the Hamaker constant between the swollen PNIPAM particles and the aqueous medium
[
8
]
.
The reported results complement the temperature sweep data in Fig.
1
and suggest that the viscoelastic properties below the VPTT are primarily influenced by hydrophobicity-induced reduction in particle size and consequently the suspension volume fraction, a common factor prevalent in both dissociating and non-dissociating additives.
Near and above the VPTT, though, bulk rheological properties are governed mainly by hydrophobicity alone in the case of sucrose, whereas for salts, it is a combined effect of hydrophobicity and electrostatic attraction.

4
Conclusions:

The combined effects of hydrophobicity and electrostatics in determining the bulk rheological properties of dense aqueous suspensions of PNIPAM particles is investigated in this study. Two dissociating (NaCl and KCl) and one non-dissociating (sucrose) additive were added to dense aqueous suspensions of PNIPAM particles to tune the sizes and inter-particle interactions between the particles. FTIR showed that particle hydrophobicity increases systematically with increase in additive concentration across the VPTT. Zeta potential measurements suggested that, in addition to enhanced hydrophobicity, the presence of dissociating additives also suppresses screened Coulombic repulsion. Oscillatory rheological measurements were further carried out to understand the impact of such modified inter-particle interaction on the viscoelastic properties of dense PNIPAM suspensions. We observed that the mechanical moduli values, relaxation timescales, and yield strains all exhibited very similar concentration-dependent behaviours in the presence of both dissociating and
non-dissociating additives below the VPTT. Our rheological data suggested that the viscoelastic properties of the suspensions are mainly tuned by growing particle hydrophobicity due to the introduction of both dissociating and non-dissociating additives below the VPTT. Reduction in
\phi_{eff}
due to particle deswelling in the presence of additives also lead to faster particle-scale dynamics below the VPTT. Furthermore, our cryo-FESEM data indicated a more porous morphological structure in samples containing additives below the VPTT, providing visual evidence for the reduction of suspension rigidity under these conditions. Our observations here underline the impact of hydrophobic interaction in aqueous PNIPAM suspensions even below the VPTT in the presence of additives. It also shows that electrostatic interactions remain largely insignificant in affecting the rheological properties of dense PNIPAM suspensions in this temperature regime.

The rheological properties acquired near and above the VPTT displayed signatures consistent with the formation of colloidal gels. We argue that the attractive interaction results from a combined effect of enhanced hydrophobicity and suppression of screened Coulombic repulsion in the presence of dissociating salts in the suspension medium. In the case of sucrose, though, inter-particle attraction comes from enhanced particle hydrophobicity alone. The rheological data acquired in this temperature regime revealed that the mechanical moduli of suspensions containing dissociating salts were greater than those prepared in sucrose solutions. Our cryo-FESEM data also showed relatively denser space-spanning networks with thicker gel strands above the VPTT for samples prepared with polar salts.
These observations clearly indicate the increasingly dominant role of electrostatics in driving attractions between the collapsed microgel particles with significant surface charge densities. We also observed a two-step yielding behaviour above the VPTT in the presence of additives, suggesting colloidal gelation. However, we found that the relaxation timescale and yield strain values depended strongly on the nature of the additive used above the VPTT, even when their concentrations were kept constant, again reflecting the importance of electrostatics.

We conclude that below the VPTT, particle hydrophobicity governs the viscoelastic behaviour of dense PNIPAM suspensions, whereas near and above the VPTT, both hydrophobicity and electrostatic interaction determine the rheology and particle-scale dynamics of the suspensions.
The present study shows that the bulk viscoelastic properties of microgel suspensions can be tuned just by introducing suitable additives without changing the particle concentration.
The tunable inter-particle interaction between PNIPAM particles can be exploited to control their self-assembly in aqueous suspension. Suspensions of PNIPAM microgels can therefore emerge as excellent candidates in the design of multifunctional materials
[
41
]
. Fine-tuning the dynamics and rheology of PNIPAM suspensions
via
the incorporation of additives also holds tremendous promise in enabling the usage of PNIPAM hydrogels as actuators over a range of temperatures
[
42
]
. Additionally, the easy tunability of the VPTT of PNIPAM particles prepared in additive solutions makes microgel systems a potential candidate for use in various cargo delivery applications at specified temperatures
[
43
]
. Changing the polarity of the crosslinker during PNIPAM synthesis would provide additional control over the relative contributions of electrostatics and hydrophobicity above the VPTT.
