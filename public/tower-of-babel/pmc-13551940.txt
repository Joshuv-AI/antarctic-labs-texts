Polygenic risk score for early identification of coronary artery disease in a real-world clinical setting within the Latvian patient population
Authors: Evija Kanašniece, Elita Ozola, Līvija Bārdiņa, Dmitrijs Rots, Marija Lazovska, Baiba Vilne, Linda Gailīte, Andrejs Ērglis, Kārlis Trušinskis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy objectivePolygenic risk scores (PRS) are increasingly recognized for their potential to improve coronary artery disease (CAD) prediction beyond traditional clinical models. This study evaluated the utility of genome-wide association study (GWAS) - derived PRS and pathway-specific PRS (PS-PRS) in the Latvian population, aiming to assess their association with CAD and compare their predictive performance with conventional risk factors.Design, participants and main outcome measuresThe study included 90 early-onset CAD patients and 43 controls with no evidence of atherosclerotic lesions on coronary angiography, with next-generation sequencing performed. PRS was calculated using 192 single nucleotide variants identified from the CARDIoGRAMplusC4D GWAS meta-analysis. The predictive accuracy of PRS, PS-PRS, clinical risk factors, and their combinations was analyzed via ROC curves.ResultsThe average age was 48.7 years in CAD patients and 49.8 in controls. CAD patients showed significantly higher PRS (mean 0.31) compared to controls (mean − 0.65; p < 0.0001). PRS alone had moderate discriminatory power (AUC = 0.773), slightly lower than LDL cholesterol (AUC = 0.775) and total cholesterol (AUC = 0.821). Combining clinical risk factors improved prediction (AUC = 0.872), with the highest accuracy when PRS was integrated with all clinical factors (AUC = 0.933). The PRS distributions were significantly elevated in early-onset CAD patients across the angiogenesis/tissue repair pathway (p = 0.00038), inflammation pathway (p = 0.043), vascular remodelling pathway (p = 0.0116), and pathway of genes with unknown function in atherosclerosis (p = 0.0035), but overall PRS demonstrated superior discrimination compared to pathway-specific PRS.ConclusionsIncorporating PRS enhances early-onset CAD risk prediction. Pathway specific PRS had lower discriminative ability than the overall PRS.Keywords: Polygenic risk score, Pathway-specific polygenic risk score, Coronary artery disease, Atherosclerosis

Introduction

The increasing prevalence of conventional cardiovascular risk factors in younger people has contributed to a rising incidence of premature coronary artery disease (CAD) [1]. Identifying patients with early-onset CAD prior to the occurrence of an acute cardiovascular event is crucial for enabling timely intervention and preventing adverse outcomes. Gonuguntla et al. reported that among 3,017,826 acute myocardial infarction (MI)-related deaths from 1999 to 2020, there was a general decline in overall MI mortality; however, a significant increase in the incidence of premature MI-related mortality, with a notably greater impact among young female patients, was observed [2].

Several clinical risk assessment tools, including the Framingham Risk Score [3], PROCAM [4], and SCORE2 [5], have been developed to identify individuals at risk and guide preventive strategies. However, these risk calculators often underestimate the risk in young patients [6], [7], necessitating alternative approaches for accurate prediction and management. Epidemiological studies have shown that genetic predisposition affects 40–50% of the risk for CAD [8]. Single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) are variations at a single nucleotide position in the DNA sequence and represent a major source of genetic heterogeneity [9]. Several specific SNVs have been associated with CAD, with the 9p21.3 risk allele representing one of the most extensively supported genetic variants. The 9p21.3 risk allele is present in approximately 75% of the European population, conferring a 25% increased risk of CAD with one copy and a 50% increase with two copies; in cases of premature CAD, it is associated with a twofold increase in risk [10]. Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) and their meta-analyses, involving thousands of patients and healthy controls, have provided the statistical power necessary to detect genetic variants. To date, >390 risk loci have been identified, each conferring a modest increase in disease risk [11]. Inherited risk of CAD typically results from the combined effect of numerous common (minor allele frequency > 5%) genetic variants of small effect sizes, functioning collectively alongside environmental and lifestyle risk factors [12]. This polygenic predisposition may even exert a more significant influence on CAD development than rare monogenic mutations do [13]. The polygenic risk score (PRS) is a quantitative genetic risk score that aggregates the combined effects of genome-wide variants to enhance risk prediction for chronic diseases, including CAD [13]. Several studies have reported improvements in CAD prediction when the PRS is combined with traditional risk factors [14], [15], [16]. Recent advancements in polygenic risk score research have focused on exploring CAD risk genes within a systems biology framework, providing more detailed insights into how genetic variations within specific biological pathways influence CAD risk. Aherrahrou et al. developed a comprehensive map of CAD-associated pathological pathways, categorizing 393 CAD risk loci into 20 distinct pathophysiological pathways related to atherosclerosis [11].

This study aimed to evaluate the applicability of GWAS-derived PRS in the genomes of Latvian patients with early-onset CAD and controls using the CARDIoGRAMplusC4D dataset. The dataset, a collaboration between the CARDIoGRAM and C4D consortia, combines data from more than 200,000 individuals of European and South Asian descent, including sources such as deCODE, UK Biobank, and EPIC-CVD. Through genome-wide SNV coverage and targeted panels, this large-scale meta-analysis revealed 279 genetic loci linked to increased CAD risk. [17]. The objective of our study was to evaluate the potential of the PRS and pathway-specific PRS approach to improve risk prediction for early-onset CAD and to compare its predictive performance against those of conventional clinical risk factor models in a Latvian patient population.

Materials and methods

Patients and genomic data processing. This cross-sectional study, conducted at a single centre, received approval from the Central Medical Ethics Committee of Latvia, and the study protocol was approved by an institutional review board. The study conformed to the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki. Patients were enrolled after signed informed consent was obtained. Early-onset CAD was defined as angiographically confirmed coronary atherosclerotic plaques in men <55 years and women <65 years. Although definitions of early-onset CAD vary, this study adopted the sex-specific thresholds recommended by the European Society of Cardiology for risk stratification and risk calculation [18], which account for the later clinical onset of CAD in women and the greater enrichment of inherited susceptibility among younger cases. These thresholds were applied to preferentially enrich the cohort for early-onset disease, in which genetic predisposition is likely to contribute relatively more than cumulative, age-related risk exposure. Male patients under 55 years of age and female patients under 65 years of age were evaluated for inclusion in the study in the coronary angiography unit following elective diagnostic coronary angiography to which they had been referred by their cardiologists or family physicians. Based on age criteria and angiographic findings, the subjects were stratified into two groups: a control group (n = 43), consisting of CAD-symptom free individuals with no evidence of atherosclerotic lesions on angiography and no history of prior percutaneous coronary intervention or myocardial infarction, and an early-onset CAD group (n = 96), comprising patients with documented atherosclerotic plaques. Patients who had cardiac or noncardiac illness with a life expectancy of less than one year, acute coronary syndrome, congestive heart failure (NYHA III–IV), anamnesis of uncontrolled hypertension, obesity ≥ grade 2, family history of early-onset CAD, known familial hypercholesterolemia, diabetes mellitus, glucose-lowering medication use, and significant renal disease with a glomerular filtration rate < 30 ml/min/m2 were excluded from the study. Fasting blood samples were obtained from all the study patients to determine plasma glucose levels and liver and kidney marker and lipid levels and to perform next-generation sequencing. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) was performed using a custom Twist Bioscience target enrichment panel designed for this study. The panel targeted 241 CAD risk SNVs curated from Aragam et al. [17]. During probe design, several loci located within segmental duplications or low-complexity genomic regions were excluded because unique probe synthesis and reliable read mapping were not technically feasible. The final panel, therefore, included 200 SNVs with high sequence specificity and capture performance. Data processing and analysis were carried out using the DNANexus platform. Raw sequencing reads were aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome using BWA-MEM. Patients' baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were collected.

Data format. Genotype data, including both reference and alternative alleles for each participant, were initially formatted into PLINK-compatible .ped and .map files for downstream analysis. The .map file provided chromosome numbers, SNV identifiers, and genomic positions, whereas the .ped file contained individual identifiers and genotype calls [19]. The genotype and associated statistical data were subsequently converted into binary PLINK format files: .bed (genotype data in compressed binary form), .bim (SNV locations and allele information), and .fam (participant IDs and family structure) [19], [20], [21].

PRS calculation and quality control. Individual participant genotypes were determined for all selected CAD-associated variants. Alleles observed in the target cohort were subsequently compared with the CARDIoGRAMplusC4D variant annotations to identify the corresponding effect and non-effect alleles. Effect alleles were assigned according to the definitions and β coefficients provided by the CARDIoGRAMplusC4D consortium, and the number of effect allele copies carried by each participant was used for polygenic risk score calculation [22], [23]. The CARDIoGRAMplusC4D dataset, which served as the sole source of CAD-associated variants in this study, had already undergone extensive discovery-stage quality control, including p value thresholds (p ≤ 5 × 10−8), MAF filtering (≥0.01), linkage disequilibrium pruning, and HWE checks [24]. Additional PRS-specific quality control was performed following and linkage disequilibrium clumping using a 250 kb window and an r2 threshold of 0.1 recommended by Choi et al. [25]. PRSs were computed using two tools—PRSice and PLINK v1.90—both of which were operated via command-line interfaces, with initial quality control following Choi SW [25] and Moragas N and Moreno V [26]. The final PRS calculations included both tools; PRSice identified 32 ambiguous SNVs, and calculations were performed both including and excluding these variants; because the results were highly similar, the ambiguous SNVs were retained [21]. To facilitate interpretation, raw PRSs were standardized using pandas (with ddof = 0) to calculate population standard deviations [22].

Study cohort and PRS variant selection. A total of 96 participants with CAD was initially considered. Following quality-control procedures, three subjects were excluded for missing genotypic data and three for missing phenotypic data, yielding a final analysis sample of 90 CAD participants. The PRS panel comprised 200 CAD-associated variants. During quality control, four variants (rs11316597, rs2215614, rs11206803 and rs10951983) were excluded because of unreliable or missing genotype data in the target cohort. After linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping, four additional variants (rs149487184, rs1998043, rs67807996 and rs7177201) were excluded. As a result, 192 independent CAD-associated variants remained and were included in the final polygenic risk score analysis (Suppl. Table 1).

Assessment of the impact of sex on PRS analysis. To evaluate the potential influence of sex on polygenic risk score (PRS) analysis, effect allele frequencies (EAF) of all PRS variants were compared between males and females, and the association between PRS and CAD was assessed using binary logistic regression models with sequential adjustment for sex and age. Model performance was evaluated using measures of model fit, discrimination and calibration. Detailed descriptions of the statistical analyses and model validation are provided in the Suppl. Table 1 and 5.

Pathway-specific PRS calculation. SNVs were assigned to their nearest genes based on genomic position. The resulting genes were then mapped to specific pathophysiological pathways according to the classification proposed by Aherrahrou et al. [11] (Suppl. Table 2). In this classification, genes are allocated to 20 biologically defined categories reflecting CAD-relevant molecular functions and processes, including: developmental processes; signal transduction; energy homeostasis; cell motility; lipid metabolism; neuronal signalling; blood pressure regulation and nitric oxide signalling; inflammation, autophagy, RNA nucleoside processing, angiogenesis and tissue repair, vascular remodelling, glucose and insulin metabolism, cell cycle and proliferation, thrombosis, heart function, cellular stress resistance, transcriptional regulation, vesicle transport, and pathways with unknown molecular mechanisms. We removed pathways with fewer than 10 SNVs in the dataset to exclude overly specific pathways that would have insufficient power for enrichment testing.

Statistical analysis. The PRS model with the highest area under the ROC curve (AUC) curve performance was selected for further analysis. All the statistical analyses were conducted in Python (version 3.13.2) using custom scripts within the Visual Studio Code environment. Given the relatively small cohort size and to obtain more robust and reproducible estimates of model performance, the AUC and its 95% confidence intervals were estimated using a nonparametric bootstrap approach with 1000 resampling iterations. This bootstrapping procedure quantifies the variability of the AUC by repeatedly resampling the dataset with replacement, providing a more reliable and stable assessment of model discrimination in small-sample settings. Odds ratio were estimated using binary logistic regression models, with ORs and 95% confidence intervals obtained by exponentiating the regression coefficients. Continuous predictors, including PRS, were standardized prior to analysis; therefore, OR represent the change in CAD odds per one–standard deviation (SD) increase in the predictor. Student's t-tests and Pearson correlation analyses were performed to evaluate group differences and variable associations, and box plot visualizations were generated to illustrate PRS distributions between cases and controls. Further comparisons were made between the AUC of the top-performing PRS and the AUCs of individual phenotypic variables, as well as the combined AUC from integrating the PRS with all phenotypic variables.

Model discrimination was evaluated using ROC curves and the corresponding AUC. Differences in AUC between models were assessed using DeLong's test for correlated ROC curves. To evaluate the adequacy of the sample size and the reliability of discrimination results, statistical power for each AUC was calculated for the hypothesis test of AUC ≠ 0.5 at α = 0.05. Power was estimated analytically using the Hanley and McNeil method to derive the standard error of the AUC, assuming asymptotic normality of the AUC estimator. The minimum AUC required to achieve 80% and 90% statistical power was determined based on the observed numbers of cases and controls. This approach enabled assessment of whether non-significant findings were attributable to insufficient discriminatory effect or limited sample size.

The predictive performance of the three logistic regression models (Model 1: CAD ∼ PRS, Model 2: CAD ∼ PRS + Sex, and Model 3: CAD ∼ PRS + Sex + Age) was assessed using 10-fold cross-validation. The dataset was randomly partitioned into ten approximately equal folds. At each iteration, one-fold was used for validation while the remaining nine folds were used for model training. The Brier score was calculated as the mean squared difference between the observed outcomes and the predicted probabilities in the validation fold, and the final cross-validated estimate was obtained by averaging the fold-specific Brier scores. Bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations was used to estimate 95% confidence intervals.

Results

Patient baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. The mean ages of the participants were 48.7 years in the early-onset CAD group and 49.8 years in the control group. The majority of patients in both groups were male, comprising 86.7% of the cases and 58.1% of the controls. Sex-stratified analysis of baseline characteristics revealed no significant differences between males and females, except for age (Suppl. Table 3). Patients in the early-onset CAD group had significantly lower total cholesterol, LDL-cholesterol and HDL-cholesterol levels, likely due to the high prevalence of lipid-lowering therapy within this cohort (89.6% vs. 39.5%; p < 0.001). BMI did not differ between the two groups, likely reflecting our patient selection criteria.

Baseline characteristics.

PRS in the study groups. A significant difference in the mean PRS was detected between patients with early-onset CAD (mean PRS = 0.31) and controls (mean PRS = −0.65) (p < 0.0001) (Suppl. Fig. 1 and 2). The OR for CAD per one SD increase in PRS was 3.46, indicating that individuals with a PRS one SD above the population mean exhibited approximately threefold higher odds of developing CAD than those with an average PRS did. When the 95th percentile of the control PRS distribution (PRS > 0.772) was used as a threshold to define high genetic risk, 27.8% of individuals with CAD and 7% of controls were found to exceed this cut-off, corresponding to an approximately sixfold higher incidence of CAD among individuals with high PRS values.

Classification power of PRS versus risk factors. As shown in Fig. 1, the discriminatory performance of the PRS alone (AUC = 0.772; 95% CI 0.689–0.85) was lower than those of LDL cholesterol (AUC = 0.775; 95% CI 0.693–0.855) and total cholesterol (AUC = 0.821; 95% CI 0.748–0.890), with the greatest discriminative accuracy observed when all clinical factors were combined (AUC = 0.872; 95% CI 0.834–0.948). The combined model including the classic risk factors - arterial hypertension, smoking, hyperlipidaemia and obesity (diabetes excluded per study criteria) as defined by the Global Cardiovascular Risk Consortium [26], demonstrated limited discriminative accuracy (AUC = 0.683; 95% CI 0.585–0.770), which was lower than that achieved by the PRS alone. The weakest discriminatory power was observed for triglycerides (AUC 0.486; 95% CI 0.445–0.627) as depicted in Suppl. Fig. 3, which presents the comparative ROC analysis of all clinical risk factor models.

ROC curve comparison for clinical factor models meeting 90% power threshold (AUC ≥ 657).

Classification power of the PRS in combination with risk factors. Adding the PRS to individual risk factors or their combinations enhanced the overall discriminatory ability for predicting CAD (Fig. 2). The highest predictive accuracy was achieved with PRS combined with all clinical factors (AUC 0.933; 95% CI 0.904–0.982), exceeding the performances of both PRS alone and the clinical factors alone. The second highest performance was observed with PRS combined with total cholesterol (AUC = 0.893; 95% CI 0.838–0.949), followed by PRS with LDL (AUC = 0.862; 95% CI 0.795–0.925). The combination of PRS and the classic risk factors had fair discriminatory ability (AUC 0.809; 95% CI 0.734–0.886) (Suppl.Table 4). Given the sample size, the minimum AUCs required to attain 80% and 90% statistical power were 0.634 and 0.657, respectively. Evaluated individually, the clinical variables triglycerides, age, body mass index, history of smoking, and arterial hypertension yielded AUCs below the 0.634 threshold, indicating inadequate discriminatory performance as single predictors. At the 90% power threshold (AUC = 0.657), sex likewise failed to meet the required discriminatory criterion.

ROC curve comparison for PRS combined with various phenotypic features.

Assessment of sex-specific effect allele frequencies. No significant differences in EAF were observed between sexes, as demonstrated by both the paired Student's t-test (p = 0.588) and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (p = 0.821). Male and female EAF estimates showed very high concordance (Pearson r = 0.978, p = 8.01 × 10−136; Spearman ρ = 0.977, p = 5.69 × 10−133). Across all variants, the mean absolute difference in EAF between males and females was 0.047, with a maximum observed difference of 0.199. These findings indicate that the unequal sex distribution in the study cohort was not associated with systematic differences in PRS variant effect allele frequencies (Suppl. Table 1).

Association between PRS and CAD. PRS was significantly associated with CAD in all three logistic regression models (all p < 0.001; (Suppl. Table 5)). Adjustment for sex slightly improved model performance, increasing the estimated PRS effect, Nagelkerke's pseudo-R2 (0.282 to 0.398), and cross-validated AUC (0.772 to 0.818). Additional adjustment for age resulted in minimal changes to model performance. All models showed good calibration, with Brier scores ranging from 0.161 to 0.174 (Suppl. Table 5).

Classification power of PS-PRSs. The distribution of PRSs was significantly greater in patients with early-onset CAD than in controls in the angiogenesis/tissue repair pathway (p = 0.00038), inflammation pathway (p = 0.042), vascular remodelling pathway (p = 0.0116) and pathway of genes with unknown function in atherosclerosis (p = 0.0035) (Fig. 3). No significant differences were detected in PRS in either group in pathways associated with lipid metabolism, thrombosis, transcriptional regulation, blood pressure NO signalling, developmental processes, autophagy, cell cycle proliferation, cell motility, cellular stress resistance, energy homeostasis, glucose/insulin, heart function, neuronal activity, RNA nucleoside processing, signal transduction, or vesicle transport. The discriminatory capacity of the four PS-PRSs, which differed significantly between early-onset CAD patients and controls, was inferior to that of the overall PRS (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The objective of this study was to assess the applicability of GWAS-derived scores within the Latvian patient population, utilizing data from the CARDIoGRAMplusC4D consortium, which encompasses a substantial European-ancestry cohort and serves as the most relevant reference given the limited sample size of our own dataset. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate such a score in a Latvian population. Our secondary focus was to evaluate the significant association between the PRS and pathway-specific PRS and CAD and to compare its predictive performance with those of conventional risk factor models. The primary findings of our study indicate that the results based on this extensive dataset were informative and that this dataset could be applicable to our patient population.

In contrast to the findings of previous studies, our findings demonstrate that individual conventional risk factors, such as elevated total cholesterol and LDL cholesterol, serve as better predictors of CAD than PRS individually. This discrepancy may be attributed to the possibility that the PRS employed in our study does not encompass the full spectrum of genetic variations involved in CAD risk. Inouye et al. developed a genomic risk score (metaGRS) by integrating three previously published genetic risk scores (GRS46K, CARDIoGRAMplusC4D, and 1000 Genomes). In Cox regression analyses of incident CAD, models based on the metaGRS demonstrated higher C-indices than any of the individual conventional risk factors (smoking, diabetes, family history of CAD, BMI, hypertension and high cholesterol), with self-reported high cholesterol at assessment ranking as the second most predictive factor. Furthermore, a model incorporating all six conventional risk factors showed only marginally improved performance over the metaGRS alone [27].

Our results support previous findings indicating that the predictive strength of the PRS is maximized when it is integrated with traditional risk factors. For instance, Elliott et al. conducted an observational study involving 352,660 individuals without a prior history of CAD at baseline. They found that the C statistic for PRS alone was 0.61 (95% CI, 0.60–0.62), whereas the pooled cohort equation model achieved a C statistic of 0.76 (95% CI, 0.75–0.77). When the two approaches were combined, there was a statistically significant improvement, with the C statistic increasing to 0.78 (95% CI, 0.77–0.79) [14]. A study by Inouye et al. also revealed that combining the metaGRS with all six conventional risk factors led to a model with a C-index of 0.696 (95% CI: 0.688 to 0.703), an increase of 2.6% over the model consisting of the six conventional risk factors [28]. Furthermore, evidence suggests that the integration of PRS with conventional risk factors can improve the discriminatory accuracy of coronary atherosclerosis prediction models among young adults [15]. These findings highlight the potential utility of combining PRS with traditional risk factor-based risk assessment tools in young patients, for whom traditional risk factor assessment may not accurately capture their true risk of developing CAD. This approach is further justified by the increasing cost-effectiveness of PRS testing. For instance, Mujwara et al. conducted a study on a cohort of 40-year-old individuals with a borderline or intermediate 10-year risk (5% to <20%) for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. They found that compared with the use of PCE alone, incorporating CAD-PRS into the pooled cohort equation (PCE) not only reduced the average cost per individual but also improved quality-adjusted life-years and prevented future occurrences of CAD and ischaemic stroke [29].

CAD PRS operates via multiple biological pathways, including lipid metabolism, inflammation, vascular remodelling, and NO signalling [11]. PS-PRS is emerging as a powerful tool for predicting the risk of CAD through the aggregation of genetic variants across specific biological pathways. PS-PRS can provide more detailed insights into how genetic variations in these pathways influence CAD risk. Our findings indicated that patients with CAD exhibited elevated PRS distributions in four pathways: angiogenesis/tissue repair, inflammation, vascular remodelling, and genes with unknown function in atherosclerosis. However, the discriminative abilities of these PS-PRSs were lower than that of the overall PRS, possibly because of the underlying genetic complexity and the intricate gene–gene interactions contributing to the pathogenesis of CAD. Contrary to our findings, a study by Goodman et al. demonstrated the significance of PS-PRSs. The study investigated patients with obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA), CAD, or both; the PS-PRSs for CAD were calculated, and their associations with the HIF-1, VEGF, NFκB, and TNF signalling pathways were analyzed. The results indicated that higher PS-PRSs for the VEGF pathway (comprising 39 genes) were associated with a protective effect against CAD in patients with OSA—specifically, among those with a VEGF risk score one standard deviation above the mean, the odds of CAD were reduced by approximately 14% compared with individuals with an average risk score [30]. Another study by Liou et al. demonstrated that among certain CAD subtypes, PS-PRSs have the highest predictive accuracy. Patients were categorized into subgroups based on high versus normal LDL levels, high versus normal lipoprotein A (Lpa) levels, ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) versus non-STEMI, occlusive versus nonocclusive CAD, and stable versus unstable CAD. They observed that for the Lpa subgroup, compared with genome-wide PRSs, PS-PRSs had superior discriminatory power (AUC, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.80–0.86) (AUC, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.69–0.77), with a significant improvement over models based solely on traditional risk factors. For the other subtypes, differences in predictive performance among the measured risk factor-only models, risk factor plus genome-wide PRS models, and risk factor plus pathway PRS models were less pronounced, although a similar increasing trend was noted in the LDL subgroup. In the remaining models, the addition of PS-PRS did not substantially increase the predictive accuracy compared with the use of PRS alone [31].

This study has several limitations worth noting. The relatively small sample size in each group may have limited the final results. We selected pathways based on literature-supported prior hypotheses. The small sample size of female patients limited our ability to analyze sex differences. Because our cohort comprised individuals with prevalent coronary artery disease and lacked pre-diagnostic risk-factor data, calculation of conventional clinical risk scores and their comparison with PRS was not feasible. As this study employed a cross-sectional case–control design, longitudinal follow-up data were not available for additional analyses. A substantial proportion of patients in both groups were receiving lipid-lowering therapy, which may have influenced lipid measurements and their discriminative performance. Because the study used a case–control design with an overrepresentation of CAD cases, the predicted probabilities do not reflect absolute population risk. Therefore, the study data should be interpreted with caution, and further validation using large-scale studies is needed.

Conclusion

This study revealed that GWAS-derived scores are applicable to the Latvian patient population. PRS improved the classification accuracy of the conventional risk factors for early CAD. Compared with the overall PRS, the pathway-specific PRS had a lower discriminative ability.
