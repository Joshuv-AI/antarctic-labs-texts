1.
Introduction

The Legendre’s conjecture, which states that there is always a prime number between consecutive squares, is one of Landau’s problems on prime numbers. Clearly this means that there is always a prime number in the interval
[x,x+x^{\frac{1}{2}}]
. However, we cannot prove it even on the Riemann Hypothesis. Assuming RH, one can only show that there is always a prime number in the interval
[x,x+x^{\frac{1}{2}}\log x]
. The best unconditional result is due to the author
[
16
]
, where he showed the interval
[x,x+x^{0.52}]
contains primes. Under a special condition concerning the existence of Siegel zeros, he
[
12
]
also showed that the interval
[x,x+x^{0.4923}]
contains primes for long ranges of
x
.

Instead of relaxing the length of the short interval, one can attack this conjecture by relaxing our restriction of primes. A number with a large prime factor is a good approximation of prime numbers. Thus, we can try to find numbers with a large prime factor in the interval
[x,x+x^{\frac{1}{2}}]
or
[x,x+x^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}]
. In
[
14
]
and
[
13
]
we considered the former and latter intervals respectively, and here we shall return to the former one again.

In this direction, Jutila
[
11
]
showed in 1973 that this interval contains a number with a prime factor larger than
x^{\frac{2}{3}-\varepsilon}
. The exponent
\frac{2}{3}
has been improved to

0.73,\ 0.7338,\ 0.772,\ 0.82,\ \frac{11}{12},\ \frac{17}{18},\ \frac{19}{20},\ \frac{24}{25},\ \frac{25}{26}\text{ and }\frac{25.5}{26.5}

by Balog
[
2
]

[

and the author
[

respectively. In his monograph, Harman [
[

Theorem 1
.

For sufficiently large
x
, the interval
[x,x+x^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}]
contains an integer with a prime factor larger than
x^{\frac{35}{36}-\varepsilon}
.

Throughout this paper, we always suppose that
\varepsilon
is a sufficiently small positive constant and
B=B(\varepsilon)
is a sufficiently large positive constant. We choose
\varepsilon
such that
K=\frac{8}{\varepsilon}(\frac{1}{36}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2})
is an integer. The letters
p
and
\beta
, with or without subscript, are reserved for primes and almost-primes respectively. Let
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
denote positive constants which may have different values at different places, and we write
m\sim M
to mean that
c_{1}M<m\leqslant c_{2}M
. Let
b=1+\frac{1}{\log x}
,
v=x^{\frac{35}{36}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}
and
P=x^{\frac{\varepsilon}{8}}
. We use
M(s)
,
N(s)
and some other capital letters (except
L
and
P
) to denote
1
-bounded Dirichlet polynomials

M(s)=\sum_{m\sim M}a_{m}m^{-s},\quad N(s)=\sum_{n\sim N}b_{n}n^{-s}.

We use
L(s)
to denote a “zeta-factor”

L(s)=\sum_{l\sim L}l^{-s}.

We also use
P(s)
to denote

P(s)=\sum_{P<p\leqslant 2P}p^{-s}.

Lemma 2.4

Assume that Condition (1) holds. The proof of this case is similar to that of [

Assume that Condition (2) holds. Using Hölder’s inequality and the mean value theorem for Dirichlet polynomials, we have

\displaystyle\int_{T}^{2T}\left|M\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)N\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)H\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)L\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)P^{K}\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|dt

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\ \left(\int_{T}^{2T}\left|M\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{2}\left|P^{K}\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{2}dt\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{T}^{2T}\left|N\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{4}dt\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}

\displaystyle\ \left(\int_{T}^{2T}\left|L\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{4}\left|H\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{4}dt\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\ x^{\frac{1}{4}}\left(\int_{T}^{2T}\left|N\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{4}dt\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\left(\int_{T}^{2T}\left|L\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{4}\left|H\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{4}dt\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}.

\left(\int_{T}^{2T}\left|N\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{4}dt\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\ll\left(N^{2}+T\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}.

Note that

\displaystyle\int_{T}^{2T}\left|M\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)N\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)H\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)L\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)P^{K}\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|dt

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\ \left(\int_{T}^{2T}\left|M\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{2}\left|P^{K}\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{2}dt\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{T}^{2T}\left|N\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{4}\left|H\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)\right|^{2}dt\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}

3.
The final decomposition

Let
\mathcal{C}
denote a finite set of positive integers,
p_{j}\sim v^{t_{j}}
and put

N(d)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x<pp_{1}\ldots p_{K}\leqslant x+x^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}\\
P<p_{i}\leqslant 2P\end{subarray}}1,\quad\mathcal{A}=\{n:2^{-K}v<n\leqslant 2v,\ n\text{ repeats }N(n)\text{ times}\},

\mathcal{B}=\{n:v<n\leqslant 2v\},\quad\mathcal{C}_{d}=\{a:ad\in\mathcal{C}\},\quad P(z)=\prod_{p<z}p,\quad S(\mathcal{C},z)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathcal{C}\\
(a,P(z))=1\end{subarray}}1.

Then we only need to show that
S\left(\mathcal{A},(2v)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)>0
.

Buchstab’s identity
is the equation

S\left(\mathcal{C},z\right)=S\left(\mathcal{C},w\right)-\sum_{w\leqslant p<z}S\left(\mathcal{C}_{p},p\right),

where
2\leqslant w<z
.

Our aim is to show that the sparser set
\mathcal{A}
contains the expected proportion of primes compared to the bigger set
\mathcal{B}
, which requires us to decompose
S\left(\mathcal{A},(2v)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
and prove asymptotic formulas of the form

S\left(\mathcal{A},z\right)=v^{-1}x^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}\left(\sum_{P<p\leqslant 2P}\frac{1}{p}\right)^{K}(1+o(1))S\left(\mathcal{B},z\right)

(1)

for some parts of it, and drop the other positive parts.

Let
\omega(u)
denote the Buchstab function determined by the following differential-difference equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\omega(u)=\frac{1}{u},&\quad 1\leqslant u\leqslant 2,\\
(u\omega(u))^{\prime}=\omega(u-1),&\quad u\geqslant 2.\end{cases}

Moreover, we have the upper and lower bounds for
\omega(u)
:

\displaystyle\omega(u)\geqslant\omega_{0}(u)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{u},&\quad 1\leqslant u<2,\\
\frac{1+\log(u-1)}{u},&\quad 2\leqslant u<3,\\
\frac{1+\log(u-1)}{u}+\frac{1}{u}\int_{2}^{u-1}\frac{\log(t-1)}{t}dt\geqslant 0.5607,&\quad 3\leqslant u<4,\\
0.5612,&\quad u\geqslant 4,\\
\end{cases}

\displaystyle\omega(u)\leqslant\omega_{1}(u)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{u},&\quad 1\leqslant u<2,\\
\frac{1+\log(u-1)}{u},&\quad 2\leqslant u<3,\\
\frac{1+\log(u-1)}{u}+\frac{1}{u}\int_{2}^{u-1}\frac{\log(t-1)}{t}dt\leqslant 0.5644,&\quad 3\leqslant u<4,\\
0.5617,&\quad u\geqslant 4.\\
\end{cases}

We shall use
\omega_{0}(u)
and
\omega_{1}(u)
to give numerical bounds for some sieve functions discussed below. We shall also use the simple upper bound
\omega(u)\leqslant\max(\frac{1}{u},0.5672)
(see Lemma 8(iii) of
[
10
]
) to estimate high-dimensional integrals.

By Prime Number Theorem with Vinogradov’s error term and the inductive arguments in [
[
5
]
, Chapter A.2], we know that, for sufficiently large
z
,

S\left(\mathcal{B},z\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathcal{B}\\
(a,P(z))=1\end{subarray}}1=(1+o(1))\frac{v}{\log z}\omega\left(\frac{\log v}{\log z}\right),

(2)

and we expect that the similar relation also holds for
S\left(\mathcal{A},z\right)
:

S\left(\mathcal{A},z\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in\mathcal{A}\\
(a,P(z))=1\end{subarray}}1=(1+o(1))\left(\sum_{P<p\leqslant 2P}\frac{1}{p}\right)^{K}\frac{x^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}}{\log z}\omega\left(\frac{\log v}{\log z}\right).

(3)

If (1) holds for
S\left(\mathcal{A},z\right)
, then we can deduce (3) easily from (1) and (2). Otherwise we must drop this
S\left(\mathcal{A},z\right)
. We define the
loss
from this term by the size of corresponding
S\left(\mathcal{B},z\right)
:

S\left(\mathcal{B},z\right)=(\textit{loss}+o(1))\frac{v}{\log x}.

(4)

We note that for the lower bound problem, we can only drop positive parts and the total loss of the dropped parts must be less than
1
.

Before decomposing, we define the asymptotic regions
\mathcal{T}_{0}
–
\mathcal{T}_{2}
,
\mathcal{I}_{n}

\displaystyle\ \left\{m_{1}\leqslant\frac{18}{35},\ m_{2}\leqslant\frac{9}{35}\right\},
