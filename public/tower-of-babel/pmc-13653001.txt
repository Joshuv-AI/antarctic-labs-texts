Proton-density fat-saturated non-contrast MR imaging is comparable to post-contrast MR imaging for revealing wrist synovitis
Authors: Su Wu, James Francis Griffith, João Dourado, Lai Shan Tam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesStandard MR imaging (MRI) for rheumatoid arthritis (RA) patients comprises a post-contrast T1-fat-saturated (T1FS + C) sequence to detect wrist synovitis and tenosynovitis. This study investigates whether non-contrast sequences can provide similar information to post-contrast imaging.Materials and methodsSeventy-seven RA patients (F/M: 64/13; age: 61 ± 12 years) underwent MRI examination of the most symptomatic wrist on a 3.0-T system. Axial T1FS + C as well as proton-density imaging with fat-saturation (PDFS) and T2-weighted imaging with fat-saturation (T2FS) sequences were performed. Two radiologists independently scored synovitis and tenosynovitis severity on all sequences, using the rheumatoid arthritis magnetic resonance imaging scoring system (RAMRIS). Intra- and interclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were assessed.ResultsFor synovitis severity scoring, PDFS was practically identical to T1FS + C, while T2FS overestimated synovitis severity by up to 19%. For synovitis severity, PDFS imaging correlated better with T1FS + C than T2FS imaging (ICC: Reader 1: 0.931 vs 0.897; Reader 2: 0.969 vs 0.948). For tenosynovitis severity, PDFS and T2FS both yielded higher tenosynovitis scores than T1FS + C (p < 0.001). Compared to T1FS + C imaging, PDFS overestimated tenosynovitis severity by up to 39%, while T2FS overestimated tenosynovitis severity by up to 54%. For tenosynovitis severity, PDFS imaging correlated better with T1FS + C than T2FS imaging (ICC: Reader 1: 0.856 vs 0.731; Reader 2: 0.808 vs 0.800).ConclusionPDFS imaging had good (for tenosynovitis) to excellent (for synovitis) intra- and inter-reader reliability and provides comparable scoring to T1FS + C imaging when evaluating synovitis severity, though it tends to overestimate tenosynovitis severity. PDFS imaging can reasonably replace contrast-enhanced imaging for assessing wrist synovitis in RA patients.Key PointsQuestion Post-contrast imaging is recommended for evaluating synovitis and tenosynovitis in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) patients, though it involves higher cost, scan time, and discomfort.Findings Proton-density imaging with fat-saturation (PDFS) imaging had good to excellent intra- and inter-reader reliability for evaluating synovitis and tenosynovitis in RA wrists. Compared to post-contrast T1-fat-saturated (T1FS + C) imaging, PDFS imaging provides comparable semi-quantitative synovitis severity grading but tends to overestimate tenosynovitis severity.Critical relevance statement Post-contrast imaging for synovitis and tenosynovitis in RA patients involves discomfort, a longer time, and cost. PDFS imaging has good reliability and compares well with post-contrast imaging. Introduction

In rheumatoid arthritis (RA) patients, MR imaging (MRI) exquisitely reveals all clinically relevant markers of bone and soft tissue inflammation, namely bone marrow edema, synovitis, and tenosynovitis [1, 2]. The severity of ongoing inflammation is the most important parameter to consider when determining the need for treatment or treatment-change in RA patients [3]. The severity of synovitis present is more relevant than the presence of synovitis, as most RA patients will have some degree of residual synovitis even when in clinical remission [4–7]. Ultrasound is very helpful at detecting and grading the severity of synovitis and tenosynovitis in RA patients [8, 9]. Ultrasound, however, cannot quantify the degree of synovitis as efficiently as MRI, is not as sensitive at detecting erosions or joint space narrowing, and cannot identify bone marrow edema [8, 10].

A standard MR scan protocol for RA patients includes T1-weighted fat-saturated imaging after contrast to detect and quantify synovitis and tenosynovitis [4–7]. Synovitis seen on MRI correlates well with synovitis seen histologically [11]. The Rheumatoid Arthritis Magnetic Resonance Imaging Score (RAMRIS) system is a widely applied method for semi-quantifying synovitis, tenosynovitis, and bone marrow edema in RA patients [7, 12]. For evaluating bone marrow edema (‘osteitis’) and structural damage (bone erosion, joint space narrowing), non-contrast imaging is preferred [12–14].

Administering intravenous gadolinium contrast has several disadvantages. It requires venous cannulation, which is time-consuming, requires expertise, and is uncomfortable for patients. There is also a low risk of adverse effects to contrast agent such as acute kidney injury, nephrogenic systemic fibrosis, as well as mounting patient and medical concerns related to gadolinium retention in brain tissues, prompting prudence, whenever possible, on the use of gadolinium contrast [15]. Gadolinium is also costly, and post-contrast imaging prolongs examination time. That said, gadolinium administration is undoubtedly helpful at depicting tissue perfusion and is the current standard for identifying synovial inflammation. Nevertheless, it is feasible that non-contrast sequences could provide similar information without the need for gadolinium injection.

This study was designed to investigate whether other sequences could also accurately quantify soft tissue inflammation in the wrists of RA patients. Two frequently used sequences, namely axial T2WI fat-saturated (T2FS) and axial PDWI fat-saturated (PDFS), were added to the routine MRI wrist protocol for RA patient assessment. Axial T1WI fat-saturated post-contrast images (T1FS + C) were used as the reference standard.

Materials and methods

Patients

RA patients were prospectively recruited at the Rheumatology Clinic from 2022 to 2024. All patients fulfilled the 2010 American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European League Against Rheumatism classification (EULAR) criteria and were regularly followed up clinically. MRI examination was performed within 4 weeks of clinical assessment. Patients who refused MRI examination or had a contraindication to MRI were excluded. The study protocol was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Joint Chinese University of Hong Kong (CUHK) and the New Territories East Cluster (NTEC) (Ref. No: 2021.398), with signed informed consent obtained from all participants.

Clinical assessment

Clinical assessments were performed at the Rheumatology Clinic and included the Disease Activity Score 28 (DAS28) and Simple Disease Activity Index (SDAI) to assess disease activity, as well as Health Assessment Questionnaire–Disability Index (HAQ-DI), to assess functional status. Serology (ESR, CRP) was also performed.

MRI protocol

MRI examination of the most clinically affected wrist was performed, in the prone position, on a 3.0-T system (Philips Ingenia, Best, Netherlands). Scan plane extended from the distal radio-ulnar to the carpometacarpal joints, using a dedicated wrist coil. MRI analysis

All MRI measurements were made at a personal computer using dedicated DICOM image assessment software (RadiAnt). Synovitis and tenosynovitis were scored using the RAMRIS system [12]. Two radiologists (with 3 and 4 years of MRI reporting experience, respectively, much of which focused on assessment of inflammatory and structural wrist MRI changes) independently compared synovitis and tenosynovitis severity on the following three sequences: T2FS, PDFS, and T1FS + C. The more experienced (4 years) reader was designated as the main reader. Both readers were trained by a senior musculoskeletal radiologist with more than 30 years of MRI reporting experience. Both readers independently scored each sequence group. Only after all scoring was completed were the results compared and analyzed. Both readers repeated the same scoring 2 weeks after the initial assessment, blinded to the earlier assessments. The scoring order was randomized during assessment.

Synovitis grading

Synovitis was evaluated on axial images. The wrist joint was divided into three areas, namely the radio-ulnar joint, the radio-carpal joints, and the intercarpal and carpometacarpal joints. Synovitis was graded from 0 to 3. Grade 0: no synovitis. Grade 1: mild synovitis, i.e., less than 1/3 of the expected maximum synovial volume or thickness. Grade 2: moderate synovitis, i.e., 1/3 to 2/3 of the expected maximum synovial volume or thickness. Grade 3: severe synovitis, i.e., close to or at the expected maximum synovial volume or thickness.

Tenosynovitis grading

Tenosynovitis was evaluated on axial images at nine areas: the six extensor tendons and three flexor tendon compartments. Each tendon compartment was scored separately from 0 to 3. Grade 0: no tendon sheath effusion or post-contrast enhancement. Grade 1: < 1.5 mm tendon sheath effusion ± post-contrast enhancement. Grade 2: ≥ 1.5 and < 3.0 mm tendon sheath effusion ± post-contrast enhancement. Grade 3: ≥ 3.0 mm tendon sheath effusion ± post-contrast enhancement.

Statistical analysis

SPSS software (SPSS for Microsoft Windows, version 24.0, IBM) was used. Quantitative variables were expressed as means, standard deviations (SD), and ranges. Qualitative variables were expressed as raw numbers, proportions, and percentages. Inter-sequence agreement, inter-observer and intra-observer agreement were assessed using two-way mixed effects, consistency, single rater/measurement intraclass Pearson correlation coefficients (ICC) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). The strength of agreement was interpreted as follows: ≤ 0, poor; 0.01–0.20, slight; 0.21–0.40, fair; 0.41–0.60, moderate; 0.61–0.80, substantial; and ≥ 0.81, excellent. Bland–Altman plots were constructed for the inter-sequence comparison from which the mean bias (indicating a potential overestimation from one reader compared to another one), the regression line of paired differences (indicating a potential proportionality between differences and the magnitude of measurements), and the limits of agreement (defined as mean bias 1.96 SD on paired differences) were derived.

Discussion

MRI identifies inflammatory changes and structural damage within and around joints [4, 16]. MRI can be utilized to help diagnose inflammatory arthropathy, quantify the type and level of inflammatory activity and structural damage, and determine response to treatment [16]. The degree of structural damage evident on MRI at presentation is a better predictor of long-term structural damage progression than clinical, radiographic, or serological features [17]. The main limitations to more frequent usage of MRI are its cost and limited availability, which are partially related to long scan times, exacerbated by the perceived need for intravenous contrast administration [18]. The main purpose of contrast administration in RA patients is to identify enhancing (i.e., hyperperfused) synovial or tenosynovial tissue [16]. In this study, we investigated whether two non-contrast sequences, namely T2FS and PDFS, could accurately quantify the level of synovial or tenosynovial inflammation, using T1FS + C imaging as the comparative standard.

This study showed that PDFS sequences performed better than T2FS sequences for grading both synovitis and tenosynovitis severity. For grading synovitis severity, PDFS imaging had RAMRIS severity scores almost identical to T1FS + C imaging, as well as excellent agreement with T1FS + C imaging and excellent intra- and interclass correlation (Fig. 3). T2FS imaging tended to overestimate synovitis and had poorer intra- and interclass correlation for both readers than PDFS imaging (Fig. 4). The most likely reason for better quantification of synovitis severity with PDFS rather than T2FS imaging is that PDFS imaging reflects differences in proton content between tissues and thus provides better tissue contrast and visualization of non-fatty soft tissues compared to T2FS imaging, which is primarily geared toward being fluid sensitive [19, 20]. This leads to synovitis being more conspicuous on PDFS than T2FS imaging (Fig. 4). Also, the T1FS + C sequence utilized had a relatively long TR to mitigate T1-weighting of native tissues and thereby maximized gadolinium-induced T1-shortening as the primary source of contrast. For grading tenosynovitis, PDFS and T2FS imaging consistently yielded higher tenosynovitis severity scores compared to T1FS + C imaging. This higher tenosynovitis RAMRIS scoring with non-contrast PDFS and T2FS imaging is most likely due to tendon sheath effusions resulting in hyperintense tendon sheath distension on PDFS and T2FS imaging, which does not usually enhance avidly with contrast. This leads to increased conspicuity of tenosynovitis on non-contrast, rather than post-contrast sequences. T1FS + C imaging highlights enhancing tenosynovium but does not highlight the tendon sheath effusion, which is seen as a non-enhancing isointense rim around tendons (Fig. 5). As tendon sheath effusion is a marker of tenosynovitis, it could be argued that T1FS + C imaging possibly underestimates tenosynovitis severity while both PDFS and T2FS imaging provide a truer measure of tenosynovitis. That said, RAMRIS scoring is based on T1FS + C imaging, and this was used as the assessment method in this study. The RAMRIS scoring system is a semi-quantitative, observer-dependent tool developed and validated for use in clinical trials, but not necessarily applicable in daily practice. For clinicians interpreting RAMRIS scores, our data suggest a need for cautious interpretation as PDFS/T2FS will yield a higher tenosynovitis severity score than T1FS + C imaging. This does not invalidate the utility of non-contrast MRI for monitoring wrist tenosynovitis in RA patients, but it does imply that the threshold for defining inflammatory activity or change should be considered sequence-specific. PDFS also had a higher correlation than T2FS imaging compared to T1FS + C imaging for tenosynovitis scoring, though the correlation was not as high as that seen with synovitis. PDFS scan time (4 min) was longer than T1FS + C (2 min 52 s), but post-contrast imaging also involves preliminary plain sequence scanning, contrast agent injection, and additional observation regarding the possibility of allergic reaction. Several previous studies have addressed the possibility of using various non-contrast sequences as an alternative to T1FS + C imaging to quantify wrist synovitis and tenosynovitis severity in RA patients [18–23]. No previous studies have assessed the value of PDFS imaging in this regard. As found in our study, T2FS imaging was found to correlate well with T1FS + C imaging for assessing synovitis [21, 22] with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.75 to 0.82 [22] and with good reader reliability [21, 22]. Also, as shown in previous studies, tenosynovitis tended to have higher RAMRIS scores on T2FS imaging than T1FS + C imaging, as tendon sheath effusions are more conspicuous on fluid-sensitive sequences [18, 21, 22]. The Dixon technique was found to correlate poorly (r = 0.36) with T1FS + C imaging for quantifying synovitis, though it had a modest (r = 0.64) correlation for revealing tenosynovitis [20, 22]. In contrast, excellent correlation was found between double inversion recovery (DIR) imaging, which enables both high contrast and high spatial resolution, and T1FS + C imaging for assessing both the presence and severity of wrist synovitis [23]. This DIR-based study, however, only employed coronal plane imaging, which is sub-optimal to axial imaging for quantifying wrist synovitis and tenosynovitis [22, 23].

The main limitation of our study is that the assessment was confined to three MRI sequences only, and other sequences, such as DIR imaging, Dixon, or 3D Dixon sequences, were not included. Second, this study was conducted exclusively on the wrist joint, which limits the applicability of findings to other commonly involved joints, such as the metacarpophalangeal (MCP), metatarsophalangeal (MTP), or knee joints. In this study, only synovitis and tenosynovitis severity were compared between different sequences. Further study should be performed to find out if PDFS also showed good performance in other RA-related parameters and joints. Third, as only RA patients were studied, the value of non-contrast sequences in other arthritic conditions, such as psoriatic arthritis or osteoarthritis, was not assessed. Fourth, the limited MR experience of the two readers (at 3 and 4 years) may hinder the generalizability of results, though both readers had considerable experience in evaluating MRI wrist images and had excellent reliability for evaluating inflammatory severity changes. Finally, as most patients in the study had mild to moderate synovitis, the ability of T2FS and PDFS imaging for detecting subtle synovitis compared to post-contrast imaging was not fully addressed.

In conclusion, this study shows that PDFS imaging provides comparable semi-quantitative scoring to T1FS + C when evaluating wrist synovitis with good to excellent intra- and inter-reader reliability for quantifying both wrist synovitis and tenosynovitis severity in RA patients. Tenosynovitis severity tends to be overestimated on PDFS and T2FS imaging compared to T1FS + C imaging, due to increased conspicuity of tendon sheath effusions. PDFS imaging can be used as an alternative to contrast imaging in the assessment of wrist synovitis in RA patients.
