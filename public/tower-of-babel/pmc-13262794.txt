Do motives for and contexts of cannabis use interact to predict heavy cannabis use at the event‐level?
Authors: Christina Dyar, Elise Green, Christine M Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesMotives for and contexts of cannabis use (CU) are typically examined as independent characteristics of CU events in ecological momentary assessment (EMA) studies. However, theories posit that some combinations of motives and contexts (e.g., using to cope in a solitary setting) may contribute to heavier substance use than others (e.g., using to cope in a social setting). The current study aimed to test whether these combinations are associated with heavier substance use at the event‐level. We aimed to do so among a sample of young adult females given sex differences in cannabis risk processes, which oversampled sexual minority women and gender diverse individuals due to their high rates of CU.MethodsWe utilized EMA data from a study of young adult females who regularly used cannabis (n = 571; 77.6% sexual minority women and gender diverse individuals).ResultsCoping motives and solitary settings interacted such that coping motives for CU were associated with longer and higher subjective intoxication but only in solitary settings. Social and enhancement motives did not interact with social contexts; however, social and enhancement motives, as well as social contexts, were associated with longer intoxication and higher subjective intoxication.Discussion and ConclusionsFindings indicated that coping motives were only associated with CU when combined with solitary use. Social and enhancement motives and social contexts produced additive effects.Scientific SignificanceFindings indicate that studies examining the effects of coping motives or solitary cannabis use in isolation may not accurately characterize these effects.

INTRODUCTION

Studies have demonstrated event‐level associations between motives and contexts of cannabis use (CU) and CU outcomes (e.g., consequences). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 While motives and contexts are typically treated as independent, researchers have posited that motives and contexts interact synergistically, leading to heavier use when enhancement‐motivated use occurs in social settings and coping‐motivated use in solitary settings. 3 , 6 However, we are aware of only one study that has examined motive‐by‐context interactions at the event‐level, producing mixed results. 3 The current study aimed to examine motive by context interactions at the event‐level among a sample of young adult females, which oversampled sexual and gender minority individuals, as these groups have experienced recent increases in CU disorder. 7 , 8

Event‐Level associations between motives, contexts, and CU outcomes

Ecological momentary assessment studies have linked coping, social, and enhancement motives with more sessions, longer intoxication, higher subjective intoxication, and larger quantity of cannabis consumed. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 However, results vary across studies. While the majority of studies find support for these associations, a handful have not. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Results are also mixed for associations between using cannabis in social compared to solitary settings. Studies have linked solitary use with fewer CU sessions 3 and lower subjective intoxication compared to social use, 3 , 5 but another found no differences. 9 In sum, three common motives have been linked with CU outcomes at the event‐level, and using cannabis in social settings has been linked to CU outcomes, but findings are inconsistent across studies.

Motive by context interactions

While motivational and contextual factors are treated as distinct predictors of substance use, researchers have proposed that contexts and motives interact to contribute to more consumption and consequences. 3 , 6 They have posited that enhancement/socially‐motivated use would be associated with more consumption and consequences in social than in solitary settings. 3 , 6 They have also posited that coping‐motivated use would be associated with more consumption and consequences in solitary than social settings. 3 , 6 Jackson et al. 3 described motive‐context combinations theorized to produce more consumption and consequences as “matching” but provide little explicit rationale. Using to enhance a social situation or to be more sociable explicitly acknowledges that participants are using with others or intend to be intoxicated in a social situation, suggesting that social motives and contexts interact to contribute to more consumption and consequences. Cooper et al. 10 posited that using to cope in solitary settings may be associated with more consumption and consequences because of the lack of feedback and context‐specific normative information about substance use from others to inhibit use. Potential reasons for expecting more consumption and consequences when enhancement motives are endorsed in social compared to solitary contexts are less clear.

We are only aware of a few studies that have examined differences in alcohol consumption based on both motives and contexts. O'Hara et al. 11 , 12 demonstrated that social and enhancement drinking motives were associated with more alcohol consumption in social contexts. However, associations between social and enhancement motives and number of drinks in solitary contexts were more mixed, with one study linking fewer drinks in solitary contexts with enhancement and social motives while another did not. 11 , 12 The pattern of associations between coping motives and the number of drinks in social and solitary settings was also mixed. 11 , 12 These findings are partially consistent with motive‐by‐context interactions proposed by Cooper et al. 10 and Jackson et al. 3 While these studies examined associations between motives and number of drinks in social and solitary contexts, these associations were not compared statistically and only examined alcohol. 11 , 12

While no studies have examined differences in CU using O'Hara et al.'s 11 , 12 approach, some studies have examined how social context and affect interact to predict CU. One study found that negative affect was elevated when cannabis was used in solitary compared to social settings. 13 However, among individuals with social anxiety, another study found that elevated anxious affect was associated with a higher likelihood of CU when others were using cannabis and a lower likelihood when others were not using cannabis. 14 These findings provide mixed evidence for the idea that CU is more likely when solitary contexts and coping motives are both present, but these studies did not directly examine motives or other cannabis outcomes.

Jackson et al. 3 addressed these limitations by examining interactions between social, enhancement, and coping motives and social context for both alcohol and cannabis. However, results were mixed, with most motive‐by‐context interactions being non‐significant. Two interactions between social motives and social contexts were significant. When only cannabis was used, social motives were more strongly associated with more sessions of CU when participants were alone compared to with others. This is the opposite of the pattern described by Cooper et al. 10 and Jackson et al. 3 The second significant moderation provided some support for their hypothesis, with social motives having a nonsignificant positive association with CU sessions when more intoxicated individuals were present and a nonsignificant negative association with CU sessions when fewer intoxicated people were present. In sum, Jackson et al. 3 provided relatively little support for the idea that motives and contexts interact to predict more sessions of CU or higher subjective intoxication.

While Jackson et al. 3 provides a foundation for research examining interactions between contexts and motives, it also has several limitations. Jackson et al. 3 did not examine CU consequences, and their findings are nuanced because occasions with simultaneous alcohol and cannabis use and cannabis only occasions were examined separately, making it unclear to what extent findings apply to outcomes across all observations with CU. The current study aimed to systematically examine interactions between motives and contexts of CU to determine how they were associated with CU outcomes at the event‐level.

Sex differences in motives and their effects

In recent years, females have experienced rapid increases in CU disorder. 7 , 8 Further, sex differences in motives for CU and associations between CU motives and problems have emerged. While findings vary, studies typically find higher coping motives 15 , 16 , 17 and lower social 16 or enhancement motives among females compared to males. 15 Coping motives are also more strongly associated with CU problems among females than males, 17 , 18 , 19 although some studies have not found differences. 16 , 20

Sexual minority women and gender diverse individuals have experienced even larger increases in CU disorder than heterosexual cisgender women. 8 This population experiences additional stressors as a result of stigmatization. 21 It has been theorized that these added stressors and more permissive norms about CU in the sexual and gender minority community may lead to differences in motive endorsement and the impact of motives on CU for sexual and gender minorities compared to heterosexual cisgender individuals. 22 This highlights the need for research on motives among sex‐specific samples and comparisons of results by sexual and gender minority status.

The current study

This study aimed to test CU motive‐context interactions. We examined event‐level interactions between three common CU motives (i.e., social, enhancement, coping) and a single contextual variable (i.e., solitary vs. social context) in predicting three CU outcomes (i.e., duration of intoxication, subjective intoxication, consequences). We focused on duration and subjective intoxication as these are measures of cannabis consumption that can be used across modes of CU, while analyses of quantity require separate analyses by product type (e.g., grams for plant material, mg THC for edibles). 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 We examined these effects among young adult females who used cannabis regularly, which oversampled sexual and gender minority individuals. We hypothesized that CU context would moderate effects of CU motives, resulting in the following patterns: (1) social and enhancement motives would be associated with longer intoxication, higher subjective intoxication, and more consequences when cannabis was used in social compared to solitary contexts; (2) coping motives for CU would be associated with longer intoxication, higher subjective intoxication, and more consequences when cannabis was used in solitary compared to social contexts. We also hypothesized that sensitivity analyses focused on the heterosexual female subsample would identify the same pattern of results as analyses of the full sample.

METHODS

Participants and procedures

The current analyses used data from a longitudinal study of CU among young adult females. Participants were recruited via online advertisements on social media (e.g., Facebook) in 2024. The study included a baseline assessment (Day 0) and a 14‐day EMA study (Days 1–14). During the EMA, participants completed one survey in the morning (8:00 am–1:00 pm in their time zone) and one in the evening (6:00 pm–12:00 am in their time zone). The study received IRB approval at Ohio State University.

Eligible participants were U.S. residents, aged 18–25; assigned female at birth; identified as (a) a sexual minority and a woman; (b) a sexual minority and gender diverse; or (c) heterosexual and a woman; and used cannabis at least four times in the past month at baseline. Purposive sampling was used to ensure diversity in sexual orientation, gender, and race/ethnicity. Participants were paid up to $140: $30 for baseline, $2.50 for each EMA survey, and a $10 bonus for each six surveys completed in a row. Participants who appeared eligible based on the screening survey were sent a text message by study team members to verify eligibility. Participants were asked to text demographic information (i.e., age, state of residence, email address), which was cross‐checked with their eligibility survey. Only participants who provided a valid non‐VOIP (voice over internet phone) number and correctly answered questions about their demographic information were enrolled.

The analytic sample included individuals who reported CU at least once during the EMA (n = 571). The sample was diverse in race/ethnicity, with 49.4% identifying with at least one marginalized racial/ethnic identity. Participants included 128 heterosexual cisgender women (22.4%), 291 sexual minority cisgender women (50.9%), and 152 (26.6%) gender diverse sexual minorities (Table 1).

Measures

EMA measures

EMA measures asked participants to consider the time since the last survey if they had completed the prior survey and since the beginning of the prior survey period if they had not (e.g., since 6 pm for morning surveys when prior evening survey was not completed). Participants were asked the following questions when they reported CU.

CU motives

Participants were asked whether they used cannabis to cope (3 items; e.g., “to reduce my anxiety,” “to cheer me up or forget my worries or problems,” “to relax”); to enhance positive emotions (1 item; “to feel good or have fun”); or to enhance social situations (1 item; “to be social or make a social gathering more fun”). Response options were no (0) and yes (1). Participants could endorse multiple motives. Items were adapted from Patrick et al. 27 Consistent with Dyar et al., 28 three coping motive items were used to create a binary variable (0 = endorsement of no coping motives, 1 = endorsement of at least one coping motive).

Solitary CU context

Participants were asked whom they used marijuana with. Options included: “I used marijuana alone,” “friend(s),” “romantic or sexual partner, spouse, or date,” “relative(s),” and “stranger(s),” and participants could select multiple responses. These responses were categorized into a binary variable with 0 indicating social use and 1 indicating solitary use. Approximately 10.1% of CU observations included both social and solitary use and were excluded from analyses to simplify interpretation. Results were similar when they were included and excluded.

CU outcomes

Duration of Intoxication was measured by asking “How many hours were you high?” Responses were provided in increments of 1 hour from 0 to 12+ hours.

Subjective Intoxication was measured by asking “How high did you get when you used marijuana?” on a scale of 0 (not at all high) to 4 (extremely high).

Cannabis Consequences were measured only during morning assessments by using eight items from two measures of marijuana consequences. 29 , 30 Participants were asked which “of the following things happened to you as a result of your marijuana use yesterday?” (e.g., “I felt dizzy or sick”). A sum of consequences was calculated. See Table 2 for all items.

Analytic plan

Analyses were conducted in Mplus 8.11. The analytic sample included 5321 completed surveys from 571 participants in which CU was reported. The median individual‐level completion rate was 89.3% (M = 76.7%, SD = 28.0%). Within completed surveys, <1% of data were missing and were handled using Bayesian methods. 31

Bayesian multilevel structural equation modeling (MSEM) 32 with non‐informative priors was used for multilevel analyses. We used Markov Chain Monte Carlo algorithms to generate a series of 10,000 random draws from the multivariate posterior distribution of our sample for each model. Trace plots and the Gelman–Rubin potential scaling reduction were used to determine whether convergence was achieved. 33

We examined within‐person (i.e., event‐level) and between‐person concurrent associations between a CU motive, social versus solitary context of use, and their interaction and a CU outcome (i.e., duration of intoxication, subjective intoxication, consequences). The interaction term was created by multiplying the motive and context variables, and this interaction term was included at both within and between person levels. In all models, we controlled for day of assessment and assessment type (weekend/weekday; morning/evening) at the within‐person level. Within‐person associations among motives, contexts, their interaction, and CU outcomes were allowed to vary across individuals. As consequences were only assessed once per day, day‐level aggregates of motives and contexts were utilized in analyses of consequences. All outcomes were treated as continuous, as Bayesian MSEM is currently unable to handle count distributions. Age, sexual identity, sex/gender identity, and race/ethnicity were included as covariates at the between‐person level. We also conducted sensitivity analyses in which we examined whether the same pattern of effects was present among the subsample of heterosexual cisgender women.

The most commonly endorsed motives were coping (78% of CU observations; 4150 observations), followed by enhancement (39%; 2075 observations) and social motives (13%; 692 observations). Similarly, coping motives were associated with becoming more intoxicated when in solitary settings (b = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.13, 0.29) but not in social settings (b = 0.01, 95% CI: −0.07, 0.08). No interactions involving social motives and solitary context were significant. However, endorsing social motives and social contexts were both associated with longer intoxication and higher subjective intoxication, indicating an additive effect of social motives and social contexts. Neither social motives nor contexts significantly predicted consequences.

A similar pattern was present for enhancement motives, with enhancement motives and solitary context not interacting to predict any CU outcomes. Endorsing enhancement motives and using in social contexts were both associated with longer and higher subjective intoxication, again indicating an additive effect. Enhancement motives, social motives, coping motives, contexts, or their interactions did not predict consequences.

Sensitivity analyses

Among the subsample of heterosexual women, results displayed the same pattern as the results from the full sample described above.

DISCUSSION

The current study aimed to advance our understanding of interactions between motives and contexts of CU among young adult females, including sexual and gender minorities. Analyses of coping motives and solitary use were consistent with interactions proposed by prior researchers, 3 , 10 with coping motives being associated with longer and higher intoxication only in solitary settings. This finding was not consistent with Jackson et al., 3 who did not find an interaction between coping motives and solitary context. One potential explanation may lie in sample differences. Jackson et al. 3 reported using cannabis on 14% of observations and coping motives on 15% of CU occasions and included males and females who used cannabis regularly. In comparison, the current sample reported CU on 42% of observations and coping motives on 78% of CU observations, included only females, and oversampled sexual and gender minorities. Findings were similar for heterosexual, cisgender women and sexual and gender minority individuals, indicating that if these effects are unique to the current sample, they are unique to females broadly. Given higher rates of coping motives, the current study may have found an interactive effect of coping motives and solitary context because participants used cannabis to cope more frequently in the current study than in Jackson et al. 3 While coping motives were endorsed frequently by the current sample, this is consistent with a broader pattern of higher rates of coping motives in samples that use cannabis more frequently. 3 , 34

Although social and enhancement motives did not interact with social context, there was an additive effect indicated by significant main effects of social and enhancement motives and social context. Specifically, longer and higher intoxication occurred when individuals used for social or enhancement reasons and when they used in social contexts compared to if they endorsed these motives in a solitary context. This is consistent with Jackson et al., 3 who also found main effects of social and enhancement motives and social contexts on frequency of CU and subjective intoxication. Together, these results provide support for an additive effect of using for social and enhancement reasons in social contexts but indicate that social/enhancement motives and context do not interact to predict CU outcomes.

Motives, contexts, and their interactions did not significantly predict consequences. One potential explanation may be that motive‐context combinations contribute to longer and higher intoxication in ways that do not lead to more consequences (e.g., also lead to protective behavioral strategy use). Alternatively, this may arise from the low prevalence of consequences in the current sample, with consequences reported on 32% of CU observations. This may have reduced our ability to detect associations with consequences.

CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS

Study findings highlight two motive‐context combinations that may contribute to longer and higher intoxication, and this may inform clinical treatment and interventions. Clinicians working with individuals who wish to reduce their use or engage in harm reduction may help their clients identify protective behavioral strategies that they can use when motive‐context combinations associated with longer and higher intoxication occur. Adaptive just‐in‐time interventions may also encourage the use of protective behavioral strategies in these contexts. Adaptive brief interventions may be ideal for helping people who want to reduce their CU or consequences 1 , 2 , 3 , 35 , 36 because of their high temporal precision and potential to be informed by individuals' current contexts and motives for use.

LIMITATIONS

The current study should be considered in light of its limitations. Given that the sample was comprised of 18–25‐year‐old females who used cannabis regularly and lived in the US, it is unclear whether findings will generalize to males, other age groups, lower frequencies of CU, or other countries. The sample also included an oversampling of sexual minority women and gender diverse individuals assigned female at birth, but findings were similar among heterosexual women, indicating that these results are not unique to sexual minority women and gender diverse individuals. However, given differences in coping motive results between Jackson et al. 3 and the current study, it is unclear whether the interactions between coping motives and solitary context identified in this study will generalize to males or if this finding may be unique to females.

Second, motives were assessed using a small set of binary items, rather than the more commonly used Likert response scales. While this reduced participant burden, this approach may have lost some nuance that may have been present if Likert scales or more items were used. Third, although we took several rigorous steps to check that participants were providing consistent information and were not participating multiple times, participants' identities were not validated via video call. As the duration of intoxication and subjective intoxication are self‐reported measures and CU can affect the perception of time, these measures may not be precise. Given low rates of consequences, these analyses may have been underpowered. Further, this study examined interactions between motives and social versus solitary contexts and did not examine multiple motives endorsement or use in both social and solitary contexts. Additionally, we did not ask when participants began a CU session, and thus, it is possible that the duration of intoxication may have been affected during observations when participants used cannabis for the first time during a survey window shortly before completing a survey. Most consequence items captured relatively minor events (as reflect the typical consequences experienced on a day‐to‐day basis) and not more severe consequences (which are rarer and thus less amenable to EMA designs).

CONCLUSIONS

Overall, results provided some support for the theory that certain motive‐context combinations produce more substance use and consequences among a sample of females who use cannabis regularly (including an oversampling of sexual and gender minority individuals). Consistent with this, coping motives were only associated with longer intoxication and higher subjective intoxication when used in solitary settings. While no significant interaction was detected between social or enhancement motives and social contexts, additive effects were present with using for social and enhancement reasons in social settings associated with longer intoxication and higher subjective intoxication than using for these reasons in solitary settings.
