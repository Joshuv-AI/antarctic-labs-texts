Recent Increase in Rotavirus Gastroenteritis After Five Years of Low Activity in Hokkaido Prefecture, Japan, Associated With Equine‐Like G3P[8] and G8P[8] Strains, 2025
Authors: Yuya Fukuda, Yoshiki Fujii, Atsuo Togashi, Tomohiro Nawa, Satoshi Nakamura, Ryoma Kimura, Jun Kunizaki, Yuuki Ando, Kouhei Nishino, Toshitaka Kizawa, Dai Yamamoto, Yoshiyuki Iizuka, Yuta Sasaoka, Masayoshi Kikuchi, Kousuke Nishikawa, Kazushige Nagai, Hirofumi Asakura, Shoya Shimokawa, Hiroyuki Kabutoya, Seiji Kobayashi, Takeshi Tsugawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRotavirus gastroenteritis decreased substantially worldwide during the COVID‐19 pandemic, and activity remained minimal in Hokkaido Prefecture, Japan, for 5 years after 2020. Hospital‐based surveillance in Hokkaido since July 2019 identified the first resurgence of rotavirus gastroenteritis in 2025. We analyzed temporal trends, age distribution, and viral genotypes among hospitalized children to characterize this resurgence. Over the full observation period (July 2019–Oct 2025), 113 hospitalizations were recorded, with 49 cases occurring between January and October 2025 after near‐absence from 2020 to 2024. Whole‐genome sequencing revealed dominance of equine‐like G3P[8] and G8P[8] strains closely related to contemporary international lineages and genetically distinct from strains previously detected locally. A marked shift in age distribution was evident: children aged 0–1 years accounted for 40.9% of cases before routine vaccination introduction (July 2019–Sep 2020), but none in 2025, whereas hospitalizations among children aged 7–12 years increased from 13.6% to 51.0%. These findings suggest that infants remained well protected by vaccination, but reduced circulation during the pandemic and waning immunity in older children enabled viral re‐emergence. Sustained genomic and epidemiological surveillance will be critical to monitor rotavirus evolution and inform vaccine impact in the post‐pandemic era.

Introduction

Group A rotaviruses (RVAs) are major causes of severe diarrhea in young children and many animal species worldwide and possess eleven segments of double‐stranded RNA encoding six structural and six nonstructural proteins [1]. Genotyping based on all 11 genome segments has shown that most human RVAs belong to either the Wa‐like (G1/3/4/9‐P[8]‐I1‐R1‐C1‐M1‐A1‐N1‐T1‐E1‐H1) or DS‐1‐like (G2‐P[4]‐I2‐R2‐C2‐M2‐A2‐N2‐T2‐E2‐H2) genotype constellations [2]. Following the global introduction of the Rotarix and RotaTeq vaccines in 2006, previously uncommon DS‐1‐like genotypes such as equine‐like G3P[8] and G8P[8] emerged worldwide [3, 4, 5].

In Japan, Rotarix and RotaTeq were introduced on a voluntary basis in 2011 and 2012, respectively. Vaccine coverage subsequently increased from approximately 30% in 2012% to 78.4% in 2019, accompanied by a marked decline in rotavirus‐associated hospitalizations [6]. After their inclusion in the national immunization program in October 2020, coverage further increased to 93.6% by 2023 [7].

Following the first reports of COVID‐19 in Wuhan, China, in December 2019, the pandemic spread globally, leading to widespread implementation of non‐pharmaceutical interventions such as mask wearing, alcohol‐based hand hygiene, and physical distancing. These measures resulted not only in reduced COVID‐19 transmission but also in substantial declines in many respiratory and gastrointestinal infections, including rotavirus gastroenteritis [8, 9]. In Hokkaido Prefecture, Japan, the closure of all elementary through high schools from February 28 to May 31, 2020 contributed to the absence of the usual March–June rotavirus epidemic [8]. Although norovirus re‐emerged with a peak in April 2021, rotavirus hospitalizations remained extremely low through June 2022 [10].

As COVID‐19 control measures were gradually relaxed, several countries reported a resurgence of rotavirus activity [11]. In Hokkaido, the stepwise easing of restrictions led to the re‐emergence of multiple respiratory infections between 2021 and 2024 [10, 12, 13], yet rotavirus gastroenteritis continued to remain historically low during this period.

In this study, we describe the first re‐emergence of rotavirus gastroenteritis in Hokkaido in 2025, 5 years after the last documented seasonal epidemic. Using whole‐genome sequencing of rotavirus strains from hospitalized patients combined with clinical data, we aimed to characterize the epidemiological and virological features of the 2025 epidemic.

Materials and Methods

Study Period and Participating Patients

This multicenter hospital‐based surveillance study included patients aged 0–15 years who were newly hospitalized with rotavirus gastroenteritis at 18 hospitals in Hokkaido Prefecture, Japan, between July 1, 2019, and October 31, 2025. Hospitalization was not restricted by dehydration status. Inclusion criteria included a positive rapid antigen detection test (i.e., IMMUNOCATCH™ Control Norovirus/Rotavirus/Adenovirus: Eiken Chemical Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan; RapidTesta Rota–Adeno II: Sekisui Medical Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan; Quick Chaser Rota–Adeno: Mizuho Medy Co. Ltd., Saga, Japan) or a positive multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) result (BioFire FilmArray Gastrointestinal Panel, bioMérieux Japan) for rotavirus. Information on which test was used for each patient was not collected. Patients were categorized into six age groups: 0 years, 1 year, 2 years, 3–6 years, 7–12 years, and 13–15 years.

Clinical Information of Hospitalized Patients in 2025

For patients hospitalized between January 1 and October 31, 2025, data on sex, age at admission, and rotavirus vaccination history were collected. Whether patients represented sporadic cases or outbreak‐associated cases was not determined. For patients from whom stool samples were available, clinical symptoms were additionally evaluated, including duration of fever, maximum body temperature, duration of diarrhea and vomiting, and the maximum number of diarrhea and vomiting episodes within 24 h.

Sample Collection and RNA Extraction

Stool specimens from hospitalized patients between January 1 and October 31, 2025 were used for molecular characterization. Viral RNA was extracted using the Direct‐zol RNA MiniPrep kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). Briefly, 240 μL of TRIzol LS Reagent was added to 80 μL of stool suspension, vortexed, incubated for 5 min at room temperature, followed by addition of 320 μL of ethanol. The mixture was loaded onto a spin column, centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 1 min, washed according to the manufacturer's protocol, and eluted with 40 μL of DNase/RNase‐free water.

Next‐Generation Sequencing (NGS)

NGS was performed as previously described [14]. Briefly, a 200‐bp fragment library was constructed for each sample using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina v1.2 (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Library purification was performed using Agencourt AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter, Pasadena, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. DNA concentrations were determined on a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer using the Qubit HS DNA Assay (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Paired‐end sequencing (2 × 151 bp) was performed on an iSeq. 100 Sequencing System (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using the iSeq. 100 i1 Reagent v2. Sequence data were analyzed using CLC Genomics Workbench Software v25.0.1 (CLC Bio, Aarhus, Denmark). The sequences of the RVAs used in this study were submitted to the DNA Data Bank of Japan (DDBJ) under accession numbers LC912034–LC912386.

Genotyping and Phylogenetic Analysis

The genotype of each of the 11 genome segments was determined with the Rotavirus A Genotype Determination online classification tool [15]. All 11 segments of each complete genome were separately aligned with MUSCLE. Phylogenetic trees were constructed with the maximum likelihood method and 1,000 bootstrap replicates with the MEGA 11 software. Representative reference sequences were selected for comparison from GenBank, available on the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) website. Nucleotide sequence identities were calculated using Clustal W online alignment tool [16].

Statistical Analysis

Age distributions were compared between two groups: cases collected from July 2019 to September 2020 (before incorporation of rotavirus vaccines into the national immunization program) and cases from January to October 2025 (the first epidemic after a 5‐year hiatus). Chi‐squared tests and subsequent residual analyses were used. Adjusted standardized residuals were calculated when chi‐squared results were significant. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R version 4.0.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Comparison of VP7 and VP4 Amino Acid Sequences

VP7 and VP4, the major antigenic targets of protective immunity, contain well‐characterized epitopes [17, 18]. VP7 includes three epitopes (7‐1a, 7‐1b, and 7‐2; 29 amino acids), and VP4 includes nine epitopes (eight in VP8*, five in VP5*; 37 amino acids). Amino acid residues at these epitopes were compared between strains detected in 2025 and the vaccine strains Rotarix and RotaTeq.

Results

Trends in Hospitalized Patients

Between July 1, 2019 and October 31, 2025, 113 patients were hospitalized with rotavirus gastroenteritis (Figure 1A). In 2019, 33 cases were recorded between July and December. Following the implementation of strict COVID‐19 control measures in late February 2020, the typical March–June seasonal epidemic did not occur, and only 11 cases were reported throughout 2020. No patients hospitalized with rotavirus gastroenteritis were reported in 2021, followed by 9 in 2022, 3 in 2023, and 8 in 2024.

Age Distribution of Hospitalized Patients

Among the 44 patients hospitalized between July 2019 and September 2020, prior to routine rotavirus vaccination in Japan, the age distribution was as follows: 0 years, 8 patients (18.2%); 1 year, 10 patients (22.7%); 2 years, 6 patients (13.6%); 3–6 years, 14 patients (31.8%); 7–12 years, 6 patients (13.6%); and no hospitalizations among those aged 13–15 years (Figure 1B).

In contrast, among the 49 patients hospitalized between January and October 2025, no cases occurred among children aged 0 or 1 year, nor adolescents aged 13–15 years. The age distribution was: 2 years, 2 patients (4.1%); 3–6 years, 22 patients (44.9%); and 7–12 years, 25 patients (51.0%) (Figure 1B).

A significant shift in age distribution was observed between the two periods (χ²(4) = 33.25, p < 0.01, V = 0.598). Adjusted standardized residual analysis indicated that children aged 0 and 1 year were significantly overrepresented in 2019–2020 and underrepresented in 2025 (0 years: 18.2% vs. 0%, p = 0.003; 1 year: 22.7% vs. 0%, p = 0.001). Conversely, children aged 7–12 years were significantly overrepresented in 2025 (13.6% vs. 51.0%, p < 0.001) (Figure 1B).

The median age of the 49 hospitalized patients in 2025 was 7 years 0 months (interquartile range, 5 years 4 months to 9 years 9 months), and 51.0% (25/49) were male. Most cases occurred in Sapporo (21 cases), Muroran (10 cases), and Kushiro (7 cases), accounting for 77.5% of all hospitalizations (Figure 2).

Whole Genome Analysis

Whole genome sequencing was performed on viral strains obtained from stool samples of 31 of the 49 RVA‐positive patients. Complete genotypes were obtained for 30 strains except for strain KU25‐7. Of these, 18 strains (60.0%) were equine‐like G3P[8] (DS‐1‐like), 4 strains (13.3%) were typical‐human G3P[8] (Wa‐like), and 8 strains (26.7%) were G8P[8] (DS‐1‐like), with all genotypes detected across multiple municipalities (Figure 2).

Most strains from Sapporo (14/16) were equine‐like G3P[8], whereas all 6 strains from Kushiro were G8P[8]. Four samples from Muroran comprised all three genotypes. Among the 11 samples from vaccinated children, 10 were equine‐like G3P[8] and one was typical‐human G3P[8] (NS25‐12).

Equine‐Like G3P[8] (DS‐1‐Like)

The 18 equine‐like strains shared ≥ 98.0% nucleotide identity across 7 genome segments (VP7, VP4, VP6, VP1–3, and NSP3) (Supporting Information: Figure 1A). Fourteen strains from Sapporo showed ≥ 98.2% identity across all 11 segments, and NS25‐3 was selected as a representative. Four strains from Urakawa, Muroran, and Yakumo (UR25‐1, UR25‐2, MU25‐3, YA25‐3) showed ≥ 98.8% identity across all segments, and UR25‐1 was selected as a representative (Supporting Information: Figure 1B).

NS25‐3 exhibited ≥ 99.6% identity across all 11 genome segments to strain HEB17‐269ds detected in China in 2025, and ≥ 98.0% identity to multiple equine‐like G3P[8] strains circulating in Brazil (e.g., BRA/LABIOMIC‐R1/2024) and Belgium (e.g., BEL/F14353G3equine/2022) (Figure 4A) [11]. UR25‐1 showed similarly high identity to Chinese strain SC23‐251ds (≥ 99.3%) and to recent Brazilian and Belgian strains (Figure 4B).

KU25‐10 (G8P[8]) belonged to lineage I together with the other G8P[8] strains identified in this study (Figure 5B). Within lineage I, KU25‐10 formed a sub‐lineage with human G8P[8] strains circulating globally from 2013 to 2025 (98.2–99.9% identity), as well as with bovine and caprine strains detected between 1988 and 2010 (92.9–97.7% identity). All 30 strains in this study belonged to lineage I, clustering with global G1, G3, G8, and G9P[8] strains detected between 1999 and 2025 (Figure 8). Nine Other Genome Segments

In the phylogenetic trees of VP6, VP1–VP3, and NSP1–NSP5, the equine‐like G3P[8], typical human G3P[8], and G8P[8] strains detected in Hokkaido in 2025 clustered within the same lineages as contemporary global strains circulating in Asia, Europe, and the Americas (Supporting Information: Figure 2A–I).

Amino Acid Differences in VP7 and VP4

For VP7, the 2025 equine‐like G3P[8], typical‐human G3P[8], and G8P[8] strains from Hokkaido Prefecture showed 15, 12, and 16 differences from Rotarix G1, and 4, 4, and 12 amino acid differences from RotaTeq G3 (Figure 9A). Differences were distributed across all three epitopes: 7‐1a, 7‐1b, and 7‐2. The equine‐like G3P[8] strains uniquely possessed serine at position 87.

Discussion

In this study, we documented the resurgence of rotavirus gastroenteritis in Hokkaido Prefecture, Japan, in 2025, after 5 years of minimal activity. Although this study was based on hospital‐based surveillance, national sentinel surveillance data also indicated an increase in rotavirus cases in 2025 compared with the preceding COVID‐19 pandemic years [22]. The epidemic showed a seasonal peak in March–April, consistent with pre‐pandemic patterns [23]. Whole‐genome analysis demonstrated the co‐circulation of three genotype constellations, equine‐like G3P[8] (DS‐1‐like), typical‐human G3P[8] (Wa‐like), and G8P[8] (DS‐1‐like), with marked geographic heterogeneity, including the predominance of equine‐like G3P[8] in Sapporo and G8P[8] in Kushiro (Figure 2). Consistent with previous reports that multiple lineages of the same genotype can circulate concurrently [20], genetically distinct lineages of all three genotypes were co‐circulating in Hokkaido in 2025.

Equine‐like G3P[8] rotavirus was first reported in humans in 2013 [3], and subsequently in Japan during a 2016 outbreak in Hokkaido (e.g., JPN/To16‐12/2016) [19]. In the 2020 s, outbreaks have been reported in Belgium, Australia, Iran, Venezuela, and other countries [11, 24, 25]. Similar to equine‐like G3P[8] strains, DS‐1‐like G8P[8] rotavirus has increased in prevalence across Asia since the 2010s [4, 21], including an outbreak in Hokkaido in 2014 (e.g., JPN/To14‐0/2014) [5]. Notably, genomic surveillance in Vietnam revealed two temporally distinct waves of G8P[8] activity during 2014–2017 (e.g., RVN1149/2014) and 2018–2021 (e.g., RVN19‐0608/2019), with analyses indicating that the predominant strains in the second wave originated from a lineage genetically distinct from that of the first wave [21]. Importantly, multiple G8P[8] outbreaks reported in China during the 2020 s [26] appear to involve strains that are genetically closely related to the second‐wave Vietnamese strain RVN19‐0608/2019, suggesting that this lineage may have served as a common ancestral source for recent G8P[8] circulation in East Asia.

As shown in Figure 3, the equine‐like G3P[8] and G8P[8] strains circulating in Hokkaido in 2025 were genetically distinct from strains detected locally in the 2010s but closely related to strains circulating internationally in the 2020 s, particularly those reported in China between 2022 and 2025. Japan resumed general entry permissions on October 11, 2022, after COVID‐19–related restrictions. According to the Immigration Services Agency of Japan [27], 79.4% of all international arrivals between October 2022 and October 2025 were from Asia, with travelers from China being the second most common (16.9%), following those from South Korea (24.7%) (Supporting Information: Figure 3). Taken together, the close phylogenetic relatedness to recent Chinese strains and the resumption of international travel raise the possibility that the re‐emergent rotavirus strains detected in Hokkaido in 2025 were introduced from China or other Asian countries.

A marked shift in the age distribution of hospitalized patients was observed in 2025. Before routine vaccination (July 2019–September 2020), children aged 7–12 years accounted for only 13.6% (6/44) of cases, whereas this age group represented 51.0% of cases in 2025 (Figure 1B). This shift is likely explained primarily by reduced natural rotavirus exposure during the COVID‐19 pandemic, which may have led to waning neutralizing antibody titers in older children. In addition, the relatively modest rotavirus vaccination coverage among children of this age group in Hokkaido (approximately 40–60%) [6], reflecting their birth during a period when rotavirus vaccination was voluntary in Japan, may have further contributed. Declines in population‐level antibody titers against several viral pathogens have been documented during periods of suppressed viral circulation under stringent COVID‐19 control measures [28]. Given the minimal rotavirus activity in Hokkaido from 2020 to 2024, older children may not have received periodic immune boosting, increasing susceptibility in this age group.

Conversely, infants aged 0–1 years, who comprised 40.9% (18/44) of cases before routine vaccination, represented none of the 2025 cases (Figure 1B). Routine rotavirus vaccination was introduced in Japan in October 2020, and most children aged ≤ 4 years in 2025 would have been vaccinated. These findings suggest that Rotarix and RotaTeq effectively prevented severe disease among infants and young toddlers, even during the 2025 circulation of equine‐like G3P[8], G8P[8], and typical‐human G3P[8] strains. National sentinel surveillance in Japan also indicated that the overall magnitude of the 2025 rotavirus season was only one‐half to one‐third that of typical pre‐pandemic years (2015–2019) [22]. Because infants and young toddlers usually account for most severe rotavirus cases, their effective protection by routine RVA vaccination likely constrained the overall scale of the 2025 epidemic. In addition to vaccination, breastfeeding has also been reported to contribute to protection against severe rotavirus gastroenteritis in early infancy [29], suggesting that changes in infant nutrition and feeding practices may also warrant consideration in future studies.

Nevertheless, breakthrough hospitalizations were observed among Rotarix‐ or RotaTeq‐vaccinated children aged ≥ 2 years, with 14 cases identified, most of whom were school‐aged children. As discussed above, reduced rotavirus circulation during the COVID‐19 pandemic may have limited immune boosting in older children, contributing to symptomatic infections even among some vaccinated individuals. Among the vaccinated patients for whom stool samples were available, equine‐like G3P[8] strains predominated (10/11 cases) (Figure 3). A similar pattern has been reported in Belgium, where equine‐like G3P[8] was frequently detected in vaccinated patients between 2009 and 2023 [11], raising the hypothesis that equine‐like G3P[8] strains may be less susceptible to vaccine‐induced immunity. However, our amino acid analyses do not support this hypothesis. G8P[8] strains exhibited more extensive differences from both Rotarix and RotaTeq strains in the VP7 and VP4 antigenic epitopes than did equine‐like G3P[8] strains (Figure 6). Although equine‐like G3P[8] strains uniquely harbored a serine substitution at VP7 residue 87, which has been reported as a neutralization escape site, there is currently no evidence that this single substitution alone substantially reduces vaccine‐induced neutralization. Furthermore, increasing evidence suggests that protection conferred by rotavirus vaccines is mediated not only by neutralizing antibodies against VP7 and VP4 but also by immune responses targeting other viral proteins, including VP6 and NSP4 [30, 31]. Taken together, these findings indicate that sequence variation in VP7 and VP4 alone is insufficient to explain the frequent detection of equine‐like G3P[8] among vaccinated individuals, and that additional immunological and epidemiological factors likely contribute to this phenomenon.

Despite the re‐emergence of other enteric viruses, such as norovirus and adenovirus, from 2021 to 2024 in Hokkaido [10], rotavirus activity remained minimal during this period, likely reflecting sustained vaccine‐derived population immunity. A similar pattern was observed in South Korea, where rotavirus vaccination coverage reached 85.6% by 2017; although gastroenteritis caused by norovirus, adenovirus, astrovirus, and sapovirus increased following relaxation of COVID‐19 measures, rotavirus did not re‐emerge through April 2023 [32]. However, evidence from Belgium suggests that high vaccination coverage alone is insufficient to fully explain the timing of rotavirus resurgence. Belgium, which has maintained rotavirus vaccination coverage of 85–90% since 2006, experienced a marked resurgence of rotavirus gastroenteritis between December 2021 and March 2022, shortly after COVID‐19 control measures were relaxed [11]. In Japan, public health measures were relaxed more gradually, and personal adherence to infection control practices such as mask use remained relatively high [33]. Together with routine rotavirus vaccination, these behavioral and policy factors may have contributed to the delayed resurgence of rotavirus gastroenteritis in Hokkaido compared with other countries.

This study had several limitations. First, we could not calculate positivity rates of rotavirus because diagnostic testing volumes were not recorded. Second, stool samples could not be obtained from some hospitalized patients, which may have led to underestimation of rotavirus cases. Third, rapid antigen tests, although widely used, have lower sensitivity than RT‐PCR [34, 35]. In this study, patients testing positive for rotavirus by either rapid antigen test or the BioFire FilmArray Gastrointestinal Panel were included. Those tested only with rapid antigen assays may have been under‐detected if viral loads in stool were low, potentially leading to an underestimation of the number of rotavirus‐positive cases. Fourth, clinical information for rotavirus gastroenteritis patients enrolled prior to 2025 was not analyzed in this study, making it impossible to directly compare symptom severity between patients hospitalized from July 2019 to September 2020 and those hospitalized from January to October 2025. Fifth, selection of reference sequences from GenBank may introduce bias, as the available sequences may not fully represent the global diversity of circulating rotavirus strains.

Conclusion

Rotavirus gastroenteritis re‐emerged in Hokkaido Prefecture in 2025 after a prolonged period of minimal circulation. The epidemic was characterized by the predominance of equine‐like G3P[8] and G8P[8] strains that were genetically distinct from earlier local strains and closely related to recent Asian lineages. Although routine rotavirus vaccination has effectively protected infants from severe disease, the accumulation of susceptible older children following reduced natural exposure may have contributed to the observed resurgence. Continued genomic and epidemiological surveillance of RVA will be essential to monitor the evolution of circulating strains and to assess long‐term vaccine impact in the post‐pandemic era.
