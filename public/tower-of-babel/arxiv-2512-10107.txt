Ellis Buckminster, Samuel J. Taylor

Abstract
. Thurston introduced the notion of a universal circle associated to a taut foliation of a 3 3 -manifold as a way of organizing the ideal circle boundaries of its leaves into a single circle action. Calegari–Dunfield proved that every taut foliation of an atoroidal 3 3 -manifold M M has a universal circle, but the uniqueness (or lack-thereof) of this structure remains rather mysterious. In this paper, we consider the foliations associated to an Anosov flow φ \varphi on M M , showing that several constructions of a universal circle in the literature are typically distinct. Moreover, the underlying action of the Calegari–Dunfield leftmost universal circle is generally not even conjugate to the universal circle arising from the boundary of the flow space of φ \varphi . Our primary tool is a way to use the flow space of φ \varphi to parameterize the circle bundle at infinity of φ \varphi ’s invariant foliations.

1.
Introduction

Let
M
be a closed, oriented, irreducible, and atoroidal (i.e.
\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\nleq\pi_{1}(M)
)
3
–manifold. A
taut foliation

\mathcal{F}
is a cooriented foliation of
M
by two-dimensional leaves with the property that each leaf is intersected by a closed curve that is positively transverse to
\mathcal{F}
. Such foliations have been central objects in the study of
3
–manifolds for decades
[
Nov65
,
Gab83
,
Thu97
,
Cal07
]
and have recently received a resurgence of interest due to their role in the
L
-space conjecture
[
BGW13
,
OS04
,
Juh15
]
. In this paper, we study the taut foliations that arise as the invariant foliations of an Anosov flow on
M
(i.e.
Anosov foliations
) and the lack of uniqueness of their
universal circles
.

If
\mathcal{F}
is a taut foliation of an atoroidal
3
–manifold
M
, then there is a metric on
M
that restricts to a hyperbolic metric on each leaf of
\mathcal{F}

[
Can93
]
. Lifting to the universal cover
\widetilde{M}
, each leaf
\lambda
of the lifted foliation
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
admits a well-defined ideal boundary
\partial_{\infty}\lambda
homeomorphic to a circle, and the fundamental group of
M
acts on this collection of ideal circles. A
universal circle

\mathcal{C}
for
\mathcal{F}
, originally introduced by Thurston, is a natural way of organizing this data into a
single
circle action
\pi_{1}(M)\curvearrowright\mathcal{C}
. The action comes equipped with a family of monotone maps
\pi_{\lambda}\colon\mathcal{C}\to\partial_{\infty}\lambda
, one for each leaf
\lambda
of
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
, that intertwines the universal circle action with the action on the disjoint union of all
\partial_{\infty}\lambda
. We refer the reader to
Section

3.1
for the complete definition, which was precisely formulated by Calegari–Dunfield
[
CD03
]
. They additionally proved every taut foliation of an atoroidal manifold admits a universal circle.

Despite their role as a basic object in the study of flows and foliations, very little is know about the possible universal circles for a fixed foliation, or under what conditions a
minimal
universal circle is unique. See, for example,
[
CD03
, Remark 6.24]
or Question 8.9 on Calegari’s list
[
Cal02
]
.
Indeed, even the Calegari–Dunfield construction itself has built in flexibility:
as one navigates the various ideal circle boundaries one can choose to take either the leftmost or rightmost turns, thereby leading to both a leftmost universal circle
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
and a rightmost universal circle
\mathcal{C}^{r}
; see
Section

3.2
for the details.

More recently, the second author together with Landry and Minsky
[
LMT25
]
showed that for any foliation
\mathcal{F}
(almost) transverse to a pseudo-Anosov flow
\varphi
, the boundary
\partial\mathcal{O}_{\varphi}
of
\varphi
’s flow space is naturally a universal circle for
\mathcal{F}
(see
Section

3.3
). This gives new constructions of universal circles, which are necessarily distinct for non orbit equivalent flows.

However, there are situations where minimal universal circles are quite constrained. Calegari establishes a version of uniqueness for
\mathbb{R}
–covered foliations
[
Cal00
]

and foliations with one-sided branching
[
Cal03
]
.
More recently, Huang proved that if
\mathcal{F}
is a depth one foliation transverse to a pseudo-Anosov flow
\varphi
without perfect fits, then the leftmost universal circle
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
, the rightmost universal circle
\mathcal{C}^{r}
, and the universal circle
\partial\mathcal{O}_{\varphi}
associated to the flow space
\mathcal{O}_{\varphi}
are all isomorphic as universal circles
[
Hua24
]
.

We will consider the invariant foliations of an orientable Anosov flow and essentially come to the opposite conclusion: for such foliations all three universal circles are distinct. We next turn to more formally summarize our results.

Results

Throughout the paper,
M
is a closed, oriented, atoroidal
3
-manifold and
\varphi
is a Anosov flow on
M
with orientable stable/unstable foliations, which are denoted
W^{s/u}
. We remark that for an arbitrary Anosov flow on
M
, its invariant foliations become orientable after taking a double cover. Although our results hold for either invariant foliation, we will state everything in terms of the unstable foliation
W^{u}
.

It is easy to see that the foliation
W^{u}
of
M
is taut, and so the Calegari–Dunfield construction gives two (potentially isomorphic) universal circles; the leftmost universal circle
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
and the rightmost universal circle
\mathcal{C}^{r}
.

Following Fenley, the foliation can also be
tilted
by an isotopy that makes it transverse to
\varphi
. We denote this tilted foliation
W^{u}_{\mathrm{tilt}}
and refer to
Section

4.1
for additional details. By
[
LMT25
]
, the boundary
\partial\mathcal{O}
of
\varphi
’s flows space
\mathcal{O}
is naturally a universal circle for
W^{u}_{\mathrm{tilt}}
and thus for
W^{u}
; see
Section

3.3
.

In his survey of Anosov flows, Potrie suggest exploring the connection between these two notions of a universal circle for Anosov foliations
[
Pot25
, Section 6]
.
Our first main theorem is that these constructions generally determine different universal circles:

Theorem A
.

For a non
\mathbb{R}
–covered Anosov flow,
the minimal universal circles
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
,
\mathcal{C}^{r}
, and
\partial\mathcal{O}
are all distinct, up to isomorphism.

The case of
A
for an
\mathbb{R}
–covered
\varphi
is better understood and is summarized in
Section

6
.

In
A
, two universal circles are isomorphic if the underlying actions are conjugate by a homeomorphism that intertwines the monotone maps to the
\partial_{\infty}\lambda
; see
Section

3.1
.
In fact, one can also tilt the foliation
W^{u}
in the opposite direction, and this too results in a distinct universal circle, even though the underlying circle action is again
\pi_{1}(M)\curvearrowright\partial\mathcal{O}
.

Furthermore, we prove the following result that shows the Calegari–Dunfield and flow space boundary constructions give different circles in the strongest sense:

Theorem B
.

The universal circle actions
\pi_{1}M\curvearrowright\partial\mathcal{O}
and
\pi_{1}(M)\curvearrowright\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
are not conjugate.

\partial\mathcal{O}^{\pm}
. All four universal circles are nonisomorphic.

The results of
A
and
B
are summarized in
Figure

2
.

B
is proven using a detailed analysis of Calegari–Dunfield construction applied to
W^{u}
. To explain, let
\mathcal{L}=\widetilde{M}/\widetilde{W}^{u}
be the leaf space of the foliation
W^{u}
, i.e. the quotient space of the universal cover
\widetilde{M}
obtained by identifying points contained in the same leaf of the lifted foliation
\widetilde{W}^{u}
.
To each leaf
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}
, there is a ideal circle
\partial_{\infty}\lambda
and we let
E_{\infty}
be the
circle bundle at infinity
over
\mathcal{L}
(also called the
ideal tubulation
in
[
Pot25
]
), which as a set is the union of the
\partial_{\infty}\lambda
. For details, see
Section

3.1
.

At least morally, a universal circle is constructed by finding a sufficiently large
\pi_{1}(M)
–invariant collection of ‘noncrossing’ sections
s\colon\mathcal{L}\to E_{\infty}
of the circle bundle
E_{\infty}\to\mathcal{L}
.
The noncrossing condition implies that these sections admit a natural circular order that is imposed by the circles
\partial_{\infty}\lambda
for each
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}
. The associated universal circle
\mathcal{C}
then arises from the completion of this circularly ordered set, and there is an induced action
\pi_{1}(M)\curvearrowright\mathcal{C}
by orientation preserving homeomorphisms.
Hence, understanding the structure of
E_{\infty}
is a key step toward understanding the possible universal circles for a foliation.

Since
W^{u}
is the unstable foliation of the Anosov flow
\varphi
, the orbits in a given leaf are asymptotic in backwards time. This translates into the statement that for each leaf
\lambda
of
\widetilde{W}^{u}
, there is a unique point
\theta_{\lambda}\in\partial_{\infty}\lambda
that is the backwards endpoint of each orbit in
\lambda
of the lifted flow
\widetilde{\varphi}
. The assignment
\lambda\mapsto\theta_{\lambda}
determines a (continuous) section
s_{\bf nm}\colon\mathcal{L}\to E_{\infty}
that we call the
nonmarker section
(
Section

5.1
). The following result is our main tool to understand
E_{\infty}
.

Theorem C
.

The assignment of each
p\in\lambda
to the endpoint in
\partial_{\infty}\lambda
of its forward
\widetilde{\varphi}
–orbit induces an equivariant homeomorphism

\Phi\colon\mathcal{O}\to E_{\infty}\smallsetminus s_{\bf nm}(\mathcal{L}).

As before,
\mathcal{O}
is the orbit space of
\varphi
, defined as
\mathcal{O}=\widetilde{M}/\widetilde{\varphi}
where
\widetilde{\varphi}
is the lifted flow. By work of Barbot
[
Bar95
]
and Fenley
[
Fen94
]
,
\mathcal{O}
is a plane bifoliated by
\mathcal{O}^{u/s}
which are the projections of the foliations
\widetilde{W}^{u/s}
of
\widetilde{M}
. The importance of
C
lies in the fact that
\Phi
maps the foliations
\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
to two natural foliations of
E_{\infty}\smallsetminus s_{\bf nm}(\mathcal{L})
. Essentially by definition, the unstable foliation
\mathcal{O}^{u}
is mapped to the foliation of
E_{\infty}\smallsetminus s_{\bf nm}(\mathcal{L})
by open arcs
\partial_{\infty}\lambda\smallsetminus\theta_{\lambda}
, for
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}
. More importantly, the stable foliation
\mathcal{O}^{s}
is mapped by
\Phi
to the foliation by
markers
(justifying the name given to
s_{\bf nm}
). Informally, markers are arcs in the
E_{\infty}
determined by the directions where leaves of
\widetilde{W}^{u}
are nonexpanding (see
Section

3.2
) – they are the central object in the Calegari–Dunfield construction of
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
and
C^{r}
.

In this sense,
C
together with a understanding of the foliations
\mathcal{O}^{u/s}
is the main tool to study the relation between
\partial\mathcal{O}
and leftmost and rightmost universal circles that is required to establish
A
and
B
. Indeed, Potrie suggests directly studying the action on the ‘more canonical’ circle bundle at infinity
E_{\infty}

[
Pot25
, Section 6]
, and
C
provides a direct approach for doing so in terms of the flow space
\mathcal{O}
.

2.
Background

Here we collect some background needed throughout the paper.

2.1.
Circular orders and monotone maps

A
circular order
on a set
X
is a map

\langle\cdot,\cdot,\cdot\rangle\colon X\times X\times X\to\{-1,0,1\}

such that

(1)

\langle a,b,c\rangle=\pm 1
if and only if
a,b,c
are pairwise distinct, and

(2)

for all
a,b,c,d\in X
,

\langle b,c,d\rangle-\langle a,c,d\rangle+\langle a,b,d\rangle+\langle a,b,c\rangle=0.

Intuitively, a nondegenerate ordered triple gets mapped to
1
if it is ordered clockwise and to
-1
if it is ordered counterclockwise.

Whenever we have a circular order on an uncountable, second countable set
X
, we can complete it to a circle
\mathcal{C}
. We refer the reader to
[
Fra13
,
Bon23
]
for the precise details, but informally summarize this process as follows:
First, embed
X
into the circle
S^{1}
in a circular order preserving way. Take the closure of this set in
S^{1}
, obtaining a closed subset of
S^{1}
whose complement is an at most countable union of open intervals. By collapsing the closures of these intervals, we obtain the circle
\mathcal{C}
. The map
i\colon X\to\mathcal{C}
is an embedding so long as no point in
X
is isolated on one or both sides.

A
monotone map

\pi\colon\mathcal{C}_{1}\to\mathcal{C}_{2}
between oriented circles is a degree one map such that the preimage of each point is connected. A maximal open interval in a point preimage is called a
gap
, and the
core
of
\pi
is the complement (in
\mathcal{C}_{1}
) of its gaps.

2.2.
Anosov flows and their flow spaces

Throughout the paper, we work in the category of
topological
Anosov flows. Since our manifolds are atoroidal, such flows are transitive (see
[
Mos92
, Proposition 2.6]
and more generally
[
BBM24
, Corollary 1.6]
) and hence orbit equivalent to
smooth
Anosov flows by work of Shannon
[
Sha20
]
. Here, an orbit equivalence is a homeomorphism sending oriented orbits to oriented orbits. Thus, our results also hold for smooth Anosov flows, but we will make use of the greater flexibility in the topological setting. We refer the reader to the first section of the recent book by Barthelmé and Mann
[
BM25
]
for the full story.

To state the definition of a topological Anosov flow, following
[
BM25
]
, we first recall that if
W
is a foliation of
M
and
x\in M
, then
W_{\varepsilon}(x)
is the component of
W(x)\cap B_{\varepsilon}(x)
containing
x
. Here,
W(x)
is the leaf through
x
and
B_{\varepsilon}(x)
denotes the
\varepsilon
–ball around
x
. Also, a standard reparameterization is an increasing homeomorphism
\sigma\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
that fixes
0
.

A
(topological) Anosov flow

\varphi
on
M
is a flow leaving invariant a pair of topologically transverse two-dimensional foliations
W^{s}
and
W^{u}
whose leaves intersect along orbits of
\varphi
such that

•

For any
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, if
y\in W^{s}_{\varepsilon}(x)
(resp.
y\in W^{u}_{\varepsilon}(x)
), there is a standard reparameterization
\sigma
such that
d(\varphi_{t}(x),\varphi_{\sigma(t)}(y))\to 0
as
t\to\infty
(resp. as
t\to-\infty)
.

•

For any
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, there exits
\delta>0
such that if
y\in W^{s}_{\varepsilon}(x)
(resp.
y\in W^{u}_{\varepsilon}(x)
) is not in the same
\varepsilon
–local orbit as
x
then for any standard reparameterization
\sigma
, there exists
t<0
(resp.
t>0
) with
d(\varphi_{t}(x),\varphi_{\sigma(t)}(y))>\delta
.

The foliation
W^{s}
is called the (weak)
stable foliation
and
W^{u}
is called the (weak)
unstable foliation
.

For any 2-dimensional foliation
\mathcal{F}
on a 3-manifold
\widetilde{M}
, we define the
leaf space
of
\mathcal{F}
by
\mathcal{L}=\widetilde{M}/\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
. In general, this is a simply connected, possibly non-Hausdorff 1-manifold. If
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathbb{R}
, we say
\mathcal{F}
is
\mathbb{R}
-covered
, otherwise it is
non
\mathbb{R}
-covered
. In the case of an Anosov flow, we denote the leaf space of
W^{s/u}
by
\mathcal{L}^{s/u}
. Furthermore, we say the flow is
\mathbb{R}
-covered
if both
W^{s}
and
W^{u}
are, and
non
\mathbb{R}
-covered
otherwise.

2.2.1.
The flow space and its boundary

An important tool in the study of Anosov flows is the
flow space
(also called the
orbit space
), originally studied by Barbot
[
Bar95
]
and Fenley
[
Fen94
]
. The flow space of an Anosov flow
\varphi
, denoted
\mathcal{O}
, is defined as

\mathcal{O}=\widetilde{M}/\widetilde{\varphi},

where
\widetilde{M}
is the universal cover of
M
, and
\widetilde{\varphi}
is a lift of
\varphi
to
\widetilde{M}
. Let

\Theta\colon\widetilde{M}\to\mathcal{O}

denote projection to the flow space. The flow space is topologically a plane and inherits an action by
\pi_{1}(M)
coming from the action by deck transformations. The lifted weak stable and unstable (two-dimensional) foliations
\widetilde{W}^{s/u}
descend to a pair of one-dimensional foliations

\mathcal{O}^{s/u}=\Theta(\widetilde{W}^{s/u}),

called the
stable and unstable foliations of
\mathcal{O}
. In figures, we will always draw
\mathcal{O}^{s}
as blue and
\mathcal{O}^{u}
as red. These foliations are preserved by the
\pi_{1}(M)
action and can be quite complicated; see
Figure

3
.

Since
W^{s}
and
W^{u}
are orientable by assumption, the foliations
\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
admit a
\pi_{1}(M)
–invariant orientation. Using the induced orientation on
\mathcal{O}
, we fix orientations on
\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
so that
\mathcal{O}
is oriented by charts taking leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{u}
to the oriented
x
–axis and leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{s}
to the oriented
y
–axis.

Figure 3.
The orbit space of a non
\mathbb{R}
-covered Anosov flow.

Topologically, the flow space with its two foliations falls into one of three categories; see
[
Bar95
]
,
[
Fen94
]
, and
[
BM25
, Theorem 2.6.1]
.
It is either

(1)

trivial
, i.e. homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the horizontal and vertical foliations,

(2)

skew
, i.e. homeomorphic to the strip

\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\>\mid\>x-1<y<x\}

with the horizontal and vertical foliations, or

(3)

branching
, meaning the foliations
\mathcal{O}^{s}
and
\mathcal{O}^{u}
are both non
\mathbb{R}
-covered.

Since
\mathcal{L}^{s/u}\cong\mathcal{O}/\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
by a canonical homeomorphism, we don’t distinguish between the leaf spaces of
W^{s}
(respectively
W^{u}
) and
\mathcal{O}^{s}
(respectively
\mathcal{O}^{u}
).

By work of Fenley
[
Fen12
]
, the flow space can by compactified with a circle, called
the boundary of the flow space
and denoted
\partial\mathcal{O}
. This circle can be constructed by way of the foliations
\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
as follows. Consider the set

\mathcal{C}=\{\text{ends of leaves of }\mathcal{O}^{s/u}\}.

This set has a natural circular order by considering the intersections of rays in
\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
with the boundaries of larger and larger closed disks in
\mathcal{O}
. As described in
Section

2.1
,
\mathcal{C}
can be completed to a circle, which is what we call
\partial\mathcal{O}
. The topology on
\mathcal{O}\cup\partial\mathcal{O}
is defined so that leaves in
\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
limit onto the corresponding ends in
\partial\mathcal{O}
. We use
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
to denote
\mathcal{O}\cup\partial\mathcal{O}
. The action
\pi_{1}(M)\curvearrowright\mathcal{O}
extends continuously to an action
\pi_{1}(M)\curvearrowright\overline{\mathcal{O}}
.

2.2.2.
Structures in the flow space

Leaves
\ell^{s}
and
\ell^{u}
of
\mathcal{O}^{s}
and
\mathcal{O}^{u}
make a
perfect fit
if there is an embedded rectangle

R=i([0,1]\times[0,1])\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{O}}

such that

(1)

i(x,y)\in\partial\mathcal{O}
if and only if
(x,y)=(0,0)
,

(2)

for all
x>0
,
i(x,0)\subseteq\ell^{s}
,

(3)

for all
y>0
,
i(0,y)\subseteq\ell^{u}
, and

(4)

the interior of
R
is trivially foliated by
\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
.

See
Figure

4
. The rectangle
R
is called a
perfect fit rectangle
.

Figure 4.
On the left, a perfect fit rectangle in yellow. On the right, pairwise nonseparated leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{s}
form a chain and force adjacent perfect fit rectangles, in yellow.

Leaves of
\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
that are nonseparated in the leaf space (i.e. fail to have disjoint neighborhoods) are called
branching
or
nonseparated leaves
. By
[
Fen98
, Theorem B]
, sets of pairwise nonseparated leaves form a chain, and consecutive leaves in the chain make
adjacent perfect fits, as in
Figure

4
. By
[
Fen98
, Corollary F]
, such chains always have finite length for an Anosov flow on an atoroidal 3-manifold.

A leaf of
\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
that doesn’t make any perfect fits is called a
regular
leaf. Such a leaf is necessarily non-branching.

Since the foliations
\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
have been oriented,
given a pair of nonseparated leaves in
\mathcal{O}^{s/u}
, it makes sense to say whether they are
branching from above
or
branching from below
. See
Figure

5
.

Figure 5.
The unstable leaves
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
are branching from above, while the unstable leaves
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
are branching from below. The arrows on stable leaves indicate their orientations, which in turn determine the coorientations on intersecting unstable leaves.

Fenley showed that if an Anosov flow with orientable
foliations
W^{s/u}
is non
\mathbb{R}
-covered, then it has two sided branching, i.e. both foliations have leaves that are branching from below, as well as leaves that are branching from above
[
Fen95
]
.

2.2.3.
“Good form” for the flow

Following Barthelmé, Fenley, and Potrie
[
BFP23
, Corollary 5.23]
(see also
[
Pot25
, Proposition 5.3]
), we can replace
\varphi
with an orbit equivalent (topological) Anosov flow whose unstable foliation
W^{u}
has smooth leaves with the additional properties that:

(1)

there is a continuous metric
g
on
M
so that the restriction of
g
to each unstable leaf of
W^{u}
is hyperbolic,

(2)

orbits of
\varphi
in each leaf of
W^{u}
are geodesic, and

(3)

the strong unstable foliations
W^{uu}
in each leaf of
W^{u}
are projections of horocycles.

With these properties, we say the flow
\varphi
is
adapted
to
W^{u}
. Throughout the paper, we fix
\varphi
to be the flow adapted to
W^{u}
. This will be especially useful in characterizing the markers of
W^{u}
as we will do below.

Similarly, there is an orbit equivalent flow that is adapted to
W^{s}
. We will also use this fact when carefully describing the ‘tilting’ of
W^{u}
in
Section

4
.

3.
Universal circles, markers, and the Calegari–Dunfield construction

In this section, we define the notion of a universal circle for a foliation and review both the Calegari–Dunfield construction as well as the Landry–Minsky–Taylor construction (in the presence of a transverse pseudo-Anosov flow). Much of this involves explaining the tools used to define the Calegari–Dunfield universal circle, and we refer the reader to
[
CD03
]
for additional details.

3.1.
The circle bundle at infinity and universal circles

We begin by reviewing the structure associated to a taut foliation that is needed to define a universal circle.

E_{\infty}
for a non
\mathbb{R}
-covered foliation. The intersticial regions indicate the topology around branching leaves. Selected circle fibers are in red.

Suppose
\mathcal{F}
is a taut foliation of
M
. By a theorem of Candel
[
Can93
]
, we can put a
leafwise hyperbolic metric
on
M
, i.e. a continuous Riemannian metric
g
such that the pullback to each leaf is hyperbolic. Lifting everything to the universal cover
\widetilde{M}
, we have that
\widetilde{M}\cong\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and each leaf of
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
is topologically a plane.

Let
\mathcal{L}
denote the leaf space of
\mathcal{F}
, i.e.
\mathcal{L}=\widetilde{M}/\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
.
Recall that if
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathbb{R}
, we say
\mathcal{F}
is
\mathbb{R}
-covered
, otherwise it is
non
\mathbb{R}
-covered
. For a non
\mathbb{R}
-covered foliation, two leaves of
\mathcal{L}
are
comparable
if there is a transversal in
\widetilde{M}
connecting them and
incomparable
otherwise. Two incomparable leaves are in the same
cataclysm
if they are nonseparated as points in
\mathcal{L}
.

Geometrically, each leaf
\lambda
of
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
is isometric to
\mathbb{H}^{2}
under the pullback of the lifted metric
\widetilde{g}
. Thus, we can compactify
\lambda
with a circle
\partial_{\infty}\lambda
via the standard compactification of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
with
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{2}
. As a set, we define the
circle bundle at infinity
by

E_{\infty}=\bigcup_{\lambda\in\mathcal{L}}\partial_{\infty}\lambda.

We call the circles
\partial_{\infty}\lambda
the
circle fibers
.

We topologize this set as follows. Let
\tau
be a transversal to
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
, i.e. an embedded interval in
\widetilde{M}
transverse to
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
. Then
\tau
determines an embedded interval in
\mathcal{L}
, which we also call
\tau\subset\mathcal{L}
. We define a map

\text{UT}\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}\mid_{\tau}\to E_{\infty}\mid_{\tau}

by sending a point
(p,v)
to the forward endpoint of the geodesic through
p
with tangent vector
v
. We then topologize
E_{\infty}
such that this map is a homeomorphism for all
\tau
. See
[
CD03
]
for the details and
Figure

6
for an illustration.

With this topology,
E_{\infty}
has the structure of a circle bundle over the leaf space, where the fibers are the various circles at infinity for leaves of
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
. This space inherits an action of
\pi_{1}(M)
coming from the action by deck transformations on
\widetilde{M}
.

We are now ready to define the notion of a universal circle for
\mathcal{F}
.

3.0
.

A
universal circle
for
\mathcal{F}
is a circle
\mathcal{C}
together with an action
\pi_{1}(M)\curvearrowright\mathcal{C}
and a set of monotone maps

\{\pi_{\lambda}\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\partial_{\infty}\lambda\>\mid\>\lambda\in\mathcal{L}\}

such that the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)

For all
g\in\pi_{1}(M)
and for all
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}
, the following diagram commutes, where the horizontal maps come from the respective actions
\pi_{1}(M)\curvearrowright\mathcal{C}
and
\pi_{1}(M)\curvearrowright E_{\infty}
.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{C}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{C}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\partial_{\infty}\lambda}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\partial_{\infty}g\cdot\lambda}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdot g}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{\lambda}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{g\cdot\lambda}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdot g}

(2)

The function
\pi\colon\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{L}\to E_{\infty}
defined by

\pi(z,\lambda)=\pi_{\lambda}(z)

is continuous.

(3)

For any pair
\lambda
,
\mu
of incomparable leaves of
\mathcal{L}
, the core of
\pi_{\lambda}
is contained in a single gap of
\pi_{\mu}
.

The universal circle
\mathcal{C}
is
minimal
if no open interval in
\mathcal{C}
is contained in a gap of
\pi_{\lambda}
for all
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}
. Equivalently, there are no distinct points
x,y\in\mathcal{C}
such that
\pi_{\lambda}(x)=\pi_{\lambda}(y)
for each
\lambda
. Indeed, if such points are always identified, then each
\pi_{\lambda}
either collapses
[x,y]
or
[y,x]
as oriented subintervals of
\mathcal{C}
, and we can partition
\mathcal{L}
into
\mathcal{L}_{[x,y]}
or
\mathcal{L}_{[y,x]}
accordingly. These are disjoint closed sets whose union is
\mathcal{L}
and so one of them must be empty. Thus, we can collapse such intervals to build a minimal universal circle.

For each point
z\in\mathcal{C}
, we can define a (continuous) section
\sigma_{z}\colon\mathcal{L}\to E_{\infty}
of
E_{\infty}
, as a circle bundle over the leaf space, by

\sigma_{z}(\lambda)=\pi_{\lambda}(z).

The set of sections for a given universal circle can coalesce, but they can’t cross, essentially because the maps
\pi_{\lambda}
are monotone.

Given two universal circles
\mathcal{C}
,
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
with monotone maps
\{\pi^{\mathcal{C}}_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda},\{\pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\}_{\lambda}
, we will say they are
isomorphic
as universal circles, denoted
\mathcal{C}\cong\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, if there is a
\pi_{1}(M)
-equivariant homeomorphism
h\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
that intertwines the monotone maps, i.e. such that

\pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{C}}=\pi_{\lambda}^{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\circ h

for all
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}
. By the following lemma, isomorphic universal circles determine the same sets of sections, so this is a rather strong notion of isomorphism.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}
be universal circles for
\mathcal{F}
with sets of sections
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
. If
\mathcal{C}_{1}\cong\mathcal{C}_{2}
, then
\mathcal{S}_{1}=\mathcal{S}_{2}
. The converse is true if
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
are minimal.

Proof.

Let
\{\pi^{i}_{\lambda}\>\mid\>\lambda\in\mathcal{L}\}
be the monotone maps for
\mathcal{C}_{i}
, and let
h\colon\mathcal{C}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{2}
be the conjugating homeomorphism. Let
z\in\mathcal{C}^{1}
determining a section
\sigma^{1}_{z}
. Then
\sigma^{2}_{h(z)}
is a section of
\mathcal{C}_{2}
, and for all
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}
, we have

\sigma^{1}_{z}(\lambda)=\pi^{1}_{\lambda}(z)=\pi^{2}_{\lambda}\circ h(z)=\sigma^{2}_{h(z)}(\lambda).

Thus
\sigma^{1}_{z}=\sigma^{2}_{h(z)}
.

For the converse, note that a universal circle
\mathcal{C}
is minimal if and only if the map
z\mapsto\sigma_{z}
from
\mathcal{C}
to the space of all sections of
E_{\infty}\to\mathcal{L}
is injective. The image is the set
\mathcal{S}
of sections of
\mathcal{C}
, and
\mathcal{C}
can be recovered from
\mathcal{S}
by taking the induced circular order. From this, the required statement follows.
∎

A weaker notion of equivalence is that of a conjugate group action – part of the data of a universal circle is an action of
\pi_{1}(M)
on a circle, and one could ask whether two universal circles give rise to conjugate actions. This is the question we address for
W^{u}
in
Section

8
.

3.2.
Markers and the Calegari–Dunfield construction

To describe the Calegari–Dunfield construction, we first need to define
markers
in
E_{\infty}
. The basic idea behind markers is that they track directions in which leaves of
\mathcal{\widetilde{F}}
stay bounded distance as they go out to infinity.

The
separation constant
for
\mathcal{F}
is the largest number
\delta
such that each leaf of
\mathcal{F}
is quasi-isometrically embedded in its
\delta
-neighborhood (using the Riemannian metric
g
as above). For any
\varepsilon<\delta
, and
\varepsilon
-marker
is an embedding
m\colon[0,1]\times[0,\infty)\to\widetilde{M}
such that the following properties are satisfied.

(1)

For each
t\in[0,1]
,
m(t,[0,\infty))
is contained in a single leaf of
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
, and is a geodeisc ray in that leaf. These are called
horizontal rays
.

(2)

For each
x\in[0,\infty)
,
m([0,1],x)
is a transversal of height less than
\varepsilon/3
.

Since each horizontal ray in a marker is a geodesic in a leaf, it limits to a point on the boundary of that leaf. Thus, markers limit onto embedded intervals in
E_{\infty}
, and we will also call these
markers
. A point in
E_{\infty}
contained in a marker will be called a
marker point
.

Markers in
E_{\infty}
can be amalgamated by joining them at their endpoints, and doing this always yields an embedded interval transverse to the circle fibers
[
CD03
, Lemma 6.11]
. Abusing notation, we call a maximal such amalgamation a
maximal marker
, and consider it a marker (though it may be too tall to be a true marker). A key property of markers is that they cannot cross.

A section
s\colon\mathcal{L}\to E_{\infty}
is called
admissible
if it does not cross markers. Given a point
p\in E_{\infty}
, we define the
leftmost section based at
p
, denoted
s^{\ell}_{p}
, as follows. Let
\tau
be a line in
\mathcal{L}
such that
p\in E_{\infty}\mid_{\tau}
. In
E_{\infty}\mid_{\tau}
and traveling up from
p
,
s^{\ell}_{p}
is defined to be the section that is ‘leftmost’ among all admissible sections through
p
. Traveling down from
p
over
\tau
,
s^{\ell}_{p}
is the section that is ‘rightmost’ among admissible sections through
p
. This is made precise by approximating
s^{\ell}_{p}\mid_{\tau}
by paths
\gamma_{i}
that travel left above
p
and right below, except when it would mean crossing a marker in the set
M_{i}
, where
M_{i}\subseteq M_{i+1}
, and
\cup_{i}M_{i}
is the set of all markers. See
Figure

7
for the idea, and
[
CD03
]
for details.

Figure 7.
Approximating the leftmost section based at
p
. Markers are drawn in blue, and the highlighted point is
p
. On the left and in the middle are successive aproximations, and on the right is
s^{\ell}_{p}
, which looks something like a sideways graph of the Cantor function.

To extend
s^{\ell}_{p}
to the rest of
\mathcal{L}
, we use the ‘turning corners’ rule. Suppose we’ve defined
s^{\ell}_{p}
at a leaf
\lambda
, and need to extend it to a leaf
\mu
nonseparated from
\lambda
. By Lemma 6.18 of
[
CD03
]
, there is a canonical ‘monotone relation’ between
\partial_{\infty}\lambda
and
\partial_{\infty}\mu
. Effectively, this means that there are canonical points
p_{\lambda\mu}\in\partial_{\infty}\mu
and
p_{\mu\lambda}\in\partial_{\infty}\lambda
with the property that if
\{\lambda,\mu\}\subset C\subset\mathcal{L}
is path connected and
s\colon C\to E_{\infty}|_{C}
is an admissible section, then
(1)

s(\mu)\neq p_{\lambda\mu}
implies
s(\lambda)=p_{\mu\lambda}
, and similarly
(2)

s(\lambda)\neq p_{\mu\lambda}
implies
s(\mu)=p_{\lambda\mu}
.

Returning to the definition of
s^{\ell}_{p}
, we set

s^{\ell}_{p}(\mu)=p_{\lambda\mu}

and then continue to define
s^{\ell}_{p}
using the leftmost up, rightmost down rule from
s^{\ell}_{p}(\mu)
. Proceeding this way defines a section on all of
\mathcal{L}
.

Let

\mathcal{S}=\{s^{\ell}_{p}\;\mid\;p\in E_{\infty}\}.

The circular orders on
\partial_{\infty}\lambda
over various
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}
are compatible along the sections of
\mathcal{S}
, and they thereby induce
a well-defined circular order on
\mathcal{S}
. Furthermore, the action by
\pi_{1}(M)
on
E_{\infty}
gives an action on
\mathcal{S}
that preserves the circular order. Completing the order and collapsing gaps as described in
Section

2.1
yields a circle,
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
, called the
leftmost universal circle
, with an action by
\pi_{1}M
. Each point in
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
gives us a section, where limit sections are defined by pointwise convergence. Monotone maps

\pi_{\lambda}^{\ell}\colon\mathcal{C}^{\ell}\to\partial_{\infty}\lambda

are defined by evaluating sections on circle fibers. Thus,
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
is a universal circle for
\mathcal{F}
in the sense of
Section

3.1
.

To define the
rightmost universal circle

\mathcal{C}^{r}
, we run the same construction, but starting with
rightmost sections

s^{r}_{p}
that are rightmost up and leftmost down.

We will call a universal circle
admissible
if its sections do not cross markers. By construction,
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
and
\mathcal{C}^{r}
are admissible.

3.3.
The flowspace universal circle

Now suppose that
\mathcal{F}
is a foliation of

that is transverse to a pseudo-Anosov flow

4.
Tilting, shadows, and the flow space boundary

In this section, we isotope the foliation
W^{u}
so that it becomes transverse to
\varphi
. We call this ‘tilted’ foliation
W^{u}_{\mathrm{tilt}}
. A variation of this construction was first defined and studied by Fenley for
\mathbb{R}
–covered foliations in
[
Fen05
, Section 5]
. Also see
[
BWZ24
, Section 5]
for a related construction in a more general setting. For us, it will be important to understand the shadows of leaves of
W^{u}_{\mathrm{tilt}}
in terms of the original leaves of
W^{u}
, so we describe our ‘tilting’ rather explicitly.

4.1.
Tilting
W^{u}
to
W^{u}_{\mathrm{tilt}}

To begin, recall that our flow
\varphi
is fixed and in the form adapted to
W^{u}
. For the construction, we make the temporary switch to the orbit equivalent flow that is adapted to
W^{s}
. Since the stable/unstable foliations of these orbit equivalent flows are homeomorphic, we do not change our notation to make the distinction.

Informally, we set
h_{t}

to be the continuous flow on
M
defined by moving each point along its leaf of
W^{ss}
in the positive direction at unit speed. Here
W^{ss}
is the strong stable foliation of
\varphi
(i.e. the one-dimension foliation whose leaves consist of points that are asymptotic under the forward flow), the existence of which is guaranteed because the flow is adapted to
W^{s}
.
Then we will set
W^{u}_{\mathrm{tilt}}=h_{1}(W^{u})
.
To make this more precise (and to verify continuity) we work in the universal cover
\widetilde{M}
.

Recall that since
\varphi
is adapted to
W^{s}
, each leaf
\lambda
of the lifted foliation
\widetilde{W}^{s}
is a copy of the hyperbolic plane with the lifted metric, and these metrics vary continuously.
Define
\widetilde{h}_{t}\colon\widetilde{M}\to\widetilde{M}
to be the map defined on each leaf
\lambda
by moving each point in
\lambda
along the length
t
arc of its strong stable leaf in the positive direction. We recall that the leaves of
\widetilde{W}^{ss}
in
\lambda
are precisely the horocycles of
\lambda
based at the forward limit of orbits in
\lambda
.

Claim 4.1
.

The maps
\widetilde{h}_{t}\colon\widetilde{M}\to\widetilde{M}
determine a continuous, equivariant flow. Hence, they descend to a continuous flow
h_{t}\colon M\to M
that preserves the leaves of
W^{s}
and has smooth orbits.

Proof.

Since
W^{u}
is cooriented, the leaves of
W^{ss}
are oriented. Hence, the flow
h_{t}
is obtained by parameterizing the oriented foliation
W^{ss}
at unit speed.

Continuity follows from the properties of the adapted flow (established in
[
BFP23
, Corollary 5.23]
). First, the foliation
W^{ss}
is continuous. Indeed, we have a continuous, leafwise hyperbolic metric
g
on the leaves of
\widetilde{W}^{s}
, and in each leaf
\lambda
of
\widetilde{W}^{s}
the leaves of
\widetilde{W}^{ss}
are
horocycles based at the point
\theta_{\lambda}\in\partial_{\infty}\lambda
that is the common forward endpoint of the orbits in
\lambda
. As the endpoint
\theta_{\lambda}
varies continuously along leaves of
\widetilde{W}^{s}
(as in
[
BFP23
, Corollary 5.23]
), so does the horocyclic foliation.

Finally, again using the continuity of
g
, the unit speed parameterization of the oriented leaves of
\widetilde{W}^{ss}
defines a continuous flow of
\widetilde{M}
that is smooth on each leaf of
\widetilde{W}^{s}
(since the foliation by horocycles is smooth on the leaves of
\widetilde{W}^{s}
).
∎

Finally, we let
h_{t}\colon M\to M
be the induced flow on

, set

, and fix

the

4.2
Tilting the other way.

Let
\lambda

Given a point

\mathcal{O}^{s}_{-}(X)=\{q\in\mathcal{O}^{s}(p)\text{ on the negative side of }p\>\mid\>p\in X\}.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\gamma
be a flow line of
\varphi
in
\lambda

\Theta\circ\widetilde{h}^{+}(\gamma)=\mathcal{O}^{s}_{+}(\Theta(\gamma))

and

\Theta\circ\widetilde{h}^{-}(\gamma)=\mathcal{O}^{s}_{-}(\Theta(\gamma)).

Proof.

Let
\mu
be the leaf of
\widetilde{W}^{s}
containing
\gamma

4.2
, we see that
\widetilde{h}^{+}(\gamma)
intersects exactly the orbits of
\varphi

\mu

4.3.
The flowspace universal circles for

5.
Parameterizing the circle bundle at infinity

To understand the leftmost and rightmost universal circles for
W^{u}
, we need a much better understanding of the structure of
E_{\infty}
, and in particular what the set of markers looks like. In this section, we describe how to obtain
E_{\infty}
by gluing together a copy of
\mathcal{O}
with a copy of
\mathcal{L}^{u}
in a way that captures the set of markers as well as the
\pi_{1}(M)
action.

5.1.
Markers in
E_{\infty}

For a leaf
\lambda
of
\widetilde{W}^{u}
, let
\theta_{\lambda}\in\partial_{\infty}\lambda
be the common backward endpoint of all orbits in
\lambda
. The function
\mathcal{L}\to E_{\infty}
mapping
\lambda
to
\theta_{\lambda}
is known to be continuous (as in the proof of
4.1
), but this will also follow from observations below.

Lemma 5.1
.

Leaves of
\widetilde{W}^{s}
define
\varepsilon
–markers for
E_{\infty}
, for every
\varepsilon>0
. Moreover, these markers pass through every point of
\partial_{\infty}\lambda\smallsetminus\theta_{\lambda}
for
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}^{u}
.

Proof.

Pick
\eta\in\partial_{\infty}\lambda\smallsetminus\theta_{\lambda}
. Then
\eta
is the forward endpoint of an orbit
\gamma
in
\lambda
and we take
\mu
to be the leaf of
\widetilde{W}^{s}
such that
\lambda\cap\mu=\gamma
. Let
\tau
be a small arc of
\mu
crossing
\gamma
and let
m
the positive saturation of
\tau
under the flow. More precisely,
m\colon\tau\times[0,\infty)\to\widetilde{M}
is the embedding given by
(x,t)\mapsto\varphi_{t}(x)
.
The map
m
is a marker: for each
x\in\tau
,
m(x,[0,\infty))
is a geodesic ray in a leaf of
\widetilde{W}^{u}
since the flow is adapted to
\widetilde{W}^{u}
, and the height of each transversal is decreasing since
m
consists of forward orbits in a stable leaf (where the holonomy is contracting). This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

There is an
\varepsilon>0
so that for each leaf
\lambda
of
\widetilde{W}^{u}
,
\theta_{\lambda}
is the unique point in
\partial_{\infty}\lambda
that is not in the image of an
\varepsilon
–marker, and this point varies continuously in
\mathcal{L}^{u}
.

Proof.

We first show that
\theta_{\lambda}
is not in the image of a marker. More precisely, let
\varepsilon
be smaller than the separation constant of
\widetilde{W}^{s}
and have the property that if
p\in\lambda
is distance at most
\varepsilon
from some leaf
\lambda^{\prime}
of
\widetilde{W}^{u}
, then the leaf
\nu
of
\widetilde{W}^{s}
through
p
also intersects
\lambda^{\prime}
. We show that
\theta_{\lambda}
is not in the image of an
\varepsilon/2
–marker.

Suppose not, and let
m
be an
\varepsilon/2
–marker through
\theta_{\lambda}
based at an geodesic ray
\gamma
in
\lambda
.
Note that
\gamma
is also a (backwards) orbit in
\lambda
.
The marker
m
determines an arc
\partial m\subseteq E_{\infty}
(also called a marker) that does not cross the markers coming from the stable foliation, as in
Lemma

5.1
. Here, we essentially aim to prove that all markers come from the stable foliation, but for the purposes of this proof, we call such markers in
E_{\infty}

stable arcs
.

Since
\partial m
does not cross any stable arcs, and such arcs pass through all points that are not of the form
\theta_{\lambda}
by
Lemma

5.1
, then up to replacing
m
with a smaller maker and possibly replacing
\lambda
, either
\partial m
consists entirely of points of the form
\theta_{\lambda}
or there is an open stable arc
a
that agrees with
\partial m\smallsetminus\theta_{\lambda}
.

In the first case, let
\nu
be the leaf of
\widetilde{W}^{s}
whose intersection with
\lambda
contains backwards orbit
\gamma
. Note that the backwards orbit
\gamma^{\prime}
obtained by intersecting
\nu
with any leaf
\lambda^{\prime}
has its endpoint at
\theta_{\lambda^{\prime}}
. Hence, the arc
a
determined by
\nu
(starting at
\theta_{\lambda})
agrees with the marker
\partial m
. This implies that, for each leaf
\lambda^{\prime}
sufficiently close to
\lambda
and meeting
m
, the geodesic rays
m\cap\lambda^{\prime}
and
\nu\cap\lambda^{\prime}
are asymptotic. Hence, up to shortening the maker
m
, we can suppose that they have distance less than
\varepsilon/4
in each such
\lambda^{\prime}
. By the Anosov dynamics, the distance
in
the leaf
\nu
between the backward orbits
\nu\cap\lambda
and
\nu\cap\lambda^{\prime}
goes to
\infty
. On the other hand, in
\widetilde{M}
, one can pass from
\nu\cap\lambda^{\prime}
to
m\cap\lambda^{\prime}
, then to
\lambda
along
m
, then back to
\nu
in
\lambda
. This shows that the distance between the orbits
\nu\cap\lambda^{\prime}
and
\nu\cap\lambda
is at most
\varepsilon
in
\widetilde{M}
, contradicting that
\nu
is quasi-isometrically embedded in its
\varepsilon
neighborhood.

In the second case, let
\nu
be the leaf of
\widetilde{W}^{s}
that induces a marker containing
a
. Note that since we are assuming that
a
is a marker, the points in
a
correspond to forward orbits in
\nu\cap\lambda^{\prime}
.
Since
a
agrees with
\partial m
, and
m
is an
\varepsilon/2
–marker,
\nu
must also intersect
\lambda
and by continuity, we must have that
\lambda\cap\nu
has its forward orbit
\theta_{\lambda}
. But we also know that the backward orbit of
\lambda\cap\nu
is also
\theta_{\lambda}
, contradicting that the orbit is a geodesic in
\lambda
.

It only remains to prove that
\lambda\mapsto\theta_{\lambda}
is continuous. For this, fix
\lambda_{0}\in\mathcal{L}
and consider
x\in\partial\mathcal{O}
that is contained in the interior of the gap

\pi_{\lambda_{0}}^{-1}(\theta_{\lambda_{0}})
with the property that
x
is the endpoint of a leaf of
\mathcal{O}^{s}
that crosses
\Theta(\lambda_{0})
.
Then
x
is also contained in the gap
\pi_{\lambda}^{-1}(\theta_{\lambda})
for leaves
\lambda
sufficient close to
\lambda_{0}
. Hence
\lambda\mapsto\theta_{\lambda}
agrees with the (continuous) section
\sigma_{x}
near
\lambda_{0}
. Since
\lambda_{0}
was arbitrary, this proves the required continuity.
∎

With this in mind, we define the
nonmarker section

s_{\textbf{nm}}\colon\mathcal{L}\rightarrow E_{\infty}
by

s_{\textbf{nm}}(\lambda)=\theta_{\lambda}.

5.2.
The stitching map
\Phi\colon\mathcal{O}\to E_{\infty}

In this section, we introduce the key technical tool to understand the structure of markers on
E_{\infty}
; an identification of
E_{\infty}\smallsetminus s_{\rm{nm}}
with the flow space
\mathcal{O}
itself.

For this, we define the
stitching map

\Phi\colon\mathcal{O}\to E_{\infty}
as follows: First, let
\widetilde{\Phi}\colon\widetilde{M}\to E_{\infty}
be the map that associates each
p\in\widetilde{M}
contained in a leaf
\lambda
of
\widetilde{W}^{u}
to the endpoint in
\partial_{\infty}\lambda
of the ray
(\varphi_{t}(p))_{t\geq 0}\subset\lambda
. This map is clearly equivariant and constant on orbits of
\varphi
, and we let
\Phi
denote the induced map on
\mathcal{O}
.

Theorem 5.3
.

The stitching map
\Phi
is onto the complement of the nonmarker section and induces an equivariant homeomorphism
\Phi\colon\mathcal{O}\to E_{\infty}\smallsetminus s_{\textbf{nm}}
.

Further,
\Phi
identifies the stable foliation
\mathcal{O}^{s}
of
\mathcal{O}
with the foliation of
E_{\infty}\smallsetminus s_{\textbf{nm}}
by markers and identifies the unstable foliation
\mathcal{O}^{u}
of
\mathcal{O}
with the foliation of
E_{\infty}\smallsetminus s_{\textbf{nm}}
by open arcs
\partial_{\infty}\lambda\smallsetminus\theta_{\lambda}
for
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}^{u}
.

Proof.

For each unstable leaf
\lambda
, the only point of
\partial_{\infty}\lambda
that is not the endpoint of a forward orbit of the flow is the unique nonmarker point. This shows that
\Phi\colon\mathcal{O}\to E_{\infty}\smallsetminus s_{\textbf{nm}}
is surjective. Since no two forward orbits in
\lambda
have the same endpoint in
\partial_{\infty}\lambda
, we also see that
\Phi
is injective. Moreover, it follows that if
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}^{u}
with
\ell=\Theta(\lambda)
, then
\Phi
maps
\ell
homomorphically onto
\partial_{\infty}\lambda\smallsetminus\theta_{\lambda}
.

Next we show that
\Phi
takes the stable foliation
\mathcal{O}^{s}
to the foliation of
E_{\infty}\smallsetminus s_{\textbf{nm}}
by markers. Fix a leaf
\mu
of the stable foliation
\widetilde{W}^{s}
and let
m
denote its projection to a leaf of
\mathcal{O}^{s}
. Unpacking the definitions, the image
\Phi(m)
is obtained by, for each leaf
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}^{u}
that intersects
\mu

\lambda\cap\mu
in
\partial_{\infty}\lambda
. By
Lemma

5.1
, these are markers of
E_{\infty}
, and since they cover
E_{\infty}\smallsetminus s_{\textbf{nm}}
, every marker is the image of such a leaf.

Using this structure, we now prove that
\Phi
is a homeomorphism. Fix a stable leaf
m
of
\mathcal{O}^{s}
and consider the restricted circle bundle
E_{\infty}|_{m}
over
m
, which is an open cylinder in
E_{\infty}
. Here, we are also considering

as a line in
\mathcal{L}^{u}
.
The preimage
\Phi^{-1}(E_{\infty}|_{m})
is exactly the unstable saturation
\mathcal{S}^{u}(m)
in
\mathcal{\mathcal{O}}
, i.e. all points of
\mathcal{O}
whose unstable leaf crosses
m
. Since
\mathcal{S}^{u}(m)
is open in
\mathcal{O}
, it suffices to show that the restriction of
\Phi
to
{\mathcal{S}^{u}(m)}
is a homeomorphism onto
E_{\infty}|_{m}\smallsetminus s_{\rm{nm}}
. Actually, by invariance of domain, we only need to show that the map is open.

8.
Nonconjugate universal circle actions

Our goal in this section is to prove
Theorem

8.1

\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
is replaced by
\mathcal{C}^{r}
.

Theorem 8.1
.

Suppose
\varphi
is not
\mathbb{R}
-covered. Then the actions
\pi_{1}(M)\curvearrowright\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
and
\pi_{1}(M)\curvearrowright\partial\mathcal{O}
are not conjugate.

To see that the actions
\pi_{1}(M)\curvearrowright\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
and
\pi_{1}(M)\curvearrowright\partial\mathcal{O}
aren’t conjugate, we’ll study the dynamics of a group element
g\in\pi_{1}(M)
acting with a single fixed point in the interior of
\mathcal{O}
. Such elements exist; in fact, the fixed points of these elements are dense by
[
BFM25
, Lemma 2.30]
.
So, pick
g\in\pi_{1}(M)
that fixes a unique
p\in\mathcal{O}
as well as the leaves
\ell^{s}
,
\ell^{u}
of
\mathcal{O}^{s}
and
\mathcal{O}^{u}
with
p=\ell^{s}\cap\ell^{u}
. Up to replacing
g
by its inverse, we may suppose that it acts on
\ell^{u}
with contracting dynamics and
\ell^{s}
with expanding dynamics.

By
[
BFM25
, Proposition 3.7]
,
g
acts on
\partial\mathcal{O}
with four fixed points, the endpoints of
\ell^{s}
and
\ell^{u}
, and these fixed points alternate between sinks and sources. We will see that
g
acts on
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
with a single sink and a single source, so the two actions can’t be conjugate. To see this, we need to understand the dynamics of
g
on
E_{\infty}
.

Since
g
fixes
\ell^{s}
, it fixes the cylinder
E_{\infty}\mid_{\ell^{s}}
. To understand the dynamics of
g
on this cylinder, we use the stitching map
\Phi
. Keeping with our convention, we denote by
\lambda^{u}
the leaf of
\widetilde{W}^{u}
sent to
\ell^{u}
by
\Theta
. We have that

\Phi(\ell^{u})=\partial_{\infty}\lambda^{u}\smallsetminus s_{\textbf{nm}}(\lambda^{u}),

and
\Phi(\ell^{s})
is a marker. Their intersection,
\Phi(p)
, is a fixed point for
g
.
On
\Phi(\ell^{u})
we see contracting dynamics, and on
\Phi(\ell^{s})
we see expanding dynamics. The point
s_{\textbf{nm}}(\lambda^{u})
is also fixed, so
g
acts on
\partial_{\infty}\lambda^{u}
with sink source dynamics, where
s_{\textbf{nm}}(\lambda^{u})
is the sink and
\Phi(p)
is the source.

g

As
p
is the only fixed point of
g
in
\mathcal{O}
,
\Phi(p)
is the only fixed point in
E_{\infty}\smallsetminus s_{\textbf{nm}}
. As for
s_{\textbf{nm}}

g
are conjugated by the map
h\colon\mathcal{L}^{u}\rightarrow s_{\textbf{nm}}

h(\lambda)=s_{\textbf{nm}}(\lambda)

s_{\textbf{nm}}
is
s_{\textbf{nm}}(\lambda^{u})

g
acts on
s_{\textbf{nm}}\mid_{\ell^{s}}

z=s_{\textbf{nm}}(\lambda^{u})

It’s clear there are at least two fixed points of
g

\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
, those corresponding to the special sections

and

. We will show that any other point in
\mathcal{C}^{\ell}
converges to
s^{\ell}_{z}
under backward iteration by
g
and to
s^{\ell}_{\Phi(p)}
under forward iteration, showing that
s^{\ell}_{\Phi(p)}
and
s^{\ell}_{z}
are the only two fixed points. We do this by showing that it is true for special sections based at points on
\partial_{\infty}\lambda^{u}

The first step towards showing this is noting that near

s^{\ell}_{\Phi(p)}
is a sink, at least for special sections based on

Lemma 8.4
.

The line
\ell^{s}\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{u}
is properly embedded.

Proof.

Suppose not, so there exists

\tau^{u}\in\overline{\ell^{s}}\smallsetminus\ell^{s},

where the closure is taken in
\mathcal{L}^{u}
. Then
t^{u}=\Theta(\tau^{u})
is an unstable leaf not intersecting
\ell^{s}
, but that is a limit of unstable leaves
t_{i}
that do intersect
\ell^{s}
. The accumulation set of the
t_{i}
in
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
includes an endpoint of
\ell^{s}
in
\partial\mathcal{O}
and so

\ell^{s}
makes a perfect fit with
t^{u}
or a leaf nonseparated from
t^{u}
. However,
g
would then also fix a point in
t^{u}
and
this contradicts the fact that
g
acts on
\mathcal{O}
with exactly one fixed point.
∎
