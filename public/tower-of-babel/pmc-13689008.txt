Post-hemorrhagic ventricular dilatation and hydrocephalus in preterm infants: a five-year retrospective cohort study from a tertiary referral center
Authors: Hamide Barzegar, Elham Khorasani, Hossein Molavi vardanjani, Zahra Hashemi, Seyed Mostajab Razavinejad, Roya Oboodi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-hemorrhagic hydrocephalus (PHH) is a serious complication of intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) in preterm infants and is associated with increased morbidity and mortality. Although neonatal risk factors are well described, the contribution of maternal and perinatal factors remains inconsistent across studies.ObjectiveTo determine the incidence, clinical characteristics, and independent predictors of PHH among preterm neonates with IVH, and to evaluate early outcomes in a tertiary referral center.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included preterm neonates (< 34 weeks’ gestation or birth weight ≤ 1500 g) with ultrasound-confirmed IVH admitted to Namazi Hospital, Shiraz, Iran, between March 2019 and March 2024. Demographic, maternal, perinatal, and neonatal variables were extracted from medical records. PHH was defined as progressive ventricular dilatation following IVH. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of PHH.ResultsAmong 507 neonates with IVH, 154 (30.4%) developed PHH. Infants with PHH had significantly lower birth weight and gestational age and higher rates of maternal diabetes and neonatal meningitis.Each 100-g increase in birth weight was associated with a 24% reduction in the odds of developing post-hemorrhagic hydrocephalus (OR 0.76; 95% CI 0.7–0.83; p < 0.001).Maternal gestational diabetes mellitus/diabetes was a significant risk factor (OR 2.14; 95% CI 1.39–3.29; p = 0.001) Antenatal steroid exposure was associated with lower odds of PHH, possibly reflecting reduced hemorrhage severity(OR 0.53; 95% CI 0.35–0.80; p = 0.003).Mortality was borderline higher among infants with PHH (p = 0.05), while the need for surgical intervention was significantly higher in this group (p < 0.001).The primary clinical model demonstrated good calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.19) and acceptable discrimination (AUC = 0.75).ConclusionPHH remains a frequent and severe complication of IVH in preterm neonates. Introduction

Post-hemorrhagic hydrocephalus (PHH) in preterm infants occurs when bleeding from fragile germinal matrix vessels disrupts cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow, causing progressive ventricular enlargement and potential brain injury [1]. It is diagnosed by serial imaging that shows increasing ventriculomegaly after intraventricular hemorrhage(IVH) [2]. About 25% of very low birth weight preterm infants experience germinal matrix hemorrhage-intraventricular hemorrhage (GMH-IVH) [3]. In one study of 122 infants with moderate to severe IVH, 18% eventually needed a permanent shunt within two years [4]. Reported rates differ widely depending on infant characteristics and the quality of neonatal care [5, 6].

Post-hemorrhagic ventricular dilatation (PHVD) represents a dynamic process that may stabilize spontaneously, require temporizing cerebrospinal fluid drainage, or progress to permanent shunt-dependent hydrocephalus. Distinguishing PHVD from established PHH is clinically important because the severity of ventricular enlargement, associated symptoms, and need for intervention vary substantially among affected infants. Early identification of infants at risk for progression may facilitate timely monitoring and management [7].

The likelihood of PHH increases with higher IVH severity [4, 8], younger gestational age [1, 8], and very low birth weight [3, 5]. Progressive ventricular dilation or accelerated head growth also signals higher risk and guides management [4, 8]. Additionally, clinical complications such as sepsis, unstable circulation, or coagulation problems may contribute to worse IVH and greater chance of developing PHH [8]. In contrast to well-established neonatal risk factors, the role of maternal and perinatal factors in the development of post-hemorrhagic hydrocephalus remains controversial [9, 10].

PHH can aggravate brain injury through pressure effects, inflammation, and damage to developing white matter, leading to loss of neural progenitors in preterm infants [4]. Management with shunts or temporary drainage carries risks such as infection, malfunction, and long-term device dependency [11, 12]. Ongoing ventricular enlargement is linked to poorer neurodevelopmental outcomes, emphasizing the need for early detection and treatment to reduce hydrocephalus-related morbidity [3, 13].

Despite advances in neonatal care, uncertainties remain regarding the true burden of PHH, optimal timing of intervention, and short-term outcomes in different clinical settings. Existing data show wide variability across centers, suggesting that local risk factors and management practices may influence prognosis. Therefore, evaluating preterm infants with IVH in our center from March 2019 to March 2024, identifying those who develop PHH, those requiring neurosurgical intervention, and their early outcomes will help clarify current gaps and guide improvements in care. In particular, this study aims to explore the contribution of maternal and perinatal factors with previously inconsistent evidence, including maternal diabetes, PROM, and antenatal corticosteroid exposure, to the development of PHH.

Method

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Namazi Hospital, a tertiary referral center affiliated with Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, Iran, between March 2019 and March 2024. All preterm neonates with a gestational age < 34 weeks or a birth weight ≤ 1500 g who had a confirmed diagnosis of IVH through ultrasound were eligible for inclusion. Neonates with congenital hydrocephalus, hydrocephalus unrelated to IVH, major congenital or chromosomal anomalies, central nervous system malformations, or incomplete medical records were excluded.

In our center, preterm neonates with gestational age < 32 weeks or birth weight ≤ 1500 g routinely undergo cranial ultrasonography within the first three days of life for IVH screening. Infants diagnosed with IVH subsequently underwent serial follow-up cranial ultrasonography during hospitalization according to NICU protocols and clinical status. These follow-up examinations were used to monitor ventricular size and identify the development of progressive post-hemorrhagic ventricular dilatation and PHH. IVH was classified according to the standard Papile grading system: Grade I, hemorrhage limited to the germinal matrix; Grade II, blood occupying < 50% of the ventricular cavity without ventriculomegaly; Grade III, blood occupying > 50% of the ventricular cavity accompanied by ventricular dilatation (defined as anterior horn width > 6 mm); and Grade IV, defined as periventricular hemorrhagic infarction (PVHI), reflecting venous infarction associated with IVH rather than simple parenchymal extension of hemorrhage [14]. Infants with IVH underwent serial cranial ultrasonography during hospitalization. Post-hemorrhagic hydrocephalus (PHH) was defined as progressive ventricular dilatation following IVH, characterized by a Ventricular Index (VI) exceeding the 97th percentile for gestational age and/or an Anterior Horn Width (AHW) > 6 mm on serial cranial ultrasonography, together with evidence of progressive ventricular enlargement requiring clinical monitoring. PHH requiring neurosurgical intervention was defined as progressive ventricular enlargement accompanied by clinical or radiological deterioration prompting cerebrospinal fluid diversion procedures according to neurosurgical assessment.

Neonatal meningitis was defined as a clinical diagnosis of meningitis supported by cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis obtained via lumbar puncture and documented in the patient’s medical record.

Management of PHVD was guided by contemporary recommendations and included serial cranial ultrasonography with measurement of the VI and AHW. Infants demonstrating progressive ventricular enlargement underwent pediatric neurosurgical consultation. Clinical indicators, including rapidly increasing head circumference and signs of raised intracranial pressure (e.g., bulging fontanelle, widening cranial sutures, apnea, bradycardia, lethargy, or feeding intolerance), were incorporated into management decisions regarding continued monitoring and potential intervention.

Data were extracted from hospital medical records using a structured checklist and included prenatal characteristics (maternal age and comorbidities such as diabetes and preeclampsia), intrapartum variables (antenatal corticosteroid administration, mode of delivery, and gestational age at birth), and neonatal characteristics (sex, birth weight, Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min, IVH grade, presence of hydrocephalus, meningitis, sepsis, type and duration of respiratory support, duration of hospitalization, need for surgical intervention, and occurrence of retinopathy of prematurity). Short-term clinical outcomes were also recorded, including early mortality and the need for ventricular shunt placement.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate. Continuous variables were analyzed using Student’s t-test for normally or approximately normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test together with visual inspection of histograms, Q–Q plots, and P–P plots.

Univariable logistic regression analyses were initially performed to evaluate associations between individual variables and PHH development. Candidate variables for multivariable analysis included those with p < 0.20 in univariable analyses as well as clinically important variables selected a priori based on biological plausibility and previously reported associations with PHH, including gestational age, birth weight, antenatal corticosteroid exposure, maternal diabetes, Apgar score 1 and 5 min, preeclampsia, premature rupture of membrane, perinatal MgSo4 and IVH grade. Variable selection was performed using backward elimination.

Two multivariable logistic regression models were constructed. Model 1 included baseline clinical variables without IVH grade to explore clinical associations preceding hemorrhage severity assessment. Model 2 additionally incorporated IVH grade and was considered the primary model for inference regarding predictors independent of hemorrhage severity.

The final multivariable models included 17.11 events per variable (EPV) for model 1 and 15.4 EPV for model 2 (supplementary Tables).Missing data were minimal and no imputation procedures were performed.The results of logistic regression analyses are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) and P-values.

A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (IR.SUMS.MED.REC.1403.580).

Result

During the five-year study period, approximately 11,250 preterm infants (GA < 37 weeks) were born and/or admitted to our center. Among them, 507 infants developed IVH, corresponding to approximately 45.1 cases per 1,000 preterm infants. PHH occurred in 154 infants, equivalent to about 13.69 cases per 1,000 preterm infants.

The mean onset of IVH was 3.10 ± 0.77 days (range 2–7), while hydrocephalus occurred at 12.97 ± 2.61 days (range 8–20). IVH grades were distributed as follows: 51.5% Grade I, 13.2% Grade II, 30.2% Grade III, and 5.1% periventricular hemorrhage. Infants who developed hydrocephalus had significantly lower birth weight and gestational age and were more likely to have maternal diabetes, receive fewer antenatal corticosteroids, and develop neonatal meningitis compared with those without hydrocephalus.

Several clinical outcomes differed significantly between neonates with and without hydrocephalus. The need for mechanical ventilation was more frequent in infants who developed hydrocephalus (38.3% vs. 28%; p = 0.02. OR 1.593; 95%CI 1.069–2.376). Although the proportion of infants requiring CPAP did not differ between groups (33.8% vs. 31.7%; p = 0.65. OR 1.097;95%CI 0.734–1.640), the duration of CPAP support was markedly longer in the hydrocephalus group (3.47 ± 1.23 vs. 2.26 ± 1.01 days; p < 0.001. OR 2.749;95% CI 1.764–4.283).

Rates of pneumothorax were similar between groups (18.8% vs. 18.4%; p = 0.91. OR 1.028; 95%CI 0.633–1.670). However, infants with hydrocephalus had a substantially higher need for surgical intervention (37.0% vs. 9.9%; p < 0.001). Retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) was also significantly more common in the hydrocephalus group (46.1% vs. 26.1%; p < 0.001. OR 2.419; 95%CI 1.630–3.591). Mortality was higher among neonates with hydrocephalus (22.1% vs. 15%; p = 0.05).

Maternal gestational diabetes mellitus/diabetes was a significant risk factor, more than doubling the odds of hydrocephalus(OR 2.14; 95% CI 1.39–3.29; p = 0.001). Each 100-gram increase in birth weight (as entered in the model) was associated with 24% reduction in risk (OR 0.76; 95% CI 0.7–0.83; p < 0.001).

Mechanical ventilation showed an association with hydrocephalus (OR 1.70; 95% CI 0.97–2.96; p = 0.012). Other variables, including maternal age, preeclampsia, premature rupture of membranes, antenatal magnesium sulfate, mode of delivery, infant sex, sepsis, use of continuous positive airway pressure, pneumothorax, and ROP stage and zone, were not significantly associated with hydrocephalus in the final adjusted model (all p > 0.05). The clinical model demonstrated good calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.19) and acceptable discrimination (AUC = 0.75). After incorporating IVH severity, the model showed markedly improved discrimination (AUC = 0.97); however, calibration was less satisfactory, with a significant Hosmer–Lemeshow test result (p = 0.02), suggesting inadequate model fit.

Among neonates with hydrocephalus, 66 infants (42.9%) underwent shunt placement. Of these 66 cases, shunt malfunction occurred in 10 infants (15.2%), and shunt infection was observed in 20 infants (30.3%).

Discussion

IVH continues to represent a major contributor to neurologic morbidity in preterm infants, and the development of PHH reflects a critical escalation in disease severity. Our findings reaffirm well-established risk determinants—greater prematurity, very low birth weight, and higher IVH grade—collectively reflecting greater vulnerability of the immature brain and its fragile germinal matrix vasculature. Importantly, we identified additional modifiable and clinically relevant factors: maternal diabetes was associated with increased PHH risk. Conversely, antenatal corticosteroid exposure appeared protective. Beyond structural brain injury, our study highlights that PHH is a marker of overall clinical fragility. Affected infants demonstrated higher rates of secondary morbidities—particularly prolonged respiratory support and retinopathy of prematurity—emphasizing that PHH does not occur in isolation but rather within a broader phenotype of multisystem vulnerability. These observations underscore the need for comprehensive risk-stratification and early preventive strategies targeting both neurological and systemic outcomes in this high-risk population. Although IVH severity was the strongest predictor of PHH in analyses incorporating IVH grade, the resulting model demonstrated statistical instability with extremely large odds ratios and wide confidence intervals, likely reflecting quasi-complete separation. Therefore, these estimates should be interpreted as indicating a strong association rather than precise measures of effect size, and the primary clinical model without IVH grade remains more informative for perinatal risk assessment.

The association between maternal diabetes and post-hemorrhagic complications remains complex. In our cohort, infants born to mothers with diabetes showed a higher rate of PHH, suggesting that maternal metabolic dysregulation may contribute to more severe post-hemorrhagic outcomes. However, current literature provides mixed evidence. A multicenter study in very-low-birth-weight infants reported that insulin-requiring diabetes—likely reflecting pregestational or poorly controlled disease—was linked to increased risk of severe IVH, whereas non-insulin-treated diabetes did not confer similar risk [15]. Because diabetic status in our study was analyzed as a composite variable and information on glycemic control, diabetes subtype, and insulin treatment was unavailable, this association should be considered exploratory.

Conversely, several cohorts and pooled analyses have not demonstrated a significant overall increase in IVH among infants of diabetic mothers [9, 10]. Data on IVH severity are similarly inconsistent, with some studies indicating higher rates of severe IVH in infants of mothers requiring insulin or with gestational diabetes [15, 16], while another report observed higher IVH incidence in non-diabetic pregnancies [15]. Notably, in analyses focused specifically on progression to PHH, maternal diabetes has not emerged as an independent predictor once prematurity, IVH grade, and prenatal steroid exposure are considered [17]. These discrepancies underscore the possibility that maternal diabetes may contribute to risk primarily through accentuating underlying vulnerabilities—such as increased IVH severity—rather than directly driving PHH development, highlighting the need for more granular evaluation of diabetic subtypes and glycemic control in future studies.

Our findings that antenatal corticosteroid exposure reduced the likelihood of PHH are biologically and clinically plausible. Corticosteroids enhance vascular stability and maturity of the germinal matrix, thereby lowering the risk of severe IVH—the principal precursor of PHH [18, 19]. Evidence from multiple cohorts supports this mechanism: in very-low-birth-weight infants in Saudi Arabia, lack of antenatal dexamethasone was a strong independent predictor of high-grade IVH, while a complete steroid course significantly reduced severe bleeding (OR 0.219; 95% CI 0.087–0.546; p < 0.001) [20]. Similarly, Kenyan data revealed that antenatal steroid administration shifted IVH severity toward predominantly mild cases, with only one-third classified as severe [21]. Since severe IVH strongly predicts subsequent hydrocephalus, these protective effects likely translate into lower PHH risk. However, current literature has not directly evaluated PHH outcomes in relation to steroid exposure, and studies reporting hydrocephalus among IVH survivors have not stratified by steroid use. Importantly, no evidence suggests that corticosteroids worsen PHH, and systematic reviews describe their benefits in IVH prevention without identifying safety concerns regarding hydrocephalus [22]. Taken together, antenatal corticosteroids appear to mitigate PHH indirectly by preventing the severity of the initial hemorrhagic insult—an effect that warrants further investigation in prospective studies designed to capture both IVH progression and CSF-related outcomes.

An unexpected finding was the association between higher 1-minute Apgar scores and PHH development. This observation should be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect survivor bias, residual confounding, or correlations with other clinical characteristics rather than a direct biological effect. Infants with severe IVH who died early may not have survived long enough to develop or be diagnosed with PHH, potentially influencing the observed relationship.

This study has several notable strengths. It captures a five-year experience from the largest tertiary referral center in the region, ensuring inclusion of the sickest and most vulnerable preterm infants at highest risk for IVH and PHH. The use of standardized neuroimaging follow-up allows reliable identification of progressive ventriculomegaly and PHH development. By evaluating both risk factors and short-term outcomes, the study provides clinically actionable information that can improve surveillance strategies, timing of neurosurgical consultation, and family counseling. Importantly, this is the first comprehensive assessment of PHH in our center, establishing a benchmark for ongoing quality improvement and future research initiatives.

Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The single-center design may limit generalizability to populations with different perinatal care practices, and the absence of structured long-term neurodevelopmental follow-up precluded assessment of the full burden of PHH beyond the neonatal period. Due to the retrospective design, the temporal relationship between neonatal meningitis, PHH development, and neurosurgical interventions could not be consistently established. In addition, comparisons of mortality between infants with and without PHH may be affected by survivor bias, as infants who died early after IVH may not have survived long enough to develop or be diagnosed with PHH. Future multicenter studies with standardized imaging protocols and long-term outcome assessment are needed to validate these findings and clarify optimal intervention strategies.

Conclusion

Post-hemorrhagic hydrocephalus remains a significant complication of severe IVH in preterm infants, reflecting both the fragility of the immature brain and the complexity of neonatal critical illness. Our findings reaffirm established risk factors such as extreme prematurity and higher IVH grade, while also highlighting the potential influence of maternal diabetes and the protective role of antenatal corticosteroids. Infants who develop PHH experience a greater burden of comorbidities, underscoring the importance of early identification and targeted monitoring. By characterizing local patterns of PHH over a five-year period, this study provides valuable evidence to guide clinical decision-making in our center and sets the foundation for future research addressing long-term outcomes and preventive strategies.
