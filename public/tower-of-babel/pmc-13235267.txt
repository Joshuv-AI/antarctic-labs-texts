Ischaemic conditioning to improve patient and organ outcomes after kidney transplantation
Authors: Robert Stabler, Helen McKenna, Madelaine Gimzewska, Dan Harvey, Muhammad A Khurram, Kieran Mccafferty, Kristin Veighey, Jeremy Fabes, supported by the Cochrane Kidney and Transplant Review Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis is a protocol for a Cochrane Review (intervention). The objectives are as follows:To evaluate the benefits and harms of ischaemic conditioning on patient and organ outcomes in kidney transplantation.

Background

Description of the condition

Kidney transplantation is the best treatment for kidney failure that requires dialysis, improving outcomes and quality of life (QoL) and saving the NHS around £25,000/year per person [1, 2]. However, organ shortages have left over 9,700 people waiting over three years on dialysis with falling QoL and 5% to 10% annual mortality [3]. Meanwhile, organ demand continues to grow, and transplant failure causes 600 transplant recipients to return to the waiting list annually [2]. Transplant failure is multi‐factorial, resulting in progressive decline in function. The better the initial post‐transplant kidney (graft) function, measured by the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), the longer a graft lasts and the recipient remains off dialysis and the transplant waiting list [4].

Initial graft function is dependent on the severity of ischaemia‐reperfusion injury (IRI) during transplantation, where the organ blood supply is interrupted on retrieval (ischaemic injury) and restored in the recipient (reperfusion injury). IRI comprises multiple parallel and interacting pathways that lead to further tissue damage following an ischaemic insult. These include a local and systemic inflammatory response with immune cell activation, mitochondrial dysfunction, the production of reactive oxygen species leading to cellular damage, increased cellular permeability, and disruption of the vascular endothelium [5]. IRI mediates poor outcomes such as delayed graft function (DGF), acute and chronic rejection, transplant failure, recipient morbidity and death [6].

Maximising the function of a transplanted kidney is of utmost importance: lower kidney function following transplantation predicts graft failure, necessitating a return to dialysis or further transplantation surgery, and death. These outcomes lead to significant patient morbidity, deterioration in QoL, as well as substantial healthcare costs.

Description of the intervention and how it might work

Ischaemic conditioning comprises brief and repetitive cycles of sub‐noxious ischaemia and reperfusion of the limbs or internal organs to induce local organ and systemic protection against subsequent IRI [7]. Conditioning treatments may be applied to the organ donor, the recipient, or both, before, during or after transplant surgery and may be non‐invasive (i.e. blood pressure cuff) or invasive (i.e. arterial clamping) [8]. Treatments may be applied directly, modifying the blood supply to the organ to be donated, or indirectly, causing ischaemia of a limb or other structure, thereby inducing systemic effects. Indirect ischaemic conditioning, also known as remote ischaemic conditioning, is a specific form of conditioning in which treatment of one limb or organ can confer protection to a distant target organ, thought to be mediated through neuronal and humoral signals.

Ischaemic conditioning works through multiple parallel pathways that are still being elucidated [8]. A key mechanism involves inhibition of mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening through the PI3‐K/AKT/PKB pathway, which is necessary for cell death following ischaemic injury. Ischaemic conditioning also upregulates the expression of protective proteins such as iNOS (inducible nitric oxide synthase), COX‐2 (cyclooxygenase‐2), and anti‐apoptotic proteins via NF‐kB and other transcription factors. Other potential protective mechanisms include the induction of heat shock proteins, which help stabilise cellular proteins during stress, and the activation of the autophagy pathway, which removes damaged cellular components.

Why it is important to do this review

Ischaemic conditioning presents an opportunity to reduce IRI and thereby improve post‐transplant organ function (eGFR), extend transplant lifespan, keep people free of dialysis for longer, reduce organ demand, yield health and QoL benefits and reduce costs. Transplant organ longevity and its associated patient and population benefits are top 10 priorities as defined by clinicians and public expert participants as part of the James Lind Priority Setting Partnership for Kidney Transplant, and well‐represented in the current recommended outcome sets for kidney transplantation [9, 10].

While clinical trials of ischaemic conditioning in kidney transplantation have suggested improvements in death, graft loss and DGF, none have sufficient independent power to result in a change to clinical practice. The topic has been previously meta‐analysed, but with methodological concerns, and potential errors in data extraction and analysis that have limited the conclusions which can be drawn [11, 12, 13]. These analyses did not include data on long‐term outcomes, despite these being the main patient‐centred outcomes and published data being available in the largest trials. This has led to a lack of clarity around conditioning in kidney transplantation, leading to a failure of translation to clinical practice. Accurate analysis of long‐term outcomes is necessary to provide a transparent synthesis of the data and guide future research and clinical management.

Objectives

To evaluate the benefits and harms of ischaemic conditioning on patient and organ outcomes in kidney transplantation.

Methods

We will follow the Methodological Expectations for Cochrane Intervention Reviews (MECIR) when conducting the review [14], and PRISMA 2020 for the reporting [15].

Criteria for considering studies for this review

Types of studies

We will consider randomised controlled trials (RCTs) that evaluate a form of ischaemic conditioning in either the organ donor, recipient or both, in live‐ or deceased‐donor kidney transplantation. Studies may be available in full‐text or abstract form, or unpublished, with no restrictions on language or publication year. Open‐label trials will be eligible. We will include trials with and without prospective registration; where lacking, this will be highlighted and included in risk of bias considerations, especially for final reports published after 2010. We will not include quasi‐RCTs (assignment to an intervention using methods that are not formally random) due to the potential for confounding.

Types of participants

We will include studies of adults and children undergoing live‐ or deceased‐donor kidney transplantation. There will be no age, sex, or comorbidity limitations. Participants undergoing multi‐organ transplantation or re‐transplantation will be included.

When a trial includes a mixed population, we will only use the data for our population of interest. If these data are not available, either in the report or through contacting the corresponding author, we will include the study only if at least 80% of the sample size is from our population of interest.

Data collection and analysis

Review authors who were directly involved in a study included in the searches or review will not make study eligibility decisions about, extract data from, carry out the risk of bias assessment for, or perform GRADE assessments of that study.

Selection of studies

Two review authors (HM, MG) will independently screen each title and abstract identified in the searches against the review inclusion criteria. Any disagreements will be referred to a third review author (JF or KV) as required. This review will follow the PRISMA reporting guidelines [15] and include a PRISMA‐compliant flow diagram outlining the search and study selection process.

Search results will be combined in Covidence [22], duplicates excluded, and screened at the title and abstract levels, and then at the full‐text level prior to a final inclusion decision. Where material of interest identified in the searches is only available in a non‐English format, we will use DeepL [www.deepl.com/en/translator] or Google Translate [translate.google.co.uk/] during screening and data extraction. If the output does not permit a screening decision or there are uncertainties relating to study data extraction, we will seek support from a native speaker of the document language.

Data extraction and management

Data extraction will be performed using a digital form developed by three review authors (HM, MG, JF). We will pilot the form on three studies to ensure all relevant review data is captured. Two review authors (HM, MG) will independently perform data extraction, with post‐collection comparison to identify discrepancies, and a third review author (JF or KV) will review where necessary.

Data will be extracted to describe study publication year and authorship, study design, the trial population (sample size, number in each trial arm, proportions of live‐ and deceased donor transplants), intervention (type and timing of ischaemic conditioning, details of application and dose), control or comparators and outcomes.

Data required to complete a risk of bias assessment will also be extracted, including randomisation methods, allocation concealment, blinding, frequency and handling of missing data, protocol deviations, adherence to intention‐to‐treat (ITT), and selective reporting of outcomes [23]. The presence of a pre‐trial registration will also be recorded.

Where a review outcome of interest has not been reported by an included trial, we will attempt to contact the corresponding author (and others, where necessary) to obtain specific additional trial information. Where we identify abstracts, pre‐publication material, and other information that present data relevant to the review, we will endeavour to contact the authors for additional data.

Data on covariates of interest that might influence the size of an intervention effect will be collected to enable subgroup and/or sensitivity analyses. These include participant age, the presence of general anaesthesia or sedation at the time of ischaemic conditioning, donor or recipient diabetes mellitus, the method of ischaemic conditioning (direct or indirect application), the use of ex vivo machine perfusion of the kidney before implantation (normothermic or hypothermic), the use of normothermic regional perfusion during organ retrieval, the type of organ donation (live‐donor or after circulatory death or death by neurological criteria), and kidney‐only or multi‐organ transplantation.

Risk of bias assessment in included studies

Risk of bias in included studies will be independently assessed by two review authors (HM, MG) using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool [23]. Results will be compared and any discrepancies resolved through discussion with a third review author (JF or KV). We do not anticipate cluster‐RCTs or cross‐over RCTs, but if these are included, we will use the dedicated RoB 2 tool version with appropriate adjustments to the risk of bias assessment [24].

The effect of interest will be assigned to the intervention at baseline, regardless of whether the intervention is received as intended (i.e. effect). The risk of bias for each outcome will be assessed within the timeframes described in Outcome measures (early: zero to three months post‐transplant; intermediate: three to 12 months post‐transplant; late: 12 to 60 months post‐transplant). GRADE assessments will be informed by the risk of bias for each outcome in each predefined time frame.

We will assess the risk of bias for the critical outcomes of eGFR (intermediate and late timeframes), patient survival post‐transplant or death (intermediate and late timeframes), graft survival post‐transplant or graft loss (intermediate and late timeframes), transplant survival or participant death/graft loss (intermediate and late timeframes), DGF (early timeframe), serious adverse events (early timeframe) and participant‐reported outcome measures (early and intermediate timeframes), and the important outcomes of acute rejection and chronic rejection (intermediate and late timeframes).

Measures of treatment effect

We will present the risk ratio (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for dichotomous outcomes. Where the number of observed events is small (< 5% of the sample per group) and trials have balanced treatment groups, we will report the Peto odds ratio (OR) with 95% CI [[26]. We will express treatment effects for continuous data outcomes as mean differences (MD) with 95% CI; if the outcomes are measured using different scales or instruments, we will use standardised mean differences (SMD) with 95% CI. If study time points do not align, we will use data from the last time point within our specified outcome timeframes (early, intermediate, late) as specified in this protocol.

If adjusted data (for participant death and graft loss) are available for eGFR, we will prioritise their use. For graft survival, participant survival and transplant survival comparisons using time‐to‐event data, we will perform generic inverse variance meta‐analysis using hazard ratios (HR) and standard errors (SE) (log scale).

If we include cluster‐RCTs, we will use the generic inverse‐variance approach in Review Manager (RevMan) [27], using adjusted odds reported in the studies and calculating SE from the 95% CIs provided using the formula: SE = (upper limit – lower limit)/3.92. Where a cluster‐RCT could contribute data to a meta‐analysis, we will report data for all contributing trials using the inverse‐variance approach.

Unit of analysis issues

For parallel‐design RCTs with two arms, the study participants in each trial arm will be the unit of analysis. For dichotomous outcomes that may occur multiple times (i.e. acute rejection), participants, rather than events, will be the unit of analysis. However, if incidence at a trial arm level is the only data available (rather than at a participant level) then we will analyse data in this manner.

Where multi‐arm trials that contain two comparisons of interest (i.e. one form of ischaemic conditioning versus another form of ischaemic conditioning versus placebo) are included in the same meta‐analysis, we will either halve the comparison group data to avoid duplication or combine data from both intervention arms.

We do not anticipate any cross‐over or cluster‐RCTs, but if these are included, we will analyse the results according to Chapter 23 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [24].

Dealing with missing data

We will contact study authors to request missing or additional data, where necessary. Where it is unclear from a study report how missing data was handled, we will contact the authors for clarification. Where we cannot obtain missing data, we will report this as missing. All author communications will be documented in an appendix.

We will record the number of participants lost to follow‐up for each trial. Where possible, we will analyse data on an ITT basis, but if insufficient data are available, we will also present a per‐protocol analysis.

We will handle missing data using the approach discussed in Chapter 10 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews Interventions [26].

Reporting bias assessment

Literature searches will be comprehensive and, where we meta‐analyse 10 or more studies, we will construct funnel plots and perform and Egger's regression test to determine possible publication bias [28]. Selective outcome reporting will be identified through comparison of study protocol and study reports.

Synthesis methods

We will perform meta‐analyses when two or more trials are sufficiently homogenous in terms of population, interventions, comparisons and outcomes. We will assess for statistical heterogeneity using the I2 test, with an I2 > 75% supporting a decision not to pool data. Studies will also be assessed for types and sources of heterogeneity, either clinical or methodological, when deciding whether to pool data. Meta‐analyses will be performed according to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [26] and all statistical analyses undertaken in RevMan [27]. We will include all eligible studies, regardless of their risk of bias.

We will use a random‐effects model to pool the data, as it considers the variability within a study as well as among studies, and we expect that the treatment effect will vary between studies due to heterogeneity. The Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimator will be used to estimate between‐trial variance. The Hartung‐Knapp‐Sidik‐Jonkman method will be used to calculate a CI for the meta‐analysis effect estimate when there are at least three studies, and the estimate of heterogeneity is greater than zero. In other scenarios (i.e. in pooled analyses of two studies or when the estimate of heterogeneity is zero), we will use the Wald‐type method.

Where possible, we will combine data at common time points reported across trials; if necessary, we will group time points into early, intermediate, and late time periods.

If meta‐analysis of an outcome is not feasible due to a lack of data or clinical/methodological heterogeneity preventing data pooling, we will conduct a narrative synthesis following the Synthesis Without Meta‐Analysis (SWiM) guidance [29].

Investigation of heterogeneity and subgroup analysis

Statistical heterogeneity will be assessed through visual inspection of forest plots and using the I² statistic. An I² statistic of 0% to 40% will be of low concern, 30% to 60% likely represents moderate heterogeneity, 50% to 75% represents probable substantial heterogeneity, and 75% to 100% represents considerable heterogeneity [30]. Possible reasons for heterogeneity will be explored through subgroup analysis, depending on the number of studies available for each analysis. If we cannot identify the cause of heterogeneity, we will downgrade the certainty of the evidence due to inconsistency during GRADE assessments [31].

Subgroup analysis will be performed for critical and important outcomes, using the formal test for subgroup differences in RevMan [27].

Equity‐related assessment

Many of the interventions evaluated in this review are performed around the time of organ retrieval from a deceased donor. Equity of access to an intervention for deceased donor populations is clearly an ethically complex area. Donors and their families are likely to wish for the best possible outcomes for organ recipients and, hence, would likely want access to potential treatments that could improve outcomes. As such, it is important to assess equity of access for these patients. Furthermore, access to kidney transplantation is recognised as a source of healthcare inequity [32], and included studies may apply further constraints on the included populations (either live‐donors or recipients of live‐ or deceased‐donor organs) through inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Hence, we will collect, where available, data from included studies on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, age, ethnicity, spoken languages, gender/sex (as recorded), and geography of the included populations in each study. We will provide a narrative synthesis of equity of access to the included studies and how this may impact the interpretation of findings and population‐specific treatment effects, as well as summarising evidence of inequality in trial designs and, where relevant, its relevance to future research recommendations.

Review authors who were directly involved in a trial will not perform GRADE assessments of that study.
