Bivariate rational approximations of the general temperature integral
Alireza Aghili, aghili@iaushiraz.ac.ir, alirezaaghili@yahoo.com, Nadezda Sukhorukova nsukhorukova@swin.edu.au, Julien Ugon julien.ugon@deakin.edu.au

Abstract
The non-isothermal analysis of materials with the application of the Arrhenius equation involves temperature integration. If the frequency factor in the Arrhenius equation depends on temperature with a power-law relationship, the integral is known as the general temperature integral. This integral which has no analytical solution is estimated by the approximation functions with different accuracies. In this article, the rational approximations of the integral were obtained based on the minimization of the maximal deviation of bivariate functions. Mathematically, these problems belong to the class of quasiconvex optimization and can be solved using the bisection method. The approximations obtained in this study are more accurate than all approximates available in the literature. Keywords: Thermal analysis, general temperature integral, bivariate rational approximate, quasiconvex optimization, bisection method, uniform approximation Subclass: 90C26, 90C90, 90C47, 65D15, 65K10, 80A30



1
Introduction

Thermal analysis consists of different techniques, which are used for understanding and modeling the thermal behavior of materials. The analysis is often carried out in
non-isothermal conditions with a constant heating rate, and the Arrhenius equation is used for the reaction rate.

The rate of reaction,
d\alpha/dt
, depends on the temperature and the type of reaction, as given by the following equation:

\frac{d\alpha}{dt}=k\left(T\right)f\left(\alpha\right),

(1)

where
k(T)
is the rate constant and
f(\alpha)
is a function of
conversion.
k(T)
can be expressed by the modified Arrhenius equation

[
21
]
as:

k(T)=\left(AT^{m}\right)\exp\left(-E/RT\right),

(2)

where the term
\left(A{\ T}^{m}\right)
is the
temperature-dependent frequency factor,
A
and
m
are constants,

E
is the activation energy,
R
is the universal gas constant and

T
is the absolute temperature, respectively.

The simple form of the Arrhenius equation is a special case with

m=0
, that means the frequency factor is assumed to be constant.
However, for some solid-state reactions
m
tends to range from
-1.5 to 2.5
[
13
]
.

If
\beta
is the constant heating rate, then
dT=\beta\ dt
, and
it follows that:

\frac{d\alpha}{dT}=\frac{A}{\beta}{\ T}^{m}\exp\left(-\frac{E}{RT}\right)f\left(\alpha\right)

(3)

which, upon integration, becomes:

F\left(\alpha\right)=\int_{0}^{\alpha}\frac{d\alpha}{f\left(\alpha\right)}=\frac{A}{\beta}\int_{T_{0}}^{T}T^{m}\exp\left(-\frac{E}{RT}\right){dT}=I_{m}\left(T\right)-I_{m}\left(T_{0}\right),

(4)

where
T_{0}
is the initial reaction temperature and

I_{m}\left(T\right)
is:

I_{m}\left(T\right)=\frac{A}{\beta}\int_{0}^{T}T^{m}\exp\left(-\frac{E}{RT}\right)dT

(5)

The temperature integral in equation (
4
) is known as the
general
temperature integral

[
5
]
, and if we let
m=0
, the well-known

Arrhenius integral
will be obtained:

I_{0}\left(T\right)=\frac{A}{\beta}\int_{0}^{T}\exp\left(-\frac{E}{RT}\right){dT}

(6)

If we let
x=E/RT
, then:

I_{m}\left(T\right)=\frac{A}{\beta}\left(\frac{E}{R}\right)^{m+1}\int_{x}^{\infty}{\frac{e^{-x}}{x^{m+2}}dx}=\frac{A}{\beta}\left(\frac{E}{R}\right)^{m+1}g(m,x)

(7)

and

g(m,x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}{\frac{e^{-x}}{x^{m+2}}dx}

(8)

and for the case of
m=0
,

g(0,x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}{\frac{e^{-x}}{x^{2}}dx}

(9)

The integrals, in general form, have no analytical solution. Integration
by parts results in
[
13
]
:

g(m,x)=\frac{e^{-x}}{x^{m+2}}\left(1-\frac{\left(m+2\right)}{x}+\frac{\left(m+2\right)\left(m+3\right)}{x^{2}}-\frac{\left(m+2\right)\left(m+3\right)\left(m+4\right)}{x^{3}}+-\ldots\right)

(10)

The series expansion shows infinite terms, which means
g(m,x)
is not
an elementary function. Usually different elementary approximation
functions, with finite terms and different accuracies, are employed to
evaluate the integrals. In the literature,
g(0,x)
has been
substituted by several series, asymptotic, rational, or semi-empirical
functions. Deng et al
[
14
]
, Flynn and Wall
[
16
]
, Lyon
[
24
]
, Pérez-Maqueda et al
[
27
]
and Órfão
[
39
]
have provided excellent
reviews of these functions. The function
g(m,x)
has also been
estimated with different series, rational or semi-empirical functions
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
18
,
22
,
36
,
37
,
38
]

with different accuracies.

In this study, new rational approximations of the general temperature
integral are obtained based on the minimization of the maximal deviation
of bivariate functions. Minimization of maximal deviation is also known
as uniform (or Chebyshev) approximation.

The theory of univariate uniform approximation by rational function was
originally developed by Achieser
[
1
]
. Then the results were
extended by Loeb
[
23
]
to the case when the approximations are ratios of linear forms and the elementary functions of these linear forms are not
limited to monomials. This is also known as generalized rational
approximation. The procedure was extended by Diaz Millán et al
[
25
]

to multivariate approximation.

Rational approximations are viewed as a natural extension to polynomial
approximations
[
9
,
26
,
28
]
(just to name a few). Rational approximations are more flexible than polynomials and therefore are suitable for
approximating non-smooth and non-Lipschitz functions (functions with
abrupt changes).

The numerical methods for rational and generalized rational
approximation can be divided into two groups. The first group aims at
finding ”nearly optimal” solutions and their constructions are based
on Chebyshev polynomials (Trefethen
[
32
]
). These methods are fast
and efficient, but the solutions are not always optimal, especially in
the case of multivariate approximation. The second group of methods are
searching for optimal solutions
[
23
,
25
]
. This research is based on
the computational procedure, developed by Diaz Millán et al
[
25
]
.
This research targets multivariate rational approximations, while the
procedure from
[
25
]
is also suitable for generalized rational
approximations. The results of numerical experiments demonstrate that,
despite its simplicity, this procedure is robust and accurate. The
procedure is described in the next section.

2
Theory

The rational approximates, in this study, have been obtained to estimate the function
h(m,x)
,
the term in the last brace in equation (
10
):

h(m,x)={e^{x}}{x^{m+2}}g(m,x)

(11)

It was mentioned that for some solid-state reactions
m
varies
between
-1.5
and
2.5

[
13
]
, however, for pyrolytic reactions or gas combustion reactions, a wider range, between
-4
and

3.2
, has also been reported
[
20
,
33
]
. Therefore, we can assume that
m
varies generally between
-4
and
4
. On the other hand, Starink
[
30
]
showed that the majority of reactions occur in the range
15<x<60
and only the range
9<x<100
is of practical significance for approximation functions. But some reactions occur at
x
as low as
4.6

[
31
]
. Therefore, the intervals
-4\leq m\leq 4
and
4\leq x\leq 100
were used to find the approximation functions.

It was demonstrated in
[
25
]
that the objective functions (i.e., the
functions that are subject to minimization) in the corresponding
optimization problems are quasiconvex. The notion of quasiconvexity was
originally introduced by Bruno de Finetti’s
[
15
]
, a mathematician
who was working with the area of financial mathematics. The term,
however, was introduced later as a possible extension to convexity.

For
a given value
u
the set
S_{u}=\left\{X:f(X)\leq u\right\}

is called a sublevel set of the function
f
.
A quasiconvex function is a function whose sublevel sets are all convex.

The method of bisection for quasiconvex functions
[
3
]
is an algorithm for minimizing a quasiconvex function subject to convex (or linear) constraints or without constraints. The procedure terminates in a finite
number of steps and the solution is within a given precision of the optimal solution. The main difficulty of this procedure is the solution of the so-called convex feasibility problem, which may be a difficult
problem
[
2
]
. Diaz Millán et al
[
25
]
demonstrated that in the
case of rational and generalized rational approximation on finite grids, the constraints are linear and the convex feasibility problems can be reduced to solving linear programming problems and therefore can be
efficiently implemented and the solution is within a prespecified
precision of the optimal solution.

The problem of approximating the function
h\left(m,x\right)
with a rational function
r\left(m,x\right)
, where
r=p/q
, the numerator is a polynomial
p\left(m,x\right)
of degree

n_{1}
:
p\in\Pi_{n_{1}}
and the denominator
q\left(m,x\right)
is a polynomial of degree
n_{2}
:
q\in\Pi_{n_{2}}
can be formulated as follows:

{\rm minimize}~u

subject to

\left|h\left(m,x\right)-\frac{p\left(m,x\right)}{q\left(m,x\right)}\right|\leq u,\ \forall\ m\ \in\ [-4,4],x\in\ [4,100]\

The value of
u
represents the maximal deviation. For a fixed
u
,
the set of feasible points (that is, the
sublevel set
) satisfying
the constraint:

S_{u}=\left\{m,x:\left|h\left(m,x\right)-\frac{p\left(m,x\right)}{q\left(m,x\right)}\right|\leq u\right\}

(12)

is convex, and so the problem is quasiconvex. Therefore, the aim is to
find the smallest
u
such that
S_{u}
is nonempty. This leads to a
natural application of the bisection algorithm as follows:

1.

Set
u_{-}=0
and
u_{+}=\parallel h\parallel_{\infty}
.

2.

For
u=\frac{u_{-}+u_{+}}{2}
, solve the convex feasibility
problem
[
25
]
. If this
problem has a solution, the sublevel set
S_{u}
is nonempty, and so
set
u_{+}=u
. If the problem has no solution, the set
S_{u}

is empty, and so set
u_{-}=u
.

3.

Repeat Step 2 until
u_{+}-u_{-}
is below a desired accuracy.

At every step of the algorithm, it is easy to see that the solution to
the problem
u^{*}\in\left[u_{-},u_{+}\right]
, and
that the size of that interval is halved at every iteration. The
algorithm returns an approximate solution to the problem of
approximating the function
h
with a rational function
r
.

In this paper we are using a discretization of the intervals, and
therefore the set
S_{u}
is a polytope, delimited by a finite number
of hyperplanes (linear equations). The convex feasibility subproblem
solved at each iteration of the bisection algorithm can be solved with
any linear programming toolbox.

3.1
Accuracy of the rational approximation functions

To evaluate the accuracy of the obtained rational approximations, they
should be compared with the high precision values of the integral. The
relative deviation is defined by the following equation:

\epsilon=\left(\frac{g_{n}(m,x)\ }{g(m,x)}-1\right)

(14)

In the above equation,
g(m,x)
must be computed with high precision
by numerical integration. The intervals
-4\leq m\leq 4
and

4\leq x\leq 100
with the step sizes of
\Delta x=1
and
\Delta m=0.1
were used for evaluation of the accuracy of the
approximation functions.

The accuracy of the rational approximations to the general temperature
integral (equations in table
1
), was evaluated and the results are

m
and

. The maximum values of the relative deviations,

, and sum of squared errors, SSE, are

3.2
Application and comparison with other approximation
functions

The Arrhenius integral has been widely used for kinetic modeling of
thermal analysis of materials. For example, in a single step reaction
with relatively constant activation energy (
E
), to find the best
model for the thermal behavior of a substance, the functions

y(\alpha)
and
z(\alpha)
are evaluated by the following equations
and compared with master plots provided in the literature
[
34
]
.

y\left(\alpha\right)=Af\left(\alpha\right)=\beta\left(\frac{d\alpha}{dT}\right)_{\alpha}\exp\left(\frac{E}{RT_{\alpha}}\right)

(15)

z\left(\alpha\right)=f\left(\alpha\right)F\left(\alpha\right)=\frac{A}{\beta}\frac{E}{R}f\left(\alpha\right)g(0,x)

(16)

The Arrhenius integral,
g(0,x)
, in this equation is usually
estimated by the approximation functions.

Another example for the application of the Arrhenius integral is the
Vyazovkin’s advanced isoconversional method
[
35
]
in which the
activation energy is determined as a function of conversion. For a
series of runs performed at different heating rates, the

value can be determined by minimizing the following function:

\Phi(E_{\alpha})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\sum_{j\neq i}^{n}\left[\frac{\beta_{j}I\left(E_{\alpha},T_{\alpha,i}\right)}{\beta_{i}I\left(E_{\alpha},T_{\alpha,j}\right)}\right]},

(17)

where

I\left(E_{\alpha},T_{\alpha}\right)=\int_{T_{\alpha-\Delta\alpha}}^{T_{\alpha}}\exp\left(-\frac{E_{\alpha}}{RT}\right){dT}=\frac{E_{\alpha}}{R}[g(0,x_{\alpha})-g(0,x_{\alpha-\Delta\alpha})]

(18)

In this method, the univariate function
g(0,x)
is evaluated numerically or estimated by
the approximation functions
[
17
,
35
]
.

The general temperature integral has also been used in the literature
for thermal analysis of the materials. For example, an integral method
of combined kinetic analysis of reactions (ICKA) has been proposed by
Casal and Marbán
[
8
]
. In this method,

f\left(\alpha\right)=Z\left[1-\left(1-\alpha^{a}\right)^{b}\right]^{1-c}\left(1-\alpha^{a}\right)^{1-b}\alpha^{1-a}

(19)

in which
Z
,
a
,
b
, and
c
are parameters that depend on
the kinetic model. Then the conversion (
\alpha
) will be calculated
by the following relationship:

\alpha=\left\{1-\left[1-\left(\frac{abcZA}{\beta}\left(\frac{E}{R}\right)^{m+1}g(m,x)\right)^{1/c}\right]^{1/b}\right\}^{1/a}

(20)

In this equation,
g(m,x)
is the general temperature integral that
is estimated by the approximation functions.

As a consequence, it has been shown that the rational approximates to the general temperature integral obtained by minimization of the maximal deviation of bivariate functions are more accurate than all approximates available in the literature. 4
Conclusion

Application of the Arrhenius equation in the non-isothermal analysis
with linear heating involves the calculation of a temperature integral.
This integral is known as the Arrhenius integral if the frequency factor
in the Arrhenius equation is constant, or the general temperature
integral if the frequency factor depends on temperature. The
integrals have no analytical solutions and the researchers
often use the approximation functions to estimate them. In this article,
by using the minimization of the maximal deviation of bivariate
functions the rational approximations of the general temperature
integral with excellent accuracies were obtained and it was shown that new approximations are more accurate that all approximates in the literature.
