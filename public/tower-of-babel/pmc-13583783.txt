A transglutaminase 2 mediates the hemocyte proliferation through regulating mRNA expressions of CgCyclinA and CgCDC45 in the oyster
Authors: Liyan Wang, Jiejie Sun, Junyan Zhao, Lingling Wang, Xiaodong Li, Linsheng Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•An oyster TG2 homolog CgTG2 was identified with higher expression level in hemocytes and gills.•The expression of CgTG2 in granulocytes was higher than that in other hemocytes.•CgTG2 regulated the mRNA expressions of CgCyclinA and CgCDC45.•CgTG2 was involved in the regulation of hemocyte proliferation induced by V. splendidus.Keywords: Transglutaminase, Hemocyte proliferation, Crassostrea gigas, Immune response

Introduction

Transglutaminases (TGs), also known as protein-glutamine gamma-glutamyltransferases, are multifunctional proteins that possess both enzymatic and scaffolding functions [[1], [2], [3]]. There are nine members in TG family [1], which are all composed of four distinct domains: an N‑terminal β‑sandwich domain, a central catalytic core domain, and two C‑terminal β‑barrel domains. responsible for enzymatic activity [4]. TG2 is a key cell survival factor that drives epithelial to mesenchymal transition, inflammation, stem cell survival and stemness, invasion, and migration [5].

TG2 is a member of tissue/cytosolic transglutaminase widely identified in both vertebrates and invertebrates, which is composed of an N-terminal domain, a transamidation core domain, and two C-terminal domains [6,7]. Invertebrate TG2 homologs typically possess a conserved catalytic core domain and maintain the similar function observed in vertebrate TG2s. In Apostichopus japonicas, a TGase2-like homolog (AjTGase2-like) was identified as a typical cytoplasmic protein containing a Transglut_N domain, a TGc domain, and two Transglut_C domains [8], and the TG homolog from Macrobrachium rosenbergii also reported to contain a TGc domain [9]. SpTGase from Scylla paramamosain was found to be highly expressed in the hemocytes [11], Pl_TGase1 and Pl_TGase2 from Pacifastacus leniusculus were specific expressed in different hemocyte types [12]. Functionally, invertebrate TG2s have been found to play a critical role in immunity. TG in Eriocheir sinensis was involved in immune defense against Vibrio parahaemolyticus and Staphylococcus aureus via regulation of antimicrobial peptides [10].

TG2 regulates the proliferation of cells through mediating various pathways in vertebrates [13]. It has been reported that TG2 promotes cell proliferation, migration and invasion in cancer cells through the activation of the ERK1/2 pathway [14] and β-catenin pathway [15]. In invertebrates, there are relatively few reports to address the role of TG2 in regulating cell proliferation. For example, AjTGase2‑like has been cloned and shown to mediate coelomocyte autophagy and immune responses [8]. In Litopenaeus vannamei, LvTGI and LvTGII were found to regulate the proliferation of circulating hemocytes [16]. However, the detailed mechanism of TG2 in regulating cell proliferation remains unclear in invertebrates.

Oysters are globally important aquaculture animals, which not only play vital roles in aquatic ecosystems, but also contribute to the coastal economy of many countries [17]. However, the frequently outbreaks of disease have become a major constraint on the sustainable development of oyster aquaculture, making the investigation of immune defense mechanisms particularly important for disease prevention and control. The increasing multi-omics data and related sequence information of the Pacific oyster Crassostrea gigas has facilitated the study of molluscan innate immunity [18]. As the major immune effector cells and the central of host defense in oysters, hemocytes undergo dynamic proliferation and differentiation during immune response [19]. In the present study, a TG homolog was identified from C. gigas (designed as CgTG2) with the objectives to (1) investigate its expression in tissues and hemocytes, (2) elucidate its regulatory effects on the expression of cell cycle-related genes CgCyclinA and CgCDC45, and (3) explore the mechanism underlying its regulation of hemocyte proliferation. The results will be helpful for understanding the activation mechanism of TG2 and its function in regulating hemocytes proliferation in invertebrates.

Material and methods

Animals and sample collection

The Pacific oysters, C. gigas (about two years old) were collected from a local farm, in Dalian, Liaoning Province, China, and raised in seawater with the water changed daily at 15–18 °C for two weeks. All the experiments were performed following the animal ethics guidelines approved by the Ethics Committee of Dalian Ocean University. Different tissues (gonad, adductor muscle, mantle, gills, hemolymph, and hepatopancreas) were collected from nine oysters to examine the mRNA distribution of CgTG2. One hundred and forty-four oysters were randomly divided to two groups: a V. s group (received an injection with 100 μL of V. splendidus at 1 × 109 CFU mL−1 in seawater) and a SW group (received an injection with 100 μL of seawater) and the hemolymph were collected from nine oysters at 0, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72 and 96 h post-injection. The hemolymph from three oysters were pooled together as one sample, and there were three replicates for each time point. The hemocytes were harvested by centrifugation at 800 × g, 4 °C for 8 min. All the samples were stored at −80 °C for subsequent RNA extraction by using Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

The RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was extracted from hemocytes using Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the protocol. The extracted RNA was quantified by Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher, USA). The cDNA synthesis was conducted with the total RNA as template according to the direction of manufacturer (Takara, China) [20]. The reaction mixtures were incubated at 42 °C for 1 h and then terminated by heating at 95 °C for 5 min.

Reverse transcription quantitative-PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis of CgTG2

RT-qPCR was performed to detect the tissue distribution of CgTG2 mRNA by the specific primers CgTG2-RT-F and CgTG2-RT-R (Table 1). CgEF (Accession No. NP_001292242.2) amplified with the primers of CgEF-RT-F and CgEF-RT-R (Table 1) was used as control. The thermal profile for RT-qPCR program was 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, 60 °C for 31 s. The relative mRNA expression levels of CgTG2 were analyzed by comparative Ct method (2-ΔΔCt method) [21,22].

Sequence and structure analysis of CgTG2

Protein sequences of CgTG2 and its homologs from diverse species were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI, http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ebinet.htm) database, and analyzed using Clustal X v2.0 and GeneDoc software (http://www.nrbsc.org/gfx/genedoc/ebinet.htm). The domains of CgTG2 protein were predicted by the website of SMART sites (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/). A phylogenetic tree was constructed based on TG2 protein sequences using MEGA 7.0 software with the neighbor-joining (NJ) method [23].

Recombinant expression and purification of CgTG2

The cDNA fragment encoding the functional domain Transglut_N of CgTG2 was amplified by primers CgTG2-ExF and CgTG2-ExR with BamH Ⅰ and Hind Ⅲ sites (Table 1), which were designed according to the ORF sequence of CgTG2. The PCR procedure was conducted as follows: one cycle at 95 °C for 3 min, 35 cycles at 94 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 1 min (35 cycles), and 72 °C for 10 min. PCR fragments were inserted into the expression vector pET28a (Novagen, Germany) using T4 DNA ligase. Recombinant plasmid pET-28a-CgTG2-Transglut_N was transformed into E coli Transetta (DE3) (TransGen Biotech, China) and induced with IPTG. The recombinant CgTG2-Transglut_N protein (rCgTG2-R1) was purified by His-tag purification resin (Sangon Biotech, China) and pooled by elution with 400 mmol/L imidazole under denatured condition (8 mol/L urea). All experimental procedures in this study were conducted following previously established methodologies [24].

Antibody preparation and western blotting analysis of CgTG2

Six-week old mice were immunized by using the purified rCgTG2-R1 to acquire the polyclonal antibody according to the previous description [25]. The specificity of antibody was identified by western blot. The protein samples extracted from oyster hemocytes were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and then diverted onto nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST (20 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Tween-20, pH 8.0) for 1 h, and then incubated with 1/100 diluted antiserum against CgTG2 with 5% non-fat milk for 4 h. Membranes were sequentially incubated with primary antiserum (1:100) and HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:1000), each followed by TBST washes. Protein bands were visualized using NBT/BCIP substrate (10 mL TBS containing 45 μL NBT and 35 μL BCIP, Sangon Biotech, China).

Immunocytochemical assay and the flow cytometry (FCM) analysis of CgTG2

The hemolymph was fixed with an equal volume of 4% paraformaldehyde in anticoagulant for 15 min. Hemocytes were collected by centrifugation (600 × g, 4 °C, 4 min), washed, and adhered to slides. After blocking with 3% BSA (37 °C, 30 min), hemocytes were incubated with anti-CgTG2 antibody (1:300) at 4 °C overnight. Following PBS washes, the samples were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (1:1000, 37 °C, 1 h, dark) and counterstained with DAPI (1 µg/mL, 10 min). Slides were mounted and observed under a fluorescence microscope. The hemocytes collected from oysters were incubated with FITC-labeled V. splendidus at room temperature for 1 h. Cell morphology was analyzed by flow cytometry (FACS Arial II) based on FSC and SSC profiles as the previous report [26].

RNA interference of CgTG2

CgTG2 RNA interference fragment was amplified by PCR to composite cDNA of CgTG2 and EGFP with the primers (CgTG2-Fi, CgTG2-Ri, EGFP-Fi and EGFP-Ri, Table 1). The dsRNAs of CgTG2 and EGFP (Table 1) were synthesized by using T7 polymerase. Eighteen oysters were divided into three groups: TG2-RNAi group, EGFP-RNAi group, and blank group. The oysters in TG2-RNAi group and EGFP-RNAi group received an injection with 100 μL (1 µg µL−1) dsTG2-RNA and dsEGFP-RNA, respectively. The untreated oysters were employed as blank group. To enhance the RNAi efficiency, a second injection was conducted at 12 h after the first injection [27], and the RNAi efficiency was evaluated by RT-qPCR using primers CgTG2-RT-F/R (Table 1). After the dsRNA injection, the oysters in TG2-RNAi and EGFP-RNAi groups were challenged with V. splendidus. Hemocytes were subsequently collected for total RNA extraction, and the expression level of CgCyclinA and CgCDC45 mRNA was quantified by RT-qPCR.

The detection of EdU labeled hemocytes by flow cytometry

Eighteen oysters were treated with 100 μL of 0.2 mM EdU, and divided into two groups as TG2-RNAi group and EGFP-RNAi group to receive the injection with 100 μL (1 µg µL−1) dsRNAs of CgTG2 and EGFP. Another nine oysters received the injection with EdU were employed as blank group. The hemolymph from three oysters were collected at 12 h after the V. splendidus injection and pooled together as one sample, and there were three replicates for each group. The EdU-positive signals labeled with BeyoClick™ EdU Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) in hemocytes were detected and calculated by flow cytometry according to the protocol of the manufacturer [28].

Statistical analysis

All the data were shown as mean ± standard deviation. The two-sample Student’s t-test was performed for the comparisons between groups. Multiple group comparisons were executed by one-way ANOVA and followed by a Tukey multiple group comparison test using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) 20.0 software. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Molecular characteristic and phylogenic relationship of CgTG2

The full-length cDNA sequence of CgTG2 was obtained from NCBI database, with an open reading frame (ORF) of 2283 bp, encoding a polypeptide of 761 amino acids. The sequence was deposited in GenBank under accession number CGI_10001530 (LOC105334492). CgTG2 contained a conserved Transglut_N domain and a TGc domain in CgTG2 (Supplement Fig. 1), and it shared 38% to 62.8%similarities with the TG2s from other species (Supplement Fig. 2). Fourteen TG proteins from diverse vertebrate and invertebrate species were selected for constructing phylogenetic analysis, and they were clearly separated into vertebrate and invertebrate clades in the phylogenetic tree. CgTG2 were clustered with MyTG-like from Mizuhopecten yessoensis within the invertebrate branch (Fig. 1).

Phylogenetic analysis of CgTG2 with other TG family members from different animals using MEGA 7.0 software.

The distribution of CgTG2 mRNA in various tissues and hemocytes

The mRNA transcripts of were found to distribute in hemocytes and all the tested tissues including hepatopancreas, adductor muscle, gonad, gills, labial palp and mantle. The relatively expression levels were higher in gills, hemocytes and labial palp, which were 31.87-fold, 28.81-fold and 9.63-fold (p < 0.05) of that in mantle, respectively. The mRNA expressions of CgTG2 in adductor muscle and hepatopancreas were 2.57-fold (p < 0.05) and 1.32-fold (p < 0.05) of that in mantle, respectively (Fig. 2A). The mRNA expressions of CgTG2 in hemocytes increased significantly and reached peak at 12 h (4.19-fold, p < 0.01) after V. splendidus stimulation, compared with that in SW group (Fig. 2B).

The distribution of CgTG2 protein in hemocytes

CgTG2 positive cells (FITC⁺ hemocytes) were quantified, and the percentage of FITC⁺ cells among agranulocytes, semi-granulocytes, and granulocytes were 50.60%, 81.14%, and 88.17%, respectively. The abundance of CgTG2 was higher in granulocytes, which was 4.43-fold (p < 0.05) and 1.87-fold (p < 0.05) of that in agranulocytes and semi-granulocytes, respectively (Fig. 3C–D). The nucleus of hemocytes, stained by DAPI was observed in blue, and CgTG2 conjugated with anti-CgTG2 and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated Goat anti-mouse IgG was observed in green. The immunocytochemical assay was conducted by fluorescence microscope to detect the subcellular localization of CgTG2 protein in hemocytes. The positive green fluorescence signals of CgTG2 were mainly located in cytoplasm of hemocytes. CgTG2 was distributed in the three subpopulations of hemocytes, predominantly in granulocytes (Fig. 3E).

The proportion of EdU-labeled hemocytes in CgTG2-RNAi oysters following V. splendidus challenge

The EdU positive signals indicated the renewal rate of circulating hemocytes and they were determined by flow cytometry after CgTG2 was knocked down by RNAi. The newborn cells labeled with EdU (EdU+) were observed in green, which were mainly distributed in the CgTG2-RNAi group. The number of hemocytes with EdU+ signals in CgTG2-RNAi oysters were significantly declined compared with EGFP-RNAi group (0.64-fold, p < 0.05) (Fig. 4).

The mRNA expressions of cell cycle related genes (CgCyclinA and CgCDC45) in hemocytes were examined with RT-qPCR after the RNA interference of CgTG2. The expression level of CgTG2 mRNA in the hemocytes decreased significantly (0.50-fold of that in EGFP-RNAi oysters, p < 0.01) at 24 h after the injection of CgTG2 dsRNA (Fig. 5). The mRNA expression of CgCyclinA (Fig. 5C) and CgCDC45 (Fig. 5B) in the hemocytes of CgTG2-RNAi group decreased significantly after V. splendidus stimulation, which were 0.55-fold (p < 0.01) and 0.45-fold (p < 0.01), compared with EGFP-RNAi group.

Discussion

TG2 is a multifunctional protein that localizes to the cytoplasm, nucleus and plasma membrane in mammalian cells, playing a role in the regulation of cell proliferation in various animal species, from invertebrates to vertebrates [[29], [30], [31]]. However, the specific molecular mechanism about TG2 in regulating proliferation was unclear in lower vertebrates and invertebrates. In the present study, CgTG2 was identified from C. gigas with the aim to clarify its role in the proliferation of hemocytes.

TG2 is composed of four domains including an N-terminal β-sandwich, a catalytic core domain and two C-terminal β-barrel domains [32]. In the present study, CgTG2 was found to contain a Transglut_N domain and a TGc domain, indicating a conserved domain architecture shared with other invertebrate TG2 homologs. The Transglut_N domain, corresponding to the N‑terminal β‑sandwich fold, is structurally conserved across metazoan TGs and has been implicated in substrate and partner protein interactions. The TGc domain corresponds to the transglutaminase catalytic core, which underlies protein cross‑linking activities characteristic of TG2 and is essential for processes such as matrix stabilization, protein polymerization, and post‑translational modification [33]. In Penaeus monodon, the domains of STG II are organized serially as one N-terminal domain, one catalytic core and two C-terminal domains [34]. Phylogenetic analysis further demonstrated that the selected TG2 proteins were clearly divided into vertebrate and invertebrate branches, with CgTG2 first clustered with MyTG-like and grouped within the invertebrate branch. These results suggested a high degree of evolutionary conservation of TG2 and implied that CgTG2 may exert functions comparable to those of its homologs in other species.

TG2 is ubiquitously expressed in vertebrate cells and ubiquitously localized in multiple subcellular compartments, with its localization often determining its specific biochemical and cellular roles [35]. In the present study, the abundance of CgTG2 protein was observed to be at a markedly higher level in granulocytes than that in semi-granulocytes and agranulocytes. In C. gigas, granulocytes, semi-granulocytes and agranulocytes are characterized as three major types of hemocytes [36]. Among them, granulocytes are considered the main immunocompetent hemocytes, exhibiting relatively higher phagocytic capacity and greater production of immune effectors. Similarly, AjTGase2-like in A. japonicus was reported to regulate cell growth in muscle tissue under normal physiological conditions [8]. These results collectively suggest that CgTG2 might play a crucial role in granulocyte-mediated innate immune response in oysters.

TG2 involves in the regulation of cell proliferation through different mechanism across vertebrates and invertebrates [37]. In vertebrates, extensive evidence shows that TG2 contributes to the regulation of cell proliferation and growth. In hepatocellular carcinoma models, knockdown of TG2 significantly reduced cellular proliferation and altered key signaling pathways such as PI3K‑Akt and Wnt/β‑catenin, suggesting a direct role in proliferative signaling [38]. In invertebrates, TG2 was highly expressed in immune tissues like hemocytes, and its expression could be induced after pathogen infection [10]. TG2 was found to be localized in hemocytes and hematopoietic tissues in crustaceans and contribute to hemocyte homeostasis [25,39,40]. In the present study, the new born hemocytes were labeled by EdU, and the percentage of EdU+ hemocytes was found to decrease significantly after V. splendidus stimulation in CgTG2-RNAi oysters, indicating that CgTG2 promoted the proliferation of hemocytes in C. gigas. The above results indicated that CgTG2 was involved in the hemocyte proliferation under V. splendidus stimulation.

TG2 is known to regulate cell proliferation in vertebrates through context-dependent mechanisms, including the modulation of cell-cycle genes via Wnt/β-catenin pathway and ERK1/2 pathway [15,41,42]. CDC45 is a conserved protein required for firing of replication origins and processive DNA replication, as a regular standard of cell-division-cycle arrest phenotype [43]. CyclinA1 functions as an oncogene that controls proliferative and survival activities in tumourigenesis and chemoresistance of ovarian cancer [44]. Previous studies have shown that TG2-knockdown in human umbilical vein endothelial cells disrupts cell-cycle progression and alters gene expression of cyclin E and cyclin B, suggesting that TG2 may regulate these genes as downstream effectors of cell-cycle control [[45], [46], [47]]. In C. gigas, CgTNF-2 promotes cell proliferation and S-phase progression by inducing the expression of CgCyclin A and CgCDC45, demonstrating the regulation of CgTNF-2 in proliferation of hemocytes [48]. In the present study, the effect of CgTG2 on core cell cycle regulators CgCyclinA and CgCDC45 was examined to investigate the conserved mechanism in oysters. The mRNA expressions of CgCyclinA and CgCDC45 were both decreased after CgTG2 was knocked down. These results indicated that CgTG2 promoted the proliferation of oyster hemocytes by regulating the expressions of cell proliferation regulators CgCyclin A and CgCDC45.

In conclusion, CgTG2 with a Transglut_N and a TGc domain was identified from C. gigas. CgTG2 protein was mainly located in the cytoplasm of hemocytes with highest level in granulocytes. The cell proliferation decreased significantly after CgTG2 was interfered with RNAi. All findings collectively demonstrate that TG2 plays a critical role in promoting hemocyte proliferation, thereby offering valuable insights for the further investigation of TG-mediated activation and regulatory mechanisms in invertebrates.
