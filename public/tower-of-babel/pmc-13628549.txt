A distinct gene expression mechanism for d‐amino acid utilization in hyperthermophilic archaeon Pyrococcus horikoshii
Authors: Ryushi Kawakami, Tomoki Kawase, Taira Uehara, Haruhiko Sakuraba, Tomohiko Matsuzawa, Junji Hayashi, Toshihisa Ohshima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGrowing the hyperthermophilic archaeon Pyrococcus horikoshii OT‐3 in medium supplemented with d‐allo‐Ile instead of l‐Ile markedly upregulates the activity of broad substrate specificity amino acid racemase (BAR). In P. horikoshii genome, the BAR gene (PH0138) forms a cluster with PH0137, which encodes a putative transporter protein, and PH0140 that encodes a feast/famine regulatory protein (FFRP), involved in the transcriptional regulation of metabolic pathway genes. Here, we performed gene expression, protein–DNA interaction, and crystallographic analyses to elucidate the expression mechanism of the BAR cluster. Gene expression analysis revealed that d‐allo‐Ile simultaneously induces PH0138 and PH0137. Electrophoretic mobility shift assays demonstrated that the PH0140 protein binds to the PH0137 promoter in the presence of l‐Ile, but this interaction is disrupted by d‐allo‐Ile, identifying PH0140 as a d‐amino acid‐responsive regulatory protein (DARP). The crystal structures of DARP bound to l‐Ile and d‐allo‐Ile were compared with those of another FFRP family member, P. horikoshii FL11. Our findings reveal a dimeric arrangement of l‐Ile‐bound DARP resembling the DNA‐bound (open) form of FL11, whereas d‐allo‐Ile‐bound DARP corresponds to the DNA‐unbound (closed) form. These conformational changes result from subtle alterations in hydrogen bonding around the coregulators. Furthermore, cultivation in d‐allo‐Ile substitution medium only impacted the expression of PH0138 and PH0137, and not the other genes, suggesting that DARP regulates a more limited gene set than FL11. Cumulatively, these results reveal a distinct mechanism by which an FFRP homolog controls d‐amino acid utilization.Keywords: amino acid racemase, archaea, d‐amino acid, Pyrococcus horikoshii, transcription factor

Introduction

d‐Amino acids have been shown to have diverse physiological functions in vivo. In humans, d‐Ser and d‐Asp are associated with higher brain functions and the regulation of hormone synthesis, respectively [1, 2]. d‐Ala contributes to osmotic pressure regulation as an osmolyte in aquatic crustaceans [3, 4]. Additionally, d‐Ala and d‐Glu are essential constituents of bacterial cell wall peptidoglycan [5, 6]. Various d‐amino acids are synthesized by fermentative microorganisms, particularly lactic acid bacteria [7, 8]. Additionally, an intestinal environmental maintenance system has been proposed that involves d‐amino acids produced by gut microbiota [9].

A range of free d‐amino acids has also been detected in extracts from archaeal cells. Notably, Thermoplasma acidophilum, Thermococcus spp. KS‐1, KS‐8, Pyrococcus spp. OII, GB‐D, Desulfurococcus sp. SY, and Halobacterium salinarum exhibited high proportions (13–50%) of d‐Asp, d‐Ala, d‐Leu, and d‐Lys relative to their respective total amino acid contents [10, 11]. Free d‐amino acids within these cells are predominantly generated via pyridoxal 5′‐phosphate (PLP)‐dependent or PLP‐independent racemization of their corresponding l‐isomers by amino acid racemases. Both PLP‐dependent (Asp and Ser racemases) and PLP‐independent (Asp and Pro racemases) enzymes have been identified and characterized in archaeal cells [12, 13, 14, 15]. Furthermore, dehydrogenase activities related to d‐amino acid catabolism including d‐Ala, d‐Asp, d‐Ser, and d‐Pro have been identified in Pyrobaculum islandicum [16, 17]. Indeed, genes encoding various d‐amino acid metabolizing enzymes are frequently present in archaeal genomes; however, the specific physiological role of d‐amino acids in archaeal cells remains unclear.

Pyrococcus horikoshii OT‐3 is a hyperthermophilic archaeon isolated from a hydrothermal vents [18]. Recently, we investigated the l‐amino acid requirement and d‐amino acid utilization for P. horikoshii growth, reporting growth on media supplemented with d‐amino acid (Leu, Ile, Val, Met, Phe, Trp, Tyr, or Arg) instead of the respective l‐isomer [19]. Novel PLP‐dependent amino acid racemase activity toward Met, Leu, and Phe was also identified in the crude extract of P. horikoshii, leading to the identification and cloning of the enzyme gene (ORF ID: PH0138) and characterization of the recombinant protein [19, 20]. This enzyme, termed broad substrate specificity amino acid racemase (BAR), forms a dimeric structure with subunit molecular masses of 52 kDa and exhibits activity toward ten amino acids, including Met, Leu, Ile, and Phe (25.9–65.7 μmol/min/mg for the l‐amino acid‐forming reaction). BAR activity in the crude extract of P. horikoshii cells grown in l‐Ile‐deficient medium supplemented with d‐allo‐Ile is 34 times higher than in cells grown in standard medium. Meanwhile, BAR activity is not upregulated in the presence of Met, Leu, Phe, Val, Trp, Tyr, or Arg [19]. These findings collectively indicate that BAR facilitates the utilization of d‐amino acids for P. horikoshii growth.

Within the P. horikoshii genome, the BAR gene (PH0138) is positioned downstream of PH0137, which encodes a putative Ykvl family transporter. Directly downstream, in the opposite orientation, lies PH0140, which encodes a feast/famine regulatory protein (FFRP), identified as FL5 [21]. Together, these three genes comprise the BAR cluster (Fig. 1).

In the current study, the enzymatic activity and gene expression of BAR were analyzed under various growth conditions, revealing that gene expression is regulated by PH0140 protein binding to the PH0137 promoter in the presence of l‐Ile—a process inhibited by d‐allo‐Ile. Cumulatively, the results suggest that the PH0140 protein functions as a d‐amino acid‐responsive regulatory protein (DARP). Furthermore, the crystal structures of l‐Ile‐ and d‐allo‐Ile‐bound DARP were determined and their coregulator‐binding modes compared. This work presents a novel gene expression mechanism in archaea mediated by an FFRP homolog that is responsive to d‐ and l‐amino acid isomers.

Results

Determination of BAR activity under various cultivation conditions

The BAR activity in the crude extracts from P. horikoshii cells grown under various cultivation conditions was determined using a spectrophotometric assay with l‐Met as substrate. In addition to previous results [19], substitution of l‐amino acids such as Ala, Asn, Asp, Gln, Glu, His, Lys, Pro, Ser, or Thr in the l‐amino acid medium (containing all 20 l‐amino acids) with the corresponding d‐isomer did not increase BAR activity. This suggests that BAR activity increased only when l‐Ile was replaced by d‐allo‐Ile in the medium.

The time‐dependent increase in BAR activity was investigated by adding d‐allo‐Ile to the medium after P. horikoshii cells had been cultivated for 20 h in the absence of l‐Ile. An increase in BAR activity was observed within 1 h and continued for at least 12 h (Fig. 2A). In contrast, simultaneous addition of l‐Ile and d‐allo‐Ile did not increase BAR activity (Fig. 2B).

Gene expression analyses by RNA‐seq and quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT‐qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells cultivated for 24 h in l‐amino acid medium containing 20 l‐amino acids or in the d‐allo‐Ile substitution medium—l‐Ile in the l‐amino acid medium was replaced with d‐allo‐Ile. After extracting mRNA, the synthesized cDNAs were sequenced using NextSeq 500, and gene expression levels were evaluated using transcripts per million (TPM) values. The expression level of the PH0138 gene increased significantly from 23.3 TPM in l‐amino acid cultivation to 92.7 TPM in d‐allo‐Ile substitution cultivation (~4‐fold; Table 1). The adjacent PH0137 gene also showed a 6‐fold increase in expression from 12.1 to 71.8 TPM. These results suggest that d‐allo‐Ile simultaneously induced both genes, which likely function as an operon. On the other hand, the expression level of the PH0140 gene slightly decreased from 56.4 to 33.7 TPM. No significant changes were observed in the expression of other genes encoding functional proteins after adding d‐allo‐Ile (Table 1).

The effects of d‐allo‐Ile on the expression of PH0137, PH0138, and PH0140 were further investigated by RT‐qPCR. Expression of PH0137 and PH0138 was induced within 0.5 h after adding d‐allo‐Ile to the culture; their expression levels increased by more than 10‐fold within the first hour compared to those cultivated without d‐allo‐Ile (Fig. 3, Conditions 1–2). Although expression levels decreased slightly (condition 3), the genes remained overexpressed by 4–6 times at 8 h (unpublished data) and 24 h (RNA‐seq data) after induction. In contrast, no such induction was observed when l‐Ile was added after d‐allo‐Ile (Fig. 3, Condition 4) or when d‐allo‐Ile and l‐Ile were added simultaneously (Fig. 3, Condition 5). These results suggest that d‐allo‐Ile induces the expression of both genes, whereas l‐Ile suppresses their expression. RT‐qPCR experiments revealed no change in PH0140 expression (Fig. 3).

Purification of DARP and digestion by thrombin (Tb)

Initially, the expression vector, pET11a/PH0140 was constructed, and untagged proteins were used for the electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) and crystallographic analysis. Heat treatment of the crude extract resulted in a distinct protein band at approximately 18 kDa (Fig. 4A, lanes 1 and 2), demonstrating efficient production of the untagged DARP protein in Escherichia coli. While certain DARP was bound to the DEAE Sepharose column, a substantial portion did not (Fig. 4A, lanes 3 and 4). However, post‐purification analysis revealed that E. coli DNA fragments contaminated the untagged DARP (Fig. 4B), and neither ammonium sulfate precipitation nor streptomycin sulfate treatment removed these contaminants. Thus, His‐tagged proteins were used for subsequent analyses.

His‐tagged DARP (approximately 20 kDa) was overexpressed in E. coli transformed with pET15b/PH0140 vector and purified His‐tagged DARP without DNA contaminants was obtained by heat treatment and Ni2+‐chelating Sepharose purification (Fig. 4A,B). Preliminary EMSA demonstrated DNA‐binding activity with the untagged DARPs, whereas the His‐tagged variants lacked this activity (Fig. 4C, lanes 1 and 2). These results suggested that the N‐terminal His‐tag sequence (20 amino acids) inhibited DARP's DNA‐binding activity. To address this, the His‐tag was removed by Tb digestion, yielding Tb‐digested DARP with three additional amino acids (GSH), which restored DNA‐binding activity comparable to untagged form (Fig. 4C,D). The molecular mass of Tb‐digested DARP was determined to be 113–124 kDa using an SEC650 gel filtration column (Bio‐Rad, Tokyo, Japan) in the absence and presence of l‐Ile or d‐allo‐Ile (Fig. 4E), indicating that its oligomeric structure remained unaffected by ligand type.

Interaction between DNA probe and DARP

For EMSA, a region spanning −200 bp to +15 bp relative to the start site of each gene (PH0134, PH0137, PH0138, and PH0140) was amplified by PCR and used as a DNA probe. The PH0134 gene was selected as the negative control.

When Tb‐digested DARP and DNA probes were mixed in the presence or absence of l‐Ile and incubated at 70 °C for 15 min, protein–DNA interactions were observed only with the PH0137 probe in the presence of l‐Ile (Fig. 5A). Next, we examined the protein–DNA interactions at various concentrations of l‐Ile and d‐allo‐Ile. Equivalent band shifts were detected within the 0.1–1.0 mm l‐Ile range (Fig. 5B). In the presence of a fixed concentration of d‐allo‐Ile (0.5 mm and 1.0 mm), the band shift gradually disappeared as l‐Ile concentration decreased (Fig. 5B), though non‐specific bands appeared. These results indicate that in the presence of d‐allo‐Ile, the DARP protein does not interact with the PH0137 probe and that d‐allo‐Ile counteracts the interaction observed with l‐Ile.

The interaction between DARP and the PH0137 probe was examined in the presence of various l‐amino acids at 1 mm. Specific band shifts were observed for l‐Ile, l‐Leu, l‐Met, and l‐Val at identical positions but not for other l‐amino acids, namely Ala, Cys, Asp, Glu, Gly, His, Lys, Asn, Pro, Gln, Arg, Ser, Thr, Trp, and Tyr (Fig. 5C). With the exception of l‐Ile, these band shifts disappeared after the addition of 1 mm d‐allo‐Ile (Fig. 5C). The indistinct bands observed during interaction with other amino acids were interpreted as non‐specific. Furthermore, in the presence of l‐Ile, the band shift nearly disappeared with the addition of d‐allo‐Ile and d‐Val, but only slightly with d‐Leu and d‐Met (Fig. 5D). These results suggest that several l‐ and d‐amino acids are involved in the interaction between DARP and the PH0137 probe, with l‐Ile and d‐allo‐Ile being the most effective coregulators.

In addition to these amino acids, the interaction between DARP and the PH0137 probe was assessed with several non‐canonical amino acids: nor‐leucine, nor‐valine, and 2‐aminobutyrate. Band shifts were observed with l‐nor‐leucine and l‐nor‐valine, but not with l‐2‐aminobutyrate (data not shown). When l‐Ile was present, d‐nor‐leucine and d‐nor‐valine slightly reduced band shifts, similar to the effects of d‐Leu and d‐Met, whereas d‐2‐aminobutyrate did not have this effect. Given that the potential existence of these compounds in deep‐sea environments is currently unknown, these results can be considered part of ligand specificity.

Overall structure of DARP

His‐tagged DARP was used to determine the crystal structure. The structure of l‐Ile‐bound DARP was refined at a resolution of 1.39 Å (Table 2). The protein was then assembled into an octamer (Fig. 6). The asymmetric unit comprised one subunit with a solvent content of 50.8%, corresponding to a Matthews coefficient [22] of 2.5 Å3 Da−1. The model contained 10–158 ordered amino acid residues, one l‐Ile molecule, two sulfate ions, and 129 water molecules. The N‐terminal His‐tag sequence and residues 1–9 were disordered and not visible in the electron density map.

The structure of d‐allo‐Ile‐bound DARP was refined at a resolution of 2.20 Å (Table 2). The protein also assembled into an octamer, but the asymmetric unit comprised two subunits with a solvent content of 51.0%, corresponding to a Matthews coefficient of 2.5 Å3 Da−1. The model contained ordered amino acid residues 10–158 in both subunits, along with two d‐allo‐Ile molecules, two sulfate ions, two ethylene glycol molecules, and 41 water molecules. The N‐terminal His‐tag sequence and residues 1–9 were disordered and not visible in the electron density map. Although the overall structures of l‐Ile‐bound and d‐allo‐Ile‐bound DARP were highly similar, distinct conformational changes were observed (see below).

Upon submission of the l‐Ile‐bound DARP model to the DALI server [23] for identification of structurally similar proteins (as of July 9, 2025), the most closely related protein was FL11 from P. horikoshii (Protein Data Bank [PDB] code: 2ZNZ [l‐Lys‐bound structure]), with root‐mean‐square deviations (RMSD) of 1.4–1.6 [24]. The main‐chain coordinates of the DARP monomer were similar to those of FL11, including the dimeric arrangement of the octamer. The monomer folded into two domains: a smaller DNA‐binding domain and a larger oligomerization domain (Fig. 6).

For FL11, the octamer in the l‐Arg‐bound structure adopted a horseshoe shape with a gap (PDB code: 2ZNY), whereas the l‐Lys‐bound structure lacked this gap (PDB code: 2ZNZ) [24]. This difference was not observed between the l‐Ile‐bound and d‐allo‐Ile‐bound DARP structures, which exhibited highly symmetrical octamers, in which the four dimers formed a closed ring (Fig. 6).

Coregulator‐binding site

The initial electron density map for l‐Ile‐bound DARP showed extra density at the dimer–dimer interface. After construction and refinement of the peptide chain, an l‐Ile molecule was modeled unambiguously into that density. Since l‐Ile was not present during crystal growth, this molecule may have originated in E. coli cells and was retained throughout protein purification. The map precisely defined the orientation of l‐Ile (Fig. 7A), with its carboxyl group forming hydrogen bonds with the side chains of Thr141 and Thr143 and the main‐chain NH of Thr143 and Gly108* (*: residue in the neighboring subunit [subunit G]). The α‐amino group of l‐Ile formed hydrogen bonds with the side chain of Asp111* and the main‐chain carbonyl groups of Val106*, Gly108*, and His110*. The side chain of l‐Ile formed a hydrophobic core, primary with Leu121, Ile128, Leu129, Val87*, and Ile105*.

In the initial electron density map of d‐allo‐Ile‐bound DARP, a clear electron density corresponding to the d‐allo‐Ile molecule was observed at the coregulator‐binding site. A comparison of the structures of the l‐Ile‐ and d‐allo‐Ile‐bound DARP revealed that the carboxyl group and side chain of d‐allo‐Ile occupied nearly the same positions as those of l‐Ile. The hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions around these moieties in l‐Ile were conserved in the d‐allo‐Ile‐bound structure (Fig. 7B). In contrast, the positions of the α‐amino groups of the ligands differed between the two structures. The N atom of d‐allo‐Ile rotated clockwise around the Cα atom by approximately 83° relative to that of l‐Ile. As described above, all hydrogen bonds that stabilized the α‐amino group of l‐Ile were donated by residues in the neighboring subunit (subunit G). In contrast, the α‐amino group of d‐allo‐Ile formed a hydrogen bond with the main‐chain carbonyl of Thr141 in the same subunit (subunit A), in addition to interacting with the side chain of Asp111*. This replacement of hydrogen bonds may be responsible for the conformational change of the dimer as a functional unit (see below).

His‐tagged DARP failed to bind DNA regardless of the presence of l‐Ile in the EMSA reaction buffer. In contrast, Tb‐digested DARP did bind to DNA in the presence, but not in the absence of l‐Ile (Fig. 4C). Clear electron density corresponding to the l‐Ile ligand was observed in the His‐tagged DARP structure (Fig. 8A). However, preliminary structural analysis of Tb‐digested DARP revealed no electron density for l‐Ile (Fig. 8B), suggesting that l‐Ile dissociated from the protein following removal of the His‐tag sequence.

Discussion

Postulated expression mechanism of BAR and PH0137 genes

This study found that in P. horikoshii, the PH0138 and PH0137 genes are simultaneously expressed in the presence of d‐allo‐Ile when l‐Ile is absent, but not when other l‐amino acids are limited; l‐Ile represses its expression. Gene expression levels increased significantly during the first hour, then decreased to approximately 50% of the original level. This observation suggests that l‐Ile, which is ultimately synthesized by BAR, mitigates this effect. Therefore, it is necessary to determine the concentrations of l‐Ile and d‐allo‐Ile in P. horikoshii cells grown under each condition. The PH0140 gene also forms part of the BAR cluster, but it was not induced in the analysis, suggesting that DARP controls the PH0137 and PH0138 genes via mechanisms distinct from those of other FFRPs. Thus, P. horikoshii might utilize l‐Ile as a strategic amino acid for adaptation to nutrient fluctuation, in addition to l‐Lys (as described later).

EMSA results showed that DARP specifically bound to the PH0137 promoter region in the presence of l‐Ile. While direct evidence is lacking, it appears that when l‐Ile is present, DARP binds to the PH0137 promoter and likely represses the transcription of the PH0137 and PH0138 genes. In contrast, when d‐allo‐Ile is available, DARP releases from DNA, allowing transcription to proceed.

The PH0137 protein was identified via BLAST (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) as a probable member of the YkvI transporter family. Additionally, analysis of the SOSUI program (https://harrier.nagahama‐i‐bio.ac.jp/sosui/) [27] indicated that it is a membrane protein possessing 10 transmembrane regions. These findings suggest that PH0137 may act as an amino acid transporter, facilitating the import of d‐amino acids into the cell. Accordingly, the following scenarios are possible (Fig. 9): (a) When l‐Ile is largely present in the environment (feast mode) and available for the cells, it binds to DARP. The complexed form of the regulatory protein subsequently binds the PH0137 promoter, repressing PH0137 and PH0138 expression. (b) When l‐Ile is depleted, and d‐allo‐Ile is present in the environment (famine mode), d‐allo‐Ile is taken up by the cell through the PH0137 protein expressed at a basal level. (c) The binding of d‐allo‐Ile to DARP causes DARP to be released from the PH0137 promoter, effectively turning on the d‐amino acid utilization switch via transcription of the PH0137 and PH0138. However, the environmental origin of d‐allo‐Ile remains unclear. Further information will become available in the future.

Proposed regulatory mechanism of the broad substrate specificity amino acid racemase (BAR) gene cluster. In feast mode, l‐amino acids (squares) in the medium are incorporated into cells via l‐amino acid transporter. Under these conditions, d‐amino acid‐responsive regulatory protein (DARP) selectively binds to l‐Ile. l‐Ile‐bound DARP binds to PH0137 promoter, repressing PH0137 and PH0138 expression. In the absence of l‐Ile (famine mode), d‐amino acids (circles) in the medium are incorporated into cells via the PH0137 protein (putative d‐amino acid transporter). When d‐allo‐Ile binds to DARP, DARP is released from the PH0137 promoter, effectively turning on the transcription of the PH0137 and PH0138 genes. Overexpressed BAR and PH0137 proteins accelerate the incorporation of d‐amino acids and racemization to l‐amino acids.

The BAR cluster containing the DARP was exclusively present in P. abyssi, Pyrococcus sp. NA2, and P. horikoshii. DARP homologs PAB2257 and PNA2_0668 show approximately 90% similarity to P. horikoshii's DARP, suggesting possible responsiveness to d‐amino acids. However, this has not been experimentally validated. Hence, future studies should employ in vitro DNA‐binding or reporter‐based transcriptional assays with various d‐amino acids, as well as structural analyses to identify the molecular determinants of ligand specificity.

Structural insight into coregulator‐binding modes

The structures of P. horikoshii FL11 in complex with a DNA duplex (PDB code: 2E1C) and in the DNA‐unbound form (PDB code: 1RI7) have been reported [28, 29]. Comparison of the FL11 dimer in the DNA‐bound and DNA‐unbound forms showed that the distance between the two subunits in the former was larger than that in the latter. This open form may maintain DNA binding in the bent conformation (Fig. 10A). In DNA‐bound structures, Leu24, Arg25, Ala34, Glu35, Ser36, Thr37, His39, and Arg41 make chemical contacts with DNA through hydrogen bonds or hydrophobic interactions [29]. We observed that the distances between the Cα atoms of these residues were reduced in the DNA‐unbound (closed) dimer compared with the DNA‐bound (open) dimer: 0.51–2.23 Å (Fig. 10A). In the DARP structure, Glu35, Ser36, Thr37, and Arg41 in FL11 corresponded to Glu42, Ser43, Thr44, and Arg48, respectively. Although Leu24, Arg25, Ala34, and His39 were replaced with Tyr31, Ser32, Pro41, and Arg46, respectively, the Cα atoms of the corresponding residues occupied similar positions. Among these residues, the interatomic distances between the Cα atoms in the dimer were all reduced in the d‐allo‐Ile‐bound structure compared with the l‐Ile‐bound structure (reductions: 0.28–1.71 Å) (Fig. 10B). These observations indicate that the dimeric arrangement of l‐Ile‐bound DARP resembles that of the DNA‐bound form of FL11 (open form), whereas that of d‐allo‐Ile‐bound DARP corresponds to the DNA‐unbound form (closed form).

Functional difference between DARP and FL11

FFRPs are major transcription factors in archaea and are homologs of the leucine‐responsive regulatory protein (Lrp) in E. coli [30, 31, 32]. By sensing rich nutrition through high concentrations of l‐Leu [33], E. coli shifts its metabolism from famine to feast mode, thereby regulating 40–70 transcription units [34, 35]. To summarize this global regulation, the term feast/famine regulation was introduced [34], and the Lrp homologs are referred to as “feast/famine regulatory proteins (FFRPs).” Fourteen FFRPs have been reported in P. horikoshii to date [21, 32]. They are divided into 11 full‐length types (FL1–FL11), which contain both DNA‐binding and assembly domains, and three demi types (DM1–DM3), which lack the DNA‐binding domain [28]. FL11 comprises 151 amino acids, has a molecular mass of approximately 18 kDa, and primarily regulates various metabolic processes, such as ATP synthesis, transmembrane transport, and DNA synthesis by altering the association states of the dimer and octamer in response to l‐Lys concentrations in the cells [21]. In nutrient‐rich conditions with abundant l‐Lys, FL11 forms an octamer through the association of four dimers [36]. This octamer preferentially binds to the fl11 promoter and inhibits its transcription. The consequent decrease in FL11 levels releases the repression of roughly 200 transcriptional units, thereby stimulating metabolic activity [29]. When l‐Lys becomes scarce under famine conditions, the octamer dissociates into dimers [36]. This change weakens repression of fl11 transcription, allowing FL11 dimers to accumulate. Elevated levels of FL11 dimers then repress the transcription of many genes, leading to growth arrest [29].

The DARP is an FFRP designated FL5 [21] and has the highest sequence identity (38%) with FL11 among the FFRP homologs. However, the function of DARP observed in the present study differed from that of FL11. The expression of genes other than PH0138 and PH0137 did not change in the presence or absence of d‐allo‐Ile (Table 1). This indicated that DARP, unlike FL11, regulates a limited number of genes. Furthermore, FL11 regulates its own expression by binding the fl11 promoter, whereas DARP binds to the PH0137 promoter but does not regulate its own expression. Additionally, the association and dissociation of oligomeric structures observed in FL11 do not likely occur in DARP (Fig. 4E) [36].

l‐Phe, l‐Ile, l‐Leu, l‐Val, and l‐Met have been reported as potential ligands for FL5 [21]. Although weaker than that of l‐Ile, the DNA‐binding activity of DARP was also observed in the presence of the l‐isomers of Leu, Met, and Val and was suppressed in the presence of their d‐isomers (Fig. 5C,D). This suggested that PH0138 and PH0137 expression could also be induced by culturing P. horikoshii with these d‐amino acids. When P. horikoshii was grown in the absence of l‐Ile and induced with d‐Val for 1 h, BAR gene expression increased 2.5‐fold (data not shown). In addition, the specific activity of BAR in the crude extracts increased 15‐fold when induced with d‐Val for 8 h (data not shown). Amino acid concentrations used in the cultivation experiments were 0.5–1.3 mm. In contrast, amino acid concentrations are below μm levels, and d/l ratios of Asp, Glu, and Ala are quite low in water samples from deep‐sea hydrothermal vents [37]. Several hyperthermophilic archaea including P. horikoshii exhibit no or weak growth due to the lack of hydrophobic and aromatic amino acids in the medium [19], suggesting they require a nutrient‐rich environment for growth. Although investigating the source of d‐amino acids in these environments is important, racemic amino acids are believed to form in deep‐sea hotspots where P. horikoshii was found [37]; therefore, these organisms may have developed an on/off switch mechanism for d‐amino acids utilization via DARP. This represents a novel gene expression mechanism in d‐amino acid metabolism regulated by an FFRP homolog.

Cumulatively, the results of this study provide novel insights into the physiological roles of d‐amino acids and their metabolic enzymes in archaeal cells. While this study provides a potential mechanistic explanation for d‐allo‐Ile–dependent regulation, its physiological significance is not yet clear and warrants additional investigation under natural environmental conditions.

Experimental procedures

Growth of P. horikoshii

P. horikoshii OT‐3 strain was grown at 90 °C for 18–24 h under anaerobic conditions. Standard medium containing tryptone and yeast extract was used for pre‐pre‐cultivation and defined medium was used for pre‐ and main cultivation, as previously described [19]. Yeast extract (0.1 g/L) was added to the medium without l‐Ile. Cultured cells were collected by centrifugation (7000 × g , 10 min) washed twice with 3% NaCl, and used for subsequent experiments.

Determination of the BAR activity and protein concentration

P. horikoshii cells were suspended in 10 mm HEPES (pH 7.5), and crude extracts were prepared by sonication. BAR activity was spectrophotometrically assayed as previously described using a U‐2900 spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) [19]. In this assay, l‐Met was routinely used as the substrate. Protein concentration was determined using a Protein Assay (Bio‐Rad) with bovine serum albumin as the standard.

RNA‐seq analysis

P. horikoshii was cultivated in defined medium containing 20 types of l‐amino acids (24 h) and in a substitution medium in which l‐Ile was replaced with d‐allo‐Ile (24 h) (d‐allo‐Ile substitution medium). Cells collected from 400 mL of culture were used for total RNA extraction with the RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen, Tokyo, Japan). Subsequently, ribosomal RNA in 1 μg of total RNA was removed using the NEBNext rRNA Depletion Kit (New England Biolabs, Tokyo, Japan). cDNA was synthesized with a TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, Tokyo, Japan) and analyzed using a NextSeq 500 (Illumina). RNA‐seq reads were trimmed with the CLC Genomics Workbench 11 (Qiagen). Gene expression levels were calculated as TPM.

RT‐qPCR

For RT‐qPCR, after extracting total RNA, cDNA was synthesized using ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix (Toyobo, Tokyo, Japan). The reaction mixture (10 μL) containing 5 × RT Master Mix and 1 μg of total RNA that had been preincubated at 60 °C for 5 min was prepared, and reverse transcription and enzyme denaturation were performed as follows: 37 °C for 15 min, 50 °C for 5 min, and 98 °C for 5 min using a thermal cycler (Bio‐Rad).

qPCR analysis was performed with KOD SYBR qPCR Mix (Toyobo). The reaction mixture containing 2 × qPCR Master Mix, 0.2 μm each primer set (Table 3), and cDNA in a total volume of 20 μL was prepared, and the reaction was performed as follows: one cycle of 98 °C for 2 min and 40 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 10 s, and 68 °C for 30 s. Specific amplification of the target was confirmed by melting curve analysis. 23S rRNA was used as the reference gene for normalization of gene expression. Primer sequences were designed using Primer‐BLAST (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer‐blast/) to amplify the 100–150 bp of fragment. Three different cDNA concentrations were used to confirm PCR efficiency.

Expression and purification of DARP and digestion with thrombin

DARP was produced in recombinant E. coli. The PH0140 gene was PCR‐amplified from P. horikoshii genomic DNA using PH0140‐NdeI and PH0140‐BamHI primers (Table 3) and cloned into the pET11a and pET15b vectors. E. coli BL21 (DE3) CodonPlus RIPL cells harboring pET15b/PH0140 were cultivated in Luria‐Bertani medium containing 50 μg/mL ampicillin, and gene expression was induced for 3 h with 0.5 mm isopropyl‐β‐d‐thiogalactopyranoside. After centrifugation at 5000 × g for 10 min, the cells were suspended in 10 mm Tris/HCl (pH 8.0), treated with lysozyme, and lysed by sonication. After centrifugation at 15000 × g for 10 min, the crude extract was heated at 90 °C for 30 min and centrifuged again to remove precipitates. The supernatant was purified using Ni2+‐chelating Sepharose with stepwise imidazole elution. The purified preparation was desalted with 10 mm Tris/HCl (pH 8.0), concentrated by ultrafiltration using Amicon Ultra (MWCO 10000) (Millipore, Tokyo, Japan), and used for crystallization. About 10 mg of purified protein was obtained from 1 L of culture.

EMSA

For EMSA, 1 mg of the purified protein was digested with 1 U thrombin in 1 mL reaction buffer (20 mm Tris/HCl [pH 8.0], 150 mm NaCl, and 2.5 mm CaCl2) at 20 °C for 16 h. Digestion was confirmed by SDS/PAGE, followed by desalting and concentration using 10 mm Tris/HCl (pH 8.0).

DNA probes were prepared by PCR using the primer sets listed in Table 3. Each primer set was designed to amplify a 200‐bp region upstream and a 15‐bp region downstream of the start sites of PH0134, PH0137, PH0138, and PH0140. The amplified DNA probes were purified with the Nucleospin Gel and PCR Clean‐up Kit (Macherey‐Nagel). For the assay, 25, 50, or 100 nm DARP (as octamer) was incubated with 10 nm DNA probe at 70 °C for 15 min in binding buffer (10 mm Tris/HCl [pH 7.0], 1 mm DTT, and 400 mm KCl) in the presence or absence of d‐ or l‐amino acids. After incubation, the samples were loaded onto a 5% polyacrylamide gel, and electrophoresis was performed using 0.5 × TBE buffer at 100 V for 60 min. After electrophoresis, gels were stained with ethidium bromide. Band shifts were detected using a UV illuminator.

Crystallization and data collection

His‐tagged DARP was used for crystallization analyses. Crystals of l‐Ile‐bound DARP were obtained using the sitting‐drop vapor diffusion method, in which a 1.5 μL drop of 10.4 mg/mL protein solution was mixed with an equal volume of mother liquor composed of 0.1 m sodium cacodylate‐HCl buffer (pH 6.5) and 1.26 m ammonium sulfate. For the crystallization of d‐allo‐Ile‐bound DARP, a protein solution containing 5 mm d‐allo‐Ile was incubated at 80 °C for 20 min. The crystals were grown in sitting drops comprising 1.5 μL heat‐treated protein solution (10.9 mg/mL) and 1 μL mother liquor containing 2.0 m ammonium sulfate and 6% (v/v) 2‐propanol. In both cases, the sitting drops were equilibrated against 0.1 mL of mother liquor, and the crystals were grown for 1 week at 20 °C.

Data were collected using a Dectris Pilatus3 S2M detector system on the AR‐NW12A beamline for l‐Ile‐bound DARP and a Dectris Pilatus 2 M‐F detector system on the AR‐NE3A beamline for d‐allo‐Ile‐bound DARP at the Photon Factory, Tsukuba, Japan. Measurements were performed on crystals cooled to 100 K in a stream of liquid nitrogen gas. Cryoprotection was performed with mother liquor containing 30% (v/v) glycerol for l‐Ile‐bound DARP and 30% (v/v) ethylene glycol for d‐allo‐Ile‐bound DARP. Data were processed using HKL2000 [38] for l‐Ile‐bound DARP and with XDS [39] for d‐allo‐Ile‐bound DARP.

Phasing, refinement and structure analysis

The structure of the l‐Ile‐bound DARP was solved at a resolution of 1.39 Å by molecular replacement using MOLREP [40] in the CCP4 program suite [41]. The monomer structure predicted with AlphaFold2 [42] was used as the search model. Data in the resolution range of 50–3 Å were used for molecular replacement. The model was built with Coot [26]. Maximum‐likelihood refinement at 1.39 Å resolution was performed with REFMAC5 [43]. After several inspection cycles of the 2Fo‐Fc and Fo‐Fc density maps, the model was rebuilt. The structure of d‐allo‐Ile‐bound DARP was solved at a resolution of 2.20 Å by molecular replacement with MOLREP [40]. The structure of l‐Ile‐bound DARP was used as the search model. Data in the resolution range of 50–3 Å were used for molecular replacement. The model was then rebuilt using Coot [26], and refinement at maximum resolution was performed with REFMAC5 [43], as described for l‐Ile‐bound DARP. In all cases, water molecules were incorporated with Coot [26] and model geometry was analyzed with MolProbity [44]. Data collection and refinement statistics are presented in Table 2.

The crystal structure of the P. horikoshii PH0140 protein (DARP) bound to l‐Ile has been deposited in the PDB (PDB code: 7FBY, released July 20, 2022); however, the detailed discussion based on this structure has not been reported to date.
