Evaluation of the effect of platelet-rich plasma on the rate of orthodontic tooth movement : A split-mouth controlled study
Authors: Megha R. Revaneti, Riannee C Johnson, Azhar Mohammed, Numa Dhamnekar, M. S. Ravi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AIM:To evaluate the effect of platelet-rich plasma (PRP) on the rate of orthodontic tooth movement during maxillary canine retraction.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This split-mouth controlled clinical study included 15 patients aged between 18 and 30 years requiring bilateral maxillary first premolar extractions. One side of the maxillary arch was randomly assigned as the experimental side and received a single submucosal injection of autologous PRP around the distal aspect of the canine, while the contralateral side served as the control. Mini-implants were placed bilaterally between the maxillary first and second molars and loaded with NiTi closed-coil springs delivering a retractive force of 150 g for canine retraction. Orthodontic tooth movement was evaluated using intraoral scans obtained with Trios 3 scanner at baseline (T0) and at 4-week intervals up to 16 weeks (T1–T4). The rate of canine movement was calculated using Ortho Systems Orthodontics software. Intergroup comparisons were performed using an unpaired t-test, and within-group comparisons were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance.RESULTS:The mean rate of canine retraction on the PRP side was 0.37 ± 0.34 mm, 0.37 ± 0.34 mm, 0.66 ± 0.46 mm, and 0.52 ± 0.44 mm across successive intervals. On the control side, the corresponding values were 0.31 ± 0.24 mm, 0.31 ± 0.24 mm, 0.23 ± 0.27 mm, and 0.38 ± 0.53 mm. A statistically significant difference was observed during the T2–T3 interval (P < 0.05).CONCLUSION:A single submucosal PRP injection may enhance canine retraction during orthodontic treatment, although the effect appears transient and time dependent.

Introduction

In recent years, there has been a significant rise in the demand for comprehensive orthodontic treatment. On average, the duration of orthodontic treatment is around 24 months, but it can be even longer depending on the severity of the case, treatment plan, and individual characteristics. This extended duration can lead to frustration among adult patients and increases the risk of problems such as tooth decay, receding gums, and root damage.

Efforts to address these challenges can be broadly classified into four categories: biological, physical, biomechanical, and surgical approaches. Currently, there are various treatment modalities available, including the use of cytokines, prostaglandins, vitamin D3, parathyroid hormone, relaxin, PRP (platelet-rich plasma), PRF (platelet-rich fibrin), direct electric currents, pulsed electromagnetic fields, static magnetic fields, resonance vibration, low-level laser therapy, interseptal alveolar surgery, osteotomy, corticotomy, and the piezocision technique.[1] This study focused on PRP to accelerate tooth movement in one quadrant, while using the contralateral quadrant as control.

PRP is defined as an autologous concentration of platelets in a minute volume of plasma. The seven known growth factors in PRP are: platelet-derived growth factor aa (PDGFaa), PDGFbb, PDGFab, transforming growth factor beta-1 (TGF-b1), TGF-b2, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and epithelial growth factor (EGF).[2]

Understanding the biology and mechanism of action of PRP in orthodontics is a crucial challenge. PRP contains various biologically active components that have multiple and overlapping effects. For instance, PDGF acts as a potent chemoattractant and stimulator of cell growth. It promotes the proliferation of osteoprogenitor cells while also enhancing resorption by increasing the number of osteoclasts.

Therefore, the effectiveness of PRP relies not only on platelet quantity but also on achieving a balance between catabolism and anabolism, as well as the cellular composition of PRP.[3]

Most research regarding PRP tends to focus on its applications in regenerative medicine. However, the use and applications of PRP in orthodontics have also drawn some attention in the literature recently.[4]

Some recent studies have demonstrated a correlation between the local injection of PRP and an accelerated rate of orthodontic tooth movement, whereas a few have reported no effect of PRP on orthodontic tooth movement.[56789]

These conflicting findings in the literature lead to a lack of clarity regarding the effect of PRP on orthodontic tooth movement.

Hence, the present study was undertaken to assess and compare the effect of PRP on the rate of orthodontic tooth movement.

Source of data

The present study included patients who reported to the Department of Orthodontics and Dentofacial Orthopaedics at AB Shetty Memorial Institute of Dental Sciences, Nitte (Deemed to be University) during the period from 2020 to 2023. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of A B Shetty Memorial Institute of Dental Sciences, Nitte (Deemed to be University) before the commencement of the study (ETHICS/ABSMIDS/282/2022). The study was conducted using a split-mouth design.

PRP Preparation [Figures 1 and 2]

Duo Quattro centrifugating system

PRP prepared in a test tube. PRP = Platelet-rich plasma

The PRP was prepared by the double-spin technique as described by Marx and Garg.[11] PRP was prepared from autologous venous blood using a two-step centrifugation technique. A total of 36 mL of venous blood was drawn from each patient using a 22-gauge needle and collected in tubes containing anticoagulant citrate dextrose solution A (ACD-A).

The collected blood samples were subjected to an initial soft spin at 900 g for 10 min using the Duo Quattro centrifugation unit (Process for PRF, Nice, France). This first centrifugation resulted in the separation of three layers: a bottom layer of red blood cells (RBCs), a middle buffy coat layer, and a supernatant plasma layer. The upper plasma layer, along with the buffy coat, was carefully separated from the RBC layer.

The collected fraction was then subjected to a second concentration spin at 1500 g for 15 min to obtain a higher platelet concentration. Following the second centrifugation, a thin layer of RBCs with a buffy coat was observed above the straw-colored plasma. The platelet concentrate was present within the RBC button, buffy coat, and the lower portion of the plasma fraction.

The remaining plasma was aspirated into a syringe and gently mixed by ejecting it along the walls of the container three times to obtain a uniform suspension of concentrated platelets along with small amounts of RBCs, white blood cells, and plasma, thereby forming the PRP. The platelet concentration achieved in the present study ranged from 7 to 10 times the baseline platelet concentration of whole blood.

A split-mouth randomization protocol was used. The side of the maxillary arch receiving PRP injection (experimental side) was determined using a computer-generated random sequence. The contralateral side served as the control. All orthodontic procedures were performed by a single trained orthodontist to minimize operator variability.

Mini-implant placement and PRP injection

PRP was administered as a single injection before the initiation of canine retraction. This protocol was selected based on evidence, indicating that platelet-derived growth factors (PDGFs) are released rapidly after activation; active secretion begins within 10 min, and more than 95% of the presynthesized growth factors are released within the first hour.[12]

Both the intervention and control side were anaesthetized with 2% lignocaine hydrochloride and adrenaline bitartrate (1:80000) for the insertion of mini screws. An infiltration of local anesthesia was also given on the experimental side to preemptively reduce any pain to the patient [Figure 3]. A mini-screw (Absoanchor: 1.3 mm diameter × 8 mm height) was inserted in the interradicular region between the upper first and second molars on each side at the level of the junction between the attached and nonkeratinized gingiva [Figure 4]. Fifty units (0.5 mL) of PRP were injected in the middle, distobuccal, and distopalatal areas of the distal surface of the canines (10 units each area) together with submucosal injections buccally and palatally (10 units each area), using a 31-gauge needle. PRP was injected once before retraction.

Application of 2% lignocaine hydrochloride and adrenaline bitartrate (1:80000)

Mini-screw attachment between first and second molars and closed coil spring attached from the mini-screw to the hook of the canine

Immediately following PRP injection, the same volume of 10% CaCl2 solution was injected using a 31-gauge needle for activation of the PRP on the experimental side [Figures 5 and 6]. Both the PRP and control side mini-implants were immediately loaded using NiTi closed-coil springs from the mini-implant to the canine hook, delivering a constant retractive force of 150 g, measured using a Correx force gauge to ensure standardization of orthodontic mechanics [Figure 4]. Direct intraoral scan images were made using Trios 3 scanner (3 Shape; Copenhagen, Denmark) at the intervals 0 (T0), 4 weeks (T1), 8 weeks (T2), 12 weeks (T3), and 16 weeks (T4). The change in canine position for each side was measured using the software Ortho Systems Orthodontics 2021 for each interval to obtain the rate of canine retraction [Figure 7]. All measurements were performed by a single calibrated examiner. Each measurement was repeated three times, and the mean value was recorded to minimize measurement error and improve reliability.

As the participants were undergoing comprehensive orthodontic treatment as part of routine clinical care in the department, subsequent treatment, including completion of space closure, was continued by the treating clinicians according to the planned orthodontic protocol. Therefore, follow-up beyond the 16-week observation period was not included within the scope of the present study.

PRP was administered as a single injection before the initiation of canine retraction. This protocol was selected based on available evidence, suggesting that PDGFs are released rapidly following activation and exert their biological effects primarily during the early phases of bone remodeling.

Results

The study included 15 individuals (a total of 30 quadrants) who had come for orthodontic treatment and needed their upper first premolars extracted bilaterally, as part of their treatment plan.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25.0, Armonk, NY: IBM Corp. The results on continuous measurement were presented as mean and standard deviation (SD), and categorically as frequency and percentage. Data were analyzed using an unpaired t-test to compare between two groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the data within the group.

The differences in the measurement of distance between the canine and premolar were measured for both the control and PRP side, and the average for both groups was then listed under four time-intervals using the unpaired T-Test: viz, T0–T1, T1–T2, T2–T3, T3–T4 [Table 1].

Discussion

Orthodontic treatment with therapeutic extractions often extends to nearly 31 months.[13] which can compromise patient compliance and elevate the risk of complications. Despite various attempts to shorten treatment duration, a predictable, minimally invasive, and cost-effective method to enhance tooth movement remains elusive.

Therefore, this split-mouth study was undertaken to evaluate whether a submucosal injection of autologous PRP could enhance the rate of orthodontic tooth movement during anterior retraction.

PRP has been used extensively in various fields of dentistry, showing vast advantages and applications.[1114] Previous literature revealed that methods of PRP preparation have evolved in many techniques,[14] all aiming at standardization of the procedure for PRP preparation. The PRP was widely used on humans with no evidence that local PRP injections have systemic effects. Due to its autologous nature, no immune reaction or allergy was reported.[15]

Textor and Tablin[16] demonstrated that calcium chloride represents an efficient and economical activator, though it may require a slightly longer activation time.

Calcium chloride (CaCl2) plays a central role in PRP activation by enabling fibrinogen binding to platelet receptors, thereby inducing aggregation and degranulation. This process triggers the release of bioactive molecules, which contribute to cell recruitment, proliferation, angiogenesis, and bone turnover. The conversion of fibrinogen into fibrin further stabilizes the injected PRP into a clot or membrane, enhancing its regenerative potential.[12]

The injection protocol followed Rashid et al.[5] using five sites around the canine, whereas Gulec et al.[4] injected PRP only in the buccal vestibule near the first molar. The decision to include intraligamentary injections at the canine root surfaces was guided by findings from Von Bohl et al.,[1718] indicating that hyalinized areas commonly occur lingually and buccally due to localized stress concentrations. Rapid bone turnover in these regions is essential for advancing tooth movement.[19]

Prior studies on local pharmacologic agents generally used fewer injection sites, typically one or two.[4202122] This study employed a single pre-retraction injection session over a four-month period to evaluate the sustained effect of PRP, contrasting with modalities such as corticotomy[23] or repeated low-level laser applications that require ongoing intervention.[24]

The results revealed statistical significance only during the third month (P = 0.007), the general trend consistently favored PRP, which supports a positive association between PRP administration and enhanced orthodontic tooth movement.

The biological plausibility of these findings is attributed to the multifaceted actions of PRP components. PDGF promotes osteoprogenitor proliferation and osteoclastic activity, while transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) modulates tissue repair and inflammation with variable biological effects. Leukocytes and cytokines may further enhance periodontal inflammation, accelerating bone remodeling. Thus, PRP efficacy depends not only on platelet concentration but also on the balance between anabolic and catabolic processes and its cellular composition.[3]

Güleç et al.[4] demonstrated increased dental movement and osteoclast numbers in mice following PRP injection, with peak effects at 60 days. In this study, the most pronounced effect occurred between 60 and 90 days, consistent with the period of active bone turnover. Hosseini et al.[25] correlated elevated dentin sialoprotein (DSP) levels with enhanced root resorption and osteoclast activity after PRP injection, further supporting its influence on bone resorption dynamics. Several studies have also documented increases in dentin matrix acidic phosphoprotein 1 (DMP1), DSP, and ALP (alkaline phosphatase) levels following PRP administration, indicating heightened mineralization and remodeling activity. It stimulates the formation of mineral nodules over time, in the presence of stimulants such as ascorbic acid and β-glycerophosphate in vitro.[262728] However, Hosseini’s study[25] lacked parallel assessment of bone remodeling to root resorption, which limits interpretability.

In the present study, a statistically significant increase in the rate of canine retraction on the PRP side compared to the control side was observed only during the 8–12-week interval (T2–T3). No statistically significant differences were detected during the other observation periods. Tooth movement often stalls during the lag phase due to hyalinization, lasting 2–10 weeks, until undermining resorption restores periodontal ligament vitality and initiates the secondary phase of rapid movement.[29] In the present study, this phase occurred at weeks 8–12 on the PRP side and at weeks 12–16 on the control side. The increased movement during T2–T3 (PRP) and T3–T4 (control) corresponds with this timeline and supports findings by Alaway,[30] who reported a similar increase in the third month following PRP administration.

Comparison with existing literature reveals that corticotomy produces a two-fold acceleration, as reported by Aboul-Ela et al.[23] Gulec et al.[4] documented a 1.4–1.7-fold increase with PRP, while Desai et al.[31] observed 1.5–2-fold enhancement like the present findings. El Timamy et al.[6] found significant acceleration during the first two months only, with diminishing long-term effects. Alaway[30] also reported a 1.5-fold increase comparable to the current study. Rashid et al.[5] found a higher rate (2.13:1), possibly due to animal model parameters and variations in PRP composition. Since statistically significant differences were not observed at other time intervals, the results of the present study do not support a consistent or overall acceleration of tooth movement throughout the study duration. Further studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods are required to better understand the temporal effects of PRP on orthodontic tooth movement.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of the present study, PRP administration was associated with a significantly greater rate of canine retraction only during the 8–12-week interval compared with the control side. No statistically significant differences were observed during the other time intervals. Therefore, while PRP may influence orthodontic tooth movement during specific phases of treatment, the findings of this study do not support a consistent overall acceleration of tooth movements.

Patients reported no discomfort at the time of injection or at subsequent appointments.

PRP can be safely used as a minimally invasive and cost-effective method to reduce the otherwise lengthy duration of orthodontic treatment and thereby improve patient compliance.

Limitations and future research directions

The present study has certain limitations. The follow-up period was limited to 16 weeks, which may not fully represent the long-term effect of PRP on orthodontic tooth movement or the total time required for complete space closure. In addition, the sample size was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of the findings.

Future studies with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up periods extending until complete space closure, and evaluation of additional clinical parameters are recommended to further clarify the role of PRP in accelerating orthodontic tooth movement.
