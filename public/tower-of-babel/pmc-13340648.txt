Cervical gunshot injury with esophageal perforation and bullet migration to the stomach: a rare case report
Authors: Reza Hosseinpour, Sepehr Moradi, Taha Negahdari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Penetrating neck injuries (PNIs) are uncommon but are associated with considerable morbidity and mortality, with reported rates approaching 10% due to the concentration of vital structures in the cervical region. Esophageal injuries – often initially missed – are a major contributor to delayed mortality. Prompt assessment of the airway, hemodynamic stability, and associated injuries is essential. Here, we report a rare case of a previously healthy patient who sustained a PNI with a subsequent delayed presentation of esophageal perforation.Case presentation:A 17-year-old male presented 3 hours after sustaining a Zone II gunshot wound to the right side of the neck. He was hemodynamically stable, and initial imaging showed no abnormalities. Twelve hours later, the development of fever prompted further evaluation with abdominal computed tomography (CT), which revealed the projectile in the stomach, suggesting migration through the esophagus.Discussion:Even hemodynamically stable patients with PNIs may develop delayed complications, including esophageal perforation and bullet migration, which can be missed during the initial assessment. Recognition of evolving symptoms, early repeat imaging, and timely surgical intervention are essential in achieving favorable outcomes.Conclusion:PNIs require vigilant monitoring and a structured, guideline-based diagnostic approach to avoid missed aerodigestive injuries. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a cervical gunshot projectile migrating into the stomach through the esophagus.

Introduction

Penetrating neck injury (PNI) is defined as trauma in which a projectile or sharp object breaches the platysma muscle, extending from the skull base superiorly to the sternum inferiorly[1]. Although PNIs account for a relatively small proportion of traumatic injuries, they are associated with significant morbidity and mortality, with reported death rates of approximately 10%[1]. This heightened risk is due to the dense concentration of vital structures in the neck, including major vascular, airway, digestive, and neurologic systems. Among these, esophageal injuries are particularly important, as they represent a major cause of delayed mortality and frequently remain undetected during the initial evaluation[2].

Most esophageal injuries result from penetrating trauma, with gunshot wounds responsible for 70–80% and stab wounds accounting for an additional 15–20% of cases. Blunt mechanisms are rare, comprising fewer than 1% of all esophageal injuries[3].

Effective management of PNI requires rapid intervention, thorough airway and circulatory assessment, and evaluation for skeletal and neurologic involvement[2]. In the present case, despite a clear projectile entry wound in the right lateral neck (Fig. 1), the patient exhibited no early signs of esophageal or airway involvement, including no dysphagia, odynophagia, hematemesis, hemoptysis, or subcutaneous emphysema, during the initial clinical evaluation. The wound was limited to a single, non-actively bleeding entry site measuring approximately two fingertip widths. Although cervical penetrating gunshot wounds with esophageal perforation have been reported, the migration of the projectile into the stomach appears to be exceedingly rare. We present this case to emphasize the possibility of delayed aerodigestive injury and to highlight important diagnostic considerations in neck trauma. Case presentation

A 17-year-old male presented to the emergency department approximately 3 hours after sustaining a gunshot wound to the right lateral neck (Zone II) during a street altercation (Fig. 1). On arrival, he was alert, communicative, and hemodynamically stable. His initial vital signs included a blood pressure of 120/80 mmHg, a heart rate of 75 beats per minute, a respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute, a temperature of 37°C, and a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 15. A complete ABCDE assessment was performed in accordance with Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) principles.

Physical examination revealed a single, well-defined projectile entry wound on the right side of the neck, measuring approximately two fingertip widths. No external active bleeding was present, and only mild localized tenderness was noted. There was no evidence of neurologic deficit, hoarseness, stridor, subcutaneous emphysema, hematemesis, hemoptysis, dysphagia, or odynophagia. No respiratory or digestive compromise was detected.

Initial laboratory investigations – including complete blood count and serum biochemistry – were unremarkable. The patient reported sustaining the injury at close range with a civilian handgun during an altercation, although the bullet caliber was unknown.

Initial imaging was performed in sequence and consisted of lateral and anteroposterior (AP) neck radiographs, followed by head and neck computed tomography angiography (CTA), and subsequently a barium swallow study (Fig. 2). All studies demonstrated normal findings, with no vascular injury, no aerodigestive tract disruption, and no evidence of contrast leak on the swallow examination. Despite these evaluations, the bullet remained undetected. The patient stayed clinically stable and continued to tolerate oral intake throughout observation. Figure 2.Barium swallow. Approximately 12 hours after admission, he developed an isolated fever without additional symptoms. Given the new-onset fever and the unexpected identification of a high-density projectile within the stomach on abdominal CT, delayed esophageal perforation with bullet migration was suspected, leading to the decision for surgical exploration. The abdominal CT demonstrated a round metallic foreign body within the stomach, consistent with migration through the esophagus (Fig. 3). The patient underwent surgical exploration via a left cervical incision along the sternocleidomastoid (SCM) muscle. The bullet tract was identified, revealing a 10 × 10 mm perforation of the cervical esophagus. The defect was repaired and reinforced with an SCM muscle flap to ensure adequate vascular coverage. A Penrose drain was placed for postoperative drainage.

Postoperatively, the patient received intravenous vancomycin and meropenem, along with intramuscular pethidine for analgesia. His postoperative course was uneventful. During the postoperative observation period, the bullet was neither retrieved surgically nor removed endoscopically. Follow-up abdominal radiography and CT performed several days later showed no remaining foreign body within the gastrointestinal tract. These findings suggested that the bullet had passed through the gastrointestinal system and was expelled spontaneously in the stool. The patient was discharged in good condition after 7 days of inpatient care.

Discussion

We report the case of a 17-year-old male who sustained a penetrating cervical gunshot wound that resulted in esophageal perforation and subsequent migration of the projectile into the stomach – an exceedingly rare trajectory. While cervical esophageal injuries from gunshot trauma have been reported, the passage of a bullet from the neck into the stomach through the esophagus appears to be very uncommon in the literature[5–7].

Early imaging may miss small or evolving cervical esophageal perforations. Contrast esophagography can have false-negative rates of up to 10–40% in small or contained injuries[8], and fluoroscopic swallow studies may fail to detect subtle leaks[9]. CTA is also limited in identifying early mucosal defects[10]. Thus, a negative initial study does not exclude perforation.

The likely mechanism of migration involves the bullet entering the cervical esophagus and subsequently progressing distally under the influence of esophageal peristalsis, demonstrating that projectiles can occasionally traverse natural anatomic conduits even in the absence of overt obstructive or severe tissue disruption[11]. This case reinforces the principle that PNIs demand a cautious and systematic approach, regardless of the patient’s initial hemodynamic stability.

According to current trauma guidelines, including ATLS and the Western Trauma Association recommendations, early evaluation must prioritize airway, breathing, and circulation, followed by a detailed assessment for “hard signs” of vascular or aerodigestive injury[12,13]. In our patient, the absence of hallmark esophageal symptoms – such as dysphagia, odynophagia, hematemesis, hoarseness, or subcutaneous emphysema – contributed to the initially reassuring clinical picture. However, esophageal perforation remains one of the most frequently missed injuries in penetrating neck trauma and carries significant morbidity when the diagnosis is delayed[3]. CTA serves as the diagnostic modality of choice for penetrating neck trauma, as it provides rapid assessment of vascular and aerodigestive structures with high accuracy. Complementary imaging methods, including contrast swallow studies or endoscopy, may be warranted when suspicion persists despite unremarkable imaging. In this case, initial AP/lateral radiographs, CTA, and barium swallow imaging demonstrated normal findings with no evidence of a leak or injury. Nonetheless, the development of fever 12 hours after admission – along with subsequent CT identification of the bullet within the stomach – promptly raised suspicion for esophageal perforation, underscoring the importance of clinical vigilance despite normal initial imaging.

Early surgical exploration and primary repair remain the cornerstone of management for cervical esophageal perforations, particularly when recognized within the early window of injury. In our patient, direct repair reinforced with an SCM muscle flap provided stable coverage and favorable postoperative healing. The subsequent spontaneous passage of the bullet through the gastrointestinal tract further highlights the unpredictable nature of missile trajectories following penetrating trauma.

Endoscopic retrieval was not pursued, as the patient remained clinically stable, and imaging confirmed intragastric migration of the projectile without evidence of obstruction, perforation, or ongoing bleeding, supporting a conservative, expectant management approach.

This case emphasizes that even clinically stable patients with initially unremarkable findings may harbor delayed or occult injuries. Timely recognition of evolving symptoms and adherence to structured diagnostic algorithms are essential for optimizing outcomes in penetrating neck trauma.

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for aerodigestive tract injury in all penetrating neck wounds, even when the initial clinical evaluation and imaging appear unremarkable. Delayed esophageal perforation may remain clinically silent until secondary signs, such as fever, develop. Prompt recognition of evolving symptoms, repeat imaging, and timely surgical intervention are essential to prevent serious complications. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a cervical gunshot projectile migrating through the esophagus into the stomach. Vigilant monitoring and adherence to structured trauma evaluation protocols are crucial for achieving favorable outcomes in PNIs.
