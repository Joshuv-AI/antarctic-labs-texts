Identification of angiogenesis-related hub genes in proliferative diabetic retinopathy via integrated transcriptomics and machine learning
Authors: Yan Wang, Yun Zhang, Qing Lu, Jie Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR), characterized by pathological neovascularization, is one of the leading causes of blindness in diabetic patients. A better understanding of its molecular mechanisms is crucial for identifying novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets.MethodsThe dataset GSE102485 was retrieved from the GEO database to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was applied to detect PDR-associated gene modules. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using SangerBox. Key genes were further refined through LASSO regression. Immune cell infiltration was assessed using the MCPcounter algorithm. Introduction

Globally, diabetic retinopathy (DR) remains a predominant contributor to permanent visual impairment in adults of working age [1, 2]. As the end-stage manifestation of diabetic retinopathy, proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) demonstrates hallmark pathological features including aberrant blood vessel formation and uncontrolled cell growth, presenting significant therapeutic challenges [3]. Although intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy has improved clinical outcomes, many patients exhibit incomplete responses or develop resistance [4], indicating the urgent need to explore novel molecular targets and underlying pathogenic mechanisms.

The pathological features of PDR include neovascularization, fibrovascular membrane formation, and retinal detachment. While previous studies have primarily focused on aberrant neovascularization, recent evidence suggests that the infiltration of inflammatory mediators also contributes to PDR progression [5, 6]. High glucose (HG) has been widely recognized as a key pathogenic trigger in diabetic retinopathy, as confirmed by numerous studies [7–9]. Chronic injury and pathological alterations of vascular endothelial cells represent the central histopathological changes underlying DR-related damage [10]. In PDR, the loss of immune privilege and blood-retinal barrier (BRB) disruption allow immune cells to infiltrate the retina and vitreous, leading to vascular injury marked by chronic inflammation and neuronal damage [11]. Retinal endothelial cells (RECs) are among the first cells affected during BRB breakdown, yet the overall immune infiltration pattern remains unclear.

The application of cutting-edge high-throughput platforms and computational biology approaches has significantly deepened the mechanistic comprehension of PDR pathogenesis, facilitating innovative treatment discovery [12]. This study elucidated the molecular characteristics of PDR by integrating transcriptomic analysis, machine learning, and immune infiltration profiling. Several hub genes closely associated with disease progression were identified and validated. Functional investigation of laminin subunit beta 1 (LAMB1) in HG-induced HRECs further supported its potential role in PDR. The study outcomes provided novel perspectives on the molecular mechanisms underlying PDR and highlighted the effectiveness of integrating transcriptomics with machine learning approaches for biomarker identification.

Materials and methods

Collection and preprocessing of data

Transcriptomic data from 19 PDR samples and 5 normal retinal tissues were retrieved from the GEO database (GSE102485). To ensure consistency across samples, raw count data were normalized using the “DESeq” function in the DESeq2 package. Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2, and genes with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 and |log2 fold-change (FC)| ≥ 1 were initially identified as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). To obtain a high-confidence gene set for downstream analyses, a more stringent threshold (adjusted P < 0.05 and |log2 FC| ≥ 2) was subsequently applied to refine the DEG list. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to assess sample clustering and batch consistency. The distribution and expression profiles of DEGs were visualized using volcano plots and clustered heatmaps.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA was performed using the WGCNA R package to identify gene modules related to PDR. Gene expression data of 19,416 genes across 24 samples were used, downloaded from the GEO database. Initially, genes with the lowest 50% median absolute deviation (MAD) were removed to exclude low-variability genes. The goodSamplesGenes function was applied to filter out outlier genes and samples. Network topology validation was performed using the pickSoftThreshold algorithm, which determined β = 5 (R2 = 0.87) as the optimal soft-threshold value for achieving scale-free properties. Following adjacency matrix generation, topological overlap matrices (TOMs) were computed to quantify gene-gene interaction strengths. Module detection was conducted through hierarchical clustering of TOM-based dissimilarity measures (1-TOM), employing dynamic tree cutting with a minimum cluster size threshold of 30 genes and deepSplit = 3. Gene modules exhibiting eigengene dissimilarity below 0.25 were consolidated, yielding a total of 40 consolidated gene modules. Quantitative assessments of module membership (MM) and gene significance (GS) were computed to pinpoint modules demonstrating significant correlations with clinical phenotypes of PDR. The grey module, containing genes not assigned to any module, was excluded from further analysis. Each step was visualized using WGCNA and related R packages, and key modules were retained for downstream analyses.

Functional enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment analysis encompassing Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways was conducted on identified key genes using the R package “clusterProfiler” for comprehensive annotation and statistical interpretation. The GO analysis covered three categories: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF), with the top 10 significantly enriched terms selected from each category. KEGG pathway analysis identified the top 20 pathways ranked by significance (P < 0.05) for presentation.

Identification of angiogenesis-related genes

Genes associated with angiogenesis were retrieved from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) by searching the term “angiogenesis”. These genes were intersected with DEGs and PDR-related WGCNA module genes. The overlapping genes were subjected to KEGG and GO enrichment analysis to identify pathways and functions associated with angiogenesis.

Construction of protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks and analysis of key hub genes

The 105 common target genes were analyzed in STRING (interaction confidence threshold: 0.7) to generate a PPI network. These network results were then visualized in Cytoscape, where node coloring was applied according to connectivity scores to emphasize key network components. Then, the DMNC algorithm within the CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape was used to select the top 15 hub genes in the network. Clustering analysis was performed using the MCODE plugin in Cytoscape, identifying three gene clusters, from which 15 genes in cluster 1 were selected for further analysis. A Venn diagram was then conducted to identify overlapping genes between DMNC and MCODE analyses, yielding 11 common genes. Through implementation of the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression algorithm, pivotal target gene selection was refined. The LASSO coefficient plot (lasso.Coef) and cross-validation plot (lasso.CV) were used to select the optimal model, resulting in six key genes.

Immune cell infiltration analysis

The MCPcounter algorithm was employed to quantify the relative abundance of ten immune and stromal cell populations. The normalized expression matrix was used as input, and the estimated cell infiltration levels were compared between PDR and Normal groups using unpaired t-tests. The relationships between expression levels of six hub genes and immune cell infiltration patterns were examined through Pearson correlation analysis.

Validation of hub genes

The diagnostic efficacy of the six candidate hub genes was evaluated by constructing receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves with the R package “pROC”. The training dataset (GSE102485) and the independent validation dataset (GSE94019) were both analyzed. Predictive performance of individual genes was evaluated through area under the curve (AUC) calculation. Concurrently, differential expression patterns of hub genes were examined between fibrovascular membrane (FVM) samples from PDR patients and retinal endothelial cell (REC) samples from non-diabetic controls in both datasets using two-tailed unpaired t-test.

Cell culture

Human retinal endothelial cells (HRECs) were acquired from ScienCell (Carlsbad, CA, USA) and propagated in endothelial cell medium (ECM, ScienCell) containing 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS, ScienCell). Cultures were incubated at 37℃ with 5% CO2 in a humidified atmosphere. To model hyperglycemic conditions, HRECs were divided into four groups: normal glucose (NG), low-concentration high glucose (HG, 20 mM, Aladdin, Shanghai, China), medium-concentration HG (60 mM), and high-concentration HG (120 mM). A mannitol-treated group (60 mM, Aladdin) was included to control for osmotic pressure. To further simulate the PDR microenvironment, HRECs were also exposed to hypoxic conditions (1% O2, 5% CO2, 94% N2) for 24 h, with or without HG treatment. This resulted in four additional experimental groups: NG + normoxia, HG + normoxia, NG + hypoxia, and HG + hypoxia.

Cell transfection

Two short hairpin RNA plasmids targeting LAMB1 (shLAMB1 #1 and shLAMB1 #2, GenePharma, Shanghai, China) were delivered into HRECs via Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Western blotting

Cellular proteins were isolated from HRECs with RIPA extraction reagent (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Following quantification, protein samples underwent electrophoretic separation via SDS-PAGE and were subsequently electroblotted onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Beyotime). The membranes were first blocked with 5% skim milk (Beyotime) for 60 min, then exposed to primary antibodies targeting LAMB1 (1:1000, ab256380, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), Wnt3a (1:1000, ab219412, Abcam), β-catenin (1:1000, ab68183, Abcam), c-Myc (1:1000, ab185655, Abcam) and β-actin (1:1000, ab8227, Abcam). Subsequently, the membranes were treated with HRP-linked secondary antibody (1:1000, ab6721, Abcam) for 1 h at room temperature. Signal detection was performed using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL, Invitrogen), and images were captured using a chemiluminescence detection system.

Transwell assay

Cell migratory capacity was assessed employing transwell chambers (Corning, NY, USA). Serum-deprived HRECs (2.5 × 103 cells/well) were plated in upper compartments, with 10% FBS-containing medium as chemoattractant in lower chambers. Following 24-h culture at 37℃, non-migratory cells remaining on the membrane’s apical side were carefully eliminated. Migrated cells adherent to the basal surface were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime) and stained with crystal violet (Beyotime) for 20 min. Quantitative analysis was performed by enumerating cells from five random microscopic areas per sample using bright-field microscopy.

Tubule formation assay

A tube formation assay was conducted using Matrigel (10 mg/mL, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) to evaluate the angiogenic potential of cells. 96-well plates were pre-coated with 50 µL Matrigel (diluted in serum-free medium) per well and polymerized at 37℃ for 30 min. Following HG treatment or transfection, HRECs were plated onto the gel matrix and maintained for 6 h under standard culture conditions. Microscopic examination revealed tubular structures under light microscopy, and the degree of vascular network formation was quantitatively measured using ImageJ software.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection by flow cytometry

Intracellular ROS level in HRECs was measured with the Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (Beyotime). Cells were exposed to 10 µM DCFH-DA (Beyotime) or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Beyotime) diluted in serum-free medium. This incubation occurred at 37℃ for 30 min under dark conditions. Subsequently, following trypsinization, cells were PBS-washed and reconstituted in serum-deprived medium (1 × 106 cells/mL). Flow cytometric analysis was then conducted using a Novocyte instrument.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The secreted levels of vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) in HREC-conditioned medium were determined using ELISA kits (CUSABIO, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were implemented via R software (version 4.2.1) and GraphPad Prism 9.0. Quantitative data were expressed as mean ± SD. Two-group comparisons utilized unpaired t-tests, while multi-group evaluations employed one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc analysis. The statistical significance threshold was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Identification of DEGs in PDR

To identify DEGs in PDR, differential expression analysis was carried out using the DESeq2 package on the GSE102485 dataset, which contained 19 PDR and 5 normal retinal samples. PCA revealed a clear separation between the PDR and normal groups, indicating good inter-group discrimination and reasonable sample distribution (Fig. 1A). Sample correlation analysis further confirmed high intra-group consistency and reliable data quality (Fig. 1B). Based on the criteria of |log2 FC| ≥ 1 and P < 0.05, a total of 5,275 DEGs were identified, including 3,453 upregulated and 1,822 downregulated genes. The volcano plot visualized the overall distribution of DEGs, highlighting the top 10 upregulated and downregulated genes (Fig. 1C), while the heatmap clearly illustrated distinct expression patterns between the two groups (Fig. 1D). Additionally, the heatmap illustrated the expression patterns of the top 10 upregulated and downregulated DEGs across the samples (Fig. 1E).

(C) A volcano plot was generated to visualize DEGs, with upregulated and downregulated genes marked in red and blue, respectively. (D) Heatmap of DEGs. WGCNA analysis

To explore gene co-expression patterns in PDR, the top 50% of genes with the highest MAD were selected for WGCNA. The scale-free topology model fit (R2) was evaluated for various soft-thresholding powers, with β = 5 chosen as the optimal power (R2 = 0.87) as shown in Fig. 2A. Sample clustering dendrogram analysis confirmed the absence of significant outliers (Fig. 2B). Using dynamic tree cutting and merging modules with a dissimilarity threshold of 0.25, a total of 40 gene modules were identified (Fig. 2C). The relationships between these modules and clinical traits were assessed, revealing a strong positive correlation of the blue module with PDR (r = 0.96, P = 3.2e-13) and a significant negative correlation of the darkorange module (r=−0.70, P = 1.6e-4) as depicted in Fig. 2D. Scatter plots of MM versus GS for both the blue and darkorange modules further confirmed their strong association with PDR pathogenesis (Fig. 2E).

(E) Scatter plots showing the correlations between MM and GS in the blue and darkorange modules

Functional enrichment and intersection analysis

From the initially identified 5,275 DEGs, a stricter cutoff (adjusted P < 0.05 and |log2 FC| ≥ 2) was subsequently applied to obtain 2,885 highly significant DEGs for downstream analyses. In parallel, 468 genes from PDR-related WGCNA modules were obtained. Their intersection yielded 418 overlapping genes (Fig. 3A). These genes were subjected to KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using SangerBox, and the top 20 enriched pathways were visualized (Fig. 3B), including focal adhesion, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, tuberculosis, and hematopoietic cell lineage. To further identify angiogenesis-related genes, 1385 targets were retrieved from the GeneCards database using the keyword “angiogenesis.” Intersection with the 418 candidate genes resulted in 105 overlapping genes (Fig. 3C). KEGG analysis of these 105 genes showed enrichment in pathways such as focal adhesion, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, ECM-receptor interaction, and human papillomavirus infection (Fig. 3D). GO enrichment analysis was conducted for these 105 genes and summarized in Fig. 3E. In the BP category, top terms included anatomical structure morphogenesis, cell adhesion, and biological adhesion. In the CC category, genes were mainly enriched in extracellular matrix, vesicle, and extracellular region. In the MF category, dominant terms included extracellular matrix structural constituent, extracellular matrix structural constituent conferring tensile strength, and signaling receptor binding.

Core gene identification by PPI and machine learning

The 105 intersecting genes were imported into the STRING database for PPI analysis with a confidence score > 0.7. The resulting PPI network was visualized and optimized in Cytoscape according to node degree (Fig. 4A). Using the CytoHubba plugin, the top 15 hub genes were identified based on the DMNC algorithm (Fig. 4B). In parallel, MCODE clustering analysis revealed three significant gene clusters, with Cluster 1 consisting of 15 genes (Fig. 4C). A Venn diagram was generated to identify overlapping genes between the DMNC and MCODE results, yielding 11 common core genes (Fig. 4D). These 11 genes were then subjected to LASSO regression analysis, ultimately narrowing down to six key genes: collagen type XV α1 chain (COL15A1), integrin α1 (ITGA1), LAMB1, thrombospondin-1 (THBS1), periostin (POSTN), and collagen type V alpha 1 (COL5A1) (Fig. 4E).

Immune infiltration analysis

Immune cell infiltration in PDR and Normal samples was assessed using the MCPcounter algorithm. As shown in Fig. 5A, the abundance of all ten immune-related cell types—including T cells, CD8 T cells, cytotoxic lymphocytes, B lineage cells, NK cells, monocytic lineage cells, myeloid dendritic cells, neutrophils, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts—was markedly elevated in the PDR group compared to the Normal group. The correlation heatmap in Fig. 5B further revealed that all six key genes (COL15A1, ITGA1, LAMB1, THBS1, POSTN, and COL5A1) were positively correlated with these immune cell types.

The diagnostic effect of key genes in PDR

To further evaluate the association of the identified genes with PDR, ROC analysis was conducted for the six key genes in both the training and validation sets. As shown in Fig. 6A, all six genes (POSTN, THBS1, LAMB1, ITGA1, COL5A1, COL15A1) showed high AUC values in the GSE102485 training set, indicating a strong association with PDR. Importantly, in the independent validation dataset GSE94019, five of the six genes maintained robust performance, with POSTN and THBS1 reaching an AUC of 1.000, followed by LAMB1 (AUC = 0.9444), COL5A1 (AUC = 0.9167), and COL15A1 (AUC = 0.7778). In contrast, ITGA1 showed a decreased AUC of 0.5833 (Fig. 6B). Unpaired t-tests further confirmed that the expression levels of all six genes were significantly elevated in FVMs group (PDR group) compared to RECs group (Normal group) in the training set (Fig. 6C). In the validation set, POSTN, THBS1, COL5A1, and LAMB1 remained significantly upregulated in the FVMs group compared to the RECs group, while ITGA1 and COL15A1 did not exhibit significant expression difference between the two groups (Fig. 6D).

Knockdown of LAMB1 inhibits HG-induced angiogenesis in HRECs

To investigate the role of LAMB1 in PDR-associated angiogenesis, an in vitro model mimicking PDR was established by exposing HRECs to HG conditions. With increasing glucose concentrations (20 mM, 60 mM, and 120 mM), LAMB1 protein expression showed a dose-dependent upregulation (Fig. 7A). No significant difference in LAMB1 level was observed in the Mannitol group compared to the NG group, indicating that the upregulation of LAMB1 was specifically induced by HG (Fig. 7B). To further investigate whether LAMB1 expression is affected by additional pathological factors associated with PDR, HRECs were exposed to hypoxic conditions in the presence or absence of HG. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S1, compared to the NG + normoxia group, LAMB1 expression was significantly upregulated not only under HG + normoxia but also under both NG + hypoxia and HG + hypoxia conditions. These results indicated that LAMB1 responded synergistically to hyperglycemia and hypoxia, further supporting its role in PDR’s multifactorial microenvironment. Two interference sequences (shLAMB1 #1 and shLAMB1 #2) were used to knock down LAMB1 expression, both demonstrating effective silencing efficiency (Fig. 7C). Western blotting results showed that LAMB1 knockdown reversed the HG-induced upregulation of LAMB1 in HRECs (Fig. 7D). Functional assays revealed that silencing LAMB1 significantly reduced HG-induced increases in HREC migration (Fig. 7E), tube formation (Fig. 7F), intracellular ROS accumulation (Fig. 7G), and the elevated levels of VEGFA (Fig. 7H), ICAM-1 (Fig. 7I), IL-1β (Fig. 7J), and TNF-α (Fig. 7K). These findings suggested that LAMB1 might promote angiogenesis in PDR. Furthermore, Western blotting results showed that knockdown of LAMB1 suppressed the HG-induced upregulation of key molecules in the classical Wnt/β-catenin pathway, including Wnt3a, β-catenin, and c-Myc (Fig. 7L), suggesting that LAMB1 may promote angiogenesis by regulating the Wnt/β-catenin–VEGF signaling axis.

Discussion

The pathogenesis of PDR is highly complex, with mounting evidence indicating that inflammation plays a pivotal role. In a hyperglycemic environment, immune cells become activated and release large quantities of inflammatory mediators, triggering a sustained inflammatory response in retinal tissues [13]. Although microangiopathy has traditionally been regarded as the central pathological basis of PDR, recent studies have highlighted the crucial contributions of immune activation, endothelial dysfunction, and chronic inflammation to disease progression [5, 14, 15]. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the regulatory mechanisms governing angiogenesis and immune cell infiltration in RECs is essential for improving the prevention and treatment of PDR.

Recent advances in bioinformatics have enhanced its applications in both diagnostic assistance and identification of PDR-related druggable targets [16, 17]. Based on the GSE102485 dataset, a total of 5,275 DEGs were identified. Subsequently, WGCNA was applied to identify co-expression modules associated with the PDR phenotype, resulting in 40 modules. By intersecting DEGs, genes from key WGCNA modules, and angiogenesis-related targets from the GeneCards database, 105 candidate genes were obtained. The GO term analysis showed these genes were chiefly enriched in anatomical structure morphogenesis, cell adhesion, extracellular matrix, and extracellular matrix component. KEGG analysis further revealed significant enrichment in pathways such as ECM-receptor interaction, focal adhesion, and PI3K-Akt signaling, all of which were linked to pathological neovascularization observed in PDR [18–20]. By constructing a PPI network and integrating the CytoHubba and MCODE algorithms, 11 overlapping hub genes were initially identified. Further refinement using LASSO regression resulted in six key genes: COL15A1, ITGA1, LAMB1, THBS1, POSTN, and COL5A1. ROC curve analysis indicated that, except for ITGA1, which showed suboptimal performance in the validation set, the remaining five genes exhibited good diagnostic value. ITGA1, a member of the integrin protein family, mediates cellular communication by binding to extracellular matrix (ECM) components, thereby playing a critical role in regulating tumor cell proliferation and migration [21]. Dysregulation of ITGA1 is closely associated with the progression of various cancers. Additionally, studies have demonstrated that knockdown of ITGA1 ameliorates inflammation and angiogenesis in STZ-induced diabetic retinopathy [22]. Previous studies have demonstrated that THBS1 and POSTN are critically involved in angiogenesis. Elevated THBS1 expression impairs murine retinal vascular development and neointima formation [23]. Conversely, deficiency of THBS1 in RECs accelerates proliferation and enhances angiogenesis [23, 24]. The interaction between POSTN and tenascin-C contributes to preretinal angiogenesis, establishing POSTN as a potential therapeutic target for PDR [25]. COL5A1 has been implicated in multiple biological pathways, including the extracellular matrix-receptor interaction pathway, which shows upregulated expression in both active and quiescent fibrovascular membranes associated with PDR [26]. COL15A1 serves as an angiogenic structural support for ECs. Fibroblast-derived COL15A1 overexpression significantly enhances EC migratory capacity and vascular network formation in vitro [27]. It has been recently reported that the Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascade, regulated by LAMB1, promotes angiogenic activity in endothelial cells during bone fracture repair [28].

The current study utilized the MCPcounter method to quantify immune cell populations. The results demonstrated a significant elevation in the levels of ten immune cell subsets in the PDR group. All six hub genes exhibited positive correlations with these immune cells, suggesting their potential involvement in modulating the inflammatory microenvironment. In summary, these results suggest that, although each gene has distinct functional roles, they may collectively contribute to pathological angiogenesis and immune regulation in PDR. For example, ITGA1, THBS1, POSTN, COL5A1, COL15A1, and LAMB1 may modulate the local retinal microenvironment by influencing extracellular matrix composition, endothelial cell proliferation, and the recruitment or activation of immune cells. Among these hub genes, LAMB1 is particularly noteworthy, as it shows robust correlations with multiple immune cell types and possesses significant potential in regulating endothelial cell angiogenesis. Through interactions with cell surface integrins, proteoglycans, and other receptors, LAMB1 is essential for the regulation of tissue development, cell adhesion, and intracellular signaling [29]. It exhibited high diagnostic performance in both the training cohort and the independent validation cohort (GSE94019), and previous studies have indicated that its expression can be rapidly induced under diabetic conditions, such as HG and hyperinsulinemia [30]. Compared with other genes that have been previously reported, the role of LAMB1 in PDR remains largely unexplored. Considering its potential involvement in endothelial cell angiogenesis via VEGF-related signaling pathways [28], LAMB1 was selected for further functional validation in this study, aiming to elucidate its role in retinal vascular remodeling under diabetic conditions. In this study, LAMB1 expression was examined under hypoxic conditions, and hypoxia alone or in combination with HG significantly upregulated LAMB1 protein, suggesting that LAMB1 responds to multiple pathological stimuli present in the PDR microenvironment. LAMB1 knockdown significantly inhibited HG-induced HREC migration and tube formation, and downregulated the levels of VEGFA, ICAM-1, TNF-α, and IL-1β, suggesting its role in promoting angiogenesis, endothelial activation and inflammation under hyperglycemic conditions. The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is a well-established regulator of angiogenesis [31, 32]. Wnt3a, a canonical Wnt ligand, activates β-catenin, which then translocates into the nucleus and promotes the transcription of downstream target genes, including c-Myc and VEGF, both of which are critical for endothelial cell proliferation and tube formation [33]. Studies have shown that Wnt signaling is abnormally activated in the diabetic retina and contributes to the pathogenesis of DR [34]. The results of the present study revealed that knockdown of LAMB1 suppressed the HG-induced upregulation of key molecules in the classical Wnt/β-catenin pathway, including Wnt3a, β-catenin, and c-Myc. These findings suggested that LAMB1 might promote angiogenesis through regulation of the Wnt/β-catenin–VEGF signaling axis, providing initial insight into the molecular mechanism underlying its pro-angiogenic effect.

In summary, this study integrated transcriptomic analysis, machine learning approaches, and in vitro functional experiments to explore the regulatory mechanisms underlying PDR. LAMB1 was identified as a gene of interest and shown to play a potential role in modulating retinal angiogenesis and immune-related responses. These findings contributed to a better understanding of PDR pathogenesis and might offer preliminary insights into novel strategies for targeting endothelial dysfunction in DR.
