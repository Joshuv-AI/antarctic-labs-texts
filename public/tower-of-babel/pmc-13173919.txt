The mediating role of death anxiety between meaning in life and spiritual care competence among Chinese nursing interns: implications for healthcare training
Authors: Zhenshuang Zhang, Lida Yang, Xiao Zong, Ni Ketut Kardiyudiani, Li Han, Rui Xie, Rui Zhang, Chunting Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn Chinese culture, discussions about death are often considered taboo, which may intensify death anxiety among nursing interns when facing end-of-life situations. Death anxiety may influence their ability to provide spiritual care, yet the underlying mechanisms remain unclear.AimThis study aimed to examine the relationship between meaning in life, death anxiety, and spiritual care competence among nursing interns, and to explore the mediating role of death anxiety.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study. A cross-sectional survey was conducted among 737 final-year vocational nursing interns from a medical college in China using whole-cohort sampling. Data were analyzed using SPSS 25.0, including t-tests, ANOVA, Pearson correlations, and mediation analysis via Hayes’ process plug-in.ResultsSignificant associations were observed among meaning in life, death anxiety, and spiritual care competence. Meaning in life, was positively correlated with spiritual care competence (r = 0.520, p < 0.001) and weakly negatively correlated with death anxiety (r = -0.077, p = 0.036). Death anxiety was negatively correlated with spiritual care competence (r = -0.196, p < 0.001). Mediation analysis showed that death anxiety partially mediated the relationship between meaning in life, and spiritual care competence, with an indirect effect of 0.019 (95% CI: 0.001–0.041), accounting for 2.24% of the total effect.ConclusionDeath anxiety partially mediated the association between meaning in life and spiritual care competence, with a small but statistically significant indirect effect. Introduction

Spiritual care in nursing involves addressing patients′ spiritual needs within a holistic model of care [1], recognizing the multidimensional nature of health. However, evidence suggests that nursing students often feel insufficiently prepared to provide spiritual care, underscoring the need to strengthen spiritual care competence (SCC) within nursing education [2]. Limited preparation may reduce students’ readiness to offer effective spiritual support in clinical settings.

The Chinese cultural context presents unique considerations for understanding SCC. Influenced by Confucianism, Taoism, and Buddhism, traditional Chinese culture often regards death as a sensitive or even taboo topic [3], which may heighten discomfort when nursing interns encounter end-of-life situations. Moreover, meaning in life (MIL) within Chinese culture is frequently shaped by collectivist values that emphasize family harmony, social responsibility, and intergenerational continuity [4]. These cultural characteristics may shape how nursing interns construct professional meaning and cope with death-related stress.

Within this context, SCC may be shaped by an interplay of existential and psychological factors. Providing spiritual care often requires engaging with suffering, loss, and mortality, suggesting that nurses’ personal existential resources may influence their professional competence. MIL can function as a psychological resource that supports resilience and professional engagement. In contrast, exposure to death and dying may evoke death anxiety (DA), which could undermine confidence and willingness to engage in spiritually sensitive care.

Drawing on Terror Management Theory, which posits that awareness of mortality can influence psychological functioning and behavior, this study proposes that MIL is positively associated with SCC and that DA may partially mediate this relationship. Examining these associations within the Chinese cultural context may contribute to a more culturally responsive understanding of spiritual care development in nursing education.

From an educational perspective, nursing students frequently encounter death-related experiences during clinical training without adequate psychological preparation. Elevated DA may impair communication with dying patients and hinder spiritual care delivery, whereas a stronger sense of MIL may buffer existential distress and enhance professional commitment. Despite these theoretical links, empirical research examining the interrelationships among MIL, DA, and SCC in nursing interns remains limited. Addressing this gap is essential for informing educational strategies aimed at strengthening SCC.

Background

SCC involves recognizing and addressing the needs of the human spirit during times of trauma, illness, or grief [5]. Previous research suggests that nurses’ ability to provide spiritual care is influenced not only by professional training but also by their personal spiritual resources [6]. Greater personal spirituality has been associated with increased sensitivity to patients existential concerns and stronger confidence in delivering spiritual care [7].

Spirituality is often conceptualized as the capacity to seek meaning, purpose, and connectedness, particularly in the face of suffering and mortality. Within this framework, MIL represents a central existential resource [8]. MIL refers to the extent to which individuals perceive their lives as purposeful and coherent [9]. Empirical research has consistently demonstrated that a stronger sense of meaning is associated with greater psychological resilience when confronting adversity and death.

Importantly, MIL has been linked to lower levels of DA. Individuals who perceive their lives as meaningful tend to experience less existential distress when facing mortality [10]. In contrast, death anxiety—defined as a negative emotional response triggered by awareness of death—has been associated with psychological strain and burnout among healthcare providers [11]. Collectively, these findings suggest that existential resources may buffer mortality-related fears.

Given that spiritual care frequently involves engagement with seriously ill or dying patients, nursing interns’ own levels of MIL and DA may influence their SCC. From the perspective of Terror Management Theory, awareness of mortality can threaten existential security; however, strong meaning systems may mitigate this threat and reduce anxiety responses. Accordingly, DA may function as a psychological mechanism through which MIL influences SCC.

Although SCC has received increasing attention in nursing education, psychological barriers that may impede its development remain underexplored. DA is commonly experienced by nursing students during clinical exposure and may influence their willingness to engage in conversations about suffering, meaning, and mortality. Students with elevated DA may avoid emotionally demanding interactions, thereby limiting opportunities to develop SCC. Nevertheless, empirical research examining DA as a potential mediating mechanism between MIL and SCC is scarce.

In Chinese higher education, final-year students enrolled in three-year vocational nursing diploma programs who have completed their full-time clinical internship represent a transitional stage between formal education and entry into professional practice. In this study, these students are referred to as “nursing interns.” To date, no studies have examined whether DA mediates the relationship between MIL and SCC in this population.

Therefore, this study aims to:

Assess the levels of MIL, DA, and SCC among junior nursing interns; and

Assess the levels of MIL, DA, and SCC among junior nursing interns; and

Examine whether DA mediates the relationship between MIL and SCC in this population.

Examine whether DA mediates the relationship between MIL and SCC in this population.

Hypothesized model

This study is grounded in Terror Management Theory (TMT). TMT posits that awareness of mortality generates the potential for existential anxiety, which individuals manage by investing in cultural worldviews and sources of meaning that provide psychological security and symbolic continuity [12]. Applied to the nursing context, a strong sense of MIL may function as a psychological resource that helps buffer death-related anxiety. For nursing interns, a higher level of MIL may be associated with reduced DA when encountering patient suffering and mortality during clinical training.

Lower levels of DA may, in turn, be associated with greater emotional and cognitive capacity to engage with patients’ spiritual and existential concerns, potentially facilitating the development of SCC. Prior empirical findings are consistent with this framework. Research has demonstrated that a stronger sense of MIL is associated with lower levels of DA [13] and more positive attitudes toward death [14]. Within nursing contexts, perspectives on death have also been linked to spirituality and the provision of spiritual care [15].

Methods

Design, setting and participants

A convenience sampling approach was adopted. The study was conducted at a vocational medical college in Heilongjiang Province, Northeast China, between November and December 2023. All 22 classes in the final-year nursing cohort were invited to participate, representing the entire graduating cohort at the institution during the study period. A total of 810 students were eligible for inclusion.

Eligible participants were final-year students enrolled in a three-year vocational college nursing diploma program who had completed their mandatory full-time clinical internship prior to graduation (hereafter referred to as “nursing interns”). Students who had withdrawn from the program or were on leave were excluded.

At the time of data collection, hospice care, spiritual care, and death education were not offered as standalone mandatory courses within the institution’s official curriculum. Therefore, reported training experiences referred primarily to supplementary or elective learning activities.

Instruments

Demographic information

Participants reported demographic characteristics and educational experiences, including gender, household location, sibling status, religious beliefs, student leadership roles, reasons for choosing nursing, first-choice major status, participation in student organizations, professional satisfaction, future career plans, and prior exposure to training related to hospice care, spiritual care, or death education.

The Chinese version of Meaning in Life Questionnaire (C-MLQ)

We used the Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ) developed by Steger et al. [16]. The MLQ is a 10-item scale comprising two 5-item subscales: the presence of meaning and the search for meaning. Responses are recorded on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (absolutely untrue) to 7 (absolutely true). The original validation study reported Cronbach’s α coefficients of 0.84 and 0.85 for the presence and search subscales, respectively. In the present study, the overall scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.846. Higher total scores indicate a stronger sense of MIL, with possible total scores ranging from 10 to 70.

The Chinese version of Templer Death Anxiety Scale (CT-DAS)

We used the Chinese version of the Templer Death Anxiety Scale (CT-DAS) developed by Templer [17]. The CT-DAS is a 15-item unidimensional scale. Responses are recorded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Possible total scores range from 15 to 75, with higher scores indicating higher levels of DA. The original validation study reported a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.71. In the present study, the overall scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.843.

The Chinese version of Spiritual Care Competence Scale (C-SCCS)

We used the Spiritual Care Competence Scale (SCCS) developed by van Leeuwen et al. [18]. The SCCS is a 27-item scale comprising three domains: assessment and implementation of spiritual care, professionalization and quality improvement, and personal support and patient counseling. Responses are recorded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 27 to 135. with higher scores indicating a higher level of SCC. The original validation study reported Cronbach’s α coefficients of 0.93, 0.92, and 0.89 for the three domains, respectively. In the present study, the overall scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.852.

Ethical considerations

The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Heilongjiang University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (approval number: KY2023-066). Furthermore, prior to completing the necessary data collection forms, participating nursing interns must provide their consent. The consent forms provided to each participant included details regarding the purpose, methods, benefits, and possible risks of the study. The participants were able to decline participation at any moment; the choice to participate was entirely theirs. Participants’ privacy was scrupulously protected, and no identity information was collected.

Data collection

The importance and objective of this cross-sectional study were outlined in an Internet-based questionnaire (https://www.wjx.cn/vm/OFwglp5.aspx) and a mobile phone application. Participants were given the choice to accept or reject the survey after following the instructions. Anonymity and confidentiality were guaranteed. 26 nursing interns were pre-surveyed prior to the official study. In 4–6 min, the questionnaire was completed. The items on the questionnaire were logically arranged, and the topic was communicated clearly.

Prior to the study, the researchers contacted the educators or counselors and provided them with a survey (Additional File 1). Participants could only submit one survey at a time. After removing 73 invalid surveys (obvious logic flaws or short answer durations) removed, 737 questionnaires were retained, yielding an effective response rate of 92%.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all study variables. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations after assessment of normality.

Independent-samples t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were performed to examine differences in SCC across categorical variables. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to assess associations among MIL, DA, and SCC.Effect sizes are interpreted as weak (r = 0.10–0.29), moderate (r = 0.30–0.49), or strong (r ≥ 0.50).

To examine the mediating role of DA, mediation analysis was conducted using Hayes’ PROCESS macro (Model 4). The significance of indirect effects was tested using the bootstrap method with 5,000 resamples, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. An indirect effect was considered statistically significant if the 95% CI did not include zero. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Effects of independent variables on vocational college nursing interns’ SCC

Significant differences in SCC were observed among nursing interns who held student leadership roles (t = 2.391, p = 0.017). In addition, interns who had received training related to hospice care, spiritual care, or death education demonstrated significantly higher SCC scores than those who had not (t = 4.932, p < 0.001).

Post-hoc analyses indicated that students who chose nursing autonomously had significantly higher SCC scores than those influenced by family or teacher recommendations and those who selected nursing for other reasons. Furthermore, students reporting strong enjoyment of the nursing profession and high willingness to pursue a nursing career exhibited significantly higher SCC scores compared to those expressing lower enjoyment or hesitation (see Table 2).

The mediating effect of DA between MIL and SCC

As shown in Tables 4 and 5, mediation analysis using bootstrap confidence intervals revealed that MIL negatively predicted DA (β = -0.077, 95% CI = -0.146, -0.005, t = -2.096, p = 0.036) and positively predicted SCC (β = 0.805, 95% CI = 0.709, 0.901, t = 16.488, p < 0.001), whereas DA negatively predicted SCC (β = -0.249, 95% CI = -0.346, -0.152, t = -5.052, p < 0.001). The direct effect of MIL on SCC remained significant after including DA as a mediator (β = 0.786, 95% CI = 0.691, 0.880, t = 16.322, p < 0.001), indicating a partial mediating role of DA. The indirect effect of MIL on SCC through DA was statistically significant but small (β = 0.019, 95% CI = 0.001, 0.041), accounting for 2.24% of the total effect. These results suggest that DA partially mediates the association between MIL and SCC; however, the magnitude of the mediation effect is minimal and should be interpreted cautiously (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The SCC of nursing interns

The present study found that nursing interns demonstrated a moderate level of SCC, slightly lower than levels reported among registered nurses in China [19] and above the scale midpoint (27–135), with a mean score of 95.42 ± 14.34. This finding is consistent with previous research [20] and slightly lower than the levels reported among practicing nurses. This pattern is understandable given the transitional status of interns, who are still consolidating clinical judgment, professional identity, and interpersonal confidence. Clinical practice serves as a crucial developmental context, as it provides experiential opportunities to integrate theoretical knowledge with the relational and reflective capacities required for holistic care.

Leadership experience was positively associated with higher SCC. Interns who had served in student leadership roles reported greater competence, suggesting that extracurricular professional engagement may contribute to competence development. Leadership roles often foster responsibility, empathy, communication skills, and collaborative capacity—attributes closely aligned with the relational dimensions of spiritual care. From a professional identity perspective, leadership experiences may strengthen self-efficacy and role internalization, thereby enhancing readiness to engage in spiritually sensitive interactions.

Intrinsic professional motivation also emerged as a significant factor. Students who chose nursing autonomously and expressed stronger career commitment demonstrated higher SCC levels. These findings underscore the importance of internalized professional values in competence formation. When nursing is perceived as meaningful and self-endorsed, students may be more inclined toward reflective practice and emotionally engaged care. Educational strategies such as mentorship programs, early clinical immersion, and structured career planning may therefore play a critical role in nurturing sustained professional commitment and spiritual caregiving capacity.

Furthermore, exposure to spiritual care training and death education was associated with higher SCC. Structured educational experiences may extend beyond knowledge acquisition by fostering self-reflection, emotional preparedness, and communication skills necessary for addressing patients’ existential concerns. Integrating systematic spiritual care and end-of-life education into nursing curricula appears essential for strengthening competence in this domain.

Mediating effects of DA on MILand SCC

The present study further examined the mediating role of DA in the relationship between MIL and SCC. Consistent with theoretical expectations, MIL was positively associated with SCC and negatively associated with DA. Mediation analysis indicated that DA partially mediated this relationship. Although the indirect effect accounted for a relatively small proportion (2.24%) of the total effect, it remained statistically significant.

The modest magnitude of the indirect effect suggests that DA represents one identifiable existential mechanism linking personal meaning and professional competence, rather than the primary explanatory pathway. The association between MIL and SCC is likely multifactorial. A stronger sense of meaning may enhance empathy, moral sensitivity, and professional self-efficacy [21], thereby facilitating deeper engagement with patients’ spiritual needs. Meaning may also foster resilience and compassion satisfaction [22], enabling nursing interns to manage the emotional demands of spiritually intensive clinical situations.

The small yet significant mediating effect suggests that DA represents one identifiable existential mechanism linking personal meaning and professional competence, but not the dominant pathway. The association between MIL and SCC is likely multifactorial.

Importantly, a small indirect effect does not diminish theoretical relevance. From the perspective of Terror Management Theory, existential buffering processes may operate alongside multiple psychological mechanisms. The findings suggest that the anxiety-buffering function of meaning systems may extend beyond personal psychological adjustment and influence professional competence within contexts involving suffering and mortality. In this sense, existential resources may shape not only how nursing interns cope with death-related concerns but also how effectively they provide compassionate spiritual care.

Practically, the results support a multidimensional educational approach. Interventions designed to enhance SCC may benefit from simultaneously strengthening existential resources and addressing death-related distress. Meaning-centered reflective activities can cultivate professional purpose and internal coherence, while structured death education may normalize discussions of mortality and promote adaptive coping strategies. Although DA accounted for a modest proportion of the overall association, addressing it may facilitate the translation of personal meaning into competent and compassionate care.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as the research was conducted in a single institution rather than through multi-regional random sampling, the findings may not be fully generalizable to all nursing interns in China. Second, personal spiritual experiences were not assessed, although such experiences may influence SCC. Third, the study relied exclusively on self-report measures without incorporating observational or behavioral assessments of MIL, DA, and SCC, which might have provided more objective data. Fourth, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference and does not allow for examination of longitudinal changes.

Additionally, given the relatively large sample size, some statistically significant findings—particularly the mediating effect of DA—were associated with small effect sizes. Therefore, these results should be interpreted cautiously with respect to their practical or educational significance. Future research employing longitudinal, experimental, or mixed-methods designs is warranted to further validate and expand upon these findings. Qualitative inquiry may also provide deeper insight into the lived experiences underlying the relationships among MIL, DA, and SCC.

Conclusions

This study indicates that MIL is positively associated with SCC among Chinese nursing interns, with DA serving as a statistically significant, albeit modest, mediator. These findings suggest that the development of SCC may involve not only technical and clinical skills but also underlying psychological and existential factors relevant to holistic care.

To translate these findings into educational practice, several strategies may be considered within nursing curricula. First, integrating structured death education may help normalize discussions about mortality and enhance communication skills in end-of-life care. Second, meaning-centered educational activities, such as reflective workshops or group counseling, may support students in exploring and strengthening their personal sense of purpose. Third, expanding experiential training in spiritual care—through simulations, standardized patient encounters, and supervised clinical practice—may further reinforce competence in spiritual assessment and empathetic communication.

Taken together, these complementary approaches may contribute to preparing nursing students for the spiritual dimensions of patient care. However, given the cross-sectional design and modest effect sizes observed, longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to further examine causal pathways and evaluate the long-term effectiveness of these educational strategies. Future research should also explore additional mediators, such as empathy and self-efficacy, to refine theoretical models of SCC development.
