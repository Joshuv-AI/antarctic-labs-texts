1
Introduction

Let
D
be an oriented graph.
For a vertex
v\in V(D)
, denote by
N_{1}^{+}(v)
its out-neighborhood, and by

N_{2}^{+}(v)
the set of vertices reachable from
v
by a directed path of length
two that are neither out-neighbors of
v
nor equal to
v
.
We refer to
N_{2}^{+}(v)
as the set of
new second out-neighbors
of
v
.

The
Second Neighborhood Conjecture
, formulated by Seymour, asserts that
every oriented graph contains a vertex
v
satisfying

|N_{2}^{+}(v)|\geqslant|N_{1}^{+}(v)|.

This conjecture has attracted considerable attention and is known to hold in a
number of special cases, most notably for tournaments

[
5
,
3
,
6
]
.
Further partial results and related investigations appear in

[
7
,
8
,
2
,
9
]
.
Probabilistic aspects of the conjecture are an emergin and promising avenue in the topic (e.g. see
[
10
,
4
,
1
]
). In particular,
random oriented graphs are known to satisfy the second neighborhood property
with high probability, suggesting that potential counterexamples, if they
exist, must exhibit strong structural constraints
[
4
]
.

[
11
]
wrote about possible structural properties of counterexamples to the discussed conjecture.

It is convenient to encode the conjecture via the graph invariant

\Delta(D)=\max_{v\in V(D)}\bigl(|N_{2}^{+}(v)|-|N_{1}^{+}(v)|\bigr),

so that Seymour’s conjecture is equivalent to the assertion that

\Delta(D)\geqslant 0
for all oriented graphs.

Despite sustained effort, the conjecture remains open in full generality and no general inductive mechanism is currently known that extends the
validity of the conjecture from small graphs to arbitrarily large ones.

The purpose of this paper is twofold.
First, we provide a complete computational verification of the inequality

\Delta(D)\geqslant 0
for all oriented graphs on at most seven vertices.
Second, we introduce an inductive graph operation, which we call the

split–twin extension
, and prove that it preserves the nonnegativity of

\Delta(D)
.

Together, these establish the second neighborhood property for all
oriented graphs of order at most seven and for all graphs obtained from them by
iterated split–twin extensions.

2
Preliminaries

An
oriented graph
is a directed graph with no loops and no pair of
oppositely oriented edges. Throughout the paper, all graphs are finite.

Let
D
be an oriented graph with vertex set
V(D)
.
For a vertex
v\in V(D)
, the
out-neighborhood
of
v
is defined as

N_{1}^{+}(v)=\{u\in V(D):v\to u\}.

The
new second out-neighborhood
of
v
is the set

N_{2}^{+}(v)=\bigl\{w\in V(D)\setminus(N_{1}^{+}(v)\cup\{v\}):\exists\,u\in V(D)\text{ with }v\to u\to w\bigr\}.

That is,
N_{2}^{+}(v)
consists of vertices reachable from
v
by a directed
path of length two that are neither out-neighbors of
v
nor equal to
v
.

We measure the discrepancy between first and second out-neighborhoods via the
following quantity.

Definition 1
.

For a vertex
v\in V(D)
, the
second neighborhood margin
of
v
is

\mathrm{margin}(v)=|N_{2}^{+}(v)|-|N_{1}^{+}(v)|.

The
second neighborhood invariant
of
D
is

\Delta(D)=\max_{v\in V(D)}\mathrm{margin}(v).

Note that Seymour’s Second Neighborhood Conjecture is equivalent to the
assertion that
\Delta(D)\geqslant 0
for every oriented graph
D
.

Definition 2
.

Let
D
be an oriented graph.
A vertex
v\in V(D)
is called a
Seymour vertex
if

|N_{2}^{+}(v)|\geqslant|N_{1}^{+}(v)|,

or equivalently, if
\mathrm{margin}(v)\geqslant 0
.

Equivalently,
D
contains a Seymour vertex if and only if
\Delta(D)\geqslant 0
.

Observation 3
.

If
v\in V(D)
has outdegree zero, then

|N_{1}^{+}(v)|=|N_{2}^{+}(v)|=0
, and hence
\mathrm{margin}(v)=0
.

Proof.

If
v
has outdegree zero, then
N_{1}^{+}(v)=\varnothing
.
Since there is no vertex
u
with
v\to u
, there is no directed path of length
two starting at
v
, and thus
N_{2}^{+}(v)=\varnothing
as well.
∎

Accordingly, throughout this paper we restrict attention to oriented graphs
with minimum outdegree at least one, as vertices of outdegree zero trivially
satisfy the second neighborhood inequality.

3
Computational Verification for Small Graphs

We begin by establishing the Second Neighborhood Conjecture for all
oriented graphs of small order by exhaustive computation.

Theorem 4
.

Let
D
be an oriented graph on at most seven vertices.
Then
\Delta(D)\geqslant 0
.
Equivalently, every such graph contains a Seymour vertex.

Proof.

For each integer
n\leqslant 7
, all oriented graphs on the labeled vertex set

\{1,\dots,n\}
were generated.
If
D
contains a vertex of outdegree zero, then
D
contains a Seymour vertex
by Observation
3
; hence such graphs require no further checking
and were skipped during enumeration.

For every remaining graph
D
, the values
|N_{1}^{+}(v)|
and
|N_{2}^{+}(v)|

were computed exactly for each vertex
v
, and the invariant

\Delta(D)=\max_{v\in V(D)}\bigl(|N_{2}^{+}(v)|-|N_{1}^{+}(v)|\bigr)

was evaluated.
No graph with
\Delta(D)<0
was encountered.
∎

Remark 5
.

The verification for
n=7
involves

3^{21}\approx 1.05\times 10^{10}
labeled oriented graphs,
of which more than
5.2\times 10^{9}
satisfy the minimum outdegree condition.
The computation completed in about
1
–
2
hours on a standard laptop
(single CPU core, no GPU acceleration).

For details on the computational aspects, please refer to App.
A
.

4
Split–Twin Extensions and Inductive Preservation

In this section we introduce an inductive graph operation and show that it
preserves the nonnegativity of the second neighborhood invariant.
This yields infinite families of oriented graphs satisfying the
second neighborhood property.

4.1
The split–twin extension

Definition 6

(Split–Twin Extension)
.

Let
D
be an oriented graph and let
x\in V(D)
.
A
split–twin extension
of
D
at
x
is the oriented graph
D^{\prime}

obtained as follows.
The vertex
x
is removed and replaced by two new vertices
x^{\prime}
and
x^{\prime\prime}
,
subject to the following rules:

1.

For every vertex
u\neq x
, if
u\to x
is an edge of
D
, then
both
u\to x^{\prime}
and
u\to x^{\prime\prime}
are edges of
D^{\prime}
.

2.

The out-neighborhood
N_{1}^{+}(x)
is partitioned into two (possibly empty)
sets
A
and
B
.

3.

For each
a\in A
we add the edge
x^{\prime}\to a
, and for each
b\in B

we add the edge
x^{\prime\prime}\to b
.

4.

No edge is added between
x^{\prime}
and
x^{\prime\prime}
.

All other edges of
D
are inherited unchanged by
D^{\prime}
.

Intuitively, the split–twin extension replaces a vertex by two “twins”
that share all incoming edges while dividing the outgoing edges between them.

4.2
Preservation of Seymour vertices

The key observation is that the split–twin extension preserves the
second neighborhood inequality at vertices sufficiently far from the split.

Lemma 7
.

Let
D
be an oriented graph and let
v\in V(D)
satisfy

|N_{2}^{+}(v)|\geqslant|N_{1}^{+}(v)|.

Let
x\in V(D)
be a vertex distinct from
v
such that

x\notin N_{1}^{+}(v)
, and let
D^{\prime}
be obtained from
D
by a split–twin
extension at
x
.
Then

|N_{2}^{+}(v)|_{D^{\prime}}\geqslant|N_{1}^{+}(v)|_{D^{\prime}}.

Proof.

Since
x\notin N_{1}^{+}(v)
, neither of the new vertices
x^{\prime}
nor
x^{\prime\prime}

lies in the out-neighborhood of
v
in
D^{\prime}
.
Hence

|N_{1}^{+}(v)|_{D^{\prime}}=|N_{1}^{+}(v)|.

Let
w\in N_{2}^{+}(v)
in
D
.
If
w\neq x
, then any directed path
v\to u\to w
in
D
remains unchanged
in
D^{\prime}
, so
w\in N_{2}^{+}(v)
in
D^{\prime}
.
If
w=x
, then there exists a directed path
v\to u\to x
in
D
.
By construction, both
u\to x^{\prime}
and
u\to x^{\prime\prime}
are edges of
D^{\prime}
,
so at least one of
x^{\prime}
or
x^{\prime\prime}
lies in
N_{2}^{+}(v)
in
D^{\prime}
.

Therefore no element of
N_{2}^{+}(v)
is lost under the extension, and possibly
one additional second out-neighbor is gained.
Consequently,

|N_{2}^{+}(v)|_{D^{\prime}}\geqslant|N_{2}^{+}(v)|.

Combining this with the equality for first neighborhoods proves the claim.
∎

4.3
Inductive consequence

The lemma immediately yields an inductive preservation result for the
second neighborhood invariant.

Theorem 8
.

Let
D
be an oriented graph with
\Delta(D)\geqslant 0
.
Suppose
v
is a vertex of
D
satisfying

|N_{2}^{+}(v)|\geqslant|N_{1}^{+}(v)|
, and let
x\in V(D)
be any vertex such that

x\notin N_{1}^{+}(v)
.
Then every split–twin extension of
D
at
x
produces a graph
D^{\prime}

with
\Delta(D^{\prime})\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

By Lemma
7
, the vertex
v
continues to satisfy

|N_{2}^{+}(v)|\geqslant|N_{1}^{+}(v)|
in
D^{\prime}
.
Hence
\Delta(D^{\prime})\geqslant 0
.
∎

Corollary 9
.

There exist infinite families of oriented graphs of arbitrarily large order
for which
\Delta(D)\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

Starting from any oriented graph
D
with
\Delta(D)\geqslant 0
, repeated
application of split–twin extensions at vertices outside the first
out-neighborhood of a Seymour vertex produces graphs of unbounded order.
Each such extension preserves the inequality
\Delta(D)\geqslant 0
by
Theorem
8
.
∎

5
Concluding Remarks

The split–twin extension introduced in this paper provides a simple and
structurally transparent inductive mechanism that preserves the second
neighborhood property.
Together with the exhaustive verification for small graphs, this yields an
explicit infinite class of oriented graphs for which

\Delta(D)\geqslant 0
holds.

The computational verification demonstrates that all oriented graphs on at most
seven vertices with minimum outdegree at least one satisfy the conjectured
inequality.
While the enumeration for
n\leqslant 7
is feasible on standard hardware, the total
number of oriented graphs grows as
3^{\binom{n}{2}}
, making further exhaustive
verification computationally demanding.
Nevertheless, the methodology described here is fully parallelizable, and
larger values of
n
may become accessible through the use of high-performance
computing resources.

Beyond numerical verification, the observed absence of counterexamples at small
orders suggests that extremal configurations are subject to strong structural
constraints.
The split–twin construction provides one concrete framework in which such
constraints can be exploited inductively, and it is natural to ask whether
additional reduction or extension operations may be identified that preserve
the nonnegativity of
\Delta(D)
.

Future work includes the incorporation of complete computational data,
refined structural analysis of extremal graphs, and the investigation of further
inductive schemes that may contribute toward a full resolution of Seymour’s
Second Neighborhood Conjecture.

Appendix A
Computational Methodology

Here, we describe the computational approach used to verify
Theorem
4
.
All computations were performed by exhaustive enumeration and exact
combinatorial evaluation.
No heuristics, randomization, or probabilistic sampling were employed.

A.1
Representation of oriented graphs

For a fixed integer
n
, an oriented graph on the vertex set

\{1,2,\dots,n\}
is determined by an assignment to each unordered
pair
\{i,j\}
,
i<j
, of one of three states:

1.

no edge between
i
and
j
;

2.

a directed edge
i\to j
;

3.

a directed edge
j\to i
.

Hence the total number of oriented graphs on
n
labeled vertices is

3^{\binom{n}{2}}
.

In the implementation, each graph is encoded as a vector of length

\binom{n}{2}
over the alphabet
\{0,1,2\}
, corresponding to the
three states above.
For efficient neighborhood computations, the graph is additionally
represented by bitmasks:
for each vertex
v
, a bitmask
M(v)
encodes its out-neighborhood

N_{1}^{+}(v)
.
We identify
V(D)=\{1,\dots,n\}
with the first
n
bits of a machine word, so unions and set differences are computed via bitwise OR and AND-NOT operations.

A.2
Enumeration

For each
n\leqslant 7
, all
3^{\binom{n}{2}}
oriented graphs were generated
by iterating through all base–
3
representations of length

\binom{n}{2}
.
This guarantees exhaustive coverage without duplication.

Graphs with minimum outdegree zero were discarded immediately, as such
vertices trivially satisfy the second neighborhood inequality.
No isomorphism reduction was applied; all graphs were treated as labeled.
This avoids the overhead of canonical labeling and ensures that the
verification is purely combinatorial.

A.3
Computation of second neighborhoods

For each vertex
v
, the out-neighborhood
N_{1}^{+}(v)
is read directly
from the corresponding bitmask.
To compute the new second out-neighborhood
N_{2}^{+}(v)
, we proceed as follows.

Define

U(v)=\bigcup_{u\in N_{1}^{+}(v)}N_{1}^{+}(u),

the set of vertices reachable from
v
by a directed path of length two.
Then

N_{2}^{+}(v)=U(v)\setminus\bigl(N_{1}^{+}(v)\cup\{v\}\bigr).

All set operations are implemented at the bitmask level, allowing exact
computation of the cardinalities
|N_{1}^{+}(v)|
and
|N_{2}^{+}(v)|
.

The second neighborhood margin

\mathrm{margin}(v)=|N_{2}^{+}(v)|-|N_{1}^{+}(v)|

is computed for each vertex
v
, and the invariant

\Delta(D)=\max_{v}\mathrm{margin}(v)
is recorded.

Verification criterion and results

A graph is declared to satisfy the second neighborhood property if

Appendix B
Runtime Environment and Performance

This appendix documents the computational environment and performance
characteristics of the exhaustive verification described in
Section
3
.

B.1
Hardware and execution environment

All computations were performed on a consumer-grade laptop with the
following specifications:

•

CPU: AMD Ryzen 7 6800HS Creator Edition;

•

RAM: 28 GB;

•

Operating system: Microsoft Windows 11 Home (64-bit);

•

Julia version: 1.11.2 (official release).

The implementation was executed in a strictly single-threaded mode
(
Threads.nthreads() = 1
).
No GPU acceleration, distributed computing, or external high-performance
resources were used.

B.2
Runtime measurements

Performance was evaluated by measuring sustained throughput during long
runs of the enumeration.
The program processes approximately
5\times 10^{6}
oriented graphs in
about
3.25
seconds, corresponding to an average throughput of
approximately
1.5\times 10^{6}
graphs per second.

For
n=7
, the exhaustive verification requires evaluating approximately

5.2\times 10^{9}
oriented graphs satisfying the minimum outdegree
constraint.
Based on the observed throughput, the total runtime is on the order of

1
–
2
hours of wall-clock time, depending on I/O and reporting settings.

For comparison, the complete enumeration for
n=6

(
3^{15}\approx 1.43\times 10^{7}
graphs) completes in under 6 seconds
on the same machine.

B.3
Exactness and determinism

The implementation relies exclusively on integer and bitwise operations.
No floating-point arithmetic, randomization, heuristic pruning, or early
termination is employed.
The enumeration order is deterministic, and each oriented graph is
processed exactly once.

As a result, the verification for
n\leqslant 7
is complete and exact: the
absence of graphs with
\Delta(D)<0
is a definitive computational fact,
not a probabilistic observation.

B.4
Scalability considerations

The number of labeled oriented graphs grows as
3^{\binom{n}{2}}
, making
naive exhaustive verification infeasible beyond
n=7
on a single
machine.
For instance,
n=8
would require examining

3^{28}\approx 2.2\times 10^{13}
graphs.

Nevertheless, the enumeration is embarrassingly parallel and could be
distributed across multiple cores or compute nodes.
This suggests that verification for larger values of
n
may be feasible
using modern high-performance computing facilities.
