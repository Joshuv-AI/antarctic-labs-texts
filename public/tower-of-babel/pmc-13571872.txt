EPHX2 Loss Promotes CRC Migration via EET‐Dependent Chemokine Elevation and Mitochondrial Defect
Authors: Jianjun Zhu, Jingjia Chang, Zhiyong Li, Jin Wu, Pengfei Yu, Jingjing Zheng, Li Li, Jia Tao, Hao Wu, Yintao Li, Ming Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEpoxide hydrolase 2 (EPHX2) is a key enzyme in fatty acid metabolism that metabolizes epoxyeicosatrienoic acids (EETs), lipid mediators involved in inflammation, angiogenesis, and carcinogenesis. However, the role and mechanism of EPHX2 in colorectal cancer (CRC) migration remain unclear. In this study, we employed an integrated approach of bioinformatics and functional experiments to examine EPHX2 expression, its clinical implications, and its mechanistic role in CRC migration. Our results showed that EPHX2 was significantly downregulated in CRC tissues, and its low expression correlated with poor prognosis. Overexpression of EPHX2 led to accumulation of HDHA, HEPE, and HETE in CRC cells, whereas loss of EPHX2 increased CXCL levels and suppressed mitochondrial biogenesis via EET accumulation. Furthermore, loss of EPHX2 promoted cell migration through ROS and CXCL signaling. Collectively, loss of EPHX2 accelerates cell migration via EET accumulation‐dependent chemokine elevation and mitochondrial biogenesis impairment in CRC. These findings reveal a novel metabolic‐mitochondrial axis in CRC progression and highlight EPHX2 as a potential therapeutic target.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains one of the most prevalent and lethal malignancies worldwide, with metastasis being the primary cause of cancer‐related mortality [1]. Despite advances in targeted therapies and immunotherapy, the molecular mechanisms underlying CRC progression, particularly those involving metabolic reprogramming and cellular energetics, remained incompletely understood [2]. Mounting evidence underscored the pivotal role of mitochondrial function in cancer cell proliferation and metastasis [3]. In particular, mitochondrial biogenesis, the process by which cells increase their mitochondrial mass and function, has been implicated in the initiation and progression of malignancies [3]. Notably, emerging evidence suggests that dysregulation of mitochondrial biogenesis can drive aggressive phenotypes in CRC, yet the underlying regulatory mechanisms connecting metabolic reprogramming to metastatic dissemination are poorly understood.

The arachidonic acid metabolic pathway has been implicated in carcinogenesis [4]. Epoxide hydrolase 2 (EPHX2), a bifunctional enzyme involved in fatty acid metabolism, has drawn increasing attention due to its function in regulating epoxyeicosatrienoic acids (EETs), lipid mediators that modulate inflammation, angiogenesis, and cell signaling [5]. While EPHX2 has been studied in the context of cardiovascular and inflammatory diseases, its function in cancer, particularly in CRC, was poorly defined [6]. Recently, several studies demonstrated a possible tumor‐suppressive role for EPHX2 in gastrointestinal cancers, with its loss correlating with advanced stage and poor prognosis [5, 7, 8, 9]. EPHX2 modulated cell cycle progression and inhibited tumor growth through its interaction with multiple signaling pathways [10]. Importantly, EPHX2‐mediated hydrolysis of EETs was known to reduce their bioavailability, suggesting that EPHX2 deficiency might lead to intracellular accumulation of EETs, which in turn could influence cancer‐relevant pathways [11].

Based on evidence from recent cellular and animal studies, we proposed that loss of EPHX2 drove cell migration through a novel mechanism involving EETs accumulation and subsequent impairment of mitochondrial biogenesis in CRC. Studies have reported that EETs could modulate mitochondrial function, possibly through interference with key transcriptional regulators such as PGC‐1α [12]. In this study, we systematically investigate the functional impact of EPHX2 deficiency on mitochondrial biogenesis and metastatic capability in CRC. Using multi‐omics approaches and functional experimental assays, we aimed to establish EPHX2 as a metabolic checkpoint that restricts CRC progression by maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis.

Material and Methods

Antibodies and Reagents

Primary antibodies used in this study are provided in Table S1. Detailed information of the reagents and the detailed procedures for EETs, MitoTEMPO, and AMG487 are provided in Data S1.

Data Collection

RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) data from 430 colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) samples were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Corresponding clinical information was sourced from the UCSC Xena platform (http://xena.ucsc.edu). After excluding 22 samples with incomplete clinical data or an overall survival of less than 30 days, a total of 408 tumor samples were included in the final analysis. Additionally, 39 adjacent non‐tumor samples were retrieved from TCGA. Three independent validation cohorts (GSE39582, GSE41258, and GSE44076) were also utilized, with the following sample composition: GSE39582 (535 tumor vs. 17 normal), GSE41258 (69 tumor vs. 25 normal), and GSE44076 (98 tumor vs. 98 normal). The UALCAN database was used to analyze the protein levels of EPHX2 in tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues in COAD.

Identification of Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs)

DEGs were identified using the “limma” package (version 3.5.1) in R. Analyses were conducted to compare gene expression between normal and tumor samples, as well as between high‐ and low‐EPHX2 expression groups within the TCGA‐COAD dataset. Genes meeting the criteria of |log2fold change| > 1 and adjusted p‐value < 0.01 were considered differentially expressed. Visualization of the DEGs was performed using the “GdcVolcanoPlot” package in R to generate volcano plots.

Survival Analysis in COAD Patients

The association between EPHX2 expression and overall survival (OS) in COAD patients was evaluated using the GEPIA2 database. Additionally, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed, with curves generated using the “survival” and “survminer” packages in R. Patients were stratified into high‐ and low‐expression groups based on the optimal cut‐off value for each gene.

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Analyses

To explore the functional implications of DEGs identified between high‐ and low‐expression EPHX2 groups, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed in R (version 3.5.1). The analyses were carried out using the “clusterProfiler”, “org.Hs.eg.db”, “enrichplot”, and “ggplot2” packages, with functional terms considered statistically significant at an adjusted p < 0.05.

Gene Set Enrichment Analyses (GSEA)

GSEA was conducted on the whole transcriptome of tumor samples using GSEA software (version 4.2.1). The curated gene set collection (v7.4) from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) was used as the reference. Gene sets were considered statistically significant if they met the stringent criteria of a nominal p < 0.001 and a false discovery rate (FDR) q < 0.001.

Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) and Correlation Analysis

The PPI network was constructed using genes extracted from the KEGG‐enriched signaling pathways. Network assembly was performed via the STRING database, followed by visualization in Cytoscape software (version 3.9.0). Correlation heatmaps and lollipop plots were generated using the online SRplot tool [13].

Cell Culture and Tissue Collection

Human colon cell lines were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). CRC tissues and paired adjacent normal colorectal tissues were obtained from 31 patients at the First Affiliated Hospital of Shanxi Medical University. Pathological examination confirmed the absence of cancer cells in all collected normal tissues. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanxi Medical University, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

qRT‐PCR, Western Blot, and Immunohistochemistry (IHC) Staining

Total RNA was extracted from CRC tissues and cell lines using RNAiso Plus, followed by reverse transcription into cDNA with PrimeScript RT Master Mix. Quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR) was conducted using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II, with β‐actin serving as the internal reference gene. Primer sequences were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) and are provided in Table S2. Western blot analysis was performed according to previously described methods [14]. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis was carried out as described in Data S1.

Knockdown and Overexpression of Target Genes

Small‐interfering RNAs (siRNAs), eukaryotic recombinant plasmid overexpressing EPHX2 (EPHX2‐OE), and the plasmid encoding short hairpin RNA targeting EPHX2 (shEPHX2) were purchased from General Biol Company (Chuzhou, China). The siRNA and primer sequences were listed in Table S2. For transfection, cells were seeded in 6‐well plates and transfected with plasmids or siRNAs at 60%–80% confluence using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent, following the manufacturer's instructions.

Targeted Arachidonic Acid Lipidomics Analysis

Cells were extracted with a solution containing 0.1% BHT/BHA, formic acid, and mixed internal standards, followed by incubation with methanol‐washed ceramic beads at 4°C and 1500 rpm for 12 h. After centrifugation (4°C, 12,000 rpm, 10 min), the supernatant was collected and the extraction was repeated twice. Combined supernatants underwent solid‐phase extraction (SPE) using an Oasis Prime HLB cartridge. The SPE eluate was evaporated under nitrogen after adding ethanol: glycerol (1:1), then reconstituted in 50 μL of water: acetonitrile: formic acid (63:37:0.02). Eicosanoid analysis was performed on an Exion AD30‐UPLC system coupled to a Sciex 6500 Plus Qtrap mass spectrometer, with a Phenomenex Kinetex‐C18 column and gradient elution using water: acetonitrile: formic acid (63:37:0.02) and acetonitrile: isopropanol (1:1) as mobile phases A and B, respectively. Metabolomics analysis was performed by LipidALL Technologies (Changzhou, China).

Measurement of Cytokines Levels by ELISA

The detection of CXCL was performed using a commercially available enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, 50 μL of standards or samples were added to the pre‐coated wells, followed by 50 μL of the provided antibody cocktail. After incubation at room temperature for 1 h, each well was aspirated and washed three times with 350 μL of 1 × wash buffer. Then, 100 μL of TMB development solution was added and incubated for 10 min. The reaction was stopped by adding 100 μL of stop solution, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Scratch Wound‐Healing Assay

Cells were seeded in six‐well plates and cultured until reaching approximately 90% confluence. A linear scratch was made in each well using a sterile pipette tip, after which the cells were incubated in serum‐free growth medium. Images were captured at 0 and 48 h using a microscope (Nikon, Japan). Cell migration distance was measured at both time points, and the migration rate (%) was calculated as follows: (migrated distance at indicated time−initial distance)/initial distance × 100%.

Cell Migration and Invasion Assay

Transwell chambers were utilized to assess cell migration and invasion. For invasion assays, chambers were precoated with Matrigel, whereas migration assays were performed without Matrigel. Cells were seeded into the upper chamber, and medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. After incubation for 24 h (migration) or 48 h (invasion), cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and counted. Cell migratory and invasive abilities were evaluated by averaging the number of cells from five random microscopic fields per well.

Detection of Lamellipodia Formation

Lamellipodia formation was assessed by staining actin filaments. After permeabilization, cells were stained with rhodamine‐phalloidin following established protocols [14].

Detection of EPHX2 Subcellular Location

The subcellular localization of EPHX2 was examined by immunofluorescence staining. Briefly, cells were labeled with MitoTracker Red (1 μM) at 37°C for 45 min in the dark. After washing, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X‐100 for 10 min, and blocked with 2% BSA for 30 min. Subsequently, cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with an EPHX2 primary antibody, followed by incubation with a fluorophore‐conjugated secondary antibody (1:200 dilution). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:200 dilution) for 15 min. Images were acquired using a FV1000 confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus).

Measurement of Mitochondrial Mass, mtDNA Copy Number, and Cellular ATP Level

A three‐dimensional (3D) mitochondrial model was constructed according to the method established by Cheverollier et al. [15]. Total genomic DNA was isolated from cell lines using the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit following the manufacturer's instructions. Relative mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) copy number was quantified by PCR as described previously [16]. The cellular ATP level was measured using ATP Assay Kit, according to the manufacturer's protocols.

Detection of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS)

Intracellular ROS levels were detected using a fluorescent ROS assay kit. Briefly, cells were incubated with the cell‐permeable probe DCFH‐DA, which diffused into cells and was hydrolyzed by intracellular esterases to DCFH. In the presence of ROS, DCFH is oxidized to the fluorescent compound DCF. Fluorescence intensity was measured and considered proportional to intracellular ROS content.

Detection of Mitochondrial Membrane Potential

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) was assessed using the fluorescent probe TMRM, following the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were stained with 10 nM TMRM for 10 min at 37°C in the dark. Fluorescence images were acquired with a FV1000 laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus) and analyzed using “ImagePro” software.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 3.5.1) and GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Group differences in gene expression were analyzed by Student's t‐test. One‐way ANOVA with post hoc tests was used for all comparisons involving three or more groups. Differences in clinical factors between groups were assessed using the chi‐squared test. Correlations were evaluated with the Spearman method. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Downregulation of EPHX2 Is Associated With Poor Prognosis in Patients With CRC

Analysis of the mRNA expression of EPHX2 in multiple independent cohorts revealed its consistent and significant downregulation in tumor tissues compared to paired adjacent peritumor tissues (Figure 1A). Further analysis revealed that the expression level of EPHX2 was progressively lower in tumor tissues with higher T stage, N stage, and TNM stage (Figure 1B and Table S3). Moreover, we evaluated the mRNA expression level of EPHX2 in 31 paired tumor and peritumor tissues from CRC patients. qRT‐PCR analyses showed that EPHX2 was significantly downregulated in CRC tissues compared to peritumor tissues (Figure 1C). This reduction in EPHX2 expression was further confirmed at the protein level by IHC analysis (Figure 1D). Public data also indicated that the protein level of EPHX2 was significantly lower in colon cancer tissues compared to peritumor tissues (Figure S1). When stratified by clinicopathological TNM stage, EPHX2 expression was significantly lower in advanced‐stage tumors (III–IV) than in early‐stage tumors (I–II) (Figure 1E). Correspondingly, survival analysis in the TCGA‐COAD cohort demonstrated that patients with low EPHX2 expression exhibited significantly poorer OS compared to those with high expression (p = 0.045) (Figure 1F). This prognostic significance of low EPHX2 expression was successfully validated in an independent external cohort, where patients with low EPHX2 levels similarly showed worse survival outcomes (Figure 1G). Collectively, these results indicate that EPHX2 is downregulated in tumors, associated with advanced disease stage, and serves as a robust prognostic biomarker.

Overexpression of EPHX2 Leads to the Accumulation of HDHA, HEPE, and HETE in CRC Cells

Given that EPHX2 plays a critical role in arachidonic acid metabolism, we investigated the impact of EPHX2 overexpression on arachidonic acid‐derived metabolites in CRC cells through targeted arachidonic acid metabolomics. The overexpression and knockdown of EPHX2 in SW480 and HCT116 cells were confirmed by qRT‐PCR and western blot (Figure 2A,B). Among the three siRNAs tested, siEPHX2‐3 exhibited the highest silencing efficiency and was therefore used for all subsequent knockdown experiments (Figure S2A). The results of targeted AA lipid metabolomics showed that 71 intermediates of arachidonic acid metabolism were detected, and overexpression of EPHX2 resulted in a marked elevation of HDHA, HEPE, and HETE in CRC cells (Figures 2C,D, and S2B–F). Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), a key omega‐3 fatty acid, undergoes enzymatic oxidation by lipoxygenases and cytochrome P450 to yield hydroxylated derivatives collectively termed hydroxydocosahexaenoic acid (HDHA). In CRC cells, EPHX2 overexpression significantly elevated the levels of 10‐HDHA and 14‐HDHA compared to controls (p < 0.05), whereas increases in 8‐HDHA and 14‐HDHA did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05) (Figure 2E). Similarly, hydroxyeicosapentaenoic acids (HEPEs), bioactive metabolites derived from eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), were examined. Overexpression of EPHX2 markedly raised the concentrations of 8‐HEPE, 15‐HEPE, and 18‐HEPE (p < 0.05), while the elevation in 12‐HEPE was not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (Figure 2F). Furthermore, 12‐HETE, predominantly synthesized by platelet 12‐lipoxygenase, was also significantly upregulated upon EPHX2 overexpression in CRC cells (p < 0.05), while the increase in 8,9‐diHEPE following EPHX2 overexpression did not show statistical significance (p > 0.05) (Figure 2G).

Loss of EPHX2 Increases the Content of CXCLs via EETs Accumulation in CRC Cells

To further elucidate the functional role of EPHX2 in CRC, we identified 715 DEGs between the high and low EPHX2 expression groups, which were visualized using a volcano plot (Figure 3A). A heatmap depicting the top 50 DEGs revealed distinct transcriptional signatures between the high and low EPHX2 expression groups (Figure S3A). GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the DEGs upregulated in the low‐EPHX2 expression group were significantly enriched in pathways related to cytokine‐cytokine receptor interaction, PI3K‐Akt signaling pathway, transcriptional misregulation in cancer, and viral protein interaction with cytokine and cytokine receptor (Figures 3B and S3B). Protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis indicated no direct binding between EPHX2 and key proteins within the aforementioned signaling pathways, with the exception of IL‐6 (Figures 3C and S3C). These findings suggested that EPHX2 likely contributed to CRC progression not through direct PPI, but rather via modulation of upstream or downstream arachidonic acid metabolism intermediates.

By intersecting the 31 genes enriched in the “cytokine‐cytokine receptor interaction” pathway with DEGs in CRC, we identified 12 cytokines or cytokine receptors that exhibited differential expression in CRC (Figure 3D). We constructed a PPI network comprising the aforementioned 12 genes together with EPHX2, which revealed interactions among CXCL5, CXCL8, CXCL9, CXCL10, CCL13, and CCL21. This finding suggests that these six cytokines may play critical roles in CRC cells with low EPHX2 expression (Figure 3E). Analysis of public datasets revealed that the expression levels of CXCL5, CXCL8, CXCL9, and CXCL10 were significantly upregulated, while the expression levels of CCL13 and CCL21 were significantly downregulated in CRC tissues (Figure 3F). Correlation analysis further indicated that EPHX2 exhibited a significant negative correlation with CXCL5, CXCL8, CXCL9, CXCL10, CCL13, and CCL21 (Figure 3G). Consistent patterns were also identified in the GSE39582 dataset (Figure S3D,E).

The primary function of EPHX2 is to hydrolyze EETs into DHETs and other metabolites. Our results showed that exogenous EET supplementation had no significant effect on EPHX2 expression levels in CRC cells (Figure 4A). Furthermore, EPHX2 overexpression significantly reduced EET levels in CRC cells, and the cellular EET exposure was significantly elevated following exogenous supplementation in CRC cells (Figure 4B). In addition, ELISA assays further demonstrated that overexpression of EPHX2 led to a significant reduction in the levels of CXCL5, CXCL8, CXCL9, and CXCL10 in CRC cells (Figure 4C). Moreover, exogenous EET supplementation reversed the EPHX2 overexpression‐mediated reduction in CXCL5, CXCL8, CXCL9, and CXCL10 levels in CRC cells (Figure 4C). Collectively, these findings indicate that reduced EPHX2 expression leads to EET accumulation, which in turn increases CXCL levels in CRC cells.

Loss of EPHX2 Suppresses Mitochondrial Biogenesis in CRC Cells

GSEA results indicated that the up‐regulated genes in the low EPHX2 expression group were also predominantly enriched in mitochondrial protein import pathways in CRC (Figure 5A). By intersecting a total of 39 genes enriched in the mitochondrial protein import pathway, correlation analysis revealed that EPHX2 was positively correlated with all 39 aforementioned genes, suggesting that mitochondrial biogenesis may be suppressed in CRC cells with low EPHX2 expression (Figure S4A). Based on these findings, we investigated the subcellular localization of EPHX2 in CRC cells. The results showed that EPHX2 was widely expressed in the cytoplasm and colocalized with the majority of mitochondria in CRC cells (Figure 5B). Mitochondrial mass assays revealed that the knockdown of EPHX2 significantly reduced mitochondrial mass, while its overexpression increased mitochondrial mass (Figure 5C). Furthermore, mtDNA copy number analysis showed that EPHX2 knockdown led to a marked decrease in mtDNA copy number, whereas overexpression elevated it (Figure 5D). Analysis of public datasets showed that the expression level of EPHX2 was positively correlated with all 13 polypeptide molecules encoded by mtDNA in TCGA‐COAD and GSE39583 datasets (Figure S4B). Regarding ATP levels, EPHX2 knockdown resulted in a significant rise in ATP content, while overexpression induced a notable decline (Figure 5E). Given that ROS are primarily generated by mitochondria and mitochondrial damage is typically accompanied by diminished MMP and enhanced ROS production, our study found that EPHX2 knockdown significantly increased ROS generation, whereas its overexpression substantially reduced it (Figure 5F). Correspondingly, MMP assays demonstrated that EPHX2 knockdown significantly lowered the MMP, while its overexpression significantly elevated it (Figures 5G and S4C). Collectively, these results indicate that EPHX2 is involved in regulating mitochondrial function and oxidative metabolic homeostasis.

Loss of EPHX2 Suppresses Mitochondrial Biogenesis via EETs Accumulation in CRC Cells

Furthermore, we investigated whether EETs were involved in EPHX2‐mediated mitochondrial biogenesis. Mitochondrial mass assays demonstrated that overexpression of EPHX2 significantly increased mitochondrial mass. Notably, EET treatment markedly reversed this promoting effect on mitochondrial biogenesis induced by EPHX2 overexpression (Figure 6A,B). Similarly, mtDNA copy number analysis showed that overexpression of EPHX2 significantly elevated mtDNA copy numbers in CRC cells, an effect that was also substantially reversed by EET treatment (Figure 6C). ATP assays revealed that EPHX2 overexpression significantly decreased ATP content in CRC cells, and this reduction was markedly reversed by EET treatment (Figure 6D). In addition, overexpression of EPHX2 substantially suppressed ROS production, and this inhibitory effect was significantly attenuated by EET treatment (Figure 6E). Taken together, these results indicate that loss of EPHX2 suppresses mitochondrial biogenesis via EETs accumulation in CRC cells.

Loss of EPHX2 Promotes Cell Migration and Invasion in CRC

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis also revealed that the DEGs upregulated in the low‐EPHX2 expression group were significantly enriched in pathways related to focal adhesion, cell adhesion molecules, and ECM‐receptor interaction (Figure 7A). Based on these findings, we evaluated the impact of EPHX2 on the migration and invasion of CRC cells. Scratch wound healing and transwell migration assays demonstrated that the knockdown of EPHX2 significantly enhanced the migratory capacity of CRC cells. Conversely, overexpression of EPHX2 markedly attenuated cell migration compared with control cells (Figure 7B,C). Similarly, transwell Matrigel invasion assays revealed that EPHX2 knockdown substantially increased the invasive ability of CRC cells, while EPHX2 overexpression exerted a suppressive effect (Figure 7D). In addition, our results demonstrated that knockdown of EPHX2 significantly increased the proportion of CRC cells with invasive pseudopodia (Figure S5). Taken together, these results indicate that the loss of EPHX2 promotes cell migration and invasion in CRC.

Loss of EPHX2 Promotes Cell Migration via ROS and CXCLs Signaling in CRC

Based on the previous results, we next aimed to clarify whether ROS or CXCLs signaling is involved in EPHX2‐mediated colorectal cancer migration. Transwell assays revealed that knockdown of EPHX2 significantly increased the migration of CRC cells. Remarkably, MitoTEMPO, a ROS scavenger, substantially reversed the EPHX2 knockdown‐induced promotion of cell migration in CRC cells (Figures 8A and S6). Moreover, AMG487, an inhibitor of the CXCL5/8/9/10 receptor, also substantially reversed the EPHX2 knockdown‐induced promotion of cell migration in CRC cells (Figure 8A). Similarly, pseudopodia analysis further revealed that the significant increase in pseudopodia formation induced by EPHX2 knockdown was markedly reversed by treatment with MitoTEMPO and AMG487 (Figure 8B). Collectively, these findings suggest that loss of EPHX2 promotes EET accumulation, which in turn enhances CXCL and ROS production, thereby promoting the migratory and invasive abilities of CRC cells (Figure 9).

Discussion

Recently, the existing literature indicated that the expression of EPHX2 was significantly downregulated in a broad spectrum of solid tumors, including CRC, HCC, clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), prostate cancer (PCa), and laryngeal cancer [7, 9, 10, 17, 18]. Notably, its downregulation had been established as a valuable diagnostic biomarker in several cancers, such as ccRCC and cervical squamous cell carcinoma [5]. More importantly, across these malignancies, low EPHX2 expression was consistently associated with more advanced disease and unfavorable clinical outcomes. In the present study, lower EPHX2 levels correlated with higher T, N, and TNM stages and predicted poorer patient prognosis. Similarly, in HCC, PCa, and laryngeal cancer, reduced EPHX2 expression was linked to worse survival, recurrence, or served as an independent risk factor [7, 17, 18, 19]. However, the role of EPHX2 appeared to be context‐dependent and might exhibit cancer‐type specificity. Contrary to the prevailing pattern, some studies reported its elevated expression in certain contexts. For example, one study indicated high EPHX2 expression in prostate cancer, and nuclear overexpression of EPHX2 was identified as a predictor of poor prognosis in breast cancer [4, 20]. These divergent findings suggest that the functional impact of EPHX2 may vary across different tissue origins or molecular subtypes, warranting further investigation to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

The upstream regulatory mechanisms of EPHX2 have been preliminarily explored in several studies. For instance, LPS stimulation was shown to downregulate the expression of EPHX2 [21]. In the normal colon, the transcription factor STAT3 positively regulated EPHX2 expression, but not in colon cancer [22]. Previous work has further established that the inhibitory effect of EPHX2 on CRC was associated with fatty acid degradation [8]. EETs exhibited anti‐inflammatory properties [23]. In cancer‐associated fibroblasts (CAFs), CYP4X1/sEH‐derived 14,15‐EET‐EA upregulated PD‐L1, CXCL12, and TGF‐β via the GPR119‐Gs/β‐arrestin 2 signaling axis [24]. Moreover, the accumulation of 11,12‐EET accelerated muscle regeneration [24]. Single‐nucleus RNA sequencing further revealed that treatment with the sEH inhibitor TPPU upregulated genes involved in actin cytoskeleton organization and excitatory synaptic pathways [23]. In line with prior reports, the present study demonstrated that EPHX2 overexpression significantly elevated the levels of HDHA, HEPE, and HETEs in CRC cells. However, the mechanistic basis by which EPHX2 leads to increased hydroxylated forms, and the functional significance of these changes in CRC progression, remain to be fully elucidated. Subsequent analysis indicated that, while EPHX2 might interact directly with IL6, it didn't engage with most genes enriched in relevant signaling pathways. Collectively, these results suggest that EPHX2 likely contributes to colorectal carcinogenesis mainly by regulating the production of HDHA, HEPE, and HETEs.

The anti‐inflammatory effects are attenuated when EETs are hydrolyzed by EPHX2 into less active diols [25]. In the present study, targeted metabolomics did not yield detectable EET signals in either the control or EPHX2‐overexpressing groups. This may be explained by the relatively low basal EET levels in cells, compounded by enhanced hydrolysis to DHETs driven by EPHX2 overexpression, both of which could result in EET concentrations falling below the detection limit of the metabolomics platform. Conversely, ELISA‐based measurements demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in EET levels following EPHX2 overexpression, a finding that aligns with the expected catalytic activity of EPHX2. However, it is important to acknowledge that ELISA exhibits limited specificity relative to LC–MS/MS for analyzing small lipid mediators, and the possibility of cross‐reactivity with other oxylipins cannot be entirely ruled out. Moreover, the ELISA is indeed unable to distinguish endogenous EET from exogenously administered 14,15‐EET, which represents a limitation of this study. Thus, while the ELISA data provide supportive evidence for the functional role of EPHX2, they should be interpreted with appropriate caution, and complementary LC–MS/MS analysis would be beneficial for future studies. In human macrophages, EETs have been shown to regulate cellular function [25]. Specifically, disruption of EPHX2 reduced the induction of proinflammatory cytokines, suppressed the upregulation of Tlr2 and Pgylrp1 receptors, and inhibited the activation of Ras‐related C3 botulinum toxin substrates 1/2 (Rac1/2) and cell division control protein 42 homolog (Cdc42) in peptidoglycan (PGN)‐stimulated macrophages [25]. In the present study, EPHX2 and its related genes were significantly enriched in signaling pathways such as the cytokine‐cytokine receptor interaction pathway. Further analysis identified CXCL5, CXCL8, CXCL9, and CXCL10 as core genes within this pathway. Overexpression of EPHX2 significantly decreased the levels of these four cytokines, suggesting that loss of EPHX2 may promote the production of CXCL5, CXCL8, CXCL9, and CXCL10, thus contributing critically to the malignant progression of CRC.

Single‐cell sequencing and gene set variation analysis (GSVA) underscored the involvement of EPHX2 in regulating multiple cancer‐related biological processes, such as cell cycle and apoptosis [5]. EPHX2 protein expression has been shown to be negatively correlated with Ki67 expression [17]. In ccRCC, overexpression of EPHX2 significantly inhibited the proliferative, migratory, and invasive capacities of tumor cell lines [10]. Consistent with these findings, our study demonstrated that EPHX2 overexpression suppressed the migration and invasion of CRC cells, potentially through a reduction in pseudopodia formation. In contrast, several studies have reported a tumor‐promoting function for EPHX2 in specific cancer types. For example, in vitro and in vivo experiments revealed that knockdown of EPHX2 inhibited the growth of endometrial cancer cells [26]. Moreover, EPHX2 expression has been closely linked to metabolic reprogramming in cancer [7]. Corroborating this, our functional enrichment analysis suggested that EPHX2 might exert a critical role in the arachidonic acid metabolism pathway via interaction with CYP2J2 and CYP4F2. Furthermore, bioinformatic analyses identified EPHX2 as a key gene related to methylation and expression patterns in cervical squamous cell carcinoma [27]. GSEA also indicated EPHX2 involvement in several biological processes, including cytokine‐cytokine receptor interaction, proteasome function, ribosome biogenesis, and apoptosis [28].

The exon‐specific expression of EPHX2 has been associated with different clinical outcomes in patients undergoing neoadjuvant chemotherapy [28]. Notably, the exon 7 variant of EPHX2 (EPHX2‐exo7) represents a potential therapeutic target in breast cancer [29]. Furthermore, several membrane‐associated proteins, including HMOX1, TMB1, EPHX2, and NEU1, have been identified as correlates of drug resistance levels in DLKP subpopulations [30]. Consistently, EPHX2 has also been recognized as a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in other contexts [31]. Taken together, these observations support the hypothesis that EPHX2 may contribute to malignant tumor drug resistance by modulating intracellular metabolites derived from arachidonic acid metabolism, although this mechanism warrants further experimental validation.

In conclusion, our study showed that EPHX2 was significantly downregulated in human CRC tissues, and its low expression correlated with poor prognosis. Furthermore, loss of EPHX2 promoted EET accumulation, which subsequently increased CXCL levels and suppressed mitochondrial biogenesis in CRC cells. For the mechanism, loss of EPHX2 enhanced cell migration via ROS and CXCLs signaling. Loss of EPHX2 accelerates CRC cell migration through EET accumulation‐dependent chemokine elevation and mitochondrial biogenesis impairment, and targeting the EPHX2/EETs/CXCLs axis may represent a potential novel therapeutic strategy for CRC treatment.
