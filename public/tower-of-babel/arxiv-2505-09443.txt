Variational formulations of transport on combinatorial meshes Journal: Applied Mathematical Modelling
Kiprian Berbatov, Andrey P. Jivkov

Abstract
We develop analogues of the primal and mixed variational formulations of the conservation laws describing transport of scalar physical properties for topological spaces referred to as cell complexes. The development is restricted to cell complexes with cells having connectivity of simple polytopes. Such spaces are suitable representations of physical systems composed of elements of different apparent topological dimensions, where all elements might have individual properties, and elements of a given dimension might interact via common elements of lower dimensions. This modelling foundation can be considered as intermediate between the foundation for particle-based modelling, which is the discrete topology, and the foundation for continuum modelling, which is the smooth topology. The new foundation offers advantages for the analysis of the behaviour of physical systems with complex internal structures. The development is based on a calculus with combinatorial differential forms which is a discrete analogue of the smooth exterior calculus with differential forms. We call the resulting calculus combinatorial mesh calculus (CMC) as it is based on combinatorial meshes, for which embedding is forgotten and only the connectivity between cells and measures of the cells are used in calculations. We discuss how the obtained formulation is specialised for several different problems, including mass diffusion, heat conduction, fluid flow in porous media, and charge diffusion, and provide details about the formulation of initial boundary value problems for these transport cases. Examples and results for selected boundary value problems are given to demonstrate the capabilities of the CMC formulations.

Introduction

1.1
Motivation

Modern experimental techniques are revealing, with increasing details, the complexity of the internal structures of materials
kumar2023MC
,
hayashi2024MC
and the evolution of these structures caused by various forces
stock2020XCT
,
woodruff2021SCT
. The observations clearly show that
the internal structures on any length scale consist of finite-size components that appear with different dimensions
. For example, relative to the bulk on a given length scale, monolithic materials contain defects, and composites contain intentionally inserted inclusions that appear as points, lines, and surfaces. Specifically, at the polycrystalline level, metals and alloys appear to be collections of 3D (grains), 2D (grain or interphase boundaries) and 1D (boundary junctions). At sub-grain level, they appear to have 3D (regions with perfect atomic lattice), 2D (stacking faults), and 1D (dislocations) components. This view can be extended to the atomic scale, where the 3D components are the Wigner-Seitz cells around atoms, while the 2D and 1D components are their faces and edges
hunklinger2022SSP
.

The behaviour of a structure at a given length scale emerges from interactions between components of all dimensions. Assuming local interactions, this means that in principle
any given component affects all components of lower dimensions on its boundary, as well as all components of higher dimensions which have it on their boundaries
. The observations also clearly show that
the evolution of a structure at a given length scale is a sequence of discrete, finite changes
of the arrangement, and/or shape, and/or size, and/or nature of its components due to, e.g., shear, separation, diffusion, dissolution, chemical/electrochemical reactions, etc. The components of different dimensions and nature react to and transmit differently the evolution forces, i.e., the internal structures localise and channel the evolution.

The understandings of the finiteness of microstructural components and evolution events, as well as the geometry of local interactions, are not consistently reflected in the existing methods for materials’ modelling. The state-of-the-art offers advanced representations of observed structures
trovalusci2016MSM
,
brancherie2017MSM
,
burczynski2022MSM
,
baniassadi2023MCM
but the descriptions of interactions and evolution mechanisms do not respect all observations because of the constraints imposed by their topological foundations. The descriptions used to date have been developed for two conceivable topologies: a discrete topology,
T(D)
, where the system is considered as a collection of finitely many distinct constituents (points or particles); and a continuous/smooth topology,
T(C)
, where the system is considered as a collection of infinitely many infinitesimal points forming a smooth manifold.

T(D)
is the foundation for several modelling approaches, most popular of which are molecular dynamics
santamaria2023MD
, peridynamics
madenci2014PD
, smoothed particle hydrodynamics
filho2019SPH
, and discrete element method
jebahi2015DEM
.
T(D)
dictates operations with discrete fields over constituents or pairs of constituents, namely discretely distributed
extensive
physical properties (mass, energy, momenta, charge), discretely distributed fluxes of these properties, and discretely distributed material characteristics (diffusivity, conductivity, stiffness, etc.). The resulting methods are useful for capturing atomic and mesoscopic phenomena but their representation of material structures lacks explicit topological connectivity, making it difficult to enforce constraints and predict emergent material behaviours accurately. Furthermore, their lack of an intrinsic notion of volume means that they struggle to handle long-range interactions effectively. A significant drawback of
T(D)
-based methods is the necessity for heuristic force laws, which may not always be physically justified.

T(C)
is the foundation for continuum physics and mechanics
morro2023Continuum
. These operate with fields of
intensive
(averaged) physical properties, which are densities of the extensive properties. This foundation leads to formulations of the conservation laws in integral (weak) form and, under the assumption of locally continuous fields, in differential (strong) form. With very few exceptions with specific domain geometries and boundary conditions, the continuum formulations have no closed-form solutions and require approximate numerical approaches. The most popular are the finite element methods
brenner2008FEM
, the finite difference methods
thomas1995FDM
, and the finite volume methods
leveque2012FVM
. These methods effectively capture macroscopic mechanical properties but struggle with representing discontinuities such as cracks, defects, and phase boundaries. These are presently considered by different numerical approximations, such as cohesive zone models
ruiz2001CZM
,
park2011CZM
, extended finite element method
mohammadi2012XFEM
,
vellwock2024XFEM
, and phase-field methods
chen2022phase
,
chen2024phase
. The latter use artificial smoothing to handle sharp transitions, which can lead to inaccuracies in representing localized phenomena. Finally, continuum-based methods rely on constitutive laws that may not always adequately capture the complexities of real-world materials, particularly in cases involving extreme deformations, multi-phase interactions, or non-equilibrium processes.

The constraints of the discrete and continuous topologies can be avoided by adopting another topological foundation for modelling - cell complexes. These are collections of cells of different topological dimensions arranged/glued following specific rules and are studied in algebraic topology
kozlov2008
,
knudson2022
. Unlike the continuum approach, which relies on smooth differentiable manifolds, or discrete particle methods that lack explicit topological structure, cell complexes naturally encode the connectivity and geometry of complex material domains. This makes them particularly well-suited for modelling materials with intricate microstructures, such as polycrystalline metals, composite materials, and biological tissues. Therefore, we will refer to cell complexes as the natural topology
T(N)
.

In a previous work
berbatov2022diffusion
, we showed a formulation of the conservation of scalar quantities (mass, energy, charge) in cell complexes that was analogous to the strong (differential) form in the continuum settings. The formulation used combinatorial differential forms, introduced by Forman
forman2002combinatorial
, for which we proposed the metric-dependent operations required to describe the physics, showed the construction of boundary value problems on cell complexes, and applied the method to analysis of diffusion in composites with complex arrangements of components. One drawback of the strong form’s analogue is the approximate way of applying Neumann boundary conditions. The weak (integral) formulations of the conservation laws avoid this approximation.

1.2
Contribution

The aim of this work is to develop the two weak formulations of conservation of scalar quantities on cell complexes – primal (in terms of the scalar potential only), and mixed (in terms of the scalar potential and the quantity flow rate). The weak formulations with combinatorial differential forms are analogous to the continuum weak formulations with exterior differential forms. The latter have not been published to date to the best of our knowledge. It is therefore necessary and instructive to make this contribution first before describing the discrete analogues. This also helps to identify the similarities and differences between the continuum and discrete operations and formulations. The continuum formulations are derived directly with exterior differential forms, without reference to the traditional vector calculus. To this end, we provide an overview of smooth exterior calculus in
Section

2
and then derive the continuum formulations in
Section

3
. The basic notions and operations related to cell complexes required for this work will be introduced in
Section

4
. These are then used to describe the physics on cell complexes in
Section

5

T(N)
-based formulations are used to solve a series of boundary value problems with domain geometries and boundary conditions that have closed form (exact) continuum solutions to facilitate comparison. Selected examples are given in
Section

6
, and a larger set of examples can be found at
https://github.com/kipiberbatov/cmc
.

The sections are broken down into items named discussion, definition, notation, remark, proposition, corollary, formulation, and example, which are numbered consecutively to facilitate cross-referencing.
All unary operators (excluding taking the additive inverse) have higher precedence than binary operators (excluding taking exponents).
For instance,

(All except the last one are base SI quantities; for historical reasons electric current

2
Overview of exterior calculus

Discussion 2.1
.

In this section, we provide an overview of the exterior calculus apparatus required in the rest of the work.
Some basic knowledge of general topology is required.
Experience with smooth manifolds is also beneficial, although we introduce the terminology we use anyway.
A comprehensive book on smooth manifolds is, for instance,
lee2012introduction
.
We find many benefits in using exterior calculus instead of vector calculus.

1.

There is a clear distinction between manifold’s intrinsic (non-metric) calculus (
Section

2.1
) and (additional) calculus depending on a structure such as a Riemannian metric (
Section

2.2
).
This elucidates the distinction between different types of physical relations and the mapping of these relations straightforwardly to their discrete counterparts, for which we build a similar calculus.

2.

Physical quantities are represented by differential forms ready to be integrated over their respective domains.
In contrast, in
3
D vector calculus, there are only scalar and vector fields, and is impossible to differentiate between “scalar” quantities living on
0
- and
3
-cells, and “vector” quantities living on
1
-cells and
2
-cells.
Terms like
pseudo-scalar fields
and
pseudo-vector fields
are often used, but they are related to sign invariance under change of orientation. Because sign invariance is an important notion, we will use of the term
pseudo-forms
.

3.

Differential forms can express extensive physical quantities, not just densities (intensive ones).

4.

The post-processing of non-primary quantities in their respective formulations, such as flow rate in primal formulations and potential in mixed formulations, is clear in the respective discrete formulations.

Remark 2.19
.

Orientation is the first additional structure imposed on a smooth manifold.
Indeed, a smooth manifold is a topological space with a smooth structure, from which we can canonically construct vector fields, differential forms, the wedge product and the exterior derivative.
Orientation, however, is a choice of a sign that is not made canonically.
It will be required for the definitions of integration of forms and the Hodge star operator (the latter requiring a metric structure as well).

With potential exceptions not considered in this article, invariant physical quantities should not depend on the choice of an orientation.
For this reason we introduce the informal notion of
pseudo-forms
, that is differential forms that change sign when orientation changes.
More generally, we can talk about
pseudo-objects
, that is objects that change sign when orientation changes.
For instance, the amount form that we will define later is a pseudo-form – integrating it leads to a positive amount, but since integration is also orientation-dependent, it must change sign when orientation is changed.
Note that orientation-independent operators (like wedge product and exterior derivative) will not change the orientation type of an object, while orientation-dependent ones (like integration or Hodge star) will reverse it.

We can do the treatment of pseudo-objects formally, but that would require the introduction of orientation-aware analogues of all the operators we use, which will unnecessarily complicate our formalism.
Note that the distinction between objects and pseudo-objects is usually not done extensively in the mathematical literature. It is usually clear when orientation is used but a careful analysis of differentiating between objects and pseudo-objects is lacking.
We, however, believe it is worth mentioning, for the following reasons:

1.

the analogical terms “pseudo-scalars” and “pseudo-vectors”, which may be considered special cases in our formalism, are notably used by physicists to distinguish between orientation-dependent and orientation-independent quantities, the latter having more explicit physical meaning;

2.

just like the notion of
physical dimension
gives a “reality check” for invariance under scalar multiplication,
the distinction between forms and pseudo-forms checks invariance under orientation reversal. Note, that we will systematically analyse the physical dimensions of all objects and operators in this article.
For instance, just like two non-zero objects of different dimensions cannot be equal, a non-zero form and a non-zero pseudo-form could not be equal as well.

Definition 2.28

Remark 2.29

The metric tensor is a symmetric tensor, and in a local coordinate system can be represented as an expression of the form

\begin{split}\langle d^{\star}_{D-p}\star_{p}\omega,\eta\rangle_{D-p-1}&=\langle\star_{p}\omega,\tilde{d}_{D-p-1}\eta\rangle_{D-p}\\

&=\int_{\partial M}(\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits_{\partial M,p}(\omega)\wedge\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits_{\partial M,D-p-1}(\eta))+(-1)^{p+1}\int_{M}(d_{p}\omega\wedge\eta)\\
&=\langle(-1)^{p+1}\star_{p+1}\tilde{d}_{p}\omega,\eta\rangle_{D-p-1}.\end{split}

(2.75)

The proposition follows since
\omega
and
\eta
are arbitrary.
∎

3
Exterior calculus variational formulations

Notation 3.1
.

Throughout this section
D
will denote a positive integer, specifying the space dimension.
For real applications
D
is expected to be
3
but in simpler models
D=1
or
D=2
may suffice.
Since we are not going to make any special assumptions about the ambient space, we will work with arbitrary
D
.
Moreover, many of the operators in this article have signs dependent on the space dimension, and so with general
D
we could capture these signs in a unified way.

Our model will be built on a bounded open body
M
, with fixed orientation, and embedded in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
We assume that we have a scalar extensive quantity
A
, representing the
amount
of conserved quantity on
M
, with a given physical dimension
\mathsf{X}
; examples include mass, energy, charge, volume.

We will also fix an initial time
t_{0}[\mathsf{T}]
and the time interval
I=[t_{0},\infty)
.

Time-dependent quantities will be mostly represented by objects whose domain is

C^{\infty}(I,S_{1})\times...\times C^{\infty}(I,S_{n})
,
where
S_{1},...,S_{n}
are vector spaces representing the spatial part.
Previously introduced operators (wedge product, exterior derivative, pullback/trace, integration, metric tensor, inner product, Hodge star, codifferential) will be applied pointwise to the spatial parts
S_{1},...,S_{n}
.
In other words, if
S
is some other vector space, and we have an operator

H\colon S_{1}\times...\times S_{n}\to S

(e.g., exterior derivative), we overload
H
to a map

H\colon\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I,S_{1})\times...\times\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I,S_{n})\to\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I,S),\ H(\omega_{1},...,\omega_{n})(t):=H(\omega_{1}(t),...,\omega_{n}(t))\in S.

(3.1)

On the other hand, partial derivative with respect to time is overloaded to an operator on temporal objects, i.e.,

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\colon\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I,S)\to\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I,S),\ \frac{\partial\omega}{\partial t}(t):=\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{\omega(t+\tau)-\omega(t)}{\tau}\in S.

(3.2)

Definition 3.2
.

The
amount pseudo-form

Q[\mathsf{X}]\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I,\Omega^{D}M)

(3.3)

is characterised as follows: for any
D
-dimensional subregion
V
of
M
, and any time interval
[t_{1},t_{2}]\subset I
,

\textrm{``amount difference of $A$ inside $V$ in $[t_{1},t_{2}]$''}=\int_{V}Q(t_{2})-\int_{V}Q(t_{1}).

(3.4)

Definition 3.3
.

The
flow rate pseudo-form

q[\mathsf{X}\mathsf{T}^{-1}]\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I,\Omega^{D-1}M)

(3.5)

is characterised as follows.
Let
[t_{1},t_{2}]\subset I
be a time interval,
S
be a
(D-1)
-dimensional hypersurface on
M
.
Consider a subregion
V
of
M
partitioned into two parts
V_{+}
and
V_{-}
sharing
S
as a common boundary,
with relative orientations

\varepsilon(V_{+},S)=1
and
\varepsilon(V_{-},S)=-1
.
Define the
net flow
of the amount
A
through
S
in
[t_{1},t_{2}]
to be be the difference between the flow from
V_{+}
to
V_{-}
in
[t_{1},t_{2}]
and the flow from
V_{-}
to
V_{+}
in
[t_{1},t_{2}]
.
Then

\textrm{``net flow of $A$ through $S$ in $[t_{1},t_{2}]$''}=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{S}q\,dt.

(3.6)

Remark 3.4
.

In
Equation

3.6

the perspective is from the point of view of the hypersurface
S
.
However, if we view it through the eyes of
V_{+}
, the aforementioned term is the
net outflow
of
A
from
V_{+}
to
V_{-}
through
S
in
[t_{1},t_{2}]
,
while from the perspective of
V_{-}
it is the
net inflow
of
A
in
V_{-}
from
V_{+}
through
S
in
[t_{1},t_{2}]
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
V
be a subregion of
M
,
[t_{1},t_{2}]\subset I
.
Then

\textrm{``net outflow of $A$ from $V$ through its boundary in $[t_{1},t_{2}]$''}=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\partial V}q\,dt.

(3.7)

Proof.

By definition, the boundary
\partial V
has the induced boundary orientation, i.e.,

\varepsilon(V,\partial V)=1,\ \varepsilon(M\setminus\overline{V},\partial V)=-1
.
The net outflow of
A
from
V
through its boundary in
[t_{1},t_{2}]
, hence, equals to the net flow of
A
through
\partial V
in
[t_{1},t_{2}]
,
which by
Definition

3.3
is

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\partial V}q\,dt
.
∎

Definition 3.6
.

The
internal production rate pseudo-form

f[\mathsf{X}\mathsf{T}^{-1}]\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I,\Omega^{D}M)

(3.8)

is characterised as follows: for any
D
-dimensional subregion
V
of
M
, and any time interval
[t_{1},t_{2}]\subset I
,

\textrm{``internal production of $A$ inside $V$ in $[t_{1},t_{2}]$''}=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{V}f\,dt.

(3.9)

Discussion 3.7

(Derivation of conservation law for transport phenomena)
.

The conservation law for an extensive quantity
A
(e.g., energy) has the following intuitive formulation:
for any
D
-dimensional subregion
V
of
M
, and any time interval
[t_{1},t_{2}]\subset I
,

\begin{split}\text{``amount difference of $A$ inside $V$ between $t_{2}$ and $t_{1}$''}=&\text{``net production of $A$ inside $V$ in $[t_{1},t_{2}]$''}\\
&-\text{``net outflow of $A$ through $\partial V$ in $[t_{1},t_{2}]$''}.\end{split}

(3.10)

Having defined variables for denoting these quantities, we arrive at the equation

\int_{V}Q(t_{2})-\int_{V}Q(t_{1})=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{V}f\,dt-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\partial V}q\,dt.

(3.11)

Using the Newton-Leibniz theorem for the first integral and the Stokes-Cartan theorem
(
Equation

2.47
)
for the third integral, we get:

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left(\int_{V}\frac{\partial Q}{\partial t}\right)\,dt=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left(\int_{V}f\right)\,dt-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left(\int_{V}\,d_{D-1}q\right)\,dt.

(3.12)

Since
V
and
[t_{1},t_{2}]
are arbitrary, we can remove the integrals over them.
Thus, we arrive at the differential formulation of the conservation law:

\frac{\partial Q}{\partial t}=f-d_{D-1}q.

(3.13)

Discussion 3.8

(Neumann boundary conditions)
.

In formulations of initial-boundary value problems for transport phenomena, one type of boundary condition is the imposition of flow rate on part of the boundary, i.e., the prescription of flow rate between subregions touching the boundary and the external world.
To formulate such constraint, let
\Gamma_{\mathbf{N}}\subseteq M
be the part of the boundary where the flow rate is imposed,

g_{\mathbf{N}}\colon I\to\Omega^{D-1}_{\mathbf{N}}

(3.14)

be the
prescribed flow rate pseudo-form
.
Then the Neumann boundary condition reads as

\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits_{\Gamma_{\mathbf{N}},D-1}(q)=g_{\mathbf{N}}.

(3.15)

Discussion 3.9

(Dirichlet boundary conditions and potential)
.

The other common boundary condition, Dirichlet boundary condition, is related to the prescription of an intensive counterpart

u[\mathsf{Y}]\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I,\Omega^{0}M)

(3.16)

of the extensive quantity
Q
, called
potential
.
Precisely, if
\Gamma_{\mathbf{D}}
is the other part of the boundary of
M
, the
Dirichlet boundary
, so that

\partial M=\Gamma_{\mathbf{D}}\cup\Gamma_{\mathbf{N}},

(3.17)

and

g_{\mathbf{D}}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I,\Omega^{0}\Gamma_{\mathbf{D}}),

(3.18)

then the
Dirichlet boundary condition
is described by the equation

\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits_{\Gamma_{\mathbf{D}},0}(u)=g_{\mathbf{D}}.

(3.19)

Remark 3.10
.

Potentials for mass (mass transfer), energy (heat transfer), charge (charge transport), volume (volume transport) are concentration, temperature, electric potential, and pressure respectively.

Discussion 3.11

(Relation between potential and amount)
.

Let

\pi[\mathsf{X}\mathsf{Y}^{-1}\mathsf{L}^{-D}]\colon\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I,\Omega^{D}M)\to\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I,\Omega^{D}M)

(3.20)

be the
volumetric capacity
of the material.
Define the
dual volumetric capacity

\widetilde{\pi}[\mathsf{X}\mathsf{Y}^{-1}\mathsf{L}^{-D}]\colon\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I,\Omega^{0}M)\to\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I,\Omega^{0}M)

(3.21)

by

\widetilde{\pi}=\star_{0}^{-1}\circ\pi\circ\star_{0}=\star_{D}\circ\pi\circ\star_{0}.

(3.22)

The potential
u
and the amount
Q
are related by the equation

\frac{\partial Q}{\partial t}=\pi\star_{0}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\star_{0}\widetilde{\pi}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}.

(3.23)

Remark 3.12
.

In heat transfer the volumetric capacity is the volumetric heat capacity;
in charge transport the volumetric capacity is the volumetric capacitance;
in mass and volume transport the potentials are already the densities of the extensive quantities and so the respective volumetric capacities are not presented (they are identically
1
).

Discussion 3.13

(Drivers for flow)
.

The most common transport mechanisms of physical properties are diffusion and advection, which we will assume to be present in our model.

1.

Diffusion is the spontaneous transport of
A
from regions with higher potential to neighbouring regions with lower potential. The driver for diffusive flow is the potential difference.
The
diffusive flow rate
is expressed by the pseudo-form

q_{\mathcal{D}}[\mathsf{X}\mathsf{T}^{-1}]\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I,\Omega^{D-1}M).

(3.24)

2.

Advection is the bulk transport of
A
caused by the some prescribed volumetric flow rate, which is the driver for advective flow.
The
advective flow rate
is expressed by the pseudo-form

q_{\mathcal{A}}[\mathsf{X}\mathsf{T}^{-1}]\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I,\Omega^{D-1}M).

(3.25)

The total flow rate is the sum of diffusive and advective flow rates, i.e.,

q=q_{\mathcal{D}}+q_{\mathcal{A}}.

(3.26)

In the following paragraphs we will relate
q_{\mathcal{D}}
and
q_{\mathcal{A}}
to the other model’s variables.

Discussion 3.14

(Constitutive law for diffusive flow rate)
.

Let

\kappa[\mathsf{X}\mathsf{Y}^{-1}\mathsf{L}^{2-D}\mathsf{T}^{-1}]\colon\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I,\Omega^{D-1}M)\to\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I,\Omega^{D-1}M)

(3.27)

be the
conductivity
of the material, and

\tilde{u}[\mathsf{Y}\mathsf{L}^{D}]\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I,\Omega^{D}M),\quad\tilde{u}=\star_{0}u

(3.28)

be the
dual potential
.
Then
q_{\mathcal{D}}
is calculated via the
constitutive law

q_{\mathcal{D}}=\kappa d^{\star}_{D}\tilde{u}=\kappa d^{\star}_{D}\star_{0}u.

(3.29)

Discussion 3.15
.

Define the
dual conductivity

\widetilde{\kappa}[\mathsf{X}\mathsf{Y}^{-1}\mathsf{L}^{2-D}\mathsf{T}^{-1}]\colon\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I,\Omega^{1}M)\to\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I,\Omega^{1}M)

(3.30)

by

\widetilde{\kappa}=\star_{1}^{-1}\circ\kappa\circ\star_{1}=(-1)^{D-1}\star_{D-1}\circ\kappa\circ\star_{1}.

(3.31)

This means that

(3.32)

.

The conductivities for mass, heat energy, charge and volume transport are mass diffusivity, thermal conductivity, electrical conductivity and hydraulic conductivity.

3.3
Domains for physical quantities

Discussion 3.28
.

We end this section with a short commentary on the domains used for the physical quantities appearing in the exterior calculus models.
To simplify discussion we always assumed that all variables are infinitely smooth.
This assumption is too strong condition and has to be relaxed in order to guarantee the existence of solutions.
For weak formulations the appropriate domains for spaces of
p
-forms are the Sobolev spaces of weakly differentiable
p
-forms
arnold2018finite

H\Lambda^{p}M:=\{\omega\in L^{2}\Lambda^{p}M\mid d\omega\in L^{2}\Lambda^{p+1}M\},

(3.56)

where
L^{2}\Lambda^{q}M
denotes the space of
q
-forms whose components (in any coordinate system) are square-integrable.
Domains for boundary conditions also need to be adjusted, and we refer to the cited book for details.

Also, the requirement for space domain to be a smooth manifold boundary can be weakened so that the boundary is Lipschitz continuous, thus including general polytopes as valid domains.

4
Combinatorial mesh calculus (CMC)

Discussion 4.1
.

The smooth exterior calculus introduced in
Section

2
generalises the vector calculus and can replace it in modelling continuous media as presented in
Section

3
.
As discussed in
Section

1.1
, media with internal structures are appropriately described by algebraic topological structures called cell complexes.
The basics of these structures are given in
Section

4.1
and a discrete analogue of the smooth exterior calculus is presented in the rest of this section.

4.1
Cell complexes (meshes)

Definition 4.2
.

A
combinatorial cell complex
(or
combinatorial mesh
, or simply
mesh
)
\mathcal{M}
is a collection of elements, called
cells
, that is a realisation of a subdivision of a topological space.
More formally, it is a partially ordered set, with partial order
\preceq
, such that there exist a topological space
M
and a function

\varphi\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{P}(M)

(
\mathcal{P}(M)
is the power set of
M
),
called
embedding
of
\mathcal{M}
into
M
, satisfying the following conditions:

1.

for any
a\in\mathcal{M}
,
\varphi(a)\subseteq M
is homeomorphic to an open ball;

2.

for any
a,b\in\mathcal{M}
, if
a\neq b
, then
\varphi(a)\cap\varphi(b)=\emptyset
;

3.

for any
a\in\mathcal{M}
,

\overline{\varphi(a)}=\bigcup_{b\preceq a}\varphi(b),

(4.1)

where
\overline{X}
denotes the closure of
X
.
Equivalently, if
\partial X=\overline{X}\setminus{\rm int(X)}
is the topological boundary of
X
, then

\partial(\varphi(a))=\bigcup_{b\prec a}\varphi(b),

(4.2)

We say that
(M,\varphi)
is a
realisation
of
\mathcal{M}
if

M=\bigcup_{a\in\mathcal{M}}\varphi(a).

(4.3)

Remark 4.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{M},\preceq)
be a mesh.
It has been constructed by the requirement of embedding but intrinsically it is a partial order, which we call the
topology of
\mathcal{M}
.
(A similar definition for CW complexes, requiring only the partial order, is given in
bjorner1984posets
.)
Henceforth, we say that a construction based on the partial order and the relative orientations defined shortly is a
topological concept on a mesh
.
Such concepts will form the backbone of the discrete theory.
Considering an intrinsic metric, such as positive numbers associated with the cells of the mesh, will introduce metric concepts, but the theory will still be intrinsic, e.g., independent of any embedding of the mesh in a manifold.

Notation 4.4
.

Let
(\mathcal{M},\preceq)
be a mesh.
The elements of
\mathcal{M}
are called
cells
.
If
a,b\in\mathcal{M}
and
a\preceq b
, we say that
a
is a
subface
of
b
.
The relations
\prec
,
\succeq
and
\succ
are respectively the strong, reverse, and strong reverse versions of
\preceq
.
If
a\prec b
and there does not exist
c\in\mathcal{M}
such that
a\prec c\prec b
, we say that
a
is a
hyperface
of
b
and write it as
a\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 3.8889pt\hskip-3.8889pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\prec$}$}\hskip-3.8889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-0.02223pt}{\hbox{$\rule{2.58334pt}{0.0pt}\rule{2.58334pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\cdot$}$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 3.8889pt}}b
or
b\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 3.8889pt\hskip-3.8889pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\succ$}$}\hskip-3.8889pt\hskip-1.38889pt\raisebox{-0.02223pt}{\hbox{$\rule{-2.58334pt}{0.0pt}\rule{-2.58334pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\cdot$}$}}\hskip-1.38889pt\hskip 3.8889pt}}a
.
(The order theory terminology is “

We say that

is a

if it is a minimal element, i.e., there does not exist

such that

.
For

the

of

.

Let

be a mesh,

Proof.

Discussion 4.25
.

Until now we have not introduced a discrete analogue to the wedge product, called a
cup product
, denoted by
\smile
.
A cup product is a family of bilinear maps
\smile_{p,q}\colon C^{p}\mathcal{M}\times C^{q}\mathcal{M}\to C^{p+q}\mathcal{M}
, that satisfies the graded Leibniz rule with respect to the coboundary operator, and is local in the sense that for
\pi\in C^{p}\mathcal{M},\rho\in C^{q}\mathcal{M},\ a\in\mathcal{M}_{p+q}
,

(\pi\smile\rho)(a_{\bullet})=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{M}_{p},b\preceq a}\,\sum_{c\in\mathcal{M}_{q},c\preceq a}\lambda_{a,b,c}\,\pi(b_{\bullet})\,\rho(c_{\bullet}),

(4.38)

for some coefficients
\lambda_{a,b,c}
, depending only on the topology and the relative orientations of
\mathcal{M}
.
In other words, when the cup product is applied to a cell, only the values of the cochains of the cell boundary are taken into account.

Cup products exist for simplicial
wilson2007cochain
, cubical
arnold2012discrete
, and polygonal
ptackova2017discrete
meshes.
In all cases they are graded commutative and posses the graded Leibniz rule, but are not associative (even in dimension
1
!).
However, for simplices the cup product converges to the smooth wedge product
[
wilson2007cochain
, Theorem 5.4]
and cochains coming from de Rham maps are almost associative
[
wilson2007cochain
, Theorem 5.9]
.
Cup product can also be defined easily for products of meshes with using the tensor product of non-associative graded commutative differential graded algebras, thus allowing to define it over prisms as well.
However, we are unaware of a cup product for a generic polytopal mesh.
Moreover, we want to use a notion of topological orthogonality which works well for cubical meshes.
For this reason we will use a construction of a
quasi-cubical mesh

\mathcal{K}
(i.e., all its cells have face lattices isomorphic to those of cubes) from a generic mesh
\mathcal{M}
of simple polytopes, called the
Forman subdivision
, outlined in detail in
[
berbatov2022diffusion
, Section 2]
.
Another reason to use the Forman subdivision is to allow for more complex material properties (conductivities), as discussed in
[
berbatov2022diffusion
, Section 4]
.
In this article’s numerical examples we will use only constant conductivities but the intention is to use the discrete formulations (
Sections

3.1
and
3.2
) with different conductivities of cells of different dimensions as dictated by practical applications.

Remark 4.46
.

We are ready to reformulate the exterior calculus models in a discrete setting.
Before doing that, in
Table

5
we summarise both formalisms (continuous and discrete) and how they relate to one another.
The central column, “Correspondence”, describes the level of accuracy between a continuous and a discrete notion.
More precisely, some relationships are exact by definition, namely those relating objects by discretising them.
For relationships between operators, a commutative diagram approach is used – an operator is exact if first discretising it, and then applying it on discrete objects gives the same result as first applying it at continuous objects and then discretising them.

For instance, all topological operators except the wedge/cup products correspond exactly.
The accuracy of the correspondence between the wedge product and the cup product on a simplicial mesh is discussed in detail in
wilson2007cochain
;
one of the crucial differences is the non-associativity of the cup product.
For metric operators, all relationships are approximate, which is inescapable.
However, our definitions are based on the following principles:

1.

the Hodge star and adjoint coboundary operators have analogues definitions to their continuous counterparts, so that they share some common algebraic expressions with them;

2.

the basis cochains are orthogonal with respect to the inner product so that discrete Hodge star and adjoint coboundary are local operators;

3.

the discrete counterparts of the volume form and the identity function are discrete Hodge-dual;

4.

for regular cubical grids of mesh size
h>0
, the discrete operators have optimal accuracy (
O(h^{2})
in the interior) when viewed as approximations of their respective continuous counterparts.

That being said, the discrete calculus presented here (CMC) is fully intrinsic and self-contained, i.e., it should be considered as independent of the continuous one. The smooth exterior calculus is primarily a source of inspiration on how to build the discrete one.

5.3
Comparison with other methods

Discussion 5.8
.

Two methods that are notoriously based on differential geometry and meshes (i.e, forms and cochains) are discrete exterior calculus (DEC,
hirani2003discrete
) and finite element exterior calculus (FEEC,
arnold2018finite
).

DEC is usually presented as a discretisation method based on simplicial meshes and their circumcentric dual, used for producing diagonal Hodge star matrices, where Hodge star operators act between primal
p
-cochains and dual
(D-p)
-cochains.
The method does not define the full metric calculus (inner product, adjoint coboundary operator) and cannot be applied to our mixed formulations.

FEEC is a formalism for creating finite element spaces in a structured way based on smooth exterior calculus.
It is purely a discretisation method and cannot accommodate material properties of microstructural features of different dimensions.
Polynomial finite elements are generally constructed via polynomial mappings of reference elements and cannot handle general curvilinear meshes, although a large class of such elements can be handled with polynomial approximations
botti2018assesment
.
Virtual finite elements, on the other hand, can handle general shapes
beirao2019virtual
.

In CMC the shapes of the cells do not matter for calculations as all the required information is encoded in the face lattice, relative orientations and cell measures – everything else is canonically represented.
The shapes may matter in the pre-processing phase if the discrete input comes from a continuous problem.
The embedding is also required for visualisation.

Simulation examples

Discussion 6.1
.

Although the aim of this article is to develop intrinsic spatial-discrete formulations for transport phenomena, we will verify the proposed CMC formulations by comparing them to formulations with exact solutions in the continuum, as if the discrete formulations were discretisations.
Indeed, we first introduced the necessary apparatus of exterior calculus on manifolds and used it to formulate strong, primal weak and mixed weak formulations.
Then we introduced an analogous apparatus on meshes (CMC), made the connection with exterior calculus, and used it to formulate analogous discrete formulations.
Since we are not doing a discretisation, we could directly derive strong and weak formulations.
However, the reasons for our approach are the following:

1.

exterior calculus is a well established mathematical theory, and so is instructive to relate to it; clearly, it has guided us in the development of the discrete theory;

2.

we could pose problems with manufactured solutions using exterior calculus, and pre-process (discretise) them for input to discrete problems.
We can them compare the discrete solutions with the discretised versions of the continuum solutions.

3.

since Hodge stars
\star_{p}
and
\star_{D-p}
do not cancel (up to a sign
(-1)^{p(D-p)}
) in the discrete case, we would have to keep Hodge stars in the bilinear forms.
Going through exterior calculus formulations and translating the resulting final forms allows us to motivate not using Hodge star.
Hodge stars are problematic since they increase the stencil and may have non-full rank for some mesh topologies.

f^{\mathcal{K}}=R_{\mathcal{K},D}(f),\ v^{\mathcal{K}}=R_{\mathcal{K},D-1}(v),\ g_{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathcal{K}}=R_{\Gamma_{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathcal{K}},0}(g_{\mathbf{D}}),\ g_{\mathbf{N}}^{\mathcal{K}}=R_{\Gamma_{\mathbf{N}}^{\mathcal{K}},D-1}(g_{\mathbf{N}}).

(6.1)

Hence, we get the following discrete input for

5.5
and

5.7
:

(\mathcal{K},\Gamma_{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathcal{K}},\Gamma_{\mathbf{N}}^{\mathcal{K}},\kappa^{\mathcal{K}},f^{\mathcal{K}},g_{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathcal{K}},g_{\mathbf{N}}^{\mathcal{K}}).

(6.2)

In the examples,
v^{\mathcal{K}}
is assumed to be
0
, i.e., we will not include advective terms.

Discussion 6.3

(Translating densities to differential forms)
.

In this article we have done all continuous modelling in the language of smooth exterior calculus which is better suited to be mimicked in a discrete formulation, and as a source of continuous input.
We will not discuss in detail the full translation of smooth exterior calculus formulations into the familiar vector calculus ones.
However, the input parameters, represented as differential forms, may be supplied as densities for which we have to do a continuous-to-continuous pre-processing before doing the continuous-to-discrete pre-processing.

If internal production rate is supplied as a density, i.e., as
0
-form

\tilde{f}[\mathsf{X}\mathsf{T}^{-1}\mathsf{L}^{-D}]
,
we define
f:=\star_{0}\tilde{f}
.

If the prescribed flow rate is given as a density (a
0
-form)

\widetilde{g_{\mathbf{N}}}[\mathsf{X}\mathsf{T}^{-1}\mathsf{L}^{1-D}]
on
\Gamma_{\mathbf{N}}
,
we define

g_{\mathbf{N}}:=\star_{\Gamma_{\mathbf{N}},0}\widetilde{g_{\mathbf{N}}}
.

Pre-processing vector fields is a bit more involved, e.g., as in the case of the volumetric flow rate
v
when advection is present.
Assume instead of
v
we have the flow velocity vector field
{\bf v}
, usually stated in Cartesian coordinates by its velocity components, i.e.,

{\bf v}=\sum_{p=1}^{D}v_{p}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{p}},\ v_{p}[\mathsf{L}\mathsf{T}^{-1}].

(6.3)

Because

[[\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{p}}]]=\mathsf{L}^{-1}

(since
[[dx^{p}]]=\mathsf{L}
and vector fields are dual to forms),

[[{\bf v}]]=\mathsf{T}^{-1}
.
(This may sound counter-intuitive but note that the
components

\{v_{p}\}_{p=1}^{D}
of
{\bf v}
have the dimension of speed.)
Next, define the
flat operator
(one of the
musical isomorphisms
, the other one being its inverse, the
sharp operator
),

\flat\colon\mathcal{X}M\to\Omega^{1}M
,
as follows: for any
X,Y\in\mathcal{X}M
,

(X^{\flat})(Y)=g(X,Y),

(6.4)

whose dimension is the same as
g
, i.e,
\mathsf{L}^{2}
.
Hence, we get the volumetric flow rate density
\tilde{v}[\mathsf{L}^{2}\mathsf{T}^{-1}]:={\bf v}^{\flat}.

Finally, we apply the Hodge star to get the actual volumetric flow rate
v[\mathsf{L}^{D}\mathsf{T}^{-1}]
, i.e.,

v:=\star_{1}\tilde{v}
.

Discussion 6.4

(Solving discrete systems)
.

We can solve the resulting problem
(primal:
Equation

5.4
,
or mixed:
Equation

5.9
)
with standard methods to get
the potential
u^{\mathcal{K}}\in C^{0}\mathcal{K}
and
the flow rate
q^{\mathcal{K}}\in C^{D-1}\mathcal{K}
.
Indeed, the operators and boundary conditions involved have the same forms for all discrete or discretised weak formulations, and so the LHS matrices, RHS vectors, and boundary constraints have the same form
(see
[
berbatov2021guide
, Equations 2.7–2.10, 2.21–2.24]
).
However, one important feature of the mixed weak CMC formulation presented here is that the matrix
A
is diagonal, and so the straightforward solution method of variable elimination works without introducing dense matrices, thus transforming a mixed LHS matrix to a a sparse symmetric positive definite one.
Indeed, the mixed system is represented in the form
Aq-B^{T}u=-g,\ -Bq=f
, and we can express
q
from the first equation by
q=A^{-1}(-g+B^{T}u)
, which leads to

-BA^{-1}(-g+B^{T}u)=f\Rightarrow BA^{-1}B^{T}u=BA^{-1}g-f,

(6.5)

and the matrix
BA^{-1}B^{T}
is sparse (since
A
is diagonal) and positive definite.
Hence, both primal and mixed formulations can be solved using sparse Cholesky decomposition.

Discussion 6.6
.

We will consider four examples for the steady-state formulation, that will illustrate the CMC formulations for various problems with prescribed exact solutions (we start with desired potential and flow rate and derive the input data that leads to these solutions).
In all examples the conductivity will be constant across their domains, and there will be no advective terms.

In

2.

In

Discussion 6.12
.

In the first three examples we observed small relative errors for both potentials and flow rates.
A common feature of those examples was that in the respective embedded meshes the topologically orthogonal sides were also geometrically orthogonal. This is not the case for the irregular mesh in the last example, suggesting that non-orthogonality of the embedded mesh is the reason for the high relative errors. Moreover, our tests on a problem with a parallelogram grid showed similarly high errors.
A compelling reason for this is that the inner product on
1
-cells does not produce optimal Hodge star when topologically orthogonal edges are not geometrically orthogonal.
This can be mended potentially by using non-diagonal inner products, i.e., by introducing additional measures for pairs of
p
-cells having a common boundary
(p-1)
-cell.
Such an inner product will lead to non-local Hodge star (see
[
wilson2007cochain
, Section 8]
) and adjoint coboundary operators but will still be computationally efficient.
Indeed, the resulting linear systems for both primal and mixed formulations use only inner products, and can be expressed as sparse linear systems (although elimination of variables in the mixed formulation will not be effective).
Regarding post-processing in the primal weak formulation, it can be reduced to solving a linear system with a sparse positive definite matrix, the matrix of the inner product.

The definition and usage of inner products with non-orthogonal basis cochains (but still local) is a topic of an ongoing research.

7
Conclusions

In this article we developed a new formalism for modelling transport phenomena based on discrete cochains on quasi-cubical meshes that represent combinatorial differential forms on cell complexes with simple polytopes.
We first described the exterior calculus formulation and using it as a benchmark formulated a discrete model where quantities and operators in exterior calculus are substituted with their discrete counterparts.
However, it is clear that the derivation of the discrete formulation can be done without any reference to the continuum one and we believe this is a more natural approach.
By using a computational mesh partition instead of the original mesh, we were able to: (a) express more complex cell interactions, and (b) use the already developed operators from
berbatov2022diffusion
and
berbatov2023discrete
.
The key benefit over the already explored applications
[
berbatov2022diffusion
, Section 4]
is the use of variational primal and mixed formulations for the main model.
The discrete variational formulations are very similar to those in the continuum for which finite (or virtual) elements are used, but in our case they are solved directly as the problems are already finite dimensional.
We presented several numerical examples of discrete problems whose initial parameters are discretisation of continuum models, and demonstrated similarity to the discretised exact continuum solutions.
Thus, although the primary aim of using CMC is for modelling micro-structures, we showed computationally (yet, without rigorous error analysis), that CMC formulations can also be viewed as new discretisation methods on quasi-cubical meshes with much less computational effort and with the ability to work directly on curved polytopes.

Some final remarks. A major challenge in materials modelling is the computational burden of solving governing equations at multiple scales. Cell complexes enable an efficient decomposition of space, allowing localized computations within individual cells while maintaining global consistency through their topological structure. This results in reduced computational overhead and improved parallelization capabilities. Moreover, the hierarchical nature of cell complexes aligns well with multigrid and multi-scale methods, making it easier to refine models adaptively based on physical requirements. The explicit topological structure of cell complexes allows for a more accurate representation of defects, such as dislocations, cracks, and voids. In continuum methods, discontinuities must be artificially introduced via enrichment techniques like extended finite element methods, while particle-based methods struggle with enforcing constraints. Cell complexes inherently represent discontinuities as boundaries between higher-dimensional elements, enabling more accurate and physically realistic modelling of defect dynamics. The work on formulation of conservation of momenta, required for modelling deformation and defect dynamics, is ongoing and will be reported in a future publication.
