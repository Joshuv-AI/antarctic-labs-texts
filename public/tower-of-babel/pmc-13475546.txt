RNF213-related vasculopathy beyond a linear pre-moyamoya model: authors’ reply
Authors: Takeshi Yoshimoto, Yorito Hattori, Masafumi Ihara
Published: 2026

Dear Editor

We thank Roy and colleagues for their interest in our study1 and would like to clarify their concern that the term “pre-moyamoya” implies a linear and inevitable progression to moyamoya disease (MMD). We do not propose that all carriers of the RNF213 p.R4810K variant will progress to MMD, but rather frame RNF213 p.R4810K-related vasculopathy as a spectrum, where definite MMD represents the most extreme phenotype.2 The pre-moyamoya stage is an operational designation for an antecedent, longitudinally identifiable subset within this spectrum.

We used the term “pre-moyamoya stage” for two reasons as stated in our article. First, in our previous 15-year retrospective longitudinal cohort study, we reported that the annual risk of progression to MMD was approximately 0.88% among the variant carriers with intracranial major artery stenosis or occlusion.3 Second, the current study provides direct prospective evidence: one of the 36 variant carriers was diagnosed with definite MMD during a median follow-up period of 3.61 years.1 We acknowledged that the number of variant carriers in our article was relatively low and that larger prospective cohorts with longer follow-up will be needed to refine this estimate. However, this limitation, in our view, does not undermine the central inference that a pre-moyamoya stage exists.

Roy and colleagues correctly note that several susceptibility loci in RNF213 have been implicated in MMD, as we also indicated in a previous review.2 However, a single founder allele, RNF213 p.R4810K, is detected in 80%–90% of patients with familial MMD in Japan and South Korea and in 20%–30% of those with familial MMD in China, whereas approximately 1.5% of the general population in Japan and South Korea and 0.5% of that in China are unaffected carriers of this particular variant.2 Therefore, focusing on this variant maximizes the inferential power of East Asian stroke research but does not eliminate the need for broader sequencing and functional studies.

Finally, we read with interest the description of “smokeless moyamoya” by Roy and colleagues,4 who stated that baseline “smokeless moyamoya” was not systematically followed up with angiography. Therefore, longitudinal studies are warranted to investigate specifically how variant carriers remain at a pre-moyamoya stage, incorporating detailed angiographic phenotyping and in silico protein structure modeling.

We thank Roy and colleagues for prompting this clarification and look forward to multiregional cohorts that integrate long-term follow-up with broader RNF213 sequencing to delineate the full spectrum of RNF213-related vasculopathy.
