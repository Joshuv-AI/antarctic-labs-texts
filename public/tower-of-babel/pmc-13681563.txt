Ayurvedic approaches to scorpion stings: Historical insights, modern scientific perspectives and challenges
Authors: Susmita Nath, Naba K. Hazarika, Ashis K. Mukherjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Scorpion stings are medical emergencies that require immediate attention and are frequently overlooked. Commercial antivenom available only in India is for a single species of venomous scorpion, with significant limitations. Additionally, access to antivenom therapy is limited in rural areas. Examining the ancient Indian traditional medicine, Ayurveda, reveals insights into the underlying mechanisms of scorpion envenomation and potential treatments. Thus, this study examines the Ayurvedic treatment of scorpion stings from a historical and traditional viewpoint and a modern scientific standpoint, noting its shortcomings and recommending topics for further investigation. This study used a narrative integrative review approach. Relevant literature was searched in Google Scholar, PubMed, and Scopus using the terms ‘Ayurveda,’ ‘Traditional Indian medicine’, ‘scorpion sting treatment’, and ‘Vrishchika Damsha’. Classical Ayurvedic texts, ethnomedicinal reports, preclinical studies, and selected clinical accounts were reviewed and summarized to relate traditional Ayurvedic concepts to current knowledge on scorpion envenomation. However, Ayurvedic knowledge of 'Vrishchika Damsha' (scorpion sting) focused only on the history and Ayurvedic perspective on scorpions, as well as on approaches to treating scorpion envenomation in India. The study showed that Agada Tantra, a specialized branch of Ayurvedic Toxicology, focuses on understanding and treating various toxins, including those produced by scorpions (Vrishchika). Ayurvedic texts provide detailed descriptions of scorpion sting symptoms and categorize the venom type as Manda visha (mild), Madhya visha (moderate), and Ugra visha (highly venomous) based on its toxicity. Ayurvedic classics such as Susihruta Samhita, Agnivesha Samhita, and Charak Samhita outline various treatment approaches for Vrishchika Damsha, including ointments, tablets, fumigation techniques, sudation therapy, chants, and herbal solvents. Significant gaps in knowledge regarding the effectiveness, safety, and standardization of Ayurvedic treatments remain, so a more in-depth investigation is needed to examine its potential for treating scorpion stings and assess its limitations through a modern scientific lens. This study will help identify knowledge gaps, ensuring a more evidence-based integration of traditional and contemporary healthcare methods.

Overview of scorpion envenomation

Scorpion sting is an often overlooked, acute, life-threatening, time-limited medical emergency, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions [1]. There are numerous cases of venomous incidents that occur annually, among which scorpion stings have significantly contributed to the rise in this number, with 1 million cases and 3250 fatalities worldwide [2]. This study discusses the Ayurvedic concept of Vrischika Dansha (scorpion sting) and reviews relevant observations from modern pharmacological literature. Ancient Ayurvedic texts describe detailed therapeutic approaches for scorpion envenomation, emphasizing detoxification and symptom-specific management. On the other hand, modern studies have begun to summarize reported biochemical and pharmacological observations related to selected traditional interventions, emphasizing their potential to mitigate systemic toxicity. By integrating both perspectives, this review seeks to present a balanced view of historical practices, current scientific evidence, and existing challenges in translating traditional wisdom into modern clinical contexts, while highlighting the limitations associated with them.

A narrative integrative review approach was adopted for this review. Literature was identified from public databases Google Scholar, PubMed, and Scopus. The search words were ‘Ayurveda’, ‘traditional Indian medicine’, ‘scorpion sting treatment’, and ‘Vrishchika Damsha’. The search was intended to capture a broad range of classical Ayurvedic texts, ethnomedicinal reports, preclinical experimental studies, and illustrative clinical accounts published in English between 1996 and 2025. Duplicate and clearly unrelated records were excluded during screening. The retrieved literature was qualitatively analyzed to interpret traditional Ayurvedic concepts in the context of contemporary toxicological knowledge regarding scorpion envenomation.

Scorpion species of clinical relevance

Scorpions inhabit warm regions of India, dwelling in crevices, burrows, debris, and agricultural areas such as sugarcane or banana plantations [3]. They are primarily active during summer and less active during winter; hence, incidents increase dramatically during the summer months [4]. Stings usually result from accidental contact; scorpions sting to defend or catch prey. Moreover, a scorpion can inflict a sting without injecting venom (dry sting). Until now, 2500 scorpion species have been reported worldwide, but approximately only 58 are known to be dangerous to humans, mainly from the Buthidae and Scorpionidae families [5], whereas in India, 86 scorpion species have been described [6,7]. The medically important scorpions of India are Mesobuthus tamulus, Palamneus swammer-dami, and Heterometreus bengalensis [[5], [6], [7]]. However, the limited literature suggests that the Indian Red Scorpion (Mesobuthus tamulus) venom is more potent than that of other Indian scorpions. Maximum mortalities are reported from southern India, such as Kerala, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Saurashtra, Karnataka, the Konkan region, Pondicherry, Chennai, Bellary, Rayalaseema, and Western Maharashtra [6,7]. In Indian states such as Tamil Nadu, Kerala, and Karnataka, M. tamulus causes significant morbidity and mortality; however, timely diagnosis and intervention can prevent fatalities. In contrast, the other mildly venomous scorpions in the genus Heterometrus are not fatal but cause severe pain, swelling, redness, and inflammation. However, more in-depth studies are needed to understand the pharmacology and pathophysiology of scorpion envenomation in this species.

Current management approaches

Although immediate medical attention may not be necessary for mild envenomation or dry sting, untreated cases could lead to further complications. While antivenom remains a specific and effective treatment for severe scorpion stings, it must be used in conjunction with supportive measures. In addition, the use of antivenom is associated with particular challenges, including the risk of allergic reactions and reduced effectiveness across different geographical origins. Moreover, getting sufficient raw materials (venom) for antivenom production is challenging [8]. Therefore, complexities surrounding antivenom production, economic factors, and its overall effectiveness limit its use. As noted in developing countries like India, poor transportation infrastructure and consequent delays in antivenom administration make antivenom administration less effective for the management of scorpion envenomation [8].

Epidemiological burden of scorpion envenomation

Though the reported cases of scorpion stings are approximately 1 million, resulting in around 3250 deaths; however, the accurate epidemiological data remains unknown [1]. This underreporting is mainly due to a lack of attention to mortality from venomous animal bites and to underreporting from rural areas where scorpions are prevalent and people are at high risk of scorpion stings, especially children, leading to frequent incidents [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Furthermore, the disorganization within health services also plays a significant role in poor reporting, insufficient awareness, inadequately coordinated surveys, and subpar record-keeping regarding the regional clinical manifestations of envenomation, significantly impeding the accurate representation of the severity of this neglected health concern [13,14]. Even qualified health professionals often lack the necessary skills to identify and manage scorpion envenomation. The absence of standardized management protocols for such cases at the hospital level, along with the lack of a comprehensive national program addressing animal bites, exacerbates this issue [3].

Moreover, a significant segment of the population tends to seek the counsel of local healers before visiting the hospital, a phenomenon that can be linked to the lower epidemiological data [15]. Poor rural medical facilities are the primary reason for the need for faith healers [16]. Even when medical help is sought, poor transport hampers timely access to hospital care. Additionally, inadequately equipped health facilities, untrained doctors, a lack of standardized management guidelines for scorpion envenomation at the hospital level, and lower socioeconomic conditions contribute to higher mortality rates due to scorpion and other venomous stings [1,17].

In that case, healthcare providers must recognize the significance of scorpion envenomation for public health and actively participate in data-collection initiatives and spread awareness, given existing deficiencies in systematic reporting and the prevalence of underreporting [18].

After snakebite envenomation, scorpion stings represent a major contributor to the burden of venomous animal injuries worldwide, with an adjusted mortality rate of 0.9 per 100,000 population reported [3,19]. However, the actual incidence of scorpion envenomation is believed to be substantially higher than reported figures because of underreporting, limited healthcare access, and deficiencies in surveillance and reporting systems [18]. Also, it is not formally listed as a neglected tropical disease (NTD) by the World Health Organization (WHO). Nevertheless, the case fatality rate in India varies from 1 to 10.7%, as reported by various studies across different regions [2,3,20,21]. Scorpion stings predominantly affect children and individuals in rural areas, reflecting the higher risk where scorpions are abundant [22,23]. Hospital-based reports from different parts of India indicate these incidents are widespread, with fatalities occurring primarily due to delayed or inadequate treatment [24,25]. These observations highlight the necessity for prompt diagnosis, efficient management, and awareness initiatives to reduce morbidity and mortality associated with scorpion stings. Notably, despite scorpions having existed for over 430 million years, it was only recently, in 2019, that the WHO declared that antivenom use is mandatory in affected countries, highlighting a significant gap in addressing the longstanding threat posed by scorpion envenomation [26]. Despite this, only 19 ASAs are manufactured worldwide for human use against scorpion stings, and in India, only two leading antivenom manufacturing companies produce antivenom against Mesobuthus tamulus [26].

Notably, while modern medical science has developed antivenoms over the past century, challenges remain in producing, funding, and distributing effective antivenoms that cover a wide range of scorpion species, making their use debatable. On the other hand, Ayurveda has described the scorpion and the pathophysiology following envenomation, providing valuable insights. Therefore, there is a need to look back, explore the science behind Ayurveda, and discover possible treatment methods derived from it.

Classical Ayurvedic concepts

India is renowned for its rich plant diversity, among which the Indian medicinal plants stand out exceptionally. With over 20,000 recorded species, India is a major producer of medicinal plant remedies [27]. These medicinal plants act as a key to a resource called Ayurveda, often called the "science of life" [Ayur means life, and Veda means science]. It is the traditional Indian medical system, rooted in folk medicine, that believes nature holds the power to heal and protect humankind [27,28]. This concept evolved between 2500 and 5000 BC, and this ancient wisdom has been passed down through generations, with a clear focus on preventive measures rather than curative approaches [27]. Ayurveda was later divided into the Atreya (physicians) and Dhanwantari (surgeons) schools, leading to the Charak and Susruta Samhitas [28]. It has eight branches, including Kayachikitsa (Internal Medicine), Kaumar Bhritya (Pediatrics), Bhootavidya (Psychiatry), Shalakya (ENT & Ophthalmology), Shalya Tantra (Surgery), Agada Tantra (Toxicology), Rasayana (Geriatrics), and Vajikarana (Aphrodisiacs) [28].

As one of the earliest medical systems, Ayurveda features a specialized discipline called toxicology (Agada Tantra), which describes the science of visanivarana aaushadhis (medicines to treat toxins or poisons), various kinds of toxins or poisons (vishas) and their treatment. Agada Tantra deals with the Vishavidya/Damstra Chikitsa. Toxicology has a specific section that deals with envenomation, the process by which venomous animals actively deliver toxins to their targets. From ancient times to the 21st century, envenomation has remained a significant burden and challenge for medical science. Scorpions (Vrishchika) stand out for their deadly venomous stings (Damsha), particularly in rural areas of India. Toxicology describes Visha as either Kritrima (artificial) or Akritrima (natural), which can further be Sthavara (inanimate), Jangama (animate). It defines Laghu (lightness), Ruksha (Dryness), Ashu (quickness), Vishada (non-sliminess/clear), Vyavayi (spreading property) Tikshna (sharpness), Vikasi (relaxant), Sukshma (easily absorbed), Ushna (hotness), and Anirdishya rasa (indistinct taste) as some properties of poison (Visha) that contribute to hindering normal body functions of the victim.

Symptom descriptions in classical texts and modern science

The Agadatantra explained the symptoms caused by the scorpion sting (Vrischika Visha Prabhava) as a burning sensation like fire (Vahnivat dahanam), quick spreading in an upward direction (Kshipram urdhvarohanam), which means moving towards the vital organ of the body located in the upper part of the body, pain remaining at the site of the sting (Danshe ruja tishthati), instant severe pain (Sadyah ati ruk), brownish discolouration of the sting site (Danshashyavata), and pricking the type of pain at the sting site (Dansha toda). The Ayurvedic classics have described the three types of scorpions as Manda visha (mild), Madhya visha (moderate), and ugra visha (highly venomous) based on the Vishadamsa Lakshanas.

However, modern medical science explains the venoms as a complex mixture of neurotoxins, cardiotoxins, nephrotoxins, and hemolytic agents, resulting in diverse clinical and pathophysiological effects such as myocardial injury, arrhythmia, pulmonary edema, and shock [7]. The neurotoxins in scorpion venom induce symptoms like sweating, vomiting, nausea, excessive salivation, hyperactivity, heart failure, and, in severe cases, coma by significantly enhancing neurotransmitter release [7,29]. Additionally, ion channel toxins in the venom, targeting sodium, potassium, calcium, and chloride channels, stimulate the release of acetylcholine and catecholamines, causing intense localized pain [30].

Venoms from certain scorpions, such as M. tamulus, contain serotonin, a monoamine linked to vomiting and severe local pain [7,29]. Moreover, scorpion venom triggers inflammatory responses by stimulating the immune system to release proinflammatory cytokines and mediators [31]. Molecules such as TNF, IL-1, and IL-6, induced by venom, activate macrophages and lymphocytes, amplifying the inflammatory response [31]. These cytokines cause vasodilation, immune cell recruitment, and nitric oxide release, potentially leading to shock, organ dysfunction, and cellular damage [22]. Phospholipases in the venom act as powerful hemolytic agents by hydrolyzing phospholipids in cell membranes, leading to tissue necrosis, hemorrhage, inflammation, and edema [29]. Phospholipase activity has been identified in various scorpion species, including Opisthacanthus cayaporum and Heterometrus laoticus [7,29].

Classical treatments and formulations

As mentioned, most envenomation occurs in remote, underdeveloped regions where proper medical facilities are unavailable or limited; in such cases, people often rely on traditional treatment methods [1]. For instance, about 70% of the Indian rural population and about 40% of people in Western countries depend on traditional herbal remedies [27]. One such method is ethnomedicine, a substantial part of Ayurveda, which includes the study of the conventional medicine of ethnic communities, their knowledge and practices for the management of health and treatment, transmitted not through proper documentation or scientific validation but orally over centuries [28]. Several such medicinal plants are described in Ayurveda for both external and internal use for the treatment of scorpion stings. Sushruta Samhita, Agnivesha Samhita, and Charak Samhita provide detailed guidance on the management of Visha, including treatment approaches for Vrischika as described in Charak Samhita (a revised version of Agnivesha Samhita). These Ayurvedic texts recommend various therapeutic measures, including herbal ointments (lepa), tablets/capsules (gutika), fumigation (dhupanam), sudation therapy (ushna-swedana), medicated washes (parisheka), chants (mantra chikitsa), and dietetic interventions. Collectively, these sources present a systematic Ayurvedic approach to treating scorpion stings. In the following section, this review discusses a few Ayurvedic formulations used to treat mild cases of scorpion envenomation, focusing on symptomatic treatment.

Ethnomedical observations and case descriptions

There is an increasing demand for practical research to modernize Ayurvedic methodologies for Vrishchika Damsha Chikithsa. Interestingly, several Ayurvedic detoxification strategies, such as the use of herbal antidotes, fomentation, and oil-based formulations, show conceptual parallels with modern therapeutic approaches targeting inflammation, neuromuscular blockade, and cardiovascular stabilization following scorpion envenomation. These types of therapy reflect how ancient Ayurvedic practices may align with the physiological principles recognized in modern toxicology. Several recent cases have highlighted the use of Ayurvedic remedies for the treatment of scorpion stings, focusing primarily on symptomatic relief. Numerous instances of effective treatment for clinical scorpion sting cases by Ayurvedic treatments are detailed in the supplementary file. Such treatment methods include the use of Ghritasaindhava dhara in combination of Vilwadya Gulika, Kulkadi Kashya, along with Higuadi Gulika, use of Kaidaryayuktatakra lepa, Dasanga Gulika, and Arkapatra gritika dhara.

The composition Ghritasaindhava dhara primarily consists of 250 ml of cow's ghee, a typical Indian dairy fat, combined with 2-3 pinches of saindhava lavana, commonly known as rock salt. The ghee is poured into a tumbler in a boiling water pan, and the preparation is maintained at a constant temperature (40 °C) throughout the dhara procedure for 45 min. This mode of treatment is said to have anti-inflammatory, analgesic, antitoxic, antipyretic, immunomodulatory, and antihistaminic properties that help reduce pain and swelling. Ghritasaindhava Dhara is said to be effective because of its various gunas (properties); as Vagbhata said, Ghrita (ghee) is the perfect medicine for all stages of poisoning, as it has a sweet taste (Madhu rasa), is heavy (guru guna), unctuous (snigdha guna), and can reduce heat-related effects (seta veerya), which opposes the lightness-heat properties (laghu-teekshna gunas) of the poison (visa) that helps to reduce the pain, burning sensation, swelling, and erythema. On the other hand, rock salt's ability to be easily absorbed (sookshma guna) enhances ghee's penetration through the channels.

Wood apple (A. marmelos) is a traditionally used medicinal plant known to have anti-inflammatory effects and to be effective in treating pain and swelling, which are common symptoms of scorpion envenomation [32]. Similarly, turmeric rhizome paste (C. longa) also holds significant traditional value; however, for scorpion envenomation, it is applied externally to the site of a sting to treat the sting [1]. The juice of the leaf of Indian gooseberry (E. officinalis), along with black pepper (P. nigrum), is given to treat scorpion stings [1]. Notably, P. nigrum is a multipurpose traditional herb used as a seasoning and has a therapeutic role. It is occasionally combined with specific herbs as a topical paste or decoction to help alleviate pain and swelling from scorpion stings. However, there is no clinical or scientific evidence that black pepper neutralizes scorpion venom. Another ingredient of the Vilwady Gulika is Holy Basil (O. sanctum), a widely known, multipurpose traditional medicinal plant whose therapeutic properties are now well established. It has been well valued in Ayurvedic medicine in India for about 3000 years [33]. It has a track record of being used for treating a wide range of common ailments, including snake bites and scorpion stings [33]. It has been reported that O. sanctum is traditionally used as an antidote for scorpion stings [1]. Moreover, the antivenomous activity of O. sanctum has also been studied and found to be effective against other animal bites, such as dog bites and insect bites [33,34].

The Indian long pepper (P. longum) has gained importance in traditional systems of medicine. Traditionally, unripe fruits are used to treat several diseases, as well as scorpion stings and snake bites [1]. Pongame oil tree (P. glabra), also known as Pongamia pinnata (L.) Pierre, is one of the most commonly and widely used medicinal herbs in Ayurvedic medicine, having several therapeutic uses, including treating scorpion stings. Not only this, Nath and Mukherjee (2023) [1], have described the use of P. pinnata roots along with some other plants (rhizomes of sweet flag (Acorus calamus L.), roots of Chinese chaste tree (Vitex negundo L.) in fomentation for the symptomatic treatment of scorpion stings; all are boiled in water, and the vapor is directed to the site of scorpion sting [1].

Beleric myrobalan (T. bellerica) is another significant traditional medicinal plant native to India and Southeast Asia, having esteemed traditional medicinal value in Ayurveda and Unani. Various parts of the T. bellirica plant are used for their therapeutic properties, such as the wood ash of T. bellerica, which is taken orally, whereas, on the other hand, its fruit is traditionally used to treat pain due to the scorpion sting; notably, the mode of application is not defined [35]. Similarly, Chebulic Myrobalan (T. chebula) is another ingredient of Vilwady Gulika, a unique herb used since ancient times, as mentioned by Charak, and is included in many traditional herbal formulations. It is generally used for fevers, asthma, and scorpion stings; however, the mode of application is not documented. It promotes the normal downward movement of Vata (Vayu), which is considered an important mechanism for interrupting the disease process responsible for swelling (Shotha) and pain (Vedana) [32]. Similarly, ginger (Z. officinale), a widely used plant traditionally used for a variety of medicinal purposes. Overall, the individual botanical components of Vilwadya Gulika, either independently or in combination, have traditionally been used to treat scorpion stings. Similarly, P. glabra, C. longa, and C. fenestratum reduce skin lesions and itching; likewise, T. chebula and T. belerica reduce burning sensation and erythema and have antipyretic properties, which synergistically help reverse the clinical effects that occur post-envenomation. Consistently, Kulkadi Kashya is made by a combination of several medicinal plants, such as (i) Macrotyloma uniflorum (horse gram), (ii) Allium sativum (garlic), (iii) Ricinus communis (castor), and (iv) Sesamum indicum (sesame) [36]. Its formulation was explained in erysipelas (Visarpa prakarana) in Sahasra Yoga for relieving fever (Jwara), vomiting (Chardi), poisoning (Visha), burning sensation (Daha), delirium (Bhranti), thirst (Trishna), and pain (Ruja). The constituent herbs possess diverse pharmacological properties that may be relevant in the management of scorpion envenomation. M. uniflorum has been reported to exhibit antihistaminic and antioxidant activities, while A. sativum demonstrates anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, antioxidant, and cardioprotective effects. R. communis possesses anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, wound-healing, and cytoprotective properties, whereas S. indicum is rich in bioactive lignans such as sesamin and sesamolin, which exhibit antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities. Collectively, these pharmacological effects may help mitigate scorpion or other venom-induced inflammation, oxidative stress, local tissue damage, and pain, thereby supporting the traditional use of “Kulathadi Kashayam” (an Ayurveda formulation) in conditions associated with envenomation. However, the direct effect of individual medicinal plants on the treatment of scorpion stings has not been scientifically proven [36].

Similarly, the traditional use of Sesamum indicum L. seeds, along with the leaves or stem bark of A. indica and the fruits of jackfruit (Artocarpus heterophyllus Lam.), is described, where the ingredients are wrapped in a cloth, soaked in ghee, and burnt to fumigate the site of the sting [1]. Notably, the mechanism of action is not explained.

On the other hand, the Hinguadi Gulika contains Citrus medica L. (wild lemon/Matulunga rasa) and Ferula asafetida Bosc. (devil's dung/Hingu) and yellow orpiment (Haritala) (Fig. 1a). These ingredients are claimed to play a significant role in relieving severe pain from scorpion stings; for instance, Nath et al. [1] documented the traditional use of F. asafetida for treating scorpion stings. Similarly, one such remedy using C. medica and F. asafetida (a synonym of Ferula narthex Bioss.) and other such medicinal plants has been discussed in the supplementary file. All these medicinal plants also have antitoxic, analgesic, and sthambana properties (drugs that arrest the mobility of a moving substance), which prevent the venom from spreading throughout the body. Additionally, the anti-nociceptive property of F. asafetida is due to the presence of ferulic acid (Fig. 1b), which reduces inflammation. Ferulic acid (FA) functions as an anti-inflammatory drug by regulating essential inflammatory signaling pathways, including the NF-κB pathway (Fig. 2a) and the JAK-STAT system (Fig. 2b). NF-κB is a crucial transcription factor in the inflammatory response, facilitating the expression of genes associated with inflammation. It stimulated the production of many pro-inflammatory cytokines and markers, including COX-2, iNOS, IL-6, and TNF-α. FA functions as an NF-κB inhibitor by obstructing the synthesis of pro-inflammatory cytokines and markers essential for pathway activation [37]. Moreover, Mir et al. [38] indicated that FA inhibits TLR-4-mediated NF-κB activation in lipopolysaccharide-induced inflammation in Balb/c mice [37]. Likewise, the JAK-STAT pathway serves as an additional signaling pathway for several growth factors and cytokines. In this instance, FA's anti-inflammatory action is probably due to PIAS1-mediated negative regulation of STAT1 in 2METH-induced inflammation [64]. It was discovered that FA suppresses the JAK-STAT pathway by downregulating STAT expression and phosphorylation, hence diminishing the mechanism's contribution to inflammation. Furthermore, FA enhances PIAS1 (Protein Inhibitor of Activated STAT1) expression, a negative regulator of STAT that associates with the activated STAT dimer, thereby inhibiting its interaction with nuclear DNA [37]. The aggregate impact of these mechanisms results in decreased levels of pro-inflammatory markers, such as IL-6, TNF-α, iNOS, and COX-2. On the other hand, yellow orpiment, a mineral-based substance (Khata Visha), has traditionally been used for scorpion management. Historical accounts describe the use of orpiment fumigation to repel or eliminate scorpions from their habitats, thereby reducing the risk of scorpion stings. Such traditional practices suggest a potential role for yellow orpiment in the prevention and management of scorpion stings [39]. Additionally, the carrier property of the yellow orpiment enhances the pain-relieving (shoolaharatwa) property of F. asafetida, while the minuteness (Sookshma) property enhances the penetrating ability of the ointment. Nevertheless, both formulations showed encouraging results, with the patient showing improvement in symptoms and signs of a scorpion sting. The decoction was shown to lessen the internal toxicity caused by scorpion stings, while the ointment reduced outward symptoms after 9 days of therapy.

Kaidaryayuktatakra lepa is prepared from the leaves of Murraya koenigii (L.). Spreng and buttermilk. The leaves, bark, root bark juice, and powder of M. koenigii (administered orally and externally) are traditionally reported to treat scorpion stings (Table 1) [1]. The effectiveness of the ointment is mainly due to its cold potency (himaguna). The buttermilk has blood-clearing potency (asravishodhanam), hot potency (ushnaveerya), and an antitoxic effect (vishaharatva). It has the potency to clear the channels (Srotosodhana), which ultimately helps remove the blockages of the channels (srotosanga) created by venom at the sting site [40].

The curry leaves contain several bioactive compounds, such as koenigin, koenine, koenimbine, carbazole alkaloids, murrayazoline, mahanimbine, etc., which possess anti-inflammatory, analgesic, and antioxidant properties [40]. Safety considerations of the traditional therapies

Although the aforementioned case studies revealed successful Ayurvedic treatment of scorpion stings, it is crucial to acknowledge their limits as well. Although Ayurvedic medicines are effective for various conditions [47], the terms 'natural' and 'safe' should not be used interchangeably [48,49]. It should not be assumed that herbal medicines originate from natural sources; they have low/no adverse effects or toxicity. This study opposes the misconception that natural products are inherently non-toxic and devoid of adverse effects; instead, it aims to highlight an unbiased view by highlighting both the potential benefits and risks of natural substances. All medicines may cause adverse effects with long-term use if not used at appropriate doses, in accordance with standard protocols or prescriptions, and herbal medicine is no exception [50]. Many plants are reported to cause toxicity at higher or long-term doses. Moreover, numerous methods are accessible to the public without a prescription, often due to inadequate regulation [49]. This complicates and obstructs their widespread adoption by professional healthcare and modern medical science [49]. Previous research [1] has highlighted the importance of validating traditional treatments and addressing misconceptions about their misuse, driven by insufficient information. This study highlights the importance of scientific evaluation to ensure the safe and effective implementation of these applications.

Likewise, none of these formulations and therapeutic remedies are scientifically standardized. Therefore, they should not be considered a substitute for standard antivenom therapy, which remains the only evidence-based treatment for severe scorpion stings. Additionally, the pharmaceutical properties of these medicinal plants have not been systematically investigated, either in vitro or in vivo, specifically against scorpion venom. This scarcity of research creates a significant knowledge gap, particularly regarding rigorous in vivo and in vitro experimental analyses of their pharmacological and venom-neutralization potential.

Furthermore, many of the mentioned Ayurveda formulations contain animal-derived substances, such as goat and cow urine, which lead to serious safety issues. Such ingredients in the formulation may lead to zoonotic diseases, bacterial contamination, and potential infections [51,52]. Urine from livestock has been documented to harbor bacteria of zoonotic importance, including Leptospira spp., Brucella spp., and enteric pathogens, which may be transmitted through direct contact, improper handling, or mucosal exposure, leading to localized or systemic infections [53]. In addition, inadequate collection and storage conditions can facilitate secondary bacterial contamination and microbial proliferation, further increasing infection risk. These concerns highlight the need for microbiological quality control and rigorous safety evaluation before considering any animal-derived products for therapeutic use.

Furthermore, exposure to bovine by-products presents risks of tuberculosis, brucellosis, leptospirosis, and other diseases [54]. Moreover, the risk of damage and dangerous effects is associated with the inappropriate use of herbal medicines without any guidelines or regulations. Additionally, due to the presence of multiple bioactive components, the risk of interactions with conventional medicine increases, complicating their safe and rational use [50]. Also, several formulations include the use of minerals, such as haritala, which may further cause degradation into soluble, toxic arsenic oxides, leading to acute poisoning, skin lesions, and respiratory issues. Several reports also suggested that it has carcinogenic effects like squamous cell carcinoma, lung cancer, and bladder cancer, whereas non-carcinogenic effects include hyperpigmentation, keratosis, cardiovascular and neurological disorders, diabetes, and respiratory issues [55].

Standardization and quality control gaps

Ayurveda has incorporated at least 7000 plants and 8000 remedies into its medical system, but only a few are used in modern medicine [28]. This represents that, despite growing interest in modern medical science in ethnomedicine, the potential of Ayurveda to mitigate envenomation has remained largely unexplored. Many formulations remain at a very early stage of development, lack scientific refinement, require further validation, and have poorly monitored applications. Additionally, there is a limited understanding of their mode of action, other potent therapeutic roles, potential side effects or adverse effects, and interactions with conventional medicine, raising concerns about their long-term safety, potency, and purity, which needs to be addressed [17,49].

Contemporary physicians are experimenting with these compositions in conventional medical procedures, possibly due to insufficient training in Ayurvedic principles, lack of belief in their clinical efficacy or scientific grounding, and obsolete production methods that do not meet current pharmaceutical standards. Nevertheless, all the above proposed formulations require rigorous preclinical toxicological evaluation, drawing on advanced medical knowledge and methodologies. Verification and revision of these aged treatments in accordance with contemporary requirements are necessary. This underscores the importance of noting that, in severe envenomation cases, the patient should not rely exclusively on traditional treatment and that timely administration of antivenom remains the primary and advisable treatment.

Future research may systematically explore and validate the anti-scorpion venom (venom-neutralizing) properties of these plants and their formulations to advance understanding of their pharmacological potential and to translate traditional knowledge into evidence-based, clinically relevant therapeutic interventions. However, it is important to note that safe implementation results from multiple factors such as practitioner competence, product quality, effective communication, and strong, standard regulatory support. Failure in any of these factors can cause adverse effects at any step. Nonetheless, with proper training, quality assurance, and clear frameworks, traditional medicine is generally a safe and trusted source of treatment.

Opportunities for future evaluation

In recent years, the use of ethnomedicinal plants has been widely accepted in developing and developed countries such as Europe and the US [49]. For instance, about 3.5 billion people use plant-based medication, and approximately half of the drugs are products of natural origin. In contrast, about 11% of the 252 drugs listed in the WHO Essential Medicines List are exclusively of plant origin [28]. Therefore, 'The WHO Traditional Medicine Strategy 2014-2023′ was launched by the WHO in 2013 to integrate traditional and complementary medicine to promote universal healthcare to ensure the quality, safety, and effectiveness of such medications. Hence, exploring plants to address envenomation is crucial, and more research and a science-based approach are needed to provide proper documentation and scientific validation to conserve this traditional oral knowledge from the verge of extinction. Therefore, it is necessary to establish this traditional therapeutic knowledge for use in the 21st century, when drugs must be standardized using modern quality control parameters to be accepted by clinicians, biomedical practitioners, and the pharmaceutical industry. Future evaluation should focus on systematic phytochemical profiling, mechanism-based studies against venom-induced pathophysiological pathways (e.g., neurotoxicity, cardiotoxicity), in vivo validation, and formulation development to translate traditional remedies into evidence-based therapeutics.

Conclusion

The study of Ayurvedic approaches to scorpion envenomation highlights both opportunities and challenges. While antivenom is the primary treatment for severe cases, it is not free of limitations. In rural areas of developing countries like India, medical facilities are scarce, and therefore, people often rely on traditional treatments like Ayurveda. Therefore, Ayurvedic remedies are a critical part of healthcare in these regions, from both cultural and accessibility perspectives. However, a significant challenge in promoting Ayurvedic treatments is the need for more scientific validation. Most Ayurvedic remedies for envenomation have not yet been systematically evaluated using modern experimental, toxicological, or clinical research methodologies. This gap prevents the broader medical community from fully accepting these treatments. Ayurveda may offer a valuable body of knowledge for generating research hypotheses only if subjected to rigorous standardization, quality control, and toxicological evaluation. Then these conventional therapies could be explored as complementary or supportive approaches, particularly in areas with limited access to antivenom. Further well-designed preclinical and clinical studies are essential before any claims regarding safety or therapeutic efficacy can be substantiated. This review thus integrates classical Ayurvedic perspectives with modern scientific insights while acknowledging the limitations of the existing evidence. The modernization and integration of Ayurveda with modern medicine should proceed cautiously, guided by principles of evidence-based medicine, aiming to improve patient safety while responsibly preserving traditional knowledge relevant to scorpion sting management.
