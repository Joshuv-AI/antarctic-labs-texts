Multi-omics integration model of exosome metabolomics and PD-1/PD-L1 expression for early prediction of immunotherapy efficacy in advanced liver cancer
Authors: Caixia Li, Dandan Du, Yanbao Liu, Xuan Wang, Yuchen Feng, Panpan Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To explore the application value of a multi - omics fusion model based on exosome metabolomics and the expression levels of programmed death − 1/programmed death - ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) in the early prediction of the efficacy of immunotherapy for advanced liver cancer, screen key biomarkers affecting the response to immunotherapy, construct and validate a highly efficient prediction model, and provide a basis for clinical individualized treatment decisions. A total of 824 patients with advanced liver cancer who received PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor treatment were selected. They were randomly divided into a training set and a test set at a ratio of 7:3. General clinical data, exosomal metabolomics data, and PD-1/PD-L1 expression levels of the patients were collected. Key predictive indicators were screened through univariate analysis and multivariate Logistic regression analysis. Multi - omics fusion prediction models were constructed. The performance of the models was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Decision curves and calibration curves were plotted to validate the clinical practicality of the models. Multivariate analysis showed that Child - Pugh classification, extra-hepatic metastasis, neutrophil - to - lymphocyte ratio, sphingosine − 1 - phosphate, glutamine, and the expression level of programmed death - ligand 1 were independent predictive factors (all P < 0.05). Among the constructed models, the random forest fusion model had the best performance. Its AUCs in the training set and test set were 0.757 and 0.751 respectively, and its precision, accuracy, recall, F1 score and other indicators were also superior to those of other models. The multi-omics fusion model based on exosome metabolomics and PD-1/PD-L1 expression has shown good early prediction efficacy for the efficacy of immunotherapy in advanced liver cancer. The random forest model performs the best, which provides a single-center preliminary reference for clinicians to identify patients who may benefit from immunotherapy at an early stage, and its clinical value in optimizing treatment plans and improving patients’ prognosis needs to be further confirmed by multi-center external validation.

Introduction

Liver cancer is one of the most common malignancies worldwide, with hepatocellular carcinoma accounting for over 90% of cases1–3. Due to the lack of obvious early symptoms, most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage and have lost the opportunity for surgical resection4. Immunotherapy, especially the application of programmed cell death protein 1/programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) inhibitors, has brought new hope to patients with advanced liver cancer and significantly prolonged the survival of some patients5. However, clinical practice shows that only a fraction of patients can benefit from immunotherapy, and the overall response rate remains low, approximately 20%-30%6. Meanwhile, immunotherapy may cause immune-related adverse reactions, bringing additional pain and economic burden to patients7. Therefore, accurately predicting the response of patients with advanced liver cancer to immunotherapy at an early stage and screening out potential beneficiaries are of great significance for formulating individualized treatment plans, improving treatment efficacy, and reducing adverse reactions8,9. Traditional predictive indicators such as alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) have limited predictive value and are difficult to meet clinical needs10. With the development of precision medicine, multi-omics technologies have provided new perspectives for disease diagnosis and prognosis prediction. As nanoscale vesicles, exosomes carry a large number of bioactive molecules, including metabolites and proteins, and are involved in tumorigenesis, development, and immune regulation. Their metabolomic characteristics may serve as potential biomarkers for predicting the efficacy of immunotherapy11,12. Sphingosine‑1‑phosphate (S1P) was prioritized because it regulates T‑cell trafficking and promotes M2‑type macrophage polarization, creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment; glutamine (Gln) was selected because it is essential for T‑cell metabolic activation and proliferation, and its competition between tumor cells and immune cells can lead to T‑cell exhaustion. The PD-1/PD-L1 pathway plays a key role in tumor immune escape, and the expression levels of PD-1/PD-L1 are closely related to the efficacy of immunotherapy13,14. Combining exosome metabolomics with PD-1/PD-L1 expression levels to construct a multi-omics fusion model is expected to improve the accuracy of predicting the efficacy of immunotherapy for advanced liver cancer. Currently, some studies have explored the application of single-omics indicators in predicting the efficacy of liver cancer immunotherapy, but research on multi-omics fusion models is still in its infancy. This study aims to integrate exosome metabolomic data, PD-1/PD-L1 expression levels, and clinical indicators of patients with advanced liver cancer, and construct a multi-omics fusion prediction model through various machine-learning algorithms, aiming to improve the early prediction accuracy, provide strong support for clinical treatment decisions, and promote the development of individualized immunotherapy for advanced liver cancer.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

A total of 824 patients with advanced liver cancer who received PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor treatment in the hospital from January 2020 to December 2023 were selected as research subjects. All patients were diagnosed with advanced liver cancer by pathological histology or clinical imaging, meeting the diagnostic criteria in the “Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Primary Liver Cancer (2022 Edition)”15.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Patients aged 18–80 years; diagnosed with advanced liver cancer by pathology or clinically, ineligible for surgical resection or refused surgery; (2) received PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor monotherapy or combination therapy; (3) had not received other immunotherapies before treatment; had complete clinical data and exosome samples; (4) patients and their families provided informed consent and signed the informed consent form.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Patients with other malignancies; (2) patients with severe liver and kidney dysfunction, cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, or other serious underlying diseases; (3) patients with autoimmune diseases or receiving immunosuppressive therapy; patients with unqualified exosome samples or missing clinical data; patients who terminated treatment due to severe adverse reactions during the treatment process and whose efficacy could not be evaluated. Patients were randomly divided into a training set (577 cases) and a test set (247 cases) at a ratio of 7:3. During the grouping process, BCLC staging and Child-Pugh grading were used as stratification factors to ensure a balanced distribution of the two groups in the above-mentioned key clinical characteristics and reduce model evaluation bias caused by baseline differences.

Study design

This study is a single-center prospective study, and the research process is as follows: In January 2020, the research protocol design and ethical approval were initiated. In March 2020, patients meeting the criteria were prospectively enrolled. Baseline data (including clinical indicators, exosome metabolomic data, PD-1/PD-L1 expression levels) of the enrolled patients were collected, and dynamic follow-up was conducted until December 2023 to record the efficacy evaluation results of patients 12 weeks after receiving immunotherapy. During the data collection process, real-time data entry into the database was adopted to avoid the bias of retrospective data. In January 2024, statistical analysis of all the prospectively collected complete data was started to ensure that the study design met the core characteristics of a prospective study (clearly defining the research purpose and design protocol before the study, prospectively collecting data, and following up on outcome events).

Data collection

The following information of patients was collected through the hospital’s electronic medical record system and laboratory information management system:

General information: Age, gender, body mass index (BMI), smoking history (yes/no), drinking history (yes/no), family history of liver cancer (yes/no).

Clinical indicators: Tumor diameter, number of tumors, Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC, a liver cancer staging system based on tumor size, number, liver function reserve, and general condition, used to guide treatment strategy selection, where stage C is defined as advanced liver cancer with features such as portal vein invasion or extra-hepatic metastasis) staging, portal vein tumor thrombus (yes/no), extra-hepatic metastasis (yes/no), liver function Child-Pugh classification (Child-Pugh), previous treatment history (such as surgery, interventional therapy).

Laboratory test indicators: AFP, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), liver function indicators (alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, total bilirubin, albumin).

Exosome metabolomic data: A 5-mL peripheral blood sample was collected from patients before treatment, and exosomes were isolated by differential ultra-centrifugation: at 4 °C, cells were removed by centrifugation at 300×g for 10 min, cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 2000×g for 20 min, microvesicles were removed by centrifugation at 10,000×g for 30 min, and finally, exosome pellets were obtained by ultra-centrifugation at 110,000×g for 70 min, resuspended in PBS, and purified by filtration through a 0.22-µm filter membrane. The isolated exosomes were subjected to quality control and characterization: (1) Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) showed that the particle size was mainly distributed at 30–150 nm, with an average concentration of (1.25 ± 0.32)×10¹⁰ particles/mL; (2) Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed typical cup-shaped exosome morphology; (3) Western blot detection of exosome-specific markers (CD63, CD81, TSG101) showed high expression of the three markers, confirming the high purity of the exosomes. Strict quality control specifications were implemented throughout the metabolomics detection: experimental samples were assigned to detection batches by random principle, and 3 blank controls, 3 quality control samples (mixing all patients’ exosome samples) and 2 standard samples were set in each batch to monitor and correct system drift during the detection process; missing value handling was completed by K-nearest neighbor algorithm (KNN), metabolites with missing value ratio < 5% were retained, and those with > 5% were excluded.The metabolites in exosomes were detected by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) with the following specific parameters: the chromatographic column was a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm); mobile phase A was ultrapure water containing 0.1% formic acid, and mobile phase B was acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid; the gradient elution program was as follows: 0–2 min, 5% B; 2–15 min, 5%-95% B; 15–18 min, 95% B; 18–19 min, 95%-5% B; 19–22 min, 5% B; the flow rate was 0.3 mL/min, the column temperature was 40 °C, and the injection volume was 5 µL. The mass spectrometer was a Thermo Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer in positive ion scanning mode, with a scanning range of m/z 70-1050, a resolution of 70,000, and collision energies of 20/40/60 eV for alternating acquisition. Metabolites were identified by comparing the exact mass numbers, retention times, and secondary fragment information in the Metlin and HMDB databases, and quantitative analysis was normalized by the internal standard method (sphingosine-1-phosphate-d7, glutamine-¹³C5, etc.) to ensure the reproducibility and accuracy of the detection results. Metabolites were identified by comparing the exact mass numbers, retention times, and secondary fragment information in the Metlin and HMDB databases, and quantitative analysis was performed using the internal standard method (sphingosine-1-phosphate-d7, glutamine-13C5, etc.). The detected metabolites included sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P), arachidonic acid (AA), glutamine (Gln), and other metabolites, covering multiple categories such as lipids, amino acids, and nucleotides. The reasons for selecting S1P, AA, and Gln for in-depth analysis were as follows: (1) In the initial untargeted LC-MS analysis, 18 differentially expressed metabolites (P < 0.001, fold change > 1.5) were identified between the treatment-effective group and the ineffective group, and these three metabolites had the most significant differential expression; (2) Previous studies have confirmed their close involvement in tumor immune regulation and immunotherapy resistance (e.g., S1P regulates T-cell migration and induces macrophage M2 polarization, and Gln affects T-cell metabolic activation and proliferation); (3) They are stably present in liver cancer exosomes with the highest detection reproducibility (coefficient of variation < 5%), while other differential metabolites have poor detection stability (coefficient of variation > 15%) or unclear biological correlation with liver cancer immunotherapy, thus being excluded after rigorous screening.

PD-1/PD-L1 expression levels: The expression levels of PD-1/PD-L1 in tumor tissues and tumor-infiltrating immune cells were detected by immunohistochemistry. The specific process was as follows: Paraffin-embedded tumor tissue sections were dewaxed to water, incubated with 3% H₂O₂ at room temperature for 10 min to block endogenous peroxidase, subjected to high-temperature antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 20 min, and blocked with 5% BSA for 30 min. The primary antibodies (PD-L1 antibody purchased from Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. No. 13684, diluted at 1:200; PD-1 antibody purchased from Abcam, Cat. No. ab237724, diluted at 1:150) were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the HRP-labeled secondary antibody (Beijing Zhongshan Jinqiao, Cat. No. PV-9000) was added and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. DAB staining was performed, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining, dehydration, clearing, and mounting. The scoring standard was a comprehensive scoring method: the proportion of positive cells (0 points: 0%; 1 point: 1%-25%; 2 points: 26%-50%; 3 points: 51%-75%; 4 points: 76%-100%) was multiplied by the staining intensity (0 points: no staining; 1 point: weak staining; 2 points: moderate staining; 3 points: strong staining), with a total score of 0–12 points. PD-1 expression was defined as positive when the comprehensive score of tumor cells or tumor-infiltrating immune cells was ≥ 3 points (corresponding to a proportion of positive cells ≥ 1% and staining intensity ≥ 1+). PD-L1 expression was defined as positive when the comprehensive score of tumor cells or tumor-infiltrating immune cells was ≥ 3 points (corresponding to a proportion of positive cells ≥ 1% and staining intensity ≥ 1+). The tumor tissue used for PD‑L1 IHC was obtained from diagnostic biopsy or surgical resection performed within 4 weeks prior to the start of immunotherapy (median interval 18 days, IQR 10–25 days). Exosome blood samples were collected on the day of the first immunotherapy dose (within 1 day before treatment). Thus, the tissue‑blood collection interval was consistently < 4 weeks in our study. This threshold was determined based on the ROC curve optimal cutoff value of the study (Youden index = 0.628, sensitivity = 72.5%, specificity = 90.3%), and is consistent with the latest combined scoring standards for PD-L1 detection in liver cancer immunotherapy research (e.g., KEYNOTE-224, CheckMate 040). In contrast, the tumor proportion score (TPS) ≥ 1% standard for NSCLC only counts the positive rate of tumor cells, and its AUC for predicting immunotherapy efficacy in this study is only 0.582, with poor predictive value.

Efficacy evaluation criteria

All included patients received a unified immunotherapy regimen: PD-1 inhibitor (pembrolizumab, 200 mg per dose, intravenous infusion every 3 weeks) or PD-L1 inhibitor (atezolizumab, 1200 mg per dose, intravenous infusion every 3 weeks), either as monotherapy or in combination with lenvatinib (8 mg/day for patients with body weight < 60 kg or 12 mg/day for patients with body weight ≥ 60 kg) orally. The treatment regimen was uniformly determined based on the patient’s physical condition and clinical decisions.

Efficacy was evaluated according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors: Complete response (CR): All target lesions disappeared, no new lesions appeared, and tumor markers were normal, which was maintained for more than 4 weeks. Partial response (PR): The sum of the diameters of target lesions decreased by ≥ 30%, which was maintained for more than 4 weeks. Stable disease (SD): The sum of the diameters of target lesions decreased but did not reach the criteria for PR, or increased but did not reach the criteria for progression. Progressive disease (PD): The sum of the diameters of target lesions increased by ≥ 20%, or new lesions appeared.

CR and PR were defined as treatment-effective, and SD and PD were defined as treatment-ineffective. In this study, the efficacy evaluation results of patients 12 weeks after receiving immunotherapy were used as the outcome event (effective = 1, ineffective = 0). The reasons for selecting 12 weeks as the evaluation time point were as follows: (1) It is in line with clinical practice: Currently, the efficacy evaluation of advanced liver cancer immunotherapy mostly refers to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST 1.1), which clearly recommends performing imaging evaluation every 6–8 weeks. Twelve weeks is a time point between two regular evaluations and can stably reflect the early treatment response trend. (2) It meets the need for early prediction: Although there are individual differences in the onset of immunotherapy, most clinical studies have shown that obvious efficacy differences can be observed around 12 weeks after treatment, which can meet the research purpose of “early prediction”. (3) It balances timeliness and accuracy: Evaluating too early (e.g., at 6 weeks) may lead to false-positives due to immune-related inflammatory reactions, while evaluating too late (e.g., at 24 weeks) may delay the timing of treatment adjustment. Twelve weeks has been proven to be the optimal time point to balance the two.

Sample size estimation

Sample size estimation was performed prior to patient enrollment to ensure the stability and generalizability of the prediction model. The calculation was based on the following parameters: Primary outcome: Efficacy of immunotherapy (binary: effective/ineffective). Based on preliminary data, the expected response rate (effective cases) was approximately 30%. Number of predictors: 6 independent factors identified in preliminary analysis (Child-Pugh classification, extra-hepatic metastasis, NLR, S1P, Gln, PD-L1 expression). Statistical requirements: For logistic regression models, a minimum of 10 events per predictor is recommended to avoid overfitting. With 6 predictors, the required number of ‘effective’ events was 60. Power and significance: Assuming a two-sided α of 0.05 and power (1-β) of 80%, the minimum sample size was calculated using PASS 15 software. Considering a 30% response rate, the estimated minimum sample size was 200 (60 events / 0.3 response rate). Test set requirement: To ensure the test set had sufficient power, a 7:3 split (training: validation) was adopted. Thus, the total sample size needed to be expanded to 700 (including a 10% dropout rate). Finally, 824 patients were enrolled, exceeding the estimated minimum, which guaranteed adequate statistical power for model development and validation.

Statistical analysis

Data processing was performed using SPSS 26.0 software. Count data were expressed as the number of cases (%), and the chi-square test was used for comparisons between groups. Before analysis, missing data were assessed. Each clinical variable had < 2% missing values (e.g., AFP 1.2%, NLR 0.8%). For clinical indicators, multivariate imputation by chained equations with 5 iterations was performed. For exosomal metabolomics, metabolites with > 5% missing values were excluded, and remaining missing values were imputed using K‑nearest neighbors (k = 5). After imputation, all 824 patients had complete data. Measurement data conforming to the normal distribution were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation, and the t-test was used. For data not conforming to the normal distribution, they were expressed as the median (inter-quartile range). The Mann-Whitney U test was used for comparisons between two groups, and the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for comparisons among multiple groups. Indicators with P < 0.05 were screened through univariate analysis and included in the multivariate Logistic regression analysis. Before the analysis, the variance inflation factor (VIF) was used for collinearity diagnosis. The results showed that the VIF values of all independent variables were < 5, indicating no obvious collinearity problem. Finally, independent predictive factors (P < 0.05) were determined.

To assess the potential impact of temporal mismatch between tissue acquisition and blood sampling, a sensitivity analysis was performed. The time interval (in days) between PD‑L1 IHC tissue collection and exosome blood sampling was added as an additional covariate in the multivariate logistic regression model. Its association with immunotherapy efficacy was evaluated, and the change in the random forest model’s AUC after including this interval was compared using the Delong test.

Three multi-omics fusion models were constructed using Python 3.9:

Logistic regression fusion model: The screened exosome metabolites and PD-1/PD-L1 expression levels were weighted and fused through a logistic regression equation.

The data were divided into a training set (70%) and a validation set (30%). For the random forest fusion model, grid search combined with 5-fold cross-validation was used to optimize its core hyperparameters, including n_estimators, max_depth, min_samples_split, and min_samples_leaf. The search ranges and optimal values were as follows: n_estimators (50–500, step 50) → 300, max_depth (5–30, step 5) → 15, min_samples_split (2–10, step 2) → 4, min_samples_leaf (1–5, step 1) → 2. Based on the optimized parameters, three multi-omics fusion models—random forest, logistic regression, and deep learning—were constructed using Python 3.9 and relevant toolkits (sklearn, tensorflow).

Deep learning fusion model: A three-layer neural network was constructed based on the characteristics of clinical small sample dataset and the principle of simplicity to avoid overfitting. The input layer contained 12 neurons (6 predictive factors were subjected to feature expansion to 12 dimensions), the hidden layers contained 24 and 16 neurons respectively (2–4 times the number of input layer neurons), and the output layer contained 1 neuron (for binary classification output). The activation functions were ReLU (for the hidden layer) and Sigmoid (for the output layer). The optimizer was Adam (learning rate 0.001), the loss function was binary cross-entropy, the number of iterations was 50, and the batch size was 32. To prevent overfitting to the small dataset, we adopted multiple targeted measures: L2 regularization (weight decay coefficient = 0.001) was added to the hidden layer, early stopping criterion (patience = 10) was set in the training process, and all continuous variables were standardized and Z-score normalized before training.

For feature selection, the recursive feature elimination (RFE) method was applied using the random forest model as the base classifier, with one feature removed per iteration. Model performance was evaluated by 5-fold cross‑validation, and the optimal feature combination was determined based on the change in accuracy. This process identified 6 predictive factors that contributed most to the model performance. Separately, multivariate logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors and estimate odds ratios for clinical interpretation. The five-fold cross-validation was used to optimize the model. The accuracy, precision, recall, F1 value, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) were calculated to evaluate the performance. The clinical net benefit of the model was analyzed through decision curve analysis (DCA, a method for evaluating the clinical practicability of a prediction model, which determines whether the model can provide additional value for clinical decision-making by calculating the net benefit at different threshold probabilities). The calibration curve was used to verify the consistency between the predicted probability and the actual results through the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (P > 0.05 indicates good consistency). GraphPad Prism 9 was used to draw the ROC curve, DCA curve, and calibration curve.

Construction of the prediction model for the efficacy of immunotherapy in patients with advanced hepatocellular carcinoma

Six independent predictive factors (Child-Pugh classification, extra-hepatic metastasis, NLR, S1P, Gln, PD-L1 expression level) were screened by univariate and multivariate Logistic regression. Three multi-omics fusion models (logistic regression, random forest, deep learning) were constructed based on Python 3.9 software (sklearn, tensorflow toolkits). The core hyperparameters of the random forest model were optimized by grid search + 5-fold cross-validation, with the optimal parameters as follows: n_estimators = 300, max_depth = 15, min_samples_split = 4, min_samples_leaf = 2. Since the three models could not be visualized, the feature importance of the risk factors in the optimal model, the random forest fusion model, was visualized. The results were as follows: Child-Pugh (0.010, 95% CI: 0.006–0.014, P = 0.021), extra-hepatic metastasis (0.035, 95% CI: 0.028–0.042, P < 0.001), NLR (0.029, 95% CI: 0.022–0.036, P < 0.001), S1P (0.018, 95% CI: 0.012–0.024, P = 0.003), Gln (0.028, 95% CI: 0.021–0.035, P < 0.001), PD-L1 expression level (0.039, 95% CI: 0.031–0.047, P < 0.001).

Prediction performance of the multi-omics fusion model

The prediction performance indicators of the three multi-omics fusion models in the training set and test set are shown in Tables 5 and 6, and Fig. 1. The random forest fusion model had the optimal performance, with an AUC of 0.757 in the training set and an AUC of 0.751 in the test set, which were significantly higher than those of the logistic regression fusion model and the deep learning fusion model. We used the Delong test combined with Bonferroni correction to conduct pairwise statistical comparison of the AUC values of the three models. The results showed that in the training set, the AUC of the random forest fusion model was significantly higher than that of the logistic regression fusion model (P = 0.023) and the deep learning fusion model (P = 0.008); in the test set, the AUC of the random forest fusion model was also significantly higher than that of the logistic regression fusion model (P = 0.029) and the deep learning fusion model (P = 0.006) (all P < 0.05 after Bonferroni correction). We further conducted a head-to-head comparison of the prediction performance of the multi-omics fusion model with single biomarkers (PD-L1 alone, AFP alone) and the clinical indicator-only combination model (Child-Pugh + NLR + extra-hepatic metastasis), and quantified the incremental value after adding exosomal metabolites (S1P, Gln) and PD-L1. The results showed that the AUC of PD-L1 alone was 0.612 (training set) and 0.605 (validation set), the AUC of AFP alone was 0.587 (training set) and 0.579 (validation set), and the AUC of the clinical indicator-only combination model was 0.628 (training set) and 0.621 (validation set). The ΔAUC of the multi-omics fusion model in this study relative to the clinical indicator-only combination model reached 0.129 (training set) and 0.130 (validation set), and the Delong test confirmed that the differences were statistically significant (all P < 0.001). The Brier score of the model decreased from 0.182 of the clinical indicator-only model to 0.145, indicating a significant improvement in prediction accuracy. In addition, within the clinically meaningful threshold probability range of 0.10 ~ 0.85, the clinical net benefit of the multi-omics fusion model was significantly higher than that of the clinical indicator-only combination model, further confirming the incremental value of adding exosomal metabolites and PD-L1.

Tumor specificity verification of exosomal S1P/Gln

To verify the tumor specificity of exosomal S1P/Gln, we randomly selected 50 patients and detected the S1P/Gln levels in their tumor tissue, paired peripheral blood exosomes and plasma, and also detected the exosomal S1P/Gln levels in 30 healthy volunteers and 30 patients with chronic hepatitis B (non-tumor). The results showed that the levels of S1P and Gln in tumor tissue were significantly higher than those in plasma (all P < 0.001), and the exosomal S1P/Gln levels were highly positively correlated with tumor tissue levels (S1P: r = 0.82, P < 0.001; Gln: r = 0.78, P < 0.001), while there was no significant correlation between plasma levels and tumor tissue levels (P > 0.05). In addition, the exosomal S1P/Gln levels in advanced liver cancer patients were significantly higher than those in healthy volunteers and chronic hepatitis B patients (all P < 0.001). These results confirmed that exosomal S1P/Gln was mainly derived from tumor tissue and had good tumor specificity.

Discussion

In this study, a multi-omics fusion prediction model for the efficacy of immunotherapy in advanced liver cancer was constructed by integrating exosome metabolomics, PD-1/PD-L1 expression levels, and clinical indicators. The results showed that Child-Pugh classification, extra-hepatic metastasis, NLR, S1P, Gln, and PD-L1 expression levels were independent predictors of immunotherapy efficacy (P < 0.05). The random forest fusion model constructed based on the above indicators had the optimal performance. The areas under the receiver operating characteristic curves of the training set and the test set were 0.757 and 0.751, respectively, and its precision, accuracy, recall, and F1 value were significantly better than those of the logistic regression fusion model and the deep learning fusion model. This result confirmed that the multi-omics fusion model could effectively predict the efficacy of immunotherapy in advanced liver cancer patients at an early stage, providing a reliable basis for clinical individualized treatment decisions.

The advantage of the multi-omics fusion model lies in its ability to integrate biological information from different dimensions, achieving a “1 + 1 > 2” improvement in prediction efficacy. In terms of data complementarity, exosome metabolomics (S1P, Gln) reflects the metabolic reprogramming characteristics of tumor cells, PD-L1 expression level reflects the state of immune escape, and clinical indicators (Child-Pugh classification, NLR) reflect the overall condition of the body. The three together constitute a multi-dimensional evaluation system of “metabolism-immunity-clinic”. For example, for patients with positive PD-L1 expression, if they are also accompanied by high S1P and high NLR, their actual immune response rate is significantly lower than that of patients with only positive PD-L1 expression (32.6% vs. 58.1%), suggesting that the combined analysis of metabolic and immune indicators can correct the misjudgment of a single indicator. From the perspective of model algorithm characteristics, the reasons why the random forest model is superior to other models include: (1) It has a stronger ability to capture non-linear relationships and can effectively handle the interaction between metabolites and immune indicators (e.g., S1P affects efficacy by regulating PD-L1 expression); (2) It has outstanding anti-overfitting ability. Through bootstrap sampling and decision tree integration, it reduces the interference of noise in single-omics data (e.g., batch effects in metabolomic detection) on the results; (3) It has higher robustness to confounding variables. It can simultaneously incorporate categorical variables (e.g., extra-hepatic metastasis) and continuous variables (e.g., Gln concentration) and automatically assign reasonable weights, avoiding the limitations of variable distribution assumptions in logistic regression.

A noteworthy observation is that the deep learning fusion model performed worse than the random forest model (training AUC 0.717, test AUC 0.707). The most plausible explanation is the limited sample size, particularly the relatively small number of effective cases. Deep neural networks typically require thousands of samples to avoid overfitting; our model had about 1,200 trainable parameters, giving a parameter‑to‑sample ratio of 4.5:1 for effective cases. Despite L2 regularization and early stopping, the model still showed signs of overfitting (e.g., a larger gap between training and test AUC compared to random forest). For small‑scale clinical datasets, tree‑based ensemble methods remain more robust.

This study found that Child-Pugh classification was an independent predictor of immunotherapy efficacy (P < 0.05), which was highly consistent with clinical cognition. Child-Pugh classification comprehensively evaluates liver function reserve through indicators such as bilirubin, albumin, coagulation function, hepatic encephalopathy, and ascites. The higher the classification (grade B or C), the more severe the hepatocyte damage and the worse the liver synthesis function16. From the perspective of immunotherapy, liver insufficiency may affect efficacy through multiple mechanisms. On the one hand, as an immune-tolerant organ, liver damage may lead to disorders of the immune microenvironment, such as abnormal activation of Kupffer cells and imbalance of cytokine secretion (e.g., increased IL-6, TGF-β), thereby inhibiting the anti-tumor immune response17,18. On the other hand, patients with Child-Pugh B/C often have malnutrition and metabolic disorders, which may reduce the activity and proliferation ability of immune cells (e.g., T cells, NK cells), making it difficult for PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors to effectively activate anti-tumor immunity. In addition, liver insufficiency may also affect drug pharmacokinetics, such as a decrease in the clearance rate of PD-1 inhibitors, increasing the risk of immune-related adverse reactions (e.g., hepatitis) and indirectly reducing treatment compliance. The results of this study further support that the liver function reserve of patients should be preferentially evaluated in immunotherapy decision-making. For patients with Child-Pugh B, treatment options should be carefully selected, and liver-protective treatment may be combined if necessary to improve tolerance.

The presence of extra-hepatic metastasis was a strong predictor of poor immunotherapy efficacy (P < 0.05), which was consistent with the conclusions of multiple clinical studies on immunotherapy for advanced liver cancer. Extra-hepatic metastasis (e.g., lung metastasis, bone metastasis) indicates that tumor cells have stronger invasiveness and immune escape ability. The underlying mechanisms include: tumor cells obtain migratory ability through epithelial-mesenchymal transition and simultaneously highly express immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1 and IDO to escape immune system surveillance; the microenvironment where the metastatic foci are located (e.g., bone marrow, lung interstitium) is usually an immunosuppressive microenvironment, rich in regulatory T cells, M2 macrophages, and fibrotic matrix, which hinders the infiltration of cytotoxic T cells; in addition, patients with extra-hepatic metastasis have a greater tumor burden, which may induce immune exhaustion-long-term antigen stimulation leads to high expression of inhibitory receptors such as PD-1 and Tim-3 on the surface of T cells, making it difficult to reverse their function even with the use of PD-1 inhibitors. From the perspective of clinical practice, the objective response rate (ORR) of immunotherapy in patients with extra-hepatic metastasis is usually 30%-40% lower than that in patients without metastasis. The results of this study further confirmed that the extra-hepatic metastasis status can be used as an important indicator for screening potential beneficiaries of immunotherapy. For patients with extra-hepatic metastasis, local treatment (e.g., radiotherapy, radiofrequency ablation) or anti-angiogenic drugs (e.g., lenvatinib) may need to be combined to enhance the immune response.

NLR, as a simple indicator reflecting the systemic inflammatory state, was confirmed as an independent predictor of immunotherapy efficacy in this study (P < 0.05). An elevated NLR (usually defined as > 3) indicates an increase in neutrophils or a decrease in lymphocytes, which together disrupt the immune balance. Neutrophils can directly inhibit T cell activity and promote tumor invasion by releasing neutrophil extracellular traps, matrix metalloproteinases, and other substances, while a decrease in lymphocytes (especially CD8⁺ T cells) weakens the core effect of anti-tumor immunity, making it impossible for PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors to effectively activate the immune response. Mechanistically, patients with advanced liver cancer often have a chronic inflammatory state. Cytokines such as granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) and IL-8 secreted by tumor cells can induce the proliferation of neutrophils and mediate the apoptosis of lymphocytes through immune checkpoint molecules (e.g., PD-L1)19. Clinical studies have shown that the median progression-free survival (PFS) of patients with NLR > 3 treated with PD-1 inhibitors is approximately 50% shorter than that of patients with NLR ≤ 3, which is consistent with the results of this study20,21. It is worth noting that the dynamic change in NLR may be more predictive than the baseline value-a decrease in NLR after treatment often indicates an improvement in the inflammatory state and a more durable immune response, which provides an idea for clinical regulation of NLR through anti-inflammatory treatment (e.g., low-dose glucocorticoids, IL-6 receptor antagonists) to enhance immune efficacy22.

High expression of exosomal S1P, a key molecule in lipid metabolism, was an important predictor of favorable immunotherapy response (P < 0.05). S1P is a multifunctional lipid mediator that regulates cell migration, proliferation, and immune function by binding to S1P receptors (S1PR1-S1PR5) on the cell membrane23,24. In the tumor microenvironment, exosomal S1P inhibits the immune response mainly through the following pathways. On the one hand, exosomal S1P secreted by tumor cells can induce the internalization of S1PR1 on the surface of T cells, hindering the migration of T cells to the tumor site and leading to a decrease in tumor-infiltrating T cells25. On the other hand, S1P can activate macrophages to polarize to the M2 type, promoting the secretion of immunosuppressive factors such as IL-10 and TGF-β, forming an “immune desert”-type microenvironment26,27. Nevertheless, in our patient cohort, higher S1P levels were paradoxically associated with better immunotherapy efficacy. This discrepancy may be explained by the possibility that exosomal S1P, at certain concentrations or in the context of PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, enhances T cell trafficking from lymphoid organs into the tumor bed, thereby counteracting its local immunosuppressive effects. In addition, S1P is also involved in the metabolic reprogramming of tumor cells. By activating the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, it promotes glycolysis and glutamine decomposition, providing energy for tumor proliferation while inhibiting the metabolic adaptation of effector T cells28. This study first confirmed the association between exosomal S1P and the efficacy of liver cancer immunotherapy, suggesting that detecting the baseline exosomal S1P level can assist in screening potential beneficiaries. Further studies are needed to elucidate the precise mechanisms underlying this positive association.

Abnormal levels of exosomal Gln were associated with the efficacy of immunotherapy (P < 0.05), reflecting the central position of amino acid metabolism in tumor-immune interactions. Gln is a key nutrient for both tumor cells and immune cells. Tumor cells decompose Gln by highly expressing GLS, providing substrates (e.g., α-ketoglutarate) for the tricarboxylic acid cycle and synthesizing nucleotides and lipids to support proliferation29. The activation and differentiation of effector T cells also depend on Gln metabolism-a lack of Gln leads to metabolic disorders in T cells, a decrease in the secretion of cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α, and functional exhaustion30. In this study, exosomal Gln levels may affect the efficacy of immunotherapy through two mechanisms. One is that exosomal Gln is a marker of “metabolic hijacking” by tumor cells. Its elevation indicates that tumor cells competitively consume Gln in the microenvironment, resulting in insufficient nutrition for T cells. The other is that exosomal Gln can be taken up by macrophages, promoting their transformation to an immunosuppressive phenotype and further weakening the immune response. In recent years, targeting glutamine metabolism has become a new direction in tumor treatment. For example, the combined use of GLS inhibitors and PD-1 inhibitors can enhance T cell function. The results of this study provide a biomarker basis for patient screening in this combined strategy.

PD-L1 expression level, as a classic predictor of immunotherapy efficacy, was again confirmed as an independent predictor in this study (P < 0.05). The expression of PD-L1 on the surface of tumor cells or tumor-infiltrating immune cells can transmit an immunosuppressive signal by binding to PD-1 on the surface of T cells, leading to T cell exhaustion31. In this study, the immunotherapy response rate of patients with high PD-L1 expression was significantly higher than that of patients with low expression, which was consistent with the conclusions of studies such as KEYNOTE-224 and CheckMate 04032. It is worth noting that there is a “heterogeneity” challenge in the detection of PD-L1 expression-the PD-L1 expression in different regions of tumor tissue and different metastatic foci may vary. This study partially overcame this limitation through the combined detection of exosomes and circulating tumor cells, improving the stability of prediction. The PD-L1 positivity threshold (comprehensive score ≥ 3) in this study is a liver cancer tissue-specific threshold determined based on the study, which is different from the TPS standard of NSCLC because liver cancer is a tumor with abundant immune cell infiltration, and the PD-L1 expression of tumor-infiltrating immune cells is also an important factor affecting the efficacy of immunotherapy. The combined scoring method of tumor cells and immune cells can more comprehensively reflect the immune microenvironment state of liver cancer, and the optimal cutoff value of 3 points is consistent with the existing liver cancer immunotherapy research, which has high rationality and clinical applicability.

This study innovatively integrated exosomal metabolomics (S1P, Gln), immune markers (PD-L1), and clinical indicators (Child-Pugh classification, extra-hepatic metastasis, NLR) to construct a multi-omics fusion model, significantly improving the prediction efficacy. The advantage of this multi-dimensional integration lies in that exosomal metabolites reflect the metabolic phenotype of tumor cells, PD-L1 expression reflects the state of the immune microenvironment, and clinical indicators reflect the overall progression of the disease. The three are complementary and can comprehensively capture the complex factors affecting the efficacy of immunotherapy. For example, for patients with similar PD-L1 expression, combining exosomal S1P and NLR can further distinguish the responsive population, avoiding “false positive” or “false negative” judgments33. Selecting exosomal metabolites as prediction indicators in this study has significant clinical advantages. Exosomes are derived from tumor cells, and their metabolomic characteristics can reflect the real-time biological state of the tumor and are not significantly affected by tumor heterogeneity. Exosomes can be extracted from peripheral blood, avoiding the invasiveness and sampling limitations of tissue biopsy, especially suitable for advanced patients who cannot obtain tumor tissue. Exosomal metabolite detection can be completed before treatment, providing a time window for early prediction. Compared with the traditional method that relies on imaging evaluation 6–8 weeks after treatment, it can guide treatment adjustment earlier. These characteristics make this model easier to promote clinically, especially suitable for primary hospitals or medical scenarios with limited resources. Although the AUC of the multi-omics fusion model is moderate for clinical translation, it has three core clinical advantages that are irreplaceable by traditional biomarkers: first, it is non-invasive, as exosomal metabolites are detected from peripheral blood, avoiding the invasiveness of tumor tissue biopsy and being suitable for advanced liver cancer patients who cannot obtain tissue samples; second, it enables early prediction, as the detection can be completed before immunotherapy, and the prediction result can be obtained 6–8 weeks earlier than the traditional imaging evaluation, providing a time window for clinicians to adjust the treatment plan in advance; third, it has high clinical practicability, as all detection indicators of the model are routine detectable indicators in clinical practice, and the detection method is simple and easy to popularize in primary medical institutions.

This study has the following limitations. (1) Sample selection bias: As a single-center study, all the enrolled patients were from the same region, with 92.3% of the etiology being hepatitis B-related liver cancer, while the proportions of alcohol-related or non-alcoholic fatty liver-related liver cancer were very low. In addition, the usage rate of PD-1 inhibitors combined with lenvatinib in the treatment regimen reached 62.5%, which may be different from the treatment background in other regions or medical centers. Meanwhile, the study excluded patients with autoimmune diseases or prior immunotherapy to reduce confounding factors, which leads to a certain gap between the study and the real-world population, and limits the applicability of the model to real-world advanced liver cancer patients with complicated underlying diseases or multiple lines of treatment. (2) Limited scope of metabolomic detection: This study only focused on S1P, AA and Gln in exosomal metabolomics–selected based on a pilot study showing their significant differential expression in immunotherapy responders–and did not cover other critical metabolites (e.g., lactate, adenosine) or metabolic pathways such as glycolysis and nucleotide metabolism. Although the selected metabolites have well-documented roles in tumor immune regulation and high detection stability, the narrow scope limits a comprehensive understanding of metabolic reprogramming in advanced liver cancer and may lead to the loss of potential predictive information. (3) Lack of external validation: Limited by the research cycle, this study has not completed multi-center external validation, and the generalization ability of the model needs further verification. Notably, the single-center data and hepatitis B-dominant etiology (92.3% of the study) further restrict the model’s applicability to patients from different regions or with other etiologies (e.g., alcohol-related liver cancer, non-alcoholic fatty liver-related liver cancer). The main reasons for not conducting external validation are: (1) During the study period (from January 2020 to December 2023), the data of patients with advanced liver cancer receiving immunotherapy in other cooperative centers were not fully accumulated; (2) There are differences in detection methods (e.g., exosome isolation protocol, PD-L1 detection antibody) and technical parameters (e.g., LC-MS metabolomic detection conditions) among different centers, which may lead to inconsistent results and affect the reliability of external validation. To address this limitation, we plan to conduct multi-center validation in subsequent studies: (1) Expand the sample source to include patients from 3 to 5 cooperative medical centers in different regions, covering various etiologies to improve cohort representativeness; (2) Standardize experimental methods and detection processes across centers to reduce technical differences; (3) Optimize inclusion criteria to include special populations (e.g., patients with mild autoimmune diseases, those with prior immunotherapy) and construct sub-models; (4) Simultaneously perform temporal validation to verify model stability. Failure to consider other factors in the immune microenvironment. This model did not incorporate immune microenvironment-related indicators such as tumor mutation burden, tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte subsets (e.g., CD8⁺/Treg ratio), and gut microbiota composition. These factors have been confirmed to be closely related to the efficacy of immunotherapy and may further improve the prediction accuracy of the model. (4) Although we confirmed that the tissue‑blood collection interval was within 4 weeks and did not significantly affect model performance, the lack of strict standardization of this interval across different clinical settings remains a potential concern. Future multi‑center studies should mandate a uniform time window (e.g., ≤ 4 weeks) and prospectively register the interval as a prespecified covariate. (5) In the present study, the random forest feature importance analysis was conducted on the six variables preselected based on clinical relevance and prior literature. Although this approach does not invalidate our findings, it does not fully exploit the capacity of the random forest algorithm to independently select features from a high‑dimensional dataset. As the reviewer suggested, a more rigorous approach would be to initiate the RFE process from the full pool of candidate variables (including those not statistically significant in univariate analysis), allowing the random forest to complete feature selection independently. Future studies should adopt this strategy to achieve genuine cross‑methodological validation and to avoid any risk of circular reasoning.

In this single‑center preliminary study, the multi‑omics fusion model integrating exosomal metabolomics (S1P, Gln), PD‑L1 expression, and clinical indicators (Child‑Pugh classification, extra-hepatic metastasis, NLR) demonstrated good early predictive efficacy for immunotherapy response in advanced liver cancer, with the random forest model achieving the best performance (training AUC = 0.757, test AUC = 0.751). The primary limitation is the single‑center design with a hepatitis B‑dominant cohort (92.3%), which limits generalizability. Therefore, multi‑center external validation across diverse geographic regions and etiological backgrounds is urgently needed before clinical implementation.
