Quantifying intrafractional colon tumor motion on 1.5 T MR-linac cine-MRI and applying anisotropic residual-motion margins for SBRT
Authors: Guoqing Liu, Min Liu, Li Pan, Bisheng Liu, Na Huang, Yanhua Liu, Gang Niu, Shuanghong Wu, Xianliang Wang, Bo Song, Qian Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo noninvasively quantify the intrafraction motion of unresectable locally advanced colon cancer (UNLACC) via 1.5 T magnetic resonance imaging and linear accelerator (MR-linac) cine-MRI and assess the preliminary feasibility of translating motion envelopes into individualized, anisotropic residual-motion margins for stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) workflow.MethodsThis prospective, single‑centre, observational cross‑sectional study serves as a preliminary test to validate the workflow of a phase I single-arm clinical study and falls under the preliminary feasibility verification phase (Clinical Trials.gov NCT06244537; Initial Release Time: 01/25/2024). We first performed internal validation of a rolling–ball–based motion extraction (RBME) algorithm using a dynamic phantom on the same 1.5 T MR linac, which demonstrated negligible systematic error with a Bland‒Altman mean bias of 0.01 mm (standard deviation 0.49 mm) and 95% limits of agreement of -0.98~0.95 mm. We then prospectively accrued 24 patients for a simulated MR-linac workflow and applied RBME to cine-MRI to generate patient- and axis-specific motion envelopes. Intrafraction motion was analysed in a repeated-measures framework to test axis-by-site effects. Finally, we conducted a planning simulation prescribing 25 Gy in 5 fractions; no patients yet treated according to the proposed strategy. The simulated planning target volume (PTV) is the gross tumor volume (GTV) plus an RBME-derived anisotropic residual-motion margin and an additional 3 mm setup margin.ResultsPhantom testing supported the accuracy of RBME ( ~ ± 1 mm agreement). In patients, the mean excursions peak in the caudal/right/anterior directions and are smallest in the cranial/left/posterior directions, with the average of means centered at approximately 4 ~ 5 mm. Across all lesions, mean root-mean-square (RMS) displacement was 2.64 ± 1.32 mm cranio–caudal, 2.91 ± 1.05 mm anterior–posterior, and 2.75 ± 1.43 mm left–right. The corresponding 95th percentile (P95) excursions were 4.49 ± 2.43 mm, 4.97 ± 1.74 mm, and 4.76 ± 2.62 mm. Axis-by-segment interactions were significant for RMS (χ² 19.93, df 8, P = 0.011) and P95 (χ² 20.44, df 8, P = 0.008), indicating location-dependent anisotropy.ConclusionA phantom-validated RBME algorithm was used to characterise irregular intrafraction motion for UNLACC on a 1.5T MR-linac. The resulting motion envelopes should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and intended to inform further method development and protocol design.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13014-026-02818-9.Keywords: Unresectable locally advanced colon cancer (UNLACC), Magnetic resonance imaging and linear accelerator (MR-linac), Rolling ball-based motion extraction (RBME), Stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT)

Introduction

Magnetic resonance-guided radiotherapy (MRgRT) is well suited for stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) in the abdomen. Compared with cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT)-based image-guided radiotherapy, magnetic resonance imaging and linear accelerator (MR-linac) systems provide superior soft-tissue visualisation and enable real-time cine-MRI during beam delivery, which is essential for intrafraction motion assessment [1, 2]. The principal advantages of MR-linac are real-time cine-MRI and online adaptive radiotherapy, and any improvement in organ-at-risk (OAR) sparing is a direct consequence of these capabilities [3]. These features are particularly valuable in abdominal tumors with high interfractional and intrafractional motion. Cine-MRI allows high-temporal-resolution imaging of intrafraction motion along the cranio-caudal (CC), anterior-posterior (AP), and left-right (LR) axes, increasing tumor target conformality and minimizing motion-induced geometric uncertainty [4–7]. Owing to the hypofractionated nature of SBRT and the prolonged beam-on time per fraction, online tumor motion monitoring facilitates real-time adaptation of target coverage, thus broadening its applicability across abdominal and pelvic tumors [3, 8–10]. Earlier case reports have shown that colon tumors exhibit large intrafraction motion and that MRgRT is a feasible solution [11, 12]. Auto beam gating is optimized for quasiperiodic respiration but responds poorly to peristalsis, filling changes, and baseline drift; unless the gating latency and detection error are explicitly quantified and safeguarded with appropriate safety windows, mistimed beam holds, duty cycle loss, and geometric misses can occur [6, 13]. Therefore, we employed the rolling-ball-based motion extraction (RBME) algorithm to characterize intrafraction colon tumor motion. Using the 1.5T MR-linac adaptive radiotherapy platform, the RBME algorithm was applied to review intrafraction motion before daily treatment. The anisotropic boundaries generated by the RBME algorithm were designed to minimize beam interruption, thereby enhancing intrafraction motion management and addressing interfraction boundary variations.

This preliminary feasibility research aims at workflow validation and motion quantification of unresectable locally advanced colon cancer (UNLACC) on 1.5T MR-linac cine-MRI and to translate the resulting motion envelopes into simulated, individualized anisotropic residual-motion margins for SBRT, with all treatments simulated and no patients yet treated according to the proposed strategy.

Methods

Patients

This prospective, single-centre, observational cross-sectional study serves as a preliminary test to validate the workflow of a phase I single-arm clinical study and falls under the preliminary feasibility verification phase; all participants provided written informed consent (Clinical Trials.gov NCT06244537). We quantify intrafraction motion of colon tumor on cine-MRI and to simulate anisotropic residual-motion margins for SBRT workflow; no therapeutic intervention was delivered. Twenty-four patients with 25 unresectable colon lesions underwent simulation with a 1.5T MR-linac (Elekta AB, Stockholm, Sweden) between April and December 2024. None had received prior systemic therapy, and no anticancer treatment was ongoing. Eligibility required cT4a-b, N1-2, and M0 disease per the American Joint Committee on Cancer Control (AJCC) version 8 and the ability to tolerate 30–45 min of supine positioning; exclusions were nonadenocarcinoma histology and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) contraindications. Lesion location was abstracted from colonoscopy and diagnostic imaging reports and assigned to a colonic segment using prespecified anatomic landmarks. The cecum was defined by proximity to the ileocecal valve. The ascending colon extended from the ileocecal valve to the hepatic flexure, and the transverse colon spanned the hepatic to splenic flexures. The descending colon extended from the splenic flexure to the descending–sigmoid junction, and the sigmoid colon was defined as the segment distal to this junction, approaching the rectosigmoid junction. When a lesion was reported to involve contiguous segments, it was assigned to the documented segment of origin. If the segment of origin was not specified, the lesion was coded as spanning adjacent segments and handled according to the prespecified statistical analysis plan [14]. When colonoscopy and imaging descriptors were discordant, the segment assignment was adjudicated by consensus review of the diagnostic imaging report. This study adheres to the STROBE statement for cross-sectional studies; a completed STROBE checklist is provided as an Additional file. Figure 1 summarizes the enrollment of UNLACC patients.

RBME algorithm

We propose a novel RBME algorithm, which is specifically designed to extract the outermost contour accurately and quantify irregular, noncyclical motion from the created contour polygons in each plane of cine-MRI. RBME uses manually delineated two-dimensional gross tumor volume (GTV) contours sampled across cine frames and analyzes each imaging plane separately. This contour-driven formulation also permits explicit direction-resolved centroid excursions and percentile-based motion envelopes that can be translated into axis-specific residual-motion margins for planning feasibility analyses. In this setting, this contour-based strategy is therefore well-suited for offline or semi-offline motion characterisation in colon tumours.

Contours are first transformed into a centroid-referenced coordinate system to standardise frame-to-frame positioning. A local-density filter then suppresses isolated contour noise and outliers to improve robustness. The external tumour boundary is subsequently reconstructed with a modified rolling-ball boundary-following procedure, with internal cavities handled when present, to ensure consistent boundary identification across frames. Tumour motion is quantified by tracking boundary intercepts along the CC, AP, and LR axes passing through the GTV centroid, thereby generating axis-specific displacement time series. These trajectories are summarised using root-mean-square (RMS) displacement and percentile-based motion envelopes. Full mathematical definitions, implementation details, and parameter definitions are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

RBME algorithm validation in a dynamic phantom

The RBME algorithm was validated on an MR-compatible dynamic phantom (CIRS MRgRT 008Z) [15]. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the insert was programmed for sinusoidal CC motion (50 mm peak-to-peak) with superimposed AP and LR motion (7 mm peak-to-peak) and rolling about the LR axis (Fig. 2a). The 50 mm CC excursion was intentionally selected as a high-amplitude stress test that exceeds the typical intrafraction motion magnitudes observed in our clinical cine-MRI data set, with the goal of challenging the tracking workflow under extreme motion rather than reproducing the cohort average. The rationale was to probe algorithm robustness and potential failure modes over a broad operating range, including larger displacements and correspondingly higher velocities than are commonly encountered clinically. Offline acquisitions on the 1.5T MR-linac system were obtained in three orthogonal cine-MRI planes (Fig. 2b–g). Tumor surrogates were contoured in LabelMe software. Bland–Altman analysis was used for comparison with the controller ground-truth logs. Smaller mean squared error (MSE) values indicate higher fidelity to the reference. Phantom validation was performed for quality control and feasibility verification and was not used to derive the numerical value of the clinical planning margin. We performed a one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis to quantify the impact of key RBME hyperparameters on centroid-aligned, one-sided boundary excursion estimates. Sensitivity results are reported in Supplementary Table S1 and demonstrate limited variation in one-sided excursion estimates across the tested ranges.

Preradiation therapy workflow for the 1.5 T MR-linac

The workflow is illustrated in Fig. 3. Patients were supine with shallow breathing; abdominal compression was used when tolerated [16]. The GTV encompassed the thickened bowel wall (≥ 4 mm on cross-sectional imaging) and any immediately contiguous invasion, edited to anatomic boundaries [17]. Three orthogonal planes in cine-MRI (≥ 50 frames per plane) were acquired, and motion-extreme frames were contoured by Lableme software [18]. Cine-MRI acquired on a 1.5T MR linac was exported in vendor format and converted to frame sequences with the original pixel spacing and frame rate preserved (matrix 1108 × 1108). Real-time 2D cine MRI was acquired at 5 frames per second for intrafraction motion monitoring. The images were deidentified and encoded as 8-bit PNG for manual review. To sample the full motion excursion, frames were algorithmically prescreened for displacement extrema via boundary-trajectory features and then confirmed by a gastrointestinal radiologist. The online MRI-defined GTV served as the reference. The radiation oncologist utilized the GTV defined in the online MRI as a reference. Two radiation oncologists independently delineated tumor boundaries on each selected frame via LabelMe software. We targeted ≥ 50 annotated frames per plane (axial, coronal, sagittal) per patient to ensure phase coverage. Discrepancies most commonly arose in frames with limited soft-tissue contrast at the tumour interface and typically involved boundary adjustments within 1–2 pixels; these were resolved during the consensus review. Most discrepancies were minor boundary edits at motion extremes, with no systematic directional bias observed. The final contours were exported in a machine-readable annotation format (JavaScript Object Notation, JSON) and provided as structured input to the RBME algorithm to derive patient-specific motion envelopes. Before each intrafraction, cine-MRI would be reacquired to re-verify intrafraction motion and to update the RBME-derived motion envelope and corresponding margins as needed; this study did not evaluate whether a single baseline acquisition can reliably predict motion behaviour in subsequent fractions.

Preradiotherapy workflow for UNLACC on a 1.5-T MR-linac

The planning target volume (PTV) was defined as the geometric union of the GTV, an anisotropic residual-motion margin equal to the RBME-derived motion envelope from retrospective frame-level cine-MRI analysis, and a uniform 3 mm setup margin based on institutional practice [19], and no radiotherapy was delivered. The RBME envelope characterizes residual intrafraction displacement, which is used to establish reference bounds for cine-MRI-based monitoring and automatic beam control during beam-on.

A medical physicist subsequently developed a radiotherapy treatment plan. The prescription dose for the GTV was 25 Gy delivered in 5 fractions, designed as a conversion therapy to downstage the tumor. This regimen aims to facilitate resection surgery or further therapies. The prescribed dose is based on the SBRT protocol for locally advanced rectal cancer, while fully considering the dose limits for gastrointestinal OARs [20]. PTV prescription dose coverage was guaranteed to reach at least 95%. When dose delivery to the PTV conflicted with protection for adjacent normal organs, priority was crucially given to safeguarding the surrounding normal tissues, as detailed in Table 1. Specific OAR dose constraints followed AAPM Task Group 101 and UK consensus guidelines [21, 22]. Adjustments to irradiation volumes and anatomical structures were made on the basis of patient-specific treatment sites. Dose‒volume histogram (DVH) analysis was employed to evaluate the treatment plan, incorporating the following indices. A medical physicist developed the treatment plan by optimizing the total number of monitor units, segments, beam irregularity, and beam modulation. The plan, developed via simulation computed tomography (CT), employs 7 MV flattening-filter-free intensity-modulated radiation therapy. The following indices were used in this study. The conformity index describes the degree to which the prescribed isodose volume conforms to the shape and size of the target volume. The heterogeneity index describes the uniformity of the dose within a target volume and is directly calculated from the statistics of the DVH.

Statistical analysis

Phantom validation included Bland–Altman analysis of translational error and measurement of centroid tracking fidelity as the MSE of tracked centroids. Patient intrafraction motion was quantified using two prespecified endpoints, RMS displacement and 95th-percentile (P95) displacement, calculated along the CC, AP, and LR axes, with motion magnitudes (mean ± SD) for each segment and axis calculated per lesion (two lesions in one patient analysed separately). Segment and axis effects on motion were examined with a linear mixed-effects model including axis, segment, and their interaction as fixed effects, and a random intercept for patient (no additional covariates). Model residuals met normality assumptions. The axis-by-segment interaction was the primary test; all other comparisons were exploratory and adjusted by the Holm method. Results were interpreted with caution given the small sample. Standardised effect sizes were calculated (Hedges’ g for between-segment and Cohen’s d_z for within-lesion axis differences). The analyses used Python 3.9.21 (Amsterdam, The Netherlands). Dosimetric feasibility was evaluated descriptively using lesion-level DVH metrics for targets and organs at risk.

Results

Phantom validation

A dynamic phantom experiment confirmed the accuracy of the RBME algorithm. As shown in Fig. 4a-c, the RBME algorithm-extracted motion closely matched the phantom’s programmed motion in all axes. Phantom experiments demonstrated that (Table 2A) RBME centroid-tracking error varied with both imaging plane and phantom position. In the axial plane, MSE values were low and relatively consistent, with 0.06 at the top and bottom positions and 0.17 at the left and right positions, yielding a mean of 0.12 (SD 0.06) and a range of 0.06–0.17. In the coronal plane, MSE values spanned a wider range from 0.04 to 0.60, with a mean of 0.30 (SD 0.29). The sagittal plane showed the largest errors overall, with a mean MSE of 0.57 (SD 0.52) and values ranging from 0.11 to 1.08; errors at the top and bottom positions (1.08 and 0.96) exceeded those at the left and right positions (0.11 and 0.14).Translational validation (Table 2B) showed close agreement between programmed inputs and RBME outputs, RBME measured corresponding peak-to-peak amplitudes of 50.55 mm, 6.08 mm, and 7.30 mm, yielding errors of 0.55 mm, − 0.92 mm, and 0.30 mm, respectively. Bland–Altman analysis was performed on the one-sided boundary excursions after centroid alignment. The bias was 0.28 mm in the cranio–caudal direction with a standard deviation of 0.08 mm and 95% limits of agreement from 0.12 to 0.43 mm. The anteroposterior direction showed a bias of − 0.46 mm with a standard deviation of 0.25 mm and limits of agreement from − 0.96 to 0.04 mm. The left–right direction showed a bias of 0.15 mm with a standard deviation of 0.72 mm and limits of agreement from − 1.26 to 1.56 mm. Across all directions, the overall mean one-sided error was − 0.01 mm with a standard deviation of 0.49 mm and limits of agreement from − 0.98 to 0.95 mm.

Tumor motion envelopes and anisotropic margins

UNLACC cohort lesions arose in multiple colonic segments, allowing segment-level evaluation of intrafraction motion and formal statistical testing of segment-dependent differences. Motion amplitude varied as a function of both motion axis and colonic segment, as summarised in Table 4. Across all lesions, the overall mean ± standard deviation of RMS displacement was 2.64 ± 1.32 mm in the cranio–caudal direction, 2.91 ± 1.05 mm in the anterior–posterior direction, and 2.75 ± 1.43 mm in the left–right direction. The corresponding P95 values were 4.49 ± 2.43 mm cranio–caudal, 4.97 ± 1.74 mm anterior–posterior, and 4.76 ± 2.62 mm left–right, indicating that high-percentile excursions consistently exceeded mean RMS motion across all axes.

The full set of prespecified pairwise comparisons is provided in Supplementary Materials Table S2. For each contrast, Table S2 reports the estimated difference in displacement in millimetres, the associated standard error, the Z statistic, and both nominal and Holm-adjusted P values within each predefined family of tests. Standardised effect sizes are reported as Cohen’s d_z or Hedges’ g, depending on the contrast structure. After adjustment for multiple testing, most contrasts did not retain statistical significance, reflected by Holm-adjusted P values frequently equal to 1.0000 and the corresponding significance indicator marked as false. Only a limited subset of contrasts retained significance after Holm adjustment, with the significance flag marked as true and accompanied by comparatively larger standardised effect sizes. Prespecified post hoc contrasts were then evaluated with multiplicity control using Holm adjustment, as reported in Table S2. The most reproducible between-segment signal was observed in the cranio–caudal component. Cranio–caudal motion was greater for the cecum than for the descending colon for both endpoints, with an RMS difference of 2.62 mm and a Holm-adjusted P value of 0.0010, and a P95 difference of 4.38 mm with a Holm-adjusted P value of 0.0016. Grouped analyses corroborated this pattern by showing larger proximal-versus-distal cranio–caudal motion for RMS and P95, with differences of 1.30 mm and 2.27 mm, and Holm-adjusted P values of 0.0043 and 0.0034, respectively. In contrast, no within-segment pairwise axis comparison remained statistically significant after multiplicity correction, consistent with the interaction pattern and the limited number of lesions available within each segment for axis-stratified testing.

We proceeded to a feasibility evaluation of motion-informed anisotropic expansion for simulated treatment planning. In a representative case with an ascending colon tumour, Fig. 5a-c shows that the RBME processing of cine imaging generated a lesion-specific motion envelope that enclosed tracked intrafraction positions and defined the directional extent of motion. This envelope was used to construct a motion-informed planning target volume by applying a uniform 3 mm safety margin to the envelope-derived target (Fig. 5d-f). DVH evaluation of the simulated plan showed that doses to adjacent organs at risk, including small bowel, spinal cord, right kidney, and colon, remained within the prespecified allowable limits (Fig. 5g). This evaluation was performed as a planning simulation, and no radiotherapy was delivered.

Figure 6a summarises the adjacent structures most frequently abutted or encroached upon by tumour-related colon motion. The abdominal wall was most commonly involved, accounting for approximately 39% of cases, followed by the liver at about 19% and the small bowel at about 17%. The bladder was involved in roughly 11% of cases, with less frequent proximity to the spleen at about 6% and to the stomach, psoas, and right kidney at about 3% each. From a planning perspective, these findings support prioritising small-bowel sparing, while the high rate of abdominal wall involvement warrants careful attention to skin and subcutaneous dose. Figure 6b shows that across colon segments, this radar plot demonstrates anisotropic intrafractional tumor motion: the mean excursions peak in the caudal/right/anterior directions and are smallest in the cranial/left/posterior directions, with the average of means centered at approximately 4–5 mm, supporting direction-dependent, anisotropic margin design rather than isotropic expansion. A radar chart analysis (Fig. 6c–g) revealed that each colonic segment exhibited a distinct motion signature. The cecum displayed the largest excursion laterally right while showing relatively small caudal and posterior motion. The ascending colon, located near the liver, had the widest range of motion in the cranial and caudal directions, suggesting significant variability among patients in the vertical axis, perhaps due to differences in hepatic flexure mobility. The transverse colon showed broad movement in the cranial and lateral directions but much less motion caudally. In contrast, the motion envelope of the descending colon was most variable in the anterior-posterior axis, yet it was comparatively constrained laterally. The sigmoid colon demonstrated the most limited and uniform motion, with a narrow range in all directions.

Treatment plan

All SBRT plans were generated solely for planning simulation and were not delivered. Prescription-dose coverage of the PTV typically achieved 98–100% in these simulations. Plan-quality indices generally met protocol expectations, and OAR constraints were satisfied or showed only minor deviations. No small-bowel or colonic volume exceeded 32 Gy. Detailed dosimetric data are presented in Table 6. In the sigmoid case with bladder involvement, anisotropic margins led to significant target under-coverage. Specifically, PTV V23.75 was 93.86%, falling 1.14% points below the planning objective of 95%. Interpolation between V22.5 and V23.75 indicated a PTV D95 of approximately 23.33 Gy. Bladder D15cc decreased from 21.3 Gy with isotropic 5 mm margins to 18.3 Gy with anisotropic margins, and D0.5 cc decreased from 25.1 Gy to 24.9 Gy, demonstrating the practical trade-off achieved through margin anisotropy.

Discussion

This exploratory study introduces RBME as a novel boundary-trajectory method. We have demonstrated the feasibility of RBME for quantifying anisotropic tumor motion and informing margin design.

When tumor fixation or invasion into adjacent organs constrains displacement along specific axes, static-image-based recipes risk over- or undercoverage of the moving target [23]. Short-term gastrointestinal motion can create unintended hotspots; quantifying it and integrating findings into SBRT planning is essential to mitigate toxicity [24]. Chiang et al. reported a sigmoid colon cancer case treated on a 0.35T MRIdian system where the GTV shifted 2.58 cm laterally, 1.24 cm cranio-caudally, and 0.40 cm anterior-posterior between fractions. Only with daily online adaptation and gating was the patient able to complete treatment safely; the tumor shrank by 62% without toxicity [11]. Treatment completion required daily online adaptation and gating, highlighting the potential magnitude of positional variability in abdominal targets. In our cohort, we also observed marked directionality, including an extreme caudal excursion of 11.7 mm, while the lateral expansion required for setup remained at the minimum 3 mm. Together, these observations underscore that colonic target motion can be substantial and highly anisotropic, supporting the need for motion management approaches that are responsive to direction-specific variability.

Automated segmentation can expedite target delineation by propagating contours between images, but its accuracy can suffer when image contrast or anatomy changes between sequences [25, 26]. Deformable image registration with deep learning achieves near-real-time target tracking on MR-guided radiotherapy systems, but its accuracy relies on stable image intensities and degrades with rapid deformations or out-of-plane motion [27, 28]. Kernel-based tracking matches small image patches across cine frames to estimate motion and works well as long as the target’s intensity pattern remains distinctive and stable [29]. However, bowel gas, luminal filling, peristalsis, and other nonrigid changes can alter tumor appearance between frames, causing drift or loss of correspondence. While comprehensive motion management (CMM) and other gating implementations on 1.5T MR-linacs increasingly address respiratory periodicity, bowel peristalsis, filling, and baseline drift are irregular and may escape purely respiratory triggers [10]. Recent MR-guided gastrointestinal studies corroborate that peristaltic components are irregular and sometimes comparable in magnitude to breathing motion, underscoring the need for strategies beyond respiratory gating alone [28–30]. Therefore, the RBME approach serves as a complementary tool to CMM.

A prior gastrointestinal MR-guided radiotherapy analysis reported that substantial margin expansion may still be accompanied by increased high-dose exposure to adjacent bowel, illustrating the practical trade-off between target coverage and organ-at-risk sparing [31]. In this context, RBME provides lesion-specific motion envelopes. In the planned Phase I workflow, cine MRI will be acquired prior to each treatment fraction. If more than 5% of the tumour volume lies outside the P95-based anisotropic motion envelope from the previous day, that envelope will be invalidated [32]. A new motion envelope will then be derived from the current day’s cine MRI data using the RBME algorithm. This updated envelope will guide margin adaptation for that day’s treatment.

The research has several limitations. First, this was a single-centre study with a limited sample size and an uneven distribution of lesions across colonic segments, with some segments represented by as few as five lesions. This limits the precision of segment-level estimates and increases the risk of overinterpreting between-segment differences. Any quantitative margin suggestions derived from these data should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in larger, multi-centre cohorts. This feasibility planning study was designed to test the practicality of RBME-informed anisotropic margins and their dosimetric impact on organ-at-risk sparing, not to establish the optimal SBRT dose and fractionation for UNLACC. Robust confirmation of feasibility and clinical utility, therefore, requires multi-centre, prospective trials with predefined endpoints. Secondly, although our workflow may offer pragmatic guidance for centres without CMM, we did not undertake a formal, head-to-head benchmark against CMM solutions. As such, we cannot claim equivalence or anteriority in spatial–temporal fidelity, end-to-end latency, or event detection (false holds/misses). Future work should include phantom and comparisons synchronised to reference traces and machine log-files, assessing margin impact, beam-hold performance, and delivered-dose reconstruction. Our findings should be viewed as hypothesis-generating and operationally informative where CMM is unavailable, pending rigorous external validation before routine adoption. By design, 2D cine acquires fixed planes and is vulnerable to through-plane motion, an issue repeatedly noted in orthogonal-plane MR-linac tracking and CMM evaluations [33, 34]. Finally, future work will formally compare isotropic versus anisotropic margin strategies, with a focus on their impact on OAR dose while maintaining target coverage without increasing the risk of geographic miss.

Conclusion

A phantom-validated RBME algorithm was used to characterise irregular intrafraction motion for UNLACC on a 1.5T MR-linac. The resulting motion envelopes are hypothesis-generating and are intended to inform further methodological development and protocol optimisation. These preliminary findings support prospective evaluation of fraction-specific assessment and adaptive strategies, with additional validation required prior to broader clinical translation.
