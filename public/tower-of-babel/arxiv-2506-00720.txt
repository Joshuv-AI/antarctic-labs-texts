Bi-Level optimization for parameter estimation of differential equations using interpolation
Siddharth Prabhu, Srinivas Rangarajan, Mayuresh Kothare

Abstract
Inverse problem or parameter estimation of ordinary differential equations is a process of obtaining the best parameters using experimental measurements of the states. Single (Multiple)-shooting is a type of sequential optimization method that minimizes the error in the measured and numerically integrated states. However, this requires computing sensitivities i.e. the derivatives of states with respect to the parameters over the numerical integrator, which can get computationally expensive. To address this challenge, many interpolation-based approaches have been proposed to either reduce the computational cost of sensitivity calculations or eliminate their need. In this paper, we use a bi-level optimization framework that leverages interpolation and exploits the structure of the differential equation to solve an inner convex optimization problem. We apply this method to two different problem formulations. First, parameter estimation for differential equations, and delayed differential equations, where the model structure is known but the parameters are unknown. Second, model discovery problems, where both the model structure and parameters are unknown.

1
Introduction

Inverse problem or parameter estimation is a process of obtaining the best parameters of, in this case, ordinary differential equations, given measurements of states. Sequential optimization or single-shooting method formulates this problem as a nonlinear optimization problem
(
Vassiliadis et al. 1994a
;
Vassiliadis et al. 1994b
)
. This optimization problem, with the parameters as the decision variables, minimizes the mean squared error between predicted and measured states. Single-shooting method is difficult to converge for long trajectories, poor initial estimates of the parameters, and highly nonlinear or complex dynamics. Multiple-shooting addresses some of these issues by dividing the trajectory into small intervals, which can be handled in parallel, while adding continuity equations as equality constraints to the optimization problem
(
Bock and Plitt 1984
)
. This comes at the cost of incorporating states as decision variables, thereby increasing the dimensions of the optimization problem. However, because of the dynamic nature of constraints, the Jacobians and the Hessians of the optimization problem can be computed efficiently. Additionally, the sparse structure of the Hessian can be leveraged by sparse linear solvers, to solve the KKT system more efficiently
(
Bock 1983
;
Betts and Huffman 2003
;
Gebremedhin et al. 2005
;
Andersson et al. 2019
)
. On the other hand, condensing
(
Bock and Plitt 1984
;
Bock 1983
)
replaces the updates in the states with updates only in the parameters, consequently reducing the dimensionality of Newton-based optimization problems.

These methods, however, require calculating sensitivities
(
Kidger 2021
)
, which can be computationally expensive. To address this, we use interpolation for parameter estimation as in
(
Bellman and Roth 1971
;
Varah 1982
)
, which is demonstrated only for linearly separable parameters. Interpolation techniques have been used to obtain initial estimates of linearly separable parameters that are sufficiently close to their original value
Calver et al. 2021
, which are then used in shooting-based optimization problems. Interpolation has also been used as a computationally cheaper and more stable alternative to computing sensitivities of ordinary differential equations
(
Vajda et al. 1986
)
. It is also applied in cascading or bi-level optimization frameworks
(
Ramsay et al. 2007
)
, where, at each iteration, the inner problem fits a linear combination of basis functions or an interpolation function based on the parameters at that iteration, while the outer problem minimizes the squared error between experimental and predicted states using the interpolation function. On the other hand, interpolation can also be used to approximate derivatives
(
Biegler 2007
;
Chen and Wu 2008
)
thereby eliminating the need for integration and solving the overall optimization problem over algebraic equations.

Figure 1:
A pictorial representation of the proposed bilevel optimization procedure for parameter estimation of ordinary differential equations

In this paper, we extend the work done in
Prabhu et al. 2025
but for a broader class of ordinary differential equations. We formulate a bi-level optimization problem, where the inner problem solves a convex optimization problem in the linear parameters
(
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
)
, while the outer problem optimizes over the nonlinear parameters. We use the implicit function theorem
(
Dontchev and Rockafellar 2009
;
Griewank and Walther 2008
)
over the KKT conditions of the inner optimization problem to derive the forward-mode gradient of the optimal solution with respect to nonlinear parameters. A pictorial representation of the overall procedure is shown in Figure
1
. We demonstrate the efficacy of this method on two different problem formulations. First, parameter estimation for ordinary differential equations (ODE), and delayed differential equations (DDE), where the model structure is known but the parameters are unknown. Second, model discovery problems, where both the model structure and parameters of ODE are unknown.

2
Method

We extend the problem formulation developed in
Prabhu et al. 2025
to a broader class of parameter estimation of ordinary differential equation problems. As a result, we consider the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{p,\phi}f(p,\phi)=&\ \frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=t_{i}}^{t_{f}}||\hat{X}(t)-\hat{X}(0)-\int_{0}^{t}\Theta(X,p,\phi)dt||^{2}\\
\text{subject to}&\\
&g(p,\phi)=0\\
&h(p,\phi)\leq 0\\
&g^{\prime}(\phi)=0\\
&h^{\prime}(\phi)\leq 0\end{split}

(1)

where
\hat{X}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are the measurements of the states at time
t
,
\Theta(X,p,\phi)
is a function whose parameters
p
and
\phi
need to be estimated. We distinguish between these parameters such that
p
appears linearly, while
\phi
appears nonlinearly in the function
\Theta
. As discussed in
Prabhu et al. 2025
, we approximate
\Theta
using interpolation over measurements,
\Psi(X)(t):\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{n}
, making
\Theta
only a function of
p,\Phi,t
. We can further separate the linear parameters and integrate as follows

\int_{0}^{t}\Theta(X,p,\phi)dt\approx p\int_{0}^{t}\Theta(\Psi(X)(t),\phi)dt

(2)

Furthermore, we assume that the equality constraints,
g\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{e}}
are affine in
p
, and the inequality constraints,
h\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
, are convex in
p

(
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
)
. On the other hand,
g^{\prime}
and
h^{\prime}
can be any nonlinear equality and inequality constraints. Thus, given the value of
\phi
, the optimization problem becomes convex. We leverage these assumptions and solve a bi-level optimization problem where the inner problem is convex in
p
given
\phi
, and the outer problem is non-convex in
\phi
.

2.1
Derivative of inner optimization problem

Since we solve a bi-level optimization problem, we need to find the derivatives across the inner optimization problem. We form the Lagrangian of the inner convex optimization problem as follows

\displaystyle L(p,\lambda,\mu\ |\ \phi)

\displaystyle=f(p|\phi)+\lambda^{T}g(p|\phi)+\mu^{T}h(p|\phi)

(3)

\displaystyle C(p^{*},\lambda^{*},\mu^{*})

\displaystyle=\ \begin{cases}f_{p}(p^{*}|\phi)+\lambda^{*}{}^{T}g_{p}(p^{*}|\phi)+\mu^{*}{}^{T}h_{p}(p^{*}|\phi)=0\quad\text{Stationarity}\\[4.0pt]
g(p^{*}|\phi)=0\quad\text{Primal feasibility}\\[4.0pt]
\text{diag}(\mu^{*})h(p^{*}|\phi)=0\quad\text{complementary Slackness}\end{cases}

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{e}}
and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
are the Lagrange variables of equality and inequality constraints, respectively. The optimal solution that satisfies Equation
3
is given as
(p^{*},\lambda^{*},\mu^{*})
. The derivative of the optimal solution with respect to
\phi
can be efficiently calculated using the implicit function theorem
(
Krantz and Parks 2002
;
Griewank and Walther 2008
)
. We derive equations used in forward-mode derivative calculations as follows

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\phi}C(p^{*}(\phi),\lambda^{*}(\phi),\mu^{*}(\phi))

\displaystyle=0

(4)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\frac{dp^{*}}{d\phi}\\[6.0pt]
\frac{d\lambda^{*}}{d\phi}\\[6.0pt]
\frac{d\mu^{*}}{d\phi}\\[6.0pt]
\end{bmatrix}v

\displaystyle=-\begin{bmatrix}L_{pp}&g_{p}^{T}&h_{p}^{T}\\[3.0pt]
g_{p}&0&0\\[3.0pt]
\text{diag}(\mu)h_{p}&0&\text{diag}(h)\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}L_{p\phi}\\[3.0pt]
g_{\phi}\\[3.0pt]
\text{diag}(\mu^{*})h_{\phi}\end{bmatrix}v

where
v
is the tangent vector used in forward-mode automatic differentiation
(
Griewank and Walther 2008
)
.
(g_{p},h_{p})
and
(g_{\phi},h_{\phi})
are the Jacobian of equality and inequality constraints with
p
and
\phi
, respectively.
L_{pp}
is the Hessian of the Lagrangian with respect to
p
. The factorization of the KKT matrix, or the Hessian of the Lagrangian at the optimal point, can be obtained from the optimization solver and reused in derivative calculations. We provide the necessary steps in case the factorization of the KKT matrix is unavailable.

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}L_{pp}&g_{p}^{T}&h_{p}^{T}\\[3.0pt]
g_{p}&0&0\\[3.0pt]
\text{diag}(\mu)h_{p}&0&\text{diag}(h)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}\\[3.0pt]
w_{2}\\[3.0pt]
w_{3}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}\\[3.0pt]
v_{2}\\[3.0pt]
v_{3}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}L_{p\phi}v\\[3.0pt]
g_{\phi}v\\[3.0pt]
\text{diag}(\mu^{*})h_{\phi}v\end{bmatrix}

(5)

where
v=[v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}]^{T}
is the Jacobian-vector product of the optimality conditions with the tangent vector
v
and
w=[w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}]^{T}
is a vector of appropriate dimensions.

\displaystyle L_{pp}w_{1}+g_{p}^{T}w_{2}+h_{p}^{T}w_{3}

\displaystyle=v_{1}

(6)

\displaystyle g_{p}w_{1}

\displaystyle=v_{2}

\displaystyle w_{3}

\displaystyle=[\text{diag}(h)]^{-1}\left[v_{3}-\text{diag}(\mu)h_{p}w_{1}\right]

Let
H=\text{diag}(h)
and
M=\text{diag}(\mu)
then, substituting for
w_{3}^{T}
gives

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}L_{pp}-h_{p}^{T}MH^{-1}h_{p}&g_{p}^{T}\\[3.0pt]
g_{p}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}\\[3.0pt]
w_{2}\\
\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}-h_{p}^{T}H^{-1}v_{3}\\[3.0pt]
v_{2}\end{bmatrix}

(7)

Let
\hat{L}_{pp}=L_{pp}-h_{p}^{T}MH^{-1}h_{p}
and
\hat{v}_{1}=v_{1}-h_{p}^{T}H^{-1}v_{3}
. Note that for equality constraint optimization problem, we get
\hat{L}_{pp}=L_{pp}
and
\hat{v}_{1}=v_{1}
. We get the remaining vectors of
w
as follows

\displaystyle w_{2}

\displaystyle=\left[g_{p}^{T}\hat{L}_{pp}^{-1}g_{p}\right]^{-1}[-v_{2}+g_{p}\hat{L}_{pp}^{-1}\hat{v}_{1}]

\displaystyle w_{1}

\displaystyle=\hat{L}_{pp}^{-1}\left[\hat{v}_{1}-g_{p}^{T}w_{2}\right]

Finally, we return the sensitivity vector

\frac{d\lambda^{*}}{d\phi}\\[4.0pt]

\end{bmatrix}v

w_{2}\\[3.0pt]
w_{3}\\[3.0pt]

2.2
Derivative of outer optimization problem

We consider the Lagrangian of the inner optimization problem as the objective of the outer optimization problem. We also assume that at the optimal solution of the inner optimization problem, none of the inequality constraints are active, i.e.
h(p^{*}|\phi)\neq 0
and therefore
\mu^{*}=0
. These assumptions make the KKT point regular
(
Gros and Diehl 2020
)
and simplify the computation of the gradient and Hessian of the outer objective with respect to
\phi
, as shown in Equation
10
.

\displaystyle\text{Outer Objective}

\displaystyle=L(p^{*}(\phi),\phi)

(10)

\displaystyle\text{Gradient}

\displaystyle=\cancelto{0}{\frac{\partial L}{\partial p^{*}}}\frac{dp^{*}}{d\phi}+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\phi}

\displaystyle\text{Hessian}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{dp^{*}}{d\phi}\right)^{T}\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial{p^{*}}^{2}}\left(\frac{dp^{*}}{d\phi}\right)+\cancelto{0}{\frac{\partial L}{\partial p^{*}}}\frac{d^{2}p^{*}}{d\phi^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial\phi^{2}}

Since we only require the gradient of
p^{*}
with respect to
\phi
, its computation can be accelerate by storing the decomposition of the
\hat{L}_{pp}
and
g_{p}\hat{L}_{pp}g_{p}
during the forward pass, and reusing them when computing derivatives either using forward-mode (equation
8
) or reverse-mode automatic differentiation. However, storing this decomposition compromises the accuracy of any higher-order derivatives of
p^{*}
with respect to
\phi
. Fortunately, this is acceptable in our case, as we do not need any higher-order derivatives as shown in Equation
10
. An additional advantage of reusing this decomposition, particularly in JAX, is that it makes the forward-mode equations
8
linear in the input tangent space
(
Frostig et al. 2021
)
. As a result, a custom forward rule is sufficient for both forward- and reverse-mode automatic differentiation. This enables faster Hessian computation using the forward-over-reverse approach
(
Griewank and Walther 2008
)
, compared to reverse-over-reverse mode in case when the decomposition is not reused.

2.3
Sensitivities of ordinary differential equations

Computing derivative of
p^{*}
with respect to
\phi
also requires computing sensitivities across the differential equation solver. Using the forward-mode optimize-then-discretize
Kidger 2021
differentiation approach gives

\displaystyle\frac{dX}{dt}

\displaystyle=\Theta(X,p^{*}(\phi),\phi)

(11)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\phi}\frac{dX}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\phi}\Theta(X,p^{*}(\phi),\phi)=\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial X}\frac{dX}{d\phi}+\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial p^{*}}\frac{dp^{*}}{d\phi}+\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial\phi}

\displaystyle\frac{dS}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial X}S+\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial p^{*}}\frac{dp^{*}}{d\phi}+\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial\phi}

However, using interpolation makes the sensitivity calculations cheaper and relatively more stable by preventing sensitivities over unstable trajectories.

\displaystyle\frac{dX}{dt}

\displaystyle=\Theta(\Psi(X)(t),p^{*}(\phi),\phi)

(12)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\phi}\frac{dX}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\phi}\Theta(\Psi(X)(t),p^{*}(\phi),\phi)=\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial p^{*}}\frac{dp^{*}}{d\phi}+\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial\phi}

\displaystyle\frac{dS}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial p^{*}}\frac{dp^{*}}{d\phi}+\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial\phi}

For parameter estimation in delayed differential equations, as discussed in the next section, the sensitivities, using interpolation, are calculated as follows

\displaystyle\frac{dX}{dt}

\displaystyle=\Theta(\Psi(X)(\tau),p^{*}(\phi),\phi)

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\phi}\frac{dX}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\phi}\Theta(\Psi(X)(t),p^{*}(\phi),\phi)

\displaystyle\frac{dS}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial\Psi}\frac{d\Psi}{d\tau}\frac{d\tau}{d\phi}+\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial p^{*}}\frac{dp^{*}}{d\phi}+\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial\phi}

where
\tau=t-\phi
and
\frac{d\Psi}{d\tau}
is the derivative of the interpolation with respect to time. These results hold for reverse-mode and discretize-then-optimize differentiation approaches.

3
Experiments

We demonstrate the efficacy of our method on two different problem formulations. First, parameter estimation, where we estimate the parameters of an ordinary differential equation and a delayed differential equation using state measurements. In this formulation, we assume that the structure of the function
\Theta(X,p,\phi)
is known, while the parameters
p
and
\phi
are unknown. Second, we consider model discovery, where both the parameters and the function
\Theta(X,p,\phi)
are unknown. To make this problem formulation tractable, we assume that the original function can be represented using a set of basis functions selected from a large collection.

The code is made available online at
https://github.com/siddharth-prabhu/BiLevelParameterEstimation
. We use SciPy
(
Virtanen et al. 2020
)
for interpolations, IPOPT
(
Wächter and Biegler 2006
)
for nonlinear optimization, and JAX
(
Bradbury et al. 2018
)
for derivative calculations.

Conclusion

In this work, we developed a bilevel optimization formulation for parameter estimation of ordinary and delayed differential equations. By interpolating state measurements, we exploit convexity in linear parameters and reduce the computational cost of computing sensitivities. The derivatives across the inner optimization problem can be calculated much faster by reusing the decomposition of the KKT matrix from the forward pass. We demonstrate that this formulation successfully identifies optimal parameters in various examples where conventional sequential optimization methods fail. Interpolating noisy measurements—common in real-time data—can lead to inaccurate integral approximations, in which case, smoothing techniques can be applied. Additionally, interpolation based on infrequently measured data can also produce incorrect integrals, in which case conventional sequential or simultaneous methods may be more appropriate.
