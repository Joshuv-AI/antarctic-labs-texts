A Case of Platypnea‐Orthodeoxia Syndrome Caused by Patent Foramen Ovale, Accompanied by Dilated Ascending Aorta, Thoracic Kyphosis and Persistent Eustachian Valve
Authors: Ayaka Kishimoto, Tomohiro Namiki, Yudai Ogata, Hiroya Sunabe, Toshihide Inui, Hiroaki Ishikawa, Hiroko Watanabe, Chihiro Ohta, Ruriko Numata, Naoto Kawamatsu, Tomoko Machino‐Ohtsuka, Tomoya Hoshi, Tohru Sakamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe present a case of platypnea‐orthodeoxia syndrome (POS) associated with patent foramen ovale (PFO). In the upright position, right atrial compression resulting from a dilated ascending aorta and thoracic kyphosis modifies the spatial alignment of the PFO, orienting it toward inferior vena cava (IVC) inflow and increasing its tendency to remain patent. Further, a prominent Eustachian valve redirected blood returning from the IVC toward the PFO and thereby promoting right‐to‐left shunting. The patient subsequently underwent percutaneous transcatheter PFO closure. Even 10 months after the procedure, oxygen saturation measured on room air remained stable at 99% in both supine and upright positions.

Introduction

Platypnea‐orthodeoxia syndrome (POS) is a clinical disorder characterised by dyspnoea and arterial oxygen desaturation upon transition from supine to an upright position. Although the definition of POS varies depending on the literature, it is generally defined as a reduction of more than 4 mmHg in arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) or a decrease of more than 5% in percutaneous oxygen saturation (SpO2) following this positional change [1]. POS results from intra‐ or extracardiac right‐to‐left (R‐L) shunting and is most often associated with patent foramen ovale (PFO). In addition to the presence of PFO, other anatomical and functional factors that facilitate R‐L shunting are necessary for POS to develop. Here, we present a case of POS caused by PFO in conjunction with a dilated horizontal aorta, thoracic kyphosis and persistent Eustachian valve.

Case Report

A 75‐year‐old woman with no history of smoking was admitted to our hospital because of progressively worsening exertional dyspnoea. Her medical history included Sjögren's syndrome, for which she had been receiving prednisolone at 4 mg/day. She also had prior compression fractures involving the 11th thoracic (Th11) and 1st lumbar (L1) vertebrae. On physical examination, she was 138 cm tall, weighed 48 kg and had a body mass index of 25.2 kg/m2. Both breath and heart sounds were normal on auscultation. The neurological examination revealed no abnormalities. Laboratory findings demonstrated normal hepatic and renal functions. Arterial blood gas analysis conducted under room air revealed that PaO2 was 78.5 Torr in the supine position, decreasing to 56.3 Torr in the sitting position and POS was diagnosed.

The electrocardiogram was normal, as were the results of pulmonary function testing. Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the chest showed no abnormalities in the pulmonary fields and no evidence of pulmonary arteriovenous malformations or thromboembolism. In contrast, the ascending aorta was dilated to 44 mm (Figure 1A), and coronal CT demonstrated an increased aortic angulation of 67°, indicating a horizontal configuration of the aorta (Figure 1B). Thoracic kyphosis was also identified, considered secondary to compression fractures of the Th11 and L1 vertebrae (Figure 1A,C). The combined presence of the enlarged horizontal aorta and thoracic kyphosis led to compression of the right atrium.

(B) Coronal‐view CT demonstrates an increased aortic angulation of 67°, indicating a horizontal aorta. The right atrium is compressed by the enlarged aorta. (C) Sagittal‐view magnetic resonance imaging on admission shows compression fractures involving the 11th thoracic (Th) and 1st lumbar (L) vertebrae.

Although transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated normal right atrial pressure (estimated right atrial pressure = 3 mmHg), a microbubble study conducted in the sitting position revealed a substantial R‐L shunt across the interatrial septum within three cardiac cycles. Subsequent transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) in the sitting position identified a pronounced R‐L shunt in association with a flapping mobile atrial septal aneurysm, a dilated PFO measuring 13.4 mm and a prominent Eustachian valve (Figure 2A). The distance between the aortic root and the posterior wall of the atrium was reduced to 22 mm. The PFO seemed to be reoriented toward inferior vena cava (IVC) inflow. The prominent Eustachian valve appeared to channel blood flow from the IVC directly toward the PFO. Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with POS caused by PFO. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain revealed several lesions compatible with lacunar infarctions, suggesting the possibility of paradoxical cerebral embolism.

Flow toward the ultrasound probe is displayed in red, whereas flow away from the probe is displayed in blue. (B) The fluoroscopic image shows percutaneous transcatheter closure of the PFO under TEE guidance. Ao, aorta; LA, left atrium; RA, right atrium.

Given the recommendation for closure in cases of PFO‐associated POS, particularly those with paradoxical cerebral embolism [2], the patient underwent percutaneous transcatheter PFO closure under TEE guidance. The PFO measured 13.4 mm in height and 12.6 mm in tunnel length, with an atrial septal aneurysm exhibiting 16 mm of bidirectional excursion. Given the complex anatomy, a 30‐mm GORE Cardioform Septal Occluder (W. L. Gore & Associates, Tokyo, Japan) was selected for adequate coverage and stable septal apposition (Figure 2B). Immediately after the procedure, the patient's dyspnoea markedly improved, and at 10‐month follow‐up, the SpO2 on room air remained stable at 99% in both sitting and supine positions.

Discussion

The present case demonstrated POS secondary to PFO in the absence of elevated right atrial pressure. PFO is a relatively common finding, occurring in approximately 25% of the adult population [3]. The majority of individuals with PFO are asymptomatic because left atrial pressure is higher than right atrial pressure, resulting in functional closure of the PFO. Only a small proportion of individuals with a PFO develop POS [4], suggesting that additional anatomical or functional factors are required beyond the presence of a PFO alone. Such factors include abnormalities of the ascending aorta, a prominent Eustachian valve, a prominent Chiari network, elevation of the right hemidiaphragm and spinal kyphosis, all of which may promote redirection of blood flow toward the PFO, particularly in the upright position [5]. Notably, an interatrial pressure gradient (right > left) is not necessarily required when preferential redirection of blood flow is the predominant mechanism.

In the present case, ascending aortic dilatation and tortuosity, together with thoracic kyphosis, may have altered atrial geometry. Right atrial compression caused by a dilated aorta reportedly shortens and loosens the atrial septum, increasing its mobility and thereby reorienting the PFO toward the IVC inflow and thus promoting opening of the PFO [6]. A prominent Eustachian valve may have further facilitated R‐L shunting by directing IVC flow toward the PFO, thereby potentially increasing the risk of paradoxical cerebral embolism [7]. The thoracic kyphosis was considered to have resulted from vertebral compression fractures associated with prolonged oral prednisolone therapy and overweight.

Although the present case had a pre‐existing PFO and prominent Eustachian valve, the clinical manifestation of POS occurred only in later life following the development of age‐related secondary factors, including aortic dilatation and spinal deformity. Long‐term mortality remains substantial among patients with POS after PFO closure, with age‐related comorbidities reported as a contributing factor [8]. Thus, the diagnosis of POS may provide an opportunity to identify clinically significant comorbid conditions. In patients with unexplained hypoxemia, particularly elderly patients, it is important to consider the possibility of POS and to carefully monitor for changes in respiratory symptoms or reductions in SpO2 during transition from supine to an upright position.
