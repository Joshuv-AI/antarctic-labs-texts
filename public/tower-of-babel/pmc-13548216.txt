RIGHTS principles for early intervention: right care at the right time for every infant, child and family in low and middle-income countries
Authors: Álvaro Hidalgo-Robles, Daniel Cid-Cofré, María Lucía Mesa-Rubio, Antonio Rizzoli-Córdoba, Anahí Venzor Strader, Helia Molina-Milman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly intervention programmes for infants and young children at developmental risk are implemented inconsistently and inequitably worldwide. Variation in access, surveillance, referral, follow-up and service quality means that children and families may receive very different opportunities depending on where they live, system capacity and resources, laws, policy and local pathways. Practical, evidence-informed guidance is needed to translate ‘what should be done’ into ‘how to implement it’ across diverse settings.MethodsThis conceptual, practice-oriented article used a narrative review and an evidence-informed iterative framework-development process. Sources included global frameworks, clinical guidelines and evidence syntheses, randomised and longitudinal studies relevant to low and middle-income contexts and implementation science frameworks.ResultsWe propose six Reach, Identify early, align Goals, Help families deliver, Track and improve and ensure Sustainability (RIGHTS) principles for early intervention programmes: (1) Reach underserved infants, children and families through proactive, equity-oriented and outreach-capable models; (2) Identify developmental risk early through standardised, feasible pathways with clear timepoints, tools, thresholds and referral circuits; (3) align Goals with family priorities, functioning and everyday routines through shared decision-making; (4) Help families deliver intervention using coaching, routines-based strategies and realistic doses of practice; (5) Track and improve systems through standards, service indicators, family experience measures and continuous learning cycles; and (6) ensure Sustainability by institutionalising governance, financing, workforce capacity and policy alignment for durable impact.ConclusionsEarly intervention programmes should be designed to connect timely identification with family-centred, functionally meaningful and feasible support. Embedding standardisation, equity, caregiver partnership and data-informed improvement into service design may reduce unwarranted variation and strengthen continuity of care. Priority research areas include implementation-effectiveness studies, equity impacts, minimum data sets and the cost-effectiveness of scalable and sustainable early intervention programmes.

Introduction

Developmental delays and disabilities in early childhood are common and unequally distributed. Global estimates indicate that 52.9 million children under 5 years live with developmental disabilities, with 95% living in low and middle-income countries.1 These children are more likely to experience adverse health, educational and social outcomes, risks which can be amplified by poverty, undernutrition, exposure to violence, environmental risks and caregiver mental health.2 International agendas increasingly recognise early childhood development as a human right and a prerequisite for sustainable development.3 Yet, for many families around the world, the first years of life remain a period of missed opportunities.

Early intervention refers to coordinated biopsychosocial supports for families with infants and young children—typically under 6 years of age—who have or are at risk of developmental delays or disabilities, aiming to promote development, functioning and participation, while strengthening caregiver capacity and family well-being. Contemporary early intervention frames health, education and social-care professionals as facilitators who help families access everyday supports, identify learning opportunities within daily routines across home, school and community settings and build strategies that optimise the child’s functional development while sustaining family balance.4 5

However, implementing and sustaining large-scale early intervention programmes is challenging. Workforce capacity—particularly for rehabilitation and developmental services—remains insufficient, and families commonly face geographic, financial, administrative, educational and sociocultural barriers that delay or prevent access, especially in low-resource settings.6 Consistent with global guidance, early intervention can, therefore, be conceptualised as a universal platform that integrates nurturing care and caregiver support across routine contacts from pregnancy through early childhood,7 complemented by targeted and indicated specialist supports. This layered approach reflects the principle of proportionate universalism: support should be available to all children and families, while its scale and intensity increase in proportion to developmental, family and social need.8

Conceptual approach and framework development

The Reach, Identify early, align Goals, Help families deliver, Track and improve and ensure Sustainability (RIGHTS) framework was developed through a narrative review and an evidence-informed, iterative framework-development process to identify implementable principles for strengthening early intervention pathways in low and middle-income countries. Throughout this process, a lens grounded in child rights and equity lens guided the interpretation of evidence and its translation into candidate principles.

A structured, targeted search was conducted between December 2025 and May 2026. Evidence and guidance were drawn from four complementary sources: (1) global frameworks and normative guidance on early childhood development, disability inclusion and quality of care (identified through relevant organisational repositories, including those of the WHO and United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF); (2) clinical guidelines and evidence syntheses on developmental surveillance, postdischarge follow-up of high-risk infants and early detection and intervention (eg, guidelines from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), Cochrane and large meta-analyses); (3) randomised trials and long-term follow-up studies relevant to early intervention and early childhood development in low and middle-income countries and (4) implementation science frameworks that support translation of evidence into feasible systems, including determinants, outcomes, phases and implementation strategies. PubMed, the Cochrane Library, Scopus and Web of Science were the primary databases used to identify sources in categories 2–4.

Within these categories, search terms and source selection were organised around key components of early intervention programmes: access, developmental surveillance, screening, referral and follow-up pathways, family-centred practices, caregiver-delivered or routine-based intervention, programme monitoring and quality improvement and service implementation and sustainability. Priority was given to evidence directly relevant to low and middle-income countries. Evidence from high-income countries was used selectively when it offered potentially transferable approaches. Its applicability to low and middle-income countries was assessed in relation to workforce availability, training requirements, infrastructure, cost, cultural relevance and overall feasibility in resource-constrained settings.

Framework development was iterative and interpretive. Relevant recommendations, programme components, implementation barriers, enabling factors and proposed indicators were extracted and grouped according to their principal function within the early intervention pathway. Through repeated thematic extraction, related concepts were grouped, and overlaps in evidence were combined into principles that were refined as the working group discussed them iteratively. Candidate principles were assessed according to four criteria: relevance to children and families, actionability at programme or system level, applicability across diverse low and middle-income contexts and potential to support implementation and routine evaluation. These considerations provided a common thread for the final principles definition and are reflected in the programme actions and system enablers summarised in table 1.

This process informed the definition of six interdependent RIGHTS principles for early intervention programmes: Reach, Identify early, Goal alignment with families, Help families deliver, Track and improve and Sustainability. The principles were designed to be actionable at programme level, compatible with layered service models, measurable through routine indicators and aligned with equity-based, rights-based and sustainability-based commitments (figure 1).

The RIGHTS principles for early intervention programmes

Principle 1: R (Reach)

Within a universal approach, intentional outreach and additional resources must prioritise reaching children at the highest risk of suboptimal development, including those living in rural settings, migrant, displaced or crisis-affected families, marginalised language, cultural or Indigenous groups, households experiencing poverty, and children with or at risk of developmental disabilities. Equity and inclusion are explicit priorities in global early childhood development and disability-rights agendas, yet children with disabilities remain among the most excluded groups.9

Reach requires programmes to be designed around the social and material conditions that determine whether families can access, use and remain engaged with support. An equity-oriented approach, therefore, prioritises those furthest behind by shifting from passive, clinic-centred models to proactive, outreach-capable services that reduce the practical burdens created by transport and costs, food and housing insecurity, loss of income, caregiving demands, administrative barriers, digital exclusion and limited access to social protection. Early intervention programmes can address these barriers through home-based and community-based services, culturally and linguistically adapted communication and interpretation, partnerships with community health workers, schools and social services, care coordination and navigation support, and telehealth services that demonstrably reduce barriers.10–12 This broader understanding of reach is grounded in the Convention on the Rights of the Child and the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities and reflects the interconnected commitments embodied in the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) related to poverty and social protection (SDG 1), universal access to health services (SDG 3), inclusive early childhood development and education (SDG 4) and reduced inequalities (SDG 10).3 13 14

Examples from Latin America illustrate the layered approach: Chile Crece Contigo combines universal services with additional support for children and families with greater developmental or socioeconomic needs,15 while at the targeted level, Colombia’s Programa Madre Canguro supports preterm and low-birth-weight infants by connecting early hospital discharge with structured outpatient follow-up.16 In low and middle-income settings where access to specialists is limited, implementation of early intervention at scale depends largely on supporting non-specialist providers already embedded in routine services to deliver key components of care. Implemented models suggest that scale in resource-constrained contexts is most likely to be achieved when early intervention is built into existing service platforms—eg, including developmental support delivered through community-based home visiting and group contacts.17

Principle 2: I (Identify early)

Prompt identification of developmental concerns and risks is essential to connect children and families to appropriate services and optimise outcomes. This requires clear standardisation of surveillance, screening and assessment processes, while allowing enough flexibility for cultural adaptation within each setting.18 In early intervention programmes, local practice variation is common because of differences in workforce training and experience, time constraints and service organisation. When these local norms generate inconsistent thresholds for concern, infants with comparable developmental risk may follow different care trajectories simply because they are assessed by different professionals.

This issue is well documented in preterm follow-up, where substantial between-country and between-programme heterogeneity exists in eligibility criteria, follow-up strategies—including assessment frequency and tools used—and professional involvement across Europe.19 20 Such inconsistency can amplify uncertainty for families and delay action during time-sensitive developmental windows. In cerebral palsy, families frequently describe a prolonged period of limited clarity and a ‘wait and see’ approach, often associated with dissatisfaction, frustration and stress.21

Developmental surveillance pathways and screening models aim to reduce unwarranted variation and inconsistent adoption of guidelines18 19 by defining: (1) universal entry points with clear eligibility for specialist support (which risks and signs are assessed?); (2) core timepoints (when do assessments occur?); (3) a small set of feasible, culturally adapted and validated tools for developmental and functional monitoring (which tools are used?); and (4) explicit thresholds linked to referral decisions, follow-up intensity and escalation (which thresholds trigger actions?). This is especially important in low and middle-income contexts. Experiences from Ethiopia and Fiji show that feasible tools support earlier identification only when embedded in routine services, linked to referral and intervention, and reinforced through local training, leadership and feedback.22 23 When a more comprehensive diagnostic assessment is warranted, it should be proportionate to its purpose and the available resources, with careful consideration given to the feasibility and contextual relevance of lengthy, specialist-led assessment models transferred from high-income settings.

Early intervention should avoid diagnosis-led, ‘fixing’ models that frame support primarily around impairment correction rather than family priorities and functioning. An approach informed by the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) and the F-words for Child Development—functioning, family, fitness, fun, friends and future—shifts decision-making towards family-centred actions based on what will most meaningfully support the child and family.24 25 It directs attention towards what children can do, what they enjoy, the relationships and environments that support them and what matters to them and their families.

Principle 3: G (Goal alignment with families)

Family-centred early intervention recognises caregivers as partners in shared decision-making and aligns intervention targets with what children and families value and prioritise, combining relational practices (respect, empathy and positive beliefs about family strengths) with participatory practices (informed choice, collaboration and caregiver skill-building).26 27 Evidence-informed approaches such as the Family Partnership Model can support this process by positioning families and professionals as partners with complementary expertise, working together to explore concerns, recognise strengths, agree on priorities and develop meaningful goals.28

Goal-setting that reflects family priorities and is built into everyday activities—feeding, play, mobility routines, communication moments—creates repeated opportunities to practice in naturally occurring contexts, supporting carryover and maintenance of functional skills over time.29 In this sense, the ICF-inspired F-words provide a practical, strength-based framework for broadening these conversations beyond impairment.25 Resources such as the F-words Lens Tool may further support collaborative clinical reasoning by helping families and professionals connect meaningful goals with the specific intervention components required to pursue them.30

This perspective also builds on families’ existing capabilities, strategies already used successfully in daily life and naturally occurring opportunities for learning and participation. It avoids treating vulnerability as an inherent characteristic of families, recognising that it is often created or intensified by services that are inaccessible, fragmented, culturally unresponsive, overly burdensome or driven primarily by professional priorities. Accordingly, progress should be judged not only by change in child-level measures but also by whether families feel more confident and supported, and more able to help their child participate in everyday life.31

Principle 4: H (Help families deliver)

Evidence from parenting interventions shows that supporting caregivers to use developmentally supportive strategies in daily life can improve child development and parenting practices, including in low and middle-income contexts.32 33

Across clinical practice guidelines, more effective early interventions share several common features: they are family-centred, goal-directed, embedded in natural contexts and delivered at sufficient intensity to support learning and functional change.34 To translate these features into deliverable care, intervention plans should make the ‘why’ explicit by linking each intervention component to a plausible mechanism of change, such as increasing self-initiated exploration, supporting caregiver responsiveness, enriching task-specific practice or adapting the environment to make participation easier.30

Within this principle, families should, therefore, be supported not as recipients of advice, but as active partners in implementation.35 Coaching approaches help translate goals into routine-based strategies that families can implement and adapt over time.36 37 The aim is to strengthen their confidence and capacity to create developmentally rich opportunities within everyday life.

The emphasis on intensity should not be understood as a call to ‘more therapy at any cost’. Rather, it refers to enough repeated, meaningful practice to support learning, generalisation and functioning, while remaining feasible within the family’s routines, capacities and preferences. Interventions should be supported by a strong evidence base to avoid legitimising practices that require substantial family time and effort without clear benefit, particularly given concerns about time toxicity,38 and the limited evidence base for some high-demand approaches.39

Principle 5: T (Track and improve)

Explicit standards are essential for accountability and improvement. Without defined processes and measurable expectations, services cannot reliably identify delays in identification and referral, loss to follow-up, geographic inequities or whether corrective changes are effective.40

Operationalising this principle depends on robust governance and oversight (coverage of eligible children, visit completion rates, time to first developmental review, variability between services and loss to follow-up), protected professional development and equity-oriented infrastructure to close access gaps for families who are geographically distant or resource-constrained.41–43 Continuous improvement cycles should combine service indicators with family-reported experiences (barriers to access, information and shared priorities). Experience from integrating a parenting programme into primary healthcare in rural Bangladesh illustrates how routine process indicators can help determine whether implementation gaps concern programme delivery, family engagement, workforce support or implementation fidelity.44 These data can then guide targeted corrective action through audit and feedback, local problem-solving and cross-sector review.

Principle 6: S (Sustainability)

Sustainability determines whether early intervention programmes are maintained beyond pilot projects, individual champions or political and administrative cycles. Within the RIGHTS framework, sustainability means designing programmes so they can be institutionalised as long-term public policy: embedded in existing service platforms, governed through clear accountability arrangements, supported by stable financing and coordinated across levels of care. This is consistent with the Pan American Health Organization’s (PAHO/WHO) perspective on sustainable health systems, which emphasises the institutionalisation of integrated models of care within Integrated Health Services Networks based on Primary Health Care.45 It also aligns early intervention with the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, particularly universal health coverage under SDG 3 and the need for effective, accountable and resilient institutions under SDG 16.3

The WHO health-system building blocks provide a useful lens for examining the capacities that support sustainable early intervention. These include accessible and coordinated services, a capable workforce, actionable information, equitable access to essential resources, adequate financing with financial protection and effective policy, oversight and accountability.46 Portugal’s national early intervention programme highlights how legislation and cross-sector governance can connect these system capacities to coordinated delivery through local multidisciplinary teams and community-based networks.47 Although Portugal is a high-income country, the transferable principle is consistent: coordination is a design feature. Policies, pathways and professional routines should connect health, education and social protection, ensuring that identification reliably leads to timely, continuous and inclusive support.

Sustainability, therefore, requires more than financial continuity. In low and middle-income contexts, sustainable early intervention also requires reducing fragmentation, strengthening institutional capacity, protecting workforce development, integrating indicators into routine information systems and monitoring whether scale-up preserves quality and equity.48 49 Sustainability, therefore, connects all preceding RIGHTS principles: reach, identification, family-centred goals, caregiver support and continuous improvement can only produce durable impact when they are embedded in governance, financing, data and workforce strategies that make the pathway resilient over time.

Conclusions

The RIGHTS principles frame early intervention as a coordinated system function. Their central implication is that early intervention must be organised to connect early identification with timely, family-centred, functionally meaningful and sustainable support, delivered through pathways that are consistent, equitable and feasible in real-world settings.

This is especially important in low and middle-income contexts, where specialist availability is often limited, services are unevenly distributed, and families may face substantial barriers related to poverty, geography, language, transport and fragmented care pathways. In these contexts, the central policy question is how to define an early intervention programme that can be delivered widely, with additional layers of support according to developmental risk and complexity. Proportionate universalism provides a useful organising principle: early intervention should be available to all children and families, while the scale and intensity of support increase according to developmental, family and social need. The RIGHTS principles operationalise this approach across the pathway, from equitable reach and early identification to family partnership, continuous improvement and sustainable system capacity.

At scale, early intervention must be embedded within existing health, early childhood, education and social-care platforms and sustained through governance, financing, workforce and data systems. This also requires practical pathways for outreach, early identification, family-centred goal setting, caregiver support, continuous monitoring and long-term institutionalisation. In this way, the RIGHTS principles offer a conceptual framework and pragmatic roadmap for building early intervention systems that are more deliverable, inclusive, resilient and responsive to the realities of children and families living in resource-constrained settings.
