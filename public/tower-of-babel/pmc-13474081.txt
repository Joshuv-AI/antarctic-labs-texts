Late-pregnancy air pollution exposure and neonatal anthropometry with and without gestational hypertension
Authors: Tai-Ho Hung, Te-Yao Hsu, Chia-Yu Ou, Liang-Ming Lo, Steven W Shaw, Gwo-Hwa Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis study examined the relationships among gestational hypertension (GH), peridelivery inflammatory biomarkers, late-pregnancy air pollution exposure, and neonatal anthropometric outcomes. Pregnant women recruited during the second trimester were classified into the GH or normotensive group. Maternal characteristics, blood pressure, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), interleukin 1β (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and neonatal outcomes were assessed. Exposure to PM10, PM2.5, NO2, SO2, carbon monoxide (CO), and ozone (O3) during the 30 days before delivery was estimated using ambient monitoring data. Women with GH had higher maternal hs-CRP concentrations and delivered neonates with lower birth weight and smaller chest and abdominal circumferences. PM2.5, NO2, SO2, and CO exposures were inversely associated with birth length. Mediation analyses showed no significant indirect effects through hs-CRP, IL-1β, or TNF-α, although maternal and cord blood TNF-α concentrations were positively correlated. These findings indicate that GH and late-pregnancy air pollution exposure are associated with less favorable neonatal anthropometric outcomes through pathways not explained by the measured circulating inflammatory biomarkers.Keywords: gestational hypertension, maternal blood pressure, inflammatory biomarkers, air pollution, neonatal growth

Introduction

Air pollution remains a major global public health concern, accounting for a substantial portion of the global disease burden. Inhalation of air pollutants generates free radicals in the lungs, leading to oxidative stress and systemic inflammation, which have been associated with increased risks of cardiovascular disease, respiratory disorders, and cancer.1,2,3 Exposure to ambient particulate matter (PM), carbon monoxide (CO), sulfur oxides (SOx), nitrogen oxides (NOx), and ozone (O3) has been further linked to altered inflammatory responses, specifically fluctuations in circulating high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), interleukin 1β (IL-1β), IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α).4,5,6,7

Despite growing evidence, the effects of air pollutant exposure on maternal and fetal outcomes remain inconsistent, and the biological pathways underlying these associations are not fully established. Several studies have reported significant associations between maternal exposure to PM2.5 and elevated CRP levels during pregnancy,8,9 whereas others found no significant association with NO2 exposure.10 While increased NO2 exposure has been linked to higher maternal IL-10 concentrations,11 findings regarding cord blood inflammatory responses, including IL-6 and TNF-α, remain fragmented and contradictory.12,13

Crucially, these environmental exposures do not act in isolation but intersect dynamically with maternal physiological conditions. Prenatal exposure to air pollution has been consistently associated with adverse neonatal outcomes, including low birth weight, small-for-gestational-age status, reduced head circumference, and preterm birth.14,15,16,17 However, pollutant-specific effects appear to vary across different cohorts, indicating that further investigation into potentially susceptible subgroups is highly warranted.18

Gestational hypertension (GH), affecting approximately 5%–10% of pregnancies globally, is a major contributor to maternal and perinatal morbidity.19,20 GH is characterized by structural and functional placental alterations, including vascular thrombosis, accelerated maturation, and reduced placental axial growth, all of which collectively impair fetal development.21 Given that elevated maternal blood pressure has been associated with reduced birth weight and birth length,22,23,24 it is important to understand whether the pathophysiological state of GH may exacerbate the adverse effects of environmental pollutants.

To date, maternal blood pressure, inflammatory responses, and environmental exposures have rarely been examined within a unified analytical framework. This represents an important gap, particularly because pregnancies complicated by GH may involve concurrent maternal vascular dysfunction, inflammatory activity, and an increased susceptibility to environmental insults. Accordingly, this study aimed to investigate the relationships among maternal exposure to ambient air pollution, maternal blood pressure, inflammatory biomarkers in maternal and cord blood, and neonatal anthropometric outcomes. We further examined whether maternal inflammatory biomarkers mediated the associations between air pollution and neonatal outcomes, while treating GH status as a clinically relevant maternal condition within the cohort.

Results

Participant characteristics and neonatal anthropometry

A total of 192 pregnant women were enrolled, including 64 women in the GH group and 128 women in the normotensive group. Women in the GH group were older and exhibited higher pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) and ponderal index than those in the normotensive group, whereas education level, smoking status, husband’s smoking status, and allergy history did not differ significantly between groups (Table 1). The median age was 36 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 33.0–41.0) in the GH group and 33.1 years (IQR: 30.0–36.8) in the normotensive group (p = 0.001). The proportion of participants aged ≥ 35 years was also higher in the GH group than in the normotensive group (60.9% versus 37.5%, p = 0.002). In addition, the GH group demonstrated a significantly higher median pre-pregnancy BMI (27.5 kg/m2 [IQR: 22.8–32.8] versus 22.2 kg/m2 [IQR: 20.0–25.4], p < 0.001) and ponderal index (17.1 kg/m3 [IQR: 14.5–20.7] versus 13.6 kg/m3 [IQR: 12.3–15.8], p < 0.001).

Maternal blood pressure was significantly higher in the GH group than in the normotensive group during both the second and third trimesters (all p < 0.05) (Table 2). As elevated blood pressure is a defining clinical criterion for GH, these comparisons are presented here as descriptive cohort characteristics rather than primary analytical findings. Notably, the cesarean section rate was significantly higher in the GH group than in the normotensive group (71.9% versus 40.0%, p < 0.001). The GH group also exhibited lower median placental weight (547.5 versus 597.5 g, p = 0.013) and shorter gestational age at delivery (263.0 versus 273.0 days, p < 0.001). Regarding neonatal outcomes, neonates born to women in the GH group had lower birth weight and reduced chest and abdominal circumferences compared to those born to normotensive women (all p < 0.01). Birth length showed a downward trend in the GH group, though the difference did not reach statistical significance, and no significant difference in head circumference was observed (Table 2).

Maternal and cord blood inflammatory biomarkers

Maternal inflammatory biomarker levels measured within 24 h prior to delivery are shown in Figure 1. Maternal hs-CRP concentrations were significantly higher in the GH group than in the normotensive group (median, 6.08 versus 2.33 mg/L, p < 0.001), whereas maternal IL-1β and TNF-α concentrations did not differ significantly. In cord blood, hs-CRP and IL-1β concentrations were below the assay detection limit in all participants. Interestingly, while cord blood biomarkers did not vary by group, maternal and cord blood TNF-α concentrations were strongly and positively correlated (rs = 0.692, p < 0.001).

Late-pregnancy air pollutant exposure

Maternal exposure to ambient air pollutants during the 30 days before delivery is shown in Figure 2. Among the six pollutants examined, only NO2 exposure differed significantly between groups, with higher levels in the GH group (median, 14.90 versus 12.62 ppb, p = 0.035). Overall, late-pregnancy ambient pollutant exposure was similar between groups except for NO2. Spearman correlation analyses revealed that maternal exposure to NO2 (rs = −0.208, p = 0.005), SO2 (rs = −0.258, p < 0.001), and CO (rs = −0.188, p = 0.011) was inversely associated with neonatal birth length.

Associations between air pollutant exposure and neonatal anthropometry

In multivariable linear regression models adjusted for maternal group, pre-pregnancy BMI, and gestational age, maternal exposure to PM10, PM2.5, NO2, SO2, and CO was inversely associated with neonatal birth length (Table 3). Specifically, each 10 μg/m3 increase in PM2.5 was associated with a 0.088 cm decrease in birth length (95% confidence interval [CI], −0.148–−0.028; p = 0.004), while each 10 ppb increase in NO2 correlated with a 0.104 cm reduction (95% CI, −0.159–−0.049; p < 0.001). SO2 and CO were also significantly associated with shorter birth length (SO2: β = −0.966 cm, 95% CI, −1.449–−0.483, p < 0.001 and CO: β = −2.819 cm, 95% CI, −4.610–−1.029, p = 0.002). The association between PM10 exposure and birth length was borderline significant (β = −0.035 cm, 95% CI, −0.069–0.000; p = 0.049). After false discovery rate (FDR) correction, the inverse associations of PM2.5, NO2, SO2, and CO with neonatal birth length remained statistically significant, whereas the association for PM10 did not (FDR-adjusted p = 0.059). No significant associations were observed for other anthropometric parameters, although SO2 and CO exhibited negative trends. O3 was not significantly associated with neonatal birth length or other anthropometric outcomes in the adjusted models.

Sensitivity, subgroup, and exposure-window analyses

Sensitivity analyses excluding gestational age confirmed that the inverse associations of NO2 and SO2 with birth length remained evident after FDR correction (Table S1). Age-stratified analyses suggested that these associations were more apparent among women aged < 35 years. In this subgroup, PM2.5, NO2, SO2, and CO remained significantly associated with shorter birth length, whereas these effects were not observed among women aged ≥ 35 years (Table S2). Similarly, pre-pregnancy BMI-stratified analyses suggested that inverse associations were more apparent among women with BMI < 24 kg/m2 (Table S3). Formal interaction analyses confirmed that maternal age significantly modified the association between NO2 exposure and neonatal birth length (FDR-adjusted pinteraction = 0.018), whereas the interaction terms for PM2.5, SO2, and CO did not remain significant after FDR correction (Table S4). Furthermore, temporal analyses demonstrated that the inverse associations of NO2, SO2, and CO were consistently observed across 7-day and 14-day averages, while PM2.5 appeared most robust in the primary 30-day model (Table S5).

Mediation analysis of maternal inflammatory biomarkers

To elucidate the biological pathways, Table 4 presents the mediation analysis results assessing whether maternal inflammatory biomarkers mediated the associations between maternal air pollutant exposure and neonatal birth length. Indirect effects were rigorously estimated using bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals based on 5,000 resamples. Our analysis revealed consistent direct effects of pollutants across different mediation models. When hs-CRP was included as the mediator, significant direct effects on birth length were observed for NO2 (β = −0.094, 95% CI, −0.160–−0.028, p = 0.006) and CO (β = −3.239, 95% CI, −5.545–−0.934, p = 0.006). Similarly, when IL-1β was considered, the direct effects of PM2.5 (β = −0.080, 95% CI, −0.158–−0.002, p = 0.044), NO2 (β = −0.111, 95% CI, −0.188–−0.035, p = 0.005), and CO (β = −3.394, 95% CI, −5.950–−0.838, p = 0.010) remained statistically significant. Furthermore, in the model utilizing TNF-α as the mediator, both NO2 (β = −0.121, 95% CI, −0.196–−0.046, p = 0.002) and CO (β = −3.738, 95% CI, −6.302–−1.174, p = 0.005) again demonstrated significant direct effects. Crucially, however, no statistically significant indirect effects through hs-CRP, IL-1β, or TNF-α were detected in any of the models. Overall, these findings indicate that while short-term air pollutant exposure is significantly associated with reduced birth length, this relationship is likely not mediated by the specific maternal inflammatory biomarkers measured in this study.

Discussion

This study evaluated the interplay among maternal blood pressure, inflammatory biomarkers, ambient air pollutant exposure, and neonatal anthropometric outcomes, with particular attention to pregnancies complicated by GH. Women in the GH group exhibited significantly higher pre-pregnancy BMI and ponderal index than women in the normotensive group. These findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that elevated pre-pregnancy BMI is a well-recognized risk factor for GH, likely driven by systemic inflammation and vascular dysfunction.25,26 A higher proportion of women in the GH group were primiparous and delivered by cesarean section, patterns that have also been reported in previous studies of GH.27,28,29

The etiology of GH remains incompletely understood, but it likely involves multiple mechanisms, including maternal circulatory overcompensation, placental abnormalities, vascular injury, genetic predisposition, and dysregulated maternal immune responses, which may culminate in endothelial dysfunction and abnormal placentation.30 In the present study, both systolic and diastolic blood pressure values were significantly higher in the GH group than in the normotensive group during the second and third trimesters. While these differences primarily validate the clinical cohort classification, the concomitant reduction in placental weight and shorter gestational age in the GH group underscore the hypothesis that GH-associated vascular stress compromises the maternal-fetal interface and impairs intrauterine conditions.31,32,33

Neonates born to mothers with GH exhibited significantly lower birth weight and smaller chest and abdominal circumferences. Birth length also tended to be lower in the GH group, although the difference did not reach statistical significance, whereas head circumference did not differ significantly between groups. This pattern may suggest that some anthropometric measures were more affected than others in pregnancies complicated by GH. However, the present study was not designed to evaluate fetal body proportionality, placental hemodynamics, or neurodevelopmental adaptation, and these findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously. The discrepancy between our findings and some previous reports, such as by Das et al., may reflect heterogeneity in study populations, clinical management, and environmental exposure levels.29

Previous studies have reported associations between GH and elevated circulating levels of hs-CRP, IL-1β, and TNF-α.27,34,35,36,37,38 In the present study, maternal hs-CRP levels near delivery were significantly higher in the GH group, consistent with enhanced systemic inflammatory activity in GH.27 In contrast, maternal IL-1β and TNF-α concentrations did not differ significantly between groups. In cord blood, hs-CRP and IL-1β were below the assay detection limit in all participants, suggesting that these specific markers were not markedly elevated at birth. However, the positive correlation between maternal and cord blood TNF-α concentrations may indicate related maternal and fetal inflammatory conditions near delivery. These cord blood findings should be interpreted as a peridelivery snapshot rather than as evidence of chronic intrauterine inflammatory exposure across the entire gestation.

In this study, we specifically focused on maternal exposure to air pollutants during the 30 days preceding delivery rather than across the entire pregnancy because this short-term exposure window was temporally aligned with biomarker sampling conducted within 24 h prior to delivery. This design allowed us to examine whether recent ambient exposure was associated with peridelivery inflammatory biomarker levels and neonatal anthropometric outcomes. Longer exposure windows were not included as primary analyses because they would be less directly aligned with the peridelivery biomarker measurements and could reflect exposure periods biologically distinct from the inflammatory status assessed near delivery. Therefore, the primary analysis focused on the 30-day average exposure window, while the 7-day and 14-day analyses were conducted as supplementary evaluations of shorter term exposure patterns. Within this exposure window, no significant differences in ambient pollutant levels were observed between women with and without GH, except for NO2. These findings indicate that the between-group differences in pollutant exposure were limited, and that the observed associations with neonatal birth length were not simply explained by marked group-level contrasts in overall late-pregnancy ambient exposure.

Our findings indicate that maternal exposure to PM10, PM2.5, NO2, SO2, and CO during late pregnancy was inversely associated with neonatal birth length in adjusted models, whereas significant associations with birth weight, head circumference, chest circumference, or abdominal circumference were not observed. These results suggest that short-term pollutant exposure near term may be more closely related to linear growth at birth than to other neonatal anthropometric measures in this dataset. This interpretation is broadly consistent with previous epidemiological evidence linking traffic-related pollutant exposure with reduced neonatal birth length.38 At the same time, caution is required because several pollutants were examined and the primary regression analyses were based on single-pollutant models. Importantly, the inverse associations of PM2.5, NO2, SO2, and CO with neonatal birth length remained significant after FDR correction in the primary 30-day models, whereas the association for PM10 did not. Taken together, these findings suggest that birth length may have been a more sensitive indicator of late-pregnancy pollutant exposure than the other neonatal anthropometric measures in this cohort. Additional supplementary analyses showed that the inverse associations for NO2 and SO2 remained evident after excluding gestational age, that the NO2 association varied by maternal age, and that the inverse associations of NO2, SO2, and CO with neonatal birth length were also observed using the 7-day and 14-day exposure windows. These patterns further support the robustness of the main finding for birth length, while also suggesting that the relevant exposure window may not have been identical across pollutants.

Although the biological mechanisms remain incompletely understood, it is plausible that ambient air pollutants may induce oxidative stress, vascular dysfunction, hypoxic stress, or placental inflammation, which in turn could impair placental perfusion and nutrient or oxygen transfer to the fetus. Such mechanisms may be particularly relevant during late gestation, when fetal growth velocity remains high. However, the present study did not directly measure placental molecular responses, oxidative stress pathways, endothelial injury, or uteroplacental blood flow. Therefore, the current findings should not be interpreted as providing direct mechanistic proof, but rather as supporting an association between short-term exposure to selected pollutants and shorter birth length near term.

In our cohort, some women were diagnosed with GH in the second trimester and others in the third trimester. Following diagnosis, some women with GH may have received low-dose aspirin as part of standard clinical management aimed at improving uteroplacental circulation and reducing preeclampsia-related complications.39 Previous studies have shown that low-dose aspirin can exert anti-inflammatory effects by lowering circulating hs-CRP levels and modulating cytokine activity in reproductive-age and pregnant women.40,41 Therefore, aspirin use in this cohort may have influenced systemic inflammatory activity, which could have affected the observed associations between pollutant exposure and maternal inflammatory biomarkers. However, this possibility should be interpreted with caution because complete medication records were not available for all participants.

Nevertheless, mediation analyses consistently revealed no significant indirect effects through maternal hs-CRP, IL-1β, or TNF-α. These findings indicate that the measured circulating inflammatory biomarkers did not statistically account for the association between maternal pollutant exposure and reduced neonatal birth length in this dataset. Several potential explanations exist. First, maternal biomarkers were measured only once within 24 h before delivery and therefore could not capture inflammatory trajectories across pregnancy. Second, circulating biomarkers may not adequately capture localized placental inflammatory or oxidative responses that may be more directly involved in influencing fetal growth. Third, the sample size may have limited power to detect subtle indirect effects. Collectively, these findings suggest that the observed association between short-term air pollutant exposure and neonatal birth length may operate through biological pathways extending beyond the circulating inflammatory markers measured in this study.

Future studies with larger cohorts, repeated biomarker measurements, refined exposure assessment, mixture-based exposure modeling, and integration of placental molecular profiling and medication histories are warranted to clarify the biological pathways linking late-pregnancy air pollutant exposure with neonatal growth characteristics.

Taken together, this study showed that GH was associated with less favorable neonatal anthropometric outcomes in this cohort. In addition, maternal exposure to PM2.5, NO2, SO2, and CO during the 30 days before delivery was inversely associated with neonatal birth length. The measured maternal inflammatory biomarkers did not significantly mediate these associations. Overall, these findings suggest that GH and late-pregnancy ambient air pollution exposure may both contribute to adverse neonatal outcomes through pathways not fully explained by the circulating inflammatory biomarkers measured in this study.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, inflammatory biomarkers were measured only once within 24 h before delivery and therefore should be interpreted as indicators of the peridelivery inflammatory milieu rather than longitudinal inflammatory or oxidative stress changes across pregnancy. Second, maternal exposure was estimated using the ambient monitoring station nearest to each participant’s residential address and may not fully reflect individual-level exposure, thereby introducing potential exposure misclassification. Personal exposure may differ from station-based ambient estimates because of indoor environments, commuting patterns, occupational settings, and individual time-activity patterns. Future studies incorporating personal exposure assessment or higher resolution exposure metrics may help reduce exposure misclassification and provide more refined estimates of late-pregnancy pollutant exposure. Third, the sample size was modest, particularly for mediation and subgroup analyses, which may have limited the ability to detect subtle indirect effects or subgroup-specific associations; therefore, some stratified and mediation estimates should be interpreted cautiously. Fourth, although the models adjusted for maternal group, pre-pregnancy BMI, and gestational age, residual confounding by unmeasured factors, including socioeconomic conditions, diet, physical activity, and other lifestyle-related variables, cannot be excluded. Fifth, this study evaluated neonatal anthropometric outcomes at birth only, and postnatal follow-up data were not available to determine whether these differences persisted into infancy or childhood. Finally, the single-pollutant models may not fully capture the joint effects of co-occurring air pollutants. Mixture-based exposure modeling is therefore an important direction for future research.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study was approved by the Chang Gung Medical Foundation Institutional Review Board (IRB# 201901952A3 and 104-0056A3), and all participants provided written informed consent. Pregnant women were recruited from obstetrics and gynecology outpatient departments at Taipei and Kaohsiung Chang Gung Memorial Hospitals in Taiwan. Inclusion criteria were women aged >20 years, in their second trimester of pregnancy (13−28 weeks of gestation), and residing in Taiwan. Ethnicity was not specifically collected as a study variable. After 20 weeks of gestation, participants were classified as either normotensive or as having gestational hypertension by their attending obstetricians. Because the study population consisted of pregnant women, sex-based comparisons were not applicable.

GH was defined as new-onset elevated blood pressure (systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg on at least two separate occasions ≥ 6 hours apart after 20 weeks of gestation) in the absence of chronic hypertension or proteinuria. Participants were recruited using a 1:2 ratio (GH:normotensive) to enhance statistical power. Exclusion criteria included known cardiovascular disease, chronic hypertension, pregestational diabetes mellitus, and multifetal pregnancy. The cohort included both GH and normotensive pregnancies, and GH status was treated as a clinically relevant maternal condition within the cohort rather than as the sole target population. All participants were followed from the second trimester until delivery to document maternal health, blood pressure progression, and neonatal outcomes. A total of 192 pregnant women were included, comprising 64 women with gestational hypertension and 128 normotensive women.

Method details

Participant characteristics and birth outcomes

Maternal sociodemographic and clinical data were collected through structured interviews and medical record review. Variables included age, education level, marital status, occupation, pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), and allergy history. Obstetric history and family history of chronic conditions were also documented. Clinical assessments were performed during pregnancy and included measurements of maternal weight, blood pressure, and urine screening results for glucose and protein. Pregnancy complications and relevant laboratory findings were recorded. After delivery, neonatal anthropometric measurements were recorded, including gestational age at birth, birth length, birth weight, head circumference, chest circumference, and abdominal circumference.

Maternal and cord blood sample collection and analysis

Maternal peripheral blood samples (10 mL each) were collected by trained clinical staff during the third trimester, within 24 hours before delivery, without requiring fasting. Samples were processed within 2 hours of collection by centrifugation, and serum aliquots were stored at ‒80°C until analysis. Inflammatory biomarker assays included hs-CRP measured by immunoturbidimetric assay (Abbott Diagnostics, Netherlands) and IL-1β and TNF-α measured using human cytokine enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (RayBiotech Inc., USA). At birth, 10 mL of umbilical cord blood was collected, processed, and stored using the same protocols. Cord blood hs-CRP, IL-1β, and TNF-α concentrations were analyzed using the same assay methods to evaluate fetal inflammatory biomarker levels. Because maternal blood sampling was performed within 24 hours before delivery and cord blood was obtained at birth, these biomarkers were interpreted as indicators of the peridelivery inflammatory milieu rather than longitudinal inflammatory changes across pregnancy.

Outdoor air pollutant data collection

Air quality data were obtained from the monitoring network operated by the Ministry of Environment (MOENV) of Taiwan. Northern Taiwan was represented by 25 monitoring stations located in Taipei, New Taipei, and Taoyuan, whereas southern Taiwan included 18 stations located in Tainan, Kaohsiung, and Pingtung. Six ambient air pollutants were continuously monitored: PM10, PM2.5, NO2, SO2, CO, and O3. For each participant, the MOENV monitoring station closest to her residential address was identified. Maternal exposure during late pregnancy was estimated using the average concentrations of each pollutant during the 30 days before delivery based on data from the nearest monitoring station. The 30-day exposure window was selected a priori because it was temporally aligned with maternal blood sampling performed within 24 hours before delivery and was intended to capture short-term late-pregnancy exposure. Additional analyses using 7-day and 14-day average concentrations before delivery were conducted as supplementary analyses to evaluate whether the associations were consistent across shorter exposure windows.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and visualizations were generated using GraphPad Prism version 5.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). The individual pregnant woman was considered the biological unit for maternal analyses, and the corresponding neonate was considered the biological unit for neonatal outcome analyses. Statistical significance was initially defined as a two-sided p value < 0.05. Raw p values are reported, and false discovery rate-adjusted p values were additionally calculated for multiple comparisons where appropriate. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test, and categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Spearman correlation analyses were conducted to assess the relationships among air pollutants, inflammatory biomarkers, and neonatal outcomes.

Multivariable linear regression models were constructed to examine the associations between maternal air pollutant exposure during the 30 days before delivery and neonatal anthropometric measurements. The primary models were adjusted for maternal group, pre-pregnancy BMI, and gestational age. Because gestational age may plausibly lie on the causal pathway between late-pregnancy exposure and neonatal anthropometric outcomes, additional sensitivity analyses excluding gestational age from the model were performed. Additional analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness and potential heterogeneity of the main findings. Age-stratified (< 35 and ≥ 35 years) and pre-pregnancy BMI-stratified (< 24 and ≥ 24 kg/m2) multivariable linear regression analyses were performed for neonatal birth length. Formal interaction analyses were performed by adding pollutant-by-age group interaction terms to the regression models. To assess the influence of exposure-window definition, additional multivariable linear regression analyses were performed using 7-day and 14-day average air pollutant concentrations before delivery, in addition to the primary 30-day average exposure metric. These additional analyses were conducted using the same model structure as the primary analysis. The main regression analyses were based on single-pollutant models.

Causal mediation analyses were conducted to assess whether maternal inflammatory biomarkers (hs-CRP, IL-1β, and TNF-α) mediated the associations between air pollutant exposure and neonatal birth length. The significance of mediation effects was evaluated using bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals based on 5,000 bootstrap resamples, implemented via the PROCESS macro v3.4 (Model 4) by Andrew F. Hayes in SPSS version 28.0.
