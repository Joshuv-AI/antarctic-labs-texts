Australia as the destination for study abroad: International students’ mobility
Authors: Luis Miguel Dos Santos, Ho Fai Lo, Ching Ting Tany Kwee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
International students have become one of the main populations in Australian universities. However, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, many international students decided to go to other countries and regions for their higher education due to the border control policies in Australia. Based on the social cognitive career and motivation theory, this study explored the motivations and reasons why Chinese postgraduate international students decided to return to Australia after the Australian government officially re-opened the border for on-campus and face-to-face education and why Chinese postgraduate international students decided to study in Australia instead of other countries, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic. Based on the general inductive approach with semi-structured interviews, focus group activities, and member-checking interviews, 20 Chinese postgraduate international students' stories were captured. The findings indicated that financial, academic, and personal considerations are significant in the participants’ decision-making processes. The results will fill the research gaps and become a reference in international student mobilities, decisions, and decision-making processes of international students, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic in the Australian university environment.

Introduction

Globalisation and international mobility have made studying abroad an option for undergraduate and postgraduate students to complete their qualifications outside of their home country [[1], [2], [3]]. In recent decades, the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand have become popular choices for international students studying abroad, particularly for those wishing to improve their English language proficiency [4,5]. The Chinese student population is one of the largest groups studying abroad [6,7]. Although many Chinese students decide to go to the United States for their undergraduate and postgraduate education, Australia has become a popular option for many undergraduate and postgraduate students over the past few years. According to a recent report [8], the United States (1,075,496), the United Kingdom (551,495), Canada (503,270), Australia (463,643), France, and Russia (353,331) were among the top six destinations for international students in 2020. Although social distancing and travel restrictions due to the COVID-19 pandemic may impact student enrolment, economic development has the potential to drive increases in the student population [9,10].

According to statistics from the Australian Department of Education, a total of 478,260 international students joined Australian communities for the period from January to June 2022. The top three countries for international student enrolment in Australia in 2022 were China (133,200), India (77,357), and Nepal (44,969). Although international student enrolment has been affected by political, health, and financial factors, Chinese international student enrolment has been at the top of the list for the past decade. Although Australia's overall international student population is large, most previous studies have focused on undergraduate, traditional-age students and have taken a global perspective [6,14,15]. Over the past few years, some studies have been conducted about international students' motivations, decisions, and decision-making processes in Australia [[16], [17], [18]]. Although international students' issues in Australia have been discussed, there is a need to examine the mobilities and behaviours of Chinese international students who studied for their postgraduate degrees and courses in Australian schools and universities, particularly based on the lens of social cognitive career and motivation theory [18,19] with qualitative research methods during the COVID-19 pandemic. The social cognitive career and motivation theory [18,19] is one of the latest theories in education, motivation, psychology, and social sciences. Seeking the motivations and reasons of individuals and groups is one of the purposes of this theory. Using this theory for the investigation, the outcomes of this study will fill the research gaps for Chinese postgraduate students who decide to come to Australia for their master's or doctoral degree education, particularly non-traditional-age students.

In the early 2020s, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, the Australian government decided to close its border to anyone who was not an Australian citizen or a permanent resident, and this restriction included international students with a student visa. In late 2021, the Australian government lifted the border control regulation, allowing international students to return to Australia with student visas. Chinese international student population occupied a significant portion of Australian universities before and during the COVID-19 pandemic. During the period of border controls, many Chinese postgraduate students chose to start their qualifications in other countries, such as South Korea [20] or via distance learning and online degree options, as online learning has no significant restrictions [[21], [22], [23], [24]]. As there are many options and opportunities for students who could complete their degree, coming to Australia for on-campus and blended courses might not be one of the priorities for many international students, particularly Chinese students. Based on this background, it is important to understand the motivations, decisions, decision-making processes, and mobilities of postgraduate international students coming to Australia for their qualifications, particularly Chinese students, during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Significance of the study

First, international students' mobility has received attention from many higher education researchers, particularly in Australia [25,26]. A wide range of political, health, and financial factors and challenges can severely limit the mobility of international students. Some students choose to continue their education via distance learning and online platforms [25,26], a trend that has been facilitated through the development of technology. Over the past few decades, a few Australian universities, such as Central Queensland University and the University of New England, have developed online or blended learning degree programmes and qualifications for students who could not experience on-campus studies. As for international students, students have the option of completing their qualifications via online platforms and distance learning. However, some still decide to come to Australia for on-campus learning and face-to-face education. For a decade, due to the trend of online education, some studies [6,15,27] have addressed these issues or investigated international students studying for Australian qualifications on-campus with face-to-face interactions. However, only a few studies explored the abovementioned issues for Chinese international students. Therefore, this study's outcomes will respond to this research gap.

Second, during the COVID-19 pandemic, Australia closed its borders to everyone except Australian citizens and permanent residents. This restriction included international students with valid visas who could not return to Australia. The Australian government did not re-open the borders to international candidates until December 15, 2021, when international students could enter Australia without needing a travel exemption. Although the social distancing and restrictions were terminated, the aftermaths, mobilities, and behaviours of international students were completely impacted due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Also, the Chinese international student population is one of the largest groups in Australian universities. Therefore, it is important to understand the mobilities and behaviours of these population groups in Australia. Although some studies have been conducted about international students' issues in Australia [[16], [17], [18]], only a few studies have focussed on Chinese postgraduate international students’ mobilities and behaviours in Australia based on the social cognitive career and motivation theory [18,19] with qualitative research methods, particularly after the re-opening of the border. Therefore, the outcomes of this study will fill this research gap in the Australian context.

Theoretical framework and Literature review

Social cognitive career and motivation theory

The social cognitive career and motivation theory [18,19] was employed as the theoretical framework to investigate this study. The theory indicated that internal thinking and external factors might influence individuals' and groups’ motivations, career decisions, and decision-making processes. Although internal and external factors may not influence individuals and groups, behaviours may be impacted by at least one factor in their life [28,29].

Internal thinking refers to the psychological and internal factors with self-efficacy [30,31]. In this category, behaviours and decision-making processes are complex and comprehensive based on the internal thinking and beliefs of the individuals. Three categories can be groups, including academic interests, personal considerations, and achievements of education and career goals.

External factor refers to the relationships among social, external, and environmental factors of the given individuals and groups, particularly how these factors impact their behaviours and decision-making processes. In this category, behaviours and decision-making processes are significantly impacted by interests in career development, financial considerations, and surrounding environments and individuals [28,29]. International students’ mobility

There are many pathways through which students can study abroad in an overseas higher education institution [32]. In the European Economic Area (EEA) and Switzerland, citizens and eligible residents can work, study, and live in any member state without a visa [33]. Agreements between many of the member states allow students to enjoy local tuition fees and benefits. The Erasmus Scholarship has become an effective pathway for EEA and international students to receive European education and training [34].

Globalisation has driven the growing trend for university students to decide to study abroad for undergraduate and postgraduate qualifications. Tuition and administrative fees are important income streams for universities, and while domestic students are charged these fees in some countries, including Australia, the United States, Canada, and the United Kingdom, income from international students is increasingly important [35]. However, the COVID-19 pandemic prevented many international students from attending on-campus for face-to-face education and training. Many educational institutions offer online learning options, allowing students to continue their education remotely. This allowed fully paid-up international students to continue their education and pay their tuition fees without any breaks [22,23,36]. Although COVID-19 may have limited international students’ mobility, online learning continued to offer remote programmes and courses, thus mitigating significant impacts on student enrolment [37].

International students’ mobility in Australia

Over the past decade, many higher education institutions, particularly Australian colleges and universities, have accepted many international students to increase sociocultural diversity and international experience [38]. Tuition fees and income from international student enrolment have become increasingly important in Australia over the past decade. According to a recent report from the Australian Parliament [39], Australian university revenue from fully paid-up international students was $3,881,656 (about 17.5 % of total revenue) in 2010, and this increased to $ 9,978,794 in 2019 (about 27.3 % of total revenue). These figures demonstrate the important relationship between international students and university revenue, indicating Australian higher education institutions’ reliance on international student enrolment.

The 2019 statistics show that many universities in Australia accepted international students into their on-campus and online learning programmes. For example, one of the urban-based universities had a total student enrolment of 72,728, including 33,850 international students (around 46.5 %). Nearly half of Australia's university population was international students before the COVID-19 pandemic [39]. Accepting a large number of international students is not uncommon in many Australian universities, particularly those in the Group of Eight, due to their global reputations.

In short, the international student population was an important asset for the Australian government and communities before the COVID-19 pandemic. Due to the reputation and academic performance of Australian colleges and universities, many students overcame sociocultural challenges, admission requirements, and cultural shocks in order to pursue their qualifications in Australia. However, the COVID-19 pandemic has changed the overall picture and management of many parties. Therefore, the research and practice gaps should be filled.

Methodology

The general inductive approach [40] was employed to investigate Chinese postgraduate international students’ mobilities, motivations, decisions, and decision-making processes in the Australian university environment. According to Thomas [40], the general inductive approach has three characteristics that can be useful in this study, including (1) categorising massive and original qualitative materials into summaries, (2) creating connections and groups to answer the research questions, and (3) connecting the theoretical framework and the themes based on the qualitative materials.

Unlike some other qualitative research designs, such as case study [[41], [42], [43]], which may need to follow some limitations based on the sites and populations, the researchers can use the general inductive approach to investigate based on the nature of the study. More importantly, due to the nature of the general inductive approach, the researchers may use different qualitative data collection tools, such as semi-structured interviews for in-depth information, that would be useful for this study. Using this qualitative design, the researchers could capture useful information to answer the research questions [40].

Participants and recruitment

The snowball sampling strategies [44,45] were employed to recruit participants from different parts of the Australian states and territories. First, the researchers contacted three participants based on personal networks. After the first group of participants completed the interview sessions, the participants should refer at least one participant with a similar background. In this case, the richer groups and comments could be captured beyond the researchers' personal networks. After several rounds of referral, a total of 20 participants were willing to join and share their stories and experiences with the researchers. According to different qualitative researchers [[45], [46], [47]], a qualitative study usually collects data from 8 to 25 participants, particularly based on their stories and materials [48]. Therefore, in this study, a total of 20 participants would be enough for an effective qualitative research study, particularly based on their rich stories and lived experiences. More importantly, the participants’ qualitative data and materials can answer the research questions.

The researchers sent and emailed the participants the invitation letter, research protocol, risk statement, and contact information before the data collection procedure [49]. If the participants would like to join, the participants might contact the researchers’ email or telephone number on the invitation letter. More importantly, although the participants joined the study, the participants researved the right to withdraw from the study without any punishment or problems. However, none of them decided to leave the study.

Data collection

Three data collection tools were used, including (1) semi-structured interview sessions [45], (2) focus group activities [50], and (3) member-checking interview sessions [44]. Using both individual interview tools and focus group activities would be useful for participants to share their stories and experiences as an individual and peers within a small group [[50], [51], [52], [53]]. Therefore, using interview sessions, focus group activities, and member-checking interview sessions would be useful in this study.

First, the private, individual, and semi-structured interview sessions were used to collect in-depth data from the participants based on their stories and experiences. During the interview sessions, the participants could share their in-depth stories and experiences with the researchers individually. Participants may share some personal problems, such as financial concerns and family responsibilities, that could not be shared in a group. Therefore, individual interview sessions were useful. The interview sessions lasted from 78 min to 129 min.

Second, once the participants completed their semi-structured interview sessions [45], they were invited to join a focus group activity [50]. As there were 20 participants, two focus group activities were formed (i.e., 10 participants per group). All participants were encouraged to share their experiences and stories about their mobilities, motivations, career decisions, and decision-making processes as group mates. During the focus group activities, the researchers were the hosts and did not share personal stories and experiences about international education, mobilities, and decision-making processes. The participants shared their stories and experiences with other participants who also decided to study in Australia. Many participants received some insights and expanded their understanding and beliefs based on other participants’ stories. The participants indicated that they shared their meaningful experiences and stories with the researchers. The focus group activities lasted from 132 to 153 min without any breaks. All participants attended the entire duration.

Third, once the participants completed their semi-structured interview sessions and focus group activity, the researchers gathered the relevant information and data based on the participant's personal files. All the voiced messages were transcribed into written transcripts for data analysis. The researchers sent the personal file to each individual participant for confirmation. The member-checking interview sessions were conducted for each participant in order to confirm the data. More importantly, the researchers needed to ask permission for further studies and steps. All participants agreed with their data.

Please note that the researchers offered both face-to-face and online data collection procedures and arrangements to the participants. However, due to the social distancing recommendation, geographic challenges, and the requests from the participants, the participants could only attend and join the online data collection procedure (i.e., WeChat and Zoom). All participants agreed with the online-based data collection procedures.

Triangulation and trustworthiness

Triangulation is an important element for many qualitative studies to confirm trustworthiness and content validity [45]. Currently, the researchers employed (1) semi-structured interview sessions [45], (2) focus group activities [50], and (3) member-checking interview sessions [44] in order to confirm and reduce the subjectivities of the qualitative materials. Therefore, based on this arrangement, the current qualitative materials should be useful for this study.

Data analysis

The general inductive approach [40] and grounded theory approach [43] were used to narrow down the massive data to meaningful themes and subthemes. First, based on the open-coding technique, the researchers re-read the written transcripts multiple times to categorise the potential themes and subthemes. From this stage, 20 themes and 21 subthemes were categorised. However, further analysis should be conducted based on the axial-coding technique. With the 20 themes and 21 subthemes, the researchers further categorised the themes and subthemes. As a result, three themes and three subthemes were yielded.

Human subject protection

Privacy is the most important factor in this study. Therefore, the researchers kept the signed agreements, research protocols, personal contacts, voiced messages, written transcripts, computers, and relevant materials in a password-protected cabinet. Only the researchers could read the materials. Once the study was completed, the researchers deleted and destroyed the materials to protect the privacy of all parties. The study was approved by the institutional review department at Woosong University (#2022-03-11/11-AB) in March 2022. All participants signed the consent forms and agreed to join the study. The participants could withdraw from the study without any penalty.

Discussion

Previous studies [1,[54], [55], [56], [57]] have indicated that the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, European countries, and Australia are popular educational destinations for international students, regardless of their place of origin. Individuals have their own preferences regardless of external factors. Australia has become the preferred choice for some Chinese postgraduate international students [6,58]. Although border restrictions prevented a group of international students from choosing Australian universities, the restrictions did not play a role in the motivations, decisions, and decision-making processes of the Chinese postgraduate international students in this study [59].

First, financial challenges and factors [18,19] could impact individuals’ motivations and decision-making processes, particularly for international students [[60], [61], [62]]. In this study, financial considerations played a significant role in the decisions and decision-making processes about mobility in the case of Chinese postgraduate international students in Australia. The participants expressed that Australian living costs, rental fees, and university tuition fees were affordable for most middle-class Chinese families [15,25]. Moreover, the participants indicated that there was a favourable return-on-investment for Australian education. Although participants received admission offers from other countries, a group of them could not afford the living costs and tuition fees in alternative destinations, such as the United States. Australia, therefore, became one of the only options, particularly from the financial point of view [25,35,63].

Second, academic considerations played a significant role in international students' motivations, decisions, and decision-making processes [18,19]. For example, some studies [64,65] indicated that the academic reputation of universities could impact international students' motivations and decision-making processes. In this study, a large group of participants shared their desire to receive training and education from one of the Group of Eight universities in Australia, with a group indicating that their reputation was their only priority for selecting these institutions. In other words, the participants decided to go to Australia for their qualifications due to the reputation of the Group of Eight universities [12,15,27,66]. A group of participants said that the Group of Eight universities' networks, professional recognition, and alumni connections were significant in their decisions and decision-making processes. The participants believed the Australian universities’ reputations and international profiles could help them achieve upper management positions and better career profiles [67].

Besides external and environmental factors, such as alumni networks and the reputation of the universities, the participants indicated that they wanted to gain excellent training, vocational skills, and personal development from their Australian universities [68]. A group of participants believed that, although the tuition fees and living costs in Australia are lower than in other countries, such as the United States and the United Kingdom, the positive achievements they could gain from their degree programmes are equivalent to others internationally [15,69].

Third, personal considerations and immigration visas also played a significant role in the motivations, decisions, and decision-making processes of the participants [18,19]. According to the social cognitive career and motivation theory [18,19], personal considerations, elements, and factors could be very personal. In other words, both internal and external personal considerations could impact international students' motivations and decision-making processes [[70], [71], [72]]. Traditionally, Australia welcomes skilled professionals to stay for long-term development, particularly Australian-trained graduates who understand the country's culture and society. Therefore, many international students apply for their Temporary Graduate Visa (Subclass 485) or one of the skilled visas, such as the Subclass 189 visa, after university graduation [73]. In this study, a group of participants indicated they wanted to stay in Australia for at least two years to pursue postgraduate training and career opportunities.

It is common for international students to gain a few years of international experience to develop their knowledge and apply their skills in the local community, regardless of the location [65,74]. Skilled professionals with Australian qualifications are no exception to this. Some participants in this study stated that they might return to their home country after a few years of local work experience, as they wanted to apply the skills acquired in Australia to contribute to a better environment in China [23,75,76]. Besides spending a few years in the host country for career development, the participants indicated that they would like to stay in Australia for long-term development and gain permanent residency based on their Australian qualifications. Australia welcomes skilled candidates for career and personal development, particularly those from the surrounding regions [[77], [78], [79]]. In this case, a group of participants expressed their desire to stay after completing their Australian qualifications.

Limitations and further research

There are four limitations and future research directions are categorised. First, lockdown and social distancing recommendations do not only impact border control and management in Australia. Many other countries and regions faced the same problems due to the COVID-19 pandemic. However, the current study could only focus on one country for an in-depth study. Future research directions may expand this study and use it as a reference to collect and gather data from their targeted countries and regions.

Second, only Chinese postgraduate international students’ voices and comments were captured. However, not only Chinese postgraduate international students are in Australia. Many international students joined Australian universities for higher education. However, due to the limited resources, only Chinese international students might join the study to share. Future research studies may expand the population to other international students from other parts of the world.

Third, the COVID-19 pandemic significantly impacted international student mobility, regardless of their place of origin or education destinations. In order to continue their university education, many universities decided to offer online completion plans for students who could not come back on-campus for their degree programmes. However, some domestic students continued to join the on-campus courses with limited access. Further investigations can collect data from domestic students who want to complete their degrees via other alternatives.

Fourth, participants in different majors and age groups could have different perspectives and motivations. However, this study only captured the qualitative data from international students without the information of their majors and actual age. Therefore, future research studies could further study international students in different majors and age groups in order to have a better understanding of the issues.

Contribution to practice

First, international students’ behaviour and mobility are important factors and directions for many university leaders, department heads, government agencies, and non-profit organisations to manage the policies, regulations, and plans for students from different parts of the global communities. Based on the qualitative data from this study, the relevant personnel can use this study as a reference to establish additional plans and programmes for international students who decide to study and continue their education in Australia.

Second, the qualitative data from this study is rich, which can capture the in-depth understanding and comments from the participants, in this case, Chinese postgraduate international students. Based on the qualitative data, many researchers and university leaders can understand the stories and experiences and potential students from the global communities. As many universities want to establish sociocultural diversity for both domestic and international students who want to study in a safe and rich campus environment, university leaders may use this study as a reference to reform and upgrade their policies for cultural diversity, particularly in the Australian university environment.

Third, many universities offer counselling services to international students, minority students, and students with special needs. Based on the results from this study, counselling centres and experts may use this study as a reference to establish new counselling services and plans for international students, particularly during and after the COVID-19 pandemic.
