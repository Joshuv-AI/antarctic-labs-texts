Abstract
. . Consequently, by the global regularity result (Theorem 1.1) of
[
3
]
, the sequence
\{w_{n}\}
is bounded in
C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
. By the compact embedding, upto a subsequence,
\{w_{n}\}
converges in
C^{1,\alpha^{\prime}}(\overline{\Omega})
for some
0<\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
. Therefore, passing to the limit in (
5.1
) as
n\rightarrow\infty
and using the uniqueness of solutions, we obtain
w_{n}\rightarrow w
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. In fact, every subsequence of
\{w_{n}\}
has a further subsequence converging to
w
in
C^{1,\alpha^{\prime}}(\overline{\Omega})
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Consequently, the map
T:X\rightarrow X
is continuous.

To prove the compactness let us take a bounded sequence
\{u_{n}\}
in
X
and let
T(u_{n})=w_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then a similar argument yields that
T:X\rightarrow C^{1,\alpha^{\prime}}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})
is compact. Since
C^{1,\alpha^{\prime}}_{0}(\overline{\Omega})\hookrightarrow C_{0}(\overline{\Omega})
and
T(X)\subset X
, it follows that the map
T:X\rightarrow X
is completely continuous.

Next, we make the following claim and proving this will confirm that all hypotheses of Proposition
5.1
are satisfied.

Claim- 2:
There exist open subsets
U_{1},U_{2}
of
X
such that
U_{1}\subset X_{1}
,
U_{2}\subset X_{2}
and
T
has no fixed points in
(X_{1}\setminus U_{1})\cup(X_{2}\setminus U_{2})
.

Consider the set
X_{1}
and from equation (
3
) and part (b) of Theorem
1.2
recall that the functions
\phi_{1}
and
z_{1}
satisfy the following equations:

\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}\phi_{1}+(-\Delta_{p})^{s}\phi_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda f(\phi_{1})-\epsilon_{1}\text{ in }\Omega

\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}z_{1}+(-\Delta_{p})^{s}z_{1}

\displaystyle=\lambda f(z_{1})\text{ in }\Omega.

Therefore, due to the monotonicity of
f
, we have

-\Delta_{p}\phi_{1}+(-\Delta_{p})^{s}\phi_{1}\leq-\Delta_{p}z_{1}+(-\Delta_{p})^{s}z_{1}\leq K\text{ in }\Omega

where
K=\lambda f(\|\psi_{2}\|_{\infty})
. Then, by Proposition
5.2
, we obtain
z_{1}-\phi_{1}\in C_{e}^{+}(\overline{\Omega})
. Similarly, we get
\psi_{2}-z_{1}\in C_{e}^{+}(\overline{\Omega})
. Now, since
z_{1}
is the maximal positive solution of (

P
λ

) in
X_{1}
, we define
U_{1}
to be the largest open set in
X_{1}
containing
z_{1}
such that
X_{1}\setminus U_{1}
contains no fixed point of the map
T
. Similarly, we define
U_{2}
to be the largest open set in
X_{2}
containing
z_{2}
such that

Therefore, by Proposition
5.1
,
T
has another fixed point
z_{3}\in X\setminus(X_{1}\cup X_{2})
. Consequently, the problem (

P
λ

) has three distinct positive solutions
z_{1}\in X_{1}
,
z_{2}\in X_{2}
and
z_{3}\in X\setminus(X_{1}\cup X_{2})
whenever
\lambda\in(\lambda_{*},\lambda^{*})
. ∎

6.
A Third Solution in The Singular Case for The Linear Operator

In this case, (

P
λ

) simplifies to the following form:

(6.1)

\displaystyle-\Delta u+(-\Delta)^{s}u=\lambda\frac{f(u)}{u^{\beta}}\text{ in }\Omega

\displaystyle u>0\text{ in }\Omega,\ u=0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega

where
0<\beta<1
. While the overall idea remains similar to that employed in the previous section, the inclusion of the singular term introduces additional challenges requiring more refined analysis. From the proof of Theorem
1.3
, it is evident that the main difficulty lies in proving the strongly increasing nature of the map
T
, that is, if
u_{1}\leq u_{2}
with
u_{1}\not\equiv u_{2}
in
\Omega
, then
T(u_{2})-T(u_{1})\in\ C_{e}^{+}(\overline{\Omega})
. This stronger condition, beyond mere monotonicity, is essential for verifying the hypotheses of Amann’s fixed point theorem, specifically Claim- 2 in the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

In
[
15
]
, the authors successfully handled the singular case (
0<\beta<1
) for the fractional Laplacian by analyzing an infinite semipositone problem. However, even in this linear case, substantial analysis was required to obtain a positive solution, behaving like the function
d^{s}(x)
, for the corresponding infinite semipositone problem. Next, weak comparison principle is applied to show that this solution acts as a lower bound for
T(u_{2})-T(u_{1})
in
\Omega
and consequently the map
T
becomes strongly increasing.

Although a similar approach can be adapted to derive a Hopf-type Strong Comparison Principle (Hopf-type SCP) for singular problems involving the mixed local-nonlocal Laplacian, here we provide an independent proof for the same (Theorem
6.3
). The proof begins by establishing a strict comparison between the functions, followed by a strict comparison between their normal derivatives on the boundary of
\Omega
. The key idea is to construct a function
v
, inspired by Dhanya-Ko-Shivaji
[
12
]
, that mimics the behavior of
d(x)
near
\partial\Omega
and satisfies
T(u_{2})-T(u_{1})\geq Cv(x)
in
\Omega
for some
C>0
. We now introduce the following lemma, whose proof closely follows Theorem 2.8 of
[
11
]
. For brevity, we state the lemma without reproducing its proof.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
u_{1},u_{2}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
, for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
u_{1},u_{2}>0
in
\Omega
satisfy

\displaystyle-\Delta u_{1}+(-\Delta)^{s}u_{1}-\frac{1}{u_{1}^{\beta}}=f_{1}\text{ in }\Omega

\displaystyle-\Delta u_{2}+(-\Delta)^{s}u_{2}-\frac{1}{u_{2}^{\beta}}=f_{2}\text{ in }\Omega

\displaystyle u_{1}=u_{2}=0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega

where
f_{1},f_{2}
are continuous functions with
0\leq f_{1}\leq f_{2}
,
f_{1}\not\equiv 0
and
f_{1}\not\equiv f_{2}
in
\Omega
. Then
u_{1}<u_{2}
in
\Omega
.

Next, we present an important observation concerning the
C^{1,\alpha}
-extension of a suitable function to the entire
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. This extension serves as a key component in proving Theorem
6.3
(Hopf-type SCP).

We use the following notations: for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
we write
x=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,

x_{N})=(x^{\prime},x_{N})
and define
\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:x_{N}>0\}
and
\mathbb{R}^{N}_{-}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:x_{N}<0\}
. For any
r>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
B_{r}(x)
denotes the ball of radius
r
centered at
x
with
B_{r}^{+}(x)=B_{r}(x)\cap\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}
and
B_{r}^{-}(x)=B_{r}(x)\cap\mathbb{R}^{N}_{-}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
e_{0}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
, for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, be the unique solution of

(6.2)

\displaystyle-\Delta e_{0}

\displaystyle=1\text{ in }\Omega

\displaystyle e_{0}

\displaystyle=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega.

Then there exists an extension
\tilde{e}_{0}
of
e_{0}
such that
\tilde{e}_{0}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
\tilde{e}_{0}=e_{0}
in
\overline{\Omega}
and
\tilde{e}_{0}\leq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
.

Proof.

Let
x_{0}
be a point on
\partial\Omega
. At first, we extend the function
e_{0}
in a neighborhood of the point
x_{0}
by using the straightening of boundary technique. Since
\partial\Omega
is smooth, for chosen
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
, there exist
r_{0}>0
and a
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism
\Psi_{x_{0}}:B_{r_{0}}(x_{0})\rightarrow\Psi\big(B_{r_{0}}(x_{0})\big)
such that

\displaystyle\Psi_{x_{0}}\big(\Omega\cap B_{r_{0}}(x_{0})\big)

\displaystyle\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}

\displaystyle\text{and }\Psi_{x_{0}}\big(\partial\Omega\cap B_{r_{0}}(x_{0})\big)

\displaystyle\subset\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:x_{N}=0\}.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\Psi_{x_{0}}(x_{0})=0
and
B_{1}^{+}(0)\subset

\Psi_{x_{0}}\big(B_{r_{0}}(x_{0})\big)
.

Define
q_{x_{0}}(y):=(e\circ\Psi_{x_{0}}^{-1})(y)
, for
y\in B_{1}^{+}(0)
. We extend
q_{x_{0}}(y)
to a function
\tilde{q}_{x_{0}}(y)
defined on the entire
B_{1}(0)
by the method of reflection. Let
y=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{N})=(y^{\prime},y_{N})\in B_{1}(0)
. Now for
y_{N}<0
we define

\tilde{q}_{x_{0}}(y^{\prime},y_{N})=-\tilde{q}_{x_{0}}(y^{\prime},-y_{N}),\text{ for }y\in B_{1}^{-}(0).

With this definition, it is now straightforward to check that
\tilde{q}_{x_{0}}\in C^{1,\alpha}(B_{1}(0))
. Therefore,
\tilde{e}_{x_{0}}(x)=(\tilde{q}_{x_{0}}\circ\Psi_{x_{0}})(x)
is a
C^{1,\alpha}
-extension of
e_{0}
to
\Psi_{x_{0}}^{-1}(B_{1}(0))
. Moreover, it is evident from the definition that
\tilde{e}_{x_{0}}\leq 0
in
\Psi_{x_{0}}^{-1}(B_{1}^{-}(0))
.

Since
\partial\Omega
is compact, let
\big\{U_{i}=\Psi_{x_{i}}^{-1}(B_{1}(0)):i=1,2,\dots,k\big\}
be a finite subcover of
\partial\Omega
, where
x_{i}\in\partial\Omega
,
i=1,2,\dots,k
. Let
U_{0}=\Omega
. Then
\big\{U_{i}\big\}_{i=0}^{k}
is a cover of
\overline{\Omega}
. Consider a smooth partition of unity
\{\xi_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}
subordinate to this cover
\{U_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}
, that is,
\xi_{i}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(U_{i})
satisfying
0\leq\xi_{i}\leq 1
,
i=0,1,2,\dots,k
and
\sum_{i=0}^{k}\xi_{i}=1
on
\overline{\Omega}
. Let
\tilde{e}_{x_{i}}
be the
C^{1,\alpha}
-extension of
e_{0}
to
U_{i}
,
i=1,2,\dots,k
as constructed above. Define

\tilde{e}_{0}:=\xi_{0}e_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\xi_{i}\tilde{e}_{x_{i}}.

Then, clearly
\tilde{e}_{0}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
with
\tilde{e}_{0}=e_{0}
in
\overline{\Omega}
, there exists an open set
\Omega_{1}
such that
\Omega\Subset\Omega_{1}
,
\tilde{e}_{0}\leq 0
in
\Omega_{1}\setminus\Omega
and
\tilde{e}_{0}=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega_{1}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 6.3

(
Hopf-type Strong Comparison Principle
)
.

Let
u_{1},u_{2}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
, for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
u_{1},u_{2}>0
in
\Omega
satisfy

(6.3)

\displaystyle-\Delta u_{1}+(-\Delta)^{s}u_{1}-\frac{1}{u_{1}^{\beta}}=f_{1}\text{ in }\Omega

\displaystyle-\Delta u_{2}+(-\Delta)^{s}u_{2}-\frac{1}{u_{2}^{\beta}}=f_{2}\text{ in }\Omega

\displaystyle u_{1}=u_{2}=0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega

where
f_{1},f_{2}
are continuous functions with
0\leq f_{1}\leq f_{2}
,
f_{1}\not\equiv 0
and
f_{1}\not\equiv f_{2}
in
\Omega
. Then
\frac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial\nu}<\frac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial\nu}<0
on
\partial\Omega
.

Proof.

We first establish that
\frac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial\nu}\leq\frac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial\nu}<0
on
\partial\Omega
. Subsequently, we perform a more detailed analysis to show that
u_{2}-u_{1}
is bounded below by a constant multiple of the distance function
d(x)
, thereby completing the proof.

Claim- 1:

\frac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial\nu}\leq\frac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial\nu}<0
on
\partial\Omega
.

From lemma
6.1
,
u_{2}>u_{1}
on
\Omega
and
u_{1}=u_{2}=0
on
\partial\Omega
so that
\frac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial\nu}\leq\frac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial\nu}
on
\partial\Omega
. Thus it remains to show that
\frac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial\nu}<0
on
\partial\Omega
. Since
u_{1}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
, there exists a constant
\eta>0
such that
\frac{1}{u^{\beta}_{1}}\geq\eta
in
\Omega
. Let
e
denote the solution to (
3.1
) for
p=2
, the case under consideration. Since
f_{1}
is non-negative in
\Omega
, we have

-\Delta u_{1}+(-\Delta)^{s}u_{1}=f_{1}+\frac{1}{u^{\beta}_{1}}\geq\eta=-\Delta(\eta e)+(-\Delta)^{s}(\eta e)\text{ in }\Omega

with
u_{1}=\eta e=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
. Thus, by weak comparison principle,
u_{1}\geq\eta e
in
\Omega
. Now, by Theorem 1.2 of
[
3
]
, we have
\frac{\partial e}{\partial\nu}<0
on
\partial\Omega
and hence
\frac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial\nu}\leq\frac{\partial(\eta e)}{\partial\nu}<0
on
\partial\Omega
.

We now refine the result and obtain a strict inequality between the normal derivatives of
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
on
\partial\Omega
.

Claim- 2:

\frac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial\nu}<\frac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial\nu}
on
\partial\Omega
.

Define
w:=u_{2}-u_{1}
. Then
w>0
in
\Omega
, by lemma
6.1
. Now by the mean value theorem, there exists a function
\xi\in[u_{1},u_{2}]
such that
w
satisfies the following:

-\Delta w+(-\Delta)^{s}w+\frac{\beta}{\xi(x)^{\beta+1}}w=f_{2}-f_{1}\geq 0\text{ in }\Omega.

Since
u_{1},u_{2}
satisfy (
6.3
),
u_{1},u_{2}\sim d(x)
in
\Omega
. Thus there exists
k>0
such that

(6.4)

-\Delta w+(-\Delta)^{s}w+\frac{k}{d(x)^{\beta+1}}w\geq 0\text{ in }\Omega.

Define
v(x):=\tilde{e}_{0}(x)+e_{0}(x)^{\gamma}
, where
\tilde{e}_{0}
is the extension given by lemma
6.2
,
e_{0}
(by abuse of notation) denotes the function solving (
6.2
) with zero extension in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega
and the exponent
\gamma\in(1,2)
is to be chosen later. Then

(6.5)

-\Delta v+\frac{k}{d(x)^{\beta+1}}v=1+\gamma\tilde{e}_{0}^{\gamma-1}-\gamma(\gamma-1)e_{0}^{\gamma-2}|\nabla e_{0}|^{2}+\frac{k}{d(x)^{\beta+1}}(\tilde{e}_{0}+e_{0}^{\gamma})\text{ in }\Omega.

Since
\tilde{e}_{0}=e_{0}\sim d(x)
in
\Omega
and
\inf_{\partial\Omega}\big|\frac{\partial e_{0}}{\partial\nu}\big|>0
, choosing
\eta>0
small enough and
\gamma\in(1,2-\beta)
, we obtain

-\Delta v+\frac{k}{d(x)^{\beta+1}}v\leq-\frac{k_{1}}{d(x)^{2-\gamma}}\text{ in }\Omega_{\eta}

where
\Omega_{\eta}=\{x\in\Omega:d(x)<\eta\}
and
k_{1}>0
is a constant. Moreover, since
v\in C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, by Proposition 2.6 of
[
20
]
, there exists a constant
k_{2}>0
such that
|(-\Delta)^{s}v|\leq k_{2}
in
\overline{\Omega}_{\eta}
. Therefore, choosing
\eta
smaller if required, we get

(6.6)

-\Delta v+(-\Delta)^{s}v+\frac{k}{d(x)^{\beta+1}}v\leq 0\text{ in }\Omega_{\eta}.

Since by lemma
6.1
,
w>0
in
\Omega
, we choose
\epsilon>0
small enough so that
\epsilon v\leq w
in
\Omega\setminus\Omega_{\eta}
. Then, from (
6.4
) and (
6.6
) we obtain

(6.7)

\displaystyle-\Delta w+(-\Delta)^{s}w+\frac{k}{d(x)^{\beta+1}}w

\displaystyle\geq 0\text{ in }\Omega_{\eta}

\displaystyle-\Delta(\epsilon v)+(-\Delta)^{s}(\epsilon v)+\frac{k}{d(x)^{\beta+1}}(\epsilon v)

\displaystyle\leq 0\text{ in }\Omega_{\eta}

\displaystyle w\geq\epsilon v\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega_{\eta}.

Thus, by weak comparison principle, we have
w\geq\epsilon v=\epsilon(\tilde{e}_{0}+e_{0}^{\gamma})
in
\Omega_{\eta}
. Since
\gamma>1
, there exists
k_{3}>0
such that
w(x)\geq k_{3}d(x)
whenever
d(x)
is sufficiently small and hence
\frac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial\nu}<\frac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial\nu}
on
\partial\Omega
. This completes the proof.
∎

Now, we are in a position to present the proof of Theorem
1.4
. While the central idea mirrors that of Theorem
1.3
, we provide a concise outline for clarity and completeness, highlighting the key differences and modifications required to address the current scenario.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
:
We fix
\lambda\in(\lambda_{*},\lambda^{*})
and define the sets
X
,
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
analogously to the proof of Theorem
1.3
. Let
z_{1}
be the maximal positive solution of (

) in

and
z_{2}
be the minimal positive solution in

, obtained by the monotone iteration method.

In view of the boundary regularity result (Theorem 2.3) of
[

]
for singular problems, the proof of Claim-1, that is,

is completely continuous, proceeds analogously. Similarly, Claim-2 follows by the direct application of the Hopf-type SCP presented in Theorem

As a result, the conditions for Proposition

are satisfied and we obtain a third fixed point

Concluding Remark

In essence, Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.4
establish three-solution theorems in two special cases: the non-singular case (
1<p<\infty
and
\beta=0
) and the singular case with the linear operator (
p=2
and
0<\beta<1
) respectively. However, in the general case when
p\not=2
and
0<\beta<1
, we could only obtain two positive solutions. Thus, the existence of a third solution for the following equation

(6.8)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}u+(-\Delta_{p})^{s}u

\displaystyle=\lambda\frac{f(u)}{u^{\beta}}\text{ in }\Omega,\text{ with }p\not=2,\ 0<\beta<1

\displaystyle u>0\text{ in }\Omega,\ u

\displaystyle=0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega

still remains an open question. We emphasize that the main difficulty in establishing the third solution is proving the Hopf-type Strong Comparison Principle for the nonlinear operator, as Theorem
6.3
only addresses the linear case. Given the sub- and supersolutions developed in Section
3
and the recently established boundary regularity results (
[
11
]
,
[
6
]
), if the Hopf-type Strong Comparison Principle (Hopf-type SCP) for mixed local-nonlocal operators is available for singular problems, one can prove the three-solution theorem for the above equation (
6.8
). The proof of the existence of third solution will follow the arguments similar to that of Theorem
1.4
. Furthermore, the Hopf-type SCP is significant in its own right and establishing this could also pave the way for further advancements in the study of multiplicity results involving mixed operators.
