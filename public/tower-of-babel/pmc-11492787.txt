Multi-omics profiling reveals the molecular mechanisms of H2O2-induced detrimental effects on Thamnaconus septentrionalis
Authors: Chengcheng Li, Xuanxuan Zhang, Linlin Zhao, Shenghao Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHydrogen peroxide (H2O2), a novel water treatment agent, can be used for disinfection, water quality adjustment, and disease prevention, while excessive H2O2 can injure farm animals, even leading to death. Hydrogen peroxide is a recommended disinfectant and bactericide for treating gill diseases and vibriosis in the greenfin horse-faced filefish Thamnaconus septentrionalis. However, its cumulative effect, toxic molecular mechanism and relevant signal transduction/metabolic networks in marine fishes are largely unknown.ResultsWe employed a multi-omics approach to investigate the detrimental effects of 50 mg/L H2O2 exposure (2 h/d) on filefish for 2 d, 4 d, and 6 d. Transcriptome sequencing showed that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were mainly classified into functions such as signal transduction, nervous system, liver and bile acid metabolism, energy metabolism, cell adhesion and communication, inflammation and immune response. Metabolomic analysis found that the significantly changed metabolites (SCMs) were involved in phenylalanine metabolism, inflammatory mediator regulation, linoleic acid metabolism, and necroptosis. The main SCMs were cholic acid, carnitine C12:1, dimethylmalonic acid, glutamic acid, L-lactic acid, shikimic acid, 2-methylsuccinic acid, and others. Moreover, H2O2-induced oxidative stress also disturbs the balance of the gut microbiota, altering the microbial composition and affecting digestive processes.ConclusionsIntegrated multiomics analysis revealed that H2O2-induced detrimental impacts include mucosal damage, inflammatory and immune responses, altered energy metabolism, and gut microbiota disorders. These findings offer novel insights into the harmful effects and signal transduction/metabolic pathways triggered by H2O2 exposure in marine fishes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-10903-0.Keywords: Hydrogen peroxide, Oxidative stress, Transcriptome sequencing, Metabolomic analysis, Macrogenomic sequencing

Introduction

The greenfin horse-faced filefish (Thamnaconus septentrionalis, hereafter referred to as “filefish”) is an important commercial fish species found in the Indo-West Pacific Ocean. Filefish meat is tender and high in protein, making it a nutritious and popular aquatic product. The muscles and liver contain a large amount of taurine, which can lower blood pressure [1]. Many researchers have studied their nutrient composition[1], growth morphology [2], and multigene bioinformatics [3]. However, due to overfishing, environmental pollution and habitat destruction, the natural resources of filefish have declined considerably. Since filefish have the advantages of fast growth and easy management, researchers have carried out studies on the artificial reproduction and culture of filefish and have made initial progress [4].

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), a kind of reactive oxygen species (ROS), is classified as a strong oxidizing agent [5]. H2O2 has significant potential in aquaculture due to its ability to enhance water quality by effectively oxidizing organic matter [6], detoxifying harmful substances [7], increasing oxygen levels [8], and acting as an alternative disinfectant [9]. H2O2 serves as both a disinfectant and bactericide, applied either directly or adjunctively in disease treatment, thereby mitigating disease outbreaks. H2O2 is an effective disinfectant and bactericide for controlling Vibrio anguillarum in Japanese flounder and gill flukes in filefish species like Takifugu rubripes, Pervagor aspricaudus and Filefish [6, 8, 9]. Dosage levels are critical when used in recirculating aquaculture systems [9, 10]. H2O2 is frequently applied as a bath treatment throughout the aquaculture industry for amoebic gill disease with doses 1000 mg/L for > 10 min by salmon farmers [11]. Treatment with high dosages of H2O2 ranging from 1.5 ~ 2.0 g/L is also a typical practice against ectoparasites, however, if not used correctly, it will be accompanied by severe toxicity, stress and detoxification responses in fish [12]. Salmonids have been found to have safe values between 70 ~ 100 mg/L of H2O2 with exposure times up to two hours, whereas juvenile shrimp had safe concentrations of 14.3 μL/L (using 29 μL/L equivalent to 100 mg/L) [13]. A high concentration of H2O2 (200 mg/L) resulted in gill injury and substantial mortality in rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss) after 1 ~ 1.5 h of exposure [14], whereas a relative low concentration of H2O2 (50 mg/L) induced a rapid biological stress response in sea bass with higher levels of plasma cortisol, glucose, and lactate, which appears to require more than 24 h to fully recover [15]. H2O2 exposure also disrupts the gut microbial community composition. After exposure to H2O2, the gut community of lake sturgeon larvae is dominated by Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteria [16]. However, the cumulative effect and toxic molecular mechanism of H2O2 in aquatic environments are still largely unknown. An in-depth study is essential to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying H2O2's effects on oxidative stress, energy metabolism, and immune suppression in marine fish species. Findings will be crucial for optimizing H2O2 applications and ensuring sustainable development in aquaculture.

This study aimed to reveal the gene‒metabolite-microbiota regulatory relationship in response to H2O2 stress through multi-omics technology and to assess the detrimental effects of H2O2 exposure on gill and intestinal tissues. We conducted the H2O2 exposure experiment with 50 mg/L of H2O2 for 2 h each day for either 2, 4, or 6 days, and then observed changes in the gene expression, metabolites and microbes. These findings offer new insights into the toxic effects and signal transduction/metabolic pathways of H2O2 exposure in aquatic environments.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and sampling

Filefish (six months old with a body length of 15 ± 2 cm and a weight of 100 ± 5 g) were obtained from Qingdao Haichang Polar Ocean World. These fish were obtained from the same batch of hatched healthy individuals. They were acclimated to laboratory conditions for two weeks in water tanks (72.2 cm × 52.5 cm × 43.3 cm, 110 L) that were equipped with inflatable filtration systems. The animals were placed under natural light with a 12-h light/12-h dark regime during the acclimation and the experiment of H2O2 exposure. The water conditions were as follows: temperature, 21.5 ± 1.0 °C; dissolved oxygen, 7.5 ± 1.0 mg/L; salinity, 32‰; and pH, 7.5 ± 0.1. To maintain normal vital functions, the fish were given frozen brine shrimp once daily at 9:00 at a rate of approximately 3% of their body weight (i.e., 3 g). The seawater was entirely changed after feeding or H2O2 treatment to maintain water quality.

Forty adult fish were randomly assigned to four experimental groups, irrespective of their gender. Each treatment group consisted of 10 fish. H2O2 treatments were applied by stopping the filtration and aeration system and adding hydrogen peroxide to give a concentration of 50 mg/L (i.e., 1.47 mM) of H2O2 for 2 h each day (Fig. 1A). The H2O2 concentration was normal in accordance with earlier publications [10, 15, 17]. The control group received the same treatment as the exposure group with the exception that H2O2 was not added to the tank. After 2 h of treatment, the inflatable filtration systems were turned back on. During the treatment of H2O2, filefish are not fed with food. Four groups of fish were randomly sampled after the following treatments: (i) control group: filtered sea water, (ii) H2O2.2d group: 50 mg/L H2O2 for 2 h/day lasting for 2 days, (iii) H2O2.4d group: 50 mg/L H2O2 for 2 h/day lasting for 4 days, (iv) H2O2.6d group: 50 mg/L H2O2 for 2 h/day lasting for 6 days. Filefish from the exposure and control groups were selected and dissected at the same time, and filefish from exposure group were sampled immediately after 2 h of H2O2 treatment. To avoid the effect of circadian rhythm, filefish from each group were sampled at 6:00 p.m. Gills or intestines from two individuals were mixed as one parallel sample. Each group have three parallel replicates. The gills were collected for biochemical parameter assays, transcriptome sequencing, and widely targeted metabolome analyses, and the intestines were used for gut microbiota analyses. Samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen and then stored at − 80 °C. Given care for animal welfare, the fish were quickly anesthetized with MS-222 (100 ppm, Sigma, MO, USA). All experimental protocols strictly followed the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (No. YZ202334, Ministry of Science and Technology of China, 2006) and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the First Institute of Oceanography, MNR (Qingdao, China). There were no fish deaths throughout the experiment, except for sampling.

Biochemical parameter analysis

Gill tissues were pulverized into powder by freezing them in liquid nitrogen. Commercial kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China, were used to analyze the biochemical characteristics. In brief, 0.1 g of the powdered sample was mixed with an appropriate extraction buffer to create a 10% tissue homogenate. The supernatant was obtained by centrifugation (5000 × g, 10 min, 4℃), and utilized for biochemical analysis following the kit instructions. The malondialdehyde (MDA) content was quantified using a thiobarbituric acid assay (product no. A003-2–3). Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined via the xanthine-xanthine oxidase-nitro blue tetrazolium assay (product no. A001-3). Catalase (CAT) activity was detected by the colorimetric ammonium molybdate method (product no. A007-1–1). Glutathione peroxidase (GSH-PX) facilitated the reaction between H2O2 and glutathione, generating water and oxidized glutathione. The speed of this enzymatic reaction served as a measure of GSH-PX activity (product no. A005-1). The hydroxyl radical scavenging ability (OH−) was assessed based on the decrease in absorbance of the chromogenic substance ortho phenanthrene using the Fenton reaction (product no. A018-1–1). The total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) was evaluated through the ferric-reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) method (Product No. A015-1–2). Acid phosphatase (ACP) or alkaline phosphatase (AKP) catalyze disodium phosphate, leading to the production of free phenols and phosphate. The phenol then reacted with 4-aminoantipyrine in an alkaline solution and was oxidized by potassium ferrocyanide to form a red quinone derivative. Enzyme activity was determined based on absorbance measurements (Product No. A060-1–1 and A059-1–1). Each experiment was conducted as three biological replicates.

RNA isolation and UMI library preparation

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA), followed by assessment of RNA degradation via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and determination of RNA purity and concentration using a NanoPhotometer® spectrophotometer (Implen, München, Germany). mRNA was isolated and purified from the total RNA using mRNA Capture Beads (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China). Subsequently, the mRNA was fragmented to a size range of 100 ~ 200 nt using fragmentation buffer and subjected to reverse transcription into cDNA. The synthesized cDNA was then purified using DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme Biotech).

To minimize sequence-dependent bias and amplification noise, we employed a sequencing strategy incorporating optimized single-molecule barcodes [18, 19]. Purified cDNA was ligated to an adaptor containing a unique molecular identifier (UMI) and further purified using DNA clean beads. cDNA libraries were generated using PCR amplification (NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit, Ipswich, MA), and the quality of the resulting cDNA libraries was evaluated using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (DNA Technologies Core, Davis, CA).

Transcriptome sequencing, sequence assembly, and functional annotation

The libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating 150-nt paired-end reads with 6 Gb of raw data each. Raw reads in FASTQ format were processed using in-house scripts to remove adapters, poly-N-containing reads, and low-quality reads. High-quality clean reads were then used for downstream analyses. UMI reads were identified using UMI-tools v1.0.0 [20]. Duplicate reads were identified by first removing UMIs from each read and then analyzing the remaining parts. The quality of the sequencing data was assessed, including the raw reads, clean reads, Q20/Q30 ratio, UMI reads, UMI clean reads, and deduplicated mapped UMI reads. Alignment of sequencing reads to the reference genome of filefish was conducted using HISAT2 v2.1.0 [21]. Gene-specific read counts were determined using HTSeq v0.6.1 [22], and the expression levels of genes were quantified using fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differential expression analysis was conducted utilizing the DESeq R package v1.12.0 [23], and the DEGs were identified using an adjusted P value threshold of < 0.05. DEGs were further analyzed Gene Ontology (GO) using GOseq R package and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses using KOBAS v3.0 software.

Widely targeted metabolome analysis

Considering that the accumulation of metabolites requires a period of time, metabolite analysis was conducted using gill tissues obtained from the H2O2 exposure group (H2O2.6d) and control group (Control). The extraction, qualitative, and quantitative analysis of metabolites were carried out following standard protocols. Briefly, the samples were frozen using liquid nitrogen and ground, after which a 20 mg portion was mixed with an extraction solution (400 μL, methanol:water = 7:3, v/v) containing an internal standard. The mixture was shaken at 2500 rpm for 5 min and chilled on ice for 15 min. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min (4 °C), supernatant (300 μL) was collected and placed in − 20 °C for 30 min to promote precipitation. Next, the sample underwent another centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 3 min (4 °C), and a 200 μL portion of the supernatant was transferred for LC‒MS analysis. A UPLC system (ExionLC™ AD) and a QTRAP® System mass spectrometer (LC‒ESI‒MS/MS) were utilized for the LC‒MS analysis. To assess reproducibility, a quality control mixture prepared by combining extract samples was analyzed in parallel.

The raw data signals were processed using Analyst 1.6.3 software (AB Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA). To normalize the data, the original metabolite abundances underwent log transformation. For cultivar-specific metabolite accumulation analysis, principal component analysis (PCA), hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA), and orthogonal projections to latent structure-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were performed using R software v4.1.0 [24, 25]. To prevent overfitting, we conducted a permutation test with 200 iterations. The variable importance in projection (VIP) values were calculated for all metabolites in the OPLS-DA model to assess their relative importance. Significantly changed metabolites (SCMs) between the two groups (H2O2.6d vs. control) upon H2O2 exposure were identified based on a fold change ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5 and a VIP ≥ 1.

Gut DNA extraction and high-throughput sequencing

Intestinal tissues from the H2O2 exposure group (H2O2.6d) and control group (Control) were collected and subjected to metagenomic sequencing. Genomic DNA was extracted from the intestinal contents using a QIAamp DNA Extraction Kit (Cat. No: 51604; QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). The DNA concentration was determined using the Qubit® dsDNA Assay Kit on a Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, CA, USA). For each sample, 1 μg of DNA was used for library construction. Using the NEBNext® UltrTM DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA), we generated the sequencing libraries. Index codes were added to assign sequences to each sample. Briefly, DNA was sonicated to obtain fragments of approximately 350 bp, followed by end-polishing, A-tailing, and ligation with Illumina adaptors. PCR amplification was performed, and the resulting PCR products were purified using the AMPure XP system. Real-time PCR was used for library quantification, and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer was used to analyze the library size distribution. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform utilizing paired-end reads. The index-coded samples were clustered using a cBot Cluster Generation System according to the manufacturer's instructions. Following cluster generation, the library preparations were sequenced to generate paired-end reads.

Gut microbiota analysis and function prediction

Clean data were generated from the Illumina paired-end reads after quality control using FastQC software (Babraham Institute, Cambridge, UK). Contamination was removed by comparison with the host database via Bowtie2 v2.3.4 (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012). Assembly analysis of the clean data was performed using MEGAHIT v1.2.9 [26]. Bowtie2 v2.3.4 was used to compare the clean data with the contigs by extracting PE reads that were not utilized. Unutilized reads from each sample were combined for mixed assembly. MetaGeneMark predicted open reading frames (ORFs) based on contigs (≥ 500 bp). Predicted genes shorter than 100 nt were filtered out. Redundancy was removed using CD-HIT v4.8.1 [27], resulting in a nonredundant initial gene inventory. Gene abundances were calculated by Bowtie2 by comparing the clean data with the original gene catalog. Genes with ≤ 2 supporting reads were filtered out, yielding the gene catalog (unigenes). Unigenes were compared to those in the NCBI nr database using DIAMOND software (BLASTP, e-value ≤ 1E-5). The LCA algorithm in MEGAN v1.2.9 provided final species annotations. We acquired abundance data at each classification level based on LCA annotation and gene abundance tables. LEfSe and Metastat analyses were conducted based on functional abundance.

Statistical data analysis

The biochemical data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (San Diego, CA). The statistical significance of differences between different groups was determined using one-way ANOVA (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01). The multivariate statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using R software (version 4.0.2). The data are presented as the average ± standard deviation. Three biological replicates were used for each experiment.

Results

Determination of biochemical parameters upon H2O2 exposure

To determine the effects of H2O2 on marine fishes, the fish were exposed to 50 mg/L H2O2 for 2 h per day for 6 days. The biochemical parameters of the samples treated with H2O2 for 0 d, 2 d, 4 d, and 6 d were detected using commercial kits. Overall, the activities of antioxidant enzymes (i.e., SOD, CAT and GSH-PX) significantly increased under hydrogen peroxide treatment (Fig. 1B-D). The T-AOC increased at 2 d and 4 d and then significantly decreased at 6 d (Fig. 1E). The ability to inhibit OH production always showed a significant upward trend with a time-dependent increase, reaching its maximum at 4 d (Fig. 1F). The MDA content, which indicates lipid peroxidation in the cell membrane, first significantly decreased and subsequently dramatically increased with prolonged exposure to H2O2 (Fig. 1G). In addition, the activities of ACP and AKP were significantly decreased upon H2O2 exposure (Fig. 1H-I). Therefore, within a short period of time, filefish actively responded to H2O2 stress by enhancing their antioxidant enzyme activities.

Transcriptome analysis of filefish gill tissues upon H2O2 exposure

To determine the transcriptional profiles of filefish upon H2O2 exposure, we performed quantitative transcriptome sequencing analysis. Following quality trimming, a total of 80.39 Gb of clean reads were obtained from 12 samples, with UMI-labeled reads representing 95.32% to 96.82% of the clean reads. The Q30 percentage exceeded 91.00% for all the libraries, while the average GC content was 51.96%. Deduplicated UMI reads accounted for 66.13% to 76.43% of the UMI reads in the reference genome, and the average percentage of UMI clean reads mapped to the filefish genome was 88.34%. These results collectively indicated that the transcriptome data were of high quality.

To investigate the global gene expression patterns, we assessed the DEGs linked to filefish upon H2O2 exposure. A total of 3,315 DEGs (1,491 up- and 1,824 downregulated) in H2O2.2d vs control, 2,886 DEGs (1,363 up- and 1,523 downregulated) in H2O2.4d vs control, and 1,789 DEGs (807 up- and 982 downregulated) in H2O2.6d vs control were identified (Fig. 2A-C). Judging from the number of DEGs in the transcriptome, the response of filefish to H2O2 occurs more in the early stages of stress. Venn diagram showed that 685 DEGs were shared between the H2O2-treated groups (Fig. 2D). We also found that 1639, 1298, and 420 of DEGs were unique to the H2O2.2d vs. Control, H2O2.4d vs. Control, and H2O2.6d vs. Control groups, respectively. These results showed that a substantial number of genes are activated when filefish are under H2O2 stress.

Subsequently, GO functional enrichment analysis was conducted on the DEGs obtained from the different stress groups. The top 10 significantly enriched GO terms were associated with molecular function, biological processes, and cellular composition (Fig. 2E, Fig. S1). GO functional enrichment analysis revealed that H2O2 exposure can activate the activity of several catalytic enzymes in filefish, as well as redox processes in mitochondria. All DEGs were matched to the KEGG metabolic pathway to uncover the cellular metabolic pathways, and 20 significant specific pathways were identified. DEGs at different stress durations were mainly clustered in metabolic pathways associated with “Glutathione metabolism”, “Carbon metabolism”, “Glycolysis/Gluconeogenesis”, “Aminoacyl-tRNA”, and “Metabolism of xenobiotics by cytochrome P450 biosynthesis” (Fig. 2F, Fig. S1). We summarized the representative DEGs and classified them into different classes, including gene transcription regulation, signal transduction, nervous system, liver and bile acid metabolism, energy metabolism, protein and metabolite modification, cell adhesion and communication, ion channel, inflammation and immune response, and tumor occurrence and proliferation (Table S1). We suggest that the H2O2 stress response in filefish gill tissue may be fine-tuned through the global transcriptional control of stress-related genes.

Metabolite profiling and DCM identification upon H2O2 exposure

The metabolites were detected using UPLC-MS/MS technology, which allowed us to know the metabolic changes in the gills of filefish upon H2O2 exposure. A total of 610 metabolites were detected, including amino acid and its metabolites (28.52%), fatty acids (16.89%), organic acid and its derivatives (14.1%), glycerophospholipids (9.34%), nucleotide and its metabolites (6.89%), benzene and substituted derivatives (4.92%), alcohol and amines (4.43%), heterocyclic compounds (4.26%), carbohydrates and their metabolites (3.93%), hormones and hormone-related compounds (1.48%), coenzymes and vitamins (1.31%), sphingolipids (0.98%), bile acids (0.82%), glycerolipids (0.82%), aldehydes, ketones, esters (0.66%), tryptamines, catechines, pigments (0.16%), and others (0.49%). The principal components of PCA score plot (PC1 and PC2) were calculated to be 32.67%, and 24.45%, respectively. Two groups (i.e., H2O2.6d and Control) were clearly separated, and three biological replicates of each group were clustered together (Fig. S2). In addition, the supervised model of OPLS-DA compared metabolite contents and identified the variables responsible for differences between groups. Then, the differences were calculated by OPLS-DA model between H2O2.6d and Control (R2X = 0.566, R2Y = 1, Q2 = 0.509) (Fig. S2). These results showed that H2O2 exposure strongly changed the metabolite profiles of the filefish gills. A total of 80 significantly changed metabolites (SCMs) were found between H2O2.6d and the control (43 upregulated, 37 downregulated) (Fig. 3A, Table S2). Organic acids and their derivatives, small peptides, amino acid derivatives, and oxidized lipids were the main types of SCMs. According to the log2(fold change) values, the top 20 SCMs between the two groups are shown in Fig. 3B. The most significantly changed metabolite in the comparison of H2O2.6d vs. the control was deoxycholic acid, a metabolite of bile acids, with a log2(fold change) of 4.25. In addition, cholic acid and gamma-methylcholic acid, two other kinds of bile acids, also significantly accumulated. The metabolite 10(S),17(S)-dihydroxy-E,Z,E-docosahexaenoic acid (protectin DX, PDX), an oxidized lipid, was the second most significantly changed metabolite, with a log2(fold change) of 3.48. KEGG metabolic pathway analysis revealed that SCMs were enriched in the following pathways: 2-oxocarboxylic acid metabolism, necroptosis, lysine degradation, the Fc epsilon RI signaling pathway, phenylalanine metabolism, inflammatory mediator regulation, linoleic acid metabolism, and others (Fig. 3C). To assess metabolic correlation between SCMs, Pearson correlation analysis was employed to determine collaborative or exclusionary relationships. Chord diagrams were generated to show the paired SCMs based on the screening criteria of p < 0.05 and | r |≥ 0.8. There were greater metabolic correlations among metabolites, including cholic acid, carnitine C12:1, dimethylmalonic acid, glutamic acid, L-lactic acid, shikimic acid, 2-methylsuccinic acid, 3-reidopropionate, ethyl hydrogen malonate, and SPH (d18:1) (Fig. 3D).

Gut microbial responses of the filefish to H2O2 stress

The microbiota reads were selected after eliminating the host genome. After quality trimming, an average of 2.09 Gb of clean reads were obtained from each sample by metagenomic sequencing (Table S3). After assembly, an average of 9,950 contigs (≥ 1000 bp) and 2,782 contigs (≥ 5000 bp) were obtained from each sample. Based on the contigs (≥ 500 bp) of each sample and mixed assembly, we obtained a total of 404,434 unigenes, with an average of 16,298 unigenes per sample, of which 220,429 (54.5%) had a complete open reading frame. Based on species annotation analysis, the taxonomic information of the intestinal flora was obtained by comparing the unigene sequences with those of Viruses, Archaea, Bacteria, and Fungi extracted from the NCBI nr database.

Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was conducted to analyze the difference in bacterial composition between H2O2.6d and Control, and the first two principal components (PCo1 and PCo2) accounted for 88.24% and 8.38% of variance in genus level, and 85.72% and 8.88% of variance in species level, respectively. The PCoA score plot revealed significant differences between the H2O2.6d and Control groups, indicating high reproducibility and reliability of the experiment (Fig. S3). We investigated the microbial community structure and listed the top 10 most abundant classifications at the phylum and genus levels. Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Bacteroidota, Uroviricota, Lentisphaerae, and Actinobacteria were the most abundant phyla (Fig. 4A), while Lawsonia, Candidatus_Marinarcus, Vibrio, Anaerorhabdus, Clostridium, Holdemania, Arcobacter, Aliarcobacter, Bacillus, and Malaciobacter were the most abundant genera (Fig. S4A). At the phylum level, the relative abundances of Firmicutes and Tenericutes in the H2O2 exposure groups were significantly greater than those in the control group, while the relative abundances of Uroviricota and Verrucomicrobia were significantly lower (P < 0.05, Fig. 4B). At the genus level, the relative abundances of Aliarcobacter, Arcobacter, Candidatus Marinarcus, Malaciobacter, and Clostridium significantly increased upon H2O2 exposure, but the relative abundances of Lawsonia significantly decreased (P < 0.05, Fig. S4B). These findings implied that the gut microbiota structure of filefish greatly changed upon H2O2 exposure.

We used a rank-sum test to identify the species between the groups and linear discriminant analysis (LDA) to minimize the dimension to select important biomarkers following H2O2 exposure. Then, we assessed the impact of different species through the LDA score. We generated a cladogram of the different species (Fig. 4C). The concentric circles depicted in the diagram illustrate the hierarchical classification levels, ranging from phylum to genus (or species). Each smaller circle within these levels represents a specific classification, with its diameter proportional to the corresponding relative abundance. Additionally, the LDA value distribution bar chart shows species that exhibit an LDA score exceeding the predetermined threshold (default set to 4). These species are considered biomarkers, indicating statistically significant differences between groups (Fig. S5). The length of each bar in the chart corresponds to the influence of the respective species, as denoted by their LDA score. According to the LDA score, the order Desulfovibrionales, the class Deltaproteobacteria, the family Desulfovibrionaceae, the species Lawsonia intracellularis, the genus Lawsonia, and others were the top ranked classification categories in the control group, while the phylum Firmicutes, the order Campylobacterales, the class Epsilonproteobacteria, the family Arcobacteraceae, and the order Eubacteriales were the important classification categories in the H2O2 treatment group (Fig. S5).

Metastat analysis revealed a total of 1285 species with marked differences between the two groups. Some representative strains were selected for functional analysis (Fig. 4D). For example, we detected four strains of bifidobacteria (i.e., Bifidobacterium bohemicum, B. colobi, B. longum, and B. margollesii), of which three strains had increased levels and one strain had decreased levels under H2O2 stress. The abundance of two Parabacteroides species, which are considered beneficial bacteria in the intestines, decreased upon H2O2 exposure. Similarly, the abundances of beneficial strains belonging to Porphyromonas, Butyricimonas, Coprobacter, and Bacteroides largely decreased under oxidative stress. However, we found that the abundances of several pathogenic bacteria (i.e., Methanobrevibacter millerae, Rhodococcus qingshengii, Propionibacterium freudenreichii, Methanocorpusculum bavaricum, and M. labreanum) increased significantly. In particular, the abundance of Collinsella tanakaei, which is involved in the synthesis of deoxycholic acid, significantly increased in the intestine upon H2O2 exposure. These results indicated that under H2O2 stress, the abundance of beneficial microorganisms in the fish intestine significantly decreased, while the abundance of unfavorable microorganisms increased.

Gut microbiota function was predicted by KEGG and eggNOG annotation. Six categories of the KEGG pathways were enriched across all groups (Fig. 5A). These pathways included amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, glycan biosynthesis and metabolism, metabolism of cofactors and vitamins, signal transduction, and membrane transport. In addition, the relative abundance of eggNOG functional annotations was determined using Metastat analysis. The clustering heatmap shows the functional categories with significant differences (*P < 0.05). Upon exposure to H2O2, the gut microbiota at eggNOG level 1 displayed significantly enriched pathways related to defense mechanisms, RNA processing and modification, amino acid transport and metabolism, replication recombination and repair, energy production and conversion, signal transduction mechanisms, and cell cycle control cell division (Fig. 5B). Enriched pathways at eggNOG level 2 included oxidoreductase activity, transmembrane signaling receptor activity, succinate dehydrogenase, and others (Fig. 5C).

Discussion

H2O2 treatment is an alternative to disinfection that may be beneficial since it dissociates and disinfects while raising the concentration of water oxygen in recirculating aquaculture systems [9]. H2O2 is widely used as a treatment of ectoparasite (e.g. salmon lice, amoebic gill disease) affected the cultivation of Atlantic salmon [28]. H2O2 is also an officially recommended disinfectant and bactericide for treating gill diseases and vibriosis in filefish species [9]. To optimize H2O2 applications and ensuring sustainable development in aquaculture, we conducted an in-depth investigation into the molecular mechanisms underlying H2O2-induced detrimental effects on oxidative stress, energy metabolism, and immune suppression in filefish.

Hydrogen peroxide exposure causes inflammatory and immune responses

H2O2-induced oxidative stress might primarily affect the function of the mucosal layer in gill tissues. The gill tissue serves as an interface between the organism and its surroundings and is more threatened [29]. In particular, the mucosal barrier serves as the first line of innate immune defense against foreign toxins and pathogens. The mucosal layers of fish cover their gills, skin, and digestive tract. These layers serve as a naturally occurring, physical, biochemical, dynamic, semipermeable barrier separating the animal's body from its surroundings. The main components of mucus are glycoproteins [30]. Our study revealed that the expression levels of von Willebrand factor C domain-containing protein, a glycoprotein localized to the matrix side of the membrane, were markedly increased in the gills of filefish upon H2O2 exposure (Table S1). Many physiologically active chemicals, such as adhesion molecules, complements, lectins, agglutinin, immunoglobulins, and antimicrobial peptides, are abundant in mucus and contribute to innate immune defense [31]. Our study revealed that the expression levels of claudin, cadherin, integral membrane protein, tetraspanin, immunoglobulin, and hepcidin were markedly increased in the gills of filefish upon H2O2 exposure (Table S1). Hepcidin is an antimicrobial peptide rich in cysteines that is produced and secreted by the liver. It is highly expressed and has a role in immunological responses throughout the immune process [32].

H2O2-induced oxidative stress triggers innate immune responses. The fish innate immune system is essential for identifying and eliminating pathogens, initiating an inflammatory response, and coordinating with the adaptive immune system [33]. Under stressful conditions, the innate immune system is essential for preserving a complicated physiological steady state [33, 34]. Excessive ROS production can trigger an inflammatory response in fish by activating signaling pathways, causing the release of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines. Our study revealed that the expression levels of alpha-2-macroglobulin, immunoglobulin, interleukin, leukocyte elastase inhibitor, soluble toll-like receptor, and signaling lymphocytic activation molecule family member were markedly increased in the gills of filefish upon H2O2 exposure (Table S1, Fig. 6). Alpha-2-macroglobulin is a highly conserved and widely present large glycoprotein in various organisms. It has multiple functions, including antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and enzyme activity inhibition, and participates in immune responses [35]. Leukocyte elastase is an intracellular protein. Its involvement in the recovery from chronic inflammatory lung and bowel illnesses makes its role in inflammation obvious [36].

The conceptual map of filefish responses upon H2O2 exposure

H2O2-induced oxidative stress can modulate the fish innate immune system through feedback regulation. H2O2-induced oxidative stress also disturbed the balance of the gut microbiota, altering the microbial composition and abundance (Fig. 4). The gut microbiota is a key component influencing immune responses and immune-related diseases. Dysbiosis of gut bacteria has been shown to cause disease and alter the synthesis of microbial metabolites, leading to immune and metabolic disorders [37]. To scavenge and neutralize ROS and preserve cellular homeostasis, the fish innate immune system uses the first line of antioxidant defense mechanisms, which includes enzymes such as glutathione peroxidase (GPx), catalase (CAT), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) [38]. In the early stages of H2O2 exposure, there is an upregulation of antioxidant enzyme activities in gill tissues as a protective response (Fig. 1). These increased enzymatic activities help to scavenge excess ROS and reduce oxidative damage to cellular components. With prolonged hydrogen peroxide treatment, the activities of antioxidant enzymes significantly decreased in the gill tissue of the filefish (Fig. 1). According to Hwang et al. (2016) [39], high H2O2 may therefore result in redox imbalance, which in turn causes aberrant immunological activity in aquatic species [39]. The ACP and AKP activities were served as useful markers for activated immune system, while increased or decreased enzyme activities had been thoroughly documented when exposed to environmental toxins [17, 40, 41]. In this experiment, both the ACP and AKP activities in filefish gills were suppressed upon H2O2 exposure. These results are consistent with previous studies on the shrimp Litopenaeus vannamei and Procambarus clarkii [40, 42].

Hydrogen peroxide stress affects the nervous and digestive systems

The brain controls several vital functions, including hormone production, behavior, learning, the stress response, and food consumption. Oxidative stress is a major factor in neurotoxicity and brain injury since the brain, which is rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), is particularly vulnerable to ROS attack [43]. H2O2 has been extensively investigated for its capacity to increase MDA production and cause lipid peroxidation in the rat brain, which may cause neuronal and brain damage [44]. H2O2 exposure can cause dysfunction in the brain by damaging antioxidant enzymes, nonenzymatic antioxidants, unsaturated fatty acids, and catecholamines [45]. H2O2 exposure also induces neurotoxicity and oxidative damage in common carp, most likely by disrupting calcium/cAMP signaling pathways and neurotransmitter systems [10]. In our study, we found that the expression levels of metabotropic glutamate receptor, glutamate receptor ionotropic receptor, G-protein coupled estrogen receptor, growth hormone-releasing hormone receptor, mitogen-activated protein kinase, and calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase were markedly increased in the gills of filefish upon H2O2 exposure (Table S1, Fig. 6). Therefore, H2O2-induced oxidative stress activated a wide range of neurotransmitter signaling pathways in filefish.

The metabolism of nutrients and digestion depend heavily on the balance of the gut microbiota. Numerous chemical pollutants have been demonstrated to pose serious harm to the gut microbiota, altering host health [40, 46]. The adverse consequences of chemotherapeutant therapies in fish farming include alterations in the composition and diversity of the microbial community throughout the early stages of life [16]. These changes have the potential to reverse the beneficial functions of the gut microbiota for maintaining host biological processes and homeostatic regulation [46]. However, there are few reports on how hydrogen peroxide affects the fish gut microbiota. In our study, H2O2 exposure not only changed the abundance and diversity of the gut microbiota in filefish but also reshaped the intestinal microbiota structure (Fig. 4A and B). H2O2 stress disrupted the balance of the gut microbiota and selectively promoted the growth of certain harmful bacteria while suppressing beneficial bacteria (Fig. 4D).

Bile acids are important signaling molecules that control the homoeostasis of glucose, cholesterol and energy, as well as intestinal inflammation; thus, they are believed to be closely related to the development of metabolic disorders (Fig. 6) [47, 48]. We found that the contents of bile acids, including deoxycholic acid, cholic acid, and gamma-mercholic acid, were significantly increased in the gill tissues of filefish upon H2O2 exposure (Table S2). By triggering xenobiotic metabolism, cholic acid increases stress resistance and reduces oxidative stress, consequently reducing the mortality caused by T-2 toxin [49]. Primary bile acids (BAs) are produced by hepatocytes, while secondary bile acids are the result of the metabolism of intestinal bacteria [48]. In particular, the microbiota shapes the intestinal bile salt pool based on the metabolic capabilities of the bacterial community and external factors [50]. Additionally, bile acids also impact microbial community structure and function [48, 51]. Bile salt hydrolase, which is highly conserved in all main gut microbiota phyla (Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, and Actinobacteria), hydrolyzes the amino acid moiety as the initial step in secondary BA metabolism [48]. H2O2-induced oxidative stress increased the abundance of Firmicutes (Fig. 4A) and thereby increased secondary bile acid levels (Table S2). We also found that the abundances of Collinsella and Bifidobacterium were significantly increased upon H2O2 exposure (Fig. 4C). Collinsella strains can promote the synthesis of deoxycholic acid in the intestine, improve the conversion efficiency of material and energy metabolism, and enhance the body's antioxidant capacity and immune function [52–54]. Bifidobacterium also possesses bile salt hydrolase activity and is involved in bile acid transformation [50, 55]. In contrast, the abundances of Porphyromonas loveana and Porphyromonadaceae bacterium significantly decreased. These bacteria belong to the genus Porphyromonas, which does not hydrolyze sugars and is not tolerant to 2% bile salts. Bile acids, immunoglobulin A (IgA), and short-chain fatty acids can also affect metabolism through the microbiota-gut-liver axis [56, 57].

The gut microbiota regulates the number of gut peptides to control the vagal afferent pathway, which in turn regulates intestinal metabolism through the microbiota-gut-brain axis [58]. The liver is thought to play a crucial role in the interaction between the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis and the gut-liver axis [59, 60]. When thyroid hormones are released from the thyroid gland under the influence of the HPT axis, they can bind to thyroid hormone receptors in liver tissue, regulating the hepatic metabolism of glucose, lipids, and cholesterol largely by modulating the transcriptional activities of associated target genes (Fig. 6) [59, 61]. However, there are few reports on the regulation of gill tissue by the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis. Interestingly, we found that the expression levels of luteinizing hormone receptor, neuropeptide Y receptor, neuronal acetylcholine receptor, neurotensin/neuromedin N, and leucine-rich repeat neuronal protein were markedly increased in the gills of filefish upon H2O2 exposure (Table S1).

The gut is critical for nutrient absorption, digestion, and immune function, while the liver acts as a central organ for the detoxification, metabolism, and secretion of bile acids [29, 58, 61, 62]. However, the relationships among the gut, liver, and gill have largely gone unnoticed. Interestingly, we found that the expression levels of hepatocyte nuclear factors, liver-expressed antimicrobial peptide, bile salt-activated lipase, and Fer3-like protein were markedly increased in the gills of filefish upon H2O2 exposure (Table S1). Hepatocyte nuclear factor stimulates gene expression related to liver function, thereby maintaining the regular morphology and function of liver cells [63]. These findings indicate the functional diversity of gill tissue and interactions between gill tissue and the liver in response to oxidative stress.

Hydrogen peroxide stress affects the energy metabolism of filefish

Oxidative stress can affect glucose metabolism, lipid metabolism, and protein metabolism [64]. These disruptions can lead to imbalances in energy metabolism and nutrient homeostasis. The transition from anaerobic glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation causes a marked increase in the amounts of lactate and glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) [64, 65]. The pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) is activated in anaerobic conditions following H2O2-induced stress [65]. The PPP plays a dual role in both free radical synthesis and detoxification. Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) is the first enzyme in the pentose phosphate pathway. G6PD deficiency typically manifests as hemolytic anemia due to oxidative damage to red blood cells. However, in severe cases, G6PD deficiency can also lead to susceptibility to infections resulting from impaired leucocyte oxidative bursts [66]. We found that the expression of the committed pathway enzyme G6PD markedly increased in gill tissues of filefish upon H2O2 exposure (Table S1). Through NADPH, the PPP provides high-energy electrons for reductive biosynthesis and antioxidant defense [65, 66]. In addition, we also found that the content of L-lactic acid significantly increased in the gill tissues of filefish upon H2O2 exposure (Table S2). Compared to D-lactate, L-lactate is more prevalent in mammals and is primarily produced by mammals through glycolysis. Circulating L-lactate, also known as the "lactate shuttle", is an oxidative and gluconeogenic substrate that moves between organs, cells, and organelles. It is essential for maintaining redox balance, energy balance, acid‒base balance, inflammation reduction, wound healing, and immune homeostasis (Fig. 6) [67, 68].

Fatty acids serve as important energy substrates that undergo oxidation in the mitochondria via beta-oxidation, producing acetyl-CoA, which enters the Krebs cycle for further energy generation [64]. Fatty acids account for 60% ~ 95% of total oxidative phosphorylation [69]. H2O2 can directly oxidize lipids present in fish cell membranes, leading to lipid peroxidation. In the present study, the content of most free fatty acids significantly decreased, while the content of most oxidized lipids increased in gill tissues of filefish upon H2O2 exposure (Table S2). Excessive ROS can oxidize PUFAs, resulting in increased MDA levels (a byproduct of lipid peroxidation) [10, 61]. We also found that the content of MDA increased in gill tissues of filefish upon H2O2 exposure (Fig. 1), which was consistent with the upregulation of oxidized lipids. The digestive system and nervous system are intricately connected through neuroendocrine signaling [59]. Leptin is a pluripotent peptide hormone produced mainly by adipocytes [70]. Leptin governs energy homeostasis and neuroendocrine function primarily by acting on the hypothalamus of the central nervous system, particularly the hypothalamus [21]. We found that the gene expression levels of leptin (gene ID EVM0011377) were markedly increased in gill tissues of filefish upon H2O2 exposure (Table S1). Carnitine is essential for energy metabolism. The primary roles of carnitine include facilitating the movement of long-chain fatty acids from the cytoplasm into the mitochondrial matrix and promoting the β-oxidation of fatty acids [64]. In our study, all the carnitine compounds accumulated in the gills of the filefish (Table S2). Oxidized lipids play important roles in tissue injury, the inflammatory response, immune defense, and endocrine regulation (Fig. 6) [71].

Specific amino acids and branched-chain amino acids can be restricted or supplemented to increase metabolic homeostasis and energy balance. The addition of branched-chain and essential amino acids can boost mitochondrial biogenesis and activate antioxidant defense systems under a variety of physiological and pathological conditions [72]. In the present study, we found that the levels of most amino acids and their metabolites were significantly decreased in the gill tissues of filefish upon H2O2 exposure (Table S2). Through the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway, oxidative stress triggers the breakdown of proteins that are stimulated by apoptotic factors, which in turn causes the deterioration of fish muscle [73]. The expression level of E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase, which controls targeted protein degradation through the ubiquitin‒proteasome pathway, was significantly decreased in gill tissues upon H2O2 exposure (Table S1). Therefore, oxidative stress diverts metabolic flux away from amino acid synthesis and toward those involved in cellular defense and repair mechanisms. In addition, fish may switch to anaerobic metabolism, resulting in the accumulation of organic acids as byproducts. Lactic acid, citric acid, and malonic acid are a few examples of accumulating organic acids in filefish gill tissues following H2O2 exposure (Table S2). Organic acids are proposed to be involved in regulating energy production, detoxification, and gut microbial activity [74, 75]. Overall, our integrated multiomics analysis revealed that H2O2-induced detrimental impacts include activated innate immune responses, altered energy metabolism, and gut microbiota disorders (Fig. 6).

Conclusions

In aquaculture, H2O2 is now considered as a possible disinfectant. Excessive H2O2 exposure causes inflammatory responses by affecting the function of the mucosal layer and triggers the fish innate immune system by activating macroglobulin, leukocytes, interleukins, and antioxidant enzymes. H2O2-induced oxidative stress activates a wide range of neurotransmitter signaling pathways. Our findings also indicated that the gut, liver, and gill tissues can respond to oxidative stress through interaction and feedback regulation, as evidenced by the upregulation of hepatocyte nuclear factors, liver-expressed antimicrobial peptide, and bile acids in gill tissues, as well as the bile salt-related gut microbiota. Furthermore, H2O2-induced oxidative stress promoted the development of gastrointestinal disorders, altering the microbial composition and affecting the homeostasis of the gut microbiota. In general, these changes in the inflammatory, immune, nervous, and digestive systems represented adaptive responses of filefish to cope with H2O2-induced oxidative stress. Our study offers a comprehensive assessment of the detrimental impacts of H2O2and signal transduction/metabolic pathways triggered by H2O2 exposure in marine fishes.
