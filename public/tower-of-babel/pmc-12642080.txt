Compression garments in hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome and hypermobility spectrum disorders: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Xavier Boulu, Jean-Denis Karam, Jean Schmidt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypermobility spectrum disorders (HSD) and hypermobile Ehlers–Danlos syndrome (hEDS) are associated with chronic pain, proprioceptive impairments, and poor quality of life (QoL). Compression garments (CGs) are occasionally prescribed to modulate sensory feedback and reduce symptoms. Although several small studies have explored CG use in hEDS, the scientific evidence is limited for hEDS and almost inexistant for HSD - despite similar clinical burdens.ObjectiveTo assess the clinical effectiveness of custom-made CGs with regard to pain, analgesic consumption, fatigue, and QoL in adults with HSD or hEDS.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study at a single tertiary hospital. By inspecting the center’s medical records from January 2013 to March 2025, we identified 28 adults having been prescribed CGs for symptomatic HSD or hEDS. Twenty of the 28 met the inclusion criteria. Data on demographics, the diagnostic classification, clinical history, Beighton score, five-part questionnaire score, the characteristics of the CG (type, target site, and mean time worn per day), “on-off” effects, tolerance, and adherence to physiotherapy were extracted. The primary outcome was a composite of responses to treatment at follow-up (mean ± standard deviation (SD) duration: 4.4 ± 1.0 months), defined as one or more of a subjective reduction in pain, a reduction in analgesic use (excluding acetaminophen), an improvement in QoL, and a reduction in fatigue.ResultsOf the 20 patients (females: 85%; mean age 39.2 ± 13.0 years; mean body mass index (BMI) 24.4 ± 7 kg/m²), 13 (65%) had hEDS and seven (35%) had HSD. The median [interquartile range] duration of chronic pain was 84 [36–144] months. At follow-up, 16 (80%) of the patients reported a response to treatment: 16 (80%) reported a reduction in pain, seven out of 13 patients with available data (53.8%) reported a reduction in analgesic use, eight (40%) reported an improvement in QoL, and three (15%) reported a reduction in fatigue. There were no significant differences in any of the outcomes when comparing the HSD and hEDS groups. A univariate analysis showed trends towards a beneficial effect for female sex (p = 0.08), a history of sprains (p = 0.08), the presence of an “on-off” effect (p = 0.094), and impaired proprioception (p = 0.11). Compared with nonresponders, responders wore CGs for significantly longer (p = 0.0013; r² = 0.45) and had a higher BMI (17.5 ± 1.0 vs. 26.1 ± 6.1 kg/m², respectively; p = 0.005). Clinical effectiveness was not correlated with the Beighton score.ConclusionsCGs may constitute a well-tolerated, nonpharmacological intervention associated with clinically relevant reductions in pain and analgesic use in patients with HSD or hEDS. The similar degrees of effectiveness observed in HSD and hEDS suggest that the reimbursement of CGs by health system payers should be broadened.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09318-z.Keywords: Joint instability, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, Compression therapy, Clothing, Proprioception disorders, Pain management, Complementary therapies

Introduction

Hypermobility spectrum disorders (HSD) and hypermobile Ehlers–Danlos syndrome (hEDS) belong to a clinical continuum characterized by excessive joint hypermobility and chronic musculoskeletal complaints [1].

According to the 2017 international classification [2], hEDS is a clinical diagnosis that requires the fulfilment of all of the following three criteria: (i) generalized joint hypermobility using age-adjusted Beighton cut-offs (or a Beighton score one point below the cut-off plus supportive historical items); (ii) two or more of the following features: (A) ≥ 5 systemic manifestations of a generalized connective-tissue disorder, (B) a positive family history of hEDS, or (C) musculoskeletal complications (e.g., chronic pain and/or recurrent dislocations/instability); and (iii) the ruling out of other heritable/acquired connective-tissue disorders and alternative diagnoses. In contrast, individuals with symptomatic joint hypermobility but who do not meet al.l three hEDS criteria are – after other possible causes have been ruled out – classified as having generalized, peripheral, localized, or historical HSD. This distinction provides a consistent, contemporary framework for categorizing patients and enabling research that compares hEDS with HSD. Unlike the genetically defined forms of Ehlers–Danlos syndrome for which specific mutations have been identified, neither HSD nor hEDS is currently linked to an identified genetic anomaly. Nevertheless, these two conditions appear to share common pathophysiological abnormalities in collagen structure, production, and/or maturation, which contribute to tissue instability and the observed clinical manifestations [1].

Relative to the general population, patients with HSD or hEDS have a greater prevalence of chronic pain syndromes [3], chronic fatigue [4], proprioceptive dysfunction [5], and poor quality of life (QoL) [6], – all of which are difficult to manage.

The pain mechanisms in these conditions are complex and multifactorial [7]. It is thought that a nociceptive component related to chronic joint instability [8] (capsuloligamentous stretching and local microtrauma) combines with a neuropathic component [9, 10] arising from repeated microlesions of proprioceptors in the joints (Pacinian corpuscles) and tendons (Golgi tendon organs). These repeated microlesions might lead to a somatosensory anomalies (i.e. an abnormal signal sent from the proprioceptive receptors to the central nervous system), impaired postural information processing, and central sensitization [11, 12].

In this context, compression garments (CGs) are occasionally used as a nonpharmacological, adjunct treatment for patients with hEDS [13]. Custom-made to apply constant pressure over soft tissues, CGs are thought to restore deficient subcutaneous tension, thereby enhancing sensory afferents to the central nervous system, improving postural integration, and modulating pain [14, 15]. Beyond hEDS, compression therapy has been employed for decades in various medical and rehabilitational fields [16], including scar management [17], orthopedic and postoperative support [18], venous insufficiency [19], sports medicine [20] and certain neurodevelopmental conditions21 (e.g., autism spectrum disorder).

Several clinical studies in patients with hEDS [21–24] have suggested that CGs can reduce pain, improve joint stability, have a positive effect on postural balance, and modulate nociception through gate-control mechanisms. However, scientific data remain limited in hEDS and nearly non-existent in patients with HSD, even though they share similar sensorimotor impairments. This lack of evidence contributes to unequal access to care: in France, health insurance generally reimburses CGs for patients with hEDS but often refuses coverage for those diagnosed with HSD due to the lack of recognized scientific data.

The objective of the present study was to evaluate the effectiveness of CGs in both HSD and hEDS, particularly with respect to pain, analgesic consumption, fatigue, and QoL.

Materials and methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study by reviewing the medical records of adult patients treated with CGs for HSD or hEDS in the Department of Internal Medicine at Amiens University Medical Center (Amiens, France). CGs were prescribed in hospital by a specialist physician and then custom made (Cerecare SAS, Sailly-lez-Cambrai, France). All garments were flat-knitted and manufactured from standardized, certified OEKO-TEX® fabric (56% polyamide and 44% elastane; weight 265 g/m²), corresponding to medical compression class II. Depending on the clinical presentation, long- or short-sleeved vests, long or short pants, elbow sleeves, gloves, mittens, knee sleeves and/or ankle sleeves were prescribed.

The patients were identified by searching the hospital’s electronic medical record system (DxCare v7.7, Dedalus France, Le Plessis-Robinson, France) with the keywords “hypermobility spectrum disorder” or “Ehlers–Danlos” from January 2013 (the date of introduction of electronic medical records in the center) through to March 2025. Twenty-eight patients matched these criteria.

Each set of medical records was reviewed manually by the study’s principal investigator (XB). For patients initially diagnosed before the publication of the 2017 Ehlers–Danlos classification, historical diagnoses were re-evaluated retrospectively according to the 2017 international diagnostic criteria [2]. Using a specific case report form (see the supplementary file), data were entered into a database (EpiData, version 3.1, EpiData Association, Odense, Denmark). EpiData software was also used to analyze the data.

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were (i) age ≥ 18 years; (ii) joint hypermobility, defined as a Beighton score ≥ 5/9 between the ages of 18 and 50 or ≥ 4/9 for the over-50s [25] (if the Beighton score was one point below the threshold, a five-part questionnaire (5PQ) score ≥ 2/5 was required [26]); (iii) (for patients with hEDS only); fulfillment of the 2017 international hEDS diagnostic criteria [2]; (iv) (for patients with HSD only) symptomatic joint hypermobility not meeting the full hEDS criteria from the 2017 international consensus, after ruling out other causes (the clinical picture had to be suggestive, including a history of spontaneous or traumatic dislocations/subluxations/sprains/tears, documented alterations in peripheral (but not visceral) proprioception, skeletal deformities, and minimal skin involvement); (v) the presence of a chronic pain syndrome, defined as articular, periarticular or neuropathic persistent pain for more than three months at two or more (axial and/or peripheral) sites and that was not explained by an identifiable disease (e.g., inflammatory rheumatism, connective tissue disease, myopathy, metabolic bone disease, metastatic neoplasia, active large-fiber peripheral neuropathy, uncontrolled thyroid disease); (vi) a medical indication for CGs, based on insufficient symptom control with pharmacotherapy (particularly in relation to alterations in proprioception, joint instability, muscle hypotonia, exercise intolerance, or pain hypersensitivity); and (vii) concurrent referral for specialist physical therapy with a physiotherapist (rehabilitation program including motor strengthening).

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria were: (i) the absence of follow-up date after the prescription of CGs and (ii) a diagnosis of hEDS or HSD without a formal medical indication for CGs.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was a composite of the clinical effectiveness of CGs, defined as the presence of at least one of the following outcomes at follow-up: (i) a subjective reduction in pain levels; (ii) a reduction in the consumption of analgesics (excluding acetaminophen); (iii) a perceived improvement in QoL; or (iv) a reduction in fatigue. Furthermore, each of these four components was analyzed and reported on individually, in order to provide a detailed description of the clinical response profile.

Collected data

We recorded the participants’ demographic characteristics (age, sex, and body mass index (BMI)), diagnosis (HSD or hEDS), relevant personal and family medical histories (hypermobility, repeated sprains, dislocations/subluxations, and mast cell activation syndrome), pain sites (axial and/or peripheral), impairments in peripheral proprioception (defined as impaired position sense, non-cerebellar coordination impairments, or a proprioceptive Romberg sign) and/or visceral proprioception (defined as the presence of functional signs suggestive of altered autonomic or deep visceral sensory processing, such as spontaneous breath-holding, orthostatic intolerance, postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome, vestibular hypersensitivity, and bladder/bowel dysfunction), clinical hypermobility scores (the Beighton score and the 5PQ score), the details of the CGs, any clear “on-off” treatment effects (i.e., a rapid reduction in pain when the CG is put on, and a rapid disappearance of the relief upon removal of the CG), tolerance of the CG, time worn per day, and adherence to the prescribed physiotherapy program.

Statistical analysis

In a descriptive analysis, quantitative variables were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed or as the median [interquartile range (IQR)] if not. Qualitative variables were presented as n (% of the total). Comparisons of quantitative variables were based on the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and comparisons of qualitative variables were based on a chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All tests were two-sided, and the threshold for statistical significance was set to p < 0.05. Univariate analyses explored potential factors associated with a clinical response to treatment, and an odds ratio (OR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were computed when feasible. A linear regression was used to assess correlations between continuous variables (e.g., symptom duration, symptom response, CG wearing time, and effectiveness). The small sample size and the risk of model overfitting prevented the application of a multivariate analysis.

Ethical considerations

The study’s procedures complied with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and with French regulations on retrospective analyses of routine clinical data. The study database was registered with the French National Data Protection Commission (Commission nationale de l’informatique et des libertés, Paris, France; reference: PI2025_843_0107). According to French legislation (CNIL MR-004 reference methodology), informed consent was not required for this retrospective study.

Results

Of the 28 patients identified, 20 met the inclusion criteria and were analyzed (Table 1). The reasons for non-inclusion included the lack of a CG prescription, the lack of actual CG use, and the absence of a follow-up evaluation. Seventeen (85%) of the participants were female. The mean age was 39.15 ± 13.03 years, and mean BMI was 24.35 ± 7 kg/m². Thirteen (65%) of the patients had a diagnosis of hEDS, and seven (35%) had a diagnosis of HSD. No patients with genetically confirmed EDS, localized HSD, or previous (non-current) HSD were included.

A family history of hypermobility was present in 14 patients (70%), with repeated sprains in 10 (50%), repeated dislocations/subluxations in 11 (55%), and mast cell activation syndrome in four (20%). The median [IQR] duration of chronic pain before CG initiation was 84 [36–144] months. An axial pain syndrome was reported for 19 patients (95%), and a peripheral pain syndrome was reported for 20 (100%). Impaired peripheral proprioception was noted in 12 patients (60%), and altered visceral proprioception was noted in 12 (60%). The median Beighton score was 5 [4.5–7.5], and the median 5PQ score was 4 [3–5].

The CG prescription targeted the trunk in 18 cases (90%) and the limbs in 19 (95%). The mean daily wearing time was 8.3 ± 6.4 h. The mean time interval until the clinical follow-up assessment was 4.4 ± 1.03 months. An “on-off” effect of treatment was reported in 9 cases (45%). CG use was poorly tolerated or discontinued in five cases (25%). Fifteen patients (75%) adhered to the prescribed physical therapy program.

Primary outcome: the composite clinical endpoint

At last follow-up, 16 patients (80%) reported a reduction in pain, while 4 (20%) reported no change. Seven of the 13 patients with available data (53.8%) reported a reduction in analgesic use, whereas 6 (46.2%) reported no change. Eight patients (40%) reported an improvement in QoL, whereas 12 (60%) reported that their QoL was stable. Three patients (15%) noted a reduction in fatigue, while 17 (85%) reported no change. Overall, 16 of the 20 patients (80%) were responders (i.e., with a response for at least one of the composite endpoint’s four items). However, intolerance to CGs was significantly associated with the absence of clinical benefit: the proportion of patients reporting an improvement was 20% in the intolerant group and 100% in the tolerant group (p = 0.001).

A comparison of the HSD and hEDS groups

The proportions of patients achieving the key outcomes did not differ significantly when comparing the HSD and hEDS groups:

The composite endpoint was reached in 85.7% of patients with HSD (6 out of 7) and in 76.9% of those with hEDS (10 out of 13) (p = 1.00). Similarly, pain reduction was reported in 85.7% of HSD patients versus 76.9% in the hEDS group (p = 1.00). Reduction in analgesic use occurred in 40% versus 62.5% of patients, respectively (p = 0.592), while quality-of-life improvement was reported in 28.6% versus 46.2% (p = 0.642). Lastly, fatigue reduction was noted in 28.6% of HSD patients and 7.7% of hEDS patients (p = 0.27). These results are summarized in Fig. 2.

However, the proportion of patients with altered visceral proprioception was significantly higher in the hEDS group than in the HSD group (84.6% vs. 14.3%, respectively; p = 0.004); this finding is in line with the literature data on hEDS phenotypes. There were no other significant intergroup differences in demographic or clinical characteristics.

Responders versus nonresponders

Patients who met the composite endpoint (n = 16) had a significantly higher BMI than nonresponders (n = 4), with values of 26.06 ± 6.08 kg/m² and 17.5 ± 1 kg/m², respectively (p = 0.005). There were no significant differences between responders and nonresponders with regard to age (37.5 ± 11.44 vs. 45.7 ± 18.67 years, respectively; p = 0.39), duration of pain (81.76 ± 57.24 vs. 225 ± 258.37 months; p = 0.36), Beighton score (5.8 ± 1.92 vs. 4.7 ± 2.36; p = 0.32), or adherence to physiotherapy (81.3% vs. 50%; p = 0.248).There was a modest, inverse correlation between the duration of chronic pain before CG initiation and the treatment response (p = 0.066; r² = 0.21). Furthermore, responders wore CGs significantly longer than nonresponders (p = 0.0013; r² = 0.45). The treatment response was not significantly correlated with the Beighton score (p = 0.329; r² = 0.05).

Discussion

This retrospective study evaluated the effects of CGs in adults with HSD or hEDS. We observed effectiveness (notably a pronounced reduction in pain) in 80% of the patients. These results are in line with the small amount of literature data on CG use in hEDS. Furthermore, the present study is one of the first to have included patients with HSD.

Clinical interpretation

Our observation of a reduction in pain is in line with Bénistan et al.’s22 report of significant decreases in (i) pain at the most affected joint, (ii) neuropathic components, and (iii) joint instability. Over half of our participants reported a reduction in analgesic use (excluding acetaminophen), which underscores the potential economic value of a CG-based approach. However, given our study’s retrospective design, we could not systematically assess the analgesic drug classes and initial dose levels or reductions in dose levels or dosing frequency. Although this limitation ruled out a detailed assessment of the total medication burden, the observed trend towards drug sparing might be clinically relevant and warrants confirmation in prospective studies.

An improvement in QoL was reported by only 40% of patients, and a reduction in fatigue was reported by 15%; these low values reflect the multifactorial nature of fatigue in this patient population (with sleep disturbances, deconditioning, etc.)22. CGs appear to be more effective for locomotor and proprioceptive symptoms but have only a limited systemic impact.

Similar treatment response rates in HSD vs. hEDS patient groups

There were no significant differences in the endpoints between patients with HSD and patients with hEDS. Although the proportion of patients with an alteration in visceral proprioception was higher in the hEDS group, both groups responded to CGs to a similar extent. This novel finding has not been previously reported in the literature and suggests that patients with HSD may benefit from CGs as much as those with hEDS do.

The comparison of our present results with previous studies is challenging because the effects of CGs on patients with HSD had not been specifically evaluated; previous studies focused exclusively on individuals with hEDS. Nevertheless, it is known that some aspects of the disease mechanism are common to HSD and hEDS and that the two disorders share a broad spectrum musculoskeletal and proprioceptive symptoms [1]. These results lend support to the concept of a common sensory–motor dysfunction that underlies the two ying both disorders and justify the future inclusion of HSD in prospective studies of compression therapy.

In France, reimbursement of CGs by the national health insurance fund currently covers the indication of hEDS but is frequently refused in an indication of HSD, owing to insufficient scientific evidence. Our studymight justify the reconsideration of reimbursement criteria and providing access to a broader patient population.

Predictors of the treatment response

The time per day wearing CGs was a strong predictor of a treatment response (p = 0.0013; r² = 0.45) and thus highlighted the importance of treatment adherence. In line with this finding, intolerance to CGs was significantly associated with the absence of clinical benefit (p = 0.001). These preliminary observations suggest that sustained use of CGs favors better clinical outcomes, although this hypothesis requires confirmation. The apparent lack of a statistically significant correlation between the Beighton score and clinical effectiveness confirmed that the severity of hypermobility alone is not predictive of a response to CGs; in fact, a documented impairment in proprioception might be more relevant.

Interestingly, a higher BMI was significantly associated with a more frequent clinical response to CGs. One plausible hypothesis is that in individuals with a greater body volume, CGs might achieve a more uniform pressure distribution across subcutaneous tissues and might thereby enhance joint coaptation, increase sensory stimulation of the skin, strengthen proprioceptive feedback, and ultimately contribute to pain reduction and stability. This concept is supported by biomechanical and experimental studies showing that garment–skin interface pressure and the internal stress distribution vary with the wearer’s morphology and the garment fit [16, 27, 28]. Nevertheless, there is no direct clinical evidence to show that a higher BMI alone amplifies the effectiveness of CGs; hence, this aspect remains speculative and warrants further investigation in studies of the possible interplay between body composition (i.e. lean vs. adipose mass), morphology, and pressure distribution.

The results of our pilot study suggested that patients reporting an “on-off” effect (defined as immediate symptom relief while wearing the CG and then disappearance of that relief upon removal) were more likely to respond to CG-based treatment. Although the association did not reach the usual threshold for statistical significance, there was a clear trend in which all patients experiencing an on-off effect showed an overall response to treatment. A similar, immediate improvement in balance upon donning CGs has also been reported by Benistan et al.23 in a study of patients with hEDS, which suggests that our observation is relevant. Taken as a whole, these findings suggest that the presence of an on-off effect is a potential clinical indicator of responsiveness to CG therapy. However, this hypothesis requires confirmation.

Limitations

The present study had several limitations. Firstly, given that the diagnostic terminology changed during the study period (2013–2025), patients diagnosed before 2017 were retrospectively reclassified as having hEDS or HSD, according to the updated criteria [2]. Although this approach ensured consistency, it may have led to minor misclassification bias. Secondly, the sample size was small (n = 20) and limited the study’s statistical power - especially for group comparisons. Thirdly, QoL and fatigue were not measured with standardized, validated instruments (e.g., the SF-36 questionnaire and the Fatigue Severity Scale), which reduced our ability to compare the present results with the literature data. Fourthly, the retrospective design introduced selection bias and restricted data standardization—particularly for the patients with HSD. Fifthly, the use of a composite endpoint that corresponded to at least one positive item among the four considered (a reduction in pain, a reduction in analgesic consumption, an improvement in QoL, and a reduction in fatigue) might have provided an overestimate of effectiveness.

Finally, another limitation concerns potential confounding factors. Most patients had already undergone long-term physiotherapy or rehabilitation programs before the prescription of compression garments, and these programs were usually continued during the follow-up period. Furthermore, the drug regimen might have changed for some patients. Although the rehabilitation context was therefore relatively stable at the time of CG initiation, these concomitant treatments might still have contributed to the observed reduction in symptoms, making it difficult to attribute the observed effects solely to the use of CGs. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as associations, rather than evidence of causality.

Perspectives

Future research should seek to clearly define the target population, i.e. those patients most likely to benefit from CGs. Profiles that might be more responsive include patients with a shorter duration of chronic pain, those with peripheral proprioceptive disturbances, and those with a higher BMI. Accurate identification of these predictive factors might guide the more judicious prescription of CGs. This aspect is especially important, given the substantial cost of CGs; in France, these garments are reimbursed by the social security system only under specific conditions and must be replaced every 6 to 24 months, due to the progressive loss of elasticity and mechanical effectiveness. Hence, in a context of healthcare resource optimization, it is essential to target patients for whom CGs are most likely to be effective.

Prospective, controlled studies are also needed, with a particular focus on HSD; the latter condition might be more prevalent than hEDS but has not been studied as extensively. These studies should incorporate validated outcome measures (e.g. pain scales, QoL questionnaires, and fatigue questionnaires), objective assessments of postural control and sensory afferents, and subgroup analyses by clinical phenotype (axial, peripheral, and visceral). Lastly, future research work should investigate the optimal daily duration of CG use, the most treatment-responsive anatomic sites, and potential synergistic effects with other proprioceptive aids and treatments (e.g., soft braces, foot orthoses, and tailored physiotherapy).

Conclusion

The results of this pilot study suggest that CGs represent a well-tolerated, clinically relevant, nonpharmacological treatment option for reducing pain and limiting analgesic use in patients with hEDS or HSD, regardless of the individual’s Beighton score. The treatment response appeared to be linked to the presence of impaired proprioception at baseline, higher body mass index, good adherence to CG use, and immediate symptom relief while wearing the garment (an “on–off” effect). Our results also suggest that patients with HSD (an indication for which CGs are not usually reimbursed) may derive benefits similar to those observed in patients with hEDS; today’s reimbursement policies might therefore warrant reevaluation.
