Menin-driven mTOR signaling sustains taxane resistance in CRPC and reveals a targetable vulnerability for combination therapy
Authors: Ipek Bulut, Buse Cevatemre, Neslihan Yuksel-Catal, Hamzah Syed, Tamer Onder, Ceyda Acilan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractProstate cancer (PC) progression is predominantly driven by androgen signaling, making androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) the standard treatment. However, the transition to castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) significantly reduces ADT efficacy. While taxanes such as docetaxel (Dtx) and cabazitaxel (Cbz) are widely employed, therapeutic resistance remains a major clinical obstacle. To address this, we established docetaxel-resistant CRPC models and performed an epigenetic drug screen, identifying MLL-Menin and MLL-WDR5 inhibitors as potent agents capable of restoring taxane sensitivity through G2/M arrest and apoptosis induction. Functional depletion of Menin (MEN1) revealed its critical role in sustaining chemoresistance, selectively impairing proliferation in resistant cells, and preventing resistance emergence in parental lines.Integrative transcriptomic (RNA-seq) and epigenomic (CUT&RUN-seq) analyses suggested that Menin may play a regulatory role in mTOR signaling and E2F target pathways. Menin binding to the promoters of mTOR and Cyclin D1 was confirmed, and rescue experiments further validated its regulatory role. Analysis of TCGA datasets demonstrated co-expression of MEN1 and mTOR in advanced metastatic PC, supporting clinical relevance. Moreover, combination treatment with the mTOR inhibitor Torin-1 and docetaxel synergistically enhanced therapeutic response in Menin-depleted resistant cells. MEN1 knockdown also abrogated tumor growth in vivo.These findings identify Menin as one of the key mediator of taxane resistance in CRPC through the regulation of mTOR. Targeting Menin, alone or in combination with mTOR inhibition, represents a promising strategy to overcome resistance and improve therapeutic outcomes in taxane-refractory PC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02594-1.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PC) is the second most common cause of cancer-related mortality among men, with an estimated 1.4 million new cases reported each year [1]. Removal of the prostate gland (prostatectomy) and radiotherapy are considered initial therapeutic protocols [2], followed by castration (androgen deprivation) therapy, either reducing androgen synthesis or blocking the androgen receptor (AR) activity [3]. Despite the castration therapy, the localized tumors may develop metastatic features, allowing cells to migrate to other areas of the body (bones, lymph nodes, lungs, or brain), and the disease is referred to as castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) [4]. Taxanes such as docetaxel or cabazitaxel are the chemotherapy options for CRPC [5]; however, the therapy response is compromised by the emergence of drug resistance. Therefore, revealing the molecular mechanisms underlying drug resistance is essential for developing strategies to restore sensitivity to taxane-based treatments.

Numerous research groups, including ours, have extensively investigated the mechanisms underlying taxane resistance, which include the upregulation of Multidrug resistance 1 (MDR1) [6], microtubule mutations [7], drug-metabolizing enzymes [8], anti-apoptotic pathways [9, 10], epithelial to mesenchymal transition (EMT) [11], dysregulation of centrosomal kinases and cell cycle checkpoints [12, 13], non-coding RNA’s [14], and aberrant activation of growth-regulating signals such as receptor tyrosine kinases, PI3K/AKT, and mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway [15–19]. mTOR plays a crucial role in the regulation of protein synthesis, proliferation, cell cycle progression, and survival [20]. It is essential for cell growth and G1/S transition in cancer cells [21], by inducing translation and stability of Cyclin D1 [22]. The mTOR activation supports survival under therapeutic stress, which may lead to a drug-resistant phenotype [15, 23]. Additionally, epigenetic alterations may influence drug resistance, and many clinical studies endorse co-treatment with conventional chemotherapy and epigenetic modifier drugs [24–26].

Targeting mixed lineage leukemia (MLL or KMT2A) histone methyltransferase has been explored in drug-resistant cancers, including CRPC, leukemia, and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) [27–30]. Menin, the N-terminal MLL partner, is of particular interest due to its role in key oncogenic pathways involving MLL, Lens epithelium-derived growth factor (LEDGF), Enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2), and MYC [30–35]. While several studies have shown the importance of silencing in PC or CRPC [29, 36, 37], the role of Menin in taxane resistance in CRPC has not been fully understood. Since Menin inhibitors are mainly being tested in clinical trials for hematological malignancies, non-small cell lung cancer, pancreatic, and colorectal cancer [38, 39], understanding Menin’s role in drug resistance could quickly adapt its consideration to overcome taxane resistance in CRPC patients.

Previously, we generated the taxane-resistant CRPC models and explored their epigenetic vulnerabilities [6]. Herein, we identified MLL-Menin and MLL-WD Repeat Domain 5 (MLL-WDR5) inhibitors as promising epigenetic therapies to overcome drug resistance. Targeting Menin selectively inhibited the growth of resistant cells, while Menin depletion in parental cells prevented the development of drug resistance, emphasizing its critical role in resistance acquisition. Notably, Menin knockout caused negative enrichment of mTOR signaling and E2F target gene sets in resistant cells. Furthermore, co-treatment with docetaxel and the mTOR inhibitor (Torin-1) improved the taxane response in Menin-depleted resistant cells. CUT&RUN-sequencing and ChIP-qPCR analyses confirmed that Menin binds to the promoters of mTOR, Cyclin D1, and CDK20, highlighting its role in sustaining taxane resistance and regulating cell cycle progression. This regulatory activity led to reduced cellular proliferation and induced G1 cell cycle arrest in Menin-depleted, taxane-resistant cells. Ultimately, Menin knockout completely abolished tumor formation in our in vivo docetaxel-resistant CRPC model, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target.

Materials and methods

Generation of resistant cells

The Du145 and 22Rv1 cells (ATCC, #HTB-81; ATCC, #CRL-2505) were grown in RPMI-1640 media (Gibco, #11875093) supplemented with 10% FBS (Biowest, S1810) and 1% streptomycin/penicillin (Biowest, #L0022) in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. Du145 and 22Rv1 cells were exposed to Docetaxel (Sigma #01885) for 72 h with the IC50 dose (1 nM, initially) to establish drug resistance. The remaining population was allowed to recover in a drug-free medium. Subsequently, drug-recovered cells were subjected to a doubled dose of Docetaxel, and this cycle continued until cells reached a more than 100-fold increase in IC50. Docetaxel was prepared as a 500 mM main stock in DMSO (Sigma, #D2650). Throughout the manuscript, drug-sensitive parental cells are referred to as “P”, whereas docetaxel-resistant cells are designated “DtxR”.

Sulforhodamine B (SRB) viability assay

P or DtxR cells (4 × 103 cells for Du145, and 8 × 103 cells for 22Rv1) were seeded on 96-well plates 16 h before drug treatment. At the end of the exposure period, the cells were fixed with 10% (w/v) TCA (Sigma, #T6399) at 4 °C for 1 h, then TCA was washed off with dH2O, and the plates were dried. The wells were stained with 50 µl of 0.4% (w/v) SRB (Santa Cruz, #sc-253615) for 30 min, followed by washing with 1% acetic acid and drying the plates. The SRB dye was dissolved using 150 µl of 10 mM Trizma base (Sigma, #T1503), and measurements were performed at 564 nm using a microplate reader (Synergy H1 Hybrid reader, BioTek). MI-2 (Cayman Chemical, #29218), MI-3 (Cayman Chemical, #27214), MI-136 (Cayman Chemical, #19245), MI-463 (Cayman Chemical, #33525), WDR5-0103 (Cayman Chemical, #13945), Palbociclib (Cayman Chemical, #16273), Abemaciclib (Cayman Chemical, #17740), CINK4 (Cayman Chemical, #17974), Ribociclib (Cayman Chemical, #17666), Ryuvidine (Cayman Chemical, #16614), MHY1485 (Cayman Chemical, #18008), Epigenetics Screening Library (Cayman Chemical, #11076) were used for cell viability assays.

Colony formation assay

P or DtxR cells (1 × 103 cells for Du145, and 2 × 103 cells for 22Rv1) were seeded on 12-well plates 16 h before drug treatment. After treatment for 72 h, cells were cultured in a drug-free medium for 10–12 days. Colonies were fixed with methanol (Merck, #1.06009) for 10 min at -20 °C, stained with 0.5% (w/v) crystal violet (Merck, #1.09218) for 20 min, and then the dye was washed twice with distilled water. The quantification of colonies was performed using CellCounter software by Nghia Ho.

Cell cycle analysis

Cells were fixed with 70% ethanol (Merck, #100983) at -20 °C for at least 3 h. Residual ethanol was removed with PBS wash, and the cell pellet was stained with 200 µl of Cell Cycle Reagent (Luex Corporation, #MCH100106) for 20 min at RT in the dark. DNA content was analyzed by using the Muse Cell Analyzer, counting 5000 events.

Caspase-3/7 assay for apoptosis analysis

DtxR (1 × 105 cells/well) cells were seeded on 6-well plates 16 h before drug treatment. Muse™ caspase-3/7 reagent (CYTEK BIO, SKU #MCH100108) was diluted in PBS with a ratio of 1:8 and mixed with cell suspension. The mixture was incubated for 30 min in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. Muse™ Caspase 7-AAD working solution was added (Muse™ Caspase 7-AAD was diluted in 1 × Assay Buffer with a ratio of 1:75), and the tubes were incubated at RT for 5 min in the dark. Cells were counted by using the Muse Cell Analyzer, counting 5000 events.

siRNA-mediated silencing

siControl (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #4390843), siMLL (Ambion, #AM107890), siMEN1 (Ambion, #AM143227), and siWDR5 (Ambion, #AM136959) were used for silencing experiments. P and DtxR (2 × 10 5 cells/well) cells were seeded on 6-well plates 16 h before transfection. 75–100 pmol of each siRNA was transfected into the cells using Lipofectamine (Invitrogen, #L3000001) following the manufacturer’s protocols.

RT-qPCR

RNA samples were extracted using the Quick-RNA™ Microprep Kit (Zymo Research, #R1050) and reverse transcribed into cDNA with M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #28025013). RT-qPCR amplification was performed with 10 ng of cDNA and 150 nM of gene-specific primers (listed in Table S1) using LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I Master mix (Roche Diagnostics, #04707516001). The experiment was run on a LightCycler 480 (Roche Diagnostics) and incubated at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 58 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. The relative amounts of gene expression were calculated with the 2(−ΔΔCT) method.

Protein extraction and western blotting (WB)

Cells were lysed in RIPA Lysis Buffer (Sigma Aldrich, #R0278) containing 1 × Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Cell Signaling Technology, #5871) on ice for 15 min. The suspension was centrifuged at 17000 g for 20 min at 4 °C. Concentration analysis was performed with the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific™, #23225). Samples (15–20 µg of whole protein) were loaded on a 4–15% SDS-PAGE gel (Bio-Rad, #161–0317). Electrophoresis was performed at 90 V for 75 min. The separated proteins were transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Bio-Rad, #1620177) at 200 mA for 120 min and immunoblotted with primary antibodies (Table S2) overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000 dilution for #ab205718, 1:5000 dilution for #ab205719) for 1 h at RT. The signal was developed by using Immobilon Forte Western HRP substrate (Millipore, #WBLUF0100) and detected with the BIORAD Chemi-Doc XRS + System. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Analysis of clinical data

Menin mRNA levels in normal and tumor samples of Prostate Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-PRAD) were obtained from the Wanderer database using the Illumina HiSeq RNA sequencing data type [40]. Menin expression in PRAD with different Gleason scores was obtained from the TCGA Firehose Legacy study through cBioPortal [41]. Menin and mTOR mRNA expression correlation in metastatic prostate cancer was obtained from the Metastatic Prostate Cancer (SU2C/PCF Dream Team, Cell 2015) through cBioPortal [42]. Menin and Ki-67 correlation was obtained from the PRAD (TCGA, GDC) study. Clinical data showing the mTOR expression panel in patients with TNBC cancer receiving docetaxel therapy was obtained from the CTR-DBv2 database [43, 44].

Generating MEN1 and LEDGF knockout cells

The gRNA sequences were designed using Benchling software [45] as listed in Table S1. gRNA oligos were phosphorylated with T4 PNK (New England Biolabs, #M0201S) and annealed via the Feng Zheng protocol [46]. Briefly, the LentiCRISPR v2 plasmid (Addgene, 52961) was digested with BsmBI-v2 (NEB, R0739), dephosphorylated with Antarctic Phosphatase (NEB, #M0289), and purified using the NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Cleanup kit (Macherey-Nagel, #740609). Phosphorylated gRNA oligos and LentiCRISPR v2 plasmid were ligated with T4 DNA ligase (NEB, #M0202S). Transformation was performed with stbl3-competent bacteria with a heat shock protocol, and plasmids were isolated with the Macherey-Nagel MidiPlus Plasmid Isolation Kit (#740410). HEK293T (ATCC, CRL-11268) cells were co-transfected with psPAX2 (Addgene, #12260), pVSVg (Addgene, #14888), and guide-cloned lentiCRISPRv2 plasmids with PEI (ABP Bioscience, #P017-1) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Lentiviral particles were collected and filtered (0.45 μm) on the 2nd and 3rd days of the transfection. Protamine sulfate (8 µg/ml, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A11559II) was used to improve transduction efficiency; 2–10 µg/ml of puromycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #J61278.MB) was used for selection, and knockout efficiency was confirmed via western blotting using anti-MEN1 antibody (Table S2).

Generation of the MEN1-rescue system

To circumvent Cas9 activity, MEN1 cDNA was mutated. To destroy the NGG site with point silent mutation, Q5 Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (NEB, #E0554) protocols were followed. pBABE Hygro WT MEN1 (Addgene plasmid, #11024) was used, and the obtained plasmid carrying PAM mutation was generated. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1. To improve viral transduction efficiency, WT-MEN1 sequences were cloned into a lentiviral overexpression plasmid (Addgene, #163447). All mutations were confirmed by whole plasmid sequencing. Transfection of HEK293T cells and virus production were performed as described in detail above.

Competition assay

Menin knockout-resistant cells were labeled with mCherry fluorescent protein and sorted with the Sony SH800 Cell Sorter. Equal numbers (1:1) of control and Menin knockout-resistant cells were mixed, seeded, and co-cultured for 10 days. At the end of the co-culturing period, the percentage of mCherry signal in the mixed population was measured by Beckman Coulter Cytoflex flow cytometry.

RNA-seq and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

RNA-seq analysis was performed as formerly described by [6], and GSEA was performed using GSEA_4.3.2.app. Preranked DEG file.rnk (FDR ≤ 0.05) was uploaded, and the comparison was performed with h.all.v2023.1.Hs.symbols.gmt, c5.go.bp.v2023.2.Hs.symbols.gmt, c5.go.cc.v2023.2.Hs.symbols.gmt, c5.go.mf.v2023.2.Hs.symbols.gmt, c6.all.v2023.2.Hs.symbols.gmt datasets in the MsigDB database. Enrichment bubbles were generated by using the SRplot software [47].

Generation of flag-tagged MEN1 overexpression system

MEN1 cDNA sequence was amplified using Du145 cDNA pool with the forward and reverse primers covering the beginning and end of the cDNA sequences with NheI and NotI cut site additions, respectively. Stop codon was excluded for 3×FLAG addition. A PCR reaction was performed with NEB Phusion High Fidelity DNA polymerase, using 100 ng cDNA and 125 nM of each primer. PCR reaction incubated at 98 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 72 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 120 s. PCR products were run on agarose gel electrophoresis for base pair validation. PCR products were cleaned with Macherey-Nagel Gel and PCR clean-up kit (#740609). The pLJC2-GPT2-3×FLAG (Addgene, #163447) plasmid was cut with NheI (NEB, #R3131) and NotI (NEB, #R3189) enzymes, and the backbone was dephosphorylated with Antarctic Phosphatase (NEB, #M0289) and purified. The PCR product with compatible ends and the lentiviral backbone was ligated with T4 DNA ligase (NEB, #M0202). The transformation was performed with stbl3-competent bacteria with a heat shock protocol, and plasmids were isolated with the Macherey-Nagel MidiPlus Plasmid Isolation Kit (#740410). HEK293T cells were co-transfected with psPAX2 (Addgene, #12260), pVSVg (Addgene, #14888), and EV-3×FLAG or MEN1-3×FLAG plasmids using PEI (ABP Bioscience, #P017-1) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Lentiviral particles were collected, filtered, and transduced into cells with Protamine sulfate (8 µg/ml). Selection was performed with 2–10 µg/ml of puromycin, and validation of the overexpression was confirmed via western blotting using FLAG and MEN1 antibodies (antibodies are listed in Table S2).

Constitutive silencing via ShRNA

HEK293T cells were co-transfected with pUMVC (Addgene, #8449), pVSVg (Addgene, #14888), and MEN1sh-pLKO1 plasmid carrying puromycin resistance with PEI (ABP Bioscience, #P017-1) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. shFF (firefly luciferase) or shMEN1 carrying retroviral particles were collected, filtered, and transduced into the cells with Protamine sulfate (8 µg/ml). 2 µg/ml of puromycin was used for selection, and validation of silencing was confirmed via western blotting using MEN1 antibody (Table S2).

CUT&RUN assay and data analysis

CUT&RUN was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 2 × 10⁵ cells were bound to Concanavalin A magnetic beads for each condition. Digitonin was used to permeabilize the cell membrane, allowing primary antibodies and the pAG-MNase fusion enzyme to access the nuclei. Antibody treatment was applied to the permeabilized, bead-bound cells using 5 µg of FLAG antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, #F3165) and 5 µg of a non-specific IgG control antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #10400 C). The samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the pAG-MNase enzyme was added, and DNA cleavage was initiated by adding 3 µl of cold Ca²⁺, followed by incubation at 4 °C for 30 min. To release DNA fragments, samples were incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. DNA was then purified using spin columns, following the protocol of the CST DNA Purification Kit (#14209). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB #E7645S/L) and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 3000 platform. Raw FASTQ files were processed using the nf-core CUT&RUN pipeline (v3.2.2). Adapter sequences and low-quality bases were trimmed with Trim Galore (v0.6.6), and quality was assessed using FastQC (v0.12.1). Clean reads were aligned to the hg38 reference genome using Bowtie2 (v2.4.4). PCR duplicates were removed using Picard or SAMtools to reduce bias in downstream analyses. Peak calling was performed with SEACR (v1.3), and consensus peaks were merged using bedtools (v2.31.0). Genomic tracks were visualized using IGV (v2.16.1). Final outputs—including peak statistics, fragment size distribution, and signal tracks—were summarized and visualized using deepTools. Peak annotation was carried out using HOMER’s annotatePeaks.pl script.

ChIP-qPCR

EV-3×FLAG and MEN1-3×FLAG overexpressing resistant cells (3 × 106 cells/pulldown) were crosslinked, sheared, and pulled down as described in our previous report [6]. Briefly, 5 µg of FLAG antibody (Sigma Aldrich, #F3165) and a non-specific IgG antibody (Thermo Fischer Scientific, #10400 C) were mixed with Dynabeads® (Thermo Fisher protein A magnetic beads, #10001D). Next, the antibody-bead complex was incubated with pre-cleared chromatin overnight at 4 °C with rotation. The magnetic beads were washed with ChIP lysis buffer, low-salt buffer, high salt buffer, LiCl-containing buffer, and Tris-EDTA solution twice for 5 min, respectively. ChIP’ed DNA was eluted using an elution buffer containing 1% SDS (Bio-Rad, #1610418) and NaHCO3 (0.1 M, Merck, #S6014) for 30 min at RT with rotation. The eluted DNA-protein complex was reverse crosslinked and purified by using the ChIP DNA Clean & Concentrator kit (Zymo Research, #D5205). Promoter regions for target genes were obtained from the Eukaryotic Promoter DataBase (EPD) [48]. Primers were designed using the NCBI primer designing tool [49], and primer sequences are listed in Table S1. For the quantitative and simultaneous analysis of immunoprecipitated DNA, qPCR was run on the LightCycler 480 (Roche). ChIP-qPCR results were calculated by the % input method.

Generation of CRPC in vivo model

Twelve SCID mice (6–8 weeks old) were housed under appropriate conditions at the Koç University Animal Facility. All experimental procedures were approved by the Koç University Institutional Ethics Board (Protocol No: 2022.HADYEK.030). Stable Du145-P and Du145-DtxR cell lines expressing Firefly Luciferase (Fluc) and mCherry were generated via viral transduction. For tumor implantation, six mice were used per group. Du145-P (1 × 106 cells) and Du145-DtxR (6 × 106 cells) were injected subcutaneously into each mouse in 100 µl of 1:2 diluted Matrigel (Cultrex, 3432-005-01). Tumor growth was monitored weekly using an IVIS Lumina III Bioluminescence Imaging System. Fluc activity was assessed by intraperitoneal injection of D-Luciferin (GoldBio, 115144-35-9) at a dose of 150 µg/g body weight. Mice were sacrificed four weeks post-injection for downstream analyses.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed in Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, Inc.), and statistical tests were conducted as described in the figure legends.

Results

Targeting MLL-Menin restores taxane susceptibility in resistant CRPC

We generated docetaxel-resistant Du145 and 22Rv1 cells as previously described [6]. Briefly, cells were treated with taxane for 72 h, and the surviving fractions were allowed to grow until they completely recovered (Fig. 1A). This dose increment strategy was implemented until the cells achieved more than a 100-fold increase in their IC50 value. Drug-sensitive parental cells (‘P’) and docetaxel-resistant cells (‘DtxR’) were used throughout the study, and the resistance profile was evidenced through cell viability assays. In both cell line models, even at elevated doses, the resistant cells retained significantly higher viability compared to their drug-naive counterparts (Fig. 1B-C and Figure S1A-B). In our previous study, we identified five groups of epigenetic enzymes that confer vulnerability in taxane-resistant CRPC models [6]. Specifically, combining taxanes with inhibitors targeting MLL-Menin, MLL-WDR5, CBP/p300, BRPF, PRMT5, and SIRT1 restored sensitivity in docetaxel- and cabazitaxel-resistant CRPC (Du145 [6], and 22Rv1) cells (Fig. 1D). It is noteworthy that this sensitization was unique to the resistant counterparts (Figure S1C). As inhibitors of MLL complex proteins are effective revertants, the focus has initially been placed on these two inhibitors, considering the vacancy in the literature regarding these inhibitors and resistance mechanisms.

Based on results from the chemical screen, in both cell lines, we tested and validated the efficacy of various inhibitors of MLL-Menin (MI-2, MI-3, MI-136, and MI-463) and MLL-WDR5 (WDR5-0103) with docetaxel (Fig. 1E-F and Figure S1D-E). Interestingly, MLL inhibitors synergistically reversed the resistance when combined with taxanes in both cell models (Figure S2). G2/M arrest is a significant indicator of taxane efficacy through inhibition of microtubule depolymerization [50]. Due to the resistance profile, docetaxel treatment was insufficient to induce an arrest in resistant cells. However, co-administration of an MLL-Menin inhibitor and docetaxel restored G2/M arrest, further supporting the re-sensitization (Fig. 1G). This prolonged G2/M arrest in combination treatments ultimately led to cell death through apoptosis, as evidenced by the Caspase 3/7 activation (Fig. 1H). MI-2, MI-3, MI-136, and MI-463 disrupt the MLL–Menin interaction, whereas WDR5-0103 inhibits the catalytic activity of MLL and WDR5 (Fig. 1I). Since these compounds target three distinct components of the MLL complex, we aimed to investigate the roles of these three proteins in docetaxel resistance.

siRNA knockdown of MLL, Menin, and WDR5 demonstrated that MLL and WDR5 are essential for the survival of both parental and resistant cells, consistent with their broad roles in epigenetic regulation and oncogenic transcription [51, 52]. In contrast, Menin silencing selectively impaired the proliferation of resistant cells (Fig. 2A). We subsequently assessed Menin mRNA levels between prostate adenocarcinoma samples and normal prostate tissues using publicly available RNA-seq data (TCGA-PRAD) obtained by the TCGA Wanderer database. Notably, Menin mRNA was significantly upregulated in tumor samples compared to normal tissues (Fig. 2B), which directed our interest toward investigating Menin dependency in resistant cells. While few studies have highlighted the role of Menin and MLL complexes in androgen-dependent PC [29, 36, 37], their significance in docetaxel-resistant CRPC has remained largely unexamined, making this study the first to address this gap.

Depletion of Menin specifically reduces taxane-resistant Du145 growth

Next, we assessed the Menin expression in a TCGA data set comprising 501 samples (Firehose Legacy) from patients who underwent radical prostatectomy (GDAC-PRAD-20160128). The analysis revealed that Menin expression was elevated in samples with higher Gleason scores (Fig. 2C), which prompted us to generate Menin knockout cells and examine its impact on both parental and resistant cells. We isolated two individual knockout clones from the parental and the resistant cells (Fig. 2D). The absence of Menin in parental cells had almost no effect on cell growth, similar to what was observed with siRNA. However, in resistant cells, Menin knockout led to a significant reduction in cell growth, with KO#1 showing the most substantial decrease (Fig. 2E). The growth pace of resistant KO#1 was notably slow, which made experimental time management challenging. Therefore, in further experiments, we proceeded with KO#2, which showed a complete knockout of Menin and yielded a more manageable population size for phenotypic assessments (Fig. 2E-F). To evaluate whether the observed phenotype was Menin-specific, we performed a rescue experiment. Wild-type Menin cDNA, with a guide-specific PAM mutant, was generated to circumvent Cas9 activity. This Menin sequence was cloned into a lentiviral overexpression plasmid and introduced into the Menin-depleted cells (Fig. 2G). As demonstrated in Fig. 2H-I, wild-type Menin overexpression restored the colony numbers in Menin knockout-resistant cells.

Additionally, we generated Menin knockout models in our AR-positive PC cell line (LNCaP) and a second docetaxel-resistant CRPC cell line (22Rv1-DtxR) (Figure S3). As previously reported, targeting Menin in AR-positive PC has been demonstrated to be an effective strategy, due to direct interaction with Menin and AR [29]. Notably, our findings in LNCaP cells supported this reduction in cell growth (Figure S3A-B). Additionally, Menin depletion reduced cell growth in both parental and resistant 22Rv1 cells (Figure S3C-D). The divergent responses to Menin loss between the two parental CPRC models may be explained by their differential AR expression. Although both cell lines represent CRPC, 22Rv1 cells express AR, whereas Du145 cells lack AR expression [53]. However, which advantages Menin conferred to docetaxel-resistance, particularly in AR-absent models such as Du145, still intrigued us.

Menin is indispensable for the acquisition of drug resistance in CRPC

To assess the role of Menin in the acquisition of drug resistance, we silenced Menin constitutively in parental cells and attempted to re-establish resistance by exposing these cells to docetaxel (Fig. 2J-K). Following the fourth treatment cycle, control cells (shFF) survived the docetaxel treatment, and this remaining population exhibited a drug-resistant phenotype. In contrast, approximately 80% of the Menin-silenced parental cells underwent apoptosis, suggesting that Menin is essential for the cells’ acquisition of drug resistance, as the remaining population was unable to recover and failed to adapt to the process (Fig. 2L).

Transcriptomic analysis reveals key biological processes contributing to drug resistance

These cumulative findings prompted us to focus on the N-terminal MLL target Menin to uncover the mechanisms contributing to taxane resistance. To reveal Menin-dependent transcriptional differences between parental and resistant cells, we performed RNA sequencing comparing (i) parental versus resistant cells, and (ii) wild-type versus Menin-null conditions in both parental and resistant settings. The transcriptomic differences between our parental and resistant cells, as well as the ABCB1-mediated drug resistance mechanism through BRPF1, have been reported previously [6]. To assess whether Menin’s role in resistance might involve ABCB1, we first examined their interaction using Menin inhibitor or Menin knockout models; however, no significant regulation was observed. Additionally, Menin did not show significant enrichment on the ABCB1 promoter in our resistant cell model (Figure S4), supporting the conclusion that Menin-mediated resistance occurs through alternative pathways. We then specifically analyzed RNA-seq data from Menin wild-type and two different Menin null clones in resistant cells (NT, KO#1, and KO#2). As illustrated in the PCA plot (Fig. 3A), each clone clustered closely with its biological replicate, and Menin loss resulted in similar transcriptomic alterations across both clones. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) those were commonly altered in both clones (MENKO#1 ∩ MENKO#2) in the same direction (upregulation or downregulation) relative to gNT were overlapped, and genes meeting the criteria FDR < 0.05 and |Log2FC| ≥ 0.5 were considered statistically significant. In common DEGs between two Menin knockouts, 2077 genes were upregulated, and 2120 genes were downregulated upon Menin knockout, as depicted in Fig. 3B.

Given that Menin ablation significantly impeded the growth of resistant colonies, we focused on the potential advantages Menin may confer to resistant cells. To this end, we compared the genes showing opposite directions in DtxR and following Menin knockout. Among these, 798 (330 up to down and 468 down to up) genes reversed direction in response to Menin ablation (Figure S5A). Interestingly, as cells transition from a drug-naive state to a resistant phenotype, gene sets related to E2F targets, G2M checkpoint, and mTOR signaling showed positive enrichment (NES values of 2.78, 2.09, and 1.78, respectively) (Figure S5B) [6]. In contrast, these gene sets exhibited a complete reversal in the absence of Menin, with NES values of (-1.46), (-1.32), and (-1.04), respectively (Fig. 3C-D). Menin ablation led to negative enrichment of gene sets, including the MLL complex and histone lysine methylation, providing additional validation of our knockout models (Fig. 3C).

Menin regulates mTOR expression and its depletion, along with mTOR inhibition, further sensitizes the cells to docetaxel

To investigate how Menin supports the transcriptional programs active in resistant cells, we revisited our GSEA results. During the acquisition of resistance, mTOR signaling, E2F targets, and the G2/M checkpoint were enriched in the positive direction, reflecting coordinated activation of these pathways. Upon MEN1 knockout, these pathways shifted to negative enrichment, indicating their attenuation (Fig. 3D). The leading-edge genes associated with this negative transition are shown in Fig. 3D, providing a clearer view of the transcriptional basis underlying the pathway-level changes. Collectively, this data indicates that docetaxel-resistant cells adopt a growth-signaling–activated, checkpoint-engaged transcriptional state, characterized by activation of mTOR signaling, E2F-driven transcription, and the G2/M checkpoint. Functional perturbation reveals that Menin is required to maintain these pathway activities. Thus, resistant cells exhibit a dependency on Menin to sustain adaptive pathway activation during taxane resistance, and targeting Menin may contribute to re-sensitization. Supporting this connection, a review of publicly available ChIP-seq datasets revealed prominent Menin binding peaks around the mTOR promoter in leukemia cell lines [54–58]. These findings prompted us to further explore the Menin-mTOR relationship in our taxane-resistant model.

We conducted CUT&RUN analysis to assess Menin’s chromatin occupancy in Du145-DtxR cells. Using SEACR (v1.3), we identified 2589 chromatin peaks. Menin binding was predominantly enriched at transcription start sites (TSS) of target genes (Fig. 4A). IGV track analysis revealed significant Menin occupancy at the promoter regions of mTOR, Rictor, RPS6KB1, and EIF4EBP1 genes (Fig. 4B). These findings were further validated by ChIP-qPCR, confirming Menin’s enrichment at the mTOR promoter (-500 bp to + 100 bp) in Du145-DtxR cells (Figure S6A-B). We next assessed the expression of these target genes in Menin knockout and rescued cells. Gene expression levels were significantly reduced in the absence of Menin and were restored upon Menin re-expression (Fig. 4C). Lastly, analysis of patient datasets revealed a significant correlation between Menin and mTOR expression in metastatic PC, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.47 (Fig. 4D).

Given that several mTOR inhibitors are in clinical trials, we asked whether they could be used on taxane-resistant cells. Indeed, simultaneous mTOR inhibition (via Torin-1) and Menin depletion further enhanced the taxane response (Fig. 4E, Figure S5C). This phenotype was reversed upon the restoration of Menin expression, showing that Menin is involved in regulating mTOR expression and determining the effectiveness of mTOR signaling inhibition. The critical role of Menin status in determining the taxane response may be linked to the decreased expression of mTOR and its related downstream signaling in the absence of Menin. In this cellular environment, the use of an inhibitor seems to affect the residual mTOR signaling essential for taxane resistance, resulting in a significantly improved taxane response compared to Menin wild-type cells. Accordingly, Menin knockout cells demonstrated increased levels of apoptosis in response to the docetaxel and Torin-1 combination (Fig. 4F). We also used the DEPMAP portal to analyze drug response in different cell lines regarding mTOR expression levels. In 328 cell lines tested, higher mTOR expression positively correlated with the increase in Docetaxel IC50 (Fig. 4G). Finally, activation of the mTOR pathway via MHY1485 also decreased the Docetaxel response in our parental cells (Fig. 4H). These findings underscore the functional interaction between Menin and mTOR and support the therapeutic potential of co-targeting these pathways to overcome taxane resistance in PC.

Menin is involved in cell cycle machinery and regulates cyclin D1 and CDK20 expression

The observed reduction in cell proliferation, accompanied by decreased mTOR expression and negative enrichment of cell cycle-related genes in Menin knockout cells, prompted further investigation into the underlying mechanisms.

To investigate whether Menin promotes cell growth, we analyzed its expression in clinical data from PC cases, alongside the cell growth indicator Ki-67. Interestingly, a positive correlation was observed between high Menin expression and Ki-67 levels, suggesting a potential role for Menin in driving cell proliferation (Fig. 5A). To functionally assess this, we conducted a competition assay, labeling Menin knockout-resistant cells with a mCherry reporter and co-culturing them with Menin-wild-type cells for 10 days. The analysis of the mCherry signal revealed that the mixed population predominantly consisted of Menin-wild-type cells, suggesting that Menin knockout-resistant cells had a slower division rate (Fig. 5B). Consistently, cell cycle analysis showed an increased G1 population in both Menin knockout-resistant clones (Fig. 5C). Previous studies have shown that CDK4/6 inhibitors are particularly effective against rapidly proliferating cancer cells [59, 60]. In line with their slow-cycling phenotype, Menin-knockout DtxR cells displayed reduced sensitivity to several CDK4/6 inhibitors, including Palbociclib, Abemaciclib, CINK4, and Ribociclib (Figure S7).

Next, we assessed the levels of key cyclins in non-synchronized populations of Menin wild-type and knockout clones. Cyclin D1 is essential for initiating cell division during the G1 phase, while Cyclin E1 is predominantly expressed in late G1 and is critical for the G1/S transition [61]. Menin knock-out DtxR cells displayed reduced levels of Cyclin D1 and elevated levels of Cyclin E1 (Figure S8A), consistent with the G1/S arrest phenotype.

Previous studies have shown that MEN1 enhances MYC expression, which in turn upregulates Cyclin D1 and promotes the G1/S cell cycle progression [62, 63]. CDK20, a cyclin-dependent kinase and key regulator of the cell cycle, has been reported to activate CDK2, facilitating the G1/S transition [64, 65] and regulating Cyclin D1 expression in tumor cells [66]. To explore whether Menin directly regulates the expression of these cell cycle-related factors, we assessed its chromatin binding profile. Our CUT&RUN-seq analysis revealed significant Menin enrichment at the promoter regions of the Cyclin D1, CDK20, MYC, and E2F1 genes (Fig. 5D). ChIP-qPCR further confirmed Menin occupancy at the promoters of these key regulators (Figure S8B). Consistently, Menin knockout cells exhibited a marked reduction in the expression of these factors (Fig. 5E), and the re-expression of Menin in knockout cells restored the expression levels of these genes (Fig. 5F-G).

Finally, a total of 157 genes were identified as commonly upregulated during resistance development, downregulated upon Menin depletion, and directly bound by Menin on chromatin (Fig. 5H). Once again, when analyzed in GSEA, computing overlaps databases, these common genes were enriched in mTOR and cell cycle gene sets (Fig. 5I). Together, these findings suggest that Menin directly contributes to the transcriptional regulation of mTOR and critical cell cycle regulators—particularly Cyclin D1 and CDK20—in taxane-resistant CRPC.

Menin loss in docetaxel-resistant CRPC led to complete suppression of tumor growth

To explore Menin’s role in more clinically relevant settings, we examined the effect of Menin loss in an in vivo model. Specifically, we used xenograft models with Firefly Luciferase-mCherry-expressing Du145-P and Du145-DtxR cells, comparing control guide and Menin knockout conditions (n = 6) (Fig. 6A). Cells were injected subcutaneously, and tumor growth was monitored over four weeks. Parental cells successfully formed tumors, whereas tumor size was reduced in Menin-ablated cells at 3 and 4 weeks post-injection. Furthermore, Menin knockout in docetaxel-resistant cells completely abolished tumor growth compared to control cells (Fig. 6A and Figure S9-10). This loss of tumor signal likely reflects tumor-intrinsic consequences of Menin depletion, potentially driven by mechanisms such as downregulated mTOR signaling, growth arrest, apoptotic induction, or impaired vascularization. These results demonstrate an essential role for Menin in the establishment of docetaxel-resistant CRPC tumors in vivo.

We also analyzed mRNA levels in our xenograft samples and found that mTOR expression was upregulated in wild-type resistant tumors (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, IHC data from the Human Protein Atlas indicated that mTOR expression was elevated in high-grade PC tissues compared to low-grade PCs (Fig. 6C). Finally, we aimed to analyze PC patients treated with docetaxel and evaluate drug response patterns using the CTR-DBv2 database. However, suitable data for PC were unavailable. Interestingly, in the treatment response analysis of TNBC patients receiving docetaxel, a notable pattern emerged. Patients with higher mTOR pathway expression were significantly non-responsive, whereas those with lower mTOR pathway expression exhibited better responses to docetaxel treatment (Fig. 6D) [44].

Overall, MLL-Menin inhibitors are effective in reversing taxane resistance in CRPC. Additionally, Menin contributes to taxane resistance through mTOR, making the combined targeting of Menin and mTOR a promising strategy. Furthermore, Menin depletion results in G1 arrest and significant disruption in tumor formation in taxane-resistant CRPCs. Key findings of this study are illustrated in the graphical abstract shown in Fig. 6E.

Discussion

Drug resistance is a common phenomenon that is an obstacle to successful therapy due to several emerging mechanisms [67]. Identifying drug-specific mechanisms is crucial for uncovering potential therapeutic targets, which can lead to improved treatment outcomes. As the dynamic and reversible nature of epigenetic regulation allows for broad and impactful targeting, drug resistance research has also embraced this approach, leading to the testing of the efficacy of epigenetic inhibitors in clinical trials and FDA approval [68]. The effects of MLL targeting in both leukemia and PC have been previously demonstrated [29, 69–72]. Our group is the first to demonstrate the efficacy of MLL-Menin inhibitors in overcoming docetaxel-resistant CRPC. We found that several MLL-Menin inhibitors (MI-2, MI-3, MI-136, and MI-463) effectively re-sensitized resistant cells, yielding a significant synergy.

Menin was initially described as a tumor suppressor [73], and also exerted regulatory effects in immune cells by limiting mTORC1 activation to prevent T-cell exhaustion [74]. Later on, Menin was found to play a role in MLL-related leukemogenesis [35], and is now recognized for its oncogenic roles in various cancers, including breast, glioma, hepatocellular carcinoma, sarcoma, and PC [29, 33, 36, 37, 75–77]. Consistent with previous reports, Menin inhibition suppressed cellular growth in AR-positive PC and CRPC cell line models. In contrast, in AR-negative CRPC, it selectively impaired the growth of docetaxel-resistant cells while exerting only a minor effect on parental cells, as shown by our in vitro and in vivo studies. Menin’s clinical significance was further supported by its elevated expression in tumor samples compared with normal tissues and its positive association with higher Gleason scores in PRAD. Collectively, these findings underscore the context-dependent nature of Menin–mTOR signaling, and position Menin as a promising yet cell–type–specific therapeutic target.

Menin plays a critical role in linking transcription factors to histone-modifying complexes, influencing gene expression and chromatin dynamics [78]. Menin expression was crucial for enabling drug-naive cells to acquire resistance, potentially through the upregulation of mTOR and MYC signaling, both of which are commonly associated with drug resistance and advanced tumors [23, 79–86]. Menin depletion significantly reduced these targets in our docetaxel-resistant CRPC model, and similar to our findings, Menin silencing in PC cells resulted in the downregulation of the PI3K-AKT pathway [36]. Additionally, the co-localization of Menin and MYC on chromatin emphasized their collaborative role in cancer progression [63]. Although targeting MYC directly is challenging due to the lack of druggable pockets, mTOR inhibition with temsirolimus was investigated in clinical trials for CRPC. However, the trial was discontinued due to the limited efficacy of the drug and its adverse effects, and future studies were suggested with combination approaches or novel PI3K pathway inhibitors [87]. On the other hand, Menin inhibitors have recently attracted considerable attention in clinical research, with Revumenib receiving FDA approval in November 2024 for the treatment of relapsed or refractory acute leukemias [39]. As Menin targeting and mTOR inhibition via Torin-1 exhibited significant synergy in the docetaxel-resistant CRPC model, we recommend a similar approach for future therapies.

Most cancer cells heavily rely on growth factors due to their critical role in promoting the G1 to S phase transition, which facilitates rapid cell division [88]. Activation of receptor tyrosine kinases increases expression of Cyclin D1, the primary cyclin in the G1 state, facilitating progression into the S phase [89]. A key phenotype observed in Menin knockout-resistant cells was their slower progression through the cell cycle, likely due to reduced mTOR and MYC activity, which may delay the rapid transition from the G1 to the S phase. Furthermore, Menin exhibited significant enrichment of Cyclin D1 and CDK20 promoter, suggesting Menin-dependent transcriptional regulation also contributes to cell cycle progression. CDK20 and Cyclin D1 interaction was reported in promoting ovarian carcinoma growth [66]. Additionally, CDK20 depletion was shown to inhibit cancer cell proliferation [90, 91]. In line with these findings, we showed that Menin is required for maintaining Cyclin D1 and CDK20 expression, ensuring the growth of CRPC cells.

In summary, MLL-Menin inhibitors effectively reverse docetaxel resistance in CRPC. Menin acts as an essential contributor, promoting docetaxel resistance by regulating mTOR signaling. Menin also supports the growth of taxane-resistant CRPC cells, and its loss leads to G1/S arrest. Finally, the loss of Menin eliminates tumorigenicity in docetaxel-resistant CRPC cells in xenograft models. These findings establish Menin not only as an important factor for docetaxel resistance but also as a therapeutic cornerstone, with dual targeting of Menin and mTOR offering a promising strategy to redefine treatment paradigms in taxane-refractory PC.
