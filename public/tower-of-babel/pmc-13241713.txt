Protocol for evaluating salt and alkaline tolerance in maize seedlings using a sand-bed germination assay
Authors: Jiaxin Yang, Ye Wang, Yajun Zhang, Jinyu Xi, Sunil S. Gangurde, Linmao Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a protocol to independently and concurrently evaluate salt and alkaline tolerance in maize at the seedling stage using a controlled sand-bed germination assay. We describe steps for preparing standardized stress solutions, establishing the sand-bed assay, applying stresses either independently or in combination, and measuring multiple phenotypic traits. Furthermore, we detail procedures for ranking germplasm using a comprehensive scoring system based on a membership function. This protocol enables reproducible, large-scale screening of maize germplasm at the seedling stage.

Before you begin

Soil salinity and alkalinity constitute major abiotic stresses limiting global maize production.1 The development of salt- and alkaline-tolerant maize varieties is crucial for the agricultural use of affected lands. Progress, however, has been hindered by the absence of precise and standardized phenotyping methods, which limits the identification and deployment of resilient germplasm. Critically, salt stress (neutral pH, dominated by NaCl/Na2SO4) and alkali stress (alkaline pH, dominated by Na2CO3/NaHCO3) induce distinct physiological injuries, yet they are often confounded in screening. Traditional field-based evaluation is both time-consuming and susceptible to environmental variability, further impeding reproducible selection. To address these limitations, this protocol establishes a controlled, high-throughput system for the independent and parallel evaluation of salt and alkaline tolerance in maize at the seedling stage. The experimental protocol detailed herein entails the use of genetically purified maize seeds, clean quartz sand, germination boxes, and controlled-environment growth room facilities. Prior to setup, verify that all seed accessions have been genetically purified and stored under uniform conditions. Unless otherwise specified, all chemical solutions should be prepared fresh immediately before use.

Innovation

Discovery of a stable sand-bed system retains consistent moisture and stress levels, eliminating the need for daily watering

We present a phenotyping protocol designed to systematically differentiate between salt and alkali stress responses in maize seedlings. By employing a modular, standardized sand-bed system, we overcome key limitations of conventional soil-based and hydroponic assays, such as fluctuating ion concentrations and pH instability. The method ensures consistent moisture and stress distribution, eliminates environmental variability, and supports reproducible, high-throughput screening across large germplasm panels.

A tripartite stress regime enables independent assessment of salt stress, alkaline stress, and their interactions

A principal innovation lies in the tripartite stress regime, which facilitates the controlled application of salt (NaCl/Na2SO4) and alkali (Na2CO3/NaHCO3) treatments either independently, in combination, or in sequence. This experimental framework empowers the precise assignment of physiological responses to ionic toxicity, elevated pH, or their interaction a mechanistic distinction that is challenging to achieve with conventional assays.

A buffered alkaline treatment using a NaHCO3/Na2CO3 mixture ensures a stable target pH (e.g., 9.0) is maintained for the duration of the experiment

A key feature is a buffered alkaline treatment that locks in a target pH (e.g., 9.0), preventing the drift common in traditional assays. This creates a stable, realistic environment that accurately mirrors field alkaline stress.

The proposed framework employs a multi-trait scoring system that amalgamates key morphological and growth metrics, yielding a composite index for reliable germplasm comparison and ranking

A final innovation is a composite tolerance index, generated through membership function analysis, which synthesizes multiple seedling traits into a single robust score. This advances beyond single-trait screening, enabling reproducible, quantitative germplasm ranking. The integrated system provides a scalable and physiologically relevant tool for breeding and functional studies of saline-alkaline tolerance.

Sand-bed germination assay

As described in Preparation (Step 7), we determined the gravimetric SWC of sand to be approximately 0.29 g water/g dry sand. To achieve 60% saturation, the required water volume per 1000 g dry sand is: 1000 g × 0.29 × 0.60 = 174 mL.b.Stir thoroughly to achieve uniform moisture (∼60% of saturated water content).Note: Hydrate the sand until it reaches 60–80% of its maximum water-holding capacity. Maintain this standard consistently across both control and stress treatments.2.Set up germination boxesa.Place 1250 g of moist sand into a germination box to form a 5 cm-thick base layer.b.Level the sand surface using a flat card.c.Arrange 30 seeds per accession in a 3 × 10 grid on the sand surface.Note: Germination box is a rigid plastic container with internal dimensions of approximately 33 cm (Length) × 20 cm (Width) × 18 cm (Height), equipped with removable plastic dividers (baffles) that allow four independent accessions to be placed in the same box without mixing (Figure 1F).d.Cover seeds with 750 g of moist sand to form a 3 cm-thick overlay and level the surface.Note: We adopted the sand germination method by placing the seeds in the middle of the sand, which facilitates seed water absorption and germination.e.Seal the box with transparent plastic wrap to prevent moisture loss.f.Label each box carefully with accession and treatment information.Notes: Three independent biological replicates in separate boxes per accession per treatment are highly recommended. Under plastic wrap, the internal temperature remains very close to the chamber setting (±1°C) due to the thin wrap and the large thermal mass of the wet sand. Relative humidity in the headspace reaches near saturation (>90%) within the 7-days period. Importantly, this condition is consistent across all treatments (control and stresses) and does not confound comparisons, as all boxes are sealed uniformly. The plastic wrap is not opened at any point during germination and growth. The system is designed to be completely closed to eliminate manual intervention, which would otherwise introduce variability. With moisture content maintained at 60% saturation and the wrap sealed, stable moisture levels are preserved throughout the experiment. Thus, no replenishment is required for water and stress solution. Only accessions with a germination percentage of ≥80% under control conditions should proceed to stress treatments. For accessions with borderline germination (75%–80%), we recommend increasing the number of seeds per replicate to 40 and performing four biological replicates as a practical precaution, although this adjustment has not been statistically validated. Accessions with <75% control germination should be excluded from stress tolerance evaluation, as poor seed quality will confound any meaningful comparison. In such cases, obtain fresh seeds or omit the accession entirely.

Comprehensive evaluation of maize resistant germplasm

Timing: 2 h

The maize inbred lines were simultaneously evaluated under three parallel stress regimes: salt only (100 mM NaCl), alkali only (35.2 mM NaHCO3/Na2CO3 buffer, pH 8.9), and combined saline-alkaline stress (40 mM mixture with a NaCl:NaHCO3:Na2SO4 ratio of 1:2:1, pH 8.3), alongside a control treatment (ddH2O). For each accession, relative phenotypic indices such as relative germination percentage, relative shoot length, and relative dry weight were calculated for each regime. Using the membership function method, separate comprehensive D values were subsequently derived for salt tolerance, alkali tolerance, and combined tolerance. These three D values can be directly compared to determine whether a given accession exhibits specific tolerance to salt (ionic toxicity), to alkali (high pH), or to both.

Expected outcomes

Successful execution of this protocol will yield quantitative data for determining optimal stress tolerance levels and for identifying the best maize germplasm tolerant to salt stress, alkali stress or combined salt/alkali stress. Also, this protocol provides information on the number traits to be considered during screening of salt or alkali stress.

We describe below the typical results from the optimization phase, which validates the recommended conditions specified in the ‘materials and equipment’ section.

Determination of optimal salt stress concentration

To determine an appropriate salt stress level for screening, we evaluated eight maize inbred lines under three NaCl concentrations (85, 100, and 115 mM) using a sand-bed assay. Key relative indices namely Relative Germination Rate (RGR), Relative Germination Index (RGI), Relative Shoot Length (RSL), and Relative Dry Weight (RDW) were calculated. A representative dataset revealed that 85 mM NaCl produced excessively high indices with insufficient discriminatory power, while 115 mM caused severe, uniform growth suppression with overly low indices. In contrast, 100 mM NaCl induced significant divergence in RSL and RDW values compared to 85 mM, yielding clear phenotypic separation without excessive mortality. Determination of optimal alkaline stress system and concentration

To establish a reproducible alkaline stress regime, we systematically optimized buffer composition and concentration. An initial system (NaHCO3:Na2CO3 = 5:1, pH 9.5, 48–72 mM) inhibited seedling emergence across all lines. We therefore adjusted the buffer to a 10:1 molar ratio (pH 8.9). Screening concentrations from 33 mM to 66 mM showed that 55–66 mM caused severe phenotypic damage. In summary, our optimization process confirms that the protocol effectively identifies stress levels with clear physiological relevance. The standardized treatments, 100 mM NaCl, 35.2 mM alkaline buffer (10:1 NaHCO3:Na2CO3), and 40 mM mixed saline-alkaline solution support reproducible phenotyping. Consequently, maize germplasm can be consistently and reliably ranked via the integrated scoring framework detailed in ‘quantification and statistical analysis.’

The successful application of this protocol yields quantitative phenotypic data across control and stress conditions, enabling the ranking of maize germplasm via a comprehensive membership function score (D). Tolerant accessions, characterized by higher D values, exhibit superior germination rate, reduced growth inhibition, and stable biomass allocation under stress. The protocol’s discriminatory power is demonstrated by its clear discrimination between known tolerant and sensitive accessions. For example, in a practical application screening of 340 diverse maize accessions, the seven relative indices under salt stress were integrated into a D value for each entry via the membership function method (Tables S1 andS2). Subsequent cluster analysis classified the population into five distinct tolerance categories: highly salt-tolerant (17 accessions), salt-tolerant (63 accessions), moderately salt-tolerant (154 accessions), salt-sensitive (83 accessions), and highly salt-sensitive (23 accessions) (Figure 6 and Table 1). Limitations

While this protocol provides a controlled and reproducible system for screening salt and alkaline tolerance at the seedling stage, several limitations should be considered.

Higher setup complexity and material requirements: Preparing the sand (washing, sterilization, and moisture calculation) demands more time and effort than simply placing filter paper in Petri dishes.

No direct visualization of germination: In the sand system, seeds are covered with sand, making daily germination count dependent on careful observation or gentle probing. By contrast, filter paper allows direct, unobstructed visualization.

Stress concentrations may need adjustment: The optimized concentrations (100 mM NaCl, 35.2 mM alkaline buffer, and 40 mM mixed stress) were determined using a subset of inbred lines. For germplasm exhibiting extreme sensitivity or tolerance, pilot testing is recommended. Further, the use of a fixed alkaline stress condition (pH 8.9, NaHCO3/Na2CO3 buffer) may not represent the diverse pH and ionic compositions found in natural saline-alkaline soils. Researchers are encouraged to adjust stress levels or solution compositions based on local soil characteristics.

Field conditions might have slight differences: Although seedling-stage evaluations under controlled conditions show correlation with field performance in saline-alkaline soils, they may not fully capture the complex interactions and cumulative effects experienced by mature plants in the field. The protocol is most effective for assessing relative tolerance among genotypes rather than predicting absolute field resistance.

Seed quality may interfere with germination %: Seed quality strongly influences germination and early seedling performance under stress. Although relative indices can partially mitigate variation due to seed vigor, low-quality or long-stored seeds often exhibit disproportionately poor stress responses. We recommend using seeds with ≥80% germination under control conditions to ensure reliable and comparable results across genotypes.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Low or uneven germination rate under stress (Steps 4–7).

Potential solution

Reason: Poor seed viability or vigor. Solution: Ensure seed viability by pre-testing germination under control conditions. Use fresh stress solutions and maintain consistent sand moisture.

Problem 2

pH of alkaline solution drifts during experiment (Step 6)

Potential solution

The alkaline buffer solution is prepared fresh daily and adjusted to pH 8.9–9.1 prior to mixing with sand. Because the boxes remain sealed without replenishment, pH drift is minimal. Our validation further indicates that maize germination and early growth are unaffected by pH variations within the range of 8.2–9.2. Consequently, no in-experiment pH adjustment is required. If a user observes extreme pH deviation (e.g., >9.2), it likely stems from incorrect initial preparation; in such cases, the solution should be discarded and freshly prepared.

Reason: CO2 absorption/loss from bicarbonate buffer. Solution: Prepare alkali solution fresh daily. Use a pH-stable buffer system or monitor and adjust pH every 24 h if necessary.

Problem 3

Seedling etiolation or abnormal growth (Step 3).

Potential solution

Reason: Insufficient light intensity in greenhouse. Solution: Verify light intensity settings. Avoid overcrowding of seedlings in germination boxes.

Problem 4

Fungal growth on sand or seedlings (Step 3).

Potential solution

Reason: Incomplete sand sterilization or high humidity. Solution: Sterilize sand by autoclaving or baking at 140°C for 4 h before use. Avoid over-wetting sand. Ensure greenhouse or growth chamber has adequate air circulation.

Problem 5

Roots are difficult to separate from sand without damage

Potential solution

Reason: Sand is too dry or too compacted. Solution: Ensure moisture content is correct (60% saturation). Gently rinse sand away under a slow stream of water.

Problem 6

High variability seedling length/biomass within a treatment replicate. (Steps 2, 9).

Potential solution

Reason: Uneven sand moisture, seed placement depth or seed quality. Solution: Standardize sand mixing procedure and seedling selection rigorously. Ensure seeds are placed on a leveled surface and covered uniformly.

Problem 7

Inconsistent results between biological replicates

Potential solution

Reason: Environmental gradients in growth chamber (temperature, light). Solution: Randomize the positions of germination boxes within the greenhouse daily or use a rotating tray. Perform at least 4 replicates and remove the replicates with large variation according to statistical methods.
