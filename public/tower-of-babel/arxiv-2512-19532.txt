A perturbed preconditioned gradient descent method for the unconstrained minimization of composite objectives
Jea-Hyun Park, Abner J. Salgado, Steven M. Wise

Abstract
. We introduce a perturbed preconditioned gradient descent (PPGD) method for the unconstrained minimization of a strongly convex objective G G with a locally Lipschitz continuous gradient. We assume that G ⁡ ( v ) = E ⁡ ( v ) + F ⁡ ( v ) G(v)=E(v)+F(v) and that the gradient of F F is only known approximately. Our analysis is conducted in infinite dimensions with a preconditioner built into the framework. We prove a linear rate of convergence, up to an error term dependent on the gradient approximation. We apply the PPGD to the stationary Cahn-Hilliard equations with variable mobility under periodic boundary conditions. Numerical experiments are presented to validate the theoretical convergence rates and explore how the mobility affects the computation.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Problem of interest

Let
\mathbb{H}
be a Hilbert space and
E,F:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}
be smooth. Define the composite objective

(1)

G(v)\coloneqq E(v)+F(v),\qquad\forall v\in\mathbb{H}.

We assume that
G
is strongly convex with Lipschitz continuous derivative. We are interested in the following composite optimization problem: Find

(2)

\min_{v\in\mathbb{H}}G(v).

Our main source of difficulty and originality is that the derivative of
F
is only known approximately. We cast our problem in the optimization language, but the main applications we have in mind are related to the numerical solution of nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs) where, in many cases, the solution to a PDE minimizes an associated energy functional.

The structure of
G
naturally suggests the use of gradient-based methods; see
[
22
,
6
,
18
,
24
]
. However, the restrictions on
F
make this approach infeasible. In Section
4
we consider a scenario where this framework naturally arises.

1.2.
Literature

For basic results on smooth optimization we refer to
[
23
]
. For convex objectives with globally Lipschitz derivative, the
gradient descent
method (
[
23
, p. 25 (1.2.9)]
) converges to the minimizer with rate
{\mathcal{O}\left(1/k\right)}
; whereas the
optimal
or
accelerated gradient descent
method (
[
23
, p. 76 (2.2.6)]
) converges with rate
{\mathcal{O}\left(1/k^{2}\right)}
, where
k
is number of iterations. When the objective is, in addition, strongly convex, the convergence rates become linear:
{\mathcal{O}\left((1-\mu/L)^{k}\right)}
and
{\mathcal{O}\left((1-\sqrt{\mu/L})^{k}\right)}
, respectively. Here
\mu
and
L
are the best strong convexity and Lipschitz constants, respectively, that can be found.

Composite optimization, i.e., minimizing an objective consisting of two terms with different structural features, is intimately related to inexact optimization, and explicit discussions date back at least to 1979
[
19
]
. The authors of
[
19
]
, inspired by
[
26
,
15
]
, studied certain
splitting algorithms
for monotone operators.
In
[
21
]
a composite objective
\phi=f+\Psi
is assumed, where
f
is smooth and convex and
\Psi
is non-smooth, but
simple
. This reference also addresses the strongly convex case and shows that the gradient method (with
composite gradient mapping

[
21
, p. 4 (2.6)]
), and its accelerated version are as efficient as the smooth objective case.

In a similar vein
[
9
]
shows that the accelerated descent method of
[
20
]
with
noisy
gradient retains the same convergence rate up to the gradient error:
{\mathcal{O}\left(1/k^{2}\right)}+3\delta
, where
k
is the number of iterations and
\delta
is the gradient error parameter. Reference
[
27
]
studies convergence of inexact methods for composite, convex objectives with non-smooth terms. It shows that the
proximal-gradient descent
method has convergence rate
{\mathcal{O}\left(1/k\right)}
and the
accelerated proximal-gradient
method
{\mathcal{O}\left(1/k^{2}\right)}
, respectively, up to an error term. Their analysis leads to a similar convergence rate without error terms if these satisfy suitable summability conditions. When the smooth part of the objective is strongly convex, the rates are as in the smooth case, provided one assumes suitable decay rates on the error.

References
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
discuss inexact optimization in a comprehensive way. Their study includes convergence and error behaviors of the
primal gradient descent
method (PGM) and
fast gradient descent
method (FGM) with inexact
oracle
. For both convex and strongly convex objectives, the rates for PGM are, up to oracle error, the same as those for PGM with exact oracle; see
[
12
, p. 4 Definition 1]
and
[
13
, p. 2 Definition 1]
, or
[
11
, Definition 4.1 and 5.1]
for more details. These references also study how errors accumulate and are
amplified
for FGM. The
amplifying errors
may seem at odds with the result of
[
9
]
, where the magnitude of errors caused by noisy gradients stays bounded by
3\delta
as mentioned earlier. For detailed discussions and comparisons, see
[
11
, pp. 133-135]
.

In signal processing applications, the optimization of composite functions arise naturally as the objective often consists of a (smooth) fidelity term and a non-smooth one, usually related to deblurring or sparsity promotion; see, for instance,
[
10
]
. This reference seems to be responsible for popularizing the so-called ISTA (
iterative shrinkage-thresholding algorithm
), for sparse signal processing problems. In essence, this is a forward-backward splitting method. In
[
3
]
, the
fast iterative shrinkage-thresholding algorithm
(FISTA), which is an accelerated version of ISTA, was developed.

Our work can be situated between many of the previous ones. It can be seen, for example, as the PGM with inexact oracle of
[
13
]
, where the computation of the gradients introduces inexactness. It can also be seen from the perspective of
[
27
]
. However, there, the inexactness aims to provide a general framework and is treated abstractly. On the other hand, we make the inexactness explicit and the analysis is done in settings that are suitable for the numerical solutions of PDEs, which make the results of the previous works not applicable, as they assume finite dimensions and a global Lipschitz condition.

We preemptively make some valid criticisms of our work. First, we introduce a
uniform vanishing property
for perturbations to treat inexactness explicitly. This may seem too strong to be useful in applications. Nevertheless, this assumption fits the applications we have in mind, as we illustrate in Section
4
. Second, we do not use an accelerated scheme. As existing results show, including our own (e.g.,
[
24
,
25
]
), it is safe to assume that, at least in the practical sense,
once basic gradient methods are established, the accelerated version will certainly show improved convergence
, especially in the strong convex setting. Thus, we focus on perturbations and the gradient method. Accelerated methods involve several more complications and will be the target of future study.

1.3.
Summarized contributions

Our contributions are summarized as follows.

1.

We propose what we call the
perturbed preconditioned gradient descent
method (PPGD) for the composite optimization of objectives like those described in Section
1.1
and give a convergence analysis of the method. Unlike most existing works, we work in infinite dimensions and only assume a
local
Lipschitz condition on the gradient of the objective. In the numerical approximation of PDEs, global Lipschitz conditions are too restrictive to be useful, and this always requires a growing dimensionality of the problem. In addition, we include preconditioning explicitly into our analysis. In the infinite dimensional setting, this is crucial to establish norm equivalences, which is at the core of our convergence analysis. Finally, we discuss inexactness explicitly using the
uniform vanishing property
for perturbations; see Section
3
. This clarifies the restrictions imposed on computations of the inexact gradients. Also, it is tailored to a common case: the approximate solution of subproblems.

2.

As a tool for convergence, we prove estimates (
8
) and (
11
), which hold for strongly convex objectives with
locally
Lipschitz gradients. These are a generalization of some of central estimates that hold for convex, globally Lipschitz smooth objectives. They play an essential role in the convergence proof of the PPGD method by allowing us to obtain an
invariant set
for the iterates. For descent-type methods the extension from global to a local Lipschitz condition is not relevant. However, due to the inexactness of the gradient, PPGD is not a descent method. This is the significance of our extension.

3.

As an application, we provide a numerical study of the stationary Cahn-Hilliard (CH) equations with variable mobility subject to periodic boundary conditions. We investigate not only the convergence of our numerical solver, but we also discuss how our method allows us to use the
fast Fourier transform
(FFT). Due to the variable mobility, this is not possible with a standard approach.

1.4.
Organization

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we collect notation and preliminary notions that are the basis of our analysis and results. In Section
3
, we prove convergence of the PPGD method, where the uniform vanishing property for perturbations is introduced. In Section
4
, the stationary CH equation is fit into the PPGD framework and we study convergence of the resulting method. Lastly, in Section
5
, we present numerical experiments for the stationary CH equation and discuss their theoretical and numerical implications.

2.1.
Strongly convex, locally Lipschitz smooth functions

We begin by defining a preconditioner.

Definition 2.1

(preconditioner)
.

A
preconditioner
is
\mathcal{L}\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbb{H},\mathbb{H}^{*})
such that the bilinear form

(u,v)_{\mathcal{L}}\coloneqq\bigl\langle\mathcal{L}u,v\bigr\rangle

satisfies, for any
u,v\in\mathbb{H}
,

\displaystyle(u,v)_{\mathcal{L}}=(v,u)_{\mathcal{L}},\quad(u,v)_{\mathcal{L}}\leq C_{2}\left\|u\right\|_{\mathbb{H}}\left\|v\right\|_{\mathbb{H}},\quad C_{1}\left\|u\right\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\leq(u,u)_{\mathcal{L}},

for some
C_{1},C_{2}>0
. We call these properties
symmetry
,
continuity
, and
coercivity
of the bilinear form, respectively.

Throughout, we assume that we have a preconditioner
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}^{*}
available. All metric notions will be understood up to norm equivalence.

Proposition 2.2

(properties of
\mathcal{L}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}\in{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbb{H},\mathbb{H}^{*})
be a preconditioner. Then,
(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,)_{\mathcal{L}}
is an inner product on
\mathbb{H}
and its induced norm,

\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{\mathcal{L}}
,
is equivalent to
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{\mathbb{H}}

\sqrt{C_{1}}\left\|v\right\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq\left\|v\right\|_{\mathcal{L}}\leq\sqrt{C_{2}}\left\|v\right\|_{\mathbb{H}}.

Moreover, the bilinear form

(\phi,\psi)_{{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}}\coloneqq\bigl\langle\phi,{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}\psi\bigr\rangle,\qquad\forall\phi,\psi\in\mathbb{H}^{*},

is an inner product on
\mathbb{H}^{*}
, which makes it complete. The induced norm,

\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}}
,
is equivalent to
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{*}}
:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{C_{2}}}\left\|\phi\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{*}}\leq\left\|\phi\right\|_{{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{C_{1}}}\left\|\phi\right\|_{\mathbb{H}^{*}},

and it coincides with the operator norm with respect to the
\mathcal{L}
–norm, i.e.,

\left\|\phi\right\|_{{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}}\coloneqq\sup_{0\neq v\in\mathbb{H}}\frac{\bigl\langle\phi,v\bigr\rangle}{\|v\|_{\mathcal{L}}}.

Lastly,
\left\|{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}\phi\right\|_{\mathcal{L}}=\left\|\phi\right\|_{{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}}
, for all
\phi\in\mathbb{H}^{*}
and
\|\mathcal{L}v\|_{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}=\|v\|_{\mathcal{L}}
, for every
v\in\mathbb{H}
.

We will call
(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,)_{\mathcal{L}}
the
\mathcal{L}
–inner product
. Notice that, if
\mathcal{L}
is a preconditioner, then its inverse is the Riesz map with respect to the
\mathcal{L}
–inner product.

Next we define strong convexity, Lipschitz continuity, and Lipschitz smoothness.

Definition 2.3

(strong convexity)
.

Let
G:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}
be Fréchet differentiable, and
\delta G
be its Fréchet derivative. We say that
G
is
\mu
–strongly convex
(with respect to the
\mathcal{L}
–norm) if there exists a constant
\mu>0
such that

(3)

\bigl\langle{\delta G}(u)-{\delta G}(v),u-v\bigr\rangle\geq\mu\left\|u-v\right\|_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}\quad\forall\,u,v\in\mathbb{H},

or, equivalently,

(4)

G(v)\geq G(u)+\bigl\langle{\delta G}(u),v-u\bigr\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|v-u\right\|_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}\quad\forall\,u,v\in\mathbb{H}.

If one of these holds with
\mu=0
, then
G
is convex.

Condition (
4
) is often called the
lower quadratic trap
.

Remark 2.4

(existence and uniqueness)
.

If an objective
G:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\mu
–strongly convex (
\mu>0
), then it has a unique minimizer. A necessary and sufficient condition for the minimum is that the gradient vanishes; see
[
23
,
22
,
8
]
.

Definition 2.5

(Lipschitz smoothness)
.

Let
G:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}
. If, for every
B\subset\mathbb{H}
that is bounded and convex, there is
L_{B}>0
such that

(5)

\bigl\langle{\delta G}(u)-{\delta G}(v),u-v\bigr\rangle\leq L_{B}\left\|u-v\right\|_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}\quad\forall\,u,v\in B,

or, equivalently,

(6)

G(v)\leq G(u)+\bigl\langle{\delta G}(u),v-u\bigr\rangle+\frac{L_{B}}{2}\left\|v-u\right\|_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}\quad\forall\,u,v\in B,

then we say that
G
is
locally Lipschitz smooth
with respect to the
\mathcal{L}
–norm. If the constant
L_{B}
can be chosen independently of
B
, then
G
is said to be
globally Lipschitz smooth
with respect to
\mathcal{L}
–norm.
If
{\delta G}
satisfies

(7)

\left\|{\delta G}(u)-{\delta G}(v)\right\|_{{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}}\leq L_{B}\left\|u-v\right\|_{\mathcal{L}}\quad\forall\,u,v\in B,

we say that
G
has locally Lipschitz derivatives
or
{\delta G}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}^{*}
is
locally Lipschitz continuous
with respect to
\mathcal{L}
–norm. If
{\delta G}
satisfies (
7

L_{B}=L
can be chosen independently of

{\delta G}
is

with respect to the
\mathcal{L}
–norm.

Notice that (

(dual lower trap)

3.
Perturbed preconditioned gradient descent

Here we define our perturbed preconditioned gradient descent (PPGD) method and give a convergence result for it.

Definition 3.1

(PPGD)
.

Let
G:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}
admit the decomposition (
1
). Given a preconditioner
\mathcal{L}
, a set of solver parameters
\Theta
, and a family of gradient approximations
\left\{A(\,\cdot\,;\theta)\,\middle|\,\theta\in\Theta\right\}\subset{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbb{H},\mathbb{H}^{*})
we define the
perturbed preconditioned gradient descent
(PPGD) method as the following iterative scheme. Given
v_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
and
{\beta}>0
, called the step size, compute

(13)

{v_{k+1}}={v_{k}}-{\beta}{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}({\delta G}({v_{k}})+{\eta_{k}})\quad\,k\geq 0,

where, as in (
15
) below,

(14)

\eta_{k}\coloneqq\eta_{v_{k},\theta_{k}}\coloneqq A(v_{k};\theta_{k})-\delta F(v_{k}),

is a perturbation of the residual at the
k
–th iteration.

Here
A
is an approximation for
\delta F
. If
A=\delta F
, the perturbation
\eta_{k}
above is zero, and the algorithm is just the preconditioned steepest descent method (PSD). To analyze the algorithm, we must quantify the size of perturbations and, for this, we introduce a uniform approximation property for
A
with respect to
\delta F
. Later, in Section
4
, we will show how this property is verified in applications.

Definition 3.2

(perturbations uniformly vanish)
.

Let
\Theta
be a directed solver parameter set with ordering
\preceq
. Let

\left\{A(\,\cdot\,;\theta)\,\middle|\,\theta\in\Theta\right\}\subset{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbb{H},\mathbb{H}^{*})

be a family of approximation operators with respect to
\delta F
and define the perturbation (error) with respect to this approximation as

(15)

\eta_{v,\theta}\coloneqq A(v;\theta)-\delta F(v),\quad\forall\ v\in\mathbb{H}.

We say that the
perturbations uniformly vanish
iff, for any bounded
B\subset\mathbb{H}
and any
\epsilon>0
, there is
\theta_{0}=\theta_{0}(B)\in\Theta
such that, for all
\theta_{0}\preceq\theta
,

\sup_{v\in B}\left\|\eta_{v,\theta}\right\|_{{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}}\leq\epsilon.

In the previous definition, we think of
\Theta
as the set of practically adjustable parameters in an approximation scheme. For instance, we may set
\Theta=\{(v_{0},{\alpha},N)\}\subset\mathbb{H}\times(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{N}
, where
v_{0}
is the initial guess,
{\alpha}
is the step size, and
N
the number of iterations. The ordering may then be given by the Cartesian products of neighborhoods of the minimizer, zero, and infinity (in their respective spaces).

Theorem 3.3

(invariant set)
.

Let
G
admit the decomposition (
1
) and
\{v_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{H}
be obtained via the PPGD method (
13
) with preconditioner
\mathcal{L}
, initial guess
v_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
, and with
\left\{A(v;\theta)\,\middle|\,\theta\in\Theta\right\}
such that perturbations, defined in (
14
), uniformly vanish. Set
u\coloneqq\argmin_{v\in\mathbb{H}}G(v)
. Let
{C_{B1}}>0
and
{C_{B2}}>0
be the constants appearing in (
11
). Define

d_{0}\coloneqq\left\|v_{0}-u\right\|_{\mathcal{L}},\quad{\beta}_{0}\coloneqq\min\{{C_{B2}},{C_{B1}}^{-1}\},\quad\epsilon_{0}\coloneqq\left(\frac{1}{{C_{B1}}}+\frac{5{C_{B2}}}{4}\right)^{-1}d_{0}^{2}{C_{B1}}.

If the step size is chosen so that

(16)

{\beta}\in(0,{\beta}_{0}],

and the solver parameters
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
are chosen such that the perturbations
\eta_{k}
satisfy, as guaranteed by the assumed uniform vanishing property,

(17)

\left\|\eta_{k}\right\|_{{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}}^{2}\leq\epsilon_{0}.

Then, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the iterates of PPGD satisfy

(18)

\displaystyle{v_{k}}\in B\coloneqq\left\{v\in\mathbb{H}\,\middle|\,\left\|v-u\right\|_{\mathcal{L}}\leq d_{0}\right\}.

Proof.

Set, for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
d_{k}\coloneqq\left\|v_{k}-u\right\|_{\mathcal{L}}
. We will show inductively that
d_{k}\leq d_{0}
, with the base for induction being trivial. Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and suppose
{v_{k}}\in B
. The assumptions on
G
allow us to invoke Lemma
2.7
over
B
so that

(19)

\bigl\langle{\delta G}({v_{k}}),{v_{k}}-u\bigr\rangle\geq{C_{B1}}d_{k}^{2}+{C_{B2}}\left\|{\delta G}({v_{k}})\right\|_{{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}}^{2}.

In addition, we trivially have

(20)

\bigl\langle{\eta_{k}},{v_{k}}-u\bigr\rangle\leq\frac{1}{2{C_{B1}}}\left\|{\eta_{k}}\right\|_{{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}}^{2}+\frac{{C_{B1}}}{2}d_{k}^{2}

and

(21)

\bigl\langle{\delta G}({v_{k}}),{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}{\eta_{k}}\bigr\rangle\leq\left\|{\delta G}({v_{k}})\right\|_{{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|{\eta_{k}}\right\|_{{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}}^{2}.

Subtract
u
from (
13
) and take
\mathcal{L}
–norms. The choice of
{\beta}
, (
19
), (
20
), and (
21
) yield,

(22)

\displaystyle d_{k+1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|{v_{k}}-u-{\beta}{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}\delta G({v_{k}})-{\beta}{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}{\eta_{k}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}

\displaystyle=d_{k}^{2}+{\beta}^{2}\left\|\delta G({v_{k}})\right\|_{{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}}^{2}+{\beta}^{2}\left\|{\eta_{k}}\right\|_{{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}}^{2}-2{\beta}\bigl\langle{\delta G}({v_{k}}),{v_{k}}-u\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle-2{\beta}\bigl\langle{\eta_{k}},{v_{k}}-u\bigr\rangle+{\beta}^{2}\bigl\langle{\delta G}({v_{k}}),{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}{\eta_{k}}\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle\leq(1-2{\beta}{C_{B1}})d_{k}^{2}-{\beta}(2{C_{B2}}-{\beta})\left\|\delta G({v_{k}})\right\|_{{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}}^{2}+{\beta}^{2}\left\|{\eta_{k}}\right\|_{{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}}^{2}

\displaystyle-2{\beta}\bigl\langle{\eta_{k}},{v_{k}}-u\bigr\rangle+{\beta}^{2}\bigl\langle{\delta G}({v_{k}}),{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}{\eta_{k}}\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-{\beta}{C_{B1}}\right)d_{k}^{2}-2{\beta}\left({C_{B2}}-{\beta}\right)\left\|\delta G({v_{k}})\right\|_{{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}}^{2}

\displaystyle+{\beta}\left(\frac{1}{{C_{B1}}}+\frac{5}{4}{\beta}\right)\left\|{\eta_{k}}\right\|_{{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-{\beta}{C_{B1}}\right)d_{k}^{2}+{\beta}\left(\frac{1}{{C_{B1}}}+\frac{5{C_{B2}}}{4}\right)\left\|{\eta_{k}}\right\|_{{\mathcal{L}^{-1}}}^{2},

where
{\beta}\leq{C_{B2}}
was used in the last step and
0<{\beta}\leq{C_{B1}}^{-1}
ensures
0\leq\left(1-{\beta}{C_{B1}}\right)<1
.

Now, since the perturbations uniformly vanish, for the bounded set
B
, we can choose
\theta_{k}
such that (
17
) holds.
Thus, we continue estimate (
22
) by using (
17
) and
{\beta}\leq{C_{B1}}^{-1}

d_{k+1}^{2}\leq\left(1-{\beta}{C_{B1}}\right)d_{k}^{2}+d_{0}^{2}{\beta}{C_{B1}}\leq\left(1-{\beta}{C_{B1}}\right)d_{0}^{2}+d_{0}^{2}{\beta}{C_{B1}}=d_{0}^{2}.

Therefore, we have
d_{k+1}\in B
, which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.4

(step size)
.

In view of Remark
2.8
, the step size restriction (
16
) is comparable to the classic one:
{\beta}\leq 1/L_{B}
.

Theorem 3.5

(convergence of PPGD)
.

Let
G
admit the decomposition (
1
) and
\{v_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{H}
be obtained via the PPGD method (
13
) with preconditioner
\mathcal{L}
, initial guess
v_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
, and with
\left\{A(v;\theta)\,\middle|\,\theta\in\Theta\right\}
such that perturbations, defined in (
14
), uniformly vanish.
Set
u\coloneqq\argmin_{v\in\mathbb{H}}G(v)
,
d_{0}=\left\|v_{0}-u\right\|_{\mathcal{L}}
, and
\rho=1-\mu{\beta}\in(0,1)
.
If the step size
{\beta}
satisfies (
16
), then the best approximation,
z_{k}
, up to
k
–th iterates, i.e.,
z_{k}=\argmin_{i=0}^{k}{\|v_{k}-u\|_{\mathcal{L}}}
, satisfies

(23)

\displaystyle\left\|z_{k}-u\right\|_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}\leq\frac{d_{0}^{2}}{\mu{\beta}}\rho^{k}+\epsilon d_{0},

where the iterates
v_{k}
’s involve perturbations satisfying (
17
).

Proof.

Note that, by Theorem
3.3
, we have
\{v_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}\subset B
, where
B
was defined in (
18
). As a consequence the same Lipschitz constant,
L_{B}
, can be used for all iterates.

The proof adapts the arguments of
[
11
]
. Set, for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

d_{k}=\left\|v_{k}-u\right\|_{\mathcal{L}},\qquad\Delta G_{k}\coloneqq G(v_{k})-G(u)\geq 0,\qquad e_{k+1}=\bigl\langle{\eta_{k}},u-{v_{k+1}}\bigr\rangle.

Observe that

(24)

d_{k+1}^{2}=d_{k}^{2}-\left\|{v_{k+1}}-{v_{k}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}+2\left({v_{k+1}}-{v_{k}},{v_{k+1}}-u\right)_{\mathcal{L}}.

Take the inner product of (
13
) with
{v_{k+1}}-u
to obtain

(25)

\left({v_{k+1}}-{v_{k}},{v_{k+1}}-u\right)_{\mathcal{L}}={\beta}\bigl\langle{\delta G}(v_{k}),u-{v_{k+1}}\bigr\rangle+{\beta}\bigl\langle{\eta_{k}},u-{v_{k+1}}\bigr\rangle.

We may then combine (
6
), (
4
), and
{\delta G}(u)=0
to conclude

(26)

\displaystyle\bigl\langle{\delta G}(v_{k}),u-{v_{k+1}}\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle{\delta G}(v_{k}),u-{v_{k}}\bigr\rangle+\bigl\langle{\delta G}(v_{k}),{v_{k}}-{v_{k+1}}\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle\leq G(u)-G({v_{k+1}})-\frac{\mu}{2}d_{k}^{2}+\frac{L_{B}}{2}\left\|{v_{k+1}}-{v_{k}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}.

Using (
25
) and (
26
) we may rewrite (
24
) as

\displaystyle d_{k+1}^{2}

\displaystyle=d_{k}^{2}-\left\|{v_{k+1}}-{v_{k}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}+2{\beta}\bigl\langle{\delta G}(v_{k}),u-{v_{k+1}}\bigr\rangle+2{\beta}\bigl\langle{\eta_{k}},u-{v_{k+1}}\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle\leq(1-\mu{\beta})d_{k}^{2}+2{\beta}\left(G(u)-G({v_{k+1}})\right)-(1-{\beta}L_{B})\left\|{v_{k+1}}-{v_{k}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}

\displaystyle+2{\beta}\bigl\langle{\eta_{k}},u-{v_{k+1}}\bigr\rangle.

Note that from (
16
) and Remark
2.8
we have
{\beta}\leq 1/L_{B}
. The previous inequality then implies the key estimate

(27)

\displaystyle d_{k+1}^{2}\leq\rho d_{k}^{2}+2{\beta}e_{k+1}-2{\beta}\Delta G_{k+1},

which, when iterated, implies

d_{k}^{2}\leq\rho^{k}d_{0}^{2}+2{\beta}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\rho^{i}e_{k-i}-2{\beta}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\rho^{i}\Delta G_{k-i}.

Rearranging we obtain

(28)

\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\rho^{i}\Delta G_{k-i}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\rho^{i}\Delta G_{k-i}+\frac{d_{k}^{2}}{2{\beta}}\leq\rho^{k}\frac{d_{0}^{2}}{2{\beta}}+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\rho^{i}e_{k-i}.

Define
z_{k}\subset\{v_{i}\}_{i=0}^{k}
as

z_{k}\coloneqq\argmin_{i=1}^{k}\Delta G_{k-i}

and use (
4
) to estimate

\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|z_{k}-u\right\|_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}\leq G(z_{k})-G(u).

This, together with the fact that, for any

,
\langle\eta_{i},u-v\rangle\leq\epsilon d_{0}
, yields

\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|z_{k}-u\right\|_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\rho^{i}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\rho^{i}\Delta G_{k-i}\leq\rho^{k}\frac{d_{0}^{2}}{2{\beta}}+\epsilon d_{0}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\rho^{i},

which readily implies (
23
).
∎

4.
Application: The stationary Cahn-Hilliard equations with variable mobility

4.1.
Motivation

The stationary CH equations with a variable mobility arise after time discretization of the CH equations along with so-called
convexity splitting

[
17
]
.
These equations are an important model that is used in many physical and biological phenomena, including spinodal decomposition
[
4
]
, diblock copolymer
[
7
]
, two-phase fluid flows
[
1
]
, and tumor growth
[
29
]
, to name a few.
Its numerical implementation, however, is challenging due to their high order and nonlinear structure. Such a challenge becomes greater when the
mobility
is not constant. In particular, in the context of materials science applications, where one usually imposes periodic boundary conditions, a variable mobility prevents the use of FFT for fast inversion of the ensuing linear systems. Here we will show how the PPGD allows us to decompose the computational difficulties: the nonlinearity corresponds to
E
in (
1
) whereas
F
encodes a linear, but challenging component stemming from the variable mobility.

4.2.
Notation and settings

Let
d\in\{2,3\}
and
\Omega=(0,\ell)^{d}
, where
\ell>0
. For conciseness we will write
L^{2}\coloneqq L^{2}(\Omega)
and

L^{2}_{0}=\left\{f\in L^{2}\,\middle|\,{\langle f\rangle}=0\right\},

where
{\langle f\rangle}=\frac{1}{L^{d}}\int_{\Omega}f(\boldsymbol{x}){\operatorname{d}\!\boldsymbol{x}}
.
The
L^{2}
-inner product and norm are
(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|
, respectively.
{{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}
is the subspace of functions in
H^{1}(\Omega)
that are periodic, and
{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}={{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}\cap L^{2}_{0}
.
Their inner-products are

(u,v)_{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}=(\nabla u,\nabla v)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\partial_{i}u,\partial_{i}v),\quad\text{and}\quad(u,v)_{{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}=(u,v)+(u,v)_{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}},

respectively. We recall the following Poincaré inequality: there is
C_{P}>0
such that

(29)

\|v\|\leq C_{P}\|v\|_{{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}},\qquad\forall v\in{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}.

Define

(30)

\displaystyle{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{-1}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{\phi\in({{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}})^{*}\,\middle|\,\langle\phi,1\rangle=0\right\},\quad\left\|\phi\right\|_{{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{-1}}}\coloneqq\sup_{0\neq v\in{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}}\frac{\langle\phi,v\rangle}{\left\|v\right\|_{{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}}}.

Note that
({\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}},\left\|{\ \cdot\ }\right\|_{{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}})^{*}\cong({\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{-1}},\left\|{\ \cdot\ }\right\|_{{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{-1}}})
.

The following result defines an inner product on
{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{-1}}
. Its proof is standard.

Proposition 4.1

(Riesz isometry)
.

Given
\phi\in{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{-1}}
, let
\mathsf{T}(\phi)\in{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}
, solve

(31)

\left(\nabla\mathsf{T}(\phi),\nabla v\right)=\langle\phi,v\rangle\qquad\forall v\in{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}.

This defines
\mathsf{T}\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{-1}},{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}})
. In addition, the bilinear form

(32)

\left(\phi,\xi\right)_{{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{-1}}}\coloneqq\left(\nabla\mathsf{T}(\phi),\nabla\mathsf{T}(\xi)\right)=\langle\phi,\mathsf{T}(\xi)\rangle=\langle\xi,\mathsf{T}(\phi)\rangle.

defines an inner product on
{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{-1}}
, and the induced norm coincides with
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{-1}}}
.

Remark 4.2

(distributional identification)
.

Notice that
{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}\hookrightarrow L^{2}_{0}\hookrightarrow{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{-1}}
forms a Gelfand triple. This gives a meaning to linear combinations of elements in any of these three spaces.

4.3.
The stationary Cahn-Hilliard equations with variable mobility

Let
M:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be such that there are
0<M_{1}\leq M_{2}
for which

(33)

M_{1}\leq M(\boldsymbol{x})\leq M_{2},\quad\forall\,\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega.

Define

\Delta_{M}v\coloneqq\nabla\cdot\left(M\nabla v\right),\quad\forall\,v\in{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}.

With this notation at hand, the problem we wish to solve is as follows: given
f\in L^{2}
and
u_{\star}\in{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}
, find
u\in{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}
,
w\in{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}
, and
C\in\mathbb{R}
that satisfy

(34)

\displaystyle u-u_{\star}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{M}w,

(35)

\displaystyle w+C

\displaystyle=u^{3}-\Delta u-f.

These are the
stationary nonlinear
CH equations with variable mobility;
M
is the
mobility
,
u
is the
density field
or
phase variable
, and
w
is the
chemical potential
. The chemical potential can be eliminated from the problem. By writing

w=-(-\Delta_{M})^{-1}\left(u-u_{\star}\right),

and substituting in (
34
) we obtain

(36)

\mathcal{N}(u)\coloneqq(-\Delta_{M})^{-1}\left(u-u_{\star}\right)-C+u^{3}-\Delta u=f.

The constant
C
appears because of the nonlinearity and can be eliminated in the weak formulation of the problem.

The weak form of (
36
), which reads,

\left((-\Delta_{M})^{-1}(u-u_{\star}),\psi\right)+(u^{3},\psi)+\left(\nabla u,\nabla\psi\right)=(f,\psi),\quad\forall\,\psi\in{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}

is clearly the Euler-Lagrange equation for the strictly convex energy

(37)

G(v)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left\|v-u_{\star}\right\|_{{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per},M}^{-1}}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|v\right\|_{L^{4}}^{4}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|v\right\|_{{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}}^{2}-(f,v),\qquad\forall v\in{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}},

where

(38)

\left\|\psi\right\|_{{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per},M}^{-1}}}\coloneqq\sqrt{\left((-\Delta_{M})^{-1}\psi,\psi\right)_{L^{2}}},\quad\forall\,\psi\in\mathring{L}^{2}.

4.4.
PGD for a non-constant coefficient elliptic problem

Rewriting the stationary CH problem as (
36
) requires the inversion of a non-constant coefficient elliptic operator. Namely, given
M\in L^{\infty}
that satisfies (
33
) and
\phi\in{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{-1}}
, find
u_{\phi}\in{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}
such that

(39)

(M\nabla u_{\phi},\nabla v)=\langle\phi,v\rangle,\qquad\forall v\in{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}.

To approximate the solution to this problem, we employ the PGD method (
12
) with the Laplacian as preconditioner. In this setting, given
u_{0}\in{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}
, this method computes, for
n\geq 0
,

(40)

\displaystyle r_{n}

\displaystyle=\phi+\Delta_{M}u_{n},

(41)

\displaystyle d_{n}

\displaystyle=(-\Delta)^{-1}r_{n},

(42)

\displaystyle u_{n+1}

\displaystyle=u_{n}+{\alpha}_{n}d_{n}.

where
{\alpha}_{n}>0
is the step size.

It is a trivial observation to note that

(u,v)_{{\mathring{H}^{1}_{\mathrm{per},M}}}\coloneqq(M\nabla u,\nabla v)

is an inner product on
{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}
and the induced norm satisfies, for all
u\in{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}
,

(43)

\sqrt{M_{1}}\|u\|_{{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}}\leq\|u\|_{{\mathring{H}^{1}_{\mathrm{per},M}}}\leq\sqrt{M_{2}}\|u\|_{{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}}.

By duality, we also have

(44)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{M_{2}}}\|\phi\|_{{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{-1}}}\leq\|\phi\|_{{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per},M}^{-1}}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{M_{1}}}\|\phi\|_{{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{-1}}},\quad\forall\phi\in{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{-1}}.

With this notation, we write the energy associated to (
39
)

(45)

\tilde{F}(v)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|v\right\|_{{\mathring{H}^{1}_{\mathrm{per},M}}}^{2}-\langle\phi,v\rangle\qquad\forall v\in{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}.

Since this energy is quadratic, we can compute the optimal step size. Namely,

(46)

\displaystyle{\alpha}_{n,opt}=-\frac{\left(M\nabla u_{n},\nabla d_{n}\right)-\left\langle\phi,d_{n}\right\rangle}{\left(M\nabla d_{n},\nabla d_{n}\right)}.

Observe also that
\tilde{F}
is
M_{1}
–strongly convex and
M_{2}
–globally Lipschitz smooth. With these results at hand, we may apply Theorem
2.9
to assert convergence of PGD. The following result recasts this convergence in a manner that will be useful to establish the uniform vanishing property for perturbations.

Corollary 4.3

(perturbations uniformly vanish)
.

Let
u_{0}\in{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}
and assume that the sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}\subset{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}
is computed by (
42
) with a suitable
\{{\alpha}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
, which depends on
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
, that guarantees convergence. Then, for every
\epsilon>0
there is
N\geq\mathbb{N}
that depends only on
\epsilon
,
M_{1}
,
M_{2}
,
\|\phi\|_{{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{-1}}}
and
\|u_{0}\|\in{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}
such that

(47)

\left\|u_{\phi,n}-u_{\phi}\right\|_{{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}}\leq\epsilon

whenever
n\geq N
, where
u_{\phi}
and
u_{\phi,n}
are the solution to the Poisson equation (
39
) and the
n
-th iterate of PGD computed by (
42
).

Proof.

This is nothing but the linear convergence rate of Theorem
2.9
together with the observations that the contraction factor,
\rho
, only depends on
M_{1}
,
M_{2}
; and
\|u\|_{{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}}}
can be bounded by
M_{2}
and
\|\phi\|_{{\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{-1}}}
.
∎

4.5.
PPGD for the stationary Cahn-Hilliard equation

We now show how to apply the PPGD method to solve (
36
). The main issue is that we do not wish to exactly invert the operator
-\Delta_{M}
. Thus, we replace it by one involving constant coefficients, which is easier to invert. This perturbation will allow us to use fast solution techniques, like FFT.

4.5.1.
Preconditioner

As preconditioner we use

(48)

6.
Conclusion

We have proposed the perturbed preconditioned gradient descent method to solve composite unconstrained minimization problems, where the objective function includes a term whose exact gradient is known only approximately. The notion of a uniform vanishing property for perturbations is introduced, and we have proved convergence result for this method that is similar to other methods suited for this scenario. To be specific, when the perturbations vanish uniformly and the objective function is strongly convex with locally Lipschitz continuous derivatives, we have linear convergence, up to an error term. As a technical tool, we proved two estimates that generalize existing results to locally Lipschitz smooth functions.
These allowed us to show the existence of an invariant set for the iterations, and thus the analysis follows the one for globally Lipschitz smooth objectives, even when the method does not have a
descent
property.

To illustrate the PPGD method, a numerical study of the stationary Cahn-Hilliard equations with variable mobility under periodic boundary conditions has been conducted. The method allows us to use FFT when it is not possible in usual formulations. This study has confirmed theoretical analysis, including linear convergence when the effect of perturbation seems minor. Numerical results have also given some insights into how the ratio between maximum and minimum values of the mobility affects computational efficiency.
