Comparative efficacy of new wake-promoting agents for narcolepsy-a network meta-analysis
Authors: Zian Yan, Jiwei Li, Yixin Yu, Shenghan Qiu, Bing Wang, Jiyou Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs a rare sleep disorder, narcolepsy is treated with various therapeutic options. This article systematically analyzes the efficacy and safety of novel approved wake-promoting drugs.MethodsRandomized controlled trials (RCTs) for patients with narcolepsy and treated with approved interventions were searched. Outcome measures included Epworth sleepiness scale (ESS) score, mean latency in the maintenance of wakefulness test (MWT), cataplexy frequency, number of subjects with ESS scores < 10, standard deviation of lane position (SDLP) score, and adverse events (including anxiety, dizziness). This paper was registered in INPLASY (INPLASY2024120052).ResultsThe final analysis included 13 RCTs with four drugs involved: modafinil/armodafinil, sodium oxybate (SXB), pitolisant, and solriamfetol, at different dosages or combinations. All drugs were effective in treating narcolepsy compared to placebo. Solriamfetol 300 mg was more effective than other treatments in reducing ESS scores (MD = −4.74, 95%CI: −6.51,-2.97) and prolonging sleep latency (MD = 10.82 min, 95%CI: 6.99, 14.31). Several novel wake-promoting drugs did not show significant differences in improving cataplexy frequency, the number of people with ESS scores < 10, and SDLP scores. The novel wake-promoting drugs compared in this article could improve adverse effects, but some drugs showed an elevated likelihood of some adverse effects compared to controls or other drugs. Compared to placebo, SXB yielded the highest risk of gastrointestinal adverse effects such as diarrhea [8.53, (1.14,263)] and vomiting [29.5, (2.46,1.46e+03)], pitolisant induced the highest risk of nausea [4.22e+08, (4.09,1.26e+32)], and SXB+modafinil had the highest risk of dizziness [7.67, (1.01,58.7)]. There were no considerable differences among drugs in other adverse effects.ConclusionAll of the approved new wake-promoting drugs are effective in controlling narcolepsy symptoms with acceptable adverse effects.Trial Registration The study was prospectively registered in the INPLASY database (INPLASY2024120052).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04328-9.

Introduction

Narcolepsy is a sleep disorder featuring excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS), cataplexy, sleep paralysis, and behavioral abnormalities during the rapid eye movement (REM) phase [1–4]. The disorder can be categorized as type 1 narcolepsy (NT1) or type 2 narcolepsy (NT2). Patients with NT1 often exhibit decreased orexin levels with or without cataplexy. In contrast, patients with NT2, although they do not have cataplexy, exhibit typical EDS, manifesting as REM sleep in ≥ 2 naps and an average sleep latency of ≤ 8 min for 5 naps in the multiple sleep latency test (MSLT) [5]. The prevalence of narcolepsy, as a rare condition, ranges from around 1/5000 to 1/3300 [6]. Patients with narcolepsy often suffer from impaired daytime functioning, and their mental health is also undermined to a certain extent, which is a great burden for patients themselves and society [7]. In addition, narcolepsy is associated with psychiatric symptoms. Large population studies have shown that psychiatric symptoms are more common in patients with narcolepsy than in the general population [8]. Narcolepsy is also associated with an increased risk of schizophrenia [9].

Five drugs have been approved for narcolepsy by the US FDA or the European EDA, including methylphenidate/amphetamines, modafinil/armodafinil, sodium oxybate (SXB), pitolisant, and lower-sodium oxybate (LXB) [10–12]. A recent report has evinced the efficacy of solriamfetol in daytime sleepiness in patients with narcolepsy [13].

Articles have compared the efficacy of several wake-promoting drugs in EDS in patients with narcolepsy from the perspectives of Epworth sleepiness scale (ESS) scores and the maintenance of wakefulness test (MWT) results [14]. Other articles further estimate the clinical global impression of change (CGI-C) and patient global impression of change (PGI-C) and compare the safety of drugs in narcolepsy [13]. The current meta-analysis does not compare the effects of the above new wake-promoting drugs on the symptoms of sudden collapse and the number of patients with ESS score < 10. Narcolepsy, as a sleep disorder, can impair the executive function in patients. There are limited studies on executive function impairments associated with excessive sleepiness on wake-promoting drugs. This paper attempts to compare the results and inform subsequent drug studies. We also wanted to compare adverse effects under different treatment regimens and the incidence of related symptoms, to provide references for clinical use of medications and to help patients understand the possible side effects of the medications they are taking, thus improving compliance with medications. This paper further compares the efficacy of different drugs in other symptoms (e.g., cataplexy) and daytime functioning (e.g., driving) in patients with narcolepsy, as well as examines the efficacy of the same drug at different doses and combinations.

Methods

Study registration

This paper complied with the guidelines of systemic review and network meta-analysis (NMA) and was registered in INPLASY (INPLASY2024120052). All data were derived from published literature and were publicly available at the time of data extraction.

Eligibility criteria

The articles included met the following criteria:

Population: Patients with a definite diagnosis of narcolepsy, without restrictions on race, nationality, sex, age, or disease duration.

Intervention: Novel wake-promoting drugs, including solriamfetol (75 mg, 150 mg, 300 mg), SXB, and pitolisant.

Comparison: Placebo and conventional wake-promoting drug modafinil.

Outcome: Primary outcomes: (a) ESS, a scale used to subjectively assess EDS, is now widely utilized in sleep centers. ESS scores evaluated sleepiness in patients in eight different states during daily activities. Each item was scored on a scale of 0–3 (0 = never, 3 = often), with a total score of 0–24. An ESS score of > 9 was deemed probable drowsiness. The patients were compared in terms of the reduction in ESS scores after drug application and the number of people with scores below 10. (b) MWT: to compare sleep latency after drug application. 2. Secondary outcome: 1. Cataplexy frequency; 2. PGI-C score; 3. Daytime executive functioning (e.g., driving). 4. Safety for drugs (mainly comparison of the incidence of different adverse reactions of multiple drugs).

Study design: Randomized controlled trial (RCT).

Articles were excluded for the following reasons:

Population: patients with moderate to severe sleep apnea syndrome.

Intervention: None.

Comparison: None.

Outcome: None.

Study design: conference abstracts without peer review.

Data sources and search strategy

Pubmed, Embase, and Cochrane Library were thoroughly searched until May 18, 2024 for RCTs of novel wake-promoting drugs in patients with narcolepsy. A combination of subject terms and free words was adopted for the search (Appendix 1 for search strategy).

Study selection

The retrieved documents were imported into EndNoteX9, and duplicates were eliminated through automatic marking and manual identification. The remaining documents were read through the titles and abstracts to remove unqualified documents. The full texts of the eligible literature were downloaded and reviewed. The included trials met the requirements of this NMA. The literature was screened by 2 researchers (Zian Yan, Yixin Yu) independently and cross-checked. In case of disagreement, it was discussed with the third researcher (Jiwei Li).

Data extraction

Two researchers (Zian Yan, Jiwei Li) designed the data extraction form and independently extracted (1) basic information: title, authors, year, study type, diagnostic criteria, interventions, duration, and outcome indicators; (2). demographic characteristics: sample size, age, sex; (3) methodological information: randomization method, allocation concealment, and blinding. When two researchers extracted inconsistent information, it was tackled via discussion.

Risk of bias

The risk of bias was appraised by 2 researchers using the Cochrane risk of bias tool in 7 domains: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other biases. Each domain was rated as low bias, high bias, or unclear and manifested using Revman 5.4. Synthesis methods

Bayesian random-effects model was leveraged to compare the efficacy of various interventions. The Markov chain Monte Carlo method was utilized for modeling, with four Markov chains running simultaneously, with 20,000 annealings and 50,000 iterations. Deviation information criterion was utilized for assessing model fitting and global consistency. If closed loops existed, local consistency was checked using the node splitting method. In addition, each intervention was ranked for SUCRA, and league tables were generated to compare differences among interventions.

In the included trials, the same drug was studied in a multi-arm study at different doses and durations of treatment. Therefore, compared with placebo, Bayesian NMA was leveraged to analyze differences in efficacy among novel drugs at different doses and treatment regimens. Funnel plots were mapped to visualize publication bias when the number of trials was ≥ 10. The analysis was done in R4.3.0, with P < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Results

Study selection

The preliminary search obtained 2927 articles, among which 1024 duplicates were eliminated. 28 articles were retained after reading the titles and abstracts, and 15 articles were excluded after full-text screening (3 articles for pronounced differences in research methods and 12 articles for not providing sufficient data or indexes). SDLP

Links between interventions

SDLP (standard deviation of lateral position) is the core parameter of a standardized on-road driving test, which is primarily used to assess the effects of central nervous system medications on driving ability and is considered to be the “gold standard” for such research. This experiment required trained participants to maintain a steady lane position and constant speed on a 100-km public highway, using a roof camera and infrared light to measure the lateral position of the vehicle relative to the lane markings. Low SDLP indicated that the vehicle remained stable with good driving control. High SDLP indicated that the vehicle shifted frequently from side to side, reflecting reduced driver control due to medication, fatigue, and other factors.

SDLP was reported in 2 trials [15, 16], including modafinil and solriamfetol (2 h and 6 h after drug administration). The trials reported only drug versus placebo comparisons, without pairwise comparisons between drugs. There was only one trial for both drugs and the graph showed no closed loops (Fig. 12).Fig. 12Network relationship diagram of using SDLP as the outcome indicator to measure the effects of wakefulness-promoting drugs in the treatment of narcolepsy. Adverse effects

All trials reported adverse effects, including anxiety, diarrhea, dizziness, headache, irritability, nasopharyngitis, nausea, somnolence, and vomiting. Of these, solriamfetol had the lowest risk of anxiety but the highest risk of headache. SXB had the highest risk of vomiting [29.5, (2.46,1.46e+03)] and diarrhea [8.53, (1.14,263)], and SXB+modafinil increased the risk of dizziness [7.67, (1.01,58.7)]. Pitolisant had the highest risk of nausea symptoms [4.22e+08, (4.09,1.26e+32)]. For other adverse effects, no differences were demonstrated between drugs and placebo. Discussion

In 13 trials that compared the efficacy and safety of wake-promoting drugs for narcolepsy, most novel wake-promoting drugs were superior to traditional drugs or placebo. Adverse effects were all tolerated within tolerable limits. In terms of wake-promoting efficacy, solriamfetol stood out, with solriamfetol 300 mg showing the best efficacy. In terms of drug safety, novel wake-promoting drugs showed more adverse effects than the traditional wake-promoting drug (modafinil). Pitolisant yielded the highest risk of anxiety, solriamfetol yielded the highest risk of headache, and SXB yielded the highest risk of dizziness. However, none of the novel wake-promoting drugs showed significant advantages in cataplexy and daytime executive functioning.

Regarding the therapeutic mechanism of several therapeutic agents for narcolepsy, pitolisant is an H3 receptor antagonist that blocks histamine receptors and augments the activity of histaminergic and noradrenergic neurons in the brain [17]. Solriamfetol exerts wake-promoting effects through its activity on dopamine and norepinephrine transporters. Solriamfetol is an orally available, small-molecule dopamine and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, which can increase the levels of dopamine and norepinephrine in the body by inhibiting their reuptake and can enhance excitability in animal experiments. Solriamfetol has dual activity on dopamine and norepinephrine transporters and does not have significant monoamine-releasing properties, which may explain why its mechanism of action in the body is different from that of modafinil or amphetamine [15]. Although the molecular mechanism has not been clarified, several clinical trials have stated that SXB can improve the quality of nighttime sleep, including sleep structure, stage shifts, nocturnal arousals, and subjective perception of sleep quality by patients [16]. Additionally, some studies suggest that SXB reduces tyrosine hydroxylase levels in the locus coeruleus, a primary descending projection of the hypocretin system [18]. This may be a new direction for subsequent trials on the specific mechanism of related drugs.

The included trials did not demonstrate therapeutic effects on cataplexy. Relevant animal studies also fail to prove the efficacy of modafinil and solriamfetol for cataplexy [19]. Regarding the mechanisms involved in the treatment of cataplexy, evidence has shown that the HNMT inhibitor metoprine, which has high blood–brain barrier permeability, is more effective than pitolisant in treating cataplexy in mice with a weaker effect of orexin in vivo, possibly related to the ability of metoprine to increase the cerebral concentration of histamine [20]. SXB has therapeutic effects on cataplexy when the dose is gradually elevated over a period [16]. More studies are warranted to better evaluate the efficacy of SXB and other medications on cataplexy. Notably, in practice, there are cases of misdiagnosis of cataplexy as epileptic seizures, leading to underdiagnosis of NT1 [21]. The good news is that cataplexy can be identified by facial expressions using predictive models [22].

The present study, like other related studies, evaluated the therapeutic effects of multiple novel wake-promoting drugs through subjective scale scores and objective indexes. Nonetheless, the advantages of this paper over other studies mainly lie in two aspects. The first is the increase in comparative indicators. In addition to the conventional subjective evaluation indicators ESS scores and MWT sleep latency, we also added the comparative analysis of cataplexy frequency and executive functioning indicator (SDLP). Cataplexy is a typical symptom of narcolepsy, especially NT1 [1]. Regarding commonly used drugs for cataplexy treatment, such as tricyclic antidepressants like venlafaxine, no literature demonstrates a significant wake-promoting effect. Adding the evaluation of cataplexy indicators could help comprehensively assess the efficacy of related medications in patients with narcolepsy. Given that EDS in narcolepsy often impairs patients’ cognition, which in turn affects daytime functioning, the improvement in related executive functions should also be included in the evaluation of treatment outcomes for patients with narcolepsy.

Besides, this study also added comparisons of adverse effects of these drugs. Solriamfetol had a significantly higher risk of headache but a lower incidence of anxiety. SXB had a notably higher risk of diarrhea than solriamfetol and other drugs, and pitolisant had the highest risk of nausea. SXB and SXB+modafinil had a higher risk of dizziness than modafinil and solriamfetol alone, and modafinil and solriamfetol did not differ from placebo in terms of the risk of dizziness.

There are also certain shortcomings. First, it should be noted that the literature and trials included were limited, making it difficult to avoid bias caused by the small sample size. Too few trials were involved in the comparisons, and many of the comparison networks failed to loop, which could result in a disproportionate share of indirect comparisons and a small share of direct comparisons. It may amplify the possible effects of small differences in experimental conditions on the comparison results, which may affect the consistency assessment and the accuracy of comparisons. Second, it is important to note that no distinction was made between NT1 or NT2 for patients with narcolepsy included in this study, although the main difference between the two types is that patients with NT1 often had much lower levels of orexin (< 110 pg/μl) and were accompanied by sudden onset of collapse. Considering that some drugs such as SXB are effective against NT1-specific symptoms of sudden collapse, this may have an impact on the heterogeneity of this study. Although there is a lack of relevant analyses regarding drowsiness and sleep structure in NT1 and NT2 patients, a systematic review of NT1/NT2 patients versus patients with idiopathic hypersomnia suggested that the gap in sleep fragmentation is greater in NT1 versus idiopathic hypersomnia than in NT2 versus idiopathic hypersomnia [23]. This provides ideas for future comparisons on the severity of symptoms such as drowsiness in NT1/NT2 patients and the therapeutic effects of the same drug on both. Finally, the efficacy of wake-promoting agents is still evaluated in terms of subjective scores and changes in objective sleep latency, and there is a lack of improvement in daytime cognitive and executive functioning following medication (given that narcolepsy has been documented to impair cognitive and executive functioning in patients [24]). While this paper attempted to explore the extent to which wake-promoting drugs improve cognitive impairment due to narcolepsy through SDLP-related comparisons, the following limitations remain. First, the number of relevant studies is limited, resulting in fewer studies available for comparative analyses, which may have affected the results. In addition, executive function is not limited to driving but also includes memory, speech, and computation. Due to the lack of research on the extent to which medications improve these symptoms, comparisons are not made in this study. Further comparisons should be made in future studies.

In addition, it is also important to note that in a study published in 2024, Francesco Biscarini et al. mentioned that NT1 patients often suffered from mental disorders such as anxiety, depression, and suicidal tendencies [25]. Therefore, future research should not only evaluate the presence and severity of NT1 patients’ mental symptoms, but also compare the efficacy of drug and non-drug treatments for their mental symptoms to avoid serious consequences such as suicide caused by mental symptoms and improve the quality of life and mental health of NT1 patients, which may play a role in the treatment of narcolepsy.

Conclusion

The new wake-promoting drugs mentioned above have shown varying degrees of efficacy in improving EDS in patients, both at subjective scores and objective eletroneurophysiological indicators, with an acceptable safety profile. Among them, solriamfetol shows the best efficacy in drowsiness.
