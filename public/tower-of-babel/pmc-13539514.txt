Mesenchymal stem cells as cellular factories for targeted P2X7 blockade after traumatic brain injury
Authors: Lucia Fadon‐Padilla, Chengyan Chu, Lorissa McDougall, Yajie Liang, Chinmoy Sarkar, Marta Lipinski, Tim Magnus, Miroslaw Janowski, Piotr Walczak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraumatic brain injury (TBI) causes long‐term disability, with inflammation contributing to both primary and secondary brain damage. Extracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and its receptor purinergic receptor X7 (P2X7) drive neuroinflammation and represent promising therapeutic targets. Therefore, we developed a strategy to mitigate acute post‐TBI inflammation using mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) engineered to deliver a P2X7‐blocking nanobody (P2X7nb).MethodsPrimary MSCs were transfected in vitro with bicistronic messenger RNA (mRNA) encoding P2X7nb and a haloalkane dehalogenase Tag reporter, with nanobody secretion confirmed by enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). In vivo, TBI was induced in mice using the closed‐head injury model. Twenty‐4 hours post‐TBI, MSCs labeled with iron oxide nanoparticles were administered via intra‐arterial injection into the internal carotid artery. Dynamic 9.4 T magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) using susceptibility‐weighted imaging (SWI) allowed real‐time monitoring of cellular accumulation. Behavioral performance was assessed using the beam walk test, while mice were euthanized on Day 4 post‐TBI for immunofluorescence analysis of cell localization. Data were analyzed using either an unpaired t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test, depending on data distribution, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsTransfection (38.9% efficiency) and release of P2X7nb were confirmed, with a peak secretion of 17.29 ± 1.84 ng/mL. SWI MRI revealed accumulation of labeled MSCs in the brain post‐injection, with a persistent hypointense signal ranging from 2.45% ± 2.93% to 0.77% ± 0.88% of cell area (% of right hemisphere) over 3 days. Behavioral assessment using the beam walk test revealed no cell transplantation‐related motor impairment, while histology confirmed the presence of transfected MSCs in injured brain tissue.ConclusionMSCs expressing a P2X7nb are detectable in the injured brain following intra‐arterial delivery for at least 3 days, without producing adverse effects. These findings provide insights into cell‐based immunomodulatory therapies targeting neuroinflammation in TBI and highlight the utility of noninvasive MRI to monitor therapeutic cell biodistribution and persistence.

INTRODUCTION

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a leading cause of long‐term neurological disability, particularly in younger, working‐age populations. Current treatments remain largely supportive, with a limited capacity to promote tissue repair. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have recently emerged as a promising therapeutic tool owing to their immunomodulatory, anti‐inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties. In preclinical models, MSC administration following TBI has been associated with improved functional recovery and attenuation of neuroinflammation.1, 2, 3 Despite these encouraging results, the reported therapeutic effects are moderate, leaving room for the optimization of MSC‐based therapies in the context of TBI.

Genetic engineering of MSCs to express molecules with neuroprotective or anti‐inflammatory effects has been proposed as a strategy to improve their therapeutic potential. For example, MSCs engineered to overexpress fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21), 4 interleukin‐10 (IL‐10), 5 or interleukin‐4 (IL‐4) 6 have shown enhanced efficacy in rodent models, while expression of the chemokine receptor C‐X‐C motif receptor 4 (CXCR4) 7 or the antioxidant enzyme superoxide dismutase (SOD2) has been used to improve migration and resistance to oxidative stress. 8 Moreover, several clinical studies have shown that the intracranial (IC) implantation of modified MSCs (SB623) is safe and can significantly improve motor function in patients with chronic TBI, 9 underscoring the translational potential of engineered MSCs.

In this context, purinergic signaling plays a central role in the regulation of neuroinflammation and has recently emerged as a promising target for therapeutic intervention following TBI. 10 Among purinergic receptors, P2X7 has attracted particular attention owing to its pivotal role in coordinating inflammatory responses, including the activation of microglia, disruption of the blood–brain barrier (BBB), and recruitment of immune cells.10, 11, 12 Several experimental model studies have demonstrated that P2X7 expression and associated inflammatory pathways are upregulated acutely following TBI, highlighting this receptor as a promising target for therapeutic strategies designed to mitigate post‐injury inflammation and improve functional recovery.12, 13, 14 P2X7 is considered an attractive therapeutic target owing to its central role in post‐TBI inflammation. Nanobodies directed against P2X7 (P2X7nb) have been shown to offer high specificity and local activity, making them suitable for targeted intervention in the injured brain.

In the present study, we evaluated the short‐term brain homing and safety of human MSCs transfected with messenger RNA (mRNA) encoding a P2X7nb in a mouse model of TBI. Through magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) tracking, along with histological and functional analyses, we assessed the presence of MSCs in the brain and their ability to release P2X7nb during the acute phase post‐injury. This approach provides foundational insights into the feasibility of MSC‐mediated nanobody delivery and establishes a basis for the future optimization of cell‐based therapies targeting neuroinflammation after TBI.

METHODS

Culturing of MSC

MSCs (Lonza Inc., Walkersville, MD, USA) were maintained in RoosterNourish™‐MSC‐XF medium (RoosterBio, Frederick, MD, USA) under standard culture conditions (21% O2, 5% CO2, 95% humidity, 37°C). The medium was replaced twice weekly. Cells were passaged to 70%–80% confluence, corresponding to intervals of 5–7 days. Cells in Passages 4–5 were used for experiments.

MSC transfection and labeling

Three days prior to cell infusion, MSCs were seeded in 24‐well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells/cm2. The following day, cells were transfected with mRNA encoding P2X7nb (TriLink BioTechnologies, San Diego, CA, USA), a nanobody specifically designed to target the murine P2X7 receptor. 15 The growth medium was removed 30 min before transfection, cells were washed with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), and 200 µL of Opti‐Mem® I (1×) Reduced Serum Medium (31985070; ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was added per well. For each well, 48 µL of Opti‐Mem® I (1×) Reduced Serum Medium was prepared; 2 µL of Lipofectamine® 2000 (11668019; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) was added to this, followed by a 5‐min incubation at room temperature (RT) (20°C–23°C). Additionally, 0.5 µg of mRNA was diluted in 50 µL of Opti‐Mem® I (1×) Reduced Serum Medium. The liposome reagent and mRNA solution were mixed and incubated for 20 min at RT (20°C–23°C) to allow lipoplex formation. Subsequently, 100 µL of the reaction mixture was added to the cells. This was followed by a 5 h incubation at 37°C, 5% CO2. After incubation, the transfection medium was removed and replaced with standard growth medium. MoldayTM silane‐coated biodegradable iron microparticles labeled with rhodamine B (CL‐01Q02‐B‐50; BioPAL, Worcester, MA, USA) were used for cell labeling, as previously described. 16 In brief, MSCs were incubated overnight with Molday at a concentration of 20 µg Fe/mL, washed thrice in PBS, and resuspended in PBS on ice until use.

Flow cytometry analysis of transfection efficiency and cell size

The transfection efficiency of MSCs was evaluated by flow cytometry. One day post‐transfection, transfected and control MSCs were trypsinized and resuspended in 200 µL of PBS. To exclude dead cells, DAPI (4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole) was added to a final concentration of 1 µg/mL 10 min prior to acquisition. Flow cytometry was conducted using the Amnis ImageStream MkII flow cytometer to determine the percentage of positive cells.

In addition to flow cytometry, the cytometer's imaging function was applied to capture individual cell images. For each condition, 50 images each of live transfected and control MSCs were selected. Cell size was measured manually for each image using Fiji (ImageJ, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay for secreted protein quantification

Protein secretion was measured using the cell culture supernatants. MSCs were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells/cm2 in 24‐well plates; supernatants were collected at defined time points, replaced with fresh medium, and stored at −80°C until analysis. At each time point, supernatants from multiple wells were pooled, and the experiment was independently repeated twice. Protein concentrations were determined using the His Tag ELISA Detection Kit (L00436; GenScript, Piscataway, NJ, USA), following the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance was read at 450 nm. Standard curves were generated in GraphPad Prism, and the sample concentrations were interpolated according to the kit protocol.

Cell viability

Cell viability was measured using a Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) assay (B34304; Selleckchem, Houston, TX, USA). In brief, the CCK‐8 reagent was diluted 1:10 in RoosterNourish™‐MSC‐XF medium and incubated for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Measurements were performed in triplicate wells for each condition, and the assay was independently repeated twice.

Animal use statement

All experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the University of Maryland, Baltimore. Mice were housed at the University of Maryland, School of Medicine under a 12‐h light/dark cycle with a regular chow diet provided ad libitum. The animals were randomly assigned to groups.

Study design

All experiments were conducted using C57BL/6 J male mice (9–10 weeks old). The study design is shown in Figure 1. On Day 0, MSCs were transfected with P2X7nb mRNA and labeled with Molday. Ten mice were subjected to TBI using the closed‐head injury (CHI) repetitive model, as described previously. 17 On Day 1, MSCs (n = 5) or saline (n = 5) was injected through the right internal carotid artery (ICA), either under MRI guidance or followed by MRI acquisition. On Day 2, motor performance was assessed using the beam walk test. On Day 3, a second MRI session was conducted, after which animals were perfused with 5% sucrose followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA).

Experimental workflow for utilizing mesenchymal stem cells as cellular factories to deliver targeted P2X7 receptor blockade after traumatic brain injury. IA, intra‐arterial; MRI, magnetic resonance imaging; MSC, mesenchymal stem cell; TBI, traumatic brain injury.

TBI induction

For the CHI TBI model, 17 anesthesia was induced with 4% isoflurane and maintained with 1.5%–2% isoflurane. The hair covering the skull was removed using a commercial depilatory cream, after which the skull was exposed by incising the skin along the midline. CHI was carried out using the Benchmark CCI Stereotaxic Impactor (LinTech, Monrovia, CA, USA). The impact tip (5 mm in diameter) was then centered perpendicularly over the skull surface, at the midline between bregma and lambda. The injury was produced with the following parameters: 3 m/s velocity, 0.1 s duration, and 2 mm depth. The head was stabilized in a stereotactic apparatus under anesthesia without any rigid fixation, and the mouse was repositioned after each impact. Five consecutive impacts were administered within the same anesthesia session. Finally, the scalp was sutured. The temperature was maintained at 37.0°C ± 2°C using an electronic thermostat‐controlled heating pad (Kent Scientific, Torrington, CT, USA).

IA injection

One day following TBI, animals were anesthetized with 1%–2% isoflurane in air using a facemask. The right ICA was exposed through a lateral neck incision, and the pterygopalatine and external carotid arteries were temporarily ligated to direct the blood flow to the brain. MSCs (5 × 105 cells in 100 µL) were infused IA using an automated syringe pump (PHD 2000, Harvard Apparatus Inc., Holliston, MA, USA) at a rate of 150 µL/min. In one MSC‐treated animal, the IA catheter was secured in place and positioned in a Bruker 9.4 T MRI scanner to allow real‐time monitoring. The infusion was conducted under MRI guidance, as described previously. 18 Dynamic imaging during infusion comprised a 30 s baseline, two 20 s injections at 150 µL/min separated by a 20 s pause, followed by a final susceptibility‐weighted imaging (SWI) to confirm the cell distribution. This approach enabled the precise delivery of MSCs and the simultaneous assessment of their cerebral localization. One MSC‐treated animal received the infusion under real‐time MRI guidance, while the remaining MSC‐ and saline‐treated animals were injected and imaged afterwards. Animals received a subcutaneous injection of carprofen (5 mg/kg) post‐surgery for analgesia.

MRI acquisition and analysis

Multimodal MRI was used to longitudinally monitor the MSC engraftment and migration. Baseline T2‐weighted images of the head were acquired using a rapid acquisition with relaxation enhancement (RARE) sequence (repetition time [TR]/echo time [TE] = 2500/33 ms, matrix = 200 × 200, field of view [FOV] = 1.5 × 1.5 cm, slice thickness = 1 mm, 11 slices, and acquisition time = 125 s). SWI images were obtained using a fast low angle shot (FLASH) gradient‐echo sequence (TR/TE = 386/7.8 ms, matrix = 256 × 256, FOV = 1.5 × 1.5 cm, slice thickness = 0.7 mm, 13 slices, and acquisition time = 197.6 s). Quantitative analysis of MRI datasets enabled the characterization of MSC spatial distribution within the brain.

Brain histology

At the experimental endpoint, mice were transcardially perfused sequentially with PBS containing 5% sucrose, followed by 4% PFA in PBS. Following perfusion, the brains were carefully extracted and fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h at 4°C. Following fixation, the tissue was transferred to a 30% sucrose solution in water and incubated until it sank, indicating complete cryoprotection. For freezing, the cryoprotected brains were embedded in dry ice powder and stored at −80°C until further processing. The brain slices were then cut in a cryostat, from front to back, in 20 µm sections at −20°C.

For immunofluorescent staining, samples were washed with PBS, after which antigen retrieval was carried out with citrate buffer solution (1 M citric acid and 1 M sodium citrate; Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), heating in a microwave, and allowing it to cool to RT (20°C–23°C). The slides were then washed in permeabilizing solution (PBS + 3% Triton‐100; Amresco, Solon, OH, USA) five times for 10 min, and incubated with blocking solution (PBS + 3% Triton‐100 + 2% bovine serum albumin; Sigma‐Aldrich) for 30 min at RT (20°C–23°C).

Next, the slides were incubated with the primary antibody (Anti‐Von Willebrand Factor 1:250, catalog no. ab287962, Abcam, Cambridge, UK; HuNu 1:200, catalog no. ab191181, Abcam; IBA1 1:300, catalog no. 019‐19741, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan; GFAP 1:300, catalog no. Z 034, Dako/Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA; and IL‐1 beta 1:100, catalog no. ab283818, Abcam) in a humid chamber at 4°C overnight. The next day, the slides were washed and incubated with the secondary Ab in a humid chamber at RT (20°C–23°C) for 1 h. After washing, the slides were mounted using Vectashield mounting medium with DAPI (H‐1200, Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA). The slides were then observed under a Leica or ZEISS confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany; Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany).

Behavioral beam walk test

The beam walk was conducted in a room with dim light and at reduced noise, to which mice were allowed to habituate for 30–60 min. Motor function and coordination were assessed by measuring the ability of mice to cross an elevated narrow beam (length 1 m; diameter 5 mm) to reach a stable platform placed on the opposite side. They were then allowed to stay for 30 s. This procedure was repeated four times and recorded with a video camera. The number of hind faults was counted. All mice were trained for three consecutive days on the beam before the injury. The beam walking test was subsequently carried out on Day 2.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation, unless otherwise specified. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.1 (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Based on data distribution, either an unpaired t‐test or a Mann–Whitney U test was applied for group comparisons. For descriptive purposes, values are presented as mean ± standard deviation even when nonparametric tests were applied. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

Induction of P2X7‐blocking nanobody expression in MSCs

MSCs were transfected with mRNAs encoding the P2X7nb and characterized in vitro. The transfection efficiency, assessed by flow cytometry, reached 38.9% (Figure 2A). As the cytometer acquires individual cell images, these were also evaluated to determine cell size. Both transfected (24.77 ± 5.11 µm) and non‐transfected (25.69 ± 4.69 µm) MSCs showed comparable mean diameters, indicating that transfection did not affect the cell morphology (Figure 2B).

(B) Size distribution of MSCs showing a Gaussian‐like pattern. (C) Enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) quantification of nanobody levels in the supernatant of transfected MSC cultures. The culture medium was replaced and collected daily for seven consecutive days to assess the secretion dynamics over time. (D) Cell viability of transfected MSCs relative to control MSCs, measured by Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) assay. (E) Representative images of MSCs prepared for transplantation. Scale bars: 100 µm. DAPI, 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole.

To confirm that transfected cells secreted P2X7nb into the culture medium, the protein concentration in the supernatant was quantified by enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) over 7 days. This analysis demonstrated a marked increase in P2X7nb levels in the supernatant of MSCs‐P2X7nb compared with control MSCs, peaking within the first 24 h (17.29 ± 1.84 ng/mL), gradually declining thereafter up to 4 days, indicating efficient nanobody production and release by transfected MSCs (Figure 2C). In parallel, cell viability of MSCs seeded in 96‐well plates was assessed using the CCK‐8 assay and monitored over 7 days. Transfection resulted in a reduction in MSC viability compared with control cells, to 71.7% ± 14.1%, 63.8% ± 5.5%, and 73.12% ± 3.87% of the control levels over the first 3 days (Figure 2D). Thereafter, cell viability progressively recovered, and no significant differences were observed between transfected and control MSCs from Day 3 onwards.

Brain engraftment after IA delivery

Following in vitro testing and characterization, MSCs were transfected and labeled with iron oxide nanoparticles (Molday). Representative images of these MSCs prepared for transplantation are presented in Figure 2E. Cells were transfected the same day of TBI induction and delivered IA 1 day after TBI. The next day, the cells were administered IA under MRI guidance, enabling the real‐time visualization of cell delivery and engraftment in the brain. The sequential accumulation of labeled cells in the ipsilateral hemisphere during the injection process is presented in Figure 3A. The quantitative analysis of MRI signal intensity during IA injection showed a gradual accumulation of hypointense signal in the ipsilateral hemisphere. The initial drop in signal was caused by the hypointense contrast generated by Molday‐labeled MSCs, reflecting their accumulation. Following this initial accumulation, the signal stabilized at a reduced plateau, indicating sustained retention of the delivered cells (Figure 3B).

In addition, following completion of cell infusion and dynamic imaging, high‐resolution SWI MRI was conducted, providing enhanced sensitivity and spatial detail to assess the homing and biodistribution of MSCs throughout the ipsilateral hemisphere (Figure 3C,D). MRI was repeated on Day 3 post‐injection to evaluate cell persistence. While a partial recovery of signal intensity was observed, with the hypointense SWI signal decreasing from 2.45% ± 2.93% on Day 1 to 0.77% ± 0.88% on Day 3, the labeled cells remained clearly detectable, indicating a gradual clearance but sustained presence within the brain.

Interestingly, the engraftment pattern was not fully consistent among all subjects (Figure 3D). Comparison of MRI images from the five mice that received IA MSC infusion revealed variability in cell distribution. Most animals exhibited the expected moderate accumulation in the ipsilateral hemisphere, while others showed either minimal or more extensive bilateral cell presence. This was consistent with our prior reports. 19

Brains were collected for histological evaluation after MRI on Day 3. Analysis of histological sections confirmed the presence of labeled cells within the brain parenchyma, validating successful engraftment (Figure 4A,C). This distribution spatially correlated with that detected on MRI (Figure 4B). To further confirm the human origin and persistence of transplanted MSCs within the host brain tissue, sections were subjected to immunofluorescence staining using HuNu, a specific marker of human cell nuclei. HuNu‐positive cells were clearly identified within the brain parenchyma, supporting the survival and identity of the administered MSCs (Figure 4D).

To determine whether the administered cells remained trapped within the cerebral capillaries or were able to cross the vascular barrier and reach the brain tissue, the sections were stained for Von Willebrand Factor and analyzed by confocal microscopy to assess colocalization. Numerous cells were observed within the lumen of the blood vessels, while others appeared partially or fully embedded within the parenchyma, indicating that a subset of MSCs was capable of transmigrating across the vessel wall (Figure 4E). Quantitative analysis revealed that 43% of the detected MSCs were located outside of the vessels, confirming substantial extravasation into the brain parenchyma.

As a considerable proportion of cells remained intravascular, we subsequently examined whether their vascular location influenced the detectable release of P2X7nb within the tissue. Assessment of the in vitro release profile revealed that the secretion peak of P2X7nb had already passed by Day 3, although residual protein levels were expected. Fluorescence signals corresponding to the cell label (Molday) and the nanobody (HaloTag) were thresholded using identical parameters (Figure 4F), and their respective areas were separately quantified for intravascular and extravascular cells. The resulting ratios are presented in Figure 4G. As the data were not normally distributed, the results are presented as the median (range). Extravascular cells exhibited a significant tendency for the HaloTag signal to extend beyond the Molday‐positive area, although this effect was far less apparent in intravascular cells (1.03 ± 0.06 vs. 0.99 ± 0.12; p < 0.05, Mann–Whitney U test). These observations suggest that detectable nanobody release in the tissue was more commonly associated with cells located outside the vascular lumen.

Absence of adverse markers supports the safety of IA MSC delivery

To investigate the potential therapeutic and anti‐inflammatory effects of IA MSC delivery, inflammatory and glial markers were analyzed in the brain regions specifically containing the transplanted MSCs and compared with anatomically equivalent areas from control mice administered IA saline. Brain sections collected on Day 3 post‐delivery were immunostained for IBA1 (microglial activation), GFAP (astrocyte reactivity), and IL‐1β (inflammatory response). Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity did not reveal any significant differences between MSC‐ and saline‐treated animals for any of the markers evaluated (Figure 5A–F), indicating the lack of any detectable anti‐inflammatory effect under these conditions.

Immunofluorescence images of HaloTag‐labeled MSCs co‐stained with (A) IBA1, (B) GFAP, and (C) IL‐1β. Quantification of fluorescence intensity for (D) IBA1, (E) GFAP, and (F) IL‐1β. Further, the absence of increased glial activation or inflammatory signaling, together with the lack of change in motor performance, as assessed by the beam walk test at Day 2 post‐administration (Figure 5G), supports the safety of IA MSC delivery. Beam walk performance was comparable between groups (CTL: 45.00 ± 4.20 vs. treated: 43.40 ± 11.72 foot faults). These results indicate that MSC infusion does not exacerbate neuroinflammation or induce functional impairment compared with saline‐treated controls, underscoring the tolerability and feasibility of this administration route.

DISCUSSION

In the present study, we investigated the short‐term engraftment, safety, and early biological impact of P2X7nb‐expressing MSCs administered intra‐arterially in a mouse model of TBI, primarily aiming to evaluate the feasibility and safety of IA administration, as well as early cellular behavior following administration.

Although the potential anti‐inflammatory effects were explored, no clear therapeutic benefit was detected at the analyzed time point. This lack of effect may be due to several factors, including the limited number of MSCs reaching the brain parenchyma, modest nanobody expression, short 3‐day observation window, and inherent complexity of investigating early neuroinflammatory responses following TBI. Previous studies have demonstrated that pharmacological inhibition of P2X7 using Brilliant Blue G, a P2X7 receptor antagonist, can reduce cerebral edema, astrocyte activation, IL‐1β expression, and motor deficits following brain injury. 20 , 21 Similarly, treatment with P2X7 antagonists and other immunomodulators has been shown to decrease inflammatory microvesicles, glial activation, neuronal apoptosis, and IL‐1β levels in rat models, resulting in improved neurobehavioral outcomes. 22 In accordance with these findings, we measured the expression levels of IL‐1β, IBA1, and GFAP in regions surrounding MSCs to assess the potential anti‐inflammatory effects of P2X7nb‐MSCs. However, no clear reduction in these markers was observed, which may reflect the limited degree of receptor blockade under the current experimental conditions and/or the short assessment window.

Although MSCs were successfully detected in the brain, the low number of cells indicates that the nanobody concentrations may not have reached therapeutic levels under these conditions. Further strategies to enhance engraftment should therefore be explored. For example, transient BBB opening with mannitol has been shown to improve MSC delivery and survival in models of both Parkinson's disease 23 and TBI. 24 Such approaches could thus help increase MSC accumulation and nanobody release in the injured brain, potentially enabling a measurable therapeutic effect. Additionally, assessing MSC distribution in the peripheral organs would be valuable, as prior work has reported accumulation in the spleen and lungs up to 28 days post‐infusion. 23 Indeed, PET imaging can capture the dynamics of the whole‐body biodistribution of therapeutic agents after IA delivery, and would likely be beneficial for stem cell delivery. 25 Furthermore, the modest transfection efficiency and reduction in MSC viability following transfection likely further limited the effective nanobody dose achieved in vivo. Future studies should therefore systematically optimize these parameters, such as adjusting transfection timing relative to cell confluency, or implementing less cytotoxic delivery approaches. Accordingly, CCK‐8 analysis revealed a transient decrease in MSC viability after transfection, an expected consequence of the transfection procedure. From Day 4 onwards, the viability levels between transfected and control MSCs became comparable, coinciding with the attainment of confluency in vitro, indicating that the later plateau may reflect the spatial constraints of the assay, rather than persistent cytotoxicity.

Another important consideration is the short observation window of the present study. Only the acute phase was evaluated in this study, with experiments conducted up to 3 days post‐infusion. Previous studies have shown that MSCs can remain within cerebral vessels for several days before migrating into the brain tissue, 23 indicating that longer observation times may reveal higher parenchymal accumulation. As detectable nanobody release was more evident in MSCs that successfully crossed into the parenchyma, maximizing extravasation may be essential to achieve meaningful therapeutic exposure. In addition, recent evidence has suggested that delayed P2X7 antagonism may yield greater benefits than immediate blockade, 26 indicating that timing could also be critical to achieving therapeutic effects.

An important factor to consider when evaluating therapeutic effects is the use of human MSCs in immunocompetent mice. In the present study, we were not keen on using immunocompromised mice or immune system blockers, as this would modify the inflammatory response. Therefore, although our results do not provide any evidence of acute anti‐inflammatory efficacy, they highlight the safety of IA MSC administration and lay the groundwork for optimizing future studies aimed at achieving more robust P2X7 modulation.

An additional conceptual consideration of this study is the dual role of neuroinflammation following TBI. While early inflammatory responses contribute to debris clearance, tissue remodeling, and the initiation of repair processes, prolonged or dysregulated inflammation can become maladaptive and drive secondary injury. 27 Therapeutic strategies that broadly suppress inflammation risk disrupting this delicate balance. In this context, targeting a single signaling node, such as P2X7, may be insufficient to recalibrate the complex inflammatory milieu towards a net‐beneficial state, particularly during the acute phase. This complexity may partly explain the lack of any detectable benefit at the early time point examined here, and underscores the importance of considering timing, dosage, and multimodal immunomodulatory strategies when designing cell‐based or biologic interventions for TBI. This complexity, together with the short study duration, may explain why no therapeutic benefit was detected in our experiments.

In the present study, we investigated the IA administration of MSCs as an alternative delivery route in a murine model of TBI. Intravenous (IV) delivery, the most commonly used route, has shown beneficial effects in rats by modulating neuroinflammation, 1 , 2 and in combination with adjunct therapies such as regulatory T cells 28 or propranolol. 29 However, this route remains suboptimal, as extensive evidence has shown pulmonary engraftment. 30 Conversely, IC implantation provides direct neuroprotective effects 3 , 31 ; however, this technique is highly invasive and limits clinical translation. As such, IA administration represents a promising alternative, enabling more direct brain access and higher engraftment rates. Successful IA administration of MSCs has been described in several experiments using rat TBI models,24, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36 some of which reported that IA delivery results in significantly greater cerebral MSC accumulation than IV delivery does. 32 , 34 , 35 Our findings confirm the presence of MSCs in brain parenchyma on Day 3 post‐infusion. These results support IA administration as feasible and potentially more effective than systemic routes, while highlighting the need for strategies to enhance persistence and therapeutic potential.

Despite these advantages, IA administration is limited by the challenge of the BBB. The BBB has long limited the success of brain‐targeted therapies; however, during pathological conditions such as TBI, transient disruption provides a unique opportunity for cell‐based delivery. MSCs are large cells (approximately 25 µm in diameter in the present study), but they possess intrinsic homing capabilities that enable them to migrate actively towards injury sites through transient endothelial fenestrations. 30 , 37 In our study, some MSCs remained within the blood vessels, while others were identified in the parenchyma, consistent with transient BBB opening after TBI. Moreover, HaloTag signals more commonly extended beyond the cell boundaries in extravascular MSCs, suggesting that nanobody release may be more effective once MSCs reach the parenchyma.

The variability observed in cell distribution among the mice highlights the necessity of real‐time, noninvasive monitoring of IA stem cell administration to the brain. In the present study, we were able to observe the different cell retention profiles in the brains of five mice administered IA MSCs on Days 1 and 3. In this context, dynamic MRI represents a powerful tool to assess delivery efficiency in vivo, accounting for both technical and biological factors that might influence engraftment patterns. For example, engraftment can vary with species and model, as seen in a mouse model of Alzheimer's disease where MSCs were barely detectable 6 h after infusion. 38 Similarly, longitudinal MRI studies in TBI models have revealed that the use of high‐relaxivity contrast agents and T2w imaging enables the precise tracking of MSC localization over time, thus capturing the subtle differences in cell distribution and retention at the injury site. 39 , 40 Along the same lines, prior studies have proven the value of MRI for tracking stem cell delivery and biodistribution in vivo. 41 Incorporating real‐time imaging into IA delivery protocols allows for immediate adjustments, enhancing the precision and reproducibility of the procedure, a critical consideration for clinical translation, in which patient variability and procedural nuances can significantly affect outcomes.

Overall, our findings support IA administration as a feasible and safe route for MSC‐based delivery to the injured brain, thus offering more direct access than systemic routes and less invasiveness than IC implantation. Although no acute therapeutic effects were observed, this approach was well tolerated, highlighting safety as a key outcome. Future studies should therefore focus on optimizing engraftment conditions and extending the observation period to fully evaluate MSC persistence, biodistribution, and therapeutic potential in TBI.

CONCLUSION

Our findings demonstrate the feasibility and safety of IA administration of human MSCs expressing P2X7nb in a murine model of CHI TBI. The findings also confirm short‐term engraftment and nanobody release within the injured brain, while highlighting the technical and biological factors that influence delivery efficiency. The integration of real‐time MRI monitoring also proves valuable for optimizing IA injection and could be used to enhance reproducibility in translational applications. Taken together, these results indicate that MSC‐mediated nanobody delivery represents a promising strategy for the targeted modulation of neuroinflammation after TBI, warranting further studies to optimize engraftment, assess long‐term persistence, and evaluate therapeutic efficacy.
