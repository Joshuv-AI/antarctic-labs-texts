An improved Q-learning approach for rescue path planning in mass casualty incidents under damaged road network conditions
Authors: Sheng Wang, Jingrong Yang, Peng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmergency rescue path planning faces severe challenges due to the uncertainty of the road network and the complexity of road damage following mass casualty incidents (MCIs) such as earthquakes. Traditional static path planning algorithms struggle to cope with such dynamic environments, while standard reinforcement learning methods often cause agents to fall into local optima, failing to meet rescue safety requirements. To address this dilemma, this paper proposes an improved Q-learning algorithm that integrates Adaptive Simulated Annealing (ASA) and Experience Replay (ER) mechanisms. The algorithm dynamically regulates the exploration degree via the ASA strategy, ensuring the agent can escape local traps in complex damaged environments. Furthermore, it incorporates the ER mechanism to reuse key path experiences, thereby improving convergence speed. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed method demonstrates efficient online re-planning capabilities in response to structural environmental updates. It provides high-confidence decision support for time-critical post-disaster rescue, balancing the trade-off between accessibility and safety.Subject terms: Engineering, Natural hazards

Introduction

Mass casualty incidents (MCIs), typically triggered by natural disasters, technological accidents, or social conflicts, are characterized by their sudden onset and the difficulty of early warning. In recent years, the increasing frequency of natural disasters and man-made accidents worldwide has made MCIs increasingly prevalent1. According to data from emergency event databases, in 2024 alone, the world experienced 393 natural disasters, resulting in over 16,000 deaths and affecting as many as 167 million people2. In these incidents, the “golden hour” for rescue is extremely precious. The ability of emergency response units to reach the scene promptly is a critical determinant of victim survival rates and the overall success of the rescue operation. Therefore, establishing a rapid and safe lifeline for rescue forces remains one of the most critical challenges in MCI emergency response.

MCIs, particularly geological disasters like earthquakes, often devastate urban infrastructure, most notably by crippling the road network. This destruction manifests not only as physical disruptions—such as collapse and damage—but, more critically, as extreme environmental uncertainty. Pre-disaster maps and navigation data are rendered instantly obsolete. The actual navigability of roads becomes completely unknown, and the situation remains highly dynamic, subject to continual change from aftershocks and secondary disasters. This redefines the core emergency response challenge, shifting it from a simple shortest path problem to a complex dynamic decision-making task within an unknown, high-risk environment characterized by incomplete information3. Consequently, rescue vehicles must be capable of dynamically exploring and planning optimal paths that simultaneously balance safety and efficiency, all while navigating around hazardous areas in real-time.

While conventional path planning algorithms, such as A* or Dijkstra, excel in static and fully observable environments, they rely heavily on precise a priori map information. In the event of unforeseen environmental changes, these algorithms are often rendered ineffective by erroneous information4. Consequently, the research community has shifted towards model-free Reinforcement Learning (RL) methods. RL was initially applied extensively in disciplines such as game theory, information theory, control theory, and operations research5. Over time, its application expanded to autonomous mobile robotics and path planning6. Q-learning, as a representative algorithm, is theoretically capable of autonomously learning optimal paths in unknown environments through its trial-and-error interaction mechanism7. However, when standard Q-learning is directly applied to high-risk, complex real-world scenarios like MCIs, its inherent deficiencies are exposed. Firstly, its inefficient exploration-exploitation mechanism is highly prone to converging on local optima. In a rescue context, this translates to a catastrophic risk: leading teams into dead ends or hazardous zones, potentially causing catastrophic consequences. Secondly, its slow learning process makes it incapable of quickly generating a reliable route plan within the brief decision-making window available for emergency response.

To address the aforementioned research gap and the pressing need for rapid, reliable path decisions in uncertain road networks, this paper proposes a novel emergency decision-support method based on an improved Q-learning algorithm. The core advancement of this approach is the synergistic integration of Adaptive Simulated Annealing (ASA) and Experience Replay (ER) mechanisms, which specifically overcomes the sparse reward and local optima traps inherent in damaged road environments. While conventional Q-learning often struggles with state-space bottlenecks when key road segments are disconnected, the ASA strategy introduces a dynamic exploration mechanism that facilitates escape from dead-end states. Concurrently, the ER mechanism ensures that rare but critical high-safety rescue trajectories are preserved and repeatedly utilized to stabilize policy convergence. Related work

The uncertainties inherent in MCI emergency response pose a severe challenge to conventional path planning algorithms, prompting a research shift towards model-free Reinforcement Learning methods. Q-learning, in particular, has garnered significant attention due to its autonomous learning capabilities. However, standard Q-learning exhibits limitations when directly applied to MCI scenarios. Consequently, the current research focus has converged on improving the algorithm to enhance its effectiveness in this specific context.

Routing challenges in emergency response

In MCIs, emergency vehicle routing is a critical determinant of successful rescue operations8 and represents a core issue within the fields of emergency logistics and disaster management. Traditionally, research in this domain has centered on optimization methods derived from Operations Research (OR). This typically involves modeling the emergency routing challenge as a variant of the Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP) and employing graph-based or heuristic algorithms to derive solutions.

However, the post-disaster environment diverges significantly from conventional routing scenarios that typically prioritize the shortest path; its most salient feature is extreme uncertainty. As Wang noted9, factors such as demand volume, demand locations, and vehicle travel times become highly unpredictable due to disaster impacts, while the scarcity of real-time data renders precise planning exceptionally difficult. Furthermore, severe traffic congestion emerges as a major impediment, occurring even where roads remain physically intact. This congestion is often driven by massive evacuation flows, the influx of rescue vehicles, and the reduced capacity of damaged road segments. Bahar et al.10 identified congestion as a critical factor impeding the efficiency of victim transport during earthquake response. By employing the Bureau of Public Roads (BPR) function to quantify the impact of congestion on travel times, their work demonstrated that the geographically shortest path is frequently not the temporally optimal one.

The physical destruction and extreme informational uncertainty caused by disasters render a priori map-based methods ineffective11. While heuristics like Genetic Algorithms (GAs) have been applied to post-disaster VRP, they often suffer from unstable and suboptimal solutions. Thus, robust path planning in incomplete and highly uncertain networks remains a significant technological bottleneck in emergency management. This challenge has driven the research community to pivot towards model-free machine learning, where RL has shown immense potential.

Q-learning, as a representative algorithm, is a foundational approach valued for its ability to learn optimal policies through direct, trial-and-error interaction with unknown environments, thereby autonomously planning paths to the target12. For instance, Rocha et al.13 proposed a method to dynamically adjust Q-learning iterations for online planning. Their approach aims to adaptively determine sufficient iterations based on environmental complexity, seeking a balance between solution quality and reduced decision time in unknown complex environments. Similarly, focusing on Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) search and rescue in unknown environments, Wu et al.14 designed an adaptive-velocity Q-learning algorithm. This method uses composite rewards to adaptively adjust flight speed and incorporates sub-domain search and Q-table initialization strategies to accelerate target discovery and convergence to the optimal path.

The SARSA algorithm15 is another representative RL approach. Unlike Q-learning, SARSA eliminates the maximization operator in its update rule, tending to plan trajectories that, while potentially longer, maintain an optimal safety distance from hazardous areas. For example, Mohan et al.16 proposed an Iterative SARSA algorithm for mobile robot navigation in unknown environments. By alternately executing multiple SARSA iterations between the start and goal points to progressively refine the path, this method aims to address the execution risks of standard Q-learning and the sub-optimality of standard SARSA, ultimately achieving an optimal path that ensures safety.

While the aforementioned studies demonstrate the potential of Q-learning and its variants in handling dynamics, improving online efficiency, and accelerating convergence, directly applying these methods to the specific MCI scenarios addressed in this paper remains challenging. The MCI environment is not merely unknown or dynamic; more critically, it is characterized by high risk-uncertainty stemming from road damage and extremely stringent temporal constraints.

Enhanced exploration strategies

In MCI rescue, merely finding a suboptimal path is wholly insufficient; discovering the global optimum is imperative to avoid critical delays. A core bottleneck restricting standard Q-learning in routing applications is the efficacy of its exploration mechanism, notably the widely adopted ε-greedy strategy. This strategy introduces a probability, ε, for selecting random actions, aiming to escape local policies and explore the broader state space. However, this simplistic mechanism presents a fundamental dilemma: on one hand, a low ε value leads to insufficient exploration, making the algorithm highly prone to premature convergence into a local optimum trap rather than the global optimum17. Conversely, a high or fixed ε value compromises convergence stability. The algorithm continues to perform unnecessary random exploration even in the late stages of training, leading to suboptimal pathing efficiency and a failure to stabilize. In the high-risk environment of MCI rescue characterized by a complex state space, converging on a local optimum is tantamount to selecting a suboptimal or even hazardous route, potentially resulting in catastrophic outcomes.

To address the aforementioned problems, researchers have proposed various optimization schemes for exploration mechanisms. The most straightforward improvement is to implement a dynamic rather than static exploration factor, ε, which typically maintains a high value during the initial learning phase and gradually decays as training episodes increase18. For instance, Li et al.19 segmented the learning process into three distinct phases—exploration, exploration-exploitation, and exploitation. By dynamically adjusting the exploration factor across these stages, this staged mechanism enables the agent to adopt exploration strategies that are better suited to specific learning periods, thereby effectively balancing the algorithm’s strategic requirements. Furthermore, researchers have designed more sophisticated dynamic adjustment mechanisms. Notably, Ben-Akka et al.20 proposed a novel ε-greedy decay strategy that dynamically adjusts the exploration rate based on cumulative rewards, aiming to ensure a smooth transition from exploration to exploitation and to stabilize the agent’s behavior.

A second class of methods selects actions probabilistically based on Q-value estimates, with Boltzmann exploration being a representative example. To combine the advantages of ε-greedy and Boltzmann exploration, Fallooh et al.21 proposed a hybrid strategy that blends both methods during the exploration phase to achieve a better balance between exploration and exploitation. However, Boltzmann strategies suffer from significant drawbacks: they are highly sensitive to the relative magnitude of Q-values, requiring careful tuning of the temperature parameter, and may degenerate into a purely greedy policy when Q-value disparities are significant. More advanced strategies attempt to actively guide exploration towards regions of the state space with the highest epistemic uncertainty or potential value. For instance, the Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) algorithm22 estimates a confidence interval for each action and preferentially selects the one with the highest upper bound. Building on this, Sheng et al.23 proposed an improved ε-UCB action selection policy that employs a neural network to model the uncertainty of state-action pairs, thereby effectively enhancing exploration efficiency. Similarly, methods such as count-based exploration24 or intrinsic curiosity25 incentivize agents to explore novel or unpredictable states by introducing additional reward signals. Zhou et al.25, for example, integrated an Intrinsic Curiosity Module (ICM) into DQN, utilizing prediction errors to quantify state novelty and guide UAVs in performing more effective coverage path planning. While these methods theoretically enable more effective exploration, they are often hindered by high computational complexity or the requirement for complex models to quantify uncertainty. A distinct and sophisticated approach involves integrating the principles of Simulated Annealing (SA) into the decision-making process. For instance, Mao et al.26 incorporated SA principles into the action selection policy of Q-learning. In their method, the agent probabilistically accepts a suboptimal random action based on a temperature parameter that changes dynamically, thereby increasing environmental exploration capabilities and better balancing the exploration-exploitation dilemma. Zhong and Wang27 utilized SA principles to guide the adjustment of a dynamic exploration factor. Furthermore, Shi et al.28 combined an improved SA algorithm with Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) to solve the coverage path planning problem for cleaning robots, demonstrating SA’s potential in complex spatial searches. Recently, Zhong and Wang27 proposed an improved Q-learning algorithm, termed Heuristic and Simulated Annealing-based Q-learning (HSA-QL), which incorporates a heuristic reward based on Euclidean distance and a simulated annealing-guided exploration factor to enhance convergence in cross-regional planning. Compared to the random exploration of ε-greedy, this approach is inherently more goal-directed and possesses a greater potential to escape deep local optimum traps.

While these improved strategies enhance the exploration performance of Q-learning in various aspects, applying them to MCI response scenarios requires cautious evaluation. The MCI environment is not only complex and filled with traps formed by high-risk road segments, but also imposes stringent and often conflicting requirements for both real-time decision-making and global optimality.

Enhanced learning efficiency

Emergency decision-making imposes exceptionally stringent requirements on timeliness. However, the standard Q-learning algorithm is hindered by its online mechanism where experiences are utilized only once and then immediately discarded. This results in low sample efficiency and a sluggish learning process, making it difficult to rapidly generate reliable solutions within the brief decision-making window of an MCI rescue. To address the core challenges of slow convergence and low learning efficiency, the research community has explored solutions from multiple dimensions.

One of the most direct methods to enhance learning efficiency is Reward Shaping—optimizing the reward function to transition from sparse to dense, thereby providing the agent with clearer gradient guidance. For instance, Jaramillo-Martínez et al.29 focused on reward function strategies, proposing a function that incentivizes agents to select paths with fewer turns, thereby reducing total distance while simultaneously accelerating learning. Hao et al.30 combined Q-learning with the Artificial Potential Field (APF) method and designed a dynamic reward function specifically to improve convergence speed. In the multi-agent context, Song et al.31 proposed a cooperative reward shaping technique that designs rewards by evaluating an agent’s influence on its neighbors, fostering cooperation and enhancing overall learning efficiency.

Learning efficiency is governed not only by reward signals but also by the stability of the value function update mechanism. Standard Q-learning and its Deep Q-Network (DQN) variants suffer from a known overestimation bias, wherein Q-values tend to be systematically overestimated. Van Hasselt et al.32 explicitly identified this issue and demonstrated that such overestimation is detrimental to performance. To address this, Double Q-learning and its deep variant, DDQN, were introduced. These methods effectively mitigate Q-value overestimation by decoupling action selection from value evaluation, thereby improving algorithmic stability and convergence efficiency. This approach has found application in emergency scenarios; for example, Zhou et al.33 employed a Double DQN (DDQN) framework to guide multi-agent path planning in a virtual fire emergency simulation.

Despite the stability improvements offered by the aforementioned methods, they fail to address the fundamental limitation of data inefficiency in standard Q-learning, where experiences are typically discarded immediately after a single update. Experience Replay (ER) serves as a cornerstone for enhancing sample efficiency, not only in Deep Reinforcement Learning but also in tabular settings. By constructing a replay buffer to store historical data, ER allows the agent to revisit past transitions and perform offline updates without direct interaction with the real environment. This mechanism maximizes the utility of individual interactions, significantly accelerating policy propagation within complex state spaces. A representative application is found in the work of Jiang et al.34, who integrated ER with Deep Q-Networks (DQN) and heuristic knowledge for robotic path planning. Their results demonstrated that the inclusion of ER enabled the robot to converge to the optimal policy in significantly less time and fewer steps. Building on this foundation, researchers have developed a more advanced variant known as Prioritized Experience Replay (PER), which prioritizes the learning of experiences with higher Temporal Difference (TD) errors. Studies by Gök35 and Zhu36 both combined PER with Dueling Double Deep Q-Networks (D3QN) for dynamic path planning, further corroborating the significant advantages of PER in accelerating convergence and enhancing path quality.

Environment modelling

Grid map modeling

Environment modeling is the foundational process of translating the physical space of an MCI rescue into a mathematical construct that a Reinforcement Learning (RL) agent can comprehend and interact with. Various modeling approaches exist in the field of path planning, such as geometric feature-based, topological, and grid-based methods. Geometric methods often incur high computational costs in complex or dynamic environments, while topological methods disregard geometric information, making them ill-suited for MCI rescue scenarios that require precise routing37.

This study adopts the grid-based method as the foundational modeling tool. By discretizing the complex continuous environment into a series of uniform grid cells, this approach aligns perfectly with the discrete state space definition of RL, where each cell constitutes an independent state38. Crucially, the grid-based method possesses robust capabilities for environmental information representation, allowing rich attributes to be assigned to each state. This feature is vital for MCI scenarios as it enables the model to transcend traditional binary representations. Instead, it assigns a reliability attribute to each grid cell, thereby quantifying the degree of damage. The specific map used in this study is illustrated in Fig. 2.

The Q-learning solution algorithm integrating ASA and ER

The proposed approach enhances the standard Q-learning framework by embedding two complementary mechanisms: Adaptive Simulated Annealing (ASA) and Experience Replay (ER). In this architecture, the ASA strategy replaces the traditional static exploration policy to facilitate escape from local optima in damaged road segments. Simultaneously, the ER mechanism decouples the temporal correlation of rescue sequences by sampling past transitions from a memory buffer, ensuring stable policy convergence in high-uncertainty environments.

Exploration strategy optimization

To address the propensity of the standard Q-learning algorithm and its ε-greedy policy to converge on local optima, this paper integrates the principles of SA to enhance the exploration mechanism. SA is a stochastic optimization algorithm based on the Monte Carlo method. Inspired by the physical annealing process in solid-state physics, it is highly effective at finding the global optimum for complex optimization problems. The core advantage of the SA algorithm lies in its intrinsic stochastic “jump” mechanism. This mechanism allows the algorithm to probabilistically accept a suboptimal solution (i.e., one that temporarily degrades the objective function) during its search, thereby enabling it to effectively avoid the pitfall of converging on a local optimum.

Once a promising global basin of attraction is identified, the subsequent rapid cooling serves as a deliberate design choice tailored for MCI rescue scenarios. Compared to traditional logarithmic or exponential cooling schedules, which frequently expend critical computational time on redundant late-stage exploration, this rapid decay compels the algorithm to swiftly transition into pure exploitation. Consequently, it accelerates the stabilization of the optimal policy to meet the stringent temporal constraints of emergency response.

Learning efficiency optimization

To address the challenge of low learning efficiency in the standard Q-learning algorithm, this study incorporates the ER mechanism to optimize its learning process. While the ER mechanism is widely recognized as a cornerstone technique in Deep Reinforcement Learning to stabilize neural network training, its integration into tabular Q-learning is theoretically justified and highly beneficial for MCI scenarios. Although the Markov property guarantees that environmental state transitions are independent, the agent’s actual sampling trajectory consists of highly correlated sequential steps. In the context of damaged road networks with sparse and high-variance rewards, early successful trajectories to the MCI site are exceedingly rare and valuable. Standard tabular updates propagate these delayed rewards backward by only one step per episode. The ER mechanism acts as a background planning process, randomly sampling past transitions to break trajectory correlation and accelerating the backward propagation of delayed rewards across the tabular state space without requiring additional physical environment interactions. The Standard Q-learning algorithm employs an online learning approach: for every step the agent takes, it uses the generated experience transition (s, a, r, s’) to update the Q-table once. This experience is then immediately discarded.

This use-and-discard model exhibits two major deficiencies when applied to complex problems:

Low Sample Efficiency: Experiences are severely wasted. Many valuable—and often rare—successful experiences are utilized only a single time.

High Sample Correlation: Consecutive experiences are highly similar in time and space. This highly correlated sequence of training samples introduces bias and high variance into the Q-value updates. This, in turn, causes the learning process to oscillate violently and prevents the policy from converging stably.

To counter these challenges, the ER mechanism creates a replay buffer D with a fixed capacity to store historical experiences. During training, the algorithm no longer learns solely from the most recent, real-time experience. Instead, it randomly samples a mini-batch of historical transitions from D to perform its updates.

While ER is conventionally employed in Deep Reinforcement Learning to stabilize neural network gradients, its integration into this tabular Q-learning framework serves a distinct, problem-specific purpose. In severely fragmented MCI road networks, the terminal path guidance reward is extremely sparse. By shifting from a sequential, single-step update to a randomized batch update (Eq. 9), this modification decouples the learning process from the agent’s immediate physical trajectory. This specifically accelerates the backward propagation of rare, successful terminal signals across the disconnected grid states, thereby addressing the low sample efficiency inherent in standard tabular Q-learning when applied to disaster scenarios. Specifically, the ER mechanism enhances the accuracy of action selection in the current state through the global propagation of Q-values. By continuously sampling and learning from diverse historical transitions that are not temporally or spatially adjacent to the current position, the algorithm effectively propagates delayed rewards across the broader state space. Consequently, when the agent evaluates candidate actions in its current state, the corresponding Q-values have already been refined by these historical updates. This provides a more accurate and unbiased estimation of the long-term expected return, thereby preventing the agent from making suboptimal action selections based merely on short-sighted local evaluations.

Sensitivity analysis of cooling schedules

To evaluate the impact of different temperature update functions on the algorithm’s convergence performance and global exploration capabilities, a sensitivity analysis was conducted. The proposed ASA strategy was benchmarked against two widely adopted conventional simulated annealing decay functions: exponential decay and logarithmic decay, under identical experimental conditions.

As illustrated in Fig. 11, the varying cooling schedules exhibit distinctly different convergence behaviors in the complex MCI environment. The exponential decay schedule features a rapid initial temperature drop, which causes the agent to prematurely transition into the exploitation phase. Consequently, the rapid loss of exploration momentum leads the algorithm to converge to a local optimum, evidenced by a suboptimal average reward and a persistently high step count. Conversely, the logarithmic decay schedule maintains a higher temperature over a prolonged period. While this prolonged exploration prevents early convergence to local optima, it induces continuous random actions during the later stages of training. This results in significant performance oscillations and an inability to stabilize an optimal path within the required timeframe.

Sensitivity analysis of different cooling schedules.

In contrast, the proposed ASA algorithm utilizes an initial sustained high-temperature phase to effectively bypass high-risk local traps. The subsequent rapid cooling phase compels a swift transition to pure exploitation. The empirical results demonstrate that this two-stage mechanism enables the ASA algorithm to converge to the highest average reward and the lowest step count. This indicates that the customized decay strategy successfully accelerates policy stabilization while preserving global optimality, rendering it highly suitable for time-critical post-disaster routing tasks.

Conclusion

This paper addresses the critical challenge of emergency path planning following MCIs, a problem characterized by severe road network damage and high environmental uncertainty, by proposing a novel decision-support algorithm. To overcome the inherent limitations of standard Q-learning—specifically its difficulty in balancing exploration efficiency with convergence speed, its propensity to fall into local optimum traps, and its sluggish learning process—we propose an improved Q-learning algorithm that integrates an ER mechanism with an ASA exploration strategy. Simulation results strongly validate the efficacy of the proposed method. The improved Q-learning algorithm demonstrated significant performance gains; compared to both the high-performing baseline algorithms and the safer, more reliable SARSA algorithm, agents guided by the improved algorithm achieved higher reward values. This indicates the capability to identify paths with lower traversal costs, thereby providing rescue teams with faster and more reliable routing solutions.

Despite these positive results, this study has certain limitations, which also highlight directions for future research. First, the current environment modeling is based on grid maps, which simplifies the complex topological structure of real-world urban road networks. Future research should focus on migrating and applying this algorithm to graph-based real-world road network data. Second, this study currently considers only single-agent path planning, whereas real-world MCI response requires multi-party coordination. Therefore, extending this framework into a Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning system to achieve coordinated scheduling of rescue fleets represents a highly valuable research direction. Finally, while this study introduced a simulated aftershock to validate the algorithm’s online re-planning capabilities, this localized disruption remains a simplified abstraction. Real-world MCI environments are characterized by compounding, multi-variable uncertainties. Therefore, future work should incorporate continuous and large-scale dynamic factors—such as cascading aftershocks, evolving traffic congestion, and asynchronous road repair progress—to evaluate the algorithm’s real-time adaptability in truly complex, high-fidelity disaster scenarios.
