From classroom to the counter: exploring knowledge, attitudes, and practices toward Down syndrome among pharmacy students and pharmacists
Authors: Isra Dmour, Khawla Dhamen Al-Hamaideh, Mohanad Odeh, Sanaa Al-Dmour, Omar Mohammad Alzubi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe worldwide prevalence of Down syndrome (DS) is increasing. Limited DS-specific information and dosing guidelines can hinder pharmacists’ ability to effectively respond to DS related inquiries. As accessible healthcare providers, pharmacists are well positioned to identify and address these challenges, yet their role in optimizing DS care remains underexplored. This study aimed at evaluating community pharmacists’ knowledge, confidence, and practices related to DS pharmacotherapy, and to identify training needs that can improve their role in optimizing medication management for individuals with DS.MethodsA cross-sectional online questionnaire was conducted among community pharmacists and pharmacy students to assess their knowledge, practices and confidence regarding DS pharmacotherapy. The survey included questions on educational background, experience with DS-related inquiries, and training needs. Data was analyzed using descriptive statistics, scoring, and tests for statistical significance.ResultsFour hundred and two participants completed the study. The results revealed that a majority had limited familiarity with DS pharmacotherapy. Levels in managing medication needs for DS patients were generally low, particularly for prescription medications. More than 90% of participants support the integration of specialized courses, ongoing education, and pharmacist training within the pharmacy curriculum. Positive attitudes toward integrating DS-related content in the curriculum were reported by 94% of respondents. They also emphasized the active involvement of public authorities, collaboration with community organizations, expansion of clinical research, and greater use of social media to increase DS awareness and improve early healthcare access. Knowledge scores were highest for Clinical Causes, Symptoms and Treatment (12.90 ± 5.84; range − 14 to 14) and lowest for Causes and Prevalence (− 3.61 ± 3.04; range − 14 to 14), with modest scores for Prevention (2.20 ± 2.10; range − 6 to 6) and Treatment (3.05 ± 1.81; range − 10 to 10). The mean of overall total knowledge score was 12.20 ± 7.23 (range − 44 to 44). Among participants, only 27% demonstrated good knowledge scores (≥ 70%), while 62% showed moderate knowledge and 11% poor knowledge Scoring analysis indicated significant gaps in specific knowledge domains related to DS treatment and dose adjustment.ConclusionsThis study highlights gaps in participants’ knowledge and confidence regarding DS pharmacotherapy, emphasizing the need to update curricula to a more structured DS-focused curricula and mandatory continuing education. Strengthening pharmacists’ competencies in this area can enhance their ability to provide safe, effective care for patients with DS and potentially contribute to positive outcomes in the health system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09513-z.Keywords: Down syndrome, Awareness, Knowledge, Pharmacists, Pharmacy education, Pharmacy practice, Scoring, Cross-sectional, Online study

Introduction

Down syndrome (DS) is a genetic disease in which the number of chromosomes increases from 46 to 47 due to abnormal cell division in the genetic material from chromosome 21. DS is the most reported autosomal trisomy [1]. DS has three main cytogenetic types: non-disjunction (trisomy 21), mosaic, and Robertsonian translocation. Non-disjunction is the most common form, accounting for about 95% of cases, and occurs when abnormal cell division before fertilization results in three copies of chromosome 21 in all cells. Mosaic DS arises from abnormal cell division after fertilization, producing a mixture of normal and trisomy 21 cells. In Robertsonian translocation, chromosome 21 attaches to another chromosome before or at conception. Less common types of DS have also been reported [2, 3]. The global incidence of DS is estimated to be between 1 and 10 per 1000 live births [4, 5]. Its prevalence, however, rises among infants born to mothers over 40 years [5, 6]. In a retrospective study conducted in Jordan, DS was reported to be frequently seen among mothers younger than 25 [7]. Results of chromosomal analysis in 80 patients showed that free trisomy (non-disjunction) was the most observed type, followed by mosaics and translocation (92.5%, 3.8%, and 2.5%, respectively) [7]. Although studies have explored in-utero gene and cell therapies for DS, further research is needed to confirm their safety and effectiveness [8].

DS lifelong complications include mental disability, delays in development, and learning difficulties in affected children. Common health issues in children with DS aged 1–14 include ophthalmologic abnormalities, heart defects, gastrointestinal anomalies, hypothyroidism, hearing and vision disorders, sleep apnea, hip dislocation, and leukemia [2]. Other associated conditions are obesity, epilepsy, diabetes, infections, sensory impairments, learning disabilities, and mental health problems. Early intervention, starting as early as 60 days after birth, has been shown to improve long-term developmental outcomes significantly [7, 9, 10]. Early therapies- behavioral, emotional, occupational, speech, and physical—should begin shortly after birth [11, 12]. Delays in early intervention for DS may result in extended hospitalizations and health complications due to limited awareness, low parental program involvement, lack of financial support, medical complications, high costs, and a shortage of specialized centers [13, 14].

Effective DS management requires a multidisciplinary approach involving early intervention, continuous medical care, tailored education, and family support [11, 13, 15, 16]. Reports have shown that people with DS live an average of over 60 years, with no medication or surgery that can manage or cure DS [1, 16–19]. Management also addresses medical and developmental issues such as celiac disease, thyroid dysfunction, hypogonadism, congenital heart defects, Alzheimer’s, and obesity. Treatment may include physical therapy, medications (e.g., levothyroxine, GI and sleep aids, antiepileptics, antipsychotics, cardiac drugs), assistive devices, and surgeries [13, 17, 20]. While compounds like apigenin show promise in preclinical studies, progress in DS research remains slow due to limited funding, support, and awareness, despite rising global prevalence [11, 19].

Published reports have shown that genetic variation greatly impacts drug pharmacokinetics in DS patients, resulting in comorbidities, organ dysfunction, obesity and drug interactions necessitating dose alteration. Examples include sedatives and muscle relaxants [21–23], anticonvulsants [24], chemotherapeutic medications [25–27] and other medication [27–29].On the other hand, medical conditions common in DS patients, like celiac disease, can alter the absorption of the medications besides mal-absorption of iron, cyanocobalamin and folic acid, which is considered a predisposing factor of anemia in DS people that require supplementation [18, 30]. Hormone replacement therapy is often essential for females with DS to restore hormonal balance and manage related symptoms [31]. Many dietary supplements (antioxidants, choline, essential oils, herbs, enzymes, minerals and vitamins) are marketed to improve cognitive and health outcomes in DS. However, caregivers should consult healthcare providers to avoid potential side effects and interactions [15, 31–35].

The Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices (KAP) framework is a widely used research model in public health and healthcare education that evaluates three essential domains influencing professional behavior. Knowledge refers to the factual understanding and awareness individuals have about a condition or practice; Attitudes encompass their beliefs, perceptions, and confidence toward that condition; and Practices represent the actual application of knowledge and attitudes in real-life decision-making and service delivery. Educating healthcare providers- including pharmacists- is crucial to enhancing the quality of life for individuals with DS and their families [28]. Pharmacists’ growing role in community healthcare is largely due to their accessibility compared to physicians [36–38]. They can support patients by offering medication reviews, dosing guidance, adherence strategies, health education, supplement advice, and promoting screenings. A previous report from the UK highlighted the critical role of pharmacists in consultations related to learning difficulties using DS as an example through delivering clear and easy-to-understand information and adequate support about DS medications [10]. Positive governmental procedures were reported in some countries like the UK, where guidance for pharmacists was issued on dealing with patients with learning disabilities like DS [39]. Evaluating their knowledge and attitudes toward DS can outline curriculum improvements and training needs [10, 40]. This study aims to assess pharmacy students’ and pharmacists’ knowledge and practices in managing DS drug-related inquiries, explore curriculum gaps, identify factors affecting student engagement, and highlight the value of educational and continuous training for practicing pharmacists. Methods

Study design and questionnaire generation

This cross-sectional study included pharmacy students and registered pharmacists in Jordan. The draft questionnaire was developed by academic members from the Faculty of Pharmaceutical Sciences at Hashemite University and the School of Medicine at Al-Balqa Applied University, focusing on direct, comprehensive, and informative items about DS, including causes, treatments, pharmacists’ roles, and available community services. A group of pharmacology and clinical pharmacy specialists evaluated the content validity, clarity, and layout of the questionnaire. A web-based pilot study with 20 participants informed revisions, though responses were excluded from the final analysis. The questionnaire began with an introductory statement outlining the study objectives, confidentiality, anonymization, and the expected completion time. It consisted of 72 closed-ended and multiple-choice questions across five sections, created using Microsoft Forms.

Evaluation of content validity

Content validity was assessed using Lawshe’s Content Validity Ratio (CVR) at the item level and the Content Validity Index (CVI) at the section level [41–43]. A panel of 7 experts independently rated each item for necessity (essential, useful but not essential, or not essential). The panel comprised 2 disease-area specialists, 2 academic researchers, 1 pharmaceutical education specialist, and 2 senior community pharmacists.

Item-level CVR was calculated as CVR = (n_e - N/2) / (N/2), where n_e is the number of experts rating the item as “essential” and N is the total number of experts (N = 7). Items were retained if CVR exceeded the critical value for N = 7 at α = 0.05 (one-tailed test, based on exact binomial probabilities as per revised Tables [41, 42]), typically requiring at least 6 experts to rate the item essential (CVR ≥ 0.714–0.75; we used a conservative threshold of ≥ 0.741 to ensure robustness). All items met or exceeded this threshold and were retained.

Section-level CVI (S-CVI/Ave) was calculated as the mean of item-level CVIs (I-CVI = proportion of experts rating the item relevant/essential). The following section-level CVI values were obtained: (1) Knowledge of the causes, prevalence, symptoms, and complications of DS (7 items, 0.837); (2) Knowledge of DS prevention (3 items, 0.905); (3) Pharmacists’ knowledge of medication/infant formula intake by DS patients (5 items, 0.828); (4) Participants’ clinical knowledge of DS health problems and treatment (7 items, 0.755); (5) Participants’ practice regarding the role of community pharmacists for patients with DS (14 items, 0.796); (6) Participants’ practice regarding medication dispensing for DS (13 items, 0.959); (7) Participants’ confidence and training needs in handling medications for DS (4 items, 0.857); and (8) Participants’ practice regarding DS information resources and training needs (6 items, 0.905).

The overall section-level CVI (S-CVI/Ave) was 0.855, exceeding the commonly accepted threshold of ≥ 0.80 for good content validity.

Following the ethical approval of the study protocol and the questionnaire (both Arabic and English) on 6th Sep 2023, we utilized the questionnaire link to collect responses in the current study via Facebook pages for student communities and WhatsApp-restricted pharmacists’ groups in Jordan from September 20, 2023, to January 24, 2025. The link was also distributed to students via their study groups on Microsoft Teams®. The response rate was calculated by dividing the number of completed and eligible responses by the total number of individuals who received the survey and multiplying by 100.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated based on the total number of registered pharmacists listed by the Jordan Pharmaceutical Association (JPA) and the available pharmacy students enrolled in pharmacy colleges in Jordan (total population ≈ 52,000) [44]. This method allowed for a focused and engaged group of respondents, enhancing the reliability and richness of the data collected [45, 46]. A novel non-probability “voluntary sampling” design suggested by Murairwa was employed to ensure the collection of high-quality and dependable data [45]. The final sample comprised eligible individuals who voluntarily agreed to participate (N = 402) including 276 pharmacy students and 126 registered pharmacists in this cross-sectional survey [46].

Statistical analysis

All variables were summarized using frequencies (%) for categorical data and means ± standard deviations for continuous data, as appropriate. Data were cleaned, validated, and coded prior to analysis, and analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (v25). Knowledge was operationalized as composite scores (domain scores and a total score) derived by coding each response on point scale (+ 2, + 1, 0, − 1, −2; with reverse coding where applicable) and summing item scores within domains and overall. The assessed domains were Knowledge of the causes, prevalence, symptoms and complications (− 14 to 14), Knowledge of DS Prevention (− 6 to 6), knowledge of medication /infant formula intake (− 10 to 10), clinical knowledge of DS health problems and treatment (− 14 to 14), and Total Knowledge Score (− 44 to 44). Accordingly, the dependent variables were summed knowledge scores (or knowledge domains), treated as approximately continuous for group comparisons, consistent with evidence supporting parametric analyses of multi-item composite scales. Group differences in knowledge scores were examined using independent-samples t-tests (two groups) or one-way ANOVA (> 2 groups). The null hypothesis for each comparison was that the mean composite knowledge score did not differ across the relevant categories, as detailed in the corresponding table and figure footnotes. Assumptions were evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test (normality). A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d. Associations between knowledge scores domains were assessed using Pearson’s correlation (r), interpreted as weak (0.10–0.29), moderate (0.30–0.49), and strong (≥ 0.50) [47, 48].

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of participants

In this study, 402 responses were eligible for analysis (response rate was 73%). As seen in Fig. 1, the majority of the respondents (73.6%) were female, while 26.4% were male. This gender distribution likely reflects the predominance of female enrollment in pharmacy programs in Jordan; a trend observed in recent years among pharmacy students and practicing pharmacists [46]. One-third of participants (33.8%) lived in the capital Amman, followed by those living outside Amman in a city (15.4%), and the rest were in towns. Almost two-thirds (68.6%) of the respondents were students, while one-third (31.3%) were pharmacists, categorized as follows: 24.9% held a Bachelor of Pharmacy degree, 4.5% had a postgraduate qualification, and 2.0% held a PharmD. Regarding the students’ category, the majority of respondents were either fourth and fifth year (43 and 14.2%, respectively), with relatively fewer early-year students participating. Among the students’ category, more than two-thirds (66.2%) of respondents were from public (governmental) universities, 2.5% were from private universities, and the rest (31.3%) were unspecified. The employees’ working experience ranged from less than 2 years (23.9%) to those with 2–5 years of experience (16%). Among the employed pharmacists, 16.7% work in the capital, Amman, followed by the central province (7.5%), and the rest are distributed across other provinces (data not shown).

On the other hand, consistent with the correct response pattern, around 70% of participants agree (50.5%) and strongly agree (20.4%) about DS complications manifested by delayed speech, intellectual disabilities, growth delays, and endocrine issues,

When considering DS preventive measures, the majority of participants (48.5% agree or 27.1% strongly agree) correctly indicated that advanced maternal age is a major risk factor for DS. In addition, most participants (45.8% agree and 25.6% strongly agree) agree that early detection of DS during pregnancy will help families to be involved in early healthcare provider consultation. Further, around half of the participants agreed (49.5%) that older adults suffering from DS may develop additional disorders and may require extra treatment, with agreement being the correct answer to this statement. Further, one-third of the respondents (34.6%) were uncertain about the importance of dental care and obesity prevention compared to 27.1% who agreed and 7.5% who strongly agreed, with agreement being the correct answer to this statement.

Participants’ clinical knowledge of DS health problems and treatment

Regarding respondents’ familiarity with the presence of several DS types, 32.8% were moderately familiar, 14.7% were very familiar, and 25.9% of respondents were extremely familiar, compared to only 6.5% who were not familiar at all (Table 2). A noticeable familiarity among respondents about DS - associated health problems (e.g., congenital heart disease, thyroid disorders) was observed where more than two-thirds were familiar (26.9% moderately familiar, 23.4% very familiar, and 29.9% extremely familiar). Likewise, susceptibility to infections due to immune disorders and the frequency of common medical disorders (e.g., epilepsy, Alzheimer’s) show strong recognition among more than two-thirds of participants with varying degrees of familiarity. Regarding medication intake for DS-related problems like stimulants, thyroid hormone, and anticonvulsants and their side effects, more than two-thirds of the participants were familiar with varying degrees. When comparing treatment administered to DS and non-DS patients, varying degrees of familiarity were observed, ranging from 25.6% who were not familiar or slightly familiar (25.6% and 27.1%, respectively) compared to 31.3% participants who were moderately familiar, 4.0% very familiar and 11.9% who were extremely familiar.

Participants’ practice regarding enhancing the role of community pharmacists as healthcare providers for patients with DS

Table 3 shows that personal experience with DS individuals is notably low, with 75.4% lacking this direct exposure and only 24.6% affirming such interaction. Considering the inclusion of DS-related topics in the university curriculum, a variable degree of familiarity ranging from 68.7% reported receiving information about DS to 31.3% who did not, suggesting inconsistencies in university curricula. 78.6% of the respondents believe the pharmacy curriculum and scientific research on DS are insufficient, and only 21.4% find them adequate. Furthermore, participation in continuing education programs on DS is strikingly low at 8%, with 92% not engaging. Even though 72.6% did not receive inquiries about DS medications, 79.9% have reported that medication inserts, an accessible source of information for practicing pharmacists or trainees, lack special instructions related to DS.

Participants’ practice regarding medications dispensing for DS

Considering the study participants familiarity with inquiries related to drug dispensing for DS patients and /or their caregivers, a varied pattern of response was observed (Fig. 2). Since pharmacy students carry out dispensing under the supervision of their training pharmacists, questions regarding dispensing by students reflect the structured training process. For example, among prescription drugs, anticonvulsants were dispensed 6 or more times by 12.7% of participants, 3–5 times by 40.8%, and 29.6% were unaware of their use by DS patients, indicating limited experience with this category. Sleeping medications follow a similar trend, with 14.2% of participants reporting regular dispensing alongside 40.3% moderate dispensing compared to high uncertainty (28.4%). On the other hand, cardiovascular drugs show a slightly higher regular dispensing rate at 24.4% to 36.6% dispense moderately, and 25.1% were uncertain. When considering the dispensing of dietary supplements for DS patients, the frequencies were similar, ranging from 34.1% regularly dispensing of folic acid, followed closely by multivitamins (33.8%), vitamin E/antioxidants (31.3%), minerals (30.8%), to amino acids (30.6%). Uncertainty regarding dietary supplements with “I do not know” responses remain present but lower than with prescription drugs, ranging from 23.9% (multivitamins) to 24.9% (folic acid). This higher frequency of dispensing supplements compared to prescription drugs complies with the widespread belief in their general safety [48]. Replacement therapies present a mixed picture, with dispensing frequency varying by type. Growth hormone is noted, with 31.8% regular dispensing, 28.9% moderately dispensing compared to 28.1% uncertain. Thyroid hormones follow, with 25.9% dispensing them regular frequency, 35.8% at a moderate level, and 29.4% expressing uncertainty, indicating broader but not widespread familiarity. Sex hormone therapy, however, receives the lowest overall consideration, with only 14.4% regularly dispensing it, 27.6% moderately dispensing, 20.4% rarely dispensing, and a high 37.6% expressing uncertainty—suggesting it is less commonly utilized or understood in DS conditions.

Participants’ practice on dispensing prescription and OTC products for DS

Participants’ confidence and training needs in managing medications for DS

The study results reflect a varied level of confidence among participants regarding the ease of counseling about DS medications and their ability to manage DS treatment and provide advice (Table 4). On advising parents about medications and side effects, only 16.2% agreed, and 6.7% strongly agreed they felt confident. In comparison, more than one-third disagreed (22.9% and 11.2% disagreed or strongly disagreed, respectively), and 43.0% were uncertain. Similarly, when considering dispensing medications for DS with complete comfort, only 17.6% felt at ease (13.4% agree, 4.2% strongly agree), whereas 40.1% expressed discomfort (27.4% disagree, 12.7% strongly disagree), while 42.3% remained uncertain. This suggests a lack of confidence among many respondents in handling DS-related counseling. As expected, there was a strong desire (agreed (35.8%) or strongly agreed (30.3%)) for continued education/training in DS related topics compared to only 6.9% disagreeing or strongly disagreeing and 26.9% uncertain. This interest in training programs involving direct interaction for pharmaceutical counseling was seen in 67.7% of the respondents (35.6%and 32.1%) agreed and strongly agreed, respectively) with just 9% disagreeing or strongly disagreeing and 23.4% uncertain.

On the other hand, employees scored significantly higher scores (p = 0.031) regarding clinical knowledge of DS health problems and treatment, as shown in Fig. 3(B). While overall knowledge gaps were evident, practicing pharmacists demonstrated modestly higher clinical knowledge compared to students, likely due to greater exposure to patient care.

No significant differences in knowledge scores between participants who have and have not participated in continuing education programs on DS (p > 0.05 across all domains). This study highlights gaps in knowledge and confidence regarding DS pharmacotherapy among both pharmacy students and practicing pharmacists in Jordan, with students comprising the majority of respondents (68.6%). These findings may partly reflect incomplete curriculum coverage at students’ current academic stage, while practicing pharmacists showed modestly higher clinical knowledge (p = 0.031), underscoring experiential learning. Updating curricula and implementing mandatory continuing education remain essential to address these gaps and strengthen the role of community pharmacists in DS care.

No significant differences were observed in the remaining domains (p > 0.05). Overall, these findings suggest that consultation-related exposure may be associated with improved DS-related knowledge, especially for causes and clinical features, although the magnitude of the differences is limited.

Discussion

The role of pharmacists in handling DS-related issues can help in disease management through successful patient and caregiver counseling [49–51]. Pharmacists have ethical duties toward patients, and more specifically toward those with physical and intellectual disabilities [51–54]. They ensure safe medication use, communicate clearly to respond to medication queries, and advocate for equitable care. Pharmacists address gaps in conditions such as DS and respect patient dignity. They regularly update skills and adapt counseling to each patient’s cognitive needs. By following international and local ethical standards, pharmacists build trust, improve health, and promote equity for people with intellectual disabilities [44, 53, 54]. Study results showed that participants’ knowledge varies between accurate knowledge in understanding common DS characteristics and less certainty on specific aspects of DS like frequency and curability (Tables 1 and 2). The awareness of advanced maternal age significance (≥ 35 years) as a risk factor, the complications associated with DS, and the importance of early detection are also observed in the study results (Table 1) [30, 51, 52]. Variations in knowledge and awareness among respondents concerning the treatment and care of DS patients are evident (Tables 1, 2 and 3). More specifically, moderate to strong familiarity exists for health conditions associated with DS-related illnesses and their prevalence (Tables 1 and 2). The significance of dental care, obesity prevention, medication dosage modifications, and nutritional guidance for DS patients is evident in participants’ responses (Table 1). The uncertainty in responses indicates a lack of confidence or knowledge on specific factors of managing DS-related health issues, including clinical trials investigating fetal therapies and nutritional aspects (Tables 1 and 2). Regarding medication dispensing for DS patients, most respondents showed knowledge gaps in specific areas about medication use in DS (Fig. 2 and Table 4). More than half of the participants stressed the need for dosage adjustment in individuals with DS with careful evaluation for elderly patients with DS (Tables 1 and 2). A notable gap was identified in understanding whether DS treatment is like non-DS patients, highlighting an area for educational involvement (Table 2). The study results also stress the need to re-evaluate medication package leaflet, particularly concerning dose adjustments for individuals with DS (Table 3). A special emphasis should be placed on dispensing and counseling about DS-related medications, supplements, and hormone replacement therapies (Table 2; and Fig. 2). The study findings emphasize participants’ demand for a revised university curriculum and continuous training. In particular, the inadequacy of formal instructions in the pharmacy curriculum about dietary supplements suitable for DS was highlighted (Tables 3 and 6 and Fig. 2). On the other hand, the study underscores a gradient of experience: supplements are dispensed most confidently and frequently, followed by select replacement therapies, while prescription drugs lag, reflecting potential gaps in training or exposure specific to DS pharmacotherapy (Fig. 2). The significance of various dietary supplements—including antioxidants, choline, herbs, enzymes, minerals, and vitamins—in supporting cognitive and health outcomes in DS should be highlighted in relevant pharmacy course [15, 31–35].

In addition, the scoring results show a strong agreement among participants regarding collaboration between different healthcare professionals (Table 3). The study results underlined the effective use of social media to enhance their support for patients with DS. Furthermore, collaboration between the public health sector and community organizations is critical in delivering satisfactory care to individuals with DS (Table 3). These high agreement rates -consistently above 90% reflect a strong consensus that both educational reforms and multi-stakeholder involvement are critical to enhancing pharmacists’ capabilities and confidence in supporting DS patients, addressing the deficiencies highlighted in their current training and experience (Fig. 3 and Table 6). These findings reflect a broad recognition among respondents of existing knowledge gaps and a proactive interest in professional development. In particular, hands-on training is strongly supported to address the uncertainties noted earlier regarding advising on prescribed medications for DS patients (Table 4). While respondents exhibit a solid foundation in certain DS-related domains, significant gaps, especially in emerging research and localized support systems, could be effectively addressed through the training initiatives they overwhelmingly endorse (Table 4).

Finally, the correlation analysis across knowledge domains reveals that Prevention knowledge has the strongest influence on Total knowledge, indicating that a solid understanding of protective measures is closely linked to better comprehensive knowledge (Tables 6 and 7 and Fig. 4). Interestingly, participants who demonstrate greater knowledge of Causes and Prevalence tend to have lower scores in Prevention and Treatment-related areas, and this subdomain shows a weak association with the total knowledge score. In contrast, knowledge in the Treatment domain contributes moderately to the overall score. Understanding Clinical Causes, Symptoms, and Treatment emerges as the most robust predictor of total knowledge among all domains. The negative correlation between Causes and Prevalence and other knowledge areas may reflect an imbalance in learning or suggest the need to re-categorize and better integrate teaching topics. Notably, Prevention and Treatment knowledge are moderately and positively correlated with each other and the total score underscoring their collective importance in achieving well-rounded competency (Fig. 4).

This study found that knowledge, confidence, and practice levels are similar to those in other KAP studies in pharmacy and healthcare [42, 54]. Rabbani et al. found that healthcare students often lack knowledge and show varied attitudes toward DS. They recommended updating curricula and providing targeted training [42]. A similar study conducted in Rwanda, healthcare providers had limited to moderate knowledge about children with DS, showing the need to strengthen knowledge [54]. Other KAP studies found that pharmacists dealing with chronic conditions and disabilities have moderate knowledge, positive attitudes, but limited confidence in practicing their duties [10, 45, 52, 55–57]. These findings show that pharmacists and students recognize their role in supporting patients with disabilities but often lack adequate education and training. Curriculum committees should add condition-specific modules to pharmacy education, and professional bodies should improve CPD programs on DS. These actions will address the knowledge, confidence, and practice gaps in healthcare.

This study underscores a clear opportunity to empower community pharmacists by addressing the lack of confidence and knowledge in managing pharmacotherapy for individuals with DS. To bridge the identified gaps in knowledge and practice, we recommend the following actions:

Revise pharmacy curricula to build student confidence by addressing key concerns in prescribing and counseling for individuals with DS [57] (see Fig. 5).

Pharmacy curricula can address these gaps by integrating DS-specific pharmacotherapy content into undergraduate training, covering areas such as comorbidities, medication dose adjustments, patient counseling, and caregiver support. Embedding DS case studies, simulations, and interprofessional learning modules would build both knowledge and confidence early in students’ education.

Continuous Professional Development (CPD) programs can complement this by providing ongoing, practice-oriented training for licensed pharmacists, with emphasis on emerging evidence, individualized care, and communication strategies tailored to patients with DS and their caregivers. CPD activities, whether through workshops, accredited online modules, or hands-on training—ensure that practicing pharmacists remain up to date with evolving clinical guidelines and best practices.

Updating the pharmacy curriculum, along with implementing CPD strategies, prepares pharmacists to provide patient-centered care for DS patients [53, 57]. Accordingly, they will deliver equitable and ethical care without any discrimination, utilizing current medical knowledge, adjusting doses as needed, and employing personalized communication strategies [54]. The engagements of pharmacists in continuous learning courses are dedicated to handling the complex medication needs of individuals with disabilities like DS work in partnership with caregivers and healthcare professionals and extend their role beyond dispensing to promote health equity, enhance quality of life, and foster social inclusion [54–56].

Future work will recruit a larger sample of practicing pharmacists to assess their experiences and challenges in addressing DS-related concerns, and to examine the relationship between knowledge and practices in detail. Additionally, another study will involve parents and caregivers of individuals with DS from all provinces of Jordan. This study aims to generate a deeper insight into the needs expected from pharmacist services, as well as to strengthen communication between pharmacists and caregivers. Ultimately, these steps will facilitate more effective and individualized healthcare for individuals with DS.

Study limitations

A notable limitation of the study is the predominance of pharmacy students (68.6%), whose responses may reflect academic knowledge rather than established practice, thereby limiting generalizability to community pharmacists in Jordan. However, the observed association between receiving DS-related inquiries and higher knowledge in certain domains (Table 7) suggests that experiential exposure, more common among practicing pharmacists, may enhance knowledge levels. Future research intends to recruit a larger and more diverse sample of practicing pharmacists to strengthen generalizability and external validity. Additionally, studies based on cross-sectional design do not allow for the determination of causality. Furthermore, there may be a sampling bias toward younger pharmacists who are more active on social media and more likely to engage with online questionnaires. However, the high response rate (73%) mitigates this concern, indicating a strong interest among respondents in participating in the study. Further, an anonymous online survey accompanied by participation from all Jordanian provinces ensures representative findings, as demonstrated by consistent patterns across the results. These findings highlight the essential need to implement the study’s recommendations within the practice of community pharmacists across Jordan.

Conclusion

This study assessed various aspects of pharmacy education and professional exposure related to DS, including the integration of DS-specific content within the pharmacy curriculum, the perceived adequacy of academic preparation and research opportunities, engagement in continuing education programs, and the frequency of patient inquiries regarding prescription and over-the-counter medications for individuals with DS. As the majority of respondents were pharmacy students, the findings reflect perspectives from both students and practicing pharmacists, offering insights across different stages of professional development, while interpretations specific to community pharmacists should be considered within this context. Overall, the findings revealed a limited familiarity with DS among respondents and a noticeable lack of confidence in addressing the medication-related needs of this population. Educational updates and ongoing professional development programs are essential to overcome these insufficiencies. Pharmacy students and practicing pharmacists would benefit from enhanced training emphasizing DS pharmacotherapy, individualized treatment planning, and appropriate medication dosing adjustments. Furthermore, health authorities should prioritize pharmacy training programs to meet the medical needs of individuals with DS more effectively.
