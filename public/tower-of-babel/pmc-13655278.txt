Subsequent Knee Arthroplasty Does Not Influence Patient-Reported Outcomes Following Lumbar Fusion
Authors: Joshua Mathew, Roy Y. Miloh, Mitchell Ng, Emily Berthiaume, Yulia Lee, Gregorio Baek, William Green, Yasmine Eichbaum, Morgan Hitchner, Steven J. Banko, Jonathan Dalton, Dipti Venkatesh, Vinay Bijoor, Paul Gunter, Gus Duininck, Andrew Vanichkachorn, Brandon Martinazzi, Ali Farooqi, Alec Giakas, Jose A. Canseco, Alan S. Hilibrand, Alexander R. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Study DesignRetrospective Cohort Study.ObjectivesThe impact of knee arthritis and early total knee arthroplasty (TKA) on lumbar fusion recovery remains underexplored. This study aimed to identify demographic differences between lumbar fusion patients that had subsequent TKA and patients with OA without TKA, assess perioperative differences; and compare patient-reported outcomes (PROMs).MethodsRetrospective review was performed at one tertiary care center (2016–2025). Patients undergoing lumbar fusion with concurrent knee osteoarthritis and had 1-year Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) scores were included. Patients with prior joint arthroplasty or concomitant hip arthritis were excluded. Kellgren-Lawrence grading (KL) was collected via chart review and radiographs. Cohorts were stratified by future arthroplasty status and compared using bivariate analysis, with P set at <0.05.Results148 patients were included (OA-104; TKA-44). Overall, there were no significant demographic differences between groups (Age: 64.9 years; 54.1% female). Perioperatively, patients who eventually underwent TKA had higher KL grading at the time of surgery (Grade 4: 56.8% vs 20.2%; P < 0.001). The average time to TKA from lumbar fusion was 679 days. Preoperative ODI was not significantly different between groups (42.9 vs 46.8; P = 0.174). Likewise, at 12 months, postoperative ODI (21.9 vs 27.2; P = 0.137) and improvement from baseline (−20.96 vs −19.61; P = 0.730) were similar between groups.ConclusionThese findings suggest knee osteoarthritis and future TKA should not be considered a contraindication for lumbar fusion and may aid in counseling and expectation management following lumbar fusion in the presence of knee osteoarthritis.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is one of the most common degenerative diseases in an aging population. Knee OA is a relatively common and prevalent OA type, with certain studies showing that radiographic knee OA was present in 37% of all patients aged 60 or older. 1 Primary knee OA occurs very commonly in adjunction with degenerative lumbar spinal disease, with large population studies reporting that up to 67% of primary knee OA patients may report back pain issues. 2 The baseline pathophysiology of these conditions substantially overlap through degenerative processes consisting of cartilage destruction, extracellular matrix degeneration and osteophyte formation. 3 Both conditions contribute to pain, disability, and reduced quality of life. As the population continues to age, a growing proportion of patients present with concomitant degenerative lumbar disease and knee OA, creating a challenging clinical scenario: which procedures should take priority, and does the sequencing of these procedures affect outcomes?

Lumbar fusion is an established surgical intervention for degenerative spinal disease. Lumbar fusion has shown the ability to help patients restore function, especially in the lower extremity when nerves are compressed.4,5 Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is commonly performed to treat symptomatic knee OA unresponsive to nonoperative management to decrease pain and improve ambulation/function. 6 As patients are recovering from lumbar fusion, early and continuous ambulation has been shown to play a role in improving long term recovery. 7 This raises several questions regarding the role of TKA in lumbar fusion outcomes, as well as timing of TKA in lumbar fusion outcomes in patients with severe knee OA.

To date, existing literature is limited that evaluates patients with progressive knee OA requiring TKA following lumbar fusion surgery. Several studies have looked at the effect of prior TKA on lumbar fusion outcomes, but no studies to date have looked at TKA that has followed fusion procedures.8,9 To this end, it is important to provide more data for surgeons to help guide through these situations. Therefore, the aim of this study was to: 1) identify demographic differences between patients that eventually required TKA and patients with knee OA that did not eventually undergo surgery; 2) assess perioperative differences between those cohorts; and 3) compare patient-reported outcomes (PROMs). We hypothesized that patients that eventually underwent TKA following lumbar fusion would have worse PROMs and decreased function compared to the group that did not require TKA.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

Following Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval, a retrospective review was conducted at a single tertiary center from 2016-2025. A structured query language (SQL) was used to identify patients with a diagnosis of knee osteoarthritis undergoing lumbar fusion. Patients were included if they had completed 1-year Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) scores. Patients were excluded if they had a diagnosis of osteoarthritis in the hip at the time of surgery. Patients were also excluded if they had undergone a TKA or THA prior to spine surgery. An SQL search was used to collect patient demographics such as age, sex, race, and body mass index (BMI), and was confirmed with manual chart review. Knee osteoarthritis grading was obtained from radiology reports and clinic visits. Manual radiographic grading was done using the Kellgren-Lawrence system (KL) when a report was unavailable. KL grading was collected preoperatively to spine surgery for both cohorts.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome of interest for this study was PROMs, specifically differences in 12-month ODI scores between both groups. PROMs were collected preoperatively, and at 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. Changes between preoperative values and each postoperative timepoint were calculated. Secondary PROMs included Visual Analog Scale (VAS) back and leg. Secondary outcomes included number of revision surgeries and time to revision surgery between groups.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to define baseline demographics and clinical variables between patients who underwent TKA and those who did not. Patient demographics, clinical variables, and PROMs were reported as means with standard deviations. Categorical variables were compared using Chi-squared tests. Meanwhile, continuous variables were compared using t-tests.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of 148 patients that underwent lumbar fusion, subsequent TKA surgery following fusion was not associated with increased revision surgeries or decreased time to revision surgery. Additionally, patients that had subsequent TKAs had comparable PROMs scores, including similar improvements from baseline. Overall, our findings suggest that severe knee osteoarthritis and future TKA should not be considered a contraindication for lumbar fusion and may aid in patient counseling and expectation management following lumbar fusion in the presence of knee osteoarthritis.

Regarding our aim on identifying demographic differences between post lumbar fusion patients with OA and those that underwent TKA, we found no significant differences in age, race, or sex. There were also no differences in comorbidities in the patients that underwent TKA, including diabetes, smoking, osteoporosis, or Charlson comorbidity index. Our findings in this population vary significantly from the classic demographic of patients with OA that undergo TKA, which typically are older, are diabetic, and have higher Charlson comorbidity indexes.10,11 Our second aim was to assess perioperative differences in our lumbar fusion cohorts between patients with eventual TKAs and patients that did not undergo TKA. Our study found comparable perioperative differences including revision rates and time to revision. Our third aim was to compare PROMS between these cohorts and saw that while patients that eventually underwent TKA had higher KL grading, their 12-month ODI were comparable to patients with lower KL grade that did not undergo eventual TKA.

Spine surgeons frequently encounter patients with severe concomitant knee OA, and there is real concern in practice that progressive knee disease will limit ambulation, impair fusion recovery, and ultimately diminish the benefits of spinal surgery. Evidence has shown that lumbar fusion outcomes are improved as patients ambulate more. 7 Many times, patients with more severe degenerative disc disease will also have more severe knee OA. One large retrospective study found that OA burden was significantly and independently associated with intervertebral disc degeneration in patients with degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis. 12 Therefore, the populations that present for spine surgery consultations often have concomitant OA burden, elevating the importance of understanding the relationship between TKA and lumbar fusion in this population.

It is important to assess current literature that has already been done regarding the relationship between these two operations. Studies have looked out the outcomes of TKA on lumbar fusion patients with prior TKA, but few to no studies have viewed outcomes of patients that eventually require TKA after lumbar fusion. Kim et al looked at a cohort of 72 patients that underwent elective lumbar surgery and found that TKA before lumbar fusion resulted in significantly more improvement in ODI and leg VAS compared to the group without preoperative TKA. 8 One potential mechanism is related to improvements in biomechanics after a TKA, improving sagittal spinopelvic alignment such as lumbar lordosis, pelvic tilt, and sacral slope. This in turn facilitated better correction during subsequent spinal fusion. In our cohort, lumbar fusion was performed prior to TKA, meaning that spinal alignment had already been surgically addressed at the time of follow-up. The biomechanical benefit of TKA on spinopelvic parameters, as described by Kim et al, may therefore be less relevant when TKA follows fusion, as the spine has already undergone surgical stabilization. On the other hand, Shichman et al looked at 113 TKA patients of which 19 had prior lumbar fusion, and found significant pre-to-postoperative changes in pelvic tilt (ΔPT = −8.6°, P = 0.018) and sacral slope (ΔSS = 8.6°, P = 0.037) in the previous fusion subgroup. 13 Importantly, however, Shichman et al did not evaluate postoperative PROMs or functional status, leaving a critical gap in understanding whether these radiographic changes translate into meaningful clinical benefit for the patient. Despite measurable spinopelvic changes demonstrated by Shichman and significantly more baseline knee OA, patients that went on to TKA had equivalent spine-specific functional recoveries compared to those that did not eventually undergo TKA. This finding suggests the spine surgical benefit was durable. Functional gains from lumbar fusion were preserved even when the knee continued to deteriorate enough to require replacement nearly two years later on average. The critical clinical message is spine surgeons can reassure patients that proceeding with TKA after lumbar fusion will not undermine their spinal outcomes, and that the functional gains from fusion are durable even in the setting of subsequent knee replacement.

Spine surgeons must be considerate of the conversations they must have with their patients that present to clinic for lumbar fusion evaluation with concomitant severe knee OA. If the patient’s main concern is their spine, they may also ask if they need to undergo immediate surgery for their knees. It is important to mention that coexisting lumbar back pain with OA has been shown to have worse pain, quality of life, and outcomes. 14 This study helps to provide quantitative data to help guide improved patient education and expectations that a spine surgeon can communicate to patients with multiple coexisting degenerative diseases. The optimal sequencing remains debated and likely depends on which disease is most symptomatic as well as the relative severity of each condition. Shared decision-making between spine and arthroplasty surgeons is recommended, with thorough evaluation of both regions before proceeding with either intervention.

This study has several potential limitations that should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, its retrospective single-center design has inherent selection bias, and findings may not be generalizable to other practice settings or patient populations. The TKA cohort was relatively small (n = 44), which may limit statistical power to detect modest differences in PROMs between the two groups. Follow up of the PROMs was also limited to 12 months following lumbar fusion. Longer term outcomes over time should be followed in future studies as these diseases are chronic. The OA group that did not undergo TKA represents a heterogenous population, as some patients may have had mild disease not yet requiring surgery, while others may have had barriers to TKA. These differences were analyzed via demographics that showed no significant differences, however it is important to acknowledge this can still be a limitation and should require further research in this field. Finally, radiographic grading was performed through manual chart review when formal radiology reports were unavailable, which introduces potential variability in KL grading.

Future studies should build on these findings in several important ways. Prospective, multicenter studies with larger cohorts and longer follow up periods are needed in order to fully validate the results and further assess the durability of lumbar fusion outcomes longer term in patients that eventually undergo subsequent TKA. Future work could incorporate spine specific and knee specific PROMs, such as the KOOS or Oxford Knee score, in order to capture the full functional trajectory of managing both conditions when the present together. Investigating the optimal timing of TKA following lumbar fusion is another important question, as the average interval of 679 days in our cohort may not reflect the ideal window of subsequent surgery.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort study, patients that had subsequent TKA following lumbar fusion was not associated with worse spine-specific PROMS at 12 months. Despite having significantly more severe knee OA at baseline verified with KL Grade, patients who eventually underwent TKA achieved equivalent ODI and VAS improvements compared to those that did not. These findings address a gap in the literature by demonstrating that the functional gains of lumbar fusion are durable even as knee disease progresses to the point of requiring arthroplasty nearly two years later. Knee osteoarthritis, regardless of severity, should not be considered a contraindication to lumbar fusion, nor should the anticipated need for future TKA prompt surgeons or patients to defer necessary spinal surgery. As the aging population drives a growing prevalence of concomitant lumbar and knee disease, our findings provide a meaningful foundation for evidence-based patient counseling and shared decision-making between spine and arthroplasty surgeons.
