Overview of allergic disease: Pathogenesis of allergic inflammation – WAO White Book on Allergy 2026 – 2.1☆
Authors: Luis Caraballo, Kevin Llinás-Caballero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAllergic inflammation underlies several diseases such as asthma, rhinitis, and atopic dermatitis). It involves a great array of cells and molecules of innate and adaptive immunity. Components of the innate inflammatory pathways, among them epithelium receptors, alarmins and ILC2, are the earliest participants in some inflammatory processes, mainly the house dust mite induced bronchial inflammation in asthma.The importance of innate mechanisms, not only as a necessary step for inducing adaptive Th2 response but also to maintain chronic inflammation, is being increasingly recognized in recent years. In addition, the specificity of the Th2 cells and IgE is not only a crucial component of allergic inflammation but allows also identification of the causal inducers and their effects on eosinophils, neutrophils, and basophils recruitment and activation. The regulatory mechanisms of the allergic responses include Treg and Breg activity but the most important action to control this process is stopping the specific allergen exposure. However, this is very difficult to achieve in asthma because, in general, not all the allergens involved can be identified; in addition, non-immune pathways play also a role. Since most people exposed to proallergic environments do not develop allergic diseases and it is well known that these conditions run in families, their genetic and epigenetic influences are a matter of intensive research. In this review we make an update of the basic mechanisms of allergic inflammation, trying to identify important pathways that involve both innate and adaptive immunity, including environmental factors that can modify important regulators of immunity such as intestinal microbiota and air pollution.Keywords: Asthma, Type-2 immunity, Allergy, House dust mite, Blomia tropicalis, Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus, IgE, ILC2, Inflammation

Introduction

Our knowledge of the pathogenesis of allergic diseases (such as asthma, rhinitis, atopic dermatitis) and allergic reactions (eg, to insect stings, or food or drug ingestion) is increasing; however, crucial aspects of the origin, risk factors, and natural history of these conditions remain to be defined. Experimental and clinical evidence supports the presence of multiple mechanisms involved in the origin and evolution of these complex, multifactorial diseases. These mechanisms include the molecular and cellular basis of the allergic responses, their genetic and epigenetic influences, the role of allergen exposure and air pollution, the effects of diet and microbiota composition, and other factors. The integration of experimental, clinical, and epidemiological findings has resulted in various hypotheses about the pathogenesis and pathophysiology of allergic diseases and allergic inflammation. This chapter is based on the general concept that the environment acts on the genetically susceptible population to induce allergic response, inflammation, and clinical symptoms.

The allergic response

All allergic diseases have in common a predominantly type-2 immune response to allergens; however, the relative role of this response, and particularly that of specific IgE, varies from being central in anaphylaxis to being only part of more complex pathways, as in asthma or atopic dermatitis. The reactivity to allergens involves both the innate and adaptive branches of immunity (Fig. 1), based mainly on specific IgE and type-2 cytokines, such as IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13. A specific IgE primary response to allergens is produced through the adaptive pathways of immunity. After sensitization, the re-exposure to allergens initiates the IgE-dependent inflammatory cascade, which involves additional cells and cytokines that actively participate in the inflammatory reaction.

Innate immune mechanisms contribute to HDM-induced allergic inflammation. House dust mites affect the integrity of the airway epithelium, which in turn releases alarmins (IL-25, IL-33, and TSLP) that activate ILC2s to produce type-2 cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13). ILC2-derived IL-13 further contributes to the disruption of the airway epithelial barrier, maintaining a vicious cycle between the damaged epithelium and the activated immune system.1 Additionally, HDM stimulate the secretion of VEGF (known for its pro-angiogenic and vasodilating properties) in the airways and can activate pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) such as TLR4, SAA1, and dectins (eg, dectin-2) associated with or secreted by epithelial and/or immune cells, resulting in type-2 responses. TLR4 activation upregulates the expression of some microRNAs, including miR-16, miR-21, and miR-126. Immune cells exposed to HDM experience eicosanoid reprogramming modifying their production pattern of arachidonic acid metabolites (eg, dectin-2-mediated enhancement of cysteinyl leukotriene production by dendritic cells and mast cells). Moreover, exposure to HDM induces changes in the DNA methylation pattern of B cells and airway cells, including airway smooth muscle cells. IL-, interleukin; ILC2, group 2 innate lymphoid cell; miRNA, microRNA; PRR, pattern recognition receptor; SAA1, serum amyloid A1; TLR4, Toll-like receptor 4; TSLP, thymic stromal lymphopoietin; VEGF, vascular endothelial growth factor.

Traditionally, specific IgE levels have been used as the biological marker of allergic sensitization, and most IgE-binding molecules are considered allergens; however, this does not mean that allergens have the same proinflammatory properties — that is, the same allergenic activity. Allergenic activity can be increased if the allergen also induces inflammation by other ways. In fact, several non-IgE-mediated inflammatory mechanisms have been reported, including the allergen's ability to bind adjuvants such as chitin or lipopolysaccharide or stimulate innate immunity via Toll-like receptors and other receptors of the bronchial epithelium. Der p 1 and Der p 2 (from the house dust mite [HDM] Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus) are good examples of molecules with high allergenic activity, probably because they stimulate both the innate and adaptive pathways of the allergic response.

It is generally accepted that allergen-induced inflammation underlies most of the processes involved in the pathophysiology of allergic diseases. Here we will take asthma as a model of allergic inflammation where the activation of diverse immune and non-immune pathways leads to bronchial hyperreactivity and clinical symptoms.

Cellular and molecular mechanisms of allergic asthma

The sensitization phase

A large number of cases of allergic asthma are induced and triggered by HDM allergens, such as those derived from D. pteronyssinus and Blomia tropicalis. The sensitization phase comprises the production of allergen-specific IgE and its binding, through high- (FceRI) and low- (FceRII) affinity IgE Fc receptors on the membranes of mast cells, basophils, eosinophils, macrophages, and other cells. This process ordinarily starts during childhood. The specificity of the IgE antibodies is controlled by the major histocompatibility complex, but other genes, such as IL4, IL5, and IL13 on chromosome 5, and probably others on different chromosomes, determine the strength of the response. The production of allergen-specific IgE is not always associated with symptoms; in some studies, around 30% of the sensitized population does not have symptoms.2,3 The reasons why these specific IgE antibodies do not induce clinical symptoms are under investigation, attracting the attention of several groups. This discrepancy highlights the conceptual difference between 2 allergens properties: allergenicity (IgE-binding capacity) and allergenic activity (ability to induce allergic inflammation).

The sensitization process implies that the allergens contact the bronchial epithelium and stimulate innate inflammatory mechanisms.4, 5, 6 Dysfunction of the epithelial barrier has been shown to play a role in the pathogenesis of allergic airway inflammation (Fig. 2). For instance, dysregulated expression of proteins involved in cell-cell adhesion and epithelial differentiation, like claudin1, plays a significant role in asthma.7 Upon damage and stress, epithelial cells produce several alarmins such as thymic stromal lymphopoietin [TSLP], IL-33, and IL-25, that act independently of and even before IgE production. Genetic variants of these alarmins have been associated with asthma, suggesting that these innate mechanisms are important in the pathogenesis of this disease. IL-25 is also constitutively produced by airway tuft cells8 (a type of epithelial chemosensory cell) and, together with the other alarmins, can activate group-2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s), which in turn supply proinflammatory type-2 cytokines that enhance IgE synthesis and the recruitment of other cells.

Epithelial dysfunction in allergic inflammation. Comparison of the traditional (A) and contemporary (B) views of epithelial dysfunction in the pathogenesis of the allergic response. In the past, allergens (eg, mites) were thought to act on a healthy epithelium at the onset of sensitization (A1). Later, re-exposure to the allergen would trigger effector mechanisms that would result in tissue damage, altering the integrity and proper function of a previously normal epithelium (A2). Currently, the notion is that some grade of epithelial dysfunction likely precedes the first encounter with the allergen and that the mechanisms of allergic sensitization might also contribute to this derangement (B1). Allergen re-exposure, and thus the effector phase, would then enhance the tissue damage, worsening the epithelial dysfunction (B2).

The effector phase

Once cells are IgE sensitized, allergen molecules cross-link membrane-bound IgE, which activates the cells and starts the release of stored proinflammatory mediators such as histamine and the synthesis of lipid mediators (arachidonic acid metabolites like leukotrienes and prostaglandins) and cytokines that will act a few hours later. This activation can also be induced by other substances capable of directly activating mast cells. Among them are some chemokines; anaphylatoxins derived from complement (C3a and C5a); and neuropeptides like substance P, somatostatin, calcitonin gene-related peptide, and vasoactive intestinal peptide. In the lung and other tissues, peripheral neurons colocalize with various types of immune cells, establishing neuroimmune crosstalk pathways that are important in allergic inflammation. Neurotransmitters (catecholamines, g-aminobutyric acid, and acetylcholine) and neuropeptides produced by neurons and neuroendocrine cells regulate the function of immune cells, the production of mucus by goblet cells, and the smooth muscle tone.9, 10, 11 One example of these neuroimmune mediators is neuromedin U (NMU), a neuron-derived peptide that activates ILC2s. Cardoso et al have shown that ILC2s selectively express neuromedin U receptor 1 (Nmur1), and mucosal neurons express NMU. Cell-autonomous activation of ILC2s with NMU resulted in immediate and strong NMUR1-dependent production of innate inflammatory and tissue repair cytokines.12 Inflammatory mediators also influence the physiology of peripheral neurons.13

The vasoactive mediators are responsible for the immediate effects, mainly vasodilation and bronchoconstriction, which occur in the first minutes after contact with the allergen. This is considered the first phase of a dual response. Chemotactic agents, including cytokines, produce a cascade of reactions that attract and involve diverse cells like neutrophils, eosinophils, macrophages, lymphocytes, and platelets to the inflammatory process. This leads to the persistence of inflammation beyond the immediate phase because it happens 2–4 h after contact and lasts for several hours. ILC3 cells (producing IL-17 and IL-22) also seem to have an important role because they are components of the IL-17/IL-22 axis, which is involved in certain asthma endotypes, especially severe asthma.14

Repeated exposure to allergens and failures of regulatory mechanisms increase and expand the cellularity of bronchial inflammation; then, starting with interactions among dendritic cells, B lymphocytes, and TH2 cells and the activation of epithelial cells, the inflammation progresses to recruit eosinophils, macrophages, and innate lymphoid cells. The importance of eosinophils for bronchial inflammation has been recognized for a long time, and additional recent information from epigenetic studies in humans has reinforced the idea that they are key cells in the process. In addition, eosinophils alter the structure and density of bronchial nerves, leading to a lack of bronchodilator responsiveness and increased irritant sensitivity.15

Chronic inflammation and bronchial remodeling

The inflammatory model induced by the type-2 response does not satisfactorily explain some histopathological changes observed in the airways during asthma. In this case, a process of chronic injury and tissue repair by mechanisms including neutrophils, eosinophils, growth factors, and metalloproteinases is also present. Some researchers believe that the bronchial obstruction that occurs in chronic asthma is secondary to remodeling, which has the histopathological features of epithelial changes, subepithelial fibrosis (thickening of the basement membrane), infiltration of inflammatory cells, hyperplasia and hypertrophy of the bronchial smooth muscle, and mucus hypersecretion. This condition could originate without the participation of the immune response, although most evidence indicates that remodeling may be caused or increased by the allergic response.

Downregulation of allergic inflammation

Among the regulatory mechanisms that control the allergic inflammation, T regulatory lymphocytes (Treg) play an essential role; indeed, regulatory deficiencies from these cells have been described as a predisposing factor for allergic diseases,16, 17, 18, 19 a hypothesis that is supported by the fact that Treg and IL-10 increase during allergen-specific immunotherapy to a degree directly associated with symptom amelioration.20, 21, 22 In addition, the anti-inflammatory properties of several parasite-derived immunomodulatory molecules are exerted by increasing Treg and IL-10;23 furthermore, it has been shown that the anti-inflammatory effect of resveratrol in a mouse model of asthma is due to the increase in expression of FOXP3, a master regulator of Treg development and function24 Regulatory B cells, characterized by producing IL-10, are also critical in curbing the pathologic consequences of allergic inflammation in asthma.25 In addition to this, mast cells and basophil activation can be diminished by the interaction of IgG4 with the allergen, which impedes its binding to specific IgE and the IgG4 activation of intracellular signals through the inhibitory FcgRIIB receptor.

Another physiologic component with immunomodulatory properties is the host microbiome, which has a variety of physiologic functions26 and, in general, influences the quality and strength of the immune responses.27 The composition and abundance of the intestinal microbiota have been associated with asthma symptoms and severity, suggesting that bacterial products can exert their effects in organs beyond the intestine, modifying some asthma phenotypes.28 For instance, high levels of short-chain fatty acids (lipids produced by the gut microbiota from the metabolism of dietary fibers) during early life have been associated with a reduced risk of atopic sensitization, asthma, and other allergic diseases.29,30 This effect is mediated at least in part by induction of Tregs via epigenetic changes.31 In general, the immune responses are downregulated mainly by the elimination of the stimulus, that is, the antigen. For allergic inflammation, this clearly works in cases of highly specific allergic conditions, such as those induced by stinging insects and drug or food ingestion. However, for asthma or rhinitis, the absolute avoidance of allergen stimulation is more difficult. In addition, it is now understood that some allergens induce inflammation by acting directly on the epithelium.

Considering the previous comments, it is pertinent to ask what maintains the inflammation and progressive bronchial deterioration in asthma. This is still an unanswered question; theoretically, allergic asthma inflammation will stay and get worse as allergen exposure persists, but there are probably genetic anomalies that make patients more susceptible to the activation of inflammatory pathways that interact with other, less-explored, links such as the neuroendocrine system or the oxidative network to maintain what (apparently) was originally induced by allergens.

The role of environmental factors

The pathogenesis of asthma involves genetic and environmental factors. The genetics of asthma is analyzed in another chapter of this book. Environmental conditions affect not only the adaptive type-2 response to allergens but also the inception of allergic diseases.32,33 Although the association of allergen concentration and IgE sensitization is not always positive,34 in theory, high environmental allergen levels will increase the probability that genetically susceptible individuals inhale them and become sensitized.35 Several characteristics of allergens and the environment, sometimes acting concomitantly, modulate the IgE primary response and sensitization process. Among these are the conformational stability, level of exposure, permanence in the air, rate of allergen degradation, persistence of exposure, age of exposed individuals, boosting of the IgE responses by conditions such as air pollution or helminth infections, and stimulation of a type-1 response by bacterial and other products. The influence of environmental changes on each of these factors can modify the frequency and strength of allergen sensitization and allergic diseases. For example, it is well known that lifestyle (urban vs. rural settings, diet) has an impact on the commensal microbiota. This alteration influences the immune system and, therefore, modulates the risk of asthma and other allergic diseases. Moreover, lowering planet biodiversity has been associated with more allergic inflammation, which could be explained by the determining role it has in the shaping of our own microbiota, as well as by decreasing levels of protective volatile biogenic compounds such as monoterpens.36

Natural history of allergic diseases

Even though they have in common the ill-characterized genetic background of allergic diathesis, each allergic disorder (asthma, rhinitis, atopic dermatitis) has its own genotypes and natural history, which are modified by the environment. For example, in African-ancestry populations living in the tropics, the first allergic manifestation in children is respiratory (wheezing), and the prevalence of atopic dermatitis is low.37 Therefore, in these cases, the concept of “atopic march”, as seen in populations with different genetics and environmental backgrounds, does not apply. Other modifying conditions acting on multifactorial diseases, such as infection prevalence (including parasites) in the population,38 microbiota composition, air pollution, diet, and lifestyles, also introduce, through epigenetic mechanisms, phenotype variations that make it difficult to find a general behavior for asthma from childhood to adulthood. Therefore, the natural history of complex allergic diseases is greatly influenced by the environment acting on a polymorphic genome.

Perspectives and future challenges

A growing tendency in allergy research and clinical practice is to consider the role of the innate type-2 response on the allergic mechanisms of inflammation. In addition, research on the allergenic activity and clinical relevance of many individual IgE-binding molecules is increasing, paving the way not only for interesting findings but also for challenging hypotheses. Furthermore, the genetic, epigenetic, and environmental factors determining the sensitization and the inception/maintenance of allergic inflammation; the impact of microbiota on immune system functioning and risk of allergic diseases; the dialogue between immune cells and neurons; and the mechanisms regulating allergic inflammation are matters of active research. Continuing research on these topics will likely expand our knowledge of the pathophysiology of allergies, as well as improve the diagnostic and therapeutic approaches for these diseases.
