Burnout among healthcare workers in Lebanon: a multicentred cross-sectional study
Authors: Elie Bou Sanayeh, Carolla El Chamieh, Georges Khattar, Angeles Garcia, Edmond Jalkh, Suzanne El Sayegh, Diala El Khoury
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBurnout among healthcare workers (HCWs) is a growing concern as it has reached global levels. Despite the various crises that have afflicted Lebanon since 2020, there is a shortage of recent data regarding burnout among HCWs in the country.AimsThis study assessed burnout levels and financial well-being among HCWs in Lebanon and identified associated risk factors.MethodsThis is a cross-sectional multicentre study conducted in Lebanon. It included 907 HCWs, comprising medical students, registered nurses, physicians and postgraduate trainees. Burnout was measured using the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory and financial distress using the Incharge Financial Distress/Financial Well-being scale. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was used to identify associated factors.Results69.2% of HCWs were at moderate/high risk of burnout, with 76% susceptible to personal burnout, 73% to work-related burnout and 56% to patient-related burnout. Factors reducing burnout risk included age over 50, better financial well-being, higher income and being a person who has never smoked or a former infrequent alcohol consumer. Higher burnout risk was linked to widowhood, public sector employment, long work hours, stressful workplaces, migration intent, psychiatric history and current alcohol consumption.ConclusionBurnout risk among HCWs in Lebanon is high, necessitating urgent measures from the public health authorities to improve HCWs’ mental well-being.

Introduction

Professional burnout is a state of mental, emotional and physical exhaustion resulting from prolonged exposure to work-related stressors.1 2 It is characterised by three key dimensions: emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation and reduced personal accomplishment.1 3 Healthcare workers (HCWs) are up to 15 times more likely than other professionals to experience burnout,46 due to routine exposure to demanding challenges,6 7 including patient care responsibilities,4 8 addressing aggressive behaviours,3 navigating the moral distress from limited care capacity9 and coping with the emotional strain of end-of-life situations.2 Unmanaged burnout can severely affect multiple domains.7 10 Mentally, HCWs might face an increased risk of substance abuse,11 12 relationship breakdowns,13 psychiatric disorders14 and suicide.247 14 Physically, burnout has been associated with road traffic accidents,10 15 cardiovascular disorders1618 and even all-cause mortality.18 19 Professionally, it hinders career growth and undermines healthcare system performance.7 10 18 Burned-out HCWs display reduced engagement and professionalism, leading to higher rates of patient dissatisfaction,2 4 medical errors,712 2022 absenteeism, job turnover and early retirement.716 2325 This results in further staffing shortages and increased workloads for the remaining staff, thus perpetuating a closed loop of dissatisfaction and burnout.2628

Burnout is a growing concern, as it is reaching global levels among HCWs.3 4 29 A recent meta-analysis of 43 studies from low- and middle-income countries reported burnout prevalence ranging from 2.5% to 87.9%.30 Even in high-income countries, the situation remains troubling with 40–50% of HCWs in the USA reporting at least one symptom of burnout.29 31 Worldwide, HCWs have remained on the front lines during crises, providing essential care to their communities.9 32 The mission becomes challenging in countries facing complex challenges, such as Lebanon.33 Since 2019, Lebanon has endured a cascade of overlapping crises that have severely strained its healthcare system.32 34 35 These included nationwide crises such as the COVID-19 pandemic,32 36 the 2020 Beirut port explosion,34 36 political instability,35 economic downturn2 35 and the influx of Syrian refugees.37 In addition to these systemic disruptions, the economic crisis has profoundly impacted HCWs’ financial well-being, with drastic salary devaluation, reduced purchasing power and inconsistent payment structures that eroded their financial security. Many HCWs were forced to take on multiple jobs or seek employment abroad to sustain their livelihoods, compounding work-related stress and contributing to emotional exhaustion and job dissatisfaction. Within hospitals, internal pressures including administrative burdens,9 38 staffing shortages,26 28 33 extended working hours,32 heavy workloads26 32 and shortages of medications and supplies32 39 further amplified these challenges. Collectively, these factors have placed HCWs in a state of social, political and economic turmoil heightening their vulnerability to burnout.

Despite the multiple crises that have afflicted Lebanon since 2020, recent data on burnout among HCWs in the country remain scarce. Although several studies were published in 2023, their data collection occurred in 2020 or 2021, before the full escalation of the crises.24043 During that period, reported burnout prevalence ranged between 40% and 90%.24043 The most recent study, conducted in Lebanon in 2022, was a single-centre study involving 188 postgraduate trainees.44 Given the urgent need for updated insights into burnout prevalence among HCWs in Lebanon following the compounded national crises, and the importance of identifying key determinants to guide targeted interventions that support their well-being and sustain high-quality patient care, this study was undertaken. Our primary objective was to assess in a nationally representative sample of HCWs serving Lebanon burnout levels and financial well-being, and to identify associated risk factors, including sociodemographic, occupational and psychological parameters. Outcomes of interest are overall burnout (OB) and its subdomains (personal, work-related, patient-related), as well as financial distress, measured by validated scales. The secondary objective was to explore plausible associations between burnout and participants’ sociodemographic parameters, psychological aspects, occupational factors and financial well-being.

Methods

Study design, settings and population

This multicentre, quantitative, cross-sectional, questionnaire-based study was conducted between 1 April and 30 June 2023 using a combination of convenience sampling and snowball sampling technique. We opted to use the snowball sampling technique due to its effectiveness in reaching a diverse and geographically dispersed population of healthcare professionals across Lebanon, thereby enhancing the generalisability of our findings. Data collection involved the dissemination of an electronic survey via WhatsApp Messenger (Facebook) to representatives in various hospitals across the eight Lebanese provinces. These representatives were tasked with distributing the survey link to eligible staff within their respective institutions. Additionally, the Lebanese Order of Nurses facilitated the distribution of the survey to all its registered nurses (RNs). Inclusion criteria included 3rd-year and 4th-year medical students, RNs, physicians and postgraduate trainees (clinical fellows, residents and interns) actively practicing in Lebanon. Exclusion criteria comprised 1st-year and 2nd-year medical students (due to their lack of clinical rotations and patient encounters), retired HCWs, those practicing outside Lebanon, those without internet access or who declined consent. This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Questionnaire

The primary research instrument was an anonymous self-administered open survey designed using Google Forms (Google) and presented in English. Despite the diverse linguistic backgrounds of the participants, English remains the predominant language of medical education in Lebanon, ensuring accessibility and comprehension among healthcare professionals.

Regarding the consenting process, since the research entailed no foreseeable risks and solely involved a survey where written consent is generally not required, electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants. At the beginning of the questionnaire, participants were presented with a consent statement detailing the study’s purpose, estimated duration and investigator information. To continue, participants were required to click on the ‘consent and proceed’ button, indicating their voluntary agreement to participate. Participation was entirely voluntary, and respondents could withdraw their consent or discontinue the survey at any time. No incentives, compensation or reimbursements were provided for participation.

The questionnaire comprised three sections with a total of 52 closed-ended, fixed-response questions (online supplemental material A). Respondents were given the flexibility to review and modify their responses at any stage before submission.

In the first section, we evaluated sociodemographic characteristics, including age, gender, marital status, occupation, health behaviours (smoking and alcohol consumption) based on the ‘Center for Disease Control and Prevention’ classification.45 Additional variables included participants’ medical history, mental health status, willingness to migrate and health condition of household members. Work-related information was also collected, including workplace location, hospital type (private or public), average weekly working hours, monthly personal income, years of experience and perceived workplace stress levels.

In the second section, we used the validated Incharge Financial Distress/Financial Well-being (IFDFW) scale to evaluate participants’ perceived level of financial distress.46 47 The scale comprises eight items rated on a 10-point Likert-type scale, where 0 indicates ‘overwhelming stress’ and 10 indicating ‘no stress at all’.47 Higher scores correspond to greater financial well-being and lower financial distress.47 The total score is calculated by summing the points for eight items and dividing the total by eight.47 The resulting average score can range between 1 (representing ‘overwhelming financial distress’ or the ‘lowest level of financial wellbeing’) to 10 (representing ‘no financial distress’ or the ‘highest level of financial wellbeing’).47 For this study, the internal consistency of the scale was assessed, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.9, indicating excellent reliability.

In the third section, we used the validated Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI) scale to assess OB among HCWs.48 Although the Maslach Burnout Inventory is widely regarded as the gold standard questionnaire for screening burnout,49 the CBI was selected for this study due to its predominant use in previous research involving HCWs in Lebanon. Aligning with these studies ensures methodological consistency and facilitates meaningful comparison.

The CBI comprises 19 items divided into three subscales, each assessing a distinct dimension of burnout: personal burnout (PB), work-related burnout (W-RB) and patient-related burnout (P-RB).48 Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert-type scale, ranging from ‘never’ or ‘to a very low degree’ to ‘always’ or ‘to a very high degree’.48 Scores are calculated by summing the responses for each subscale, with higher scores indicating greater levels of burnout. The total score, obtained by combining the three subscales, ranges from 0 to 100.48 Scores between 50 and 74 denote ‘moderate burnout’,48 while scores between 75 and 100 indicate ‘high burnout’, reflecting a more severe experience of the condition. Conversely, scores below 50 suggest the absence of burnout-related symptoms.48 The internal reliability of the CBI in this study was excellent with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.96.

The estimated time to complete this survey was approximately 5–7 min. To ensure clarity and comprehension of all the questions, a pilot study was conducted with 15 HCWs. Responses from the pilot participants were not included in the final analysis.

Regarding data management, responses were automatically captured and stored in a database for subsequent analysis. Following data collection, thorough checks were performed to ensure completeness and accuracy. Duplicate entries were identified based on user IDs, with only the first entry retained for analysis. Stringent measures were implemented to safeguard confidentiality and data security throughout the process. All data were securely stored on institution-affiliated computers, with access restricted solely to the authors responsible for data analysis and interpretation. To maintain anonymity, no identifiable information of participants was collected or stored. Data handling procedures adhered to institutional guidelines, incorporating encryption and password protection to prevent unauthorised access. On completion of the study, all electronic and hardcopy data were securely archived in accordance with institutional policies.

Public involvement

Participants and the public were directly involved in the design and conduct of this research. The study targeted HCWs who play a central role in patient care, and the findings indirectly reflect patient-related outcomes through the assessment of provider well-being. We will seek public involvement in the development of an appropriate method of dissemination.

Sample size

According to the following formula50: n=Z2* P * (1-P)d2, where n is the sample size, ‘Z’=1.96 is the statistic corresponding to a 95% level of confidence, ‘P’= 86.3% is the expected proportion of PB among Lebanese HCWs based on previous studies42 and ‘d’=5% is the precision, the calculated needed minimal sample size is 186 individuals.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages) and compared between groups using the χ² test. Continuous variables were presented as median (IQR). A bidirectional stepwise linear regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with burnout scores as dependent variables, including all variables with a p value <0.2 in the univariate analysis as independent variables. All necessary assumptions for linear regression were examined and met in all instances. Statistical significance was defined as a p value <0.05. All analyses were conducted using R statistical software, V.4.0.2.

Results

The participation rate, defined as the proportion of visitors who completed the first survey page divided by those who accessed the informed consent page, was 97% (1114/1149 participants). The completion rate, defined as the proportion of respondents who submitted the final questionnaire page divided by those who provided consent, was 81.4% (907/1114 participants).

In terms of work-related characteristics, participants were primarily employed in urban regions (39.6% in Beirut and 19.6% in Mount Lebanon), with 68.6% working in the private sector, 72.4% working more than 40 hours per week and 60.4% earning less than US$1000 per month. Enrolled subjects were representatives of the various departments and job types. Significant differences in burnout levels were observed across several variables, including work location, type of health facility (private or public), monthly income, job type, years of experience, weekly work hours, financial distress score and changes in workplace stress levels (table 2).

Results of the ‘Copenhagen Burnout Inventory’ and the ‘Incharge Financial Distress/Financial Wellbeing’ scales

Online supplemental figures S1A–C in online supplemental material B provide an overview of participants’ responses to the CBI questionnaire, categorised into three dimensions according to the CBI classification. Overall, 69.2% of participants exhibited a moderate to high risk of burnout, including 30.4% at high risk and 38.8% at moderate risk. When examining the scale in finer detail across its three subtypes, PB was observed in its moderate and high-level aspects among 76% of participants, with 44% of them exhibiting a high level. W-RB was moderate to high in 73% of HCWs, with 39% in the high-risk category. P-RB was present in 56% of responders, 35.4% of whom were at high risk.

Concerning participants’ financial well-being, 64.1% reported high financial distress based on the IFDFW scale. Furthermore, variations in financial distress were strongly associated with burnout levels, as demonstrated in table 2.

Factors associated with burnout after multiple linear regression

Personal and work-related characteristics

The multivariable regression model exhibited strong explanatory performance, explaining 84% of the variance in burnout risk (R²=0.84). Factors independently associated with OB are summarised in table 3. Personal and demographic variables linked to higher OB scores included being widowed (p=0.03), a prior history of burnout (p<0.001), having been diagnosed by a specialist with a history of stress, anxiety or depression (p=0.04), and current use of prescribed psychiatric medication (p=0.02) (table 3). These factors were individually associated with respective increases of 9.5, 6.9, 3.0 and 4.9 points in the burnout score. Conversely, participants aged ≥50 years demonstrated significantly lower mean OB scores than their younger counterparts. Regarding health behaviours, people who do not smoke had lower OB scores, while current alcohol consumers, regardless of consumption intensity, showed higher OB levels; former infrequent consumers exhibited lower scores.

These included having a chronic medical condition, residing with an older person, having a family member with a chronic illness and having a family member with a disability.

Regarding work-related characteristics, employment in the public sector, working more than 40 hours per week, reporting a stressful workplace during the past 3 years and having the will to migrate were all independently associated with higher OB score.

Financial wellness

The stepwise linear regression analysis revealed that for each 1-point increase in the IFDFW score, reflecting an improvement in financial well-being, the CBI score significantly decreased by an average of 2.6 points (p<0.001), indicating a lower risk of OB (table 3). Similarly, monthly average income demonstrated a strong inverse relationship with burnout risk, as HCWs earning between US$3000 and US$10 000 and more than US$10 000 per month exhibited average decreases of 12 and 18 points, respectively, on the CBI scale (table 3).

Willingness to migrate

Among the 907 participants, 645 (71.1%) expressed a desire to migrate (table 2), which was significantly associated with higher burnout scores (table 3). Discussion

The effectiveness of healthcare services is inherently linked to the well-being of the individuals delivering them.51 Therefore, addressing the mental health of HCWs, particularly burnout and its associated factors, represents an urgent quality improvement priority to ensure the provision of high-quality patient care. The finding that 69.2% of participants exhibited a moderate to high risk of developing OB is particularly concerning and warrants attention.

Our findings fall within the spectrum of assessments conducted in various countries since 2020 using the CBI scale, with estimates ranging from 18.5%52 to 90.1%.2 compared with previous studies, our findings diverge from studies reporting higher OB prevalence including Lebanon (90.1% in 2020),2 Saudi Arabia (75% in 2020)53 and Egypt (77% in 2021).54 Conversely, our findings indicate a more concerning prevalence than studies conducted in 2021 in Belize (27.76%),55 Lebanon (40%),43 Italy (18.5%),52 Greece (49.7%)56 and the USA (21.7%).57 Notably, our results align with other studies showing moderate to high burnout risk in France (71.3% in 2020),54 Austria (70.7% in 2020)58 and Sudan (71% in 2021)59 among HCWs. It is essential to note that reported dates correspond to survey administration rather than publication. The broad variation in prevalence likely reflects differences in assessment methods, political contexts, healthcare specialties, infrastructure and cultural factors, such as societal frameworks, attitudes, religion and stressors.60

When examining OB by its three subdomains, PB and W-RB emerge as the predominant components, with average prevalence of 76% and 73%, respectively, while P-RB is lower at 56%, consistent with previous literature.244 55 56 6168 Our findings align with prior studies conducted in Lebanon following the onset of the multifaceted crises in 2020. These studies, using the CBI scale, reported varying rates of PB, W-RB and P-RB, ranging from 39.36% to 86.3%, 41.52% to 79.2% and 35.1% to 83.3%, respectively.24044 Compared with international studies, our results indicate consistently higher burnout levels across all subdomains, with PB ranging from 41.6% to 65.9%, W-RB from 30.6% to 68.7% and P-RB from 8.9% to 54.7%.5556 6168 This highlights the heightened exhaustion HCWs experience in both professional and personal spheres. However, the relatively lower P-RB suggests that despite elevated OB levels, HCWs in Lebanon continue to find satisfaction in direct patient care.

With high reported burnout rates, understanding associated factors is crucial. We highlight significant associations after multiple linear regression analysis, categorised into personal and work-related factors. Notably, and in line with previous studies, HCWs aged 50 and older scored lower on OB, likely reflecting their accumulated experience in stress management and emotional resilience.2 30 52 64 69 In contrast, younger professionals grapple with intense pressure and limited control over their work circumstances. Regarding marital status, only widowed individuals showed a positive association with burnout rates. This association might stem from the challenging loneliness and social isolation experienced after losing their partners.70

The findings of this study demonstrate a significant association between mental health conditions, including a history of stress, burnout or depression and the use of psychiatric medications, and OB, consistent with prior research.71 Furthermore, consistent with previous studies,55 maladaptive coping behaviours, such as alcohol consumption at any intensity (light, moderate or heavy), were positively associated with burnout, whereas never smoking was inversely associated.

Work-related factors exerted a stronger influence on OB rates than personal factors. Specifically, in terms of economic aspects, having a current income of US$3000 per month or below or experiencing high financial distress, according to the IFDFW scale, were associated with a greater risk of developing burnout. This aligns with existing findings that suggest that individuals with lower income levels tend to have poorer mental health due to the stress resulting from an imbalance between the demands they face and the resources available to cope with them.2 42 69 72 73 The current economic downturn in Lebanon further magnifies the income–mental health link. Many HCWs are grappling with inadequate compensation, leading to diminished motivation.33 35 A significant portion of HCWs in our study showed an inclination to seek opportunities abroad, and intriguingly, such an inclination was inversely associated with their monthly income. Furthermore, the desire to migrate was associated with higher burnout rates and longer work hours. Extended working hours were also linked to greater susceptibility to burnout, consistent with previous research.42 54 69 This trend could be attributed to significant HCW emigration from Lebanon,33 35 leading to staff shortages and increased workloads for those who remained, which in turn fuelled their desire to migrate.2628 Prolonged work hours also limit opportunities for social engagement, family time and disrupt circadian rhythms, all of which contribute to diminished social well-being.

Additionally, consistent with earlier research,63 this study highlights the association between burnout and employment in the public sector. Even prior to Lebanon’s recent crises, public hospitals faced budgetary constraints, staff and resource shortages, neglect and heavy workloads, whereas private hospitals were comparatively better resourced.74 Moreover, HCWs who perceived their work environment as stressful were at a higher risk of burnout, in line with the WHO’s definition of burnout as ‘a syndrome resulting from workplace stress that has not been effectively managed’.75 Given Lebanon’s fragile healthcare system, HCWs often operate in high-pressure settings with limited infrastructure,35 making workplace stress a major contributor to burnout.54

Clinical implications

Addressing HCW burnout is pivotal not only for the well-being of the individuals affected but also for maintaining high-quality patient care and overall system performance. However, effective interventions tailored to this issue, particularly in Lebanon, remain elusive. While personal attributes influence burnout scores, occupational factors emerged as predominant contributors, suggesting that institutions themselves are significant sources of HCW stress.

Effective system-level interventions at the institutional level are essential. Developing anonymous screening tools can help identify institution-specific burnout risk factors, with longitudinal evaluations to assess the effectiveness of interventions. Raising awareness about well-being and burnout through open dialogue and safe discussion spaces is vital. Although personal characteristics cannot be altered, acknowledging them allows for targeted support of high-risk individuals. Actively involving HCWs in guideline development and contingency planning, supported by institution-funded initiatives, fosters a supportive network and ensures access to feedback channels and expert guidance.

Managing workload is also critical. High workload adversely affects patient care and HCW satisfaction. Interventions such as task off-loading, eliminating inefficiencies and implementing electronic medical records, alongside hiring additional staff, can reduce burnout. Providing financial incentives, access to medical services and family-friendly policies further supports HCWs’ well-being and promotes work-life balance. Establishing a dedicated committee for HCWs’ well-being within institutions can drive education on burnout, mindfulness programmes and targeted interventions, while addressing negative leadership behaviours and fostering camaraderie through events and team-building activities.

Policy implications

At the public health and policy level, a comprehensive approach is required to ensure the well-being of HCWs and sustainable healthcare delivery. National and local health authorities, professional societies and academic institutions should collaborate to develop national strategies for HCW mental health, including tailored screening tools and standardised assessment protocols. Organising conferences, summits and workshops can raise awareness, promote best practices and facilitate knowledge exchange regarding burnout mitigation.

Investment in research focused on HCW well-being, including longitudinal studies evaluating intervention efficacy, is crucial to guide evidence-based policies and interventions. Institutional and national policies should emphasise workload management, financial support, flexible work schedules and family-friendly initiatives to promote resilience and satisfaction among HCWs. Creating a culture that recognises HCWs as multidimensional individuals and ensures the active involvement of frontline staff in policy development strengthens both workforce retention and the quality of patient care.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations that warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design inherently precludes establishing causal relationships between identified risk factors and burnout outcomes; thus, the findings reflect associations observed at a single point in time rather than longitudinal trends. Second, the reliance on online self-reporting introduces potential biases, including recall bias and response bias influenced by participants’ motivation or interest in the topic. Third, the use of snowball sampling may have introduced selection bias, as participants were likely to recruit individuals within their own social or professional circles. This could have affected the representativeness of the sample, contributing to the over-representation of HCWs from urban centres such as Beirut, where major medical faculties and hospitals are concentrated. Nonetheless, this approach was selected to facilitate data collection during a period of significant logistical challenges and workforce instability in Lebanon. Fourth, the study did not investigate some major adaptive coping mechanisms, such as exercise, illicit drug use or altered eating habits; nor did it include clinical or administrative data, such as absenteeism, turnover rates or patient workload, which could have provided objective context to complement the self-reported findings. Finally, the study was conducted in 2023, 3 years after the onset of the crises, possibly resulting in an underestimation of burnout rates, as many HCWs had already left the country.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our multi-institutional study highlights the substantial burnout risk faced by HCWs in Lebanon amid the enduring economic crisis. Urgent actions by public health authorities are imperative to bolster the mental well-being of these professionals. Burnout’s systemic nature necessitates evidence-based interventions at national, organisational and individual levels, encompassing resource provision, work-life balance, recognition of efforts and mental health support. Future research should embrace longitudinal and interventional approaches, prioritising burnout prevention and emphasising systemic and protective strategies.
