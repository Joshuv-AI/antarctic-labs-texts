Persistent oxidized regenerated cellulose mimicking locoregional recurrence after thyroidectomy: diagnostic challenges and associated risk factors
Authors: Mustafa Anıl Turhan, İlgiz Tüzken, Mustafa Şahin, Atilla Halil Elhan, Koray Ceyhan, Volkan Genç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOxidized regenerated cellulose (ORC) is widely used as a local hemostatic agent during thyroid surgery. Although manufacturers report complete absorption within 7–14 days, persistent ORC is frequently observed in postoperative follow-up, often mimicking residual or recurrent thyroid tissue. This misinterpretation can result in unnecessary investigations or biopsies. The present study aimed (1) to define the ultrasonographic features of persistent ORC to prevent misleading radiological interpretations and (2) to identify the risk factors associated with delayed ORC resorption.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted on 145 consecutive patients who underwent thyroid surgery between May 2022 and April 2024. Ultrasonographic follow-up findings were analyzed to identify persistent ORC. Potential clinical and hematological predictors of delayed absorption were evaluated. Statistical analyses included chi-square tests, t-tests, and Cox regression models to determine independent risk factors influencing ORC persistence.ResultsPersistence of ORC was significantly associated with a history of reoperation (p = 0.002), presence of coronary artery disease (p = 0.006), low neutrophil count (< 3.785 × 10⁹/L; p = 0.003), and the use of the Pahacel® brand of ORC. In contrast, ORC from the Surgicel® brand showed significantly faster absorption. These findings suggest that both patient-related and product-related factors contribute to the delayed resorption of ORC.ConclusionsPersistent ORC may mimic recurrent thyroid tissue on postoperative imaging. Awareness of its ultrasonographic appearance and understanding of related risk factors are essential to avoid unnecessary diagnostic procedures. Surgeons, endocrinologists, and radiologists should exercise caution during postoperative assessment, especially in patients with risk factors for delayed absorption.Trial registrationThis study is a retrospective analysis and was not prospectively registered.

Introduction

Surgical treatment of thyroid gland diseases is one of the fundamental practices in endocrine surgery and holds a significant place in general surgical practice. The surgical management of both benign and malignant thyroid disorders is being performed in an increasing number of patients, and various surgical techniques and adjunctive materials have been developed to enhance the safety of these procedures [1, 2]. Although complication rates are relatively low when thyroid surgery is performed in high-volume centers, the large size of the patient population requiring such procedures means that thyroid surgery remains a significant clinical challenge [3, 4]. The most common complications of thyroid surgery include hypoparathyroidism and recurrent laryngeal nerve injury. Despite advancements in surgical techniques and technologies, postoperative hemorrhage, though rare (with an incidence up to 3%), remains a serious complication due to its potential for severe clinical consequences [4–6]. In this context, achieving effective hemostasis at the surgical site is critically important for minimizing postoperative complications.

Oxidized regenerated cellulose (ORC) is a water-insoluble derivative of cellulose, a key structural component of plant cells, and is widely used in clinical practice as a local hemostatic agent [7]. As in many other surgical fields, ORC is frequently used during thyroid surgery to achieve intraoperative hemostasis [8–10]. Although the exact mechanism of action of ORC has not been fully elucidated, it is believed that upon contact with blood, it transforms into a gelatinous mass that provides a physical matrix for platelet adhesion and aggregation, thereby promoting the formation of a platelet-fibrin clot and facilitating hemostasis [10–12].

One of the key advantages of ORC is its bioabsorbability, which allows it to be gradually resorbed by the body without the need for removal. Based on current evidence, ORC is expected to be completely absorbed within one to eight weeks [12, 13]. However, in clinical practice, persistent ORC may be detected during long-term follow-up in some patients, and it can be misinterpreted as foreign body reaction, infection, abscess, hematoma, or even tumor recurrence, potentially leading to unnecessary diagnostic or surgical interventions [12, 14–18].

In surgical practice, in addition to the proven safety and efficacy of ORC, the persistence of this material and its potential to lead to misinterpretation must also be taken into account. This issue can complicate patient management and may result in additional costs and time burdens during the treatment process. However, there is currently no study in the literature specifically addressing the risk factors associated with the prolonged persistence of ORC following thyroid surgery. Identifying the factors that influence the resorption of ORC materials, including patient related variables, surgical technique, and product specific characteristics, appears to be of direct relevance to clinical practice.

In this study, we aimed to identify clinical, surgical, and laboratory factors associated with the non-absorption of ORC and its persistence as residual material during postoperative follow-up in patients who underwent thyroid surgery.

Methods

A total of 145 patients who underwent thyroidectomy between May 2022 and April 2024 were included in this retrospective observational study. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ankara University (approval no: i06-562-25) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All procedures were performed by the same high-volume endocrine surgeon (V.G) using a standardized subcapsular thyroidectomy technique.

Patients aged 18 years or older who underwent thyroidectomy with intraoperative use of ORC were included. Patients who underwent concurrent parathyroid surgery were excluded from the study. Surgical procedures included total or subtotal thyroidectomy, with or without central or lateral neck dissection. Data collected included demographic characteristics (age, sex), surgical details (extent of resection, lymph node dissection, use of drains), the brand of ORC used, and final histopathological diagnoses. Two different ORC products of the same dimensions (5 × 7.5 cm) were used: Surgicel® (Johnson & Johnson Medical Inc., Arlington, TX) and Pahacel® (Altaylar Medical Inc., Ankara, Türkiye).

Oxidized regenerated cellulose was routinely applied over the thyroid bed for hemostatic purposes in all procedures. In hemithyroidectomy cases, the entire ORC was placed in the surgical field to extend to the upper and lower pole regions and the trachea, while in total thyroidectomies, two ORCs were placed separately on each side in the same manner. ORC brand was randomly allocated based on availability during the surgery and used whatever brand was in the operating room inventory at the time. Surgical drains were used solely at the surgeon’s discretion, guided by intra-operative findings and clinical experience.

Postoperative follow-up was conducted using ultrasonography (US), performed by an experienced endocrinologist (M.Ş) specialized in thyroid imaging. The present study was conducted as a retrospective review of data documented during postoperative clinical follow-up. Postoperative US evaluation was not performed according to a strict predefined schedule. In asymptomatic patients, US was generally not performed before the 6th postoperative month; however, due to the clinical focus of this study, some patients underwent earlier assessment, particularly from approximately the 8th postoperative week onward. The endocrinologist performing the ultrasonographic examinations was not routinely informed about the brand of ORC. Persistent ORC was defined as the appearance of a well-defined or mildly irregular margin, hypoechoic and/or isoechoic, avascular, foreign body-like mass on ultrasonographic examination performed at least eight weeks postoperatively. This image usually contained fine internal hyperechoic lines or spots and was easily distinguishable from seroma or normal postoperative tissue changes. The ultrasonographic appearances of persistent ORC are shown in Fig. 1. According to the manufacturer, ORC should be completely absorbed within 7–14 days [19, 20]. However, clinical imaging studies show that in vivo resorption is often slower and more variable, with several sonographic and histopathology series detecting persistent ORC up to 4–8 weeks postoperatively [21]. Therefore, in our study, we classified any material still visible on ultrasound performed after the eighth postoperative week as “persistent ORC.”

Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) was not performed routinely but only in selected patients with a persistent thyroid bed lesion on follow-up US when malignancy could not be confidently excluded. The decision to perform FNAC was additionally guided by biochemical suspicion of recurrence, particularly elevated thyroglobulin levels. All FNAC procedures were performed after the early postoperative period (≥ 8 weeks after surgery).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were summarized as counts and percentages for categorical variables, median and interquartile range for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality assumption for continuous variables. Differences in categorical variables among groups were assessed using the Fisher-Freeman-Halton Exact Test, Fisher’s Exact test and Chi-Square test. Differences between two groups for non-normally distributed continuous variables were assessed by Mann-Whitney U test. The survival estimations were performed using the method of Kaplan-Meier algorithm, and the comparison between groups was evaluated with Log-rank test. Variables with a p-value of less than 0.25 in the univariable Cox proportional hazards regression were selected as candidates for the multivariable Cox model along with all variables of known clinical importance using purposeful selection method. The final model was constructed using variables with a p-value less than 0.05, which were considered statistically significant. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using both statistical test and graphical diagnostics based on the scaled Schoenfeld residuals. The hazard ratio (HR) and the associated 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 30.0 and R version 4.5.0. A two tailed p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 145 patients were included in the study, of whom 29 (20%) were male. The mean age of the patients was 49.6 ± 11.52 years. The median follow-up was 9 months (IQR: 10.0) overall, 8 months (IQR:10.0, range 2–34) in the disappeared group, and 12 months (IQR:17.0, range 3–37) in the persistent group (Table 1). A total of 36 patients (24.8%) had benign thyroid pathology. Persistent ORC was detected on follow-up US in 42 patients (29.0%). Surgicel® (Johnson & Johnson Medical Inc., Arlington, TX) was used in 68 patients (46.9%), and the remaining 77 patients (53.1%) received the Pahacel® (Altaylar Medical Inc., Ankara, Turkiye). Total thyroidectomy was performed in 121 patients, hemithyroidectomy in 19, and completion thyroidectomy in 5. Among these, 48 patients underwent central neck dissection, and 12 underwent lateral neck dissection.

Ultrasonographic assessment of the 42 persistent ORC foci identified in our series revealed lesion sizes ranging from 3.1 mm to 38.6 mm, with a median diameter of 14 mm (IQR 8.6). Echogenicity was predominantly hypoechoic (64%), followed by markedly hypoechoic (17%), hyperechoic (5%), and centrally hyperechoic with a hypoechoic peripheral rim (14%). Internal spots were detected in only 29% of cases. In terms of morphology, 67% of lesions were oval and 33% were thyroid-shaped; edge characteristics were smooth in 69% and irregular in 31%. Collectively, these findings indicate that persistent ORC most often appears as small-to-moderate, hypoechoic, oval masses with well-defined margins, although larger, more hyperechoic variants can also be encountered. FNAC had been performed in eight of the persistent ORC cases. The median time from surgery to FNAC was 10.5 (12, IQR) months. Cytological evaluation revealed features consistent with a foreign-body reaction, including degenerating basophilic material and multinucleated giant cells, which are characteristic of oxidized cellulose–induced tissue response (Figs. 2 and 3). No malignant cytological findings were identified in any of these cases. The comparisons of demographic characteristics, comorbidities, surgical procedures, laboratory parameters, and the brands of ORC used between the disappeared and persistent groups are presented in Table 1. In this comparison, the presence of persistent ORC was found to be significantly more frequent in patients who underwent completion thyroidectomy (p = 0.025), received the Pahacel® brand ORC (p < 0.001), had lower neutrophil (p < 0.001) and white blood cell counts (p = 0.030), higher PT (p = 0.017) and aPTT levels (p = 0.044), and larger thyroid volumes (p = 0.012).

To identify variables independently associated with persistent ORC, a Cox regression model was constructed. Univariable Cox regression analysis results are presented in Table 2. In this analysis, recurrent surgery, coronary artery disease (CAD), use of Pahacel brand ORC, lower neutrophil, white blood cell, and platelet counts were significantly associated with persistent ORC. A surgical drain was not used in 21 patients (14.7%). However, no significant association was found between the use of a drain and persistent ORC in the Cox regression analysis (p = 0.715).

Discussion

In this study, clinical, surgical, and laboratory factors associated with the persistence of residual ORC following thyroid surgery were investigated. This series represents one of the most comprehensive multivariable analyses to date exploring predictors of persistent ORC that remain detectable on US ≥ 8 weeks postoperatively, despite its routine use for intraoperative hemostasis. Although manufacturers typically indicate that ORC should be fully absorbed within 7–14 days [19, 20], persistent ORC was still visible at a median 12-month follow-up in 29% of patients in our cohort. This finding underscores a clinically relevant concern: persistent ORC may be misinterpreted by endocrine surgeons or radiologists as hematoma, abscess, foreign body reaction, or tumor recurrence, potentially leading to unnecessary biopsy or surgical intervention.

Notably, four variables emerged as independent predictors of persistent ORC: use of the Pahacel® brand, history of reoperation, presence of CAD, and neutrophil count < 3.785 × 10⁹/L. These findings were consistently demonstrated in both univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses. Furthermore, Kaplan–Meier analyses showed that persistent ORC appeared significantly earlier in these subgroups during postoperative follow-up.

This study demonstrated that resorption of ORC period (7–14 days according to both manufacturers) was exceeded in both the Surgicel® and Pahacel® groups, with a higher incidence of persistent ORC observed in the Pahacel® group [19, 20]. While Surgicel® is typically provided in a loose-knit structure, Pahacel® is marketed in multiple structural forms—Standard, Knit, and Fibril—with varying fiber densities. In addition to these structural differences, the sterilization methods also differ: Surgicel® products are primarily sterilized using ethylene oxide, whereas Pahacel® uses gamma irradiation [19, 20]. These variations may influence fluid permeability and tissue response, potentially contributing to a longer resorption time. These findings suggest that the physical and manufacturing characteristics of ORC materials should be taken into consideration when selecting hemostatic agents for surgical use.

In our study, persistent ORC was more frequently observed in patients with a history of reoperation, likely due to postoperative fibrosis and scar formation [22]. These changes may alter local tissue architecture anatomy and impair lymphatic drainage, thereby trapping the material in the tissue and delaying its degradation. Similarly, in patients with CAD, microcirculatory insufficiency may restrict oxygen and inflammatory cell delivery to the wound site, potentially slowing the macrophage- and neutrophil-mediated breakdown of ORC [23]. Our study also identified a low neutrophil threshold, which supports this mechanism: since ORC is gradually degraded by neutrophil elastase and other proteases involved in phagocytosis, a reduced neutrophil count may slow down its resorption rate [24]. When these findings are considered together, patients with a history of reoperation, CAD, or low preoperative neutrophil counts appear to have a higher likelihood of delayed ORC resorption. Therefore, in such cases, masses detected on follow-up imaging may represent residual biomaterial. Recognizing this possibility can aid in clinical decision-making and help avoid unnecessary invasive procedures.

Reports in the literature indicate that persistent ORC fragments in the body typically appear as “hypoechoic, oval, well-defined, and avascular” masses [15, 17, 25–27]. Our 42 cases showed very similar features: 64% were hypoechoic, 67% had an oval shape, and 69% had well-defined margins. The proportion of lesions containing punctate hyperechoic echoes was 29%, which is consistent with the average reported in the literature. In contrast, our series also included hyperechoic and larger residual foci: 14% of the lesions fell into this category, with median diameters reaching up to 20 mm. Additionally, 33% of the foci exhibited a thyroid-shaped appearance. This morphology resembles the “second pattern” described by Dolcetti et al., although it was slightly less frequent in our cohort [17]. Our ultrasonographic findings corroborate the typical appearance of persistent ORC described in the literature. These insights may assist radiologists and surgeons in distinguishing ORC remnants from tumor recurrence, thereby preventing unnecessary diagnostic or surgical interventions. Numerous case reports in the literature have shown that persistent ORC can easily be mistaken for tumor recurrence, abscesses, or foreign body reactions on imaging. For example, Liberale et al. described a patient who underwent unnecessary FNAC and surgical exploration due to a suspicious neck mass identified five years after thyroidectomy [12]. Similarly, Alameer reported a case of a “Surgicel® granuloma” that was misinterpreted as recurrent thyroid cancer and subsequently excised two years postoperatively [16]. A 2024 systematic review focusing on the head and neck region emphasized that most ORC remnants appear as oval, hypoechoic, and avascular lesions on imaging; however, misinterpretation can lead to unnecessary biopsies or even neck dissections under computed tomography, positron emission tomography, or US guidance [15]. In thoracic surgery, Roshkovan presented cases where residual Surgicel® mimicked gas-containing collections or infections, prompting further imaging and empiric antibiotic therapy [18]. Collectively, these findings suggest that residual ORC may closely resemble pathological entities such as hematomas, abscesses, foreign body reactions, or tumor recurrence on imaging. Recognizing this possibility, particularly in high-risk patients with delayed absorption, may help clinicians implement more rational follow-up strategies and avoid unnecessary invasive procedures. Although persistent ORC is not a frequent postoperative finding, it remains clinically relevant. Contrast-enhanced MRI may be helpful in selected equivocal cases, but the main diagnostic challenge in daily practice usually arises during routine US follow-up.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, because of the retrospective design and the non-standardized timing of postoperative US, the exact resorption time of persistent ORC could not be determined precisely. Second, all data were collected at a single center by a single high-volume surgeon; therefore, the findings may not be directly generalizable to different surgical techniques or institutional settings. Third, the use of Pahacel® or Surgicel® was determined by operating room inventory availability, introducing the possibility of unmeasured confounding factors influencing brand distribution. Another limitation is the absence of a control group without ORC placement. However, since ORC is routinely used in our institution for hemostasis in thyroid surgery, a comparable non-ORC cohort was not available for retrospective analysis. For these reasons, our findings should be validated by larger, multicenter, prospective studies with standardized ultrasonographic follow-up schedules.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study investigated various risk factors that may be associated with the persistence of residual ORC following thyroid surgery. Notably, the use of the Pahacel brand, a history of reoperation, the presence of CAD, and low neutrophil counts were identified as potential contributors to delayed ORC resorption. These findings highlight the need for increased vigilance in the follow-up of patients receiving ORC during thyroid surgery. Future prospective and biomechanical studies are warranted to confirm these observations and provide guidance for optimal surgical practice.
