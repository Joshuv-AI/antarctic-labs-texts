Comparative effects of hydrophobic cellulose I and Ⅱ nanocrystals on the physicochemical properties of gelatin-based films for oil packaging
Authors: Hong He, Xinyu Liu, Jinqiu Wang, Qun Huang, Lei Chen, Hongbo Song, Shugang Li, Fang Geng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gelatin is a natural biopolymer for biodegradable food packaging films, but its inherent hydrophilicity and unsatisfactory mechanical properties limit its application in packaging lipid-rich foods and edible oils. This study aimed to investigate the effects of cellulose I/Ⅱ nanocrystals (CNC-I, CNC-Ⅱ) and their lauric acid-modified derivatives (LCNC-I, LCNC-Ⅱ) on the physicochemical properties of gelatin-based films. The results showed that both CNCs and LCNCs were uniformly dispersed in the gelatin matrix. Compared with CNCs, the addition of LCNCs further increased the microstructural compactness, structural disorder, and disulfide bond content of gelatin-based films, and improved their hydrophobicity, mechanical properties, as well as UV, oxygen, and water vapor barrier properties. These improvements were attributed to the plasticizing effect of LCNCs, together with strengthened physical entanglement and interfacial compatibility between LCNCs and the gelatin matrix. Notably, 10% LCNC-I showed superior performance in reducing film solubility (25.51 ± 1.15%), prolonging dissolution time (1085.71 ± 31.16 s), and enhancing hydrophobicity (105.0 ± 1.3°). By contrast, 10% LCNC-Ⅱ exhibited greater potential in enhancing tensile strength (51.29 ± 0.79 MPa) and improving oxygen barrier properties. In camellia oil packaging and storage tests, compared with commercial films, gelatin-based films incorporated with 10% LCNCs effectively retarded oil oxidation and rancidity during storage; especially, films incorporated with LCNC-Ⅱ exhibited a slightly better protective effect. These findings demonstrate that LCNCs can serve as promising reinforcements to optimize the overall performance of gelatin films for food packaging.

Introduction

Food packaging is indispensable for food processing, playing a critical role in extending food shelf life and ensuring product quality. Traditional food packaging is fabricated from non-biodegradable synthetic polymers, such as polyethylene and polypropylene, which can cause severe environmental pollution (Abdulla et al., 2024; Peighambardoust et al., 2020). Biodegradable food packaging films prepared from natural components (e.g., proteins and polysaccharides) have attracted growing research attention (Norozi et al., 2025; Nottagh et al., 2018). Gelatin, a partial degradation product of collagen in animal connective tissues, is widely available, inexpensive, and has been designated as GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) by U.S. regulatory agencies (Abdelhedi et al., 2022). Gelatin possesses functional properties including film-forming ability, gelling property, foaming capacity, emulsifying property, biodegradability, and biocompatibility, making it an ideal food packaging material (Lu et al., 2022; L. Peng et al., 2021). However, due to the broad molecular weight distribution and strong inherent hydrophilicity of gelatin, gelatin-based films exhibit poor water resistance, low mechanical strength, and high water vapor permeability (WVP), which limit their application for packaging lipid-rich foods (D. Li et al., 2024).

Recently, hydrophobically modified cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs), with a large specific surface area, high mechanical strength, and excellent hydrophobicity, have been widely utilized as nano-reinforcements in polymer matrix composites to improve the functional properties of hydrophilic films. For instance, Chen et al. (2020) used stearic acid-modified microcrystalline cellulose (M-MCC)/nanocellulose (M-NCC) as reinforcements, which significantly improved the mechanical properties and hydrophobicity of cassava starch composite films. The addition of 2% M-MCC and 0.5% M-NCC increased film hydrophobicity by 65.0% and 30.3%, respectively. Dissanayake et al. (2022) used oleic acid-modified nanocrystalline cellulose to effectively enhance the tensile strength, elongation at break, thermal stability and water barrier performance of canola protein nanocomposite films. Particularly, dialdehyde-modified cellulose I nanocrystals (CNC-I) enhanced the mechanical properties and water resistance of gelatin-based films, which could be controlled by varying the dialdehyde-modified CNC-I loading (Kwak et al., 2020). Dialdehyde-modified CNC-I also enhanced the mechanical properties and swelling capacity of chitosan/gelatin composite films (Wegrzynowska-Drzymalska et al., 2022). Our previous studies demonstrated that lauric acid-modified CNC-I and lauric acid-modified cellulose Ⅱ nanocrystals (CNC-Ⅱ) with particle sizes of 90-160 nm possessed favorable hydrophobicity and dispersion stability (H. He et al., 2026). Lauric acid-modified CNCs (LCNCs) with distinct allomorphs (LCNC-I, LCNC-Ⅱ) possessed inherent differences in crystal structure, morphology, and physicochemical properties, which might influence the performance of gelatin-based composite films. Therefore, it is hypothesized that LCNCs might be used as a reinforcing material to improve the hydrophobicity and other properties of gelatin-based films. However, the effects of LCNCs on the physicochemical properties of gelatin-based films, as well as the differing influences of LCNCs with different crystalline allomorphs, remain unclear. Thus, it is necessary to investigate and compare the effects of LCNC-I and LCNC-Ⅱ on the physicochemical properties of gelatin-based films.

This study evaluated the differential effects of CNC-I, CNC-Ⅱ, and their lauric acid-modified derivatives (LCNC-I, LCNC-Ⅱ) as reinforcements on the hydrophobicity, optical, mechanical, and barrier properties of gelatin-based films. Structural changes were analyzed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM), differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and chemical interactions. Furthermore, the fabricated gelatin-based composite films were used for camellia oil packaging, and oil quality changes were evaluated during storage. This study provides a foundation for developing highly hydrophobic biodegradable gelatin-based packaging materials and offers guidance on the application of LCNCs with distinct crystalline allomorphs in biodegradable food packaging films.

Materials and methods

Materials

CNCs (CNC-I, CNC-Ⅱ) and LCNCs (LCNC-I, LCNC-Ⅱ) were obtained using a previously established laboratory method, with substitution degrees of 0.48 and 0.39 for LCNC-I and LCNC-Ⅱ, respectively (H. He et al., 2026). Gelatin (Type A, food grade) was obtained from YuanYe Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Chloroform, glacial acetic acid, urea, β-mercaptoethanol, sodium thiosulfate (Na2S2O3), NaCl, Mg(NO3)2, and MgCl2 were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Fuzhou, China). Soybean oil and camellia oil (Golden Dragonfish, food grade) were purchased from a local Yonghui Supermarket (Fuzhou, China).

Preparation of gelatin-based film

Gelatin-based composite films containing LCNCs or CNCs were prepared as described by Kwak et al. (2020). Gelatin (0.5 g) was added to deionized water (10 g), hydrated for 20 min, stirred at 50 °C for 30 min, then mixed with glycerol (0.075 g) and stirred for 10 min. Subsequently, LCNC or CNC dispersion was mixed with gelatin solution at loadings of 1% and 10% (based on the mass of gelatin), followed by stirring at 30 °C for 30 min. The film-forming solution was then cast onto plastic culture dishes (10 cm × 10 cm). The culture dishes were kept for 12 h, followed by oven-drying at 30 °C for 48 h, and the resulting films were peeled off for subsequent use. Dried films were equilibrated at 25 °C and 53% relative humidity (RH) for 2 d before determining their basic properties. A portion of the films was stored in a desiccator containing indicator silica gel for 7 d for structural characterization.

The gelatin composite films containing 1% and 10% of CNC-I, CNC-Ⅱ, LCNC-I, and LCNC-Ⅱ were named as Gel/CNC-I 1, Gel/CNC-Ⅱ 1, Gel/LCNC-I 1, Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 1, Gel/CNC-I 10, Gel/CNC-Ⅱ 10, Gel/LCNC-I 10, and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 10, respectively. Pure gelatin film was named Gel.

Zeta potential measurement of film-forming solution

Zeta potential of the film-forming solution was determined using a ZS-90 Nano laser particle size analyzer (Malvern Instruments, UK). The sample solution was diluted 50 times with deionized water and measured at 25 °C. The refractive index was set to 1.472 for particles and 1.333 for the dispersant (Lee et al., 2023).

Characterization of gelatin-based film thickness and moisture content

Film thickness was determined using an IP65 electronic digital micrometer. Film moisture content was determined by oven drying. Specifically, film samples (2.0 cm × 2.0 cm) were weighed to obtain the initial weight. The samples were then dried at 105 °C to constant weight and reweighed. Moisture content was calculated by Equation (1).(1)Moisturecontent(%)=M0−M1M0×100Where M0 and M1 are the initial weight (g) and dried weight (g) of the film, respectively.

Assessment of gelatin-based film hydrophilicity

Water solubility

The water solubility was evaluated using a modified method described by Sahraee et al. (2017). The film samples (2.0 cm × 2.0 cm) were dried at 105 °C to constant weight. Then, each sample was immersed in 30 mL deionized water contained in a centrifuge tube and shaken in a 30 °C water bath for 24 h. The insoluble residues were collected on filter paper and dried at 105 °C until constant weight. Water solubility was calculated according to Equation (2).(2)Watersolubility(%)=M1−M2M1×100Where M1 and M2 are the initial dry weight of film before water immersion (g) and the weight of insoluble residues of the film (g), respectively.

Dissolution time

The dissolution time was determined according to a reported method (Huang et al., 2020) with slight modifications. The film samples (3.0 cm × 3.0 cm) were immersed in 50 mL deionized water at 90 °C in a beaker and stirred at 200 r/min until fully dissolved, with no lumps visible to the naked eye. The dissolution time was recorded.

Contact angle

The films (3.0 cm × 2.0 cm) were placed on the horizontal stage of a contact angle measuring instrument (Kruss DSA25E, Germany). Ultrapure water (2 μL) was dropped onto the film surface, equilibrated for 3 s, and the contact angle was measured (S. Liu et al., 2025; Zhai et al., 2026).

Assessment of gelatin-based film optical property

Light transmittance and opacity

Light transmittance of the films was determined over the 200-800 nm range as reported by Ortiz et al. (2018), with minor modifications. The films were cut into strips of 0.8 cm × 4.0 cm while wearing gloves, and then placed into cuvettes for measurement. An empty cuvette was used as the blank control. The transmittance values at 280 nm (T280) and 600 nm (T600) were recorded to evaluate the ultraviolet (UV)-blocking performance and optical transparency of the films, respectively. Film opacity (OP, A/mm) was calculated by Equation (3).(3)OP(A/mm)=−logT600XWhere T600 and X refer to the film transmittance (%) at 600 nm and film thickness (mm), respectively.

Color difference

The color of films was determined as described by Peng et al. (2021). Lightness (L*), red/green (+a*/-a*), and yellow/blue (+b*/-b*) values of the films were measured, with background correction using a white standard plate (L* = 97.75, a* = 0.50 and b* = 2.31). Total color difference (ΔE*) was calculated by Equation (4).(4)ΔE*=(ΔL*)2+(Δa*)2+(Δb*)2

Determination of gelatin-based film mechanical properties

Tensile strength (TS) and elongation at break (EAB) were measured according to ASTM D882 using an electronic tensile testing machine (WDW-5, Changchun Xinte Testing Machine Co., Ltd., China). The film samples (5.0 cm × 1.0 cm) were equilibrated at 25 °C and 53% RH for 48 h. The film was then fixed to an electronic tensile testing machine, and its thickness was input into the system. The initial grip distance and stretching speed were set to 50 mm and 10 mm/min, respectively. TS and EAB were automatically output by the system and recorded.

Determination of gelatin-based film barrier properties

Oxygen barrier performance

Soybean oil (10.0 g) was placed in a weighing bottle, which was then sealed with the film (R = 5.0 cm) and kept at 50 °C for 7 d. The peroxide value (POV) of soybean oil was determined according to GB 5009.227-2016 (China) to indirectly assess film oxygen barrier performance based on the degree of lipid oxidation.

A 3.0 g portion of soybean oil from the sealed weighing bottle was transferred into a 250 mL iodine flask. Then, a chloroform-glacial acetic acid mixture (30 mL) was added, and the flask was gently swirled until the sample dissolved completely. Saturated KI solution (1 mL) was added dropwise; the flask was immediately stoppered, shaken for 30 s, and then stored in the dark for 3 min. Subsequently, 100 mL of deionized water was added, and the mixture was immediately titrated with Na2S2O3 solution until pale yellow. Finally, 1 mL of starch indicator was added, and the titration was carried out until the blue color vanished. A blank test was performed in parallel. POV was calculated using Equation (5).(5)POV(mmol/kg)=(V−V0)×c×10002×mWhere V and V0 are the volumes (mL) of Na2S2O3 solution for the sample and blank, respectively; c is the concentration of Na2S2O3 (mol/L), and m is the mass of the sample (g).

WVP

The WVP of films was determined following the method reported by Yan et al. (2022). Different RH levels were established inside desiccators using saturated solutions. A weighing bottle containing 2.0 g of anhydrous CaCl2 was covered with the film to maintain a 0% RH environment. The bottle was then placed in the separate desiccators containing saturated solutions of NaCl (76% RH), Mg(NO3)2 (55% RH), and MgCl2 (33% RH). All desiccators were transferred to an incubator maintained at 25 °C. The bottle was weighed every 12 h, and WVP was calculated by Equation (6).(6)WVP(g·mm/(m2·h·kPa))=(dm/dt)×LA×△PWhere dm/dt, L, A, and △P represent the weight change rate (g/h), film thickness (mm), film area affected by moisture (m2), and vapor pressure difference (Pa), respectively.

Structural characterization of gelatin-based film

Microstructure

The film microstructure was characterized following the method described by Huang et al. (2020), using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Nova NanoSEM 230, FEI Company, USA). Cross-sections of the films were prepared by cryo-fracturing in liquid nitrogen. Observations were conducted at an accelerating voltage of 5.0 kV, at magnifications of 5000× for the film surface and 3000× and 10,000× for the cross-sections.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

The melting temperature (Tm) and melting enthalpy (ΔHm) of the films were measured using DSC (Netzsch, Germany) according to Pulidori et al. (2025). The film was sealed in an aluminum pan and heated from 40 to 120 °C at 5 °C/min.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The crystalline structure of films was analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (DY1602, PANalytical, Netherlands) according to He et al. (2021). Scanning was conducted over the 5-70° (2θ) range at 5°/min. The operating voltage and current were 45 kV and 40 mA, respectively.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

Structural analysis of films was performed by an FTIR-8400S spectrometer (Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with an attenuated total reflection (ATR) accessory. Spectra were collected over the range of 4000-400 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1 with 32 scans. Second-order derivative and Gaussian curve fitting were performed on the amide I band (1600-1700 cm−1) in the acquired spectra to quantify the relative proportion of secondary structure (Hu et al., 2022).

Determination of gelatin-based film chemical force

The chemical interactions within the films were determined following the method of Yi et al. (2020). Films (0.1 g each) were separately mixed with 10 mL of different solutions, namely: S1 (0.05 mol/L NaCl), S2 (0.6 mol/L NaCl), S3 (0.6 mol/L NaCl + 1.5 mol/L urea), S4 (0.6 mol/L NaCl + 8 mol/L urea), and S5 (0.6 mol/L NaCl + 8 mol/L urea + 0.05 mol/L β-mercaptoethanol). After vortexing for 10 min, incubation at 4 °C for 1 h, and centrifugation at 10,000 ×g for 15 min, the supernatant was collected. Protein content in each supernatant was used to quantify the chemical interactions, which were calculated as follows: ionic bonds (S2-S1), hydrogen bonds (S3-S2), hydrophobic interactions (S4-S3), and disulfide bonds (S5-S4).

Application of film in camellia oil packaging

The gelatin-based films and a commercial oil-packaging polyethylene terephthalate (PET) film were made into small three-side-sealed pouches (4.0 cm × 8.0 cm). Each pouch was filled with 10.0 g of camellia oil and heat-sealed. The sealed pouches were stored in a desiccator (33% RH), which was subsequently placed in an oven (50 °C) to accelerate oil oxidation. A beaker containing camellia oil without film coverage served as the control group. Samples were collected every 5 d to determine the POV and acid value (AV). The POV was determined according to the method described in Section 2.8.1. The AV was determined following GB 5009.229-2016 (China) and calculated using Equation (7).(7)AV(mg/g)=(V−V0)×c×40mWhere V and V0 are the titration volumes (mL) for sample and blank, respectively; c is the molar concentration (mol/L) of the titration solution; 40 is the molar mass (g/moL) of NaOH; and m is the oil mass (g).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least three times, and the data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Film type (Gel, Gel/CNC-I, Gel/CNC-Ⅱ, Gel/LCNC-I, Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ) and reinforcement loading (1% and 10%) were set as the independent variables. Statistical analysis was performed via one-way ANOVA and Duncan's multiple range test using SPSS 21.0 software, with a statistically significant difference defined as P < 0.05.

Results and discussions

Zeta potential analysis of film-forming solution

In aqueous solutions, net charge is crucial for the interactions between charged biopolymers (Behrouzain et al., 2020). Zeta potential reflects the changes in the net charge of film-forming solutions (Fig. 1A). Generally, a higher absolute zeta potential value corresponds to stronger interparticle electrostatic repulsion and superior colloidal stability (Cruz et al., 2024). Suspensions with absolute zeta potentials exceeding 30 mV are regarded as physically stable (Loo et al., 2012). Additionally, Cruz et al. (2024) reported that an absolute zeta potential above 15 mV provided sufficient electrokinetic stability. The zeta potential of the neat gelatin film-forming solution was only −0.24 ± 0.02 mV, close to zero, indicating weak electrostatic repulsion between particles. CNCs and LCNCs significantly increased the absolute zeta potential of gelatin-based film-forming solutions (P < 0.05), and this value increased significantly with higher loading, thereby strengthening electrostatic repulsion. At identical loadings, gelatin-based film-forming solutions incorporated with LCNCs exhibited a significantly lower absolute zeta potential than CNC-filled counterparts (P < 0.05). This difference might be attributed to the inherently lower absolute zeta potential of LCNCs relative to CNCs (H. He et al., 2026). Similarly, film-forming solutions of Gel/CNC-I and Gel/LCNC-I exhibited higher absolute zeta potential values than Gel/CNC-Ⅱ and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ systems at the same loading, which might also be attributed to the inherently higher surface charge of CNC-I and LCNC-I compared with CNC-Ⅱ and LCNC-Ⅱ, respectively (H. He et al., 2026). Notably, the absolute zeta potentials of all film-forming solutions were below 15 mV, indicating that colloidal stability might not be solely dependent on electrostatic repulsion, but also on steric hindrance or other non-electrostatic interactions. The low absolute zeta potentials of gelatin composite film-forming solutions were similar to the findings of Jiang et al. (2010), who observed that the zeta potential decreased from −6.9 mV to −2.6 mV as gelatin concentration rose from 2.5% to 10%, and from −18.5 mV to −2.6 mV when the mass ratio of whey protein isolate to gelatin shifted from 100:0 to 0:100.Fig. 1Effect of different loadings of CNCs and LCNCs on the zeta potential (A) of gelatin film-forming solution and the thickness (B) and moisture content (C) of gelatin-based film. Analysis of thickness and moisture content of gelatin-based film

All composite films exhibited a thickness of 44-48 μm (Fig. 1B), which was similar to that of the dialdehyde-modified cellulose nanocrystal/gelatin composite films (41-49 μm) prepared by Kwak et al. (2020). The addition of 1% CNCs and LCNCs did not significantly affect the gelatin-based film thickness (P > 0.05), whereas 10% loading significantly increased the film thickness (P < 0.05). Increased film thickness resulted from the higher solid content of film-forming solution at elevated CNC and LCNC loadings (Y. Li et al., 2025). Previous studies also reported that film thickness increased after rosemary essential oil was added to carboxymethyl cellulose/polyvinyl alcohol composite films (Fasihi et al., 2017).

CNCs are abundant in hydroxyl groups and exhibit high hydrophilicity (Matsuo et al., 2024). Compared with Gel film, Gel/CNC-I 1 and Gel/CNC-Ⅱ 1 films showed no significant difference in moisture content, while Gel/CNC-I 10 and Gel/CNC-Ⅱ 10 films exhibited significantly lower moisture content (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1C). This indicated that high-loading CNCs might reduce film moisture content by enhancing the film compactness and exposing hydrophobic groups within the gelatin matrix (Xiao et al., 2021). Furthermore, Gel/LCNC-I 10 and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 10 exhibited lower moisture content than Gel/CNC-I 10 and Gel/CNC-Ⅱ 10 (P < 0.05), which could be attributed to the superior hydrophobicity of LCNC-I and LCNC-Ⅱ. Studies have shown that the addition of hydrophobic fatty acids as reinforcements reduced the interactions between the matrix and water molecules (Wang et al., 2025). Similar to gelatin films filled with CNCs, there was no significant difference in moisture content between Gel/LCNC-I and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ at the same loading (P > 0.05). This suggested that the difference in crystalline allomorphs might not affect the moisture content of gelatin-based films.

Hydrophilicity analysis of gelatin-based film

Solubility and dissolution time

Solubility is essential for the practical application of edible films. Fig. 2A illustrated that CNC addition significantly reduced the solubility of gelatin-based films (P < 0.05) in a concentration-dependent manner. This might be because CNCs served as rigid nanofillers and formed a network structure, thereby hindering water penetration into composite films (Xiao et al., 2021). Gel/CNC-I and Gel/CNC-Ⅱ exhibited similar solubility, suggesting that the different crystalline allomorphs of CNCs had little impact on film solubility. Furthermore, at 1%, Gel/LCNC-I and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ showed solubility comparable to that of CNC-containing gelatin-based films (P > 0.05); however, at 10%, their solubility was significantly lower (P < 0.05). This could be attributed to the dense gelatin-LCNC network formed at 10%, which could reduce the exposure of gelatin chains to water molecules. Additionally, the nonpolar tails of lauric acid contributed to improved hydrophobicity of the composite films (Wang et al., 2025). Notably, Gel/LCNC-I 10 (25.51 ± 1.15%) exhibited lower solubility than Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 10 (28.33 ± 1.02%) (P < 0.05). This difference could arise from the presumably higher hydrophobicity and larger particle size of LCNC-I (H. He et al., 2026).Fig. 2Effect of different loadings of CNCs and LCNCs on solubility (A), dissolution time (B) and contact angle (C and D) of gelatin-based film. Different lowercase roman letters indicate statistically significant differences (P < 0.05).

All gelatin-based films dissolved at 90 °C (Fig. 2B). At a low addition level (1%) of CNCs and LCNCs, the composite films showed no significant difference from the Gel film, with all samples dissolving fully within 25 s owing to the good thermal solubility of gelatin (L. He et al., 2020). The dissolution time of Gel/CNC-I 10 increased to 108.81 ± 8.92 s, which might stem from the dense network structure formed between CNC-I and gelatin. In particular, the dissolution times of Gel/LCNC-I 10 and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 10 were 1085.71 ± 31.16 s and 637.69 ± 15.88 s, respectively, consistent with their solubility trends, which further suggested that incorporated LCNCs improved the hydrophobicity of the films. Therefore, the addition of a high concentration (10%) of LCNCs, particularly LCNC-I, significantly prolonged the dissolution time of composite films.

Contact angle

The contact angle value reflects the surface wettability of composite films. Generally, a contact angle greater than 90° indicates a hydrophobic surface, whereas a value below 90° represents a hydrophilic surface (Kabir and Garg, 2023). Fig. 2C and D showed that the contact angle of the Gel film (88.6 ± 2.1°) was slightly higher than that reported by Sahraee et al. (2017) for gelatin film (79.38 ± 1.02°), which could be attributed to the differences in gelatin source. 10% CNCs significantly increased the contact angle of gelatin-based films (P < 0.05), as sufficient CNCs were filled into the gelatin matrix, forming a dense structure, thereby enhancing the hydrophobicity of composite films. LCNCs also significantly increased the contact angle of films (P < 0.05). Specifically, the contact angles of Gel/LCNC-I 10 and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 10 reached 105.0 ± 1.3° and 103.2 ± 2.4°, respectively, which indicated that LCNCs could enhance the water resistance of gelatin-based films more efficiently than CNCs. Although our previous study demonstrated that LCNC-I exhibited higher hydrophobicity than LCNC-Ⅱ (H. He et al., 2026), the contact angles of Gel/LCNC-I 10 were only slightly higher than those of Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 10 (P > 0.05). This might be because LCNC-Ⅱ forms more extensive hydrogen-bond networks with gelatin than LCNC-I (Fig. 7B), which enhanced the hydrophobicity of the composite films and led to comparable surface hydrophobicity.

Optical properties analysis of gelatin-based film

Light transmittance

Typically, UV irradiation can lead to the generation of free radicals, thereby affecting the shelf life of food. All films exhibited UV-blocking properties in the short-wave UV (UVC, 200-280 nm) region (Fig. 3A), attributed to UV absorption by aromatic amino acids (e.g., tyrosine and phenylalanine) inherent in gelatin (Theerawitayaart et al., 2025). All films exhibited a certain degree of transmittance (<30%) in the 250-280 nm range. Notably, only the addition of 10% LCNCs significantly reduced the film transmittance across the 250-280 nm range, which might imply that LCNCs increased the exposure of aromatic amino acids within gelatin, thereby enhancing UV absorption. Consistent trends were observed for T280, where only LCNCs significantly reduced the UV transmittance of composite films (P < 0.05), while no significant difference was found between LCNC-I and LCNC-Ⅱ (Fig. 3B). The light transmittance of film materials directly influences the appearance of packaged food. All films displayed high visible-light transmittance (T600 > 87%) in the 400-800 nm range (Fig. 3C), indicating good compatibility between the two polymers. The addition of 10% CNCs or LCNCs significantly reduced the T600 transmittance of the films (P < 0.05), particularly the latter. This might be related to the formation of a denser network after the addition of CNCs or LCNCs to gelatin, which could hinder light transmission (Oun and Rhim, 2016).Fig. 3Effect of different loadings of CNCs and LCNCs on transmittance (A), T280 (B), T600 (C), opacity (D) and color parameters (L*, a*, b*, ΔE*) (E, F) of gelatin-based film. Opacity and color difference

As shown in Fig. 3D, Gel/CNC-Ⅱ 10 exhibited significantly higher OP than Gel/CNC-I 10 (P < 0.05), possibly due to the lower absolute zeta potential of the Gel/CNC-Ⅱ 10 film-forming solution (see Fig. 1A), which facilitated particle aggregation and thus increased opacity. Notably, 1% LCNCs significantly increased the OP of gelatin-based films (P < 0.05) in a concentration-dependent manner, suggesting that LCNCs might form a denser crosslinked network with gelatin and enhance light scattering owing to their larger particle size, thereby increasing opacity (Ortiz et al., 2018). However, OP values were comparable for Gel/LCNC-I and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ films, consistent with the UV and visible light transmittance results. Limpan et al. (2010) suggested that film light transmittance was controlled by the arrangement of polymers within the film network.

The color of edible films affects product appearance and consumer acceptability. L*, a* and b* values were used to characterize the color properties of films (L. Peng et al., 2021). As shown in Fig. 3E and F, all films exhibited L* above 88 and a* close to zero, indicating high brightness and no obvious red or green hues (Musso et al., 2017). The b* values of all films ranged from 3.2 to 5.5, showing a slight yellowish color. Compared with CNC, the addition of LCNC increased the b* value of composite films, particularly at 10%, and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ displayed a higher b* value than Gel/LCNC-I. The addition of CNC and LCNC significantly increased the ΔE* of composite films (P < 0.05), and CNC-Ⅱ exerted a greater enhancing effect on ΔE* than CNC-I. Notably, Gel/LCNC-I and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ films displayed significantly higher ΔE* values compared with Gel/CNC-I and Gel/CNC-Ⅱ films (P < 0.05), respectively. The addition of LCNCs to the film increased both the b* and ΔE* values, indicating that LCNCs might have better compatibility with the gelatin matrix than CNCs, thus affecting the overall color of the film. This finding was consistent with the observation by Oun and Rhim (2016), who reported that the ΔE* of carboxymethyl cellulose films was associated with the type and content of added reinforcements.

Mechanical property analysis of gelatin-based film

Fig. 4A illustrated that CNC addition significantly enhanced the TS of gelatin-based films. Studies have demonstrated that CNCs possess a high Young's modulus (130-250 GPa) and are commonly used as reinforcements (Ghilan et al., 2023). Meanwhile, the abundant hydroxyl groups on CNCs might form hydrogen bonds with amino/carboxyl groups on gelatin, strengthening the TS of gelatin-based films. However, the TS of composite films with 10% CNCs was lower than that with 1% CNCs, particularly for CNC-Ⅱ, which could be attributed to partial phase separation between the excessive CNC-Ⅱ and gelatin matrix, resulting in a decrease in TS. The gelatin composite films containing LCNCs exhibited significantly higher TS (P < 0.05) than those containing CNCs, likely due to the larger particle size of LCNCs and the grafting of lauric acid, which acted as a plasticizer, strengthening the network. Kwak et al. (2020) reported that dialdehyde nanocellulose used as a crosslinking agent enhanced the TS of gelatin-based films. The Gel film exhibited brittle characteristics with an EAB of 4.50 ± 0.20% (Fig. 4B). The addition of CNCs and LCNCs significantly elevated the EAB of composite films (P < 0.05) in a concentration-dependent manner, thereby enhancing film elasticity and flexibility. Gel/CNC-Ⅱ 10 exhibited greater EAB values than Gel/CNC-I 10, probably due to the smaller particle size of CNC-Ⅱ and its superior compatibility with the gelatin matrix. Furthermore, compared with CNCs, 10% LCNCs significantly improved EAB of composite films (P < 0.05), which might arise from the plasticizing effect of lauric acid grafted onto LCNCs. Chen et al. (2022) reported that the EAB of soy protein isolate/sodium alginate/lauric acid composite films gradually increased with increasing lauric acid content. Gel/LCNC-I 1 exhibited higher EAB than Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 1, whereas Gel/LCNC-I 10 and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 10 showed comparable EAB values. This might be because the larger particle size of LCNC-I effectively restricted gelatin chain mobility at low filler loading, whereas at 10%, both LCNC-I and LCNC-Ⅱ attained high packing density and network connectivity within the gelatin matrix, resulting in similar EAB contributions. Therefore, LCNCs, especially LCNC-Ⅱ, acted as both reinforcements and plasticizers to more effectively improve the mechanical properties of gelatin-based films, simultaneously enhancing strength and flexibility.

Barrier performance analysis of gelatin-based film

Analysis of oxygen barrier properties

Oxygen barrier properties of films were indirectly evaluated by determining the POV of packaged oil (Fig. 4C). The addition of 1% CNCs and LCNCs significantly reduced the POV values of oil packaged with gelatin-based films (P < 0.05). This improvement might be attributed to the higher absolute zeta potential of film-forming solutions containing gelatin and CNCs/LCNCs relative to neat gelatin (see Fig. 1A). Increased electrostatic repulsion improved filler dispersion and facilitated the formation of a denser polymeric network (L. W. Jiang et al., 2021). No significant differences were observed in the POV of oils packaged with Gel/CNC-I and Gel/CNC-Ⅱ films. However, the POV of Gel/CNC-Ⅱ packaging oil decreased significantly with increasing CNC-Ⅱ loading (P < 0.05), suggesting that CNC-Ⅱ improved oxygen barrier properties more effectively at higher loadings, possibly due to the smaller particle size and higher crystallinity of CNC-Ⅱ than CNC-Ⅰ (H. He et al., 2026). Furthermore, POV values of oil packaged with composite film containing 1% LCNCs were comparable to those of films filled with 10% CNCs, indicating that LCNCs were more efficient in enhancing the film oxygen barrier capacity. This might be related to LCNCs acting as a plasticizer in the gelatin matrix, which formed a more tortuous pathway for oxygen permeation (H. Chen et al., 2022). The incorporation of 10% LCNC-I or LCNC-Ⅱ significantly reduced the POV of oil packaged with gelatin-based films. Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 10 showed slightly lower POV values than Gel/LCNC-I 10, demonstrating comparable oxygen barrier performance at high addition levels.

WVP analysis

WVP of films reflects moisture exchange capacity between food and atmosphere (Yan et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 4D, at 33% RH, all composite films exhibited significantly lower WVP (below 0.11 g· mm/m2·h·KPa) than the Gel film (P < 0.05). Generally, the addition of CNCs elevates film tortuosity, retards water vapor diffusion and thereby reduces permeability of composite films (Q. Q. Sun et al., 2018). However, no significant differences in WVP reduction were observed among composite films filled with different types and concentrations of CNCs and LCNCs (P > 0.05). At 55% RH, all composite films showed significantly lower WVP than the Gel film (0.140 ± 0.010 g ·mm/m2·h·KPa) (P < 0.05). No significant difference in WVP was observed between Gel/CNC-I and Gel/CNC-Ⅱ (P > 0.05), whereas the WVP of the latter decreased significantly as CNC-Ⅱ loading increased, consistent with the trend observed in POV results. Compared with Gel/CNC-I 10 and Gel/CNC-Ⅱ 10, the Gel/LCNC-I 10 and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 10 exhibited significantly lower WVP (P < 0.05). Film WVP is primarily influenced by film hydrophilicity and internal microstructure (Z. Shi et al., 2025). Therefore, the low WVP of Gel/LCNC-I 10 and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 10 might be associated with their stronger surface hydrophobicity (Fig. 2). At 76% RH, 1% CNCs or LCNCs did not reduce the film WVP. This was because, under high RH, water vapor was more easily adsorbed onto the film surface, and water molecules tended to aggregate, thereby increasing the permeation probability (Hakalahti et al., 2017). Notably, adding 10% CNCs or LCNCs significantly reduced the film WVP (P < 0.05), especially for LCNCs, showing a trend consistent with their contact angles (Fig. 2C).

Structural characterization of gelatin-based film

SEM analysis

Film microstructure is primarily governed by molecular arrangement and the interactions between reinforcement and gelatin. The Gel film displayed a smooth, uniform, and dense surface (Fig. 5A). All composite films exhibited smooth and dense surfaces with few protrusions and depressions, indicating the uniform dispersion of CNCs and LCNCs in the gelatin matrix. Cross-sectional morphology of the films (Fig. 5B) showed slight nanoparticle aggregation only in Gel/CNC-Ⅱ 10 and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 1, further confirming the good compatibility of CNCs and LCNCs to gelatin. Moreover, the cross-section of Gel film showed an orderly striped microstructure. The addition of CNCs disrupted the regular film structure in a concentration-dependent manner, presumably via interfacial hydrogen bonding and physical filling to disrupt gelatin molecular alignment. Interestingly, Gel/CNC-I and Gel/CNC-Ⅱ exhibited dense structures, which could be attributed to the reinforcing effect of nanocellulose and intermolecular interactions (Kwak et al., 2020). Notably, the cross-sections of Gel/LCNC-I and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ were even denser than those of Gel/CNC-I and Gel/CNC-Ⅱ, possibly resulting from the plasticizing effect of LCNCs (H. Chen et al., 2022). Gel/LCNC-I 1 had a smoother cross-section than Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 1, whereas Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 10 exhibited a denser and more uniform structure compared with Gel/LCNC-I 10, which was mainly responsible for the superior tensile strength and oxygen barrier properties of Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 10.Fig. 5Effect of different loadings of CNCs and LCNCs on surface topography (A) and cross-sectional morphology (B) of gelatin-based film.

DSC analysis

The melting temperature (Tm) of gelatin films, corresponding to the temperature at which ordered or aggregated structures break down, reflects the thermal stability and hydrogen-bonding network of the gelatin film (Alexandre et al., 2016). A single endothermic peak was observed for all films (Fig. 6A), indicating the homogeneous structure of the composite films. Tm of Gel film was 82.49 ± 0.71 °C (Fig. 6B). Both CNCs and LCNCs significantly elevated the film Tm (P < 0.05), which was attributed to their high compatibility and filling effect in the gelatin matrix (Sahraee et al., 2017), thus enhancing the thermal stability of composite films. With increasing nanocellulose content, the Tm of Gel/CNC-I and Gel/CNC-Ⅱ increased significantly (P < 0.05), whereas that of Gel/LCNC-I and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ decreased, indicating that the low melting transition feature of lauric acid was fully manifested at high loadings. Notably, Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 1 exhibited the highest Tm, suggesting that 1% LCNC-Ⅱ could significantly enhance film thermal stability. The ΔHm represents the content of triple-helix structures within gelatin (Pulidori et al., 2025). The addition of 1% CNCs increased the ΔHm of the films, especially for CNC-Ⅱ, presumably suggesting that CNCs promoted the formation of triple-helix structures in gelatin (Fig. 6B). However, the ΔHm of Gel/CNC-I and Gel/CNC-Ⅱ decreased significantly with increasing CNC content (P < 0.05), which might be ascribed to steric hindrance due to excessive CNC agglomeration that disrupted gelatin hydrogen-bond networks and restrained triple-helix arrangement. In contrast to CNCs, LCNCs did not significantly affect the ΔHm of films (P > 0.05), possibly because the plasticizing effect of lauric acid increased gelatin chain mobility (Sahraee et al., 2017). XRD analysis

Gelatin film formation is associated with the renaturation of the triple-helix structure of protein (Pulidori et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 6C, Gel film exhibited a characteristic sharp peak at 7.73° (2θ) and a broad peak at 19.79° (2θ), corresponding to the α-helix and β-sheet structures of gelatin secondary conformation (X. R. Liu et al., 2019; X. R. Liu et al., 2018). Composite films containing 1% CNCs or LCNCs retained the characteristic peaks of gelatin, and no new diffraction peaks were observed. Nevertheless, Gel/CNC-I 10 and Gel/LCNC-I 10 exhibited characteristic diffraction peaks of cellulose Ⅰ at 16.57° and 22.60°, corresponding to (110) and (200) crystal planes, respectively. Gel/CNC-Ⅱ 10 and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 10 presented typical diffraction peaks of cellulose Ⅱ at 12.48°, 19.93°, and 21.92°, corresponding to (1-10), (110), and (020) crystal planes, respectively (H. He et al., 2026; Jin et al., 2017). Additionally, the addition of CNCs and LCNCs altered the characteristic broad peak at 19.79° of the gelatin film, resulting in variations in the amorphous structure of composite films. These results demonstrated that, at sufficiently high concentrations, both CNCs and LCNCs might be miscible with gelatin at molecular level, uniformly dispersed in the films, and maintained their inherent crystallinity.

The characteristic peak at 7.73° (2θ) of all composite films shifted slightly, probably because CNCs or LCNCs disrupted hydrogen bond formation and long-range ordered structures in gelatin (G. G. Chen et al., 2016). The addition of 10% CNCs or LCNCs slightly reduced the peak intensity at 7.73° for composite films, possibly because intermolecular interactions between the reinforcements and gelatin interfered with the physical gelation process, thus inhibiting the formation of gelatin triple-helix structures. Compared with Gel/CNC-Ⅱ and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ, Gel/CNC-I and Gel/LCNC-I exhibited lower peak intensities at 7.73°, which might stem from the steric hindrance caused by larger particle size and the electrostatic repulsion from higher zeta potential, further hindering the formation of triple-helix structures (H. He et al., 2026). Although 10% CNCs and LCNCs decreased the triple-helix content, the composite films exhibited lower water solubility and better mechanical, barrier, and thermal stability properties than Gel film. This suggested that the performance improvement of gelatin-based films was attributed not only to triple-helix content but also to the excellent properties of nanocellulose and its intermolecular interactions with gelatin.

FTIR analysis

All films exhibited five similar regions (Fig. 6D), namely 3200-3300 cm−1 (amide A), 2900-3000 cm−1 (amide B), 1600-1700 cm−1 (amide I), 1500-1600 cm−1 (amide Ⅱ), and 1200-1300 cm−1 (amide III) (Qian et al., 2021; Ye et al., 2022). All films displayed an absorption peak at 1033.34 cm−1, corresponding to C-O stretching vibration of glycerol (Ye et al., 2022). Compared with the Gel film, all composite films, especially Gel/CNC-Ⅱ and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ, exhibited a shift of the amide A band toward higher wavenumbers. This resulted from new interfacial hydrogen bonding between hydroxyl groups on CNC/LCNC surfaces and carbonyl groups in gelatin, thereby weakening N-H hydrogen bond interactions within gelatin. The amide B band of all composite films shifted toward lower wavenumbers, further verifying the interactions between CNC/LCNC and gelatin. Furthermore, Gel/CNC-I 10 and Gel/LCNC-I 10 exhibited a peak at 1107.47 cm−1 (C-O skeletal vibration) and all composite films displayed a peak at 1159.49 cm−1 (C-O asymmetric bridge stretching) (S. Peng et al., 2016). These peaks corresponded to characteristic absorption peaks of cellulose, which presumably indicated strong interactions between CNC/LCNC and gelatin at adequate loadings.

The amide I band vibration is almost unaffected by side-chain properties and mainly depends on the secondary structure of protein backbone (Zhang et al., 2020). Typically, amide I bands appear at 1650-1660 cm−1 for α-helix, 1610-1640 cm−1 for β-sheet, 1660-1700 cm−1 for β-turn, and 1640-1650 cm−1 for random coil (J. Y. Jiang et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2020). The α-helix and β-sheet correspond to tightly ordered structures, the β-turn represents a relatively loose and partially ordered structure, and the random coil refers to a loose and disordered structure. At 1%, CNCs and LCNCs exerted no significant effects on the α-helix, β-sheet, and random coil content of gelatin, but significantly increased the β-turn content (P < 0.05) (Fig. 6E), suggesting a transformation from ordered to disordered protein conformations. Both 10% CNCs and LCNCs significantly reduced β-sheet content and increased β-turn and random coil contents in composite films (P < 0.05). Moreover, 10% CNC-I and LCNC-I significantly decreased the α-helix content. These results indicated that 10% CNCs and LCNCs partially disrupted the inherent rigid structure of gelatin and enhanced its flexible conformations. A possible reason was that newly formed interactions between CNC/LCNC and gelatin disrupted the inherent secondary structure of gelatin.

Chemical forces of gelatin-based film

The three-dimensional network structure formed by chemical interactions among gelatin molecules is closely related to macroscopic properties of films. As shown in Fig. 7, the stability of protein networks for all films primarily relied on hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, whereas ionic bonds (electrostatic interactions) and disulfide bonds exerted secondary effects. Fig. 7A showed relatively weak ionic interactions in all films, and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ exhibited the weakest ionic bond interactions (P < 0.05), which was consistent with the lowest absolute zeta potential of its film-forming solution (see Fig. 1A). Fig. 7D showed that the Gel film had relatively low disulfide bond content because of the low levels of sulfur-containing amino acids (such as cysteine and cystine) (X. Sun and Holley, 2010). The addition of 1% CNCs caused no significant change in disulfide bond content of films, whereas 10% CNCs addition significantly reduced this value (P < 0.05), which might be ascribed to intermolecular interactions between CNCs and gelatin partially shielding sulfhydryl groups (Zhou et al., 2020). Compared with CNCs, the addition of LCNCs at 1% significantly decreased the disulfide bond content of gelatin films, whereas LCNCs at 10% induced a significant increase (P < 0.05). A possible explanation was that the gelatin film-forming solution containing LCNCs possessed a lower absolute zeta potential than the CNC-containing counterpart (seeFig. 1A), and the former was more prone to intermolecular crosslinking driven by electrostatic interactions, thereby consuming free sulfhydryl groups and reducing disulfide bond content. When the LCNC loading increased to 10%, excessive fillers had a pronounced plasticizing effect on the gelatin matrix, disrupting the original gelatin network and facilitating massive exposure of sulfhydryl groups. During film formation, these exposed sulfhydryl groups were readily oxidized and crosslinked to form new disulfide bonds, ultimately increasing disulfide bond levels (H. Chen et al., 2022). However, similar to the Gel/CNC-I and Gel/CNC-Ⅱ films, no significant difference in disulfide bond content was detected between Gel/LCNC-I and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ films (P > 0.05), presumably indicating that LCNC crystalline allomorphs had little effect on the disulfide bond content of gelatin-based films.

The stability of protein secondary structure is attributed to intermolecular hydrogen bonding (Xu et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 7B, all composite films except Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 1 had significantly lower hydrogen bond contents than the Gel film (P < 0.05). This finding was consistent with the shift of the amide A band toward higher wavenumbers in the FTIR spectra (see Fig. 6D). This was attributed to the conformational transitions of the ordered structure in gelatin induced by CNCs and LCNCs, which weakened intrinsic hydrogen-bonding interactions within the gelatin matrix. The changes in CNCs concentration exerted no significant influence on the hydrogen bond content of composite films, whereas increasing LCNCs content significantly reduced this index (P < 0.05), particularly for LCNC-I. This might be because the larger particle size of LCNC-I generated steric hindrance and disrupted hydrogen-bonded triple-helix structures of gelatin (H. He et al., 2026). Hydrophobic interactions are the main forces maintaining protein tertiary structure (G. D. He et al., 2019). Fig. 7C showed that the Gel film exhibited the strongest hydrophobic interactions, likely owing to its abundant hydrophobic amino acids that promoted protein crosslinking and molecular aggregation. Except for 1% CNC-Ⅱ, CNCs addition did not affect film hydrophobic interactions. Compared with CNC-I, 1% LCNC-I significantly increased hydrophobic interactions of composite films, whereas 10% LCNC-I significantly decreased hydrophobic interactions (P < 0.05). This trend might be ascribed to the higher hydrophobicity and larger particle size of LCNC-I (H. He et al., 2026), as it crosslinked with gelatin to expose more hydrophobic groups and acted as a matrix filler (Mi et al., 2021). However, 10% LCNC-I might block hydrophobic group exposure and weaken hydrophobic interactions. Furthermore, no significant difference in hydrophobic interactions was found between gelatin-based films loaded with CNC-Ⅱ and LCNC-Ⅱ, which might be due to their relatively small particle sizes (H. He et al., 2026).

Application of film in camellia oil packaging

The protective effects of different films on the quality of packaged camellia oil under accelerated oxidation at 50 °C were investigated (Fig. 8). All packaging films remained intact without visible damage or oil leakage (Fig. 8A), indicating that the Gel film, PET film, and composite films could effectively protect camellia oil under accelerated oxidation conditions. POV and AV values of packaged edible oils can reflect the barrier properties of the film to a certain extent (Meng et al., 2021). The POV of unpackaged camellia oil increased from 3.325 ± 0.035 mmol/kg at 5 d to 5.625 ± 0.106 mmol/kg at 20 d (Fig. 8B). This phenomenon could be explained by the abundance of unsaturated fatty acids in camellia oil, which were prone to oxidation upon direct exposure to ambient oxygen, especially under high-temperature accelerated oxidation (T. Shi et al., 2021). Compared with unpackaged oil, the oil packaged with PET or Gel film exhibited significantly lower POV (P < 0.05), with no significant difference between the two packaging materials, indicating that PET and Gel films could retard oil oxidative rancidity. Notably, after 10 d of accelerated oxidation, the POV of camellia oil packaged in composite films was significantly lower than that of oil packaged in Gel or PET film (P < 0.05), demonstrating that composite films provided superior oxygen barrier properties. After 20 d, camellia oil packaged with Gel/LCNC-I 10 and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 10 films exhibited lower POV values (P < 0.05) compared with those packaged with Gel/CNC-I 10 and Gel/CNC-Ⅱ 10, and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 10 performed slightly better than Gel/LCNC-I 10, which was consistent with the oxygen transmission rate results. AV reflects the content of carboxylic acid groups from free fatty acids, with higher AV values indicating greater free fatty acid content (Manzari Tavakoli et al., 2025). The AV of unpackaged camellia oil increased substantially with extended storage (Fig. 8C), as direct exposure to ambient air induced lipid oxidation and hydrolysis, leading to the accumulation of acidic compounds. After 5 d of storage, only camellia oil packaged with Gel/LCNC-I 10 and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 10 had significantly lower AV than unpackaged oil, indicating superior barrier performance. Therefore, Gel/LCNC-I 10 and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 10 films exhibited excellent performance for oil packaging. The superior oxygen barrier properties of the composite films effectively retarded lipid oxidation and rancidity, which was critical for extending the shelf life of camellia oil. The composite films remained structurally intact during storage, meeting the packaging requirements of edible oil condiment packets. Since LCNC-I and LCNC-Ⅱ had distinct advantages in improving the physicochemical properties of gelatin films, the appropriate LCNC crystal form should be selected based on specific application scenarios and practical demands.

Conclusions

This study explored the influences of cellulose I/Ⅱ nanocrystals (CNC-I, CNC-Ⅱ) and their modified derivatives (LCNC-I, LCNC-Ⅱ) on the physicochemical properties of gelatin-based composite films, and evaluated the quality of camellia oil packaged with the resulting composite films under accelerated oxidation. The results showed that the addition of 1% CNCs significantly reduced the solubility and oxygen barrier properties of gelatin-based films, and at 10% loading, especially for CNC-Ⅱ, solubility and light transmittance decreased further, while hydrophobicity, elongation at break and barrier properties increased significantly. The addition of 1% LCNCs significantly improved the physicochemical properties of composite films, with even greater improvements at 10% loading, which were closely associated with the plasticizing effect of LCNCs and their strong intermolecular interactions with gelatin. At 10% loading, CNCs and LCNCs were uniformly dispersed in the gelatin matrix and increased the structural disorder, compactness and disulfide bond content of the films, among which Gel/LCNC-I 10 and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 10 showed denser and smoother cross-sections. Notably, 10% LCNC-I more effectively reduced film solubility, prolonged dissolution time, and enhanced hydrophobicity owing to its stronger physical entanglement and steric hindrance with gelatin; whereas 10% LCNC-Ⅱ more effectively enhanced the tensile strength and oxygen barrier properties due to its denser interfacial bonding with gelatin. Furthermore, compared with commercial films, Gel/LCNC-I 10 and Gel/LCNC-Ⅱ 10 could effectively retard the oxidation and rancidity of oil during storage, with the latter showing a marginally superior protective effect. Therefore, LCNC-reinforced gelatin composite films are promising natural and sustainable alternatives to synthetic plastics in food packaging.
