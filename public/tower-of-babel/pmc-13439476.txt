Transforming preservice teachers’ emotions and appraisals on outdoor learning in early childhood education
Authors: Cristina Valares-Masa, José María Marcos-Merino, Jesús A. Gómez-Ochoa de Alda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Outdoor learning offers well-documented benefits for children’s health and learning, yet its integration into Early Childhood Education remains limited, partly due to barriers related to teacher preparation. This study investigates the impact of an educational intervention designed to integrate outdoor learning across the early childhood curriculum on preservice teachers’ appraisals, emotions, and perceived preparedness. Using a mixed-methods approach with 185 preservice teachers, results showed significant associations with reductions in negative emotions, increases in positive emotions, and enhanced perceived importance of outdoor activities for children’s learning. Additionally, perceived preparedness in implementing outdoor learning methodologies improved. Network analysis and structural equation modeling indicated that pre-intervention boredom was a critical link between pre- and post-intervention emotions, influencing post-intervention perceived preparedness. These findings highlight the relevance of addressing emotional and motivational processes within teacher education programs to support the adoption of outdoor learning methodologies. Integrating structured outdoor learning experiences into preservice teacher training may strengthen professional readiness and foster the pedagogical use of natural environments in Early Childhood Education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54253-1.

Introduction

Outdoor learning is gaining increasing attention in teacher education as a pedagogical approach that promotes active, experiential, and inquiry-based teaching in Early Childhood Education (ECE). A clear distinction must be made between related concepts that are frequently conflated in the literature. Outdoor learning refers to curricular activities intentionally conducted outside the classroom; outdoor education usually denotes structured educational programs in outdoor or semi-natural settings; and nature-based learning places direct contact with natural environments at the centre of the learning experience1,2. Together, these outdoor and nature-based methodologies aim to support children’s holistic development while positioning the environment as a pedagogical resource3,4.

A substantial body of research shows that engaging with nature in early childhood supports cognitive, emotional, social, and physical development5,6. Natural and semi-natural settings offer rich opportunities for exploration, investigation, and play, fostering critical thinking, problem-solving, and emotional regulation7–9. Exposure to green spaces has been associated with enhanced neuropsychological development, improved attention and higher IQ, as well as reduced stress, developmental risks, and socio-emotional difficulties10–12. Nature-based learning has also been linked to creativity, emotional resilience, and motor development1,9, and outdoor educational methodologies have proven effective in delivering science content, fostering environmental exploration, and building affective bonds with nature13–15. In parallel, outdoor activities contribute to health promotion by supporting immune function, reducing myopia risk, and encouraging active lifestyles and healthier habits8,16,17.

Beyond its benefits for children, outdoor learning has also been shown to support teachers’ well-being, motivation, and professional development. In initial teacher education, outdoor field experiences are associated with enhanced preservice teachers’ self-efficacy as conceptualized in sociocognitive theory18, as well as increased emotional engagement and a stronger sense of professional capability. While prior studies have explicitly operationalized self-efficacy within Bandura’s framework19, the present study does not directly measure self-efficacy, focusing instead on confidence and perceived preparedness as motivational appraisals. More recent research similarly indicates that immersive, nature-based fieldwork can foster preservice teachers’ pedagogical insights, confidence, and readiness to apply outdoor methodologies in their future classrooms20. This growing body of evidence reinforces the need to incorporate outdoor learning experiences within initial teacher education programs. Recent literature reviews further emphasize that, although research on outdoor teaching in initial teacher education is expanding, it remains fragmented, highlighting the need for structured, evidence-based approaches that effectively foster preservice teachers’ competence and readiness to teach outdoors21.

Despite these well-documented benefits, outdoor education remains underused in ECE. Preservice and in-service teachers frequently report time constraints, limited access to suitable outdoor spaces, lack of institutional support, and safety concerns as major barriers to implementing outdoor methodologies22,23. Managing groups of young children outdoors and aligning outdoor activities with curricular goals are perceived as particularly challenging22,24. These difficulties are exacerbated by insufficient exposure to outdoor pedagogies during initial teacher education, which often leaves future teachers feeling unprepared to design and facilitate outdoor learning experiences23,25,26. This is especially problematic in ECE, where the curriculum is highly integrated, and disciplines such as language, arts, science, and physical expression are expected to be approached holistically1,3,27.

There is, therefore, a growing consensus that outdoor education should be more systematically integrated into teacher training programs21–23. Preservice teacher preparation plays a decisive role in shaping future educators’ pedagogical beliefs and practices. Studies highlight the importance of providing authentic experiences in outdoor settings so that preservice teachers can recognize the pedagogical value of nature-based methodologies, overcome perceived barriers, and develop a sense of preparedness for teaching outdoors1,2,4,27. Such experiences may also contribute to more innovative practice and a stronger commitment to sustainability in educational contexts3,4.

In this context, it is essential to distinguish clearly between self-efficacy and confidence, which are often used interchangeably but refer to distinct psychological processes. According to Bandura’s sociocognitive theory, self-efficacy is individuals’ belief in their capability to organize and execute the actions required to accomplish a specific task18. It reflects a task-specific cognitive appraisal shaped by mastery experiences, vicarious learning, verbal persuasion, and affective states28. Confidence, in contrast, refers to an expectancy-related control appraisal, that is, a (meta-)cognitive judgment about the likelihood of successful task execution, rather than an achievement emotion such as assurance29. Clarifying this distinction is essential for understanding preservice teachers’ beliefs and emotions during outdoor learning experiences.

Motivational, cognitive, and emotional processes jointly shape preservice teachers’ adoption of outdoor methodologies. According to Expectancy-Value Theory (EVT), engagement in an activity depends on (a) individuals’ expectations of success and (b) the value they attribute to the task30. In this framework, confidence functions as a core component of expectancy beliefs, reflecting preservice teachers’ perception of their ability to handle outdoor teaching demands. Together with perceived preparedness, confidence contributes to teachers’ judgments about their capacity to implement outdoor learning in their future classrooms29. In parallel, the value component of EVT is reflected in teachers’ appraisals of outdoor learning, including perceived health benefits, educational potential across curricular areas, importance for children’s development, and the equivalence of skills acquired outdoors vs. indoors. These cognitive evaluations influence whether preservice teachers consider outdoor learning worthwhile, feasible, and pedagogically meaningful.

Complementarily, the Control-Value Theory (CVT) of achievement emotions provides a framework to understand how confidence and value appraisals shape preservice teachers’ emotional experiences. CVT posits that emotions arise from appraisals of control (e.g., confidence, preparedness) and value (e.g., perceived importance, usefulness)31. Positive activating emotions, such as enjoyment and enthusiasm, are more likely when both perceived control and value are high, fostering engagement with innovative pedagogies. Conversely, negative or deactivating emotions, such as frustration or boredom, emerge when control or value appraisals are low, limiting professional growth and hindering the adoption of outdoor methodologies29,32,33. Additionally, shifts in emotional states during learning experiences can influence how preservice teachers encode and remember these experiences, reinforcing particularly meaningful or challenging episodes34.

These motivational and emotional patterns are not uniform: previous research indicates that gender, prior learning experiences, and rural or urban background shape preservice teachers’ confidence, emotions, and relationships with nature and science35–39. For this reason, designing effective outdoor learning interventions requires accounting for individual differences.

Although emerging evidence suggests that immersive outdoor learning can enhance preservice teachers’ appraisals, emotions, confidence, and readiness to teach outdoors13,40,41, there is limited empirical research examining how integrative, curriculum-wide outdoor learning interventions in ECE influence the interplay of cognitive appraisals (health, learning potential, importance, equivalence), motivational beliefs (confidence, preparedness), and emotional responses. The present study addresses this gap by analyzing how an outdoor learning intervention affects preservice teachers’ appraisals, emotions, confidence, and perceived preparedness, and how these outcomes vary by provenance, sex, and prior learning experiences. By integrating cognitive, motivational, and emotional perspectives, this study aims to inform teacher education programs on how to prepare future educators better to implement outdoor learning as a core methodology in ECE.

Research objectives

Building on the theoretical and empirical background outlined above, this study examines the impact of an integrative outdoor learning intervention in ECE on preservice teachers’ motivational, cognitive, and emotional responses. Specifically, the study addresses the following research questions:

RQ1. Does the intervention modify preservice teachers’ cognitive appraisals of outdoor learning, including perceived health benefits, educational potential, importance for children’s development, and equivalence of skills learned outdoors vs. indoors?

RQ2. Does the intervention influence preservice teachers’ motivational beliefs, particularly confidence and perceived preparedness for teaching outdoors?

RQ3. How does the intervention affect preservice teachers’ emotional responses to outdoor learning?

RQ4. Do individual factors, such as provenance, sex, and prior learning experiences, predict differences in these appraisals, motivational beliefs, and emotional responses?

To facilitate the interpretation of the results, a brief conceptual and design overview is provided prior to the Results section, while full methodological details are reported in the Methods section.

Conceptual framework and study design

This study adopts an integrative design to examine how an outdoor learning intervention influences preservice teachers’ appraisals, motivational beliefs, and emotional responses to outdoor learning in ECE. Quantitative analyses are complemented by a qualitative component that provides descriptive insight into preservice teachers’ experiences and reflections during the intervention, supporting the interpretation of observed changes in appraisals, motivation, and emotions.

Guided by EVT30,42, cognitive appraisals are conceptualized as preservice teachers’ value-related evaluations of outdoor learning, including perceived health benefits, educational potential across curricular areas, importance for children’s development, and perceived equivalence between skills learned outdoors and indoors. These appraisals reflect judgements of task value that are central to engagement and the willingness to adopt pedagogical practices perceived as meaningful and worthwhile42.

Motivational beliefs are operationalized through confidence and perceived preparedness for implementing outdoor learning activities. Although self-efficacy is not directly assessed, these constructs capture task-specific perceptions of control relevant to instructional engagement. Following CVT, confidence is conceptualized as an expectancy-related control appraisal (i.e., a cognitive judgment about the likelihood of successful task execution), which is theoretically distinct from achievement emotions but closely related to their emergence29. Confidence is therefore considered alongside perceived preparedness as an indicator of motivational readiness to teach outdoors, rather than as a competence-based self-efficacy judgement.

Emotional responses are examined through both positive and negative activating achievement emotions associated with outdoor learning. In line with CVT, these emotions are understood to arise from appraisals of value (e.g., perceived importance and usefulness) and control (e.g., confidence and preparedness). Consistent with the aims of the intervention, the analysis focuses on emotions theoretically linked to engagement and action, allowing for the examination of changes in preservice teachers’ emotional disposition relevant to implement outdoor learning.

Children’s health. Participants reported significantly broader recognition of health-related benefits after the intervention. The mean number of positive health impacts identified increased from 3.58 to 4.23 (p < 0.001). Minor increases were also observed in the reporting of potential negative aspects, such as susceptibility to illness, although these remained infrequent.

Children’s learning. Recognition of outdoor environments as suitable for curricular learning expanded significantly across all curriculum areas. The mean number of curricular areas considered compatible with outdoor learning increased from 4.42 to 5.59 out of 6 (p < 0.001; rbiserial = −0.94). Perceived equivalence between outdoor and indoor activities and the perceived importance of these activities for children’s learning also increased significantly, both with large effect sizes. Reports of difficulties related to group control remained low and unchanged.

Perceived preparedness. The largest pre-post change was observed in perceived preparedness to design and implement outdoor activities, which increased from 2.91 to 4.26 (p < 0.001; rbiserial = −0.98).

No significant differences in appraisal gains were observed as a function of sex or prior learning experiences (Yuen’s test, p > 0.05). Provenance was associated with perceived importance for children’s learning, with urban participants rating outdoor learning as less important than rural participants both before (p = 0.038) and after the intervention (p = 0.026).

Interplay of emotions, confidence and perceived preparedness

To explore the associations between emotional responses and motivational beliefs, discrete activating emotions were grouped into latent positive activating emotion (PAE) and negative activating emotion (NAE), while boredom was analysed separately as a deactivating negative emotion. Latent factors were constructed for both pre- and post-intervention measures (Supplementary Figure S1). No significant differences were detected in the PAE and NAE scale scores by sex, prior learning experiences, or provenance (p > 0.05).

Network analysis, including latent PAE and NAE, boredom, confidence, and perceived preparedness, identified pre-intervention boredom as a central node linking pre- and post-intervention variables (Fig. 2A). Most activities (65.2%) integrated at least two curricular areas, and 34.8% incorporated three or more. Participants identified benefits related to learning (71.7%), particularly environmental awareness and interdisciplinarity, as well as health-related benefits (28.3%), including social competence and emotional well-being.

Reflections on professional utility primarily emphasized methodological and didactic value (92.8%). Participants frequently referred to specific components of the intervention in their reflections, particularly hands-on outdoor tasks, collaborative fieldwork, and guided reflective discussions, when describing changes in their affective responses and appraisals of outdoor teaching. Implementation challenges were infrequent, with 73.7% of groups reporting no difficulties.

Discussion

This study examined the potential of an integrative outdoor learning intervention to modify preservice ECE teachers’ appraisals and emotions toward outdoor learning, as well as their perceived preparedness to design and implement outdoor activities. Across quantitative and qualitative strands, findings converge to suggest that structured experiences combining outdoor practice, collaborative design, and guided reflection are associated with more favorable appraisals of outdoor learning, more positive emotional responses than anticipated, and higher perceived preparedness for outdoor teaching.

The qualitative analysis provided a detailed post-intervention perspective, focusing on participants’ reflections on activity design and professional utility. Although pre-intervention qualitative comparisons were not available, these accounts offered valuable insights into the perceived benefits of the intervention. High levels of curricular alignment in activity designs (particularly in Natural Sciences and Mathematics) converged with the quantitative increases observed in perceived preparedness and confidence for implementing outdoor learning strategies. Given that these curricular areas are often perceived as conceptually demanding in ECE, their frequent inclusion suggests that participants increasingly viewed natural environments as suitable contexts for addressing core curricular competencies, consistent with research emphasizing the integrated nature of early childhood curricula and the potential of nature-based experiences to support interdisciplinary learning1,2,27. Similarly, qualitative reflections highlighting natural environments as valuable educational resources complement quantitative gains in appraisals and emotional responses associated with readiness to implement outdoor learning, collectively supporting the interpretation that the intervention contributed to participants’ pedagogical orientation toward outdoor methodologies21–23.

Quantitative findings indicate that participants’ appraisals of outdoor learning strengthened across perceived benefits for children’s learning and health, perceived importance for children’s education, perceived equivalence between outdoor and indoor activities, and perceived preparedness. These results align with prior work showing that preservice educators’ perceptions of outdoor settings as learning environments can improve following targeted training experiences and exposure to outdoor pedagogies1,22,23,25. The improvement in perceived preparedness is particularly relevant for teacher education because perceived capability is a key precursor of instructional adoption and persistence, and field-based experiences have been identified as powerful contexts for developing teaching-related beliefs and readiness18–20. In this sense, the intervention may help address a widely reported barrier: insufficient preparation for outdoor pedagogies during initial teacher education, which can limit teachers’ willingness to implement outdoor learning despite recognizing its benefits21–23.

Affective results indicated that participants’ post-intervention emotional responses were more positive and less negative than they had anticipated prior to the intervention. This discrepancy suggests that initial expectations about the emotional demands of outdoor learning may overestimate its difficulty or discomfort, particularly for preservice teachers with limited prior experience. The largest shifts were observed in confidence and satisfaction, pointing to the role of direct, structured engagement in reshaping emotional responses to teaching outdoors. From a theoretical perspective, these changes are consistent with models linking perceived control and task value to achievement-related emotions31,43. When preservice teachers experience outdoor activities as manageable, meaningful, and successful, positive activating emotions are more likely to emerge, while negative emotions diminish. Such emotionally engaging learning experiences may also become more salient and memorable, increasing their motivational relevance for future practice34.

Network analysis and structural equation modelling jointly indicated that boredom, a deactivating negative emotion, played a central role in linking pre- and post-intervention affective and appraisal variables. This pattern is consistent with CVT, which posits that deactivating negative states can reduce engagement and perceived control, thereby constraining adaptive motivational trajectories31. Higher levels of pre-intervention boredom predicted higher post-intervention NAE (β = 0.24) and lower post-intervention perceived preparedness, both directly (β = −0.24) and indirectly through confidence. Together, these findings suggest that boredom may operate as an affective barrier to engagement with pedagogical innovation. This interpretation aligns with emerging evidence indicating that boredom can meaningfully shape learning processes and performance in preservice teacher contexts33. From an intervention-design perspective, these results imply that identifying and addressing boredom early (through autonomy-supportive tasks, clear task relevance, and active involvement) may be necessary for optimizing emotional responses and perceived capability for outdoor teaching.

Individual differences showed a selective pattern. Provenance (rural vs. urban) was associated with perceived importance for children’s learning, with urban participants rating outdoor learning as less important than rural participants. This is consistent with research suggesting that rural identity and familiarity with local environments can shape attitudes toward, and perceived relevance of environmental learning contexts36,39. Sex differences were observed only for anticipated nervousness, with male participants reporting higher levels before the intervention; however, these differences were not evident at the level of latent emotional factors, suggesting that aggregated emotional dimensions may be less sensitive to demographic variation than discrete anticipatory responses. Prior learning experiences did not significantly moderate appraisals or emotional outcomes, suggesting that the intervention may be applicable across preservice teachers with varied academic backgrounds, a desirable feature in ECE programs, where cohorts are often heterogeneous in prior disciplinary training23.

Practical considerations reported by participants align with known constraints on outdoor learning implementation, including time, weather, resources, safety concerns, and institutional conditions22. Nevertheless, participants reported relatively few implementation difficulties and emphasized methodological and didactic benefits of outdoor settings, consistent with literature showing that structured preparation can reduce perceived barriers and support the translation of outdoor learning intentions into feasible practices1,22,23. Taken together, the findings support the inclusion of outdoor learning within teacher education not only as an add-on activity but as a deliberate methodology linked to curricular integration, environmental responsibility, and children’s holistic development3,6,15,44.

Several limitations should be noted. The sample was predominantly female and drawn from a specific regional context, which may limit generalizability. Outcomes were largely assessed through self-report and post-intervention reflections; future studies should incorporate behavioral indicators (e.g., observed activity implementation), longer follow-up, and designs that allow direct qualitative pre–post comparison. In addition, all instruments were administered in Spanish, and some constructs relied on discrete emotion labels and subjective appraisals; linguistic and cultural nuances in emotion-related terms may not be fully equivalent across languages, which warrants caution when generalizing findings to other linguistic contexts. Additionally, although the SEM measurement model demonstrated overall adequate fit, the AVE for the post-intervention NAE construct was marginally below the conventional threshold, suggesting that refinement of latent affect indicators may further strengthen measurement precision. Future research could also examine how institutional support and access to suitable outdoor settings shape the stability of these changes, particularly in urban contexts with limited natural environments11,14. Despite these limitations, this study contributes to research on outdoor learning in teacher education by integrating appraisal, affective, and qualitative design evidence to illuminate how preservice teachers may become more prepared and emotionally disposed to adopt outdoor methodologies. The convergence of improved appraisals, more positive emotional responses than anticipated, and enhanced perceived preparedness (together with the central role of boredom in the emotions-preparedness network) highlights the importance of designing interventions that address both pedagogical competence and affective engagement. Such approaches may be especially valuable in ECE, where teachers’ affective orientations and perceived capability can strongly shape instructional choices and learning environments.

Conclusion

This study examined the impact of an integrative outdoor learning intervention on preservice ECE teachers’ cognitive appraisals, motivational beliefs, and affective responses toward outdoor learning. The findings indicate that participation in the intervention was associated with more positive cognitive appraisals of outdoor learning, including its perceived educational value, health benefits, and equivalence to indoor activities. The intervention was also associated with strengthened motivational beliefs, particularly preservice teachers’ confidence and perceived preparedness to teach outdoors. Affective responses following the intervention were more positive and less negative than anticipated, with boredom emerging as a relevant deactivating emotion linked to post-intervention perceived preparedness. Individual factors showed a selective influence, with provenance shaping appraisals, while sex and prior learning experiences played a limited role.

Overall, the findings highlight the potential of integrative outdoor learning interventions to support preservice teachers’ cognitive, motivational, and emotional dispositions toward outdoor education. Embedding such interventions within teacher education programs may contribute to more confident, context-sensitive, and sustainable teaching practices in ECE.

Methods

Participants and ethical considerations

Participants were 185 third-year bachelor’s students enrolled in the ECE degree and registered in the mandatory course Knowledge of the Natural Environment in ECE at the Faculty of Teacher Training of the University of Extremadura (Cáceres, Spain) across two academic years. The sample was predominantly female (89%), with participants from rural (54%) and urban (46%) backgrounds. The mean age was 21.6 ± 3.0 years, and 15% reported a scientific background in upper secondary education. Of the 185 participants, 159 completed the pretest questionnaire, and 135 completed the posttest. A total of 109 participants consistently used the same anonymous four-digit code in both administrations, allowing for paired pretest–posttest analyses (Table 2). All students enrolled in the course were invited to participate. Participation was voluntary, and completion of the questionnaires was not linked to course assessment or grades. Recruitment took place during the first seminar session, during which the study’s aims and procedures were explained, and informed consent was obtained.

Variations in sample size across analyses were anticipated and addressed using robust statistical approaches to mitigate potential information loss, reduce selection bias, and preserve statistical power. In parallel, qualitative data were collected from 46 group assignments, which were not paired with questionnaire responses due to the absence of anonymous codes. This mixed strategy ensured comprehensive data collection while maintaining participant anonymity and research integrity.

All procedures complied with the ethical standards of the Bioethics and Biosafety Committee of the University of Extremadura and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (ethical approval registry no. 24/2023).

Educational intervention

The educational intervention was one of the practical components of the course and aimed to provide preservice teachers with structured, hands-on experience in designing and implementing outdoor learning activities. The intervention consisted of two in-person seminars and one asynchronous online collaborative activity, complementing the course’s theoretical content. Figure 1 shows the structure and sequence of the intervention.

Two two-hour seminars were held two weeks apart, each with groups of 28-31 students. During the first seminar, the instructor introduced the study objectives, administered the pretest questionnaire, and delivered an introductory session on nature-based education and active pedagogical methodologies. Students were provided with the book La escuela a cielo abierto (The Open-Air School)41, which served as a guiding resource for integrating outdoor learning across the ECE curriculum.

In the first seminar, students designed outdoor learning activities during a 30-minute planning session and a 30-minute exploratory field trip around the faculty’s ecosystem-rich surroundings. Groups identified suitable locations, collected natural materials (e.g., sticks, stones, flowers), and geolocated proposed activities using a collaborative Google Maps platform.

The second seminar focused on implementation and refinement. Each group presented its activity design, which peers at the designated locations then trialed. Observations were used to identify challenges and propose improvements. The session concluded with a guided reflective discussion and administration of the posttest questionnaire. The following week, students participated asynchronously via a Padlet collaborative platform.

Padlet was used to compile activity designs and reflective contributions in multiple formats, facilitating participation, motivation, and peer interaction45. Groups documented curricular alignment, implementation strategies, pedagogical intentions, and professional reflections. These materials were analyzed qualitatively to complement quantitative findings. A rubric was used to assess activity proposals on structure, originality, curriculum alignment, educational effectiveness, group interaction, and integration with the natural setting.

Research design and instruments

An exploratory mixed-methods design was adopted, combining quantitative questionnaire data with qualitative analysis of collaborative reflections. Quantitative data were collected through pretest and posttest questionnaires administered via Google Forms, with the posttest conducted 15 days after the intervention concluded. Qualitative data were obtained from written reflections posted on the Padlet platform in response to open-ended prompts. This design was selected to capture both changes in participants’ appraisals, motivational beliefs, and emotional responses, as well as contextualized reflections on their learning experiences, thereby enhancing the ecological validity of the study.

Quantitative measures

The structured questionnaire (Supplementary Text), developed and refined with expert input, included closed-format items assessing cognitive appraisals, motivational beliefs, emotional responses, and demographic variables (age, sex, educational background, provenance, and motivation for choosing the ECE degree). Table 1 provides an overview of all constructs assessed in the questionnaire, including their conceptual definitions, corresponding items, and response formats.

Motivational beliefs

Participants’ motivational beliefs were assessed through two single-item measures: confidence in conducting outdoor activities and perceived preparedness for teaching outdoors. Both items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (none) to 5 (high). These items were designed to capture participants’ subjective readiness and expectancy-related beliefs, rather than Bandura’s self-efficacy as defined within sociocognitive theory.

Cognitive appraisals of outdoor learning

Appraisals of the benefits of outdoor learning were assessed using two multiple-choice questions. The first addressed perceived health impacts, including both positive (e.g., physical, social, and emotional development; immune system benefits; motor skills; self-esteem) and negative aspects (e.g., increased susceptibility to illness; difficulties with group management). The total number of positive selections was used to create the variable conducive to health.

The second question examined perceived educational potential across curricular areas (e.g., language, mathematics, natural sciences, physical education, arts). The number of areas selected was used to generate the variable conducive to learning.

Participants also rated the importance of outdoor learning for children on a 10-point scale (1 = not important, 10 = very important) and the equivalence of skills developed outdoors versus indoors on a 5-point scale (1 = not equivalent, 5 = fully equivalent).

Emotional responses

Emotions were assessed using a 5-point Likert scale, with item order randomized to reduce response bias. Factor analysis was applied to extract latent variables representing positive activating emotions (PAE) (joy, fun, satisfaction, enthusiasm) and negative activating emotions (NAE) (frustration, concern, uncertainty, nervousness), following a validated model33. Although confidence was assessed using the same response scale as emotional items for practical reasons, it is analytically treated as a motivational control appraisal rather than as an achievement emotion.

All quantitative variables were derived exclusively from closed-format items and used in statistical analyses. All constructs included in the quantitative analyses were operationalized as described in Table 1.

Qualitative analysis

Qualitative data consisted of 46 group-based Padlet contributions and accompanying reflections (Supplementary Table S1). Data were analyzed using qualitative content analysis according to Mayring’s framework46, which allows systematic categorization of structured written data while remaining sensitive to emergent themes.

Participants responded to three open-ended prompts: (1) justification and curricular alignment of the outdoor activity; (2) implementation process, challenges, and potential improvements; and (3) perceived relevance of the activity for their professional preparation as future ECE teachers. Coding categories were developed using a combination of predefined criteria, such as curricular alignment41, with emergent themes identified during the review process. Coding was conducted independently by the authors, with discrepancies resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Relative frequencies of categories and subcategories were calculated to provide a structured summary of qualitative findings.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and visualizations were conducted using Jamovi (version 2.3.28) and JASP (version 0.17.2.1). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, indicating non-normal distributions (p < 0.001). Accordingly, non-parametric analyses were applied.

Pretest-posttest comparisons were conducted using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Effect sizes for these analysis are reported as rank-biserial correlations (rbiserial), interpreted as small (0.1–0.3), medium (0.3–0.5), and large (> 0.5). Associations between variables were examined using Spearman correlation coefficients. Robust analyses were conducted using the Walrus module in Jamovi, applying Yuen’s trimmed mean (trim proportion = 0.1) to mitigate the effects of outliers and unequal sample sizes. For Yuen’s tests, effect sizes are reported as robust standardized effect sizes.

Latent emotional variables (PAE and NAE) were evaluated for reliability using Cronbach’s alpha (α > 0.70) and structural validity via confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Model fit was assessed using χ2/df < 3, RMSEA < 0.08, SRMR < 0.10, CFI > 0.90, and TLI > 0.9047. Convergent validity was established with AVE > 0.50, and discriminant validity was confirmed using HTMT ratios below 0.90.

Network analysis was conducted using the qgraph module in JASP to explore interrelationships among variables48,49. Sparse networks were estimated using EBIC graphical LASSO (tuning parameter = 0.5). The resulting network structure was integrated with structural equation modeling (SEM) using the SEM syntax module in Jamovi, modeling within-time associations as covariances and cross-time associations as regressions (Supplementary Note). Latent emotional factors were incorporated into the SEM to preserve their factorial structure. Model fit was evaluated using standard indices (CFI, TLI, RMSEA, SRMR).
