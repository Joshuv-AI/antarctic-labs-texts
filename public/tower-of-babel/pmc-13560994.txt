Short-term exposure to heat associated with lower lung function in older adults: analysis of the Lung VITAL cohort
Authors: Nicholas Nassikas, Wenli Ni, Heike Luttmann-Gibson, Vadim Bubes, Antonella Zanobetti, Brent Coull, Julie E Buring, JoAnn E Manson, Diane R Gold, Mary B Rice
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh temperatures are associated with respiratory symptoms, hospitalisations and mortality. Yet, the effects on lung function are not well described. The aim of this study was to determine associations of acute exposure to high temperatures with lung function in adults.MethodsWe included 1923 study participants from Lung VITamin D and OmegA-3 TriaL (VITAL) cohort, an ancillary study of VITAL, who underwent spirometry (two per participant) between 2010 and 2018. We assigned daily mean temperature, relative humidity, air pollution to geocoded participant home addresses. Forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC) and forced expiratory flow (FEF25–75) were obtained at baseline and 2-year follow-up across 11 US sites. We used distributed lag nonlinear models to examine potentially nonlinear associations of temperature with FEV1 and FVC in primary analyses as well as FEF25–75. Effect estimates reported as estimated mean difference in lung function for a contrast of 30°C vs 20°C. All models adjusted for age, sex, height, weight, trial intervention arm, current tobacco use, season, social vulnerability, relative humidity, PM2.5 and O3, testing site.ResultsMedian (IQR) daily mean temperature was 13.6°C (17.4°C) for the study population. Daily exposures moderately correlated (rs−0.31 to 0.34). Cumulative short-term exposure to high temperature (lag 1–7 days) was associated with 49.96 mL (95% CI −96.32 to −3.61) lower FEV1 and 89.74 mL (95% CI −157.07 to −22.42) lower FVC compared with reference temperature (30°C vs 20°C).ConclusionsShort-term exposure to high temperatures over the preceding 7 days was associated with lower lung function in older US adults. Increases in heat exposure warrant interventions to protect older adults.Keywords: Asthma Epidemiology, Clinical Epidemiology, Respiratory Measurement

Introduction

Extreme heat and heat waves are associated with all-cause mortality,1 but the acute effects of high temperatures on respiratory health are not well understood. Breathing in hot air may affect the lungs directly through engorgement of airway capillaries, leading to constriction of airways,2 or indirectly through heat-sensitive mediators, such as ozone, pollen and mould spores, all of which are known to affect respiratory health.

Lung function measurement is an important marker of respiratory health, used clinically to diagnose chronic lung diseases, assess treatment responses and monitor disease progression. Repeated short-term declines in lung function, whether mediated by infection or inflammation, can harm respiratory health over the long term, including accelerating lung function decline in people with asthma.3 4 Lower lung function is also a predictor of premature death.5 A previous study in a Massachusetts-based cohort found that higher ambient air temperatures were associated with lower lung function.6 However, summertime temperatures were mild, on average 17.2°C (SD 2.7) for the study population. Another large US-based study found associations of higher temperature with lower lung function in adults but focused on annual mean temperature (mean 14°C) rather than acute heat exposures.7 How acute exposure to extreme high temperature may impair lung function is not well-described. As extreme temperatures become more frequent due to climate change, there is a need to understand the potential impacts of high temperature on markers of respiratory health, such as lung function.

The aim of this study was to determine the extent to which short-term exposure to high daily mean temperature is associated with lung function in adults across the USA.

Methods

Our study population included participants in the Lung VITAL study, an ancillary study of VITamin D and OmegA-3 TriaL (VITAL), a nationwide, randomised, placebo-controlled trial of vitamin D and marine omega-3 fatty acids supplements in the primary prevention of cancer and cardiovascular disease in older adults. The details of the trial have been published previously.8 A subset of the 25 871 patients enrolled in the main trial were invited to undergo spirometry testing between 2010 and 2018 on the basis of residence in 1 of 11 urban locations in the USA. VITAL and Lung VITAL (2010P000622) were approved by the institutional review board of Partners-Brigham and Women’s Hospital and were monitored by an external data and safety monitoring board. Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of our research

Exposure assessment

We merged temporally and spatially resolved daily mean air temperature and relative humidity data linked to the date of spirometry to participant home addresses. Weather metrics (temperature and relative humidity) were obtained from the Parameter-elevation Relationships on Independent Slopes Model (PRISM), an 800 m resolution climate dataset for the US maintained by Oregon State University.9

We also linked daily fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and ozone (O3) to geocoded home addresses of VITAL participants. Air pollution concentrations were based on modelled high-resolution (1×1 km) air pollution datasets for the contiguous US developed at the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health.10 11

We characterised additional demographic and socioeconomic factors based on the Social Vulnerability Index (SVI), which measures census tract-level factors that may influence health outcomes.12 The SVI is a composite index comprised of 16 US Census variables across four themes—socioeconomic status, household characteristics, racial and ethnic minority status and housing type and transportation—which are used to generate an overall vulnerability index score. SVI is scored 0–1 and typically classified by quartile with 0.25 representing least vulnerable to 0.75 and greater representing the highest vulnerability.

Outcome assessment

Our primary outcomes were forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC), and we included expiratory flow between 25–75% of forced vital capacity (FEF25–75) as a secondary outcome. We obtained spirometry measurements at baseline and at 2-year follow-up. Participants who were in the spirometry arm of the parent trial underwent pulmonary function testing at home or at a Boston-based clinic for participants in Boston, across 11 US urban sites—Boston, Baltimore, Philadelphia, Chicago, Detroit, St. Louis, Minneapolis/St. Paul, Phoenix, Houston, Dallas and Los Angeles. Spirometry was obtained by trained technicians according to American Thoracic Society guidelines for acceptability and reproducibility.13 All spirometry results were subsequently graded and adjudicated to ensure validity.

Statistical approach

We tested for correlation between mean temperature, relative humidity, PM2.5 and ozone at 1-day lag using Spearman correlation coefficients. We evaluated the linearity of associations between daily mean air temperature and FEV1, FVC and FEF25–75 using generalised additive mixed models with penalised splines. These preliminary analyses revealed non-linear exposure–response functions, prompting the use of distributed lag non-linear models (DLNM) with non-linear function for subsequent analyses. The DLNM approach includes in the model multiple lagged exposures and allows each of these lagged effects to have a potentially non-linear association with the outcome.14 We then fit the DLNMs within generalised additive models to evaluate associations of short-term exposure to temperature as a continuous variable across the 7 days preceding the study visit (lag 1–7 days) as well as each individual lag day, with spirometry measures. The reference temperature was set at 20°C and serves as the baseline against which the lagged effects are reported for interpretation purposes. The 20°C temperature approximates the temperature most people will experience indoors as it reflects the recommended thermostat temperature by the US Department of Energy and aligns with the WHO recommended indoor temperature of at least 18°C for elderly people.15 We report results as absolute differences in lung function between 30°C daily mean temperature and the reference temperature of 20°C. We also evaluated effect estimates for an alternative exposure contrast using 25°C and 35°C to ensure we were not masking meaningful effects by choosing 30°C.

All models were adjusted for age, sex, height, baseline weight, SVI, trial intervention arm (either supplementation with vitamin D3 at a dose of 2000 IU per day or marine omega-3 fatty acids at a dose of 1000 mg capsule containing 840 mg EPA and DHA), current tobacco use and season as the sine and cosine of the spirometry date to control for seasonal factors that could potentially confound the temperature-lung function associations. Fixed effects were included for each spirometry testing site to avoid confounding due to site-to-site differences. Random effects were included for study participants to account for repeated measures within individuals. We additionally adjusted for relative humidity at the same exposure windows as mean temperature. Finally, we adjusted for prior day PM2.5 and ozone air pollution exposures. Covariates were decided a priori based on prior literature and we report fully adjusted models as our primary analysis given the well-established role of confounders on lung function.6

We performed a sensitivity analysis limiting our primary outcome measurements (FEV1 and FVC) to include only those performed during warm season months (April to September). We also assessed if associations between temperature and lung function varied by several factors, including air pollution levels, sex, obstructive lung disease and SVI. We tested effect modification by stratifying ozone (dichotomised to high and low based on the internal distribution median at lag day 1, 38.28 ppb) and relative humidity (dichotomised to high and low based on the internal distribution median at lag day 1, 65.4%). Additionally, we tested for effect modification by stratifying sex (male/female), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) diagnosis and asthma diagnosis. We defined asthma diagnosis based on participant response (yes/no) to the question ‘Has a physician EVER told you that you have asthma?’ We also tested whether social vulnerability modified associations based on SVI dichotomised as high versus low using a 0.75 cut-off, where values 0.75–1.0 represent the upper quartile indicating high social vulnerability.

Measures of association are reported as absolute differences in lung function with 95% CIs. A two-sided p value <0.05 was used to define statistical significance for main effects. All analyses were performed using R V.4.1.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). We used the dlnm and gamm4 packages for analyses.

Patient and public involvement

This study used anonymised national cohort data. No patients or members of the public were involved in setting the research question, study design or outcome measures for this analysis. Results of this study may be shared to a wider audience through our institutional newsletter and press release.

Discussion

In older adults across the USA, acute exposures to hot temperatures over the preceding 7 days were associated with lower lung function. We also found potential differences in susceptibility, where heat exposure was associated with 3–4 times lower lung function among participants with asthma, COPD and higher social vulnerability, though the tests of interaction did not reach statistical significance. These exploratory analyses in higher risk subgroups warrant further research, especially as global temperatures increase, driving more frequent and intense extreme heat events.

Acute exposures to higher temperatures have already been linked to respiratory symptoms, such as breathlessness and wheeze, as well as respiratory hospitalisations and respiratory mortality. In a study of older adults across 120 cities in the USA, high temperatures in the preceding 7 days were associated with increases in hospitalisations for respiratory disease, particularly respiratory tract infections.16 In that study, 7-day cumulative mean temperatures were 23.1°C, with a range of 0.1–42.6°C. Heat waves are also associated with respiratory mortality, potentially even more so than for cardiovascular mortality, and older adults are particularly vulnerable.17 Building on previous studies that have shown that even moderate increases in temperature may influence lung function,6 we find that extreme heat is associated with substantially lower lung function, an important marker of respiratory health. Lung function also has implications for cardiovascular health with studies identifying lower lung function as a risk factor for developing cardiovascular disease, even without a clinical diagnosis of pulmonary disease.18

Heat may affect the lungs in several ways. Human-controlled exposure studies in people with asthma and allergic rhinitis have shown that breathing hot humid air induces bronchoconstriction and cough.19 20 Heating tracheal and bronchiolar smooth muscle tissue from sheep induces airway contractions, suggesting that heat exposure could lead to airway obstruction.21 Other potential mechanisms for heat-related effects on the airways may be related to engorgement of airway capillaries, leading to airway obstruction, changes in the rheological properties of airway mucus or respiratory tract microbiota dysbiosis.2 22 While some effects could occur within hours (eg, airway capillary changes to dissipate heat), other mechanisms may act on airways over the course of days (eg, airway mucus and microbiota changes). These latter mechanisms, which are more protracted in action, may explain why we found the strongest associations of heat on lung function at lag day 4. Heat may also act through temperature-sensitive mediators, such as aeroallergens (eg, pollen, mould spores) and air pollution. Heat promotes formation of ozone air pollution, which causes oxidative stress and can increase the permeability of the respiratory tract lining, impair mucociliary clearance and the ability of the lungs to clear foreign substances and weaken defences against bacteria.23 In our study, we did not find that high ozone exposure-modified associations of heat with lung function, but both exposures appear to have independent effects on the airway, and both O3 and heat exposure are enhanced as a result of climate change.6 24

Beyond recommending avoiding time outside on hot days or turning on air conditioning, there is a need for public health interventions that can reduce the risks of heat for respiratory health among more sensitive populations. Potential interventions that have been evaluated include establishing cooling centres in vulnerable communities, soaking clothing in water to promote evaporative cooling and addressing urban heat islands through greenspace development. The elderly, children, pregnant people, people with higher social vulnerability and those with underlying chronic diseases are typically identified as being at higher risk for adverse effects from heat. In our study, participants with higher SVI appeared to have greater heat-related reductions in lung function than those with lower SVI, which may reflect access to air conditioning, poor housing conditions, among other social determinants of health.25 26 We also found that the association of heat with lung function was stronger among participants with asthma (N=284) and COPD (N=96) compared with those without – temperature associations with lower lung function were about 3–4 times greater in participants with asthma than those without asthma—though these analyses did not reach statistical significance. Amplified associations among participants with obstructive lung disease are mechanistically plausible, with heat-related dilation of airway capillaries leading to airflow obstruction. On the other hand, our results would also suggest that, even in the absence of health conditions like asthma that are thought to confer higher risk, higher temperatures may be harmful for lung health. Placed in the context of prior studies evaluating the effects of heat in people with obstructive lung disease, such as asthma and COPD, there is still a lack of agreement. Some studies find higher temperatures are associated with more symptoms and exacerbations27–29 and lower lung function,30 31 while others find the opposite, reporting higher lung function32 33 or no associations with temperature.31 34–36 For example, in other work by our research team, among older adults with COPD, higher short-term temperature exposures in the warm season were associated with a higher odds of breathlessness, chest tightness or wheeze, but not with lower lung function.27 The differences or disagreements in heat-related respiratory health outcomes highlight the importance of understanding possible differences in susceptibility in people with underlying diseases, like asthma, people with higher social vulnerability, and in older adults.

Perhaps another explanation for the lack of agreement around heat-related respiratory outcomes relates to how heat is defined. Despite the growing focus on temperature and health effects, there is no consensus on what heat metric should be used in research.37 Indeed, systematic reviews have highlighted the non-standard definitions of heatwaves as a notable problem.38 39 Heat, extreme heat and heatwaves are defined differently across studies. The manner in which thresholds of exceedance are defined varies—some studies use an absolute threshold, while others use a relative threshold (eg, 95th or 99th percentiles). In our study, we used absolute temperatures rather than percentiles for several reasons. First, absolute temperatures are easier to interpret and offer greater generalisability across populations and locations. Second, using absolute temperatures offers a threshold temperature for conveying respiratory health impacts (eg, 30°C). Other studies have suggested using metrics for heat that combine temperature with humidity (eg, apparent temperature or heat index). However, the mechanisms by which heat exerts its effect on the human body may be mediated more by temperature than by humidity—holding relative humidity constant, higher temperatures were associated with lower lung function in our study and in prior studies.6 Furthermore, we did not find that high or low relative humidity-modified associations of temperature with lung function.

Strengths of our study include the use of validated high-resolution spatiotemporal temperature, relative humidity and air pollution exposure datasets assigned to geocoded participant addresses to estimate exposures more precisely. Addresses were updated when participants moved during the trial, and exposure data reflected the new address. Our outcome data on lung function underwent rigorous adjudication to ensure validity and reproducibility. Our study population was well-characterised, allowing us to control for individual confounders. To avoid comparing participants who may be acclimated to region-specific temperatures (eg, Phoenix vs Boston), we used a fixed effects approach to analyse the location-specific heat and lung function dose response relationship. Our focus on short-term exposures, pairing each lung function measurement with a 7-day exposure window, including random effects for study participants, and adjusting for age, allows us to isolate acute temperature effects from the expected longitudinal decline in lung function over time in adults.

Our study also has several limitations. Our study population was older adults with a mean age of 65 years, who may be particularly susceptible to heat due to impairments in thermoregulation, either age-related reduced sweating capacity or from medications that impair heat-sensing or increase the risk of heat stress (eg, diuretics). Thus, our findings may not be generalisable to younger populations of people, particularly children. Additionally, asthma and COPD diagnosis were self-reported in the parent trial and our study population had a relatively small proportion of people with an asthma or COPD diagnosis. This limited our ability to detect statistically significant differences in associations of temperature and lung function stratified by presence or absence of asthma or COPD. While we did not have information on air conditioner use, which may be an important intervention to mitigate the health risks related to heat, we did adjust for SVI, which includes housing characteristics in calculating the overall index score.

Conclusions

Short-term exposure to extreme high temperatures over the preceding 7 days was associated with lower lung function in older adults in the contiguous USA. As high temperature extremes become more common, identifying the respiratory health effects related to extreme heat and who may be at greater risk becomes more critical to inform interventions.
