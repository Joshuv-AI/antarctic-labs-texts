Deep learning–based artificial intelligence models predict survival in patients with oral cavity squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Yung Jee Kang, Yun Gon Lee, Myung Jin Chung, Junghyun Kim, Nayeon Choi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditional survival predictions for oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) rely on TNM staging, which lacks individualized prognostic value. Clinical factors such as performance status, age, sex, and lifestyle affect outcomes but are underrepresented in conventional models. This study applied artificial intelligence (AI) to integrate diverse factors for OSCC survival prediction. We retrospectively analyzed 1,018 OSCC patients surgically treated between 1996 and 2020. Variables included demographics, lifestyle, ASA classification, TNM stage, PET SUVmax, peri-neural and lympho-vascular invasion, extranodal extension, depth of invasion, resection margin, and treatment modalities. A deep neural network (DNN) for multi-group classification was developed and compared with regression-based DNN, Cox proportional hazards, and random survival forest models. To address class imbalance, least squares and multi-task learning were applied. Performance was assessed with concordance index and linearity testing. Death occurred in 18.1% of patients, with mean survival of 36.8 months. Recurrence occurred at 33 months. The DNN achieved an AUC of 0.922, sensitivity 0.514, specificity 0.992, and concordance index 0.888. Linearity testing confirmed strong correlation between predicted and observed outcomes. AI models integrating clinical variables provide more accurate OSCC survival predictions than conventional staging. The multi-group DNN is a promising tool for individualized prognosis and treatment planning.Keywords: Oral cancer, Survival, Prediction, Artificial intelligence, Deep neural networkSubject terms: Cancer, Medical research, Oncology, Risk factors

Introduction

Oral cancer is a growing global health concern, with a substantial number of cases reported each year1. Among the various types of oral cancers, oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) accounts for more than 90%2–4. OSCC is particularly concerning because its aggressiveness and ability to spread to other parts of the body produce high mortality and morbidity rates5,6. Despite advances in diagnostic tools and treatment strategies, the incidence of OSCC has continued to rise, further intensifying its global burden4.

The 5-year survival rate of OSCC is around 50%, and it varies depending on the patients’ tumor stage and various clinical factors7. Early diagnosis is critical for improving survival rates and reducing the morbidity associated with OSCC3,8,9. However, a significant proportion of patients is diagnosed at advanced stages, when the disease is more difficult to treat and survival outcomes are generally poor10,11. Whereas early-stage detection provided an 84% survival rate, late detection of cancer in stages III and IV showed a smaller survival rate to 39%9.

The 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system has become the standard for predicting survival in OSCC, and it offers reasonable accuracy12,13. However, staging discrepancies contribute to inaccurate prognostication in OSCC patient14, and it is known that analyzing with various clinical factor beyond TNM stage allows for a more accurate prediction of prognosis15. Various factors could affect the prognosis of OSCC. Previous studies reported that depth of invasion (DOI) and tumor thickness are significant prognostic factors16,17. Age, sex, drinking and smoking habits, tumor sites, cell differentiation, peri-neural invasion (PNI), lympho-vascular invasion (LVI), postoperative radiotherapy (RT), neck dissection, and recurrence can all influence the survival of OSCC patients18–21. Therefore, more precise tools are needed for survival prediction and individualized treatment planning to improve the clinical outcomes of OSCC patients.

Artificial intelligence (AI) enables early diagnosis, screening and prognosis prediction of OSCC22. AI could be used to reduce the high mortality rates associated with OSCC23,24. AI has been used to detect oral cancer25–27 and to predict recurrence28,29 and survival rates of oral cancer30,31. Combining epigenetic, gene expression and histologic signature well predicts 5-year mortality in OSCC patients32. Prognostic biomarkers such as genes related with relapse or recurrence of OSCC were identified in previous study33. Predicting OSCC tumor progression using disease staging and hematological indicators with machine learning models was also reported34. One study demonstrated that pathogenomics and AI-based techniques improve the accuracy of prognostic prediction in OSCC35. The complex combinations of variables that affect prognosis in OSCC require an AI-based predictive system to yield accurate results36. AI could have great potential for predicting the survival of OSCC patients by organizing diverse variables such as age, sex, smoking and alcohol consumption, and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification.

Cox proportional hazards (COX-PH), random survival forest (RSF), and deep neural network (DNN) can all be used for prediction of oncologic outcomes37. The COX-PH model is a conventional statistical tool widely used in medical research to examine the associations between survival time and predictor variables38. RSF models have been identified as an alternative to COX-PH when analyzing time-to-event data because they offer more flexibility in handling non-linear relationships39. A DNN, an advanced form of machine learning, consists of multiple layers that learn complex, non-linear relationships among variables40. DNN algorithms process input data through several hidden layers to transform and refine the output and improve prediction accuracy41.

For this study, we used AI algorithms to develop a survival prediction model for patients with OSCC. The clinical factors used for model development were all readily available and included age, sex, TNM staging, ASA classifications, treatment modalities, and recurrence patterns. By incorporating those variables into AI models, we sought to improve the accuracy of survival predictions and provide clinicians with a valuable tool for using individualized treatment strategies for OSCC patients. Our study has novelty in that it incorporated age, sex, TNM staging, ASA classifications, treatment modalities, and recurrence patterns to predict OSCC prognosis using an AI model. Also, our AI techniques are unique in the DNN model development.

Materials and methods

Patients

We conducted a retrospective review of the medical records of patients who received definitive surgical treatment for OSCC in a single tertiary hospital between July 1, 1996, and January 31, 2020. During that period, 1264 patients received treatment for OSCC, but we excluded patients lost to follow up and those who received further treatment at other hospitals. Therefore, we enrolled 1018 patients who received definitive surgical treatment for OSCC. This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of author’s institution (No. 2024-12-147). All research involving human participants was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB of our institution (No. 2024-12-147) due to its retrospective nature.

Pretreatment work-up, treatment, and follow-up

In their pre-treatment evaluations, the patients provided their medical history and received a physical examination, endoscopic examination, ultrasonography (USG), enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the neck, and positron emission tomography (PET)-CT. The primary tumor was pathologically diagnosed by biopsy under local or general anesthesia. When cervical lymph node metastasis was suspected, USG-guided fine needle aspiration or biopsy was performed. Surgical treatment was performed to eradicate the oral cavity tumor and neck diseases. In cases of cN0 patients, we performed the elective neck dissection or sentinel lymph node biopsy. In case of cN + patients, we performed the therapeutic neck dissection. Adjuvant therapy after surgery was performed based on the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines. As set out in institutional protocols, RT was performed mainly using the intensity modulated technique, and chemotherapy was performed most commonly by cisplatin-based regimen.

Patients visited the hospital at 3–6 month intervals for follow-up. At each visit, the patients provided their medical history and received a physical examination, endoscopic examination, and neck CT. Chest CT or PET-CT was performed 12–18 months after treatment completion.

Clinicopathologic features and outcome variables

We evaluated the clinical variables of age, sex, smoking and alcohol consumption, ASA classification, and tumor stage. Smoking and alcohol consumption were classified into current (lifetime consumption and within the previous one month), ex- (lifetime consumption but not within the previous one month), and never. Performance status was based on ASA classification.

Tumors were evaluated using the PET maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax), PNI, LVI, extra-nodal extension (ENE), DOI, and resection margin. Staging analysis was performed based on the AJCC 8th edition TNM staging manual. We restaged patients who were treated before the release of AJCC 8th edition using the AJCC 8th edition based on a comprehensive review of imaging and pathologic findings.

Treatment methods and oncologic outcomes were also collected. Treatment methods were categorized as surgery only, surgery with adjuvant RT, and surgery with concurrent chemoradiation therapy (CCRT). Recurrence and death were investigated, and recurrence was divided into local, regional, and distant. Times to recurrence and death were calculated from the start of surgical treatment. Survival analyses were performed with the Kaplan-Meier method to estimate recurrence-free survival (RFS), overall survival (OS), disease specific survival (DSS), locoregional RFS, and distant metastasis RFS using R package version 4.5.1 (The Comprehensive R Archive Network, http://www.R-project.org).

AI method

The dataset included a variety of clinical and pathological features such as smoker, alcohol consumption, ASA score, TNM stage, PET SUVmax (primary tumor), DOI, resection margin, WPOI (worst pattern of invasion), LVI, PNI, treatment methods, and recurrence-free survival. To handle missing data, a negative value of − 1 was applied to these features to explicitly indicate missing entries. Furthermore, the dataset was divided into numeric and categorical variables. The numeric columns included age, PET SUVmax, DOI, and recurrence-free survival. The categorical columns consisted of sex, smoker, alcohol, ASA, hypertension, diabetes, TNM stage, resection margin, WPOI, LVI, PNI, ENE, treatment methods, local, regional, distant, recur, and further resection. This distinction between numeric and categorical variables allowed for appropriate preprocessing strategies tailored to each data type.

In addition, we used one-hot encoding for categorical data to convert them into a multiple binary format that does not imply any ordinal relationship, which reduces bias and ensures compatibility with various machine learning algorithms42. The number of features increased from 28 to 71 after we applied one-hot encoding. The standard scaler is indispensable for the development of robust machine learning because it ensures that all the features contribute equally, improving convergence speed and simplifying model interpretation.

Feature importance is a technique used to identify features in a dataset with the most significant effect on the predictive performance of a model. In this study, we used permutation analysis to quantify the importance of each feature by measuring the change in model performance when the feature values were shuffled randomly. We also used a feature importance metric that combines both statistical error caused by the randomness of data splitting during five-fold cross-validation and mechanical error caused by systematic biases such as random numbers, iterations, scoring, and inaccuracies inherent in the modeling process. In our DNN, we performed feature elimination to identify variables that did not significantly influence the model.

The DNN model was developed with three layers to effectively capture the nonlinearity of the data. The SoftMax function was applied to the output layer to enable the model to infer the probability distribution across each class and we have divided it into 60 monthly intervals which facilitate multi-labels classification by assigning a probability value to each potential outcome. The performance of the DNN model for OSCC survival prediction was compared with a DNN regression model and traditional statistical methods and machine learning approaches (COX-PH and RSF models).

To optimize the hyper-parameters, such as number of layers, number of nodes, and batch size, we used a grid search with the least squares method (LSM) to systematically search through a specified subset of hyper-parameters to determine the optimal combination for the DNN model. Evaluation of AI models

To evaluate the performance of the AI models, a dataset comprising 1018 patients was prepared. A stratified 5-fold cross-validation was performed to evaluate the AI models; 80% of the dataset was prepared to train AI models, and the other 20% was prepared to validate the AI model. The validation set was randomly divided 5-fold, and an internal validation test was performed 5 times. In the finally developed AI model, evaluation was performed 5 times with the last 20% of the dataset separated for hold-out cross validation instead of external validation44.

A concordance index (C-index) was used to evaluate the performance of the AI-based survival period prediction models. The C-index is a goodness-of-fit measure for models that produce risk scores and is commonly used in survival analyses, particularly when dealing with censored data. One advantage of the DNN model is that it includes a module to directly obtain the C-index without the need to calculate risk scores.

In addition, we applied a linearity test, a technique derived from statistics, physics, and medical laboratory testing. A linearity test determines whether the test results are directly proportional to the concentration of the measurement (quantity to be measured) in a test sample. It is defined by the equation y = ax + b, where x and y denote the measurement and prediction, respectively, and a and b represent the slope and y-intercept. The linearity test is used to evaluate training or fit bias between the expected and measured values. In an ideal scenario, a = 1 and b = 045.

Overall survival prediction using AI models

We tested five OSCC overall survival prediction models: a multi-classification DNN, regression DNN, COX-PH, RSF, and DNN using only T/N staging. The overall performance of those five models is presented in Table 3. DNN with only T/N staging showed the poorest predictive performance, with no available linear slope or linearity-y-intercept. The C-index of the DNN with only T/N staging was 0.504 ± 0.007. On the other hand, the multi-classification DNN had the best predictive performance, with a micro-average area under the curve (AUC) of 0.922 ± 0.002, linearity slope of 0.948 ± 0.051, linearity-y-intercept of 2.820 ± 2.323, and C-index of 0.888 ± 0.007. The DNN with multi-group classification model had the best micro-average AUC, linearity slope and linearity-y-intercept (0.922 ± 0.002, multi-label sensitivity 0.514 ± 0.011, 0.948 ± 0.051, and 2.820 ± 2.323 respectively). We evaluated the performance of the other three AI models (DNN with regression, COX-PH, and RSF) to provide a comparison with the multi-class DNN model. Each model was trained and tested using a consistent dataset split, with 80% allocated for training and 20% allocated for testing. The DNN with regression had the second-best predictive performance, with a micro-average AUC of 0.610 ± 0.009, linearity slope of 0.944 ± 0.041, linearity-y-intercept of 2.205 ± 1.791, and C-index of 0.905 ± 0.007. The COX-PH and RSF models showed similar low predictive performance, with micro-average AUCs of 0.604 ± 0.005 and 0.602 ± 0.05, respectively; linearity slopes of 0.231 ± 0.065 and 0.028 ± 0.083; linearity-y-intercepts of 47.292 ± 3.317 and 52.141 ± 3.014, and C-indexes of 0.637 ± 0.022 and 0.557 ± 0.017. The C-indices of the multi-class DNN model and the regression DNN model were not statistically different. Among the four multi-classification models, DNN showed the best performance in an OSCC survival analysis. Statistical comparison of models against the DNN multi-classification baseline was represented in Table 4. The DNN multi-classification model significantly outperforms COX-PH and RSF in C-index, while it is slightly lower than DNN with regression in C-index but retains superior AUC and closer agreement between predicted and observed Kaplan-Meier curves, indicating better overall discrimination and calibration. Taken together, considering both concordance and discrimination, the DNN multi-classification model provided the most reliable performance.

Discussion

Our results show the value of using AI algorithms to build survival prediction models for patients with OSCC. To improve the performance of survival prediction models, we used clinical factors (age, sex, TNM staging, ASA classifications, treatment modalities, and recurrence patterns) to develop AI models. By incorporating those variables into our AI models, survival prediction improved compared with using only TNM staging. Our results indicate that the multi-classification DNN best predicted the overall survival of OSCC patients. Although the C-index was higher for the regression DNN than the multi-classification DNN, the micro-AUC, linearity slope, and linearity-y-intercept were superior for the multi-classification DNN. Because the multi-classification DNN better predicts the overall survival of OSCC patients, this AI model can be used in clinical practice to predict patient prognosis and provide patients with more accurate prognosis explanations. By incorporating various clinical variables into AI multi-classification DNN models, clinicians can provide a valuable tool for using individualized treatment strategies for OSCC patients.

While recent studies have utilized AI for the early diagnosis of OSCC46,47 and for predicting OSCC prognosis based on immune cells48–50 or biomarkers51, this study combined clinical data to create an AI model for OSCC prognosis prediction.

Time to recurrence, WPOI, alcohol and smoking history, PET SUVmax, old age, T staging, resection margin, and DOI are the top 10 most significant factors for survival of OSCC patients. Time to recurrence is the most powerful factor in survival and is associated with a poor survival prognosis52. Alcohol is more significant than smoking in our results. Although the PET SUVmax of the primary tumor was not a predictor of overall survival, disease-specific survival, or regional recurrence-free survival in a previous study53, our results show PET SUVmax to be one of the top 10 factors in the survival of OSCC patients. In head and neck cancer, PET SUVmax is used to predict diagnostic accuracy54. However, in lung cancer, PET SUVmax is commonly used to predict of disease recurrence or death55–59. As shown in our results, PET SUVmax can also predict survival in OSCC patients. Age is a known prognostic factor in OSCC60. Although T staging, DOI, and resection margins were significant factors in the survival of OSCC patients in our results, N staging was not. Some previous studies suggested that N staging was also a determinant factor in OSCC survival61,62. However, other studies reported that lymph node burden or number of metastatic lymph nodes rather than simple N staging were significant factors in survival63–65. Because we did not analyze the lymph node burden or the details of lymph node metastasis such as lymph node ratio or number of metastatic lymph nodes, we cannot speak to the relationship between lymph node burden and survival, but N staging did not significantly affect survival.

The five-year survival rates of OSCC countries are around 50%, and most patients survive a short time after diagnosis7 because most tumors are identified late, which compromises treatment, prognosis, and patient survival11. The prognosis of OSCC has not improved significantly in recent years, creating a persistent challenge in the biomedical field66,67.

OSCC has many risk factors, including tobacco and alcohol consumption, which are critical etiological factors for OSCC68. Moreover, chronic irritation, such as from sharp teeth or ill-fitting dentures, and a poor diet such as sausages and fried and spicy foods also cause OSCC69,70. Additionally, socioeconomic factors, such as poor oral hygiene and limited access to healthcare services, can affect early detection and treatment outcomes71. Although many risk factors are known, no survival prediction models consider all those factors.

Traditionally, cancer treatment depends mainly on tumor staging. However, staging discrepancies contribute to inaccurate prognostication in OSCC patients14. Despite the increasing number of prognostic markers, the overall prognosis of the disease has not changed significantly72. This could be due to the challenges of integrating all the markers into the current staging system73,74. Our study suggests that using AI to combine individualized clinical factors can produce more accurate survival prediction models. This is the major strength of our study.

Traditional statistical techniques, such as COX-PH, have been used to predict the survival of OSCC patients; however, they are inadequate under complex conditions. AI can assist in time-sensitive, critical clinical decision-making and decrease the chance of human error, but it requires large datasets and resources for analysis75. Regression DNNs have limitations in that their error range can be large due to the inherent difficulty of accurately predicting continuous values. The Cox model does not adequately model the nonlinear relationships among variables. Therefore, regression DNN and Cox models underperformed.

The role of AI in OSCC is various76. It can be used to diagnose oral cancer77 and predict occult node metastasis78,79. Using machine learning techniques, a web-based tool was developed to predict loco-regional recurrence and showed promising results because it could discern the complex relationships among variables in the dataset80. Also, the survival of OSCC patients can be predicted through AI14,81. A previous study tried to develop a novel prognostic algorithm for OSCC using a combination of pathogenomics and AI-based techniques.

Our study has limitations. First, this is a single-institution retrospective study with selection bias and no external validation. Early OSCC patients predominated in our study. Second, we did not categorize tumors by location in the oral cavity. Third, we did not consider gene expression, biological pathways, or biomarkers. Fourth, we did not incorporate the lymph node burden, such as the number of metastatic nodes. Fifth, there are issues of class imbalance handling, missing data imputation validity, and absence of biomarker/genomic data.

Despite those limitations, the study has the merit of using AI to incorporate a wide range of clinical factors to predict more precisely the survival of OSCC patients than is possible using conventional methods. Although the predictions of the different models were evaluated using a comprehensive dataset with missing data, both the multi-group classification and regression deep learning models demonstrated excellent predictive performance and reliability. Future studies incorporating genomic data and biomarkers into AI models is needed in future research. Categorizing tumor subsites and considering lymph node burden need to be included in further studies.
