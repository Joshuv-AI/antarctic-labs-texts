Long-term outcomes of S-1 and oxaliplatin neoadjuvant chemotherapy with total mesorectal excision and lateral lymph node dissection for rectal cancer
Authors: Hajime Morohashi, Yoshiyuki Sakamoto, Takuya Miura, Takuji Kagiya, Kentaro Sato, Shinji Tsutsumi, Seiji Takahashi, Yoshihito Nakayama, Hiroaki Tamba, Shuntaro Matsumoto, Daiki Kasai, Kenichi Hakamada
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeChemoradiotherapy (CRT) for rectal cancer is limited by its harmful side effects and its insufficient benefit on lateral lymph node metastases. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the long-term outcomes of S-1 and oxaliplatin with total mesorectal excision (TME) and lateral lymph node dissection (LLND) without radiation for rectal cancer.MethodsThe inclusion criteria were patients with stage II or III rectal cancer located within 10 cm from the anal verge. Fifty-two patients who underwent neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) followed by TME and LLND were evaluated. The primary endpoint was the 3-year local recurrence. The secondary endpoints were the 3-year rates of relapse-free survival and overall survival. Expected post-NAC and surgical outcomes were prospectively analyzed.ResultsThe overall recurrence rate was 15.4%, with eight patients developing distant recurrences. The local recurrence rate was 7.7% (n = 4). Among the 4 patients with local recurrence, 3 (5.8%) patients had central pelvis recurrence, and 1 (1.9%) patient had lateral pelvic recurrence. The 3-year survival rate was 98.1%, and the 3-year relapse-free survival rate was 84.6%. The risk factors for local recurrence were mucinous carcinoma (p = 0.016) and a positive resection margin (p = 0.009). Pathological mesorectal lymph node metastasis and local recurrence were independent risk factors for poor survival.ConclusionsAlthough preoperative chemotherapy and TME with LLND are sufficient to control local recurrence, some cases will require more aggressive chemotherapy with radiation therapy.

Background

Surgical resection is a crucial modality in the treatment of colorectal cancer. Resectable rectal cancer is primarily treated with total mesorectal excision (TME), in which lymphatic tissue and microvessels attached to the mesorectum are removed while still wrapped in the intrinsic fascia of the rectum [1]. Lymph node dissection along the inferior mesenteric artery and the internal iliac artery is also important for radical resection [2]. Lateral regional lymph node metastasis along the lymphatic flow to the side of the internal iliac artery occurs in 20.1% of lower rectal cancer cases (i.e., cT3 or cT4) [3], and thus, lateral lymph node metastasis should be addressed to improve treatment outcomes. As such, many centers in Japan traditionally use TME with lateral lymph node dissection (LLND) as the standard of care. In the Japan Clinical Oncology Group (JCOG) 0212 trial, LLND for lower rectal cancer decreased local recurrence and comparable postoperative complications to mesorectal excision without lateral dissection. However, lateral dissection also showed no survival advantage [4, 5].

It has been established that preoperative chemoradiotherapy (CRT) controls local recurrence of locally advanced rectal cancer, and thus, preoperative CRT has become the standard treatment modality for rectal cancer [6–8]. In lower rectal cancer, the recurrence rate is higher in positive cases with circumferential resection margins (CRM) less than 1 mm [9, 10]. The addition of CRT to TME is accepted as a major treatment strategy to ensure a CRM of at least 1 mm [11]. However, CRT has limited efficacy for lateral lymph node metastases, with the possibility of residual cancer in 9.3–28% of cases [12–14]. In addition, radiation-induced adverse events such as radiation dermatitis, proctitis, and diarrhea are a concern [15–18]. We previously hypothesized that the combination of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) with TME and LLND without radiotherapy would avoid the adverse events of radiotherapy and provide early control of local and distant metastasis. Accordingly, our previous clinical study using S-1 and oxaliplatin (SOX) with TME + LLND for rectal cancer in 2015 found a complete response rate of 19.2% and an R0 resection rate of 98.0% [19]. The purpose of the current study was to evaluate the long-term outcomes of SOX and TME with LLND.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

This prospective, multi-institutional phase II study enrolled 58 patients who underwent NAC followed by TME and bilateral LLND between October 2015 and October 2020 at four study institutions. A total of six patients were excluded: two patients who refused surgery after NAC, hoping for another therapy; one patient on whom LLD could not be performed because the tumor was located on the oral side of the peritoneal reflection; and three patients who had to drop out due to drug toxicity. In this study, we examined 52 patients who underwent TME and bilateral LLD after completing three cycles of SOX (Fig. 1). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status Score of 0–1; (2) pathologically confirmed rectal cancer; (3) tumor must be located < 10 cm from the anal verge; (4) the tumor must be categorized as cStage II–III; and (5) age 20–80 years. Table 1 summarizes the clinicopathological characteristics of the patients. Staging was according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer TNM classification system, 7th edition [20]. The preoperative stage was evaluated using diagnostic imaging such as multidetector-row computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), colonoscopy, and barium enemas.

Patient characteristics

Human ethics and consent to participate declarations

This study was approved by the Committee of Medical Ethics of Hirosaki University Hospital (approval number: 2015 − 118) as well as all other registered hospitals and was conducted according to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was registered with the Registry of Clinical Trials (jRCTs021180033) in Japan. First trial registration was 25/03/2019. The research volunteers were given oral explanations and gave consent in advance regarding the purpose, method, safety considerations, and risks of the experiment.Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy

Three courses of S-1 and oxaliplatin (SOX) NAC were administered before surgery. S-1 was administered orally at 80 mg/m2/day for 14 consecutive days, followed by a 7-day resting period. Oxaliplatin was given intravenously on the first day at a dose of 130 mg/m2/day. The duration of one cycle was considered to be 21 days, and NAC consisted of three cycles.

Treatment and surgical protocol

Three courses of SOX were administered preoperatively. Surgery was carried out between 21 and 56 days after SOX. Surgery always included ligation of the lower mesenteric artery, TME, and bilateral LLND. Lateral dissection was performed with internal vessel lymph node dissection and obturator lymph node dissection along the bilateral internal iliac artery and vein. The autonomic nerve, and obturator nerves were preserved, and the obturator artery and vein were dissected. The criteria for lateral metastasis is a lesion of 5 mm or greater. In cases where lateral lymph node metastasis was suspected on preoperative imaging and it was determined intraoperatively that metastatic nodes were in close proximity to the internal iliac arteries or veins, the vessels were removed together. However, in cases where no lymph node metastasis was found, the internal iliac artery and the internal iliac vein were preserved.

Adjuvant therapy

In cases of recurrence, S-1 or uracil-tegafur plus leucovorin therapy is initiated within 8 weeks after surgery; at least 3 weeks would have elapsed since adequate oral intake. However, depending on the postoperative stage, the attending physician may permit the addition of L-OHP or the absence of adjuvant chemotherapy. In principle, postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy is administered for 6 months.

End points and statistical analysis

The primary endpoint was the 3-year local recurrence rate. The secondary endpoints were the 3-year rates of relapse-free survival and overall survival. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Univariate analysis was performed using Pearson’s Chi-squared test, while multivariate prognostic analysis was performed using the Cox proportional hazard model. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 29 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

The median patient age was 63 years, and 75% of the patients were male. The median body mass index was 21.4 kg/m2. In total, 65% underwent laparoscopic surgery, while 35% underwent robotic surgery (Table 1). The rate of lateral lymph node involvement after surgery was 11.5%. The rate of pathologically complete resection was 98.1% [19].

The overall recurrence rate was 15.4%, with 8 patients developing distant recurrences. The local recurrence rate was 7.7% (n = 4) (Fig. 2). Among the 4 patients with local recurrence, 3 (5.8%) and 1 (1.9%) patient had recurrence in the central and lateral pelvis, respectively. The median observation period was 52 months (range: 36–81 months). The 3-year survival rate was 98.1% (Fig. 3), and the 3-year relapse-free survival rate was 84.6% (Fig. 4). Pathologic lateral lymph node metastasis occurred in 6 (11.5%) patients. One of them developed relapsed and died, while 5 (83%) patients survived without recurrence. Table 2 shows the risk factors of local recurrence identified on univariate and multivariate analyses. The risk factors for local recurrence were mucinous carcinoma not adenocarcinoma (p = 0.016) and a positive resection margin (p = 0.009). Table 3 shows the prognostic factors on univariate and multivariate analysis. Pathological mesorectal lymph node metastasis was found to be an independent risk factor for poor survival (p = 0.033).

Discussion

In this study, among patients who underwent NAC followed by TME and LLND, local recurrence in the lateral pelvis constituted 1.9% (n = 1 patient), and local recurrences in the central pelvis came to 5.8% (n = 3 patients). Advanced rectal cancer has a worse prognosis than colon cancer, which is influenced by the higher incidence of local recurrence in the former [21]. Without adequate preoperative treatment, the local recurrence rate is as high as approximately 20% [22, 23]. Local recurrence can occur in the central pelvis around the primary tumor or in the lateral pelvis in the lymph nodes of the internal iliac vascular region. One study showed preoperative radiation therapy reduced the local recurrence to 2.4% [8]. However, many reports refer only to the central pelvis in defining local recurrence, and thus, it is difficult to make a general comparison. In the present study, preoperative chemotherapy and lateral dissection reduced recurrence to the lateral pelvis to 1.9% and provided adequate control of lateral lymph node recurrence. However, recurrence to the central pelvis was observed in 5.8% of patients, and improvements are needed to further reduce the recurrence rate in this site. Patients with recurrence in the central pelvis require more aggressive preoperative treatment or an extended resection with concomitant resection of other organs. In this study, poorly differentiated histology and failure of R0 resection were identified as risk factors for local recurrence. Patients with local recurrence showed no shrinkage on MRI after NAC and were characterized by a pathologic response rate of grade 1.Patients with poorly differentiated histology may be less likely to respond to chemotherapy and CRT, so it has been suggested that mucinous carcinoma may not have a better response to CRT than non-mucinous carcinoma [24]. For cases in which CRM is expected to be difficult to achieve by conventional rectal resection alone, selective CRT would be more effective in improving local control while preserving organ function as much as possible for cases in which CRT is effective. If it is still difficult to obtain sufficient CRM, an extended surgery with a total pelvic exenteration that requires the combined resection of other organs should be planned after preoperative treatment.

To control local recurrence, preoperative CRT has become the standard of care worldwide. The problem with CRT, however, is that its effect on lateral lymph node metastases is limited, and it has not demonstrated significant survival improvements [12–14]. The key points in the treatment strategy of this study were to avoid adverse events from preoperative radiotherapy and to completely resect by lateral lymph node dissection all lateral lymph node metastases that are uncertain to respond to radiotherapy [12–14]. Avoiding radiation has significant benefits because adverse events from radiation are not only limited to short-term complications, but also include long-term complications including radiation enteritis and radiation-related carcinogenesis in the late stages. Meanwhile, LLND carries a risk of urinary and sexual dysfunction. However, autonomic neuropathy can be avoided by preserving the autonomic nervous system and avoiding nerve damage intraoperatively [4, 25]. The present study found that although the rate of neurogenic bladder was 7.7%, temporary symptoms in all cases were addressed, and all patients recovered after receiving medication [19]. Minimally invasive procedures, i.e., laparoscopy or robotic surgery are useful for autonomic preservation surgery, and the fact that surgery was performed entirely under an endoscopic view may have helped to avoid nerve complications.

In this study, the completion rate of preoperative chemotherapy was 94.8%, and all patients with recurrence risk factor were able to receive adjuvant chemotherapy postoperatively. These results suggest that preoperative chemotherapy may result in high treatment compliance. However, although adjuvant chemotherapy after preoperative radiotherapy has been reported to improve prognosis for rectal cancer located 10–15 cm from the anal verge [26, 27], postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy is difficult to administer in sufficient doses due to patient refusal and complications [28, 29]. The advantage of preoperative chemotherapy is that it is effective against systemic micrometastases during the early stage of treatment, and the increase in treatment compliance in turn increases the effectiveness of treatment. We selected the SOX regimen as it was reported to be noninferior to mFOLFOX6 with a higher response rate [30]. In addition, SOX regimens are the best treatment for neoadjuvant chemotherapy because this is a regimen that does not require an infusion pump.

Pathological mesorectal lymph node metastasis was found to be an independent risk factor for poorer survival. All patients with lateral lymph node metastasis who underwent NAC followed by LLND had long-term survival, and lateral lymph node metastasis was not a prognostic factor. In the JCOG0212 study, which was performed on subjects with a low likelihood of lateral lymph node metastasis, bilateral prophylactic lateral lymph node dissection with or without metastases achieved control of local recurrence, but did not improve prognosis [4]. However, there are no reports of large clinical trials demonstrating the efficacy of lateral dissection in patients with clinically evident lateral lymph node metastases. Although cases of lateral lymph node metastasis are expected to have a high likelihood of local or distant recurrence, the present examination of prognostic factors suggests that both systemic control and local control were achieved by NAC in combination with resection. An analysis of prognostic factors in lateral lymph node metastasis reported that dissection was more effective for cases in whom ≤ 2 lymph nodes in one side were involved and in whom positive nodes were limited to the internal iliac lymph node vascular area [31]. Some reports suggest that LLND dissection is more beneficial than dissection of the lymph nodes along the root of the inferior mesenteric artery [32]. Patients with lateral lymph node metastases have a significantly better prognosis than those with distant metastases [33].

It is useful to perform resection for lateral lymph node metastases, but in terms of metastatic risk factors, preoperative accurate diagnosis of lateral lymph node metastasis is challenging. Although magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has been reported to be useful for predicting lateral lymph node metastasis in lymph nodes measuring ≥ 5 mm [34], but it is difficult to determine lymph node metastasis by MRI alone because it yields many false negatives and false positives; MRI is inaccurate as a preoperative diagnosis of lateral lymph node metastasis. Although prophylactic lateral lymph node dissection was performed in this study, there are negative attitudes toward prophylactic lateral dissection [35–37]. A recent study reported that machine learning using MRI has 90.0% sensitivity and 79.4% specificity, respectively, for predicting lateral lymph node metastasis [38]. Thus, artificial intelligence-based diagnostic methods are expected to increase the diagnostic rate and ability for lateral lymph node metastasis. If lateral lymph node metastasis can be accurately diagnosed preoperatively, selective lateral dissection, whereby metastatic lymph nodes are resected only in cases of lateral lymph node metastasis, may be a viable option in the future [12, 13].

Although some cases of lateral lymph node metastasis can be cured, distant metastasis may occur during recurrence [39]. Therefore, it is also important to add effective systemic chemotherapy strategies, with consideration of the possibility of systemic micrometastasis, to improve the prognosis of patients with lateral lymph node metastases. Total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT) combined with preoperative induction chemotherapy or consolidation chemotherapy and CRT have been reported to achieve a high pathologic complete response rate of 39% and significantly fewer distant metastases [40–42]. For T4 cases and cases of locally advanced rectal cancer in which it is difficult to secure a CRM of 1 mm, TNT is performed preoperatively, and the addition of TME and lateral lymph node dissection is expected to further improve prognosis.

While there are reports of difficulty in suppressing local recurrence in cases of NAC alone without radiation therapy [43, 44], the non-inferiority of preoperative chemotherapy to preoperative chemoradiotherapy was reported for cases excluding local recurrence risk as occurs in cT4, N2, CRM(+), and anorectal non-sparing surgery [45]. It is suggested that preoperative treatment without radiation therapy may be an option after patient selection.

The present study has some limitations. First, the patient population was small, especially with regard to R1, 2 resections, the number of cases of local recurrence or tumor histology without adenocarcinoma, and the fact that this was a single-arm phase II study. In addition, we did not compare NAC with CRT, TNT, or molecular targeted drugs. Future studies should evaluate the benefit of preoperative chemotherapy with TME and LLND in patients who require more aggressive preoperative treatment or more extensive surgery.

Conclusion

Preoperative chemotherapy with TME and LLND is useful for avoiding adverse radiation events and improving prognosis in rectal cancer. Although preoperative chemotherapy and TME with LLND are sufficient to control local recurrence, some cases will require more aggressive chemotherapy with radiation therapy.
