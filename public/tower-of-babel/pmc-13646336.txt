Beyond the CE Mark: A Call to Action for Minimum CGM Accuracy Standards in Central and Eastern European Nations
Authors: Tomasz Klupa, Silvija Canecki‐Varžić, Adriana Ilavská, Andrej Janez, Sanja Klobučar, Mladen Krnić, Tatjana Milenkovic, Peter Novodvorský, Martin Prázný, Marko Simunovic, Jan Šoupal, Anca Pantea Stoian, Agnieszka Szadkowska, Tsvetelina Tankova, Tadej Battelino
Published: 2026

Introduction

For people with diabetes, glucose monitoring is an essential component of daily diabetes self‐management that aids in early detection and limiting of hyperglycemia, related to severe chronic complications and avoidance of hypoglycemia [1]. In European healthcare services, continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) has become the standard of care for people with type 1 diabetes (T1D) and is increasingly recommended in guidance for the glycemic management of people with type 2 diabetes (T2D) treated with insulin, and for those on any therapy [2, 3]. The European region carries the largest absolute burden of T1D worldwide, estimated at 2.7 million individuals, and an overall diabetes prevalence of 9.8% affecting some 66 million adults, projected to increase by 10% by 2050 [4]. The incidence of T1D is rising in south‐eastern and north‐eastern Europe, whereas rates in north‐western Europe are in decline [5]. In parallel, the earlier onset of T2D across the nations in Central and Eastern Europe (CEE) represented by the author group is widening the population eligible for insulin therapy, emphasising the need for accurate CGM glucose data for safe insulin dosing. The accuracy and performance of CGM devices entering these markets are therefore critical determinants of population‐level safety.

Most of the growing number of CGM devices introduced into the European marketplace have Conformité Européenne (CE) marks and claims for nonadjunctive use (i.e., not requiring a confirmatory blood‐glucose test reading for diabetes‐related treatment decisions, including insulin dosing or management of hypoglycemia). However, this proliferation of devices has come with concerns that some have lower accuracy and performance metrics and may lack safety when used by people with diabetes. A recent publication has detailed examples of such issues [6], including a case from Italy in which a CE marked CGM device was found to compromise patient safety due to poor accuracy at low and high glucose levels, a case from the UK where a CE‐marked hybrid closed loop (HCL) system was shown to lack accuracy, and instances where CGM systems were marketed for use in children with T1D, despite the absence of any data in this population. The limitations of relying on CE marking as evidence of clinical suitability have prompted European diabetes experts to call for CGM devices in Europe to meet more rigorous minimum standards for accuracy and performance than those required for the award of a CE mark [7]. Specifically, experts advocated alignment with the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) integrated CGM (iCGM) criteria [8], which establish transparent and clinically relevant performance benchmarks for devices intended to guide therapeutic decisions, although standardised study design guidance is not included.

This issue is particularly relevant to healthcare systems in CEE nations, where reimbursement and procurement decisions may be made through centralised bidding and contracting processes that frequently rely on CE certification as the principal indicator of device quality. Because errors in CGM glucose measurements directly impact therapeutic decisions, the regulation of CGM accuracy should be recognised as a patient safety issue as much as a performance issue [7, 8, 9]. This is amplified by the growing adoption of automated insulin delivery (AID) systems, in which CGM sensor glucose values directly influence insulin dosing algorithms, meaning inaccuracies may be directly propagated into therapeutic decisions. Consequently, minimum quality standards for CGM devices are increasingly relevant [9, 10, 11]. The clinical implications are even greater in vulnerable populations such as children, older adults, and pregnant women.

In this call to action, we highlight the potential limitations of relying solely on CE marking when selecting CGM technologies for diabetes care and explore the implications of this approach for healthcare systems in CEE nations. In our opinion, taking action at the level of national diabetes clinical guidelines and standards of care may be a practical and effective way to strengthen patient safety and support evidence‐based procurement and reimbursement decisions. By incorporating transparent minimum performance expectations for CGM systems, such frameworks could help ensure that people with diabetes have access to technologies that meet clinically meaningful standards of accuracy, reliability and safety.

Limitations of the European Regulatory Landscape for Medical Technologies

Medical device regulation (MDR) in Europe is covered by MDR 2017/745, introduced in May 2021 to harmonise the process for placing medical technologies on the European market, to strengthen evaluation of patient safety and to increase transparency of the market authorisation procedure that precedes the award of the CE mark. MDR 2017/745 is also intended to improve the traceability of medical devices and to guarantee stronger premarketing and postmarketing oversight.

CGM devices are designated as Class IIb medical devices and must undergo a Conformity Assessment process and the award of a CE mark, before being marketed in Europe. This process is initiated by the CGM manufacturer, who is responsible for describing the intended purpose of all components of the candidate device in line with definitions set out in MDR 2017/745, which also specifies the requirements for preclinical and clinical evidence. Following the award of a CE mark, all components of the Conformity Assessment process should be available and accessible via the European Database on Medical Devices (EUDAMED), the centralised EU platform for medical devices, established under the MDR 2017/745. The manufacturer is also responsible for the selection and contracting of a Notified Body to undertake the Conformity Assessment and to recommend CE certification or not.

This is where the transparency of the Conformity Assessment process reveals its limitations. Although MDR 2017/745 requires an Expert Panel for Conformity Assessment of Class III medical devices deemed high‐risk, CGM systems are classified as medium‐high‐risk Class IIb devices and do not meet the criteria for an Expert Panel assessment (Box 1). MDR 2017/745 considers CGM devices to be well‐characterised, with extensive clinical evidence and standards, and therefore not requiring additional scrutiny. The assessment of clinical evidence in support of the accuracy and performance characteristics of a CGM device, and its overall risk profile, is undertaken by the Notified Body. This is done through a review of the Technical Documentation provided by the manufacturer in alignment with their own Quality Management System (QMS). The task of the Notified Body is to audit the components within the QMS framework and assess whether they adequately describe how the CGM has been designed and tested, both technically and clinically, and whether the individuals who performed this work were qualified to do so.

Both the Technical Documentation and the QMS should comply with the manufacturer's obligations under MDR 2017/745. For a CGM device, preclinical and clinical data can include studies conducted in vitro, in animals, and in humans. However, MDR 2017/745 does not specify whether preclinical and clinical data submitted in a Clinical Evaluation Report (CER) within the Technical Documentation must be publicly accessible, or which type of study design should have been performed in evaluating the performance of the CGM system [12, 13]. More importantly, the data included in the CER or elsewhere in the Technical Documentation does not necessarily include data from investigations on the candidate CGM but may be sourced from clinical evidence involving so‐called ‘equivalent devices’ already marketed or from the published scientific literature on equivalent devices (Box 1). Although the MDR requires that ‘equivalence’ reflects that the comparator device has sufficiently matching technical, biological, and clinical characteristics that any differences create no clinically significant difference in safety or clinical performance, there is no requirement for the ‘equivalent data’ to be disclosed for scrutiny and the full documentation on an equivalent device may not be fully provided, depending on the source of the equivalent device. The lack of detail about the study design undertaken and submitted by the manufacturer has previously been raised as a critical flaw in the process [14, 15].

A further issue from a European perspective is that the implementation of EUDAMED, intended to be fully operational before the May 2021 application date for MDR 2017/745, has experienced significant delays and mandatory use across the complete system is not yet possible (Box 1). Significantly, the elements that require registration of clinical studies and those that are dedicated to postmarket vigilance and surveillance are not expected to become mandatory until late 2027. Together, these issues create the potential for a loss of transparency within the provisions of MDR 2017/745 for CGM device Conformity Assessment.

These limitations become more obvious when the current European regulatory framework is compared with those in other major health care systems. In the United Kingdom (UK), following its exit from the European Union, the Medicines & Healthcare Regulatory Agency (MHRA) has initiated reforms to improve transparency, postmarket surveillance and evidence requirements for medical technologies [16]. The main changes involve more prescriptive postmarket surveillance and data‐collection plans, along with enhanced reporting of serious incidents and trends. More transparency of the manufacturer's clinical investigation report is proposed, but the final scope of this has not yet been determined. CE‐marked devices will still be granted access to the UK marketplace until June 2030, but the changes illustrate a shift toward more rigorous regulatory oversight of digital diabetes technologies and support the argument that a CE mark alone is not a valid proxy for the accuracy and performance of a CGM system [16, 17]. The UK reforms are aligned with the wider international trend toward increased oversight of digital health technologies and devices that directly impact therapeutic decision‐making, such as the FDA‐mandated iCGM requirements [8]. Since 2025, the European Commission itself has adopted Health Technology Assessment (HTA) regulation 2021/2282 [18], which establishes a legally binding system for cooperation on HTA requirements, including joint clinical assessments of medical technologies and the quality and limitations of the evidence in each case. Ideally, future changes to EU MDR should introduce predefined and transparent criteria that can also be modified to keep pace with the state of the art, which continues to advance rapidly. At this point, Europe remains the only major regulatory region where CGM can be authorised for marketing as a medical device in the absence of publicly available, harmonised performance requirements specifically tailored to CGM technologies [19].

Clinical Guidelines Can Specify CGM Device Accuracy and Performance Requirements

Currently, the presence of a CE mark can be misinterpreted as a hallmark of a CGM device being safe, accurate, and effective. Similarly, the accuracy of CGM devices is often promoted by quoting the mean absolute relative difference (MARD) [20] between CGM glucose readings and a series of blood glucose reference samples taken at the same time. A MARD of less than 10% is often promoted as a safe threshold for therapeutic decision‐making without SMBG confirmation, but this key metric should never be used in isolation as an indicator of clinical performance [20, 21, 22], since this does not necessarily reveal problems with accuracy at low or high glucose readings, or when glucose levels are changing [20, 23]. No single accuracy metric can fully reflect clinical utility, sensor durability, connectivity issues, data continuity and batch‐to‐batch consistency, all of which are important determinants of real‐world performance.

Publicly available evidence regarding the impact of clinically relevant interfering substances should also be considered a prerequisite for independent assessment of CGM performance, particularly as interference profiles may differ substantially between technologies, particularly for commonly encountered substances such as vitamin C, paracetamol (acetaminophen), hydroxyurea and other compounds known to affect sensor performance. That the combination of a CE mark and a low MARD value has become shorthand for CGM accuracy within the European diabetes healthcare environment is an important issue with implications for patient safety that needs to be addressed and is unlikely to be resolved through changes to MDR 2017/745.

One strategy to ensure that healthcare services in CEE countries provide access only to CGM devices that meet the minimum expected accuracy and performance standards is to enshrine this in expert diabetes clinical guidelines. The attributes of a CGM device that meet minimum standards for accuracy and performance can be assessed using several indicators. One important indicator is whether a device has been evaluated under regulatory frameworks with predefined, transparent performance requirements, such as the FDA's integrated continuous glucose monitoring (iCGM) pathway [8]. Currently, these include the following CGM devices: Dexcom G6 and G7 sensors; FreeStyle Libre 2, Libre 2 Plus and FreeStyle Libre 3 Plus sensors; and Senseonics Eversense. FDA approval under the iCGM pathway should not be regarded as the only acceptable pathway, but rather as one example of a transparent and clinically meaningful framework for evaluating CGM performance. Devices without an existing FDA approval may be assessed by guideline committees based on criteria that extend beyond the requirements of MDR 2017/745. These include: accuracy and performance data must be publicly available from studies that meet all predefined study design criteria; sufficient data pairs have been collected at all glucose ranges and at differing rates of change; data have been collected from all intended use populations and ages; performance is consistent with existing guidelines and recommendations from international expert groups [15]. Particular attention should be paid to paediatric populations, where independent evidence of sensor performance is often limited despite widespread clinical use. For example, although they underwent conformity assessment under the previous Medical Devices Directive 93/42/EEC, two CE‐marked devices have been licensed in Europe for paediatric use in T1D, despite the absence of peer‐reviewed studies in the paediatric population [24], or with peer‐reviewed data acquired in only eight adults [25]. Devices with legacy marketing approval under MDD 93/42/EEC would need to complete conformity assessment under MDR 2017/745 by 31 December 2028 to maintain their CE mark. In the 2026 clinical recommendations for the management of people with diabetes, authored by the Polish Diabetes Association, the necessary quality standards for CGM are presented in Section 3 of the clinical recommendations in a table that unambiguously states the 10 minimum requirements for accuracy and performance of a CGM device for use in people with diabetes in Poland [26]. Importantly, the Polish Diabetes Association has implemented a fully transparent process for device assessment and endorsement, including a provision allowing manufacturers to update their submissions and provide additional evidence when necessary. The Polish initiative is consistent with a wider international movement to standardise the accuracy and performance evaluation of CGM systems, reflected in expert consensus statements from Europe [7], Latin America [27] and the Asia‐Pacific region [28], and in the proposed minimum requirements and acceptance criteria advanced by the IFCC Working Group on CGM [15]. Guideline committees in CEE nations adopting an equivalent standard would therefore be aligning their national recommendations with an emerging global consensus on what constitutes a fit‐for‐purpose CGM device, rather than imposing an isolated or arbitrary national requirement.

Call‐to‐Action

Based on the available examples and the level of concern expressed by key stakeholders, we believe that the application of MDR 2017/745 in Europe does not exclude CGM devices with poor accuracy and performance characteristics. This is a function of risk‐determination criteria that exclude expert clinical review from the Conformity Assessment and of the lack of transparency regarding which clinical evaluation data have been included in the Technical Documentation reviewed by the Notified Body. In order to ensure that people with diabetes are provided with CGM devices that meet minimum standards for accuracy and performance, we believe strongly that professional diabetes clinical associations within CEE national healthcare services have the responsibility to explicitly specify the required attributes of the CGM devices that should be provided to people with diabetes within the scope of national clinical guidelines. By doing so, this can encourage manufacturers to ensure that the data on their own CGM devices are provided for review, to meet the clinical needs described.

A clear, consistent, and unequivocal recommendation from clinicians for the immediate implementation of minimum quality and clinical performance standards for CGM systems by national health authorities is a fundamental obligation toward people with diabetes using these technologies. Such measures are essential, not only to ensure safety and reliability in current diabetes management, but also to safeguard future standards of care in light of the rapidly expanding role of advanced technologies in diabetes care. Consequently, we call on the national diabetes associations of the CEE states to adopt, within the next guideline cycle, an explicit and publicly stated table of minimum accuracy and performance requirements for reimbursable CGM systems, modelled on the standard already set out by the Polish Diabetes Association, [26] and to commit to reviewing these criteria annually as the evidence base and device landscape evolve.

Conclusions

The rapid advances in diabetes technologies, including CGM and AID systems, has outpaced the applicable MDR regulatory framework in Europe. There is an unmet need for robust and transparent performance standards to ensure the safety of people with diabetes who increasingly have access these technologies. Discussion of CGM regulation must now move beyond market access and increasingly centre on clinical accountability. Until this need for improved technology regulation is delivered via a robust European regulatory framework, it is incumbent on national healthcare systems and professional diabetes societies to work independently and together to ensure that technologies used in routine diabetes care meet transparent, evidence‐based and clinically meaningful performance standards.
