The Experience of Social Alienation in Patients with Chronic Cancer-Related Pain: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Yeyu Xie, Xiuxiu Song, Dan Zhang, Qiongqiong Zang, Jingyuan Song, Yuqin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aims to explore the lived experience of social alienation in patients with chronic cancer-related pain (CCRP), identify its underlying drivers, delineate their unmet multidimensional needs, and establish an evidence base for precise psychosocial intervention strategies.Patients and MethodsBetween February and March 2026, 22 patients with CCRP were selected using purposive sampling from a tertiary hospital in Xuzhou, Jiangsu Province, China, to participate in semi-structured, in-depth interviews. Data were analyzed using Colaizzi 7-step method.ResultsFive main themes emerged as the underlying drivers of social alienation: Pain-Induced Confinement, Cognitive-Affective Overload, Relational Misalignment and Absence, Pain Concealment, and Attentional Competition. These drivers interact dynamically to form a vicious cycle of social alienation and pain amplification, with attentional competition functioning as the underlying mechanism. Furthermore, corresponding unmet multidimensional needs were mapped to each driver, highlighting critical gaps such as dignified mobility assistance, destigmatization support, and caregiver empathy training.ConclusionSocial alienation in patients with CCRP is a complex, dynamic process driven by factors including pain-induced confinement, cognitive-affective overload, and relational misalignment and absence. Effective pain management must transcend the biomedical paradigm to incorporate holistic psychosocial reconstruction, family empowerment, and the restoration of social connectivity to overcome underlying pain management barriers.Keywords: pain management, social isolation, phenomenology, psycho-oncology

Introduction

Cancer, as the second leading cause of death globally, poses a severe public health challenge.1 According to GLOBOCAN data, there were nearly 20 million new cancer cases worldwide in 2022.2,3 With advancements in diagnostic and therapeutic technologies, alongside improved early screening strategies, cancer survival rates have significantly increased, leading many types of cancer to be managed as chronic diseases.4 Despite this prolonged survival, patients continue to face persistent health challenges, among which pain is one of the most prevalent and distressing symptoms. Chronic cancer-related pain (CCRP) refers to pain caused by the primary tumor, metastases, or cancer treatments (eg, surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy) that typically persists or recurs for more than three months.5 A meta-analysis indicated that the prevalence of cancer pain is 55.0% during anti-tumor treatment, 39.3% after curative treatment, and up to 66.4% in advanced, metastatic, or terminal stages, with 38.0% of patients reporting moderate to severe pain.6 CCRP entails not only persistent physical discomfort but also severe psychological distress, significantly impairing patients’ social functioning and frequently precipitating social alienation.7,8

Social alienation refers to the weakening or detachment of a patient’s interpersonal relationships. Subjectively, it manifests as negative emotional experiences, such as loneliness and helplessness; objectively, it presents through behavioral characteristics, including social avoidance and a diminished social network.9 Due to physical limitations, patients with CCRP often struggle to participate in social activities.10 Beyond physical suffering, CCRP also triggers negative emotions such as anxiety and depression. These psychological factors, along with cognitive burdens including stigma and low self-worth, further undermine patients’ motivation for social interaction.10–13 Moreover, pain-related communication difficulties, combined with potential societal misconceptions about cancer, contribute to a reduction in actual social support received by patients.14–17 The synergistic effect of these risk factors predisposes patients with CCRP to social alienation. Once established, social alienation diminishes social support, reinforces negative emotions and cognitions, and in turn exacerbates pain perception. This process may ultimately heighten barriers to pain management and reduce motivation for recovery, perpetuating a vicious cycle.18–20

Several quantitative studies have documented a strong link between chronic pain and social alienation, suggesting a complex and reciprocal relationship between the two.21,22 Yet social alienation does not occur immediately at pain onset; instead, it develops through a gradual and dynamic process. Although existing qualitative research has extensively explored social alienation in the context of specific cancer types or treatments,9,23–25 it remains limited in fully explaining the psychological distress and interpersonal struggles patients with CCRP experience during prolonged pain, prior to eventually becoming socially alienated. Therefore, this study adopts a phenomenological design to investigate the lived experiences of patients with CCRP. By clarifying the developmental process and real-life experiences underlying social alienation, it seeks to identify unmet multidimensional needs and offer evidence to support clinicians in designing tailored psychosocial interventions.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and the Participants

A descriptive phenomenological study design was adopted to explore the lived experience of social alienation in patients with CCRP. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (Decision number: XYFY2026-KL001-01) and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Trial number: ChiCTR2600119190). This study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The study was carried out in the departments of general surgery and oncology of the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University between February and March 2026. After admission, patients were screened by research assistants, and those who met the inclusion criteria were further recruited. All participants were informed of the study purpose, data collection methods, data usage, and potential risks before the study commenced, and they provided written informed consent, which included permission for the publication of anonymized responses and direct quotes. Participation was entirely voluntary, and participants had the right to withdraw from the study at any stage without providing any reason. To protect participant privacy, all interview data were de-identified. The data were stored on encrypted devices, accessible only to the research team, and were scheduled to be securely destroyed after the study was completed in accordance with relevant regulations.

Using purposive sampling, we selected patients with CCRP based on the principle of maximum variation regarding gender, age, education, marital status, career, current treatment, tumor site, pain duration, and pain intensity. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a pathological diagnosis of cancer; (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) self-reported persistent or recurrent pain lasting > 3 months, clinically confirmed to originate directly from the cancer or cancer-related treatments; (4) fully conscious with normal cognitive and language functions; (5) awareness of their cancer diagnosis; and (6) provision of informed consent with voluntary participation. The exclusion criteria were: (1) pain caused by other comorbidities; (2) severe systemic complications or critical illness; and (3) a history of psychiatric or psychological disorders. The final sample size was determined by data saturation. After collecting interview data from 20 patients, no new information emerged. To verify that data saturation was truly achieved, two additional interviews were conducted. As no new themes were identified, interview recruitment was concluded. Consequently, a total of 22 respondents voluntarily participated in and completed this study.

Formulation of the Interview Outline

An initial interview guide was developed based on an extensive literature review and the study aims. A pilot study involving two patients was conducted to test and refine the questions. Following expert consultations with clinical oncology nurses, pain management specialists, and psychologists, the research team reached consensus on the final interview guide. To build rapport, researchers initiated each interview with an ice-breaker question: “How has your pain been lately? Could you tell me a little about it?” The core questions were as follows: (1) Could you describe the changes in your daily life and psychological state since the onset of CCRP? How have these emotional and cognitive changes influenced your willingness to engage socially? (2) How have your social interactions with family, friends, and colleagues changed since the pain began? (3) Have you experienced any shifts in your attitude toward socializing? For instance, have you ever felt the desire to withdraw from social situations, and what were the underlying reasons? (4) How do you communicate the discomfort caused by your pain to those around you? Could you provide a specific example? (5) What kind of support or assistance have you received from your family, partner, friends, and healthcare providers since experiencing this pain? (6) What is the greatest difficulty or barrier you currently face when participating in social activities or interacting with others? Is there any specific thought or feeling you would like to share? (7) Could you describe how your experience of pain might differ when you are spending time with family or friends, compared to when you are resting alone?

Data Collection and Quality Control

Two graduate students trained in qualitative research methodology and with specialized knowledge in oncology and pain management conducted the semi-structured, face-to-face interviews. One student led the interview while the other observed and took field notes. The interviews took place in a quiet, private room free from distractions, with each session lasting 20 to 40 minutes. Before each interview, the researchers explained the study’s purpose, methodology, and content to the participants. Written informed consent was obtained, and the entire interview process was audio-recorded. During the sessions, the interviewer followed the interview guide and practiced active listening without providing leading prompts or suggestions, encouraging participants to share their genuine experiences and feelings. The observing researcher objectively documented the participants’ tone of voice, emotions, facial expressions, and body language. To ensure data accuracy and authenticity, the interviewers promptly clarified any ambiguous statements and maintained detailed records of the actual interview content.

Data Analysis

Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim by the primary interviewer within 24 hours of each session and cross-verified by the second researcher to ensure accuracy and completeness. Data were analyzed using Colaizzi 7-step method: (1) reading and re-reading the transcripts to familiarize with the data; (2) extracting significant statements related to participants’ social alienation; (3) formulating meanings and assigning codes to recurring statements; (4) aggregating coded information to identify common characteristics or concepts, thereby organizing them into themes; (5) exhaustively describing the formulated themes; (6) comparing similar concepts to finalize the overarching themes; and (7) conducting member checking by returning the findings to the participants for validation. Any discrepancies between the two researchers during the analytical process were resolved through discussion within the research team until a consensus was reached.26

Themes, Subthemes, and Unmet Multidimensional Needs

Theme 1: Pain-Induced Confinement

Our findings revealed that worsening pain limits patients’ independent mobility, confines their physical range, and increases reliance on others. This gradual loss of autonomy weakens their motivation to engage socially.

When it hurts, I can’t go out to have fun or take a walk. Normally, I would walk over 10,000 steps a day, but now I can barely manage two or three thousand. (Male, 61, Liver cancer, Surgery+Chemotherapy)

Now, whenever I go out, I have to use a wheelchair. If there are stairs, someone has to carry me on their back. Even going to the toilet, I need my partner to support me. So, I don’t even know what the point of going out is anymore. (Female, 35, Breast cancer, Surgery+Chemotherapy)

In addition, the relentless burden of CCRP depletes both physical and psychological energy, turning social activities into an exhausting demand and leading to defensive self-isolation.

When it hurts, I feel completely drained of energy. A few days ago, I felt a bit dizzy while walking; I was terrified I might just pass out on the street. So, I don’t really go out much anymore. (Female, 53, Lung cancer, Surgery+Chemotherapy)

Long-term pain has drained all my energy. Just enduring it each day uses up all my strength. Now, forget about socializing, I can’t even be bothered with my family, because I truly have no energy left to give. (Male, 28, Gastric cancer, Surgery+Chemotherapy)

Theme 2: Cognitive-Affective Overload

Our findings revealed that pain-related irritability often triggers interpersonal conflicts with caregivers, which in turn evokes feelings of guilt. To avoid further friction, patients tend to withdraw or remain silent.

I’m often in a bad mood and get irritable. If I want my partner to do something and they don’t, I sometimes lose my temper. So, most of the time when I’m in pain, I try to control myself and speak less.(Female, 54, Breast cancer, Surgery+Chemotherapy+Radiotherapy)

If I’m feeling pain, I get extremely irritable and don’t want to talk. When I do speak, my temper is very aggressive, and the things I say are quite unpleasant.(Female, 35, Breast cancer, Surgery+Chemotherapy)

Visible physical deterioration also causes profound body image disturbance, leading patients to avoid social situations out of fear of being judged.

I don’t want others to see me like this, looking so lethargic and relying on pills just to keep myself alive. (Female, 42, Breast cancer, Surgery+Chemotherapy)

As soon as my neighbor heard my voice, they asked what happened to me. They looked scared when they saw me. After that, I stopped going out; I was afraid they would laugh at me. (Female, 73, Parotid gland cancer, Surgery+Radiotherapy)

In addition, internalizing negative labels associated with cancer leads patients to anticipate rejection, prompting them to preemptively avoid social interactions out of fear of ostracism or misconceptions about contagion.

Having this disease, you need to know your place. When people see you are sick, they get suspicious and fear it might be contagious, so I don’t want to talk to anyone anymore. (Male, 72, Pharyngeal cancer, Radiotherapy)

When a person falls ill and hits rock bottom, even relatives and friends look down on you. They don’t even have to say it; I can just guess that they despise me, so I don’t initiate contact with them either. (Male, 55, Colon cancer, Surgery+Chemotherapy)

Theme 3: Relational Misalignment and Absence

Our findings revealed that patients’ survival-focused mindset creates a disconnect from the everyday concerns of those around them. At the same time, frequent questions about their illness reinforce an unwanted “patient” identity, further reducing their willingness to communicate.

I feel like I’m on a completely different wavelength from everyone else. They care about promotions and dating; I care about pain relief. We just can’t connect. (Male, 28, Gastric cancer, Surgery+Chemotherapy)

After the surgery, once I quit smoking and drinking, I stopped hanging out with them. If you can’t smoke or drink, what’s the point of being with them? (Male, 77, Gastric cancer, Chemotherapy)

Now, whenever I see relatives or friends, the first thing they ask is how my health has been lately. This special attention makes me very uncomfortable; I just want to live a normal life. (Female, 35, Breast cancer, Surgery+Chemotherapy)

Moreover, patients faced a lack of social support even as they endured significant suffering. Financial pressures led family members to prioritize work, leaving limited time for meaningful emotional connection and caregiving presence. The healthcare system, for its part, focused largely on symptom control, with less attention given to psychosocial needs.

They rarely pay attention to my psychological needs. They think as long as the body is healed, everything is fine. But honestly, the loneliness in my heart is harder to bear than the physical pain. (Female, 42, Breast cancer, Surgery+Chemotherapy)

My partner is out working every day, building houses for others to earn money, so there’s no way he can stay home to take care of me. (Female, 50, Breast cancer, Surgery+Chemotherapy)

This illness is incredibly expensive. I don’t even know what to do anymore, and there’s no one to help me. My life is extremely difficult right now. (Female, 53, Lung cancer, Surgery+Chemotherapy)

Theme 4: Pain Concealment

Our findings revealed that CCRP lacks objective, visible indicators. Under prolonged stress, caregivers may develop compassion fatigue, becoming numb to or even skeptical of patients’ expressions of suffering. When patients’ attempts to vent were met with superficial responses or misunderstanding, they chose to bury their pain to avoid further frustration.

Over time, my family has become numb to it. If you keep saying it hurts, they might wonder why you’re always complaining, or even think you’re faking it. So sometimes, I just don’t tell them. If they ask, I just say I’m okay. (Male, 37, Retroperitoneal tumor, Surgery+Chemotherapy)

When it hurts, I get annoyed myself, and my partner just tells me that getting annoyed won’t help. So sometimes I tell him it hurts, and sometimes I just stay quiet. (Male, 55, Gastric cancer, Surgery+Chemotherapy)

In addition to external responses, patients’ silence also stems from internalized cultural values. Driven by traditional virtues that emphasize familial harmony and self-sacrifice, they actively conceal their pain to avoid burdening their loved ones, suppressing their own need for expression.

My child is in high school right now and under a lot of academic pressure. I’m afraid my illness will affect his studies, so I rarely tell him about my pain and discomfort. I try my best to pretend everything is fine. (Female, 42, Breast cancer, Surgery+Chemotherapy)

Crying out in pain is useless; the people around me can’t hurt for me. If I complain too much, I just feel like a burden. I’m already suffering; dragging my family down so that everyone suffers would be the worst. (Male, 75, Prostate cancer, Radiotherapy)

Theme 5: Attentional Competition

Our findings revealed that attention allocation shaped patients’ pain perception. When left alone or in the quiet of night, with few external distractions, their attention turned inward and they became absorbed in rumination. Pain signals then dominated their consciousness, intensifying the perceived pain.

As soon as night falls and you’re lying motionless in bed, you feel the pain. If you keep moving around, you might not even remember it. (Female, 64, Lung cancer, Surgery+Chemotherapy)

I don’t know if it’s because things distract me during the day that it doesn’t hurt as much, but as soon as it gets quiet at night, the pain starts. (Male, 70, Lung cancer, Surgery+Chemotherapy)

In contrast, social or recreational activities drew attention outward, which helped inhibit somatic pain perception to some degree. The strength of this analgesic effect varied depending on the level of social engagement and the severity of pain.

I feel like when I’m playing mahjong, sometimes I just completely forget about the pain. (Male, 58, Rectal cancer, Surgery+Chemotherapy+Radiotherapy)

Usually, when I’m playing Guandan [a regional card game] with them, I forget about the pain. I’m too busy arguing with them and getting absorbed in the game. (Female, 70, Pancreatic cancer, Chemotherapy)

Talking with others helps distract me and eases the pain a bit. However, no amount of conversation can relieve that kind of extreme pain. (Male, 37, Retroperitoneal tumor, Surgery+Chemotherapy)

Discussion

The Bidirectional Regulation of Social Interaction and Pain Perception

This study suggests a dynamic interplay where attention is competitively allocated between social interaction and pain perception. Beyond buffering emotional distress,27,28 patients in our study perceived high-quality social engagement as providing a potential source of subjective pain relief. Participants’ narratives align with the principles of Gate Control Theory,29 immersive social activities were described as cognitive distractions that occupy limited attentional resources, which may help in effectively “closing the pain gate” to nociceptive signals.30,31 Additionally, positive interactions can also raise pain thresholds neurochemically by promoting endogenous opioid and oxytocin release.32 In contrast, when external stimuli decreased, patients in our study felt their attention driven inward, heightening their hypervigilance to discomfort. This finding corroborates the empirical study by Amja et al,33 which revealed that social isolation shrank the lifeworlds of patients with chronic pain and exacerbated their pain experiences.

These perceived analgesic benefits present a painful paradox: the physical exhaustion and restricted mobility of CCRP often preclude the social interactions that participants feel provide relief. Christidis et al34 stated that patients with chronic or cancer pain frequently withdraw or completely cease social activities due to the pain itself or the fear of pain. While offering short-term protection, this withdrawal ultimately shrinks social networks, depriving patients of crucial social support and its associated cognitive analgesic effects.

Protective Isolation Within a Cultural Context

Unlike passive Pain-Induced Confinement, the social alienation identified in this study appears as an active form of self-protection rooted in traditional Chinese cultural values. Participants described this withdrawal as serving dual functions: an altruistic retreat to maintain family stability, and a defensive avoidance to preserve self-image.

Participants noted that irritability from chronic pain often triggered family conflicts. The guilt that followed led them to adopt avoidance strategies, aiming to prevent projecting negative emotions onto their loved ones. This aligns with findings by Xu et al,35 who reported that even patients who previously had good tempers became easily agitated during pain episodes; however, driven by concern for their family, they often chose to endure the suffering silently to avoid disturbing them or increasing the caregiving burden In addition, deeply influenced by the traditional Chinese value of “not burdening others”, patients often experienced a sense of self-perceived burden.36 From their perspective, repeatedly voicing pain felt like placing demands on their family, which led them to internalize their suffering. Although motivated by love and a sense of family responsibility, this behavior ultimately hindered the timely expression and management of pain-related needs,37 sacrificing deeper emotional connections.

Furthermore, social alienation serves as a defensive strategy. Participants in our study expressed shame related to altered body image, as well as self-stigmatization. As revealed by Song and Yao’s qualitative research,9 Chinese patients with cancer frequently adopt avoidant social behaviors to cope with disease-induced shame and stigmatizing labels. Visible physical changes caused by cancer and its treatments, such as hair loss, weight changes, facial disfigurement, and the loss of body parts, not only damage body image but also represent a profound loss of dignity in Chinese culture, where “face” is highly valued.38–40 Moreover, patients with cancer often experience intense social anxiety, fearing that changes in appearance may invite stares or irrational labels, such as bringing bad luck or being contagious.41 To protect their remaining self-esteem and avoid anticipated discrimination or pity in social settings, patients choose to withdraw from their social networks. This self-isolation is, at its core, an act of self-protection aimed at evading social rejection.42

The invisibility of CCRP also widens the empathy gap between healthcare providers, patients, and family members. Long-term pain complaints can easily lead to compassion fatigue among caregivers. As Toye et al noted, patients with chronic pain often face a credibility crisis: without objective, visible evidence of pain, they are frequently suspected by those around them of exaggerating their suffering or seeking attention.43 When patients repeatedly express their distress only to encounter numbness, perfunctory responses, or even skepticism from those closest to them, the frustration of this ineffective communication can become a form of secondary trauma.44,45 Moreover, shaped by the traditional Chinese value of “forbearance”, expressing pain too openly is often viewed as a sign of weak willpower or immaturity.9 As a result, to avoid damaging interpersonal relationships and protect themselves from further emotional distress, patients gradually choose to hide their pain.

The Structural Absence and Functional Misalignment of Social Support

This study highlights several perceived external drivers of social alienation, particularly the structural gaps and functional mismatches within social support systems. Previous research confirms that social support is a vital resource for patients coping with psychological distress.46 However, similar to the empirical findings revealed by Gruner et al,47 our study demonstrates that although patients are embedded within family and healthcare support networks, some of this support fails to translate into effective assistance This observation resonates with Cohen et al’s stress-buffering model, which suggests support is most protective when its form matches specific coping needs.48

The functional mismatch within support systems was described by participants as identity-related tensions and gaps in lived experience. Participants expressed that frequent inquiries about their condition from family and friends, though well-intentioned, inadvertently reinforced their “sick role.” This tends to overshadow their other social identities and runs counter to their desire to normalize life through social interaction. This is consistent with the analytical findings of Zarei et al,42 who showed that mismatched social expectations and overwhelming sympathy often drive patients toward self-imposed isolation for self-preservation At the same time, a significant experiential divide exists between patients focused on day-to-day survival and those around them who are more engaged with career and daily routines. Morrell et al49 found that when patients compare themselves with others who are better off, they often feel more discouraged and may experience heightened inferiority and depression. In addition, research has also found that desynchronized life rhythms leave patients with little common ground for conversation, contributing to a sense of interactional alienation. As a result, even when physically present in social settings, patients often feel disconnected, unable to truly connect with others.9

The structural gaps in support systems arise from disconnects in both family and medical resources. Within the family, the financial burden and continuous care requirements of cancer treatment often plunge caregivers into role conflicts.50 Research suggests that intense caregiver burden can lead to emotional exhaustion, resulting in physical absence or emotional withdrawal precisely when patients need companionship most.51 On the medical side, although authoritative international guidelines have explicitly integrated psychosocial support into the core framework of cancer pain management, current clinical practice remains shaped by the traditional biomedical model. There is a strong emphasis on pharmacological analgesia, while the critical role of psychological and social support in modulating pain perception is often overlooked. This has led to a severe shortage of targeted intervention resources.15,52 Together, this dual absence, including limited family companionship and insufficient medical humanism, leaves patients with CCRP in a state of helplessness, gradually pushing them toward social isolation.

Clinical Implications: Constructing a Multidimensional Biopsychosocial Support System

Our findings suggest that social alienation in patients with CCRP arises not from a single factor but from the interplay of physical pain, psychological distress, and impaired social interaction. Relying solely on pharmacological analgesia may therefore insufficient to break this vicious cycle. Healthcare professionals need to adopt a holistic, biopsychosocial perspective in order to build a multidimensional support system.

Optimizing Pain Management Strategies to Break Physical Barriers

Because pain-induced physical impairment is the initiating factor in social alienation, clinical efforts should prioritize functional recovery. Individualized multimodal analgesic plans that combine routine medications with physical therapy and rehabilitation can help enhance patients’ activity tolerance. To ease concerns about pain flare-ups when going out, clinicians may guide patients in anticipatory pain management, such as using short-acting analgesics before planned social activities, which can strengthen their confidence in leaving home. Home-based remote pain monitoring systems also offer timely medical guidance and help reduce anxiety related to symptom fluctuations.

Strengthening Psycho-Cognitive Interventions to Rebuild Self-Image

Addressing shame associated with altered body image and self-stigmatization requires that psychological care become a routine component of treatment. Clinicians may integrate Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) or Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) to help patients identify and reframe irrational beliefs, such as viewing themselves as a burden or expecting rejection from others. These approaches guide patients toward accepting physical changes and rebuilding self-worth. In parallel, image rehabilitation initiatives, such as providing guidance on makeup and appropriate dressing techniques to camouflage physical alterations, can help ease anxiety about being scrutinized in social settings and enhance social confidence.

Empowering the Family Caregiving System to Bridge the Empathy Gap

The family serves as the patient’s core social unit. In response to issues such as absent support and silenced pain, medical teams should extend interventions to family members. One approach is to offer educational programs that help relatives recognize non-verbal pain cues and develop effective communication skills. This includes avoiding an excessive focus on disease-related topics during interactions and instead engaging in ordinary, everyday conversations to meet patients’ psychological need to be treated as “normal” individuals. Equally important is attending to caregivers’ mental health: providing support groups can help alleviate compassion fatigue and prevent emotional distance from developing within the family.

Expanding Pathways for Social Connection to Leverage the Analgesic Effect of Socialization

Drawing on the attention competition mechanism identified in this study, clinical settings can promote social prescribing as a supportive strategy. Healthcare providers may encourage patients to take part in peer support activities, including cancer clubs or mutual aid groups. In these homogeneous settings, patients often find emotional resonance and relief from the loneliness of feeling misunderstood. For those with severe pain who are physically unable to leave home, internet technology offers a way to build virtual social platforms or online support communities, helping patients stay connected beyond physical limitations. Encouraging participation in moderate entertainment and social activities can also create immersive experiences that divert attention from pain, offering the dual benefits of auxiliary analgesia and improved psychological well-being.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, given the small, single-center sample, the generalizability of our findings may be limited. Second, detailed subgroup analyses across different tumor types were not conducted, and future studies could explore the differences among patients with CCRP across various tumor types. Finally, as the majority of our participants were elderly, further research focusing on younger populations is warranted to better meet the specific needs of these patients.

Conclusion

Drawing on in-depth interviews with a sample of 22 patients, this study suggests that social alienation among patients with CCRP is a complex, dynamic process driven by factors including pain-induced confinement, cognitive-affective overload, and relational misalignment and absence. To address these psychosocial barriers and meet patients’ multidimensional needs, effective pain management must transcend the biomedical paradigm, through the dynamic assessment of pain and social alienation, incorporating holistic psychosocial reconstruction, family empowerment, and the restoration of social connectivity to overcome underlying pain management barriers.
