Paediatric cardiac care in low resource settings evidence from the Gambia
Authors: Lamin Makalo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPaediatric congenital heart disease remains an important contributor to childhood morbidity in The Gambia. Evidence from 2008 to 2022 shows improved detection and reduced in-hospital mortality due to strengthened diagnostics and clinical care. However, late presentation and limited access to definitive cardiac interventions continue to drive adverse outcomes, highlighting the need for enhanced early detection, regional cardiac services, and integrated preventive strategies.Subject terms: Cardiology, Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Congenital heart disease (CHD) remains one of the most important yet under-recognized contributors to childhood morbidity and mortality globally, with the burden falling disproportionately on low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)1. While survival for children with CHD has improved dramatically in high-income settings due to advances in early diagnosis, interventional cardiology, and cardiac surgery, outcomes in many African countries remain poor2.

This commentary synthesises evidence from three key studies conducted in The Gambia between 2008 and 2022, encompassing an early institutional surgical-referral era, a mid-period hospital-based epidemiological study, and a more recent paediatric cardiology cohort at the national tertiary hospital. Together, these studies provide a longitudinal perspective on the burden, determinants, management practices, outcomes, and evolving trends of paediatric CHD in a resource-limited health system. Burden and prevalence of CHD (2008–2022)

Across the reviewed period, CHD consistently accounted for a meaningful proportion of paediatric morbidity at tertiary level. During the 2008–2018 period, institutional data from the Medical Research Council Unit, The Gambia (MRCG) documented 73 diagnosed CHD cases over the study period, reflecting relatively low absolute numbers3. This likely represents under-diagnosis due to limited access to echocardiography and reliance on selective referral for overseas cardiac surgery, rather than true disease rarity.

In the subsequent 2018–2020 period, Emmanuel et al. reported a hospital-based prevalence of approximately 2–3% of paediatric admissions at Edward Francis Small Teaching Hospital (EFSTH), Banjul, The Gambia4. Although this proportion appears modest, CHD represents a chronic and resource-intensive condition, requiring long-term follow-up, specialised diagnostics, and complex management. As such, even a 2–3% prevalence constitutes a disproportionately high burden on paediatric services in resource-limited settings.

In the most recent cohort (2020–2022), Nyang et al. documented a marked increase to 89 diagnosed cases, compared to 73 cases in the earlier 2008–2018 cohort and 111 cases in the 2018–2020 cohort.5 This increase reflects improved case detection associated with expanded echocardiography services, increased clinician awareness, and the growing presence of specialised paediatric cardiology input, rather than a true rise in incidence.

Taken together (Table 1), these findings demonstrate a progressive increase in the detected burden of CHD over time, most plausibly attributable to improved diagnostic capacity and health system strengthening. This trend is consistent with global evidence indicating that CHD birth prevalence remains relatively stable worldwide, with observed increases largely driven by enhanced detection1,5,6.

Risk factors, ethnic patterns, and spectrum of disease

Congenital heart disease in The Gambia reflects a complex interplay of genetic, maternal, and perinatal determinants. Down syndrome remains the most prominent associated condition, particularly among children born to mothers aged over 35 years, reinforcing the established relationship between chromosomal abnormalities and structural cardiac defects7,8. Maternal diabetes and positive family history—defined as congenital heart disease or congenital anomalies in first-degree relatives—were less frequently reported but remain clinically relevant contributors8.

A notable and consistent observation across cohorts is the disproportionate representation of children of Sarahule (Soninke) ethnicity, a minority ethnic group in The Gambia as documented in national demographic data9. While causality cannot be inferred, this pattern raises hypotheses regarding genetic susceptibility, familial clustering, founder effects, or sociocultural practices such as endogamy1,10. Alternative explanations—including referral bias, geographic clustering, and differential access to tertiary care—must also be considered4,7.

The burden of Down syndrome–associated CHD highlights the intersection between maternal health, reproductive timing, and intergenerational risk. Advanced maternal age is a well-established biological risk factor for trisomy 21 and associated cardiac defects; however, in resource-limited settings, reproductive patterns are often shaped by educational, economic, and sociocultural constraints rather than informed choice11,12.

Within this context, the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)—particularly SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being), SDG 4 (Quality Education), and SDG 5 (Gender Equality)—provide a useful framework for understanding upstream determinants of CHD risk. Female education is strongly associated with delayed and planned childbearing, improved antenatal care utilisation, and enhanced recognition of pregnancy-related risks12,13. Supporting women to achieve their desired family size before 35 years of age, where feasible, may reduce chromosomal risk while improving maternal and child health outcomes14.

Maternal diabetes represents an additional, modifiable contributor to congenital heart disease. Although underreported in the reviewed cohorts, the rising burden of non-communicable diseases among women of reproductive age in sub-Saharan Africa suggests that undiagnosed or poorly controlled diabetes may increasingly contribute to congenital anomalies14,15. Integrating screening and prevention of diabetes into routine antenatal care aligns with SDG 3 and reinforces the need to frame congenital disorders as part of a broader women’s health and equity agenda11,13,16.

Spectrum of disease

Across all study periods, ventricular septal defect (VSD) consistently remained the most common lesion, followed by atrial septal defect (ASD), tetralogy of Fallot (TOF), patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), and atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD)3,4,7.

As shown in Table 2, VSD increased from 31.5% in the 2008–2018 cohort to 39.3% in the 2020–2022 cohort, while ASD demonstrated a relative increase across cohorts. In contrast, TOF showed a proportional decline, and PDA and AVSD remained comparatively stable.

This spectrum is consistent with reports from other sub-Saharan African tertiary centres17–19.

Clinically, late presentation with pallor (severe anaemia), failure to thrive, and heart failure was strongly associated with adverse outcomes. Pallor remained an independent predictor of mortality in multivariable analysis, highlighting the interaction between cardiac disease severity, comorbid conditions, and delayed diagnosis in shaping outcomes.

Management practices and the impact of system strengthening

Management of CHD across the study period remained predominantly medical, relying on diuretics, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, and beta-blockers for heart failure control.5 During the 2008–2018 period, access to definitive care depended heavily on overseas cardiac surgery facilitated through external referral programmes, with improved survival among selected children but prolonged waiting times and limited reach3.

A critical inflection point occurred from 2021 onward, coinciding with the expansion of postgraduate paediatric training at EFSTH. The presence of paediatric cardiologists and trainees with enhanced cardiology exposure contributed to earlier clinical suspicion, increased echocardiography utilisation, improved documentation, and more standardised heart failure management. This period also benefited from continued collaboration with international partners, including the Scottish Paediatric Cardiology Team, which strengthened diagnostic capacity through mentorship and visiting specialist support20,21. These system-level improvements align with SDG, which emphasizes strengthening health systems and ensuring access to quality care for children with non-communicable diseases, including congenital conditions22.

Outcomes and temporal trends

As shown in Table 1, in-hospital mortality declined from approximately 10–11% in the 2018–2020 cohort to 6–7% in the 2020–2022 cohort, despite the continued absence of local paediatric cardiac surgical services4,7. Heart failure, pneumonia, anaemia, and malnutrition remained the leading contributors to mortality across all periods.

The observed improvement in outcomes over time aligns with the combined effects of postgraduate training, protocol-based care, and improved diagnostic accuracy, demonstrating that meaningful survival gains are achievable even before advanced surgical infrastructure is established. These gains reflect incremental progress toward SDG 3 targets on reducing under-five mortality and improving child health outcomes, particularly in resource-constrained settings22.

Broader context and policy implications

The Gambian experience mirrors broader regional and global patterns, where children in LMICs continue to face delayed diagnosis and limited access to definitive care1,6,10. Disruptions to overseas surgery pathways during the COVID-19 pandemic further exposed the fragility of external dependence, reinforcing the need for sustainable in-country and regional solutions.

Emerging strategies such as percutaneous closure of septal defects through South–South cooperation initiatives, including PAN procedures, offer promising interim solutions that can substantially reduce morbidity from common acyanotic lesions while building local capacity23. These approaches are consistent with SDG 17, which promotes global partnerships and knowledge transfer, as well as SDG 9, which supports innovation and infrastructure development in healthcare systems22.

Conclusion

Between 2008 and 2022, paediatric CHD care in The Gambia has evolved from a context of under-diagnosis and dependence on overseas surgical referral toward improved case detection, standardised medical management, and better short-term survival. These gains reflect expanded access to echocardiography, strengthened postgraduate paediatric training, and sustained international collaboration. Despite this progress, preventable morbidity and mortality remain substantial, largely driven by late presentation, comorbid conditions, and limited access to definitive cardiac interventions.

The observed burden of CHD is further shaped by upstream risk factors, including genetic conditions, advanced maternal age, and emerging maternal metabolic risks, underscoring the importance of early-life and prenatal influences on disease outcomes. Addressing these determinants within the broader continuum of care aligns with SDG 3 (good health and well-being) and SDG 5 (gender equality), particularly through improved maternal health, reproductive care, and risk reduction strategies22.

Future progress will depend on strengthening early detection systems, expanding in-country and regional interventional cardiac services, and investing in a skilled multidisciplinary workforce. In parallel, reinforcing preventive strategies—particularly those related to maternal health, health education, and chronic disease prevention—will be critical to mitigating intergenerational risk. A coordinated, system-level approach that integrates paediatric and maternal health priorities, supported by SDG 17 partnerships, offers the most sustainable pathway to reducing CHD-related morbidity and mortality in The Gambia1.
