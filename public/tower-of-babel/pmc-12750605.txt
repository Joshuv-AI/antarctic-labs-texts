Coexisting congenital ano-urethral fistula with posterior urethral valve: a case report and literature review

Background

H-type anorectal malformation (ARM) is an extremely rare congenital anomaly, accounting for approximately 3% of all anorectal malformations. This variant appears to be more prevalent among females in Asia, with reported rates reaching up to 12% of ARM cases [1]. These malformations are frequently associated with other congenital anomalies, most commonly involving the urogenital system. Vertebral, cardiac, and tracheoesophageal anomalies are also frequently observed in patients with ARM.

Among urologic anomalies linked with ARM, renal agenesis, hydronephrosis, vesicourethral reflux (VUR), and urethral stenosis are the most commonly reported. However, only a few cases in the literature describe the co-occurrence of ARM with posterior urethral valve (PUV), and the specific association between H-type ARM and PUV has not, to our knowledge, been previously reported.

Diagnosing this rare combination poses significant challenges and requires a high index of suspicion. Clues such as oligohydramnios during pregnancy, postnatal oliguria, or watery effluents in a colostomy bag accompanied by rising serum creatinine and metabolic acidosis should raise the possibility of PUV in patients with ARM, prompting further urologic evaluation [2].

Surgical management of ARM varies depending on the type and level of fistula, the location and size of the anus, and the presence of associated anomalies. Approaches range from simple fistula excision to anterior or posterior sagittal anorectoplasty and abdominoperineal pull-through procedures [3]. Notably, patients with H-type ARM tend to have a favorable prognosis due to the normal or near-normal position and function of the anal sphincter.

Case presentation

A 9-year-old male presented to our hospital with a two-year history of passing urine through the anal orifice, which had progressively worsened over the past year. He also reported nocturnal enuresis and recurrent urinary tract infections since early childhood, for which he had received multiple courses of antibiotics; otherwise, he denied any pneumaturia or feces in the urine. The patient had a normal urinary stream without straining, difficulty, or a history of trauma. He was the first child in the family. His mother did not receive antenatal care, and delivery occurred at home.

On examination, the patient appeared well-nourished, and vital signs were stable. The external genitalia were male, uncircumcised, and well-developed, with an orthotopic urethral meatus of adequate caliber. The anal opening was orthotopic, with good sphincter tone and caliber. Digital rectal examination did not reveal any fistulous opening. The remainder of the physical examination was unremarkable.

Baseline laboratory investigations were within normal limits, including complete blood count, renal and liver function tests, serum electrolytes, and urinalysis. Abdominal ultrasound showed no evidence of hydronephrosis, bladder wall thickening, or renal parenchymal abnormalities.

Voiding cystourethrography (VCUG) demonstrated a dilated posterior urethra with a rectourethral fistula (Fig. 1), without evidence of vesicoureteral reflux.

CUG demonstrating dilated posterior urethra, otherwise no evidence of bladder wall changes or vesicourethral reflux

Cystoscopy. performed with an 8 Fr pediatric cystoscope, revealed a Type I posterior urethral valve arising from the verumontanum. There was no stenosis to the urethra. Both ureteral orifices were orthotopic and appeared normal. The bladder exhibited mild trabeculation, (Fig. 2).

Cystoscopic image of a type I PUV (arrow)

Initial management included successful ablation of the posterior urethral valve. During cystoscopy, the rectourethral fistula was not directly visualized; however, fluid was observed emerging from the anal canal. Application of a Lone Star retractor revealed a fistulous opening on the anterior wall of the anal canal, distal to the dentate line, approximately 1 cm from the anal verge. A guidewire was passed through the fistula, and its distal end was visualized at the prostatic urethra, just distal to the verumontanum (Fig. 3). From measurement using the guidewire, the fistula was long (approximately 3–4 cm), and enough tissue could be comfortably apposed between the urethral and anal openings. Otherwise, the anus was patent and wide enough, and there was no excoriation or signs of infection in the fistula or perianal area. After doing valve ablation and cystoscopic evaluation, the patient left the operating room with the decision to deal with the ARM after a week.

Intraoperative cystoscopic image showing a guidewire (arrow) visualized in the posterior urethra, having been passed through the fistula tract from the anal end. The guidewire clearly appears within the posterior urethral lumen

After bowel preparation in the ward, the patient was scheduled for the ARM. We performed bowel preparation by administering a cleansing enema and adhering to a clear liquid diet for 3 days. Considering the adequate tissue between the anus and urethral and healthy anorectum, we decided to proceed with simple fistula excision without colostomy. After catheterizing the urethra in lithotomy position, Lone Star retractor was applied. Then, circumferential silk sutures were placed around the fistula while maintaining the guidewire in situ (Fig. 4a). The fistula tract was mobilized to its urethral origin (Fig. 4b), ligated, and excised. The intervening tissue was appropriately layered and apposed, and the fistula site was covered with healthy anorectal mucosa. The specimen was sent for histopathological examination. A leak test using saline infusion through the cystoscope into the prostatic urethra confirmed a watertight repair. The anal sphincter was interposed over the repair site, and the anal canal was reconstructed (Fig. 5). A 10 Fr nasogastric tube was inserted as a urethral catheter following circumcision.

Intraoperative image of fistula tract mobilization through trans anal approach. (a) shows the fistula cannulated with a probing guidewire and stay stitches applied circumferentially for mobilization. (b) shows the fistula being mobilized from the anal end. The urethra was catheterized (not included in the photo) to make identifying the urethra easier and to avoid urethral injury

Postoperatively after excision of the fistula and wound repair

Postoperatively, the patient received analgesics and antibiotics and was started on sitz baths after 24 h. Due to limited access to parenteral nutrition, he was kept nil per os (NPO) for three days, followed by a liquid diet for another three days. The urethral catheter was removed on postoperative day 10, and the patient was discharged on day 11, passing stool and urine normally, with no urinary leakage via the anus.

Histopathology of the excised fistulous tract revealed stratified squamous epithelium. At three months follow-up, the patient was continent for both urine and feces, with no evidence of wound infection or fistula recurrence. He continued to void with a strong urinary stream and was scheduled for further follow-up at six months.

Discussion and conclusion

The incidence of ARM is reported as 2.0–2.5 per 10,000 live births, although there are significant variations in prevalence between regions worldwide. It occurs more frequently in boys than girls, and the ARM in boys tends to be supralevator compared to girls, which is usually of low type [4]. H-type ARM is included in the rare/regional variants [5]. This malformation has been termed N-type fistula, perineal canal, or double termination of the alimentary tract in different literature [6–8]. It is less common in the Western world, while its incidence could be as high as 12% in Asia, which is even < 1% in Africa [5–7]. It is characterized by a congenital recto-urogenital fistula, which may occur in the presence of a normal or ectopic anal opening. Females are affected 2.5 to 6 times more frequently than males [3]. Although the literature describes these lesions from both congenital and acquired perspectives, their congenital origin is considered predominant, primarily due to the frequent association with other developmental anomalies. Some of these patients have anal stenosis, presacral mass, and anterior mislocation of the anus, while male patients could even demonstrate urethral anomalies like hypoplasia, atresia, and stenosis [9, 10]. Associated anomalies have been uncommon in girls, but males have serious additional malformations [11].

There is still some controversy about the exact normal and abnormal development of the human anorectum [3]. Different theories have been proposed to explain the embryologic basis of H-type ARM. Among which failure of fusion of median genital folds, a remnant of the open cloacal duct, a failure of fusion of perineal raphe defect in the development of the uro-anal septum, embryological remnant of the uro-rectal septum, a faulty embryonic caudal folding process are the main proposed theories to explain the formation of an H-type anorectal malformation defect [10–14].

The diagnosis is rarely established at birth since the anal opening is normally or near normally positioned in most cases [3]. A history of passage of stool from the vestibule is the most common presentation in girls, while a passage of urine from the rectum is the usual presentation in boys. Other presenting symptoms include constipation, labial abscess, and recurrent urinary tract infection [9, 15]. In addition to clinical evaluation, imaging studies serve as valuable adjuncts for establishing an accurate diagnosis and formulating an effective management plan. Contrast studies, including VCUG, barium enema, and Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), are imaging choices, though cystourethroscopy is even more helpful to localize fistula in boys, as it helps with identification during exploration [3].

Anorectal malformations (ARMs) are rarely isolated and are often associated with other congenital anomalies. Commonly affected systems include the vertebral, anorectal, cardiac, tracheoesophageal, renal, and genitourinary systems—collectively referred to as the VACTERL association. Many of these associated anomalies are serious, and the long-term prognosis of a child with anorectal malformation is often more dependent on the extent of these associated anomalies than on the anorectal malformation itself. Therefore, early detection of these associated anomalies is crucial for timely intervention and comprehensive management [16]. Renal and genitourinary anomalies, followed by spinal anomalies, are the most common associated malformations. Renal and genitourinary anomalies have been reported in up to 50% of patients with anorectal malformations (ARM) [16, 17]. Generally, the higher the anorectal anomaly, the more probable and severe the urogenital anomaly [16, 18]. There is a gender disparity as far as associated anomalies are concerned, with many series with girls reporting no associated anomalies, and more commonly seen with H type in males [3]. Males tend to have urinary tract anomalies, while females will sustain genital anomalies. Renal agenesis, ectopic kidney, hydronephrosis, VUR, ectopic ureter, bladder dysfunction, urethral stenosis, and stricture are the commonly encountered urinary tract anomalies, some of which will have a huge impact on the upper urinary tract function. In addition, there are a few reports of ARM associated with posterior urethral valve [14–16]. Posterior urethral valves are the most common cause of congenital bladder outlet obstruction in boys, which could lead to end-stage renal failure in childhood [17–19]. Posterior urethral valves appear as intraluminal folds located immediately proximal to the verumontanum, which is believed to result from anomalous insertion of the mesonephric duct into the cloaca or an incomplete involution of plicae colliculi during the embryogenesis period [20]. Presentation was 1/3 antenatal/neonatally, 1/3 postnatal, 1/3 late (>1 year). Antenatally diagnosed PUV is associated with significantly higher postnatal plasma creatinine, more hydroureteronephrosis, and renal dysplasia than those diagnosed in infancy or later, which generally carries a poor prognosis [19].

Our case involves a rare form of ano-urethral fistula combined with a less commonly associated urinary tract anomaly, posterior urethral valves (PUV), which was diagnosed late. Diagnosing this rare association is not a straightforward process and requires a high index of suspicion. The presence of oligohydramnios, postnatal oliguria, or watery effluents in a colostomy bag with rising renal function and acidosis should raise the suspicion of PUV in patients with ARM, and prompt investigation is needed [2]. A rectourethral fistula could serve as a “pop-off” mechanism and protect the upper urinary tracts [2]. This seems to truly explain our patient’s condition, including the congenital nature of the fistula. Had the fistula been acquired, the child would have developed bladder and upper urinary tract changes, which are absent in this particular patient. The renal parenchyma and upper urinary tract were normal, having no signs of hydronephrosis and VUR on ultrasound and VCUG. We strongly believe that the fistula was congenital and served as a pop-off for the obstructed lower urinary tract, thereby preserving bladder and upper tract function. However, it is also important to consider that the absence of bladder or upper urinary tract changes, lack of voiding lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), and the delayed presentation may indicate that the posterior urethral valves (PUV) were of a low-grade variant. In addition, he is from a remote area, and parents with such a background usually won’t be curious enough to detect such a non-life-threatening medical condition till the child grows and finds out he has some functional, continence, and hygienic limitations. It’s a common experience for us and physicians working in the developing world to see people who present late for such non-life-threatening conditions. Many conditions can be overlooked in young children who can’t report their health status. Therefore, we strongly believe that the fistula was congenital but went unrecognized by the family. The inherently mild nature of the condition may have contributed to the preservation of the urinary tract and renal parenchyma, despite the delayed presentation. Several studies have reported that boys with more severe forms of posterior urethral valves (PUV) typically present earlier in childhood and are at higher risk of developing chronic kidney disease (CKD) and progressing to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [21, 30].

Diagnosis of patients with PUV who present late is challenging. Late presentation, where the diagnosis was made during adolescence or adulthood, occurs in 10% of cases [22, 23]. VCUG is mainly practiced, but late-presented PUVs may be missed on VCUG. Hence, whenever there is a strong suspicion of PUV, urethrocystoscopic examination should be preferred regardless of VCUG results [22, 24]. PUV should be considered in boys with a history of recurrent urinary infections, voiding dysfunction, persistent reflux, and repetitive daytime incontinence [22, 24]. Generally, late presenters will improve dramatically after valve ablation and have a good prognosis, as late presentation is a sign of lesser degrees of obstruction [25, 26]. However, a few could continue to have enuresis, urinary frequency, and poor bladder emptying and require further treatment follow-up. The continued bladder dysfunction could suggest that irreversible urinary tract damage may have occurred [25].

No consensus exists on the standard of treatment for H-type ARM. Various procedures have been used to correct this malformation. The anatomy of the urethra, genitalia, and anorectum should be clearly defined before embarking on any surgical intervention [3].Various surgical techniques have been employed, with the choice of approach depending on factors such as the type and level of the fistula, the location and size of the anus, and the presence of associated anorectal anomalies. It could range from a simple fistula excision to an abdominoperineal pull-through(5,8,31,32). The preferred surgical approach is primarily based on the complete excision of the fistula tract and anatomical reconstruction, with careful attention to avoiding overlapping suture lines to minimize the risk of fistula recurrence. Adequate mobilization with sufficient intervening tissue to prevent apposing suture lines is core to decreasing the likelihood of recurrence. A trans-anal approach, leaving the perineal body intact, is an excellent option [27]. Because it avoids the appearance of a huge anoplasty wound, which has the potential to dehisce and cause morbidity in addition to its cosmetic healing. While adhering to this fundamental surgical principle, the choice of procedure can be tailored to the surgeon’s expertise and comfort. In females, various techniques may be employed, including perineal repair, vestibulo-anal pull-through, posterior sagittal transrectal surgery (limited or formal posterior sagittal anorectoplasty), anterior perineal anorectoplasty, abdominoperineal or endorectal pull-through, and transanal excision of the fistulous tract. In males, the options include perineal repair, limited or formal posterior sagittal anorectoplasty, abdominoperineal pull-through, and anterior sagittal anorectoplasty [3]. Dilatation of the urethra can be done if there is associated urethral hypoplasia, and it might even need suprapubic urinary diversion if the bladder is not well decompressed via the urethra [3, 28]. colostomy isn’t routinely needed. However, it could be applicable in some cases, like difficult recurrent cases, high anomalies, and for surgeons who have little experience in handling such cases [3]. The presence of a colostomy does not necessarily reduce the risk of fistula recurrence. Successful healing primarily depends on an adequate repair using healthy, well-vascularized tissue, which can achieve good outcomes even without a diverting colostomy. Preoperative preparation should include thorough bowel cleansing. Postoperatively, many studies recommend measures such as maintaining the patient in a position that minimizes contamination, keeping the patient nil per os (NPO) for 3–5 days in cases without a diverting colostomy, and performing regular perineal toileting and douching with antiseptic solutions. The overall prognosis is excellent, largely due to the preservation of the normal anatomy of the anal sphincter and anus.

In our case, cystoscopic valve ablation was done. After completing the ablation, the guide wire was passed via the fistula and used to guide our dissection and complete trans-anal excision of the fistula. After excising the fistula, the anal sphincter muscle was interposed between the suture lines as an intermediate layer. The anorectal mucosa was closed, and a lubricated gauze pack was placed in the rectum and left in situ for six hours.

In conclusion, H-type fistulas are diagnostically challenging and are often identified late in the clinical course. To the best of our knowledge, the coexistence of an ano-urethral fistula with a posterior urethral valve has not been previously reported and necessitates a high index of suspicion for timely diagnosis and management. Performing cystoscopy prior to definitive management of the fistula can provide valuable diagnostic information regarding the nature of the fistula and facilitate better treatment planning.
