Quantitative LFQ-DIA proteomics reveals FTH1-MCM5/WNT axis mediated osteoblastic dysfunction via ferroptosis drives diabetic osteoporosis
Authors: Jiebin Lin, Yongze Zhang, Zhenrun Zhan, Dan Liang, Xuan Zhou, Ke Zheng, Yalong Tu, Sunjie Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn diabetes mellitus osteoporosis (DMOP), a common and severe chronic complication of diabetes mellitus (DM), long-term challenges are posed to public health. Recent evidence has implicated ferroptosis—a form of regulated cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation—in the pathogenesis of DMOP. Ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1) plays a critical role in regulating iron metabolism during ferroptosis. To elucidate the regulatory mechanisms by which FTH1 modulates osteoblast (OB) ferroptosis and aberrant bone metabolism under high glucose and high fat (HGHF) conditions, we performed quantitative LFQ-DIA proteomics combined with bioinformatic analysis. MC3T3-E1 cells cultured under high glucose and high palmitic acid (HGPA) conditions were subjected to lentiviral-mediated FTH1 knock down (KD) or over expression (OE), and their protein expression profiles were systematically compared. We identified 857 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in the FTH1KD/HGPA group and 129 DEPs in the FTH1OE/HGPA group. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that, relative to HGPA/NC controls, DEPs in the KD/HGPA group were predominantly enriched in pyruvate biosynthesis and ADP metabolic processes, whereas DEPs in the OE/HGPA group were mainly associated with retinoid-like and glucuronate metabolic processes; in the HGPA/NC group, DEPs were enriched in cell–cell adhesion and regulation of inflammatory response. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis indicated that these DEPs are involved in glycolysis, glutathione metabolism, and ferroptosis pathways. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis further identified minichromosome maintenance complex component 5(MCM5) and glucose 6 phosphate isomerase (GPI) as top hub proteins in the KD group. Functional validation of MCM5—the highest-scoring node by MCODE (score = 18.0)—demonstrated that FTH1KD significantly upregulated MCM5 expression, whereas MCM5KD reduced OBs ferroptosis, enhanced osteogenic differentiation, and activated WNT signaling. These results suggest that MCM5 is a key mediator of OB differentiation under HGHF conditions. Collectively, our findings reveal that altered FTH1 expression under HGHF conditions reshapes the OB protein interaction network and identify MCM5 as a potential therapeutic target for DMOP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27559-9.Keywords: FTH1, MCM5, Proteomic analysis, Ferroptosis, WNT signalingSubject terms: Endocrine system and metabolic diseases, Cell death, Proteome informatics

Introduction

In diabetes mellitus osteoporosis (DMOP), a common and severe chronic complication in patients with diabetes mellitus (DM)1, the hallmarks are reduced bone mass, deterioration of bone microarchitecture, and a significantly increased risk of fragility fractures2, posing a long-term challenge to public health and human well-being. Under diabetic conditions, hyperglycemia together with the frequently accompanying high-fat milieu disrupts bone metabolism via multiple pathways3,4, among which impaired osteoblast (OB) differentiation5 and enhanced osteoclast (OC) activity6 are key pathological alterations. Traditional studies have focused on mechanisms such as necrosis7, apoptosis8, and autophagy9, but more recently ferroptosis—a form of regulated cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation—has been shown to play a critical role in DMOP10. Nevertheless, the precise regulatory mechanisms of ferroptosis in this disease remain to be elucidated.

Ferroptosis is a regulated cell death driven by iron overload and lipid peroxidation, primarily controlled by antioxidant systems11, especially glutathione and GPX412,13. Ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1), the major intracellular iron-storage protein14,is crucial for iron homeostasis. Downregulation of FTH1 reduces cell viability and mitochondrial function, thereby inducing cell death15; ; conversely, ferritinophagy inhibitors suppress ferroptosis in PC-12 cells16. In mouse cardiomyocytes, FTH1 knockout causes iron overload, ROS accumulation, and ferroptosis17. Notably, FTH1 expression is reduced in diabetic mice, where FGF21 interacts with ferritin chains18. Based on these findings, we propose that FTH1 dysregulation promotes ferroptosis in DMOP, representing a key pathogenic mechanism.

Minichromosome maintenance complex component 5 (MCM5) encodes part of the MCM2–7 helicase, essential for DNA replication19,20. Mutations in this complex cause Meier–Gorlin syndrome21, while replication defects contribute to skeletal disorders with impaired OB function22. MCM5 has also been linked to bladder carcinoma progression23, and endometrial carcinoma24. Given its dual roles in replication and disease, we hypothesize that MCM5 may act as a hub connecting FTH1 dysregulation, ferroptosis, and osteoblast dysfunction under metabolic stress. WNT signaling, a key regulator of bone metabolism, promotes OB differentiation and bone formation while inhibiting OC genesis25,26. Following injury, it activates proliferation genes (Cyclin D1, c-Myc), driving G1–S cell-cycle transition and regeneration27; for example, WNT activation boosts alveolar stem cell proliferation by > 50%28.

In the present study, we employed quantitative LFQ-DIA proteomics combined with functional assays to systematically characterize protein expression changes and dissect the molecular network linking ferroptosis to osteogenic differentiation in MC3T3-E1 cells cultured under high glucose and high fat (HGHF) conditions following lentiviral-mediated knockdown (KD) or overexpression (OE) of FTH1. Our analysis identified numerous FTH1-regulated differentially expressed proteins (DEPs)—including MCM5, glucose-6-phosphate isomerase (GPI), FAM241A, HIF-1α, and LRRC58—that participate in glycolysis, glutathione metabolism, and ferroptosis pathways, underscoring FTH1’s critical role in maintaining cellular iron homeostasis and modulating aberrant bone metabolism under diabetic-mimetic stress. Further protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis, together with pharmacological inhibition of ferroptosis by deferoxamine (DFO) and targeted gene silencing, revealed that FTH1KD markedly upregulates Mcm family members particularly MCM5 as hub proteins. Importantly, either DFO treatment or MCM5KD reduced ferroptosis and partially rescued osteogenic differentiation. Collectively, our findings illuminate a novel FTH1-centered regulatory network linking iron dysregulation to OBs ferroptosis and differentiation blockade under HGHF conditions, and nominate MCM5 as a potential therapeutic target for DMOP.

Results

Data quality control

Prior to mass-spectrometric analysis, we first assessed transduction efficiency by immunofluorescence in MC3T3-E1 cells infected at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 20 for 48 h (Fig. 1A). Over 90% of cells exhibited robust green fluorescence. We then generated lentiviral FTH1KD + HGPA (KD group) and FTH1OE + HGPA (OE group) groups, along with vector control (NC group) and vector + HGPA controls (HGPA group). Western blot (WB) confirmed successful FTH1 knockdown and overexpression (Fig. 1B). After 14 days of culture, total protein was extracted and evaluated by SDS–PAGE, demonstrating intact bands and sufficient quantity for LC-MS/MS (Fig. S3C). Mass spectrometry identified 74,207 unique peptides corresponding to 7679 proteins, of which 7666 were quantitatively comparable across all groups (Fig. 1C). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear clustering of KD and OE samples away from the HGPA control (Fig. 1D). Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) analysis showed high inter-group correlation, with KD and OE each correlating with HGPA at values approaching 1.0 (Fig. 1E). A boxplot of relative standard deviations (RSD) for protein quantitation across replicates demonstrated low RSD values, indicating excellent quantitative reproducibility (Fig. 1F). Violin plots of protein intensity distributions confirmed uniform mean intensities across samples, reflecting consistent sample quality (Fig. S3D). Peptides length distribution analysis showed that most peptides ranged from 7 to 20 amino acids, with 11-residue peptides being most abundant; both peptide length and corresponding protein molecular weight distributions met quality-control criteria (Figs. S3E–F). Finally, density plots of log2-transformed protein intensities across all samples displayed a dominant peak at approximately 4.0, indicating the greatest protein count in this intensity range (Fig.S3G). Collectively, these metrics confirm that our proteomic dataset is of high quality—peptides are uniformly distributed by length, quantity, and molecular weight—and provide a solid foundation for subsequent in-depth analyses.

DEPs screening

Prior to proteomic profiling, DEPs were defined by P-value < 0.05 and a fold-change > 1.5 for up‐regulation or < 1/1.5 for down‐regulation. Figure 1G shows the number of DEPs for each pairwise comparison among the four groups: the FTH1KD + HGPA (KD/HGPA) group exhibited the greatest number of changes, with 319 proteins up‐regulated and 538 down‐regulated. To visualize relative expression levels across comparisons, we generated a heatmap of all DEPs, which highlighted the pronounced differences in the KD/HGPA group (Fig. 1H), The heatmap color scale represents log-transformed protein expression values, and the normalized protein intensities (LFQ values) after MaxQuant processing were log-transformed before differential expression and clustering analysis. Volcano plots for HGPA versus NC, KD/HGPA, KD/OE, and OE/HGPA comparisons (Fig. 1I–L) revealed the top five up‐ and down‐regulated proteins in each contrast: HGPA/NC: top 5 up, FGG, Q8R3Y5, ZFP26, MFAP3l, FGA; top 5 down, LRRC58, KLB, RCBTB1, RHBDD2, ATP5MC2. KD/HGPA: top 5 up, FTL1, PTH1R, PANX3, IBSP, LRRC58; top 5 down, CRYAB, F13A1, TNN, AHSG, DPEP1. KD/OE: top 5 up, FTL1, PHEX, IBSP, LRRC58, PANX3; top 5 down, PTX3, F13A1, DPEP1, ALDH1A7, AHSG. OE/HGPA: top 5 up, HAPLN1, THAP4, HJURP, IRS2, NOL12; top 5 down, CRYAB, UGT1A7, GGT5, NMES1, SYBU. The full lists of the 30 most significantly altered proteins for each comparison are provided in Supplementary Table S1. We then plotted radar charts of the top-30 DEPs to compare relative expression changes across groups (Fig. 1M–P). In the HGPA/NC comparison, APOD, RHBDD2, and ABAT exhibited the greatest spikes; in KD/HGPA, FTL1, CRYAB, and PZP dominated; in KD/OE, FTL1, PHEX, and SLIT2 were most prominent; and in OE/HGPA, CRYAB, TGM2, and TNC were highlighted. Notably, several of these DEPs play established roles in osteogenesis, ferroptosis, and metabolic regulation. FGFs are broad mitogens that regulate migration, proliferation, differentiation, and survival; among them, FGF15 (and its human ortholog FGF19) is an enteric hormone that, via the FGFR4–KLB co‐receptor complex, inhibits hepatic bile acid synthesis and regulates systemic energy and glucose homeostasis29. Parathyroid hormone 1 receptor (PTH1R), a class B G protein–coupled receptor activating adenylyl cyclase and phosphoinositide–calcium signaling, mediates PTH-driven bone remodeling in OBs/osteocytes; its mutation underlies Jansen’s and Blomstrand chondrodysplasias, underscoring its importance in OB differentiation30,31. Ferritin light chain 1 (FTL1) and pannexin-3 (PANX3) have been implicated in ferroptosis, obesity/metabolic disease, and musculoskeletal disorders; carvedilol attenuates hypoxia/reoxygenation–induced cardiomyocyte injury by up‐regulating GPX4, FTH1, and FTL1 mRNA to suppress lipid peroxidation32. PANXs form large‐pore glycoprotein channels expressed in skin, bone, and cochlea; bone marrow stromal cells sense OB‐released ATP via P2RX4–PANX3 signaling to regulate osteogenesis33,34. Klotho beta (KLB), a single‐pass transmembrane co‐receptor for FGF21, fine‐tunes glucose‐stimulated insulin secretion by modulating β-cell glycolysis independent of FGF21’s endocrine actions35. Leucine rich repeat containing protein 58 (LRRC58) is predicted to participate in intracellular signaling and to regulate cysteine and H₂S production36. Finally, hyaluronan and proteoglycan link protein 1 (HAPLN1), a component of collagenous extracellular matrix, binds hyaluronan and proteoglycans to stabilize ECM architecture and is implicated in skeletal and neural development37.In summary, these DEPs—including members of the FGF family, PTH1R, FTL1, PANX3, KLB, LRRC58, and HAPLN1—are integrally involved in osteogenic differentiation, ferroptosis, and metabolic regulation. They establish a novel protein–interaction framework for bone–lipid crosstalk under metabolic stress and nominate multiple candidate targets for future mechanistic studies in DMOP.

Functional classification of deps

We annotated the DEPs by subcellular localization. Rose plots (Fig. 2A–D) show that in the HGPA/NC comparison, DEPs were predominantly extracellular (35.48%) and nuclear (32.26%); in the KD/HGPA and KD/OE groups, DEPs localized mainly to cytoplasm and nucleus; whereas in OE/HGPA, DEPs were chiefly nuclear (24.03%), cytoplasmic (21.71%), and extracellular (20.93%). Next, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) classification and enrichment analyses of DEPs. At the level-2 classification, all comparisons (HGPA/NC, KD/HGPA, KD/OE, OE/HGPA) were dominated by regulation-related Biological Processes, intracellular structural Components, and protein-binding Functions (Fig. 2E–H), indicating shared perturbations in regulation, subcellular architecture, and protein interactions.COG/KOG analysis revealed distinct functional shifts: HGPA/NC DEPs were enriched in cell cycle and energy conversion; KD/HGPA and KD/OE groups showed enrichment in signal transduction, transcription, and carbohydrate metabolism; while OE/HGPA DEPs highlighted ribosomal biogenesis, translation, and secondary metabolite metabolism (Fig. S3H–K). These patterns suggest that under HGHF stress, FTH1 knockdown favors metabolic reprogramming through signaling and transcriptional regulation, whereas FTH1 overexpression preserves redox and biosynthetic capacity.GO enrichment further underscored these functional differences: HGPA/NC DEPs were associated with cell adhesion and inflammatory regulation; KD/HGPA and KD/OE groups with pyruvate/ADP metabolism; and OE/HGPA with retinoid and monocarboxylic acid metabolism (Fig. 3A–D). Circos and chord plots (Figs. 3E–L) visualized the links between enriched terms and representative DEPs, highlighting context-specific drivers such as GPX1, ALDH1A3, and CRYAB. These results establish FTH1 as a molecular switch coordinating proliferation, signaling, and iron-metabolic homeostasis under glucolipotoxic stress.

Overall, these GO enrichment results indicate that FTH1KD may drive compensatory energy stress via activation of the pyruvate–ADP metabolic axis and associated signaling pathways, whereas FTH1OE appears to maintain redox homeostasis by modulating diterpenoid/retinoid metabolism and enhancing ribosomal function. Thus, under a HGHF microenvironment, FTH1 acts as a pivotal molecular switch governing cellular metabolic plasticity, inflammatory response, and ferroptosis defense, offering new targeted intervention strategies for metabolic bone disease.

Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment

To further elucidate the functional mechanisms of DEPs, KEGG pathway enrichment was performed. In HGPA/NC, pathways related to complement/coagulation and platelet activation were predominant, reflecting inflammatory activation. In KD/HGPA, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and fructose–mannose metabolism were enriched, alongside ferroptosis, suggesting that FTH1 knockdown reprograms energy metabolism while sensitizing cells to iron-dependent oxidative stress. In contrast, KD/OE highlighted retinol metabolism and xenobiotic detoxification, while OE/HGPA emphasized glutathione metabolism and redox balance (Fig. 3M–P).

Together, these results indicate that FTH1 knockdown favors metabolic reprogramming to sustain energy demand, whereas FTH1 overexpression confers protection through redox and detoxification pathways. Thus, FTH1 emerges as a central regulator linking inflammation, metabolism, and ferroptosis defense under glucolipotoxic stress.

PPI network construction

To further investigate the key mediators of FTH1KD-induced perturbations, we focused on the KD/HGPA group, which showed the most pronounced proteomic changes. PPI network construction using STRING (score > 0.9) identified 793 high-confidence interactions (Fig. 4A). Module analysis highlighted the MCM2–7 complex, with MCM5 as the top hub protein (Fig. 4B). These findings suggest that replication licensing factors, particularly MCM5, may couple ferroptosis-related metabolic stress with genomic instability, thereby influencing osteoblast fate under glucolipotoxic conditions.

HGHF-mediated knockdown of FTH1 induces ferroptosis and upregulates MCM5, thereby inhibiting osteogenic differentiation

To verify the accuracy of the DEPs identified by our proteomic analysis, we selected MCM5—the top-scoring hub in our previously constructed PPI network—for further study. MCM5 has been reported to play critical roles in cell-cycle regulation, ferroptosis, and bone-metabolism disorders38,39. As an essential subunit of the MCM2–7 complex, MCM5 not only participates in both the initiation and elongation phases of “once-per-cell-cycle” DNA replication, but also serves within the CDC45–MCM–GINS (CMG) helicase core that unwinds template DNA and orchestrates replisome assembly40. Our proteomic data revealed that MCM5 was significantly upregulated in the FTH1KD group versus the HGPA control (ratio 1.81154), a finding we confirmed by WB (Fig. 4C). Moreover, the WNT/β-catenin pathway is recognized as a central regulatory axis in bone metabolism, maintaining skeletal development, homeostasis, and repair by balancing OBs and OCs activity. Prior studies have shown that FoxA1 knockdown activates ERK1/2 signaling to prevent ovariectomy-induced bone loss and thereby promotes osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs)26,41,42. Under HGHF treatment, Western blotting demonstrated that FTH1KD markedly decreased expression of the osteogenic markers ​​alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and OCN (Fig. 4C), while also downregulating WNT3A, β-catenin, and the ferroptosis-inhibitory proteins SLC7A11 and GPX4 (Fig. 4D). ALP and Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining further confirmed reduced ALP activity and fewer mineralized nodules upon FTH1 silencing (Fig. 4E).Using ferrous-ion assays, dihydroethylenediamine (DHE) staining for intracellular ROS, and JC-1 measurements of mitochondrial membrane potential, we observed that FTH1KD led to ferrous-iron accumulation and elevated ROS levels, concomitant with a significant loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 4F–H).Taken together, these results suggest that in an HGHF milieu, FTH1 silencing in OBs causes intracellular Fe²⁺ accumulation and ROS elevation, thereby inducing ferroptosis. This is accompanied by upregulation of MCM5, shifting cells toward a state of suppressed differentiation and enhanced proliferation, ultimately resulting in downregulated osteogenic differentiation.

DFO reverses FTH1 knockdown-induced ferroptosis, and MCM5 Silencing activates WNT signaling to promote osteogenic differentiation

Next, under HGHF conditions, MC3T3-E1 cells were treated with DFO (10 µM) or subjected to MCM5KD. ALP and ARS staining showed that, compared with the FTH1KD group, both the FTH1KD + DFO group and the FTH1KD + MCMKD group exhibited markedly increased ALP activity and mineralized nodule formation (Fig. 4I). FerroOrange assays revealed that intracellular ferrous-iron levels were reduced in both the FTH1KD + DFO and FTH1KD + MCMKD groups relative to FTH1KD alone (Fig. 4J). DHE fluorescence demonstrated lower ROS accumulation in these two intervention groups compared with FTH1KD (Fig. 4K). JC-1 staining indicated that mitochondrial membrane potential was restored in both the FTH1KD + DFO and FTH1KD + MCMKD groups relative to FTH1KD (Fig. 5A). Transmission electron microscopy further showed that mitochondrial membranes and cristae remained intact in the FTH1KD + DFO and FTH1KD + MCMKD groups, in contrast to the disrupted ultrastructure seen with FTH1KD alone (Fig. 5B). CCK-8 assays revealed that HGHF treatment reduced cell viability (OD) relative to NC; FTH1KD further decreased OD. Interestingly, DFO cotreatment restored OD in the HGHF + FTH1KD + DFO group, whereas MCM5KD reduced OD in the HGHF + FTH1KD + MCMKD group (Fig. 5C). Western blotting confirmed that, compared with FTH1KD alone, both DFO treatment and MCMKD decreased MCM5 expression while increasing ALP, OCN, SLC7A11, GPX4, WNT3A, and β-catenin levels (Fig. 5D–E). Collectively, these data indicate that HGHF suppresses OBs proliferation, an effect exacerbated by FTH1KD. DFO rescues proliferation and reverses FTH1KD induced ferroptosis, whereas MCM5KD similarly promotes osteogenic differentiation—albeit at the expense of some proliferative capacity—thereby facilitating bone mineralization.

Discussion

DMOP is one of the most common chronic complications of diabetes, imposing a long-term challenge to patient health and public-health expenditures43. Recent studies have revealed a close link between ferroptosis and DMOP pathology44,45. To further understand this process, we applied label-free LFQ-DIA proteomics combined with bioinformatic and functional analyses. Our findings highlight MCM5 as a potential hub factor downstream of FTH1KD, suggesting that it may coordinate OBs proliferation, differentiation, and ferroptosis under HGHF conditions. This work provides new mechanistic insights into ferroptosis in DMOP and suggests novel molecular targets for therapeutic intervention.

Proteomic approaches have been instrumental in dissecting the interplay between ferroptosis and bone loss. In this study, we selected trichloroacetic acid (TCA) precipitation for protein extraction. Compared with methanol or acetonitrile methods, TCA provides higher protein recovery and more efficient removal of salts, lipids, and other small molecules that may interfere with enzymatic digestion and LC–MS/MS. These advantages are particularly important for samples exposed to high-glucose and high-fat conditions, where lipid and metabolite accumulation is prominent. Thus, TCA precipitation improves protein purity and enhances the reproducibility of downstream proteomic analyses. All experiments were performed in biological triplicate, which represents the minimum standard for replication. Although this design provides reasonable confidence in the observed trends, the results should be interpreted with caution and validated in future studies with larger sample sizes. Xu et al. used quantitative proteomics to demonstrate that ferroptosis contributes to age-related OP, and that vitamin D receptor (VDR) activation mitigates OB ferroptosis via the Nrf2/GPX4 axis46. Proteomic profiling of clinical samples from steroid-induced osteonecrosis of the femoral head (SIONFH) patients revealed that SIRT3 regulates mitochondrial homeostasis and ferroptosis, and that restoring SIRT3 suppresses BNIP3/NIX-mediated pathological mitophagy47. Moreover, Xu and colleagues combined proteomics with ELISA in osteoporotic cohorts to identify COPB1 as a critical regulator: COPB1 deficiency impaired OB osteogenesis, induced ferroptosis and endoplasmic-reticulum stress, and was shown to act via ATF6-dependent repression of SLC7A11 transcription, thereby limiting cystine uptake and triggering cell death48.These and other studies underscore the power of proteomics for mapping ferroptosis-related networks in DMOP and for mining deep molecular mechanisms. Here, our proteomic analysis mapped the effects of HGHF treatment and FTH1 perturbation (KD/OE) on OBs, highlighting pathways through which ferroptosis may impair osteogenic differentiation. Notably, DEPs were enriched in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, glutathione metabolism, and HIF-1 signaling—pathways closely associated with redox balance and ferroptosis. Indeed, Fang et al. demonstrated that the SWI/SNF subunit ACTL6A protects gastric-cancer cells from ferroptosis by inducing glutathione synthesis49. Thus, FTH1KD not only governs ferroptosis, but also perturbs glucose metabolism and cellular repair, reinforcing its central role in OB dysfunction. PPI network analysis identified multiple MCM family members (MCM2, MCM3, MCM4, MCM5, MCM7) and the glycolytic enzyme GPI as hub proteins. Beyond its canonical role in cytosolic glycolysis50, GPI exerts extracellular neurotrophic and angiogenic functions51, supporting the survival of motor neurons and endothelial cells in bone52. GPI also modulates glucose metabolism via interaction with oxidative-stress regulator 1, thereby influencing neuronal survival53; its overexpression enhances glycolysis and delays senescence, whereas loss of GPI shortens cellular lifespan54. Collectively, these data point to GPI as a key regulator of OB glycolysis and survival.

MCM5 emerged as the highest-scoring hub protein in our PPI network, where it functions in activation of the DNA-replication initiation complex. Notably, Cai et al. recently showed that m⁶A-dependent regulation promotes epithelial–mesenchymal transition and metastasis in lung adenocarcinoma via the IGF2BP3/MCM5/Notch axis38. Moreover, MCM5 mutations cause Meier–Gorlin syndrome, characterized by skeletal dysplasia and severe growth retardation21, underscoring its importance in bone formation and development. To dissect MCM5’s role, we combined FTH1KD with either DFO or MCM5 silencing under HGHF conditions. Mechanistically, FTH1 loss diminishes iron-chelation, causing Fe²⁺ overload and ROS-driven lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and ferroptosis activation55. Concurrent MCM5 upregulation suggests a compensatory response linking ferroptosis to impaired OB proliferation and differentiation. We hypothesize that, in response to ROS-induced DNA damage, cells upregulate MCM5—potentially via HIF-1α and other transcription factors—to mitigate ferroptosis by sustaining proliferation, albeit at the expense of osteogenic differentiation and partial WNT pathway suppression. Co-treatment with DFO or MCM5KD on the FTH1-deficient background largely restored osteogenic phenotypes, normalizing Fe²⁺/ROS balance, mitochondrial integrity, and osteogenic protein expression, while reducing ferroptosis markers. Notably, DFO rescued proliferation to some extent, whereas MCM5KD further suppressed viability, suggesting functional divergence despite their comparable effects on differentiation. Together, these findings suggest that FTH1KD induced iron overload and ROS accumulation feedback to elevate MCM5 expression, thereby bolstering proliferation to counteract ferroptosis, but sacrificing osteogenic differentiation capacity due to diverted energy resources. This compensatory mechanism may be critical in the early ferroptosis response of DMOP.

Although our in vitro OB model validates this axis, further studies in DMOP animal models and clinical samples are needed to confirm physiological relevance and translational potential. A limitation of our study is that the current enrichment analysis pipeline did not generate adjusted p-values, and thus GO terms were ranked based on unadjusted p-values in combination with the number of mapped proteins. While this approach provides useful insights, it may overestimate the significance of broader categories. In future work, we plan to employ alternative analysis platforms or statistical methods that incorporate false discovery rate (FDR) correction, thereby enabling GO term ranking by adjusted p-values and improving the robustness of enrichment interpretation.

The direct molecular link by which FTH1 downregulation under HGHF upregulates MCM5 also warrants elucidation. Moreover, the complex in vivo DMOP microenvironment—including BMSC–adipocyte crosstalk and systemic inflammatory mediators—may modulate the FTH1/MCM5 axis, meriting future investigation. While our results highlight MCM5 as a potential mediator connecting metabolic stress, ferroptosis, and osteoblast dysfunction, these observations are presently limited to cellular and proteomic levels. Direct evidence from animal models or patient-derived samples is still lacking, and thus the translational implications should be interpreted with caution. Future studies will be necessary to determine whether modulation of MCM5 activity can indeed influence bone homeostasis or therapeutic outcomes in diabetic osteoporosis. Our findings identify MCM5 as a potential hub linking FTH1 dysregulation, ferroptosis, and osteoblast dysfunction under metabolic stress. However, these results are preclinical and mechanistic, and direct therapeutic implications cannot yet be drawn. Validation in animal models of diabetic osteoporosis and patient-derived samples will be essential. Thus, this study should be viewed as a mechanistic framework that generates hypotheses for future in vivo investigations. Although our current study did not fully elucidate the upstream mechanism underlying the upregulation of Mcm5 upon FTH1 loss, it is plausible that transcriptional regulators sensitive to oxidative stress may be involved. For example, FTH1 deficiency may increase intracellular ROS levels, which could in turn activate ROS-responsive elements or HIF-1α, thereby contributing to the transcriptional induction of Mcm5. Related literature also reported that reducing FTH1 levels can lead to the accumulation of intracellular free iron and ROS, resulting in mitochondrial damage and ferroptosis57.Future studies will be needed to clarify these potential regulatory pathways and further strengthen the mechanistic link.

In summary, our proteomic profiling systematically delineates the impact of HGHF treatment and FTH1 perturbation on the OB proteome. We demonstrate that HGHF-induced FTH1 downregulation triggers Fe²⁺/ROS accumulation, ferroptosis activation, and WNT/β-catenin inhibition, thereby suppressing osteogenic differentiation. Mechanistically, MCM5 upregulation represents a compensatory response to ferroptosis stress that preserves proliferation at the cost of differentiation. This proposed mechanism is summarized in the schematic diagram (Fig. 5F), which integrates our findings and highlights the hypothesized FTH1–MCM5–WNT axis.These findings define the dual regulatory role of the FTH1/MCM5 axis in OB ferroptosis and function, and nominate it as a promising therapeutic target in DMOP.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

MC3T3-E1 preosteoblastic cells were obtained from the National Cell Culture Collection (Shanghai, China). Cells were maintained in α-minimum essential medium (α-MEM; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Oricell, Guangzhou, China) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂. Culture medium was refreshed every 48 h, and cells were passaged approximately every 3 days. To induce osteogenic differentiation, culture medium was further supplemented with ascorbic acid (50 mg/L; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and β-glycerophosphate (5 mM; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). All experiments were conducted under these osteoinductive conditions using cells between passages 6 and 10 to ensure phenotypic stability and experimental consistency.

HGHF intervention

Consistent with prior studies56, the HGPA intervention was performed by supplementing culture medium with 25 mmol/L glucose (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and 0.2 mmol/L palmitic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) to mimic a HGHF environment. Cells were maintained under these conditions for 14 days to induce the effects of glucolipotoxicity. This model conforms to established standards for studying glucolipotoxic stress and effectively recapitulates the HGHF milieu characteristic of metabolic disorders such as DM.

Lentiviral transduction of FTH1 and MCM5

The mouse FTH1 and MCM5 coding sequence was retrieved and used to design both knockdown and overexpression constructs via the Invitrogen RNAi Designer (https://rnaidesigner.thermofisher.com/). For RNAi-mediated silencing, we selected the following target sequence: FTH1-RNAi(107779-1): GACTTCATTGAGACGTATTAT, MCM5-RNAi(107780-1): GCAAGAUGAUCUACGAUAUTT. For overexpression, primers flanking the FTH1 open reading frame were designed as follows: Forward primer: 5′-GGGTCAATATGTAATTTTCAGTG-3′,Reverse primer: 5′-CCTTATAGTCCTTATCATCGTC-3′.Each insert was cloned into a lentiviral vector and packaged into viral particles. Target cells were transduced with these lentiviruses, and 48 h post-infection, FTH1 protein levels were assessed by fluorescence imaging (reporter) and by quantitative Western blotting to confirm efficient knockdown or overexpression.

Protein extraction and digestion

Samples were slowly equilibrated to room temperature, followed by the addition of four volumes of lysis buffer (8 M urea, 1% protease inhibitor; Jingjie Biotechnology, Hangzhou, China) and ultrasonic disruption. Cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the protein concentration in the supernatant was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein were precipitated by the addition of trichloroacetic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) to a final concentration of 20%, vortexed, incubated on ice for 2 h, and pelleted by centrifugation at 4,500 × g for 5 min. The pellets were washed three times with pre-cooled acetone (Sigma-Aldrich) and air-dried. The dried pellets were resuspended in 200 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB, pH 8.5; Sigma-Aldrich) and sonicated until completely dispersed. Reduction was performed by adding dithiothreitol (DTT, final concentration 5 mM; Sigma-Aldrich) and incubating at 56 °C for 30 min, followed by alkylation with iodoacetamide (IAA, final concentration 11 mM) at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. Finally, proteins were digested with trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) at a 1:50 (enzyme: substrate, w/w) ratio and incubated overnight at 37 °C.

LC–MS/MS analysis

Prior to LC-MS/MS analysis, the peptide mixture was desalted using C18 StageTips (Thermo Scientific) to remove salts and contaminants. Peptide mixtures were reconstituted in mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid [LC–MS grade, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA], 2% acetonitrile [LC–MS grade, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA]; pH 2.7–3.0) and separated on an Easy-nLC 1000 system (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using a step gradient of mobile phase B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile ; pH 2.7–3.0) at 450 nL/min: 9–24% B over 8 min, 24–35% B over 8–12 min, 35–90% B over 12–16 min, hold at 90% B for 16–20 min. Eluted peptides were ionized via a nano-ESI source (1.75 kV ; Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) and analyzed on a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) in dia-PASEF mode. MS1 scans were acquired from m/z 100–1,700, followed by 10 PASEF MS/MS scans; MS/MS spectra were collected from m/z 400–1,200 with 25 Th isolation window. PASEF acquisition parameters – The number of PASEF scans per cycle (10), the ion accumulation and ramp time (100 ms), and the quadrupole isolation window.Collision energy settings – Collision-induced dissociation (CID) with stepped collision energy of 20/40/60 eV was applied to optimize peptide fragmentation.TOF analyzer resolution – The timsTOF Pro instrument was operated at a resolving power of ~ 60,000 at m/z 1222, ensuring high mass accuracy across the entire acquisition.

Protein identification and quantification

The raw mass spectrometry data were imported into DIA-NN (v1.8) software for spectral library-free data processing. Default parameters were used to search against the Mus_musculus_10090_SP_20230103.fasta database (17,132 sequences). Trypsin/P was specified as the digestion enzyme, with a maximum of one missed cleavage; fixed modifications included N-terminal methionine excision and C-terminal carbamidomethylation. The theoretical spectral library was constructed using the integrated deep-learning algorithm, and a decoy database was added to estimate false discovery rates (FDR). Both precursor- and protein-level identifications were filtered at FDR ≤ 1%, requiring at least one unique peptide.Normalized intensity values of each protein in each sample were exported and log2-transformed. To account for multiple testing across > 7,000 quantified proteins, differential expression analysis was performed using a two-sample, two-tailed Student’s t-test, followed by Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) false discovery rate correction. Proteins with adjusted p-value (adj.P) < 0.05 and fold change > 1.5 were considered significantly differentially expressed.For biological replicates, the average relative quantification value for each protein was calculated, and the consistency of replicates was evaluated using Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC), principal component analysis (PCA), and relative standard deviation (RSD). This workflow reduces the likelihood of false positives and enhances the robustness of DEP identification. Before the analysis of the current history, the change in the strength of the change in the log₂ change, the difference in the direction of the change, and the maximum limit of the decrease in the strength of the protein quality deviation. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine ​​was set as a fixed modification, while oxidation of methionine was considered a variable modification. Spectral libraries were constructed using Spectronaut (Biognosys, Switzerland), and a reverse sequence decoy library was generated to allow target-decoy-based FDR estimation at the peptide and protein levels.

Bioinformatic analysis

DEPs were defined by fold-change > 1.5 and p < 0.05. GO classification (biological process, cellular component, molecular function) was performed via geneontology.org. KEGG pathway annotation (release 113.0) was assigned using KAAS and mapped with KEGG Mapper; enrichment was tested by two-tailed Fisher’s exact test with adjusted p < 0.05. A PPI network for 857 DEPs was constructed in STRING v13.0 (full network, evidence channels: text mining, experiments, databases, co-expression, neighborhood, co-occurrence, gene fusion; confidence ≥ 0.9), removing disconnected nodes. The MCODE plugin in Cytoscape v3.10.3 identified the top 18 hub proteins by cluster score.

Western blotting

After treatment, cells were lysed on ice for 15 min in RIPA buffer (4 °C, pH 7.4; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), and total protein was collected by centrifugation. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA), which were blocked in 5% non-fat milk (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies (all 1:1,000): FTH1 (ab266581, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), OCN (ab133612, Abcam), ALP (ab307726, Abcam), GPX4 (ab262509, Abcam), SLC7A11 (ab307601, Abcam), WNT3A (ab219412, Abcam), β-catenin (ab32572, Abcam), and β-actin (AF7018, Affinity Biosciences, Changzhou, China). After three washes in TBST (20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20; pH 7.4), membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Blots were developed using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and band intensities were quantified in ImageJ (v2.1.4.7).

ARS staining

Treated cells were seeded in 6-well plates and grown to confluence. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; pH 7.4; Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 20 min at room temperature, then washed three times with PBS (pH 7.4; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Fixed cells were stained with 0.2% Alizarin Red S (pH 4.2; G1405, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 30 min at room temperature. Excess dye was removed by thorough washes with distilled water. After staining with 2% Alizarin Red S, the bound dye was extracted using 10% (w/v) cetylpyridinium chloride (CPC; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) solution adjusted to pH 7.0, and the absorbance was measured at 562 nm with a microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

ALP staining

Cells were grown in 6-well plates to ~ 80% confluence, fixed in 4% PFA (pH 7.4; Solarbio) for 15 min at room temperature, and washed three times with PBS (pH 7.4; Gibco). ALP activity was visualized using a commercial kit (D7027, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Cells were incubated in ALP staining solution at 37 °C for 30–60 min until brown coloration appeared, then rinsed with distilled water. Stained wells were imaged under an inverted microscope (Olympus CKX41, Tokyo, Japan), and ALP-positive area was quantified in ImageJ (v2.1.4.7). Experiments were repeated at least three times.

Intracellular Fe²⁺ measurement (FerroOrange)

Culture medium was removed and cells were washed three times with HBSS (pH 7.4; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were incubated with 1 µM FerroOrange (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Kumamoto, Japan) for 30 min at 37 °C, 5% CO₂. Without further washes, fluorescence was captured by microscopy, and intracellular Fe²⁺ was quantified in ImageJ (v2.1.4.7).

ROS measurement (DHE Assay)

Cells were washed with PBS (pH 7.4; Gibco) and incubated with 5 µM dihydroethidium (DHE; S0064S, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 30 min at 37 °C. Following incubation, ROS-dependent DHE fluorescence was imaged, and mean fluorescence intensity was quantified in ImageJ.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) by JC-1

Cells in 12-well plates were incubated with JC-1 dye (C2003S, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 30 min at 37 °C. After two PBS (pH 7.4; Gibco) washes, red (aggregates) and green (monomers) fluorescence were immediately imaged by fluorescence microscopy (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The red/green ratio was calculated in ImageJ as a measure of ΔΨm. The experiments were performed using an Olympus IX83 inverted fluorescence microscope, and JC-1 monomers were detected at Ex 485 nm / Em 530 nm (green channel) and JC-1 aggregates were detected at Ex 540 nm / Em 590 nm (red channel).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Cells were fixed in iPGell (GenoStaff, Tokyo, Japan), post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA) at 4 °C for 90 min, dehydrated through graded ethanol (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent, Shanghai, China) with epoxypropane, and embedded in epoxy resin (Electron Microscopy Sciences). Ultrathin Section (75 nm) were mounted on copper grids and examined under a Hitachi H-7650 TEM (Hitachi High-Technologies, Tokyo, Japan). TEM images were acquired at magnifications of 8,000× and 20,000×. The instrument was operated at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV. Prior to imaging, ultrathin sections were stained with 2% uranyl acetate followed by lead citrate to enhance contrast. Images were obtained using a conventional TEM imaging system equipped with a digital CCD camera under standard bright-field mode.

Cell viability assay (CCK-8 assay)

Cell viability was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan). Cells were seeded into 96-well plates, treated as indicated, and incubated with 10 µL CCK-8 reagent for 1–2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured with a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy HTX, Agilent Technologies, USA). Relative viability was calculated against the control group, and all assays were performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed in SPSS 17.0. Two-group comparisons used two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test; multiple groups were compared by one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc tests. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p -value < 0.05).
