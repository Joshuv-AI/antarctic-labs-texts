Body roundness index and mortality risk in chronic diseases: a national prospective longitudinal study in China
Authors: Xuxin Sun, Qian Deng, Zhongtang Li, Chizhen Lin, Gaofeng Song, Sheng Chen, Yihou Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe body roundness index (BRI) has emerged as a refined anthropometric indicator that integrates waist circumference and height to quantify body shape characteristics and metabolic risk. However, its longitudinal associations with mortality across diverse chronic diseases remain underexplored. This study aims to investigate these associations and their potential mechanisms.MethodsThis longitudinal study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), covering a 9-year follow-up from baseline (2011–2012) to 2020. The study cohort comprised 11,750 middle-aged and older Chinese adults. BRI was calculated using waist circumference and height. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality. Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess associations, restricted cubic spline (RCS) models explored nonlinear effects, and Kaplan-Meier survival curves provided survival rate estimates.ResultsHigher BRI levels conferred significant protective effects against all-cause mortality in the overall population (HR = 0.94, 95% CI = 0.89–0.98), hypertension (HR = 0.93, 95% CI = 0.87–0.98), lung disease (HR = 0.87, 95% CI = 0.78–0.97), asthma (HR = 0.77, 95% CI = 0.63–0.95), and dyslipidemia (HR = 0.90, 95% CI = 0.84–0.97). Conversely, elevated BRI increased liver disease mortality risk (HR = 1.32, 95% CI = 1.04–1.68). RCS modelling revealed significant non-linear relationships between BRI and mortality risk for hypertension and lung disease (non-linear P < 0.05), whereas the associations with dyslipidemia, asthma and the overall population remained essentially linear (non-linear P > 0.05).ConclusionsBRI is a multifaceted predictor of chronic disease mortality, with associations varying by disease pathophysiology and population characteristics. It offers a pragmatic tool for refining risk stratification in aging populations and challenges one-size-fits-all approaches to obesity management. Future research should investigate dynamic BRI trajectories and interactions with disease-specific biomarkers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-025-03252-9.

Background

The escalating global burden of chronic diseases poses significant challenges to healthcare infrastructure and socioeconomic stability, with aging populations bearing a disproportionate share of this impact [1]. China exemplifies this crisis, where demographic shifts are projected to expand the population aged ≥ 65 years to 400 million by mid-century. Compounding this issue, over three-quarters of Chinese older adults (≥ 60 years) live with at least one chronic condition [2], amplifying demands for innovative strategies to manage multimorbidity and its cascading effects. Conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disorders now represent a pressing public health priority, particularly in resource-limited settings where healthcare systems struggle to meet growing needs [3].

Emerging as a refined anthropometric indicator, the body roundness index (BRI) integrates waist circumference and height to quantify body shape characteristics and metabolic risk. Distinct from conventional metrics like body mass index (BMI), BRI uniquely accounts for both central adiposity and body proportionality, enhancing its capacity to detect visceral fat accumulation and associated pathophysiological processes [4, 5]. Empirical evidence underscores its superiority over BMI in predicting cardiometabolic outcomes, including dyslipidemia and insulin resistance [6, 7]. Despite these advancements, critical knowledge gaps remain regarding BRI’s longitudinal associations with mortality across heterogeneous chronic diseases. Current literature predominantly relies on cross-sectional designs or single-disease analyses [8, 9], offering limited insights into temporal relationships and multimorbidity interactions. Furthermore, the modifying effects of coexisting chronic conditions on adiposity-mortality associations remain underexplored, necessitating rigorous adjustment for baseline comorbidity profiles. The absence of large prospective cohorts further impedes the translation of BRI into actionable clinical protocols for risk prediction and prevention.

To address these methodological gaps, this study leverages data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to investigate the longitudinal associations between BRI and mortality risks spanning 14 chronic diseases. Our research aims to characterize dose-response relationships between BRI and disease-specific mortality, evaluate the modifying role of baseline multimorbidity in these associations, and identify high-risk subgroups through stratified analyses. These findings seek to advance personalized risk stratification frameworks and inform evidence-based interventions for aging populations.

Methods

Data source

This study utilized data from the CHARLS, a nationally representative cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese community-dwelling adults [10]. CHARLS systematically collects comprehensive socioeconomic and health-related data through biennial surveys, with baseline data collected between June 2011 and March 2012 across 450 urban/rural communities in 28 Chinese provinces. Our analysis incorporated 9-year follow-up data from baseline. The study protocol was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), with all participants providing written informed consent.

Study design and population

Figure 1 illustrates the participant selection process. We excluded individuals with missing body roundness index (BRI) data (n = 4,079), incomplete survival data (n = 1,251), and those with missing demographic/covariate data, aged < 45 years, or deceased in 2011 (n = 625). The final analytical sample comprised 11,750 participants.

Primary outcome

All-cause mortality served as the primary endpoint, ascertained through follow-up interviews in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. Exact death dates were available for 2013 and 2020 cases, while survival time for other cases was estimated using median intervals between baseline and death year.

Assessment of diseases

For each participant, the disease status (yes or no) of 14 chronic diseases, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, heart disease, stroke, chronic lung disease, asthma, liver disease, cancer, gastrointestinal diseases, kidney disease, arthritis, mental disorders, and memory-related diseases, was confirmed through self-reports of physician diagnoses by the participants or in combination with health assessments and medication data from the 2011 CHARLS survey. Detailed definitions of these diseases are provided in Supplemental Material 1.

Measurement of BRI

BRI was assessed by measuring height and waist circumference (WC) to evaluate body shape. The height and WC data for this study were obtained from the participants’ baseline physical examinations and measured by professionals. To ensure accuracy, participants were required to remove their shoes and stand up straight when measuring height. WC was measured by placing a tape measure horizontally around the waist at the level of the umbilicus. Covariates

Based on published studies and clinical judgment, several variables were identified as potential confounding factors, including age, gender, marital status, education level, alcohol consumption, and smoking status [12, 13]. To address potential biological confounders and improve estimate precision, we further incorporated key laboratory indicators: C-reactive protein (CRP), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c). In addition, in recognition of the interrelation among chronic diseases and in line with previous studies, we included the history of the 14 chronic diseases at baseline as covariates for the disease cohorts (excluding the specific chronic disease under investigation in each cohort) to adjust for the impact of baseline history on outcome events.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were depicted according to the quartiles of BRI. Continuous variables are presented as mean and standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables are described using frequency counts and percentages (%). For continuous variables, independent sample t-tests were used for group comparisons, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare differences among groups. Chi-square tests were used to assess differences between categorical variables, and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used to evaluate differences between ordered categorical variables.

Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the association between BRI and incident chronic diseases. Model 1 was a crude model without any covariates. Model 2 was adjusted for age, gender, marital status, education level, alcohol consumption, and smoking status. Model 3 was adjusted for age, gender, marital status, education level, alcohol consumption, smoking status, CRP, TC, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, HbA1c and the history of 14 chronic diseases at baseline (excluding the specific chronic disease under investigation in each cohort). Categorical variables were treated as continuous variables to examine potential linear trends in the models. After adjusting for all potential confounders, restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were used to address potential non-linear effects, with 3 knots located at 10%, 50%, and 90%. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to estimate survival rates for each cohort based on baseline quartiles of BRI, and comparisons were made using the log-rank test. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.3). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

Table 1 summarises the baseline profile of 11,750 CHARLS participants across BRI quartiles. The overall cohort had a median age of 59.0 years, and 6,152 (52.4%) were women. A pronounced sex gradient was observed: the proportion of women rose steeply from 32.2% in the lowest quartile (Q1) to 75.5% in the highest (Q4). Higher BRI values were accompanied by a progressively heavier cardiometabolic burden. The prevalence of hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, heart disease, stroke and arthritis/rheumatism all increased monotonically across quartiles (all P < 0.001). Conversely, the prevalence of current smoking and alcohol consumption declined linearly with rising BRI. Lung disease displayed a modest, non-linear pattern, while digestive disease prevalence remained relatively stable. Laboratory values also paralleled BRI: median CRP, TC, TG, LDL-C and HbA1c levels increased across quartiles, whereas HDL-C declined (all P < 0.001).

Association of body roundness index with risk of death from chronic disease

As shown in Table 2, in the fully adjusted model (Model 3) each one-unit increment in BRI was associated with a 6% reduction in all-cause mortality in the overall population (HR = 0.94, 95% CI 0.89–0.98, P = 0.009), a 7% reduction among participants with hypertension (HR = 0.93, 95% CI 0.87–0.98, P = 0.012), a 13% reduction in those with lung disease (HR = 0.87, 95% CI 0.78–0.97, P = 0.016), a 23% reduction in those with asthma (HR = 0.77, 95% CI 0.63–0.95, P = 0.016), and a 10% reduction in those with dyslipidemia (HR = 0.90, 95% CI 0.84–0.97, P = 0.005). In contrast, BRI conferred a 32% higher mortality risk among participants with liver disease (HR = 1.32, 95% CI 1.04–1.68, P = 0.024).

Discussion

This longitudinal study elucidates the complex associations between BRI and mortality risks across multiple chronic diseases in middle-aged and older Chinese adults. Our analyses demonstrate that elevated BRI levels confer significant protective effects against all-cause mortality in the overall cohort (HR = 0.94), hypertension (HR = 0.93), lung disease (HR = 0.87), asthma (HR = 0.77), and dyslipidemia (HR = 0.90). Notably, participants in the highest BRI quartile exhibited markedly reduced mortality risks for hypertension and stroke, with robust dose-response relationships confirmed by RCS analyses. Conversely, higher BRI was associated with increased mortality risk in liver disease (HR = 1.32). Collectively, these findings challenge conventional obesity paradigms, suggesting that BRI’s sensitivity to body shape configuration—rather than absolute adiposity—may underpin its prognostic value.

BRI’s integration of waist circumference and height enables precise quantification of central obesity (visceral fat accumulation), addressing critical limitations of traditional BMI in assessing body composition [14]. While obesity is widely recognized as a health risk, emerging evidence supports the “obesity paradox,” wherein moderate adiposity may reduce mortality risk in specific populations [15]. This paradox is particularly pronounced in older adults [16], exemplified by studies showing lower mortality in overweight/obese patients with heart failure, chronic kidney disease [17], and COPD [18].A study based on the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) found a significant U-shaped relationship between BRI and all-cause mortality [9], with both low BRI (< 3.4) and high BRI (≥ 6.9) associated with increased mortality risk. However, such a U-shaped pattern was not observed in our study, which may be attributed to the limited sample size of individuals with extremely high BRI in our cohort, as well as differences in age, ethnicity, and other factors. Nevertheless, low BRI was indeed linked to elevated mortality risks for certain diseases. Another study investigating BRI levels and mortality risk among Chinese adults demonstrated that the additional risks of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and death associated with BRI diminished with advancing age, while a significantly harmful effect of low BRI on mortality was observed in individuals aged ≥ 75 years [19], aligning with our findings.

The study by Du et al. demonstrated that BRI was positively correlated with systemic inflammatory markers (e.g., neutrophil count, platelet count), while these inflammatory markers were negatively associated with levels of the aging biomarker α-Klotho [20], suggesting that BRI may influence chronic disease prognosis by modulating metabolic and inflammatory balance [21, 22]. The protective effects of BRI for cardiometabolic diseases (e.g., hypertension, dyslipidemia) may be mediated by its sensitivity to metabolically favorable fat distribution [23]. Unlike BMI, BRI’s integration of waist circumference and height captures both central adiposity and body proportionality, potentially identifying individuals with preserved muscle mass and reduced visceral fat [24]. For pulmonary diseases, higher BRI levels may reflect adequate energy reserves from subcutaneous adipose tissue, buffering metabolic stress during chronic respiratory exacerbations, while reduced smoking prevalence in higher BRI quartiles could partially explain this protective trend. These findings underscore BRI’s dual role as both a metabolic buffer and inflammation modulator, advocating its integration into multidimensional risk assessment frameworks for chronic inflammatory and respiratory conditions. However, future studies should address limitations such as static adiposity measurements and explore dynamic interactions between BRI trajectories and disease-specific biomarkers (e.g., CRP, eosinophil counts) to refine clinical applications.

While our study enhances understanding of BRI’s longitudinal associations, certain limitations exist. Firstly, static BRI measurements prevent assessment of dynamic adiposity changes impacting mortality. Secondly, residual confounding from unmeasured factors like dietary patterns and genetic predispositions remains despite thorough adjustments. Thirdly, the study’s generalizability to non-Chinese populations needs validation, especially in diverse ethnic cohorts.

Future research should investigate organ-specific fat distribution using imaging techniques such as MRI to better define BRI’s pathophysiological correlates. Developing dynamic BRI trajectories to evaluate how adiposity changes affect disease outcomes is also recommended. Furthermore, exploring interactions between BRI and inflammation biomarkers like CRP can clarify the mechanistic pathways involved.

Conclusion

This large-scale longitudinal study positions BRI as a multifaceted predictor of chronic disease mortality, with directionality and magnitude contingent on disease pathophysiology and population characteristics. By reconciling its protective and detrimental effects, BRI offers a pragmatic tool for refining risk stratification in aging populations, challenging one-size-fits-all approaches to obesity management.
