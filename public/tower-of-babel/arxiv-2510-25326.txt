Abstract
. We consider stochastically perturbed wave maps from ℝ 1 + d {\mathbb{R}}^{1+d} into 𝕊 d \mathbb{S}^{d} , in all energy-supercritical dimensions d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 . We show that corotational non-degenerate Gaussian additive noise leads to self-similar blowup with positive probability for any corotational initial data. The same result without noise is conjectured, but unknown, for large data.



1.
Introduction

The wave maps equation represents a generalization of the linear wave equation to maps that take values in Riemannian manifolds. More precisely, a map
\mathcal{U}
from the flat Minkowski spacetime
({\mathbb{R}}^{1+d},\eta)
into a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
is called a
wave map
if it is a critical point (under compactly supported perturbations) of the Lagrangian action functional

\mathcal{S}[\mathcal{U}]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{1+d}}\big(-|\partial_{t}\mathcal{U}|^{2}_{g}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\partial_{i}\mathcal{U}|_{g}^{2}\big)d\mu_{\eta}.

By putting local coordinates on
M
, and using the Einstein summation convention, we arrive at the following, more compact, form
1
1

1

Here, Greek indices go from 0 to
d
and Latin indices go from 1 to
n
, where
n
is the dimension of the target manifold
M
. According to the Einstein summation convention, whenever an index variable appears twice in a single term, once as a subscript and once as a superscript, it implies a summation over the understood range of values for that index. We also note that the indices are raised and lowered according to the Minkowski metric
\eta=\text{diag}(-1,1,\dots,1)
; in particular
\partial^{\alpha}=\eta^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\beta}
.

\mathcal{S}[\mathcal{U}]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{1+d}}g_{ij}(\mathcal{U})\partial_{\alpha}\mathcal{U}^{i}\partial^{\alpha}\mathcal{U}^{j}d\mu_{\eta}.

The associated Euler-Lagrange equations consequently read as

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\,\partial^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{U}^{i}+\Gamma^{i}_{jk}(\mathcal{U})\partial^{\mu}\mathcal{U}^{j}\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{U}^{k}=0,\quad i=1,\dots,n,\\
\,\mathcal{U}(0,\cdot)=\mathcal{U}_{0},\\
\,\partial_{t}\mathcal{U}(0,\cdot)=\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{0},\end{cases}

where
n
is the dimension of the manifold
M
,
\Gamma_{jk}^{i}
are the Christoffel symbols associated with the metric
g
, and
\partial_{t}
, according to custom, stands for
\partial_{0}
.

In contrast to the linear wave equation, the wave maps system (
1.1
) is inherently nonlinear due to the curvature of the target manifold
(M,g)
. From a dynamical perspective, a general goal is to understand the interplay between the geometry of the target and the behavior of solutions to the associated Cauchy problem (
1.1
). In particular, one of the central questions is that of finite-time blowup:

Can smooth and localized initial data
(\mathcal{U}_{0},\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{0})
lead to singularity formation for (
1.1
) in finite time?

Much research in the past fifty years has addressed this question, as we to some extent review in
Section 1.4
below. In this section we mention only (some of) the works relevant for our main results below.
We proceed by pointing out that, in the context of finite-time blowup, there is a useful guiding principle based on the
energy functional

(1.2)

\displaystyle E[\mathcal{U}](t):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\delta^{\alpha\beta}g_{ij}(\mathcal{U}(t,X))\partial_{\alpha}\mathcal{U}^{i}(t,X)\partial_{\beta}\mathcal{U}^{j}(t,X)dX,

which is formally conserved by solutions of the system, and the scaling transform
\mathcal{U}(t,X)\mapsto\mathcal{U}^{\lambda}(t,X):=\mathcal{U}(t/\lambda,X/\lambda)
, which preserves the wave maps equation (
1.1
). Namely, the functional
E
transforms under the scaling
\mathcal{U}\mapsto\mathcal{U}^{\lambda}
according to

\displaystyle E[\mathcal{U}^{\lambda}](t)=\lambda^{d-2}E[\mathcal{U}](t/\lambda),

which shows that shrinking the solution locally (i.e., letting
\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}
) leads to local energy dissipation precisely when
d\geq 3
. Singularity formation is therefore said to be energetically favourable in the so-called
energy-supercritical
case,
d\geq 3
, and one therefore heuristically expects finite-time blowup, at least for some large initial data.
We remark in passing that for the
energy-subcritical
case,
d=1
, where local energy dissipation precludes concentration of solutions, compactness of the target ensures coercivity of the energy
E
, which in turn provides sufficient control over the wave maps flow to guarantee global regularity for any smooth and localized initial data.

In this paper, we consider the energy-supercritical case of a canonical model of the wave maps equation, where the target manifold is the
d
-dimensional sphere
(\mathbb{S}^{d},h)
endowed with the standard round metric
h
induced by the embedding into
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
. For our analysis, it is convenient to work with the so-called
normal coordinates
on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
. Namely, for a function
\mathcal{U}
with values in
\mathbb{S}^{d}
, we consider the coordinate representation
\mathcal{U}=(\mathcal{U}^{1},\dots,\mathcal{U}^{d})
where

\mathcal{U}^{i}:=\theta\Omega^{i},\quad\text{for}\quad i=1,\dots,d,

with
\theta
being the polar angle, and
\Omega^{i}
the coordinate functions of the embedding
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Concerning existence of blowup for this model, it is known that the corresponding wave maps equation (
1.1
) admits, in all supercritical dimensions
d\geq 3
, an
explicit
self-similar solution
\mathcal{U}_{T}
, which is given in the normal coordinates by

(1.3)

\mathcal{U}^{i}_{T}(t,X)=\Phi\left(\frac{|X|}{T-t}\right)X^{i},\quad\text{with}\quad\Phi(\rho)=\frac{2}{\rho}\arctan\left(\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{d-2}}\right),

and which forms singularity at the origin as
t\rightarrow T^{-}
.
Originally identified by Turok and Spergel in
[
62
]
for
d=3
, this solution was subsequently generalized by Bizoń and Biernat in
[
7
]
to dimensions
d\geq 4
. We note that
\mathcal{U}_{T}
belongs to the class of the so-called
corotational
solutions

(1.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{i}(t,X)=u(t,\lvert X\rvert)X^{i},\quad i=1,\dots,d.

In fact, for initial data of corotational form

(1.5)

\mathcal{U}_{0}(X)=u_{0}(|X|)X\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{0}(X)=\hat{u}_{0}(|X|)X,

(
1.1
) reduces by means of (
1.4
) to a Cauchy problem for a single
(d+2)
-dimensional radial semilinear wave equation for the profile
u=u(t,r)

(1.6)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\,\partial_{t}^{2}u-\partial_{r}^{2}u-\dfrac{d+1}{r}\partial_{r}u=\dfrac{d-1}{2r^{3}}\big(2ru-\sin(2ru)\big),\\
\,u(0,\cdot)=u_{0},\\
\,\partial_{t}u(0,\cdot)=\hat{u}_{0},\end{cases}

with the corresponding self-similar blowup solution

(1.7)

\displaystyle u_{T}(t,r)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{T-t}\Phi\left(\frac{r}{T-t}\right),\quad T>0,

where
\Phi
is given in (
1.3
).

To understand the role that
u_{T}
plays in the dynamics of (
1.6
), numerical simulations have been performed, in
[
8
]
for
d=3
, and in
[
7
]
for
d\geq 4
. Surprisingly, these have revealed a striking universality in the blowup behavior:

For generic large corotational initial data, the solution blows up at the origin via the self-similar profile
u_{T}
.

In other words, irrespective of the choice of initial data
(u_{0},\hat{u}_{0})
leading to finite-time blowup, if one dynamically self-similarly rescales the solution as it approaches the blowup time, then the profile
\Phi
emerges as an attractor of the rescaled evolution, i.e.,

(1.8)

(T-t)u(t,(T-t)\cdot)\rightarrow\Phi

locally uniformly on
[0,+\infty)
as
t\rightarrow T^{-}.
This, in turn, translates for the corotational map
\mathcal{U}
into

(1.9)

\mathcal{U}(t,(T-t)\cdot)\rightarrow(\cdot)\Phi(|\cdot|),

which takes place locally uniformly on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
as
t\rightarrow T^{-}.

Following these observations, considerable effort has been devoted to their rigorous justification, starting with the pioneering works of Aichelburg, Donninger, and Schörkhuber
[
28
,
27
,
31
]
. This line of research, which relies on stability analysis localized to lightcones, eventually lead to a rigorous verification of, what could be called, a perturbative local version of the numerical observations above. Namely, for any fixed ball
B_{R}
centered at the origin with radius
R>0
, all corotational data that are close enough to that of the blowup solution
u_{T}
, lead to finite-time blowup with (
1.9
) taking place uniformly on
B_{R}
; see
[
18
,
17
,
14
,
6
,
30
]
. This was then followed by the work of the first author
[
40
]
, which takes the global-in-space stability point of view, and thereby removes the assumption of a pre-fixed ball
B_{R}
, hence establishing the convergence (
1.8
) globally on
[0,+\infty)
, and consequently obtains (
1.9
) uniformly on compact sets; see
[
40
, Theorem 1.1]
for a precise, quantitative, statement of this result. The non-perturbative counterpart of this result, i.e., the assertion that blowup is governed by
u_{T}
for (generic) large data that are
not necessarily close
to those of
u_{T}
, is, however, open. Motivated by this, in this paper we take a probabilistic point of view, that is, we consider the stochastic variant of (
1.1
) with additive corotational noise. We then show that for arbitrary corotational initial data, the corresponding solution does, in fact, blow up via (
1.3
) with positive probability.

1.1.
Wave maps with additive noise

We consider the following stochastic evolution equation on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

(1.10)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\,\partial^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{U}^{i}+\Gamma^{i}_{j,k}(\mathcal{U})\partial^{\mu}\mathcal{U}^{j}\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{U}^{k}=X^{i}\dot{{\mathcal{W}}},\ i=1,\dots,d,\\
\,\mathcal{U}(0,\cdot)=\mathcal{U}_{0},\\
\,\partial_{t}\mathcal{U}(0,\cdot)=\hat{\mathcal{U}}_{0},\end{cases}

for an infinite dimensional Brownian motion
\mathcal{W}
on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
satisfying suitable assumptions described below; see
2.1
and
2.4
. To preserve the corotational structure of the solutions to (
1.10
), we assume that the noise
\mathcal{W}
lies in the space of radially symmetric functions, and the initial data are corotational, i.e., (
1.5
) holds for some radial
u_{0},\hat{u}_{0}
. Then, similarly to above, by setting
\mathcal{U}(t,X)=u(t,\lvert X\rvert)X
and
n=d+2
, the system (
1.10
) reduces to a single stochastic scalar wave equation
2

2

For

(1.11)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\,\partial_{t}^{2}u-\Delta u=\dfrac{n-3}{2\lvert x\rvert^{3}}\big(2\lvert x\rvert u-\sin(2\lvert x\rvert u)\big)+\dot{\mathcal{W}},\\
\,u(0,\cdot)=u_{0},\\
\,\partial_{t}u(0,\cdot)=\hat{u}_{0},\end{cases}

with radial data posed on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.
Accordingly, we exclusively study the dynamics of solutions to (
1.11
), which we then ultimately translate back to the context of (
1.10
).

There are at least two additional motivations for studying the system (
1.10
). In realistic physical models, idealized equations such as the wave maps system inevitably neglect small-scale external perturbations, imperfections in the environment, and unresolved random fluctuations. A standard modeling approach to incorporate such effects is to introduce stochastic forcing terms. In the context of wave-type systems, additive noise has been employed to model, for instance, thermal fluctuations in plasma physics; see
[
43
]
. Following this modeling philosophy, we consider perturbations by an additive noise term. The specific form of the noise, being proportional to the spatial variable
X
, is consistent with the imposed corotational symmetry and preserves the geometric structure of the problem. This choice ensures that the stochastic perturbation respects the corotational ansatz, and can be interpreted as modeling small, isotropic ambient forces. Moreover, additive noise introduces randomness independently of the solution itself, making it a clean model for external fluctuations that do not alter the underlying geometric structure. Consequently, additive noise acts as a “non-resonant” perturbation, allowing testing whether a mechanism of blowup is stable in a probabilistic sense. This is particularly important in supercritical problems, where the deterministic dynamics are often sensitive to small perturbations.

The past forty years have seen a substantial amount of research on wave equations under stochastic forcing. Without aiming to be exhaustive, we refer to
[
11
,
12
,
24
,
52
,
15
]
and references therein for some of the earliest works on the subject. For wave equations taking values in Riemannian manifolds, we mention
[
9

10
]
. In situations where the equation becomes singular and renormalization procedures are required, we refer to
[
3

41

42
,

. Concerning local well-posedness of the stochastic equation (
1.11
) (or, equivalently, (
1.10

1.2.
The main results

Due to the presence of noise, we have to work with a weak notion of corotational solutions to (

Theorem 1.1
.

Let

\displaystyle\frac{n}{2}-1<s<\frac{n}{2}-1+\frac{1}{2n-4},\ k>n,\ k\in{\mathbb{N}}.

1.3.
On regularization by noise phenomena

Starting with the seminal works
[
64
,
63
]
, it has become well established that the addition of noise to (partial) differential equations can help prevent pathological behaviors such as non-uniqueness or blowup of solutions. We refer in particular to
[
34
,
22
,
21
,
53
,
5
,
4
,
35
,
16
,
43
,
1
,
19
]
for various instances of the so-called phenomenon of
regularization by noise
, including examples arising in the context of fluid dynamics. The heuristic underlying these results is that pathological behaviors in (P)DEs typically occur only along exceptional trajectories and are not generic. Consequently, a small random perturbation can move the system away from them, thereby restoring uniqueness or preventing blowup. For a detailed exposition of this idea, we refer to the lecture notes
[
33
]
.

In light of the results mentioned above and the preceding discussion,
Theorem 1.5
suggests that the blowup profile
\mathcal{U}_{T}
(or
(\cdot)\Phi(|\cdot|)
) is not only in some sense generic but also that the corresponding blowup mechanism is so robust that the addition of noise, at least in the additive case, does not prevent the system’s pathological behavior. On the contrary, it may even amplify it, as we are able to establish blowup for arbitrarily small initial data, for which the deterministic dynamics would otherwise be globally well-posed. From this perspective, our result is in line with those obtained for the Schrödinger equation in
[
25
,
26
]
, where it was shown that the presence of smooth noise, whether additive or linear, provokes blowup with positive probability for
generic
initial data. Unlike our work, however,
[
25
,
26
]
do not provide asymptotic information on the behavior of solutions near the blowup time. We also point to works
[
50
,
49
]
, which investigate the effect of multiplicative noise on the heat equation, demonstrating that such noise can induce blowup of solutions with positive probability. Further examples of this phenomenon can be found in
[
38
,
37
,
36
,
44
]
.

We conclude by noting that, as with all previously cited results on blowup induced by noise, we provide no information on the size of the probability of blowup. Furthermore, we cannot assert that every instance of blowup occurs in a self-similar fashion. Although we expect this to be the case, and moreover that
\mathbb{P}\left(\tau^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{U}}_{0})<T\right)\rightarrow 1
as
T\rightarrow+\infty
, we have no rigorous proofs.

1.4.
Brief history of blowup for (deterministic) wave maps

Wave maps provide a geometric generalization of geodesics to higher dimensions. More precisely, the wave maps equation for maps from the flat Minkowski spacetime
({\mathbb{R}}^{1+d},\eta)
into a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
defines a motion of the Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
embedded into
M
, where in the extreme case,
d=0
, the system reduces to the geodesic equation. Unlike geodesics, however, wave maps in general exhibit rich dynamical behavior, including the formation of singularities in finite time.

For the energy-subcritical case,
d=1
, blowup is actually ruled out for compact target manifolds, regardless of their geometry. This simply follows from the strong well-posedness of the one-dimensional wave maps at the energy regularity, together with the positive definiteness of the energy functional (
1.2
). For non-compact targets, however, there seems to be no known results on the existence of blowup so far.

In the energy-critical case,
d=2
, the fine geometric properties of the target manifold become relevant, even in the compact case. For spherical targets, a remarkable series of works has addressed the existence and stability of blowup; see, e.g.,
[
47
,
55
,
54
,
45
,
46
]
. In all these cases, the blowup mechanism is non-self-similar and corresponds to the so-called bubbling off of a harmonic map. In contrast, for targets of constant negative curvature, e.g., the hyperbolic plane, blowup is known to be impossible; see, e.g.,
[
60
,
58
,
59
,
48
]
.

In the energy-supercritical case,
d\geq 3
, the construction of blowup is generally more accessible, as self-similar solutions often exist. The earliest results in this direction are works of Shatah and Tahvildar-Zadeh on rotationally symmetric targets with positive curvature
[
56
,
57
]
. For targets of constant negative curvature, on the other hand, the existence of blowup remains a major open problem. However, when the assumption of constant curvature is dropped and the target is merely required to have negative curvature, self-similar blowup solutions have been constructed in sufficiently high dimensions; see, e.g.,
[
13
,
29
]
.

First, by the representation

solving

\,w(0)=u_{0},\\

for

Furthermore, we define the new dependent variables (in their vector form)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}(\tau,\xi):=(Z(\tau,\xi),\hat{Z}(\tau,\xi)),\;\;\text{for}\;\;Z(\tau,\xi)=(\tilde{T}-t)z(t,x)\;\,\text{and}\;\,\hat{Z}(\tau,\xi)=(\tilde{T}-t)^{2}\hat{z}(t,x),

\displaystyle{\mathbf{W}}(\tau,\xi):=(W(\tau,\xi),\hat{W}(\tau,\xi)),\;\;\text{for}\;\;W(\tau,\xi)=(\tilde{T}-t)w(t,x)\;\,\text{and}\;\,\hat{W}(\tau,\xi)=(\tilde{T}-t)^{2}\hat{w}(t,x).

Throughout the paper, we use the ‘hat’ notation to shortly denote the partial derivative with respect to the physical time

and

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\,\partial_{\tau}\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{L}_{0}\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{Z}),\\
\,\mathbf{W}(0,\cdot)=\mathbf{W}_{0}(\tilde{T}),\end{cases}

where the linear operator

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{0}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}-1-\xi\cdot\nabla,&1\\
\Delta&-2-\xi\cdot\nabla\end{bmatrix},

the nonlinearity

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{K})

\displaystyle:=(0,n_{0}(\mathbf{K})),\;\,\text{where}\;\,n_{0}(\mathbf{K})=\frac{n-3}{2|\cdot|^{3}}\left(2|\cdot|K-\sin(2|\cdot|K)\right)\;\,\text{for}\;\,\mathbf{K}=(K,\hat{K}),

and the trace of

shows up only in the initial data in the following manner

We then proceed by analyzing stability of the static profile

\displaystyle w(t,x)=\frac{1}{\tilde{T}-t}\Phi\left(\frac{\lvert x\rvert}{\tilde{T}-t}\right)+\frac{1}{\tilde{T}-t}{\Psi}\bigg(\log\bigg(\frac{\tilde{T}}{\tilde{T}-t}\bigg),\frac{\lvert x\rvert}{\tilde{T}-t}\bigg),

{\Psi}(\tau,\cdot)

) pathwise. The existence of a positive-probability set follows from
2.5
, which ensures that
\mathbf{z}
has the required properties, together with the fact that
\mathbf{z}
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{0}
.

3.1.1.
Analysis of the perturbed system

Let us define

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}:=\left\{f\in C([0,+\infty);\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}):\left\|\phi\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}=\sup_{t\in[0,+\infty)}e^{\overline{\omega}t}\left\|\phi(t)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}\right\},

where
\overline{\omega}
is from the previous section; see (
2.9
).
Let
\mathcal{X}_{r}
(resp
B_{r},\ C_{T}B_{r}
) denote the closed ball of radius
r
in
\mathcal{X}
(resp.
\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k},\ C([0,2T];\mathbf{H}^{k+\frac{n+1}{2}})
) and
P
be the Riesz projection of
\mathbf{L}
associated to the eigenvalue
1
.
We study (
1.23
) for a generic initial condition
\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}
using a Lyapunov–Perron type argument.
For
\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k},\ \mathbf{K}\in\mathcal{X},\ \mathbf{Z}\in C([0,+\infty);\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}),\ \mathbf{Z}=(Z,\hat{Z})
, define

\displaystyle C(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{Z}):=P\left(\mathbf{v}+\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-s}\left(\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{K}+\mathbf{Z})+\mathbf{V}\mathbf{Z}\right)ds\right).

We look for fixed points of the map

\displaystyle K_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{\Psi})(\tau)

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{S}(\tau)(\mathbf{v}-C(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{\Psi},\mathbf{Z}))+\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathbf{S}(\tau-s)\left(\mathbf{N}(\Psi+\mathbf{Z})+\mathbf{V}\mathbf{Z}\right)ds,

where
\mathbf{Z}
is obtained from
\mathbf{z}
via the change of variables (
3.1
).
This is the content of the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

For all sufficiently small
\delta>0
and all sufficiently large
\mathcal{C}>0
, the
following holds. If
\mathbf{v}\in B_{{\delta}/{\mathcal{C}}}
and
\mathbf{z}\in C_{T}B_{\delta/\mathcal{C}}
, then there exits a unique
\mathbf{\Psi}\in\mathcal{X}_{\delta}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Psi}=K_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{\Psi}).

Moreover, the map
(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{z})\rightarrow\mathbf{\Psi}
is Lipschitz continuous from
B_{\delta/\mathcal{C}}\times C_{T}B_{\delta/\mathcal{C}}
into
\mathcal{X}_{\delta}
.

Proof.

We start with the following observation on
\mathbf{V}\mathbf{Z}
. For each
l\in{\mathbb{N}},\ l\geq\frac{n}{2}-3
and for a multi-index
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
of degree
\lvert\alpha\rvert=l
, the Leibniz rule gives

\partial^{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{(\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}+n-4)^{2}}Z(\tau,\xi)\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n}:\\
\beta\leq\alpha\end{subarray}}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\partial^{\beta}\left(\frac{1}{(\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}+n-4)^{2}}\right)\partial^{\alpha-\beta}Z(\tau,\xi)\\
=\sum_{i=0}^{l}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n}:\\
\lvert\beta\rvert=i\end{subarray}}\tilde{T}^{1+l-i}e^{-(1+l-i)\tau}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\partial^{\beta}\left(\frac{1}{(\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}+n-4)^{2}}\right)\partial^{\alpha-\beta}z(\tilde{T}(1-e^{-\tau}),\xi\tilde{T}e^{-\tau}).

We now estimate the
L^{2}
norm of each term. For a given
i=\lvert\beta\rvert
, we have two cases.
If
i\leq\frac{n-8}{2}
, choosing
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{4}
, by Hölder’s inequality with
p=\frac{n+\varepsilon}{8+2i}
, we have

\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left\lvert\partial^{\beta}\left(\frac{1}{(\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}+n-4)^{2}}\right)\partial^{\alpha-\beta}z(\tilde{T}(1-e^{-\tau}),\xi\tilde{T}e^{-\tau})\right\rvert^{2}d\xi\right)^{1/2}\\
\lesssim\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left\lvert\nabla^{l-i}z(\tilde{T}(1-e^{-\tau}),\xi\tilde{T}e^{-\tau})\right\rvert^{\frac{2(n+\varepsilon)}{n+\varepsilon-8-2i}}d\xi\right)^{\frac{n+\varepsilon-8-2i}{2(n+\varepsilon)}}\\
=\frac{\left\|\nabla^{l-i}z(\tilde{T}(1-e^{-\tau}))\right\|_{L^{\frac{2(n+\varepsilon)}{n+\varepsilon-8-2i}}}}{(\tilde{T}e^{-\tau})^{\frac{n(n+\varepsilon-8-2i)}{2(n+\varepsilon)}}}\lesssim\frac{\left\|z(\tilde{T}(1-e^{-\tau}))\right\|_{H^{l}\cap W^{l,\infty}}}{(\tilde{T}e^{-\tau})^{\frac{n(n+\varepsilon-8-2i)}{2(n+\varepsilon)}}}.

On the other hand, if
i>\frac{n-8}{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left\lvert\partial^{\beta}\left(\frac{1}{(\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}+n-4)^{2}}\right)\partial^{\alpha-\beta}z(\tilde{T}(1-e^{-\tau}),\xi\tilde{T}e^{-\tau})\right\rvert^{2}d\xi\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|\nabla^{l-i}z(\tilde{T}(1-e^{-\tau}))\right\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|z(\tilde{T}(1-e^{-\tau}))\right\|_{H^{l}\cap W^{l,\infty}}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla^{l}\left(\frac{1}{(\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}+n-4)^{2}}Z(\tau,\xi)\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\lesssim\tilde{T}e^{-\tau}\left\|z(\tilde{T}(1-e^{-\tau}))\right\|_{H^{l}\cap W^{l,\infty}}\left(\max_{i=0,\dots\lfloor{\frac{n-8}{2}}\rfloor}(\tilde{T}e^{-\tau})^{l-i-\frac{n(n+\varepsilon-8-2i)}{2(n+\varepsilon)}}+1\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{-\tau}\left\|z(\tilde{T}(1-e^{-\tau}))\right\|_{H^{l}\cap W^{l,\infty}}\left(1+e^{-\tau(l-\frac{n}{2}+4-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2(n+\varepsilon)})}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{-\tau}\left\|z(\tilde{T}(1-e^{-\tau}))\right\|_{H^{l}\cap W^{l,\infty}},

due to our choice of
\varepsilon
. Consequently, by interpolation and since
k>s>\frac{n}{2}-1
and
\overline{\omega}<s+1-\frac{n}{2}<1
, we obtain

(3.3)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{V}\mathbf{Z}(\tau)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}=\left\|\frac{8(n-4)(n-3)}{(\lvert\cdot\rvert^{2}+n-4)^{2}}Z(\tau)\right\|_{\dot{H}^{s-1}_{rad}\cap\dot{H}^{k-1}_{rad}}

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{-\tau}\sup_{t\in[0,2T]}\left\|z(t)\right\|_{H^{k+\frac{n+1}{2}}_{rad}}.

Similarly, and more simply,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{Z}(\tau)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{-\hat{\omega}\tau}\sup_{t\in[0,2T]}\left\|\mathbf{z}(t)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}},

where
\hat{\omega}=s+1-\frac{n}{2}<1
. Note that, by our assumptions,
\overline{\omega}<\hat{\omega}<1
.
Having these relations in mind, we can run the fixed point argument. First, we need to show that
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{z}}
maps
\mathcal{X}_{\delta}
into itself. In view of (
2.10
), we rewrite it as

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{\Psi})(\tau)=\

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}(\tau)(I-P)\mathbf{v}+\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathbf{S}(\tau-s)(I-P)\left(\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{\Psi}+\mathbf{Z})+\mathbf{V}\mathbf{Z}\right)ds

\displaystyle-\int_{\tau}^{+\infty}e^{\tau-s}P\left(\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{\Psi}+\mathbf{Z})+\mathbf{V}\mathbf{Z}\right)ds.

Thanks to our assumptions and estimates (
2.10
), (
2.11
), (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), we have

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{\Psi})(\tau)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}\lesssim\

\displaystyle e^{-\overline{\omega}\tau}\frac{\delta}{\mathcal{C}}+\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\overline{\omega}(\tau-s)}\left(e^{-2\overline{\omega}s}\delta^{2}+e^{-2\hat{\omega}s}\frac{\delta^{2}}{\mathcal{C}^{2}}+e^{-s}\frac{\delta}{\mathcal{C}}\right)ds

\displaystyle+\int_{\tau}^{+\infty}e^{\tau-s}\left(e^{-2\overline{\omega}s}\delta^{2}+e^{-2\hat{\omega}s}\frac{\delta^{2}}{\mathcal{C}^{2}}+e^{-s}\frac{\delta}{\mathcal{C}}\right)ds

\displaystyle\lesssim\

\displaystyle e^{-\overline{\omega}\tau}\left(\delta^{2}+\frac{\delta}{\mathcal{C}}\right),

since
\overline{\omega}<\hat{\omega}<1
.
The last estimate implies the claim provided
\delta
is sufficiently small and
\mathcal{C}
is sufficiently large. Secondly, we need to show that
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{z}}
is a contraction. This follows from computations analogous to those above. Indeed, using (
3.4
), (
2.10
), and (
2.11
), we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{1})

\displaystyle(\tau)-\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{2})(\tau)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\overline{\omega}(\tau-s)}\left(\left\|\mathbf{Z}(s)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}+\left\|\mathbf{\Psi}_{1}(s)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}+\left\|\mathbf{\Psi}_{2}(s)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}\right)\left\|\mathbf{\Psi}_{1}(s)-\mathbf{\Psi}_{2}(s)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}ds

\displaystyle+\int_{\tau}^{+\infty}e^{\tau-s}\left(\left\|\mathbf{Z}(s)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}+\left\|\mathbf{\Psi}_{1}(s)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}+\left\|\mathbf{\Psi}_{2}(s)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}\right)\left\|\mathbf{\Psi}_{1}(s)-\mathbf{\Psi}_{2}(s)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}ds

\displaystyle\lesssim\

\displaystyle e^{-\overline{\omega}\tau}(\delta+\frac{\delta}{\mathcal{C}})\left\|\mathbf{\Psi}_{1}-\mathbf{\Psi}_{2}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}.

Hence by choosing
\delta
sufficiently small and
\mathcal{C}
sufficiently large, we obtain the contraction estimate

(3.5)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{1})-\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{2})\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}\leq\frac{1}{4}\left\|\mathbf{\Psi}_{1}-\mathbf{\Psi}_{2}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}.

It remains to show the continuity of the solution map. Let
\mathbf{\Psi}_{1}
corresponds to
\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{1}
and
\mathbf{\Psi}_{2}
corresponds to
\mathbf{v}_{2},\mathbf{z}_{2}
. Using the triangle inequality, (
2.10
), and (
2.11
), we get

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{\Psi}_{1}(\tau)

\displaystyle-\mathbf{\Psi}_{2}(\tau)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}=\left\|\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{1}}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{1})(\tau)-\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{v}_{2},\mathbf{z}_{2}}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{2})(\tau)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{1}}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{1})(\tau)-\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{v}_{2},\mathbf{z}_{1}}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{1})(\tau)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}

\displaystyle+\left\|\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{v}_{2},\mathbf{z}_{2}}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{1})(\tau)-\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{v}_{2},\mathbf{z}_{1}}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{1})(\tau)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}

\displaystyle+\left\|\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{v}_{2},\mathbf{z}_{2}}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{2})(\tau)-\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{v}_{2},\mathbf{z}_{2}}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{1})(\tau)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\

\displaystyle e^{-\overline{\omega}\tau}\left\|\mathbf{v}_{1}-\mathbf{v}_{2}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}

\displaystyle+e^{-\overline{\omega}\tau}\left(\left\|\mathbf{z}_{1}\right\|_{C([0,2T];\mathbf{H}^{k+\frac{n+1}{2}})}+\left\|\mathbf{z}_{2}\right\|_{C([0,2T];\mathbf{H}^{k+\frac{n+1}{2}})}+\delta\right)\left\|\mathbf{z}_{1}-\mathbf{z}_{2}\right\|_{C([0,2T];\mathbf{H}^{k+\frac{n+1}{2}})}

\displaystyle+e^{-\overline{\omega}\tau}\left\|\mathbf{z}_{1}-\mathbf{z}_{2}\right\|_{C([0,2T];\mathbf{H}^{k+\frac{n+1}{2}})}

\displaystyle+\left\|\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{v}_{2},\mathbf{z}_{2}}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{2})(\tau)-\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{v}_{2},\mathbf{z}_{2}}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{1})(\tau)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}.

Therefore, applying (
3.5
), we deduce

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{\Psi}_{1}(\tau)-\mathbf{\Psi}_{2}(\tau)\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}\lesssim\

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{v}_{1}-\mathbf{v}_{2}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}

\displaystyle+\left(\left\|\mathbf{z}_{1}\right\|_{C([0,2T];\mathbf{H}^{k+\frac{n+1}{2}})}+\left\|\mathbf{z}_{2}\right\|_{C([0,2T];\mathbf{H}^{k+\frac{n+1}{2}})}+\delta\right)\left\|\mathbf{z}_{1}-\mathbf{z}_{2}\right\|_{C([0,2T];\mathbf{H}^{k+\frac{n+1}{2}})}

\displaystyle+\left\|\mathbf{z}_{1}-\mathbf{z}_{2}\right\|_{C([0,2T];\mathbf{H}^{k+\frac{n+1}{2}})}

and the claim follows.
∎

Note that, due to our construction, the initial perturbation
\mathbf{\Psi}_{0,\tilde{T}}

and the stochastic term
\mathbf{Z}
both depend on
\tilde{T}

. Explicitly, we have

(3.6)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Psi}_{0,\tilde{T}}:=U(\tilde{T},u_{0},\hat{u}_{0})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{T}u_{0}(\tilde{T}\cdot)-{T}u_{0}({T}\cdot)\\
\tilde{T}^{2}\hat{u}_{0}(\tilde{T}\cdot)-{T}^{2}\hat{u}_{0}({T}\cdot)\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}{T}u_{0}({T}\cdot)-\Phi\\
{T}^{2}\hat{u}_{0}({T}\cdot)-\hat{\Phi}\end{bmatrix}

(3.7)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\tilde{T}}{T}\Psi_{0,T}(\frac{\tilde{T}}{T}\cdot)\\
\frac{\tilde{T}^{2}}{T^{2}}\hat{\Psi}_{0,T}(\frac{\tilde{T}}{T}\cdot)\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\tilde{T}}{T}\Phi(\frac{\tilde{T}}{T}\cdot)-\Phi\\
\frac{\tilde{T}^{2}}{T^{2}}\hat{\Phi}(\frac{\tilde{T}}{T}\cdot)-\hat{\Phi}\end{bmatrix}.

Similarly, the stochastic term in self-similar coordinates is

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}(\tau,\xi)

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{Z}_{\tilde{T}}(\tau,\xi)=(Z_{\tilde{T}},\hat{Z}_{\tilde{T}}),\text{ where }

\displaystyle\ Z(\tau,\xi)

\displaystyle:=Z_{\tilde{T}}(\tau,\xi)=\tilde{T}e^{-\tau}z(\tilde{T}(1-e^{-\tau}),\xi\tilde{T}e^{-\tau}),

\displaystyle\hat{Z}(\tau,\xi)

\displaystyle:=\hat{Z}_{\tilde{T}}(\tau,\xi)=\tilde{T}^{2}e^{-2\tau}\hat{z}(\tilde{T}(1-e^{-\tau}),\xi\tilde{T}e^{-\tau}).

Both expressions (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) will be used in the following analysis.

The result of
3.2
applies to our initial condition
\mathbf{\Psi}_{0,\tilde{T}}
, provided that
\tilde{T}
is sufficiently close to
T
and that the perturbation of the initial data is small in the sense that
\left\|\mathbf{u}_{0}-\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{\Phi}\left(\frac{\cdot}{T}\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}
is sufficiently small.
The following lemma makes this statement precise and quantifies the dependence of
\mathbf{\Psi}_{0,\tilde{T}}
on
\tilde{T}.

Lemma 3.3
.

For each
\delta\in(0,\frac{T}{2}]
and
(u_{0},\hat{u}_{0})\in\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}
the map

\displaystyle\tilde{T}\mapsto U(\tilde{T},u_{0},\hat{u}_{0}):[T-\delta,T+\delta]\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}

is continuous. Moreover,

\displaystyle\lVert U(\tilde{T},u_{0},\hat{u}_{0})\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}\lesssim\lvert\tilde{T}-T\rvert+\left\|\mathbf{u}_{0}-\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{\Phi}\left(\frac{\cdot}{T}\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}\quad\text{for all}\quad\tilde{T}\in\left[\frac{1}{2}T,\frac{3}{2}T\right].

Proof.

Both statements follow from
[
40
, Lemma 8.2]
, by observing that (
3.7
) corresponds to
[
40
, equation (8.7)]
, up to renaming
\frac{\tilde{T}}{T}
as
T
.
∎

Thanks to
3.3
, there exists
\delta>0
sufficiently small and
N>0
large enough such that
\frac{\delta}{N}<\frac{T}{2}
and, for each
\tilde{T}\in[T-\frac{\delta}{N},T+\frac{\delta}{N}]
and
\left\|\mathbf{u}_{0}-\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{\Phi}\left(\frac{\cdot}{T}\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}<\frac{\delta}{N^{2}}
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert U(\tilde{T},u_{0},\hat{u}_{0})\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}\leq\frac{\delta}{\mathcal{C}},

for
\mathcal{C}
sufficiently large, as required in
3.2
. Consequently, there exists a unique
\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{T}}
solving

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{T}}(\tau)=\

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}(\tau)\left(U(\tilde{T},u_{0},\hat{u}_{0})-C(U(\tilde{T},u_{0},\hat{u}_{0}),\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{T}},\mathbf{Z}_{\tilde{T}})\right)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathbf{S}(\tau-s)\left(\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{T}}+\mathbf{Z}_{\tilde{T}})+\mathbf{V}\mathbf{Z}_{\tilde{T}}\right)ds

It remains to show that there exists a choice of
\tilde{T}
such that

\displaystyle C(U(\tilde{T},u_{0},\hat{u}_{0}),\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{T}},Z_{\tilde{T}})=0.

This is the content of the following lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

There exist constants
\delta>0
sufficiently small and
N>0
sufficiently large such that the following holds. If

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{u}_{0}-\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{\Phi}\left(\frac{\cdot}{T}\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}<\frac{\delta}{N^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{z}\in C_{T}B_{\delta/N^{2}}

then there exists
\tilde{T}\in[T-\frac{\delta}{N},T+\frac{\delta}{N}]
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{T}}(\tau)=\mathbf{S}(\tau)\left(U(\tilde{T},u_{0},\hat{u}_{0})\right)+\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathbf{S}(\tau-s)\left(\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{T}}+\mathbf{Z}_{\tilde{T}})+\mathbf{V}\mathbf{Z}_{\tilde{T}}\right)ds.

Proof.

By
3.2
and
3.3
, we can choose
\delta,N>0
such that
\frac{\delta}{N}\leq\frac{T}{2}
and for each
\tilde{T}\in[T-\frac{\delta}{N},T+\frac{\delta}{N}]
we have a unique
\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{T}}
solving

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{T}}(\tau)=\

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}(\tau)\left(U(\tilde{T},u_{0},\hat{u}_{0})-C(U(\tilde{T},u_{0},\hat{u}_{0}),\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{T}},\mathbf{Z}_{\tilde{T}})\right)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathbf{S}(\tau-s)\left(\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{T}}+\mathbf{Z}_{\tilde{T}})+\mathbf{V}\mathbf{Z}_{\tilde{T}}\right)ds.

By the definition of the map
C(U(\tilde{T},u_{0},\hat{u}_{0}),\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{T}},\mathbf{Z}_{\tilde{T}})
, it is sufficient to show that there exists a choice of
\tilde{T}
possibly depending on
u_{0},\hat{u}_{0},T,\mathbf{z}
such that

\displaystyle\langle C(U(\tilde{T},u_{0},\hat{u}_{0}),\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{T}},\mathbf{Z}_{\tilde{T}}),\,\mathbf{g}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}=0.

First, we observe that, arguing as in
[
40
, Lemma 8.2]
, we have

\displaystyle\langle PU(\tilde{T},u_{0},\hat{u}_{0}),\mathbf{g}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}=2\sqrt{n-4}\left\|\mathbf{g}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}^{2}\left(\frac{\tilde{T}}{T}-1\right)+R_{1}\left(\mathbf{u}_{0}-\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{\Phi}\left(\frac{\cdot}{T}\right),\frac{\tilde{T}}{T}\right),

where
R_{1}(\mathbf{u}_{0}-\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{\Phi}\left(\frac{\cdot}{T}\right),\frac{\tilde{T}}{T})
is continuous in
\tilde{T}
and satisfies

\displaystyle\left\lvert R_{1}\left(\mathbf{u}_{0}-\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{\Phi}\left(\frac{\cdot}{T}\right),\frac{\tilde{T}}{T}\right)\right\rvert\lesssim\frac{\delta}{N^{2}}.

Here we used again the argument from
[
40
, Lemma 8.2]
, which applies to our case by renaming
\frac{\tilde{T}}{T}
as
T
.
Next, set
R_{2}(z,\mathbf{u}_{0}-\mathbf{\Phi},\tilde{T})=P\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-s}\left(\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{T}}+\mathbf{Z}_{\tilde{T}})+\mathbf{V}\mathbf{Z}_{\tilde{T}}\right)ds
. Then the problem reduces to finding
\tilde{T}
such that

\displaystyle\tilde{T}=T-T\frac{R_{1}(\mathbf{u}_{0}-\mathbf{\Phi},\tilde{T})}{2\sqrt{n-4}\left\|\mathbf{g}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}^{2}}-T\frac{R_{2}(z,\mathbf{u}_{0}-\mathbf{\Phi},\tilde{T})}{2\sqrt{n-4}\left\|\mathbf{g}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}^{2}}.

Defining the map

\displaystyle F(\tilde{T}):\left[T-\frac{\delta}{\mathcal{C}},T+\frac{\delta}{\mathcal{C}}\right]\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}},\ F(\tilde{T})=T-T\frac{R_{1}(\mathbf{u}_{0}-\mathbf{\Phi},\tilde{T})}{2\sqrt{n-4}\left\|\mathbf{g}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}^{2}}-T\frac{R_{2}(z,\mathbf{u}_{0}-\mathbf{\Phi},\tilde{T})}{2\sqrt{n-4}\left\|\mathbf{g}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}^{2}},

we are left to find a fixed point of
F.

Observe that
R_{2}
is continuous in
\tilde{T}
. Indeed, for
\tilde{T}_{1},\tilde{T}_{2}>0
, we can write

\displaystyle Z_{\tilde{T}_{2}}(\tau,\xi)

\displaystyle=\tilde{T}_{2}(1-e^{-\tau})z(\tilde{T}_{2}e^{-\tau},\xi\tilde{T}_{2}(1-e^{-\tau}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{T}_{2}}{\tilde{T}_{1}}\tilde{T}_{1}(1-e^{-\tau})z\left(\frac{\tilde{T}_{2}}{\tilde{T}_{1}}\tilde{T}_{1}e^{-\tau},\xi\frac{\tilde{T}_{2}}{\tilde{T}_{1}}\tilde{T}_{1}(1-e^{-\tau})\right)

\displaystyle=\tilde{T}_{1}(1-e^{-\tau})\tilde{z}_{\tilde{T}_{1},\tilde{T}_{2}}\left(\tilde{T}_{1}e^{-\tau},\xi\tilde{T}_{1}(1-e^{-\tau})\right),\quad\tilde{z}_{\tilde{T}_{1},\tilde{T}_{2}}(t,x)=\frac{\tilde{T}_{2}}{\tilde{T}_{1}}z\left(\frac{\tilde{T}_{2}}{\tilde{T}_{1}}t,\frac{\tilde{T}_{2}}{\tilde{T}_{1}}x\right).

In particular
\tilde{z}_{\tilde{T}_{1},\tilde{T}_{2}}\rightarrow z\in C([0,2T];H^{k+\frac{n+1}{2}}_{rad})
as
\tilde{T}_{1}\rightarrow\tilde{T}_{2}.
A similar argument applies to the second component of
\mathbf{Z}
, and by the continuity of the solution map,
cf.

3.2
, we also have
\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{T}_{1}}\rightarrow\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{T}_{2}}
in
\mathcal{X}
as
\tilde{T}_{1}\rightarrow\tilde{T}_{2}.
Combining this information with the estimates (
3.3
), (
3.4
) and (
2.11
), implies that
R_{2}(z,\mathbf{u}_{0}-\mathbf{\Phi},\tilde{T})
is continuous in
\tilde{T}
. Moreover, by (
3.3
), (
3.4
) and (
2.11
), we have that

\displaystyle\lvert R_{2}(z,\mathbf{u}_{0}-\mathbf{\Phi},\tilde{T})\rvert

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|\mathbf{g}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-s}\left(e^{-2\overline{\omega}s}\delta^{2}+e^{-2\overline{\omega}s}\frac{\delta^{2}}{N^{4}}+e^{-\overline{\omega}s}\frac{\delta}{N^{2}}\right)ds.

Hence, by taking
\delta>0
sufficiently small and
N>0
sufficiently large, we can ensure

\displaystyle\lvert R_{1}(\mathbf{u}_{0}-\mathbf{\Phi},\tilde{T})\rvert+\lvert R_{2}(z,\mathbf{u}_{0}-\mathbf{\Phi},\tilde{T})\rvert\leq\frac{\delta}{TN}\left(2\sqrt{n-4}\left\|\mathbf{g}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}^{2}\right).

Therefore,
F
is continuous and, by choosing
\delta>0
sufficiently small and
N>0

∎

Lastly, we need a lemma that relates the mild solution constructed in self-similar variables to that obtained in physical variables. Indeed, we have shown that there exists a unique mild solution

Lemma 3.5
.

If

Proof.

Let us introduce, on

for

\displaystyle[\tilde{S}_{\tilde{T}}(t)\mathbf{u}_{0}](x):=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\tilde{T}-t},&0\\

\mathbf{U}(\tau,\xi)=[\mathbf{S}_{0}(\tau)\mathbf{U}_{0}](\xi).
Observing that equation (
3.8

\mathbf{v}
, previous step and the chance of variables

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}(t,x)

\displaystyle=[{S}(t)\mathbf{u}_{0}](x)

\displaystyle+\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\tilde{T}-t},&0\\
0&\frac{1}{(\tilde{T}-t)^{2}}\end{bmatrix}\left[\int_{0}^{\log\left(\frac{\tilde{T}}{\tilde{T}-t}\right)}\mathbf{S}_{0}\left(\log\left(\frac{\tilde{T}}{\tilde{T}-t}\right)-s\right)\frac{(n-3)\mathbf{f}(\lvert\cdot\rvert({W}+Z)(s))}{\lvert\cdot\rvert^{3}}ds\right]\left(\frac{x}{\tilde{T}-t}\right)

\displaystyle+\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\tilde{T}-t},&0\\

where

To identify the second term, note that by the definition of

\displaystyle\frac{(n-3)\mathbf{f}(\lvert\cdot\rvert(W+Z)(\log\frac{\tilde{T}}{\tilde{T}-r},\cdot))}{(\tilde{T}-r)\lvert\cdot\rvert^{3}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{T}-r,&0\\

\left[\mathbf{S}_{0}\left(\log\left(\frac{\tilde{T}-r}{\tilde{T}-t}\right)\right)\frac{(n-3)\mathbf{f}(\lvert\cdot\rvert(W+Z)(\log\frac{\tilde{T}}{\tilde{T}-r}))}{(\tilde{T}-r)\lvert\cdot\rvert^{3}}\right](\cdot)\\

4.
Irreducibility of the stochastic corotational wave maps

This section is devoted to establishing the second key component in the proof of
Theorem 1.1
, i.e., the following result.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that
2.4
holds and let

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\dot{H}^{s-1,k+2}_{rad}\times\dot{H}^{s-1,k}_{rad}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k},\quad\mathbf{u}_{1}\in\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k},\quad\text{and}\quad t,\varepsilon>0

Let
\mathbf{u}
denote the unique solution of (
1.11
) with initial condition
\mathbf{u}_{0}
given by
Theorem 1.1
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tau^{*}(\mathbf{u}_{0})\geq t,~\left\|\mathbf{u}(t)-\mathbf{u}_{1}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}<\varepsilon)>0.

This result establishes the irreducibility of the stochastic system (
1.11
). As a direct consequence, it implies that, with positive probability, the blowup of
\mathbf{u}
can be prevented up to time
t
. The proof proceeds in two steps: (i) an approximate controllability argument, and (ii) continuous dependence of the solution map on the controls.

4.1.
Approximate controllability

We study the translated nonlinear wave map

(4.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}^{2}w-\Delta w=\frac{n-3}{2\lvert x\rvert^{3}}(2\lvert x\rvert(w+z)-\sin(2\lvert x\rvert(w+z))),\\
w(0)=u_{0},\\
\partial_{t}w(0)=\hat{u}_{0},\end{cases}

where
z
is, as before, the stochastic convolution associated with the wave equation. We adopt (
4.1
) instead of the more standard formulation

(4.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}^{2}u-\Delta u=\frac{n-3}{2\lvert x\rvert^{3}}(2\lvert x\rvert u-\sin(2\lvert x\rvert u))+\partial_{t}f,\\
u(0)=u_{0},\\
\partial_{t}u(0)=\hat{u}_{0},\end{cases}

since
w
enjoys better continuity properties with respect to
z
than
u
does with respect to
f
.

For convenience, we define, for any
T_{1}>0
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{s,k}_{0,T_{1}}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\mathbf{z}=(z,\hat{z})\in C([0,T_{1}];\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}):z(0)=\hat{z}(0)=0,\ z(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\hat{z}(s)ds\right\}.

This is a separable Banach space if endowed with the topology of
C([0,T_{1}];\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k})
. Here, the equality
z(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\hat{z}(s)ds
has to be understood in the sense of tempered distributions, i.e. in
\mathscr{S}^{\prime}
.
Now we can prove the approximate controllability result for equation (
4.1
).

Proposition 4.2
.

For any
T_{1}>0\ \text{and }\mathbf{u}_{0}=(u_{0},\hat{u}_{0}),\ \mathbf{u}_{1}=(u_{1},\hat{u}_{1})\in\dot{H}^{s-1,k+2}_{rad}\times\dot{H}^{s-1,k}_{rad}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}
, there exists
\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{E}^{s,k}_{0,T_{1}}
such that
\mathbf{w}=(w,\partial_{t}w)
solving (
4.1
) exists on
[0,T_{1}]
and satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}(0)=\mathbf{u}_{0},\quad\mathbf{w}(T_{1})+\mathbf{z}(T_{1})=\mathbf{u}_{1}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, let us assume
T_{1}=1
.
We aim to construct
\mathbf{u}=(u,\partial_{t}u)
such that

\displaystyle u(0)=u_{0},\ u(1)=u_{1},\ \partial_{t}u(0)=\hat{u}_{0},\ \partial_{t}u(1)=\hat{u}_{1}.

Since four conditions must be satisfied, it is clear that if
u
is taken as a cubic polynomial in time, the coefficients can then be chosen to meet all the conditions. However, in order to relax the regularity assumptions on
\mathbf{u}_{0}
and
\mathbf{u}_{1}
, and to construct
\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{w}
such that
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{w}
, with both
\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{w}
satisfying the requirements of the statement, it is necessary to modify the polynomial behavior through a suitable regularization procedure. With this in mind, and with a slight abuse of notation, let
P(t)
denote the semigroup on
\dot{H}^{l}_{rad}
associated to the infinitesimal generator
\Delta-I:D(\Delta-I)=\dot{H}^{l}_{rad}\cap\dot{H}^{l+2}_{rad}\subseteq\dot{H}^{l}_{rad}\rightarrow\dot{H}^{l}_{rad}
for each
l>-\frac{n}{2}
.
Let

\displaystyle u(t)=\,

\displaystyle\left(1+2t^{3}-3t^{2}\right)u_{0}+t^{2}(3-2t)u_{1}

\displaystyle-t^{2}(3-2t)\left((I-\Delta)^{-1}-(I-\Delta)^{-2}+(I-\Delta)^{-2}P(1)\right)\hat{u}_{0}

\displaystyle-(I-\Delta)^{-1}((1-t)P(t)-I-(I-\Delta)^{-1}P(t)+(I-\Delta)^{-1})\hat{u}_{0}

\displaystyle+t^{2}(3-2t)\left((I-\Delta)^{-1}+(I-\Delta)^{-2}-(I-\Delta)^{-2}P(1)\right)\hat{u}_{1}

\displaystyle-(I-\Delta)^{-1}\left(tP(1-t)+(I-\Delta)^{-1}P(1-t)-(I-\Delta)^{-1}P(1)\right)\hat{u}_{1},

\displaystyle\hat{u}(t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}u(t),\ \mathbf{u}(t)=(u,\hat{u})(t).

Then, clearly,

\mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}_{0},\ \mathbf{u}(1)=\mathbf{u}_{1}.
Moreover, we have the regularity

\displaystyle u\in C([0,1];\dot{H}^{s-1,k+2}_{rad}),\quad\hat{u}\in C([0,1];\dot{H}^{s-1,k}_{rad}).

Next, define

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle=\hat{u}(t)-\hat{u}_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\left(\Delta u(s)+n_{0}(u(s))\right)ds,\quad\mathbf{F}(t)=\left(0,f(t)\right).

By the regularity of
u,\hat{u}
, together with
2.2
, we have
f\in C([0,1];\dot{H}^{s-1,k}_{rad})
. Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}\in C([0,1];\dot{H}^{s-1,k+2}_{rad}\times\dot{H}^{s-1,k}_{rad}).

Now set

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{T}(t-s)\mathbf{A}\mathbf{F}(s)ds.

It is straightforward to check that
\mathbf{z}
satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}+\partial_{t}\mathbf{F}\\
\mathbf{z}(0)=0,\end{cases}

and that
\mathbf{u}
together with
\mathbf{F}
solves equation (
4.2
). In particular, due to the smoothness of
\mathbf{F}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}\in C([0,1];\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}).

The claim follows.
∎

4.2.
Continuity of the solution mapping along the controllers

The main result of this section is the following.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}
and
\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{E}^{s,k}_{0,T_{1}}
be such that there exists a unique solution
\mathbf{w}\in C([0,T_{1}];\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k})
to (
4.1
). Then, there exist neighborhoods
\mathcal{H}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}
of
\mathbf{u}_{0}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{E}^{s,k}_{0,T_{1}}
of
\mathbf{z}
such that for any
\mathbf{\tilde{u}}_{0}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}

\mathbf{\tilde{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}_{0}
, there exists a unique solution
\mathbf{\tilde{w}}\in C([0,T_{1}];\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k})
of (
4.1
). Moreover the solution map
(\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{z})\rightarrow\mathbf{w}
is continuous from
\mathcal{H}_{0}\times\mathcal{Z}_{0}

Proof.

We divide the proof into two steps.

Step 1: Preparation.
Let

be the constant appearing in (

). We first show the existence of a time

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{u}_{0}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}\leq r,\ \lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{C([0,T_{1}];\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k})}\leq R,

\mathbf{w}
to (
4.1
) belonging to the closed ball of radius

in

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{z}}[\mathbf{h}](t)=\mathbf{T}(t)\mathbf{u}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{T}(t-s)\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{h}(s)+\mathbf{z}(s))ds.

By the properties of the wave semigroup on
\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}
, it follows immediately that
\Gamma_{\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{z}}[\mathbf{h}](t)\in C([0,T_{1}];\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k})

, we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert\Gamma_{\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{z}}[\mathbf{h}](t)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}

\displaystyle\leq Me^{T_{1}}r+C_{M,s,k}\left(R+r\right)^{3}(1+R^{2k}+r^{2k})(e^{T_{1}}-1)

\displaystyle\leq(M+1)r

provided that

in

into itself. Next, we have to show that
\Gamma_{\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{z}}
is a contraction. Using the properties of the wave semigroup on

, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\Gamma_{\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{z}}[\mathbf{h}_{1}](t)-\Gamma_{\mathbf{u}_{0},\mathbf{z}}[\mathbf{h}_{2}](t)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert\mathbf{h}_{1}-\mathbf{h}_{2}\rVert_{C([0,T^{*}];\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k})}\left(R+r\right)^{2}(1+R^{2k}+r^{2k})(e^{T^{*}}-1)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{h}_{1}-\mathbf{h}_{2}\rVert_{C([0,T^{*}];\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k})},

provided that
T^{*}
is sufficiently small. This choice of
T^{*}
may be smaller than in the previous step, but it depends only on
r
and
R
.

Similarly, we can solve (
4.1
) on any interval
[T^{*}(r,R)k,T^{*}(r,R)(k+1)]
for

provided that the initial condition satisfies
\lVert\mathbf{u}_{k}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}\leq r.
To keep track of the initial condition and the time interval considered, we denote by
\mathbf{w}^{k+1}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{u}_{k},\cdot)
the unique solution of (
4.1
) in
[T^{*}(r,R)k,T^{*}(r,R)(k+1)]
with initial condition
\mathbf{u}_{k}.
We now show that
\mathbf{w}^{k+1}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{u}_{k},\cdot)
depends continuously on
\mathbf{u}_{k}
and
\mathbf{z}
in
C([T^{*}(r,R)k,

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{w}^{k+1}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{k,1},t)-\mathbf{w}^{k+1}(\mathbf{z}_{2},\mathbf{u}_{k,2},t)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\lVert\Gamma_{\mathbf{u}_{k,1},\mathbf{z}_{1}}[\mathbf{w}^{k+1}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{k,1},\cdot)](t)-\Gamma_{\mathbf{u}_{k,2},\mathbf{z}_{1}}[\mathbf{w}^{k+1}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{k,1},\cdot)](t)\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k}}

Step 2: End of the proof.

Let us introduce

\displaystyle R=1+\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{C([0,T_{1}];\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k})},\ r=1+\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_{C([0,T_{1}];\mathcal{H}_{rad}^{s,k})},

with

and

and
