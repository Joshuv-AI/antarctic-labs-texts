Sex-based survival differences in patients with untreated advanced oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma receiving chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors: a meta-analysis
Authors: K. Sugiyama, P. Ntellas, I. Chau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeChemotherapy combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) is the standard first-line treatment for patients with advanced oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC). Evidence from other tumour types suggests that biological sex may influence ICI efficacy, but this remains unclear in OSCC.Patients and methodsWe conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of phase III randomised trials comparing chemotherapy plus ICIs with chemotherapy alone in advanced OSCC. Overall survival (OS) was the primary endpoint, stratified by sex.ResultsEight trials comprising 4904 patients (14.7% female) were analysed. Chemotherapy plus ICIs significantly improved OS overall [hazard ratio (HR) 0.68, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.62-0.73], in females (HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.64-0.98, I² = 19%), and in males (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.63-0.74, I² = 0%). Subgroup testing (P = 0.19, I² = 42.8%) indicated moderate heterogeneity; however, meta-regression analysis (P = 0.20) showed no significant interaction by sex. Sensitivity analysis revealed that the survival benefit in females was mainly driven by two studies.ConclusionsChemotherapy plus ICIs improves OS in both sexes, although the magnitude is less in females. These findings highlight the importance of considering sex as a biological variable in the design and interpretation of future clinical trials.

Introduction

In the treatment of oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have demonstrated significant benefits.1 However, factors influencing ICI response remain unclear.

Sex-specific differences in tumour immunity and drug metabolism are increasingly being recognised as critical determinants of treatment efficacy.2 The proposed explanations include sex-based immunomodulatory effects, chromosomal differences, and epigenetic regulation.2 Females who underwent cytotoxic chemotherapy for both resectable and advanced/metastatic oesophagogastric cancer had significantly better overall survival (OS) than males.3 Among phase III trials investigating chemotherapy plus ICIs for untreated, advanced OSCC, only the RATIONALE-306 trial demonstrated significantly greater OS in females.4 Given the emerging evidence suggesting a more favourable response to ICIs in male patients, we evaluated whether the survival benefit of first-line chemotherapy plus ICIs, compared with chemotherapy alone, differs between male and female patients with untreated advanced OSCC.

Specifically, we assessed the robustness of survival estimates in each sex through comprehensive sensitivity analyses, an aspect that has not been fully explored in previous aggregate studies.

Patients and methods

Study selection and search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.5

The primary objective was to evaluate OS in female and male patients with untreated, advanced OSCC by comparing chemotherapy plus ICIs versus chemotherapy alone. In cases of significant heterogeneity between the sexes, a meta-regression analysis was carried out to examine its impact (secondary objective). A literature search was conducted on 19 November 2025, to identify all randomised phase III trials that compared systemic anticancer treatment with chemotherapy plus a single programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) or programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) antibody versus chemotherapy alone. We systematically searched PubMed, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library for prospective clinical trials. The search strategy was revised to strictly follow PRISMA guidelines and explicitly include both American and British spellings (e.g. ‘Esophageal’ OR ‘Oesophageal’) to ensure comprehensive coverage. We used a combination of keywords and Medical Subject Headings related to (i) tumour location (e.g. ‘Esophageal’, ‘Oesophageal’); (ii) histology (e.g. ‘Squamous’, ‘Carcinoma’), (iii) stage (e.g. ‘Metastatic’, ‘Advanced’, ‘First-line’), and (iv) intervention (e.g. ‘PD-1’, ‘PD-L1’, ‘Immune checkpoint inhibitors’). The search was updated on 19 November 2025, and no language restrictions were applied. The detailed search strings for all databases are provided in Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100343. Publications extracted from the database were screened to exclude duplicates. Two investigators independently carried out the initial review based on the articles’ titles and abstracts to identify potentially eligible studies. Conference abstracts and meeting summaries were excluded. Potentially relevant reports were subjected to full-text review, and their relevance was confirmed during data extraction.

Statistical analyses

Pooled hazard ratios (HRs) were calculated with inverse-variance statistical methods using trial-level data. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochrane’s Q test and Higgins I2 statistic. In cases of statistically significant heterogeneity (Q test, P < 0.1), a random-effects model was used; otherwise, a fixed-effects model was adopted to estimate the pooled HRs. Heterogeneity was categorised as follows: low (<30%), moderate (30%-50%), or high (>50%). A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. HRs for OS with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were extracted from the included studies. A summary of the HRs was illustrated in a forest plot. Analyses were conducted using Review Manager Web (Cochrane, 2025). Meta-regression analysis was examined with EZR version 1.68, a graphical user interface for R version 4.0.2. (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan) A sensitivity analysis using a leave-one-out approach, sequentially removing each study and recalculating the pooled effect size, was conducted to assess the robustness of our findings. Quality assessment was examined with the revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool. A funnel plot showed the assessment of publication bias (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100343).

Primary endpoint (OS)

Chemotherapy plus ICIs was associated with significantly improved OS compared with chemotherapy alone (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.62-0.73, P < 0.00001) in the overall population (Figure 2A). Chemotherapy plus ICIs significantly improved OS in males (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.63-0.74, P < 0.00001), without heterogeneity (Q test P = 0.77, I2 = 0%) and in females (HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.64-0.98, P = 0.03), with low heterogeneity (Q test P = 0.28, I2 = 19%; Figure 2B). The test for subgroup differences showed moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 42.8%), with numerically fewer survival benefits in females than in males. However, meta-regression analysis showed no significant interaction between females and males (P = 0.20).

Sensitivity analysis confirmed that male patients had robust OS benefits (HR 0.67-0.69, I2 = 0% across all exclusions). In contrast, excluding certain studies (such as RATIONALE 306 and ASTRUM-007) significantly altered HRs and reduced their statistical significance in females (HR 0.85, P = 0.16). The increase in heterogeneity (I2 19%-30%) suggests that the survival benefit in females is highly influenced by these two studies. Notably, when RATIONALE 306 was excluded, the I2 for subgroup differences increased to 72.3% (P = 0.06), indicating greater heterogeneity between males and females. Quality assessments

Quality assessment revealed that the CHECKMATE 648 study had a risk of performance bias (blinding of participants and personnel) and detection bias (blinding of outcome assessment) because it is not a double-blinded study. As the CHECKMATE 648 study had a third treatment arm of ipilimumab plus nivolumab with a different dose schedule, which was not examined in this meta-analysis, a double-blinded placebo-controlled study design was not feasible. The funnel plot demonstrated a symmetrical distribution of studies around the pooled HR (HR 0.69), indicating an absence of significant publication bias.

Discussion

In our meta-analysis, although chemotherapy plus ICIs improved OS in both sexes, sensitivity analysis suggests that, although survival benefits were uniform in males across all eight trials, in females, these may be mainly driven by the results of two studies. Sex hormones may modulate antitumour immunity through their effects on T-cell function. In experimental models, androgen receptor signalling promoted CD8⁺ T-cell exhaustion and impaired responsiveness to PD-1 blockade, whereas inhibition of androgen receptor activity restored IFN-γ production and enhanced the efficacy of checkpoint blockade.14 These findings provide a plausible biological basis for sex-related differences in ICI efficacy, although their relevance to OSCC remains uncertain. Furthermore, genomic factors such as increased copy number alterations in males with OSCC have been associated with enhanced immunogenicity, contributing to improved ICI response.15

Previous meta-analyses have suggested that female patients, particularly those with melanoma and lung cancer, derive fewer benefits from ICIs.16 In the present study, moderate heterogeneity was observed in subgroup differences (female and male) with I2 = 42.8%; however, further meta-regression analysis demonstrated no significant interaction between the two groups (P = 0.20), indicating that chemotherapy plus ICIs is effective for patients with advanced OSCC, regardless of sex. Recently, Masetti et al.17 conducted a meta-analysis of 15 first-line randomised trials in advanced gastroesophageal cancers, including seven trials in ESCC. In the ESCC population, a significant OS benefit was observed in males however not in females, although the interaction between sex and treatment effect was not statistically significant. A key distinction of our study is the sex-specific sensitivity analysis, particularly the leave-one-out analysis, which revealed a striking contrast in robustness between sexes. Specifically, male patients demonstrated a highly consistent survival benefit of chemotherapy plus ICIs, with HR and heterogeneity remaining unchanged across all exclusions. In contrast, the female subgroup exhibited variability, with HR fluctuating between 0.76 and 0.85, and statistical significance was lost in some cases (P = 0.16 after excluding RATIONALE 306). Extending these observations, our findings uniquely highlight the potential fragility of evidence in the female subgroup. This observed fragility likely stems from a multifactorial combination of limited statistical power due to smaller female cohorts (14.7%) and distinct immunological profiles—specifically the complex interplay of sex hormones, which may introduce heterogeneity in treatment response—alongside differences in trial populations such as anti-PD-(L)1 expression and regional treatment approaches.

The observed sensitivity to study exclusion in our meta-analysis suggests that sex-specific differences warrant further investigation in larger, well-stratified cohorts. Notably, a meta-analysis of ICI monotherapy in OSCC in a second-line setting suggested that female patients may experience greater survival benefits than males.18 This difference may be explained by the effects of chemotherapy. A pharmacodynamic study demonstrated significant sex-based variability in chemotherapy.19 Furthermore, women tend to experience higher toxicity from immunotherapy, and this may influence treatment outcomes.20 As noted before, females with both resectable and advanced/metastatic oesophagogastric cancer exhibited improved OS but significantly higher chemotherapy-related toxicity.5 Therefore, in our current meta-analysis, all participants received cytotoxic chemotherapy; thus, it is possible that females in the control arm of chemotherapy alone had better survival; hence, there was less incremental survival benefit with the addition of anti-PD-1 antibodies. In CHECKMATE 648, an additional chemotherapy-free treatment with ipilimumab plus nivolumab was also included, with OS having an HR 1.36 (95% CI 0.85-2.20) in females compared with HR 0.70 (95% CI 0.57-0.86) in males. Understanding these sex-based differences is crucial for customising treatment strategies. Although our results do not support excluding females from ICI treatment, the observed instability of survival benefits in females—combined with reports of higher immunotherapy-related and chemotherapy-related toxicities in women—suggests that female patients may require more vigilant toxicity monitoring and a nuanced risk-benefit discussion during shared decision-making. Future trials should prioritise sex-stratified randomisation to generate robust evidence for this underrepresented subgroup.

Some limitations should be noted. Firstly, the proportion of females in the OSCC trials was low due to sex-based differences in OSCC incidence, limiting the statistical power of the sex-specific subgroup analysis. Secondly, this meta-analysis never adjusted for key confounders such as smoking history and anti-PD-(L)1 expression. Smoking is more prevalent in males with OSCC and is associated with increased tumour mutational burden, which may enhance ICI efficacy. Notably, in CHECKMATE 648,8 the HR for OS in female patients was 1.02 (95% CI 0.64-1.63). In contrast, OS was much more favourable in the anti-PD-(L)1-positive female population (HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.25-0.97), highlighting the importance of anti-PD-(L)1 status in sex-based analyses. Therefore, individual patient data are valuable for further insight beyond aggregated meta-analyses to examine the impact of sex differences on other covariables. Finally, regional variations in OSCC epidemiology and chemotherapy regimens may have influenced our findings. Five of the eight included trials were from China, and the chemotherapy backbones differed between global (fluoropyrimidine/platinum) and Chinese (mainly paclitaxel/cisplatin) studies. Finally, given the limited number of included phase III randomised trials (n = 8), formal publication bias assessment using Egger’s test was not carried out, as these methods are unreliable for meta-analyses with <10 studies. However, since all included trials were industry-sponsored, potential sponsorship bias cannot be fully excluded.

In conclusion, chemotherapy plus ICIs significantly improved OS regardless of sex; however, the observed differences in survival magnitude suggest potential sex-specific immunological and genomic influences. Future clinical trials should encourage sex-balanced enrolment and integrate molecular profiling to elucidate better the interplay between sex, biological mechanisms, and treatment efficacy.
