The tournament ratchet’s clicktime process, and metastability in a Moran model
Jan Lukas Igelbrink, Charline Smadi, Anton Wakolbinger

Abstract
. Muller’s ratchet, in its prototype version, models a haploid, asexual population whose size N N is constant over the generations. Slightly deleterious mutations are acquired along the lineages at a constant rate, and individuals carrying less mutations have a selective advantage. In the classical variant, an individual’s selective advantage is proportional to the difference between the population average and the individual’s mutation load, whereas in the ratchet with tournament selection only the signs of the differences of the individual mutation loads matter. In a parameter regime which leads to slow clicking (i.e. to a loss of the currently fittest class at a rate ≪ 1 / N \ll 1/N ) we prove that the rescaled process of click times of the tournament ratchet converges as N → ∞ N\to\infty to a Poisson process. Central ingredients in the proof are a thorough analysis of the metastable behaviour of a two-type Moran model with selection and deleterious mutation (which describes the size of the fittest class up to its extinction time) and a lower estimate on the size of the new fittest class at a clicktime.

1.
Introduction

Muller’s ratchet
is a prototype model in population genetics; among the pioneering papers are
[
18
,
26
,
16
]
. Originally, this model was conceived to explain the ubiquity of sexual reproduction among eukaryotes despite its many costs
[
23
,
13
]
. In its bare-bones version, Muller’s ratchet models a haploid asexual
population whose size
N
is constant over generations. Slightly deleterious mutations are acquired along the lineages of descent at a constant rate, and an individual’s mutational load (i.e. the number of mutations that was accumulated along the individual’s ancestral lineage) compared to the mutational loads of its contemporaries is decisive for the individual’s success in the selective part of the reproduction dynamics.

With the type of an individual being its current mutational load, the population dynamics leads to a type frequency profile that is “driven upwards” by the effect of mutation and “kept tight” by the effect of selection. Due to mutation and randomness in the individual reproduction, the currently fittest class, i.e. the subpopulation of individuals carrying the currently smallest mutational load,
will eventually get extinct; this is a
click of the ratchet
.

Tournament versus fitness proportionate selection

For quantifying the effect of an individual mutational load within a population there are two prototypic ways: in the so-called
fitness proportionate selection
, the
value
of the individual mutational load is compared to the
mean value
of the mutational loads in the population, and the individual’s selective advantage is proportional to the resulting difference. In the so-called
tournament selection
, the selective advantage of an individual is determined by the
rank
of its mutational load within the contemporary population. We will work with the (continuous-time) Moran model for Muller’s ratchet with tournament selection, and refer to it as the
tournament ratchet
for short.
Here we give a brief verbal description of the individual-based, Poisson-process driven dynamics of the tournament ratchet as specified by the graphical representation in
[
15
]
, which is in line with the definition of the type frequency process given at the beginning of Section
2
.

- For each ordered pair of individuals, neutral reproduction events come at rate
\tfrac{1}{2N}
, resulting in a binary reproduction of the first and the death of the second individual.

- For each pair of individuals carrying different mutational loads, selective reproduction events come at rate
s_{N}/N
, resulting in a binary reproduction of the fitter and the death of the less fit individual.

- Each individual’s mutation load is increased by 1 at rate
m_{N}
.

An appealing feature of the tournament ratchet is that for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
the dynamics of the
k
fittest classes is autonomous up to the time of extinction of the fittest class. In particular, the size of the currently fittest class is described by a two-type Moran model with selection and deleterious mutation.
This “hierarchical autonomy” of the fitness classes does not hold true for Muller’s ratchet with fitness proportionate selection, and to the best of our knowledge the asymptotic analysis of the click rate of the latter so far has resisted a complete and rigorous solution
despite several attacks, among them
[
11
,
24
,
21
]
.
Links between the two ratchet models are established in
[
19
]
, showing that under an appropriate transformation of the mutation-selection ratio
\rho_{N}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=m_{N}/s_{N}
in the
near-critical parameter regime

\rho_{N}\uparrow 1
the dynamics of the size of the fittest class of the tournament ratchet becomes what is called in
[
11
]
the Poisson profile approximation of the size of the fittest class of Muller’s ratchet with fitness proportionate selection.

Model and main results

2.1.
The tournament ratchet and its clicktime process

We start by defining the state space and the transition rates of the type frequency process which result from the individual-based dynamics described in the Introduction.

Definition 2.1
.

For given parameters

, and

,

E_{N}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{(n_{0},n_{1},\ldots)\,|\,

- Neutral reproduction: for
\kappa\neq\kappa^{\prime}

AAA

(\mathfrak{N}_{\kappa},\mathfrak{N}_{\kappa^{\prime}})

(\mathfrak{N}_{\kappa}+1,\mathfrak{N}_{\kappa^{\prime}}-1)

AAA

(\mathfrak{N}_{\kappa},\mathfrak{N}_{\kappa^{\prime}})

- Mutation: for

As in this definition with

The

K_{N}^{\star}(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\min\left\{\kappa\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathfrak{N}^{(N)}_{\kappa}(t)>0\right\}.

The times

For

Remark 2.2
.

As observed in
[

]
,
for each

(and with

up to the time of its extinction, with

\lambda_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\lambda^{(N)}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=n\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{n}{N}\right)+s\left(1-\frac{n}{N}\right)\right),

(2.5)

\mu_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mu_{n}^{(N)}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=n\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{n}{N}\right)+m\right).

This is the dynamics of the number of type-0 individuals in a two-type Moran model with one-way mutation (from type 0 to type 1) at rate

With

as in Definition

for the

Loosely spoken, assumption (

We now introduce (and interpret) two quantities

\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{a}_{N}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=N(1-\rho_{N})

\mathfrak{c}_{N}\ll\mathfrak{a}_{N}\,\mbox{ as }N\to\infty.

Under condition (

2.2.
Metastability in a two-type Moran model with selection and deleterious mutations

Proposition 3.1
.

With

(3.4)

\displaystyle U^{(N)}(\mathfrak{a}+K\sigma)-U^{(N)}(\mathfrak{a})=K^{2}(1+O(m_{N}+K/\sqrt{u_{N}})),\qquad K\in\mathbb{R}.

To prove (

that takes

and

as units of population size and potential depth, respectively.
In

it is proved that
the potential function can be represented as

(3.5)

U^{(N)}(n)=-2\mathfrak{u}_{N}H\left((m_{N},\rho_{N}),\frac{n}{\mathfrak{a}_{N}}\right),

with
H((m,\rho),y)
defined for
y\in\left[0,\frac{1}{1-\rho}\right]
as

(3.6)

H((m,\rho),y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-\frac{y}{2m}\left[\frac{1}{1-\rho}\log\left(\frac{1+2m}{1+2m/\rho}\right)+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{1}{(1+2m)^{\ell}}\right)\frac{(1-\rho)^{\ell-1}y^{\ell}}{\ell(\ell+1)}\right]>-\infty.

Several properties of the function

can be derived from

]
and will be key in showing (

) as well as in forthcoming proofs. We collect them in the next lemma:

Lemma 3.2
.

a) For
(m,\rho)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times(0,1)
,

•

H
is strictly concave function
with
H^{\prime}(1)=0
and its maximal value given by
H((m,\rho),1)=\eta(m,\rho)
stated in (
2.14
).

•

If
\rho\leq 2/3
,
H
is nonnegative on
[0,1/(1-\rho)]
, and if
\rho>2/3
,
H
is nonnegative on
[0,y_{0}]
and negative on
(y_{0},1/(1-\rho)]
, with
y_{0}=y_{0}(m,\rho)
satisfying

y_{0}\sim\frac{2}{\rho}\quad as\ m\to 0.

•

The first derivative of
H
is

(3.7)

H^{\prime}(y)=-\frac{\rho}{2m(1-\rho)}\log\left(1+\frac{2m}{\rho}\frac{(1-\rho)(y-1)}{(1+2m/\rho)(1-(1-\rho)y)}\right).

b) As
m\to 0
the function
H

satisfies the following asymptotics:

(3.8)

\eta(m,\rho)=H(1)\sim\frac{1}{(1-\rho)^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\rho}-1+\log\rho\right)>\frac{1}{2\rho},

(3.9)

H^{\prime}(y)\sim\frac{1-y}{\rho(1-(1-\rho)y)}\quad\mbox{ for all }y\in[0,1/(1-\rho)),

(3.10)

H^{\prime\prime}(1)\sim-1/\rho^{2},\qquad H^{\prime\prime\prime}(1)\sim-2(1-\rho)/\rho^{3},\quad H^{\prime\prime\prime}\mbox{ is bounded close to }1,

(3.11)

H(y)-H(1)+\frac{(1-y)^{2}}{2\rho^{2}}=O\left((1-y)^{2}m+(1-y)^{3}\right)\quad\mbox{as }\,y\to 1.

Proof.

Only (
3.10
) and (
3.11
) have to be proven, for the other statements see
[
19
, p. 135]
.
From

H^{\prime\prime}(y)=\frac{1}{2m}\Big[\frac{1}{1+2m-(1-\rho)y}-\frac{1}{1-(1-\rho)y}\Big]

(see
[
19
, Sec. 4.7]
), we derive

\displaystyle H^{\prime\prime\prime}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\rho}{2m}\Big[\frac{1}{(1+2m-(1-\rho)y)^{2}}-\frac{1}{(1-(1-\rho)y)^{2}}\Big]

(3.12)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2(1-\rho)(1+m-(1-\rho)y)}{(1+2m-(1-\rho)y)^{2}(1-(1-\rho)y)^{2}}.

Hence

(3.13)

H^{\prime\prime\prime}(1)\sim-\frac{2(1-\rho)}{\rho^{3}}\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{0\leq y\leq 1}\big|H^{\prime\prime\prime}(y)\big|\leq\frac{2(1-\rho)(1+m)}{\rho^{4}}.

Now, from Taylor-Lagrange formula, we know that for
y\in(0,1)
, there exists
z\in(y,1)
such that

\displaystyle H(y)

\displaystyle=H(1)+(y-1)H^{\prime}(1)+\frac{(y-1)^{2}}{2}H^{\prime\prime}(1)+\frac{(y-1)^{3}}{6}H^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=H(1)+\frac{(y-1)^{2}}{2}\frac{1}{2m}\Big[\frac{1}{\rho+2m}-\frac{1}{\rho}\Big]+\frac{(y-1)^{3}}{6}H^{\prime\prime\prime}(z).

According to the second part of (
3.13
),

\Big|\frac{(y-1)^{3}}{6}H^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)\Big|\leq\frac{2(1-\rho)(1+m)}{\rho^{4}}\frac{(1-y)^{3}}{6}.

From the expression of
H^{\prime\prime}
we also get

\Big|H^{\prime\prime}(1)+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\Big|=\frac{2m}{\rho^{2}(\rho+2m)}\leq\frac{2m}{\rho^{3}}.

This ends the proof of the lemma.∎

Proof of Proposition

.

As
N\to\infty
, Equations (

), (
3.5
) and (

\displaystyle U(\mathfrak{a}+K\sigma)-U(\mathfrak{a})

\displaystyle=U(\mathfrak{a}(1+K\sigma/\mathfrak{a}))-U(\mathfrak{a})

Lemma 3.3
.

Let us denote by
T_{\ell}
the first time at which

reaches the state
\ell

(A)

Let
\mathfrak{d}_{N}\leq\mathfrak{a}_{N}
such that
\mathfrak{d}_{N}=o(\sigma_{N})
. Then

\mathbf{P}_{\lfloor\mathfrak{a}_{N}-\mathfrak{d}_{N}\rfloor}\left(T_{0}<T_{\mathfrak{a}_{N}}\right)\sim\frac{2}{\mathfrak{c}_{N}}\mathfrak{d}_{N}e^{-2\mathfrak{u}_{N}H(1)}\quad\mbox{as }N\to\infty.

(B)

Let
1\ll\mathfrak{d}_{N}<\mathfrak{a}_{N}
,
\mathfrak{f}_{N}>2
. Then for
N
large enough,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\left\lfloor\mathfrak{a}_{N}-\mathfrak{d}_{N}\right\rfloor}(T_{\mathfrak{a}_{N}-\mathfrak{f}_{N}\mathfrak{d}_{N}}<T_{\mathfrak{a}_{N}})

\displaystyle\leq e^{-(\mathfrak{f}_{N}-2)\mathfrak{d}_{N}^{2}\mathfrak{u}_{N}/\mathfrak{a}_{N}^{2}}.

Proof.

We begin with the proof of
(A)
.
As already observed the function
R=R^{(N)}
defined in (
3.3
) is harmonic for the time-discrete birth-and-death chain associated with
Y_{0}
, hence

(3.14)

\mathbf{P}_{\lfloor\mathfrak{a}-\mathfrak{d}\rfloor}\left(T_{0}<T_{\mathfrak{a}}\right)=\frac{R_{\mathfrak{a}}-R_{\mathfrak{a}-\mathfrak{d}}}{R_{\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor}}.

Because of (
3.2
), (
3.5
) and Lemma
3.2
, and as
\mathfrak{d}_{N}=o(N/m)
we have for every
\mathfrak{a}_{N}-\mathfrak{d}_{N}\leq n\leq\mathfrak{a}_{N}

r_{n}\sim e^{-2\mathfrak{u}_{N}H(1)}.

According to

19

R_{\lfloor\mathfrak{a}_{N}\rfloor}\sim\frac{\rho_{N}}{2m_{N}(1-\rho_{N})}=\frac{\mathfrak{c}_{N}}{2}.

This concludes the proof of
(A)
.

Now let
1\ll\mathfrak{d}_{N}<\mathfrak{a}_{N}

\mathfrak{f}_{N}>2

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\left\lfloor\mathfrak{a}-\mathfrak{d}\right\rfloor}(T_{\mathfrak{a}-\mathfrak{f}\mathfrak{d}}<T_{\mathfrak{a}})

\displaystyle=\frac{R_{\mathfrak{a}}-R_{\mathfrak{a}-\mathfrak{d}}}{R_{\mathfrak{a}}-R_{\mathfrak{a}-\mathfrak{f}\mathfrak{d}}}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\int_{1-\mathfrak{d}/\mathfrak{a}}^{1}e^{-2\mathfrak{u}_{N}H(z)}dz}{\int_{1-\mathfrak{f}\mathfrak{d}/\mathfrak{a}}^{1}e^{-2\mathfrak{u}_{N}H(z)}dz}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\int_{1-\mathfrak{d}/\mathfrak{a}}^{1}e^{-2\mathfrak{u}_{N}H(z)}dz}{\int_{1-\mathfrak{f}\mathfrak{d}/\mathfrak{a}}^{1-(\mathfrak{f}-1)\mathfrak{d}/\mathfrak{a}}e^{-2\mathfrak{u}_{N}H(z)}dz}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-2\mathfrak{u}_{N}(H(1-\mathfrak{d}/\mathfrak{a})-H(1-(\mathfrak{f}-1)\mathfrak{d}/\mathfrak{a})}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-2(\mathfrak{f}-2)(\mathfrak{d}/\mathfrak{a})\mathfrak{u}_{N}H^{\prime}(1-\mathfrak{d}/\mathfrak{a})},

We deduce that for

which ends the proof of

Lemma 3.5
.

Under assumptions (
2.6
) and (
2.11
), for
N
large enough,

(3.16)

\mathbf{E}_{N}[T_{\mathfrak{a}}]\leq(1\vee 4\rho)\rho\,\mathfrak{c}_{N}\log\mathfrak{a}.

Proof.

We are interested in the expectation of the first hitting time of
\mathfrak{a}
when the initial state is
N
. According to Equation (4.5) in
[
19
]
, if we denote by
T_{j}=T_{j}^{(N)}
the first hitting time of
j\in\mathbb{N}
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{N}[T_{\mathfrak{a}}]=\mathbf{E}_{N}[T_{0}]-\mathbf{E}_{\mathfrak{a}}[T_{0}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\frac{R_{n\wedge N}-R_{n\wedge\mathfrak{a}}}{\lambda_{n}r_{n}}+\frac{R_{N}-R_{\mathfrak{a}}}{\mu_{N}r_{N-1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=\mathfrak{a}+1}^{N-1}\frac{R_{n}-R_{\mathfrak{a}}}{\lambda_{n}r_{n}}+\frac{R_{N}-R_{\mathfrak{a}}}{\mu_{N}r_{N-1}}.

1) Let us first consider integers
n
such that
n\leq C\mathfrak{a}
with
C>2/\rho
.
Following the proof of Lemma 4.6 in
[
19
]
, we obtain for such
n
,

\displaystyle\frac{R_{n}-R_{\mathfrak{a}}}{\lambda_{n}r_{n}}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{2}{n(1-n/N)}\int_{\mathfrak{a}}^{n}e^{-2{u}_{N}(H(y/\mathfrak{a})-H(n/\mathfrak{a})}dy

\displaystyle=\frac{2N(1-\rho)}{n(1-n/N)}\int_{1}^{n/\mathfrak{a}}e^{-2{u}_{N}(H(z)-H(n/\mathfrak{a})}dz

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2N(1-\rho)}{n(1-n/N)}\int_{1}^{n/\mathfrak{a}}e^{2{u}_{N}H^{\prime}(z)(n/\mathfrak{a}-z)}dz

where we have used that
H
is concave (see
[
19
, p. 135]
and Lemma
3.2
).
For
1<z\leq C
, we obtain from (
3.9
) that

H^{\prime}(z)\sim-\frac{z-1}{\rho(1-(1-\rho)z)}\leq-\frac{z-1}{\rho^{2}}.

We deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{R_{n}-R_{\mathfrak{a}}}{\lambda_{n}r_{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2N(1-\rho)}{n(1-n/N)}\int_{1}^{n/\mathfrak{a}}e^{2{u}_{N}(z-1)(n/\mathfrak{a}-z)/\rho^{2}}dz

\displaystyle=\frac{2N(1-\rho)}{n(1-n/N)}e^{-2{u}_{N}((n/\mathfrak{a}-1)/2)^{2}/\rho^{2}}\int_{-(n/\mathfrak{a}-1)/2}^{(n/\mathfrak{a}-1)/2}e^{2{u}_{N}u^{2}/\rho^{2}}dz

\displaystyle=\frac{2N(1-\rho)}{n(1-n/N)}e^{-2{u}_{N}((n/\mathfrak{a}-1)/2)^{2}/\rho^{2}}\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{2{u}_{N}}}2\int_{0}^{(\sqrt{2{u}_{N}}/\rho)(n/\mathfrak{a}-1)/2}e^{u^{2}}dz.

Using that for any
A>0
,

\int_{0}^{A}e^{x^{2}}dx\leq 2\int_{A/2}^{A}\leq\frac{2}{2(A/2)}\int_{A/2}^{A}2xe^{x^{2}}dx\leq\frac{2}{A}e^{A^{2}},

and that
1-n/N\sim 1
,
we get

\displaystyle\frac{R_{n}-R_{\mathfrak{a}}}{\lambda_{n}r_{n}}

\displaystyle\leq 4\rho^{2}\mathfrak{c}_{N}\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{n(n-\mathfrak{a})}.

2) Let us now consider
n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
2\mathfrak{a}/\rho\leq n\leq N(1-\sqrt{m})
. Then according to Lemma 4.6 in
[
19
]
,

R_{n}\sim\frac{\rho(1-n/N)\mathfrak{c}_{N}}{2(n/\mathfrak{a}-1)}e^{-2u_{N}H(n/\mathfrak{a})}.

This implies that

\displaystyle\frac{R_{n}-R_{\mathfrak{a}}}{\lambda_{n}r_{n}}\sim\frac{2}{n(1-n/N)}\frac{\rho(1-n/N)}{2(n/\mathfrak{a}-1)}\mathfrak{c}_{N}=\rho\mathfrak{c}_{N}\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{n(n-\mathfrak{a})}

3) Let us now consider
n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
N(1-\sqrt{m})\leq n\leq N-1
. Then according to (
3.7
) (or Equation (4.37) in
[
19
]
),

\displaystyle H^{\prime}(n/\mathfrak{a})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathfrak{c}_{N}}{2}\log\Big(1+\frac{2m}{\rho}\frac{(1-\rho)(n/\mathfrak{a}-1)}{(1+2m/\rho)(1-n/N)}\Big)

\displaystyle\sim-\frac{\mathfrak{c}_{N}}{2}\log\Big(1+\frac{2m}{1-n/N}\Big)=-\frac{\mathfrak{c}_{N}}{2}\log\Big(1+\frac{2mN}{N-n}\Big)

Hence

|H^{\prime}(n/\mathfrak{a})|\geq\frac{\mathfrak{c}_{N}}{2}\log\Big(1+\frac{2mN}{N\sqrt{m}}\Big)\sim\sqrt{m}\mathfrak{c}_{N}.

In particular,

\displaystyle\frac{R_{n}-R_{\mathfrak{a}}}{r_{n}}

\displaystyle\leq N(1-\rho)\int_{1}^{n/\mathfrak{a}}e^{2u_{N}(n/\mathfrak{a}-z)/(\sqrt{m}(1-\rho))}dz\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{m}}

and

\displaystyle\frac{R_{n}-R_{\mathfrak{a}}}{\lambda_{n}r_{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}(N-n)}.

4) Finally, for
n=N
, as the
r_{k}
’s are increasing with
k\geq\mathfrak{a}
,

\frac{R_{N}-R_{\mathfrak{a}}}{\mu_{N}r_{N-1}}\leq\frac{(N-\mathfrak{a})r_{N-1}}{mNr_{N-1}}\leq\frac{1}{m}.

Combining points 1) to 4) we may now conclude the proof:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{N}[T_{\mathfrak{a}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=\mathfrak{a}+1}^{N-1}\frac{R_{n}-R_{\mathfrak{a}}}{\lambda_{n}r_{n}}+\frac{R_{N}-R_{\mathfrak{a}}}{\mu_{N}r_{N-1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=\mathfrak{a}+1}^{3\mathfrak{a}/\rho}\frac{R_{n}-R_{\mathfrak{a}}}{\lambda_{n}r_{n}}+\sum_{n=3\mathfrak{a}/\rho+1}^{N(1-\sqrt{m})}\frac{R_{n}-R_{\mathfrak{a}}}{\lambda_{n}r_{n}}+\sum_{n=N(1-\sqrt{m})+1}^{N-1}\frac{R_{n}-R_{\mathfrak{a}}}{\lambda_{n}r_{n}}+\frac{R_{N}-R_{\mathfrak{a}}}{\mu_{N}r_{N-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=\mathfrak{a}+1}^{N(1-\sqrt{m})}\rho\mathfrak{c}_{N}(1\vee 4\rho)\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{n(n-\mathfrak{a})}+\sum_{n=N(1-\sqrt{m})+1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}N}+\frac{1}{m}

\displaystyle\leq\rho\mathfrak{c}_{N}(1\vee 4\rho)\sum_{n=\mathfrak{a}+1}^{N(1-\sqrt{m})}\Big(\frac{1}{n-\mathfrak{a}}-\frac{1}{n}\Big)+1+\frac{1}{m}\sim(1\vee 4\rho)\rho\mathfrak{c}_{N}\log\mathfrak{a}.

This ends the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

With

visits
\lfloor\mathfrak{a}_{N}\rfloor

\E_{0}[T_{\mathfrak{a}}^{*}]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\E[T_{\mathfrak{a}}^{*}\mid Y_{*}^{(N)}(0)=0]=O(\mathfrak{c}\log\mathfrak{a}).

Proof.

Proceeding similarly as in

)

\displaystyle\E_{0}[T_{\mathfrak{a}}^{*}]-\sum_{k=0}^{\mathfrak{a}-1}r_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\mathfrak{a}-1}\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}\sum_{k=n}^{\mathfrak{a}-1}\frac{r_{k}}{r_{n}}

(3.18)

\displaystyle\sim 4\sum_{n=1}^{\mathfrak{a}-1}\frac{1}{n(1-(1-\rho)(n/\mathfrak{a}))}\int_{n}^{\mathfrak{a}-1}e^{-2u_{N}(H(y/\mathfrak{a})-H(n/\mathfrak{a}))}dy.

We will decompose the last sum in (

) into two parts. First notice that if
\mathfrak{a}-\sigma\leq n\leq y\leq\mathfrak{a}
,

\frac{1}{n(1-(1-\rho)(n/\mathfrak{a}))}\sim\frac{1}{\rho\mathfrak{a}}.

Hence, as

to

(3.19)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=\mathfrak{a}-\sigma}^{\mathfrak{a}-1}\frac{1}{n(1-(1-\rho)(n/\mathfrak{a}))}\int_{n}^{\mathfrak{a}-1}e^{-2u_{N}(H(y/\mathfrak{a})-H(n/\mathfrak{a}))}dy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho\mathfrak{a}}\sum_{n=\mathfrak{a}-\sigma}^{\mathfrak{a}-1}(\mathfrak{a}-n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho\mathfrak{a}}\frac{\sigma(\sigma+1)}{2}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho\mathfrak{a}}\frac{\rho^{2}N}{2m}=\frac{\rho\mathfrak{c}}{2}.

Recall that

We deduce that

\displaystyle\int_{n}^{\mathfrak{a}}e^{-2u_{N}(H(y/\mathfrak{a})-H(n/\mathfrak{a}))}dy

\displaystyle\leq\int_{n}^{\mathfrak{a}}e^{-\frac{2u_{N}}{\rho\mathfrak{a}^{2}}(y-n)(\mathfrak{a}-y)}dy

\displaystyle=\int_{-(\mathfrak{a}-n)/2}^{(\mathfrak{a}-n)/2}e^{-\frac{2u_{N}}{\rho\mathfrak{a}^{2}}((\mathfrak{a}-n)^{2}/4-z^{2})}dz

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{a}\sqrt{\frac{2\rho}{u_{N}}}e^{-\frac{2u_{N}}{\rho\mathfrak{a}^{2}}(\mathfrak{a}-n)^{2}/4}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{2u_{N}/\rho\mathfrak{a}^{2}}(\mathfrak{a}-n)/2}e^{x^{2}}dx.

Let us introduce the Dawson function, for
x\geq 0

F(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=e^{-x^{2}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{t^{2}}dt.

Then it is known that
F(x)\sim 1/2x
for large
x
(see for instance
[
27
, (1), (9)]
).
We have just proven that

\int_{n}^{\mathfrak{a}}e^{-2u_{N}(H(y/\mathfrak{a})-H(n/\mathfrak{a}))}dy\leq\mathfrak{a}\sqrt{\frac{2\rho}{u_{N}}}F\left(\frac{\mathfrak{a}-n}{\mathfrak{a}}\sqrt{\frac{u_{N}}{2\rho}}\right).

But
\mathfrak{a}-n\geq\sigma
implies

\frac{\mathfrak{a}-n}{\mathfrak{a}}\sqrt{\frac{u_{N}}{2\rho}}\geq\frac{\sigma}{\mathfrak{a}}\sqrt{\frac{u_{N}}{2\rho}}=\frac{\rho\sqrt{N}}{\sqrt{m}N(1-\rho)}\sqrt{\frac{mN(1-\rho)^{2}}{2\rho}}=\sqrt{\frac{\rho}{2}}.

As this last term is lower bounded by
\sqrt{\rho^{\ast}}/2
for
N
large enough, we deduce that there exists a positive constant
C
such that for
N
large enough and
\mathfrak{a}-n\geq\sigma
,

F\Big(\frac{\mathfrak{a}-n}{\mathfrak{a}}\sqrt{\frac{u_{N}}{2\rho}}\Big)\leq\frac{C}{2}\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{(\mathfrak{a}-n)\sqrt{u_{N}}}

and thus

\int_{n}^{\mathfrak{a}}e^{-2u_{N}(H(y/\mathfrak{a})-H(n/\mathfrak{a}))}dy\leq C\frac{\mathfrak{a}^{2}}{(\mathfrak{a}-n){u_{N}}}.

We thus obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\mathfrak{a}-\sigma}\frac{1}{n(1-(1-\rho)(n/\mathfrak{a}))}\int_{n}^{\mathfrak{a}-1}e^{-2u_{N}(H(y/\mathfrak{a})-H(n/\mathfrak{a}))}dy

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C\mathfrak{a}}{\rho{u_{N}}}\sum_{n=1}^{\mathfrak{a}-\sigma}\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{n(\mathfrak{a}-n)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{C\mathfrak{c}}{\rho^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{\mathfrak{a}-\sigma}\Big(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{\mathfrak{a}-n}\Big)\sim\frac{2C\mathfrak{c}}{\rho^{2}}\log\mathfrak{a}.

Combining this with (
3.19
), we see that the r.h.s. of (
3.2
) is
O(\mathfrak{c}\log\mathfrak{a})
. This readily imples the assertion of the lemma, since
\sum_{k=0}^{\mathfrak{a}-1}r_{k}=O(\mathfrak{c})
(see
[
19
, Lemma 4.6]
).
∎

Next we observe that

(3.20)

\E[T_{\mathfrak{a}}\,|\,Y_{0}^{(N)}(0)=1\mbox{ and }T_{0}>T_{\mathfrak{a}}]\leq\E[T_{\mathfrak{a}}^{*}\mid Y_{*}^{(N)}(0)=0].

Indeed, decomposing
(Y_{*}(t\wedge T^{*}_{\mathfrak{a}}))_{t\geq 0}
into its excursions from
0
that remain below
\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor
, and the piece that goes from
0
to
\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor
without ever returning to
0
, we see that the latter, when observed from the time at which it jumps from
0
to
1
, has (up to a time shift) the same distribution as
(Y(t\wedge T_{\mathfrak{a}}))_{t\geq 0}
under
\mathbf{P}_{1}(\cdot\mid T_{\mathfrak{a}}<T_{0})
. This proves the inequality (
3.20
).
Combining (
3.20
) with Lemma
3.7
we see (always
under assumptions (
2.6
) and (
2.11
)) that

(3.21)

\E[T_{\mathfrak{a}}\,|\,Y_{0}^{(N)}(0)=1,T_{0}>T_{\mathfrak{a}}]=O(\mathfrak{c}\log\mathfrak{a}).

To conclude the proof of Theorem
2.4
.
a)
, because of (
3.16
) and (
3.21
) it only remains to show

(3.22)

\E\left[T_{0}\,\mid\,Y_{0}^{(N)}(0)=\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor-1,\,T_{0}<T_{\mathfrak{a}}\right]=O(\mathfrak{c}\log\mathfrak{a}).

This, however, is a direct consequence of (
3.21
) combined with the next lemma.

Lemma 3.8
.

The number of steps of the last excursion of
Y_{0}
from
\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor
has (when decreased by
1
) the same distribution as the number of steps it takes
Y_{0}
to reach
\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor
when started in
1
and being conditioned on the event
\{T_{\mathfrak{a}}<T_{0}\}
.

Proof.

We consider the discrete-time Markov chain
V
on
\{0,1,\ldots,\lfloor a\rfloor,\lfloor a\rfloor+1\}
with transition probabilities

P(n,n+1)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\lambda_{n}}{\lambda_{n}+\mu_{n}}\quad\text{and}\quad P(n,n-1)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=1-P(n,n+1)\quad\text{for}\quad n=1,\ldots,\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor,

and

P(0,1)=P(\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor+1,\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=1.

The chain
V
has a reversible equilibrium distribution which assigns non-zero weights to both
0
and
\lfloor a\rfloor
. Let
V_{0}
(resp.
V_{\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor}
) be the Markov chain with transition probability
P
starting in
0
(resp. in
\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor
). Say that an excursion of
V_{\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor}
from
\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor
is successful if it ever reaches
0
, and that an excursion of
V_{0}
from
0
is successful if it ever reaches
\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor
.
Let
\widetilde{T}_{\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor\to 0}
be the random number of steps which the first successful excursion of
V_{\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor}
from
\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor
needs to reach
0
, and let
\widetilde{T}_{0\to\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor}
be the number of steps which the first successful excursion of
V_{0}
needs to reach
\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor
.
We conclude from
[
4
, Proposition 4]
that
\widetilde{T}_{\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor\to 0}
has the same distribution as
\widetilde{T}_{0\to\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor}
. The claim follows by noting that
\widetilde{T}_{\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor\to 0}
has the same distribution as the number of steps of the last excursion of
Y_{0}
from
\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor
, and that
\widetilde{T}_{0\to\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor}-1
has the same distribution as the number of steps it takes

to reach
\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor
when started in

and being conditioned on the event

.
∎

3.3.
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck fluctuations: Proof of Theorem
2.4
.
b)

We will prove the “convergence of generators” of the processes
\mathcal{H}_{N}
(defined in (
2.18
)) to the generator of the Ornstein Uhlenbeck processes
\mathcal{H}
, and check a compact containment condition. A reference in which results contained in
[
12
]
are nicely refined in a framework suitable for our purpose is
[
5
, Proposition 2.5]
. In view of the definitions (
2.7
), (
2.17
) and (
2.9
), the process
\mathcal{H}_{N}
jumps from
x
to
x+\frac{1}{\sigma_{N}}=x+\sqrt{\tfrac{s}{\rho N}}
at rate

\beta_{N}(x)=\frac{\rho}{m(1-\rho)}\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{\rho N}{s}}x+N(1-\rho)\Big)\Big(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{\rho}\Big)\Big(\rho-\frac{x\sqrt{\rho}}{\sqrt{sN}}\Big)

and from
x
to
x-\sqrt{\tfrac{s}{\rho N}}
at rate

\delta_{N}(x)=\frac{\rho}{m(1-\rho)}\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{\rho N}{s}}x+N(1-\rho)\Big)\Big(\frac{1}{2}\Big(\rho-\frac{x{\sqrt{\rho}}}{\sqrt{sN}}\Big)+m\Big).

In particular,

(3.23)

\beta_{N}(x)-\delta_{N}(x)=-\frac{1}{1-\rho}\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{\rho N}{s}}x+N(1-\rho)\Big)\frac{x\sqrt{\rho}}{\sqrt{sN}}

and

(3.24)

\beta_{N}(x)+\delta_{N}(x)=\frac{\rho}{m(1-\rho)}\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{{\rho}N}{s}}x+N(1-\rho)\Big)\Big(\rho+2m-\frac{x{\sqrt{\rho}}}{\sqrt{sN}}\Big(1+\frac{m}{\rho}\Big)\Big).

Applied to a function
f
with three bounded continuous derivatives, the generator of
\mathcal{H}_{N}
thus takes the form

\displaystyle G_{N}f(x)

\displaystyle=\beta_{N}(x)\Big(f\Big(x+\sqrt{\tfrac{s}{{\rho}N}}\Big)-f(x)\Big)+\delta_{N}(x)\Big(f\Big(x-\sqrt{\tfrac{s}{{\rho}N}}\Big)-f(x)\Big)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{s}{{\rho}N}}(\beta_{N}(x)-\delta_{N}(x))f^{\prime}(x)+\frac{s}{2N{\rho}}(\beta_{N}(x)+\delta_{N}(x))f^{\prime\prime}(x)+O\Big(\frac{s}{N}\Big)^{3/2}.

Using (
3.23
) one obtains

\sqrt{\tfrac{s}{{\rho}N}}(\beta_{N}(x)-\delta_{N}(x))=-\Big(1+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\mathfrak{u}_{N}}}x\Big)x,

and (
3.24
) yields

(3.25)

\frac{s}{2N{\rho}}(\beta_{N}(x)+\delta_{N}(x))=\frac{1}{2{\rho}}\Big(1+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{u_{N}}}x\Big)\Big(\rho+2m-\frac{x\rho}{\sqrt{mN}}\Big(1+\frac{m}{\rho}\Big)\Big).

We conclude that

G_{N}f(x)=\Big(1+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{u_{N}}}x\Big)\Big[-xf^{\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\Big(1+\frac{2m}{\rho}-\frac{x}{\sqrt{mN}}\Big(1+\frac{m}{\rho}\Big)\Big)f^{\prime\prime}(x)\Big]+O\Big(\frac{m}{N}\Big)^{3/2}.

In particular, the “generator convergence” condition 3 of
[
5
, Proposition 2.5]
is satisfied with

Gf(x)=-xf^{\prime}(x)+\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(x)}{2},

which is the generator of the standard Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process.
Now we will prove that the
compact containment condition
(condition 4 in
[
5
, Proposition 2.5]
) is fullfilled as well. For this we have to show that for any given (arbitrarily large)
T>0
and (arbitrarily small)
\varepsilon>0
there exists
K>0
such that

(3.26)

\mathbf{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\mid\mathcal{H}_{N}(t)\mid\,\geq K\right)<\varepsilon.

We first prove that there exists a natural number
n
(independent of
N
) such that

(3.27)

\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{H}_{N}\mbox{ travels between times }0\mbox{ and }T\mbox{ more than }n\mbox{ times from }0\mbox{ to }-1)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

To this end we denote by
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{N}
the process that arises by reflecting
\mathcal{H}_{N}
from below in
0
, and by
\mathcal{H}_{N}^{\ast}
a “driftless version” of
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{N}
. More precisely, for
x<0
the jump rates
\widehat{\beta}_{N}(x)
and
\widehat{\delta}_{N}(x)
of
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{N}
are the same as those of
\mathcal{H}_{N}
, while
\widehat{\beta}_{N}(0)=0
and
\widehat{\delta}_{N}(0)=\beta_{N}(0)+\delta_{N}(0)
. The jump rates
\beta^{\ast}_{N}(x)
and
\delta^{\ast}_{N}(x)
of
\mathcal{H}_{N}^{\ast}
are the same as those of
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{N}
for
x=0
, and are

\beta^{\ast}_{N}(x)=\delta^{\ast}_{N}(x)=(\widehat{\beta}_{N}(x)+\widehat{\delta}_{N}(x))/2

for
x<0
. Let
\zeta_{N}
(resp.
\widehat{\zeta}_{N}
resp.
\zeta_{N}^{\ast}
) be the first time at which
\mathcal{H}_{N}
(resp.
\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{N}
resp.
\mathcal{H}_{N}^{\ast}
) hits
-1
when started in
0
. By construction these three stopping times are stochastically ordered in the sense that for all
t>0

(3.28)

\mathbf{P}(\zeta_{N}>t)\geq\mathbf{P}(\widehat{\zeta}_{N}>t)\geq\mathbf{P}(\zeta_{N}^{\ast}>t).

To bound this from below, let
\tau_{N}^{\ast}
be the time at which
\mathcal{H}_{N}^{\ast}
first hits
\{0,-1\}
when started in
-1/2
. We observe that

(3.29)

\mathbf{P}(\zeta_{N}^{\ast}\leq t)\leq\mathbf{P}(\tau_{N}^{\ast}\leq t)=\mathbf{P}_{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sup_{0\leq u\leq t}|\mathcal{H}_{N}^{\ast}(u)-(-\tfrac{1}{2})|\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\right)

According to (
3.24
) we have for
x<0
,

\displaystyle\frac{\beta^{\ast}_{N}(x)+\delta^{\ast}_{N}(x)}{\sigma_{N}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{N}(x)+\delta_{N}(x)}{\sigma_{N}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N\rho(1-\rho)}\Big(\sqrt{\tfrac{{\rho}N}{s}}x+N(1-\rho)\Big)\Big(\rho+2m-\frac{x{\sqrt{\rho}}}{\sqrt{sN}}\Big(1+\frac{m}{\rho}\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\Big(1+x\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{u_{N}}}\Big)\Big(1+\frac{2m}{\rho}-\frac{x}{\sqrt{mN}}\Big(1+\frac{m}{\rho}\Big)\Big)

Hence for any
x\in[-1,0)
,

\frac{\beta^{\ast}_{N}(x)+\delta^{\ast}_{N}(x)}{\sigma_{N}^{2}}\leq 1+\frac{2m}{\rho}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{mN}}\Big(1+\frac{m}{\rho}\Big)\to 1,\quad\mbox{ as }N\to\infty.

Thus for
N
large enough,

(\beta^{\ast}_{N}(x)+\delta^{\ast}_{N}(x))\frac{1}{\sigma_{N}^{2}}\leq 2.

Consequently, the quadratic variation of the martingale
{\mathcal{H}}_{N}^{\ast}(t\wedge\tau_{N}^{\ast})
,
t\geq 0
is bounded from above by
2t
. Thus, by Doob’s
L^{2}
inequality the r.h.s. of (
3.29
) is bounded from above by
2t
. Combining this with (
3.28
) and (
3.29
) we obtain

(3.30)

\mathbf{P}(\zeta_{N}>1/4)\geq\frac{1}{2}.

With
\zeta_{N}^{(1)},\zeta_{N}^{(2)},\ldots
independent copies of
\zeta_{N}
, we conclude from (
3.30
) that there exists a natural number
n
(not depending on
N
) for which

\mathbf{P}(\zeta_{N}^{(1)}+\cdots+\zeta_{N}^{(n)}\leq T)<\varepsilon/2.

By construction of
\zeta_{N}
, (
3.27
) is satisfied for this
n
.

Applying Lemma
3.3
(B)
we get that for any
K>2
,

\mathbf{P}_{\left\lfloor\mathfrak{a}-\sigma\right\rfloor}(T_{\mathfrak{a}-K\sigma}<T_{\mathfrak{a}})\leq e^{-(K-2)}.

While this estimate deals with the large excursions of
\mathcal{H}_{N}

below

0
, an analogous estimate is obtained for the excursions of
\mathcal{H}_{N}

above

0
by noting that the reverting drift of
Y_{0}
above
\mathfrak{a}
is not smaller than that below
\mathfrak{a}
. In order to satisfy (
3.26
) it thus suffices to choose
K=K(\varepsilon,T)
such that
ne^{-(K-2)}<\varepsilon/2
.

Let

Remark 3.9
.

For the
\sigma
-scale around
\mathfrak{a}
, an inspection of (
2.4
) gives for all

and sufficiently large

the identity

(3.33)

\frac{\lambda_{\lfloor\mathfrak{a}+\sigma K\rfloor}}{\lambda_{\lfloor\mathfrak{a}\rfloor}}=\Big(1+\frac{\rho K}{\sqrt{u_{N}}}\Big)\Big(1-\frac{(1-\rho)K}{\sqrt{u_{N}}}\Big)\quad\mbox{as }N\to\infty.

.

Under assumptions (
2.6
) and (
2.11
)
the sequence of equilibrium distributions

is asymptotically normal (with mean

and variance

\pi_{*}^{(N)}(\lfloor\mathfrak{a}_{N}+K\sigma_{N}\rfloor)\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\sigma_{N}}e^{-K^{2}}\quad\mbox{as }N\to\infty,

\mbox{the image of }\pi_{*}^{(N)}\mbox{under the transformation }n\mapsto\frac{n-\mathfrak{a}}{\sigma}\mbox{converges weakly to }\mathcal{N}(0,1/2).

]

3.5.
Asymptotic normality of the quasi-equilibria: Proof of
Theorem
2.4
.
c)

We first derive a rough “large deviation” bound for the quasi-equilibrium distribution.

Lemma 3.11
.

The quasi-equilibrium
\alpha_{N}
of
Y_{0}^{(N)}
obeys

(3.38)

\alpha_{N}(\{\lfloor\mathfrak{a}/2\rfloor,\lfloor\mathfrak{a}/2\rfloor+1,\ldots,N\})\to 1\quad\mbox{as }N\to\infty.

Preparing for an application of Lemma
3.7
later in the proof, we define

(3.39)

t_{N}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathfrak{c}_{N}(\log\mathfrak{a}_{N})^{2}.

Because of (
2.16
), the l.h.s. of (
3.38
) equals

(3.40)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\alpha_{N}}\Big(Y_{0}(t_{N})\geq\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{2}\,\big|\,T_{0}>t_{N}\Big)=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{N}(j)\mathbf{P}_{j}(Y_{0}(t_{N})\geq\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{2})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{N}(j)\mathbf{P}_{j}(Y_{0}(t_{N})>0)}=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\beta_{N}\leq 1.

as

.

\liminf\beta_{N}<1

\mathbf{P}_{\left\lfloor\mathfrak{a}-\sigma\right\rfloor}(T_{\mathfrak{a}/2}<T_{\mathfrak{a}})\leq e^{-\rho^{2}(\sqrt{u_{N}}/(2\rho)-2)}.

\mathbf{P}_{j_{N}}\left(T_{\mathfrak{a}}\leq\frac{t_{N}}{2}\,\Big|\,T_{0}>t_{N}\right)\to 1\quad\mbox{ as }N\to\infty.

Recall from Definition

n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
are

\mathfrak{b}(n_{1})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=n_{1}\beta(n_{1})=n_{1}\cdot\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{n_{1}}{N}\right)+\frac{m}{\rho}\left(1-\frac{2\varepsilon\mathfrak{a}}{N}-\frac{n_{1}}{N}\right)\right],

\mathfrak{d}(n_{1})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=n_{1}\delta(n_{1})=n_{1}\cdot\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{n_{1}}{N}\right)+m+\frac{m}{\rho}\cdot\frac{2\varepsilon\mathfrak{a}}{N}\right].

Define
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\min\left(\mathfrak{P}\cap\{t\mid Y_{1}(t)\leq\mathfrak{\lfloor}\mathfrak{a}\rfloor/4\}\right)
. Then on the event
\{T<\infty\}
the processes
Y_{1}
and
\widetilde{Y}_{1}
can be coupled such that

Y_{1}(T)=\widetilde{Y}_{1}(T)\,\mbox{ and }\,Y_{1}(t)\geq\widetilde{Y}_{1}(t)\,\mbox{ a.s. for }t\in\mathfrak{P}\cap[T,\infty).

To ease notation, let us shift time and assume (again omitting the floor brackets for convenience) that

\widetilde{Y}_{1}(0)=Y_{1}(0)=\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{4}.

Let us denote by
W
a discrete random walk making
+1
jumps with probability
p
and
-1
jumps with probability
q=1-p
. Then if we take
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
and denote by
T_{k}^{W}
the walk’s
W
first hitting of
K
, we know that

\mathbf{P}(T^{W}_{-a}<T^{W}_{b})=\frac{(p/q)^{b}-1}{(p/q)^{a+b}-1}.

Let
\mathfrak{a}/6\leq k\leq\mathfrak{a}/2
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{\beta_{k}}{\beta_{k}+\delta_{k}}\geq\frac{\beta_{\mathfrak{a}/2}}{\beta_{\mathfrak{a}/2}+\delta_{\mathfrak{a}/2}}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{2\rho}\Big(\frac{(1+\rho)/2-4\varepsilon(1-\rho)-\rho}{1+m/\rho+m}\Big)\geq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{m}{8\rho}(1-\rho)

for
\varepsilon\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=1/24
and
N
large enough. In particular,

\frac{\beta_{k}}{\delta_{k}}\geq 1+\frac{m}{2\rho}(1-\rho),

\frac{\beta_{k}}{\beta_{k}+\delta_{k}}-\frac{\delta_{k}}{\beta_{k}+\delta_{k}}\geq\frac{m}{4\rho}(1-\rho)

and if we denote by
\tilde{T}_{k}
the hitting time of
k
by
\tilde{Y}_{1}
we obtain for
N
large enough

\mathbf{P}_{0}\big(\tilde{T}_{-\mathfrak{a}/12}<\tilde{T}_{\mathfrak{a}/4}\big)\leq\Big(1+\frac{m}{2\rho}(1-\rho)\Big)^{-\mathfrak{a}/12}\leq e^{-(\mathfrak{a}/12)m(1-\rho)/2\rho}=e^{-u_{N}/24\rho}.

Let us now consider the sequence
(\mathscr{P}_{i},i\in\mathbb{N})
of successive parts of paths of the process
\tilde{Y}_{1}
with initial state
\mathfrak{a}/4
and which come back to
\mathfrak{a}/4
after having visited
\mathfrak{a}/2
and not
\mathfrak{a}/6
. According to the previous result, there are more than a geometric of parameter
e^{-u_{N}/24\rho}
of such paths before reaching
\mathfrak{a}/6
. Then each
\mathscr{P}_{i}
is made of at least
\mathfrak{a}/12
steps of independent exponential random variables of intensity at most
2\mathfrak{a}
. Let us denote by
|\mathscr{P}_{i}|
the duration of the path
\mathscr{P}_{i}
. We will prove that

\mathbf{P}\big(|\mathscr{P}_{i}|<1/36\big)\leq e^{-\mathscr{Q}\mathfrak{a}}

where

\mathscr{Q}=1/36+\log 2-\log 3<0.

For this, we denote by
\mathcal{E}_{i}
a sequence of independent exponential random variables with parameter
2\mathfrak{a}
. We get, using Markov inequality

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\Big(|\mathscr{P}_{i}|<\frac{1}{36}\Big)\leq\mathbf{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\mathfrak{a}/12}\mathcal{E}_{i}<\frac{1}{36}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\Big(e^{-\mathfrak{a}\sum_{i=1}^{\mathfrak{a}/12}\mathcal{E}_{i}}>e^{-\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{36}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq e^{\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{36}}\E\Big[e^{-\mathfrak{a}\sum_{i=1}^{\mathfrak{a}/12}\mathcal{E}_{i}}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq e^{\frac{\mathfrak{a}}{36}}\Big(\frac{2}{3}\Big)^{\mathfrak{a}/12}=e^{-\mathscr{Q}\mathfrak{a}}.

We thus have the following properties:

(1)

The period
\mathfrak{P}
has an expected duration
O(\mathfrak{c}\log\mathfrak{a})
(see (
3.22
)).

(2)

If during
\mathfrak{P}
the process
Y_{1}
ever reaches
\mathfrak{a}/4
, then with a probability larger than
1-e^{-u_{N}/30\rho}
it will reach
\mathfrak{a}/2
and come back to
\mathfrak{a}/4
before reaching
\mathfrak{a}/6
and this excursion will take at least a time
1/36
.

Since

\mathfrak{c}\log\mathfrak{a}e^{-u_{N}/24\rho}\leq u_{N}e^{-u_{N}/24\rho}\to 0\quad\mbox{ as }N\to\infty,

this concludes the proof of claim (C).

We now consider the events

G_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{Y_{1}(L)>\mathfrak{a}/4\},\,G_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{Y_{1}(L)\leq\mathfrak{a}/4\},\,F\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{Y_{1}(t)=\mathfrak{a}/4\mbox{ for some }t\in\mathfrak{P}\}.

Because of Claim (C) we have
Y_{1}(T_{0})\gg\mathfrak{c}
with high probability on the event
F
, and obviously we have
Y_{1}(T_{0})>\mathfrak{a}/4\gg\mathfrak{c}
on the event
G_{1}\cap F^{c}
.

It remains to consider the event
G_{2}\cap F^{c}
, on which
Y_{1}
does not exceed
\mathfrak{a}/4
during the period
\mathfrak{P}
.

We know from Theorem
2.4
.
e)
that the number of mutants that “immigrate” into the second fittest class during period
\mathfrak{P}
is with high probability
\gg\mathfrak{c}
.
The difference between the upward and the downward jump rates of the second fittest class is

(4.2)

\mathfrak{b}(n_{0},n_{1})-\mathfrak{d}(n_{0},n_{1})=mn_{0}+\frac{mn_{1}}{\rho N}\left[N(1-\rho)-2n_{0}-n_{1}\right].

Thus for
\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{8}
, as long as the size of the fittest class is smaller than
2\varepsilon\mathfrak{a}
and the size of the second fittest class is
\leq\mathfrak{a}/4
, the supercriticality of the second fittest class is
\geq\frac{1}{2}m(1-\rho)=\frac{1}{2\mathfrak{c}}
. Hence on the event
G_{2}\cap F^{c}
we can lower-bound
Y_{1}
by a branching process with supercriticality
\geq\frac{1}{2\mathfrak{c}}
and a number of immigrants during period
\mathfrak{P}
that is
\gg\mathfrak{c}
. This process will reach a size
\gg\mathfrak{c}
with high probability, which allows to conclude the proof of the Proposition. ∎
