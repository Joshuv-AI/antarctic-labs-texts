Firearm-related deaths in Tennessee children
Authors: Anthony W Strode, Molly Golladay, Shilin Zhao, Yasmeen Z Qwaider, Adele Lewis, Rosemary K Nabaweesi, Harold N “Bo” Lovvorn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe healthcare burden and fatality rate associated with pediatric firearm injuries is increasing substantially. Most published studies evaluating outcomes and disparities of pediatric firearm-related injuries have focused primarily on hospital-level data. This study aimed to investigate mortality indicators among pediatric decedents referred to the Tennessee Chief Medical Examiner (CME) having a confirmed firearm-related cause of death.MethodsIn this retrospective cross-sectional study, the Tennessee CME database was queried to reveal 643 death certificates of children ages 17 years and younger who died from a firearm-related injury between 2012 and 2022. Death certificates were surveyed for demographics and residential data. Autopsy reports were reviewed for anatomic injury patterns. The neighborhood of residence for each decedent was geocoded for analysis using the Child Opportunity Index (COI). Pearson, Kruskal-Wallis, Wilcoxon, and linear regression modeling were completed.ResultsSignificant increases in both homicide (n=369; p<0.0001) and suicide (n=202; p=0.001) were documented over time. Firearm deaths increased with age (median age, 16.0 years; IQR (13–17)), with ≥70% of homicide and suicide cases occurring between ages 15 years and 17 years. 272 homicide decedents (76%) were black, and 165 (82%) suicides were white (p<0.001). Over half of decedents sustained a head wound (p<0.001). Geocoded density maps indicated a correlation between pediatric population race density and predominating manners of firearm death. Residences with a lower COI level were associated with a higher risk of homicide.ConclusionIn this query of the Tennessee CME registry, homicide rates increased and remained higher than all other firearm manners of death impacting children. This study revealed opportunities to affect meaningful change regarding different ages and manners of lethal firearm injuries among children.Level of evidenceIIIKeywords: children; Firearms; Wounds, Gunshot; death

Introduction

In 2020, firearm injuries surpassed motor vehicle collisions (MVCs) to become the leading cause of death in children aged 1–17 years in the United States.1,7 Pediatric firearm-related injuries continue to result in discernible healthcare burdens that encompass medical cost, chronic or permanent disability, rehabilitation, mental health, and other societal impacts.8 9 In attempts to mitigate the avoidable strain on healthcare resources, demographic disparities, incidence trends, and the high fatality rates associated with pediatric firearm injuries have been rigorously investigated. Research conducted in the preceding decade ostensibly emphasized independent risk factors and outcomes. Previous studies have highlighted demographic factors significantly associated with the odds of surviving a pediatric firearm injury.10,13 More recent studies have attempted to elucidate the catalyst for the increase in pediatric firearm mortality, with the COVID-19 pandemic being investigated as an exacerbating cause.3 14 15 Regardless of the primary outcome being evaluated, there is a unanimous priority placed on developing firearm-injury prevention initiatives.

Reporting systems such as the Center for Disease Control’s Web-based Injury Statistics and Query Reporting System (WISQARS) comprehensively survey and capture deidentified firearm fatalities in the United States, based on data reported to the National Center of Health Statistics.1 6 These high-level data sources provide weighted estimates that limit the ability to evaluate local data and offer little information about pertinent social factors, context, or the intent behind the firearm incidences.16 Including mortality data from additional sources like the WISQARS may result in a more accurate representation of firearm violence consequences.9 Studies that have incorporated pediatric firearm data from both institution-level trauma databases and population-level surveillance tools have identified a significant hidden mortality of pediatric firearm violence, inclusive of those who were referred directly to the medical examiner (ME).9 10 Considered together, hospital-level data and national registries demonstrate the impact different scopes of data have on analytical interpretations.

Measuring the opportunity for childhood access to important features and essential resources within a neighborhood is nuanced. The variability in equitable access to resources between neighborhoods is vast and has a profound impact on childhood development.17 Research using the Child Opportunity Index (COI), as a proxy for exposure to violence, may strengthen firearm-injury preventative efforts by revealing contextual factors associated with previously established disparities. The correlation between child opportunity and increased risk of sustaining homicidal firearm injuries mandates further investigation. Therefore, to further investigate the hidden mortality of pediatric firearm-related deaths not included in hospital databases, this study aimed to characterize trends and investigate mortality indicators among children who sustained fatal firearm injuries.

Methods

Study design and data source

The data set for this retrospective, cross-sectional study was built from the death certificate registry in the Tennessee Office of the State Chief Medical Examiner (TN-OSCME). The TN-OSCME is responsible for keeping record of deaths investigated by all 95 county medical examiners and assuming investigative authority in cases of interest to the state, including mass fatalities and threats to public health. Medicolegal death investigation reports from the TN-OSCME were reviewed to collect more data regarding firearm types. Available autopsy reports, relevant to this study, were also reviewed. The National Violent Death Reporting System (NVDRS) collects facts from death certificates, medical examiner reports, and law enforcement reports into one anonymous database.18 Available NVDRS firearm data for the decedents in our sample were manually added to the data set. The COI 3.0 was used to assess the neighborhood conditions that the decedents in this study experienced. The COI 3.0 assigns a nationally normalized opportunity score and level to each US census tract by compiling 44 independent indicators of neighborhood characteristics and features into a composite index for measuring childhood opportunity.17 Both density and heat maps of Tennessee were generated using population gradients from 2022 census data for persons ages 0–17 years.

Study population

Children aged 17 years and younger who died in Tennessee between January 1, 2012 and December 31, 2022, from an underlying cause of death associated with a firearm-related (International Classification of Disease) ICD-10 code were identified in the query of the TN-OSCME database (online supplemental figure S1). To ensure that all appropriate decedents were captured, death certificates were also screened for a cause of death text field containing a term related to a firearm, such as: ‘shot’, ‘gun’, ‘ballistic’, ‘bullet’, or ‘GSW’ (gun shot wound). Decedents who did not meet the requirement of dying by firearm were manually excluded. There were no cases containing text field terms that did not have a validICD code. No cases that met inclusion criteria were completely excluded from analysis. Investigators were blinded to all decedent identifiers.

Outcomes and covariates

The associated variables in the data set included demographic characteristics (age, sex, bridged race, ethnicity), death characteristics (year, quarter, weekday, manner of death, death place type), injury characteristics (underlying ICD-10 code, firearm type, body wound location), geographic characteristics (injury state, death state, residence census tract), and neighborhood characteristics (zip code and COI data). ‘Pending’ refers to death certificates in which the manner of death text was not updated prior to the yearly file closeout. Race and ethnicity were reported separately on the death certificate in accordance with standards set forth by the Office of Management and Budget. Information included on the death certificate about the race and Hispanic ethnicity of the decedent was reported by the funeral director as provided by an informant, often the surviving next of kin, or, in the absence of an informant, based on observation.2 Forty-four neighborhood indicators were used to produce standardized composite z-scores that were then used to report COI 3.0 data as an overall opportunity score and level. Neighborhood metrics are nationally normalized by comparing and ranking the z-score for each census tract to all other tracts in the United States. COI scores are numeric labels, from 1 (lowest opportunity) to 100 (highest opportunity), assigned to each neighborhood, representing its national z-score (percentile) ranking. COI levels are categorical labels (very low, low, moderate, high, and very high) that represent the nationally normalized quintile each neighborhood falls into. Specifically, census tracts with composite z-scores at or below the 20th percentile are assigned a ‘very low’ COI level, and tracts above the 20th and at or below the 40th percentile are classified as having a ‘low’ opportunity level.17

Statistical analysis

The study population was characterized by descriptive statistics using R. Continuous variables were reported as median with IQR. Categorical variables were reported as counts with percentages. Comparative analysis of demographic and circumstance data by mechanism of death or death place type was completed using the χ2 test for categorical variables, and Kruskal-Wallis test or Wilcoxon test for continuous variables. Tennessee population totals for the years 2012–2022 were averaged, by age and race, to normalize incidence rates by manner of death. Due to a limited number of samples in this analysis, linear regression analysis was used to assess the trend of the rate of death by year and manner. An ⍺ level was set a priori at 0.05 to determine significance for all analyses.

Results

Description of the study sample and incidence trends

A total of 11 772 children aged 17 years or younger were included in the TN-OSCME death certificate database, of whom 643 (5.5%) died from a firearm-related injury between 2012 and 2022. Descriptive statistics were conducted on 636 of the decedents, as 7 children were excluded from further evaluation due to missing manner of death data (n=2) or for having a manner of death listed as ’pending’ (n=5). When stratifying the data by manner of death, 369 (58%) of the cases were classified as homicide, 202 (32%) were suicide, 36 (6%) were accidental, and 29 (5%) had an undetermined manner of death. On annual comparison, homicides remained higher than all other manners of death (range, 14–60 deaths per year; figure 1A). During the 11 years of this study, the incidence rates increased significantly for both homicides (p<0.0001) and suicides (p=0.001). No significant increase in incidence rates of accidental (p=0.3972) or undetermined (p=0.77) deaths was observed. Linear regression modeling demonstrated a strong positive correlation between firearm manner of death and rates per year (figure 1B). Almost all variation in firearm death rates was explained by changes over time within the model (R2=0.919).

Comparing sex groups across different manners of death and ages suggested that female sex was a protective factor against accidental firearm injuries, particularly after age 6 years (online supplemental figure S2). Whereas the median age of the entire firearm decedent sample was 16.0 years (IQR, 13.0–17.0 years), the median age for children who died from an accidental gun discharge was 5.5 years (IQR, 3.0–13.5 years) (p<0.001). Notable increases in homicide and suicide firearm death rates can be appreciated starting at age 14 years (online supplemental figure S3). Age groupings demonstrated that decedents aged 15–17 years constituted 258 (70%) of the homicide cases and 144 (71%) of the suicide cases, whereas the accidental deaths demonstrated a bimodal age distribution for age groups 3–5 years (31%; p<0.001) and 15–17 years (25%; p<0.001).

When analyzing race and ethnicity by manner of death, 17 additional cases were excluded due to their race and ethnicity classification being non-Hispanic with an unknown or other race (n=5), an unknown ethnicity (n=2), or Asian/Pacific Islander race (n=10). Over half of the remaining pediatric decedents were identified as black, non-Hispanic (52%, n=321), 267 (43%) were white, non-Hispanic, and 31 (5%) were identified as Hispanic. Homicide cases were disproportionately comprised of black children (76%, n=272) relative to white (17%, n=62) or Hispanic (6%, n=23) children. Conversely, suicides predominantly occurred in white children (83%, n=165) relative to black (13%, n=26) or Hispanic (4%, n=7) children (all p<0.001). During this study period, the incidence of homicidal firearm deaths in black children increased and persisted above white or Hispanic children (figure 1C). The homicide incidence in white children was consistently the lowest and showed no marked changes over time. Fluctuations in the incidence of suicidal firearm deaths can be seen for all race and ethnicity groups during this period (figure 1D).

Since firearms became the leading cause of pediatric mortality in 2020, evaluating the incidence of firearm-related suicides revealed a 1 year spike in Hispanic children, an increase in black children, and a plateau among white children. White children were also more likely to die from accidental (60%, n=21) and undetermined (66%, n=19) firearm injuries than all other children (all p<0.001). Regions of the state that have a higher black pediatric population were found to have higher rates of firearm homicides (figure 2). Injury circumstance

Discharges from handguns and unknown firearm types formed two significant clusters of cases through analysis of weapons. Notably, handguns accounted for 257 (40%) of the deaths in this study, and 302 (47%) of deaths were caused by an unknown firearm type. Deaths without a denoted firearm type may represent situations in which a weapon could not be recovered from a scene or during a death investigation. Regarding homicides, handguns were linked to 127 (34%) of the cases, and 209 (57%) of the homicide cases had no weapon type identified. Handgun discharges led to 91 (45%) of suicide deaths and 20 (56%) of the accidental deaths. Moreover, no weapon type was identified in 75 (37%) of the suicides and 10 (28%) of accidents. Death caused by larger firearms, such as a rifle (n=36) or shotgun (n=38), collectively constituted 12% of the cases in this study (all p<0.001).

Over half (55%, n=352) of the fatalities in this study sustained firearm injuries to the head. Ballistic head trauma led to 178 (88%) of suicide deaths, and 129 (35%) of homicides. Other noteworthy wound locations within this sample included the chest (11%, n=72), torso (6%, n=37), and ballistic injuries to the back (5%, n=31). Of the homicide cases, 21% had multiple wound sites, either specified (n=24) or unspecified (n=55), suggesting increased violence in these cases (all p=0.001). Whereas most deaths in this study were related to firearm injuries that occurred within Tennessee (57%, n=364), 29 (5%) injuries occurred outside of the state, and the geographic location of injury in 243 (38%) cases was not known. Death circumstance

Pediatric firearm-related deaths ensued annually with no significant difference in the quarter of occurrence (p=0.189). Analysis of death place type revealed emergency rooms (30%, n=193), decedent’s homes (21%, n=136), and inpatient settings (21%, n=134) as the three most prominent death places in this study. Deaths that occurred in an emergency room constituted 140 (38%) of the homicides, and 14 (39%) accidental deaths. By contrast, most of the suicide cases (43%, n=86) died in their home, versus 27 (13%) who died in an emergency room. Deaths that occurred in an inpatient setting accounted for 74 (20%) of homicides, 39 (19%) of suicides, and 11 (31%) of the accidental deaths (all p<0.001). Child Opportunity Index

In this sample, there were 592 Tennessee residents, compared with 51 non-Tennessee residents. Evaluating opportunity indices of each decedent’s residence, at the census tract level and relevant to the year of death, disclosed statistically significant disparities across the different manners of death. The median COI Score for the residential neighborhoods of all decedents in this sample was 21.0 (IQR 5.3–39.0). The median COI Score for the homicide decedents was 11.0 (IQR 2.0–27.0). Despite most cases being classified as ‘low’ in opportunity, suicide decedents had a higher median COI Score of 34.5 compared with all other manners of death and the widest IQR (21.0–59.0) (all p<0.001).

Assessing opportunity levels indicated that an overwhelming majority of the decedents in this study (76%, n=483) lived in a neighborhood with a nationally normalized composite z-score at or below the 40th percentile, meaning their residential neighborhood was classified as having either a ‘low’ or ‘very low’ opportunity level. The rate of homicides increased significantly with each drop in COI quintile. As demonstrated, 244 (66%) of the homicide decedents lived in neighborhoods with a ‘very low’ opportunity level, 72 (20%) were from ‘low’ opportunity neighborhoods, 33 (9%) with ‘moderate’ opportunity, and <20 homicide cases occurred in children with ‘high’ or ‘very high’ opportunity levels. In contrast, firearm-related suicides occurred more frequently in children from neighborhoods with a ‘low’ (36%, n=72) versus ‘very low’ (23%, n=46) opportunity level (figure 3). Suicides also showed the greatest proportion of decedents from neighborhoods with a ‘high’ (17%, n=34) or ‘very high’ (6%, n=13) opportunity level compared with all other manners of death (all p<0.001; online supplemental table S4).

The hidden mortality

An additional statistical analysis was conducted to investigate the hidden mortality of firearm-related deaths. The data set was stratified and grouped by the death place variable into deaths that occurred in a hospital setting (emergency room, inpatient, and dead on arrival) and deaths that were directly referred to the ME. Although most of the firearm deaths in this sample (54%, n=350) were declared within a hospital setting, 293 (46%) of cases were referred directly to the county or state ME. Suicidal firearm injuries were more likely to result in direct ME referrals (n=123) than a hospital death (n=79). However, all other manners of firearm deaths led to more hospital referrals (all p<0.001; online supplemental table S3).

The median age of decedents that were referred directly to the ME was 16.0 years (IQR, 14.0–17.0) compared with the median age of victims referred to a hospital being 15.0 years (IQR, 12.0–17.0) (p=0.006). Victims aged 15–17 years were more likely to be referred directly to the ME in contrast to all other age groups (all p=0.004; online supplemental table S1). Lethal firearm injuries that occurred in white, non-Hispanic and Hispanic children were more likely to be referred directly to the ME. In fact, white, non-Hispanic victims made up a majority (51%, n=144) of the cases that were referred directly to the ME. In contrast, most of the cases referred to the hospital were black, non-Hispanic (59%, n=203), whereas only 37% (n=126) of the hospital deaths were white, non-Hispanic, and 4% (n=13) were Hispanic (all p<0.001). Deaths caused by a handgun made up 45% (n=132) cases that were referred to the ME versus the 129 (37%) handgun cases that led to hospital deaths (p<0.001; online supplemental table S2). Of the cases referred directly to the ME, 58% (n=169) were deaths related to ballistic head injuries (p<0.001).

Discussion

Key results

This state-wide study revealed a significantly increased rate of children who died via lethal firearm injury in Tennessee, from 2012 through 2022. Alarmingly, homicide rates increased most significantly and remained higher than all other firearm manners of death, although suicides also increased significantly over time. Both homicides and suicides predominately occurred in older adolescents. Black children were disproportionately affected by violent lethal firearm injuries and at significantly increased rates compared with white or Hispanic children. Notably, homicides predominantly affected black children, and suicide rates were highest among white children. Firearm-related suicides were more than 1.5 times more likely to be directly referred to the ME than deaths within a hospital. Most striking, children who resided in neighborhoods with ‘very low’ opportunity levels appeared at an increased risk of death by homicide.

Interpretations

Descriptive analyses are frequently used to associate demographic factors with increased risk of sustaining firearm injuries. Some specific measures of social deprivation have been significantly associated with increased risk of firearm injury in younger compared with older children.7 In fact, machine-learning tools have recently been developed utilizing features such as ‘third grade math scores’ and ‘percent parents jailed’ to predict shooting incidence in independent cities.19 Case fatality rate (CFR) analyses have been useful in describing the evolving threat of pediatric firearm injuries and trends. Variations in firearm injury CFR, reflected in different studies, have been attributed to independent study variables such as intent, as well as the use of different data sources.9 20 Discrepancies in pediatric firearm CFR can differ by as much as 10 times that of MVCs at an institutional level, and 49 times that of MVCs at a population level.9 Moreover, a previous study documented that firearm injury mortality rates were underestimated by 8% when medical examiner data were excluded.10 Analyzing diverse data that includes medical examiner cases and COI data may be essential for fully contextualizing the perilousness of pediatric firearm injuries.

Fatal pediatric firearm injuries are notably less common than non-fatal injuries.6 7 10 13 However, using death as a primary outcome represents a binary and definitive consequence of firearm-related injuries, eliminating the implicit variability and inconsistencies with reporting injury severity. Here, we exclusively evaluate lethal pediatric firearm injuries, at the neighborhood level, across the entire state of Tennessee. By restricting our investigation to pediatric decedents, the risks and disparities identified in this study highlight an extreme baseline vulnerability, emphasizing the importance of preventative innovations as a mechanism to mitigate this preventable healthcare and societal burden. Notwithstanding, the abundance of influences on firearm injury risks and outcomes presents a multitude of challenges when considering the development of firearm preventative interventions. Analyzing the opportunity levels of decedent residences provides a mechanism for evaluating factors and circumstances that influence a child’s exposures to lethal firearm injuries. While validating disparities known to increase risk of firearm injuries in children, our work highlights systemic and social factors related to the overall lethality of firearm-related deaths in the pediatric population, particularly in cases of homicide and suicide.

Despite the black pediatric population within Tennessee declining about 1%–2% from 2012 to 2022, we found that homicide occurrences were concentrated in areas of the state with higher populations of black children. Incidentally, about 75% of Tennessee’s black pediatric population resides within only 5 out of 95 counties (Shelby, Davidson, Hamilton, Montgomery, and Rutherford); these census estimates did not change between 2012 to 2022. The topography of Tennessee is diverse, containing mixed landscaping features that can represent several US landforms and economies. When considering urban and rural communities, the Tennessee population demographics are similar to national distributions, increasing the generalizability of these study results.21

Exploiting the nationally normalized COI levels and scores facilitated comparison of neighborhood opportunity across the nation at large.17 Using opportunity-level data as a proxy for estimating exposure to violence, mental health vulnerability, or firearm accessibility may help identify unique communities at risk for homicidal, suicidal, or accidental firearm deaths, respectively. This study advances the transition away from assessing patient-level risk factors towards addressing the environmental and social factors that most influence a child’s risk of firearm-related death.4 Findings from previous studies have demonstrated lower firearm-related pediatric mortality rates in states with stricter gun laws.22,24 Indeed, this study revealed opportunities to impact change in preventative efforts regarding different manners of lethal firearm injuries, the different populations at risk, and the different environments in which they emerged. For example, not only do we understand that homicide deaths are highest in Shelby County, but we now know which of the 223 census tracts within that county house children most at risk for homicidal firearm deaths.

Limitations

The authors acknowledge several notable limitations that should be considered while interpreting the findings in this study. First, no data from police reports were accessed during this study. Therefore, complete ballistic data regarding velocity and caliber of projectiles were not obtained, which restricted conclusions concerning exposure to greater violent intent. Provided there is no mechanism to assess if homicide decedents were victims of a targeted attack or bystanders, a complete understanding of the circumstances and context in which these deaths occurred remains to be elucidated. Second, in 2018, Tennessee Vital Records migrated from collating paper death certificates to an electronic system. Before 2018, death files were generated via manual transcription of paper certificates, resulting in missing variables such as geographic location of injury. Third, there is currently no mechanism for evaluating the circumstances that facilitated the accidental discharges resulting in a child’s death, which limits our ability to recommend feasible interventions to manage or prevent these lethal occurrences. Finally, no mechanism to review the mental health status of the decedents by suicide was available in the CME registry for this study.

Future directions

We envision one day using intervention programs to mitigate foremost the violence to which children are increasingly exposed and access to firearms that lead to accidental and self-harm deaths or survivable injuries. Further research is necessary to identify the most challenging barriers to firearm injury prevention and determine which obstacles, if removed, would result in the greatest impact. Collaborative efforts to address the fragmented work being pursued within the fields of healthcare, public health, mental health, law enforcement, and education may address many intervention obstacles. Because of the current available data on risks, trends, and the development of prediction tools, it is now possible to target at-risk youth to provide various culturally competent preventative resources. Development of accessible services is desperately needed to address the lack of firearm safety education, the stigma around common sense gun laws, conflict resolution among adolescents, and improving community resources.
