Comment on: Changes in microbiological characteristics of bloodstream infections before and after the COVID-19 pandemic in a tertiary teaching hospital in Iran
Authors: Sebastiana Silva Sabino, Alexia Máximo Fernandes, Natália Ludendorff Queiroz, Cristiane Silveira de Brito, Lícia Ludendorff Queiroz, Celsa Raquel Villaverde Melgarejo, Paulo P Gontijo Filho, Rosineide Marques Ribas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis correspondence comments on study published in this journal investigating bloodstream infection microbiology before and during the COVID-19 pandemic in Iran, contextualized with Brazilian intensive care data: 44.5% developed microbiologically confirmed secondary infections, Gram-negative pathogens predominated, antibiotic resistance reached 79.4%, supporting stronger antimicrobial stewardship globally through coordinated surveillance efforts urgently.Keywords: COVID-19, Secondary infection, Multidrug resistance, Healthcare-associated infections, Antibiotic use

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic has profoundly affected healthcare systems worldwide [1]. The situation in Brazil was even more challenging, given the already high burden of healthcare-associated infections (HAIs). This scenario is further aggravated by the clinical complexity of patients in intensive care units (ICUs), who often require multiple invasive procedures and are managed with broad empirical antimicrobial therapy [2].

In this context, the study by Jafarpour et al. (2025) [3] on changes in microbiological characteristics of bloodstream infections (BSIs) before and after the pandemic in a tertiary teaching hospital in Iran provides important insights into shifting pathogen profiles and resistance patterns. The authors conducted a retrospective study, analyzing bloodstream infections over a six-year period and comparing pre-pandemic and pandemic phases. Overall, the etiology profile remained dominated by Gram-negative organisms, consistent with the findings of previous studies [4, 5]. However, important changes were observed in pathogen distribution, particularly an increase in Enterobacterales such as Escherichia coli, Klebsiella spp., and Enterobacter spp., alongside a marked decline in Stenotrophomonas maltophilia.

These changes were accompanied by a concerning rise in antimicrobial resistance, especially among Gram-negative pathogens, with increased resistance to multiple antibiotic classes, including carbapenems. Additionally, the proportion of Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) increased substantially, and high rates of vancomycin-resistant enterococci persisted. Collectively, these findings suggest that, while the overall epidemiological pattern of bloodstream infections remained stable, the COVID-19 pandemic may have contributed to a shift toward more resistant organisms.

These patterns likely reflect intensified and often empirical antibiotic use during the COVID-19 pandemic, which has contributed to increased selective pressure and antimicrobial resistance globally. Taken together, these results underscore the critical importance of continuous surveillance to better inform empirical antibiotic therapy and to strengthen infection control strategies.

Hospital-acquired infections and antimicrobial resistance remain major global challenges, particularly in ICUs. In Brazilian tertiary hospitals, reported HAI prevalence ranges from 44.3% to 79.4% [6–8], with pneumonia and BSIs among the most frequent infections [8]. From an epidemiological perspective, the microorganisms most frequently isolated in infections associated with higher morbidity and mortality, such as BSIs and pneumonia, in countries such as Brazil, are Gram-negative bacilli, notably non-fermenting bacilli (Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Acinetobacter baumannii) and members of the Enterobacterales (Klebsiella pneumoniae and Escherichia coli) [8, 9].

The authors also reported increased antimicrobial resistance, with Escherichia coli showing reduced susceptibility to meropenem, Klebsiella spp. exhibiting significant rises in resistance to tetracycline, ciprofloxacin, and gentamicin, and Enterobacter spp. demonstrating increased resistance to most evaluated antibiotics, reinforcing the trend of expanding resistance among Gram-negative bacilli. Our data confirm and expand upon these observations. However, these results should be interpreted considering the differences among methodological designs of the studies.

This descriptive, single-center study was conducted in Brazil and included 474 patients with laboratory-confirmed COVID-19 who were admitted to a dedicated COVID-19 (ICU) between June 2020 and December 2021. Clinical data were retrospectively collected from medical records.

HAIs were defined as infections whose acquisition is related to a healthcare procedure or hospitalization. Chronologically, when there is no clinical or laboratory evidence of infection at the time of patient admission, infections are considered HAIs when their symptoms occur 48 h after admission [10]. Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) was defined by the presence of ≥ 10⁵ CFU/mL in tracheal aspirate cultures collected from patients undergoing invasive mechanical ventilation for a period ≥ 48 h after admission to the Unit [11]. BSI was defined as the presence of bacteria or fungi in the bloodstream, confirmed by a positive blood culture collected within a period ≥ 48 h after hospital/ICU admission and/or by two blood cultures showing the presence of coagulase-negative Staphylococcus [11]. Non-susceptibility was defined according to Magiorakos et al. [12] as including isolates classified as either resistant or intermediate based on the interpretive criteria established by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI).

Additionally, bacterial identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing were performed by the Microbiology Laboratory of the Clinical Hospital of the Federal University of Uberlândia using the VITEK® 2 automated system. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software, version 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). We observed a 44.5% (211/474) incidence of secondary healthcare-associated infections diagnosed based on microbiological criteria, of which 45.3% were caused by Gram-negative microorganisms, predominantly involving the lungs and bloodstream. Notably, a total of 757 microorganisms were recovered from 474 patients with COVID-19, of which 79.4% (601/757) were antimicrobial-resistant (Table 1). Overall, antimicrobial use was high in both groups. The main emphasis was on β-lactams, particularly broad-spectrum agents; however, the use of polymyxins and antifungals was unexpectedly high. A trend toward increased antimicrobial prescribing was observed among patients with BSI, possibly related to the more rapid clinical progression and higher mortality in these cases, which may justify the empirical use of these agents. These data align with those reported by Jafarpour et al. [3] indicating a notable antimicrobial resistance increased during the COVID-19 pandemic. They also align with Brazilian data from Braga et al. [13] and Urzedo et al. [14], which showed that antimicrobial resistance rates and antibiotic consumption were lower before the pandemic, suggesting that the pandemic contributed to the worsening of the antimicrobial resistance scenario. Together, these findings emphasize the importance of continuous surveillance to guide empirical antibiotic treatment strategies and strengthen infection control protocols in future pandemics.

The convergence of evidence from different geographic regions highlights a critical global issue: the consequences of widespread, intense and empirical antimicrobial use during the COVID-19 pandemic. The widespread use of antibiotics in Brazil represents a major healthcare challenge, particularly among critically ill hospitalized patients.

Studies conducted across Latin America consistently report high prevalence rates of antimicrobial use, particularly involving broad-spectrum antibiotics, especially during the COVID-19 pandemic. The indiscriminate use of these agents is increasingly concerning, as it exerts selective pressure on microorganisms, promoting the development of acquired resistance mechanisms and consequently limiting the available therapeutic options [2].

Similar to Brazil, Iran has implemented institutional antimicrobial stewardship programs; however, chronic challenges remain, including the high use of empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics, particularly in hospital and ICU settings [15, 16].

Our study was conducted at a single Brazilian tertiary university hospital during the COVID-19 pandemic. Comparisons with other cohorts should be interpreted with caution due to differences in population characteristics, clinical settings, and study designs. The retrospective design and sample size may have limited some analyses.

However, our findings highlight the substantial burden of HAIs caused by resistant organisms among critically ill COVID-19 patients, particularly VAP and BSIs, in a Brazilian tertiary hospital. These results emphasize the importance of continuous surveillance, strengthened infection prevention measures, and optimized antimicrobial stewardship strategies in intensive care settings. Addressing these challenges is essential to mitigate the impact of antimicrobial resistance during future public health emergencies.
