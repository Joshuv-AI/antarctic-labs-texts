The impact of vitamin D supplementation on anti-Müllerian hormone levels: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Parmis Mirzaei, Ghazaleh Eslamian, Mohammad Hassan Sohouli, Zeinab S Tadayoni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe possible anti-Mullerian hormone (AMH)-reduction impact of vitamin D supplementation has been assessed in a number of interventional investigations. The outcomes are still incongruous, however. This research sought to thoroughly examine the effects of vitamin D supplementation on AMH in order to have a better understanding of them.MethodsUsing standard keywords, the Scopus, PubMed/Medline, Web of Science, Cochrane, and Embase databases were searched to find all controlled trials examining the levels of AMH after vitamin D treatment. For the best result estimate, random-effects model analysis produced 95% confidence intervals and a pooled weighted mean difference.ResultsThis article includes sixteen research with a total of 484 participants. Vitamin D supplementation did not significantly affect AMH levels in single-arm (pooled WMD of -0.22 ng/ml; 95% CI -0.68 to 0.23; P = 0.333) or randomized clinical trial (pooled WMD of -0.07 ng/ml; 95% CI -1.01 to 0.87; P = 0.887) studies, according to pooled results from the random-effects model. Vitamin D during the intervention < 8 weeks and in individuals with low baseline levels of vitamin D serum induces a higher drop in AMH levels, according to the findings of subgroup analysis.ConclusionsDespite the inconclusive and conflicting evidence on the relationship between vitamin D supplementation and AMH levels in women, it is evident that vitamin D may play a role in mitigating the negative impact of AMH on follicular development and granulosa cell differentiation.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-025-02143-4.Keywords: Vitamin D, AMH, Women, PCOS, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Vitamin D, a steroid hormone known for its effects on calcium and bone metabolism, has been suggested to have potential effects on ovarian function and hormone levels. Vitamin D, beyond its classical role in calcium and bone metabolism, plays a critical role in female reproductive physiology, particularly through its involvement in ovarian follicular development, steroid hormone production, and regulation of ovulation [1, 2]. Humans get vitamin D from three sources including exposure to sunlight, diet, and dietary supplements [3]. Local synthesis of the active form of vitamin D occurs by 1a-hydroxylase also in various other tissues such as ovaries, breasts, prostate, brain, and colon [4]. Only a few food sources provide adequate vitamin D [3]. Achieving a serum level of 30 ng/ml is crucial for maximizing the benefits of vitamin D for physical health [5]. In cases of insufficient vitamin D intake, a daily supplement of 800–1000 units is recommended. These findings underscore the importance of maintaining optimal vitamin D levels for overall health [6].

Anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), one of the many hormones influenced by vitamin D, has garnered significant scientific attention due to its pivotal role in reflecting ovarian reserve. It is produced by the granulosa cells of the ovary throughout a woman’s reproductive life. Initially, AMH levels begin to rise during adolescence as follicular development becomes more active. This upward trend continues, and the hormone typically reaches its peak concentration around the age of 25. At this stage, AMH plays a crucial role in regulating the sensitivity of ovarian follicles to follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), thereby influencing folliculogenesis and ovulatory potential [7]. Conversely, a deficiency in AMH may accelerate the loss of ovarian follicles, compromising reproductive capacit [8]. As women age, the pool of primordial follicles naturally diminishes, leading to a gradual decline in AMH levels. Ultimately, AMH becomes undetectable after menopause, marking the near exhaustion of the ovarian reserve [9].

Several studies have investigated the association between vitamin D levels and AMH, yielding inconsistent results. While some research has reported a positive correlation between serum 25(OH)D levels and AMH concentrations [10, 11], suggesting a potential regulatory role of vitamin D in ovarian reserve, other studies have found no significant association [4]. These discrepancies may stem from differences in study design (e.g., cross-sectional vs. longitudinal), population characteristics (such as age, ethnicity, BMI, and baseline vitamin D status), sample sizes, and the assays used to measure vitamin D and AMH. Acknowledging these methodological variations is crucial for interpreting existing findings and guiding future research in this area. Some studies have suggested that vitamin D may influence AMH signaling by decreasing the expression of the AMH receptor type II gene, modulating sensitivity to FSH, and affecting progesterone synthesis; however, these mechanisms remain under investigation and are not yet conclusively established. It also has important functions in follicle growth, differentiation, and luteinization. These results imply that the inhibitory impact of AMH on granulosa cell differentiation and follicular development may be offset by vitamin D [12, 13].

Given the conflicting evidence and the growing interest in the role of vitamin D in female reproductive health, further investigation is necessary to better understand its potential impact on AMH levels. Despite several studies, the underlying mechanisms and the consistency of this relationship remain unclear. Notably, previous literature lacks a comprehensive synthesis of existing evidence, particularly focusing on the effect of vitamin D supplementation on AMH concentrations across different populations and clinical conditions. This gap underscores the need for a consolidated analysis. Therefore, this article presents a systematic review and meta-analysis aimed at summarizing and critically evaluating the current evidence on the relationship between vitamin D supplementation and AMH levels, while also highlighting directions for future research.

Method

Search strategy

The present systematic review and meta-analysis has been registered on the PROSPERO website (registration number: CRD42043493694 For the extensive review of the existing literature, the researcher utilized the following electronic databases: Scopus, PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, and Embase. The search was carried out until 30 December 2024, not limited to papers written in the English language. Moreover, similar papers and gray literature were considered in the search. Medical subject headings (MeSH) and Emtree (Embase subject headings) were selected to search the online databases, as follow: (“Vitamin D”) AND (“Anti-Mullerian Hormone” OR “AMH”) AND (“Clinical Trials as Topic” OR “Single-Blind Method” OR “Clinical Trial”). To find possibly missed qualifying trials, the reference lists of the articles that were obtained and the related review papers were manually searched.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data extraction was independently performed by two reviewers using a standardized extraction form. In cases of disagreement, discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. If consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted to adjudicate. The variables that were extracted from the publications that met our inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) the name of the first author (2) the year of publication (3) diseases (4) the study country (5) the study duration (6) study design (7) Number of individuals participating in the study (intervention and control group) (8), participants mean age, body mass index (BMI), mean AMH level before and after intervention, vitamin D baseline, change of AMH (9), dose per day or week of vitamin D intake (10), SD before and after for AMH and the change of AMH.

Risk of bias assessment and grading the evidence: Cochrane

The Cochrane risk-of-bias assessment for randomized trials, version 2 (RoB 2), was used to examine the included studies [14]. Two reviewers independently evaluated the studies across five key domains: bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. Each domain was rated as “low risk of bias,” “some concerns,” or “high risk of bias,” according to Cochrane guidance. Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

The methodology used for evaluating the quality of the studies relied on the Cochrane risk of bias assessment tool, which examines key domains such as random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding, incomplete outcome data, and selective reporting. However, no strict predefined criteria were applied to exclude studies based on their quality rating. Instead, all eligible studies, regardless of their assessed risk of bias, were included in the meta-analysis to ensure a comprehensive synthesis of available evidence. To address the potential impact of including lower-quality studies, sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess whether their exclusion would significantly alter the overall conclusions. Additionally, careful interpretation of the findings was emphasized, acknowledging the possible influence of methodological limitations on the robustness of the results. Future research should aim to incorporate stricter quality-based selection criteria to enhance the reliability of meta-analytic conclusions.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using STATA version 12.0 software. Different data types were converted to the mean and standard deviations (S.D.s) using a predetermined procedure [15]. In cases without standard deviations, the change was calculated using the following method: the standard deviation change is calculated as the square root of (baseline S.D. 2 + final S.D. 2) - (2 * baseline S.D. * final S.D.). To convert the standard error of the mean (SEM) to standard deviation (SD), the following formula was used: S.D. = SEM × √ 𝑛, where 𝑛 represents the total number of participants in each group. The study findings were analyzed using a random-effects model, and the weighting was done using the standard inverse variance method. Multiple evaluations within a single research group could be handled since the analysis was conducted using the data from the longest time point. I-squared (I2) and Q Statistics were used to assess the degree of research heterogeneity; I2 values ranged from 0% to 25, 26% to 50%, 51% to 75%, and 76% to 100%, respectively, indicating low, moderate, substantial, and considerable heterogeneity [16]. A predetermined subgroup analysis based on the dose and duration of the intervention was carried out in order to find potential reasons for heterogeneity. Egger’s test was used to examine if publication bias existed, and a sensitivity analysis was conducted to ascertain each study’s contribution to the total mean difference [17].

Results of a meta-analysis

Pooled results from the random-effects model showed that vitamin D supplementation did not significantly affect AMH levels in single-arm studies, with a weighted mean difference (WMD) of -0.22 ng/ml (95% CI: -0.68 to 0.23; P = 0.333).

Similarly, no significant change in AMH levels was observed in randomized clinical trials (RCTs), with a pooled WMD of -0.07 ng/ml (95% CI: -1.01 to 0.87; P = 0.887) (Fig. 2).

Forest plot of single arms clinical trial of the effects of vitamin D on Anti-Mullerian Hormone (AMH) (ng/ml) levels

Furthermore, considerable heterogeneity was seen between the studies for this outcome (P < 0.001, I2 = 94.1% for single arms clinical trial studies and P < 0.001, I2 = 87.1% for randomized clinical trial studies, according to the Cochran Q test). To determine the potential source of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were conducted (Fig. 3).

Forest plot of randomized controlled trials investigating the effects of vitamin D on Anti-Mullerian Hormone (AMH) (ng/ml) levels

Analysis of subgroups

In the randomized clinical trials, subgroup analyses were conducted based on intervention duration and baseline vitamin D levels (Fig. 4). For intervention duration, supplementation lasting ≤ 8 weeks was associated with a significant reduction in AMH levels (WMD: -0.66 ng/ml; 95% CI: -1.21 to -0.10; I² = 12.1%), whereas interventions > 8 weeks showed a non-significant increase (WMD: 0.66 ng/ml; 95% CI: -0.17 to 1.49; I² = 64.2%). Regarding baseline vitamin D status, individuals with low baseline serum vitamin D levels experienced a significant reduction in AMH levels (WMD: -0.74 ng/ml; 95% CI: -1.24 to -0.24; I² = 0.0%), while those with high baseline levels showed a significant increase (WMD: 0.67 ng/ml; 95% CI: 0.05 to 1.29; I² = 37.2%).

Analysis of sensitivity

We carefully eliminated each trial from the analysis to see how each article (in the single arms and randomized clinical trials) affected the aggregate effect size for AMH levels. The findings’ robustness was shown by the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Bias in publications

When the publication bias was assessed visually by looking at the funnel, no indication of publication bias was found using Begg’s tests (P = 1.00 for randomized clinical trial studies and P = 0.142 for single arms clinical trial studies) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion

The research findings indicate that vitamin D supplementation does not have a significant effect on AMH levels in either randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or single-arm clinical trials. Both study designs demonstrated statistically non-significant changes in AMH concentrations following intervention. However, by analyzing randomizes control trials as a subgroup we observed that interventions lasting ≤ 8 weeks with low baseline vitamin D levels were associated with a greater decrease in AMH serum levels.

Vitamin D has been shown to influence AMH signaling by reducing the expression of the AMH receptor-II gene. It also decreases sensitivity to follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and lowers progesterone synthesis. Additionally, vitamin D plays an important role in follicle growth, differentiation, and luteinization. These findings suggest that vitamin D may help mitigate the inhibitory effects of AMH on follicular development and granulosa cell differentiation [12, 13].

The research findings on the link between vitamin D and AMH levels in women are inconsistent. Some studies have indicated a positive connection between vitamin D levels and AMH bsed on sign test [11], while others have presented conflicting results [4]. For instance, a systematic review and meta-analysis failed to show any significant correlation between vitamin D and AMH in either fertile or infertile women [18]. However, a study involving infertile women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) and vitamin D deficiency demonstrated a significant decrease in AMH levels after vitamin D treatment [12]. These contradictory outcomes may be attributed to various factors, including the distinct AMH status and follicular conditions of the women under study. For instance, women with PCOS may react differently to vitamin D supplementation than women without PCOS since they usually have abnormal blood AMH levels. Furthermore, ovarian reserve may vary according on a person’s race/ethnicity, locality, and season, all of which have an impact on their vitamin D levels [19]. These factors also influence vitamin D status, which is closely linked to AMH regulation. For example, individuals of different ethnic backgrounds may have varying skin pigmentation, affecting their ability to synthesize vitamin D from sunlight. Geographic location determines sun exposure intensity, and seasonal changes can lead to fluctuations in vitamin D levels due to varying sunlight duration. Consequently, these variables may contribute to differences in both vitamin D status and ovarian reserve markers such as AMH, highlighting the importance of considering them in related research. Furthermore, the data currently available is based on a small number of RCTs, underscoring the need for further study to completely understand the connection between women’s vitamin D and AMH levels [20–26].

In summary, the evidence regarding the correlation between vitamin D and AMH levels in women is still inconclusive and conflicting. While some studies have suggested a positive association, others have reported opposing findings, possibly due to the diverse characteristics of the study populations and the intricate interplay between vitamin D, AMH, and various reproductive conditions. More well-designed RCTs and larger studies are necessary to clarify the impact of vitamin D on AMH levels in women.

Only ten studies met inclusion criteria for this meta-analysis, and some lacked appropriate control groups, limiting generalizability. Despite some limitations that should be considered, this research has several notable strengths. Firstly, it represents the first systematic review and meta-analysis conducted in this specific field, providing a comprehensive overview of existing evidence. Secondly, subgroup analyses were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity and better understand differential effects. Thirdly, the study bias risk was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool and that NutriGrade was used for quality scoring, ensuring methodological robustness. Finally, the review protocol was registered in PROSPERO, which enhances the transparency and reproducibility of the research process.

Clinical and statistical heterogeneities were observed among the included studies, which may affect the overall interpretation of the results. Clinical heterogeneity refers to differences in participant characteristics, such as age, baseline vitamin D levels, and intervention duration, as well as variations in study design and settings. Statistical heterogeneity, indicated by high I² values, reflects variability in effect sizes beyond what would be expected by chance alone. These heterogeneities suggest caution when generalizing the findings, as they may arise from differences in intervention-specific factors (such as drug dosages and protocol duration), differences in the control group status (e.g., metformin/insulin vs. lifestyle modifications) or from baseline disease comorbidities, and patient-specific characteristics (including genetics, age, sex, ethnicity, medical history, and dietary carbohydrate and fat intake).

Conclusion

In conclusion, while evidence on the effect of vitamin D supplementation on AMH levels remains inconclusive, this systematic review and meta-analysis suggests that vitamin D may reduce AMH levels in specific subgroups, particularly in individuals with low baseline vitamin D and shorter intervention durations. These findings highlight a potential modulatory role of vitamin D on ovarian function. However, further well-designed randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes are necessary to confirm these effects and clarify underlying mechanisms. Despite its limitations, this study adds important insights to the current understanding of vitamin D’s impact on female reproductive health.
