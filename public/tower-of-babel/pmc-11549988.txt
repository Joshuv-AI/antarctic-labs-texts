Ultrasound treatment improved gelling and emulsifying properties of myofibrillar proteins from Antarctic krill (Euphausia superba)
Authors: Chang Zhou, Ruoyan Liu, Di Zhao, Kai Shan, Weixin Ke, Chunbao Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Antarctic krill, as one of the largest biomass on the Earth (about 0.65–1 billion tons), is a pivotal species in the Southern Ocean food chain and has incalculable development prospects in food processing [1]. Antarctic krill is rich in various nutrients, especially high-quality proteins, as well as unsaturated fatty acids such as DHA, astaxanthin, minerals and other bioactive ingredients. The Antarctic krill meat is a typical high-protein, low-fat food that meets the current demand for a healthy diet. More importantly, the protein content of Antarctic krill meat is up to 11.9–15.4 %, which contains all the essential amino acids required by human body. It is especially rich in glutamic acid and lysine, which can effectively compensate the amino acids in plant diets [2]. Furthermore, the biological valence of Antarctic krill protein is higher than some common meat proteins and milk proteins, such as casein [3]. However, due to the existence of an efficient low-temperature endogenous enzyme system, Antarctic krill is prone to autolysis after death, resulting in an inevitable degradation of processing properties during storage and transportation [4]. Antarctic krill meat is mostly processed into animal feed or low-value-added products, including dried krill, krill powder and frozen krill, which cannot fully utilize its original nutritional value [5]. Measures to enhance the technological properties of Antarctic krill meat are limited and inefficient, including rinsing and the addition of exogenous additives such as proteins and polysaccharides. Zhang et al. [6] prepared a composite gel by mixing Antarctic krill and white shrimp surimi with four exogenous additives. Although the gelling property was improved, the proportion of Antarctic krill was reduced and the functionality of Antarctic krill meat was not optimally harnessed. At present, protein modification is a prevailing method to elevate the functional attributes of Antarctic krill proteins, particularly substances like carrageenan and basic amino acids [7], [8]. However, the existing product form is still monolithic and consumers are increasingly opting for natural nutrition and food safety without additives. It is imperative to seek more effective methods to enhance the technological properties of Antarctic krill meat without reducing nutritional value. Notably, while physical field technologies have received significant attention in various applications, there is a paucity of research directed towards enhancing the processing performance of Antarctic krill.

Ultrasound has been widely used in meat processing as an emerging non-thermal processing method. Studies have explored ultrasonic pretreatment to enhance the properties of Penaeus white shrimp, chicken, scallops and other materials [9], [10], [11]. Ultrasonic treatment improves gelling and emulsifying properties through cavitation without introducing additives or heating process, offering advantages in safety, environmental friendliness, and efficiency [12]. Myofibrillar proteins (MPs) are crucial for gelation and emulsification properties in minced products [13], [14]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the cavitation effect and mechanical shear lead to MPs dissociation and loss of helix structure, thereby affecting physicochemical properties [10], [15]. These properties contribute to regulating interfacial properties and determining the gelling, emulsifying and foaming characteristics of products. Li et al. [16] found that ultrasonic treatment exposed hydrophobic residues and sulfhydryl groups within proteins, facilitating protein–protein interactions and enhancing gel strength and emulsifying properties. Notably, the effect of ultrasonic treatment depends upon treatment time, ultrasonic power, and the type of meat. Therefore, whether ultrasonic treatment can effectively enhance the gelling and physicochemical properties of Antarctic krill meat and its MPs, and the mechanisms involved require further investigation.

This study aimed to demonstrate the effects of ultrasonic treatment on the gelling and emulsifying properties of Antarctic krill meat, including water holding capacity, texture, gel microstructure and internal water distribution. Then, to elucidate the underlying mechanisms, this study was aimed to explore the protein composition, molecular weight, secondary and tertiary structures of MPs from Antarctic krill meat, as well as the particle size, zeta-potential and other microscopic indicators of MPs. The results will provide important supports for improving the technological properties of Antarctic krill meat, promoting its deep utilization and the development of new minced products.

Materials and methods

Materials

Frozen Antarctic krill meat was purchased from Liaoyu Group Co., Ltd. (Dalian, China) and stored at −20 °C until use. The Antarctic krill meat had a moisture content of 80.33 ± 0.77 % (w/w), a protein content of 13.31 ± 0.15 % (w/w), and a fat content of 0.5 ± 0.04 % (w/w). BCA protein assay kit for protein quantification was purchased from Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China). Protein markers used for SDS-PAGE were purchased from GenScript (NJ, USA) and stored at −20 °C. The other reagents were purchased from Shanghai Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.

Preparation of Antarctic krill meat gels

The preparation of meat gels consisted of four steps: chopping, salting, ultrasonic treatment, and two-stage heating. First, the samples were chopped for 2 min to destroy the muscle fibers of Antarctic krill meat. Then 2 % (w/w) salt was added and further chopped for 3 min to fully extract the salt-soluble proteins and increase the viscosity of krill meat. The krill meat was subsequently encapsulated in watertight bags and subjected to ultrasonic treatment at power levels of 0, 100, 200, 300, 400, or 500 W for 30 min (ensuring the water temperature below 15 °C). The ultrasonic treatment was performed with the pulse-on and pulse-off time of 2 s according to Deng et al. [17]. Finally, the minced krill meat was heated to form heat-induced gels at 40 °C for 30 min and then at 90 °C for 30 min [2]. The gels were cooled to room temperature and kept overnight at 4 °C for subsequent analysis.

Water holding capacity (WHC) measurement

WHC was assessed through cooking loss during heating process and centrifugal loss, which reflects the ability of gels to retain water.

The surface of Antarctic krill meat gels was dried lightly with absorbent paper and weighed (M2). The cooking loss was the percentage of the mass loss after heating relative to the mass of unheated mince (M1).(1)Cookingloss%=M1-M2M1×100Approximately 2 g of gel was wrapped with two layers of absorbent cotton and centrifuged at 4000 g for 20 min. The mass of the gel before and after centrifugation was denoted as W1 and W2, respectively. Centrifugal loss was calculated as:(2)centrifugalloss(%)=W1-W2W1×100

Texture profile analysis (TPA)

Cooled krill meat gels were returned to room temperature, and then sliced into uniformly sized cylinders in 1 cm height and 1.5 cm diameter. Textural properties were evaluated using a TA-XT2i texture analyzer (Stable Micro Systems, England) equipped with a P/50 probe. The main parameters were set as follows: pre-test speed of 2 mm/s, test speed of 1 mm/s, post-test speed of 5 mm/s, compression repeated 2 times with a 5 s interval, strain at 30 %, and trigger force at 5.0 g.

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR)

2 g of sonicated krill meat was loaded into NMR tubes and processed as described in section 2.2 to form heat-induced gels. After drying the surface of the NMR tubes, the water distribution of the krill meat gels was determined using a LF-NMR analyzer (NMI20-040H-I, Suzhou Niumag Instrument Co., Ltd, China). The CPMG pulse sequence parameters were set as follows: SW, 200 KHz; TW, 7000 ms; NS, 8; TE, 0.4 ms; NECH, 10000. The peak areas (A2b, A21, A22), the proportions of different statuses of water (P2b, P21, P22) and the relaxation time (T2b, T21, T22) were calculated

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

According to Chen et al. [18], the structure of the Antarctic krill meat gels was observed using a SEM (FlexSEM 1000 II, Hitachi, Japan). The gels were sliced into 2 mm-thick sections with a cross-section of 5 mm × 5 mm, frozen overnight at −80 °C and then subjected to freeze-drying. The dehydrated samples were affixed to the SEM stage with a special double-sided tape (section side faced up), and observed at a voltage of 1.5 kV.

Extraction of MPs from Antarctic krill meat

MPs were extracted according to Xu et al. [19] with minor modifications. Briefly, the thawed Antarctic krill meat was chopped at 3000 rpm for 45 s. The minced meat was then mixed with 4 times volume (w/v) of extraction buffer (0.1 mol/L KCl, 1 mmol/L ethylene glycol tetraacetic acid, 20 mmol/L K2HPO4/KH2PO4, 2 mmol/L MgCl2, pH 7.0) using a homogenizer (PD500, PRIMA Technology Co., Ltd, England) in an ice bath at 7000 rpm for 60 s. The homogenate was filtered through double gauzes, and the precipitates were collected and centrifuged at 3000 g for 10 min (Avanti J-26S XP, Beckman Coulter, CA) at 4 °C. The precipitates were subjected to two additional extraction cycles repeating the above steps. Subsequently, the precipitate was homogenized in a cold solution (0.1 mol/L KCl, ratio 1:4, w/v) at 7000 rpm for 60 s, followed by centrifugation at 3000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. After two washes, the precipitate was dissolved in sodium phosphate buffer (PBS, 0.6 mol/L NaCl, 20 mmol/L Na2HPO4/NaH2PO4, pH 7.0). The resulting mixture was centrifuged at 6000 g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain MPs solution, and the protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit.

Ultrasonic treatment for MPs

The MP samples were treated using an ultrasonic cell grinder (YM-1500Y, Yuming Instrument Co., Ltd, China) with a frequency of 20 KHz. A 100 mL breaker containing 40 mL of MPs solution (2 mg/mL) was placed in an ice bath to keep the solution at a low temperature. An ultrasonic probe with a 22 mm diameter was submerged in the MPs solution to a depth of 10 mm below the liquid surface. Consistent with the protocol in section 2.2, ultrasonic treatment was applied to the MP solution at power levels of 0, 100, 200, 300, 400, or 500 W for 6 min. The samples were then stored at 4 °C for subsequent analysis.

Surface hydrophobicity (S0-ANS) of MPs

According to Wan et al. [20], surface hydrophobicity was determined based on the binding of 8-Anilino-1-naphthalenesulfonic acid (ANS) as a fluorescent probe to the protein hydrophobic region. 20 µL of ANS solution (8 mmol/L, pH 8.0) was vortexed with 4 mL of MPs solution (diluted to 0.125, 0.25, and 0.5 mg/mL with PBS buffer). The mixture was then incubated at 25 °C for 20 min in the dark. Then, fluorescence intensity was measured with an excitation wavelength of 375 nm and an emission wavelength of 420–540 nm. The fluorescence intensity of PBS buffer with ANS added was subtracted from the samples as background. The maximum fluorescence intensity was linearly fitted to the protein concentration, and the initial slope of the curve was used to represent surface hydrophobicity [20].

Total and active sulfhydryl (SH) contents of MPs

5,5′-Dithiobis (2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) can react with SH groups in a colored manner to quantify the SH groups of proteins. According to Zhang et al. [21], 0.3 mL of MPs solution (1 mg/mL) was mixed with 20 µL of Ellman's reagent (4 mg/mL DTNB, pH 8.0) and 1.5 mL of tris–glycine buffer (8 mol/L urea, 86 mmol/L tris, 4 mmol/L ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid, 90 mmol/L glycine, pH 8.0). The resulting mixture was allowed to react at 25 °C in the dark for 1 h. After centrifugation at 10,000 g for 10 min, the absorbance of the supernatant at 412 nm was recorded.

The active SH content was determined using tris–glycine buffer without urea. The mixture was allowed to react at 4 °C in the dark for 1 h, following the protocol described by Shi et al. [22]. The formula for calculating the SH content was as follows:(3)sulfhydrylcontent(μmol/gprotein)=73.53×A412×ncwhere: A412 is the difference in absorbance between the sample and the blank control (PBS buffer) at 412 nm; n is the dilution ratio; and c is the protein concentration (mg/mL).

Secondary structures of MPs

The secondary structures of MPs were measured using Fourier transformed infrared spectroscopy (Nicolet iS10, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). An appropriate volume of MPs solution (2 mg/mL) was placed on the test stage. The parameters were set to a resolution of 8 cm−1, a scan accumulation of 32 times, and a spectral range of 4000–400 cm−1 [23]. Prior to sample analysis, the spectrum of PBS buffer was recorded as the background to mitigate the influence of the sample buffer. The spectral data within the 1700–1600 cm−1 range (amide I region) was analyzed using peakfit 4.12 software. Experiments were repeated 6 times.

Sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE)

According to Xiong et al. [24], the MPs solution (1 mg/mL) was mixed with 5 times the sample loading buffer (non-reducing) and β-mercaptoethanol solution (β-ME, 10 %) at a volume ratio of 4:1:4, and then incubated at 95 °C for 5 min. After cooling, the samples were loaded into the wells of precast gels (4–20 %, 15 wells). Each sample volume was 10 µL, and the protein marker (5–270 KDa) volume was 8 µL. Electrophoresis was conducted using a two-stage constant voltage mode with the low-pressure stage at 80 V for 30 min and the high-pressure stage at 120 V for 70 min. The non-reducing group was prepared as described above without β-ME. Subsequently, the gels were shock-stained with Coomassie Blue Fast Staining Solution for 1 h.

Particle size and zeta potential of MPs

The Malvern Zetasizer Nano (ZsS90, Malvern, England) was utilized to measure the particle size and zeta potential of MPs. For particle size analysis, the refractive index of protein was set to 1.450, while that of the water phase was set to 1.330. A loading volume of 1 mL was used, with the MPs concentration set at 1 mg/mL [25]. For zeta potential determination, the samples were measured using a dip cell at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL.

Protein solubility and turbidity of MPs

The MPs solution (2 mg/mL) was centrifuged at 10,000 g for 15 min, and solubility was determined as the ratio of the protein concentration after centrifugation (C2) to that of the original sample (C1). The method of turbidity was referred to Tian et al. [26], with the concentration adjusted to 1 mg/mL.(4)Proteinsolubility(%)=C2C1×100

Emulsifying activity index (EAI) and emulsion stability index (ESI) of MPs

EAI and ESI were determined according to the method of Li et al. [12]. MPs solution (1.5 mg/mL) was mixed with corn oil at a ratio of 4:1 (v/w) and homogenized at 8000 rpm for 1 min. Subsequently, 50 µL of the resulting emulsion was diluted into 5 mL of 0.1 % SDS solution at 0 and 10 min, respectively, and the absorbance of the diluted solution was measured at 500 nm. The EAI and ESI were calculated as:(5)EAIm2/g=2×2.303×A0×Nc×φ×10000(6)ESI%=100×(A0-A10)A0where: A0 and A10 are the absorbance at 0 min and 10 min; N is the dilution ratio; c is the protein concentration (g/mL); and φ is the volume fraction of the oil.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) of MPs solutions and emulsions

MPs solution (1 mL) was mixed with 40 µL of Nile Red (2 mg/mL) and allowed to react in the dark for 10 min. Similarly, 1 mL of emulsion was combined with 40 µL of a mixed dye composed of Nile Red (2 mg/mL) and Nile Blue (2 mg/mL). Nile Red and Nile Blue were excited at wavelengths of 488 and 633 nm, respectively. The samples were applied to slides, and the distribution of droplets was observed under a 20◊ eye object. Oil signals were represented in green, while protein signals appeared in red.

Statistical analysis

Ultrasonic power was set as the independent, while the measured variables were set as the dependent. A significance level of 0.05 (p < 0.05, significant) was established for the statistical tests. SPSS Statistics 23.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA) was used to conduct one-way ANOVA and Duncan's multiple range test, which were used to assess significance. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation with five repetitions.

Results and discussion

WHC of Antarctic krill meat gels

Cooking loss and centrifugal loss are standard parameters to assess WHC, which directly influences the qualities of meat products such as tenderness, color and juiciness. Ultrasonic treatment significantly decreased cooking loss and centrifugal loss from 41.34 % and 43.52 % (untreated group) to 33.04 % and 32.36 % (300 W-treated group), respectively (Fig. 1A and 1B). Similarly, Shao et al. [27] pointed out that ultrasonic treatment could induce myosin oxidation and polymerization to improve water retention and reduce cooking loss. Feng et al. [28] found that the WHC of BSM gels was approximately doubled after 480 W ultrasonic treatment, attributing to the exposure of hydrophilic groups, which improved the water-binding capacity of proteins. Moreover, ultrasonic treatment changes the spatial structure of proteins, exposing more active groups and resulting in a more ordered and uniform network structure. This facilitates greater water retention through enhanced interactions among hydrogen bonds, disulfide bonds and ionic bonds [12], [29]. Water distribution of Antarctic krill meat gels (LF-NMR)

Based on the macroscopic differences in water holding capacity, we further investigated the water distribution and migration patterns within the gels by LF-NMR. LF-NMR is a non-destructive method used to assess water distribution within a sample. According to relaxation time T2, water can be categorized into bound water (T2b), immobilized water (T21) and free water (T22) [32].

As shown in Table 2, Antarctic krill meat gels were predominately composed of immobilized water, followed by free water. The minced meat formed heat-induced gels in NMR tubes without water escaping, so the total peak area showed no significant difference. Additionally, the A2b did not significantly change under different ultrasonic treatments, potentially attributing to the fact that T2b is tightly bound to proteins and other macromolecules and not susceptible to change [33]. With the increase of ultrasonic power, A21 exhibited an increase followed by a decrease, while A22 showed the opposite changes. Fig. 1C shows the relative percentages of different statuses of water calculated from the peak areas. Specifically, at 300 W ultrasonic power, P21 increased from 52.23 % to 59.84 % and P22 decreased from 46.19 % to 38.19 %. Immobilized water is tightly bound within the gel structure, contrasting to free water, which is located outside the gel [5]. The transformation of free water to immobilized water indicated that ultrasonic treatment enhanced interactions between MPs and water molecules, weakening water mobility [34]. Additionally, free and immobilized water were related to water holding capacity, with the decrease in P22 aligning with reduced cooking and centrifugal losses in this study. The larger the relaxation time, the stronger the mobility of water. The wider the peak, the more complex the water composition. As seen in Fig. 1D, ultrasonic treatments at power levels of 100, 200, 300 and 400 W resulted in widening of T21 peak and narrowing of T22 peak, indicating that part of the free water transformed into immobilized water. Although the changes in peak width caused the vertices of the T21 and T22 peaks to shift towards the direction of increasing relaxation time (Table 2), the overall water mobility was still weakened.

Gel microstructure of Antarctic krill meat gels

The enhancement of water holding capacity and texture can be better understood by characterizing the microstructure of the gel matrix. As seen in Fig. 2, the gel matrix of the untreated group was rough and fragile with flaky structure and large irregular pores. After sonication, the microstructure of gels was flatter and denser, especially at an ultrasonic power of 300 W. However, with further increases in power to 400 W and 500 W, the cross-linking of gel structure was weakened and larger pores reappeared. These changes in gel microstructure were consistent with the observed experimental phenomena. Previous studies have shown that particle size and protein structure are critical factors affecting gelling property [29], [35]. Cavitation effect induced protein disintegration, structural stretching, and exposure of reactive groups, which promoted orderly protein cross-linking to form a regular gel structure [29]. Li et al. [12] similarly noted that ultrasonic treatment improved the gel structure of minced pacific white shrimp. However, excessive ultrasonic power led to protein aggregation and loss of functionality in Antarctic krill, forming a rough gel network structure, as described by Sun et al. Tertiary conformations of MPs

The interaction forces mediating gel formation are influenced by the secondary and tertiary structures of proteins. The tertiary structure of proteins relies on non-covalent interactions among side-chain residues [37]. Moreover, disulfide bonds play a crucial role in maintaining correct protein folding [38]. Therefore, this study revealed the changes in tertiary structure by determining the total and reactive SH groups and the surface hydrophobicity of MPs.

The SH groups are the most active and sensitive functional groups of MPs, and reflect the degree of oxidation. Total SH groups include active SH groups on the surface of proteins and SH groups embedded in proteins [34]. In Fig. 3A, as ultrasonic power increased to 500 W, the total SH continued to decrease, reaching a minimum value at 500 W (33.33 µmol/g, p < 0.05), representing a 25.62 % decline. Studies have shown that ultrasonic treatment can generate reactive free radicals by promoting water molecule decomposition [39], with myosin being a preferred target for free radicals. Thus, internal SH groups are exposed and vulnerable to oxidation, resulting in the formation of inter- and intramolecular disulfide bonds catalyzed by ROS [24], [40]. The decline of total SH groups was in accordance with the above findings of SDS-PAGE in this study and reflected the formation of disulfide bonds. This was consistent with previous work by Liu et al. [41], which showed a significant decline in total SH groups with increased ultrasonic power or duration. During processing, ultrasonic treatment can damage muscle tissue and cell membrane, releasing metal ions, heme and other pro-oxidants that accelerate SH oxidation [12], [34].Fig. 3Effect of ultrasonic power on total sulfhydryl groups and active sulfhydryl groups (A), surface hydrophobicity (B), Fourier infrared spectrogram (C) and composition of the secondary structures (D) of MPs. The change in active SH groups was similar to that of total SH groups, reaching a minimum value at 500 W (32.46 µmol/g, p < 0.05) with a decline of 19.55 %. However, the exposure of internal SH groups and the breakage of disulfide bonds due to cavitation can increase active SH groups [9], [42]. The active SH groups exhibited a different trend, probably due to the partial unfolding of MPs from Antarctic Krill meat by endogenous enzymes, resulting in fewer internal SH groups. During sonication, fragmentation, rearrangement and polymerization occurred simultaneously. The oxidation rate of SH groups was higher than the production rate, leading to an eventual reduction in active SH groups. Notably, the total SH groups and active SH groups were closely aligned, with a ratio close to 1 with the increasing ultrasonic power. This phenomenon suggested that MPs were unfolded and oxidized with less internal SH groups. Furthermore, the reduction in total SH groups was greater than that of active SH groups, indicating that ultrasonic treatment promoted MPs to unfold, consistent with findings of Liu et al. [41].

S0-ANS characterizes the exposure degree of hydrophobic groups to the external environment, providing insights into the protein oxidation and folding [43]. As ultrasonic power increased, S0-ANS also increased, reaching a maximum at 300 W, which was 1.3 times that of the untreated group (p < 0.05, Fig. 3B). Cavitation can break down MPs, exposing hidden hydrophobic groups to the external environment. In addition, hydrophobic groups are partially buried inside the α-helix structure of myosin [44]. The destruction of α-helix also contributes to higher hydrophobicity. However, once ultrasonic power exceeded 300 W, protein aggregation and α-helix recovery caused the hydrophobic groups to become reburied within molecules [45]. Consistent with previous findings [46], S0-ANS showed a negative correlation with α-helix content.

Secondary conformations of MPs

The ordered cross-linking of MPs tended to form a denser gel structure with the conversion from α-helix to ordered β-sheets [47], and thus this study further measured the changes of secondary structures of MPs under ultrasonic treatment by FT-IR. Previous studies have shown that the amide I band (mainly based on the stretching vibration of the C = O) is the most sensitive region for protein skeleton analysis [48], including α-helix (1650–1660 cm−1), β-sheet (1600–1640 cm−1), β-turn (1660–1700 cm−1) and random coil (1640–1650 cm−1) [11]. Fig. 3C shows the FT-IR spectra of MPs under different ultrasonic powers. The increased transmittance peak at 1650–1660 cm−1 indicated a reduction in α-helix content.

As seen in Fig. 3D, β-sheet and β-turn were the predominant components (> 70 %) of Antarctic krill MPs, consistent with findings by Zhang et al. [11] regarding shrimp surimi. The 200 W, 300 W and 400 W ultrasonic power resulted in the conversion of α-helix and random coil structures into β-sheet and β-turn (Table 3). The α-helix and β-sheet rely on hydrogen bonds within and between polypeptide chains to form stable structures, respectively. Protein composition and molecular weight of MPs

Gel electrophoresis is a method for separating proteins based on their differential migration rates in a gel matrix. In reducing gel electrophoresis, the protein-polymers are reduced to monomers or subunits, and the aggregation of proteins can be observed by comparing with non-reducing gel electrophoresis. Therefore, the changes in the composition and molecular weight of MPs from Antarctic krill meat under various ultrasonic intensities were investigated using both non-reducing and reducing gel electrophoresis. Fig. 4 illustrates that tropomyosin (35 kDa) and actin (43 kDa) were the dominant proteins. Myosin constitutes the primary component of MPs, governing their functional properties and determining the gelling capacity [36]. The shallower bands observed, possibly due to the loss of myosin heavy chains (MHC) (around 200 kDa) and myosin light chains (MLC) (17–23 kDa) during frozen storage [50], indicated that the Antarctic krill mince possessed diminished gelling property. Additionally, the MPs from Antarctic krill meat contained paramyosin (100 kDa), a protein characteristic of invertebrates.Fig. 4Effect of ultrasonic power on the protein composition and molecular weight of MPs (SDS-PAGE).

Effect of ultrasonic power on the protein composition and molecular weight of MPs (SDS-PAGE).

The composition of MPs bands remained constant after sonication, corroborating the findings of Li et al. [16] that increasing ultrasonic power to 500 W did not change the electrophoretic profile of MPs. The aggregates (>270 kDa) might be attributed to cross-linking of denatured MPs during frozen storage. Besides, the reduction in aggregate levels post-sonication suggested that cavitation effect may disintegrate these aggregates into smaller, soluble molecules, and increase solubility [51]. Furthermore, ultrasonic treatment led to more pronounced diffusion of paramyosin and bottom bands (Fig. 4). The addition of β-ME, a reducing agent that broke disulfide bonds within and between protein polymers [52], resulted in brighter bands of actin and tropomyosin, indicating that ultrasonic treatment induced MPs aggregation through disulfide bond formation.

Besides, a new band at 196 kDa appeared, potentially corresponding to MHC based on its molecular weight, further suggesting protein aggregation during storage. Thus, MPs from Antarctic krill, especially MHC, were highly susceptible to degradation and oxidative aggregation during storage. It is essential to employ ultrasound and other technologies to enhance the technological properties of Antarctic krill.

Solubility and turbidity of MPs

Solubility is determined by the balance of the attractive and repulsive forces among particles [53]. Fig. 5A and 5B demonstrate the solubility and turbidity of MPs under various ultrasonic powers. An inverse relationship between turbidity and solubility was observed, with 300 W identified as the optimal condition. Solubility increased from 91.90 % (untreated group) to 98.48 % (300 W-treated group), while turbidity decreased from 0.069 (untreated group) to 0.026 (300 W-treated group) (p < 0.05). These changes can be explained by the alterations in secondary and tertiary structures observed in 3.5, 3.6. The exposure of hydrophilic groups enhanced the ionic interactions between MPs and solvent [15], [17]. Additionally, ultrasonic treatment disrupted hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions among MPs, thereby impeding aggregation [54]. In general, the reduction in MPs-MPs hydrophobic interactions and the enhancement of MPs-solvent ion interactions contributed to increased solubility and decreased turbidity. The reduction in solubility as the ultrasonic power exceeded 300 W can be caused by protein aggregation resulting from the formation of disulfide bonds and hydrophobic interactions [55]. Similarly, Liu et al. [41] observed a positive correlation between myosin solubility and ultrasonic power and time, suggesting that although increased surface hydrophobicity might induce protein aggregation, particle size reduction played a more prominent role.Fig. 5Effect of ultrasonic power on the protein solubility (A), turbidity (B), zeta-potential (C), particle size distribution and Z-average (D) of MPs. Particle size and zeta-potential of MPs

The zeta-potential reflects the surface charge of the particles, and its absolute value (|zeta-potential|) is used to characterize the interface properties and electrostatic interactions [56], [57]. A deviation from 0 in zeta-potential indicates stronger interactions between adjacent particles. As shown in Fig. 5C, the zeta-potential of MPs consistently exhibited negative values. With increasing the ultrasonic power, the |zeta-potential| increased and peaked at 300 W (p < 0.05, 11.05 mV). The increase was attributed to the transfer of negatively charged polar groups from the interior of molecules to the surface due to the cavitation effect, thus increasing the net charge of MPs [51], [58]. However, at higher ultrasonic power levels, the |zeta-potential| decreased due to MPs re-aggregation, with polar groups being encapsulated again [59]. The reduction in net charge was also influenced by the exposure of positively charged groups, which counteracted the negative charge [59].

Particle size is an essential indicator to characterize the macroscopic properties of protein aggregates, particularly influencing interfacial properties [25]. As shown in Fig. 5D, the Z-average of MPs significantly decreased after ultrasonic treatment, reaching a minimum value of 227.10 nm at 300 W, representing a 31.64 % reduction (p < 0.05). Notably, the differences between sonicated groups (200–400 W) were minimal, probably due to the fact that the MPs structure was partially unfolded, limiting size reduction with increased ultrasonic power. Moreover, the untreated group exhibited a unimodal distribution (300–700 nm), while the sonicated groups displayed bimodal and trimodal distributions with broader ranges (20–800 nm), indicating that mechanical vibration induced by cavitation fragmented protein aggregates into smaller pieces. The shift of peak position to the left also reflected protein dissociation. Similarly, Khatkar et al. [60] found that whey protein collisions under cavitation reduced the particle size from 20 µm to less than 1 µm. Gordon and Pilosof [61] highlighted that cavitation and turbulence dismantled whey protein structure, resulting in smaller particles and smoother surface.

Emulsifying properties of MPs

Protein acts as a natural emulsifier and interacts with fat in food, affecting the quality of minced products. Studies have shown that the emulsification process involves the stretching and adsorption of proteins on the oil–water interface, and proteins with high surface hydrophobicity and flexible conformation have lower energy barriers to overcome during adsorption [62]. In the present study, MPs from Antarctic Krill exhibited characteristics of small particle size and a stretched molecular structure, and then further tested the emulsifying properties of MPs.

ESI characterizes the ability of emulsions to remain stable and avoid aggregation. With increasing ultrasonic power, ESI reached the maximum value at 300 W, significantly increasing from 20.23 % to 29.22 % (an increase of 44.44 %, Fig. 6A) compared to the untreated group. This improvement was related to the observed changes by ultrasonic treatment in particle size and structure of MPs, because smaller particle sizes were likely to allow more proteins to participate in constructing the adsorption layer, uniformly and fully covering oil droplets to prevent aggregation [13]. Besides, the structural flexibility of MPs promoted MPs-oil droplets interactions and the formation of a stable protein interface film by enhancing the hydrophobic adsorption of non-polar amino acid residues with oil droplets. Amiri et al. [13] found that ultrasonic treatment with proper power and exposure time could significantly improve emulsion stability by increasing the exposure of hydrophobic groups within proteins, thereby promoting adjacent molecules to react on the interfaces.Fig. 6Effect of ultrasonic power on the ESI (A) and EAI (B) of emulsions. EAI characterizes the adsorption capacity of proteins on the oil–water interface. In this study, the EAI of MPs did not significantly change under different ultrasonic treatments (Fig. 6B). O'Sullivan et al. [63] found similar results that the lipid adsorption rate of cold-water fish gelatin and soy protein isolate were not different from that of the untreated group by comparing the changes in the emulsifying properties of different proteins under ultrasonic treatment. This similarity could be explained by the lower molecular weight of these proteins, enabling them to rapidly migrate to the surface of the oil droplet.

Protein and oil distribution of MPs solutions and emulsions

We further characterized the distribution and morphology of droplets in MPs solutions and emulsions by CLSM, visualizing the changes of ultrasonic treatment on the protein structure and lipid adsorption.

Fig. 7 shows the morphology and distribution of MPs under different ultrasonic power levels. Obviously, the MPs displayed a reduction in particle size with a smooth and uniform surface in the 100 to 300 W power groups, while MPs aggregated again at 500 W. The distribution of emulsions is shown in Fig. 8. The emulsions in the untreated group exhibited large droplets, flocculation, and uneven distribution. In this group, the MPs were free and large in the water phase, and could not be completely adsorbed at the interface. As the ultrasonic power increased, the MPs became smaller and more evenly distributed, forming interfacial films and wrapping oil droplets. This prevented emulsion aggregation through electrostatic interactions and spatial site resistance. However, at 500 W, the MPs were excessively denaturized, weakening their emulsifying properties and resulting in the aggregation of oil droplets. Conclusion

Ultrasonic treatment, especially at 300 W, significantly improved the gelling and emulsifying properties of Antarctic krill meat, as evidenced by enhanced water-holding capacity, increased texture, and enhanced emulsion stability. MPs formed a more stable and denser gel structure, thereby retaining more water. In addition, MPs covered the surface of oil droplets and interacted with them to improve emulsifying properties. Ultrasonic treatment changed protein structure by disrupting the α-helix and exposing internal sulfhydryl and hydrophobic groups, which regulated intermolecular interactions and interface properties. In general, ultrasonic treatment provides a robust method to enhance the functional characteristics of Antarctic krill meat, offering significant potential for its high-value utilization.
