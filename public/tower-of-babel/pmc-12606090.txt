Minimal clinical predictors enable machine learning detection of hepatocellular carcinoma in a Filipino cohort
Authors: Ranzivelle Marianne L. Roxas-Villanueva, Mojhune Gabriel Manzanillo, Edhel S. Tripon, Dannah Zemirah G. Junio, Marc Daniel O. Jamindang, Ramonito B. Nuique, Beatrice J. Tiangco, Jason R. Albia, Christian M. Alis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Despite significant advancements in the diagnosis and treatment of Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the overall prognosis remains challenging due to its multifactorial nature. Recently, machine learning has been increasingly utilized in HCC-related research, offering significant advancements in diagnostic accuracy and early detection. However, the application of these techniques in diverse populations, particularly underrepresented groups, remains limited. In this study, seven machine learning algorithms: logistic regression (LR), k-nearest neighbor (KNN), support vector classifier (SVC), decision tree (DT), random forest (RF), stochastic gradient boosting (GB) and LightGBM, were tested. Optimal hyperparameters were determined using a grid-search approach, and several feature selection techniques were applied to identify key predictors in detecting HCC. Among the tested algorithms, the RF, with its optimal hyperparameters, demonstrated superior predictive performance at 98.9 % accuracy, 90.5% sensitivity, 99.8% specificity, 99% NPV, 98% PPV, and an AUC of 0.99. LightGBM delivered similarly strong results, attaining an AUC of 0.99, 99.1% accuracy, 94.9% sensitivity, 99.5% specificity, 99.5% NPV, and 95.5% PPV. Notably, only seven clinical predictors: age, albumin, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), des-gamma-carboxy prothrombin (DCP), aspartate transaminase, and platelet count, were necessary to achieve these results. In addition, we illustrate that RF has a robust capability to detect HCC irrespective of the clinical risk factors. This study underscores the potential of machine-learning-based detection models as an alternative for HCC detection and screening. The use of a limited set of predictors not only simplifies the diagnostic process but is also advantageous for resource-limited and remote settings. Using machine learning in HCC detection can significantly improve diagnostic efficiency and enable earlier detection, potentially leading to better patient outcomes and more effective treatment strategies. This research is the first comprehensive analysis of HCC clinical data from a Filipino cohort, offering critical insights into the disease within an underrepresented group.

Introduction

Liver cancer presents a significant global challenge, ranking as the sixth most common cancer and the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide1 . In 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) reported 900,000 new cases of liver cancer and 800,000 related deaths2 . While the incidence of other cancers is declining, GLOBOCAN predicts that liver cancer cases will rise to 1.4 million, with 1.3 million deaths by 20403 .

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the predominant subtype of liver cancer, accounting for 75-85% of cases4 . The primary risk factors for HCC include chronic hepatitis B and C infections, alcohol-related liver disease, metabolic dysfunction-related steatotic liver disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and obesity2,5.

HCC is often diagnosed in its advanced stages because it lacks early symptoms, resulting in poor prognosis across all regions6. Medical societies advocate for systematic screening and surveillance targeting high-risk populations to enable early-stage detection and pursue appropriate disease management5. However, the lack of access to primary and cost-effective diagnostic tests for liver cancer poses a challenge in achieving this goal7, especially for low-resource settings such as the Philippines.

Most professional society guidelines including the Philippine Clinical Practice Guidelines (PCPG) for Hepatoceullar Carcinoma, use Ultrasound with Alpha Fetoprotein (AFP) as the screening tool for HCC in high risk populations. Ultrasonography, owing to its affordability, non-invasiveness, and accessibility, is recommended for HCC surveillance. However, its sensitivity in detecting early-stage HCC remains inadequate, warranting the exploration of alternative diagnostic routes. In addition, in an archipelago like the Philippines, travel to facilities with ultrasound might be difficult compared to blood-based screening tests. Serum biomarkers such as alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), AFP-L3, and des-gamma-carboxy prothrombin (DCP) have been extensively evaluated as diagnostic markers and have shown potential in detecting hepatocellular carcinoma. However, their performance remains limited when using conventional cutoff values8, highlighting the need for optimized thresholds or complementary diagnostic approaches to enhance their clinical utility.

Considering the limitations of image and biomarker-based procedures in detecting HCC from at-risk populations, we sought to develop a machine learning model using defined clinical and serologic parameters. Recent advances in machine learning (ML) have demonstrated substantial potential in addressing the diagnostic challenges of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) by identifying complex patterns within clinical and laboratory data. A convolutional neural network (CNN) developed to predict HCC in hepatitis B virus (HBV) patients achieved an AUC of 0.886 and an accuracy of 0.980, using data from the Taiwan Health database9. In another study, a deep neural network applied to HBV-related cirrhosis patients treated with entecavir yielded a c-index of 0.78210. Gradient boosting methods have consistently outperformed other machine learning algorithms in several comparative studies11,12, and their effectiveness has been further supported by external validation in HBV patients undergoing antiviral therapy13. In a comparative evaluation of modeling techniques, ridge regression and random forest achieved an AUROC of approximately 0.84, surpassing traditional risk scoring systems for both HBV and HCV cohorts14. Collectively, these findings underscore the versatility and clinical promise of ML-based approaches, although differences in data sources, patient populations, and feature selection strategies remain.

To address common issues such as class imbalance, small datasets, and heterogeneous features, recent studies have introduced advanced ML strategies. One such method applied cluster-based oversampling to improve survival prediction in highly variable datasets with missing values, demonstrating enhanced performance using neural networks and logistic regression15. Another approach integrated seven classifiers into a stacked ensemble model, supported by genetic optimization for feature selection, and achieved a detection accuracy of 90.3% and an F1-score of 88.57% across 49 clinically significant features16.

Feature selection has emerged as a critical step in reducing dimensionality, improving generalization, and enhancing interpretability. A feature selection pipeline employing recursive feature elimination with gradient boosting resulted in over 96% accuracy and a 95.66% F1-score in HCC survival prediction tasks17. In a hybrid modeling effort, a combination of Naïve Bayes, logistic regression, random forest, linear discriminant analysis, and deep neural networks was used as base learners, while the meta-classifier was optimized using the Dipper Throated Optimization and Particle Swarm Optimization (DTPSO) algorithm. This model achieved an accuracy of 98.52%, outperforming several conventional metaheuristic optimization techniques18. Another study introduced a two-level selection method that combined neighborhood component analysis and a genetic algorithm with a support vector machine classifier (NCA-GA-SVM), with an accuracy of 96.36% and an F1-score of 95.52% for HCC fatality prediction19.

The present study contributes to this growing body of work in two key ways. First, it represents one of the early efforts to develop and evaluate an ML-based HCC prediction model using clinical data from a Filipino cohort. Different racial and ethnic groups may have distinct genetic predispositions that influence the risk, progression, and response to treatment of HCC20,21. Also, environmental, lifestyle, and socioeconomic factors that contribute to HCC risk can vary significantly across different racial and ethnic groups. The data and findings from this study offer crucial insights into the disease within the Filipino population by addressing this previously underrepresented group.

Second, we identified relevant predictors for HCC detection using several feature selection algorithms (Pearson correlation, random forest feature selection, information gain feature selection, recursive feature elimination and feature selection using Lasso Regression). Feature selection reduces the complexity associated with high-dimensional data, prevents issues related to overfitting, decreases the time required to train models, and improves the models’ ability to generalize to new, unseen data22. Limiting the number of predictors to only the most relevant ones reduces the need for extensive testing, which can be costly and time-consuming. By focusing on a smaller set of highly predictive variables, healthcare providers can streamline the diagnostic process, reducing both the cost and time required for accurate diagnosis. This approach is crucial in settings where healthcare budgets are constrained and access to advanced testing facilities is limited.

Tables 1 and 2 compare the demographic and clinical characteristics of the 73 HCC and 658 non-HCC patients. There is a significant relationship (p < 0.0001) between being male and the development of HCC. Furthermore, age over 40 years is associated with an increased risk of HCC development. Our results also indicated that chronic hepatitis B and presence of cirrhosis are associated with an increased risk of HCC development.

There are also significantly higher levels of AFP and DCP in HCC patients than in non-HCC patients. In addition, the following clinical variables were also found to be significantly different between the two cohorts: total cholesterol, HDL, creatinine, albumin, alkaline phosphatase, ALT, AST, total bilirubin, neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocyte, platelet count, and INR. The difference is indicative of variation in disease etiology and, therefore, can be explored further to come up with a set of these variables for potential clinical utility in HCC detection.

Results from RFE-CV indicate that seven is the optimal number of features with a mean test accuracy of 0.989 as shown in Fig. 1a. The identified predictive features are AFP, DCP, age, alkaline phosphatase, AST, albumin, and platelet count. This is consistent with the results of the random forest algorithm where the seven features have the top Gini importance in Table 3. For the information gain and Pearson correlation, only AFP, DCP, age, alkaline phosphatase, AST, and albumin are consistently in the top six rank with high feature importance score. Platelet count has low Pearson correlation coefficient but it is also identified as a relevant predictor using Lasso regression. The Lasso penalty reduces the coefficient values towards zero, allowing less significant variables to have coefficients that are zero or close to zero. Figure 1b shows the features with non-zero coefficient in Lasso regression. It can be observed that platelet count has a negative coefficient which indicates that a low platelet count can be a marker for the identification of individuals at high-risk for HCC.

From a clinical point of view, HCC detection models should reliably identify positive cases irrespective of the patient’s present risk factors, allowing for effective triaging and reducing the unintended consequences of further testing. In this view, we evaluated the model’s performance in identifying HCC cohorts from non-HCC cohorts (Table 4) and identifying HCC cohorts with different risk factors (Table 5).

Our model evaluation procedure demonstrates that all machine learning modes can identify HCC. In the validation group (Table 4), AUC values range from 0.958-0.999. Among the tested algorithms, the best performance was achieved by the Random Forest model, which obtained an AUC of 0.999, 98.9% accuracy, 90.5% sensitivity, 99.8% specificity, 99% negative predictive value (NPV), and 98% positive predictive value (PPV). LightGBM achieved similarly strong results, with an AUC of 0.999, 99.1% accuracy, 94.9% sensitivity, 99.5% specificity, 99.5% NPV, and 95.5% PPV. Stochastic Gradient Boosting (0.998 AUC, 99.1% accuracy, 94.9% sensitivity, 99.6% specificity, 99.5% NPV, and 95.8% PPV) and Decision Trees (0.996 AUC, 97.6% accuracy, 95.2% sensitivity, 97.9% specificity, 99.5% NPV, and 82.2% PPV) also performed well. The models’ performance is comparable to the results of a study that predicted HCC in hepatitis B virus (HBV) patients, which achieved an AUC of 0.886 and an accuracy of 0.9809.

The confusion matrix for the Random Forest model (Fig. 2a) showed that nearly all patients without HCC were correctly classified, with only a single false-positive case, and the majority of HCC patients were correctly identified, with very few false negatives. The LightGBM confusion matrix (Fig. 2b) revealed a similarly high true negative rate with only a small number of false positives, while demonstrating slightly higher sensitivity than Random Forest by correctly identifying more HCC cases at a small trade-off in specificity.

The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for both Random Forest and LightGBM (Fig. 2c further highlight their strong discriminatory power, with both models achieving an AUC of 0.99. The curves are positioned near the top-left corner of the plot, indicating high sensitivity and specificity across a range of decision thresholds. The SHAP value provides an interpretable measure of feature importance for both the RF and LightGBM models, revealing strong agreement with the results obtained from the RFE-CV procedure and other feature selection methods. In both models, DCP, AFP, and age consistently emerge as the top predictors for HCC detection. These findings align with previous RFE-CV results, which identified seven optimal predictors, AFP, DCP, age, alkaline phosphatase, AST, albumin, and platelet count. For the RF model (Fig. 3a), DCP had the highest SHAP value (+0.06), followed by AFP (+0.04) and age (+0.03), highlighting their substantial predictive contributions. Alkaline phosphatase, AST, albumin, and platelet count also ranked among the top features, confirming their relevance in earlier feature selection analyses. Similarly, in the LightGBM model (Fig. 3b), AFP was the most influential variable (+0.06), followed by age (+0.05) and DCP (+0.04). Although the order differs slightly from the RF model, platelet count, AST, and albumin again appear among the important predictors, reinforcing their clinical utility in assessing HCC risk. The consistency of these results across multiple methods strengthens confidence in the robustness of these predictors. Subsequently, we used the Random Forest model to identify stratified HCC cohorts based on risk factors (Table 5). RF reliably identified cohorts irrespective of risk factors with an accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity ranging from 0.89–1.00, 71–100%, and 85–100%, respectively. The models exhibited the best performance in populations with alcohol-related liver disease and dyslipidemia, while the model performed comparatively poorer in patients with metabolic dysfunction-related steatotic liver disease considering the sensitivity and PPV.

Discussion

The identification of predictive features is a key step in improving the outcomes for patients at risk of HCC through early detection, personalized care, and optimized use of healthcare resources. Through statistical analysis, we determined a significant association between the occurrence of HCC and those over 40, consistent with the observations of Lim et al.23 Following the results of Ornos et al.24, our results also indicated that chronic hepatitis B and presence of cirrhosis were associated with an increased risk of HCC development. We note that these results align with the current PCPG recommendations to start HCC screening for men over 40 with hepatitis B25.

Similar to the findings in the literature26,27, we identified significantly higher levels of AFP and DCP in HCC patients than in non-HCC patients. These are also the top predictors identified by the feature selection methods that we employed. Consistent with the literature26–29, each of these predictors has been associated with HCC detection or prognosis, with AFP recommended as a screening mechanism by the PCPG25.

The negative coefficient of platelet count in Lasso regression indicates its inverse relation with the risk for HCC. Lu et al. elucidate that thrombocytopenia, a condition in which a person has low blood platelet count, can serve as an effective surrogate indicator of cirrhosis and a valid marker for identifying individuals at high risk of developing HCC30. There are, however, conflicting results on the association between blood platelet count and HCC outcome. The relationship between blood platelet count and disease outcomes is still a contentious issue, as both thrombocytosis and thrombocytopenia have been recognized as risk factors in the development and prognosis of HCC31. Further research is necessary to explore this issue.

Furthermore, we note that the GALAD scoring system, for estimating the likelihood of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) in patients with chronic liver disease, also uses these variables. The GALAD score, a diagnostic model that combines gender, AFP, AFP-L3, age, and DCP, has been shown to have worse predictive performance in East Asian countries than in Western countries32, suggesting its limited performance for the Filipino population. Nevertheless, our findings support the recommendation of PCPG to use AFP as an HCC screening mechanism25. Alternatively, our results suggest that in addition to AFP, the five clinical variables (DCP, alkaline phosphatase, AST, albumin, and platelet count) could be additional biomarkers for more sensitive HCC screening.

In this study, RF and LGBM yielded the highest performance (AUC = 0.999) in distinguishing patients with HCC and without HCC. The application of RF and LGBM in evaluating high-dimensional clinical data has garnered significant attention in the scientific community33–35. For instance, Singal et al. conducted a comprehensive study comparing conventional regression models with the random forest algorithm for predicting the development of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)36. Their analysis revealed that RF outperformed the regression models, yielding a higher c-statistic of 0.64 compared to 0.61. Notably, the study identified aspartate-aminotransferase (AST) as the optimal predictor for HCC development, underscoring the clinical relevance of RF in identifying key prognostic factors. Similarly, Wu et al. evaluated machine learning algorithms for predicting fatty liver disease, and their findings demonstrated that RF achieved an AUROC of 0.9250 with an accuracy of 86.48% using 10-fold cross-validation37. Jiang et al. applied RF and LightGBM to transcriptome data for HCC progression classification. Using 118 samples spanning normal liver, precancerous lesions, and HCC, LightGBM achieved accuracy, precision, and recall of 0.96 (F1-score = 0.95), while RF achieved 0.83 across these metrics38.

Current diagnostic approaches for HCC include imaging techniques such as CT, MRI, and ultrasound, along with tissue biopsy. While tumor biopsy has a specificity and positive predictive value of 100%, its sensitivity ranges from 66 to 93%39. The primary drawback of biopsy is its invasive nature, with bleeding being the most significant risk40. Other HCC screening methods involve abdominal ultrasound and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) biomarker testing41. Ultrasound is favored over CT and MRI due to its lower cost and greater accessibility, though its sensitivity for early-stage HCC detection is approximately 45%41. When combined with AFP testing, detection sensitivity increases to about 63%41. CT, like ultrasound, is a fast and quantifiable imaging option. Contrast-enhanced CT offers high sensitivity (93%) and specificity (100%) for identifying liver metastases42, but it involves exposure to radiation. The methods presented in this study is a viable alternative for HCC detection.

The incidence of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is notably higher in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in regions such as East Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa4,43. Resource-constrained settings face a disproportionate burden of HCC-related morbidity and mortality due to limited access to early detection and treatment services44,45. For instance, GLOBOCAN 2020 reported an age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) for liver cancer of 10.8 in the Philippines, more than double that of the United States (4.7)46.

This study contributes to addressing that disparity by developing a high-performing diagnostic model that integrates clinically relevant variables with robust feature selection strategies. By narrowing down to a compact and practical set of predictors, the model offers a cost-effective and scalable solution for early HCC detection. Unlike imaging-based diagnostics, which despite their effectiveness are often inaccessible in rural and low-income settings due to cost and infrastructure requirements, our model relies solely on routine blood tests. This enables remote screening and facilitates broader clinical adoption, particularly in under-resourced healthcare environments.

Nonetheless, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The dataset was imbalanced, reflecting the real-world prevalence of HCC, which posed challenges for model training and may have influenced the model’s performance. Although resampling techniques such as oversampling and class weighting were explored, they did not yield notable improvements. The retrospective design of the study also introduces potential selection bias which may limit the generalizability of the findings. While cross-validation and multiple feature selection methods were employed to reduce the risk of overfitting, external validation using larger and more diverse datasets is necessary to ensure the model’s robustness and applicability across different clinical settings. In addition, the absence of imaging data, such as ultrasound, CT, or MRI, represents a limitation, as such modalities are routinely used in clinical HCC diagnosis and may offer complementary information when integrated with serologic and clinical variables. These limitations should be addressed in future works.

Prospective validation of the model can be done, incorporated in screening programs done for populations at high risk for HCC. The model’s performance can also be compared to other clinical scoring models that have more evidence such as the GALAD Model. We plan to explore its incorporation in computer aided diagnostic systems integrated into electronic medical records in primary care to aid primary care physicians in clinical decision-making thru detection of patients that need confirmatory tests for HCC diagnosis.

Methods

Study population and data collection

This retrospective study used clinical data of 731 patients collected from December 2019 to January 2023. The data was collected as part of the research project “Early Cancer Detection in the Liver of Filipinos with Chronic Active Hepatitis B using AI-Driven Integration of Clinical and Genomic Biomarkers (CANDLE) Study,” a longitudinal epidemiologic study whose objective is to establish the clinical and genomic biomarker profile of Filipinos at risk for HCC, from five outpatient clinics of major hospitals in the Philippines. Eligible patients were adults aged 18 years or older and Filipino by descent (from the patient to both grandparents). We excluded patients diagnosed with other forms of cancer (non-liver cancer) within the last five years and those related up to the second degree of consanguinity.

The study collected baseline serologies to evaluate seropositivity for hepatitis B and C. Baseline liver biochemistries (e.g. AST, ALT, Albumin), metabolic profile (e.g. Lipid profile, FBS etc.), and a complete blood count was taken for all patients. HCC was diagnosed via MRI or CT imaging findings of liver masses with characteristic arterial non-peripheral hyperenhancement or histology. These clinical and serologic variables were routinely available and most were selected due to their known associations with liver disease progression and HCC risk.

The Single Joint Research Ethics Board of the Philippine Department of Health approved the study. Each participant provided informed written consent, and the analysis was performed following the Philippine National Ethical Guidelines for Health and Health-related Research 2017 and the Philippine Data Privacy Act of 2012. All research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines/regulations and as indicated in the approved research protocol. All de-identified study data were entered into REDCap, a web-based electronic data-capture system.

Feature selection

It is crucial to select the most predictive features to eliminate redundancy, minimize overfitting or bias, and enhance the model’s accuracy. Dimensionality reduction and feature selection were performed using Information Gain, Pearson correlation, Random Forest feature selection, Recursive Feature Elimination based on Cross-Validation (RFE-CV), and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) algorithm. Our approach utilized the intersection of these three techniques, i.e., the final set of features used to optimize the detection models were all identified by the five feature selection routines, while the optimal number of features was determined via RFE-CV. This feature selection strategy mitigates the inherent algorithmic biases of individual feature selection methods.

Model development and training

To ensure a robust evaluation of the models, a randomized population stratification was employed, creating a training set that accounted for 80% (n = 584) of the data, and the remaining 20% (n = 147) formed the validation test set. Furthermore, this stratification procedure considered the positive-to-negative proportion of patients, ensuring a representative case distribution.

Seven machine learning classifiers were employed to develop predictive models for HCC detection: logistic regression, naïve Bayes, k-nearest neighbor, decision tree, random forest, gradient boosting, support vector classifier, and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM). These models represent a diverse set of learning paradigms, ranging from linear models to tree-based and ensemble methods.

Logistic Regression performs classification by modeling the probability of a binary outcome using a logistic (sigmoid) function. It estimates a set of linear coefficients that best separate the two classes. A decision threshold (typically 0.5) is then applied to the predicted probabilities to assign class labels.

K-Nearest Neighbor is a non-parametric algorithm that assigns a class label based on the majority vote of the k closest training instances in the feature space. Distance metrics such as Euclidean or Manhattan distance are used to determine similarity between data points.

Decision Tree classifiers use a tree structure where internal nodes represent decisions based on feature thresholds, and leaf nodes represent class labels. The tree is built by recursively splitting the data at each node to maximize class purity (e.g., using Gini impurity or information gain), until a stopping criterion is met.

Random Forest aggregates the predictions of an ensemble of decision trees, each trained on a bootstrapped sample of the data with random feature selection at each split. The final classification is determined by majority voting among the trees. This ensemble approach improves stability and reduces overfitting.

Gradient Boosting builds a strong classifier by sequentially adding weak learners, typically shallow decision trees, that correct the errors of the ensemble built so far. At each stage, a new tree is trained on the residuals (errors) of the previous model. The predictions are combined through a weighted sum to produce the final class label.

Support Vector Classifier constructs an optimal hyperplane that maximizes the margin between classes in a high-dimensional space. For non-linearly separable data, kernel functions (e.g., radial basis function) are used to transform the input space. Classification is based on which side of the hyperplane a data point lies.

LightGBM is a gradient boosting framework that builds decision trees in a leaf-wise manner, selecting splits that lead to the largest reduction in loss. It uses histogram-based algorithms to speed up training and reduce memory usage. Like other boosting models, LightGBM combines the outputs of multiple weak learners to make a final classification decision.

All models were implemented in Python using the Scikit-learn package, with LightGBM trained using their dedicated libraries. Hyperparameter optimization was conducted via grid search, which systematically evaluated a predefined set of parameter combinations for each classifier. The list of optimized parameters is in the Supplementary Material. The hyperparameter tuning process was nested within a repeated stratified 10-fold cross-validation framework, where the training data were split into 10 stratified folds and the procedure repeated multiple times with different random seeds. This approach ensures that each observation is used for both training and validation across iterations while maintaining the class distribution, thereby providing a more stable and less biased estimate of model performance.

The best-performing hyperparameter combination for each model was selected based on the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). After tuning, the optimized models were retrained on the entire training set and evaluated on the independent validation set to assess final performance. Statistical analysis and performance calculations

Statistical analysis was conducted using STATA v15 https://www.stata.com/stata15 using a two-tailed p-value threshold of less than 0.05. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov normality test was employed to assess the normality of the data. Quantitative variables were reported with appropriate measures based on their distribution: normally distributed data were summarized using mean and standard deviation, while non-normally distributed data were described using the median and interquartile range. The student’s t-test and the Mann-Whitney U-test were utilized to compare the quantitative variables, depending on the distributional characteristics of the data. Receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to evaluate model performance to detect HCC.
