Cognitive Surgery: The Awakening of Implicit Territorial Awareness in LLMs
Yinghan Zhou, Weifeng Zhu, Juan Wen, Wanli Peng, Zhengxian Wu, Yiming Xue

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) have been shown to possess a degree of self-recognition capability—the ability to identify whether a given text was generated by themselves. Prior work has demonstrated that this capability is reliably expressed under the Pair Presentation Paradigm (PPP), where the model is presented with two texts and asked to choose which one it authored. However, performance deteriorates sharply under the Individual Presentation Paradigm (IPP), where the model is given a single text to judge authorship. Although this phenomenon has been observed, its underlying causes have not been systematically analyzed. In this paper, we first replicate existing findings to confirm that LLMs struggle to distinguish self- from other-generated text under IPP. We then investigate the reasons for this failure and attribute it to a phenomenon we term Implicit Territorial Awareness (ITA)—the model’s latent ability to distinguish self- and other-texts in representational space, which remains unexpressed in its output behavior. To awaken the ITA of LLMs, we propose Cognitive Surgery (CoSur), a novel framework comprising four main modules: representation extraction, territory construction, authorship discrimination and cognitive editing. Experimental results demonstrate that our proposed method improves the performance of three different LLMs in the IPP scenario, achieving average accuracies of 83.25%, 66.19%, and 88.01%, respectively. code — https://github.com/CAU-ISS-Lab/Interpretability-of-LLMs/tree/main/CoSur

Introduction

It has recently been found that large language models (LLMs) possess self-recognition capability, enabling them to distinguish their own writing (”self text”) from that of humans and other models (”other text”)
(
Panickssery, Bowman, and Feng 2024
;
Ackerman and Panickssery 2025
)
.
As shown in Figure
1
, this capability is particularly evident in the Pair Presentation Paradigm (PPP).
In this paradigm, the model is shown two texts, one of which has been generated by the model being tested and the other of which has been generated by a human or another model.
Then, the model is asked to output the label of the text it thinks it wrote.
However, this self-recognition capability encounters a bottleneck when faced with the Individual Presentation Paradigm (IPP), where the LLM is shown a single text and asked to indicate whether the text was generated by itself. In the IPP scenario, accuracy is computed by capturing the probabilities assigned to the ”Yes” and ”No” tokens, which serves as the primary evaluation metric.
As reported in Table 1 of
Ackerman and Panickssery 2025
, the base LLM achieves prediction accuracies below 50.3% across four datasets, indicating worse-than-random performance. Despite these observations, prior studies have not conducted an in-depth analysis of this failure mode.

Ackerman and Panickssery 2025
attempted to address this by extracting a self-recognition vector.
They demonstrated that steering the model with this vector in the positive direction makes it claim authorship, while steering it in the negative direction makes it deny authorship, regardless of true authorship.
This approach essentially does not enhance the self-recognition capability of LLMs.
This raises an interesting question:

Why do LLMs, despite possessing self-recognition capability in paired settings, fail in the IPP scenario?

In this paper, we first empirically verify the existence of self-recognition capability in LLMs.
Then, we analyze the reasons behind the failure of this capability in the IPP scenario, which is regarded as
Implicit Territorial Awareness (ITA)
of LLMs.
The ITA refers to the awareness of the LLM to clearly distinguish its own text from that authored by others in the feature space, yet fail to reflect this distinction in its responses.

Figure 1:
The prompt diagrams for the pair presentation paradigm and individual presentation paradigm.

Building on this, we further raise a new question:
Is it possible to enhance the performance of LLMs in the IPP scenario by awakening ITA?

To explore this, we conduct an in-depth analysis of the feature distributions across different categories of texts.
Interestingly, we find that although these feature distributions are very similar, the internal structure of these features differs significantly across sources.
This observation suggests that it is possible to identify and extract subspaces where the features exhibit greater differences.
Inspired by representation editing
(
Kong et al. 2024a
;
Wang et al. 2024a
)
, the model’s output can be aligned with the desired attributes by directly manipulating its latent representations.
Based on this, we propose a novel method named Cognitive Surgery (CoSur) to awaken ITA, thereby enhancing the self-recoginition capability of LLMs in IPP scenarios.
The CoSur consists of four modules: representation extraction, territory construction, authorship discrimination and cognitive editing.
Specifically, we first extract the feature representation from the final layer of the LLM to construct the feature subspaces (territories) for each text category using singular value decomposition (SVD).
Then, we guide the LLM to determine the authorship of a text by calculating the projection energy of its feature vector onto different territorial regions.
Finally, we design the cognitive editing to induce the LLM to generate the correct response.
Extensive experiments on three LLMs demonstrate that CoSur effectively enhances their self-recognition capability in the IPP scenario.
Our contributions are as follows:

•

We provide the first in-depth analysis of why LLMs fail in the IPP setting, attributing it to unexpressed Implicit Territorial Awareness (ITA).

•

We propose Cognitive Surgery (CoSur), a method that awakens ITA to enhance the self-recognition capability of LLMs in the IPP scenario. We first identify and construct territorial spaces to assist the LLM in determining the authorship of the text. Then, we design cognitive editing to induce the LLM in generating correct responses.

•

Experiments demonstrate that our proposed method enhances the performance across three different LLMs in the IPP scenario, achieving average accuracy of 83.25%, 66.19%, and 88.01%, respectively.

Related Work

Self-Recognition Capability of LLMs

The self-recognition capability of LLM refers to their capacity to identify texts they have generated
(
Laine et al. 2024b
;
Laine, Meinke, and Evans 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024b
;
Cotra 2021
)
.
Panickssery et al.
(
Panickssery, Bowman, and Feng 2024
)
reported that several LLMs, including Llama2-7b-chat, demonstrated out-of-the-box (without fine-tuning) self-recognition capabilities using a summary writing and recognition task.
Laine et al.
(
Laine et al. 2024a
)
used more challenging text continuation and recognition tasks to demonstrate self-recognition abilities in larger language models. It highlighted how task success could be elicited with different prompts and across different models.
Ackerman et al.
(
Ackerman and Panickssery 2025
)
found that the llama3-8b-Instruct succeeded at self-recognition across diverse tasks, whereas the base model performed poorly, especially in the IPP scenario. They also extracted a ”self-recognition” vector in the residual stream, allowing users to steer the LLM to claim or disclaim authorship during generation and to believe or disbelieve that it had written arbitrary texts when reading them.
These studies demonstrated that LLMs possess self-recognition capability.

Representation Editing

Representation editing is a class of techniques that directly manipulate the latent representations of a model to improve its performance and align it with desired attributes
(
Kong et al. 2024b
;
Wu et al. 2024a
)
.
Liang et al.
(
Liang et al. 2024
)
found that representation editing could control aspects of text generation, such as safety, sentiment, thematic consistency, and linguistic style.
Adila et al.
(
Adila et al. 2024
)
used embedding editing for general, rather than personalized, alignment to broad human preferences, relying on self-generated synthetic data.
Wu et al.
(
Wu et al. 2024b
)
showed that the representation editing can even surpass fine-tuning
based methods by intervening on hidden representations within the linear subspace defined by a
low-rank projection matrix.
Ma et al.
(
Ma et al. 2025
)
leveraged the over-parameterized nature of LLMs to disentangle a style-relevant subspace within the model’s representation space to conduct representation editing, ensuring a minimal impact on the original semantics. By applying adaptive editing strengths, they dynamically adjusted the steering vectors in the style subspace to maintain both stylistic fidelity and semantic integrity.
Inspired by representation editing, as long as the true authorship of the text is determined, we can directly manipulate the LLM’s hidden representations to generate the correct response.
Based on this, we propose CoSur, the details of which will be introduced in the section
4
.

The Implicit Territorial Awareness of LLMs

We believe the LLM demonstrates self-recognition abilities because it exhibits Territorial Awareness (TA)
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
, i.e., when responding to authorship attribution questions, the LLM tends to emphasize its own characteristics while reducing its sensitivity to differences between texts generated by others.
However, in the IPP scenario, when the LLM is asked about the authorship of a single text, this territorial awareness is not reflected in its response.
Therefore, we regard this phenomenon as Implicit Territorial Awareness (ITA).

We argue that the diminished TA in the IPP scenario stems from information loss during the mapping from the feature space to the discrete vocabulary space in the LLM.
Formally, let
\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the hidden state vector from the last layer of the LLM. The probability distribution
\mathbf{P}
over the vocabulary is obtained via a linear transformation followed by a softmax function:

\mathbf{P}=softmax(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{h}+\mathbf{b}),

(1)

where
softmax(\cdot)
represents the softmax function,
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V|\times d}
is the output projection matrix,
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V|}
is the bias term, and
|V|
is the vocabulary size.
This projection maps the high-dimensional semantic space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to the
(|V|-1)
-dimensional probability simplex
\Delta^{|V|-1}
.
However, due to the non-invertibility of the softmax function and the limited rank of
\mathbf{W}
, this transformation inevitably introduces information loss.
Additionally, we analyze the mutual information
I(\mathbf{h};y)
between the hidden state
\mathbf{h}
and the C
y
and the mutual information
I(\mathbf{P};y)
between the probability distribution of output tokens
\mathbf{P}
and
y
.
Due to the low-rank projection and entropy-reduction effects of the softmax layer, the two mutual information terms satisfy
I(\mathbf{P};y)\ll I(\mathbf{h};y)
.
This prevents the LLM from exhibiting explicit TA in its outputs, resulting in ITA.
Our findings align with the information bottleneck theory
(
Tishby, Pereira, and Bialek 2000
)
, where
\mathbf{P}
acts as a compressed representation that inadequately preserves task-relevant information about the correct output token
y
.

We empirically validate the above findings through an in-depth analysis of the features from different categories of texts.
Specifically, We collect 400 texts each from three categories: (1) Qwen-generated texts, (2) Human-written texts, (3) Chatgpt-generated text.
These texts are then fed into Qwen3-8B (Qwen)
(
QwenTeam 2025
)
, and we extract the feature representations from each layer for further analysis.

Firstly, we map these feature representations to the vocabulary space to obtain output probability distributions, from which we calculate the Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence
(
Menéndez et al. 1997
)
between the different distributions.
As shown in Figure
2
(a) and
2
(c), a statistically significant separation exists between the distributions of different text categories.
Furthermore, the T-sne visualization in Figure
3
(a) and
3
(b) demonstrate that the resulting clusters exhibit low overlap and clear separation, suggesting that the LLM demonstrates a distinct awareness in differentiating its own text from others.
Additionally, as shown in Figure
2
(e) and
3
(c), we observe that the LLM can also distinguish between different categories of others-generated texts.

Secondly, we extract text features in the IPP scenario to validate the LLM’s territorial awareness.
As shown in the bottom row of Figure
3
, when the LLM is prompted under the IPP scenario, the boundary between self-generated and other generated texts becomes more distinct.
In contrast, the boundary between human-texts and chatgpt-texts becomes blurred.
This shift in representational distinctiveness indicates that the LLM possesses territorial awareness.

which aligns with our previous analysis.

Based on the above analysis, we argue that actively mitigating information loss during the mapping from the feature space to the discrete vocabulary space can improve the LLM’s self-recognition capability in the IPP scenario.
Therefore, we propose CoSur, the details of which will be introduced in the following sections.

4
CoSur

To activate ITA of LLM in IPP scenario, we propose CoSur, which consists of four modules: representation extraction, territory construction, authorship discrimination, and cognitive editing.
The framework of CoSur is shown in Figure
4
.

Representation Extraction

Let
\mathbf{T_{s}}=\{\mathbf{t}_{s,1},\mathbf{t}_{s,2},\ldots,\mathbf{t}_{s,N}\}
denotes a set of texts generated by the LLM itself, and
\mathbf{T_{o}}=\{\mathbf{t}_{o,1},\mathbf{t}_{o,2},\ldots,\mathbf{t}_{o,N}\}
represents a set of texts from other source.
Under the IPP scenario, each text
\mathbf{t}_{s,i}\in\mathbf{T_{s}}
and
\mathbf{t}_{o,i}\in\mathbf{T_{o}}
is independently fed into the LLM to extract the hidden representation of its last token from the final layer of the LLM, denoted as
\mathbf{h_{s,i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbf{h_{o,i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, respectively:

\mathbf{h}_{s,i}=LLM(\mathbf{t}_{s,i}),\quad\mathbf{h}_{o,i}=LLM(\mathbf{t}_{o,i})

(2)

The representations for each category are stacked to form the sets

\mathbf{H_{s}}=\{\mathbf{h}_{s,1},\mathbf{h}_{s,2},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{s,N}\}

and

\mathbf{H_{o}}=\{\mathbf{h}_{o,1},\mathbf{h}_{o,2},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{o,N}\}
, respectively.

Territory Construction

We believe that due to the ITA of LLMs, there are differences between self-generated texts and other-generated texts in the LLM’s feature space.
Therefore, we select 400 samples from each category and analyze the differences in feature distributions, extracted from Qwen, between different categories of texts using Cosine Similarity (CS), Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD), and Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA).
The definitions of these metrics and their measurement results on other LLMs are provided in Appendix B.

As shown in Table

, these features exhibit consistently high CS and low MMD, indicating that they are highly similar in feature space.
This also explains why the LLM performs poorly on IPP scenario.
However, the CKA scores between these representation sets, which evaluate the structural similarity in the feature space by calculating the similarity between the Gram matrices of two feature matrices, are notably low.
This reveals a different perspective: although the features are close in space, the internal structure of the representations differs significantly across sources.
This observation suggests that it is possible to identify and extract subspaces where the representations exhibit greater differences, thereby enhancing the distinction between text categories.

Based on this observation, we apply singular value decomposition (SVD) to extract the most discriminative components between categories.

\mathbf{H_{s}}=\mathbf{U_{s}}\mathbf{\Sigma_{s}}\mathbf{V_{s}}^{\top},\quad\mathbf{H_{o}}=\mathbf{U_{o}}\mathbf{\Sigma_{o}}\mathbf{V_{o}}^{\top}

(3)

where
\mathbf{U_{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
and
\mathbf{U_{o}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
are the left singular matrices,
\mathbf{\Sigma_{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma_{o}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
are diagonal matrices containing the singular values and
\mathbf{V_{s}^{\top}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\mathbf{V_{o}^{\top}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are the right singular matrices.

We extract the top-
k
right singular vectors from
\mathbf{V_{s}^{\top}}
and
\mathbf{V_{o}^{\top}}
to serve as the bases defining their respective territories
\mathbf{\mathcal{V}_{s}}
and
\mathbf{\mathcal{V}_{o}}
.

\mathbf{\mathcal{V}_{s}}=span(\mathbf{V_{s}}^{(k)}),\quad\mathbf{\mathcal{V}_{o}}=span(\mathbf{V_{o}}^{(k)})

(4)

where
span(\cdot)
represents the set of all possible linear combinations of the vectors contained in the input set, forming a vector space.
\mathbf{V_{s}}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
and
\mathbf{V_{o}}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
denote the matrices consisting of the top-
k
right singular vectors.
We conduct experiments to examine the impact of the choice of
k
on the results, as detailed in section
5
.

Authorship Discrimination

We introduce projection energy
E
to quantify the intensity of text representations projected onto each territory, which serves to determine the authorship of a given text. For a given sample

t
, the last token feature vector
h
from the final layer is extracted and its projection energy
E_{s}
and
E_{o}
onto
\mathbf{V_{s}}
and
\mathbf{V_{o}}
are computed to infer the authorship of
t
.

E_{s}=\|\mathbf{V_{s}}^{\top}\mathbf{h}\|_{2},\quad E_{o}=\|\mathbf{V_{o}}^{\top}\mathbf{h}\|_{2}

(5)

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes the Euclidean norm.

Finally, the authorship of the text
t
is determined by comparing its projection energies
E_{s}
and
E_{o}
onto the respective territories.

O(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}s,&if\quad E_{s}>E_{o}\quad,\\
o,&otherwise\end{array}\right.

(6)

where
O(t)
represents the authorship of
t
.

Cognitive Editing

To guide the LLM toward producing the desired response, we first identify the target tokens
tok_{s}
and
tok_{o}
.
The output-layer weight vectors corresponding to the two target tokens, denoted as
\mathbf{w}_{s}
and
\mathbf{w}_{o}
, respectively, are obtained from the LLM.
We then normalize the two weight vectors as follows:

\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{o}=\frac{\mathbf{w}_{o}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{o}\|},\quad\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{s}=\frac{\mathbf{w}_{s}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{s}\|}

(7)

The target direction
\mathbf{d_{target}}
is determined according to the value of
O(t)
:

\mathbf{d_{target}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{o},&if\quad O(t)=o\quad,\\
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{s},&if\quad O(t)=s\end{array}\right.

(8)

The hidden representation
\mathbf{h}
is steered toward the target direction to obtain the edited representation
\mathbf{\tilde{h}}
, thereby facilitating the LLM to output the target token.

\mathbf{\tilde{h}}=\mathbf{h}+\alpha\cdot\mathbf{d_{target}}

(9)

where
\alpha
represents editing strength hyperparameter.
We also conduct experiments to examine the impact of the choice of
\alpha
on the results, as detailed in section
5
.
The complete algorithmic procedure is detailed in the Appendix D.

5
Experiments

Dataset

We incorpate HC3
(
Guo et al. 2023
)
, a large-scale comparison dataset comprising responses from both human experts and chatgpt across diverse domains, including open-domain, finance, medicine, law, and psychology.
Additionally, we use the same set of questions as prompts to generate responses from Qwen, Llama and DeepSeek.

Experimental Setting

We evaluate the performance of CoSur on three different LLMs, including Qwen3-8B (Qwen)
(
QwenTeam 2025
)
, Llama-3.1-8B (Llama)
(
AI@Meta 2024
)
, and DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B (Deepseek)
(
DeepSeek-AI 2025
)
.
We select the top-64 right-singular vectors to construct the subspace (
k=64
) and set the editing strength
\alpha=100
.
All experiments are run on two NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB GPUs.
Additioanally, we use stricter evaluation metrics, where accuracy (ACC) and F1 score (F1) are computed based solely on the output of LLMs, rather than the probability of the target token.
Since current research lacks targeted methods to enhance the self-recognition capability of LLMs, in this experiment, we use the LLM’s direct performance in the IPP scenario (base) as the baseline to evaluate the performance of CoSur.

Main Results

We evaluate the effectiveness of CoSur on three different mainstream LLMs. As shown in Table
3
, all three LLMs perform below 50% in the base setting (base) without CoSur on the IPP scenario. After applying CoSur, each LLM demonstrates a significant improvement. Specifically, across four tasks, the three LLMs achieve average accuracies of 83.25%, 66.19%, and 88.01%, with corresponding improvements of 43.85%, 17.51%, and 39.22%.
These results suggest that CoSur is effective in mitigating the implicit territorial awareness exhibited by LLMs.
However, its improvements are less pronounced on Llama compared to Qwen and DeepSeek.
As shown in Appendices A and C, the feature space of LLaMA exhibits inherently lower inter-class separability, indicating a limited capacity for native self-recognition.
This constraint reduces the effectiveness of subsequent editing operations.
We present a case study comparing the top-10 tokens and their logit changes before and after editing, with detailed results provided in the Appendix E.

We evaluate the generalization of CoSur through the scenario below:
Given the territories of its own and chatgpt texts, assess the LLM’s ability to distinguish between its own text and that of other LLMs.

Ablation Study

The key to CoSur’s performance lies in the construction of the territory and the authorship discrimination based on projection energy. To evaluate the effectiveness of CoSur, we design two variants:

(1) CS-based Authorship Identification:
For a given test sample, its authorship is determined by computing the cosine similarity between the sample and each class center.

(2) PCA-based Territory Construction:
Territories are constructed using Principal Component Analysis (PCA), and authorship is determined based on the projection energy onto the respective territories.
As shown in Table
4
, CoSur outperforms the other two variants.
We attribute the relatively poor performance of the cosine similarity-based variant to the inability of the feature mean to capture the critical characteristics that distinguish different texts.
Additionally, we argue that while PCA focuses on maximizing variance when selecting principal components, SVD directly leverages the magnitude of singular values to capture the most important features of the data.
Therefore, using SVD to construct the territory is more effective in capturing the core structure and distinguishing features.

We explore the impact of territorial dimensions
k
on the self-recognition capability of LLMs in the IPP scenario using Qwen.
A small
k
fails to capture all the distinguishing features, especially for tasks like self vs ChatGPT and self vs human.
Conversely, most categories show accuracy declines after feature saturation due to the introduction of irrelevant features.
Overall, Qwen achieve best average performs across the four different tasks when
k=64
. Therefore, this value is adopted in our experiments.
We also investigate the effect of editing strength
\alpha
on CoSur using Qwen.
As demonstrated in Figure
7
, when the editing strength exceeds 100, the average recognition accuracy across the four different text combinations in IPP scenarios remains nearly unchanged.
This suggests this threshold provides sufficient guidance for the LLM to generate target-aligned responses.
We further investigated the performance of CoSur on a related task, AI-generated text detection, as detailed in Appendix F.

Conclusion

In Conclusion, we empirically verify the existence of the self-recognition capability of LLMs and analyze the reasons behind the failure of this capability in the IPP scenario, which is regarded as the Implicit Territorial Awareness (ITA) of LLMs.
Base on this, we propose a novel method named Cognitive Surgery (CoSur) to awaken the ITA in LLMs, thereby enhancing LLMs’ performance in the IPP scenario.
Experimental results demonstrate that CoSur significantly enhances LLMs’ performance in IPP scenario.
