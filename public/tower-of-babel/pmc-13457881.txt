Deep learning algorithm for automatic detection of acute ischemic stroke on noncontrast brain CT
Authors: Tae Jin Yun, Jin Wook Choi, Hoon Na, Miran Han, Woo Sang Jung, Ji Ye Lee, Roh-Eul Yoo, In Pyeong Hwang, Seung Hong Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of a deep learning-based algorithm for detecting acute ischemic stroke (AIS), including small infarcts, on non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT). A retrospective, multi-reader, pivotal, crossover, randomized study involving 917 cases was conducted to validate the performance of an artificial intelligence (AI) algorithm. NCCT images were independently reviewed by nine readers categorized into three subgroups (three non-radiologist physicians, three board-certified radiologists, and three neuroradiologists), both with and without the assistance of the AI algorithm. In standalone analysis, the AI model achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.8144, with an accuracy of 73.8%, sensitivity of 75.8%, and specificity of 72.6%. AI-assisted brain CT interpretation showed higher diagnostic accuracy than AI-unassisted interpretation (75.63% vs. 72.03%, with improvement of 3.60%, p < 0.001). Among the three subgroups of reviewers, non-radiologist physicians showed the greatest improvement in diagnostic accuracy with AI assistance compared with without AI assistance (75.35% vs. 69.97%, with improvement of 5.38%, p < 0.001). Our results suggested that AI-assisted interpretation can improve diagnostic performance in the detection of AIS on NCCT across a range of infarct volumes and reader expertise levels. Its integration into clinical workflows could facilitate more timely and equitable AIS diagnosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55530-9.

Introduction

Over the past decade, multiple multicenter randomized controlled trials have demonstrated the clinical efficacy of endovascular thrombectomy in treating acute ischemic stroke (AIS), which has led to greater reliance on neuroimaging—including computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)—for diagnosis and therapeutic planning1–8. Although angiography and perfusion imaging using CT or MRI offer detailed insight into infarct core, penumbra, large vessel occlusion (LVO), and collateral circulation, non-contrast CT (NCCT) remains the most widely used initial diagnostic modality due to its rapid availability, low cost, and high patient accessibility9.

NCCT is especially critical in emergency settings as it allows prompt exclusion of intracranial hemorrhage and can reveal early signs of ischemia. However, the interpretation of NCCT in cases of AIS—especially when infarcts are small or subtle—can be challenging, even for experienced readers. This diagnostic complexity is compounded in settings where expert neuroradiologists may not be readily available.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning techniques, have enabled the development of tools that assist in image interpretation by identifying disease patterns from large datasets10,11. Numerous AI models have been developed for AIS detection, particularly for LVO-related strokes, showing high performance in identifying vessel occlusion and related ischemic changes12–21. Despite this progress, existing studies have largely focused on AIS related to LVO, while limited effort has been devoted to the development and validation of AI models capable of detecting AIS due to other etiologies, such as small vessel occlusion (SVO), cardioembolism, or cryptogenic causes.

The clinical importance of detecting small AIS is substantial but often underappreciated. Although strokes associated with LVO often result in more severe neurological deficits, strokes caused by SVO or other non-LVO etiologies are more common overall13–17. Furthermore, small infarcts are frequently underdiagnosed due to their radiologic similarity to chronic white matter changes, such as leukoaraiosis or lacunes, especially in elderly patients. Timely and accurate detection of small AIS is essential for determining treatment strategies, including the initiation of thrombolytic therapy within the therapeutic window. Importantly, in emergency or resource-limited settings, the ability to detect small infarcts early—even if not directly treatable with thrombectomy—can inform patient triage, imaging follow-up, and admission decisions.

In this study, we developed a deep learning-based algorithm combining convolutional and recurrent neural networks (CNN-RNN) to detect AIS lesions, including small infarcts, on NCCT. We validated the algorithm using an external multicenter dataset and conducted a reader study to evaluate the algorithm’s impact on diagnostic accuracy across clinicians with different levels of radiologic expertise.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed as a retrospective, multicenter, randomized, crossover diagnostic evaluation of an AI-based algorithm for the detection of acute ischemic stroke (AIS) on NCCT. The study was conducted across two academic medical centers—Seoul National University Hospital (Institution A) and Ajou University Medical Center (Institution B). The reader assessment component involved physicians with different levels of radiologic expertise and was structured into two sequential sessions separated by a washout period.

Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was obtained from both participating institutions (H-2212-076-1385 for Institution A and AJOUIRB-DE-2023-028 for Institution B). The study was registered in the Clinical Research Information Service of Korea (CRiS; Identifier: KCT0008553). Informed consent was waived for all participants due to the retrospective design and minimal risk nature of the study.

The study followed a crossover design in which readers interpreted original CT images with and without AI assistance in two separate sessions. Randomization determined the order in which AI-assisted and unassisted images were presented. Each reader was blinded to the decisions of the gold-standard neuroradiologist panel as well as to the prevalence of AIS in the datasets.

Development dataset and external validation dataset

We developed a deep learning-based AI algorithm for automatic AIS detection using brain CT scans. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of each participating institution (H-2106-175-1230, Seoul National University Hospital Institutional Review Board [Institution A] and AJOUIRB-MDB-2021-294, Ajou University Medical Center Institutional Review Board [Institution B]).

The algorithm was trained using 71,534 CT slices from 1,972 patients, consisting of 882 with AIS (44.7%) and 1,090 stroke-negative controls (55.3%), collected from two institutions between 2011 and 2017 (Institution A) and 2020–2021 (Institution B). All enrolled patients — both AIS cases and controls — presented with symptoms suggestive of acute stroke and underwent both NCCT and paired diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) mapping within 6 h of CT acquisition. AIS was confirmed by the presence of diffusion restriction on DWI/ADC, while controls were patients whose DWI/ADC showed no acute infarct (i.e., clinical stroke mimics). Among the 882 AIS cases, 593 (67.2%) had infarcts ≤ 5 mL, 83 (9.4%) between 5 and 10 mL, 146 (16.6%) between 10 and 50 mL, and 60 (6.8%) > 50 mL, reflecting a cohort intentionally enriched for small-volume infarcts to align with the study’s primary objective of optimizing detection of subtle lesions.

For external validation of the diagnostic performance, CT images from 1,400 patients, including 700 with AIS (50.0%) and 700 controls (50.0%), were obtained from four independent institutions (Yonsei University Severance Hospital, Korea University Anam Hospital, Seoul Metropolitan Government-Seoul National University Boramae Medical Center, and Soonchunhyang University Bucheon Hospital), which are distinct from the development sites (Seoul National University Hospital and Ajou University Medical Center). This study for external validation was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of each institution (following approval by Seoul National University Hospital Institutional Review Board, H-2401-136-1505).

To ensure a representative study population and minimize selection bias, all patients who presented with symptoms suggestive of AIS and underwent both NCCT and DWI/ADC within 6 h of CT acquisition were enrolled consecutively at each institution. From this initial pool, we randomly selected 175 AIS-positive cases and 175 AIS-negative controls per institution to construct a balanced dataset. AIS-positive cases were defined by the presence of diffusion restriction on DWI/ADC, while AIS-negative controls were patients who presented with stroke-like symptoms but had no evidence of acute infarct on subsequent DWI/ADC (i.e., clinical stroke mimics). For all subjects, including controls, the onset time was defined as the moment when stroke-like symptoms were first observed, as this represented the clinical trigger for emergency neuroimaging in both groups.

Details regarding patient information and equipment are provided in Appendix Tables E1 and E2.

Reviewer assessment study dataset

For the reader assessment study, an independent dataset was collected from two institutions (Seoul National University Hospital [Institution A] and Ajou University Medical Center [Institution B]), temporally distinct from the development dataset.

No patients included in the development dataset were enrolled in the reader assessment study dataset. All patients who presented with symptoms suggestive of acute ischemic stroke (AIS) and underwent both NCCT and DWI/ADC within 6 h—a prespecified interval to minimize the temporal mismatch between the index NCCT and the DWI/ADC based reference standard—were enrolled consecutively. From this initial pool, we randomly selected AIS-positive cases and AIS-negative controls. As in the external validation cohort, AIS-positive cases were defined by diffusion restriction on DWI/ADC, while AIS-negative controls were patients with stroke-like symptoms but no acute infarct on DWI/ADC (clinical stroke mimics). For all subjects, including controls, the onset time was defined as the moment when stroke-like symptoms were first observed.

This dataset included CT images of 917 individuals, consisting of 367 AIS-positive cases (40.0%) and 550 normal controls (60.0%). All 917 images met predefined image quality criteria, adapted from established standards22,23.

A total of 367 CT images exhibited AIS (consisting of small AIS with less than or equal to 5 mL in more than half of total CT images). Distribution of the AIS volume was as follows: AIS volume ≤ 5 mL in 187 (51.0%) CT images; 5 mL < AIS volume ≤ 10 mL in 34 (9.3%) CT images; 10 mL < AIS volume ≤ 50 mL in 96 (26.2%) CT images; and AIS volume > 50 mL in 50 (13.6%) CT images.

Volume information was calculated based on DWI annotations. Further dataset information is available in the Appendix Document. A summary of reader demographics is shown in Table 1.

Development of the AI algorithm

The AI algorithm was developed using a hybrid architecture combining CNN and RNN. A total of 71,534 slices from 882 AIS patients and 1,090 normal controls were annotated by board-certified neuroradiologists using nordicICE software version 4.1.2 (NordicNeuroLab, Bergen, Norway). Ground truth was based on DWI, referenced by ADC maps, acquired within 6 h of the initial CT scan, coregistered to the CT space.

To generate high-quality ground truth annotations, DWI and ADC images obtained within 6 h after NCCT acquisition were coregistered to CT space using affine registration via Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs). CT preprocessing included gantry tilt correction, skull stripping, and reorientation. Coregistration quality was visually inspected in axial, coronal, and sagittal planes, and only cases with < 3 mm misalignment were retained.

The AI model combined a CNN for per-slice feature extraction and a two-layer RNN to capture inter-slice spatial continuity. Each CT slice was first analyzed independently to predict local infarct probability. Subsequently, the sequence of slice-level features was processed through the RNN, generating a patient-level probability for AIS presence. In addition to classification, a patch-based segmentation module localized suspected infarct areas, creating probability heatmaps to assist visual interpretation. The model output included infarct probability score and infarct localization overlays, formatted for integration into picture archiving and communication system (PACS).

To align our architectural design with the clinical objective of enhancing the detection of small infarcts (≤ 5 mL), our methodology incorporates specific image processing and structural optimizations. We utilized high-resolution input data and a precision co-registration process, which are essential for preserving the spatial integrity of minute ischemic features. These features are then processed through the hybrid CNN-RNN pipeline, ensuring that the model maintains high sensitivity for subtle, small-volume lesions throughout the feature extraction and classification stages.

The AI algorithm used in this study, designated as SKH-BCI-001, was developed by SK Inc. C&C for academic research purposes at the time of the clinical trial and has since been commercialized as ‘Medical Insight+ Brain Infarct.’ The model was designed as an imaging-only algorithm in accordance with the prespecified clinical trial protocol (CRiS Identifier: KCT0008553), which evaluated the diagnostic performance of NCCT-based AI without integration of clinical metadata.

Reviewer assessment study

A retrospective, randomized, crossover reader study was conducted to evaluate the clinical utility of the AI model. Nine physicians from three different subgroups with equal numbers (i.e., three non-radiologist physicians with 5–7 years of experience in that role, three board-certified radiologists with 5–7 years of experience in that role, and three subspecialty-trained neuroradiologists with 7–11 years of experience as radiologists, including 3–7 years of experience as neuroradiologists) participated as reviewers. Each reader interpreted all 917 CT cases twice (once with and once without AI assistance), separated by a 4–5 weeks washout period to minimize recall bias. Cases were randomized into two sets, and the sequence of AI-assisted vs. unassisted sessions was counterbalanced across readers. Within each session, the presentation order of the 917 cases was independently randomized for each reader to minimize potential recall or order bias.

Prior to the first assessment, the full CT dataset was split into groups A and B, each comprising CT images from 459 to 458 patients, respectively. Numbers for sequential assessment were randomly assigned. Group A consisted of original CT images and corresponding AI-assisted CT images, while group B consisted of only original CT images without AI-assisted CT images. During AI-assisted sessions, readers were explicitly aware of the assistance as the algorithm provided probability scores and spatial heatmaps directly on the PACS viewer. Each reviewer independently reviewed CT images for the detection of AIS. All nine readers were strictly blinded to clinical metadata, including patient age, sex, symptoms, and National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scores, to evaluate the standalone diagnostic value of the imaging. Reviewers were blinded to decisions of the gold-standard review board with regard to AIS and proportion of AIS cases in the assessed dataset. To minimize potential recall bias, a washout period of 4–5 weeks was implemented between reading sessions, consistent with prior observer performance studies24,25, In the second assessment, the group A dataset comprising original and AI-assisted CT images during the first assessment was changed to include only original CT images without any AI-assisted CT images, whereas AI-assisted CT images were added to group B dataset that had previously included only original CT images without AI-assisted CT images. Numbers for sequential assessments were randomly re-assigned. Each reviewer repeated the same review process as used for the first assessment. Figure 2 provides an overview of the study design.

Statistical analysis

The standalone diagnostic performance of the AI algorithm was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUROC) on the internal and external validation datasets, with sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and F1 score reported at a prespecified probability threshold of 50%. Model calibration was assessed using the observed-to-expected ratio in the external validation dataset.

In the reader study, the diagnostic performance of AI-assisted versus AI-unassisted reads was compared using accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. While sensitivity served as the primary metric for evaluating detection capability within AIS-positive subgroups, accuracy and specificity were assessed across the entire study population to ensure a comprehensive performance evaluation.

Generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) with a logit link function were used for patient-wise analysis. AI assistance (assisted vs. unassisted) was modeled as a fixed effect, while patient and reader were included as random intercepts to account for patient-level clustering across paired reads of the same case and between-reader variability across the nine readers, respectively. For slice-wise analysis, generalized estimating equations (GEE) with an exchangeable working correlation structure were applied to account for the clustered nature of multi-slice data and repeated measures across readers. Inter-reader agreement was assessed using intra-class correlation coefficients with 95% confidence intervals. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), and a two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

AI performance on the internal validation dataset

On the internal validation dataset, the AI algorithm achieved an accuracy of 0.748, with a sensitivity of 0.762 and a specificity of 0.733. Additional metrics included a precision of 0.741, F1 score of 0.751, and a negative predictive value of 0.755.

AI performance on the external validation dataset

When tested on an external validation dataset, the algorithm achieved an accuracy of 0.736, sensitivity of 0.756, and specificity of 0.716. The corresponding precision, F1 score, and negative predictive value were 0.727, 0.741, and 0.746, respectively. Observed-to-Expected Ratio was 0.96. Standalone AI performance in the reader study dataset

Using the reader study dataset, the standalone AI model yielded an AUROC of 0.8144. At an optimal threshold of 53%, sensitivity and specificity were 75.2% and 73.6%, respectively. At a 50% threshold, the diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity were 73.83%, 75.75%, and 72.55%, respectively (Fig. 1). In terms of sensitivity according to subgroups by AIS volume, for subgroup with AIS volume > 50 mL, the sensitivity was 100% (50/50) with AI standalone assessment. Sensitivities showed higher values for higher AIS volumes: 65.78% for a subgroup with AIS volume ≤ 5 mL (N = 187), 76.47% for a subgroup with AIS volume higher than 5 mL but equal to or less than 10 mL (N = 34), 82.29% for a subgroup with AIS volume higher than 10 mL but equal to or less than 50 mL (N = 96), and 100% for a subgroup with AIS volume higher than 50 mL (N = 50). The AI model achieved a Dice similarity coefficient of 0.5784 in the reader study dataset.

For small infarcts (≤ 5 mL), the model demonstrated higher sensitivity in cases with 4.5–5 mm slice thickness (69.72%) than in those with 3 mm slice thickness (60.26%).

Reader study performance with and without AI Assistance

In the crossover reader study, AI-assisted interpretations significantly outperformed unassisted reads in diagnostic accuracy (0.7563 vs. 0.7203; +3.60%, p < 0.001), sensitivity (0.6966 vs. 0.6540; +4.26%, p < 0.001), and specificity (0.7962 vs. 0.7646; +3.16%, p < 0.001) (Table 3; Fig. 3). The intra-class correlation coefficient was 0.6831 (95% CI, 0.6610–0.7052) for the AI-assisted group and 0.5151 (95% CI, 0.4886–0.5422) for the AI-unassisted group, indicating good and moderate inter-reader agreement, respectively, with substantial improvement of agreement under AI assistance.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated a deep learning-based algorithm using a hybrid CNN-RNN architecture for detecting AIS on NCCT, with a specific focus on improving the detection of small-volume infarcts. The AI algorithm demonstrated strong standalone performance and significantly enhanced diagnostic accuracy when used to assist readers with varying levels of expertise.

Beyond statistical significance, the clinical impact of our AI algorithm is most evident in its ability to bridge the diagnostic gap for non-radiology physicians, who are often the first responders in emergency settings. By significantly enhancing the sensitivity and specificity of these front-line clinicians, the AI-assisted workflow facilitates earlier detection of subtle ischemic changes that might otherwise be overlooked. This improvement is particularly crucial within the hyperacute window (< 6 h), where every minute saved in diagnosis translates to the preservation of viable brain tissue. Consequently, the integration of this AI tool into the clinical pathway can streamline triage decisions, accelerate time-sensitive interventions such as thrombolysis or thrombectomy, and potentially reduce the long-term morbidity associated with delayed AIS recognition. Our findings suggest that AI assistance does not merely provide a diagnostic second opinion but serves as a critical safety net that can improve real-world patient outcomes by ensuring timely and accurate stroke management.

While prior AI research in AIS has primarily focused on LVO13–21, our study expands the focus to include SVO, cardioembolic strokes, and other etiologies by specifically optimizing the detection of subtle, non-LVO infarcts across the entire brain. Although these strokes may not be candidates for thrombectomy, they still carry important clinical implications for thrombolysis eligibility, patient triage, and admission decisions. Moreover, non-LVO AIS subtypes account for more than half of all AIS cases in many populations13–17, and their accurate diagnosis—especially when lesion volume is small—remains challenging due to mimics such as leukoaraiosis and lacunes.

Our results showed that AI assistance improved sensitivity across all infarct volume subgroups, including small lesions. For lesions ≤ 5 mL, sensitivity increased from 53.89% to 57.93% (+ 4.04%, p = 0.002). While the absolute magnitude of this gain is modest, this improvement may translate into meaningful aggregate benefit in high-volume emergency settings, where small infarcts are most frequently missed and where even incremental gains in detection sensitivity can affect downstream triage and treatment decisions. For 5–10 mL lesions, sensitivity increased more substantially from 58.82% to 66.67% (+ 7.84%, p = 0.007), and the algorithm demonstrated perfect detection for lesions > 50 mL. These findings indicate that the algorithm can effectively detect AIS across a wide range of lesion volumes, including small infarcts, without compromising performance for larger infarcts.

Furthermore, our model was designed to detect acute ischemic stroke across all vascular territories, including the posterior circulation. We acknowledge the technical difficulty in detecting such infarcts on NCCT due to bone artifacts in the posterior fossa. In this study, we defined small vessel occlusions primarily based on lesion volume (≤ 10 mL) and etiology. While the model showed effectiveness in detecting these lesions, we recognize that posterior circulation infarcts remain particularly challenging for standard NCCT readings, representing a key area for model refinement.

Interestingly, our study revealed that for small infarcts (≤ 5 mL), AI-standalone sensitivity outperformed AI-assisted human readers (66% vs. 58%). This highlights the complexity of human-AI interaction in clinical workflows. Such a gap likely stems from cognitive conflicts: when AI suggestions deviate from a reader’s judgment, the resulting cognitive load can impair diagnostic efficiency. Furthermore, psychological factors like confirmation bias—leading to suboptimal reliance on or discounting of AI—may have influenced final decisions. These results suggest that successful clinical implementation requires more than algorithmic improvement; future efforts should focus on intuitive interface design and clinical guidelines that help practitioners effectively interpret and incorporate AI-driven insights.

Our subgroup analysis revealed a sensitivity gap for small AIS (≤ 5 mL), with higher baseline performance in 4.5/5 mm slice thickness cases (69.72%) compared to 3 mm cases (60.26%). This performance disparity is likely attributable to the higher prevalence of thicker-slice images in our training cohort, which may have biased the model toward optimal feature representation at these specific resolutions.

Notably, in the external validation cohort, the AI algorithm demonstrated a robust sensitivity and specificity of 75.6% and 71.6%, respectively. Furthermore, the overall observed-to-expected ratio across external cohorts was 0.96, suggesting that the model provides well-calibrated predictions with minimal systematic bias. Across the four external institutions, accuracy remained within a narrow range (0.714–0.746) and observed-to-expected ratios clustered closely around unity (0.92–0.99) despite differences in scanner manufacturers and acquisition protocols, indicating that performance was preserved across heterogeneous imaging environments. These findings demonstrate the model’s generalizability and clinical reliability, supporting its potential application across diverse, real-world imaging environments.

A key factor in the algorithm’s success may lie in the robust ground truth annotation, achieved through coregistration of DWI and ADC images to the CT space in the present study. Although co-registration itself is a standard technique, its application here enabled the creation of high-fidelity training data, particularly for subtle infarcts. This process was essential for labeling small infarct regions on NCCT, where direct visualization is often limited.

To evaluate the clinical feasibility of our model, we integrated the AI pipeline into the hospital’s PACS. The integration introduced a total processing latency of approximately 65 s. Given the critical nature of emergency AIS triage, this delay is considered clinically acceptable, as the AI’s automated triage and heatmap generation provide significant diagnostic support that outweighs the minor latency overhead during the image interpretation workflow.

Despite the promising findings, this study has several limitations. First, our study used DWI/ADC within 6 hours as the ground truth. While sensitive for defining the infarct core, diffusion restriction does not always translate to NCCT hypodensity, particularly in the ultra-hyperacute phase or with robust collaterals. Consequently, our model is optimized to detect ‘infarct demarcation’ rather than the full spectrum of ischemia, potentially causing false negatives in cases like small vessel occlusions without established tissue damage. These physiological discrepancies underscore that NCCT alone is insufficient for AIS diagnosis; thus, CT angiography and CT perfusion remain indispensable for identifying distal occlusions and salvageable penumbra. Future iterations should integrate multimodal data, including CT angiography and CT perfusion, to enhance sensitivity for early-stage ischemia prior to infarct demarcation. Second, we observed a significant discrepancy between patient-wise (69.66%) and slice-wise (44.95%) sensitivities. This gap seems to be primarily attributed to the high prevalence of small-volume infarcts (≤ 5 mL), which constituted 51% of our AIS cases. While our model consistently identifies stroke-positive patients, precise slice-level localization of minute infarcts remains inherently challenging. Nevertheless, our segmentation module achieved a Dice similarity coefficient of 0.5784, which we consider a robust performance given the high proportion of small-volume infarcts (≤ 5 mL). This confirms that our module provides clinically meaningful spatial localization, effectively assisting clinicians in identifying target regions despite the spatial complexities associated with such small lesions. Third, while DWI-based annotations provided a robust ground truth, the accuracy of coregistration may be affected by differences in image resolution and orientation. Though care was taken to ensure alignment, minor registration errors could influence lesion labeling. Fourth, our study is subject to potential selection bias inherent in our institutional stroke protocol. Since we utilized follow-up MRI (including DWI) within 6 h as the reference standard to confirm infarct presence, our study cohort primarily includes patients who were stable enough to undergo MRI assessment. Consequently, patients with critical clinical instability who required immediate reperfusion therapy without preceding MRI were not included in our dataset. Nevertheless, our model demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance for large infarcts (≥ 50 mL), suggesting that it may serve as a reliable diagnostic aid in such time-critical scenarios. Addressing these limitations necessitates a multi-faceted research trajectory: establishing prospective clinical trials to validate diagnostic accuracy and real-world clinical utility; transitioning to transformer-based architectures to enable superior 3D spatial modeling; integrating multimodal data, specifically clinical metadata (e.g., NIHSS, onset time, vascular risk factors) alongside CT angiography and CT perfusion imaging for comprehensive hemodynamic assessment; and implementing small-lesion enhancement strategies to maximize detection sensitivity for minute infarcts. Future efforts in these directions are essential to evolve such AI-driven models into robust, reliable diagnostic aids for emergency stroke care.

In conclusion, our deep learning algorithm improved AIS detection on NCCT across a range of infarct volumes and reader expertise levels, with the greatest gains observed among non-radiologist physicians. While the absolute improvement for small infarcts was modest, the algorithm may serve as a useful diagnostic aid in settings with limited radiologic expertise, pending validation in prospective clinical studies.
