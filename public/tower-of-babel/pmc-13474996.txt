Synthesis of photoactive compounds from 2H-pyran-2-ones and their photophysical properties
Authors: Ranjay Shaw, Rohit Yadav Kosliya, Ismail Althagafi, Pooja, Ramendra Pratap
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
2H-Pyran-2-ones and their derivatives constitute a versatile class of heterocycles that exhibit unique structural and electronic features, making them valuable building blocks for photoactive compounds. Their partial aromatic character, diverse reactivity, and inherent UV/visible absorption enable the construction of conjugated systems with tunable photophysical properties. This review highlights recent advances in the synthesis of photoactive compounds derived from 2H-pyran-2-ones and provides a detailed analysis of their photophysical properties. Particular emphasis is placed on structure–property relationships, light-induced transformations, and design principles that govern emission behaviour and stability. Case studies on metal ion sensors, ratiometric probes, and aggregation-induced emission luminogens are presented to illustrate the functional versatility of these scaffolds. In addition, the review underscores the relevance of pyranone-based systems in sustainable chemistry, as many of their photochemical transformations proceed under mild conditions. By consolidating current knowledge, this article aims to guide future efforts in the rational design of 2H-pyran-2-one-derived photoactive materials for emerging applications in optoelectronics, diagnostics, and responsive materials science.

Introduction

Heterocyclic compounds comprising a six-membered ring containing an oxygen atom and an adjacent carbonyl group are classified as 2H-pyran-2-ones or α-pyrone. Pyran-2-ones have a resonance structure known as the pyrylium betaine B (Fig. 1). As anticipated, pyran-2-one exhibits a degree of aromatic character, which influences certain aspects of its reactivity. Nonetheless, its behavior also reflects the typical chemistry of both 1,3-dienes and lactones, with the dominant reactivity pattern varying according to the specific reaction conditions.1 Similarly, coumarins are benzene-fused derivatives of pyran-2-ones, and many possess notable pharmaceutical and antibiotic properties.2 Less commonly encountered members of this family include isocoumarins and arylcoumarins, which also originate from the pyran-2-one core structure.

Various 2H-pyran-2-ones rings.

These structures, along with their fused derivatives, are widespread in natural products derived from diverse biological sources, including plants, fungi, marine organisms, bacteria, insects, and vertebrates. Natural products incorporating pyran and benzopyran frameworks display a broad spectrum of biological activities, including defence mechanisms, biosynthetic pathways, and as metabolites.3 Although known for over a century, their flexibility in organic synthesis for generating molecular diversity was recognized only after 1960.4 As an important chemical entity, 2-pyranones serve as precursors in synthesizing pharmacologically active compounds like HIV protease inhibitors,5 cardiac stimulants,6 antifungals,7 anticonvulsants,8 antitumor agents,9 pheromones,10 antimicrobials,11 natural pigments,12 and plant growth regulators.13 Thereby they have garnered considerable attention from synthetic chemists, pharmacologists, and medicinal chemists for their synthesis, structural characterization, biological evaluation, and chemical reactivity.14 Along with bioactivity, the 2H-pyran-2-one ring was an excellent precursor for photoactive compounds.

The partial aromatic behaviours of the 2H-pyran-2-ones exhibited electrophilic substitution reactions. They also participate in Diels–Alder reactions as a diene, resulting in various cyclic adducts with suitable dienophiles. As a lactone ring, they are susceptible to nucleophilic attack, leading to ring-opening and ring transformation reactions under basic or nucleophilic conditions. Their unique electronic structure confers intrinsic UV/visible light absorption and makes them excellent precursors for the design of extended conjugated systems. A variety of five-, six-, and seven-membered mono-, di-, and polycyclic heterocycles containing nitrogen, oxygen, and sulfur can be synthesized from suitably functionalized 2H-pyran-2-ones.15 These scaffolds can undergo a range of photochemical transformations, including electrocyclic ring-opening, [2+2] and [4+2] cycloadditions, and photorearrangements, enabling the generation of structurally diverse and functionally rich photoactive molecules. Moreover, substitution at various positions on the 2H-pyran-2-one ring offers a straightforward approach to modulate the electronic properties and tailor absorption/emission characteristics.

In recent years, the strategic functionalization of 2H-pyran-2-ones has enabled the synthesis of a wide variety of photoactive compounds, including coumarins, naphthopyran derivatives, polyaromatic systems, and donor–acceptor architectures.16 Many of these derivatives exhibit notable photophysical properties, such as strong fluorescence, photochromism, solvatochromism, and high quantum yields.17 These characteristics make them highly suitable for use in light-emitting diodes (LEDs), solar cells, photoresponsive materials, and biological imaging agents.

These compounds can often be synthesized under mild conditions, with excellent atom economy, and in some cases using sustainable resources such as biomass-derived precursors.18 Additionally, their photochemical transformations often proceed without the need for harsh transition-metal reagents or elevated temperatures, making them environmentally benign platforms for molecular design. Therefore, the growing interest in pyran-2-one-based photoactive systems is also consistent with several principles of green chemistry,19,20 although a detailed assessment of their green chemistry metrics is beyond the scope of this review.

Despite their promising potential, a comprehensive understanding of the synthetic methodologies, structure–property relationships, and practical applications of photoactive compounds derived from 2H-pyran-2-ones is still evolving. While several review articles have explored the broader use of pyran-2-one derivatives, particularly from a biological perspective4,16,21 Focused insights into their roles in photophysical applications are comparatively scarce. In a notable contribution, Manjula et al. reviewed carbanion-induced ring transformations of 2H-pyran-2-ones leading to aromatic fluorophores.22 However, this work emphasizes a specific synthetic strategy with references limited to their applications in OLEDs, chemosensors, and cell imaging. Therefore, a dedicated review that consolidates recent advancements in the synthesis of photoactive compounds from 2H-pyran-2-ones and critically examines their photophysical properties is timely and essential. Such a review will bridge existing knowledge gaps and guide future research in the design and functional optimization of these promising materials.

This review aims to bridge that gap by providing a detailed overview of the synthetic approaches used to access photoactive compounds from 2H-pyran-2-ones, along with an in-depth analysis of their photophysical properties. Special emphasis is placed on structure–function relationships, mechanisms of light-induced behaviour, and recent innovations in molecular design. We also highlight key applications across photonic and biomedical domains and outline current challenges and opportunities for future exploration. By consolidating the knowledge surrounding 2H-pyran-2-one-derived photoactive compounds, this review seeks to provide a valuable resource for researchers working at the interface of synthetic organic chemistry, photophysics, and materials science.

Classification and application of organic photoactive molecules

Photoactive compounds are defined by their ability to absorb light and undergo structural or electronic transitions. π-Conjugated systems enhance light absorption and emission capabilities of organic compounds, allowing for fine control over photophysical behaviour through heteroatom substitution, conjugation length, and functional group modification.23 Unlike their inorganic counterparts, organic compounds offer advantages such as synthetic flexibility, lightweight construction, solution processability, and the ability to tailor optical and electronic properties through rational design.24 As a result, they are central to advances in optoelectronics, nanotechnology, and biomedicine.

Types of organic photoactive compounds

Understanding the fundamental photophysical and photochemical properties of photoactive compounds is essential for designing light-responsive systems with high specificity and tunability. Based on the nature of light interaction, the type of excited-state behaviour, electronic structure, and functional outcome, these compounds are divided into the following classes.

Photochromic compounds

Photochromic molecules undergo reversible structural changes upon light exposure, typically switching between two isomers with different absorption spectra.25 These are widely used in molecular switches, smart coatings, and light-controlled systems. Owing to their reversible properties, these compounds not only fulfill the essential criteria but also represent some of the most promising candidates for applications in rewritable optical recording media such as CDs and DVDs.26 Notable examples include azobenzenes, which undergo reversible trans–cis isomerization; spiropyrans and their merocyanine forms, which exhibit reversible ring-opening and closing reactions; and diarylethenes, distinguished by their high fatigue resistance and excellent bistability.

Photosensitizers

A photosensitizer is a molecule capable of absorbing light, typically in the ultraviolet or visible range, and transferring the absorbed energy to nearby molecules, thereby initiating photochemical reactions.27 Importantly, the photosensitizer often remains chemically unaltered during this process, enabling it to function catalytically. Upon photon absorption, the molecule transitions from its ground electronic state (S0) to an excited singlet state (S1). It may then return to the ground state via fluorescence or undergo intersystem crossing (ISC) to form a longer-lived triplet state (T1). From this triplet state, the photosensitizer can engage in either energy transfer or electron transfer reactions to other species, which underpin its role in photosensitization. Photosensitizers are widely used in photodynamic therapy (PDT), photocatalysis, solar energy conversion, and photochemical sensing.28,29 Chlorophyll, a natural photosensitizer in plants, transforms light into chemical energy through the photosynthesis process.

Organic semiconductors

Organic semiconductors are carbon-based materials that exhibit semiconducting properties. It consists of alternating single and double bonds, enabling delocalized π-electrons, which are essential for charge mobility. The energy gap between HOMO and LUMO levels define the electronic bandgap, which is crucial for determining a semiconductor's electrical conductivity and optical properties.30 Generally, the mobility of organic semiconductors is lower than that of inorganic semiconductors, but it is sufficient for many applications.31 Charge transport in organic semiconductors takes place via two primary mechanisms: hopping between localized states or band-like transport in more ordered systems. These materials are widely employed in applications, such as organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs), organic photovoltaics (OPVs), organic field-effect transistors (OFETs), and bioelectronics. Their key advantages include lightweight nature, mechanical flexibility, low-cost processing, and the ability to fabricate over large areas.32 However, organic semiconductors also face several challenges, such as lower charge carrier mobility compared to inorganic semiconductors, susceptibility to degradation in air, and difficulties in achieving uniform doping.32,33

Fluorophores and chromophores

A chromophore is a molecular moiety that absorbs light at specific wavelengths within the ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) region, resulting in electronic transitions from the ground state to an excited state. These transitions typically involve π to π* or n to π* excitations and are primarily responsible for the molecule's colour.34 While chromophores are generally non-emissive, certain structures may exhibit fluorescence under specific conditions.35 Chromophores have widespread applications in the dye and pigment industry, as UV-absorbing agents in sunscreens, and in photodynamic therapy for medical treatments.

In contrast, a fluorophore is a subclass of chromophores capable of emitting light via fluorescence following photon absorption.36 Upon excitation, the fluorophore relaxes back to the ground state by emitting a photon of longer wavelength, a phenomenon characterized by the Stokes shift. Fluorophores are extensively utilized in biological imaging, fluorescence microscopy, chemical and biological sensors, flow cytometry, and Förster Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET)-based techniques for studying molecular interactions.

Applications of organic photoactive molecules

Organic photovoltaics (OPVs)

Organic photovoltaics (OPVs) are a class of solar cells that utilize organic semiconducting materials, typically π-conjugated polymers or small molecules, to convert sunlight into electricity.37 These materials absorb light to generate excitons, which dissociate at donor–acceptor interfaces into free charges, subsequently transported to electrodes. Despite historically lower power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) compared to silicon-based photovoltaics, recent advances, especially in non-fullerene acceptors, have pushed laboratory-scale PCEs above 19%.38,39 Challenges remain in terms of long-term operational stability, morphological control of the active layer, and non-radiative voltage losses.40,41 OPVs hold promise for applications in portable electronics, building-integrated photovoltaics, and transparent solar panels.

Photodynamic therapy (PDT)

Photodynamic therapy (PDT) is a minimally invasive therapeutic approach used to treat cancer, microbial infections, and certain skin conditions.42 Upon irradiation with light of a specific wavelength, the photosensitizer transitions to an excited state and transfers energy to molecular oxygen, generating reactive species (RS) such as singlet oxygen (1O2), hydroxyl radical (HO˙), superoxide anion (O2˙−), and hydroperoxyl radical (HOO˙).43 These RS induce localized cytotoxic effects, including apoptosis, necrosis, and autophagy, leading to the destruction of targeted cells while minimizing damage to surrounding healthy tissue.44 PDT offers several advantages: it could be highly selective, repeatable, non-invasive, and non-toxic compared to conventional chemotherapy and radiation. Moreover, it can stimulate immune responses against tumours, potentially aiding long-term disease control. Current research in this field focuses on enhancing selectivity toward target cells, designing photosensitizers activated by low-energy light, and improving the overall efficacy of PDT.45

Organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs)

Organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs) are a type of optoelectronic device that produce light when an electric voltage is applied, utilizing organic semiconductor materials. Typically, an OLED comprises several ultra-thin layers: a transparent anode, a hole-transport layer, an emissive layer, an electron-transport layer, and a cathode.46 When a voltage is applied, electrons and holes are injected from the electrodes, recombine within the emissive layer, and emit visible light, a process known as electroluminescence. Compared to conventional light-emitting technologies, OLEDs offer distinct advantages, including high contrast ratios, wide viewing angles, rapid response times, and the potential for flexible, transparent, and ultra-thin display designs.47 As a result, they are commonly integrated into devices like smartphones, televisions, wearable tech, and lighting panels. The emissive materials in OLEDs may be fluorescent, phosphorescent, or based on thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF), with the latter two enabling superior internal quantum efficiencies.48 However, the technology still faces challenges, including short lifespans, especially in blue emitters, susceptibility to environmental degradation, and the high production costs associated with scaling up to larger formats.49

Molecular switches and actuators

Photoresponsive compounds like azobenzenes and diarylethenes are used in nanomachines, soft robotics, and responsive materials due to their light-triggered conformational changes.50 These are dynamic molecular systems that undergo reversible structural or electronic changes in response to external stimuli such as light, pH, temperature, redox potential, or chemical signals.51 Molecular switches typically toggle between two or more stable states, making them essential in molecular electronics, data storage, and responsive materials.52 These compounds change conformation or isomerize upon irradiation with light. Molecular actuators, on the other hand, convert chemical or physical inputs into mechanical motion at the molecular level and can induce macroscopic movements when integrated into materials.53

Fluorescent dyes

Fluorescent dyes are essential tools for sensing and bioimaging because they emit light upon excitation, enabling the visualization and quantification of biological molecules, structures, and dynamic processes with high sensitivity and spatial resolution. These dyes, often designed with specific functional groups, can respond selectively to ions, pH, reactive oxygen/nitrogen species, or biomolecular interactions, making them ideal for chemical and biological sensing.54 In bioimaging, fluorescent dyes such as fluorescein, rhodamine, and cyanine derivatives are widely used in fluorescence microscopy, flow cytometry, live-cell imaging, and super-resolution techniques.55 Small-molecule fluorophores can be chemically modified to tailor their spectral properties, reactivity, and biological compatibility; their utility spans diverse applications, including enzyme sensing, cellular imaging, FRET, and voltage detection.56 Furthermore, recent development of near-infrared (NIR) and two-photon-excitable dyes has enhanced imaging depth and reduced photodamage in live tissues.57 Despite these innovations, challenges such as limited photostability, potential cytotoxicity, and non-specific interactions persist. Ongoing research aims to overcome these hurdles by designing more robust fluorophores and employing nanostructured carriers to improve targeting and performance.

Photoactive sensors

Metal-sensors

Zinc-sensor

In this context, Pratap and his team reported the design, synthesis, and application of a novel thieno[3,2-c]pyran-based compound (3) as a selective and sensitive fluorescent “turn-on” chemosensor for the detection of Zn2+ ions (Scheme 1a).58 The compound incorporates an excited-state intramolecular proton transfer (ESIPT) mechanism and exhibits aggregation-induced emission (AIE) behaviour, both of which play critical roles in its sensing performance. Sensor 3 was synthesized via a one-step reaction from methyl 4-(methylthio)-2-oxo-6-phenyl-2H-pyran-3-carboxylate (1) and methyl 2-mercaptoacetate (2) in the presence of l-proline (30 mol%) and triethylamine (20 mol%) in DMSO at 90 °C. The resulting compound was fully characterized using single-crystal X-ray diffraction, NMR, FTIR, and mass spectrometry.

(a) The synthetic protocol of chemosensor 3. (b) The figure illustrates the potential binding mode of chemosensor 3 with Zn2+ ions.

Photophysical investigations revealed that 3 exhibits low fluorescence in its free form due to ESIPT. However, upon coordination with Zn2+ (Scheme 1b), a strong fluorescence enhancement was observed at λem = 484 nm. This change is attributed to the suppression of ESIPT and the formation of a more rigid complex. UV-vis and fluorescence titrations, along with Job's plot analysis, confirmed a 2 : 1 binding stoichiometry between 3 and Zn2+ ions. Time-resolved fluorescence studies showed an increase in the average lifetime (τavg) from 1.68 ns (free 3) to 3.25 ns (3–Zn2+ complex) (Fig. 2a), further supporting the “turn-on” fluorescence mechanism. Furthermore, the limiting concentration of detection (LOD) for Zn2+ was calculated to be 0.67 µM.

(b) The emission spectra of chemosensor 3 when exposed to different metal salts, along with zinc ions. (c) The lifetime profiles of chemosensor 3 without and with Zn2+ ions, excitation wavelength of 373 nm and observing emission at 484 nm. (d) The HOMO–LUMO diagrams of chemosensor 3 and the (3 + Zn2+) complex, highlighting their frontier molecular orbitals. Reproduced from ref. 58 with permission from the Royal Society of Chemistry, copyright 2025.

The chemosensor exhibited outstanding selectivity for Zn2+ ions over a range of other metal ions, including Na+, K+, Ca2+, Cr3+, Mn2+, Fe3+, Co2+, Ni2+, Cu2+, Hg2+, and Al3+. Notably, the presence of competing cations or counter anions had a negligible influence on its sensing performance (Fig. 2b). The sensor's practical utility was demonstrated via colorimetric and fluorometric changes, both in solution and on paper-based test strips, where the presence of Zn2+ induced a visible yellow-to-dark yellow shift in normal light and a green fluorescence under UV light (Fig. 2c).

Additionally, sensor 3 exhibited AIE behaviour in a THF–H2O mixture, where fluorescence intensity increased with water content due to aggregation. Quantum yield measurements also confirmed this enhancement, rising from 0.31 (in THF) to 0.53 (in 60% H2O). The experimental findings were supported by density functional theory (DFT) calculations, which showed a reduced HOMO–LUMO gap upon Zn2+ binding and confirmed tetrahedral coordination via hydroxyl and carbonyl oxygen atoms (Fig. 2d).

In conclusion, the thieno[3,2-c]pyran-based chemosensor 3 represents a promising, highly selective, sensitive, and reversible fluorescent probe for Zn2+ detection, with potential applications in environmental monitoring and biological systems. The reusability of the chemosensor was assessed by monitoring its reversible interaction with Zn2+ ions upon addition of disodium EDTA (10 mM).59 The chelating agent effectively sequestered Zn2+ ions, regenerating the chemosensor to its initial state and thereby permitting its repeated application for subsequent Zn2+ detection cycles.

Palladium sensor

A study by Shally et al. presents the design and synthesis of two new fluorescent chemosensors, 7b and 7c, based on functionalized 8-(thiophen-2-yl)-1,2,3,4-tetrahydroquinoline scaffolds, for the selective detection of palladium(ii) ions (Scheme 2).60 A structurally related compound, 7a, was also synthesized to investigate the role of the thiophene ring in metal binding. The chemosensors 7b and 7c demonstrated strong fluorescence “turn-off” behaviour in the presence of Pd2+ ions, attributed to the heavy atom effect of palladium and its strong quenching capabilities. 1H-NMR titration experiments revealed downfield shifts and disappearance of the N–H proton, supporting coordination via deprotonation and bidentate binding through nitrogen and sulfur atoms.

(a) Synthesis of chemosensors 7a–c. (b) Possible binding for the structure of 7b–7c with Pd2+ ions.

Both chemosensors were synthesized via a ring transformation strategy using functionalized pyran-2-one precursors, and structures were confirmed by spectral and crystallographic analyses.61 UV-vis and fluorescence spectroscopy in methanol revealed that 7b and 7c exhibit strong emission at 515 nm and 485 nm, respectively, with excitation wavelengths (λex) at 390 nm and 376 nm, respectively (Fig. 3a and b). Upon addition of Pd2+ ions, a significant decrease in fluorescence intensity was observed, with 7c showing greater sensitivity and a stronger quenching response than 7b. Additionally, fluorescence lifetime measurements showed a decrease in excited-state lifetimes upon Pd2+ binding, further confirming the quenching mechanism.

(a) The emission spectra of chemosensors 7b and (b) the emission spectra of chemosensors 7c, each at a concentration of 20 µM, as they are exposed to increasing concentrations of PdCl2 ions ranging from 0 to 50 µM. The inset shows the decrease in emission intensity upon adding PdCl2. (c) The lifetime of chemosensors 7b and (d) the lifetime of chemosensors 7c in methanol, comparing their fluorescence decay lifetimes in both the absence and presence of Pd2+ ions. Reproduced from ref. 60 with permission from the Royal Society of Chemistry, copyright 2025.

Selectivity studies demonstrated that 7b and 7c were highly selective for Pd2+ over other metal ions, including Na+, Ca2+, Mn2+, Co2+, Ni2+, Cr3+, Fe2+/3+, Cu2+, Zn2+, Hg2+, Pb2+, Pt2+, Ag+, Cd2+, and Al3+. The limiting concentration of detection (LOD) for Pd2+ was determined to be 49 nM for 7b and 44 nM for 7c, among the lowest reported values. Job's plot analyses and HRMS data confirmed a 1 : 1 binding stoichiometry between the chemosensors and Pd2+ ions. The selectivity of chemosensors 7b and 7c toward Pd2+ was further examined using various palladium salts such as Pd(PPh3)2Cl2, Pd(PPh3)4, Pd(OAc)2, and PdCl2, to evaluate the influence of counterions. All salts induced fluorescence quenching in both sensors, with PdCl2 producing the largest decrease in emission (Fig. 4a and b). This enhanced quenching is attributed to PdCl2's greater ionization in solution, generating solvated [Pd(solvent)x]2+ species that efficiently coordinate with 7b and 7c, thereby suppressing fluorescence. In contrast, Pd(PPh3)4 and Pd(OAc)2 exhibited reduced ionization due to stabilizing ligand effects, limiting the availability of free Pd2+ ions. These findings indicate that the detection efficiency of Pd2+ by 7b and 7c is closely linked to the ionization capacity of the palladium salt, with the simple structure of PdCl2 favoring effective coordination and quenching.

Panel (c) presents the emission spectra of both 7b and 7c when tetrabutylammonium chloride is added in a 20-fold excess. To clarify the role of chloride ions, tetrabutylammonium chloride was introduced in excess (20 equivalents) to solutions of 7b and 7c. No significant change in fluorescence was observed (Fig. 4c), indicating that chloride ions alone neither interact with the sensors nor induce quenching. However, in PdCl2, chloride ions enhance solvation and facilitate Pd2+ complexation with the chemosensors. Thus, chloride ions indirectly promote Pd2+ solubility rather than directly participating in fluorescence quenching.

Density Functional Theory (DFT) studies corroborated these findings, indicating stable complexation and redistribution of electron density upon Pd2+ binding. 7c–Pd2+ complexes were found to be more stable than 7b–Pd2+, matching the experimental sensitivity data. For practical applications, test strips coated with 7b or 7c were fabricated and successfully detected Pd2+ solutions via a visible fluorescence quenching response under UV light. In conclusion, chemosensors 7b and 7c, particularly 7c, are effective, highly selective, and sensitive probes for Pd2+ detection, offering potential utility in environmental monitoring and industrial applications.

Recently, Chahal et al. reported a new class of N-amino-thieno[3,2-c]pyridin-4-one derivatives synthesized from 2H-pyran-2-one precursors, which exhibit excellent photophysical properties and function as fluorescence-based Pd2+ sensors (Scheme 3).62 Subsequent cyclization of 2H-pyran-2-one precursors 8 with methyl mercaptoacetate afforded thieno[3,2-c]pyran-4-one intermediates 9, which on treatment with hydrazine hydrate underwent ring transformation to furnish the target N-amino-thieno[3,2-c]pyridin-4-one derivatives (10a–c) in good yields. The synthesized fluorophores displayed solvent-dependent emission, while two derivatives also showed aggregation-induced emission (AIE) behavior arising from the restriction of intramolecular motion in the aggregated state. Upon coordination with Pd(OAc)2, all compounds underwent pronounced fluorescence quenching, enabling highly selective detection of palladium ions over a broad range of competing metal ions. The sensing mechanism was supported by fluorescence titration, DFT calculations, and 1H NMR studies, which indicated coordination through the carbonyl oxygen and amino functionalities. Furthermore, the probes were successfully applied to TLC-based test strips for rapid visual detection of Pd(OAc)2 under UV irradiation, highlighting their potential as simple and practical photoactive chemosensors for palladium monitoring. Importantly, the probes 10a and 10c demonstrated excellent selectivity toward Pd2+ over a wide range of competing metal ions, including Fe3+, Mn2+, Co2+, Ni2+, Cu2+, Zn2+, Ag+, Cd2+, and Hg2+, with negligible interference from these species. However probe 10b shows fluorescence quenching for Hg(OAc)2 as well as Pd(OAc)2 over the other metal ions. The fabricated TLC strips exhibited selective fluorescence quenching in the presence of Pd(OAc)2 under UV irradiation, demonstrating the potential of these photoactive compounds as simple and cost-effective platforms for rapid visual palladium detection.

Iron sensor

Goel et al. reported in 2015 the design, synthesis, and biological application of a novel dual-colorimetric and ratiometric fluorescent probe, NAP-3 (14c), derived from a 2H-pyran-2-one scaffold.63 This work introduces a new class of donor–acceptor (D–A) functionalized naphtho[2,1-b][1,10]phenanthroline (NAP) compounds specifically designed for the selective detection of labile Fe3+ ions in vitro and in vivo, including live imaging in the multicellular organism Caenorhabditis elegans.

The synthesis began with the preparation of 2H-pyran-2-one intermediates via the reaction of α-oxo-ketene-S,S-acetal with substituted acetophenones. Subsequent substitution of the methylsulfanyl group with piperidine provided D–A-type pyran-3-carbonitriles. These were further transformed through a Michael addition with a benzo[1,10]phenanthrolinone precursor, followed by intramolecular cyclization and DDQ-mediated aromatization, to afford the final NAP dyes (14a–d) (Scheme 4), including the lead compound NAP-3.

Synthetic protocol of naphtho[2,1-b][1,10]phenanthrolines (NAPs).

Photophysical studies of NAP-3 in aqueous solution (pH 7.0–7.4) revealed strong absorption around 300 nm (π–π*) and a weak n–π* transition at 368 nm, along with green-yellow fluorescence emission at 544 nm (Fig. 5a and b). TDDFT calculations revealed that the HOMO was localized on the donor piperidine unit while the LUMO resided on the phenanthroline acceptor, indicating intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) (Fig. 5c). The ICT mechanism was key to its fluorescence response and its ability to engage in ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT) upon Fe3+ binding. Upon binding Fe3+, a significant spectral shift occurred: the 544 nm peak diminished and a new emission at 605 nm emerged, yielding a ratiometric fluorescence response (I605/I544) with an 8.5-fold enhancement. A clear isoemission point at 590 nm was observed, confirming true ratiometric behaviour. The probe showed a detection limit as low as 9.1 nM, with a 2 : 1 binding stoichiometry confirmed by Job's plot and mass spectrometry. NAP-3 demonstrated high selectivity for Fe3+ over other metal ions, including Fe2+, even in reducing or oxidizing environments (Fig. 5d). The probe was unaffected by biological reductants like cysteine and glutathione, and retained its fluorescence characteristics over a physiological pH range (5.5–8.3).

Biological validation was performed in HepG2 liver cells and C. elegans. In HepG2 cells, NAP-3 showed strong cell permeability and non-toxic behaviour (Fig. 6). Without Fe3+ supplementation, only green emission (505–550 nm) was observed. With ferric citrate treatment, red emission (≥575 nm) significantly increased, indicating in situ Fe3+ binding. In C. elegans, NAP-3 enabled the first direct in vivo imaging of labile Fe3+ pools. Wild-type worms showed low red fluorescence, while ferritin-silenced (ftn-1 RNAi) worms exhibited a 3-fold higher red/green intensity ratio. RT-qPCR confirmed altered ftn-1 gene expression correlated with intracellular Fe3+ levels (Fig. 6).

(a) Confocal microscopy images show HepG2 cells treated with NAP-3 (3 mM, 24 hours) and (b) after supplementation with ferric citrate (5 × 10−4 M, 24 hours). In summary, the study demonstrates that a 2H-pyran-2-one-based synthetic route can be used to produce highly specific and sensitive D–A fluorophores for bioimaging applications. NAP-3 exemplifies a new generation of ratiometric sensors that combine aqueous stability, low cytotoxicity, and metal ion selectivity, providing a powerful platform for real-time imaging of biologically relevant metal ions in complex systems.

Aluminium sensors

Hara et al. developed a novel water-soluble fluorescent probe, PSP [dimethyl 2-(6-(4-(dimethylamino)phenyl)-2-oxo-3-(phenylsulfonyl)-2H-pyran-4-yl)malonate, 18], based on a phenylsulfonyl-2H-pyran-2-one scaffold (17) for the selective detection of Al3+ ions in biological systems (Scheme 5).64 The probe was synthesized through a three-step strategy involving the preparation of an intermediate 16 from 2-(phenylsulfonyl)acetonitrile (15), followed by a one-pot condensation with p-dimethylaminoacetophenone and subsequent reaction with dimethyl malonate. Structurally, PSP contains a donor–acceptor π-conjugated system comprising an electron-donating dimethylamino group and electron-withdrawing pyrone carbonyl and sulfone functionalities, which are responsible for its favorable photophysical characteristics.

Synthetic protocol of PSP.

The photophysical investigation revealed that PSP exhibits an absorption maximum at 444 nm, which shifted bathochromically to 505 nm upon coordination with Al3+ ions, indicating a significant alteration in the electronic structure of the molecule (Fig. 7a). Fluorescence studies demonstrated that PSP shows a strong turn-on fluorescence response with an emission maximum at 580 nm in the presence of Al3+ (Fig. 7b).

The probe 18 exhibited exceptional selectivity toward Al3+ over a wide range of competing metal ions, including Na+, K+, Ca2+, Mg2+, Fe2+, Fe3+, Co2+, Ni2+, Zn2+, Cd2+, Cu2+, and Mn2+ (Fig. 7d). Arsenic or ruthenium sensor

A new class of photoactive 2H-pyran-2-one derivatives incorporating sulfonyl hydrazide functionality was synthesized through an efficient one-pot multicomponent strategy (Scheme 9).68 The synthetic strategy involved the reaction of 6-(4-bromophenyl)-4-(methylthio)-2-oxo-2H-pyran-3-carbonitrile (27) with hydrazine monohydrate and benzene sulfonyl chloride under reflux conditions in ethanol, affording a library of functionalized derivatives (28a–f) in moderate to excellent yields. The incorporation of sulfonyl hydrazide groups into the pyranone framework introduced additional donor and acceptor sites, enhancing both the photophysical and sensing characteristics of the molecules in solutions as well as in solid state (Fig. 8a). Photophysical studies revealed absorption bands in the range of 300–370 nm, attributed to π–π* and n–π* electronic transitions, while fluorescence emission maxima were observed between 450 and 480 nm. The fluorescence efficiency was strongly dependent on the nature of the substituents, with electron-donating groups producing higher quantum yields than electron-withdrawing substituents. Notably, compound 28a exhibited aggregation-induced emission (AIE) behavior, displaying weak fluorescence in dilute DMSO solution but significantly enhanced emission in DMSO–water mixtures due to aggregate formation and restricted intramolecular rotations. The sensor properties of 28a were evaluated toward various metal ions and demonstrated remarkable selectivity for As3+ and Ru3+ (Fig. 8b). Coordination with As3+ resulted in a pronounced fluorescence enhancement through a chelation-enhanced fluorescence (CHEF) mechanism and suppression of photoinduced electron transfer (PET) (Fig. 8c), whereas Ru3+ induced fluorescence quenching through ligand-to-metal charge transfer and heavy-atom effects (Fig. 8d). The probe exhibited 1 : 1 binding stoichiometry with both metal ions and showed excellent sensitivity, with detection limits of 1.91 × 10−7 M for As3+ and 2.01 × 10−7 M for Ru3+. Furthermore, the sensor maintained high selectivity in the presence of competing ions and was successfully applied to detect these toxic metal ions in environmental water samples, highlighting the potential of functionalized 2H-pyran-2-one derivatives as versatile fluorescent chemosensors for environmental and analytical applications.

Biomolecule sensor

Insulin sensor

Goel et al. in 2013 reported the design, synthesis, and biological evaluation of a novel series of pyranone-based hybrid molecules aimed at addressing the limitations of traditional thiazolidinedione (TZD) insulin sensitizers.69 These compounds were developed to retain insulin-sensitizing activity while minimizing associated risks such as hepatotoxicity and cardiovascular complications. The authors employed 2H-pyran-2-one as a core structural motif, owing to its documented hepatoprotective and antidiabetic potential, to synthesize new hybrid molecules incorporating TZD or fibrate moieties.

The synthetic approach began with the preparation of 6-aryl-4-(methylthio)-2-oxo-2H-pyran-3-carbonitriles via ketene-S,S-acetal methodology. These were converted to hydroxyethyl-substituted pyranones through substitution with N-methylethanolamine. Subsequent bromination and coupling with either TZD or fibrate pharmacophores led to two libraries of hybrid compounds (34a–h and 35a–h for TZD hybrids; 37a–h for fibrate hybrids) (Scheme 10). All compounds were fully characterized using spectroscopic techniques.

Synthetic protocol of pyranone–thiazolidinedione hybrids 34a–h, 351a–h, and pyranone–fibrate hybrid compounds 37a–h.

Among forty compounds screened, three compounds, 32b (chloro-substituted), 35e, and 37e (both are methoxy-substituted) showed promising biological activity. These were tested for their ability to induce adipogenesis in 3T3-L1 preadipocyte cells, which is a hallmark of insulin-sensitising effects via PPARγ activation. Microscopy and Oil Red O staining revealed significant lipid accumulation in treated cells (Fig. 9a and b), comparable to the reference drug rosiglitazone. Western blot analysis confirmed increased expression of adipogenic markers aP2 and fatty acid synthase (FAS), particularly for compounds 35e and 37e, indicating PPARγ pathway involvement (Fig. 9c). The selected compounds were further assessed for glucose uptake enhancement in differentiated adipocytes. Treatment with 32b, 35e, and 37e significantly increased glucose uptake in the presence of insulin (Fig. 9d), comparable to rosiglitazone. Notably, 32b and 37e also prevented the development of insulin resistance in adipocytes subjected to chronic insulin exposure, demonstrating their potential to maintain insulin sensitivity (Fig. 9e).

Histopathological examination of liver tissues corroborated the biochemical data, showing improved hepatic architecture and reduced necrosis in treated groups.

In conclusion, this study highlights the successful synthesis of multifunctional 2H-pyran-2-one-based hybrid molecules that possess both insulin-sensitizing and hepatoprotective activities. Compounds 32b, 35e, and 37e emerged as strong candidates with potential therapeutic relevance for type 2 diabetes and related liver dysfunctions. Their dual action—stimulating glucose uptake while protecting hepatic tissue—addresses major limitations of conventional TZD drugs, positioning 2H-pyran-2ones as a valuable platform for developing safer antidiabetic agents. Further preclinical development and mechanistic studies are warranted to translate these findings into clinical applications.

Lipid sensor

Goel and his team further introduced a novel class of donor–acceptor (D–A) based teraryl aggregation-induced emission luminogens (AIEgens), specifically compound 40, designed for selective imaging of intracellular lipid droplets (LDs) in live cells and tissue sections.70 The work addresses the limitations of conventional lipid-staining dyes, such as Nile red and BODIPY, which suffer from poor photostability, low specificity, and aggregation-caused quenching (ACQ). In contrast, compound 40 exhibited strong AIE characteristics, a high Stokes shift (≈195 nm), a long fluorescence lifetime, and robust biocompatibility, making it a superior fluorescent probe. Compound 40 showed enhanced fluorescence emission in non-polar solvents like toluene, with a quantum yield of 30%, compared to polar aprotic solvents like DMSO.

Compound 40 was synthesized through a multi-step route involving pyranone intermediates and optimized for high yield (Scheme 11). Photophysical studies revealed its strong emission in nonpolar solvents and increased fluorescence intensity in aqueous environments due to AIE behaviour (Scheme 11a and b). Compound 40 uniquely exhibited dual emission in both solution and solid states (SSDE), as well as positive solvatochromism, attributes indicating intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) from a dimethylamino donor to a nitrile acceptor. For compound 40, measurements in both DMSO and a 99% water/DMSO mixture (aggregate state) revealed a significantly higher average lifetime of 12.67 ns in the aggregated form. This elevated lifetime aligns with studies where restricted molecular motion in aggregated states reduces non-radiative decay pathways, enhancing radiative transitions.

Glutathione sensor

Jiang et al. developed a reversible reaction-based ratiometric fluorescent probe, ThiolQuant Green (TQ Green), incorporating a 2H-pyran-2-one (coumarin) fluorophore for the quantitative detection of intracellular glutathione (GSH) (Scheme 13a).73 TQ Green was synthesized via a modular condensation strategy in which 54 was condensed with the appropriate ketone in a CH2Cl2/EtOH (1 : 1) mixture using pyrrolidine as a base catalyst at room temperature for 12 h. To improve intracellular delivery, TQ Green was converted into its acetoxymethyl ester (TQ Green-AM) by esterification of the carboxylic acid group with bromomethyl acetate in anhydrous DMF using DIPEA under nitrogen, providing the membrane-permeable derivative in 69% yield. The electronic properties of the probe were systematically optimized by varying the substituents on the Michael acceptor to achieve an appropriate equilibrium constant for reversible GSH recognition. To facilitate cellular uptake, the resulting probe was further converted into its acetoxymethyl ester (TQ Green-AM), which readily undergoes esterase-mediated hydrolysis inside cells to regenerate the active fluorescent probe. Organic materials have emerged as highly attractive sensing platforms for explosive detection due to their high sensitivity, excellent selectivity, rapid response, low cost, ease of synthesis, structural tunability, and compatibility with portable optical devices. Photoactive compounds with applications yet to be explored

Similar to the aforementioned literature, several other reports describe the synthesis of photoactive compounds derived from 2H-pyran-2-ones. However, their potential applications remain largely unexplored and have not been comprehensively evaluated. Despite this, the promising properties of these compounds suggest significant potential. In this context, representative examples within this category are summarized in detail.

A novel, metal-free synthetic strategy was introduced for the formation of spirocyclic heterocycles (148, spiranes) via carbanion-induced ring-switching transformations of 2H-pyran-2-ones (2012).97 The process involves the reaction of 6-aryl-4-sec-amino-2H-pyran-2-ones (145) with 2-tetralone (146) in dry THF using NaH as a base at low temperatures of 15–20 °C (Scheme 36). This method allows the efficient and selective formation of phenanthrene derivatives 147 and complex spiranes 148. The reaction of fused pyranones (145) with tetralone results in spiranes 149a–g in moderate yield, which are structurally significant for photophysical applications. The reaction mechanism proceeds through a Michael addition, followed by CO2 elimination and ring rearrangement, yielding structurally diverse products. Optimization studies revealed that temperature and base concentration are critical for achieving high spirane yields, with reduced yields or undesired products forming at higher temperatures or under alternate base/solvent conditions.

Synthetic protocol of phenanthrenes 147 and spiranes 148, 149.

Crystallographic analyses confirmed the three-dimensional, non-planar geometry of key spirane products, especially compound 148, which showed unique halogen-hydrogen bonding leading to an elliptical cavity—a feature that could facilitate host–guest interactions for specific cation binding (Fig. 46a). Quantum chemical calculations further revealed a low helimerization energy barrier for compound 149a, indicating rapid conformational inversion at room temperature. Photophysical studies of these spiranes 149a–g, supported by TD-DFT calculations, showed dominant π–π* transitions with minor contributions from n–π* transitions. The presence of heteroatoms and substituents affected absorption properties, suggesting tunable electronic behavior suitable for optical and electronic materials (Fig. 46b). In summary, this work provides a sustainable route to functional spiranes with significant structural, electronic, and photophysical potential, paving the way for future applications in host–guest chemistry, optoelectronics, and materials science.

Conclusions

In this study, 2H-pyran-2-one derivatives have been established as versatile precursors for the synthesis of a wide range of photoactive compounds through systematic functional modifications. Comprehensive spectroscopic and photophysical analyses confirmed their favorable optical and electronic characteristics. Several derivatives exhibited strong absorption in the visible region coupled with high fluorescence quantum yields, highlighting their suitability for applications in optoelectronic devices and chemical sensing. In particular, the pyrene-substituted pyranone 86g, fluorene derivatives 79e and 81e, and benzo[f]quinoline derivative 104c demonstrated excellent photophysical properties, making them strong candidates for organic light-emitting diode (OLED) applications. Furthermore, the structural diversity of 2H-pyran-2-one derivatives facilitated the development of selective sensors for transition metals—including Zn2+ (3), Pd2+ (7c), and Fe3+ (14c)—as well as for explosives (70) and biologically relevant targets such as lipids (47, 52), insulin (32b), and protein (63). Along with these, several compounds show solvatochromic properties, emphasizing the pronounced sensitivity of their emission behaviour to environmental polarity, underscoring the possibility of fine-tuning optical responses through medium-dependent modulation. In addition, thermal stability investigations revealed that most derivatives maintained structural and photophysical integrity at moderately high temperatures, ensuring their reliability for practical applications in solid-state devices. Despite these encouraging outcomes, certain limitations warrant attention. A few derivatives showed reduced product yields and poor crystallinity, complicating purification and structural resolution. In some cases, overlapping spectral features hindered the precise assignment of electronic transitions, pointing to the need for advanced computational modeling to support experimental interpretation. The observed low quantum efficiency of specific analogs also limits their immediate applicability in device fabrication. Furthermore, the fluorescence behaviour of some compounds was strongly dependent on solvent conditions, which may restrict stability and reproducibility under operational environments.

Looking ahead, several directions can be pursued to overcome these challenges and expand the applicability of 2H-pyran-2-one-based systems. Structural optimization by introducing electron-donating or withdrawing substituents can fine-tune absorption/emission characteristics and improve quantum yields. Integration of these derivatives into hybrid nanomaterials, thin films, or polymeric matrices could enhance stability and broaden their utility in light-harvesting, bioimaging, and photonic technologies. Computational chemistry approaches such as TD-DFT simulations should be systematically employed to gain deeper insights into structure–property relationships, thereby enabling predictive design of next-generation photoactive materials. In addition, further exploration of solid-state photophysics and device-level testing will be crucial to validate their long-term functional potential.

Overall, this work establishes 2H-pyran-2-one derivatives as versatile building blocks for photoactive molecular design, while also highlighting key challenges that must be addressed to fully harness their optoelectronic capabilities in future applications.
