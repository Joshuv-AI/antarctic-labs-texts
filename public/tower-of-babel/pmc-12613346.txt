How do individuals with body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) perceive their own cognition? A qualitative investigation into subjective cognition in BDD
Authors: Katrina Holmes à Court, Tamsyn E. Van Rheenan, Susan L. Rossell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCognition is implicated in the aetiology and maintenance of body dysmorphic disorder (BDD); however, there is almost no research concerning individuals’ perception of their cognition. Understanding real-world, subjective cognitive experiences will improve understanding of the functional impacts of BDD and could alter treatment approaches. This study aimed to examine the subjective impressions of the prominence, type and impact of cognitive impairments experienced by adults with BDD.MethodsParticipants responded to a global, online self-report survey asking qualitative questions regarding cognitive experiences in BDD (N = 316). Data was analysed using Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA). Five superordinate themes were identified: (1) the cognitive experiences of BDD, (2) functional impairment, (3) relationship between cognitive impairment and BDD, (4) social and emotional impacts, and (5) strategies.ResultsIndividuals recognise impaired cognition as relating to their experience of illness. Many individuals report impairments across multiple specific cognitive domains (attention, memory, executive function, social cognition and processing speed) and some perceive themselves as generally ‘cognitively flawed’.ConclusionsThe self-reported social and emotional consequences of cognitive impairments in BDD are significant and many individuals report functional impairment. Being aware of patients’ perceptions of their cognitive impairments may help clinicians tailor or modify their strategies. Questioning of cognitive experiences should form part of patient assessments.

Introduction

Body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) is an under-recognised and under-diagnosed psychiatric disorder, [1] in which excessive appearance-related obsessions and behaviours cause individuals significant distress and dysfunction. [2, 3] BDD is associated with reduced psychosocial functioning, including across the areas of employment, education, quality of life and suicide rates. [4, 5] Given that cognitive factors such as misperception, maladaptive beliefs, and selective attention to specific parts of the body are prominent in its symptom profile, BDD has been conceptualised as a disorder of distorted cognition [6, 7] and cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) is the recommended treatment framework [8]. Indeed, objective impairments have been found in research comparing clinically diagnosed individuals with BDD to healthy controls. In a sample of 21 individuals with BDD, Toh, Castle and Rossell [9] found overall impaired cognition and specific impairments in immediate memory and attention using the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status, and in a large sample (N = 65), Malcolm et al. [10] also found impaired overall cognitive functioning and specifically across the domains of visual learning, processing speed and executive functioning using the MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery. This study further characterised two cognitive subgroups in BDD, those who are mildly cognitively impaired and those who experience broad cognitive impairments. A recent systematic review of 54 studies found inconsistencies across all cognitive domains but noted that impairments were most consistently observed in executive function and social cognition, both classified as higher-order processes in Harvey’s [11] hierarchical model of cognition [12]. Despite its importance to both the theoretical model and clinical framework, the cognitive profile of BDD remains unclear.

Further definition of the cognitive profile may be achieved through examining subjective cognition, which are valuable ‘real life’ measures of dysfunction, addressing the limitations of many objective measures of cognition, which are often challenged by their lack of ecological validity [13]. Compared to research on objective cognitive impairments in BDD, there has been no empirical research on subjective cognitive experiences in BDD. However, one recent qualitative study of the BDD experience showed that patients were aware of their tendency to focus on specific details of the body at the expense of the whole, [14] a pattern which has been previously demonstrated in research employing objective measures of bodily perception (e.g. [15, 16].

Subjective cognition in related disorders such as schizophrenia is only weakly correlated with objective measures but remains important for understanding everyday cognitive impairment [17]. This discrepancy highlights the need to consider both subjective and objective assessments to fully capture the complexity of cognitive deficits. In BDD, exploring individuals’ subjective cognitive experiences could provide valuable context for inconsistent objective findings. Understanding patients’ perceptions of their cognitive challenges may also help clinicians tailor treatments, especially by addressing issues like catastrophising—common in BDD [18] —and metacognitive confidence, which can influence therapy goals [19]. Improving treatment outcomes is crucial, as around 35—40% of BDD patients undergoing CBT either do not improve or relapse [20]. However, the scope and effects of subjective cognitive impairments in BDD remain largely unexplored.

Thus, the aim of this study was to comprehensively investigate the subjective experience of cognitive impairments in individuals with BDD for the first time. We used Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) to do so. IPA is a qualitative research method that enables rich analysis of how individuals perceive and understand their experiences [21, 22]. It employs a method of double hermeneutics in which participants interpret their own world, and the researcher subsequently decodes that interpretation. Here, we asked individuals with BDD to provide qualitative, open-ended reports of everyday cognitive functioning. They were able to describe in detail how they perceived their cognitive abilities to be impacted in everyday life, while also considering their personal and environmental contexts. The research questions ‘What are the subjective experiences of cognition in BDD?’ and ‘To what extent do individuals perceive general or specific cognitive domain impairments?’ were posed in line with IPA guidelines, and the data subsequently interpreted to better understand how these subjective experiences fit with current theoretical understandings of BDD.

Materials and methods

Design

A qualitative research design, using IPA, [21, 22] similar to previous work in BDD was employed [14]. This enabled a contextualist approach to thematic analysis, in which individual and social meanings of responses were used to both reflect and understand subjects’ lived experience [21, 22]. The project was approved by Swinburne University’s Human Research Ethics Committees (20,247,235–17814) and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants and recruitment

Participants were recruited globally via the BDD Foundation (website and Instagram), social media (Tik Tok, Instagram, Reddit), Swinburne University’s Research Experience Program and Body Image Disorders Participant Registry, and an email campaign targeting clinics globally that specialise in BDD treatment. Eligibility required that participants were aged ≥ 18 years, were proficient in written English, and responded ‘yes’ to the question ‘do you have symptoms of body dysmorphic disorder?’ To support generalisability, no participants were excluded due to comorbidities. Participants were categorised as having been either clinically diagnosed with BDD, unsure of diagnosis (that is, individuals reported being unsure whether health practitioners had ‘officially’ diagnosed them) or have not received a diagnosis. Given the reported under-diagnosis of BDD, [23] the Dysmorphic Concern Questionnaire (DCQ; [24]) was used to establish severity of dysmorphic concern. In line with previous research, a very conservative DCQ score of ≥ 14 was determined as a cut-off for likely BDD diagnosis [25–27]. Thus, the ‘BDD group’ was formed by including all respondents who either reported a clinical diagnosis or had a DCQ score of ≥ 14.

Dysmorphic concern questionnaire

The seven-item self-report DCQ was used to measure body image concern severity [24]. Items are scored from 0 (not at all) to 3 (much more than most people) and summed item scores form total scores ranging between 0–21, with higher total scores denoting increased body image concern. The validity, reliability, and uni-dimensionality of the DCQ is cross-culturally established [28]. Discriminant and convergent validity with the Body Dysmorphic Disorder Diagnostic Module [29] and the Yale–Brown Obsessive–Compulsive Scale Modified for Body Dysmorphic Disorder [30] have been demonstrated, [27] and the DCQ showed good internal consistency over the total study sample (α = 0.74).

Procedure

Participants accessed an online Qualtrics survey, reviewed and completed the Participant Information and Consent Form, and were invited to complete a range of questionnaires covering the topics (a) demographic details; (b) diagnoses and treatments; (c) appearance concerns; and (d) experiences of everyday thinking skills. The data was unidentified, with no explicit identification information collected. Only group general demographics are reported. The survey lasted approximately 15 min and was conducted between October 2023 and August 2024.

Data analysis

All qualitative responses were downloaded from SPSS v29 to Microsoft Excel v16.87 and Microsoft Word v16.87 and analysed according to IPA methodology [21]. The IPA data analysis stream is depicted in Fig. 1. A detailed analysis of each participants’ response was conducted manually by two authors. KHàC is a cisgender, Caucasian woman and PhD candidate researching cognition in BDD. SLR is a cisgender, Caucasian woman and Professor of Neuropsychology with over 15 years’ experience working with individuals with BDD. TVR is a cisgender, Caucasian woman and Associate Professor with expertise related to cognition and psychiatric illness. KHàC initially coded all responses at a semantic level using Harvey’s [11] hierarchical model of cognition, which classifies attention, memory, and processing speed as lower-order domains, and executive function (including working memory) and social cognition as higher-order domains. Accordingly, difficulties were mapped onto the cognitive domains of attention, memory, processing speed, social cognition, executive function. SLR checked all responses and categorisation. Responses not fitting into these domains were re-evaluated by KHàC and SLR, leading to the creation of an additional ‘verbal communication’ code to capture difficulties initiating or maintaining conversations distinct from broader social cognition processes. Interpretive level analysis was used by KHàC to understand the implications of individuals’ statements, to identify patterns of convergence and divergence, and to generate an initial list of themes. Factors such as prevalence, level of meaning held for individuals, and richness or contribution to a narrative of cognitive experience were used to guide the development of themes. Following qualitative research guidelines, [31] a clean copy of responses was checked against the table of codes, themes and subthemes by SLR. Finally, both authors re-examined the themes, subthemes and codes to ensure that the cases were fully represented by them.Fig. 1IPA data analysis stream

Results

One thousand five hundred one individuals accessed and 659 responded to the survey. The final sample comprised 316 participants who endorsed experiencing cognitive difficulties (Question 1), answered Question 2, and were in the BDD group.

Themes, codes and key quotes

Table 2 displays key participant narrative responses according to subtheme.

Superordinate theme: cognitive experience of BDD

This theme represents an individual’s subjective experience of their thinking processes. It encompasses two subthemes; the first capturing the perception that BDD impacts cognition generally, and the second highlighting impairment in specific cognitive domains. Three hundred and thirteen individuals made comments relevant to this superordinate theme.

Subtheme: general impairments

The perception that cognition was generally impaired was commented upon by 142 individuals. They described their thinking skills as being “completely overtaken” (42) by their BDD, experiencing this as “non-stop” (304) and “constantly on my mind” (410), and rendering their functioning “outside of my control” (453) and “very difficult” (42). Some respondents hyper-fixated on irrelevant information, losing sight of the bigger picture, which further impaired their cognitive efficiency and effectiveness (619). The degree of general impairment was different for individuals. Some people felt “restricted” or “hampered” (183), describing a lower level, “back of my head” (155) impairment which impacted their cognitive abilities. Others described more intense impacts, experiencing “brain fog” (147, 332, 409, 410, 633), “fuzzy and white like static” (107), which paralysed their thinking in a general way. Others experienced this even more severely, describing “panic” (107), “freezing and dissociating” (56) “thoughts suddenly stop(ping)” (414) and “going blank” (410, 538), which prevented useful cognitive function completely. These general impacts were characterised as “exhausting and stressful” (147), leading to individuals feeling that they are “passive passengers” (453) of their cognition; they are “constantly fatigued” (633) and it is a “huge effort” (155) for them to think/function.

Subtheme: specific impairments

Attention

Attention, focus and preoccupation were the most prevalent specific cognitive domains implicated, with 173 referring to impairments in this domain. Many respondents explicitly referred to their BDD symptoms impacting their ability to concentrate and focus. Participants “struggled” (399) to concentrate in varied contexts, “feeling scatterbrained or bouncy” (323) and in a “loop” (5) about their bodies. Rumination on, and preoccupation with, ‘flaws’ were cited as key barriers to full attention; “it’s hard to concentrate when the feature I worry about is so huge in my head” (301). Varied types of attention were also mentioned, with participants referring to impacts on their “full” (108), sustained (323), selective (145), and divided attention (451).

Memory

Memory deficits were widely indicated, with 137 referring to this domain across all memory types. Episodic memory difficulties were described as “blackout memory” (654) for both recent and past events (524, 627). Participants described forgetting names, faces, words, objects, directions, instructions and important information such as birthdays and anniversaries (149, 163, 613, 618, 647).

Processing speed

Impaired processing was mentioned by 77 respondents. Participants felt that they had “difficulty” (171) thinking and were “slow” (141, 180, 390) to process requests and respond appropriately. Auditory and numeric processing were highlighted as specific areas of slow processing (279, 390, 606), and participants often referred to ‘brain or mental fog’ to characterise their difficulty in processing, reporting that they “can’t think fast and don’t like to be put on the spot” or “under pressure” (217, 483).

Visual perception

This domain was explicitly referred to by 12 respondents. Participants highlighted concerns with how they appear in mirrors and photos (283, 533, 653), with one reporting that they couldn’t “see myself” (653), being able to perceive shapes, but not recognising them as belonging to their own body (533). Another reported visual distortions such as stretching, warping and blurred images, causing “deep discomfort and motion-nausea” (533). Several participants described “attention to details” (596) such as “a 2 cm patch of cheek” (533), yet being unable to integrate multiple elements to perceive their entire face or body “as a whole” (533), losing sight of the “bigger picture” (619, 374). Participants were unconfident processing faces (348, 360, 533), harbouring a sense that they do not “see what other people see” (374, 533).

Verbal communication

Verbal communication impairments were raised by 63 respondents reporting difficulties across skills such as articulating, enunciating, initiating, and maintaining conversations. Participants reported difficulties “verbalizing thoughts” (627), “formulate sentences” (649), “articulating words in the moment” (589), “making a point as intended” (561), “trying to say one word and saying a completely random, wrong and unrelated word instead (606)”. Normally articulate individuals reported being so distracted by their body-related thoughts that they were “unable to communicate” (52, 649), struggling to remember the “right word” (305, 524) or find that “words losing all meaning…in the middle of conversation” (210) and “lose track” (98) of conversations, not “knowing when to talk in a conversation” (98) or were unable to “to process basic requests or questions” (210).

Social cognition

Ninety-eight participants described impairments in multiple subdomains of social cognition, including social perception, mentalising and emotion processing. They reported experiencing “social ineptitude” (404), being “socially tone-deaf” (62) to cues (55, 153, 231). Participants expressed their struggle to “interpret people's expressions” (657), misinterpreting faces as hostile (324), “fearing everyone is upset with me” (388). They “take things very literally” (659) and “react inappropriately to situations” (113), “misreading the room” (645), and are unable to “connect with” (645) and feel “misunderstood” by others (62) due to their BDD preoccupations. Uncertainty about how others perceived them was common (87, 335, 401, 637) with fear of negative evaluation prominent. One participant reported “when in social situations, my mind always feels like that other person is judging me.” (621).

Executive function

Experience of executive function impairments were reported by 169 respondents. Participants reported difficulties with cognitive inhibition, having problems resisting distraction (7, 185) and staying “on task” (149, 332). Cognitive flexibility impairments were revealed, with one participant stating “(I) just wish my brain would just let me think about other things” (29). Decision-making and planning were also often described as “problematic” (48) and “difficult” (310, 658). Working memory difficulties were expressed as being “unable to remember what I just set out to do” (606), or “forgetting things I have just said” (307).

Theme: functional impairment

A majority of participants (200) viewed themselves as functionally impaired by cognitive difficulties associated with their disorder. This was true both for everyday life and for critical tasks. Respondents’ daily thinking capacity was dominated by body concerns which prevented them from pursuing hobbies and work (29, 230, 483, 566, 621) and extended to sexual function (533). One participant expressed the crippling social impact of their disorder reporting “sometimes I get out of the wrong bus station because I can’t tell the conductor to stop” (395). Several participants reported “extreme indecision” (471, 478, 538) rendering them unable to complete critical tasks such as finding accommodation and paying bills (535). Others expressed disappointment at their diminished cognitive capacities to engage fully in conversations, careers and passions (98, 107, 313, 332). Numerous participants reported interference from obsessive thoughts resulting in the abandonment of education and other goals (487, 520, 561, 637).

Superordinate theme: relationship between cognitive impairment and BDD

Subtheme: cognition impacts BDD

Fifteen respondents expressed that cognitive difficulties negatively impacted how they view their bodies. They conceptualised their disorder as arising from or being maintained by their cognitive impairments, with one individual believing that an “inability to maintain focus (underlies) a negative spiral” (237). Another participant attributed distress about their body to faulty memory “I can't remember what my body looked like the day before, so I freak out because I feel like I got bigger (123)”. Attention (5, 237, 333, 399, 566, 592), visual perception (533) and social cognition (387) impairments were also implicated in underlying respondents’ appearance concerns.

BDD impacts cognition

Forty-four respondents made reference to their BDD symptoms negatively impacting their cognition, particularly on memory (183, 526, 649) and attention (56, 155, 308, 538). They reported that concern about their flaws dominate their minds, burdening their brains with an excessive cognitive load which leaves little room for other cognitive activities (155, 183, 410). Participants described BDD symptoms undermining their capabilities, leading to loss of focus and making it difficult to maintain their train of thought and engage effectively. One participant reported “if I let myself think about how I look for a second, I go completely blank and have a very hard time getting back on track” (538), and a highly educated and articulate respondent reported encountering no cognitive difficulties when not overwhelmed by BDD symptoms, yet experiencing significant memory and concentration problems, particularly in conversations, when preoccupied with body-related thoughts (649).

Superordinate theme: social and emotional impact of BDD-related cognitive difficulties

Subtheme: social impact

Social impacts of cognition in BDD were reported by 131 respondents, with participants highlighting that their cognitive difficulties are exacerbated in social contexts (56, 155, 183, 308, 378). They believed that others see them as “objectively unattractive” (533), “deformed… like a witch” (348), “abnormal” (560) and as “both really large and tall, as well as really malnourished and small” (533). One individual found it challenging to focus on conversations when others looked at their face (56), another described the negative impact of anxiety when in public on their memory (308). A preoccupying fear of being negatively judged by others inhibits participants’ engagement; “I feel if I don't look good enough, my opinion has little value” (183). These perceptions lead to a lack of confidence and social withdrawal (360, 378, 646).

Subtheme: emotional impact

Respondents underscored the profound emotional impacts of impaired cognition on individuals, with 123 reporting experiencing negative emotions. Anxiety, fear and despair were reported to underlie feelings of being rushed, distracted, and insecure at work (275, 378, 486). Participants expressed frustration at being unable to switch their focus away from their appearance-related thoughts (29, 107, 155) and reported short-tempered and aggressive behaviours, particularly when looking in mirrors (86, 288). A heightened sensitivity to others' behaviours, expressions, or words (222), a tendency to excessive worry, and overthinking others’ perceptions were pronounced (173, 308, 388, 645). Emotional responses varied, with some respondents describing feeling numb and disconnected from their bodies (386), while others describing themselves as “incredibly neurotic” (173), struggling between rational and emotional responses depending on their state of mind (589). A pervasive sense of loneliness and shame was revealed, with respondents feeling trapped in a loop of self-doubt, expressing concerns about their attractiveness and its impact on their social and romantic lives (236, 388, 393, 395, 409).

Theme: strategies

Participants’ responses revealed a variety of strategies employed by 12 individuals to manage their cognitive difficulties. Concentration and memory aids, such as note and list making, and fidget toys were reported by many (7, 44, 533), while slow and repetitive instruction helped to aid learning for others (45, 478). Mimicking others and overthinking were reported as unhelpful (464), whereas mental rehearsal and intense focus improved cognitive ability (7, 649). One participant suggested that calming techniques could help, but was unsuccessful in applying them (107), while nodding and pretending to understand conversations provided extra time to process information for another (478). One participant endorsed that engaging with their emotions helped their memory (430).

External support mechanisms reported were; assistance with “daily tasks like cooking and cleaning” (318); needing someone to snap them out of hyperfocus (141); and participation in support groups, such as those provided by the BDD Foundation, which helps practice distraction and promotes engagement in hobbies (301). Furthermore, medical treatments, such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors were reported as beneficial in improving mood, cognitive functioning, and overall perspective (287). Insight into the effects of the disorder was associated with participants’ recognition of their cognitive difficulties and potential openness to their remediation. One participant reported experiencing long-standing social cognition impairments but recognising their connection to BDD only in the “past few years” (143). Others recognised their false assumptions regarding others’ negative evaluation of them (38, 275), although this did not necessarily translate to improved social cognition (275).

Discussion

This study aimed to understand the subjective, lived cognitive experiences of those living with BDD. The analysis identified five superordinate themes: 1) cognitive experiences of BDD, 2) functional impairment, 3) relationship between cognitive impairment and BDD, 4) social and emotional impacts, and 5) strategies.

The first theme, cognitive experiences of BDD, summarised what respondents think about their cognition. Participants strongly identified experiencing their cognition as generally compromised. They viewed themselves as individuals whose lives are impaired by their diminished thinking abilities, which were reported as ranging from lower level to constant and overwhelming. This subjective perception of global cognitive deficit aligns with empirical evidence showing objective global cognitive impairment in BDD [9, 10] and the range of responses echo the Malcolm et al [9] finding of cognitive subgroups (mildly and broadly cognitively impaired). While these subjective responses show more frequent severe general cognitive functional impairment than demonstrated in the Malcolm et al [9] study, it is possible that the study design, in which individuals chose to respond to the open-ended question, reflected the experiences of individuals who experience broad cognitive impairments more acutely. However, the number and intensity of responses speak to the importance of the experience of cognitive impairments in BDD. The findings strongly resonate with the cognitive-behavioural model of BDD in which disturbed cognition is posited to impact individuals’ perceptions of attractiveness [6, 7]. Consistent with previous qualitative literature revealing that individuals with BDD consider themselves to be “fundamentally flawed” [13] p8, respondents in the current study perceived their thinking skills to be negatively impacted in an overall sense, representing a domain-global cognitive dysfunction affecting all aspects of their lives.

Respondents also commonly endorsed having impairments in specific domains, notably (in order of prevalence) in attention, executive function, memory, social cognition, processing speed, verbal communication and visual perception. The comments pertaining to visual perception were more consistently specifically driven by core BDD symptomology than other domains. Subjective attentional impairments align to the notion that attentional biases are critical to the aetiology and maintenance of BDD, [7] with preoccupation being a core element of BDD’s classification as an obsessive–compulsive related disorder in the DSM-5 [2]. The finding of subjective cognitive impairments in attention and other specific domains also align with clinical observations and objective neurocognitive and neurobiological research in BDD that demonstrates reduced attention, [32] memory, [15] executive function, [33] social cognition [34] and processing speed [10] performance. However, a recent systematic review of cognition in BDD demonstrated that objective cognitive impairments are more consistently found in the higher-level domains of executive function and social cognition [12]. Yet, the current study showed that people with BDD perceived the lower-order cognitive domains of attention and memory as more subjectively problematic. It is possible these findings may represent a true dissociation between objective and subjective cognitive problems. An alternative explanation, however, is that attention and memory problems are just more easily recognised by the layperson and may thus more readily come to mind [35].

The second theme highlighted widespread subjective recognition of functional impairments arising from impaired cognition, as demonstrated for both every day and critical tasks and underscoring the extensive real-world impact of BDD on individuals. Cognition is critical in relation to psychosocial functioning. Study respondents recognised this in relation to their experience of BDD, and the literature has demonstrated poor psychosocial functioning in individuals with the disorder, impacting employment, educational, household, interpersonal relations, recreation, and satisfaction [5].

Theme three summarised respondents’ perception of the relationship between cognitive impairment and BDD. While they expressed the view that cognition impacts BDD, more frequently respondents experienced their BDD impacting cognition. These findings support the conceptualisation of cognitive distortions acting in a negative feedback loop in BDD, [12] as well as revealing differing perceptions among individuals regarding what is underlying their condition. These differences may have clinical implications; individuals who perceive their disorder to be ‘caused’ by poor cognitive skills may be more open-minded towards cognitive remediation than individuals who believe their cognitive difficulties are secondary to their disorder. Direct questioning regarding willingness to undertake specific cognitive skill remediation is warranted.

The theme of social and emotional impacts characterised the profound impact of cognition in BDD on individuals' emotions and their interactions with others. For many respondents, the social elements of their BDD-related cognitive difficulties were core to their dysfunction and impairment. The exacerbation in social contexts of a persistent and crippling fear of negative evaluation expressed by respondents highlights the key relevance of this element to individuals’ experience. Emotional impacts were also commonly reported, with fear, anxiety, despair, shame, loneliness prominent and social withdrawal quite pronounced. The frequency of respondents indicating social and emotional consequences due to their cognitive impairment was striking and impacted individuals' self-perception, confidence, and social interactions, leading to a cycle of withdrawal and reinforcement of their negative self-image. The significance of these emotions to individuals with BDD is widely acknowledged in the literature, however their relationship with subjective cognitive difficulties is novel.

Finally, the theme of strategies illustrates the various ways individuals cope with the cognitive challenges posed by BDD. Respondents revealed that they incorporate behavioural adjustments, external support, and personal insights into their daily lives. Although the proportion of respondents indicating their use of coping strategies was relatively small, it reflected their need to manage the cognitive impacts of BDD and potentially a willingness to seek help.

Clinical implications

The results offer key insights for clinicians working with BDD. Asking about cognition during assessment can help validate patients’ concerns and identify where scaffolding or allied professional support (e.g., occupational therapy) may be needed. Participants also reported using strategies such as memory aids and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor medications to manage cognitive difficulties, suggesting practical avenues for intervention.

Our findings also point to possible discrepancies between subjective and objective impairments, as seen in related disorders where individuals often report greater subjective than objectively measured deficits. In BDD, this may reflect self-critical tendencies and overemphasis on perceived problems. Psychoeducation clarifying the difference between actual and perceived cognitive performance, together with approaches fostering self-compassion and metacognitive awareness, could help reduce distress, support engagement with therapy, and improve functional outcomes.

Limitations

While the study benefitted from a large sample which resulted in many responses, participants were individuals who self-selected and responded to a study advertisement. This may have resulted in a greater likelihood of individuals who experience cognitive challenges completing the survey. On the other hand, those who were overwhelmed by their cognitive difficulties or had poor insight may not have been able to complete it. In fact, some participants did comment that the survey was difficult for them to complete. Thus, the sample was comprised of individuals who self-reported experiencing BDD symptoms, endorsed experiencing cognitive difficulties, and who chose to answer the single open-ended question which did not allow for follow-up questions. The unrestricted nature of the single question, however, did provide opportunity for individuals to fully express their experiences, and many of the responses provided detailed descriptions regarding the severity of their dysfunction. We acknowledge the inherent limitations of self-reported data, but since examining subjective cognition requires individuals to report their experiences, this approach remains essential. Previous research has shown that people are quite reliable in being able to self-report cognitive difficulties in related disorders [36].

While notably large for BDD research, the sample comprised a majority of educated Western women. Even participants who were confident that they had been diagnosed with BDD by their clinical team (N = 78) did not have a formal clinical research interview to confirm their diagnosis. Instead, the research team elected to include participants who acknowledged they had dysmorphic concerns and scored over a conservative threshold on the DCQ, suggesting a significant likelihood that they would indeed have a BDD diagnosis. The size of the group and the consistency of the findings across the group, counteract this limitation. The sample also reported a significant number of comorbidities, some of which may influence individuals’ cognitive experiences. The decision was made not to exclude individuals with comorbidities with known cognitive challenges as BDD is a complex disorder and individuals with BDD are known to have multiple comorbidities, commonly including depression and anxiety. While the influence of these other diagnostic conditions is difficult to determine in the current sample, attempting to obtain a ‘pure’ BDD sample is both unrealistic and invalid.

Future directions

Future research should confirm the current findings in a group who have undergone a structured clinical interview confirming diagnosis and comorbidities, so that both unique and widespread cognitive difficulties in relation to BDD and the comorbidities can be disentangled. The effect of comorbidities, particularly of conditions with known cognitive impacts, on cognition in individuals with BDD may be significant. A study design which could enable follow up questions regarding the frequency and severity of cognitive difficulties could help to further understand their impact on individuals with BDD. It is recommended that future research employ standardised assessment tools to enable comparison of metacognitive ability across tasks, compare subjective and objective measures of cognition in BDD to see if they align, determine the relationship between perception of cognition, cognitive confidence and objective performance, and explore whether subjective cognition assessment can assist clinicians to better prepare and deliver cognitive remediation.

Conclusion

In summary, we examined a large sample of qualitative accounts of everyday, subjective cognitive experiences in BDD. Overall, participants expressed a sense of diminished cognitive capacities, commonly attributing these to a preoccupation with thinking about appearance, to the detriment of their daily functioning and personal fulfilment. The findings underscore the importance of cognitive impairments in the overall profile of BDD and reveal the following: i) a considerable number of individuals with BDD perceive disturbed cognition as relating to their disorder; ii) some perceive themselves as ‘cognitively, generally flawed’, and many recognise impairments across multiple specific cognitive domains; and iii) the perceived social and emotional consequences of impaired cognition in BDD are pronounced. These deeply affect respondents' ability to engage with their environment, participate in meaningful discussions, and maintain a balanced perspective, leading to significant disruptions in their daily lives and cognitive functioning. Specific questioning of cognitive experiences should form part of patient assessments.
