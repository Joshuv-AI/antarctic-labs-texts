Acquired Blastomycosis infection in a patient with prior Roux-en-Y bariatric surgery: A case report
Authors: Brittany Love, Richard G Jung, Adhora Mir, Deborah O’Keefe, M Fahad Chowdhury, Chloé Thabet, Michael Quon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBlastomycosis is a fungal infection endemic to parts of North America, often presenting with subtle respiratory symptoms but capable of affecting multiple organ systems. We describe a 41-year-old woman with a history of Roux-en-Y gastric bypass who presented with non-specific gastrointestinal complaints and severe nutritional deficiencies. Imaging revealed cavitary lesions in the lungs, and bronchoscopy confirmed Blastomyces sp. infection. She improved with prolonged itraconazole therapy. In patients with severe malnutrition leading to immunosuppression, especially in endemic areas, clinicians should consider blastomycosis in the differential diagnosis when subtle respiratory symptoms are presents to facilitate timely diagnosis and treatment.Keywords: Blastomycosis, Malnutrition, Bariatric, Immunosuppression, Respiratory

Introduction

Blastomyces spp. is a dimorphic fungus primarily found in soil or decomposing organic matter that is endemic to the regions that border the Ohio, Mississippi, St. Lawrence River Valleys, and the Great Lakes region in North America [1]. Blastomycosis, the infection caused by Blastomyces spp., is generally caused by inhalation of fungal spores and presents with a wide range of clinical manifestations of varying severity [2]. The most common symptoms are respiratory in nature, where patients can present with flu-like symptoms including constitutional symptoms, cough, myalgias, and joint pain. Extrapulmonary disease can occur, with the most common form being cutaneous blastomycosis [3]. Symptoms are typically vague leading to delays in diagnosis and treatment, as well as multiple failed courses of antibiotics. Aside from environmental exposures or residence in endemic regions, few clues exist to raise clinical suspicion for blastomycosis. Routine diagnostic tests, such as Blastomyces culture from bronchoscopy or tissue samples, are definitive; however, colony growth may take several weeks, often resulting in delayed diagnosis. Histopathologic examination is faster but may lack the sensitivity required to confirm blastomycosis. Similarly, while Blastomyces antigen specific testing offers a relatively quick turnaround, its sensitivity remains limited [3]. This case highlights how nutritional deficiency associated immunosuppression from a prior gastric bypass may raise suspicion for blastomycosis in patients with subtle respiratory symptoms.

Case

A 41-year-old woman with a history of Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (2019), idiopathic intracranial hypertension with a ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and iron deficiency anemia presented to the emergency department (day 0) with a 3-month history of progressive generalized weakness, postprandial nausea/vomiting, and poor oral intake. She reported intermittent diarrhea over three months, worsening over the past two weeks to 5–10 episodes of watery, non-bloody diarrhea daily. There were no associated fevers, night sweats, new rashes or headaches. Her weight was 90 lbs (from 130 lbs previously; 30 % body weight loss) and was struggling to ambulate independently due to weakness.

She also endorsed a several-month history of a mild, intermittent productive cough with yellow sputum. The rest of her infectious review of systems was unremarkable. She had no history of fevers.

She resides in Ottawa, Canada, near the Rideau River and frequently walks in local parks. She had no known infectious contacts, or any known contacts for blastomycosis. She did not handle soil and denied recent travel or significant animal exposures and lived in an urban area not directly adjacent to a body of water.

On physical examination, the patient appeared cachectic. She had bilateral lower limb weakness (3/5 in hip flexors, 4/5 ankle dorsiflexion) and 2+ bilateral peripheral edema. She had two dental fractures. Otherwise, her cardiac, respiratory and abdominal exams were unremarkable. Initial laboratory investigations are summarized in Table 1. Anti-transglutaminase antibodies, Astrovirus, Norovirus, Adenovirus, Sapovirus, Rotavirus, Coronavirus disease 2019, Influenza A and B, and Respiratory Syncytial Virus and Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) tests were all negative. She was found to be malnourished, with severe vitamin A, C and D deficiency.

Bariatric surgery consultants found no anatomical cause to explain her symptoms. Given her history of a VP shunt and presentation of nausea and vomiting, a computed tomography scan of her (CT) head was performed and showed a stable shunt, unchanged small ventricular size, and no hydrocephalus. A CT of the abdomen and pelvis was unremarkable except for ground-glass opacities and an area of consolidation in the left lung base. A chest X-ray revealed multifocal consolidations, most prominent in the right upper and left lower lobes (Fig. 1). A CT chest demonstrated multiple bilateral cavitary lesions within upper lobe predominance (Fig. 2), along with bronchiolitis and bronchopneumonia in both lower lobes. She was admitted on January 31, 2023, (day 0), for further investigation and nutritional support.

Blood cultures drawn during admission were negative. An echocardiogram showed no vegetations and normal valve function. To evaluate the cavitary lung lesions, bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was performed. The BAL was obscured by acute and chronic inflammatory cells and pulmonary macrophages. The fungal smear showed yeast cells consistent with Blastomyces species. Cultures from the right upper lobe later confirmed Blastomyces dermatitidis/gilchristii and co-infection with methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA). Antigen testing for Blastomyces in urine, blood, or BAL fluid was not sent. A brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) ruled out central nervous system involvement, with no abnormal enhancement or masses. Serum immunoglobulins, checked 6 months later, were normal. Flow cytometry, purified protein derivative (PPD) and QuantiFERON gold testing were not performed.

Neurology was consulted for her weakness. Her examination suggested disuse atrophy secondary to weight loss, with no central neurologic process. A lumbar puncture and electrodiagnostic testing were not pursued. Gastroenterology was also consulted to investigate her nausea, diarrhea, vomiting and weight loss. An upper endoscopy showed a small, deep oozing ulcer at the gastrojejunostomy site, grade B esophagitis, and a Schatzki ring at the gastroesophageal junction. There was active bleeding from a visible vessel which was cauterized and likely contributing to her anemia. Jejunal biopsies were histologically normal. Her colonoscopy was endoscopically normal, with terminal ileum and colonic biopsies also showing normal tissue histology.

During hospitalization, the patient was treated for blastomycosis with oral itraconazole – the first line agent for non-CNS disease. Given her concurrent use of pantoprazole – a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) known to reduce itraconazole capsule absorption – the suspension formulation was selected. It has greater bioavailability and less interaction with PPIs. The suspension formulation was also chosen over capsule as absorption of itraconazole is unclear in the setting of a prior RYGB. Itraconazole levels were regularly monitored to ensure adequate absorption, and dosing was optimized to be taken on an empty stomach (i.e. 1 h before or 2 h after meals).

Her MSSA co-infection was treated with IV cefazolin (2 g q8h, Feb 2–14 corresponding to day 10–22), followed by oral cephalexin (500 mg QID, Feb 14–28, corresponding to day 22–36). This duration was chosen to treat a complicated pneumonia with favorable clinical evolution and no other evidence of deep-seated infection identified. For severe malnutrition, she was started on total parenteral nutrition (TPN), electrolyte repletion, and vitamins A,C, and D. She responded well to antifungal and antibiotic therapy. With nutritional support, her vomiting and diarrhea resolved, and she was weaned off TPN prior to discharge. She was discharged with ongoing itraconazole (200 mg oral suspension BID for six months), vitamin supplementation, and follow-up for drug level monitoring. Because the diagnosis was confirmed through culture, and no rapid in-house assay for Blastomyces antigen testing is available, antigen levels are not routinely monitored at our center as a marker of therapeutic response.

Prior to discharge, her itraconazole level was 1.64μg/mL. At our institution, levels > 0.5μg/mL are considered therapeutic. The itraconazole solution was covered through the Ontario Trillium Drug Program for high prescription drug costs. She remained on pantoprazole 40 mg oral twice daily.

Five weeks later, she was briefly re-hospitalized with nausea, vomiting, and weakness. Her itraconazole level was low (<0.20μg/mL), likely due to a combination of vomiting and intermittent oral intake. It is possible that due to sporadic oral intake, her dose was not reliably taken in a fasting state. Her dose was increased to 300 mg TID for 3 days, then 300 mg BID. She was subsequently followed by the Infectious Diseases clinic, though communication challenges delayed follow up and therapeutic drug monitoring. Between July and September 2023, her itraconazole levels remained subtherapeutic. At her August 2023 visit, despite low levels, she showed ongoing clinical improvement, and the current dose was continued with plans for repeat levels.

After six months of treatment, she reported improved energy, appetite, modest weight gain, and full resolution of gastrointestinal symptoms. A repeat CT scan of the chest on September 1, 2023, showed near-complete resolution of prior pulmonary disease, with only minimal scarring and mucoid impaction. Her itraconazole was discontinued at that time.

Discussion

Blastomycosis can be notoriously difficult to diagnose as it resembles conditions such as tuberculosis, malignancy, or bacterial pneumonia due to its vague respiratory and constitutional symptoms [3]. It remains rare, with an incidence of 0.4–1.3 cases per 100,000 annually in endemic areas [4] and roughly 50 % of cases are asymptomatic [5]. Symptom onset can occur from 2 weeks to four months from inhalation of spores further contributing to diagnostic uncertainty and delays in treatment [6]. Most commonly, blastomycosis will cause pulmonary and dermatologic symptoms; however, there is a wide spectrum of clinical manifestations including neurological, genitourinary and gastrointestinal which also makes diagnosis challenging [7].

While both immunocompetent and immunocompromised individuals may be affected, immunocompromised patients are more likely to develop severe disease, complications, and mortality [8]. A retrospective study performed in 2021 revealed that individuals with acquired immunodeficiency were at a greater risk for developing severe symptoms and have a higher risk of both complications and mortality [8]. A systematic review by Carignan et al. [9] similarly found an increase in the severity of cases of blastomycosis and an increase in the proportion of immunocompromised hosts over the last 30 years, indicating there may be an association between immunosuppression and severity of disease.

In this report, we describe a case of symptomatic blastomycosis in a malnourished patient with a prior RYGB. Although there are no prior studies demonstrating a specific link between malnutrition and blastomycosis, the association between malnutrition and immunosuppression is well established. Vitamin D deficiency has been associated with increased respiratory tract infections [10] and malnutrition has been associated with an increased risk of acquisition and greater severity of infections from other fungal organisms such as: Candidiasis [11], pneumocystis [12], aspergillosis [13] and histoplasmosis [14]. A recent case series described two patients with pulmonary blastomycosis who were obese and severely vitamin D deficient [15]. The authors proposed that obesity-related vitamin D deficiency may contribute to disease severity. Vitamin D plays a key role in immune regulation by modulating innate and adaptive responses, promoting antimicrobial peptide production, and controlling inflammation [15]. Together, these factors suggest that obesity and vitamin D deficiency may predispose individuals to more severe manifestations of blastomycosis. In contrast, our case highlights a patient with severe malnutrition and multiple micronutrient deficiencies, including vitamin D, following Roux-en-Y gastric bypass. Despite differing nutritional states, both reports highlight the potential importance of vitamin D deficiency as a common pathway influencing disease severity. Given the limitations of case reports, however, prospective studies are required to clarify whether vitamin D status independently modifies risk or severity, and whether vitamin D repletion alters outcomes.

In our case, malnutrition likely contributed to her immunocompromised state, based on the overall clinical presentation and no other immunodeficiency noted. IgG levels taken 6 months later were normal which may have improved with adequate nutrition. Alternatively, it is worth considering that her malnutrition may have been driven by her subacute to chronic blastomycosis infection. Unfortunately, nutritional markers prior to her presentation were unavailable to assess the chronicity of her malnutrition. The patient’s gastrointestinal symptoms were likely multifactorial – related to vitamin deficiencies, malnutrition, esophagitis and ulcerative disease, as gastrointestinal blastomycosis is rare. Her symptoms were consistent with those associated with vitamin A deficiency (impaired nocturnal vision), vitamin C deficiency (myalgia, weakness, fatigue, GI bleed) and vitamin D deficiency (hair thinning, bone pain, and fractured teeth). Her selenium levels were elevated, contrarily to what is commonly seen in patients with RYGB can present [16]. Selenium toxicity, which can present with diarrhea, fatigue, and hair loss, may have contributed to her symptoms, however her levels later normalized without intervention [17].

Blastomycosis is more prevalent in certain immunocompromised states such as patients with solid organ transplants [18]. While the patient lived in Ontario, an endemic region for Blastomyces, she did not reside in a geographical hotspot as per epidemiological analyses done by Brown et al. [19]. She did not report common exposures such as soil disruption, pets or travel to wooded areas. To our knowledge, no studies have specifically examined the incidence of blastomycosis infection in patients with prior bariatric bypass surgery. Studies have described significant changes in fungal and bacterial microbiota following RYGB, but further studies are required to ascertain the impact of this on developing future fungal infections [20]. A single centre case series reported on three cases of fungal infections in patients post jejunoileal bypass surgery, however only one case involved blastomycosis [21]. They postulated that patients with rapid weight loss post-surgery were at higher risk of fungal infections such as blastomycosis.

Together, it is possible that her prior RYGB and nutritional deficiencies contributed to her presentation with symptomatic blastomycosis. Further studies are required to establish a link between severe malnutrition, bariatric bypass surgery and susceptibility to blastomycosis infection. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for blastomycosis, even in the absence of typical risk factors. Lastly, this case also illustrates successful treatment with itraconazole suspension despite numerous factors impairing gastrointestinal absorption.
