Cost-benefit analysis of an AI-driven operational digital platform for integrated electric mobility, renewable energy, and grid management Journal: Applied Energy
Arega Getaneh Abate, Xiao-Bing Zhang, Xiufeng Liu, Dogan Keles

Abstract
Integrating electric mobility (electric vehicles (EVs), electric trucks (ETs)) and renewable energy sources (RES) with the power grid is paramount for achieving decarbonization, efficiency, and stability. Given the rapid growth of decentralized technologies and their critical role in decarbonization, two critical challenges emerge: first , the development of a digital platform for operational coordination; and second , rigorous research into their cost–benefit profile. This paper addresses this by presenting a comprehensive cost–benefit analysis (CBA) of an AI-driven operational digital platform (ODP) designed for holistic, cross-sectoral optimization. The ODP aims to enhance energy efficiency, grid reliability, and environmental sustainability. A seven-step CBA framework, aligned with EU guidelines, quantifies economic, reliability, and environmental benefits against capital and operational expenditures, explicitly linking benefit magnitude to AI-driven ODP and optimization efficiencies, such as quantified improvements in market arbitrage from ODP, enabled forecasting, and enhanced operational efficiencies across various services. The framework is illustrated with case studies from Austria, Hungary, and Slovenia. Results show a robust benefit-cost ratio (BCR) of approximately 1.41 and a net present value (NPV) exceeding €356 million over a ten-year operational period (2026–2035). Extensive sensitivity analyses, including discount-rate variability and Monte Carlo simulations on BCR and NPV, confirm the framework’s robustness. This research underscores AI-powered integration as critical for achieving decarbonization and efficiency goals in the evolving energy and transport sectors.

Introduction

The European Union (EU) is undergoing a profound transformation towards sustainable energy and transport systems, characterized by the accelerated adoption of electric vehicles (EVs), electric trucks (ETs), and an increasing share of renewable energy sources (RES) in the power grid

Psarros and Papathanassiou 2023
]

These include managing the spatiotemporal mismatch between variable renewable supply and dynamic charging demand, enhancing grid flexibility to accommodate new, potentially high-impact loads, and overcoming the lack of coordinated platforms for holistic management across the energy and transport sectors. Traditional approaches often operate in silos, leading to suboptimal asset utilization, grid congestion, curtailment of renewable energy, and underutilization of emerging flexibility resources.

The challenge becomes more pronounced for heavy-duty vehicles. Studies have shown that battery-electric propulsion is technically viable but economically constrained for long-haul freight applications due to battery weight, range limitations, and charging infrastructure gaps
[
Çabukoglu et al. 2018
,
Cunanan et al. 2021

Deshpande et al. 2023

Börjesson and Proost 2025
emphasizes that without coordination, smaller countries may opt out of ERS investments, even if the overall EU welfare would benefit from collaboration, underscoring the need for integrated digital solutions that can bridge national and sectoral divides.

Cost-benefit analysis (CBA) offers a systematic framework for evaluating the economic, financial, and societal viability of complex integrated solutions like the one proposed herein. In European projects, standardized CBA methodologies have been developed to assess smart grid and sustainable energy projects. Notably, the European Commission Joint Research Centre (EC JRC)

However, applying these CBA frameworks to comprehensively evaluate platforms that simultaneously manage energy and transport systems, particularly those leveraging advanced AI across multiple sectors and national borders, requires careful and detailed consideration of unique benefit streams and cost structures. While existing frameworks provide a robust foundation, their application must rigorously quantify the specific contributions of AI functionalities and cross-sectoral synergies. Even recent evaluations of integrated e-mobility systems often remain confined to national boundaries or siloed sectors

In this work, we propose a CBA framework to assess an AI-driven operational digital platform (ODP) that integrates EVs/ETs, RES, and the grid. While many platforms focus on specific aspects such as EV smart charging

Schott et al. 2019

or RES forecasting

Specifically, this integrated management, orchestrated by the ODP’s AI engine is designed to boost grid flexibility by dynamically aligning EV/ET charging patterns with RES availability, improve overall system reliability through predictive load balancing and congestion management, and yield substantial economic benefits via optimized energy procurement, trading, operational cost reductions, and potentially extended EV/ET battery lifespans. To quantify these outcomes, we apply a seven-step CBA framework, thoroughly informed by

and
Stromsather et al. 2018

Our application of this framework emphasizes the careful quantification of benefits directly attributable to the ODP’s capabilities (e.g., enhanced forecasting accuracy, superior optimization outcomes) and the synergistic effects of cross-sectoral integration, ensuring that AI-specific development, operational costs, and performance variabilities are explicitly addressed. This detailed application ensures alignment with EU best practices while providing a thorough assessment of this specific AI-driven, multi-sector and cross-border platform. We illustrate the framework to a comprehensive
ODP deployments in Austria, Hungary, and Slovenia, comparing quantified benefits against investment and operational expenditures.

The main contributions of this paper are:

i)

Proposing a seven-step CBA framework to evaluate an AI-driven cross-sector ODP, explicitly identifying and monetizing AI-specific cost drivers (e.g., predictive analytics, optimization) and quantifying AI-driven benefit improvements (such as short-term RES forecasting and EV/ET charging optimization) as they propagate through the integrated transport–energy system.

ii)

Demonstrating a multi-layered, AI-driven ODP architecture by enumerating its core AI modules for RES forecasting, battery-health–aware EV charging, E-VRPTW routing for electric trucks, and co-optimization of grid ancillary services, and showing how verifiable performance metrics (e.g., improvement in price forecasting, reduction in battery degradation). This helps quantify economic, reliability, and environmental benefits, while addressing large-scale computational considerations (e.g., real-time neural network inference and parallelized mixed-integer programming).

Applying the proposed framework to real-world case studies in Austria, Hungary and Slovenia by utilizing country specific input data and projections (CAPEX, OPEX, RES capacity factors, EV/ET fleet sizes and their projections, and wholesale price time series) and computing present-value streams for each benefit (economic, reliability and environmental) alongside CAPEX/OPEX.

iv)

Deriving actionable policy and managerial insights (e.g., data-infrastructure requirements for 2026–2035, applicability to other EU Member States, and critical success factors such as interoperability standards and regulatory incentives for EV/ET aggregation) to inform Fit for 55 and REPowerEU objectives.

Kaushik et al. 2022

Focusing specifically on electric mobility, the systematic reviews by

The anecdotal analyses of AI techniques, by enhancing EV performance and battery health, directly underpin the ODP’s smart charging functionalities and logistics optimization modules, which notably incorporate considerations for battery degradation. The emphasis in their review on real-time adaptation to dynamic driving conditions and charging availability further informs the ODP’s AI-driven routing and intelligent charging strategies, aiming to maximize user benefits and overall system efficiency.

The challenge of integrating large-scale RES into existing energy systems is another critical focus area where AI demonstrates considerable promise. Ref.
Liu et al. 2022
investigates AI’s pivotal role in managing RES within multi-energy systems, particularly for achieving carbon neutrality. They emphasize AI’s capacity for intelligent operational control, achieved through precise forecasting of RES generation and effective demand-response strategies. This aligns closely with the ODP’s objective of synchronizing EV/ET charging demand with intermittent RES availability, thereby minimizing curtailment and maximizing the utilization of clean energy. The practical applications and identified limitations discussed by

The
Perception layer
is responsible for real-time data acquisition from a multitude of distributed assets, including EVs, ETs, RES installations (solar PV, wind turbines), charging infrastructure, and environmental sensors. This layer employs various sensors (BMS data, GPS, smart meters, pyranometers, anemometers) and communication technologies (5G, LTE-M, LoRaWAN) to ensure robust and timely data transmission. Data is collected at granular resolutions (e.g., 1-minute intervals for critical parameters). The
Data layer
manages the ingestion, processing, storage, and secure handling of these voluminous and heterogeneous data streams. It leverages communication protocols (MQTT, OPC-UA), message queuing systems (Apache Kafka), and a hybrid data storage architecture comprising time-series databases (InfluxDB, TimescaleDB) for operational data and data lakes (e.g., Azure Data Lake) for large-scale analytics and AI model training. Data quality validation, anomaly detection, and missing data imputation are integrated processes within this layer to ensure robust inputs for the AI engine.

The
Middleware layer
provides crucial interoperability services, enabling seamless integration with existing utility systems (SCADA/DMS/EMS), energy market platforms (e.g., EPEX SPOT, Nord Pool APIs), and emerging EU data spaces (e.g., Gaia-X, IDS connectors). It utilizes enterprise integration patterns, API management gateways, and connectors designed for specific data exchange standards (e.g., ENTSO-E data models, Open Charging Protocols). This layer ensures secure, standardized, and real-time data flow necessary for cross-sectoral coordination.

The
Business layer
constitutes the AI engine core of the ODP, housing the advanced algorithms and models that drive intelligent decision-making. Key AI components include:

Forecasting models
: Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) based Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), enhanced with multi-head attention mechanisms, are employed for forecasting EV/ET charging demand, RES generation, and grid load at various temporal and spatial resolutions (e.g., 15-minute intervals up to 72 hours ahead). These models capture complex temporal dependencies and non-linear relationships inherent in energy data, using inputs like historical load/generation, high-resolution weather forecasts (temperature, irradiance, wind speed, cloud cover from numerical weather prediction models and local sensors), calendar effects (day-of-week, holidays), and real-time market signals. Model training is performed on extensive historical datasets (e.g., 2-3 years of data from ENTSO-E, national TSOs/DSOs, and representative EV/ET usage profiles) using scalable cloud-based GPU resources. Performance is continuously validated through rolling-window cross-validation and backtesting against established baseline methods (e.g., ARIMA, persistence), achieving a target Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) below 5% for day-ahead RES and load forecasts, demonstrating a significant uplift in predictive power essential for subsequent optimization steps. Energy price forecasting models, potentially hybrid approaches combining statistical models (e.g., ARIMA-GARCH) with machine learning for residual analysis, are also developed to predict day-ahead and intra-day electricity prices.

EV/ET smart charging and V2X dispatch
: mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) or mixed-integer quadratic programming (MIQP) formulations optimize charging/discharging schedules for individual vehicles and aggregated fleets. The objective functions are multi-faceted: minimize total energy cost for users, maximize self-consumption of local RES, minimize carbon emissions, reduce peak grid load, and critically, promote battery longevity. Battery degradation is explicitly incorporated into the objective by penalizing charging strategies known to accelerate degradation(e.g., frequent deep discharges, high state-of-charge (SoC) extremes, high C-rates). For instance, additional cost term
C_{\text{deg}}\times f_{\text{deg}}(\text{SoC},P_{\text{charge}})
is included, where
f_{\text{deg}}
represents a battery aging model based on SoC range, C-rates, and temperature, informed by established research
[
Maheshwari et al. 2020
,
Curry et al. 2023
,
Pacific Northwest National Laboratory
(2022) PNNL
]
. The AI optimizer thus actively minimizes this degradation cost as part of its multi-objective optimization. Constraints include battery SoC limits (
SoC_{\text{min}}\leq SoC_{t}\leq SoC_{\text{max}}
), user-defined departure times and required SoC targets, maximum charging/discharging rates, local grid capacity limits, charger availability (from Perception layer), and electricity tariff structures. Commercial solvers like CPLEX or Gurobi are employed via Python APIs. For very large problem instances (thousands of vehicles), decomposition techniques (e.g., column generation) or distributed optimization methods ensure solutions within operational timeframes (minutes for day-ahead scheduling, seconds for real-time adjustments). In addition, V2G/V2B services are managed by reinforcement learning (RL) agents (e.g., Deep Q-Networks or Proximal Policy Optimization). These agents are trained on high-fidelity simulations of real-time energy markets (reflecting bidding rules, settlement periods for frequency regulation services) and grid conditions. The RL reward function incentivizes revenue from ancillary services while safeguarding battery health and user mobility needs. Simulations incorporate historical market data, realistic network constraints, and stochastic elements to approximate real-world uncertainty.

ET route and logistics optimization
: Sophisticated vehicle routing problem (VRP) algorithms, specifically tailored for electric fleets (E-VRPTW – Electric VRP with time windows and charging stops), are implemented. These algorithms optimize routes to minimize total operational cost (combining energy consumption, time, and potential battery degradation costs) while considering real-time traffic conditions, terrain (for energy consumption estimation), payload, dynamic weather conditions, charging station availability, charging duration, and potential queuing times at charging points. The integration with battery degradation models ensures that routing decisions do not inadvertently lead to accelerated battery wear. Algorithms are based on advanced metaheuristics (e.g., tabu search, genetic algorithms) or commercial solvers (e.g., Google OR-Tools). The performance is evaluated by metrics such as route efficiency (km/kWh), total travel time, and number of required charging stops, compared to static or less intelligent routing systems.

Demand response (DR) management
: AI-driven tools automate the identification and aggregation of flexible EV/ET loads, predict their response potential based on historical patterns and current vehicle states, and manage their participation in DR events or ancillary service markets. This adheres to standards like OpenADR 2.0b. The system uses AI-driven baseline calculation methods to accurately measure DR event effectiveness and optimize dispatch signals to individual vehicles or aggregated charging stations.

These AI models are developed using frameworks like TensorFlow, Keras, and PyTorch, integrating with optimization solvers such as CPLEX or Gurobi via Python APIs. Model training utilizes high-quality historical and real-time data from the Data layer. Crucially, the ODP incorporates continuous operational monitoring, performance validation metrics (e.g., forecast accuracy, optimization solution quality, solver runtime), and scheduled retraining pipelines to proactively address potential data drift, concept drift, or model degradation over time, ensuring sustained AI model efficacy and robust decision-making. Cloud-native scalable infrastructure supports the computational demands of large-scale AI model training and real-time inference.

Finally, the
Application layer
delivers user-facing interfaces and services. This includes intuitive mobile and web applications for EV/ET drivers and fleet managers (e.g., charge planning, station booking, real-time route guidance with charging recommendations), as well as comprehensive analytics dashboards and administrative consoles for system operators, charging point operators (CPOs), and energy providers. These dashboards provide transparent insights into system performance, AI model outputs, and optimization outcomes.
This layered, AI-centric architecture allows the ODP to ingest vast quantities of real-time data, transform it into actionable intelligence through its AI engine, and orchestrate coordinated actions across the transport and energy sectors. The platform’s design inherently facilitates interoperability, dynamically aligning EV/ET charging with RES availability and grid conditions, while integrating market signals to optimize economic, environmental, and battery health outcomes, thereby creating an intelligent nexus for sustainable energy and mobility.

3.2
Cost-benefit analysis framework

To evaluate the proposed AI-driven ODP, we employ a structured seven-step CBA framework, guided by European Commission smart grid guidelines
[
EU Commission JRC 2012
]
and lessons from pilot studies such as Isernia
[
Stromsather et al. 2018
]
. This framework systematically captures AI-related costs (platform development, sensors and IoT devices, model training and maintenance) and the benefits directly attributable to AI-driven performance (e.g., improved forecast accuracy, optimization savings). The framework starts with a precise definition of the project, its objectives, engineering features, geographic scope, stakeholders, and expected socio-economic impact, and delineation of its functional scope. Next, it maps the enabling technologies (especially AI modules) to the key functions they perform, and links those functions to the spectrum of benefits they generate. Subsequently, each category of benefit is quantified and monetized, and all associated costs (capital and operating) are tallied over the project’s life cycle. Finally, total benefits and costs are compared, and a rigorous sensitivity analysis, including scenario variations and Monte Carlo simulation, is conducted to assess how results vary under key uncertainties (such as AI performance and adoption rates). 4
Cost-benefit analysis steps with case study

We now apply the seven-step CBA framework to the AI-driven ODP project, evaluating its deployment across Austria (AT), Hungary (HU), and Slovenia (SI). For the economic appraisal, all future cash flows are expressed in constant 2025 Euros and discounted to present value using a social discount rate of 4%. This choice is consistent with EU recommendations for long-term public infrastructure projects
[
European Commission Directorate-General for Regional and Urban
Policy 2021
]
. The results highlight the value created by the ODP’s AI-driven features and provide insight into the project’s economic viability under various scenarios.

Step 1: Define the project

The project under consideration is the deployment and operation of the AI-driven ODP for the integrated management of EVs, ETs, RES, and the electric grid across three countries: Austria, Hungary, and Slovenia. The primary objective of this project is to leverage real-time data and advanced AI (for forecasting and control) to enhance grid flexibility, reduce energy procurement and operational costs, minimize greenhouse gas emissions and local air pollutants, improve power system reliability, and enable seamless interoperability between transport electrification and power system operations. The implementation scale is national/regional within these countries, which have been chosen for their diverse EV/ET adoption rates, renewable energy capacities, and grid characteristics, thereby providing a representative range of EU contexts for analysis.

Key engineering features of the ODP critical to the project include: (a) AI-powered predictive algorithms for demand forecasting (EV/ET charging, overall grid load), RES generation forecasting, and energy price forecasting. These provide high accuracy (e.g., day-ahead load and RES forecasts with MAPE
<5\%
), superior to conventional methods, directly translating to improved operational decisions and cost savings. (b) Optimization engines (MILP-based solvers and RL agents) for smart charging scheduling, V2X services, electric fleet logistics (routing and charging), and demand response dispatch. These engines optimize multi-objective functions, including cost reduction, emissions reduction, and battery health preservation, thus enabling coordinated control strategies that balance the grid and maximize economic returns. (c) IoT infrastructure comprising a dense network of sensors and smart meters to provide granular, real-time monitoring of vehicles(battery SoC, location, charging status), charging stations (availability, power flow), grid nodes (voltage, load), and RES installations (output, status). This high-resolution data collection is foundational for the AI engine’s situational awareness. (d) High-speed, reliable communication networks (5G, LTE-M) ensuring low-latency data transfer and control signal dissemination. This is critical for real-time AI inference and control, allowing the ODP to rapidly respond to changing conditions (such as sudden RES output fluctuations or grid contingencies). (e) Robust middleware integration with existing energy management systems (utility SCADA/DMS/EMS) and market platforms. By using standardized interfaces and data exchange protocols, the ODP can seamlessly interoperate with legacy systems and participate in energy markets (for energy trading and ancillary services), thus embedding the platform within the broader energy ecosystem.

The primary stakeholders expected to benefit from this ODP include: EV owners and ET fleet operators (through optimized charging costs, reduced range anxiety, improved charging availability, extended battery lifespan, and efficient routing); charging point operators (CPOs) (via enhanced asset utilization and new service offerings); distribution and transmission system operators (DSOs/TSOs) (through reduced grid congestion, deferred network reinforcement investments, improved grid stability, and enhanced situational awareness due to AI-driven insights); renewable energy producers (via reduced curtailment and better market integration of variable generation); and society at large (through reduced carbon emissions, improved air quality, and enhanced energy security).

The baseline scenario (counterfactual case without the ODP) assumes that EV and ET adoption and renewable deployment continue to grow in the three countries, but in a conventional manner: EV charging remains largely uncoordinated (users charge at will, leading to sharper peaks and low overnight utilization), V2G and other vehicle-to-grid services have minimal participation, RES curtailment issues persist at times of excess generation, and grid operators have limited visibility/control over distributed EV chargers and other flexible resources. Essentially, the baseline reflects current practices where the transport and energy sectors operate with only rudimentary interaction and rely on existing, less sophisticated forecasting and control tools. The cost-benefit analysis will evaluate the incremental impacts of implementing the ODP relative to this baseline, thereby isolating the net benefits attributable to the platform’s advanced AI-driven coordination.

Step 2: Map technologies to functions

In this step, each core technology component of the ODP, particularly its AI modules as detailed in Section
3.1
, is systematically mapped to the specific operational functions it enables. The ODP integrates a comprehensive suite of technologies (sensors, communication networks, data platforms, AI algorithms, user interfaces), which together deliver several key functions for the system’s operation:

(A) Advanced data and information collection & processing
: Real-time gathering, validation, and processing of granular data from EVs/ETs (e.g., battery SoC, location, charging patterns), RES installations (generation output, availability), grid sensors (voltage levels, loading), charging infrastructure status, and external sources (weather, market market prices). This function is enabled by IoT sensors, connectivity solutions, and the ODP’s Data layer; it ensures that the AI engine has accurate and timely information about the system’s state at all times.

(B) Enhanced interoperability and system integration
: Seamless data exchange and coordinated operation among diverse systems and stakeholders (vehicles, grid operators, CPOs, energy markets, end-users). This is facilitated by the Middleware layer and use of standardized communication protocols and interfaces (e.g., OCPP for charging, OpenADR for demand response signals, API gateways for market data). Enhanced interoperability ensures that decisions made by the AI core can be effectively communicated and executed across different subsystems in real time.

(C) AI-driven forecasting, optimization, and control
: This core function is where the ODP’s AI engine directly drives value. It has two sub-functions:

i) Intelligent forecasting
: The AI models (LSTMs, ML models, etc., as described in Section
3.1
) provide highly accurate predictions for EV/ET charging demand, RES generation, grid load, and electricity prices. These forecasts improve upon traditional methods, reducing uncertainty and prediction error in the system’s operation.

ii) Smart load management and grid balancing
: AI optimization algorithms (MILP, RL, E-VRPTW, as detailed in Section
3.1
) enable dynamic smart charging/discharging of EVs/ETs, optimal dispatch of V2X services for grid support, dynamic EV/ET routing considering energy and battery health, and coordinated demand response actions. These control decisions continuously balance supply and demand, flatten load curves, alleviate grid constraints, and maximize the use of renewable energy.

(D) Decision support and automation
: User-friendly interfaces (mobile apps, dashboards) provide actionable insights and recommendations to end-users (e.g., optimal charging times) and system operators (e.g., congestion alerts and recommended mitigation actions). Automated control systems execute optimized schedules and dispatch signals directly generated by the AI engine, ensuring real-time response and reducing manual intervention.

Step 3: Map functions to benefits

This step identifies the specific categories of benefits generated by each core ODP function. The anticipated benefits are categorized into three primary domains:

i) Economic benefits
: Direct financial gains or cost savings, encompassing reduced operational expenditures for grid operators and end-users, new revenue streams from energy services (e.g., ancillary services, energy arbitrage), enhanced energy efficiency, and optimized asset utilization.

ii) Reliability benefits
: Improvements in power system reliability and resilience, leading to reduced outage costs for consumers and businesses, enhanced service quality for end-users, and potentially deferred or optimized investments in grid infrastructure due to better load management.

iii) Environmental benefits
: Reductions in greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions (primarily CO
2
) and local air pollutants (SOx, NOx, PM
10
), contributing to climate change mitigation, improved public health, and greater integration of clean renewable energy.

Each ODP function (A-D) contributes to one or more of these benefit categories. For example, Advanced data collection and processing (A) provides the necessary real-time data for precise grid state estimation and load forecasting, enabling more efficient asset utilization and informed decision-making, which directly translates to economic efficiency and enhanced system reliability. Enhanced interoperability (B) allows for dynamic coordination between different market participants and grid entities, enabling new revenue streams from flexible assets and reducing system imbalances. The core AI-driven forecasting, optimization, and control functions (C) are pivotal in generating significant economic benefits (e.g., reduced energy procurement costs through peak load shaving and optimized energy trading facilitated by highly accurate price predictions, and increased revenues from ancillary service provision). These AI functions also yield substantial reliability benefits (e.g., prevention of grid overloads, improved voltage/frequency stability through predictive control, and reduced RES curtailment by matching demand with variable generation). Furthermore, these AI functions directly contribute to environmental benefits by maximizing the use of RES, optimizing EV charging for lower emission periods, and reducing fossil fuel-based generation for balancing. Decision support and automation (D) translates AI-generated insights into tangible outcomes, yielding direct economic benefits for users (e.g., lower charging costs due to optimized schedules, time savings from efficient routing) and operators (e.g., automated response to grid events), and indirectly fostering environmental benefits by promoting behaviors aligned with RES availability and lower carbon intensity.

5
Discussion

The comprehensive CBA conducted for the AI-driven ODP clearly indicates that the proposed platform offers substantial net benefits for the integrated management of electric mobility, renewable energy, and grid operations. The aggregated positive net present value (NPV) of €356.7 million and a benefit-cost ratio (BCR) of 1.41 provide a strong economic rationale, signifying that every Euro invested generates approximately €1.41 in benefits over the 10-year analysis period.

The main insight from this study is the substantial economic and operational value unlocked specifically by the ODP’s integrated AI engine. The largest share of the quantified economic benefits stems directly from AI-enabled functionalities, a linkage systematically established through specific ODP performance factors integrated into the benefit monetization (as detailed in Section
4
and
A.1
). For instance, the revenue from optimized energy trading and ancillary services (ROETAS) and cost savings from demand response and peak load reduction (CSDR-PLR) combined account for over 25.8% of the total benefits, directly attributable to the superior performance of the ODP’s AI forecasting and optimization models.

The case studies across Austria, Hungary, and Slovenia demonstrate the ODP’s adaptability and value proposition across varied national energy and transport landscapes. The substantial reliability benefits (€348 million in GSMS, alongside €171 million in CSDR-PLR as an economic benefit) underscore the ODP’s potential as a highly effective non-wires alternative, achieved through improved system visibility, AI-driven proactive control, and enhanced coordination across sectors. From a societal and policy perspective, the significant environmental benefits (€127 million from CO
2
and air pollutant reductions) are critical, highlighting the ODP’s role in accelerating decarbonization. The robustness of AI models to real-world conditions such as data drift, concept drift, and unexpected events is planned to be managed through continuous performance monitoring, automated retraining pipelines, and robust data validation, which are all factored into the recurring OPEX (
A.3
), ensuring sustained long-term benefits.

While the comprehensive CBA provides a strong economic rationale, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. Firstly, while rigorous backtesting and validation were performed, and simulations incorporate realistic assumptions, there remains a potential for variance in real-world AI model performance versus simulated benchmarks, particularly under unforeseen extreme conditions. Future pilot projects are essential for further empirical validation. Secondly, the optimal functioning of the ODP’s AI engine relies on sustained, high-quality, and comprehensive data availability from a multitude of disparate sources. These include real-time EV and ET fleet telemetry, smart charging infrastructure, renewable energy forecasts, market data from EPEX Spot and ENTSO-E, weather models, and public datasets from Eurostat and the EEA.

Thirdly, certain intangible benefits, such as enhanced user convenience, improved public health outcomes from cleaner air that are difficult to fully monetize, or the empowerment of consumers in energy management, were not explicitly quantified in monetary terms, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true societal value. Additionally, the study makes assumptions regarding rational user behavior in response to ODP incentives and information, which may vary in practice. The dynamic nature of the regulatory landscape influencing energy markets, data sharing, and cross-border energy flows introduces inherent uncertainties. Finally, while the three selected EU member states represent a diverse range, there are inherent challenges in quantitatively generalizing the precise findings to contexts vastly different from those studied, although the qualitative benefits of integrated, AI-driven energy and transport management are broadly applicable globally.

6
Conclusion

This paper has presented a comprehensive cost-benefit analysis of an AI-driven operational digital platform developed for the integrated management of electric vehicles, electric trucks, renewable energy sources, and the power grid. The CBA framework was designed in alignment with European Commission guidelines for energy infrastructure projects and tailored to explicitly capture AI-specific value streams. These include improvements in forecasting accuracy, operational optimization, grid reliability, and emissions reduction, each monetized using transparent and reproducible methodologies. Importantly, the framework incorporates both capital and operational expenditures, particularly those linked to AI model development, deployment, and maintenance, alongside benefits categorized as economic, reliability, and environmental.

The analysis demonstrates that the proposed AI-driven ODP yields substantial net economic and societal value. With a projected net present value of approximately €356.7 million and a benefit-cost ratio of 1.41 over the 2026–2035 operational period, the investment case is strong. These results are driven by the ODP’s multi-layered architecture, where the AI core, featuring LSTM-based forecasting models, MILP-based optimization algorithms, and electric vehicle routing solvers, serves as a critical value enabler. These technologies jointly support enhanced energy and operational efficiency, predictive congestion mitigation, optimized demand response, and battery degradation reduction through health-aware charging strategies. The robustness of these benefits was confirmed through extensive stress-testing, including sensitivity analyses and Monte Carlo simulations.

To validate cross-border applicability, the CBA was applied to Austria, Hungary, and Slovenia, each reflecting different energy and transport profiles. The findings illustrate how national characteristics influence the distribution of benefits: Austria achieves substantial fleet efficiency gains and extended battery life; Hungary benefits from improved RES integration and reduced curtailment; and Slovenia sees value in grid flexibility and optimized ET logistics. These variations underscore the scalability and adaptability of the ODP to diverse regulatory, technical, and market contexts.

Beyond financial metrics, the study offers strategic insights. For policymakers, the results affirm the role of advanced digital infrastructure in achieving EU-wide decarbonization goals under REPowerEU and Fit for 55. The ODP presents a viable pathway to accelerate transport electrification, enhance grid reliability, and reduce emissions through AI-based coordination. For industry stakeholders, including DSOs, TSOs, fleet operators, and aggregators, the analysis provides a quantitative basis for investing in interoperable, intelligent systems capable of delivering both operational gains and new service opportunities.
At the same time, the study acknowledges key limitations. The optimal functioning of the AI engine depends on sustained access to high-quality, heterogeneous data sources, such as real-time telemetry from EVs and ETs, smart charging infrastructure, market platforms (e.g., EPEX Spot, ENTSO-E), and weather models. Moreover, generalizing the results to other countries requires careful consideration of differences in data infrastructure, policy environments, and electrification maturity. Additionally, intangible benefits, such as improved public health from air quality gains or increased user convenience, were not monetized, likely leading to a conservative estimate of the platform’s true societal value.

In conclusion, the integrated management of EVs, ETs, RES, and the grid via AI-driven digital platforms is shown to be not only technically feasible but also economically and environmentally beneficial. The proposed ODP offers a compelling blueprint for next-generation energy–mobility convergence, supporting the emergence of smart, flexible, and low-carbon energy ecosystems. By holistically orchestrating distributed assets through AI, the platform bridges the gap between high-level policy goals and on-the-ground operational needs.

Future work should focus on several key areas to further validate and expand the ODP’s capabilities and impact. This includes the initiation of pilot projects for empirical validation of the ODP’s AI models and overall system performance in real-world operational environments, providing concrete data on benefits and operational challenges. A deeper exploration of advanced V2X (Vehicle-to-Everything) services, including vehicle-to-home (V2H) and vehicle-to-grid (V2G) applications beyond ancillary services, could unlock further synergies. Detailed distribution grid impact modeling, specifically for local voltage stability and transformer loading under high EV penetration and dynamic charging, is crucial. Research into advanced cybersecurity measures tailored for AI-driven energy platforms, including AI-specific threat detection and mitigation, is paramount given the critical infrastructure nature. Policy and market design innovation is essential to incentivize broad stakeholder participation and ensure fair value distribution across the ecosystem. Finally, extending the ODP concept to integrate other flexible resources like smart heat pumps, stationary battery storage, and industrial flexible loads could further enhance system-wide optimization and accelerate decarbonization efforts.
