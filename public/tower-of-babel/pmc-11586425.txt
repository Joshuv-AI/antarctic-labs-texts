Unveiling therapeutic targets for spinal stenosis from genetic insights: a Mendelian randomization analysis
Authors: Zhaopeng Fan, Bohong Chen, Le Ding, Hua Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Spinal stenosis is a commonly chronic spinal degenerative disease, which is a major cause of pain and dysfunction in the elderly. Mendelian randomization (MR) has been widely applied to repurpose licensed drugs and identify novel therapeutic targets. Consequently, we intended to identify new therapeutic targets for spinal stenosis and to analyze their possible mechanisms and potential side effects.We conducted the Mendelian randomization analysis to identify potential drug targets for the management of spinal stenosis. Cis-expressed quantitative trait loci (cis-eQTL) data as genetic instrumental variables were acquired from the eQTLGen consortium. The summary statistics for single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) associations of spinal stenosis were obtained from the FinnGen study(20,807 cases and 294,770 controls). Co-localization analysis was performed to determine whether there was shared causal variation between the SNPs associated with spinal stenosis as well as the eQTL. Multiple external validations were performed to reinforce the reliability and stability of the findings utilizing the cis-eQTL from the GTEx portal, the Ferkingstad et al. pQTL dataset, and the Sun et al. pQTL dataset. The viability of the identified drug targets for future clinical applications was elucidated through the phenome-wide association study and drug candidate prediction. Three drug targets (BMP6, DLK1, and GFPT1) exhibited significant causal associations with spinal stenosis in the eQTLGen cohort by MR analysis, which was strongly supported by the results of the co-localization analysis. The causal association of DLK1 and GFPT1 with spinal stenosis remained remarkable with multiple external validations. Multivariate MR and phenome-wide association study analysis indicated that both targets were not associated with other traits. In addition, phenome-wide association study analysis and drug prediction analysis demonstrated the potential of these two targets for future clinical applications. In this study, DLK1 and GFPT1 were identified as promising novel therapeutic targets for spinal stenosis, providing initial genetic insights for drug development in spinal stenosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-80697-4.

Introduction

Spinal stenosis is a commonly chronic spinal degenerative disease caused by a variety of factors which is the leading cause of pain and dysfunction in the elderly1,2. It is characterized by the narrowing of the spinal canal resulting in compression of the spinal cord and nerve roots, which causes conditions such as low back pain, leg pain, and intermittent claudication3. According to the site of stenosis, spinal stenosis is classified as lumbar spinal stenosis, cervical spinal stenosis, and thoracic spinal stenosis, with lumbar spinal stenosis being the most common4. With the aging of the population worldwide, the incidence of spinal stenosis has further risen, and it has been reported that approximately 100 million people worldwide suffer from spinal stenosis, which severely aggravates the economic and medical burden on their families and society5. Currently, the treatment strategy for spinal stenosis is mainly conservative, with surgical treatment being necessary in the presence of secondary progressive neurologic deficits or cauda equina syndrome6. Because of the progressive character of lumbar spinal stenosis, making it unlikely to be completely cured, the patients require long-term treatment to reduce the pain and to improve their physical performance. Given the great pathologic and clinical variability of lumbar spinal stenosis and the lack of therapeutic recommendations as well as the large amount of different therapeutic approaches, it is difficult to select an appropriate treatment7. Accordingly, it is of great significance to identify effective genetic drug targets to ameliorate the symptoms and improve the life quality of the patients.

While large randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are effective approaches for evaluating pharmacological interventions, performing RCTs demands extensive planning and consumes considerable resources and time8. Mendelian randomization, as a novel statistical analysis method, utilizes single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in genome-wide association studies (GWAS) as instrumental variables to specify the causal relationship between exposures and outcomes through simulating RCTs9. Compared to observational studies, it minimizes the interference of lifestyle and environmental factors and increases the stability and reliability of the results10. In recent years, MR has been widely used to explore new drug therapeutic targets for various diseases, such as ankylosing spondylitis, rheumatoid arthritis, sarcopenia, and inflammatory bowel disease11–13. Multi-omics studies provide robust evidence for discovering potential novel drug targets by integrating data from different biological fields, like transcriptomics and proteomics14. Integrating GWAS datasets with expression quantitative trait loci(eQTL) linked to changes in gene expression and protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) associated with quantitative traits in protein abundance as instrumental variables enables the exploration of potential causality between exposures and outcomes at both the gene expression and protein levels to screen out new drug targets for relevant diseases15,16. Therefore, in this study, we identified effective drug targets for spinal stenosis by combining druggable genomic datasets, transcriptomic datasets, proteomic datasets, and spinal stenosis GWAS summary data.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study aims to identify novel therapeutic targets related to spinal stenosis. The design procedure for our study is demonstrated in Fig. 1. Further information regarding the data sources can be found in Table S1. Firstly, we obtained eQTL datasets of 1639 druggable genes by integrating publicly available eQTL data in blood tissues and 4479 actionable druggable genes data. Secondly, the preliminary findings were further validated by heterogeneity testing, horizontal pleiotropy assessment, reverse causality testing, and Bayesian co-localization analysis. Thirdly, the analysis was replicated in the cis-eQTL dataset from the GTEx portal, the Ferkingstad et al. pQTL dataset, and the Sun et al. pQTL dataset as an external validation to strengthen our findings. Then, multivariate MR analysis was performed to clarify the direct effect of the identified druggable genes on the risk of spinal stenosis. Finally, we performed a Phenome-Wide Association Study (PheWAS) analysis and drug candidate prediction to clarify the feasibility of the identified drug targets in future clinical applications. The STROBE-MR checklist is provided as Table S217, and the study was performed following the current guideline.

Actionable drug targets

The druggable genes as reported by Finan et al. were incorporated into our study18. A total of 4479 druggable genes with HGNC annotations on autosomes were identified for analysis. It contains 1,427 genes encoding protein targets currently in clinical development, 682 genes associated with proteins involved in the approved drugs or compounds, and 2,370 genes belonging to families of identified drug targets.

Quantitative trait loci datasets for selection of genetic instrumental variables

eQTL data for identification of genetic instrumental variables

The eQTL data employed in the discovery phase were obtained from the eQTLGen consortium (https://eqtlgen.org/). Considering that cis-regulatory elements are closer to genes of interest in drug development studies compared with trans-regulatory elements19, we obtained publicly available cis-eQTL data for blood tissues from the eQTLGen consortium (probe locations ± 1 Mb, FDR < 0.05). The cis-eQTL data in blood contains information on 16,987 genes from 31,684 individuals, with the majority of participants being of European ancestry. To generate genetic instruments representing 4479 druggable targets, we obtained cis-eQTL from the range of cis-eQTL located 100 kb upstream of transcriptional start sites or 100 kb downstream of transcriptional termination sites of druggable genes and ultimately obtained the eQTL data of 1639 druggable genes.

The blood tissue and musculoskeletal tissue eQTL data employed in the replication phase were obtained from the GTEx database version 8 (https://gtexportal.org/home/)20.

pQTL data for identification of genetic instrumental variables

In the replication phase, we downloaded two independent sources of pQTL datasets for external validation. Summary statistics on the genetic levels associated with 4907 circulating protein levels were extracted by Ferkingstad et al. from a large-scale pQTL study of 35,559 Icelanders21. Summary pQTL datasets for 2923 plasma proteins were reported by Sun et al. by extracting plasma proteome profiles derived from 54,219 UK Biobank participants from the UK Biobank-PPP database22.

Summary statistics of GWAS data for spinal stenosis and associated risk factors employed in this study

GWAS summary statistics on spinal stenosis were obtained from the FinnGen Release 10 (https://r10.finngen.fi/). The study project comprised 20,807 patients suffering from spinal stenosis and 294,770 controls. Participants were of European ancestry and informed consent was obtained. Cases were identified on the basis of the M13 code in ICD-10. Specific information about the participants was available through the Finnish governmental network (https://finngen.gitbook).

We identified two relevant risk factors for spinal stenosis: body mass index (BMI) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM, specific information is available in Table S1). In a previous MR study, Maria K. Sobczyk et al. reported a positive causal relationship between BMI and spinal stenosis23. In addition, in our previous study, we clarified the positive causal relationship between T2DM and spinal stenosis and that the effect of T2DM on spinal stenosis was independent of BMI24.

Mendelian randomization analysis

Genetic instrumental variables(IVs) utilized for MR analysis must meet three assumptions: (1) the IVs are strongly associated with the selected exposures, (2) the IVs are independent of confounders affecting the selected exposures and outcomes, and (3) the IVs affect outcomes only through the selected exposures and not through other potential pathways25. Firstly, we obtained eQTL datasets of druggable genes by intersecting 4479 potentially druggable genes with human blood eQTL datasets. Then, to enable our study to fully satisfy the three main hypotheses of MR, we conducted rigorous quality control of the SNPs. We adopted P < 5e-07 and F statistic ≥ 10 (F = (beta/se)2) to obtain enough strongly correlated IVs ensuring a strong correlation with the exposure of interest, the LD factor r2 was set to 0.01, and the LD window width was set to 10 Mb to remove chained SNPs and to ensure independence between SNPs to address the independence assumption and minimize potential linkage disequilibrium between SNPs26,27, to minimize the likelihood that any SNPs employed as IVs may affect the results through pathways other than exposure. In preliminary analyses, Wald ratios were used to calculate MR estimates for individual SNPs. If two or more SNPs were available, we estimated the relationship between exposure and outcome with a combined approach incorporating inverse variance weighting (IVW)28, MR- egger29, and a weighted median model30. The Bonferroni correction was applied to determine the significance threshold adjusted for multiple tests, taking into account false positives generated by multiple tests. In the discovery cohort, P-value < 3.05e-05 (P = 0.05/1639) was considered statistically significant. IVW and MR-Egger were applied to estimate Cochran’s Q statistic thus testing the heterogeneity31. The presence of horizontal pleiotropy was examined by the MR-Egger intercept test for genes containing more than two instrumental variables29. We then performed reverse MR analysis to clarify whether there was reverse causality between druggable genes and spinal stenosis (P < 0.05 was considered significant). In reverse MR studies, we performed SNP screening criteria and effect estimation methods (IVW32, MR- egger33, and weighted median mode) similar to those used in the eQTL screening process. Rigorous quality control was performed for significant genes by checking the consistency of the three methods in the direction of the estimated effect and excluding horizontal pleiotropy by MR-Egger test as well as excluding reverse causality by reverse MR analysis. Repetitive external validation was performed on significant genes that passed quality control to reduce the likelihood of false-positive results and increase the reliability and stability of the study results. In the replication phase, we used the same SNP screening criteria employed in the discovery phase (P < 5e-07 and F ≥ 10). Since the number of SNPs for BMP6 in the GTEx whole blood samples and for DLK1 in the GTEx musculoskeletal samples was limited under this criterion, we appropriately relaxed it to 5e-0634. In the eQTL replication phase, The thresholds for p-values of druggable genes after Bonferroni correction were 0.05/2 for blood eQTL and 0.05/2 for musculoskeletal eQTL. In the pQTL replication phase, P < 0.05 was considered significant. In addition, we performed multivariate MR analyses to explore potential independent associations of genetic proxies for DLK1 and GFPT1 with the risk of spinal stenosis. All MR analyses were performed in R software (version 4.3.2) using the program packages “TwoSampleMR” (version 0.5.11) and “Mendelian Randomization” (version 0.10.0).

Bayesian co-localization analysis

Co-localization analyses for the risk of spinal stenosis were performed with the R package “coloc” for significant genes that passed rigorous quality control35. Bayesian co-localization analyses tested whether the associations of the two traits (trait 1: potential drug genes, trait 2: spinal stenosis) were derived from the shared variation rather than being driven by linkage disequilibrium. Specifically, Bayesian co-localization analyses involved five hypotheses: (H0) no association with either trait; (H1) association with trait 1 only; (H2) association with trait 2 only; (H3) association with both traits but with independent causal variation; and (H4) association with both traits driven by common causal variation. We employed a prior probability of 1e − 04 for both trait 1 (p1) and trait 2 (p2) and set the prior probability of affecting both traits (p12) to 1e − 05. If the posterior probability PPH3 + PPH4 > 80%, we suggest that there is robust evidence that potentially druggable genes and spinal stenosis share common genetic variants36.

PheWAS

To further comprehensively assess the horizontal pleiotropy and possible side effects of potential drug targets, we performed the phenome-wide MR analysis36,37. We conducted the PheWAS study by utilizing the AstraZeneca PheWAS portal (https://azphewas.com/). P < 5e-08 was considered as significant association of the two drug targets with the other traits at the gene level.

Drug candidate prediction

To assess the druggability of the identified genes, we searched the identified genes in the Drug Signature Database (DSigDB, http://dsigdb.tanlab.org/DSigDBv1.0/). Specifically, DSigDB is a critical database with 22,527 gene sets and 17,389 different compounds spanning 19,531 genes. This huge database allows us to connect drugs and other chemicals to their target genes. The identified target genes DLK1 and GFPT1 were uploaded to the DSigDB drug database on enrichment (https://maayanlab.cloud/modEnrichr/) for potential drug candidate prediction38.

Results

In the discovery phase, three druggable genes showed a significant causal association with the risk of spinal stenosis

In the discovery phase, we finally identified 1639 druggable genes after a rigorous instrumental variable screening process using cis-eQTL data from the eQTLGen consortium. At Bonferroni significance (P < 3.05 × 10 − 5), MR analysis revealed that three genes (BMP6, DLK1, and GFPT1) had a significant causal association with the risk of spinal stenosis (Figs. 2 and 3). To be specific, increased expression of DLK1 (OR = 0.45; 95% CI 0.31–0.64; P = 9.79e-06) decreased the risk of spinal stenosis; whereas expression of BMP6 (OR = 2.03; 95% CI 1.46–2.83; P = 2.78e-05) and GFPT1 (OR = 1.42; 95% CI 1.27–1.59; P = 1.08e-09) increased the risk of spinal stenosis. The orientation of all significant druggable genes was consistent across the three methods (Table S3). In sensitivity analyses, the findings did not reveal the presence of heterogeneity (Table S4) or horizontal pleiotropy (Table S5). Reverse Mendelian randomization showed no evidence of a causal effect of spinal stenosis on the expression of the three significant genes (Fig. S1).

Bayesian co-localization analysis

Previous studies have shown that the association of SNPs for exposure and outcome may originate from two different causal SNPs due to the presence of linkage disequilibrium, leading to the generation of false-positive results39. When SNPs are significantly associated with exposure and outcome, co-localization analyses could be applied to explore whether the association between exposure and outcome is derived from the shared SNPs rather than driven by linkage disequilibrium35. Therefore, we performed co-localization analyses on three significant genes (BMP6, DLK1, and GFPT1) that passed stringent quality control. The results of Bayesian co-localization indicated that BMP6, DLK1, and GFPT1 all shared the common causal variant with spinal stenosis (Table S6), which implies that these three druggable genes are more likely to be promising drug targets40.

In the replication phase, two genes remained significant in the GTEx eQTL database

In external validation, we performed a two-sample MR analysis utilizing eQTL data from GTEx whole blood samples and musculoskeletal samples with GWAS summary data for spinal stenosis, respectively. MR analysis in the replication phase was performed with the same methods as in the original discovery cohort. Since DLK1 was not available in GTEx whole blood samples, we only performed MR analysis of BMP6 and GFPT1. MR analyses showed that the genetically predicted expression of GFPT1 was replicated as causally associated with the risk of spinal stenosis, as shown in Table S7(P < 0.05/2 was significant after Bonferroni correction). Therefore, BMP6 was excluded from subsequent studies. The same methods were used for MR analysis of eQTL data (absence of BMP6) in musculoskeletal samples, which showed that the causal relationship between genetically predicted expression of DLK1 and GFPT1 and the risk of spinal stenosis remained significant after Bonferroni correction (p < 0.05/2, Table S8). None of the above preliminary analyses showed the presence of horizontal pleiotropy (p < 0.05) yet there was heterogeneity which may be due to the fact that the data came from different populations (Table S9-S10). Importantly, the directionality of the effect for the two genes was consistent across all methods in the discovery and replication phases.

In the replication phase, the pQTL data provided further genetic evidence for the identification of feasible therapeutic targets for spinal stenosis

Since we employ proteins rather than gene expression as drug targets in most settings of clinical practice and since there is a certain nonlinear relationship between protein and mRNA levels41, it is necessary and significant to utilize pQTL to simulate drug-targeting effects15. Therefore, we performed MR analysis with the Ferkingstad et al. and Sun et al. pQTL datasets in the replication phase. Because GFPT1 was absent in both datasets and BMP6 was excluded because it was not significant in GTEx whole blood samples, we conducted MR analysis of only DLK1. The results of MR analyses of both datasets showed powerful evidence that the genetically predicted expression of DLK1 remained significantly causally associated with the risk of spinal stenosis after Bonferroni correction (p < 0.05, Fig. 4). Sensitivity analyses revealed the presence of heterogeneity, which may be due to population differences, and excluded the presence of horizontal pleiotropy (Table S112-S13, S15-S16). In addition, the directionality of the effect of DLK1 was consistent across all methods in all discovery and replication phases.

Multivariate MR analysis revealed that DLK1 and GFPT1 were independently associated with spinal canal stenosis

Following multiple multi-omics validations in the discovery and replication phases, DLK1 and GFPT1 still maintained significant causal associations with the risk of spinal stenosis, and the directionality of effect for both was consistent across all methods in the discovery and replication phases which was validated by Bayesian co-localization analyses, having a high likelihood of being promising druggable targets. Among the 2 risk factors for spinal stenosis, GFPT1 was significantly associated with BMI (p = 0.04), whereas DLK1 was not significantly associated with either (Fig. 5). To examine the potential independent relationship between GFPT1 and spinal stenosis, we performed multivariate MR analysis and found that there was a significant independent association between GFPT1 and spinal stenosis (P = 0.014 [IVW], Table S17).

PheWAS

To further assess the horizontal pleiotropy and possible adverse drug reactions of the two potential drug targets identified, PheWAS Portal was used in this study to PheWAS MR. In the PheWAS Portal, neither of these two drug targets was significantly correlated with the other traits at the gene level (Figure S2-S3). The results of the PheWAS study indicated that the likelihood of adverse drug reactions in these possible drug targets and the possibility of the presence of horizontal pleiotropy in these genes is remarkably low, further illustrating the reliability of the results of this study.

Drug candidate prediction for target genes

We predicted possible effective intervening drugs for two candidate drug genes for spinal stenosis with the DSigDB drug database. The results of the study demonstrated that toxic carotenoids (67526-95-8 CTD 00007263), isotretinoin PC3 UP, and bisphenol A diglycidyl ether (BISPHENOL A DIGLYCIDYL ETHER CTD 00000976) were the most notable drugs associated with DLK1; whereas toxic carotenoids (67526-95-8 CTD 00007263), monensin (monensin MCF7 UP) and cyclosporin (ciclosporin PC3 UP) were the most significant drugs associated with GFPT1 (Table 1).

Discussion

In this study, a large-scale MR analysis was performed by integrating GWAS datasets, pharmacogenomic data, and gene expression data (eQTL and pQTL), employing a variety of methods. We identified three promising druggable targets for spinal stenosis by co-localization analysis of whole blood MR analysis results. Ultimately, we identified two potentially druggable targets with sufficiently strong MR evidence including DLK1 and GFPT1 employing multiple external validations. In addition, PheWAS analysis did not reveal potential horizontal pleiotropy and adverse effects of these 2 two drug target genes. Finally, drug prediction of these genes was performed in this study, further demonstrating the medicinal value of these target genes.

Delta-like 1, also known as preadipocyte factor 1 (Pref-1), is an imprinted gene encoding a membrane-bound protein that belongs to the Notch/Serrate/Delta family42. DLK1 is hydrolyzed by ADAM17/TACE to soluble cyclin (sDLK1) and acts predominantly in this form to regulate several physiological processes including adipocytogenesis and osteoclastogenesis42,43. In previous studies, DLK1 was reported to act in a paracrine or endocrine manner to inhibit osteoblast differentiation and bone formation and stimulate bone resorption, and inhibit adipocyte differentiation44. Spinal stenosis is commonly caused by a variety of factors, with hypertrophy and ossification of the ligamentum flavum being a major cause of spinal stenosis45. Previous studies have shown that ligamentum flavum hypertrophy is associated with lipid accumulation, and we could inhibit lipid accumulation to treat spinal stenosis46,47. Yanlin Cao et al. found that ACSM5 inhibits ligamentum flavum hypertrophy by regulating FABP4/PPAR signaling pathway-mediated lipid accumulation47. Interestingly, our findings suggested that DLK1 is a negative modifier of the risk of spinal stenosis (eQTLGen OR = 0.45, P = 9.79e-06; GTEx musculoskeletal samples OR = 0.90, P = 1.07e-02; Ferkingstad et al. dataset OR = 0.92, P = 2.33e-02; Sun et al. dataset OR = 0.94, P = 1.01e-02). On the other hand, there was strong evidence for co-localization between DLK1 and spinal stenosis. The results of the PheWAS MR and multivariate MR analyses showed no significant correlation between DLK1 and other factors that may influence the risk of spinal stenosis, suggesting that there is no widespread horizontal pleiotropy of DLK1. In addition, activation of DLK1 was shown not to be associated with adverse effects in the PheWAS analysis. These results suggested that DLK1 has high medicinal value as a promising potential drug target for spinal stenosis and deserves further in-depth study.

Glutamine-fructose-6-phosphate transaminase 1 (GFPT1) is a protein that is ubiquitous in all cellular organisms and plays a remarkably important role in maintaining normal cellular function. It is the rate-limiting enzyme in the hexosamine biosynthesis pathway that yields precursors required for protein and lipid glycosylation48. It has been shown that overexpression of GFPT1 is associated with apoptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma and poor prognosis in patients with resectable pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma49,50 and that GFPT1 deficiency is associated with congenital myasthenia gravis51. More importantly, two previous GWAS studies proposed that GFPT1 is associated with the risk of spinal stenosis52,53, which corroborates our findings. Our MR analysis identified a positive genotype-phenotype association between GFPT1 and spinal canal stenosis (eQTLGen OR = 1.42, P = 1.08e-09; GTEx whole blood samples OR = 1.50, P = 2.92e-11; GTEx musculoskeletal samples OR = 1.42, P = 3.59e-03), similarly strongly supported by the results of the co-localization analysis. In addition, PheWAS MR and multivariate MR analyses showed that GFPT1 was significantly associated with BMI. The significant independent association between GFPT1 and spinal stenosis remained after adjusting for the role of BMI (OR = 1.57, P = 0.014). TUSTY-JIUAN HSIEH et al. found that GFPT-1 activation may play an important role in adipogenesis by regulating glucose uptake, nonesterified fatty acid synthesis, and other pathways in adipocytes, and inhibition of GFPT-1 inhibits protein glycosylation and adipogenesis54. Therefore, we hypothesized that the relationship between GFPT1 and the risk of spinal stenosis might be related to hypertrophy of the ligamentum flavum caused by lipid accumulation46,47, whose exact mechanism needs further investigation. In addition, the results of the PheWAS study suggested that inhibition of GFPT1 function does not produce potential adverse effects. Therefore, GFPT1 holds the potential to become a more complete target for the treatment of spinal stenosis.

There are certain strengths of this study. First, to the best of our knowledge, this study was the first to integrate druggable genomic data, transcriptomics data, proteomics data, and spinal stenosis GWAS summary data for multiple validations and to combine co-localization analyses to identify robust druggable genes. Second, we excluded horizontal pleiotropy by PheWAS analysis and multivariate MR analysis and clarified the independent causal association between druggable genes and spinal stenosis to strengthen the persuasiveness of our results. In addition, we verified the safety of druggable genes by PheWAS analysis, which provided guidance for subsequent drug development.

However, the existence of limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, due to the different degrees of lack of data related to DLK1 and GFPT1 in public databases, we could only analyze them on the basis of the available data, limiting our ability to perform an exhaustive analysis. Second, early clinical trials are needed to assess the efficacy and safety of targeting DLK1 and GFPT1 for the treatment of spinal stenosis. Third, we did not obtain positive results when using a separate GWAS dataset for external outcome validation. This may be attributed to several factors, including differences in sample characteristics or limited statistical power in the independent GWAS datasets, which did not undermine the overall findings of the study. Finally, the GWAS database we selected was based on European populations, limiting the generalization of the findings to other populations.

In conclusion, this study clarified DLK1 and GFPT1 as promising potential drug targets for spinal stenosis by integrating Mendelian randomization and co-localization analyses and provided preliminary genetic insights for drug development for spinal stenosis treatment. Our further step is to collect blood and musculoskeletal samples from patients suffering from spinal stenosis and healthy controls to test the protein levels of the targets, in order to verify whether the expression of DLK1 and GFPT1 in the above tissues is consistent with the results of the MR analyses. In addition, the therapeutic targets derived from the MR analysis results must be validated by well-documented clinical trials.
