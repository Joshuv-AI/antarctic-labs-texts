Direct Sampling of Confined Polygons in Linear Time
Clayton Shonkwiler, Kandin Theis

Abstract
We present an algorithm for sampling tightly confined random equilateral closed polygons in three-space which has runtime linear in the number of edges. Using symplectic geometry, sampling such polygons reduces to sampling a moment polytope, and in our confinement model this polytope turns out to be natural from a combinatorial point of view. This connection to combinatorics yields both our fast sampling algorithm and explicit formulas for the expected distances of vertices to the origin. We use our algorithm to investigate the expected total curvature of confined polygons, leading to a very precise conjecture for the asymptotics of total curvature.

Introduction

In this paper we introduce the Confined Polygons from Order Polytopes (CPOP) algorithm for sampling random equilateral polygons in tight confinement. This is an interesting mathematical story, using symplectic geometry to reduce the problem to sampling a particular polytope, which turns out to be essentially the order polytope of the zig-zag poset, but it is also relevant to polymer science and biochemistry, as random polygons provide a simple model of ring polymers and the confinement model we use was introduced to try to understand DNA packing in, e.g., viral capsids.

For our purposes, a
polygon
is a piecewise-linear embedding of the circle into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and an
equilateral polygon
is a polygon in which all the segments have unit length. We can think of a random polygon as a random walk which is conditioned to close up and form a loop.

In the polymer literature, random walks in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
are often called
freely-jointed chains
and provide a simple, well-understood, yet somewhat robust model for polymers in solution
[
72
,
45
,
49
]
. In turn, random polygons provide the analogous model for ring polymers
[
68
]
, including biopolymers like bacterial DNA.

However, biopolymers are often confined into small volumes and it remains an active area of research to understand how, for example, viral DNA gets packed into viral capsids
[
55
,
60
,
61
,
6
,
7
,
50
,
73
]
. This motivates the question of how to sample random equilateral polygons in tight confinement.

The closure condition imposes correlations between the edge directions which makes sampling polygons rather challenging: algorithms for sampling (unconfined) random equilateral polygons have been proposed for at least four decades
[
1
,
53
,
69
,
86
,
63
,
65
,
64
,

,
22
,
18
,

,
19
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
. Of particular note are the Progressive Action-Angle Method (PAAM)
[
21
]
, which produces random equilateral
n
-gons in
\Theta(n^{2})
time, and the Conformal Barycenter Sampler (CoBarS)
[
19
]
, which produces reweighted samples in
\Theta(n)
time.

Random confined polygons must satisfy not only the closure constraint which says that the path must eventually get back to its starting point, but also the confinement constraints, which have a variable effect depending on locations of vertices. The simplest confinement constraint is
rooted spherical confinement of radius

R
, in which the first vertex is placed at the center of a sphere of radius
R
, and all subsequent vertices are required to lie within this sphere. For equilateral polygons with unit-length edges,
R\geq 1
since the second and last vertices must lie at distance 1 from the first vertex.

In this regime there are two notable algorithms in use: Diao, Ernst, Montemayor, and Ziegler’s method
[
31
]
based on generating successive marginals of the joint distribution of vertex distances from the center of the confining sphere, and the Toric Symplectic Markov Chain Monte Carlo (TSMCMC) algorithm introduced in
[
22
]
. Both have disadvantages: Diao et al.’s algorithm is rather computationally expensive, so it is challenging to generate large ensembles of confined
n
-gons when
n
is large; on the other hand, TSMCMC can generate large ensembles, but it is a Markov chain with essentially useless bounds on its rate of convergence, so the effective size of an ensemble is hard to estimate.

CPOP combines the best of both worlds in the case
R=1
: it directly samples uniformly distributed and independent confined
n
-gons like Diao et al., so there is no concern about convergence rates, and it is even faster than TSMCMC. Indeed, as we will see in

5

n
-gons in rooted spherical confinement of radius 1 in time

, which is theoretically optimal, and in practice this is quite fast: for example, we can generate confined 20,000-gons at a rate of about 500/second on a desktop computer (see
Figure

3
). The drawback relative to other methods is that (so far at least) CPOP only applies in the case
R=1
, which is the smallest possible radius for rooted spherical confinement.

2
Polygons and Polytopes

As mentioned in the introduction, a
polygon
is a piecewise-linear mapping of the circle into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and an
equilateral polygon
is such an embedding for which each linear segment has the same length. Up to scaling, we may as well assume that length is 1. We can represent a polygon by the locations of its vertices
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and the equilateral polygon condition is equivalent to requiring that
|v_{i+1}-v_{i}|=1
for
i=1,\dots,n-1
and
|v_{1}-v_{n}|=1
.

We can instead think in terms of edge vectors
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
, where
e_{i}=v_{i+1}-v_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n-1
and
e_{n}=v_{1}-v_{n}
. The edges of an equilateral polygon will be unit vectors, so we can equivalently think of an equilateral polygon as a collection
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})\in(S^{2})^{n}
of unit vectors. The fact that these edges fit together to form a closed loop means that they satisfy the vector equation
e_{1}+\dots+e_{n}=0
, so the collection of equilateral polygons

\operatorname{Pol}(n):=\{(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})\in(S^{2})^{n}:e_{1}+\dots+e_{n}=0\}

forms a codimension-3 subset of the product
(S^{2})^{n}\subset(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{n}
of
n
-tuples of unit vectors. One can show that the subset
\operatorname{Pol}(n)^{\times}
on which all edges are distinct forms a smooth
(2n-3)
-dimensional submanifold of
(S^{2})^{n}
and that the
(2n-3)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure of
\operatorname{Pol}(n)\backslash\operatorname{Pol}(n)^{\times}
vanishes; in this sense,
\operatorname{Pol}(n)
is almost everywhere a submanifold of
(S^{2})^{n}
which inherits the submanifold Riemannian metric and associated volume measure.

Thinking in terms of edge vectors gives a translation-invariant representation of polygons; if we also want a rotation-invariant representation, it is natural to take the quotient

\widehat{\operatorname{Pol}}(n):=\operatorname{Pol}(n)/\operatorname{SO}(3),

which we call
equilateral polygon space
. In turn, this quotient has a Riemannian metric defined by the condition that
\operatorname{Pol}(n)\to\widehat{\operatorname{Pol}}(n)
is a Riemannian submersion, and hence a natural probability measure given by normalizing the Riemannian volume.

We now describe some coordinates on
\widehat{\operatorname{Pol}}(n)
with respect to which this probability measure has a particularly simple form. These coordinates are inspired by symplectic geometry
[
51
]
, but are entirely elementary.

Figure 1:
Illustrating the reconstruction map
\alpha:\mathcal{P}_{n}\times T^{n-3}
which takes diagonal lengths and dihedral angles to an equilateral polygon. Top left shows the triangulation of an abstract hexagon. Given
d_{1},d_{2},d_{3}
which obey the triangle inequalities (
1
), build the four triangles in the triangulation from their side lengths (top right). Given dihedral angles
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3}
, we can build a piecewise-linear surface out of these triangles (bottom left). The boundary of this surface is the resulting polygon in space (bottom right).

Connecting
v_{3},\dots,v_{n-1}
to
v_{1}
—as in
Figure

1
(top left)—decomposes an abstract polygon into
n-2
triangles. Those triangles are determined up to congruence by their side lengths, which are given by
|e_{1}|=\dots=|e_{n}|=1
and by the diagonal lengths
d_{i}:=|v_{i+2}-v_{1}|
for
i=1,\dots,n-3
. Hence, if we view an equilateral polygon as the boundary of the piecewise linear surface whose faces are these triangles—as in
Figure

1
(bottom left)—then the geometry of the surface (and hence also of its boundary) is completely determined by
d_{1},\dots,d_{n-3}
and the dihedral angles
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n-3}
between adjacent triangles. Therefore,
d_{1},\dots,d_{n-3},\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n-3}
give a system of (almost-everywhere defined) coordinates on
\widehat{\operatorname{Pol}}(n)
.

In symplectic terms, these coordinates are
action-angle coordinates
on
\widehat{\operatorname{Pol}}(n)
, as first explained by Kapovich and Millson
[
51
]
. While the dihedral angles can be chosen completely independently, the diagonal lengths cannot be independent, as they must satisfy the following system of triangle inequalities:

0\leq d_{1}\leq 2\qquad\begin{matrix}1\leq d_{i}+d_{i+1}\\
-1\leq d_{i+1}-d_{i}\leq 1\end{matrix}\qquad 0\leq d_{n-3}\leq 2.

(1)

Let
\mathcal{P}_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-3}
be the convex polytope determined by the above inequalities. Letting
T^{n-3}=(S^{1})^{n-3}
be the product of unit circles, the action-angle coordinates
((d_{1},\dots,d_{n-3}),(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n-3}))
are defined on
\mathcal{P}_{n}\times T^{n-3}
, and the product of Lebesgue measure on
\mathcal{P}_{n}
and the standard product measure on
T^{n-3}
is measure-theoretically equivalent to the natural measure on
\widehat{\operatorname{Pol}}(n)
:

Theorem 1

(Cantarella–Shonkwiler
[
22
]
)
.

The map
\alpha:\mathcal{P}_{n}\times T^{n-3}\to\widehat{\operatorname{Pol}}(n)
defining the action-angle coordinates (as illustrated in
Figure

1
) is measure-preserving.

In consequence, to sample equilateral
n
-gons according to the natural measure on
\widehat{\operatorname{Pol}}(n)
, it suffices to sample
(d_{1},\dots,d_{n-3})
from Lebesgue measure on
\mathcal{P}_{n}
and
(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n-3})
from the product measure on
T^{n-3}
. The latter is straightforward, so the only challenge is sampling from Lebesgue measure on
\mathcal{P}_{n}
. In
[
21
,
18
]
we showed how to do this efficiently, yielding an algorithm for sampling equilateral
n
-gons in expected time
\Theta(n^{2})
.
1
1

1

In practice, the fastest algorithm for Monte Carlo integration on
\widehat{\operatorname{Pol}}(n)
is Cantarella and Schumacher’s CoBarS algorithm
[
19
,
20
]
, which uses reweighted sampling.

Of course, this is only an algorithm for sampling unconfined polygons. We now add a confinement restriction, and consider equilateral polygons in
rooted spherical confinement
, meaning that all vertices lie within a sphere of radius
R
centered at a chosen (root) vertex. Given our choice of coordinates, it is most natural to let
v_{1}
be the root vertex, so that the confinement condition is simply
d_{i}\leq R
for
i=1,\dots,n-3
. Adding these inequalities to the triangle inequalities (
1
) defines a new polytope
\mathcal{P}_{n}(R)
. Letting
\widehat{\operatorname{Pol}}(n;R)
be the space of equilateral
n
-gons in rooted spherical confinement of radius
R
, we have the following analog of
Theorem

1
:

Theorem 2

(Cantarella–Shonkwiler
[
22
]
)
.

The action-angle map
\alpha:\mathcal{P}_{n}(R)\times T^{n-3}\to\widehat{\operatorname{Pol}}(n;R)
is measure-preserving.

We showed in
[
22
]
how to use the hit-and-run Markov chain
[
15
,
81
,
2
]
on
\mathcal{P}_{n}(R)
to get an ergodic Markov chain on
\widehat{\operatorname{Pol}}(n;R)
, and subsequently used this Markov chain to generate large ensembles of tightly-confined polygons which provided new bounds on stick numbers and superbridge indices of a large number of knots
[
39
,
40
,
12
,
78
,
77
,
79
]
.

In contrast, Diao, Ernst, Montemayor, and Ziegler
[
30
]
developed a non-Markov chain method for sampling
\widehat{\operatorname{Pol}}(n;R)
by generating successive marginals of Lebesgue measure on
\mathcal{P}_{n}(R)
and used it to perform various experiments on this model
[
31
,
32
,
36
,
28
,
35
,
34
,
42
,
33
]
.

The above approaches are generic, in the sense that they work for any
R\geq 1
(of course, the radius
R
of the confining sphere cannot be less than 1 since
v_{2}
is always at distance 1 from
v_{1}
), though as pointed out in the introduction, both have substantial drawbacks. In this paper, our goal is to show that we get a dramatically better sampling algorithm in the tightest possible confinement, namely when
R=1
.

3
Polytopes and Alternating Permutations

To do so, note that the confinement inequalities
d_{i}\leq 1
for all
i=1,\dots,n-3
make most of the inequalities in (
1
) redundant: we can simplify the defining inequalities of
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)
as follows:

0\leq d_{i}\leq 1\qquad 1\leq d_{i}+d_{i+1}.

(2)

In other words

\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)=\{(d_{1},\dots,d_{n-3})\in[0,1]^{n-3}:d_{i}+d_{i+1}\geq 1\text{ for all }i=1,\dots,n-4\}.

Though presented in lightly-disguised form, this is a well-known polytope in combinatorics. Under the affine transformation
\varphi:(d_{1},\dots,d_{n-3})\mapsto(d_{1},1-d_{2},d_{3},1-d_{4},\dots)
,
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)
maps to the polytope

\mathcal{O}_{n-3}:=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-3})\in[0,1]^{n-3}:x_{1}\geq x_{2}\leq x_{3}\geq x_{4}\leq\dots\},

which Stanley
[
82
, Section 3.8]
calls the
zig-zag order polytope
, since it is the order polytope of the zig-zag (or fence) poset
[
85
, Example 4.3]
.

A map
\sigma:\mathcal{O}_{n-3}\to\{1,\dots,n-3\}
is order-preserving if and only if the permutation
i\mapsto\sigma(x_{i})
is
alternating
, which we define as follows:

Definition 3
.

A permutation
\pi\in S_{m}
is
alternating
(or
down-up
) if
\pi(1)>\pi(2)<\pi(3)>\dots
, and
reverse-alternating
(or
up-down
) of
\pi(1)<\pi(2)>\pi(3)<\dots
. Let
AP_{m}\subset S_{m}
be the set of alternating permutations, and let
RP_{m}\subset S_{m}
be the set of reverse-alternating permutations.

Any alternating permutation
\pi\in S_{n-3}
determines a simplex

\Delta_{\pi}=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-3})\in[0,1]^{n-3}:x_{\pi^{-1}(1)}\leq x_{\pi^{-1}(2)}\leq\dots\leq x_{\pi^{-1}(n-3)}\}\subset\mathcal{O}_{n-3}.

(3)

Each such
\Delta_{\pi}
is congruent to the standard orthoscheme
\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-3})\in[0,1]^{n-3}:x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\dots\leq x_{n-3}\}
, which has volume
\frac{1}{(n-3)!}
. Hence, since the
\Delta_{\pi}
have disjoint interiors and union equal to
\mathcal{O}_{n-3}
, we see that
\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{O}_{n-3})=\frac{E_{n-3}}{(n-3)!}
, where
E_{n-3}
is the number of alternating (or reverse-alternating) permutations in
S_{n-3}
(see also
[
84
, Exercise 3.66(c)]
). In turn, since the affine transformation
\varphi:\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)\to\mathcal{O}_{n-3}
has determinant
\pm 1
and hence is volume-preserving, we see that
\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{P}_{n}(1))=\frac{E_{n-3}}{(n-3)!}
as well.

The number
E_{n-3}
is known as an
Euler number
, and appears as
A000111
in the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences (OEIS)
[
66
]
. The exponential generating function for the Euler numbers has been known since the 1870s, when André
[
3
,
4
]
proved that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}E_{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=\sec x+\tan x.

See Stanley’s excellent survey
[
82
]
for a wealth of information about alternating permutations and Euler numbers. For example, the Euler numbers satisfy the simple recurrence

2E_{n+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}E_{k}E_{n-k}

and have the convergent asymptotic series

\frac{E_{n}}{n!}=2\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{n+1}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k(n+1)}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^{n+1}}.

(4)

In particular, this implies that
\frac{E_{n}}{n!}
is asymptotically equivalent to
2\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{n+1}
(cf.
[
44
, Example IV.35]
) and hence that

\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal{P}_{n}(1))=\frac{E_{n-3}}{(n-3)!}\sim 2\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{n-2}.

In other words,
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)
is an exponentially small subset of the unit hypercube
[0,1]^{n-3}
, so the naïve sampling strategy of rejection sampling the hypercube is not computationally feasible for large
n
.

Alternatively, the transformation
\psi:(d_{1},\dots,d_{n-3})\mapsto(1-d_{1},\dots,1-d_{n-3})
maps
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)
to

\mathcal{C}_{n-3}:=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-3})\in[0,1]^{n-3}:x_{i}+x_{i+1}\leq 1\text{ for all }i=1,\dots,n-4\},

the
zig-zag chain polytope
(again, see
[
82
, Section 3.8]
or
[
85
, Example 4.3]
). The polytope
\mathcal{C}_{n-3}
seems to have been first considered in questions posed by Stanley
[
83
]
and Doberkat
[
37
]
.

By way of the maps
\varphi
and
\psi
, the polytopes
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)
,
\mathcal{O}_{n-3}
, and
\mathcal{C}_{n-3}
all have the same volume and are combinatorially equivalent; this is a special case of a theorem of Stanley
[
85
, Theorem 2.3]
. In particular, they all have the same number of vertices, namely
F_{n-1}
, the
(n-1)
st Fibonacci number
[
84
, Exercise 1.35(e)]
. More generally, the full
f
-vector of these polytopes is known
[
24
, Corollary 3.5]
.

4
Sampling

Since the
\Delta_{\pi}
which decompose
\mathcal{O}_{n-3}
are indexed by alternating permutations and since sampling from Lebesgue measure on simplices is straightforward, any fast algorithm for sampling random alternating permutations can be adapted to give an algorithm for sampling from Lebesgue measure on
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)
. For example, there are fast Boltzmann samplers
[
14
,
38
]
for generating uniformly random alternating permutations on
\approx n
letters, and a quadratic time algorithm
[
59
]
for generating uniformly random permutations with prescribed descent set based on the density method
[
9
,
58
]
.

The fastest algorithm for directly sampling alternating permutations seems to be that of Bodini, Durand, and Marchal
[
13
]
, which is a modification of an earlier algorithm of Marchal
[
57
]
. Both of these algorithms produce random alternating permutations in
O(n\log n)
time, but in examining the details, it turns out that both are actually sampling points from
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)
in
O(n)
time, applying
\varphi
to get points in
\mathcal{O}_{n-3}
, and then sorting the coordinates to get alternating permutations. Indeed, Marchal’s older algorithm
[
57
]
is most appropriate for our purposes, since it generates samples from Lebesgue measure on
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)
.

The basic observation underlying Marchal’s approach is that a sequence of random draws
u_{1},u_{2},\dots
from Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
can be interpreted as a Markov chain, where the transition probability is just uniform on
[0,1]
(independent of the previous state). The initial segment
(u_{1},\dots,u_{n-3})
is likely to fail one of the defining inequalities
u_{i}+u_{i+1}\geq 1
, so it is unlikely to be in
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)
; if we want to get points in
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)
we should forbid certain transitions. The simplest way to do this is, given the present state
x\in[0,1]
, to define a transition measure
P_{x}:\mathcal{B}\to[0,x]
on the Borel sets of
[0,1]
by

P_{x}(A):=\lambda(A\cap[1-x,1])

where
\lambda
is Lebesgue measure. This is not a probability measure unless
x=1
; to get a probability measure, we might hope to find a conditional density
f_{x}
supported on
[1-x,1]
so that

Q_{x}(A):=\int_{[0,1]}f_{x}(y)d\lambda(y)

is a probability measure, and hence a Markov kernel, and we could generate a sequence
x_{1},x_{2},\dots
by letting
x_{1}=u_{1}\sim U([0,1])
and sampling
x_{i+1}
from
Q_{x_{i}}
for each
i=1,2,\dots
.

The resulting joint density of the initial sequence
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-3})
is then

f_{x_{n-4}}(x_{n-3})\dots f_{x_{1}}(x_{2})\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]},

(5)

which means that the obvious choice of just letting
f_{x}(y)=\mathbf{1}_{\{y\geq 1-x\}}\frac{1}{x}
(that is, normalized Lebesgue measure on
[1-x,1]
) is not promising.

Instead, the expression (
5
) for the joint density suggests that a conditional density
f_{x}
of the form

f_{x}(y)=\mathbf{1}_{\{y\geq 1-x\}}a\frac{h(y)}{h(x)}

(6)

for some function
h
might work well, since then the product in (
5
) telescopes:

f_{x_{n-4}}(x_{n-3})\dots f_{x_{1}}(x_{2})\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}=\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{2}\geq 1-x_{1},\dots,x_{n-3}\geq 1-x_{n-4}\}}a^{n-4}\frac{h(x_{n-3})}{h(x_{1})},

(7)

which only depends on the first and last states.

Suppose we have a conditional density of the form (
6
). While this might seem like a strong constraint on
f_{x}
, densities of this form are in some sense entropy-maximizing
[
11
,
10
]
, so it is not unreasonable to assume they exist.

While the joint distribution of
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-3})
is not yet uniform on
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)
, we can get a new joint distribution which interchanges the roles of the first and last states by reversing the sequence: the density of the reversed initial sequence
(x_{n-3},\dots,x_{1})
is

f_{x_{2}}(x_{1})\dots f_{x_{n-3}}(x_{n-4})\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}=\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{2}\geq 1-x_{1},\dots,x_{n-3}\geq 1-x_{n-4}\}}a^{n-4}\frac{h(x_{1})}{h(x_{n-3})}.

Then we expect a mixture of the initial sequence and the reversed initial sequence to cancel the dependence on first and last states.

More precisely, define the tuple
(d_{1},\dots,d_{n-3})
as follows:

•

with probability
\frac{1}{\frac{h(x_{n-3})}{h(x_{1})}+\frac{h(x_{1})}{h(x_{n-3})}}
, let
(d_{1},\dots,d_{n-3}):=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m-3})
;

•

with probability
\frac{1}{\frac{h(x_{n-3})}{h(x_{1})}+\frac{h(x_{1})}{h(x_{n-3})}}
, let
(d_{1},\dots,d_{n-3}):=(x_{n-3},\dots,x_{1})
;

•

and with the complementary probability

So if we could find a conditional density in the form (

is a density, then it must integrate to 1, so we have the constraint

1=\int_{0}^{1}f_{x}(y)dy=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{1}_{\{y\geq 1-x\}}a\frac{h(y)}{h(x)}dy=\int_{1-x}^{1}a\frac{h(y)}{h(x)}dy=\frac{a}{h(x)}\int_{1-x}^{1}h(y)dy.

In other words, the function

h(x)=a\int_{1-x}^{1}h(y)dy.

This equation has a unique solution

5.1
Entringer Numbers

We now take a digression into some combinatorial results which will help us to determine expected chord lengths in random confined polygons. First, recall the definition of an Entringer number
[

Definition 7
.

Let

and define the
Entringer number

as the number of alternating permutations

\{1,\dots,n+1\}
with
\pi(1)=k+1

See Conway and Guy
[

[

[

[

for more on Entringer numbers, which satisfy the following recurrence (see, e.g.,

E_{0,0}=1,\quad E_{n,0}=0\text{ for }n\geq 1,\quad E_{n+1,k+1}=E_{n+1,k}+E_{n,n-k+1}\text{ for }n\geq k\geq 0.

(9)

In fact, Entringer
[
41
]
showed that the Entringer numbers have the following formula in terms of Euler numbers:

E_{n,k}=\sum_{r=0}^{\lfloor(k-1)/2\rfloor}(-1)^{r}\binom{k}{2r+1}E_{n-2r-1}.

(10)

An exponential generating function for the classical Entringer numbers is also known:

Proposition 8

(see Graham, Knuth, and Patashnik
[

and Stanley
[

, (2.2)]
)

\sum_{m,n=0}^{\infty}E_{m+n,[m,n]}\frac{x^{m}}{m!}\frac{y^{n}}{n!}=\frac{\cos x+\sin x}{\cos(x+y)},

where

[m,n]=\begin{cases}m&\text{if }m+n\text{ odd}\\
n&\text{if }m+n\text{ even.}\end{cases}

If we sum the Entringer numbers
E_{n,k}
over

, we get the count of all alternating permutations on
\{1,\dots,n+1\}
:

E_{n+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}E_{n,k}.

(11)

5.3
The Number of Linear Extensions of

In

to the

[

16
]
, but Atkinson
[
8

showed that there is an

algorithm for computing the number of linear extensions of any poset of size

is a tree, Atkinson’s result applies and, by unpacking the algorithm a bit, we will get a formula for

in terms of Entringer numbers, for which we have the recurrence (

To explain Atkinson’s algorithm, we introduce some terminology.

Definition 10
.

Suppose
P=(X,\preceq)
is a poset, where
X
is a finite set of cardinality
m

\alpha\in X
, the
\alpha
-spectrum of
P
is the sequence
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m})
where
\lambda_{i}
is the number of linear extensions of

so that
\alpha
has rank

Proposition 11

(Atkinson
[
8
, Lemma 1]
)
.

Let
P=(X,\preceq_{P})
and
Q=(Y,\preceq_{Q})
be disjoint posets of sizes
p
and
q

\alpha\in X
and
\beta\in Y
, let
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{p})
be the
\alpha
-spectrum of
P
, and let
(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{q})
be the
\beta
-spectrum of
Q
. Consider the poset
R=(X\cup Y,\preceq)
whose covering relations are those of
P
and
Q
together with the relation
\alpha\preceq\beta
. Then the
\alpha
-spectrum of
R
is
(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{p+q})
where

\nu_{k}=\sum_{i=\max\{1,k-q\}}^{\min\{p,k\}}\lambda_{i}\binom{k-1}{i-1}\binom{p+q-k}{p-i}\sum_{j=k-i+1}^{q}\mu_{j}.

Then Atkinson’s algorithm consists of splitting the cover graph of the poset (which, again, is a tree) at some vertex and recursively computing the spectra of each side, then computing the
\nu_{k}
with
Proposition

11
and summing over
k

Consider
Z_{m+1}
, the zigzag poset. This is the poset on
\{1,\dots,m+1\}
with
1\preceq 2\succeq 3\preceq\dots

1

consists of counts of the number of reverse-alternating permutations
\pi
of
\{1,\dots,m+1\}
with
\pi(1)
specified. Since the reverse map
\pi\mapsto\overline{\pi}
defined by
\overline{\pi}(i):=m+2-\pi(i)
sends reverse-alternating permutations to alternating permutations, these are counted by Entringer numbers:

Lemma 12
.

The 1-spectrum of
Z_{m+1}
is

\left(E_{m,m},\dots,E_{m,0}\right);

that is, the
r
th term in the 1-spectrum is

Summing gives the number of linear extensions of

, which we know is the Euler number
E_{m+1}

Now we will compute the 2-spectrum of

Proposition 13
.

The

is

Proof.

Equivalently, we are counting the number of reverse-alternating permutations

with

P_{\mathfrak{o}}
whose ground set consists of the vertices of
\Gamma
and whose cover relations are determined by the orientation. The
principal number
of
\Gamma
is

\sigma(\Gamma):=\max\{e(P_{\mathfrak{o}}):\mathfrak{o}\text{ is an orientation on }\Gamma\}.

Saito proved that the orientations on a tree
\Gamma
which maximize
e(P_{\mathfrak{o}})
are the two bipartite (or principal) orientations, namely those with no directed path of length 2. In particular, one of the principal orientations on
\Gamma_{n,i}
induces the poset
Z_{n,i}
, proving the following:

Proposition 19
.

For
1\leq i\leq n
,

e(Z_{n,i})=\sigma(\Gamma_{n,i}).

Saito gave a recursive expression for
\sigma(\Gamma)
which was refined by Sano
[
75
]
as follows:

Proposition 20

(Sano
[
75
, Corollary 2.2]
)
.

For a tree
\Gamma
with vertex set
V
,

\sigma(\Gamma)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in V}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{c_{v}}\binom{|\Gamma|-1-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}|\Gamma_{v}^{(i)}|}{|\Gamma_{v}^{(k)}|}\prod_{k=1}^{c_{v}}\sigma(\Gamma_{v}^{(k)})\right],

where the
\Gamma_{v}^{(k)}
with
k=1,\dots,c_{v}
are the connected components of the graph
\Gamma\backslash\{v\}
.

Since we know from
Proposition

19
that
e(Z_{n,i})=\sigma(\Gamma_{n,i})
, a straightforward but tedious calculation yields a recursive formula for
e(Z_{n,i})
:

Corollary 21
.

For
1<i\leq n
,

e(Z_{n,i})=\frac{1}{2}\left[E_{n}+e(Z_{n-1,i-1})+\sum_{j=2}^{i-1}\binom{n}{j-1}E_{j-1}e(Z_{n-j,i-j})+n\binom{n-1}{i-1}E_{i-1}E_{n-i}\right.\\
\left.+\sum_{\ell=i+1}^{n}\binom{n}{\ell}e(Z_{\ell-1,i})E_{n-\ell}\right].

(13)

It is not clear what
Z_{m,i}
should mean when
m<i
, but—following Sano’s example
[
75
]
—it is possible to assign values of
e(Z_{m,i})
for
m<i
so that the recursive expression in
Corollary

21
simplifies to

e(Z_{n,i})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\binom{n}{\ell}e(Z_{\ell-1,i})E_{n-\ell}

(14)

for
n\geq i
. Notice that the coefficient of
E_{n-i}
in (
13
) is
n\binom{n-1}{i-1}E_{i-1}=\binom{n}{i}iE_{i-1}
, so we will automatically assign
e(Z_{i-1,i}):=iE_{i-1}
. Moreover, expanding the
e(Z_{n-j,i-j})
terms in the first sum in (
13
), we see that there is no
E_{n-(i-1)}
term, so we will assign
e(Z_{i-2,i}):=0
.

Therefore, (cf.
[
62
]
), the exponential generating function
f_{i}
of the sequence
e(Z_{0,i}),e(Z_{1,i}),\dots
has the form

f_{i}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{2}f_{i}(x)(\sec x+\tan x)+p_{i}(x),

(15)

where
p_{i}
is a polynomial of degree
i-1
. Using
e(Z_{i-2,i})=0
,
e(Z_{i-1,i})=iE_{i-1}
, and
e(Z_{i+j,i})=e(Z_{i+j,j+1})
for
j=0,\dots,i-2
, we can deduce
i+1
initial conditions

f_{i}^{(i-1)}(0)=0,\quad f_{i}^{(i)}(0)=iE_{i-1},\quad\text{and}\quad f_{i}^{(i+j+1)}(0)=e(Z_{i+j,j+1})\text{ for }j=0,\dots,i-2.

(16)

These are enough to allow us to uniquely solve (
15
) for the exponential generating function
f_{i}
for any particular
i
, though we have not been able to find a general expression for
p_{i}(x)
.

5.5
Generalized Entringer Numbers

As our final excursion in pure combinatorics, we define generalized Entringer numbers and show that they satisfy an analog of
Corollary

14
. This will then provide the connection in
Section

6
between the expected values of the chord lengths
d_{i}
and the
e(Z_{n,i})
.

Definition 22
.

Let
1\leq i\leq n+1
,
0\leq k\leq n
, and define the
generalized Entringer number

E_{n,k}^{(i)}
as follows. If
i
is odd, let
E_{n,k}^{(i)}
be the number of alternating permutations
\pi\in S_{n+1}
with
\pi(i)=k+1
; if
i
is even, let
E_{n,k}^{(i)}
be the number of reverse-alternating permutations
\pi\in S_{n+1}
with
\pi(i)=k+1
.

Note that
E_{n,k}^{(1)}=E_{n,k}
, so this is a generalization of the Entringer numbers.

While the generalized Entringer numbers do not seem to be well-studied, so we don’t know much about them, they certainly satisfy the analog of (
11
): for any
1\leq i\leq n+1
,

E_{n+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}E_{n,k}^{(i)}.

(17)

Our main reason for defining the generalized Entringer numbers is the following generalization of
Corollary

14
:

Theorem 23
.

\sum_{k=1}^{n}kE_{n-1,k-1}^{(i)}=e(Z_{n,i}).

Proof.

Suppose
\sigma
is a linear extension of
Z_{n,i}
; that is,
\sigma:\{0,\dots,n\}\to\{0,\dots,n\}
is an order-preserving bijection:
\sigma(i)\leq\sigma(j)
if
i\preceq j
. If we remove 0 from the ground set of
Z_{n,i}
and define
\widetilde{\sigma}:\{1,\dots,n\}\to\{1,\dots,n\}
by

\widetilde{\sigma}(i)=\begin{cases}\sigma(i)+1&\text{ if }\sigma(i)<\sigma(0)\\
\sigma(i)&\text{ else,}\end{cases}

then
\widetilde{\sigma}
is a linear extension of the zigzag poset
Z_{n}
(if
i
is odd) or its opposite
Z_{n}^{\text{op}}
(if
i
is even).

In other words, the mapping
R:\sigma\mapsto\widetilde{\sigma}
gives a surjective map from the set of linear extensions of
Z_{n,i}
to the set of linear extensions of either
Z_{n}
or
Z_{n}^{\text{op}}
. Of course,
R
is not injective; for example, if
n=2
and
i=1
, both
\sigma_{1}:(0,1,2)\mapsto(0,2,1)
and
\sigma_{2}:(0,1,2)\mapsto(1,2,0)
map to the same alternating permutation
\widetilde{\sigma}:(1,2)\mapsto(2,1)
.

So how many
\sigma
are in the preimage of a given
\widetilde{\sigma}
? The only freedom is in the choice of
\sigma(0)
, and the only constraint is that
\sigma(0)<\sigma(i)=\widetilde{\sigma}(i)
, so there are
\widetilde{\sigma}(i)
possible choices for
\sigma(0)
, namely
0,\dots,\widetilde{\sigma}(i)-1
.

Therefore, each of the
E_{n-1,k-1}^{(i)}
(alternating or reverse-alternating) permutations
\widetilde{\sigma}
in the range of
R
with
\widetilde{\sigma}(i)=k
have
k
preimages, so

\sum_{k=1}^{n}kE_{n-1,k-1}^{(i)}

must equal the total number of elements in the domain of
R
, which is exactly
e(Z_{n,i})
.
∎

Unlike
and
21
,
Theorem

23
does not seem to be of much practical use for computing
e(Z_{n,i})
, but it will be an essential piece of our proof of
Theorem

29
, which relates the expected values of the
d_{i}
to the
e(Z_{n,i})
.

Before we get to that, one last combinatorial observation: we can use the same proof as in
Corollary

15
to show that
Theorem

23
implies the following:

Corollary 24
.

If
i
is odd, the average of
\pi(i)
over all alternating permutations on
\{1,\dots,n\}
is

\mathbb{E}[\pi(i)\,|\,\pi\in AP_{n}]=\frac{e(Z_{n,i})}{E_{n}}.

If
i
is even, the average of
\pi(i)
over all reverse alternating permutations on
\{1,\dots,n\}
is

\mathbb{E}[\pi(i)\,|\,\pi\in RP_{n}]=\frac{e(Z_{n,i})}{E_{n}}.

Similarly, if
\pi
is an alternating permutation, then its reverse
\overline{\pi}
is reverse-alternating, so linearity of expectations implies

\mathbb{E}[\pi(i)\,|\,\pi\in AP_{n}]=n+1-\mathbb{E}[\pi(i)\,|\,\pi\in RP_{n}],

which proves:

Corollary 25
.

If
i
is even, the average of
\pi(i)
over all alternating permutations on
\{1,\dots,n\}
is

\mathbb{E}[\pi(i)\,|\,\pi\in AP_{n}]=n+1-\frac{e(Z_{n,i})}{E_{n}}.

If
i
is odd, the average of
\pi(i)
over all reverse alternating permutations on
\{1,\dots,n\}
is

\mathbb{E}[\pi(i)\,|\,\pi\in RP_{n}]=n+1-\frac{e(Z_{n,i})}{E_{n}}.

6
Expected Chord Lengths

We return to confined polygons and consider the question: what is the expected value of
d_{i}=|v_{i+2}-v_{1}|
as a function on the space
\widehat{\operatorname{Pol}}(n;1)
of equilateral polygons in rooted spherical confinement of radius 1?

Since the vector
(d_{1},\dots,d_{n-3})
is distributed according to (normalized) Lebesgue measure on
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)
,

\mathbb{E}[(d_{1},\dots,d_{n-3})]=(\mathbb{E}[d_{1}],\dots,\mathbb{E}[d_{n-3}])

is exactly the center of mass of
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)
. If we want to compute the center of mass of
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)
, it is enough to compute the center of mass
\overline{x}
of the order polytope
\mathcal{O}_{n-3}=\varphi(\mathcal{P}_{n}(1))
, since then

\mathbb{E}[(d_{1},\dots,d_{n-3})]=\varphi(\overline{x})

(recall that
\varphi
is an involution, so
\varphi=\varphi^{-1}
). The map
\varphi
fixes coordinates of odd index, so we record the following observation:

Lemma 26
.

For
i
odd,

\mathbb{E}[d_{i}]=\overline{x}_{i}.

Similarly, the map
\varphi\circ\psi
fixes coordinates of even index. This map sends
\mathcal{P}_{n}(1)
to the polytope

\mathcal{U}_{n-3}:=\{(y_{1},\dots,y_{n-3})\in[0,1]^{n-3}:y_{1}\leq y_{2}\geq y_{3}\leq y_{4}\geq\dots\},

which is the order polytope of
Z_{n-3}^{\text{op}}
, the opposite of the zigzag poset. Analogously to
\mathcal{U}_{n-3}
, any map
\sigma:\mathcal{U}_{n-3}\to\{1,\dots,n-3\}
is order-preserving if and only if the permutation
i\mapsto\sigma(y_{i})
is reverse-alternating, and hence
\mathcal{U}_{n-3}
decomposes into orthoschemes

\Delta_{\pi}=\{(y_{1},\dots,y_{n-3})\in[0,1]^{n-3}:y_{\pi^{-1}(1)}\leq y_{\pi^{-1}(2)}\leq\dots\leq y_{\pi^{-1}(n-3)}\}\subset\mathcal{U}_{n-3}

(18)

indexed by reverse-alternating permutations.

Let
\overline{y}
be the center of mass of
\mathcal{U}_{n-3}
. Analogously to
Lemma

26
we have

Lemma 27
.

For
i
even,

\mathbb{E}[d_{i}]=\overline{y}_{i}.

These interpretations are not obviously helpful, since computing the centroid of a convex polytope is
\#P
-hard, even when the polytope is an order polytope
[
71
]
.
However, we have unimodular triangulations of
\mathcal{O}_{n-3}
and
\mathcal{U}_{n-3}
into the orthoschemes
\Delta_{\pi}
defined in (
3
) and (
18
), so the center of mass of
\mathcal{O}_{n-3}
is the mean of the centers of masses of the orthoschemes:

\overline{x}=\frac{1}{E_{n-3}}\;\sum_{\pi\in AP_{n-3}}\;m(\Delta_{\pi}),

(19)

where
m(\Delta_{\pi})
is the center of mass of
\Delta_{\pi}
, and similarly

\overline{y}=\frac{1}{E_{n-3}}\;\sum\limits_{\pi\in RP_{n-3}}\;m(\Delta_{\pi}).

(20)

In turn, the center of mass of a simplex is simply the center of mass of its vertices (see Krantz, McCarthy, and Parks
[
54
]
for much more on centroids of simplices), so

m(\Delta_{\pi})=\frac{1}{n-2}\;\sum_{v\text{ a vertex of }\Delta_{\pi}}\;v.

(21)

A vertex
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n-3})
of an orthoscheme
\Delta_{\pi}
is determined by assignments of 0s and 1s to the coordinates that satisfy the system of inequalities

v_{\pi^{-1}(1)}\leq v_{\pi^{-1}(2)}\leq\dots\leq v_{\pi^{-1}(n-3)}.

Clearly, then, only one vertex (namely
(1,\dots,1)
) can have
v_{\pi^{-1}(1)}=1
, only two can have
v_{\pi^{-1}(2)}=1
, etc. In other words:

Lemma 28
.

Let
\pi\in S_{n-3}
be a permutation, and let
\Delta_{\pi}
be the corresponding orthoscheme. Then
\pi(i)
of the vertices of
\Delta_{\pi}
have
i
th coordinate equal to 1.

Combining this lemma with (
19
) and (
21
) yields

\overline{x}_{i}=\frac{1}{(n-2)E_{n-3}}\;\sum_{\pi\in AP_{n-3}}\;\pi(i).

In turn, if
i
is odd then there are
E_{n-4,k-1}^{(i)}
alternating permutations with
\pi(i)=k
, so we can re-write the above sum as

\overline{x}_{i}=\frac{1}{(n-2)E_{n-3}}\sum_{k=1}^{n-3}kE_{n-4,k-1}^{(i)}=\frac{e(Z_{n-3,i})}{(n-2)E_{n-3}}

by
Theorem

23
. Similarly, when
i
is even we have

\overline{y}_{i}=\frac{1}{(n-2)E_{n-3}}\sum_{k=1}^{n-3}kE_{n-4,k-1}^{(i)}=\frac{e(Z_{n-3,i})}{(n-2)E_{n-3}}.

Combined with
Lemmas

26
and
27
, this tells us the expected values of the
d_{i}
:

Theorem 29
.

The expected value of the
i
th chordlength
d_{i}
of a random confined equilateral polygon is

\mathbb{E}[d_{i}]=\frac{e(Z_{n-3,i})}{(n-2)E_{n-3}}.

Note that the denominator is
A065619
in OEIS
[
66
]
.

For example, we know that
e(Z_{n-3,1})=e(Z_{n-3,n-3})=E_{n-2}
, so we see that average first and last chord lengths are

\mathbb{E}[d_{1}]=\mathbb{E}[d_{n-3}]=\frac{E_{n-2}}{(n-2)E_{n-3}}\sim\frac{2}{\pi}\approx 0.6366

using the asymptotic expansion (
4
).

More generally, we can use Sano’s exponential generating functions for
e(Z_{n,1})
,
e(Z_{n,2})
, and
e(Z_{n,3})

[
75
]
and straightforward generalizations to slightly bigger
i
given by using
Corollary

21
or solving (
15
) and (
16
) to prove the following asymptotics for a few of the
\mathbb{E}[d_{i}]
:

Proposition 30
.

The first few chord lengths have the following asymptotics as the number of edges

:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[d_{1}]

\displaystyle\sim\frac{2}{\pi}\approx 0.6366

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[d_{2}]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[d_{3}]

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\pi}{4}-2+\frac{6}{\pi}\approx 0.6952

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[d_{4}]

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}-\frac{\pi}{2}+4-\frac{8}{\pi}\approx 0.7051

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[d_{5}]

\displaystyle\sim\frac{5\pi^{3}}{192}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}+\frac{3\pi}{4}-4+\frac{10}{\pi}\approx 0.7018

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[d_{6}]

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\pi^{4}}{120}-\frac{5\pi^{3}}{96}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}-\pi+6-\frac{12}{\pi}\approx 0.7029

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[d_{7}]

\displaystyle\sim\frac{61\pi^{5}}{23040}-\frac{\pi^{4}}{60}+\frac{5\pi^{3}}{64}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}+\frac{5\pi}{4}-6+\frac{14}{\pi}\approx 0.7025.

This suggests the following conjecture:

For

,

\mathbb{E}[d_{k}]\sim\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-2}(-1)^{\ell+1}\frac{\ell E_{k-\ell}}{2^{k-\ell-1}(k-\ell)!}\pi^{k-\ell-1}+(-1)^{k}\left(2\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor-\frac{2k}{\pi}\right).

Even for relatively small
i
, these values are rapidly approaching the overall limiting value

from

We now move beyond the realm of things we can prove and use CPOP to explore the average total curvature of tightly confined equilateral polygons.

The
total curvature
of a polygon
P
represented by edge vectors
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
is the quantity

\kappa(P)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(e_{i},e_{i+1}),

where
\phi(v,w)
is the angle between the vectors
v
and
w
(in particular,
\phi(e_{i},e_{i+1})
is the
i
th exterior angle of
P
) and we compute indices cyclically so that
e_{n+1}=e_{1}
. To compare total curvatures for polygons with different lengths, we will use the
average turning angle
for
P
, which is simply the average of the
\phi(e_{i},e_{i+1})
, namely
\frac{\kappa(P)}{n}
.

For unconfined equilateral polygons, Grosberg
[
47
]
showed that the expected total curvature is

\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\operatorname{Pol}}(n)}[\kappa]=n\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{3\pi}{8}+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right);

an integral formula for the exact value of
\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\operatorname{Pol}}(n)}[\kappa]
is given in
[
22
, Theorem 12]
. When edge vectors are chosen independently (that is, in a random walk), the expected turning angle is exactly
\frac{\pi}{2}
; intuitively, the excess in Grosberg’s formula is due to the fact that each edge in a closed polygon feels some pressure to return to the starting point. Notice that the average turning angle is decreasing as a function of
n
, as one would expect: the closure constraint is felt by all edges equally, so with more edges the effect on each individual edge is smaller.

In tight confinement consecutive edges can almost never point in even approximately the same direction, so we expect the typical turning angle to be larger, and hence for total curvature to increase; see, e.g.,
[
22
,
35
,
29
]
.

and found that we get an excellent fit (

. This is somewhat bigger than but broadly compatible with Diao, Ernst, Rawdon, and Ziegler’s estimate of

((d_{1},\dots,d_{n-3}),(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n-3}))
give coordinates on
\widehat{\operatorname{Pol}}(n;1)
, we can compute the asymptotic expected value of
\phi_{i}
by expressing it as a function of the action-angle coordinates, and then integrating against the asymptotic distribution.

Proposition 32
.

The turning angle
\phi_{i}
can be expressed in terms of action-angle coordinates as

\phi_{i}=\arccos\left(\frac{d_{i-2}^{2}+d_{i}^{2}}{2}-1-\frac{(d_{i-2}^{2}+d_{i-1}^{2}-1)(d_{i-1}^{2}+d_{i}^{2}-1)-A\cos\theta_{i-1}}{4d_{i-1}^{2}}\right),

where

A=\sqrt{(d_{i-2}+d_{i-1}-1)(d_{i-2}-d_{i-1}+1)(-d_{i-2}+d_{i-1}+1)(d_{i-2}+d_{i-1}+1)}\\
\times\sqrt{(d_{i-1}+d_{i}-1)(d_{i-1}-d_{i}+1)(-d_{i-1}+d_{i}+1)(d_{i-1}+d_{i}+1)}.

We defer the proof of this proposition until the end of the section.

The goal is to integrate the expression (
22
) against the distribution of
((d_{1},\dots,d_{n-3}),(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n-3}))
. Since (
22
) depends only on
d_{i-2},d_{i-1},d_{i},
and
\theta_{i-1}

(d_{i-2},d_{i-1},d_{i},\theta_{i-1})
.

The random variate
\theta_{i-1}
is independent of the rest and has a very simple distribution: it is uniform on
[0,2\pi)
.

In the asymptotic limit,
Corollary

6
implies that the diagonal lengths
d_{i-2},d_{i-1}
, and
d_{i}
will be chosen from the limiting density
f(t)=1-\cos(\pi t)
. However, if we just take independent draws from this distribution, we have no guarantee that they will satisfy the defining inequalities
1\leq d_{i-2}+d_{i-1}
and
1\leq d_{i-1}+d_{i}
.

To guarantee that the inequalities are satisfied, we can generate
d_{i-2}
with density
f
, and then generate
d_{i-1}
with conditional density
f_{d_{i-2}}
and
d_{i}
with conditional density
f_{d_{i-1}}
, proving the following.

Theorem 33
.

The expected turning angle in the asymptotic limit is

\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\phi_{i}]=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\!\int_{0}^{1}\!\phi_{i}f(d_{i-2})f_{d_{i-2}}(d_{i-1})f_{d_{i-1}}(d_{i})\operatorname{d}\!d_{i-2}\operatorname{d}\!d_{i-1}\operatorname{d}\!d_{i}\operatorname{d}\!\theta_{i-1}\\
=\frac{\pi}{8}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\!\!\int_{1-d_{i-1}}^{1}\!\int_{1-d_{i-2}}^{1}\!\int_{0}^{1}\!\phi_{i}(1-\cos(\pi d_{i-2}))\frac{\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}d_{i}\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}d_{i-2}\right)}\operatorname{d}\!d_{i-2}\operatorname{d}\!d_{i-1}\operatorname{d}\!d_{i}\operatorname{d}\!\theta_{i-1},

(23)

where
\phi_{i}
has the explicit expression (
22
).

Numerically integrating this in
Mathematica
yields
2.14625
(to 5 decimal places), perfectly matching our fit for the asymptotic limit of average turning angle.

All of this gives compelling evidence for the following conjecture on the form of the asymptotic expansion of expected turning angle for random polygons in
\widehat{\operatorname{Pol}}(n;1)
.

Conjecture 34
.

For large
n
, the expected
i
th turning angle of a random polygon in
\widehat{\operatorname{Pol}}(n;1)
is

\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}]=\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\phi_{i}]-\frac{b}{n}+o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right),

where
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\phi_{i}]\approx 2.14625
is the explicit integral given in (
23
), and
b
is a numerical constant
b\approx 0.46742
.

Equivalently, the expected total curvature on
\widehat{\operatorname{Pol}}(n;1)
is

\mathbb{E}[\kappa]=\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\phi_{i}]n-b+o(1).

We don’t have a guess for an explicit formula for
b
.

Unlike with unconfined polygons, the average turning angle increases with
n
rather than decreasing. This phenomenon only seems to occur in very tight confinement (see, e.g.,
[
22
, Figure 12]
).

We conclude this section with the proof of
Proposition

32
.

Proof of
Proposition

32
.

Since the edges
e_{i}=v_{i+1}-v_{i}
and
e_{i+1}=v_{i+2}-v_{i+1}
have unit length,
\cos\phi_{i}
is simply their dot product:

\cos\phi_{i}=(v_{i+1}-v_{i})\cdot(v_{i+2}-v_{i+1})=v_{i+1}\cdot v_{i+2}-v_{i}\cdot v_{i+2}-\|v_{i+1}\|^{2}+v_{i}\cdot v_{i+1}.

(24)

Since the root vertex
v_{1}
is at the origin,

\|v_{i+1}\|^{2}=d_{i-1}^{2}

(25)

and

1=\|e_{i}\|^{2}=(v_{i+1}-v_{i})\cdot(v_{i+1}-v_{i})=\|v_{i+1}\|^{2}-2v_{i}\cdot v_{i+1}+\|v_{i}\|^{2}=d_{i-1}^{2}-2v_{i}\cdot v_{i+1}+d_{i-2}^{2},

so

v_{i}\cdot v_{i+1}=\frac{d_{i-1}^{2}+d_{i-2}^{2}-1}{2}\quad\text{and similarly}\quad v_{i+1}\cdot v_{i+2}=\frac{d_{i}^{2}+d_{i-1}^{2}-1}{2}.

(26)

Plugging (
25
) and (
26
) into (
24
) yields

\cos\phi_{i}=\frac{d_{i-2}^{2}+d_{i}^{2}}{2}-1-v_{i}\cdot v_{i+2}.

(27)

On the other hand, the dihedral angle
\theta_{i-1}
is the angle between the unit normal vectors
\frac{v_{i}\times v_{i+1}}{\|v_{i}\times v_{i+1}\|}
and
\frac{v_{i+1}\times v_{i+2}}{\|v_{i+1}\times v_{i+2}\|}
to the two triangles, so

\cos\theta_{i-1}=\frac{(v_{i}\times v_{i+1})\cdot(v_{i+1}\times v_{i+2})}{\|v_{i}\times v_{i+1}\|\|v_{i+1}\times v_{i+2}\|}.

(28)

Using the Binet–Cauchy identity, the numerator

(v_{i}\times v_{i+1})\cdot(v_{i+1}\times v_{i+2})=(v_{i}\cdot v_{i+1})(v_{i+1}\cdot v_{i+2})-(v_{i}\cdot v_{i+2})(v_{i+1}\cdot v_{i+1})\\
=\frac{1}{4}(d_{i-2}^{2}+d_{i-1}^{2}-1)(d_{i-1}^{2}+d_{i}^{2}-1)-d_{i-1}^{2}v_{i}\cdot v_{i+2}

(29)

where we’ve used (
25
) and (
26
) to express three of the dot products in terms of the
d
’s.

In the denominator of (
28
),
\|v_{i}\times v_{i+1}\|
is twice the area of the triangle with side lengths
d_{i-2},d_{i-1}
, and
1
. We can compute this area using Heron’s formula, yielding

\|v_{i}\times v_{i+1}\|=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(d_{i-2}+d_{i-1}-1)(d_{i-2}-d_{i-1}+1)(-d_{i-2}+d_{i-1}+1)(d_{i-2}+d_{i-1}+1)}.

Substituting this, the analogous formula for
\|v_{i+1}\times v_{i+2}\|
, and (
29
) into (
28
) yields

\cos\theta_{i-1}=\frac{(d_{i-2}^{2}+d_{i-1}^{2}-1)(d_{i-1}^{2}+d_{i}^{2}-1)-4d_{i-1}^{2}(v_{i}\cdot v_{i+2})}{A}.

(30)

Solving (
30
) for
v_{i}\cdot v_{i+2}
and plugging into (
27
) gives an expression for
\cos\phi_{i}
:

\cos\phi_{i}=\frac{d_{i-2}^{2}+d_{i}^{2}}{2}-1-\frac{(d_{i-2}^{2}+d_{i-1}^{2}-1)(d_{i-1}^{2}+d_{i}^{2}-1)-A\cos\theta_{i-1}}{4d_{i-1}^{2}}.

Taking the inverse cosine of both sides completes the proof.
∎

8
Questions and Future Directions

From our point of view, the most pressing question coming out of this work is whether it is possible to modify CPOP to cover confinement radii other than
R=1
. It is of substantial interest how confinement radius affects both geometric features like total curvature and topological features like knotting
[
33
,
29
,
28
,
34
,
35
,
42
]
, and a generalized CPOP that could handle a range of
R
would provide a new tool for exploring these problems.

For example, since
\mathcal{P}_{n}(R)\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{n}(R^{\prime})
when
R\leq R^{\prime}
, similar combinatorial interpretations of
\mathcal{P}_{n}(R)
for integer
R
might enable reasonably efficient rejection samplers even for non-integer
R
. In an unpublished paper
[
23
]
, Chapman and Schreyer gave a combinatorial orthoscheme decomposition of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, though it is somewhat unwieldy in practice. This might be a useful starting point for further exploration.

It would be satisfying to get a more elementary description of the quantity
E_{\infty}[\phi_{i}]\approx 2.14625
than that in (
23
) or to get any sort of analytic description of the quantity
b\approx 0.46742
appearing in
34
. Indeed, without more understanding of these quantities, a proof of
34
seems out of reach.

More generally, in the
R=1
regime large-
n
numerical explorations of other geometric quantities—like total torsion or writhe—or of topological features—like knotting—are now possible. In particular, since complicated knots are likely to be highly compact
[
28
,
34
,
65
,
67
]
, polygons generated by CPOP are likely to give a form of “enriched sampling” for complicated knots (cf.
[
40
]
). Moreover, it is generally believed that tightly confined polygons preferentially form prime knots
[
42
]
, as opposed to unconfined polygons, which are exponentially likely to be composite
[
27
]
; as a model for random knots, where does
\widehat{\operatorname{Pol}}(n;1)
fit into Even-Zohar’s taxonomy
[
43
]
?
