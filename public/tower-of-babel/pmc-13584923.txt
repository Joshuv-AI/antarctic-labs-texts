Persistent Müllerian structures in an infant with complete androgen insensitivity syndrome
Authors: Kimberly K Vidmar, Tess I Jewell, Kim M Keppler-Noreuil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractComplete androgen insensitivity syndrome (CAIS) is a variation in sex characteristics that occurs due to a pathogenic loss-of-function variant in the androgen receptor gene. CAIS classically presents as an individual with 46,XY karyotype, apparent female external genitalia, a blind-ending vaginal pouch, internal testes, and absence of Müllerian structures; however, there are case reports describing Müllerian remnants in CAIS with unclear underlying pathophysiology. We describe a case of a 46,XY infant with CAIS and a uterus on postnatal imaging. Genetic testing revealed a hemizygous pathogenic androgen receptor (AR) gene variant and 2 variants of unknown significance in CYP11B1 and WNT4, which offer theoretical mechanisms for persistent Müllerian structures. Management included multidisciplinary care with counseling around sex assignment and anticipatory guidance regarding gonadal surveillance, gender identity, and future puberty. This case contributes to the limited literature on atypical phenotypes in CAIS demonstrating the need for further research into the underlying genetic and pathophysiologic mechanisms of this condition.Keywords: androgen insensitivity syndrome, persistent Müllerian structures, variations in sex characteristics, differences of sex development, variants of unknown significance

Introduction

Variations in sex characteristics (VSC), sometimes referred to as differences of sex development (DSD), describe conditions in which sex chromosomes, gonads, and/or genitalia do not conform to the typical societal expectations of male or female bodies [1]. Androgen insensitivity syndrome (AIS) is a rare X-linked genetic condition caused by loss-of-function pathogenic variants in the androgen receptor (AR) gene leading to partial or complete cellular inability to respond to androgens [2]. The estimated incidence of CAIS is 1 in 40 000 to 60 000 births [3, 4]. Patients with CAIS and 46,XY karyotype with the SRY gene are born with apparent female external genitalia with labia, a clitoris, and typically a blind-ending vaginal pouch. They also have internal testes, which may be found in the abdomen, inguinal canal, or labia majora. They typically have normal to elevated levels of androgens without the expected physiologic changes (eg, testicular descent, differentiation of the Wolffian ducts, and virilization of external genitalia) and have elevated luteinizing hormone (LH) levels due to lack of negative feedback. Some testosterone produced by Leydig cells will be aromatized to estrogen, which stimulates breast development and growth during puberty [5]. Sertoli cells produce anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), which typically induces regression of the Müllerian ducts, preventing the formation of the uterus, cervix, and upper vagina [6].

Rare cases of patients with CAIS and persistent Müllerian structures are reported, with variable genetic pathogenic variants in the AR gene [7-16], but the mechanism for the persistence of Müllerian structures is unknown. We describe an infant with undervirilized external genitalia, bilateral inguinal gonads, persistent Müllerian structures and 46,XY, SRY positive genetics who was found to have a pathogenic loss-of-function variant in the AR gene, consistent with a diagnosis of AIS. Additional variants of unknown significance (VUS) were identified in WNT4 and CYP11B2. We compare this case to other similar but rarely reported individuals with AIS and explore possible pathogenetic mechanisms underlying the atypical finding of persistent Müllerian structures.

Case presentation

An infant born at 39 weeks 1 day via spontaneous vaginal delivery was admitted for routine newborn care and VSC workup. Noninvasive prenatal testing (NIPT) twice demonstrated XY fetal chromosomes, but a 35-week ultrasound showed a possible phallic structure, absent scrotum, and possible labial scrotal folds. Family history was notable for a maternal great-aunt with Müllerian agenesis or Mayer-Rokitansky-Küster-Hauser (MRKH) syndrome.

Diagnostic assessment

Postnatal exam demonstrated labial folds without posterior fusion, no phallic structure, a vaginal opening, no visible urethral opening, and a palpable mass in left suprapubic region. Postnatal ultrasound showed bilateral homogenous intra-abdominal gonads and presence of a uterus as well as what appeared to be a cervix connected to the vaginal opening (Fig. 1). Initial newborn screen demonstrated a normal 17-hydroxyprogesterone (17-OHP) level, and second-tier steroid profiling (including 17-OHP, cortisol, 11-deoxycortisol, androstenedione, and 21-deoxycortisol) was normal with the exception of mild elevation in androstenedione. Examination at 13 days of life revealed gonads easily palpable in the bilateral inguinal canals. The family opted to assign female sex of rearing and use she/her pronouns. Luteinizing hormone (LH), follicular stimulating hormone (FSH), total testosterone, and AMH were obtained at 18 weeks of life and are shown in Table 1. Genetic testing was conducted using a focused Disorder of Sex Development Gene panel with 53 genes, which identified a hemizygous pathogenic AR gene variant: AR:c.1462del (p.Gln488Argfs*22), which is associated with X-linked AIS. There were also 2 VUS identified: a heterozygous c.55C>A (p.Gln19Lys) in CYP11B1, which is associated with autosomal recessive congenital adrenal hyperplasia due to 11-beta-hydroxylase deficiency and autosomal dominant familial hyperaldosteronism type 1; and c.875G>C (p.Arg292Thr) in WNT4, which is associated with autosomal dominant Müllerian agenesis (MRKH syndrome). Targeted genetic testing for the AR and WNT4 variants in this patient's mother demonstrated the variants in both genes.

At 11 months of life, an external examination was notable for bilateral gonads palpable at the internal inguinal ring and a reducible right-sided hydrocele. Cotton tip applicator exam of a visible vaginal introitus revealed a vaginal length of less than 1 cm. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the abdomen and pelvis completed at 13 months of age showed a tiny focus of tissue along the posterior bladder wall, anterior to the rectum, presumed to be a small uterus (Fig. 2).

Treatment

Management to date has included multidisciplinary care from pediatric endocrinology, urology, gynecology, and genetics, including counseling and support around sex assignment and anticipatory guidance about gonadal surveillance, gender identity, and future puberty. The patient has not yet undergone medical or surgical intervention.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient continues to follow with a multidisciplinary care team with expertise in VSC for ongoing monitoring and discussion of potential future interventions. If surgical intervention for the hydrocele is warranted, there will be consideration of gonadectomy vs gonad biopsy vs repositioning of the gonads.

Discussion

We report a case of CAIS caused by a known pathogenic frameshift variant in the AR gene with persistent Müllerian structures, notably a normal-appearing uterus on postnatal ultrasound. Müllerian duct remnants are thought to be uncommon in CAIS, but previous reports suggest the presence of Müllerian duct structures in up to one-third of CAIS cases [7-16]. Individuals with CAIS are expected to have normal production of AMH by Sertoli cells, resulting in absent uteri, fallopian tubes, and upper vaginas [15]. The mechanism for persistence of these structures in some cases of CAIS is unclear. Persistence could theoretically be related to coincidental occurrence of pathogenic variants in AMH genes affecting the androgen receptor and AMH signaling, exposure to drugs such as diethylstilbestrol (DES), defects in the secretion or function of AMH, the loss of AMH paracrine action due to early testicular descent, estrogen interference with AMH signaling, and a complex gene regulatory network beyond AMH-AMH-2 [10, 12, 13, 15, 16]. However, increasing reports of cases of CAIS with Müllerian remnants without co-occurring pathogenic gene variants in AMH makes structural defects in AMH unlikely. DES was banned from use in pregnancy in 1971, making a potential influence of DES improbable. Defective secretion of AMH is unlikely to play a role given robust AMH levels being reported in numerous cases, including ours [8-10]. A proposed mechanism for persistent Müllerian structures is loss of AMH paracrine action due to early testicular decent; however, there have been cases in which the gonads were found adjacent to well-developed persistent Müllerian structures [12].

Variability of findings in AIS, including presence of Müllerian structures could be caused by somatic mosaicism of AR gene variants in affected tissues [19]. De novo and, in particular, somatic gene variants, occur at an unexpectedly high rate in AIS. Different degrees of androgen insensitivity have been reported to be caused by identical pathogenic variants in the AR gene. In some cases, the phenotypic variability was due to somatic mosaicism (ie, somatic pathogenic variants that occurred in only certain cells of androgen-sensitive tissue) [20].

Another possible etiology for the persistence of Müllerian structures with CAIS is the presence of co-occurring pathogenic variants in other genes, in addition to the AR variant, which have not been recognized before the use of broader genetic testing including next-generation sequencing and large gene panels. The DSD gene panel in our case identified 2 gene variants, WNT4 and CYP11B1, presently classified as VUS. Pathogenic variants in these genes are known to cause VSC, which could offer contributing potential mechanisms for our patient's persistent Müllerian structures. Firstly, homozygous mutations in CYP11B1 are associated with autosomal recessive congenital adrenal hyperplasia due to 11-beta-hydroxylase deficiency. The single CYP11B1 variant detected in our case is likely not clinically relevant as it is a heterozygous nucleotide variant. Furthermore, with 11-beta-hydroxylase deficiency we would expect excess androgens during fetal development to cause virilization in individuals with 46,XX karyotype, but no clinically significant phenotype in individuals with 46,XY karyotype. However, one could postulate that if a patient had 11-beta-hydroxylase deficiency in addition to CAIS, this could lead to even higher androgen levels, some of which would be peripherally converted to estrogen. While not completely understood, it has been theorized that a hyperestrogenic environment may interfere with the paracrine action of AMH, resulting in persistent Müllerian structures [9]. The lack of significantly elevated androgen levels or low cortisol makes this mechanism unlikely in our case. Secondly, pathogenic mutations in WNT4 are associated with a rare subtype of autosomal dominant Müllerian agenesis (MRKH syndrome). This is particularly interesting given the family history of a maternal aunt with MRKH syndrome. Since WNT4 is a signaling protein involved in development of the ovaries and Müllerian structures, one could postulate if this variant in WNT4 is an activating pathogenic variant, it could explain the persistence of Müllerian structures. Lastly, although comprehensive gene panels are standard in VSC evaluation, they may miss pathogenic variants. While whole-genome sequencing was unavailable and not covered by insurance in this case, it may be valuable in the evaluation of atypical VSCs.

While lack of virilization in this infant with 46,XY karyotype strongly suggested CAIS, the presence of Müllerian structures called this diagnosis into question prior to confirmatory genetic testing. Before routine NIPT, also known as cell-free DNA (cfDNA), CAIS often went undiagnosed until pubertal age or adulthood with presentation of primary amenorrhea. CAIS cannot be reliably diagnosed biochemically in infancy, as testosterone levels are often paradoxically low. This is likely due to lack of the postnatal testosterone and LH surge observed in most CAIS infants. The absence of this “mini-puberty” surge is not fully understood but is thought to reflect a requirement for prenatal androgen action on the hypothalamic-pituitary axis to prime the postnatal LH surge. Without functional androgen receptors this priming does not occur resulting in low LH and consequently low testosterone postnatally [5]. Genetic testing both prenatally and postnatally facilitates prompt diagnosis and counseling to assist families with children with VSC in navigating sex-of-rearing decisions and, in some cases, raising a child in a generally binary-gendered society. While prior observations report that 92% to 100% of individuals with CAIS maintain a female gender identity [21, 22], recent evidence challenges the perception of uniformly female gender development in CAIS [23]. These nuances are imperative in appropriately counseling families around sex assignment and gender development outcomes.

In CAIS, the risk of gonadal malignancy pre-pubertally is only 0.8% to 2%, but it increases gradually after puberty [5]. Current management recommendations favor postponing gonadectomy until after spontaneous puberty to allow feminization and to avoid the need for hormone replacement therapy. Whether to reposition the gonad(s) subcutaneously or within the abdomen during a hernia or hydrocele repair necessitates counseling and shared decision-making regarding increased risk of malignancy associated with intra-abdominal testes, patient preference, physical comfort, aesthetic concerns, and future surgical plans.

In conclusion, this novel case of CAIS caused by a known frameshift pathogenic variant in the AR gene with persistent Müllerian structures highlights several unique features that prompt thoughtful management considerations and underscore areas for future investigation.
