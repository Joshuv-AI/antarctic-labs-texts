Recognition of Patau Syndrome (Trisomy 13) Based on Clinical Features in a Resource‐Limited Setting: A Case Report
Authors: Sayed Hussain Amiri, Nasir Ahmad Anwari, Dawood Hossaini, Abdulhaq Qiam, Murtaza Haidary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPatau syndrome (trisomy 13) is a life‐limiting chromosomal disorder with multiple congenital anomalies. We report a term male neonate with bilateral cleft lip and palate, aplasia cutis congenita, postaxial polydactyly, hypotonia, congenital heart disease, and presumed neonatal sepsis. Clinical diagnosis was made without cytogenetic confirmation; the infant died on day five.Keywords: aplasia cutis congenita, cleft lip and palate, congenital anomalies, congenital heart disease, neonate, Patau syndrome, postaxial polydactyly, trisomy 13

Introduction

Patau syndrome (trisomy 13) is a rare autosomal chromosomal disorder caused by an extra copy of chromosome 13. It is the third most common autosomal trisomy after trisomies 21 and 18, occurring in approximately 1 in 10,000–20,000 live births. Most cases result from meiotic nondisjunction, and advanced maternal age is a recognized risk factor [1]. Patau syndrome is characterized by severe multisystem congenital anomalies involving the central nervous, cardiovascular, craniofacial, renal, and musculoskeletal systems. Common features include holoprosencephaly, cleft lip and/or palate, microphthalmia, postaxial polydactyly, congenital heart defects, scalp defects, and profound neurodevelopmental impairment [1, 2, 3]. Historically, trisomy 13 has been associated with high neonatal and infant mortality, with most affected infants dying within the first year of life [2]. However, recent studies have demonstrated improved survival among selected patients receiving intensive medical, surgical, and multidisciplinary supportive care, challenging the traditional perception of the condition as uniformly lethal [3, 4, 5]. Advances in prenatal ultrasonography, noninvasive prenatal testing, and cytogenetic analysis have significantly improved antenatal detection of trisomy 13 in high‐resource settings [3, 4]. In resource‐constrained settings, limited access to prenatal screening, genetic testing, and specialized neonatal care often delays the diagnosis of Patau syndrome. Therefore, careful clinical recognition of characteristic congenital anomalies remains crucial for timely diagnosis, family counseling, and appropriate management [2, 5]. We report a neonate born in a setting where access to advanced genetic diagnostic services was limited, including the unavailability of conventional karyotyping, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), and chromosomal microarray analysis. This case highlights the continuing importance of meticulous clinical examination and recognition of characteristic phenotypic features in establishing a presumptive diagnosis when cytogenetic confirmation is unavailable.

Case History/Examination

A term male neonate was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit immediately after birth because of poor postnatal adaptation and multiple congenital anomalies. He was the second child of healthy, non‐consanguineous parents. His mother was a 22‐year‐old woman with no history of chronic medical illness, antenatal complications, medication use, or exposure to known teratogens during pregnancy. Her obstetric history was notable for one previous unexplained neonatal death. There was no family history of congenital malformations, chromosomal abnormalities, or inherited genetic disorders. The pregnancy was otherwise uneventful, and the infant was delivered by elective cesarean section at term.

On admission, the neonate had a heart rate of 158 beats/min, respiratory rate of 63 breaths/min, and axillary temperature of 38.2°C. Capillary refill time was less than 2 s. No jaundice, pallor, cyanosis, or peripheral edema was observed. Ophthalmologic examination demonstrated normally developed globes without evidence of microphthalmia, anophthalmia, or coloboma. Neurological examination demonstrated generalized hypotonia, diminished spontaneous activity, an absent sucking reflex, and a sluggish and incomplete Moro reflex. The infant also showed reduced responsiveness to tactile and auditory stimuli and decreased alertness.

Physical examination revealed multiple congenital anomalies, including bilateral complete cleft lip and palate (Figure 1), aplasia cutis congenita involving the vertex of the scalp (Figure 2), and bilateral postaxial polydactyly affecting both hands (Figure 3). These characteristic clinical findings are further illustrated in Video 1. Cardiac auscultation detected a Grade II/VI systolic murmur. Respiratory examination revealed coarse bilateral breath sounds without focal crackles. Urine output and passage of meconium were normal.

Differential Diagnosis

Important clinical differentials include Meckel‐Gruber syndrome, Smith‐Lemli‐Opitz syndrome, and pseudotrisomy 13. Meckel‐Gruber syndrome is characterized by the clinical trial of occipital encephalocele, postaxial polydactyly, and cystic renal abnormalities, none of which were identified clinically in this patient. Smith‐Lemli‐Opitz syndrome may present with multiple congenital anomalies and may phenotypically overlap with trisomy 13. However, the combination of bilateral complete cleft lip and palate, bilateral postaxial polydactyly, aplasia cutis congenita, hypotonia, and congenital heart disease was highly suggestive of trisomy 13. Another important consideration is pseudotrisomy 13, particularly in patients with holoprosencephaly, facial anomalies, and polydactyly. Although bedside cranial ultrasonography was available, it was not performed during the infant's hospitalization; therefore, the presence or absence of holoprosencephaly could not be confirmed. Because cytogenetic and metabolic testing were not available, these differential diagnoses could not be definitively excluded. Nevertheless, the characteristic combination of congenital anomalies favored a presumptive clinical diagnosis of trisomy 13.

Investigations

Initial laboratory investigations from a heel‐prick blood sample revealed leukocytosis with neutrophilia (white blood cell count, 25.8 × 109/L; neutrophils, 84.9%), lymphopenia (6.7%), elevated hemoglobin (17.9 g/dL) and hematocrit (50.7%), and macrocytosis (mean corpuscular volume, 107.0 fL). The platelet count was within the normal range (186 × 109/L), while serum C‐reactive protein was markedly elevated at 87 mg/L. In the context of fever, respiratory distress, feeding difficulty, leukocytosis with neutrophilia, and markedly elevated C‐reactive protein, these findings raised clinical concern for early‐onset neonatal sepsis.

Chest radiography demonstrated cardiomegaly with an increased cardiothoracic ratio of approximately 65% (Figure 4). Transthoracic echocardiography showed normal cardiac situs and preserved left ventricular systolic function with an estimated ejection fraction of 70%. A large patent ductus arteriosus (approximately 2.5 mm) and a patent foramen ovale with left‐to‐right shunting were identified. Mild tricuspid regurgitation with dilatation of the right atrium and right ventricle was also present. The interventricular septum was intact, and no pericardial effusion, intracardiac thrombi, valvular vegetations, or outflow tract obstruction were identified. Overall, biventricular systolic function remained preserved despite right‐sided chamber enlargement.

Definitive cytogenetic confirmation could not be performed because conventional karyotyping, FISH, and chromosomal microarray analysis were unavailable at our institution. Nevertheless, the constellation of bilateral complete cleft lip and palate, aplasia cutis congenita, bilateral postaxial polydactyly, generalized hypotonia, absent sucking reflex, and congenital heart disease was highly suggestive of Patau syndrome, allowing a presumptive clinical diagnosis.

Treatment

Following admission, supplemental oxygen therapy was initiated, and peripheral intravenous access was established. Empirical intravenous cefotaxime and amikacin were administered because of clinical concern for early‐onset neonatal sepsis based on documented fever, respiratory distress, feeding difficulty, leukocytosis with neutrophilia, and markedly elevated C‐reactive protein. Intravenous 10% dextrose was provided to maintain adequate blood glucose levels, and vitamin K (1 mg intramuscularly) was administered according to standard neonatal care protocols.

During hospitalization, the infant developed an episode of apnea requiring immediate neonatal resuscitation. Airway repositioning, suctioning of oral secretions, positive‐pressure ventilation with a bag‐mask device, and supplemental oxygen were administered in accordance with neonatal resuscitation guidelines. Intravenous aminophylline was subsequently administered according to institutional protocol for severe neonatal apnea. Spontaneous respiration was restored, and the infant remained hemodynamically stable thereafter.

After initial stabilization, enteral feeding was withheld because of ongoing respiratory distress and significant aspiration risk associated with the unrepaired cleft palate. The infant received intravenous fluid therapy, ongoing cardiorespiratory surveillance, frequent airway suctioning, and general supportive care. Caffeine citrate was subsequently initiated to reduce the risk of recurrent apneic episodes. The parents were counseled regarding the presumptive diagnosis of trisomy 13, the clinical findings, anticipated clinical course, prognosis, and available management options.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

Despite intensive supportive management, the infant's clinical condition deteriorated with recurrent episodes of apnea and worsening cardiorespiratory instability. Despite supportive treatment and resuscitation attempts, he died on the fifth day in the hospital. The most likely immediate cause of death was progressive cardiorespiratory failure in the setting of clinically suspected trisomy 13 with severe multisystem congenital abnormalities and neonatal complications. Genetic confirmation and postmortem examination were not performed because these services were not available at our facility.

Discussion

Patau syndrome is a rare chromosomal disorder characterized by multiple congenital anomalies involving the central nervous, cardiovascular, craniofacial, and musculoskeletal systems. Although cytogenetic analysis remains the gold standard for diagnosis, recognition of the characteristic clinical phenotype remains essential, particularly in resource‐limited settings where genetic testing is unavailable or inaccessible [6, 7]. Here, the association of bilateral complete cleft lip and palate, aplasia cutis congenita, bilateral postaxial polydactyly, generalized hypotonia, feeding difficulty and congenital heart disease was highly suggestive of trisomy 13, allowing a presumptive clinical diagnosis despite the absence of cytogenetic confirmation.

The clinical manifestations observed in our patient closely resemble the well‐established phenotypic spectrum of trisomy 13 reported in previous studies. Orofacial clefts, postaxial polydactyly, congenital heart defects, scalp defects, and neurological abnormalities are among the most frequently reported manifestations [1, 6]. The coexistence of these characteristic abnormalities in our patient underscores the importance of meticulous physical examination in establishing a presumptive diagnosis when molecular diagnostic facilities are unavailable.

Important clinical differentials include Meckel‐Gruber syndrome, Smith‐Lemli‐Opitz syndrome, and pseudotrisomy 13. Meckel‐Gruber syndrome is characterized by the clinical triad of occipital encephalocele, postaxial polydactyly, and cystic renal abnormalities, none of which were identified clinically in our patient [8, 9]. Smith‐Lemli‐Opitz syndrome may present with multiple congenital anomalies and may phenotypically overlap with trisomy 13. However, the combination of bilateral complete cleft lip and palate, bilateral postaxial polydactyly, aplasia cutis congenita, hypotonia, and congenital heart disease in our patient was highly suggestive of trisomy 13 [10]. Another important consideration is pseudotrisomy 13, particularly in patients with holoprosencephaly, facial anomalies, and polydactyly [11]. Although bedside cranial ultrasonography was available, it was not performed during the infant's hospitalization; therefore, the presence or absence of holoprosencephaly could not be confirmed. Because cytogenetic and metabolic testing were not available in our setting, these differential diagnoses could not be definitively excluded. Nevertheless, the association of bilateral complete cleft lip and palate, bilateral postaxial polydactyly, aplasia cutis congenita, generalized hypotonia, and congenital heart disease favored a presumptive clinical diagnosis of trisomy 13 [12].

Congenital heart disease is a major contributor to morbidity and mortality in infants with trisomy 13. Cardiac abnormalities occur in approximately 60%–80% of affected infants and commonly include ventricular septal defects, atrial septal defects, patent ductus arteriosus, patent foramen ovale, and more complex structural malformations [13].

Echocardiography in our patient demonstrated a large patent ductus arteriosus, patent foramen ovale with left‐to‐right shunting, mild tricuspid regurgitation, and right‐sided chamber dilatation with preserved biventricular systolic function. Although these cardiac lesions were not immediately life‐threatening, they may have contributed to the infant's respiratory compromise and overall clinical instability. Importantly, current evidence suggests that selected individuals with trisomy 13 may benefit from cardiac intervention and other intensive treatments. Management decisions should therefore be individualized according to the severity of associated anomalies, neurological status, clinical condition, anticipated outcomes, and family preferences [13, 14].

Neurological dysfunction was another prominent feature in our patient, manifesting as generalized hypotonia, an absent sucking reflex, diminished responsiveness, and recurrent apnea. Neurological abnormalities are common in trisomy 13 and are frequently associated with structural brain malformations, particularly holoprosencephaly, although neuroimaging could not be performed in our patient because of limited diagnostic resources [6]. Central apnea, feeding difficulties, aspiration, and respiratory insufficiency are recognized contributors to neonatal morbidity and mortality [7, 14]. In our patient, recurrent apnea and progressive respiratory compromise required close cardiorespiratory monitoring and supportive interventions. Despite respiratory support, treatment of suspected neonatal sepsis, and recurrent apnea, the infant died on the fifth day of hospitalization from progressive cardiorespiratory failure. This outcome reflects the severe multisystem involvement present in this particular neonate but should not be interpreted as representative of the outcome of all individuals with trisomy 13.

The recurrent apneic episode requiring positive‐pressure ventilation in our patient further emphasizes the need for close cardiorespiratory monitoring and timely supportive interventions during the neonatal period. An additional challenge was the coexistence of suspected early‐onset neonatal sepsis, as evidenced by fever, respiratory distress, marked leukocytosis with neutrophilia, and markedly elevated C‐reactive protein levels. Although these findings were unlikely to be directly attributable to trisomy 13, neonates with severe congenital anomalies are particularly vulnerable to infectious complications because of physiological instability, prolonged hospitalization, and the need for invasive supportive care. Prompt initiation of empirical antimicrobial therapy and supportive management was therefore clinically justified. Careful respiratory assessment, appropriate feeding and aspiration precautions, nutritional support, and ongoing reassessment are important components of individualized neonatal care.

The absence of cytogenetic confirmation represented a major limitation in the diagnostic evaluation of this patient. Conventional karyotyping, fluorescence in situ hybridization, and chromosomal microarray analysis remain the recommended methods for confirming trisomy 13 and distinguishing complete trisomy from mosaic or Robertsonian translocation forms, which may differ in clinical presentation, prognosis, and recurrence risk [6, 13]. Unfortunately, these investigations were unavailable at our institution, reflecting the diagnostic constraints frequently encountered in low‐resource healthcare settings. Similar challenges have been reported in other developing countries, where careful recognition of characteristic phenotypic features continues to play a pivotal role in clinical diagnosis, parental counseling, and management decisions [7].

Importantly, trisomy 13 should not be regarded as uniformly lethal or described as “incompatible with life.” Clinical outcomes are variable, and individuals with trisomy 13, particularly those with mosaicism, may survive for many years and achieve a range of developmental skills. Recent evidence has demonstrated improved survival among selected patients receiving individualized neonatal, medical, surgical, and multidisciplinary care [1, 7, 13, 14]. Accordingly, management should focus on the individual patient's needs rather than on the diagnosis alone. Respiratory support, nutritional and feeding management, treatment of infections, cardiac assessment and intervention when appropriate, developmental surveillance, and involvement of relevant specialist teams should be considered according to the clinical circumstances.

Counseling should provide balanced and evidence‐based information regarding the potential benefits and burdens of treatment, associated anomalies, expected clinical course, and the possibility of prolonged survival. In the present case, management focused on stabilization, treatment of suspected neonatal sepsis, respiratory support, prevention of recurrent apnea, and comprehensive parental counseling. Enteral feeding was withheld because of persistent respiratory distress and the substantial risk of aspiration associated with the unrepaired bilateral cleft palate. Nevertheless, the management approach should be understood within the context of this infant's severe clinical presentation and limited diagnostic and therapeutic resources. A multidisciplinary, goal‐directed approach with ongoing reassessment and transparent communication with families is appropriate for infants with trisomy 13 and may help optimize outcomes and quality of life [7, 14].

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of recognizing the characteristic clinical features of Patau syndrome in resource‐limited settings where cytogenetic testing is unavailable or inaccessible. Although genetic confirmation could not be obtained, the constellation of congenital anomalies strongly supported a presumptive clinical diagnosis, enabling timely supportive management and informed parental counseling. This report underscores the continuing value of meticulous neonatal physical examination in the early recognition of trisomy 13 and emphasizes the need to improve access to prenatal screening, genetic diagnostic services, multidisciplinary care, and family support to optimize the management of affected infants in low‐resource healthcare settings.
