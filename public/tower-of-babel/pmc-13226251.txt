Ventricular preexcitation-induced subclinical LV dysfunction: Insights from pressure-strain loop imaging
Authors: Mingxia Li, Fen Chen, Jing Yao, Xinmin Chen, Xiaoxian Wang, Beibei Ge, Fang Xu, Pingyang Zhang, Shukui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVentricular preexcitation causes subclinical LV dysfunction despite preserved LVEF. Pressure-strain loop (PSL) analysis, a novel method, sensitively quantifies myocardial work to assess such functional alterations. This study evaluated global and segmental myocardial work alterations by PSL in these patients.MethodsSeventy-seven patients with ventricular preexcitation (stratified into right-AP [n = 33], left-AP [n = 16], and septal-AP [n = 28] groups) and 25 controls underwent speckle-tracking echocardiography. Global longitudinal strain (GLS) and mechanical dyssynchrony indices—the standard deviation of time-to-peak strain (PSD) and maximal temporal difference (ΔT)—were quantified. PSL analysis derived myocardial work parameters (work efficiency [WE], constructive work [CW], wasted work [WW]) at global, basal-apex, and segmental levels, with focused assessment of six basal LV segments.ResultsSeptal AP and right AP demonstrated significantly increased global and basal-middle ventricular WW along with reduced WE compared to controls (all P < 0.01). Additionally, the GLS in right AP was mildly less negative than in controls (P < 0.05). PSD and ΔT were significantly elevated in the basal-middle ventricular levels of septal and right AP compared to controls (P < 0.05). Furthermore, PSD exhibited a moderate negative correlation with GWE (r = −0.59, P < 0.001). Multivariate linear regression analysis identified PSD as an independent factor associated with increased GWW (β = 1.71, P = 0.03), decreased GWE (β = −0.136, P < 0.001), and reduced GCW (β = −7.93, P < 0.001).ConclusionPSL analysis effectively identifies subclinical left ventricular dysfunction in ventricular preexcitation patients with preserved LVEF, particularly pronounced in right-sided and septal accessory pathway subgroups. Ventricular mechanical dyssynchrony emerges as a key mechanistic contributor to these functional impairments, highlighting its pivotal role in early-stage electromechanical uncoupling.

Introduction

Cardiac pumping efficiency depends on synchronized electrical activation and mechanical contraction across left ventricular (LV) segments [1]. In ventricular preexcitation, accessory pathways (APs) bypass the atrioventricular node, inducing premature ventricular activation and LV mechanical dyssynchrony [2], [3]. Chronic dyssynchrony may lead to progressive myocardial remodeling and dysfunction termed preexcitation-induced cardiomyopathy [4], [5]. Crucially, subclinical myocardial impairment often precedes overt cardiomyopathy in patients with preserved LVEF.

Conventional echocardiography lacks sufficient sensitivity for early dysfunction detection. Although speckle-tracking echocardiography enhances the identification of subtle impairment via global longitudinal strain (GLS) analysis [6], [7], this approach remains fundamentally constrained by inherent afterload dependency [8] and the inability to quantify energy losses resulting from left ventricular mechanical dyssynchrony. Non-invasive pressure-strain loop (PSL) analysis addresses these shortcomings by integrating dynamic LV pressure-strain relationships to quantify myocardial work, a parameter validated against invasive pressure-volume loop measurements [9]. This method enables load-independent assessment of myocardial efficiency and captures regional mechanical inefficiencies [10].

This study enrolled ventricular preexcitation patients with preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). Pressure-strain loop (PSL) analysis was systematically applied to assess global and segmental myocardial work parameters, with specific focus on their correlation with left ventricular mechanical dyssynchrony.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective study included patients with ventricular pre-excitation referred for radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) at the electrophysiology departments of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University and Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital from April 2009 to December 2017. All enrolled patients had manifest preexcitation (resting ECG showing a delta wave, QRS duration ≥120 ms, and PR interval < 120 ms) and none had sustained tachyarrhythmias at rest. From an initial screening of 103 patients with Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) syndrome, 88 patients met preliminary inclusion criteria based on complete digital ECG and echocardiography data. Exclusion criteria included prior RFCA (n = 3), valvular heart disease (n = 2), cardiomyopathy (n = 1), and poorly controlled cardiovascular risk factors such as diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, and hypertension. After additionally excluding 5 patients with suboptimal echocardiographic image quality, the final cohort comprised 77 patients (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Flowchart of patient enrollment and data collection.Fig. 1

Flowchart of patient enrollment and data collection.

The AP location (left, right, or septal) was determined using intracardiac electrophysiological results. A cohort of 25 age- and gender-matched controls with confirmed cardiovascular health was enrolled during the same period. All controls had no history of heart disease or other medical conditions that could affect cardiac function, presented with normal resting electrocardiographic findings, and were not taking any cardiovascular medications.

The study was approved by the institutional review board and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design.

Electrocardiographic assessment

Standard 12‑lead ECGs were recorded during sinus rhythm using the ECG-Net V3.0 System (Nalong Health Technology Co., Ltd., China). Key ECG parameters, including PR interval and QRS duration, were measured using the ECG-Net V3.0 platform by experienced cardiologists following standardized protocols.

Echocardiographic analysis

Echocardiographic imaging was performed using a Vivid E9/E90 system (GE Medical Systems, USA) with a 2.0–5.0 MHz phased-array transducer (penetration depth: 7–12 cm; sector angle: 90–120°). All studies followed standardized protocols [11], including 2D imaging, Doppler modalities (pulsed/continuous-wave/color), and ECG-gated acquisitions. Images were acquired during sinus rhythm ≥3 days after paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (PSVT) episodes to exclude acute electrophysiological interference. Three consecutive end-expiratory cycles (frame rate ≥ 50 fps) were recorded and stored for offline analysis using EchoPAC v204 (GE Healthcare, USA).

Conventional echocardiographic study

Conventional parameters were quantified as follows: Parasternal long-axis view: Left atrial anteroposterior diameter (LAD), left ventricular end-diastolic/systolic diameters (LVIDd/LVIDs), posterior wall (LVPWd), and septal thickness (IVSd) in end-diastole. Apical views: Biplane Simpson's method was applied on apical four-chamber and two-chamber views to obtain LV volume in end-diastole (LVEDV), LV volume in end-systole (LVESV) and LV eject fraction (LVEF). Tissue Doppler measured early diastolic velocities at lateral/septal mitral annulus (e’), averaged as mean e’. Pulsed-wave Doppler assessed early (E) and atrial (A) filling velocities for E/A and E/e’ ratios.

Speckle tracking echocardiography

Longitudinal strain analysis was performed using vendor-specific software (EchoPAC v204, GE Healthcare) [12]. Three endocardial reference points were manually marked at end-diastole in apical long-axis views (four-chamber, three-chamber, and two-chamber), followed by automated endocardial and epicardial border tracing with myocardial motion tracking throughout the cardiac cycle. Segments with suboptimal tracking underwent manual border adjustment. GLS was calculated as the average peak systolic strain across all 18 left ventricular (LV) segments. Mechanical dyssynchrony parameters included:1) Peak strain dispersion (PSD): Standard deviation of time-to-peak strain (TTP) across all segments (global PSD) and within Basal/mid/apical levels (Bas-PSD, Mid-PSD, Api-PSD). 2) Maximal temporal difference (ΔT): Maximum TTP interval difference within each level (Bas-ΔT, Mid-ΔT, Api-ΔT).

Myocardial work analysis was conducted via PSL analysis according to validated methods [13]. Brachial artery systolic and diastolic pressures were measured using an automated cuff sphygmomanometer in the supine position during the echocardiographic examination. Three consecutive readings were taken 1 min apart and averaged for pressure-strain loop analysis. These measurements were then integrated with valvular event timings (aortic/mitral opening/closure) to construct PSL. Key global parameters included:•Global Constructive Work (GCW): Myocardial work generated during left ventricular (LV) systolic shortening or isovolumic relaxation lengthening.•Global Wasted Work (GWW): Myocardial work expended during LV systolic lengthening or isovolumic relaxation shortening.•Global Work Efficiency (GWE):GWE=GCW/GCW+GWW∗100%, reflecting the proportion of physiologically effective work.

For regional analysis, the LV was divided into 18 segments (basal/mid/apical levels, 6 segments per level) [9], [14]. Regional work indices (Bas/Mid/Api -CW, Bas/Mid/Api-WW, Bas/Mid/Api-WE) were averaged per level. The basal level was further subdivided into six segments (anteroseptal, anterior, lateral, posterior, inferior, inferoseptal) for segmental work quantification. Tracking quality was validated through frame-by-frame verification; studies with >2 non-trackable segments after optimization were excluded.

Reliability assessment

The reproducibility of GLS and myocardial work indices (GCW, GWW, GWE) was evaluated in 20 randomly selected subjects. Intraobserver variability was assessed by a single observer repeating measurements at 3-day intervals, while interobserver variability was determined through independent analyses by two investigators blinded to clinical data and prior results.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and categorical variables as frequencies (%). Group comparisons were assessed using one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc correction (parametric data) or Kruskal-Wallis test (non-parametric data). Pearson's correlation coefficient (r) evaluated linear associations between PSD and GCW, GWW, GWE. Multivariate linear regression identified independent predictors of myocardial work abnormalities, adjusting for key clinical/echocardiographic covariates. We first performed univariate analysis, and variables with P < 0.1 were entered into the multivariable model. Intra-observer and inter-observer variability for GLS and GCW, GWW, GWE was quantified via intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC, two-way mixed-effects model) and Bland-Altman analysis (95% limits of agreement). An ICC >0.8 was predefined as excellent agreement. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 29.0 and GraphPad Prism 10.2, with P < 0.05 denoting statistical significance.

Results

Study population demographics and clinical characteristics

The study included 77 ventricular preexcitation patients (28 septal AP, 33 right AP, 16 left AP) and 25 controls. No significant differences were observed in age, sex distribution, or blood pressure between groups (P > 0.05). Preexcitation patients exhibited shorter PR intervals and prolonged QRS durations compared to controls, except for non-significant QRS prolongation in the left AP group (P = 0.07). Segmental heterogeneity in basal myocardial work parameters

In the septal AP group, basal anteroseptal, septal, and inferior segments exhibited reduced CW, elevated WW, and lower WE compared to anterior and lateral segments (P < 0.01). The right AP group demonstrated higher CW in basal lateral and posterior walls versus anteroseptal/septal regions (P < 0.01), though no significant differences in WW or WE were observed between anterior and anteroseptal/septal segments (P > 0.05). Both the left AP group and controls displayed uniform myocardial work distribution across all basal segments, with no significant inter-segment differences in CW, WW, and WE (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Main Findings:1)Patients with septal or right-sided accessory pathways (APs) demonstrated elevated global myocardial wasted work (GWW) and reduced global work efficiency (GWE) despite preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), with these abnormalities most pronounced in basal and mid-ventricular regions.2)The severity of these functional impairments showed a direct correlation with left ventricular mechanical dyssynchrony (quantified by peak strain dispersion, PSD), suggesting electromechanical discordance as a central mechanism underlying myocardial inefficiency.

While prior studies documented impaired global longitudinal strain (GLS) in preexcitation syndromes [6], our application of pressure-strain loop (PSL) analysis extends beyond deformation assessment to energy utilization efficiency. These results are consistent with prior validations of myocardial work indices, which have demonstrated sensitivity to electromechanical inefficiencies in heterogeneous activation patterns [15], [16].

The anatomical location of AP dictates distinct patterns of myocardial work impairment. Regional inefficiency is observed with septal APs, global dysfunction with right-sided APs, and minimal impact with left-sided APs. In patients with ventricular preexcitation, abnormal activation sequences caused by accessory pathways generate pressure gradients between early-activated regions and delayed myocardium, leading to mechanical dyssynchrony and energy dissipation [17]. This correlation may stem directly from their varying anatomical proximity to and interaction with the primary conduction pathways, leading to specific electromechanical dyssynchrony. Below, we detail the mechanisms by which each AP location impacts myocardial work parameters:

Septal accessory pathways

Left ventricular mechanical dyssynchrony diverts energy into non-constructive work rather than effective ejection [15], a phenomenon particularly prominent in patients with ventricular preexcitation and septal APs. These patients exhibit significantly higher WW and reduce WE in basal and mid-ventricular regions compared to the apex, accompanied by less negative LS and greater mechanical dyssynchrony in these zones. Detailed segmental analysis of the basal left ventricle further reveals marked heterogeneity: septal, anteroseptal, and inferior segments demonstrate substantially higher WW and lower WE than anterior, posterior, and lateral segments. This regional impairment pattern directly correlates with the preferential localization of septal APs near the annuloventricular junction. Early electrical activation from these sites disrupts mechanical synchrony in adjacent myocardium, diverting energy into non-productive contractions (evidenced by reduced regional CW) while sparing the distal apical regions due to their remoteness from the AP origin [18].

Right-sided accessory pathways

Patients with right-sided APs display generalized impairment of myocardial work efficiency, characterized by elevated WW and reduced WE throughout basal, mid-ventricular, and apical segments. This global dysfunction stems from AP-mediated pre-excitation of the right ventricular free wall, which disrupts the physiological apex-to-base activation sequence [19]. Subsequent delayed left ventricular activation generates opposing contractile forces between the early-activated right ventricle and late-activated left ventricle, causing extensive mechanical dyssynchrony and inefficient energy dissipation. Anatomical proximity of right-sided APs to the sinoatrial node facilitates pronounced right ventricular pre-excitation, inducing premature elevation of right ventricular intracavitary pressure during early systole. This hemodynamic alteration culminates in paradoxical septal motion—characterized by initial posterior displacement followed by midsystolic anterior rebound [20]. Collectively, these mechanisms significantly increase GWW and impair GWE.

Left-sided accessory pathways

Left-sided APs, owing to their distance from the sinus node, result in limited pre-excitation and therefore minimal hemodynamic consequences [21]. Although trends toward less negative basal longitudinal strain (LS), higher WW, and lower WE were observed in basal segments, these changes lacked statistical significance, consistent with the confined zone of pre-excitation. This aligns with the rapid normalization of QRS morphology following left-sided AP ablation, indicating negligible baseline electromechanical disruption. Notably, the mean heart rate of the left AP subgroup was lower than that of the control group, though this difference did not attain statistical significance. Considering that a higher heart rate has been linked to reduced myocardial work efficiency in healthy individuals [22], the slightly lower heart rate in the left AP subgroup might have potentially alleviated the influence of pre-excitation on myocardial work parameters, which contributed to the absence of significant differences in global myocardial work indices when compared to the control group. Additionally, the anatomical location of left-sided APs, often along the mitral annulus, may result in a more dispersed and less synchronous activation pattern within the left ventricle, but the limited extent of pre-excitation could restrict the magnitude of mechanical disturbance. This is further supported by the absence of significant differences in GLS between the left AP subgroup and controls, as GLS is a sensitive marker of global left ventricular systolic function.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size, particularly in the left-sided AP subgroup (n = 16), may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Second, the cross-sectional design prevents the establishment of causal relationships between myocardial work parameters and clinical outcomes, including the potential influence of the frequency or duration of paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia episodes and other arrhythmias on myocardial work parameters. Third, the PSL technique is inherently limited by speckle tracking dependencies (image quality, frame rate, intervendor variability) [23], [24], [25] and by the use of brachial artery pressure as a surrogate for left ventricular pressure, which may be less accurate in patients with right or septal APs due to paradoxical septal motion. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether impaired myocardial work parameters predict adverse clinical events or normalize following catheter ablation.

Conclusion

Our study suggests that non-invasive pressure-strain loop analysis may aid in identifying subclinical left ventricular dysfunction in patients with ventricular preexcitation, especially in those with septal or right-sided accessory pathways. Despite preserved ejection fraction, these patients exhibited mechanical dyssynchrony with elevated global wasted work and reduced work efficiency. These findings may support integrating myocardial work parameters into clinical evaluation to refine management strategies. Further validation in larger cohorts and post-ablation functional assessments are needed to clarify their prognostic relevance.
