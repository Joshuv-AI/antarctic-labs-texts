Temporal distribution of recorded intensive care unit deaths across the 24-hour cycle: a 15-year retrospective hospital-based study
Authors: Meltem Ceylan Delice, Serpil Sevimli Deniz, Ibrahim Dogan, Baran Tureli, Halide Aydin Sakar, Nilgun Kavrut Ozturk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Mortality in the intensive care unit (ICU) remains one of the most important indicators of critical care performance and results from complex interactions among patient characteristics, disease severity, therapeutic interventions, and healthcare system organization. Consequently, previous ICU research has primarily focused on identifying patient-level predictors of mortality, including physiological severity scores, comorbidities, organ dysfunction, and treatment-related factors [1–3]. In contrast, relatively little attention has been directed toward the timing at which deaths occur or are recorded within the 24-hour cycle, despite growing interest in temporal variation in healthcare delivery and patient outcomes [4, 5].

Temporal variation in clinical outcomes has been investigated across several medical disciplines. Studies evaluating the so-called off-hours effect have examined differences between daytime and nighttime care or weekday and weekend admissions, suggesting that organizational factors such as staffing levels, workload, resource availability, and handover processes may influence healthcare outcomes [6–9]. However, evidence remains inconsistent, particularly in critical care, where continuous monitoring and around-the-clock physician coverage may attenuate differences observed in other hospital settings [10, 11]. Moreover, most previous studies have focused on mortality risk following admission, rather than on the temporal distribution of deaths themselves.

Another important consideration is that many investigations classify clock time into broad categories such as daytime versus nighttime or working hours versus off-hours [6, 8, 12]. Although operationally convenient, categorization of continuous time may obscure finer temporal heterogeneity and reduce statistical efficiency [13–16]. Continuous-time approaches allow temporal variation to be evaluated without imposing arbitrary cut-points and are particularly appropriate for circadian phenomena because clock time is inherently cyclic rather than linear [17, 18].

Several biological and healthcare-system mechanisms could plausibly contribute to temporal variation in the timing of recorded ICU deaths. Circadian rhythms influence cardiovascular physiology, endocrine regulation, autonomic nervous system activity, inflammatory responses, coagulation, and immune function, all of which may affect the progression of critical illness [19–22]. Simultaneously, healthcare delivery follows predictable operational cycles characterized by shift changes, multidisciplinary rounds, diagnostic workflows, and documentation practices [7, 9, 23]. These factors may influence not only clinical decision-making but also when deaths are recognized and documented within routine clinical practice. However, few studies have directly examined the temporal distribution of ICU deaths while acknowledging both biological and organizational influences.

Importantly, analyses based exclusively on death records evaluate the temporal distribution of recorded deaths rather than temporal variation in mortality risk. Without information on patient occupancy, admissions, exposure time, illness severity, or the population at risk during each time interval, observed clustering cannot be interpreted as evidence that the probability of death increases during specific periods of the day [24]. Instead, such findings describe when deaths occurred or were documented within the healthcare system and should therefore be interpreted at the system level.

Accordingly, this study aimed to characterize the temporal distribution of recorded ICU deaths over a 15-year period using complementary analytical approaches. Continuous-time modeling with cyclic cubic splines was adopted as the primary analytical strategy to evaluate temporal variation across the entire 24-hour cycle, whereas predefined interval-based analyses were performed to facilitate comparison with previous operational studies. In addition, temporal stability was examined across calendar periods and ICU types.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational study was conducted at Van Training and Research Hospital, a tertiary referral hospital providing multidisciplinary adult and pediatric intensive care services. The study included all recorded ICU deaths occurring between 1 January 2010 and 30 June 2025. Data were extracted directly from the hospital information system using predefined inclusion criteria. Following routine updates to the institutional database, all eligible records within the study period were retrieved and analysed.

Throughout the study period, the ICU system underwent substantial organizational expansion. Initially consisting of a single 8-bed general ICU, the institution progressively developed into a multi-unit critical care system comprising specialized anesthesiology, internal medicine, general, surgical, pediatric, coronary, cardiovascular, and neurological ICUs, with total bed capacity exceeding 100 beds in the later years of the study period. Calendar-period and ICU-type sensitivity analyses were therefore performed to evaluate the robustness of temporal patterns despite these structural changes.

Data structure

Data were obtained from the hospital information system and included the exact recorded time of death, date of death, age, sex, and ICU type for every eligible patient. No information was available regarding ICU occupancy, patient census, admissions, illness severity, severity scores, treatment characteristics, or the number of patients at risk during specific time periods. The recorded time of death represented the officially documented time registered in the hospital information system.

Consequently, the study evaluated the temporal distribution of recorded ICU deaths rather than temporal variation in mortality risk. The absence of denominator data precluded estimation of interval-specific mortality rates or risk.

Time definitions

Clock time was analysed using both continuous and predefined interval-based approaches. In the primary analysis, clock time was modelled as a continuous cyclic variable over the 24-hour period using cyclic cubic regression splines, thereby avoiding arbitrary temporal cut-points. Because clock time is inherently periodic rather than linear, this approach preserved continuity across midnight and enabled the characterization of smooth temporal patterns throughout the entire circadian cycle.

To facilitate comparison with previous operational studies, two predefined interval classifications were also examined as secondary analyses. The three-interval model divided the day into 08:00–16:00, 16:00–24:00, and 00:00–08:00, representing daytime, evening, and nighttime periods, respectively. In addition, a higher-resolution four-interval model further subdivided the nighttime period into 00:00–04:00 and 04:00–08:00, while retaining the daytime and evening intervals. This four-interval classification was prespecified before statistical analysis to explore potential intranight heterogeneity and to distinguish the early-morning transition period from the remainder of the night. Accordingly, continuous-time modeling constituted the primary analytical approach, whereas interval-based analyses were performed as secondary descriptive analyses to facilitate comparison with previous operational studies.

Statistical analysis

The primary analysis evaluated the temporal distribution of recorded ICU deaths using generalized additive models (GAMs) with cyclic cubic regression splines, thereby modelling clock time as a continuous periodic variable. Daily death counts were incorporated as an offset term in the Poisson generalized additive model, thereby estimating the relative temporal distribution of recorded deaths within each day while accounting for between-day variation in the total number of deaths. Cluster-robust standard errors were calculated using calendar day as the clustering variable.

Secondary analyses examined predefined three- and four-interval classifications using χ² goodness-of-fit tests. Expected frequencies were calculated according to interval duration under the assumption of a uniform temporal distribution. Standardized residuals (Z-scores) were computed to identify intervals contributing most strongly to deviations from expected values. Positive residuals indicated more recorded deaths than expected, whereas negative residuals indicated fewer recorded deaths than expected. Cramer’s V was calculated to quantify effect size.

Sensitivity analyses were performed according to calendar period (2010–2014, 2015–2019, and 2020–2025) and ICU type to assess whether temporal patterns remained consistent despite substantial organizational changes during the study period. All reported confidence intervals were calculated at the 95% level.

All statistical analyses were performed using Python in the Google Colaboratory environment. Data management was conducted using pandas and NumPy; statistical analyses were performed using SciPy and statsmodels; cyclic spline models were implemented using patsy; and figures were generated using Matplotlib. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Methodological considerations

Because information regarding patient occupancy, ICU admissions, illness severity, exposure time, and the underlying population at risk was unavailable, the study was not designed to estimate temporal variation in mortality risk. Instead, the analyses describe the temporal distribution of recorded ICU deaths within the healthcare system.

Furthermore, although age, sex, and ICU type were available, important patient-level clinical variables—including comorbidities, physiological severity, organ dysfunction, treatment limitations, and life-support interventions—were unavailable. Therefore, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and the observed temporal patterns should be interpreted as descriptive rather than causal.

In addition, the recorded time of death may have been influenced by institutional documentation practices, physician certification, or delays between clinical death and formal recording. Consequently, clustering of recorded deaths may partially reflect healthcare processes rather than the exact biological timing of death.

Results

Study population

Between 1 January 2010 and 30 June 2025, a total of 9,889 recorded ICU deaths were identified and included in the analysis. During the study period, the ICU system underwent substantial organizational expansion, evolving from a single 8-bed general ICU into a multidisciplinary critical care system comprising specialized anesthesiology, internal medicine, general, surgical, pediatric, coronary, cardiovascular, and neurological ICUs. Total ICU bed capacity increased from 8 beds in 2011 to a peak of 176 beds in 2022, before stabilizing at approximately 118 beds in 2025 (Supplementary Figure S1). Continuous-time analysis

The primary analysis evaluated the temporal distribution of recorded ICU deaths across the 24-hour cycle using a generalized additive model with cyclic cubic splines. A significant overall temporal effect was identified (Wald χ² = 101.88, df = 12, p < 0.001), indicating that the timing of recorded ICU deaths was not uniformly distributed across the 24-hour cycle (Fig. 1). The fitted spline demonstrated the highest relative frequency of recorded deaths during the late-morning period (approximately 11:15), whereas the lowest relative frequency occurred during the early overnight hours (approximately 03:36). The fitted curve remained continuous across midnight, reflecting the cyclic nature of clock time, and represented the estimated relative temporal distribution of recorded ICU deaths after accounting for day-to-day variation in the total number of recorded deaths. Although the overall temporal pattern was statistically significant, the magnitude of variation was modest, consistent with the small effect sizes observed in the interval-based analyses.

Three-interval analysis

For comparison with previous operational studies, recorded deaths were subsequently analysed using the predefined three-interval model. Standardized residuals were interpreted according to Agresti [25], with absolute values ≥ 2 considered to indicate intervals contributing substantially to the overall χ² statistic. The distribution differed significantly from a uniform temporal distribution (χ² = 60.28, df = 2, p < 0.001). As shown in Table 2 and Fig. 2, the 08:00–16:00 interval contained significantly more recorded deaths than expected (Z = + 6.09), whereas the 00:00–08:00 interval contained fewer recorded deaths than expected (Z = − 4.57). The 16:00–24:00 interval did not differ significantly from the expected distribution (Z = − 1.52). Despite statistical significance, the overall effect size remained small (Cramer’s V = 0.055).

Four-interval analysis

To further characterize temporal variation, the nighttime period was subdivided into midnight (00:00–04:00) and early morning (04:00–08:00) intervals.

The four-interval model also demonstrated significant deviation from a uniform temporal distribution (χ² = 65.25, df = 3, p < 0.001). As shown in Table 3 and Fig. 3, the 08:00–16:00 interval contained significantly more recorded deaths than expected (Z = + 6.09). In contrast, the 00:00–04:00 interval demonstrated substantially fewer recorded deaths than expected (Z = − 4.81), whereas the 04:00–08:00 interval showed only a modest negative deviation (Z = − 1.65). The 16:00–24:00 interval remained close to the expected distribution (Z = − 1.52). The overall effect size was again small (Cramer’s V = 0.047).

Sensitivity analyses

Calendar-period sensitivity analyses demonstrated that the overall temporal pattern remained statistically significant throughout the study period despite substantial expansion of ICU capacity. Similar late-morning peaks and early overnight nadirs were observed in the 2010–2014, 2015–2019, and 2020–2025 periods (all p < 0.01) (Fig. 4). Additional analyses stratified by ICU type also demonstrated temporal heterogeneity, although the magnitude of temporal variation differed between ICU specialties (Supplementary Table S1).

Data quality

Inspection of recorded death times demonstrated clustering at rounded clock times. Approximately 18.3% of deaths were recorded exactly on the hour, whereas 63.8% occurred at quarter-hour time points (:00, :15, :30, or :45), suggesting that documentation practices may have influenced the temporal distribution of recorded death times.

Discussion

In this retrospective observational study of 9,889 recorded ICU deaths, continuous-time modeling demonstrated that the temporal distribution of recorded deaths was not uniform across the 24-hour cycle. The fitted generalized additive model identified the highest relative frequency during the late-morning period, whereas the lowest relative frequency occurred during the early overnight hours. Although these temporal differences were statistically significant, their magnitude remained modest, indicating that the observed clustering should primarily be interpreted as a descriptive characteristic of the timing of recorded deaths rather than evidence of clinically meaningful differences in mortality risk.

The use of continuous-time cyclic modeling represents an important methodological strength of the present study. Most previous investigations examining temporal variation in healthcare outcomes have relied on broad dichotomous classifications, such as daytime versus nighttime or working hours versus off-hours [6–16]. Although operationally intuitive, these approaches assume homogeneity within predefined intervals and may obscure more complex temporal patterns. By modelling clock time as a continuous cyclic variable, our analysis avoided arbitrary cut-points and demonstrated that the temporal distribution of recorded deaths followed a gradual circadian pattern rather than abrupt transitions between predefined time periods. Consistent with this observation, interval-based analyses also demonstrated statistically significant temporal heterogeneity; however, the corresponding effect sizes were small (Cramer’s V = 0.055 and 0.047), indicating that the absolute magnitude of temporal variation remained limited despite the large sample size.

The mechanisms underlying these temporal patterns remain uncertain. Previous studies have suggested that ICU outcomes may reflect interactions between patient-related characteristics and organizational factors, including staffing models, workload distribution, handover processes, clinician fatigue, and operational efficiency [1–3, 7–16]. In addition, circadian physiology influences cardiovascular function, autonomic regulation, endocrine activity, and inflammatory responses, all of which may affect the progression of critical illness [5, 17–21]. However, none of these biological or organizational factors were directly measured in the present study. Consequently, any relationship between the observed temporal distribution of recorded deaths and healthcare delivery processes should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than causal.

An additional consideration is that the recorded time of death may not always correspond precisely to the biological time of death. Institutional documentation practices, physician assessment, certification procedures, and delays between clinical recognition of death and formal documentation may contribute to temporal clustering of recorded death times. The observed concentration of records at rounded clock times supports the possibility that documentation practices influenced the temporal distribution. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted as describing the timing of recorded deaths within the healthcare system rather than the precise biological timing of death itself.

The substantial organizational evolution of the ICU throughout the study period also warrants consideration. Between 2010 and 2025, the institution expanded from a single general ICU into a large multidisciplinary critical care system comprising multiple specialized units with markedly increased bed capacity. Such structural changes may have influenced patient case-mix, referral patterns, workflow organization, and documentation practices over time. Nevertheless, calendar-period sensitivity analyses demonstrated broadly consistent temporal patterns across successive study periods, suggesting that the principal findings were relatively robust despite major institutional expansion.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, denominator data—including ICU occupancy, admissions, patient census, and the population at risk during each time interval—were unavailable. Consequently, this study was designed to characterize the temporal distribution of recorded deaths rather than to estimate temporal variation in mortality risk. Second, although age, sex, and ICU type were available, important patient-level variables—including illness severity, comorbidity burden, organ dysfunction, treatment limitations, withdrawal or withholding of life-sustaining therapies, and resuscitation intensity—were unavailable and therefore could not be incorporated into the analyses. Residual confounding cannot be excluded. Third, direct measurements of staffing intensity, workload, handover quality, clinician experience, and operational performance were unavailable; accordingly, the proposed organizational mechanisms remain speculative. Fourth, documentation and certification practices may have influenced the recorded timing of death. Finally, this was a single-centre study, and the generalizability of these findings to healthcare systems with different organizational structures should be interpreted cautiously.

Despite these limitations, the study has several notable strengths. It includes one of the largest single-centre cohorts evaluating the temporal distribution of recorded ICU deaths over a 15-year period and, to our knowledge, is among the first ICU studies to combine continuous cyclic spline modeling with conventional interval-based analyses. This analytical framework provides a more comprehensive characterization of temporal patterns than predefined interval analyses alone and may serve as a methodological framework for future multicentre investigations integrating patient-level clinical, organizational, and operational data.

Conclusion

Recorded ICU deaths were not uniformly distributed across the 24-hour cycle. Continuous-time modeling identified modest but statistically significant temporal clustering, with the highest fitted frequency occurring at approximately 11:15 and the lowest fitted frequency at approximately 03:36. Because patient occupancy, denominator data, illness severity, and operational variables were unavailable, these findings should be interpreted as describing the temporal distribution of recorded deaths rather than temporal variation in mortality risk. Future multicentre studies integrating patient-level clinical characteristics, denominator data, staffing metrics, and operational healthcare variables are needed to determine whether the observed temporal clustering reflects biological, organizational, or documentation-related mechanisms.
