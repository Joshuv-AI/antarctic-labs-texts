Occipital lobe glial and glioneuronal tumors: A systematic review of clinical presentation, surgical management, and outcomes
Authors: Ali K. Al-Shalchy, Nooruldeen H. Al-Khafaji, Rania H. Al-Taie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Glial and glioneuronal tumors in the occipital lobe are uncommon lesions that have unique clinical and surgical considerations because of their connection to the visual cortex, optic radiations, and posterior associative networks. Information on these tumors is scattered across case reports, small series, and limited retrospective studies.Methods: A systematic review was conducted to identify studies reporting primary occipital lobe tumors such as glial, ependymal, or glioneuronal types. Eligible tumors included diffuse gliomas, glioblastoma, astrocytoma, oligodendroglioma, pilocytic astrocytoma, pleomorphic xanthoastrocytoma, astroblastoma, ependymoma, ganglioglioma, dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumor, papillary glioneuronal tumor, and subependymal giant cell astrocytoma (SEGA)/SEGA-like tumors. Data were summarized descriptively, using crude proportions. The denominators varied based on the outcome and included only patients with available data.Results: A total of 25 studies, including 334 patients, were analyzed. Men represented 169 out of 287 cases (58.9%). The most common symptom was visual disturbance, seen in 23 of 36 patients (63.9%), followed by seizures in 13 of 28 (46.4%), headaches in 7 of 20 (35.0%), and focal neurological deficits in 6 of 27 (22.2%). Tumors on the right side were slightly more prevalent than those on the left (154/288, 53.5% vs. 132/288, 45.8%). High-grade tumors, primarily glioblastoma/grade 4 diffuse glioma, made up 96.1% of cases (320/333), mainly driven by the World Health Organization grade 3–4 lesions. Surgery was performed on 301 patients (90.4%), with gross-total resection in 165 (49.5%), subtotal or partial resection in 125 (37.5%), and biopsy only in 4 (1.2%). Recurrence or progression was observed in 6 of 25 patients (24.0%), and among 81 patients with available survival data, 50 (61.7%) died.Conclusion: Occipital lobe glial and glioneuronal tumors are rare and diverse, with most literature focusing on high-grade astrocytic tumors. Visual symptoms are the primary clinical feature. Surgical treatment should be tailored to balance oncological control with the risk of visual impairment. Consistent reporting on occipital sublocation, molecular diagnosis, visual results, resection extent, recurrence, and survival is essential for better future evidence synthesis.

INTRODUCTION

Occipital lobe gliomas and glioneuronal tumors represent a rare and uniquely located subset of supratentorial brain tumors. Epidemiological data show that gliomas vary significantly by brain region, with the occipital lobe being one of the least common sites. A large, multi-dataset analysis of adult diffuse gliomas identified occipital tumors as the rarest lobar subgroup, and occipital glioblastomas displayed distinct survival outcomes and patterns of white-matter tract infiltration compared to tumors in other regions.[38] Nevertheless, the literature on occipital tumors is fragmented across small surgical series, studies on radiotherapy patterns, operative reports, and individual case studies.[9,30,31,35] This rarity is clinically significant because the occipital lobe houses the primary visual cortex, associative visual networks, and posterior white-matter pathways. Consequently, tumor growth or treatment can lead to visual deficits such as homonymous hemianopia, quadrantanopia, visual hallucinations, palinopsia, alexia, seizures, headaches, or other focal neurological symptoms.[31,35]

Managing these lesions involves balancing oncological control with the preservation of visual and functional capabilities. Maximal safe resection is often aimed for, especially in high-grade gliomas, but occipital surgery carries an inherent risk of permanent visual field deficits if tumors involve the calcarine cortex, optic radiations, or occipital pole.[9,31,35] Studies differ significantly in histology, grading, molecular reporting, operative approaches, and outcome measures, making direct comparisons difficult. This systematic review offers a descriptive overview of occipital lobe glial and glioneuronal tumors, highlighting demographics, clinical signs, anatomical locations, imaging features, pathology, treatment strategies, and outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Search strategy and study selection

This systematic review followed the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.[26] A thorough literature search was carried out in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, from the start of each database up to May 1st, 2026. The search combined terms related to occipital location and glial or glioneuronal tumor pathology, such as: “occipital lobe,” “occipital,” “glioma,” “glioblastoma,” “astrocytoma,” “oligodendroglioma,” “pilocytic astrocytoma,” “pleomorphic xanthoastrocytoma,” “astroblastoma,” “ependymoma,” “ganglioglioma,” “dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumor,” “DNET,” “papillary glioneuronal tumor,” and “subependymal giant cell astrocytoma.” Reference lists of eligible studies were manually reviewed to find additional relevant articles. No publication year restrictions were applied. Only articles published in English were included.

After removing duplicates, two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts. Full-text articles were then evaluated for eligibility, with disagreements resolved through discussion. Studies were included if they reported patients with primary occipital lobe glial, ependymal, or glioneuronal tumors and provided extractable data on clinical, radiological, pathological, treatment, or outcomes. Eligible tumor types encompassed diffuse gliomas, glioblastoma, astrocytoma, oligodendroglioma, pilocytic astrocytoma (PXA), pleomorphic xanthoastrocytoma, astroblastoma, ependymoma and its variants, ganglioglioma, dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumor (DNET), papillary glioneuronal tumor, and subependymal giant cell astrocytoma (SEGA) or SEGA-like tumors. Exclusion criteria included lesions outside the occipital region, non-glial/non-glioneuronal tumors, metastases to the occipital lobe from systemic primary tumors, review articles without original patient data, conference abstracts with insufficient data, and duplicate reports of the same patient cohort. When overlaps among cohorts were suspected, the most comprehensive or recent report was retained.

Data extraction

Two reviewers extracted data into a standardized spreadsheet, verifying against the full text. Study-level variables collected included first author, publication year, country, study design, sample size, tumor type, and the number of eligible occipital cases. Depending on reporting granularity, patient-level or aggregate variables such as age, sex, pediatric or adult status, presenting symptoms, visual symptoms, seizure, headache, focal neurological deficits, language or cognitive issues, tumor laterality, occipital sublocation, involvement of the calcarine cortex or visual pathways, extension to parieto-occipital or occipitotemporal regions, involvement of the periventricular or occipital horn, and imaging features such as contrast enhancement, edema, cystic components, hemorrhage, calcification, necrosis, and ependymal or subependymal spread were recorded.

Pathological variables encompassed histopathological diagnosis, tumor category, the World Health Organization (WHO) grade as documented by the authors, modern grade grouping when applicable, and molecular markers such as isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) status, O6-methylguanineDNA methyltransferase (MGMT) promoter methylation, 1p/19q codeletion, serine/threonine-protein kinase B-Raf (BRAF) alterations, Fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 (FGFR1) alterations, alpha-thalassemia/mental retardation, X-linked (ATRX) status, p53 expression, and the Ki-67/MIB-1 index if available. Treatment variables included surgery type, extent of resection, biopsy, gross-total, near-total, subtotal, or partial resection, as well as radiotherapy, chemotherapy, reoperation, awake mapping, direct electrical stimulation (DES), diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), neuronavigation, visual evoked potentials, and fluorescence-guided surgery. Outcome variables covered postoperative complications, new or worsened visual deficits, seizure outcomes, recurrence or progression, local or distant intracranial recurrence, ependymal/subependymal spread, extracranial metastasis, malignant transformation, mortality, survival status, and follow-up duration.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Given the expected diversity in study design, tumor histology, grading systems, treatment approaches, and reporting completeness, the analysis was designed as a descriptive synthesis rather than a formal meta-analysis. Continuous variables were summarized either by mean with standard deviation or median with range/interquartile range, depending on how consistently they were reported. Categorical variables were presented as raw counts and percentages.

For each variable, proportions were determined by dividing the number of patients with available data for that variable by the total number of patients for whom data were available, recognizing that denominators may differ across outcomes. The crude proportion was calculated as:

Crude proportion = event n/analyzable N × 100

where “event n” indicates the number of patients with a specific feature or outcome, and “analyzable N” refers to the number of patients for whom that variable was explicitly reported. Data that were missing, unclear, or not reported were not assumed to mean the event was absent. Results were thus presented as n/N available (%). When data were only available at the study level, aggregate values were extracted and described accordingly. Formal comparative analyses were not conducted due to significant clinical and methodological differences, as well as the prevalence of case reports and small case series.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was evaluated based on the study design. Case reports were checked with the Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal checklist for case reports[20] and case series were assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal checklist for case series.[24] The assessment concentrated on clear patient selection, diagnostic confirmation, thoroughness of clinical and treatment details, sufficient follow-up, and proper outcome measurement [Tables 1 and 2].[1-8,10,11,14-21,23,25-28,33,34] Overall, the included studies showed acceptable methodological quality. Most of the case reports clearly described presentation, diagnosis, treatment, and outcomes, although some older reports had limited adverse-event or follow-up details. The case series generally had clear inclusion criteria, reliable diagnostic methods, and adequate outcome reporting. Two reviewers independently performed the bias assessments, resolving disagreements through consensus. The results of the bias assessment were integrated qualitatively into the interpretation of the findings, not used to automatically exclude any studies.

RESULTS

Study characteristics

As shown in the PRISMA flow diagram, 2,572 records were identified, 30 full-text reports were assessed for eligibility, and 25 studies were ultimately included in the review, comprising 334 patients with occipital lobe glial or glioneuronal tumors [Figure 1]. Because the included literature consisted mainly of case reports, small case series, and a limited number of larger retrospective/database studies, all outcomes were summarized using crude descriptive proportions rather than formal pooled comparative estimates. Denominators varied across variables because reporting was inconsistent across studies; therefore, all proportions are presented as n/N available (%).

Demographic characteristics

Sex was reported for 287 patients. Among these, 169/287 (58.9%) were male, and 118/287 (41.1%) were female. Age-group data were available for 28 individually reported patients, of whom 6/28 (21.4%) were pediatric and 22/28 (78.6%) were adults [Supplementary Table S1 and Table 3].

Clinical presentation

Presenting symptoms were variably reported. Visual symptoms were the most frequent clinical manifestation, identified in 23/36 patients (63.9%) with available data. Seizures were reported in 13/28 patients (46.4%), headache in 7/20 patients (35.0%), and focal neurological deficits in 6/27 patients (22.2%). These findings reflect the expected functional anatomy of occipital lesions, with visual pathway involvement representing the dominant clinical theme.

Tumor laterality and anatomical distribution

Tumor laterality was available for 288 patients. Right-sided lesions were slightly more frequent, occurring in 154/288 patients (53.5%), followed by left-sided lesions in 132/288 (45.8%) and bilateral involvement in 2/288 (0.7%).

Among studies reporting more detailed anatomical sublocation, occipital pole involvement was reported in 10/14 patients (71.4%). Medial and lateral occipital localization were reported in 20/50 (40.0%) and 26/50 (52.0%), respectively. Calcarine or visual cortex involvement was documented in 2/4 patients (50.0%). Parieto-occipital and occipitotemporal extension were each reported in 1/5 patients (20.0%), while periventricular or occipital horn involvement was identified in 6/54 patients (11.1%).

Imaging features

Imaging characteristics were reported inconsistently, but among available cases, contrast enhancement was universal, present in 18/18 patients (100%). Peritumoral edema was reported in 6/8 patients (75.0%). Hemorrhage or calcification, when coded as a combined imaging feature, was present in 7/7 patients (100%) among studies that explicitly reported this variable. These imaging findings should be interpreted cautiously because many studies only reported positive imaging features and did not consistently state their absence.

Histopathology and tumor grade

The WHO grade was available for 333 patients. High-grade tumors predominated, with 320/333 patients (96.1%) classified as WHO grade 3–4 and 13/333 (3.9%) as WHO grade 1–2. WHO grade 4 tumors accounted for 315/333 patients (94.6%), whereas grade 3, grade 2, and grade 1 tumors accounted for 5/333 (1.5%), 7/333 (2.1%), and 6/333 (1.8%), respectively [Supplementary Table S2].

By histologic category, glioblastoma or grade 4 diffuse glioma represented the dominant entity, reported in 315/316 patients (99.7%) among studies with relevant classification data. Astrocytic tumors overall accounted for 323/334 patients (96.7%). Ependymal tumors were reported in 3/334 patients (0.9%), glioneuronal or neuronal tumors in 4/334 (1.2%), PXA in 4/334 (1.2%), astroblastoma in 1/334 (0.3%), DNET in 1/334 (0.3%), ganglioglioma/ganglioneuronal tumor in 1/334 (0.3%), and SEGA/SEGA-like tumor in 1/334 (0.3%).

Molecular characteristics

Molecular reporting was sparse. MGMT promoter methylation was reported in 19/47 patients (40.4%) with available data. No 1p/19q-codeleted tumors were identified among 301 patients with available data. FGFR1 alteration was reported in 1/4 patients (25.0%), whereas BRAF alteration was not identified among the 4 patients with available data. ATRX loss or alteration was recorded in 1/334 patients (0.3%).

Treatment patterns

Treatment data were available for most patients. Surgery was performed in 301/333 patients (90.4%). Gross-total resection (GTR) was achieved in 165/333 patients (49.5%), near-total resection in 7/333 (2.1%), subtotal or partial resection in 125/333 (37.5%), and biopsy only in 4/333 (1.2%). Radiotherapy was reported in 79/334 patients (23.7%), and chemotherapy or other systemic therapy in 66/334 patients (19.8%).

The use of operative adjuncts was less commonly reported. Awake mapping or DES was used in 7/82 patients (8.5%), DTI or neuronavigation in 3/82 (3.7%), and fluorescence-guided surgery in 3/82 (3.7%).

Outcomes

Postoperative complications were reported in 6/36 patients (16.7%) with available data. Recurrence or progression occurred in 6/25 patients (24.0%). Extracranial metastasis was rare, reported in 1/334 patients (0.3%). Survival status was available for 81 patients; mortality was reported in 50/81 patients (61.7%), while 31/81 patients (38.3%) were alive at last follow-up.

Overall, the crude descriptive synthesis suggests that occipital lobe glial and glioneuronal tumors are dominated in the available literature by high-grade astrocytic tumors, particularly glioblastoma, while low-grade glial, ependymal, and glioneuronal tumors are represented largely by isolated reports. Visual symptoms were the most frequent presenting feature, and surgery was the principal treatment modality, with approximately half of the analyzable patients undergoing GTR.

DISCUSSION

This systematic review offers a descriptive overview of the evidence regarding occipital lobe glial and glioneuronal tumors, which are a rare and uniquely located tumor subgroup. The majority of the evidence focused on high-grade astrocytic tumors, especially glioblastoma, while low-grade glial, ependymal, and glioneuronal tumors were mainly documented through small series and case reports. This pattern likely reflects both their actual epidemiologic rarity and publication bias, as unusual low-grade or rare histologies tend to be reported individually. Large studies based on tumor location support that the occipital lobe is among the least common sites for adult diffuse glioma and glioblastoma, with emerging evidence indicating that occipital glioblastoma might differ from other lobar glioblastomas in terms of structural infiltration and survival patterns.[30,38]

This review found that visual symptoms are the most common clinical sign, which is expected given the anatomy. The occipital lobe houses the primary visual cortex along the calcarine fissure, as well as surrounding extrastriate areas and posterior white-matter pathways involved in visual perception, reading, and object recognition. As a result, occipital tumors can cause not only homonymous hemianopia or quadrantanopia but also higher-level visual disturbances such as visual hallucinations, palinopsia, alexia, and issues with visual recognition. Shinoura et al. demonstrated that the connection between tumors, optic pathway anatomy, and the calcarine fissure correlates with postoperative visual field deficits, highlighting the importance of analyzing occipital tumors in relation to visual pathways rather than just lobar location.[31] Similarly, Horton and Hoyt emphasized the role of the extrastriate occipital cortex in producing well-defined quadrantic visual field defects, underlining the significance of subregional occipital anatomy.[16]

The distribution of tumors across different brain regions emphasizes the importance of a detailed sublobar classification. Tumors located in the occipital pole, medial occipital, lateral occipital, calcarine, parieto-occipital, occipitotemporal, and periventricular/occipital horn areas are not all functionally equivalent. For instance, medial occipital and calcarine tumors are more closely associated with the primary visual cortex and can cause homonymous field deficits. In contrast, lateral and occipitotemporal tumors might affect associative visual pathways and language-related visual processing, especially in the dominant hemisphere. In addition, occipital glioblastomas can infiltrate along white-matter tracts, progressing to connected regions instead of being confined to the initial cortical site, a pattern detailed in a dedicated study on occipital glioblastoma infiltration and recurrence.[30]

Surgical treatment of occipital lobe glioma mainly involves balancing oncological removal with preserving function, specifically preventing lasting visual impairment. To access occipital pole lesions, surgeons may use a tailored posterior occipital craniotomy or a keyhole occipital lobectomy. Conversely, medial occipital tumors typically require a parasagittal or interhemispheric approach, while lateral tumors can be reached through lateral occipital or parieto-occipital craniotomy. Conner et al. introduced a keyhole lobectomy method for occipital pole gliomas, achieving high resection rates without permanent postoperative issues in their series. Nonetheless, this technique is best suited for carefully selected occipital pole lesions, especially when visual deficits already exist.[9] Overall, evidence in glioma surgery indicates that maximal safe resection is critical for oncological outcomes but must be balanced with functional anatomy and individual patient risk factors.[13,29]

Deciding to perform aggressive resection in low-grade occipital tumors is complex. Viegas et al. described a series of patients with occipital WHO grade II gliomas where extensive removal was intentionally done despite risking permanent hemianopia, yet patients still retained good social and professional lives afterward.[35] This highlights a key issue in occipital glioma surgery: For certain patients, particularly those with progressive low-grade tumors or existing visual issues, accepting a visual field deficit might be worthwhile if it enhances cancer control. Conversely, for patients with intact vision, the decision to limit resection to preserve function is often easier. Preoperative discussions should therefore include not only survival prospects and recurrence risks but also considerations such as driving, reading, work needs, independence, and long-term adjustments to visual field loss.

Operative adjuncts can assist in refining this balance, though their reporting varies across the included studies. Neuronavigation and DTI tractography help estimate the distance between the tumor and optic radiations or long association tracts, but tractography remains reliant on models and does not assure functional preservation. Intraoperative identification of the optic radiations can be achieved through direct subcortical electrical stimulation, reducing the risk of permanent hemianopia.[12,13] Visual evoked potentials serve as supplementary monitoring tools, although they might miss some postoperative visual field deficits. Awake mapping combined with DES is well-established in glioma surgery for cortical and subcortical eloquent areas, though visual mapping lacks the standardization seen in language or motor mapping.[5,11] Fluorescence-guided techniques with 5-Aminolevulinic acid (ALA) or fluorescein can enhance tumor visualization in selected high-grade cases, but their efficacy in heterogeneous occipital glial and glioneuronal tumors remains preliminary and descriptive rather than conclusive.[32,37]

The imaging findings in this review varied and should be interpreted with caution. Reports often highlighted positive or unusual features, leading to variability in findings such as contrast enhancement, edema, hemorrhage, calcification, cystic changes, and periventricular involvement. Occipital tumors can resemble vascular malformations, metastases, abscesses, or other non-glial tumors, especially when presenting with hemorrhage, calcification, or ring enhancement. This is particularly important for low-grade and glioneuronal tumors, which may seem radiologically aggressive despite their slow-growing nature, and for high-grade tumors that may present suddenly with hemorrhage or venous complications. Therefore, imaging must be considered alongside clinical signs, patient age, tumor location, advanced imaging when available, and confirmed with histopathology.

Molecular reporting was inconsistent and limited across the included studies, mainly because many early reports predated modern integrated CNS tumor classification systems. This presents a significant limitation since current glioma classification relies heavily on molecular markers such as IDH mutation, 1p/19q codeletion, ATRX loss, telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) promoter mutation, BRAF alterations, FGFR1 alterations, and MGMT promoter methylation. The 2021 WHO classification formally combines histology with molecular diagnostics, and the EANO guidelines also stress the importance of integrated histomolecular diagnosis in adult diffuse gliomas.[22,36] Consequently, older diagnoses such as “oligodendroglioma,” “astrocytoma,” “anaplastic astrocytoma,” or “glioblastoma” are not always directly translatable into current entities without molecular data.

Outcome interpretation is also constrained by the predominance of high-grade tumors and inconsistent follow-up reporting. While mortality and recurrence/progression were common among patients with available data, these figures are heavily skewed by the high proportion of glioblastoma and other aggressive astrocytic tumors. Conversely, low-grade glial, ependymal, and glioneuronal tumors were primarily documented in isolated case reports, often with limited follow-up, complicating the assessment of their long-term recurrence risk. Visual outcomes were also underreported, despite their significance as a key functional endpoint in occipital tumor surgery. Future research should include detailed preoperative and postoperative visual field assessments, higher-order visual symptoms, quality-of-life metrics, ability to drive, return to work or school, and long-term functional adaptation.

This review has several limitations. First, the evidence included is diverse in clinical and methodological aspects, comprising registry and database cohorts, small surgical series, technical notes, and individual case reports. Second, the denominators varied widely since many studies did not report all variables, so crude proportions should be viewed as descriptive summaries of available data rather than population estimates. Third, older studies lack modern molecular classification, which limits diagnostic accuracy. Fourth, publication bias is probable, especially for rare tumors and unusual presentations. Despite these issues, the review compiles scattered evidence and emphasizes the unique clinical, radiological, surgical, and functional features that differentiate occipital lobe glial and glioneuronal tumors from gliomas in other lobes.

CONCLUSION

Occipital lobe glial and glioneuronal tumors are uncommon and diverse, with clinical features heavily influenced by involvement of the visual cortex and posterior white-matter pathways. In this overview, high-grade astrocytic tumors were most common, while low-grade, ependymal, and glioneuronal tumors were mostly documented through isolated reports. Visual symptoms were the primary presentation. Surgery remained the main treatment approach, but operative planning must carefully balance oncological benefits with the risk of permanent visual impairment. Future research should focus on reporting occipital subregions, molecular diagnoses, visual outcomes, extent of resection, recurrence patterns, and survival, all using standardized definitions.
