Prevalence of work-related health conditions among hotel housekeepers: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Isaac Sánchez-González, Juan Jesús García-Iglesias, Juan Gómez-Salgado, María Isabel Tovar-Gálvez, Carlos Ruiz-Frutos, Blanca Prieto-Callejero, Luis El Khoury-Moreno, Julio Torrejón-Martínez, Nadine Badillo-Sánchez, Israel Macías-Toronjo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHotel housekeepers are exposed to high physical workload, repetitive tasks, time pressure, and limited social recognition. These occupational conditions may increase the risk of work-related health problems, particularly musculoskeletal disorders, and negatively affect functional capacity and long-term work sustainability. The objective of this systematic review was to synthesise the available evidence on the most prevalent work-related health conditions among hotel housekeepers and to examine their association with work-related risk factors.MethodsA systematic review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Electronic searches were performed in PubMed, Scopus, CINAHL, Embase, and Web of Science in November 2025 and updated on 18 March 2026. Eligible observational studies reporting the prevalence of work-related health conditions among hotel housekeepers were included. Study selection, data extraction, and quality appraisal (Joanna Briggs Institute tools) were conducted independently by reviewers. Findings were synthesised narratively and, where appropriate, pooled using random-effects meta-analysis with inverse-variance weighting, Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation, and restricted maximum likelihood estimation of between-study variance (τ²). Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic.ResultsEight studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the systematic review. All examined musculoskeletal disorders, consistently reporting high prevalence rates. Five of these studies contributed to the meta-analysis; pooled 12-month prevalence was highest for low back pain (73.0%; 95% CI: 42.9–90.7), followed by wrist/hand (54.7%; 95% CI: 27.7–79.2), shoulder (48.2%; 95% CI: 10.8–87.7), and neck (40.1%; 95% CI: 3.2–93.2). Several studies identified significant associations between musculoskeletal symptoms and labour exposures, including biomechanical load, repetitive movements, work organisation, adverse psychosocial conditions, and specific factors such as the number of beds made per shift, lack of training, and low job satisfaction. Substantial heterogeneity limited comparability across studies.ConclusionsHotel housekeepers represent a particularly vulnerable occupational group with a high burden of work-related musculoskeletal disorders. The limited number of studies and their heterogeneity highlight the need for more robust, standardised epidemiological research. Preventive strategies addressing ergonomic design, workload management, and psychosocial risk factors are warranted to mitigate health risks and promote long-term occupational well-being in this workforce.RegistrationThe review protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261288316).Keywords: Prevalence, Systematic review, Risk factors, Occupational exposure, Working conditions, Ergonomics, Occupational health, Occupational diseases, Occupational injuries, Musculoskeletal pain, Musculoskeletal disorders, Women workers, Occupational risks

Background

Tourism constitutes a major driver of economic activity and employment worldwide. In 2022, the most recent year with internationally comparable post-pandemic data, the tourist accommodation subsector accounted for approximately 1–3% of GDP in major economies and directly employed millions of workers globally [1, 2]. Within this labour-intensive industry, cost-containment strategies and global competition have contributed to the consolidation of employment models characterised by subcontracting, temporary contracts, and workforce flexibilisation [3, 4].

Hotel housekeepers hold a structurally central role within hotel operations but receive limited recognition and support. Responsible for the provision of core room services, they represent a predominantly female workforce, with a high proportion of migrant and minority workers concentrated in low-quality jobs marked by low wages, limited decision latitude, precarious employment arrangements, and pronounced seasonality [3–10]. These structural determinants shape a context of cumulative vulnerability that amplifies exposure to occupational hazards and constrains access to preventive and compensatory mechanisms.

The housekeeping labour process entails sustained physical workload, repetitive biomechanical demands, low task autonomy, and intense time pressure. Routine activities, such as bed-making, bathroom cleaning, surface disinfection, load handling, and trolley pushing, require repetitive upper-limb movements, force exertion, trunk flexion and rotation, awkward postures, and manual handling of weights. Consequently, workers are simultaneously exposed to ergonomic, chemical, biological, and psychosocial risk factors [3, 7–9, 11]. Importantly, progressive upgrades in hotel service standards have not been matched by proportional adjustments in staffing levels or work organisation, resulting in work intensification and an increased cumulative risk profile [5, 7, 10].

Although numerous primary studies and some narrative reviews have documented elevated rates of musculoskeletal disorders, mental health problems, and other work-related conditions among hotel housekeepers, the existing literature remains fragmented across disciplines, geographic contexts, and methodological approaches. Prior reviews have typically focused on specific outcomes (e.g., musculoskeletal disorders) or isolated risk factors, without offering an integrated synthesis of the full spectrum of work-related health conditions within a unified analytical framework. Moreover, research has largely overlooked how physical and psychosocial exposures interact, how structural employment conditions influence health, and how underreporting and limited recognition of health issues may affect our understanding of workers’ well-being [3, 4, 7, 8, 12–15].

Validated ergonomic assessment tools have been designed to characterise physical workload and posture, providing a methodological framework for evaluating risk. These include the Workplace Ergonomic Risk Assessment (WERA) [16], which evaluates physical demands across body regions and tasks, the Rapid Entire Body Assessment (REBA) [17] for scoring posture, force, and movement, and direct observation methods, including video-supported task analysis. The Washington State Department of Labor and Industries (WISHA) Caution and Hazards checklist [18] is another standardised observational framework for identifying ergonomic hazards in occupational settings. However, barriers to accessing work-related benefits, incomplete documentation of injuries, and discrepancies between workers’ and healthcare professionals’ perceptions make it difficult to accurately estimate the magnitude of the problem [3, 5, 8, 13, 19–21].

In this context, the present review advances the field by: (i) systematically identifying and synthesising the range of work-related health conditions affecting hotel housekeepers; (ii) integrating evidence on physical, psychosocial, and organisational risk factors within a comprehensive occupational health perspective; and (iii) critically examining structural and reporting-related factors that may obscure the true burden of disease. By moving beyond outcome-specific or risk-specific analyses, this review provides a consolidated and analytically coherent overview of occupational health among hotel housekeepers, thereby addressing a substantive gap in the current literature.

The objective of this study, therefore, is to identify and synthesise the most prevalent work-related health conditions among hotel housekeepers in the hotel sector, while contextualising these conditions within their broader organisational and structural determinants.

Methods

Search procedure

A systematic review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines [22]. The review protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261288316).

The search strategy was developed using the Condition–Context–Population (CoCoPop) framework [20]. The condition was defined as work-related somatic health conditions; the context as the hotel sector; and the population as hotel housekeepers. This framework informed the research question: “What are the most prevalent work-related health conditions among hotel housekeepers?”

Electronic searches were performed in PubMed, Scopus, CINAHL, Embase, and Web of Science on 17 November 2025 and updated on 18 March 2026. Controlled vocabulary terms (e.g., MeSH) were combined with free-text keywords using Boolean operators (AND, OR). The full search strategy is presented in Table 1. No restrictions were applied regarding year of publication. Where applicable, filters were limited to human studies. Additionally, reference lists of included articles were manually screened to identify further relevant studies.

Selection criteria

Original quantitative studies were eligible if they: Included hotel housekeepers or equivalent cleaning staff working in hotel establishments; Assessed exposure to occupational hazards; and reported prevalence data on work-related somatic health outcomes.

Eligible designs included cross-sectional and observational epidemiological studies reporting primary data.

Studies were excluded if they were systematic reviews, meta-analyses, qualitative studies, case reports, brief communications, editorials, or letters. Studies were also excluded if: (i) data for hotel housekeepers were not disaggregated; (ii) occupational exposure was not assessed; (iii) the outcome was unrelated to work; or (iv) the primary focus was mental health outcomes, due to conceptual and methodological heterogeneity relative to somatic conditions.

Methodological assessment

Methodological quality was independently assessed by two reviewers using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for cross-sectional studies (8 items) [23]. Each domain was rated as “yes,” “no,” “unclear,” or “not applicable.” Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Studies achieving at least five positive responses were considered to have acceptable methodological quality for inclusion in quantitative synthesis. To account for study quality in the evidence synthesis, sensitivity analyses were performed restricting meta-analyses to studies at low risk of bias, as defined by the JBI checklist, and studies with extreme prevalence estimates or small sample sizes were also examined separately. This allowed assessment of the robustness of pooled prevalence estimates to methodological quality.

Furthermore, study quality was explicitly considered in the sensitivity analyses, as well as in the interpretation and synthesis of results: pooled estimates were contextualised with reference to the methodological strengths and limitations of the included studies, acknowledging that biases related to non-representative samples, self-reported outcomes, and the exclusion of workers with pre-existing conditions could have influenced the prevalence estimates.

Data extraction

After removal of duplicates, two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts against eligibility criteria. Full texts of potentially relevant articles were then assessed independently by the same reviewers. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or consultation with a third reviewer.

Data were extracted using a standardised form including: Study characteristics (author, year, country); Study design and sample size; Participant characteristics; Exposure assessment methods; Health outcomes and case definitions; Prevalence estimates and corresponding confidence intervals; Statistical associations reported.

When absolute numbers of cases were not reported, they were calculated from prevalence and total sample size when possible. In cases where multiple publications referred to the same study population, complementary data were consolidated to avoid duplication.

Meta-analysis and data synthesis

Between-study heterogeneity was anticipated due to variability in: work organisation (e.g., room quotas, shift patterns, subcontracting); hotel characteristics (e.g., category, country, labour regulations); workforce composition (e.g., age distribution, migration status, employment stability); measurement instruments and case definitions; recall periods used to assess symptoms; sample size; and study design.

To account for this variability, prevalence meta-analyses were conducted within a frequentist random-effects framework, which allows for the incorporation of both within- and between-study variability. The random-effects model was selected a priori because substantial heterogeneity was expected based on differences in populations, exposures, and study methods, and because it provides more conservative pooled estimates than fixed-effect models under conditions of variability.

Pooled prevalence estimates with 95% confidence intervals were calculated using inverse-variance weighting after variance stabilisation of proportions via the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation. Confidence intervals were computed on the transformed scale using the normal approximation and subsequently back-transformed to the original proportion scale. Between-study variance (τ²) was estimated under the random-effects assumption using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimator, and statistical heterogeneity was quantified with the I² statistic, with values > 75% interpreted as indicating substantial inconsistency.

Prediction intervals were considered but not formally calculated due to the small number of studies included per outcome. Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of pooled estimates, including leave-one-out influence analyses (sequentially excluding each study to evaluate its impact on pooled prevalence and heterogeneity) and restrictions to studies at low risk of bias or those excluding extreme prevalence estimates or small sample sizes. Subgroup analyses were explored according to methodological and contextual characteristics; however, formal subgroup analyses or meta-regression were not feasible due to the limited number of studies and lack of comparable data.

Quantitative synthesis was restricted to studies employing sufficiently homogeneous case definitions and measurement instruments to enhance comparability. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the meta package. Results were presented in summary tables and forest plots, including pooled estimates, confidence intervals, heterogeneity statistics (I², τ²), and sensitivity analyses.

Results

Selection process

The search strategy yielded a total of 1,971 records. After removal of duplicates and initial screening of titles and abstracts based on the review objectives and predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 22 articles were selected for full-text assessment. Of these, 10 studies met the eligibility criteria [3, 4, 6, 9, 19, 24–28]. Following assessment according to the JBI methodological quality criteria, six studies were ultimately included in the systematic review [3, 6, 25–28].

Manual screening of the reference lists of the full-text articles identified eight additional potentially eligible studies, of which five met the inclusion criteria after full-text review [15, 29–32]. After methodological quality assessment, two studies were finally included in the review [30, 32]. Subsequent analysis revealed that one of the studies identified through the initial electronic search [3] was a secondary report of a larger primary study identified through reference searching [32]. As the secondary report provided additional statistical analyses of the primary data, both were included in the systematic review; however, neither was included in the meta-analysis, as they did not employ measurement methods comparable to those used in the other studies.

The population and data of one of the selected studies [28] were also found to originate from the same primary study previously identified [32]. As this study provided additional relevant information on psychosocial factors associated with shoulder pain, it was retained in the systematic review. However, it was excluded from the meta-analysis because it did not use comparable measurement methods.

Two other studies [26, 27] were based on the same population but reported prevalence estimates for different health conditions, with no overlap in the outcomes analysed. Therefore, both studies were considered complementary, included in the systematic review, and their data were combined for the purposes of the meta-analysis.

Characteristics of the selected studies

Of the eight included studies, three were conducted in the United States [3, 28, 32], sharing the same population and dataset; two were conducted in Ethiopia [26, 27], also based on a shared population; two were conducted in Malaysia [6, 30]; and one was conducted in Spain [25]. All studies assessed the presence of musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) among hotel housekeepers.

All included studies were observational and employed a cross-sectional design, with both descriptive and analytical components. In several studies, workplace selection was based on non-probability sampling methods, such as the inclusion of unionised hotels in a single urban setting [3, 28, 32] or the voluntary participation of hotels [6, 30]. Regarding participant selection, most studies included only active hotel housekeepers, with explicit or implicit exclusion of workers on sick leave or with a history of MSDs [6, 26, 27, 30]. Sample sizes varied considerably, with a minimum of 40 participants in smaller studies [6] and a maximum of 1,043 participants in larger studies [25].

Outcome assessment was primarily based on self-reported questionnaires referring to different recall periods, ranging from the previous 7 days to the past 12 months. To estimate the prevalence of MSDs, five studies used the Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire (NMQ) with a 12-month recall period [6, 25–27, 30]. Of these, two also assessed 7-day prevalence [25, 30], and one additionally reported the prevalence of chronic pain [25]. Three studies used ad hoc questionnaires derived from the SF-36, with a recall period of four weeks [3, 28, 32].

In addition to health outcomes, several studies assessed working conditions and occupational risks using different methodological approaches, with a particular focus on ergonomic and psychosocial factors. One study [25] analysed occupational risk variables using official assessment tools, specifically the Guideline for the Prevention of Ergonomic and Psychosocial Risk of the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (Spain), which integrates both types of risk. Three studies [3, 28, 32] characterised physical workload using specific indices and assessed psychosocial work factors using the Job Content Questionnaire and Siegrist’s Effort–Reward Imbalance model. One study [6] focused on ergonomic risks in the workplace, using the WERA, REBA, and direct task observation supported by video recordings. Finally, one study [30] conducted an ergonomic risk assessment through direct observation using the WISHA checklist.

Most studies performed multivariable analyses to control for potential confounding factors, with the exception of the Malaysian studies [6, 30], which were purely descriptive in nature.

Main findings

Beyond musculoskeletal disorders, no studies meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria were identified that addressed other types of work-related injuries or health conditions, particularly those associated with exposure to chemical or biological hazards.

With regard to chronic pain, one study [25] reported a prevalence of 51% in at least one body region, low back pain being the most prevalent condition, accounting for 28.7% of cases (95% CI: 25.9–31.5), followed by the wrists/hands (23.7%; 95% CI: 21.1–26.4).

For 12-month prevalence, the low back was consistently the most affected anatomical region across the five studies that used the Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire (NMQ) [6, 25–27, 30]. The second most frequently affected regions varied between studies, with the shoulder and neck being the most commonly reported. In one study [6], 100% of hotel housekeepers reported pain in at least one anatomical region. In another study [3], at least 78% of hotel housekeepers reported having experienced an MSD in some part of the body; however, this estimate was not restricted to chronic pain or a specific anatomical region, and the NMQ was not used.

Studies assessing 4-week prevalence showed a similar pattern, with low back pain being the most prevalent condition. In one study [32], 62% of female hotel housekeepers reported severe or very severe low back pain, while the prevalence of lower limb pain was slightly higher than that of upper limb pain. Likewise, analysis of the disaggregated data reported by Krause [3] indicated that up to 95% of female workers had experienced low back pain of any intensity in the weeks preceding the questionnaire. In the two studies that assessed 7-day prevalence [25, 30], low back pain also remained the most prevalent condition. Frequent risk factors

Both high workload and the presence of ergonomic problems (including excessive reaching or stretching movements) were identified as risk factors for the development of pain in the neck [3, 26], upper back [3], and low back regions [3, 27], as well as in the shoulder/upper limbs [26, 28] and for general body pain [3]. Increased workload was also associated with the onset of neck, lower back [3], and shoulder pain [28], as well as with general body pain [3]. Repetitive movements were associated with the development of low back [27], neck, and upper limb pain [26]. A higher number of beds made per shift was positively associated with the development of low back pain, whereas adequate occupational health follow-up and safety training showed a negative association [27].

Taking breaks was negatively associated with the development of neck pain, while low job satisfaction showed a positive association. Low job satisfaction and older age were both positively associated with upper limb pain, whereas greater perceived organisational concern for workers’ health and safety was associated with lower risk [26].

Regarding chronic pain, the number of beds made and age were positively associated, whereas taking breaks, applying occupational risk prevention protocols, and task rotation were negatively associated [25].

Quantitative synthesis and meta-analysis

Given the limited number of available studies and the need to ensure comparability of outcome measures through the use of a standardised and validated instrument, only the five studies that used the Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire (NMQ) with a 12-month recall period [6, 25–27, 30] were included in the meta-analysis. All but two studies [26, 27] provided data for all back, upper limb, and lower limb regions. One study [26] reported data for the neck and upper limbs only, whereas another [27] reported data exclusively for the low back.

Pooled prevalence estimates with corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated for each anatomical region. Given the small number of studies available per region and the high heterogeneity observed (I² > 80% in all regions), the results of the meta-analysis are presented in summary form in Table 11 rather than using forest plots.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesises the available evidence on the prevalence of work-related health conditions among hotel housekeepers. To our knowledge, this is the first review to provide a comprehensive synthesis of work-related health conditions in this specific workforce. The only previous review identified [37] focused exclusively on musculoskeletal disorders and combined hotel housekeepers with other cleaning occupations.

Although hotel housekeepers share physically demanding tasks and feminised and precarious working conditions with other cleaning workers, differences in specific job tasks and work environments may lead to distinct exposure profiles and, consequently, to different patterns of work-related outcomes [38–40].

Prevalence of musculoskeletal disorders

The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis suggest that hotel housekeepers experience a high burden of work-related musculoskeletal disorders, with multiple anatomical regions simultaneously affected. This pattern is consistent with the high physical demands of housekeeping work and points to cumulative exposure that likely promotes muscle overuse [41]. Nevertheless, the wide confidence intervals observed in several pooled estimates reflect considerable uncertainty and variability across studies. These results should therefore be interpreted with caution, as they may be influenced by heterogeneity in study populations and methodologies.

The higher prevalence observed in the axial regions, particularly the low back and neck regions, is in line with the characteristics of the tasks performed by hotel housekeepers and the physical load they entail [6]. Studies based on direct biomechanical measurements have shown that low back risk among hotel housekeepers exceeds that observed in manufacturing occupations and in patient handling, with bed making and bathroom cleaning identified as particularly high-risk activities [13, 42]. Repeated trunk flexion and overexertion during bed making may exceed recommended limits for safe lifting [6, 43]. One study reported a positive association between the number of beds made per day and the development of low back pain, suggesting that increased workload translates into longer exposure to physical strain [27]. Other studies, however, found no association with the number of beds but rather with physical workload indices and work intensification [3, 4, 7]. Cleaning tasks and manual load handling are also characterised by unfavourable body mechanics, which may be further exacerbated by restrictive work uniforms that limit mobility and promote compensatory strategies based on trunk flexion and rotation [11, 44].

The high prevalence of upper limb pain points to a work-related origin associated with repetitive upper limb movements, tasks performed above shoulder level, sustained gripping, and repeated force application during activities such as bed making and bathroom cleaning [6, 42]. Inadequate cleaning products and basic equipment may increase movement intensity and speed [3], and high workloads have been shown to correlate with upper limb injuries and pain [7]. In addition, psychosocial factors such as low reward, poor recognition, and limited career opportunities showed an independent association with upper limb pain, even after adjustment for physical workload and other confounding variables [28]. These findings are further supported by evidence from occupational accident data in the hotel sector. Injuries among hotel housekeepers are predominantly musculoskeletal, mainly affecting the back and upper limbs, particularly the shoulder [13, 14].

In the lower limbs, the prevalence of pain highlights the relevance of sustained bending postures, frequent kneeling, and pushing beds with the knees, as well as organisational and contextual factors such as prolonged walking, floor surfaces, work footwear, and overall ergonomic conditions [6, 9, 30, 38, 45].

With regard to age, an association with a higher prevalence of MSDs might be expected. However, only one study [25] reported a positive association with chronic pain, together with other factors such as a higher number of beds per day, more years of employment as a hotel housekeeper, contract type, and longer weekly working hours, and another study found an association with upper limb pain over a 12-month period [26]. Some studies suggest that the relationship between poor working conditions and adverse health outcomes is largely independent of age, with age likely acting as a proxy for cumulative lifetime occupational exposure [3].

Regarding the number of beds per day as a potential determinant of MSDs, methodological differences prevented direct comparison across the studies that included this variable [3, 25, 28, 32].

Regarding contract type, the population with the highest prevalence of MSDs [25] had a lower proportion of permanent contracts compared to the other studies that allowed for such a comparison [25–27]. Several studies indicate that job insecurity influences task allocation and contributes to work intensification, driven by fear of dismissal or non-renewal of contracts [5, 8, 14, 27, 46].

Additional variables associated with the development of MSDs are summarised in Table 10, together with the anatomical regions affected. Particularly relevant were ergonomic risk factors [3, 28], repetitive movements [26, 27], excessive reaching and stretching [26, 27], increased workload [3, 28], and work intensification [3, 28]. Psychosocial factors, such as low job satisfaction [26] and having a temporary employment contract [27], were also associated with the occurrence of MSDs. A high effort–reward imbalance at work has been significantly associated with somatic symptoms and functional limitations, even after adjustment for physical workload, ergonomic factors, and sociodemographic variables [12].

Conversely, preventive measures such as taking frequent breaks [25, 26], task sharing among colleagues [25], and appropriate occupational health follow-up and safety training [27] showed protective effects in the studies analysed. The impact of these factors, together with other interventions that have proven beneficial, such as adapting tools and trolleys, changing bed linen types, introducing bed-lifting mechanisms, and increasing organisational commitment to workplace safety, reinforces the need for workplace-specific risk assessments, context-sensitive prevention strategies, and the systematic implementation and enforcement of preventive protocols [25, 26, 44, 47–50].

The role of sex is difficult to interpret due to the predominance of female participants in the included studies. One study found no association between sex and the presence of MSDs [26]. Other research suggests that the higher risk observed in feminised sectors is primarily driven by work-related risk factors and exposure rather than by sex itself [51, 52]. Exposure limits and the design of workstations and equipment are often based on the average male worker, which may increase women’s vulnerability, particularly when men and women in the same occupation do not perform identical tasks or experience the same exposures [8, 53]. Non-work-related factors, such as double workload and work–life imbalance, may also contribute to the overall burden of disease [52, 54]. Although two of the studies included did not find a significant association between household responsibilities and MSDs [3, 28], this issue warrants further investigation.

The potential effect of belonging to immigrant or minority populations was not examined in any of the studies included in this review. Although some studies have reported differences in pain prevalence among minority groups [20], several authors point to social determinants as key drivers of differential exposure to occupational risks and working conditions, which may account for these disparities [5, 9, 19, 46, 55].

The meta-analysis revealed consistently high heterogeneity across all anatomical regions (I² > 80–90%), which, together with the small number of available studies, limits the precision and interpretability of pooled prevalence estimates. Although this heterogeneity is unlikely to be explained by methodological differences, given the consistent use of the NMQ and 12-month recall periods, or by the influence of individual studies, it instead suggests substantial intrinsic variability between populations. This variability is likely driven by differences in work intensity, work organisation, cumulative physical workload, and socioeconomic contexts.

The high level of heterogeneity observed reduces the comparability and precision of pooled estimates, although it does not invalidate the overall pattern of findings. These results support the identification of hotel housekeepers as a potentially vulnerable occupational group with respect to work-related MSDs and highlight the need for preventive interventions tailored to specific workplace contexts. In this regard, the most relevant comparison is with the meta-analysis by Sánchez-Rodríguez [37], which included hotel housekeepers and other cleaning workers. That study also reported extreme heterogeneity (I² = 95.8%) and a lower pooled prevalence (53.9%; 95% CI: 43.4–64.6) than observed in the present meta-analysis (73.0%; 95% CI: 42.9–90.7).

The findings of this review should be interpreted cautiously due to the high heterogeneity and differences in population composition. The focus solely on hotel housekeepers may have contributed to the higher prevalence estimates observed, emphasizing the need for occupation-specific comparative studies. Planned additional analyses, including subgroup analyses or meta-regression, could not be performed because of the limited number of studies and the lack of comparable data across them.

Methodological biases inherent to cross-sectional research in the included studies substantially affect the interpretation of the results. The frequent use of non-representative samples, resulting from convenience sampling or the selection of hotels with specific characteristics, such as unionised hotels [3, 28, 32] or voluntary participation [6, 30], limits the external validity and generalisability of the findings. In addition, the predominant inclusion of active hotel housekeepers, with the exclusion of workers with pre-existing health conditions [6, 26, 27, 30], may have obscured the full extent of MSDs. Outcome assessment relied primarily on self-reported questionnaires, sometimes with recall periods of up to 12 months, introducing potential reporting and recall biases across all studies.

Although some studies applied multivariable regression models and adjusted for biomechanical, sociodemographic, and psychosocial factors [25, 28], residual confounding cannot be ruled out. These inherent limitations of cross-sectional research likely contribute to the high heterogeneity observed in the meta-analysis, highlighting the need to interpret pooled prevalence estimates with caution.

Other work-related health conditions

Although no studies directly assessed the prevalence of non-musculoskeletal occupational conditions among hotel housekeepers, existing evidence of workplace exposures linked to disease in other settings suggests that significant work-related health risks may also affect this population. This highlights the need for further research to investigate these underexplored risks.

Exposure to cleaning products represents a significant risk to respiratory health, as these substances frequently have irritant properties and contain solvents and other potentially harmful agents [40, 56]. Qualitative studies indicate that hotel housekeepers themselves attribute respiratory symptoms, allergies, and various skin disorders to the regular use of cleaning products [7, 8], which is consistent with the exposure profiles described in the hotel sector [46].

Both the onset of asthma, with or without a latency period, and the exacerbation of pre-existing asthma following occupational exposure to cleaning products have been documented [57–59]. Epidemiological surveillance studies report that cleaning occupations account for a substantial proportion of work-related asthma and reactive airway dysfunction syndrome cases attributed to cleaning agents, including documented exposures in the hotel sector [59]. Moreover, a systematic review and meta-analysis showed that cleaning work is associated with an increased risk of asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, with dose–response relationships and a predominantly irritant pattern [60]. Together, these findings support the biological and occupational plausibility of asthma as a relevant condition among hotel housekeepers, even though this group is not always specifically identified in epidemiological studies [56–61].

Beyond respiratory outcomes, hotel housekeepers experience high levels of skin exposure to chemicals, disinfectants, and wet work, which are key determinants of dermatitis, particularly irritant contact dermatitis and hand eczema. These conditions are widely documented in working groups that include hotel cleaning workers [46, 62–64]. Studies based on official records and specific samples of hotel housekeepers indicate that skin injuries, including dermatitis, cuts, chemical burns, and loss of fingerprints, represent a substantial proportion of occupational injuries, sometimes presenting in severe or very severe forms [9, 24, 64].

A high prevalence of other non-musculoskeletal symptoms has also been reported, including headaches, chest pain, and eye irritation, some of which are severe in a substantial proportion of cases. This finding highlights a broad symptomatic burden that is rarely addressed from an occupational health perspective [24].

In addition, the work of hotel housekeepers involves exposure to potentially infectious biological hazards arising from contact with contaminated objects, human fluids, excreta, and microbial agents, which represents an additional occupational risk [9, 65]. Environmental studies have also documented high concentrations of dust mite allergens in mattresses, carpets, floors, and air conditioning systems in hotel settings. These levels often exceed sensitisation thresholds and increase the occupational risk of allergic respiratory and skin diseases among hotel housekeepers [66–71].

Future research

To improve the comparability of findings, future studies should prioritise representative samples of hotel housekeepers, avoiding the inclusion of other cleaning subgroups and specific subpopulations, such as unionised workers [3, 28, 32], studies should also explicitly address potential biases, including the healthy worker effect, through recruitment strategies independent of employers [25], and recall bias.

In addition, a detailed description of sociodemographic characteristics and a rigorous, quantitative assessment of work-related risks are essential, using validated instruments to measure both exposures and health outcomes. Finally, given the wide range of risks to which this occupational group is exposed, future research should extend beyond musculoskeletal disorders to include other work-related diseases, particularly respiratory and dermatological conditions.

Limitations

Regarding the search strategy, although sensitivity was prioritised, some relevant studies may not have been identified, given the broad range of occupational risks and health conditions considered. This limitation was partially addressed by screening the reference lists of the full-text articles included.

In line with the exclusion criteria, studies focusing primarily on mental health outcomes were not included. However, given the close relationship between psychosocial factors and somatic health outcomes among hotel housekeepers, this decision may have resulted in the exclusion of studies containing relevant information.

The results of the meta-analysis should be interpreted with caution, given the small number of included studies and the substantial heterogeneity observed. This variability likely reflects structural differences between populations and variations in occupational exposures, as well as limitations inherent to cross-sectional study designs, which dominate the evidence base. In addition, working conditions and outcomes were assessed using a mix of instruments, including both ad hoc questionnaires and validated tools, further limiting comparability across studies. Planned subgroup analyses or meta-regression to explore potential sources of heterogeneity could not be conducted due to the limited number of studies and the lack of sufficiently comparable data.

Conclusions

The findings of this review indicate that hotel housekeepers are a clearly vulnerable occupational group from a public health perspective, with a high prevalence of work-related conditions, predominantly musculoskeletal in nature. Disorders affecting the neck, low back, shoulder, and upper limbs consistently emerge as the main health problems, showing high and persistent prevalence across studies. This confirms the substantial disease burden associated with the specific physical and organisational demands of hotel work.

However, interpretation is limited by high heterogeneity and methodological constraints, including the predominance of cross-sectional designs, variable measurement instruments, and potential selection biases such as the healthy worker effect. These factors reduce comparability, limit causal inference, and prevent assessment of the temporal evolution of musculoskeletal and other health problems.

Available evidence suggests that occupational risk factors, particularly high physical workload, repetitive movements, awkward postures, and intense work pace, play a central role in the development of these conditions, while personal and sociodemographic factors show less consistent associations and have been insufficiently studied. In addition, systematic exposure to chemical and biological hazards may contribute to other health problems, including respiratory and dermatological conditions, which have been scarcely studied. These findings highlight the need for future research with representative samples of hotel housekeepers, standardised definitions and measurement of exposures and outcomes, and rigorous control of confounding factors, to inform effective preventive interventions and occupational health policies.
