Nighttime sleep, midday napping, and mortality in Chinese older adults: A China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Peng Ren, Weichao Zhang, Zekun Du, Tianci Gao, Qianfei Wang, Mengxing Hou, Yaru Qiu, Fenqiao Chen, Jianqiang Mei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the independent and combined associations of nighttime sleep duration and midday napping duration with all-cause mortality and premature death in Chinese older adults and to examine potential sex-specific differences.MethodsThis longitudinal cohort study analyzed 11,054 adults aged ≥60 years from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study 2018–2020. Sleep is a key factor in the health of older adults, but prior research has focused on nighttime sleep in Western populations, with limited attention to the culturally common practice of midday napping in China and no systematic analysis of sex differences. Nighttime sleep and daily midday napping durations were collected using standardized questionnaires. Two-year all-cause mortality and premature death were tracked as the primary outcomes. Cox regression, restricted cubic spline analysis, and multiple sensitivity analyses were performed. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals (all p > 0.05).ResultsAfter adjustment for covariates (age, sex, education level, marital status, smoking, alcohol consumption, and number of chronic diseases), daily napping ≥60 min was independently associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality (overall hazard ratio = 1.289, p = 0.013; males: hazard ratio = 1.298, p = 0.049). The “long sleep + long nap” pattern (>8 h nighttime sleep + ≥60 min nap) was associated with the highest risk of all-cause mortality (hazard ratio = 3.048, p < 0.001). Males with 8–10 h of nighttime sleep had an elevated risk of all-cause mortality (hazard ratio = 3.095, p = 0.001), whereas females with 4–6 h of nighttime sleep had a lower risk of premature death (hazard ratio = 0.522, p = 0.039). Restricted cubic spline analysis confirmed a nonlinear U-shaped association between nighttime sleep duration and all-cause mortality.ConclusionsIn Chinese older adults, prolonged daily napping (≥60 min) was associated with a higher risk of mortality after adjustment for covariates. Sex-specific differences were observed, although the formal interaction test did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.112). Nighttime sleep of 6–8 h and avoiding daily napping ≥60 min may be associated with a lower risk of mortality; however, as this was an observational study, causal inference cannot be established.Keywords: Sleep duration, midday napping, older adults, all-cause mortality, premature death, sex differences, China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study

Introduction

Population aging has become a global public health challenge. 1 According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the global population aged 60 years and older is projected to increase from 1 billion in 2020 to 1.4 billion by 2030, accounting for one-sixth of the total population. 2 In China, the population aging process is even more rapid, with the population aged 65 years and older expected to reach 480 million by 2050, representing 34.9% of the total population. 3 Therefore, identifying modifiable risk factors affecting the health and survival of older adults is of great significance for extending healthy life expectancy and reducing the healthcare burden.

Sleep, a basic physiological need throughout the human lifespan, is crucial for maintaining physical health, preventing chronic diseases, and extending healthy longevity.4,5 Previous large-scale epidemiological studies have confirmed a U-shaped or J-shaped association between sleep duration and all-cause mortality, with both short and long sleep durations associated with an increased risk of mortality.6,7 The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and Sleep Research Society (AASM/SRS) consensus recommends that adults sleep 7 or more hours per night, whereas 6–8 h is commonly used as the reference category in sleep–mortality epidemiological studies, although optimal sleep duration may vary by population, cultural background, and age.8,9

Despite existing research providing important evidence for understanding the relationship between sleep and health, three significant research gaps remain. First, most studies have been based on Western populations, limiting the generalizability of their conclusions to Asian countries such as China. Kripke 10 found in the American Cancer Prevention Study cohort that older adults with sleep durations of ≤5 h or ≥9 h had a significantly increased risk of all-cause mortality. However, sleep patterns among Chinese older adults are influenced by multiple factors, including traditional culture, lifestyle habits, and health status, and may differ from those of Western populations. 11 Therefore, relevant research in Chinese populations is urgently needed.

Second, midday napping, a common sleep behavior in Chinese culture, remains controversial regarding its health effects. 12 Midday napping is also prevalent in Mediterranean and Latin American countries where “siesta” is a cultural tradition. Studies from these regions have reported mixed findings: a Greek cohort study found that habitual siesta was associated with reduced coronary mortality, 13 whereas a British cohort study reported that napping >60 min was associated with increased all-cause mortality. 14 However, context of the napping differs across cultures—Chinese midday napping typically occurs after lunch in a home setting, whereas the Mediterranean siesta may involve different timing and social contexts. Understanding these cross-cultural similarities and differences is important for assessing the generalizability of the associations between of napping and mortality. Some studies suggest that short naps (<30 min) can improve cognitive function, alleviate daytime fatigue, and are associated with a reduced risk of all-cause mortality.15,16 However, other studies indicate that prolonged napping (>60 min) may be associated with insulin resistance and an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, ultimately elevating mortality risk. 17 However, previous studies have mostly examined nighttime sleep or midday napping separately, rarely exploring their combined effects. Considering that nighttime sleep and midday napping may interact with each other, understanding their joint effects is crucial for a comprehensive assessment of sleep health.

Finally, sex differences are also an important consideration in the relationship between sleep and health. 18 Females and males differ significantly in sleep patterns, the prevalence of sleep disorders, and physiological responses to sleep deprivation. 19 Postmenopausal women have a significantly higher prevalence of insomnia than men because of hormonal changes, 20 whereas men face different sleep challenges because of social roles and lifestyle differences. 21 However, evidence regarding sex differences in the association between sleep and mortality is limited, particularly because systematic sex-stratified analyses in Chinese older adult populations are lacking.

Based on this, the present study utilized the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to construct a longitudinal cohort, systematically evaluate the independent and combined associations of nighttime sleep duration and midday napping duration with all-cause mortality and premature death in older adults, and explore potential sex-specific differences in these associations. This study aimed to provide scientific evidence for optimizing sleep health guidance for older adults, reducing mortality risk, and improving healthy life expectancy.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study is a longitudinal cohort study used data from the 2018 (fourth wave) and 2020 (fifth wave) surveys of the CHARLS. 22 As a nationally representative longitudinal survey, CHARLS focuses on middle-aged and older adult populations, covering 28 provinces, 150 districts/counties, and 19,816 respondents in China, thereby ensuring the representativeness of the sample. CHARLS participants were originally recruited in 2011–2012 using a multistage stratified probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling design, with follow-up surveys conducted every 2–3 years.

The participant selection process is illustrated in Figure 1. From the CHARLS 2018 baseline survey (Wave 4), which included 19,816 respondents, we applied the following sequential exclusion criteria: (a) age <60 years (excluded n = 7762); (b) missing information on nighttime sleep duration (excluded n = 523); (c) missing information on midday napping duration (excluded n = 298); and (d) missing information on mortality outcomes at the 2020 follow-up (excluded n = 179). The final analytical sample comprised 11,054 participants.

Participant flow diagram.

This study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and written informed consent was obtained from all respondents.

Primary variables of interest

Assessment of midday napping and nighttime sleep. Information on midday napping and nighttime sleep duration at baseline was collected through face-to-face questionnaire investigation by well-trained interviewers. Midday napping was evaluated by asking the question, “During the past month, how long did you take a nap after lunch?” The napping question specifically referred to postlunch napping; therefore, naps taken at other times of the day were not captured by this measure. Midday napping duration was categorized into four groups: 0, >0 to <30, ≥30 to <60, and ≥60 min, with 0 min as the reference group. Napping duration should be interpreted as the average daily postlunch napping time during the past month, consistent with the framing of the nighttime sleep question.

Nighttime sleep was ascertained using the following question: “During the past month, how many hours of actual sleep did you get at night (average hours for one night)?” We classified nighttime sleep duration into five groups: ≤4, >4 to <6, ≥6 to ≤8, >8 to <10, and ≥10 h, with ≥6 to ≤8 h as the reference group. These categories were selected based on prior epidemiological literature on sleep and mortality.23,24

To evaluate the combined effects of nighttime sleep and midday napping, we constructed a combined sleep pattern variable. Nighttime sleep was reclassified into short sleep (<6 h), normal sleep (6–8 h), and long sleep (>8 h); midday napping was reclassified into no nap (0 min), short nap (<60 min), and long nap (≥60 min), forming nine mutually exclusive combined patterns, with “normal sleep + no nap” as the reference group.

All-Cause mortality and premature death. All-cause mortality information was obtained from the 2020 follow-up survey. If participants died during the follow-up period, the date of death was reported by family members or proxies. Premature death was defined as death before reaching China's 2018 average life expectancy, that is, <75 years for males and <77.9 years for females. 25 Follow-up time was calculated as the time interval from the baseline survey to outcome occurrence or the end of follow-up. For deceased individuals with missing specific death dates, follow-up time was conservatively estimated as 2 years.

Assessment of covariates. Based on previous studies, the following potential confounders were selected as covariates. Demographic characteristics included age, sex, education level, marital status, and residence. Education level was categorized as illiterate, primary school, junior or senior high school, and college or above. Marital status was categorized as married, divorced or unmarried, and widowed. Health behaviors included smoking status and alcohol consumption. Health status included the number of chronic diseases, calculated by summing self-reported diagnoses of 14 chronic diseases including: hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes or high blood sugar, cancer or malignant tumor, chronic lung disease, liver disease, heart disease, stroke, kidney disease, stomach or other digestive disease, emotional or psychiatric problems, memory-related disease, arthritis or rheumatism, and asthma. The number of chronic diseases was further categorized as 0, 1, and ≥2. All covariate information was obtained from the 2018 baseline survey.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD, and independent-samples t tests (unpaired, two-tailed) were used to compare sex differences. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and chi-square tests were used to compare group differences. Two-sided p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Missing data handling. Participants with missing data on the primary exposure variables and mortality outcomes were excluded from the main analysis. For covariates with missing values, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was performed as a sensitivity analysis. The imputation model included all covariates, exposure variables and the outcome variable. Predictive mean matching (PMM) was used for continuous variables, and logistic regression was used for categorical variables. Five imputed datasets were generated, and results were pooled using Rubin's rules to account for within-imputation and between-imputation variance. The main analysis was based on complete cases, with multiple imputation results reported in the sensitivity analysis section to assess the robustness of the findings to missing data assumptions.

Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to evaluate the association between sleep exposure and mortality risk. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Specifically, scaled Schoenfeld residuals were plotted against transformed time for each covariate, and a global goodness-of-fit test was performed. Visual inspection of the residual plots showed no systematic time-dependent patterns, and the statistical tests yielded p values >0.05 for all primary exposure variables, confirming that the proportional hazards assumption was satisfied.

Two models were constructed: Model 1 was an unadjusted model including only sleep exposure variables, and Model 2 was a fully adjusted model. The overall-population model adjusted for age, sex, education level, marital status, smoking, alcohol consumption, and number of chronic diseases. In the sex-stratified models, sex was the stratification variable and therefore was not included as a covariate; the remaining covariates were the same as those in the overall model. Results are reported as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Cox regression models were constructed separately by sex to explore sex-specific patterns in the sleep–mortality association. Likelihood ratio tests were used to evaluate the interaction between sex and sleep duration. Combined nighttime sleep and midday napping patterns were used as the exposure variables, with Cox regression employed to evaluate the association between each combined pattern and mortality risk. Likelihood ratio tests were also used to compare the goodness of fit between models including only the main effects and those including interaction terms.

Restricted cubic spline analysis was used to evaluate the nonlinear association between sleep duration as a continuous variable and mortality. In the adjusted models, four knots were placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the sleep duration distribution, with the median as the reference value. p overall and p for nonlinearity were reported.

To verify the robustness of the results, multiple sensitivity analyses were conducted: (1) Stepwise adjustment models. A series of models with progressive covariate adjustment were constructed to assess the confounding structure (Model 1: exposure only; Model 2: + demographics; Model 3: + behavioral factors; Model 4: + health status). (2) Exclusion of extreme sleep values. Participants reporting nighttime sleep duration <3 h or >12 h were excluded. (3) Exclusion of early deaths. Deaths occurring within the first 6 months of follow-up were excluded to minimize reverse causation. (4) Age-stratified subgroup analyses. The subgroup analysis was stratified by 60–69, 70–79, and ≥80 years. (5) Chronic disease–stratified analyses. The subgroup analysis was stratified by 0, 1, and ≥2 chronic diseases. (6) Change of reference group. A nighttime sleep duration of 4–6 h served as the reference. (7) Multiple imputation. MICE was performed with five imputations using the PMM algorithm, with results pooled using Rubin's rules. (8) Interaction testing. Interaction testing comprised likelihood ratio tests for sex × sleep duration, age × sleep duration, and nighttime sleep × midday napping interactions. (9) Weighted analysis. Survey-weighted Cox regression was performed incorporating CHARLS sampling weights.

All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1 software. Two-sided p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of participants

This study included 11,054 older adults aged 60 years and older, with a mean age of 69.4 ± 7.2 years, including 5659 females (51.2%) and 5395 males (48.8%). Regarding sleep patterns, the 4–6 h nighttime sleep group had the highest proportion (34.3%). Males had a higher proportion of 6–8 h sleep (36.7%) than females (29.6%), whereas females had a higher proportion of ≤4 h short sleep (26.2%) than males (16.8%). For napping duration, females had a higher proportion of no napping (41.4%) than males (32.4%), whereas males had a higher proportion of long napping (≥60 min, 24.5%) than females (18.2%), with statistically significant differences (p < 0.05).

Significant sex differences were observed in education level, marital status, nighttime sleep duration, napping duration, alcohol consumption status, and smoking status (p < 0.05). Statistically significant differences were also found in the distribution of the number of chronic diseases (p < 0.05), with females showing a higher proportion of ≥2 chronic diseases (20.2%) compared to males (18.8%), whereas males had a higher proportion of no chronic diseases (53.3%) compared to females (51.1%). However, no statistically significant differences were observed in age or age group distribution (p > 0.05). The baseline characteristics of the study participants are shown in Table 1.

Nighttime sleep duration and mortality risk

Nighttime sleep and all-cause mortality. During the 2-year follow-up period, 545 all-cause deaths and 210 premature deaths were recorded. In the unadjusted model, compared with the 6–8 h reference group, the ≤4 h (HR = 1.645, 95% CI: 1.314–2.059, p < 0.001), 8–10 h (HR = 1.760, 95% CI: 1.330–2.330, p < 0.001), and ≥10 h (HR = 3.236, 95% CI: 2.118–4.944, p < 0.001) groups all showed significantly increased all-cause mortality risk. After adjustment for all covariates, only the ≥10 h group showed a marginally significant trend toward increased risk (HR = 1.795, 95% CI: 0.986–3.267, p = 0.056). No statistically significant differences were observed between the other groups and the reference group (p > 0.05).

Sex-stratified analysis showed that among males, the 8–10 h group in the adjusted model had a significantly increased risk of all-cause mortality risk (HR = 3.095, 95% CI: 1.545–6.199, p = 0.001), whereas no significant differences were observed among the other groups. Among females, no significant differences were observed between any sleep duration groups and the reference group in the adjusted model (see Figure 2 for details).

Nighttime sleep and premature death. Due to the limited number of premature death events (n = 210), the following analyses should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution.

In the premature death analysis, the unadjusted model showed that the ≤4 h (HR = 1.561, 95% CI: 1.106–2.205, p = 0.011) and ≥10 h (HR = 2.342, 95% CI: 1.123–4.885, p = 0.023) groups had a significantly higher risk of premature death than the 6–8 h reference group. Midday napping duration and mortality risk

Midday napping and all-cause mortality. In the unadjusted model, the ≥60 min group had a significantly higher risk of all-cause mortality than the 0-min reference group (overall population HR = 1.396, 95%CI: 1.167–1.671, P < 0.001; males HR = 1.421, 95%CI: 1.118–1.807, P = 0.004; females HR = 1.363, 95%CI: 1.066–1.742, P = 0.013). After adjusting for covariates, the ≥60 min group remained significantly associated with elevated risk (overall population HR = 1.289, 95%CI: 1.055–1.576, P = 0.013; males HR = 1.298, 95%CI: 1.001–1.684, P = 0.049). Among females, the ≥60 min group showed a marginally significant trend (HR = 1.272, 95%CI: 0.978–1.655, P = 0.074). The other napping duration groups (<30 min and 30–60 min) showed no significant differences from the reference group (see Figure 3 for details).

Midday napping and premature death. In the premature death analysis, no statistically significant differences in premature death risk were observed between any napping duration groups and the 0-min reference group in either the unadjusted or adjusted models (p > 0.05) (see Figure 3 for details).

Combined effects of nighttime sleep and midday napping. For the combined pattern analysis, nighttime sleep duration was reclassified into three mutually exclusive categories: short sleep (<6 h), normal sleep (6–8 h), and long sleep (>8 h). Midday napping was reclassified into no napping (0 min), short napping (<60 min), and long napping (≥60 min). These classifications yielded nine nonoverlapping combined patterns, with “normal sleep + no napping” serving as the reference group.

Combined patterns and all-cause mortality. In the fully adjusted model, the “long sleep + long napping” pattern showed the highest risk of all-cause mortality: overall population (HR = 3.048, 95% CI: 1.681–5.527, p < 0.001), males (HR = 5.579, 95% CI: 1.941–16.040, p = 0.001), and females (HR = 2.123, 95% CI: 1.007–4.476, p = 0.048). The other combined patterns showed no significant differences compared with the “normal sleep + no napping” reference group (see Table 2 for details). Notably, the “normal sleep + long napping” pattern (6–8 h nighttime + ≥60 min nap, with total sleep duration depending on nap length) showed a nonsignificant trend toward increased risk (HR = 1.519, p = 0.184), whereas “normal sleep + no napping” (6–8 h nighttime sleep only) served as the reference with the lowest risk. This suggests that the distribution of sleep across nighttime and daytime periods may matter beyond total sleep duration alone, although this comparison did not reach statistical significance (see Table 2 for details).

Dose–response relationships

Nighttime sleep duration and mortality. Restricted cubic spline analysis showed a significant nonlinear association between nighttime sleep duration and all-cause mortality in the overall population (p overall < 0.001, p nonlinearity = 0.0017), displaying a U-shaped curve with the lowest risk at approximately 7 h of nighttime sleep. Among males, a showed significant nonlinear association was also observed (p overall = 0.015, p nonlinearity = 0.014). Discussion

Based on longitudinal data from the CHARLS, this study conducted a 2-year follow-up of 11,054 older adults aged 60 years and older, systematically exploring the independent and combined associations of nighttime sleep duration and midday napping duration with all-cause mortality and premature death, as well as potential sex-specific differences in these associations. The results revealed specific characteristics of the associations between sleep patterns and mortality risk among Chinese older adults, providing preliminary observational evidence that may inform sleep health guidance strategies for older adults.

The main findings of this study are as follows: 1. In the unadjusted models, both short nighttime sleep (≤4 h) and long nighttime sleep (≥10 h) were significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality, but after adjustment for sociodemographic factors and health status, most associations were attenuated and became nonsignificant, with only the ≥10 h group showing a marginally significant trend. 2. Midday napping ≥60 min was significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality, and this association remained significant after adjustment for covariates. 3. The combined pattern of long nighttime sleep and long midday napping (>8 h nighttime sleep + ≥60 min napping) was associated with the highest risk of all-cause mortality and was significant in both males and females. 4. Sex-stratified analyses revealed distinct association patterns, with males in the 8–10 h sleep group and females in the 4–6 h sleep group each exhibiting different risk profiles, although the formal sex × sleep duration interaction test was not statistically significant (p = 0.112).

Association between nighttime sleep duration and mortality and sex differences

This study identified a “U-shaped” association between nighttime sleep duration and all-cause mortality in the unadjusted models, consistent with previous large-scale epidemiological studies. 26 Kripke et al. 10 reported in a prospective cohort study that older adults with sleep durations of ≤5 h or ≥9 h had a significantly increased risk of all-cause mortality risk, and the present study further supports the applicability of this pattern in Chinese older adult. However, after adjustment for age, sex, education level, marital status, smoking, alcohol consumption, and number of chronic diseases, the association between nighttime sleep duration and all-cause mortality was substantially, with only the ≥10 h group showing a marginally significant trend toward increased risk (HR = 1.795, p = 0.056). These findings suggests that the association between sleep duration and mortality may be partially confounded by sociodemographic factors and health status, with abnormal sleep duration more likely reflecting underlying health problems (such as cardiovascular disease, metabolic disorders, and depression) rather than acting as an independent causal factor for mortality. 27

Sex-stratified analyses demonstrated clear differences. Among males, the 8–10 h sleep group remained at significantly increased risk of all-cause mortality after adjustment (HR = 3.095, p = 0.001), whereas among females, no significant differences were observed between any sleep duration groups and the reference group. These sex differences may stem from three factors. First, males and females differ in sleep physiology, including sleep structure and sleep disorder prevalence, with males generally having lower proportions of deep sleep and potentially weaker tolerance to long sleep. 28 Second, postmenopausal hormonal changes in females primarily affect insomnia prevalence 29 but may have relatively minor direct impacts on mortality risk. Third, males have higher proportions of smoking and alcohol consumption because of differences in social roles and lifestyle, which may amplify health risks associated with prolonged sleep. 30 In the premature death analysis, females in the 4–6 h sleep group had a significantly lower risk (HR = 0.522, p = 0.039). This unique finding may be related to higher sleep quality and relatively mild effects of shorter sleep among females. 31 However, given the exploratory nature of the premature death analysis, observation should be interpreted cautiously and confirmed in larger studies.

These findings are consistent with previous reports of sex differences in sleep-health associations. For example, Mallampalli and Carter reported that women generally have higher subjective sleep quality despite reporting more sleep complaints, 19 whereas Krishnan and Collop suggested that sex-specific physiological mechanisms may contribute to differences in sleep-health relationships. 32

Characteristics of association between midday napping duration and mortality

This study found that midday napping of ≥60 min was significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality, and this association remained statistically significant after adjustment for covariates (overall population: HR = 1.289, p = 0.013; males: HR = 1.298, p = 0.049; females: HR = 1.272, p = 0.074), consistent with some previous study conclusions. 33 Leng et al.14 reported that daytime napping of >60 min was associated with increased all-cause mortality in a British cohort, and a meta-analysis by Pan et al. 34 found that long napping (≥60 min) was associated with a 30% increase in all-cause mortality risk across diverse populations. However, a Greek cohort study reported that habitual siesta was associated with reduced coronary mortality, 13 suggesting that the health effects of napping may depend on its duration, frequency, and cultural context.

Several potential mechanisms may explain the observed association between prolonged napping and increased mortality risk. First, prolonged napping may reflect underlying sleep disorders or poor sleep quality, and such populations often have sleep apnea syndrome, which is associated with increased risk of cardiovascular disease and metabolic syndrome. 35 Second, prolonged napping may further reduction in nighttime sleep, disrupting normal circadian rhythms, and thereby affecting metabolic regulation and immune function. 36 Third, prolonged napping is often associated with lower levels of physical activity and greater social isolation, which may indirectly increase health risks. 37

Notably, short napping (<30 min) was not significantly associated with all-cause mortality risk, consistent with research conclusions that short napping can improve cognitive function and alleviate daytime fatigue. 38 These findings suggest that the health effects of midday napping may present a “J-shaped” association, with moderate napping (<60 min) potentially being beneficial, whereas prolonged excessive napping (≥60 min) may be associated with higher risk. This pattern appeared generally consistent in both males and females without significant sex differences.

Combined effects of nighttime sleep and midday napping

This study systematically evaluated the associations between nine mutually exclusive sleep-napping patterns and mortality among older adults. The “long sleep + long napping” pattern was associated with the highest risk of all-cause mortality (overall population: HR = 3.05, 95% CI = 1.68–5.53; males: HR = 5.58, 95% CI = 1.94–16.04; females: HR = 2.12, 95% CI = 1.01–4.48). These findings suggest that different dimensions of sleep duration may have additive associations with health outcomes among older adults, although the cross-sectional nature of exposure assessment precludes causal conclusions.

An important question raised by these findings is whether total sleep duration (nighttime sleep + napping) is more important than the distribution of sleep across daytime and nighttime. Our results suggest that sleep distribution may also be important. The “normal sleep + long napping” pattern (6–8 h nighttime sleep + ≥60 min napping, with total sleep duration depending on nap length) showed a nonsignificant trend toward increased risk (HR = 1.519, p = 0.184), whereas the “normal sleep + no napping” (6–8 h nighttime sleep only) served as the reference group with the lowest risk. These finding suggest that equivalent total sleep time distributed differently between nighttime and daytime may be associated with different mortality risk, although this comparison requires confirmation in larger samples.

The “long sleep + long napping” combined pattern may represent a special sleep phenotype characterized by excessive total sleep time and fragmented sleep. This sleep pattern may be associated with four types of factors. First, severe sleep disorders or sleep apnea syndrome may result in poor nighttime sleep quality and excessive daytime sleepiness. 39 Second, early manifestations of neurodegenerative diseases, as Alzheimer's disease patients often exhibit circadian rhythm disruption and excessive sleep. 40 Third, severe depression or other mental health disorders, with sleep abnormalities being typical symptoms. 41 Fourth, undiagnosed cardiovascular or metabolic diseases, with declining physical function leading to abnormally increased sleep needs. 42 Therefore, this combined sleep pattern may be a marker of underlying serious health problems rather than evidence that sleep duration directly causes increased mortality risk.

Interaction testing showed no significant interaction between nighttime sleep and midday napping with respect to mortality (p = 0.342), suggesting that their associations with mortality are relatively independent rather than synergistic or antagonistic. These findings indicate that nighttime sleep and midday napping may warrant separate investigation when evaluating sleep-health associations, rather than emphasizing potential interaction effects.

Characteristics of premature death association

Because of the limited number of premature death events (n = 210), the following analyses of premature death should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution.

In the premature death analysis, the associations of nighttime sleep duration and midday napping duration with premature death were relatively weak, with most associations becoming nonsignificant after adjustment for covariates. This may be explained by several factors. First, the definition of premature death (males <75 years; females <77.9 years) was relatively stringent, resulting in a limited number of events (210 cases) and reduced statistical power. Sex-stratified analyses further reduced the number of events within each subgroup (males: 96 events; females: 114 events), leading to wide CIs and potentially unstable estimates. Second, the association between sleep and premature death may be largely confounded by the progression of chronic diseases, many of which may not have advanced to the point of death during the 2-year follow-up period. 43 Third, premature death may be more strongly influenced by acute events, such as myocardial infarction and stroke, which may have weaker associations with sleep than with all-cause mortality. 44 Additionally, in unadjusted models, <30 min napping showed a marginally significant protective trend in the overall population and among males, but this trend disappeared after adjustment for covariates, further suggesting that premature death is influenced by multiple factors and that sleep may play a relatively limited role. 45

Innovation and significance of the study

The innovation of this study is reflected in four main aspects. First, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically evaluate the independent and combined associations of nighttime sleep and midday napping with mortality among Chinese older adults, addressing the lack of observational evidence from China, where previous studies have largely focused on Western populations. Second, the study used a large, nationally representative longitudinal cohort of 11,054 participants, enhancing the generalizability of the findings. Third, detailed sex-stratified analyses were conducted, revealing distinct association patterns between males and females and providing preliminary observations that may inform future sex-specific sleep research. Fourth, the robustness of the findings was supported by nine sensitivity analyses, including stepwise covariate adjustment, exclusion of extreme values, multiple imputation, weighted analysis, and interaction testing, thereby strengthening the credibility of the study conclusions.

From clinical and public health perspectives, these findings may have several practical implications. First, older adults who maintain 6–8 h of nighttime sleep may have a lower risk of mortality, although these observational findings should not be interpreted as establishing a causal recommendation. Second, the observed associations suggest that moderate midday napping (<30 min or 30–60 min) may be preferable to excessive napping (≥60 min). Third, for older adults with the “long sleep + long napping” pattern, clinicians may consider comprehensive health assessments to identify underlying sleep disorders, cardiovascular disease, or neurodegenerative disease. Fourth, the observed sex-specific patterns warrant further investigation before sex-specific recommendations can be made. Males may require greater attention regarding the potential risks associated with prolonged sleep, whereas females may have different thresholds for short sleep; however, these observations require confirmation in larger studies with longer follow-up.

Conclusions

This study found that among Chinese older adults, both nighttime sleep duration and midday napping duration were associated with the risk of all-cause mortality, with prolonged midday napping (≥60 min) independently associated with a higher mortality risk. The combined pattern of long nighttime sleep and prolonged midday napping was associated with the highest mortality risk. Sex-stratified analyses revealed distinct association patterns, although the formal interaction test did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.112). These findings provide preliminary observational evidence that may inform future sleep health research and clinical practice for older adults. However, because this was an observational study with self-reported sleep measures and a relatively short follow-up period, the findings do not establish causal relationships and should be interpreted with caution.

Future studies should incorporate longer follow-up periods together with objective sleep monitoring to further investigate whether sleep patterns have a causal influence on mortality risk. Larger studies are also needed to confirm the exploratory findings regarding premature death and sex-specific associations observed in this study. Cross-cultural comparisons involving populations with different napping practices may further clarify the generalizability of these findings. In addition, randomized controlled trials or well-designed quasi-experimental studies evaluating sleep interventions in older adults would provide important evidence for establishing causality and informing future clinical guidelines.
