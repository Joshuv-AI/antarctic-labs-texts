Relapsing polychondritis presenting with sero-negative limbic encephalitis
Authors: Salman Husein, Yuna Murayama, Andrew Koo, Michael Wakefield, Rosaria Buccoliero
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The presented case highlights a rare instance of relapsing polychondritis (RP) manifesting as seronegative limbic encephalitis, an uncommon neurological complication. A 70-year-old female patient with a history of RP-related inflammation, along with neuropsychiatric symptoms, was diagnosed through multidisciplinary collaboration. Swift administration of steroid therapy, followed by azathioprine, led to remarkable physical and cognitive recovery. This case emphasises the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in diagnosing and treating complex autoimmune disorders with neurological manifestations.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old woman presented with urinary retention and confusion. Her past medical history included recurrent episodes of localised, transient inflammation in the last several months preceding admission, including proctitis on computed tomographic (CT) imaging (without findings on colonoscopy) and episcleritis of both eyes. She also had two suspected transient ischemic attacks within the 3 months prior to presentation. Collateral history revealed several hospital visits and a 7-month prodrome characterised by worsening anxiety, emotional lability, fatigue and short-term anterograde amnesia.

On admission, vital signs were unremarkable and Glasgow Coma Score was 15/15. She exhibited tremulous speech, paraphasia, upper limb intention tremor, left-sided dysdiadochokinesia and lower limb hypertonicity. The patient subsequently developed bilateral auricular chondritis, resolving spontaneously within 5 days and leaving a residual cauliflower deformity of the pinnae (Fig 1a). She also developed a self-resolving plantar–palmar erythema (Fig 1b). Initial blood tests revealed normal full blood count, liver/kidney function tests, calcium, phosphate, magnesium, vitamin B12, folic acid, thyroid function test and vitamin D. C-reactive protein was raised to 25 mg/L (normal range 0–10 mg/L), with negative blood and urine culture. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed increased total protein count to 1.06 g/L (normal range 0.2–0.4 g.L), white cell count to 20 × 106/L (normal range 0–5 × 106/L), normal glucose and negative culture. Viral encephalitis was excluded by CSF PCR testing. Assays for ANA, ANCA, complement, paraneoplastic antibodies and autoantibodies associated with autoimmune encephalitis (NMDAR, Glycine, LGI1, CASPR2, IgLON5, AMPA1/2, GABAa and DPPX) were negative. There was no evidence of solid-organ neoplasm on CT imaging of the thorax, abdomen and pelvis. Brain MRI revealed increased T2/FLAIR signals involving the medial temporal lobes and basal ganglia (Fig 1c–f).

Diagnosis

Delirium secondary to urinary tract infection was initially suspected, but this was excluded by negative urine culture. Acute psychosis was considered but was inconsistent with the neurological examination findings. Development of auricular chondritis with a previous history of episcleritis led to a diagnosis of relapsing polychondritis (RP) based on accepted criteria.1 Encephalitis, likely associated with RP, was considered the most probable cause of her clinical presentation. Infectious, paraneoplastic and autoimmune causes of encephalitis were also investigated.

Initial management and prognosis

Risperidone 250 μg nocte was commenced early to manage the psychotic symptoms of delirium. Suspected encephalitis led to the initiation of intravenous acyclovir. This was later suspended following negative PCR.

The patient was commenced on intravenous methylprednisolone, 1 g once daily for 5 days. Nasogastric feeding was initiated while she remained catatonic. After 5 days of intravenous therapy, she regained sufficient consciousness to switch to oral prednisolone at 60 mg daily. On recovery of her mobility and cognition, she was commenced on azathioprine.

Case progression and outcome

Seronegative limbic encephalitis was diagnosed on the basis of negative autoantibody assays and the constellation of MRI findings on the T2/FLAIR sequences. Based on the progression of her symptoms, the timing of disease manifestations and a review of prior literature, we established a link between her diagnosis of RP and the development of encephalitis.

Intravenous methylprednisolone resulted in significant physical improvement and reduction in psychotic symptoms. Cognitive testing demonstrated an ACE-III score rising from 68/100 to 82/100 over 8 days during treatment. At a 2-month follow-up, she was left with some degree of emotional lability and mild deficits in cognitive function.

Discussion

RP is a rare, systemic inflammatory disease which primarily affects type II collagen and proteoglycan-rich structures. The exact aetiology of RP is unknown, but it is regarded as immune-mediated. The primary sites affected are the ears, nose, larynx, trachea, bronchi and joints. While there are no official diagnostic criteria for RP, Michet et al1 provide a framework for clinicians. To meet their criteria, a patient must exhibit either cartilage inflammation in two or more locations or have chondritis of one site and at least two of the following: ocular inflammation, hearing loss, seronegative arthritis and/or vestibular dysfunction. However, these are suggested guidelines and patients may not meet all the criteria for a clinician to diagnose RP.2 In this patient's case, the history of episcleritis and typical auricular chondritis was considered sufficient for diagnosis.

CNS involvement is rare in RP, occurring in approximately 3% of patients, with 37 previously reported cases.3 The mortality rate of RP is 8.1%, increasing to 18% with CNS manifestations.4 There is a significant correlation between patients with ear and eye involvement of RP and CNS manifestations.5 CNS manifestations may present as aseptic meningitis, encephalitis, vascular events or cranial neuropathies.5 Encephalitis, including limbic encephalitis, is the most common CNS sequela of RP.5 Typical features include headache, impaired consciousness and cognitive impairment,3 with or without fever or CSF pleocytosis. Neuroimaging findings may be non-specific or appear normal.

There are no standardised treatment guidelines for RP. For CNS manifestations, including encephalitis, the use of glucocorticoids, methylprednisolone infusion, disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), conventional immunosuppressive agents or biologics is proposed.3 Regarding autoimmune encephalitis, initial pulse therapy with intravenous methylprednisolone has been reported to be highly effective.3 Previously, patients who have had the most favourable outcomes were commenced on additional immunosuppressive therapy following initial high-dose glucocorticoid.2 There are several reports of neurological relapse during steroid tapering, particularly in patients not taking concomitant immunosuppresants.2 In our case, the use of steroids, and later on azathioprine, was successful.

Multi-specialty involvement was key for the diagnosis and management of this patient and we advocate this for achieving the best outcome for patients.

Summary

Relapsing polychondritis may present as encephalitis with the absence of other manifestations. This should be included in a differential for a patient with non-infective encephalitis. •Connective tissue diseases involve multiple organ systems.
