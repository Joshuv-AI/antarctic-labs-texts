Secondary Carpal Tunnel Syndrome Due to Flexor Tenosynovitis in a Diabetic Patient
Authors: Pankaj Sharma, Amit Kale, Dheerajkumar Jadhav
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is the most common entrapment neuropathy of the upper limb. Although many cases are idiopathic, structural or inflammatory pathology of the flexor tendon sheath can result in secondary median nerve compression. Diabetes mellitus predisposes to such changes due to chronic connective tissue alterations, but secondary CTS arising solely from noninfective flexor tenosynovitis remains uncommon. A 49-year-old woman with recently diagnosed type 2 diabetes presented with a 2-month history of progressive right-wrist pain, paresthesia, and volar swelling. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed marked tenosynovitis of the finger flexors with palmar bowing of the flexor retinaculum and associated median nerve edema, whereas nerve conduction studies confirmed compressive neuropathy. Conservative therapy failed, and open carpal tunnel release was performed, yielding immediate symptom relief and improved hand function at 6-week follow-up. Secondary CTS due to chronic flexor tenosynovitis should be suspected in diabetic patients with refractory symptoms. Early multimodal evaluation and timely decompression may prevent irreversible neuropathy.

INTRODUCTION

Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is the most common entrapment neuropathy, affecting 3%–6% of the general population.[1] Although frequently idiopathic, 10%–15% of cases are secondary to conditions such as space-occupying lesions, trauma, or systemic disorders.[2] Flexor tenosynovitis is an important yet underrecognized etiology, particularly among individuals with diabetes mellitus, who exhibit a 2–3-fold increased risk of CTS due to chronic connective tissue alterations.[3]

Persistent synovial inflammation leads to tendon sheath thickening and elevation of intracarpal pressure, ultimately resulting in median nerve compression.[4] Histopathological studies report tenosynovial hypertrophy in up to 90% of surgically treated CTS cases, even without overt inflammatory markers.[5] However, isolated noninfectious flexor tenosynovitis progressing to secondary CTS in diabetic patients remains infrequently documented.

We report a case of secondary CTS resulting from chronic flexor tenosynovitis in a newly diagnosed diabetic patient, emphasizing the importance of early diagnosis and surgical intervention to prevent irreversible nerve dysfunction.

CASE REPORT

A 49-year-old right-hand dominant woman presented with a 2-month history of progressive right-wrist discomfort, described as moderate, persistent throughout the day, and exacerbated by wrist activity. She also reported paresthesia involving the radial three digits and bilateral hand stiffness, more pronounced on the right. There was no preceding trauma, fever, joint involvement, or morning stiffness. Her medical history included systemic hypertension for 3 years and recently diagnosed type 2 diabetes mellitus. There were no symptoms suggestive of tuberculosis, rheumatoid arthritis, or other connective tissue disorders.

Examination revealed diffuse soft-tissue swelling over the volar aspect of the right wrist and mild thenar muscle wasting. Durkan’s compression test, Tinel’s sign, and Reverse Phalen’s test were all positive, whereas wrist range of motion was preserved. Laboratory evaluation showed elevated C-reactive protein (29.70 mg/L), with normal rheumatoid factor and anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibody levels.

High-resolution ultrasonography demonstrated thickening of the flexor tendon sheaths with increased Doppler vascularity, suggestive of active tenosynovitis. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed marked synovial inflammation involving the flexor digitorum superficialis, flexor digitorum profundus, and flexor pollicis longus tendons, along with anterior bowing of the flexor retinaculum. The median nerve was enlarged (16.4 mm² at the tunnel inlet and tapering to 9.7 mm² at the outlet) with increased signal intensity on fat-suppressed sequences, consistent with edema [Figure 1]. Nerve conduction studies demonstrated prolonged distal motor latency, slowed sensory conduction, and reduced velocity, indicating compressive median neuropathy.

Initial management consisted of wrist splinting, analgesics, and activity modification; however, symptoms progressively worsened over 2 months, prompting surgical intervention. Under local anesthesia, a 3–4 cm curved longitudinal incision was made over the volar aspect of the wrist. Following superficial dissection, the palmar fascia was incised to expose the transverse carpal ligament, which was released longitudinally from distal to proximal, achieving adequate decompression of the median nerve. No mass lesions or rice bodies were identified intraoperatively. The wound was closed in layers, and the wrist was temporarily immobilized.

Postoperatively, the patient reported marked relief of paresthesia and nocturnal symptoms within 1 week. Progressive improvement in grip strength and hand function was observed over subsequent weeks. At a 6-week follow-up, there was no tenderness or recurrence of symptoms, and thenar muscle bulk had improved clinically.

DISCUSSION

The present report describes a rare but clinically important presentation of secondary CTS caused by chronic flexor tenosynovitis in a middle-aged woman with recently diagnosed type 2 diabetes mellitus. Unlike idiopathic CTS, which is the predominant form encountered in practice, this case illustrates a structural mechanism of nerve compression driven by soft-tissue hypertrophy within the carpal tunnel. Sequential documentation of tenosynovial inflammation, median nerve distortion, and subsequent recovery following decompression highlights the dynamic progression of pathology and the value of timely surgical intervention in selected cases.

MRI demonstrated marked synovial inflammation of the finger flexors with anterior bowing of the flexor retinaculum and increased median nerve signal intensity, consistent with secondary CTS. Prior MRI neurography studies have similarly demonstrated flexor tenosynovitis in a substantial proportion of CTS cases, reinforcing its diagnostic utility in identifying soft-tissue causes of median nerve compression.[4] Electrophysiological evaluation confirmed compressive neuropathy, corroborated by prolonged latencies and reduced conduction velocities changes consistent with demyelination and axonal dysfunction associated with chronic mechanical pressure.[6]

The etiopathogenesis of secondary CTS in diabetes is likely multifactorial. Chronic hyperglycemia promotes nonenzymatic glycation of connective tissue proteins, resulting in synovial thickening, tendon stiffness, and impaired gliding.[7] Epidemiological data show a higher prevalence and greater severity of CTS among diabetic individuals, often accompanied by poor response to conservative therapy.[1] These metabolic alterations may explain the progressive symptomatology and limited efficacy of nonoperative management in the present case.

Surgical decompression resulted in rapid resolution of paresthesia and functional improvement, consistent with published evidence demonstrating superior outcomes after decompression in moderate-to-severe or refractory CTS.[89] A recent meta-analysis concluded that surgery provides greater symptomatic and electrophysiological improvement than conservative treatment at mid-term follow-up.[9] In addition, tenosynovectomy has been associated with favorable outcomes in CTS secondary to tendon sheath pathology,[10] underscoring the role of operative intervention when soft-tissue causes predominate.

Intraoperative findings excluded other etiologies such as tubercular or rheumatoid synovitis, and histological assessment was not undertaken due to clear clinical–radiological correlation and symptom resolution. This diagnostic certainty, combined with the absence of systemic inflammatory markers, supported a diagnosis of isolated noninfective tenosynovitis.

Overall, this case contributes to current literature by demonstrating a structurally mediated pathway to CTS in a metabolically predisposed patient and underscores the diagnostic value of MRI and nerve conduction studies in evaluating atypical or progressive presentations. Early recognition of secondary causes and timely decompression are essential to prevent irreversible median nerve dysfunction, particularly in high-risk populations such as individuals with diabetes.

CONCLUSION

Flexor tenosynovitis represents an uncommon but clinically relevant etiology of secondary CTS, particularly in patients with recent-onset diabetes mellitus. Multimodal diagnostic evaluation confirmed progressive median nerve compression unresponsive to conservative measures. Surgical decompression resulted in marked symptomatic and functional improvement, emphasizing the importance of timely operative intervention. Recognition of such secondary causes is essential for preventing irreversible neuropathic sequelae and optimizing patient outcomes.
