The characteristics of TCR CDR3 repertoire in COVID-19 patients and SARS-CoV-2 vaccine recipients
Authors: Dewei Zhou, Yan Luo, Qingqing Ma, Yuanyuan Xu, Xinsheng Yao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe COVID-19 pandemic and large-scale administration of multiple SARS-CoV-2 vaccines have attracted global attention to the short-term and long-term effects on the human immune system. An analysis of the “traces” left by the body’s T-cell immune response is needed, especially for the prevention and treatment of breakthrough infections and long COVID-19 and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) variant infections. T-cell receptor complementarity determining region 3 (TCR CDR3) repertoire serves as a target molecule for monitoring the effects, mechanisms, and memory of the T-cell response. Furthermore, it has been extensively applied in the elucidation of the infectious mechanism and vaccine refinement of hepatitis B virus (HBV), influenza virus, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), and SARS-CoV. Laboratories worldwide have utilized high-throughput sequencing (HTS) and scTCR-seq to characterize, share, and apply the TCR CDR3 repertoire in COVID-19 patients and SARS-CoV-2 vaccine recipients. This article focuses on the comparative analysis of the diversity, clonality, V&J gene usage and pairing, CDR3 length, shared CDR3 sequences or motifs, and other characteristics of TCR CDR3 repertoire. These findings provide molecular targets for evaluating T-cell response effects and short-term and long-term impacts on the adaptive immune system following SARS-CoV-2 infection or vaccination and establish a comparative archive of T-cell response “traces.”KEYWORDS: COVID-19, TCR CDR3 repertoire, SARS-CoV-2 vaccine, high-throughput sequencing, single-cell TCR sequencing

Introduction

The global coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, the large-scale administration of multiple severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) vaccines developed in a short time, and the ongoing evolution of SARS-CoV-2 pose short-term and long-term impacts on the human immune system, requiring practical monitoring and systematic evaluation. SARS-CoV-2 is a single-stranded positive-sense RNA virus with an envelope and no segment. Its envelope protein, membrane protein, nucleocapsid (N) protein, and spike (S) protein contain the major antigenic epitopes that induce adaptive immune responses in T cells and B cells [1,2]. Among them, the S protein of SARS-CoV-2 contains a receptor-binding domain (RBD) that can directly bind to the angiotensin converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor, thereby mediating viral membrane fusion and receptor recognition to enter host cells [3]. Therefore, the full-length S protein with the RBD has become the primary target for inducing T and B-cell responses in SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccines and recombinant protein vaccines. Inactivated vaccines based on traditional methods, which contain more viral epitopes, can activate a variety of T and B cells to produce effective responses. Currently, the number of people vaccinated with SARS-CoV-2 vaccines far exceeds 10 billion doses [4].

Most SARS-CoV-2-infected individuals present with asymptomatic, mild, or moderate symptoms, with a few infected individuals rapidly progressing to severe pneumonia [5]. More severe comorbidities, advanced age, and incomplete vaccination increase the risk of COVID-19 mortality [6–8]. Specific T- and B-cell responses are triggered after SARS-CoV-2 infection and vaccination [9,10]. COVID-19 patients are usually characterized by lymphopenia [11], including a reduction in CD4+ T and CD8+ T cells, especially CD4+ T cells [12]. T-cell responses play a critical role in the early viral clearance after SARS-CoV-2 infection, and the involvement of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells reduces disease severity [13]. Memory T cells after infection and vaccination exert a longer-lasting protective effect than the protective neutralizing antibody response of B cells [14]. The presence of SARS-CoV-derived B-cell and T-cell epitopes in immunogenic structural proteins allows the identification of identical SARS-CoV-2 profiles that can help guide vaccine development [15]. In 2003, durable memory T cells from SARS-CoV recovered patients exhibited strong cross-reactivity to the SARS-CoV-2 N protein [16], suggesting that monitoring the TCR “traces” of T cells could provide a feasible molecular target for the mechanism, prevention, and treatment of SARS-CoV-2 infection and vaccination. For COVID-19 patients and SARS-CoV-2 vaccinees, the characterization, sharing, and application of the TCR CDR3 repertoire have been carried out by several laboratories worldwide using different sequencing techniques, such as HTS or single-cell TCR sequencing (scTCR-seq). This article focuses on a comparative analysis of the characteristics and dynamics of the TCR CDR3 repertoire in COVID-19 patients of varying severity and vaccinees of different ages who received different SARS-CoV-2 vaccines in terms of diversity, clonality, V&J gene usage and pairing, CDR3 length, shared CDR3 sequences, and motifs (Graphical abstract).

The analysed data was sourced from PubMed and other public databases. The selected keywords included SARS-CoV-2, SARS-CoV-2 vaccine, COVID-19, TCR repertoire, CDR3 repertoire, high-throughput sequencing, and single-cell sequencing. A total of 62 articles published from 4 May 2020, to 7 January 2024, were screened. We summarized the sources, research subjects and samples of each article, the methods of sequencing and analysing the CDR3 repertoire, and their main characteristics in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

Methods for monitoring and analysing TCR CDR3 repertoire after SARS-CoV-2 infection or vaccination

The formation of peripheral T-cell TCR CDR3 repertoire is based on the random recombination of germline VDJC genes and self-tolerance selection during the development of T cells in the thymus. Approximately 95% of human T-cell TCRs are composed of α and β polypeptide chains, each of which can be divided into a variable region and a constant region. The variable region includes three hypervariable regions (CDR1, CDR2, and CDR3), with the CDR3 region complementary to antigen epitopes. In theory, each individual can produce more than 1015 types of TCRs, representing the “immune fingerprint” of the individual T-cell response [17].

The library construction of TCR CDR3 from DNA or RNA samples is commonly achieved using techniques such as nested PCR, multiplex PCR, 5’ RACE, and single-cell RNA labeling. Based on the distinct methods of library construction, corresponding sequencing techniques, namely, Sanger sequencing, next-generation sequencing (NGS), and 10×Genomics single-cell VDJ sequencing (scRNA-seq), are employed to obtain the CDR3 repertoire [18]. The HTS data from multiple PCR or 5’RACE library construction can only be used to analyse the CDR3 sequence characteristics of a single TCR chain in the sample, while scTCR-seq can provide detailed sequence information of both paired chains in each T cell. Currently, the characterizations of CDR3 repertoire after SARS-CoV-2 infection or vaccination have been carried out by several laboratories based on the 10×Genomics platform using scTCR-seq [19–26]. In recent years, various tools and software for visualization, digitization, and standardization have been developed and continuously improved for feature annotation and analysis of large CDR3 repertoires, mainly including IMGT/High V-Quest, MiXCR, VDJtools, Immunearch, ImmuneML, CoNGA, scRepertoire, DeepTCR, and R language. We have summarized the library construction, sequencing, and analysis methods and tools used for CDR3 in each study in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

Characterization, changes, and significance of TCR CDR3 repertoire in COVID-19 patients and SARS-CoV-2 vaccinees

Diversity and clonality

The diversity of the TCR CDR3 repertoire is commonly interpreted and evaluated using Simpson’s index, anti-Simpson’s index, Shannon’s index, Chao1 index, and D50 index. The peripheral TCR CDR3 repertoire is highly diverse under physiological conditions, but the diversity changes dynamically in response to acute and chronic infections. The CDR3 diversity can monitor the effect of antigen exposure on the T-cell response and assess the response capacity of different individuals [27]. The clonality of the TCR CDR3 repertoire, which monitors the degree of clonal expansion of specific responding T cells in various pathological states and at different stages of the disease, is usually compared among small, medium, large, and extra-large clones or among rare versus high-frequency clones or quantified using a specific clonal index [28].

COVID-19 patients had a general reduction in TCR CDR3 diversity and underwent varying degrees of clonal expansion during the infectious phase. Hou et al. [29], Wang et al. [20], and Park et al. [30] found that the diversity of COVID-19 patients was lower than that of health controls (HCs). These results suggest that SARS-CoV-2 infection triggers the clonal expansion of specific T cells in the body, leading to a reduction in the diversity of TCR CDR3 repertoire in the peripheral blood. Further studies revealed a positive correlation between the decrease in TCR CDR3 diversity and the clinical symptoms of COVID-19 patients. The more severe the clinical symptoms are, the more pronounced the reduction in diversity, accompanied by a high frequency of clonal expansions, which can gradually recover during the recovery phase. Zhang et al. [22] found comparable clonal expansion of T cells in severe and moderate COVID-19 patients but lower diversity in severe patients than in mild patients. Zhou et al. [31] revealed that COVID-19 patients admitted to the ICU had significantly lower diversity, which recovered during the recovery period but was also significantly lower than that of HCs. Chang et al. [32] found that COVID-19 patients with pneumonia (6 patients) exhibited greater clonal expansion and reduced diversity during recovery than patients with mild disease (3 patients). Unterman et al. [19] observed that COVID-19 patients in progressive stages exhibit greater clonal expansion of CD8+ T cells and fewer CD4+ T cells than stable patients. The diversity of both memory and naive CD8+ T cells was lower. Bai et al. [25] comparatively analysed COVID-19 patients and reported that the diversity of symptomatic individuals (33 patients) was lower than that of HCs (11 patients), while asymptomatic individuals (5 patients) were not significantly different from HCs. Wang G et al. [33] discovered less diversity in the recovery group than in the baseline and acute groups. Overall, the diversity and clonality of the TCR CDR3 repertoire have gained widespread application in disease progression monitoring and prognostic assessment of COVID-19 patients.

However, several studies have reported no significant changes in the diversity of the TCR CDR3 repertoire after SARS-CoV-2 infection [23,34–38]. These findings indicate that there are large differences in T-cell responses across SARS-CoV-2-infected individuals. Some patients may undergo more expansion of low-frequency T-cell clones, so significant changes in the CDR3 repertoire may not be detectable using HTS.

The changes in the clonality of TCR CDR3 sequences in SARS-CoV-2 vaccinated individuals are similar to those in infected individuals. Sureshchandra et al. [39] analysed four volunteers vaccinated with two doses of the mRNA vaccine, and clonal expansion was evident 14 days after the second dose. Kavazović et al. [40] observed that BNT162b2 vaccine administration results in a higher frequency of clonal expansion. Consistent results were obtained by Swanson et al. for AZD1222 vaccination [41] and by Hansen et al. for BNT162b2 vaccination [42]. This suggests that the SARS-CoV-2 vaccine stimulates a T-cell response similar to that following infection.

However, Wang et al. [43] did not find large-scale clonal expansion in their study of six CoranaVac vaccine volunteers. No significant alterations in CDR3 diversity or clonality were found in some studies of mRNA vaccine [44], BNT162b2 vaccine [45], ChAdOx1 vaccine [46], and Ad5-nCoV vaccine [47]. This finding suggested that multiple SARS-CoV-2 vaccinations did not induce efficient responses in the corresponding T cells compared to COVID-19 patients. This may be associated with multiple factors, such as the age of the volunteers. After the BNT162b2 administration, there are significant differences in the diversity and clonality of TCR CDR3 between young and elderly individuals [42].

V&J gene preferential usage and pairing

TCR is immunoglobulin-like molecules composed of the variable (V), diversity (D), join (J), and constant (C) gene segments [48]. The specificity of the recognition depends on the specific selection of the V and J gene segments during the rearrangement of TCR genes [49]. Upon monitoring the characteristics of TCR CDR3 repertoire following exposure to HBV, HIV, or influenza virus infection or vaccination, predominant V and J gene usage and pairing were observed in some families, which suggests a close correlation with the predominant viral antigenic epitopes [50–52]. In research on the TCR CDR3 repertoire of COVID-19 patients (Supplementary Table S1), there were discernible differences in the V and J dominant usage and pairing families, suggesting the diversity of SARS-CoV-2 antigenic epitopes and the complexity of individual responses. There are several common V and J genes and paired families. The prevalent TRBV genes include V3, V9, V11, V12, V15, V21, V24, and V27. The predominant TRBJ genes are J1–2, J1–6, J22, and J2–1. The dominant TRBV and TRBJ pairings are V7J2–7, V6J2–3, V4-2J2-2, and V7-7J2-3. The prevailing TRAV genes include V1–2, V3, V4, V9–2, V12–2, V20, V22, and V25. The dominant TRAJ genes include J49, J27, J33, and J52. The most common pairs of TRAVs and TRAJs are V8-1J32 and V26-1J26. Although there is currently a lack of specific research on common V and J usage and the corresponding antigenic epitopes, it is possible to monitor preferential V&J usage and characteristic V-J pairing in COVID-19 patients with different symptoms. Zhang et al. [53] observed an elevation in the expression of AJ39, AJ43, and BJ1–1 in severe COVID-19 patients, while BJ2–1 was preferred in moderate and convalescent patients. Zhou et al. [31] found that the selection frequencies of BV28, BJ2–7, BJ1–5, and BJ1–4 were greater in virus-negative convalescent patients than in virus-positive patients. Wen et al. [24] observed that AV8–6-AJ45 and BV7-8-BJ2-1 were the dominant pairs in early recovery patients. Wang et al. [43] discovered that severe COVID-19 patients exhibited dominant pairings of BV9/BJ1-6, BV27/BJ1-4, and BV4–1/BJ1-5, whereas mild patients did not. Bai et al. [25] reported an increased frequency of AJ16 and BJ2–1 in asymptomatic COVID-19 patients, with predominant pairings of AV1–2/AJ33, BV6–4/BJ2-2, AV1–2/AJ33, and BV6–1/BJ2-3. The frequencies of AV2, AJ8, AJ40, BV3–1, and BV5–1 were the highest in symptomatic patients. Wang et al. [33] observed that BV12, BV20, BV24, BV3, BV9, and BJ2–5 exhibited higher levels in COVID-19 patients compared to HCs. The expression levels of BJ1–3 and BJ1–4 were significantly elevated in both the baseline and acute groups compared to the recovery group. The variation in the usage of dominant V and J genes and pairing families in COVID-19 patients with different disease severities indicates that there are dynamic alterations in the dominant viral antigenic epitopes and the corresponding T-cell responses during the infection process. This finding offers specific responding T-cell targets for studying the distinctive pathogenic mechanism of SARS-CoV-2, which differs from that of other viruses.

In the TCR CDR3 repertoire of individuals vaccinated with the inactivated SARS-CoV-2 vaccine, Cao et al. [26] observed a high frequency of specific gene families, such as BV7–6, BJ2–1, AV23, and AJ52, which are similar to those observed in COVID-19 patients. They may target the same epitopes, providing a basis for detailed mechanisms of T-cell responses to SARS-CoV-2 infection and for enhancing the T-cell response to vaccines.

The length of TCR CDR3

The CDR3 length of TCR repertoire is the basis for analysing the characteristics of T cells and their corresponding antigenic epitopes. Under physiological conditions, the length of human TCR β CDR3 follows a normal distribution with a predominance of 14–15 amino acids (AAs). However, there was a significant disparity between the longest and shortest CDR3 lengths. In 2006, it was initially noted that the longest and shortest CDR3 sequences of TCR α chains exhibited a discrepancy of 18 codons in healthy individuals, and those of TCRβ chains exhibited a discrepancy of 19 codons [54,55]. Analysis of the CDR3 length variation can reveal the relationship between the structure of different TCRs and the corresponding specific epitopes [27].

COVID-19 patients exhibited longer biased usage of TCR CDR3 compared to HCs. Hou et al. [29] discovered that the CDR3 length of COVID-19 patients was longer than that of HCs. Wang et al. [33] observed that patients with COVID-19, regardless of baseline, acute, or recovery group, had a broader CDR3 distribution compared to HCs. Ruiz et al. [56] found an increased CDR3 length in COVID-19-related clones compared to ordinary clones through computer simulation analysis. Chang et al. [32] demonstrated the frequency of longer CDR3s in pneumonia patients was greater than that in mild patients. Although the relationship between the length of TCR CDR3 and other viral infections has not been reported, in the study of HIV B-cell responses, it was discovered that an excessively long BCR HCDR3 can target conserved epitopes in deep and obscured regions of the HIV-1 envelope, leading to the production of broadly neutralizing antibodies [57]. The skewed length of the TCR CDR3 in COVID-19 patients may be associated with the specific advantageous epitopes of the SARS-CoV-2 antigen, representing a novel avenue for investigating the differences between SARS-CoV-2 and other viruses. Similar to other studies on the characteristics of the TCR CDR3 repertoire, certain laboratories have not detected significant alterations in the length of TCR CDR3 for COVID-19 patients compared to HCs [20,23,30,43,58]. The diversity of SARS-CoV-2 antigen epitopes and the heterogeneity of MHC-antigen peptides among individuals could influence the distribution and bias of TCR CDR3 length.

There is limited research on the analysis of TCR CDR3 length after SARS-CoV-2 vaccination. Sureshchandra et al. [39] observed that the administration of mRNA vaccines (BNT162b2 and mRNA-1273) also led to an increase in the length of CDR3. However, Kavazović et al. [40] compared three groups: individuals with prior infection, individuals vaccinated with the BNT162b2 vaccine, and those with both. They found that individuals infected with SARS-CoV-2 had the longest CDR3, while those vaccinated had the shortest CDR3. Tong et al. [59] discovered that there were no significant changes in the CDR3 length distribution in nine healthy volunteers before and after administration with the inactivated BBIBP-CorV vaccine. These results indicate that unlike during SARS-CoV-2 infection, the administration of SARS-CoV-2 vaccines does not lead to significant alterations in the distribution of TCR CDR3 length.

TCR CDR3 shared clone

The shared CDR3 sequences of T and B cells in human responses to viral infections serve as a foundation for the study of adaptive immune response mechanisms, disease treatment, and vaccine development. Laboratories worldwide have analysed shared TCR CDR3 sequences of COVID-19 patients or SARS-CoV-2 vaccine recipients, providing practical technical solutions for understanding the molecular mechanisms of T-cell responses related to SARS-CoV-2, monitoring disease progression, and refining vaccines. Zhang et al. [22] observed the proportion of shared CDR3 is higher in the mild COVID-19 patients compared to the severe patients or HCs. Wang et al. [33] collected samples from the baseline, acute phase, and recovery phase COVID-19 patients, revealing that the overlap index of TCR CDR3 AA sequences was significantly higher in patients compared to HCs. However, the overlap index remained similar across the distinct infection stages of COVID-19 patients, and 63 shared CDR3s between COVID-19 patients and HC group. Hou et al. [29] analysed 593 COVID-19 patients from three different global locations and identified the shared sequences CASSPWTGQETQYF, CASSLNRAGNTIYF, CASSPGGRGNQPQHF, CASSARLAGGTDTQYF, and CASVGRGSYNEQFF, suggesting that patients from different geographic regions may share common TCR CDR3 repertoires for SARS-CoV-2 infection. Zhang et al. [53] discovered the shared sequences of CASSQGFQRGTNEQYF, CSARVTGTTYNEQFF, and CASSPGLAGDNEQF in moderate and convalescent COVID-19 patients. Zhou et al. [31] analysed and found that CSARDRGYGYTF, CSARSSYEQYF, and CASSETGELFF were common among non-ICU patients, ICU patients, convalescent patients with negative viral, and re-infected patients. In the public TCR CDR3 repertoire of COVID-19 patients, Jian et al. [36] identified sequences such as CASSIEGQLSTDTQYF, CASSFGVAGGIYSPLHF, CASRLGLAGGNEQFF, and CASQGLVDENEQFF, which were found in a higher proportion among moderate and severe cases. Huang et al. [60] discovered that CAMKTSYDKVIF and CASTPGDTIYF clonotypes persisted from the disease state to the cure state. Georgakilas et al. [61] utilized machine learning to analyse and retrieve data from a database, revealing that the CASSLSSPQETQYF+TRBV27-1+TRBJ2-5 clonotype was only detected in convalescent individuals. The authors have identified six specific TCR CDR3 sequences that interact with surface glycoproteins, ORF1ab, nucleocapsid phosphoprotein, and envelopes.

In addition to analysing the shared TCR CDR3 sequences, some researchers have conducted analyses of the shared CDR3 motifs. Unterman et al. [19] discovered a common motif, “QDIG,” in COVID-19 patients. Schultheiss et al. [28] identified a shared motif, “S-GGTGYE,” in convalescent COVID-19 patients. Park et al. [30] found some shared motifs in mild and moderate COVID-19 patients through machine learning analysis, such as YNE, NEQ, EQF, QFF, TEA, EAF, and AFF. Rowntree et al. [62] observed that compared to adult COVID-19 patients, children have fewer clonal expansions and shared motifs in SARS-CoV-2-specific T cells.

The shared TCR CDR3 sequences and motifs among SARS-CoV-2 vaccine recipients resemble those observed in COVID-19 patients. Minervina et al. [63] discovered that many spike-specific TCR CDR3 motifs were shared following SARS-CoV-2 infection and vaccination. Armistead et al. [44] observed a low degree of TCR CDR3 overlap between CD45+ BMCs and PBMCs in the breast milk of mRNA vaccine recipients. Henze et al. [46] identified overlapping TCR CDR3 clonotypes within and between groups of homologous and heterologous BNT162b2 vaccine recipients. Ren et al. [64] found that after BBIBP-CorV vaccination, the following shared CDR3 sequences were identified: CASSLLGVNTEAFF, CASSPGLGDTQYF, CASSPLGNTEAFF, CASSSYSGGATDTQYF, and CSVGGQGYEQYF. Ford et al. [65] discovered the expansion of heterogeneous T-cell clones and highly public TCR motifs after COVID-19 patients received three doses of mRNA vaccines.

Summary and prospects

Changes in the TCR CDR3 repertoire of T cells, including decreased diversity, increased clonal expansion, increased CDR3 length, preferential usage and pairing of specific V&J genes, and shared CDR3 sequences and motifs, are observed in COVID-19 patients and SARS-CoV-2 vaccine recipients. These typical features show common dynamic changes in COVID-19 patients at different stages of infection and in different SARS-CoV-2 vaccine recipients of various ages. Similar patterns of changes in TCR CDR3 repertoire have been observed in other respiratory infections. For instance, there is cross-reactivity between influenza virus and SARS-CoV-2 T cells, and the cross-reactive T cells and influenza virus-specific T cells shared similarities in terms of TCR CDR3β sequence, TRBV19–01 gene usage, and clonal overlap [66]. However, some studies have not observed characteristic changes in TCR CDR3 repertoire, which may be closely related to differences in viral strains, vaccine quality from different manufacturers, and individual variations in the study population. During the early COVID-19 pandemic, as individuals were more SARS-CoV-2 naive, initial infection or vaccination triggered a widespread and significant T-cell response, with evident TCR expansion. However, as the immune memory was established, these responses became more focused and specific [63,67].

Currently, several laboratories have reported partial characterization of the immune system in patients with long COVID and vaccinated individuals with breakthrough infections, but these findings have not shed light on the underlying mechanisms involved. Based on the characterization of the TCR CDR3 repertoire in COVID-19 patients and SARS-CoV-2 vaccine recipients, multiple laboratories have established a shared platform for SARS-CoV-2-related TCR CDR3 analysis [36,68–70]. The platform allows long-term monitoring and comparison of T-cell response “trace” targets and provides a basis for improving the T-cell response effect induced by the SARS-CoV-2 vaccine and for targeted T-cell immunotherapy in severe COVID-19 patients. The development of HTS, machine learning, and public databases has made it possible to identify and annotate epitope-specific TCRs. These tools and methods can predict epitopes that may not be included in current MegaPools, enhancing our understanding of T cell responses [71–73]. This will provide a practical method for evaluating the short- and long-term effects on the human immune system caused by mass vaccination of humans with various types of SARS-CoV-2 vaccines and the prevalence of persistent SARS-CoV-2 mutations in humans and establish a dynamically comparable archive of the adaptive immune response.
