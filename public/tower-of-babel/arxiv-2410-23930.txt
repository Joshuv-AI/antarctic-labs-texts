Hall Transport of Charged Particles in Magnetic Disk Array
Linlin An, Peifeng Fan

Abstract
The concept of periodic structures has driven the development of advanced materials like photonic and phononic crystals. These metamaterials typically rely on complex repeating units or meta-atoms, limiting their adaptability after fabrication. To overcome this limitation, we introduce the concept of metafields, which are repeating patterns of local magnetic fields instead of material structures. Unlike metamaterials, which consist of atoms arranged in structured patterns, metafields focus on the patterns of fields alone, allowing for dynamic property adjustments through external electric currents. This study explores a specific metafield where the repeating pattern is the magnetic disk (MD), defined by a uniform magnetic field perpendicular to its surface. By arranging multiple MDs, we form a magnetic disk array (MDA) and theoretically investigate the charged particle dynamics within this array. Our analysis highlights Hall transport phenomena, such as Hall diffusivity, conductivity, and thermal Hall effects. Using complex variables, we derive the collision integral and Boltzmann equation for particle distribution, applying perturbation methods and Fourier analysis to calculate transport coefficients. Simulations reveal a one-way preferential diffusion at the interface between MDAs with opposing field directions, where diffusion intensity varies with the particle movement direction. This highlights metafields’ potential for dynamic particle control applications.

The concept of crystals, which are defined by the periodic repetition

into two categories: wave metamaterials and diffusion metamaterials

[

,

. For example, photonic crystals are
classified as electromagnetic (wave) metamaterials, while phononic
crystals belong to the category of acoustic wave metamaterials. A
defining feature of traditional metamaterials is the use of basic
repeating elements, or "meta-atoms," each
consisting of numerous molecules and atoms.

However, a significant drawback is that once fabricated, these metamaterials
have limited flexibility, as key parameters such as the lattice structure
and material properties are difficult to modify post-production. This
rigidity restricts their ability to adapt to changing external conditions
or demands
[
21
,
22
]
. To overcome this
limitation, we propose a new class of materials called metafields,
where the repeating elements are not material structures but local
magnetic fields. This approach offers a major advantage: the ability
to dynamically adjust the properties of these metafields through the
external control of electric currents. Unlike traditional metamaterials,
metafields can be fine-tuned in real time, providing an unprecedented
level of adaptability. In this study, we focus on a specific type
of meta-atom within a metafield, known as a magnetic disk (MD), which
is defined as a circular region with a uniform magnetic field perpendicular
to its surface. By arranging multiple MDs, we construct a magnetic
disk array (MDA). We theoretically investigate the evolution of charged
particles within the MDA and analyze the resulting transport properties.
In particular, we discuss various Hall transport phenomena, such as
Hall diffusivity, Hall conductivity, and thermal Hall effects, that
arise in this system. In the literature, the term "Hall"
is sometimes replaced by "odd" to reflect
the antisymmetric nature of certain transport tensors

For example, the Hall diffusion tensor exhibits opposing off-diagonal

where phenomena such as Hall viscosity commonly occur.

In our approach, we treat the interaction between particles and MDA
as collisions. We derive the collision integral (or collision operator)
and the corresponding Boltzmann equation to describe the particle
distribution. To simplify this derivation, we employ a complex variable
formulation to represent the two-dimensional motion of particles,
which proves to be more efficient than traditional vector formalism.
This technique also allows for the straightforward derivation of single
and inverse collision processes. Using perturbation methods and Fourier
analysis, we compute the perturbation distribution of particles. Assuming
local equilibrium is characterized by a mono-kinetic distribution,
we calculate key transport coefficients, including the Hall diffusion
tensor, Hall conductivity tensor, and thermal Hall conductivity tensor.
Furthermore, we simulate the Hall diffusion equation to further understand
these transport properties. A particularly interesting finding emerges
when particles evolve in regions where two MDAs with opposing field
directions are present. At the interface between these two MDAs, we
identify a one-way preferential diffusion phenomenon, where diffusion
intensity varies depending on the direction of particle movement.
This highlights the unique transport properties introduced by metafields
and suggests potential for new applications in controlling particles.

II.3
The inverse collision in a MD

In this study, we consider a charged particle crossing the region
of a MD as a 2D binary collision process. In the collision process,
the MD remains stationary, and the particle’s momentum is not conserved;
however, its energy is conserved. To derive the collision integral
of the Boltzmann equation (see Sec.
III
),
we need to determine the inverse of the collision process. Specifically,
we need to know the velocity at which the particle must enter the
MD so that it can escape the region with a given initial velocity.
To clearly convey the fundamental physical process, we represent the
collision as

\left(\text{intial state};\mathrm{parameters}\right)\rightarrow\left(\text{final state};\mathrm{parameters}\right),

(20)

where the state refers to the position and velocity of a particle,
i.e., a state is a 2-tuple
\left(r,v\right)
, and the parameters
are the invariants of the scattered particle and target object, such
as mass, charge, field strength, etc. We use the right arrow
"\rightarrow"

to denote a possible process, and
"\nrightarrow"
to indicate a
forbidden process.

We now define several basic discrete transformations of the collision
process. The time reversal transformation is defined as

T\left[\left(r_{A},v_{A}\right)\rightarrow\left(r_{B},v_{B}\right)\right]=\left(r_{B},-v_{B}\right)\rightarrow\left(r_{A},-v_{A}\right),

(21)

where we omit the parameters for simplicity. From Eq. (
21
),
we can see that time reversal implies a reversed collision process;
that is, if we reverse the direction of the final velocity, the particle
will retrace its path and return to its original position. The parity
transformation is defined as

P\left[\left(r_{A},v_{A}\right)\rightarrow\left(r_{B},v_{B}\right)\right]=\left(-r_{A},-v_{A}\right)\rightarrow\left(-r_{B},-v_{B}\right),

(22)

which represents a centrosymmetric collision process. The mirror transformation
of the collision process about the
x_{i}
-axis, where
x_{i}=x,y
,
is collision process in a mirror perpendicular to
x_{i}
-axis. This
transformation is denoted by
P_{x_{i}}
and is defined as follows:

\displaystyle P_{x}\left[\left(r_{A},v_{A}\right)\rightarrow\left(r_{B},v_{B}\right)\right]=\left(-r_{A}^{*},-v_{A}^{*}\right)\rightarrow\left(-r_{B}^{*},-v_{B}^{*}\right),

(23)

\displaystyle P_{y}\left[\left(r_{A},v_{A}\right)\rightarrow\left(r_{B},v_{B}\right)\right]=\left(r_{A}^{*},v_{A}^{*}\right)\rightarrow\left(r_{B}^{*},v_{B}^{*}\right),

(24)

where the asterisk
*

the associated complex number. The rotation of the collision process
around the

\displaystyle\sigma_{f}\left(F\right)=-F,

\displaystyle\sigma_{c}\left(q\right)=-q,

where

magnetic field. In Eqs. (

we have assumed that all the collision processes are allowed.

dt

is

dtd^{2}rd^{2}v\int_{-|r_{D}|}^{|r_{D}|}f_{\alpha}\left(t,r,v\right)n_{D}(t,r)|v|db.

(36)

The “gains”, or the collisions that bring particles into the the
range
v
during
dt
, are given by

\displaystyle dtd^{2}rd^{2}\tilde{v}\int_{-|r_{D}|}^{|r_{D}|}f_{\alpha}\left(t,r,\tilde{v}\right)n_{D}(t,r)|\tilde{v}|db

\displaystyle=dtdrdv\int_{-|r_{D}|}^{|r_{D}|}f_{\alpha}\left(t,r,\tilde{v}\right)n_{D}(t,r)|v|db,

(37)

where
\tilde{v}
is the inverse velocity of
v
. In deriving Eqs. (
36
)
and (
37
), we used the relations
d\tilde{v}=dv
and

|\tilde{v}|=|v|
(since the Lorentz force in a magnetic field does
not change the speed of the charged particle). Additionally, we assumed
that the particle’s position is approximately the same as the center
of the MD when they encounter each other. Subtracting the “losses”
(
36
) from the “gains” (
37
) and divide
it by
dtdrdv
, we thus derived the collision integral as

\left(\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial t}\right)_{c}=n_{D}(t,r)|v|\int_{-|r_{D}|}^{|r_{D}|}\left[f_{\alpha}\left(t,r,\tilde{v}\right)-f_{\alpha}\left(t,r,v\right)\right]db.

(38)

Since the inverse velocity
\tilde{v}
is determined by
\varphi

(see Eqs. (
35
) and (
12
)),
we need to transform the integral in Eq. (
38
)
into a form that is expressed in terms of
\varphi
. From Eq. (
18
),
we can see that the scattering property is totally different for different
sign of gyro frequency. Using Eq. (
18
), we have

db=s_{\alpha}|r_{D}|\mathrm{sin\varphi d\varphi},

(39)

where

s_{\alpha}\equiv\mathrm{sgn(\omega_{c\alpha})}.

(40)

In this section and the ones that follow, we will use the subscript

\alpha
to distinguish between different physical quantities. For
example,
\omega_{c\alpha}
in Eq. (
40

the gyrofrequencies of different particle species, given by
\omega_{c\alpha}=-q_{\alpha}B_{z}/m_{\alpha}c
,
where
q_{\alpha}
and
m_{\alpha}
denote the charge and mass of
the
\alpha
-species.

By combining Eqs. (

),
and substituting Eq. (

the collision integral becomes

\text{$\left(\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial t}\right)$}_{c}^{\left(+\right)}=n_{D}(t,r)|v||r_{D}|\int_{0}^{\pi}\left[f_{\alpha}\left(t,r,\frac{\gamma_{\alpha}-e^{i\varphi}}{\gamma_{\alpha}-e^{-i\varphi}}v\right)-f_{\alpha}\left(t,r,v\right)\right]\mathrm{sin\varphi}d\varphi,

(41)

where

III.2
Boltzmann equation and the moment equation

With the collision integral derived, the Boltzmann equation can be
obtained as

\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial t}+\bm{v}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial\bm{r}}+\frac{\bm{F}_{\alpha}}{m_{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial\bm{v}}=\left(\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial t}\right)_{c}^{\pm},

(45)

where
\bm{F}_{\alpha}=\bm{F}_{\alpha}\left(t,\bm{r},\bm{v}\right)

is the force field. Further details on this topic can be found in
the literature, such as in Ref.
Lifshitz and Pitaevskii [31]
. In this
study, we assume that the force can be expressed as

\bm{F}_{\alpha}\left(t,\bm{r},\bm{v}\right)=\bm{F}_{\alpha}^{\left(1\right)}\left(t,\bm{r}\right)+\bm{F}_{\alpha}^{\left(2\right)}\left(t,\bm{r},\bm{v}\right),

(46)

where
\bm{F}_{\alpha}^{\left(1\right)}
is only dependent
on the position
\bm{r}
and
\bm{F}_{\alpha}^{\left(2\right)}

is perpendicular to the velocity, i.e.,
\bm{F}_{\alpha}^{\left(2\right)}\cdot\bm{v}
.
In cases where no confusion arises, the complex form of collision
integral in Eq. (
45
) is automatically identified
as the vector form. Using Wirtinger derivative notation
Remmert [32]
,
Kreutz-Delgado [33]
,
the Boltzmann equation (
45
) can also be expressed
in the form of complex variables as

\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{2}\left[v\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial r^{*}}+v^{*}\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial r}\right]+\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{F_{\alpha}}{m_{\alpha}}\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial v^{*}}+\frac{F_{\alpha}^{*}}{m_{\alpha}}\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial v}\right]=\left(\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial t}\right)_{c}^{\pm}.

(47)

From the microscopic description of the Boltzmann transport equation
(
45
) or (
47
), we
can transition to a fluid mechanics framework by averaging over velocity-dependent
functions. This approach, known as the moment method, yields a macroscopic
transport model
Swanson [34]
.

Suppose
\psi\left(\bm{v}\right)
is an arbitrary function
of velocity. Its average value, taken over the velocity space, is
defined as

\left\langle\psi\left(\bm{v}\right)\right\rangle_{\alpha}\equiv\frac{1}{n_{\alpha}\left(t,\bm{r}\right)}\int f_{\alpha}\left(t,\bm{r},\bm{v}\right)\psi\left(\bm{v}\right)d^{2}\bm{v},

(48)

where

n_{\alpha}\left(t,\bm{r}\right)=\int f_{\alpha}\left(t,\bm{r},\bm{v}\right)d^{2}\bm{v}

(49)

is the local number density of particles of
\alpha
-species. By
multiplying Eq. (
45
) with
\psi\left(\bm{v}\right)

and averaging the entire equation, we obtain

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[n_{\alpha}\left\langle\psi\left(\bm{v}\right)\right\rangle_{\alpha}\right]+\nabla\cdot\left[n_{\alpha}\left\langle\psi\left(\bm{v}\right)\bm{v}\right\rangle_{\alpha}\right]-\frac{n_{\alpha}}{m_{\alpha}}\left\langle\bm{F}_{\alpha}\cdot\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\bm{v}}\right\rangle_{\alpha}=\int\psi\left(\bm{v}\right)\left(\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial t}\right)_{c}^{\pm}d^{2}\bm{v}.

(50)

The detailed derivation of Eq. (
50
) can
be found in Ref.
[
34
]
.

When
\psi\left(\bm{v}\right)=1
, the average

\left\langle\psi\left(\bm{v}\right)\right\rangle_{\alpha}=1,

(51)

which represents the zeroth moment of the velocity. In this case,
the integral on the right-hand-side of Eq. (
50
)
equals zero, i.e.,

\int\left(\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial t}\right)_{c}^{\pm}d^{2}\bm{v}=0.

(52)

This result can be easily derived using Eq. (
44
)
and indicates that collisions do not change the total number of particles.
Substituting Eqs. (
51
) and (
52
)
into Eq. (
50
), we obtain the continuity
equation

\frac{\partial n_{\alpha}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\left(n_{\alpha}\bm{V}_{\alpha}\right)=0.

(53)

where
\bm{V}_{\alpha}=\left\langle\bm{v}\right\rangle_{\alpha}

is the average velocity of the fluid element (or fluid velocity) of

\alpha
-species.

When
\psi\left(\bm{v}\right)=m_{\alpha}\bm{v}
,
the average value
\left\langle\psi\left(\bm{v}\right)\right\rangle_{\alpha}

becomes

\left\langle m_{\alpha}\bm{v}\right\rangle_{\alpha}=m_{\alpha}\bm{V}_{\alpha}

(54)

which represents the first moment of the velocity. In this case, the
integral on the right-hand side of Eq. (
50
)
represents the scattering force exerted by the background MDA, which
is given by

\int m_{\alpha}\bm{v}\left(\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial t}\right)_{c}^{\pm}d^{2}\bm{v}=\int m_{\alpha}\bm{w}_{\alpha}\left(\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial t}\right)_{c}^{\pm}d^{2}\bm{v}\equiv\bm{R}_{\alpha}^{\pm}\left(t,\bm{r}\right),

(55)

where
\bm{w}_{\alpha}=\bm{v}-\bm{V}_{\alpha}

is the perturbation from the average velocity (
\left\langle\bm{w}_{\alpha}\right\rangle=0
).
Here,
\bm{R}_{\alpha}^{\pm}\left(t,\bm{r}\right)

represents the momentum transferred from the background MDA to the

\alpha
-species particles per unit time, or equivalently, the scattering
force exerted by the MDA. In deriving Eq. (
55
),
we used the relation

\int m_{\alpha}\bm{V}_{\alpha}\left(\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial t}\right)_{c}^{\pm}d^{2}\bm{v}=\bm{V}_{\alpha}\left[\int m_{\alpha}\left(\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial t}\right)_{c}^{\pm}d^{2}\bm{v}\right]=0.

(56)

By directly integrating the left-hand side of Eq. (
55
),
it is straightforward to verify, using Eq. (
44
),
that the scattering force vanishes in the absence of macroscopic flow,
i.e.,

\bm{R}_{\alpha}^{\pm}\left(t,\bm{r}\right)=0,\text{ when }\left\langle\bm{v}\right\rangle_{\alpha}=\bm{V}_{\alpha}=0.

(57)

Substituting Eqs. (
54
) and (
55
)
into Eq. (
50
), we can obtain the momentum
equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(m_{\alpha}n_{\alpha}\bm{V}_{\alpha}\right)+\nabla\cdot\left(m_{\alpha}n_{\alpha}\bm{V}_{\alpha}\bm{V}_{\alpha}\right)=-\nabla\cdot\bm{p}_{\alpha}+\bm{R}_{\alpha}^{\pm}+n_{\alpha}\bm{F}_{\alpha},

(58)

where
\bm{p}_{\alpha}\equiv m_{\alpha}n_{\alpha}\left\langle\bm{w}_{\alpha}\bm{w}_{\alpha}\right\rangle

is the stress tensor and
\bm{F}_{\alpha}=\bm{F}_{\alpha}\left(t,\bm{r},\bm{V}_{\alpha}\right)
.

Let
\psi\left(\bm{v}\right)=m_{\alpha}\bm{v}^{2}/2
,
the average value
\left\langle\psi\left(\bm{v}\right)\right\rangle_{\alpha}

becomes

\left\langle\frac{1}{2}m_{\alpha}\bm{v}^{2}\right\rangle_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}m_{\alpha}\bm{V}_{\alpha}^{2}+\frac{U_{\alpha}}{n_{\alpha}},

(59)

which represents the second moment of the velocity. Here,
U_{\alpha}=m_{\alpha}n_{\alpha}\left\langle\bm{w}_{\alpha}^{2}\right\rangle
/2
is the internal energy density. Since collisions do not change the
energy, the integral on the right-hand side of Eq. (
50
)
equals zero, i.e.,

\int\frac{1}{2}m_{\alpha}\bm{v}^{2}\left(\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial t}\right)_{c}^{\pm}d^{2}\bm{v}=0.

(60)

This result, which can also be derived directly from Eq. (
44
),
implies that

\bm{R}_{\alpha}^{\pm}\cdot\bm{V}_{\alpha}=0,

(61)

meaning that the macroscopic force
\bm{R}_{\alpha}^{\pm}

does no work on the fluid element. The detailed proof is provided
in Appendix.
A
. Substituting Eqs. (
59
)
and (
60
) into Eq. (
50
),
we can obtain the energy equation

\frac{\partial U_{\alpha}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\left(U_{\alpha}\bm{V}_{\alpha}\right)=-\bm{P}_{\alpha}:\nabla\bm{V}_{\alpha}+\nabla\cdot\bm{q}_{\alpha},

(62)

where

\bm{q}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}m_{\alpha}n_{\alpha}\left\langle\bm{w}_{\alpha}^{2}\bm{w}_{\alpha}\right\rangle_{\alpha}

(63)

is the heat flux density. The technique used to derive Eq. (
62
)
can be found in Refs.
[
31
]
and
[
34
]
.
A detailed derivation is provided in Appendix.
A
.

IV
linear solution of Boltzmann equation

IV.1
Collision integrals via Fourier decomposition

We begin with Eqs. (
44
) and (
45
).
Let

v^{\pm}=\frac{\gamma_{\alpha}-s_{\alpha}e^{i\varphi}}{\gamma_{\alpha}-s_{\alpha}e^{-i\varphi}}v.

(64)

From Eqs. (
11
) and (
35
),
we have
\left|v^{\pm}\right|=\left|v\right|
. Therefore, we can
rewrite
v
and
v^{\pm}
as

\displaystyle v=\left|v\right|e^{i\xi},\quad v^{\pm}=\left|v\right|e^{i\xi^{\pm}},

(65)

\displaystyle e^{i\xi^{\pm}}=\frac{\gamma_{\alpha}-s_{\alpha}e^{i\varphi}}{\gamma_{\alpha}-s_{\alpha}e^{-i\varphi}}e^{i\xi}.

(66)

By rewriting the distribution function
f_{\alpha}\left(t,r,v\right)

in terms of
|v|
and
\xi
,
f_{\alpha}\left(t,r,v\right)
becomes
a periodic function of
\xi
, i.e.,

f_{\alpha}\left(t,r,v\right)=f_{\alpha}\left(t,r,\left|v\right|,\xi\right)=f_{\alpha}\left(t,r,\left|v\right|,\xi+2\pi\right).

(67)

Hence, we can decompose
f_{\alpha}\left(t,r,v\right)
with respect
to
\xi
into a Fourier series

f_{\alpha}\left(t,r,v\right)=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty}f_{\alpha m}\left(t,r,\left|v\right|\right)e^{im\xi}.

(68)

Thus,
f_{\alpha}\left(t,r,v^{\pm}\right)
becomes

f_{\alpha}\left(t,r,v^{\pm}\right)=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty}f_{\alpha m}\left(t,r,\left|v\right|\right)e^{i\xi^{\pm}}=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty}f_{\alpha m}\left(t,r,\left|v\right|\right)\left(\frac{\gamma_{\alpha}-s_{\alpha}e^{i\varphi}}{\gamma_{\alpha}-s_{\alpha}e^{-i\varphi}}\right)^{m}e^{im\xi^{\pm}},

(69)

where we used Eq. (
66
). Substituting Eq. (
68
)
and Eq. (
69
) into Eq. (
44
),
the collision integral becomes

\text{$\left(\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial t}\right)$}_{c}^{\pm}=-n_{D}(t,r)\left|v\right|\left|r_{D}\right|\sum_{m\neq 0}K_{m}^{\pm}\left(\gamma_{\alpha}\right)f_{\alpha m}\left(t,r,\left|v\right|\right)e^{im\xi},

(70)

where the function
K_{m}^{\pm}
is defined as

K_{m}^{\pm}\left(\gamma_{\alpha}\right)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\pi}\left[1-\left(\frac{\gamma_{\alpha}-s_{\alpha}e^{i\varphi}}{\gamma_{\alpha}-s_{\alpha}e^{-i\varphi}}\right)^{m}\right]\mathrm{sin\varphi}d\varphi.

(71)

When
m=0
, it follows that

K_{0}^{\pm}\left(\gamma_{\alpha}\right)\equiv 0,\quad\forall\gamma_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R},

(72)

and
\forall m\in\mathbb{Z}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left[K_{m}^{\pm}\left(\gamma_{\alpha}\right)\right]^{*}

\displaystyle=K_{-m}^{\pm}\left(\gamma_{\alpha}\right),

(73)

\displaystyle\left[K_{m}^{+}\left(\gamma_{\alpha}\right)\right]^{*}

\displaystyle=K_{m}^{-}\left(\gamma_{\alpha}\right).

(74)

The derivation of Eqs. (
73
) and (
74
)
is provided in the Appendix.
B
.

IV.2
Calculation of the linear distribution function

Assume that the distribution function can be expressed as

f_{\alpha}=f_{\alpha 0}+\delta f_{\alpha}

(75)

where
f_{\alpha 0}
is isotropic with respect to velocity and represents
the local equilibrium function, i.e.,
f_{\alpha 0}=f_{\alpha 0}\left(r,\left|v\right|\right)
,
and
\delta f_{\alpha}\ll f_{\alpha 0}
represents a small deviation
from
f_{\alpha 0}
. Substituting Eq. (
75
)
into Eq. (
45
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\delta f_{\alpha}}{\partial t}+\left(\bm{v}\cdot\nabla f_{\alpha 0}+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla\delta f_{\alpha}\right)+\left(\frac{\bm{F}_{\alpha}}{m_{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{\alpha 0}}{\partial\bm{v}}+\frac{\bm{F}_{\alpha}}{m_{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{\partial\delta f_{\alpha}}{\partial\bm{v}}\right)

\displaystyle=n_{D}(t,r)\left|v\right|\left|r_{D}\right|\int_{0}^{\pi}\left[\delta f_{\alpha}\left(t,r,v^{\pm}\right)-\delta f_{\alpha}\left(t,r,v\right)\right]\mathrm{sin\varphi}d\varphi,

(76)

where we used the isotropic condition of
f_{\alpha 0}
, which causes
the integral involving
f_{\alpha 0}
to vanish, i.e.,

\int_{0}^{\pi}\left[f_{\alpha 0}\left(r,\left|v^{\pm}\right|\right)-f_{\alpha 0}\left(r,\left|v\right|\right)\right]\mathrm{sin\varphi}d\varphi=0.

(77)

Given that
\bm{v}\cdot\nabla f_{\alpha 0}\gg\bm{v}\cdot\nabla\delta f_{\alpha}

and
\left(\bm{F}_{\alpha}/m_{\alpha}\right)\cdot\left(\partial f_{0\alpha}/\partial\bm{v}\right)\gg\left(\bm{F}_{\alpha}/m_{\alpha}\right)\cdot\left(\partial\delta f_{\alpha}/\partial\bm{v}\right)
,
equation (
76
) can be simplified by neglecting
the higher-order terms

\frac{\partial\delta f_{\alpha}}{\partial t}+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla f_{0\alpha}+\frac{\bm{F}_{\alpha}}{m_{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{0\alpha}}{\partial\bm{v}}=n_{D}(t,r)\left|v\right|\left|r_{D}\right|\int_{0}^{\pi}\left[\delta f_{\alpha}\left(t,r,v^{\pm}\right)-\delta f_{\alpha}\left(t,r,v\right)\right]\mathrm{sin\varphi}d\varphi.

(78)

We now decompose
\delta f\left(t,r,v\right)
into a Fourier series

\delta f_{\alpha}\left(t,r,v\right)=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty}\delta f_{\alpha m}\left(t,r,\left|v\right|\right)e^{im\xi}.

(79)

Assuming that
\delta f\left(t,r,v\right)\propto e^{-i\omega t}\delta f\left(r,v\right)
,
we obtain

\frac{\partial\delta f_{\alpha}}{\partial t}=-i\omega\sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty}\delta f_{\alpha m}\left(t,r,\left|v\right|\right)e^{im\xi}.

(80)

Substituting Eqs. (
79
) and (
80
)
into Eq. (
78
), we get

\displaystyle-i\omega\sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty}\delta f_{\alpha m}\left(t,r,\left|v\right|\right)e^{im\xi}+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla f_{\alpha 0}+\frac{\bm{F}_{\alpha}}{m_{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{\alpha 0}}{\partial\bm{v}}

\displaystyle=-n_{D}(t,r)\left|v\right|\left|r_{D}\right|\sum_{m\neq 0}K_{m}^{\pm}\left(\gamma_{\alpha}\right)\delta f_{\alpha m}\left(t,r,\left|v\right|\right)e^{im\xi},

(81)

which can be equivalently rewritten as

\bm{v}\cdot\nabla f_{\alpha 0}+\frac{\bm{F}_{\alpha}}{m_{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{0\alpha}}{\partial\bm{v}}=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty}-\left[\nu_{\alpha m}^{\pm}\left(t,r,\left|v\right|\right)-i\omega\right]\delta f_{\alpha m}\left(t,r,\left|v\right|\right)e^{im\xi},

(82)

where

\nu_{\alpha m}^{\pm}\left(t,r,\left|v\right|\right)=n_{D}(t,r)\left|v\right||r_{D}|K_{m}^{\pm}\left(\gamma_{\alpha}\right).

(83)

The Fourier coefficient
\delta f_{\alpha m}
of
\delta f_{\alpha}

can be determined by calculating the Fourier coefficients of Eq. (
82
)

-2\pi\left[\nu_{\alpha m}^{\pm}\left(t,r,\left|v\right|\right)-i\omega\right]\delta f_{\alpha m}=\left(e^{im\xi},\bm{v}\cdot\nabla f_{\alpha 0}+\frac{\bm{F}_{\alpha}}{m_{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{0\alpha}}{\partial\bm{v}}\right).

(84)

Here, we have used the inner product notation
\left(\>,\>\right)
,
defined as

\left(h\left(\xi\right),g\left(\xi\right)\right):=\int_{0}^{2\pi}h^{*}\left(\xi\right)g\left(\xi\right)d\xi,

(85)

where
h\left(\xi\right)
and
g\left(\xi\right)
are complex functions
of
\xi
. We also used the fact that

\left(e^{im\xi},e^{in\xi}\right)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{-im\xi}e^{in\xi}d\xi=2\pi\delta_{mn},

(86)

where
\delta_{mn}
is Kronecker delta, which equals
1
if 1 if

m=n
and
0
otherwise. The right-hand side of Eq. (
84
)
can be transformed into

\displaystyle\left(e^{im\xi},\bm{v}\cdot\nabla f_{\alpha 0}+\frac{\bm{F}_{\alpha}}{m_{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{\partial f_{0\alpha}}{\partial\bm{v}}\right)=\left(e^{im\xi},\left|\bm{v}\right|\bm{e}_{v}\cdot\nabla f_{\alpha 0}+\frac{\partial f_{\alpha 0}}{\partial\left|\bm{v}\right|}\bm{e}_{v}+\frac{1}{\left|\bm{v}\right|}\frac{\partial f_{\alpha 0}}{\partial\xi}\bm{e}_{\xi}\right)

\displaystyle=\left|\bm{v}\right|\nabla f_{\alpha 0}\cdot\left(e^{im\xi},\bm{e}_{v}\right)+\frac{\partial f_{\alpha 0}}{\partial\left|\bm{v}\right|}\frac{\bm{F}_{\alpha}}{m_{\alpha}}\cdot\left(e^{im\xi},\bm{e}_{v}\right)=\left[\left|\bm{v}\right|\nabla f_{\alpha 0}+\frac{\bm{F}_{\alpha}}{m_{\alpha}}\frac{\partial f_{\alpha 0}}{\partial\left|\bm{v}\right|}\right]\cdot\left(e^{im\xi},\bm{e}_{v}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\hat{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{\alpha}f_{\alpha 0}\right)\cdot\left(e^{im\xi},\bm{e}_{v}\right),

(87)

where the operator
\hat{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{\alpha}
is defined
as

\hat{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{\alpha}\equiv\left|\bm{v}\right|\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{r}}+\frac{\bm{F}_{\alpha}}{m_{\alpha}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\left|\bm{v}\right|}.

(88)

Here,
\bm{e}_{v}
and
\bm{e}_{\xi}
are unit vectors
in the radial and azimuthal directions, respectively, in the polar
coordinate system of velocity space, defined as

\displaystyle\bm{e}_{v}

\displaystyle=\bm{v}/\left|\bm{v}\right|=\mathrm{cos\xi}\bm{e}_{x}+\mathrm{sin}\xi\bm{e}_{y},

(89)

\displaystyle\bm{e}_{\xi}

(90)

In deriving Eq. (

0,&m\neq\pm 1.\end{cases}

V
typical transport coefficients

We start with Eq. (
100
) to calculate the transport
coefficient, applying the vector form of all functions in the following
context. Assuming that the local equilibrium is described by a mono-kinetic
distribution, we have

f_{\alpha 0}\left(\bm{r},\left|\bm{v}\right|\right)=\frac{n_{\alpha 0}\left(\bm{r}\right)}{2\pi\left|\bm{v}\right|}\delta\left(\left|\bm{v}\right|-u_{\alpha}\left(\bm{r}\right)\right)=\frac{n_{\alpha 0}\left(\bm{r}\right)}{2\pi u_{\alpha}\left(\bm{r}\right)}\delta\left(\left|\bm{v}\right|-u_{\alpha}\left(\bm{r}\right)\right),

(103)

indicating that all particles have the same speed
u\left(\bm{r}\right)

at position
\bm{r}
, but with different directions.. It
is straightforward to verify that
\int f_{\alpha 0}\left(\bm{r},\left|\bm{v}\right|\right)d^{2}\bm{v}=n_{\alpha 0}\left(\bm{r}\right)
.

V.1
Particle transport and diffusion tensor

Suppose the external force field
\bm{F}_{\alpha}=0
, and
the particle speed is constant, i.e.,
u_{\alpha}\left(\bm{r}\right)=u_{\alpha}
,
but the particle density depends on
\bm{r}
. Under these
conditions, the operator simplifies to
\hat{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}=\left|\bm{v}\right|\partial/\partial\bm{r}
.
Using Eq. (
100
), the perturbed distribution
function
\delta f_{\alpha}^{\pm}
is then given by

\delta f_{\alpha}^{\pm}=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\delta\left(\left|\bm{v}\right|-u_{\alpha}\right)\bm{e}_{v}\cdot\bm{M}_{\alpha}^{\pm}\cdot\nabla n_{\alpha 0}\left(\bm{r}\right).

(104)

The particle flux of the
\alpha
-species is calculated as

\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}_{\alpha}^{\pm}\left(\bm{r}\right)

\displaystyle=n_{\alpha}\left(\bm{r}\right)\bm{V}_{\alpha}=\int f\bm{v}d^{2}\bm{v}

\displaystyle=\int\left(f_{0\alpha}+\delta f_{\alpha}^{\pm}\right)\bm{v}d^{2}\bm{v}=\int\delta f_{\alpha}^{\pm}\left|\bm{v}\right|\bm{e}_{v}d\bm{v}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\int\left|\bm{v}\right|\delta\left(\left|\bm{v}\right|-u_{\alpha}\right)\bm{e}_{v}\bm{e}_{v}\cdot\bm{M}_{\alpha}^{\pm}\left(\bm{r},\left|\bm{v}\right|\right)d^{2}\bm{v}\right]\cdot\nabla n_{\alpha 0}\left(\bm{r}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\left\{\left[\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bm{e}_{v}\bm{e}_{v}d\xi\right]\cdot\left[\int_{0}^{+\infty}\bm{M}_{\alpha}^{\pm}\left(\bm{r},\left|\bm{v}\right|\right)\left|\bm{v}\right|^{2}\delta\left(\left|\bm{v}\right|-u_{\alpha}\right)d\left|\bm{v}\right|\right]\right\}\cdot\nabla n_{\alpha 0}\left(\bm{r}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{u^{2}}{2}\bm{M}_{\alpha}^{\pm}\left(\bm{r},u_{\alpha}\right)\cdot\nabla n_{\alpha 0}\left(\bm{r}\right),

(105)

where we used

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bm{e}_{v}\bm{e}_{v}d\xi=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\mathrm{cos\xi}\bm{e}_{x}+\mathrm{sin}\xi\bm{e}_{y}\right)\left(\mathrm{cos\xi}\bm{e}_{x}+\mathrm{sin}\xi\bm{e}_{y}\right)d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left[\mathrm{cos^{2}\xi}\bm{e}_{x}\bm{e}_{x}+\mathrm{cos\xi}\mathrm{sin}\xi\left(\bm{e}_{x}\bm{e}_{y}+\bm{e}_{y}\bm{e}_{x}\right)\mathrm{sin}^{2}\xi\bm{e}_{y}\bm{e}_{y}\right]d\xi

\displaystyle=\pi\left(\bm{e}_{x}\bm{e}_{x}+\bm{e}_{y}\bm{e}_{y}\right)=\pi\bm{I},

(106)

and
\bm{I}
is the 2D identity tensor.

According to the equipartition theorem of energy
T_{\alpha}/2=\left\langle\frac{1}{2}m_{\alpha}v_{x}^{2}\right\rangle_{\alpha}=\left\langle\frac{1}{2}m_{\alpha}v_{y}^{2}\right\rangle_{\alpha}
,
where
T_{\alpha}
is the temperature of the
\alpha
-species. We
have set the Boltzmann constant to 1, i.e.,
k_{B}=1
, yielding

\frac{u_{\alpha}^{2}}{2}=\left\langle\frac{1}{2}\left(v_{x}^{2}+v_{y}^{2}\right)\right\rangle_{\alpha}=\left\langle\frac{\left|\bm{v}\right|^{2}}{2}\right\rangle_{\alpha}=\frac{T_{\alpha}}{m_{\alpha}}.

(107)

Substituting Eq. (
107
) into Eq. (
105
),
we finally obtain

\bm{\Gamma}_{\alpha}^{\pm}\left(\bm{r}\right)=-\bm{D}_{\alpha}^{\pm}\left(\bm{r},u_{\alpha}\right)\cdot\nabla n_{\alpha 0}\left(\bm{r}\right),

(108)

where

\bm{D}_{\alpha}^{\pm}\left(\bm{r},u_{\alpha}\right)=\frac{T_{\alpha}}{m_{\alpha}}\bm{M}_{\alpha}^{\pm}\left(\bm{r},u_{\alpha}\right)

(109)

is the Hall (or odd) diffusion tensor. Its off-diagonal elements are
antisymmetric due to Eq. (
102
).

Since
\bm{M}_{\alpha}^{\pm}
is related to the direction
of the magnetic field within the magnetic region (see Eqs. (
71
),
(
83
), (
95
) and (
101
)),
when the magnetic field in the MDA changes direction, the gyrofrequency

\omega_{c\alpha}
will change its sign, and
\nu_{\alpha 1}^{\pm}\left(r,\left|v\right|\right)

will transform into their conjugates, i.e.,
\nu_{\alpha 1}^{\pm}\rightarrow\left(\nu_{\alpha 1}^{\pm}\right)^{*}

(or
\tau_{\alpha 2}\rightarrow-\tau_{\alpha 2}
). As a result, we
have

M_{\alpha ij}^{\pm}\left[\bm{B}\right]=M_{\alpha ji}^{\mp}\left[-\bm{B}\right].

(110)

Consequently, the diffusion tensor will satisfy the relation

D_{\alpha ij}^{\pm}\left[\bm{B}\right]=D_{\alpha ji}^{\mp}\left[-\bm{B}\right],

(111)

which is consistent with Onsager’s reciprocal relations for kinetic
coefficients.

Assume that
n_{\alpha 0}
varies slowly over time, ensuring that
each state is in a quasi-stationary condition, and Eq. (
108
)
continues to hold. Substituting Eq. (
108
)
into Eq. (
53
), we obtain the Hall diffusion
equation

\frac{\partial n_{\alpha 0}}{\partial t}-\nabla\cdot\left[\bm{D}_{\alpha}^{\pm}\left(\bm{r},u_{\alpha}\right)\cdot\nabla n_{\alpha 0}\right]=0,

(112)

where the perturbed density
\delta n_{\alpha}=n_{\alpha}-n_{\alpha 0}

is neglected. For stationary and uniform MDA (or
n_{D}
is constant),

\tau_{\alpha 1}
and
\tau_{\alpha 2}
is then independent of
\bm{r}
,
consequently, the diffusion tensor is a constant tensor. Equation
(
112
) is then reduced to

\frac{\partial n_{\alpha 0}}{\partial t}-\bm{D}_{\alpha}^{\pm}:\nabla\nabla n_{\alpha 0}=0,

(113)

Substituting Eqs. (
101
) and (
109
)
into Eq. (
113
), Eq. (
113
)
can be equivalently transformed into

\frac{\partial n_{\alpha 0}}{\partial t}-\frac{T_{\alpha}\tau_{\alpha 1}}{m_{\alpha}}\nabla^{2}n_{\alpha 0}=0.

(114)

To simulate the diffusion properties of particles using Eq. (
113
),
it is convenient to nondimensionalize the problem using the following
substitutions:

\displaystyle\tilde{n}_{\alpha}=\left(\pi\left|r_{D}\right|^{2}\right)n_{\alpha},

(115)

\displaystyle\tilde{t}=\left|\omega_{c\alpha}\right|t,\;\tilde{\bm{r}}=\frac{\bm{r}}{\left|r_{D}\right|}.

(116)

Substituting these nondimensional quantities into Eq. (
113
),
we have

\frac{\partial\tilde{n}_{\alpha 0}}{\partial\tilde{t}}-\tilde{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{\pm}:\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{\nabla}\tilde{n}_{\alpha 0}=0,

(117)

where
\tilde{\nabla}\equiv\partial/\partial\tilde{\bm{x}}

and

\tilde{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{\pm}=\frac{\bm{D}_{\alpha}^{\pm}}{\left|\omega_{c\alpha}\right|\left|r_{D}\right|^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{\alpha}\left|\omega_{c\alpha}\right|\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\tau_{\alpha 1}&\mp\tau_{\alpha 2}\\
\pm\tau_{\alpha 2}&\tau_{\alpha 1}\end{array}\right)

(118)

nondimensionalized diffusion tensor. In this study, we simulate the
behavior of diffusion equations using the COMSOL Multiphysics software.
We examine three distinct types of diffusion equations, each corresponding
to a different diffusion tensor
\tilde{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{-}
,

\tilde{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{+}
and
\tilde{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{0}
,
the simulation results are shown in Fig.
3
(a-c),
(d-f), and (g-i). Here,
\tilde{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{0}
denotes
the normal diffusion tensor, which is obtained by setting
\tau_{\alpha 2}=0

in
\tilde{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{\pm}
. This results in

\tilde{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{0}=\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{\alpha}\left|\omega_{c\alpha}\right|\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\tau_{\alpha 1}&0\\
0&\tau_{\alpha 1}\end{array}\right).

(119)

The simulations are performed within a
1\times 2
rectangular domain.
We set the parameters as
\tau_{\alpha 1}=2/\gamma_{\alpha}\left|\omega_{c\alpha}\right|

and
\tau_{\alpha 2}=\pi/\left(2\gamma_{\alpha}\left|\omega_{c\alpha}\right|\right)
.
Under these conditions, the diffusion tensors are defined as

\tilde{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{+}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&-\frac{\pi}{4}\\
\frac{\pi}{4}&1\end{array}\right),\>\tilde{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{-}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\frac{\pi}{4}\\
-\frac{\pi}{4}&1\end{array}\right),\>\tilde{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{0}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right).

(120)

The boundary conditions are specified as follows: at the top and bottom
boundaries, a flux condition is imposed with
\bm{\Gamma}_{\alpha}^{\pm}\text{ and }\bm{\Gamma}_{\alpha}^{0}=0
,
ensuring that there is no net particle flux across these boundaries,
where
\bm{\Gamma}_{\alpha}^{0}=-\tilde{\bm{D}}_{\alpha}^{0}\cdot\nabla n_{\alpha 0}
.
Dirichlet boundary conditions are applied at the left and right boundaries
to fix the particle density: at the left boundary
n\left(t,x=0,y\right)=n_{0}

with
n_{0}=10
, and at the right boundary
n\left(t,x=2,y\right)=0
.
The initial particle density is given by a Gaussian distribution
n\left(t=0,x,y\right)=n_{0}e^{-x^{2}/L^{2}}
,
where
L=0.08
.

in the other, the diffusion

V.2
Electric transport and the Hall conductivity tensor

Suppose the system is under a uniform electric field
\bm{E}=E_{x}\bm{e}_{x}+E_{y}\bm{e}_{y}
,
and the force field acting on
\alpha
-species is thus given by
\bm{F}_{\alpha}=q_{\alpha}\bm{E}

Assuming the system comprises two species of particles, electrons

which is represented
u_{\alpha}\left(\bm{r}\right)=u_{\alpha}
.
Given that electrons move much faster than ions due to their lighter
mass, we assume the ions are stationary, i.e.,
u_{i}=0

subscript

refers to ions. The particle density is uniform throughout

n_{\alpha 0}\left(\bm{r}\right)=n_{\alpha 0}
.

\sum_{\alpha}q_{\alpha}n_{\alpha 0}=-en_{e0}+q_{i}n_{i0}=0.

(121)

\delta f_{\alpha}^{\pm}

\displaystyle\delta f_{e}^{\pm}

\displaystyle\delta f_{i}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=0.

(123)

\displaystyle=-\frac{e^{2}n_{e0}}{2\pi m_{e}u_{e}}\int\frac{\partial\delta\left(\left|\bm{v}\right|-u_{e}\right)}{\partial\left|\bm{v}\right|}\left|\bm{v}\right|\bm{e}_{v}\bm{e}_{v}\cdot\bm{M}_{e}^{\pm}\left(\bm{r},\left|\bm{v}\right|\right)d^{2}\bm{v}\cdot\bm{E}

\displaystyle=-\frac{e^{2}n_{e0}}{2\pi m_{e}u_{e}}\left[\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bm{e}_{v}\bm{e}_{v}d\xi\right]\cdot\left[\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\partial\delta\left(\left|\bm{v}\right|-u_{e}\right)}{\partial\left|\bm{v}\right|}\bm{M}_{\alpha}^{\pm}\left(\bm{r},\left|\bm{v}\right|\right)\left|\bm{v}\right|^{2}d\left|\bm{v}\right|\right]\cdot\bm{E}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}n_{e0}}{2\pi m_{e}u_{e}}\left[\pi\bm{I}\right]\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\left|\bm{v}\right|}\mid_{\left|\bm{v}\right|=u_{e}}\left[\bm{M}_{\alpha}^{\pm}\left(\bm{r},\left|\bm{v}\right|\right)\left|\bm{v}\right|^{2}\right]\cdot\bm{E}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}n_{e0}}{2m_{e}u_{e}}\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{e}}\left[u_{e}^{2}\bm{M}_{\alpha}^{\pm}\left(\bm{r},u_{e}\right)\right]\cdot\bm{E},

(124)

where we used the following equation

f\left(x\right)\delta^{\prime}\left(x\right)=-f^{\prime}\left(0\right)\delta\left(x\right)+f\left(0\right)\delta^{\prime}\left(x\right).

(125)

Equation (
124
) can be rewritten as

\bm{J}^{\pm}=\bm{\sigma}_{e}^{\pm}\cdot\bm{E},

(126)

where

\bm{\sigma}_{e}^{\pm}=\frac{e^{2}n_{e0}}{2m_{e}u_{e}}\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{e}}\left[u_{e}^{2}\bm{M}_{\alpha}^{\pm}\left(\bm{r},u_{e}\right)\right]

(127)

is the Hall conductivity tensor of electrons. From Eq. (
127
),
we can see that

\sigma_{eij}^{\pm}=-\sigma_{eji}^{\pm},\text{ for }i\neq j.

(128)

V.3
Heat transport

As discussed in Subsec.
V.2
, we consider
only two species in our system: electrons and ions. The ions are stationary
(
\bm{v}_{i}=0
, or in other words, they are cold ions).
The electron microscopic speed
u_{e}\left(\bm{r}\right)

depends on the position
\bm{r}
, which implies that the
electron temperature and particle density is not uniform at equilibrium.
Assuming there is no equilibrium flow and no external force field,
we have
\bm{V}_{\alpha}=0
and
\bm{F}_{\alpha}=0
.
Therefore, the operator becomes
\hat{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}=\left|\bm{v}\right|\partial/\partial\bm{r}
.
The perturbed distribution function
\delta f_{e}^{\pm}
is then
calculated as

\delta f_{e}^{\pm}\left(\bm{r},\bm{v}\right)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\bm{e}_{v}\cdot\bm{M}_{e}^{\pm}\left(\bm{r},\left|\bm{v}\right|\right)\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{r}}\left[n_{e0}\left(\bm{r}\right)\delta\left(\left|\bm{v}\right|-u_{e}\left(\bm{r}\right)\right)\right].

(129)

The electron stress tensor at equilibrium is calculated as follows

\displaystyle\bm{p}_{e0}

\displaystyle\equiv m_{e}n_{e0}\left(\bm{r}\right)\left\langle\bm{w}_{e}\bm{w}_{e}\right\rangle_{e}=m_{e}n_{e}\left(\bm{r}\right)\left\langle\bm{v}\bm{v}\right\rangle_{e}

\displaystyle=m_{e}n_{e0}\left(\bm{r}\right)\left\langle v_{x}^{2}\bm{e}_{x}\bm{e}_{x}+v_{x}v_{y}\left(\bm{e}_{x}\bm{e}_{y}+\bm{e}_{y}\bm{e}_{x}\right)+v_{y}^{2}\bm{e}_{y}\bm{e}_{y}\right\rangle_{e}

\displaystyle=n_{e0}\left(\bm{r}\right)\left\langle\frac{1}{2}m_{e}\left|\bm{v}\right|^{2}\right\rangle_{e}\bm{I}=p_{e0}\left(\bm{r}\right)\bm{I},

(130)

where

p_{e0}\left(\bm{r}\right)=n_{e0}\left(\bm{r}\right)\left\langle\frac{1}{2}m_{e}\left|\bm{v}\right|^{2}\right\rangle_{e}=n_{e0}\left(\bm{r}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}m_{e}u_{e}^{2}\left(\bm{r}\right)\right)

(131)

is the electron pressure. In deriving Eq. (
130

VI
Conclusions

In this work, we explored the concept of metafields, a class of materials
that utilize local magnetic fields as the fundamental repeating units,
offering advantages over traditional metamaterials. Unlike conventional
metamaterials, where the repeating elements consist of fixed atomic
or molecular structures, metafields provide real-time tunability through
the external control of electric currents. This dynamic adaptability
opens new possibilities for controlling transport phenomena and manipulating
particle behavior.

We focused on a specific metafield system: the magnetic disk array
(MDA), which consists of magnetic disks (MDs) generating uniform magnetic
fields perpendicular to their surfaces. By analyzing the transport
properties of charged particles in the MDA, we explored various Hall
transport phenomena, including Hall diffusivity, Hall conductivity,
and thermal Hall effects. These Hall effects arise due to the antisymmetric
nature of the transport tensors, where longitudinal gradients can
induce transverse fluxes.

Using a combination of complex variable formulation, perturbation
methods, and Fourier analysis, we derived the key transport coefficients
such as the Hall diffusion tensor, Hall conductivity tensor, and thermal
Hall conductivity tensor. These analytical methods provided efficient
and straightforward solutions for understanding particle behavior
in the metafield system. Additionally, through simulations, we demonstrated
the occurrence of a one-way preferential diffusion phenomenon at the
interface of two MDAs with opposing field directions, revealing the
system’s unique directional transport properties.

Overall, this study highlights the potential of metafields, particularly
MDAs, in advancing the design of tunable materials with precise control
over particle dynamics. The concept of metafields provides a novel
platform for further exploration in both theoretical and applied physics.
