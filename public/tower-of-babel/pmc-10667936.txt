An exceptional cause of dyspnea: The uncommon case report of the association between Chilaiditi syndrome and diaphragmatic eventration

Introduction

Chilaiditi's syndrome is characterized by the interposition of the small or large bowel between the inferior surface of the diaphragm and the liver. It was first described by Viennese radiologist Chilaiditi in 1910. Diaphragmatic eventration is the permanent elevation of one hemidiaphragm without a discontinuity. The occurrence of Chilaiditi's syndrome in association with diaphragmatic eventration is even rarer, with only a few cases reported in the literature.

This work has been reported in line with the SCARE 2020 criteria [1].

Case report

A 71-year-old male patient, a heavy smoker, with no medical historpresented to the emergency department with a history of abdominal pain, constipation, and breathlessness. The patient had previously undergone a colonoscopy for chronic constipation, which revealed no abnormalities.

There was no history of trauma, previous surgery, or relevant medical, surgical, or family history. The patient had no comorbidities. Physical examination revealed obliteration of the hepatic dullness and absence of breath sounds in the lower part of the right chest. The patient was breathless, with oxygen saturation falling to 92 %. Digital rectal examination revealed impacted stool. The onset of dyspnea occurred approximately 4 h prior to the consultation. Blood tests, including complete blood count, blood sugar, urea, and creatinine, were normal. A thoracoabdominal CT scan was performed to aid in the etiological diagnosis of dyspnea. The patient was managed with intravenous fluids and laxatives. Subsequent per-rectal enema dramatically improved the patient's condition after the passage of stool, relieving respiratory distress. The patient has been doing well and is regularly followed up, with no recurrence of symptoms observed in the last four months.

Discussion

We present a successful case of conservative management for the rare combination of Chilaiditi's syndrome and diaphragmatic eventration, thus avoiding surgical intervention. The primary highlight of our study lies in the prompt diagnosis and effective conservative approach, facilitating the patient's recovery without resorting to surgery. However, a notable limitation is the relatively short duration of follow-up.

Chilaiditi's sign or syndrome is a rare condition characterized by a radiological finding first described in 1910 by the Greek radiologist Demetrius Chilaiditi [2]. This radiological peculiarity involves the interposition of the colon between the liver and the right diaphragmatic dome [3].

The incidence of Chilaiditi's syndrome is more common in men over 60 years old than in women. It presents with clinical symptoms such as abdominal distension, pain, breathlessness, or anorexia, which are caused by the interposition of the intestine, typically a portion of the hepatic flexure of the colon, between the liver and the diaphragm. The asymptomatic condition is known as Chilaiditi's sign, and it is often diagnosed incidentally during abdominal or chest radiographs. In case of uncertainty, a CT scan can be useful to confirm the diagnosis. Predisposing factors may include liver ptosis, ascites, megacolon, abnormal colonic mobility, diaphragmatic thinning, and phrenic nerve injury [4,5].

The simultaneous occurrence of hepatodiaphragmatic colonic interposition and diaphragmatic eventration is extremely rare. It is challenging to determine whether this is a coincidental simultaneous occurrence or if one condition is secondary to the other. One possible explanation is that the compression of the diaphragm caused by the colonic interposition could be the causative factor [3].

The management of Chilaiditi syndrome typically involves a conservative approach, which includes intravenous fluid therapy, nasogastric bowel decompression, stool softeners, and/or enemas, along with a high fiber diet to alleviate constipation [6]. However, if the pain persists and signs of an acute abdomen develop, surgical intervention may be required.

In cases of persistent pain, refractory ileus, colonic volvulus, or bowel ischemia, surgical treatment such as colopexy or colectomy may be necessary [7]. The management approach for diaphragmatic eventration varies significantly. In most cases occurring in adulthood, conservative treatment is recommended unless there is severe dyspnea affecting normal activities, orthopnea, or gastrointestinal symptoms clearly associated with the elevated position of the diaphragm [8].

When encountering the presence of air under the diaphragmatic dome, it is crucial to consider the differential diagnosis, as the most dangerous condition to consider is the perforation of a hollow viscus or a subdiaphragmatic abscess [9]. Although Chilaiditi's sign is a rare differential diagnosis, in case of uncertainty, it is important to pursue further investigations, including a CT scan, to avoid unnecessary surgery.

Conclusion

The association between Chilaiditi syndrome and diaphragmatic eventration is exceedingly rare and demands clinician awareness to avoid overlooking this uncommon diagnosis. While typically manageable, unrecognized cases can lead to serious complications. Conservative treatment is generally preferred, with surgical intervention considered when necessary. Our case report provides valuable insights into this uncommon association and explores potential therapeutic approaches.
