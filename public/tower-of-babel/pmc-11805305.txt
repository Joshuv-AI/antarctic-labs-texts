Comparative Effectiveness of Modified Manual and Powered Toothbrushing Interventions in Individuals with Disabilities: A Randomized Controlled Trial Assessing Gingival Health
Authors: Abhishek Sinha, Sumit Kumar, Vinod Sargaiyan, Ruby H. Kottapalli, Dheeraj Sharma, Ankit Goyal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Individuals with disabilities face unique challenges in maintaining optimal oral health due to physical, cognitive, and sensory impairments. Gingivitis is a common oral health problem in this population, necessitating effective interventions tailored to their specific needs.Methods:Participants (n = 162) aged 18–65 years, diagnosed with gingivitis and varying disabilities, were recruited. They were randomly assigned to the modified manual toothbrush group or the powered toothbrush group. The interventions included comprehensive training programs that addressed the specific needs of each group. Gingival index and plaque index scores were recorded at baseline and at 4, 8, and 12 weeks. Patient-reported outcomes, including satisfaction, comfort, and ease of use, were assessed. Statistical analyses were performed, including t-tests, Chi-square tests, and subgroup analyses based on disability types.Results:Baseline characteristics did not demonstrate significant differences between groups. Over 12 weeks, both groups showed a significant reduction in gingival and plaque index scores (P < 0.001). The powered toothbrush group exhibited a slightly greater reduction in gingival inflammation compared to the manual toothbrush group (P = 0.03). Patient-reported results favored the powered toothbrush group in satisfaction (P = 0.02) and ease of use (P = 0.04). Comfort levels did not show significant differences (P = 0.11).Conclusions:This randomized controlled trial provides evidence supporting the effectiveness of modified manual and powered toothbrushing interventions in reducing gingival inflammation among people with disabilities diagnosed with gingivitis.

INTRODUCTION

Oral health is a cornerstone of overall well-being, having a significant impact on the quality of life of an individual.[1] However, people with disabilities often face unique challenges in maintaining optimal oral hygiene, leading to a greater prevalence of oral health issues such as gingivitis.[2] Gingivitis, characterized by inflammation of the gingival tissues, presents a significant health concern, particularly among individuals with disabilities who may face difficulties in executing adequate oral care practices.[23]

The challenges faced by people with disabilities to maintain good oral health are multifaceted. Physical disabilities can affect manual dexterity, making tasks such as brushing more difficult.[4] Cognitive impairments may influence the understanding of oral care instructions and the ability to follow the prescribed routines.[4] Furthermore, sensory impairments can further complicate the recognition of oral health issues and hinder the adoption of proper oral hygiene practices.[24]

Recognizing the unique oral health needs of people with disabilities, it becomes imperative to tailor interventions that address their diverse challenges. Traditional oral health interventions, such as manual toothbrushing, may need to be modified or supplemented with alternative approaches to ensure effectiveness in this population. One promising avenue is the use of powered toothbrushes, which have been shown to be effective in various populations.[5]

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A total of 162 disabled patients, aged 18–65 years, diagnosed with gingivitis, were recruited for the study, ensuring a representative sample by collaborating with various healthcare facilities specializing in disability care. Strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to maintain a clinically relevant and uniform study population, enhancing the evaluation of toothbrushing methods. Randomization was meticulously executed using a computer-generated sequence, with stratification based on the type and severity of disabilities to ensure balanced group allocation. Blinding was implemented for outcome assessors and data analysts to minimize bias.

Participants were divided into two groups: those using a modified manual toothbrush and those using a powered toothbrush. Each group received comprehensive training tailored to their abilities, with a focus on optimizing oral hygiene practices. The primary outcome was the reduction of gingival inflammation, assessed using gingival index scores at baseline and at 4, 8, and 12 weeks. Secondary outcomes included plaque accumulation, evaluated using plaque index scores, and patient-reported outcomes, which assessed satisfaction, comfort, and ease of use through structured interviews and questionnaires.

Data collection involved baseline and follow-up assessments at regular intervals, with statistical analysis performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24.0.

RESULTS

Demographic characteristics

The demographic characteristics of participants in the modified manual toothbrush and powered toothbrush groups were similar, with no statistically significant differences in age, gender, type, or severity of disability, ensuring a balanced study population.

Baseline gingival and plaque index scores

At baseline, the gingival index scores were comparable between the modified manual toothbrush group (1.8 ± 0.4) and the powered toothbrush group (1.7 ± 0.3), with no significant difference (P = 0.21). Similarly, baseline plaque index scores showed no significant difference between the groups (2.2 ± 0.5 vs. 2.1 ± 0.4, P = 0.39).

Gingival and plaque index scores over 12 weeks

Both groups experienced significant reductions in gingival inflammation and plaque accumulation over 12 weeks. The gingival index decreased from 1.8 ± 0.4 to 1.0 ± 0.1 in the modified manual toothbrush group and from 1.7 ± 0.3 to 0.8 ± 0.1 in the powered toothbrush group (both P < 0.001). Plaque index scores also dropped significantly in both groups (2.2 ± 0.5 to 1.2 ± 0.2 and 2.1 ± 0.4 to 1.0 ± 0.1, both P < 0.001). The differences between the groups were not statistically significant [Tables 1 and 2].

Both groups reported high levels of satisfaction, comfort, and ease of use at 12 weeks. DISCUSSION

Oral health is a critical aspect of overall well-being, particularly for individuals with disabilities who often face unique challenges in maintaining optimal oral hygiene.[12] This randomized controlled trial (RCT) evaluated the effectiveness of modified manual toothbrushing versus powered toothbrushing in individuals with disabilities diagnosed with gingivitis. The study’s rigorous design, including well-distributed demographic characteristics and meticulous outcome measures, enhances the validity of the findings. Baseline assessments showed no significant differences in gingival and plaque index scores between the groups, ensuring comparability at the start of the study.

Over the 12-week intervention, both groups demonstrated significant reductions in gingival inflammation and plaque accumulation, with no statistically significant differences between them. These results suggest that both modified manual and powered toothbrushing are equally effective in improving oral hygiene among individuals with disabilities. The high satisfaction levels, comfort, and ease of use reported by participants, particularly in the powered toothbrush group, emphasize the importance of considering patient preferences in oral health interventions.[34] While statistically significant differences were observed in satisfaction and ease of use, the clinical significance of these differences may be minimal.

The study’s strengths include a robust randomization process, stratification by disability type and severity, and dynamic monitoring over 12 weeks, contributing to the reliability of the findings. However, limitations such as the challenge of blinding participants and the study’s short-term focus should be considered. Future research should explore the long-term durability of these interventions to better understand their sustained impact on oral health.[45]

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this RCT contributes valuable information to the oral health management of people with disabilities, specifically those diagnosed with gingivitis. Both modified manual toothbrushing and powered toothbrushing interventions were effective in reducing gingival inflammation and plaque accumulation.
