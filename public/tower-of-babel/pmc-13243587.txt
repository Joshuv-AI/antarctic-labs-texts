Plasma proteome is altered in a piglet model of fetal growth restriction
Authors: Hannah Musco, Aven Lee, Kirat K Chand, Paul B Colditz, Julie A Wixey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFetal growth restriction (FGR) is associated with increased risk of mortality and morbidity and impacts 5–10% of pregnancies. The chronic hypoxic environment the FGR fetus is exposed to impacts normal development for multiple organs including the heart, brain and lungs. Investigating alterations in the blood plasma proteome would further our understanding of the pathways, networks and functions involved in these associated adverse outcomes. An established spontaneously occurring piglet model of FGR and ZenoTOF liquid-chromatography mass spectrometry (LCMS) was used to identify alterations in the plasma proteome when compared to normally grown (NG) piglets. Technological advancements, including improvements in sensitivity, allowed for twice the number of proteins to be quantified while halving the sample load required. This study identified 106 dysregulated proteins in FGR compared to NG piglet plasma samples. Alterations to multiple pathways and functions predicted interactions between apolipoproteins, T cell factors, Fetuin A and AKT. These alterations to protein levels were also associated with the protein profile of multiple disease-states such as cardiovascular, neurological, metabolic and gastrointestinal previously identified with long-term adverse outcomes in FGR. Moreover, alterations to immune and inflammatory responses were also observed in FGR plasma. By using LCMS to identify dysregulated proteins associated with disease states, this study provides an important step forward in understanding the underlying mechanisms of injury in FGR. Additionally, the identified pathways have potential for prognostication and therapeutic targeting, offering a foundation for future clinical applications in managing FGR-related diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48665-2.Keywords: Fetal growth restriction, Data-independent acquisition, SWATH, Quantitative proteomics, Non-depleted plasmaSubject terms: Developmental biology, Biomarkers

Introduction

Fetal growth restriction (FGR) impacts 5–10% of pregnancies with higher rates in low-income countries1,2. Diagnosis of FGR includes ultrasound estimated fetal weight below 10th percentile with other clinical factors such as umbilical artery Doppler flow assessment, placental dysfunction and neonatal features of malnutrition3–5. The causes of FGR are multifactorial including maternal, placental and fetal factors arising from genetic predisposition in the fetus or from the mother and environmental contributors such as infections or nutrition6,7. However, the most common cause is placental insufficiency where a lack of supply of oxygen and nutrients impacts the normal growth and development of the fetus. FGR has serious developmental impacts on multiple organs including the heart, brain, lungs and kidneys1. Alterations to cardiac morphology in FGR infants, including greater septal hypertrophy and more globular hearts, contribute to altered cardiac function including higher systolic and diastolic blood pressure8–10. Further, FGR has been associated with lifelong increased risk of cardiovascular disease, diabetes and stroke11. FGR has also been associated with neurodevelopmental delay with adverse long-term neurological outcomes reported in 24–53% of FGR infants12,13, including language delays, learning and behavioral problems and cerebral palsy (CP)14–16. The chronic hypoxic environment also impacts brain development with lower grey and white matter complexity being major pathophysiological features of FGR17. Currently, there are no interventions available to prevent these adverse outcomes. Unfortunately, up to 50% of FGR cases go undiagnosed until around the time of, or at birth and this diagnosis does not indicate the level of risk of adverse outcomes for an individual infant18.

Investigating proteome alterations in FGR improves our understanding in two main ways. Firstly, it furthers our understanding of potential mechanisms involved in FGR and its associated adverse outcomes. Secondly, it will provide direction for future studies aimed at identifying potential blood biomarkers to indicate risk of these adverse long-term outcomes. The clinical need for predictors of risk for adverse outcomes in FGR infants is evidenced by recent studies aimed at identifying potential blood biomarkers. Studies investigating blood biomarkers for FGR have largely utilized hypothesis-based approaches to investigate specific proteins of interest19–22. Clinical studies have identified protein alterations however a high degree of variability in findings is observed, likely due to a lack of consistency in gestational age, sample types and sampling time points taken19–21,23. During the early perinatal time period, it is likely that the proteome is dynamic and changing, reflecting the critical developmental changes occurring during this time. Therefore, further investigation of the neonatal proteome would assist in identifying normal developmental proteome to compare with FGR proteome alteration that may indicate risk of adverse long-term outcomes.

For other neonatal conditions, such as Necrotizing Enterocolitis (NEC), there is a move towards broader proteomics screening approaches24–26. Recent advances in technology have enabled broader screening for many sample types, such as blood and stool samples24,25. Using mass spectrometry, these studies identified altered protein profiles in blood, stool and serum, demonstrating the utility of this approach across multiple sample types24,25. Further, using broader screening approaches allows for investigation of potential pathway and network alterations involved in FGR and its associated outcomes. Given this potential of broad proteomic screens and the lack of consistent clinical studies, we examined the plasma proteome in a pre-clinical model of FGR. Here, we used liquid-chromatography mass spectrometry (LCMS) to identify alterations in the plasma proteome of an established spontaneously occurring piglet model of FGR compared to normally grown piglets < 18 h after birth.

Differentially expressed proteins in FGR plasma

A total of 411 proteins were identified across all plasma samples with a false discovery rate (FDR) of < 1%. Of the identified proteins, 106 were found to be differentially expressed in FGR compared to NG piglets (p < 0.05) (Supplementary table S1). Of these, 62 were decreased in abundance and 44 were increased in FGR animals. The most highly modulated proteins (expression changes by 3-folds or more) were: Cadherin 11, L-lactate dehydrogenase, Alpha-mannosidase, Biliverdin reductase B, Inducible T cell costimulator ligand, Protein HP-20 homolog, Nectin cell adhesion molecule 2, Hemoglobin subunit alpha and Apolipoprotein D (APOD) (Table 2). We also observed significant increases in expression levels of Apolipoprotein F and A-I, indicating alterations impacting the Apolipoprotein family in the same direction (Table 2).

Functional characterization of dysregulated proteins using ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA)

Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) was used to interpret the differentially expressed proteins in terms of predominant canonical pathways, and identifications of mechanistic networks and functions (QIAGEN Inc., https://www.qiagenbioinformatics.com/products/ingenuity-pathway-analysis). Previous studies have associated observed morphological changes with proteome alterations32–35. IPA uses the current literature to develop a database of protein interactions and functional pathways. This database assists in visualizing protein interactions and identifies potential pathway alterations based on the differentially expressed proteins. IPA also identifies diseases and functions that are associated with these differentially expressed proteins based on the current literature. Using this analysis, the top ranked diseases and functions associated with FGR were cardiovascular disease, neurological disease, metabolic disease as well as gastrointestinal disease. These adverse outcomes have previously been linked with FGR (Fig. 2)1,12,13,36–38. IPA also revealed several top ranked molecular and cellular functions that were significantly impacted in FGR (Supplementary table S2), including cellular function and maintenance, cell-to-cell signalling and interaction and protein synthesis.

Alterations to immune and inflammatory responses have also been associated with FGR during the early post-natal period.

The top canonical pathways that were significantly altered in FGR were LXR/RXR activation, DHCR24 (24-dehydrocholesterol reductase) signalling, acute phase response signalling, and regulation of IGF transport and uptake by insulin-like growth factor-binding proteins (IGFBPs) (Fig. 3). In addition, pathways associated with the innate immune system (especially the compliment cascade) and the coagulation/clotting system were found to be significantly altered in FGR further reflecting alterations to immune and inflammatory responses observed in FGR infants19,20,22.

Protein-protein interaction networks

IPA identified alterations to protein interaction networks in FGR compared to NG piglet plasma. The top network based on the high percentage of molecules detected in our samples is shown in Fig. 4. This network of 36 molecules included 20 of our statistically significantly altered proteins which can be broken into three key interconnecting nodes. Macrophage stimulating 1 (MST1), membrane attack complex (MAC), Kininogen 1 (KNG1) and Alpha-2 Heremans Schmid Glycoprotein (AHSG or Fetuin A) are all predicted to inhibit Protein kinase B (AKT). Fetuin A and KNG1 link via FAM20C substrates to Inter-alpha trypsin inhibitors (ITI, ITIH1, 2, 3 and 4). These were all upregulated in FGR compared to NG. Apolipoproteins formed the third key node in this network with T cell factors 1, 3 and 4 (TCF1/3/4) bridging this node to Fetuin A. Apolipoproteins, APOM, APOD and APOH were all upregulated in the FGR plasma proteome and activated other apolipoproteins.

Discussion

This is the first study to our knowledge to investigate the proteomic profile of FGR and NG neonatal piglet plasma using LCMS. The similarities between the newborn piglet and the human newborn make it an ideal model for investigating brain development in the context of adverse pregnancy environments39,40. Specifically, the newborn piglet has similar development, circulation, metabolism and brain growth spurt to the human infant allowing for a higher degree of clinical translation41,42. Unlike human studies which have predominantly used targeted proteomic approaches to distinguish FGR from NG19,20,22,43, we conducted untargeted screening using data-independent acquisition SWATH-MS to identify many plasma proteins in complex samples. We identified 106 dysregulated proteins in FGR plasma that were associated with altered functions, pathways and networks. This study lays the foundation for future studies investigating potential mechanisms, a prognostic biomarker panel or therapeutic targets for FGR and its associated adverse outcomes.

The high complexity and extreme dynamic range of blood plasma significantly impacts the number of proteins identified in the samples44. Compared to previous studies44, up to twice the number of proteins were identified using non-depleted plasma and the ZenoTOF 7600 system. Although other biological matrices (e.g., brain and tissue extracts) can yield much higher protein identification, the clinical availability of blood samples makes this an ideal biofluid for OMICS research. We have also compared the performance of ZenoTOF 7600 to the previous generation TripleTOF 5600 for untargeted screening proteomics, revealing that ZenoTOF can quantify up to twice the number of plasma proteins using half of the sample load and a third of the instrument time. Such findings indicate that improvements in available technology will further improve sensitivity and drive faster discovery of novel protein networks and disease markers.

A limitation to the clinical translation of these results is the use of piglet plasma. We used IPA to map functional pathways and networks that may be involved in modulating the observed changes in our FGR pig model. The OMICS landscape of Sus Scrofa is increasing, with up to 80% of the predicted protein-coding genes having 1:1 orthology with human45. Recent comparative proteomic analysis between human and pig also revealed a high degree of correlation in expression of proteins among orthologs, particularly in samples derived from brain, heart, kidney and liver46. Such findings assure a high relevance of the data obtained in this species for human-related research. Although this study did not identify prognostic biomarkers, it represents a step forward and lays the foundation for future research in this area. Secondly, although there are known sex differences in FGR, this study did not investigate sex differences in the piglet proteome. Larger cohorts would be required to have sufficient power for this analysis. The primary aim of this study was to investigate proteome alterations that were consistent for both males and females. Given our piglets were collected from a commercial piggery, we have limited information on details regarding sows, parity or litter size. Piglets were collected on 8 farrowing (birthing) dates across a 6-month period with an FGR and normally grown littermate included in each collection. Further information regarding littermates, parity and litter size would have better informed the analysis of this data and therefore this lack of detail is a limitation of this study. Finally, although IPA can identify proteome alterations that are associated with pathways, networks and functions, future studies are required to determine associations between these plasma proteome alterations and organ development. This study aimed to identify these plasma proteome alterations as a foundation for such future studies.

The plasma proteome of newborn piglets is influenced by the ingestion of colostrum which results in a surge in total protein and immunoglobulins. At birth, piglet plasma protein levels are low (especially gamma-globulins), however within 12–24 h this is reversed by the absorption of colostrum resulting in increased immunoglobulin concentrations which are vital for passive immunity47,48. Although passive immunity (IgG) peaks within the first 24 h49, we observed a decrease in IgG in the FGR piglet compared with NG piglets which may result in reduced passive immunity in the FGR piglets. As plasma was collected at 12–18 h after birth in the current study, when the piglets had consumed colostrum, this is a timeframe where IgG levels are rapidly changing. Examining later time points (> 24) would be beneficial to determine whether IgG levels recover in the FGR plasma. Examining extended time points would also assist in understanding likely temporal changes in the piglet plasma proteome and whether interventions can modify protein levels.

The alterations to abundance levels of the apolipoprotein family in FGR identified here is consistent with a previous study that used apolipoprotein profiles from cord blood serum to differentiate between FGR and NG infants at birth50. Although able to distinguish between these groups using this apolipoprotein profile, the study did not identify specific individual proteins that could be utilized for clinical prediction of risk of long-term adverse outcomes. Here, our broader proteomic screen and pathway analysis identified associations between the apolipoprotein family within the primary network, multiple pathways (including LXR/RXR activation, DHCR24 Signalling Pathway and Acute Phase Response Signalling) and the top ten most significant diseases and functions. Apolipoproteins are central to multiple different pathways, including roles in lipoprotein metabolism, transferring lipids, enzyme cofactors and immune response51–53. The most statistically significantly associated pathway, LXR/RXR activation, is crucial for regulation of lipid metabolism, cholesterol homeostasis and inflammation54,55. Further, LXR signalling has been shown to be crucial in maintaining fetoplacental lipid homeostasis56. Given the multiple pathways and individual proteins altered in FGR compared to NG piglet plasma related to lipid metabolism, future studies investigating the lipidomic profile and associated signalling pathways could be crucial in understanding the mechanisms involved in FGR. There is also evidence that Apolipoprotein D can cross the blood brain barrier57. Future studies investigating whether these alterations in Apolipoprotein D and the apolipoprotein family levels in FGR compared to NG piglet plasma are associated with neuronal alterations which we have previously characterized in these piglets42 would be of particular interest. This could inform future studies in developing a blood biomarker panel to predict risk of long-term adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Fetuin A is a multifunctional protein and is associated with osteogenesis, calcium metabolism, insulin signalling, endocytosis and brain growth58. Previously, large and small for gestational age has been associated with higher levels of Fetuin A in cord plasma samples30 and another study has observed higher human maternal plasma levels of Fetuin A in FGR pregnancies59. However, one study observed decreased levels of Fetuin A in umbilical cord plasma for FGR compared to NG infants60. Further investigation revealed unique isoforms of Fetuin A present in the FGR umbilical cord blood plasma samples60. Studies in multiple animal models have observed Fetuin A expression and localization in different layers of the cortex during development29,61. Further, human fetal and newborn plasma levels of Fetuin A have been shown to be much higher than those observed in adults62. Our results confirm that dysregulation of Fetuin A is associated with FGR and provide further support for the piglet model of FGR. Further studies are required to improve our understanding of the role of Fetuin A during the neonatal period but current evidence indicates that appropriate levels of Fetuin A are important for normal and healthy development.

The predicted interaction between upregulation of Fetuin A and downregulation of Macrophage Stimulating 1 (MST1) in FGR along with inhibition of Protein Kinase B (PKB or AKT) in the primary network is a novel association for FGR. Inhibition of AKT could have important downstream consequences as the PKB/AKT pathway regulates multiple substrates63. AKT has important roles in cell survival, proliferation, metabolism, growth and migration64. Previous studies have observed lower levels of Insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), an upstream regulator of the AKT/PKB pathway, in maternal venous blood samples and placenta samples from FGR pregnancies65,66. Further, studies have observed lower AKT levels, and levels of activated AKT were undetectable, in FGR placenta samples67. This primary network highlights the many molecular mediators involved in multiple interactive pathways that drive AKT and MST1 downregulation and Fetuin A upregulation. These complex interactions could have implications for the mechanisms involved in FGR and associated adverse outcomes.

The complex brain-immune network is critical during development and throughout life68,69. Here we observed significant alterations to immune and inflammatory responses in FGR compared with NG plasma proteome. Previous studies have shown associations between immune and inflammatory markers and FGR reflected a temporal evolution19,20,22,23, which indicates a dynamic immune and inflammatory response during the early postnatal period. We have also reported that neuroinflammation in FGR piglets evolves during the early postnatal period and is associated with neuronal and white matter impairment42. Associated with these neuroinflammatory changes were alterations to neurovascular unit integrity which has also been observed in the FGR sheep model27,70,71. The neurovascular unit comprises a complex network of cells including endothelial cells, astrocytes, microglia and neurons72. A crucial role of this complex network to maintain normal brain function is to regulate infiltration of peripheral immune cells73. Studies have identified correlations between severity of outcomes in Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI) patients and alterations to blood biomarker levels74,75. This indicates the potential for markers of neuroinflammation and brain alterations to cross into the periphery where they can be detected in blood as well as infiltrating into the FGR brain and perpetuating further damage. Future studies investigating the dynamic and changing profile of these inflammatory markers around the time of birth may both identify a biomarker profile to indicate risk of long-term adverse outcomes and provide insight into causal mechanisms.

Finally, the statistically significant diseases associated with the protein alterations in the FGR piglet plasma, including cardiovascular, neurological, metabolic and gastrointestinal diseases, are adverse long-term outcomes that have previously been associated with FGR1,12,13,36–38. Early association of these disease states lends support to the Barker hypothesis, that a lack of nutrients and oxygen supply during pregnancy is a significant risk factor for major chronic diseases later in life such as cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes38. Future studies could utilize these identified associations to determine whether these proteins are effective biomarkers to indicate risk of these adverse long-term outcomes in FGR infants. The identified alterations to pathways and protein interaction networks also potentially indicated mechanisms for developing therapeutic interventions. For example, dietary alterations in pigs with FGR was able to reduce plasma glucose levels by improving muscle protein kinase B phosphorylation76. Therefore, the pathways and protein interactions identified as associated with the proteins dysregulated in FGR here could form an important foundation for future research into interventions and therapies to improve long-term outcomes for FGR infants. This study is important in adding to our understanding of FGR and its associated adverse outcomes and can serve as the foundation to developing biomarker panels for prognosis, and as a screening tool for inclusion in therapeutic studies.

Methods

Sample collection

Ethics Committee approval was granted for this study by The University of Queensland Animal Ethics Committee (2022/AE000519) and the study was carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations including ARRIVE guidelines and the National Health and Medical Research Council (Australia) guidelines. Newborn Large White piglets were collected from a commercial piggery (FGR n = 15; NG n = 14) 12–18 h after birth. Piglets were collected from eight sows with littermate NG controls. This spontaneously occurring model of FGR was defined by birth bodyweight (< 10th percentile on the day of birth) and confirmed by brain to liver weight ratio (B: L) ≥ 1 at postmortem in consensus with previous literature77,78. This asymmetrical growth observed through B: L is also observed in human FGR infants78,79. The NG cohort was also confirmed to have birthweight ≥ 40th percentile. Both FGR and NG cohorts had equal distribution of males and females. Appropriate sample size was determined using the analysis by Levin80, allowing for up to 50% combined technical and biological variation with power set to 0.8 (1 minus type II error) and confidence at 0.05 (type I error) such that a fold change of ± 1.5 could be measured reliably. Upon arrival at the Herston Medical Research Centre (HMRC), piglets were euthanised via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbitone sodium (650 mg/ kg; Lethabarb, Virbac, Australia). After no pain reflexes were observed, blood samples were collected directly from the heart and immediately transferred to Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes for plasma collection (3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C). Plasma samples were aliquoted into 0.6mL collection tubes and stored at -80 °C for later use. After blood collection, piglets were perfused with phosphate buffered saline (PBS). Brain tissue was then collected and weighed to confirm FGR diagnosis.

Concentrations of protein in the plasma samples were determined by Bicinchoninic acid assay (BCA) and volume required for 100 µg of protein was calculated.

Filter aided sample preparation (FASP) and digestion

FASP was used to process plasma protein extracts for proteomic analysis as detailed previously81. Plasma samples each containing 100 µg of protein was mixed with 100 µl of Buffer I (6 M guanidine chloride, 50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 10 mM DTT81 in a microcentrifuge tube and incubated at 30 °C for 30 min to reduce the proteins. Alkylation (in the dark) was carried out by addition of 3 µl of 0.5 M iodoacetamide and incubation at 30 °C for 50 min. Afterwards, samples were transferred to a 10 kDa cut-off Amicron Ultra device (Merck) which was placed into a collection tube and centrifuged at 14,000xg for 30 min. Two hundred microlitre of 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate was added to the column followed by centrifugation at 14,000xg for 30 min to wash out the DDT and Iodoacetamide81. The column was placed into a new collection tube and 130 µl of 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate containing 2 µg Trypsin was added, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 20 h to digest proteins81. Digested peptides were recovered by centrifugation at 14,000xg for 30 min. To ensure collection of remaining peptides, 50 µl of 0.1% formic acid was then added to the spin column followed by centrifugation at 14,000xg for 30 min. Enzymes and salts were removed using C18 ZipTips (Merck) with a capacity of 5 µg and the desalted peptides were eluted with 80% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid into microcentrifuge tubes. To increase concentration of digested peptide, samples were dried down using a vacuum concentrator (Concentrator plus, Eppendorf)81 and reconstituted with 60 µl 0.1% formic acid prior to LC-MS analysis.

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry

Digested peptides were analysed on a ZenoTOF 7600 mass spectrometer and data was acquired using a SWATH-MS workflow for data independent acquisition, following precisely the procedure described in Meizoso-Huesca, Lamboley82.

The entire 29 samples were processed and run on the instrument in a single batch to minimize technical variations. Calibration experiments were run every 3 samples using the automatic calibration function from SCIEX – this ensures accurate and reliable analytical results. Stability of signals over time were monitored by repeat injection of Hela protein digest – the number of protein IDs detected at 1% FDR threshold at batch-start and batch-end were consistently > 1000, demonstrating that sensitivity of the instrument had not dropped during data acquisition.

Analysis of proteomics data

Product ion spectra were processed using the Paragon search engine on ProteinPilot Software (version 5.01, AB Sciex, Redwood City, CA) against a curated Sus Scrofa Uniprot database83. Each raw file from each sample was searched as single input and the resulting output file used as a reference ion library for processing of SWATH-MS data. PeakView software (version 1.2.0.3, Sciex) was used to interrogate the SWATH-MS data against the reference ion library. For normalization, we used MarkerView’s MLR (Most Likely Ratio) algorithm to account for differences due to experimental artifacts. MarkerView was used to generate the list of dysregulated proteins in Sus Scrofa plasma by performing a pair wise (student’s t-test) comparison of molecules in the FGR vs. NG group. Differences in protein abundance between FGR vs. NG groups are presented as fold changes.

Ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA) of identified proteins

To facilitate biological interpretation of the many proteins identified in the comparisons, Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) was used. The tool can be used to identify the most relevant functions, pathways and networks represented in experimental proteomics datasets. Identified proteins that show significant difference in expression between FGR and NG controls were analysed by IPA to map functional pathways and protein-protein interaction networks as well as upstream/downstream regulators (QIAGEN Inc., https://digitalinsights.qiagen.com/IPA). In IPA, the Fisher’s Exact Test was applied to test the set of up- or down-regulated proteins in our proteomics data set to identify canonical pathways impacted in FGR.

Group characteristic comparisons

Statistical analyses of FGR compared to NG for body, brain, liver weights and brain: liver weight ratio were performed using Graph Pad Prism 10.4.1 software (San Diego, CA, USA). Data were analysed using unpaired Welch t-tests. Results were expressed as mean ± SEM with statistical significance accepted at p < 0.05.
