Health Behaviors in Adolescent and Young Adult (AYA) Childhood Cancer Survivors: Prevalence, Determinants, and Comparison With Population Controls
Authors: Ismay A. E. de Beijer, Selina R. van den Oever, Oluwaseyi A. Lawal, Heide Adham, Tamilore Ashofor, Amanda Button, Jenny Duong, Rachel Taylor, Jennifer Giles, Saskia M. F. Pluijm, Kathleen Reynolds, Fiona Schulte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionChildhood cancer survivors (CCS) are at lifelong risk for late adverse health outcomes. Healthy behaviors, including physical activity, substance avoidance, adequate sleep, and sun protection, are critical to mitigating these risks. Yet, data on their prevalence and determinants in this population remain limited. This study aims to describe health behaviors among adolescent and young adult (AYA) CCS in Alberta, Canada, compare them with population‐based controls, and identify associated factors for suboptimal health behaviors.MethodsThis is a longitudinal cohort study of 312 CCS aged ≥ 11 years enrolled in the Long‐Term Survivor Clinic at the Alberta Children's Hospital between July 2021 and July 2025. Self‐reported questionnaires captured health behavior outcomes and covariates. Comparisons were made with Canadian Community Health Survey (CCHS) controls. Risk factors were determined using Bayesian analyses.ResultsAmong 312 participants (median age 22.1 years [interquartile range 8.78]; 50.6% female) at baseline, 31% did not meet physical activity recommendations, 13% reported current tobacco use, 29% reported current cannabis use, 0.1% reported poor sleep quality, and 47% reported only sometimes or rarely using sunscreen, with 14% never using sunscreen. Only 5.8% reported ever using artificial tanning devices. Compared with controls, CCS had higher rates of insufficient physical activity and e‐cigarette use but lower rates of alcohol and medicinal cannabis use. Older age predicted artificial tanning, while male sex was associated with tobacco use and lower sunscreen adherence. Treatment intensity was not associated with any of the health behaviors.ConclusionIn this study, age and sex rather than treatment intensity predicted health behaviors. Emerging behaviors, such as e‐cigarette and medicinal cannabis use, highlight gaps in current survivorship care and the need for standardized counseling frameworks. Overall, the integration of routine health behavior assessments and targeted interventions addressing these behaviors and associated factors is warranted to optimize long‐term AYA survivorship outcomes.

Introduction

Recent advances in pediatric oncology have led to improved survival rates in high‐income countries, resulting in a growing global population of childhood cancer survivors (CCS) [1, 2, 3, 4]. As a result of their cancer and treatment, CCS are at lifelong risk for late adverse health conditions such as second cancers [5, 6, 7, 8], fatigue [9, 10], and cardiovascular toxicities [11, 12]. Such conditions can contribute to reduced quality of life and premature mortality [13, 14, 15].

Engaging in healthy behaviors such as regular physical activity, maintaining a healthy weight, avoiding substance use, and getting enough sleep may mitigate adverse health conditions later in life and promote long‐term well‐being in CCS [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. On the other hand, making unhealthy choices can increase the risk of developing adverse health conditions or premature mortality [19]. As such, the adoption and maintenance of healthy behaviors play an essential role in childhood cancer survivorship care. However, CCS may struggle to adopt and maintain healthy behaviors because of barriers such as fatigue, unmet information needs, time constraints, lack of motivation, social influences, and poor mental health [22].

To optimize long‐term well‐being in CCS, it is first necessary to understand the patterns and trends of health behaviors within this group. This information can help identify behavioral domains in which survivors may fall behind norm groups or national health standards, highlighting areas for targeted intervention. Examining the risk factors associated with these behaviors is equally important, as it allows identification of subgroups at higher risk of unhealthy behaviors and informs the development of targeted interventions. In addition, documenting trends over time can help determine if current survivorship care strategies effectively support the adoption and maintenance of healthy behaviors or if adjusted approaches are needed.

To address these needs, the current study aims to describe the prevalence of several health behaviors among adolescent and young adult (AYA) survivors of childhood cancer, aged 11 years and older at follow‐up in Alberta, Canada. Specifically, we will examine the prevalence of physical activity levels, tobacco use, alcohol consumption, cannabis use, sleep quality, artificial tanning, and sunscreen use over time. Where possible, we will compare these outcomes with those of age‐matched individuals from the general Canadian population. Finally, we will determine the factors associated with these health behaviors.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This study included AYA CCS enrolled in the Long‐Term Survivor Clinic (LTSC) at the Alberta Children's Hospital in Calgary, Alberta, Canada. The LTSC provides follow‐up care to CCS, offering comprehensive assessments that include physical, psychological, and behavioral health evaluations. Data for this study were collected from self‐reported questionnaires administered between July 2021 and July 2025 that are part of standard survivorship care at the LTSC. This LTSC questionnaire is emailed or mailed to all survivors 1 week before their routine clinic appointment and can be completed before or during their clinic visit. Depending on the physician's risk assessment, survivors attend the clinic twice a year, once a year, or once every 2 or 3 years. Eligible participants were CCS diagnosed before the age of 21, who had completed active treatment at least 2 years before completing the questionnaire, and were at least 11 years of age at the time of the study. To ensure the validity of the self‐reported outcomes, only responses from participants who completed questionnaires themselves (as opposed to a parent proxy report) were included. Upon inclusion in this study, participants had to provide informed consent for the use of their questionnaire data for research purposes. In instances where participants provided consent for some visits but not others, only data from visits with documented consent were included in the analysis. The LTSC questionnaire received ethical approval from the Health Research Ethics Board of Alberta Cancer Committee (HREBA.CC‐19‐0498; Edmonton, AB, Canada). This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study Measures

Physical Activity

We assessed physical activity through two questions. The first question asked, “Which statement best describes your usual daily activities?” Response options reflected increasing levels of physical exertion: (1) “I mostly sit during the day and do not walk about very much,” (2) “I stand or walk about quite a lot during the day, but do not have to carry or lift things very often,” (3) “I carry light loads or climb stairs or hills often,” and (4) “I do heavy work or carry heavy loads.” The second question asked, “Over the past month, how many hours per week do you typically exercise?” Response categories included “less than 1 hour,” “1 hour,” “2 hours,” “3 hours,” “4 hours,” and “5 hours or more.”

To enable comparison with controls, we categorized physical activity as either sufficient or insufficient. While the questions were not directly based on formal guidelines, responses were considered consistent with recommendations of the World Health Organization (WHO) for adults if they indicated heavy work or carrying heavy loads with any weekly exercise; light load carrying or frequent stair or hill climbing with at least 1 h of exercise per week; or any of these activities with at least 3 h of exercise per week [23]. For participants under 18, the WHO recommends at least 1 h of daily exercise. However, since the response options only went up to at least 4 h per week, we applied the same adult‐based criteria.

Tobacco Use

First, participants were asked, “Have you ever used any tobacco products?” (yes/no). Those who responded “yes” were then asked a follow‐up question: “In the past year, have you ever used any of these tobacco products?” Participants were instructed to select all that applied from three categories: (1) chewing or snuff tobacco; (2) pipes, cigars, or cigarettes; and (3) e‐cigarettes or vapes. For each category, participants selected one of four usage frequency options: “Never used,” “No longer use,” “Occasional use,” or “Regular use.” We categorized any report of occasional or regular use of one of the three categories as current tobacco use. While recognizing that e‐cigarettes and vapes are not inherently tobacco products, they have been categorized as such for methodological consistency in this study.

Alcohol Use

Participants were asked, “Have you ever consumed alcohol?” (yes/no). Those who answered “yes” were then asked, “How many alcoholic drinks do you consume in an average week?” with response options ranging from “0” to “15+” drinks per week. To assess heavy drinking behavior, participants were asked, “Do you ever drink to intoxication (get drunk)?” Those who answered “yes” were asked, “How often do you drink to intoxication?” with the following response options: “Less than 1 time per month,” “1–2 times per month,” “3–4 times per month,” or “4+ times per month.”

Although the WHO states that no level of alcohol consumption is safe, the Canadian low‐risk drinking guidelines recommend a maximum of two standard drinks per day, or 10 drinks per week, for adult women; and a maximum of three standard drinks per day, or 15 drinks per week, for adult men [24]. To consistently and conservatively classify alcohol exposure across sexes, we combined these recommendations and categorized consumption of fewer than five standard drinks per week as meeting the recommendations. Given that the legal drinking age in Alberta is 18, abstaining from alcohol (i.e., reporting never consumption or an average of zero drinks per week) and not becoming intoxicated at least once a month considered to be in line with the recommendations for participants under 18. Among adults, consuming five or more drinks per week or self‐reporting monthly intoxication was classified as not meeting the recommendations. Any alcohol consumption among participants under 18 years of age was also classified as not meeting the recommendations.

Cannabis Use

Cannabis use was assessed through a series of self‐report items evaluating lifetime and past‐year use of cannabis. First, participants were asked, “Have you ever used cannabis/marijuana?” (yes/no). Those who answered “yes” were then asked to describe their cannabis use over the past year across three modes of consumption: smoking/inhalation, ingestion, and topical application. For each mode, response options included: “Never used,” “No longer use,” “Occasional use,” and “Regular use.” We categorized any report of occasional or regular use as current cannabis use. Furthermore, participants were asked whether their cannabis use was medicinal with the question, “Are the cannabis products you use prescribed by a doctor?” (yes/no).

Sleep Quality

Sleep quality was assessed using the PROMIS Sleep Disturbance Short Form 8b, a validated instrument designed to capture various aspects of sleep over the past 7 days [25]. Participants responded to eight items on a five‐point Likert scale. The items assessed included: “My sleep was restless,” “I was satisfied with my sleep,” “My sleep was refreshing,” and “I had difficulty falling asleep,” with response options ranging from “Not at all,” “A little bit,” “Somewhat,” “Quite a bit,” to “Very much.” Three additional items—“I had trouble staying asleep,” “I had trouble sleeping,” and “I got enough sleep”—used frequency‐based response options: “Never,” “Rarely,” “Sometimes,” “Often,” and “Always.” Lastly, the item “My sleep quality was” was rated on a qualitative scale: “Very poor,” “Poor,” “Fair,” “Good,” or “Very good.” Responses to the eight items were used to calculate a PROMIS T‐score. A T‐score of 60 or higher was considered unhealthy, as this indicates a clinically meaningful sleep disturbance.

Sun Exposure

Participants were asked two questions about sun exposure behaviors: “Have you ever used artificial tanning devices, such as a sunlamp or tanning booth?” (yes/no) and “Do you wear sunscreen?” (never, rarely, sometimes, often, always). As there are currently no harmonized guidelines on sun exposure in CCS, we made no distinction between healthy and unhealthy levels of sun exposure.

Data Analysis

We used descriptive statistics to summarize the characteristics of participating survivors and their reported lifestyle behaviors. Where applicable, prevalence estimates among survivors were compared with those from the 2019–2020 Canadian Community Health Survey (CCHS; www23.statcan.gc.ca) using chi‐square tests, incorporating CCHS survey weights to generate population‐representative control estimates. Only respondents with complete data were included in the analysis. Furthermore, we made comparisons using survivors' first LTSC measurement to approximate the CCHS timeframe. Comparisons were made with both the national Canadian population and the subset from Alberta to provide contextual reference. Only variables available and comparable in the CCHS were analyzed, including compliance with physical activity guidelines, ever using alcohol, current tobacco use, medicinal cannabis use, and e‐cigarette use/vaping. Physical activity in the CCHS was classified as meeting or falling below Canadian Physical Activity Guidelines (CPAG)‐recommended levels [26], which are broadly comparable to WHO guidelines. Ever‐use of alcohol was assessed with the question, “Have you ever had a drink in your lifetime?” (yes/no). Current tobacco use was determined from respondents' smoking status, with “Current daily smoker” and “Current occasional smoker” categorized as current use (other responses including “Former daily smoker,” “Former occasional smoker,” “Experimental smoker (>1 cigarette, <100 cigarettes)”, and “Lifetime abstainer” were considered non‐current users). Medicinal cannabis use was identified by a “yes” response to “Do you have a medical document from a healthcare professional to use cannabis for medical purposes?” E‐cigarette or vaping use was defined as any use of an e‐cigarette or vaping device at least once in the past month.

We fitted binary logistic regression models for all outcomes used in multivariable models to examine potential non‐response bias, comparing participants with complete data to those with missing data. To assess potential participation bias, baseline characteristics were compared between survivors who participated in only one follow‐up assessment and those who participated in multiple follow‐up assessments. Furthermore, we evaluated frequentist GLMMs but results showed separation/near‐separation and unstable random‐effect variance estimates for sparse outcomes. Therefore, we used Bayesian multilevel models as our primary approach to determine associations between risk factors and health behavior outcomes. We specified weakly informative priors to regularize estimation while allowing the data to primarily determine posterior estimates. Fixed effects were assigned Normal(0, 2.5) priors, intercepts Student‐t(3, 0, 10) priors, and random‐effect standard deviations Student‐t(3, 0, 2.5) priors. We assessed model accuracy via posterior predictive checks. Binary outcomes (insufficient exercise, unhealthy alcohol consumption, current tobacco use, medicinal cannabis use, recreational cannabis use, poor sleep quality, artificial tanning) were modeled with Bernoulli likelihood and a logit link, while sunscreen use, a four‐level categorical outcome, was analyzed using a Bayesian multilevel categorical regression model. We addressed study‐level clustering via random intercepts for participant' study ID. These models accounted for within‐person correlation across repeated observations but did not estimate longitudinal change trajectories. The fixed effects included age, sex, treatment intensity level according to the ITR‐3 classification system (levels 1–4) [27, 28], educational level (binary) relationship status (yes/no), and employment status (yes/no). Education level was collapsed into two groups to facilitate model convergence: High school or less (grade school, high school but did not graduate, and completed high school/GED) and post‐secondary (some post‐secondary/university, graduated from post‐secondary/university, training after high school other than college, and post‐graduate level). Observations classified as Other were excluded due to insufficient interpretability and low frequency (n = 3). To facilitate convergence of the sunscreen use model, treatment intensity was collapsed into two categories (low vs. high). The sleep quality model did not converge because of low power and was therefore not interpreted. The models were estimated using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (NUTS) with four chains and 2000 iterations per chain (1000 warm‐up iterations). Convergence was evaluated using Rhat, effective sample sizes and visual inspection of chain mixing. Posterior estimates and 95% credible intervals (CrIs) were used to assess effect sizes and uncertainty. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY) and R version 4.3.1 using the brms package version 2.22.0. We report posterior summaries (posterior means or medians) with 95% credible intervals and summarize evidence for association using posterior probabilities of direction (e.g., Pr(β > 0)).

At baseline, the sample (n = 312) consisted of 154 men (49.4%) and 158 women (50.6%) with a median age of 22.07 years (interquartile [IQR] range = 8.78). Participants completed one to six LTSQ assessments, with only one participant providing six measurements. They had a mean of 2.07 LTSQ assessments (SD 1.13). The mean time interval between LTSQ assessments was 13.2 months (SD = 4.95), with a median of 12.2 months (range 1.3–40.5). The median age at diagnosis was 7.35 years (IQR = 10.22) and participants were, on average, 13.08 years (SD = 6.23) off therapy. The most frequent cancer types were leukemia (n = 116; 37.2%) and lymphoma (n = 60; 19.2%), followed by central nervous system tumors (n = 26; 8.3%), bone tumors (n = 23; 7.4%), renal tumors (n = 21; 6.7%), neuroblastoma (n = 20; 6.4%), soft tissue sarcoma (n = 22; 7.0%), and other lower‐frequency entities (≤ 2.2% each). A total of 198 survivors (63.1%) had surgery, 302 (96.2%) received chemotherapy, and 115 (36.6%) received radiotherapy. Specifically, most participants had moderate to high treatment intensity, with 106 (34.0%) classified as Level 2, 124 (39.7%) as Level 3, and 55 (17.6%) as Level 4, while 9 (2.9%) had received Level 1 treatment. Treatment intensity data were missing for 18 participants (5.8%). Relapse occurred in 31 individuals (9.9%). Educational attainment ranged from grade school (n = 2; 0.6%) to completion of post‐secondary or university programs (n = 81; 25.9%). In addition, 143 participants (45.8%) were employed and 105 (33.7%) reported being in a relationship. Binary logistic regression analyses indicated that respondents with higher treatment intensity levels had significantly higher odds of having missing sleep quality data compared with the reference treatment intensity group (OR = 1.91, 95% CI [1.15, 3.18], p = 0.013). All other associations with outcome‐related missingness were non‐significant (data not shown). Participants who completed multiple follow‐ups (n = 214) were more likely to be female than those who completed only one follow‐up (n = 98; 54.7% vs. 41.8%, p = 0.035). We observed no other significant differences (Table S1).

Prevalences of health behaviors across measurements (n = 312).

At baseline, 214 (68.6%) participants met recommended physical activity thresholds. A total of 75 (24.0%) and 41 (13.1%) participants reported ever having used or currently using tobacco, respectively. In particular, 15 (4.8%) participants reported regular e‐cigarette/vape use, compared to 17 (5.4%) occasional users, 20 (6.4%) participants no longer using, and 19 (6.1%) never users (N.B. there were 241 (77.2%) participants with missing data). 232 (74.4%) participants reported having ever consumed alcohol, with 23 (7.4%) participants who did not meet alcohol recommendations. In terms of cannabis use, 141 (45.2%) participants reported ever‐use versus 171 (54.8%) non‐use. Current cannabis use was reported by 91 (29.2%) participants. Recreational cannabis use was reported by 83 participants (26.6%) versus 225 (72.1%) non‐users, while medicinal use was reported by 34 (10.9%) participants versus 278 (89.1%) non‐users. Insufficient sleep quality was reported by 1 (0.1%) participant. Ever‐use of artificial tanning was reported by 18 (5.8%) participants. Lastly, sunscreen behaviors ranged between always: 34 (10.9%), often: 99 (31.7%), sometimes: 111 (35.6%); rarely: 50 (16.0%), and never: 11 (3.5%), with 7 (2.2%) respondents missing. Of note, the decline in the absolute number of participants reporting unhealthy behaviors across T1–T6 shown in Table 2 is a function of sample attrition and rising missing data at later clinic visits, rather than documented individual behavioral improvements over time.

Discussion

This study provides an overview and comparison of the prevalence of health behaviors among Canadian AYA CCS and controls. In addition, we determined factors associated with suboptimal health behaviors in CCS. Comparisons showed that survivors were significantly more likely than controls to report insufficient physical activity, with nearly one‐third failing to meet the recommended levels. Furthermore, survivors were less likely to report ever having consumed alcohol or use cannabis medicinally but showed a higher prevalence of e‐cigarette use/vaping compared with the control group. Current tobacco use rates were comparable to those in the general population. Sleep quality, artificial tanning and sunscreen use could not be compared with population controls. Only one participant reported poor sleep quality at baseline. While artificial tanning was uncommon, consistent sunscreen use was low. In terms of risk factors, older adolescents and young adults showed a higher risk of recreational cannabis use and artificial tanning. In addition, male survivors and those in a relationship were more likely to report current tobacco use. Compared to women, men were less likely to use sunscreen regularly. Notably, treatment intensity was not associated with any of the health behaviors. Although this aligns with a previous systematic review [22], our non‐response analyses indicated that survivors with higher treatment intensity were more likely to have missing sleep quality data. This raises the possibility of selective non‐response and suggests that associations between treatment intensity and the health behaviors examined in this study may have been underestimated. Nearly one‐third of survivors in our study failed to meet recommended activity levels. Our findings are consistent with prior literature showing reduced activity levels among survivors relative to peers [29, 30, 31]. These findings may indicate that CCS' knowledge about the importance of physical activity to prevent or mitigate late effects is insufficient or not translating into durable behavior change. Evidence from prospective and randomized trials indicates that tailored exercise programs can facilitate lifestyle changes [32], reduce cardiovascular risk [16] and improve quality of life in CCS [33], but implementing and scaling such interventions within real world survivorship settings remains a challenge and adequate implementation strategies are needed.

In terms of substance use, survivors were less likely to report ever consuming alcohol and medicinal cannabis use as compared with controls, a pattern consistently observed in the literature [34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40]. Younger survivors were more likely to report medicinal cannabis use, consistent with previous studies showing that younger survivors are more likely to use cannabis in general [41, 42]. A potential explanation for the higher likelihood of medicinal cannabis use among younger survivors is the management of symptoms related to cancer or its treatment, such as pain, nausea, sleep disturbances, and anxiety, which is a commonly cited motive for cannabis use among cancer patients and survivors [43]. Younger CCS, being closer to the end of treatment, may therefore experience more acute treatment‐related symptoms that drive medicinal cannabis use.

Compared with controls, survivors reported higher rates of e‐cigarette use and vaping. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the questionnaire structure may have underestimated vaping prevalence by not capturing exclusive e‐cigarette users without prior tobacco use. This limitation also reduces direct comparability with population‐based CCHS estimates, which assessed vaping independently of tobacco exposure. Despite this limitation, our findings are consistent with a recent review which reported higher rates of inhaled substance use among adolescent and young adult cancer survivors than in the general population [44]. Dewar et al. propose two main reasons for the increased use of e‐cigarettes and vaping in this population, namely the impact of marketing and normalization among younger adults, and use of e‐cigarettes/vaping as a harm‐reduction or cessation strategy for combustible cigarettes [45]. This is concerning, as multiple studies have demonstrated that e‐cigarette aerosols contain biologically active constituents, including nicotine, carbonyl compounds, volatile organic compounds, and heavy metals, which can induce oxidative stress, inflammation, and DNA damage [46, 47, 48]. Furthermore, modern, high‐concentration nicotine formulations can deliver nicotine at levels that equal or even exceed those of conventional cigarettes, raising concerns about addiction and associated cardiopulmonary effects. While long‐term data on inhaled substance use in CCS remain limited [44], survivorship programs have yet to implement standardized guidance or counseling frameworks regarding inhaled substance use, highlighting an emerging and under‐addressed gap in survivorship care delivery.

Only one survivor reported clinically significant sleep disturbance at baseline, which does not align with higher prior estimates [10]. Poor sleep and sleep disorders in CCS have been linked to neurocognitive impairment, fatigue, depression, anxiety, and heart failure risk [10, 21, 49]. Despite these consequences, sleep assessment is not consistently embedded in routine survivorship care [50, 51], even though sleep disturbances are explicitly included in the most recent Children's Oncology Group guideline (available via www.survivorshipguidelines.org). Evidence‐based interventions, including cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT‐I) and family education on sleep hygiene (e.g., consistent sleep–wake schedules and pre‐sleep routines) have been shown to mitigate sleep disturbances during cancer treatment and could be continued into survivorship [50, 52].

Although artificial tanning was uncommon among CCS, fewer than half of the participants said they “always” or “often” used sunscreen. The observed associations between older age, male sex, and less frequent sunscreen use are consistent with established patterns in both CCS and the general population [53, 54, 55, 56]. Declining sunscreen adherence with increasing age possibly reflects reduced parental monitoring and attenuation of perceived cancer‐related vulnerability [57, 58]. Lower sunscreen use among male survivors is potentially driven by social framing of sun protection as a cosmetic rather than medical behavior [56]. This raises concerns given the well‐established elevated risk of skin cancer following cancer treatment [59, 60]. Behavioral reinforcement and risk personalization could support survivors in protecting themselves from sun damage.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to describe repeated health behaviors among AYA CCS in the Canadian context. Furthermore, it is the first study to describe patterns of e‐cigarette and medicinal cannabis use in Canadian survivors; behaviors that are becoming increasingly relevant in contemporary health care. Another strength is the direct comparison with population‐based controls, which allows survivors' behaviors to be interpreted relative to national norms. However, several limitations merit consideration. First, as health behaviors were self‐reported, the findings may be subject to misclassification and social desirability bias. Second, increasing levels of missing data across repeated measurements may have introduced selection and attrition bias. However, non‐response analyses suggested that missingness was largely unrelated to participant characteristics, with the exception of sleep quality data, for which higher treatment intensity was associated with an increased likelihood of non‐response. Furthermore, we found no substantial evidence of participation bias based on measured baseline characteristics. Third, comparisons with the controls from the CCHS were limited by data availability and measurement differences. Nevertheless, these findings provide a practical and clinically relevant evaluation of health behaviors in a real‐world survivorship setting and highlight potential areas for preventive intervention.

In conclusion, the findings of this study emphasize the importance of routinely assessing health behaviors in AYA CCS and, when necessary, implementing targeted health behavior interventions.
