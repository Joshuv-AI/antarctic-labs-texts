A Riemannian Autocorrelation Function and its Application to Non-Local Isoperimetric Energies
Michael Bleher, Denis Brazke, Sebastian Nill

Abstract
. We study a family of non-local isoperimetric energies E γ , ε E_{\gamma,\varepsilon} on the round sphere M = S n M=S^{n} , where the non-local interaction kernel K ε K_{\varepsilon} is the fundamental solution of the Helmholtz operator 1 − ε 2 ​ Δ 1-\varepsilon^{2}\Delta . To analyse these energies, we introduce a Riemannian autocorrelation function c Ω c_{\Omega} associated to a measurable set Ω ⊂ M \Omega\subset M , defined on any compact, connected, oriented Riemannian manifold without boundary ( M n , g ) (M^{n},g) of dimension n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 . This function is intimately linked to Matheron’s set covariogram from convex geometry. By establishing a characterisation of functions of bounded variation B ​ V ​ ( M ) BV(M) in terms of geodesic difference quotients, we show that Ω \Omega has finite perimeter if and only if c Ω c_{\Omega} is Lipschitz, and we relate the Lipschitz constant to the perimeter of Ω \Omega . We show that on the round sphere E γ , ε E_{\gamma,\varepsilon} admits a reformulation in terms of c Ω c_{\Omega} , which allows us to compute the limit as ε → 0 \varepsilon\to 0 in a variational sense, that is, in the framework of Γ \Gamma -convergence.

In this article, we are interested in non-local isoperimetric energies of the form

\displaystyle E_{\gamma,\varepsilon}(\Omega)\coloneqq\operatorname{Per}(\Omega)-\frac{\gamma}{\varepsilon}\int_{M\times M}K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\,|\ind_{\Omega}(x)-\ind_{\Omega}(y)|\,\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\,\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y,

This energy provides a sharp interface model for pattern formation in biological membranes and has previously been studied in the flat case

However, many pattern formation phenomena occur not on flat domains, but on curved surfaces (see e.g. the survey

In the Euclidean case, the non-local term in (

\displaystyle c_{\Omega}^{\mathrm{Eucl}}(r)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{n-1}}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}\\

The autocorrelation function is the average of the

2.
Riemannian manifolds and the geodesic flow

Throughout this section
(M^{n},g)
denotes a connected, oriented, complete Riemannian manifold without boundary of dimension
n\geq 2
.
Completeness ensures that geodesics are globally defined, which suffices for the geometric machinery presented in this section.
Later, when discussing functions of bounded variation and studying pattern formation on manifolds, we will additionally require compactness to obtain uniform bounds on various geometric quantities.

Our approach to generalizing the autocorrelation function relies on replacing averaged Euclidean translations with the geodesic flow on the unit tangent bundle
SM
.
More specifically, on Euclidean space, the autocorrelation function relies fundamentally on translation invariance of the Lebesgue measure.
On a general Riemannian manifold, translations do not exist.
Instead, the geodesic flow on the unit tangent bundle, together with the invariance of the Liouville measure under this flow (Proposition
2.2
), provides the correct substitute for translation invariance.
It allows us to define averaged quantities that generalise the Euclidean autocorrelation function to arbitrary manifolds.
This section collects the necessary geometric preliminaries.

2.1.
The unit tangent bundle and averaged integrals

For
x\in M
we write
T_{x}M
for the tangent space at
x
equipped with the inner product
g_{x}
and the associated norm
\|v\|_{g_{x}}=\sqrt{g_{x}(v,v)}
.
The unit sphere in
T_{x}M
is denoted by
S_{x}M\coloneqq\bigl\{w\in T_{x}M:\|w\|_{g_{x}}=1\bigr\}.

The unit tangent bundle is

(2.1)

\displaystyle SM\coloneqq\bigl\{(x,w):x\in M,\ w\in S_{x}M\bigr\}\subset TM,

equipped with the canonical projection

(2.2)

\displaystyle\pi\colon SM\longrightarrow M,\qquad\pi(x,w)=x.

We shall often write
\theta=(x,w)\in SM
.

The Riemannian metric
g
induces a Riemannian volume form
\vol_{M}
on
M
, as well as a canonical volume form
\vol_{SM}
on
SM
, usually called the Liouville measure.
We work with its fibrewise normalisation

(2.3)

\displaystyle\overline{\smash{\mathrm{vol}}\raisebox{0.0pt}{}}\vphantom{\mathrm{vol}}_{SM}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sigma_{n-1}}\,\vol_{SM},

where as before, we write
\sigma_{k}
for the
k
–dimensional volume of the Euclidean unit sphere
S^{k}\subset\mathbbm{R}^{k+1}
.
Integrals with respect to
\overline{\smash{\mathrm{vol}}\raisebox{0.0pt}{}}\vphantom{\mathrm{vol}}_{SM}
are written as

(2.4)

\displaystyle\fint_{SM}F(\theta)\,\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sigma_{n-1}}\int_{SM}F(\theta)\,\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\vol_{SM}(\theta)

for integrable
F\colon SM\longrightarrow\mathbbm{R}
.

For
x\in M
and an integrable function
\varphi\colon S_{x}M\longrightarrow\mathbbm{R}
we use the normalised spherical average

(2.5)

\displaystyle\oint_{x}\varphi(w)\,\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sigma_{n-1}}\int_{S_{x}M}\varphi(w)\,\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}S_{x}(w),

where
\mathrm{d}S_{x}
is the
(n-1)
–dimensional surface measure on
S_{x}M
induced by the inner product
g_{x}
on
T_{x}M
.
The next proposition records the disintegration of the Liouville measure along the fibres of
\pi
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For every
F\in L^{1}(SM)
,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\fint_{SM}F(\theta)\,\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta=\int_{M}\oint_{x}F(x,w)\,\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w\,\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x.

See e.g.
[
6
, Theorem VII.1.3.]
.
∎

We will later repeatedly need the average of the absolute value of the Riemannian metric over the unit sphere.
The next proposition shows that this average is proportional to the norm of the underlying vector and identifies the proportionality constant.

Proposition 2.2
.

For
x\in M
and
u\in T_{x}M
we have

(2.7)

\displaystyle\oint_{x}|g_{x}(u,w)|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w=k_{n}\lVert u\rVert_{g_{x}},

where
k_{n}
is the
sphere volume ratio
, given by

(2.8)

\displaystyle k_{n}\coloneqq\frac{2}{n-1}\frac{\sigma_{n-2}}{\sigma_{n-1}}=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n-1}}=\frac{2}{\sigma_{n-1}}\frac{\pi^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}{\Gamma(\tfrac{n+1}{2})}.

Every inner product space is isometric to the standard Euclidean space.
Indeed, the Gram-Schmidt process allows us to construct an orthogonal linear isomorphism

(2.9)

\displaystyle\psi:(T_{x}M,g_{x})\xrightarrow[\mspace{40mu}]{\cong}(\mathbbm{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)\qquad\text{satisfying}\qquad g_{x}(v_{1},v_{2})=\langle\psi(v_{1}),\psi(v_{2})\rangle

for all
v_{1},v_{2}\in T_{x}M
.
We set
z\coloneqq\psi(u)
.
Since
\lVert w\rVert_{g_{x}}=\lVert\psi(w)\rVert
,
the transformation
w\longmapsto\psi(w)=y
sends the unit sphere in
T_{x}M
to the one in
\mathbbm{R}^{n}
.
Hence, the integral can be rewritten as

(2.10)

\displaystyle\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in T_{x}M\\
\lVert w\rVert_{g_{x}}=1\end{subarray}}|g_{x}(u,w)|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w=\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}\\
\lVert y\rVert=1\end{subarray}}|\langle y,z\rangle|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y.

As rotations act transitively on
S^{n-1}
,
we may choose
R\in SO(n)
with
Rz=\lVert z\rVert e_{1}
.
Then

(2.11)

\displaystyle\langle y,z\rangle=\langle Ry,Rz\rangle=\lVert z\rVert\langle Ry,e_{1}\rangle=\lVert u\rVert_{g_{x}}\langle x,e_{1}\rangle=\lVert u\rVert_{g_{x}}x_{1},

where we set
x\coloneqq Ry
and use that
\lVert z\rVert=\lVert\psi(u)\rVert=\lVert u\rVert_{g_{x}}
.
Thus,

(2.12)

\displaystyle\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}\\
\lVert y\rVert=1\end{subarray}}|\langle y,z\rangle|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y=\lVert u\rVert_{g_{x}}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}\\
\lVert x\rVert=1\end{subarray}}|x_{1}|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x.

Using poly-spherical coordinates to evaluate the remaining integral, we obtain

(2.13)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}|x_{1}|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{S^{n-2}}|\cos(\phi)|\sin^{n-2}(\phi)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}z\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\phi

(2.14)

\displaystyle=2\,\sigma_{n-2}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\sin^{n-2}(\phi)\underbrace{\cos(\phi)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\phi}_{=\mathrlap{\,\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\sin(\phi)}}

(2.15)

\displaystyle=2\,\sigma_{n-2}\int_{0}^{1}s^{n-2}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s

(2.16)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n-1}\,\sigma_{n-2}.

Normalising by the sphere volume
\sigma_{n-1}
yields the claimed identity

(2.17)

\displaystyle\oint_{x}|g_{x}(u,w)|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w=\frac{1}{\sigma_{n-1}}\cdot\frac{2}{n-1}\,\sigma_{n-2}\cdot\lVert u\rVert_{g_{x}}=k_{n}\lVert u\rVert_{g_{x}}.

The alternative expressions for
k_{n}
follow from the well-known sphere volume formulas

(2.18)

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}=\frac{2\pi}{n-1}\sigma_{n-2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{n}=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{n+1}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{n+1}{2})}.

∎

2.2.
The geodesic flow

For
(x,v)\in TM
we denote by
\gamma_{x,v}\colon\mathbbm{R}\longrightarrow M
the unique geodesic with initial data

(2.19)

\displaystyle\gamma_{x,v}(0)=x,\qquad\dot{\gamma}_{x,v}(0)=v.

Completeness of
(M,g)
ensures that
\gamma_{x,v}
is defined for all
t\in\mathbbm{R}
.
The exponential map at
x
is given by

(2.20)

\displaystyle\exp_{x}\colon T_{x}M\longrightarrow M,\qquad\exp_{x}(v)\coloneqq\gamma_{x,v}(1).

Equivalently,
\gamma_{x,v}(t)=\exp_{x}(tv)
.

The geodesic flow is the flow on the tangent bundle
TM
obtained by following geodesics in phase space.
For
t\in\mathbbm{R}
define

(2.21)

\displaystyle\Phi_{t}\colon TM\longrightarrow TM,\qquad\Phi_{t}(x,v)\coloneqq\bigl(\gamma_{x,v}(t),\dot{\gamma}_{x,v}(t)\bigr).

In terms of the exponential map this can be written as

(2.22)

\displaystyle\Phi_{t}(x,v)=\bigl(\exp_{x}(tv),\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}_{tv}\exp_{x}(v)\bigr).

The flow property
\Phi_{s+t}=\Phi_{s}\circ\Phi_{t}
and
\Phi_{0}=\mathrm{id}_{TM}
is immediate from the uniqueness of geodesics.

Since geodesics preserve the speed of their tangent vectors, the geodesic flow preserves the unit tangent bundle:

(2.23)

\displaystyle\Phi_{t}(SM)\subset SM\qquad\text{for all }t\in\mathbbm{R}.

We shall use the notation

(2.24)

\displaystyle\theta^{t}\coloneqq\Phi_{t}(\theta)\qquad\text{for }\theta\in SM.

The following invariance property is the cornerstone of our approach.
It plays exactly the role that translation invariance plays in Euclidean geometry, and will be used throughout the article.

Proposition 2.3
.

The normalised Liouville measure is invariant under the flow.
In particular, the following statements hold:

a)

Let
G\in L^{1}(SM)
. Then for every
t\in\mathbbm{R}
,

(2.25)

\displaystyle\fint_{SM}G(\theta)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta=\fint_{SM}G(\theta^{t})\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta.

b)

Let
F\in L^{1}(SM\times SM)
.
Then for all
r,s,t\in\mathbbm{R}
,

(2.26)

\displaystyle\fint_{SM}F(\theta^{r},\theta^{s})\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta=\fint_{SM}F(\theta^{r+t},\theta^{s+t})\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta.

c)

Let
f\colon M\times M\longrightarrow\mathbbm{R}
be measurable.
For all
r,s,t\in\mathbbm{R}
,

(2.27)

\displaystyle\int_{M}\oint_{x}f\bigl(\exp_{x}(rw),\exp_{x}(sw)\bigr)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\oint_{x}f\bigl(\exp_{x}((r+t)w),\exp_{x}((s+t)w)\bigr)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x.

The Liouville invariance in (a) is classical and can be proved either in local canonical coordinates on
T^{*}M
using the Hamiltonian description of geodesic flow or directly in
(TM,g)
using the Sasaki metric and divergence-free properties of the geodesic vector field.
We refer to
[
27
, Chapter 1]
for details.

Statement (b) is obtained from (a) by composing
G
with
(\theta,\eta)\longmapsto F(\theta,\eta)
and using the fact that
\Phi_{t}
acts diagonally on the two arguments.
Statement (c) follows from (b) by applying Lemma
2.1
with

(2.28)

\displaystyle F(\theta^{r},\theta^{s})=f\bigl(\pi(\theta^{r}),\pi(\theta^{s})\bigr)=f\bigl(\exp_{x}(rw),\exp_{x}(sw)\bigr),

where
\theta=(x,w)\in SM
.
∎

A frequently used special case of Proposition
2.1
is obtained by taking a function on
M
and lifting it constantly along the fibres of
SM
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(M)
and
\hat{f}\colon SM\longrightarrow\mathbbm{R}
be the constant lift
\hat{f}(x,w)\coloneqq f(x)
.
Then

(2.29)

\displaystyle\fint_{SM}\hat{f}(\theta)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta=\int_{M}f(x)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x,

and for every
r\in\mathbbm{R}
,

(2.30)

\displaystyle\fint_{SM}\bigl|\hat{f}(\theta^{r})-\hat{f}(\theta)\bigr|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta=\int_{M}\oint_{x}\bigl|f(\exp_{x}(rw))-f(x)\bigr|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x.

The first identity is
Section

2.1
applied to the constant lift.
For the second identity, apply

2.3.
The Jacobian of the exponential map

We will later need quantitative control of the Jacobian of the exponential map in Riemannian normal coordinates.
Fix
x\in M
and consider the exponential map
\exp_{x}
defined on a neighbourhood
U\subset T_{x}M
of the origin.
The Jacobian determinant
J_{x}\colon U\to\mathbbm{R}
is defined as the proportionality factor between the pullback of the Riemannian volume form on
M
and the Euclidean volume form on
T_{x}M
, that is

(2.31)

\displaystyle(\exp_{x}^{\ast}\vol_{M})(v)=J_{x}(v)\,\vol_{\mathrm{eucl}},\qquad v\in U,

where
\vol_{\mathrm{eucl}}
is the Euclidean volume form on
T_{x}M\simeq\mathbbm{R}^{n}
induced by
g_{x}
, which coincides with the usual Lebesgue measure on
\mathbbm{R}^{n}
.

Let
y\in M
and let
0<R
be smaller than the injectivity radius at
y
.
Then the change-of-variables formula to polar normal coordinates reads

(2.32)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}(y)}f(x)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x=\sigma_{n-1}\int_{0}^{R}\oint_{y}f(\exp_{y}(rw))J_{y}(rw)r^{n-1}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}r.

In particular, for the round sphere
M=S^{n}
of radius 1 the Jacobian determinant of the exponential map is given by
J_{y}(rw)=\big(\frac{\sin(r)}{r}\big)^{n-1}
and we obtain

(2.33)

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n}\setminus\{\pm y\}}f(x)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x=\sigma_{n-1}\int_{0}^{\pi}\oint_{y}f(\exp_{y}(rw))\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w\ \sin(r)^{n-1}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}r.

The following proposition records the asymptotic expansion we will use later in
subsection 3.5
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
x\in M
and
w\in S_{x}M
.
Then, as
r\to 0
, the Jacobian determinant of the exponential map satisfies

(2.34)

\displaystyle J_{x}(rw)=1-\frac{1}{6}\,\operatorname{Ric}_{x}(w,w)\,r^{2}+\mathcal{O}(r^{3}),

where
\operatorname{Ric}
denotes the Ricci curvature.

See, for example,
[
20
, Chapter XV, Corollary 3.3]
.
∎

Corollary 2.6
.

Assume that
M
is compact.
Then there exist constants
r_{0}>0
and
C>0
such that for all
x\in M
, all
w\in S_{x}M
, and all
r\in[0,r_{0}]
,

(2.35)

\displaystyle J_{x}(rw)\leq 1+Cr^{2}.

Since
M
is compact, the Ricci curvature is bounded on
SM
.
Thus, the
O(r^{3})
term in
Section

2.3
can be chosen uniformly in
(x,w)\in SM
, which yields the existence of

and

with the stated inequality.

3.
Functions of Bounded Variation on Riemannian Manifolds

Throughout this section
(M^{n},g)
is a compact, connected, oriented Riemannian manifold without boundary of dimension
n\geq 2
, equipped with the volume measure
\vol_{g}
induced by the metric
g
.
We first recall the definition and relevant properties of functions of bounded variation on
M
, following
[
24
,
19
]
.
We then introduce two key intermediate objects: the
mean geodesic variation

G_{f}(r)
, which measures the average
L^{1}
-change of
f
along geodesics of length
r
, and the associated
mean geodesic difference quotient

Q_{f}(r)=G_{f}(r)/r
.
The main result (Theorem
3.6
) establishes that
\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}Q_{f}(r)=k_{n}V[f]
, providing a characterisation of
BV(M)
entirely in terms of geodesic behaviour.
The proof proceeds in three steps:
We first establish an upper bound via smooth approximation (
Section

3.4
), then a matching lower bound via radial mollifiers (
Section

3.5
), and finally combine these to obtain the limit (
Section

3.6
).

3.1.
Basic definitions and properties

Denote by
\Gamma(TM)
the space of smooth vector fields on
M
and write
\operatorname{div}(X)
for the divergence of a vector field
X
with respect to
g
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(M)
. The
total variation
of
f
is defined as

(3.1)

\displaystyle V[f]\coloneqq\sup\left\{\int_{M}f\,\operatorname{div}(X)\,\mathrm{d}\vol_{M}:X\in\Gamma(TM),\,\|X(p)\|_{g_{x}}\leq 1\text{ for all }x\in M\right\}.

We say
f\in L^{1}(M)
has
bounded variation
and write
f\in BV(M)
, if
V[f]<\infty
.
We call
\Omega\subset M
a set of finite perimeter if its characteristic function
\ind_{\Omega}\in BV(M)
.
In that case we set
\operatorname{Per}(\Omega)\coloneqq V[\ind_{\Omega}]
.

For differentiable functions
f\in C^{1}(M)
, the total variation coincides with the
L^{1}
-norm of the function’s gradient
V[f]=\lVert\nabla f\rVert_{L^{1}(M)}
(see e.g.
[
19
, p.454]
).
Relatedly, for domains
\Omega
with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
, the perimeter coincides with the
(n-1)
-dimensional Riemannian volume of the boundary.

The space
BV(M)
becomes a Banach space when equipped with the norm

(3.2)

\displaystyle\lVert f\rVert_{BV(M)}\coloneqq\lVert f\rVert_{L^{1}(M)}+V[f],\quad f\in BV(M).

The upcoming results record standard density and compactness properties of
BV(M)
that will be used throughout this article.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
f_{k}\in BV(M)
and
f\in L^{1}(M)
such that
f_{k}\to f
in
L^{1}(M)
. Then

(3.3)

\displaystyle V[f]\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}V[f_{k}].

Let
X\in\Gamma(TM)
such that
\lVert X(p)\rVert_{g(p)}\leq 1
for all
x\in M
.
It follows from the assumed
L^{1}
–convergence that

(3.4)

\displaystyle\int_{M}f\,\operatorname{div}(X)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{g}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{M}f_{k}\,\operatorname{div}(X)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{g}\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}V[f_{k}].

Taking the supremum over all vector fields with
\lVert X(p)\rVert_{g(p)}\leq 1
proves the claim.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Then for every
f\in BV(M)
and for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(M)
such that
\|f-\varphi\|_{L^{1}(M)}+\bigl|V[f]-V[\varphi]\bigr|<\varepsilon
.

See
[
24
, Proposition 1.4]
.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
f_{k}\in BV(M)
such that
\sup_{k}\lVert f_{k}\rVert_{BV(M)}<\infty
.
Then there exists
f\in BV(M)
and a subsequence (not relabeled) such that
f_{k}\to f
in
L^{1}(M)
.

Using
Section

3.1
we find a sequence
\varphi_{k}\in C^{\infty}(M)
such that

(3.5)

\displaystyle\lVert f_{k}-\varphi_{k}\rVert_{L^{1}(M)}+\bigl|V[f_{k}]-V[\varphi_{k}]\bigr|<\frac{1}{k}\ ,\quad k\in\mathbbm{N}.

Thus
\varphi_{k}
is a bounded sequence in the Sobolev space
W^{1,1}(M)
. The Rellich-Kondrachov Theorem (see
[
17
, Theorem 2.9]
) implies that there exists
f\in L^{1}(M)
and a subsequence (not relabeled) such that
\varphi_{k}\to f
in
L^{1}(M)
. Using (
3.5
) we find that
f_{k}\to f
in
L^{1}(M)
. Using
Section

3.1
we find
f\in BV(M)
, which concludes the proof.
∎

The following result by Kreuml-Mordhorst provides a way to calculate the variation of
f\in L^{1}(M)
.
This result is a generalisation of the famous Bourgain-Brezis-Mironsecu formula from the Euclidean setting to Riemannian manifolds (see
[
10
,
2
]
).
It expresses the total variation as a limit of non-local functionals built using radial mollifiers.

A family of
radial mollifiers

(\rho_{s})_{s\in(0,1)}
is a set of functions

(3.6)

\displaystyle\rho_{s}\colon\mathbbm{R}_{\geq 0}\longrightarrow\mathbbm{R}_{\geq 0},\ 0<s<1

satisfying the following properties:

a)

Each
\rho_{s}
is monotonically decreasing on
\mathbbm{R}_{\geq 0}
,

b)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho_{s}(r)\,r^{n-1}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}r=\frac{1}{\sigma_{n-1}}
for all
s\in(0,1)
,

c)

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to 0}\int_{\delta}^{\infty}\rho_{s}(r)\,r^{n-1}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}r=0
for all
\delta>0
,

d)

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to 0}\sup_{r\in K}\rho_{s}(r)=0
for all compact
K\subset\mathbbm{R}_{\geq 0}
.

Theorem 3.5

(
[
19
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(M)
and
\rho_{s}
a family of radial mollifiers.
Then the variation of
f
is given by

(3.7)

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to 0}\int_{M}\int_{M}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{d(x,y)}\,\rho_{s}\big(d(x,y)\big)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y=k_{n}V[f],

where
k_{n}
is the sphere volume ratio introduced in
Section

2.1
.

3.2.
The mean geodesic variation

To connect the total variation
V[f]
with Riemannian geometry, we need a way to measure how
f
varies along geodesics.
The classical difference quotient approach in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
measures
|f(x+h)-f(x)|/|h|
for small displacements
h
.
On a manifold, we replace the displacement
x\longmapsto x+h
with the geodesic flow: we follow geodesics of length
r
from each point
x
in all directions
w\in S_{x}M
and average the resulting variation.
This leads to the following definition.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(M)
.
The
mean geodesic variation
of
f
is the function
G_{f}\colon\mathbbm{R}\longrightarrow\mathbbm{R}
defined for each
r\in\mathbbm{R}
by

(3.8)

\displaystyle G_{f}(r)\coloneqq\fint_{SM}\bigl|\hat{f}(\theta^{r})-\hat{f}(\theta)\bigr|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta,

where
\hat{f}\colon SM\longrightarrow\mathbbm{R}
is the constant lift of
f
.

To make the geometric meaning of this definition more apparent, we may rewrite
G_{f}
in terms of the exponential map using
Section

2.1
.
For each
r\in\mathbbm{R}
, we have

(3.9)

\displaystyle G_{f}(r)=\int_{M}\oint_{x}\bigl|f\bigl(\exp_{x}(rw)\bigr)-f(x)\bigr|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x.

For fixed
x\in M
, the inner integral measures the mean variation of
f
along all geodesics of length
r
emanating from
x
.
The outer integral over
M
then collects these averages over all base points.

It follows from the definition and the properties of the Liouville measure that for each
r\in\mathbbm{R}
the map
f\longmapsto G_{f}(r)
satisfies the triangle inequality
G_{f+g}(r)\leq G_{f}(r)+G_{g}(r)
and is absolutely homogeneous
G_{\alpha f}(r)=|\alpha|G_{f}(r)
.
In other words,
G_{\bullet}
is a one-parameter family of seminorms on
L^{1}(M)
indexed by
r\in\mathbbm{R}
.
By the same arguments, one moreover obtains the reverse triangle inequality:

(3.10)

\displaystyle|G_{f}-G_{g}|\leq G_{f-g},\ f,g\in L^{1}(M).

For fixed
f
and as a function of
r
, the mean geodesic variation has the following properties.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(M)
and
r,s\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
G_{f}
satisfies the following properties:

a)

(Uniform bounds)
0=G_{f}(0)\leq G_{f}(r)\leq 2\lVert f\rVert_{L^{1}(M)}
.

b)

(Reflection invariance)
G_{f}(-r)=G_{f}(r)
.

c)

(Subadditivity)
G_{f}(r+s)\leq G_{f}(r)+G_{f}(s)
.

d)

(Reverse subadditivity)
\bigl|G_{f}(r)-G_{f}(s)\bigr|\leq G_{f}\bigl(|r-s|\bigr)
.

e)

(Continuity)
G_{f}
is continuous on
\mathbbm{R}
.

a)

G_{f}(0)=0
and
0\leq G_{f}(r)
are immediate from the definition.
The triangle inequality and the invariance of the Liouville measure (
Section

2.2
) yield

(3.11)

\displaystyle G_{f}(r)

\displaystyle\leq\fint_{SM}\lvert\hat{f}(\theta^{r})\rvert\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta+\fint_{SM}\lvert\hat{f}(\theta)\rvert\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta=2\lVert f\rVert_{L^{1}(M)}.

b)

The invariance of the Liouville measure under the geodesic flow (
Section

2.2
) implies

(3.12)

\displaystyle G_{f}(-r)

\displaystyle=\fint_{SM}\lvert\hat{f}(\theta^{-r})-\hat{f}(\theta)\rvert\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta=\fint_{SM}\lvert\hat{f}(\theta)-\hat{f}(\theta^{r})\rvert\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta=G_{f}(r).

c)

The triangle inequality and the invariance of the Liouville measure (
Section

2.2
) imply

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}G_{f}(r+s)=\fint_{SM}\lvert\hat{f}(\theta^{r+s})-\hat{f}(\theta)\rvert\,\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta\\
\leq\fint_{SM}\lvert\hat{f}(\theta^{r+s})-\hat{f}(\theta^{s})\rvert\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta+\fint_{SM}\lvert\hat{f}(\theta^{s})-\hat{f}(\theta)\rvert\,\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta=G_{f}(r)+G_{f}(s).\end{multlined}

d)

Replacing
r
by
r-s
in c) yields
G_{f}(r)-G_{f}(s)\leq G_{f}(r-s)=G_{f}(|r-s|)
,
where the last step comes from the symmetry proven in b).
The claim follows after reversing the role of
r
and
s
.

e)

Due to the reverse subadditivity
|G_{f}(r)-G_{f}(s)|\leq G_{f}(|r-s|)
in c), it suffices to show continuity at
r=0
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
.
Since
C^{0}(M)
is dense in
L^{1}(M)
, we can find
g\in C^{0}(M)
such that
\lVert f-g\rVert_{L^{1}(M)}\leq\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2C}
.
Since the geodesic flow is smooth,
G_{g}
is continuous on
\mathbbm{R}_{\geq 0}
.
In particular,
G_{g}(r)\to G_{g}(0)=0
for
r\to 0
.
This allows us to pick
r_{0}>0
such that
G_{g}(r)\leq\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}
for all
0<r<r_{0}
.
Together with the reverse triangle inequality of the seminorm
G_{\bullet}(r)
in (
3.10
) and the homogeneous upper bound in d), we obtain the following estimate for
r\in(0,r_{0})
:

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}G_{f}(r)\leq G_{g}(r)+\bigl|G_{f}(r)-G_{g}(r)\bigr|\leq G_{g}(r)+G_{f-g}(r)\\
\leq G_{g}(r)+C\lVert f-g\rVert_{L^{1}(M)}\leq\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}+C\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2C}=\varepsilon.\end{multlined}

∎

3.3.
The mean geodesic difference quotient

The mean geodesic variation
G_{f}(r)
measures the average
L^{1}
–change of
f
along geodesics.
Normalising
G_{f}(r)
by the length
r
of the geodesics leads to the natural Riemannian analogue of the
L^{1}
–difference quotients used to describe
BV(\mathbbm{R}^{n})
in terms of finite difference quotients
[
12
]
.

Definition 3.8
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(M)
.
The
mean geodesic difference quotient
of
f
is the function
{Q_{f}\colon\mathbbm{R}_{>0}\longrightarrow\mathbbm{R}}
defined for each
r>0
by

(3.17)

\displaystyle Q_{f}(r)\coloneqq\frac{G_{f}(r)}{r}=\fint_{SM}\biggl|\frac{\hat{f}(\theta^{r})-\hat{f}(\theta)}{r}\biggr|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta,

where
\hat{f}\colon SM\longrightarrow\mathbbm{R}
is the constant lift of
f
.

We can again rewrite
Q_{f}
in terms of the exponential map using
Section

2.1
.
More precisely, for each
r>0
, we have

(3.18)

\displaystyle Q_{f}(r)=\int_{M}\oint_{x}\biggl|\frac{f\bigl(\exp_{x}(rw)\bigr)-f(x)}{r}\biggr|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x,

so
Q_{f}
measures the absolute difference quotient of
f
, averaged over all geodesics of length
r
.

Since
G_{f}
is continuous, so is
Q_{f}
except possibly in
r=0
.
In the remaining parts of
section

3
, we analyse
Q_{f}
as a function on
\mathbbm{R}_{>0}
, with particular emphasis on its behaviour as
r\to 0^{+}
.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(M)
. Then

a)

(Continuity)
Q_{f}
is continuous on
\mathbbm{R}_{>0}
.

b)

(Integer Contraction)
Q_{f}(mr)\leq Q_{f}(r)
for all
r\in\mathbbm{R}_{>0}
and
m\in\mathbbm{N}
.

a) is a direct consequence of the continuity of
G_{f}
(
Section

3.2
e).
b) follows by induction on the subadditivity of
G_{f}
yields
G_{f}(mr)\leq mG_{f}(r)
for all
m\in\mathbbm{N}
, from which the claim for
Q_{f}(r)
follows immediately.
∎

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(M)
.
Then the right-sided lower and upper limit of
Q_{f}
for
r\to 0^{+}
agree:

(3.19)

\displaystyle\liminf_{r\to 0^{+}}Q_{f}(r)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{r\to 0^{+}}Q_{f}(r)=\sup_{r>0}Q_{f}(r).

It suffices to show that
Q_{f}(r)\leq\liminf_{s\to 0}Q_{f}(s)
for all
r\in\mathbbm{R}_{>0}
.
Let
r\in\mathbbm{R}_{>0}
and
\varepsilon>0
.
Since
Q_{f}
is continuous at
r
, there is
\delta>0
such that
|Q_{f}(r)-Q_{f}(s)|<\varepsilon
for all
s\in\mathbbm{R}_{>0}
with
|r-s|<\delta
.
Since the limit inferior is an accumulation point, we find
t\in\mathbbm{R}_{>0}
with
t<\delta
such that
|Q_{f}(t)-\liminf_{s\to 0}Q_{f}(s)|<\varepsilon
.
Now, we set
m\coloneqq\lceil\frac{r}{t}\rceil\in\mathbbm{N}
, defined as the unique natural number
m
such that
m-1<\frac{r}{t}\leq m
.
After reshuffling the terms, we obtain
0\leq mt-r<t
and in particular
|mt-r|<\delta
.
Therefore

(3.20)

\displaystyle Q_{f}(r)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon+Q_{f}(mt)\leq\varepsilon+Q_{f}(t)\leq 2\varepsilon+\liminf_{s\to 0}Q_{f}(s).

The first inequality follows from the continuity of
Q
at
r
, the second from
Section

3.3
b), and the third from the definition of limes inferior and the choice of
t
.
As this is true for all
\varepsilon>0
, the claim follows.
∎

3.4.
Upper bound via smooth approximation

It turns out that the mean geodesic difference quotient
Q_{f}
is bounded from above by a multiple of the total variation of
f
(see also
[
12
, Proposition 11]
).
In the smooth setting, the increment
f(\exp_{x}(rw))-f(x)
along a geodesic segment of length
r
can be written as an integral of the differential of
f
along the geodesic flow, and the invariance of the Liouville measure then yields a bound in terms of the
L^{1}
–norm of
\nabla f
, uniform in
r>0
.
This gives a pointwise inequality
Q_{f}(r)\leq k_{n}V[f]
for
C^{1}
–functions.
Using the density of smooth functions in
BV(M)
(
Section

3.1
), we can then transfer this estimate to general
BV
–functions and obtain an upper bound on
\limsup_{r\to 0^{+}}Q_{f}(r)
in terms of the total variation.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(M)
, then for all
r>0
,

(3.21)

\displaystyle Q_{f}(r)\leq k_{n}V[f].

If
f
does not have bounded variation, the inequality is trivially satisfied.
For
f\in BV(M)
, we prove the bound by smooth approximation.

Step 1: The smooth case.

Let
f\in C^{1}(M)
and fix
x\in M
,
w\in T_{x}M
and
r>0
.
We use the geodesic path
\gamma\colon[0,1]\longrightarrow M
given by
\gamma(t)=\exp_{x}(trw)
and the fundamental theorem of calculus to find

(3.22)

\displaystyle f\bigl(\exp_{x}(rw)\bigr)-f(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigl(f\circ\gamma\bigr)(t)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t.

We evaluate the derivative with the chain rule:

(3.23)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigl(f\circ\gamma\bigr)(t)

\displaystyle=\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}_{\gamma(t)}f(\dot{\gamma}(t))=\bigl(\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}_{\exp_{x}(trw)}f\circ\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}_{trw}\exp_{x}\bigr)(rw)=r\,\bigl(\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}f\circ\Phi_{tr}\bigr)(x,w),

where
\Phi
is the geodesic flow on the unit tangent bundle
SM
.
Consequently, we can estimate

(3.24)

\displaystyle Q_{f}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\int_{M}\oint_{x}\bigl|f\bigl(\exp_{x}(rw)\bigr)-f(x)\bigr|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x

(3.25)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{r}\int_{M}\oint_{x}\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigl(f\circ\gamma\bigr)(t)\Bigr|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x

(3.26)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{M}\oint_{x}\bigl|(\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}f\circ\Phi_{tr})(x,w)\bigr|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t

(3.27)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(1)}}{=}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{M}\oint_{x}\bigl|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}f(x,w)\bigr|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t

(3.28)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(2)}}{=}\int_{0}^{1}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t\int_{M}\oint_{x}\bigl|g_{x}\bigl(\nabla f(x),w\bigr)\bigr|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x

(3.29)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(3)}}{=}k_{n}\int_{M}\lVert\nabla f(x)\rVert_{g_{x}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\;=\;k_{n}\,V[f].

Equality (1) is the invariance of the Liouville measure under the geodesic flow
\Phi
(
Section

2.2
).
In equality (2), we first use the definition of the gradient.
Then we note that the integrand is now independent of
t
, so we can perform the integration over
[0,1]
.
Finally,
Section

2.1
yields equality (3).

Step 2: The general case.

Let
f\in BV(M)
,
\varepsilon>0
and
r>0
.
By
Section

3.1
, we can find a smooth function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(M)
such that

(3.30)

\displaystyle\lVert f-\varphi\rVert_{L^{1}(M)}<\frac{r}{4}\varepsilon\quad\text{and}\quad\bigl|V[f]-V[\varphi]\bigr|<\frac{1}{2k_{n}}\varepsilon,\quad\text{in particular }k_{n}V[\varphi]<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+k_{n}V[f].

We then manipulate
Q_{f}(r)
by adding and subtracting
\hat{\varphi}(\theta)
and applying the triangle inequality twice:

(3.31)

\displaystyle Q_{f}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\fint_{SM}\bigl\lvert\hat{f}(\theta^{r})-\hat{\varphi}(\theta^{r})+\hat{\varphi}(\theta^{r})-\hat{\varphi}(\theta)+\hat{\varphi}(\theta)-\hat{f}(\theta)\bigr\rvert\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta

(3.32)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{r}\fint_{SM}\bigl\lvert\hat{f}(\theta^{r})-\hat{\varphi}(\theta^{r})\bigr\rvert\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta+\frac{1}{r}\fint_{SM}\bigl\lvert\hat{\varphi}(\theta^{r})-\hat{\varphi}(\theta)\bigr\rvert\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta+\frac{1}{r}\fint_{SM}\bigl\lvert\hat{\varphi}(\theta)-\hat{f}(\theta)\bigr\rvert\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta

(3.33)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(1)}}{=}\frac{2}{r}\,\lVert f-\varphi\rVert_{L^{1}(M)}\;+\;Q_{\varphi}(r)

(3.34)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(2)}}{\leq}\frac{2}{r}\,\lVert f-\varphi\rVert_{L^{1}(M)}+k_{n}V[\varphi]

(3.35)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(3)}}{\leq}\varepsilon+k_{n}V[f].

Here, the invariance of the Liouville measure under the geodesic flow (
Section

2.2
) provides us with equality (1).
Inequality (2) employs the smooth case proven in Step 1.
Finally, the choice of
\varphi
yields inequality (3).
Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, this is the desired result.
∎

3.5.
Lower bound via radial mollifiers

We will now show that, as
r\to 0^{+}
, the upper limit of the mean geodesic difference quotient is bounded from below by
k_{n}V[f]
.
This is a consequence of the characterisation of
BV(M)
due to Kreuml and Mordhorst
[
19
]
.
By choosing a particularly simple family of mollifiers supported in small geodesic balls, and rewriting the corresponding double integral on
M\times M
in polar normal coordinates, we obtain an average of the geodesic difference quotients
Q_{f}(r)
over small radii.
Uniform control on the Jacobian of the exponential map then allows us to pass to the limit and deduce the desired bound.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(M)
, then

(3.36)

\displaystyle k_{n}V[f]

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{r\to 0}Q_{f}(r).

The following set of piecewise-continuous, bounded functions provides a family of radial mollifier:

(3.37)

\displaystyle\rho_{s}(r)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{n-1}\,s\,r^{n-1}}\ind_{[0,s]}(r).

By the result of Kreuml and Mordhorst (
Section

3.1
), the total variation of
f
is given by

(3.38)

\displaystyle k_{n}V[f]

\displaystyle=\lim_{s\to 0}\int_{M}\int_{M}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{d(x,y)}\,\rho_{s}\big(d(x,y)\big)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y

(3.39)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(1)}}{=}\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{1}{s}\int_{M}\frac{1}{\sigma_{n-1}}\int_{B_{s}(x)}\frac{|f(y)-f(x)|}{d(x,y)^{n}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x

(3.40)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(2)}}{=}\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{1}{s}\int_{M}\int_{0}^{s}\oint_{x}\frac{|f(\exp_{x}(rw))-f(x)|}{r^{n}}\,J_{x}(rw)\,r^{n-1}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x

(3.41)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{(3)}}{\leq}\limsup_{s\to 0}\fint_{0}^{s}\int_{M}\oint_{x}\frac{|f(\exp_{x}(rw))-f(x)|}{r}(1+Cr^{2})\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}r

(3.42)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{s\to 0}\sup_{0<r<s}\bigg\{\int_{M}\oint_{x}\frac{|f(\exp_{x}(rw))-f(x)|}{r}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\bigg\}\underbrace{\fint_{0}^{s}(1+Cr^{2})\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}r}_{=1+\frac{C}{3}s^{2}}

(3.43)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{r\to 0}\int_{M}\oint_{x}\frac{|f(\exp_{x}(rw))-f(x)|}{r}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x

(3.44)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{r\to 0}Q(r).

Equality (1) uses the definition of
\rho_{s}
, equality (2) expresses the integration in polar normal coordinates using the formula (
2.32
), and inequality (3) uses that
M
is compact, such that the bound of
Section

2.3
holds.
∎

3.6.
Total variation as limit of the geodesic difference quotient

We are now in the position to relate the mean geodesic difference quotient
Q_{f}
to the total variation
V[f]
.
The next result shows that, for every
f\in L^{1}(M)
, the right-sided limit
\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}Q_{f}(r)
coincides with
k_{n}V[f]
(possibly
+\infty
).

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(M)
, then

(3.45)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}Q_{f}(r)=k_{n}V[f].

Since the lower and upper limit agree according to
Section

3.3
, the claim follows by combining the pointwise upper bound in
Section

3.4
and the lower bound on the limsup in

Section

3.5
.
∎

An immediate consequence of Theorem
3.6
is a characterisation of the space
BV(M)
in terms of the mean geodesic variation
G_{f}
:

Theorem 3.14
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(M)
.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

a)

f\in BV(M)
.

b)

G_{f}
is Lipschitz continuous.

In that case, the right-sided derivative of
G_{f}
in
r=0
exists and takes the value

(3.46)

\displaystyle G^{\prime}_{f}(0)=\|G^{\prime}_{f}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbbm{R})}=k_{n}V[f].

Assume that
f\in BV(M)
. We use
Section

3.2
c),
Section

3.3
and
Section

3.6
to obtain

(3.47)

\displaystyle\bigl|G_{f}(r)-G_{f}(s)\bigr|\leq G_{f}\bigl(|r-s|\bigr)\leq|r-s|\,\sup_{|r-s|>0}Q_{f}\bigl(|r-s|\bigr)=k_{n}V[f]\,|r-s|

for all

. Thus
G_{f}
is Lipschitz continuous.

Assume now that
G_{f}
is Lipschitz continuous. Then we find
L>0
such that

(3.48)

\displaystyle|G_{f}(r)-G_{f}(s)|\leq L\,|r-s|

\displaystyle\text{for all }r,s\in\mathbbm{R}.

Choosing

and using

3.6
we find that

(3.49)

\displaystyle k_{n}V[f]=\limsup_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{G_{f}(r)}{r}\leq L,

which shows that

. The formula for the right-sided derivative now follows from
Section

3.6
.

To obtain the

-estimate, we note that since

is Lipschitz, it is also differentiable almost everywhere by Rademacher’s theorem. Let
r>0
such that

is differentiable in

3.2
we find that

(3.50)

\displaystyle|G_{f}^{\prime}(r)|=\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{|G_{f}(r+h)-G_{f}(r)|}{h}\leq\liminf_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{G_{f}(h)}{h}=k_{n}\,V[f],

which concludes the proof.
∎

4.
The Riemannian Autocorrelation Function

In this section, we introduce and study the autocorrelation function on compact, connected, oriented Riemannian manifolds without boundary
(M^{n},g)
of dimension
n\geq 2
.
Using the theory developed in
section

3
, we show that the autocorrelation function defined on manifolds shares the key properties of its Euclidean counterpart.

The Euclidean autocorrelation function
c_{\Omega}^{\mathrm{Eucl}}(r)
in (
1.2
) arises by averaging the covariogram
C_{\Omega}^{\text{Eucl}}(h)=\vol(\Omega\cap(\Omega+h))
over all translations
h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
of length
r
.
On a general manifold, however, there is no global notion of translation, and hence no canonical covariogram to average.
One natural approach is to generalise the covariogram to homogeneous spaces, i.e. manifolds that are equipped with a transitive group action that can replace the notion of translations.
But the existence of such an action is a restrictive assumption that may exclude many geometrically interesting manifolds.
We instead bypass the covariogram entirely and define the autocorrelation function essentially by changing the order of integration: first average over geodesics of length
r
emanating in all directions, then integrate over all starting points.
This is naturally realised by following the geodesic flow on the unit tangent bundle
SM
and integrating with respect to the Liouville measure, which captures the simultaneous average over all directions and all starting points.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset M
be measurable and let
\widehat{\Omega}\coloneqq\pi^{-1}(\Omega)
be its preimage with respect to the canonical projection
\pi\colon SM\longrightarrow M
.
We define the
autocorrelation function

c_{\Omega}\colon\mathbbm{R}\longrightarrow\mathbbm{R}
for all
r\in\mathbbm{R}
via

(4.1)

\displaystyle c_{\Omega}(r)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\fint_{SM}\mspace{-7mu}\ind_{\widehat{\Omega}}(\theta)\ind_{\widehat{\Omega}}(\theta^{r})\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta,

or equivalently,

(4.2)

\displaystyle c_{\Omega}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\oint_{x}\ind_{\Omega}(x)\ind_{\Omega}\bigl(\exp_{x}(rw)\bigr)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x.

The autocorrelation function
c_{\Omega}(r)
captures all the geometric information about how
\Omega
intersects with itself, on average, under the geodesic flow.
More precisely, the intersection between any two geodesic translates
\Phi_{r}\widehat{\Omega}
and
\Phi_{s}\widehat{\Omega}
depends only on their relative geodesic displacement
r-s
, not on the individual parameters
r
and
s
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset M
be measurable.
Then for all
r,s\in\mathbbm{R}

(4.3)

\displaystyle\overline{\smash{\mathrm{vol}}\raisebox{0.0pt}{}}\vphantom{\mathrm{vol}}\bigl(\Phi_{r}\widehat{\Omega}\cap\Phi_{s}\widehat{\Omega}\bigr)=c_{\Omega}(r-s).

In particular, the value of the autocorrelation function at
r
is given by
c_{\Omega}(r)=\overline{\smash{\mathrm{vol}}\raisebox{0.0pt}{}}\vphantom{\mathrm{vol}}\bigl(\widehat{\Omega}\cap\Phi_{r}\widehat{\Omega}\bigr)
.

By definition we have

(4.4)

\displaystyle\overline{\smash{\mathrm{vol}}\raisebox{0.0pt}{}}\vphantom{\mathrm{vol}}\bigl(\Phi_{r}\widehat{\Omega}\cap\Phi_{s}\widehat{\Omega}\bigr)=\fint_{SM}\ind_{\widehat{\Omega}}(\theta^{r})\ind_{\widehat{\Omega}}(\theta^{s})\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta=\fint_{SM}\ind_{\widehat{\Omega}}(\theta^{r-s})\ind_{\widehat{\Omega}}(\theta)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\theta=c_{\Omega}(r-s),

where the second equality is exactly the invariance of the Liouville measure under the geodesic flow described in
Section

2.2
, while the final equality is again by definition.
∎

The following formula, analogous to Matheron’s classical result
[
21
]
(see also
[
12
, Lemma 10]
), is the crucial link that connects the autocorrelation function
c_{\Omega}
to the mean geodesic variation
G_{\chi_{\Omega}}
.
This relationship enables us to translate properties of
G_{f}
into properties of
c_{\Omega}
and ultimately to characterise sets of finite perimeter via the autocorrelation function.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
r\in\mathbbm{R}
and
\Omega\subset M
be measurable.
Then

(4.5)

\displaystyle c_{\Omega}(0)-c_{\Omega}(r)=\tfrac{1}{2}G_{\ind_{\Omega}}(r).

Since the difference of two characteristic functions only takes the values
\{0,\pm 1\}
, we find that

(4.6)

\displaystyle\left|\ind_{\Omega}(q)-\ind_{\Omega}(q^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle=\left(\ind_{\Omega}(q)-\ind_{\Omega}(q^{\prime})\right)^{2}=\ind_{\Omega}(q)^{2}-2\ind_{\Omega}(q)\ind_{\Omega}(q^{\prime})+\ind_{\Omega}(q^{\prime})^{2}

Setting
q^{\prime}\coloneqq x
and
q\coloneqq\exp_{x}(rw)
allows us to rewrite the upper integral as follows:

(4.7)

\displaystyle\int_{M}\oint_{x}\bigl|\ind_{\Omega}\bigl(\exp_{x}(rw)\bigr)-\ind_{\Omega}(x)\bigr|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=c_{\Omega}(0)-2\,c_{\Omega}(r)+c_{\Omega}(0)

(4.8)

\displaystyle=2\,c_{\Omega}(0)-2\,c_{\Omega}(r).

∎

The following properties follow almost immediately from
Section

4
and the theory developed in
section

3
. They are consistent with the properties of the autocorrelation function with flat underlying geometry (see
[
18
,
4
]
).

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\Omega\subset M
be measurable and
r,s\in\mathbbm{R}
.
Then the autocorrelation function
c_{\Omega}
satisfies the following properties.

a)

(Uniform Bounds)
0\leq c_{\Omega}(r)\leq c_{\Omega}(0)=|\Omega|
.

b)

(Reflection invariance)
c_{\Omega}(r)=c_{\Omega}(-r)
.

c)

(Sum Estimate)
c_{\Omega}(r)+c_{\Omega}(s)\leq c_{\Omega}(0)+c_{\Omega}(r-s)
.

d)

(Pre-Lipschitz estimate)
|c_{\Omega}(r)-c_{\Omega}(s)|\leq c_{\Omega}(0)-c_{\Omega}(r-s)
.

e)

(Complement relation)
c_{\Omega^{c}}(r)=|M|-2|\Omega|+c_{\Omega}(r)
.

Using Matheron’s formula established by
Section

4
, statements a)–d) follow immediately from the corresponding properties of the geodesic variation in
Section

3.2
.
The last assertion follows from the relation
\ind_{\Omega^{c}}=1-\ind_{\Omega}
together with a direct computation.
∎

We are now in the position to state the characterisation of sets of finite perimeter in terms of the autocorrelation function.
As a reminder, we say that
\Omega\subset M
is a set of finite perimeter, if
\ind_{\Omega}\in\mathrm{BV}(M)
. In that case we write
\operatorname{Per}(\Omega)\coloneqq V[\ind_{\Omega}]
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\Omega\subset M
be measurable.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

a)

\Omega
is a set of finite perimeter.

b)

The autocorrelation function
c_{\Omega}
is Lipschitz continuous.

In that case, the right-sided derivative at
r=0
is

(4.9)

\displaystyle c^{\prime}_{\Omega}(0)=-\lVert c^{\prime}_{\Omega}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbbm{R}_{\geq 0})}=-\tfrac{1}{2}k_{n}\operatorname{Per}(\Omega).

For any
r>0
,
Section

4
states that
c_{\Omega}(0)-c_{\Omega}(r)=\frac{1}{2}G_{\ind_{\Omega}}(r)
. The theorem then follows from
Section

3.6
.
∎

We remark that the constant factor
\frac{k_{n}}{2}
is the exact same constant as in the case of flat spaces (see
[
12
,
18
,
4
]
). In other words: no geometric information is stored in this constant (other than dimension). Instead, the influence of the Riemannian metric on the autocorrelation function to first order is stored in the perimeter.

5.
Application to Pattern Formation

In this section, we analyse the asymptotic behaviour of non-local isoperimetric energies on
S^{n}
as the interaction length scale
\varepsilon\to 0
, using the techniques developed above. More precisely, let
n\in\mathbbm{N}
,
n\geq 2
, and let
M=S^{n}
be the
n
-dimensional unit sphere equipped with the round metric
g
. We denote by
d(x,y)
the corresponding geodesic distance between
x,y\in M
, and by
\mathrm{d}x,\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y
etc. the corresponding volume form. Let
0<\theta<|M|
and define the class of admissible sets

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}\coloneqq\{\Omega\subset M:\ind_{\Omega}\in BV(M),\ |\Omega|=\theta\}.

Let
\gamma,\varepsilon>0
. We define the non-local isoperimetric energy

\displaystyle E_{\gamma,\varepsilon}(\Omega)\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\operatorname{Per}(\Omega)-\frac{\gamma}{\varepsilon}\int_{M\times M}K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\,\bigl|\ind_{\Omega}(x)-\ind_{\Omega}(y)\bigr|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y&\quad\text{if }\Omega\in\mathfrak{A},\\[10.0pt]
+\infty&\quad\text{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

where
K_{\varepsilon}\colon M\times M\longrightarrow\mathbbm{R}
is the fundamental solution of the Helmholtz equation

(5.4)

\displaystyle K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)-\varepsilon^{2}\Delta_{x}K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=\delta_{y}

\displaystyle\text{for all }x,y\in M,

where
\Delta
denotes the Laplace-Beltrami operator with respect to the metric
g
.
The parameter
\gamma
controls the strength of the non-local interaction relative to the perimeter, while
\varepsilon
sets the length scale of the interaction.
Such energies arise in models of pattern formation where systems tend to minimise interfaces while maintaining interactions between separated regions
[
4
]
.

We define the quantity
\gamma_{\mathrm{crit}}\geq 0
via

(5.5)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mathrm{crit}}\coloneqq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\gamma_{\varepsilon},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}\coloneqq k_{n}\int_{M}\frac{d(x,y)}{\varepsilon}\,K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x

for some
y\in M
. We show in
Section

5.1
that this quantity is independent of
y\in M
and
0<\gamma_{\mathrm{crit}}<\infty
is given explicitly. In this section, we prove the following
\Gamma
–convergence result regarding the non-local isoperimetric energy
E_{\gamma,\varepsilon}
, where for two measurable sets
\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}\subset M
, we denote the symmetric difference by
\Omega\bigtriangleup\Omega^{\prime}\coloneqq(\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime})\cup(\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\Omega)
and its volume by
|\Omega\bigtriangleup\Omega^{\prime}|
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
0<\gamma<\gamma_{{\mathrm{crit}}}
. Then
E_{\gamma,\varepsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma}}{{\longrightarrow}}E_{\gamma,0}
in the
L^{1}
topology, where

\displaystyle E_{\gamma,0}(\Omega)\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\bigl(1-\frac{\gamma}{\gamma_{{\mathrm{crit}}}}\bigr)\operatorname{Per}(\Omega)&&\quad\text{if }\Omega\in\mathfrak{A},\\[6.0pt]
+\infty&&\quad\text{else.}\end{array}\right.

More precisely:

•

Liminf inequality:
For every measurable
\Omega\subset M
and for every sequence of measurable sets
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\subset M
such that
|\Omega\bigtriangleup\Omega_{\varepsilon}|\to 0
we have

(5.8)

\displaystyle E_{\gamma,0}(\Omega)\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}E_{\gamma,\varepsilon}(\Omega_{\varepsilon}).

•

Limsup inequality:
For every measurable
\Omega\subset M
there exists a sequence of measurable sets
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\subset M
such that
|\Omega\bigtriangleup\Omega_{\varepsilon}|\to 0
and

(5.9)

\displaystyle E_{\gamma,0}(\Omega)\geq\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}E_{\gamma,\varepsilon}(\Omega_{\varepsilon}).

The strategy to prove
Section

5
is as follows. In
Section

5.2
and
Section

5.2
, we reformulate the energy in terms of the autocorrelation function (similar to
[
4
, Lemma 4.2]
, see also
[
18
, Proposition 2.6]
). Using the sharp bounds on the autocorrelation function, we find optimal lower bounds of the energy in terms of the perimeter functional. Compactness (see
Section

5.2
) as well as the liminf inequality follow from these sharp bounds. In addition, this reformulation quantifies the error from the limit functional to zeroth order (see also
[
25
, Lemma 3.1]
). For the limsup inequality, we again use the reformulation in terms of the autocorrelation function together with explicit computations regarding integrals of the Helmholtz kernel. We find the pointwise limit of the energy
E_{\gamma,\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
(see
Section

5.2
) and use the constant sequence as a recovery sequence to conclude the proof of
Section

5
.

We remark that the the case of the sphere with radius
R
follows from the case of the unit sphere by rescaling.
To be more precise, consider the the energy

(5.10)

\displaystyle E^{(R)}_{\gamma,\varepsilon}(\Omega)\coloneqq\operatorname{Per}^{(R)}(\Omega)-\frac{\gamma}{\varepsilon}\int_{M^{(R)}\times M^{(R)}}K_{\varepsilon}^{(R)}(x,y)\,|\ind_{\Omega}(x)-\ind_{\Omega}(y)|\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x^{(R)}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y^{(R)},

where
M^{(R)}\coloneqq(S^{n},R^{2}g)
is the
n
-dimensional round sphere of radius
R
,
\operatorname{Per}^{(R)}
denotes the perimeter with respect to the metric
R^{2}g
,
\mathrm{d}x^{(R)},\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y^{(R)}
denote the volume forms with respect to the metric
R^{2}g
, and
K_{\varepsilon}^{(R)}\colon M^{(R)}\times M^{(R)}\longrightarrow\mathbbm{R}
is the solution of

(5.11)

\displaystyle K_{\varepsilon}^{(R)}(\ \cdot\ ,y)-\varepsilon^{2}\Delta^{(R)}K_{\varepsilon}^{(R)}(\ \cdot\ ,y)=\delta_{y},

where
\Delta^{(R)}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator corresponding to the metric
R^{2}g
. Simple calculations reveal that

(5.12)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Per}^{(R)}=R^{n-1}\operatorname{Per},

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x^{(R)}=R^{n}\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle K_{\varepsilon}^{(R)}=\frac{1}{R^{n}}K_{\varepsilon/R},

and thus

(5.13)

\displaystyle E^{(R)}_{\gamma,\varepsilon}=R^{n-1}E_{\gamma,\frac{\varepsilon}{R}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma}}{{\longrightarrow}}R^{n-1}\big(1-\frac{\gamma}{\gamma_{\mathrm{crit}}}\big)\operatorname{Per}=\big(1-\frac{\gamma}{\gamma_{\mathrm{crit}}}\big)\operatorname{Per}^{(R)}.

In particular, the value
\gamma_{\mathrm{crit}}
is independent of the radius of the sphere.

5.1.
Kernel on the Sphere

In this section, we take a closer look at the Helmholtz kernel
K_{\varepsilon}\colon M\times M\longrightarrow\mathbbm{R}
defined as the unique solution to the equation

(5.14)

\displaystyle K_{\varepsilon}(\ \cdot\ ,y)-\varepsilon^{2}\Delta K_{\varepsilon}(\ \cdot\ ,y)=\delta_{y}

\displaystyle\text{in }M,

where the equation is to be understood in the distributional sense, and
\delta_{y}
denotes the Dirac distribution with support
y\in M
. The operator
\Delta
denotes the Laplace-Beltrami operator (with respect to the round metric). It follows from standard elliptic theory that there exists a unique solution to the equation (
5.14
) which is smooth outside the diagonal (see also
[
9
, Theorem 4.8]
where the solution is given explicitly).

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and let
K_{\varepsilon}
be the Helmholtz kernel. Then:

a)

\displaystyle\int_{M}K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x=1
for all
y\in M
.

b)

K_{\varepsilon}
only depends on the distance, i.e.
K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=K_{\varepsilon}\bigl(d(x,y)\bigr)
for all
x,y\in M
.

c)

K_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
.

d)

As
\varepsilon\to 0
it holds

(5.15)

\displaystyle\int_{M}\frac{d(x,y)}{\varepsilon}K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y+\int_{M}\frac{d(x,y)^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y=\mathcal{O}(1).

The first assertion immediately follows from testing (
5.14
) with the constant function.
Assertion b) follows from the explicit representation of the Helmholtz kernel proved in
[
9
, Theorem 4.8]
.

To show assertion c), we note that since the integral over
K_{\varepsilon}(\ \cdot\ ,y)
is positive, we know that the set
U_{y}\coloneqq\{x\in M:K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\geq 0\}\neq\emptyset
. Let
V_{y}\coloneqq\{x\in M:K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\leq 0\}
. If
V_{y}=\emptyset
then there is nothing to show. If
V_{y}\neq\emptyset
, it is easy to see that
y\notin V_{y}
. Moreover
V_{y}
is compact and

(5.16)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\Delta_{x}K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\leq 0

\displaystyle\text{for all }x\in V_{y},

so
K_{\varepsilon}(\ \cdot\ ,y)
is superharmonic in
V_{y}
. It follows from the strong maximum principle (see e.g.
[
29
, Theorem 7.1.7]
) that
K_{\varepsilon}(\ \cdot\ ,y)=0
in
V_{y}
, and thus
K_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
.

To see assertion d), we first note that it suffices to show the bounds for only one
y\in M
since the kernel only depends on the distance. We test (
5.14
) with
\varphi(x)\coloneqq\frac{d^{2}(x,y)}{\varepsilon^{2}}
and find

(5.17)

\displaystyle\int_{M}\frac{d(x,y)^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x=\int_{M}\Delta_{x}(d(x,y)^{2})\,K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x.

Using the identity
\Delta_{x}d(x,y)^{2}=2+2(n-1)\,d(x,y)\,\cot(d(x,y))
(see e.g.
[
16
, example 3.23, eq. (3.84)]
), we further compute

(5.18)

\displaystyle\int_{M}\Delta_{x}(d(x,y)^{2})\,K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=2+2(n-1)\int_{M_{+}}d(x,y)\,\cot(d(x,y))\,K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x

(5.19)

\displaystyle\quad+2(n-1)\int_{M_{-}}d(x,y)\,\cot(d(x,y))\,K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x,

where
M_{+}=B_{\frac{\pi}{2}}(y)
and
M_{-}=M\setminus M_{+}
. For the integration in
M_{+}
, we note that
r\longmapsto r\cot(r)
is bounded by
1
for
0<r<\frac{\pi}{2}
, and thus

(5.20)

\displaystyle\int_{M_{+}}d(x,y)\,\cot(d(x,y))\,K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{M_{+}}K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\leq 1.

For the integral over
M_{-}
, we note that since
K_{\varepsilon}\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}0
pointwise, for every
\varepsilon_{0}>0
there exists a constant
\mathfrak{m}(\varepsilon_{0})>0
such that
K_{\varepsilon}\leq\mathfrak{m}
in
M_{-}
for every
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
. Thus we find after employing polar coordinates (see (
2.33
))

(5.21)

\displaystyle\int_{M_{-}}d(x,y)\,\cot(d(x,y))\,K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\mathfrak{m}\int_{M_{-}}d(x,y)\,\cot(d(x,y))\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x

(5.22)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{m}\,\sigma_{n-1}\int_{\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\pi}r\cot(r)\sin(r)^{n-1}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}r<\infty,

where we recall
\sigma_{n-1}
is the volume of the unit sphere (see (
2.18
)). Altogether, the second moment is uniformly bounded provided that
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
. Having obtained the uniform bound on the second moment, we find that

(5.23)

\displaystyle\int_{M}\frac{d(x,y)}{\varepsilon}K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(y)}\frac{d(x,y)}{\varepsilon}K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{M\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(y)}\frac{d(x,y)}{\varepsilon}K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x

(5.24)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{\varepsilon}(y)}K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{M\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(y)}\frac{d(x,y)^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}K_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x,

and thus is uniformly bounded provided
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
.
∎

We now show that the first moments are converging and thus, that the critical value
