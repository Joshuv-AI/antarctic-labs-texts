Political breakups: Interpersonal consequences of polarization
Authors: Mertcan Güngör, Peter H Ditto, David Campbell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe explored the phenomenon of “political breakups,” i.e. losing relationships with friends, family members, romantic partners, or others due to political differences. We studied the conditions surrounding political breakups in the United States across four datasets (combined n = 3,791) and supplemented our findings using a proxy measure from the American National Election Studies. In a survey we conducted in April 2025, we found that 37% of Americans reported having had a political breakup, mostly with friends. The prevalence of breakups in the United States appears to have increased since 2016, although evidence is limited. Across all datasets, Democrats were more likely to have experienced a breakup compared with Republicans and were usually the initiator. Political breakups were associated with increased partisan hostility, above and beyond strength of partisanship: Those who reported breakups had more negative feelings toward their political opponents (especially voters rather than party elites), perceived them as more extreme, and ascribed selfish views to them. We discuss potential causes and call for more research on this phenomenon.Keywords: affective polarization, interpersonal relationships, political psychology

It is common, though not ideal, for politicians to be in heated conflict. It is much more concerning when ordinary citizens are divided by politics. We found that more than a third of Americans reported that they have lost relationships with friends, family, romantic partners, and coworkers over political differences. Those who lost relationships were more hostile toward their political opponents, voters more so than party elites. Given the role of exposure to opposing views in building political tolerance, these “political breakups” are a troubling sign for the health of a democracy. And given the importance of relationships for well-being, they have implications for the health of citizens as well. Our findings demonstrate how political polarization impacts our everyday lives.

Introduction

Political polarization is driving Americans apart. As Democrats and Republicans report increasingly negative feelings toward each other in surveys (1), they also have less contact in real life. Partisans prefer to associate with fellow co-partisans within their workplace (2–5), neighborhoods (6–8), and marriages (9). People's tendency to seek others with similar politics is stronger than their tendency to seek similarity in other characteristics (e.g. education) (9), likely because they see their political views as a reflection of their core moral values (10, 11). Polarization on what are seen as moral issues has important political implications, such as increased endorsement of anti-democratic practices to suppress one's morally depraved political opponents (12). But its effects are also felt interpersonally as political identities and the animosity accompanying them become increasingly salient in our everyday lives.

Screening out strangers based on their politics is relatively costless for individuals, and they prefer doing so when they know about the political views of a prospective friend (13, 14) or romantic partner (15). Political differences can also fray existing relationships with friends (14), romantic partners (16–18), and family (19–21), but their damage is not limited to reduced relationship satisfaction. Sometimes, politics can strain relationships to the point of terminating them, with important consequences for society as well as individuals. Losing someone with different views may rob people of a chance to learn the reasons and experiences behind those views, making them less tolerant toward their political opponents (22–25). Given the importance of relationships for physical and mental health (26–30), losing one over political disagreement would be a clear instance where politics can have a direct negative impact on the well-being of citizens. Despite these implications, previous research has paid little attention to when and how people make the costly decision to part ways with friends or family over politics.

In this report, we explore the phenomenon of “political breakups,” i.e. losing a relationship with a friend, family member, or romantic partner due to political differences. We present results from three samples that reflect the US population and another sample representative of a particularly “purple” county in the United States (total n = 3,791; Table 1). Across multiple datasets, we investigate the prevalence of political breakups; when, how, and with whom they happen; their predictors (e.g. partisanship); and related attitudes they can potentially impact (e.g. perceptions of political opponents). We supplement our findings using a proxy measure from the American National Election Studies that focuses on family relationships.

Results

We found that 37% of Americans reported having had a political breakup in a survey we fielded in April 2025 with YouGov (Study 1); this was our most recent and the highest estimate (see Table 1 for other datasets). Of those who reported political breakups, 62% had a breakup with a friend (23% of the full sample), 40% with a family member (15% of the sample), 29% with a coworker (11% of the sample), and 10% with a romantic partner (4% of the sample); most (56%) reported losing more than one kind of relationship (21% of the sample). In another survey we conducted a week before the 2024 election (Study 2), we asked people about their most impactful political breakup. Most of these breakups (69%) happened with a friend, whereas 20% happened with a family member, 5% with a romantic partner, and 2% with a coworker (Tables S1–S3).

It is a challenge to evaluate historical trends in political breakups, as they have not been directly and repeatedly measured in previous surveys. However, our limited evidence is suggestive of an increase in breakups in recent years. In Study 2, 96% of those who reported political breakups recalled that their most impactful breakup happened in 2016 or later. Figure 1A shows an overall increase in recalled breakups in recent years, with peaks in the years when presidential elections were held. It is plausible that earlier breakups were underreported because they were less likely to be remembered or because they had more time to be remedied by the time of measurement, even though these do not explain the sharp trend starting in 2016. To test whether there was a genuine increase between election years, we specifically asked about breakups that resulted from the 2024 presidential election in Study 1 and compared it with another national survey from October 2017 (Study 3). Almost a year after the 2016 election, 14% of Americans reported breakups over it, whereas only 5.5 months after the 2024 election, 18% reported consequent breakups (Figure 1B; difference between intercepts: Z = −2.94, P = 0.003). Surpassing the previous election's rate in half the time would suggest an increase if we assumed that breakups due to elections kept adding up over time in a linear fashion. But it is possible that politics becomes less salient in people's lives farther past the election, allowing some of the breakups to be mended. To rule out the potential effect of proximity to elections, we compared responses from the ANES Time Series Studies (31, 32) that were collected during similar timeframes after the 2020 and 2024 elections, using an item about family relationships as a proxy. After the 2020 election, 33% of Americans reported that their family relationships were hurt by political differences to some extent (“A little” to “A great deal”) in the last 4 years (n = 7,359), whereas 39% of them did so after the 2024 election (n = 4,887). We also compared the responses from the panel participants who responded at both waves (n = 2,035) and found a small but significant increase in hurt family relationships from 2020 to 2024 (b = 0.07, SE = 0.029, P = 0.021).

We found persistent partisan differences in the prevalence of political breakups. In Study 1, 47% of Democrats reported having lost relationships, compared with just 29% of Republicans and 39% of Independents (Table 1). Democrats were more likely to report having had a political breakup even after controlling for strength of partisanship and demographic characteristics on which Democrats and Republicans might differ (estimate for the 7-point party identification measure: odds ratio [OR] = 0.87, 95% CI [0.81–0.94], P < 0.001). We observed the same partisan asymmetry in Studies 2 (OR = 0.85, 95% CI [0.78–0.93], P < 0.001), 3 (OR = 0.75, 95% CI [0.63–0.88], P < 0.001), and 4 (OR = 0.88, 95% CI [0.81–0.97], P = 0.009; Table S4), as well as in the 2024 ANES data using the proxy measure (OR = 0.87, 95% CI [0.84–0.90], P < 0.001; Table S5). Interest in politics and identifying outside of the gender binary (whenever they were available in datasets) were also associated with an increased chance of reporting a breakup (Tables S6 and S7).

In Study 2, we asked how the breakups happened. Those who had breakups were more likely to report initiating the breakup (48%) than either being on the receiving end (27%) or having a mutual breakup (20%; Table S8). We observed partisan differences here as well. Most Democrats (66%) reported they were the ones who ended the relationship, compared with only 27% of Republicans (Table S9).

We also asked participants about where they would place the person they broke up with on the political spectrum (Study 2). Most Democrats (62%) and Republicans (61%) characterized the other person as being on the extreme ends of the spectrum (i.e. very conservative/liberal). Independents were more likely to have a breakup with someone they perceived as conservative (57%) than liberal (39%; Table S10, Figure S1).

Political breakups were associated with having more negative attitudes toward political opponents, even after adjusting for strength of partisanship. In Study 2, we found that those who lost relationships felt colder toward the opposing candidate (b = −3.64, SE = 1.01, P < 0.001) and their voters (b = −7.87, SE = 1.63, P < 0.001). Interestingly, breakups predicted a larger increase in dislike toward voters than those toward the candidate (difference between coefficients: b = 4.47, SE = 1.53, P = 0.011). And among people who had political breakups, those who initiated the breakup had an even stronger dislike of opposing voters (b = −11.1, SE = 2.51, P < 0.001), whereas they were not particularly loathsome toward opposing candidates (b = −2.86, SE = 1.47, P = 0.052; difference between coefficients: b = 8.19, SE = 2.25, P = 0.001; Table S12). Breakups had no significant relationship with feelings toward one's preferred candidate (b = 0.53, SE = 1.81, P = 0.768) or their voters (b = 1.27, SE = 1.48, P = 0.289; Table S11, Figure 2). These differential associations show that political breakups are not just a symptom of being a strong partisan; they entail a specific kind of animosity toward ordinary people from the other side.

Those who reported political breakups also had more negative perceptions about their opponents’ beliefs and motives (Table 2). In Study 3, participants reported their agreement with certain views and then estimated the percentage of Democrats and Republicans who agree with those views. In line with previous research, partisans exaggerated the extremity of their opponents’ views (33–36), but those who lost relationships as a result of the 2016 election exaggerated even more (Figure S2). For instance, when asked to estimate the percentage of Republicans who agreed with White nationalists, Democrats who reported breakups gave estimates that were, on average, 12.6 percentage points higher than their co-partisans’ (who already overestimated by 31 percentage points). When asked to guess the percentage of Democrats who thought that most White people in America are racist, Republicans who reported breakups gave estimates that were 14.6 percentage points higher than their co-partisans’ (who already overestimated by 30 percentage points). In Study 4, those who had breakups were 13% more likely than their fellow partisans to say that those who voted for the opposing candidate had selfish motives, rather than say they had good intentions at heart.

Putting the numbers in perspective

There are some points to keep in mind when interpreting our prevalence estimates. Except for Study 3, all of our data came from online opt-in panels. Even though our samples were stratified and weighted to reflect the population, these widely used panels tend to include participants who are more interested in politics compared with the general population and that might have inflated our estimates. Online surveys may also raise concerns about overestimation of the prevalence of political breakups due to careless responding (37, 38) or trolling (39). We tried to address the latter two concerns in our supplementary analyses and did not find evidence of a significant threat to our estimates (Appendix S1).

All of our data were self-reported with known limitations; we will briefly discuss the cognitive and motivational factors that may impact the reporting of political breakups. As mentioned, earlier breakups may be harder to remember than more recent ones, and those that ended more distant relationships (e.g. acquaintances) are harder to remember than closer ones (e.g. family members). Reporting of such breakups may vary based on the question format (see Appendix S2 for a discussion). Also, our estimates only include those who were “aware” that the relationship ended due to politics, but the two partners may interpret the breakup differently, depending on how it was initiated (40). Friendships, for example, are typically dissolved by gradually reducing contact with the other person rather than direct confrontation (41–45). Under such circumstances, those on the receiving end of a breakup might not necessarily know that the relationship ended or that it ended due to politics, biasing our prevalence estimates downward.

The potential effects of socially desirable responding must also be considered. Even when people recognize a political breakup correctly, they may refrain from reporting it (46–50), especially if they are the ones being broken up with (30). Those who put a higher moral value on maintaining certain relationships (e.g. family) may be particularly hesitant to report political breakups (Appendix S3; Table S19). On the other hand, some partisans may see political breakups as a badge of honor and over report them (51). In our supplemental analyses, we found evidence that suggests Republicans may find it more socially desirable to underreport breakups, whereas Democrats may do the opposite (Appendices S3 and S4). As one strategy to gauge the impact of social desirability, we then compared how partisans responded to the ANES proxy question in online surveys compared with face-to-face interviews—as the latter should promote more socially desirable responding (47, 52). We found some modest and nonsignificant underreporting among Republicans in face-to-face interviews, but no mode differences in Democrats’ responses (Appendix S5).

Discussion

In our most recent study, we found that more than a third of Americans reported having lost relationships due to politics, most of them losing more than one kind of relationship. As there is no clear alternative method for measuring lost relationships, readers should take the aforementioned limitations of self-reports into account when interpreting our results. We should also keep in mind that relationships can be strained in various ways without being terminated. Political differences have likely degraded the relationships of a larger share of the population than what was captured in our data.

We found that the majority of reported breakups, especially those that were the most impactful, happened with friends. The differences in representation among relationship categories can be explained by the contrasting effects of relationship closeness on political breakups. Closeness might make political breakups more likely by eliciting political differences, given that people feel more comfortable discussing political matters with close others (53) and expressing disagreement with them (54). Indeed, our supplemental analyses showed that those who frequently discussed politics with friends were more likely to report breaking up with one over politics (Table S20). Once the differences are salient, close relationships are likely more resistant to breakups due to both internal (e.g. satisfaction, commitment, etc.) and external (e.g. social norms, financial dependency, etc.) constraints (55–57). Friendships may be uniquely vulnerable to political breakups: they can be close enough to allow for political differences to surface while lacking the commitments and constraints that hold romantic and family relationships together.

Our evidence suggests that the prevalence of political breakups in the United States has increased in recent years. We found that breakups resulting from the 2024 election matched those resulting from the 2016 election in half the time. Participants’ recall of their most impactful breakups also showed a noticeable uptick after 2016 that is hard to explain by recency bias. Between 2020 and 2024, panel participants became more likely to report that political differences hurt their family relationships. Although each of these findings has its own limitations, they all point in the same direction. The increase in breakups also aligns with affective polarization trends, as 2016 was the year out-party hate started noticeably prevailing over in-party love (11). Since then, controversial figures (e.g. Donald Trump) and events (e.g. George Floyd's murder, COVID-19 measures) gave Americans multiple occasions to draw lines in the sand, which were apparently seized.

Democrats were more likely to report political breakups, mostly as the initiating party. Our results align with recent studies showing that Democrats express more hostility toward, and desire to socially distance from Republicans than vice versa, driven by moralization of political issues (58, 59). Because the available data were collected in 2016 or later, we do not know whether this asymmetry represents some stable differences between liberal and conservative partisans (e.g. moral foundations; 60) or a reaction to the aforementioned politicians and events. Future research should examine whether aversion to political opponents is consistently higher among Democrats and whether a similar asymmetry exists in other countries.

The experience of a political breakup predicted greater partisan animosity, especially toward voters, as well as more exaggerated perceptions of opponents’ views. Even though partisans typically have more favorable feelings toward out-party voters compared with out-party elites (61, 62), for some, the dislike toward the elites may “spill over” onto voters. For example, partisans might put more responsibility on out-party voters for the actions of the elites, and assume out-party voters endorse all of the elites’ views. Alternatively, their dislike toward out-party voters may “catch up” to their dislike of elites after being acquainted with an out-party voter who is genuinely more extreme than the average out-partisan, skewing their perceptions of and feelings toward all out-party voters. While these processes can make partisans more prone to breakups, the breakup itself can also be considered a form of negative intergroup contact. Negative contact can disproportionately strengthen prejudice and stereotypes (63–65); this might explain why those who had breakups have even more exaggerated perceptions of opponents compared with their fellow partisans, whose misperceptions are typically attributed to a lack of contact (33). Additionally, after a breakup, partisans can selectively attend to exaggerated depictions of opponents by the media as a way to rationalize their costly decision (66, 67). None of these processes are mutually exclusive. Due to the cross-sectional nature of our studies, we cannot make strong claims about whether the additional hostility develops before breakups or as a result of them. Future studies should employ longitudinal designs to disentangle the causes of political breakups from their consequences.

Amid concerted efforts by individuals both inside and outside of academia to bridge political divides (68), many Americans end up burning bridges. While political breakups are a reflection of heightened polarization, they can also contribute to it, as decreased exposure to opposing views may eventually decrease tolerance (22–25). And while the US Surgeon General described polarization as a downside of our social connections in a report on the “epidemic of loneliness” (69), our work shows that polarization can sever existing ties and contribute to loneliness, potentially worsening people's physical and mental health (26–28). In this context, political breakups represent a threat to the well-being of a democracy—and likely to the well-being of its citizens. We call for more research on other predictors and outcomes of political breakups in the United States and around the world. As the measure does not require a specific target group or policy content, it is also suitable for comparative research (e.g. across two-party and multiparty systems). This costly consequence of polarization can allow researchers to gauge the degree to which ordinary citizens are divided by politics.

Materials and methods

Studies designed and conducted by the authors (1 and 2) were approved by the University of California—Irvine Institutional Review Board (protocols #3298 and #2007-5740, respectively). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. In Appendix S6, we provide the questions for these original surveys and direct the readers to the respective documentation for the other datasets we used.

All estimates reported in the main text and tables were adjusted using poststratified sampling weights. Analyses were performed in R (70), using the “survey” and “srvyr” packages to incorporate sampling weights (71, 72). While the estimates for the prevalence of political breakups were calculated using the full sample sizes reported in Table 1, the regression analyses contained fewer observations due to missing data in covariates (see SI Appendix for number of observations in each model). Parameter estimates in regression tables presented in the SI Appendix may slightly differ from those reported in the main text and Table 2, as the former are conditional on the reference categories of the other covariates whereas the latter are averaged across all levels of covariates (calculated using the avg_slopes() function from the “marginaleffects” package (73)). When describing covariates in the regression models throughout the paper, we use “party identification” to refer to the linear term for the 7-point party identification measure (unless noted; see Study 3) with higher scores indicating stronger Republican identification and “strength of partisanship” to refer to the quadratic term for the party identification measure. All continuous covariates used in the regression analyses were standardized, with the exception of party identification, which was centered around the midpoint (corresponding to true Independents).

Study 1

Participants

We recruited a nationally representative sample of 1,000 US adults from YouGov using their National Omnibus panel. The survey was fielded between 2025 April 10–14. YouGov matched participants from their panel to a sampling frame. The weights were generated using propensity scores and then poststratified based on gender, age, race, education, and vote choice in the 2020 and 2024 presidential elections.

Materials

We measured political breakups using a checklist-style question: “Have you ever lost a relationship due to political differences with any of the following people? (Select all that apply)” with the following options: “A friend,” “A family member,” “A romantic partner or spouse,” “A coworker,” “A neighbor,” “An acquaintance,” “Someone else” (with a text box), or “I have not lost relationships with anybody due to political differences.” Participants were able to select multiple options, except for the last one. We measured breakups resulting from the 2024 election with the following question: “Have you lost any friendships or other relationships as a result of the 2024 presidential election?” with the options “Yes,” “No,” or “Not sure.”

Data analysis

We created a binary measure of political breakups, such that the option “I have not lost relationships with anybody due to political differences” was coded as 0, and selecting one or more of the other options was coded as 1. We examined the predictors of political breakups using a logistic regression model with the following covariates: party identification, strength of partisanship, age, gender, race (White vs. non-White), education, and household income. The preregistration for our analysis plan can be found on OSF. In a separate analysis, we also added interest in politics to the list of covariates (Table S6).

Study 2

Participants

For Study 2, we recruited a sample of 1,000 adults from Prolific stratified to reflect the age, sex, and party identification of the US population. The survey was fielded on 2024 November 1. After filtering out participants who failed the attention and honesty checks, as well as those who gave inconsistent responses (see below), 953 participants were included in the analyses. We contacted 902 of these participants after the 2024 election, between 2024 November 12 and November 20; 839 participants passed the attention and honesty checks. In this postelection survey, we included questions on covariates that were not measured in the preelection survey. While we used the larger preelection sample to calculate our estimates about the prevalence of breakups and how they happen, we used the postelection sample for regression analyses to keep the list of covariates consistent across studies. The results were similar across both samples. We generated weights for both samples using iterative poststratification (a.k.a. raking) based on the age, sex, race (taken from the 2023 American Community Survey), and party identification (taken from Gallup) benchmarks for the adult population in the United States using the “anesrake” R package (74).

Materials

As part of a larger survey, participants answered a series of questions about their desires and expectations regarding the upcoming 2024 presidential election. They first answered who they wanted to win the 2024 presidential election in a multiple-choice question (“Donald Trump,” “Kamala Harris,” or “Neither”), then on a sliding scale from −10 to 10, with “Strongly want [Donald Trump/Kamala Harris] to win” on each end of the scale. Those who gave inconsistent answers on these two items were excluded from the analyses. Participants also reported their feelings toward Donald Trump, Kamala Harris, and their voters on feeling thermometer measures (coded as in-party or out-party depending on who they wanted to win) before answering the questions about political breakups.

To measure political breakups, participants were asked “Have you lost any friendships or other relationships due to political differences?” with the response options “Yes,” “No,” and “Maybe.” Those who selected “Yes” were asked a series of follow-up questions, starting with this prompt:

Data analysis

We used a logistic regression model to examine the predictors of political breakups (i.e. “Yes” responses to our main measure) using the following covariates: party identification, strength of partisanship, age, gender, race (White vs. non-White), and education. To examine whether political breakups predict more hostility toward political opponents, we used four regression models with the feeling thermometer scores for candidates and voters (coded in- vs. out-party based on participant's vote choice). We used a binary measure of political breakups (i.e. “Yes” = 1), as a predictor, along with party identification, strength of partisanship, desire for the victory of one's preferred candidate, age, gender, race (White vs. non-White), education, and income. We also examined the effects of initiating a breakup using similar models restricted to people who reported breakups; we only replaced the measure of political breakups with a binary measure of initiating the breakup (i.e. “I ended the relationship” = 1) and kept the rest of the covariates the same. To compare the coefficients of breakups (as well as initiating breakups), we reshaped the data into “long” format such that there were four rows for each participant corresponding to their feeling thermometer scores for the different targets. We then examined the interaction between the thermometer targets and breakup status and ran pairwise comparisons between the coefficients for breakups, adjusting for multiple comparisons using the Holm correction.

Study 3

Participants

We used a national sample of 1,000 US adults from the Wave 2 of the Pulse of the Nation Poll by Cards Against Humanity (75). Survey Sampling International (now Dynata) fielded a phone survey between 2017 October 1–15. Similar to Study 2, we generated weights based on age, sex, race (taken from the 2017 American Community Survey), and party identification (taken from Gallup) benchmarks for the adult population in the United States.

Data analysis

There were a couple points to note about the dataset for researchers who want to replicate our results. The dataset contained string and numeric versions of variables, but for some of the variables (e.g. partisanship, education, etc.), we found that the levels did not match between the two versions. This was because numeric variables were coded based on the alphabetical order of the levels instead of the correct order (e.g. “College degree” being the lowest level for education). We used the correctly recoded versions of those variables for our analyses (our analysis script can be found on OSF). Additionally, the survey did not contain a follow-up question for Independents about which party they felt closer to. Thus, we had to compute a 5-point measure for party identification (as opposed to the 7-point version used in the rest of the studies) that did not distinguish leaners from true Independents. For the sake of consistency across studies, we did not code leaners as partisans when reporting estimates from other datasets throughout the main text and Table 1.

To examine the predictors of political breakups resulting from the 2016 election, we used a logistic regression model with the same list of covariates as Study 2. We investigated whether the experience of political breakups predicted perceptions of opponents using four separate regression models. Partisans’ estimates of Republicans and Democrats holding certain views were used as outcome variables; each analysis was restricted to the opponents of the target group (e.g. Democrats estimating the percentage of Republicans who agree with White nationalists). All models contained political breakups, strength of partisanship (1 = “Strong,” 0 = “Not strong”), age, gender, race (White vs. non-White), and education as predictors. Because income data were missing for 70% of the sample, we did not use income as a covariate.

To compare the prevalence of breakups resulting from the 2016 election to those resulting from the 2024 election (Study 1), we fit intercept-only logistic regression models with the respective breakup questions as outcomes. We then used Clogg et al.'s (76) test to compare the intercepts.

Study 4

Participants

We used a sample of 838 residents of Orange County, California, from the November 2024 OC Poll, conducted by the School of Social Ecology at the University of California—Irvine. TrueDot fielded the survey between 2024 November 12–18. The final sample consisted of a probability sample of 228 residents drawn from the voter file records and a nonprobability sample of 610 residents from an online panel. The weights were generated based on the age, gender, educational attainment, race, and ethnicity of the Orange County population, as well as the results of the 2024 presidential election.

Supplemental analyses using the ANES data

Participants

Data were obtained from the American National Election Studies 2020 and 2024 Time Series Studies. There were 7,359 participants who responded to our measure of interest (see below) in the full 2020 sample (i.e. cross-sectional and panel samples), 4,887 in the full 2024 sample, and 2,035 in the panel sample (i.e. responded in both the 2020 and the 2024 waves).

Materials

We used the following question from the ANES postelection surveys: “In the past four years, how much have political differences hurt your relationships with family members?” (5-point scale from “Not at all” to “A great deal”). Although this question only focused on family relationships and did not directly ask about relationships that ended, we used it as a proxy for political breakups.

Data analysis

We used the full postelection samples from 2020 and 2024 to calculate the overall percentages of participants who reported their family relationships being hurt to some extent (i.e. those who selected “A little” to “A great deal” on the proxy measure). For the regression models, the proxy measure was reverse scored so that the higher scores indicated more damage to family relationships. To formally test whether there was an increase in hurt family relationships among panel participants, we used an intercept-only regression model with difference scores between years as the outcome.

To investigate the predictors of hurt family relationships, we used a linear regression model with the proxy measure as the outcome and the same list of covariates as Studies 1 and 4, using the full sample from the 2024 wave. We observed that the residuals were not normally distributed in the linear model, likely due to the high positive skew in the outcome measure; log transforming the outcome measure did not sufficiently improve model diagnostics. We then tried a logistic regression approach by converting the proxy measure into a binary variable that represents family relationships hurt to any extent (i.e. selecting “A little” to “A great deal” = 1, “Not at all” = 0). Model diagnostics were acceptable for the logistic model; hence, we reported the coefficient estimate for party identification from this model in the main text (party identification was a significant predictor in both models, Ps < 0.001; see Table S5). We also added interest in politics as a covariate to these models (Table S7).
