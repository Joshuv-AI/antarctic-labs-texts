Case report: Incidental finding of a duplicated right vertebral artery in a 17-year-old female with suspected systemic lupus erythematosus
Authors: Mohammad Bagher Shamsodini Moghadam, Sepideh Sefidbakht, Parisa Pishdad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present the case of a 17-year-old female with suspected systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), receiving prednisolone and hydroxychloroquine, who was evaluated for a difference in blood pressure between the upper extremities and an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR). CT angiography (CTA) of the cervical vessels, performed because of clinical suspicion of vasculitis, incidentally demonstrated duplication of the right vertebral artery (VA), with both limbs originating from the right subclavian artery. The medial limb coursed posterior to the common carotid artery and anterolateral to the vertebral transverse foramen. No stenosis, occlusion, aneurysm, dissection, or other associated vascular abnormality was identified. The patient had no neurological symptoms attributable to the vascular variant. Although vertebral artery duplication is a rare congenital vascular anomaly, its identification in a young patient with suspected SLE undergoing evaluation for possible vasculitis represents an unusual clinical context. Recognition of this variant on CTA is important because its anomalous course may have implications for future cervical spine, vascular, or neurointerventional procedures. This case emphasizes the importance of careful evaluation of vertebral artery anatomy and accurate differentiation of congenital vascular variants from acquired vascular abnormalities.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a multisystem autoimmune disease characterized by autoantibody production and immune-mediated tissue injury. Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular involvement may occur during the course of the disease, including vasculitis, accelerated atherosclerosis, and thromboembolic complications [1,2].

Duplication of the vertebral artery (VA) is a rare congenital vascular variant in which two arterial channels arise separately and subsequently converge to form a single vertebral artery. The duplicated vessels may demonstrate variable origins and courses, and the anomaly is often detected incidentally on vascular imaging [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Although most cases are asymptomatic, recognition of the variant is clinically important because an anomalous vertebral artery may have a close relationship with the common carotid artery, cervical vertebrae, and other critical neurovascular structures, with potential implications for cervical spine surgery and endovascular or neurointerventional procedures [4,5,7,8].

In this report, we describe an incidental duplicated right VA with both limbs originating from the right subclavian artery in a 17-year-old female with clinical suspicion of SLE who underwent CT angiography for evaluation of suspected vasculitis. To our knowledge, this represents an unusual clinical presentation of this vascular variant. The case highlights the importance of recognizing congenital vertebral artery variants and accurately characterizing their origin and course on CT angiography, particularly when vascular imaging is performed to investigate suspected acquired vascular disease.

Case Presentation

A 17-year-old female with a high clinical suspicion of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) was referred to the rheumatology department of Faghihi Hospital, Shiraz, Iran, for evaluation of a difference in blood pressure between the two upper extremities. She was suspected of SLE at age 16 following initial manifestations of fever, fatigue, and neck swelling, without signs of renal, neurological, cardiovascular, or other major organ involvement. Her medical history was otherwise unremarkable for relevant conditions. She had no history of previous cervical or cardiovascular surgery or vascular intervention. Her current medications included prednisolone and hydroxychloroquine.

There was no known family history of SLE, other systemic autoimmune disorders, congenital vascular anomalies, or premature cerebrovascular disease.

At presentation, the patient presented with extreme fatigue, low fever, headache, and pain in the joints, with a blood pressure of 110/60 mmHg in the right arm and 135/85 mmHg in the left arm. She denied dizziness, vertigo, syncope, visual disturbance, neck pain, limb weakness, sensory disturbance, or other symptoms suggestive of vertebrobasilar insufficiency. Physical examination was unremarkable except for the blood pressure differences between the upper extremities.

Laboratory evaluation demonstrated an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 100 mm/h. The complete blood count showed hemoglobin 12 g/dL, white blood cell count 8 × 10⁹/L, and platelet count 244 × 10⁹/L. Renal function was normal with a serum creatinine level of 0.8 mg/dL. The initial anti-dsDNA was positive, and several RBCs were reported in the urinalysis. No laboratory evidence of relevant abnormality was identified.

Because of the inter-arm blood pressure discrepancy and elevated inflammatory markers, large-vessel vasculitis was considered in the differential diagnosis in the context of suspected SLE. CT angiography (CTA) of the cervical vessels was therefore performed using an Ingenuity CT scanner. The examination demonstrated two separate origins of the right vertebral artery from the right subclavian artery. The medial limb coursed posterior to the right common carotid artery and anterolateral to the vertebral transverse foramen. The two limbs subsequently fused at the upper neck (distal V2 segment). Multiplanar coronal and axial reformations and a volume-rendered reconstruction confirmed the vascular configuration. No stenosis, occlusion, dissection, aneurysm, or other associated vascular malformation was identified.

The left vertebral artery and the remaining major cervical arteries were unremarkable. The patient had no clinical symptoms attributable to the vascular anomaly. As there was no evidence of vasculitis or acute vascular complication, no intervention directed at the vertebral artery anomaly was considered necessary. Her existing treatment with prednisolone and hydroxychloroquine was continued without modification.

At 10-month follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable without neurological symptoms or other complications related to the duplicated vertebral artery. Discussion

Duplication of the vertebral artery (VA) is an uncommon congenital vascular variant characterized by two separate arterial origins that subsequently converge to form a single vertebral artery. It should be distinguished from vertebral artery fenestration, in which a vessel with a single origin divides into two channels and subsequently reunites. The distinction is clinically relevant because the two configurations have different anatomical and developmental characteristics [4,5].

The present case demonstrates a duplicated right VA with both limbs originating from the right subclavian artery, with the medial limb coursing posterior to the common carotid artery and anterolateral to the vertebral transverse foramen. This configuration is consistent with the characteristic anatomy described in previous CTA studies. In a series of 3386 CTA examinations, Kim et al. identified 10 patients (0.295%) with dual-origin VA; seven had right-sided duplication, and in all seven cases both limbs originated from the right subclavian artery. The medial limb was located posteriorly and medially to the common carotid artery and anteriorly and laterally to the vertebral transverse foramen [4]. Similar anatomical patterns have been described in individual case reports and case series [5,6].

Other reports have demonstrated considerable anatomical variability in the origin and course of duplicated VAs. Kritikos et al. reported four cases of VA duplication and emphasized that recognition of this uncommon vascular variant is important for planning head and neck surgery and neurointerventional procedures [5]. Uchino and Kurita described a duplicate origin of the right VA in which both channels arose from the extreme proximal right subclavian artery and subsequently fused at the cervical transverse foramen [6]. These reports emphasize that careful characterization of the origin, course, and fusion level of the duplicated vessels is important to avoid misinterpretation and inadvertent vascular injury.

The clinical significance of the present case lies particularly in the age and clinical context of the patient. Unlike many previously reported cases, our patient was 17 years old and was undergoing evaluation for suspected systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). To our knowledge, a duplicated vertebral artery in this specific clinical context has not been previously reported. Nevertheless, the coexistence of a suspected autoimmune disorder and the vascular anomaly should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal relationship. Vertebral artery duplication is generally regarded as a congenital developmental vascular variant rather than an acquired manifestation of systemic inflammation [4,5].

This distinction is particularly important in the present patient because CT angiography was performed because of an inter-arm blood pressure discrepancy and elevated inflammatory markers, raising clinical concern for large-vessel vasculitis in the setting of suspected SLE. The CTA demonstrated no stenosis, occlusion, dissection, aneurysm, or other imaging evidence of vasculitis. Therefore, the duplicated VA should be regarded as an incidental congenital vascular variant rather than an imaging manifestation of an autoimmune vasculopathy. The inter-arm blood pressure discrepancy should likewise not be attributed to the duplicated VA in the absence of evidence of hemodynamically significant vascular disease [2,9,10].

Although most duplicated VAs are asymptomatic, recognition of this variant has important practical implications. The anomalous medial limb may course unusually close to the common carotid artery and other important cervical structures, potentially increasing the risk of inadvertent injury during cervical spine surgery, vascular procedures, or neurointerventional interventions [4,5,7,8]. Knowledge of the vascular anatomy before such procedures is therefore important for appropriate surgical and endovascular planning.

CT angiography is particularly useful for demonstrating the origin, course, relationship to the common carotid artery and transverse foramina, and point of convergence of the duplicated limbs. Multiplanar and three-dimensional reconstructions can further facilitate recognition of the anomaly and provide useful anatomical information for procedural planning [4,5,11]. In the present case, coronal and axial multiplanar reconstructions and three-dimensional volume-rendered images clearly demonstrated the duplicated right VA and its relationship to the adjacent cervical structures.

The main lesson from this case is that an unusual vertebral artery configuration should be carefully evaluated before being interpreted as an acquired vascular abnormality, particularly in patients undergoing vascular imaging for suspected vasculitis. In young patients with autoimmune disease, the presence of a duplicated VA should be documented clearly because the anomaly may remain clinically silent but may become relevant if future cervical or neurovascular intervention is required [4,5,7,8].

In conclusion, this case illustrates an incidental duplicated right vertebral artery in a 17-year-old patient undergoing evaluation for suspected SLE. Although the anatomical variant itself is a recognized rare developmental anomaly, its identification in this young patient undergoing evaluation for suspected vasculitis represents an unusual clinical context. Careful characterization of the vascular origin and course on CTA is important to avoid misdiagnosis and to alert clinicians to potentially relevant anatomy before future vascular or cervical procedures.
