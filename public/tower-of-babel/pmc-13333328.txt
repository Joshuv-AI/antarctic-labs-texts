Impact of pre-operative dementia or cognitive impairment on outcomes after elective primary total shoulder arthroplasty
Authors: Alexander R. Zhu, Grace Q. Chen, Ashley R. Zhu, Albert Wu, Umasuthan Srikumaran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCognitive impairment or dementia has been linked to adverse outcomes in orthopedic surgery. However, studies evaluating its impact on perioperative outcomes after total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) have been limited. The aim of this study is to examine the association between pre-operative dementia and short-term outcomes following TSA.MethodsPatients undergoing elective primary TSA over 2021-2023 were retrospectively identified and analyzed in the National Surgical Quality Improvement Program database. Patients with and without dementia were matched based on patient demographics and comorbidities. Thirty-day post-operative outcomes such as length of stay and complications were analyzed via univariate analyses.ResultsA total of 6,120 patients who underwent elective primary TSA were identified. After 5:1 propensity matching, 219 patients with pre-operative dementia and 1,095 patients without pre-operative dementia were included. The dementia cohort, on average, experienced a longer length of stay (2.2 ± 3.0 days vs. 1.7 ± 4.3 days, P = .047), more frequent discharge to a nonhome setting (26.5% vs. 13.3%, P < .001), and a higher rate of readmission within 30 days (8.7% vs. 4.7%, P = .029). In addition, this cohort had higher rates of overall complications (10.0% vs. 6.0%, P = .043) and minor complications (7.8% vs. 3.9%, P = .021), as well as the rate of bleeding resulting in transfusion (5.5% vs. 2.0%, P = .007).ConclusionsPre-operative dementia is associated with significant increases in adverse short-term outcomes in TSA patients. Dementia may limit patients' ability to follow post-operative instructions, participate in rehabilitation, and manage comorbidities, contributing to worse outcomes. These findings underscore the need for healthcare teams to optimize perioperative and post-operative care in patients with dementia.

Introduction

Total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) cases have increased greatly in recent years in the United States, growing at a faster rate compared to total knee and hip arthroplasties.34 By successfully restoring shoulder joint function, this procedure serves as a viable option for patients facing degenerative or inflammatory shoulder conditions. TSA incidence continues to increase throughout the decade, with the number of TSA cases projected to reach 400,000 by 2030.5,24 Therefore, it is important to identify relevant risk factors when assessing short-term outcomes and potential complications of TSA.

Dementia or cognitive impairment presents with a decline in cognitive function that frequently develops in the age range of patients undergoing total joint arthroplasties such as TSA.10,12 Previous studies have shown how dementia leads to adverse outcomes in orthopedic surgery, including elevated rates of post-operative complications in total hip arthroplasty (THA).16 Another study reported that patients with pre-existing cognitive impairment experienced poorer post-operative recovery after total knee arthroplasty (TKA), with higher rates of delirium, reduced functional independence, and a greater likelihood of discharge to rehabilitation or assisted nursing facilities.19 Nevertheless, the broader impact of dementia on post-operative outcomes in TSA remains understudied. Existing research is limited, with one study reporting increased surgical complications over a 2-year follow-up in patients with pre-operative dementia and another identifying increased risk of perioperative outcomes in patients with pre-operative psychiatric illnesses who underwent shoulder arthroplasty from 1990 to 2007.4,33

Given the projected rise in TSA utilization and the growing prevalence of cognitive impairment in the population, this study aimed to determine the effect of pre-operative dementia on short-term outcomes for patients who have undergone TSA. The authors hypothesized that patients with dementia would be at increased risk for adverse outcomes post-operatively following TSA. Understanding the link between cognitive impairment and TSA may inform clinical decision-making and guide perioperative care of these patients.

Methods

Because this was a retrospective analysis of a publicly available, deidentified national database, it did not require institutional review board approval.

Patient selection

The American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP) database collects patient demographics, surgical case profiles, and 30-day post-operative outcomes from over 700 hospitals in the United States and Canada. Using the Current Procedural Terminology code 23472, patients who underwent primary reverse and anatomic TSA between 2021 and 2023 were identified.

Baseline demographics and comorbidities included age, sex, height, weight, smoking status, diabetes mellitus, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, pre-operative functional status, hypertension requiring medication, history of severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, ascites, heart failure within 30 days before surgery, disseminated cancer, dialysis use, ventilator use, bleeding disorder, and pre-operative packed red blood cell transfusion <72 hours before surgery. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated using the following equation: BMI = (weight in pounds)/(height in inches)2 × 703.

Exclusion criteria included patients who had missing baseline characteristics (N = 13,687), who did not have a documented discharge (N = 1), who passed away during hospitalization (N = 4), who left against medical advice (N = 2), or whose surgeries were emergent or urgent cases (N = 167). Using the “HXDEMENTIA” variable, patients were divided into 2 groups: those with a history of dementia or cognitive impairment, and those without a history of dementia or cognitive impairment.

Thirty-day post-operative outcomes of interest were evaluated. The following complications were classified as minor: superficial surgical site infection, urinary tract infection, deep venous thrombosis, and bleeding requiring transfusion. The following complications were defined as major: ventilator use for more than 48 hours, unplanned intubation, pneumonia, myocardial infarction, stroke, progressive renal insufficiency or acute renal failure requiring dialysis, pulmonary embolism, deep surgical site infection, wound dehiscence, sepsis, or septic shock.

Statistical analyses

Before matching, demographics and comorbidities were compared between patients with and without dementia or cognitive impairment using univariate analyses (2-sided independent-samples t-tests, chi-square tests, or Fisher's exact tests). A 5:1 nearest-neighbor propensity score matching without replacement was then performed, controlling for baseline demographics and comorbidities. Significant differences in demographics, comorbidities, and 30-day post-operative outcomes between the matched cohorts were identified via 2-sided independent-samples t-test, chi-square test, and Fisher's exact test when appropriate.

As a sensitivity analysis, multivariable regression analyses in the full eligible cohort (N = 6,120) assessed the independent association between pre-operative dementia or cognitive impairment and 30-day outcomes. Logistic regression was used for binary outcomes, and linear regression was used for length of stay. Models adjusted for age, sex, BMI, smoking status, diabetes, ASA class ≥3, functional status, hypertension requiring medication, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, ascites, congestive heart failure, disseminated cancer, dialysis use, ventilator dependence, bleeding disorder, and pre-operative transfusion within 72 hours.

All analyses were performed using R programming (RStudio version 4.4.0, Boston, MA), with statistical significance set at P < .05.

Discussion

In the present study, patients with dementia or cognitive impairment were found to have worse short-term outcomes than propensity-matched controls without dementia or cognitive impairment. Specifically, the dementia cohort experienced a longer length of stay, was more frequently discharged to a nonhome setting, and had higher 30-day readmission rates. They also had higher 30-day overall and minor complication rates, driven largely by increased bleeding requiring transfusion.

Few studies have investigated the association between dementia or cognitive impairment and post-operative outcomes after elective TSA. Existing conclusions are largely extrapolated from broader populations of orthopedic and general surgery patients and may include procedures for emergent and urgent indications. For instance, Hu et al investigated dementia patients undergoing a variety of surgeries including not only musculoskeletal but also neurosurgical, gastrointestinal, urologic, and cardiovascular procedures and found significantly higher post-operative complication rates, medical resource use, and in-hospital expenditures compared to controls.15

Even within orthopedic surgery, most of the data regarding cognitive impairment come from lower extremity procedures such as THA and TKA.9,11,16,19 For instance, Krishnan et al found that patients with cognitive impairment undergoing TKA had higher rates of post-operative complications, worsened post-operative functional status, and a higher likelihood of nonhome discharge.19 Johnson et al similarly studied complications up to 5 years following THA and TKA and found patients with dementia were more likely to be readmitted and that patients undergoing THA with dementia had worse outcomes than both those undergoing THA without dementia and those undergoing TKA with dementia.16 Similarly, while a systematic review by Chen et al on patients with cognitive impairment undergoing a variety of noncardiac surgeries included 4 studies on orthopedic patients, these were limited to outcomes following THA, TKA, and spinal procedures.7

Even with the majority of TSA procedures being performed electively, the overall rate of elective TSA continues to grow due to increases in life expectancy, expansion of indications for TSA, and improvements in implant technology. Given the prevalence of cognitive impairment among patients undergoing TSA, a few studies have examined the association between psychiatric and neurologic disorders and shoulder arthroplasty. Bot et al evaluated conditions such as depression, anxiety, schizophrenia, and dementia in relation to perioperative outcomes following partial or total shoulder arthroplasty.4 Wong et al also investigated the impact of mental health conditions, though dementia was not included in their analysis.38 Serna et al specifically examined dementia in the context of shoulder arthroplasty using the PearlDiver database.29 However, their study did not delineate the timing of complications within the 2-year follow-up period, whereas our NSQIP-based analysis provides more temporally specific insight by focusing on the immediate 30-day post-operative period.

The present study thus sought to address this gap by specifically evaluating short-term outcomes of elective TSA in patients with cognitive impairment. This study found that the overall prevalence of dementia or cognitive impairment among TSA patients is 3.6%, a rate comparable to those reported by Serna et al (3.0%) and Bot et al (1.5% for dementia only, excluding milder degrees of cognitive impairment).4,29

An important finding of this study is that patients with dementia or cognitive impairment are more likely to have minor medical complications in the immediate 30-day period following TSA, especially bleeding that requires transfusion. This can be compared to Serna et al, who found that patients with neurocognitive impairment had elevated odds of major medical complications, including acute myocardial infarction and cerebrovascular accident in the 2 years following surgery.29 This suggests that more severe complications may emerge later in patients with cognitive impairment undergoing TSA. Several factors may explain this higher risk of medical complications in cognitively impaired patients undergoing TSA. These patients have a higher burden of medical comorbidities, are more likely to be frail, and often present with unrecognized or undertreated conditions such as anemia and malnutrition.22,26,33,36,39 These factors all contribute to increase the risk of perioperative complications and transfusion requirements after TSA, as demonstrated by multivariable analyses identifying frailty, malnutrition, higher ASA class, low pre-operative hematocrit, and comorbidities as key predictors of bleeding and transfusion.1,3,6,13,21,31,35 While we adjusted for ASA class and several major comorbidities in our analysis, patients with dementia and these risk factors may have had suboptimal ability to ensure proper pre-operative optimization of these conditions. Additionally, patients with dementia or cognitive impairment are at increased risk for post-operative delirium, which can precipitate agitation, hemodynamic instability, and increased risk of falls or inadvertent removal of lines, further contributing to medical complications, including bleeding requiring transfusion.7,20,23,28

Another key finding was that patients with dementia or cognitive impairment are also more likely to be discharged to a nonhome setting and be readmitted within 30 days, even after adjusting for pre-operative dependent functional status. This could be due to several factors, including impaired ability to follow post-operative instructions, increased risk of delirium, greater need for supervision, and limited capacity to safely manage self-care and rehabilitation at home.2,14,18,37 The cumulative effect of these vulnerabilities results in patients with dementia or cognitive impairment being at higher risk for short-term complications after elective TSA. To mitigate these risks, patients with dementia or cognitive impairment should undergo careful pre-operative optimization as well as early and comprehensive discharge and rehabilitation planning before undergoing elective TSA. This could include patient and caregiver education before surgery, including instruction on post-operative exercises, modifications to activities of daily living, and planning for additional post-operative assistance and home environment adjustments.8,17,27 Special consideration should be given to modifiable risk factors including correcting pre-operative anemia, optimizing nutrition, and managing polypharmacy.25,30,40 Early involvement of case management and social work in combination with early identification of patients at high risk for nonhome discharge can help facilitate appropriate discharge placement. Lastly, post-operative rehabilitation protocols should be tailored to cognitive status, emphasizing clear, simple instructions, and caregiver involvement.32

This study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, the substantial number of patients excluded due to missing baseline data and during subsequent 5:1 propensity score matching may have introduced selection bias and reduced the representativeness of the final analytic cohort, thereby limiting generalizability. However, findings were consistent in both the propensity-matched analysis and the multivariable full-cohort sensitivity analysis, supporting the robustness of the observed associations. Second, as with all large administrative datasets, NSQIP is subject to coding inaccuracies and data entry errors, which may lead to misclassification of dementia diagnoses or post-operative complications. Another limitation is that NSQIP only captures outcomes within 30 days post-operatively, limiting assessment of long-term complications such as implant failure, loosening, periprosthetic fracture, or revision surgery, as well as functional recovery and mortality beyond this period. Furthermore, NSQIP does not provide the granularity to determine which patients received an anatomic TSA or reverse TSA. In addition, NSQIP does not offer detailed clinical information such as the severity of cognitive impairment, specific implant type, surgical technique, anesthesia management, or surgeon experience, all of which may influence outcomes after TSA. Important patient-level context, especially for those with dementia or cognitive impairment, such as living situation and social support, is also not available. Finally, as NSQIP represents only participating hospitals, there may be selection bias that limits the generalizability of our findings to all U.S. institutions. Nonetheless, the strengths of the NSQIP database include its large, nationally representative cohort with prospectively collected and standardized clinical data, which allows for robust risk-adjusted analyses.

Conclusion

Cognitive impairment is associated with worse 30-day post-operative outcomes following TSA, leading to both immediate risk and overall health burden. Therefore, in light of a pre-operative dementia diagnosis, surgeons should consider tailored care and incorporate cognitive status into pre- and post-operative management. Further studies are needed to identify modifiable risk factors linked to cognitive impairment in orthopedic surgery.
