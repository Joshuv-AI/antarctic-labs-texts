Human vs. cells vs. machine: A comparative analysis of toxicological points of departure derived from quantitative read-across (qRAx), in vitro data, and In silico predictions
Authors: Weihsueh A. Chiu, Hannah M. Roe, Ivan Rusyn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Read-across is an expert-driven new approach methodology (NAM) used to fill gaps in chemical toxicity data. While qualitative read-across is widely used, quantitative read-across (qRAx) for deriving points of departure (PODs) has received limited attention. We compared the applicability domain, consistency, and conservatism of PODs derived from qRAx, in vitro data, and in silico predictions. Specifically, we first identified 41 substances evaluated by the U.S. EPA’s Provisional Peer-Reviewed Toxicity Value (PPRTV) program for qRAx-derived oral chronic PODs. For these same substances, we generated PODs using: (i) in vitro-to-in vivo extrapolation from ToxCast bioactivity; (ii) database-calibrated in silico assessment from ToxValDB; and (iii) three quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) models. Success rates for generating PODs varied considerably: qRAx 83% (34/41), ToxCast 22% (9/41), ToxValDB 66% (27/41), and QSAR 46–100% (19–41/41). qRAx yielded the most conservative PODs in the largest number of cases (44–54%). Combining multiple NAMs including at least one of ToxValDB or a QSAR model results in coverage exceeding 90% and including both in a tiered approach produces PODs that are on average within one order of magnitude of qRAx-derived values. We conclude that well-calibrated in silico methods can rapidly derive PODs with defined uncertainty, supporting time-sensitive health risk decisions.

Introduction

Chemical risk assessments have traditionally relied on toxicity data from animal testing to establish health-based guidance values (National Research Council, 1983). However, growing interest in incorporating data from alternative testing methods into regulatory decision-making, together with the need to address the vast number of chemicals lacking adequate human or animal toxicity data, has driven the development of new approach methodologies (NAMs) as critical tools for filling data gaps (Berggren and Worth, 2026). While much attention has focused on the development of human cell-based methods as NAMs for regulatory decision-making, many regulatory agencies have a broader outlook and also consider computational predictions, weight-of-evidence approaches, and read-across as practical and informative NAMs (Avila et al., 2023; ECHA, 2017; Yao et al., 2025). Read-across is one such approach, whereby data gaps for required animal tests on a “target” chemical are filled using existing, regulatory-acceptable toxicity data from one or more similar “source” chemicals (Patlewicz et al., 2019).

Read-across is most frequently applied in the context of regulatory decisions under European Union’s REACH (Registration, Evaluation, Authorisation and Restriction of Chemicals) and similar regulatory frameworks in other jurisdictions (Rovida et al., 2020). In REACH-related regulatory submissions for industrial chemicals over the past 15 years, read-across has been the most common adaptation to standard safety testing requirements (Roe et al., 2026). The adequacy of registrant-submitted read-across hypotheses used to justify chemical similarity is evaluated by the European Chemicals Agency through dossier compliance checks or evaluation of testing proposals (Roe et al., 2025). Most of these read-across justifications are qualitative in nature and focus on hazard identification rather than quantitative dose-response assessment. Comparatively little attention has been given to the application of “quantitative read-across” (qRAx), an approach that aims to derive a regulatory-suitable “safe exposure level” for a target chemical by extrapolating dose-response information from source analogues. One example of the limited application of qRAx in a regulatory setting is the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency’s (EPA) Provisional Peer-Reviewed Toxicity Value (PPRTV) program, which has systematically applied a qRAx framework to derive points of departure (PODs) for data-poor chemicals (Lizarraga et al., 2023).

Although conceptually similar to qualitative read-across, qRAx implementation requires substantially greater amount of expert judgment. Not only does qRAx involves identification of suitable analogues for the target chemical and crafting a hypothesis justifying similarity, it also requires evaluation of the quality and relevance of available dose-response data for the analogues, and explicit characterization of uncertainty (Lizarraga et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2012). In qualitative read-across cases under REACH, there is never a situation in which all required tests on a target chemical are waived; the regulators typically have at least some data on the target chemical to support hazard identification and other decisions. Even so, the success rate of read-across hypotheses in REACH dossier compliance has been limited, with only 7.5% of more than 1000 cases deemed acceptable by ECHA (Roe et al., 2026). In contrast, a PPRTV-type qRAx may be used to derive a safe exposure level without the need to identify specific underlying human health hazards. Consequently, the gravity of regulatory decisions based purely on a read-across hypothesis may be substantially greater for qRAx applications than for qualitative read-across under REACH.

While cell-based NAMs and other adaptations to standard testing requirements were originally pursued largely independently, the past decade has seen growing recognition that in vitro test data can be used to evaluate “biological similarity” between analogues or to justify chemical classes or categories (Berggren et al., 2015; Grimm et al., 2016; Helman et al., 2019; Low et al., 2013; Patlewicz et al., 2024). In vitro NAMs data can be used across a wide range of decision contexts, including prioritization, hazard identification, and the derivation of quantitative toxicological values such as PODs. These PODs may be used to calculate reference doses (RfDs), other health-based guidance values, or safety margins through in vitro-to-in vivo extrapolation (IVIVE). However, such approaches generally do not allow inference of hazard in specific organs or tissues, and the resulting PODs are typically assumed to be broadly protective rather than predictive of a particular adverse health effect or a direct substitute for a required animal test (Berggren and Worth, 2026; Paul Friedman et al., 2025). In parallel, a variety of in silico methods, including quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) models and other computational approaches, have been used to derive quantitative estimates of chemical hazard rather than binary predictions of specific toxicological endpoints (Rusyn et al., 2012; Worth et al., 2013).

Overall, at least three distinct approaches exist for deriving a safety value for chemicals lacking requisite animal or human toxicity data: qRAx, which relies heavily on expert judgment (the “human”); in vitro NAMs (the “cells”); and in silico modeling approaches (the “machine”). Each approach carries distinct assumptions, uncertainties, and resource requirements. Expert-driven (“human”) approaches are labor-intensive, fraught with judgment calls, and are frequently perceived as biased (Steenland et al., 2020); cell-based (“cells”) approaches lack full metabolic competence, are typically short-term, and pose challenges for in vivo translation (Kavlock et al., 2018; Zink et al., 2020); and computational (“machine”) approaches are often opaque, heavily dependent on the quality and scope of input data, and limited in their generalizability to chemical structures not well represented during model development (Alves et al., 2021; Cronin et al., 2025).

Because all three approaches have now been applied across large chemical inventories, there is a unique opportunity to benchmark different NAM-based strategies against each other and against data derived from standard animal studies to evaluate whether expert-driven qRAx represents a sensible strategy for regulatory decision-making. Accordingly, the present study aimed to compare the performance of several recently developed NAM approaches for deriving oral chronic PODs across three key dimensions. First, we evaluated the applicability domain of each approach, that is, the proportion of chemicals that each method can successfully assess. Second, we examined conservatism by determining which methods tend to produce more protective (lower) PODs. Third, we assessed consistency by evaluating the degree of agreement among the different approaches. By systematically comparing expert-based qRAx, database-calibrated approaches, IVIVE-based methods, and machine-learning QSAR models, this study seeks to inform the development of efficient, transparent workflows for deriving PODs when traditional toxicity data are unavailable.

Materials and methods

An overview of our workflow is shown in Fig. 1.

Chemical selection

From all 410 substances evaluated by the U.S. EPA PPRTV program as of September 2, 2025, we identified 41 substances for which quantitative read-across was attempted to derive oral route PODs or reference doses (Table S1). The PPRTV program applies a structured qRAx framework originally described by (Wang et al., 2012), which involves systematic analogue identification, similarity assessment, and uncertainty characterization. These 41 substances span diverse chemical classes (see Table 1), including halogenated organics (e.g., 1-bromo-2--chloroethane, cis-1,2-dichloroethylene), nitroaromatics (e.g., 2,4,6-trinitrophenol, 1,4-dinitrobenzene), polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (e.g., benzo[e]pyrene, perylene), and various other industrial chemicals. Chemical identifiers (CASRN, SMILES, DTXSID, InChIKey) were obtained by batch-searching CASRNs against the EPA CompTox Dashboard using the ctxR package. Monoisotopic mass and other chemical descriptors were also retrieved to support subsequent NAM workflows.

NAM approaches evaluated

For each of the 41 substances, we attempted to derive oral chronic PODs using six different NAMs. We recorded three outcomes: (1) Whether a POD could be derived (binary outcome), (2) the point estimate POD value in mg/kg-day human equivalent dose (HED), and (3) POD uncertainty as indicated by in the lower confidence bound (LCB) reflecting model uncertainty. Note that PODs were all expressed as HED, applying allometric scaling or high throughput toxicokinetics (HTTK) if necessary, to account for the fact that in vivo, in vitro, and in silico PODs were derived from different species and models.

Expert-based quantitative read-across (qRAx, U.S. EPA PPRTV)

PODs were derived by the U.S. EPA PPRTV program using the expert judgment framework described by (Wang et al., 2012). This approach involves identifying suitable source analogues based on structural similarity and other relevant properties, followed by expert evaluation of available dose-response data from the source chemicals and extrapolation to the target chemical. The PPRTV qRAx framework incorporates systematic consideration of toxicokinetic and toxicodynamic similarity, data quality assessment, and explicit characterization of uncertainty through the application of uncertainty factors. Experimental animal PODs were converted to human equivalent dose (HED) using allometric scaling from the test species to humans (U.S. EPA, 2013). The qRAx lower bound was calculated by dividing the point estimate HED by the product of uncertainty factors for LOAEL-to-NOAEL, Subchronic-to-Chronic, and Database Deficiencies.

Database-calibrated surrogate in vivo PODs (ToxValDB)

These PODs are derived from the systematic calibration approach described by (Aurisano et al., 2023), which leverages existing in vivo toxicity data compiled in the U.S. EPA ToxValDB database. The method applies a Bayesian hierarchical framework to estimate general non-cancer (PODgen) and reproductive/developmental (PODrd) PODs by calibrating predicted values against PODs from authoritative regulatory assessments (U.S. EPA, CalEPA, ATSDR). We obtained PODs converted to HEDs from the Aurisano et al. (2023) supplemental table and selected the more conservative between the PODgen and PODrd values for each chemical. Lower bounds were derived using the reported geometric standard deviation (GSD), which characterizes the uncertainty distribution around each POD estimate. This database-calibrated approach was recently formalized by the U.S. EPA as the Database Calibrated Assessment Product (DCAP) (U. S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2025), demonstrating its regulatory acceptance and operational readiness.

In vitro-to-in vivo extrapolation (ToxCast/IVIVE)

Oral equivalent doses were calculated from ToxCast high-throughput screening bioactivity data using the reverse dosimetry workflow implemented in the ctxR (version 1.1.3) and httk (version 2.7.0) packages (Kruse et al., 2025; Pearce et al., 2017). For each chemical, ToxCast bioactivity was obtained from its DTXSID using get_bioactivity_details(). We computed AC50 quantiles in μM restricted to assays with hit call >0.95 to focus on confidently active responses (median = 50th percentile; lower bound = 5th percentile). Using HTTK predictions were obtained using get_httk_data(), and we extracted the predicted 95th percentile human steady-state plasma concentration (Css) for the 3-compartment model. This approach follows established IVIVE frameworks that assume steady-state conditions and use conservative toxicokinetic parameters to derive broadly protective estimates rather than endpoint-specific predictions. Css (mg/L) was converted to μM using monoisotopic mass and oral equivalent HED PODs were derived by dividing the AC50 quantiles by the Css.

QSAR model 1: conditional toxicity value predictor

We applied the machine learning-based CTV predictor developed by (Wignall et al., 2018), trained on PODs from regulatory assessments. A key feature of the CTV approach is the incorporation of an applicability domain metric based on the distance in chemical structure/property descriptor space, allowing transparent identification of chemicals for which predictions are reliable. We prepared an input file containing CASRN, SMILES, chemical name, and monoisotopic mass and processed it through the batch version of the CTV tool. We retained only predictions within the applicability domain (z-score <3). The model predicts both RfD-associated NOAELs and benchmark dose lower bounds (BMDLs) on an animal dose basis, along with 95% prediction intervals derived from cross-validation. We converted predicted values to HED PODs using a rat-to-human allometric scaling factor of 0.25 (i.e., HED = 0.25 × predicted POD) and selected the more conservative (minimum) NOAEL vs. BMDL value for each chemical.

QSAR model 2: two-stage machine learning

We obtained predictions from the two-stage machine learning framework developed by (Kvasnicka et al., 2024), which builds upon the database-calibrated PODs (already expressed as HEDs) from (Aurisano et al., 2023). This approach incorporates an intermediate prediction layer for physical, chemical, and toxicological properties, enabling improved generalization to chemicals outside the original training set. The model architecture includes separate modules for general non-cancer and reproductive/developmental endpoints, with uncertainty quantification provided through cross-validation. We intersected the PPRTV chemical list with precomputed predictions from (Kvasnicka et al., 2024), available at https://wchiu.shinyapps.io/Two-Stage-ML-Results-Browser/, and selected the minimum of the predicted general non-cancer and reproductive/developmental PODs.

QSAR model 3: uncertainty-aware machine learning

We applied the uncertainty-aware POD prediction model described by (von Borries et al., 2026). Like the (Kvasnicka et al., 2024) approach, this model is trained on the database-calibrated PODs (expressed as HEDs) from Aurisano et al. (2023) but employs conformal prediction methods to characterize prediction-specific uncertainty in a distribution-free manner. This approach provides well-calibrated prediction intervals that account for both epistemic uncertainty (model limitations) and aleatoric uncertainty (inherent data variability) We submitted SMILES for the PPRTV chemicals to the web application accompanying the manuscript (PODUAM: POD Uncertainty-Aware Model, available at https://dtu-quantitative-sustainability-assessment.shinyapps.io/poduam/) and imported predictions provided on the log10(mg/kg-day) scale for both general non-cancer and reproductive/developmental PODs, including 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. We transformed to linear scale (10predicted value) and selected the minimum across the two endpoints.

Evaluation metrics

We then evaluated the results across four key metrics.

Applicability domain

Percentage of the 41 chemicals for which a POD was successfully derived.

Conservatism

Frequency with which each method produced the lowest (most protective) POD among all methods, evaluated using point estimates, lower bounds, as well as a hybrid comparison using qRAx lower bounds vs. other methods’ point estimates.

Precision (uncertainty)

Fold-difference between point estimate and lower bound.

Consistency

Correlation coefficients (Spearman) and fold-differences in POD values between methods, evaluated using point estimates, lower bounds, as well as a hybrid comparison using qRAx lower bounds vs. other methods’ point estimates. We also evaluated the performance of combining multiple NAMs, testing all possible combinations of 2 and 3 methods to identify which combinations maximized chemical coverage while maintaining reasonable agreement. This tiered approach follows emerging best practices for integrating multiple lines of evidence in NAM-based risk assessment.

Data availability

All input data, model code, and results are available on GitHub (https://github.com/wachiuphd/qRAx-invitro-insilico-PODs).

Results

The results of applying all six NAMs to the 41 selected chemicals are shown in Fig. 2. Variation in NAM-derived PODs for the same chemical ranged from ~2-fold (e.g., point estimate for 2-methyl-2-pentanol and lower bound for 2,3-benzofluorene) to spanning multiple orders of magnitude (e.g., delta-hexachlorocyclohexan and p,p’-DDD).

POD coverage across NAMs

Success rates for generating PODs varied substantially across approaches (Fig. 3A). Notably, while the one of the original quantitative POD QSAR models we tested (Wignall et al., 2018) achieved only 46% coverage (19/41), the two most recent machine learning models (Kvasnicka et al. (2024) [88%, 36/41] and Von Borries et al. (2026) [100%, 41/41]) achieved greater coverage than qRAx (83%, 34/41), showing how progress in QSAR modeling can be more broadly-applicable than expert-based approaches. The in vitro IVIVE approach based on ToxCast had the poorest coverage at only 22% (9/41), reflecting a combination of the absence of tests for bioactivity in the ToxCast program for some chemicals and the lack of HTTK parameters required for dose extrapolation. Finally, the coverage of ToxValDB approach (66%, 27/41) simply reflects the availability of in vivo data in the database.

Relative conservatism: frequency of minimum PODs

When examining which method produced the most protective (lowest) POD for each chemical, qRAx was most frequently the most conservative approach, whether when using point estimates (Fig. 3B) or lower bound estimates (Supplemental Figure S3). Interestingly, the ToxValDB approach was the next most conservative for point estimates (Fig. 3B), but not for lower bound estimates, reflecting their lower uncertainty. Among the QSAR models, the (Wignall et al., 2018) model was most frequently the most conservative, either for point estimates or lower bounds. Finally, ToxCast was most conservative for only two substances, which is 5% overall (2/41) and 22% (2/9) of the cases where values were available. Overall, this demonstrates that expert-based qRAx point estimates are often conservative relative to other NAM point estimates. Furthermore, the application of conservative composite uncertainty factors (often 100-fold or greater) makes qRAx lower bounds substantially more protective than other methods.

Precision (uncertainty) of PODs

Fold uncertainty, defined as POD(point)/POD(lower bound), also differed across methods (Fig. 3C). The ToxValDB approach demonstrated the lowest median uncertainty (approximately 5-fold), reflecting its calibration against existing in vivo data and use of empirically-derived geometric standard deviations. QSAR models exhibited median uncertainties ranging from approximately 15- to 50-fold, with variations reflecting differences in training data, model architecture, and uncertainty quantification approaches: the (Wignall et al., 2018) model and the (Kvasnicka et al., 2024) two-stage model both estimate prediction error from cross-validation residuals, whereas (von Borries et al., 2026) applies conformal mapping for distribution-free uncertainty bounds. qRAx (PPRTV) showed the median uncertainty of approximately 30-fold, reflecting the multiplicative effect of applied uncertainty factors for LOAEL-to-NOAEL, Subchronic-to-Chronic, and Database deficiencies. ToxCast showed intermediate uncertainty (approximately 15-fold) based on the 5th-to-50th percentile range of active assay AC50 values. The chemical-to-chemical variation in uncertainties was greatest for qRAx, ranging from 1- to 1000-, followed by the (von Borries et al., 2026) QSAR, with a range from 4- to almost 300-fold due to its estimates of uncertainty being tailored to the precision for each prediction.

Consistency across methods

Fold-differences between NAM-derived PODs and qRAx typically ranged from 0.1- to 100-fold (Fig. 4). For point estimates (Fig. 4A), all methods except ToxCast had median fold-differences compared to qRAx within an order of magnitude, with ToxValDB and (Wignall et al., 2018) QSAR showing the tightest agreement. This concordance likely reflects the fact that all these NAMs are calibrated to PODs derived from regulatory assessments, creating methodological alignment with the expert-driven PPRTV framework, whereas the ToxCast approach appears to be less protective on average. For lower bounds (Fig. 4B), all the fold-differences are shifted to larger values, reflecting differences in how uncertainty is quantified across methods, and in particular the fact that the qRAx method has the greatest range and greatest values of uncertainty.

Pairwise correlation analysis revealed varying degree of agreement between methods. For point estimates (Fig. 5), the strongest correlations with qRAx were observed for ToxValDB (Spearman ρ = 0.700, p < 0.001) and von Borries (ρ = 0.866, p < 0.001). Different QSAR models also showed moderate to strong correlations with each other (ρ = 0.558–0.899), reflecting shared reliance on molecular descriptors and, in some cases, shared training data. Notably, correlations were stratified by the magnitude of uncertainty factors applied in qRAx, with chemicals requiring larger composite uncertainty factors showing greater divergence (i.e., among significant correlations, ρ was greater for substances with lower uncertainty factors).

For lower bound estimates for all methods or PPRTVs alone (Figures S4–S5), correlations were generally lower, but the patterns were similar. The strongest (significant) correlations with qRAx continued to be for ToxValDB and (von Borries et al., 2026) QSAR (ρ = 0.621–0.654 and 0.520–0.685, respectively; all p < 0.01), and the various QSAR models showed correlations with each other (ρ = 0.688–0.842, all p < 0.01).

Combined NAM approaches

Because no NAM was top across all evaluation metrics, combining methods can be a way to improve overall performance (Figures S1–S2). For instance, combining qRAx with either ToxValDB or any of the QSARs can increase coverage to >90%, and including a trio of qRAx, ToxValDB, and any of the QSARs can increase coverage to >95%. Similar coverage can be obtained by combining ToxValDB with either the (Kvasnicka et al., 2024) or (von Borries et al., 2026) QSAR, thus combining the lower uncertainty from ToxValDB as compared to qRAx with the greater coverage offered by QSARs. This result supports the conclusion that well-calibrated in silico methods with quantified uncertainty and defined applicability domains with respect to chemical structure/properties can provide consistent, transparent alternatives to resource-intensive expert judgment, particularly when time-sensitive human health risk decisions are needed. Interestingly, adding ToxCast to any method provided only marginal increases in coverage. Overall, this suggests that a strategic combination of expert judgment, database calibration, and machine learning can maximize applicability while maintaining reasonable consistency.

Discussion

Advantages and trade-offs among NAM approaches for prediction of PODs

The comparative analysis presented in this study revealed that each NAM-related approach to quantitative risk assessment has distinct strengths and limitations, underscoring that no single method is universally optimal for deriving points of departure (PODs) in the absence of traditional animal or human toxicity data. Instead, the relative value of each approach depends on the regulatory context, resource availability, and tolerance for uncertainty. Expert-based qRAx – the “human” – demonstrated strong overall performance, achieving high chemical coverage (83%) and producing the most conservative POD estimates among three evaluated approaches. This conservatism may be desirable from a public health protection perspective, particularly in regulatory settings where underestimation of hazard carries substantial consequences. The high coverage achieved by expert-based qRAx reflects its flexibility, as human judgment by experienced risk assessors can consider diverse chemical structures, effects of any potential activity cliffs and metabolic pathways, and leverage heterogeneous data sources that are used to justify similarity. However, this strength is also a limitation: qRAx is resource-intensive, time-consuming, and inherently dependent on assessor’s expertise and potential implicit biases. Regulatory experts may not be as familiar with the latest scientific evidence and NAMs as those in the academia, industry or consulting; therefore, the conservatism in expert decisions may slow adoption of NAMs. In this respect, previous evaluations of qualitative read-across justifications under REACH have shown low acceptance rates (Roe et al., 2026), highlighting concerns about disagreements in data interpretation between the industry and the regulators that may be difficult to systematize or address. These concerns are amplified for qRAx applications, where decisions may rely almost entirely on extrapolated dose-response information and be deemed too conservative by the regulated parties.

Database-calibrated approaches – the machine – exemplified here by ToxValDB-based methods, achieved moderate coverage (66%) while demonstrating strong concordance with expert-based qRAx (low fold-difference and high correlation). Notably, these methods exhibited the lowest uncertainty among the evaluated computational approaches. By systematically leveraging curated in vivo toxicity data, database-calibrated methods retain chemical-specific information that may be lost in purely structure-based predictive models. This likely explains their strong agreement with expert judgment while offering greater transparency, reproducibility, and scalability. These approaches differ from more traditional QSARs because they are less of a “black box” of a model and the end-users can examine the underlying data and assumptions more easily than that in a purely machine learning approach. Therefore, our findings support for the utility of, and growing regulatory interest in database-calibrated workflows, such as those formalized in the recent U.S. EPA’s DCAP (U. S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2025).

Recent machine learning–based QSAR models demonstrated the highest coverage among all approaches, with applicability domain with respect to chemical structure/properties ranging from 88% to 100%. This near-universal coverage highlights the scalability and efficiency of modern computational methods, particularly for large chemical inventories or time-sensitive assessments. However, these models exhibited greater uncertainty than database-calibrated approaches and, in some cases, produced less conservative POD estimates than expert-based qRAx. This trade-off reflects the reliance of machine learning models on training data density and representativeness within specific regions of chemical space. While uncertainty-aware implementations partially mitigate this limitation, these findings reinforce the need to interpret QSAR-derived PODs within the context of model applicability domains and uncertainty estimates.

In contrast, IVIVE-based PODs derived from ToxCast bioactivity data – the “cells” – showed limited applicability, covering only 22% of the evaluated chemicals. This restricted coverage likely reflects multiple factors, including the absence of measurable bioactivity for many chemicals, limitations in assay sensitivity, and the relatively narrow biological domains represented in current high-throughput screening platforms. It has been noted that IVIVE-derived PODs are generally intended to be broadly protective rather than predictive of specific adverse outcomes (Paul Friedman et al., 2025), which may further limit their suitability for chemicals that do not elicit robust in vitro responses. However, we found that IVIVE is actually not any more protective, and is sometimes less protective, than either database-calibrated or machine learning approaches. While in vitro NAMs coupled to IVIVE remain a powerful tool for prioritization and screening, our results suggest that, in its current form and with currently available large-scale data such as ToxCast, they are unlikely to serve as a standalone solution for deriving chronic oral PODs across broad chemical inventories.

Practical recommendations for POD derivation workflows

Building on these comparative results, we propose a tiered workflow for deriving PODs when traditional animal or human toxicity data are unavailable. This workflow is designed to balance efficiency, transparency, and scientific robustness while prioritizing methods with demonstrated concordance to expert judgment.

In Tier 1, we recommend beginning with a database-calibrated in silico assessment, such as the ToxValDB approach proposed by Aurisano et al. (2023) and recently formalized by the U.S. EPA as DCAP (U. S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2025). This tier offers a favorable balance between coverage, uncertainty, and agreement with expert-derived qRAx values, while remaining rapid and reproducible.

S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2025). This tier offers a favorable balance between coverage, uncertainty, and agreement with expert-derived qRAx values, while remaining rapid and reproducible.

(2026), particularly when Tier 1 does not yield a POD or when additional lines of evidence are desired. (2026), there are certain areas of chemical space, such as inorganics and polychlorinated/brominated compounds, where there are “hotspots” of uncertainty.

Our results indicate that combining database-calibrated and machine learning approaches can achieve coverage exceeding 95%, while maintaining reasonable agreement with expert-based qRAx and enabling rapid deployment.

Comparison with traditional toxicity-based risk assessment

Traditional chemical risk assessment relies heavily on animal toxicity studies, which provide direct biological observations but are costly, time-consuming, and increasingly constrained by ethical and practical considerations. The NAM approaches evaluated in this study offer several clear advantages over traditional testing paradigms. First, expediency is a critical advantage: computational methods can generate POD estimates within minutes to hours, compared to months or years required for animal studies, in vitro tests, or expert-driven qRAx. Second, transparency and reproducibility are enhanced for algorithm-based approaches, which reduce assessor-to-assessor variability inherent in expert-driven evaluations. Third, computational approaches are highly cost-effective, requiring minimal resources once models and databases are established. Finally, modern machine learning methods offer broad applicability, enabling the evaluation of nearly any organic chemical while providing uncertainty estimates that reflect data density and consistency within the training space.

Nevertheless, NAM-derived PODs have important limitations. Uncertainty remains higher for chemicals outside well-represented regions of chemical space, and current NAMs cannot fully capture complex toxicodynamic processes or chronic adaptive responses. Despite these limitations, the observation that most NAM-derived PODs fall within one order of magnitude of expert-based qRAx values suggests that these approaches provide reasonable approximations for screening-level assessments, prioritization, and preliminary risk evaluation. Moreover, uncertainty-aware machine learning algorithms, such as those implemented by von Borries et al. (2026), provide a quantitative characterization of the degree of uncertainty due to degree of representation in the training set and of model and data precision, allowing one to give appropriate weight to each prediction.

Regulatory implications

The demonstrated performance of computational NAMs has important implications for regulatory decision-making. In situations requiring rapid responses, such as emerging contaminants, data-poor chemicals, or large-scale inventory assessments, well-calibrated in silico approaches can provide scientifically defensible PODs without the delays associated with traditional testing or extensive expert review. The formalization of database-calibrated methods into regulatory workflows, such as DCAP, represents a significant step toward broader regulatory acceptance of NAM-derived toxicity values. Our findings further suggest that confidence in NAM-based PODs can be enhanced through the combined use of multiple approaches. Agreement across independent NAMs, particularly when aligned with expert-based qRAx, may serve as a practical surrogate for traditional validation in data-poor contexts. Given the increased interest in chemical regulation that is risk-based, which requires quantitative hazard indicators such as PODs, rather than hazard-based, which only require identifying toxic endpoints, (Marx-Stoelting et al., 2026), the comparisons between the quantitative approaches presented herein provide practical path forward for how different types of NAMs can be used to inform regulatory decisions.

Study limitations and data needs

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analysis was limited to 41 chemicals previously evaluated by the EPA PPRTV program, chemical space that does not fully represent the diversity of chemicals of concern to the regulators. Second, the focus on oral chronic PODs limits generalizability to other exposure routes, durations, or regulatory contexts. Third, true predictive accuracy could not be assessed in the absence of comprehensive animal toxicity data for all evaluated chemicals. Additionally, the use of ToxCast to represent “cells” was based on its being the largest available high-throughput screening database; however, not all chemicals were tested in all assays, reverse dosimetry-based IVIVE was not available for many chemicals, and many assays were cell-free or lacked metabolism. Finally, expert-based qRAx values themselves are subject to uncertainty and should not be interpreted as definitive benchmarks, but rather as informed reference points.

Future work should expand these comparisons to larger and more chemically diverse datasets, including substances with emerging in vivo data. A larger sample size will also enable closer investigation for “cells” as to whether certain in vitro assays have better concordance than others. Additional priorities include the development of standardized confidence metrics to identify conditions under which NAM predictions are most reliable, integration of multiple NAMs through ensemble or Bayesian frameworks, and extension of these approaches to other toxicological endpoints and exposure scenarios.

Conclusions

As computational methods continue to mature and regulatory frameworks evolve, NAM-derived PODs are likely to play an increasingly central role in chemical risk assessment. This comparative analysis demonstrates that well-calibrated and uncertainty-aware in silico approaches can provide rapid, transparent, and broadly applicable alternatives to resource-intensive expert judgment and traditional animal testing. In this dataset of 41 chemicals, modern machine learning QSAR models achieved coverage approaching 100%, while database-calibrated approaches showed strong concordance with expert-based qRAx. A workflow combining database-calibrated assessments with machine learning predictions can therefore deliver high coverage while maintaining POD estimates largely within one order of magnitude of expert-derived values. Such approaches offer consistent and scalable solutions for time-sensitive human health risk decisions when traditional toxicity data are unavailable.
