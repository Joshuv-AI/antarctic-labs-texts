Pain-responsive to digital self-management in lumbar disc herniation: the mediating role of patient activation and the moderating effect of pain intensity
Authors: Li-bing Liang, Hong-yun Zhao, Hui-juan Cao, Li-hua Wang, Min-rui Shi, Shan Wang, Cai-qin Wu, Kun-peng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelf-management behaviors (SMB) are fundamental to outcomes in lumbar disc herniation (LDH), yet those remain frequently suboptimal. Electronic health literacy (eHL) may support SMB, but the mechanisms are unclear. Guided by the Information-Motivation-Behavioral Skills model, we investigated whether patient activation (PA) mediates the association between eHL and SMB among LDH patients, and whether pain intensity moderates this pathway.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, 402 patients with LDH were recruited from a tertiary grade-A hospital in Shanghai, China. This study was conducted from November 2023 to April 2025. Data were collected using a general information questionnaire, the eHealth Literacy Scale, the Patient Activation Measure, the Self-Management Scale for Chronic Patients, and the Numerical Pain Rating Scale. Statistical analyses were performed using independent samples t-tests, one-way analysis of variance, Pearson correlation analysis, multiple linear regression, and the PROCESS macro for SPSS.ResultsThe average SMB score among LDH patients was 24.58 ± 9.67, with significantly higher scores observed in those under 40 years old, employed, holding a bachelor’s degree or higher, urban residents living alone, individuals with lower monthly income, and those with fewer hospitalizations. It showed that eHL was positive correlations with PA and SMB, while pain intensity was negatively correlated with three all. PA partially mediated the relationship between eHL and exercise (indirect effect: β = 0.132, 95% CI [0.035, 0.228]) and doctor–patient communication (β = 0.053, [0.014, 0.093]) dimensions of SMB, but not cognitive symptom management. Critically, Pain intensity negatively moderated the indirect effect of eHL through PA: the mediated pathway was decreased by over 60% at high pain levels compared to low pain.ConclusionsThis study provides evidence that PA mediates the association between eHL and SMB in LDH. More importantly, his mediation pathway is progressively attenuated by increasing pain intensity, indicating that pain functions as a barrier to the translation of digital health skills into motivation and behavior. These findings support the development of pain-responsive self-management interventions that dynamically adjust support based on real-time pain levels.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14539-6.Keywords: Lumbar disc herniation, Lumbar disc disease, eHealth literacy, Patient activation, Self-management behaviors, Pain

Introduction

Lumbar Disc Herniation (LDH) is a leading cause of disability worldwide, with a prevalence of 8–25% in China and an increasing trend among younger populations due to sedentary lifestyles [1–4]. While non-surgical management is the first-line approach and spontaneous resolution can occur [5], current treatments—both conservative and surgical—primarily offer short-term symptomatic relief [6]. Critically, they fail to reverse the underlying degenerative process and largely overlook the psychosocial dimensions of chronic pain and the necessity for sustained to prevent recurrence [6, 7]. This fundamental gap is particularly evident in the difficulty of maintaining patient adherence to exercise and lifestyle modifications beyond the acute pain phase [7], underscoring the need for innovative approaches that address the root causes of poor self-management.

Self-management behaviors (SMB), defined as an individual’s active role in controlling their health condition, has become a cornerstone of rehabilitation for chronic musculoskeletal pain [8, 9]. For patients with LDH, effective SMB is vital to slow disease progression, minimize recurrence, and improve overall physical function and quality of life [10]. In reality, however, the execution of SMB remains generally suboptimal, with particular shortfalls observed in adherence to functional exercise and necessary long-term behavioral adaptations [11, 12]. Access to health information is a well-established driver of health behavior change [13–15], but traditional self-management support is often hampered by geographical, time, and resource constraints [16].

The digital health era offers new pathways to overcome these limitations. eHealth literacy (eHL), defined as the ability to seek, understand, and apply health information from electronic resources, has emerged as a potential enabler for enhancing SMB [17]. Specifically, it measures the patient’s core capacity to access, comprehend, and apply health-related information within digital environments. This capacity serves not only as an instrumental foundation for behavioral change but also as an indirect reflection of the caliber of information available to the patient [17]. Empirical studies support the effectiveness of web-based interventions in improving outcomes for chronic low back pain [18, 19]. However, within the LDH context, the specific mechanisms linking eHL and SMB remain unclear, and low eHL has thus far only been preliminarily associated with an increased risk of delayed medical consultation [20]. A larger debate persists regarding whether eHL can meaningfully drive changes in SMB, and whether it serves as a critical mediator translating digital information acquisition into actual behavioral change [21].

The sustained translation of advantages in accessing, understanding, and applying information into tangible health behaviors necessitates robust intrinsic motivation [22, 23]. Patient Activation (PA) refers to an individual’s awareness, knowledge, skills, and confidence in managing their own health and healthcare, serving as a pivotal indicator of their subjective agency in health management [24]. Research indicates that patients with higher activation levels are not only more adept at disease self-management but also more inclined to collaborate with healthcare professionals, acquire knowledge to prevent disease exacerbation, and proactively seek health information. This enables them to consciously maintain and promote positive health behaviors, thereby achieving superior health outcomes [25]. Such a process enhances the efficiency of healthcare service utilization, curtails resource wastage, and consequently reduces treatment costs [26]. PA is not a unidimensional psychological trait but rather a progressive psychological developmental process reflecting the patient’s transition from a “passive recipient” to an “active manager.” This evolution encompasses four incremental stages: first, recognizing the importance of one’s role in health management; second, acquiring relevant knowledge and building confidence; third, translating confidence into tangible actions; and fourth, sustaining health behaviors even under stressful circumstances [24]. Thus, PA embodies the patient’s intrinsic readiness, confidence, and self-efficacy—encapsulating the internal drive of “whether I should participate” and “whether I am capable of doing so.”

Individuals with high PA demonstrate stronger health-promoting behaviors and disease management capabilities, whereas low PA is linked to inadequate health cognition and limited problem-solving abilities [27]. Unfortunately, PA levels are generally low among LDH patients [7]. While eHL contributes to the “skills” of health management, PA represents the “mindset” that drives behavioral change [28]. A key research gap remains: the interactive pathways among eHL, PA, and SMB within the LDH population are not yet established. In particular, the mechanism through which eHL may enhance SMB by elevating PA urgently requires empirical validation.

The Information-Motivation-Behavioral Skills (IMB) model provides a robust theoretical framework for elucidating this complex mechanism [29], and has been successfully applied in various health behavior studies [30–33]. The model emphasizes that behavioral change requires the synergistic interaction of three elements: information (accurate cognition), motivation (internal drive), and behavioral skills (practical ability) [29]. In this study, a digital health behavioral pathway was constructed based on the IMB model. Specifically, eHL functions as a meta-skill—an instrumental capacity for accessing and applying informational resources. It constitutes the foundational skill set enabling patients to effectively perform SMB and indirectly reflects the quality of information accessible to them. Within the IMB framework, eHL is positioned as a specific manifestation or critical subset of “Behavioral Skills” in the digital health era. PA comprehensively represents the patient’s intrinsic readiness, confidence level, and perceived capability—precisely encapsulating the core essence of the “Motivation” construct in the IMB model: the personal attitudes, intrinsic drive, and resilience that propel behavioral change. SMB, in turn, is clearly defined as the behavior itself—the health management activities patients actually execute in daily life (e.g., exercise, cognitive symptom management, patient-provider communication). According to the IMB model, behavioral skills (eHL) facilitate improvements in behavior (SMB) by enhancing motivation (PA). Furthermore, Kratz et al. identified self-efficacy as a key mediator in the translation of information into behavior among individuals with multiple sclerosis [34], providing cross-disease empirical support for the core pathway of this study: PA (motivation) mediating the relationship between eHL (behavioral skills) and SMB (behavior). Notably, pain, as a core symptom of LDH, may interfere with these transformational pathways [35]. Pain intensity not only directly diminishes both PA and SMB [36], but may also hinder the effective translation of eHL into PA, PA into SMB, and eHL directly into SMB [37]. Empirical evidence indicates that although most chronic pain patients rely on online resources for pain-related information [38], high pain intensity significantly reduces patients’ efficiency in utilizing health information to enhance self-management effectiveness, thereby creating barriers to SMB implementation [39].

Despite recognition of these individual factors, existing research often examines them in isolation. A systematic investigation is notably absent regarding the mediating pathway through which eHL influences SMB via PA in LDH populations, as well as the potential moderating effect of pain on this pathway. Therefore, grounded in the IMB model, this study aims to address two primary questions: (1) Does PA mediate the relationship between eHL and SMB? (2) Does pain intensity moderate this mediated pathway? The specific hypothesized model is presented in Fig. 1. Elucidating this moderated mediation mechanism holds substantial value for understanding the root causes of self-management difficulties in LDH patients and for informing the development of precise, dynamically tailored interventions, such as adjusting motivational support in response to fluctuating pain levels.

Methods

Research design and participants

This cross-sectional study employed convenience sampling to recruit patients with lumbar disc herniation receiving non-surgical treatment from the orthopedic departments of a tertiary hospital in Shanghai, China, between November 2023 and April 2025.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) meeting the diagnostic criteria outlined in the Chinese Pain Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of LDH [40], which requires fulfilling at least three of the following five clinical features—radiating lower limb pain consistent with the affected nerve root distribution, sensory abnormalities in the corresponding dermatome, positive straight leg raise test (or its variants), diminished tendon reflexes compared to the unaffected side, and muscle weakness—in combination with (6) lumbar MRI or CT evidence of disc herniation corresponding to the symptomatic nerve compression; (2) undergoing non-surgical management for LDH; (3) age ≥ 18 years; (4) conscious, literate, and able to communicate effectively; (5) provision of informed consent.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) concomitant other lumbar conditions such as spinal tumors or spondylolisthesis; (2) history of psychiatric or cognitive disorders; (3) major impairment of heart, lung, kidney, or other organs.

Sample size

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power 3.1 software. Based on the study design (multiple regression analysis) and primary analytical objectives (testing mediation and moderation effects), the parameters were set as follows: medium effect size (f² = 0.25), significance level (α = 0.05), and statistical power (1-β = 0.95). The total number of predictor variables was set at 18, including 10 general demographic variables, 1 pain-related variable, 3 dimensions of eHL, and 4 dimensions of PA. The initial calculation indicated a minimum required sample size of 213 participants. To account for a potential 20% attrition rate, the sample size was increased to 267 participants. To ensure analytical robustness and accommodate potential model complexity, the study ultimately successfully included and analyzed valid data from 402 patients with lumbar disc herniation.

Data collection

Trained researchers systematically recruited eligible LDH patients from the orthopedic outpatient clinics and inpatient wards of the hospital. The measurements for this study were conducted during patient consultations or hospitalizations, meaning that questionnaires were completed on-site at the time participants sought medical attention due to symptoms or were admitted for treatment. Potential participants received comprehensive information regarding the study’s purpose and voluntary nature. Those who agreed to participate signed an informed consent form and completed a questionnaire requiring approximately 15 to 20 min. All data were collected on a one-on-one basis, using either paper-based forms or electronic questionnaires (https://www.wjx.cn/), allowing sufficient time and privacy for independent completion. Researchers were available to address questions and provide necessary assistance. No instances of withdrawal occurred, as the questionnaire was completed in a single session. Upon completion, all questionnaires were reviewed to ensure data completeness. A total of 420 questionnaires were distributed, and 402 valid responses were collected, yielding an effective response rate of 95.7%. The 18 invalid questionnaires were excluded for the following reasons: patterned responses (e.g., identical answers across all items, n = 6), missing key variables (n = 8), and excessively short completion times (less than 5 min, n = 4). In cases of missing key variables, the corresponding cases were directly excluded without further analysis to ensure data integrity.

Measures

Demographic characteristics

Participant data were collected using a self-developed information form (see Supplementary File 1), which included gender, age, education level, employment status, marital status, place of residence, living arrangement, average monthly income per capita, healthcare payment methods, number of chronic conditions, pain intensity, duration of illness, and frequency of hospital admissions.

eHealth literacy scale (eHEALS)

The study employed the Chinese version of the eHEALS scale, adapted by Guo Shuaijun et al. (2013) [41], to assess eHealth literacy. This instrument comprises eight items distributed across three dimensions: applied competence in using online health information and services (5 items), critical evaluation ability (2 items), and decision-making capacity (1 item). Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”). Higher total scores indicate stronger eHL levels. The scale demonstrated excellent reliability with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.913, and factor loadings ranged from 0.692 to 0.869, confirming its robust psychometric properties [41].

Patient activation measure (PAM)

The PAM was originally developed by Hibbard et al. (2004) to assess patient activation levels [27]. This scale measures patients across four progressive stages—from “recognizing the importance of their role” to “sustaining behaviors under stress”—comprehensively reflecting their confidence, knowledge, and intrinsic drive in managing their health. Raw scores were standardized and converted to a 0–100 scale, with higher scores indicating greater activation levels, i.e., stronger motivation. Participants were categorized into four activation levels: ≤45.0 indicates low activation; 45.1–55.1 represents low to moderate activation; 55.2–67.0 signifies moderate activation; and ≥ 67.1 reflects high activation. Hong Yang et al. demonstrated its strong psychometric properties in patients undergoing lumbar fusion surgery, reporting a Cronbach’s α of 0.835 [42].

Numerical rating scale (NRS)

Participants rated their low back pain intensity using an 11-point NRS ranging from 0 (“no pain”) to 10 (“worst pain imaginable”): no pain (0), mild pain (1–3), moderate pain (4–6), and severe pain (7–10) [43]. A reduction of ≥ 2 points on the NRS was considered clinically meaningful, corresponding to the Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID). The test-retest reliability of the NRS was 0.92 [44]. This study measured patients’ average pain intensity over the past week.

Chronic disease self-management behavior measuring scale (CDSMBMS)

The Self-Management Behavior Scale, originally developed by Lorig et al. [45], comprises 15 items organized into three dimensions. Responses are recorded using a 5-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 0 to 69. In the cognitive symptom management dimension, scores from 0 to 4 correspond to frequency levels: “never,” “occasionally,” “sometimes,” “frequently,” and “very frequently.” This dimension measures the frequency with which patients employ cognitive strategies—such as attentional diversion, relaxation exercises, and positive self-talk—to manage disease-related symptoms (e.g., pain, discomfort). The doctor-patient communication dimension follows the same scoring pattern. For the exercise dimension, scores 0 through 4 represent weekly exercise durations: “no exercise,” “less than 30 minutes,” “30–59 minutes,” “1–3 hours,” and “over 3 hours.” The scale demonstrates good reliability with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.866 [46].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS (Version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Participants’ demographic characteristics were described using mean (M) and standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed variables; otherwise, median (M) and interquartile range (IQR) were reported. Categorical data were summarized using frequencies (N) and percentages (%). Normality of the data was assessed through skewness and kurtosis values, which ranged from − 0.35 to + 1.92. Falling within an acceptable range, these values justified the use of parametric tests in subsequent analyses [47]. Homogeneity of variance was examined using Levene’s test, which confirmed that the assumption of homogeneity of variance was met for all primary grouping variables (P > 0.05). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and independent samples t-tests were used to compare SMB scores across different demographic subgroups of LDH patients. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine relationships among key variables: eHL, PA, pain, and SMB. Variables showing statistical significance in univariate analyses were further analyzed using multiple linear regression. Mediation and moderated mediation analyses were carried out using the PROCESS macro (Version 4.0) developed by Hayes (2018). Bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated based on 5000 bootstrap samples. Model 4 was applied to examine whether PA mediated the relationship between eHL and SMB. Model 59 was used to test whether pain moderated the indirect effect of eHL on SMB through PA. All models adjusted for covariates including age, gender, education level, employment status, marital status, monthly income per capita, duration of illness, and number of hospital admissions. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P-value < 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics and sociodemographic disparities in self-management

A total of 420 questionnaires were distributed, and 402 valid questionnaires were retained, yielding a valid response rate of 95.7%. The mean scores for the key variables were as follows: SMB 24.58 ± 9.67, eHL 25.60 ± 7.67, PA 38.10 ± 4.48, and Pain 4.84 ± 1.75. The sociodemographic characteristics of the participants are detailed in Table 1. The sample comprised predominantly older adults (56.8% aged 61 and above), females (66.2%), urban residents (89.6%), and individuals with a monthly income of USD 725–1,450 (69.2%). More than half of the participants (55.7%) had been hospitalized 2–5 times. Univariate analyses revealed significant differences in SMB total and dimension scores across various sociodemographic factors (Table 1). Higher SMB total scores were observed in participants who were younger (< 40 years), employed, held a bachelor’s degree or higher, lived alone, resided in urban areas, had a monthly income below USD 725, and had fewer hospitalizations. This pattern of significant associations was consistent for the exercise and doctor-patient communication dimensions. For the cognitive symptom management dimension, higher scores were significantly associated with younger age, male gender, higher education, employment, living alone, and shorter disease duration.

Correlations among eHealth literacy, patient activation, pain, and self-management

Pearson correlation analysis revealed significant interrelationships among the key study variables (Table 2). As hypothesized, eHL demonstrated significant positive correlations with PA (r = 0.359, P < 0.01) and with overall SMB and its three dimensions (r ranging from 0.302 to 0.389, all P < 0.01). Patient activation also showed consistent positive associations with overall SMB and each subdomain (r ranging from 0.314 to 0.413, all P < 0.01). Conversely, pain intensity was negatively correlated with eHL (r = -0.154, P < 0.01), overall SMB (r = -0.115, P < 0.05), and the exercise and doctor-patient communication dimensions. No significant association was found between pain and cognitive symptom management.

Factors associated with self-management: multiple linear regression analyses

Multiple linear regression analyses identified factors independently associated with SMB after controlling for covariates (Table 3). The overall SMB model was significant (F = 16.520, P < 0.001, adjusted R² = 0.335), with higher PA (B = 0.499, P < 0.001) and greater eHL (B = 0.189, P = 0.002) emerging as the primary independent predictors. Several demographic factors were also significantly associated with the total score (see Table 3). For the exercise dimension (adjusted R² = 0.238), both PA (B = 0.130, P = 0.008) and eHL (B = 0.105, P < 0.001) remained significant positive predictors. A distinct pattern emerged for cognitive symptom management (adjusted R2 = 0.236): while PA was a significant driver (B = 0.127, P = 0.005), the direct effect of eHL was not (B = 0.090, P = 0.079). For doctor-patient communication (adjusted R² = 0.267), higher PA (B = 0.241, P < 0.001) and eHL (B = 0.089, P = 0.010) were both associated with better communication. These results highlight a domain-specific pattern, wherein PA consistently predicts all SMB dimensions, whereas eHL exerts a direct effect on exercise and communication but not on cognitive symptom management.

Mediation analysis

A series of mediation analyses were performed using Andrew Hayes’ PROCESS macro version 4 (Model 4) to investigate the mediating effect of PA on the association between eHL and the overall SMB score, along with its dimensions. The findings revealed that PA significantly mediated the relationships between eHL and the overall SMB score, the exercise dimension, and the doctor-patient communication dimension. Specifically, PA partially mediated the association between eHL and the exercise dimension (β = 0.132, 95% CI [0.035, 0.228], P < 0.001) and between eHL and the doctor-patient communication dimension (β = 0.053, 95% CI [0.014, 0.093], P = 0.008), with mediated proportions accounting for 37.8% and 28.3% of the total effects, respectively. Moderated mediation analysis

Moderated mediation analysis revealed that pain severity significantly moderated the indirect pathway from eHL to SMB via PA (Table 4). For the overall SMB score, pain served as a negative moderator on the eHL-PA relationship (β = -0.042, 95% CI [-0.074, -0.010], P = 0.010), thereby attenuating the indirect effect. A consistent pattern emerged across the exercise and doctor-patient communication dimensions, with pain negatively moderating the eHL-PA pathway (β = -0.047, 95% CI [-0.079, -0.015], P = 0.004).

Simple slope analysis demonstrated a graded effect of pain intensity (Table 5). In both the exercise and doctor-patient communication dimensions, the eHL→PA effect was strongest under low pain conditions (β = 0.226 and 0.224, respectively; both P < 0.001), weakened at moderate pain levels (β = 0.108 and 0.106; both P < 0.001), and declined to marginal significance under high pain (β = 0.085 and 0.083, P = 0.006 and 0.009). This represented a reduction in effect size of approximately 62% in the high pain group compared to low pain. Conditional indirect effect analysis confirmed this graded attenuation: under high pain, the mediated impact on exercise (β = 0.011) and doctor-patient communication (β = 0.017) was substantially weaker than under moderate or low pain conditions. These findings indicate that elevated pain levels progressively diminish the effectiveness of eHL in enhancing self-management behaviors through patient activation.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study revealed that SMB among LDH patients were generally found to be low. Notably, patients under 40 years of age, those with higher education levels, and those currently employed demonstrated significantly higher SMB. This research is also the first to validate a digital health behavior pathway based on the IMB model within an LDH population: PA mediates the association between eHL and SMB. Specifically, PA served as a partial mediator between eHL and both exercise and doctor-patient communication. In the dimension of cognitive symptom management, PA acted as a sole and direct driver. More importantly, the study uncovered a graded moderating effect of pain intensity: pain negatively moderated the pathways from eHL through PA to both exercise and communication. When patients experienced high levels of pain, the indirect effects in these two dimensions exhibited a stepwise attenuation pattern compared to those in low and moderate pain groups. This finding provides a mechanistic explanation for understanding barriers to SMB in LDH patients—pain is not only a core symptom but also a critical moderating variable in self-management behavior change.

Mediation mechanisms: IMB model interpretation in the digital context

The findings of this study indicate that the average SMB score among LHD patients was 24.58 ± 9.67, reflecting an overall low level of self-management behavior—a result consistent with previous research [12]. Furthermore, PA was identified as a partial mediator between eHL and SMB. This finding aligns with the mediating role of self-efficacy observed in Kratz et al.‘s study on multiple sclerosis [34]. As a critical behavioral skill, eHL significantly enhances patients’ PA, which in turn promotes improvements in their SMB—validating the core pathway of the IMB model, wherein “skills empower motivation, and motivation drives behavior.” Individuals with more information and stronger personal and social motivation tend to exhibit better self-care and self-management behaviors [48]. Supporting this, Sheng et al. found that individuals with high eHL are more inclined to seek health information and utilize available resources, which encourages better health management and leads to higher levels of PA [49]. Furthermore, Li et al. demonstrated that older adults with high eHL are more likely to exhibit strong agency in adopting health-promoting behaviors [50]. Another study indicated that patient engagement directly influences the initiative taken in coping with chronic disease [51]. Therefore, reinforcing cognitive health management, strengthening confidence in disease self-management, and improving relevant skills are essential to enhancing long-term treatment awareness among patients with chronic conditions [52].

The “high motivation dependence” mechanism in the dimension of physical exercise

This study provides evidence that PA serves as a significant partial mediator between eHL and exercise behavior, accounting for 37.8% of the mediating effect, with this pathway being significantly moderated by pain intensity. These findings provide insight into the “high motivational demand” characteristic of exercise behavior as a SMB among patients with LDH. Such behavior appears to be associated with dual challenges of physiological barriers and psychological fears. For instance, physiological factors related to LDH, such as nerve root compression, decreased muscle strength, muscle inhibition impairing lumbar stability, restricted joint mobility, and central sensitization causing pain hypersensitivity, may compromise physical capacity [53, 54]. It is hypothesized that these factors indirectly weaken the pathway through which eHL supports the translation of health knowledge into sustained exercise behavior by continuously engaging patients’ limited motivational resources [55]. This interpretation aligns with the theoretical proposition that ongoing physiological demands may compete for cognitive and motivational resources.

Concurrently, evidence indicates that kinesiophobia is prevalent among chronic pain patients, particularly those with LDH [56]. A systematic review further elucidates that kinesiophobia beliefs represent a common psychophysiological phenomenon in LDH patients, characterized by a pathological fear of movement/physical activity, which may stem from catastrophic pain cognitions and negative expectations [56]. Importantly, this study did not directly assess kinesiophobia, cognitive resource depletion, or prefrontal cortex dysfunction. Therefore, the following discussion of these factors should be considered hypothetical. Based on existing literature, it is plausible that kinesiophobia may not only directly restrict daily activities and exercise engagement, potentially contributing to secondary dysfunctions such as muscle weakness, joint stiffness, and reduced cardiorespiratory fitness, but may also be associated with heightened pain perception, potentially contributing to a persistent “pain-fear-avoidance” cycle [57]. Furthermore, kinesiophobia may interact with negative emotions such as anxiety and depression, which could collectively influence patients’ belief in their ability to perform specific tasks and their intrinsic motivation [55].

Based on these observations and the existing theoretical framework, we propose the following hypothesis for the mechanism underlying our findings. It is hypothesized that eHL provides knowledge and resources that may effectively activate intrinsic motivation—PA—by influencing LDH patients’ efficacy beliefs, perceived skills, and sense of responsibility in managing their condition. Patients with high PA may be better able to employ goal-setting strategies and pain-adaptive adjustments to address exercise barriers [58]. This proposed pathway is consistent with the critical “motivation → behavioral skills” sequence in the IMB model. Collectively, these hypotheses underscore the potential central role of enhancing intrinsic motivation in addressing SMB barriers and optimizing the translation of eHL into sustained exercise behavior. Future research measuring these proposed psychological and neurophysiological mechanisms is needed to directly test these hypotheses.

The “instrumental social support motivation” mechanism in the dimension of doctor-patient communication

The results of this study indicate that PA partially mediates the relationship between eHL and patient-physician communication behavior, accounting for 28.3% of the mediating effect. This proportion is notably smaller than its role in exercise behavior, suggesting that the motivational drivers underlying communication as a behavior aimed at acquiring instrumental social support may be distinct [59]. From an informational perspective, clearly explaining the nature of the condition, outlining treatment options, and managing expectations are considered indispensable for optimizing pain and disability management in LDH, ultimately contributing to enhanced treatment outcomes [60]. In this process, eHL plays a crucial role by effectively bridging health information gaps and preparing patients for consultations—for instance, by screening key information such as surgical risks and drug interactions. This may enhance the perceived value of “being well-informed to achieve better treatment,” potentially influencing motivation by strengthening the intrinsic belief that “I am confident in asking precise questions.”

Compared to exercise behavior, communication relies less on physical capacity, which may allow for a more efficient translation of information into action. It is hypothesized that patients with high PA are better able to convert communicative motivation into behavioral skills for acquiring instrumental support. This might be demonstrated through strategies such as using guidelines to formulate question lists and paraphrasing to confirm medical instructions, thereby proactively seeking specific medical resources like prescription adjustments and examination arrangements. Regression analysis further indicates a significant association between PA and patient-physician communication behavior, which is consistent with the view that instrumental social support-seeking behavior is fundamentally motivation-driven.

This study focuses on patient-physician communication as a core pathway for instrumental social support, whose motivational mechanisms exhibit distinct characteristics within the IMB framework. A primary feature is its strong goal orientation. The complexity of lumbar disc herniation treatment stems from diverse clinical presentations and highly individualized therapeutic needs, wherein patients’ communication behaviors are directly associated with the explicit and practical objective of acquiring specific medical resources [61]. A second characteristic is pronounced power asymmetry. Complex treatment options and professional recommendations may exacerbate patients’ information anxiety [62], while the inherent knowledge gap between patients and clinicians itself constitutes a communication barrier. This suggests that patients not only require information but also sufficient motivation to navigate psychological resistance in communication and employ effective behavioral skills for expression.

Notably, generative artificial intelligence technologies—particularly interactive tools capable of delivering personalized and clear medical information—are beginning to transform patient education [63]. Preliminary studies demonstrate the significant potential of large language models in disseminating information to lumbar surgery patients, with positive feedback received [62]. Future research should focus on leveraging such advanced information technology models to more effectively enhance information delivery, support patients’ communication motivation, and strengthen their communicative behavioral skills. This approach could systematically address the unique challenges—as observed in this study—of acquiring instrumental social support within power-asymmetric environments, thereby potentially supporting the IMB framework’s application in clinical communication.

The “motivational core” mechanism in the dimension of cognitive symptom management

This study identifies a notable finding: in the dimension of cognitive symptom management, PA is the only statistically significant driver, while the direct effect of eHL is not significant. This finding aligns with Cashin et al.‘s study on chronic low back pain, which demonstrated that self-efficacy predicts functional outcomes independently of pain intensity—collectively underscoring the unique contribution of motivational variables [64]. This suggests that the fundamental impetus behind effective cognitive self-management may not be directly attributable to the ability to acquire information (eHL), but rather may be primarily associated with patients’ intrinsic composite of knowledge, skills, and confidence—namely, PA. Unlike the behavioral domains of exercise or communication, cognitive symptom management focuses more on the regulation and application of endogenous psychological processes. The present results are consistent with the following proposed mechanism, and offer empirical grounding for its further exploration:

It is hypothesized that eHL enables patients to access information relevant to treatment or disease improvement, but the internalization and translation of this information into sustained cognitive practices may depend substantially on the self-management confidence, disease knowledge, and problem-solving skills encompassed by PA. This interpretation aligns with Hibbard et al.‘s core definition of PA [27]. Many cognitive management strategies—such as techniques from cognitive behavioral therapy and pain diary recording—require patients to actively and consciously execute them amid discomfort or pain. This itself may constitute an effortful behavior that draws upon psychological resources [65]. The intrinsic drive and resilience associated with high PA may provide a crucial internal resource for sustaining this “cognitive effort,” whereas possessing information alone (eHL) may not directly supply such ongoing momentum [66]. This proposed role of PA is consistent with Bandura’s self-efficacy theory, which posits that confidence is central to an individual’s willingness to invest effort and persist in confronting challenges [67]. Furthermore, although eHL lacks direct statistical significance in this model, it correlates significantly with PA. This pattern suggests that eHL may function as a foundational capability, potentially supplying informational raw material and skill support for the construction of PA, thereby indirectly influencing cognitive management behavior through its association with PA. This could represent an “enable-activate” indirect pathway [68].

This finding offers a compelling contrast and theoretical complement to the other two dimensions, collectively contributing to a more complete picture of the IMB model’s application. Within the model, the relative contributions of “Information (I)” and “Motivation (M)” may vary significantly across different “Behaviors (B).” For cognition-intensive behaviors, the weight of motivation (M) may exceed that of information (I) [48]; whereas for execution-oriented and social-interaction behaviors, information (I) may function in concert with motivation (M). This insight refines and expands the application of the IMB model in digital health contexts, suggesting that the role of eHL should not be generalized. Consequently, clinical interventions targeting cognitive symptom management may require a strategic shift in focus: the priority may not merely be to deliver more or higher-quality health information, but to deliberately design activities that enhance PA. Simultaneously, within eHealth platforms, cognitive management modules could be integrated with PA-building components to facilitate the transition from “information” to “motivation and action.”

In summary, this study demonstrates that PA is a key factor associated with cognitive symptom management behaviors in LDH patients. While eHL serves as an important foundational capacity, it is not a direct driver in this context. This finding underscores that in chronic pain management, fostering patients’ inner confidence, knowledge, and skills (i.e., PA) may be more fundamental than merely providing information, offering a theoretical perspective for developing motivation-centric, evidence-based interventions.

Pain’s dual effects and clinical translation: from barrier to intervention cue

The findings of this study indicate that pain intensity exerts a stepwise negative moderating effect on the pathway through which eHL is associated with SMB: specifically, across both physical activity and patient-provider communication dimensions, the indirect effect of eHL on SMB through PA gradually weakens as pain intensity increases. To our knowledge, this study is the first to report that pain functions as a continuous behavioral interfering variable in this context. The observation suggests that beyond a certain high-pain threshold, the association between eHL and PA may diminish, potentially marking a transition of pain from a “manageable symptom” to a “dominant behavioral barrier.” Echoing the findings of Turner et al. in the context of pain treatment, pain not only manifests as a symptom but also functions as a confounding variable in the cognitive-behavioral transformation process. This perspective provides explanatory resources at the psychological mechanism level for the moderating effect of pain observed in the present study [69].

Based on existing theoretical and empirical literature, several mechanisms may underlie this observed moderating effect, though they were not directly tested in the present study and should be considered hypothetical. First, pain may consume cognitive resources; severe pain likely occupies substantial attentional capacity, potentially compromising patients’ ability to process online health information and integrate knowledge, which could in turn hinder the translation of information into motivation (PA) [70]. Second, high pain often co-occurs with negative emotional states such as anxiety and depression, which may directly undermine intrinsic motivation for behavioral change. This pattern is consistent with the “pain-fear-avoidance” model, wherein cycles of negative emotions may further suppress proactive behavioral intentions that eHL might otherwise stimulate [55, 57]. Third, persistent or high-intensity pain may lead to the formation of “effort-outcome futility” beliefs, potentially contributing to learned helplessness and reducing the likelihood that individuals will utilize health information to enhance self-management confidence [71]. Finally, emerging research has further elucidated that chronic pain states are associated with aberrant functional connectivity within the ventral tegmental area-striatal-prefrontal cortex circuit, a disruption directly implicated in motivational behavioral deficits and diminished cognitive control [72, 73]. Pain not only consumes cognitive resources but, more critically, undermines the intrinsic drive to translate health information into sustained behavioral change by disrupting the neural circuits encoding motivation and reward value.

However, clinical strategies predicated solely on pain intensity possess inherent limitations: mild pain may mask the risk of chronification (e.g., through catastrophizing or fear-avoidance), whereas individuals with severe chronic pain often experience information overload due to frequent healthcare encounters, where information input fails to translate into behavioral change. Furthermore, chronic pain can induce functional connectivity abnormalities within the mesocorticolimbic circuit, impairing goal-directed behavior and motivational encoding. This suggests that the intervention starting point for patients in the chronic phase is fundamentally distinct from that in the acute phase [72, 73]. Consequently, clinical translation necessitates a bidimensional or multidimensional assessment framework that concurrently considers pain intensity and chronicity, integrating psychosocial risk indicators. Future self-management support should incorporate dynamic assessment modules enabling adaptive content delivery, embedding pain intensity, pain duration, and psychological risk within a unified framework to achieve truly “pain-responsive care.”

Addressing socio-digital disparities: equity considerations in ehealth implementation

The univariate and multivariate analyses revealed that self-management capabilities were not uniformly distributed across the sample. Specifically, younger age, higher education, urban residence, and living alone were associated with stronger self-management behaviors across multiple dimensions, even among individuals with relatively lower income. These patterns suggest that educational attainment and access to urban resources may buffer the effects of economic disadvantage. In contrast, a higher frequency of hospital admissions was associated with lower self-management scores, which may reflect reduced confidence or perceived ability to manage one’s condition following repeated institutional care. These observed disparities underscore a potential risk: digital health interventions that do not account for sociodemographic differences may unintentionally reinforce existing inequities, particularly among older, rural, and less-educated populations. However, due to the inherent limitations of self-report questionnaires, the average level of eHL observed in this study may be overestimated relative to true population parameters. Consequently, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings to populations with lower educational attainment, rural communities, and older adults.

While these findings cannot support causal or prescriptive policy recommendations, they offer useful signals for future research and hypothesis generation. For instance, the association between higher education and stronger self-management may indicate that tailored digital tools could be more effective when aligned with users’ literacy levels and learning preferences. Similarly, the link between frequent hospitalizations and lower self-management scores invites further investigation into whether discharge planning or follow-up support could be enhanced for this subgroup. Future studies could explore whether peer-supported digital literacy programs, simplified app interfaces, or integrated family-use models might address the needs of underserved groups, particularly in rural or low-education contexts. Nurses and community health workers may play a key role in bridging digital divides, but such strategies require rigorous testing in implementation research before being adopted at scale. Ultimately, ensuring equitable access to eHealth tools will require continued attention to the social determinants of health and their interaction with digital engagement.

Furthermore, this study identified a noteworthy phenomenon: higher income was associated with lower levels of self-management behavior. A plausible explanation is that higher-income participants in this sample were predominantly employed in managerial or professional roles, occupations often characterized by prolonged sedentary behavior and elevated work-related stress. Consequently, time scarcity may constrain their capacity to engage in regular exercise and consistent self-management practices. Additionally, higher-income patients tend to have greater access to high-quality healthcare resources (e.g., specialist consultations, physical therapy), potentially rendering them more inclined to “outsource” the resolution of health issues to professional services, thereby diminishing intrinsic motivation for active self-management. Notably, the “lower-income” group in this study included a substantial proportion of retired individuals, who may invest more time in health management due to greater temporal availability. This suggests that the relationship between income and self-management may be moderated by factors such as time availability, occupational characteristics, and attitudes toward healthcare dependency. Future research should employ objective measures of time use and incorporate occupational variables to further elucidate this nuanced interplay, avoiding the simplistic assumption that economic resources uniformly confer advantages in health behaviors.

Limitations and future research directions

Several limitations warrant consideration. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and cannot rule out reverse causality, while disease duration heterogeneity—despite statistical control—may introduce variability. Reliance on self-report instruments risks social desirability bias and may systematically exclude individuals with lower literacy, potentially overestimating eHL levels. Pain was assessed unidimensionally, and the moderating effect observed may involve unmeasured psychological mechanisms (e.g., anxiety, catastrophizing) or vary across pain types (neuropathic vs. nociceptive). Additionally, the model focused on PA’s mediating role without examining potential synergistic effects between eHL and PA.

Future research should employ longitudinal designs with repeated measures across clinical milestones to establish temporal precedence and capture dynamic relationships among eHL, PA, and SMB. Objective metrics (e.g., wearable devices, pain diaries) and multidimensional pain assessments (e.g., DN4, Oswestry) should complement self-reports to enhance measurement robustness. Mixed methods (e.g., face-to-face interviews) or simplified literacy-appropriate instruments are needed to improve population representativeness. To clarify mechanisms, studies should incorporate repeated psychological assessments and examine pain types separately. Statistical rigor should include sensitivity analyses (e.g., E-value estimation) to quantify unmeasured confounding. Finally, structural equation modeling should explicitly test eHL–PA interactions to verify IMB model assumptions regarding synergistic effects on SMB dimensions.

Conclusions

This study supports the role of PA as a mediator in the association between eHL and SMB in LDH. A central finding is the negative moderating role of pain intensity, which progressively weakens the mediating effect of eHL via PA on exercise and patient-provider communication as pain levels rise. This underscores the necessity of adapting self-management support to a patient’s real-time pain experience. Furthermore, the drivers of SMB vary by domain: motivation (PA) is the primary force behind cognitive symptom management, whereas both information (eHL) and motivation are essential for exercise and communication behaviors. Clinical strategies should leverage digital tools for activated patients while ensuring robust offline support for vulnerable groups, integrating dynamic pain assessment to guide both the intensity and modality of self-management support.
