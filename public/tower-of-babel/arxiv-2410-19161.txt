Conjugating by singular operators On the boundedness of similarity transforms near singular points
Daniel Falkowski & Carl-Fredrik Lidgren

Abstract
We consider the question of, given operators A , Z A,Z and a sequence of invertible operators U n → Z U_{n}\to Z , whether the sequence U n ​ A ​ U n − 1 U_{n}AU_{n}^{-1} is bounded in norm, as well as generalizations of this where U n ​ A ​ U n − 1 U_{n}AU_{n}^{-1} is modified by some bounded linear map on bounded linear operators. In the setting of Hilbert spaces, we provide a complete classification in terms of algebraic criteria of those A A for which such a sequence exists, as long as Z Z is of generalized index zero, which always holds in finite-dimensional contexts. In the process, we prove that particular coefficients arising in inverses of certain good paths going to Z Z can also be classified in terms of an entirely algebraic criterion.

Introduction

1.1
The Problem & the Approach

This document deals with trying to answer the following basic question, which comes up naturally in the context of jointly diagonalizing matrices (see e.g.
[
Tro+24
]
):

Question 1.1.

Let
U_{k}\to Z
be a sequence of invertible matrices converging to a singular matrix
Z
, and let
A
be a square matrix. Does the sequence

\|U_{k}AU_{k}^{-1}\|

diverge to infinity? What if one modifies the sequence by, for example, deleting the diagonal of
U_{k}AU_{k}^{-1}
?

This question is in general hard to answer, as the behaviour of a given “path”
U_{k}\to Z
may be highly particular to the specific path itself. Therefore, as
a simplifying approximation, we may wish to instead ask about all approach paths at once. Doing this naturally introduces two extremes: first, asking for which
pairs
(A,Z)
it holds that for
every
sequence
U_{k}\to Z
, the sequence as in Question
1.1
does not diverge to infinity, which can be rephrased as

Question 1.2.

Let
V=\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Determine for which
Z\in\operatorname{End}(V)
and
A\in\operatorname{End}(V)
it holds that

\limsup_{r\to 0}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\operatorname{Aut}(V)\\
\|U-Z\|<R\end{subarray}}\|UAU^{-1}\|=\limsup_{r\to 0}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\operatorname{Aut}(V)\\
\|U-Z\|<r\end{subarray}}\sup_{w\not=0}\frac{\|UAw\|}{\|Uw\|}<\infty.

Secondly, asking for which pairs
(A,Z)
there exists
some
sequence
U_{k}\to Z
such that the sequence in Question
1.1
does not diverge to infinity, which
may be rephrased as

Question 1.3.

Let
Z
be singular, and let
A
be arbitrary. Determine when

\limsup_{r\to 0}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\\
\|U-Z\|<r\end{subarray}}\|UAU^{-1}\|<\infty.

Taking into account that we may also wish to “modify”
U_{k}AU_{k}^{-1}
by some operation, such as deleting the diagonal as suggested in Question
1.1
, there is a modification
one can make to the above questions to facilitate that. In particular,

Question 1.4.

Let
\varphi\!:\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\to\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
be a bounded linear map. Determine for which
A,Z\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
we have

\limsup_{r\to 0}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{C}^{n}),\\
\|U-Z\|<r\end{subarray}}\|\varphi(UAU^{-1})\|<\infty.

Question 1.5.

Let
\varphi\!:\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\to\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
be a bounded linear map. Determine for which
A,Z\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
we have

\limsup_{r\to 0}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\\
\|U-Z\|<r\end{subarray}}\|\varphi(UAU^{-1})\|<\infty.

That the above questions do indeed make sense given the situation is justified in Proposition
1.3
. We would like to highlight that the primary case of interest is the
one for which the map
\varphi
is given by a Hadamard product, and in particular the Hadamard product with a matrix
J

whose entries are all one aside from the diagonal, where it is zero. In other words, while we state the question in this generality, the intended application is to the concrete situation where one
deletes the diagonal as mentioned in Question
1.1
.

Now, the above questions are still fairly tricky to answer at first glance—indeed, naively it is
a priori
very hard to check how a pair
(A,Z)
fares in any of them—so the strategy we take throughout this paper will be to study
the pairs
(A,Z)

in families
as opposed to individually, and in particular, we will parametrize these families by the
Z
’s. In an effort for maximum generality, we will now state some definitions
which will help us organize the above in far more flexible circumstances than we are actually concretely interested in.

Definition 1.6.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra, let
X
be a Banach space, and let
\varphi\!:V\to X
be a bounded linear map. Define the functions

\mathfrak{N}^{\varphi}_{\cap}\!:\operatorname{End}(V)\times\operatorname{End}(V)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{N}^{\varphi}_{\cup}\!:\operatorname{End}(V)\times\operatorname{End}(V)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}

by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{N}^{\varphi}_{\cap}(a,z)

\displaystyle:=\limsup_{r\to 0}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in V^{\times},\\
\|u-z\|<r\end{subarray}}\|\varphi(uau^{-1})\|

\displaystyle\mathfrak{N}^{\varphi}_{\cup}(a,z)

\displaystyle:=\limsup_{r\to 0}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in V^{\times},\\
\|u-z\|<r\end{subarray}}\|\varphi(uau^{-1})\|.

When
X=V
and
\varphi=\mathrm{id}_{V}
, we write
\mathfrak{N}_{\cap}:=\mathfrak{N}_{\cap}^{\mathrm{id}_{V}}
and
\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}:=\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}^{\mathrm{id}_{V}}
.

So, choosing
V=\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
, Question
1.1
asks for which
A
and
Z
we have
\mathfrak{N}_{\cap}(A,Z)<\infty
, and similarly Question
1.1
asks
for which
A
and
Z
we have
\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}(A,Z)<\infty
. More generally, the finiteness of the functions
\mathfrak{N}_{\cap}^{\varphi}
and
\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}^{\varphi}
allow us to express
Questions
1.1
and
1.1
.

Definition 1.7.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra, let
X
be a Banach space, and let
\varphi\!:V\to X
be a bounded linear map. For each
z\in V
, define the sets

\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\cap}(z)=\{a\in V\mid\mathfrak{N}^{\varphi}_{\cap}(a,z)<\infty\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(z)=\{a\in V\mid\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(a,z)<\infty\}.

As before, we write
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}(-):=\mathfrak{S}^{\mathrm{id}_{V}}(-)
and
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(-):=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\mathrm{id}_{V}}(-)
.

Terminology 1.8.
Elements of
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}(z)
and
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(z)
are said to be
sup-/inf-bounded at

z
, respectively (or just
bounded at

z
if context makes it clear which one is meant). Convsersely,
elements in the complement are said to be
sup-/inf-unbounded at

z
(or just
unbounded at

z
, subject to contextual clues).

Choosing
V=\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
, we note that by introducing these sets, one “reduces” finding pairs
(A,Z)
such that e.g.
\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}(A,Z)<\infty
to computing the family
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)
.
In this way, we can “remove” an entire dependency on
A
by blurring our eyes until they become simply the set
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)
, which can be a useful tool for finding exploitable structure.
In essence, one hopes to turn answering the questions provided earlier into determining the “algebra/calculus” of the families
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(-)
.

.

, giving two different kinds of approaches: in Section

, we detail a relation between

\varphi\preceq\psi
which descends to give comparisons like
\mathfrak{S}^{\psi}_{\cup}(-)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\cup}(-)

While some of the main results have already been alluded to above, we now present them in a bit more detail. In Section
2.3
, we define what
it means for a bounded linear operator
Z
on a Banach space to be of
generalized index zero.
These are operators
Z
which have closed image and for which there is
some invertible operator
U
such that
UZ
is self-adjoint (see Corollary
2.3
). This turns out to be a very important notion, and as suggested by the name, is a generalization
of the condition of being index zero. In particular, any index zero operator is also generalized index zero, and hence any linear map
\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
automatically satisfies this condition.
Operators with this property turn out to exhibit good behaviours. For example, an arbitrary operator need not be the limit of a sequence of invertible operators, but any generalized index zero
operator is, and we give a construction for this. The below is a combination of Theorem
2.1
, Corollary
2.1
,
and Theorem
2.3
.

Theorem 1.9.
Let
X
be a Hilbert space, and let
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
be an operator of generalized index zero. Then there is a sequence of invertible operators converging to
Z
, and

\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)=\{A\in\mathsf{B}(X)\mid A\ker{Z}\subseteq\ker{Z}\}.

In particular, if
X
is finite-dimensional and
Z
is singular and non-zero, then for any sequence of invertibles
U_{n}\to Z
and almost all
A
, we will have

\|U_{n}AU_{n}^{-1}\|\to\infty.

The proof of the above result leverages the existence of
good paths

Z+tE(t)
. These are paths which are invertible near zero and of the form

Z+tE(t)=Z+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}t^{k}E_{k}\quad\text{where}\quad(Z+tE(t))^{-1}=C_{-1}t^{-1}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}t^{k}C_{k}.

In other words, they are invertible paths to
Z
with a prescribed form for their inverse. It is an independently interesting question to study the structure such objects. The below is a rephrasing of
Theorem
2.3
and Corollary
2.4
, which also expands upon a statement in the last result.

Theorem 1.10.
Let
X
be a Hilbert space,
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
of generalized index zero. Then there exists a good path converging to
Z
. Furthermore, an operator
C\in\mathsf{B}(X)

arises as the
(-1)
th coefficient in the inverse of a good path converging to
Z
if and only if

\ker(C)=\operatorname{im}(Z),\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{im}(C)=\ker(Z).

Moreover, if we have a good path
Z+tE(t)
as above, then there exists some
n>0
such that

\forall m<n,\,\ker{Z}\subseteq\ker{E_{m}},\quad\text{and}\quad\ker{Z}\cap\ker{E_{n}}=0.

The above two results came out of our investigation of the ordinary, unmodified Question
1.1
. For the more complex Question
1.1
, we have the following
result, which is a rephrasing of Theorem
2.6
.

Theorem 1.11.
Let
X
be a Hilbert space,
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
of generalized index zero, and let
\varphi\!:\mathsf{B}(X)\to Y
be a bounded linear map with codomain a Banach space
Y
. Then
A\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(Z)

if and only if there is some
C\in\mathsf{B}(X)
such that
\operatorname{im}(C)=\ker(Z)
,
\ker(C)=\operatorname{im}(Z)
, and
\varphi(ZAC)=0
.

This theorem suggests that it would be nice if one were able to simplify the collection of
C
’s one has to consider nicely (so that one could realistically compute
\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\cup}(Z)
), but to the authors’ knowledge there is
no good way to do this. On the other hand, since the ordinary
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)
is much more computable, one may wonder under what conditions
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)
for all
Z
of generalized index zero. While
Section
2.5
gives one approach to this kind of question, the theorem above actually yields a property completely characterizing these
\varphi
’s. The below is
a combination of Theorem
2.7
and Corollary
2.7
.

Theorem 1.12.
Let
X
be a Hilbert space,
Y
a Banach space, and let
\varphi\!:\mathsf{B}(X)\to Y
be a bounded linear map. Then
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\varphi}(Z)
for
all
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
of generalized index zero if and only if for all non-zero
T\in\mathsf{B}(X)
such that
T^{2}=0
, we have
\varphi(T)\not=0
.

In particular, if
X
is finite-dimensional and
\varphi
is given by taking the Hadamard product
A\mapsto H*A
with some matrix
H
, then
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)

for all
Z
if and only if all non-diagonal entries in
H
are non-zero.

Here, we will interject and mention one of the ingredients in proving the results about
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(-)
: a slightly sharpened polar decomposition for operators of generalized index zero.
In general, polar decomposition provides for you a decomposition of an operator into a partial isometry and a self-adjoint operator. However, when the operator is assumed to be of generalized index zero,
this can be strengthened to where the partial isometry is actually invertible. Below is a slight paraphrasing of Theorem
2.3
.

Theorem 1.13: Sharpened polar decomposition.
Let
X
be a Hilbert space, and let
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
be of generalized index zero.
Then there is some
U\in\mathsf{B}(X)^{\times}
, which is also a partial isometry, such that
Z=U\sqrt{Z^{*}Z}
. Furthermore, under some conditions,
U
is unitary.

For the purposes of Section
3
, we remark that Proposition
1.3
below implies that we may always restrict our attention
solely to singular operators. With this, we come to the of the main theorems of Section
3
, one of which unfortunately only works in finite-dimensions. The statement
below is a combination of Theorem
3.2
and Theorem
3.3
.

Theorem 1.14.
Let
X
be a Hilbert space, and let
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
be singular. Then
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}(Z)=\{\lambda I\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\}
.

If
X\cong\mathbb{C}^{n}
is finite-dimensional and
\varphi\!:\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\to\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
is some bounded linear map, then either
\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\cap}(Z)=\{\lambda I\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\}
or
\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\cap}(Z)=\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
.

The theorem provides us with two possibilities for
\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}(Z)
, but we have no immediate way to tell which one will hold. However, we would still like to know what
\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}(Z)
looks like for at least

some
more interesting choices of
Z
, for which we have the following, which is a paraphrasing of Theorem
3.3
.

Theorem 1.15.
Let
\varphi\!:\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\to\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
be given by
A\mapsto J*A
, where all non-diagonal elements of
J
are non-zero, and let
Z\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
. Then
\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\cap}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}(Z)

In particular, suppose

\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\cap}(Z)=\{\lambda I\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\}
.

1.3
Some Basic Preliminaries

We collect some miscellaneous preliminary notions which will be useful throughout the paper. To start, we want to justify that the constructions
\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\cap}(-)
and
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(-)

really do what we claim. To this end, we introduce a much more fine-grained set specifically containing information about a particular path.

Definition 1.16.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra,
X
a Banach space,
\varphi\!:V\to X
a bounded linear map, let
z\in V
, and consider a sequence
u_{k}\to z
, with
u_{k}\in V^{\times}
for all
k
. Define the vector space

\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(z;u_{k}):=\left\{a\in V\biggm|\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\varphi(u_{k}au_{k}^{-1})\|<\infty\right\}.

If the path
u_{k}\to z
is determined by some function
z+e(t)
where
e(t)\to 0
as
t\to 0
, we will alternatively write
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(z;z+e(t))
or
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(z;e(t))
.
If
X=V
and
\varphi=\mathrm{id}
, we write
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}(-)
instead of
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\mathrm{id}}(-)
.

Remark 1.17.
The set
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(z;u_{k})
of Definition
1.3
captures boundedness along the given path
u_{k}\to z
, and we have

\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\cap}(z)\subseteq\bigcap_{u_{k}\to z}\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(z;u_{k})=\bigcap_{z+e(t)}\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(z;e(t)).

Proposition 1.18.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra,
X
a Banach space,
\varphi\!:V\to X
and let
z\in V
. Then

\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\cap}(z)=\bigcap_{u_{k}\to z}\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(z;u_{k})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(z)=\bigcup_{u_{k}\to z}\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(z;u_{k}).

Proof.
For the left assertion, one inclusion is clear, as remarked above. For the other inclusion, we proceed by proving the contrapositive: suppose

\mathfrak{N}^{\varphi}_{\cap}(a,z)=\infty
. Then we know that

\forall r>0,\quad\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|u-z\|<r\\
u\in V^{\times}\end{subarray}}\|\varphi(uau^{-1})\|=\infty.

In particular, we may define a sequence of invertibles
u_{k}\to z
by choosing
u_{k}\in V^{\times}
such that
\|u_{k}-z\|<1/k
and
\|u_{k}au_{k}^{-1}\|>k
. Then, by construction

\lim_{k\to\infty}\|u_{k}au_{k}^{-1}\|=\infty

so that
a\not\in\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{pw}}(z;u_{k})
.

To prove the right assertion, note that one easily sees that

\bigcup_{u_{k}\to z}\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(z;u_{k})\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(z).

For the other inclusion, let
A\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(z)
, and let
\lambda=\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(a,z)<\infty
. This tells us, in particular, that

\forall r>0,\quad\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|u-z\|<r\\
u\in V^{\times}\end{subarray}}\|uau^{-1}\|\leq\lambda,

so we may pick
u_{k}\in V^{\times}
such that
\|u_{k}-z\|<1/k
and
\|u_{k}au_{k}^{-1}\|\to\lambda
,
so that
a\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(z;u_{k})
.

\blacksquare

This yields a number of corollaries. An immediate one is that we may restrict our attention entirely to singular operators, as demonstrated below.

Proposition 1.19.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra. If
u\in V^{\times}
is invertible, then

\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\cap}(u)=V.

Proof.
If
u
is invertible and
u_{k}\to u
is a sequence of invertibles, then
u_{k}^{-1}\to u^{-1}
and for any
a\in V
we have

u_{k}au_{k}^{-1}\to uau^{-1}.

In particular,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\|u_{k}au_{k}^{-1}\|=\|uau^{-1}\|<\infty

so by Proposition
1.3
we are done.

\blacksquare

Here is a basic observation about the structure of the family
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(-)
, and in particular
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}(-)
, which together imply some structure
on
\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\cap}(-)
following from Proposition
1.3
.

Theorem 1.20.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra, let
z\in V
, and let
u_{k}\to z
be a sequence of invertibles.

(a)

For a Banach space
X
and bounded linear map
\varphi\!:V\to X
, the set
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(z)
forms a vector space over
\mathbb{C}
.

(b)

For a Banach algebra
W
and continuous algebra map
\varphi\!:V\to W
, the vector space
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(z)
forms an algebra over
\mathbb{C}
.

In particular,
\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\cap}(z)
is always vector space, and choosing
V=W
and
\varphi=\mathrm{id}
, we see that
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}(z)
is an algebra.

Proof.
Part (a) follows immediately from the linearity of
\varphi
, and the subadditivity and homogeneity of the norm
\|\cdot\|
. For part (b), since
\varphi
is
an algebra map, we have

\|\varphi(u_{k}abu_{k}^{-1})\|=\|\varphi(u_{k}au_{k}^{-1}\cdot u_{k}bu_{k}^{-1})\|\leq\|\varphi(u_{k}au_{k}^{-1})\|\cdot\|\varphi(u_{k}bu_{k}^{-1})\|

from which the result follows.

The final statement follows immediately by applying Proposition
1.3
since the intersection of vector spaces (resp. algebras) is a vector space (resp. an algebra).

\blacksquare

Another interesting property of the “pathwise spaces” is that one can relate boundedness along one path to boundedness along another. This fact will
implicitly be used in Section
3
.

Theorem 1.21.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra, let
\varphi\!:V\to X
be a bounded linear map, let
z\in V
, and let
u_{k}\to z
be a sequence of invertibles. Then, for all
p\in V^{\times}
, we have

\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(zp;u_{k}p)=p^{-1}\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(z;u_{k})p\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}(pz;pu_{k})=\mathfrak{S}(z;u_{k}).

Proof.
Simply observe that we may write

\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\varphi(u_{k}au_{k}^{-1})\|=\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\varphi\!\left((u_{k}p)(p^{-1}ap)(u_{k}p)^{-1}\right)\!\|.

Therefore,
a\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(z;u_{k})
if and only if
p^{-1}ap\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(zp;u_{k}p)
, and
b\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(zp;u_{k}p)
if and only if
pbp^{-1}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(z;u_{k})
,
so that
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(zp;u_{k}p)=p^{-1}\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(z;u_{k})p
. This proves the first statement.

For the second statement, note that for all
x\in V
, we have

\frac{1}{\|p\|\cdot\|p^{-1}\|}\cdot\|pxp^{-1}\|\leq\|x\|\leq\|p^{-1}\|\cdot\|p\|\cdot\|pxp^{-1}\|.

Therefore,

\frac{1}{\|p\|\cdot\|p^{-1}\|}\cdot\|(pu_{k})a(pu_{k})^{-1}\|\leq\|u_{k}au_{k}^{-1}\|\leq\|p^{-1}\|\cdot\|p\|\cdot\|(pu_{k})a(pu_{k})^{-1}\|

which yields the result.

\blacksquare

Corollary 1.22.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra, let
\varphi\!:V\to X
be a bounded linear map, and let
z\in V
. Then, for all
p\in V^{\times}
, we have

\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\cap}(zp)=p^{-1}\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}(z)p\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\cup}(zp)=p^{-1}\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\cup}(z)p.

Proof.
Observe that there is a bijection between the sets

\{\text{sequences }u_{k}\to z\}\quad\text{and}\quad\{\text{sequences }v_{k}\to zp\}

given by multiplication on the right by
p
or
p^{-1}
. In particular, applying Proposition
1.3
and Theorem
1.3
, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}(zp)=\bigcap_{v_{k}\to zp}\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(zp;v_{k})

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{u_{k}\to z}\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(zp;u_{k}p)

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{u_{k}\to z}p^{-1}\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(z;u_{k})p=p^{-1}\left(\bigcap_{u_{k}\to z}\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(z;u_{k})\right)p=p^{-1}\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}(z)p.

The proof for
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(-)
is identical.

\blacksquare

Clearly, if
\varphi
is some arbitrary bounded linear map, the problem of computing the families
\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\cap}(-)
and
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(-)
is harder than computing

\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}(-)
and
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(-)
, so these are what we begin with. A starting point is a sufficient condition for being unbounded:

Proposition 1.23.

Let
X
be a Banach space, and let
A,Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
. If the two equivalent conditions

(i)

\ker(Z)\not\subseteq\ker(ZA)
,

(ii)

A\ker(Z)\not\subseteq\ker(Z)
,

are satisfied, then
\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}(A,Z)=\mathfrak{N}_{\cap}(A,Z)=\infty
. In particular,
A\not\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)
and
A\not\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}(Z)
.

Proof.
That the two conditions are equivalent is simply the following observation:

\displaystyle\ker(Z)\not\subseteq\ker(ZA)

\displaystyle\iff\exists x\in\ker(Z)\text{ s.t. }ZAx\not=0

\displaystyle\iff\exists x\in\ker(Z)\text{ s.t. }Ax\not\in\ker(Z)

\displaystyle\iff A(\ker(Z))\not\subseteq\ker(Z)

Now, suppose we have some
x\not=0
in
X
such that
Zx=0
but
ZAx\not=0
. Then

\displaystyle\limsup_{r\to 0}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\mathsf{B}(X)^{\times},\\
\|U-Z\|<r\end{subarray}}\left(\sup_{w\not=0}\frac{\|UAw\|}{\|Uw\|}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{r\to 0}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\mathsf{B}(X)^{\times},\\
\|U-Z\|<r\end{subarray}}\frac{\|UAx\|}{\|Ux\|}

\displaystyle=\limsup_{r\to 0}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\mathsf{B}(X)^{\times},\\
\|U-Z\|<r\end{subarray}}\frac{\|UAx\|}{\|(U-Z)x\|}

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{r\to 0}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\mathsf{B}(X)^{\times},\\
\|U-Z\|<r\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{r}\frac{\|UAx\|}{\|x\|}=\infty

where the equality is because
Ux=Ux-0=Ux-Zx
, and where we note that
\|UAx\|\not\to 0
as
r\to 0
since
ZAx\not=0
.
We conclude that
\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}(A,Z)=\infty
. Finally, it is clear that by the definitions of
\inf
and
\sup
that
\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}(A,Z)\leq\mathfrak{N}_{\cap}(A,Z)
, so that the latter is also infinite.

\blacksquare

From the above, we introduce the following gadget to allow us to fit the criterion provided into the general formalism we are working with.

Definition 1.24.

Let
X
be a Banach space. For any
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
, define the set

\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z):=\{A\in\mathsf{B}(X)\mid A\ker(Z)\subseteq\ker(Z)\},

i.e. the set of operators which keep the kernel of
Z
invariant.

Theorem 1.25.

Let
X
be a Banach space, and let
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
. Then

(a)

\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
forms an algebra, and

(b)

we have the inclusions

\{\lambda I\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}(Z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z).

Proof.
(a) Let
A,B\in\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
. Then, clearly,

AB\ker(Z)\subseteq A\ker(Z)\subseteq\ker(Z)

so that
AB\in\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
. Similarly, if
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}
and
x\in\ker(Z)
, we have
Z(\lambda A+\mu B)x=\lambda ZAx+\mu ZBx=0
,
so
(\lambda A+\mu B)x\in\ker(Z)
, and hence
\lambda A+\mu B\in\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
.

(b) The first inclusion is trivial since
\lambda I
commutes with everything. That
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}(Z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)
is immediate since
\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}(A,Z)\leq\mathfrak{N}_{\cap}(A,Z)
, and that
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)

is a consequence of Proposition
1.3
.

\blacksquare

2
Existence of Bounded Paths

The goal of this section is to treat the computation or characterization of
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(-)
and
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(-)
in as great a generality as we can muster. Since the latter
is harder, more finicky, and generally less elegant, we will start with the former.

2.1
Existence Without a Modifier

Our general approach is to make use of the algebra
\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
described in Definition
1.3
.
In principle, since Theorem
1.3
tells us that
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
, we could simply compute e.g. the dimension of
\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
and be done (at least in
the finite-dimensional setting). However, we will instead prove the stronger result that
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)
and
\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
are
equal,
in essentially full generality, and only then compute
\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
. To do this,
we will pass through yet another intermediary. The idea here is as follows: the algebra
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)
concerns boundedness properties of arbitrary paths converging to
Z
; we will consider the more modest
setting of a path converging to
Z
which has a prescribed form for its inverse, allowing us to deduce boundedness along that particular path very easily.

Terminology 2.1.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra, and let
z\in V
.

(1)

An
admissible path
to
z
is a path
\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to V
, invertible near zero, of the form

z+t\cdot e(t),

where
e(t)
is analytic for sufficiently small
t
, i.e. is of the form

e(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e_{k}t^{k}.

(2)

We say an admissible path
z+te(t)
is
linear
if
e(t)
is a constant function.

(3)

An admissible path
z+te(t)
is
good
if the inverse is of the form

c_{-1}t^{-1}+c(t)

where
c(t)
is analytic.

Notation 2.2.
If
C(t)
is some analytic function, we will write
C_{k}
for the
k
th coefficient in the series expansion of
C(t)
.

Of course, one can write down any definition one wants, and it could all be for nothing if the set of things satisfying the definition is empty. However, we defer the issue of
good paths for Section
2.3
, focusing instead on how they ought to be used. We make a definition, the succeeding Proposition
2.1
being the motivation:

Definition 2.3.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra, let
z\in V
, and let
c\in V
be such that
zc=cz=0
. Define the algebra

\mathfrak{S}_{c}(z):=\{a\in V\mid zac=0\}.

Lemma 2.4.

Let
X
be a Banach space, and let
Z,C\in\mathsf{B}(X)
be such that
ZC=CZ=0
. Then

\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{C}(Z).

Proof.
Saying that
ZC=0
is the same as saying that
\operatorname{im}{C}\subseteq\ker{Z}
. Now, if
A\in\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
, then
A
keeps the kernel
of
Z
invariant, and hence

A\operatorname{im}(C)\subseteq A\ker(Z)\subseteq\ker(Z)\implies ZAC=0

as desired.

\blacksquare

Proposition 2.5.

Let
V
be Banach algebra, let
z\in V
, and let
z+te(t)\in\mathcal{E}(z)
be a good path to
z
with inverse
c_{-1}t^{-1}+c(t)
. Then
c_{-1}z=zc_{-1}=0
.

Proof.
We have that

(z+te(t))(c_{-1}t^{-1}+c(t))=I=(c_{-1}t^{-1}+c(t))(z+te(t)).

Expanding
e(t)
and
c(t)
in their series forms, then multiplying this out and comparing coefficients, yields the result.

\blacksquare

These good paths have predictable boundedness properties, characterized by the simple algebraic criterion of
\mathfrak{S}_{C_{-1}}(Z)
,
and furthermore, Lemma
2.1
relates this to
\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
. Using this, we obtain the following lemma, and one of the main
theorems of this section.

Lemma 2.6.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra, let
z\in V
, and let
z+te(t)
be a good path with inverse
c_{-1}t^{-1}+c(t)
. Then
\mathfrak{S}_{c_{-1}}(z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}(z;z+te(t))
. In particular,

\mathfrak{S}_{c_{-1}}(z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(z)
.

Proof.
Suppose
a\in\mathfrak{S}_{c_{-1}}(z)
. Note that

\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}(a,z)=\limsup_{r\to 0}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in V^{\times}\\
\|u-z\|<r\end{subarray}}\|uau^{-1}\|\leq\lim_{t\to 0}\|(z+te(t))a(z+te(t))^{-1}\|.

Writing out the latter using the fact that we have an explicit inverse and that
zac_{-1}=0
, we get

\displaystyle(z+te(t))a(c_{-1}t^{-1}+c(t))

\displaystyle=zac_{-1}t^{-1}+zac(t)+e(t)ac_{-1}+e(t)ac(t)\cdot t

\displaystyle=zac(t)+e(t)ac_{-1}+e(t)ac(t)\cdot t=O(1)

which is bounded in norm as
t\to 0
. Finally, see Proposition
1.3
.

\blacksquare

Theorem 2.7.

Let
X
be a Banach space, let
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
, and suppose that there is some good path
Z+tE(t)
going to
Z
. Then

\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{C_{-1}}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}(Z;Z+tE(t))=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z),

where
(Z+tE(t))^{-1}=C_{-1}t^{-1}+C(t)
. In particular,
\mathfrak{S}_{C_{-1}}(Z)
is independent of
E(t)
.

Proof.
By Lemma
2.1
and Lemma
2.1
together with
part (b) of Theorem
1.3
, we have

\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{C_{-1}}(Z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}(Z;Z+tE(t))\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)

which yields the result.

\blacksquare

Corollary 2.8.

Let
Z\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
. Then
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
.

Proof.
By Theorem
2.1
, if a good path exists for
Z
, then the result holds. By our later Theorem
2.3

(see also Theorem
2.2
and Corollary
2.3
), good paths exist for all
Z
.

\blacksquare

Remark 2.9.
Before moving on, we would like to highlight the following remarkable observation: Corollary
2.1
implies
that for all
Z\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
,
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)
, which was
a priori only a set,
is actually an
algebra.
We do not know any other way to show this,
aside from as a corollary as just explained.

So the good paths have allowed us to show that
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
(momentarily assuming that they exist; when we establish that they do, we will also be given a more
general veresion of Corollary
2.1
). However, for this to be a useful computation in practice,
we need to know what
\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
is, or at least the dimension. To solve this issue, we will reduce the problem to computing
\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)

for just a few choices of
Z
where one can do a calculation by hand.

Theorem 2.10.

Let
X
be a Banach space, let
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
, and let
M\in\mathsf{B}(X)^{\times}
. Then

(a)

M^{-1}\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)M=\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(ZM)
,

(b)

\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(MZ)=\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
, and

(c)

for any other
M^{\prime}\in\mathsf{B}(X)^{\times}
, there is an isomorphism
\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)\cong\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(MZM^{\prime})
.

In particular, if
X=\mathbb{C}^{n}
, the dimension of
\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
depends only on the rank of
Z
.

Proof.
Observe that
\ker(MZ)=\ker(Z)
, so (b) is trivial. To see that (a) holds, note first that
x\in\ker(ZM)
if and only if
Mx\in\ker(Z)
, so that

\ker(ZM)=M^{-1}\ker(Z)
. Now, if
A\in\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
, then
M^{-1}AM
satisfies

M^{-1}AM\ker(ZM)=M^{-1}A\ker(Z)\subseteq M^{-1}\ker(Z)=\ker(ZM).

Therefore,
M^{-1}\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)M\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(ZM)
. Conversely, if
B\in\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(ZM)
, then

MBM^{-1}\ker(Z)=MB\ker(ZM)\subseteq M\ker(ZM)=\ker(Z)

so that
M\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(ZM)M^{-1}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
, that is
\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(ZM)\subseteq M^{-1}\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)M
.

Part (c) is a formal consequence of (a) and (b). In particular, we have

\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(MZ)\cong\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(MZM^{\prime}),

where the equality is by (b) and the isomorphism is by (a).

\blacksquare

Remark 2.11.
A priori, computing
\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
is very hard: it requires identifying the invariant subspaces of a matrix. However, since
\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
only cares
about the rank of
Z
, we may now replace
Z
entirely by the diagonal matrix with
m=\dim\operatorname{im}{Z}
one’s on the diagonal and everything else zero. Due to
the simplicity of this matrix, it is now much more tractable to compute
\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
, at least up to an isomorphism given by conjugation.

Notation 2.12.
Let
m\leq n
. We write
D_{n,m}
for the
n\times n
diagonal matrix with
m
ones on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere. That is,

D_{n,m}:=\begin{pmatrix}I_{m}&0_{m\times(n-m)}\\
0_{(n-m)\times m}&0_{(n-m)\times(n-m)}\end{pmatrix}=I_{m}\oplus 0_{(n-m)\times(n-m)}.

Proposition 2.13.

Consider
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, and let
n=m+k
. We have

\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(D_{n,m})=\bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}X&0_{m\times k}\\
Y&W\end{pmatrix}\biggm|X\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m},\,Y\in\mathbb{C}^{k\times m},\text{ and }W\in\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}\bigg\}.

In particular, for any singular
Z
with
\dim\operatorname{im}{Z}=m
, we have
\dim\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)=n^{2}-mn+m^{2}
.

Proof.
It is easily seen that
\ker(D_{n,m})
is given by all vectors of the form
v=0_{m}\oplus v^{\prime}
, where
v^{\prime}
is of dimension
k
. Thus, writing

A=\begin{pmatrix}X&W^{\prime}\\
Y&W\end{pmatrix},\quad W^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times k},

to have
Av\in\ker(Z)
, we must have that

Av=\begin{pmatrix}X&W^{\prime}\\
Y&W\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0_{m}\\
v^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}W^{\prime}v^{\prime}\\
Wv^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\in\ker(Z),

which then means we must have
W^{\prime}v^{\prime}=0
. However,
v^{\prime}
was totally arbitrary, so this implies that
W^{\prime}=0
.

The final assertion is just the observation that

\displaystyle\dim\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(D_{n,m})

\displaystyle=m^{2}+mk+k^{2}

\displaystyle=m^{2}+m(n-m)+(n-m)^{2}=n^{2}-mn+m^{2}

and that
\dim\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
only depends on the rank of
Z
.

\blacksquare

Corollary 2.14.

Let
Z\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
, and let
m=\dim\operatorname{im}{Z}
. Then
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)
is a linear subspace of
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
of dimension

n^{2}-mn+m^{2}
. In particular, if
Z
is singular, then for almost all
A\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
we have
\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}(A,Z)=\infty
.

Proof.
Apply Corollary
2.1
and Proposition
2.1
to see that

\dim\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)=\dim\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)=n^{2}-mn+m^{2}

as desired.

\blacksquare

2.2
Duality in Coefficients

We begin this subsection with the following remarkable observation:

Proposition 2.15.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra, let
z\in V
, and let
z+te(t)
be a good path to
z
with inverse
c_{-1}t^{-1}+c(t)
. Then
c_{-1}+tc(t)
is a good path with
inverse
zt^{-1}+e(t)
.

Proof.
Note that, for small enough
t
, we have

1=(z+te(t))\cdot(c_{-1}t^{-1}+c(t))=(zt^{-1}+e(t))t\cdot(c_{-1}t^{-1}+c(t))=(zt^{-1}+e(t))\cdot(c_{-1}+tc(t))

which yields the result.

\blacksquare

That is, one sees that there is some kind of duality between good paths going to some
z
and good paths going to the
(-1)
th coefficient of a good path going to
z
.
That this is the case suggests that there is some kind of deeper relationship at play, and the purpose of this section is to uncover at least some aspects of this.

Definition 2.16.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra, and let
z\in V
. Define the set
\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(z)
to be the set of all
c_{-1}\in V
for which there exists a good path
z+te(t)

going to
z
such that
(z+te(t))^{-1}=c_{-1}t^{-1}+c(t)
.

Theorem 2.17.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra.

(a)

Let
c,z\in V
. Then
c\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(z)
if and only if
z\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(c)
.

(b)

Let
z\in V
. Then
\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(z)
is non-empty if and only if there exists a good path converging to
z
.

(c)

Let
z\in V
. For any
c\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(z)
, we have
cz=zc=0
.

(d)

Let
z\in V
, and let
p\in V^{\times}
. Then
\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(zp)=p^{-1}\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(z)
and
\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(pz)=\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(z)p^{-1}
.

Proof.
(a) is a reformulation of Proposition
2.2
, and (c) is a reformulation of Proposition
2.1
.
Part (b) is just by definition. To obtain (d), note that, given a good path
z+te(t)
, multiplication
by
p
on the left and right yields good paths
zp+te(t)p
and
pz+tpe(t)
, and these operations are bijections. Computing the inverse of these paths
yields the desired equalities.

\blacksquare

Remark 2.18.

The duality in Proposition
2.2
can now be phrased in the following manner: let
\overset{*}{\sim}
denote the
relation given by
c\overset{*}{\sim}z
iff
c\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(z)
. We now observe that (a) in the above theorem says that
\overset{*}{\sim}
is a
symmetric relation.

Now, the question to ask about is the following: where exactly does this duality come from? From the proof of Corollary
2.1
,
we know that good paths are intimately related to answer questions like the ones we consider in this document. Here we have a hint:
in, say, Question
1.1
, there is a certain
parity
to the quantity of interest. That is, we considered
things of the form
\|UAU^{-1}\|
, rather than
\|U^{-1}AU\|
. A priori, this may seem like a trivial difference, but the two versions are actually
different; in fact, them being different yet obviously similar suggests that there should be a
duality
between them. Since we now have
two sources of duality, it is sensible to conjecture that they are the same. To prove this, we will first provide a classification, as we did in the
last section, of the
\|U^{-1}AU\|
version of the problem.

Definition 2.19.
Let
V
be a Banach algebra. Define the function

\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}^{*}\!:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}

by

\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}^{*}(a,z):=\limsup_{r\to 0}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in V^{\times}\\
\|u-z\|<r\end{subarray}}\|u^{-1}au\|

and the algebra

\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{*}(z):=\{a\in V\mid\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}^{*}(a,z)<\infty\}.

Thus, we are now asking about when
\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}^{*}(a,z)<\infty
, rather than
\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}(a,z)
. In order to implement the proof
strategy of the last section, we need dual versions of the sets
\mathfrak{S}_{C}(Z)
and
\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
, and we need a dual
version of the kernel criterion, Proposition
1.3
.

Definition 2.20.
Let
V
be a Banach algebra, and let
c,z\in V
. Define the algebra

\mathfrak{S}_{c}^{*}(z):=\{a\in V\mid caz=0\}.

If
X
is a Banach space and
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
, define the algebra

\mathfrak{S}_{\operatorname{im}}(Z):=\{A\in\mathsf{B}(X)\mid A\operatorname{im}{Z}\subseteq\operatorname{im}{Z}\}.

The above give the desired dual versions of
\mathfrak{S}_{C}(Z)
and
\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
. In order to confirm that this is the case, we prove
a dual version of the kernel criterion, which in this instance is an
image
criterion instead.

Lemma 2.21.

Let
X
be a Banach space, let
A,Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
. Assume that
A\operatorname{im}(Z)\not\subseteq\operatorname{im}(Z)
.
Then
\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}^{*}(A,Z)=\infty
. In particular, we have the inclusion
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{*}(Z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\operatorname{im}}(Z)
.

Proof.
Suppose
x\in\operatorname{im}(Z)
, so
x=Zy
, but
Ax\not\in\operatorname{im}(Z)
. Then

\displaystyle\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}^{*}(A,Z)=\liminf_{r\to 0}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\mathsf{B}(X)^{\times}\\
\|U-Z\|<r\end{subarray}}\sup_{w\not=0}\frac{\|U^{-1}Aw\|}{\|U^{-1}w\|}

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{r\to 0}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\mathsf{B}(X)^{\times}\\
\|U-Z\|<r\end{subarray}}\frac{\|U^{-1}Ax\|}{\|U^{-1}x\|}

\displaystyle=\liminf_{r\to 0}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\mathsf{B}(X)^{\times}\\
\|U-Z\|<r\end{subarray}}\frac{\|U^{-1}Ax\|}{\|U^{-1}Zy\|}

\displaystyle=\liminf_{r\to 0}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\mathsf{B}(X)^{\times}\\
\|U-Z\|<r\end{subarray}}\frac{\|U^{-1}Ax\|}{\|U^{-1}Uy\|}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|y\|}\liminf_{r\to 0}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\mathsf{B}(X)^{\times}\\
\|U-Z\|<r\end{subarray}}\|U^{-1}Ax\|=\infty

as desired.

\blacksquare

With these tools available to us, we now just step through exactly the same proof as in the last section, except dualized.

Proposition 2.22.
Let
X
be a Banach space, and let
Z,C\in\mathsf{B}(X)
be such that
ZC=CZ=0
. Then
\mathfrak{S}_{\operatorname{im}}(Z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{C}^{*}(Z)
.

Proof.
Saying that
CZ=0
is the same as saying that
\operatorname{im}(Z)\subseteq\ker(C)
. Now, if
A\in\mathfrak{S}_{\operatorname{im}}(Z)
, then
A
keeps the image of
Z

invariant, and hence

A\operatorname{im}(Z)\subseteq\operatorname{im}(Z)\subseteq\ker(C)\implies CAZ=0

as desired.

\blacksquare

Lemma 2.23.
Let
V
be a Banach algebra, let
z\in V
, and let
z+te(t)
be a good path with inverse
c_{-1}t^{-1}+c(t)
. Then we have the inclusion
\mathfrak{S}_{c_{-1}}^{*}(z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{*}(z)
.

Proof.
Suppose
a\in\mathfrak{S}_{c_{-1}}^{*}(z)
. Noting that

\mathfrak{N}^{*}_{\cup}(a,z)=\limsup_{r\to 0}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in V^{\times}\\
\|u-z\|<r\end{subarray}}\|u^{-1}au\|\leq\lim_{t\to 0}\|(z+te(t))^{-1}a(z+te(t))\|

we compute

\displaystyle(c_{-1}t^{-1}+c_{0}+O(t))a(z+te_{0}+O(t^{2}))

\displaystyle=c_{-1}azt^{-1}+c_{-1}ae_{0}+c_{0}az+O(t)

\displaystyle=c_{-1}ae_{0}+c_{0}az+O(t)

where we used that
c_{-1}az=0
by assumption. This is bounded in norm as
t\to 0
, so we are done.

\blacksquare

Theorem 2.24.

Let
X
be a Banach space, let
Z\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
be such that
\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)
is non-empty, and let
C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)
. Then

\mathfrak{S}_{\operatorname{im}}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}^{*}_{C}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{*}(Z).

Proof.
Combining the above results, we have that

\mathfrak{S}_{\operatorname{im}}(Z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}^{*}_{C}(Z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{*}(Z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\operatorname{im}}(Z)

which yields the first part.

\blacksquare

Once again, for this result to be effective practically at least in the finite-dimensional case, we need to be able to calculate (the dimension of)
\mathfrak{S}_{\operatorname{im}}(Z)
. Luckily for us,
we do not need to work nearly as hard to do this, because we already did it for
\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
.

Proposition 2.25.

Let
Z\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
, and let
k=\dim\ker{Z}
. Then

\dim{\mathfrak{S}_{\operatorname{im}}(Z)}=n^{2}-kn+k^{2}.

Proof.
Let
P_{Z}
be the orthogonal projection onto
\ker(Z)
. Note that
\ker(P_{Z})=\operatorname{im}(Z)
, and therefore

\mathfrak{S}_{\operatorname{im}}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(P_{Z}).

Thus, we know that
\mathfrak{S}_{\operatorname{im}}(Z)
is an algebra, and that

\dim{\mathfrak{S}_{\operatorname{im}}(Z)}=\dim{\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(P_{Z})}=n^{2}-mn+m^{2},

where
m=\dim\operatorname{im}(P_{Z})
, by Proposition
2.1
. By our choice of
P_{Z}
, we have that
\operatorname{im}(P_{Z})=\ker(Z)
, so
m=k
.

\blacksquare

With the classification done, we move to the first genuine “duality-type” theorem.

Lemma 2.26.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra, and let
c,z\in V
. Then
\mathfrak{S}_{c}(z)=\mathfrak{S}_{z}^{*}(c)
.

Proof.
This is a consequence of noticing the tautology that

\mathfrak{S}_{c}(z)=\{a\in V\mid zac=0\}=\mathfrak{S}_{z}^{*}(c)

as desired.

\blacksquare

Let
X
be a Banach space, and let

, and part (a) of Theorem

, note that

\blacksquare

With the above theorem in mind, we might ask the following question: for which pairs
(Z,Z^{\prime})
are the sets
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)
and
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{*}(Z^{\prime})
exactly the same?
In other words, supposing one asks to find all

\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}(A,Z)<\infty
, which

can be used to transform this to equivalently asking

\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}^{*}(A,Z^{\prime})
? Due to Theorem

Lemma 2.28.

Let
X
be a Hilbert space, and let

be subspaces of

. Suppose that

\{A\in\mathsf{B}(X)\mid AU\subseteq U\}\subseteq\{A\in\mathsf{B}(X)\mid AU^{\prime}\subseteq U^{\prime}\}.

. In particular, if we have equality above, then
U=U^{\prime}
.

Proof.
Let

, let

, let

be the projection onto
\operatorname{Span}(x)
. Define the map

as the composition

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phantom{.}X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Span}(x)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Span}(y).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P_{x}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x\,\mapsto\,y}

. However, since

\blacksquare

Theorem 2.29.

Let
X
be a Banach space, and let

Suppose

2.3
Good Paths in Hilbert Spaces & Operators of Generalized Index Zero

In order to apply Theorem
2.1
, we must establish the existence of good paths. This is a priori very difficult, as there is seemingly nothing
to grasp onto in order to do such a thing. Furthermore, unlike in the finite-dimensional case, approximating an operator by invertible ones—which is implicit in the existence of
good paths—is no longer generally possible (for example, the left shift on
\ell^{2}
cannot be the limit of invertible operators), so one cannot expect good paths to exist for all operators.

Fortuituously, we know that in the setting of a Hilbert space, every element of
\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)
satisfies an algebraic property, provided by Corollary
2.2
:

Definition 2.32.

Let
X
be a Banach space, and let
Z\in\sf(X)
. Define the set

\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(Z):=\{C\in\mathsf{B}(X)\mid\operatorname{im}(C)=\ker(Z)\text{ and }\operatorname{im}(Z)=\ker(C)\}.

Corollary
2.2
tells us that
\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)\subseteq\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(Z)
. Furthermore, the
definition of
\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(Z)
provides us with a natural obstruction to consider: if we want
\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(Z)
to be non-empty, i.e. for there to exist an operator
C
such that

\operatorname{im}(C)=\ker(Z)
and
\ker(C)=\operatorname{im}(Z)
, then we must in particular also have an isomorphism

\operatorname{coker}(Z)=X/\operatorname{im}(Z)=X/\ker(C)\xrightarrow{\sim}\operatorname{im}(C)=\ker(Z).

Heuristically, this is saying that
Z
is “index zero” although without the finiteness assumptions. With this in mind, we may hope that satisfying this kind of condition
removes any pathologies that may appear.

Definition 2.33.
Let
X
be a Banach space, and let
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
. We say
Z
is of
generalized index zero
if there exists a continuous isomorphism
\ker(Z)\xrightarrow{\sim}\operatorname{coker}(Z)
.

Remark 2.34.
Let
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
be of index zero. Then
Z
is also of generalized index zero: indeed, since both
\ker(Z)
and
\operatorname{coker}(Z)
are finite-dimensional vector spaces of equal dimension,
one may find a (necessarily continuous) isomorphism
\ker(Z)\xrightarrow{\sim}\operatorname{coker}(Z)
. In particular, if
X
is finite-dimensional, then every
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
is of generalized index zero.

Remark 2.35.

If
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
is of generalized index zero, then
Z
has closed image. Indeed, the quotient
\operatorname{coker}(Z)=X/\operatorname{im}(Z)
is a Banach space if and only if
Z
has closed image,
and since
\ker(Z)
has a continuous isomorphism to
\operatorname{coker}(Z)
, the latter must be a Banach space by a generalization of the open mapping theorem. In particular, it follows
that the inverse map
\operatorname{coker}(Z)\xrightarrow{\sim}\ker(Z)
is also continuous. Similarly, it follows that
Z
is of generalized index zero if and only if it has closed image and there is
a continuous isomorphism
\operatorname{coker}(Z)\to\ker(Z)
.

Theorem 2.36.

Let
X
be a Banach space, and let
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
. Then there is a bijection

\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(Z)\cong\left\{\,\text{continuous isomorphisms }\ker(Z)\xrightarrow{\sim}\operatorname{coker}(Z)\,\right\}

which associates to
C\in\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(Z)
the isomorphism
\theta_{C}
given by

\ker(Z)=\operatorname{im}(C)\xrightarrow{\sim}X/\ker(C)=X/\operatorname{im}(Z)=\operatorname{coker}(Z).

In particular,
Z
is of generalized index zero if and only if
\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(Z)
is non-empty.

Proof.
We have already provided a map

\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(Z)\to\left\{\,\text{continuous isomorphisms }\ker(Z)\xrightarrow{\sim}\operatorname{coker}(Z)\,\right\}

and so it remains to construct an inverse. Let
\theta\!:\ker(Z)\to\operatorname{coker}(Z)
be a continuous isomorphism, and define
C_{\theta}\!:X\to X
by the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{coker}(Z)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ker(Z)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{\theta}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\theta^{-1}}

Trivially, since
C_{\theta}
is a composition of continuous maps, it follows that
C_{\theta}
is continuous. Furthermore,
\ker(C_{\theta})=\ker(X\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{coker}(Z))=\operatorname{im}(Z)
, and
\operatorname{im}(C_{\theta})=\ker(Z)
, so
C_{\theta}\in\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(Z)
.

We need to check that for all
C\in\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(Z)
, we have
C_{\theta_{C}}=C
, and that for all continuous isomorphisms
\theta\!:\operatorname{coker}(Z)\xrightarrow{\sim}\ker(Z)
we have
\theta_{C_{\theta}}=\theta
.

\star

If
C\in\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(Z)
, by the first isomorphism theorem and that
\operatorname{im}(Z)=\ker(C)
, the map
C_{\theta_{C}}
is given by
x\mapsto\theta_{C}^{-1}(x+\ker(C))=Cx
.

\star

If
\theta\!:\ker(Z)\to\operatorname{coker}(Z)
is a continuous isomorphism, then
\theta_{C_{\theta}}
is given by sending
z\in\ker(Z)
to

C_{\theta}^{-1}\{x\}=\theta(x).

This concludes the proof.

\blacksquare

In the beginning of this section, as well as in Section
2.2
, we implicitly promised a complete algebraic description of the elements of
\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(-)
,
in particulary given by
\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(-)
. Because of Theorem
2.3
, proving this would also yield the other main goal of this section, namely
the existence of good paths, thereby fulfilling all our previous promises. Our approach is a fairly standard one: begin with self-adjoint operators, then lift using a slightly sharpened version of Polar decomposition.

Lemma 2.37.

Let
X
be a Hilbert space, and let
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
be a self-adjoint operator. Then
Z
is of generalized index zero if and only if
Z
has closed image, in which case

\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)=\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(Z)\not=\varnothing.

Proof.
If
Z
is of generalized index zero, then
Z
has closed image, as argued in Remark
2.3
. Conversely, if
Z
has closed image, we can write

X=\operatorname{im}(Z)\oplus\operatorname{im}(Z)^{\perp}=\operatorname{im}(Z)\oplus\ker(Z^{*})=\operatorname{im}(Z)\oplus\ker(Z)

since
Z
is self-adjoint. In particular,

X/\operatorname{im}(Z)=(\operatorname{im}(Z)\oplus\ker(Z))/\operatorname{im}(Z)\cong\ker(Z)

so
Z
is of generalized index zero.

We now prove the second half of the result. That
\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(Z)
is non-empty follows immediately by Theorem
2.3
. Furthermore, Corollary
2.2
shows
that
\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)\subseteq\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(Z)
, so it remains only to show the reverse inclusion. For this, let
C\in\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(Z)
; one sees that, in the decomposition given above, the operators
Z,C
have the form

Z=\begin{pmatrix}Z_{11}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad C=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&C_{22}\end{pmatrix}

where
Z_{11}\!:\operatorname{im}(Z)\xrightarrow{\sim}\operatorname{im}(Z)
and
C_{22}\!:\ker(Z)\xrightarrow{\sim}\ker(Z)
are continuous isomorphisms. Let
E\!:X\to X
and
M\!:X\to X
be given by

E=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&C_{22}^{-1}\end{pmatrix},\quad M=\begin{pmatrix}Z_{11}^{-1}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Then, for
t>0
,

(Z+tE)(Ct^{-1}+M)=\begin{pmatrix}Z_{11}&0\\
0&C_{22}^{-1}t\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}Z_{11}^{-1}&0\\
0&C_{22}t^{-1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{id}_{\operatorname{im}(Z)}&0\\
0&\mathrm{id}_{\ker(Z)}\end{pmatrix}=\mathrm{id}_{X}

and similarly for
(Ct^{-1}+M)(Z+tE)
. Therefore,
Z+tE\in\mathcal{E}(Z)
and thus
C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)
.

\blacksquare

Lemma 2.38.

Let
X
be a Hilbert space, and let
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
. Then
Z
has closed image if and only if there exists some
c>0
such that
\|Zx\|\geq c\|x\|
for all
x\in\ker(Z)^{\perp}
.

Proof.
If
Z
has closed image, then
\operatorname{im}(Z)
is itself a Hilbert space, and the map
Z|_{\ker(Z)^{\perp}}\!:\ker(Z)^{\perp}\to\operatorname{im}(Z)
is a bounded isomorphism. By the open mapping theorem, one then
sees that there is some
c>0
such that
\|Zx\|\geq c\|x\|
for all
x\in\ker(Z)^{\perp}
.

Conversely, suppose
\|Zx\|\geq c\|x\|
for all
x\in\ker(Z)^{\perp}
, and consider a Cauchy sequence
Zx_{n}\in\operatorname{im}(Z)
. Since we may write

X=\ker(Z)\oplus\ker(Z)^{\perp},

we may assume that
x_{n}\in\ker(Z)^{\perp}
. Using our assumption, we see that

\|x_{n}-x_{m}\|\leq\frac{1}{c}\|Zx_{n}-Zx_{m}\|

which implies that the sequence
x_{n}
is also Cauchy, and hence convergent to some
x\in\ker(Z)^{\perp}
. By continuity, we then have
Zx_{n}\to Zx
, so that
\operatorname{im}(Z)
is closed.

\blacksquare

Lemma 2.39.
Let
X
be a Hilbert space, and let
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
. Then
Z
has closed image if and only if
\sqrt{Z^{*}Z}
has closed image.

Proof.
Since
\|Zx\|=\|\sqrt{Z^{*}Z}x\|
for all
x\in X
, we deduce that

\|Zx\|\geq c\|x\|\iff\|\sqrt{Z^{*}Z}x\|\geq c\|x\|

which yields the result, applying Lemma
2.3
.

\blacksquare

Theorem 2.40: Sharpened polar decomposition.

Let
X
be a Hilbert space, and let
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
be of generalized index zero with associated isomorphism
\theta\!:\ker(Z)\xrightarrow{\sim}\operatorname{coker}(Z)
.
Then there is some
U\in\mathsf{B}(X)^{\times}
, which is also a partial isometry, such that
Z=U\sqrt{Z^{*}Z}
. Furthermore, if
\theta
is an isometry, then
U
is unitary.

Proof.
The operator
\sqrt{Z^{*}Z}
has closed image since
Z
has closed image. Furthermore, since
\|Zx\|=\|\sqrt{Z^{*}Z}x\|
, we also have that
\ker(Z)=\ker(\sqrt{Z^{*}Z})
. Therefore,
we obtain an isomorphism
U^{\mathrm{pre}}
using the first isomorphism theorem

U^{\mathrm{pre}}\!:\operatorname{im}(\sqrt{Z^{*}Z})\xrightarrow{\sim}V/\ker(\sqrt{Z^{*}Z})=V/\ker(Z)\xrightarrow{\sim}\operatorname{im}(Z),

given explicitly by
\sqrt{Z^{*}Z}x\mapsto Zx
. This is an isometry since
\|Zx\|=\|\sqrt{Z^{*}Z}x\|
. We would now like to extend this to a continuous invertible operator
X\to X
. To do this, we make use of
the fact that we have two separate decompositions for
X
, namely

X=\operatorname{im}(\sqrt{Z^{*}Z})\oplus\ker(\sqrt{Z^{*}Z})=\operatorname{im}(\sqrt{Z^{*}Z})\oplus\ker(Z)

and

\phantom{.}X=\operatorname{im}(Z)\oplus\operatorname{im}(Z)^{\perp}.

For notational purposes, we let
\iota\!:\operatorname{coker}(Z)\xrightarrow{\sim}\operatorname{im}(Z)^{\perp}
be the obvious isomorphism. From this, we define
U\!:X\xrightarrow{\sim}X
by

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{im}(\sqrt{Z^{*}Z})\oplus\ker(Z)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{im}(Z)\oplus\operatorname{coker}(Z)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{im}(Z)\oplus\operatorname{im}(Z)^{\perp}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sim}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(U^{\mathrm{pre}}\text{,}\,\theta)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\mathrm{id}\text{,}\,\iota)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sim}

where we note that every map involved is a continuous isomorphism, so that
U\in\mathsf{B}(X)^{\times}
. It is now clear by the first decomposition that for all
x=x_{0}+\sqrt{Z^{*}Z}x_{1}\in X
, we will have

Zx=Z\sqrt{Z^{*}Z}x_{1}=U^{\mathrm{pre}}\sqrt{Z^{*}Z}\sqrt{Z^{*}Z}x_{1}=U^{\mathrm{pre}}\sqrt{Z^{*}Z}(x_{0}+\sqrt{Z^{*}Z}x_{1})=U\sqrt{Z^{*}Z}x

so that
Z=U\sqrt{Z^{*}Z}
. Finally, when
\theta
above is an isometry, we see that
U
is a surjective isometry, hence unitary.

\blacksquare

Corollary 2.41.

Let
X
be a Hilbert space, and let
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
. Then
Z
is of generalized index zero if and only if
\operatorname{im}(Z)
is closed and there is some invertible operator
U\in\mathsf{B}(X)^{\times}
such
that
UZ
is self-adjoint.

Proof.
If
Z
is of generalized index zero, then this is a straightforward consequence of Remark
2.3
and Theorem
2.3
. Conversely,
if
Z
has closed image and we are given some
U\in\mathsf{B}(X)^{\times}
such that
UZ
is self-adjoint, then we see that
UZ
is of generalized index zero by Lemma
2.3
.
Since
\operatorname{im}(UZ)=U\operatorname{im}(Z)\cong\operatorname{im}(Z)
and
\ker(UZ)=\ker(Z)
, we see that
Z
is of generalized index zero.

\blacksquare

Lemma 2.42.

Let
X
be a Hilbert space, and let
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
. If
Z
is of generalized index zero, then so is
\sqrt{Z^{*}Z}
.

Proof.
By the proof of Theorem
2.3
, we have an isometric isomorphism

U^{\mathrm{pre}}\!:\operatorname{im}(\sqrt{Z^{*}Z})\to\operatorname{im}(Z),

and thus we have an induced isomorphism given by the composite

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ker(\sqrt{Z^{*}Z})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ker(Z)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{coker}(Z)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{coker}(\sqrt{Z^{*}Z})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sim}

\blacksquare

Theorem 2.43.

Let
X
be a Hilbert space, and let
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
be of generalized index zero. Then

\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)=\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(Z)\not=\varnothing.

Proof.
Using Theorem
2.3
, write
Z=U\sqrt{Z^{*}Z}
. By Lemma
2.3
,
\sqrt{Z^{*}Z}
is of generalized index zero. Furthermore,
since it is self-adjoint, Lemma
2.3
means we can compute

\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)=\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(U\sqrt{Z^{*}Z})=\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(\sqrt{Z^{*}Z})U^{-1}=\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(\sqrt{Z^{*}Z})U^{-1}=\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(U\sqrt{Z^{*}Z})=\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(Z).

This completes the proof.

\blacksquare

Corollary 2.44.

Let
X
be a Hilbert space, and let
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
be of generalized index zero. Then

\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{C}(Z)

where
C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)\not=\varnothing
.

Proof.
Apply Theorem
2.1
and Theorem
2.3
.

\blacksquare

Notably, we can rather remarkably determine
\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)
entirely through an algebraic description, despite its analytic nature.
The theorem can also be realized in the following highly suggestive form:

Corollary 2.45.

Let
X
be a Hilbert space,
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
of generalized index zero. Then

\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)=\{C\in\mathsf{B}(X)\mid\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{*}(C)\text{ and }\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(C)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{*}(Z)\}.

Proof.
Apply Theorem
2.3
together with Theorem
2.2
.

\blacksquare

Remark 2.46.
As a consequence of Theorem
2.3
, we see that for
C,Z\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
, we have

if and only if the sequence

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}^{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}^{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}^{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Z}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Z}

is exact. Furthermore, denoting a complex of the form
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}^{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}^{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{C}^{n}}

by
\mathsf{C}(X)
, if

\mathsf{C}(X)\cong\mathsf{C}(C)

we would have
\operatorname{H}^{k}(\mathsf{C}(X))\cong\operatorname{H}^{k}(\mathsf{C}(C))=0

2.4
Pathwise Boundedness Along Admissible Paths

Ultimately, aside from computing such things as
\mathfrak{S}(-)
and
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(-)
, we are really also interested in boundedness behaviour along
specified
paths. Indeed,
it is an immediate refinement of both
\mathfrak{S}(-)
and
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(-)
to characterize boundedness along a given arbitrary path; understanding pathwise boundedness necessarily gives us more
information about the fuzzier questions we have been addressing above.

A valuable approach now is to try to investigate
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}(Z;E(t))
(see Definition
1.3
) for particularly nice functions
E(t)
, namely analytic ones, and hope that this can in some way be characterized by
an algebraic condition. To this end, we introduce yet another gadget:

Definition 2.47.

Let
X
be a Banach space, let
V\subseteq X
be a closed subspace, and let
F^{\bullet}V
be a filtration of
V

V=F^{0}V\supseteq F^{1}V\supseteq F^{2}V\supseteq\cdots

by closed subspaces. Define the algebra

\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{F}}(F^{\bullet}V):=\{A\in\mathsf{B}(X)\mid\forall i\geq 0,\,\,A(F^{i}V)\subseteq F^{i}V\}.

To any sequence of closed subspaces
V_{k}\subseteq X
,
k\geq 0
, one may associate a filtration
F^{\bullet}_{\cap V_{k}}V_{0}
of
V_{0}
given by

F^{i}_{\cap V_{k}}V_{0}:=\bigcap_{k=0}^{i}V_{k}.

In the situation of such a filtration, we use the notation
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{F}}(\cap_{k}V_{k}):=\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{F}}(F^{\bullet}_{\cap V_{k}}V_{0})
.

Notation 2.48.
Observe that for any filtration
F^{\bullet}V
of
V
by closed subspaces, one can for each
n\geq 0
obtain a new filtration, which we denote
F^{\bullet\leq n}V
, simply by truncating
F^{\bullet}V
after the
n
th term,
i.e. by setting

F^{i\leq n}V=\begin{cases}F^{i}V&\text{if }i\leq n,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

When
F^{\bullet}V=F^{\bullet}_{\cap_{k}V_{k}}V_{0}
is the filtration of a closed subspace
V=V_{0}
induced by a sequence of closed subspaces
V_{k}
, we will write

\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{F}}(F^{\bullet\leq n}_{\cap_{k}V_{k}}V_{0}):=\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{F}}(\cap_{k}^{\leq n}V_{k}).

Proposition 2.49.

Let
X
be a Banach space, let
V\subseteq X
be a closed subspace, and let
F^{\bullet}V
be a filtration of
V
by closed subspaces. Then, for all
0\leq m\leq n\leq\infty
,

\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{F}}(F^{\bullet\leq n}V)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{F}}(F^{\bullet\leq m}V).

In particular, choosing
n=\infty
,
m=0
, and
V=\ker(Z)
for some
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
, we have

\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{F}}(F^{\bullet}\ker(Z))\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z).

Proof.
Follows immediately from the definitions.

\blacksquare

Proposition 2.50.
Let
X
be a Banach space, let
E_{k}\in\mathsf{B}(X)
for all
k\geq 0
be such that
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}E_{k}t^{k}
is an admissible path converging to
E_{0}
. Then there
exists some
0\leq n\leq\infty
such that

\bigcap_{k=0}^{n}\ker{E_{k}}=0.

In particular, for all
m\geq n
(including
m=\infty
), we have

\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{F}}(\cap_{k}^{\leq m}\ker{E_{k}})=\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{F}}(\cap_{k}^{\leq n}\ker{E_{k}}).

If
X
is finite-dimensional, then one may choose
n<\infty
.

Proof.
If the given intersection is non-trivial, then the path could not be invertible—which is required, locally to be admissible—hence yielding a contradiction. The second
assertion follows by noting that all additional conditions imposed in higher truncations are trivial, as

\bigcap_{k=0}^{m}\ker{E_{k}}\subseteq\bigcap_{k=0}^{n}\ker{E_{k}}=0.

That
n
can be chosen to be finite when
X
is a finite-dimensional space is clear, as any strictly descending chain of subspaces will stabilize at some point.

\blacksquare

Proposition 2.51.

Let
X
be a Banach space, let
E_{k}\in\mathsf{B}(X)
,
k\geq 0
, and consider an admissible path
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}E_{k}t^{k}
, together with its induced
sequence of subspaces
\ker(E_{k})
,
k\geq 0
. Then

\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}\left(E_{0};\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}E_{k}t^{k}\right)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{F}}(\cap_{k}\ker(E_{k})).

Proof.
Let
E(t)=\sum_{k}E_{k}t^{k}
. If
A\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}(E_{0};E(t))
, then, per the definition, we have

\lim_{t\to 0}\|E(t)AE(t)^{-1}\|=\lim_{t\to 0}\sup_{x\in X}\frac{\|E(t)Ax\|}{\|E(t)x\|}<\infty

and thus, in particular,

\lim_{t\to 0}\sup_{x\in\ker{E_{0}}}\frac{\|E(t)Ax\|}{\|E(t)x\|}\leq\lim_{t\to 0}\sup_{x\in X}\frac{\|E(t)Ax\|}{\|E(t)x\|}<\infty.

For notational simplicity, we will omit the limit over
t
. Expanding the above out and using that the left supremum is over
\ker{E_{0}}
, we have

\sup_{x\in\ker{E_{0}}}\frac{\|E_{0}Ax+tE_{1}Ax+t^{2}E_{2}Ax+\cdots+t^{k}E_{k}Ax+\cdots\|}{\|tE_{1}x+t^{2}E_{2}x+\cdots+t^{k}E_{k}x+\cdots\|}<\infty

which implies that we necessarily have
E_{0}Ax=0
when
x\in\ker{E_{0}}
, i.e. that
A\ker{E_{0}}\subseteq\ker{E_{0}}
. However, using that fact and factoring out the
t
, we obtain

\sup_{x\in\ker{E_{0}}}\frac{\|E_{1}Ax+tE_{2}Ax+\cdots+t^{k-1}E_{k}Ax+\cdots\|}{\|E_{1}x+tE_{2}x+\cdots+t^{k-1}E_{k}x+\cdots\|}<\infty.

We now proceed by induction: taking a supremum over
\ker(E_{0})
will be greater than taking a supremum over
\ker(E_{0})\cap\ker(E_{1})
, and so on. In particular, repeatedly applying the above reasoning,
we see that, for each
j\geq 0
,

A\left(\bigcap_{k=0}^{j}\ker(E_{k})\right)\subseteq\ker(E_{j}).

For all
0\leq j^{\prime}\leq j
we have

\bigcap_{k=0}^{j}\ker(E_{k})\subseteq\bigcap_{k=0}^{j^{\prime}}\ker(E_{k})\implies A\left(\bigcap_{k=0}^{j}\ker(E_{k})\right)\subseteq A\left(\bigcap_{k=0}^{j^{\prime}}\ker(E_{k})\right)\subseteq\ker(E_{j^{\prime}})

and thus

A\left(\bigcap_{k=0}^{j}\ker(E_{k})\right)\subseteq\bigcap_{k=0}^{j}\ker(E_{k})

which is exactly the statement that
A\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{F}}(\cap_{k}\ker{E_{k}})
.

\blacksquare

Remark 2.52.
In the case where the path is linear, i.e. of the form
Z+tE
, note that invertibility forces
\ker(Z)\cap\ker(E)=0
. Applying the above proposition, one just obtains the kernel criterion
\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)

on the right-hand side, since the filtration will be given by

\ker(Z)\supseteq\ker(Z)\cap\ker(E)=0\supseteq 0\cdots.

In other words, the proposition suggests that boundedness along a linear path is strongly related to the kernel criterion of
\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
, which aligns with the results of Section
2.1
,
particularly in light of the below Theorem
2.4
.

Proposition
2.4
turns out to imply following
striking
result about the rigidity of good paths, which, following the above remark, suggests that
good paths are fundamentally controlled by linear phenomena.

Theorem 2.53.

Let
X
be a Banach space, and let
E_{0}\in\mathsf{B}(X)
be such that there exists a good path
E_{0}+tE(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}E_{k}t^{k}
, with corresponding operator
C_{-1}\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(E_{0})
. Then

\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}(E_{0};tE(t))=\mathfrak{S}_{C_{-1}}(E_{0})=\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{F}}(\cap_{k}\ker{E_{k}})=\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(E_{0}).

In particular, for all
0\leq m\leq n\leq\infty
, we have
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{F}}(\cap_{k}^{\leq n}\ker(E_{k}))=\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{F}}(\cap_{k}^{\leq m}\ker(E_{k}))
.

Proof.
By Proposition
2.4
, Proposition
2.4
, the results of Section
2.1
, and the remarks above, we have

\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(E_{0})=\mathfrak{S}_{C_{-1}}(E_{0})\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}(E_{0};tE(t))\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{F}}(\cap_{k}\ker{E_{k}})\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(E_{0})

yielding the first result. The second follows by noting that, for all
m,n
as stated, we have

\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{F}}(\cap_{k}^{\leq n}\ker(E_{k}))\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{F}}(\cap_{k}^{\leq n}\ker(E_{k}))\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{F}}(\cap_{k}^{\leq m}\ker(E_{k}))\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{F}}(\cap_{k}^{\leq 0}\ker(E_{k}))=\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(E_{0})

providing us the desired equalities.

\blacksquare

Corollary 2.54.

Let
X
be a Banach space, and let
E_{0}\in\mathsf{B}(X)
be such that there exists a good path
E_{0}+tE(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}E_{k}t^{k}
. Then there exists some
n>0
such that

\forall m<n,\,\ker{E_{0}}\subseteq\ker{E_{m}},\quad\text{and}\quad\ker{E_{0}}\cap\ker{E_{n}}=0.

Proof.
Apply the second statement in Theorem
2.4
. In particular, since

\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{F}}(\cap_{k}^{\leq 1}\ker(E_{k}))=\mathfrak{S}_{\mathfrak{F}}(\cap_{k}^{\leq 0}\ker(E_{k}))=\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(E_{0}),

in order to avoid a strictly non-trivial condition, we must either have
\ker{E_{0}}\subseteq\ker{E_{1}}
, or we must have
\ker{E_{0}}\cap\ker{E_{1}}=0
. In the latter case, we are done; in the former,
we repeat the same reasoning. Eventually, this must terminate: if it did not, we would have
\ker(E_{0})\subseteq\ker(E_{k})
for every
E_{k}
, in which case the path could not be invertible.

\blacksquare

Remark 2.55.
Consider two paths
U_{k}^{(1)}\to Z
and
U_{k}^{(2)}\to Z
. Then one may form a new path to
Z
given by

(U_{k}^{(1,2)})_{k\geq 1}:=(U_{1}^{(1)},U_{1}^{(2)},U_{2}^{(1)},U_{2}^{(2)},\ldots,U_{k}^{(1)},U_{k}^{(2)},\ldots),

that is, by intertwining the paths given by
U_{k}^{(1)}
and
U_{k}^{(2)}
. The pathwise algebra
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}(Z;U_{k}^{(1,2)})
then acts like taking the intersection between
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}(Z;U_{k}^{(1)})
and

\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}(Z;U_{k}^{(2)})
. Furthermore, the same general trick can be done with a countable sequence of paths
U_{k}^{(n)}
,
n\geq 1
, by considering the path

U_{k}^{(\geq 0)}:=(U_{1}^{(1)},U_{1}^{(2)},U_{2}^{(1)},U_{1}^{(3)},U_{2}^{(2)},U_{3}^{(1)},U_{1}^{(4)},U_{2}^{(3)},U_{3}^{(2)},U_{4}^{(1)},\ldots).

Remark 2.56.
While it is generally hard to produce elements of
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}(Z;U_{k})
for some
U_{k}\to Z
, there are certain simple conditions that can be used to produce examples, at least in principle. Notably,
if
A
satisfies
U_{n}A=BU_{n}
, then
A\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}(Z;U_{k})
; in particular, then one simply has that
U_{n}AU_{n}^{-1}=B
is constant.

Remark 2.57.
There is a more general approach one can use to determine
\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{pw}}(Z;U_{k})
in some cases. Let
X
be a Banach space, let
\varphi\!:\mathsf{B}(X)\to\mathsf{B}(X)
be a bounded linear operator,
and let
U\in\mathsf{B}(X)^{\times}
be an invertible operator. To this, we can associate a bounded operator

\Omega_{\varphi,U}\!:\mathsf{B}(X)\to\mathsf{B}(X),\quad A\mapsto\varphi(UAU^{-1}).

In particular, if
U_{k}\to Z
is some convergent sequence of invertible operators on
X
, then one obtains from this a sequence of bounded operators
\Omega^{(k)}:=\Omega_{\varphi,U_{k}}

on
\mathsf{B}(X)
. Taking the limit, one obtains an
unbounded
operator
\Omega
on
\mathsf{B}(X)
, and the task of determining
\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{pw}}(Z;U_{k})
is exactly that of determining the
domain of
\Omega
.

If
X
is
finite-dimensional,
this can in principle be done computationally on a case-by-case basis. Picking a basis on
X\cong\mathbb{C}^{n}
, one obtains a basis on
\mathsf{B}(X)\cong\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})

given by the elementary matrices, and the operators
\Omega^{(k)}
can be expressed as
n^{2}\times n^{2}
matrices under this basis, with entries
\omega^{(k)}_{ij}
. By the equivalence of norms in finite-dimensional spaces, we
can assume that the norm on
\mathsf{B}(X)
is the
L^{\infty}
-norm on
\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}
, in which case

\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\Omega^{(k)}A\|<\infty\iff\lim_{k\to\infty}\max_{1\leq i\leq n^{2}}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n^{2}}\omega^{(k)}_{ij}A_{(j)}\right|<\infty

where by
A_{(j)}
we mean the
j
th coefficient of
A
as a vector in
\mathsf{B}(X)\cong\mathbb{C}^{n^{2}}
. When everything is explicit, this is something which could be checked on a computer, as it corresponds
to what is essentially a system of linear constraints.

Remark 2.58.
If one constrains the situation even more, to the case of a finite-dimensional
X
and a
polynomial
path
Z+tE(t)
(i.e. where
E(t)
is a polynomial in
t
with coefficients in
\mathsf{B}(X)\cong\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
),
then one can characterize
\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{pw}}(Z;Z+tE(t))
in a yet simpler way. In general, for any path
Z+tE(t)
, since for all (sufficiently small)
t>0
the matrix
Z+tE(t)
is invertible, we can observe that

\operatorname{adj}(Z+tE(t))\cdot(Z+tE(t))=\det(Z+tE(t))\cdot I\implies(Z+tE(t))^{-1}=\frac{\operatorname{adj}(Z+tE(t))}{\det(Z+tE(t))}

where
\operatorname{adj}(-)
denotes the adjucate matrix. In particular,

\lim_{t\to 0}\|(Z+tE(t))A(Z+tE(t))^{-1}\|=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\|(Z+tE(t))A\operatorname{adj}(Z+tE(t))\|}{\left|\det(Z+tE(t))\right|}<\infty

happens if and only if

\|(Z+tE(t))A\operatorname{adj}(Z+tE(t))\|=O(\det(Z+tE(t)))\quad\text{as }t\to 0.

When
E(t)
is chosen to be a polynomial in
t
(and the norm is chosen appropriately), both sides of the above will be polynomials in
t
, and thus comparing
growth rates comes down purely to comparing which one has the lowest degree power of
t
with a non-zero coefficient. This means that it is possible, in a situation where everything
is explicit, to completely determine necessary and sufficient conditions for a matrix
A
to be bounded along the path
Z+tE(t)
.

2.5
A Preorder on Bounded Linear Maps

In the preceding sections, we have focused our attention solely on
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(-)
. We will now turn to the harder question of understanding
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(-)
. To help us along
with this, and to give ourselves a framework in which to place this question, we will note that
there is some interesting behaviour exhibited when
comparing
different choices of
\varphi
. This will allow us to answer certain cases of Question
1.1
.
To study this more systematically, we will now introduce a relation to encode these comparisons in a very general context. Most of this will not be necessary for our purposes.

Notation 2.59.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra, and
X
,
Y
be Banach spaces. Let
\varphi\!:V\to X
and
\psi\!:V\to Y
be bounded linear maps. We define the relation
\preceq
by
\varphi\preceq\psi
if and only if for all
a\in V
,
there exists a right-continuous order-preserving
f_{a}\!:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
depending only on the conjugacy class of
a
, such that
\|\varphi(a)\|\leq f_{a}(\|\psi(a)\|)
. If
\varphi\preceq\psi

and
\psi\preceq\varphi
, then we write
\varphi\simeq\psi
.

Proposition 2.60.
The relation
\preceq
is a preorder, i.e. it is reflexive and transitive. In particular,
\simeq
is an equivalence relation.

Proof.
That
\psi\preceq\psi
for all
\psi\!:V\to X
is clear. If
\psi\preceq\psi^{\prime}\preceq\psi^{\prime\prime}
is exhibited by the sets of functions
\{f_{a}\}_{a}
and
\{g_{a}\}_{a}
, then

\|\psi(a)\|\leq f_{a}(\|\psi^{\prime}(a)\|)\leq f_{a}(g_{a}(\|\psi^{\prime\prime}(a)\|))

so that
\psi\preceq\psi^{\prime\prime}
. That
\simeq
is now an equivalence relations follows trivially.

\blacksquare

The below proposition will be useful, at least in principle, to reduce the complexity of our situation (primarily for finite-dimensional spaces). To be as general as possible, we introduced a relation on a very loose set of maps (and, in fact, being
a
set
here is subject to some set-theoretical subtleties), but we are primarily interested in the case when
V
is a
finite-dimensional
Banach algebra. In this case, one can reduce to considering linear endomorphisms of
V
itself;
see Corollary
2.5
.

Proposition 2.61.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra, and suppose we have bounded linear maps

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces V}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{\prime}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho}

Then
\rho\circ\varphi\preceq\varphi
. If, in additon, there is some constant
C>0
such that
\|x\|_{X}\leq C\|\rho(x)\|_{X^{\prime}}
(for example, if
\rho
is an isometry), then
\rho\circ\varphi\simeq\varphi
.

Proof.
We have that for all
a\in V
,

\|(\rho\circ\varphi)(a)\|\leq\|\rho\|\cdot\|\varphi(a)\|.

Therefore,
\rho\circ\varphi\preceq\varphi
. For the final statement, note that, given the assumptions, we have

\|\varphi(a)\|_{X}\leq C\cdot\|(\rho\circ\varphi)(a)\|

which supplies the desired
\varphi\preceq\rho\circ\varphi
.

\blacksquare

Corollary 2.62.

Let
V
be a finite-dimensional Banach algebra, let
X
be a Banach space, and consider a bounded linear map
\varphi\!:V\to X
. Then there exists a linear map
\varphi^{\prime}\!:V\to V

such that
\varphi\simeq\varphi^{\prime}
.

Proof.
Since
V
is finite dimensional,
\varphi
has finite dimensional image in
X
, and therefore we may factor it as

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces V}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{im}(\varphi)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{\varphi}}

so that
\varphi\simeq\tilde{\varphi}
since the inclusion
\operatorname{im}(\varphi)\to X
is an isometry (when
\operatorname{im}(\varphi)
is endowed with the norm induced by
X
). Now, since
\operatorname{im}(\varphi)
is finite-dimensional of dimension
\leq\dim(V)
,
we can find some embedding
\iota\!:\operatorname{im}(\varphi)\to V
which, by the equivalence of norms in finite-dimensional vector spaces, will satisfy the conditions of Proposition
2.5
.
Setting
\varphi^{\prime}=\iota\circ\tilde{\varphi}
completes the proof.

\blacksquare

Lemma 2.63.
Let
V
be a Banach algebra, and let
\varphi\!:V\to V
be a bounded linear map. Then
\varphi\preceq\mathrm{id}
.

Proof.
Since
\varphi
is continuous, we have for all
a\in V
that

\|\varphi(a)\|\leq\|\varphi\|\cdot\|a\|

which immediately tells us that
\varphi\preceq\mathrm{id}
.

\blacksquare

For our purposes, the below Lemma
2.5
is fundamental: it is what allows us to relate the relation
\preceq
to the questions investigated in this document.

Lemma 2.64.

Let
f\!:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be an order-preserving right-continuous function. Then, for any sequence
(x_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
, we have

\limsup_{k\to\infty}f(x_{k})\leq f(\limsup_{k\to\infty}x_{k}).

Proof.
An order-preserving (i.e. monotone) function is right continuous if and only if it preserves infima. Therefore, we have

\limsup_{k\to\infty}f(x_{k})=\inf_{k_{0}\geq 0}\{\sup_{k\geq k_{0}}f(x_{k})\}\leq\inf_{k_{0}\geq k}\{f(\sup_{k\geq k_{0}}x_{k})\}=f(\inf_{k_{0}\geq k}\{\sup_{k\geq k_{0}}x_{k}\})=f(\limsup_{k\to\infty}x_{k})

as desired.

\blacksquare

Lemma 2.65.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra, and let
\varphi,\psi\!:V\to V
be bounded linear maps. Then the following statements are equivalent.

(a)

The two maps satisfy
\varphi\preceq\psi
.

(b)

For all
a\in V
, and any sequence of invertibles
u_{k}\in V
, we have

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\|\psi(u_{k}au_{k}^{-1})\|<\infty\implies\limsup_{k\to\infty}\|\varphi(u_{k}au_{k}^{-1})\|<\infty.

Proof.
To see that (a) implies (b), observe that by Lemma
2.5
we have

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\|\varphi(u_{k}au_{k}^{-1})\|\leq\limsup_{k\to\infty}f_{a}\!\left(\|\psi(u_{k}au_{k}^{-1})\|\right)\leq f_{a}\!\left(\limsup_{k\to\infty}\|\psi(u_{k}au_{k}^{-1})\|\right)<\infty

which is the statement we wanted.

To show that (b) implies (a), we must, for each
a\in V
, construct a function
f_{a}\!:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with some properties, and in particular it must not depend
on the conjugacy class of
a
. Thus, consider the functions

f_{a}^{\mathrm{pre}}(x):=\sup\left\{\|\varphi(uau^{-1})\|\mid u\in V\text{ invertible, }\|\psi(uau^{-1})\|\leq x\right\}

and

f_{a}(x_{0}):=\lim_{x\to x_{0}^{+}}f_{a}^{\mathrm{pre}}(x).

We must show that
f_{a}^{\mathrm{pre}}
is well-defined; in other words, we must show that it is finite. If there is some
x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
f_{a}^{\mathrm{pre}}(x)=\infty
, then there must
in particular exist some sequence of invertibles
u_{k}\in V
such that
\lim_{k\to\infty}(\|\varphi(u_{k}au_{k}^{-1})\|)=\infty
and such that, for all
k
, we have
\|\psi(u_{k}au_{k}^{-1})\|\leq x
.
However, by the latter condition we have

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\|\psi(u_{k}au_{k}^{-1})\|<\infty\implies\limsup_{k\to\infty}\|\varphi(u_{k}au_{k})\|<\infty

which contradicts the aformentioned divergence. Therefore, we must have
f_{a}^{\mathrm{pre}}(x)<\infty
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. As an immediate corollary of the definition, we see that
f_{a}

is right-continuous and order-preserving. Finally, we have that, for all invertible
u\in V
,

\|\varphi(uau^{-1})\|\leq f_{a}^{\mathrm{pre}}(\|\psi(uau^{-1})\|)\leq f_{a}(\|\psi(uau^{-1})\|)

so that
\varphi\preceq\psi
as desired.

\blacksquare

In particular, we can now relate pathwise
\varphi
-boundedness and pathwise
\psi
-boundedness whenever
\varphi
and
\psi
are related, which is the concrete reason
one might care about the
\preceq
relation in our context.

Proposition 2.66.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra, let
z\in V
, and let
u_{k}\to z
be a sequence of invertibles. If
\varphi\preceq\psi
, then

\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\psi}(z;u_{k})\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(z;u_{k}).

In particular,

(a)

if
\varphi\simeq\psi
, then
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\psi}(z;u_{k})=\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(z;u_{k})
, and

(b)

for all
\varphi\!:V\to V
, we have
\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}(z;u_{k})\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\varphi}(z;u_{k})
.

Proof.
Suppose that
\varphi\preceq\psi
, and that
a\in\mathfrak{S}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{\psi}(z;u_{k})
.Then, by Lemma
2.5
,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\psi(u_{k}au_{k}^{-1})\|<\infty\implies\lim_{k\to\infty}\|u_{k}au_{k}^{-1}\|<\infty

which means
a\in\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\mathrm{pw}}(z;u_{k})
. The remaining assertions follow trivially.

\blacksquare

Corollary 2.67.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra, and let
\varphi\preceq\psi
. Then, for all
z\in V
,

\mathfrak{S}^{\psi}(z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}(z)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\psi}(z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(z).

In particular, if
\varphi\simeq\psi
, then

\mathfrak{S}^{\psi}(z)=\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}(z)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\psi}(z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(z).

Proof.
Apply Proposition
2.5
and Proposition
1.3
.

\blacksquare

2.6
Existence With a Modifier

We first apply Corollary
2.5
to reduce a particular case of computing
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(-)
to computing
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(-)
, which we have already done. To do this, we need
a preliminary lemma which tells us that a particularly interesting class of
\varphi
’s is equivalent to the identity map. The proof makes use of Gershgorin’s circle theorem;
see e.g.
[
HJ12
, p. 389,  Corollary 6.1.3]
.

Theorem 2.68: Gershgorin’s circle theorem.
Let
A=(a_{ij})
be a complex
n\times n
matrix, let
R_{i}=\sum_{j\not=i}|a_{ij}|
, and let
D(a_{ii},R_{i})
be the disk of radius
R_{i}
centered at
a_{ii}
. Then
all eigenvalues are contained within the region
\mathbf{G}:=\bigcup_{i}D(a_{ii},R_{i})
. Furthermore, each connected component of
\mathbf{G}
contains exactly as many eigenvalues (counted
with their algebraic multiplicty) as it contains
a_{ii}
’s.

Lemma 2.69.

Let
J=\mathbb{1}-I
, and let
\phi\!:\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\to\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
be the bounded linear map given by
\phi\!:A\mapsto J*A
. Then
\phi\simeq\mathrm{id}
.

Proof.
Observe that we trivially have
\phi\preceq\mathrm{id}
, so it remains only to check that
\mathrm{id}\preceq\phi
. We first note the following inequalities

\|A\|\leq c\sum_{i,j}|a_{ij}|\leq c^{\prime}\|J*A\|+c\sum_{i}|a_{ii}|,

which we derive from the equivalences of norms on finite-dimensional vector spaces. We will use Gershgorin’s circle theorem to estimate the latter sum. Let
\mathbf{G}
be
as in the statement of Gershgorin’s circle theorem, and let
(\lambda_{i})
be the eigenvalues of
A
, counted with algebraic multiplicity and numbered such that
\lambda_{i}

is in the same connected component of
\mathbf{G}
as
a_{ii}
, which is possible by Gershgorin. We then have

\sum_{i}|a_{ii}|=\sum_{i}|a_{ii}-\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{i}|\leq\sum_{i}|a_{ii}-\lambda_{i}|+\sum_{i}|\lambda_{i}|.

We now specialize down to estimating just one term
|a_{ii}-\lambda_{i}|
. Let
\mathbf{G}_{i}
be the connected component of
\mathbf{G}
containing
a_{ii}
. Since
\mathbf{G}_{i}
is the union of a number of disks
D(a_{jj},R_{j})
,
it is geometrically immediate that any two points of
\mathbf{G}_{i}
are certainly within
2\sum_{a_{jj}\in\mathbf{G}_{i}}R_{j}
of each other, and therefore, since
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbf{G}_{i}
, we may as well
make an even more extreme estimate summing over
all

j
, so

|a_{ii}-\lambda_{i}|\leq 2\sum_{j=1}^{n}R_{j}=2\sum_{j}\sum_{k\not=j}|a_{jk}|\leq c^{\prime\prime}\|J*A\|.

Putting this together, we get

\|A\|\leq c^{\prime}\|J*A\|+c\sum_{i}c^{\prime\prime}\|J*A\|+\sum_{i}|\lambda_{i}|=(c^{\prime}+ncc^{\prime\prime})\|J*A\|+c\sum_{i}|\lambda_{i}|

which shows that
\mathrm{id}\preceq\phi
, as desired.

\blacksquare

Theorem 2.70.

Denote by
\mathbb{J}
the set of bounded linear maps
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\to\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
that under some basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
are of the form
A\mapsto(\mathbb{1}-I)*A
, and let
Z\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
. If
\varphi\in\mathbb{J}
, then

\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(Z).

In particular,
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
.

Proof.
Supposing we can prove the first assertion, the second follows by Corollary
2.1
. To prove our main claim,
apply Corollary
2.5
and Lemma
2.6
.

\blacksquare

Now, in Lemma
2.6
, we used a rather specialized trick to show that
(J*)\simeq\mathrm{id}
, and then used general theory to compute
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{J}(-)
. This trick has no reason
whatsoever to work for more general choices of pairs
(\varphi,\mathrm{id})
, so we have to change tactics completely if we want a hope of understanding the wider situation. Based on the results of
Section
2.1
, we expect that
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(Z)
should be related to some modified version of the sets
\mathfrak{S}_{C}(Z)
for
C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)
. We introduce this modification now:

Definition 2.71.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra, let
\varphi\!:V\to X
be a bounded linear map to a Banach space
X
, and let
c,z\in V
be such that
zc=cz=0
. Define the set

\mathfrak{S}_{c}^{\varphi}(z):=\ker(a\mapsto\varphi(zac))=\{a\in V\mid\varphi(zac)=0\}.

Note that
\mathfrak{S}_{c}(z)=\mathfrak{S}_{c}^{\mathrm{id}}(z)
.

Proposition 2.72.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra, let
\varphi\!:V\to X
be a bounded linear map, let
c,z\in V
, and let
p\in V^{\times}
. Then

\mathfrak{S}_{c}^{\varphi}(zp)=p^{-1}\mathfrak{S}_{c}^{\varphi}(z)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{S}_{p^{-1}c}^{\varphi}(z)=\mathfrak{S}_{c}^{\varphi}(z)p.

In particular,
\mathfrak{S}_{p^{-1}c}^{\varphi}(zp)=p^{-1}\mathfrak{S}_{c}^{\varphi}(z)p
.

Proof.
These equalities follow immediately from

\varphi(zac)=\varphi((zp)(p^{-1}a)c)\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi(zac)=\varphi(z(ap)(p^{-1}c))

respectively. The final statement is an immediate combination of the first two.

\blacksquare

Proposition 2.73.
Let
V
be a Banach algebra, let
\varphi\!:V\to X
be a bounded linear map, let
z\in V
, and let
c\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)\not=\varnothing
. Then

\mathfrak{S}_{c}^{\varphi}(z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(z).

Proof.
Since
c\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)
, we know that there is at least one corresponding good path
z+te(t)
converging to
z
. Thus, for any
a\in\mathfrak{S}_{c}^{\varphi}(z)
we have

\varphi\left((z+te(t))A(z+te(t))^{-1}\right)=\varphi(zact^{-1}+O(1))=O(1)

where the last equality uses linearity and continuity. As in Lemma
2.1
, we are done.

\blacksquare

Corollary 2.74.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra, let
\varphi\!:V\to X
be a bounded linear map, and let
z\in V
. Then

\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(z)\supseteq\bigcup_{c\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(z)}\mathfrak{S}_{c}^{\varphi}(z).

Based on this, one can make a reasonably natural conjecture: that the above inclusion should be an equality.

Conjecture 2.75.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra, and let
\varphi\!:V\to X
be a bounded linear map. Then, for all
z\in V
,

\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(z)=\bigcup_{c\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(z)}\mathfrak{S}_{c}^{\varphi}(z).

Remark 2.76.
When
X=\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
and
\varphi=\mathrm{id}
, the above is trivially true with the theory we have developed. In particular, we know that in fact

\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\mathrm{id}}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{C}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{C}^{\mathrm{id}}(Z)

and so we also have
\mathfrak{S}_{C_{1}}^{\mathrm{id}}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{C_{2}}^{\mathrm{id}}(Z)
for all
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)
. In other words, when
\varphi=\mathrm{id}
, the conjecture is easily verified and is fairly tautological.
However, in general, we have no reason to expect
\mathfrak{S}_{C_{1}}^{\varphi}(Z)
to be equal to
\mathfrak{S}_{C_{2}}^{\varphi}(Z)
, but nonetheless we know that for all
C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)
, we have
\mathfrak{S}_{C}^{\varphi}(Z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(Z)

following the exact same proof as in the case
\varphi=\mathrm{id}
(see below).

In the cases we are most interested in, Conjecture
2.6
turns out to be true.

Lemma 2.77.

Let
X
be a vector space, and suppose there is a direct sum decomposition of
X
of the form

X=X_{1}\oplus X_{2}.

Let
U\!:X\to X
be a linear map of the form

U=\begin{pmatrix}Q&R\\
S&T\end{pmatrix}

such that
Q\!:X_{1}\to X_{1}
is invertible. Then

(a)

the map
P:=T-SQ^{-1}R
is invertible if and only if
U
is invertible, and

(b)

if
U
is invertible, the inverse is given by

U^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}Q^{-1}+Q^{-1}RP^{-1}SQ^{-1}&-Q^{-1}RP^{-1}\\
-P^{-1}SQ^{-1}&P^{-1}\end{pmatrix}

Proof.
Note that we can write

U=\begin{pmatrix}Q&R\\
S&T\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{id}_{X_{1}}&0\\
SQ^{-1}&\mathrm{id}_{X_{2}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}Q&0\\
0&P\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{id}_{X_{1}}&Q^{-1}R\\
0&\mathrm{id}_{X_{2}}\end{pmatrix}

and that the two surrounding maps are invertible on account of being lower/upper block triangular. Therefore, the invertibility of
U
is equivalent to the invertibility of the middle map,
whose invertibility, due to being block diagonal, depends only on
Q
and
P
. Since
Q
is invertible by assumption, we see that
P
is invertible if and only if
U
is. This proves (a).
The proof of (b), then by (a) we know that
P
is invertible, and that
U^{-1}
is of the given form is just a standard computation coming from the expression given above.

\blacksquare

Theorem 2.78.

Let
X,Y
be Hilbert spaces, let
\varphi\!:\mathsf{B}(X)\to Y
be a bounded linear map, and let
Z^{\prime}\in\mathsf{B}(X)
be of generalized index zero. Then

\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(Z^{\prime})=\bigcup_{C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)}\mathfrak{S}_{C}^{\varphi}(Z^{\prime}).

Proof.
Using Theorem
2.3
, write
Z^{\prime}=MZ
where
M
is invertible and
Z
is self-adjoint. Now, consider the decomposition

X=\operatorname{im}(Z)\oplus\ker(Z),\quad\text{under which }Z=\begin{pmatrix}Z_{11}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}

where
Z_{11}
is invertible. From now on, we will work in the setting of this decomposition.

If
A\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(Z^{\prime})
, then there is some sequence
U_{n}^{\prime}\to Z^{\prime}
exhibiting this fact. Since
Z^{\prime}=MZ
, we may write
U_{n}^{\prime}=MU_{n}
where
U_{n}\to Z
, i.e.

U_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}Z_{11}+Q_{n}&R_{n}\\
S_{n}&T_{n}\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{where }Q_{n},R_{n},S_{n},T_{n}\to 0\text{ as }n\to\infty.

For simplicity, we write
\tilde{Q}_{n}=Z_{11}+Q_{n}
. In what follows, we will neglect to include the subscript
n
to keep the notation uncluttered.

We are interested in the product
U^{\prime}AU^{\prime-1}
, which by Lemma
2.6
can be written as

MUAU^{-1}M^{-1}=M\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{Q}&R\\
S&T\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}&A_{12}\\
A_{21}&A_{22}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{Q}^{-1}+\tilde{Q}^{-1}RP^{-1}S\tilde{Q}^{-1}&-\tilde{Q}^{-1}RP^{-1}\\
-P^{-1}S\tilde{Q}^{-1}&P^{-1}\end{pmatrix}M^{-1}

where
P_{n}=T_{n}-S_{n}(I+Q_{n})^{-1}R_{n}
. We consider the top-right block of this, which (as in the proof of Theorem
2.6
) is given by

(\tilde{Q}_{n}A_{12}+R_{n}A_{22}-(\tilde{Q}_{n}A_{11}+R_{n}A_{21})\tilde{Q}_{n}^{-1}R_{n})P_{n}^{-1}.

Using that
\tilde{Q}_{n}=Z_{11}+Q_{n}
, we note that the above may be rewritten to be of the form

Z_{11}A_{12}P^{-1}_{n}+B_{n}P^{-1}_{n}

where
B_{n}\to 0
. Now, one easily checks that in all blocks, any unbounded term has its unboundedness come from
P^{-1}_{n}
, and furthermore,
that the top-right is the only block containing a term of the form
\text{const}\cdot P^{-1}_{n}
. As a result, after applying the reverse triangle inequality, one sees that in

\displaystyle\|\varphi(MU_{n}AU_{n}^{-1}M^{-1})\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\varphi\!\left(M\begin{pmatrix}\bullet&Z_{11}A_{12}P_{n}^{-1}+B_{n}P^{-1}_{n}\\
\bullet&\bullet\end{pmatrix}M^{-1}\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\geq\left|\left\|\varphi\!\left(M\begin{pmatrix}\bullet&B_{n}P_{n}^{-1}\\
\bullet&\bullet\end{pmatrix}M^{-1}\right)\right\|-\left\|\varphi\!\left(M\begin{pmatrix}0&Z_{11}A_{12}P^{-1}_{n}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}M^{-1}\right)\right\|\right|

the term

\left\|\varphi\!\left(M\begin{pmatrix}0&Z_{11}A_{12}P^{-1}_{n}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}M^{-1}\right)\right\|

dominates as
n\to\infty
. By assumption,
A
and the sequence
U_{n}
were chosen such that this is finite, and therefore, we deduce that we must necessarily have

\varphi\!\left(M\begin{pmatrix}0&Z_{11}A_{12}P^{-1}_{n}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}M^{-1}\right)=0

once
n
is sufficiently large. Clearly, we have that

C:=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&P_{n}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)\quad\leadsto\quad C^{\prime}:=CM^{-1}\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z^{\prime})

so that

\displaystyle 0=\varphi\!\left(M\begin{pmatrix}0&Z_{11}A_{12}P^{-1}_{n}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}M^{-1}\right)=\varphi(MZACM^{-1})=\varphi(Z^{\prime}AC^{\prime})

We conclude that

\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(Z^{\prime})\subseteq\bigcup_{C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z^{\prime})}\mathfrak{S}_{C}^{\varphi}(Z^{\prime})

as desired.

\blacksquare

Theorem 2.79.

Let
X
be a Hilbert space, and let
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
. We let

\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}^{\varphi,*}(A,Z)=\limsup_{r\to 0}\inf_{\|U-Z\|<r}\|\varphi(U^{-1}AU)\|,

and define the sets

\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi,*}(Z)=\{A\in\mathsf{B}(X)\mid\mathfrak{N}_{\cup}^{\varphi,*}(A,Z)<\infty\},\quad\mathfrak{S}_{C}^{\varphi,*}(Z)=\{A\in\mathsf{B}(X)\mid\varphi(CAZ)=0\}.

Then, if
Z
is of generalized index zero,

\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi,*}(Z)=\bigcup_{C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)}\mathfrak{S}_{C}^{\varphi,*}(Z).

Proof.
First, note that one may reduce to the case where
Z
is self-adjoint. In particular, for any
Z^{\prime}\in\mathsf{B}(X)
, we write
Z^{\prime}=MZ
with
M
invertible
and
Z
self-adjoint by Theorem
2.3
. Then dual versions of the tranformation laws for
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(-)
and
\mathfrak{S}_{C}^{\varphi}(-)

mean that it will suffice to consider
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi,*}(Z)
. After this, simply apply a dual version of the proof strategy of Theorem
2.6
,
considering the bottom-left block instead of the top-right block.

\blacksquare

While we have already solved the case when
X
is finite-dimensional and
\varphi(A)=J*A
by reducing it to the unmodified version of the problem, which in turn was done by a clever trick, we may employ
Theorem
2.6
to give a different proof of Theorem
2.6
. To begin, we make the following observations:

Lemma 2.80.

Let
Z,C\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
be such that
CZ=0
. Then for any matrix
A
we have
(ZAC)^{2}=0
, and in particular,
ZAC
is nilpotent.

Proof.
Trivially,
(ZAC)^{2}=ZACZAC=ZA(CZ)AC=0
.

\blacksquare

Proposition 2.81.

Let
J=\mathbb{1}-I
, let
\varphi\!:\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\to\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
be the map given by
A\mapsto J*A
, and let
Z,C\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
be matrices such that
CZ=0
. Then

\mathfrak{S}_{C}^{\varphi}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{C}(Z).

Proof.
If
ZAC=0
, then clearly also
J*(ZAC)=0
. Conversely,
J*(ZAC)=0
is equivalent to saying that
ZAC
is diagonal, which means
in particular that any basis vector
e_{k}
is an eigenvector of
ZAC
. However, by Lemma
2.6
the matrix

ZAC
is nilpotent, so all eigenvalues are zero. Therefore,
ZACe_{k}=0
, so that
ZAC=0
.

\blacksquare

Alternative proof of Theorem
2.6
.
Let
Z\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
and let
J=\mathbb{1}-I
. By Proposition
2.6
we have that for all
C,C^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)
,

\mathfrak{S}_{C}^{(J*)}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{C}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{C^{\prime}}(Z).

Therefore, the union in Theorem
2.6
collapses to give us

\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{(J*)}(Z)=\bigcup_{C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}}\mathfrak{S}_{C}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)

as desired.

\blacksquare

With having now proven Conjecture
2.6
in an interesting case and given an application of it, we will point out a downside of it: in practice, one must in principle check an
infinite number of conditions (one for each
C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)
) in order to know if some
A
is in
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(Z)
or not.
It would be extremely convenient if one could somehow reduce this to checking a
single
condition. However, as it turns out, this is too good to be true in general.

Example 2.82.

Here is an example showing that for at least one choice of
H,Z
, there is no
C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)
for which
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{(H*)}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{C}^{(H*)}(Z)
. Let

Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad H=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

In this case, by Theorem
2.3
, we know that all
C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)
are of the form

C=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&x&y\\
0&z&w\end{pmatrix}.

Now, a computation shows that

ZAC=\begin{pmatrix}0&xa_{12}+za_{13}&ya_{12}+wa_{13}\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{where }A=(a_{ij}).

Now the condition that
H*(ZAC)=0
just becomes
ya_{12}+wa_{13}=0
.

For any pair
(y,w)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\backslash\{(0,0)\}
, one may find a matrix
C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)
of the above form. Indeed, if they are non-zero, then choosing
z=0
and
x=1

yields that the bottom right of
C
is an upper triangular matrix, hence invertible. If only one is zero, then one can perform a similar trick. Now, by the above computation and
Theorem
2.6
, we have

\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{(H*)}(Z)=\bigcup_{C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)}\mathfrak{S}_{C}^{(H*)}(Z)=\bigcup_{(y,w)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\backslash\{(0,0)\}}\{(a_{ij})\mid ya_{12}+wa_{13}=0\}.

However, since for any pair
(a_{12},a_{13})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
we may find some pair
(y,w)\not=(0,0)
such that
ya_{12}+wa_{13}=0
, we see that

\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{(H*)}(Z)=\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{3}).

On the other hand, it is clear that for all
C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)
, we have a
strict
inclusion

\mathfrak{S}_{C}^{(H*)}(Z)\subsetneq\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n}).

Remark 2.83.
The above example also implies that, in general, it is impossible to pick a finite subset of
\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)
which completely represents
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{H}(Z)
. If it were possible,
then it is easy to see that in fact
one

C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)
would be enough, which is ruled out by the example.

2.7
Modifiers Equivalent to the Identity

We finish the section with a characterizing condition for those
\varphi
such that
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(-)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(-)
. This yields a necessary
criterion for having
\varphi\simeq\mathrm{id}
, and a complete classification in the case when
\varphi
is given by a Hadamard product.

Lemma 2.84.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra,
X
a Banach space,
\varphi\!:V\to X
be a bounded linear map, and let
c,z\in V
be such that
cz=zc=0
. Then
\mathfrak{S}_{c}(z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{c}^{\varphi}(z)
.

Proof.
If
a\in\mathfrak{S}_{c}(z)
, we will have
zac=0
, so that
\varphi(zac)=0
, and hence
a\in\mathfrak{S}_{c}^{\varphi}(z)
.

\blacksquare

Proposition 2.85.

Let
X
be a Hilbert space,
Y
a Banach space,
\varphi\!:\mathsf{B}(X)\to Y
be a bounded linear map, and let
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
be of generalized index zero. Then

\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)\iff\forall C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z),\,\mathfrak{S}_{C}^{\varphi}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{C}(Z).

Proof.
If the latter condition is satisfied, then by Theorem
2.6
we will have

\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(Z)=\bigcup_{C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)}\mathfrak{S}_{C}^{\varphi}(Z)=\bigcup_{C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)}\mathfrak{S}_{C}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z).

Conversely, suppose
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)
and fix some
C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)
. Then, by Corollary
2.3
and Lemma
2.7
,

\mathfrak{S}_{C}(Z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{C}^{\varphi}(Z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{C}(Z)

so that
\mathfrak{S}_{C}^{\varphi}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{C}(Z)
as desired.

\blacksquare

Definition 2.86.
An
orthogonal algebra
is an algebra
\mathcal{A}
with the property that
xy=0
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
. Given an orthogonal subalgebra
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathsf{B}(X)
where
X
is a Hilbert space, we define two associated
closed subspaces

\operatorname{im}(\mathcal{T}):=\bigcap_{S\in\mathcal{T}}\ker(S),\quad\ker(\mathcal{T}):=\operatorname{im}(\mathcal{T})^{\perp}.

Proposition 2.87.

Let
X
be a Hilbert space, consider an orthogonal subalgebra
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathsf{B}(X)
, and let
T\in\mathcal{T}
. Then, under the decomposition
X=\ker(\mathcal{T})\oplus\operatorname{im}(\mathcal{T})
, we can write
T
in the form

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{im}(\mathcal{T})\oplus\ker(\mathcal{T})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{im}(\mathcal{T})\oplus\ker(\mathcal{T}).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\begin{pmatrix}0&T_{12}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}}

In particular, there is a bounded operator
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
of generalized index zero and some
C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)
such that for all
T\in\mathcal{T}
we have
T=ZTC
.

Proof.
By definition of
\operatorname{im}(\mathcal{T})
, we have
\operatorname{im}(\mathcal{T})\subseteq\ker(T)
. Since
\mathcal{T}
is an orthogonal algebra, we have that
ST=0
for all
S\in\mathcal{T}
, so
\operatorname{im}(T)\subseteq\operatorname{im}(\mathcal{T})
. Therefore,
in total, we have

\operatorname{im}(T)\subseteq\operatorname{im}(\mathcal{T})\subseteq\ker(T).

From this, it is clear that
T
has the claimed form. Setting

Z=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{id}_{\operatorname{im}(\mathcal{T})}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad C=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\mathrm{id}_{\ker(\mathcal{T})}\end{pmatrix}

one obtains the final statement. That
Z
is of generalized index zero is clear by Theorem
2.3
, since
C\in\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(Z)
.

\blacksquare

Lemma 2.88.

Let
X
be a Hilbert space, and let
T\in\mathsf{B}(X)
. Then
T^{2}=0
if and only if there exists
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
of generalized index zero and
C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)
such
that
T=ZAC
.

Proof.
If
T
is of the form
T=ZAC
, then trivially
T^{2}=ZACZAC=0
. Conversely, apply Proposition
2.7
to the subalgebra
\mathbb{C}[T]\subseteq\mathsf{B}(X)

generated by
T
, which is an orthogonal algebra since
T^{2}=0
.

\blacksquare

Theorem 2.89.

Let
X,Y
be Hilbert spaces, and let
\varphi\!:\mathsf{B}(X)\to Y
be a bounded linear map. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

For all
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
of generalized index zero, we have
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)
.

(ii)

For all
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
of generalized index zero and for all
C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)
, we have
\mathfrak{S}_{C}^{\varphi}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{C}(Z)
.

(iii)

For all
T\in\mathsf{B}(X)
such that
T^{2}=0
, we have
\varphi(T)=0
if and only if
T=0
.

Proof.
The equivalence between (i) and (ii) follows by Prooposition
2.7
. We prove that (iii) is equivalent to (ii). Suppose that (ii) holds; it is equivalent to saying that

\forall Z\in\mathsf{B}(X),\,C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z),\quad[\varphi(ZAC)\not=0\iff ZAC\not=0]

and thus, if
T\not=0
is such that
T^{2}=0
, Lemma
2.7
tells us precisely that
\varphi(T)\not=0
. Therefore, (ii) implies (iii)

Conversely, if (iii) holds, then this is the same as saying that for all
T
such that
T^{2}=0
, we have
\varphi(T)=0
if and only if
T=0
.
However, since for all
A,Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
where
Z
is of generalized index zero and
C\in\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(Z)
, we have
(ZAC)^{2}=0
, this means

ZAC=0\iff\varphi(ZAC)=0

so that
\mathfrak{S}_{C}^{\varphi}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{C}(Z)
. Thus, (iii) implies (ii).

\blacksquare

Corollary 2.90.

Let
\varphi\!:\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\to\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
.

(a)

If
\varphi\simeq\mathrm{id}
and
X^{2}=0
, then
\varphi(X)=0
if and only if
X=0
.

(b)

If
\varphi
is, under some basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, given by
A\mapsto H*A
for some matrix
H
, then

\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(-)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(-)\iff\text{all non-diagonal entries of $H$ are non-zero}\iff\varphi\simeq\mathrm{id}.

Proof.
To prove (a), note that
\varphi\simeq\mathrm{id}
implies that
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(-)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(-)
, and hence Theorem
2.7
implies that
\varphi(X)\not=0
unless
X=0
.
To prove (b), first observe that the implication

\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(-)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(-)\implies\text{all non-diagonal entries of $H$ are non-zero}

follows from Theorem
2.7
, noting that the basis matrices
E_{ij}
satisfy
E_{ij}^{2}=0
as long as
i\not=j
. Next, note that if we let
\tilde{H}
be the matrix given by replacing
all non-zero entries in
H
by
1
and set
\tilde{\varphi}(A):=\tilde{H}*A
, it is clear that
\varphi\simeq\tilde{\varphi}
by the equivalence of norms on finite-dimensional spaces (for example, one may apply
Proposition
2.5
). Furthermore, it is clear that
J\preceq\tilde{\varphi}
whenever all non-diagonal entries of
H
are non-zero, so by Lemma
2.6
we get

\text{all non-diagonal entries of $H$ are non-zero}\implies\varphi\simeq\mathrm{id}.

Finally, that

\varphi\simeq\mathrm{id}\implies\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(-)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(-)

follows by Corollary
2.5
. This completes the proof.

\blacksquare

Remark 2.91.
In fact, in the above, the equivalence

\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(-)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(-)\iff\text{all non-diagonal entries of $H$ are non-zero}

follows also from noting that diagonal nilpotents are zero, and therefore if
H
satisfies the condition on the right,
X^{2}=0
, and
H*X=0
, then
X=0
. This is the same proof as
in Proposition
2.6
.

Remark 2.92.

The proof of Theorem
2.7
can be replicated more generally whenever one has an analogue of Lemma
2.7
and one
knows that Conjecture
2.6
is true. In more general settings, however, one may have to contend with
\mathfrak{C}_{-1}(-)
and
\mathfrak{C}^{\prime}(-)
no longer
obviously being identical.

3
Existence of Unbounded Paths

This section is dedicated to understanding
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}(-)
. Before we get on with our main results, though, we point out a few central devices we will use in our strategy.

3.1
Generalities

We start with a remarkably powerful observation based on the following fact: if we have some family of sets
S_{r}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
,
r>0
, such that
S_{t}\subseteq S_{t^{\prime}}
whenever
t<t^{\prime}
, then

\lim_{r\to 0}\sup{S_{r}}<\infty\implies\exists r^{\prime}>0\text{ such that }\sup{S_{r^{\prime}}}<0.

Applying this fact to
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(-)
yields the following theorem:

Theorem 3.1.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra, let
Y
be a Banach space, let
\varphi\!:V\to Y
be a bounded linear map, and let
z\in V
. Then

\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z)=\bigcup\left\{\bigcap_{z^{\prime}\in U}\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z^{\prime}):U\subseteq V\text{ open and bounded, }z\in U\right\}.

Here, by
bounded,
we mean
bounded in diameter
(as a subset of a metric space).

Proof.
If
a\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z)
, then, by definition,

\lim_{r\to 0}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|u-z\|<r\\
u\in V^{\times}\end{subarray}}\|\varphi(uau^{-1})\|<\infty.

However, for this to hold, it must be that for some
r^{\prime}>0
,

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|u-z\|<r^{\prime}\\
u\in V^{\times}\end{subarray}}\|\varphi(uau)^{-1}\|<\infty.

In other words,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z)

\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\subseteq V\\
U\text{ bounded}\\
z\in U\end{subarray}}\{a\in V\mid\sup_{u\in U\cap V^{\times}}\|\varphi(uau)^{-1}\|<\infty\}

\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\subseteq V\\
U\text{ bounded}\\
z\in U\end{subarray}}\bigcap_{z^{\prime}\in U}\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z^{\prime})\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z),

as was to be proven.

\blacksquare

Here is the really useful way we may utilize the above:

Corollary 3.2.

Let
V
be a Banach algebra,
Y
a Banach space,
\varphi\!:V\to Y
a bounded linear map, and
z\in V
. If
a\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z)
, then there is some
r>0
, depending on
a
, such
that for all
x\in V
satisfying
\|x\|<r
, we have
(1+x)^{-1}a(1+x)\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z)
. In particular, the following hold:

(i)

For any
x\in V
, let
e^{x}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{k}}{k!}
. For all
a\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z)
, there is some
r^{\prime}>0
such that if
\|x\|<r^{\prime}
, then
e^{-x}ae^{x}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z)
.

(ii)

For any
x\in V
such that
e^{\varepsilon x}=f(\varepsilon)+g(\varepsilon)x
where
f
is even and
g
is odd, for example when

(a)

x^{2}=0
, or

(b)

x^{2}=1
,

and for all
a\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z)
, we have
xax\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z)
and
[x,a]\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z)
.

Proof.
Let
a\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z)
. Then, for some
r_{0}>0
we have that
a\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z^{\prime})
for all
\|z-z^{\prime}\|<r_{0}
by Theorem
3.1
. In particular,
if we pick
r>0
so that whenever
x\in V
satisfies
\|x\|<r
it is small enough that
1+x
is invertible and
\|z-z(1+x)^{-1}\|<r_{0}
, then

a\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}(z(1+x)^{-1})=(1+x)\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}(z)(1+x)^{-1},

by Corollary
1.3
, so that
(1+x)^{-1}a(1+x)\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}(z)
.

To prove (i), note that if
\|x\|
is chosen to be small enough (less than some threshold
r^{\prime}>0
), then

\left\|x+\frac{x^{2}}{2!}+\frac{x^{3}}{3!}+\cdots\right\|<r,

so that we may employ the first part to see that
e^{-x}ae^{x}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z)
.

Let
\varepsilon
be such that
\|\varepsilon x\|<r^{\prime}
. To prove (ii), note that if
e^{\varepsilon x}=f(\varepsilon)+g(\varepsilon)x
with
f
even and
g
odd, then
e^{-\varepsilon x}=f(\varepsilon)-g(\varepsilon)x
, so

\displaystyle e^{-\varepsilon x}ae^{\varepsilon x}

\displaystyle=(f(\varepsilon)-g(\varepsilon)x)a(f(\varepsilon)+g(\varepsilon)x)

\displaystyle=f(\varepsilon)^{2}a+f(\varepsilon)g(\varepsilon)ax-f(\varepsilon)g(\varepsilon)xa-g(\varepsilon)^{2}xax

\displaystyle=f(\varepsilon)^{2}a+f(\varepsilon)g(\varepsilon)[a,x]-g(\varepsilon)^{2}xax\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z)

which, since
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z)
is a vector space, implies that

e^{-\varepsilon x}ae^{\varepsilon x}-f(\varepsilon)^{2}a=f(\varepsilon)g(\varepsilon)ax-f(\varepsilon)g(\varepsilon)xa-g(\varepsilon)^{2}xax\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z).

Swapping
x
for
-x
and noting that
[a,x]=-[x,a]
, we see that, in total,

\displaystyle f(\varepsilon)g(\varepsilon)[a,x]-g(\varepsilon)^{2}xax

\displaystyle\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z),\text{ and}

\displaystyle-f(\varepsilon)g(\varepsilon)[a,x]-g(\varepsilon)^{2}xax

\displaystyle\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z).

Once again using that
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z)
is a vector space, we may add and subtract to see that

-2g(\varepsilon)^{2}xax\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z),\quad\text{and}\quad 2f(\varepsilon)g(\varepsilon)[a,x]\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z).

Scaling then proves the result.

We need to show that the provided examples for
x
in (ii) satisfy the property we described. This is a reasonably easy computation obtained from just expanding
out the definition of
e^{\varepsilon x}
.

(a)

If
x^{2}=0
, then

e^{\varepsilon x}=1+\varepsilon x+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}x^{2}}{2!}+\frac{\varepsilon^{3}x^{3}}{3!}+\cdots=1+\varepsilon x.

(b)

If
x^{2}=1
, then

e^{\varepsilon x}=1+\varepsilon x+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2!}+\frac{\varepsilon^{3}x}{3!}+\cdots=\cosh(\varepsilon)+\sinh(\varepsilon)x.

Thus, we are done.

\blacksquare

3.2
Classification for Existence With a Modifier in Finite Dimensions

We begin with a construction which will allow us to show there are only two possible ways
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(Z)
can look for
Z\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
.

Definition 3.3.
Let
X
be a Hilbert space, and
x,y\in X
. Define the operator

E_{xy}\!:X\to\operatorname{Span}\{x\}\subseteq X,\quad E_{xy}u:=(y,u)x.

In other words,

E_{xy}y:=x,\quad\forall z\in\operatorname{Span}\{y\}^{\perp},\,Ez:=0.

Proposition 3.4.

Let
X
be a Hilbert space, and
x,y,z\in X
have unit norm.

(i)

We have
E_{xy}E_{yz}=E_{xz}
. In particular,
E_{xx}^{2}=E_{xx}
.

(ii)

We have
(x,z)=0
if and only if
E_{yz}E_{xy}=0
. In particular, we have
E_{xy}^{2}=0
when
(x,y)=0
.

(iii)

For any operator
A\in\mathsf{B}(X)
, there is some
a_{yx}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

E_{xy}AE_{xy}=a_{yx}E_{xy}.

(iv)

We have
a_{yx}=0
if and only if
(Ax,y)=0
. Furthermore,

(a)

a_{yx}=0
for all
x,y\in X
, if and only if
A=0
, and

(b)

a_{yx}=0
for all perpendicular
x,y
, if and only if
A=\lambda I
.

Proof.
To prove (i), write
u=\mu z+z^{\prime}
,
z^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Span}\{z\}^{\perp}
, and compute

E_{xy}E_{yz}u=E_{xy}E_{yz}(\mu z+z^{\prime})=\mu E_{xy}y=\mu x=E_{xz}u.

To prove (ii), assume
(x,z)=0
, and write
u=\mu y+y^{\prime}
,
y^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Span}\{y\}^{\perp}
, and note that

E_{yz}E_{xy}u=E_{yz}E_{xy}(\mu y+y^{\prime})=\mu E_{yz}x=0

since
x\in\operatorname{Span}\{z\}^{\perp}
. The other direction is similar.

For (iii), write
u=\mu y+y^{\prime}
and
Ax=a_{yx}y+y^{\prime\prime}
, where
y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime}\in\operatorname{Span}\{y\}^{\perp}
, and note that

E_{xy}AE_{xy}u=\mu E_{xy}Ax=\mu a_{yx}x=a_{yx}E_{xy}u.

Finally, for (iv), note that
a_{yx}=0
if and only if
Ax\in\operatorname{Span}\{y\}^{\perp}
by the above. If this holds for all
x,y
, then
(Ax,y)=0
for all
y
, so
Ax=0
, proving (iv)(a).
For (iv)(b), note that we are given that

(x,y)=0\implies(Ax,y)=0.

Thus, writing
Ax=\lambda x+x^{\prime}
, we see that
(x^{\prime},y)=0
for all
y\in\operatorname{Span}\{x\}^{\perp}
, so
(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})=0
, meaning
x^{\prime}=0
. We conclude that
Ax=\lambda x
, where
\lambda
now depends on
x
.
However, by linearity, it must be independent of
x
, so that
A=\lambda I
.

\blacksquare

Lemma 3.5.

Let
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Then

I=\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_{e_{i}e_{i}}.

Proof.
Note that for all
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we have
E_{e_{i}e_{i}}v=(e_{i},v)e_{i}
. In particular,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_{e_{i}e_{i}}v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(e_{i},v)e_{i}=v

as desired.

\blacksquare

Here is the central trick we will use to deduce our result. It tells us that any subspace of
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
satisfying a meagre amount of closure properties must contain all matrices.

Lemma 3.6.

Let
V\subseteq\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
,
n\geq 2
, be a linear subspace with the following properties:

(1)

I\in V
.

(2)

For some
x,y\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
of unit length such that
(x,y)=0
, we have
E_{xy}\in V
and
E_{yx}\in V
.

(3)

If
A\in V
, then for all
v,w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
(v,w)=0
, we have
[A,E_{vw}]\in S
.

Then
V=\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
.

Proof.
By Proposition
3.2
, we have

E_{vw}E_{wu}=E_{vu},\quad\text{and if }(u,v)=0,\quad E_{wu}E_{vw}=0.

Extend the vectors
\{x,y\}
from (2) to a basis
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
, where
x=e_{1}
and
y=e_{2}
. For simplicity, we will write
E_{ij}:=E_{e_{i}e_{j}}
. Using property (2) along with property (3) twice,
as long as
k\not=\ell
and at least
k\not=2
or
\ell\not=1
, we have

\displaystyle[[E_{12},E_{2\ell}],-E_{k1}]

\displaystyle=[E_{12}E_{2{\ell}}-E_{2{\ell}}E_{12},-E_{k1}]

\displaystyle=[E_{k1},E_{1{\ell}}-E_{2{\ell}}E_{12}]

\displaystyle=E_{k1}E_{1{\ell}}-E_{k1}E_{2{\ell}}E_{12}-E_{1{\ell}}E_{k1}+E_{2{\ell}}E_{12}E_{k1}

\displaystyle=E_{k{\ell}}+E_{2{\ell}}E_{12}E_{k1}=E_{k\ell}\in V.

Since
E_{21}\in V
by assumption, we have that
E_{ij}\in V
for all
i\not=j
.

This leaves the “diagonals”
E_{ii}
. To get
E_{ii}\in V
, we use that, by the above, we have
E_{ij}\in V
for all
i\not=j
. Therefore, we see, for
i\not=k
, that
E_{ki}\in V
.
Applying (3), we have

[E_{ik},E_{ki}]=E_{ik}E_{ki}-E_{ki}E_{ik}=E_{ii}-E_{kk}\in V.

Therefore, fixing
i
and summing up over all
k\not i
we have

(n-1)E_{ii}-\sum_{k\not=i}E_{kk}=(n-1)E_{ii}-(I-E_{kk})=nE_{ii}+I\in V

by Lemma
3.2
, which, using (1), means that
E_{kk}\in V
.

\blacksquare

Theorem 3.7.

Let
Z\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
. Then
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(Z)=\{\lambda I\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\}
or
\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
.

Proof.
Suppose
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(Z)\not=\{\lambda I\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\}
. We use Corollary
3.1
to show that
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(Z)
satisfies the conditions of Lemma
3.2
.
Condition (1) is clear, and condition (3) follows by part (ii)(a) of Corollary
3.1
. It remains to show that
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(Z)
satisfies property (2). For this,
pick
A\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(Z)
such that
A\not=\lambda I
, and note that for all
(x_{0},y_{0})=0
we have

E_{x_{0}y_{0}}AE_{x_{0}y_{0}}=a_{y_{0}x_{0}}E_{x_{0}y_{0}}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(Z).

Since
A\not=\lambda I
, we know by (iv)(b) that there is some choice of
(x,y)=0
such that
a_{yx}\not=0
, so that
E_{xy}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}
. Finally, let
T_{xy}
be the
operator given by

T_{xy}x:=y,\quad T_{xy}y:=x,\quad\forall z\in\operatorname{Span}\{x,y\}^{\perp},\,Tz=z.

Then
T_{xy}^{2}=I
, so

T_{xy}E_{xy}T_{xy}=E_{yx}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(Z)

by Corollary
3.1
.

\blacksquare

3.3
Existence Without a Modifier & With Some Modifiers

Now, Theorem
3.2
tells us that in finite dimensions, for general
\varphi
, the space
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(Z)
has two possible forms. We now show that in a very general setting,
we can say exactly what
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}(Z)
is. Using the same exact trick as in Section
2.6
, via the
\preceq
relation of Section
2.5
, we will also be able to
extend this to a few more choices of
\varphi
.

Lemma 3.8.

Let
X
be a Hilbert space, and let
V\subseteq X
be a (not necessarily closed) linear subspace such that

(1)

there are some orthonormal
e,e^{\prime}\in X
such that
E_{ee^{\prime}}\in V
and
E_{e^{\prime}e}\in V
,

(2)

if
A\in V
, then for all orthonormal
v,w\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we have
[A,E_{vw}]\in S
, and

(3)

if
A\in V
, then for all operators
T\in\mathsf{B}(X)
such that
T^{2}=I
, we have
TAT\in V
.

Then, for any non-trivial linear subspace
Y\subseteq X
, there is some
A\in V
such that
AY\not\subseteq Y
.

Proof.
By Zorn’s lemma, we may extend
\{e,e^{\prime}\}
to an orthonormal basis
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
X
, where
I
may be uncountable. Write
E_{ij}:=E_{e_{i}e_{j}}
. Following the argument of
Lemma
3.2
, (1) and (2) imply that
E_{ij}\in V
for all
i\not=j\in I
. To get
E_{ii}\in V
for all
i\in I
, define an operator
T_{kj}
for all
k\not=j\in I

by

T_{jk}e_{j}=e_{k}-e_{j},\quad T_{jk}(e_{k}-e_{j})=e_{j},\quad\forall z\in\operatorname{Span}\{e_{j},e_{k}-e_{j}\}^{\perp},\,T_{jk}z=z.

Then
T_{kj}^{2}=I
, and we note that for
j\not=i

T_{ji}E_{ij}T_{ji}(e_{i}-e_{j})=T_{ji}E_{ij}e_{j}=T_{ji}e_{i}=T_{ij}(e_{i}-e_{j}+e_{j})=e_{j}+e_{i}-e_{j}=e_{i},

and more generally,

T_{ji}E_{ij}T_{ji}=E_{e_{i}(e_{j}-e_{i})}

which is in
V
by (3). However, we also have

E_{e_{i}(e_{j}-e_{i})}=E_{ij}-E_{ii}\implies E_{ii}=E_{ij}-E_{e_{i}(e_{j}-e_{i})}\in V

so that
E_{ii}\in V
.

Now, for any
y\in X
, there is some countable subset
I_{y}\subseteq I
for which we may write

y=\sum_{i\in I_{y}}(e_{i},y)e_{i}.

Since
Y
is non-trivial, we can find
y\in Y\backslash\{0\}
and some basis vector
e_{k}\not\in Y
. We then have

E_{e_{k}y}=\sum_{i\in I_{y}}(e_{i},y)E_{ki}.

Choosing a bijection
|\cdot|\!:I_{x}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathbb{N}
, for any
K>0
we define

E_{e_{k}y}^{(K)}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I_{x}\\
|i|<K\end{subarray}}(e_{i},y)E_{ki}

and note that for all
K>0
, we have
E_{e_{k}y}^{(K)}\in V
by the first half of the proof. Finally, since

E_{e_{k}y}^{(K)}\to E_{e_{k}y}\quad\text{as }K\to\infty,

we may find some
N>0
such that

E_{e_{k}y}^{(N)}y\not=0,

which, setting
A:=E^{(N)}_{e_{k}y}
, means we have some
A\in V
such that
Ay=\lambda e_{k}
for some
\lambda\not=0
, and in particular,
AY\not\subseteq Y
since
e_{k}\not\in Y
.

\blacksquare

Theorem 3.9.

Let
X
be a Hilbert space, and let
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
be a singular operator. Then

\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}(Z)=\{\lambda I\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\}.

Proof.
First consider the case when
Z
is non-zero. We will argue by contradiction: suppose the assertion were
not
true. By Theorem
1.3
, we have that

\{\lambda I\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}(Z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)

and since
Z\not=0
and is singular, we know that
\ker(Z)
is a non-trivial subspace of
X
. Therefore, we may try to employ Lemma
3.3
.
However, conditions (2) and (3) follow trivially from Corollary
3.1
, and condition (1) follows by the same argument as in
Theorem
3.2
.

When
Z=0
, note that for any
x\in X
, we may consider the orthogonal projection
P_{x}
onto
\operatorname{Span}\{x\}^{\perp}
, which has
\ker(P_{x})=\operatorname{Span}\{x\}
. For
any
A\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}(Z)
, we will then have that
A\in\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}(\varepsilon P_{x})\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(\varepsilon P_{x})=\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(P_{x})
for some sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
by Theorem
3.1
,
and in particular,
Ax=\lambda_{x}x
for all
x
. A simple argument by linearity then shows that
A=\lambda I
.

\blacksquare

From the above, we now know exactly how
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}(-)
looks in Hilbert spaces. In finite dimensions, therefore, we can then apply the general Corollary
2.5

to deduce
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(-)
looks for some simple but interesting choices of
\varphi
.

Theorem 3.10.

Let
\mathbb{J}
be as given in Theorem
2.6
, and let
Z\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
. If
\varphi\in\mathbb{J}
, then

\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}(Z).

In particular, suppose
Z
is singular. Then
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(Z)=\{\lambda I\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\}
.

Proof.
Combine Theorem
3.3
with Corollary
2.5
and Lemma
2.6
.

\blacksquare

Remark 3.11.
Like with
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(-)
and
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{*}(-)
, one may define a dual version,
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{*}(-)
, of
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}(-)
. This has the peculiar property that it is very

&

4
A Few Conjectures & A Question

We end this paper with a few conjectures on the objects we have considered. The first is that Theorem
3.2
can be extended to all Hilbert spaces.

Conjecture 4.1.
Let
X
be a Hilbert space,
Y
a Banach space, and let
\varphi\!:\mathsf{B}(X)\to Y
be a bounded linear map. Then, for all singular
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
, either
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(Z)=\{\lambda I\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\}

or
\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}(Z)=\mathsf{B}(X)
.

For our second conjecture, we would like to say that the families
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(-)
and
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(-)
are tightly related to each other. Since we have a necessary and sufficient condition
for all
\varphi
that satisfy
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(-)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(-)
(given by Theorem
2.7
), ideally we would like those same criteria
to determine the situation for
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(-)
.

Conjecture 4.2.
Let
X
be a Hilbert space,
Y
a Banach space, and let
\varphi\!:\mathsf{B}(X)\to Y
be a bounded linear map. Then the following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

For all
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
of generalized index zero,
\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}(Z)
.

(2)

For all
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
of generalized index zero,
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}^{\varphi}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)
.

Our third conjecture is harder to motivate. Essentially, in the process of proving the results of Section
3
, several approaches came up. One
of them involved doing a computation of the kernel of a particular map, which conjures forth ideas that results in Section
3
should be
approachable with the concepts developed in Section
2
.

Conjecture 4.3.
Let
V
be a Banach algebra,
Y
a Banach space, and
\varphi\!:V\to Y
a bounded linear map. Then

\forall z\in V,\quad\mathfrak{S}_{\cap}^{\varphi}(z)=\bigcup\left\{\bigcap_{z^{\prime}\in U}\mathfrak{S}^{\varphi}_{\cup}(z^{\prime}):U\subseteq V\text{ open and bounded, }z\in U\right\}.

In Section
2.1
, we crucially make use of the condition that our operators are generalized index zero in order to prove our results (in particular, to write
our base space as a direct sum so we can produce good paths and do computations).

Question 4.4.
Does Corollary
2.3
hold even for operators
Z
that are not of generalized index zero? That is, is it true that
as long as
Z\in\mathsf{B}(X)
can be approximated by invertible operators, then
\mathfrak{S}_{\cup}(Z)=\mathfrak{S}_{\ker}(Z)
?

Appendix A
Variant of the Proof of the Lemma
2.6

We provide here a variant of the proof of Lemma
2.6
, which chronologically came first and is due to the first author.

Lemma A.1.

Let
Z,A\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
, let
U_{k}\to Z
be a sequence of invertible matrices converging to
Z
, and let
J=\mathbb{1}-I
. Then

\lim_{k\to\infty}\|J*(U_{k}AU_{k}^{-1})\|<\infty\iff\lim_{k\to\infty}\|U_{k}AU_{k}^{-1}\|<\infty.

Proof.
The
(\Leftarrow)
direction is clear. To prove the
(\Rightarrow)
direction, set
B_{k}:=U_{k}AU_{k}^{-1}
. Then, by assumption,
we have that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|J*B_{k}\|<\infty
, so that the non-diagonal entries of
B_{k}
remain bounded as
k\to\infty
.
Now we have the following facts:

(i)

Since each
B_{k}
is a conjugate of
A
, they all have the same eigenvalues as
A
.

(ii)

By the Gershgorin circle theorem, the eigenvalues of
A
lie within some bounded disks centered on the diagonal entries of
A
.

(iii)

On the other hand, also by the Gershgorin circle theorem, the eigenvalues of
B_{k}
must lie within some
bounded radius, which is independent of

k
,
of the diagonal entries of
B_{k}
because, by assumption, the non-diagonal entries stay bounded as
k\to\infty
. That is,
the supremum of the radii of the Gershgorin circles of the
B_{k}
is some finite number.

If some diagonal entry of
B_{k}
exploded as
k\to\infty
, this would imply that one of the eigenvalues of
B_{k}
, i.e. one of the eigenvalues of
A
,
lies outside the Gershgorin circles of
A
. This is impossible, hence the diagonal entries must necessarily also stay bounded, and therefore

\|B_{k}\|=\|U_{k}AU_{k}^{-1}\|
stays bounded as
k\to\infty
.

\blacksquare
