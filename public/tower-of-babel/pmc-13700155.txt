Long-term donor and graft safety despite subclinical mitochondrial abnormalities: mechanistic insights from a 22-year follow-up of a carrier-to-MELAS kidney transplant
Authors: Shizuka Kobayashi, Tomokazu Shimizu, Shun Manabe, Momoko Seki, Yusuke Ushio, Rina Takahashi, Shiho Makabe, Naoko Ito, Kohei Unagami, Hiroshi Kataoka, Sekiko Taneda, Toshihiro Hirai, Kazuho Honda, Kosaku Nitta, Toshio Takagi, Hideki Ishida, Junichi Hoshino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIdeally, deceased donor kidney transplantation is preferred for patients with mitochondrial nephropathy caused by the m.3243 A > G mutation to avoid genetic risks. However, in regions where deceased donation is limited and living donation predominates, such as Japan, family members are often the only available donors. The safety of using maternal relatives, who are obligate carriers of the mutation, remains highly controversial due to the risk of unmasking renal failure in both the donor and the allograft.Case presentationWe report a 22-year follow-up of an inadvertent living kidney transplant from a 55-year-old mother to her 26-year-old daughter. The recipient presented with MELAS (Mitochondrial Myopathy, Encephalopathy, Lactic Acidosis, and Stroke-like episodes) and end-stage kidney disease due to focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), with the m.3243 A > G mutation confirmed prior to transplantation. Conversely, the donor mother was phenotypically normal regarding renal function without diabetes, and conventional genetic testing at the time failed to detect the mutation. Consequently, the recipient’s mutation was presumed to be de novo, allowing the donation to proceed. Notably, the donor’s zero-hour allograft biopsy revealed ultrastructural mitochondrial abnormalities but no overt light microscopic damage (i.e., no FSGS). At 22 years post-transplant, both the recipient’s graft function (serum creatinine 1.16 mg/dL, albuminuria 5.0 mg/gCr) and the donor’s remaining kidney function (serum creatinine 0.98 mg/dL, albuminuria 10.4 mg/gCr) remain excellent. Current highly sensitive genetic analysis revealed an extremely low blood heteroplasmy in the donor (0.1%) compared to 20% in the recipient, demonstrating a severe “genetic bottleneck” effect. Furthermore, the recipient’s older sister was found to have a 4% mutational load, definitively confirming maternal inheritance rather than a de novo mutation.ConclusionsThis case represents the longest reported follow-up of a living kidney transplant from an m.3243 A > G carrier. It demonstrates that maternal carriers with extremely low heteroplasmy and no light microscopic evidence of overt renal damage on biopsy can be safe donors and provide excellent long-term graft outcomes. Precise risk stratification based on genotype and biopsy findings, rather than uniform exclusion, may expand transplant options.

Introduction

Mitochondrial diseases are systemic disorders caused by mitochondrial dysfunction, affecting high-energy demand organs such as the central nervous system, musculoskeletal system, and heart. The kidney is also a frequently affected organ, and mitochondrial nephropathy can progress to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) [1, 2]. These disorders arise from pathogenic variants in either nuclear genes (encoding mitochondrial-related proteins) or mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). Among adult cases, mitochondrial nephropathy is most frequently associated with the m.3243 A > G variant in MT-TL1 [2]. Because mtDNA variants are maternally inherited via the oocyte, there is a high probability that the patient’s mother and siblings harbor the same variant. This presents a complex challenge in living kidney transplantation [3].

While patient survival after transplantation is generally favorable for recipients with mitochondrial diseases [1], the safety of using living donors from maternal lineages or siblings remains controversial. The KDIGO Clinical Practice Guideline states that candidates with genetic kidney diseases capable of progressing to kidney failure, including pathogenic mtDNA mutations, should not donate [3]. However, in regions like Japan where deceased donation is limited, maternal relatives and siblings are often the only available candidates [4]. The challenge lies in the nature of mtDNA inheritance: heteroplasmy (the ratio of mutant to wild-type mtDNA) varies across tissues and generations due to the “genetic bottleneck” effect, making risk prediction difficult [5].

Here, we present a case report of a living kidney transplant from a mother to her daughter with MELAS (Mitochondrial Myopathy, Encephalopathy, Lactic Acidosis, and Stroke-like episodes) caused by the m.3243 A > G variant. Due to diagnostic limitations at the time (2003), the donor’s carrier status was unknown, leading to an inadvertent transplantation. Notably, despite the donor’s zero-hour allograft biopsy revealing ultrastructural morphological changes suggestive of a pathogenic variant [1, 2, 6], it showed no light microscopic evidence of overt renal damage, both the donor and recipient have maintained excellent renal function over a 22-year follow-up. A genetic re-evaluation in 2025 confirmed that the donor indeed carried the m.3243 A > G variant, albeit at a low heteroplasmy level. This case offers valuable insights into the long-term prognosis of donors with low heteroplasmy and no light microscopic evidence of overt renal damage, suggesting criteria for safe donation in this complex genetic context.

Case description

Recipient history

A Japanese woman presented with proteinuria at age 15. At age 22, a kidney biopsy revealed focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) with global sclerosis in 15 of 33 glomeruli and severe interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy (IFTA) (Fig. 1A). Electron microscopy (EM) showed abnormal mitochondrial proliferation in podocytes (Fig. 1B) and tubular epithelial cells (Fig. 1C). She exhibited short stature (140 cm) and low body weight (36.6 kg). She subsequently developed sensorineural hearing loss, diabetes mellitus, and chronic headaches with loss of consciousness. Genetic testing confirmed the m.3243 A > G mutation (MELAS). She initiated hemodialysis at age 24.

The “inadvertent” transplantation and zero-hour biopsy

At age 26, she underwent ABO-compatible living kidney transplantation from her 55-year-old mother. The mother had a history of gestational diabetes but no overt neurological or renal symptoms (serum creatinine 0.65 mg/dL, eGFR 72.7 mL/min/1.73 m², normoalbuminuria). Although details are unavailable, the mother reportedly tested negative for the mutation at that time, and was cleared for donation. The transplantation was successful without rejection under standard immunosuppression. However, the donor’s zero-hour allograft biopsy revealed a critical finding: while light microscopy showed normal glomeruli with minimal sclerosis, EM demonstrated scattered mitochondria with morphological abnormalities in tubular epithelial cells (Fig. 1D, E). Additionally, rare cells suspicious for granular swollen epithelial cells (GSECs), a pathological hallmark of mitochondrial nephropathy [1, 2, 6], were observed (Fig. 1F). These findings suggested the graft carried the m.3243 A > G mutation.

Long-term outcome (22 Years)

Despite the baseline mitochondrial abnormalities, the clinical course of the grafted kidney has been excellent. At the 22-year follow-up (age 50), the recipient’s serum creatinine is 1.16 mg/dL (eGFR 39.9 mL/min/1.73 m²) with normoalbuminuria (5.0 mg/gCr). Regarding her systemic condition, however, she required insulin for diabetes post-transplantation and received a cochlear implant at age 36. Cardiomyopathy is managed with medication. The donor (now 78) has maintained stable renal function (serum creatinine 0.98 mg/dL, eGFR 42.0 mL/min/1.73 m², albuminuria 10.4 mg/gCr), appropriate for a uninephric elderly individual [7], despite diabetes treated by oral hypoglycemic agents.

Genetic re-evaluation

To investigate the discrepancy between the initial “de novo” assumption and the histological findings, we performed genetic analysis using the Invader assay (detection limit 0.1%) [8]. The recipient harbored 20% heteroplasmy in blood, whereas the donor carried an extremely low level of 0.1%. The recipient’s older sister (diabetes, mild hearing loss, serum creatinine 0.72 mg/dL, eGFR 65.7 mL/min/1.73 m², microalbuminuria 38.0 mg/gCr) had 4%. These findings confirmed maternal inheritance and the “mitochondrial genetic bottleneck” effect [5].

Discussion

The central insight from this 22-year follow-up is that a “safe window” for living kidney donation may exist for m.3243 A > G carriers, provided the mutational burden is below the threshold required to withstand transplantation stresses. These include compensatory hyperfiltration in the donor, and recipient-specific factors such as hemodynamic stress, rejection, corticosteroid-induced metabolic abnormalities, and calcineurin inhibitor (CNI) toxicity [9].

The “threshold effect” is a hallmark of mitochondrial disease [10, 11]. While data on the renal threshold are limited, Kobayashi et al. demonstrated that granular swollen epithelial cells (GSECs) harbored high heteroplasmy loads (63.4%), whereas phenotypically “normal-appearing” tubules in the same tissue contained significantly lower loads (32.5%) [6]. This implies the renal threshold for overt histological damage lies around 30–60%. However, these data are derived from native kidneys; the threshold in a solitary kidney or graft under transplant-related stress remains unknown.

As summarized in Table 1, outcomes of living kidney transplantation in families with mitochondrial mutations vary [12–14]. While recipient outcomes have been followed for up to 10 years, reports on long-term donor outcomes are remarkably scarce, with only one case documenting the new onset of proteinuria. Successful long-term outcomes, such as the mother-to-daughter transplant reported by Roper et al. and our case, appear associated with low donor blood heteroplasmy and the absence of pre-existing donor phenotypes [12]. Although our case involves a longer 22-year follow-up and a different mtDNA mutation, both our donor and the donor in Roper et al.‘s report shared a notably low heteroplasmy rate (0.1% and 4%, respectively). In contrast, cases reported by de Laat et al. and Nishida et al. resulted in graft deterioration or donor proteinuria [13, 14]. Notably, in the case reported by Nishida et al., the graft developed recurrent FSGS within 1 year and was lost by 5 years. Furthermore, the donor developed proteinuria at 4 years post-donation, raising serious concerns about long-term donor prognosis. These reports suggest that meeting KDIGO clinical criteria alone is insufficient; safety likely depends on confirming a low donor heteroplasmy rate and the absence of overt histological damage.

In our case, the donor’s blood heteroplasmy was notably low (0.1%) at the time of re-evaluation (age 78). However, this does not necessarily imply that a rate of < 1% is a strict requirement for safety. Unlike post-mitotic tissues, heteroplasmy levels in dividing leukocytes decrease over time [15, 16]. Grady et al. reported that blood heteroplasmy declines by approximately 2.3% per year and demonstrated that age-corrected blood levels correlate with those in post-mitotic tissues like smooth muscle [16]. Applying this correction to our family suggests that the recipient likely possessed ~ 80% and the sister ~ 20% at younger ages, which aligns well with their respective phenotypes (MELAS vs. diabetes/deafness). Although specific data for 80-year-olds are lacking, it is reasonable to infer that our donor’s heteroplasmy was higher at the time of donation (age 55) and is higher in her kidney than in her blood.

The zero-hour biopsy findings are pivotal for defining this “safe window.” Mitochondrial nephropathy typically manifests as proteinuria and renal dysfunction, characterized pathologically by FSGS [2, 6, 17]. In our case, while the recipient’s native kidney showed definitive FSGS (“damage”), the donor’s zero-hour biopsy exhibited only ultrastructural morphological changes, GSECs by light microscopy and abnormal mitochondria by EM, without overt histological damage. This distinction between “changes” and “damage” is crucial. As suggested by the dissociation in heteroplasmy loads reported by Kobayashi et al. [6], our donor’s renal tissue load was likely sufficient to cause ultrastructural changes but remained below the threshold required to trigger podocyte failure [17]. This suggests that kidneys with subclinical mutation loads, manifesting as ultrastructural “changes” rather than overt histological “damage”, can tolerate the metabolic stress of ischemia-reperfusion, compensatory hyperfiltration, and exposure to potential mitochondrial toxins like calcineurin inhibitors without accelerating injury.

A fundamental question remains: If a donor meets KDIGO guidelines, is genetic/pathological assessment necessary? Reports of early graft failure warn that “phenotypic normality” at donation does not guarantee safety [13, 14]. The metabolic stress of compensatory hypertrophy may exceed the bioenergetic capacity of cells already harboring mutations [18]. We propose that safety is best predicted by the combination of preserved phenotype (normal function/histology) and a low heteroplasmy rate. Precise risk stratification, rather than uniform exclusion, may expand transplant options in regions where living donation is essential.

Although deceased donor transplantation remains preferable whenever available, our findings provide supportive evidence that carefully selected maternal carriers with extremely low heteroplasmy may represent acceptable living donor candidates in settings where living donation is often the only feasible option [4].

This report has several important strengths, including the exceptionally long 22-year follow-up of both the donor and recipient, together with pathological reassessment and highly sensitive genetic re-evaluation. These comprehensive data provide unique mechanistic insights into the relationship between heteroplasmy, pathological findings, and long-term transplant outcomes. However, several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, this is a single case and therefore cannot establish definitive donor selection criteria. Second, heteroplasmy was measured in peripheral blood rather than kidney tissue at long-term follow-up. Third, the donor’s heteroplasmy at the time of transplantation could only be inferred because contemporary samples were unavailable. Therefore, our findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger cohorts.

Conclusion

This case represents the longest reported follow-up of living kidney transplantation from an m.3243 A > G carrier. It suggests that carefully selected maternal carriers with very low heteroplasmy and no overt renal pathology may be suitable living kidney donors, although further studies are needed to validate these findings.
