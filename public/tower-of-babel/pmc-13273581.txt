Synergy of non-thermal plasma and ozonation for enhanced real industrial wastewater treatment
Authors: Fatemeh Amani, Fatemeh Mohammadi, Narges Ziveh, Mahdiyeh Bakhtiyari-Ramezani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryRapid population growth and industrialization necessitate more effective ways of eliminating refractory pollutants in industrial wastewater. This study examined the synergistic effects of ozone and non-thermal plasma, produced by dielectric barrier discharge (DBD), on Kaveh industrial wastewater. After aeration and pre-filtration, the optimal ozone treatment was established at 10 min. Wastewater was pre-treated, ozonated, and then exposed to DBD plasma for 10 to 50 min to optimize the process and test the synergy of these two techniques. The results showed that the optimal physicochemical reduction in wastewater was achieved with 30 min of DBD treatment. At this optimal time, major wastewater parameters, namely biological oxygen demand (BOD) and chemical oxygen demand (COD), were reduced by 59.30% and 51.41%, respectively. A major contribution to this reduction strategy could be attributed to the formation of highly reactive oxidizing agents, which facilitate the degradation of biodegradable and non-biodegradable compounds. Therefore, the synergistic application of ozone and cold plasma presents a flexible and environmentally friendly alternative for advanced oxidation.Subject areas: Environmental chemical engineering, Water geochemistry, Pollution, Environmental engineering

Introduction

As the demand for water across the globe increases due to the rapid industrial growth and the increased population, the challenge of treating industrial wastewater, one of the worst polluters, has also been compounded.1

Wastewater discharged by the chemical and pharmaceutical industries, as well as textile industries, contains a variety of contaminants, including heavy metals, organic compounds, and persistent pollutants.2,3,4,5 The discharge of untreated or inadequately treated industrial wastewater into aquatic ecosystems poses a significant threat to environmental and human health.6

The standard methods of wastewater treatment (sedimentation, flocculation, and biological treatment) are not always successful in removing persistent pollutants because of their stable chemical structure and biological inertness.7,8,9 Furthermore, conventional treatment methods generate a large amount of sludge, necessitating further treatment and disposal, increasing financial and environmental costs.10 Therefore, it is essential to create and use modern treatment techniques when discussing the shortcomings of conventional methods.7,11

Beyond conventional methods, the advanced oxidation processes (AOPs), such as photocatalytic oxidation, Fenton processes, electrochemical oxidation, and persulfate-based AOPs, have emerged as attractive options for the remediation of wastewater, which provide the potential for overcoming the limitations of traditional wastewater treatments.11,12,13 Among these, non-thermal plasma (NTP) technology is a promising AOP that has received increased attention for wastewater treatment applications.2,14,15

Dielectric barrier discharge (DBD)-based NTP stands out for its operational flexibility, diverse range of reactive species, multi-pollutant removal capability, low energy consumption,7,10 and compatibility with hybrid systems,16 making it a sustainable and scalable solution for modern wastewater challenges.

DBD, which can generate a complex mix of charged particles, UV radiation, and reactive oxygen species (ROS), is one of the many techniques for producing NTP.7,10 Some research has revealed the effectiveness of NTP to eliminate several kinds of pollutants from wastewater. For example, Iervolino et al.17 demonstrated the ability of the DBD reactor to fully degrade and mineralize methylene blue, phenol, paracetamol, caffeine, and ceftriaxone. Back et al.14 further studied the application of NTP for the elimination of pharmaceuticals from wastewater treatment plant effluent, covering its potential for micropollutant removal. Additionally, Ghaisani et al.18 and Fernandes et al.19 summarized the potential uses of NTP in addressing textile wastewater contamination and related challenges, and presented some case studies of its high efficiency, with over 85% pollutant removal occurring in a comparatively short period.

NTP technology has a lot of promise, but in many cases, combining it with other treatment modalities can increase its effectiveness and ability to target pollution selectively.20 Raji et al.6 studied synergistic effects of NTP with photocatalysis for the treatment of wastewater and showed an increase in the inactivation of bacteria and destruction of poisonous compounds. Similarly, Saoud et al.21 also investigated the synergism between NTP and photocatalysis in the post-discharge of ozone, further supporting the benefits of combining AOPs. These studies highlight the potential of combining NTP with other oxidation processes to achieve improved wastewater treatment outcomes. However, these integrated systems also have issues. Plasma-photocatalysis must recover catalysts and may be deactivated and produce secondary pollution,21 whereas plasma-UV/O3 systems have a high energy requirement, rely on the turbidity of the water, and external UV lamps must be maintained.

Another well-known AOP is ozonation, which is typically used in water treatment to disinfect and oxidize it.22,23,24

Although ozonation and NTP (produced by DBD) are known to be powerful individual AOPs, the simultaneous application of the two AOPs emerges as a novel and promising area of research. This inclusive combination utilizes the characteristics of each technology. The key innovation of the combined use of ozone and cold plasma for wastewater treatment is the synergistic effect. This synergy enhances pollutant degradation rates, creates more potent oxidative species, and improves the removal of recalcitrant compounds compared to using either technology alone. Ultimately, this combined approach provides a more efficient and potentially cost-effective way to meet strict wastewater standards and facilitate water reuse. This research aims to provide essential insights into the optimization and application of ozone/DBD plasma technology for industrial wastewater treatment, thereby bridging the gap between fundamental hybrid AOP research and field application. The development of more efficient and sustainable treatment strategies is crucial for mitigating water pollution, protecting environmental and human health, and ensuring the availability of clean water resources for future generations.

Results and discussion

Optimizing ozone exposure time post-aeration/filtration

At the beginning of the wastewater treatment process, large pollutants are eliminated to protect both downstream operations and environmental elements. In some contexts, primary aeration and filtration play crucial roles in the initial stage.

Aeration treatment followed by filtration and ozonation results in changes to multiple physicochemical measurements (Figure 1). Microbial aeration utilizes aerobic microorganisms to degrade organic pollutants. Microbial aeration is an essential process in effluent treatment that provides oxygen to microorganisms, enabling them to efficiently break down pollutants (reducing biological oxygen demand [BOD] and chemical oxygen demand [COD]), promote the settling of solids (reducing total suspended solids [TSS]), remove nutrients, and control odors.25,26,27

The primary function of triple filtration is to use coarse silica sand to physically trap larger particles and sediments.28 The filtration process significantly improves when mixed silica sand of coarse and fine sizes operates together because different-sized pores trap large and small particles, which enhances COD removal and phosphate elimination.29

Including activated carbon as the third filtration stage further reduces organic pollutants and enhances the removal of COD and TSS.28 The highly porous structure of the activated carbon facilitates physical adsorption of organic and suspended contaminants using van der Waals forces and pore entrapment,30 as well as chemical adsorption of heavy metals and ionic pollutants via its surface functional groups. Additionally, it promotes the degradation of complex organic molecules by catalytic oxidation and reduction processes, thereby contributing to overall pollutant removal.31,32

While the biological decomposition of organic compounds in wastewater requires microbial aeration for effective treatment, this system encounters difficulties in removing heavy metals and emerging contaminants due to their complex chemical composition.33 Therefore, comprehensive wastewater treatment requires something beyond triple filtration as a sole method. The combination of filtration with ozonation provides benefits for wastewater treatment despite the individual limitations of each method.34

Following initial purification (aeration and filtration), ozonation revealed that the reduction of turbidity occurred after 5 min of ozone treatment. Notably, extending the ozone treatment beyond this point did not significantly influence turbidity. Ozone treatment for 10 min proved most effective for lowering the concentrations of electrical conductivity (EC), COD, total dissolved solids (TDS), and TSS. Further extension of the ozonation duration beyond this point yielded no further substantial reduction in these parameters. Regarding COD reduction, the most significant physicochemical factor in this wastewater, a 10.41% reduction was achieved after 10 min of ozone treatment compared to the control. Similarly, ozone treatment following aeration and filtration resulted in a reduction of BOD and dissolved organic matter (DOM), with the optimal treatment time identified as 10 min. However, extending ozonation beyond this optimal time led to an increase in these parameters. Overall, these findings demonstrated that post-treatment with ozone following aeration and filtration resulted in enhanced pollutant elimination through an optimal contact period of 10 min.

Ozone breaks down within water systems to produce highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (·OH), which degrade various persistent organic compounds, including trichlorobiphenyl and transformer oil.35 Ozone (O3) degrades complex compounds (e.g., dyes and phenols) by initiating electrophilic attacks on aromatic systems (forming quinones) (Equation 1) and through cycloaddition to C=C double bonds (forming ozonide) (Equation 2) and by radical oxidation (forming ·OH radicals) (Equation 3). Such processes transform harmful substances into CO2 and H2O.36,37 The comprehensive investigations for the application of ozone to dissociate three prevalent fuel oxygenates were performed in aqueous solutions, indicating improved degradation of pollutants in both aqueous solutions and real wastewater samples.38

Ozonation efficiency relies on the ozone dosage and treatment duration combined with operational design choices. The resistance of specific organic compounds to ozonation presents challenges that motivate integrating this process with other AOPs, especially NTP, to overcome challenges.

Spectral analysis of DBD plasma

The diagnostic method of optical emission spectroscopy (OES) detects the light emitted from excited species during cold atmospheric plasmas to establish plasma compositions and essential attributes.39 The spectral analysis between 200 and 980 nm measured the species distribution across all regions, from ultraviolet to near-infrared.

This allows for the identification of various atoms, molecules, and radicals (e.g., OH, O, and N2) based on their unique emission wavelengths due to the relaxation of excited species to their ground state, N2-SPS, N2-FNS, and OH radicals emitting in the ultraviolet range (approximately 200–400 nm), and O and N2-FPS in longer wavelengths (visible and near-infrared).

The OES optical emission spectrum from the generated plasma in the DBD device is shown in Figure 2. NOx species are dominant in DBD plasma. The peak formed at a wavelength of 283 nm is related to nitrogen oxide (NO),40 and the peak formed at 309 nm corresponds to hydroxyl radicals (OH).41 The peaks formed in the range of 337–380 nm are due to the transitions of the second positive system (SPS) of nitrogen between the C3Πu→B3Πu levels, and the relatively minor peaks formed in the range of 391–470 nm are related to the transitions of the first negative system (FNS) of nitrogen between the B2Σu+→X2Σu+ levels. The visible peaks around the 750–800 nm range are related to oxygen species (excited oxygen and singlet oxygen).42 Furthermore, the more apparent peaks formed in the range of 625–715 nm correspond to the transitions of the first positive system (FPS) of nitrogen between the B3Πu→A3Πu levels. The remaining peaks in the spectrum are derived from nitrogen transitions, which are expected considering air as the plasma gas in the device.43 The spectrum was dominated by N2 (SPS and FPS) primarily due to the high concentration of molecular nitrogen in the atmosphere. Increased nitrogen concentration enhances N2-SPS and N2-FPS band intensity due to increased collisional excitation and ionization.44 Furthermore, the plasma environment facilitated the formation of OH radicals through free electron dissociation of water and O3-mediated enhancement of OH in liquid phases.45 O3 is produced via electron-impact dissociation of O2 in DBD, with nitrogen mitigating ozone destruction.46

DOM control by optimized ozone and DBD treatment

DOM refers to the fraction of organic material that is dissolved in the water or is in the form of very small particles. The performance of aeration, filtration, ozone, and DBD was investigated on DOM removal in real samples. As shown in Figure 3, a 41.3% reduction (2.66 mg/L) in DOM was achieved compared to the control group after combined aeration/filtration and 30 min of plasma/10 min of O3 treatment. Hydroxyl radicals (·OH) are among the most powerful oxidizing agents, with a high standard reduction potential. They are primarily generated through the dissociation of water molecules by free electrons in the plasma (Equation 4) or through the decomposition of ozone in water (Equation 5).10,45 Ozone is a strong oxidizer that directly reacts with organic pollutants or decomposes into secondary oxidants like ·OH and H2O2. Its generation is facilitated by plasma-liquid interactions, particularly in DBD configurations.10,47

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is formed through the recombination of ·OH radicals or the reaction of ozone with water (Equations 6 and 7). As a less reactive variant of ROS, H2O2 functions as a reservoir for radical oxygen species that can generate ·OH radicals under certain conditions.10,45

Different reaction routes are available to break down organic pollutants via either direct oxidation pathways or indirect oxidation pathways. Ozone directly reacts with organic pollutants through electrophilic addition or substitution reactions (Equation 1). This process is highly selective and depends on the molecular structure of the pollutant. Under ozone treatment, the aromatic ring undergoes electrophilic attack, followed by ring-opening reactions that yield aldehydes or carboxylic acids.37,48,49

ROS, particularly OH, oxidize organic pollutants through non-selective reactions. For instance, radicals transform into oxidized intermediates by either abstracting hydrogen atoms or adding to double bonds. ·OH radicals add to aromatic rings or unsaturated bonds, forming hydroxylated intermediates.50 This oxidation continues, leading to the breaking of molecular bonds and the production of smaller fragments until complete mineralization into CO2 and H2O.51

The combination of ozone and plasma-generated ROS often leads to synergistic effects, enhancing the degradation efficiency. For example, ozone can react with H2O2 to generate additional ·OH radicals, amplifying the oxidative capacity of the system.45 Overall, the integrated system combines different mechanisms that improve DOM removal performance at reduced operational costs.52,53

When the treatment time was increased to over 30 min, the value was also increased, but significantly less than the control and aeration/filtration method. This diminishing effect of treatment can be ascribed to two simultaneous processes.

First, although ozone and DBD mineralize organic matter to CO2, the long-term exposure may selectively remove large molecules to soluble biodegradable intermediates without fully mineralizing the substance, resulting in the accumulation of organic by-products.54

Second, when there is a microbial biomass in the system (e.g., activated sludge), the plasma treatment ruptures the microbial cells and breaks the extracellular polymeric compounds, which releases intracellular proteins and other organics into the dissolved phase.55 Notably, such intermediates and liberated cellular substances would most probably be easily biodegradable relative to parent compounds.56 Their buildup is temporary and can be effectively mineralized by extending the plasma treatment time or in later degradation processes occurring in the environment.57

Turbidity reduction by optimized ozone and DBD plasma treatment

Turbidity in wastewater refers to the appearance of cloudiness resulting from suspended particles such as silt, organic matter, and microorganisms.58 Elevated turbidity in wastewater interferes with treatment processes and drives up costs while endangering ecosystems and raising health-related problems that need detailed monitoring combined with comprehensive removal techniques.59 Results from this study demonstrated an enhanced pollutant removal efficiency when combined ozone and plasma treatments were applied. Notably, this combined approach significantly outperformed standalone aeration and filtration. The highest turbidity removal rate for the effluent, 90.87%, was achieved when ozone (applied for 10 min) and DBD plasma (applied for 10 min) were used simultaneously after initial aeration and filtration (Figure 4). Further extending the duration of plasma treatment up to 50 min did not significantly impact this parameter.

The application of cold plasma technology presents a promising solution for water treatment because it shows effective performance in water quality enhancement.7 The effect of plasma on turbidity in wastewater involves a dual-phase mechanism, influenced by reactive species and physicochemical changes.

Plasma-generated ozone (O3) and ROS break down large suspended particles into smaller ones.60 Plasma exposure further oxidizes fragmented organic matter (e.g., lipids and dyes) into soluble compounds or gases (CO2 and H2O), thereby reducing suspended particles and turbidity.61 Plasma disrupts colloidal particle structure by adjusting pH and eliminating charge, leading to floc formation. This leads to their aggregation into larger flocs and subsequent settling of the suspension.62 When cold plasma operates in conjunction with ozonation in wastewater treatment, both mechanisms synergistically improved degradation of pollutants while removing turbidity. A DBD jet generates energetic electrons in addition to UV light waves and reactive oxygen molecules such as OH, O3, and H2O2.63 Ozone (O3), whether generated by plasma or external ozonation, further oxidizes organic pollutants and colloidal particles. This ensemble of species performs a multi-stage oxidation cascade that degrades complex organic molecules (such as dyes, along with phenols) into their basic components. Plasmas emit ultraviolet light to activate ozone, leading to its decomposition into non-selective hydroxyl radicals (·OH).

This “UV-O3 synergy” enhances the mineralization of persistent contaminants such as textile dyes and pharmaceuticals. UV (200–280 nm) dissociates O3 to H2O2, producing highly oxidative hydroxyl radicals (·OH, E° = 2.8 V) that decompose complex organics into CO2, H2O, and ions.64,65 O3 also directly oxidizes electron-rich groups, while UV targets others (e.g., pharmaceuticals) via photolysis, leading to broader degradation and fewer by-products.66 This process accelerates organic breakdown and reduces turbidity by disrupting colloids.

In this regard, plasma-ozonation degraded methyl red dye with significantly higher efficiency (114.7% improvement) compared to standalone treatments.67 In textile wastewater, UV-O3 degraded >90% of azo dyes via ·OH attack on chromophores.65 Balancing plasma energy input and ozone dosage necessitates an optimized reactor design for maximum efficiency.

TDS and EC control by optimized ozone and DBD treatment

TDS in wastewater refers to the collective content of dissolved substances, including inorganic salts, organic matter, minerals, metals, and various dissolved ions such as sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, chloride, bicarbonates, and sulfates, among others.68 EC in water primarily measures the concentration and mobility of these dissolved ions.

The synergistic effect of DBD with the aeration, filtration, and ozone (O3) system significantly enhanced TDS removal efficiency, primarily by promoting pollutant decomposition. Plasma treatment duration directly influenced TDS measurements, which decreased to their lowest point after 30 min of treatment (Figure 5A). Specifically, the combined pretreatment (aeration and filtration) followed by 30 min of DBD plasma concurrent with 10 min of ozone treatment, achieved a substantial 70.72% reduction in TDS compared to the control. However, the TDS concentration significantly increased with 50 min of plasma treatment combined with 10 min of ozone exposure. Similarly, the EC measurements mirrored the trend observed for TDS (Figure 5B). The untreated effluent had an EC of 6,479 μS/cm. This value decreased sequentially after each treatment stage: aeration, aeration/filtration, and aeration/filtration/O3/DBD. The lowest EC value recorded was 2,558 μS/cm after 30 min of plasma treatment, corresponding to a removal efficiency of 60.51% relative to the control. Consistent with the TDS trend, the EC also increased with longer plasma treatment times beyond the optimum.

The combined action of cold plasma and ozonation achieves enhanced TDS reduction through both oxidative and physicochemical processes. DBD plasma generates a variety of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS), including hydroxyl radicals (·OH), ozone (O3), atomic oxygen (O), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and nitric oxide (·NO) (Liu et al., 2024). Ozonation directly introduces ozone, a potent oxidant, into the wastewater.69 The plasma-generated O3 complements and amplifies the oxidative potential of the externally introduced ozone.70 Highly reactive ·OH radicals, formed through O3-NTP-based AOPs, immediately break down electron-rich organic molecules. Cold plasma and ozone reactions facilitate the conversion of pollutants into relatively polar, less harmful substances, as well as carbon dioxide and water. Furthermore, plasma-generated acidic or basic species, along with ozone, can alter the wastewater pH (often decreasing it), which destabilizes dissolved solids and promotes the precipitation of dissolved salts.53,67,69,71 For instance, ozone oxidizes redox-sensitive ions (e.g., Fe2+ to Fe3+, Mn2+ to MnO2), producing insoluble precipitates.70 Electrochemical reactions induced by plasma neutralize dissolved salts (e.g., NaCl → Cl− + Na+ → Cl2↑ + NaOH). This precipitation process, coupled with organic pollutant decomposition, works to lower the amount of dissolved ions, thus leading to decreased EC. Rao et al.72 demonstrated that integrating plasma-ozonation methods eliminated 91.9% of the ionic dye methyl orange, thereby reducing the overall ionic nature and conductivity.

However, extending the plasma treatment duration beyond the optimal time (e.g., to 40 and 50 min) resulted in increased TDS and EC levels. This phenomenon is attributed to the continued action of plasma and ozone. Prolonged plasma treatment leads to greater ionization and an increased concentration of active species, promoting further breakdown of complex organic molecules. This process can generate smaller, more soluble by-products, including organic acids and various ions, which remain dissolved in the water due to incomplete mineralization.63,73,74

Additionally, prolonged treatment and increased ionization can oxidize micronutrients like iron (Fe) and manganese (Mn) into soluble ionic forms (e.g., Fe3+ and Mn2+), further contributing to the elevation of TDS.70 The synergistic effect of ozone and cold plasma over extended periods amplifies these processes, leading to a net increase in dissolved solids and, consequently, an increase in EC.

Nonetheless, since this growth rate was much less than the control and conventional techniques (aeration and filtration), as described in section 'DOM control by optimized ozone and DBD treatment', and bearing in mind that such compounds are biodegradable, the environmental risk posed by this process is insignificant. However, full confidence would be made possible through specific toxicity studies to complete a risk assessment.

Despite this, there is still great potential for this method because, with optimization, it could be an effective treatment strategy with low chemical use and low secondary pollution for both industrial and municipal wastewater applications.63,67,70

TSS reduction by optimized ozone and DBD treatment

TSS measurement in wastewater relies on measuring the amount of solid particles greater than 2 μm trapped by filters within water solutions. These particles include organic and inorganic materials such as silt, clay, algae, microorganisms, and organic debris. The level of TSS removal depends on the duration and nature of the treatment.

Figure 6 illustrates the reduction in TSS resulting from combined time-dependent ozone and DBD treatment. During this combined NTP treatment, the degradation and removal of contaminants were enhanced with increasing plasma treatment times, achieving 100% TSS removal after 50 min. Therefore, a combination of aeration with filtration, ozonation (10 min), and DBD treatment significantly improved pollutant removal in the effluent.

DBD plasma and ozone can decrease TSS through various mechanisms. First, ions formed by plasma (e.g., H+, O−) act as neutralizing agents for colloidal particles, which reduces their electrostatic forces and promotes flocculation.63 Second, oxidation processes, such as ozone exposure, render particulate surfaces more adhesive by introducing oxygen-containing functional groups (e.g., carboxyl or hydroxyl), allowing them to form larger, more readily settling aggregates.72 Third, plasma (ionized gas containing reactive oxygen/nitrogen species) and ozone disrupt microbial cell walls through lipid peroxidation (damaging cell membranes), oxidation of proteins and peptidoglycan (weakening structural integrity), and DNA/RNA damage, leading to the lysis of bacteria and algae. This releases intracellular debris, which is subsequently oxidized into dissolved solids or gases, thereby reducing TSS.75,76 Hamza et al. (2023) stated that cold plasma inactivated bacteria in river water, reducing biomass-related TSS. Additionally, cold plasma treatment significantly reduced coliform and E. coli concentrations, removed water odor, increased dissolved oxygen, and decreased COD.53

The synergy between plasma and ozone significantly enhances the effects observed from each element alone. The NTP reactor design can facilitate efficient mass transfer and intimate contact between ozone and organic pollutants, leading to high pollutant degradation performance.77,78 Furthermore, as plasma produces microbubbles and creates turbulent water flow, it expands the gas-liquid surface contact area, thereby promoting effective ozone dissolution.79,80 UV photons emitted from plasma weaken hydrogen bonds in water, thereby improving ozone’s solvation and dispersion.81 The high-energy electrons in plasma split ozone (O3) molecules into atomic oxygen (O) and hydroxyl (·OH) radicals, which are highly reactive substances that degrade various persistent organic compounds (Equation 3). In other words, the fragmentation of ozone produces species with higher oxidation potential (from 2.07 V for O3 to 2.8 V for OH), enabling faster degradation of recalcitrant organic solids (e.g., cellulose and microbial biomass).82 Ozone also regenerates plasma-generated radicals. A reaction between ozone and plasma-produced superoxide radicals (O2−) results in ozonide ions (O3−) that split apart to create additional ·OH. Furthermore, the breakdown of hydrogen peroxide molecules sustains oxidative activity by continuously generating ·OH radicals. The chain reaction maintains ·OH amounts to overcome its fast dissipation rate of less than 1 μs, thereby sustaining oxidative activity.25,50 Moreover, ozone competes with OH for reaction with scavengers (e.g., bicarbonate ions), thereby reducing radical loss and extending their availability for TSS degradation.

Ozone and plasma also have synergistic TSS degradation pathways. ·OH and O3 oxidize complex organics (e.g., fats and proteins) into soluble compounds (e.g., carboxylic acids) or mineralize them to CO2/H2O, reducing particulate TSS.83,84 In this regard, plasma-ozonation degraded starch particles in food wastewater by breaking glycosidic bonds.85 Charge neutralization by plasma ions (e.g., H+) destabilizes clay/silt particles, while ozone oxidizes surface organic coatings, promoting aggregation and settling.86,87 These mechanisms demonstrate that hybrid AOPs offer excellent potential for sustainable wastewater management. The combined system consistently outperforms individual methods in both efficiency and cost-effectiveness, particularly for recalcitrant or colloidal solids.

Biochemical oxygen demand (COD and BOD5) reduction by optimized ozone and DBD treatment

BOD measures the amount of dissolved oxygen consumed by aerobic microorganisms (bacteria and fungi) to decompose organic matter, which indicates the amount of oxygen needed by living organisms to break down the biodegradable organic pollutants present in the water.88,89 COD measures the amount of oxygen required to chemically oxidize all organic (and some inorganic) compounds in a water sample using a strong chemical oxidizing agent. It indicates the total amount of substances in wastewater that can be oxidized chemically. This includes both biodegradable and non-biodegradable organic matter, as well as some oxidizable inorganic substances such as ammonia and nitrites.88,89

Analyses of COD and BOD performed on wastewater samples subjected to combined DBD and O3 treatment revealed that their values were dependent on the DBD discharge exposure time in the presence of ozone (Figure 7). Significant decreases in both COD and BOD concentrations were observed during the initial 30 min of treatment. Beyond this duration, further increases in plasma treatment time yielded only a marginal increase. Compared to the control, the combined treatment involving aeration/filtration followed by 10 min of O3 and 30 min of DBD achieved COD and BOD degradation rates of 51.41% and 59.30%, respectively.

Ozone combined with DBD treatment facilitates the decrease of COD and BOD via amplified oxidation processes. Ozonation can fragment recalcitrant organic molecules (e.g., lignin derivatives) into smaller, more biodegradable fragments and directly oxidize complex organic compounds, breaking carbon-carbon bonds and converting them into simpler, less harmful substances like CO2 and H2O.90 This action indirectly lowers BOD by improving the efficiency of subsequent biological treatment, as the fragmentation and oxidation render the organic matter more accessible and readily consumable by microorganisms in downstream biological treatment stages.

Plasma is known to produce ROS, including hydroxyl radicals (·OH), along with atomic oxygen and UV radiation, which enhance organic pollutant decomposition and oxidize inorganic substances.63 In this regard, plasma can enhance ozone decomposition into OH, thereby amplifying oxidative capacity. For example, the combination of O3 with plasma-produced radicals in ozone-plasma nanobubble reactors enhanced the removal of COD/BOD from landfill leachate.91

The integration of ozone and plasma allows for both direct oxidation by O3 and secondary radical-driven reactions by ·OH, enabling the treatment of diverse pollutants. Studies have shown that ozone treatment as a single process achieved COD removal between 41% and 55%,92 whereas combined systems (e.g., plasma-ozone) achieved significantly higher reductions. For instance, combined ozone-plasma systems reduced COD by 93.93% and BOD by 74.12% in landfill leachate, substantially outperforming standalone methods.91 Plasma effectively degrades persistent organic pollutants (e.g., dyes and pharmaceuticals) that ozone alone may not fully mineralize, further contributing to COD reduction.63,93 Overall, the combined system outperforms individual methods in both efficiency and cost-effectiveness, particularly for recalcitrant or colloidal solids.

The rising trends in BOD and marginal rise in COD with time beyond 30 min of prolonged plasma treatment are explained by two major mechanisms. To begin with, incomplete mineralization produces soluble, partially oxidized intermediates; they are quantified as COD and have a greater bioavailability to microbes, which cause increased BOD.2,63 Second, complex polymers and colloidal structures are hydrolyzed and result in the release of previously encapsulated organic matter into the soluble phase. This observed increase in the two parameters is normally temporary; through further treatment, these liberated and transformed organics are mineralized, thus leading to general COD and BOD reduction.

Assessment of Potential, Practical Considerations for Using the Hybrid Treated Wastewater

Among the strategies of water scarcity mitigation, the reuse of non-conventional water resources, especially treated wastewater, can be mentioned. Nevertheless, the absence of coherent rules and standardized schemes is still one of the greatest barriers to the effective adoption of this strategy. There are guidelines which are provided by organizations like the EPA, ISO, and FAO, and the comparison of their qualitative parameters and allowable limit with the outcome of this study can give a clear view of how much can be improved in the quality of treated water (Table 1).

These findings of the study have shown a remarkable decrease in physicochemical parameters, including the BOD, COD, TSS, and turbidity of the raw wastewater after the hybrid treatment process. The BOD levels (31 ppm) were within the threshold level provided by ISO and were close to the Iranian and the EPA standard levels (Table 1). Based on the set Iranian and FAO limits, EC (2558 μs/cm) and TDS (1,129 ppm) were classified as moderate and thus were fit to irrigate salt-tolerant crops. TSS (8 ppm) was within all the Iranian, EPA, and ISO threshold values, whereas turbidity was near the ISO and EPA standards of high-quality treated wastewater. Even though the microbial parameters were not measured during this phase of the study, owing to the high disinfection ability of the ozone treatment and the DBD processes, there is a significant change in the health-related parameters, which can be examined in subsequent research works.

In accordance with the results of the conducted research and its comparison with current norms, the resulting treated water could be used in the following ways: (1) irrigation of the off-site green areas and planting of green belts at the edges of urban areas, which is one of the leading uses of treated wastewater. (2) Irrigation of non-food crops and processed agricultural products. (3) Given the right health certification and thorough monitoring of microbes, it can be expanded and offer services to urban parks, green spaces, and processed food crops. As a management approach, and in the context of the calculated EC and TDS values, the following are suggested: the treated wastewater should be used with high caution when using salinity-sensitive crops, applied when the soil has a medium to light texture, and where salt accumulation is least likely to occur. Proper irrigation timing and soil leaching methods to avoid long-term salinity concentration are developed. Finally, given adequate management and regular checks, such a solution can add to water security and environmental sustainability.

It is informative to directly compare it with the previous standalone plasma study.7 That was a multiple parallel quartz-based DBD reactor in which a total of ∼4 kWh/m3 was used, resulting in a 65% COD removal (SEC: 0.043 Wh/mg COD). The current paper used a new, patented coaxial falling-film DBD reactor of optimized design, which minimizes energy usage of the plasma to 1.0 kWh/m3. Although absolute COD removal of the hybrid system (51.41%) is lower, it has a real benefit in the form of the synergistic effect: it attains such high degradation at significantly less total energy input (2.2 vs. ∼4 kWh/m3) and a lower SEC (0.030 vs. 0.043 Wh/mg COD), which is much more energy efficient. This highlights the sustainability and cost-effectiveness of the hybrid system in terms of industrial wastewater treatment.

This research has effectively outlined the synergistic promise in combining DBD and ozonation for the advanced treatment of actual industrial wastewater. The cooperative method, applied after initial filtration and aeration, proved more energy efficient than single or less combined approaches at lowering the major pollution indicators, i.e., DOM, BOD, and COD. The significantly enhanced removal efficiencies and reaction kinetics, corroborated by high degradation rate constants, confirm the synergistic effect achieved by the concurrent application of plasma and ozone. This synergy surpassed the performance of both conventional methods and ozonation alone. This synergy is explained by the increased production of strongly reactive species, i.e., hydroxyl radicals (·OH), and the initiation of complementary degradation processes that attack a wider range of organic pollutants present in multi-component industrial effluents. The strong performance shown on genuine industrial wastewater samples emphasizes the great potential of the combined DBD-ozonation technique as a robust and effective AOP for challenging wastewater treatment applications. The water produced in this study holds significant potential for use in green belt irrigation and, with proper management considerations, in the agricultural sector—representing an effective step toward preserving freshwater resources.

Limitations of the study

This paper shows that the composite ozone/DBD plasma process is very effective in treating a representative sample of composite industrial wastewater. Nevertheless, one must also concede several limitations that specify the directions of critical paths of research in the future. Pilot-scale testing using wastewater gathered over extended and variable time periods is the next step to ensure that the process durability and stable output under varying influent conditions are completely proven.

Moreover, the heterogeneous and complex structure of the industrial wastewater matrix is quite a challenge that poses serious problems in the analysis. An in-depth study of the transformation products and the related toxicological implications, albeit analytically challenging, is a crucial and compelling trend in future studies. To establish mechanistic links between plasma chemistry and treatment efficacy, future experiments should employ time-resolved diagnostics to make correlations between the dynamics of relevant plasma parameters, e.g., electron temperature (Te) and density (ne), and real-time kinetics of degradation of individual organic pollutants.

Lastly, the national and international health-based water guidelines must be adhered to in order to make the treated effluent have practical applicability in either reuse or safe discharge. Such regulatory benchmarks as critical evaluation criteria in the future should be incorporated in the work so that the technology could demonstrate the ability to produce water, which would be safe and reusable, and which would meet the specific requirements.

Wastewater sampling and characterization

Industrial wastewater samples were collected from the Kaveh Industrial City and Special Economic Zone wastewater treatment plant, located in Arak, Markazi Province, Iran (34.1°N, 49.7°E). The Kaveh Industrial City wastewater treatment plant, situated within Arak’s industrial zone (Figure 11) and located approximately 280 km southwest of Tehran, has a total capacity of 5000 cubic meters per day. To investigate the synergistic effects of ozone and plasma treatments, this study focused on wastewater after preliminary treatment, a departure from previous research that focused solely on plasma applications.7 The facility collected wastewater following the screening, grit removal, primary sedimentation, and partial extended aeration, along with secondary sedimentation processes. A total of 500 L of wastewater was gathered at 4°C, and immediately preserved without dilution for analysis. The characteristics of the collected wastewater are presented in Table 3.

Experimental setup and procedure

The pilot-scale treatment system operated at a capacity of 100 L/h (2.4 m3/d) and consisted of sequential treatment units as shown in Figure 12. Wastewater first underwent a 24-h aeration. This duration was selected based on both operational standards at the source treatment plant and optimization data from previous studies.7 After aeration, the mixed liquor was channeled into a sedimentation tank to enable the separation of suspended solids and biomass, and hence generate more clarified effluent to be treated further.

Schematic of the treatment process

The aerated effluent was then processed through a multi-stage filtration system comprising sequentially arranged PVC columns packed with: (1) graded silica sand (coarse 1.2 mm progressing to fine 0.5 mm), (2) a carbon-silica composite layer, and (3) granular activated carbon (12 × 40 mesh). Ozonation was then conducted in a bubble column contact chamber at 25 ± 2°C. On-site ozone production occurred using a corona discharge ozonator (Model: OZ-30G, 30 g/h output) fed with pure oxygen at 100 L/h. The level of ozone was kept at 25 mg/L, which was confirmed by the conventional indigo colorimetric procedure. Contact times of 5, 10, 15, and 20 min were evaluated. Samples from each interval were analyzed for key water quality parameters—Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD), Biological Oxygen Demand (BOD5), Total Dissolved Solids (TDS), Electrical Conductivity (EC), Total Suspended Solids (TSS), Turbidity, and Dissolved Organic Matter (DOM)—to determine the optimal contact time. All trials were performed in triplicate.

The samples that had been subjected to the optimum duration of ozonation contact time were then subjected to non-thermal plasma exposure in a custom-built, patented coaxial falling-film dielectric barrier discharge (DBD) reactor (Figure 13). The reactor consisted of a central stainless-steel ground electrode, and above this electrode, a quartz tube dielectric was fitted. A copper coil high-voltage electrode was wound around the quartz tube exterior. The flow of wastewater was in the shape of a thin film (0.5 mm) on the inner stainless-steel electrode, and the discharges of plasma in the air gap between the water film and the inner quartz surface were produced. Ambient air was used as the working gas, and a sinusoidal AC mode and high-voltage supply (15 kV peak-to-peak, 8 kHz) was used to drive the system in batch recirculation mode (100 L/h). These conditions were chosen as a result of an optimization study conducted previously.

1 – Coil electrode (high voltage), 2 – stainless steel ground electrode, 3 – water falling film, 4 – quartz tube, 5 – ground low voltage, 6 – high voltage connection, and 7 – ozone generator.

The plasma treatment duration was varied (10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 min), with samples collected at each interval. The same suite of water quality parameters (COD, BOD, TDS, EC, TSS, DOM, and turbidity) was analyzed to determine the optimal plasma exposure time. This procedure was also conducted in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Controllability tests indicated that the samples of raw wastewater did not exhibit any statistically significant difference in the critical pollution indicators (e.g., COD, BOD, etc.) when kept under the trial conditions without treatment throughout the test (120 min).

Analytical methods

Water quality parameters were measured at various stages of the treatment process using standardized methods and specific instrumentation. BOD5 was determined using a BODTrak™ system. COD was analyzed using a HACH DR6000 spectrophotometer after digesting samples in a HACH DRB200 reactor at 150°C for 2 h. AQUALYTIC HACH turbidity meter was used for turbidity measurements. TSS was determined according to Standard Method 2540. TDS and EC were measured with a HACH-based HQ440d multiparameter probe. Finally, UV absorbance at 254 nm, serving as an indicator of aromatic DOM, was measured using a HACH DR6000 spectrophotometer. All HACH instrumentation was sourced from HACH Company, United States.

Optical Emission Spectroscopy

To analyze the light emitted from DBD plasma, a single-channel spectrometer (V550 model from OPTC in Iran) was employed. This device, with a resolution of 0.6 nm, recorded the optical emission spectra. Under standard room conditions, the spectrum of the DBD plasma source was measured over the 200–980 nm range by positioning an optical fiber 2 cm away from the visible plasma glow region.

Quantification and statistical analysis

After testing for normality, statistical analyses were conducted using three replicates of data obtained from each treatment (n = 3). Data are shown as means ± SD. Comparisons among groups were performed using a repeated measures ANOVA test followed by Duncan’s multiple comparison post hoc test. The statistical significance levels were set at p < 0.01 and p < 0.05. All data analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 and TIBCO Statistica™ 14.0.0. Linear regression was performed on the points obtained during the experiment, and the coefficient of determination (R2) was used to evaluate the accuracy of the regression. The slope of the fitted line was used to estimate the degradation rate constant. All graphs were produced using GraphPad Prism V.9.00 and Excel. The results for determining the optimal ozone contact time for COD, BOD5, TDS, EC, TSS, turbidity, and DOM are presented in the Results and Discussion section. Additionally, the results from the combined DBD plasma and ozone treatment for these parameters are reported individually in the same section. All analytical results, along with supporting details, are included in the relevant figures and figure legends.
