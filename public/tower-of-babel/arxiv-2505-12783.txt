Unimodular equations which do not preserve the derived length of a group
Mikhail A. Mikheenko

Abstract
It is a known fact that any unimodular equation over an abelian group has a solution in that group itself. It is also known that for metabelian groups this does not hold; moreover, there is a unimodular equation over some metabelian group which has no solutions in any larger metabelian group. Here we present the proof of an analagous fact for solvable groups of higher derived lengths.

The present article is devoted to equations over groups.

The present article is concerned with unimodular equations.

Definition
.

An equation

w(x)=1

over a group
G

is called

unimodular
,
if the exponent sum of
x

in the word
w(x)
equals
\pm 1
.

For example, the equation
xg_{1}x^{2}g_{2}x^{-4}=1
is unimodular,
while the equations
x^{2}g_{1}x^{-4}=1
and
x^{-1}g_{1}xg_{2}=1

Groups over which all unimodular equations are solvable
form a wide class, including:

•

finite groups
[
9
]
;

•

residually finite groups
(it follows from the previous item);

•

locally residually finite groups, e.g. locally finite groups
(which also follows from the first item);

•

locally indicable groups

[

4

, i.e. groups
whose every non-trivial finitely generated subgroup
admits a surjective homomorphism onto
an infinite cyclic group;

•

hyperlinear groups
[
24

(it is unknown if this class coincides with
the class of all groups);

•

torsion-free groups
[
13
]
.

In fact, for most of these subclasses unimodular equations are not the only
ones which are proved to be solvable in mentioned papers. For
complete statements, see the articles cited above.

Also, there is a following conjecture on solvability
of unimodular equations over groups.

Kervaire-Laudenbach Conjecture
.

Any unimodular equation over any group is solvable.

It is still yet unknown whether the conjecture is true.

Apart from seeking a solution of an equation over a group
G

in any group
\widetilde{G}
, no matter with which properties,
one can also ask if a solution can be found
in a group similar to the initial group.

For example, a unimodular equation
w(x)=1

over an abelian group
A
always has a solution in

A
itself
(it is an easy exercise if one notes that
since the solution is searched in an abelian group,
the variable can be assumed to commute with the coefficients).
It is in fact even true for nilpotent groups instead of abelian ones:
this is a result of A.L. Shmel’kin. Here is a very weakened
statement of that result:

Theorem

(
[
26
]
)
.

Suppose that
G
is a nilpotent group.
Then any unimodular equation over

G
has a solution in
G
.

Some of the earlier mentioned subclasses of groups
also admit solving unimodular equations over a group
in a group from the same subclass.
For instance, a unimodular equation over a finite group
is solvable in a finite group as well
[
9
]
,
and any unimodular equation over a locally indicable group
has a solution in a locally indicable group

[
11
]
.

The situation is more complicated for the class
of solvable groups.

Proposition 1

(
[
16
]
)
.

There is a unimodular equation
w(x)=1

over a metabelian group
G

such that the equation has no solutions in metabelian groups.

Moreover,
G
can be chosen both to be finite
(of order
42
) or to be torsion free (and such that any unimodular
equation over
G
has a solution in solvable groups
of derived length 3).

Both groups
G
from this proposition
have elements
a,c\in G
such that

a^{6},c\in G^{\prime}
, but
cc^{a}c^{-a^{3}}c^{-a^{4}}\neq 1
.
Unimodular equations over them which have no solutions
in metabelian groups have the form
xx^{-a}x^{a^{2}}=c
.
The group of order
42
is the semidirect product

\langle c\rangle_{7}\leftthreetimes\langle a\rangle_{6}
,
with
c^{a}=c^{5}
. Indeed, in this group

cc^{a}c^{-a^{3}}c^{-a^{4}}=c^{1+5-125-625}=c^{(1+5)(1-125)}=c^{6\cdot(-124)}\neq 1,

as both
6
and
124
are not divisible by
7
.

It is yet unknown if the equation

xx^{-a}x^{a^{2}}=c
over

\langle c\rangle_{7}\leftthreetimes\langle a\rangle_{6}

has a solution in somesolvable group.

In
[
22
]
,
\langle c\rangle_{7}\leftthreetimes\langle a\rangle_{6}

was proved to be
the minimal example of a metabelian group,
for which the proposition above holds.

However, it was still unknown if there was an analagous
example of a solvable group with a unimodular equation
over it, which has no solutions in solvable groups
of the same derived length,
for an arbitrary derived length (except 1, of course).

Here we fill this gap.

Main theorem
.

For any natural number

n\geqslant 2

there is a unimodular equation over a solvable group of derived length

n
which has no solutions in solvable groups of the same derived length.

Preliminaries

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that
G
is a metabelian group with elements
a,c\in G

such that
a^{6},c\in G^{\prime}
.
Also, suppose that
c^{a}\neq c^{3}

(in particular,
c\neq 1
).
Then
c\neq cc^{a}c^{-a^{3}}c^{-a^{4}}
.

Proof.

Assume
c=cc^{a}c^{-a^{3}}c^{-a^{4}}
.
Note that in this case
cc^{a}c^{-a^{3}}c^{-a^{4}}\neq 1
and

c^{a^{3}}=c^{a^{3}}c^{a^{4}}c^{-1}c^{-a}=\left(c^{a}cc^{-a^{4}}c^{-a^{3}}\right)^{-1}=\left(cc^{a}c^{-a^{3}}c^{-a^{4}}\right)^{-1}=c^{-1},

where the third equation holds because
c\in G^{\prime}

commutes with its conjugates.
Hence
c=cc^{a}c^{-a^{3}}c^{-a^{4}}=c^{2}c^{2a}
.
Then
c=c^{-2a}=c^{4a^{2}}=c^{-8a^{3}}=c^{8}
.
Now it follows that:

•

c^{7}=1
. As
c\neq 1
, we get that the order of
c
is
7
.

•

\langle a\rangle
acts on
\langle c\rangle_{7}
by conjugation,
and
a^{6}
acts trivially.

Consider the action of
a
on
\langle c\rangle_{7}
.
This action can not be an automorphism of order
1
,
2
or
3
,
since in each of these cases
cc^{a}c^{-a^{3}}c^{-a^{4}}=1
.
That is why
a
acts by an automorphism of order
6
.
So this automorphism is either
c\mapsto c^{3}
or
c\mapsto c^{5}
.
But it can not be
c\mapsto c^{3}
by condition, and for

c\mapsto c^{5}
we have

cc^{a}c^{-a^{3}}c^{-a^{4}}=c^{(1+5)\cdot(1-125)}=c^{6\cdot 2}=c^{5}\neq c.

We get a contradiction, hence the assumption

c=cc^{a}c^{-a^{3}}c^{-a^{4}}
is false.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Suppose that
G\ni a,c
is a metabelian group such that

a^{6},c\in G^{\prime}
and

\widetilde{G}
is a solution group for the equation

xx^{-a}x^{a^{2}}=c
.
Then
cc^{a}c^{-a^{3}}c^{-a^{4}}\in\left(\widetilde{G}\right)^{\prime\prime}
.

Proof.

Notice that
x\in\left(\widetilde{G}\right)^{\prime}

\biggl(
as it equals
c
modulo

\left(\widetilde{G}\right)^{\prime}
\biggr)
. So,
x^{a},x^{a^{2}},\ldots,x^{a^{5}}

lie in
\left(\widetilde{G}\right)^{\prime}
as well.

Now consider
\widetilde{G}

modulo its second commutator subgroup
\left(\widetilde{G}\right)^{\prime\prime}
.
In that case the elements of
\left(\widetilde{G}\right)^{\prime}
, including

x,x^{a},\ldots,x^{a^{5}}
, can be assumed to commute with each other.
Therefore we adopt the following notation:

h^{a+b}=h^{a}h^{b}

for
h\in\left(\widetilde{G}\right)^{\prime},a,b\in\widetilde{G}
.
Consider the element

g=cc^{a}c^{-a^{3}}c^{-a^{4}}
. Substitute

c
with
x^{1-a+a^{2}}
there.
In our notation we get

g=c^{1+a-a^{3}-a^{4}}=\left(x^{1-a+a^{2}}\right)^{1+a-a^{3}-a^{4}}=x^{(1-a+a^{2})(1+a-a^{3}-a^{4})}=x^{(1-a+a^{2})(1+a)(1-a^{3})}=x^{1-a^{6}}=1.

Thus,
g=1
modulo
\left(\widetilde{G}\right)^{\prime\prime}
.
This means that
g\in\left(\widetilde{G}\right)^{\prime\prime}
, as needed.

∎

Remark 1
.

In particular, if
cc^{a}c^{-a^{3}}c^{-a^{4}}\neq 1
,
then the equation
xx^{-a}x^{a^{2}}=c
over

G
from the condition of the lemma
is unsolvable in metabelian groups.

3
The proof of the main theorem

Remind the statement of the main theorem.

Main theorem
.

For any natural number

n\geqslant 2

there is a unimodular equation over a solvable group of derived length

n
which has no solutions in solvable groups of the same derived length.

Proof.

Consider a group
H=B\wr G
, where
B
is an arbitrary
solvable group
of derived length exactly
n-2
, whereas

G\ni a,c
is a metabelian group such that

a^{6},c\in G^{\prime}
but
cc^{a}c^{-a^{3}}c^{-a^{4}}\neq 1
.
We can choose
G
as one of the groups from Proposition
1
,
e.g.
\langle c\rangle_{7}\leftthreetimes\langle a\rangle_{6}

with
c^{a}=c^{5}
.
Consider the equation
xx^{-a}x^{a^{2}}=c
over
H
,
where
a,c
are the elements of the subgroup

G
corresponding to the lemma.

Suppose that
\widetilde{H}
is a solution group for this equation.
Consider
\left(\widetilde{H}\right)^{\prime\prime}
.

•

On the one hand,
\left(\widetilde{H}\right)^{\prime\prime}
contains the elements of the form

bb^{-c}b^{-a}b^{ac}
, where
b\in B

(notice that
cc^{a}c^{-a^{3}}c^{-a^{4}}\neq 1
implies
a\notin G^{\prime}
и
c\neq 1
;
so,
1,c,a,ac
are four distinct elements of
G
).
Indeed,
H^{\prime}\subset\left(\widetilde{H}\right)^{\prime}
contains

[b,a]=b^{-1}b^{a}
.
Therefore,
H^{\prime\prime}\subset\left(\widetilde{H}\right)^{\prime\prime}
contains

[b^{-1}b^{a},c]=b^{-a}bb^{-c}b^{ac}=bb^{-c}b^{-a}b^{ac},

as the different copies of
B
commute with each other.
For convenience, write this element as
h=b^{1-c-a+ac}
.

•

On the other hand, Lemma
2
states that

\left(\widetilde{H}\right)^{\prime\prime}
also contains
g=cc^{a}c^{-a^{3}}c^{-a^{4}}\neq 1
.

Then consider the commutator

[g^{-1},h]=b^{(1-c-a+ac)(1-g^{-1})}
.
It is an element of the direct product of copies of
B
.
We state that its coordinate coresponding to
1\in G
equals
b
.
For this to be true, it is enough to prove that
the term
1

appears
in the expression

(1-c-a+ac)(1-g^{-1})\in\mathbb{Z}G

only once.
Notice that the term with the element
a
does not equal

1
, as
a
does not lie in the commutator subgroup of
G

(or else
cc^{a}c^{-a^{3}}c^{-a^{4}}
equals
1
), while
g^{-1}
and
c
lie there.
Also note that
c\neq 1\neq g^{-1}
. It remains to understand that
cg^{-1}\neq 1
,
or, in other words, that
c\neq g
, which is true by Lemma
1

since
c^{a}=c^{5}\neq c^{3}
.

As a result we get that the third commutator subgroup of
\widetilde{H}

contains the elements
[g^{-1},h]
of the direct product of copies of

B
, and their coordinates corresponding to
1\in G
run over all the group
B
.
Therefore the subgroup generated by those elements
lies in the
3
rd commutator subgroup of
\widetilde{H}

and has a nontrivial

\left(n-3\right)
th commutator subgroup.
Thus,
\left(\widetilde{H}\right)^{(n)}\neq\{1\}
, as needed.
∎

Remark 2
.

Note that since the group
G
(and the group
B
)
can be chosen either to be finite or to be torsion free,
the group
H
can be chosen to satisfy one of these properties as well.

4
Further questions and surmises

The following question remains open.

Question 1
.

Does the equation

xx^{-a}x^{a^{2}}=c

over

\langle c\rangle_{7}\leftthreetimes\langle a\rangle_{6}
,
where
c^{a}=c^{5}
,
have a solution in some solvable group?

The following proposition gives a slight hope
for the negative answer.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose that
G
is a metabelian group with the elements
a,c\in G

such that
a^{6},c\in G^{\prime}
and
cc^{a}c^{-a^{3}}c^{-a^{4}}\neq 1
.
Denote
g=cc^{a}c^{-a^{3}}c^{-a^{4}}
.
If
c
has a finite order coprime to
6
,
then
gg^{a}g^{-a^{3}}g^{-a^{4}}\neq 1

and
g
has a finite order coprime to
6
.

Proof.

Note that if the order of
c
is
n
, then

g^{n}=1
, and the order of
g
divides
n
.
Particularly, if
n
is coprime to
6
, then the order of

g
is also coprime to
6
(and is not equal to
1
by condition).

Then notice that
gg^{-a}g^{a^{2}}=c^{(1-a+a^{2})(1+a)(1-a^{3})}=1
.
Also notice that
g^{a^{3}}=g^{-1}
,
and the condition
gg^{a}g^{-a^{3}}g^{-a^{4}}=1
can be rewritten as

g^{2}g^{2a}=1
. Since
g
has an odd order, it follows that
gg^{a}=1
, so
g^{a}=g^{-1}
.
Therefore
gg^{-a}g^{a^{2}}=g^{3}=1
.
But this contradicts with the condition of the proposition.
So the assumption
gg^{a}g^{-a^{3}}g^{-a^{4}}=1
is false.
∎

Remark 3
.

If the order of
c
is even or divisible by
3
, then

gg^{a}g^{-a^{3}}g^{-a^{4}}
can be equal to
1
.
To see this, consider the group

\left(\langle b\rangle_{n}\times\langle d\rangle_{n}\right)\leftthreetimes\langle a\rangle_{6}

with the action
b^{a}=bd;d^{a}=b^{-1}
.
The automorphism acts as follows:

b\mapsto bd\mapsto d\mapsto b^{-1}\mapsto b^{-1}d^{-1}\mapsto d^{-1}\mapsto b.

Take
c=b=\left[b^{-1}d^{-1},a\right]
.
Then

g=b\cdot bd\cdot b\cdot bd=b^{4}d^{2},

gg^{a}g^{-a^{3}}g^{-a^{4}}=b^{4}d^{2}\cdot b^{2}d^{4}\cdot b^{4}d^{2}\cdot b^{2}d^{4}=b^{12}d^{12}.

Now we see that if
n
equals
3
or
4
, then
g\neq 1
, but

gg^{a}g^{-a^{3}}g^{-a^{4}}=1
.

Now if for the elements
a,c
from Proposition

2
there is an element
y
of a solution group
\widetilde{G}
of equation
xx^{-a}x^{a^{2}}=c

such that
yy^{-a}y^{a^{2}}=g
,
then such group, possibly, will have non-trivial fourth
commutator subgroup as well as the third one.
Likewise,
\widetilde{G}
would have a non-trivial
fifth commutator subgroup an so on, in other words,

\widetilde{G}
will fail to be solvable.
However, it is unclear if such element
y
exists and
whether the nontriviality of the fourth commutator subgroup
actually follows from the existence of
y
.
In other words, the question needs further consideration.

The following question is connected to Question
1
.

Question 2
.

Is it true that any unimodular equation over a solvable group
has a solution in a solvable group (maybe of larger derived length) as well?

If the answer to Question
1
is negative,
then the answer to Question
2

is negative as well.
And if the answer to Question
1

is positive, then, probably, the Question
2

will have the positive answer for the groups from the proof of the main theorem.
