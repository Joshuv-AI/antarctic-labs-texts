Adaptive Tweezers Based on Differential Hydrophilic–Hydrophobic Surfaces for the Manipulation of Micro-Objects
Authors: Xiongheng Bian, Ma Feng, Xiaoyan Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Conventional micromanipulation techniques, such as optical capture and mechanical grippers, suffer from rigid contact damage and exhibit poor adaptability to objects with diverse masses. This research presents adaptive droplet tweezers featuring nonparallel structures to tackle the nondestructive micromanipulation of objects with different masses. Through the analysis of the mechanical equilibrium conditions of the liquid bridge (where the pressures on the upper and lower surfaces of the bridge are equivalent), the critical parameters for a stable liquid bridge, including the contact angle, opening distance, and height, were discussed. The results of this study suggest that pulling of the tweezers and increasing the lower opening distance of the end of droplet tweezers are conducive to object transfer. Subsequent experiments conducted on the experimental platform verified the principles. Ultimately, by adjusting the curvature of the droplet bridge structure according to the hydrophilic–hydrophobic differences, the handling of objects with different weights was accomplished. This research demonstrates that the adaptive liquid bridge structure effectively surmounts the limitations of traditional methods, offering a novel approach for the flexible and adaptive micromanipulation of microscale objects.

Introduction

Micromanipulation technology for micrometer-scale objects demonstrates significant application potential in biomedicine, analytical chemistry, − and micro/nanomanufacturing fields. , Current research primarily focuses on methods such as optical trapping, acoustic trapping, electric field manipulation, and mechanical micropinches , to achieve manipulation of micro/nano objects. However, these methods are often constrained by factors such as the size, weight, environment, and movement path of the target objects. For instance, traditional approaches struggle to simultaneously achieve flexibility and broad adaptability when handling objects ranging from a few micrograms of cell spheres to tens of micrograms of MEMS components.

Liquid bridge manipulation is a key technology in the field of precise control of micro/nanoscale objects. Its core principle involves utilizing the capillary force generated by liquid columns (i.e., liquid bridges) formed between two solid surfaces to nondestructively and flexibly grasp, transport, and release microscopic objects. − Capillary force-based micromanipulation technology has been widely applied in various scenarios. In the field of microelectromechanical systems (MEMS), capillary force enables the pickup, positioning, and placement of objects as small as 1 mm. In biomedical applications, capillary force facilitates the transfer of microorganisms and targeted drug delivery. In the realm of surface chemistry, capillary force finds application in droplet collection, oil–water separation, , and the nondestructive transfer of droplets on intelligent ciliary , surfaces. Although most of these methods rely on capillary force to achieve efficient droplet transfer and remarkable results, there remain several bottlenecks in the use of liquid bridges within capillaries for solid material transfer. These bottlenecks are primarily manifested in two aspects: (1) Lacking a quantitative method to accurately determine the maximum loadable weight and critical size of micro-objects that various liquid bridge structures can stably capture, grasp, and transfer in practical operation; (2) The ability of the same liquid bridge structure to achieve adaptive and compatible manipulation of micro-objects with a wide range of mass differences is insufficient, and it is difficult to meet the demand of flexible operation for micro-objects with different masses.

To overcome the aforementioned two limitations, this study proposes a droplet tweezers with a nonparallel structure featuring alternating hydrophilic interactions, , building upon the liquid bridge research. The design aims to adaptively counteract gravitational forces by leveraging the structure’s ability to generate varying tension magnitudes. Key innovations include establishing a mechanical model for tweezers and object manipulation and conducting force analysis to identify critical conditions for each component, particularly the stability of liquid bridges. This mechanical model provides a solid theoretical foundation for the capillary-assisted operations.

The transfer of objects with varying sizes and weights was successfully achieved, demonstrating the adaptive characteristics of the designed tweezers. This holds significant application potential in both micro/nanomanufacturing and unmanned biochemical robotics laboratories.

Materials and Methods

As shown in Figure , this experiment employed a servo-motor-powered clamp (model PGEA-50–40) to drive two glass plates (dimensions 20 mm × 20 mm × 0.1 mm) to form a nonparallel droplet-tweezer system. The plates were treated with SM-TM-101F hydrophobic nanomaterials and perfluoropolyether siloxane reagents to modulate surface hydrophilicity and hydrophobicity. Image acquisition was performed using a Daheng Mercury CCD camera with a white background light source. The experimental subjects included polyethylene sheets (5 mm × 10 mm, 5 mm × 5 mm) and monocrystalline silicon wafers (5 mm × 10 mm, 5 mm × 5 mm), with deionized water as the carrier.

Schematic diagram of the experimental platform.

The experimental procedure consists of two processes: first, the servo-motor-controlled gripper adjusts the plate’s opening angle to capture and transfer thin sheets using liquid surface tension. Second, comparative tests are designed to evaluate transfer success rates and stability across different objects while analyzing the effects of various hydrophobic reagent combinations on transfer efficiency. All operations are monitored in real time via a CCD camera, recording data such as droplet morphology, object position, and transfer trajectories.

Results and Analysis

As shown in Figure , the droplet-tweezer structure utilizes capillary forces to transfer small objects, which is mainly divided into four parts: (1) Absorption of droplets to form a liquid bridge structure (Figure a–d); (2) Utilizing the liquid bridge adhere to the object’s surface, forming a special “Liquid Bridge Clip (LBC)” (Figure e–g); (3) Using this LBC to clamp and transport the object (Figure h–j); (4) Releasing the object to be transferred by opening the clip (Figure j–l). The conditions for droplet absorption and release in the first and fourth parts have been researched in previous studies. , Therefore, the main content of this paper is to analyze the second and third steps from the following aspects: (1) Force analysis of LBC; (2) LBC forming process analysis; (3) LBC condition analysis; (4) Adaptive function realization based on LBC.

Force Analysis of LBC

The liquid bridge achieves the transfer of an object through two key steps. The first is the formation process of LBC: the liquid bridge moistens the object interface and establishes a stable structure. The second is the pulling process of LBC: after forming the stable bridge, it is pulled upward, leveraging the LBC to lift the object. We analyzed each step in detail.

Subsequently, an in-depth discussion was conducted regarding the influence of CAH on the transferability of objects. As depicted in Figure , when the substrate exhibits hydrophilic properties, the maximum tensile force shows a decreasing trend with the increase in the contact angle hysteresis (as exemplified by points A and B). This phenomenon can be attributed to the fact that a larger hysteresis leads to a decrease in the backward angle, consequently diminishing the vertical component of the force. As a result, if the object fails to be lifted initially, then subsequent lifting becomes unfeasible. Conversely, in the case of hydrophobic substrates, the maximum tensile force increases as the contact angle hysteresis becomes greater (as shown by points A1 and B1). This is because a larger hysteresis reduces the backward angle, giving rise to a stronger vertical force component. Therefore, interfaces characterized by significant contact angle hysteresis under hydrophobic conditions display superior performance.

Adaptive Function Realization Based on LBC

In order to prevent the slippage of the liquid bridge during the process of object lifting and thus improve its stability, this research puts forward an alternating structure composed of weakly hydrophobic and strongly hydrophilic phases to develop an adaptive tweezer for object lifting and transportation. Experimental results are presented in Figure . As shown in Figure a,b, when the traditional tweezers with whole structure of the weak hydrophobic surface is used, it can carry the small weight object (0.012 g), but it will fail to carry the heavy weight object (0.024 g). In Figure c, when adopting the adaptive tweezer structure with hydrophobic upper and hydrophilic lower ends (the tweezers are treated in two ways: the front-end undergoes surface treatment with hydrophilic reagents, while the rear-end is treated with hydrophobic reagents), the liquid bridge’s upper interface slides downward upon pulling. Upon contacting the hydrophilic surface below, the sliding stops, generating upward surface tension that successfully counteracts gravity and achieves successful object transportation. Notably, this curvature adjustment method enables the transportation of objects with varying weights by modifying the upper interface’s curvature, thereby realizing our adaptive functionality. The success of the adaptive tweezers in Figure c corresponds to the high F G region in Figure c, where the specific hydrophilic–hydrophobic interface effectively increases the equivalent L down, enabling the capture of the heavier object.

A set of control experiments were carried out, where the masses of polyethylene and monocrystalline silicon microsheets were adjusted to gradient values (0.012 g, 0.016 g, 0.024 g, 0.032 g, 0.040 g) via physical approaches including cutting, stacking, and bonding. The adaptive tweezers was then used to manipulate these micro-objects under standard experimental conditions (θ = 15°, L Up = 2 mm). The results show that the proposed adaptive tweezers can stably grasp and transfer all micro-objects with masses from 0.012 to 0.032 g without manual parameter retuning. Notably, the critical mass threshold for effective manipulation is determined to be 0.032 g (The experiment failed when the mass of the object reached 0.040 g): beyond this value, the tweezers fail to form a stable liquid bridge clip and thus cannot absorb and transfer the micro-objects effectively, which further clarifies the load capacity range of the designed tweezers while demonstrating its robust adaptability within the effective threshold.

Subsequently, we further investigated the principle of this method for optimizing object transportation. As shown in Figure a, the surface tension on the bottom side of the liquid bridge (F down) counteracts the force (F G), whereas the tension on the top surface (F up) counteracts the tension on the bottom side. In the event that the bottom-side tension F down fails to counterbalance F G, the object will separate from the bottom surface of the bridge. Analogously, if the top surface tension F up (which is proportional to 1/R and surface hydrophilicity) is unable to counterbalance the bottom side tension F down, the bridge will lose its stability and collapse along with the object, as shown in Figure b. To avert this situation, we augmented the curvature of the upper surface.

After that, under identical conditions (θ = 15°, L Up = 2 mm), a comparison was conducted between the upward pressure (F Up) generated by traditional tweezers and that generated by adaptive tweezers, as depicted in Figure b. The results indicate that adaptive tweezers generate significantly higher upward pressure, and the magnitude of this pressure increases in proportion to the contact angle difference between their ends. Specifically, when the contact angle of the bottom surface is 60°, a contact angle difference of 20° results in an additional 2.4 × 10–6 N/mm of pressure, while a 40° difference provides an extra 4.9 × 10–6 N/mm, which is consistent with the experimental results.

Conclusion

Traditional micromanipulation techniques, such as optical capture and mechanical grippers, are constrained by rigid contact damage and limited adaptability to objects with diverse masses. This research presents an adaptive liquid bridge structure, in which a servo-motor actuates hydrophilic/hydrophobic plates to construct a nonparallel tweezer system. In combination with the monitoring of the liquid bridge morphology via a CCD camera, the mechanical equilibrium conditions (equal pressure on the upper and lower surfaces) during the formation and stretching of the liquid bridge are analyzed. The influence of parameters, including contact angle and opening distance, on the stability (locking ability) and clamping force of the liquid bridge is disclosed. The core mechanism entails the self-adaptive clamping of objects with different weights through adjustment of the liquid bridge curvature.

The main novelty of this work is that we use a hydrophilic–hydrophobic alternating surface structure to realize adaptive curvature adjustment of the liquid bridge, which overcomes the limitations of traditional micromanipulation tools. Its application value is manifested in fields like biomedicine (fragile cells/tissues) and MEMS assembly, facilitating nondestructive flexible manipulation. The significance of this research lies in providing a novel method for the adaptive manipulation of microscale objects, promoting the practical application of liquid bridge technology in micronano manufacturing, and offering theoretical and experimental support for precision operations in relevant fields.
