Hierarchically porous biochar/cellulose aerogel based stir-bar sorptive extraction for rapid determination of trace bisphenols in canned food samples
Authors: Lin Li, Xinli Song, Xinxiu Li, Jinglong Wang, Fei Wang, Kechao Liao, Shuhan Chu, Nan Li, Haoran Liu, Yongshuai Li, Zhan Chen, Feng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The stir bar was prepared by coating biochar on a magnetic core using cellulose aerogel (CA) as support and adhesive. The stir bar is ideal for stir-bar sorptive extraction (SBSE), since macroporous CA network provides accessible transport channels for analytes. Besides, raw materials of biochar and CA are natural, and the preparation process is organic solvent-free. The stir bar was applied to extract bisphenols in canned foods. Extraction parameters were studied, and our lab-made stir bars outperformed commercial ones in extraction speed and capacity. Coupled to UPLC-MS/MS, the established approach supported food safety surveillance. To our knowledge, this is the first application of biochar/CA in SBSE, introducing a green coating binder for SBSE. This rapid (extraction time: 8 min), sensitive (LOD: 1.0–3.5 ng/L and 11–44 ng/kg for liquid and solid foods), and reduced-solvent (volume of organic solvent in SBSE: 1 mL) SBSE method offers an innovative alternative for sample preparation.

Introduction

Canned foods are widely consumed worldwide due to their portability and long shelf life. To prevent interaction between foods and metal cans, epoxy resins are usually coated on the inner surface of cans (Cao et al., 2018). In the manufacturing process of epoxy resins, bisphenols (BPs) are incorporated to improve their strength and stability (Cao et al., 2021). Despite this, the BPs residue in the coatings can be slowly transported into the packed foods (Ong et al., 2022). Unfortunately, BPs are reported as a kind of endocrine disrupting chemicals that would affect human hormonal system (Hwang et al., 2023). Owing to the concern of public health, authorities (Food and Drug Administration and European Commission's Scientific Committee on Food) have set the tolerable daily intake for BPs (Vandenberg et al., 2009). Thus, an accurate analysis method is urgently needed for the assessment of people's exposure to BPs.

Given the trace level of BPs and complexity of food samples, an efficient sample preparation protocol is required. Among the numerous sample preparation approaches, column solid phase extraction (column SPE) has been accepted as the gold standard. However, sorbents are tightly packed at the bottom of SPE column, thus, blocking phenomenon can't be neglected, which really limits the loading rate (Poole, 2003). To overcome such challenge, various novel SPE modes, including dispersive SPE, magnetic SPE and stir bar sorptive extraction (SBSE) etc. have been proposed. In the three approaches mentioned above, sorbents are directly added to sample solution, thus the problem of blocking is avoided (Płotka-Wasylka et al., 2023). In particular, SBSE first proposed by Baltussen et al. in 1999 is more attractive. In SBSE, the sorbents are coated on the surface of magnetic cores, so rapid self-stirring extraction can be achieved only with the help of a magnetic stirrer (Hasan et al., 2020). Besides, the monolithic stir bar can be more easily recycled than powdered sorbents applied in d-SPE and MSPE, which reduces tedious manual manipulation (Płotka-Wasylka et al., 2023). So far, SBSE has been successfully employed for the analysis of target analytes in a wide range of samples, including environmental matrices (Hu et al., 2007; Teixeira et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2019), foodstuffs (Wang et al., 2025) and biological samples (Khoobi et al., 2019).

Regardless of the SPE mode, sorbents occupy the central position in the extraction process. In recent years, various novel sorbents, including metal organic frameworks, covalent organic frameworks, carbon materials (carbon nanotube, graphene and biochar) etc. have been applied in the field of SPE (Chen et al., 2025; Song et al., 2025; Song et al., 2026). Among them, biochar shows unique merits. For one thing, biochar is an environmentally friendly carbon-rich material, which can be easily obtained by thermal decomposition of natural biomass, such as agricultural waste, forestry waste and animal husbandry waste etc. For another, the specific surface area of biochar is high due to abundant porous structure, which is conducive to the extraction of target analytes (Luo et al., 2022). Despite the merits mentioned above, the application of biochar in SBSE was seldom reported. Accordingly, we intend to explore the application of biochar in SBSE.

To implement this extraction technique, successful immobilization biochar onto the magnetic cores is the crucial step. Usually, magnetic bar, iron bar and hollow fiber membrane with an inserted iron bar (Hasan et al., 2020) are commonly used as magnetic cores. Among them, hollow fiber membrane with an inserted iron bar is more desirable, since the porous hollow fiber membrane provides more loading sites for sorbents. As reported, organic polymers, such as polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) (Hu et al., 2007), polyvinyl alcohol (Wang et al., 2019) and polyurethane (Teixeira et al., 2025) are frequently employed as binding agents to load sorbents onto the surface of magnetic cores. For example, a cyclodextrin-based stir bar was prepared using PDMS as the binding agent, which was applied for the extraction of estrogens in water samples (Hu et al., 2007).

Organic polymers are highly stable in both aqueous and organic solutions, and their functional groups also contribute to the extraction. However, a large amount of organic solvents are required during the production and application of organic polymers. In recent years, with the development of green chemistry principles, natural polymers, such as cellulose, have gained growing attention. Cellulose is derived from natural sources such as plants and microorganisms, making it a green and environmentally friendly material (Nagarajan et al., 2021). Similar to organic polymers, cellulose can also act as adhesive to powdered adsorbents (Chen et al., 2023). For example, rheum biochar powder was loaded on cellulose via a simple blending method, and the composite was used for the extraction of sulfonamides in water samples (Li et al., 2025). What's more, cellulose exhibits desirable moldability, and can be easily processed into different shapes, such as membrane, microspheres, hydrogel and aerogel etc. Compared with dense cellulose films and microspheres, cellulose hydrogel and aerogel usually provide more loading sites due to the interconnected macroporous structure, whereas cellulose hydrogel is prone to swelling and structural deformation during stirring and repeated handling due to its high water content. Therefore, cellulose aerogel (CA) was selected as both the support and binder for the biochar coating (Dong et al., 2013; Li et al., 2022).

In our study, biochar was produced from chicken manure waste generated from poultry farming, which was used as the primary sorbent for SBSE. After that, biochar was loaded on the magnetic core (hollow fiber membrane with an inserted iron bar) using CA as both a support and an adhesive. Biochar/CA would be appealing sorbent for SBSE, since no organic solvents were applied during synthesis. Besides, the interconnected macroporous network of CA would provide transport channels for BPs. Coupled to ultra performance liquid chromatography tandem mass/mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS), the established approach was applied for the determination of trace BPs in canned food samples, directly supporting food safety surveillance and regulatory needs. To our knowledge, this is the first time that biochar was applied as the sorbent of SBSE. Meanwhile, the macroporous CA was innovatively employed as a binder in the SBSE. This rapid and sensitive method significantly reduces organic solvent consumption during the SBSE step, offering a promising alternative for sample preparation.

Experimental

Reagents and materials

Six standards of BPs (purity>98%), including bisphenol A (BPA), bisphenol E(BPE), bisphenol F(BPF), bisphenol S(BPS), bisphenol Z(BPZ) and bisphenol AF(BPAF) were provided by Macklin Biochemical Technology Corporation (Shanghai, China). Specific information regarding the BPs (chemical structure, molecular weight and pKa value) is presented in Table S1 (Section 1 of supplementary material: Chemical properties and structures of target BPs).

Chicken manure was collected from a local poultry farm and air-dried under cool and shaded conditions. Degreased cotton was provided by Heze Sanmu Group (Heze, China). N,N′-methylenebisacrylamide was obtained from Aladdin Industrial Corporation (Shanghai, China). Chromatographic-grade methanol was purchased from Tedia Company (Fairfield, USA). Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) hollow fiber membrane was purchased from Jianghan Environmental Protection Technology (Jinan, China). Commercial stir bar coated with PDMS (length: 1 cm, layer thickness: 1 mm, PDMS coating volume: 63 μL) was obtained from J&K Scientific (Shanghai, China). All other reagents and chemicals employed in the experiments were from Damao Chemical Reagent Factory (Shanghai, China). PVDF filter membrane (ϕ = 13 mm, 0.22 μm) was purchased from Jinteng Corporation (Tianjin, China). Plasticizer-free Nalgene centrifuge tubes were acquired from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, USA).

Instrumentation and analytical conditions

Exact information of instrumentation and analytical conditions is given in the supplementary material (Section 2 of supplementary material: Instrumentation and analytical conditions).

Preparation of biochar, cellulose hydrogel and biochar/CA coated stir bar

Preparation of chicken manure biochar

Firstly, the chicken manure was dried in an oven at 80 °C for 24 h. After that, the dried chicken manure was placed in a tubular furnace and heated at the rate of 4 °C/min to 600 °C, followed by pyrolysis at 600 °C for 4 h under N2 atmosphere. In fact, heating at 400 °C is sufficient to eliminate endocrine-disrupting chemicals and organic contaminants (Vavrouš et al., 2016). So, BPs potentially present in the chicken manure can be removed during the high-temperature carbonization process. To eliminate mineral residues, the obtained biochar was treated with 0.1 mol/L HCl. Finally, biochar was washed with ultrapure water and oven-dried for further use. Preparation process of biochar and stir bar coated with biochar/CA.

Preparation of cellulose hydrogel

According to a previous study (Ren et al., 2018), cellulose hydrogel was prepared using degreased cotton as the raw material. Firstly, the chopped cotton was immersed in NaOH/urea solution (7 wt% NaOH, 14 wt% urea) at −20 °C for 2 h. Following that, the frozen mixture was vigorously stirred for 10 min. After centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min, transparent cellulose solution from the upper layer was separated. Subsequently, 0.6 g of N, N′-methylenebisacrylamide was introduced into 50 mL cellulose solution, followed by sonication to achieve a uniform dispersion. Afterwards, the dispersion was poured into the orifice plate (1 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm). 12 h later, the mixture turned into jelly-like hydrogel at room temperature. Finally, the resulting hydrogel was thoroughly rinsed with ultrapure water to eliminate residual chemicals.

Preparation of biochar/CA coated stir bar

Biochar/(CA) was fabricated via a simple blending method, and the preparation procedure is presented in Fig. 1. Firstly, bulk cellulose hydrogel was processed into a viscous cellulose hydrogel liquid using a homogenizer. Then, different amounts (250, 400, 500, 750 mg) of biochar were added in 10 mL of liquid, respectively. After stirring for 10 min, 200 μL of uniform mixture was packed into a vial insert. To achieve SBSE, a magnetic core was placed into the center of the vial insert. The magnetic core was prepared by inserting a stainless steel rod (diameter: 0.3 mm, length: 5 mm) into the PVDF hollow fiber membrane (inner diameter: 0.8 mm, wall thickness: 0.5 mm, pore size: 0.05 mm), followed by heat sealing both ends. After that, the packed vial insert was transferred to a refrigerator maintained at −20 °C. 4 h later, stir bar coated with biochar/CA was produced by freeze-drying. Besides, stir bar coated solely with CA was prepared with the same procedure without the addition of biochar. To remove potential BPs present in the adsorbent, the prepared stir bars were repeatedly washed with ethanol, acetonitrile and ultrapure water prior to the extraction.

Sample preparation and SBSE procedure

To minimize potential background interference from plastic consumables, glassware was preferentially used throughout the experiments. All glassware was rigorously cleaned using a chromic acid solution and subjected to thermal treatment at 400 °C (Vavrouš et al., 2016). In steps where plastic centrifugal tubes were unavoidable, plasticizer-free Nalgene centrifuge tubes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 2009) were utilized to minimize potential background interference.

Sample preparation

Canned liquid samples (pomegranate juice, cola and green tea) and canned solid samples (fish, pork and milk powder) were purchased from a local market (Zaozhuang, China), with six brands collected for each sample type.

To eliminate the interference of insoluble components, all liquid samples were filtered through PVDF membrane (ϕ = 13 mm, 0.22 μm) prior to SBSE.

All solid samples were extracted with methanol prior to SBSE. Fish meat and pork meat were minced and ground to increase the extraction surface area. Fine homogenate (5.0 g) was subjected to extraction with 5 mL of methanol (vortexed for 10 min, centrifuged for 8 min at 4000 rpm). The obtained supernatant was filtered through PVDF membrane (ϕ = 13 mm, 0.22 μm) and diluted to a final volume of 50 mL with ultrapure water. Milk powder (5.0 g) was dissolved in 5 mL of ultrapure water, and the resulting mixture was extracted following the same procedure as for fish and pork.

SBSE procedure

Before extraction, pH value of sample solution was adjusted to 5. Following that, a stir bar coated with biochar/CA was immersed in 50 mL of sample solution. With the assistance of a magnetic stirrer, the BPs in sample solution were extracted by the stir bar under self-stirring for 8 min (500 rpm). After extraction, the stir bar was transferred to a centrifuge tube filled with 1 mL of acetonitrile, and the analytes were eluted by ultrasonication for 10 min.

Results and discussion

Characterization of biochar, CA and biochar/CA

SEM (scanning electron microscopy), XRD (X-ray diffraction), N₂ adsorption-desorption and zeta potential analysis were conducted to characterize biochar, CA and biochar/CA.

Surface structure of biochar, CA and biochar/CA was investigated with SEM. As depicted in Fig. 2a, biochar displays irregular morphology. Upon magnification, distinct grooves on the surface are clearly found (Fig. 2b). Regarding CA, it exhibits macroporous architecture with distinguishable network (Fig. 2c). At higher magnification, well-defined smooth cellulose layer becomes evident (Fig. 2d). After loading biochar, macroporous structure of CA is well maintained (Fig. 2e). Meanwhile, the surface becomes obviously rough, with clearly visible biochar particles (Fig. 2f).Fig. 2SEM images of chicken manure biochar (a), (b) (magnification factor: 500, 3000), CA (c), (d) (magnification factor: 100, 500) and biochar/CA (e), (f) (magnification factor: 100, 500).Fig. 2

Crystal features were revealed with XRD. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, a broad diffraction peak at around 25° can be found, which is consistent with the typical plane (002) of graphene (Li et al., 2018). This suggests that chicken manure was partially carbonized with some graphite-like domains. As for CA, no obvious peaks were detected, showing that aerogel exhibits an amorphous crystal structure. Upon incorporation of biochar, the broad peak around 25° is observed, demonstrating the successful integration of biochar into the biochar/CA composite.Fig. 3XRD patterns (a) and N2 sorption isotherms (b) of biochar, cellulose aerogel and biochar/cellulose aerogel; zeta potential of biochar (c).Fig. 3

Porous characteristics were studied with N₂ adsorption-desorption analysis. As presented in Fig. 3b, biochar shows a steep rise at low relative pressure (P/P0 < 0.1), revealing the existence of micropores. Besides, biochar also presents a hysteresis loop at higher relative pressure (1.0 > P/P0 > 0.4), suggesting the abundance of mesopores. So, the porous structure of biochar would provide ample binding sites for the extraction of BPs. In terms of CA, the hysteresis loop appears at P/P0 exceeding 0.9, implying that the structure is abundant with large mesopores. As we know, N₂ adsorption-desorption is primarily utilized for characterizing micropores and mesopores. Meanwhile, SEM results confirm that macroporous structure of the aerogel is preserved both before and after biochar loading (Fig. 2c and Fig. 2e). In addition, biochar/CA shows typical biochar features, such as a sharp increase at low pressure, further demonstrating the successful synthesis of biochar/CA composite.

In addition to pore characteristics, nitrogen adsorption-desorption experiments can also provide information of the specific surface area (SSA). The mass of the biochar/CA in each stir bar is approximately 14 mg, of which the biochar loading is about 10 mg. Therefore, the aerogel accounts for 28.6 wt%, while the biochar accounts for 71.4 wt% in the composite material. The measured SSA of pure (CA) and biochar is 5.4 m2/g and 432.5 m2/g, respectively. According to the equation of theoretical SSA (Section 3 of supplementary material: Equation for calculating the theoretical SSA of biochar/CA), theoretical SSA of the composite is calculated to be 310.3 m2/g. Based on the N₂ adsorption-desorption analysis, the measured SSA of biochar/CA composite is 103.5 m2/g, which is lower than the theoretical value. This suggests that the porous structure of biochar is partially blocked by (CA).

Zeta potential of biochar was tested with zeta potential analyzer. As shown in Fig. 3c, biochar is positively charged when pH value is below 3.3, while reverses to negative at pH value above 3.3.

Optimization of the SBSE procedure

In order to achieve favorable extraction performance for BPs, key parameters, including loading amount of biochar, pH value of sample solution, extraction time, type and volume of eluent were fully investigated using extraction recovery as the index. Optimization experiments were carried out using spiked aqueous solution (5000 ng/L for six BPs).

Loading amount of biochar

To obtain desirable extraction performance with minimum amount of biochar, pure CA coated stir bar and biochar/CA coated stir bar with different loading amount (5–15 mg) were investigated. As presented in Fig. 4a, compared with pure CA, extraction recovery of six BPs is dramatically enhanced after loading biochar, which means that biochar acts as the main component for extraction. Regarding biochar/CA, extraction recovery grows rapidly as the loading amount increases from 5 to 10 mg. However, no obvious enhancement of extraction recovery is observed with a further increase to 15 mg, possibly due to the aggregation of excessive biochar. So loading amount of 10 mg was applied for following experiment.Fig. 4Influencing factors on extraction performance: loading amount of biochar (a), pH value of sample solution (b), extraction time (c), type of eluent (d), volume of eluent (e); reusability of biochar/CA coated stir bar (f).Fig. 4

pH value of sample solution

pH value affects ionization state of the analytes as well as surface charge of the sorbent. As depicted in Fig. 3c, the isoelectric point of biochar is 3.3. So, as the main sorbent in our study, biochar is positively charged when pH value is below 3.3, while reverses to negative at pH value above 3.3.

As for BPs, pKa values of the six analytes are around 7.5–9.5 (Table S1 (Section 1 of supplementary material: Chemical properties and structures of target BPs)), suggesting that the analytes exist in the anionic form (pH > pKa + 2), molecular form (pH < pKa-2), or as a mixture of both (pKa-2 < pH < pKa + 2) under different pH conditions. Therefore, pH value was investigated from 1 to 13.

As can be seen in Fig. 4b, extraction recovery of five BPs (BPA, BPE, BPF, BPZ, BPAF) remain at high level as pH value varies from 1 to 7, but falls dramatically with further increase of pH (9–13).

In the pH range of 3.3–7.5, the biochar surface is negatively charged (isoelectric point of biochar is 3.3), while the five target BPs mainly exist as neutral form (pKa values of the five analytes are around 9.5). Therefore, electrostatic interactions are not dominant, and the high extraction performance is mainly controlled by non-electrostatic interactions, including π–π interactions and hydrogen bonding (detailed adsorption mechanism is presented in Section 3.8). In the pH range of 9–13, the fraction of anionic species grows, both the five BPs and biochar are negatively charged, thus the extraction recovery decreases due to the electrostatic repulsion. As for BPS, the trend of extraction recovery differs from the five analytes above, since the pKa value of BPS is 2 units lower. Taking comprehensive consideration of the extraction performance of six BPs, pH value of 5 was selected for the following test.

Extraction time

As known, sufficient extraction time allows adequate contact between the sorbent and the target analytes. So extraction time ranging from 4 to 10 min was studied. As illustrated in Fig. 4c, extraction recovery of six BPs enhances apparently with the extension of extraction time from 4 to 8 min, and then levels off with further increment. Therefore, 8 min was adopted for extraction.

Type and volume of eluent

To achieve efficient desorption effect towards BPs, three commonly used solvents (methanol, acetonitrile and acetone) were evaluated. As shown in Fig. 4d, acetonitrile exhibits superior elution performance. After that, the volume of acetonitrile was further optimized. As given in Fig. 4e, extraction recovery of BPs enhances apparently over the range of 500 to 1000 μL, and then remains constant when elution volume exceeds 1000 μL. Thus, 1000 μL of acetonitrile was chosen in our study.

Comparison of the adsorption performance between commercial and lab-made stir bars

In the SBSE process, adsorption of target analytes is the first and most critical step. BPA was selected as the model analyte to evaluate the adsorption ability of commercial (PDMS) and lab-made (biochar/CA) stir bars. The reasons why BPA was selected are as follows. On one hand, the six target compounds share the characteristic BP structure containing two aromatic rings and phenolic hydroxyl groups, and their molecular dimensions are relatively similar (0.87–0.99 nm, Table S1). On another, BPA is the most widely studied compound among the BP group, and is commonly regarded as the representative member of this class (Rifa et al., 2024). Details of the experiment are given in the supplementary material (Section 4 of supplementary material: Comparison of the adsorption performance between commercial and lab-made stir bars).

As shown in Fig. S1, adsorption ratio of commercial and lab-made stir bars increases with increasing adsorption time. Compared with stir bar coated with PDMS (40 min), the lab-made stir bar (20 min) reaches equilibrium in a shorter time. Meanwhile, adsorption capacity (amount of BPA adsorbed per unit mass of coating) of lab-made stir bar is obviously higher than commercial PDMS stir bar (Table S3). For BPA under the tested conditions, the adsorption results prove that the prepared stir bar coated with biochar/CA is superior to commercial PDMS stir bar in terms of extraction speed and capacity.

Reusability of biochar/CA coated stir bar

The reusability of the prepared stir bar was investigated over five consecutive adsorption and desorption cycles under optimal conditions. After each extraction cycle, the stir bar was rinsed with methanol and dried before being reused in the subsequent cycle. As illustrated in Fig. 4f, extraction recovery remains relatively stable throughout five cycles, implying considerable prospects for reuse.

Method validation

To demonstrate the absence of background interference from BPs, all procedural blanks were prepared using ultrapure water. The operations were carried out with pre-cleaned adsorbents, pre-cleaned glassware and plasticizer-free centrifuge tubes. The chromatograms of ultrapure water after pretreatment are presented in Fig. S2 (Section 5 of supplementary material: Chromatograms of procedural blank experiment). The results show that all BPs were not detectable in the blank experiment.

Under the optimized conditions, the proposed method was validated. Validation data of liquid (pomegranate juice, cola and green tea) and solid (fish, pork and milk powder) samples is listed in Table 1 and Table 2, respectively. To construct matrix-matched calibration curves, BP standards were spiked into six different matrices at various concentrations, followed by the established sample pretreatment procedure. After processing, the samples were analyzed by UPLC-MS/MS. The resulting calibration curves show desirable linearity (r>0.99). Besides, the method is sensitive, limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ) of liquid samples varies from 1.0 to 3.5 and 3.0 to 29 ng/L, LOD and LOQ of solid samples ranges from 11 to 44 and 53 to 384 ng/kg, respectively. To avoid mutual interference among structurally similar BP standards, each individual standard solution was injected separately at a concentration of approximately 250 μg/L, and all six MRM channels were monitored simultaneously. As illustrated in Fig. S3, Fig. S4, Fig. S5, Fig. S6, Fig. S7 and Fig. S8 (Section 6 of supplementary material: Chromatograms of high-concentration BP single standard), the resulting chromatograms demonstrate that each single-standard solution produces a clear response only in its corresponding MRM transition and retention time window, while no detectable signals above the LOD are observed in the channels of the other five BPs. Usually, the extraction speed of column SPE is limited, as the sorbents are tightly packed at bottom of column, resulting in a low sample loading rate. As shown in Table 5, the ratio of sample loading volume to extraction time for column SPE ranges from 0.1 to 3.0 mL/min. Regarding d-SPE and MSPE, the ratio is obviously higher (5.0–10.0 mL/min), since full dispersion of sorbents in solution promotes faster and more effective extraction. As for our method, the ratio is 6.3, comparable to ratio of most d-SPE and MSPE procedures. Meanwhile, our method is easier to perform, since the monolithic stir bar can be easily separated from solution, avoiding the tedious steps of centrifugation or material transfer required in d-SPE and MSPE.

Besides, our SBSE method reduces sorbents (14 mg) and organic solvents (1 mL) consumption. Moreover, the current method exhibits higher sensitivity with lower LOD (1.0–3.5 ng/L for liquid samples, 12–44 ng/kg for solid samples).

Adsorption mechanism

In the biochar/CA composite, biochar serves as the main sorbent. The suitability of biochar for BPs is associated with the mutual interaction forces and pore structure.

Mutual interaction forces

In our study, biochar adsorbs BPs by the following interaction forces. For one thing, at the optimized extraction pH of 5.0, the biochar surface is negatively charged because the pH is above the isoelectric point of 3.3 (Fig. 3c). However, the target BPs (pKa = 7.42–9.84) (Table S1) mainly exist in their molecular forms at this pH value. Therefore, electrostatic attraction is not the dominant adsorption force at this pH value. For another, according to XRD results (Fig. 3a), the biochar contains graphitic domains, and the π-π stacking interactions between graphitic domains and the benzene rings of BPs contribute to the adsorption. In the process of method optimization, extraction recovery of BPAF and BPS is obviously lower than other analytes (Fig. 4). This can be attributed to the presence of strongly electron-withdrawing groups (fluorine atoms in BPAF and a sulfonyl group in BPS), which reduce the electron cloud density on the benzene ring, thereby weakening the π–π stacking interactions. What's more, hydroxyl and carboxyl groups are generated during the carbonization process of biochar (Lopez et al., 2025), which can form hydrogen bonds with the phenol hydroxyl groups of the BPs.

Considering the types of interaction forces, biochar is not a highly specific recognition sorbent for BPs. Nevertheless, the application scope of this study is strictly limited to BPs.

Pore structure of biochar

In terms of nitrogen adsorption-desorption results (Fig. 3b), biochar possesses abundant mesoporous structures. Meanwhile, molecular sizes of all six BPs are below 1 nm (Table S1, Section 1 of supplementary material: Chemical properties and structures of target BPs). Such favorable size compatibility facilitates the penetration of BP molecules into the internal pore sites, thereby promoting adsorption.

Conclusion

A novel SBSE method based on biochar/CA was introduced and evaluated. CA, as an alternative of traditionally applied organic binders, is demonstrated to be a desirable binding agent for SBSE. For one thing, macroporous CA network provides accessible transport channels for analytes. For another, the stir bar coating follows a solvent-free fabrication route, eliminating the use of organic solvents throughout the fabrication process. Furthermore, the SBSE procedure itself significantly minimizes organic solvent consumption (only 1 mL of solvent for desorption) compared with conventional SPE techniques. In combination with UPLC-MS/MS, the proposed method was successfully employed for the analysis of BPs in canned food samples. To sum up, the established method is rapid and sensitive, while significantly reducing organic solvent consumption in SBSE, presenting an innovative approach for sample preparation.
