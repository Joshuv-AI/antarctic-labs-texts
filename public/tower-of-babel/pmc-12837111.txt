Spatio-temporal trends of insecticide resistance in Indian malaria vectors (2000–2024): a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Rajit Mullick, Chander Prakash Yadav, Richa Singhal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInsecticide resistance in malaria vectors poses a significant threat to the effectiveness of current vector control strategies. The prolonged use of insecticides in Long Lasting Insecticidal Nets (LLIN), Insecticide Treated Nets (ITNs), and Indoor Residual Spraying (IRS) has led to the emergence of resistance among Anopheles mosquito population. This study aims to evaluate the spatial and temporal insecticide resistance trends against dichloro-diphenyl-trichloroethane (DDT), malathion, and deltamethrin among the malaria vectors prevalent in India. Methods A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus using Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) terms and keywords. The studies meeting the predefined eligibility criteria, reporting state or district-level insecticide resistance, and published from the year 2000 to 2024 were included for systematic review and meta-analysis. Results A total of 51 studies selected according to the predefined eligibility criteria were included. The results of the meta-analysis highlighted the prevalence of resistance to DDT in almost all Indian states, with the north-eastern region being an exception. Anopheles culicifacies showed very low susceptibility to DDT, whereas Anopheles stephensi, and Anopheles fluviatilis displayed moderate resistance, with notable regional variation. Resistance to Deltamethrin was majorly noticed in Chhattisgarh and Madhya Pradesh with an average percent mortality of 89% (95% CI: 79% − 94%) reported in Anopheles culicifacies, 93% (95% CI: 78% − 98%) in Anopheles stephensi, and 100% (95% CI: 99% − 100%) in Anopheles fluviatialis. Anopheles culicifacies displayed resistance to deltamethrin in some regions, while Anopheles stephensi and Anopheles fluviatilis were highly susceptible to deltamethrin with notable regional variations. Anopheles culicifacies and Anopheles stephensi were highly resistant against Malathion, while Anopheles fluviatialis displayed complete susceptibility. While Anopheles. sundaicus, Anopheles minimus, and Anopheles baimaii were found to be susceptible to all three insecticides. Conclusion Resistance to DDT and Malathion was particularly pronounced across India, whereas Deltamethrin was mostly resistant in the central states. Malaria vectors in the north-eastern states generally remained susceptible, suggesting opportunities for targeted intervention. This study underscores the need for continuous surveillance and monitoring of vector population, disease prevalence, and utilization of personal protection vector management tools like LLIN.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25977-y.Keywords: Malaria, Anopheles, Resistance, Insecticide, India

Introduction

Malaria is a mosquito-borne infectious disease caused by the Plasmodium parasite. It remains a major global health challenge, particularly in regions with warm climate and high humidity [1], including countries such as India. One significant factor impacting malaria control efforts is the development of insecticide resistance in mosquito. Insecticide resistance refers to the ability of mosquito to survive the lethal doses of insecticides commonly used to control their population [2]. Prolonged and intensive use of insecticides in malaria endemic areas, where Long Lasting Insecticidal Nets (LLINs) and Indoor Residual Spraying (IRS) are being used as vector control tool, has resulted in the emergence of resistance. Previous studies reported various mechanisms that had led to the development of resistance phenotypes [3]. Resistance can arise through a variety of biological processes that alter how vectors respond to insecticidal pressure [4]. Such processes whether occurring individually or in combination can lead to substantial reductions in susceptibility and may result in high levels of resistance across multiple insecticide groups. The use of DDT and synthetic pyrethroids has led to the emergence of knockdown resistance (kdr), mutation mainly the L1014F and L1014S in An. stephensi across various states in India, highlighting the need for region specific management strategies [5]. For instance co-occurrence of the L1014F and L1014S kdr mutations was observed in An. culicifacies populations across the Indian states of Chhattisgarh and Odisha, but at lower frequencies, primarily in heterozygous genotypes and were significantly associated with Deltamethrin and DDT resistance [6].

In India, various classes of insecticides have been used to target and control the population of malaria vectors. The selection of insecticides is influenced by factors such as education, farming practices (e.g., types of crops grown and pesticide usage), various traditional and domestic vector control strategies, availability, and cost-effectiveness [7]. Frequent use of pyrethroids in different formulations for agricultural purposes has led to the emergence of pyrethroid resistance in the An. gambiae population in Tanzania [8].

Studies conducted in Asian countries had reported varying degrees of resistance to DDT, Malathion and some pyrethroids accounting for less than 80% mortality[9]. Historically, India has used various classes of insecticides, such as organochlorines (mainly DDT), organophosphates (which include malathion and fenitrothion) and pyrethroids (deltamethrin, permethrin, and alpha-cypermethrin, which are widely used for IRS and LLINs, for malaria vector control (Fig. 1). However, resistance to these insecticides has been reported in different parts of India [10]. Implementation of the right intervention using the available resources is crucial, including the insecticide resistance management (IRM) plan in selecting insecticide, using an insecticide rotational system during interventions in the field, and regular insecticide resistance monitoring [11].

A chronological representation of the introduction and utilization of different insecticides in India

In this study, we have systematically collected and analysed all available peer-reviewed literature from India over the past 24 years reporting susceptibility of malaria vectors to key classes of insecticides. By conducting a meta-analysis and presenting state-wise average mortality rates, we aim to highlight spatial and temporal trends of insecticide resistance and susceptibility. The findings of this study are expected to give an insight to the national level programmes, which can aid in modifying the current vector control strategies in India. Improvement and modification in the current vector management strategies and emphasizing more on the areas that need special attention will aid in the elimination efforts.

Methods

Systematic review and meta-analysis were performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis 2020[12] (PRISMA) (Supplementary Table 1). The review was prospectively registered on Generalized Systematic Review Registration of OSF registry on the 14th of March 2024 (10.17605/OSF.IO/DT76R).

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted through three databases, PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus, by using the combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and keywords such as malaria, insecticide resistance, vector resistance, Anopheles, etc. The reference lists of the selected studies were also searched manually for additional studies. The literature search was performed by two authors, RM and RS, independently, and disagreements, if any, were solved in consultation with the third author, CPY. The filters on language being English and date of publication between January 2000 to January 2024 were applied during the search. This period was selected as the first report of resistance from India was in the year 2000. The search strategy and selection criteria were first validated before their implementation (Supplementary Table 2).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The original studies, reporting the resistance and susceptibility status of major malaria vectors, namely An. culicifacies, An. stephensi, An. fluviatialis, An. sundaicus, An. minimus and An. baimaii against the insecticides, namely DDT, Malathion, and Deltamethrin, were included in the review. Conference abstracts and grey literature were excluded.The studies lacking the district-wise data and reporting the results for the whole of India were excluded. The studies about genetic alteration leading to resistance and those reporting the data on drug resistance in malaria were excluded. Further, the studies reporting the resistance in laboratory-based experiments rather than field-based results were also excluded, except where field mosquitoes were collected and reared in vitro for F1 generations. (Supplementary Table 3).

Data extraction

The database of all the studies was created in the reference manager software Zotero, which was also used for removing duplicates. Thereafter, all the studies were screened based on their titles and abstracts. Full text of the studies that were found relevant in the title and abstract search was assessed for eligibility based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. From the studies found eligible, pertinent information was extracted using a data extraction tool and entered into MS Excel. The extracted data included the author’s name, year of publication, study period, state, district, insecticide used, name of mosquito species, number of Anopheles mosquitoes collected, and mortality rate. (Supplementary Table 4)

Data analysis

Data extracted from eligible studies were synthesized quantitatively according to their mortality rate as per WHO guidelines [9]. As per these guidelines, mortality of less than 90% is classified as resistance, 90% to 97% mortality is termed as possible resistance, and mortality of more than 98% is characterized as susceptible.

The average percent mortality of malaria vectors under study against the three insecticides DDT, Malathion, and Deltamethrin was pooled using the random effects model and common effect model as per the level of heterogeneity. I2 statistics was used to evaluate the statistical heterogeneity among the studies. Random effects meta-analysis was used if the I2 value was more than 40% to substantiate the effect of variability in the studies. The meta-analysis was performed using the meta package in R. The spatial and temporal distribution of insecticide resistance across Indian states was plotted in Geographical Information System (GIS) maps using the open-source software QGIS version 3.30. The Publication Bias was assessed using funnel plots in R.

Results

The literature search from the three databases reported 336 articles (PubMed-119, Scopus-102 and Web of Science-115). Following the removal of 132 duplicates, 204 articles were retrieved and screened based on their titles and abstracts, 108 records were selected for full-text review, of which 51 studies met the selection criteria and were included in the final analysis. (Fig. 2). Resistance to DDT was reported in one (01) study only [13]. The studies which reported resistance against both DDT and Deltamethrin were six (06) [14–19], while those reporting resistance against both DDT and Malathion were three (03) [20–22]. It was observed that 30 studies[23–51] reported resistance against all the three insecticides under study. Resistance against Deltamethrin alone was reported in ten (10) [52–60] studies, while one (01) [61] study reported resistance against both Deltamethrin and Malathion.

PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) flowchart of the included studies in the review

Data obtained from all the selected studies were analysed for the prevalence of insecticide resistance in malaria vectors. The study primarily focused on evaluating the average mortality status across various Indian states against individual insecticide using forest plot and GIS map to depict the resistance intensity or susceptibility in a particular state. GIS mapping has also been used to depict the temporal changes in the resistance pattern of last two decades across different states.

Insecticide resistance in major vectors

A. DDT

Spatial resistance spread

Resistance against DDT was highly prominent in the common malaria vectors, i.e., An. culicifacies, An. stephensi, An. fluviatialis. The highest level of resistance to DDT was reported in An. culicifacies from the states of West Bengal and Karnataka, where average mortality was found to be 5% and 6% respectively. The average mortality against DDT across all study regions was found to be 25% (95% CI: 16% − 36%) (Fig:3 A), indicating the insecticide’s diminished efficacy against this vector. Although fewer studies have been conducted on An. stephensi, yet resistance was found to be notably pronounced in the states of Goa, Haryana, and Uttar Pradesh, with an average mortality rate of 51% (95% CI: 29%−73%) (Fig:3B) across the study sites. An. fluviatialis, a key vector predominantly inhabiting forest-fringed regions, demonstrated resistance to DDT at all study sites (average mortality − 57% (95% CI: 15%−91%)) except in the eastern coastal state of Odisha, where susceptibility was observed (average mortality – 99%) (Fig:3 C).

Map depicting spatio-temporal spread of resistance

In contrast, DDT maintained high efficacy against other vector species viz., Anopheles sundaicus, An. minimus, and An. baimaii particularly in the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and in north-eastern states, where the average mortality remained at 99% (95% CI: 98%−99%) across study regions. However, some states reported a possible risk of resistance against DDT among these vectors in the near future, wherein an average mortality of 93–97% was reported in the states of Odisha, Jharkhand, and Tripura (Fig: 3D).

Temporal resistance spread

During the last two decades, the earliest documented case of DDT resistance was reported from the western state of Gujarat in 2000, where An. culicifacies exhibited a mortality of only 6.7%. This was followed by Uttar Pradesh in 2001, reporting a mortality of 15.5%. By 2002, resistance had rapidly emerged in several neighbouring states, including Chhattisgarh (13.8%), Odisha (21.1%), and Uttarakhand (24.3%). By 2010, DDT resistance had become widespread across nearly all malaria-endemic states, with An. culicifacies in Madhya Pradesh showing 15% mortality, while Assam reporting 70% mortality. By the year 2019, resistance had extended to the Himalayan region, with An. culicifacies in Himachal Pradesh exhibiting a mortality of 24.6% (Supplementary Fig. 1).

B. Malathion

Spatial resistance spread

Malathion is an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor, and resistance was prominently observed in two major malaria vectors viz., An.culicifacies and An.stephensi. Highest degree of resistance against An.culicifacies was exhibited in the southern states of Andhra Pradesh and Telangana. While the neighbouring state of Tamil Nadu reported susceptibility, with 100% mortality. The average mortality across all study sites for An.culicifacies was 83% (95% CI: 74%–89%), indicating suboptimal effectiveness of Malathion against this vector. Susceptibility was recorded in Himachal Pradesh and Tamil Nadu, while emerging resistance in An.culicifacies was reported from Uttarakhand, Uttar Pradesh, and Jharkhand, highlighting the need for more studies on resistance monitoring (Fig. 4A).

Similarly, An.stephensi demonstrated resistance in the states of Goa, Haryana, and Gujarat, with an average mortality of 80% (95% CI: 47%–95%) (Fig. 4B) across study locations. While it remained highly effective against An. fluviatialis, with an average mortality of 99% (95% CI: 95%–100%), except in Maharashtra, where resistance (83% average mortality) was observed (Fig. 4 C).

Complete susceptibility to Malathion was reported in An. sundaicus and An. baimaii, with 100% mortality across study regions, particularly in the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and the north-eastern states (Fig. 4D). These findings underscores the growing resistance in primary vector species and regional variability in insecticide efficacy.

Temporal resistance spread

During the study period, the first documented case of resistance to Malathion was reported from Gujarat in 2000, where An.stephensi exhibited a mortality rate of 51.7%. The spread of Malathion resistance was multidirectional across the years. In the early 2000 s, resistance had expanded to the northern state of Uttarakhand (An.culicifacies 90% mortality), the central region of Chhattisgarh (An.culicifacies – 74.1%), and the eastern state of Odisha (An.culicifacies – 50%). By 2010, resistance was established across a broader geographical area as reported from southern states of India, viz., Karnataka (An.stephensi – 54.9%), Telangana (An.culicifacies – 63.3%), and Andhra Pradesh (An.culicifacies – 46.6%). Concurrently, high resistance levels were also detected in the eastern and central states of India, viz., West Bengal (An.culicifacies – 90.8%) and Madhya Pradesh (An.culicifacies – 74.9%). By 2020, the resistance had become widespread across nearly all malaria-endemic states. By the year 2018, An. culicifacies in Uttar Pradesh showed a mortality of 93.1%, and An.stephensi in Haryana with 46.6% mortality. In the east, Jharkhand reported An. culicifacies mortality at 69%, and in the northeast, Assam recorded 91.8% mortality. This nationwide resistance during the last two decades underscores the declining efficacy of Malathion and the urgent need for adaptive and evidence-based vector control strategies. (Supplementary Fig. 2).

C. Deltamethrin

Spatial resistance spread

Deltamethrin, a new-age synthetic pyrethroid, was more effective against the malaria vectors across many states. The highest degree of resistance in An. culicifacies was reported in the southern states of Karnataka and Telangana. Average mortality across all the sites was found to be 89% (95% CI: 79%−94%) (Fig. 5A), making it effective against An. culicifacies, however, reduced mortality reported is indicative of future risk of resistance development. An. stephensi reported resistance in the states of Karnataka, Goa, and Haryana. Average mortality was found to be 93% (95% CI: 78%−98%) (Fig. 5B), which is indicative of the effectiveness of the deltamethrin but also raises an alarming sign of the development of future resistance. Deltamethrin was found to be effective against An. fluviatialis (Fig. 5C), An. sundaicus and An. minimus across all the regions under study, with an average mortality of 100% namely Andaman & Nicobar islands, Assam, Jharkhand, Odisha, and Tripura (Fig. 5D).

A An. culicifacies, (B) An. stephensi, (C) An. fluviatialis and (D) other vectors (An. baimaii, An. minimus and An. sundaicus). n represents the observed mortality, N is the total number of mosquitoes collected Map depicting spatio-temporal spread of resistance

An. culicifacies showed maximum resistance in Karnataka (30% mortality) followed by Telangana (43% mortality). Possible resistance was observed in the states of Tamil Nadu (94%), Odisha (91%), Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, and Assam, while susceptibility was reported in Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, and the eastern state of West Bengal. An. stephensi was susceptible in the states of Gujarat, Rajasthan, and Uttar Pradesh, whereas Haryana (81%), Karnataka (65%), and Goa (85%) reported resistance to Deltamethrin. An. fluviatialis along with An. minimus and An. sundaicus were susceptible across all the states (Odisha, Maharashtra, Himachal Pradesh, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh) where studies were conducted.

Temporal resistance spread

The earliest evidence of resistance to Deltamethrin emerged in Gujarat in 2000, where An. culicifacies exhibited a mortality rate of 69.2% [15]. Over the following years, resistance was detected in neighbouring and geographically diverse regions. During the year 2002, Chhattisgarh reported An. culicifacies mortality of 89.5%, while Odisha reported the mortality of 81.7%. Karnataka documented resistance in An. stephensi with 88% mortality. By 2010, the spread had extended in Rajasthan (An. stephensi – 94.11% mortality), Andhra Pradesh (An. culicifacies – 93.3%) and Telangana (An. culicifacies – 43.3%), Madhya Pradesh (An. culicifacies – 71.6%), and in Assam, where An. culicifacies mortality was as low as 30%. By 2018, Deltamethrin resistance had become widespread across the entire Indian subcontinent. Resistance was recorded in Uttar Pradesh (An. culicifacies – 91.5%) and Haryana (An. culicifacies – 78.7%) in northern India, Jharkhand (An. culicifacies – 85%) in the east, and Maharashtra (An. culicifacies – 94%) in the west. This extensive temporal and spatial spread suggests that Deltamethrin, although still relatively effective, but under increasing selective pressure and may face declining efficacy in the near future (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Publication bias analysis

The publication bias was analysed using funnel plots. Analysis revealed varying degrees of asymmetry for DDT, Malathion, and Deltamethrin, suggesting differential risks of publication bias (Supplementary Fig. 4). For DDT (Supp. Fig. 4 A), the funnel plot demonstrated pronounced asymmetry, with a clustering of smaller studies reporting negative log odds indicative of higher resistance and a notable absence of small studies reporting high susceptibility. This pattern suggests potential publication bias, particularly underreporting of studies with more favourable efficacy outcomes.

The funnel plot for Malathion (Supp. Fig. 4B) also showed asymmetry, though to a lesser extent. The presence of small studies with disproportionately low efficacy and a few outliers with high susceptibility suggests possible small-study effects, compounded by variability due to differences in vector species, geographic location, or bioassay protocols.

In contrast, the funnel plot for Deltamethrin (Supp. Fig. 4C) appeared largely symmetric, with studies evenly distributed around the pooled log odds ratio and falling within expected confidence bounds. This pattern indicates a lower likelihood of publication bias and consistent reporting across study sizes, supporting the robustness of pooled efficacy estimates for Deltamethrin.

Discussion

It is evident from the results of this study that the major malaria vectors like An. culicifacies and An. stephensi are resistant to DDT and Malathion, but susceptible to Deltamethrin. However, the less common vectors viz.; An. fluviatialis, An. minimus and An. sundaicus were found to be susceptible to all the three insecticides.

The emergence and spread of resistance to DDT among malaria vectors in India presents a significant obstacle to the country’s malaria elimination efforts. An. culicifacies, one of the primary malaria vectors in India, has shown pronounced resistance to DDT, particularly in the states of West Bengal and Karnataka [26]. Since its extensive use began in the 1950 s, DDT’s efficacy has declined steadily, as evidenced by growing resistance trends [10].

The situation in Madhya Pradesh further underscores this concern, where resistance to DDT in An. culicifacies has been well documented [23]. The early detection of resistance in Gujarat around 2000 marked the beginning of a broader geographic spread, suggesting that resistance has not only emerged but has also become entrenched in several regions.

In contrast, other vector species such as An. fluviatilis, An. sundaicus, An. minimus, and An. baimaii have remained largely susceptible to DDT, particularly in the northeast and the Andaman & Nicobar Islands, with mortality rates consistently exceeding 99%. Odisha remains an exception, where An. fluviatilis has begun to show early signs of reduced susceptibility [26]. These regional differences highlight the importance of species-specific and geographically tailored surveillance.

The persistence of DDT resistance in An. culicifacies raises serious concerns about the continued use of this insecticide in vector control programs. Its reduced effectiveness could contribute to increased vector survival, expansion of vector populations, and, ultimately, elevated malaria transmission risk [24].

The increasing resistance to malathion, an organophosphate insecticide and acetylcholinesterase inhibitor, presents another serious challenge to malaria vector control efforts in India. Once widely effective, malathion has shown declining efficacy across several regions over the past two decades. Notably, An. culicifacies populations have demonstrated resistance in various southern states, while in contrast, the northern state of Himachal Pradesh has largely retained full susceptibility [24].

An. stephensi has also shown signs of resistance to malathion, with confirmed cases reported in Goa, Gujarat, and Haryana [26, 27]. Meanwhile, An. fluviatilis remains predominantly susceptible, with the exception of Maharashtra where resistance has been detected [35]. Other vectors such as An. sundaicus and An. baimaii continue to exhibit full susceptibility, particularly in north-eastern and island regions, reflecting regional variability in selection pressure and insecticide exposure.

The trajectory of malathion resistance suggests a multidirectional spread, beginning in Gujarat in the early 2000 s and expanding progressively to states such as Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, and Madhya Pradesh [25, 26]. By 2020, resistance had been documented in multiple regions across the country. Recent reports further indicate an increase in resistance cases in northern states, likely driven by persistent insecticide pressure, changing land use, and shifting vector dynamics potentially influenced by climate variability [23].

These trends emphasize the importance of continuous and geographically targeted insecticide resistance monitoring. The expansion of malathion resistance, especially in major vector species like An. culicifacies and An. stephensi raises concerns about the sustained efficacy of this insecticide in malaria control programs. Reliance on malathion in areas with confirmed resistance may not only reduce operational effectiveness but also contribute to the further spread of resistance alleles.

With the transition from DDT to deltamethrin, particularly through the use of long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs), resistance to deltamethrin has become widespread in India [36]. An. culicifacies has shown reduced susceptibility in Karnataka and Telangana. An. stephensi has exhibited emerging resistance in Karnataka, Goa, and Haryana, though it remains largely susceptible in Gujarat and Rajasthan.

In contrast, species such as An. fluviatilis, An. minimus, and An. sundaicus have retained full susceptibility across surveyed regions. Resistance was first observed in Gujarat in the early 2000 s and gradually spread eastward to Odisha, Chhattisgarh, and northeastern states like Assam. Central states including Chhattisgarh and Uttar Pradesh reported resistance in An. culicifacies during the same period [25, 26], followed by increased resistance in Madhya Pradesh and Andhra Pradesh [28–30] by the late 2000s. An. stephensi resistance also expanded to Maharashtra and Rajasthan [26–29].

By the 2010 s, resistance had become more entrenched across the endemic belt, with additional reports from Jharkhand and Odisha [13]. Declining mortality in An. culicifacies in Madhya Pradesh suggested a growing trend of resistance, potentially driven by continuous pyrethroid use in LLINs, ITNs, and IRS, combined with limited insecticide rotation and possible genetic adaptation. These patterns emphasize the importance of timely resistance surveillance and insecticide management to preserve the effectiveness of deltamethrin based interventions in India’s malaria control strategy.

The consistent and widespread resistance in An. culicifacies, India’s primary rural vector, across all three insecticide classes, is particularly concerning. The spread of resistance from the western and central regions to the northeast and northern belt reflects both ecological drivers and programmatic factors, such as repeated use of the same insecticides and delayed rotation strategies [43].

We also observed varying levels of bias and heterogeneity for each insecticide. DDT has been extensively used for decades in vector control programs, especially in countries like India, leading to widespread resistance and a larger body of observational studies. As a result, studies reporting low effectiveness (high resistance) are more commonly published, while those showing high susceptibility may be seen as less publishable or less relevant, contributing to publication bias. Additionally, older DDT studies may lack rigorous design or standardization, increasing heterogeneity. Malathion is often used as a secondary insecticide, and studies on its efficacy may be influenced by localized usage and resistance mechanisms. Researchers may be more likely to report or publish studies highlighting growing resistance or failure, especially when used as a comparative arm against other insecticides. Smaller studies may also be more prone to methodological limitations or selective reporting, leading to observed small study effects. The widespread use of Deltamethrin in recent years has been accompanied by better quality trials and more balanced reporting, reducing the likelihood of publication bias. Furthermore, since resistance to Deltamethrin may be less widespread or only recently nascent in many regions, studies reporting both high and low efficacy are seen as scientifically valuable and are equally likely to be published. This contributes to the more symmetrical and robust evidence base seen in its funnel plot.

These findings have significant implications for India’s malaria elimination goals under the National Framework for Malaria Elimination (NFME 2016–2030) [11]. The widespread inefficacy of DDT and Malathion, coupled with development of resistance to pyrethroids like Deltamethrin, calls for an urgent shift toward integrated vector management (IVM). This includes insecticide rotation, deployment of dual-active ingredient LLINs, real-time resistance surveillance, and the adoption of non-chemical or biological control strategies [11, 62].

Finally, this review demonstrates an alarming trend of rising insecticide resistance in India’s primary malaria vectors, threatening the sustainability of existing control tools. Immediate investment in resistance monitoring, innovation in vector control technologies, and strategic and evidence based implementation of integrated vector management tools are essential to safeguard current gains and achieve malaria elimination by 2030.

Limitation of the study

In this study, only percentage mortality against a specific insecticide was reported without accompanying data on the total number of mosquitoes tested and the number of dead mosquitoes. The same has been suggested by Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidelines67. This approach allowed for the inclusion of more studies in the meta-analysis while maintaining methodological consistency.

For the meta-analysis, heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the I² statistic. When I² exceeded 40%, indicating moderate to high heterogeneity, a random effects model was applied to account for variability across studies. In contrast, when heterogeneity was low (I² ≤ 40%), a common (fixed) effects model was used, assuming a consistent effect size across studies. This methodological distinction ensures more accurate and reliable summary estimates, reflecting both within and between study variability as appropriate. The meta-analysis has been done only on those studies which were published and the unpublished literature which may have some negative findings has not contributed to results.

Conclusion

Reduced effectiveness of insecticides like DDT, deltamethrin, and malathion are a serious obstacle in India’s goal of malaria elimination by 2030. The study revealed that there is widespread resistance to DDT and Malathion across India, while emerging resistance has been noted in Deltamethrin. Resistance is significant in the north-eatsern states of India, which needs to be addressed on priority. Since, the north-eastern states are mostly malaria endemic, there is a growing need of introduction of integrated and new vector control tools in this regions. To counter the resistance India must implement regular monitoring of vector susceptibility to insecticides. This could be achieved by creation of interactive dashboard for data visualisation and combatting future risk [63]. Implementing IRM (insecticide-resistant management) practices like rotating insecticides, combining methods, and treating breeding sites with multiple strategies can slow down the resistance development. Evidence based insecticide application in endemic and outbreak conditions will also help to reduce the resistance phenomenon. Exploring and adopting new tools like bio-larvicides, bed nets treated with different insecticides, and genetic modification techniques hold promise.

Overall, combating malaria vector resistance requires a multi-pronged approach involving continuous monitoring, innovative strategies, and community engagement. Addressing this challenge is crucial for India’s success in achieving its malaria elimination goal by 2030.
