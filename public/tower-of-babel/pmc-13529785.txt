A comprehensive German claims data analysis of care pathways, risk factors and comorbidities among acetylcholinesterase inhibitor initiators in Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Christopher Maas, Laura Fang, Elke Mathey, Christian Jacob, Timotheus Stremel, Tammo Viering, Roland Fliedner, Marlena Schnieder, Christine A. F. von Arnim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early stages of Alzheimer’s Disease (AD), commonly referred to as early AD (eAD), represent a critical phase for timely diagnosis and intervention. However, significant gaps remain in understanding real-world care of individuals initiating first-line treatment such as acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (AChE-I) in Germany. A retrospective study was conducted using the German InGef claims database with a pragmatic proxy approach, identifying de novo AChE-I initiators as individuals suspected of having eAD. Two cohorts were defined: a pre-cohort (2022) capturing eight-years prior to AChE-I initiation, and a post-cohort (2017), analyzing five-years following initiation. Patients were matched 1:1 to controls without AD based on demographic characteristics. Outcomes included comorbidities, risk factors, diagnostics, prescriptions, and survival. The study identified 1,994 (pre-cohort) and 2,051 (post-cohort) AChE-I initiators, and respective controls. Mobility disorders increased significantly (+ 55%) over the eight years preceding treatment. Eight years pre-initiation, 81.9% of initiators exhibited at least one modifiable risk factor compared to 76.2% in controls (p < 0.001). Advanced diagnostic procedures were underutilized (MRI: 39.0%, cerebrospinal fluid analysis: 9.2%, in year −1). Only 78.6% received an AD-specific diagnosis within the first year post-initiation. Extensive use of psycholeptics and psychoanaleptics was observed, with a notable decline in dementia-specific medications post-initiation. Post-initiation, initiators showed higher comorbidity burden (e.g., urinary incontinence: 40.2% vs. 10.7% by year 5) and higher observed, unadjusted mortality (53.3% vs. 21.7%, p < 0.001). This study highlights patterns suggestive of gaps in the management of AChE-I initiators in Germany, including underutilization of guideline-based diagnostics, high comorbidity burden, and higher observed mortality. Improved management strategies and the development of more precise diagnostic coding that reflects disease stages could help to address these issues.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-67494-x.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a major and growing global health challenge worldwide, with its prevalence expected to rise substantially in the coming decades1. In Germany, demographic trends suggest that the number of individuals affected by all-cause dementia could increase from nearly 1.8 million to up to 2.8 million by 20552. As the most common form of primary dementia, AD accounts for 50–70% of all cases in Europe, and is characterized by extracellular amyloid beta plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tau tangles along with neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative processes3–7. Early Alzheimer’s disease (eAD), encompassing mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and mild dementia due to AD, represents a critical phase where timely diagnosis and intervention is essential, as cognitive impairments are present but daily functioning is still largely preserved8,9, which can improve outcomes and healthcare resource allocation for people living with AD2.

Despite the significant burden of AD, therapeutic options in Europe as of Q1 2025 remain limited to symptomatic treatments, such as acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (AChE-I) and memantine, which do not directly address the underlying neuropathological progression of AD10. Recently, the therapeutic landscape has begun to shift with the approval of Lecanemab and Donanemab in the EU, marking the first disease-modifying treatments targeting the underlying amyloid pathology of Alzheimer’s disease. Notably, these approvals occurred after the completion of our study. According to German and international guidelines, AChE-I are recommended as first-line pharmacological treatment for people living with mild AD dementia11–14. These medications are typically initiated when cognitive symptoms become clinically apparent but before substantial loss of independence, making the start of AChE-I therapy a practical, albeit approximate, marker for the early stages of AD in real-world data. It should be noted that AChE-I are approved for mild to moderate AD dementia and are not indicated for MCI15,16.

A major challenge in studying eAD lies in the limitations of the ICD-10-GM classification system. It provides only diagnostic codes for Alzheimer’s disease, without distinguishing between biomarker-confirmed and clinically suspected cases, nor does it allow for stage-specific categorisation. This lack of diagnostic granularity significantly impedes the accurate identification and tracking of disease stages in claims data. As a result, there are significant gaps in understanding real-world patient care pathways, risk factors, and comorbidities in this population, particularly from the perspective of the Statutory Health Insurance (SHI).

To address these evidence gaps, this study analyzes claims data from the InGef research database, using the initiation of AChE-I therapy as a proxy to identify individuals suspected of having eAD.

By evaluating comorbidities, risk factors, diagnostic and treatment patterns, this study aims to provide a comprehensive overview of care among AChE-I initiators in the German health care system and inform strategies for earlier and improved diagnostics, treatments, and care, and better resource allocation for this vulnerable population.

Methods

Data source

This study was based on SHI claims data from the InGef research database. The database contains health insurance claims for approximately 4 million insured individuals from more than 50 different health insurances. While claims data, primarily collected for reimbursement purposes, may not capture every medical condition comprehensively, it plays a valuable role in healthcare research, offering a consistent dataset due to its structured, reimbursement-driven origins. The dataset represents about 5% of the German population and is adapted to its structure in terms of age, sex, and region/federal state of residence (structure according to the Federal Statistical Office of Germany (DESTATIS)) and further provides a good representation of morbidity, mortality and drug use17.

Study design & study population

A non-interventional retrospective claims data analysis was conducted covering the period from January 1, 2014, to December 31, 2022. Given the absence of stage-specific coding, the study population was identified via de novo AChE-I prescriptions, using the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) Classification System codes for AChE-I as a pragmatic proxy criterion for individuals with eAD. As AChE-I therapies may also be initiated in moderate stages of the disease, this identification method can be regarded as an approximation of eAD based on claims data, tailored to address the aims of the study. Therefore, the identified patients of the study populations are designated as AChE-I initiators with suspected eAD.

To describe patterns before and after treatment initiation, two patient cohorts were identified: a cohort identified in the year 2022 providing an eight‑year pre‑observation period prior to AChE-I initiation (pre‑cohort) and a cohort identified in 2017 providing a five‑year post‑observation period following AChE-I initiation (post‑cohort). The two index years (2017 and 2022) were selected to allow sufficient follow‑up and look‑back periods. These cohorts are intended to provide descriptive snapshots at different calendar times. The first AChE-I prescription in the respective year was defined as the index event and only patients without any AChE-I prescriptions prior to the index date were included. For the pre-cohort, an eight-year pre-observation period was implemented to examine the care trajectory, documented comorbidities, risk factors, and diagnostic patterns in the years leading up to AChE-I initiation. Matching

A matched comparison analysis was performed with exact direct matching without replacement to compare outcomes between AChE-I initiators with suspected eAD in the pre- and post-cohorts and matched controls without evidence of AD. Each patient in the respective AChE-I initiator cohort was matched 1:1 with a control patient without AD (no AD and related diagnosis and no AD related prescriptions). Matching criteria included age, sex, place of residence, urbanization and insurance status. Potential controls had to fulfil the same observability criteria as the AChE-I initiator cohorts (see Fig. 2).

Potentially modifiable risk factors were operationalized by predefined groups of specific ICD-10-GM codes on a 3-digit level indicating presence of such factors. Patients were classified as having a specific risk factor if they had at least one diagnosis in the inpatient sector (primary or secondary diagnosis) or at least two verified diagnoses in the outpatient sector (within the reimbursement process this is marked with “Zusatzkennzeichen Diagnosesicherheit” G) corresponding to any of the ICD-10-GM codes with that risk factor (see list of codes in Additional file 1). The groups of potentially modifiable and preventable risk factors included in our analysis were derived from the German S3 dementia guideline, which in turn references the report of the Lancet Commission (2020) and the publication by Yu et al.11,18,19. Based on these sources, the following risk factors were considered: Arterial hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cerebrovascular disease, frailty, stress, hearing loss, atrial fibrillation, poor sleep, cognitive symptom coding, obesity, smoking, social isolation, head trauma, traumatic brain injury, weight loss, alcohol, orthostatic hypotension, hyperhomocysteinaemia, air pollution, low physical activity and vitamin c deficiency.

Indication-specific prescriptions were identified through ATC codes. Psychoanaleptics (including antidementia drugs and antidepressants) and psycholeptics (including antipsychotics, anxiolytics, and sedatives) manage behavioral and mood disturbances. They were analyzed as a subset of specific codes of ATC chapter N05 (e.g. risperidone) and specific codes of N06 (e.g. mirtazapine) relevant for patients with AD, respectively. Additional analyzed drug classes comprised analgesics, urinary agents, anticoagulants and diuretics that address pain and bladder issues as well as prevalent conditions like hypertension (see complete list in Additional file 1).

Indication-specific diagnostics were identified using OPS (German Surgery and Procedure Coding System) and EBM (German Uniform Assessment Standard) codes and analyzed in combination. While EBM codes are used exclusively in the outpatient care setting, OPS codes are primarily used in the inpatient setting. Diagnostic procedures were categorized by EBM/OPS code groups of basic and advanced diagnostic procedures. It is important to consider that these procedures are applied across a broad spectrum of medical conditions, with AD representing only a small proportion. Guideline-conform diagnosis of AD involves a comprehensive evaluation using clinical, laboratory, imaging, and neuropsychological methods to accurately identify the underlying causes of dementia11. Methods can be classified as basic and advanced diagnostics procedures. The categories of basic diagnostic procedures (test procedures for suspected dementia, psychometric test procedures and serum/plasma tests) and advanced diagnostic procedures (CT, MRI, electroencephalography (EEG), sonography, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) diagnostics, genetic biomarker, neurography, SPECT, PET, evoked potentials) were defined in accordance with guideline10 as well as common methods within neurological practice. Additional OPS diagnostic procedures codes were grouped in “other” and comprised “Computer-aided image data analysis with 3D evaluation” and “Virtual 3D reconstruction technology” (see complete list in Additional file 1).

Statistical analysis

The outcome analyses for the pre- and post-cohorts were conducted longitudinally in patient’s individual pre- and post-observation periods on a year-by-year basis.

For the post-cohort, outcomes were assessed for the follow-up years (year 1 until year 5) following the index date in 2017, with each individual observation year comprising 365 days, and comorbidity assessments based on quarterly defined periods. Similarly, the pre-cohort outcomes were evaluated for the pre-observation years (year −8 until year −1) preceding the index date in 2022. However, the comorbidity assessments were based on quarterly defined periods (one observation year included 4 quarters), since information on ICD-10-GM diagnoses in the outpatient setting is only available per quarter. For the post-cohort, deceased patients were right censored in the respective year of death.

Furthermore, a Kaplan–Meier analysis for survival was conducted. For statistical testing, t test and chi-square test were used with a significance level of 0.05.

Ethics statement

Claims data from the participating SHIs are joined in a specialized trust centre, anonymized, and transferred to InGef before the data are made available for research. The analysis of claims data from the SHI is fully compliant with German federal law and in accordance with the “GPS—Good Practice in Secondary Data Analysis” (Guideline 1: Ethics), the approval of an ethics committee and informed consent of the patients were not required. For this reason, an assessment was not requested from an ethics committee for this study.

Results

Patient identification

The patient selection process resulted in the identification of 1,997 patients for the pre-cohort (2022) and 2,054 for the post-cohort (2017). Subsequent matching refined these groups to include 1,994 AChE-I initiators with suspected eAD and 1,994 controls in the pre-cohort, while the post-cohort comprised 2,051 AChE-I initiators with suspected eAD and an equal number of controls. Figure 2 illustrates the selection steps along with the corresponding patient numbers.

Demographic characteristics

In both cohorts, there was a higher proportion of women than men, with women accounting for 58.9% in the post-cohort and 56.5% in the pre-cohort. The age distribution showed that the largest group included individuals aged 80 to < 90 years, with 45.3% in the post-cohort and 53.1% in the pre-cohort. The mean age of patients in the post-cohort was 79.8 years (median = 80 years; SD = 7.1 years), and 80.1 years (median = 81 years; SD = 6.9 years) in the pre-cohort. Female patients were on average slightly older than male patients, with a mean age of 80.2 years vs. 79.3 years in the post-cohort and 80.7 years vs. 79.3 years in the pre-cohort.

AChE-I initiators pre-cohort results

AD diagnoses and comorbidities

The coded prevalence of dementia among AChE-I initiators with suspected eAD increased significantly from 7.5% in year −8 to 35.9% in year −1. Examining AD-specific diagnoses using ICD-10-GM codes prior to the first treatment, the diagnosis rate was 20.5% (409 patients) in the year immediately preceding treatment (year −1, index event), compared to 8.4% in the year before (year −2). Eight years prior (year −8), only 0.9% of the patients showed AD-specific diagnoses. Diagnostics

In the pre-cohort, 94.2% of AChE-I initiators underwent at least one basic diagnostic procedure in year −1, that included test procedures for suspected dementia and blood tests as coded by EBM as well as general psychiatric OPS codes that encompass the corresponding basic diagnostic for AD. This represents a notable increase from year −8, when 78.9% of the AChE-I initiators received such diagnostics. In both years, these rates were significantly higher than those observed in the matched control cohort, where 75.5% (year −1) and 73.5% (year −8) of control patients received basic diagnostics (p < 0.001 for both years). However, it should be noted that these procedures are commonly used for a variety of medical conditions, not exclusively for AD.

Advanced diagnostic procedures, as identified by EBM and OPS codes, were performed in 75.5% of pre-cohort patients in year −1, up from 35.4% of patients in year − 8. Among control patients, advanced diagnostic procedures were performed in 35.5% (year −1) and 28.3% (year −8), a statistically significant difference to patients with AChE-I initiators in both years (p < 0.001 for both years). Notably, CSF diagnostics showed one of the largest relative increases among advanced diagnostic procedures (by EBM and OPS) rising from 1.2% of AChE-I initiators in year −8 to 9.2% in year −1, compared to 2.5% of controls in year −1 (p < 0.001), with the control cohort starting at 1.5% in year −8 (p = 0.489). MRI (by EBM and OPS) was the most frequently used advanced diagnostic procedure in year −1, performed in 39.0% of AChE-I initiators in the pre-cohort versus 3.7% of controls (p < 0.001). This represents a substantial increase from year −8, when 4.6% of patients in the AChE-I initiators pre-cohort and 2.9% of controls underwent MRI (p = 0.004). CT scans (by EBM and OPS) were also common, with 28.7% of patients in the AChE-I initiators pre-cohort receiving a CT in year −1, compared to 3.5% of controls (p < 0.001). This represents an increase from 4.1% (AChE-I initiators pre-cohort) and 2.7% (controls) in year −8 (p = 0.011).

Prescriptions

Prescriptions of specific drug classes relevant to AChE-I initiators were analyzed, focusing on treatments targeting both core symptoms and common comorbidities. Psychoanaleptics—including antidementia drugs and antidepressants—and psycholeptics—such as antipsychotics, anxiolytics, and sedatives—are primarily used to manage behavioral and mood disturbances in this population. Additionally, analgesics and urinary agents address pain and bladder issues, while anticoagulants and diuretics are used to mitigate cardiovascular risks and manage prevalent conditions like hypertension.

Figure 6 compares prescription rates of these AD related drug classes between the pre-cohort of AChE-I initiators and matched controls. In the year before AChE-I initiation (year −1) the largest difference was observed for psycholeptics, with a significantly higher proportion of AChE-I initiators receiving these medications compared to controls (p < 0.001). AChE-I initiators post-cohort results

AD diagnoses and comorbidities

In the post-cohort of AChE-I initiators, 78.6% (1,481 patients) received an AD-specific diagnosis within the first year of observation, with 68.2% (1,285 patients) diagnosed in the index quarter. The most common AD-specific diagnoses were G30 “Alzheimer’s disease” (69.5%) and F00 "Alzheimer’s dementia" (68.0%), while F06.7 "Mild cognitive impairment" was recorded in 9.2% of patients. During the first year, general practitioners were the most frequent group to assign AD-specific codes (24.3%), followed by physicians in "Nervenheilkunde" (German classification for neurologist and psychiatrist, 23.5%), neurologists (21.4%) psychiatrists (5.2%) and radiologists (0.3%). The Inpatient care setting accounted for 21.7% of AD diagnoses. By the end of the observation period, 12.0% of the initial post-cohort population had not received an AD-specific diagnosis. Among these patients, the most frequent diagnoses were F03 “Unspecified dementia” (86.0%) and F01 “Vascular dementia” (51.6%).

Analysis of comorbidities in the matched cohorts revealed statistically significant differences between AChE-I initiators and controls (see Fig. 7). In year 1, mobility disorders were present in 44.6% of AChE-I initiators compared to 19.3% of controls; by year 5, these rates increased to 46.0% and 25.4%, respectively (p < 0.001 for both years). Coding for schizophrenia among AChE-I initiators increased from 2.8% in year 1 to 5.2% in year 5, while the control cohort had fewer than five cases, precluding meaningful statistical comparison. Urinary incontinence among AChE-I initiators increased from 29.3% in year 1 to 40.2% in year 5, compared to an increase from 9.4% to 10.7% in controls (p < 0.001 for both years). These findings underscore the higher and increasing burden of comorbidities among AChE-I initiators compared to matched controls. Prescriptions

Among the initial (index) prescriptions for AChE-I in the post-cohort, donepezil was the most frequently prescribed (62.3%), followed by rivastigmine (26.1%) and galantamine (11.7%). The most common prescribers were general practitioners (29.5%), neurologists (27.6%) and physicians specializing in "Nervenheilkunde" (German classification for neurologist and psychiatrist, 28.1%), with psychiatrist accounting for 5.8% of index prescriptions. While this distribution was generally consistent across different AChE-I, rivastigmine was more often prescribed by general practitioners (38.1% of rivastigmine prescriptions) and galantamine more often by “Nervenheilkunde” specialists (34.0% of galantamine prescriptions).

With respect to indication-specific medication, psycholeptics (including antipsychotics, anxiolytics, and sedatives) were prescribed to 36.3% of AChE-I initiators in year 1, significantly higher than the 6.8% in the control group (p < 0.001). By year 5, psycholeptic prescriptions increased to 47.9% among AChE-I initiators, compared to 5.9% in controls (p < 0.001). Prescription rates for antidepressants, analgesics and agents for frequent bladder emptying and urinary incontinence were also significantly higher among AChE-I initiators than in controls, although the rates for analgesics and urinary incontinence agents declined by year 5 (see Fig. 8). Survival

Over the five-year observation period, 53.3% of AChE-I initiators in the post-cohort deceased, compared to 21.7% in the control group—a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001). Within the first year following the index event, mortality among AChE-I initiators was 8.4%, more than double that of the control group (3.7%, p < 0.001). By year 5, mortality increased to 16.2% in the post-cohort and 4.5% in controls (p < 0.001). Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the real-world care pathways, risk factors, comorbidities and treatment patterns among AChE-I initiators in Germany, using claims data from the SHI, highlighting several critical insights into diagnostic and therapeutic pathways. Our findings emphasize significant differences in diagnostic practices, comorbidity burden, medication use, and observed survival outcomes between AChE-I initiators and matched controls, underscoring the complex and multifaceted nature of eAD management in routine clinical practice. As controls were matched on demographic characteristics only, observed differences may partly reflect underlying frailty, healthcare utilization patterns, or comorbidity burden rather than AD-specific processes.

The demographic data show a higher proportion of females among AChE-I initiators, with the largest age group consisting of individuals aged 80 to < 90 years. This is consistent with existing literature, which indicates that older age and female sex are associated with a greater risk for AD20,21.

Delayed diagnosis

The etiological diagnosis of MCIs and dementias, including AD, is inherently complex, and requires a multifaceted approach involving clinical evaluation, neuropsychological testing, and biological markers, which often involves collaboration across various medical specialties10,11. Our data reveal low rates of documented AD-specific diagnoses (by ICD-10-GM codes) preceding first treatment among AChE-I initiators in the pre-cohort. While in the post-cohort, 68.2% of patients received an AD-specific diagnosis in the same quarter as their first (index) AChE-I treatment, 12.0% remained without any AD-specific diagnosis throughout the observation period, even after starting AChE-I therapy.

Current guidelines emphasize comprehensive diagnostic workups, including neuroimaging, and biomarker-based assessments10,11,22. However, observed rates in the pre-cohort of advanced diagnostics such as MRIs, CTs, and CSF analysis were relatively low in routine care. Notably, only 9.2% of patients underwent CSF diagnostic in the year before treatment initiation, despite CSF biomarkers being a guideline recommended method for etiological detection of AD23. This gap between recommended and actual diagnostic practices likely contributes to delayed or missed diagnoses and may result in suboptimal treatment for a significant subset of patients.

The limited use of advanced diagnostics and the absence of AD-specific coding highlight the need for improved implementation of guideline-based diagnostic pathways. Enhanced diagnostic efforts could particularly benefit the 12.0% of patients who start therapy without a documented AD diagnosis, leading to more timely and appropriate management. This is in accordance with recent calls from the German Alzheimer Society for improved diagnostic capacity and timely access to therapy, given the shortage of specialist physicians24.

Our study also found that general practitioners, neurologists, and specialists in "Nervenheilkunde" are substantially involved in diagnosing AD, each group accounting for more than 20% of AD-related diagnoses. In contrast, psychiatrist—despite their expertise—were responsible for only 5.2% of AD-related diagnoses (5.2%) within the first year post-treatment, indicating underutilization of this particularly qualified specialist group in the diagnostic process11,25,26. Increasing the involvement of psychiatrists could improve diagnostic accuracy and access to comprehensive assessments, especially as new targeted therapies become available.

The lack of stage-specific ICD codes further complicates early diagnosis and disease monitoring, potentially delaying appropriate intervention. Only 9.2% of AChE-I initiators in the post-cohort received a diagnosis of F06.7 "Mild cognitive impairment" in the first year, and the high burden of comorbidities and prescriptions suggests that many patients may already be at an advanced disease stage by the time of first treatment.

Our findings highlight persistent gaps between guideline recommendations and clinical practice in the diagnosis of AD. Addressing these gaps—through improved diagnostic coding with progression-specific codes, greater use of advanced diagnostics, and broader specialist involvement—will be essential for ensuring timely, accurate diagnosis and optimal care for patients with eAD.

Underdiagnosed & undercoded

Medication patterns among AChE-I initiators reflect both the symptomatic management of cognitive decline and the treatment of behavioral and psychological symptoms. Psycholeptics and psychoanaleptics are used to manage behavioral and mood disturbances in patients with AD, at the same time addressing core symptoms and common comorbidities. There was a notable increase in the prescription of psycholeptics and antidepressants in the year preceding AChE-I initiation, suggesting that neuropsychiatric symptoms often prompt further diagnostic evaluation and treatment escalation. This trend emphasizes the importance of enabling an etiological diagnosis for AD-specific treatment. While the use of anti-dementia medications declined over time, likely reflecting disease progression and treatment discontinuation, the persistent and increasing use of psycholeptics and other central nervous system-active drugs raises important questions about the appropriateness and safety of these therapies in older adults with cognitive impairment.

Screening, risk factors & prevention

Our analysis also shows that AChE-I initiators had a higher coded prevalence of comorbidities and potentially modifiable risk factors compared to controls. Conditions such as mobility disorders, urinary incontinence, and cardiovascular risk factors were significantly more prevalent, and many increased over time. Notably, schizophrenia (+ 350%), seizures (+ 119%), and dysphagia (+ 115%) showed higher rates in the pre-cohort. These findings are consistent with previous research indicating that multimorbidity is common in AD and can complicate both diagnosis and management27–29.

These comorbidities raise important questions about their role as early indicators or risk factors for AD. For example, depression is recognized as both an early symptom of dementia (prodromal feature) and a potentially modifiable risk factor for dementia30–32. Similarly, motoric cognitive risk syndrome (MCR), characterized by the coexistence of motor impairments and cognitive issues, has been associated with reported risk increases of approximately 2- to 3-fold. In our analysis, comorbidities indicating MCR, such as mobility disorders and falls, were notably prevalent up to eight years prior to AChE-I initiation and increased over time. Thus, these comorbidities may serve as early recognition indicators for AD33–35.

Frequent coding of conditions like low cognitive activity, frailty, and senility further underscores the diagnostic challenge. These symptoms may reflect underlying AD but are not always attributed as such, potentially delaying diagnosis and appropriate treatment. Clinicians should be vigilant in evaluating these comorbidities and consider differential diagnostics to facilitate earlier detection and intervention for AD. In the post-cohort, the comorbidity burden likely complicated the AD management and affected patients quality of life. For instance, the incidence of epileptic seizures increased over the observation period, supporting recent findings that epilepsy often appears early in AD36.

Our analysis of established AD risk factors—based on recommendations from the Lancet Commission, Yu et al., and the German S3 dementia guideline11,18,19—showed elevated prevalence rates up to eight years before first treatment, confirming their relevance and consistency with other studies37,38.

These findings highlight the importance of comprehensive risk management strategies in dementia prevention. While screening for cognitive impairment in individuals without cognitive complaints or evidence of cognitive disorders is not currently recommended11, proactive management of potentially modifiable risk factors remains essential. Increased physician awareness of the relationship between risk factors and dementia could enhance timely and accurate diagnosis when cognitive symptoms first emerge and help avoid misdiagnoses or unnecessary psychiatric medication, as advocated by the German Alzheimer Society, which calls for enhanced knowledge on prevention and targeted dementia prevention programs24.

Finally, our survival analysis demonstrated higher observed mortality among AChE-I initiators compared to controls over the follow‑up period. These mortality differences should be interpreted descriptively, as we did not adjust for baseline comorbidities, frailty, or healthcare use. This aligns with other studies showing that AD is a leading cause of death in older adults39,40. Although the average observation time until death was similar between groups, this likely reflects the fixed observation window rather than true equivalence in life expectancy. It should be noted that the fixed 5-year observation window may underestimate the true survival advantage of the control group, as many controls likely would have survived beyond the study period if follow-up had continued. Overall, these findings emphasize a higher observed mortality among AChE-I initiators after inhibitor treatment initiation compared to matched controls and the urgent need for earlier intervention and improved disease-modifying therapies.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, this study relied on claims data, which are primarily collected for reimbursement rather than research purposes. Consequently, diagnoses and comorbidities may be underreported or misclassified, which could lead to an underestimation or mischaracterization of disease prevalence and comorbidity burden. Second, the absence of stage-specific diagnostic codes for AD in the German healthcare system further limits the precision of patient identification.

In this context, the use of newly initiated AChE-I therapy as a proxy for suspected eAD represents a pragmatic claims-based approach which may result in both under- and overestimation of patients due to different reasons. Patients with eAD who were untreated or treated off-label would not have been captured, while some patients identified through AChE-I initiation may not strictly represent the intended eAD population. AChE-I are approved for mild to moderate AD dementia and are not indicated for MCI; therefore, patients identified through AChE-I initiation likely represent individuals with clinically recognized dementia, potentially biasing the cohort toward patients with more clearly documented disease. In addition, AChE-I may also be prescribed for non-AD dementias, including dementia with Lewy Bodies (DLB) and mixed dementias, which may reduce the specificity of the study population for suspected AD. Although rivastigmine users with concurrent Parkinson’s disease diagnoses were excluded to reduce potential misclassification, some patients with DLB or other non-AD dementias may have remained in the cohort. Moreover, AChE-I initiation may occur at moderate disease stages, meaning that not all identified patients necessarily represent the earliest phase of AD. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as pertaining to patients newly initiated on AChE-I therapy with suspected eAD—a pragmatic claims-based proxy—rather than to a biomarker-confirmed or clinically staged eAD population.

To describe patterns before and after treatment initiation, two cohorts were defined, providing snapshots for care pathways, risk factors and comorbidities in relation to AChE-I initiation. A pre-cohort (2022), examining the care trajectory, documented comorbidities, risk factors, and diagnostic patterns in the eight years leading up to AChE-I initiation. A post-cohort (2017), approximating AChE-I initiators and analyzing disease progression following treatment initiation. Additionally, the observational study design does not allow for causal inference regarding the impact of specific interventions on outcomes, and residual confounding may persist despite robust matching, possibly influencing the observed associations. Finally, results may not be generalizable to populations outside the SHI system, such as those with private insurance, which could limit the applicability of findings to the broader German population.

The matched comparison analysis was based on exact matching for demographic and structural characteristics, including age, sex, place of residence, urbanization, and insurance status. Matching did not include baseline morbidity, frailty, or healthcare utilization intensity. Therefore, observed differences between AChE-I initiators and matched controls may partly reflect differences in underlying health status, clinical attention, or frequency of healthcare contacts in addition to AD-related processes. Consequently, comparisons with matched controls should be interpreted as descriptive associations within the applied study design, rather than as causal effects.

The interpretation of mortality outcomes also requires caution. Although survival was assessed using Kaplan–Meier analysis, observed mortality differences between AChE-I initiators and matched controls may be influenced by residual differences in baseline morbidity, frailty, disease severity, and healthcare utilization intensity. As the analysis did not adjust for these factors beyond the applied matching criteria, survival differences should be interpreted as observed differences between the proxy-defined study population and matched controls, rather than mortality that is directly attributable to eAD or AChE-I initiation.

Differences between the cohorts may partly reflect period effects, including changes in coding behavior, diagnostic availability, guideline updates, and potential COVID‑related disruptions. Therefore, cross-cohort comparisons should not be interpreted as representing a continuous eAD disease continuum over time and are intended for cautious, descriptive interpretation only.

Further, results should also be interpreted in light of healthcare‑contact bias. In claims data, diagnoses and outcomes can only be captured when patients interact with the healthcare system. Patients with more frequent healthcare utilization may therefore have a higher likelihood of having comorbidities, treatments, or outcomes recorded, independent of their true underlying disease burden. This may lead to differential detection across comparison groups and cannot be fully addressed in administrative data.

Strength

Despite the aforementioned limitations, this study offers severable notable strengths. The use of a large, representative claims database allows for comprehensive, real-world insights into the management of AChE-I initiators in Germany. The InGef dataset closely mirrors the age and sex structure of the German population, and nearly 80% of insured individuals are observable for the entire study period, enabling robust longitudinal analyses17,41. Our matched cohort design enhances comparability among AChE-I initiators and controls, and the long observation period provides valuable information on disease progression, care pathways, and outcomes.

By developing a pragmatic approach identification strategy in the absence of stage-specific coding, this study addresses a critical evidence gap in claims‑based dementia research and underscores the importance of developing more precise diagnostic criteria, enhancing early detection, and implementing comprehensive care strategies to address the complex needs of this growing patient population.

Conclusion

The objective of this study was to gather comprehensive insights into the care of AChE-I initiators with suspected eAD in Germany from the perspective of the SHI. In the absence of stage-specific diagnostic coding, newly initiated treatment with AChE inhibitors was used as a pragmatic proxy to identify patients at an early disease stage to address existing evidence gaps in this population. The aim is not only to identify care pathways, including diagnostics and treatment, potentially modifiable risk factors, and comorbidities, but also to integrate this knowledge to improve clinical practice, healthcare resource allocation, and patient care delivery.

In Germany, the aging population is expected to substantially increase the number of people affected by dementia in the coming decades. This study highlights significant gaps in the early diagnosis and management of AD, with no standardized patient pathway and considerable variation in the involvement of general practitioners, neurologists, and other specialists. Diagnostic and pharmacological practices often fall short of guideline recommendations, and people living with AD experience a high burden of modifiable risk factors, comorbidities, and mortality.

To address these challenges, a multifaceted approach is needed—to diagnose the disease in its early stages, initiate AD-specific treatment in a timely manner, and thereby improve outcomes and efficiently utilize healthcare resources for people living with eAD.

Addressing these issues requires a multifaceted approach, including comprehensive management strategies for modifiable risk factors, awareness of early disease-specific symptoms on both the physician and patient sides, the development of more precise diagnostic codes reflecting disease stages, and improved therapeutic interventions.

Further research should validate claims-based definitions of eAD, assess the impact of emerging disease-modifying therapies, and explore the integration of claims data with clinical and biomarker information to enhance disease monitoring and management.

Finally, improving early diagnosis and care pathways is essential not only for optimizing clinical outcomes but also for supporting people living with the disease and caregivers in managing the complex challenges of eAD.
