Laws of Quantum Programming
Mingsheng Ying, Li Zhou, Gilles Barthe

Abstract
. In this paper, we investigate the fundamental laws of quantum programming. We extend a comprehensive set of Hoare et al.’s basic laws of classical programming to the quantum setting. These laws characterise the algebraic properties of quantum programs, such as the distributivity of sequential composition over (quantum) 𝐢𝐟 {\mathbf{if}} -statements and the unfolding of nested (quantum) 𝐢𝐟 {\mathbf{if}} -statements. At the same time, we clarify some subtle differences between certain laws of classical programming and their quantum counterparts. Additionally, we derive a fixpoint characterisation of quantum 𝐰𝐡𝐢𝐥𝐞 {\mathbf{while}} -loops and a loop-based realisation of tail recursion in quantum programming. Furthermore, we establish two normal form theorems: one for quantum circuits and one for finite quantum programs. The theory in which these laws are established is formalised in the Coq proof assistant, and all of these laws are mechanically verified. As an application case of our laws, we present a formal derivation of the principle of deferred measurements in dynamic quantum circuits. We expect that these laws can be utilised in correctness-preserving transformation, compilation, and automatic code optimisation in quantum programming. In particular, because these laws are formally verified in Coq, they can be confidently applied in quantum program development.

1.
Introduction

It is widely accepted that scientists and mathematicians uncover the fundamental laws of nature and mathematical structures, respectively, while engineers leverage these discoveries to address practical problems.
This fundamental concept spurred Hoare et al.
(
Hoare87
;
Hoare88
;
Hoare93
)
to pose a crucial question:

•

What are the laws governing computer programming that establish the formal foundation for the field of software engineering?

Their argument, rooted in the perspective that computer programs are essentially mathematical expressions, led them to establish a series of algebraic laws for programming, ranging from sequential to concurrent processes.
The foundational laws articulated in
(
Hoare87
;
Hoare88
;
Hoare93
)
serve as a cornerstone in the establishment of the science of computer programming.
Simultaneously, they prove invaluable in the realm of software engineering, encompassing tasks such as compilation, optimisation, and program transformation.
For instance, during program development, a programmer may create a program aligning with end-user requirements.
Utilising these laws, an optimising compiler can then transform it into an efficient program that effectively exploits the underlying architecture of the target machine (refer to
(
Hoare93
)
for a detailed example).
This strategic approach has the advantage of ensuring correctness by construction during compilation.
Each transformation within the process is mathematically proven based on these laws, providing a robust framework for the development and optimisation of computer programs.

The past decade has witnessed remarkable advancements in quantum computer hardware, leading to a surge in research on quantum programming languages and platforms
(

;

;

.
The research on quantum programming also spans from theoretical aspects, such as formal semantics, program logic, static analysis, and verification, to more practical considerations like compilation, optimisation, testing, and debugging.
Despite this progress, the knowledge base of quantum programming as a scientific discipline remains fragmented and disjointed.
Consequently, two fundamental questions naturally arise:

•

What are the foundational laws that underpin the methodologies and techniques of quantum programming?

•

Are there discernible differences between these laws and those governing classical programming, as established in works like
(
Hoare87
;
Hoare88
;
Hoare93
)
?

Addressing these questions is crucial for consolidating the understanding of quantum programming as a coherent and well-defined field, bridging the gap between theoretical insights and practical applications.

At the bottom is a description language for quantum circuits.

•

The middle layer introduces a language for writing purely quantum programs (without classical variables).
Building upon quantum circuits, an
{\mathbf{if}}

This layer also defines the loop and recursion of quantum programs (with classical control flow).
Nondeterministic choice is added for the specification and refinement of quantum programs.

•

:
At the top is the classical-quantum hybrid programming layer, where purely quantum programs are embedded into a classical programming language.
This integration enables the programming of classical and quantum computation together, a necessity for most practical quantum algorithms.

While the components of this framework are not novel and have been extensively studied in previous literature, we organise them into a unified framework that clearly delineates the application of each law of quantum programming.

Within this three-layer framework, we establish a comprehensive set of basic laws of quantum programming.
This includes two subsets of laws:

•

The first subset is proven for quantum circuits at the bottom layer of Figure

Among them, several laws about the distributivity of quantum
{\mathbf{if}}
-statements are particularly useful.

•

The second subset is established for purely quantum programs at the middle layer of Figure

A congruence property is introduced, facilitating the lifting of laws for quantum circuits to quantum programs.
Then a diverse range of laws governing
{\mathbf{if}}
-statements, loops, recursion, and nondeterministic choice are derived.

These quantum programming laws can be further integrated with existing laws for classical (and probabilistic) programs

Noteworthy technical contributions of this paper encompass:

•

Normal forms
:
We prove that every quantum circuit is equivalent to a normal form featuring a sequential composition of flat (but large) quantum
{\mathbf{if}}
-statements.
A similar normal form (with an
{\mathbf{if}}
-statement) is presented for finite quantum programs.
The significance of the normal forms lies in their applications, including providing a compilation strategy of quantum programs as a generalisation of that given in
(
Hoare93
)
and offering a method for checking the equivalence of two quantum programs by reducing them to a normal form.

•

Tail Recursion
:
It is well-known in classical programming that tail recursion can be realised using loops.
We prove that this result holds true for recursive quantum programs with classical control flow.
A practical implication of this finding is that quantum programs with tail recursion can be easily implemented and optimised during compilation.

•

Verification in Coq
:
We formalise the theory in which our laws are established in the Coq proof assistant, by leveraging CoqQ
(
Zhou23
)
, a general-purpose project for quantum program verification.
The formalisation from the first principle ensures the soundness of the laws presented in this paper.
We expect that they will be confidently and frequently used in quantum development. As observed in
(
BGZ10
;
Barb21
)
, algebraic laws like these are also very helpful in the security verification of quantum cryptography.

3.
A Framework of Quantum Programming

In this section, we define a three-layer framework of quantum programming in which our laws can be properly accommodated.
As pointed out in the Introduction, all ingredients of this framework are not new and have been widely used by the quantum programming community.

We assume a set
{\mathit{QV}}
of quantum variables and a set
\mathcal{U}
of
unitary matrix constants
.
The unitary matrix constants in
\mathcal{U}
will be instantiated in applications.
For convenience, it is always assumed that the
2\times 2

unit matrix
is in
\mathcal{U}
. We fix the following notations:

-

Each quantum variable
q
stands for a quantum system with a Hilbert space denoted by
\mathcal{H}_{q}
as its state space.

-

A sequence
{\overline{q}}=q_{1},...,q_{n}
of distinct quantum variables is called a quantum register.
It denotes a composite quantum system consisting of subsystems
q_{1},...,q_{n}
.
Its state space, or type, denoted by
{\mathit{type}}({\overline{q}})
, is given by the tensor product
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{H}_{q_{i}}
.
We write
{\overline{q}}_{1},{\overline{q}}_{2}
for the concatenation of two distinct registers
{\overline{q}}_{1}
and
{\overline{q}}_{2}
.
For simplicity of the presentation, we often identify
{\overline{q}}
with the set
\{q_{1},...,q_{n}\}
of quantum variables occurring in
{\overline{q}}
.

-

Each unitary matrix constant
U\in\mathcal{U}
assumes a type of the form
{\mathit{type}}(U)=\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes...\otimes\mathcal{H}_{n}
.
This means that the unitary transformation denoted by
U
can be performed on a composite quantum system consisting of
n
subsystems with state spaces
\mathcal{H}_{1},...,\mathcal{H}_{n}
, respectively.
Thus, if
{\overline{q}}=q_{1},...,q_{n}
is a quantum register with
\mathcal{H}_{q_{i}}=\mathcal{H}_{i}
for
i=1,...,n
(that is, the types of
U
matches that of the register
{\overline{q}}
), then
U[{\overline{q}}]
can be thought of as a basic quantum gate with quantum wires
q_{1},...,q_{n}
.

3.1.
Quantum circuits

The first layer of our framework (see Figure
1
) is a description language of quantum circuits.
Formally, we have:

Definition 3.1.

Quantum circuits
C\in\mathbf{QC}
are defined by the syntax:

(7)

\displaystyle C::={\mathbf{skip}}\mid U[{\overline{q}}]\mid C_{1};C_{2}\mid{\mathbf{qif}\,[{{\overline{q}}}]\left({\square_{i=1}^{d}|\psi_{i}\rangle}\rightarrow{C_{i}}\right)\mathbf{fiq}}

More precisely, quantum circuits
C
are inductively defined by the following clauses, where
{\mathit{qv}}(C)
denotes the quantum variables in
C

(1)

Skip
:
{\mathit{qv}}({\mathbf{skip}})=\emptyset
;

(2)

Basic gates
: If

is a unitary matrix constant and
{\overline{q}}
is a quantum register such that their types match, then quantum gate
U[{\overline{q}}]
is a circuit, and
{\mathit{qv}}(U[{\overline{q}}])={\overline{q}}
;

(3)

Sequential composition
: If
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are circuits, then
C\equiv C_{1};C_{2}
is a circuit too, and
{\mathit{qv}}(C)={\mathit{qv}}(C_{1})\cup{\mathit{qv}}(C_{2})
;

(4)

Quantum
{\mathbf{if}}
-statement
: If
{\overline{q}}
is a quantum register,
\left\{|\psi_{i}\rangle\right\}_{i=1}^{d}
is an orthonormal basis of the Hilbert space
{\mathit{type}}({\overline{q}})
, and
C_{i}

(i=1,...,d)
are circuits with

(8)

{\overline{q}}\cap\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{d}{\mathit{qv}}(C_{i})\right)=\emptyset,

then

(9)

C\equiv{\mathbf{qif}\,[{{\overline{q}}}]\left({\square_{i=1}^{d}|\psi_{i}\rangle}\rightarrow{C_{i}}\right)\mathbf{fiq}}

is a circuit, and
{\mathit{qv}}(C)={\overline{q}}\cup\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{d}{\mathit{qv}}(C_{i})\right)
.
{\overline{q}}
is called the guard register, and
\{|\psi_{i}\rangle\}
is called the guard basis of the quantum
{\mathbf{if}}
-statement (
9

Intuitively, each
C\in\mathbf{QC}
represents a circuit with quantum wires
{\mathit{qv}}(C)
.
In other words, the type of
C
is
{\mathit{type}}(C)=\bigotimes_{q\in{\mathit{qv}}(C)}\mathcal{H}_{q}
, and as will be seen in Definition
3.3

is a unitary operator on
{\mathit{type}}(C)
.
The circuit constructs introduced in the above definition are explained as follows.

(i)

The circuit
C_{1};C_{2}
in Clause (
3
) stands for the sequential composition of circuits
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
.
Indeed, if
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
do not share quantum variables; that is,
{\mathit{qv}}(C_{1})\cap{\mathit{qv}}(C_{2})=\emptyset
, we can also define their parallel composition

C_{1};C_{2}
and
C_{1}\otimes C_{2}
are semantically equivalent whenever
{\mathit{qv}}(C_{1})\cap{\mathit{qv}}(C_{2})=\emptyset
.
So, the parallel composition is not included in the above definition.

(ii)

The quantum
{\mathbf{if}}
-statement defined in Eq. (
9
) has been extensively discussed in the previous literature
(
Alt05
;
Ying12
;
Sab18
;
Yuan
;
Bich
;
Voi23
)
.
The condition (
8

{\overline{q}}
is a system external to all
C_{i}

(i=1,...,d)
. Semantically, a quantum
{\mathbf{if}}
-statement (
9
) is a quantum multiplexor (i.e., a multi-way generalisation of conditional)
(
Markov
)
.
A multiplexer can be understood as a switch that passes one of its data inputs through to the output, as a function of a set of select inputs.
Here,
{\overline{q}}
is the select register, and if
{\overline{q}}
is in state
|\psi_{i}\rangle
, then a quantum datum
|\varphi\rangle
is inputted to the corresponding circuit
C_{i}
and an output
|\varphi_{i}\rangle
is obtained at the end of

A basic difference between classical and quantum multiplexors is that the quantum select register
{\overline{q}}
can be in a superposition of
|\psi_{i}\rangle

(i=1,...,d)

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}|\psi_{i}\rangle
.
In this case, the output is then
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}|\varphi_{i}\rangle
, a superposition of the outputs of different circuits
C_{i}

(i=1,...,d)
. Therefore, the control flow of a quantum
{\mathbf{if}}
-statement is inherently quantum.

For simplicity of presentation, let us introduce a syntactic sugar for quantum
{\mathbf{if}}
-statement when some of the branches are
{\mathbf{skip}}
:

{\mathbf{qif}\,[{{\overline{q}}}]\left({\square_{i\in I}|\psi_{i}\rangle}\rightarrow{U_{i}}\right)\mathbf{fiq}}\triangleq{\mathbf{qif}\,[{{\overline{q}}}]\left({\left(\square_{i\in I}|\psi_{i}\rangle\rightarrow U_{i}\right)\ \square\ \left(\square_{i\notin I}|\psi_{i}\rangle\rightarrow{\mathbf{skip}}\right)}\right)\mathbf{fiq}}

The following example illustrates how some frequently used (multi-qubit) quantum gates can be defined in terms of quantum
{\mathbf{if}}
-statements.

Example 3.1.

Assume that the single-qubit

{\mathsf{X}}

Then:

(1)

The
{\mathsf{CNOT}}
(controlled-

as its control qubit can be defined by

\displaystyle{\mathsf{CNOT}}[q_{1},q_{2}]\triangleq{\mathbf{qif}\,[{q_{1}}]\left({|1\rangle}\rightarrow{{\mathsf{X}}[q_{2}]}\right)\mathbf{fiq}}.

(2)

The
{\mathsf{Toffoli}}

\displaystyle{\mathsf{Toffoli}}[q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}]\triangleq{\mathbf{qif}\,[{q_{1},q_{2}}]\left({|11\rangle}\rightarrow{{\mathsf{X}}[q_{3}]}\right)\mathbf{fiq}}.

(3)

The

\displaystyle{\mathsf{Deutsch}}(\theta)[q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}]\triangleq{\mathbf{qif}\,[{q_{1},q_{2}}]\left({|11\rangle}\rightarrow{{\overline{r}}_{x}(\theta)[q_{3}]}\right)\mathbf{fiq}}.

Note that

(4)

The
{\mathsf{Fredkin}}

\displaystyle{\mathsf{Fredkin}}[q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}]\triangleq{\mathbf{qif}\,[{q_{1}}]\left({|1\rangle}\rightarrow{{\mathsf{SWAP}}[q_{2},q_{3}]}\right)\mathbf{fiq}}

{\mathbf{while}}

3.3.
Semantics

In this subsection, we briefly describe the denotational semantics of quantum circuits

The correctness of our laws of quantum programming will be proved based on them.

Definition 3.3.

(1)

(2)

If

{\llbracket C\rrbracket}=U_{{\overline{q}}}
;

(3)

If

{\llbracket C\rrbracket}=\left({\llbracket C_{2}\rrbracket}\otimes I_{{\mathit{qv}}(C_{1})\setminus{\mathit{qv}}(C_{2})}\right)\left({\llbracket C_{1}\rrbracket}\otimes I_{{\mathit{qv}}(C_{2})\setminus{\mathit{qv}}(C_{1})}\right)

(4)

If

{\llbracket C\rrbracket}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\psi_{i}\rangle_{{\overline{q}}}\langle\psi_{i}|\otimes\left({\llbracket C_{i}\rrbracket}\otimes I_{{\mathit{qv}}(C)\setminus{\overline{q}}\setminus{\mathit{qv}}(C_{i})}\right)

Let

denote the set of partial density operators (i.e., positive operators with traces
\leq 1
) on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathit{QV}}

Then the denotational semantics of a quantum program

The Löwner order

is positive semi-definite, can be lifted pointwise to an order between quantum operations (i.e., super-operators), also denoted

We write

for the set of quantum operations on

It can be proved that

Furthermore, the denotational semantics of quantum programs enjoys the following:

For any input

(1)

, where

(3)

{\llbracket C\rrbracket}(\rho)={\llbracket C\rrbracket}\rho{\llbracket C\rrbracket}^{{\dagger}}
2

2

.

(4)

{\llbracket P_{1};P_{2}\rrbracket}(\rho)={\llbracket P_{2}\rrbracket}({\llbracket P_{1}\rrbracket}(\rho))
.

(5)

{\llbracket{\mathbf{if}\,{M}[{{\overline{q}}}]\left(\square{m}\rightarrow{P_{m}}\right)\mathbf{fi}}\rrbracket}(\rho)=\sum_{m}{\llbracket P_{m}\rrbracket}\left((M_{m})_{\overline{q}}\rho(M_{m})_{\overline{q}}^{{\dagger}}\right)

4.

Now we are ready to introduce our laws of quantum programming.

To describe these laws, we need the notion of equivalence between quantum circuits:

But for simplicity of presentation, we often only consider quantum

Following an idea in
(

Let

Then

We often call

In the post-choice, the coin tossing operation

Example 4.1 (Quantum random walk).

The one-dimensional random walk can be thought of as a particle moving on a discrete line whose nodes are denoted by integers
\mathbb{Z}=\{...,-2,-1,0,1,2,...\}
.
At each step, the particle moves one position left or right, depending on the flip of a coin.
A quantum variant of it is the Hadamard walk, with a quantum coin operator given by the Hadamard gate
{\mathsf{H}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&1\\
1&-1\end{array}\right).

Formally, the Hilbert space of the Hadamard walk is
\mathcal{H}_{d}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{p}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{d}=\mathit{span}\{|L\rangle,|R\rangle\}
is a
2
-dimensional Hilbert space, called the direction space,
|L\rangle,|R\rangle
are used to indicate the direction Left and Right, respectively,
\mathcal{H}_{p}=\overline{\mathit{span}\{|n\rangle:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}}
is an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space,
|n\rangle
indicates the position marked by integer
n
, and
\overline{\mathit{span}\ X}
is the Hilbert space spanned by a nonempty set
X
of vectors.
One step of the Hadamard walk is modelled by the unitary operator
W=T({\mathsf{H}}\otimes I_{p}),
where the translation
T
is a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}_{d}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{p}
defined by
T|L,n\rangle=|L,n-1\rangle
and
T|R,n\rangle=|R,n+1\rangle
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
{\mathsf{H}}
is the Hadamard gate on
\mathcal{H}_{d}
, and
I_{p}
is the identity operator on
\mathcal{H}_{p}
.
The Hadamard walk is then described by repeated applications of the operator
W
.

Now we define the left and right translation operators
T_{L}
and
T_{R}
on the position space
\mathcal{H}_{p}
by
T_{L}|n\rangle=|n-1\rangle
and
T_{R}|n\rangle=|n+1\rangle
for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The translation can be written as quantum
{\mathbf{if}}
-statement
T={T_{L}}{\>\leftarrow\>}{d}{\>\rightarrow\>}{T_{R}}
, the walk operator is a quantum choice
W={T_{L}[p]}{\>\leftarrow\>}{{\mathsf{H}}[d]}{\>\rightarrow\>}{T_{R}[p]}
, and an
n
-step quantum walk is the sequential composition of
n
copies of
W
.

Several laws for quantum choices are presented in the following:

Proposition 4.3 (Laws of Quantum Choice).

(1)

(Symmetry) Let
{\mathsf{X}}
be the
NOT
gate.
Then

{C_{0}}{\>\leftarrow\>}{{\mathsf{X}}[q]}{\>\rightarrow\>}{C_{1}}\equiv{C_{1}}{\>\leftarrow\>}{[q]{\mathsf{X}}}{\>\rightarrow\>}{C_{0}}.

More generally, if
U|\varphi_{i}\rangle=\lambda_{i}|\psi_{i}\rangle
for
(i=0,1)
, then

{C_{0}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle|\psi_{0}\rangle}}{{\leftarrow}}{U[q]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle|\psi_{1}\rangle}}{{\rightarrow}}{C_{1}}\equiv{C_{0}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle|\varphi_{0}\rangle}}{{\leftarrow}}{[q]U}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle|\varphi_{1}\rangle}}{{\rightarrow}}{C_{1}}.

(2)

(Sequentiality)

\ ({C_{0}}{\>\leftarrow\>}{B[q]}{\>\rightarrow\>}{C_{1}});({D_{0}}{\>\leftarrow\>}{q}{\>\rightarrow\>}{D_{1}})\equiv{(C_{0};D_{0})}{\>\leftarrow\>}{B[q]}{\>\rightarrow\>}{(C_{1};D_{1})}
,

\hskip 52.06859pt({C_{0}}{\>\leftarrow\>}{q}{\>\rightarrow\>}{C_{1}});({D_{0}}{\>\leftarrow\>}{[q]B}{\>\rightarrow\>}{D_{1}})\equiv{(C_{0};D_{0})}{\>\leftarrow\>}{[q]B}{\>\rightarrow\>}{(C_{1};D_{1})}.

(3)

(Distributivity) If
q\notin{\mathit{qv}}(C)
, then

\displaystyle C;({C_{0}}{\>\leftarrow\>}{B[q]}{\>\rightarrow\>}{C_{1}})\equiv{(C;C_{0})}{\>\leftarrow\>}{B[q]}{\>\rightarrow\>}{(C;C_{1})},

\displaystyle({C_{0}}{\>\leftarrow\>}{B[q]}{\>\rightarrow\>}{C_{1}});C\equiv{(C_{0};C)}{\>\leftarrow\>}{B[q]}{\>\rightarrow\>}{(C_{1};C)}.

The same holds for post-choice.

It is easy to see the correspondence between the laws (Symmetry), (Sequentiality) and (Distributivity) in the above proposition and the ones in Propositions
4.1
and
4.2
.
On the other hand, the laws in the above proposition can be regarded as quantum counterparts of the corresponding laws of probabilistic programming given in

He97
)
.
