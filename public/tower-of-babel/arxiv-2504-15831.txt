Detecting genuine non-Gaussian entanglement
Serge Deside ∗, Tobias Haas ∗, Nicolas J. Cerf

Abstract
Efficiently certifying non-Gaussian entanglement in continuous-variable quantum systems is a central challenge for advancing quantum information processing, photonic quantum computing, and metrology. Here, we put forward continuous-variable counterparts of the recently introduced entanglement criteria based on moments of the partially transposed state, together with simple readout schemes that require only a few replicas of the state, passive linear optics, and particle-number measurements. Our multicopy method enables the detection of genuine non-Gaussian entanglement for various relevant state families overlooked by standard approaches, which includes the entire class of NOON states. Further, it is robust against realistic experimental constraints (losses, noise, and finite statistics), which we demonstrate by extensive numerical simulations.

PT-moment-based criteria for continuous variables

II.1
Infinite hierarchies of criteria

II.1.1
From the Stieltjes moment problem

The first set of criteria is motivated by the question of whether a given set of PT-moments
(p_{1},...,p_{n})
, defined via
p_{n}=\textbf{Tr}\{\tilde{\bm{\rho}}^{n}\}
, is compatible with a positive semi-definite partially transposed state
\tilde{\bm{\rho}}=(\mathds{1}\otimes T_{B})\bm{\rho}

Yu et al. 2021
;
Neven et al. 2021
. Corresponding to a quantum version of the classical Stieltjes moment problem, compatibility can be certified upon inspecting positivity conditions formulated in terms of Hankel matrices. Given the Hankel matrix over PT-moments of increasing order along rows and columns

P_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}p_{1}&p_{2}&p_{3}&\cdots&p_{\frac{n+1}{2}}\\
p_{2}&p_{3}&p_{4}&\cdots&\\
p_{3}&p_{4}&p_{5}&\cdots&\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\\
p_{\frac{n+1}{2}}&&&&p_{n}\end{pmatrix},\quad n=1,3,5,\dots,

(1)

the
p_{n}
-PPT criterion states
Yu et al. 2021
;
Neven et al. 2021

\bm{\rho}\text{ separable}\implies P_{n}\geq 0\quad\forall\text{ odd }n,

(2)

such that violation of the latter inequality for some
n
flags entanglement. Note that
p_{1}=1
and
p_{2}
equals the state’s purity
\textbf{Tr}\{\bm{\rho}^{2}\}
, so we need to exploit at least the third-order PT-moment
p_{3}
to flag entanglement. Sylvester’s criterion implies
P_{n+2}\geq 0\implies P_{n}\geq 0
, showing that higher-order criteria are stronger (and larger) sets of conditions on the positivity of
\tilde{\bm{\rho}}
. In fact, (
2
) is necessary and sufficient for PPT when considering
all
PT-moments.

Although the set of criteria (
2
) has been benchmarked and refined solely for discrete-variable systems, it readily extends to continuous variables since the Stieltjes-type moment problem is generally defined over non-negative numbers and, thus, does not constrain the dimension of the spectrum of the state.

II.1.2
From Descartes’ rule of signs

The second set of criteria tests for PPT by linking the eigenvalues of
\tilde{\bm{\rho}}
, more precisely, their elementary symmetric polynomials, to PT-moments via Newton’s identities
Neven et al. 2021
. The latter read

ne_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j-1}e_{n-j}\ p_{j},\quad n=1,2,\dots,

(3)

where
e_{n}
denotes the
n
th elementary symmetric polynomial of the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{1},\dots\}
of
\tilde{\bm{\rho}}
, defined as

e_{n}(\lambda_{1},\dots)=\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{n}}\lambda_{j_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{j_{n}},

(4)

with
e_{0}(\lambda_{1},\dots)=e_{1}(\lambda_{1},\dots)=1
. Then, Descartes’ rule of signs implies

\bm{\rho}\text{ separable}\implies e_{n}\geq 0\quad\forall\,n\in\mathbb{N},

(5)

from which conditions on the PT-moments can be obtained via (

2

While also the criteria (
5

II.2
Third-order criteria

Albeit the apparent outperformance of higher-order criteria, PT-moments decrease exponentially when increasing
n
, thereby complicating entanglement detection in practice. This follows from
\lvert p_{n}\rvert\leq(p_{2})^{n/2}
for
n\geq 2
, which shows that higher-order PT-moments are exponentially suppressed, especially if the considered state is highly mixed. On top of that, our proposed measurement scheme for
p_{n}
requires
n
replica states, which further increases the experimental burden for large
n
(see Sec.
III
).

Hence, PT-moment-based entanglement criteria limited to third order, that is, conditions involving only the purity
p_{2}
and moment
p_{3}
, are of particular relevance for experimental applications. They are obtained from (
2
) and (
5
) by setting
n=3
, leading to what we refer to as the
quadratic

Elben et al. 2020
and
linear

Neven et al. 2021
(in
p_{2}
)
p_{3}
-PPT criteria,

\bm{\rho}\text{ separable}\implies p_{3}-p_{2}^{2}\geq 0,\quad p_{3}-\frac{3p_{2}-1}{2}\geq 0,

(6)

respectively. Here, we have used (for the quadratic criterion) that the positivity of a
2\times 2
matrix with positive elements is equivalent to the positivity of its determinant, and (for the linear criterion) the relations
3e_{3}=e_{2}-p_{2}+p_{3}
and
2e_{2}=1-p_{2}
. Interestingly, the two conditions (
6
) coincide for pure states (
p_{2}=1
), giving
p_{3}\geq 1
, but also for mixed states with purity
p_{2}=1/2
, giving
p_{3}\geq 1/4
. In between, the quadratic criterion is stronger than the linear criterion for
p_{2}<1/2
, and vice versa when
1/2<p_{2}<1
.

This observation suggests the existence of an
optimal
criterion of third order, in the sense that given only the two lowest-order moments
p_{2}
and
p_{3}
, no stronger condition on the separability of a state can be found. This is achieved by minimizing
p_{3}
with respect to
p_{2}
, which results in the optimal
p_{3}
-PPT criterion
Yu et al. 2021
;
Neven et al. 2021

\bm{\rho}\text{ separable}\implies p_{3}-\alpha u^{3}-(1-\alpha u)^{3}\geq 0,

(7)

where
\alpha=\lfloor 1/p_{2}\rfloor
denotes the floor function of
1/p_{2}
and
u=\frac{\alpha+\sqrt{\alpha\left[p_{2}(\alpha+1)-1\right]}}{\alpha(\alpha+1)}
is a non-negative number. It is instructive to evaluate (
7
) when
1/2<p_{2}\leq 1
, in which case
\alpha=1,u=(1+\sqrt{2p_{2}-1})/2
, such that (
7
) reduces to the linear criterion. Thus, the linear criterion is optimal for states of purity
1/2\leq p_{2}\leq 1
. In contrast, both the quadratic and linear criteria (
6
) are outperformed by (
7
) for mixed states with
p_{2}<1/2
(see figure 2 in
Neven et al. 2021
). As already noted in

p_{3}
-PPT criterion (
7

Remarkably, the third-order criteria are both necessary and sufficient conditions for pure-state entanglement – also in the CV case. This follows directly from the Schmidt decomposition
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i}c_{i}\ket{i,i}
, where
c_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
are the Schmidt coefficients such that
\lambda_{i}=\lvert c_{i}\rvert^{2}
defines a probability distribution over a countably infinite set. In this case, PT-moments reduce to
L^{p}
norms, i.e.,
p_{n}=\left\lVert\lambda\right\rVert_{n}^{n}
(
p_{n}=\left\lVert\lambda\right\rVert_{n/2}^{n}
) for odd (even)
n
, which implies
p_{n}\leq 1
. Equality for
n\geq 3
holds if and only if all but one Schmidt coefficients vanish. Hence, pure product states are characterized by all PT-moments being unity, while for pure entangled states, we have
p_{n}<1
for
n\geq 3
.

III
Implementation

We now devise measurement schemes to access PT-moments in CV systems. In particular, we provide passive linear optical circuits to be applied on replicas of the state, from which the two lowest-order moments,
p_{2}
and
p_{3}
, can be extracted using particle-number measurements.

III.1
Multicopy method

On discrete-variable platforms like spin lattices, the PT-moments are conveniently estimated from randomized measurements
Elben et al. 2020
;
Neven et al. 2021
. However, the efficiency of this ansatz – in terms of the required measurements to extract a given PT-moment with high accuracy – relies
heavily
on the Hilbert space dimension of a qubit being small. More precisely, the required number of measurements to ensure
\epsilon
accuracy for an estimate of
p_{n}
is on the order of
d^{\lvert AB\rvert}/\epsilon^{2}

Elben et al. 2020
, where
d
denotes the single-spin Hilbert space dimension and
\lvert AB\rvert
the total number of spins. Therefore, the randomized measurement toolbox breaks down in the CV regime.

To overcome this limitation, we propose a readout scheme using the multicopy method
Brun 2004
(see also
Ekert et al. 2002
;
Alves et al. 2003
). This technique has been successfully applied to entanglement witnessing in various bosonic systems
Daley et al. 2012
;
Islam et al. 2015
;
Kaufman et al. 2016
;
Gray et al. 2018
;
Griffet et al. 2023b
. It relies on the central observation that any
n
th order polynomial of the state
\bm{\rho}
is measurable via a suitably chosen multicopy observable
\bm{O}_{n}
defined over
n
replicas of the state, denoted by
\bm{\rho}^{\otimes n}
. For CV optical systems, this observable can then be simply accessed by transforming it to particle-number measurements using passive, i.e., particle-number-preserving, transformations
Hertz et al. 2019
;
Arnhem et al. 2022
;
Griffet et al. 2023a
;
Griffet et al. 2023b
. Counting particles is well established experimentally and is a standard requirement for photonic quantum computing
Knill et al. 2001
;
Kok et al. 2007
;
Slussarenko and Pryde 2019
;
Romero and Milburn 2024
, integrated circuits
Moody et al. 2022
, quantum gas microscopes
Bakr et al. 2009
;
Sherson et al. 2010
;
Hume et al. 2013
;
Gross and Bakr 2021
, and when the system under scrutiny lacks a phase-stable local oscillator, e.g., in High Harmonic Generation (HHG)
Fuchs et al. 2022
.

Given a multicopy observable
\bm{O}_{n}
, the remaining task is to find a passive unitary transformation
U
appropriate for measuring
\bm{O}_{n}
by counting particles at the output,

(\bm{a}_{1},\dots,\bm{a}_{n})^{T}\to U(\bm{a}_{1},\dots,\bm{a}_{n})^{T},

(8)

which can be efficiently decomposed into beam splitters

B_{\mu\nu}(\tau)=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\tau}&\sqrt{1-\tau}\\
\sqrt{1-\tau}&-\sqrt{\tau}\end{pmatrix},

(9)

of transmissivity
\tau\in[0,1]
, and phase shifts

R_{\mu}(\varphi)=e^{-i\varphi},

(10)

with phase
\varphi\in[0,2\pi)
, acting on modes
\mu,\nu=1,...,n

;
Griffet et al. 2023a
and has been successfully implemented in cold-atom systems
Islam et al. 2015
;
Kaufman et al. 2016
, and integrated photonic devices
Goldberg et al. 2023
. It is well known that the two-mode DFT is nothing but a 50:50 beam splitter operation, i.e.,

\text{F}(2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}.

(19)

Note that in this case, the DFT matrix is involutory, i.e.,
\text{F}^{-1}(2)=\text{F}(2)
since a shift in any direction corresponds to the swap operation, which is another way of seeing that
p_{2}
equals the purity. Hence, a single beam splitter on each side suffices for Alice and Bob to access
p_{2}
.

The multicopy observable after the beam splitter has been applied reads

p_{2}=\braket{\braket{\omega_{2}^{\bm{N}_{2}^{A}}\otimes\omega_{2}^{\bm{N}_{2}^{B}}}}=\braket{(-1)^{N_{2}^{A}+N_{2}^{B}}},

(20)

and relates to a parity measurement of the total particle number across the second modes, specifically, the output modes displaying destructive interference. Thus, the experimental procedure consists of Alice and Bob counting the number of particles in these modes, sharing their information via classical communication, and assigning the values
+1
or
-1
for an even or odd
total
number of particles, respectively, and thereupon averaging over sufficiently many experimental runs, see Fig.
1b)
.

III.3.2
Third-order PT-moment
p_{3}

Following (

\text{F}(3)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\
1&e^{-i\frac{2\pi}{3}}&e^{i\frac{2\pi}{3}}\\
1&e^{i\frac{2\pi}{3}}&e^{-i\frac{2\pi}{3}}\end{pmatrix},

(21)

whose implementation requires a combination of three beam splitters and phase shifts each, namely,

\begin{split}\text{F}(3)&=R_{3}\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right)R_{2}\left(-\frac{\pi}{6}\right)\text{B}_{23}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\\
&\hskip 11.38092pt\times R_{3}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\text{B}_{13}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)\text{B}_{12}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right),\end{split}

(22)

see Fig.
1c)

\pm\pi/6
(light gray) can be left out. Then, the third-order PT-moment

\begin{split}p_{3}&=\braket{\braket{\braket{\omega_{3}^{\bm{N}_{2}^{A}+2\bm{N}_{3}^{A}}\otimes\omega_{3}^{2\bm{N}_{2}^{B}+\bm{N}_{3}^{B}}}}}\\
&=\braket{(e^{-2\pi i/3})^{N_{2}^{A}+2N_{3}^{A}+2N_{2}^{B}+N_{3}^{B}}}.\end{split}

(23)

Importantly, Alice’s prefactors appearing in the exponent are now interchanged for Bob.

IV
Benchmarks

Next, we benchmark the detection capabilities of the various third-order criteria discussed in Sec.
II.2
. We discuss their performance in the form of a general comparison to other strong criteria, upon which we explore a variety of typical state families. This includes Gaussian states and genuine (also referred to as quantum
Walschaers 2021
) non-Gaussian states – divided into superpositions of coherent or Fock states – including, e.g., NOON states, whose entanglement is known to be hard to certify.

IV.1
Comparison

IV.1.1
Shchukin–Vogel criteria

The well-known Shchukin–Vogel hierarchy constitutes another complete set of criteria testing for PPT
Shchukin and Vogel 2005
;
Miranowicz and Piani 2006
;
Miranowicz et al. 2009
. In contrast to PT-moment-based criteria, they involve moments of mode operators. The central idea is that the partially transposed state
\tilde{\bm{\rho}}
is non-negative if and only if
\textbf{Tr}\{\tilde{\bm{\rho}}\bm{f}^{\dagger}\bm{f}\}\geq 0
for all normally ordered
\bm{f}
. It is convenient to expand
\bm{f}=\sum_{i,j,k,l}c_{ijkl}\,\bm{a}^{\dagger i}\bm{a}^{j}\bm{b}^{\dagger k}\bm{b}^{l}
for some complex-valued
c
, leading to a reformulation in terms of the matrix
D_{qrst,ijkl}=\textbf{Tr}\{\bm{\rho}\,\bm{a}^{\dagger r}\bm{a}^{q}\bm{a}^{\dagger i}\bm{a}^{j}\bm{b}^{\dagger t}\bm{b}^{s}\bm{b}^{\dagger k}\bm{b}^{l}\}
, namely,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q,r,s,t\\
i,j,k,l\end{subarray}}c^{*}_{qrst}c_{ijkl}\,\tilde{D}_{qrst,ijkl}\geq 0,

(24)

for all
c
. This is, in fact, equivalent to all principal minors of
\tilde{D}
being non-negative, as implied by Sylvester’s criterion. A specific criterion is then obtained by choosing a certain
\bm{f}
(or equivalently
c
), which corresponds to selecting some submatrix of
\tilde{D}
. For instance, setting
\bm{f}=c_{1}+c_{2}\bm{a}+c_{3}\bm{b}
leads to a second-order criterion which is necessary and sufficient for Gaussian entanglement.

It is natural to ask how (
24
) relates to PT-moment-based criteria. When considered over
all

c
, the three
infinite
hierarchies (
24
), (
2
) and (
5
) are all equivalent to PPT. Still, their main conceptual difference is that while practically useful criteria of the Shchukin–Vogel hierarchy comprise only a few low-order mode-operator moments, PT-moments can contain mode-operator moments up to the highest order of the underlying state.

To make a more precise statement, we derive a relation between the linear
p_{3}
-PPT criterion (
6
) and (
24
). Starting from the state’s normally ordered expansion
\bm{\rho}=\sum_{i,j,k,l}\rho_{ijkl}\bm{a}^{\dagger i}\bm{a}^{j}\bm{b}^{\dagger k}\bm{b}^{l}
for complex-valued
\rho
, normally ordering its powers allows to write any PT-moment as

p_{n}=\sum_{i,j,k,l}[\mathfrak{p}_{n-1}(\rho)]_{ijkl}\,\tilde{d}_{ijlk},

(25)

where
d
denotes the matrix of mode-operator moments
d_{ijkl}=\textbf{Tr}\{\bm{\rho}\,\bm{a}^{\dagger i}\bm{a}^{j}\bm{b}^{\dagger k}\bm{b}^{l}\}
. Note here that
\tilde{d}_{ijlk}=d_{ijkl}
, and hence moments of the partially transposed state correspond to reordered moments of the state itself. Further,
\mathfrak{p}_{n}
are polynomials in
\rho
of order
n
which arise by repeatedly applying the bosonic commutation relations to ensure normal order in the trace. For the two lowest-order polynomials
\mathfrak{p}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{p}_{2}
, which are required for
p_{2}
and
p_{3}
, respectively, we obtain

\begin{split}[\mathfrak{p}_{1}(x)]_{ijkl}&=x,\\
[\mathfrak{p}_{2}(x)]_{ijkl}&=\sum_{q,r,s,t}\sum_{u=0}^{\min(q,s)}\sum_{v=0}^{\min(r,t)}\,u!\,v!\\
&\hskip 14.22636pt\times\binom{q}{u}\binom{s}{u}\binom{r}{v}\binom{t}{v}\\
&\hskip 14.22636pt\times x_{i-s+u\,q\,r\,l-t+v}\,x_{s\,j-q+u\,k-r+v\,t}.\end{split}

(26)

We find a similar decomposition for the Shchukin–Vogel criteria after normally ordering
\bm{f}^{\dagger}\bm{f}
, namely,

\textbf{Tr}\{\tilde{\bm{\rho}}\bm{f}^{\dagger}\bm{f}\}=\sum_{i,j,k,l}[\mathfrak{f}_{2}(c)]_{ijkl}\,\tilde{d}_{ijlk}\geq 0,

(27)

where
\mathfrak{f}_{2}
is defined in analogy to
\mathfrak{p}_{2}
, with the main difference being that indices are slightly reordered since
\mathfrak{f}_{2}
stems from
\bm{f}^{\dagger}\bm{f}
in contrast to
\tilde{\bm{\rho}}^{2}
for
\mathfrak{p}_{2}
. In general, the map
c\to\mathfrak{f}_{2}(c)
is not surjective for complex-valued
c
. However, when identifying linear combinations of (
25
) with (
27
),
\mathfrak{f}_{2}(c)
must be hermitian, in which case it can always be expressed as a bilinear form of some complex-valued
c
.

Therefore, the linear
p_{3}
-PPT criterion (
6
) equals the state-dependent Shchukin–Vogel criterion (
27
) with the matrix
c
being defined implicitly via
[\mathfrak{f}_{2}(c)]_{ijkl}=[\mathfrak{p}_{2}(\rho)]_{ijkl}-(3/2)\rho_{ijkl}+1/2
. This shows that the set of mode-operator moments constrained by a PT-moment-based criterion depends solely on the state and can, in principle, range up to arbitrarily high orders – a crucial property for capturing higher-order correlations (we provide a worked-out example for NOON states in App.
D.1
). Given that the efficient readout of low-order Shchukin–Vogel criteria also requires multiple copies
Griffet et al. 2023b
, the herein proposed scheme is expected to outperform the Shchukin–Vogel method for genuine non-Gaussian states in terms of both detection capability and experimental resources (see, e.g., Sec.
IV.4.1
).

IV.1.2
Criteria based on non-local variables

In another common approach, PPT is assessed via the non-local quadrature operators
\bm{x}_{\pm}=\bm{x}_{A}\pm\bm{x}_{B}
and
\bm{p}_{\pm}=\bm{p}_{A}\pm\bm{p}_{B}
from the EPR argument
Einstein et al. 1935
. By noting that taking the partial transpose in subsystem
B
translates into
p_{B}\to-p_{B}
in phase space
Simon 2000
, physicality (meaning non-negativity) of
\tilde{\bm{\rho}}
implies that uncertainty relations must be fulfilled by the mixed commuting variable pairs
(\bm{x}_{\pm},\bm{p}_{\mp})
for all separable states.

Many entanglement criteria have been formulated in terms of the outcome distributions of a homodyne measurement
f_{\pm}(x_{\pm})=\braket{x_{\pm}|\bm{\rho}|x_{\pm}}
,
g_{\mp}(p_{\mp})=\braket{p_{\mp}|\bm{\rho}|p_{\mp}}
. This includes constraints on their second moments, such as
\sigma_{x_{\pm}}^{2}+\sigma_{p_{\mp}}^{2}\geq 2

Duan et al. 2000
(see also
Simon 2000
;
Werner and Wolf 2001
;
Mancini et al. 2002
;
Giovannetti et al. 2003
;
Lami et al. 2018
), and entropies, like
S(f_{\pm})+S(g_{\mp})\geq\ln(2\pi e)

Walborn et al. 2009
(see also
Walborn et al. 2011
;
Saboia et al. 2011
;
Tasca et al. 2013
;
Schneeloch and Howland 2018
). Recently, entanglement criteria were introduced for the Husimi
Q
-distribution
Q_{\pm}(x_{\pm},p_{\mp})
associated with a heterodyne measurement, ranging from entropic variants
S(Q_{\pm})\geq\ln(2e)

Floerchinger et al. 2022
(see also
Floerchinger et al. 2021
) to majorization relations
Q_{\pm}\prec Q_{\pm,\text{vacuum}}

Gärttner et al. 2023a
;
Gärttner et al. 2023b
.

While all these methods fully characterize Gaussian entanglement, the entropic criteria also witness some forms of non-Gaussian entanglement, such as superpositions of Gaussian states, e.g., the entangled cat state (see Sec.
IV.3.1
) and close-to-Gaussian states, e.g., the Hermite-Gauss state [see Eq. (
45
)]. However, entanglement cannot be certified over the whole parameter range in both cases. Additionally, genuine non-Gaussian entanglement arising from finite superpositions in Fock space is entirely overlooked. Thus, such methods are efficient when dealing with Gaussian and Gaussian-like entanglement, but remain insensitive beyond.

Intuitively, this is rooted in how (quantum) correlations are analyzed: the non-local variables
(x_{\pm},p_{\mp})
are linear and hence capture mostly linear correlations between the two subsystems, while lacking information about higher-order correlations. Therefore, we expect our method to outrange all criteria based on EPR-type variables in the genuine non-Gaussian regime (see, e.g., Sec.
IV.4
).

IV.2
Gaussian entanglement

IV.2.1
Symplectic formalism and Simon’s criterion

It is convenient to group the two pairs of canonical operators into a single vector in phase space as

\bm{\chi}=(\bm{X}_{1},\bm{P}_{1},\bm{X}_{2},\bm{P}_{2})^{T}.

(28)

Then, the canonical commutation relations take the form
\left[\bm{\chi}_{j},\bm{\chi}_{j^{\prime}}\right]=i\Omega_{jj^{\prime}}
, where
\Omega=\mathds{1}_{2}\otimes(i\sigma_{2})
denotes the symplectic metric. A partially transposed Gaussian state is fully characterized by its mean and covariance matrix

\tilde{\chi}=\textbf{Tr}\left\{\tilde{\bm{\rho}}\,\bm{\chi}\right\},\quad\tilde{\gamma}_{jj^{\prime}}=\textbf{Tr}\left\{\tilde{\bm{\rho}}\,\{\bm{\chi}_{j},\bm{\chi}_{j^{\prime}}\}\right\},

(29)

respectively. While the former is irrelevant for separability, the latter is non-negative for all states but fulfills the stronger condition
\tilde{\gamma}+i\Omega\geq 0
if and only if
\bm{\rho}
is Gaussian and separable, which is known as Simon’s criterion
Simon 2000
(other well-known criteria have been introduced by Duan et al.
Duan et al. 2000
and MGVT
Mancini et al. 2002
;
Giovannetti et al. 2003
). It can be recast into a simple form in terms of the so-called symplectic eigenvalues of
\tilde{\gamma}
. To this end, we employ Williamson’s theorem
Williamson 1936
, which ensures the existence of a symplectic matrix
\mathcal{S}
that diagonalizes the covariance matrix such that
\mathcal{S}\,\tilde{\gamma}\,\mathcal{S}^{T}=\text{diag}(\tilde{\nu}_{1},\tilde{\nu}_{1},\tilde{\nu}_{2},\tilde{\nu}_{2})
, where
\tilde{\nu}_{1}
and
\tilde{\nu}_{2}
denote the symplectic eigenvalues. Hence, a Gaussian state is separable if and only if

\tilde{\nu}_{1},\tilde{\nu}_{2}\geq 1,

(30)

see the straight black curves in Fig.
2a)
and
2b)
.

IV.2.2
p_{n}
-PPT criteria for Gaussian states

A closed formula for the
n
th moment of a Gaussian state in terms of its symplectic eigenvalues has been derived in
Serafini 2017
;
Seshadreesan et al. 2018
. For the partially transposed state, it reads

p_{n}=\prod_{j=1}^{2}\,\frac{2^{n}}{(\tilde{\nu}_{j}+1)^{n}-(\tilde{\nu}_{j}-1)^{n}}.

(31)

For
n=2
and
n=3
, the formula evaluates to

p_{2}=\frac{1}{\tilde{\nu}_{1}\tilde{\nu}_{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det\tilde{\gamma}}},\quad p_{3}=\frac{16}{\left(1+3\tilde{\nu}_{1}^{2}\right)\left(1+3\tilde{\nu}_{2}^{2}\right)},

(32)

respectively. Hence, a Gaussian state is pure if and only if the product of symplectic eigenvalues equals unity, i.e.,
\tilde{\nu}_{1}\tilde{\nu}_{2}=1
. This implies that all states have to respect the physicality condition
\tilde{\nu}_{1}\tilde{\nu}_{2}\geq 1
(see dashed black curves), which is equivalent to
\tilde{\gamma}
being non-negative (
\tilde{\nu}_{1}\tilde{\nu}_{2}=\nu_{1}\nu_{2}
is ensured by symplectic matrices having unit determinant).

With (
32
) at hand, we can express all PT-moment-based criteria in terms of the symplectic eigenvalues
(\tilde{\nu}_{1},\tilde{\nu}_{2})
only. The quadratic
p_{3}
-PPT criterion reads (blue curves)

7\tilde{\nu}_{1}^{2}\tilde{\nu}_{2}^{2}-3\left(\tilde{\nu}_{1}^{2}+\tilde{\nu}_{2}^{2}\right)-1\geq 0,

(33)

while the linear criterion evaluates to (green curves)

(\tilde{\nu}_{1}^{2}+3\tilde{\nu}_{1}^{2}\tilde{\nu}_{2}^{2}+\tilde{\nu}_{2}^{2})(\tilde{\nu}_{1}\tilde{\nu}_{2}-3)+11\tilde{\nu}_{1}\tilde{\nu}_{2}-1\geq 0.

(34)

It is straightforward to check that both conditions and the optimal criterion (red curves) are implied by Simon’s criterion (
30
), showing that all three are necessary but not sufficient conditions for Gaussian separability. Sufficiency is retained in the limit
n\to\infty
, see App.
A.2
, in accordance with PPT being equivalent to separability for
1\times 1
Gaussian states
Werner and Wolf 2001
. However, one might argue that copying an entangled Gaussian state in order to witness its entanglement is excessive.

IV.2.3
Optimal PT-moment-based criterion for Gaussian entanglement

When expressing Simon’s criterion (
30
) through the two lowest-order PT-moments
p_{2}
and
p_{3}
, we find that a Gaussian state is separable if and only if

p_{3}\geq\frac{4p_{2}^{2}}{3+p_{2}^{2}},

(35)

which constitutes the optimal third-order criterion for Gaussian entanglement; see straight black curves. However, note that this criterion is biased in the sense that it only holds under the assumption that the underlying state is of Gaussian form. Translating also the physicality condition
\tilde{\nu}_{1}\tilde{\nu_{2}}\geq 1
to PT-moments by using
\tilde{\nu_{2}}=1/(\tilde{\nu_{1}}p_{2})
for
p_{3}
and maximizing over
\tilde{\nu_{1}}
yields an upper bound constraining all Gaussian states (see dashed black curves)

p_{3}\leq\left(\frac{4p_{2}}{3+p_{2}}\right)^{2}.

(36)

IV.3.2

Assessing entanglement by counting photons is particularly relevant for applications where other standard readout methods are inapplicable. IV.4
Genuine non-Gaussian entanglement from Fock states

IV.4.1

Entanglement detection is hardest when considering finite superpositions in Fock space. Of particular importance is the class of bosonic NOON states
Sanders 1989

\ket{\psi}=\alpha\ket{N,0}+\beta\ket{0,N},

(41)

with mode population

V
Application

Robustness to experimental imperfections is an essential trait of any practically useful entanglement criterion. First, we discuss typical sources of errors encountered in our detection schemes and how to model them. Then, we explore their influence in detail for NOON states.

V.1
Imperfections

We are interested in the three main types of imperfections: the inherent losses that occur in state preparation, the optical circuit and detection, the noise-induced discrepancies between different state copies, and the limitations of finite measurement statistics.

V.1.1
Losses

Particle losses are inevitable in the preparation process of a given input state
\bm{\rho}
, the optical circuit, and the detection of single particles. With advances in superconducting photon-number-resolving detectors, efficiencies in photonic setups are now on the order of
\sim 95\%

Marsili et al. 2013
;
Reddy et al. 2020
and single photons can be resolved up to
100
photons
Cheng et al. 2023
, see also
Slussarenko and Pryde 2019
and references therein. In ultracold atom experiments, quantum gas microscopes enable single-particle resolution up to
\sim 1000
particles
Bakr et al. 2009
;
Sherson et al. 2010
;
Hume et al. 2013
, see
Gross and Bakr 2021
for a review. Therefore, losses are most detrimental during state preparation and optical processing.

The effect of losses is conveniently characterized by a lossy quantum channel
\mathcal{L}_{\tau}
, where
\tau
is the loss parameter, being unity in the lossless case
Weedbrook et al. 2012
. Since losses can primarily be attributed to the optical circuit, we assume equal losses for both subsystems
A
and
B
, as well as among the copies of
\bm{\rho}
(on average, see below), which results in the input-state modification

\bm{\rho}\to(\mathcal{L}_{\tau}\otimes\mathcal{L}_{\tau})(\bm{\rho}).

(46)

This quantum channel can be represented via its Stinespring dilation as the interference of the state of interest with a vacuum state
\ket{0}\bra{0}_{E}
on a beam splitter with transmissivity
\tau\in[0,1]
, after which the environmental mode
E
is traced out, to wit,

\mathcal{L}_{\tau}(\bm{\rho}_{A})=\textbf{Tr}_{E}\left\{B_{AE}(\tau)\left(\bm{\rho}_{A}\otimes\ket{0}\bra{0}_{E}\right)B_{AE}^{\dagger}(\tau)\right\},

(47)

for subsystem
A
, and analogously for
B
.

V.1.2
Noisy copies

In real-world experiments, input-state and circuit parameters can vary from one experimental realization to another. The resulting variations among the state copies and subsequent experimental runs are commonly referred to as noise. We account for both effects by drawing any continuous parameter
x
, such as the complex phase
\alpha
of the input state or the loss parameter
\tau
, from a narrow Gaussian distribution with standard deviation
\delta x\leq x/10
centered around the corresponding expected value
x
.

We note that noise is sometimes accounted for by replacing the environmental vacuum state in (
47
) with a thermal state, where the mean particle number acts as the noise parameter, corresponding to a noisy and lossy channel
Weedbrook et al. 2012
. Although we expect the resulting effects to be very similar, this modeling presents two practical drawbacks. First, mixing the input with a thermal state leads to (exponentially suppressed) excitations of arbitrarily high Fock states. Therefore, any reasonable numerical simulation of our method would require truncating the Fock spectrum above a specific threshold. Second, while this approach handles the noise during state preparation and within the optical circuit, it does not take into account fluctuations between different experimental realizations.

V.1.3
Finite statistics

In every experiment, an observable of interest is ultimately estimated from a finite number of repetitions of the experiment, which can greatly influence the statistical significance of the expected outcome. In the context of entanglement detection, where the standard smoking-gun signal is the negativity of some witness function, finite statistics can significantly deteriorate detection capabilities by resulting in small confidence intervals.

We analyze the statistical behavior of the PT-moments and the third-order criteria in terms of the means and variances of their estimators when sampled from
k
independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) experimental runs. We define the estimate of
p_{n}
based on
k
samples as

p_{n}^{(k)}\equiv\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{n,(i)},

(48)

where
p_{n,(i)}=\omega_{n}^{\sum_{j=1}^{n}(j-1)[N_{j,(i)}^{A}-N_{j,(i)}^{B}]}
with
N_{j,(i)}^{A}
representing the number of particles in Alice’s
j
th mode during experiment
i
(analogously for Bob) and
p_{n,(i)}
being an i.i.d. random variable.

By linearity of the expectation value, we have

\text{E}[p_{n}^{(k)}]=\text{E}[p_{n,(i)}]=p_{n}.

(49)

Further,
\text{Var}[p_{n,(i)}]=\text{E}[\lvert p_{n,(i)}\rvert^{2}]-\lvert\text{E}[p_{n,(i)}]\rvert^{2}=1-p_{n}^{2}
, where
\lvert p_{n,(i)}\rvert=1
since
p_{n,(i)}
is one of the
n
roots of unity. Then, the variance of
p_{n}^{(k)}
is simply

\text{Var}[p_{n}^{(k)}]=\frac{1-p_{n}^{2}}{k}.

(50)

Upon defining the estimators for the linear and quadratic criteria given in (
6
) as

\begin{split}W_{l}^{(k)}&\equiv p_{3}^{(k)}-\frac{3p_{2}^{(k)}-1}{2},\\
W_{q}^{(k)}&\equiv p_{3}^{(k)}-\frac{k(p_{2}^{(k)})^{2}-1}{k-1},\end{split}

(51)

which ensures unbiased estimators
\text{E}[W_{l}^{(k)}]=p_{3}-(3p_{2}-1)/2
and
\text{E}[W_{q}^{(k)}]=p_{3}-p_{2}^{2}
, we obtain the variances

\begin{split}\text{Var}[W_{l}^{(k)}]&=\frac{1-p_{3}^{2}}{k}+\frac{9}{4}\frac{1-p_{2}^{2}}{k},\\
\text{Var}[W_{q}^{(k)}]&=\frac{1-p_{3}^{2}}{k}+\frac{2(1-p_{2}^{2})[1+(2k-3)p_{2}^{2}]}{k(k-1)}.\end{split}

(52)

VI
Discussion

In summary, we generalized entanglement criteria based on moments of the density operator’s partial transpose to continuous variables and exploited the multicopy approach in order to access low-order PT-moments. Our measurement schemes
use linear interferometers and a few replicas of the state, together with particle-number-resolving detectors. Compared to standard approaches, our method exhibits strong advantages regarding both detection capability – especially in the genuine non-Gaussian regime – and robustness against experimental imperfections, including losses, noise, and finite statistics. In particular, we demonstrated that our criteria can witness higher-order NOON-type entanglement under realistic experimental conditions, highlighting their usefulness in practical applications such as photonic quantum processors.

The need for interferometric stability over several state copies – though not necessarily perfect – is arguably the most significant experimental burden of our method. Importantly, our analysis of NOON states showed that the possibility of false-positive entanglement detection can be excluded. However, we believe it is of particular interest to extend our criteria, and more generally the multicopy method, to arbitrary ancilla states, which would further reduce the experimental hurdle. We refer to
Callus et al. 2025
for a recently introduced entanglement detection scheme where higher-order correlations of a given bipartite state are accessed via its interference with an arbitrary two-mode ancilla.

We consider several other directions to be interesting for future work. First, one may extend our method to capture moments of other operators resulting from applying positive but not completely positive maps. This includes, for instance, the realignment map
Rudolph 2005
;
Zhang et al. 2013
;
Hertz et al. 2021
, for which moment-based criteria have been derived recently in
Aggarwal and Adhikari 2024
. Compared to the PT-moments approach, this could enable the detection of bound entanglement, which is known to be scarce in CV systems
Horodecki et al. 2003
.

Second, since we have exclusively discussed a two-mode bipartition, one may generalize our setup to both multimode and multipartite cases to analyze many-body and/or multipartite entanglement. The extension to a multimode bipartition is straightforward: the optical circuits for measuring PT-moments have to be applied to each mode individually, upon which the particle numbers within one subsystem are summed up. As the number of copies remains constant, the total system size still scales linearly with the number of target state modes. Further, a possible generalization that would allow one to certify multipartite entanglement could be based on the corresponding variant of the PPT criterion
Jungnitsch et al. 2011
.

Third, in a somewhat different direction, one may ask whether the preferred readout scheme in the discrete-variable case, that is, randomized measurements in spin bases, can be transferred directly to the CV regime via the Jordan-Schwinger map
Dubus et al. 2024
, see also
Gandhari et al. 2024
;
Becker et al. 2024
for CV shadow tomography. This would not only constitute another way of accessing PT-moments, but could be a general alternative to the multicopy method. Since the required number of randomized measurements diverges for an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, one would have to impose a finite energy (or particle-number) cutoff, which is fairly reasonable from an experimental perspective and a standard procedure in CV quantum information
Weedbrook et al. 2012
.

A.1

. We end up with

\begin{split}\hskip-4.83691pt\rho_{ijkl}&=\lvert\alpha\rvert^{2}\frac{(-1)^{i+k-N}}{N!\,(i-N)!\,k!}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}\Theta(i-N)\\
&\hskip 8.5359pt+\lvert\beta\rvert^{2}\frac{(-1)^{i+k-N}}{N!\,i!(k-N)!}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}\Theta(k-N)\\
&\hskip 8.5359pt+\alpha\beta^{*}\frac{(-1)^{j+k}}{N!\,j!\,k!}\delta_{i\,j+N}\,\delta_{k\,l-N}\Theta(i-N)\Theta(l-N)\\
&\hskip 8.5359pt+\alpha^{*}\beta\frac{(-1)^{i+l}}{N!\,i!\,l!}\delta_{i\,j-N}\,\delta_{k\,l+N}\Theta(j-N)\Theta(k-N),\end{split}

(59)

where
\Theta(x)
denotes the Heaviside
\Theta
-function (with the convention
\Theta(0)=1
understood). This leads to the expansion for the purity

\begin{split}p_{2}&\sim\frac{\lvert\alpha\rvert^{2}}{N!}\left[\braket{\bm{a}^{\dagger N}\bm{a}^{N}}+\braket{\bm{a}^{\dagger(N+\dots)}\bm{a}^{N+\dots}\bm{b}^{\dagger(0+\dots)}\bm{b}^{0+\dots}}\right]\\
&\hskip 8.5359pt+\frac{\lvert\beta\rvert^{2}}{N!}\left[\braket{\bm{b}^{\dagger N}\bm{b}^{N}}+\braket{\bm{a}^{\dagger(0+\dots)}\bm{a}^{0+\dots}\bm{b}^{\dagger(N+\dots)}\bm{b}^{N+\dots}}\right]\\
&\hskip 8.5359pt+\frac{\alpha\beta^{*}}{N!}\left[\braket{\bm{a}^{\dagger N}\bm{b}^{N}}+\braket{\bm{a}^{\dagger(N+\dots)}\bm{a}^{0+\dots}\bm{b}^{\dagger(0+\dots)}\bm{b}^{N+\dots}}\right]\\
&\hskip 8.5359pt+\frac{\alpha^{*}\beta}{N!}\left[\braket{\bm{a}^{N}\bm{b}^{\dagger N}}+\braket{\bm{a}^{\dagger(0+\dots)}\bm{a}^{N+\dots}\bm{b}^{\dagger(N+\dots)}\bm{b}^{0+\dots}}\right],\end{split}

(60)

where
\dots
indicates higher-order contributions. Only the first term in each row survives, and the purity reduces to

\begin{split}p_{2}&=\frac{1}{N!}\Big[\lvert\alpha\rvert^{2}\braket{\bm{a}^{\dagger N}\bm{a}^{N}}+\lvert\beta\rvert^{2}\braket{\bm{b}^{\dagger N}\bm{b}^{N}}\\
&\hskip 22.76228pt+\alpha\beta^{*}\braket{\bm{a}^{\dagger N}\bm{b}^{N}}+\alpha^{*}\beta\braket{\bm{a}^{N}\bm{b}^{\dagger N}}\Big].\end{split}

(61)

Similarly, we find for the third PT-moment

p_{3}=\frac{1}{N!}\left[\lvert\alpha\rvert^{4}\braket{\bm{a}^{\dagger N}\bm{a}^{N}}+\lvert\beta\rvert^{4}\braket{\bm{b}^{\dagger N}\bm{b}^{N}}\right].

(62)

It is straightforward to verify consistency of the latter two equations with
p_{2}=1
and (
42
) since
\braket{\bm{a}^{\dagger N}\bm{a}^{N}}=\lvert\alpha\rvert^{2}N!
(analogously for
\bm{b}
), and
\braket{\bm{a}^{N}\bm{b}^{\dagger N}}=\braket{\bm{a}^{\dagger N}\bm{b}^{N}}^{*}=\alpha\beta^{*}N!
for NOON states.

Eqs. (
61
) and (
62
) nicely demonstrate the main strength of PT-moment-based entanglement criteria: in contrast to entropic and Shchukin–Vogel-type criteria, where higher-order mode-operator moments are exponentially suppressed or hard to access, respectively, PT-moments often select the right (and in principle arbitrarily high) mode-operator moments that contain the necessary information about quantum correlations.

D.2
Lossy NOON states

Since we account for noisy copies by fluctuating state parameters, we consider in general different losses for Alice and Bob. Then, the lossy NOON state reads

\bm{\rho}=(\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{A}}\otimes\mathcal{L}_{\tau_{B}})\,(\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}),

(63)

where
\ket{\psi}
is the lossless NOON state as defined in (
41
). Direct calculation leads to

\begin{split}\bm{\rho}=&|\alpha|^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\binom{N}{k}\tau_{A}^{(N-k)}(1-\tau_{A})^{k}\ket{N-k,0}\bra{N-k,0}\\
&+\sqrt{\tau_{A}\tau_{B}}^{N}\big(\alpha\beta^{*}\ket{N,0}\bra{0,N}+\alpha^{*}\beta\ket{0,N}\bra{N,0}\big)\\
&+|\beta|^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\binom{N}{k}\tau_{B}^{(N-k)}(1-\tau_{B})^{k}\ket{0,N-k}\bra{0,N-k}.\end{split}

(64)

The PT-moments evaluate to

\begin{split}p_{2}&=|\alpha|^{4}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\binom{N}{k}^{2}\tau_{A}^{2(N-k)}(1-\tau_{A})^{2k}\\
&+2|\alpha|^{2}|\beta|^{2}\big(\tau_{A}^{N}\tau_{B}^{N}+(1-\tau_{A})^{N}(1-\tau_{B})^{N}\big)\\
&+|\beta|^{4}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\binom{N}{k}^{2}\tau_{B}^{2(N-k)}(1-\tau_{B})^{2k},\end{split}

(65)

and

\begin{split}p_{3}&=|\alpha|^{6}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\binom{N}{k}^{3}\tau_{A}^{3(N-k)}(1-\tau_{A})^{3k}\\
&+3|\alpha|^{2}|\beta|^{2}\bigg(|\alpha|^{2}\tau_{A}^{N}\tau_{B}^{N}(1-\tau_{A})^{N}+|\beta|^{2}\tau_{A}^{N}\tau_{B}^{N}(1-\tau_{B})^{N}\\
&+|\alpha|^{2}(1-\tau_{A})^{2N}(1-\tau_{B})^{N}+|\beta|^{2}(1-\tau_{A})^{N}(1-\tau_{B})^{2N}\bigg)\\
&+|\beta|^{6}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\binom{N}{k}^{3}\tau_{B}^{3(N-k)}(1-\tau_{B})^{3k},\end{split}

(66)

where we considered the copies to be identical for brevity. Please note that the expressions underlying Sec.
V.2
describe the more general case of unequal copies.

Statistical error models

We validate the consistency between the statistical error models discussed in Sec.

and the numerical sampling of the third-order criteria for the noiseless and balanced (

) NOON state of order
N=1

52

\tau\gtrsim 0.6
, which comprises all cases considered in the main text, in particular, Fig.
3c)
and Fig.
4
