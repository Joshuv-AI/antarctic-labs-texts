Generation of a scalar vortex in a rotational frame
M. Bordag, D. N. Voskresensky

Abstract
We consider generation from the vacuum of a scalar charged field in a rigidly rotating frame. Adding an external magnetic field opens the way to Bose condensation of the field. This phenomenon has been studied for external uniform magnetic field occupying the whole volume of the uniformly rotating cylindrical system of finite radius R R with a Dirichlet boundary condition imposed on it. Besides continuing this study, we consider the field formed by a flux tube of small radius. We find numerical solutions of the Ginzburg-Pitaevskii equation for the charged scalar field, the critical rotation frequencies, the mean radii and the condensate energies, and compare them with those found in a linearization scheme and with approximate analytical solutions. We show that for the same input parameters the energy of the condensate in the case of the flux tube is lower than in the case of uniform magnetic field in the whole cylinder.

2
Basic formulas and notations

2.1
Resting and rotating frames

We start with the notations for four- and three-dimensional vectors. The latter are denoted by an arrow.
Coordinates and fields are taken as contravariant vectors, whereas the derivatives are covariant,

\displaystyle x^{\mu}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x_{0}\\
\vec{x}\end{array}\right),\qquad A^{\mu}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}A_{0}\\
\vec{A}\end{array}\right),\qquad\partial_{\mu}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\partial_{0}\\
\nabla\end{array}\right).

We start with a resting frame. Here, coordinates are marked by an index ’
R
’.
For the spatial part we use the cylindrical coordinates in parallel to the Cartesian ones,

\displaystyle\vec{x}_{R}=r\vec{e}_{r}(\varphi_{R}),\qquad A_{R}^{\mu}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}A_{R,0}(x_{R})\\
\vec{A}_{R}(x_{R})\end{array}\right),

and
A_{R}^{\mu}
is some vector field.
The radius
r=\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}
will not be transformed and is left without index
R
.
In the following we are concerned only with fields where the zeroth component is a function of the radius
r
and where the vector part takes the form

\displaystyle A_{R,0}(x_{R})=A_{R,0}(r),\qquad\vec{A}_{R}(x_{R})=\vec{e}_{\varphi}(\varphi_{R})\frac{\mu(r)}{r},\qquad\partial_{R,\mu}\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{R}^{\mu}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\partial_{0}\\
\nabla_{R}\end{array}\right).

Here
A_{R,0}(r)
may be used to describe an electric potential and
\vec{A}(x_{R})
is the vector potential with a dimensionless profile function
\mu(r)
.

The transition from the resting to the rotating frame is achieved by a transformation of the angular variable,

\displaystyle\varphi_{R}=\varphi+\Omega t,\quad t_{R}=t,\quad r_{R}=r,\quad z_{R}=z=x_{3},

(11)

where
the vector of external rotation frequency is
\vec{\Omega}=\Omega\vec{e}_{z}
.
The points
\vec{x}=r\vec{e}_{r}(\varphi)
and
\vec{x}_{R}=r\vec{e}_{r}(\varphi_{R})
in both frames are related by

\displaystyle\vec{x}=\bm{D}^{\top}(\Omega t)\ \vec{x}_{R},\quad\bm{D}(\Omega t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\cos(\Omega t)&-\sin(\Omega t)&0\\
\sin(\Omega t)&\cos(\Omega t)&0\\
0&0&1\end{array}\right),

where
\bm{D}
is the rotation matrix.
The speed of a point in the rotating frame is

\displaystyle\vec{v}=\dot{\vec{x}}=\Omega r\vec{e}_{\varphi}(\varphi)=\vec{\Omega}\times\vec{x}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-\Omega x_{2}\\
\Omega x_{1}\\
0\end{array}\right).

The transformation (
11
) results in a rigidly rotating frame. The speed of light restricts its applicability to
\Omega\,r\leq 1
.

From the transformation between the frames one derives the transformation matrix,

\displaystyle{T^{\mu}}_{\nu}=\frac{\partial x_{R}^{\mu}}{\partial x^{\nu}}={\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&-\vec{v}_{R}^{\top}\\
0&\bm{D}^{\top}(\Omega t)\end{array}\right)^{\mu}}_{\nu},

the interval and the metric,

\displaystyle ds^{2}

\displaystyle=\eta_{\mu\nu}dx_{R}^{\mu}dx_{R}^{\nu}=g_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu},\qquad

(20)

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle={T_{\mu}}^{\mu^{\prime}}\,{T_{\nu}}^{\nu^{\prime}}\eta_{\mu^{\prime}\nu^{\prime}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1-\vec{v}^{2}&-\vec{v}^{\top}\\
-\vec{v}&-\bm{1}\end{array}\right),\quad g^{\mu\nu}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&-\vec{v}^{\top}\\
-\vec{v}&-\bm{1}+\vec{v}\circ\vec{v}^{\top}\end{array}\right).

We mention that the metric (
20
) is flat; all curvature tensors are zero and
\det g=-1
holds.

In the rotating frame

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}={T_{\mu}}^{\nu}\ \partial_{R,\nu}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\partial_{0}-\vec{v}\,\nabla\\
\nabla\end{array}\right),\quad\partial^{\mu}={T^{\mu}}_{\nu}\ \partial_{R}^{\nu}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\partial_{0}\\
-\nabla\end{array}\right),

holds for the derivatives and

\displaystyle A^{\mu}(x)

\displaystyle={T^{\mu}}_{\nu}\ A_{R}^{\nu}(x_{R})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}A_{R,0}(x)\\
-\vec{v}\,A_{R,0}(x)+\vec{A}_{R}(x)\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle A_{\mu}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}A_{R,0}(x)-\vec{v}\,\vec{A}_{R}(x)\\
-\vec{A}_{R}(x)\end{array}\right),

for the field.
Indices are raised and lowered using the metric, as usual.

2.2
Lagrangian and field equation in the rotational frame

The Lagrangian density for a charged scalar or pseudoscalar field in a curved background is

\displaystyle{\cal L}=\sqrt{-g}\left[g^{\mu\nu}\left(D_{\mu}\phi\right)^{*}D_{\nu}\phi-(m^{2}+\xi R)\phi^{*}\phi-\frac{\lambda}{2}(\phi^{*}\phi)^{2}\right],

(33)

with the mass
m
, the coupling
\xi
to the scalar curvature
R
and the coupling constant
\lambda>0
and the ’long’ derivative,

\displaystyle D_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}-ieA_{\mu},

(34)

where
-e
is the charge of the electron,
e^{2}\approx 1/137
,
\partial_{\mu}
in the rotational frame is determined in (
2.1
). Although the most physically meaningful case is that of the pseudoscalar charged pion field, for definiteness let us further speak about the scalar field.
In the rotational frame it is a function of coordinates,
\phi(t,\vec{x})
, which we suppress temporarily for simplicity of the formulas.
The derivatives are partial ones for the time-space part (in the Lorentz indices); since
\phi
is a scalar field no Christoffel symbols enter. Below, since the metric (
20
) is flat, we need neither
\sqrt{-g}
nor the curvature
R
.
We consider the field inside a cylinder of some radius
R
, fulfilling the causality condition
\Omega R\leq 1
and must thereby impose the boundary condition. We take the Dirichlet condition,

\phi(\vec{x})=0
at
r=R
. With this condition, surface terms will not appear when integrating by parts in the Lagrangian.
Now, the Lagrangian density (
33
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle{\cal L}=\phi^{*}\left[-D_{\mu}g^{\mu\nu}D_{\nu}-m^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2}\phi^{*}\phi\right]\phi\,.

(35)

From the minimum of the action corresponding to the Lagrangian density (
35
) one derives the Euler-Lagrange equation (Klein-Gordon equation with interaction),

\displaystyle\left(-(D_{0}-\vec{v}\,\vec{D})^{2}+\vec{D}^{2}-m^{2}-{\lambda}\phi^{*}\phi\right)\phi=0,

(36)

and its complex conjugate. This equation, the GP-like equation for short, is a relativistic generalization of the Gross-Pitaevskii equation for the order parameter. In our case the classical field
\phi
appearing at some conditions from the vacuum as we will see plays the role of the order parameter. The field
A_{\mu}
in this work we treat as an external field, as we have mentioned.

In the following, dealing with a static magnetic background field
\vec{A}_{R}
we put
A_{R,0}=0
in (
2.1
) and (
2.1
). Then we are able to focus on the case of the stationary condensate field
\phi(t,\vec{x})\propto e^{-i\omega t}\phi(\vec{x})
occurring at the corresponding conditions in the rotational frame. The energy density corresponding to the Lagrangian density is
{\cal{E}}=\omega n-{\cal L}
, where

n=\partial{\cal L}/\partial\omega

is the particle density.
With increasing rotation speed
\Omega
, the ground state energy of the boson
\omega
first reaches zero,
\omega=0
, and then dives further down to
-m
. Then in the passive rotation problem bosons of one sign of charge can be produced occupying the level
\omega=-m
in a tunneling process from the lower to the upper continuum and anti-bosons go off to infinity. To be careful, note that in a short-range electric potential well at
mR\gg 1
the anti-particle energy level appears near the boundary of the lower continuum, at
\omega\simeq-m+O(1/R)
, see
[
1
]
. In the presence of the empty rotating “physical” cylinder, instability may occur already when the ground state single-particle energy level reaches zero; then the reactions with production of scalar particles and antiparticles on the surface of the cylinder become possible. Antiparticles are absorbed by the surface of the cylinder. Particles form the classical field
\phi(\vec{x})
inside the cylinder. This mechanism of production of the field
\phi
was discussed in
[
18
,
19
]
.
For
\omega>0
the number of particles,
n
, calculated within the single-particle problem is non-negative, see
[
20
]
. The minimum of
\omega n
corresponds to
\omega=0
.
In this case when the single-particle ground state energy level,
\omega
, reaches zero a new vacuum state can be occupied, characterized by the classical static (
\omega=0
) field
\phi
, which is described by the GP-like equation. The value

\omega=0
plays the role of the chemical potential for the order parameter
\phi
, see
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
.
Equation (
36
) turns then into

\displaystyle\left[-(\vec{v}\,\vec{\nabla})^{2}+\vec{D}^{2}-m^{2}-{\lambda}\phi(\vec{x})^{*}\phi(\vec{x})\right]\phi(\vec{x})=0.

(37)

The energy per unit (longitudinal) length of the classical field
\phi(\vec{x})
in the cylinder follows from the Lagrangian density (
35
) and reads

\displaystyle E_{GP}=\int d^{2}x\left[-|(\vec{v}\,\vec{\nabla})\phi)|^{2}+|\vec{D}\phi|^{2}+m^{2}|\phi|^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}|\phi|^{4}\right],

(38)

or, using eq. (
37
) and integrating by parts,

\displaystyle E_{GP}=-\frac{\lambda}{2}\int d^{2}x\ |\phi|^{4}.

(39)

The GP-like equation (
37
) is nonlinear and does not have an exact analytical solution. So one is left with approximate analytical and numerical ones. As one possibility, we use a linearization as an approximation and check the results by numerical evaluation. In eq. (
37
) we substitute the non-linear term by a constant,

\displaystyle\lambda\phi^{*}(\vec{x})\phi(\vec{x})\to{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}.

(40)

Here we follow
[
21
]
, where this method was discussed recently.
It was shown that this approximation may be considered as a zeroth step in a perturbative expansion in powers of
{\tilde{\epsilon}}
as an expansion parameter.

After substitution (
40
) into (
37
), the linearized equation becomes

\displaystyle\left((\vec{v}\,\vec{\nabla})^{2}-\vec{D}^{2}+m^{2}\right)\phi=-{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}\phi.

(41)

Formally, this looks like a Klein-Gordon equation. The ground state solution with real
{\tilde{\epsilon}}={\tilde{\epsilon}}_{0}
describes a state with the condensate. We mention that at zero temperature, which we consider here, only the lowest level will be populated by the condensate.

3
Magnetic and scalar fields in the rotating frame

3.1
Notations for the background field

In this paper we restrict ourselves to studying the response of the charged scalar field vacuum to application of the external magnetic field of a specific form in the resting frame.
As a background external field we consider a cylindrically-symmetric magnetic vector potential
\vec{A}_{R}(x_{R})
of a solenoid (hereafter flux tube). The relation between variables and fields in the resting and rotational frames is given by eq. (
2.1
). In the rotating frame in variables of the rotational frame
\vec{A}(x)
has the same profile function
\mu(r)
as in the resting frame,

\displaystyle\vec{A}(\vec{x})=\vec{e}_{\varphi}\frac{\mu(r)}{r},\qquad\mu(r)=\frac{B_{0}r^{2}}{2}\Theta(R_{s}-r)+\frac{B_{0}R_{s}^{2}}{2}\Theta(r-R_{s}),\qquad 0<R_{s}<R\,.

(42)

Here the step-function
\Theta(x)=1
for
x\geq 0
and zero for
x<0
.
It describes a homogeneous magnetic field
\vec{B}=B_{0}\vec{e}_{z}
inside a flux tube with radius
R_{s}
and zero field outside,

\displaystyle\vec{B}=\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{A}=\vec{e}_{z}\frac{\mu^{\prime}(r)}{r},\qquad\vec{B}=\vec{e}_{z}\,B_{0}\,\Theta(R_{s}-r).

(43)

For
R_{s}=R
the field (
42
) describes the field in the whole cylinder of radius
R
,
B_{0}=const
. For definiteness let
B_{0}>0
.
The corresponding classical electromagnetic energy per unit length,
E_{el}
, and the flux
\Phi\equiv 2\pi\delta_{\Phi}/e
of the background field are given by
[
13
]
,

\displaystyle\quad E_{el}=\frac{\pi}{8\pi}B_{0}^{2}R_{s}^{2}\equiv\frac{\delta_{\Phi}^{2}}{4e^{2}R_{s}^{2}},\qquad\delta_{\Phi}=\frac{eB_{0}R_{s}^{2}}{2},

(44)

where
\delta_{\Phi}
is a convenient notation below in the Kummer’s function.

In the limit
R_{s}\to R
, the uniform magnetic field fills the whole cylinder.
In the opposite limit of small
R_{s}
, the magnetic field is confined to a narrow flux tube.
Keeping the flux
\Phi
fixed, the magnetic field
B_{0}=\frac{\Phi}{\pi R_{s}^{2}}
becomes large. Mathematically, in the limit
R_{s}\to 0
, it can be defined in terms of a distribution (generalized function) and results in

\displaystyle\vec{B}=\lim\limits_{R_{s}\to 0}\vec{e}_{z}\frac{\mu^{\prime}(r)}{r}=\vec{e}_{z}\Phi\,\delta^{2}(\vec{x}_{(2)}),

(45)

where
\vec{x}_{(2)}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{array}\right)
, and
\delta^{2}(\vec{x}_{(2)})=\delta(x^{1})\delta(x^{2})
is a two dimensional delta function. This idealization is sometimes used to describe an infinitely thin flux tube.
Since we study the case
\mu=\mu(r)
, it is the same in both the resting and rotating frames.

As we have mentioned, in a formal mathematical problem it is sufficient to say that we consider the problem in uniformly rotating frame at
\Omega R<1
.
In a realistic consideration the rotation frame is associated
with a rotating body; in our case with the flux tube of the radius
R_{s}
(empty thin wall cylinder of radius
R_{s}
) placed inside the empty thin-wall cylinder of radius
R\geq R_{s}
, both rotating with the same external rotation frequency
\vec{\Omega}\parallel z
. Bearing in mind future possible applications to heavy-ion collisions we will orient on values of input parameters
\Omega
,

,
R
,
\lambda
,

,
mentioned in the Introduction, relevant for pions in relativistic heavy-ion collisions.

3.2
Ginzburg-Pitaevskii like equation for the condensate

We continue to consider a scalar field
\phi(t,\vec{x})
in the rotating frame given by the metric (
20
) inside a cylinder. Its radius
R
and the rotation frequency
\Omega
are constrained by causality condition
\Omega R<1
. Also we will focus on the physically meaningful case (e.g. for pions in heavy-ion collisions),
Rm\gg 1
.
We are interested in reaction of the charged scalar field vacuum on rotation in presence of the external magnetic field given by (
42
).

Since the background field is static and cylindrically symmetric, we make the corresponding Fourier transforms,

\displaystyle\phi(\vec{x})=e^{il\varphi+ik_{z}z}\phi(r),

(46)

using cylindrical coordinates, after which the field
\phi(r)
can be taken real. The GP-like equation (
37
) turns into

\displaystyle\left((\Omega l)^{2}+\partial_{r}^{2}+\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}-\frac{(l-e\mu(r))^{2}}{r^{2}}-k_{z}^{2}-m^{2}-\lambda\phi^{2}(r)\right)\phi(r)=0,

(47)

where we used (
42
) for the background field, and with (
11
) and (
46
),
\vec{v}\,\vec{\nabla}\phi(\vec{x})\to i\Omega l\phi(\vec{x})
. In (
46
) we employed the translation invariance in z-direction. Since we are interested in the ground state, we put the momentum
k_{z}=0
.

Equation (
47
) must be supplemented by the boundary conditions, the regularity at the origin and Dirichlet condition at the cylinder, i.e.,
\phi(R)=0
. With
\mu(r)
from (
42
) one needs still boundary conditions at
r=R_{s}
. These are continuity of the field
\phi(r)
and its first derivative at
r=R_{s}
. Example of matching of the solutions of eq. (
47
) linearized following the procedure (
40
), (
41
), is discussed in Appendix
A
.
The linearized equation,
following from (
41
),
is as follows

\displaystyle\left((\Omega l)^{2}+\partial_{r}^{2}+\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}-\frac{(l-e\mu(r))^{2}}{r^{2}}-m^{2}\right)\phi(r)={\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}\phi(r).

(48)

3.3
Energy of the linearized GP-like equation

A solution with real
{\tilde{\epsilon}}
owes its existence to the rotation term (first term in the parentheses in the above equation) and it
exists above a certain threshold where the instability sets in. For the same choice of the parameters
at
{\tilde{\epsilon}}=0
both equations, (
41
) and (
37
), coincide. While the solution of the linearized equation is determined only up to a factor, the exact solution has no freedom in rescaling. Thus it will vanish for
{\tilde{\epsilon}}=0
. The factor of proportionality is given by eqs. (21) and (29) in
[
21
]
. So, the suggested linearization scheme is expected to be good for small
{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{0}
.

Inserting the solution
\phi
in eq. (
38
) and using eq. (
41
),
we express the ground state energy of the condensate field
\phi
per unit length as

\displaystyle E_{0}^{lin}=\int d^{2}x\left(-{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}_{0}\alpha^{2}\phi^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\alpha^{4}\phi^{4}\right),

(49)

where
\alpha
is a scale factor of the solution,
\phi\to\alpha\phi
. Although it is arbitrary since eq. (
41
) is linear, its value can be determined by demanding that the energy takes a minimum. One finds

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}=\frac{{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}_{0}}{\lambda}\,\frac{a}{b},

(50)

where
a
and
b
are integrals of the function
\phi
,

\displaystyle a=\int d^{2}x\ \phi^{2},\quad b=\int d^{2}x\ \phi^{4}.

(51)

Therefore proper normalization of the function will be achieved by the substitution

\displaystyle\phi\to\alpha\phi,

(52)

independently of the initially taken function as long as it is used calculating
a
and
b
.

Inserting (
50
) into (
49
), we arrive at the result

\displaystyle E_{0}^{lin}=-\frac{{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{4}_{0}}{2\lambda}\,\frac{a^{2}}{b}.

(53)

The above scheme is a kind of variational approach and provides with (
53
) an upper bound on the true energy of the condensate, (
39
), which would follow from inserting of the exact solution of the non-linear equation (
36
) into the energy per unit length given by eq. (
38
). In section
3.4
we will give an example. We mention that the coupling constant
\lambda
, entering equation (
37
), is absorbed in (
41
) in the
{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}
-term and reappeared as factor in the denominator in the energy per unit length (
53
).

3.4
Rotation with a thin flux tube

First we will consider the case of infinitely thin flux tube and then discuss accuracy at which this solution holds for the flux tube of finite size.

3.4.1
Solution of a linearized equation

The corresponding background field is given by eq. (
45
) and the profile function becomes a constant,
\mu(r)=\delta_{\Phi}
. The GP-like equation (
47
) simplifies accordingly.
In the Appendix
A
we demonstrate that the solution of the linearized equation (

) with a finite radius

in the limit
R_{s}\to 0
turns into a Bessel function.This
demonstrates that one can take the limit

directly in eq. (

This way, our equation for all

\displaystyle\left(-\partial_{r}^{2}-\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}+\frac{\nu^{2}}{r^{2}}-\tilde{\Omega}^{2}+\lambda\phi^{2}(r)\right)\phi(r)=0,\qquad\nu=|\delta_{\Phi}-l|,

(54)

where we introduced notation

\displaystyle\tilde{\Omega}=\sqrt{(\Omega l)^{2}-m^{2}}\,.

(55)

We should recall that in our case as input parameters we use the values

and for infinitely thin flux tube the values

enter only through the value of the magnetic flux,
\delta_{\Phi}

First, we consider eq. (

. Equation (

\displaystyle\left(-\partial_{r}^{2}-\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}+\frac{\nu^{2}}{r^{2}}-\tilde{\Omega}^{2}\right)\phi(r)=-{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}\phi(r),

(56)

supplemented by the boundary condition
\phi(R)=0
and condition of regularity at
r=0

The magnetic field enters only through the flux in the shifted quantity
\nu
. The solution, which fulfills the boundary conditions, reads

\displaystyle\phi(r)=J_{\nu}\left(j_{\nu,n}\frac{r}{R}\right),

(57)

with

\displaystyle{\tilde{\epsilon}}=\sqrt{\tilde{\Omega}^{2}-\left(\frac{j_{\nu,n}}{R}\right)^{2}},

(58)

where
j_{\nu,n}
are the zeros of the Bessel function. Recall that for
\nu\neq 0
the Bessel function obeys the boundary condition
J_{\nu}(r\to 0)\to 0
for
r\to 0
and for
\nu=0

J_{0}(r\to 0)\to const
.

In this scheme, a solution with real
{\tilde{\epsilon}}
, which describes a condensate, will be precluded for large

by the growth of the zeroth of the Bessel function. It is the magnetic flux entering the index which compensates this growth. We are interested in the solution with lowest condensate energy
{\tilde{\epsilon}}={\tilde{\epsilon}}_{0}
. It is given by the lowest root,
j_{\nu,1}
, of the Bessel function in (
57
).

From (
58
) it can be seen that there exists a critical (lowest) frequency
\Omega_{cr}
corresponding to
{\tilde{\epsilon}}=0

\displaystyle\Omega_{cr}=\frac{1}{l}\sqrt{m^{2}+\left(\frac{j_{\nu,n}}{R}\right)^{2}},

(59)

such that for
\Omega>\Omega_{cr}
it is energetically favorable to produce the charged scalar field from the vacuum in the rotating frame.
For
\Omega<\Omega_{cr}
there are no solutions
\phi
with real
{\tilde{\epsilon}}
and the vacuum is stable.
Of course, the causality bound must be satisfied too, so that we get the restriction

\displaystyle\Omega_{cr}R<\Omega R<1.

(60)

3.5
Rotation with magnetic field in the whole volume of the cylinder

In this subsection we consider a homogeneous field that fills the whole cylinder. For this in the relations (
42
), (
44
) we set
R_{s}=R
, i.e. now
\mu=\delta_{\Phi}=B_{0}R^{2}/2
in the whole volume
r\leq R
.
As before we use (
46
) and study eq. (
47
) with
k_{z}=0
.

3.5.1
Linearized equation

Introducing a new variable in the radial function
\phi(r)
,

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{eB_{0}r^{2}}{2},

(72)

the solution (
48
), which is regular at the origin, can be expressed in terms of the Kummer’s (confluent hypergeometric) function,

\displaystyle\phi(r)=\rho^{|l|/2}e^{-\rho/2}M(\alpha,\beta,\rho).

(73)

The parameters are related by

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{|l|-l+1}{2}+\frac{-(\Omega l)^{2}+m^{2}+{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}}{2eB_{0}},\quad\beta=|l|+1.

(74)

For later use we mention the series expansion

\displaystyle M(\alpha,\beta,\rho)=1+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\rho+\frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)}{\beta(\beta+1)2!}\rho^{2}+\dots\,.

(75)

The Dirichlet boundary condition at
r=R
, which must be imposed on the radial function, results with (
44
) in

\displaystyle M(\alpha,|l|+1,\delta_{\Phi})=0.

(76)

This is an equation for
\alpha
and its solutions
\alpha_{n}(l,\delta_{\Phi})
(
n=0,1,...
) define the energies

\displaystyle{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{n,l}=\sqrt{(\Omega l)^{2}-m^{2}-k_{z}^{2}-eB_{0}(-2\alpha_{n}(l,\delta_{\Phi})+|l|-l+1)}.

(77)

We are interested in the energetically lowest solution and restrict ourselves therefore to
l\geq 0
.
In the case of a magnetic field homogeneous in the whole space (i.e. in the limit
R\to\infty
), the solutions
\alpha
of (
76
) are integer numbers,
\alpha_{n}(l,\delta_{\Phi})=-n
,
n=0,1,\dots
, and describe the well-known Landau levels, and the Kummer function turns into the Laguerre polynomials. These solutions are degenerate with respect to the orbital momentum
l
with a degeneracy factor
N=eB_{0}R^{2}/2=\delta_{\Phi}\geq l
, being the number of states in the interval
L_{z}\Delta k_{z}/(2\pi)
.
The energy (
77
) is minimal for
\alpha_{n}=0
. In general, the relation
N\geq l
does not apply for a field defined in a restricted space, e.g. in the case of the boundary condition at
r=R
, see the example shown in figure
8
below. Also in the latter case there is no solution at
\alpha=0
.

From
{\tilde{\epsilon}}=0
, similar to (
59
), we define the critical rotation frequency
\Omega_{cr}
, above which an instability sets in,

\displaystyle\Omega_{cr}=\frac{1}{l}\sqrt{m^{2}+eB_{0}(-2\alpha+1)},

(78)

and represent the ground state energy
{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{0}
in terms of
\Omega_{cr}
,

\displaystyle{\tilde{\epsilon}}_{0}=l\sqrt{\Omega^{2}-\Omega_{cr}^{2}}\,.

(79)

In general
\alpha
is not necessarily an integer number. Nevertheless, a strikingly simple way to get information from the Kummer function is to terminate the series expansion (
75
) by taking for
-\alpha
a natural number, which was used in
[
16
]
. In the latter case the minimal energy corresponds to
\alpha=-1
. If one took
\alpha=-2
or smaller, one could find the relations (more than one and non-linear) between the flux and the orbital number, for which the boundary conditions are fulfilled. All these have
\delta_{\Phi}>l
.
So, taking

\alpha=-1
and
\rho=\beta
in (
75
),
we arrive at a solution of the equation for the boundary condition, which is valid with
\beta
from (
74
) and
\delta_{\Phi}
from (
44
) for

\displaystyle\delta_{\Phi}=l+1,\qquad eB_{0}=\frac{2\delta_{\Phi}}{R^{2}}=\frac{2(l+1)}{R^{2}}.

(80)

Accordingly, other expressions simplify, for example,

\displaystyle\Omega_{cr}=\sqrt{\frac{6(l+1)}{l^{2}R^{2}}+\frac{m^{2}}{l^{2}}}

(81)

follows. Thus, for large
l
and flux (with (
80
)), the critical frequency goes to zero,
\Omega_{cr}\sim\sqrt{6/l}
.
For the energy parameter
{\tilde{\epsilon}}
, (
79
), we get simply
{\tilde{\epsilon}}\sim l\Omega
, i.e., it grows linearly with
l
. For the condensate energy (
53
) we need the parameters
a
and
b
, see (
51
), for large
l
. For
\alpha=-1
, the wave function is simply

\displaystyle\phi(r)=\rho^{l/2}e^{-\rho/2}\left(1-\frac{r^{2}}{R^{2}}\right),

(82)

and the integrals in (
51
) can be taken explicitly. Their values are presented in Appendix
B
.

From eqs. (
92
), (
93
) of Appendix
B
, the expansion for large
l
follows for the coefficients
a
and
b
in (
51
) and the mean radius from the Stirling approximation (and some generalization),

\displaystyle\frac{a^{2}}{b}\simeq\frac{2\sqrt{\pi}R^{2}}{3\sqrt{l}},\qquad\langle r\rangle/R\simeq 1-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi l}}.

(83)

Finally, for the ground state condensate energy per unit length (
53
), in this approximation we arrive at

\displaystyle E_{0}^{lin}\simeq-\frac{4\sqrt{\pi}R^{2}\Omega^{4}l^{7/2}}{3\lambda}\,,\quad l=\delta_{\Phi}-1\,.

(84)

If this were so for an arbitrary relation between
l
and
\delta_{\Phi}
and in the self-consistent treatment of the fields
\phi
and
\vec{A}
, it could cause a problem. Indeed, then the energy would not be bounded from below since (
84
) for large

cannot
be balanced by the classical electromagnetic energy term (

), which grows quadratically with

. However, in the given paper we treat the field

as an external one and in (
84
) we have
l=\delta_{\Phi}-1
, and the values of
B_{0}
,
R
and
\Omega
are in our case the external input parameters. In such a case the problem does not occur.

4
Conclusion

In this paper we studied the problem of the instability of the vacuum with respect to the production of a charged scalar (or pseudoscalar) field under the rapid rigid rotation in the presence of an external
magnetic background field. Of practical interest is the problem for the pion field in application to heavy-ion collisions. Therefore, for numerical estimates we used values of parameters relevant to this case; for instance, we employed that
mR\gg 1
,
B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptstyle<}}{{\phantom{}{}_{\sim}}}m^{2}_{\pi}
and
\Omega\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptstyle<}}{{\phantom{}{}_{\sim}}}0.1m_{\pi}
.

We started with the derivation of the Ginzburg-Pitaevskii-like equation for the charged scalar (pseudoscalar) mean field in the uniformly rotating frame in the presence of the external electromagnetic background field introduced initially in the resting frame.
We explicitly performed the transition from variables in the resting frame to the rotating frame used in the sense of a passive transform. We mention that for the charged scalar field the Klein-Gordon equation in the rotating frame is the same as in the local flat frame, which is also used for this kind of problems. The causality condition requires that
\Omega R<1
, which is ensured by imposing the Dirichlet boundary condition at
r=R
.
It raises the question of to what extent this circumstance influences the dynamics of the scalar field. As can be seen in figure
6
, where we showed the behavior of the scalar field in the rotating frame in the presence of the external uniform magnetic field, the condensate field is concentrated near the surface of the “mathematical” cylinder, which implies a large influence. Thereby we may conclude that passive rotation of the frame and active rotation of the empty “physical” cylinder with a non-transparent wall represent, in a sense, different cases. Although in the former case interpretation causes questions, the latter case allows for a clearer physical interpretation. Thereby we focused on the latter case. Thus we related the metric to the rotating empty cylinder and considered the case when the flux tube (solenoid) of radius
R_{s}\leq R
generating the uniform magnetic field for
r<R_{s}
, see eq. (
42
), is at rest in the frame associated with the rotating cylinder of radius
R
(rotating frame). The aim was to describe the response of the scalar field vacuum to such a magnetic field in the rotating frame.

In the foregoing section, to be specific, we considered two configurations of the magnetic field: an infinitely thin flux tube (the solenoid of radius
R_{s}\ll R
generating uniform magnetic field inside it) and a field homogeneous in the whole cylinder (the solenoid of radius
R
). The case of the uniform magnetic field in the whole volume of the rotating cylinder was previously studied in
[
16
,
17
,
20
]
. The case of a thin flux tube in the rotating frame was not studied before, to the best of our knowledge.
In both cases we found a numerical solution of the linearized equation, as an approximation, and a numerical solution of the full non-linear Ginzburg-Pitaevskii-like (GP-like) equation, as well as found approximate analytical solutions, where possible. For the solution of the linearized equation, in the case of the infinitely thin flux tube, the condensate energy is expressed in terms of zeros of the Bessel function. For the case of the uniform magnetic field in the cylinder one needs to find a zero of the Kummer function, which problem is more delicate, especially for large values of parameters like the rotation frequency. However, for some special relations between magnetic flux and orbital momentum, e.g., when the magnetic flux
\delta_{\Phi}
and the orbital momentum
l
are connected as
\delta_{\Phi}=l+1
, an explicit solution exists. In the numerical approach, we adopted a relaxation method and a 4th order Runge-Kutta approach. Moreover, for the thin flux tube in the case
\delta_{\Phi}=l
we found the approximate analytical solution of the GP-like equation. The latter case is of special interest, since we showed that the energy of the produced
condensate takes a minimum precisely for
l=\delta_{\phi}
, see figure
3
.

In the case of the flux tube, due to the interplay between rotation and magnetic background field, after the rotation frequency reaches the critical value, given by eq. (
59
) for
\delta_{\Phi}=l
,
the scalar charged field can be produced from the vacuum in the rotating frame. This instability results in the formation of the classical field (the condensate). We calculated its energy in a range of parameters, which may be of relevance in heavy-ion collisions for pions.
We demonstrated that the exact and the linearized solutions are very close in the vicinity of the critical point but begin to deviate essentially for a larger
\Omega
, see figures
1
and
2
. They show at the threshold the behavior typical of the second-order phase transition. The approximate analytical solution coincides well with the numerical solution of the GP-like equation except in the vicinity of the critical point.
We noted that the critical point obtained with the approximate analytical solution for the flux tube at
\delta_{\Phi}=l
is slightly shifted to a smaller value (shown by the bold dot in figure
3
), whereas the value of the correction is
1/(\tilde{\Omega}R)
, where
\tilde{\Omega}=\sqrt{\Omega^{2}-m^{2}}
.

For the exact and approximate analytical solutions of the GP-like equation the minimum of the energy appears precisely at
l=\delta_{\phi}
, whereas for the case of the linearized equation it is slightly shifted to
\nu\approx 1
, see figure
3
. Due to the dependence only on
\nu^{2}
the possibilities
l<\delta_{\Phi}
and
l>\delta_{\Phi}
are indistinguishable in this case.

Figure
2
shows that with increasing
\Omega R
the plateau solution is formed in the case of the flux tube at
l=\delta_{\Phi}
, which is well described by the approximate analytical solution (
65
). The mean radius of the exact solution corresponds to
r\sim 0.5R
accordingly.

We argued, under which conditions a thin flux tube can be considered as an infinitely thin one.
In the case
\delta_{\Phi}\neq l
for the thin flux tube we found an interpolating analytical solution between two asymptotic regimes, for small
r\ll\nu/\tilde{\Omega}
and large
r\gg\nu/\tilde{\Omega}
, see eq. (
71
). It demonstrates the appearance of a hole starting at small
r
, which origin is associated with the appearance of the centrifugal barrier for
\nu\neq 0
. In figure
4
we demonstrated that for
\nu\sim 1
the numerical solution of the exact GP-like equation and the analytical interpolation expression coincide well except in the vicinity of the critical point, whereas the solutions of the exact GP-like equation and the linearized one coincide well in the vicinity of the critical point.

For the uniform magnetic field in the whole cylinder our results obtained within the linearized approach are in general agreement with
[
17
]
, with regard to the critical frequency, the statement that excited states are occupied only at
\Omega
essentially larger than
\Omega_{cr}
and that the minimum of the condensate energy occurs at a finite value of the orbital moment
l
, see figure
8
.
However, in
[
16
,
17
,
20
]
the authors restricted the orbital moments by the number of states (per unit area)
N=\delta_{\phi}
. This is correct, if one considers the system in uniform magnetic field in the unbounded space, whereas the cylinder with
r<R
corresponds to a bounded space and in this case the states with any orbital momentum
l
can exist. In the case of the field in the full cylinder it proved to be that the minimum of the energy corresponds to
l<N
for
\Omega
varied in a vicinity of
\Omega_{cr}
but it corresponds to
l>N
for a larger
\Omega
, see figure
8
.

The easiest approximation method for the GP-equation is its linearization. As shown in
[
21
]
, this solution can be viewed as the first step in a perturbative expansion for small
{\tilde{\epsilon}}
and we confirmed that for small
{\tilde{\epsilon}}
the solutions of the linearized and the full equations are very close. In general, the linearized equation has a full spectrum of modes with higher
{\tilde{\epsilon}}
. These modes were used in
[
17
]
to expand the full solution. We confirm their finding that for small
{\tilde{\epsilon}}
only the lowest modes need to be included by our observation of the mentioned closeness.

We used the analytical solutions of the linearized equation at
\delta_{\Phi}=l+1
. The value of the critical angular velocity (
81
) proved to be essentially larger than in the case of the flux tube at the same value of the magnetic flux.

In the case of the field in the full cylinder, figure
5
shows energies found with numerical solutions of the GP-like equation and the linearized equation. The solution demonstrates the occurrence of the second order phase transition at
\Omega=\Omega_{cr}
. In the vicinity of the critical point the solution found in the linearization method
coincides well with the numerical solution of the GP-like equation, see figure
6
.
With increasing
\Omega
the linearization procedure becomes less appropriate. The solutions demonstrate a big hole up to
r\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptstyle<}}{{\phantom{}{}_{\sim}}}R/2
. Here the hole is larger than in the case of the thin flux tube since in the former case the centrifugal barrier is much larger.
Notice however that although in the case of the field in the cylinder the plateau does not arise, as is shown in figure
6
at
\Omega R<1
, it would reappear for
\Omega R>1
, as shown in figure
7
.

Finally, as has been mentioned, we demonstrated that the energy in the case of the thin flux tube is lower than that for the magnetic field in the whole cylinder,
see figure
9
, both for solutions of the linearized and exact GP-like equations. Thereby, although we considered the magnetic field as an external one, we may conjecture that in the self-consistent problem the uniform magnetic field applied to the rotating cylinder will be confined in a narrow flux tube. If so, it will then raise a question about the formation of the lattice
of flux tubes similarly to that which occurs for vortices in superconductors of the second kind
[
13
]
, but in our case with a large flux in each flux tube.

In the present paper we ignored the back reaction of the produced charged condensate field on the distribution of the electric and magnetic fields inside the cylinder. With increasing
\Omega
significantly above
\Omega_{cr}
the inclusion of such a response can become very important. In a somewhat similar problem of the behavior of the superconductors of the second type in the external uniform magnetic field, a back response of the order parameter on the distribution of the magnetic field proves to be rather weak only near the critical values of the magnetic field
H_{cr,1}
and
H_{cr,2}
. This problem needs further investigation.

Notice that in the case of the non-abelian gluon fields the vacuum placed in the uniform magnetic field may consist of the lattice of the Nielsen-Olesen vortices. We hope that our results can be helpful in studying this problem in the rotating frame. Further investigation of the Casimir effect under the rotation can be also of interest
[
21
]
. Under the rapid rotation of a piece of the nuclear matter the inhomogeneous
\pi^{0}
and
\pi^{\pm}
condensates can be produced via the anomalous Wess-Zumino-Witten contribution to the Gibbs free-energy density
[
23
]
. In the case of extremely rapidly rotating nuclear drops, the occurrence of a charged pion vortex condensate may stabilize them in the rotating frame
[
18
,
20
]
. In peripheral heavy-ion collisions, the presence of the strong magnetic and rotational fields may stimulate the formation of the pion condensate in the rotating frame
[
20
]
. All these problems also need further study.

Here we display the simple formulas for the solution of (

We represent the solution as consisting of two parts:
u_{i}(r)

\displaystyle u_{i}(r)

\displaystyle=\rho^{|l|/2}e^{-\rho/2}M(\alpha,|l|+1,\rho),

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi R_{s}}{2}\left(u_{i}(R_{s})Y^{\prime}_{\nu}(\Lambda R_{s})-u_{i}^{\prime}(R_{s})Y_{\nu}(\Lambda R_{s})\right),

\displaystyle C_{2}

\displaystyle\nu=|\delta_{\Phi}-l|,\quad\alpha=\frac{1}{2B_{0}}\left(m^{2}+eB_{0}\frac{|l|-l}{2}-({\tilde{\epsilon}}-\Omega l)^{2}\right),\quad\Lambda=\sqrt{{\tilde{\epsilon}}^{2}+(\Omega l)^{2}-m^{2}}.
