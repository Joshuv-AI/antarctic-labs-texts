A cell-free lipid sterosomal therapeutic enables bone regeneration via USP18-mediated noncanonical S1P signaling
Authors: Wen-Hao Ye, Jie Zhou, Junxiang Qiu, Zhengbo Liu, Ruolin He, Sixu Chen, Jing Wen, Binyang Feng, Beibei Lyu, Zhen Wang, Zhong-Kai Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEffective treatment of critical-sized bone defects remains challenging due to impaired osteogenesis, insufficient vascularization and oxidative stress–induced cell loss. Here, we report a chemically defined, cell-free sterosomal nanoplatform composed of sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) and 20(S)-hydroxycholesterol (Oxy) that integrates cytoprotective, pro-angiogenic, and osteogenic functions. S1P/Oxy sterosomes simultaneously enhance osteogenic differentiation and endothelial angiogenic activity while protecting resident cells from oxidative stress. In a rat critical-sized calvarial defect model, local administration of sterosomes promoted robust and spatially organized bone regeneration, without exogenous cells or growth factors. Mechanistically, transcriptomic and functional analyses identified ubiquitin-specific peptidase 18 (USP18) as a novel and essential mediator of sterosomal S1P-induced osteogenesis, independent of canonical S1P receptors. Notably, this effect is observed only when S1P is presented in a sterosomal assembly rather than in its free molecular form. Our work establishes a cell-free sterosomal strategy for effective bone regeneration accompanied by functional neovascularization and discovered a novel intracellular actuator for S1P.Keywords: Sterosomes, Sphingosine-1-phosphate, Self-therapeutic, Microenvironment-adaptive, Vascularized bone regeneration

Introduction

Bone fractures generally possess an intrinsic capacity for repair. However, critical-sized bone defects (CSDs) exceed this regenerative threshold and remain among the most formidable challenges in clinical orthopedics [1,2]. With the increasing global demand for orthopedic reconstruction, bone grafting has become the second most common transplantation procedure after blood transfusion [3]. Although autologous bone grafting is regarded as the clinical gold standard, its application is constrained by donor-site morbidity, limited availability, and high cost [4]. More importantly, CSDs are characterized by a hostile regenerative microenvironment marked by hypoxia, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), nutrient deprivation, and apoptosis of endogenous stem/progenitor cells [5,6]. These pathological conditions impair vascularization and osteogenic differentiation, ultimately leading to delayed healing or nonunion. Therefore, effective strategies for CSD repair must not only promote osteogenesis but also preserve the survival and function of endogenous cells within stress-laden environments [7,8].

Sphingolipids function as both structural components of cell membranes and bioactive signaling molecules, regulating diverse cellular processes including apoptosis, proliferation, and migration [9,10]. Among them, sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) is a potent lipid mediator known to suppress apoptosis, stimulate cell migration, and promote vascular regeneration [11,12]. In addition to these canonical functions, emerging evidence suggests a role for S1P in maintaining bone homeostasis [13]. However, endogenous S1P exhibits low tissue concentration and a short half-life [14], and current delivery strategies still fall short of achieving sustained and stable release [15]. This inadequacy hinders the long-term coordinated actions of anti-apoptosis, angiogenesis, and bone regeneration, thereby limiting the full biological potential of S1P [16]. These challenges have hindered the translation of S1P-based strategies for bone regeneration.

Nanomedicine has provided new opportunities for bone repair, yet most conventional nanocarriers serve primarily as inert delivery vehicles with limited intrinsic bioactivity [17,18]. Sterosomes, bilayer vesicles assembled from single-chain amphiphiles and high content sterols[19], have recently emerged as a class of self-therapeutic nanostructures with enhanced stability, and intrinsic biological function [20]. Previous studies have shown that rationally designed sterosomes can promote osteogenesis even in the absence of exogenous drugs or growth factors [21]. Nevertheless, achieving the coordinated integration of cytoprotective, pro-angiogenic, and osteogenic functions within a single nanoplatform remains an unmet challenge [22]. S1P is a single-chain amphiphile with a hydrophobic tail and a charged polar headgroup. It not only satisfies the structural requirements for sterosome self-assembly [19], but also possesses intrinsic biological activity relevant to bone regeneration[23]. Therefore, S1P-based sterosomes represent a promising strategy to integrate therapeutic functionality with nanostructural design for bone regeneration[24].

In this study, we report the rational design of multifunctional sterosomes composed of S1P/20(S)-hydroxycholesterol (Oxy) for the repair of critical-sized bone defects. We show that these S1P/Oxy sterosomes protect bone mesenchymal stromal cells (BMSCs) from ROS-induced apoptosis, enhance endothelial angiogenesis, and promote osteogenic differentiation without the use of exogenous cells or growth factors. In a rat critical-sized calvarial defect model, local administration of S1P/Oxy sterosomes induces robust and spatially organized bone regeneration accompanied by functional neovascularization. Mechanistically, S1P promoted angiogenesis via the membrane receptor S1PR1, whereas its osteogenic-differentiation-driving effect was independent of the S1PR signaling pathway. Transcriptomics and functional analyses further revealed that sterosomal S1P drives osteogenesis through a noncanonical, S1PR-independent pathway involving ubiquitin-specific peptidase 18 (USP18). Collectively, these findings establish a cell-free self-therapeutic strategy that efficiently harnesses S1P to act simultaneously on both intracellular and extracellular targets, offering a novel approach for vascularized bone regeneration under hostile microenvironmental conditions.

Results

Preparation and characterization of S1P/Oxy sterosomes

To construct a stable and bioactive sterosomal system that integrates both signaling and structural lipids, S1P was combined with Oxy at a 3:7 M ratio based on our previously established physicochemical framework for bilayer formation from single-chain amphiphiles and high content sterols [19]. The resulting formulation yielded large unilamellar vesicles (LUVs) following hydration and mechanical extrusion, as confirmed by cryo-electron microscopy (Fig. 1A). Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR) analysis revealed the disappearance of the -OH stretching vibration at 3392 cm−1 compared with spectra of individual components, indicating tight molecular packing driven by hydrophobic interactions between S1P and Oxy (Fig. S1). These S1P/Oxy sterosomes (S/O) exhibited high physicochemical stability, maintaining structural integrity across a broad pH range (4.5-8.5) (Fig. 1B), and remaining colloidally stable at 25 °C for at least 14 days, with a hydrodynamic diameter of 115 ± 5 nm, a ζ-potential of −33 ± 5 mV, and a narrow size distribution (PDI 0.130 ± 0.060) (Fig. 1C). Comparable stability was observed in phosphate-buffered saline (Fig. 1D). Fluorescence co-labeling with DiD and rhodamine B revealed strong signal overlap of red and yellow, confirming preservation of the bilayer structure even in serum-containing medium (Fig. S2). In addition to their intrinsic bioactivity, S1P/Oxy sterosomes displayed high encapsulation efficiency and sustained release of the model protein bovine serum albumin (BSA), demonstrating that S1P/Oxy sterosomes are capable of encapsulating and releasing a model protein under physiological conditions (Fig. S3).

Biocompatibility of S1P/Oxy sterosomes

The cytocompatibility of S1P/Oxy sterosomes was systematically assessed in BMSCs and Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells (HUVECs) under both 2D and 3D culture conditions. In 2D cultures, cells were exposed to increasing concentrations of S1P/Oxy sterosomes for 3 and 7 days. Up to 50 μg/mL, S1P/Oxy sterosomes not only showed no cytotoxicity but also enhanced cellular metabolic activity in both BMSCs (Fig. 2A) and HUVECs (Fig. 2B), with viability further elevated at day 7. To better recapitulate the in vivo microenvironment, a methacrylated glycol chitosan (MeGC) hydrogel was employed for 3D culture [25,26]. These visible blue light–crosslinked hydrogels exhibit excellent biocompatibility and provide a suitable platform for investigating the biological functions of S1P/Oxy sterosomes. Even at concentrations as high as 400 μg/mL, BMSCs (Fig. 2C) and HUVECs (Fig. 2D) maintained robust viability at day 7 and sustained proliferation. Live/dead staining with calcein-AM/propidium iodide (PI) confirmed excellent cytocompatibility across a wide concentration range (Fig. S4). Hemocompatibility was further validated by hemolysis assessment, which demonstrated minimal erythrocyte lysis even at 500 μg/mL of S1P/Oxy sterosomes. More than 90% of the erythrocyte membranes remained intact at concentrations up to 1000 μg/mL (Fig. S5), indicating outstanding blood compatibility.

Cellular uptake and internalization mechanism of S1P/Oxy sterosomes

Cellular uptake of S1P/Oxy sterosomes was therefore examined, as intracellular delivery is likely to contribute to their biological activity. Flow cytometry analysis of BMSCs and HUVECs treated with calcein-loaded sterosomes revealed progressive intracellular fluorescence accumulation, confirming efficient internalization. Nanoparticles typically enter cells through multiple endocytic pathways, including clathrin-mediated endocytosis, caveolae-mediated endocytosis, and macropinocytosis [27]. Pharmacological inhibition studies demonstrated that chlorpromazine (clathrin inhibitor) and amiloride (macropinocytosis inhibitor) markedly reduced sterosome uptake (Fig. S6), indicating that S1P/Oxy sterosomes are internalized predominantly via clathrin-mediated endocytosis. Furthermore, dual-labeling with rhodamine B and DiD revealed strong co-localization signals within lysosomes, suggesting that internalized sterosomes were trafficked into the lysosomal compartment (Fig. S7).

Pro-migratory effect of S1P/Oxy sterosomes

Previous studies have established that free S1P promotes stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1) secretion by BMSCs, thereby enhancing progenitor cell migration [28,29]. Whether this function is preserved after incorporation of S1P into sterosomes, where it is tightly packed within the bilayer and internalized by cells, critically determines the bioactivity of the sterosomal system. Transwell migration assays revealed that S1P/Oxy sterosomes placed in the lower chamber significantly stimulated the migration of BMSCs from the upper wells (Fig. 2E, G). Consistently, wound-healing assays demonstrated a dose-dependent enhancement of BMSCs migratory capacity in response to S1P/Oxy sterosomes, with 9 μg/mL treatment resulting in pronounced wound closure (Fig. 2F, H). These findings indicate that S1P/Oxy sterosomes preserve the ability to promote BMSC migration, suggesting their capacity to mobilize endogenous stem cells toward defect sites.

S1P/Oxy sterosomes promote osteogenic differentiation

Sterosomes incorporating Oxy have previously been shown to promote osteogenic differentiation[20]. The addition of S1P raised two key questions, whether the osteogenic activity of Oxy remains preserved, and whether S1P itself could further augment osteogenesis. Given that osteogenic differentiation of endogenous BMSCs is indispensable for skeletal repair, we next investigated the effect of S1P/Oxy sterosomes in both 2D and 3D culture systems.

In 2D cultures, S1P/Oxy sterosomes markedly enhanced osteogenic differentiation in a dose-dependent manner compared with untreated controls. The 7 μg/mL S1P/Oxy sterosome treatment group showed the most pronounced osteogenic effect, as evidenced by intensified alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining (Fig. 3A), robust calcium deposition detected by Alizarin Red S staining (ARS) (Fig. 3C), and corresponding quantitative increases in ALP activity (2.3-fold, Fig. 3B) and mineralization (1.5-fold, Fig. 3D). Three specific osteogenic gene markers were quantified with RT-qPCR on days 3 and 7, respectively, including the early osteogenic marker Alp, the most specific osteogenic differentiation marker Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), and the late osteogenic marker Osteocalcin (Ocn) [30]. Alp was up-regulated by ∼2.3-fold, Runx2 by ∼6.8-fold, and Ocn by ∼5.2-fold compared to controls (Fig. 3E), with 7 μg/mL emerging as the optimal dosage. Consistent with previous reports, Oxy has been shown to induce osteogenic differentiation through Hedgehog signaling [31], as reflected by the increased Gli1 expression observed here (Fig. S8). At higher concentrations, however, S1P/Oxy sterosomes could potentially engage liver X receptor (LXR), an antagonist of osteogenesis, thereby attenuating expression of osteogenic markers [32].

To better approximate in vivo conditions, the osteogenic potential of S1P/Oxy sterosomes was further evaluated in MeGC hydrogel-based 3D cultures. Treatment with 100 μg/mL S1P/Oxy sterosomes induced the most prominent osteogenic activity, characterized by elevated ALP staining, progressive mineralization confirmed by ARS (Fig. 3F–I), and significant upregulation of osteogenic gene markers (Fig. 3J). A clear concentration-dependent response was observed, peaking at 100 μg/mL. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that S1P/Oxy sterosomes significantly enhance osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs in both 2D and 3D environments, with an optimal dose-dependent profile and mechanistic involvement of the Hedgehog signaling.

Anti-apoptotic properties of S1P/Oxy sterosomes

S1P is a bioactive sphingolipid with a well-established role in sustaining cell survival[9]. A key question was whether its anti-apoptotic ability would be preserved when incorporated into sterosomal bilayers. To mimic the nutrient-deprived and ROS-rich microenvironment of bone defects, BMSCs were challenged with hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) under serum-free conditions [33]. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays demonstrated that S1P/Oxy sterosomes conferred significant protection against H2O2-induced oxidative stress, with the 9 μg/mL group maintaining 81.70 ± 5.82% cell viability relative to untreated controls (Fig. 4A). Consistently, RT-qPCR revealed upregulation of the anti-apoptotic gene Bcl-2 and downregulation of the pro-apoptotic gene Bax, as well as of the apoptotic executioner gene Caspase-3 following sterosome treatment (Fig. 4B). Flow cytometry analysis further confirmed a marked reduction in apoptotic rates, with only ∼9.72% apoptotic cells in the 9 μg/mL group compared to 28.16 ± 2.2% in controls (Fig. 4C, E).

Morphological assessments supported these findings as well. Phalloidin staining demonstrated nuclear disruption and cytoplasmic shrinkage in untreated cells, whereas S1P/Oxy sterosome-treated cells preserved nuclear integrity and displayed a plump, spread morphology with well-organized actin filaments (Fig. 4D). Western blot analysis corroborated these results, showing increased expression of BCL-2, decreased BAX and a significant reduction in cleaved-CASPASE-3 after treatment (Fig. 4F, S9). Together, these data demonstrate that S1P/Oxy sterosomes confer robust cytoprotection under oxidative stress by inhibiting apoptosis at both transcriptional and protein levels. This preservation of BMSCs viability is expected to support the maintenance of functional endogenous cell pool, which is essential for subsequent bone regeneration.

Pro-angiogenic properties of S1P/Oxy sterosomes

Although S1P/Oxy sterosomes effectively preserved cell viability under stress conditions, successful bone repair additionally requires sustained vascularization to supply oxygen and nutrients. S1P is well-known for its pro-angiogenic activity, but whether this function is retained in the sterosomal formulation at the concentration optimized for osteogenesis remains unclear. Transwell assays revealed that HUVECs treated with 9 μg/mL S1P/Oxy sterosomes displayed significantly enhanced migration compared with controls (Fig. 5A, D). Consistently, wound-healing assays showed accelerated closure in the same treatment group, confirming a pronounced pro-migratory effect (Fig. 5B, E). Tube-formation assays further demonstrated that HUVECs exposed to 7 μg/mL S1P/Oxy sterosomes for 6 h developed extensive tubular networks with abundant vascular junctions (Fig. 5C, F). At the molecular level, RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses showed robust upregulation of angiogenic mediators, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and fibroblast growth factor-2 (FGF-2), in HUVECs treated with 7 μg/mL S1P/Oxy sterosomes (Fig. 5G–H, S10). To validate these findings in vivo, the chick chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) assay demonstrated that the 15 μg/mL treatment group exhibited the greatest vascular outgrowth (Fig. 5I).

Together, these results indicate that S1P/Oxy sterosomes preserve and amplify the angiogenic activity of S1P, enhancing endothelial migration, tube formation, and pro-angiogenic factor expression both in vitro and in vivo. Importantly, the optimal concentration for angiogenesis (7 μg/mL) coincides with that for osteogenesis and cytoprotection, underscoring the concurrent effects of S1P/Oxy sterosomes on angiogenesis, osteogenesis, and cytoprotection. In vitro data consistently demonstrate that S1P/Oxy sterosomes exhibit significantly stronger osteogenic, angiogenic, and cytoprotective effects compared to free S1P, free Oxy, and their combination (Fig. S11).

S1P/Oxy sterosomes promote osteogenic differentiation via USP18

S1P exerts distinct physiological effects by activating downstream signaling pathways through binding to different membrane S1P receptors (S1PR1-5) [16]. S1PR1–3 are the predominant receptor subtypes expressed, whereas S1PR4 and S1PR5 are generally restricted to lymphoid and neural tissues and are not reported to be expressed in BMSCs [34]. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the biological activities of S1P/Oxy sterosomes, we first examined the classical S1P receptor pathway. As expected, S1PR1 knockdown markedly impaired HUVEC angiogenesis, confirming that the pro-angiogenic effects of S1P/Oxy sterosomes are largely mediated through S1PR1 signaling (Fig. 6A, S12). Strikingly, however, silencing S1pr1-3 failed to diminish S1P/Oxy sterosomes-induced osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs (Fig. 6B, S13), suggesting that osteogenesis proceeds via a S1PR-independent mechanism.

To explore this mechanism, transcriptomic profiling was performed on BMSCs treated with S1P/Oxy sterosomes in osteogenic medium (OM) versus OM only. Volcano plot analysis identified 1228 differentially expressed genes, including 405 upregulated and 823 downregulated transcripts (Fig. 6C). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment highlighted protein homeostasis pathways, where USP18, a deubiquitinating enzyme, emerged as a candidate regulator linking post-translational modification dynamics to protein homeostasis [35,36] (Fig. 6D). Both RT-qPCR and Western blot analysis validated a strong upregulation of USP18 at both the mRNA and protein levels following S1P/Oxy sterosome treatment (Fig. S14). To determine which sterosomal component was responsible for Usp18 induction, we treated cells with OM, free S1P, free Oxy, free cholesterol (Chol), or sterosomes formed by S1P/Oxy, S1P/cholesterol (S/C) or palmitic acid/Oxy (P/O). Interestingly, only sterosomal S1P (not free S1P), together with Oxy, induced Usp18 expression in BMSCs (Fig. 6E). Previous studies have shown that intracellular S1P can directly bind Yes-associated protein (YAP) in a S1PR-independent manner [37]. Immunofluorescence analysis further demonstrated that sterosomal S1P co-localized with YAP and promoted its nuclear translocation in HUVECs, suggesting that sterosomal assembly is required for this noncanonical intracellular activity (Fig. 6F). Furthermore, ARS staining demonstrated that free S1P alone failed to promote the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. In contrast, sterosomal S1P markedly enhanced calcium deposition, and this pro-osteogenic effect was further amplified by the presence of Oxy (Fig. 6G).

Functionally, siRNA-mediated knockdown of Usp18 significantly abrogated the osteogenic enhancement induced by S1P/Oxy sterosomes in both 2D and 3D cultures (Fig. 6H–I, S15). Together, these findings support that USP18 plays a functional role in S1P/Oxy-driven osteogenic differentiation, revealing a noncanonical, S1PR-independent pathway through which sterosomal S1P promotes bone formation.

S1P/Oxy sterosomes promote bone repair in a rat critical-sized calvarial defect model

To rigorously evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of S1P/Oxy sterosomes in vivo, we employed a rat critical-sized calvarial defect model. A 5-mm defect was created in the right calvaria and filled with MeGC hydrogels containing S1P/Oxy sterosomes at varying concentrations, while blank controls left untreated. After 6 weeks, calvaria were harvested for analysis. μCT imaging revealed that S1P/Oxy sterosome treatment markedly enhanced new bone formation compared with both blank and MeGC groups, despite the absence of exogenous cells. Notably, the 200 μg/mL group exhibited the most substantial repair, nearly bridging the defect with continuous mineralized tissue (Fig. 7A-B). Quantitative morphometric analysis confirmed significant improvements in bone parameters, including that the defect area was covered by ∼70%, BV/TV reached 34% and trabecular number (Tb.N) rose to 0.60 mm−1 in the 200 μg/mL group, representing ∼4-fold and ∼3.8-fold increases, respectively, compared to MeGC controls (8.57% and 0.16 mm−1) (Fig. 7C).

Histological evaluation corroborated these findings. H&E and Masson's trichrome staining revealed that defects treated with 200 μg/mL S1P/Oxy sterosomes were filled with thick, bone-like tissue that bridged the defect margins, whereas blank and MeGC groups contained only thin fibrous tissue with minimal osteoid deposition (Fig. 7D-E). Moreover, Masson's trichrome staining revealed well-organized collagenous bone matrix in the 200 μg/mL group, in sharp contrast to the disorganized fibrotic tissue seen in controls.

Immunohistochemistry further demonstrated strong expression of the osteogenic marker OCN and the endothelial marker CD31 in the 200 μg/mL group, consistent with coupled osteogenesis and angiogenesis (Fig. 7F) [38,39]. By contrast, staining in the blank and MeGC groups was weak and restricted to residual fibrous tissue. USP18 was also highly expressed in the 200 μg/mL group (Fig. S16). Importantly, no inflammatory responses were observed in the organs of any treatment group, underscoring the excellent biocompatibility of S1P/Oxy sterosomes (Fig. S17).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that S1P/Oxy sterosomes, even without reliance on exogenous cellular input, can effectively orchestrate robust bone regeneration in critical-sized defects, demonstrating effective bone regeneration in a rat critical-sized calvarial defect model without exogenous cells.

Discussion and conclusion

Efficient repair of critical-sized bone defects remains a pressing clinical challenge, particularly in the context of aging populations and increasing incidence of skeletal diseases. Although autologous bone grafting is considered the clinical gold standard, its widespread application is limited by donor-site morbidity, pain, and restricted tissue availability. Consequently, engineered biomaterials have evolved from “bioinert” substitutes toward “bioactive” constructs [4]. Yet, many existing strategies insufficiently address the hostile microenvironment of bone defects, which is characterized by oxidative stress, impaired vascularization, and compromised survival of endogenous regenerative cells [40]. Because bone regeneration is an intrinsically multicellular and multistep process involving coordinated osteogenesis, angiogenesis and cellular adaptation to stress, effective therapies must integrate these biological requirements rather than focusing on osteogenesis alone [39].

In this study, we demonstrate that sterosomes composed of S1P and Oxy provide a chemically defined, cell-free approach capable of promoting robust bone regeneration in a rat critical-sized calvarial defect model. The S1P/Oxy sterosomes simultaneously enhance osteogenic differentiation, support endothelial angiogenesis, and protect resident cells from oxidative stress, resulting in spatially organized bone formation accompanied by functional neovascularization. These findings highlight the importance of addressing both cellular survival and vascular support as integral components of effective bone repair.

Beyond their regenerative efficacy, a key finding of this study is revealing a previously unrecognized intracellular effector of S1P delivered via sterosomes. S1P is a pleiotropic sphingolipid that binds to membrane-bound S1P receptors to promote cell migration and angiogenesis, while also regulating gene expression through direct interaction with intracellular protein [41,42]. Its biological functions are highly dependent on its subcellular localization [43]. In osteoporosis patients, S1P serves as a critical osteoclast-osteoblast regulator involved in bone homeostasis [44,45]. Osteoclast-derived S1P promotes osteoclastogenesis through autocrine signaling and modulates osteoblast proliferation and migration through paracrine signaling [[46], [47], [48]]. Although several studies have reported a positive correlation between high plasma S1P levels and osteoporosis risk [44,49], systemic S1P lyase-knockout mice exhibit an increased bone mass phenotype [23], indicating that S1P's ability to promote bone formation generally outweighs its capacity to stimulate bone resorption. Therefore, delivering S1P itself represents a promising strategy for bone repair; however, its short half-life and low bioavailability severely limit its application [14]. The S1P/Oxy sterosomes can simultaneously target intracellular and extracellular receptors. This sterosome not only preserves the classical S1PR-mediated signaling pathways but also enables S1P to exert non-canonical intracellular effects independent of S1PRs. Our study provides the first evidence that sterosomal S1P enhances MSC osteogenesis via a noncanonical S1PR-independent pathway through USP18 upregulation, thereby establishing sterosomal S1P as a promising osteoactive molecule for bone regeneration.

The distinct contributions of the two sterosomal components further underscore the biological rationale of this design. Oxy, a known activator of Hedgehog signaling, directly promotes osteogenic differentiation [31], whereas S1P primarily supports angiogenesis and cytoprotection, thereby facilitating a permissive microenvironment for sustained bone regeneration. Importantly, the requirement for sterosomal assembly to elicit Usp18 upregulation suggests that lipid organization and intracellular accessibility, rather than molecular identity alone, are critical determinants of downstream cell fate decisions. Although the precise molecular events linking sterosomal S1P to USP18 induction remain to be elucidated, our findings establish USP18 as a functional mediator of sterosomal S1P-induced osteogenesis and provide a foundation for future mechanistic studies.

From a translational perspective, the sterosomal system offers several practical advantages, including a chemically defined composition, the absence of exogenous cells or growth factors, and effective bone regeneration through local administration []. The intracellular and extracellular mechanisms of S1P may provide new insights into its role in alleviating osteoporosis. At the same time, this study has limitations. The current findings are based on a rodent calvarial defect model, and validation in large-animal models will be required to assess long-term safety, scalability, and therapeutic efficacy under clinically relevant conditions. In addition, further investigation is required to elucidate how sterosomal assembly modulates S1P signaling at the molecular level, particularly regarding intracellular trafficking and target engagement, as well as to determine how USP18 mediates downstream osteogenic responses in different pathological contexts.

In summary, this work establishes a cell-free sterosomal strategy that achieves vascularized bone regeneration by integrating cytoprotection, angiogenesis, and osteogenesis through action on intracellular and extracellular S1P receptors. By revealing a noncanonical, S1P receptor–independent role of sterosomal S1P in regulating osteogenesis through USP18, our findings provide mechanistic insight into how lipid nanostructure assembly can modulate cell fate and regenerative outcomes.

NaCl, chloroform, absolute ethanol, glacial acetic acid, ammonia, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), and methanol were supplied by Guangzhou Guanghua (Guangzhou, China). Mineral oil and benzene were purchased from Macklin Biochemical (Shanghai, China). All solvents and reagents were used as received without further purification.

Preparation of S1P/Oxy sterosomes

S1P and Oxy (3:7 M ratio) mixtures were dissolved in benzene/methanol (8:2, v/v). The resulting solution was rapidly frozen in liquid N2 and lyophilized (LABCONCO, Kansas, USA) for at least 16 h to ensure complete solvent removal. The lyophilized powder was then hydrated in a Tris buffer (50 mM TRIS, 140 mM NaCl), followed by five freeze-thaw cycles between liquid N2 and ∼70 °C with intermittent vortexing to facilitate complete hydration. The obtained multilamellar vesicles were subsequently extruded through polycarbonate membranes using a LiposoFast LF-1 extruder (AVESTIN, Ottawa, Canada) to yield uniform large unilamellar sterosomes. The physicochemical stability of the prepared S1P/Oxy sterosomes was evaluated by monitoring their hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential at room temperature over 14 consecutive days using a ZEN3700 Zetasizer (Malvern, Malvern, UK). Based on previous experimental results, the optimal molar ratio for negatively charged single-chain amphiphilic small molecules to form sterosomes is 3:7 [19]. Accordingly, we selected a 3:7 M ratio for S1P and Oxy. Preliminary experiments showed that a 2:8 ratio failed to form a stable bilayer structure, and sterosomes formed at a 4:6 ratio disintegrated after 3 days. Therefore, 3:7 is the optimal ratio for S1P and Oxy to form stable sterosomes.

Cell culture

BMSCs were isolated from the femurs of 4-week-old Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, as previously described[50]. Cells were maintained in α-minimum essential medium (α-MEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S, Gibco). HUVECs were purchased from Sbjbio Life Sciences (Nanjing, China) and cultured in endothelial cell medium (Sciencell) under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). For 3D hydrogel cultures, photocrosslinkable MeGC was prepared following an established protocol [26]. Briefly, BMSCs or HUVECs (2 × 106 cells/mL) were suspended together with S1P/Oxy sterosomes at predetermined concentrations in a 2% w/v MeGC solution containing 6 μM riboflavin as a photoinitiator. Aliquots of 40 μL were exposed to blue light (Carent) for 40 s to initiate crosslinking. The resulting hydrogels were then transferred into uncoated 48-well plates and cultured in 500 μL of the corresponding culture medium per well.

In vitro cytocompatibility studies

The cytocompatibility of S1P/Oxy sterosomes was evaluated in both 2D and 3D culture models. For 2D assays, BMSCs and HUVECs were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 8 × 103 cells/well and treated with S1P/Oxy sterosomes at various concentrations. Cell viability was assessed on day 3 and 7 using the CCK-8 (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) and Live/Dead staining. For 3D studies, cells were encapsulated in MeGC hydrogels containing riboflavin and analyzed at identical time points. For CCK-8 assays, 100 μL of culture medium containing 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 3 h in the dark. Subsequently, 100 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a new 96-well plate, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Synergy HTX microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, USA). Wells containing culture medium and CCK-8 reagent without cells served as blanks. For Live/Dead staining, samples were gently rinsed with PBS and incubated in staining solution (PBS containing 2 μM calcein-AM and 4.5 μM propidium iodide) for 15 min at 37 °C. Stained cells were visualized using an Eclipse TE2000-U fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), where viable and dead cells appeared green and red, respectively.

Cell migration and recruitment

The migratory capacity of BMSCs and HUVECs was evaluated using 24-well Transwell inserts with 8-μm pores (Corning, USA). Cells were cultured to 80-90% confluence and serum-starved in basal medium for 12 h to synchronize the cell cycle. Following trypsinization, cells were resuspended in serum-free medium. For each assay, 600 μL of medium containing S1P/Oxy sterosomes at the indicated concentrations was added to the lower chambers, and 100 μL of the cell suspension was seeded into the upper chamber. Following incubation at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere for 24 h, non-migrated cells on the upper membrane surface were gently removed with a cotton swab. Migrated cells adhering to the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min, rinsed with distilled water, and imaged under an inverted microscope. The number of migrated cells was quantified using ImageJ.

For wound-healing assays, serum-free medium was employed to minimize the confounding effects of proliferation. Briefly, BMSCs or HUVECs were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured until confluent. A straight scratch was created across the monolayer using a sterile 200-μL pipette tip, and detached cells were removed with PBS. The cells were then cultured in serum-free medium containing S1P/Oxy sterosomes at various concentrations. Images were captured at 0 h and 24 h using the inverted microscope, and wound closure was quantified using ImageJ.

Staining and quantification of ALP and ARS

BMSCs were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well, and cultured in osteogenic medium (α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 50 μg/mL L-ascorbic acid, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, and 1% P/S) containing S1P/Oxy sterosomes (0, 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 μg/mL). The medium was refreshed every other day starting from day 3. On day 7, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained using a BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Chromogenic Kit for 10 min. For ALP activity quantification, total cellular proteins were extracted using Whole Cell Lysis Buffer without phosphatase inhibitors. Total protein concentration was determined with a BCA Protein Quantification Kit, and ALP activity was measured using an Alkaline Phosphatase Assay Kit. To assess matrix mineralization, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with 2% ARS solution for 5 min. For semi-quantitative analysis, bound dye was extracted using acetic acid, neutralized with ammonium hydroxide, and the absorbance was measured at 405 nm.

For 3D culture, MeGC hydrogels encapsulating BMSCs and S1P/Oxy sterosomes (0, 25, 50, 75, 100, 125 μg/mL) were prepared as described and cultured in OM. On day 7, hydrogels were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with BCIP/NBT solution for 5 min. Images were acquired using a stereomicroscope. For ALP activity assays, proteins were extracted from hydrogels using Whole Cell Lysis Buffer, and total protein content and ALP activity were quantified as described above. For mineralization assessment, hydrogels were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 2% ARS for 3 min, followed by extensive PBS washing (over 16 h with gentle shaking). Images were captured under the stereomicroscope, and semi-quantification was performed via acetic acid extraction, ammonium hydroxide neutralization, and colorimetric measurement at 405 nm. The results were normalized to the wet weight of the stained hydrogels.

Evaluation of the anti-apoptotic capacity of S1P/Oxy sterosomes

Cell apoptosis was evaluated using an FITC-Annexin V/PI double-staining kit (Elabscience, Wuhan, China). Briefly, BMSCs were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were then exposed to serum-free medium containing 100 μM H2O2 for 8 h to induce oxidative stress. Subsequently, cells were stained with FITC-Annexin V and PI according to the manufacturer's instructions and immediately analyzed by flow cytometry (Bruker).

For morphological assessment, H2O2-treated cells were washed three times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100. Actin filaments were visualized using phalloidin staining, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were captured using a Zeiss LSM980 microscope.

Tube formation assay of HUVECs

HUVECs were pretreated with S1P/Oxy sterosomes for 6 h and subsequently seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well into 24-well plates precoated with Matrigel (Corning). Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 for an additional 6 h to allow capillary-like tube formation. After incubation, the culture medium was gently aspirated, and cells were stained with 2 μM calcein-AM for 15 min at 37 °C. Tube networks were visualized under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon). Quantitative analyses of total tube length, number of branch points, and junctions were performed using the AngioTool v0.9 software.

Vascularization in a CAM model

Fertilized chicken eggs were incubated at 37 °C under humidified conditions for 5 days. On day 5, a 1 cm2 window was carefully created in the eggshell above the air chamber using a sterile scalpel. A 100 μL aliquot of S1P/Oxy sterosome suspension was gently applied onto the CAM surface, with saline serving as control. The opening was resealed with a sterile sealing film, and the eggs were returned to the incubator for an additional 24 h. Following incubation, the CAMs were carefully exposed, and images were captured using a digital camera (Canon). Angiogenic responses were quantified by measuring vessel density and branching using ImageJ.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from S1P/Oxy-treated BMSCs or HUVECs using Trizol reagent, followed by purification with the RNeasy Mini Kit. A total of 500 ng purified RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a commercial reverse transcription kit. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed on a StepOnePlus RT-qPCR system (Applied Biosystems, California, USA) in a 20 μL reaction mixture using SYBR Green chemistry. Gene expression levels were normalized to the housekeeping gene (α-Tubulin). Primer sequences used in this study are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blot

Cells were lysed on ice in RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor cocktail and 1% phosphatase inhibitor. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Quantification Kit. Equal amounts of protein (25 μg per lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After washing, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents and captured with a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad). Antibodies used in this study are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Critical-sized calvarial defect model

The in vivo regenerative efficacy of S1P/Oxy sterosomes was evaluated using a critical-sized calvarial defect model in SD rats. All surgical procedures strictly adhered to the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Southern Medical University. Briefly, 6-week-old male SD rats were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital, and a 5-mm-diameter critical-sized defect was created in the right calvaria. The defects were implanted with MeGC hydrogels containing S1P/Oxy sterosomes at concentrations of 0, 100, 200, or 500 μg/mL, while untreated defects served as blank controls. 6 weeks post-surgery, rats were euthanized, and calvarial specimens were harvested for further analysis.

Morphometric analysis by μCT

Samples were scanned at a 10 μm resolution using a Quantum GX μCT system (PerkinElmer). The volumetric data were converted to Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format and imported into Dolphin Imaging software (Chatsworth) to generate 3D and multiplanar reconstructed images for linear measurements. Quantitative morphometric parameters, including new bone surface area (normalized to the original 5-mm defect), bone volume fraction (BV/TV%) and trabecular number (Tb.N, mm−1) were analyzed using the SkyScan CT-Analyzer program (Bruker).

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Fixed tissues were decalcified in 0.5 M ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution (pH 7.4) at room temperature for 4 weeks with gentle agitation, refreshing the solution every 3 days. Decalcified samples were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for general morphology and Masson's trichrome to evaluate collagen deposition and bone maturation. For immunohistochemistry (IHC), deparaffinized sections underwent antigen retrieval before incubation with rabbit monoclonal antibodies against osteocalcin (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) and CD31 (Proteintech, Wuhan, China). Signals were detected using an HRP/DAB kit (ZSJQ, Beijing, China) and counterstained with hematoxylin. Images were acquired using the upright microscope.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests for multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 19.0. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
