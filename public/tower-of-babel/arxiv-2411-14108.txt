The Black Hole Singularity as a Thermodynamic System being the Seat of BH Entropy


Abstract
We argue in this paper that the entropy of the BH is located in the BH singularity and that the localization around the event horizon is perhaps a secondary effect. We show in particular that the dependence of the entropy located in the singularity is also proportional to M 2 M^{2} . We furthermore show that our analysis leads in a natural way to information loss as the micro structure of the BH singularity is of a completely different character compared to ordinary quantum space time underlying our classical space time manifold.

The Information Paradox and the Question of Pure or Mixed States

Without doubt the BH is a non-closed , i.e. open system of many DoF. That is, if one wants to treat it as a quantum system, the natural context should be the regime of (thermal)
many body systems
. In a certain approximation one may view it as a
microcanonical quantum ensemble
in
quantum statistical mechanics
. This field of physics was exactly created to deal with such problems.

On the other hand, the socalled BH information paradox results from treating the BH initial state as a pure quantum state. this is a little bit puzzling, but there are given certain arguments why this appears reasonable. For example, in the nice lecture notes of ‘t Hooft (
[
29
]
)

From a physical point of view the distinction between pure states and mixed states for macroscopic objects is pointless. Black Holes should be regarded as being macroscopic. So it is very likely that what we perceive as a mixed state is actually a pure state whose details we were unable to resolve.

A slightly more detailed remark can be found in
[
30
]
:

One can imagine a Black Hole formed from matter in a pure state, e.g. a gravitating sphere of superfluid at
T=0\,K
. The unitarity principle would thus require that the system always remain in a pure state.

While this latter remark adresses the real problems, it is, on the other hand, obvious that practically no BH-process will fulfill this requirements. We will therefore rather discuss the former remarks by ‘t Hooft as such a point of view one can find frequently in the literature concerning the BH information paradox.

In classical statistical mechanics it is correct that an ensemble like the microcanonical can be thought of as a group of individual systems each occupying a distinct point in the socalled
phase cell
in configuration space. In quantum statistical mechanics this is however in our view grossly incorrect.

Remark 2.1

It is fundamental in quantum theory that (pure) states have to be prepared. Therefore one has to make a distinction between socalled proper and improper mixed states, notions which have been introduced by D’Espagnat (
[

The former concept was introduced by v.Neumann in

\{\psi_{j}\}

\rho=\sum_{j}\,p_{j}\cdot|\psi_{j}><\psi_{j}|\quad,\quad\sum_{j}\,p_{j}=1

\psi=\sum_{j}\,c_{j}\cdot\psi_{j}\quad,\quad p_{j}=|c_{j}|^{2}

(9)

This may be called the

Perhaps more important and more typical are scenarios where one cannot assume that the individual states

But this scenario may as well prevail in quantum statistical mechanics. On the one hand we may have an open system which is entangled to its environment. On the other hand, even if the system happens to be closed and isolated from the environment to some extent, its state will, in our view, never be a proper one as the individual states making up the density matrix are usually never individually prepared pure states.

It is useful to treat the microcanonical ensemble. We assume that its total energy lies in the interval

\rho=N^{-1}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{N}\,|\psi_{j}><\psi_{j}|=N^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{E}

(12)

\{\psi_{j}\}

which formally relates the behavior of the BH event horizon to the well known laws of thermodynamics, it seemed to be more or less evident that it is the event horizon which is playing the essential role in this investigation. Only much later we find some sceptical remarks as to this widespread view. For the benefit of the reader we cite some brief remarks below, which can be found in the following papers:

It is evident that the event horizon seems to behave in a way similar to a thermodynamic system. This is, at first glance, not so clear in the case of the BH singularity. We will try to clarify this in the following investigation. We have seen that the entropy of the BH, associated with the event horizon is proportional tro the area
A

S_{BH}=A/4L^{2}_{p}\quad\text{with}\quad A=16\pi(GM/c^{2})^{2}

(16)

That is, it is proportional to
M^{2}

M^{2}
and that the proportionality to

To this end we have, in a first step, to clarify the presumed microscopic nature of the classical BH singularity. Put differently, we have to replace what is in the classical context a point singularity of the space-time manifold,
ST
, microscopically, that is, perhaps on Planck scale level, by an infinitesimal neighborhood. Usually it is in a somewhat cursory way stated that space and time are dissolved or destroyed at the BH singularity. We will try to give a more concrete meaning to this vague picture.

Remark 3.1

Note that this vague picture necessarily implies that something more primordial is expected to exist into which this singularity is dissolved. In a first step we want to describe this presumed microscopic structure. We already introduced in
[

the notion of physical points, i.e. points, having a rich internal structure under a microscope.

We assume the following to hold:

Conjecture 3.2

The local state of the infinitesimal, microscopic, Planck scale region, representing the classical BH singularity, is of the same nature as the state which prevailed befor the Big Bang and/or cosmic inflation happened.

We briefly discussed the nature of this latter scenario in sect. 2 of

and we still have to be brief in the following as a more detailed discussion is quite complex and would go beyond the aims of the present paper. Some more remarks can be found in the papers we are citing below. We will only provide the concepts we will need in the followiing investigation.

Remark 3.3

It is obvious that this topic, i.e., the phase transition, starting from some primordial initial state and unfolding ultimately into our present universe, should be closely connected with the inflationary universe scenario as it is e.g. beautifully described by A.Guth in

One should however note that in the following we will develop a picture in which space-time on its most fundamental level is no longer a smooth manifold while the socalled

We now undertake to describe the nature of this primordial phase from which our present universe did emerge in a primordial phase transition, dubbed
PT

QX
, the emergent
(quantum) space-time

We assume that our present universe emerged from a first order phase transition

QX\overset{PT}{\rightarrow}(Q)ST+(Q)M

S_{i}

In this class there exist certain maximal subsimplices, that is, every further addition of a node distroys this property. These maximal subsimplices are called cliques and are the canditates for our physical points, i.e. classical points of our assumed manifold, having an internal structure.

Remark 3.13

Note that the transition to a smooth manifold is a quite complex process with quite an amount of modern mathematics being needed. This is described in more detail in our above mentioned papers (see e.g.
[
59
]
).

3.2
The Entanglement network

We will now introduce an, as we think, important generalization. E.Schroedinger in 1935 made a crucial observation (
[
63
]
):

I would not call that one but the characteristic trait of quantum mechanics, the one that enforces its entire departure from classical lines of thought.

That is, instead of employing elementary interactions or information channels, we want to view the edges of the graph as certain entanglement relations which we will explain below.

Conjecture 3.14

While the phenomenon of entanglement, which is closely related to the property of complex linearity, is not really well understood ontologically on the present level of quantum physics, we think, it represents a kind of primordial interaction like relation on the level of pregeometry and corresponding more microscopic structures underlying our present day quantum theory.

Remark 3.15

The notion of entanglement can be succinctly expressed as follows: Two quantum systems one Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}
, with joint Hilbert space the tensor product
\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}
, and joint state
\omega_{12}
are not entangled and called separable if
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
have their own independent states
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
with

\omega_{12}=\omega_{1}\otimes\omega_{2}

(25)

with the
\omega_{i}
some density matrices. They are entangled if this does not hold.

This property played a crucial role in the early debates around the EPR-paper
[
64
]
.

In the present discussion of entanglement (e.g. in quantum information theory) a slightly more general definition of separability is used:

Definition 3.16

A bipartite state
\omega_{12}
is separable if it can be written as

\omega_{12}=\sum_{i}\,p_{i}\cdot\omega_{1}^{i}\otimes\omega_{2}^{i}\quad,\quad p_{i}\geq 0\;,\;\sum\,p_{i}=1

(26)

This implies that there do exist only classical correlations.

As the literature about entanglement is extremely large, we will mention only two representative papers (
[
65
]
and
[
66
]
). The typical fields of application of entanglement properties are quantum information theory, quantum many body physics and, to some extent, BH physics. But recently it was also applied in quantum gravity or quantum space-time physics, more specifically, in the subfield of
gauge theory/gravity duality
(see e.g.
[
67
]
and
[
68
]
).

While, in our view, it is not guaranteed that quantum theory necessarily is holding sway on the most primordial level, e.g. on the Planck scale, we assume, employing our picture of a
geometric renormalization group
, that this may hold at least on some intermediate level. That is, we assume that we have some
tensor network
living on a corresponding
simple graph
as described above. But as the notion of entanglement, in particular in such
multipartite structures
as a tensor network, is a relatively delicate and subtle property (cf. e.g. the section on multipartite entanglement in
[
65
]
), we have, in a first step, to develop and explain various points.

We assume that we are given a large tensor product of
N
Hilbert spaces,
\mathcal{H}_{i},i=1,\ldots,N
.
For convenience we furthermore assume that all the Hilbert spaces are finite dimensional. That is:

Definition 3.17

We have

\mathcal{H}:=\bigotimes_{i}\,\mathcal{H}_{i}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{H}_{N}

(27)

with monomials
\bigotimes_{i}\psi_{i}\;,\;\psi_{i}\in\mathcal{H}_{i}
. If
\{e^{i}_{\nu}\}
is a basis in
\mathcal{H}_{i}
a general Hilbert space vector
\Psi\in\mathcal{H}
can be represented as

\Psi=\sum\,c^{\nu_{1}\ldots\nu_{N}}\cdot e^{1}_{\nu_{1}}\otimes\cdots e^{N}_{\nu_{N}}

(28)

On
\mathcal{H}=\bigotimes_{i}\mathcal{H}_{i}
we are given the tensor algebra
\mathcal{A}
with monomials

A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{N}\;,\;A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}\;\text{the algebra of operators on}\;\mathcal{H}_{i}

(29)

We assume now that we have (for simplicity reasons) a pure state
\Psi
on
\mathcal{H}
and a subcomplex of Hilbert spaces

\mathcal{H}_{S}=\bigotimes\mathcal{H}_{j}\;,\;S\subset\{1,\ldots,N\}\;,\;j\in S

(30)

with corresponding tensor subalgebra
\mathcal{A}_{S}
.

Observation 3.18

The reduced state of
\Psi
relative to
(\mathcal{H}_{S},\mathcal{A}_{S})
is defined by

\omega_{S}(A_{S}):=(\Psi|A_{S}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{S^{\prime}}\Psi)=Tr\,(\rho_{S}\cdot A_{S})

(31)

S^{\prime}
the complement of
S
in
\{1,\ldots,N\}
and with
\rho_{S}
the reduced density matrix on
(\mathcal{H}_{S},\mathcal{A}_{S})
given by (for ease of notation we use indices
(12),(1),(2)
)

(\psi|\rho_{1}\phi)=\sum_{i}\,(\psi\otimes e_{i}|\rho_{12}\phi\otimes e_{i})

(32)

with
\rho_{12},\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
density matrices on
\mathcal{H}_{12},\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}
,
\psi,\phi
vectors in
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\{e_{i}\}
an orthonormal basis of unit vectors in
\mathcal{H}_{2}
(
\Psi
being expressed as
|\Psi><\Psi|=\rho_{12}
in our case). I.e., a pure state on
\mathcal{H}
becomes (in general) a mixed state on
\mathcal{H}_{S}
.

We can go one step further by taking a subset
U
of
S
and construct a corresponding mixed state on
U
:

Observation 3.19

\omega_{U}(A_{U})=\omega(A_{U}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{U^{\prime}})=\omega_{S}(A_{U}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{U^{\prime\prime}})=Tr(\rho_{U}\cdot A_{U})

(33)

with
U^{\prime},U^{\prime\prime}
the complements of
U
in
\{1,\ldots,N\},S
,
\omega_{U}
the reduced state relative to
\omega_{S}
,
\rho_{U}
the reduced density matrix relative to
\rho_{S}
or
\omega
.

We said above that entanglement properties happen to be more complicated in multipartite systems. We would like to discuss at least one, in our view, particularly important property in this context.

Definition 3.20

Let
\mathcal{H}=\bigotimes_{i}\,\mathcal{H}_{i}
be some tensor product or some sub tensor product
\mathcal{H}_{S}
contained in a larger product,
\omega
a state on
\mathcal{H}
or
\mathcal{H}_{S}
.
\omega
is called maximally entangled if the following holds:

For any bipartite decomposition,
S=U\cup U^{\prime}
,
U^{\prime}
the complement of
U
in
S
,

\omega\neq\sum\,p_{i}\cdot\omega_{U}^{i}\otimes\omega_{U^{\prime}}^{i}

(34)

Remark 3.21

We note that this property is closely related to our notion of complete subgraph and cliques we defined above in graph theory.

We will now discuss the relation of our graph theoretic approach, introduced above, to the concept of
separability
and
entanglement
of multipartite systems.

Remark 3.22

While it is not really crucial, we assume in the following that the notion of separability is adequately described on our primordial level we are envisaging by

\omega=\omega_{U}\otimes\omega_{U^{\prime}}

(35)

as we think, the sum over probabilities,
p_{i}
, is rather signalling a classical behavior which should be absent on the presumed fundamental level.

We introduced above the notion of a
simple graph
with vertices
V=\{x_{i}\}
and simple edges
E=\{e_{ij}\}
, connecting the vertices
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
. We now want to relate this picture to corresponding entanglement properties of a
tensor network
. We associate a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{i}
to each vertex
x_{i}
. Important is the distribution of edges as it encodes the pregeometric structure of our system.

We assume a state,
\omega
, be given on
\bigotimes_{i}\,\mathcal{H}_{i}
. We select two arbitrary vertices
x_{i},x_{j}
in the vertex set
V
and define the relative state
\omega_{ij}
on
\mathcal{H}_{i}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{j}
induced by the fixed given global state
\omega
.

\omega_{ij}(A_{ij})=\omega(A_{ij}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{S^{\prime}})

(36)

S^{\prime}
the complement of
\{i,j\}
in
V
.

Definition 3.23

i) If
\omega_{ij}
is separable, i.e., if there exist two states,
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}
, so that
\omega=\omega_{1}\otimes\omega_{2}
, the edge between
x_{i},x_{j}
is empty.

ii) If
\omega_{ij}
is entangled, we connect
x_{i},x_{j}
by an edge.

Conclusion 3.24

In this sense we relate an entanglement graph,
G_{e}
, to our state
\omega
on the tensor product
\bigotimes_{i}\mathcal{H}_{i}
. When we assume a kind of dynamics to exist on our system, which constantly is changing the state

, this will change, by the same token, the geometry of our entanglement graph.

While we restrict our investigation (for the time being) to simple graphs, one should note that we can introduce more complex graph structures if it would become necessary. We will now show that our above construction of an entanglement graph with the help of pairs of vertices is consistent in a sense we will now prove. We start with a simple lemma:

Lemma 3.25

The existence of entanglement in a tensor product
\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}
can already be proved on monomials
A_{1}\otimes A_{2}
.

Proof: Due to linearity, if a state is separable on all products like
A_{1}\otimes A_{2}
it is separable on the full tensor product
\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}
. This implies that in case
\omega
is not separable, i.e., is entangled, this can already be exhibited on products like
A_{1}\otimes A_{2}
.

We now prove the following:

Theorem 3.26

We divide the vertex set
V
of the graph
G_{e}
, defined by a global state
\omega
, into
S\cup S^{\prime}
and assume that there exists a pair
x_{i}\in S,x_{j}\in S^{\prime}
so that the reduced state
\omega_{ij}
is entangled on
\mathcal{H}_{i}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{j}
, that is

\omega_{ij}\neq\omega_{i}\otimes\omega_{j}

(37)

That is, there exists an edge connecting
x_{i},x_{j}
. Then
\omega
is entangled relative to
\mathcal{H}_{S},\mathcal{H}_{S^{\prime}}
, i.e.

\omega\neq\omega_{S}\otimes\omega_{S^{\prime}}

(38)

Proof: We have that

\displaystyle\omega(A_{i}\otimes\mathbf{1})

\displaystyle=\omega_{ij}(A_{i}\otimes\mathbf{1})=\omega_{i}(A_{i})=\omega_{S}(A_{i}\otimes\mathbf{1})

(39)

\displaystyle\omega(\mathbf{1}\otimes B_{j})

\displaystyle=\omega_{ij}(\mathbf{1}\otimes B_{j})=\omega_{j}(B_{j})=\omega_{S^{\prime}}(B_{j}\otimes\mathbf{1})

(40)

with
\mathbf{1}
acting on the respective dual spaces. By assumption there exist pairs,
A_{i},B_{j}
, so that

\omega_{ij}\neq\omega_{i}(A_{i})\cdot\omega_{j}(B_{j})

(41)

This implies

\omega(A_{i}\otimes B_{j}\otimes\mathbf{1})\neq\omega_{S}(A_{i})\cdot\otimes\mathbf{1})\cdot\omega_{S^{\prime}}(B_{j}\otimes\mathbf{1})

(42)

with
\mathbf{1}
the unit operators on the respective dual spaces. I.e., if
\omega_{ij}
is entangled,
\omega
is also entangled relative to
S,S^{\prime}
.

Corollary 3.27

If the entanglement graph
G_{e}
is maximally entangled according to our definition via pairs of Hilbeert spaces,
\mathcal{H}_{i},\mathcal{H}_{j}
, then
\omega
on
\mathcal{H}
is maximally entangled according to the definition of entanglement of states, that is,
\omega
is entangled relative to any bipartite division of
\mathcal{H}
into
\mathcal{H}_{S},\mathcal{H}_{S^{\prime}}
.

Proof: As above, we select in any bipartite division
S,S^{\prime}
a pair
x_{i}\in S,x_{j}\in S^{\prime}
and procede in the same way as above in the proof of the theorem.

We want to close this section by showing that one can, in a natural way, attribute a value to the edges of
G_{e}
in form of a strength of the respective connection. We will employ the notion of entropy to define such a strength of the connection. The
v.Neumann entropy
of
\rho
is given by

S(\rho):=-Tr(\rho\cdot\ln\,\rho)

(43)

Theorem 3.28

We have the important sub-additivity inequality for the relative entropy:

S(\rho_{12})\leq S(\rho_{1})+S(\rho_{2})=S(\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2})

(44)

(More on this important topic can e.g. be found in
[
69
]
,
[
70
]
or
[
71
]
).

It is then natural to compare the lhs and the rhs of the above equation and define:

Definition 3.29

(Mutual Information)

I(\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}):=S(\rho_{1})+S(\rho_{2})-S(\rho_{12})\geq 0

(45)

Corollary 3.30

Note that
I(\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2})
exactly vanishes if
\rho_{12}=\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2}
, i.e., no edge according to our definition.

Conclusion 3.31

We now attribute such a notion to the reduced states
(\omega_{ij},\rho_{ij})
and
(\omega_{i},\rho_{i}),(\omega_{j},\rho_{j})
.

4
The Entropy of the Singularity

The preceding sections represented the necessary steps to be able to motivate in this final section our claim that the BH entropy is located in the BH singularity or, to state it in a slightly different form, that the localization of the entropy is observer dependent, i.e., is after all a consequence of the equivalence of coordinate systems, that is, either a static reference system outside of the BH horizon or, for example, a freely falling observer.

We argued that the fine structure of
QX
and
QST
consists of a large array of elementary DoF which are related or connected by elementary interactions, information channels or entanglement relations. We came to the conclusion that we can represent the
wiring diagram
by a huge graph of, say
n
vertices being connected by a number of
N^{\prime}
edges.

Remark 4.1

In the following we will usually only deal with some subgraph of the total graph, for example with the subset of
QX
that represents the infinitesimal region belonging to the macroscopic BH singularity. That is, in that case the parameters
n
and
N^{\prime}
belong to the corresponding subgraph.

Observation 4.2

The maximal possible number of edges is

n\cdot(n-1)/2=\binom{n}{2}=:N

(46)

Assumption 4.3

We assume that
QX
can be approximated by a kind of equilibrium state and that the primordial dynamics (among other things) is constantly switching the various edges on and off. We hence expect that the state of
QX
is a state of maximal entropy.

We will concentrate ourselves in the following on the
configuration entropy
of our graph state as we expect this to be the main contribution. We now show the following:

Observation 4.4

The numberof different graph configurations, consisting of exactly
N^{\prime}
edges is
\binom{N}{N^{\prime}}
, that is the number of
N^{\prime}
-sets in a set of
N
elements.

Lemma 4.5

It holds

\binom{n}{r}=(n-r+1)/r\cdot\binom{n}{r-1}>(n/r-1)\cdot\binom{n}{r-1}

(47)

Proof: This follows directly from the definition

\binom{n}{r}=n!/r!\cdot(n-r)!

(48)

We therefore have for
r<n/2
:

n/r-1>1\;\text{that is}\;\binom{n}{r}>\binom{n}{r-1}

(49)

Conclusion 4.6

The maximum of
\binom{n}{r}
is attained for
r=n/2
(where, for simlicity reason, we assume
n
to be even.

In a next step we estimate the quantity
\binom{N}{N/2}=N!/(N/2)!(N/2)!
or, rather, its logarithm for large
N
. We use Stirling’s formula

N!\approx\sqrt{2\pi N}\cdot(N/e)^{N}

(50)

We have (
\ln e=1
):

\displaystyle\ln N!

\displaystyle\approx\ln\sqrt{2\pi N}+N\ln N-N

(51)

\displaystyle\ln(N/2)!^{2}

\displaystyle\approx 2[\ln\sqrt{2\pi N/2}+N/2\ln N/2-N/2]

(52)

Hence

\ln\binom{N}{N/2}\approx[\ln\sqrt{2\pi N}+N\ln N-N]-\\
2[\ln\sqrt{2\pi N/2}+N/2\ln N/2-N/2]=\ln\sqrt{2\pi N}-2\ln\sqrt{\pi N}+N\ln 2\\
\approx N\ln 2\quad(+1/2\cdot\ln N)

(53)

Conclusion 4.7

The maximum of configuration entropy is attained for
N^{\prime}
, the numberof edges in the graph, equal to
N/2
and has the value

\ln\binom{N}{N/2}\approx N\ln 2\;\text{with}\;N=\binom{n}{2}=n\cdot(n-1)/2\approx n^{2}/2

(54)

Up to now we assumed that the graph has exactly
\binom{N}{N/2}
edges. In reality this number will fluctuate around some mean value. The following result will show that the final result will be essentially unaffected by such a restriction.

Lemma 4.8

It holds

\sum_{k}\binom{N}{k}=2^{N}\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\ln\biggl(\sum_{k}\binom{N}{k}\biggr)=N\cdot\ln 2

(55)

that is, we get the same result if the number of edges is allowed to vary.

Proof: A result following from the binomial formula for

(x+y)^{N}=\sum_{k}\binom{N}{k}x^{k}y^{N-k}

(56)

with
x=y=1
.

We now proceed to establish the functional dependence of the above entropy on the mass of the BH. We think it is natural to relate the macroscopic mass,
M
, of the BH to the number of microscopic DoF and write:

Conclusion 4.9

We assume that

M=n\cdot M_{p}\quad,\quad M_{p}=\sqrt{\hbar c/G}

(57)

holds where
n
now belongs to the subgraph, representing the infinitesimal region which is associated with the macroscopic BH singularity. This implies by the same token that
M_{p}\cdot c^{2}
is the energy necessary to transform a Planck cell of size
l_{p}^{3}
located in
QST
into the corresponding cell in
QX
.
For the entropy it follows that

\hat{S}\approx N\ln 2\approx(\ln 2/2)\cdot n^{2}=(\ln 2/2)\cdot M^{2}/M_{p}^{2}

(58)

We now want to estimate the extension of the infinitesimal region which macroscopically is considered as the BH singularity. Again we invoke the fundamental role played by the system of Planck units.

Assumption 4.10

We associate an elementary DoF with the corresponding Planck volume

l_{p}^{3}=(\sqrt{\hbar G/c^{3}})^{3}\approx 10^{-105}m^{3}

(59)

To show that the region being associated to the BH singularity is indeed infinitesimal on the macroscopic level we take for example the mass of our sun

M_{sun}\approx 10^{53}g\quad\text{with}\quad M_{sun}/M_{p}\approx 10^{38}

(60)

We have

10^{38}\cdot l_{p}^{3}\approx 10^{-67}m^{3}

(61)

for the volume of the infinitesimal region and hence for the presumed radius of the infinitesimal region associated to the BH singularity:

R_{sing}(sun)\approx 10^{-22}m

(62)

Conclusion 4.11

The extension of the infinitesimal region associated to the BH singularity is indeed infinitesimal in SI-units.
