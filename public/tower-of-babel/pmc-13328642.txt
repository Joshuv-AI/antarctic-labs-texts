Assessing the temporal dynamics of airborne insect communities through DNA metabarcoding of aerial trap catches
Authors: Mincheol Kim, Wonhoon Lee, Hyobin Lee, Sanghyo Park, Yejin Kang, Jaeyun Kim, Jae Hyeon Lee, Jinseo Oh, Nu Ri Myeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInsect migration is a key ecological process with significant implications for biodiversity, ecosystem functioning, and agriculture. However, the seasonal dynamics of most airborne insect taxa remain poorly understood. Conventional monitoring methods are often constrained by low taxonomic resolution and difficulties in specimen identification. DNA metabarcoding offers a powerful alternative; however, its application to directly sampled airborne insects is limited. To address this gap, this study applied a two-marker metabarcoding framework—cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) and mitochondrial 16S rRNA—to characterize the temporal dynamics of airborne insect communities in a southern port city of South Korea. Monthly collections from April to October 2023 using a 10-m aerial trap revealed diverse taxa, including small-bodied insects, endemic species, and agricultural pests of potential concern. COI outperformed 16S by recovering a broader range of orders and resolving over half of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) to the species level, compared to less than 20% for 16S, which was largely restricted to Hemiptera despite recovering more ASVs overall. These results highlight the relative superiority of COI for species-level resolution in airborne insect monitoring and demonstrate that 16S rRNA can complement COI by capturing additional diversity. The combined use of COI and 16S rRNA was particularly effective, improving taxonomic coverage and consistently revealing three distinct temporal phases in community composition: early May to early July, late July to early September, and late September to late October. Our findings demonstrate the utility of metabarcoding for the high-resolution monitoring of airborne insect diversity and temporal dynamics. Introduction

Migration plays a key role in the evolution and life histories of many organisms, with insects representing the most abundant, diverse, and economically important group of terrestrial migrants1. Globally, billions of insects annually undertake long-range movement. Insect migrants provide numerous ecosystem services by transporting energy, nutrients, and other organisms between distant regions2–4. Over the past few decades, research has focused primarily on the migration of a limited number of large-bodied insect species (> 40 mg), providing insights into their migratory routes, physiological adaptations, and ecological roles, particularly in relation to pest dynamics and ecosystem processes5–8. However, the migration patterns of the vast majority of insect taxa remain poorly documented.

Radar studies have revealed the magnitude of insect migration, highlighting its role in ecosystem biogeochemistry through the transfer of micronutrients by trillions of insects moving annually in Europe9,10. However, radar-based approaches rarely provide species-level information, as the majority of airborne insects are small-bodied and cannot be reliably identified using radar signals11, thus limiting insights into the adaptive strategies, drivers, processes, and ecological effects of long-distance migration across diverse insect taxa9,12. Alternatively, aerial trapping followed by manual microscopic identification can provide species-level resolution. However, this method is constrained by the need for taxonomic expertise and frequent damage to specimens caused by high-altitude wind exposure, which often hinders accurate identification.

Molecular methods, such as DNA metabarcoding, offer a promising avenue for the rapid and cost-effective taxonomic identification of insect communities13,14. Previous studies have demonstrated that DNA metabarcoding can provide reliable and efficient taxonomic identification compared to traditional morphology-based approaches15–18. In addition, recent large-scale metabarcoding studies have highlighted its capacity to efficiently recover substantial proportions of insect diversity and reveal previously undetected taxa19. To date, many applications have focused on aquatic ecosystems20,21 or terrestrial soil communities22,23.

In South Korea, 10-m-high aerial traps are used by institutions such as the Animal and Plant Quarantine Agency (APQA) and the Rural Development Administration (RDA) as part of official pest monitoring programs, and this trapping height is recognized as a standard approach for surveillance under the Plant Protection Act. Aerial trapping at this height is particularly useful for capturing insects engaged in airborne dispersal, including migratory and invasive species. Although 10 m traps have been widely used for pest monitoring, previous applications have largely focused on selected target species, and comprehensive assessments of the overall insect diversity captured by these systems remain limited.

In this study, we employed DNA metabarcoding using two marker genes (mitochondrial COI and 16S rRNA) to investigate the temporal dynamics of airborne insect communities. Aerial insect samples were collected from April to October using a 10-m-high trap in a southern port city in South Korea. We evaluated the utility of metabarcoding as a practical alternative to conventional morphological identification and compared the taxonomic coverage and species-level resolution of COI and 16S markers, with a particular focus on their effectiveness for insect pest surveillance.

Material and methods

Study site and insect sampling

The study was conducted in Gwangyang-si, a port city in Jeollanam-do, South Korea (34.9210220N, 127.6959196E). Gwangyang is located in the southeastern part of Jeollanam-do on the southern coast of South Korea, and serves as a key entry point for insects and pests migrating from Southeast Asia and China. The sampling site is located within a port area rather than in peri-urban or agricultural settings. The surrounding environment primarily consists of paved surfaces and port infrastructure, with minimal presence of adjacent farmland, unmanaged vegetation, or other habitat features likely to influence the detected species. Insect samples were collected between April and October 2023 during 17 sampling events (April 25; May 3, 9, and 11; June 11 and 13; July 12 and 27; August 17 and 21; September 2, 5, and 29; October 4, 13, 17, and 31). South Korea is located in the temperate zone; therefore, the sampling period covers three seasons: April–May (spring), June–August (summer), and September–October (autumn).

A 10-m-high aerial trap (hereafter referred to as an aerial trap), a device designed by the manufacturer for monitoring flying insect populations at a height of 10 m (Fig. 1), was installed and operated continuously to capture airborne insects for subsequent analysis. Insects entering the net were guided through a tube and transported downward to ground level by a motor-driven airflow. The collected insects were first retained on a mesh and subsequently transferred to a collection chamber located below the trap, where they were preserved in a dry state for long-term storage (Fig. S1). The trap was cleared at each sampling event, and insects did not accumulate across different sampling periods. To minimize potential carryover effects between sampling events, the net positioned at 10 m height, the connecting tube, and the internal mesh within the device were replaced at each sampling event, ensuring that prior sampling did not influence subsequent sample collection. The trapping device used in this study was a non-commercial, custom-made 10-m-high aerial trap designed for aerial insect monitoring; therefore, no commercial product name, model number, catalogue number, or product code is available. In this study, each “sample” refers to the DNA extract obtained from airborne insect material collected during a sampling event.

DNA extraction and amplicon sequencing

All specimens were labeled and deposited as voucher specimens in the Insect Collection of Gyeongsang National University (GNU), Jinju, South Korea. Samples were preserved either in ethanol (April–May) or in a dry state (from June onward), reflecting a methodological adjustment during the sampling period. DNA was extracted in bulk from pooled insect samples rather than from individual specimens. Collected insects were not sorted by size prior to extraction. Whole genomic DNA was extracted from pooled whole-body insect specimens using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Prior to DNA extraction, pooled samples were homogenized using Ultra Grinder B homogenizers (UG-50; Taeshin BioScience, Namyangju, South Korea) until the insect materials weres finely fragmented and visually uniform to ensure complete tissue lysis. DNA was amplified using two primer sets targeting the mitochondrial 16S rRNA and the cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) region. For mitochondrial 16S, the primer pair AQdb-16S_F (5′-TRA CYG TRC AAA GGT AGC-3′) and AQdb-16S_R (5′-CCG GTY TRA ACT CAR ATC ATG T-3′)24 was used. For the COI region, the primer pair mICOIintF (5′-GGW ACW GGW TGA ACW GTW TAY CCY CC-3′)25 and HCO2198 (5′-TAA ACT TCA GGG TGA CCA AAA AAT CA-3′)26 were employed. The sequencing library was prepared using a two-step PCR process: an amplicon PCR, followed by an index PCR, according to the standard Illumina amplicon sequencing protocol (https://support.illumina.com/downloads/16s_metagenomic_sequencing_library_preparation.html). KAPA HiFi Hotstart ReadyMix (KAPA Biosystems, Wilmington, MA, USA) and ARAClean Beads (LAS, Gimpo, South Korea) were used for the amplicon PCR and purification of the PCR product, respectively. Each PCR reaction (25 µL total volume) contained 12.5 µL of 2 × KAPA HiFi Hotstart ReadyMix, 5 µL of each primer (1 µM), and 2.5 µL of extracted DNA. The PCR conditions for both gene regions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 25 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 51 °C for the mitochondrial 16S or 48 °C for the COI region for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. A PCR negative control (no-template control) was included in each amplification batch to monitor potential contamination. No visible amplification was detected in the negative controls. The resulting libraries were sequenced on a MiSeq platform (Illumina) using Reagent Kit v3 (2 × 300 bp) at LAS Inc. (South Korea). Raw sequencing data have been submitted to the NCBI SRA database under accession number PRJNA1332927.

Bioinformatics

Raw sequencing reads were demultiplexed using bcl2fastq v2.20 (Illumina), and adapter and primer sequences were removed using Cutadapt v2.1027. Quality filtering, paired-end read merging, chimera detection, and inference of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were performed using DADA2 v1.3028. The same filtering and trimming parameters were applied to both gene regions (maxN = 0, maxEE = c(2, 2), truncQ = 2), with different truncation lengths for each marker: COI (truncLen = c(270, 210), and mitochondrial 16S (truncLen = c(280, 120). Erroneous ASVs were further collapsed and removed using LULU v0.1.0 with default options29 to reduce erroneous sequences and curate the final dataset. The amplicon length of COI region was approximately 300 ~ 320 bp, allowing sufficient overlap between paired-end reads. In contrast, the mitochondrial 16S rRNA amplicon (~ 518 bp) resulted in most paired-end reads not overlapping. Therefore, non-overlapping paired-end reads were joined using a concatenation approach as described by Ramakodi30. This method has been shown to improve taxonomic accuracy compared to the use of only forward or reverse reads when sequencing long amplicons31. Taxonomic classification was performed using the combination of SINTAX and BLAST searches against the COInr database v3 (May 23, 2025)32 for the COI region, and the MIDORI2 database (June 19, 2025)25 for mitochondrial 16S rRNA gene sequences. Representative ASV sequences exhibiting < 80% identity to known reference sequences, or classified as non-insect taxa, were excluded. BLAST hits with ≥ 90% identity and ≥ 90% query coverage were accepted for order-level classification. The SINTAX algorithm, implemented in vsearch v2.30.033 with a cut-off value of 0.8, was used to assign taxonomic ranks above the species level. For species-level identification, the best hit was required to have ≥ 98% identity, ≥ 98% query coverage, and an e-value cutoff of 1e-10. Hierarchical taxonomic profiles were visualized using Krona v2.8.134.

Statistical analyses

Species accumulation curves were generated both randomly and in chronological order (collector’s curves) using the specaccum function in the vegan package in R35. Pairwise Jaccard dissimilarities were calculated from presence–absence data, and Bray–Curtis dissimilarities were calculated from Hellinger-transformed ASV relative abundance matrices. The results were visualized using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), where community groupings were delineated using hierarchical clustering (average linkage method) to facilitate an unbiased interpretation of the ecological patterns rather than relying on a priori group assignments. Differences in beta diversity among groups were tested using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA), with p-values determined from 999 permutations in PRIMER v736. The contribution of individual ASVs to between-group dissimilarities was assessed using similarity percentage analysis (SIMPER). Procrustes analysis was performed to evaluate compositional concordance between COI- and 16S-based communities, with the significance of the Procrustes statistic assessed through 999 permutations using the protest function in the vegan R package. All plots were generated in R v4.4.237 using the ggplot238 and ggalluvial39 packages.

Results

Sequencing results and taxonomic coverage

After quality filtering and chimera removal, amplicon sequencing yielded a total of 2,569,195 reads (mean ± SD: 151,129 ± 42,912 per sample) for COI and 2,529,103 reads (148,770 ± 45,740 per sample) for 16S across all 17 samples. Insect-derived sequences accounted for 98.8% of COI reads and 99.9% of 16S reads. The 16S rRNA dataset recovered more insect ASVs than COI (283 vs. 220). COI resolved 12 orders, 52 families, 95 genera, and 98 species, whereas 16S resolved 10 orders, 41 families, 72 genera, and 32 species (Table S1). Most COI-derived ASVs belonged to five major insect orders: Diptera (31.2%), Hemiptera (30.9%), Lepidoptera (8.1%), Hymenoptera (7.3%), and Coleoptera (3.8%). In contrast, the 16S-derived ASVs were dominated by a single order, Hemiptera (77.7%), followed by Diptera (11.9%) and Coleoptera (6.8%) (Fig. 2). At the order level, 17.8% of COI reads were unassigned compared to 0.7% for 16S.

Krona plots showing the hierarchical taxonomic composition of (a) COI and (b) 16S datasets from order to genus level. The size of each sector represents the mean relative read abundance of each taxon across all samples. Only taxa with a mean relative abundance ≥ 0.1% are shown.

Taxonomic assignment rates between COI and 16S differed across taxonomic ranks. Both markers showed similar assignment levels at higher taxonomic ranks, and assignment rates consistently increased from the genus to order levels (Fig. 3A). However, a marked difference in species-level assignment rate was observed between two markers. Nearly half of the COI ASVs were assigned to species (51.6%), whereas only 18.0% of the 16S ASVs could be assigned to known species. At the species level, COI identified 98 species and 16S identified 32, with 16 species overlapping between the two markers (Fig. 3B). The shared species belonged to the orders Diptera, Hemiptera and Lepidoptera; species from seven additional insect orders were detected exclusively by the COI dataset.

Temporal changes in airborne insect communities

Temporal variations in ASV richness fluctuated without clear seasonal trends (Fig. S2). Across both markers, richness peaked in the October 31 sample and was the lowest in the July 27 sample. ASV accumulation curves for both markers continued to increase without reaching a plateau, with 16S showing a steeper slope and yielding a greater total number of ASVs compared to the COI region (Fig. 3C). The collector’s curves indicated that both markers had similar accumulation rates until July 27. After this point, 16S showed a sharp increase in newly detected ASVs between July 17 and September 2, whereas COI maintained a relatively steady accumulation rate throughout the sampling period. Notably, most of the additional ASVs detected by 16S during this surge (n = 87) were assigned to Hemiptera (n = 34) and Diptera (n = 22), with Polionemobius spp. (Orthoptera; n = 10) detected exclusively in this interval.

We examined temporal changes in airborne insect community composition using both presence–absence and abundance matrices. Hierarchical clustering projected onto the ordination space revealed that both markers separated the communities into two major groups: cluster 1 (samples from April 4 to July 12) and cluster 2 (samples from September 29 to October 31) (Fig. 4). Samples collected between July 27 and September 5 formed a distinct transitional assemblage that was clearly separated in the ordination space but did not cluster into a single cohesive group. Multivariate analyses confirmed significant differences among clusters (ANOSIM: Global R = 0.67, P < 0.001; PERMANOVA: Pseudo-F = 3.49, P < 0.001). Results from COI and 16S markers were highly congruent across both Jaccard and Bray–Curtis dissimilarities (PROTEST: r = 0.98 and r = 0.96, respectively; all P < 0.001). The airborne insect communities exhibited a cyclical temporal pattern, with assemblages from July to August most distinct from both the early-season (cluster 1) and late-season (cluster 2), whereas those sampled in mid-to late October converged toward the early-season composition (Fig. 4).

When the SIMPER results from both markers were overlaid onto their respective ordination spaces, ASVs assigned to the genera Rhopalosiphum, Hyalopterus, Acyrthosiphon, and Aphis contributed most strongly to early-season communities (cluster 1) (Fig. 4C). Additionally, two unclassified Aphididae ASVs (16S rRNA) and one unclassified Diptera ASV (COI) were characteristic of this cluster. The transitional period (July–August) was marked by ASVs belonging to Psychoda and Nephotettix. Late-season communities (cluster 2) were primarily influenced by ASVs from Myzus, unclassified Delphacidae and Lepidoptera ASVs (COI), and an unclassified Cicadellidae ASV (16S).

Monthly patterns of insect orders varied among the markers, although some similarities were observed (Fig. S3). Diptera and Hemiptera were the predominant orders across the survey period in both datasets, with Hemiptera comprising more than 50% of all 16S samples. Peaks in the relative abundance of Diptera were detected in July and September using both markers, and an increase in the relative abundance of Coleoptera was consistently observed in July. Marker-specific patterns were also observed. In the COI dataset, Lepidoptera showed high abundance in August and September, and Hemiptera abundance increased sharply in September; these patterns were not observed in the 16S dataset. Conversely, Orthoptera were detected exclusively in the August samples of the 16S dataset. Additionally, a high proportion of unclassified insect lineages (not assigned to known insect orders) were detected only in August and October in the COI dataset.

The heat map revealed genus- and species-level temporal capture patterns of airborne insect taxa for both markers (Fig. 5). Most insect species from the ten orders were collected during restricted seasons. For instance, Reticulitermes speratus kyushuensis (Blattodea) was captured in May with both markers. Valenzuela atricornis (Psocoptera) and Scymnus trisulicus (Coleoptera) appeared in the COI dataset in June and September, respectively. In contrast, some species were collected over extended periods. For instance, Aphis spp. and Rhopalosiphum rufiabdominalis (Aphididae) were captured from April to October in the 16S and COI datasets, respectively.

Endemic species, invasive species, and agricultural pest populations

Most COI-derived species were endemic. Of the 98 COI-derived species, 33 (33.6%) had not been previously recorded in South Korea (NIBR; Table S1). Similarly, most 16S-derived species were endemic, with five species confirmed as unrecorded in South Korea (Table S1). Two species, Hydrotaea spinigera and Rhopalosiphum rufiabdominalis, were detected by both markers. The majority of unrecorded species have been reported in neighboring countries such as China, Taiwan, and Japan. To date, none of the 37 unrecorded species has been listed as a high-risk quarantine insect pest by the Animal and Plant Quarantine Agency of South Korea. Of these, two species (Rhopalosiphum rufiabdominalis and Stephanitis chinensis) are recognized as forest pests40,41.

Discussion

Comparison between COI and 16S results

The taxonomic coverage of the two markers was broadly comparable from the genus to order levels, with both showing assignment rates that improved consistently toward higher ranks. This pattern reflects the greater completeness of the reference databases at broader taxonomic levels. However, only approximately 40–45% of ASVs could be confidently assigned to known insect genera, highlighting the limited representation of airborne insect diversity in current reference libraries. At the species level, taxonomic resolution differed markedly between the two markers. COI resolved substantially more taxa and covered a broader range of insect groups than 16S. The restricted resolution of 16S likely reflects the limited availability of publicly available 16S reference sequences for insects compared to the more comprehensive COI barcode databases42. Nevertheless, despite strong support for COI, nearly half of the ASVs recovered here remained unresolved at the species level, highlighting the continued need to expand reference datasets to improve species-level assignments.

In terms of raw diversity, 16S recovered a slightly higher number of insect ASVs (283) than COI (220). This may suggest that 16S rRNA captures broader amplicon diversity; however, part of this increase could also reflect technical factors inherent to metabarcoding, such as residual sequencing artefacts, chimeric sequences, or amplification of mitochondrial pseudogenes, which may inflate ASV counts despite stringent filtering. The recovered diversity from 16S was largely confined to a limited number of insect orders (e.g., Hemiptera in this study). Developing in-house reference barcoding libraries, particularly for airborne migratory insects, which are far less represented than aquatic insect taxa, is essential to realize the full resolving power of this marker.

Despite these limitations, our results demonstrated both the constraints of single-locus approaches and the added value of a multilocus strategy for monitoring migratory insect populations. By complementing each other, COI and 16S rRNA improved detection capacity and expanded taxonomic coverage. For example, 16S uniquely detected 16 species, whereas COI uniquely identified 82 species, indicating that combining markers provides a more comprehensive picture of airborne insect diversity than either marker alone.

Temporal dynamics of airborne insect populations

A sudden increase in the number of novel insect species was observed in the 16S rRNA dataset from late July to early September, a pattern that was not mirrored in the COI dataset. Many of these novel 16S ASVs were assigned to unknown lineages within Hemiptera. The reasons for this sudden increase remain unclear; however, possible explanations include seasonal events and migratory fluxes linked to climatic conditions.

Across the sampling season, three distinct phases of temporal turnover in airborne insect communities were consistently identified by both markers and across both presence–absence and abundance datasets. Clear separations in insect community composition occurred during: (1) early May to early July, (2) late July to early September, and (3) late September to late October. These phases suggest strong temporal structuring of airborne insect assemblages, likely driven by seasonal life cycle dynamics, migration timing, and host plant phenology.

Phases 1 and 3 were dominated by aphid lineages, reflecting their biological characteristics. Aphids (Hemiptera: Aphididae) typically shift host plants seasonally during their life cycle43,44. They use woody plants as primary hosts for sexual reproduction, and herbaceous plants as secondary hosts for asexual reproduction45,46. Typically, winged adults known as “spring migrants” disperse to and colonize secondary host plants, where they remain through the summer, producing both alate and apterous females (Virginoparae). Shorter day lengths induce the production of “autumn migrants,” winged morphs that return to the primary host plants. The aphid lineages observed in Phases 1 and 3 corresponded to spring and autumn migrants, respectively.

Endemic species, migratory species, and potential agricultural pests

Using an aerial trap combined with DNA metabarcoding, we detected 12 insect orders: Hymenoptera, Hemiptera, Diptera, Dermaptera, Psocoptera, Lepidoptera, Orthoptera, Coleoptera, Neuroptera, Strepsiptera, Trichoptera, and Blattodea, including 33 species not previously recorded in South Korea. Seasonal patterns in species richness and diversity were also observed. In this study, the insect orders Megaloptera and Raphidioptera were not detected in this study. Although these are winged insects, they are generally weak or infrequent fliers with strong habitat specificity. Raphidiopterans are typically associated with vegetation, whereas Megalopterans are linked to aquatic environments47. Their absence is therefore likely attributable to habitat mismatch at the port sampling site. In addition, this pattern may indicate a limited contribution of insects associated with surrounding vegetation or aquatic habitats to the assemblage collected at the port during the sampling period.

Using DNA metabarcoding, Svenningsen et al. (2021) detected 15 orders of flying insects, including 174 known and 319 previously unrecorded species in Denmark48. The authors also reported a high proportion of Diptera in their taxonomic composition. In contrast, our study detected relatively higher proportions of Hemiptera and Diptera. The 33 previously unrecorded species identified in this study belonged to ten orders, including Coleoptera, Dermaptera, Diptera, Hemiptera, Hymenoptera, Lepidoptera, Neuroptera, Orthoptera, Psocoptera, and Strepsiptera.

This study revealed a diverse assemblage of pest species, including previously unrecorded taxa, with potential pathways for their introduction into South Korea. Two primary entry mechanisms were considered: maritime transport via ships, and aerial dispersal through prevailing air currents. Historically, port areas have had restricted access, resulting in limited surveys of insect fauna. Our findings indicate a substantial risk of introducing unrecorded species as well as agricultural and forest pests currently distributed in neighboring countries (China, Japan, and Taiwan) via port facilities. These results underscore the need for long-term biosecurity monitoring and surveillance programs to detect and mitigate potential invasions by non-native insect species.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that metabarcoding can serve as a useful method for assessing insect diversity and monitoring the temporal dynamics of airborne insects, particularly when conventional morphological surveys are constrained by wind-induced specimen damage and the need for taxonomic expertise. Using two genetic markers (COI and 16S), we detected 113 small-bodied insect species, including 33 previously unrecorded species. The unrecorded taxa included several economically important species, such as agricultural pests (e.g., Rhopalosiphum rufiabdominalis), highlighting the potential applicability of this method for airborne insect surveillance. The combined use of two loci (COI and 16S) proved complementary, broadening taxonomic coverage and consistently capturing temporal community changes that followed three distinct phases. However, because this study was conducted at a single site with no temporal replication, the observed patterns should be interpreted as site-specific and exploratory rather than broadly generalizable. In addition, this study is based on aerially collected insect specimens rather than environmental DNA, and therefore reflects captured individuals rather than passive airborne DNA signals.. Broader spatial sampling across multiple sites will be necessary to fully evaluate insect diversity and the effectiveness of this approach at larger geographic scales. In this context, the integration of an in-house reference DNA barcoding database, particularly one enriched with endemic species, may further improve the efficiency and resolution of airborne insect monitoring.
