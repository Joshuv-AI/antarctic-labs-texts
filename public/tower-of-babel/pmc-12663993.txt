A periplasmic zinc capture protein enhances the resistance of Neisseria gonorrhoeae to nutritional immunity
Authors: Ian K. Liyayi, Aloke Kumar Bera, Yasiru R. Perera, Nourin Ferdausi, Indu Bhatia, Nicholas Noinaj, Walter J. Chazin, Cynthia Nau Cornelissen, Alison K. Criss
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificancePathogenic bacteria compete with their hosts for essential metals by expressing gene products that enhance growth in metal-limited conditions, but many remain uncharacterized. Zcp from Neisseria gonorrhoeae is a member of the DUF4198 protein family that is widely distributed among Gram-negative bacteria. In the first biophysical and functional characterization of a DUF4198 protein, we report Zcp is a zinc-binding periplasmic homodimer that is highly induced when zinc is limited. Zcp enhances growth and infectivity of N. gonorrhoeae facing zinc limitation. Unlike the substrate-binding import protein ZnuA, Zcp is dispensable for zinc uptake, instead interacting with extracytoplasmic clients and enhancing envelope integrity. This work defines DUF4198 proteins as metal-buffering substrate-binding proteins that help subvert host-imposed nutritional immunity.

Zinc (Zn) and other transition metals are essential for microbial physiology. Naturally occurring as a redox-inert divalent cation, Zn is estimated to metalate ~5% of the bacterial proteome (1). Zn is a critical cofactor for enzymes found across all compartments of the bacterial cell, which function in major pathways including metabolism, protein synthesis, and proliferation. Bacteria have evolved adaptive systems to ensure the bioavailability of Zn while preventing excessive accumulation, which can cause mismetalation of proteins and subsequent cytotoxicity.

During infection, vertebrate hosts exploit the need for essential metals like Zn in a process known as nutritional immunity. Zn-sequestering S100 family proteins such as calprotectin (S100A8/A9) and psoriasin (S100A7) are enriched at sites of infection and inhibit microbial growth (2). However, pathogens such as Neisseria gonorrhoeae (Gc) have evolved mechanisms to overcome host-mediated Zn restriction. Remarkably, Gc produces the TonB-dependent transporters (TdTs) TdfH and TdfJ, which bind and extract Zn from human calprotectin and psoriasin, respectively (3, 4). TdTs show strong selectivity for human protein ligands and not those from other animals (5). The periplasmic substrate binding protein (SBP) ZnuA (also called MntC) then shuttles Zn to the inner membrane ZnuB permease that is coupled to the ZnuC cytoplasmic ATPase, for Zn import into the cytoplasm. The Zn uptake regulator (Zur) controls the transcription of these and other genes that respond to Zn restriction (5).

In metal-limited conditions, pathogenic bacteria produce periplasmic accessory proteins that enhance metal acquisition and maintain cell physiology. For instance, Escherichia coli and Salmonella enterica produce ZinT, a Zur-regulated Zn-SBP that stably complexes with ZnuA to aid in Zn import (6). In addition to ZnuABC, Paracoccus denitrificans produces a secondary ABC transporter AztCBA with its associated auxiliary Zn-SBP AztD. Similarly, Pseudomonas aeruginosa produces three additional Zn-uptake systems (7–9). Beyond acquisition systems, the periplasm also contains Zur-regulated metalloenzymes which in Acinetobacter baumanii and Vibrio cholerae have been shown to remodel peptidoglycan to bolster cell envelope integrity in low Zn (10, 11). How pathogens ensure appropriate metalation of Zn-requiring periplasmic metalloproteins while when Zn is limiting is not clear. In the case of copper (Cu), there are dedicated periplasmic chaperones that buffer and mobilize Cu to Cu-requiring proteins; however, no such metallochaperones have been defined for periplasmic Zn distribution (12, 13).

One of the most upregulated genes in the Gc response to Zn limitation is ngo1049, which encodes a predicted periplasmic protein of the domain of unknown function DUF4198 (Pfam10670/ IPR019613) family. DUF4198 proteins are found throughout Gram-negative bacteria. They were formerly misannotated as CbiM and NikM transporters, although the misannotation is maintained in some genomic databases (14, 15). Transcriptional studies have linked DUF4198 family members to the Zn starvation response in Gc and N. meningitidis and the iron starvation response in other Gram-negative bacteria, yet the functions of DUF4198 proteins are unknown (16–21). Here, we show the product of the ngo1049 locus, which we name Zcp (Zn capture protein), is produced in Zn-limited Gc in a Zur-dependent manner. The crystal structure of Zn-bound Zcp reveals tetrahedral coordination of one Zn ion in each subunit. We show Zcp is important for optimal Gc growth and infection in Zn limiting conditions. Coimmunoprecipitation analyses show that Zcp associates with machinery critical for bacterial envelope assembly and maintenance. Metal-starved ∆zcp Gc displays increased sensitivity to antibiotics and hydrogen peroxide. We propose that periplasmic DUF4198 family members buffer metals like zinc to enhance bacterial resistance to host-imposed nutritional immunity.

Results

Zcp Is a Zn and Zur-Regulated Periplasmic Protein.

zcp is predicted to be monocistronic, with a putative Zur box located upstream of the coding region (Fig. 1A). zcp transcripts were quantified from WT and Δzur Gc that were cultured in metal-rich gonococcal media (GCBL) with N,N,N′,N′- tetrakis-(2-pyridylmethyl)ethylenediamine (TPEN), a chelator with high affinity for Zn, alone or with Zn addition. In WT Gc, zcp was induced in the presence of TPEN and repressed upon addition of Zn (Fig. 1B). zcp transcript abundance was elevated in Δzur Gc and unaffected by Zn concentration (Fig. 1B). By western blot, Zcp was produced by Gc cultured in metal-depleted Chelex-treated defined media (CDM) and repressed in Gc grown in GCBL (Fig. 1C). In CDM, addition of Zn fully repressed Zcp production, while Cu minimally reduced Zcp, and Fe, Mn, and Co addition had no effect (Fig. 1D). Zcp was produced by Δzur Gc regardless of Zn concentration (Fig. 1E). These data demonstrate that Zcp is Zur-regulated and produced when Zn is limiting.

Zcp contains a cleavable N-terminal Sec-dependent signal sequence, targeting the protein for extracytoplasmic secretion (SignalP-6.0) (22). By immunofluorescence in permeabilized Gc, Zcp appeared as rings that surrounded the DAPI-positive cytoplasm (Fig. 1F). Zcp antibody did not react with unpermeabilized bacteria, with an antilipooligosaccharide antibody serving as positive control. Thus, Zcp is neither surface exposed nor cytoplasmic. Zcp partitioned to the soluble fraction of sonicated bacteria, defined by the presence of the cytoplasmic protein Zwf (Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase) and the absence of outer membrane TdfH (Fig. 1G). Zcp was also detected in naturally elaborated outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) along with the outer membrane protein TdfJ, but not Zwf (Fig. 1H). We conclude that Zcp is a soluble periplasmic protein that is produced by Gc in response to Zn limitation.

Zcp Is a Homodimer and Binds Zn.

The Zcp open reading frame (ORF) lacking the signal sequence was cloned, expressed, purified, and crystallized using hanging drop vapor diffusion. The crystal structure of Zcp was determined to 1.94 Å resolution (Table 1) (23–29). Two molecules of Zcp were observed in the asymmetric unit related by twofold symmetry, with an interfacial buried surface area of ~1,100 Å2 (Fig. 2 A and B). Each subunit has a 3-layer β-sandwich-like fold with two small helices at the periphery of the structure. A single disulfide bond is formed between C127 and C250 to staple together the central β-sheet. The primary interface is mediated by an elongated beta interaction consisting of residues 211 to 220, with several elongated loops, including residues 42 to 54 and 196 to 210. The largest loop, which consists of residues 108 to 125, appears to be quite flexible since it was not clearly resolved in all structures (Fig. 2 A and B).

To identify the metal binding sites in Zcp, the crystals were buffer-exchanged to remove ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and soaked in metal-containing solutions. The only metals to yield observable density in Zcp were Zn, Cu, Co, and Ni; no density was observed for soaks using Mn, Fe, Ca, or Mg. For the Zn-bound structure, each subunit coordinated a single Zn ion, using residues H29, H31, and H142 and one water molecule (Fig. 2 B and C). The identity of the Zn was verified by anomalous difference maps from data collected at a wavelength of 1.2825 Å. The histidine triad coordinated Cu, Co, and Ni in corresponding structures. No other metal density was detected in Zcp, indicating only a single metal binding site for each subunit.

Consistent with the crystallographic data, Zcp was only detected as a dimer by dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements (Fig. 2D). Size-exclusion chromatography-small-angle X-ray scattering (SEC-SAXS) analysis revealed a radius of gyration (Rg) of 27.6 Å, a predicted molecular weight of 57.1 kDa, and a Dmax of 84 Å, all consistent with a Zcp dimer (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and D). The experimental SAXS scattering curve was compared to calculated scattering curves from possible models of Zcp, yielding χ2 values of 36.0 (subunit) and 1.6 (dimer) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B). Without EDTA, Zcp produced a small but observable shift in the scattering profile around q = 0.01 and q = 0.1, compared to Zcp in the presence of EDTA (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). This was also reflected in broadening of the eluted peak from the SEC column, increases in the molecular weight and Dmax values, and an increase in the tendency to aggregate, reflected in the value of Rg (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). A sample in the presence of Zn was not analyzed due to an increased propensity for aggregation and precipitation. Thus, Zcp forms a dimer in solution independent of metal binding, which is consistent with the apo- and Zn-soaked crystal structures.

The affinity of Zcp for Zn was measured by recording the intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence of Zcp titrated with Zn sulfate (ZnSO4), resulting in a dissociation constant of 0.31 ± 0.04 µM (Fig. 2E). Additionally, Apo-Zcp and Zn-bound Zcp demonstrated similar thermostability with melting temperatures of 43.1 °C and 44.3 °C, respectively (Fig. 2F). Taken together, these data show Zcp is a homodimer with a single Zn binding site of submicromolar affinity per subunit.

Zcp Is Conserved across Pathogenic and Commensal Neisseria and Present across a Variety of Gram-Negative Bacteria.

The DUF4198 protein family contains over 9,000 members that are widely distributed across phylogenetically diverse bacterial species from distinct environments. We used the genomic enzyme similarity tool (EFI-EST) to contextualize Zcp in the DUF4198 family (30). The DUF4198 sequence similarity network (SSN) was segregated into clusters based on ~60% sequence identity, and sequences with >80% identity were consolidated into metanodes (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 and Dataset S1) (31).

Both pathogenic and commensal Neisseria spp. were found to harbor Zcp homologs (Dataset S1). Lysates from four commensal Neisseria in our collection produced a protein with reactivity with anti-Zcp antibody when grown with TPEN (Fig. 3A), suggesting pathogenic and commensal Neisseria produce Zcp in response to zinc limitation.

Zcp and its homologs in Neisseria spp. localized to two clusters, which also contained DUF4198 sequences from other species of Gram-negative bacteria, including Acinetobacter, Burkholderia, Eikenella, Haemophilus, Kingella, Pasteurella, Moraxella, and Salmonella (SI Appendix, Fig. S2, Fig. 3 B and C, and Datasets S1 and S2). Zcp and its homologs in both clusters readily aligned by primary sequence (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Sequences in Cluster 1 were ~270 residues in length and contained the metal-binding histidine residues around positions 29 to 31 and 142, which are absent in cluster 2 orthologs (Fig. 3 B, C, F, and I). Alpha Fold-predicted structures of Cluster 1 and 2 orthologs adopted the same characteristic 3-layer β-sheet-like fold, suggesting functional similarity (Fig. 3 F and I). The cysteine residues C127 and C250 were also conserved in both clusters (Fig. 3 B and C). In contrast, Cluster 2 proteins were on average larger, ~284 residues, with additional residues localized to the C-terminus (Fig. 3C). The Cluster 2 C-terminal extension, comprised primarily of histidines, forms an extended disordered tail (Fig. 3 C and I). Cluster 2 proteins lack the metal-coordinating histidines found in Zcp/Cluster 1. Regions upstream of Cluster 1 orthologs were more likely to contain palindromic motifs, specifically with similarity to the Neisseria consensus Zur box TGTTATAGCATAACA, compared with Cluster 2 orthologs (Fig. 3 D, E, G, and H) (17). We conclude Zcp is a member of a conserved family of metal-responsive proteins found in Gram-negative bacteria.

Zcp Is Important for N. gonorrhoeae Growth in Low Zn.

To examine the requirement for Zcp in Gc adaptation to Zn limitation, a null zcp mutant of Gc was constructed. WT and Δzcp Gc were cultured in keratinocyte serum-free medium (KSFM) containing murine calprotectin (mCP), which restricts Gc growth in a manner dependent on Zn, but not Mn or Cu (16) (Fig. 4A). WT and ∆zcp Gc grew with identical kinetics in medium without mCP. In the presence of mCP, ∆zcp Gc exhibited a statistically significant growth defect compared to the WT; both were rescued equivalently by addition of Zn. Complementation of Zcp using an anhydrotetracycline (aTc)-inducible complement significantly enhanced growth of ∆zcp Gc, to levels comparable to mCP-restricted, WT Gc (Fig. 4B). In contrast, complementing ∆zcp Gc with a mutated zcp where histidines H29, H31, and H142 were substituted to alanines (zcp-3A) was unable to rescue the growth of the Δzcp mutant in mCP-containing medium; the zcp-3A complement with or without aTc induction and Δzcp Gc were similarly sensitive to mCP (Fig. 4 A and C). By X-ray crystallography, metal binding by Zcp-3A was disrupted, but the overall protein structure was unaffected (SI Appendix, Fig. S4) (25). Thus, Zcp is important for Gc to resist Zn limitation in a manner dependent on its ability to bind Zn.

We compared the zcp mutant to ∆znuA Gc, which has a severe growth defect without Zn supplementation (32). The ∆znuA mutant appeared Zn starved, producing Zcp even when grown with 10-fold the Zn concentration necessary to repress Zcp production in WT Gc (Figs. 1 and 4D). Unlike ∆zcp Gc, ∆znuA Gc was unable to grow without Zn. Furthermore, Zcp overexpression did not overcome the growth defect of ∆znuA Gc (Fig. 4 D and E). Zn supplementation rescued growth of ∆znuA Gc, though not on par with WT or ∆zcp bacteria.

To directly test whether Zcp contributes to Zn import by Gc, WT, ∆zcp, and ∆znuA Gc were cultured in CDM or CDM supplemented with 25 µM for ∆znuA, then pulsed with the stable Zn isotope 70Zn. Bacterial metal concentrations were measured by inductively coupled mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), normalized to sulfur content. WT and ∆zcp Gc contained equivalent levels of 70Zn, and both were significantly greater than ∆znuA Gc at matched times (Fig. 4F). Intracellular levels of 66Zn and other transition metals (Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu) were similar across the three strains (Fig. 4G and SI Appendix, Fig. S5). WT and ∆zcp Gc had similar 70Zn:66Zn ratios, significantly greater than ∆znuA bacteria (Fig. 4H). Furthermore, ∆zcp Gc used S100A8/A9 (human CP) and S100A12 (Calgranulin C) as sole Zn sources (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 F–I). Together, these results indicate that unlike ZnuA, Zcp does not contribute to Zn uptake.

Zcp Contributes to N. gonorrhoeae Cell Envelope Integrity in Zn-Limiting Conditions.

Given the moderate affinity of Zcp for Zn (Fig. 2), we hypothesized that Zcp helps to capture and passively allocate Zn to extracytoplasmic Zn-requiring targets when Zn is scarce. To identify putative Zcp interactors, metal-starved Gc expressing Zcp-FLAG were treated with increasing concentrations of the crosslinker disuccinimidyl glutarate (DSG), and coimmunoprecipitation mass spectrometry (co-IP/MS) was performed (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). We identified a total of 40 secreted proteins that immunoprecipitated with Zcp-FLAG (Dataset S2) (31). By gene ontology (GO) analysis, extracytoplasmic Zcp interactors were associated with membrane assembly, cell division, metallopeptidase activity, and transport of lipids, polyamines, and amino acids (Fig. 5A). Notably, neither ZnuA nor ZnuB coprecipitated with Zcp. Ten proteins coprecipitating with Zcp are known metalloproteins, with 5 classified as Zn metalloproteins: BAM complex components BamA and BamE, metalloproteases FtsH and PepN, and an L-cysteine SBP (33–36, 37). These proteins are linked by their involvement in maintaining proper growth, cell division, and cell envelope biogenesis. To evaluate a role for Zcp in Gc integrity, survival of Zn-restricted Gc was measured after exposure to agents targeting the outer membrane and peptidoglycan. ∆zcp Gc exhibited significantly greater sensitivity to polymyxin B and ceftriaxone compared to WT (Fig. 5 B–D). These results suggest Zcp is important for Gc to maintain cell envelope homeostasis in the face of Zn limitation.

Zcp Enhances Gc Survival during Infection.

To place these results in the context of Gc pathogenesis, we examined how Zcp contributes to Gc infection of human epithelial cells and neutrophils. Human End1 endocervical cells were infected with WT and Δzcp Gc in cell culture medium alone, or with mCP to limit Zn, ±Zn add-back. In the presence of mCP, significantly fewer planktonic (released into the culture medium) ∆zcp Gc was recovered compared to WT; growth was rescued to equal levels with Zn supplementation (Fig. 6A). While mCP treatment significantly reduced cell-associated and total CFU, there was no difference between WT and Δzcp Gc, keeping with the observation that cell-associated Gc is more resistant to Zn limitation than planktonic bacteria (Fig. 6 B and C) (16).

Adherent, IL-8 primed primary human neutrophils in CDM were exposed to WT, ∆zcp, and zcpC+ Gc, and CFU were enumerated over time. ∆zcp Gc was recovered at significantly lower numbers from neutrophils after 4 h of infection and was rescued by complementation (Fig. 6D). Zcp did not significantly contribute to Gc survival from neutrophils at earlier times. Activated neutrophils produce H2O2 and other oxidants as antimicrobial species (38). ∆zcp Gc was significantly more sensitive to H2O2 than WT bacteria, in a time- and concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 6E), although zcp is reported to be downregulated by sublethal H2O2 (39). Taken together, we conclude that Zcp enables the optimal growth and survival of Gc facing Zn limitation during infection Table 1.

Discussion

Pathogens must overcome host-mediated zinc restriction for successful infection. Beyond Zn acquisition systems at the outer and inner membranes, the coordinated response to Zn restriction in Gram-negative bacteria comprises metallochaperones, metalloenzymes, and Zn-independent paralogous proteins to support critical cellular processes (16). Here, we identify Zcp, a DUF4198 protein, as a Zn-SBP that is produced by Zn-limited Gc in a Zur-dependent manner. Zcp localizes to the periplasm, but unlike ZnuA which mediates Zn uptake, Zcp associates with proteins required for maintaining cell envelope integrity. Zcp is required in Gc for maximal growth and infectivity in low Zn. We propose Zcp functions to capture Zn in the periplasm, keeping Zn bioavailable for Zn-requiring extracytoplasmic proteins to maintain envelope homeostasis.

Analysis of Zcp provides insight into the biological significance of the DUF4198 protein family. zcp and nmb1475, its homolog in N. meningitidis, are upregulated in Zn-limited bacteria, repressed by addition of Zn and not other metals, have perfect Zur boxes upstream of their translational start sites, and are derepressed in the absence of Zur (16, 17). Our promoter analysis revealed putative Zur box motifs in the ORFs of DUF4198 proteins closely related to Zcp, and commensal Neisseria produce Zcp homologs when Zn is limiting. These results suggest Zcp orthologs in Neisseria contribute to Zn starvation responses. DUF4198 sequences from the pathogenic Neisseria, along with some commensal Neisseria and other mucosa-associated Gram-negative bacteria, formed a cluster by sequence similarity. Proteins in this cluster have the three His residues that in Zcp are required for Zn binding and Gc growth in Zn-limited conditions. In contrast, Cluster 2 proteins uniquely feature an extended, histidine-rich C-terminal tail found near the region comprising the metal binding pocket of Zcp. We speculate that Cluster 2 proteins are metalloproteins with distinct metal-binding properties from Zcp and Cluster 1 orthologs, a subject for future study.

The lack of functional and biochemical interaction between Zcp and ZnuA suggests that these metalloproteins serve divergent roles in the periplasm. ZnuA is a Cluster A-I SBP (PFam 01297) with only 22% identity to Zcp. While both ZnuA and Zcp contribute to Gc growth in Zn-limited conditions, in conditions that for WT Gc are Zn-replete, Gc lacking zcp does not have a growth defect while ∆znuA Gc is severely attenuated. Along with ICP-MS for 70Zn uptake, these findings suggest Zcp is dispensable for Zn import, while ZnuA is required. Interestingly, Gc znuA is repressed by Zn and Mn, and ZnuA binds Zn and Mn with nearly equal affinity (0.104 µM and 0.100 µM respectively) (32). In contrast, we show zcp expression is regulated by Zn but not Mn, and while Zcp has an affinity for Zn (0.312 µM) that is threefold lower than ZnuA, Mn could not be soaked into crystals of Zcp. Given Zcp does not functionally interact with ZnuA for Zn import, we instead posit that Zcp enhances resistance to Zn starvation by buffering periplasmic Zn to support metalation of Zn-requiring periplasmic proteins. This is consistent with the notion that retention of periplasmic Zn enables the metalation of secreted Zn metalloproteins when Zn is limited (40). Moreover, sequestration of periplasmic Zn by Zcp may enable ZnuA to more effectively import Mn. As precedence for this concept, in Synechocystis, the FutA2 SBP sequesters periplasmic Fe, promoting the cytoplasmic uptake of Cu (41). Notably, Gc accumulates Mn as an antioxidant (42). We found Gc lacking Zcp was more sensitive to H2O2, although we did not measure any difference in intracellular Mn, suggesting additional Zcp targets promote resistance to oxidative stress.

Zcp coprecipitated with Zn-binding proteins important for cell envelope maintenance, and Gc lacking Zcp displayed greater sensitivity to cell envelope–disrupting agents. Zcp-associated proteins included subunits of the BAM complex, which incorporates beta-barrel proteins such as the Zn pirating receptors TdfH and TdfJ into the outer membrane, and metalloenzymes implicated in cell envelope quality control, cell division, and cellular stress responses (35, 36, 43). By maintaining bioavailable periplasmic Zn, Zcp may support appropriate metalation of Zn metalloproteins to sustain cell physiology. Its role may be akin to the periplasmic Cu chaperone AccA, which provides Cu to the outer membrane nitrite reductase AniA that is required for anaerobic growth of pathogenic Neisseria spp. (13). Also, Zn sequestration by Zcp may prevent mismetalation of non-Zn binding proteins. Given the Zn-coordinating histidines of Zcp are on solvent-exposed loops on the bottom face of the structure, this interface may enable a transfer of Zn from Zcp to client proteins important for cell envelope maintenance. Future studies will evaluate the structural features and consequences of interactions between Zcp and its putative clients.

Bioavailable Zn is limited within host microenvironments to limit bacterial pathogenesis, in part by the release of Zn-sequestering nutritional immunity proteins like CP and S100A7. Here, we found that Zcp enhanced Gc survival in association with epithelial cells and neutrophils. Interestingly, loss of zcp had a greater impact on the bacteria released from the surface of endocervical cells (planktonic) than those that remained attached. This finding is consistent with our previous results showing that adherent Gc exhibit increased induction of zcp, znuA, and other Zn- and Zur-responsive genes, allowing it to better resist Zn limitation (16). Similarly, the zcp homolog in N. meningitidis is upregulated in biofilms (44). Neutrophils deploy an array of antimicrobial proteins, including CP, which are released into phagosomes and extracellularly, with some in neutrophil extracellular traps (38). However, Gc has a variety of ways to resist clearance by neutrophils (38), including production of Zcp as shown here. Beyond overcoming metal limitation, Zcp may help Gc maintain integrity in the face of stressors encountered at inflamed mucosal surfaces, like H2O2 and antimicrobial peptides. Like its meningococcal homolog, Zcp is released in OMVs, which also contain ZnuA and TdTs (45). One intriguing possibility is that Zcp-laden OMVs help Gc acquire Zn in metal-limited environments, whether imposed by the human host or other bacteria Gc encounters during infection.

In vitro Zcp was able to coordinate Co, Ni, and Cu, but these metals are in low abundance in the niches Gc occupies, implicating Zcp predominantly in Zn homeostasis in Gc. In other bacteria, DUF4198 family members may contribute to homeostasis of other transition metals. For instance, DUF4198 proteins have been transcriptionally linked to Fe limitation responses in Avibacterium, Alteromonas, and Caulobacter spp, although their role in resisting Fe limitation is undefined (18–21). Given there are >9,000 DUF4198 proteins present in taxonomically diverse Gram-negative bacteria, we anticipate the specific metal(s) binding properties of proteins in this family are determined by each species’ metal requirements, metal-dependent regulation of the DUF4198-encoding gene, and niche-dependent metal availability. Future studies of this family will reveal how DUF4198-containing proteins broadly contribute to transition metal homeostasis in the periplasm of Gram-negative bacteria.

Materials and Methods

Gc FA1090 was the parental strain used to generate zcp, znuA, and zcp complemented bacteria, which were grown in Chelex-treated chemically defined medium and gonococcal base agar or liquid. Metals were restricted by adding TPEN, or mCP (prepared as described in ref. 46). Human S100A8/A9 and S100A12 were prepared as described in ref. 47. Anti-Zcp antisera was generated by Biosynth (Gardner, MA). Zcp was expressed in BL21(DE3) cells and purified via affinity and size exclusion chromatography. The Zcp structure was resolved using X-ray crystallography. Metal affinity was measured using intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence. Bioinformatic analyses were performed using EFI-EST, MEME Suite, and AlphaFold. 70Zn uptake was measured using ICP-MS. Co-IP was performed using Gc expressing Zcp-FLAG, and protein analysis was conducted at the Taplin Mass Spectrometry Facility. Infection of endocervical cells and primary human neutrophils was conducted as described in refs. 16 and 48. Comprehensive details on experimental procedures are provided in SI Appendix.
