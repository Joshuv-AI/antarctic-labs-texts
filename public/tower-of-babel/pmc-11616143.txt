Successful retrieval of a broken pituitary rongeur tip from the lumbar intervertebral disc space using a magnetic probe: illustrative case
Authors: Luis Alberto Navarro-Aceves, Luis Alberto Navarro-Orozco, Luis Miguel Rosales-Olivarez, Carla L. Garcia Ramos, Irving O. Estevez, Barón Zárate-Kalfópulos
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDUnexpected events such as instrument breakage during a routine lumbar microdiscectomy can pose a risk for nerve root injury, durotomy, infection, and anterior displacement into the retroperitoneal space. Removal of broken instruments requires planning, patience, and sometimes subsequent surgical procedures. Here, the authors explore the effectiveness of a magnetic probe for the retrieval of broken metallic (magnetic) pieces.OBSERVATIONSA 47-year-old man with right leg radiculopathy due to a right L5–S1 disc extrusion had undergone a lumbar microdiscectomy that was complicated by the pituitary rongeur tip breaking in the L5–S1 disc space. The rongeur tip was successfully retrieved by widening the entry access without damaging the adjoining facet and utilizing a blunt nerve hook, a probe dissector, forceps tips, curette heads, and a magnetic probe.LESSONSThe use of a magnetic probe provides a safe and effective method for retrieving metallic fragments during lumbar disc surgery. This case highlights the importance of considering alternative techniques such as magnetic retrieval in the management of intraoperative instrument breakage. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE24377

The breakage of surgical instruments has a reported incidence of 0.18%–0.35% in all orthopedic surgery.1, 2 Microdiscectomy is a common surgical procedure employed in the management of symptomatic lumbar disc herniation. However, despite its widespread use and generally safe and effective outcomes, microdiscectomy has potential complications such as infection, nerve injury, dural tear, persistent pain, the need for revision surgery,3 and instrument breakage. The reported incidence of surgical errors, which includes surgical instrument breakage, varies from 3.1% for open microdiscectomy to 1% for microendoscopic discectomy.4, 5 Intraoperative incidents such as inadvertent instrument trauma or excessive force application can lead to the fracture of the pituitary rongeur tip. The consequences of an instrument breakage can extend beyond the current surgical setting with the potential need for additional interventions for fragment retrieval. Moreover, the presence of a foreign body within the surgical site introduces the risk of tissue irritation, infection, and neural injury, compromising the overall success and safety of the procedure.

A methodologically safe and efficient way of removing such retained pieces is required to avoid anterior displacement, which can lead to bleeding, embolization, or other retroperitoneal insults.6–9 Here, we present a case of successful retrieval of a broken rongeur tip from the intervertebral space using a medical magnet.

Illustrative Case

A 47-year-old male presented with a 6-week history of low-back pain and right lower-limb radiculopathy. On physical examination, he had decreased muscular strength in the L5 myotome (4/5) and numbness in the L5–S1 dermatomes. He received two steroid injections and physical therapy without improvement. Magnetic resonance imaging of the lumbar spine revealed a right L5–S1 disc extrusion causing foraminal stenosis.

The patient was taken to the operating room at an outside hospital for a planned right-sided L5–S1 microdiscectomy. The extruded disc fragment was identified and removed using a 2-mm pituitary rongeur; however, during this maneuver, the pituitary rongeur tip broke within the L5–S1 disc space and could not be removed despite the use of fluoroscopic and surgical microscope guidance. The patient was then transferred to our center 3 days later.

Radiography showed the broken pituitary rongeur tip located far lateral in the right side of the disc space (Fig. 1). The extreme far lateral location of the pituitary rongeur tip was determined more precisely on axial and reconstructed sagittal and coronal computed tomography (Fig. 2). Lumbar revision surgery was performed 3 days later; the original incision was reopened, and access to the L5–S1 disc space was obtained through a right transforaminal route. The position of the broken fragment was identified with the help of an image intensifier on both the anteroposterior and lateral views, where it was found lying close to the right L5 neural foramen.

Whereas conventional retrieval of lost parts requires both the location of the fragment using fluoroscopy and direct visualization, the broken pituitary rongeur tip was identified under fluoroscopy using discectomy forceps and a blunt nerve hook (Fig. 3). Subsequently, the tip was grasped and withdrawn using a magnetic probe under fluoroscopic guidance (Video 1), avoiding a prolonged manipulation time, additional tissue dissection, or tissue damage. The patient’s postoperative course was uneventful, with complete resolution of symptoms and total recovery of muscular strength. After 3 months of follow-up, the patient reported a visual analog scale pain score of 1, which was reduced from a preoperative score of 7 (visual analog scale scores range from 0 [no pain] to 10 [severe pain]). Preoperative functional disability, measured using the Oswestry Disability Index, improved from 48% to 10%. The preoperative Roland-Morris Disability Questionnaire score of 12 improved to 1 after surgery. The patient made an excellent recovery with complete pain relief.

The probe consisted of a Teflon-coated flexible rod, measuring 300 mm in length and 2 mm in diameter, with a 3-mm long neodymium magnetic tip, and the handle was assembled by the biomedical engineering staff at our institution.

Discussion

The breakage of surgical instruments during discectomy has already been described as a procedure risk; however, since few case reports are found in the literature, no clear guidance exists for the management of this complication10 (Fig. 4). Different alternatives have been reported for the management of broken instruments during discectomy such as robot-assisted removal of a broken scalpel blade from the abdominal cavity and viscera11 or the use of arthroscopic assistance to remove a broken knife blade from the intervertebral space.12 FIG. 4.The broken pituitary rongeur and its detached tip.

In some situations, it is difficult to retrieve a broken instrument from the intervertebral space, and if the broken fragment cannot be grasped in the initial attempts, repeated manipulation can push the broken part anteriorly, allowing it to migrate into the retroperitoneal space. Reports have shown that this can result in the need for a second surgery to avoid catastrophic consequences such as a main vessel injury.12

There is no established approach for the removal of broken instruments in spine surgery, and the question remains whether fragments can be left in place as an option for asymptomatic foreign bodies without potential risks. Along these lines, Menger et al. describe a potential pathway for their safe retrieval using a magnetic probe.13 Alternatively, an approach could be designed where the removal of the foreign body is planned as a separate, second-stage intervention, requiring an additional procedure instead of completing the task during the initial operation. Similar to our case, a semiflexible magnetic probe for the retrieval of metallic fragments out of the abdominal cavity during laparoscopic procedures has been described.14 The use of a magnetic tool represents a simple, economical, safe, and effective method for retrieving a broken fragment. Moreover, it may prevent repeated manipulation, which could contribute to displacing the broken fragment anteriorly and into the retroperitoneal space.

However, for a magnetic probe to be useful, the fragment to be removed must be magnetic. The magnetic properties of medical-grade stainless steel depend on its specific composition, with austenitic grades being nonmagnetic and ferritic or martensitic grades being magnetic.15 Magnetic steel is considered unsuitable for implantable devices; however, it is preferred for surgical equipment due to its excellent wear resistance.

Furthermore, as the use of minimally invasive surgical (MIS) techniques continues to increase, including endoscopy, laparoscopy, and robotic surgery, incisions and approach corridors continue to decrease in size, limiting the surgeon’s ability to depend on direct visualization. Using fluoroscopy to position at a close distance a flexible soft rod with a magnetic tip that can attract the broken fragments without the need for direct visual guidance and while minimizing the risk of tissue damage could be of great benefit. In addition, magnetic surgical devices have augmented existing MIS techniques and instruments. Currently, magnetic surgical devices are categorized based on their core functions: guiding systems, creation of a new connection, recreation of a physiological function, or utilization of an internal-external paired magnet system.9 In this report, we illustrate how a magnetic probe can be used to stabilize and retrieve a metal fragment from the L5–S1 intervertebral space. Noting the success of this approach, it may prove useful for the retrieval of metallic fragments from damaged instruments used beyond lumbar microdiscectomy, for example, the retrieval of broker burr tips, broken guiding pins, broken drill bits, and fragments from instruments used in the cannulation of vertebral pedicles, as well as a variety of broken probes, curettes, and dissectors commonly used in spine surgery.

Observations

Intraoperative instrument breakage during microdiscectomy represents a challenging situation and requires prompt and effective management to prevent potential complications.

Lessons

The use of medical magnets provides a safe and effective method for retrieving metallic fragments during lumbar disc surgery. This case highlights the importance of considering alternative techniques, such as magnetic retrieval, in the management of intraoperative complications (Fig. 5).
