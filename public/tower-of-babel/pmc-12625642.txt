Immortalized human hair follicle-derived mesenchymal-like stromal cells for the long-term production of scalable Immunomodulatory and regenerative secretome
Authors: Maria Rossello-Gelabert, Kevin Las Heras, Ainhoa Gonzalez-Pujana, Manoli Igartua, Edorta Santos-Vizcaino, Rosa Maria Hernandez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) exert therapeutic effects primarily through their secretome, rich in bioactive factors with immunomodulatory and regenerative properties. However, clinical application of MSC-derived secretome is hindered by donor variability, limited expansion, and replicative senescence. To address these issues, we developed immortalized human hair follicle-derived mesenchymal-like stromal cells (iHF-MSCs) as a consistent and scalable source of therapeutic secretome.MethodsHF-MSCs were isolated using a minimally invasive procedure and immortalized with lentiviral SV40 large T antigen. From 576 single-cell clones, C18 and C26 were selected based on proliferation capacity, absence of senescence, and retention of a mesenchymal-like phenotype. Characterization included RT-PCR, flow cytometry for surface markers, trilineage differentiation, and CD56 expression analysis. For immunomodulatory evaluation, cells were licensed with IFN-γ and TNF-α, and the resulting conditioned media (licensed CM) were analyzed by ELISA and cytokine arrays. PBMC proliferation and Treg induction were assessed using licensed CM, while unlicensed CM was tested for its regenerative effects on dermal and epidermal cell functions, including wound repair, tube formation assay as an indicator of angiogenic potential, and oxidative stress response.ResultsThe selected clones (C18 and C26) exhibited long-term stability, rapid expansion, and preserved mesenchymal-like phenotype and multipotency. Secretome profiling revealed an enriched composition of immunoregulatory (e.g., Gal-9, IL-1Ra, TSG-6) and pro-regenerative (e.g., VEGF, PDGF-AA, EGF) factors, with enhanced responsiveness to inflammatory licensing. Notably, iHF-MSCs maintained low immunogenicity and demonstrated superior functional performance: inhibition of PBMC proliferation, Treg induction, and promotion of skin cell proliferation, migration, tube formation, and oxidative stress protection.ConclusionThese results position iHF-MSCs as a robust and scalable platform for consistent production of therapeutic secretome, paving the way for innovative, cell-free immunomodulatory and regenerative therapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04775-8.Keywords: Cell-free therapy, Hair follicle, Immortalization, Immunomodulation, Mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC), Regenerative medicine, Secretome

Background

Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) are multipotent cells with well-documented immunomodulatory and regenerative properties [1, 2], largely mediated by their secretome – a mixture of cytokines, growth factors, chemokines and extracellular vesicles (EVs) [3, 4]. Key regenerative factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), insulin-like growth factors (IGF-1 and IGF-2), hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), fibroblast growth factor (FGF), and epidermal growth factor (EGF) support tissue repair [3]. MSCs can polarize toward an MSC2 phenotype, whose secretome is enriched in immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory mediators such as indoleamine 2, 3-dioxygenase (IDO), TNF-α-induced gene/protein 6 (TSG-6), interleukin 1 receptor antagonist (IL-1Ra), prostaglandin-E2 (PGE-2) or galectin-9 (Gal-9) [1, 5, 6]. This polarization is often achieved by exposing MSCs to interferon gamma (IFN-γ) or tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) [5, 7, 8]. IFN-γ activates the JAK/STAT1 pathway, which induces the expression of soluble immunoregulatory mediators such as indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) and prostaglandin E2 (PGE-2) [9]. In parallel, TNF-α stimulates NF-κB signalling [10], which synergistically amplifies STAT1-mediated responses and promotes the secretion of additional anti-inflammatory factors, including IL-1Ra, TSG-6, and galectin-9 [11, 12]. Together, these pathways enhance the MSC2 phenotype and account for the increased immunomodulatory capacity observed after licensing.

Despite promising preclinical and clinical results, translating MSC-based therapies into routine clinical practice remains challenging. Major limitations include graft rejection, low in vivo persistence and potential adverse effects such as inflammation or the risk of thrombosis [2, 7, 8]. Additional hurdles – reproducibility, standardization, traceability, quality control, scalability, and storage – further hinder clinical translation, reinforcing the need for simplified, robust alternatives such as secretome-based therapies [13, 14]. Strong evidence shows that the MSC-derived secretome can reproduce many of the therapeutic effects of the cells themselves [1, 14–16]. Compared with whole-cell administration, secretome-based products offer improved safety, higher efficiency in bioactive factor production, and reduced variability, as their content can be quantified prior to ensure dose standardization and reproducibility [17].

Selecting a suitable cell source is key to obtain an efficient MSC-derived secretome. MSCs can be isolated from multiple human tissues, with bone marrow (BM), adipose tissue (AT) and umbilical cord (UC) being the most common sources [4, 14]. However, these sources are limited by tissue availability and the invasiveness of collection [15, 16], highlighting the need for innovative and accessible alternatives. Hair follicle-derived MSCs (HF-MSCs) offer several advantages: they can be obtained non-invasively, repeatedly, and from donors of any age [18, 19]. HF-MSCs and dermal papilla-derived stem cells (DPCs) both originate within the HF, but differ in location and properties. HF-MSCs are typically isolated from the dermal sheath, a mesenchymal compartment surrounding the follicle that is rich in MSCs with multipotent differentiation potential and high proliferative capacity [20]. In contrast, DPCs reside at the follicle base within the dermal papilla, a specialized mesenchymal niche involved in hair cycling and morphogenesis [21]. While DPCs also possess regenerative properties, their niche-specific role and gene expression profile make HF-MSCs better suited for scalable secretome production and broader regenerative applications [20, 21].

Moreover, while most studies have focused on traditional MSC sources, evidence increasingly supports alternative sources such as the HF [22, 23]. Our previous work demonstrated that HF-MSCs and their EVs possess strong immunoregulatory and regenerative properties [18, 19]. Specifically, HF-MSCs show enhanced immunoevasiveness and can modulate peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), regulatory T cells (Tregs), and macrophages [18]. HF-MSC-derived EVs promote fibroblast proliferation and migration, stimulate vessel formation, and protect cells from oxidative stress and hyperglycemia-induced damage [19]. They also exert neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects in primary microglial cultures, reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), suggesting potential applications in neurodegenerative diseases [24].

Despite their high expansion capacity in culture, primary MSCs undergo a finite number of divisions before entering replicative senescence [25], characterized by changes in morphology, gene expression, and metabolism [26, 27]. This shortens the period during which MSCs can be used for secretome production and necessitates repeated generation of fresh batches, increasing costs and introducing donor-dependent variability [17]. HF-MSCs face similar issues, as differences in proliferative, secretory, and immunomodulatory profiles between donors can compromise secretome consistency.

Immortalized cell lines can overcome replicative senescence, offering extended proliferative capacity and genomic stability for consistent, long-term secretome production. MSC immortalization has been achieved using various methods [26–28], with evidence supporting their non-tumorigenic and safe profile [29, 30]. Common approaches include viral transfection with human telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT) [28, 31] or the SV40 T antigen (SV40T) [25, 32], alone or in combination [27, 33, 34]. However, immortalized human HF-MSCs (iHF-MSCs) are not yet commercially available, and protocols for their generation remain undeveloped.

SV40T-mediated immortalization has previously been reported for MSCs derived from BM, AT, and UC [25, 28, 31, 32]. However, to our knowledge, this is the first study to generate and comprehensively validate iHF-MSCs. These cells combine the advantages of non-invasive and repeatable collection from donors of any age with well-documented immunoevasive, pro-regenerative and antioxidant properties [18, 19, 22–24], making them particularly attractive for standardized, long-term secretome production. To this end, primary HF-MSCs were isolated [18] and immortalized using the SV40T vector, yielding 576 clones, from which four were selected based on growth rate and senescence profile for detailed characterization. Given that immortalization can alter cellular and secretory properties, we evaluated phenotype, multipotent differentiation capacity, morphology, size, proliferation rate, and responsiveness to inflammatory licensing. We also profiled the conditioned media (CM) from both IFN-γ/TNF-α-licensed and unlicensed iHF-MSCs, assessing its functional activity in immune modulation, skin cell proliferation, migration, tube formation, and antioxidant responses.

Methods

Cells

The cells used in this study comprise adult human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs) (ATCC, PCS-201-012), human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) (Lonza, C2519A), HaCaT keratinocytes (DKFZ, Germany), HF-MSCs, iHF-MSCs and PBMCs. For HF-MSC obtention, hair follicles were harvested by means of the FUE (Follicular Unit Extraction) technique from occipital scalps of routine hair transplant procedures (Clínica Dermatológica Ercilla, Spain) under a protocol approved by the research ethics committee of the University of the Basque Country (UPV/EHU) (M10_2019_053, M30 _2019_054). Afterwards, HF-MSCs were isolated and characterized as previously described [18]. HF-MSCs were infected using lentiviral particles that contains simian virus 40 (SV40) T antigen to obtain iHF-MSCs as described in Sect. 2.2.

HDFs were cultured in fibroblasts basal media (ATCC, PCS-201-030) supplemented with the fibroblast growth kit-low serum (ATCC, PCS-201-041) and 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin (P/S). HUVECs were cultured in the EGM™-2 Endothelial Cell Growth media-2 BulletKit™ (Lonza, CC-3162) supplemented with 1% (v/v) P/S. HaCaTs were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, 41965-039) with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10270106) and 1% (v/v) P/S. HF-MSCs and iHF-MSCs were cultured in Human Mesenchymal-LS Basal media (Merck, SCMM-BM) supplemented with Human Mesenchymal-LS Supplement Kit (Merck, SCMM002-S) and 1% (v/v) P/S. Phosphate buffered saline (PBS) pH 7.4 was used for cell culture. All cells were incubated in a humidified incubator at 37 °C under a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Buffy coats, isolated from the blood of healthy donors at the Basque Transfusion Centre and provided by the Basque Biobank (CEIm CES-BIOEF 2023-06), were utilized in accordance with the guidelines outlined in Law 14/2007 and Royal Decree 1716/2011. Ethical approval for the research protocol was obtained from the Ethics Committee for Investigation related to Human Subjects (CEISH) at the University of the Basque Country (UPV/EHU), under approval code M10_2022_131MR1. All participants provided written informed consent for the use of their blood samples. Additionally, manipulation of these cells was approved by the Research Ethics Committee with Biological Agents and Genetically Modified Organisms (CEIAB) at the UPV/EHU (M30_2022_132MR1).

HF-MSCs SV40T immortalization

HF-MSCs were immortalized by Innovative Technologies in Biological Systems S.L. Briefly, HF-MSCs at passage 3 were seeded at a concentration of 250,000 cells per well in 6 well plates. After 24 h, cells were infected with 2 mL per well of a supernatant containing the SV40T vector at a ratio of 1 × 106 lentiviral particles per each 300,000 cells. 8 h later, the supernatant was removed and replaced by complete media, where cells grew for 2 additional days. Subsequently, cells were separated using Cytena single cell printer technology and transferred to a 96 well plate (a unique cell per well) with complete selection media containing G418 at a concentration of 100 µg/mL. A total of 576 possible clones were dispensed. Cells were maintained in selection media for approximately 2 months, changing the media every 2–3 days. During the process, growth capacity and aging were thoroughly evaluated and the 4 best clones were meticulously selected: C18, C20, C26 and C39. RT-PCR was performed to determine the SV40T expression. In brief, C18, C20, C26 and C39 were separately grown in 24 well plates and the presence of SV40LT mRNA (Simian Virus 40 Large T antigen) was analyzed. The extraction of total RNA was carried out following the Trizol RNA extraction method. The RT-PCR assay was prepared using One-Time Prime Script RT-PCR kit following manufacturer’s protocol. The positive control of the assay was the plasmid containing the cDNA of SV40T and the negative control was the total RNA from uninfected HEK 293 cells.

iHF-MSC phenotyping

Cells in passage 5–7 were stained employing the human MSC Phenotyping kit (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-125-285) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Data were acquired by flow cytometry using the MACSQuant® analyzer (Miltenyi Biotec) and analyzed using FlowJoTM v10.6.2 Software (BD Life Sciences).

Trilineage differentiation capacity of iHF-MSCs

The multipotency capacity of iHF-MSCs was verified by assessing their trilineage differentiation. To assess osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation, cells were seeded at a density of t 1 × 105 cells per well in 6 well plates and cultured using the appropriate differentiation (Merck ready-to-use PromoCell MSC Differentiation Media: C-28013 and C-28016, respectively). To study the chondrogenic differentiation, cell spheroids were formed. To do so, cells were seeded at a confluence of 2 × 105 cells per well in 96-well U-bottom suspension culture plates and incubated for 24–48 h. Spheroids were then cultured in chondrogenic differentiation media (Merck ready-to-use PromoCell MSC Differentiation Media: C-28012). After 14 days of osteogenic and adipogenic induction, and 21 days of chondrogenic induction, cells were fixed in Saccomanno Fixation Solution for 30 min and stained with the corresponding dye: Alizarin Red S staining solution (Merck, TMS008) for osteogenic differentiation, OilRed staining solution (Merck, O1391) for adipogenic differentiation and Alcian Blue staining solution (Merck, TMS010) for chondrogenic differentiation.

CD56 expression analyses

The expression of the neural marker Cluster of Differentiation (CD) 56 was explored by flow cytometry and immunofluorescence. For the former, cells were stained with either anti-CD56-FITC (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-114-740) at a 1:200 dilution or the corresponding isotype control (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-113-437) following manufacturer instructions and acquired using a MACSQuant® analyzer (Miltenyi Biotec). Data were analyzed with FlowJoTM v10.6.2 Software (BD Life Sciences). For immunofluorescence studies, the selected clones were cultured in 96 well plates at a confluence of 5 × 104 cells per well. The next day, cells were washed with PBS and fixed with formaldehyde (37% in PBS). Subsequently, cells were washed and then blocked with filtered 2% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) in PBS for 30 min. After the blocking step, cells were incubated for 1 h with the primary antibody (Anti-NCAM-1/CD56 AF-2408-SP, R&D) at a 15 µg per mL dilution. After the incubation, the primary antibody was removed, and the cells were washed with PBS twice. Then, the cells were incubated during 40 min with the secondary antibody (Donkey anti- Goat Alexa Fluor Plus 488, Invitrogen) at a 1:400 dilution. Finally, the cells were washed twice with PBS and analyzed by fluorescence emission in the “Cell insight CX7” high content equipment.

iHF-MSC morphology and growth behavior characterization

To study cell morphology and size, HF-MSCs and iHF-MSCs were seeded at a confluence of 50,000 cells per well in 24-well plates. Bright-field micrographs were taken in an optical microscope (Nikon) and cell area was subsequently measured by means of ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, MA, USA).

Growth behavior was determined by measuring the cell doubling time. HF-MSCs and iHF-MSCs were seeded at a confluency of 50,000 cells per well in 24-well plates and the number of cells was analyzed at days 2, 4 and 7 using a benchtop TC20 Automated Cell Counter (Bio Rad). Subsequent assays in this study employed iHF-MSC clones C18 and C26 at higher passages (P19–P21).

Licensed conditioned media (CM) production and characterization

In this study, “licensed” conditioned media (CM) refers to the CM obtained after exposing MSCs to pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFN-γ and TNF-α), a process known as inflammatory licensing, which enhances their immunomodulatory profile. In contrast, “unlicensed” CM corresponds to the CM collected from MSCs cultured under basal, non-stimulated conditions.

In this case, to obtain a CM rich in immunomodulatory factors, the MSC2 phenotype of HF-MSCs and iHF-MSCs was induced by means of inflammatory licensing. In particular, cells were incubated for 16 h with complete media containing 20 ng/mL of IFN-γ (Merck, SRP3058-100UG) and TNF-α (Merck, H8916-10UG). Subsequently, the licensing media was replaced by production media, consisting of non-supplemented human Mesenchymal-LS Basal Medium (Merck, SCMM-BM). After 48 h, the supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 460 x g for 10 min to eliminate cell debris. The obtained licensed CM was kept at -80 °C until further use.

Characterization of licensed CM was performed by means of enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), evaluating the following targets: Gal-9 (ThermoFisher Scientific, InvitrogenTM Human Galectin-9 ELISA Kit), IL1-Ra (Bio-Techne R&D Systems, Human IL-1ra/IL-1F3 Quantikine ELISA Kit) and VEGF (ThermoFisher Scientific, Invitrogen™ VEGF Human ELISA Kit).

Characterization of iHF-MSC secretome

Characterization of CM was performed by means of a label-based human antibody array (RayBio® L-Series Human Obesity Antibody Array, RayBiotech). Samples were biotinylated and incubated overnight with pre-coated array slides according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Detection was performed using Cy3-streptavidin, and fluorescence signals were acquired with a laser scanner (InnoScan 710, Innopsys). Densitometric quantification was carried out using RayBiotech’s Q-Analyzer software. A subset of relevant targets was selected for semi-quantitative comparison based on biological relevance and differential expression between conditions.

Additionally, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was performed to evaluate the following targets: Gal-9 (ThermoFisher Scientific, InvitrogenTM Human Galectin-9 ELISA Kit), IL1-Ra (Bio-Techne R&D Systems, Human IL-1ra/IL-1F3 Quantikine ELISA Kit) and VEGF (ThermoFisher Scientific, Invitrogen™ VEGF Human ELISA Kit).

iHF-MSC immunoevasiveness determination

To determine if HF-MSC immunoevasiveness was affected by the immortalization process, major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) and major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC-II) expression was analyzed by flow cytometry after subjecting the cells to 24, 48–72 h of inflammatory licensing (complete media supplemented with 20 ng/mL of IFN-γ and TNF-α). Cells were stained with anti-MHC-I-PeVio770 (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-120-573), anti-MHC-II-VioBlue (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-111-947) or the corresponding isotype controls (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-113-440 and 130-113-442, respectively), following the manufacturer’s instructions at a 1:200 dilution. Data were acquired using the MACSQuant® analyzer (Miltenyi Biotec) and analyzed using FlowJoTM v10.6.2 Software (BD Life Sciences).

Functional studies to evaluate iHF-MSC-mediated Immunomodulation

PBMC proliferation assay

PBMC and iHF-MSC co-culture

The ability of iHF-MSCs to suppress the proliferation of PBMCs was assessed by means of a carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) proliferation assay. To do so, 1 × 106 PBMCs were suspended in 1 mL of complete media and labeled with 5 µM CFSE for 15 min. Subsequently, a washing step was performed by adding 5 mL of ice-cold PBS supplemented with 1% BSA and centrifuging the samples at 360 x g for 6 min. Supernatants were discarded and 1 × 105 PBMCs per well were seeded in 96 well plates and cultured for 5 days in complete media supplemented with 2.5 µg/mL concanavalin A (Merck, C5275) in the presence of iHF-MSCs at different ratios (1:1, 1:2, 1:5 and 1:10 iHF-MSC: PBMCs). The control group consisted of PBMCs cultured in complete media supplemented with concanavalin A without the presence of iHF-MSCs. At day 3, half of the media was replaced by new complete media. At day 5, cells were harvested and CFSE intensity was assessed using flow cytometry. Cell acquisition was performed using the MACSQuant® analyzer (Miltenyi Biotec) and data analysis was conducted using FlowJoTM v10.6.2 Software (BD Life Sciences).

PBMC culture in iHF-MSC-derived licensed CM

The suppressive effect of iHF-MSC-derived licensed CM on PBMC proliferation was evaluated using the CFSE proliferation assay, as explained above. After the labelling and washing steps, 200,000 PBMCs were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured for 5 days in licensed CM supplemented with 10% FBS and 2.5 µg/mL concanavalin A. For the control group, complete media supplemented with 2.5 µg/mL concanavalin A was employed. On day 3, half of the media was refreshed. At day 5, cells were harvested and CFSE intensity was assessed using flow cytometry. Cell acquisition was performed using the MACSQuant® analyzer (Miltenyi Biotec) and data analysis was conducted using FlowJoTM v10.6.2 Software (BD Life Sciences).

Treg induction

Treg and iHF-MSC co-culture

PBMCs were seeded in 96-well plates at a concentration of 1 × 105 cells per well. Afterwards, PBMCs were activated with 0.3 µL of Dynabeads Human T-Activator CD3/CD28/per well (Gibco, 10310614) for 7 days, during which they were co-cultured with iHF-MSCs at varying ratios (1:1, 1:2, 1:5 and 1:10 MSCs: PBMCs). 2 different control groups were set: a control group in which PBMCs were cultured in media containing Dynabeads without the presence of iHF-MSCs and a positive control group in which PBMCs were cultured in complete media containing Dynabeads plus 5 µL/well of Interleukin-2 human (hIL-2) 10000U (Gibco, 11147528001). On day 5, half of the media was refreshed and on day 7 cells were collected and Dynabeads were removed using a magnetic field. The resulting cell population was stained 1:200 with the following antibodies: anti-CD4 (VioBlue®REA, 130-114-534), anti-CD25 (PE®REA, 130-113-286) and anti-CD127 (APC®REA 130-113-407) or with the corresponding isotype control antibodies (VioBlue®REA1, 30-113-454; PE®REA, 130-113-286; APC®REA, 130-113-446) following the manufacturer’s instructions (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). Flow cytometry analyses were conducted using a MACSQuant® analyzer (Miltenyi Biotec) and data were processed using FlowJoTM v10.6.2 Software (BD Life Sciences, Ashland, OR, USA).

Treg culture in iHF-MSC-derived licensed CM

PBMCs were seeded in 96-well plates at a concentration of 1 × 105 cells per well. Afterwards, PBMCs were activated with 0.3 µL of Dynabeads Human T-Activator CD3/CD28/per well (Gibco, 10310614) for 7 days, during which they were cultured iHF-MSC-derived licensed CM. 2 different control groups were set: a control group in which PBMCs were cultured in complete media containing Dynabeads and a positive control group in which PBMCs were cultured in complete media containing Dynabeads plus 5 µL/well of hIL-2 10000U (Gibco, 11147528001). On day 5, half of the media was refreshed and on day 7 cells were collected and Dynabeads were removed using a magnetic field. The resulting cell population was stained 1:200 with the following antibodies: anti-CD4 (VioBlue®REA, 130-114-534), anti-CD25 (PE®REA, 130-113-286) and anti-CD127 (APC®REA 130-113-407) or with the corresponding isotype control antibodies (VioBlue®REA1, 30-113-454; PE®REA, 130-113-286; APC®REA, 130-113-446) following the manufacturer’s instructions (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). Flow cytometry analyses were conducted using a MACSQuant® analyzer (Miltenyi Biotec) and data were processed using FlowJoTM v10.6.2 Software (BD Life Sciences, Ashland, OR, USA).

iHF-MSC-derived unlicensed CM production

To obtain unlicensed CM, iHF-MSCs were cultured in complete media up to a 90% of confluence. Subsequently, complete media was replaced by production media, consisting of non-supplemented human Mesenchymal-LS Basal Media (Merck, SCMM-BM). After 48 h, the supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 460 x g for 10 min to eliminate cell debris and apoptotic bodies. The obtained unlicensed CM was kept at -80 °C until further use.

Effect of iHF-MSC-derived unlicensed CM over HDF and HaCaT proliferation

To study the effect of the obtained unlicensed CM over the proliferation skin cells, HaCaTs or HDFs were seeded in 96 well plates at a density of 10,000 cells per well and cultured in 100 µL of complete media. After 6 h, cells were washed with PBS and incubated overnight in FBS free media. Subsequently, cells were washed with 100 µL of PBS and incubated for 24 h in either 100 µL of iHF-MSC-derived unlicensed CM, complete HaCaT/HDF media (in the case of the positive control), or FBS-free Mesenchymal-LS Basal Media media (in the case of the negative control). After such incubation, a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) colorimetric assay (Merck, 96992) was performed to determine cell metabolism. In brief, cells were washed with PBS and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C in complete media supplemented with 10% CCK-8 reagent. Absorbance was then read in a plate reader (Infinite® 200 PRO series, TecanTrading AG) at 450 nm, using 650 nm as the reference wavelength. Cell viability at 72 h was also studied, in this case by means of calcein AM/Ethidium homodimer staining (Invitrogen, L3224). Analyses were performed using an epi-fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Effect of iHF-MSC-derived unlicensed CM over HDF and HaCaT migration

To evaluate whether the iHF-MSCs-derived unlicensed CM can promote the migration of HDFs and HaCaTs, an in vitro scratch assay was performed. Inserts were placed on 24-well plates and surrounded with PBS to minimize the evaporation of the culture media. After an 8 h incubation, wells were washed with PBS and cultured overnight with FBS-free media. Subsequently, inserts were extracted – creating a gap – and wells were washed with PBS to discard the detached cells. Then, 300 µL of iHF-MSC-derived unlicensed CM were added to the wells. For the positive control complete culture media was employed, whereas for the negative control FBS-free Mesenchymal-LS Basal Media was used. At pre-defined time-points – 0, 8, 24 and 36 h – images of the closing area were taken by an optical microscope (Nikon, Japan). Effect of iHF-MSC-derived unlicensed CM on HUVEC tube formation

Specific 96-well plates for tube formation assays (IBIDI, 89646) were coated with 15 µL of Matrigel® (Corning, 356231), which was allowed to polymerize for 30 min at 37 °C. Subsequently, 3 × 104 HUVECs were suspended in 70 µL of iHF-MSC-derived unlicensed CM. For the positive control complete HUVEC culture media was employed, whereas for the negative control FBS-free Mesenchymal-LS Basal Media was used. The cell suspension was then seeded on each microplate well. After a 24 h incubation, HUVECs were stained with calcein AM and images of each well were taken by means of an epi-fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan). For the assessment of tube formation Image J Software was employed.

Effect of iHF-MSC-derived unlicensed CM against oxidative stress

Reactive oxygen species (ROS)

First, a ROS neutralization assay was performed. In particular, a 30 mM H2O2 solution was 1:1 mixed with iHF-MSC-derived unlicensed CM. After 2 h of mixing, 100 µL of the resulting solution were added to the 96-well plates where 10,000 HDFs and 50,000 HaCaTs had been previously seeded and incubated for 30 min. For the positive control complete culture media of HDF or HaCaTs was employed, whereas for the negative control non-supplemented Mesenchymal-LS Basal Media was used. An additional control was set in which HDFs/HaCaTs were treated with complete media without H2O2. Subsequently, wells were washed twice with PBS and incubated for 6 h with 100 µL of FBS free media. Finally, wells were washed with PBS and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C in complete media supplemented with 10% CCK-8 reagent (Merck, 96992). Absorbance was then read with a plate reader (Infinite® 200 PRO series, TecanTrading AG) at 450 nm, using 650 nm as the reference wavelength.

Second, a protection against ROS assay was conducted. In particular, 10,000 HDFs and 50,000 HaCaTs were seeded in 96 well plated and treated with 100 µL of iHF-MSC-derived unlicensed CM for 6 h. For the positive control complete culture media of HDF/HaCaTs was employed, whereas for the negative control non-supplemented Mesenchymal-LS Basal Media was used. Afterwards, wells were washed with PBS and 100 µL of a 15 mM H2O2 solution in serum free media were added for 30 min. At this point, an additional control was set in which HDFs/HaCaTs were treated with complete media in the 6 h incubation and afterwards was not exposed to H2O2. Finally, wells were washed with PBS and a CCK-8 assay was performed as above described.

The ROS neutralization assay and protection against ROS assay were repeated to assess cell proliferation. In this case, instead of performing a CCK-8 assay, HDF/HaCaTs were stained with Calcein AM micrographs were taken by means of an epifluorescence microscope (Nikon).

Protection against hyperglycemia-induced cytotoxicity

10,000 HDFs or 50,000 HaCaTs per well were seeded in a 96-well plate for 24 h. Afterward, cells were washed twice with PBS and incubated with 100 µL of iHF-MSC-derived unlicensed CM for 6 h. For the positive control, HDF/HaCaT complete media was employed, whereas for the negative control non-supplemented Mesenchymal-LS Basal was used. After the incubation, cells were washed twice with PBS and 100 µL of a 150 µM glucose solution in HDF/HaCaT media were added to each well. At this point, an additional control group (w/o glucose control) was included, in which cells previously incubated with complete media were now treated with serum-free media without glucose. After 24, 48–72 h, cells were washed twice with PBS and a CCK-8 assay was performed as described in Sect. 2.14.1.

Images of calcein/ethidium stained HDF and HaCaTs after the 72 h incubation point were taken using an epifluorescence microscope (Nikon).

Statistical analyses

Results are represented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All experiments were conducted at least in triplicate. For statistical analyses, first normality was tested. If data followed a normal distribution, the comparison between 2 independent groups was conducted using a Student’s T-test, while a one-way ANOVA test was employed for multiple comparisons. In the latter, the homogeneity of variances was studied using the Levene test. For homogeneous variances, the Bonferroni post-hoc was applied, whereas for non homogeneuos variances the Tamhane post-hoc analysis was used. For non-normally distributed data Mann-Whitney’s test was applied. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0. Significance thresholds were set at p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), and p < 0.001 (***). The specific test applied in each experiment is indicated in the corresponding figure legend.

Pro-inflammatory cytokines showed limited variation across conditions (Fig. 3A). Under the unlicensed condition, IFN-γ levels were higher in C18 (~ 1.19-fold; p < 0.001 vs. HF-MSCs) and C26 (~ 1.31-fold; p < 0.001 vs. HF-MSCs). Upon licensing, IFN-γ levels increased slightly in HF-MSCs (~ 1.27-fold) and C18 (~ 1.28-fold), but decreased in C26 (~ 0.98-fold; p < 0.05 vs. HF-MSCs, C18, and unlicensed C26). TNF-α levels were reduced in C18 under the unlicensed condition (~ 0.75-fold; p < 0.001 vs. HF-MSCs and C26) and close to basal in C26 (~ 1.08-fold). Upon licensing, TNF-α levels remained below baseline in all groups (~ 0.83-fold in HF-MSCs, ~ 0.72-fold in C18, and ~ 0.90-fold in C26; p < 0.05 vs. each licensed condition). Upon licensing, IL-6 showed a modest but consistent increase, particularly in C26 (~ 1.45-fold; p < 0.001 vs. HF-MSCs and C18), while levels in C18 reached ~ 1.25-fold.

Regarding growth factors (Fig. 3B), the unlicensed condition displayed a pro-angiogenic profile, with VEGF significantly elevated in both clones (C18: ~1.83-fold; C26: ~1.89-fold; p < 0.001 vs. HF-MSCs) and EGF also increased in C26 (~ 1.45-fold; p < 0.001 vs. HF-MSCs and C18). Following licensing, VEGF levels declined modestly (C18: ~1.37-fold; C26: ~1.33-fold; p < 0.01) but remained above those of HF-MSCs (p < 0.001 for both clones), whereas EGF increased moderately in C18 (~ 1.30-fold; p < 0.01 vs. unlicensed). PDGF-AA secretion was lower in C18 under the unlicensed condition (~ 0.73-fold; p < 0.01 vs. HF-MSCs and C26) but increased after licensing in C26 (~ 1.34-fold; p < 0.001 vs. HF-MSCs, C18, and unlicensed). Other growth factors, including Ang-1, Ang-2, FGF-10, and SDF-1, exhibited only minor variations across conditions.

Regarding ECM-related proteins (Fig. 3C), MMP-2 and MMP-9 were consistently lower in immortalized clones compared with primary HF-MSCs under the unlicensed condition. MMP-2 levels were ~ 0.67-fold in C18 and ~ 0.71-fold in C26 (p < 0.001 vs. HF-MSCs). Similarly, MMP-9 levels were ~ 0.61-fold in C18 and ~ 0.53-fold in C26 (p < 0.001 vs. HF-MSCs). This pattern persisted after licensing in both clones; however, HF-MSCs showed a reduction in their levels, with MMP-2 decreasing to ~ 0.80-fold (p < 0.001 vs. unlicensed) and MMP-9 to ~ 0.75-fold (p < 0.01 vs. unlicensed). TIMP-1 levels were slightly elevated in C26 under the unlicensed condition (~ 1.34-fold; p < 0.001 vs. HF-MSCs and C18) and decreased after licensing (~ 0.83-fold; p < 0.001 vs. unlicensed). SPARC levels increased notably in HF-MSCs after licensing (~ 1.8-fold; p < 0.001 vs. C18, C26, and unlicensed). Adiponectin was markedly increased in C26 under the unlicensed condition (~ 1.80-fold; p < 0.001 vs. HF-MSCs and C18) and showed a moderate rise in HF-MSCs after licensing (~ 1.30-fold; p < 0.001 vs. unlicensed). Other ECM-related proteins, including E-selectin, VCAM-1, Gal-1, TGF-β, and TIMP-2, exhibited only minor fluctuations across conditions.

Finally, comparison of low-passage (P6-P8) and high-passage (P19-P21) cultures revealed no significant differences in the secretion of VEGF, Gal-9, or IL-1Ra across clones (Fig. S2). In HF-MSCs under the unlicensed condition, however, IL-1Ra levels displayed a modest yet significant increase at high passage (p < 0.01).

iHF-MSC-mediated Immunomodulation

To evaluate the immunomodulatory capacity of iHF-MSCs, PBMC proliferation was assessed after a 5-day co-culture at different MSC: PBMC ratios (1:1, 1:2, 1:5, and 1:10) (Fig. 5A-B). Unstimulated PBMCs showed minimal proliferation, whereas ConA-activated PBMCs underwent extensive division, confirming assay responsiveness. The presence of MSCs markedly suppressed PBMC proliferation in a ratio-dependent manner. At the 1:1 ratio, the proportion of undivided cells was highest with C26 (~ 75%), compared with ~ 55% for both C18 and HF-MSCs (p < 0.01 for C26 vs. C18 and HF-MSCs) (Fig. 5B-I). This inhibitory effect persisted at lower ratios (1:2, 1:5, 1:10), although the magnitude of suppression progressively decreased. Conversely, the proportion of PBMCs undergoing ≥ 6 divisions was lowest with C26 (~ 5–10%), whereas C18 and HF-MSCs showed higher percentages (~ 20–30%) (p < 0.001 for C26 vs. C18 and HF-MSCs) (Fig. 5B-III).

To assess whether soluble factors mediated this response, PBMC proliferation was evaluated using CM from unlicensed and licensed cultures (Fig. 5C-D). Under unlicensed conditions, CM from both C18 and C26 reduced PBMC proliferation more effectively than CM from HF-MSCs (p < 0.001). We next examined the induction of Tregs after a 7-day co-culture (Fig. 5E). The negative control contained 3.48% CD4⁺CD25^high^CD127^low^ cells, whereas the IL-2 positive control reached 19.86%. At the 1:5 ratio, C18 (13.76%) and C26 (14.05%) remained slightly higher than HF-MSCs (13.11%), while at the 1:10 ratio, percentages decreased across all groups, with C18 (11.22%) close to HF-MSCs (10.85%) and C26 (10.56%) slightly lower.

In contrast, unlicensed CM resulted in markedly lower percentages, particularly for HF-MSCs (0.87%), while C18 (2.98%) and C26 (3.88%) retained partial activity (p < 0.01 and p < 0.001 vs. HF-MSCs, respectively).

To evaluate the antioxidant properties of unlicensed iHF-MSC-derived CM, three complementary assays were performed.

These levels were comparable to the positive control (Ctrl+: ~1.5) and significantly higher than those observed with HF-MSC CM (~ 1.2; p < 0.05 vs. C18 and C26). In the protection assay, pre-treatment with C26 yielded the highest survival (~ 1.7, p < 0.05 vs. HF-MSCs), slightly exceeding that of C18 and HF-MSCs (~ 1.5) and approaching the W/o H2O2 group (~ 1.8) (Fig. 7B). In HDFs, neutralization was markedly higher with C18 (~ 7) and C26 (~ 15) compared to HF-MSCs (~ 0.5) or Ctrl- (~ 0.5), and superior than Ctrl+ (~ 3; p < 0.001) (Fig. 7C). Moreover, protection was enhanced with C18 and C26 (~ 1.9 and ~ 1.8, respectively) compared with HF-MSCs (~ 1.0; p < 0.001) and Ctrl- (~ 1.0; p < 0.001) (Fig. 7D).

At 24 h, cells treated with iHF-MSC and HF-MSCs reached ~ 115–120% compared to ~ 100% in Ctrl- and ~ 125% in the Ctrl+. This protective effect persisted at 48 and 72 h, with C18 and C26 achieving ~ 125–130%, compared to ~ 115% for HF-MSCs (p < 0.01) and ~ 100% for Ctrl-(p < 0.01).

Discussion

Immortalization of MSCs offers a practical strategy to bypass replicative senescence and enable standardized, long-term production of therapeutic secretome. Given the central role of MSC-derived factors in immunomodulation, tissue repair, and antioxidant defense [1–4], immortalized platforms can overcome the variability and limited lifespan of primary MSCs. HF-MSCs represent an attractive source due to their non-invasive and repeatable collection and their reported immunoevasive, pro-regenerative, and antioxidant properties [24, 25, 29, 30]. Here, we provide the first comprehensive characterization of iHF-MSCs, evaluating whether immortalization preserves their identity and function while supporting reproducible large-scale secretome production.

The detection of SV40LT mRNA in all clones confirmed successful immortalization, consistent with previous studies showing that SV40LT bypasses senescence checkpoints while maintaining viability and multipotency [26, 27]. Fibroblast-like morphology was preserved across passages, indicating that cellular identity was maintained. Phenotypic analysis according to International Society for Cell & Gene Therapy (ISCT) minimal criteria – recently reinforced by the Delphi-driven consensus [35] – showed that CD73 expression remained >99% in all clones, underscoring its association with immunomodulatory potency [36, 37]. CD90 and CD105 fell below the ≥ 95% threshold in iHF-MSCs, a finding aligned with reports of reduced expression after SV40LT or MYC immortalization [26, 27, 33]. Notably, CD105 downregulation may be advantageous, given its association with senescence [38]. These results support their designation as MSC-like while confirming preservation of the most functionally relevant marker, CD73.

Multipotency was retained, with successful osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation. As in other studies [39, 40], adipogenesis appeared less efficient, likely reflecting metabolic reprogramming – adipogenic progenitors rely more on oxidative phosphorylation, while osteogenic progenitors favor glycolysis [40]. The preservation of the differentiation potential confirms that immortalization did not abolish lineage plasticity.

Functionally, iHF-MSCs remained stable across passages. Among the four clones, C18 and C26 combined consistent morphology with faster doubling times at higher passages compared with primary HF-MSCs. Such proliferative advantage, typical of immortalized MSCs [26, 27], is valuable for scalable and reproducible secretome production. Importantly, CD56 – normally reduced in late-passage primary HF-MSCs – was preserved, and even increased in C18. As CD56 is linked to dermal papilla identity [17], its stabilization suggests retention of niche-related features. Although in vivo tumorigenicity assays were not performed in this study, previous research has consistently shown that SV40LT-immortalized MSCs remain genomically stable and non-tumorigenic [41–44]. Together with our focus on a cell-free product, these findings support iHF-MSCs as a safe platform for long-term therapeutic manufacturing.

Secretome profiling confirmed that immortalization preserved – and in some cases enhanced – the functional characteristics of HF-MSCs. Under unlicensed CM, both clones showed secretion patterns comparable to primary HF-MSCs, with C26 exhibiting a slightly more immunomodulatory baseline profile. Upon IFN-γ/TNF-α licensing, both clones mounted a robust response with marked upregulation of IL-1Ra and TSG-6, indicating intact – and for C26, potentially heightened – responsiveness to inflammatory cues. This behavior aligns with the complementary activation of JAK/STAT1 and NF-κB, which drives the secretion of key immunomodulatory mediators (e.g., IDO, PGE₂, IL-1Ra, TSG-6, Gal-9) and thereby reinforces the anti-inflammatory profile of the secretome [45–50]. Consistently, pro-inflammatory cytokines remained low across conditions, supporting a predominantly anti-inflammatory composition conducive to clinical use. Importantly, licensing was performed with IFN-γ and TNF-α at 20 ng/mL each (1:1), a regimen widely reported to maximize synergistic activation of JAK/STAT1 and NF-κB while avoiding cytotoxicity, and consistently associated with the upregulation of soluble anti-inflammatory mediators under preserved viability [45, 47–50], thereby supporting both reproducibility and translational relevance.

IL-6 also increased after licensing – particularly in C26 – consistent with its pleiotropic, context-dependent role in MSC biology [51]. Beyond classical inflammation, IL-6 can also contribute to angiogenesis, fibroblast proliferation, and M2 macrophage polarization [52, 53].

Growth-factor and ECM analyses further reinforced this adaptive profile. VEGF was higher under unlicensed CM and decreased after licensing while remaining above primary levels, in line with reports that inflammatory signaling can attenuate pro-angiogenic outputs [54–58]. Conversely, EGF and FGF-10 rose after licensing – consistent with roles in keratinocyte proliferation and epidermal repair [59, 60] – and PDGF-AA was most prominent in C26, matching its function in pericyte recruitment and vascular stabilization [61]. Together, these shifts indicate that under inflammatory cues the iHF-MSC secretome prioritizes immunomodulation while preserving essential regenerative outputs.

ECM-related proteins displayed a regulated remodeling signature. Both clones maintained reduced MMP-2/MMP-9 with relatively higher TIMP-1 in C26, suggesting tighter control of proteolysis; after licensing, this restrained proteolytic profile persisted, while primary HF-MSCs showed a clearer reduction, consistent with a controlled ECM-turnover state [62]. SPARC increased in primary HF-MSCs after licensing but remained stable in immortalized clones, potentially reflecting differences in matrix-remodeling dynamics between primary and immortalized cells [63]. Adiponectin – linked to angiogenesis, anti-fibrotic effects, and metabolic regulation – was comparatively higher in C26 and rose modestly in licensed HF-MSCs, further supporting an anti-inflammatory, pro-regenerative environment [64].

Finally, the stability of VEGF, Gal-9, and IL-1Ra across low and high passages underscores the robustness of the immortalized platform for standardized, scalable secretome production. The modest IL-1Ra increase observed only in high-passage primary HF-MSCs likely reflects early senescence-associated secretory changes not apparent in iHF-MSCs, reinforcing their suitability for manufacturing.

Immunoevasiveness profiling demonstrated that iHF-MSCs maintained a low basal expression of MHC-I and an absence of MHC-II, consistent with the immune-privileged phenotype typically reported for primary MSCs. Upon inflammatory licensing, a time-dependent upregulation of both markers was observed, but with notable differences between clones. C18 consistently exhibited a more attenuated induction of MHC-I and MHC-II compared with both C26 and primary HF-MSCs, suggesting a lower immunogenic profile even under pro-inflammatory conditions. This feature is advantageous for therapeutic applications, as reduced expression of MHC molecules has been associated with decreased immune recognition and prolonged persistence of MSC-based therapies in vivo [66].

Functional assays reinforced these phenotypic observations. Both clones effectively suppressed PBMC proliferation in a dose-dependent manner, with C26 showing the strongest inhibition, consistent with the established role of direct cell-cell contact in MSC-mediated immune regulation [67, 68]. Unlicensed CM partially reproduced this suppression, and licensing further enhanced the response, likely driven by the upregulation of soluble mediators such as IDO and PD-L1 [69]. Likewise, both clones promoted the expansion of Tregs, reaching levels comparable to IL-2 supplementation and surpassing those induced by primary HF-MSCs at intermediate ratios. When CM was used instead of cells, Treg induction was less pronounced and dependent on licensing, reinforcing that paracrine and cell-cell contact mechanisms act synergistically to mediate these effects [70]. These findings are particularly relevant in the context of autoimmune and chronic inflammatory diseases, where excessive effector T-cell activation and impaired regulatory circuits contribute to disease progression. The ability of iHF-MSCs – particularly clone C18 – to suppress effector T-cell proliferation while promoting regulatory T-cell expansion highlights their strong immunomodulatory activity. While licensed CM also showed a modest capacity to increase Treg percentages, the effect was markedly less pronounced than with direct cell contact. These findings suggest that both cell-based and cell-free approaches may contribute to restoring immune homeostasis, although their mechanisms and relative potency differ. Future studies in disease-specific models, such as graft-versus-host disease or systemic lupus erythematosus, will be essential to validate these therapeutic opportunities and evaluate long-term safety.

Beyond their immunomodulatory profile, iHF-MSCs retained and even enhanced key regenerative functions. In line with their pro-angiogenic secretome, unlicensed CM promoted robust keratinocyte and fibroblast proliferation, with C18 reaching levels comparable to complete medium and C26 showing the strongest effect on keratinocytes. Scratch assays confirmed a marked acceleration of migration, particularly in keratinocytes, highlighting the potential of these cells in supporting re-epithelialization during wound repair. Consistent with the elevated basal secretion of VEGF and other angiogenic factors, iHF-MSC-derived unlicensed CM also enhanced endothelial tube formation, with both clones supporting significantly greater tube and branching point formation than primary HF-MSCs, approaching the response of positive controls. This pro-angiogenic effect aligns with previous reports on MSC-derived factors in vascular repair [7, 71–73] and further supports the functional relevance of the observed secretome profile. In addition, unlicensed CM displayed strong antioxidant effects, reducing ROS and protecting keratinocytes and fibroblasts from oxidative stress, with C26 showing slightly stronger activity in keratinocytes, while C18 provided enhanced protection in fibroblasts. These effects were consistently observed across complementary assays, including ROS neutralization, cell survival after oxidative insult, and hyperglycemia-induced stress, highlighting the broad cytoprotective capacity of the iHF-MSC-derived secretome. These findings align with the secretome profile characterized, where key anti-inflammatory and regenerative mediators such as IL-1Ra, TSG-6, IL-6, VEGF, and adiponectin were identified. Many of these factors have been linked to redox homeostasis and cytoprotection in damaged or stressed tissues [74], supporting the hypothesis that the observed antioxidant effects result from a synergistic action of multiple secreted proteins rather than a single mediator.

From a translational perspective, these antioxidant and cytoprotective properties are particularly relevant in the context of chronic wounds, where oxidative stress, inflammation, and impaired vascularization hinder healing [75]. The ability of iHF-MSC-derived unlicensed CM to enhance survival under hyperglycemic conditions is especially promising for diabetic ulcers, where metabolic stress and vascular dysfunction limit repair [76]. Given its pro-angiogenic, pro-migratory, and anti-inflammatory profile, the iHF-MSC-derived secretome also holds potential for skin graft integration, ischemic tissue repair, and regeneration of microvascular networks [77].

The comparative analysis in Table 2 highlights the complementary strengths of the two leading clones: C18, with superior immunoevasiveness and an enriched secretome, and C26, with enhanced antioxidant activity. These profiles suggest that clone-specific applications could be tailored to distinct disease contexts. The encouraging results obtained in vitro will surely be followed up with future in vivo studies, which will be critical to validate the observed effects, determine optimal dosing and delivery strategies, and assess the durability and safety of repeated administrations in clinically relevant models.

In summary, iHF-MSCs represent a stable, expandable, and functionally robust platform that preserves the immunomodulatory, regenerative, and antioxidant properties of primary HF-MSCs. Immortalization ensures an extended lifespan without compromising multipotency or responsiveness to inflammatory cues, while enabling reproducible large-scale production. These characteristics position iHF-MSCs as a promising platform for applications in regenerative, inflammatory, and oxidative stress-related disorders, effectively addressing current barriers of variability, scalability, and standardization that limit MSC-based therapies.

Conclusion

In this work, we established and characterized SV40T-immortalized HF-MSCs as a stable and reproducible source of secretome. The selected clones, C18 and C26, exhibited reduced doubling times, preserved multipotency, and maintained responsiveness to inflammatory licensing, enabling efficient large-scale production. Importantly, they secreted key immunomodulatory factors, suppressed PBMC proliferation, promoted Treg differentiation, and exerted strong antioxidant effects protecting skin cells from oxidative and hyperglycemic stress.

Together, these findings demonstrate that iHF-MSCs retain the core therapeutic functions of primary HF-MSCs while providing a scalable and standardized platform for secretome-based applications. Their complementary profiles – C18 with superior immunoevasiveness and C26 with enhanced antioxidant activity – suggest tailored use in specific clinical contexts.

Moreover, the regenerative potential of the iHF-MSC-derived secretome – particularly its pro-angiogenic, pro-migratory, and cytoprotective properties – highlights additional opportunities for clinical translation, including chronic wound healing, diabetic ulcers, and ischemic tissue repair. Overall, iHF-MSCs thus represent a robust alternative to primary MSCs, paving the way toward the development of safe, cell-free therapies for inflammatory, degenerative, oxidative stress-related, and regenerative diseases.
