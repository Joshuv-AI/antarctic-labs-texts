Adaptive determinantal scheduling with fairness in wireless networks
H.P. Keeler, B. Błaszczyszyn

Abstract
We propose a novel framework for wireless network scheduling with fairness using determinantal (point) processes. Our approach incorporates the repulsive nature of determinantal processes, generalizing traditional Aloha protocols that schedule transmissions independently. We formulate the scheduling problem with an utility function representing fairness. We then recast this formulation as a convex optimization problem over a certain class of determinantal point processes called L L -ensembles, which are particularly suited for statistical and numerical treatments. These determinantal processes, which have already proven valuable in subset learning, offer an attractive approach to network resource scheduling and allocating. We demonstrate the suitability of determinantal processes for network models based on the signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR). Our results highlight the potential of determinantal scheduling coupled with fairness. This work bridges recent advances in machine learning with wireless communications, providing a mathematically elegant and computationally tractable approach to network scheduling.



I
Introduction

Scheduling and allocating resources demands careful consideration in wireless networks and other complex systems. The algorithms must efficiently distribute time, power, frequency, and other resources among network users, responding dynamically to changing network conditions and traffic demands. The existing literature spans a wide spectrum of approaches for scheduling and allocating resources, ranging from simple heuristics to sophisticated mathematical models. These techniques seek to optimize network resources with varied aims, such as maximizing network capacity, throughput, and fairness
[
2
,
29
,
15
]

At their core, schedulers are algorithms for selecting subsets from a broader set, with the goal of optimizing a certain utility function. When allocating resources by choosing subsets, a common requirement is some degree of repulsion in the subsets, meaning the scheduler should not select resources that are close to each other in some way. This is particularly the case when working with the signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR). Besides transmitter-receiver distances, the main influence is the clustering of network nodes reducing SINR values. This effect strongly hints at choosing a scheduling (or subset selecting) method that has inherent repulsion.

I-A

From fermions to scalable learning models

Determinantal point processes, originally proposed to model repulsive particles known as fermions such as electrons, possess interesting and convenient mathematical characteristics
[
17
]
.
Determinantal processes are amenable to statistical inference methods, while they can also be easily and
exactly
simulated on computers using an algorithm that takes a matrix eigen-decomposition
[
23
]
.

Over the last decade the machine learning research community has identified determinantal point processes as particularly promising tools for selecting complex subsets. Starting with the pioneering efforts by Kulesza and Taskar
[
22
]
, researchers have successfully leveraged determinantal processes to develop sophisticated machine learning frameworks
[
16
]
, demonstrating their potential for automated subset selection and generative models in general.

Determinantal models are parametric and relatively scalable, whereas other models commonly suffer from an explosion in computational complexity. Resource allocation as subset selection is typically challenging, often falling into the dreaded NP-complete complexity class, recalling that if there are
n
elements in a set, there are
2^{n}
subsets. But determinantal point processes are well-suited for handling subset problems, and we will see that they naturally extend a classic approach for scheduling.

I-B

From spatial Aloha to proportional fairness

For wireless networks, selecting subsets of nodes to communicate is usually dictated by
medium access control
(MAC) protocols. A classic random one is the (spatial) Aloha scheme in which network transmitters independently access the network with some probability
p
, where
p
is a fixed constant sometimes called the
medium access probability
. Proposed in the 1970s
[
1
]
, this scheduling scheme is compatible with Poisson network models, where the transmitters are scattered across the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
according to a Poisson point process
\Phi=\{X_{i}\}_{i}
with intensity
\lambda>0
. Under the assumption of discrete time, at any time instant the transmitters accessing the network will form another Poisson point process
\Phi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}=\{X_{i}\}_{i}
with intensity
\lambda p
.

I-B
1
Constant Aloha in Poisson bi-pole model

Baccelli, Błaszczyszyn and Mühlethaler
[
3
,
5
,
6
]
studied a Poisson network model
\Phi_{p}
with an Aloha scheme. They assumed each transmitter is associated to a single receiver located uniformly around the transmitter at a fixed distance
r
, resulting in the so-called
bi-pole
or
bi-polar model
; see the monograph by Baccelli and Błaszczyszyn
[
4
, Chapter 16]
. They were interested the value of probability
p
that maximizes the overall coverage based on SINR values exceeding some threshold
{\tau}>0
. To this end, they assumed a receiver at the origin
o
and framed the problem in terms of the
spatial throughput
defined as

p\lambda\mathbf{P}^{o}(\textnormal{SINR}(X_{o},o,\Phi_{p})>{\tau})\,,

(1)

where
\textnormal{SINR}(X_{o},o,\Phi_{p})
is the SINR (which we define later) at the typical receiver
o
with signal coming from its transmitter
X_{o}
and
\mathbf{P}^{o}
represents the Palm distribution of the typical receiver at the origin.
We can interpret the spatial throughput as the average spatial density of transmitter–receiver pairs communicating with the SINR greater than threshold
\tau
. The resulting optimization problem requires finding the value of
p
that maximizes the spatial throughput, namely

p^{*}:=\argmax_{0\leq p\leq 1}\left[p\lambda\mathbf{P}^{o}(\textnormal{SINR}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X_{o},o},\Phi_{p})>{\tau})\right]\,.

(2)

For the path loss model, Baccelli, Błaszczyszyn and Mühlethaler used the standard (singular) power-law model

\ell(x)=(\kappa|x|)^{\beta}
, where the constants
\beta>2
and
\kappa>0
. They also assumed each wireless signal experienced independent Rayleigh fading by using independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) exponential random variables. These model assumptions, which now form a well-studied network model, gave results showing that the spatial throughput is maximized for the probability

p^{*}=\min\left[1,\frac{\beta\sin(2\pi/\beta)}{2\pi^{2}}r^{-2}{\tau}^{-2/\beta}\right]\,.

I-B
2
Adaptive, proportionally fair Aloha

Baccelli, Błaszczyszyn and Singh
[
7
]
improved on the above results in two main ways. They examined the case for transmitters being allowed to have different
p
values depending on the Poisson network configuration
\Phi
. (This ability to have different
p
value for each transmitter motivates the term
adaptive
.) Furthermore, they used a log function as a utility function to study
proportional fairness
in networks, inspired by the seminal paper by Kelly, Maulloo and Tan
[
20
]
.

For their network model, Baccelli, Błaszczyszyn and Singh assumed both a power-law path loss function
\ell(x)=(\kappa|x|)^{\beta}
and independent Rayleigh fading
{F}
. Their final solution for the Aloha probability is a function, which they refer to as a
policy
dependent on the
geometry of the bi-pole model, and in particular
the transmitter location
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

with the receiver at
o
.

This probability function
p^{*}(x;\Phi)
is the solution to the optimization problem

\displaystyle p^{*}(x;\Phi):=\argmax_{0\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p(\cdot;\cdot)}\leq 1}

(3)

\displaystyle\left[\mathbf{E}^{o}\left(\log[p({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X_{o};\Phi})\lambda\mathbf{P}^{o}(\textnormal{SINR}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X_{o}},o,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Phi_{p(\cdot;\cdot)}})>{\tau}|\Phi)\right)\right]\,,

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the transmitter’s location, with its receiver at the origin
o
. The expectation
\mathbf{E}^{o}
is taken under the Palm distribution
\mathbf{P}^{o}
of the typical receiver. The conditional probability
\mathbf{P}^{o}(\cdot\mid\Phi)
accounts for random fading variables, while the Aloha scheme is governed by the translation-invariant function
p(\cdot;\cdot)
. Specifically, in the bi-pole network
\Phi_{p(\cdot;\cdot)}
, a transmitter at
x
communicating with its receiver at
y
accesses the node independently with probability
p^{*}(x-y,\Phi-y)
.

Remarkably, by invoking the Mass Transport Principle, Baccelli, Błaszczyszyn and Singh
[
7
]
offered an explicit and, as argued, unique construction of the optimal probability function
p^{*}(\cdot;\cdot)
. The authors also considered a localized version of this function, where transmitters compute their values based solely on the geometry within a defined local region, which is formalized as a
stopping set
. This localized calculation accounts for the impact of the network configuration outside this set on the aforementioned Palm distribution
\mathbf{P}^{o}
of the typical receiver. Finally, the optimization problem (
3
) can be viewed through the lens of the more general theory of optimal stationary markings, as presented by Błaszczyszyn and Hirsch
[
14
]
.

II-C

Determinantal scheduling

In earlier work
[
9
]
, we used a determinantal (discrete) point process and introduced a novel point process defined on bounded regions of the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which offers model capabilities such as a closed-form expression for the Laplace functional and Palm distribution. We discussed the potential of using a determinantal scheduler in a wireless network and then training (or fitting) this model on pre-optimized network configurations

We then proposed in a second paper
[

a determinantal scheduler based on the SINR of a wireless network. We gave two examples of wireless network models, for which we derived expressions for the Palm distribution and Laplace functional. Using these tools, we obtained closed-form expressions for the coverage probabilities in the two respective models. Critically, this work laid the groundwork for exploring determinantal scheduling in wireless network by presenting fundamental results for coverage probabilities.

Concurrently, other researchers have begun exploring similar determinantal approaches. Shortly before and independently of our determinantal scheduling work
[
8
]
, Saha and Dhillon
[

applied discrete determinantal point processes to wireless link scheduling, focusing on maximizing the overall network rate. They found optimal network subsets with geometric programming, which they then used to train their parametric determinantal model. In our previous paper
[
8
]
, we discussed how our work diverged from their work
[
24
]
.

Recently, Tu, Saha, and Dhillon
[
26
,
27
]
continued the above line of research, which focuses on training the model, thus complementing our investigations. Tu, Saha, and Dhillon
[
26
]
started by considering a determinantal scheduler in a drone network model. The determinantal scheduler can be defined with a square matrix
S
, which is typically positive semi-definite. Tu, Saha, and Dhillon
[
26
]
proposed a

Tu, Saha, and Dhillon
[
26

They then found optimal subsets of network of transmitter-receiver pairs, meaning a bi-pole model, by employing geometric programming, a classical optimization approach. Tu, Saha, and Dhillon later extended this work in a recent preprint

II-D

Current contributions

We introduce a general optimization framework based on time-averaged rates. We then focus on a logarithmic utility function designed for proportional fairness. We apply the proportional fairness to a network under a scheduling scheme based on determinantal point processes. For an example, we apply this scheduler to a bi-pole network model. We recast the SINR-based coverage probability into a tractable kernel for determinantal process. We achieve this by using the tractable Palm and algebraic properties of determinantal processes.

III
Random medium access control

We consider a wireless network model as a finite set of points
{\cal{X}}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
.
By a (random)
medium access control (MAC)
scheduler, we understand a distribution of the
random subset

\Psi\subset{\cal{X}}
of nodes, which are authorized (that is, scheduled) to be simultaneously active (that is, transmitting). In practice, the MAC scheduler
\Psi
is an algorithm allowing one to sample from the probability distribution of the random set
\Psi
.

Example III.1

(Aloha)
.

The constant and adaptive Aloha schemes described in Section
I-B1

and
I-B2
are examples of a random MAC scheduler, where the random set
\Psi
is selected using
(independent) Bernoulli
sampling with constant probability
p
or variable probability
p(x_{i})=p(x_{i};{\cal{X}})

respectively.

III-A

Transmission rate and throughput

We assume that whenever a subset
\psi\subset{\cal{X}}
of nodes is simultaneously active, then the
transmission rate

\mathcal{R}_{i}(\psi)\geq 0
is established in the node
x_{i}\in{\cal{X}}
,
with
\mathcal{R}_{i}(\psi)=0
when
x_{i}\not\in\psi
. For a given MAC scheduler (distribution of)
\Psi
, we define the
throughput

\mathcal{T}_{i}
of the node
x_{i}\in{\cal{X}}
as the
average transmission rate achieved in the node
x_{i}

\mathcal{T}_{i}:=\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{R}_{i}(\Psi)].

(4)

We stress that
\mathcal{T}_{i}
corresponds to the time-average rate of transmitter
x_{i}
when the scheduler assigns transmissions in a time-stationary way with the marginal distribution of the scheduled transmissions corresponding to
\Psi
.

We now consider some examples of the rate function
\mathcal{R}_{i}
.

Example III.2
.

Consider rates of the general form

\mathcal{R}_{i}(\psi)=\bar{\mathcal{R}}_{i}g(|\psi|)\,,

(5)

where
g(n)\geq 0
is some given function,
|\psi|
is the cardinality of
\psi
and
\bar{\mathcal{R}}_{i}
are the “peak rates” of the nodes.
Case 1.

g(n)=1
corresponds to the rates obtained when the nodes are scheduled separately.

Case 2.

g(n)=1/n
gives the policy known as
round robin
.

Case 3.

g(n)=(\sum_{k=1}^{n}1/k)/n
arises when

\mathcal{R}_{i}(\psi)=\bar{\mathcal{R}}_{i}\mathbf{E}[\max_{x_{j}\in\psi}:F_{j}]/n
, for
x_{i}\in\psi
, where
F_{i}

are i.i.d. unit-mean exponential random variables.
This last case is the so-called
opportunistic
scheduling with respect to random propagation effects; see the paper by Borst
[
11
]
.

III-B

Utility and fairness

For a given network configuration
{\cal{X}}
, we can optimize the performance of the MAC scheduler
\Psi
by first considering some utility function
U
of the throughput, and then maximizing the total utility

U_{\Psi}:=\sum_{x_{i}\in{\cal{X}}}U(\mathcal{T}_{i}).

(6)

To reduce subscripts, we will often write such expressions involving sums or optimums taken over
{\cal{X}}
using just
i
, implying
U_{\Psi}=\sum_{i}U(\mathcal{T}_{i}).

Using this total utility
U_{\Psi}
we define our optimal scheduler
\Psi
.

Definition III.1

(Scheduler
\Psi
with optimal
U_{\Psi}
)
.

For a given network configuration
{\cal{X}}
, subsets
\psi\subset{\cal{X}}
, the rate functions
\mathcal{R}_{i}(\psi)
, and a concave utility function
U
, we define the optimal scheduler
\Psi
as the solution of general optimization problem

\argmax_{\mathcal{L}(\Psi)}U_{\Psi},

(7)

where the maximum is taken over all probability distributions

\mathcal{L}(\Psi)

of (random) subsets
\Psi\subset{\cal{X}}
.

In the terminology of Kelly, Maulloo and Tan
[
20
]
, we can now define a
proportionally fair
scheduler
\Psi
.

Definition III.2

(Proportionally fair scheduler
\Psi
)
.

The scheduler
\Psi
is a
proportionally fair
scheduler when it solves the optimization problem (
7
) with the logarithmic utility function
U(t)=\log(t)
. In other words, this scheduler is the solution to the optimization problem

\argmax_{\mathcal{L}(\Psi)}\sum_{i}\log(\mathcal{T}_{i})\,.

(8)

Note that any scheduler
\Psi
that systematically excludes any transmitters will have logarithmic utility
U_{\Psi}({\cal{X}})=-\infty
. Hence, a natural assumption is that
\mathbf{P}(x_{i}\in\Psi)>0

for all
x_{i}\in{\cal{X}}
. Furthermore, any Aloha scheme with
p(x_{i})>0
for all nodes
x_{i}
will have a finite utility.

Remark III.1
.

The solution set of the optimization problem (
7
) is non-empty and consists of a unique probability distribution

\mathcal{L}(\Psi)
that represents both a local and, consequently, a global maximum of
U_{\Psi}
.
This is due to the fact that the function
U_{\Psi}
is a continuous and convex function that maps from the compact, convex set of probability distributions defined on the power set
2^{\cal{X}}=\{\psi:\psi\subset{\cal{X}}\}
. For details, consult a text on (convex) optimization methods such as Boyd and Vandenberghe
[
12
]
.

III-C

Scheduler as a determinantal point process

We start by defining a determinantal point process on a discrete (finite) state space
{\cal{X}}
.

Definition III.3

(Determinantal point process)
.

For a real, positive semi-definite matrix
K
indexed by the points of a state space
{\cal{X}}
, having all its eigenvalues in the interval
[0,1]
, a determinantal point process
\Psi
on state space
{\cal{X}}

is defined by its finite-dimensional probabilities

\mathbf{P}(\Psi\supseteq\psi)=\det(K_{\psi}),

(9)

where
\det
denotes the determinant, and
K_{\psi}:=[K]_{x_{i},x_{j}\in{\psi}}
denotes the restriction of
K
to the points
\psi\subset{\cal{X}}
.

Typically, we will interpret this discrete random point process as a random subset of the underlying state space
{\cal{X}}
, which we now use to define our scheduler.

Definition III.4

(Determinantal determinantal scheduler
\Psi
)
.

We define a determinantal MAC scheduler
\Psi
as a random subset
\Psi\subset{\cal{X}}

whose distribution is a determinantal process on some underlying state space
{\cal{X}}

with some
(marginal) kernel

K
, as given in Definition
III.3
.

Remark III.2
.

The determinantal MAC scheduler is a natural extension of the adaptive Aloha of Section
I-B2
, where we consider it here for a finite network. We can see this by taking the diagonal matrix kernel

K=\mathrm{diag}[(p(x_{i}))_{x_{i}\in{\cal{X}}}]
, for any function
p(x)
, which
makes
\Psi
an (independent) Bernoulli thinning of
{\cal{X}}
.

A determinantal point process is stochastically
repulsive
, meaning that the presence of a point in a given region reduces the probability of finding another point within the same region.
Intuition suggests that the repulsive nature, so points tend not not cluster together, should lead to better performance (larger utility) of the determinantal scheduler. The existence of efficient sampling algorithms for (discrete) determinantal point processes is yet another motivation to use them as MAC schedulers.

We recall the throughput
\mathcal{T}_{i}=\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{R}_{i}(\Psi)]
and write
\mathcal{L}_{K}(\Psi)
to denote all the determinantal probability distributions on the space
{\cal{X}}
, which corresponds to all the possible determinantal kernels
K
. We now define the determinantal version of the proportionally fair scheduler defined in Definition
III.2
.

Definition III.5

(Proportionally fair, determinantal scheduler
\Psi
)
.

We define a
proportionally fair, determinantal MAC scheduler
as any solution of the optimization problem

\argmax_{\mathcal{L}_{K}(\Psi)}\sum_{i}\log(\mathcal{T}_{i})=\argmax_{K}\sum_{i}\log(\mathcal{T}_{i})\,,

(10)

where the maximum is taken over all marginal determinantal kernels
K
of the determinantal process
\Psi
on state space
{\cal{X}}
.

Remark III.3
.

For any given network
{\cal{X}}
and rate functions
\mathcal{R}_{i}(\psi)
, where
\psi\subset{\cal{X}}
, we assert that proportionally fair, determinantal
MAC schedulers exist because the expectation
\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{T}(\Psi)]
is a continuous function of the kernel
K
of
\Psi
, and the set of (symmetric, non-negative definite) matrices with bounded eigenvalues is compact. Unfortunately, the problem (
10
) is not a convex optimization problem.

III-D

Scheduler as a parametric
L
-ensemble

To render the above optimization problem convex, we restrict our attention to a practical class of determinantal kernels.

Definition III.6

(Determinantal
L
-ensembles)
.

For a real, semi-definite matrix
L
indexed by the points of a state space
{\cal{X}}
,
L
-ensemble
\Psi
on state space
{\cal{X}}
is defined as a determinantal point process with a kernel of the form

K=L(L+I)^{-1}\,.

(11)

where
K
is a determinantal kernel, as given in Definition
III.3
. Moreover, for all sets
\psi\subset{\cal{X}}
, the
L
-ensemble
\Psi
admits the probabilities

\mathbf{P}(\,\Psi=\psi\,)=\frac{\det(L_{\psi})}{\det{(L+I)}}\,,

(12)

where
L_{\psi}
denotes the restriction of
L
to the points
\psi\subset{\cal{X}}
.

Compare the determinantal probability expression (
9
) with the
L
-ensemble version given by expression (
12
), which implies the requirement

\det{(L+I)}\neq 0
. The kernel
L
has the same the eigenvectors as
K
, but its eigenvalues are equal to
\lambda_{i}/(1+\lambda_{i})
, where
\lambda_{i}
are the eigenvalues of
L
, so the eigenvalues of the matrix
L
need not be smaller than one. Any determinantal kernel
K
having all eigenvalues strictly smaller than one has the form (
11
) with
L=(I-K)^{-1}-I
. Restricting to
L
-ensembles is equivalent to assigning a nonzero probability to the empty set; see Kulesza and Taskar
[
22
, Section 2.2]
.

These determinantal
L
-ensembles
were originally studied in mathematical physics by Borodin and Rains
[
10
]
and then later introduced to machine learning by Kulesza and Taskar
[
22
]
, due to the closed-form probability expression (
12
) allowing for model training. Usually researchers assume that the matrix
L
is symmetric, but there is recent work on non-symmetric versions
[
13
,
16
]
.

We introduce a parametric version of the kernel matrix
L
studied by Kulesza and Taskar
[
22
]
. For a real, non-negative definite matrix
S
indexed by the elements of
{\cal{X}}
, we define an
L
-ensemble matrix (kernel)
L=L(S,q)
so that the latter has the matrix elements

[L]_{x_{i},x_{j}}=[L(S,q)]_{x_{i},x_{j}}:=q(x_{i})Sq(x_{j}),

(13)

where
q(\cdot)\geq 0
is some function defined on
{\cal{X}}
. The matrix
S
is called the
similarity matrix
, where the value
[S]_{x_{i},x_{j}}
measures how similar the points
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
are. In practice, often the general form
[S]_{x_{i},x_{j}}=\rho(|x_{i}-x_{j}|)
is assumed, where
\rho
is some non-negative (radial) function, giving a symmetric matrix
S
. The function (vector)
q
is the
quality
, which places a weight on each point, meaning points with higher qualities are more likely to be randomly selected. We use the term
L(S,q)
-ensemble
to refer to a determinantal point process having a kernel
K
in the form (
11
) with the matrix
L=L(S,q)
given by expression (
13
).

Example III.3
.

If the matrix
S=I
, then the resulting
L(S,q)
-ensemble is simply the independent thinning of
{\cal{X}}
with probabilities
p(x_{i})=q^{2}(x_{i})/(1+q^{2}(x_{i}))
, which brings us back to adaptive Aloha.

When training determinantal models, usually it is the quality function
q
that is trained, whereas
S
is treated as a hyperparameter, motivating another utility function. Recalling the state space cardinality
n=|{\cal{X}}|
, for a given similarity matrix
S
, we define a utility function
from the orthant
[0,\infty)^{n}
to the real line
\mathbb{R}
as

U_{S}(q):=\sum_{i}\log(\mathbf{E}[\mathcal{R}_{i}(\Psi)])=\sum_{i}\log(\mathcal{T}_{i})\,,

(14)

where the random scheduler
\Psi
is now an
L(S,q)
-ensemble.For a given matrix
S
, we write
\mathcal{L}_{q}(\Psi;S)
to denote all the parametric determinantal
L(S,q)
-ensemble distributions on the space
{\cal{X}}
, which corresponds to all the possible quality functions
q
.
We now define the
L(S,q)
-version of our proportionally fair scheduler defined in Definition
III.2
.

Definition III.7

(Proportionally fair
L(S,q^{*})
scheduler)
.

For a given
S
, a
proportionally fair
L(S,q^{*})
MAC scheduler
is any solution to the optimization problem

\argmax_{\mathcal{L}_{q}(\Psi;S)}\sum_{i}\log(\mathcal{T}_{i})=\argmax_{q\in[0,\infty)^{n}}\sum_{i}\log(\mathcal{T}_{i})\,,

(15)

When optimizing and fitting any function, we are interested whether it is convex (or concave). This motivates the next result, for which we write
e^{w}:=(e^{w(x_{i})}:x_{i}\in{\cal{X}})
for
w=(w(x_{i}):x_{i}\in{\cal{X}})\in[0,\infty)^{n}
.

Proposition III.1
.

The utility function
U_{S}(e^{w})
is a concave function of
w\in[0,\infty)^{n}
, implying any local maximum of

III-F

Learning the quality model through node features

The distribution of any proportionally fair
L(S,q^{*})
-ensemble

\Psi
, which is a solution of (
15
),
depends on the given network
{\cal{X}}
, the (optimal) quality
q^{*}=q^{*}({\cal{X}})
, and, implicitly, the rates
\mathcal{R}(\psi)
for
\psi\subset{\cal{X}}
. In other words, when
{\cal{X}}
is replaced by some
other network
{\cal{X}}^{\prime}
, then one has to
solve the optimization problem (
15
) again to obtain another

L(S,q^{*}({\cal{X}}^{\prime}))
-ensemble. In the spirit of statistical learning, we ultimately want to remove this step of optimizing for each new network
{\cal{X}}
.

On this note, it is often convenient to further approximate the
dependence
q^{*}({\cal{X}})
of the optimal
L
-ensemble on the network
{\cal{X}}
by
some more explicit relation, where the quality vectors
q(x_{i})
of
nodes
x_{i}\in{\cal{X}}
depend on some easily accessible characteristics
of the nodes
x_{i}
in the network
{\cal{X}}
via some simple
(say log-affine) function that is fitted by considering
some class of networks
{\cal{X}}
.

To this end, let
\{f_{i}({\cal{X}})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}:x_{i}\in{\cal{X}}\}
be some given, real,

M
-dimensional marks of the nodes
x_{i}\in{\cal{X}}
, called the
quality features
of nodes
x_{i}
.
We can think of them as describing the local geometry of all nodes in
{\cal{X}}
around

x_{i}
.
For each
x_{i}
, we assume that the quality vector takes the form

q(x_{i})=q(x_{i};\theta,{\cal{X}}):=e^{\theta^{\top}f(x_{i};{\cal{X}})}\,,

(16)

where the vector
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is the fitting parameter of the quality model and the vector function
f(x_{i};{\cal{X}})
is the feature vector of
x_{i}
in the network
{\cal{X}}
, giving
the scalar product
\theta^{\top}f(x_{i};{\cal{X}})
.
For example, one can take the two-element feature vector

f(x_{i};\theta,{\cal{X}})=\Bigl(1,\min_{j\not=i}(|x_{i}-y_{j}|)\Bigr).

IV
Bi-pole network model with determinantal MAC scheduling

We will now apply our determinantal MAC scheduler to a wireless network model with fixed transmitters and receivers, and random propagation effects.
Consider a fixed (that is, non-random) configuration of potential transmitters on the plane written as
{\cal{X}}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For each transmitter
x_{i}\in{\cal{X}}
, we consider a receiver located at
y_{i}
, which, in point process terminology, is simply a mark, resulting in a marked point process
\tilde{{\cal{X}}}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. This is a bi-pole model
[
4
, Chapter 16]
coupled with determinantal scheduling, which we introduced in a previous paper
[
8
, Section III]
.

We write
P_{x,y}
to denote the received power at location
y
of a signal emanating from a transmitter located at
x
. For a transmitter
x_{i}\in{\cal{X}}
, its SINR at location
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is given by

\textnormal{SINR}(x_{i},y)=\frac{P_{x_{i},y}}{W+\sum\limits_{x_{j}\in{\cal{X}}\setminus{x_{i}}}P_{x_{j},y}}\,,

(17)

where
W
denotes the noise power.
We can make some standard assumptions on the propagation model. For a signal traversing a distance
|x-y|
, we assume it undergoes path loss according to the function

\ell(x-y)=(\kappa|x-y|)^{-\beta}
, where
\kappa>0
and
\beta>0
. For any point
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let the random variable
F_{x_{i},y}
describe the fading of the signal propagating from transmitter
x_{j}
to location
y
. The resulting SINR expression is

\textnormal{SINR}(x_{i},y,{\cal{X}})=\frac{F_{x_{i},y}\ell(x_{i}-y)}{{W}+\sum\limits_{x_{j}\in{\cal{X}}\setminus{x_{i}}}F_{x_{i},y}\ell(x_{j}-y)}\,.

(18)

IV-A

Coverage probabilities under determinantal scheduler

We use a determinantal kernel
K
on the set of transmitters
{\cal{X}}
, as described in Section
III-C
. We assume that the kernel
K=K(\tilde{\cal{X}})
depends on the configuration of all potential transmitters and their receivers

\tilde{\cal{X}}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}
, but not on their fading variables.
The discrete point process
\Psi
is a determinantal thinning of the transmitter configuration
{\cal{X}}
.

For a function
f(x_{i})
, where
x_{i}\in{\cal{X}}
, we write
K\{f\}
to refer to a (matrix) kernel defined as

[K\{f\}]_{x_{i},x_{j}}:=\sqrt{1-f(x_{i})}[K]_{x_{i},x_{j}}\sqrt{1-f(x_{j})}\,.

(19)

For
z\in{\cal{X}}
, we write

K^{!}_{z}
to denote the kernel indexed by points of
{\cal{X}}\setminus\{z\}
with entries

[K_{z}^{!}]_{x_{i},y_{i}}=[K]_{x_{i},y_{i}}-\frac{[K]_{x_{i},z}[K]_{y_{i},z}}{[K]_{z,z}}\quad x_{i},x_{j}\in{\cal{X}}\setminus\{z\}.

(20)

This is the kernel of the reduced Palm version
\Psi^{!}_{z}
of
\Psi
given the point

z\in\Psi
, hence the probability

\mathbf{P}\{\Psi_{z}^{!}\cup\{z\}\supseteq\psi\}:=\mathbf{P}\{\Psi\supseteq\psi\,|\,z\in\Psi\}=\det((K^{!}_{z})_{\psi})

(21)

for
\psi\subset{\cal{X}}
. Similar results exist for conditioning on multiple points; see Borodin and Rains
[
10
, Proposition 1.2]
.

Example IV.1
.

Consider a state space or network
{\cal{X}}
consisting of three points,
{\cal{X}}=\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}
, and define a determinantal point process with the kernel
[K]_{i,j}=k_{i,j}
, where
1\leq i,j\leq 3
.
The diagonals are probabilities, meaning
0\leq k_{11},k_{22},k_{33}\leq 1
. For point
x_{1}
, the reduced Palm version of the kernel is

K_{x_{1}}^{!}=\begin{bmatrix}k_{22}&k_{23}\\
k_{23}&k_{33}\end{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{k_{11}}\begin{bmatrix}k_{12}^{2}&k_{12}k_{13}\\
k_{12}k_{13}&k_{13}^{2}\end{bmatrix}.

(22)

We now define some functions for the forthcoming result, Proposition
IV.1
, on the successful coverage probability.
For
\kappa
and
\beta
parameters of the signal propagation model, as well as the noise power
W
and SINR threshold
\tau>0
(see Section
IV
), we define the functions

\displaystyle h(s,r)

\displaystyle:=1-\frac{1}{\frac{1}{{\tau}}(s/r)^{\beta}+1}\quad s,r\geq 0,

(23)

\displaystyle w(s)

\displaystyle:=e^{-({\tau}{W})(\kappa s)^{\beta}}\quad s\geq 0.,

(24)

where of
s,r>0
. These functions come from a result that we presented in a previous paper
[
8
, Lemma II.1]
. They are useful for expressing the SINR coverage probability in deterministic network models with Rayleigh fading and power-law path loss function.
Furthermore, for a point
x_{i}\in{\cal{X}}
define the functions

h_{x_{i}}(x_{j}):=h(|x_{j}-y_{i}|,|x_{i}-y_{i}|),\quad x_{j}\in{\cal{X}}\setminus\{x_{i}\}

(25)

and constants
W_{x_{i}}:=w(|x_{i}-y_{i}|)
.

Now consider determinantal scheduler
\Psi
with kernel
K
on the network configuration
{\cal{X}}
. We now present a corresponding result, which we first presented in the paper
[
8
, Proposition IV.1.]
with the proof
[
8
, Appendix A]
.

Proposition IV.1
.

For given
x_{i}\in{\cal{X}}
and
\tau\geq 0
, the distribution of the SINR at
y_{i}
for a signal coming from
x_{i}
, given
x_{i}
is selected by the scheduler,
is obtained with expression

\mathbf{P}\{\,\textnormal{SINR}(x_{i},y_{i},\Psi)>\tau\,|\,x_{i}\in\Psi\,\}=\det(I-K^{!}_{x_{i}}\{h_{x_{i}}\})W_{x_{i}},

(26)

where the probability above also incorporates the random fading conditions and scheduler decisions.

We now consider the unconditional SINR (tail) distribution at
y_{i}
from
x_{i}\in{\cal{X}}
, namely

\mathcal{P}_{i}(\tau):=\mathbf{P}\{\,x_{i}\in\Psi\text{\ and\ \ }\textnormal{SINR}(x_{i},y_{i},\Psi)>\tau\,\},\quad\tau>0.

(27)

Then we have an expression for the coverage probability
\mathcal{P}_{i}
.

Corollary IV.2
.

The coverage probability is given by

\mathcal{P}_{i}(\tau)=[K]_{x_{i},x_{i}}\det(I-K^{!}_{x_{i}}\{h_{x_{i}}\})W_{x_{i}}.

(28)

Proof.

Note that
\mathbf{P}\{x_{i}\in\Psi\}=\det(K_{\{x_{i}\}})=[K]_{x_{i},x_{i}}
. Then we use Proposition
IV.1
by conditioning on
x_{i}\in\Psi
.
∎

The matrix
(I-K^{!}_{x_{i}})\{h_{x_{i}}\}
on the right-hand-side of (

{\cal{X}}\setminus\{x_{i}\}
. We extend it to the full configuration

by setting

[K_{x_{i}}(\tau)]_{x_{j},x_{k}}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}[I-K_{x_{i}}^{!}\{h_{x_{i}}\}]_{x_{j},x_{k}}&\mbox{if }j,k\not=i\\

\mathcal{P}_{i}(\tau)=\det(K_{x_{i}}(\tau)).

IV-B

Transmission rates and throughput

For our bi-pole model, we are interested in quantifying the communication rate of a wireless link between a transmitter
x_{i}
and its receiver
y_{i}
.
For a transmitter-and-receiver pair
(x_{i},y_{i})
, we introduce the
constant rate
model as

\mathcal{R}_{i}:=\mathcal{R}_{\tau,R_{0}}(x_{i},y_{i},{\cal{X}}):=R_{0}\mathbf{P}[\,\textnormal{SINR}(x_{i},y_{i},{\cal{X}})>{\tau}\,]\,,

(31)

where
R_{0}>0
is a constant and
{\tau}
is a fixed SINR threshold;
the probability corresponds to random fading variables.
We introduce the
variable rate
model as

\mathcal{R}_{r}(x_{i},y_{i},{\cal{X}}):=\mathbf{E}[r(\textnormal{SINR}(x_{i},y_{i},{\cal{X}}))]\,,

(32)

where
r
is some non-negative function that maps from the non-negative real line, that is,
r:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
. The above expectation corresponds to random fading variables.
Based on Shannon’s work, a natural choice is
r(t)=C\log(t+1)
, where the constant
C>0
.

The explicit expressions (
28
) and (
30
) for the coverage probability
\mathcal{P}_{i}(\tau)
immediately imply the following representation of the user throughput defined by the general expression (
4
).

Corollary IV.3
.

The throughput
\mathcal{T}_{i}
of
x_{i}
is given by

\mathcal{T}_{i}=R_{0}\mathcal{P}_{i}(\tau)\,,

(33)

in the case of constant rate function (
31
)
and by

\mathcal{T}_{i}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}_{i}(r^{-1}(\tau))\,d\tau

(34)

in the case of variable rate function (
32
), provided the inverse
r^{-1}

of the rate function
r
exists.

Remark IV.1
.

For the time-averaged transmission rate
\mathcal{T}_{i}=\mathbf{E}[R_{i}(\Psi)]
, the constant rate function (
31
)
gives the expression

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{i}\in{\cal{X}}}\log(\mathcal{T}_{i})

\displaystyle=m\log R_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log\lambda_{x_{i},j}(\tau)

(35)

where
\lambda_{x_{i},j}(\tau)
is
j
-th eigenvalue of the matrix
K_{x_{i}}(\tau)
defined in equation (
29
).
(Recall that a determinant of a square matrix is equal to the product of its eigenvalues.)
We do not know whether this expression can
help solve the problem of the proportionally fair determinantal scheduler (

IV-C

Controlling medium access and separating transmissions

We recall that an
L
-ensemble
\Psi
with matrix
L
satisfies
\mathbf{P}\{\,\Psi=\psi\,\}={\det L_{\psi}}/{\det(L+I)}
. Consequently, the probability of the configuration
\psi=\{x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{k}}\}\in{\cal{X}}
being scheduled for the transmission is equal to

\mathbf{P}\{\,\psi=\Psi\,\}=\frac{1}{\det(L+I)}\prod_{x_{i}\in\psi}q^{2}(x_{i})\det(S_{\psi}).

(36)

In expression (
36
), the quality-squared term

q^{2}(x_{i})
controls the medium access probability of each node
x_{i}
, whereas the matrix
S
controls the separation of
different transmissions. The term

\det(L+I)
is just the normalizing constant.

For the matrix
S
, we can use,
for example, the
Gaussian kernel

[S]_{x_{i},x_{j}}:=e^{-|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{2}/\sigma^{2}},

(37)

where the scale parameter
\sigma
controls separation of transmitting nodes.
As
\sigma\rightarrow 0
, the matrix
S
converges to the identity matrix, giving the (adaptive) Aloha model.

Numerical example

Using our fairness framework, we present a numerical example of the bi-pole network model outlined in Section
IV

3

.

We compared the determinantal model to the classic Aloha model (with fixed access probability) and the adaptive Aloha model (with varying access probability).
For each network layout, we maximized its total logarithmic utility (or fairness) with the rate set as the coverage probability
\mathcal{P}_{i}
given by equation (
30
). For the two Aloha models, the similarity matrix
S
is simply the
n\times n
identity matrix, where
n

S
kernel (
37

n

VI
Conclusion

We have presented an adaptive determinantal scheduling framework for wireless networks. By formulating the scheduling problem within the context of proportional fairness and leveraging
L
-ensembles, we have demonstrated that determinantal processes offer a mathematically elegant and computationally tractable approach for network scheduling.
Furthermore, the proposed approach is adaptable to various network configurations without requiring repeated optimization for each new network deployment.

Promising directions for future research emerge from this work.

Training on pre-optimized networks
: Using pre-optimized networks to trains models with determinantal fairness framework is the first obvious step.

Multi-objective optimization:
Incorporating additional performance metrics beyond proportional fairness, such as energy efficiency, delay constraints, or quality-of-service requirements, could lead to more comprehensive scheduling solutions that balance multiple competing objectives.

Distributed scheduling:
Developing decentralized versions of determinantal scheduling algorithms would aid their deployment in large-scale networks where centralized control is impractical.
Leveraging machine learning:
Further exploration of the connection between determinantal processes and machine learning techniques could yield powerful approaches.

Theoretical performance bounds:
Establishing theoretical guarantees on the performance gap between optimal scheduling and determinantal approximations would provide valuable insights into the fundamental limits of determinantal scheduling approaches.

In conclusion, determinantal point processes offer a promising mathematical foundation for wireless network scheduling that balances computational tractability with sophisticated spatial modelling.
