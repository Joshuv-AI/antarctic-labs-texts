Deep brain stimulation of the nucleus basalis of Meynert in severe Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Junpeng Xu, Bin Liu, Guosong Shang, Shuzhen Liu, Zhebin Feng, Haonan Yang, Di Liu, Qing Chang, Yuhan Chen, Xinguang Yu, Zhiqi Mao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlzheimer's disease (AD) is increasingly prevalent, leading to severe cognitive decline and a diminished quality of life for patients. Nucleus basalis of Meynert deep brain stimulation (NBM-DBS) is a potential treatment approach.ObjectiveThis study aims to assess the efficacy and safety of NBM-DBS for AD patients.MethodsWe conducted a clinical study involving 6 patients with severe AD who received NBM-DBS. The treatment's safety and efficacy were evaluated using cognitive function tests (Mini-Mental State Examination, Montreal Cognitive Assessment, Alzheimer's Disease Rating Scale- cognitive subscale, Clinical Dementia Rating) and assessments of neuropsychiatric symptoms and sleep disorders (Functional Activity Questionnaire, Functional Independence Measure, Zarit Burden Interview, Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale, Hamilton Depression Rating Scale, Neuropsychiatric Inventory, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index).ResultsNBM-DBS was safe, with no severe adverse events. It improved cognitive functions and self-care abilities without altering the disease's progression. Notably, NBM-DBS significantly alleviated neuropsychiatric symptoms and sleep disturbances.ConclusionsNBM-DBS could be a promising therapeutic approach for severe AD, particularly for managing neuropsychiatric symptoms and sleep disorders. Further research is warranted to confirm these preliminary findings.Keywords: cognitive impairment, deep brain stimulation, neuropsychiatric symptoms, severe Alzheimer's disease, sleep disorders

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the leading cause of cognitive impairment and dementia. 1 The prevalence of AD has increased from 0.2% in people aged 55–59 to 48.2% in people aged 95–99 2 and 2.0–16.8 new AD per 1000 person-years.3–7 The deaths due to dementia have increased steadily over time, partly due to population growth and population aging, doubling from 1990 to 2016, with dementia accounting for 4.4% of all deaths in 2016. Dementia is the fifth leading cause of death, 8 and the median survival time of patients with AD dementia is approximately seven years. 9 In addition to typical symptoms such as cognitive decline and progressive decline in intelligence and memory, approximately 8–13% have visual or motor impairments, 7–9% have language impairments, and 2% have atypical symptoms such as neuropsychiatric symptoms and sleep disorders. 10 AD has become a primary brain disease that seriously affects the health of people in our country and even around the world. But no effective clinical means to delay or reverse the progression of AD. In the past, drug development that focused on breaking down amyloid-β (Aβ) and denatured and aggregated tau protein in the brain has repeatedly failed. Two classes of drugs approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to treat AD, alkaline esterase inhibitors and N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonists, can only temporarily improve cognitive symptoms, but not reverse the disease process.11,12 Research shows that single-target drug treatment is impossible to treat AD. In the future, we should do in-depth research on the causative factors of AD neurodegeneration to find new ways to treat AD. In addition, the studies are all aimed at patients with mild to moderate AD, but few treatments slow down the disease process in severe AD. At the same time, the treatment methods are mainly to treat the symptoms of cognitive function decline and intellectual disability. Currently, there are no studies on improving the neuropsychiatric symptoms and caregiving burden of severely ill patients. With the continuous research on the pathogenesis and treatment of brain diseases such as AD by the world's “Brain Project” and the development of new technologies such as neuroimaging, and neuromodulation, the clinical diagnosis, treatment, and mechanism research of AD have ushered in new opportunities. Studies have shown that there are extensive neural circuit abnormalities in the brains of AD patients, which affect a variety of cortical and subcortical connections, especially those involved in cognition and memory. It is a brain network connection to abnormal neurodegenerative diseases caused by various reasons. Given this, the rapid development of deep brain stimulation (DBS) in recent years has proposed a new and promising solution to this problem. So far, three different locations have been tried as targets for improving memory and cognition using DBS in AD, namely the nucleus basalis of Meynert (NBM), 13 the fornix, 14 and the ventral capsule /ventral striatum region. 15 Among them, NBM-DBS can modulate specific neural circuits and networks related to cognition and has shown the potential to treat mild to moderate AD, but few studies have focused on its use in severe AD. The application in patients with mutism is mainly focused on the regulation research of animal models. At the same time, some case reports have been reported, but no definite conclusion can be drawn.16–18

The NBM has extensive projections and is involved in regulating memory, attention, and arousal states. In humans, the NBM (Ch4) is located in the posterior region of the basal forebrain, extending horizontally from the olfactory tubercle along the midline anteroposteriorly to the uncal gyrus of the hippocampus. The NBM is a major region of the brain's cholinergic system and an important component of circuits in the basal ganglia. It is composed of major neurons that provide acetylcholine projections to the neocortex and plays an essential role in the regulation of subcortical memory, attention, and arousal states. An essential role in supporting normal cognitive function. As AD progresses, NBM suffers from the loss of neurons, which are key components of the cholinergic system. Pathological changes in tau protein appear in NBM during the progression of AD. Degeneration of cholinergic neurons in the NBM is one of the characteristic pathological changes of AD and are an important cause of functional impairment in AD patients. NBM is a promising target. 19 However, there are still some leading issues that need to be resolved. In addition, most NBM-DBS studies have been conducted in patients with mild to moderate AD. However, the actual situation is that the quality of life of patients with severe AD is more seriously affected, and the need for effective treatment is more urgent. Based on this, we conducted a study to verify the efficacy of NBM-DBS in the treatment of patients with late-stage AD, not only for cognitive function decline and intellectual disability but also for the first time to discuss the improvement of neuropsychiatric symptoms and the burden on caregivers.

Methods

Ethical approval and trial registration

This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethical Committee of Chinese PLA General Hospital (S2015-013-02). The findings of the study were authorized in peer-reviewed journals with online access.

All participants provided informed consent in writing.

The trial is registered as ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03115814) on April 14, 2017.

Inclusion of patients

We published recruitment information through the hospital's official platform and conducted a preliminary screening of registered patients through telephone and video connections. Those who passed the initial testing went to the hospital's Functional Neurosurgery Clinic for further evaluation by the Chief and Associate Neurosurgeons of the Department of Neurosurgery. After further evaluation and screening, those who passed the screening were admitted to the hospital, and relevant perioperative investigations were performed to define indications for surgery and rule out contraindications. After the above work, the research assistant will talk to the eligible patients, sign the informed consent form, and enroll the patients while ensuring that the patients are fully informed.

Participants

We included six patients, five women and one man, aged between 59 and 78, in compliance with the National Institute of Neurological and Related Disorders Association's AD diagnostic criteria. 20 All subjects were diagnosed with severe AD, with a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score of <10 points and a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) level of 3.21,22 The clinical characteristics of 6 patients with severe AD in the study are shown in Table 1. The average age of the patients at the time of surgery was 57.17 ± 5.8 years, the average disease duration was 4.67 ± 2.50 years, and the average education level was 11.00 ± 3.10 years. The average preoperative MMSE was 5.00 ± 2.19, the average Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) was 4.33 ± 1.86, and the CDR classification was all grade 3. Before surgery, six patients (100.00%) all showed severe memory loss, intellectual disability, and personality changes. Among them, two cases showed impulsivity and aggression, and five cases showed depression and apathy. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed that all six patients had severe atrophy of the whole brain, especially in the bilateral hippocampus. Positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) showed that all six patients had reduced glucose metabolism and Aβ deposition.

Intervention

The patient was installed with a Leksecl-G directional device under local anesthesia and entered the CT room for a CT positioning scan; the robotic surgical planning system selected the NBM as the target for electrode implantation by calculating the acquired CT and MRI data as surgical planning (Figure 1). Following the guidance, the skull entry point was located, the scalp was incised, and the skull was drilled. In the right basal ganglia, the electrode catheter needle and PINS L-301 electrode were implanted 0 mm above the target point pulled out the outer sleeve and secured the electrode. Using the same method, the PINS L-301 electrode was implanted 0 mm above the target point. The intraoperative MRI scan showed that the electrode position was accurate. The patient was removed from the orientation frame, covered with a sterile draping, and a 1-cm skin incision was made under the right clavicle. The skin was incised, separated to create a “pocket”, and a small incision was made behind the upper edge of the right auricle. Using a puncture strip, a tunnel was made, the electrode extension wire was placed into the subcutaneous tunnel. The bilateral intracranial implanted electrodes were connected under the scalp to the upper end of the electrode connection line, and the electrode connection was locked and fixed in the bone groove (fixed with a titanium connecting piece). After connecting and locking the PINS G102RZ rechargeable DBS neurostimulator to the lower end of the electrode cable, it was placed into the right subclavian bag, and the system was tested to be sure it was running normally, with impedances below 2000 ohms. The scalp and subclavian were then sutured, respectively.

Surgery planning on Remebot.

Postoperative program control

The patients were systematically followed up and evaluated after the operation. Personalized stimulation parameters were given according to the evaluation results of each patient. The contacts were stimulated in the form of unipolar stimulation. Each electrode had four contacts, the stimulation frequency was 20 HZ, the pulse width was 60–90 μs (low-frequency excitation, high-frequency inhibition), and the voltage was 2–3 V. The patient was observed for 30 min before and after stimulation, communicating with, and observing the patient's words, deeds, and communication to comprehensively determine whether the stimulation is effective through emotional changes, memory, calculation ability, language communication, orientation, etc., to determine the therapeutic window and adverse reactions of each electric shock on the electrode, and to confirm that symptoms had improved but there are no adverse effects. Our primary objective during the turn-on process is to assess the presence of any stimulation-related complications, such as emotional fluctuations, abnormal movements, headaches, or other adverse reactions. The reaction contacts and parameters were used as stimulation contacts, then the unipolar stimulation mode was maintained, and the stimulation parameters were the same as before. Outpatient follow-up visits were conducted three months, six months, nine months, and one year after startup, and the stimulation parameters were adjusted and tested according to the above methods.

Assessments

Baseline assessments were performed preoperatively, and postoperative follow-up assessments at one, three, six, nine, and twelve months postoperatively. The MMSE, MoCA, Alzheimer's Disease Rating Scale-cognitive subscale (ADAS-Cog), and the CDR were the primary outcome measure. In addition, the Functional Activity Questionnaire (FAQ), Functional Independence Measure (FIM), Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI), Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAM-A), Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D), Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI), Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) scale were used with patient and family satisfaction as secondary outcome measures. It should be noted that since stimulation was performed one month after surgery, the assessment was one month after the operation but one day before stimulation. Evaluators ask patients whether they have any adverse reactions during each evaluation, such as intracranial hemorrhage, surgical site infection, emotional and motor dysfunction, skin itching, dizziness, tinnitus, fatigue, drowsiness, acute mood changes, and other adverse reactions and side effects.

Results

All patients (mean age 57.17 years ± 5.8 years) completed DBS implantation. All patients tolerated the surgery well, and there were no adverse reactions or side effects related to the treatment. No loss occurred during the follow-up period. Five of the six family members of the patients were satisfied with the treatment results and believed there were no improvements in the patient's cognitive function but improved neuropsychiatric symptoms and sleep disorders, which also greatly reduced the caregiver's psychological burden and emotional impact. During the follow-up period, clinical data of six patients at various time points (preoperative, one, three, six, nine, and twelve months) were collected. In our observations, Sub2 recalled having children after treatment, although she was still unable to recall their names. Sub1, Sub4, and Sub5 showed emotional improvements, engaging in simple conversations with family members and doctors. While they were still unable to comprehend and express accurately, they were no longer agitated, fearful of people, or unresponsive to external stimuli as before the surgery. In contrast, Sub3 and Sub6 did not demonstrate significant improvements initially upon the start of stimulation. All six patients did not exhibit severe adverse reactions during the turn-on process (Table 2).

Effects of DBS on cognitive function in severe AD

The average MMSE scores of the six patients who participated in all follow-up examinations at all time points (baseline and 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months) were 5.00 ± 2.19, 4.50 ± 4.58, 8.00 ± 3.84, 6.17 ± 4.26, 5.50 ± 4.04, and 4.33 ± 2.73. The MoCA scores were 4.33 ± 1.86, 4.33 ± 1.75, 7.00 ± 4.24, 5.83 ± 4.02, 4.33 ± 3.14, and 3.67 ± 2.50. The ADAS-Cog scores were 51.50 ± 10.50, 51.33 ± 11.81, 45.50 ± 16.36, 49.00 ± 14.09, 50.17 ± 12.42, and 52.33 ± 11.84. The CDR scores are all 3.00. The single-factor repeated measures analysis of variance method found no statistically significant difference in the average cognitive scores between each time point (p > 0.05). Further comparison of any two-time points showed that compared with the baseline, there was improvement only at the 3-month follow-up, and the improvement was not obvious at other periods (Table 2, Figures 2 and 3). The above results indicate that NBN-DMS can improve the cognitive function of patients with severe AD in the short term, but long-term follow-up found that it failed to change the disease progression and outcome.

Effect of DBS on quality of life and caregiver burden in advanced AD

The average BI scores of the six patients who participated in all follow-up examinations at all time points (i.e., baseline and 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months) were 59.17 ± 15.94, 58.33 ± 17.51, 63.50 ± 18.10, 59.17 ± 17.73, 55.83 ± 15.94, and 52.50 ± 17.75. The FAQ scores were 27.83 ± 1.33, 27.83 ± 1.60, 26.67 ± 1.63, 27.67 ± 1.97, 28.17 ± 1.94, and 28.33 ± 2.07. The FIM scores were 68.00 ± 20.07, 69.00 ± 22.95, 74.67 ± 28.54, 72.67 ± 26.67, 67.33 ± 30.66, and 63.50 ± 26.44 respectively. The single-factor repeated measures analysis of variance method found no statistically significant difference in the average cognitive scores between each time point (p > 0.05) (Table 2, Figures 4 and 5). However, a notable finding emerged from our analysis of the ZBI scores, which assess the caregiver burden. The ZBI scores were 51.67 ± 6.74, 50.17 ± 7.78, 44.33 ± 9.07, 45.33 ± 9.05, 45.17 ± 2.28, and 46.00 ± 9.19. The single-factor repeated measures analysis of variance method was used to find that the difference in average cognitive scores between each time point was statistically significant (p < 0.05) (Table 2, Figures 4D and 5D). The above results indicate that NBN-DMS can improve the daily living activities of patients with severe AD in the short term, and long-term follow-up found that it failed to change the disease progression and outcome. After long-term follow-up, it could significantly reduce the burden on the caregiver. After further communication with the patient's family, it was found this improvement was due to the relief of the patient's neuropsychiatric symptoms. This finding is particularly meaningful, as caregiver burden is a significant contributor to the overall societal impact of AD. The relief of neuropsychiatric symptoms observed in some patients, as confirmed through communication with their families, likely contributed to this reduction in caregiver burden. Therefore, while NBN-DMS did not improve cognitive outcomes in this study, it did demonstrate the potential to alleviate an important aspect of the disease's burden on caregivers.

Discussion

Regarding the treatment of AD, there are currently no effective measures. This is mainly because AD is a complex disease with multiple pathophysiological characteristics. Based on different pathogenesis and pathogenic hypotheses, domestic and foreign scholars have carried out a large number of studies on AD treatment clinical trials. 23 New anti-AD monoclonal antibody drugs based on bapineuzumab, 24 gantenerumab, 25 and lecanemab that clear Aβ protein have been proven to only reduce Aβ deposition in the brain, but most of the new drugs have clinical trials that did not reach the effective endpoint event, still could not prevent the progression of AD, and failed to achieve the purpose of improving cognitive function.26–28 Although methylene blue (leuco-methylthioninium bis, LMTM), which inhibits the aggregation and formation of tau protein, has been proven to improve the cognitive level of subjects, this result may be related to different baseline cognitive levels of subjects. Acceptance standards of the cognitive level of patients treated with anti-AD is poor, and most patients treated with this type of drugs are in the early stages of the disease.29,30 Phase II clinical trials of second-generation tau protein aggregation inhibitors TRx0237, gosuranemab, tilavonemab, and zagotenemab have shown no clinical benefit for patients with mild AD and have not achieved the purpose of improving cognitive function.31,32 The results of phase II clinical trial of AADvac1, a vaccine that removes pathological tau protein from the brain, shows that it is tolerable to patients with mild AD. The efficacy and safety are good, but the effectiveness still needs to be verified by further large-scale clinical trials. 33 Drugs based on inhibiting neuroinflammation, such as daratumumab, NE3107, and masitinib, have been preliminarily proven to be able to improve the cognitive function of patients with mild AD, but further verification in large-sample clinical trials is needed.34–38 Research-based on improving metabolic disorders with metformin, glucagonlike peptide-1, GLP-1, CYP46A1, etc., is ongoing and has not been further verified.39,40 In addition, other treatment measures, such as dietary adjustment and aerobic exercise to treat AD, are also being actively explored, but the efficacy is not yet clear.41,42 With the continuous research on the pathogenesis and treatment of serious brain diseases such as AD by the world's “Brain Project” and the development of new technologies such as neuroimaging and neuromodulation, the clinical diagnosis, treatment and mechanism research of AD have ushered in new opportunities. A large number of studies have shown that there are extensive neural circuit abnormalities in the brains of AD patients, which affect a variety of cortical and subcortical connections, especially those involved in cognition and memory. It is a brain network connection abnormal neurodegenerative diseases caused by a variety of reasons.43,44 Given this, the rapid development of DBS in recent years has proposed a new and promising solution to this problem. So far, three different locations have been tried as targets for improving memory and cognition using DBS in AD, namely the NBM, the fornix, and the ventral capsule/ventral striatum region. Among them, NBM DBS can modulate specific neural circuits and networks related to cognition and has shown the potential to treat mild to moderate AD, but few studies have focused on its use in severe AD. The application in patients with mutism is currently mainly focused on the regulation research of animal models. At the same time, some case reports have been conducted, but no definite conclusion can be drawn. By searching the literature (Table 3), it was found that in 1985, Turnbull et al. 45 first tried to treat AD through low-frequency electrical stimulation of Meynert's basal ganglia. During the 9-month postoperative clinical observation, although the researchers did not see any significant improvement in memory or cognitive function, it was found that electrical stimulation of Meynert's basal ganglia can delay the decline in glucose metabolism in some brain areas. In 2015, Kuhn et al. 16 reported that 6 cases of mild to moderate AD were treated with DBS targeting the basal ganglia of Meynert. The hippocampal amygdala metabolism levels and cognitive scores of 4 patients were significantly improved, suggesting that DBS targeting the basal ganglia of Meynert is available. In the same year, Kuhn et al. 43 reported using DBS targeting the basal ganglia of Meynert to treat two cases of mild to moderate AD. After 2 years of follow-up, they found that stabilizing cognitive decline was more effective in younger, less affected patients. Previous clinical studies of DBS-NBM in the treatment of AD have focused on improving cognitive symptoms in mild to moderate patients. In 2021, Zhang et al. 18 reported an 80-year-old patient with severe AD whose clinical symptoms improved after 10 weeks of NBM-DBS treatment. In 2022, Zhang et al. 46 reported on 8 patients with advanced AD who received bilateral NBM-DBS. After 12 months of follow-up, they found that NBM-DBS improved the short-term cognitive ability of patients with advanced AD, but the cognitive function of AD patients increased from baseline to There were no changes at 12-month follow-up. 46 However, the current relevant clinical and basic research is not enough to conclude that Meynert's basal ganglia is an effective or optimal target for DBS in the treatment of AD, and further research is still needed to confirm. Most of the previous NBM-DBS-related research subjects were mainly mild to moderate AD patients. Although Zhang et al. conducted some studies on moderate to severe patients, they all focused on improving the cognitive function of the patients and ignored AD. The impact of accompanying neuropsychiatric symptoms, sleep disorders, and loss on the quality of life and psychological burden of patients and their families, 47 in addition to patients with late-stage AD who are accompanied by severe neuropsychiatric symptoms and sleep disorders, patients with atypical symptoms of AD are also an important group that cannot be ignored. The only studies show that such patients account for one-third of early-onset AD patients, while 6% of late-onset AD patients.48–52 However, atypical late-onset patients may be unlikely to be referred to neurology centers in time, but are mainly distributed in psychiatric departments. Due to the lack of systematic diagnosis and treatment, many patients are already suffering from severe AD by the time they are diagnosed. Although the proportion of patients with atypical disease may be lower in the elderly than in younger adults, the greater number of patients with late-onset AD suggests that the absolute number of atypical cases may be higher in older adults than in younger adults. Few studies have compared early-onset versus late-onset atypical AD.

Based on the above situation, in addition to studying the typical symptoms of the patients themselves, this study also systematically explored the intervention and treatment effects of NBM- DBS on atypical symptoms. This is the first phase 1 study to systematically and comprehensively explore the technical feasibility of NBM-DBS in treating late-stage severe AD patients’ cognitive function, daily living ability, neuropsychiatric symptoms, and caregiver burden. During its 1-year follow-up, no serious adverse reactions related to DBS itself have been found, so it can be preliminarily considered that this technology is safe when applied to patients with late-stage severe AD. In response to the above-mentioned related problems, this study considers that the prevention and treatment of AD should not only focus on the prevention and treatment of core symptoms such as a cognitive decline in patients but also pay attention to its accompanying neuropsychiatric symptoms and atypical symptoms such as sleep disorders and their impact on patients’ families and families should be treated with multi-faceted, multi-channel, multi-angle interventions, and treatments for AD, especially for patients with severe AD, striving to improve cognitive function. While reducing the symptoms, active intervention will be carried out on related accompanying symptoms to minimize the impact on the patient's family and family in all aspects. After following up with the patients and their families, it was found that the patients’ cognition and quality of life were improving during the observation. There was no change during this period, but family care burden and neuropsychiatric symptoms such as depression, mania, and sleep disorders were significantly improved. We evaluated them mainly through the NPI and the HAM-D. Regarding NPI, we will dissect the contributions from its 12 domains, pinpointing the key factors driving the overall score reduction, thereby revealing the targeted therapeutic effects of DBS. Specifically, the NBM-DBS treatment has led to improvements in the subdomains such as Agitation/Aggression, Depression/Dysphoria, Anxiety, Apathy/Anhedonia, Disinhibition, Abnormal Motor Behavior, and Sleep/Nocturnal Behavior. As for HAM-D, the dramatic decrease in scores for five participants, transitioning them from moderate to severe depression into a mild depressive state, underscores the potential of DBS in alleviating depressive symptoms. Substantial enhancements were noted in subdomains including Depressed Mood, Insomnia, Work and Interests, Psychomotor Retardation, Agitation, Paranoid Symptoms, Diurnal Variation, Insight, and Depersonalization or Derealization. Furthermore, we will delve into the correlations between depressive symptom improvement and ancillary factors like caregiver burden alleviation and cognitive function enhancement, providing a holistic assessment of the treatment's far-reaching implications.

Further communication with family members revealed that the reduction in caregiver burden may be due to the improvement in the patient's neuropsychiatric symptoms and sleep disorders. Caregiver burden, a critical factor influencing the societal impact of the disease, is a multifaceted strain on physical, emotional, and financial health. By addressing this burden, we preserve caregivers’ well-being, sustain positive caregiving relationships, and enhance cost-effectiveness by enabling longer home-based care. It also promotes social inclusion and indirectly improves patient outcomes through better care quality. Furthermore, addressing caregiver burden propels research and practice forward, leading to more comprehensive support systems. In essence, improving caregiver burden is paramount to optimizing care for AD patients and their caregivers alike.

At the same time, this study also has many shortcomings. In this study, the group size of only 6 patients significantly reduced its statistical power, making the results still not generalizable to widespread clinical use. Continuing to use drugs during treatment is likely to have confounding effects, but considering patient health and ethical reasons, this flaw cannot be avoided because, in this preliminary study, DBS was only studied as an add-on therapy. The robust evidence from animal studies indicating the superiority of intermittent stimulation patterns over continuous 20 Hz pulse trains.54–56 While intermittent patterns may not have been within our initial scope when designing the study, we now recognize their potential importance and will incorporate this into our discussion. We will discuss the potential benefits of exploring alternate stimulation paradigms, such as intermittent or burst stimulation, in future investigations, emphasizing their potential to modulate treatment outcomes more favorably. Furthermore, we will reference the recent work by Sanskriti and Fasano in “Brain Stimulation,” highlighting the key findings and implications for our field. 57 This discussion will underscore the ongoing need for research into optimized stimulation protocols to maximize therapeutic efficacy and minimize adverse effects.58,59 In addition, due to the particularity of patients with severe AD, it is difficult to follow up on them. Many follow-up visits need to be completed with the cooperation of the patient's family members, which cannot represent the patient's intentions. More will be needed in the future. Objective standards such as PET-CT and brain imaging analysis are used for further verification.

Conclusion

The results of this study show that NBM-DBS can improve the cognitive ability of patients with advanced AD in a short period. Meynert basal ganglia low-frequency DBS is safe and reversible. No serious adverse or side effects occurred during the treatment process. The cognitive function and self-care ability can be improved in the short term but cannot change their final clinical direction and outcome. In addition, we also found that this treatment can significantly improve the cognitive function and self-care ability of patients with severe AD. The patient's neuropsychiatric symptoms and sleep disorders can be improved while reducing the caregiver's burden. There may be a clinical potential for DBS in patients with severe AD application, and more relevant research is needed to demonstrate its effectiveness and mechanism of action, which may promote the adjustment of the clinical application of new stimulation modes.
