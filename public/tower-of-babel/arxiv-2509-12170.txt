1.
Introduction

Let
\xi
be a random variable with zero mean and unit variance. Consider the Kac polynomial

P_{n}(x):=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\xi_{k}x^{k},

where
\xi_{k}
are i.i.d. copies of
\xi
. For any
D\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}
, let
N_{P_{n}}(D)
be the number of roots of
P_{n}
(counting multiplicity) in
D
.

The Kac polynomial is a classical model of random polynomials and has received renewed interest in the last two decades; see, for example, the very incomplete list
[
BRS86
,
Far96
,
SV95
,
IZ97
,
SZ03
,
TV10
,
KZ13
,
OW19
,
Mic21
,
NV22
,
NN24
,
MS24
,
MY25a
]
and the references therein. The study of real zeros of Kac polynomials dates back to Bloch and Polya
[
BP32
]
who studied the case when
\xi
is uniformly distributed in
\{-1,0,1\}
and established the upper bound

\mathbf{E}N_{n}=O(n^{1/2}).

In a series of breakthrough papers
[
LO43
,
LO45
,
LO48
]

in the early 1940s, Littlewood and Offord proved (for many atom variables

\xi
such as Gaussian, Rademacher (uniform on
\{-1,1\}
), or uniform on
[-1,1]
) that the number of real roots is bounded above and below by a polylogarithmic function of
n
with high probability.

The first precise asymptotic was obtained in Kac’s seminal papers
[
Kac43
]
, which introduced the now well-known Kac–Rice formula

{\mathbb{E}}N_{n}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|y|\,p(t,0,y)\,dy,

where
p(t,x,y)
is the joint probability density for
(P_{n}(t),P_{n}^{\prime}(t))=(x,y)
. For Gaussian coefficients, this formula can be evaluated explicitly to yield

{\mathbb{E}}N_{n}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{1}{(t^{2}-1)^{2}}+\frac{(n+1)^{2}t^{2n}}{(t^{2n+2}-1)^{2}}}\,dt=\left(\frac{2}{\pi}+o(1)\right)\log n.

Evaluating the Kac-Rice formula for non-Gaussian coefficients is notoriously difficult as it involves integrating the joint density of
P_{n}
and
P_{n}^{\prime}
. For discrete distributions, Erdős and Offord
[
EO56
]
in 1956 developed a completely different method – combining combinatorial arguments with a connection between real roots and sign changes – to handle the Rademacher case in which
\xi_{i}
takes values
\pm 1
with equal probability. They proved that with probability
1-o\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log\log n}}\right)
,

N_{n,\xi}=\frac{2}{\pi}\log n+o\!\left(\log^{2/3}n\,\log\log n\right).

In the late 1960s and early 1970s, Ibragimov and Maslova
[
IM68
,
IM71
]
extended this method and proved that
{\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}}({\mathbb{R}})=(1+o(1))\frac{2}{\pi}\log n
is universal if
\xi
has mean zero and belongs to the domain of attraction of the normal law.

In the last two decades, the method of universality was developed in random matrix theory and subsequently adopted for random polynomials with the pioneering work by Tao and Vu
[
TV14
]
. Developing this method, Do, Vu, and the second author
[
DNV18
]
showed that for any
\xi
with mean zero, variance one, and a finite
(2+\varepsilon_{0})
–moment for some
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, the error term is indeed of order
O(1)
:

{\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}}({\mathbb{R}})=\frac{2}{\pi}\log n+O(1).

While the leading-order behavior of the expected number of real roots is now known to be universal, it is natural to ask whether the finer structure continues to reflect the law of the coefficients.
Numerical experiments such as those shown in Figure
1
below suggest the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.1
.

If the random variable
\xi
has mean 0, variance 1, and a bounded
(2+\varepsilon_{0})
-moment for some
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, then there exists a constant
C_{\xi}
depending only on
\xi
such that

{\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}}({\mathbb{R}})=\dfrac{2}{\pi}\log n+C_{\xi}+o(1)\text{ as $n\to\infty$. }

Moreover, the constant
C_{\xi}
is non-universal. For instance,
C_{\xi}
may be different from
C_{\mathrm{Gau}}
if
\xi
is non-Gaussian.

Figure 1.
Sampled value of
C_{\xi}
for 4 different coefficients distributions: standard Gaussian, uniform in
[-1,1]
, Rademacher, and a distribution that matched first four moments with the standard Gaussian:
\mathbb{P}(X=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})=\frac{4}{9}\text{ each},\mathbb{P}(X=\pm\sqrt{5})=\frac{1}{18}\text{ each}
.

In the Gaussian case, the existence of this constant was first established by Wilkins
[
Wil88
]
and later by Edelman and Kostlan
[
EK95
]
through a careful evaluation of the Kac–Rice formula.

(1)

{\mathbb{E}}N_{n,N(0,1)}=\frac{2}{\pi}\log n+C_{\mathrm{Gau}}+o(1).

Here
C_{\mathrm{Gau}}\approx 0.625738072\ldots
is given by a complicated but explicit integral. Moreover, the
o(1)
term can be expanded as a convergent series of explicit functions of
n
, yielding a complete Taylor expansion.

Do–Nguyen–Vu
[
DNV15
]
made substantial progress toward this conjecture and confirmed the existence of
C_{\xi}
with the extra assumption that
\xi
is either uniform on
\{\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots,\pm N\}
or continuous with a
p
-integrable density function for some
p>1
.

In the first main result of this paper, we remove these extra conditions.

Theorem 1.2

(Existence of
C_{\xi}
in i.i.d. setting)
.

Let
\varepsilon_{0}
and
M_{0}
be any positive constants. Consider the sequence of Kac polynomials
(P_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, where
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots
are i.i.d. copies of a random variable
\xi
with zero mean, unit variance, and
{\mathbb{E}}|\xi|^{2+\varepsilon_{0}}\leq M_{0}
. Then there exist constants
C_{\xi,I},C_{\xi}
such that as
n\to\infty
,

(2)

{\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}}(I)=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\log n+C_{\xi,I}+o(1),

where
I
is any of the intervals
(-\infty,-1],[-1,0],[0,1],[1,\infty)
, with endpoints included or excluded. Summing over these intervals yields

{\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}}({\mathbb{R}})=\dfrac{2}{\pi}\log n+C_{\xi}+o(1).

This theorem is a special case of the following result, where the assumption that the random variables
\xi_{i}
are identically distributed is dropped.

Theorem 1.3

(Existence of
C_{\xi}
in non-i.i.d. setting)
.

Let
\varepsilon_{0}
and
M_{0}
be any positive constants. Consider the sequence of Kac polynomials
(P_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, where
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots
are independent random variables with zero mean, unit variance, and bounded
(2+\varepsilon_{0})
-moment:
{\mathbb{E}}|\xi_{i}|^{2+\varepsilon_{0}}\leq M_{0}
for all
i
. Assume furthermore that there exists a random variable
\xi
and a positive constant
c
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,c)
and all
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

(3)

{\mathbb{P}}(|\xi_{i}|\leq\varepsilon,\xi_{i}\neq 0)\leq\frac{1}{c}{\mathbb{P}}(|\xi|\leq\varepsilon,\xi\neq 0).

Then there exists a constant
C_{\xi_{0},\xi_{1},\dots,(0,1]}
such that as
n\to\infty
,

(4)

{\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}}((0,1])=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\log n+C_{\xi_{0},\xi_{1},\dots,(0,1]}+o(1).

The next corollary provides an alternative description of the constants
C_{\xi,I}
.

Corollary 1.4
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.3
, we have

(5)

C_{\xi_{0},\xi_{1},\dots,(0,1]}=C_{\mathrm{Gau},(0,1]}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log 2+\lim_{C\to\infty}\left[{\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{\infty}}\left(\left(0,1-\frac{1}{C}\right)\right)-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log C\right],

where the convergence in
C
is uniform over all distributions of the
\xi_{i}
, given
\varepsilon_{0}
and
M_{0}
.

This corrolary allows us to show that the constant
C_{\xi}
in Theorem
1.2
is in fact continuous.

Theorem 1.5

(Continuity of
C_{\xi}
)
.

Let
\mu_{n}
and
\mu_{\infty}
be probability measures on
{\mathbb{R}}
with zero mean and unit variance satisfying the following properties.

(1)

The measures
\mu_{n}
converge to
\mu
in distribution as
n\to\infty
. Moreover,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(0)=\mu_{\infty}(0)<1
.

(2)

There exist
M_{0}<\infty
and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that

{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi\sim\mu_{n}}|\xi|^{2+\varepsilon_{0}}\leq M_{0}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
.

Let
C_{\mu_{n}}
be the constant in Theorem
1.2
when
\xi_{0},\xi_{1},\dots
are i.i.d.
\mu_{n}
. Then
C_{\mu_{n}}\to C_{\mu_{\infty}}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Towards the second part of Conjecture
1.1
, if one allows
\xi
to have mass at 0, then the constant
C_{\xi}
can be made as large as possible. To see this, let
p={\mathbb{P}}(\xi=0)
, then the multiplicitiy at
0
has expectation

(6)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}}(\{0\})=\sum_{m=1}^{n}m{\mathbb{P}}(\xi_{0}=\dots=\xi_{m-1}=0){\mathbb{P}}(\xi_{m}\neq 0)\to\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}mp^{m}(1-p)=\frac{p}{1-p}

which can take any value in
[0,\infty)
. This, in combination with (
8
) that we prove later, implies that the constant
C_{\xi}
can be arbitrarily large.

Lemma 1.6
.

For all
M>0
, there exists a distribution
\xi
with zero mean, unit variance, and finite
(2+\varepsilon_{0})
-moment such that the constant terms
C_{\xi,[0,1]},C_{\xi,[-1,0]}
and
C_{\xi}
in Theorem
1.2
are at least
M
.

For random variables with no mass at 0, tackling the second part of Conjecture
1.1
perhaps involves answering a more challenging question.

Question 1.7
.

Can the constant
C_{\xi}
be explicitly calculated from the moments of the random variable
\xi
?

We reiterate the following question from
[
DNV15
]
: even obtaining a practical bound on
C_{\pm 1}
, the constant corresponding to the Rademacher distribution, is already difficult—for instance, proving that
|C_{\pm 1}|\leq 10
.

2.
Proof skeleton of Theorems
1.2
and
1.3

Our method, after reducing to the non-universal interval
[0,1-1/C]
, is completely different from that of
[
DNV15
]
.

Firstly, we note that it suffices to show Equation (
4
) for
I=[0,1]
, namely

(7)

{\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}}((0,1])=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\log n+C_{\xi,(0,1]}+o(1),

Indeed, the number of roots of
P_{n}(x)
in
[-1,0)
is the same as the number of roots in
(0,1]
of

P_{n}(-x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}((-1)^{i}\xi_{i})x^{i}

which is of the same form as
P_{n}
and satisfies the same properties as
P_{n}
(except that the random variables
((-1)^{i}\xi_{i})
may not be identically distributed, in view of Theorem
1.2
. However, the proof would go similarly). Therefore, (
7
) implies the same equation for
I=[-1,0)
.

Moreover, Equation (
7
) implies the same asymptotics for the interval
[0,1]
, up to an additive constant thanks to (
6
) which asserts that the expected multiplicity at
0
converges (for non-i.i.d. case, to prove convergence of the series in (
6
), one can use the fact that the probability that the
\xi_{i}
is 0 is bounded away from 1, see for instance (
13
)).

The number of roots of
P_{n}(x)
in the interval
[1,\infty)
is the same as the number of roots of
\hat{P}_{n}(x)
in the interval
(0,1]
where

\hat{P}_{n}(x)=x^{n}P_{n}(1/x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\xi_{n-i}x^{i}.

Since the sequence
(\xi_{n-i})_{i=0}^{n}
has the same distribution as
(\xi_{i})_{i=0}^{n}
under the i.i.d. setting and satisfies the same properties as
(\xi_{i})_{i=0}^{n}
under the non-i.i.d. setting, Equation (
7
) also holds for
\hat{P}_{n}
, and hence the same asymptotics applies to
N_{P_{n}}([1,\infty))
. Consequently, the result also extends to
N_{P_{n}}((-\infty,-1])
by the transformation
x\mapsto-x
as before.

It follows that, for the rest of the paper, it suffices to prove (
7
). To this end, we further decompose the interval into

(0,1]=[1-\delta,1]\cup(1-\delta^{\prime},1-\delta)\cup(0,1-\delta^{\prime}]

where
\delta,\delta^{\prime}
are positive small constants. For any
\varepsilon>0
, we need to show that there exists a constant
C
, depending only on
\varepsilon_{0},M_{0}
and
\varepsilon
, a constant
\delta
that may depend additionally on the distribution of
\xi
, and for sufficiently large
n
, the following hold:

(8)

\left|{\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}}([1-C^{-1},1])-\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log\frac{n}{C}+C_{\mathrm{Gau},[0,1]}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log 2\right)\right|\leq\varepsilon;

(9)

{\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}}((1-\delta,1-C^{-1}))\text{ converges as $n\to\infty$};

(10)

{\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}}((0,1-\delta])\leq\varepsilon.

The proof of (
8
) is given in Section
3
, where we use universality to reduce to the Gaussian case. To prove (
9
), writing
[\delta,1-C^{-1}]=[r,R]
, we first establish the weak convergence of
(P_{n}(z))_{z\in[r,R]}
to
(P_{\infty}(z))_{z\in[r,R]}
as random analytic functions.
It follows that the number of real zeros of
P_{n}
in
[r,R]
converges in distribution to that of
P_{\infty}
, provided that
P_{\infty}
has neither multiple roots nor zeros at the endpoints.
Finally, uniform integrability of these zero counts yields convergence in mean.
The details are given in Section
4
, where we emphasize the importance of showing that the infinite series
\sum_{k\geq 0}\xi_{k}x^{k}
has no double roots.
In Section
9
, we present a short proof of this fact in the case where
\xi
has a symmetric distribution on
\{0,\pm 1\}
with positive mass at 0. A short proof of (
10
) is presented in Section
5
.

3.
Proof of (
8
): Real roots near 1

Note that Theorem
1.3
still holds when all coefficients are i.i.d.
\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. When
x
is close to
1
,
[
NV21
]
(see also
[
NNV16
]
for the iid case) established universality, as follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

[
NV21
, Corollary 2.2]

Under the assumption of Theorem
1.3
, for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
C_{0}
, depending only on
\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0}
and
M_{0}
such that for all
n
sufficiently large (dependending only on
\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0}
and
M_{0}
), it holds that

\left|{\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}}([1-1/C_{0},1])-{\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}^{g}}([1-1/C_{0},1])\right|\leq\varepsilon.

Next, we evaluate the mean number of real roots for the Gaussian setting. By the Kac-Rice formula, it is well-known (see example,
[
EK95
]
) that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}^{g}}([1-1/C_{0},1])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}^{g}}([0,1])-{\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}^{g}}([0,1-1/C_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\log n+C_{\mathrm{Gau},[0,1]}+o(1)-\int_{0}^{1-C_{0}^{-1}}\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{1}{(t^{2}-1)^{2}}-\frac{(n+1)^{2}t^{2n}}{(t^{2n+2}-1)^{2}}}\,dt.

For
t\in[0,1-1/C_{0})
, it is not hard to see that

\sqrt{\frac{1}{(t^{2}-1)^{2}}-\frac{(n+1)^{2}t^{2n}}{(t^{2n+2}-1)^{2}}}=\frac{1}{1-t^{2}}+o(1)

which makes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1-C_{0}^{-1}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{(t^{2}-1)^{2}}-\frac{(n+1)^{2}t^{2n}}{(t^{2n+2}-1)^{2}}}\,dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\log\frac{1+t}{1-t}\bigg|_{0}^{1-C_{0}^{-1}}+o(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(2-C_{0}^{-1})+\frac{1}{2\pi}\log C_{0}+o(1).

Therefore,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}^{g}}([1-1/C_{0},1])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\log n-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log C_{0}+C_{\mathrm{Gau},[0,1]}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log 2+R_{C_{0}}.

where
|R_{C_{0}}|\leq\varepsilon
for sufficiently large
n
.

4.
Proof of (
9
): Roots away from 0 and 1

Let
P_{\infty}
be the following random Taylor series

P_{\infty}(z):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\xi_{k}z^{k}.

The main objective of this section is to prove (
9
) which we restate in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Fix any fixed
r<R
in
(0,1)
, we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}}([r,R])={\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{\infty}}([r,R])<\infty.

To prove the limit, we follow the general strategy in a paper by Iksanov–Kabluchko–Marynych
[
IKM16
]
. We first show that
(P_{n}(z))_{z\in[r,R]}
converges weakly to
(P_{\infty}(z))_{z\in[r,R]}
as random analytic functions. A standard argument applies to yield that the number of real zeros in
[r,R]
of
P_{n}
converges weakly to that of
P_{\infty}
, assuming that
P_{\infty}
does not have double roots. Showing that the probability of
P_{\infty}
having no double roots is zero is a challenging problem, which was only recently resolved in a beautiful paper by Michelen–Yakir
[
MY25b
]
. (In Section
9
, we present an elegant alternative proof in the case where
\xi
is a random variable taking values in
\{-1,0,1\}
with probabilities
\tfrac{1-q}{2},q,\tfrac{1-q}{2}
, respectively, for some
q>0
. For example, this includes the uniform distribution on
\{-1,0,1\}
.We note that even this case was not covered in the previous work by Do–Nguyen–Vu
[
DNV15
]
.) Finally, we prove uniform integrability of these numbers of real roots to get convergence in mean. These steps will be done, respectively, in Sections
4.1
,
4.2
,
4.3
.
The fact that the limit is bounded is a direct corrolary of Lemma
4.7
presented in Section
4.3
.

4.1.
Functional limit theorem

For any
0<R<1
, consider the half-disk

D_{R}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq R\}.

Let
\mathcal{H}(D_{R})
be the Banach space of complex-valued function continuous on
D_{R}
and analytic in the interior of
D_{R}
, endowed with the supremum norm. Let

\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}(D_{R}):=\{f\in\mathcal{H}(D_{R}):f(\mathbb{R}\cap D_{R})\subseteq\mathbb{R}\},

i.e. the closed subset of
\mathcal{H}(D_{R})
taking real values on
\mathbb{R}\cap D_{R}
. We first show the following functional limit theorem.

Lemma 4.2
.

Weakly on the space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}(D_{R})
, we have, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

(P_{n}(z))_{z\in D_{R}}\xrightarrow{w}(P_{\infty}(z))_{z\in D_{R}}.

Here,
\xrightarrow{w}
denotes weak convergence of random elements taking values in a metric space.

Proof.

By Prokhorov’s theorem, it suffices to verify convergence in finite dimensional and tightness.

Convergence of finite-dimensional distributions.

We show that for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
z_{1},\dots,z_{d}\in D_{R}
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

\begin{pmatrix}\text{Re }P_{n}(z_{1})\\
\text{Im }P_{n}(z_{1})\\
\vdots\\
\text{Re }P_{n}(z_{d})\\
\text{Im }P_{n}(z_{d})\end{pmatrix}\xrightarrow{d}\begin{pmatrix}\text{Re }P_{\infty}(z_{1})\\
\text{Im }P_{\infty}(z_{1})\\
\vdots\\
\text{Re }P_{\infty}(z_{d})\\
\text{Im }P_{\infty}(z_{d})\end{pmatrix}.

Even better, we will show that the convergence above is in
L^{2}
:

\displaystyle\ \sum_{k=1}^{d}\left((\text{Re }P_{n}(z_{k})-\text{Re }P_{\infty}(z_{k}))^{2}+(\text{Im }P_{n}(z_{k})-\text{Im }P_{\infty}(z_{k}))^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{k=1}^{d}\left|\sum_{j>n}\xi_{j}z_{k}^{j}\right|^{2}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{j>n}|\xi_{j}|\ |z_{k}|^{j}\right)^{2}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{j>n}|\xi_{j}|\ R^{j}\right)^{2}=d\left(\sum_{j>n}|\xi_{j}|\ R^{j}\right)^{2}

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ d\sum_{j\geq 2(n+1)}\left(\sum_{u=n+1}^{j-(n+1)}|\xi_{u}|\ |\xi_{j-u}|\right)R^{j},

Note that

{\mathbb{E}}|\xi_{u}|\ |\xi_{j-u}|\leq({\mathbb{E}}\xi_{u}^{2})^{1/2}({\mathbb{E}}\xi_{j-u}^{2})^{1/2}=1,

so taking the expectation of both sides of (
11
) gives

\displaystyle\ {\mathbb{E}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left((\text{Re}P_{n}(z_{k})-\text{Re}P_{\infty}(z_{k}))^{2}+(\text{Im}P_{n}(z_{k})-\text{Im}P_{\infty}(z_{k}))^{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ d\sum_{j\geq 2(n+1)}(j-2(n+1))R^{j}=d\left(\sum_{j\geq 0}jR^{j}\right)R^{2(n+1)}\longrightarrow 0,

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. This establishes the desired
L^{2}
convergence.

Tightness.
It remains to show that
(P_{n}(z))_{z\in D_{R}}
is a tight sequence on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}(D_{R})
. A standard sufficient condition for tightness (see for example,
[
KK14
, Lemma 4.2]
) is

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N},z\in D_{R}}{\mathbb{E}}|P_{n}(z)|^{2}<\infty.

Since

{\mathbb{E}}|P_{n}(z)|^{2}={\mathbb{E}}(P_{n}(z)\overline{P_{n}(z)})={\mathbb{E}}\sum_{k=0}^{n}|z|^{2k}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n}R^{2k}\leq\dfrac{1}{1-R^{2}}<\infty,

we obtained the desired tightness. This also completes the proof of Lemma
4.2
.
∎

4.2.
Convergence in distribution of the number of real roots

From the convergence of
P_{n}
, we now show that
N_{P_{n}}([r,R])\xrightarrow{d}N_{P_{\infty}}([r,R])
. We state the following analogue of
[
IKM16
, Lemma 4.1]
, whose proof carries over almost verbatim. Roughly speaking, the lemma asserts that the mapping
P\mapsto N_{P}[r,R]
is continuous, assuming that
P_{\infty}
does not have double roots nor roots at the endpoints. The result is a consequence of Hurwitz’s Theorem (see for example,
[
Con12
, Page 152]
). In essence, Hurwitz’s theorem ensures the continuity of the map that associates to an analytic function the point process formed by its complex zeros.
When focusing on real zeros, one must additionally verify that these remain confined to the real line under small perturbations.
This stability holds for analytic functions that are real-valued on
\mathbb{R}
(as in the case of
P_{n}
and
P_{\infty}
): non-real zeros necessarily occur in complex conjugate pairs, and a simple real zero cannot leave the real axis under a sufficiently small perturbation of the function. This explains the requirement that
P_{\infty}
have neither multiple roots nor zeros at the endpoints.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
A=A[a,b]\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}}(D_{R})
be the set of all
f\in\mathcal{H}_{{\mathbb{R}}}(D_{R})
which do not have multiple real zeros in
[a,b]
and satisfy
f(a),f(b)\neq 0
. Then the set
A
is open.

Moreover, let
N=N_{f}([a,b])
be the number of real zeros of
f
in
[a,b]
. Then the map
N:\mathcal{H}_{{\mathbb{R}}}(D_{R})\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}
is locally constant on A (i.e. for every
f\in A
there is an open neighborhood of
f
in
\mathcal{H}_{{\mathbb{R}}}(D_{R})
on which
N
is constant), and hence, is continuous.

To conclude that
N_{P_{n}}([r,R])\xrightarrow{d}N_{P_{\infty}}([r,R])
, it remains to show that almost surely,
P_{\infty}
does not have double roots and
P_{\infty}(r)\neq 0
,
P_{\infty}(R)\neq 0
.
As mentioned before, the first part follows from
[
MY25b
]
.

Lemma 4.4
.

[
MY25b
, Theorem 1.3]

Suppose
\xi
is such that
{\mathbb{E}}\log(1+|\xi|)<\infty
. Then

{\mathbb{P}}(\exists z\in B(0,1):P_{\infty}(z)=P^{\prime}_{\infty}(z)=0)={\mathbb{P}}(P_{\infty}(0)=P^{\prime}_{\infty}(0)=0)={\mathbb{P}}(\xi=0)^{2}.

Consequently,

{\mathbb{P}}(\exists x\in[r,R]:P_{\infty}(x)=P^{\prime}_{\infty}(x)=0)=0.

We note that their proof extends to the case where the
\xi_{i}
are not identically distributed, provided they have a finite
(2+\varepsilon_{0})
-moment. For the second part, we show that

Lemma 4.5
.

For any fixed
x\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
, we have

{\mathbb{P}}(P_{\infty}(x)=0)=0.

To prove Lemma
4.5
, we will rely on a classical anti-concentration bound.
In the setting where the
\xi_{i}
are i.i.d. Rademacher variables, this bound appears as
Erdős’s celebrated lemma for the Littlewood–Offord problem
[
Erd45
]
.
When the
\xi_{i}
are i.i.d. with a more general distribution, the statement is known as the
Lévy–Kolmogorov–Rogozin inequality (see, for instance,
[
Ess68
]
).
An extension to independent random variables with bounded

(2+\varepsilon_{0})
-moment was later obtained in
[
DNV18
, Lemma 4.1]

Lemma 4.6

(
[
DNV18
, Lemma 4.1]
)
.

Let
(\xi_{j})
be independent random variables with variance 1 and are either i.i.d. or have bounded
(2+\varepsilon_{0})
-moment.
Let
c_{j}\in\mathbb{C}

be such that
|a_{j}|\geq a
.
There exists a constant
C>0
, depending only on the distribution of

, such that

\sup_{w\in\mathbb{C}}\,\mathbb{P}\!\left(\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\xi_{j}-w\right|\leq a\right)\;\leq\;\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}.

Proof of Lemma
4.5
.

Let
C
be the constant in Lemma
4.6
. For every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, it suffices to show that

{\mathbb{P}}(P_{\infty}(x)=0)\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}.

By independence of the
\xi_{i}
, we may separate the contribution of the first
n
terms from the tail.
Conditioning on the value of the tail sum
\sum_{j=n}^{\infty}\xi_{i}x^{i}
, we obtain

{\mathbb{P}}\!\big(P_{\infty}(x)=0\big)={\mathbb{P}}\!\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\xi_{i}x^{i}+\sum_{j=n}^{\infty}\xi_{i}x^{i}=0\right)\;\leq\;\sup_{w\in{\mathbb{C}}}{\mathbb{P}}\!\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\xi_{i}x^{i}-w=0\right).

Applying the anti-concentration bound now gives

{\mathbb{P}}\!\big(P_{\infty}(x)=0\big)\;\leq\;\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}

4.3.
Uniform integrability

To guarantee convergence in mean, it suffices (for example, see
[
Pat95
, Theorem 3.5]
) to show that for all
0<r<R<1
,
(N_{P_{n}}([r,R]))_{n\geq 1}
is uniformly integrable. This follows from a more general estimate on moments of
N_{n}:=N_{P_{n}}([-R,R])
, which we state below.

Lemma 4.7
.

Denote
N_{n}:=N_{P_{n}}([-R,R])
. Then for all
\kappa\geq 1
, there exists a constant
M=M(R,\kappa,\varepsilon_{0},M_{0})\in(0,\infty)
such that for all
n
,

{\mathbb{E}}N_{n}^{\kappa}\leq M,

where
\varepsilon_{0},M_{0}
are such that
{\mathbb{E}}|\xi_{k}|^{2+\varepsilon_{0}}\leq M_{0}
for all
k
.

From the lemma above, uniform integrability holds: for any
\varepsilon>0
, we have, for all
n
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}}([r,R])1_{\{N_{P_{n}}([r,R])\geq K\}}

\displaystyle\leq\left({\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}}([r,R])^{2}\right)^{1/2}{\mathbb{P}}\left(N_{P_{n}}([r,R])\geq K\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left({\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}}([r,R])^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\dfrac{{\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}}([r,R])^{2}}{K^{2}}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{{\mathbb{E}}N_{P_{n}}([r,R])^{2}}{K}\leq\dfrac{M}{K},

and we can make the last term at most
\varepsilon
for
K
sufficiently large (independent of
n
).

Before proving Lemma
4.7
, we recall the classical Jensen’s inequality which asserts that for every entire function
f
, every
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
0<r<R
,

(12)

N_{f}\left(B(z,r)\right)\leq\frac{\log\frac{\sup_{w\in B(z,R)}|f(w)|}{|f(z)|}}{\log\frac{R}{r}}

This is a corollary of the classical Jensen’s formula (see, for example,
[
Rud86
]
).

Proof of Lemma
4.7
.

For any
d\in(0,1)
, for all
k
, by Hölder’s inequality,

1={\mathbb{E}}|\xi_{k}|^{2}={\mathbb{E}}|\xi_{k}|^{2}1_{\{|\xi_{k}|\leq d\}}+{\mathbb{E}}|\xi_{k}|^{2}1_{\{|\xi_{k}|>d\}}\leq d^{2}+P(|\xi_{k}|>d)^{\varepsilon_{0}/(2+\varepsilon_{0})}M_{0}^{2/(2+\varepsilon_{0})},

so we can choose
d=d(\varepsilon_{0},M_{0})\in(0,1)
sufficiently small such that

(13)

{\mathbb{P}}(|\xi_{k}|\leq d)<1-d=:q\in(0,1).

Let

\mathcal{B}_{k}:=\{|\xi_{0}|\leq d,\dots,|\xi_{k-1}|\leq d,|\xi_{k}|>d\},

then
{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{B}_{k})\leq q^{k}
for all
k\geq 1
. By Mean Value Theorem and Jensen’s inequality (
12
), on
\mathcal{B}_{k}
where
k\leq n+1
, we have

(14)

\displaystyle N_{n}

\displaystyle\leq k+N_{P_{n}^{(k)}}([-R,R])\leq k+\dfrac{\log\frac{M^{\prime}}{P^{(k)}(0)}}{\log\frac{\tilde{R}}{R}}\leq k+\dfrac{\log S_{k}-\log d}{\log\frac{\tilde{R}}{R}},

where
\tilde{R}:=(R+1)/2<1
,
M^{\prime}:=\sup_{|z|=\tilde{R}}|P^{(k)}(z)|
, and
S_{k}:=\sum_{j=k}^{n}\binom{j}{k}|\xi_{j}|\tilde{R}^{j-k}
. (When
k=n+1
, the bound is simply
n
) Thus,

\displaystyle N_{n}^{\kappa}

\displaystyle\leq 3^{\kappa}\left|\dfrac{\log d}{\log\frac{\tilde{R}}{R}}\right|^{\kappa}+3^{\kappa}k^{\kappa}+3^{\kappa}\left(\dfrac{|\log S_{k}|}{\log\frac{\tilde{R}}{R}}\right)^{\kappa}.

Taking expectation on both sides, it remains to show a uniform-in-
n
bound on

(15)

{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}1_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}|\log S_{k}|^{\kappa}\right)+{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}1_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}k^{\kappa}\right).

The second summation is easy since
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q^{k}k^{\kappa}<\infty
for fixed
\kappa
. For the first summation, note that
{\mathbb{E}}|\xi_{j}|\leq({\mathbb{E}}\xi_{j}^{2})^{1/2}=1
, thus,

{\mathbb{E}}S_{k}\leq\sum_{j\geq 0}\binom{k+j}{k}\tilde{R}^{j}=(1-\tilde{R})^{-(k+1)}.

On
\mathcal{B}_{k}
,

S_{k}\geq|\xi_{k}|>d.

This observation prevents
|\log S_{k}|
from blowing up when
S_{k}
is small. We partition
\mathcal{B}_{k}
into the events

\mathcal{B}_{ki}:=\mathcal{B}_{k}\cap\{e^{i}{\mathbb{E}}S_{k}<S_{k}\leq e^{i+1}{\mathbb{E}}S_{k}\}.

Moreover, denote our cut-off
i_{k}:=\lfloor q^{-k/(2\kappa)}\rfloor\geq 1
. Then

•

On
\bigcup_{i<i_{k}}\mathcal{B}_{ki}
, we have

\log d\leq\log S_{k}\leq i_{k}(k+1)\log(1-\tilde{R})^{-1}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sum_{i<i_{k}}1_{\mathcal{B}_{ki}}|\log S_{k}|^{\kappa}\right)

\displaystyle=O_{\kappa}(1)q^{k}\left(\left|\log d\right|^{\kappa}+i_{k}^{\kappa}(k+1)^{\kappa}\left(\log(1-\tilde{R})^{-1}\right)^{\kappa}\right)

(16)

\displaystyle=O_{\kappa}(1)q^{k/2}k^{\kappa}.

•

For
i\geq i_{k}>0
, by Markov’s inequality,
{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{B}_{ki}\mid\mathcal{B}_{k})\leq e^{-i}
. Moreover, on
\mathcal{B}_{ki}
,

0\leq i\log{\mathbb{E}}S_{k}\leq\log S_{k}\leq(i+1)(k+1)\log(1-\tilde{R})^{-1}.

Thus,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sum_{i\geq i_{k}}1_{\mathcal{B}_{ki}}|\log S_{k}|^{\kappa}\right)

\displaystyle\leq q^{k}\sum_{i\geq i_{k}}e^{-i}(i+1)^{\kappa}(k+1)^{\kappa}\left(\log(1-\tilde{R})^{-1}\right)^{\kappa}

(17)

\displaystyle=O_{\kappa}(1)q^{k/2}k^{\kappa}.

Combining (
16
), (
17
), and summing over all
k
gives the desired uniform-in-
n
bound for the first summation in (
15
), completing our proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.5

We start by constructing a coupling of the random series
Q_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\xi_{k,n}x^{k}
, with coefficients
\xi_{k,n}\sim\mu_{n}

\xi_{k,\infty}\sim\mu_{\infty}

Lemma 7.1
.

Assume that the sequence of probability measures
\mu_{n}
converges to
\mu_{\infty}
in distribution, then we can construct
(\xi_{k,n})_{k,n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
(\xi_{k,\infty})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}

(1)

For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
, the random variables
\xi_{0,n},\xi_{1,n},\dots
are i.i.d. with distribution
\mu_{n}

(2)

For each

converges to

Let

It is not hard to see that these random variables satisfy the requirements. Indeed, the independence across the first index is given by the independence of
U_{0},U_{1},\dots
. As for the marginal distribution, for all

\xi_{k,\infty}\sim\mu_{\infty}

It follows that for each continuity point

U_{k}
is uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
, then for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{n}|\xi_{k,n}|\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{k}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\left(\exists n:|\xi_{k,n}|\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\exists n:\xi_{k,n}\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{k}\right)+{\mathbb{P}}\left(\exists n:\xi_{k,n}\leq-(1+\varepsilon)^{k}\right).

If
n
is such that
\xi_{k,n}:=F_{n}^{-1}(U_{k})\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{k}
, then
U_{k}\geq F_{n}((1+\varepsilon)^{k})\geq 1-(1+\varepsilon)^{-2k}
, where the last equality follows from Chebyshev’s inequality and the fact that
F_{n}
has unit variance. Thus,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\exists n:\xi_{k,n}\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(U_{k}\geq 1-(1+\varepsilon)^{-2k}\right)\leq(1+\varepsilon)^{-2k}.

We obtain the same bound for
{\mathbb{P}}\left(\exists n:\xi_{k,n}\leq-(1+\varepsilon)^{k}\right)
. Thus,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{n}|\xi_{k,n}|\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2(1+\varepsilon)^{-2k},

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 0}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{n}|\xi_{k,n}|\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{k\geq 0}(1+\varepsilon)^{-2k}<\infty.

By Borel-Cantelli lemma, a.s. there are finitely many
k
such that
\sup_{n}|\xi_{k,n}|\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{k}
. Pick
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small such that
R(1+\varepsilon)<1
, then a.s.
\mathcal{A}
happens.

The remaining steps are identical to the proof of Lemma
4.1
as tightness is easily verified, and uniform integrability follows directly since all
\mu_{n},\mu_{\infty}
share the same parameters
\varepsilon_{0},M_{0}
in Lemma
4.7
.
∎

For the region near
0
, we have the analogue of Lemma
5.1
.

Lemma 7.3
.

In the same setting as Lemma
7.2
, for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta,N_{0}
(possibly depending also on
\mu
) such that for all
n\geq N_{0}
,

0\leq{\mathbb{E}}N_{Q_{n}}((0,\delta))<\varepsilon.

Proof.

Tracing the proof of Lemma
5.1
, it suffices to show that for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta,N_{0}
such that for all
n\geq N_{0}
, with
\xi_{n}\sim\mu_{n}
,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(|\xi_{n}|\leq\delta,\xi_{n}\neq 0\right)<\varepsilon.

Since
\mu_{\infty}(0)<1
, there exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that for
\xi_{\infty}\sim\mu_{\infty}
and for all
\delta<\delta_{0}
,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(|\xi_{\infty}|\leq\delta,\xi_{\infty}\neq 0\right)\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(|\xi_{\infty}|\leq\delta_{0},\xi_{\infty}\neq 0\right)<\varepsilon/2.

Let
\delta<\delta_{0}
be such that
\mu_{\infty}(\pm\delta)=0
. By convergence of
\mu_{n}
to
\mu_{\infty}
, it holds that

{\mathbb{P}}(-\delta<\xi_{n}<\delta)\to{\mathbb{P}}(-\delta<\xi_{n}<\delta).

Since
\mu_{n}(0)\to\mu_{\infty}(0)
, this yields

{\mathbb{P}}\left(|\xi_{n}|\leq\delta,\xi_{n}\neq 0\right)\to{\mathbb{P}}\left(|\xi_{\infty}|\leq\delta,\xi_{\infty}\neq 0\right)<\varepsilon/2.

Therefore, for sufficiently large
n
,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(|\xi_{n}|\leq\delta,\xi_{n}\neq 0\right)\leq\varepsilon

as desired.
∎

9.
An alternative argument for symmetric distribution on
\{-1,0,1\}

Of independent interest, we provide a short, alternative argument for showing that the probability that
P_{\infty}
has double root is 0 (as stated in Lemma
4.4
) in the special case where
\xi
has symmetric distribution in
\{-1,0,1\}
with mass
{\mathbb{P}}(\xi=0)=q>0
. In particular, we show the following.

Theorem 9.1
.

Almost surely,
P_{\infty}
does not have a double root in
(0,1)
.

Proof.

Let
R\in(0,1)
. It suffices to prove the statement for
(0,R)
.
Denote

\mathcal{A}:=\left\{{\bf\xi}=(\xi_{0},\xi_{1}\dots)\in\{-1,0,1\}^{{\mathbb{N}}}:\sum_{k\geq 0}\xi_{k}x^{k}\text{ has a double root in }(0,R)\right\}.

We will prove that
{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{A})=0
via Lévy’s zero-one law. First,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}({\bf\xi}\in\mathcal{A},\xi_{0}=0)

\displaystyle=q{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\xi_{k}z^{k}\text{ has a double root in }(0,R)\right)=q{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{A})

since
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots)
has the same distribution as
(\xi_{0},\xi_{1},\dots)
.
By symmetry,
{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{A}_{1}|\xi_{0}=1)={\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{A}_{-1}|\xi_{0}=1)
.
Since
\mathcal{A}_{-1},\mathcal{A}_{0},\mathcal{A}_{1}
forms a partition of
\mathcal{A}
, it follows that

{\mathbb{P}}(\xi\in\mathcal{A}|\xi_{0}=\pm 1)=\frac{1}{1-q}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi\in\mathcal{A},\xi_{0}=\pm 1)=\frac{1-q}{1-q}{\mathbb{P}}(\xi\in\mathcal{A})={\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{A}).

Therefore,conditioning on
\xi_{0}
does not change the probability of
P_{\infty}
getting a double root in
(0,R)
. Extending the above, conditioning on
\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{n}
does not change the probability of
\mathcal{A}
.

Denote
\mathcal{F}_{n}:=\sigma\left(\{\xi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n}\right)
, then
\mathcal{F}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{1}\subseteq\dots\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
. We then have, as
n\to\infty
,

{\mathbb{E}}1_{\mathcal{A}}={\mathbb{E}}(1_{\mathcal{A}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n})\xrightarrow{a.s.}{\mathbb{E}}(1_{\mathcal{A}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\infty})=1_{\mathcal{A}},

the first equality is due to our observation of conditioning on the first
n
coefficients, the convergence is due to Lévy’s zero-one law, and the last equality holds since
\mathcal{A}
is
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}
-measurable. Thus,
{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{A})\in\{0,1\}
. To see that this probability cannot be
1
, note that there exists
N
sufficiently large such that when all the first
N
entries are 1, namely
\xi_{0}=\xi_{1}=\dots=\xi_{N}=1
, then for all
x\in(0,R)
,

\displaystyle|P_{\infty}(x)|\geq\sum_{k=0}^{N}x^{k}-\left|\sum_{k>N}\xi_{k}x^{k}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{1-x^{N+1}}{1-x}-\sum_{k>N}x^{k}=\dfrac{1-2x^{N+1}}{1-x}>0.

Thus, with positive probability
P_{\infty}
does
not
have a root in
(0,R)
, i.e.
{\mathbb{P}}(\mathcal{A})<1
. This completes our proof.
∎
