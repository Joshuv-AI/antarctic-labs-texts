China, Email address: linz@sdu.edu.cn, Email address: feihu.liu@cnu.edu.cn, Email address: jiahang-liu@foxmail.com, Email address: lsweet@mail.sdu.edu.cn, Email address: guoce_xin@163.com

Abstract
Banderier, Marchal, and Wallner considered Young tableaux with walls, which are similar to standard Young tableaux, except that local decreases are allowed at some walls. In this work, we prove a conjecture of Fuchs and Yu concerning the enumeration of two classes of three-row Young tableaux with walls. Combining with the work by Chang, Fuchs, Liu, Wallner, and Yu leads to the verification of a conjecture on tree-child networks proposed by Pons and Batle. This conjecture was regarded as a specific and challenging problem in the Phylogenetics community until it was finally resolved by the present work.

The main result of this paper is the following unexpected relationship between
a_{n,k}
and
b_{n,k}
, which was originally conjectured by Fuchs and Yu
[
9
]
.

Theorem 1.1

(Fuchs and Yu’s enumerative conjecture)
.

For
0\leq k\leq n
, we have

\displaystyle 2^{n-k}\cdot a_{n,k}=(n-k+1)!\cdot b_{n,k}.

(4)

It is straightforward to verify that

2^{n}\cdot(2n-1)!!=(n+1)!\cdot\mathrm{Cat}(n),

(5)

from which we see that Theorem
1.1
holds for
k=0
in view of (
1
) and (
3
).
Thus, Theorem
1.1
can be considered as an extension of the relationship (
5
) between the odd factorial numbers and the Catalan numbers.
Theorem
1.1
was inspired by recent work of Chang et al. in
[
8
]
and closely connected to a conjecture of Pons and Batle
[
18
, Conjecture 1]
on counting tree-child networks that we next elaborate on.

The class of tree-child networks is an increasingly prominent class of phylogenetic networks.
Following
[

(1)

A unique

(2)

Leaves
are nodes that have indegree 1 and outdegree 0. They are bijectively labeled with elements in
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.

(3)

Tree nodes
which have indegree 1 and outdegree 2.

(4)

Reticulation nodes
which have indegree 2 and outdegree 1.

Conjecture 1.2

(
Pons and Batle
[

)
.

Let
\mathcal{TC}_{n,k}
be the set of all tree-child networks with

|\mathcal{TC}_{n,k}|=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}\cdot a_{n-1,k}.

(6)

In fact,

is interpreted in

as some Yamanouchi-like words, which are in one-to-one correspondence (see

. Conjecture

Proof of Conjecture
1.2
.

It was proved in
[

that

|\mathcal{TC}_{n,k}|=\frac{n!}{2^{n-k-1}}\cdot b_{n-1,k},

which, when combined with Theorem

, implies (

∎

2
Proof of Theorem
1.1

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Since our proof is somewhat intricate, we first intend to outline the key steps of our proof:

1.

Starting from (
2
), we derive a semi-closed-form expression for
a_{n,k}
(see Theorem
2.1
).

2.

We introduce an extension of
b_{n,k}
, denoted
b_{n,m,k}
, by generalizing the deformed Young tableaux with walls. The extension
b_{n,m,k}
admits a nice recurrence relation (see Proposition
2.5
):

b_{n,m,k}=(m-k+1)b_{n,m,k-1}+b_{n,m-1,k}+b_{n-1,m,k},

(7)

which can be applied to compute
b_{n,k}
efficiently, as
b_{n,k}=b_{n,n,k}
.

3.

Inspired by the favorable recursion for
b_{n,m,k}
and the conjectured relationship (
4
) with
a_{n,k}
, we introduce a variant of
b_{n,m,k}
, denoted
\omega_{n,m,k}
, which satisfies a variant of (
7
) with different initial conditions.

4.

To finish the proof, in view of the construction of
\omega_{n,m,k}
and Theorem
2.1
, it remains to show that
\omega_{n,m,k}=b_{n+m,m,k}
. To this end, we establish two key lemmas to verify that
\omega_{n,m,k}
and
b_{n+m,m,k}
share the same initial conditions, a part that constitutes the most technical aspect of our proof.

2.1
A semi-closed-form expression for
a_{n,k}

This subsection aims to prove the following semi-closed-form expression for
a_{n,k}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
0\leq k\leq n
. We have

\displaystyle a_{n,k}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{\gamma_{k-i}}{i!}\cdot(2n+k+i-1)!!,

where
\gamma_{k}
are rational numbers satisfying the recurrence relation

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}=\frac{1}{(3k-3)!!}\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\gamma_{k-i}}{i!}\cdot(3k+i-3)!!\right)

(8)

with the initial condition
\gamma_{0}=1
.

Remark 2.2
.

We are surprised to learn that Theorem
2.1
was already known in
[
18
, Proposition 6]
. However, unaware of their work, we independently discovered this result.
Actually, starting from (
2
) we derive the following recurrence relation for
a_{n,k}
:

a_{n,k}=a_{n,k-1}+\sum_{i=k}^{n-1}\prod_{j=i}^{n-1}(2(j+1)+k-1)\cdot a_{i,k-1}.

(9)

Using the above recursion, we produce explicit formulae of
a_{n,k}
for some fixed small values of
k
in Proposition
A.1
, which leads us to find Theorem
2.1
.

Banderier and Wallner
[
2
, Theorem 4.1]
used the density method to derive an asymptotic expression for
a_{n,n}
.
As a special case of Theorem
2.1
, we have the following semi-closed exact expression for
a_{n,n}
.

Corollary 2.3
.

For
n\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle a_{n,n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{n-i}}{i!}\cdot(3n+i-1)!!,

where
\gamma_{j}
satisfying the recurrence relation (
8
) and
\gamma_{0}=1
.

Since Theorem
2.1
is crucial in proving Theorem
1.1
, we include its proof below, for the sake of completeness.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

For convenience, we set

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{n,k}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{\gamma_{k-i}}{i!}\cdot(2n+k+i-1)!!.

We aim to show that
\tilde{a}_{n,k}
and
a_{n,k}
share the same recurrence relation and initial conditions.
Firstly, we have
\tilde{a}_{n,0}=(2n-1)!!=a_{n,0}
.
Secondly, the
\tilde{a}_{n,k}
satisfies the following equation:

\displaystyle(2n+k-1)\tilde{a}_{n-1,k}=

\displaystyle(2n+k-1)\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{\gamma_{k-i}}{i!}\cdot(2n+k+i-3)!!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2n+k+i-1-i)\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{\gamma_{k-i}}{i!}\cdot(2n+k+i-3)!!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{\gamma_{k-i}}{i!}\cdot(2n+k+i-1)!!-\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{i\cdot\gamma_{k-i}}{i!}\cdot(2n+k+i-3)!!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{n,k}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\gamma_{k-i}}{(i-1)!}\cdot(2n+k+i-3)!!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{n,k}-\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{\gamma_{k-i-1}}{i!}\cdot(2n+k+i-2)!!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{n,k}-\tilde{a}_{n,k-1}.

Therefore, we obtain

\tilde{a}_{n,k}=\tilde{a}_{n,k-1}+(2n+k-1)\tilde{a}_{n-1,k}.

In view of (
2
),
\tilde{a}_{n,k}
and
a_{n,k}
share the same recurrence relation.
Finally, it follows from (
8
) that

\tilde{a}_{k-1,k}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{\gamma_{k-i}}{i!}(3k+i-3)!!=0.

This is consistent with
a_{k-1,k}=0
, and thus
a_{n,k}=\tilde{a}_{n,k}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

2.2
An extension of
b_{n,k}
and the introduction of its variant
\omega_{n,m,k}

In this section, we consider a natural extension of
b_{n,k}
and introduce its variant
\omega_{n,m,k}
based on Theorem

.

Let
n\geq m\geq k\geq 0
and
(n,m,m)\vdash n+2m
.
We place a vertical wall (bold red edge) between each pair of adjacent cells at the top row of the Young diagram of
(n,m,m)
.
By removing any
m-k
cells at the top, we obtain a family of deformed Young diagrams with walls.
One such diagram is illustrated in Fig.
5
for
n=9
,
m=7
, and
k=4
.
Similarly, a deformed Young tableau with walls of this type is a filling of the cells by labels
1,2,\dots,n+m+k
, such that each label appears exactly once and the labels are increasing along each row and column, but two labels separated by a wall need not be increasing.
Let
b_{n,m,k}
denote the total number of deformed Young tableaux with walls on this family of deformed Young diagrams with walls.

Figure 5:
A case in a family of deformed Young diagrams with walls for
n=9
,
m=7

When
k=0
, the hook-length formula
[
23
, Corollary 7.21.6]
for counting standard Young tableaux gives

b_{n,m,0}=\frac{(n+m)!(n-m+1)}{m!(n+1)!}.

Let

\displaystyle C(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\mathrm{Cat}(n)x^{n}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}x^{n}=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4x}}{2x}

be the generating function of Catalan numbers.
Then we obtain

\sum_{0\leq m\leq n}b_{n,m,0}x^{n}y^{m}=\frac{1-yC(xy)}{1-x-y}.

Proposition 2.4
.

For
0\leq k\leq n
, we have
b_{n,n,k}=b_{n,k}
.

Proof.

A simple bijection readily yields
b_{n,n,k}=b_{n,k}
.
For example, a Young tableau with walls in the count of
b_{n,n,k}
can be transformed into one in the count of
b_{n,k}
by first flipping it vertically, then horizontally, and finally replacing each entry
i
with
2n+k+1-i
.
∎

Proposition 2.5
.

The sequence
b_{n,m,k}
is uniquely determined by the recurrence relation

b_{n,m,k}=(m-k+1)b_{n,m,k-1}+b_{n,m-1,k}+b_{n-1,m,k}

(10)

for
n\geq 2
and
0\leq k\leq m\leq n
with the initial conditions

b_{0,0,0}=b_{1,0,0}=b_{1,1,0}=b_{1,1,1}=1\quad\text{and}\quad b_{m-1,m,k}=b_{n,k-1,k}=0.

Proof.

The first part of the initial condition clearly holds. The equalities
b_{m-1,m,k}=b_{n,k-1,k}=0
are related with the
recurrence

\displaystyle b_{n,m,k}=(m-k+1)\cdot b_{n,m,k-1}+b_{n,m-1,k}+b_{n-1,m,k}\quad\text{ for }\quad n\geq 1,\quad 0\leq k\leq m\leq n,

which is obtained by considering where the largest element
v=n+m+k
is located. This is classified by the
following three cases:

Case 1:
v
is in the bottom row, which forces
n\geq m+1
. Removing
v
gives a Young tableau with walls counted by
b_{n-1,m,k}
, but note that

b_{m-1,m,k}
has to be interpreted as
0
;

Case 2:
v
is in the middle row, which forces
m\geq k+1
. Removing
v
gives a Young tableau with walls counted by
b_{n,m-1,k}
, but note that

b_{n,k-1,k}
has to be interpreted as
0
;

Case 3:
v
is in the top row. Removing
v
gives a Young tableau with walls counted by
b_{n,m,k-1}
, while inserting
v
back have
m-(k-1)
choices.

This completes the proof.
∎

Inspired by Theorem
2.1
and Proposition

, we introduce the following variant of
b_{n,m,k}

Definition 2.6

(A variant of
b_{n,m,k}
)
.

We define the sequence
\omega_{n,m,k}
by the recurrence

\omega_{n,m,k}=\omega_{n-1,m+1,k}-(m-k+2)\omega_{n-1,m+1,k-1}-\omega_{n-2,m+1,k}

(11)

for
n+m\geq 2
and
0\leq k-1\leq m
,
with the initial conditions
\omega_{1,0,0}=1
,
\omega_{-1,m,k}=0
and

\omega_{0,m,k}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{\gamma_{k-i}}{i!}\cdot\frac{2^{m-k}}{(m-k+1)!}\cdot(2m+k+i-1)!!,

(12)

where
\gamma_{j}
satisfy recurrence relation (
8
) and initial condition
\gamma_{0}=1
.

The rest of this section is devoted to proving the following relationship, which implies
Theorem
1.1
in view of Theorem
2.1
.

Theorem 2.7
.

We have
\omega_{n,m,k}=b_{n+m,m,k}
.

2.3
Two key lemmas

The most technical part of the proof of Theorem
2.7
lies in showing that
\omega_{n,k-1,k}=0
.
For this purpose, we establish two auxiliary lemmas, the first of which is an explicit recursion for
\omega_{n,k-1,k}
.

Lemma 2.8
.

For any
1\leq s\leq n
, the sequence
\omega_{n,k-1,k}
satisfies the following recursion:

\displaystyle\omega_{n,k-1,k}=\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{s+1}{2}\right\rfloor}\sum_{q=1}^{s+2-2p}\alpha_{s}(p,q)\cdot\omega_{n-s-1,k+s-p,k+1-q}+\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lceil\frac{s+1}{2}\right\rceil}\sum_{q=1}^{s+3-2p}-\alpha_{s+1}(p,q)\cdot\omega_{n-s,k+s-p,k+1-q},

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{s}(p,q)=\frac{(-1)^{q-p+1}\cdot(s-1+q-p)!}{(s-q-2p+2)!\cdot(q-1)!\cdot 2^{q-1}\cdot(p-1)!}.

(13)

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
s
.
If
s=1
, then we have

\displaystyle\omega_{n,k-1,k}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}(1,1)\cdot\omega_{n-2,k,k}-\alpha_{2}(1,1)\omega_{n-1,k,k}-\alpha_{2}(1,2)\omega_{n-1,k,k-1}

\displaystyle=-\omega_{n-2,k,k}+\omega_{n-1,k,k}-\omega_{n-1,k,k-1}.

This is consistent with the recurrence for
\omega_{n,k-1,k}
in (
11
), hence the lemma holds for
s=1
.
Assuming the formula to hold for
s-1
, we will prove it for
s
.

By the recurrence for
\omega_{n,m,k}
in (
11
), we now have

\displaystyle\omega_{n,k-1,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{s}{2}\right\rfloor}\sum_{q=1}^{s+1-2p}\alpha_{s-1}(p,q)\cdot\omega_{n-s,k+s-1-p,k+1-q}+\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lceil\frac{s}{2}\right\rceil}\sum_{q=1}^{s+2-2p}-\alpha_{s}(p,q)\cdot\omega_{n-s+1,k+s-1-p,k+1-q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{s}{2}\right\rfloor}\sum_{q=1}^{s+1-2p}\alpha_{s-1}(p,q)\cdot\omega_{n-s,k+s-1-p,k+1-q}+\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lceil\frac{s}{2}\right\rceil}\sum_{q=1}^{s+2-2p}-\alpha_{s}(p,q)\cdot\omega_{n-s,k+s-p,k+1-q}

\displaystyle+\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lceil\frac{s}{2}\right\rceil}\sum_{q=1}^{s+2-2p}-\alpha_{s}(p,q)\cdot\big(-(s+q-p)\omega_{n-s,k+s-p,k-q}-\omega_{n-s-1,k+s-p,k+1-q}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{s+1}{2}\right\rfloor}\sum_{q=1}^{s+2-2p}\alpha_{s}(p,q)\cdot\omega_{n-s-1,k+s-p,k+1-q}+\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{s}{2}\right\rfloor}\sum_{q=1}^{s+1-2p}\alpha_{s-1}(p,q)\cdot\omega_{n-s,k+s-1-p,k+1-q}

\displaystyle+\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lceil\frac{s}{2}\right\rceil}\sum_{q=1}^{s+2-2p}-\alpha_{s}(p,q)\cdot\omega_{n-s,k+s-p,k+1-q}+\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lceil\frac{s}{2}\right\rceil}\sum_{q=1}^{s+2-2p}\alpha_{s}(p,q)\cdot(s+q-p)\omega_{n-s,k+s-p,k-q},

where we use the fact
\lceil\frac{s}{2}\rceil=\lfloor\frac{s+1}{2}\rfloor
in the last equality.
It suffices to prove the following identity:

\displaystyle\sum_{p=2}^{\left\lfloor\frac{s}{2}\right\rfloor+1}\sum_{q=1}^{s+3-2p}\alpha_{s-1}(p-1,q)\cdot\omega_{n-s,k+s-p,k+1-q}+\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lceil\frac{s}{2}\right\rceil}\sum_{q=1}^{s+2-2p}-\alpha_{s}(p,q)\cdot\omega_{n-s,k+s-p,k+1-q}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lceil\frac{s}{2}\right\rceil}\sum_{q=2}^{s+3-2p}\alpha_{s}(p,q-1)\cdot(s+q-p-1)\omega_{n-s,k+s-p,k+1-q}

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lceil\frac{s+1}{2}\right\rceil}\sum_{q=1}^{s+3-2p}-\alpha_{s+1}(p,q)\cdot\omega_{n-s,k+s-p,k+1-q}.

We begin by verifying the case
p=1
, that is, Eq. (
14
) reduces to

\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{s}-\alpha_{s}(1,q)\omega_{n-s,k+s-1,k+1-q}+\sum_{q=2}^{s+1}\alpha_{s}(1,q-1)(s+q-2)\omega_{n-s,k+s-1,k+1-q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{s+1}-\alpha_{s+1}(1,q)\omega_{n-s,k+s-1,k+1-q}.

This is divided into the following three subcases:

1.

When
q=1
, the equality
-\alpha_{s}(1,1)=-\alpha_{s+1}(1,1)
follows from (
13
).

2.

When
q=s+1
, the identity
\alpha_{s}(1,s)\cdot(2s-1)=-\alpha_{s+1}(1,s+1)
holds by (
13
).

3.

When
2\leq q\leq s
, the equality
-\alpha_{s}(1,q)+\alpha_{s}(1,q-1)\cdot(s+q-2)=-\alpha_{s+1}(1,q)
still follows from (
13
).

We now turn to the case
p>1
. This requires us to distinguish the parity of
s
. We begin by assuming
s
is even.
When
p
attains its maximum value of
\frac{s+2}{2}
,
q
must be
1
, and we obtain
\alpha_{s-1}(\frac{s}{2},1)=-\alpha_{s+1}(\frac{s+2}{2},1)
by (
13
).
When
2\leq p\leq\frac{s}{2}
, we need only prove

\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{s+3-2p}\alpha_{s-1}(p-1,q)\cdot\omega_{n-s,k+s-p,k+1-q}+\sum_{q=1}^{s+2-2p}-\alpha_{s}(p,q)\cdot\omega_{n-s,k+s-p,k+1-q}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{q=2}^{s+3-2p}\alpha_{s}(p,q-1)\cdot(s+q-p-1)\omega_{n-s,k+s-p,k+1-q}

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{s+3-2p}-\alpha_{s+1}(p,q)\cdot\omega_{n-s,k+s-p,k+1-q}.

This is also divided into the following three subcases:

1.

When
q=1
, the equality
\alpha_{s-1}(p-1,1)-\alpha_{s}(p,1)=-\alpha_{s+1}(p,1)
follows from (
13
).

2.

When
q=s+3-2p
, the identity

\alpha_{s-1}(p-1,s+3-2p)+\alpha_{s}(p,s+2-2p)\cdot(2s-3p+2)=-\alpha_{s+1}(p,s+3-2p)

holds by (
13
).

3.

When
2\leq q\leq s+2-2p
, the equality

\alpha_{s-1}(p-1,q)-\alpha_{s}(p,q)+\alpha_{s}(p,q-1)\cdot(s+q-p-1)=-\alpha_{s+1}(p,q)

still follows from (
13
).

Next, we consider the case of odd
s
.
When
p
attains its maximum value of
\frac{s+1}{2}
, we need only prove

\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{2}\alpha_{s-1}(p-1,q)\cdot\omega_{n-s,k+s-p,k+1-q}+\sum_{q=1}^{1}-\alpha_{s}(p,q)\cdot\omega_{n-s,k+s-p,k+1-q}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{q=2}^{2}\alpha_{s}(p,q-1)\cdot(s+q-p-1)\omega_{n-s,k+s-p,k+1-q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{2}-\alpha_{s+1}(p,q)\cdot\omega_{n-s,k+s-p,k+1-q}.

At this time, when
q=1
, the identity
\alpha_{s-1}(\frac{s+1}{2}-1,1)-\alpha_{s}(\frac{s+1}{2},1)=-\alpha_{s+1}(\frac{s+1}{2},1)
follows from (
13
); when
q=2
, the identity
\alpha_{s-1}(\frac{s+1}{2}-1,2)+\alpha_{s}(\frac{s+1}{2},1)\cdot\frac{s+1}{2}=-\alpha_{s+1}(\frac{s+1}{2},2)
.
Similarly, when
2\leq p\leq\frac{s-1}{2}
, we need prove (
15
).
The proof follows the same argument as for even
s
and is omitted.

We have now proven that (
14
) holds, and that the formula of
\omega_{n,k-1,k}
also holds for
s
.
This completes the proof by induction.
∎

Next, we need the following two constant term facts to prove our second lemma.

Let
\mathrm{CT}_{x}F(x)
denote the constant term of the formal Laurent series expansion of
F(x)
.

Fact 1
.

Let
i
be a nonnegative integer. If
k
is a nonnegative integer, possibly less than
i
, or if
k
is a half integer, then

\mathop{\mathrm{CT}}_{x}\frac{(1+x)^{k}}{x^{i}}=\binom{k}{i}.

Fact 2
.

If
F(x)
only contains nonnegative power of
x
, then

\mathop{\mathrm{CT}}_{x}F(x)\frac{1}{1-u/x}=\mathop{\mathrm{CT}}_{x}F(x)\sum_{n\geq 0}\left(\frac{u}{x}\right)^{n}=F(u),

where
u
is independent of
x
.

This fact follows easily from the linearity of the constant term operator. For rigorous definitions, see
[
24
]
.

Lemma 2.9
.

For any
n,k,i\in\mathbb{N}
with
i\leq k
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lceil\frac{n+1}{2}\right\rceil}\sum_{q=1}^{\min\{n+3-2p,k+1-i\}}\frac{(-1)^{q-p}\cdot 2^{n-p}}{(n+3-2p-q)!\cdot(p-1)!}\cdot\binom{k-i}{q-1}\cdot\frac{(2n+4k-2p-2q+1-i)!!}{(4k-3-i)!!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{n-1}\binom{n+3k-2}{n}.

Proof.

We denote the left-hand side of the equation by
\mathrm{LHS}
.
We obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{LHS}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lceil\frac{n+1}{2}\right\rceil}\sum_{q=1}^{\min\{n+3-2p,k+1-i\}}(-1)^{p-q}2^{n-p}\binom{n+2-p-q}{p-1}\binom{k-i}{q-1}\cdot

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\cdot\frac{(4k-i-1)(4k-i+1)\cdots(4k-i+1+2n-2p-2q)}{(n+2-p-q)!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lceil\frac{n+1}{2}\right\rceil}\sum_{q=1}^{\min\{n+3-2p,k+1-i\}}(-1)^{p-q}2^{2n-2p-q+2}\binom{n+2-p-q}{p-1}\binom{k-i}{q-1}\cdot

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\cdot\binom{2k+n-p-q+\frac{1-i}{2}}{n+2-p-q}.

By Fact
1
, our problem thus reduces to computing the constant term in the following equation:

\displaystyle\mathrm{LHS}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{CT}}_{x,y,z}\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lceil\frac{n+1}{2}\right\rceil}\sum_{q=1}^{\min\{n+3-2p,k+1-i\}}(-1)^{p-q}2^{2n-2p-q+2}\frac{(1+x)^{k-i}}{x^{q-1}}\cdot\frac{(1+y)^{n+2-p-q}}{y^{p-1}}\cdot

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot\frac{(1+z)^{2k+n-p-q+\frac{1-i}{2}}}{z^{n+2-p-q}}.

At this time, extending the upper bounds of the summations is equivalent to adding some zeros by Fact
1
.
Consequently, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{LHS}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{CT}}_{x,y,z}\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{p-q}2^{2n-2p-q+2}\frac{(1+x)^{k-i}(1+y)^{n+2-p-q}(1+z)^{2k+n-p-q+\frac{1-i}{2}}}{x^{q-1}y^{p-1}z^{n+2-p-q}}.

Applying the geometric series formula yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{LHS}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{CT}}_{x,y,z}\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{p+1}2^{2n-2p+1}(1+y)^{n+1-p}}{(1+x)^{i-k}y^{p-1}z^{n+1-p}(1+z)^{p-2k-n+\frac{i+1}{2}}}\cdot\frac{1}{1+\frac{z}{2x(1+y)(1+z)}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{CT}}_{x,y,z}\frac{2^{2n-1}(1+y)^{n}}{(1+x)^{i-k}z^{n}(1+z)^{-2k-n+\frac{i+3}{2}}}\cdot\frac{1}{1+\frac{z}{4y(1+z)(1+y)}}\cdot\frac{1}{1+\frac{z}{2x(1+y)(1+z)}}.

We first eliminate
x
using Fact
2
, where
u=\frac{-z}{2(1+y)(1+z)}
. This gives

\displaystyle\mathrm{LHS}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{CT}}_{y,z}\frac{2^{2n-1}(1+y)^{n}}{z^{n}(1+z)^{-2k-n+\frac{i+3}{2}}\left(1+\frac{z}{4y(1+z)(1+y)}\right)\left(1-\frac{z}{2(1+y)(1+z)}\right)^{i-k}}.

Next, we eliminate
y
. Only the denominator factor
1+\frac{z}{4y(1+y)(1+z)}
may results in negative powers of
y
. By the quadratic formula, we can write

\frac{1}{1+\frac{z}{4y(1+y)(1+z)}}=\frac{y(1+y)}{(y-Y_{1})(y-Y_{2})}=\frac{(1+y)}{(1-Y_{1}/y)(y-Y_{2})},

where

Y_{1}=\frac{1-\sqrt{1+z}}{2\sqrt{1+z}}=O(z)\quad\text{and}\quad Y_{2}=\frac{-1-\sqrt{1+z}}{2\sqrt{1+z}}=-1+O(z).

Then applying Fact
2
with
u=Y_{1}
gives

\displaystyle\mathrm{LHS}=\mathop{\mathrm{CT}}_{z}2^{n-2}z^{-n}(1+z)^{n+2k-\frac{i+3}{2}}\frac{(1+\sqrt{1+z})^{n+1}}{(\sqrt{1+z})^{n}}\cdot\left(\frac{1+z+\sqrt{1+z}}{(1+z)(1+\sqrt{1+z})}\right)^{k-i}.

Note that some of the above computations were performed via
Maple

[
17
]
.
According to

\frac{1+z+\sqrt{1+z}}{(1+z)(1+\sqrt{1+z})}=\frac{\sqrt{1+z}}{1+z}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+z}},

we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{LHS}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{CT}}_{z}2^{n-2}z^{-n}(1+z)^{n+2k-\frac{i+3}{2}}\frac{(1+\sqrt{1+z})^{n+1}}{(\sqrt{1+z})^{n}}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+z}}\right)^{k-i}

\displaystyle=2^{n-2}\cdot\mathop{\mathrm{CT}}_{z}\frac{(1+z)^{\frac{3k+n-3}{2}}(1+\sqrt{1+z})^{n+1}}{z^{n}}.

It is noteworthy that the above expression is independent of
i
.
We assume the reader is familiar with the knowledge of residues.
Informally, the residue can be viewed as the coefficient of the
z^{-1}
term in a Laurent series expansion.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{LHS}

\displaystyle=2^{n-2}\cdot\mathop{\mathrm{Res}}_{z=0}\frac{(1+z)^{\frac{3k+n-3}{2}}(1+\sqrt{1+z})^{n+1}}{z^{n+1}}

\displaystyle=2^{n-2}\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|z|=\varepsilon}\frac{(1+z)^{\frac{3k+n-3}{2}}(1+\sqrt{1+z})^{n+1}}{z^{n+1}}dz,

where the integration path is taken near
z=0
, with
\varepsilon
sufficiently small.
Let
z=4t+4t^{2}
. Then we have
dz=4(1+2t)dt
and

\displaystyle\mathrm{LHS}

\displaystyle=2^{n-2}\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|t|=\varepsilon}\frac{(1+2t)^{3k+n-3}(2(1+t)^{n+1})}{(4t(1+t))^{n+1}}\cdot 4(1+2t)dt

\displaystyle=2^{n-2}\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|t|=\varepsilon}\frac{(1+2t)^{3k+n-2}}{2^{n-1}t^{n+1}}dt

\displaystyle=2^{-1}\cdot\mathop{\mathrm{Res}}_{t=0}\frac{(1+2t)^{3k+n-2}}{t^{n+1}}=2^{-1}\cdot\mathop{\mathrm{CT}}_{t}\frac{(1+2t)^{3k+n-2}}{t^{n}}

\displaystyle=2^{n-1}\binom{3k+n-2}{n}.

This completes the proof.
∎

1.1

In this subsection, we prove Theorem
2.7
, which completes the proof of Theorem

.

Lemma 2.10
.

For any
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\omega_{n,k-1,k}=0
.

Proof.

Taking
s=n
in Lemma
2.8
gives

\displaystyle\omega_{n,k-1,k}=\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lceil\frac{n+1}{2}\right\rceil}\sum_{q=1}^{n+3-2p}-\alpha_{n+1}(p,q)\cdot\omega_{0,k+n-p,k+1-q}.

(16)

By Eqs. (
13
) and (
12
), we have

\displaystyle\omega_{n,k-1,k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lceil\frac{n+1}{2}\right\rceil}\sum_{q=1}^{n+3-2p}\frac{(-1)^{q-p}\cdot 2^{n-p}}{(n+3-2p-q)!\cdot(q-1)!\cdot(p-1)!}\sum_{i=0}^{k+1-q}\frac{\gamma_{k+1-q-i}}{i!}\cdot(2n+3k-2p-q+i)!!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lceil\frac{n+1}{2}\right\rceil}\sum_{q=1}^{n+3-2p}\frac{(-1)^{q-p}\cdot 2^{n-p}}{(n+3-2p-q)!\cdot(q-1)!\cdot(p-1)!}\sum_{i=0}^{k+1-q}\frac{\gamma_{i}}{(k+1-q-i)!}\cdot(2n+4k-2p-2q+1-i)!!,

where
\gamma_{j}
satisfy recurrence relation (
8
) and initial condition
\gamma_{0}=1
.
By interchanging the order of the summation above, we obtain

\displaystyle\omega_{n,k-1,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lceil\frac{n+1}{2}\right\rceil}\sum_{q=1}^{\min\{n+3-2p,k+1-i\}}\frac{(-1)^{q-p}\cdot 2^{n-p}}{(n+3-2p-q)!\cdot(q-1)!\cdot(p-1)!}\cdot

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot\frac{\gamma_{i}}{(k+1-q-i)!}\cdot(2n+4k-2p-2q+1-i)!!

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\sum_{p=1}^{\left\lceil\frac{n+1}{2}\right\rceil}\sum_{q=1}^{\min\{n+3-2p,k+1-i\}}\frac{(-1)^{q-p}\cdot 2^{n-p}}{(n+3-2p-q)!\cdot(q-1)!\cdot(p-1)!}\cdot

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\frac{(k-i)!}{(k+1-q-i)!}\cdot\frac{(2n+4k-2p-2q+1-i)!!}{(4k-3-i)!!}\cdot\frac{\gamma_{i}}{(k-i)!}\cdot(4k-3-i)!!,

By Lemma
2.9
, the above expression reduces to

\displaystyle\omega_{n,k-1,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}2^{n-1}\binom{n+3k-2}{n}\cdot\frac{\gamma_{i}}{(k-i)!}\cdot(4k-3-i)!!,

\displaystyle=2^{n-1}\binom{n+3k-2}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{\gamma_{i}}{(k-i)!}\cdot(4k-3-i)!!

\displaystyle=0,

where the last equality follows from (
8
).
This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.7
.

For convenience, we set

(17)

The initial conditions for
b_{n,m,k}
in Proposition
2.5

\tilde{\omega}_{0,0,0}=\tilde{\omega}_{1,0,0}=\tilde{\omega}_{0,1,0}=\tilde{\omega}_{0,1,1}=1,\,\,\tilde{\omega}_{-1,m,k}=b_{m-1,m,k}=0

(18)

and

\tilde{\omega}_{n,k-1,k}=b_{n+k-1,k-1,k}=0.

(19)

Note that recurrence (

) for

together with the initial conditions in (

) and (

) determines

To finish the proof, it remains to show that

satisfies the same recurrence relation and initial conditions as those of

Setting

\omega_{n+1,m,k}=\omega_{n,m+1,k}-(m-k+2)\omega_{n,m+1,k-1}-\omega_{n-1,m+1,k},

Subsequently, setting

3.1
An explicit formula for

Theorem 3.1
.

The generating function for
b_{n,k}
is given by

\displaystyle D_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{k-1}{2}}\frac{\gamma_{k-2j-1}}{(2j+1)!\cdot 2^{j+\frac{3k+1}{2}}}\cdot\left(j+\frac{3k-1}{2}\right)!\cdot\binom{2j+3k-1}{j+\frac{3k-1}{2}}\cdot\frac{t^{k-1}}{(1-4t)^{j+\frac{3k}{2}}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{k-1}{2}}\frac{\gamma_{k-2j}\cdot 2^{j+\frac{3k-5}{2}}}{(2j)!}\cdot\left(j+\frac{3k-3}{2}\right)!\cdot\frac{t^{k-1}}{(1-4t)^{\frac{3k-1}{2}+j}}

when

is odd, and

\displaystyle D_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{k}{2}}\frac{\gamma_{k-2j}}{(2j)!\cdot 2^{j+\frac{3k}{2}}}\cdot\left(j+\frac{3k-2}{2}\right)!\cdot\binom{2j+3k-2}{j+\frac{3k-2}{2}}\cdot\frac{t^{k-1}}{(1-4t)^{j+\frac{3k-1}{2}}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{k}{2}-1}\frac{\gamma_{k-2j-1}\cdot 2^{j+\frac{3k-4}{2}}}{(2j+1)!}\cdot\left(j+\frac{3k-2}{2}\right)!\cdot\frac{t^{k-1}}{(1-4t)^{\frac{3k}{2}+j}}

when

Proof.

By Theorem
2.11
, we have

\displaystyle b_{n,k}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{\gamma_{k-i}}{i!}\cdot\frac{2^{n-k}}{(n-k+1)!}\cdot(2n+k+i-1)!!,

(20)

where
\gamma_{j}
satisfy the recursion (
8
) and the initial condition
\gamma_{0}=1
. We need to derive the formula for

according to the parity of

We begin with the case for

It is convenience to rewrite (

b_{n,k}=\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor}\frac{\gamma_{k-2j}}{(2j)!}\cdot\frac{(2n+k+2j-1)!!}{(n-k+1)!}2^{n-k}+\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lceil\frac{k}{2}\right\rceil-1}\frac{\gamma_{k-2j-1}}{(2j+1)!}\cdot\frac{(2n+k+2j)!!}{(n-k+1)!}2^{n-k}.

(21)

When

is odd and

, we first consider the following generating function:

\displaystyle T_{1}:=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(2n+k+2j)!!}{(n-k+1)!}\cdot 2^{n-k}t^{n}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{2^{-j-\frac{3k+1}{2}}\cdot(2n+k+2j+1)!}{(n+j+\frac{k+1}{2})!\cdot(n-k+1)!}t^{n}.

Setting

gives

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geq j+\frac{k+1}{2}}\frac{2^{-j-\frac{3k+1}{2}}\cdot(2m)!}{m!\cdot(m-(j+\frac{3k-1}{2}))!}t^{m-j-\frac{k+1}{2}}

\displaystyle=2^{-j-\frac{3k+1}{2}}\cdot t^{-j-\frac{k+1}{2}}\sum_{m\geq j+\frac{k+1}{2}}\binom{2m}{m}\binom{m}{j+\frac{3k-1}{2}}\cdot\left(j+\frac{3k-1}{2}\right)!\cdot t^{m}.

By introducing a variable

, we have

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=2^{-j-\frac{3k+1}{2}}\cdot\left(j+\frac{3k-1}{2}\right)!\cdot t^{-j-\frac{k+1}{2}}\sum_{m\geq j+\frac{k+1}{2}}\binom{2m}{m}[z^{j+\frac{3k-1}{2}}](1+z)^{m}t^{m}

\displaystyle=2^{-j-\frac{3k+1}{2}}\cdot\left(j+\frac{3k-1}{2}\right)!\cdot t^{-j-\frac{k+1}{2}}[z^{j+\frac{3k-1}{2}}]\sum_{m\geq 0}\binom{2m}{m}((1+z)t)^{m}.

Since
\sum_{s\geq 0}\binom{2s}{s}t^{s}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-4t}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=2^{-j-\frac{3k+1}{2}}\cdot\left(j+\frac{3k-1}{2}\right)!\cdot t^{-j-\frac{k+1}{2}}[z^{j+\frac{3k-1}{2}}]\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-4(1+z)t}}

\displaystyle=2^{-j-\frac{3k+1}{2}}\cdot\left(j+\frac{3k-1}{2}\right)!\cdot t^{-j-\frac{k+1}{2}}[z^{j+\frac{3k-1}{2}}]\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-4t}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{4tz}{1-4t}}}.

According to
(1-t)^{-\alpha}=1+\sum_{s\geq 1}\frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)\cdots(\alpha+s-1)}{s!}t^{s}
, we have

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=2^{-j-\frac{3k+1}{2}}\cdot\left(j+\frac{3k-1}{2}\right)!\cdot t^{-j-\frac{k+1}{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-4t}}[z^{j+\frac{3k-1}{2}}]\cdot\sum_{s\geq 0}\binom{2s}{s}\left(\frac{tz}{1-4t}\right)^{s}

\displaystyle=2^{-j-\frac{3k+1}{2}}\cdot\left(j+\frac{3k-1}{2}\right)!\cdot t^{-j-\frac{k+1}{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-4t}}\binom{2j+3k-1}{j+\frac{3k-1}{2}}\frac{t^{j+\frac{3k-1}{2}}}{(1-4t)^{j+\frac{3k-1}{2}}},

which can be simplified to

T_{1}=2^{-j-\frac{3k+1}{2}}\cdot\left(j+\frac{3k-1}{2}\right)!\cdot\binom{2j+3k-1}{j+\frac{3k-1}{2}}\frac{t^{k-1}}{(1-4t)^{j+\frac{3k}{2}}}.

(22)

Secondly, we consider the following generating function:

\displaystyle T_{2}:=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(2n+k+2j-1)!!}{(n-k+1)!}\cdot 2^{n-k}t^{n}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{2^{n+j+\frac{k-1}{2}}\cdot(n+j+\frac{k-1}{2})!\cdot 2^{n-k}}{(n-k+1)!}t^{n}.

Setting
m=n-k+1
and
k=2p+1
(since

is odd) yields

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{2^{m+j+3p}\cdot(m+j+3p)!\cdot 2^{m-1}}{m!}t^{m+2p}=2^{j+3p-1}t^{2p}\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{(3p+m+j)!}{m!}(4t)^{m}

\displaystyle=2^{j+3p-1}t^{2p}\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{(3p+j)!\cdot(3p+j+1)(3p+j+2)\cdots(3p+j+m)}{m!}(4t)^{m}

\displaystyle=2^{j+3p-1}(3p+j)!t^{2p}\cdot\frac{1}{(1-4t)^{3p+j+1}}.

Therefore, we have

T_{2}=2^{j+\frac{3k-5}{2}}\left(\frac{3k-3}{2}+j\right)!\frac{t^{k-1}}{(1-4t)^{\frac{3k-1}{2}+j}}.

(23)

Combining (

), (

for odd

Similarly, we can derive the generating function for

when

3.2
Functional equations for
B_{k}(x,t)
via the kernel method

We aim to derive a system of functional equations for
B_{k}(x,t)
using Proposition
2.5
and the kernel method.
This is an independent approach for
D_{k}(t)
.
Let
F_{0}(x,t)=1
and

F_{k}(x,t):=\sum_{k\leq m\leq n}(m-k+1)b_{n,m,k-1}x^{n-m}t^{m}

for
k\geq 1
.
It is clear that for
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle F_{k}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq k\leq m\leq n}m\cdot b_{n,m,k-1}x^{n-m}t^{m}+\sum_{0\leq k\leq m\leq n}(1-k)\cdot b_{n,m,k-1}x^{n-m}t^{m}

\displaystyle=t\cdot\frac{\partial B_{k-1}(x,t)}{\partial t}+(1-k)\cdot B_{k-1}(x,t).

By the recurrence of
b_{n,m,k}
in Proposition
2.5
, we obtain

\displaystyle B_{k}(x,t)

\displaystyle=F_{k}(x,t)+\sum_{0\leq k\leq m\leq n}b_{n,m-1,k}x^{n-m}t^{m}+\sum_{0\leq k\leq m\leq n}b_{n-1,m,k}x^{n-m}t^{m},

\displaystyle=F_{k}(x,t)+\frac{t}{x}\cdot B_{k}(x,t)-\frac{t}{x}\cdot D_{k}(t)+x\cdot B_{k}(x,t).

Therefore, we have

\left(1-x-\frac{t}{x}\right)B_{k}(x,t)=F_{k}(x,t)-\frac{t}{x}\cdot D_{k}(t).

(24)

This is a special type of functional equation with two unknowns
B_{k}(x,t)
and
D_{k}(t)
, where
the factor
\left(1-x-\frac{t}{x}\right)
on the left-hand side is called the
kernel
of (
24
).
Solving the kernel
1-x-\frac{t}{x}=0
, i.e.,
x^{2}-x+t=0
for
x
gives two solutions:

X_{1}(t)=\frac{1+\sqrt{1-4t}}{2}=1+O(t)\quad\text{and}\quad X_{2}(t)=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4t}}{2}=t\cdot C(t).

According to the kernel method
[
3
]
, we can substitute
x=X_{2}(t)
to obtain

\displaystyle D_{k}(t)=\left(\frac{x}{t}\cdot F_{k}(x,t)\right)\Big|_{x=X_{2}(t)}.

The above functional equations are summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.2
.

For
k\geq 1
, we have the following system of functional equations

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &D_{k}(t)=\left(\frac{x}{t}\cdot F_{k}(x,t)\right)\Big|_{x=X_{2}(t)},\\
&F_{k}(x,t)=t\cdot\frac{\partial B_{k-1}(x,t)}{\partial t}+(1-k)\cdot B_{k-1}(x,t),\\
&B_{k}(x,t)=\frac{F_{k}(x,t)-\frac{t}{x}\cdot D_{k}(t)}{1-x-\frac{t}{x}},\end{aligned}\right.

with the initial condition
F_{0}(x,t)=1
and
D_{0}(t)=C(t)=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4t}}{2t}
.

From a computational perspective, Theorem
3.2
is highly practical to compute expressions for
D_{k}(t)
and
B_{k}(x,t)
.
In the computational implementation, we perform the substitution
t=u(1-u)
, thus ensuring that we consistently work with rational functions and avoid fractional exponents. At this time, we have

X_{2}(t)=u=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4t}}{2},\quad B_{0}(x,t)=\frac{-1}{x-(1-u)}.

and

\frac{\partial B_{k-1}(x,t)}{\partial t}=\frac{1}{1-2u}\cdot\frac{\partial B_{k-1}(x,u(1-u))}{\partial u}.

Using Theorem
3.2
, we can easily compute expressions for
D_{k}(t)
for
k\leq 140
by
Maple
,
which agree with the results from Theorem
3.1
. This gives support for the truth of Funchs and Yu’s enumerative conjecture (Theorem
1.1
) in our earlier study.

B.1
P
-partitions and linear extensions

In this subsection, we introduce some concepts and useful lemmas regarding
P
-partitions and linear extensions.

Let
(P,\preceq)
be a finite
partially ordered set
(or
poset
).
We say that
t

covers

s
if
s\prec t
and there is no
c\in P
such that
s\prec c\prec t
.
The
Hasse diagram
of a finite poset
P
is a graph whose vertices are the elements of
P
, whose edges represent cover relations, and in which, if
s\prec t
, then
t
is drawn above
s
in the diagram.

A
P
-partition
is a map
\sigma:P\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
satisfying the following condition:
If
s\prec t
in
P
, then
\sigma(s)\geq\sigma(t)
; that is,
\sigma
is
order-reversing
.
In addition, if
\sum_{s\in P}\sigma(s)=n
, we say
\sigma
is a
P
-partition of
n
and write
|\sigma|=n
.
It is often convenient to represent the relation
s\prec t
by an arrow
s\to t
.
In this notation, a
P
-partition corresponds to a vertex labeling
\sigma
such that for every arrow
s\to t
, we have
\sigma(s)\geq\sigma(t)
.

The concept of
P
-partitions was first investigated by MacMahon
[
16
, Subsections 439,441]
.
In 1970, Knuth
[
13
]
provided clarification on MacMahon’s work within the theory of
P
-partitions.
Later, Knuth
[
13
]
offered a clarification of MacMahon’s results in his 1970 work on the theory of
P
-partitions.
The first systematic treatment of this subject, however, was established by Stanley in his seminal papers
[
20
,
21
]
.

We denote the set of all
P
-partitions on the poset
P
as
\pi(P)
.
The fundamental generating function associated with
\pi(P)
is defined as

F_{P}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{p})=\sum_{\sigma\in\pi(P)}x_{1}^{\sigma(1)}x_{2}^{\sigma(2)}\cdots x_{p}^{\sigma(p)}.

By setting
x_{i}=q
for all
i
, we obtain the
enumerative generating function
of
\pi(P)
:

\displaystyle G_{P}(q)=F_{P}(q,q,\ldots,q)=\sum_{\sigma\in\pi(P)}q^{|\sigma|}.

The set
[p]:=\{1,2,\ldots,p\}
with its usual order forms a
p
-element chain, that is, a poset in which all elements are totally ordered. This poset is denoted
\mathbf{p}
.
Let
\#P=p
. An order-preserving bijection
\tau:P\rightarrow\mathbf{p}
is called a
linear extension
of
P
.
The number of linear extensions of
P
is denoted by
e(P)
.

Now, let
(P,\preceq)
be a finite poset on the set
[p]
.
Any linear extension
\tau\colon P\to\mathbf{p}
can be identified with the permutation
\tau^{-1}(1),\tau^{-1}(2),\dots,\tau^{-1}(p)
of
[p]
.
The collection of all such
e(P)
permutations is denoted by
\mathcal{L}(P)
and is called the
Jordan–Hölder set
of
P
.
Counting linear extensions of a poset is a computationally difficult problem, and was shown by Brightwell and Winkler
[
5
]
to be #P-complete.

Let
\mu=\mu_{1}\mu_{2}\cdots\mu_{p}
be a permutation of
[p]
. For
1\leq i\leq p-1
, if
\mu_{i}>\mu_{i+1}
, then
i
is called a
descent
of
\mu
.
The
descent set
of
\mu
is defined as

D_{\mu}=\{i\in[p-1]:\mu_{i}>\mu_{i+1}\}.

Let
d(\mu)
denote the number of descents of
\mu
, that is,
d(\mu)=\#D_{\mu}
.

Theorem B.1

(
[
22
, Theorem 3.15.5]
)
.

Let
(P,\preceq)
be a finite poset on
[p]:=\{1,2,\ldots,p\}
. Then

\displaystyle F_{P}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{p})=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{L}(P)}\frac{\prod_{j\in D_{\mu}}x_{\mu_{1}}x_{\mu_{2}}\cdots x_{\mu_{j}}}{\prod_{i=1}^{p}(1-x_{\mu_{1}}x_{\mu_{2}}\cdots x_{\mu_{i}})}.

Define the
major index

\mathrm{maj}(\mu)
of
\mu
by
\mathrm{maj}(\mu)=\sum_{j\in D_{\mu}}j
.

Theorem B.2

(
[
22
, Theorem 3.15.7]
)
.

Let
(P,\preceq)
be a finite poset on

Let

Lemma B.6

Let

For a
n
-element chain, we know that
e(L_{n})=1
. It follows that
f_{n,0}=1
.
Now we consider the cases
1\leq k\leq n

Given a positive integer sequence

or

\displaystyle e(F_{(n;i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k})})=\frac{(n+k)!}{(n+k)!}\cdot i_{1}(i_{2}+1)(i_{3}+2)\cdots(i_{k}+k-1).

∎

Lemma B.7
.

Let
n
be a positive integer and

f_{n,0}=1,\quad f_{n,k}=\frac{(n-k+1)(n-k+2)\cdots(n+k)}{2^{k}\cdot k!}\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq k\leq n,

\sum_{n\geq 0}f_{n,k}x^{n}=\frac{(2k-1)!!\cdot x^{k}}{(1-x)^{2k+1}}.

(27)

Proof.

We know that

f_{n,k}=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n-k+1}\sum_{i_{2}=i_{1}+1}^{n-k+2}\cdots\sum_{i_{k}=i_{k-1}+1}^{n}i_{1}(i_{2}+1)(i_{3}+2)\cdots(i_{k}+k-1)\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq k\leq n.

The above equation can be written equivalently as

f_{n,k}=\sum_{c_{1}\geq 1;\ c_{k}\leq n+k-1\atop c_{i}-c_{i-1}\geq 2\text{ for }2\leq i\leq k}c_{1}c_{2}\cdots c_{k}.

We consider the following two cases according to

1): When

, we have
c_{k-1}\leq c_{k}-2=n+k-3
and

\displaystyle f_{n,k}

\displaystyle=(n+k-1)\sum_{c_{1}\geq 1;\ c_{k-1}\leq n+k-3\atop c_{i}-c_{i-1}\geq 2\text{ for }2\leq i\leq k-1}c_{1}c_{2}\cdots c_{k-1}

\displaystyle=(n+k-1)\cdot f_{n-1,k-1}.

2): When
c_{k}\neq n+k-1
, we get
f_{n,k}=f_{n-1,k}
. Therefore, we obtain

f_{n,k}=f_{n-1,k}+(n+k-1)\cdot f_{n-1,k-1}.

Let

g_{n,k}=\frac{(n-k+1)(n-k+2)\cdots(n+k)}{2^{k}\cdot k!}.

One can verify that

.
It is clear that

and

It follows that

f_{n,k}=\frac{(n-k+1)(n-k+2)\cdots(n+k)}{2^{k}\cdot k!}\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq k\leq n.

Now we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}f_{n,k}x^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq k}\frac{(n-k+1)(n-k+2)\cdots(n+k)}{2^{k}\cdot k!}x^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2k)!}{k!\cdot 2^{k}}\sum_{n\geq k}\frac{(n+k)(n+k-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{(2k)!}x^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2k)!}{k!\cdot 2^{k}}\sum_{n\geq k}\binom{n+k}{2k}x^{n}=\frac{(2k)!}{k!\cdot 2^{k}}\sum_{m\geq 0}\binom{2k+m}{m}x^{m+k}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2k-1)!!\cdot x^{k}}{(1-x)^{2k+1}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Let

\displaystyle b_{n,k}=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n}e(D_{(n;i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k})}).

and

Theorem B.10
.

Let
n
be a positive integer and
0\leq k\leq n
. Then we have

\displaystyle b_{n,k}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}\binom{2n+k}{k-i}\binom{n-i}{k-i}\cdot(k-i)!\cdot u_{n,i}.

Proof.

This formula is derived by applying the inclusion–exclusion principle and Lemma
B.4
.
We first illustrate the above formula with an example before proving the general case.

Let
n=4
and
k=2
. Given
i_{1}=2,i_{2}=3
, we obtain the decomposition via the inclusion-exclusion principle as shown in Fig.
10
.

Figure 10:
An example for the decomposition via the inclusion-exclusion principle.

The equation in Fig.
10
means that

\displaystyle e(D_{(4;2,3)})=e(P_{1})-(e(P_{2})+e(P_{3}))+e(P_{4}).

Recall that
I_{m}
denotes an
m
-element antichain.
It is clear that

e(P_{1})=e(I_{2}+U_{(4;0)})=\binom{10}{2}\cdot 2!\cdot\mathrm{Cat}(4)

and

e(D_{(4;2,3)})=e(I_{2}+U_{(4;0)})-(e(I_{1}+U_{(4;2)})+e(I_{1}+U_{(4;3)}))+e(U_{(4;2,3)}).

The above discussion can be generalized to the general case.
Given a positive integer sequence
1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n
, by an application of the inclusion–exclusion principle, we obtain

\displaystyle e(D_{(n;i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k})})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j}\sum_{(h_{1},\ldots,h_{j})\subseteq(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})}e(I_{k-j}+U_{(n;h_{1},\ldots,h_{j})})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j}\sum_{(h_{1},\ldots,h_{j})\subseteq(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})}\binom{2n+k}{k-j}e(I_{k-j})e(U_{(n;h_{1},\ldots,h_{j})})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j}\binom{2n+k}{k-j}(k-j)!\sum_{(h_{1},\ldots,h_{j})\subseteq(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})}e(U_{(n;h_{1},\ldots,h_{j})}).

The summation of both sides over all integer sequences
1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n
yields

\displaystyle b_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j}\binom{2n+k}{k-j}(k-j)!\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n}\sum_{(h_{1},\ldots,h_{j})\subseteq(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})}e(U_{(n;h_{1},\ldots,h_{j})})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j}\binom{2n+k}{k-j}(k-j)!\sum_{1\leq h_{1}<\cdots<h_{j}\leq n}\binom{n-j}{k-j}e(U_{(n;h_{1},\ldots,h_{j})})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j}\binom{2n+k}{k-j}(k-j)!\binom{n-j}{k-j}u_{n,j}.

This completes the proof.
∎
