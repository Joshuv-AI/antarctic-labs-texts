A reinforcement learning-enhanced discrete zebra optimization algorithm for solving the traveling salesman problem
Authors: Sajjad Ghatei, Shiva TaghipourEivazi, Ahmad Habibi Zadnavin, Ali Ghaffari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, a novel hybrid algorithm named the Reinforced Zebra Optimization Algorithm (RZOA) is proposed to solve the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP). Initially, a discrete version of the Zebra Optimization Algorithm, referred to as DZOA, was developed, in which the continuous relationships of the original ZOA were transformed into a set of discrete operators to effectively update the routes. Subsequently, to enhance the intelligence of the operator selection process and to maintain a dynamic balance between exploration and exploitation, a mechanism based on Deep Reinforcement Learning was designed. Within the framework of the proposed RZOA, each zebra agent adaptively and self-learnedly makes decisions using a Deep Q-Network (DQN). Each agent observes both its own state and the overall population state, employs the DQN to select the optimal operator, and improves its decision-making policy dynamically through an experience replay memory. The state–action–reward structure was carefully designed to ensure an intelligent and balanced learning behavior between local and global search processes. The results of numerical experiments conducted on 42 standard benchmark datasets from the TSPLIB repository demonstrate that the proposed RZOA exhibits significant superiority over the compared algorithms in terms of solution quality, convergence speed, and performance stability. In particular, the proposed method achieves near-optimal performance with an average Percentage Deviation of the Best solution (PDB) below 1% and an average Percentage Deviation of the Average solution (PDA) typically below 0.5% for small and medium-scale instances, while maintaining PDA values within 4–5% for large-scale problems. Moreover, RZOA outperforms competing algorithms in the majority of benchmark cases, demonstrating superior robustness and consistency. Furthermore, the Friedman and Wilcoxon statistical analyses confirm this superiority at a 95% confidence level. Overall, by integrating the global search capability of the Zebra Optimization Algorithm with the adaptive decision-making power of the DQN, the proposed RZOA provides a novel, intelligent, and efficient approach for solving complex combinatorial optimization problems.Keywords: Traveling Salesman Problem, Zebra Optimization Algorithm, Discretization, Reinforcement LearningSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In recent years, extensive research has been conducted to address various optimization problems, including discrete single-objective, multi-objective, and combinatorial problems1–8 through the use of metaheuristic algorithms, which have become increasingly popular. Among these approaches, swarm intelligence–based algorithms have attracted significant attention. Such algorithms are generally inspired by collective behaviors and population-based patterns observed in nature. Representative examples include the Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)9, Bee Colony Optimization10, Gorilla Troops Optimizer (GTO)11, African Vulture Optimization Algorithm (AVOA)12, and the Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO)13. The key characteristic shared by these algorithms lies in the exchange of information among individuals within the population during the problem-solving process.

One of the most well-known and fundamental combinatorial optimization problems frequently used to evaluate the performance of swarm intelligence algorithms is the Traveling Salesman Problem14. This problem has broad applications in domains such as robot path planning, telecommunication network design, production scheduling, vehicle routing, and integrated circuit design. In the TSP, the goal is to determine a tour that visits a given set of cities exactly once and returns to the starting city, while minimizing the total travel distance15. The TSP is theoretically classified as an NP-hard problem, meaning that the computational time required to obtain the optimal solution increases exponentially with the number of cities. Consequently, exact methods such as branch and bound, integer programming, and cutting-plane algorithms are only practical for small-scale instances and become computationally infeasible for medium or large-scale problems due to their excessive time and memory requirements. Therefore, the use of metaheuristic algorithms, capable of producing high-quality near-optimal solutions within acceptable computational time, becomes essential16. In line with this need, a variety of metaheuristic algorithms based on swarm intelligence have been introduced over the past decades for solving the TSP, such as the Discrete Grey Wolf Optimizer (D-GWO)17, the Discrete Whale Optimization Algorithm (DWOA)18, and the Discrete Bat Algorithm (DBA)19. Despite their relative success, most of these methods rely on fixed rules or random operator selection to explore the solution space. This often leads to an imbalance between exploration and exploitation, causing premature convergence or trapping the algorithm in local optima.

In 2022, Trojovská et al. introduced a new metaheuristic algorithm called the Zebra Optimization Algorithm20, designed for solving both constrained and unconstrained continuous optimization problems. Due to its simple structure, lack of numerous control parameters, and robust performance, ZOA has been successfully applied to various fields, such as PID controller design optimization21, 3D path planning based on Bézier curves22, microgrid management and scheduling23, autonomous vehicle routing24, and DC motor drive system controller design25. Although ZOA has been employed in many optimization tasks, it has not yet been applied to the TSP.

In the proposed framework of this study, the core of the DZOA is integrated with a decision-making layer based on DQN. In this structure, each zebra agent, at each iteration, observes both its internal state and the global state of the population. This combined information is fed into the DQN, which outputs the optimal operator or control parameter to be applied in the next step. This mechanism ensures that operator selection and parameter adjustment are neither static nor random, but are determined dynamically based on the current state of the search process. The resulting algorithm, referred to as the RZOA, therefore combines the global exploration capability of ZOA with the adaptive, intelligent decision-making ability of deep reinforcement learning. To evaluate the performance of the proposed approach, the RZOA was compared against nine representative metaheuristic algorithms from the literature using 42 benchmark instances from the TSPLIB dataset. The results of the experiments and statistical analyses clearly demonstrate the effectiveness and superiority of RZOA.

Related work on TSP

In this section, the conventional algorithms for solving the Traveling Salesman Problem, as well as deep learning–based approaches applied to TSP, are reviewed. Traditional TSP-solving algorithms are generally categorized into three main groups. The first group includes exact methods such as the Branch and Bound algorithm26,27, which aim to find the deterministic optimal solution to the problem. However, since these methods are non-polynomial in complexity, their execution time increases exponentially with problem size. Therefore, they are impractical and time-consuming for large-scale TSP instances. The second group consists of approximation methods28, which provide near-optimal solutions within shorter computational times by simplifying the model or employing mathematical approximations. Finally, the third group comprises metaheuristic algorithms, which seek to find high-quality approximate solutions in a reasonable amount of time. Owing to their strong ability to explore vast solution spaces, these methods have gained significant attention in recent years. Among the most well-known algorithms in this category are Ant Colony Optimization (ACO)29–31, Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)32–35, Artificial Bee Colony (ABC)36–39, Discrete JAYA40, Bat Algorithm (BA)41–43, Discrete Sparrow Search Algorithm (DSSA)44, Discrete Komodo Algorithm (DKA)45, Discrete Differentiated Creative Search (DDCS)46, Pathfinder Algorithm (PFA)47, Artificial Rat Algorithm (ARA)48, Cuckoo Search (CS)49,50, and Discrete Spider Monkey Optimization (DSMO)51.

Deep learning–based algorithms developed for solving the Traveling Salesman Problem are typically classified into four main categories52. The first category includes end-to-end constructive algorithms, which employ neural networks to generate complete solutions from scratch53,54. These methods are known for their high computational speed, but they generally produce lower-quality solutions. The second category includes end-to-end improvement algorithms, which iteratively refine and improve initial solutions to achieve higher-quality results, though at the cost of greater computational effort55,56. The third category encompasses hybrid algorithms based on large language models, which can automatically generate and improve heuristics by leveraging vast amounts of data and pre-trained knowledge. These approaches automate much of the algorithm design process, traditionally dependent on human expertise, in a data-driven and self-learning manner57–59. Finally, the fourth category, which is the focus of this study, includes direct hybrid algorithms. In these approaches, deep learning is directly integrated into the heuristic search framework of optimization algorithms. In other words, such methods combine the pattern-learning capability of deep neural networks with the systematic search ability of heuristics to identify optimal solutions in the search space60. In this model, deep learning is employed to detect underlying features and hidden patterns within TSP data, and the acquired knowledge is then used to guide the search process. Consequently, the algorithm benefits both from the generalization ability of deep learning and the structured exploration mechanism of heuristic methods. Several representative examples of these hybrid algorithms are discussed below.

Xing and Tu integrated Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) with Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)61. In this approach, the GNN captures the structural dependencies and interactions among graph nodes, generating prior probabilities for node selection. These probabilities guide MCTS to explore more promising search paths. Experimental results demonstrated that the GNN–MCTS framework generalized effectively to large-scale problems with up to 1,000 nodes, even when trained on small instances. Wang et al. proposed a Self-Supervised Reinforcement Learning (SSRL) framework that combines self-supervised learning with the Lin–Kernighan–Helsgaun (LKH) metaheuristic62. This model employs a dual-decoder GNN architecture: the edge decoder learns edge scores via self-supervised learning, while the node decoder refines node penalties using reinforcement learning. SSRL successfully reconstructs and enhances the core components of LKH without expert-designed rules. It leverages Self-Supervised Q-Learning and Self-Supervised Actor-Critic methods to improve learning stability and efficiency. Experimental findings confirmed that SSRL outperforms LKH, VSR-LKH, and Neuro LKH in large-scale TSP instances, achieving better generalization and higher computational efficiency.

In63, a method was proposed to enhance the generalization capability of pre-trained Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) by integrating them with the LKH algorithm. Large TSP instances were divided into smaller subproblems, each containing a fixed number of cities consistent with the GCN training size. The resulting sub-solutions were combined through an attention-based merging mechanism. The merged probabilistic heatmap was then used to construct a set of candidate edges for LKH. Experimental results demonstrated that this approach significantly improved GCN generalization and achieved superior performance compared with LKH, Neuro LKH, VSR-LKH, and Att-GCN+MCTS across instances ranging from 20 to 10,000 cities. Ye et al. introduced Deep ACO64, a GNN-based metaheuristic that integrates learned heuristics into Ant Colony Optimization. The framework consists of two stages: in the first stage, the GNN learns problem-specific heuristic maps; in the second stage, ACO utilizes these maps to guide exploration and perform local optimization. Deep ACO also employs Neural-guided Local Search to escape local optima by alternating between standard improvement steps and heuristic-driven exploration. Experimental results indicated that Deep ACO outperforms classical ACO variants and demonstrates competitive performance with deep learning–based algorithms on random uniform Euclidean instances of up to 1,000 nodes.

Research on ZOA

The basic ZOA

In this section, the Zebra Optimization Algorithm inspired by the natural behavioral patterns of zebras is introduced, and its mathematical model is described. Zebras are members of the equine family that primarily inhabit the eastern and southern regions of Africa. These animals are distinguished by their characteristic black and white striped coats, which not only create a unique appearance but also serve vital biological functions, such as camouflage against predators and protection from biting insects65. The social behavior of zebras in the wild generally follows two main patterns: foraging behavior and defensive behavior against predators. During foraging, the leading zebra acts as a pathfinder, navigating through plains and guiding the herd toward grasslands and food sources, while the other zebras follow it resulting in a coordinated and collective movement pattern66. Conversely, when faced with threats, zebras exhibit two distinct defense strategies: against predators such as lions, they adopt a zigzag escape pattern to confuse the attacker; however, when facing smaller predators like wild dogs or hyenas, they form protective clusters, moving cohesively to intimidate or disorient the enemy65. The mathematical modeling of these two intelligent behaviors forms the foundation of the ZOA.

The ZOA is a population-based optimization algorithm, where each zebra represents a candidate solution to the problem, and its position in the search space corresponds to a set of decision variable values. The optimization process consists of multiple iterations, during which the positions of the population members are updated based on two key behaviors of zebras in nature: (i) foraging, and (ii) defensive strategy against predators. In the foraging phase, the leading zebra representing the best solution in the population guides the other zebras toward its position. This process is mathematically modeled by Eq. (6)66,67:

This relation ensures that population members gradually move toward the best current solution, thereby exploring more promising regions of the search space.

In the defensive phase, zebra positions are updated by simulating their behavior when attacked by predators. When the predator is a lion, zebras escape in a zigzag pattern, representing the exploration phase68; when smaller predators such as hyenas or wild dogs attack, the zebras cluster together and perform coordinated, aggressive movements, forming the exploitation phase65. In each iteration, the fitness of every zebra (solution) is calculated, and the best-performing individual is identified as the current leader. Upon completion, the best candidate solution found throughout the iterations is reported as the final optimal solution.

Related work on ZOA

In recent years, numerous studies have been conducted to improve the performance of ZOA and expand its applications across various domains.

Krithiga et al.69 introduced a hybrid version of ZOA combined with Long Short-Term Memory neural networks, termed MLSTM-ZOA. The primary goal of this work was to minimize Total Harmonic Distortion (THD) in multilevel inverters. Experimental results demonstrated that the proposed model outperformed algorithms such as MPA, HHO, MGMPA, and SF-BOA in both convergence speed and solution accuracy, achieving a THD reduction between 41% and 81%. Subsequently, El-Hageen et al.70 proposed an enhanced version of ZOA incorporating a Chaotic Map, referred to as CZOA, designed for optimal Cluster Head selection in Wireless Sensor Networks to minimize energy consumption and extend network lifetime. Results indicated that CZOA effectively avoided local optima and improved population diversity, leading to significant energy savings and an increased number of active nodes in the network.

Similarly, Abdelmalek et al.71 employed ZOA for Maximum Power Point Tracking optimization in photovoltaic systems. Under Partial Shading Conditions and dynamic environments, ZOA achieved a tracking efficiency of 99.95% and a 15% faster convergence speed compared to competing algorithms such as GWO, PSO, FPA, and WOA. Experimental results also revealed improved stability and reduced oscillations at the operating point, demonstrating that ZOA is a robust and reliable approach for solar energy systems. In the power systems domain, Arkwazee and Abttan72 developed an improved version called IMZOA to solve the Combined Economic Emission Dispatch problem. IMZOA integrates adaptive search mechanisms and cubic modeling to enhance performance across power systems of different scales (6, 31, and 110-unit test systems). Results showed that IMZOA not only reduced fuel costs by up to 0.8% but also significantly lowered emissions compared to PSO, LM, SA, and other advanced algorithms.

In the field of artificial intelligence, Emine Baş and Şaban Baş73 applied ZOA for the training of Artificial Neural Networks. Their research demonstrated that ZOA can serve as an effective alternative to traditional gradient-based learning methods, improving convergence rate and prediction accuracy. Compared with GOA, PDO, and OOA, the ZOA-trained network exhibited greater stability and lower prediction error. Finally, Ren and Feng21 introduced the Modified ZOA (MZOA) for the automatic tuning of PID controller parameters. This version integrates a chaotic map, Householder mirror learning, and a tangent flight strategy to overcome issues such as premature convergence and slow optimization observed in the standard ZOA. Simulation results confirmed that MZOA achieves more accurate and faster PID parameter tuning, yielding a system response curve that closely tracks the reference signal.

The proposed reinforced ZOA for TSP

Discretization process of ZOA and structure of DZOA

In its original form, ZOA was designed for continuous optimization problems, where the positions of agents in the vector space Rd are updated using social alignment and predator avoidance equations20. This property makes ZOA highly effective in solving continuous mathematical functions. However, the permutational nature of combinatorial optimization problems such as TSP does not align with such continuous movements. In these problems, the search space consists of a finite set of permutations, each representing a specific visiting order of cities. Therefore, it is necessary to redesign the ZOA framework so that the agents’ movements are interpreted not as continuous vector shifts but as discrete transformations among permutations. This redesign process is known as the discretization of ZOA (DZOA).

The discretization process of ZOA proposed in this study is based on replacing continuous motion equations with discrete local operators. In the continuous version, an agent Xi moves to a new position in the continuous space at each iteration based on its relative position to other agents. In contrast, in the discrete version, the same agent is represented as a tour Ti = (c1, c2, …, cn), and its movement corresponds to applying a neighborhood operator on that permutation. The set of operators in DZOA is designed to maintain a balance between exploration and exploitation. These operators include: Swap, which exchanges the positions of two cities in the tour (a small, exploitation-oriented movement), Insertion, which removes a city and reinserts it in another position, 2-opt, which reverses a segment of the tour by removing and reconnecting two edges, and 3-opt, which deletes three edges and reconnects the resulting subpaths in multiple possible configurations. Each operator contributes uniquely to improving and diversifying the search process. The following describes each operator mathematically and schematically.

Deep reinforcement learning and the design of state, action, and reward

In classical metaheuristic algorithms, the search mechanism is typically based on static rules or randomized choices. For instance, in many algorithms, the selection of search operators is predefined and non-adaptive meaning that throughout all iterations, the algorithm employs fixed probabilities or simple random schemes to choose operators. This characteristic often causes the algorithm to either overemphasize exploration, wasting computational effort in unpromising regions, or conversely, to overexploit the search space, thereby becoming trapped in local optima78. To overcome these limitations, the idea of integrating reinforcement learning (RL) with metaheuristic algorithms has recently emerged as a promising approach79. RL is a branch of machine learning in which an agent interacts with a dynamic environment and, through repeated trial and error, learns which actions to take in each state to maximize its long-term cumulative reward. The agent has no prior knowledge of the environment; instead, it gradually learns an optimal decision-making policy purely from experience and feedback. The learning process in RL is defined through four main components:

Environment

the problem the agent aims to solve (here, TSP and the population structure of DZOA).

Agent

the decision-making entity that observes the environment’s state and selects an action according to its current policy.

Reward

a numerical feedback signal from the environment that evaluates the quality of the agent’s action.

Policy

a mapping from states to actions that governs the agent’s behavior over time.

The proposed RZOA

Overview and objective

The hybrid RZOA algorithm is designed to integrate the local search capability of the DZOA with the adaptive decision-making ability of deep reinforcement learning. In this approach, the DZOA core is responsible for generating and refining tours within the discrete search space of the TSP, while the DQN agent dynamically guides the selection of operators or the adjustment of weights. This integration transforms the search process from a static and random mechanism into an intelligent, experience-driven policy that achieves higher stability and solution quality in the long run. The proposed framework comprises three main components: (1) the DZOA core, which executes discrete operators such as Swap, Insertion, 2-opt, and 3-opt, and is responsible for tour refinement; (2) the DQN agent, acting as a centralized or shared decision-maker that receives the states of both agents and the population and selects the most appropriate action; and (3) the state–action–reward structure, which governs the interaction between these two layers. This interaction is reinforced through a solution acceptance policy that maintains a balance between exploration and exploitation during the search process.

Decision-making logic

The decision-making logic in the hybrid RZOA is founded on the dynamic interaction between the agent’s current state, the action selection through the DQN network, and the acceptance or rejection of the newly generated solution. In each iteration, the agent’s state vector is first constructed, consisting of the following features: the ratio of the agent’s tour quality to the best tour found so far, the agent’s weight as an indicator of its tendency toward exploration or exploitation, the population diversity index representing the dispersion of solutions, and the progress ratio of the current iteration to the total number of iterations. This state vector is fed into the DQN, which computes the Q-values corresponding to the possible actions. Using the ε-greedy policy, one action is then selected meaning that with probability ε, an action is chosen randomly to encourage exploration, and with probability 1–ε, the action with the highest Q-value is selected to promote exploitation. The selected action may correspond to one of the neighborhood operators Swap, Insertion, 2-opt, or 3-opt or to a weight adjustment operation (i.e., increasing or decreasing Wi). If a weight adjustment is selected, the value of Wi is dynamically updated to enhance the agent’s flexibility in adapting its exploration–exploitation inclination.

After executing the selected operator or weight modification, a new candidate solution is produced, which must be evaluated through the solution acceptance policy. In the simplest case, only improved solutions are accepted. However, to prevent premature convergence and entrapment in local optima, a Metropolis-based probabilistic acceptance rule is also employed, allowing slightly inferior solutions to be accepted with limited probability. Finally, a reward value proportional to the improvement in tour quality is assigned to the agent, and the complete experience tuple including the current state, selected action, obtained reward, and the next state is stored in the experience replay memory. This memory is periodically sampled to train the DQN using the Double DQN algorithm and mini-batch sampling, ensuring accurate and stable Q-value estimation. Through this process, the agent gradually learns which actions are most effective under specific conditions, developing an intelligent and adaptive policy that guides the search toward better solutions over time.

Comprehensive pseudocode

The hybrid RZOA is designed to enhance metaheuristic search efficiency and to intelligently adapt the operator selection process. In the initialization phase, a population of random tours representing zebra agents is generated and evaluated. Adaptive parameters such as migration weights and alignment coefficients are initialized, and both the learning Q-network and the target network are configured. During each iteration, the global population metrics including the best tour length, average diversity, and algorithmic progress ratio are computed. Each agent constructs a state vector containing local and global features and feeds it into the DQN to select one of the four discrete operators (Swap, Insertion, 2-opt, or 3-opt) according to the ε-greedy policy. The selected operator is then applied to the agent’s tour, and the reward is computed based on the improvement or degradation of the tour length. If the new solution improves the objective function, it is accepted directly; otherwise, acceptance is determined according to the Metropolis criterion, enabling the algorithm to escape local optima.

Throughout the execution, each agent’s experiences consisting of state, action, reward, and next state are stored in the experience replay buffer and periodically used to train the DQN. The network parameters are updated by minimizing the loss between the predicted and target Q-values, and the target network parameters are periodically synchronized with those of the main network. In each generation, the exploration rate (ε) is gradually decreased to maintain a proper balance between exploration and exploitation. Upon completion, the best tour obtained during the entire process is reported as the algorithm’s final output. This hybrid framework enables operator selection to be governed not by randomness but by adaptive learning from accumulated experience. Consequently, the algorithm progressively learns an optimal decision-making policy for improving tour quality and demonstrates faster convergence speed and superior solution accuracy compared to the classical DZOA. Time complexity analysis

The computational complexity of the proposed RZOA algorithm is analyzed based on its main components. Let n denote the number of cities, N the population size, and T the maximum number of iterations. The initialization phase requires O (N × n) operations to generate the initial population. During each iteration, the main computational cost arises from the application of discrete search operators such as Swap, Insertion, 2-opt, and 3-opt, as well as the evaluation of candidate solutions. Among these, the most computationally expensive operations are the 2-opt and 3-opt moves, which require O(n²) time in the worst case. Therefore, the dominant time complexity of the optimization process can be approximated as O (T × N × n²). In addition, the reinforcement learning component based on the Deep Q-Network introduces a relatively small overhead. Since the state vector is low-dimensional and only forward propagation is required during the decision-making process, the complexity of the DQN can be considered O (1) per agent per iteration. Consequently, the overall time complexity of the RZOA algorithm is O (T × N × n²), which is comparable to other metaheuristic algorithms that rely on local search operators.

Comparisons with DDCS, DKA, DSSA, D-GWO, DJAYA and DSMO

In this section, the RZOA algorithm is compared individually with each of the following recent optimization algorithms: DDCS41, DKA40, DSSA39, D-GWO12, DJAYA35, and DSMO46. These algorithms represent some of the most recent and advanced approaches in discrete optimization. A brief description of the operational principles of each algorithm is provided below. The DDCS algorithm is a population-based approach that uses Greedy Beam Search to adaptively generate the initial population. It employs multi-edge operators and a similarity attraction mechanism to guide the search process while maintaining population diversity through a random neighborhood replacement strategy.

The DKA algorithm is the discrete version of the Komodo Dragon Algorithm, which simulates the predatory and exploratory behavior of Komodo dragons. It utilizes a priority queue to select lower-weight edges and applies edge construction and destruction operators to efficiently explore the discrete search space. The DSSA algorithm is a discrete version of the Sparrow Search Algorithm specifically designed for the TSP. It integrates Gaussian mutation, the Swap operator, and a 2-opt local search to maintain a balanced trade-off between exploration and exploitation, thus enhancing solution quality. The D-GWO algorithm represents the discrete variant of the Grey Wolf Optimizer, adapted for combinatorial problems like the TSP. It incorporates the 2-opt method to improve local exploitation performance and has demonstrated significant superiority over classical metaheuristics based on statistical tests. The DJAYA algorithm is the discrete form of the JAYA algorithm, which exploits both the best and worst individuals in the population to improve diversity and convergence. It employs eight discrete transformation operators and a modified update rule, achieving competitive results in solving discrete problems such as the TSP.

Finally, the DSMO algorithm is the discrete version of the Spider Monkey Optimization algorithm, inspired by cooperative group behavior. It uses a Swap Sequence and Swap Operator to facilitate information exchange among monkeys, producing better-quality TSP solutions. Figure 11 presents a boxplot illustrating the distribution of tour lengths obtained by the algorithms RZOA, DDCS, DKA, and DSSA for several selected TSP benchmark problems. This figure highlights the fundamental differences among the algorithms in terms of statistical stability, convergence accuracy, tendency to deviate from the optimal value, and ability to avoid local optima. The vertical axis in this figure represents the tour length (objective function value), while the horizontal axis denotes the different algorithms under comparison. At first glance, the boxplot corresponding to RZOA is narrower and more compact than those of the other algorithms, indicating low variance and high consistency across multiple runs. This property is particularly evident in larger-scale problems such as ch-150 and kro-B200, where the distance between the first and third quartiles for RZOA is approximately half that of DDCS and one-third that of DKA. For example, in the kro-B200 problem, the tour lengths produced by RZOA predominantly fall within the range 29,500 to 29,900, whereas the variation range for DKA is around 1,000 units, and for DDCS about 600 units. This difference clearly indicates that RZOA consistently achieves results close to the global optimum, effectively avoiding the strong fluctuations typically observed in stochastic metaheuristics. Comparison results of RZOA, Ant System, CBA-NNM and GA-MARL.

The Ant System is a modern algorithm inspired by the foraging behavior of ant colonies. It consists of three main stages: path construction, path improvement, and the footprint mechanism. In the path construction phase, problem-specific information and collective behavior of ants are used to generate initial routes. During the path improvement phase, neighborhood operators are applied to the best routes in the population to enhance their quality. The footprint mechanism operates such that edges with stronger pheromone trails are more likely to be selected, allowing the algorithm to adaptively guide the search toward optimal paths. The CBA-NNM algorithm is a newly developed version of the hybrid artificial bee colony method. In this version, the nearest-neighbor method is used to generate the initial population, and in the local search phase, the traditional Swap operator is replaced with the Multi-Insert function to improve both diversity and search efficiency. The GA-MARL algorithm is a hybrid approach that combines the Genetic Algorithm with Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning. In this method, the initial population for the GA is generated using MARL to ensure high-quality initial solutions. Then, a novel crossover operator called Smart Multi-point Crossover is employed to enhance solution paths. Within this hybrid structure, MARL serves as the path constructor, while GA functions as the path optimizer.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate the statistical significance of the superior performance of the proposed RZOA algorithm compared to other benchmark methods, a series of parametric and non-parametric statistical tests were conducted to determine whether the observed performance differences among the algorithms were random or statistically meaningful.

In the first step, the non-parametric Friedman test was applied to compare multiple algorithms across a set of TSP benchmark problems. This test is particularly useful when the normality assumption of data cannot be guaranteed, and the goal is to compare multiple methods evaluated on the same datasets86. In this study, for each algorithm and each benchmark instance, the mean value obtained from 30 independent runs was used as input to the Friedman test. The results derived from Table 11, which compares RZOA with DDCS, DKA, and DSSA, indicate that the average rank of RZOA is lower than that of its competitors in most cases. Specifically, for standard benchmark instances such as eil-51, st-70, kro-A100, and kro-B100, RZOA consistently achieved a rank of 1 or 2, with only a marginal difference from DSSA. However, RZOA consistently outperformed both DKA and DDCS, showing approximately 40–70% lower PDA values in most datasets with 100 or 200 nodes. This advantage persisted for larger datasets such as kroB200 and tsp225, confirming the stability and efficiency of RZOA across different problem scales.

Based on the analysis involving four algorithms and 21 datasets, the Friedman test statistic was calculated as χ²F = 82.54, which, with three degrees of freedom, is statistically significant at the 95% confidence level. This result confirms that the differences among the algorithms are statistically significant and that the superior performance of RZOA is not due to random chance. In the second step, to conduct a more detailed pairwise comparison between RZOA and each algorithm, the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test was employed. However, the close ranking of RZOA and DSSA in some cases suggests that DSSA also demonstrates competitive performance for smaller-scale instances such as eil-76 and lin-105, although it lags behind RZOA in terms of accuracy and stability for larger problems.

In the next stage, a statistical comparison between RZOA and three other algorithms Ant System, CBA-NNM, and GA-MARL was performed based on the results presented in Table 13. This table provides a comprehensive overview of algorithmic performance across TSP datasets of varying scales. The results indicate that RZOA generally achieves higher accuracy than Ant System in most instances, including kro-A100, kro-B100, ch-150, pr-226, and lin-318, often ranking second or third overall. However, CBA-NNM and GA-MARL also exhibit notable performance in certain datasets. For instance, CBA-NNM achieved the known optimal solution in berlin-52 and kro-A100, while showing minimal statistical variation, highlighting its strong numerical stability. Similarly, GA-MARL produced the smallest mean errors in problems such as lin-105, pr-124, and ch-150, positioning it as the closest computational rival to RZOA in terms of solution accuracy. In larger-scale cases such as lin-318, RZOA exhibited slightly greater fluctuation compared to CBA-NNM and GA-MARL, but still maintained a reasonable range of stability and deviation, particularly visible in pr-299 and pr-1002, where its convergence remained steady. The Friedman test applied to this subset of four algorithms across 11 datasets yielded χ²F = 16.54 and p-value = 0.0015, confirming that the observed differences among algorithms are statistically significant. Additional Wilcoxon test analyses revealed that the performance difference between RZOA and Ant System is highly significant at the 95% confidence level (p < 0.01). In contrast, the difference between RZOA and GA-MARL was below the significance threshold for some smaller instances, indicating that the two algorithms exhibit comparable solution quality, particularly in medium-scale TSP problems.

In summary, these statistical evaluations validate that RZOA’s superior performance especially compared to classical and discrete optimization algorithms is statistically robust, while CBA-NNM and GA-MARL remain competitive alternatives in specific problem sizes due to their stable and learning-based adaptive structures.

Learning behavior analysis

To provide a deeper understanding of the role of the reinforcement learning component in the proposed RZOA algorithm, the learning behavior of DQN network during the optimization process is analyzed. The objective of this analysis is to evaluate how the agent adapts its decision-making policy and how this adaptation influences the overall performance of the algorithm. First, the evolution of the ε-greedy strategy is examined. At the beginning of the optimization process, the value of ε is relatively high, which encourages exploration and leads to a diverse selection of operators. As the iterations progress, the value of ε gradually decreases, allowing the algorithm to shift toward exploitation of the acquired knowledge. This gradual transition enables an effective balance between exploration and exploitation throughout the search process. Next, the operator selection behavior is analyzed. In the early iterations, all operators (Swap, Insertion, 2-opt, and 3-opt) are selected with approximately equal probability, indicating an exploration phase. As the learning process continues, the agent gradually shows a higher preference for certain operators, particularly 2-opt and 3-opt, which are more effective for local refinement in TSP. This behavior demonstrates that the DQN is capable of evaluating the effectiveness of operators and prioritizing those that contribute more significantly to solution improvement. Furthermore, the stability of the learning process is investigated by analyzing the evolution of Q-values. In the initial stages, Q-values exhibit noticeable fluctuations due to the high level of exploration. Over time, as the agent gains more experience, these values gradually stabilize and converge. This indicates that the agent successfully learns a more reliable and consistent decision-making policy.

Conclusions and future work

In this study, a novel hybrid algorithm named RZOA was introduced, which integrates the discrete mechanism of the Zebra Optimization Algorithm with deep reinforcement learning, resulting in an intelligent and self-adaptive framework for solving combinatorial optimization problems particularly the traveling salesman problem. The design of the state–action–reward structure was carefully formulated to enable agents to dynamically balance exploration and exploitation throughout the optimization process. Numerical experiments conducted on 42 standard datasets from the TSPLIB benchmark demonstrated that the proposed algorithm outperforms several advanced metaheuristic methods, including DDCS, DKA, and DSSA, in terms of solution quality, convergence speed, and stability of performance. In addition, quantitative evaluation results further highlight the effectiveness of the proposed approach. The RZOA algorithm achieves near-optimal performance across a wide range of benchmark instances, with an average Percentage Deviation of the Best solution (PDB) generally below 1% and an average Percentage Deviation of the Average solution (PDA) below 0.5% for small and medium-scale problems. For large-scale instances, the PDA remains within approximately 4–5%, demonstrating the scalability of the method. Moreover, comparative analyses indicate that RZOA outperforms competing algorithms in the majority of test cases, while maintaining lower standard deviation values, confirming its robustness and stability. Moreover, statistical analyses using the Friedman and Wilcoxon tests confirmed the superiority of RZOA at the 95% confidence level. Theoretically, the integration of the adaptive decision-making mechanism of DQN with the collective behavior of zebras allows RZOA to intelligently adjust its search dynamics during execution, effectively preventing premature convergence and avoiding local optima.

In future work, several research directions can be pursued based on RZOA. First, the development of multi-objective versions for addressing problems with conflicting objectives such as production scheduling or energy optimization can significantly broaden the algorithm’s applicability. Second, extending this framework to dynamic and uncertain domains, such as real-time vehicle routing or logistics network optimization, can further assess its adaptability under real-world conditions. Additionally, integrating more advanced deep learning architectures such as Attention Networks or Transfer Learning models into the DQN decision-making layer may enhance the agents’ learning efficiency and decision accuracy. Finally, implementing parallelized versions of RZOA on GPU architectures or cloud computing platforms could enable its effective deployment in large-scale, industrial-level optimization problems, where computational efficiency and scalability are essential.
