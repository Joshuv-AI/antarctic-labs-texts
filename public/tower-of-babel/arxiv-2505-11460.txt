Mechanistic inference of stochastic gene expression from structured single-cell data Note: Submitted invited review for the ‘Identifiability, estimation and uncertainty in mathematical modelling’ issue in Current Opinion in Systems Biology .
Christopher E. Miles

Abstract
Single-cell gene expression measurements encode variability spanning molecular noise, cellular heterogeneity, and technical artifacts. Mechanistic models provide a principled framework to disentangle these sources and extract insight, but inferring underlying dynamics from standard sequencing count data faces fundamental limitations. Structured datasets with temporal, spatial, or multimodal features offer constraints that help resolve these ambiguities, but demand more complex models and advanced inference strategies, including machine learning techniques with associated tradeoffs. This review highlights recent progress in the judicious integration of structured single-cell data, stochastic model development, and innovative inference strategies to extract gene-level insights. These approaches lay the foundation for mechanistic understanding of regulatory networks and multicellular systems.

Despite successes in quantifying transcriptional dynamics, reliably inferring kinetics and distinguishing between hypotheses using typical high-throughput single-cell data remains challenging. Standard ‘snapshot’ measurements of transcript counts from conventional scRNA-seq data often lack sufficient information to uniquely constrain parameters or discriminate between models. This review examines how overcoming these limitations through structured single-cell datasets, sophisticated stochastic models, and advanced inference strategies together enhances our ability to disentangle the dynamics.
We focus on inference for individual genes. Despite apparent simplicity, these models pose surprising inferential obstacles and provide valuable foundations for regulatory and multicellular scales.
We first illustrate fundamental challenges in mechanistic inference from snapshot data, then explore how richer experimental designs incorporating temporal dynamics (e.g., metabolic labeling), spatial organization, and multimodal measurements (e.g., nascent/mature RNA) provide critical constraints for interpretation. As datasets and models grow in complexity, we evaluate the evolving toolkit of inference methods, from classical approaches to machine learning techniques, with particular attention to reliability challenges introduced by approximations and surrogate models. Throughout, we emphasize that the integration of appropriate data structures, models, and carefully validated inference tools is essential for extracting reliable biological insights from stochastic gene expression data.

A natural entry point is the classical telegraph model. By examining its promises and limitations, we can illustrate broader challenges in mechanistic inference and motivate the need for richer data and more flexible models. This framework minimally captures the intrinsic noise of transcriptional bursting and has thus served as a cornerstone in quantitative single-cell data analyses. Furthermore, its simplicity provides a valuable basis for extensions that address practical experimental complexities, for example, accounting for measurement noise (mRNA capture efficiency) in sequencing improves inference quality

Leveraging Structured Data to Overcome Ambiguity

These challenges highlight that snapshot counts alone are often insufficient for reliable mechanistic insight.
Jiao et al. 2024
point toward a resolution: while steady-state distributions may be indistinguishable, transient dynamics often differ distinctly. Therefore, resolving these inherent ambiguities requires richer experimental data that provides additional constraints. Information embedded in temporal dynamics, spatial organization, or the relationships between multiple molecular species measured simultaneously holds the potential to distinguish between mechanisms that appear degenerate in static measurements. Understanding how to leverage these structured data types requires careful construction of appropriate mechanistic models and robust methods to connect them with data, thereby pinpointing what information can be extracted. Next, we will highlight recent case studies that exemplify successes in leveraging single-cell data structures for mechanistic insight.

3.1
Temporal Dynamics Reveal Absolute Rates and Pathway Complexity

Observing systems changing over time is the most intuitive way to resolve ambiguities inherent to static, snapshot-based inference, where often only ratios of kinetic rates can be inferred. Temporal data provides the necessary information to overcome this degeneracy and pinpoint absolute timescales. Such data in single-cell gene expression takes a variety of forms. The ideal dataset for any temporal inference is a genuine time series, where temporal correlations encode valuable information. While such systems exist (e.g., MS2/PP7 labeling
[
17
]
) and have been used with mechanistic modeling
[
18
]
, these measurements are challenging at scale. More commonly, temporal insights are gleaned from a series of destructive snapshots taken over time, where different cells are sampled, giving the marginal distributions of a population, shown in
fig.

1
. Even without connected time points, these temporal snapshots have seen recent success in distinguishing models and parameters, as discussed next.

Metabolic labeling techniques (e.g., using 4sU followed by sequencing protocols like scEU-seq or SLAM-seq
[
19
,
20
]
) are a powerful tool to generate temporal measurements. These methods allow the distinction between newly synthesized ("new" or "labeled") and pre-existing ("old" or "unlabeled") RNA within single cells, often measured across time courses or after pulse-chase experiments.
By tracking the dynamics of both labeled and unlabeled populations, one can decouple and directly estimate the absolute kinetic rates of RNA synthesis
\rho
and degradation
\gamma
, parameters that are inherently intertwined from static measurements.
Volteras et al. 2024
demonstrate the power of this approach by integrating time-resolved metabolic labeling data to resolve absolute rates. Beyond the inference of rate parameters, the authors ask: how much temporal heterogeneity can be extracted from these measurements? Remarkably, the authors can identify how the kinetic rates, e.g.,
\rho(t),\gamma(t)
, vary across the cell cycle. This genome-wide quantification of absolute, temporally-heterogeneous burst kinetics and mRNA degradation rates reveals complex regulatory patterns, such as distinct waves of burst frequency versus decay rate modulation across the cell cycle.

Beyond determining absolute rates, temporal data can also clarify the complexity of the underlying pathway, which we previously saw can be challenging with the telegraph model and snapshots (
fig.

2
, model mimicry).
Nicoll et al. 2025
provide an analytical calculation that shows that for a large class of transcription models, the initial increase in mean mRNA levels following induction (assuming negligible initial counts and a known delay) follows a general power law,
\langle m(t)\rangle\propto t^{n}
. The exponent
n
reflects the number of sequential, irreversible rate-limiting steps (promoter transitions, splicing, export) for mature transcript production. Measuring this transient scaling behavior, potentially in the minutes following induction, thus provides a method, robust to certain types of extrinsic noise, to estimate a lower bound on pathway complexity, distinguishing between models (e.g., with different numbers of inactive promoter states) that might yield identical steady-state distributions.

Surprisingly, even without any direct temporal measurements, modeling temporal variations is a valuable pursuit. For instance,
Sukys and Grima 2025
derive a latent cell cycle phase
\tau
, computationally inferred from scRNA-seq snapshots, as a biological pseudo-time axis. By fitting mechanistic models that explicitly incorporate this latent cell-cycle progression,
\rho(\tau)

Their findings reveal dosage compensation mechanisms (e.g., burst frequency halving post-replication while burst size remains constant) and highlight that neglecting such inherent temporal structure can significantly bias kinetic parameter estimates derived from naive snapshot analysis.
Popular RNA velocity methods also extract pseudotemporal information from snapshots by bridging the temporal component of this review with multimodal data (see
section

3.3
), particularly nascent and mature RNA. Recent variants employ fully mechanistic models to extract interpretable kinetics (e.g.,

These examples highlight how extracting temporal information is possible even without temporally connected time points. Understanding what information can (or cannot) be extracted from temporal snapshots (marginal distributions) remains an active area of fundamental theoretical research

3.2
Spatial Context Connects Location to Kinetics and Function

Beyond temporal observation, the spatial organization of molecules within cells and tissues is increasingly accessible through high-resolution imaging like smFISH. These spatial patterns offer another valuable data modality to dissect mechanisms obfuscated in other measurements.
Various computational toolkits and statistical frameworks (e.g.,

These methods offer functional insights, such as cell-type-specific pattern variations

Many foundational mechanistic models (e.g.,

3.3
Multimodal Readouts Decouple Linked Processes

Measuring only a single molecular species, such as mature mRNA, necessarily integrates the effects of all upstream processes. This aggregation often makes it impossible to distinguish, for example, whether low mRNA levels are due to slow transcription or rapid degradation, see
fig.

2
.
Simultaneously measuring multiple linked molecular species in the same cell provides intermediate readouts that can deconvolve these connected processes. By explicitly modeling the interactions between these species, one can infer kinetic parameters for these intermediate steps, thereby resolving ambiguities and gaining insight into otherwise unobservable aspects of the regulatory cascade.

A key example in transcriptomics is the joint measurement of nascent (e.g., unspliced/intronic) and mature (e.g., spliced/exonic) RNA, often achievable by postprocessing of standard scRNA-seq or snRNA-seq data
[
34
]
. The steady-state joint distribution,
P(n,m)
, where
n
and
m
are nascent and mature counts, contains information about the processing/splicing rate
\eta
that links the two species. This information is lost when only considering the marginal distributions
P(n)
or
P(m)

[
3
]
. Applying such mechanistic insights genome-wide requires scalable inference tools. Addressing this,
Carilli et al. 2024
developed biVI, which embeds a biophysical model of transcription and splicing within a variational autoencoder (VAE) framework. This hybrid VAE approach captures complex cell state heterogeneity in low dimensions while inferring interpretable, cell-state-dependent kinetic parameters (e.g., burst size, degradation rates) from joint nascent/mature RNA data.
This direction is further supported by other work, including models connecting fluctuating transcription rates to splicing dynamics
[
36
]
, theory of nascent RNA distributions
[
37
]
, and analyses of joint nascent/mature RNA distributions for multiple genes
[

. This synergy between multimodal data and tailored modeling strategies, integrating mechanisms with scalable inference, is proving valuable for dissecting transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation across diverse cellular contexts.

Inference in Practice

The previous section highlights how structured data and richer models can, in principle, provide deeper mechanistic insights. However, increased complexity introduces challenges in connecting structured data with models. For instance, how does one infer cell-cycle dependent forms
\rho(t)
rather than simple rates
\rho
? Or how should one handle unwieldy multivariate distributions from multimodal data or spatial patterns
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
with stochastic counts and positions relative to cell-specific geometric features? These newly introduced obstacles amplify the need for carefully chosen inference strategies that push the boundary of classical inference.

4.1
The Limits of Classical Inference for Complex Models

Classical statistical inference hinges on evaluating the likelihood function,
L(\theta)=P(\text{data}|\text{model},\theta)
. For molecular counts, often
\log L(\theta)=\sum_{m}N(m)\log P(m|\theta)
, where
N(m)
is the number of cells with RNA counts
m
. For simple models like the telegraph model,
P(m)
can be computed analytically. For more complex models, this approach becomes challenging but can still be fruitful, particularly through two distinct directions.
The first is to compute
P(m)
using generating functions,
G(z)=\sum_{m}z^{m}P(m)
, which can be derived with lengthy calculations for surprisingly complex models
[
3
]
and then inverted numerically to recover
P(m)
. Second, Finite State Projection (FSP) methods, which truncate the state space
m=0,1,\ldots,M
in a systematic way, provide approximate likelihoods for complex models
[
45
,
46
,
40
]
.
If full likelihood computation is infeasible but some analytical progress is possible, moment-based methods match model-derived statistical moments (e.g., time-dependent means
[
22
]
) to data estimates, circumventing likelihood calculation. These are often faster but may lose distributional information, and uncertainty quantification can be challenging, although recent work shows promise in deriving parameter bounds
[
41
]
. At the frontier of addressing the computational burden of computing these quantities for complex models, efficient approximation methods like Holimap
[
47
]
aim to map complex network dynamics onto simpler, solvable systems.

4.2
Expanding the Toolkit: Simulation-Based and ML-Enhanced Inference

For models incorporating temporal, spatial, or multispecies data, likelihood or moment calculations are often infeasible. Simulation-based inference (SBI) offers a practical alternative using forward simulations to infer parameters without explicit likelihoods. The most classical SBI approach, approximate Bayesian computation (ABC), compares simulations with data through the lens of user-chosen summary statistics.
Parameters with simulations “close” to the data are accepted to build an approximate posterior distribution. This compression into lower-dimensional statistics enables data-model comparison to be feasible, but the performance of ABC critically depends on the choice of context-specific summary statistics that are sufficiently informative.
For example, to infer absolute rates and cell-cycle dependence from complex metabolic labeling data,
Volteras et al. 2024
uses dynamic correlations between labeled/unlabeled RNA and moments (means, variances) of the populations to pin down dynamics and how they vary temporally.

While conceptually straightforward, ABC’s critical dependence on manually chosen summary statistics motivates the use of modern alternatives. Neural SBI approaches (e.g., neural posterior/likelihood estimation, NPE/NLE) employ deep learning to train neural networks on simulation outputs as surrogate approximations to the posterior distribution
P(\theta|\text{data})
or the likelihood function. In doing so, neural SBI methods implicitly learn informative features and show promise for superior computational efficiency to ABC
[
48
,
49
,
50
]
. However, enjoying these benefits is not entirely riskless.
The challenge shifts to decisions in network architecture, training procedures, and the crucial step of validating the surrogate. Neural SBI methods can yield overconfident posteriors when the underlying model is misspecified
[
51
]
, but diagnosing and remedying these issues remains challenging due to the opaqueness of the surrogate
[
50
]
.

There is growing momentum in combining deep learning’s representational power with the mechanistic interpretability of classical approaches. Hybrid methods at this intersection have emerged that leverage neural networks to accelerate simulations, create surrogates for model components, or embed mechanistic parameters within flexible representations. For instance, biVI
[
35
]
uses a variational autoencoder (VAE) with analytically-computed basis functions that describe an otherwise intractable joint distribution of nascent and mature mRNA counts.
Sukys and Grima 2025
demonstrates another clever hybrid by using deep learning to extract a latent cell-cycle time
\tau
for each observation, which is then used in a more traditional moment-approximated inference scheme to reveal how kinetic rates vary across the cell cycle, e.g.,
\rho(\tau)
. These two examples highlight the merit of hybrid methods, where careful construction of the interface between mechanistic and machine learning components can yield powerful interpretability, computational tractability, and statistical expressiveness.

4.3
Tradeoffs and Reliability

Beyond models of gene expression, deep-learning-based methods for general stochastic reaction models
[
43
,
52
]
and spatial stochastic models
[
50
]
show remarkable promise for scalability and flexibility. For instance, graph neural networks trained on simple geometries can extrapolate predictions of stochastic spatial reaction models to new complex geometries
[
44
]
. Despite their promise, these deep-learning methods still require caution and extensive validation to avoid "black box" issues that confound their output
[
53
]
. Because likelihood-free methods often rely on neural network surrogates to approximate the true posterior or likelihood, extra scrutiny is required. Validating the reliability of these approximations (e.g., via calibration) is crucial to ensure the resulting uncertainty estimates are not misleadingly over-confident, although such validation remains practically challenging (see e.g.,
[
50
]
). Moreover, using these modern methods often means forfeiting the valuable toolkit of likelihood-based inference. Key themes we saw earlier were issues of practical identifiability and model mimicry. Powerful approaches for evaluating these obstacles have been developed alongside classical inference. For instance, profile likelihood analysis can evaluate parameter unidentifiability
[
54
]
. Model selection often relies on likelihood-based information criteria or likelihood-based Bayesian non-parametric methods, the latter of which has been used to efficiently infer the number of promoter states from single-cell data
[
40
]
. The likelihood also lends itself to information-theoretic quantities (e.g., the Fisher information) that can direct optimal experimental design
[
39
,
55
]
. The toolkit surrounding inference for modern deep-learning methods is under active development but remains less refined. As a result, while modern inference methods themselves show great promise, their integration into broader scientific discovery remains an active frontier.

5
Conclusion and Outlook

Recent advances in mechanistic inference for stochastic gene expression signify a maturing field, moving beyond simply fitting models to critically evaluating what can be reliably learned from available data. The increasing availability of structured single-cell datasets, whether temporal, spatial, or multimodal, has created new opportunities to resolve regulatory mechanisms, while also sharpening the need for rigorous validation and principled model selection.

Frontier experimental techniques amplify this promise and complexity. CRISPR-based lineage tracing now enables live-cell tracking coupled with transcriptomic readouts
[
56
]
, and multimodal spatial imaging localizes spliced/unspliced transcripts, revealing spatially modulated post-transcriptional dynamics (e.g., near nuclear speckles)
[
57
]
. While our discussion of multimodal data was limited to similar molecular measurements (mature and nascent mRNA), genuinely multi-omic data is widely available. The discussed transcriptomics measurements alongside proteomics, epigenomic states like chromatin accessibility, or metabolomic profiles hold the most promise for mechanistic insight, but also face the most challenges in their connection. In sum, cutting-edge data bring us closer than ever to resolving regulatory processes at fine mechanistic granularity. However, the complex, high-dimensional, often intractable models needed for their interpretation raise fundamental questions about what features can be reliably inferred. This highlights the need for a more complete ecosystem surrounding inference, including experimental design, identifiability analysis, sensitivity diagnostics, and model comparison. While such tools are well-developed in classical inference, they remain limited for the simulation-based and deep-learning methods
[
58
,
50
]
that will likely be essential for analyzing these emerging datasets. Bridging this gap will require generalizable, modular tools that integrate diagnostics, design, and validation directly into the simulation-based inference workflow.

Ultimately, as data grows in richness and resolution, the bottleneck shifts from data acquisition to robust, interpretable analysis. The inferential challenges discussed here for individual genes become even more acute when scaling upward. For instance, the discussed models are building blocks for gene regulatory networks (GRNs)
[
59
,
60
]
, but it is becoming increasingly evident that transcriptomic counts are insufficient to reliably infer regulatory interactions
[
61
]
. This limitation highlights that principled approaches to single-gene inference are not merely analogous to, but form the essential groundwork for, analyzing multi-gene systems and multicellular dynamics. We argue that holistic integration of new data structures, rigorous model selection, and carefully validated inference methods will be essential for extracting mechanistic insights from rich spatial transcriptomic datasets at the developmental and tissue-level scales.
