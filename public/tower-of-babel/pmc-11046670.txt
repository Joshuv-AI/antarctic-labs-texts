Interventions to promote medication adherence for chronic diseases in India: a systematic review
Authors: Abraham Tolley, Refaat Hassan, Kirpal Grewal, Ruige Kong, Rohan Sanghera, Baani Sodhi, Saurav Basu
Published: 2024

Introduction

In India, chronic diseases account for 53% of deaths and 44% of disability-adjusted-life-years lost.1 Management of chronic diseases requires long-term medication usage, but a high burden of medication non-adherence represents a major public health challenge in India.2, 3 Low adherence is associated with poor patient outcomes that further accentuates healthcare costs.4 Implementing cost-effective interventions targeted at improving adherence are urgently warranted to improve outcomes and reduce complications arising from the growing burden of chronic diseases. However, the majority of evidence for medication adherence improving strategies is based on more economically developed countries and existing reviews in low- and middle-income countries tend to focus on specific diseases across countries.

We conducted, to our knowledge, the first systematic review evaluating interventions aimed at improving medication adherence for chronic diseases in India.

Methods

The study was conducted according to the PRISMA 2020 framework. Included studies were those that were randomised control trials (RCTs), involving subjects with non-communicable chronic diseases located in India, looking at any intervention with the aim of improving medication adherence and which measured adherence as a primary or secondary outcome. Methodological quality was assessed using the Cochrane RoB2.

Results

A PRISMA flow diagram is shown below (Fig 1). 22 RCTs were included in this review. Educational interventions provided by pharmacists (seven studies) or community health workers (four studies) showed strong evidence for improving adherence across a range of chronic diseases. Reminder-based systems were moderately effective when implemented alone (two studies) and synergised well with education-based interventions to improve adherence (four studies). Other individual studies sought to improve training for community health workers, utilise a multidisciplinary team approach, employ psychological based interventions or use polypill-based approaches. For five studies, we found that there were significant improvements in measures of adherence without corresponding improvements in clinical parameters that represented disease control. Five studies were deemed low risk of bias, six were high risk and 11 were assessed as some concerns.

Discussion

There is moderate evidence for adherence improving strategies for patients with chronic diseases in India, although further large-scale RCTs over long timeframes are required to evaluate real-world effectiveness and costs.

Patient education was found to be the most consistent intervention for improving medication adherence, reflecting reasons for non-adherence often involving patients' beliefs and poor health literacy.2, 3, 4, 5 Reminder-based systems were most useful when combined with patient education, perhaps reflecting the need to first address underlying barriers to adherence attributable to suboptimal patient provider communication. Other interventions, such as psychology-based approaches, are less cost-effective and scalable, and their usefulness may be limited to those with poor adherence refractory to other interventions. Mixed methodological quality, short study timeframes and heterogenous use of outcome measures were the primary limitations to this review.

Conclusion

Patient education strategies and reminder-based systems are effective strategies for improving medication adherence and clinical outcomes in patients with chronic diseases in India, suggesting the need for enhanced strategic focus on these areas and their incorporation in local health policy.
