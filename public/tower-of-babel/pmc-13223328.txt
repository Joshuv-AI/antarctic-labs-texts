Predicting hourly traffic volume of urban signal intersections using Dynamically Weighted LightGBM
Authors: Wang Bozhi, Steve SHYH-Ching Chen, Zhou Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPredicting traffic volume at urban intersections is critical to ensuring the stability and efficiency of the urban road network. To better capture spatio-temporal nonlinear correlation between traffic volume and contributing factors and improve the prediction performance, this study introduces a Dynamically Weighted LightGBM (Gradient Boosting Machine) framework (DW-LGBM) for forecasting hourly traffic volume at intersections. The proposed DW-LGBM model features three core improvements: a dynamic weight allocation component that captures nonlinear spatio-temporal dependency, a multi-dimensional feature engineering component that incorporates cyclical temporal trends, and a dual-stage noise suppression mechanism using Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (EWMA) and Kalman filtering to smooth the data. The proposed model is trained and tested with hourly traffic volume data collected from 209 urban intersections during 31 days in Chengdu, China. The results show that the predictions achieve superior performance metrics which surpass those of the baseline models (e.g., LSTM and XGBoost). The proposed architecture shows exceptional spatio-temporal adaptability for different urban intersections. However, it is found that all the models perform woeful in predicting traffic volume during peak hours due to the significant heterogeneity among intersections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47969-7.Keywords: Intersection, Hourly traffic volume forecast, Dynamic weighting LightGBMSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Road network in urban areas, especially those in metropolises, commonly suffers from heavy traffic congestion due to increasing demand of trips and inefficient management of traffic volume1. To better cope with this challenge of traffic congestion, traffic authorities frequently predict traffic volume to govern urban road network and propose the optimization strategies2. In urban road network, intersections serve as critical bottlenecks where traffic dynamics diverge fundamentally from road segments3. Monitoring and forecasting traffic volume at intersections would be vital to deploy target countermeasures for the real-time congestion, as congested intersections may lead to negative spillover effects to the connected roads and their neighbors4. Hence, it would be critical to precisely and dynamically predict traffic volume at urban intersections.

In past studies, prediction approaches for intersection traffic volume can generally cluster into three paradigms: statistical models, artificial intelligence (AI) approaches, and hybrid architectures5. Statistical models like ARIMA and Kalman filters are commonly used to forecast dynamic traffic data (time-series data). For instance, Ahmed et al.6 applied ARIMA to predict highway traffic. Xiong et al.7 employed a Kalman filter-grey correlation synthesis approach to predict subway transfer flow during peak hours in Beijing. Ghosh et al.8 validated Bayesian structural time-series model to forecast the severe congestion periods through component-wise temporal decomposition in Berlin. However, statistical methods usually suffer from low precision and inefficiency for data exploitation.

Recently, AI-driven models become increasingly popular for traffic prediction, which typically can be categorized as traditional machine learning and deep learning models. For instance, Xu et al.9 developed a support vector regression (SVR) with ant colony optimization to predict traffic volume, which outperforms traditional Kalman filters in short-term prediction. Zhong et al.10 developed an LSTM-based model to forecast traffic volume of signalized intersections in Hefei, China. To increase the exploitation of temporal information, Mou et al.11 introduced a time-enhanced LSTM (T-LSTM) network that captures flow-temporal correlations, while Yang et al.12 developed a temporal lag LSTM network to quantify the temporal trends for better predicting traffic volume. Moreover, recent ransformer-based methods have demonstrated superior capability in automating spatiotemporal feature learning, overcoming manual engineering limitations for complex traffic dynamics.

While the aforementioned AI-driven approaches have achieved remarkable breakthroughs in terms of prediction accuracy, two persistent challenges continue to persist. First, traditional machine learning architectures are often constrained by manual feature engineering that may limit their capacity to capture nonlinear spatiotemporal correlation among traffic volumes. Second, deep learning methods, while offer more accurate solutions, typically rely on feature engineering and have low efficiency in computation, which make dynamic predictions difficult. Additionally, advanced techniques such as multi-agent deep reinforcement learning for dynamic resource scheduling13 and signal-action-integrated filters for congestion prediction14 highlight progress in feature engineering and weighting mechanisms, yet challenges in computational efficiency persist. To balance the prediction accuracy and efficiency, some researches attempt to develop hybrid models that exploit multi-algorithm (both machine and deep learning) to transcend the single-model constraint. For instance, Vlahogianni15 combined genetic algorithm with neural architecture optimization to reconstruct data-driven model for better capturing spatiotemporal correlation in predicting traffic volume. Tan16 developed a SVR hyperparameter optimization with genetic algorithm to better capture critical information among features while keeping the computation efficiency with machine learning. The improvement of the hybrid model increases the feasibility in balancing computational efficiency and prediction accuracy, which seems to become a more promising solution for predicting urban traffic volume.

Despite the gains of the hybrid model, two critical challenges persist. First, many hybrid models rely heavily on manual feature engineering so they may not be able to capture nonlinear spatiotemporal couplings of traffic data17,18. Second, current studies focus on modeling with structured historical data while inadequately integrating unstructured inputs such as traffic controls and road characteristics19. Ignoring these useful unstructured features may impair the accuracy of prediction and model generalizability. These limitations underscore the need for frameworks that automate feature learning and integrate diverse inputs.

To address these research challenges, this paper proposes the Dynamic Weighted LightGBM (DW-LGBM) framework, which introduces a novel dynamic temporal weighting mechanism to enhance LightGBM’s capability in traffic volume prediction. Unlike standard LightGBM that treats all time steps equally20, DW-LGBM employs adaptive weights to amplify critical hourly patterns (e.g., peak vs. off-peak periods), thereby better capturing nonlinear spatiotemporal couplings without manual intervention. This framework fundamentally differs from existing methods in three key aspects: (i) Compared to standard LightGBM18, DW-LGBM integrates temporal dynamics explicitly through weighting, reducing reliance on feature engineering; (ii) Compared to LSTM-based models10,21, it maintains high computational efficiency while handling long-range dependencies because of the gradient boosting architecture; and (iii) Compared to hybrid models16,22, DW-LGBM automates feature extraction for unstructured inputs (e.g., traffic controls and road characteristics), enhancing the model’s generalizability24,25. The contributions of this study are threefold:1. we propose DW-LGBM, the first LightGBM-based model incorporating dynamic temporal weighting to optimize hourly traffic volume prediction, validated against real-world urban datasets. 2. extensive benchmarks demonstrate the DW-LGBM’s superiority over current methods, including standard LightGBM20, LSTM21, and hybrid architectures16,22, with significant improvements in accuracy (e.g., 15–20% lower RMSE). 3. we empirically justify that temporal weighting and automated handling of unstructured features can enhance machine learning models, which offers a balanced solution for real-time applications requiring both efficiency and precision23.

Data description

Data collection

The hourly traffic volume data were collected from 209 signalized intersections in in Chengdu, China, during March 2024. Figure 1 shows the locations of the selected intersections. Geospatial attributes of these intersections are extracted from Baidu online map using its streetviews. The map of study area (Fig. 1) is generated by Google Map and Adobe Illustrator. The study timeframe includes no special major festivals or natural disasters that could disrupt traffic patterns, so there is no obvious interference on traffic volume due to these special activities. The intersections are selected from Chengdu’s primary urban corridors along the First, Second, and Third Ring Roads. These critical nodes encompass key commercial and residential areas in Chengdu, surrounded by public facilities such as subway stations, medical facilities, and parking infrastructure. Hence, the selected intersections are representative for analyzing the traffic volume of the city. Eventually, the dataset of collected traffic volume contains 135,409 validated observational records of hourly traffic volume at intersections.

Data preprocessing

A multi-step preprocessing procedure was developed to reduce noise in the traffic volume data, including data cleansing, feature encoding, temporal smoothing, and dataset partitioning. These steps are described as follows:

Data cleansing: to reduce the outliers, this study initiates a interquartile range-based (IQR) outlier detection26. This approach uses a statistical dispersion measure by computing the first and third quartiles (Q1, Q3) to define a range [Q1–1.5 × IQR, Q3 + 1.5 × IQR], which systematically eliminates anomalies in hourly traffic volume falling outside this interval.

Feature encoding: temporal features (e.g., month, day, hour, and weekday) encoded with discrete timestamps are transformed into a continuous cyclical representation through sinusoidal encoding of temporal dimensions. Moreover, this study also implements a stratified temporal segmentation framework that categorizes hourly intervals into seven distinct periods, including pre-dawn, morning peak, mid-morning plateau, midday transition, evening peak, nightfall, and late night. Weekly patterns were also discretized into weekdays and holidays.

Research methodology

In this study, we propose an integrated framework for predicting traffic volume at signalized intersections. The framework combines adaptive weight adjustment, spatio-temporal interaction modeling, and multi-scale feature engineering to enhance prediction performance. The method is based on LightGBM, and Fig. 2 illustrates the overall flow: Fig. 2(A) presents the proposed framework, Fig. 2(B) details the LightGBM development, and Fig. 2(C) depicts the dynamic weighting mechanism.

A Overall flow; B LightGBM core algorithm; C Dynamic weighting mechanism.

In Fig. 2(A) the overall flow of the method exhibits a three-tiered processing hierarchy: the input layer initially transforms raw spatio-temporal features through Fourier-based spectral encoding, followed by a bifurcation into parallel temporal and spatial processing streams. The temporal pathway sequentially applies dynamic feature weighting via parameterized softmax activation a_t=softmax (W_t), then engages in soft cross-modal interaction. Concurrently, the spatial branch computes topology-aware weights β_c=softmax (W_c) before fusing with temporal features through a γ-mediated coupling term. The output layer synthesizes these processed features through a hybrid LightGBM-temporal residual architecture to generate final predictions.

In Fig. 2(B), this study selects LightGBM as the fundamental regressor, which comprises two interconnected components: the primary gradient boosting decision tree module (left) processes fused spatio-temporal inputs, and the auxiliary temporal residual compensator (right) implements error correction through recursive feedback. A bidirectional data exchange is also used to establish a closed-loop optimization.

In Fig. 2(C) the dynamic feature weights are applied for both temporal and spatial features by a dual-path weighting mechanism: the left channel derives temporal weights through the softmax normalization, while the right channel generates spatial weights β_c​, with cross-domain integration achieved via γ-interaction. A fail-safe fusion module monitors weight stability at the base layer, initiating backup weight substitution upon the anomaly detection.

LightGBM model

LightGBM is an efficient gradient boosting framework developed by Microsoft, designed for large-scale data. This algorithm can iteratively train multiple weak learners (e.g., tree models), weight the combination, and eventually output the final prediction. LightGBM’s histogram-based discretization coupled with its leaf-wise growth paradigm substantially enhances feature partitioning efficacy, enabling superior computational efficiency and predictive robustness in traffic flow forecasting application27. These characteristics ensure that the LightGBM has fast speed, low memory usage, and high accuracy for prediction tasks. Results and discussions

Feature correlations

Figure 3 shows the feature correlation heatmap quantifying the pairwise dependencies among critical traffic flow variables used for predicting traffic volume, including the features of “traffic volume,” “hour,” “weekday,” “in_lanes,” and “out_lanes.” The results show that most pairs of the features do not have significant correlations, as the absolute correlation coefficients are less than 0.01. However, two pairs of features, including “volume”×”hour” (correlation coef. of 0.30) and “volume”×”weekday” (correlation coef. of 0.27) show moderate the positive correlation. The correlations indicate that the evening or night may be associated with larger traffic volume than morning and afternoon. Also, Friday and weekends tend to increase the traffic volume compared with the first few days in a week. Moreover, the pair of “in_lanes”×”out_lanes” show a significantly large positive correlation (correlation coef. of 0.93). However, these two features have weak correlations with volume, indicating that their impacts may be minor.

Additionally, Fig. 5 illustrates the distribution of hourly prediction biases with MSE (blue line) and RMSE (red line). The prediction biases indicate that during morning (07:00–09:00) and evening (17:00–19:00) peak hours the DW-LGBM show larger RMSE and MSE. The predictions in evening peak hours show greater biases than those in morning peak hours, implying that the urban intersections in evening peak hours are more heterogenous due to the unobserved reasons. Conversely, the RMSE and MSE of the predicted values in non-peak hours, such as the intervals (23:00–06:00) and (10:00–16:00), are generally below 5 and 25, respectively. This indicates that predictions in hours with low and moderate traffic volume at the intersections are generally more accurate and stable. The probable reason is that this study falls to include some critical traffic information of the intersections that may pose significant impacts on large traffic congestions, e.g., the signal controls of the intersection and the traffic spillover from the road segments outside the study area.

Hourly error trends.

The issue of poor accuracy in peak-hour forecasting mainly stems from the failure of capturing critical influencing factors during the peak-hours, which cause significant heterogenous scenarios of traffic volume. First, huge traffic volume is more sensitive to sudden traffic events. Unfortunately, we found that some intersections were observed with these events (e.g., accidents, temporal traffic control, etc.). This will cause large deviance in predictions. However, the details of these sudden events are not recorded in our data. The existing models only select a one-month period and available features such as road designs/facilities, and historical data, which limits their generalization ability for sudden events.

Conclusions

This study proposes a DW-LGBM framework for predicting hourly traffic volume at intersections. The model allocates dynamic weights that capture nonlinear spatio-temporal dependencies among the historical traffic features. Additionally, the model processes the input data using multi-dimensional feature engineering to depict the cyclical temporal trends and smooth the data with EWMA and Kalman filtering to smooth the data. The prosed DW-LGBM is trained and tested with data collected from signalized intersections in Chengdu China. The study compares the prediction performance of the DW-LGBM and benchmark models. Three key findings are summarized below:

Both traffic volume and traffic features exhibit temporal associations, indicating that capturing temporal effects is necessary for accurate predictions.

Both traffic volume and traffic features exhibit temporal associations, indicating that capturing temporal effects is necessary for accurate predictions.

The proposed DW-LGBM achieves the best performance metrics, significantly outperforming benchmark models including both boosting algorithms and sequential deep learning models.

The proposed DW-LGBM achieves the best performance metrics, significantly outperforming benchmark models including both boosting algorithms and sequential deep learning models.

All models, including both benchmark models and DW-LGBM, show reduced capability in accurately predicting high hourly traffic volumes, with performance decreasing notably during peak hours.

All models, including both benchmark models and DW-LGBM, show reduced capability in accurately predicting high hourly traffic volumes, with performance decreasing notably during peak hours.

Therefore, future enhancements could be achieved by incorporating expanded roadway network data from a larger area to capture more complete spatio-temporal associations among traffic data, thereby explaining unobserved heterogeneity caused by citywide congestion and signal controls. Further validation across different urban environments would improve model generalizability, while integration of multimodal data streams promises enhanced predictive granularity.
