Phase-field modeling of cyclic behavior in quasi-brittle materials: a micromechanics-based approach
Mina Sarem, Nuhamin Eshetu Deresse, Els Verstrynge, Stijn François

Abstract
In this paper, we extend the micromechanics-based phase-field modeling of fatigue fracture to capture cyclic plasticity with ratcheting. This mechanism is particularly important for low-cycle fatigue, where the accumulation of inelastic strains plays an important role in the progression to final failure. The ratcheting contribution is formulated through the evolution of ratcheting strain, which accumulates over loading cycles and captures the inelastic strain growth characteristic of cyclic plasticity in a thermodynamically consistent manner. The extended plastic potential allows independent control over deviatoric and volumetric ratcheting components. Numerical simulations are performed to evaluate the model under both monotonic and cyclic loading and to assess the influence of ratcheting on material response.

Introduction

Failure occurs when a structure can no longer safely sustain applied loads or perform its intended function. Among the various mechanisms that lead to such failure, cyclic loading, repeated or fluctuating loads that cause gradual material degradation, is one of the most critical and widespread
Schijve 2009
. The degradation process, generally known as fatigue, progressively weakens the material and can lead to failure at stress levels well below its monotonic load-bearing capacity
Christensen 2013
. In quasi-brittle or pressure-sensitive materials, cyclic loading may also induce ratcheting, a progressive accumulation of plastic strain under asymmetric cycles, which further contributes to long-term deterioration. Cyclic failure often begins with microscopic damage forming at stress concentrations, grows incrementally with each cycle, and eventually results in cracking, stiffness loss, or even catastrophic collapse, sometimes without any prior warning. Understanding the mechanisms behind cyclic failure is therefore essential for designing structures that remain safe and functional throughout their service life.

Cyclic degradation has traditionally been a focus for ductile materials such as steel and aluminum, which can undergo significant plastic deformation under repeated loads. However, it is equally essential to understand this behavior in quasi-brittle materials such as concrete, masonry and rock. These materials fail predominantly in a brittle manner but exhibit limited inelastic deformation, microcracking, or damage softening before complete fracture
Bažant et al. 1998
.

Experiments are a crucial first step in studying material behavior, and decades of research have produced increasingly advanced testing techniques
Silva et al. 2023
. However, experimental studies, especially under cyclic loading, are often time-consuming and costly. While experiments help refine constitutive models by providing insights into essential parameters, modeling in turn highlights the need for specific experimental investigations to ensure a more comprehensive and accurate description of cyclic fracture. Constitutive modeling thus offers a promising way to complement and reduce the need for extensive testing, though models are inherently approximations of real behavior. Therefore, finding a balance between accuracy and efficiency remains a critical challenge, raising a key question how we can develop models that accurately capture material behavior while minimizing dependence on time-consuming experiments.

In this context, the present paper investigates cyclic behavior in quasi-brittle materials, with a focus on how microstructural features and boundary conditions influence crack initiation, propagation, and ultimate failure under repeated loads. By developing models that accurately represent these mechanisms, the aim is to improve the design, safety and durability of materials and structures subjected to cyclic loading.

In constitutive modeling within classical continuum mechanics, material behavior is typically described using macroscale mathematical models that capture key phenomena, such as damage and plasticity, using internal variables to keep track of the material state
Simo and Hughes 1998
. While macroscale models are widely used in engineering for their adaptability, they often rely on phenomenological parameters that require calibration, offering limited insight into the underlying mechanisms driving material behavior. As an alternative, micromechanical models provide physics-based insights that are crucial for accurately representing the microstructure of quasi-brittle materials, such as microcracks
You and Zhao 2024
.

The presence of microcracks in the microstructure of quasi-brittle materials results in distinct failure modes: brittle failure generally occurs under tensile loading at low confining pressures, whereas shear failure, primarily governed by frictional sliding of closed microcracks, tends to occur under compressive loading or under tensile loading at higher confining pressures
Choo and Sun 2018
. This highlights the importance of incorporating such microscale mechanisms into numerical models for quasi-brittle materials. While conventional phase-field models are effective at capturing overall failure patterns, they remain phenomenological and do not explicitly account for these underlying mechanisms.

To address this limitation, micromechanics-based phase-field modeling has emerged as a more physically motivated alternative
You et al. 2021
,
Ulloa et al. 2022
,
Wang et al. 2023
. While implemented at the macroscale, this approach is inspired by physical processes at the microscale, particularly the behavior of microcracks that open under tension and slide under shear
Liu et al. 2021
,
Zhao et al. 2018
. In this framework, microcrack opening is associated with damage (mode I fracture), while sliding corresponds to plasticity (mode II fracture)
Zhu et al. 2008a
. This distinction allows the model to naturally capture tensile and shear failure modes without relying on heuristic energy splits.

As illustrated in figure
1
, a zoomed-in view of a point in the solid shows the representative volume element (RVE), which captures the material’s behavior at a smaller scale. Within the RVE, the model assumes that penny-shaped microcracks are randomly distributed throughout the solid matrix. At any given time, all microcracks are considered either open or closed. This assumption simplifies the formulation while still capturing the key physical mechanisms
Zhu et al. 2008b
,
Ulloa et al. 2022
.

Figure 1
:
Schematic representation of the continuum solid (left), the RVE consisting of a matrix-inclusion system with microcracks (middle), and the orientation of a penny-shaped microcrack (right) (adapted from Ulloa et al.
Ulloa et al. 2022
).

The effect of these microcracks at the microscale is then linked to the macroscale response using homogenization techniques. One important tool in this process is Eshelby’s solution, which describes how an ellipsoidal inclusion (such as a microcrack) influences the stress and strain fields in an elastic material and provides the local stress and strain fields around these defects. Homogenization methods, such as the Mori–Tanaka scheme
Mori and Tanaka 1973
, then average these local fields to determine the macroscopic behavior of the material
Zhu et al. 2008c
.

Although referred to as micromechanics-based, this phase-field model remains a continuum model formulated at the macroscale. What sets it apart is that its constitutive equations, such as the free energy and dissipation potential, are inspired by micromechanical insights, rather than being purely phenomenological. It retains a variational structure and thermodynamic consistency, but the internal mechanisms it represents are rooted in the behavior of microcracks
Ulloa et al. 2022
.

One of the key strengths of this framework is its ability to capture non-associative behavior, which is characteristic of quasi-brittle materials. These materials can undergo various failure modes depending on the stress state, posing challenges in capturing these behaviors within continuum mechanics frameworks. A critical phenomenon in their physical behavior is dilatancy, the volumetric expansion that accompanies shear deformation. Dilatancy enhances shear resistance through particle interlocking and may trigger a transition from brittle to more ductile failure, often accompanied by shear band formation
Kruyt and Rothenburg 2016
,
Qu et al. 2021
. To account for this effect, the micromechanics-based phase-field model incorporates a non-associative Drucker–Prager formulation for friction.

The cyclic behavior of quasi-brittle materials has been addressed through two key components–fatigue and ratcheting–considering that both damage and plasticity exhibit different characteristics under cyclic loading. Fatigue refers to the progressive deterioration of a material under cyclic loading due to damage. Fatigue can occur due to repeated loading and unloading cycles, leading to microcrack initiation and propagation, ultimately resulting in failure
Kalina 2024
. The understanding of fatigue behavior is challenging because of the complex interplay between microstructure, fatigue damage mechanisms, and loading conditions. Predicting fatigue behavior in quasi-brittle materials has been challenging and remains a topic of interest. Many studies have investigated fatigue phenomena, employing different experimental techniques such as acoustic emission and digital image correlation
Deresse et al. 2022
,
Deresse et al. 2023
,
Deresse et al. 2024
. Moreover, various numerical methodologies and phenomenological formulations have been utilized to simulate this behavior. Among these methods, the phase-field approach to fracture is of particular interest due to the advantages discussed in the previous sections.

Different approaches incorporating fatigue into the phase-field model have been suggested in recent years. These phenomenological models aim to simulate fatigue behavior by introducing history-dependent functions that gradually diminish the material’s stiffness, ultimately leading to failure. Different models can be categorized based on their definition of the fatigue variable or how this variable is incorporated into the model to represent the material’s loading history
Kalina et al. 2023
. Some methods include increasing the crack driving force by an additional energy term
Schreiber et al. 2020
,
Haveroth et al. 2020
,
Loew et al. 2020
, or reducing the fracture energy to simulate the diminishing resistance of the material against crack propagation, achieved through the implementation of a fatigue degradation function
Alessi et al. 2018
,
Carrara et al. 2020
,
Seiler et al. 2020
,
Kristensen and Martínez-Pañeda 2020
,
Grossman-Ponemon et al. 2022
. In the first type of models, the additive fatigue contribution is an energetic quantity, whereas in the second type of models, the fatigue degradation function is scalar and without unit.

In the second type of fatigue models, the degradation function depends on a fatigue history variable, which evolves over time and is typically expressed in rate form due to its process-dependent nature. The history variable can be configured to increase only during unloading to avoid impacting the monotonic loading case, as demonstrated in
Seleš et al. 2021
, or only during loading, as shown in
Carrara et al. 2020
. This variable accumulates over loading cycles, and once a specific threshold is surpassed, fatigue degradation begins to reduce the fracture energy. This model is originally developed for brittle fracture and has been further extended to ductile fracture
Ulloa et al. 2021a
,
Khalil et al. 2022
.

Ratcheting refers to the progressive accumulation of plastic strain in materials subjected to cyclic loading
Ulloa et al. 2021b
. This behavior typically occurs when loading and unloading cycles are asymmetric (i.e., have a non-zero mean stress). With each cycle, a small amount of plastic deformation accumulates, which can lead to significant long-term damage
Paul et al. 2010
. Modeling ratcheting and stress relaxation requires non-associative plasticity models that include an internal variable to capture the accumulated inelastic strain, often referred to as ratcheting strain
Houlsby et al. 2017
. While such models have been extensively developed for metals
Chaboche 2008
, similar behaviors have been observed in quasi-brittle materials, which exhibit both brittle fracture and ductile-like deformation depending on the stress state and loading path. Under cyclic loading, frictional sliding along closed microcracks can contribute to a ratcheting-like response. Unlike classical hardening, which primarily modifies the yield surface to represent accumulated plastic strain, this mechanism is associated with irreversible microstructural rearrangements that progressively influence the direction of plastic flow. Accordingly, the ratcheting strain tensor is introduced as an internal variable that evolves through a non-associative flow rule, as proposed by Houlsby et al.
Houlsby et al. 2017
. When subjected to force-controlled loading, this results in incremental mean strain accumulation, whereas under displacement-controlled loading, it manifests as stress relaxation.

The present study extends the micromechanics-based phase-field framework for fatigue fracture to also capture ratcheting-driven cyclic plasticity. Ratcheting is modeled through an evolving ratcheting strain, which accumulates over loading cycles and reproduces the characteristic growth of inelastic strains in a thermodynamically consistent manner. The extended plastic potential allows separate control of deviatoric and volumetric ratcheting components, ensuring smooth evolution of the free energy and its derivatives. The capabilities of the model are demonstrated through numerical simulations under monotonic, low-, and high-cycle loading, highlighting its comprehensive applicability to cyclic behavior in quasi-brittle materials.

2
Cyclic plasticity via ratcheting

In quasi-brittle materials, inelastic deformation under cyclic loading does not necessarily follow the framework of classical plasticity, which is based on yield-surface evolution and dislocation motion as in metals. Instead, it can originate from microstructural changes such as local densification and pore collapse under compression
Liu et al. 2022
. These mechanisms become increasingly active during cyclic loading, where the material gradually compacts over repeated cycles. These observations suggest that ratcheting in quasi-brittle materials is primarily driven by internal structural changes, such as compaction and microcrack evolution, rather than by the dislocation-based mechanisms typically observed in metals. Consequently, incorporating a formulation that captures cyclic plasticity with ratcheting effects is essential for reproducing the cyclic behavior of such materials, especially under compressive or non-proportional loading paths.

Ratcheting is characterized by the progressive accumulation of inelastic strain with each loading cycle
Alonso-Marroquin and Herrmann 2004
, and its manifestation depends on both the applied loading path and the material’s constitutive behavior. To model this behavior, the formulation proposed by Houlsby et al.
Houlsby et al. 2017
is adopted, originally developed to describe the ratcheting behavior of cyclically loaded pile foundations
Abadie 2015
. The model is built on the classical framework of Armstrong and Frederick
Armstrong and Frederick 1966
.

To derive the governing equations, Houlsby et al.
Houlsby et al. 2017
relied on the theory of hyperplasticity
Houlsby and Puzrin 2007
, which is closely related to the broader class of generalized standard materials and ensures thermodynamic consistency. Figure
2
depicts the 1D rheological representation of an elastoplastic-ratcheting model, where ratcheting is in series with the plasticity and adds to the irreversible strain. This schematic representation shows that whenever plastic strain occurs, an additional ratcheting strain
\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}}
occurs which is a small fraction
\beta
of the plastic strain. However, irrespective of the direction of the plastic strain, the ratcheting strain always occurs in the direction of the applied stress.

Figure 2
:
1D rheological representation of elastoplastic-ratcheting model

Following this definition, the ratcheting evolution is obtained as

\dot{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}}=\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)\,\beta\,\|\dot{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}\|,

(1)

where
\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}}
denotes the ratcheting strain, and the ratcheting parameter
\beta\in[0\,,\,1]
is a material constant that determines the fraction of plastic strains that contributes to ratcheting.

3
Incorporation of ratcheting-driven cyclic plasticity

A micromechanics-based phase-field model is proposed to capture both ratcheting-driven cyclic plasticity and fatigue. The formulation builds on the energetic framework of the micromechanics-based phase-field model introduced in
You et al. 2021
,
Ulloa et al. 2022
and subsequently extended to fatigue in our earlier work
Sarem et al. 2025
. In this framework, the phase-field method characterizes damage dissipation at the macro scale, while Drucker-Prager plasticity governs the evolution of plastic strain.

3.1
Free energy

The state of the solid in this problem is defined by three field variables: the displacement field
\bm{u}\colon\Omega\times\mathrm{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the crack phase-field
\alpha\colon\Omega\times\mathrm{T}\to[0,1]
to integrate the non-local effects into the local damage variable and the plastic strain tensor
\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}\colon\Omega\times\mathrm{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\mathrm{sym}}
to incorporate a frictional sliding mechanism, and the ratcheting strain tensor
\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}}\colon\Omega\times\mathrm{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\mathrm{sym}}
to include irreversible ratcheting effects.

In the classical phase-field approach, the degradation function multiplies the elasticity tensor to reduce the energy during damage
Kuhn et al. 2015
,
Wu et al. 2020
. However, this is not realistic as it results in symmetric behavior under tension and compression. To address this limitation, various energy decomposition techniques have been introduced in the literature
Freddi and Royer-Carfagni 2011
,
Hakimzadeh et al. 2022
,
Vicentini et al. 2024
, with the two main ones, spectral
Miehe et al. 2010a
and volumetric-deviatoric
Amor et al. 2009
splits. In contrast, the micromechanics-based free energy formulation naturally captures asymmetric behavior without requiring an explicit energy split. This is achieved by distinguishing between open and closed microcracks. Based on this concept, the free energy density is expressed as:

\psi(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}},\alpha)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\,\bm{\varepsilon}:\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{dam}}(\alpha):\bm{\varepsilon}&\quad\text{if \sf{open}},\\
\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\varepsilon}-\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}-\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}}):\bm{\mathsf{C}}:(\bm{\varepsilon}-\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}-\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}})+\frac{1}{2}\,\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}:\bm{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathrm{kin}}(\alpha):\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}&\quad\text{if \sf{closed}}.\end{cases}

(2)

Equation (
2
) shows that in the presence of open microcracks, frictional sliding is not expected, resulting in the exclusion of plastic strain from contributing to the strain energy density. Consequently, the model behaves in a brittle manner. On the other hand, when closed microcracks undergo sliding, both the stored energy and the blocked friction energy contribute to the strain energy density. The ratcheting strain appears in the elastic part of the free energy because it represents a slowly evolving, irreversible shift of the elastic reference state caused by microstructural rearrangements. In the rheological model (figure
1
), it acts as a slider in series with the elastic spring, altering the stored elastic energy at each cycle.

In equation
2
, the elasticity tensor
\bm{\mathsf{C}}
is given by

\bm{\mathsf{C}}=K\bm{1}\otimes\bm{1}+2\mu\bigg(\bm{\mathsf{I}}-\frac{1}{3}\bm{1}\otimes\bm{1}\bigg),

(3)

where
K
and
\mu
are bulk and shear moduli. The damage-dependent elasticity tensor
\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{dam}}(\alpha)
is decomposed to its volumetric and deviatoric parts as follows

\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{dam}}(\alpha)\coloneqq g_{K}(\alpha)K\bm{1}\otimes\bm{1}+2\,g_{\mu}(\alpha)\mu\bigg(\bm{\mathsf{I}}-\frac{1}{3}\bm{1}\otimes\bm{1}\bigg),

(4)

in which
g_{K}(\alpha)
and
g_{\mu}(\alpha)
are the degradation functions defined as

g_{K}(\alpha)\coloneqq\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}}{1+(b-1)[1-(1-\alpha)^{2}]}\,,

(5)

where
b
is introduced as a dimensionless tuning parameter and
g_{\mu}(\alpha)
is obtained as

g_{\mu}(\alpha)=\frac{g_{K}(\alpha)}{g_{K}(\alpha)+\frac{b_{\mu}}{b_{K}}[1-g_{K}(\alpha)]}\,.

(6)

The coefficients
b_{K}
and
b_{\mu}
are obtained using the Mori-Tanaka homogenization scheme
Mori and Tanaka 1973
as

b_{K}=\frac{16}{9}\frac{1-\nu^{2}}{1-2\nu}\,,\hskip 10.00002ptb_{\mu}=\frac{32}{45}\frac{(1-\nu)(5-\nu)}{2-\nu}\,.

(7)

It is clear from equations (
5
) and (
6
) that the degradation functions approach zero for fully damaged material (
\alpha=1
). The last function from the free energy formulation to be defined is the damage-dependent kinematic hardening modulus denoted as
\bm{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathrm{kin}}(\alpha)
, which is obtained meeting the continuity of free energy for the onset of microcrack closure when no ratcheting strain has yet accumulated, as

\bm{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathrm{kin}}(\alpha)=\Big[{\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{dam}}}^{-1}(\alpha)-\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{-1}\Big]^{-1},

(8)

which takes the decomposed form of

\bm{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathrm{kin}}(\alpha)=H^{\mathrm{kin}}_{K}(\alpha)\bm{1}\otimes\bm{1}+H^{\mathrm{kin}}_{\mu}(\alpha)\bigg(\bm{\mathsf{I}}-\frac{1}{3}\bm{1}\otimes\bm{1}\bigg),

(9)

where the volumetric and deviatoric parts are given by

H^{\mathrm{kin}}_{K}(\alpha)=\frac{g_{K}(\alpha)K}{1-g_{K}(\alpha)}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptH^{\mathrm{kin}}_{\mu}(\alpha)=\frac{2\,g_{\mu}(\alpha)\mu}{1-g_{\mu}(\alpha)}\,.

(10)

The kinematic hardening, as shown in equation (
8
), is obtained from the stiffness tensor and is linked to the degradation function
g(\alpha)
. Consequently, there is no longer a necessity for phenomenological definitions of kinematic hardening.
\bm{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathrm{kin}}(\alpha)
, which here couples damage and plasticity, approaches infinity as the damage variable tends to zero. To prevent this, an initial damage is always required, representing the existence of microcracks which aligns with the physical assumptions of the micromechanics-based framework. The use of a small initial value has no effect on the final damage profile, as discussed in
Ulloa et al. 2022
.

The stress-strain relations are obtained from the free energy density (equation (
2
)) as

\bm{\sigma}(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}},\alpha)=\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial\bm{\varepsilon}}=\begin{cases}\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{dam}}(\alpha):\bm{\varepsilon}&\quad\text{if \sf{open}},\\
\bm{\mathsf{C}}:(\bm{\varepsilon}-\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}-\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}})&\quad\text{if \sf{closed}}.\end{cases}

(11)

Furthermore, the generalized stresses conjugate to the plastic strain are obtained as

\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}},\alpha)=-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}}=\begin{cases}\bm{0}&\quad\text{if \sf{open}},\\
\bm{\mathsf{C}}:(\bm{\varepsilon}-\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}-\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}})-\bm{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathrm{kin}}(\alpha):\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}&\quad\text{if \sf{closed}}.\end{cases}

(12)

\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}
is interpreted as the local stress acting on microcrack faces within the RVE
Zhu et al. 2011
. When microcracks are open, indicating no contact between them,
\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}
tends to zero. Therefore, the following conditions are employed in the model to distinguish between open and closed microcracks or to differentiate the tensile and compressive/shear regimes at a macroscopic level as

\begin{cases}\tr\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}},\alpha)=0&\quad\text{if \sf{open}},\\
\tr\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}},\alpha)<0&\quad\text{if \sf{closed}}.\end{cases}

(13)

Here,
\tr\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}
is the volumetric component of the generalized stress
\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}
. Since the free energy also depends on the ratcheting strain, the generalized stresses conjugate to the ratcheting strains are obtained as follows

{s}^{\mathrm{r}}(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}},\alpha)=-\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}}}=\begin{cases}\bm{0}&\quad\text{if \sf{open}},\\
\bm{\mathsf{C}}:(\bm{\varepsilon}-\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}-\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}})&\quad\text{if \sf{closed}}.\end{cases}

(14)

Finally, the generalized stresses conjugate to the crack phase-field are given by

{s}^{\mathrm{d}}(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}},\alpha)=-\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial\alpha}=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{2}\,\bm{\varepsilon}:{\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{dam}}}^{\prime}(\alpha):\bm{\varepsilon}&\quad\text{if \sf{open}},\\
-\frac{1}{2}{\,\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}:\bm{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathrm{kin}}}^{\prime}(\alpha):\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}&\quad\text{if \sf{closed}}.\end{cases}

(15)

It should be noted that the model employs a two-regime definition of the free energy, distinguishing between the open and closed microcrack states. This piecewise formulation intentionally reflects the unilateral mechanical behavior associated with crack opening and closure. In the original formulation, without ratcheting strain
\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}}
, the continuity of the free energy and its derivatives across the open–closed transition is enforced, leading to the definition of the kinematic hardening modulus in equation (
8
)
Ulloa et al. 2022
,
Sarem et al. 2023a
.

In the present formulation, the introduction of the ratcheting strain
\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}}
results in a controlled, regime-switching discontinuity in the free energy. The switching condition between open and closed microcracks (see equation (
13
)) ensures that this discontinuity occurs only at physically meaningful transitions, corresponding to the onset or loss of contact between crack faces. This discontinuity is therefore a representation of two distinct micro-mechanical states: open microcracks, where no contact or friction occurs, and closed microcracks, where sliding and ratcheting mechanisms are activated.

The following sections show that dissipation is consistently formulated through convex, non-negative potentials, ensuring that the overall formulation remains thermodynamically admissible. Consequently, the Clausius–Duhem inequality is satisfied even in the presence of non-smooth energy branches. The accumulated ratcheting strain introduces an additional irreversible, dissipative mechanism without violating the variational structure of the model
Francfort 2018
.

3.2
Plasticity criterion with a non-associated plastic flow rule

To define the plastic evolution equations, the generalized stress
\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}
is constrained to a non-empty, closed and convex set of admissible plastic generalized stresses
\mathbb{K}

\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}\in\mathbb{K}\coloneqq\big\{\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\mathrm{sym}}\ \ |\ \ f^{\mathrm{p}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\leq 0\big\},

(16)

The choice of the plastic yield function
f^{\mathrm{p}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})
depends on the selected plasticity model. The yield surface is a fundamental component of plasticity models that defines the boundary of the elastic domain
Ulloa et al. 2021b
. Here, the non-associative Drucker-Prager plasticity model is employed to capture frictional-dilational behavior of quasi-brittle materials for shear failure. Associative models predict excessive dilation for these materials which is unrealistic
Vermeer 1998
. From micromechanical point of view, plastic deformation in quasi-brittle materials is primarily due to the frictional sliding of microcracks. In highly porous materials, an additional contribution comes from pore collapse under compressive loading
Liu et al. 2022
. The Drucker-Prager-type yield function to characterize frictional sliding in generalized stress space is defined as

f^{\mathrm{p}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\coloneqq\|\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|+\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}A_{\varphi}\mathrm{tr}\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}},

(17)

where
A_{\varphi}
is a dimensionless friction coefficient. The set
\mathbb{K}
represents a Drucker-Prager cone in generalized stress space. The apex of this cone is placed at the origin, meaning the model does not account for cohesion in generalized stress space. This ensures that
\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dev}}
can vanish during the opening-closing transition while still allowing for damage-dependent cohesion in true stress space
Ulloa et al. 2022
.

Another key component of plasticity models is the evolution equation for plastic strain, governed by the flow rule, which determines how the plastic strain develops in response to stress once yielding occurs. In associative plasticity models, the flow rule is derived from the principle of maximum dissipation and corresponds to the normality rule, meaning the plastic strain rate is normal to the yield surface in stress space. In contrast, non-associative models abandon normality in favor of a more general flow rule, which is derived from a plastic potential different from the yield function
Ulloa et al. 2021b
.

To incorporate ratcheting behavior into the flow rule, the plastic potential function
g^{\mathrm{p}}
is defined as follows
Ulloa et al. 2021b
:

g^{\mathrm{p}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}},\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}})\coloneqq\|\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}_{{\mathrm{dev}}}\|+\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}(A_{\theta}\mathrm{tr}\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}+\beta_{K}A_{\theta}\mathrm{tr}\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}})+\beta_{\mu}\|\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}}_{{\mathrm{dev}}}\|,

(18)

where
A_{\theta}
is the dilation coefficient which is assumed to take a value between zero and
A_{\varphi}
.
\beta_{K}
and
\beta_{\mu}
are ratcheting parameters associated with volumetric and deviatoric contributions, respectively. Since the underlying plasticity model is pressure-dependent, the ratcheting mechanism is also formulated to account for both volumetric and deviatoric strain components
Ulloa et al. 2021b
. In this context, macroscopic dilatancy is directly related to the normal opening of microcracks during frictional sliding, caused by the roughness of these microcracks. It is important to note that despite the non-associative nature of the plasticity model, it remains variational. Further details on this can be found in
Ulloa et al. 2021b
,
Ulloa et al. 2022
.

The flow direction is defined by the subdifferential of the plastic potential:

\partial g(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}},\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}})=\{\hat{\bm{n}},\hat{\bm{r}}\},

(19)

with

\hat{\bm{n}}=\hat{\bm{n}}_{\mathrm{dev}}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}A_{\theta}\bm{1}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\hat{\bm{r}}=\beta_{\mu}\hat{\bm{r}}_{\mathrm{dev}}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\beta_{K}A_{\theta}\bm{1},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 5.0pt\begin{cases}\hat{\bm{n}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\in\partial\|\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|,\\[4.5pt]
\hat{\bm{r}}_{\,\mathrm{dev}}\in\partial\|\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|.\end{cases}

(20)

Here,
\hat{\bm{n}}
and
\hat{\bm{r}}
represent the directions of plastic and ratcheting strain rates, respectively. Accordingly, the non-associative flow rule is defined as

\displaystyle\{\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}},\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{r}}\}\in\mathbb{Q}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}},\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}})\coloneqq\big\{\lambda\,\{\hat{\bm{n}},\hat{\bm{r}}\}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\mathrm{dev}}\times\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\mathrm{dev}}

(21)

\displaystyle|\ \ \lambda\geq 0,\,\,\lambda=0\,\,\,\text{if}\,\,\,f^{\mathrm{p}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})<0\big\},

in which
\lambda
is the plastic multiplier and
\mathbb{Q}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})
is the plastic flow set.

3.3
Damage criterion with an associated flow rule

Similar to the work of Carrara et al.
Carrara et al. 2020
and our previous work
Sarem et al. 2025
, fatigue effects are considered by employing a fatigue variable
\mathcal{F}
and a fatigue degradation function
h(\mathcal{F})
with the following properties:

h(\mathcal{F}\leq\mathcal{F}_{0})=1,\hskip 10.00002pth(\mathcal{F}>\mathcal{F}_{0})\in[0,1),\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{\mathrm{d}h(\mathcal{F})}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}}\leq 0,

(22)

in which
\mathcal{F}_{0}
is a material threshold parameter, signifying that once the fatigue variable surpasses this threshold, fatigue-induced degradation initiates. This condition ensures that the fatigue degradation function decreases from one to zero and does not increase. The degradation function follows a phenomenological law and determines how fatigue impacts the fracture energy of the material
Li et al. 2023a
. Here, two categories of fatigue degradation functions are incorporated, namely asymptotic and logarithmic, to characterize the fatigue-induced damage. The asymptotic fatigue degradation function is given by

h(\mathcal{F})\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\quad\mathcal{F}(t)\leq\mathcal{F}_{0},\\
\bigg(\frac{2\mathcal{F}_{0}}{\mathcal{F}(t)+\mathcal{F}_{0}}\bigg)^{2}&\text{if}\quad\mathcal{F}(t)>\mathcal{F}_{0},\end{cases}

(23)

and the logarithmic fatigue degradation function is given as follows

h(\mathcal{F})\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{if}\quad\mathcal{F}(t)<\mathcal{F}_{0},\\
\bigg[1-k\log\bigg(\frac{\mathcal{F}(t)}{\mathcal{F}_{0}}\bigg)\bigg]^{2}&\text{if}\quad\mathcal{F}_{0}\leq\mathcal{F}(t)<\mathcal{F}_{0}10^{1/k},\\
0&\text{if}\quad\mathcal{F}(t)\geq\mathcal{F}_{0}10^{1/k},\end{cases}

(24)

where
k
is a material parameter that adjusts the slope of the logarithmic function. An increase in
k
evidently amplifies the stress difference between the initial and final cycles. By choosing the latter degradation function,
k
enhances the model’s adaptability during calibration against experimental data, providing additional freedom to accurately represent the material’s behavior
Sarem et al. 2023b
. Although alternative fatigue degradation functions have been proposed in the literature, it is important to highlight that these two functions have been validated
Carrara et al. 2020
to effectively capture key aspects of the fatigue phenomenon, such as the relationship between load ratio (S) and number of cycles to failure (N), commonly represented by the
S-N
curve.

In many studies, researchers have proposed degrading the fracture energy as the material accumulates strain
Alessi et al. 2018
or energy
Carrara et al. 2020
,
Kristensen and Martínez-Pañeda 2020
,
Sarem et al. 2023b
, based on the understanding that cyclic loading, inelastic deformation, or microcrack accumulation progressively reduce the material’s ability to resist crack propagation. In this study, the fatigue history variable is defined to depend on the components of the free energy that are influenced by damage, following the approach proposed in
Carrara et al. 2020
. The reasoning behind this choice is to ensure that only the energy contributing to damage evolution drives the degradation of fracture resistance. The fatigue variable
\mathcal{F}
is thus defined as

\mathcal{F}(t)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}\dot{\vartheta}H\big(\dot{\vartheta}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

(25)

in which
H
represents the Heaviside function, preventing fatigue degradation during unloading and
\vartheta
is the fatigue history variable considered as:

\vartheta(t)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}:\varepsilon:\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{dam}}(\alpha):\varepsilon\quad\text{if open},\\
\frac{1}{2}:\varepsilon^{\mathrm{p}}:\bm{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathrm{kin}}(\alpha):\varepsilon^{\mathrm{p}}\quad\text{if closed}.\end{cases}

(26)

It is worth noting that the fatigue variable starts accumulating from the first cycle, which should be considered during calibration and parameter fitting
Kalina et al. 2023
.

k

k
controls the sharpness of the drop, while the asymptotic function lacks such a parameter, resulting in a fixed decay rate.

The generalized stress constraint is defined in a non-empty, closed and convex set of admissible damage generalized stresses
\mathbb{K}^{\mathrm{d}}

s^{\mathrm{d}}\in\mathbb{K}^{\mathrm{d}}\coloneqq\big\{s^{\mathrm{d}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\ \ |\ \ f^{\mathrm{d}}(s^{\mathrm{d}};\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\leq 0\big\}.

(27)

The damage yield function
f^{\mathrm{d}}
is defined within the framework of irreversible thermodynamics as

f^{\mathrm{d}}(s^{\mathrm{d}};\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\coloneqq s^{\mathrm{d}}-h(\mathcal{F})\frac{{G}_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\alpha}{{\ell}}+{\ell}\,\mathrm{div}[h(\mathcal{F}){G}_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\nabla\alpha].

(28)

where
{\ell}
is the length scale of the phase-field model which regularizes the displacement discontinuities due to cracks. Here, the degradation function acts as a modulation parameter for dissipation without changing the fundamental structure of the micromechanics-based phase-field model. Furthermore, the fracture energy
{G}_{\mathrm{c}}
is chosen as

{G}_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\coloneqq\begin{cases}G_{\mathrm{cI}}\quad&\text{if }\tr\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}=0,\\
G_{\mathrm{cII}}\quad&\text{if }\tr\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}<0.\end{cases}

(29)

This distinction allows mode I fracture energy
G_{\mathrm{cI}}
to govern tensile fractures, while mode II fracture energy
G_{\mathrm{cII}}
accounts for compressive and shear fractures. Although the discontinuity in the fracture energy definition does not violate the variational formulation or thermodynamics consistency, it brings numerical issues due to the abrupt change in its value.

To address this challenge, a mathematical regularization is introduced to smooth the transition between tensile and shear fracture modes. The regularized function that replaces equation (
29
) is defined as

G_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\coloneqq\begin{cases}G_{\mathrm{cI}}&\text{if }\tr\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}=0,\\[6.0pt]
G_{\mathrm{cII}}+(G_{\mathrm{cI}}-G_{\mathrm{cII}})\cdot s(\xi)&\text{if }-\epsilon<\tr\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}<0,\\[6.0pt]
G_{\mathrm{cII}}&\text{if }\tr\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}\leq-\epsilon.\end{cases}

(30)

Here,
\epsilon>0
controls the width of the transition zone. The function
s(\xi)
is known as the smoothstep function, a cubic polynomial interpolant that provides a smooth transition. The normalized parameter
\xi
reparametrizes
\tr\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}
within the smoothing interval and is defined as follows
Stoer et al. 1980
:

\xi=\frac{\tr\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}+\epsilon}{\epsilon},\hskip 10.00002pts(\xi)=3\xi^{2}-2\xi^{3}.

(31)

This regularization ensures that the fracture energy function is continuous and at least
C^{1}
-differentiable across the transition zone, improving numerical robustness. The behavior of equation (
30
) is illustrated in figure

G_{\mathrm{cI}}=1
,
G_{\mathrm{cII}}=3
and
\epsilon=0.05
.

3.4
Dissipation potential

The dissipation potential characterizes the energy dissipated during irreversible processes such as damage, plastic deformation and ratcheting. It is defined as a function of the rates of internal variables. A thermodynamically admissible formulation of the dissipation potential is expressed as the sum of plastic and damage contributions
Alessi et al. 2019
,
Ulloa et al. 2021a
:

{\phi(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}},\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{r}},\dot{\alpha},\nabla\dot{\alpha};\alpha,\nabla\alpha,{\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}},{\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}}})=\phi^{\mathrm{p}}(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}},\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{r}};{\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}},{\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}}})+\phi^{\mathrm{d}}(\dot{\alpha},\nabla\dot{\alpha};\alpha,\nabla\alpha,{\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}}),}

(32)

where
\phi^{\mathrm{p}}
and
\phi^{\mathrm{d}}
represent plastic and damage dissipation potentials, respectively.

To define the plastic dissipation, the set of admissible stress states must first be specified. As shown in
Ulloa et al. 2021b
, the non-associative model maintains a variational structure if a state-dependent convex set is defined as follows
Francfort 2018
:

\mathbb{L}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}},\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}})\coloneqq\bigg\{\{\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{p}},\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{r}}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\mathrm{sym}}\times\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\mathrm{sym}}\ \ |\ \ g^{\mathrm{p}}(\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{p}},\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{r}})\leq g^{\mathrm{p}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}},\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}})-f^{\mathrm{p}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\bigg\},

(33)

where
\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{p}}
and
\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{r}}
denote admissible variations of
\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}
and
\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}}
, respectively. Based on this set, and following the principle of maximum dissipation
Hill 1948
,
Moreau 1974
, the plastic dissipation potential is defined as:

\phi^{\mathrm{p}}(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}},\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{r}};\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}},\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}})=\mathrm{sup}\big\{\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{p}}:\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}}+{\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{r}}}:\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{r}}-I_{\mathbb{L}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}},\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}})}(\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{p}},\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{r}})\big\}.

(34)

This means that for a given plastic strain rate
\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}}
and ratcheting strain rate
\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{r}}
, the plastic dissipation potential is the supremum (upper bound) of the work done by any admissible plastic stress
\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{p}}
and ratcheting stress
\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{r}}
.
From equations (
33
) and (
34
), the state-dependent plastic dissipation potential is evaluated for all
\{\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}},\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{r}}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\mathrm{sym}}\times\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{\mathrm{sym}}

\displaystyle\phi^{\mathrm{p}}(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}},\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{r}};\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}},\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}})=\sup\bigg\{{\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{p}}}:\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}}+{\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{r}}}:\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{r}}

\displaystyle|\ \ \|\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{p}}_{{\mathrm{dev}}}\|+\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}(A_{\theta}\mathrm{tr}\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{p}}+\beta_{K}A_{\theta}\mathrm{tr}\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{r}})+\beta_{\mu}\|\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{r}}_{{\mathrm{dev}}}\|

(35)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}(A_{\theta}-A_{\varphi})\tr\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}A_{\theta}\beta_{K}\tr\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}}+\beta_{\mu}\|\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}}_{{\mathrm{dev}}}\|\bigg\}.

By decomposing all stress and strain terms into their deviatoric and volumetric parts, this expression becomes:

\displaystyle\phi^{\mathrm{p}}(\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}},\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{r}};\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}},\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}})

\displaystyle=\sup\bigg\{{\tilde{\bm{s}}_{\mathrm{dev}}^{\mathrm{p}}}:\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{\mathrm{dev}}^{\mathrm{p}}+{\tilde{\bm{s}}_{\mathrm{dev}}^{\mathrm{r}}}:\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{\mathrm{dev}}^{\mathrm{r}}+\tfrac{1}{3}(\tr{\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{p}}}\tr\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}}+\tr{\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{r}}}\tr\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{r}})\ \ |

(36)

\displaystyle\tr\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{p}}\leq\frac{(A_{\theta}-A_{\varphi})}{A_{\theta}}\tr\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}-\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\frac{1}{A_{\theta}}\|\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{p}}_{{\mathrm{dev}}}\|+\beta_{K}\tr\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\frac{\beta_{\mu}}{A_{\theta}}\|\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|-\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\frac{\beta_{\mu}}{A_{\theta}}\|\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{r}}_{{\mathrm{dev}}}\|-\beta_{K}\tr\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{r}}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\sup\bigg\{\dfrac{\tr\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}}}{3A_{\theta}}\bigg((A_{\theta}-A_{\varphi})\tr\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{2}}\beta_{\mu}\|\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|+\beta_{K}A_{\theta}\mathrm{tr}\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\|{\tilde{\bm{s}}_{\mathrm{dev}}^{\mathrm{p}}}\|\bigg(\|\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{\mathrm{dev}}^{\mathrm{p}}\|-\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}A_{\theta}}\tr\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}}\bigg)+\frac{1}{3}\tr\tilde{\bm{s}}^{\mathrm{r}}(\tr\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{r}}-\beta_{K}\tr\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}})

\displaystyle+\|\tilde{\bm{s}}_{\mathrm{dev}}^{\mathrm{r}}\|\bigg(\|\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{r}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|-\frac{\beta_{\mu}}{\sqrt{6}A_{\theta}}\tr\dot{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}\bigg)\bigg\}.

It is important to note that
\|{\tilde{\bm{s}}_{\mathrm{dev}}^{\mathrm{p}}}\|
and
\|{\tilde{\bm{s}}_{\mathrm{dev}}^{\mathrm{r}}}\|
are free variables, while the terms multiplying them i.e.,
(\|\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{\mathrm{dev}}^{\mathrm{p}}\|-\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}A_{\theta}}\tr\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}})
and
(\|\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{r}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|-\frac{\beta_{\mu}}{\sqrt{6}A_{\theta}}\tr\dot{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}})
, must be bounded to ensure a finite supremum.
These conditions lead directly to the final form of the plastic dissipation potential:

\displaystyle\phi^{\mathrm{p}}(

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}},\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{r}};\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}},\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}})=

(37)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\tr\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}}}{3A_{\theta}}\bigg((A_{\theta}-A_{\varphi})\tr\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}\\[-28.45274pt]
\hskip 28.45274pt+\sqrt{\frac{2}{2}}\beta_{\mu}\|\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|+\beta_{K}A_{\theta}\mathrm{tr}\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}}\bigg)\quad\text{if}\ \begin{cases}\tr\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}}\geq\sqrt{6}A_{\theta}\|\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|,\\
\|\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{r}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|\leq\frac{\beta_{\mu}}{\sqrt{6}A_{\theta}}\tr\dot{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}},\\
\tr\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{r}}=\beta_{K}\tr\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathrm{p}},\end{cases}\\
+\infty\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This extended formulation effectively captures the progressive and irreversible nature of ratcheting in materials subjected to cyclic loading. The explicit control provided by the ratcheting parameters
\beta_{\mu}
and
\beta_{K}
enables the simulation of complex cyclic behavior, such as mean strain accumulation and asymmetric hysteresis loops.

The damage dissipation potential is derived following the same procedure as the plastic dissipation potential, starting from the generalized stress constraint (equation (
27
)), which is governed by the damage yield condition (equation (
28
)). Once again, by applying the principle of maximum dissipation to damage evolution and considering the damage flow rule:

\dot{\alpha}\in\partial I_{\mathbb{K}^{\mathrm{d}}}(s^{\mathrm{d}}),

(38)

the damage dissipation potential is defined in a variational form as:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\phi^{\mathrm{d}}(\dot{\alpha},\nabla\dot{\alpha};\alpha,\nabla\alpha,{\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{sup}\bigg\{\int_{\Omega}\Big(\tilde{s}^{\mathrm{d}}\,\dot{\alpha}-I_{\mathbb{K}^{\mathrm{d}}}(\tilde{s}^{\mathrm{d}})\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\bigg\}

(39)

\displaystyle=\sup\bigg\{\int_{\Omega}\Big(\tilde{s}^{\mathrm{d}}\dot{\alpha}\ |\ \tilde{s}^{\mathrm{d}}\leq h(\mathcal{F})\frac{G_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})}{\ell}\alpha-\ell\operatorname{div}\big[h(\mathcal{F})G_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\nabla\alpha\big]\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\bigg\}.

By substituting the upper bound of
s^{\mathrm{d}}
, the following is obtained:

\int_{\Omega}\phi^{\mathrm{d}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\sup\bigg\{\int_{\Omega}\Big(h(\mathcal{F})\frac{G_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})}{\ell}\alpha\dot{\alpha}-\ell\operatorname{div}\big[h(\mathcal{F})G_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\nabla\alpha\big]\dot{\alpha}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\bigg\}.

(40)

Applying the product rule to the second term in the integral:

\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{div}\big[h(\mathcal{F})G_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\nabla\alpha\big]\dot{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{div}\big[\dot{\alpha}h(\mathcal{F})G_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\nabla\alpha\big]\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}-\int_{\Omega}h(\mathcal{F})G_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\nabla\alpha\cdot\nabla\dot{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(41)

Next, by employing the divergence theorem:

\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{div}\big[\dot{\alpha}h(\mathcal{F})G_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\nabla\alpha\big]\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\partial\Omega}(h(\mathcal{F})G_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\nabla\alpha)\cdot\mathbf{n}\,\mathrm{d}S.

(42)

Applying the natural boundary condition, which enforces zero flux of
\alpha
at the boundary:

(h(\mathcal{F})G_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\nabla\alpha)\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on }\partial\Omega,

(43)

the equation (
41
) simplifies to

\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{div}\big[h(\mathcal{F})G_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\nabla\alpha\big]\dot{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=-\int_{\Omega}h(\mathcal{F})G_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\nabla\alpha\cdot\nabla\dot{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(44)

Substituting this result back into equation (
40
), the following expression is obtained:

\int_{\Omega}\phi^{\mathrm{d}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\sup\bigg\{\int_{\Omega}\Big(h(\mathcal{F})\frac{G_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})}{\ell}\alpha\dot{\alpha}-\ell h(\mathcal{F})G_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\nabla\alpha\nabla\dot{\alpha}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\bigg\}.

(45)

Finally, the explicit form of the damage dissipation potential is obtained as follows:

\phi^{\mathrm{d}}(\dot{\alpha},\nabla\dot{\alpha};\alpha,\nabla\alpha,{\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}})\coloneqq\begin{cases}h(\mathcal{F})\frac{{G}_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})}{{\ell}}\big(\alpha\,\dot{\alpha}+{\ell}^{2}\nabla\alpha\cdot\nabla\dot{\alpha}\big)\quad\text{if }\dot{\alpha}\geq 0,\\
+\infty\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(46)

The damage dissipation potential includes a fatigue degradation function
h(\mathcal{F})
, which reflect the progressive weakening of the material under cyclic loading. This reduces the effective fracture energy and modifies both the local and gradient contributions of damage evolution. The condition
\dot{\alpha}\geq 0
enforces the irreversibility of damage evolution.

4
Numerical implementation

This section presents the numerical implementation of the micromechanics-based phase-field model. A three-field staggered algorithm is adopted to decouple the coupled system of PDEs in order to solve a sequence of convex sub-problems. These sub-problems are obtained by minimizing the energy functional with respect to one field variable at a time
Miehe et al. 2010b
,
Wambacq et al. 2021
. Through the staggered solution technique, equilibrium, damage and plasticity equations are solved independently for the displacement field
\bm{u}
, the crack phase-field
\alpha
, and the plastic and ratcheting strains
\{\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}}\}
, using Newton-Raphson schemes. While the energy functional remains non-convex as a whole, it is convex with respect to each individual variable
Alessi 2013
.

\bm{u}
. Subsequently, the damage evolution problem is solved to obtain
\alpha
using the

approach
Miehe et al. 2010b
. Next, a return-mapping algorithm is employed to update
\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}
. In this sub-problem, the algorithm determines the state of the solid matrix, denoted as
state
, in terms of open or closed microcracks using equation (
13
). Through this iterative procedure, the governing equations are solved at each loading step and for every Gauss point, ensuring a consistent update of the field variables.

At each iteration, one field variable is solved while keeping the others fixed. The updated field is then incorporated into the system, and the process is repeated for the remaining variables until convergence is achieved. This sequential update ensures numerical stability while effectively handling the strong coupling between mechanical equilibrium, plasticity, and phase-field damage evolution. The detailed solution procedure for the three sub-problems is provided in the next subsections.

The staggered solution procedure.

4.1
Mechanical balance equation

First, the equilibrium problem is solved for the displacement
\bm{u}
by linearizing the mechanical balance equation, while the other two variables
\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}
and
\alpha
are fixed. The weak form of the mechanical balance is given by

\int_{\Omega}\big(\bm{\sigma}:\nabla^{\mathrm{s}}\tilde{\bm{u}}-\rho\bm{b}\cdot\tilde{\bm{u}}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}-\int_{\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{N}}}\bar{\bm{t}}\cdot\tilde{\bm{u}}\,\mathrm{d}S=0\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\,\tilde{\bm{u}}\in\tilde{\mathscr{U}}.

(47)

The given equation involves the body forces per unit mass denoted by
\bm{b}
, the unit mass denoted by
\rho
, and the imposed tractions denoted by
\bar{\bm{t}}
. When linearized, the equation leads to the following expression

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla^{\mathrm{s}}[\bm{u}^{(k+1)}-\bm{u}^{(k)}]:\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{ep}(k)}:\nabla^{\mathrm{s}}\tilde{\bm{u}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

(48)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\big(\bm{\sigma}^{(k)}:\nabla^{\mathrm{s}}\tilde{\bm{u}}-\rho\bm{b}\cdot\tilde{\bm{u}}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}+\int_{\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{N}}}\bar{\bm{t}}\cdot\tilde{\bm{u}}\,\mathrm{d}S\eqqcolon-r_{\bm{u}}^{(k)}\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\,\tilde{\bm{u}}\in\tilde{\mathscr{U}}.

The equation is solved iteratively, where each iteration produces a displacement denoted by
\bm{u}^{(k+1)}
. The process continues until the residual, represented by
r_{\bm{u}}^{(k)}
, is smaller than a predefined tolerance
\mathtt{TOL}_{\bm{u}}
. In this context, the index
k
indicates the current iteration counter. The terms
\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{ep}(k)}\coloneqq\partial\bm{\sigma}^{(k)}/\partial\bm{\varepsilon}^{(k)}
and
\bm{\sigma}^{(k)}
refer to the consistent tangent and stress tensor at the current iteration
De Borst et al. 2012
, respectively.

4.2
Plastic evolution problem

The plastic strain
\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}
and ratcheting strain
\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}}
are computed by solving the plastic evolution equations while keeping the displacement
\bm{u}
and damage variable
\alpha
fixed. An implicit backward Euler scheme is used, with the non-associative flow rule discretized at each iteration as:

\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}=\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}_{n}+\Delta\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002pt\Delta\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}=\Delta\gamma\,\hat{\bm{n}},\hskip 10.00002pt\hat{\bm{n}}\in\partial g^{\mathrm{p}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}).

(49)

For simplicity, the iteration counter
k
is omitted. The incremental approximation of the plastic multiplier is denoted as
\Delta\gamma
, with
\dot{\gamma}\coloneqq\lambda
. The trial stresses are computed as:

\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p\,trial}}\coloneqq\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{trial}}-\bm{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathrm{kin}}(\alpha)\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}_{n}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002pt\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{trial}}\coloneqq\bm{\mathsf{C}}:(\bm{\varepsilon}-\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}_{n}-\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}}_{n}).

(50)

The resulting incremental system is:

\begin{cases}\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}=\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}_{n}+\Delta\gamma\,\hat{\bm{n}},\quad\hat{\bm{n}}\in\partial g^{\mathrm{p}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}),\\
\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}=\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p\,trial}}-\Delta\gamma\,\big[\bm{\mathsf{C}}+\bm{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathrm{kin}}(\alpha)\big]:\hat{\bm{n}},\\
f(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})\leq 0,\quad\Delta\gamma\geq 0,\quad\Delta\gamma\,f(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})=0.\end{cases}

(51)

The elastic predictor/plastic corrector algorithm is used to solve the aforementioned system
de Souza et al. 2011
, for which the yield function is first evaluated for the trial state. If
f(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p\,trial}})\leq 0
, then the trial state is admissible and the solution to (
51
) takes the following form

\begin{cases}\Delta\gamma=0,\\
\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}=\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}_{n},\\
\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}}=\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}}_{n},\\
\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}=\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p\,trial}},\end{cases}\hskip 10.00002pt\implies\hskip 10.00002pt\begin{cases}\bm{\sigma}=\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{trial}},\\
\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{ep}}=\frac{\partial\bm{\sigma}}{\partial\bm{\varepsilon}}=\bm{\mathsf{C}}.\end{cases}

(52)

If
f(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p\,trial}})>0
, then a corrector step should be applied to return the trial state back to the set of admissible stresses and hence, the incremental system (equation (
51
)) is given by

\begin{cases}\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}=\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}_{n}+\Delta\gamma\,\hat{\bm{n}},\quad\hat{\bm{n}}\in\partial g^{\mathrm{p}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}),\\
\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}=\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p\,trial}}-\Delta\gamma\,\big[\bm{\mathsf{C}}+\bm{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathrm{kin}}(\alpha)\big]:\hat{\bm{n}},\\
f(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}})=0,\end{cases}

(53)

Now it should be determined if
\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}
is located on the smooth part of the Drucker-Prager cone or at the apex. For this purpose, an implicit return-mapping scheme similar to Sysala et al.
Sysala et al. 2016
is employed. First, the generalized stress
\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}
is decomposed into deviatoric and volumetric parts:

\displaystyle\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dev}}

\displaystyle\equiv\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p\,trial}}_{\mathrm{dev}}-\Delta\gamma\,\big[2\mu+H^{\mathrm{kin}}_{\mu}(\alpha)\big]\hat{\bm{n}}_{\mathrm{dev}}

(54)

\displaystyle\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\tr\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}

\displaystyle\equiv\tr\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p\,trial}}-3\sqrt{6}\Delta\gamma\,A_{\theta}\big[K+H^{\mathrm{kin}}_{K}(\alpha)\big].

Subsequently, the deviatoric part of the trial stress
\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p\,trial}}_{\mathrm{dev}}
is expressed as

\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p\,trial}}_{\mathrm{dev}}=\begin{cases}\bigg(1+\dfrac{\Delta\gamma\,\big[2\mu+H^{\mathrm{kin}}_{\mu}(\alpha)\big]}{\|\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|}\bigg)\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dev}}&\text{if}\quad\|\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|>0,\\
\Delta\gamma\,\big[2\mu+H^{\mathrm{kin}}_{\mu}(\alpha)\big]\hat{\bm{n}}_{\mathrm{dev}},\quad\|\hat{\bm{n}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|\leq 1&\text{if}\quad\|\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|=0.\end{cases}

(55)

The corresponding norm is defined as

\|\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|=\Big\langle\|\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p\,trial}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|-\Delta\gamma\,\big[2\mu+H^{\mathrm{kin}}_{\mu}(\alpha)\big]\Big\rangle_{+}\,,

(56)

where
\langle\Box\rangle_{+}\coloneqq(\Box+|\Box|)/2
. To express the yield condition in terms of the trial stress and the plastic multiplier, an auxiliary function is defined as:

\displaystyle Q(\Delta\gamma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Big<\|\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p\,trial}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|-\Delta\gamma\,\big[2\mu+H^{\mathrm{kin}}_{\mu}(\alpha)\big]\Big>_{+}

(57)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}A_{\varphi}\Big(\tr\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p\,trial}}-3\sqrt{6}\,\Delta\gamma\,A_{\theta}\big[K+H^{\mathrm{kin}}_{K}(\alpha)\big]\Big).

This involves replacing equation (
54
) in equation (
17
). To determine whether
\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}
lies on the smooth region or at the apex of the yield surface, the following procedure is applied:

1.

If
Q\big({\|\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p\,trial}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|}/{\big[2\mu+H^{\mathrm{kin}}_{\mu}(\alpha)\big]}\big)<0
, then
\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}
lies on the smooth part of the yield surface. The solution is then given by

\begin{cases}\Delta\gamma=\dfrac{\|\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p\,trial}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|+\sqrt{2/3}\,A_{\varphi}\tr\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p\,trial}}}{2\mu+H^{\mathrm{kin}}_{\mu}(\alpha)+6A_{\varphi}A_{\theta}\big[K+H^{\mathrm{kin}}_{K}(\alpha)\big]},\\
\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}=\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}_{n}+\Delta\gamma\,(\hat{\bm{n}}_{\mathrm{dev}}^{\mathrm{trial}}+\sqrt{2/3}\,A_{\theta}\bm{1}),\\
\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}=\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p\,trial}}-\Delta\gamma\,\big[2\mu+H^{\mathrm{kin}}_{\mu}(\alpha)\big]\hat{\bm{n}}_{\mathrm{dev}}^{\mathrm{trial}}-\sqrt{6}\,\Delta\gamma\,A_{\theta}\big[K+H^{\mathrm{kin}}_{K}(\alpha)\big]\bm{1}.\end{cases}

(58)

The stress tensor is updated as

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{trial}}-\Delta\gamma\,\bm{\mathsf{C}}:(\hat{\bm{n}}_{\mathrm{dev}}^{\mathrm{trial}}+\sqrt{2/3}\,A_{\theta}\bm{1})

(59)

\displaystyle=\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{trial}}-\Delta\gamma\,\big(2\mu\,\hat{\bm{n}}_{\mathrm{dev}}^{\mathrm{trial}}+\sqrt{6}\,KA_{\theta}\bm{1}\big),

and the consistent tangent becomes

\displaystyle\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{ep}}=\frac{\partial\bm{\sigma}}{\partial\bm{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathsf{C}}-\big(2\mu\,\hat{\bm{n}}_{\mathrm{dev}}^{\mathrm{trial}}+\sqrt{6}\,KA_{\theta}\bm{1}\big)\otimes\frac{\partial(\Delta\gamma)}{\partial\bm{\varepsilon}}-2\mu\,\Delta\gamma\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{n}}_{\mathrm{dev}}^{\mathrm{trial}}}{\partial\bm{\varepsilon}}

(60)

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathsf{C}}-\frac{\big(2\mu\,\hat{\bm{n}}_{\mathrm{dev}}^{\mathrm{trial}}+\sqrt{6}\,KA_{\theta}\bm{1}\big)\otimes\big(2\mu\,\hat{\bm{n}}_{\mathrm{dev}}^{\mathrm{trial}}+\sqrt{6}\,KA_{\varphi}\bm{1}\big)}{[2\mu+H^{\mathrm{kin}}_{\mu}(\alpha)]+6A_{\varphi}A_{\theta}\big[K+H^{\mathrm{kin}}_{K}(\alpha)\big]}-\frac{4\mu^{2}\Delta\gamma}{\|\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p\,trial}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|}\bigg(\bm{\mathsf{I}}-\frac{1}{3}\bm{1}\otimes\bm{1}-\hat{\bm{n}}_{\mathrm{dev}}^{\mathrm{trial}}\otimes\hat{\bm{n}}_{\mathrm{dev}}^{\mathrm{trial}}\bigg).

The ratcheting strain is now obtained by

\displaystyle\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}}_{n}+\Delta\gamma\,\hat{\bm{r}},

(61)

\displaystyle=\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}}_{n}+\Delta\gamma\,(\beta_{\mathrm{\mu}}\hat{\bm{r}}_{\mathrm{dev}}+\sqrt{2/3}\,\beta_{\mathrm{K}}A_{\theta}),\hskip 10.00002pt\hat{\bm{r}}_{\mathrm{dev}}=\dfrac{\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}}_{\mathrm{dev}}}{\|\bm{s}^{\mathrm{r}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|}=\dfrac{\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{dev}}}{\|\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|}.

2.

If
Q\big({\|\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p\,trial}}_{\mathrm{dev}}\|}/{\big[2\mu+H^{\mathrm{kin}}_{\mu}(\alpha)\big]}\big)\geq 0
, then
\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}
lies at the apex of the cone, corresponding to open microcracks with no frictional sliding. The stress tensor is given by

\bm{\sigma}=\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{dam}}(\alpha):\bm{\varepsilon},

(62)

and the consistent tangent is

\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{ep}}=\frac{\partial\bm{\sigma}}{\partial\bm{\varepsilon}}=\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{dam}}(\alpha).

(63)

At each iteration, these expressions are used to compute
\bm{\sigma}
and
\bm{\mathsf{C}}^{\mathrm{ep}}

4.3
Damage evolution problem

The equilibrium and plastic evolution problems are solved in the previous steps to obtain the displacements
\bm{u}
and plastic strains
\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}
. The subsequent step in the staggered solution approach is to address the following variational inequality to obtain
\alpha

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\bigg(-s^{\mathrm{d}}(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}},\alpha)\,\tilde{\alpha}+h(\mathcal{F})\frac{{G}_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}_{n})}{{\ell}}\big(\alpha\,\tilde{\alpha}+{\ell}^{2}\nabla\alpha\cdot\nabla\tilde{\alpha}\big)+\partial I_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(\Delta\alpha)\,\tilde{\alpha}\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\ni 0\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\,\tilde{\alpha}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}).

(64)

The incremental expression of the irreversibility condition is given by

\Delta\alpha\geq 0.

(65)

The indicator function is employed in the aforementioned equation to ensure that the damage variable remains irreversible. The subdifferential of the indicator function is defined as

\partial I_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(\Delta\alpha)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }\Delta\alpha>0,\\
\mathbb{R}_{-},&\text{if }\Delta\alpha=0,\\
\varnothing,&\text{if }\Delta\alpha<0.\end{cases}

(66)

The damage driving force is calculated assuming that the opening/closure state (equation (
13
)), has already been determined through the solution of the plastic evolution problem described in section
4.2
. This force is evaluated based on
\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}\big(\bm{\varepsilon}^{(j)},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}(j)},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}(j)},\alpha^{(j-1)}\big)
, where
j
refers to the current iteration counter. To rewrite the equation (
15
) in its volumetric and deviatoric parts, first
\bm{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathrm{kin}^{\prime}}(\alpha)
is derived as:

\bm{\mathsf{H}}^{\mathrm{kin}^{\prime}}(\alpha)=\frac{g^{\prime}(\alpha)\bm{\mathsf{C}}}{\big(1-g(\alpha)\big)^{2}}.

(67)

Using equations (
4
) and (
9
), together with degradation functions (
5
), (
6
), and (
10
), the damage driving force (
15

\displaystyle s^{\mathrm{d}}(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}},\alpha)=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{2}g_{K}^{\prime}(\alpha)K\,(\tr\bm{\varepsilon})^{2}-g_{\mu}^{\prime}(\alpha)\mu\,\bm{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{dev}}:\bm{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{dev}}&\quad\text{if }\tr{\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}}\big(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}},\alpha^{(j-1)}\big)=0,\\

(68)

To ensure that the irreversibility condition and equation (
64

approach is employed, which is originally proposed by Miehe et al.

. Therefore, the maximum time history values of the individual terms in the crack driving force are employed as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{K\mathrm{I}}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\max_{s\in[0,t]}\frac{1}{2}K\big[\tr\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{x},s)\big]^{2},

(69)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mu\mathrm{I}}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\max_{s\in[0,t]}\mu\,\bm{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{dev}}(\bm{x},s):\bm{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{dev}}(\bm{x},s),

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{K\mathrm{II}}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\max_{s\in[0,t]}\frac{1}{2}K\big[\tr\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}(\bm{x},s)\big]^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mu\mathrm{II}}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\max_{s\in[0,t]}\mu\,\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dev}}(\bm{x},s):\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dev}}(\bm{x},s),

and

\displaystyle s^{\mathrm{d\,hist}}(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}},\alpha)\coloneqq\begin{cases}-g_{K}^{\prime}(\alpha)\mathcal{H}_{K\mathrm{I}}-g_{\mu}^{\prime}(\alpha)\mathcal{H}_{\mu\mathrm{I}}&\quad\text{if }\tr{\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}}\big(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{r}},\alpha^{(j-1)}\big)=0,\\

(70)

Subsequently, equation (

\int_{\Omega}\bigg(-s^{\mathrm{d\,hist}}(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}},\alpha)\,\tilde{\alpha}+h(\mathcal{F})\frac{{G}_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}_{n})}{{\ell}}\big(\alpha\,\tilde{\alpha}+{\ell}^{2}\nabla\alpha\cdot\nabla\tilde{\alpha}\big)\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=0\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\,\tilde{\alpha}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}).

(71)

Finally, a standard Newton-Raphson scheme is used to handle the non-linear degradation functions in the crack driving force (
70

71

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\bigg[\bigg(-\frac{\partial s^{\mathrm{d\,hist}}(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{p}},\alpha^{(k)})}{\partial\alpha^{(k)}}+h(\mathcal{F})\frac{{G}_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}_{n})}{{\ell}}\bigg)\big({\alpha}^{(k+1)}-{\alpha}^{(k)}\big)\tilde{\alpha}+{\ell}\,h(\mathcal{F}){G}_{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{s}^{\mathrm{p}}_{n})\nabla\big[{\alpha}^{(k+1)}-{\alpha}^{(k)}\big]\cdot\nabla\tilde{\alpha}\bigg]\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

(72)

Sarem et al. 2025

6
Conclusions

This paper presented an extension of the micromechanics-based variational phase-field model of fatigue to incorporate cyclic plasticity with ratcheting in quasi-brittle materials. The ratcheting mechanism was introduced through the evolution of ratcheting strain, which accumulates over loading cycles and captures the inelastic strain growth characteristic of cyclic plasticity. This mechanism is particularly important for low-cycle fatigue, where the accumulation of inelastic strains plays an important role in the progression to final failure. The non-associative ratcheting formulation allows the use of two independent parameters to control the deviatoric and volumetric components of ratcheting, offering additional modeling flexibility.

A series of numerical simulations was conducted to evaluate the model under monotonic and cyclic loading. First, a three-point bending test was simulated to validate the model against experimental data. The same setup was then revisited under cyclic loading to assess the fatigue response. To investigate high-cycle loading, a single element was analyzed and the corresponding
S-N
curve was obtained. The role of ratcheting was examined through material point simulations under various cyclic loading scenarios. In strain-controlled compression, the model reproduced stress relaxation, while in stress-controlled compression it captured ratcheting behavior. Additional simulations under tension–compression cycles further demonstrated the ability of the framework to simultaneously capture fatigue damage and cyclic plasticity. As a final example, a perforated specimen subjected to force-controlled tension–compression cycles was analyzed, with the model successfully capturing the evolution of localized damage and inelastic strain around stress concentration zones.
