Improving engagement with preconsult patient-reported outcome measures: A mixed-methods pilot study
Authors: Chen He, Ying Shan Cheung, Jia Yi Chua, Qasrina Lim, Ellie Choi
Published: 2026

To the Editor: Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are tools completed by patients to directly report symptoms and quality of life, supporting patient-centered care.1,2 Their clinical value, however, is limited by patient noncompletion.3,4 This mixed-method study evaluated simple messaging-focused strategies to improve preconsult PROMs completion and explored broader implementational barriers in an outpatient dermatology setting.

Within each clinic list, dermatology outpatients were individually randomized to either an intervention arm testing a specific messaging strategy or a control arm using a standard template. PROM completion rates were compared between groups and supplemented by qualitative interviews and observations. This process was repeated across 6 separate comparisons evaluating different strategies, enabling independent pairwise estimation of each strategy’s effect while avoiding clinic-level clustering (Supplementary Methods, available via Mendeley at https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/cs389xczz8/3).

The control strategy involved sending the study questionnaire weblink via WhatsApp 2 days before the appointment to the patient’s registered phone number.

Three hundred seventy-one patients were recruited between June and September 2025 (Table I). PROMs completion rates varied across strategies, with the highest in the reminder arm (57.1%) and lowest in the SMS arm (19.4%), while control rates ranged from 17.1% to 53.8% (Table I). Completion was higher among younger patients and lower among review patients (Multivariable logistic regression, Supplementary Table II, available via Mendeley at https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/cs389xczz8/3), possibly reflecting greater digital familiarity among younger patients, and greater caution toward an unexpected questionnaire among review patients.

One hundred thirty-two patients were interviewed for qualitative feedback (Table II). Key facilitators included English proficiency, digital literacy, and the credibility of the messaging source. Barriers included irrelevance of questions, the perception that PROMs served research rather than clinical purposes, and workflow mismatches, such as caregivers receiving messages meant for patients and appointment lists not reflecting last-minute changes.

This study suggests that simple, low-resource strategies such as reminders and personalization may modestly enhance patient engagement in PROMs as 1 component of a broader implementation strategy. Additional strategies include providing feedback on how responses are used, leveraging social norms, and framing noncompletion as missed benefits (loss aversion).5 These strategies are particularly practical at the clinician/clinic level, complementing equally important system-level efforts to address structural barriers such as workflow, electronic medical record integration, digital access, and clinician engagement. Limitations include the small sample size in each strategy arm, which limited statistical testing but allowed a broader descriptive evaluation across more strategies.
