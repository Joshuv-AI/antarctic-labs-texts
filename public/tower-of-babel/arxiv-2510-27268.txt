Information geometry of perturbed gradient flow systems on hypergraphs: A perspective towards nonequilibrium physics
Dimitri Loutchko, Keisuke Sugie, Tetsuya J Kobayashi

Abstract
This article serves to concisely review the link between gradient flow systems on hypergraphs and information geometry which has been established within the last five years. Gradient flow systems describe a wealth of physical phenomena and provide powerful analytical technquies which are based on the variational energy-dissipation principle. Modern nonequilbrium physics has complemented this classical principle with thermodynamic uncertaintly relations, speed limits, entropy production rate decompositions, and many more. In this article, we formulate these modern principles within the framework of perturbed gradient flow systems on hypergraphs. In particular, we discuss the geometry induced by the Bregman divergence, the physical implications of dual foliations, as well as the corresponding infinitesimal Riemannian geometry for gradient flow systems. Through the geometrical perspective, we are naturally led to new concepts such as moduli spaces for perturbed gradient flow systems and thermodynamical area which is crucial for understanding speed limits. We hope to encourage the readers working in either of the two fields to further expand on and foster the interaction between the two fields.

2.1
Basics of gradient flow systems

A gradient flow system is given by an evolution equation with an additional variational structure.
In its basic form, the evolution equation for the state
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbf{X}
of the system with the state space
\mathbf{X}
(which can be discrete, continuous, or a mixture of both) is given by

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathcal{R}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},-\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x})).

(2.1)

Here,
\mathcal{E}:\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the
driving functional
such as the system energy or entropy, and
\mathcal{R}^{*}:\mathcal{T}^{*}\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the
dissipation potential
on the cotangent bundle with coordinates
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\mu})\in\mathcal{T}^{*}\mathbf{X}
.
The operators
\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
denote derivatives with respect to
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}
.
This gradient system is denoted by the triple
(\mathbf{X},\mathcal{R}^{*},\mathcal{E})
.

This structure has a clear physical meaning:
The term
-\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x})
corresponds to a thermodynamic force, and the operation
\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathcal{R}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},.)
converts this force to a velocity.
This is a generalization of the Onsager relations
Onsager 1931
which constitute a linear relation between forces and velocities, and are recovered when
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\mu})
is quadratic in the second argument
Mielke 2003
;
Mielke 2005
;
Mielke 2011
;
Mielke and
Roubíček 2015
.
Analogous to the Onsager relations,
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\mu})
and its Legendre transform in the second argument
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})
are required to satisfy

1.

\mathcal{R}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},.)
and
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x},.)
are strictly convex in the second argument,

2.

\mathcal{R}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},.)
and
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x},.)
are 1-coercive,

3.

\mathcal{R}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},.)
and
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x},.)
are even in the second argument,

4.

\min\mathcal{R}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},.)=\min\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x},.)=0
, and
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{0})=\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{0})=0

for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbf{X}
.
The Young-Fenchel relation reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})+\mathcal{R}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\mu})\geq\langle\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{\mu}\rangle

(2.2)

with equality iff
\boldsymbol{v}=\mathrm{D}_{\mu}\mathcal{R}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\mu})
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})
hold.
A central quantity of a gradient flow system is the
dissipation rate

\sigma

1

The dissipation rate

In particular, the concepts discussed in Section

\displaystyle\sigma(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v}):=\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})+\mathcal{R}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},-\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x})).

(2.3)

The dissipation rate is lower bounded by
\langle\boldsymbol{v},-\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x})\rangle
according to (

is the gradient flow vector field, then equality holds and the dissipation rate becomes

Together with the non-negativity of the dissipation functions, this implies that

Globally, the

\Sigma[.]
is defined for a differentiable curve
\boldsymbol{x}:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbf{X}

\displaystyle\Sigma[\boldsymbol{x}(.)]:=\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x},\dot{\boldsymbol{x}})+\mathcal{R}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},-\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x}))\mathrm{d}t.

(2.4)

Following the discussion of the dissipation rate, one verifies the energy-dissipation relation

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x}(T))+\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{x},\dot{\boldsymbol{x}})+\mathcal{R}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},-\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x}))\mathrm{d}t\geq\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x}(0))

with the equality if and only if
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}
and

are Legendre dual, i.e., if and only if the gradient flow equation (
2.1
) holds true.
This equivalence is known as De Giorgi’s energy-dissipation principle (EDP).

In many applications, the state space
\mathbf{X}
is associated to an underlying discrete structure.
For example, it can be the space of positive measures on the vertices of a hypergraph.
In such cases, it is more natural to consider the dynamics being generated by forces which live on the edges of the hypergraph rather than in the cotangent space of
\mathbf{X}
.
This approach is now formalized.

2.2
Gradient flow systems on hypergraphs

The gradient flow system
(\mathbf{X},\mathcal{R}^{*},\mathcal{E})
can be generalized to take into account an underlying structure of a directed weighted hypergraph.
Such systems are known as gradient flow systems in the continuity-equation format according to
Peletier and
Schlichting 2023

;
Renger 2018

A finite

Assuming that

The divergence and gradient operators give bundle morphisms

A dissipation function on a hypergraph is a function
\Psi^{*}:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which satisfies the properties

It induces the Legendre duality between forces and fluxes as
\boldsymbol{j}=\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{f}}\Psi^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{f})

is the Legendre transform of

with respect to the second argument.
Analogously to

A gradient flow system on a hypergraph is the tuple
(\mathbf{X},\Psi^{*},\mathcal{E},\mathsf{H})
with the dynamics given by the continuity equation

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{div}_{\mathsf{H}}\boldsymbol{j},

(2.7)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{j}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{f}}\Psi^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},\mathrm{grad}_{\mathsf{H}}[-\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x})]).

(2.8)

The discussion regarding the gradient flow system

remains valid in this context:

\displaystyle\textrm{The pair $\Psi$ and $\Psi^{*}$ satisfies the inequality }\Psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})+\Psi^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{f})\geq\langle\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{f}\rangle.

(2.9)

\displaystyle\textrm{The dissipation rate is given by }\sigma(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}):=\Psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})+\Psi^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},\mathrm{grad}_{\mathsf{H}}[-\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x})])

(2.10)

The energy-dissipation principle holds.

(2.11)

.

Note that, if the evolution equations (

Again,

We also remark that in the physics literature it is customary to compute the dissipation rate via the bilinear product

for a Legendre dual pair

The definition (

The advantage of moving to the hypergraph setting is the possibility to describe perturbations which often arise on the edges of the hypergraph in physical contexts and which cannot be captured by

2.3
Perturbed gradient flow systems on hypergraphs and their energetics

We describe a nonequilibrium system by a gradient flow system on a hypergraph
(\mathbf{X},\Psi^{*},\mathcal{E},\mathsf{H})
where the driving force in Equation (
2.7
) is perturbed by an
\boldsymbol{x}
-dependent (and possibly time-dependent) external force
\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{ext}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathsf{E}}
.
In other words, the perturbation is given by a (possibly time-dependent) continuous section
\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{ext}}:\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}
.
This results in the perturbed evolution equation

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{div}_{\mathsf{H}}\boldsymbol{j},

(2.12)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{j}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{f}}\Psi^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},\mathrm{grad}_{\mathsf{H}}[-\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x})]+\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{ext}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}).

(2.13)

This perturbed gradient flow system is denoted by the quintuple
(\mathbf{X},\Psi^{*},\mathcal{E},\mathsf{H},\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{ext}})
.
For any
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})\in\mathcal{J}
, the dissipation rate now acquires an additional contribution
\sigma^{\textrm{ext}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})=\langle\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{ext}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle
due to the external force:

\displaystyle\sigma(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})

\displaystyle=\Psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})+\Psi^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},\mathrm{grad}_{\mathsf{H}}[-\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x})]+\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{ext}}_{\boldsymbol{x}})

(2.14)

\displaystyle\geq\langle\boldsymbol{j},\mathrm{grad}_{\mathsf{H}}[-\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x})]+\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{ext}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle=-\dot{\mathcal{E}}(\boldsymbol{x})+\sigma^{\textrm{ext}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}),

(2.15)

where the inequality becomes an equality iff (
2.13
) is satisfied and the last equality holds true iff (
2.12
) is satisfied.
For the dynamics of the system, this is a drastic modification as
\mathcal{E}
is no longer a Lyapunov function and phenomena such as oscillations and multistability can occur even when
\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{ext}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
is time- and

-independent.
The energy-dissipation balance for a differentiable curve
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}):[0,T]\rightarrow\mathcal{J}
with
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=-\mathrm{div}_{\mathsf{H}}\boldsymbol{j}
also acquires a perturbation in the form of the external dissipation
\Sigma^{\textrm{ext}}[(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})(.)]:=\int_{0}^{T}\sigma^{\textrm{ext}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})\mathrm{d}t
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x}(T))+\Sigma[(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})(.)]\geq\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x}(0))+\Sigma^{\textrm{ext}}[(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})(.)],

(2.16)

where the system’s dissipation is
\Sigma[(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})(.)]=\int_{0}^{T}\Psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})+\Psi^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},\mathrm{grad}_{\mathsf{H}}[-\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x})]+\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{ext}}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\mathrm{d}t
.
The equality holds if and only if
\boldsymbol{j}
satisfies the Legendre duality (

).
We refer to

3
Information geometric aspects of gradient flow systems

The setup discussed so far lends itself naturally to an information geometrical description.
We establish the basic information geometric notions of the Bregman divergence, the associated Riemannian metric structure, and dual foliations, and comment on their physical relevance.

The information geometry takes place over a fixed point
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbf{X}
, within the fibers
\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
which we treat as a pair of dually flat affine spaces (in the coordinates
\boldsymbol{j}
and
\boldsymbol{f}
, respectively) equipped with the strictly convex functions
\Psi(\boldsymbol{x},.)
and
\Psi^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},.)
and the natural bilinear pairing
\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\times\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
between dual vector spaces.
The Legendre duality between
\Psi(\boldsymbol{x},.)
and
\Psi^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},.)
induces a pair of mutually inverse diffeomorphisms
\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\Psi(\boldsymbol{x},.):\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{f}}\Psi^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},.):\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\rightarrow\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
between the fibers.

3.1
The Bregman divergence and large deviations theory

The Bregman divergence between two points
\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}[\boldsymbol{j}\|\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}]:=\Psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})-\Psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime})-\langle\boldsymbol{j}-\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime},\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\Psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime})\rangle.

(3.1)

The Bregman divergence satisfies
\mathcal{D}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}[\boldsymbol{j}\|\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}]\geq 0
and
\mathcal{D}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}[\boldsymbol{j}\|\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}]=0
iff
\boldsymbol{j}=\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}
.
However, it is not symmetric in the two arguments, and therefore it is often referred to as pseudo-distance.
Analogously, for
\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
, one defines
\mathcal{D}^{\mathcal{F}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}[\boldsymbol{f}\|\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}]:=\Psi^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{f})-\Psi^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime})-\langle\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{f}}\Psi^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}),\boldsymbol{f}-\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}\rangle
. For a Legendre dual pair
\boldsymbol{f}=\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\Psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})
and
\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}=\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\Psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime})
, one verifies

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}[\boldsymbol{j}\|\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}]=\mathcal{D}^{\mathcal{F}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}[\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}\|\boldsymbol{f}].

(3.2)

Moreover, the mixed representation

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{x}}[\boldsymbol{j}\|\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}]:

\displaystyle=\Psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})+\Psi^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime})-\langle\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}\rangle

(3.3)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}[\boldsymbol{j}\|\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}].

for
\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}=\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\Psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime})
is convenient to use.
This representation shows that the Bregman divergence quantifies the failure of the Young-Fenchel relation (
2.9
) to be an equality and has an immediate physical meaning through its relation to the energetic quantities of the gradient flow system as one computes just like in (
2.15

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{x}}[\boldsymbol{j}\|\mathrm{grad}_{\mathsf{H}}[-\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x})]+\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{ext}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}]=\sigma(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})+\dot{\mathcal{E}}(\boldsymbol{x})-\sigma^{\textrm{ext}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}).

Furthermore, the Bregman divergence (

3.2
Riemannian metric structure and covariance

Continuing with this probabilistic interpretation of the Bregman divergence (
3.4
) as a rate function, it is a standard fact in large deviations theory that the inverse covariance matrix of the flux vector
\boldsymbol{j}
is given by the Hessian of the rate function, which can be explicitly computed as

\displaystyle[\mathrm{Cov}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{j})]^{-1}=\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{x}}[\boldsymbol{j}\|\mathrm{grad}_{\mathsf{H}}[-\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x})]+\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{ext}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}]}{\partial\boldsymbol{j}\partial\boldsymbol{j}}=\frac{\partial^{2}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})}{\partial\boldsymbol{j}\partial\boldsymbol{j}}=\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})}{\partial\boldsymbol{j}\partial\boldsymbol{j}}.

(3.6)

We recognize this as the natural Riemannian metric on the flux space
\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
, known as the Fisher metric, and detone it by
\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}}:\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\times\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
The Legendre duality between
\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
, induces an isometry with respect to the metric
\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{F}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{f}}:\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{f}}\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\times\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{f}}\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
on
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
, which is given by the Hessian

\displaystyle\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{F}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{f}}:=\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{f})}{\partial\boldsymbol{f}\partial\boldsymbol{f}}=\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})}{\partial\boldsymbol{j}\partial\boldsymbol{j}}\right]^{-1}=\mathrm{Cov}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{j})

(3.7)

in the
\boldsymbol{f}
-coordinates.
Note that this Riemannian metric structure can be thought of as an infinitesimal version of the pseudo-distance function provided by the Bregman divergence due to the relation
\mathcal{D}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}[\boldsymbol{j}\|\boldsymbol{j}+\delta\boldsymbol{j}]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r,r^{\prime}=1}^{|\mathsf{E}|}[\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}}]_{rr^{\prime}}\delta j_{r}\delta j_{r^{\prime}}+\mathcal{O}(\|\delta\boldsymbol{j}]\|^{3}).

3.3
Dual foliations and moduli for perturbed gradient flow systems

The combination of the hypergraph structure and gradient flow structure imposes natural foliations of

and
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}

Here, we focus on the foliation of

The two linear subspaces

This geometrical setup can be used to construct the moduli space for the perturbed gradient flow system (
2.12
)-(
2.13
) as follows:
First, we require the following definitions.

Definition 3.1
.

For a given gradient flow system on a hypergraph
(\mathbf{X},\Psi^{*},\mathcal{E},\mathsf{H})
, two continuous perturbation fields
\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{ext}},\overline{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\textrm{ext}}:\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}
are equivalent if and only if

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{b}}\left(\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{ext}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)=\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{b}}\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\textrm{ext}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)

(3.11)

for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbf{X}
.
In other words, if and only if the perturbations
\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{ext}}
and
\overline{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\textrm{ext}}
differ by a section
\boldsymbol{h}_{\boldsymbol{x}}:=\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{ext}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}-\overline{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\textrm{ext}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
, which can be written as a discrete gradient, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{h}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathrm{img}[\mathrm{grad}_{\mathsf{H}}]
.

Remark 3.1
.

This definition of equivalence is rather coarse as we do not require the existence of a potential
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}:\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
\boldsymbol{h}_{\boldsymbol{x}}=\mathrm{grad}_{\mathsf{H}}[-\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\boldsymbol{x})]
.
This additional requirement leads to finer moduli problems which we do not address here.

Definition 3.2
.

A moduli space for continuous perturbation fields
\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{ext}}:\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}
of a gradient flow system
(\mathbf{X},\Psi^{*},\mathcal{E},\mathsf{H})
is a set that is in one-to-one correspondence with the equivalence classes (according to Definition
3.1
) of continuous perturbation fields.

The Definition
3.1
states that, for a given
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbf{X}
, the equivalence classes of perturbation fields
\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{ext}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
are given by the leaves of the foliation
\{\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{f})|\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime})\}
, which we call the
local
moduli space for the moduli problem
3.2
.
In particular, choosing the base space
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{0})
for the foliation and using the Legendre duality between
\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{j}=\boldsymbol{0}
, one sees that
\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{0})
, which is the linear subspace
\mathrm{ker}[\mathrm{div}_{\mathsf{H}}]\subset\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
, is a local moduli space.
This gives the solution to the (global) moduli problem:

Theorem 3.1
.

The moduli space for continuous perturbation fields
\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{ext}}:\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}
of a gradient flow system
(\mathbf{X},\Psi^{*},\mathcal{E},\mathsf{H})
is given by the set of continuous sections
\{s:\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}\}
, where
\mathcal{K}
denotes the subbundle of
\mathcal{J}
with the fibers
\mathcal{K}_{\boldsymbol{x}}=\mathrm{ker}[\mathrm{div}_{\mathsf{H}}]\subset\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

This is a generalized reformulation of the Schnakenberg theory for the decomposition of nonequilibrium forces at steady states into cycle components
Schnakenberg 1976
.
Note that the classical Schnakenberg theory treats the case of
\boldsymbol{x}
-independent perturbation fields
\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{ext}}
where one obtains one fiber
\mathcal{K}_{\boldsymbol{x}}=\mathrm{ker}[\mathrm{div}_{\mathsf{H}}]
as the corresponding moduli space.

\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}

\boldsymbol{j}^{\textrm{b}}=\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{0})\cap\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{j})
.
Note that one can also define directly
\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{b}}:=\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{0})\cap\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{f})
to get the Legendre dual of
\boldsymbol{j}^{\textrm{b}}
, and analogously for
\boldsymbol{j}^{\textrm{c}}:=\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{j})\cap\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{0})
.

We briefly discuss the interpretation of information geometrical decomposition
4.5
for the case that the gradient flow equations (
2.12
)-(
2.13
) hold true.
If the system is unperturbed, then
\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{f})=\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{0})
and
\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{c}}=\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{0})\cap\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{0})=\boldsymbol{0}
hold.
This yields
\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{b}}=\boldsymbol{f}
and, by Legendre duality,
\boldsymbol{j}^{\textrm{b}}=\boldsymbol{j}
.
Thus, only the excess dissipation rate survives and we have
\sigma(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})=\sigma^{\textrm{ex}}_{\textrm{IG}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})
for the unperturbed case.

At a steady state
\boldsymbol{x}^{\textrm{ss}}
, which is defined by
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{ss}}=\boldsymbol{0}
, the continuity equation
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=-\mathrm{div}_{\mathsf{H}}\boldsymbol{j}
implies that
\boldsymbol{j}\in\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{0})
and thus
\boldsymbol{j}^{\textrm{b}}=\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{0})\cap\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{0})=\boldsymbol{0}
.
Moreover, the Legendre duality between
\boldsymbol{j}
and
\boldsymbol{f}
yields
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{0})
and therefore
\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{c}}=\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{f})\cap\mathcal{F}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{0})=\boldsymbol{f}
.
This shows that the excess dissipation rate
\sigma^{\textrm{ex}}_{\textrm{IG}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\textrm{ss}},\boldsymbol{j})
vanishes and we obtain
\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}^{\textrm{ss}},\boldsymbol{j})=\sigma^{\textrm{hk}}_{\textrm{IG}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\textrm{ss}},\boldsymbol{j})
.

Hence, for a perturbed gradient flow system, the excess term generalizes the dissipation away from equilibrium to the dissipation away from the steady state, and relates it to the boundary components of fluxes and forces.
The housekeeping term quantifies the dissipation due to the nonequilibrium nature of the system, i.e., due to the presence of the perturbation term, and relates it to the cycle components.

4.2
Riemmanian geometry of thermodynamic uncertainty relations and speed limits

In this section, we present a coherent information geometrical toolbox to deal with thermodynamic uncertaintly relations (TURs) and thermodynamic speed limits.

4.2.1
Thermodynamic uncertainty relations

Thermodynamic uncertaintly relations have been extensively studied in the physics literature for over a decade, and various formulations have been obtained based on explicit models
Barato and
Seifert 2015
;
Horowitz and
Gingrich 2020
;
Koyuk and
Seifert 2022
;
Kamijima
et al. 2023
;
Ray et al. 2023
.
Throughout this section, we assume that the gradient flow equations (
2.12
)-(
2.13
) hold true, set
\boldsymbol{f}=\mathrm{grad}_{\mathsf{H}}[-\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x})]+\boldsymbol{f}^{\textrm{ext}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
, and denote the corresponding flux by
\boldsymbol{j}:=\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{f}}\Psi^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})
.

The main idea of a TUR is to bound the relative precision
\mathfrak{p}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{j})
of a flux, defined as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{j}):=\boldsymbol{j}^{\mathrm{t}}[\mathrm{Cov}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{j})]^{-1}\boldsymbol{j}=\sum_{r,r^{\prime}=1}^{|\mathsf{E}|}\frac{\partial^{2}\Psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})}{\partial j_{r}\partial j_{r^{\prime}}}j_{r}j_{r^{\prime}}=\|\boldsymbol{j}\|_{\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}}}^{2},

(4.6)

through the dissipation rate
\sigma(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})
.
Here, using the notation from Section
3.2
we denote by
\|.\|_{\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}}}
the norm associated to the inner product
\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}}
on
\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
5
5

5

Note that this notation requires to treat
\boldsymbol{j}
as an element of the tangent space
\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
by making the canonical identification
\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\simeq\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
of a vector space with its tangent space at a point via
e_{r}\mapsto\partial_{r}
.
The same is required in the integral representation of the dissipation rate in formula (
4.9
).

.
By using the symmetrized Bregman divergence

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\mathcal{J},\textrm{sym}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}[\boldsymbol{j}\|\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}]:=\frac{1}{2}\left[\mathcal{D}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}[\boldsymbol{j}\|\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}]+\mathcal{D}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}[\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}\|\boldsymbol{j}]\right]

(4.7)

one can represent the dissipation rate, following (
2.14
), via

\sigma(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})=\Psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})+\Psi^{*}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{f})=2\mathcal{D}^{\mathcal{J},\textrm{sym}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}[\boldsymbol{j}\|\boldsymbol{0}].

(4.8)

Then, using the integral representation of
\mathcal{D}^{\mathcal{J},\textrm{sym}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}[\boldsymbol{j}\|\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}]
from
Amari 2016
, Theorem 4.2.,
6
6

6

The representation is given by

\mathcal{D}^{\mathcal{J},\textrm{sym}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}[\boldsymbol{j}\|\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}]=\int_{0}^{1}\|\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\tau)\|^{2}_{\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\gamma}(\tau)}}\mathrm{d}\tau,

for the curve
\boldsymbol{\gamma}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
defined by
\boldsymbol{\gamma}(\tau)=\tau\boldsymbol{j}+(1-\tau)\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}
.

one obtains the integral representation of the dissipation rate via

\displaystyle\sigma(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})=2\int_{0}^{1}\|\boldsymbol{j}\|^{2}_{\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\tau\boldsymbol{j}}}\mathrm{d}\tau

(4.9)

With the definition (
4.6
), the formula (
4.9
) gives the following general result on TUR:

Lemma 4.1
.

If
\|\boldsymbol{j}\|_{\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\tau\boldsymbol{j}}}\geq\|\boldsymbol{j}\|_{\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}}}
holds for all
\tau\in[0,1]
, then the relative precision of the flux is bounded by the dissipation rate as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})\geq\mathfrak{p}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{j}).

(4.10)

In Section
4.2.3
, we show how this implies generalizations of some known TURs.
Note that the condition
\|\boldsymbol{j}\|_{\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\tau\boldsymbol{j}}}\geq\|\boldsymbol{j}\|_{\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}}}
for
\tau\in[0,1]
holds true for chemical reaction networks and Markov jump processes, as shown in Appendix
6
.
The same argument also goes through more generally for
\cosh
-type gradient flow systems
Peletier and
Schlichting 2023
.
We leave it as an open question to establish this condition under more general assumptions.

4.2.2
Speed limits

In addition to the TUR, the integral representation (
4.9
) allows to obatain a clean geometrization of thermodynamic speed limits.
Speed limits relate the time
T
to transition between two points
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{T}
to the dissipation and have been extensively studied in the physics literature
Vo et al. 2020
;
Yoshimura and
Ito 2021
;
Nagayama et al. 2025
.

Let
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}_{\boldsymbol{x}}):[0,T]\rightarrow\mathcal{J}
be a curve which obeys the gradient flow equations (
2.12
)-(
2.13
).
Recall from Section
2.3
that the total dissipation is given by
\Sigma[(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}_{\boldsymbol{x}})(.)]=\int_{0}^{T}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\mathrm{d}t
which yields, via the formula (
4.9
):

\displaystyle\Sigma[(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}_{\boldsymbol{x}})(.)]=2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{1}\|\boldsymbol{j}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|^{2}_{\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\tau\boldsymbol{j}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}}\mathrm{d}\tau\mathrm{d}T.

(4.11)

By using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality one obtains

\displaystyle\Sigma[(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}_{\boldsymbol{x}})(.)]\geq\frac{2}{T}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{1}\|\boldsymbol{j}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\tau\boldsymbol{j}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}}\mathrm{d}\tau\mathrm{d}T\right]^{2}.

(4.12)

Then, by defining the
thermodynamic area

\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}:\boldsymbol{x}_{T}}
between two points
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{T}
via

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}:\boldsymbol{x}_{T}}:=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}_{\boldsymbol{x}}):[0,T]\rightarrow\mathcal{J}\\
\boldsymbol{x}(0)=\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\\
\boldsymbol{x}(T)=\boldsymbol{x}_{T}\\
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=-\mathrm{div}_{\mathsf{H}}\boldsymbol{j}\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{1}\|\boldsymbol{j}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\tau\boldsymbol{j}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}}\mathrm{d}\tau\mathrm{d}T,

(4.13)

we obtain the information geometric speed limit:

\displaystyle T\geq\frac{2\mathcal{A}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}:\boldsymbol{x}_{T}}^{2}}{\Sigma[(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}_{\boldsymbol{x}})(.)]}.

(4.14)

The speed limit relates the transition time between
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{T}
to the inverse dissipation via the thermodynamic area.
This area term is mathematically similar to the concept of thermodynamic length
Salamon and
Berry 1983
;
Crooks 2007
;
Sivak and
Crooks 2012
;
Mandal and
Jarzynski 2016
;
Loutchko et al. 2022
.
However, the area describes the dissipation in a genuine nonequilibirum situation whereas the length treats slowly driven equilibrium systems.

4.2.3
Infinitesimal decompositions

We end this subsection by combining the discussion of information geometric dissipation rate decompositions presented in Section
4.1
with TURs and speed limits which are best treated with the infinitesimal methods of Riemannian geometry presented in this subsection.

The relative precision of the flux vector
\boldsymbol{j}
splits into the relative precision of the velocity
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=-\mathrm{div}_{\mathsf{H}}\boldsymbol{j}
and the one of the cycle flux component
\boldsymbol{j}^{\textrm{c}}\in\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{j})
as follows:
For any flux vector
\boldsymbol{j}\in\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
, one verifies that the spaces
\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{j})
and
\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{j})
intersect
\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}}
-orthogonally at
\boldsymbol{j}
, i.e., that the tangent spaces
\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{j})
and
\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{j})
are orthogonoal with respect to the inner product on
\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
defined by the Riemannian metric
\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}}
.
Moreover, the tangent spaces
\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{j})
and
\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{j})
span the whole space
\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
.
We write

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}=\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{j})\oplus_{\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}}}\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{j})

(4.15)

for this decomposition.
Denote the respective
\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}}
-orthogonal projections
\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\mathcal{J}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
onto the first factor by
\pi_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{b}}
and onto the second factor by
\pi_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{c}}
, respectively.
One verifies that the boundary component of the flux is the velocity, i.e.,

\displaystyle\pi_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{j})=\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}

(4.16)

and that its orthogonal complement
\boldsymbol{j}^{\textrm{c}}:=\pi_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{j})
lies in
\mathrm{ker}[\mathrm{div}_{\mathsf{H}}]
and is thus a cycle component.
The orthogonal decomposition (
4.15
) leads to the decomposition of the relative precision as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{j})=\|\boldsymbol{j}\|_{\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}}}^{2}=\underbrace{\|\pi_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{j})\|_{\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}}}^{2}}_{\mathfrak{p}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{j}^{\textrm{c}})}+\underbrace{\|\pi_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{j})\|_{\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j}}}^{2}}_{\mathfrak{p}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\dot{\boldsymbol{x}})}

(4.17)

where
\mathfrak{p}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\dot{\boldsymbol{x}})
is the relative precision of the velocity and
\mathfrak{p}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{j}^{\textrm{c}})
is the relative precision of the cycle component
\boldsymbol{j}^{\textrm{c}}
.
Then, combininig this with Lemma
4.1
(which requires the gradient flow system to satisfy the assumption of the lemma) yields the inequality
\frac{1}{2}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})\geq\mathfrak{p}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\dot{\boldsymbol{x}})
which sharpens and generalizes the TUR derived in
Yoshimura and
Ito 2021
, as well as the inequality
\frac{1}{2}\sigma(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})\geq\mathfrak{p}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{j}^{\textrm{c}})
which recovers the TUR from
Polettini et al. 2022
.

Finally, we remark that the decomposition (
4.17
) can be used in the integral formula (
4.9
) to obtain a Riemannian geometric variation of the dissipation rate decomposition (
4.5
) as

\displaystyle\sigma(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})=\underbrace{2\int_{0}^{1}\|\pi_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{c}}(\boldsymbol{j})\|^{2}_{\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\tau\boldsymbol{j}}}\mathrm{d}\tau}_{\sigma^{\textrm{hk}}_{\textrm{RG}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})}+\underbrace{2\int_{0}^{1}\|\pi_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\textrm{b}}(\boldsymbol{j})\|^{2}_{\mathsf{g}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\boldsymbol{x},\tau\boldsymbol{j}}}\mathrm{d}\tau}_{\sigma^{\textrm{ex}}_{\textrm{RG}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})}

(4.18)

As discussed in last paragraph of Section
4.1
, the term
\sigma^{\textrm{hk}}_{\textrm{RG}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})
captures the dissipation due the relaxation dynamics whereas the term
\sigma^{\textrm{ex}}_{\textrm{RG}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})
is related to the nonequilibrium nature of the system due to the perturbation.
One also can tighten the TURs above to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{\textrm{ex}}_{\textrm{RG}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})

\displaystyle\geq\mathfrak{p}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\dot{\boldsymbol{x}})

(4.19)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{\textrm{hk}}_{\textrm{RG}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{j})

\displaystyle\geq\mathfrak{p}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{j}^{\textrm{c}}).

(4.20)

Note that by summing both the inequalities (
4.19
) and (
4.20
) one recovers the original TUR (
4.10
).
From the geometrical point of view, according to (
4.15
) this is the only possible orthogonal splitting of the TUR because any other decomposition - when applied to the integral expression (
4.9
) would necessarily introduce mixture terms.

5
Future perspective

In this article, we have shown how the gradient flow system formalism naturally can be transferred to an information geometrical setting:
The Bregman divergence is tightly connected to the dissipation, the Fisher metric to covarience, and the dual orthogonal foliations provide natural cycle-boundary decompositions and moduli spaces.
We have then shown how to use the geometrical formulation to derive dissipation rate decompositions, TURs, and speed limits.
It is worth stressing that these modern pricinples discovered in nonequilibrium physics are derived without any specific physical but only require the gradient flow structures.
As such, the results presented here make such principles applicable to any system which can be described by a gradient flow evolution equation.
In particuar, it will be interesting to discuss such principles within the gradient flow formulation of machine learning
Liero et al. 2025
.

This work should be thought of as first foray into the information geometry of gradient flow systems, and we hope to stimulate further developments.
We briefly mention some possible directions:

We have extensively used the finite dimensionality of the state space and of the vector bundles on that space.
The information geometry on infinite dimensional state spaces is well-developed
Ay et al. 2017
, and gradient flow systems on infinite dimensional state space are well-known.
Albeit the merging of these two formalisms is technically demanding, it will be interesting to develop this theory.

There are coordinate-free formulations of both gradient-flow systems
Mielke 2025
and of information geometry
Amari and Nagaoka 2000
, and it will be rewarding to formulate the nonequilibrium priciples in this setting.
Connected to this is the study of dissipation potentials which are not strictly convex, as the corresponding information geomery for singular models has been recently developed
Nakajima and
Ohmoto 2021
.

Most importantly, we have not touched the bread and butter of gradient flow systems which are thermodynamically consistent and mathematically rigorous limit procedures (discrete to continous, time-scale separations).
Such limits should have nice geometrical conterparts, and should allow to study the nonequilbrium physical principles in such situations.
