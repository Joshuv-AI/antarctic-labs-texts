An explicit construction of heat kernels and Green’s functions in measure spaces
Palle Jorgensen and Jay Jorgenson and Lejla Smajlović

Abstract
. We explicitly construct a heat kernel as a Neumann series for certain function spaces, such as L 1 L^{1} , L 2 L^{2} , and Hilbert spaces, associated to a locally compact Hausdorff space 𝔛 \mathfrak{X} with Borel σ \sigma -algebra ℬ \mathcal{B} , and endowed with additional measure-theoretic data. Our approach is an adaptation of classical work due to Minakshishundaram and Pleijel, and it requires as input a parametrix or small time approximation to the heat kernel. The methodology developed in this article applies to yield new instances of heat kernel constructions, including normalized Laplacians on finite and infinite graphs as well as Hilbert spaces with reproducing kernels.

†
†

Keywords: heat kernels, Neumann series, generalized time convolution, parametrix

†

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Imagining the heat kernel in the physical world

From its inception, a certain class of heat kernels
K(x,y;t)
consists of mathematical objects which are rooted in the physical world. For example,
one can imagine introducing a unit burst of heat at a point
x
in a domain
M
at time zero, after which one then can measure
the proportion of heat which has diffused to the point
y
after time
t
. This amount of heat is denoted by
K(x,y;t)
.
Equivalently, one can envision a particle which undertakes a random walk on
M
beginning at
x
at time zero, so then

K(x,y;t)
is interpreted as the probability that the particle is at the point
y
at time
t
.

Without exaggeration, there
are hundreds of mathematics articles which study the heat kernel in various settings for
M
, such as Euclidean domains,
Riemannian manifolds, projective Kähler varieties, symmetric spaces, and so on, and with some articles considering different possibilities
for time, including positive real time, positive integer time, or arbitrary time-scales; see
[
8
]
. In our opinion, it is valid to
keep the physical or probabilistic language when describing properties of a heat kernel
K(x,y;t)
in the case when
M

is modeled locally on a Euclidean domain since this means that in one way or another one can draw
M
, hence one can imagine diffusion or a random walk.

1.2.
A general point of view

Beyond a physical or probabilistic setting, there is an extensive body of literature which considers heat kernels more generally,
such as when
M
is replaced by a locally compact Hausdorff space
\mathfrak{X}
with a Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}
of subsets
and possessing a
\sigma
-finite measure
\lambda
on
(\mathfrak{X},\mathcal{B})
.
We refer the reader to the particularly eloquent article

[
13
]
where the author defines and studies a heat kernel in a metric measure space; see, specifically, Definition 2.1 on page 146 of
[
13
]
.
With various assumptions, it is shown in
[
13
]
that a heat kernel can give rise to a Markov process for which the heat kernel is
the associated transition density; see loc. cit., page 147.
In this regard, the heat kernel retains its connection to probability theory.
We also note that the heat kernel in these instances has an associated
semigroup of operators, and a heat operator is obtained by considering the infinitesimal generator of
this semigroup. In essence, the infinitesimal generator is a type of Laplacian operator.

Our main goal in the present article is to explicitly construct a heat kernel as an integral kernel
in certain measure space settings, similar to those considered in
[
13
]
, with positive real time;
see Theorems
6.3
,
6.6
and

6.9
.
In effect,
we assume that, somehow, we have obtained a parametrix or small time approximation for the heat kernel
as an integral kernel. Given this approximation, the heat kernel
itself is computed using a convergent Neumann series which is defined using the parametrix. As is commonplace, the
parametrix itself has reasonably few requirements, so in many instances our construction yields an easily attainable result.

The parametrix approach we present is modeled on the classical method from the
Riemannian setting
as given the seminal paper
[
33
]
, which itself attributes some of the main ideas to Carleman; see loc. cit., page 243.
In the recent articles
[
10
]
and
[
25
]
the authors developed the parametrix approach for constructing heat kernels in
the setting of finite and certain infinite graphs, respectively. Again, there is considerable flexibility in choosing a parametrix.
For example, it is shown in
[
10
]
and
[
25
]
that different choices of a parametrix lead to different identities, including
expressions for the classical
I
-Bessel function. With that in mind, one can say that the parametrix construction of the
heat kernel has been shown to be quite robust.

There are numerous studies in the literature which study heat kernels in metric measure spaces; see, for example
[
13
]
and
references therein. As far as we can see, the present article is the first which explores an explicit construction of a heat
kernel starting with a parametrix in such settings.

stochastic processes, directly related to (i) and (ii). Our discussion below of these themes will be supplemented by background references.
While the general literature is vast, we, of course, will focus on the details that are tailored to our needs. These ideas are connected to recent papers
by P. Jorgensen (first named author) and his (other) co-authors, especially the following:

In the various settings one then arrives at a natural choice of a measure

We are then able to obtain global results where the time variable enters at the outset and, therefore, yields a more direct solution to the particular diffusion equations for the setting at hand. 1.4.
Organization of the paper.

In section 2 we define the setup for our study and set notation. In section 3
we define the heat kernel in our context. Though this component of our paper is similar to considerations by other authors, we want to carefully state
the minimal number of conditions needed to proceed. In section 4 we define the convolution to be employed and prove its basic properties
in the setting of
L^{p}
spaces; section 5 consists of an analogous study for separable Hilbert spaces. It is important to note that
in each instance the notation of convergence means, in general terms, with respect to the norm of the given space. In section 6 we define
a parametrix in each context we study, and prove the existence of a heat kernel through explicit construction in terms of a convergent Neumann series.

Thus, existence of the heat kernel in each context follows from existence of the corresponding parametrix.

In section 7 we discuss the important question as to how one can construct a parametrix itself and set the stage for further studies of this question in specific settings. In section 8 we show how other properties of the heat kernel follow from our analysis,
and perhaps additional assumptions which, again, need to be viewed on a case-by-case basis.
In section 9 we describe how one can proceed from the heat kernel to other important
considerations, such as Green’s
function, resistance measures, wave kernels and, in some cases, a pre-trace formula. We finish with section 10 where we discuss examples of parametrix for graphs and reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces and offer some concluding remarks.

2.
Our setup

Let
\mathfrak{X}
denote a locally compact Hausdorff space, and
\mathcal{B}
a Borel
\sigma
-algebra of subsets of
\mathfrak{X}
.
Let
\lambda
denote a
\sigma
-finite measure on
(\mathfrak{X},\mathcal{B})
. Let

\mathcal{B}_{\rm fin}:=\{A\in\mathcal{B}:\lambda(A)<\infty\}

denote the subalgebra of sets of finite
\lambda
measure with associated function spaces

\mathcal{D}_{\rm fin}=\text{span}\{\chi_{A}:A\in\mathcal{B}_{\rm fin}\}\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm and}\,\,\,\,\,\mathcal{D}_{\rm fin}^{\dagger}=\text{span}\{\chi_{A}:A\in\mathcal{B}_{\rm fin}\cup\{\mathfrak{X}\}\},

where
\chi_{A}
is the indicator function of
A\in\mathcal{B}
.
If
\lambda(\mathfrak{X})<\infty
, then
\mathcal{D}_{\rm fin}=\mathcal{D}_{\rm fin}^{\dagger}
, otherwise we have
that
\mathcal{D}_{\rm fin}=\mathcal{D}_{\rm fin}^{\dagger}\cup\{\chi_{\mathfrak{X}}\}
.
We note that any
\sigma
-finite measure
\lambda
is uniquely determined by its values on
\mathcal{B}_{\rm fin}
.

2.1.
Transfer operators and conductance

As described in
[
15
]
, one defines a transfer operator
R
on

(\mathfrak{X},\mathcal{B},\lambda)
as an operator on
\mathcal{D}_{\rm fin}^{\dagger}
which satisfies
the following two conditions.

(1)

The operator
R
is symmetric, meaning that

\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}(Rf)(x)g(x)\lambda(dx)=\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}f(x)(Rg)(x)\lambda(dx)\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm for all}\,\,\,\,\,f,g\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm fin}^{\dagger}.

(2)

The operator
R
is semi-positive, which we also call non-negative, meaning that for all
f\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm fin}^{\dagger}
we have
that
Rf\geq 0
whenever
f\geq 0
.

Associated to any transfer operator
R
, there exists a measure
\rho

on
(\mathfrak{X}^{2},\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B})
such that
\lambda=\rho\circ\pi_{i}^{-1}
, where
\pi_{i}
is projection of
\mathfrak{X}^{2}

onto the variable
i
, where
i
equals
1
or
2
, and for which
\rho_{x}:=\rho(x,\cdot)
is the kernel for
R
. In other words,

(Rf)(x)=\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}f(y)\rho_{x}(dy)

for all
f\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{fin}}
.
The measure
\rho
is often called the
conductance
, and it is a symmetric measure on
(\mathfrak{X}^{2},\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B})
, meaning that

\rho(A\times B)=\rho(B\times A)\,\,\,\,\,\text{for all $A,B\in\mathcal{B}$.}

Moreover,
\rho
is a positive measure and

0\leq\rho_{x}(\mathfrak{X})<\infty\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm for all $x\in\mathfrak{X}$.}

Finally, we recall from
[
6
]
that a positive transfer operator
R
is symmetric if and only if it defines a symmetric measure
\rho
by

\rho(A\times B)=\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}\chi_{A}(x)R(\chi_{B})(x)\lambda(dx).

Remark 1
.

If
X
is a discrete space whose elements are viewed as vertices of a weighted graph, then the measure

\rho
are edge weights. In this instance, an example of a transfer operator
R
is the operator determined by the
associated adjacency matrix.

Remark 2
.

A different approach to defining a transfer operator, stemming from the properties of the symmetric measures, is described in
[
6
]
.
To do so, start with a
\sigma
-finite symmetric measure on
(\mathfrak{X}^{2},\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B})
called
\tilde{\rho}
and set
\lambda:=\tilde{\rho}\circ\pi_{1}^{-1}
. Then there exists a unique system of conditional
\sigma
-finite measures
\tilde{\rho}_{x}

for each
x\in\mathfrak{X}
such that

\iint\limits_{\mathfrak{X}^{2}}f(x,y)\tilde{\rho}(dx,dy)=\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}\left(\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}f(x,y)\tilde{\rho}_{x}(dy)\right)\lambda(dx),

for any
\tilde{\rho}
-integrable function
f
. With this set-up, the operator
\tilde{R}
defined on
\mathcal{D}_{\rm fin}^{\dagger}
by

\tilde{R}(g)(x)=\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}g(y)\tilde{\rho}_{x}(dy)

is symmetric and positive. As such,
\tilde{R}
is a transfer operator on
(\mathfrak{X},\mathcal{B},\lambda)
.

2.2.
The Laplacian

With the above notation, for a given transfer operator
R
let us define a degree function
c(x)
associated to
R
by

(2.1)

c(x)=R(\chi_{\mathfrak{X}})(x)=\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}\rho_{x}(dy)=\rho_{x}(\mathfrak{X})<\infty.

In line with the discussion of Remark
1
, the degree function can be viewed as the analogue of
the vertex degree of a finite graph.

Also, as discussed in Remark
2
, one can define a degree function associated to a symmetric measure
\tilde{\rho}
as the degree function associated to the
transfer operator
\tilde{R}
generated by
\tilde{\rho}
.

Going forward, we
assume that the degree function
x\mapsto c(x)=\rho_{x}(\mathfrak{X})
satisfies the following conditions.

Assumption C.
For
\lambda
-almost every
x\in\mathfrak{X}
, we have that

0<c(x)<\infty.

Additionally we assume, that
c\in L_{1}^{\rm loc}(\lambda)
; specifically, it is assumed that

\int\limits_{A}c(x)\lambda(dx)<\infty\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm for all}\,\,\,\,\,A\in\mathcal{B}_{\rm fin}.

Definition 2.1
.

The Laplacian on
(\mathfrak{X},\mathcal{B},\lambda)
is the closure of the symmetric operator

\Delta:=(c-R)

on the domain
\mathcal{D}_{\rm fin}^{\dagger}
. In other words, the Laplacian
\Delta f
of
f
is
defined as

\Delta f(x)=\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}(f(x)-f(y))\rho_{x}(dy)\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm for any $f\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm fin}^{\dagger}$.}

Remark 3
.

Given that
c(x)>0
for
\lambda
-almost every
x\in\mathfrak{X}
,
one can also define a
\lambda
-almost every analogue of the probabilistic/normalized Laplacian
\tilde{\Delta}
by

\tilde{\Delta}:=\frac{1}{c}\Delta=I-\frac{1}{c}{R},

so then

\tilde{\Delta}f(x)=\frac{1}{c(x)}\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}(f(x)-f(y))\rho_{x}(dy).

The normalized Laplacian can be written in terms of the Markov operator
P

P(x,dy):=\frac{1}{c(x)}\rho_{x}(dy),

where the measure
c^{-1}(x)\rho_{x}(dy)

is simply the probability measure obtained by normalizing of
\rho_{x}(dy)
to have unit mass. Explicitly, we can write that

(Pf)(x):=\frac{1}{c(x)}(Rf)(x)=\frac{1}{c(x)}\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}f(y)\rho_{x}(dy)=\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}f(y)P(x,dy).

With this notation, we have that

\tilde{\Delta}f=(I-P)f

on the domain
\mathcal{D}_{\rm fin}^{\dagger}
.

2.3.
Hilbert spaces and measure spaces of interest

Note that under Assumption C, one can define another measure
\nu
on the measurable space
(\mathfrak{X},\mathcal{B})
by

(2.2)

\nu(A):=\int_{A}c(x)\lambda(dx)=\int_{A}\int_{\mathfrak{X}}\rho_{x}(dy)\lambda(dx)=\rho(A\times\mathfrak{X}).

The measure spaces
L^{2}(\lambda)
as well as
L^{2}(\nu)
will be studied going forward.
Additionally, we will consider so-called energy space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{E}}
which is defined as follows.

Definition 2.2
.

For any
f,g\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm fin}^{\dagger}
, define the energy inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{E}}
by

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{E}}:=\frac{1}{2}\iint\limits_{\mathfrak{X}^{2}}(f(x)-f(y))(g(x)-g(y))d\rho(x,y).

The associated norm is
||f||_{\mathcal{E}}=\sqrt{\langle f,f\rangle_{\mathcal{E}}}
. With this,
the energy space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{E}}
is defined as

\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{E}}:=\{f\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm fin}^{\dagger}:||f||_{\mathcal{E}}<\infty\}.

We will use the generic notation
\mathcal{H}
to denote a Hilbert space, including any one of
the Hilbert spaces
L^{2}(\lambda)
,
L^{2}(\nu)
or
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{E}}
.

Going forward, we will need the following lemma on differentiating an inner product in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with respect to a parameter.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
f:\mathfrak{X}\times(0,\infty)
be a function such that: (i) for any
t\in(0,\infty)
, function
x\mapsto f(x,t)
belongs to
\mathcal{H}
, and (ii) for any
x\in\mathfrak{X}
, function
t\mapsto f(x,t)
is differentiable. Further assume that

\partial_{t}f(x,t)\in\mathcal{H}\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm for all $x\in\mathfrak{X}$.}

Then

\partial_{t}\langle f(x,t),g(x)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle\partial_{t}f(x,t),g(x)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm for all $g\in\mathcal{H}.$}

Proof.

The statement follows by applying the Schwarz inequality for the inner product on
\mathcal{H}
.
∎

A portion of our discussion below will consider the space
L^{1}(\lambda)
and
its subspace
L^{1}(\lambda)\cap L^{\infty}(\lambda)
. In this setting, and more generally,
it is immediate, by applying the classical Hölder inequality, that one can state and prove an
L^{p}
version of Lemma
2.3
, which we state below.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
p,q\geq 1
be real numbers such that
1/p+1/q=1
, with the usual convention that

q=\infty
if
p=1
and vice versa. Assume
f:\mathfrak{X}\times(0,\infty)
is a function with the following properties: (i) for any
t\in(0,\infty)
, function
x\mapsto f(x,t)
belongs to
L^{p}(\lambda)
, and (ii) for any
x\in\mathfrak{X}
, function
t\mapsto f(x,t)
is differentiable. Further assume that

\partial_{t}f(x,t)\in L^{p}(\lambda)\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm for all $x\in\mathfrak{X}$.}

Then

\partial_{t}\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}f(x,t)g(x)\lambda(dx)=\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}\partial_{t}f(x,t)g(x)\lambda(dx)\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm for all $g\in L^{q}(\lambda).$}

2.4.
Properties of the Laplacians

In general terms, the Laplacians we study are semi-positive and (essentially) self-adjoint. Let us discuss briefly
where these results are shown to be true in the various spaces we consider.
Throughout our paper, following
[
5
]
, we will pose an
additional assumption on the symmetric measure
\rho
on
(\mathfrak{X}^{2},\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B})
, which is the following.

Assumption E.
For every set
A\in\mathcal{B}_{\rm fin}
, the function

x\mapsto\rho_{x}(A)=\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}\chi_{A}(y)\rho_{x}(dy)

belongs to
L^{1}(\lambda)\cap L^{2}(\lambda)
.

Under this assumption, it is proved in Sections 7 and 8 of
[
5
]
that the Laplacian
\Delta
introduced in Definition
2.1
is a positive definite
and essentially self-adjoint in the
Hilbert space
L^{2}(\lambda)
. Moreover,
\Delta
acting in
L^{2}(\lambda)
is bounded if and only if

c\in L^{\infty}(\lambda)
, where function
c
is defined by (
2.1
).
According to
[
15
, Lemma 3.5]
, the Markov operator
P
is self-adjoint on
L^{2}(\nu)
, hence so is the normalized Laplacian
\tilde{\Delta}=I-P
.

When viewed as an operator on the energy space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{E}}
, the operator
\Delta
is a positive definite and symmetric operator, but in general it is not
self-adjoint. However,
\Delta
does admit a self-adjoint extension; see Theorem 8.5 of
[
5
]
. We refer an interested reader to

[
6
, Theorem 2.14]
where further properties of operators
R
and
P
are summarized. Moreover, the Laplacian, when viewed as an operator
in the energy Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{E}}
is semi-bounded on its natural dense domain; hence, by Friedrichs’ theorem,
\Delta
has a canonical semi-bounded
extension with the same lower bound as
\Delta
on
\mathcal{D}_{\rm fin}^{\dagger}
.

The Laplacians in the
L^{2}
spaces
L^{2}(\lambda)
and
L^{2}(\nu)
(where the measure
\nu
is defined by (
2.2
)) are automatically essentially self-adjoint; for details,
see, for example,
[
14
,
15
,
22
]
.

3.
Heat kernel on
L^{p}
spaces and Hilbert spaces

We will now define a heat kernel in the various spaces under consideration. Further properties of
the heat kernel will be established in a later section with further assumptions as needed.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mu
be a measure on the space
(\mathfrak{X},\mathcal{B})
.
Let
p\geq 1
be a real number and let
q
be such that
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
, where as usual for
p=1
we set
q=\infty
and vice versa.
The
L^{p}
-heat kernel associated to the Laplacian
\Delta
is a function
K:\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
with the following properties.

(1)

For all
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
, the function
K(x,y;\cdot):[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is differentiable in
t\in(0,\infty)
, continuous at
t=0

and such that
K(\cdot,y;t):\mathfrak{X}\to\mathbb{R}\in L^{p}(\mu)
for all
y\in\mathfrak{X}
and
t>0
.

(2)

The function
K(\cdot,y;\cdot):\mathfrak{X}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is a solution to the equation
(\partial_{t}+\Delta)K(x,y;t)=0

for all
(x,t)\in\mathfrak{X}\times(0,\infty)
, fixed
y\in\mathfrak{X}
,
\mu
-almost everywhere, and where
\Delta
is assumed to act on the variable
x
.

(3)

For all
f\in L^{q}(\mu)
, we have that

(3.1)

\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}K(x,y;t)f(y)\mu(dy)=f(x)\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm$\mu$-almost everywhere $x\in\mathfrak{X}$.}

Note that the Laplacian depends upon a measure
\lambda
on the space
(\mathfrak{X},\mathcal{B})
,
which is not necessarily equal to the measure
\mu
in the above definition.

In an analogous way it is possible to define the heat kernel on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space of functions which has
\mathfrak{X}
as a domain. The heat kernel on
\mathcal{H}
associated to the Laplacian
\Delta
is a function
K:\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
with the following properties.

(1)

For all
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
,
K(x,y;\cdot):[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is differentiable on
(0,\infty)
, continuous at
t=0
and such that
K(\cdot,y;t)\in\mathcal{H}
for all
y\in\mathfrak{X}
,
t>0
.

(2)

The function
K(\cdot,y;\cdot):\mathfrak{X}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is a solution to the equation

(\partial_{t}+\Delta)K(x,y;t)=0
for all
t>0
, where
\Delta
acts on
x
, for fixed
y\in\mathfrak{X}
.

(3)

For all
f\in\mathcal{H}
, we have that

(3.2)

\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\langle K(x,\,\cdot;t),f(\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=f(x),\quad x\in\mathfrak{X},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
denotes the inner product on
\mathcal{H}
.

Remark 4
.

If
\mathcal{H}
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, then one can replace (
3.2
)
with the statement that

(3.3)

\langle K(x,\,\cdot;0),f(\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=f(x)\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm for all $x\in\mathfrak{X}$}

and for all
f\in\mathcal{H}
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
denotes the inner product on
\mathcal{H}
.
In other words,
K(x,y;0)
is the reproducing kernel for the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.

Remark 5
.

In
[
13
]
the author defines a heat kernel on a measure space which includes a number of additional
properties which are assumed to hold; see also
[
12
]
. The additional properties assumed in
[
13
]
and
[
12
]
, and
elsewhere, include well-known features from geometry and probability such as positivity and symmetric of the heat kernel as
well as a semi-group property. The development we give in this paper is to use the above assumptions to prove the
existence of the heat kernel through an explicit means, assuming one has a parametrix,
after which we examine the circumstances under which the additional
assumptions from
[
13
]
and
[
12
]
become properties of the heat kernel.

Remark 6
.

The definition of the heat kernel is an adaptation from the setting of manifolds to the situation of
L^{p}
or Hilbert spaces;
however, there is an important technical difference.
Namely, if
M
is a manifold with associated volume element
\omega
, then the heat kernel
K_{M}
is assumed
to be continuous and integrable in the two space variables, smooth in the time variable on
(0,\infty)
and such that

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\int\limits_{M}K_{M}(x,y;t)f(y)\omega(dy)=f(x),

for all bounded, continuous functions on
M
. In the setting of a Hilbert space, not all bounded and continuous
functions belong to this space; the same is true for the space
L^{q}(\mu)
.

Remark 7
.

The definition of the
L^{p}
-probabilistic heat kernel and the Hilbert space probabilistic heat kernel associated to the
probabilistic/normalized Laplacian
\tilde{\Delta}
is the same as above, with the only difference being that one needs
to take the Laplacian
\tilde{\Delta}
instead of
\Delta
in the second condition. In the sequel we will work mainly with

\Delta
. However, it is straightforward to see that all our findings will hold true with
\Delta
replaced by
\tilde{\Delta}

and
L^{2}(\mu)
replaced by
L^{2}(\nu)
.

4.
Generalized time convolution

In classical analysis, convolution of functions is used in different contexts. Usually, the convolution of two functions on some space is integral of a product of a function and its shift, or the integral of a product of a function with a certain kernel. It is very useful, because, loosely speaking, it can help smoothen the function, meaning that convolution produces a new function on the underlying space which possesses somewhat nicer properties.

In our setup we are interested in constructing a heat kernel which is a fundamental solution for the heat equation and describes diffusion over time. In other words, the heat kernel, introduced in Definitions
3.1
and
3.2
, depends on two space variables and a time variable.
As such, the convolution of functions depending on two space variables and one time variable is defined as a composition of the ”classical” convolution in the space variable, followed by a convolution in the time variable.

In order to construct the heat kernel,
we need the notion of somewhat more generalized time convolution, or a time-space convolution,
which is adopted to the setting of general measurable or Hilbert spaces.

4.1.
Generalized time convolution on measurable spaces

Recall that throughout this section
\mu
is a measure on the space
\mathfrak{X},\mathcal{B}
.

Definition 4.1
.

Time convolution on

L^{p}

space
.
Assume the following.

•

The functions
F_{1},F_{2}:\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
(or
\mathbb{C}
) are such
that
F_{1}(x,\cdot;t)\in L^{p}(\mathfrak{X},\mu)
and
F_{2}(\cdot,y;t)\in L^{q}(\mathfrak{X},\mu)
for all
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
,
t\geq 0
and
p,q\in[1,\infty]
with
1/p+1/q=1
.

•

The functions
F_{1}(x,y;\cdot)
and
F_{2}(x,y;\cdot)
are integrable on the sets
[0,b)
, for all
b>0
and
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
.

Then we define the convolution of
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
as

(4.1)

(F_{1}\ast F_{2})(x,y;t):=\int\limits_{0}^{t}\left(\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}F_{1}(x,z;t-\tau)F_{2}(z,y;\tau)\mu(dz)\right)d\tau.

Trivially, by combining the Fubini-Tonelli theorem with the Hölder inequality we get that

\displaystyle(F_{1}\ast F_{2})(x,y;t)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{t}\left(\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}F_{1}(x,z;\tau)F_{2}(z,y;t-\tau)\mu(dz)\right)d\tau

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}\left(\int\limits_{0}^{t}F_{1}(x,z;\tau)F_{2}(z,y;t-\tau)d\tau\right)\mu(dz).

Remark 8
.

It is straightforward to see that (
4.1
) generalizes the definition of the convolution for
infinite, edge-weighted and vertex-weighted graphs as given on page 8 of
[
25
]
.

Let us now deduce some properties of the generalized convolution which will be needed for the construction of the heat kernel.
For
p\in[1,\infty]
we denote by
||\cdot||_{p,\mu}
the norm in the space
L^{p}(\mu)=L^{p}(\mathfrak{X},\mathcal{B},\mu)
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
F_{1},F_{2}:\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}

be as in Definition
4.1
. For some
t_{0}>0
, assume there exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}
and integers
k,\ell\geq 0

such that for all
0<t<t_{0}
and all
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
, we have that

\left\|F_{1}(x,\cdot;t)\right\|_{p,\mu}\leq C_{1}h(x)t^{k}\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm and}\,\,\,\,\,\left\|F_{2}(\cdot,y;t)\right\|_{q,\mu}\leq C_{2}t^{\ell},

for some function
h
of
x\in\mathfrak{X}
.
Then for all
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}

|(F_{1}\ast F_{2})(x,y;t)|\leq C_{1}C_{2}h(x)\frac{k!\ell!}{(k+\ell+1)!}t^{k+\ell+1}\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm for $0<t<t_{0}$.}

Proof.

From the Hölder inequality, we can write that

\displaystyle|(F_{1}\ast F_{2})(x,y;t)|

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{0}^{t}\left\|F_{1}(x,\cdot;t-r)\right\|_{p,\mu}\left\|F_{2}(\cdot,y;r)\right\|_{q,\mu}dr

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}C_{2}h(x)\int_{0}^{t}r^{k}(t-r)^{\ell}\,dr=C_{1}C_{2}h(x)\frac{k!\ell!t^{k+\ell+1}}{(k+\ell+1)!},

as claimed.
∎

Let
f=f(x,y;t):\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function with the following properties. For any
T>0
, all
t\in(0,T]
,
and all arbitrary but
\mu
almost all fixed
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
, the functions
f(\cdot,y;t):\mathfrak{X}\to\mathbb{R}
and
f(x,\cdot;t):\mathfrak{X}\to\mathbb{R}

belong to
L^{p}(\mu)\cap L^{q}(\mu)
. Also, assume that, with the same quantification, the function
f(x,y;\cdot):(0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
is integrable.
For any positive integer
\ell
and any such function
f
, we can inductively define the
\ell
-fold
convolution
(f)^{\ast\ell}(x,y;t)
for
t\in(0,T]
; set
(f)^{\ast 1}(x,y;t)=f(x,y;t)

and for
\ell\geq 2
let

(f)^{\ast\ell}(x,y;t):=\left(f\ast(f)^{\ast(\ell-1)}\right)(x,y;t),

under additional assumption that
(f)^{\ast(\ell-1)}(\cdot,x;t)\in L^{q}(\mu)
for all
t\in(0,T]
and all
\ell\geq 2
.

With this notation we have the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
f=f(x,y;t):\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Assume that for all
\mu
almost all
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
and all
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, the function
f(x,y;\cdot)
is integrable on

[0,t_{0}]
and
f(x,\cdot;t)\in L^{1}(\mu)
for all
t\in[0,t_{0}]
.
Assume further that for all
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
there exists a constant
C
, depending only upon
t_{0}
, and integer
k\geq 0
such that

|f(x,y;t)|\leq Ct^{k}\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm for $\mu$ almost all $x,y\in\mathfrak{X}$ and $0<t<t_{0}$.}

Then the series

(4.2)

F(x,y;t):=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}(f)^{\ast\ell}(x,y;t)

converges absolutely and uniformly on every compact subset of
\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
In addition, we have the following results:

(1)

(4.3)

\left(f\ast\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}(f)^{\ast\ell}\right)\right)(x,y;t)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}(f)^{\ast(\ell+1)}(x,y;t).

(2)

(4.4)

\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\left|(f)^{\ast\ell}(x,y;t)\right|=O(t^{k})\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm as $t\to 0$;}

where the implied constant is independent of
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
;

(3)

F(\cdot,y;t)\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
for
\mu
almost all
y\in\mathfrak{X}
,
t\in[0,t_{0}]
.

Proof.

Throughout the proof our discussion applies to
\mu
almost all
x
and
y
. Let
A
be an arbitrary compact subset of
\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
Let
t_{0}>0
be such that
A\subseteq\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}\times[0,t_{0}]
.
We apply Lemma
4.2
, with
p=\infty
,
q=1
to get that

\left|(f\ast f)(x,y;t)\right|\leq C\|f\|_{1,\mu}\frac{t^{k+1}}{(k+1)!},\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm for all $x,y\in\mathfrak{X}$ and $0<t<t_{0}$.}

Similarly, by induction for
\ell\geq 1
we have the bound that

(4.5)

\left|(f^{\ast\ell})(x,y;t)\right|\leq C\|f\|_{1,\mu}^{\ell-1}\frac{t^{k+\ell-1}}{(k+\ell-1)!},\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm for all $x,y\in\mathfrak{X}$ and $0<t<t_{0}$.}

The assertion regarding the convergence of (
4.2
) now follows
from the Weierstrass criterion and the fact that
A\subseteq\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}\times(0,t_{0}]
.

Fix
t>0
. The series (
4.2
) converges absolutely. When viewed as
a function of
x
, for any arbitrary but fixed
y
and for any
0<t<t_{0}
, the series belongs to

. Therefore,

(4.6)

\left(f\ast\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}(f)^{\ast\ell}\right)\right)(x,y;t)=\int\limits_{0}^{t}\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}f(x,z;t-r)\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}(f)^{\ast\ell}(z,y;r)\right)\mu(dz)dr.

From the bound (
4.5
), when combined with the Hölder inequality with
p=1
and
q=\infty
, we have
for an arbitrary

and

that

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}\left|f(x,z;t-r)(f)^{\ast\ell}(z,y;r)\right|\mu(dz)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\|f\|_{1,\mu}\cdot C\|f\|_{1,\mu}^{\ell-1}\frac{t^{k+\ell-1}}{(k+\ell-1)!}

\displaystyle=C\|f\|_{1,\mu}t^{k}\exp(t\|f\|_{1,\mu}).

Hence we may interchange the sum over
\ell
with the integral over
\mathfrak{X}
in (
4.6
). The above bound, combined with the Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem, also proves statement (3), upon replacing
\ell
by
\ell-1
.

By reasoning analogously, one easily shows that we may interchange the infinite sum over
\ell
with the integral from

to

to deduce that

\left(f\ast\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}(f)^{\ast\ell}\right)\right)(x,y;t)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}f(x,z;t-r)(f)^{\ast\ell}(z,y;r)\mu(z)dr,

which proves (
4.3
).

Finally, the bound (
4.5
), and the fact that the series (
4.2
) converges absolutely on
\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}\times(0,t_{0}]
, yields that

\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\left|(f)^{\ast\ell}(x,y;t)\right|\leq C\|f\|_{1,\mu}t^{k}\exp(t\|f\|_{1,\mu}),

which proves (
4.4
).
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let

Assume that for

and all

and
\mu

,

.
In addition, we have the following results:

(1)

(4.8)

\left(f\ast\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}(f)^{\ast\ell}\right)\right)(x,y;t)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}(f)^{\ast(\ell+1)}(x,y;t);

(2)

(4.9)

\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\left\|(f)^{\ast\ell}(\cdot,y;t)\right\|_{2,\mu}=O(t^{k})\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm as $t\to 0$;}

and

.

Proof.

Throughout the proof our discussion applies to
\mu
almost all

and

Let

be an arbitrary compact subset of
\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

Let

.
We apply Lemma

In view of the fact that

(f\ast f)(.,y;t)\in L^{2}(\mu)

5.
Properties of the generalized time convolution on a Hilbert space

5.1.
Generalized time convolution on a Hilbert space

The generalized time convolution on any Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
of real valued functions on
\mathfrak{X}
with the inner product

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
is given by the following definition.

Definition 5.1
.

Time convolution on a Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}
. Let
F_{1},F_{2}:\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}\times[0,\infty]
be such that
F_{1}(x,\cdot;t),F_{2}(\cdot,y;t)\in\mathcal{H}
for all
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
,
t\geq 0
and such that the inner product

\langle F_{1}(x,\cdot;\tau),F_{2}(\cdot,y;t-\tau)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

is integrable in
\tau
on sets
[0,t]
, for all
t>0
,
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
. Then, time convolution on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is defined as

(5.1)

(F_{1}\ast F_{2})(x,y;t):=\int\limits_{0}^{t}\langle F_{1}(x,\cdot;t-\tau),F_{2}(\cdot,y;\tau)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}d\tau.

In this section we state lemmas which are Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
analogues of Lemmas
4.2
and
4.4
. Throughout this section we fix an orthonormal basis
\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
,
\varphi_{j}:\mathfrak{X}\to\mathbb{R}

for the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
of real-valued functions on
\mathfrak{X}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
F_{1},F_{2}:\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}

be as in Definition
5.1
. Assume that
a_{j}(x,t):=\langle F_{1}(x,\cdot;t),\varphi_{j}(\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
is
continuous in
t
and that

\sum_{j\geq 1}a_{j}(x,t)^{2}=||F_{1}(x,\cdot,t)||_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

is also continuous in
t
for all
x\in\mathfrak{X}
. For some
t_{0}>0
, assume there exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}
and integers
k,\ell\geq 0

such that for all
0<t<t_{0}
and all
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
, we have that
a_{j}(\cdot;t)\in\mathcal{H}
, for all
j\geq 1
, and

\sum_{j\geq 1}||a_{j}(\cdot,t)||^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}\leq C_{1}t^{k}\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm and}\,\,\,\,\,\left\|F_{2}(\cdot,y;t)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq C_{2}t^{\ell}\,\,\text{\rm for all $y\in\mathfrak{X}$,}

where
||\cdot||_{\mathcal{H}}
denotes the norm induced by the inner product on
\mathcal{H}
. Then, for all
y\in\mathfrak{X}
and
0<t<t_{0}

we have that
F_{1}\ast F_{2}(\cdot,y;t)\in\mathcal{H}
and

||(F_{1}\ast F_{2})(\cdot,y;t)||_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq C_{1}C_{2}\frac{1}{(k+1)(\ell+1)}t^{k+\ell+2}\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm for $0<t<t_{0}$.}

Remark 9
.

Note that the conditions posed on
F_{1}
are equivalent to saying that it defines a Hilbert-Schmidt
type operator mapping from
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

Let

F_{2}(\cdot,y;\tau)=\sum_{j\geq 1}b_{j}(y;\tau)\varphi_{j}(\cdot)

be an expansion of
F_{2}
in terms of the basis of
\mathcal{H}
where
0<\tau<t_{0}
and
y\in\mathfrak{X}
. Then, for
0<\tau<t<t_{0}
and
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
we have that

\langle F_{1}(x,\cdot;t-\tau),F_{2}(\cdot,y;\tau)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{j\geq 1}a_{j}(x;t-\tau)b_{j}(y;\tau).

We claim that, when viewed as a function of
x\in\mathfrak{X}
the pairing
\langle F_{1}(x,\cdot;t-\tau),F_{2}(\cdot,y;\tau)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

is in
\mathcal{H}
for all
0<\tau<t<t_{0}
and
y\in\mathfrak{X}
.
To see this, choose any
j,\ell\geq 1
and let
c_{j\ell}(t-\tau):=\langle a_{j}(x;t-\tau),\varphi_{\ell}(x)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
. Then

\langle\sum_{j\geq 1}a_{j}(\cdot;t-\tau)b_{j}(y;\tau),\varphi_{\ell}(\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{j\geq 1}c_{j\ell}(t-\tau)b_{j}(y;\tau),

so then

\displaystyle||\langle F_{1}(x,\cdot;t-\tau),F_{2}(\cdot,y;\tau)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}||_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}c_{j\ell}(t-\tau)b_{j}(y;\tau)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}c_{j\ell}(t-\tau)^{2}\sum_{j\geq 1}b_{j}(y;\tau)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|F_{2}(\cdot,y;\tau)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\sum_{j\geq 1}\sum_{\ell\geq 1}c_{j\ell}(t-\tau)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|F_{2}(\cdot,y;\tau)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\sum_{j\geq 1}||a_{j}(\cdot,t-\tau)||^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}C_{2}\tau^{\ell}(t-\tau)^{k}.

This proves that
\langle F_{1}(x,\cdot;t-\tau),F_{2}(\cdot,y;\tau)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\in\mathcal{H}
.
Continuing, we claim that

(5.2)

\int_{0}^{t}\langle F_{1}(x,\cdot;t-\tau),F_{2}(\cdot,y;\tau)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}d\tau=\sum_{j\geq 1}\int_{0}^{t}a_{j}(x;t-\tau)b_{j}(y;\tau)d\tau.

Indeed, it is immediate that

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq 1}\int_{0}^{t}|a_{j}(x;t-\tau)b_{j}(y;\tau)|d\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}|a_{j}(x;t-\tau)b_{j}(y;\tau)|\right)d\tau

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}||F_{1}(x,\cdot;t)||_{\mathcal{H}}||F_{2}(\cdot,y;t)||_{\mathcal{H}}<\infty,

which justifies the interchange of the sum and integral in (
5.2
).

Let

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, followed by the Hölder inequality we get

\displaystyle|d_{\ell}(y)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{j\geq 1}|c_{j\ell}(t-\tau)b_{j}(y;\tau)|d\tau

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}c_{j\ell}(t-\tau)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}b_{j}(y;\tau)^{2}\right)^{1/2}d\tau

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}c_{j\ell}(t-\tau)^{2}\right)d\tau\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}b_{j}(y;\tau)^{2}\right)d\tau\right)^{1/2}.

Therefore

\displaystyle||(F_{1}\ast F_{2})(\cdot,y;t)||_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{0}^{t}\langle F_{1}(x,\cdot;t-\tau),F_{2}(\cdot,y;\tau)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}d\tau\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}d_{\ell}^{2}(y)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}c_{j\ell}(t-\tau)^{2}\right)d\tau\cdot C_{2}\int_{0}^{t}\tau^{\ell}d\tau

\displaystyle=C_{2}\frac{t^{\ell+1}}{\ell+1}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}||a_{j}(\cdot,t-\tau)||^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}\right)d\tau

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}C_{2}\frac{1}{(k+1)(\ell+1)}t^{k+\ell+2},

where we used that

\sum_{j\geq 1}b_{j}(y;\tau)^{2}=||F_{2}(\cdot,y;\tau)||_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq C_{2}\tau^{\ell}.

With all this, the proof of Lemma

6.
Parametrix construction of the heat kernel in three different settings

In this section we describe the construction of the heat kernel using the parametrix approach in three settings. We will first describe the construction
when the underlying space is a measurable space
(\mathfrak{X},\mathcal{B},\mu)
and the conjugate pairs
p,q
in the parametrix construction are

(1,\infty)
and then
(2,2)
, respectively. Then, we will describe the construction when the underlying space is a separable
Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.

A parametrix is a function that serves as an initial approximation of the heat kernel. It is chosen based on certain desirable properties
which are reasonably general. Often,
a parametrix is simpler to compute than the exact heat kernel. In essence, the parametrix construction provides a powerful and flexible framework
for studying heat kernels in various mathematical settings, particularly when dealing with complex geometric structures.
One then uses iterative techniques or formulas to improve the accuracy of the parametrix, gradually approaching the true heat kernel.
As such, the heat kernel can be viewed as a type of “fixed-point” through a functional-analytic process.

Lemma 6.2

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(H\ast f)(x,y;t)+\Delta_{x}(H\ast f)(x,y;t)

(6.10)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(H\ast f)(x,y;t)+\left((\Delta_{x}H)\ast f\right)(x,y;t),

where the second equation is proved analogously as (
6.2
), namely by
applying the Fubini-Tonelli theorem, which is justified by the Hölder inequality.

The rest of the proof is analogous to the proof of Lemma
6.2
, so we refer the reader to that
proof for further details.
∎

With all this, we now can state the main theorem in this subsection.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
H
be a
(2,2)
parametrix of
order
k\geq 0
for the heat operator on
\mathfrak{X}
.
Then the Neumann series (
6.5
)
converges absolutely and uniformly on every compact subset of
\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
Furthermore, the
L^{2}
-heat kernel
K_{\mathfrak{X}}
on
\mathfrak{X}

associated to graph Laplacian
\Delta_{x}
is given by (
6.6
) and the asymptotic (
6.7
) holds true.

Proof.

Set

\tilde{H}(x,y;t):=H(x,y;t)+(H\ast F)(x,y;t).

By Lemma
4.4
, the series
F(x,y;t)
defined in (
6.5
)
converges uniformly and absolutely. Moreover, according to (
4.9
), as a function of the first variable, it belongs to
L^{2}(\mu)
and has order
O(t^{k})
as
t\rightarrow 0
. Since
H
is in
L^{2}(\mu)
as a function of the first variable, this, combined with Lemma
4.2
with
p=q=2
implies that
\tilde{H}(x,y;t)
satisfies condition (1) in the definition of the
L^{2}
heat kernel.

Next, we want to show that

(6.11)

L_{x}\tilde{H}(x,y;t)=0\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm and}\,\,\,\,\,\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}\tilde{H}(x,y;0)f(y)\mu(dy)=f(x)\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm for all $x\in\mathfrak{X}$ and $f\in L^{2}(\mu)$.}

Combining Lemma
4.4
with Lemma
4.2
we get the asymptotic bound that

(H\ast F)(x,y;t)=O(t^{k+1})\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm as $t\rightarrow 0^{+}$.}

Therefore, for any
f\in L^{2}(\mu)

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathfrak{X}}\tilde{H}(x,y;t)f(y)\mu(dy)=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathfrak{X}}H(x,y;t)f(y)\mu(dy)=f(x).

It remains to prove the vanishing of
L_{x}\tilde{H}
in (
6.11
).
For this, we can apply Lemma
6.5
and proceed analogously as in the proof of Theorem
6.3
to get that

\displaystyle L_{x}\tilde{H}(x,y;t)

\displaystyle=L_{x}H(x,y;t)+L_{x}(H\ast F)(x,y;t)

\displaystyle=L_{x}H(x,y;t)+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}(L_{x}{H})^{\ast\ell}(x,y;t)+(L_{x}{H})*\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}(L_{x}{H})^{\ast\ell}\right)(x,y;t)

\displaystyle=0,

which completes the proof.
∎

6.3.
Parametrix construction of the heat kernel in a Hilbert space

In this section we describe the parametrix construction of the heat kernel on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
of functions
f:\mathfrak{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. Throughout this section the space
\mathcal{H}
is fixed. We denote by
\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
its orthonormal basis.

Definition 6.7
.

(
An
\mathcal{H}
parametrix.
)
Let
k\geq 0
be an integer. An
\mathcal{H}
parametrix

H
order
k
for the heat operator
L
on
(\mathfrak{X},\mu)
is any continuous function
H=H(x,y;t):\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}

which is smooth on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
in time variable
t
, belongs to
\mathcal{H}
in each space variable, and satisfies the following properties.

(1)

The function
L_{x}H(x,y;t)
extends to a continuous function of
t
on
[0,\infty)
, for all
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
.

(2)

For all
x\in\mathfrak{X}
,
t\geq 0
we have that
\Delta_{x}H(x,\cdot;t)\in\mathcal{H}
and for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, we have that
\partial_{t}H(x,\cdot;t)\in\mathcal{H}
.

(3)

Let

a_{j}(x;t):=\langle L_{x}H(x,\cdot;t),\varphi_{j}(\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},\,\,f(x,y;t):=\sum_{j\geq 1}a_{j}(x;t)\varphi_{j}(y),

x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
,
t>0
. Then,
a_{j}(\cdot;t)\in\mathcal{H}
, and
a_{j}(x,t)
is continuous in
t
variable for all
j\geq 1
,
x\in\mathfrak{X}
and such that
\sum_{j\geq 1}a_{j}(x,t)^{2}=||f(x,\cdot,t)||_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}
is also continuous in
t
for all
x\in\mathfrak{X}
. Moreover, for all
j\geq 1
and for any
t_{0}>0
there exists a constant
C=C(t_{0})
, depending only on

t_{0}
, such that

\sum_{j\geq 1}||a_{j}(\cdot,t)||^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}\leq C\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm and}\,\,\,\,\,\left\|f(\cdot,y;t)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq Ct^{k}.

for
t\in(0,t_{0}]
and all
y\in\mathfrak{X}
.

(4)

For all
x\in\mathfrak{X}
, and all
g\in\mathcal{H}

(6.12)

\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\langle H(x,\cdot;t),g(\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=g(x).

The construction of the heat kernel on
\mathcal{H}
begins with the following lemma.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
H
be an
\mathcal{H}
parametrix for the heat operator on
(\mathfrak{X},\mu)
of any order.
Let
f=f(x,y;t):\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}

be a continuous function in
t
for all
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
. Assume further that for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
the function

f(\cdot,y;t)
when viewed as a function on
\mathfrak{X}
, for all
y\in\mathfrak{X}
belongs to
\mathcal{H}
. Then

L_{x}(H\ast f)(x,y;t)=f(x,y;t)+(L_{x}H\ast f)(x,y;t)

for all
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

Proof.

From the assumptions, we have that
f(\cdot,y;t)\in\mathcal{H}
and
H(x,\cdot;t),\,\Delta_{x}H(x,\cdot;t)\in\mathcal{H}

for all
(x,y;t)\in\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}
are continuous in variable
t
. Therefore,
their inner product is integrable on
[0,t]
, which proves that convolutions
H\ast f
and
(\Delta_{x}H)\ast f
are well defined.
Next, we claim that

(6.13)

\Delta_{x}(H\ast f)(x,y;t)=\left((\Delta_{x}H)\ast f\right)(x,y;t).

In order to prove (
6.13
), let us write

H(x,\cdot;t)-H(z,\cdot;t)=\sum_{j\geq 1}h_{x,j}(z;t)\varphi_{j}(\cdot)\,\,\,\,\text{\rm and}\,\,\,\,f(\cdot,y;t)=\sum_{j\geq 1}g_{j}(y;t)\varphi_{j}(\cdot)

Then

\displaystyle\Delta_{x}(H\ast f)(x,y;t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathfrak{X}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left[\langle H(x,\cdot;t)-H(z,\cdot;t-\tau),f(\cdot,y;\tau)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right]d\tau\right)\rho_{x}(dz)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathfrak{X}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left[\sum_{j\geq 1}h_{x,j}(z;t-\tau)g_{j}(y;\tau)\right]d\tau\right)\rho_{x}(dz)

(6.14)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\left(\int_{\mathfrak{X}}h_{x,j}(z;t-\tau)\rho_{x}(dz)\right)g_{j}(y;\tau)\right)d\tau.

To be precise, the above computations involved an
interchange of the sum and the integral which is justified because

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq 1}\int_{\mathfrak{X}}\int_{0}^{t}

\displaystyle|h_{x,j}(z;t-\tau)g_{j}(y;\tau)|\rho_{x}(dz)d\tau=\int_{\mathfrak{X}}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}|h_{x,j}(z;t-\tau)g_{j}(y;\tau)|\right)\rho_{x}(dz)d\tau

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathfrak{X}}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}h_{x,j}(z;t-\tau)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}g_{,j}(y;\tau)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\rho_{x}(dz)d\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathfrak{X}}\int_{0}^{t}||H(x,\cdot;t-\tau)-H(z,\cdot;t-\tau)||_{\mathcal{H}}||f(\cdot,y;\tau)||_{\mathcal{H}}\rho_{x}(dz)d\tau<\infty,

where we used that the measure
\rho_{x}
is finite and all norms are bounded.

Continuing, we claim that

(6.15)

\int_{\mathfrak{X}}h_{x,j}(z;t-\tau)\rho_{x}(dz)=\langle\Delta_{x}H(x,\cdot;t-\tau),\varphi_{j}(\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

Since

(6.16)

\Delta_{x}H(x,\cdot;t-\tau)=\int_{\mathfrak{X}}\left(\sum_{\ell\geq 1}h_{x,\ell}(z;t-\tau)\varphi_{\ell}(\cdot)\right)\rho_{x}(dz),

in order to prove (
6.15
) it suffices to show that one can interchange the sum and the integral in (
6.16
).
This easily follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, namely

\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\int_{\mathfrak{X}}\left|h_{x,\ell}(z;t-\tau)\varphi_{\ell}(\cdot)\right|\rho_{x}(dz)\leq\int_{\mathfrak{X}}\left(\sum_{\ell\geq 1}h_{x,\ell}(z;t-\tau)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\varphi_{\ell}(\cdot)^{2}\right)^{1/2}<\infty.

Now, when combining (
6.14
) and (
6.15
) we arrive at

\displaystyle\Delta_{x}(H\ast f)(x,y;t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\langle\Delta_{x}H(x,\cdot;t-\tau),\varphi_{j}(\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}g_{j}(y;\tau)\right)d\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\langle\Delta_{x}H(x,\cdot;t-\tau),f(\cdot,y;\tau)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right)d\tau

\displaystyle=\left((\Delta_{x}H)\ast f\right)(x,y;t).

Therefore,

\displaystyle L_{x}(H\ast f)(x,y;t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(H\ast f)(x,y;t)+\Delta_{x}(H\ast f)(x,y;t)

(6.17)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(H\ast f)(x,y;t)+\left((\Delta_{x}H)\ast f\right)(x,y;t).

The function
\langle H(x,z;t-r),f(z,y;r)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
is continuous in the time variable.
This follows because the integrand is continue due to the Hölder inequality and the assumptions on the parametrix, namely boundedness
uniform boundedness in
r\in[0,t]
by an integrable function. So we can apply the
Leibniz integration formula. Upon doing so, we obtain that
the first term on the right hand side of (
6.17
) is equal to

(6.18)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{t-\varepsilon}\left(\langle H(x,\cdot;t-\tau),f(\cdot,y;\tau)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right)d\tau

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\left(\langle H(x,\cdot;\varepsilon),f(\cdot,y;\tau)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right)+\int\limits_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\langle H(x,\cdot;t-\tau),f(\cdot,y;\tau)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right)d\tau.

In view of assumption (4) on the parametrix
H
, we get from (
6.18
), combined with Lemma
2.3
that

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}H(x,z;t-r)f(z,y;r)\mu(dz)dr=f(x,y;t)+\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}H\ast f\right)(x,y;t).

Therefore,

\displaystyle L_{x}(H\ast f)(x,y;t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(H\ast f)(x,y;t)+(\Delta_{x}H\ast f)(x,y;t)

\displaystyle=f(x,y;t)+\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}H\ast f\right)(x,y;t)+(\Delta_{x}H\ast f)(x,y;t)

\displaystyle=f(x,y;t)+(L_{x}H\ast f)(x,y;t),

as claimed.
∎

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
H
be an
\mathcal{H}
parametrix of
order
k\geq 0
for the heat operator on
\mathfrak{X}
. For
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, let

(6.19)

F(x,y;t):=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}(L_{x}H)^{\ast\ell}(x,y;t).

Then the Neumann series (
6.19
) converges uniformly in the Hilbert space norm
||\cdot||_{\mathcal{H}}
on every compact subset of
\mathfrak{X}\times\mathfrak{X}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
Furthermore, the heat kernel
K_{\mathfrak{X}}
on
\mathcal{H}

associated to graph Laplacian
\Delta_{x}
is given by (
6.6
)
and

||(H\ast F)(x,y;t)||_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=O(t^{k+2})\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm as $t\rightarrow 0^{+}$.}

Proof.

Set

\tilde{H}(x,y;t):=H(x,y;t)+(H\ast F)(x,y;t).

By Lemma
5.3
(with
f=L_{x}H
), the series
F(x,y;t)
defined in (
6.19
)
converges in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Moreover, according to (
5.3
), as a function of the first variable, it belongs to
\mathcal{H}
and

(6.20)

||F(\cdot,y;t)||_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\left\|(L_{x}H)^{\ast\ell}(\cdot,y;t)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=O(t^{k}),

as
t\rightarrow 0^{+}
. Therefore,
\tilde{H}
satisfies assumption (1) in Definition
3.2
. Next, we want to show that

(6.21)

L_{x}\tilde{H}(x,y;t)=0\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm and}\,\,\,\,\,\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\langle H(x,\cdot;t),f(\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=f(x),\,\,\,\,\text{\rm$x\in\mathfrak{X}$ and $f\in\mathcal{H}$.}

Assumptions on the parametrix
H
together with (
6.20
) ensure that Lemma
5.2
can be applied with
F_{1}=H
and
F_{2}=F
to deduce that

\left\|H\ast F(x,y;t)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=O(t^{k+2})\,\,\,\,\,\text{\rm as $t\rightarrow 0^{+}$.}

Therefore, for any
f\in\mathcal{H}
and
x\in\mathfrak{X}

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\langle\tilde{H}(x,\cdot;t),f(\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\langle H(x,\cdot;t)f(\cdot)\rangle=f(x).

It remains to prove the vanishing of
L_{x}\tilde{H}
in (
6.21
).
For this, we can apply Lemma
6.8
to get that

\displaystyle L_{x}\tilde{H}(x,y;t)

\displaystyle=L_{x}H(x,y;t)+L_{x}(H\ast F)(x,y;t)

\displaystyle=L_{x}H(x,y;t)+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}(L_{x}{H})^{\ast\ell}(x,y;t)+(L_{x}{H})*\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}(L_{x}{H})^{\ast\ell}\right)(x,y;t)

\displaystyle=L_{x}H(x,y;t)+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}(L_{x}{H})^{\ast\ell}(x,y;t)+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}(L_{x}{H})^{\ast(\ell+1)}(x,y;t)

\displaystyle=0.

This completes the proof.
∎

7.
Construction of a parametrix

In Section
6
assuming that the corresponding parametrix exists we constructed an
L^{1}
heat kernel, an
L^{2}
heat kernel and
a Hilbert space heat kernel associated to the Laplacian
\Delta
, as defined in Section
2
.
Therefore, in order to construct the heat kernel in a given setting, it suffices to prove the existence of the corresponding parametrix.

As it turns out, the conditions in Defintions
6.1
(4),
6.4
(4) and
6.7
(4) are
relatively straightforward, and the remaining
conditions for each of these defintions are not. In the setting of Riemannian geometry, the construction of a parametrix is developed
using the Minakshishundaram-Pleijel recursive formulas. In this section we will outline these ideas and show how it may be possible to
extend the notions in other settings.

We will leave the thorough investigation of these points for the follow-up paper
[
21
]
. Nonetheless, we are presenting the discussion in this section
in order to complete the theoretical framework in this article.

7.1.
Compact Riemannian manifolds

Let
M
be a finite dimensional, smooth, compact Riemannian manifold of real dimension
n
.
Since
M
is locally modeled by Euclidean space, one would expect that the
heat kernel on
M
can be approximated for small
t
by the heat kernel on
n
-dimensional Euclidean space. Specifically, and
by following
[
33
]
, one takes a parametrix
for
y
sufficiently close to
x
to be given by

(7.1)

H_{k}(x,y;t)=\frac{1}{(4\pi t)^{n/2}}e^{-d^{2}_{M}(x,y)/4t}\left(\sum\limits_{j=0}^{k}u_{j}(x,y)t^{j}\right)

where
k>n/2
,
d_{M}(x,y)
is the Riemannian distance between
x
and
y
,
and differential functions
\{u_{j}\}
. The main objective is to have that
L_{x}H(x,y;t)
extends to a continuous
function on
M\times M\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. In order to fulfill this condition, and as discussed on page 149 of
[
9
]
, one
sets the
Ansatz
that

(7.2)

L_{x}H_{k}(x,y,t)=t^{k}\Delta_{x}(u_{k}(x,y))\cdot\frac{1}{(4\pi t)^{n/2}}e^{-d^{2}_{M}(x,y)/4t}.

Indeed, if one can satisfy (
7.2
), then the first three conditions of Definitions
6.1
and
6.4

will follow. In general, the Laplacian considered in this setting satisfies the product formula that

(7.3)

\Delta(fg)=(\Delta f)g+2\langle\text{\rm grad}f,\text{\rm grad}g\rangle+f(\Delta g),

for
C^{2}
functions on
M
and with first derivative operator
grad
; see page 3 of
[
9
]
.
Furthermore, as stated in
[
9
]
, it is advantageous to write all expressions in (
7.2
) in geodesic spherical
coordinates near
x
on
M
. In doing so, one obtains from (
7.2
)
a system of
first-order linear differential equations
by equating the coefficients in
t
. In doing so, one
gets an equation for
u_{0}
in terms the metric on
M
, and an equation for
u_{j}
in terms of
the metric on
M
and
u_{j-1}
whenever
j>0
; see pages 149-150 of
[
9
]
. These equations are readily solvable,
thus leading to a somewhat explicit solution to (
7.1
), as given on page 150 of
[
9
]
.

With all this, one constructs a parametrix of the form (
7.1
) from which the heat kernel is
obtained.

Remark 11
.

When considering the space of functions on a fractal space, it is not clear if a parametrix
similar to (
7.1
) exists. Indeed, if
K_{SG}(x,y;t)
denotes the heat kernel on the Sierpinski gasket,
it is stated on page 70 of
[
28
]
that one of the main results of
[
29
]
is that

\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}t^{c}\log K_{SG}(x,y;t)

does not exist for the appropriate power of
c
and various points
x
and
y
on the Sierpinski gasket. Refined results
were obtained in
[
28
]
using the measurable Riemannian structure; see Corollary 1.2(b). While the author proves upper
and lower bounds for
K_{SG}(x,y;t)
(see Corollary 1.2(a) loc. cit.), the bounds are such that one does not see a specific two-sides
Gaussian-type asymptotic which is needed in order to have a parametrix in analogy with (
7.1
).

7.2.
An
L^{1}
parametrix

Consider an
L^{1}
space with distance measure
d(x,y)
. Let
F:\mathbf{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}_{\geq 0}
be
a smooth, non-negative function whose Lebesgue integral is equal to one. Without loss of generality, assume that

F(0)\neq 0
. It is elementary to show that the function
c(x,t)F(d(x,y)/t)
satisfies Definition
6.1
(4)
where
c(x,t)
is determined by taking
H(x,y;t)=c(x,t)F(d(x,y)/t)
and
f(y)\equiv 1
in (
6.1
); see for example page 51 of
[
30
]
. With this, one can
set as an
Ansatz
that a parametrix for the heat kernel is of the form

(7.4)

H_{k}(x,y;t)=c(x,t)F(d(x,y)/t)\left(\sum\limits_{j=0}^{k}u_{j}(x,y)t^{j}\right)

together with the condition that

(7.5)

L_{x}H_{k}(x,y,t)=t^{k}\Delta_{x}(u_{k}(x,y))\cdot c(x,t)F(d(x,y)/t).

Implicit in the setting from Section
7.1
is that derivatives of the Euclidean heat kernel are
multiplies of the Euclidean heat kernel, thus one can factor the Euclidean heat kernel from all terms in (
7.2
).
Since the Euclidean heat kernel is non-vanishing,
(
7.2
) then becomes an identity which is a polynomial in
t
from which one obtains the Minakshishundaram-Pleijal
recursive formulas, which are solvable.

As described in Remark
11
, one cannot always assume that
F
is Gaussian.
However, other specific examples for
F
may be useful, at least to construct the parametrix, and thus prove the existence of the heat kernel.
For instances, one could consider
F(u)=(3/4)(1-u^{2})
when
|u|<1
and
0
elsewhere. In doing so, the

Ansatz
7.5
yields a polynomial in
t
whose coefficients involve the
u_{j}
’s. Now, if the Laplacian
admits a structure such as (
7.3
) and the operator
\text{\rm grad}u_{k}
is easily inverted, as in the
geometric case when
grad
is essentially a first derivative, then one again arrives at a system of first-order linear
differential equations. Another possibility one could consider is
F(u)=e^{-u}
.

As stated, we will leave the full development of generalized recursive formulas stemming from (
7.5
) for
[
21
]
.

7.3.
An
L^{2}
parametrix

The discussion in the previous section applies in the
L^{2}
setting as well. Additionally,
we will point out the following perhaps trivial consideration.

Assume
L^{2}
has a countable basis of eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{n}\}
with corresponding eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{n}\}
. As such,
one can write the parametrix
H(x,y,t)
as

(7.6)

H(x,y,t)=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n,m}(t)\phi_{m}(x)\phi_{n}(y).

Then, under appropriate convergence assumptions to be addressed, we have that

\displaystyle L_{x}H(x,y;t)=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}(L_{x}(a_{n,m}(t)\phi_{m}(x))\phi_{n}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}(\partial_{t}a_{n,m}(t)+\lambda_{m}a_{n,m}(t))\phi_{m}(x))\phi_{n}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}\left((\partial_{t}(e^{\lambda_{m}t}a_{n,m}(t))\right)e^{-\lambda_{m}t}\phi_{m}(x)\phi_{n}(y).

If
H(x,y,t)
is in fact the heat kernel, then
a_{n,m}(t)=0
if
n\neq m
and
a_{n,n}(t)=\exp(-\lambda_{n}t)
for all
n
.
More generally, the bounds required by Definition
6.4
are fulfilled if

a_{n,m}(t)
are
close

Lemma 8.3

Let

9.
Green’s function, resistance measures, and other derived functions

In this section we discuss how one can derive other well-known quantities from the heat kernel. Most of
the material in the section points toward future areas of investigation.

9.1.
Green’s function

The Green’s function, also known as the resolvent kernel is the kernel of the inverse of the Laplacian, if such an inverse exists. The inverse exists if and only if zero is not an eigenvalue of the Laplacian. In the setting when the Laplacian is probabilistic and the time is discrete, the heat kernel can be viewed as a Markov process and in that case existence of the inverse can be viewed as transiency property of the random walk. Its analogue in the continuous time case is the assumption that the time-integral of the heat kernel is finite.
Actually, the existence of the inverse follows from the following two assumptions we pose on the heat kernel.

Assume that the space
(\mathfrak{X},\mu)
is such that there exists a heat kernel
K_{\mathfrak{X}}
on this space and that it belongs to
L^{p}(\mu)
(
p\geq 1
).
In addition, we assume the following two properties of
K_{\mathfrak{X}}
.

HK1

For all
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}
, the functions
t\mapsto K_{\mathfrak{X}}(x,y;t)
and
t\mapsto\partial_{t}K_{\mathfrak{X}}(x,y;t)
are integrable on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

HK2

For all
x,y\in\mathfrak{X}

\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}|K_{\mathfrak{X}}(x,z;t)-H_{\mathfrak{X}}(y,z;t)|dt\rho_{x}(dy)<\infty.

In view of the fact that

\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}|H_{\mathfrak{X}}(x,z;t)|dt\rho_{x}(dy)=c(x)\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}|H_{\mathfrak{X}}(x,z;t)|dt

the assumption HK2 can be replaced with

\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}|H_{\mathfrak{X}}(y,z;t)|dt\rho_{x}(dy)<\infty.

With those two assumptions we have the following proposition.

Proposition 9.1
.

With the notation as above, assume that the heat kernel
K_{\mathfrak{X}}\in L^{p}(\mu)
for
p=1
or
p=2
satisfies assumptions HK1 and HK2. Then,

G(x,y):=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}K_{\mathfrak{X}}(x,y;t)dt

is the kernel of the inverse of the Laplacian, meaning that for every
f\in L^{q}(\mu)
with
1/p+1/q=1
we have that

\Delta\left(\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}G(x,y)f(y)\mu(dy)\right)=f(x).

Proof.

From the definition of the Laplacian we have

\displaystyle\Delta\left(\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}G(x,y)f(y)\mu(dy)\right)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}\left(\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}G(x,z)f(z)\mu(dz)-\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}G(y,z)f(z)\mu(dz)\right)\rho_{x}(dy)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}f(z)\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}(G(x,z)-G(y,z))\rho_{x}(dy)\mu(dz)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}f(z)\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}(K_{\mathfrak{X}}(x,z;t)-K_{\mathfrak{X}}(y,z;t))dt\rho_{x}(dy)\mu(dz).

Using HK2 and by applying Fubini-Tonelli theorem we get that

\displaystyle\Delta\left(\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}G(x,y)f(y)\mu(dy)\right)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}f(z)\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}(K_{\mathfrak{X}}(x,z;t)-K_{\mathfrak{X}}(y,z;t))\rho_{x}(dy)\right)dt\mu(dz)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}f(z)\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\Delta K_{\mathfrak{X}}(x,y;t)dt\mu(dz)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}f(z)\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\Delta K_{\mathfrak{X}}(x,y;t)dt\mu(dz)

\displaystyle=-\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}f(z)\int\limits_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\partial_{t}K_{\mathfrak{X}}(x,y;t)dt\mu(dz)

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\int\limits_{\mathfrak{X}}K_{\mathfrak{X}}(x,y;\varepsilon)f(z)\mu(dz)=f(x),

which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 17
.

In many instances where
(\mathfrak{X},\mu)
is a Hilbert space the Laplacian
\Delta
does, in fact, have zero eigenvalues. In that
case, one simply considers the subspace which is the orthogonal complement of the space of functions spanned by solutions
to
\Delta f=0
and proceeds as above. Equivalently, one can consider the integral

which yields an inverse to the operator

, using the proof given above. Then the Green’s function is
gotten by proving that

Assume that

In some articles,

[

the authors study the asymptotic expansion of

as

as motivation of their work, which we quote.

Our mathematical result, or at least the concrete asymptotic expansion of the entropy up to some fixed order, is needed in a

(VAE) that allows for a closed manifold as latent space, rather than the usual Euclidean latent space.

This is intriguing assertion, as is the fact that the main ingredient in the analysis of

is the parametrix construction

]

9.4.
Poisson and wave kernels

In somewhat vague terms, the Green’s function is a fundamental solution to
\Delta f(x)=0
, and the heat kernel
is a fundamental solution to
(\Delta+\partial_{t})u(x,t)=0
. The operators
\Delta
and
\Delta+\partial_{t}

are elliptic and parabolic, so in a sense there remains to consider the hyperbolic operator
\Delta+\partial_{t}^{2}
,
which is commonly called the wave operator.

A classical integral formula states that

e^{-wa}=\frac{w}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-ta^{2}}e^{-\frac{w^{2}}{4t}}t^{-1/2}\frac{dt}{t}.

Formally, if one writes the heat kernel as
e^{-t\Delta}
and the wave kernel as
e^{-w\sqrt{\Delta}}
, then
one would expect that

(9.2)

e^{-w\sqrt{\Delta}}=\frac{w}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t\Delta}e^{-\frac{w^{2}}{4t}}t^{-1/2}\frac{dt}{t}.

We refer to
[
26
]
which discusses, albeit briefly, some aspects of the integral transformation (
9.2
)
known as subordination and its place in probability theory going back to Lévy
[
31
]
. Specfically, if

w
is a real variable then one obtains the Poisson kernel and if
w
is purely complex then one obtains the wave kernel.

In
[
26
]
it is shown that one can use the small time asymptotic behavior of a heat kernel to prove that the wave
kernel admits a branched meromorphic continuation in time with singularities in (complex) time equal to the distance
between the space variables
x
and
y
. In full generality, this result is known as the Duistermaat-Guillemin theorem.
If one considers heat kernels associated to Laplacians on finite volume hyperbolic Riemann surfaces, then it is shown
in
[
27
]
that one can apply this methodology to deduce an integral representation for certain Eisenstein series, including the non-holomorphic
parabolic Eisenstein series. It would be interesting to develop this line of thought on infinite graphs, for example,
to determine if one can obtain a precise expression for the spectral measure as one has in the setting of non-compact
finite volume hyperbolic Riemann surfaces.

9.5.
Heat kernel identities

Under certain circumstances, the heat kernel is unique, so then any two expressions yield an identity.
One such expression in the setting of a separable Hilbert space is (formally) of the form

K_{\mathfrak{X}}(x,y;t)=\sum e^{-\lambda_{n}t}\phi_{n}(x)\phi_{n}(y),

where
\{\phi_{n}\}
is a complete orthonormal basis of square-integrable eigenfunctions with
eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}
. As is well-known, by comparing the spectral expansion for the heat kernel
and the parametrix construction, one obtains Weyl-type formulas for the number of eigenvalues bounded
by
T
, as an asymptotic formula in
T
, as well as the asymptotic growth of the values
\{\phi_{n}^{2}(x)\}
.
The generalization of these results to the setting of this article now are readily available.

10.
Further examples and open questions

Let us conclude this article by listing a number of examples. Within many of the examples
we will state points which we believe are interesting directions for future studies.

10.1.
Finite and infinite graphs

There are many various examples to consider here, so we will list only some of the possibilities.

Suppose
\mathfrak{X}
is a finite graph, so
\mathcal{B}
is generated by a finite subsets of singleton sets,
each with positive measure. Then, as discussed in
[
10
]
and
[
25
]
, different examples
yield various interesting results. For example, Proposition 19 of
[
10
]
studies a discrete
half-line and derives the heat kernel (with a boundary condition) using the heat kernel on the discrete line.
Along the way, the proof implies a number of identities associated to the classical

I
-Bessel function. In
[
25
]
it is shown that one can take a Dirac
delta function as a parametrix, which yields a known combinatorial formula for the heat kernel on an infinite
graph in terms of lengths of certain paths.

10.2.
Reproducing kernel Hilbert space

Consider the setting of a reproducing kernel Hilbert space with kernel
H(x,y)
. Then
the function
e^{-t}H(x,y)
is a parametrix.

In general, the function
H(x,y)
is a
called the Bergman kernel, and itself is a widely studied function and it possesses
“existence and uniqueness” features like the heat kernel, under certain circumstances.
Indeed, this is the point of view from Chapter 6 of
[
34
]
who is following a method
due to Selberg to get (what is called) the Eichler trace formula by constructing the Bergman kernel
using a second approach. (For a review of this material using
the language of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, we refer to unpublished yet available
online notes by E. Assing, who refers to the Master’s Thesis of F. Völz.)

In this setting, the small time asymptotics of the heat kernel was used to prove effective sup-norm
bounds for the Bergman kernel associated to the space of holomorphic form of fixed
weight for finite volume Riemann surfaces; see
[
11
]
. The same method of
proof has been employed in other settings; see for example
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
and references therein.
It is evident in all of these cases that the proof involves general properties of heat kernels,
and is widely adaptable under general circumstances.

There is a very general construction of positive definite kernels, which has many applications;
see section 6 of
[
24
]
. Namely, let
\{f_{n}\}
be an orthonormal basis of the separable Hilbert space
\mathfrak{X}

under study, finite or not, and let
\{Z_{n}\}
be an i.i.d. system of standard Gaussians. For every

x\in\mathfrak{X}
, set
G_{x}=\sum f_{n}(x)Z_{n}
. Then the positive definite kernel
K(x,y)=\sum f_{n}(x)f_{n}(y)

can be realized as
K(x,y)=\mathbb{E}(G_{x}G_{y})
, where
\mathbb{E}
denotes the expected value.
We refer to
[
24
]
, specifically Theorem 6.3, and references therein for further discussion and
applications. The results in the present article now provide an explicit construction of the
corresponding heat kernel, thus makes available all the corresponding mathematical tools for future studies.

10.3.
Non-separable Hilbert spaces

In the setting of Riemannian geometry, one can define
a heat kernel on a non-compact space through compact exhaustion; see, for example, Chapter VIII of
[
9
]
,
specifically page 188. If
M
is a noncompact Riemannian manifold, one considers a sequence

\Omega_{j}
for integers
j\geq 1
whose compactification
\bar{\Omega_{j}}
lies in
\Omega_{j+1}

and such that
\displaystyle\bigcup\limits_{j=1}^{\infty}\Omega_{j}=M
. Then one defines the
heat kernel on
M
to be the limit in
j
of the heat kernels on
\Omega_{j}
. We hypothesize that similarly
one can define a heat kernel on a nonseparable Hilbert space through compact exhaustion. Again, we leave that
study for a future investigation.

10.4.
Changing metrics

There are instances where a heat kernel in one setting can serve as a parametrix for another.
For example, consider the setting when
\mathfrak{X}
consists of the vertices of a finite or infinite graph.
Assume one has a heat kernel
K_{\lambda}(x,y;t)
associated to one measure
\lambda
whose associated
degree function is uniformly bounded away from zero and infinity. In this case, the small time
behavior of the heat kernel is simply
K_{\lambda}(x,y,0)=\delta_{x=y}
, meaning that the limiting
behavior of
K_{\lambda}(x,y;t)
as
t\rightarrow 0^{+}
is zero if
x\neq y
and
1
if
x=y
.
Let
\lambda^{\prime}
be another such measure. Then it is straightforward to show that
K_{\lambda}(x,y;t)

is a parametrix for the heat kernel
K_{\lambda^{\prime}}(x,y;t)
.

As similar argument holds in the setting of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces.
