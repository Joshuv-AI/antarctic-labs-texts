Periodontal Diagnosis in a Young Patient with an Unusual Presentation of Dental Calculus
Authors: Khansa Taha Ababneh, Sultan Al Nafisah, Manal Maslamani, Turki Al Asiri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis report details the periodontal diagnosis and phase I treatment of an 18-year-old male patient dissatisfied with his teeth. The patient, on 20 mg Isotretinoin for acne, exhibited heavy calculus and plaque, generalized gingival inflammation, carious lesions, remaining roots, an anterior open bite, bilateral cross-bite, and an ectopically erupted tooth (#25). Initial scaling enabled examination, revealing FMPS and FMBS of 100%, PD of 1–7 mm, gingival recession of 1–2 mm, a maximum CAL of 6 mm, and radiographic bone loss of around 48% of teeth, reaching up to 15% of the root length. Diagnosed with generalized periodontitis (stage II, localized stage III, grade B), treatment involved patient education, scaling, selective root planning, and a long-term maintenance plan. At reevaluation, the patient showed improved oral hygiene, reduced FMPS (33%) and FMBS (11%), fewer pockets, and decreased PD and CAL (deepest CAL now 6 mm). Early periodontal intervention and consistent oral hygiene are critical for subsequent dental treatments.

INTRODUCTION

Periodontitis is a multifactorial infectious disease of the tooth-supporting apparatus, primarily driven by dental biofilm (plaque) bacteria, that trigger a chronic inflammatory host response, leading to progressive destruction of the periodontal tissues.[12] The initiation and progression of periodontitis are influenced by multiple local and systemic factors.[3] Dental calculus is calcified dental plaque, formed on the supra and subgingival surfaces of teeth and restorations.[4] It is regarded as a secondary etiologic factor,[4] and as the most important local contributing factor of periodontal diseases.[5] Calculus is found in all known populations, past and present, but the extent varies widely among individuals and populations.[4] It is composed primarily of calcium phosphate mineral salts deposited between and within remnants of formerly viable microorganisms and is covered by an unmineralized bacterial layer.[6] It is believed that dental calculus does not contribute directly to gingival irritation but it provides a nidus for the continued accumulation of plaque.[4] It retains plaque close to the gingivae, thus contributing to gingivitis and periodontitis, meanwhile making plaque removal difficult.[7] However, some classic,[8] and recent research have indicated a more direct role of calculus in periodontal tissue inflammation and destruction.[9] Ziauddin et al. (2018)[10] reported that sterile calculus can induce pyroptotic cell death (an inflammatory form of lytic programmed cell death) in human oral epithelial cells in cell culture, if they phagocytized it, suggesting that the crystalline structure of calculus plays a major role in this process. They suggested that oral epithelial cell death induced by dental calculus might be important for the etiology of periodontitis.[10] In a later interesting in vitro study, they[11] suggested that calculus particles play a critical role in the breakdown of crevicular/pocket epithelium integrity. In clinical practice, removing this visible risk factor by scaling and root planning is a key part of initial periodontal therapy.[121314] The current report describes the periodontal diagnosis and treatment of a case of moderate periodontitis in a very young adult, with an unusual amount and presentation of dental calculus.

Case presentation

An 18-year-old, non-smoker male patient presented to the comprehensive clinics at the College of Dentistry, King Saud bin Abdulaziz University for Health Sciences, Riyadh, KSA. The patient expressed his complaint as generally demanding “to have his teeth fixed,” saying that everything in his mouth annoyed him, but that he had no pain. He was unaware of any family history of periodontitis or any systemic diseases. At the initial appointment, the patient reported taking 20 mg/day oral Isotretinoin (INN) (13-cis-Retinoic acid; brand name: Roaccutane/Accutane-Roche, Switzerland) for treatment of dermal acne for the last three months, based on medical advice. However, at the second appointment, he reported discontinuing the medication without medical instructions. The patient reported he does not brush his teeth and does not use any other oral hygiene aids. Written informed consent for case publication was obtained from the patient before the preparation of this report and ethical approval was obtained from King Abdullah International Medical Research Center, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia (KAIMRC), IRB approval Number: IRB/0848/23.

Clinical examination

The patient had a normal extra-oral appearance and had no facial acne. Intraoral examination [Figure 1] revealed that he had an anterior open bite of 4 mm, heavy generalized calculus, generalized severe gingival inflammation of the marginal, interdental and attached gingivae, gingival bleeding on probing, and localized areas of gingival desquamation. He had very large amounts of dental plaque and calculus deposits on all teeth, which could be observed on the facial, lingual, proximal, and occlusal surfaces of posterior teeth, covering 1/3 to 2/3 of the facial surfaces of teeth on the right side, and up to 3/3 of the facial, lingual, and occlusal teeth surfaces on the left side [Figure 1a-c]. His palatal vault was high and narrow, suggesting mouth breathing, which was confirmed by the patient [Figure 1d]. The maxillary left second premolar (#25) was ectopically erupted (palatally) and rotated. When asked about unilateral chewing habits (i.e. chewing exclusively on the right side), the patient stated that he prefers to chew usually on the right side, for no particular reason, although he had some dental pain on that side (right). The patient had multiple carious lesions, that included occlusal caries on teeth # 18, 36, 37, and 45, mesial-occlusal caries on teeth # 48 and # 16, distal-occlusal caries on teeth # 17, 15, and 47, labial caries on tooth # 13, and palatal caries on tooth #16. Remaining roots of teeth # 15 and # 46 were present [Figure 1e]. Tooth # 26 had an old coronal restoration with previously initiated root canal treatment, as evidenced by access into the pulp chamber; the tooth also had asymptomatic apical periodontitis.

Full periodontal examination was attempted but was impossible at first, due to the huge amounts of calculus and biofilm deposits on both supra- and subgingival surfaces of teeth, obstructing periodontal probing completely on most teeth. Following gross debridement of teeth to enable periodontal probing and charting [Figure 1f], the probing depth (PD) and clinical attachment level (CAL) were measured for all teeth, at six sites per tooth by walking the periodontal probe subgingivally around the whole tooth circumference, gently. The FMPS score was recorded before scaling, and the FMBS score was recorded before scaling and at sites with heavy calculus; after initial scaling; the FMPS and FMBS were both equal to 100%. The PD ranged between 1 and 7 mm, and there was generalized bleeding on probing (BOP) [Table 1]. No furcation lesions or mobility were detected and no teeth were lost. The gingival recession of 1–2 mm was noticed on the facial surfaces of mandibular teeth after initial removal of calculus [Figure 1f, g and Table 1]. The CAL value at the site of most severe attachment loss was 6 mm, whereas the average CAL at the initial examination was 3.5 mm.

Radiographic examination

The panoramic and intraoral radiographs are shown in Figure 2. Bitewing and periapical radiographs revealed up to 15% horizontal alveolar bone loss interproximal to 48% of the teeth (teeth involved were # 17, 16, 15, 11, 21, 33, 32, 31, 41, 42, 45, 46, and 47).

Pre-treatment panoramic and intraoral radiographs. Periapical radiographs show horizontal crestal bone loss, within the coronal third of roots, reaching up to 15% of root length in about 48% of interproximal sites, indicating generalized periodontitis, stage II, grade B

Periodontal diagnosis

Clinical attachment loss (CAL) around 48% of the teeth ranged between 3 and 4 mm, with 4 sites showing a CAL of 5 mm and 1 site (distal to tooth #27) showing a CAL of 6 mm. This, combined with radiographic evidence of interproximal bone resorption up to 15% around the affected teeth, indicated the presence of generalized periodontitis, stage II, localized stage III. The grading was determined by dividing the highest percentage of bone loss by the patient’s age; 15/18 = 0.83, which corresponds to grade B. According to McGuire and Nunn’s classification, the overall prognosis for the dentition was fair.

Periodontal treatment plan

The periodontal treatment plan included patient motivation, education, scaling, and root planning (SRP), a prescription of 0.2% Chlorhexidine mouthwash, reevaluation, and a long-term maintenance program. Thorough SRP was initiated during the second appointment, following initial debridement in the first appointment to allow for proper periodontal examination [Figure 1f and g].

The patient was encouraged to improve his oral health and hygiene, adopt proper toothbrushing habits, start flossing, and try chewing on both sides as much as possible. Detailed oral hygiene instructions, including the modified Bass technique, were thoroughly explained and demonstrated intraorally, and educational videos were shared with the patient.

Due to the substantial amount of calculus present, SRP was completed over four appointments within two weeks. Chlorhexidine 0.2% mouthwash was prescribed for two weeks, with instructions to use it twice daily, rinsing for one minute each time. The patient was reevaluated six weeks after completing phase I periodontal therapy. He reported being very pleased with the cleanliness of his teeth and the significant reduction in gingival bleeding.

The oral hygiene and periodontal parameters showed noticeable improvement [Figure 3]: the full mouth plaque score (FMPS) was 33%, the full mouth gingival bleeding score (FMBS) was 11%, the greatest CAL was 5 mm, and the average CAL was 3.4 mm. It was explained to the patient that the remaining parts of the treatment plan, including the extraction of hopeless teeth, as well as restorative, orthodontic, and prosthetic treatments, would be initiated after the completion of phase I periodontal therapy.

Diagnostic challenges

The initial examination was hindered by the presence of enormous amounts of dental calculus and plaque deposits. In addition, it was extremely difficult to take adequately informative bitewing radiographs, due to the ectopically erupted tooth # 25, and the other occlusal discrepancies.

DISCUSSION

In the present case, the patient was a very young adult, a university student, who had very poor oral hygiene, remaining roots, dental caries, gingivitis, and periodontitis. This patient had discomfort and dissatisfaction with the overall condition and appearance of his teeth. It is very unusual to encounter educated patients with such great amounts of soft and hard deposits on teeth, at this young age (18 years). In addition to his poor oral hygiene habits, the large amounts of calculus could be attributed to his preferred unilateral chewing on the right side, probably associated with the occlusal discrepancies that the patient had. As for his general health, although our patient did not report serious systemic conditions, he had suffered from dermal acne for which he was prescribed oral Isotretinoin. He took the medication for three months but discontinued it without medical advice. Isotretinoin is a medication that has anti-inflammatory actions and has been shown to reduce bleeding on probing in gingivitis and periodontitis patients, as well as salivary matrix metalloproteinases MMP-8 and MMP-9 in periodontitis patients.[15] Isotretinoin may have modified the inflammatory process in this patient, who demonstrated generally less than expected inflammatory signs, despite the excessive biofilm and calculus deposits on his teeth.

This patient had generalized periodontitis, stage II, localized stage III, grade B. The greatest CAL was 6 mm, the average CAL = 3.5, and the radiographic bone loss reached up to 15%. When first published by the AAP-EFP (American Academy of Periodontology-European Federation of Periodontology) in 2018, the new classification,[16] recommended utilizing the site of greatest attachment loss to diagnose periodontitis. However, recent publications by renowned periodontal researchers[1718] some of whom have developed the 2018 classification, favored the idea of patient-based diagnosis, rather than implementing the classification rigidly. Describing the periodontitis stage in our patient as “generalized stage II, localized stage III” is consistent with these case series reports discussing “gray zones,” or clinical scenarios that challenge decision-making with the 2018 classification.[1718] The authors concluded that, although guidelines and algorithms help in establishing standardized diagnoses, cases falling into gray zones require clinical judgment, which should guide the final assignment of staging.[1718]

The stage indicates disease severity, whereas the grade indicates the rate of disease progression; in this young patient, they collectively point to a high susceptibility to periodontitis, in the presence of poor oral hygiene.[4] The aims of periodontal treatment included[1]: elimination of gingival and periodontal inflammation,[2] pocket elimination with reduction of PD and CAL,[3] establishment of excellent oral hygiene,[4] improving prognosis,[5] prevention of disease recurrence and maintenance of a healthy periodontal status through supportive periodontal care. The patient was re-evaluated six weeks following the initiation of periodontal treatment. In the 1st reevaluation visit, the patient demonstrated a transformed attitude toward oral health; he showed high compliance with oral hygiene instructions, and motivation to take care of his teeth, periodontium, and overall oral health. Periodontal examination and charting revealed improvement of his periodontal parameters, although there was still room for further improvement. Of great importance in this patient is maintenance of a high standard of oral hygiene throughout life, to maintain a healthy functional dentition.[19] It is planned to continue periodontal monitoring throughout restorative, orthodontic, and prosthetic treatment, and to put this patient on a long-term periodontal maintenance program (supportive periodontal care).

CONCLUSIONS

This report presented the periodontal diagnosis and periodontal treatment of a case of moderate periodontitis, associated with abundant amounts of biofilm and calculus deposits in a very young patient. Early detection and treatment of periodontitis, especially in young individuals, is central to the preservation of a functional dentition. This report also shows that it is both possible and helpful to spend chairside time educating and instructing periodontal patients to adopt adequate oral hygiene practices, utilizing videos, step-by-step intraoral demonstrations of oral hygiene procedures, and positive encouragement. Periodontal diagnosis should be driven by following international guidelines as well as using clinical judgment.

Contribution to the field statement

Case reports utilizing the most recent AAP-EFP classification of periodontal and peri-implant diseases are still few. This case report discusses the diagnosis of a periodontitis case in a young individual, with unusual amounts of deposits, as well as the periodontal treatment and follow-up. This would help dental clinicians in diagnosing periodontitis cases and would draw their attention to early detection of periodontitis.
