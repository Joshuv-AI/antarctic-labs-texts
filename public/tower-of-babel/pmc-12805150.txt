Frailty Predicts Geriatric and Cardiovascular Adverse Outcomes After TAVR
Authors: Osamah Badwan, Issam Motairek, Fawzi Zghyer, Rishi Puri, Grant Reed, Amar Krishnaswamy, Venu Menon, Abdulla A Damluji, Samir Kapadia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) expands to include younger and lower-risk patients, traditional risk stratification based on chronological age may be insufficient. Frailty, a marker of biological aging, offers additional prognostic insight.ObjectivesThe purpose of this study was to evaluate the association between frailty, as measured by the 5-factor Modified Frailty Index (MFI-5), and long-term clinical and geriatric outcomes in patients undergoing TAVR.MethodsUsing the TriNetX Research Network, we identified patients ≥18 years who underwent TAVR from 2015 to 2023. Patients were categorized into MFI-0 (robust), MFI-2 (prefrail), and MFI-5 (frail) groups. Propensity score matching was applied for baseline characteristics. Matched cohort sizes were as follows: MFI-0 vs MFI-2 (N = 1,592 each), MFI-0 vs MFI-5 (N = 836 each), and MFI-2 vs MFI-5 (N = 1,233 each). The primary outcome was 5-year all-cause mortality. Secondary outcomes included cardiovascular events, procedural complications, and geriatric syndromes. Analyses were stratified by age groups: <65, 65 to 75, and >75 years.ResultsCompared to the robust cohort, frail patients had significantly higher 5-year mortality (27.6% vs 8.3%; HR: 1.41; 95% CI: 1.14-1.74), stroke (21.2% vs 1.2%; HR: 18.52; 95% CI: 8.13-42.19), acute coronary syndrome (20.1% vs 1.3%; HR: 8.77; 95% CI: 4.74-16.23), and major bleeding (23.2% vs 1.2%; HR: 19.23; 95% CI: 8.47-43.48). Geriatric syndromes including delirium, incontinence, depression, and malnutrition were also more prevalent in frail patients. MFI retained prognostic value across all age strata, including patients under 65 years.ConclusionsThe MFI-5 is a useful tool for predicting mortality and geriatric syndromes in TAVR patients. It offers a practical approach to risk stratification, complementing chronological age.Key words: biological age, cardiovascular outcomes, frailty, geriatric syndromes, modified frailty index, transcatheter aortic valve replacement

As the indications for transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) expand for patients with severe aortic stenosis, the number of procedures performed in practice is expected to increase across a broad range of patient risk profiles.1,2 While chronological age is a conventional marker for cardiovascular risk, it does not account for the variability in physiological reserve among older adults when exposed to stressors like cardiovascular disease.2 In contrast, biological aging reflects functional decline across multiple systems and is more closely associated with vulnerability to adverse outcomes.3

Frailty, a key marker of biological aging, is defined as increased vulnerability to stressors across multiple physiological systems that increase the risk of adverse health and cardiovascular events.4, 5, 6, 7 Frailty often coexists with geriatric syndromes such as cognitive impairment, malnutrition, impaired mobility, depression, delirium, and function dependence, which significantly impact long-term prognosis yet remain underrepresented in conventional risk stratification tools.8,9 As TAVR expands to lower-risk populations, incorporating frailty into risk assessment may offer a more comprehensive approach beyond traditional metrics.

The 5-factor Modified Frailty Index (MFI-5) is a comorbidity-based tool derived from the deficit accumulation model of frailty, originally developed using the Canadian Study of Health and Aging Frailty Index.4 It was subsequently adapted utilizing data from the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program and validated against other measures in predicting postoperative outcomes.4,10, 11, 12 The MFI-5 incorporates 5 variables: a history of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), congestive heart failure, and nonindependent functional status.4 These factors are commonly available in real-world settings and offer a pragmatic measure of frailty. In this study, we aim to use MFI-5 as a measure of frailty to predict long-term cardiovascular and geriatric outcomes following TAVR.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the TriNetX Research Network, a global federated platform that aggregates deidentified electronic health record data from over 100 health care organizations. This network provides access to a broad range of patient characteristics including demographic, clinical, and procedural information. The data are deidentified to protect patient privacy while allowing for the extraction of both structured and unstructured health data. Patients aged 18 years or older who underwent TAVR between 2015 and 2023 were identified using standardized procedural codes within the platform. The study was exempt from Institutional Review Board approval because of use of aggregate, deidentified data from research network database.

Patients were categorized into 3 frailty groups: MFI-0 (robust), MFI-2 (prefrail), and MFI-5 (frail). Due to data structure limitations, the MFI-2 group was defined using “OR” logic, approximating an average frailty burden of 2. Propensity score matching was performed on demographics, resulting in 3 matched comparisons: MFI-0 vs MFI-2 (N = 1,592 each), MFI-0 vs MFI-5 (N = 836 each), and MFI-2 vs MFI-5 (N = 1,233 each).

The primary outcome was 5-year all-cause mortality. Secondary outcomes included a range of geriatric syndromes, such as incontinence, cognitive impairment, malnutrition, delirium, and depression, as well as other clinical outcomes, including ischemic stroke, acute coronary syndrome (ACS), major bleeding, endocarditis, and pacemaker implantation. Outcomes were identified using International Classification of Diseases-10 and Current Procedural Terminology codes; detailed code lists and analytic scripts are available from the authors upon request.

Statistical analysis

To account for confounding by comorbidities, we employed propensity score methods using nearest-neighbor matching without replacement, as described by Rosenbaum and Rubin.13, 14, 15 These methods rely on the principle that, conditional on the propensity score, the distribution of observed covariates is balanced between treatment groups. This “balancing” property allows for adjustment of confounding by covariates included in the propensity score model. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression to balance baseline characteristics, including age, sex, race, and comorbidities, between the comparison groups (robust vs prefrail, robust vs frail, and prefrail vs frail). Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated, and Cox proportional hazards models were used to calculate HRs with 95% CIs for each outcome. Subgroup analyses were performed stratified by age groups (<65 years, 65-75 years, and >75 years) to assess the consistency of associations across age strata. Statistical significance was defined as a 2-tailed P < 0.05; after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, the threshold for significance was adjusted to P < 0.005. Propensity score distribution before and after matching for each pairwise was performed (Supplemental Figure 1). Analyses were performed using TriNetX Analytics, and figures were generated using R version 4.2.0.

Results

Baseline characteristics across the matched cohorts demonstrated notable differences. In the robust vs prefail group comparison, the mean age was 80.9 years (range: 76.4-77.0 years at index), with similar female representation (628/1,592 [39.4%] vs 624/1,592 [39.2%]) and racial distribution (White: 1,348/1,592 [84.7%] vs 1,351/1,592 [84.9%]). In contrast, the robust vs frail cohorts showed a mean age of 80.4 vs 80.6 years and 76.2 vs 76.4 years at index, with comparable female proportions (391/836 [46.8%] vs 388/836 [46.4%]) and White race (732/836 [87.6%] vs 735/836 [87.9%]). For the prefrail vs frail cohort, the mean age was identical at 80.3 years, and age at index was 76.5 years in both groups, with females comprising 542/1233 (44.0%) of each cohort and 934/1233 (75.8%) identified as White. Comorbidity burden increased markedly with higher frailty scores. Compared with the robust cohort, both prefrail and frail groups had higher rates of chronic kidney disease (505/1,592 [31.7%] and 513/836 [61.4%]), heart failure (934/1,592 [58.7%] and 723/836 [86.5%]), COPD (332/1,592 [20.9%] and 650/836 [77.8%]), and atherosclerotic heart disease (1,150/1,592 [72.2%] and 752/836 [90.0%]). Similar trends were seen in the prefrail and frail cohorts, with higher prevalence of chronic conditions in the frail group, including chronic kidney disease (770/1,233 [62.4%]), heart failure (1,082/1,233 [87.8%]), COPD (958/1,233 [77.7%]), and atherosclerotic heart disease (1,118/1,233 [90.7%]). Medication use also differed significantly, with greater use of lipid-lowering agents, antihypertensives, and antiarrhythmics in the higher frailty groups. Notably, body mass index (BMI) was higher in more frail patients, with values ranging from 28.5 to 30.1 kg/m2 in the lower frailty cohorts to 30.0 to 32.8 kg/m2 in the higher frailty groups. Glomerular filtration rate was consistently lower in the higher frailty cohorts, reflecting greater renal impairment (Table 1).

Outcomes worsened with increasing frailty. In the prefrail vs robust group comparison, mortality was significantly higher (320/1,592 [20.1%] vs 120/1,592 [7.5%; HR: 1.41; 95% CI: 1.14-1.74), as were ACS (149/1,592 [9.4%] vs 20/1,592 [1.3%]; HR: 5.08; 95% CI: 3.18-8.13) and stroke (153/1,592 [9.6%] vs 28/1,592 [1.8%]; HR: 3.60; 95% CI: 2.40-5.41). These risks were further elevated in the frail vs robust cohort: mortality (231/836 [27.6%] vs 69/836 [8.3%]; HR: 1.41; 95% CI: 1.07-1.85), ACS (168/836 [20.1%] vs 11/836 [1.3%]; HR: 8.77; 95% CI: 4.74-16.23), and stroke (177/836 [21.2%] vs 10/836 [1.2%]; HR: 18.52; 95% CI: 8.13-42.19). Major bleeding (194/836 [23.2%] vs 10/836 [1.2%]; HR: 19.23; 95% CI: 8.47-43.48) and endocarditis (314/836 [37.6%] vs 26/836 [3.1%]; HR: 9.43; 95% CI: 6.29-14.08) were also markedly elevated in the frail group. Pacemaker implantations occurred more frequently in the frail group (101/836 [12.1%] vs 50/836 [6.0%]; HR: 1.61; 95% CI: 1.14-2.26) (Figure 1).

When comparing frail to prefrail groups directly, 5-year mortality remained significantly higher (417/1,228 [34.0%] vs 240/1,226 [19.6%]; HR: 1.58; 95% CI: 1.35-1.86; P < 0.001), along with increased risks of stroke (HR: 2.01; 95% CI: 1.37-2.95; P < 0.001), ACS (HR: 2.11; 95% CI: 1.58-3.4; P < 0.001), and major bleeding (HR: 2.51; 95% CI: 1.8-2.9; P < 0.001). These patterns reflect a stepwise gradient in adverse outcomes with increasing frailty. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis comparing the prefrail and frail cohorts demonstrated a clear separation in cumulative survival over 5 years (Figure 2). The frail group showed a median survival of 1,705 days, with an estimated survival probability of 45.9% at the end of the follow-up, compared to 60.4% in the prefrail group. The log-rank test confirmed a statistically significant difference between the groups (chi-square = 32.6; P < 0.001). The calculated HR for mortality was 0.63 (95% CI: 0.54-0.74; P < 0.001), indicating that patients in the frail group had a significantly higher mortality risk compared to those from the prefrail cohort.

Patients who experienced the outcome prior to the time window were excluded. The frail group demonstrated significantly lower survival probability and shorter median survival compared to prefrail group, as confirmed by the log-rank test (chi-square = 32.6; P < 0.001).

Geriatric syndromes were also notably more common in frailer patients. In the frail group, the incidence of delirium (213/836 [25.5%]), incontinence (221/836 [26.4%]), depression (328/836 [39.2%]), and malnutrition (152/836 [18.2%]) was significantly higher than in the robust group (P < 0.001). Due to fewer than 10 events in the robust group, HRs were not calculated for these outcomes, though each demonstrated statistical significance (P < 0.001). Compared to prefrail, the frail group continued to show higher rates of depression (483/1,233 [39.2%] vs 225/1,233 [18.2%]; HR: 2.28; 95% CI: 1.95-2.67; P < 0.001), delirium (303/1,233 [24.6%] vs 136/1,233 [11.0%]; HR: 2.15; 95% CI: 1.76-2.63; P < 0.001), incontinence (230/1,233 [18.7%] vs 103/1,233 [8.4%]; HR: 2.14; 95% CI: 1.7-2.7; P < 0.001), and malnutrition (268/1,233 [21.7%] vs 103/1,233 [8.4%]; HR: 2.51; 95% CI: 2.0-3.15; P < 0.001).

To further explore chronological age effects, we performed an age-stratified analysis dividing the cohort into <65, 65 to 75, and >75 years (Table 2). In the <65 group (n = 43 per cohort), few outcomes exceeded 10 events and were otherwise similar between the prefrail and frail groups. In the 65 to 75 group (n = 268 per cohort), frail patients had markedly higher risk for stroke (HR: 1.96; 95% CI: 1.32-2.94; P = 0.001), ACS (HR: 2.50; 95% CI: 1.75-3.70; P < 0.001), death (HR: 2.27; 95% CI: 1.56-3.33; P < 0.001), major bleeding (HR: 1.92; 95% CI: 1.33-2.78; P < 0.001), heart failure (HR: 2.22; 95% CI: 1.72-2.86; P < 0.001), and renal failure (HR: 2.07; 95% CI: 1.59-2.70; P < 0.001). In the >75 group (n = 834 per cohort), frail patients showed significantly higher risk across nearly all outcomes, including stroke (HR: 1.61; 95% CI: 1.25-2.04; P < 0.001), ACS (HR: 1.82; 95% CI: 1.47-2.27; P < 0.001), death (HR: 1.39; 95% CI: 1.18-1.67; P < 0.001), heart failure (HR: 2.17; 95% CI: 1.89-2.50; P < 0.001), renal failure (HR: 2.27; 95% CI: 1.92-2.63; P < 0.001), and depression (HR: 2.22; 95% CI: 1.85-2.70; P < 0.001). Outcomes with fewer than 10 events were reported descriptively without formal statistical testing. These consistent patterns across age brackets reinforce that even within finer age strata, the frailty burden identified by the MFI independently stratifies risk and is associated with worse cardiovascular, procedural, and geriatric outcomes.

Discussion

This study provides a large, real-world sample, with long-term follow-up which evaluates both extreme and intermediate levels of frailty. Our findings suggest that the MFI correlates with increased risks of geriatric syndromes, mortality, cardiovascular events, and device-related complications. The consistent stepwise relationship across MFI categories also retains prognostic relevance independent of chronological age. These findings support previous work showing that frailty enhances the prognostic performance of conventional risk scores.16

Our findings differ from those of Krishnan et al,17 who did not find an association between the 11-variable modified frailty index (mFI-11) and mortality after TAVR. Instead, they found that sarcopenia and low albumin levels were better predictors. One reason for the difference could be that they used a smaller sample and included imaging and lab-based measures that more directly reflect physiologic reserve. In contrast, we used the 5-variable MFI, which is simpler and more practical for use with large data sets. While tools like imaging can give more detail, the MFI-5 still offers a useful way to identify high-risk patients, especially in broader clinical settings.

Moreover, the MFI appears to function as a surrogate for biological age, identifying physiologic vulnerability independent of chronological age.2,18 The elevated mortality risk in frail patients under 75 years of age (24.9% in the frail vs 8.3% in the robust cohorts) illustrates that biological aging is not confined to older adults. Rather, aging is a continuum, and frailty can signal physiologic decline in younger patients that may otherwise be missed with traditional age-based criteria. This aligns with prior literature suggesting that frailty is a more nuanced and predictive measure of health in older adults undergoing TAVR.18, 19, 20

As the TAVR population continues to include younger and lower-risk patients, it becomes even more important to assess frailty in a way that reflects biological aging, not just chronological age. Tools like the Clinical Frailty Scale and the Essential Frailty Toolset have been shown to predict outcomes such as mortality, decline in function, and hospital readmission after TAVR.21,22 The use of lab markers like low albumin and anemia also help identify patients at higher risk.23 The Fried physical frailty phenotype, based on weight loss, exhaustion, low activity, slow gait, and weakness, has similarly been validated in TAVR cohorts; in the Women's International Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation registry, both prefrailty and frailty were associated with higher risks of death, bleeding, and adverse events at 1 year.24 Rockwood’s cumulative deficit frailty index provides a multidimensional view of health deficits and has been shown to stratify prognosis after valve interventions.21 Recent reviews confirm that Fried’s phenotype and Rockwood’s deficit model remain the 2 dominant frameworks, each capturing distinct aspects of vulnerability and reserve.7 While these assessments provide valuable detail, they can be harder to implement across large populations. In contrast, the MFI-5 is a simpler, comorbidity-based measure that can be easily pulled from electronic health records. It may not capture physical performance or lab-based markers, but its practicality and broad accessibility make it useful in both clinical and research settings. These findings are consistent with the Frailty in Older Adults Undergoing Aortic Valve Replacement study, which highlighted the importance of focusing on physiologic reserve rather than age alone when evaluating TAVR patients.20

Notably, conditions such as depression, incontinence, delirium, and malnutrition, often overlooked in traditional risk scores, were significantly more common in frail patients.25 The high incidence of such syndromes in frail patients emphasizes the broader physiologic burden of frailty and the need for holistic, multidimensional risk assessment.

Moreover, the observation that frail patients had higher BMI may reflect the presence of sarcopenic obesity, a phenotype characterized by excess adiposity alongside reduced muscle mass and strength.26 This condition is increasingly recognized in older adults with cardiovascular disease and has been associated with impaired functional status and adverse outcomes. It underpins the limitations of BMI as a crude measure of physiologic reserve and reinforces the need for more comprehensive frailty assessments that capture body composition and function.

As the TAVR population expands to include younger and lower-risk patients, the ability to capture multidimensional risk along a continuum of biological aging becomes increasingly important.2 While more comprehensive geriatric assessments such as the comprehensive geriatric assessment frailty index offer detailed evaluations, they can be resource-intensive and less feasible for widespread clinical implementation.2 In contrast, the MFI provides an efficient and pragmatic alternative that can be integrated into clinical workflows. Its simplicity, combined with strong predictive value, supports its potential for practical use in TAVR evaluation and long-term care planning. These findings underscore the importance of adopting risk models that reflect aging as a continuous and individualized process rather than relying solely on chronological age. By capturing elements of biological aging, tools such as the MFI may help identify vulnerable patients, including those under 75 years of age, and complement existing approaches for patient selection and risk stratification in TAVR.

Study Limitations

This study has important limitations, including the use of the Modified Frailty Index, a comorbidity-based tool derived from the deficit accumulation model of frailty, which does not capture performance-based or functional components commonly represented in the Fried Physical Frailty Phenotype. As such, it may not fully reflect the multidimensional nature of frailty, particularly physical or social domains. Additionally, the MFI-5 is based on chronic, static comorbidities and does not account for the dynamic or potentially reversible aspects of frailty, particularly after interventions such as TAVR. However, the MFI-5 has been previously validated in surgical populations and shown to be associated with postoperative morbidity and mortality, supporting its utility as a surrogate marker of vulnerability in large-scale retrospective analyses.4,10, 11, 12

The use of electronic health record data introduces the potential for misclassification and underreporting of clinical variables, particularly those related to geriatric syndromes and functional status. For example, nonindependent functional status (one of the five components of the MFI) was documented in <10 patients per group in our matched cohorts. Because TriNetX does not display results below this threshold and to avoid misrepresentation of small sample sizes, this variable was not included in Table 1. This limitation highlights the broader challenge of capturing nuanced functional measures through structured EHR data. Nonetheless, it facilitates investigating larger populations with specific diagnoses and procedures to enhance health care delivery and resource utilization in vulnerable and frail groups.

Second, while patients in the MFI-2 group each had 2 frailty components, the specific combinations of comorbidities were not distinguished. This introduces heterogeneity within the intermediate frailty group, as different dyads (eg, congestive heart failure + COPD vs hypertension + diabetes mellitus) may carry different clinical implications. This limitation may affect interpretation of outcomes in this subgroup and highlights the need for future work examining the prognostic impact of specific frailty component combinations.

While complete variables are difficult to capture in administrative databases and represent a limitation in this study, the large sample size along with the application of different propensity score methods attempt to make the comparing robust and frail patients justifiable. Additionally, the retrospective observational design may be subject to residual confounding despite propensity score matching. TriNetX uses 1:1 greedy nearest-neighbor matching with a fixed caliper of 0.1 SDs, but conditional or mixed-effects c models are not supported postmatching. Similarly, formal tests of the proportional hazards assumption cannot be performed within the platform, and only visual inspection of Kaplan-Meier curves was possible. Interaction terms for subgroup analyses are also not supported.

Finally, while the TriNetX Research Network provides a large and diverse data set, results may not be fully generalizable to populations outside participating health care organizations or to clinical settings where frailty is assessed using more comprehensive instruments. Center-level identifiers are not available, and as such, random-effects modeling could not be performed to account for between-center variation. Regarding Figure 2, due to limitations of the TriNetX system available to us, we were unable to include more than 2 groups simultaneously in the survival analysis. As a result, the robust group could not be incorporated into the Kaplan-Meier survival analysis shown in this figure. CIs for Kaplan-Meier curves and tables of patients at risk are also not accessible within the system. However, the results of this study remain informative from public health perspective to highlight the influence of frailty in patients undergoing TAVR. Future studies incorporating serial assessments and performance-based frailty tools (eg, Fried Physical Frailty Phenotype or Rockwood Scale) may better capture functional recovery and the evolving trajectory of frailty in this population.

Conclusions

Assessing biological aging throughout the lifespan is particularly important for younger patients undergoing TAVR. The modified frailty index independently predicts long-term mortality and adverse events, including geriatric syndromes, in this population. Given its ease of use, reproducibility, and prognostic utility, the MFI provides a pragmatic tool which could aid in preprocedural assessment, optimize patient selection, and support shared decision-making (Central Illustration).

Frailty and Biological Aging Predict Outcomes After TAVR

Frailty, assessed using MFI-5, was associated with adverse outcomes after TAVR. Patients were categorized as MFI-0 (robust), MFI-2 (prefrail), or MFI-5 (frail). Over 5 years, higher MFI scores were linked to increased mortality and cardiovascular events, including ischemic stroke, ACS, major bleeding, endocarditis, and pacemaker implantation, as well as geriatric syndromes such as delirium, depression, incontinence, and malnutrition. Prognostic value persisted in patients younger than 65 years, supporting biological aging as a continuum beyond chronological thresholds.
