Trajectories of child undernutrition in Ethiopia: a multivariate GEE approach
Authors: Getnet Bogale Begashaw, Temesgen Zewotir, Haile Mekonnen Fenta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesChild undernutrition remains a significant public health issue in Ethiopia despite Millennium Development Goal efforts. Most prior Ethiopian studies have relied on cross-sectional designs or have modelled underweight, stunting and wasting as separate independent outcomes, limiting insight into their longitudinal interplay. This study employs a multivariate longitudinal framework to jointly examine trajectories, co-occurrence and shared determinants of all three anthropometric outcomes from infancy through adolescence.MethodsWe utilised the Young Lives Cohort Survey (YLCS), collecting data from ~1997 Ethiopian children across five time points (2002–2016). A multivariate generalised estimating equations (GEE) model with a logit link function and exchangeable working correlation structure assessed associations between covariates and three undernutrition outcomes (underweight, stunting and wasting) defined by WHO z-scores (<−2 SD=undernutrition; ≥−2 SD=normal). Covariate effects were estimated with ORs and 95% CIs.ResultsUndernutrition prevalence varied over time: underweight peaked at 52.1% at age 12, stunting was highest at 42.5% at age 1 (persisting at 25.6% by age 15), and wasting reached 41.2% at age 12. Overall, 37.2% of measurements showed underweight, 30.2% stunting and 24.7% wasting. The GEE model showed that higher wealth was associated with lower odds of underweight (OR=0.05, 95% CI: 0.02 to 0.11), stunting (OR=0.20, 95% CI 0.10 to 0.38) and wasting (OR=0.17, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.41). Females had lower odds (eg, underweight OR=0.61, 95% CI 0.53 to 0.70), while lack of safe water was associated with higher wasting odds (OR=1.40, 95% CI 1.16 to 1.69). Wasting odds surged by age 12 (OR=3.65, 95% CI 3.19 to 4.19).ConclusionUndernutrition burden shifted across childhood, with stunting predominating in early years, underweight peaking in preadolescence and wasting escalating in later childhood. Wealth-targeted economic support programmes, poverty reduction strategies and improved water purification may help to address these disparities.

Introduction

Child undernutrition is a condition characterised by an inadequate intake of essential nutrients, leading to stunted growth, weakened immune systems and developmental delays.1–3 The WHO classifies nutritional deficiencies into three main anthropometric indicators based on Z-scores derived from the WHO Child Growth Standards: underweight (weight-for-age Z-score (WAZ) <−2 SD), stunting (height-for-age Z-score (HAZ) <−2 SD) and wasting (weight-for-height Z-score <−2 SD).4–11 A Z-score below −2 from the median indicates moderate undernutrition, while a Z-score below −3 SD signifies severe undernutrition. These thresholds serve as key benchmarks for evaluating a child’s nutritional status and guiding appropriate interventions.12 13

Globally, undernutrition continues to impose a substantial burden on young children. Approximately 149 million children under 5 years are stunted and 49.5 million are wasted, accounting for over 45% of the disease burden in this age group.14 In Asia, an estimated 68 million children were stunted as of 2020, closely followed by Africa with 64 million.15 While the prevalence of stunting has declined in Asia (33% to 27%), Latin America (33% to 22%) and Europe (20% to 16%),16–18 chronic undernutrition persists across sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where approximately 50% of children under 5 are affected. An estimated 21.3% of all children under 5 globally were stunted (144 million), with 36% residing in SSA and Southern Asia, regions that also account for nearly 90% of underweight children worldwide.19–21 Although stunting prevalence in SSA declined from 34.5% in 2012 to 31.1% in 2019, progress has not been sufficient to meet global targets.20

Ethiopia bears one of the highest burdens of child undernutrition globally, with stunting rates of 38%–57%, wasting rates of 10%–17% and underweight rates of 22%–38% among children under 5 years of age.22–24 Ethiopia ranks among eight countries (alongside India, Nigeria, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Indonesia, China and the Democratic Republic of the Congo) that together account for half of all undernourished children worldwide.17 25–29 Child undernutrition in Ethiopia profoundly impacts health, cognitive development, educational outcomes and long-term economic productivity, particularly in low-income settings where access to nutritious food and healthcare remains limited.30 31 In recognition of this burden, Ethiopia has implemented nutrition-specific programmes, including breastfeeding promotion, food accessibility initiatives and the National Nutrition Program (NNP) supported by international partners. However, recent disruptions to external funding, including cuts to United States Agency for International Development, a key supporter of Ethiopia’s NNP and Productive Safety Net Program (PSNP), have endangered nutrition-specific interventions and exacerbated risks for vulnerable populations.32

Existing evidence on child undernutrition in Ethiopia has grown considerably over the past two decades. Studies using the Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS) have documented regional disparities in stunting, underweight and wasting, highlighting rural residence, low maternal education, poverty and poor sanitation as key determinants.22–24 Community-based studies have similarly identified household food insecurity, inadequate complementary feeding and recurrent infections as proximal determinants of undernutrition.30 31 Despite this growing body of evidence, most prior Ethiopian studies have relied on cross-sectional designs, which capture undernutrition at a single point in time and are unable to capture the dynamic trajectories of nutritional status across childhood. Furthermore, the majority of existing analyses have modelled underweight, stunting and wasting as separate outcomes using independent regression models, thereby limiting insight into their longitudinal interplay, particularly how these three indicators co-occur, evolve over time and share common determinants across different stages of child development.

A study conducted in Nigeria addressed this gap partially by applying a multivariate regression model and identifying meaningful associations among the three anthropometric indicators.33 However, it did not explore the long-term consequences of these relationships, nor did it account for the within-child correlations inherent in repeated-measures data. To our knowledge, no prior study in Ethiopia has jointly modelled underweight, stunting and wasting longitudinally while accounting for the correlation structure across multiple time points.

To address this gap, the present study employs a multivariate generalised estimating equations (GEE) approach, which jointly models all three outcomes while explicitly accounting for within-child correlations across repeated measures. Using data from the Young Lives Cohort Survey (YLCS), a longitudinal cohort of 1997 Ethiopian children followed across five time points from ages 1 to 15 years (2002–2016), this study aims to: (1) describe the age-specific prevalence and temporal trajectories of underweight, stunting and wasting across childhood and adolescence in Ethiopia; (2) quantify the co-occurrence and pairwise associations among the three anthropometric outcomes over time and (3) identify child-level, household-level and environmental determinants jointly associated with underweight, stunting and wasting using a multivariate longitudinal framework.

The findings of this study are intended to inform researchers, policymakers and healthcare professionals in designing age-targeted, evidence-based interventions to address the multifaceted and dynamic burden of child undernutrition in Ethiopia.

Methods

Source of data, study design and participants

The study used a longitudinal Young Lives Cohort (YLC) of Ethiopian children aged 1–15 years, collected from 1997 children (of an initial 2000, with 1997 having complete anthropometric data) through five survey rounds (2002, 2006, 2009, 2013, 2016).34 35 For broader context, the YLC was designed to examine the effects of child poverty in low-income and middle-income countries (Ethiopia, Peru, Vietnam and India) during the first two decades of the 21st century. The dataset included sociodemographic and clinical profiles of children, mothers, households, siblings, communities and school classmates. The geographic distribution of the five study regions across Ethiopia (Tigray, Amhara, Oromiya, Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples’ Region (SNNP) and Addis Ababa City Administration) is shown in online supplemental figure S1.

Outcome variables

This study analysed three anthropometric profiles (underweight, stunting and wasting) as outcome variables. Undernutrition was assessed using the WHO 2006 Child Growth Standards: underweight was defined as WAZ-score <−2 SD, stunting as HAZ <−2 SD, and wasting operationalised as thinness, defined as a body mass index (BMI)-for-age Z-score (BAZ) <−2 SD Underweight and stunting were classified against the WHO 2006 Child Growth Standards. Because the WHO weight-for-height (wasting) standards are defined only for children aged ≤60 months, and the weight-for-age (underweight) standards only to 120 months (10 years), acute undernutrition beyond these age limits was operationalised using BMI-for-age (the WHO-recommended indicator of thinness for individuals aged 5–19 years), computed against the WHO 2007 growth reference. Accordingly, wasting corresponds to weight-for-height/BMI-for-age thinness at ages 1 and 5 (closely concordant at these ages) and to BMI-for-age thinness at ages 8, 12 and 15, while the underweight indicator was based on weight-for-age to age 10 and supplemented by BMI-for-age thinness at ages 12 and 15. Height-for-age (stunting) is defined across the full 0–19-year range and was applied consistently at all five waves. Each outcome was coded as a binary variable (0=normal nutritional status; 1=undernourished). Anthropometric measurements were collected by trained field enumerators using calibrated digital weighing scales and rigid stadiometers, following standardised WHO protocols at each survey round.

Independent variables

The study examined covariates across four domains, selected based on their previously confirmed association with child undernutrition33 36–39:

Child-level sex, age, BMI, caregiver-reported health status relative to peers (better/same/worse; a Likert-type measure) and caregiver-assessed general health status (good/average/poor).

Child-level sex, age, BMI, caregiver-reported health status relative to peers (better/same/worse; a Likert-type measure) and caregiver-assessed general health status (good/average/poor).

Maternal/caregiver: mother’s age, education level (literate/illiterate) and subjective well-being.

Maternal/caregiver: mother’s age, education level (literate/illiterate) and subjective well-being.

Household: household head’s sex and age, household size, wealth index and father’s education level.

Household: household head’s sex and age, household size, wealth index and father’s education level.

Infrastructure: access to safe drinking water, toilet facilities, electricity and cooking fuel, defined as a binary variable indicating use of any fuel for cooking, predominantly biomass sources including wood, charcoal or dung (coded 1=yes, 0=no).

Patient and public involvement

None. This study used previously collected, publicly available secondary data from the YLCS; no patients or members of the public were involved in the design, conduct or reporting of the present analysis.

Statistical analysis

Longitudinal cohort data involve repeated observations on the same individual over time, which introduces within-subject correlations that standard regression models cannot adequately handle. To jointly model the three correlated binary outcomes (underweight, stunting and wasting) while accounting for these within-child correlations across five time points, we employed a multivariate GEE approach with a logit link function.40 41

To clarify this modelling structure in response to reviewer query: the three anthropometric outcomes were not stacked into a single joint response vector with a common cross-outcome correlation matrix. Instead, a separate marginal GEE was fitted for each outcome (underweight, stunting and wasting), and within each outcome-specific model, the exchangeable working correlation captured the within-child correlation across the five repeated measurements. The three outcomes were thus estimated in parallel within a shared multivariate framework (the same cohort, covariate set, link function and correlation specification), rather than through unrelated, independently specified analyses. The interdependence among the three outcomes was then characterised directly and separately through their co-occurrence (table 1) and through wave-specific pairwise ORs (figure 1 and online supplemental table S7), which quantify how the outcomes cluster within the same children at each age. This combination, comprising outcome-specific marginal GEEs for covariate effects, complemented by explicit estimation of the between-outcome association structure, is what we denote by ‘multivariate GEE’ throughout this paper.

Covariance structure

To capture the correlation between repeated measures within each child, the GEE model requires specification of a working correlation matrix R(α), where α parameterises the within-subject correlations across time points and between the three outcomes at each time point. Four structures were evaluated: exchangeable, independent, autoregressive (AR-1) and unstructured. Model fit was assessed using the Quasi-likelihood under Independence Model Criterion (QIC), a metric suited for GEE models that balances goodness of fit and model complexity, with lower values indicating better performance.

The exchangeable correlation structure, which assumes constant correlation between any two time points for the same child, yielded the lowest QIC values across all three outcomes (underweight: 1230; stunting: 1175; wasting: 1145) and was, therefore, selected for the final model (see online supplemental table S4) for full comparison across structures). This structure implies that the correlation between a child’s nutritional status at any two time points is the same regardless of how far apart in time those observations are, consistent with the stable temporal dependencies observed in this cohort.

The full specification of the working correlation matrix and the Pearson residual computation used in the iterative fitting process are provided in online supplemental appendix.

Software

Data analysis was conducted using R (V.4.3.1), with the geepack package employed to fit the Multivariate GEE models and estimate parameters.42 All preprocessing, visualisation and model diagnostics were performed within the same R environment, ensuring a streamlined and reproducible analytical pipeline.

Results

Prevalence of child undernutrition across age groups

Online supplemental table S2a presents the age-stratified prevalence of child undernutrition across five survey waves. Overall, 49.6% of observations reflected normal nutritional status. Underweight prevalence peaked at age 12 (52.1%), stunting was highest at age 1 (42.5%) and persisted at 25.6% by age 15, and wasting peaked at age 12 (41.2%); full age-stratified estimates with 95% CIs are provided in online supplemental table S2a, and figure 2 gives a visual summary of these age-specific trends.

Bars show the prevalence (%) of underweight, stunting and wasting among Ethiopian children at each of five survey waves (ages 1, 5, 8, 12 and 15 years) from the Young Lives Cohort Survey. Error bars represent 95% CIs calculated using the normal approximation method. Sample: n=1997 children across five survey waves.

Sex-stratified and wealth-stratified prevalence estimates are presented in online supplemental table S2b and c, respectively. Males showed consistently higher prevalence of underweight (40.6%), stunting (34.0%) and wasting (27.1%) compared with females (33.4%, 25.9% and 21.9%, respectively). A strong wealth gradient was observed, with underweight prevalence declining from 49.1% in the lowest wealth tertile to 27.7% in the highest.

Overall, 37.2% of measurements were underweight, 30.2% stunted and 24.7% wasted. Between-subject prevalence (the proportion of children affected in at least one wave) was 68.9% for underweight, 62.0% for stunting and 58.6% for wasting. Within-subject prevalence (the mean proportion of waves in which an affected child was classified with the condition) was 54.7% for underweight, 50.8% for stunting and 42.0% for wasting, indicating moderate-to-high persistence for underweight and stunting and greater variability for wasting (online supplemental table S3).

Distribution and persistence of undernutrition

QIC values across the four correlation structures evaluated are reported in online supplemental table S4; the exchangeable structure was selected (see the Methods section).

Sample characteristics

As detailed in online supplemental table S1, 64.4% of children resided in rural areas and 35.6% in urban areas. Overall, 46.9% of caregivers, 32.8% of fathers, 46.7% of mothers and 35% of household heads had no formal education. At the household level, 40.1% lacked safe drinking water, 36.7% lacked toilet access, 50.5% lacked electricity and 90.2% lacked access to clean cooking fuel.

Co-occurrence of undernutrition outcomes

Table 1 illustrates the co-occurrence of undernutrition outcomes, highlighting their interdependence over five potential time points. Of 3524 underweight observations, 59.9% were also stunted, with 21.8% exhibiting both stunting and wasting (770 cases), suggesting a strong overlap between chronic and acute undernutrition. Among the 5954 observations without underweight, 88.7% were also free of stunting, yet only 12.6% showed wasting alone, indicating wasting’s rarity in isolation. Wasting occurred in 24.7% of all observations, with 66.8% of these instances linked to either underweight or stunting, underscoring its frequent concurrence with other deficits. This complex interplay, with 2861 stunting and 2337 wasting cases distributed unevenly across underweight status, illustrates the correlated nature of these three outcomes across the observation period.

Figure 1 shows pairwise associations between underweight, stunting and wasting over time via ORs. Underweight and stunting exhibit a strong, persistent link (OR peaking at 4.20 at age 8), while underweight-wasting weakens (OR 1.60 at age 1 to 1.40 at age 15), and stunting-wasting shows a consistent inverse trend (OR 0.40 to 0.30). Age-specific ORs are presented in online supplemental table S7.

The heatmaps (figure 3) illustrate the within-subject correlations of underweight, stunted and wasted statuses across five developmental stages in the Ethiopian cohort, revealing distinct temporal patterns in undernutrition persistence. For underweight, correlations are moderate to strong (0.261–0.568), peaking between age 5 and age 8 (0.568), suggesting that weight deficits in early childhood often persist into middle childhood. Stunting exhibits the strongest continuity, with correlations ranging from 0.279 to 0.609, peaking between age 12 and age 15 (0.609), indicating that height deficits become increasingly stable in adolescence. In contrast, wasting shows the weakest correlations (0.023–0.467), reflecting greater variability in acute nutritional status across time points. Across all outcomes, correlations weaken with greater temporal distance.

Bivariate GEE estimates

Online supplemental table S5 presents unadjusted bivariate GEE estimates. Higher wealth was associated with substantially lower odds of underweight (OR=0.15, 95% CI 0.10-0.22), stunting (OR=0.25, 95% CI 0.18 to 0.35) and wasting (OR=0.20, 95% CI 0.14 to 0.29). Rural residence was associated with increased odds of underweight (OR=1.45, 95% CI 1.22 to 1.72). Female sex was associated with lower odds across all outcomes (stunting OR=0.78, 95% CI 0.67 to 0.91). Child age had divergent effects, increasing wasting odds (OR=1.03) while slightly reducing stunting odds (OR=0.998), highlighting the divergent temporal trajectories of acute versus chronic undernutrition (online supplemental table S5).

Multivariate GEE estimates

Online supplemental table S6 presents the full adjusted multivariate GEE estimates, revealing a nuanced interplay of factors associated with undernutrition outcomes (underweight, stunting and wasting) across a longitudinal cohort. At the child level, gender emerged as a significant determinant: females consistently exhibited lower odds of underweight (OR=0.61, 95% CI 0.53 to 0.70), stunting (OR=0.65, 95% CI 0.57 to 0.75) and wasting (OR=0.41, 95% CI 0.36 to 0.47) compared to males. Child age displayed divergent effects: each additional year increased wasting odds by 5% (OR=1.05, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.06), suggesting heightened acute malnutrition risk over time, while slightly reducing stunting odds (OR=0.997, 95% CI 0.994 to 0.999), indicative of a slower chronic progression. Underweight odds rose modestly per year (OR=1.01, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.02), reflecting a mixed trajectory. BMI had a striking influence: higher BMI strongly protected against underweight (OR=0.59, 95% CI 0.56 to 0.62) and wasting (OR=0.12, 95% CI 0.10 to 0.14), yet increased stunting odds (OR=1.11, 95% CI 1.09 to 1.13).

Table 2 presents adjusted multivariate GEE estimates. Wealth index was the strongest protective factor (underweight OR=0.05, stunting OR=0.20, wasting OR=0.17). Access to cooking fuel (predominantly biomass) was associated with increased odds across all outcomes (underweight OR=1.55; stunting OR=1.48; wasting OR=1.39; table 2). Similarly, access to safe drinking water was associated with elevated odds (underweight OR=1.26; stunting OR=1.17; wasting OR=1.40; table 2).

Discussion

Age-specific temporal patterns and prevalence trends

This study documents a shifting burden of child undernutrition in Ethiopia from 2002 to 2016, with each anthropometric indicator exhibiting a distinct age-specific trajectory. Stunting predominated in early childhood and declined gradually into adolescence, consistent with cumulative growth faltering that originates in the first 1000 days of life.43 44 Underweight peaked sharply in preadolescence, while wasting surged in later childhood, a pattern not captured by the 2016 DHS, which is restricted to children under 5 and reported wasting at approximately 10% nationally.43 It should be noted that direct comparisons with DHS estimates should be made cautiously, as the DHS uses a cross-sectional design while the present study follows children longitudinally from age 1 to 15. The elevated wasting in later waves likely reflects cumulative exposure to acute stressors, including the 2015–2016 El Niño-induced drought.44 45 The particularly high wasting prevalence at age 12 (41.2%) further reflects its operationalisation: beyond age 5, wasting was measured as BMI-for-age thinness rather than weight-for-height, and the pubertal growth spurt at this age, in which rapid linear growth transiently outpaces weight gain, lowers BMI-for-age and mechanically elevates measured thinness, compounding the effect of contemporaneous acute food stress. These findings highlight the need for age-sensitive nutrition surveillance and programming that extends beyond age 5 into middle childhood and adolescence.

Co-occurrence and inter-relationships among anthropometric outcomes

The strong and persistent co-occurrence of underweight and stunting across all age groups is consistent with Victora et al, who documented that growth faltering peaks in the first 2 years of life in low-income countries and that cumulative early deficits underlie the co-occurrence of stunting and underweight,46 suggesting limited disruption of these cycles over the study period. Conversely, the consistent inverse relationship between stunting and wasting diverges from Martorell and Young,47 who described how stunting and wasting have distinct growth patterns and respond to different underlying risk factors, consistent with the distinct aetiologies observed in this cohort, which respond to different intervention strategies. The joint modelling approach adopted here was essential for revealing these complex interoutcome dynamics, which would be invisible in separate single-outcome analyses.

Sociodemographic determinants

Household wealth was the strongest protective factor across all three outcomes and all age groups, substantially exceeding the influence of parental education, thereby reinforcing material deprivation, rather than knowledge deficits alone, as the principal associated determinant of undernutrition in Ethiopia, consistent with broader evidence from low-income settings.45 The modest influence of maternal education contrasts with findings from India, where it was a strong independent predictor of stunting,48 possibly reflecting Ethiopia’s low baseline literacy levels and the enabling conditions required to translate education into nutritional outcomes. Female sex was consistently associated with lower undernutrition odds, mirroring the male disadvantage previously documented across SSA49 and attributed to cultural caregiving biases, greater male infection susceptibility and differential responses to environmental stressors; mechanisms warrant further gender-disaggregated investigation in Ethiopia.43 50 Mother’s subjective well-being offered modest but consistent protection, highlighting the potential value of integrating maternal mental health into child nutrition programming.

Environmental exposures and regional disparities

The counterintuitive positive associations of cooking fuel and safe drinking water access with undernutrition risk challenge the broader evidence from comparable low-income settings where improved water access and clean cooking fuel are generally associated with reduced child morbidity and undernutrition risk51 and likely reflect context-specific mechanisms. Biomass fuel exposure generates indoor air pollution linked to respiratory infections and reduced nutrient absorption,52 while reported water access may not reflect actual water quality, given documented gaps between infrastructure availability and water safety in rural Ethiopia.53 Three explanations warrant particular emphasis. First, measurement limitation: access to safe water and to specific cooking fuels was self-reported by caregivers at the household level and was not accompanied by water-quality testing or direct observation, so a reported improved source need not correspond to water that is microbiologically safe at the point of use; such exposure misclassification can attenuate or even reverse a true protective association. Second, residual and unmeasured confounding: although the model adjusts for wealth index and region, these variables imperfectly capture sanitation behaviour, food security, the local disease environment and programme targeting, and disadvantaged households are frequently those prioritised for water-infrastructure and safety-net interventions, which can induce a positive exposure–outcome association through reverse targeting. Third, contextual factors: improved-source water in this setting is often shared, intermittent or stored under conditions that permit recontamination, and biomass remains the dominant cooking fuel even among relatively better-off rural households, so the binary indicators available here capture nominal access rather than the quality and consistency of use that determine health impact. These positive associations should, therefore, be read as hypothesis-generating signals of residual confounding and exposure misclassification, not as evidence that safe water or particular fuels increase undernutrition risk. Regionally, all areas showed substantially higher undernutrition risk compared with Addis Ababa, reflecting urban advantages in food market integration, healthcare access and programme coverage. The superior fit of the exchangeable correlation structure across all outcomes is consistent with the stable temporal dependencies observed in this cohort, suggesting persistent structural determinants of undernutrition (poverty, regional inequality and biomass fuel dependence), which require structural interventions beyond acute supplementation alone.

Conclusions

This longitudinal study documents a shifting burden of child undernutrition in Ethiopia over 14 years, with each anthropometric indicator showing a distinct age-specific trajectory. Stunting was most prevalent in early childhood and declined gradually into adolescence, consistent with growth faltering concentrated in the first 1000 days of life. Underweight peaked sharply in preadolescence, while wasting escalated markedly in later childhood, a pattern not captured by cross-sectional surveys restricted to children under 5. A striking overlap was observed between underweight and stunting across all age groups, while stunting and wasting showed a consistent inverse relationship, reflecting their distinct underlying causes.

Household wealth emerged as the strongest protective factor against all three forms of undernutrition, substantially exceeding the influence of parental education, reinforcing material deprivation, rather than knowledge deficits alone, as the principal associated determinant of child undernutrition in Ethiopia. Female sex was consistently associated with lower odds of underweight, stunting and wasting compared with males, highlighting a persistent gender disparity that warrants further gender-disaggregated investigation. Marked regional disparities were evident, with children in all four regions showing substantially higher undernutrition risk compared with Addis Ababa. Biomass fuel dependence and inadequate access to safe water emerged as additional modifiable environmental risk factors.

These findings underscore the need for age-targeted, wealth-sensitive interventions that extend nutrition programming well beyond the conventional under-5 focus into middle childhood and adolescence.

Strengths and limitations

The longitudinal design and large cohort spanning five survey rounds provide robust insights into undernutrition dynamics across childhood. However, reliance on self-reported access to water and cooking fuel may overestimate actual availability. Key determinants (including household food security, breastfeeding practices and dietary diversity) were unavailable at all time points and could not be modelled, limiting the comprehensiveness of the analysis. Child BMI shares mathematical components with the weight-for-age and weight-for-height outcome definitions; a sensitivity analysis excluding BMI yielded broadly consistent findings (VIF <5 for all variables), suggesting acceptable collinearity. The Ethiopian cohort focus limits generalisability, though findings resonate with broader sub-Saharan African trends.

Finally, because this is an observational cohort study, all reported associations (including those for household wealth, child sex and environmental exposures) should be interpreted as statistical associations rather than causal effects. Residual and unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded, and terms such as determinant, correlate and protective association are used throughout in this associational sense rather than to imply direct causation.

Implications and future directions

The findings call for multisectoral responses that integrate nutrition-sensitive economic support, poverty reduction strategies and improvements in water purification and sanitation infrastructure. Age-sensitive programming that extends surveillance and targeted interventions to school-age children and adolescents (groups currently underserved by most nutrition programmes) is particularly warranted. Future studies should incorporate household food security status, breastfeeding practices, and individual dietary diversity scores to more fully characterise the determinants of undernutrition across the full arc of childhood.
