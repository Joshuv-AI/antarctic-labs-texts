Seismic reflectivity inversion with mixed L1-L2 norm regularization
Authors: Jun Yang, Ronghuo Dai, Yang Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeismic reflectivity and the resulting elastic parameters can be used for mining engineering, mineral reservoir prediction, and other fields related to underground mineral resources. Seismic reflectivity is common obtained from post-stack seismic data. It is the so called seismic reflectivity inversion (SRI). Sparse regularization-based SRI (sparse-SRI) is a method capable of estimating reflectivity from seismic data, thereby enhancing the resolution of the seismic data. However, the current sparse-SRI method encounters two main challenges: (1) sparse regularization tends to cause a significant increase or numerical oscillation in the residual, which can negatively impact the inversion outcomes; (2) it provides inadequate preservation of small reflectivity components present in real seismic data. To address these limitations, this paper introduces a new mixed-norm regularization approach for SRI. The proposed method modifies the objective function by introducing the mixed-norm term that encompasses both the L1-norm and L2-norm of the model. Mixed-norm regularization is a well-established concept in statistics and machine learning for over a decade. Considered the gradual variation of underground geological features, we also add the L2-norm of the model gradients in the mixed-norm for further vertical smoothness. By tuning the weighting parameter that balances L1-norm and L2-norm regularization, one can control the sparsity of the solutions. Increasing the L1-norm’s weight favors the creation of more sparse and concentrated models. Hence, this framework efficiently reconciles the sparsity of large reflectivity features with the smoothness of small reflectivity components. Synthetic tests show that the mixed L1-L2 norm inversion produces more compact and accurate reflectivity distributions compared to conventional single norm inversion, achieving better vertical resolution and minimizing the underestimation of model values. Field testing using actual seismic data from West China provides further validation of the algorithm’s effectiveness. The application of mixed L1-L2 norm inversion results in reflectivity series distributions that are consistent with established geological structures, evidencing enhanced vertical confinement relative to conventional L1-norm or L2-norm inversion techniques.Keywords: Seismic reflectivity inversion, Mineral reservoir prediction, Sparse regularization, Mixed L1-L2 normSubject terms: Engineering, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Seismic reflectivity and the resulting elastic parameters can be used for mining engineering, mineral reservoir prediction, and other fields related to underground mineral resources. Hence, achieving higher resolution in seismic data is essential for effective seismic exploration in the fields of mineral energy. High-resolution seismic data facilitate the derivation of high-resolution subsurface elastic parameter models, such as impedance, seismic wave velocity, and density1–3. Seismic reflectivity inversion (SRI) is a method capable of enhancing seismic data resolution. Grounded in the principle that a seismic trace represents the convolution of a seismic wavelet with a reflectivity series4, the objective of SRI is to derive the underlying reflectivity series from the actual observed seismic data through the mitigation of the wavelet effect.

Nevertheless, the application of SRI is complicated by its ill-posed nature. A well-established strategy for mitigating the ill-posedness of inverse problems involves the inclusion of suitable regularization terms within the objective function5. In the context of SRI, the imposition of a sparsity constraint on the reflectivity series—termed sparse-SRI—represents a prevalent regularization method. The efficacy of this technique is well-documented in the existing literature6–8. Hence, the sparse-spike inversion method estimates a set of sparse reflection coefficients from the seismic data and constrains them with the model to produce the impedance from inverting these coefficients2,3.

The application of sparse regularization serves a dual purpose: it bolsters the stability of the inversion results and provides a more faithful representation of the sparsity characteristic inherent in seismic data. A variety of effective techniques are available for implementing sparse regularization, such as the L1-norm6, L0-norm9, weighted modified L1-norm or log regularization10, Cauchy-norm regularization11, and modified Cauchy-norm regularization12,13, among others. In the context of improving seismographic deconvolution, Sacchi studied Cauchy regularization and achieved successful retrieval of sparse, broadband reflectivity series11. To address the inverse problem in the context of pre-stack seismic data, Zhang and Dai employed a modified Cauchy-norm regularization and a variable measure algorithm13. With generalized Cauchy distribution (i.e. student distribution), Aravkin et al. studied robust seismic data processing and inversion14. Li and Zhang proposed a pre-stack seismic data amplitude inversion using total variation regularization, which is fundamentally equivalent to L1-norm regularization on the gradient of the model parameters15. To enhance sparsity in the presence of degeneracy, Candes et al. introduced a weighted modified L1-norm regularization10. Given the L0-norm possesses optimal sparsity, Dai and Yang utilized it in seismic impedance inversion9.

Nevertheless, the inherent problems associated with sparse regularization, specifically, inducing a sharp increase or numerical oscillations in the residual that compromises inversion outcomes, and its tendency to protect large coefficients while suppressing small ones10,16,17, result in the sparse-SRI suppressing certain weak reflectors4,18. Sparse-SRI uses sparse-spike assumption of a single trace inversion3. For example, the mathematical model used in the construction of the algorithm is the minimization of the L1-norm. L1- norm is minimized rather than L2-norm because the L1-norm is defined as the sum of the absolute values of the seismic trace, while the L2-norm is defined as the square root of the sum of the squares of the seismic trace values. L1-norm is usually used for large reflectivity and sequence boundaries, while L2-norm is used for small reflectivity. Consequently, the high resolution of the sparse, spike-like reflectivity series comes at the expense of diminished sensitivity to small reflectivity values.

Actually, the reflectivity series representing underground formations does not exhibit strict sparsity. It contains not only substantial large reflectivity series that align with the primary stratigraphic sequence interfaces but also minor reflectivity series signifying weak reflectors associated with thin interbeds between the larger reflectivity features. This means that while significant reflectivity values are sparse, minor reflectivity values exhibit a dense and smooth distribution.

Considered the limitations of L1-norm and L2-norm based methods, in this study, a novel SRI approach is presented for the purpose of enhancing the resolution of post-stack seismic data. In fact, mixed-norm regularization (like elastic-net, which combines L1-norm and L2-norm of model parameters18,19 is a well-established concept in statistics and machine learning for over a decade. Recent years have seen the extension of mixed L1-L2 norm inversion into areas such as the uncertainty analysis associated with deterministic inversion20, probabilistic geological differentiation21, and the assessment of physical property relationship recoverability22. This highlights its wide-ranging application prospects. The integration of L1-norm and L2-norm into a unified inversion framework (mixed L1-L2 norm regularization) is both novel and practically useful.

The proposed method in this paper modifies the objective function by introducing a mixed-norm term to SRI that encompasses both the L1-norm and L2-norm of the model. Considered the gradual variation of underground geological features, we also add the L2-norm of the model gradients in the mixed-norm for further vertical smoothness. The topic is relevant to both academic research and industrial applications in sub-surface characterization. Based on the augmented Least Squares formula23, we are able to perform the mixed L1-L2 norm inversion concurrently under a single integrated framework. Relative to conventional single-norm inversion methods such as L2-norm and L1-norm, the mixed L1-L2 norm inversion approach generates superior outcomes, exhibiting diminished numerical oscillation effects and effectively addresses known limitations of traditional sparse inversion techniques, particularly in balancing the recovery of strong and weak reflectivity components. The mixed L1-L2 norm based SRI achieves robust recovery of both large and small reflectivity features. Furthermore, application to field seismic data demonstrates that it provides a viable and effective alternative method for improving seismic resolution.

Sparse seismic reflectivity inversion

The fundamental model in seismology posits that observed seismic data is generated by convolving the subsurface formation’s reflectivity series with the source wavelet4. SRI exhibits considerable sensitivity to noise present in seismic observations. The inversion process frequently yields unstable results, where a small quantity of noise can induce significant perturbations in the estimated reflectivity series. Hence, it is necessary to introduce regularization and other a priori information as constraints within the inversion framework to ensure the dependability of the inversion outcomes7. Sparse regularization is a commonly used technique in seismic inversion. When applied, this approach is often referred to as sparse-SRI. Its underlying principle assumes that the reflectivity series of underground formations can be represented as a superposition of two components: a sparse distribution of strong reflectors and a background of weaker, less significant reflectors. Seismic wavelets are estimated employing different techniques. Thereafter, the spike-like reflectivity series is derived by deconvolving the seismic data with the application of sparse regularization. Subsequently, the derived reflectivity series can be employed to compute the impedance of subsurface formations and additional elastic properties. Hence, the resolution and fidelity of the inverted reflectivity series, as determined from seismic data, are of paramount importance in the petroleum sector.

SRI with mixed L1-L2 norm regularization

The application of sparse regularization inherently prioritizes the preservation of large reflectivity coefficients and suppresses those of smaller magnitude. As a result, seismic inversion techniques employing sparse regularization are limited in their ability to estimate weak reflectors1. The perceived high resolution of the generated reflectivity series, characterized by its sparse, spike-like nature, is therefore obtained by sacrificing the fidelity of smaller reflectivity components.

Observation of actual well logs reveals that the reflectivity series of subsurface formations is not strictly sparse. As an example, Fig. 1 presented in the subsequent section shows the reflectivity series calculated from an actual impedance well log. The dominant large reflectivity components exhibit sparsity, aligning with primary stratigraphic sequence interfaces in the subsurface, such as unconformities and lithological boundaries. In contrast, many smaller reflectivity values are present, signifying weak reflectors associated with thin interbeds situated between the major reflectors. Consequently, large reflectivity is sparse, whereas the minor reflectivity series is distributed densely and smoothly.

Applications and results

Synthetic seismic data tests

We begin by testing SRI with mixed L1-L2 norm regularization using a synthetic seismic trace, focusing on its effects and comparing them to those of traditional single-norm inversion methods (e.g., L2-norm, L1-norm). As depicted in Fig. 1, the reflectivity series was calculated based on an actual impedance well log. The synthetic seismic data, resulting from the convolution of this reflectivity series with a 40 Hz Ricker wavelet, is presented in Fig. 2a. We then proceeded to apply three different regularized inversion methods to the synthetic data: SRI-L1-L2 (SRI with mixed L1-L2 norm), SRI-L1 (sparse-SRI with L1-norm), and SRI-L2 (SRI with L2-norm). The inversion commenced with an initial zero solution. Subsequently, the inverted reflectivity series from each of the three methods were plotted, appearing in Fig. 3a and b, and 3c respectively. Throughout Fig. 3, actual reflectivity is depicted by black curves and inverted reflectivity by red curves.

The synthetic seismic trace from the convolution between a 40 Hz Ricker wavelet and the reflectivity series shown in Fig. 1. (a) noise-free synthetic trace; (b) noise-contaminated trace with SNR = 4; (c) noise-contaminated trace with SNR = 1.

The inverted reflectivity series from the noise-free synthetic trace. (a) SRI-L1-L2; (b) SRI-L2; (c) SRI-L1.

As observed in Fig. 3, the inverted reflectivity series from all three methods generally align well with the actual reflectivity and adhere closely to the relative trend of the well log. Nevertheless, notable discrepancies in finer details are apparent between the results of the three inversion techniques. SRI-L2, for instance, causes over-oscillation in the inverted series, distorting major reflections and blurring the main stratigraphic boundary. In contrast, SRI-L1 offers a more focused solution with a sparse, high-resolution output. But this benefit is offset by the over-suppression of small reflectivity events and the complete removal of certain interbeds. Ultimately, SRI-L1-L2 strikes the best balance, recovering both large and small reflectivity features well and providing the most accurate match to the actual reflectivity series.

Field data applications: case one

After evaluating SRI-L1-L2 using synthetic seismic data, its practical application was tested on a real seismic data profile shown in Fig. 5. This profile is acquired from East China and provided by an actual oilfield survey. The main geological feature of this seismic profile is the presence of a major fault. Due to the lateral sealing of the fault, the favorable reservoirs are located on both sides of the fault. The aim of this case study is to obtain reflectivity series profile with high resolution to better depict the distribution of the strata on both sides of the fault and their contact relationship with the fault.

The original seismic data profile.

Subsequently, inversions were performed on the seismic data profile using SRI-L1-L2, SRI-L1, and SRI-L2 algorithms. The initialization of the inversion procedure involved the following methodology: An interpolated impedance model was first constructed via local kriging estimation, utilizing actual impedance log curves from the project area and incorporating constraints from seismic interpretation horizons. This interpolated model was subsequently subjected to smoothing using a high-cut filter with a 15 Hz cutoff frequency, yielding an impedance model characterized by its low-frequency trend. The reflectivity series computed from this low-frequency impedance model was then designated as the initial solution for the inversion process.

The inverted reflectivity series profiles resulting from SRI-L1-L2, SRI-L2, and SRI-L1 are presented in Figs. 6 and 7, and 8, respectively. It is evident that the reflectivity series obtained via SRI-L1-L2 closely matches the original seismic data, maintaining desirable structural and stratigraphic patterns. Notably, it captures not only the major reflectivity events but also the smaller intervening features. This results in an inverted profile with clear vertical and spatial variations, which is valuable for guiding future reservoir prediction and characterization work. For example, the breakpoints of the major fault are very clear. The lateral variation of reflectivity series obtained via SRI-L1-L2 is highly beneficial for the lateral tracking of the layers on both sides of the fault, especially for some weak reflectors. Conversely, the inversion results from SRI-L2 also demonstrate a good match with the original seismic data, preserving important structural and stratigraphic features. Yet, the output is overly smoothed, indicating that the goal of enhancing resolution was not met. For example, the breakpoints of the major fault are still very dim like the original seismic data profile. While the SRI-L1-inverted reflectivity series matches the original seismic data well structurally and stratigraphically, it fails to capture many small or weak reflectivity events. This indicates a trade-off: the perceived high resolution of SRI-L1 is compromised by the loss of these smaller-scale features.

Discussions

Similar to many inversion techniques, the determination of suitable regularization parameters plays a vital role in obtaining a satisfactory solution during seismic inversion. The mixed L1-L2 norm regularization employed herein essentially integrates the strengths of both sparse and dense regularization. Therefore, the efficacy of the mixed L1-L2 norm regularization is governed by three specific parameters , and δ. Conventionally, the selection of suitable regularization parameters relies heavily on the L-curve criterion23,28,29, which identifies the best parameter as the one closest to the corner of the L-curve. Nevertheless, this widely adopted method proves unsuitable for the SRI-L1-L2 technique. In the inversion process, the parameters governing the mixed L1-L2 norm regularization serve to balance the sparsity against the density within the reflectivity series. Consequently, the determination of these parameters is contingent upon both the real-world geological characteristics of the underground formations and the specific objectives pursued by the seismic inversion.

When the subsurface formations are characterized by significant thickness, or when the primary goal of the inversion is to delineate the major stratigraphic sequence boundaries, a larger value for the regularization parameter is required. Under these conditions, the process essentially functions as sparse-spike inversion. On the contrary, when thin interbeds—crucial for reservoir characterization—are present within the subsurface formations, it becomes necessary to comparatively increase the value of . Regarding δ, its primary function is to regulate the smoothness of the resulting reflectivity series. Consequently, when the actual small-scale reflectivity variations exhibit a smooth pattern, the value assigned to δ can be appropriately increased.

This paper utilizes a quality control methodology for the determination of regularization parameters. It is essential to have accessible actual well logs that accurately depict the true geological conditions of the underground formations. The determination of the optimal regularization parameter values involves conducting quality control procedures at well locations. This entails iteratively adjusting the parameter values, obtaining inversion results for each configuration, and then choosing the parameter set whose corresponding inversion result exhibits the closest agreement with the well log data.

Theoretically, under the condition of reasonable regularization parameters and effective convergence, the quality of inversion results can be guaranteed. From the above discussions and the texts in field data applications, the proposed method actually relies on the well control to some extent. I.e., we use quality control for the determination of regularization parameters based on accessible actual well logs, and adopt the interpolated model with actual well log curves as the initial solution. Hence, the proposed method is much more feasible in areas having a certain number of wells. It is a limitation.

In this study, we employ the ISTA to solve SRI-L1-L2. The primary rationale for choosing this algorithm is its straightforward iteration process and the relative ease of programming implementation. From the flowchart of ISTA listed in the text, the extra computational expense of SRI-L1-L2 per iteration, as opposed to the traditional SRI-L1, is limited to calculation of DTD. SRI-L2 can be solved just needed only once damped least square. Its computational time is much less than SRI-L1-L2 and SRI-L1. Conclusions

SRI stands as a critical technology in the domain of mineral reservoir prediction, mining engineering, and other fields related to underground mineral resources. Consequently, the resolution quality of the reflectivity series holds direct sway over the accuracy of ensuing mineral reservoir prediction and description efforts. Sparse-SRI methodologies incorporate sparse regularization techniques specifically designed to impose a sparse structure upon the inverted reflectivity series. This approach successfully yields reflectivity series with high resolution and distinct, spike-like characteristics; however, this enhanced resolution is acquired by sacrificing the fidelity of smaller-scale reflectivity components. Actually, the reflectivity series representing underground formations does not exhibit strict sparsity. It comprises not only sparse, significant reflectivity components that align with the primary stratigraphic sequence interfaces, but also numerous smaller reflectivity components signifying weak reflections from thin interbeds situated between these major features.

In this study, we utilize mixed L1-L2 norm regularization as a substitute for traditional sparse regularization. The mixed L1-L2 norm essentially represents an efficient synthesis of sparse and dense regularization techniques. Consequently, the SRI-L1-L2 method benefits from the strengths inherent in both sparse and dense regularization approaches. The performance of the methods was evaluated using synthetic seismic data. Results indicate that SRI-L2 tends to over-amplify major reflectivity events, leading to an overly oscillatory inversion. Conversely, SRI-L1 produces a sparser solution, albeit at the cost of attenuating smaller reflectivity components. Notably, the SRI-L1-L2 approach achieves robust recovery of both large and small reflectivity features. Furthermore, application to field seismic data demonstrates that SRI-L1-L2 provides a viable and effective alternative method for improving seismic resolution.
