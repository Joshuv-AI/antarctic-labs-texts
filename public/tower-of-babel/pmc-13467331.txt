Making Veterans Visible in Australia's Alcohol and Other Drug Services: Leveraging the Alcohol and Other Drug National Quality Framework
Authors: Jane Fischer, Andrew Prevett, Kirrilly Thompson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMany veterans seek alcohol and other drug (AOD) care in mainstream services, where veteran status is not routinely identified. This limits the sector's capacity to recognise veterans' needs, plan appropriate responses and assess outcomes. This commentary examines the implications of the Royal Commission into Defence and Veteran Suicide for Australia's AOD sector. It argues that embedding veteran identification within existing accreditation processes under the AOD National Quality Framework offers a practical means of strengthening military cultural capability and improving responsiveness to veterans' needs. Although this approach does not resolve all structural challenges, it provides a concrete starting point for improving recognition and care for veterans within Australia's AOD treatment system.

Introduction

Australia is home to nearly 600,000 veterans, including both current and former serving members of the Australian Defence Force [1]. For many veterans, the effects of military service can include substantial alcohol and other drug (AOD) and mental health concerns [2, 3]. For example, 17% of veterans reported a mental disorder in the previous 12 months [2, 4]. Rates are substantially higher amongst those who have transitioned from full‐time service, with anxiety disorders affecting 37%, including 18% with post‐traumatic stress disorder, followed by affective disorders (23%) and alcohol use disorder (13%) [5]. These conditions frequently co‐occur and are associated with elevated suicidality [5, 6]. In the 2022–2023 National Drug Strategy Household Survey, 33% of veterans exceeded the lifetime risk guideline for alcohol consumption, while 46% reported lifetime illicit drug use [7]. Together, these figures highlight the disproportionate burden of AOD‐related harm amongst veterans and the importance of ensuring that mainstream services can identify and respond effectively to their needs.

Veterans' clinical needs have direct implications for AOD treatment. The frequent overlap of substance use with post‐traumatic stress disorder, depression and suicidality requires routine screening for these conditions, trauma‐informed care and ongoing suicide risk management [6, 8]. Engagement may also depend on clinicians' understanding of military culture, which can shape veterans' identity, stigma and help‐seeking behaviour [6, 8, 9].

In recommendations 72 and 73, the Royal Commission into Defence and Veteran Suicide (RCDVS) called for expanded, better‐integrated healthcare services for veterans and stronger military cultural competency amongst health professionals in mainstream settings [10]. Realising this intent within Australia's AOD sector depends on improving the identification of veterans and ensuring that their needs are addressed. However, many providers in mainstream settings lack awareness of the veterans in their care and the unique challenges arising from military service [6]. The AOD National Quality Framework (NQF), a national policy mechanism that requires treatment providers to be accredited against an approved quality standard, is well placed to advance recommendations 72 and 73 [11, 12].

The Knowledge Gap

While the Department of Veterans' Affairs (DVA) is the legislated support agency for Australian veterans, not all veterans with AOD‐related needs access care through this system [13]. Veterans can lodge claims with DVA for recognition of service‐related injuries and health conditions, with accepted claims enabling access to funded healthcare that is largely delivered through mainstream providers [6, 13]. Claims are assessed against Statements of Principles, legislative instruments determined by the Repatriation Medical Authority that specify the factors required to connect a condition, such as alcohol use disorder, to service [14]. If liability for a condition is accepted, veterans can access DVA‐funded treatment through Veteran Cards, along with rehabilitation, compensation payments and income support [13]. Some supports do not require an accepted claim: veterans with at least 1 day of continuous full‐time service can apply for fully funded mental health treatment, including for AOD conditions, under non‐liability healthcare arrangements [15].

The claims process can be difficult to navigate and can exacerbate distress [10], and veterans may face delays in seeking support and barriers in navigating post‐service systems of care [16, 17]. Moreover, many veterans do not lodge claims [5], while others may not meet the relevant Statements of Principles for acceptance. In practice, whether their care is DVA‐funded or not, veterans with AOD‐related needs are treated predominantly within mainstream services [6, 13], and DVA data capture only those who engage with the department, which amounts to around half of Australia's ex‐serving veterans [16]. Notably, of all ex‐serving veterans who died by suicide between 2002 and 2021, more than 70% were not DVA clients at the time of their death [16].

Despite their unique needs [18], there are limited mechanisms for identifying veterans when they engage with mainstream AOD services. For example, the National AOD Minimum Dataset does not include a veteran identifier, nor do many Primary Health Network‐commissioned AOD services. This limits the ability of services to monitor veterans' demand, access and outcomes, let alone provide care tailored to this population. Identifying veterans in mainstream AOD services therefore serves a dual purpose. For individual veterans, it is the first step towards culturally informed care and improved treatment outcomes [6, 18], while for the sector, it generates the data needed to plan services and develop the workforce. Addressing this gap requires embedding veteran identification into existing service infrastructure, and the NQF offers exactly that.

Discussion

Leveraging the NQF for Improved Veteran Care

A practical and immediately achievable way to make veterans visible in mainstream AOD services is for organisations to systematically collect veteran‐related data within existing accreditation processes under the NQF. The NQF comprises two key components: guiding principles and a set of approved accreditation standards, supported by arrangements for monitoring and compliance [11, 12]. Its accreditation standards are performance‐based, meaning they specify the outcomes services must achieve while allowing providers flexibility in how each standard is met. Veteran identification would therefore sit not within the standards themselves but in how providers implement them, offering one way for services to demonstrate that they meet existing requirements relating to diversity and high‐risk groups. Through this approach, service providers could implement systems to identify veterans at intake and incorporate veteran‐specific considerations into service delivery. Identification does not itself change practice, but it is the necessary first step towards care that is person‐centred, culturally safe and trauma‐informed, consistent with the RCDVS recommendations and existing AOD sector standards.

This gives services a concrete starting point for embedding veteran identification within accreditation activity they already undertake. The underlying NSQHS actions concern diversity and high‐risk groups generally, so the same approach could be adapted to other populations who remain largely invisible in mainstream AOD services, such as first responders, who experience similar patterns of occupational trauma, stigma and AOD‐related harm [9].

Government funding obligations further strengthen the NQF's reach. Contractual requirements mandate that government‐funded providers obtain accreditation against approved standards [11], and a review found that 72% of government‐funded providers held accreditation to a listed standard, compared with 44% of non‐funded providers [12]. The NQF's guiding principles also require organisations to establish partnerships that address clients' wider circumstances through referral and collaboration, an expectation that could readily encompass veterans and the agencies that support them [10, 11]. Together, these obligations and principles mean veteran identification can be embedded through commitments providers already carry, rather than requiring new regulation or investment.

The accreditation figures above also reveal the proposal's limitations. When last assessed, not all providers were accredited against an NQF‐listed standard, particularly those receiving no government funding [12]. Veterans attending services not accredited under the NQF would therefore remain invisible to any mechanism embedded in accreditation processes. For these veterans, neither the individual nor the sector‐level benefits of identification would be realised. This patchy coverage partly reflects how the framework is enforced. Although the accreditation requirement applies to all providers [11], enforcement relies largely on funding agreements [11, 12]. This leaves services outside the government‐funded system with limited direct oversight. Even where compliance processes do operate, their effectiveness remains untested. Identification further depends on disclosure, and some veterans may choose not to reveal their service history [6, 8]. These gaps do not diminish the case for embedding veteran identification within accreditation processes, but they highlight the need for complementary structural reforms, including a veteran identifier in the National AOD Minimum Dataset and requirements within Primary Health Network commissioning.

Conclusion

Embedding veteran identification and related quality measures within the NQF offers a practical, system‐level mechanism for operationalising the RCDVS's recommendations 72 and 73 within Australia's AOD sector. By making veterans visible, this approach could strengthen military cultural capability, support more responsive and culturally safe care, and generate the data needed to inform service planning and treatment pathways for veterans in mainstream services. While such complementary structural reforms remain important, leveraging existing accreditation processes provides a concrete and immediate starting point. We invite AOD sector stakeholders, policymakers and researchers to engage with this framework and consider how it might be implemented and evaluated within their own contexts.
