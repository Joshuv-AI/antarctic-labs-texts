Exploring black soil microbiota for industrial enzymes: cellulase and xylanase characterization from Bacillus subtilis SSMK413
Authors: Shweta Shah, Bairagi C. Mallick, Bhagwati Devi, Manoj Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe efficient degradation of lignocellulosic biomass is essential for sustainable bioprocessing industries, including biofuel production and agro-waste valorization. This study screened bacterial isolates from black soils in Jharkhand, India, to identify strains producing cellulolytic and xylanolytic enzymes with industrial potential. Bacillus subtilis SSMK413 was selected for its coproduction of cellulase and xylanase, and the enzymes were purified and characterized for biochemical and kinetic properties. Additionally, their application in lignocellulosic biomass saccharification was evaluated to assess industrial relevance.ResultsAmong the screened bacterial isolates, Bacillus subtilis strain SSMK413, isolated from the Baulia region of Jharkhand (24.131955°N, 85.546774°E), exhibited robust coproduction of cellulase and xylanase. Primary screening on carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) and xylan agar plates confirmed its enzymatic activities, with molecular identification via 16 S rRNA gene sequencing verifying its taxonomic classification. Optimal enzyme production was achieved after 48 h of incubation. Enzyme purification was performed using ammonium sulfate precipitation, dialysis, and Sephadex G-100 gel filtration chromatography, resulting in purification folds of 14.3 for cellulase and 11.5 for xylanase. Biochemical characterization indicated optimal activities at 40–50 °C, with pH optima of 5.0 for cellulase and 6.0 for xylanase. Enzyme activities were significantly enhanced by Mn²⁺, Mg²⁺, and Fe³⁺ ions, whereas Cs⁺ and EDTA acted as inhibitors. Kinetic studies revealed a lower Km for cellulase (0.192 mg mL− 1) compared to xylanase (2.615 mg mL− 1), indicating higher substrate affinity for cellulase. SDS-PAGE and zymogram analyses determined molecular masses of 55–65 kDa for cellulase and 240–270 kDa for xylanase, suggesting a monomeric structure for cellulase and a multimeric complex for xylanase.ConclusionsB. subtilis (SSMK413) is a promising candidate for industrial enzyme production due to its efficient coproduction of cellulase and xylanase with favorable biochemical properties. The microbial diversity from black soils in Jharkhand represents a valuable genetic resource for future biotechnological exploration. Further studies should focus on enzyme immobilization and genetic enhancement to improve catalytic efficiency and operational stability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12934-025-02898-4.

Introduction

The accelerating global shift toward sustainable industrial biotechnology has intensified the search for efficient biocatalysts capable of valorizing lignocellulosic biomass, the most abundant renewable resource on Earth. This pursuit is fueled not only by dwindling fossil fuel reserves but also by mounting regulatory mandates, climate change concerns, and a growing emphasis on bio-based economies and carbon-neutral technologies [1–4]. Lignocellulose, composed principally of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, presents an immense untapped reservoir that requires robust, nature-inspired enzymatic systems for sustainable conversion.

Central to this strategy are microbial cellulases and xylanases, which act synergistically to dismantle the complex matrix of plant cell walls. Cellulases hydrolyze the linear β-1,4-glucan chains of cellulose into fermentable glucose units, while xylanases degrade the heterogeneous xylan backbone, liberating xylose and other sugars. Together, they enable comprehensive biomass saccharification, a critical step toward producing biofuels, platform chemicals, and other bioproducts [5–8]. Indeed, microbial lignocellulolytic enzymes have shown great promise in facilitating the conversion of plant residues into valuable end-products, as thoroughly reviewed in recent literature [5, 9].

The industrial applications of these enzymes are wide-ranging. In the second-generation biofuel sector, enzymatic hydrolysis remains a significant cost driver, elevating the necessity for catalysts with enhanced activity, tolerance, and reusability [10–14]. In pulp and paper industries, xylanases aid bleaching processes, reducing chlorine consumption and improving fiber properties, a more eco-friendly alternative to harsh chemical treatments [15–18]. The textile industry benefits from cellulases in fabric softening and bio-polishing, enhancing material quality [19]. In animal feed production, inclusion of xylanase and cellulase aids nutrient assimilation and feed conversion efficiency [20–22]. Furthermore, agro-waste valorization through these enzymes supports circular bioeconomy models, converting residues into fermentable sugars and bioplastics [19, 23].

Historically, fungal producers such as Trichoderma reesei and various Aspergillus species have dominated industrial enzyme manufacturing due to their exceptional secretion capabilities [24–26]. Strains like T. reesei have been engineered into hyperproducers through mutagenesis and recombinant approaches, enabling up to 20-fold cellulase yield increases [10, 27, 28]. Nonetheless, fungal enzymes often exhibit poor stability under alkaline or thermophilic conditions, may carry excessive glycosylation, and raise production costs, limiting their utility in demanding industrial processes [29–33].

In contrast, bacterial enzymes, particularly from Bacillus spp., offer superior thermostability, alkaline tolerance, salt resistance, and relatively simpler expression profiles, ideal for industrial deployment [34–38]. In-depth structural and functional comparisons reveal that bacterial xylanases (often GH10 family), as opposed to fungal GH11 xylanases, generally exhibit more rigid, β-sheet–rich structures allowing stability in extreme conditions [6]. They also frequently demonstrate enhanced substrate specificity and efficiency in pulp bleaching due to aggressive binding domains [6, 16].

Among these bacterial hosts, Bacillus subtilis stands out as an exceptional microbial cell factory. It holds GRAS (Generally recognized as safe) status, exhibits rapid growth (fermentation cycles as short as ~ 48 h compared to ~ 180 h in Saccharomyces cerevisiae), and boasts versatile genetic tools and strong extracellular protein secretion systems [9, 39–43]. Its secretion pathways including Sec, Tat, and ABC transporters, enable efficient enzyme export [1, 44]. Notably, B. subtilis accounts for a significant share of the industrial enzyme market, producing cellulase, xylanase, amylases, proteases, and more [1, 45].

Recent advances in metabolic engineering and synthetic biology have further elevated B. subtilis’ potential. CRISPR-based editing, promoter fine-tuning, signal peptide optimization, and secretion pathway engineering have enabled dramatically improved enzyme export yields and performance [3, 39, 46]. For example, engineered xylanase systems have achieved activity levels of 38 U/mL using a SigB-dependent auto-inducible promoter [3], while optimized constructs for enzymes like alginate lyase have reached nearly 18 U/mL in WB600 strains [3]. Phosphate limitation strategies further boosted maltotetraose amylase activity by over 100% with shortened fermentation times [3].

Despite these advances, the search for novel, robust lignocellulolytic enzymes continues, especially from underexplored environmental niches. Black soils, also known as regur or chernozem, offer a compelling but undervalued reservoir. Rich in humus and organic carbon, these fertile soils harbor complex microbial ecosystems shaped by monsoonal nutrient cycles, seasonal moisture variation, and high organic matter turnover [38, 47–50]. Regions such as Jharkhand, India, contain such soils, yet their microbial richness, particularly regarding cellulase and xylanase producers, remains largely unexplored.

Emerging studies from similar habitats suggest the presence of microbes with cold-adapted, alkali-stable, or thermostable lignocellulolytic enzymes [9, 44, 51, 52]. Harnessing such diversity holds the promise of discovering enzymes tailored to specific industrial needs, especially where process conditions are extreme or variable.

Building upon this foundation, the current study focuses on isolating and characterizing a cellulase- and xylanase-producing B. subtilis strain from the black soils of Jharkhand. Our tiered approach includes: (i) isolation and taxonomic identification via morphological, biochemical, and genetic analyses; (ii) optimization of culture conditions for enhanced enzyme production; (iii) biochemical and kinetic characterization, including pH and temperature optima, substrate specificity, and kinetic parameters; and (iv) evaluation of industrial applicability, examining enzyme stability under pH- and temperature-stress and potential process integration. This work not only broadens the landscape of microbial enzyme discovery but also contributes to eco-friendly bioprocessing strategies aimed at valorizing lignocellulosic biomass.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and analysis

Black soil samples were collected from multiple ecologically distinct sites across forested and agricultural regions of Jharkhand (as shown in Fig. 1a and b), India, to isolate potential lignocellulolytic bacteria. The selected sampling locations were chosen based on their high organic matter content and minimal anthropogenic disturbance, both of which favor microbial diversity. Sampling coordinates were recorded using a handheld Global Positioning System (GPS) device to ensure precise geolocation of each site. Subsequent physicochemical characterization of the soil samples included the determination of elemental composition by atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS), which provided quantitative analysis of essential metal ions and trace elements influencing microbial enzymatic activity. Additionally, soil organic carbon content was quantified using the Walkley-Black method, a widely accepted standard protocol for estimating the organic matter content, thereby offering insights into the nutrient status and microbial substrate availability within the sampled soils.

Chemicals, media and polymers

Carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) sodium salt and Sephadex G-100 were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Co. (USA). Luria Bertani (LB) agar and broth media, as well as beechwood xylan, were obtained from HiMedia Laboratories (India). All other chemicals and reagents utilized in this investigation, unless otherwise noted, were analytical grade and purchased from reputable commercial vendors.

Isolation and screening of potential microbes

Promising bacterial colonies were isolated through serial dilution of soil samples using sterile normal saline. From each dilution, a 0.1 mL aliquot was aseptically plated onto Luria Bertani (LB) agar plates [15]. After incubation at 37 °C for 48–72 h, discrete colonies were selected and further streaked onto Berg’s agar medium supplemented individually with 0.5% (w/v) carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) and 0.5% (w/v) xylan. These plates were incubated at 37 °C for 72 h to allow for enzyme induction [53].

Colonies exhibiting visible growth were sub-cultured onto fresh CMC and xylan-supplemented plates under identical conditions. Prior to destaining with 1 N sodium chloride, the plates were flooded with 0.1% Congo Red solution to screen for cellulase and xylanase activity. The appearance of clear halos around the colonies indicated enzymatic hydrolysis of CMC or xylan, confirming cellulase and xylanase activity, respectively [53–55]. The diameter of the clearance zones was directly proportional to the level of enzymatic activity, with larger halos suggesting higher cellulolytic or xylanolytic potential on Congo Red-stained CMC and xylan agar plates [56, 57].

Molecular identification and phylogenetic analysis of the cellulolytic-xylanolytic isolate

The molecular identification and phylogenetic placement of the cellulase- and xylanase-producing bacterial isolate were performed through 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. Genomic DNA was extracted from the selected bacterial culture using standard phenol-chloroform extraction procedures, and DNA integrity was confirmed by electrophoresis on a 2% (w/v) agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide.

Amplification of the 16 S rRNA gene from the potent dual-enzyme producing isolate, designated as Bacillus subtilis strain SSMK413, was carried out via polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The universal bacterial primers 27 F (5′-GAGTTTGATCATGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-TACGGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′) were employed for PCR amplification using a Veriti® 96-well Thermal Cycler (Heredity Biosciences, Bhubaneswar, India). Amplified products were verified by electrophoresis on 2% agarose gel.

Post-amplification, PCR products were purified using magnetic bead-based purification protocols and subjected to bi-directional Sanger sequencing using the ABI 3500Dx Genetic Analyzer and BigDye® Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit, with the same primer pair (27 F/1492R) for forward and reverse reads.

Phylogenetic analysis was conducted based on the obtained consensus sequence. The sequences were aligned and analyzed using MEGA X software [58]. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) algorithm [59], and evolutionary distances were computed using the Maximum Composite Likelihood (MCL) method [60]. Using 500 replicates and bootstrap analysis, the robustness of the final topology was assessed; branches with fewer than 50% bootstrap values were collapsed [61]. At each node, the proportion of replicate trees where related taxa clustered together is displayed.

A total of 16 reference 16 S rRNA gene sequences were included in the analysis. Codon positions (first, second, third, and non-coding regions) were considered in the alignment, and all ambiguous positions were excluded using the pairwise deletion method to ensure analytical precision.

The validated sequence for Bacillus subtilis strain SSMK413 was submitted to the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) GenBank database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) and assigned a unique accession number.

Enzyme production under submerged fermentation conditions

Enzyme production was performed using submerged fermentation in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 50 mL of Berg’s minimal medium, supplemented with 0.5% (w/v) carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) for cellulase induction. The medium was further enriched with the following mineral salts: NaNO₃, MgSO₄·7 H₂O, K₂HPO₄, FeSO₄, CaCl₂, and MnSO₄. For xylanase production, an identical protocol was followed with the substitution of CMC by 0.5% (w/v) beechwood xylan. The flasks were inoculated with 2% (v/v) of a pre-culture and incubated at 37 °C under static conditions. Samples were collected at 24-hour intervals over a 96-hour fermentation period for enzyme activity quantification, following previously described methodologies [53, 62, 63].

Prior to inoculation into the production medium, bacterial strains were cultured in Luria–Bertani (LB) broth at 37 °C with shaking until an optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) of ~ 1.0 was achieved, corresponding to the mid-exponential phase. This actively growing culture was used as the inoculum source.

Enzyme activity assays

Cellulase and xylanase activities were assayed using CMC and beechwood xylan as substrates, respectively, at two concentrations (0.5% and 1% w/v) prepared in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.0), as described by Miller [64]. Enzymatic reactions were performed in a 1:1 (v/v) ratio of crude enzyme to substrate solution and incubated at 50 °C for 20 min. The reactions were terminated by the addition of dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) reagent to reach 60% of the final volume, followed by boiling at 100 °C for 10 min to develop color. After cooling to ambient temperature, absorbance was measured at 540 nm [65]. Reducing sugar concentrations were calculated from standard curves generated with glucose and xylose for cellulase and xylanase assays, respectively. All assays were conducted in biological duplicates and technical triplicates, with appropriate negative controls. The quantity of enzyme needed to release one µmol of reducing sugar (glucose or xylose) per minute under test conditions was known as one unit (U) of enzyme activity.

Partial purification of cellulase and xylanase

Partial purification of crude cellulase and xylanase was carried out using a combination of ammonium sulphate precipitation, dialysis, and size-exclusion chromatography, with modifications to the protocol previously described by [66].

A total of 120 mL of crude enzyme supernatant was subjected to ammonium sulphate fractionation by the gradual addition of a pre-chilled, 100% saturated ammonium sulphate solution to achieve 80% final saturation. After gently stirring the mixture and letting it equilibrate at 4 °C, it was centrifuged for 30 min at 4 °C at 10,000 ×g. The protein pellet that was produced was then reconstituted in a small amount of ice-cold 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.0).

The resuspended enzyme concentrate was subsequently dialyzed against 1 L of fresh ice-cold 1× PBS (pH 7.0) using dialysis tubing with an appropriate molecular weight cut-off. Dialysis was performed at 4 °C with continuous magnetic stirring for 24 h. The buffer was replaced at regular intervals (every 3–5 h) to ensure effective removal of residual salts. The dialysis tubing was weighed before and after dialysis to monitor sample recovery.

Following dialysis, the enzyme solution was subjected to size-exclusion chromatography using a Sephadex G-100 column (30 cm × 1 cm) pre-equilibrated with 1X PBS. A secondary layer of glass wool was applied to stabilize the gel matrix. The dialyzed enzyme was gently loaded onto the column and eluted using a linear gradient of NaCl (0–1 M) at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min [67]. Protein elution was monitored spectrophotometrically, and enzymatic activity was determined for each fraction. Fractions exhibiting the highest cellulase or xylanase activity were pooled for further analysis.

The active fractions were concentrated using Amicon® Ultra centrifugal filters (3 kDa molecular weight cut-off) at 5,000 rpm for 40–60 min at 4 °C to obtain the final purified enzyme preparations. The same purification protocol was employed for both cellulase and xylanase [68–71].

Effect of temperature on enzyme activity and stability

Aliquots of the purified enzymes were pre-incubated in 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.0) at different temperatures (30–90 °C) for 60 min in order to assess thermal stability. In order to prevent additional thermal denaturation, the samples were promptly cooled to room temperature in an ice bath after thermal treatment. Next, using the previously mentioned standard substrate assay procedure, residual enzymatic activity was measured at 50 °C [66, 72].

All experiments were performed in biological duplicates with technical triplicates. The optimal temperature was defined as the temperature at which the highest enzymatic activity was recorded, while thermal stability was expressed as the percentage of residual activity retained after pre-incubation.

Effect of pH on enzyme activity and stability

The pH dependence of purified cellulase and xylanase activities was evaluated by measuring enzyme activity across a pH range of 2.0 to 11.0, using a series of 100 mM buffer systems to ensure consistent ionic strength. The following buffers were used: phosphate buffer (pH 6.0–8.0), carbonate–bicarbonate buffer (pH 9.0–11.0), citrate buffer (pH 3.0–5.0), and hydrochloric acid–potassium chloride (pH 2.0). Standard operating procedures were followed for the enzymatic experiments, and the activity at each pH value was expressed in relation to the highest activity that was observed [63].

To assess pH stability, aliquots of the purified enzymes were pre-incubated in the respective buffers at 4 °C for 24 h without agitation. Following incubation, residual enzyme activity was determined under optimal temperature and substrate conditions, as previously described. Enzyme stability was expressed as the percentage of activity retained relative to a non-incubated control.

All experiments were carried out in biological duplicates with technical triplicates. Results were reported as mean ± standard deviation, and optimal pH was defined as the condition yielding maximal enzymatic activity [63].

Effect of metal ions and inhibitor on enzyme activity

The purified enzyme was incubated with various metal salts viz. ZnCl2, NaCl, KCl, FeCl3, MgCl2, CsCl, MnCl2 and inhibitor like EDTA at a final concentration of 1mM for 5 min at room temperature and then cellulase and xylanase activity was assayed. The residual activity (%) was then calculated [72].

Determination of kinetic parameters (Km and Vmax)

Enzyme activity at different substrate concentrations was used to calculate the partly purified cellulase and xylanase’s Michaelis–Menten kinetic parameters, Km and Vmax. Cellulase and xylanase were both employed as substrates for carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) and beechwood xylan, respectively, at concentrations ranging from 0.1% to 1.0% (w/v). The experiments were conducted at 50 °C in 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.0) [73].

At the specified concentrations, 150 µL of the partly purified enzyme was combined with 150 µL of substrate solution for each reaction. The 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method was used to measure the quantity of reducing sugar produced after the combinations were incubated for 30 min at 50 °C under standard assay conditions. Cellulase and xylanase calibration curves for glucose and xylose, respectively, were used to calculate reducing sugar concentrations and measure absorbance at 540 nm.

By fitting the data to the Michaelis–Menten equation using non-linear regression analysis, initial reaction velocities (V₀) and kinetic parameters (Km and Vmax) were determined. Every experiment was carried out three times, and final kinetic modeling was done using mean values. The enzyme kinetic graphs between reaction velocities (µmol ml− 1 min− 1) vs. substrate concentration (mg mL− 1) were plotted using GraphPad Prism version 5.01 and subsequently Km and Vmax were determined drawing a double reciprocal Lineweaver Burk plot employing linear regression fitting [66].

Electrophoretic and zymographic characterization

Sodium dodecyl sulphate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) was performed to estimate the molecular weight of the partially purified cellulase and xylanase enzymes. Protein samples were prepared by mixing with SDS loading buffer and denatured by brief heat treatment at 100 °C for 45 s. Electrophoresis was carried out using a 10% resolving gel and a 5% stacking gel under standard conditions. Following electrophoresis, protein bands were visualized using the silver staining method, as described previously [74, 75].

Native-PAGE was conducted in parallel using the same gel composition, excluding denaturing agents (SDS and β-mercaptoethanol) and without sample heating, to preserve enzymatic activity. Visualization was similarly performed using silver staining.

Zymogram analysis was carried out to detect enzymatic activity directly within the gel matrix. Native-PAGE gels were prepared with 0.5% (w/v) sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) incorporated into the 10% resolving gel to assess cellulase activity. After electrophoresis (conducted at 60 V), the gel was incubated in 1× PBS (pH 7.0) at room temperature for 1 h to allow substrate-enzyme interaction. Enzymatic activity was visualized by staining with 0.1% (w/v) Congo red for 30 min, followed by destaining with 1 N NaCl until clear hydrolysis zones appeared against the stained background, indicating cellulase activity. A duplicate gel without substrate incorporation was subjected to silver staining for protein localization confirmation [76].

For xylanase activity detection, the same procedure was employed with 0.5% (w/v) beechwood xylan incorporated into the resolving gel as the substrate.

Statistical analysis

The results are shown as mean values ± standard deviation (SD), and all studies were carried out in triplicate. Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to determine statistical significance and compare the experimental groups. P-values below 0.05 were regarded as statistically significant. Correlation analysis was performed wherever required. Graphical representations have been done using GraphPad Prism version 5.01 and OriginPro 2025b.

Results

Elemental composition of soil samples and potential influence on enzyme activity

The elemental profile of black soil samples collected from the study site was determined using atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS), with a focus on identifying trace and major metal ions that may influence microbial enzyme production. Among the elements analyzed—Ni, Cu, Fe, Pb, Mg, Se, Zn, Cd, As, Na, Hg, K, Co, Cr, N, and P only Ni, Fe, Mg, Zn, K, N, and P were detected at quantifiable concentrations (Table 2). The remaining elements were either below the analytical detection limits or present at trace levels deemed negligible.

Given the known modulatory effects of certain metal ions on microbial enzymatic systems, particularly those involved in lignocellulosic biomass degradation, the detected elements were selected for further investigation to evaluate their impact on the activity of purified cellulase and xylanase enzymes. The relevance of these ions is supported by previous findings suggesting that elements such as Mg²⁺, Fe²⁺, and Zn²⁺ can act as enzyme cofactors or structural stabilizers, while others may inhibit or alter enzyme conformation depending on concentration and ionic environment.

Isolation and preliminary screening of cellulolytic and xylanolytic microorganisms

Microbial isolation from the collected black soil samples yielded a diverse range of morphologically distinct colonies (Fig. 2a and b). Among these, a single bacterial isolate demonstrated prominent hydrolytic activity on both carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) and xylan agar plates, indicated by the formation of clear halos surrounding the colony.

To qualitatively assess extracellular cellulase and xylanase activity, plate assays were performed by flooding the CMC and xylan agar plates with 0.1% Congo Red solution, followed by destaining with 0.9% saline. The presence of clear zones around the colony after staining confirmed enzymatic degradation of the respective polysaccharides. Representative images of the hydrolysis zones on CMC and xylan-containing media are shown in Figs. 3 and 4, respectively.

The diameter of the clearance zones was used as a qualitative metric for enzyme production and served as the basis for selecting the most promising strain for subsequent biochemical characterization and enzyme profiling. This initial screening confirmed the isolate’s dual capability to produce both cellulase and xylanase enzymes under in vitro conditions.

The microbial community isolated from the black soil samples was predominantly composed of various Streptomyces species, followed by strains of Bacillus subtilis, other Bacillus species, and an actinomycete designated LQ18. Discussion

Despite significant progress in industrial biotechnology and microbial enzyme engineering, the complete spectrum of microbial sources and the underlying molecular mechanisms regulating cellulolytic enzyme production remain insufficiently characterized [77–81]. Efficient and economically viable bioconversion of lignocellulosic biomass into fermentable sugars, fuels, and platform chemicals continues to face substantial technical barriers. These challenges largely stem from the structural complexity and chemical recalcitrance of plant biomass, primarily composed of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin cross-linked in a highly resistant matrix [82, 83]. Unlocking this matrix requires the concerted action of multiple synergistic enzymes cellulases, xylanases, β-glucosidases, lytic polysaccharide monooxygenases (LPMOs), and esterases each targeting distinct components or bonds within the lignocellulosic network [84–87].

Pure culture isolates often fail to match the lignocellulolytic efficiency observed in natural microbial consortia, largely due to the absence of a complete enzyme repertoire or limitations in substrate sensing and enzyme secretion [88, 89]. Hence, the continued isolation and characterization of novel microbial strains from diverse and underexplored ecological niches remain a priority. A particular focus lies on strains that exhibit high catalytic efficiency, stability under industrially relevant conditions (e.g., moderate to high temperatures, acidic pH, high-solid loading), and compatibility with existing biorefinery processes [88, 90, 91].

The current study contributes to this endeavour by investigating the black soil ecosystem, a biogeochemical environment with high microbial richness but limited exploration in the context of biomass-degrading microbes. Black soils (regur), prevalent in Jharkhand, India, are rich in minerals and organic carbon and possess physicochemical features that favour the accumulation and slow degradation of lignocellulosic matter [92–94]. Previous studies in analogous Black Belt soils of Alabama, USA, have demonstrated high lignocellulose retention and microbial adaptation to plant-derived polysaccharides, reinforcing the rationale for selecting such habitats as promising reservoirs of cellulolytic and hemicellulolytic microbes [95].

From this soil environment, we isolated a novel Bacillus subtilis strain, SSMK413, capable of producing both cellulase and xylanase. The genus Bacillus—notably species like B. subtilis, B. licheniformis, and B. pumilus, has been extensively reported for its robust secretion of industrially relevant enzymes that display thermostability, activity over a broad pH range, and high expression yields [96–98]. SSMK413’s dual production of cellulase and xylanase aligns with the current industrial need for enzyme cocktails tailored for efficient biomass pretreatment. Such dual activity is particularly advantageous, considering the interlinked structure of cellulose and hemicellulose within plant cell walls, where hydrolysis of one often enhances access to the other [99, 100].

Bioprocess optimization revealed that peak production of both enzymes occurred at 48 h, using 1% (w/v) carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) and beechwood xylan as substrates for cellulase and xylanase induction, respectively. This short incubation period and substrate responsiveness are valuable for industrial scalability, as shorter fermentation cycles reduce operational costs and energy consumption—key metrics in the economic viability of biorefineries [101, 102]. Comparable rapid enzyme production has been reported in B. subtilis AS3 and B. licheniformis G3, but SSMK413 offers superior dual-activity performance under mesophilic conditions [103, 104].

Purification using sequential steps ammonium sulfate precipitation, dialysis, and Sephadex G-100 gel filtration yielded 14.3-fold purification for cellulase and 11.5-fold for xylanase, with specific activities of 212.16 IU/mg and 1267.33 IU/mg, respectively. While overall enzyme recovery was modest (2.6% for cellulase; 3.92% for xylanase), this trade-off is common in multistep purifications prioritizing homogeneity and activity [105]. The high specific activities compare favourably with those from other Bacillus strains, such as B. amyloliquefaciens (xylanase ~ 1000 IU/mg) and B. subtilis K-18 (cellulase ~ 180 IU/mg), reinforcing the strain’s biotechnological potential [106–108].

Thermal and pH profiling indicated that both enzymes exhibited optimal activity at 40–50 °C and at acidic pH ranges (pH 5 for cellulase; pH 6 for xylanase), reflecting adaptation to moderately thermophilic and acidic niches—features conducive to industrial biomass pretreatment and paper pulp hydrolysis, where acid and heat treatments are common [109]. Similar thermophilic profiles have been reported for B. subtilis C20 and B. licheniformis T7, though with narrower pH stability ranges, suggesting SSMK413 enzymes may offer better operational flexibility [110, 111].

Metal ion modulation studies revealed that Mn²⁺, Mg²⁺, and Fe³⁺ significantly enhanced xylanase activity by 57.8%, 38.8%, and 31%, respectively, consistent with their known roles as cofactors in stabilizing the enzyme-substrate complex and participating in redox-mediated catalysis [112, 113]. The observed inhibition by Cs⁺ and EDTA indicates metal-dependence and vulnerability to chelating agents, echoing trends observed in metalloprotein-dependent glycoside hydrolases from other Bacillus strains [114–116].

Kinetic analyses offered further insight into enzyme efficiency. Cellulase displayed a Km of 0.192 mg mL− 1 for CMC, demonstrating high substrate affinity compared to reported values of 0.996 mM for B. subtilis CD001—and a Vmax of 21.83 µmol/mL/min, reflecting efficient catalytic turnover [117, 118]. Xylanase showed a Km of 2.615 mg mL− 1 for beechwood xylan, which, while indicating lower affinity, remains within effective catalytic ranges, with a Vmax of 3.38 µmol/mL/min comparable to B. subtilis CCMI 966 Xyl II [72]. These kinetic parameters suggest that SSMK413’s enzymes are well-suited for industrial biomass saccharification, balancing substrate affinity with turnover efficiency.

Electrophoretic and zymographic analyses further confirmed the molecular weights of the purified enzymes 55–65 kDa for cellulase and 240–270 kDa for xylanase. While the cellulase size corresponds well with known Bacillus enzymes, the high molecular weight of xylanase suggests multimeric or complexed forms, similar to those observed in B. subtilis CCMI 966, where the functional enzyme consists of smaller catalytic subunits (~ 20 kDa) assembled into larger oligomers [119–121]. Such multimerism may confer enhanced thermal stability, resistance to proteolysis, and prolonged activity under industrially relevant stress conditions [122, 123].

Overall, Bacillus subtilis SSMK413 represents a promising candidate for integrated lignocellulose bioconversion platforms. Its rapid and dual enzyme production, favorable biochemical properties, and tolerance to industrial conditions underscore its potential as a source of robust biocatalysts for sustainable biofuel production, environmentally friendly pulp processing, and agro-residue valorization.

Conclusions

This study establishes Bacillus subtilis strain SSMK413 as a robust source of cellulase and xylanase, with promising industrial applications. The enzymes exhibit mesophilic optima (40–50 °C), acidic pH preferences (pH 5–6), favorable kinetics, and metal ion dependencies, ideal for biofuel production, pulp processing, and waste bioconversion. The purification yielded high specific activities, and molecular analyses confirmed structural integrity. The 16 S rRNA sequencing (GenBank: PB830054.1) provides a basis for genomic studies. These findings bridge enzymology and bioprocessing, advancing sustainable biomass utilization. SSMK413’s complementary enzymes enable comprehensive lignocellulose hydrolysis, boosting sugar yields for bioethanol and bioproducts. In pulp and paper, they reduce chemical needs, enhancing sustainability. Agro-industries can leverage them for biofertilizers, feed, and energy, supporting circular economies. Enzyme immobilization could improve reusability and stability for continuous systems. Genetic engineering via CRISPR-Cas9 or directed evolution may enhance thermostability and yields. Bioprocess scale-up with waste substrates promises cost reductions. Bioprospecting Jharkhand’s black soils via metagenomics could uncover novel enzymes, enabling designer consortia for biorefineries.
