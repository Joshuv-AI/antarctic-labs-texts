The pro-obesity role of G3BP1 through altering adipogenic differentiation in mice via the PI3K/AKT pathway
Authors: Haiyan Wang, Litong Sun, Haidan Mao, Yan Mei, Jinping Ni, Liren Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAs a growing but non-infectious epidemic, obesity is a common cause of several comorbidities. GTPase-activating protein SH3 domain-binding protein 1 (G3BP1) is a central regulator of stress‑granule assembly that has significant implications in metabolic disorders. This article aims to explore the functional mechanism of G3BP1 in obesity, both in vitro and in vivo. Functional assays were used to detect cellular phenotypes. Reverse transcription quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and western blotting were also used to test metastasis-, phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT) signalling- and Wnt/β-catenin signalling-related genes. Oil‑red‑O staining and haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining were used to estimate lipid deposition. RT-qPCR, western blotting, and immunofluorescence staining were used to determine adipocyte differentiation and lipid metabolism. The TOPFlash/FOPFlash reporter assay was used to identify β-catenin activity. In a murine model fed a high-fat diet (HFD, 60% fat), body weight was measured prior to the detection of adipokine levels by enzyme‑linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), triacylglycerol (TG) concentration using an assay kit, and adipogenic marker genes by western blotting. G3BP1 knockdown inhibited proliferation, migration, invasion, and adipogenesis, and induced cell cycle arrest in 3T3-L1 preadipocytes, and decreased body weight and adipogenesis and fat accumulation in obese mice. G3BP1 activated Wnt/β-catenin and PI3K/AKT pathways. LY294002 (PI3K inhibitor) and SC3036 (PI3K activator) reversed the in vitro effects of G3BP1 or silenced G3BP1 on preadipocyte invasion. G3BP1 may promote preadipocyte proliferation, migration, invasion, and adipogenesis to promote the progression of obesity, which might be relevant to the PI3K/AKT pathway.KEYWORDS: Adipogenesis, obesity, G3BP1, Wnt/β-catenin pathway, PI3K/AKT pathway, preadipocyte

Introduction

Obesity is defined as the adaptive response of an organism to chronic nutrient overflow, resulting from an imbalance between energy preservation and expenditure [1]. Obesity has emerged as a global health concern. The global prevalence of overweight and obese adults reached 38% in 2020, which is predicted to reach approximately 50% by 2035 [2]. In the long term, adipose tissue, a passive energy storage organ in the form of triglycerides (TG), is enlarged, and the dysfunction of this tissue leads to lipid overflow and triggers ectopic fat deposition in other organs, causing metabolic disorders [3]. The complications of obesity include abnormal blood sugar, blood lipids, dyslipidemia, and insulin resistance. Obesity enhances the risks of multiple non-communicable diseases, such as type 2 diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and cancer [4–8].

Stress granules (SGs) are mRNA-protein assemblies formed from non-translating mRNAs in response to stress [9]. GTPase-activating protein SH3 domain-binding protein 1 (G3BP1), also known as stress granule assembly factor 1, functions as an ‘RNA condenser’ that drives intermolecular RNA–RNA interactions, stabilizing RNA condensates and leading to RNP granule persistence [10]. As a multifunctional binding protein, G3BP1 affects cellular proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and RNA metabolism, and mediates signalling pathways implicated in neurological disorders, cancer progression, and bacterial and viral infections [11–14]. G3BP1 is overexpressed in HFD-fed mouse liver and cochlear tissues and is related to the mitigation of liver lipid deposition and insulin resistance in obese mice [15,16]. However, limited data exist that demonstrate the importance of G3BP1 in obesity.

Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT) signalling is essential for cellular behaviours, including cell growth, metastasis, survival, and others [17–19]. Importantly, the PI3K/AKT pathway plays a central role in adipogenesis. It is required for the differentiation of preadipocytes into mature adipocytes, and its activation promotes the expression of key adipogenic transcription factors such as peroxisome proliferator‑activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) and CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein alpha (C/EBPα), thereby driving lipid accumulation and adipocyte maturation [20–22]. The PI3K/AKT pathway is required for normal metabolism, and the imbalance of this pathway is a common event in obesity [23,24]. G3BP1 has been recognized as an activator of the PI3K/AKT pathway in cancers [25–27].

Overall, this study aims to investigate the functional mechanism of G3BP1 in obesity both in vitro and in vivo.

Materials and methods

Database analysis

Transcriptomics and proteomics analysis of G3BP1 in obese patients versus non-obese patients was performed in the Adipose Tissue Knowledge Portal (https://adiposetissue.org/).

Animals and diet

Pathogen-free male C57BL/6 mice, 6–8 weeks of age, housed at 23 ± 1°C and 60 ± 5% humidity, were maintained on a 12:12 h artificial light–dark cycle. During the experimental period, the animals were fed normal chow and a high-fat diet (HFD) containing 60 kcal% fat (Research Diets, Beijing, China). During the process, small interfering RNA (siRNA) complexes (5 mg/kg) dissolved in normal saline were injected via the tail vein within 5–10 s, once every 5 d. Throughout the high-fat diet intervention, animals were monitored daily for signs of distress. If a mouse exhibited severe weakness, reduced mobility, or persistent abnormal behaviour, euthanasia was performed to prevent unnecessary suffering. Body weight, food intake, and moving distance after the diet were recorded. Animals were euthanized with carbon dioxide followed by cervical dislocation. Animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Jiangsu University (UJS-IACUC-2025122909) and carried out in accordance with the institution’s ethical guidelines.

Cell differentiation

3T3-L1 murine preadipocytes were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with a mixture of 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 10% bovine calf serum (BCS; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). The cells were tested for mycoplasma contamination and were mycoplasma free.

When the cells reached confluency, the growth medium was changed to a differentiation medium consisting of 0.5 mM 3-isobutyl-1-methylxanthine (IBMX; Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA), 1 μM dexamethasone (DEX; Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA), 5 μg/mL insulin (Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA), and 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) in DMEM. After 2 days, the medium was switched to the differentiation medium containing 5 μg/mL insulin and 10% FBS in DMEM every two days.

Gene knockdown and overexpression

After dilution in 100 μl Opti-MEM medium, G3BP1 siRNA oligonucleotide duplexes or G3BP1 overexpression vectors synthesized by BioVector (Beijing, China) were mixed with Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Thermofisher, MA, USA) and transfected into cells. The siRNAs used in vitro included: si-NC (5’-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGU-3’); si-G3BP1 (5’-UCAACAUGGCGAAUCUUGGUGUGG-3’). The siRNAs used in vivo included: si-NC (5’-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGU-3’); si-G3BP1 (5’- CCACACCAAGAUUCGCCAUG-3’).

Reverse transcription quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

After being extracted from cells using the AG RNAex Pro reagent (Accurate Biology, Hunan, China), total RNA was converted to complementary DNA (cDNA) using the Evo M-MLV RT Kit (Accurate Biology, Hunan, China). PCR was performed using the SYBR green technique (Forget-Me-Not™ EvaGreen® qPCR Master Mix, Biotum, USA) in a Stratagene Mx3005p thermocycler (Agilent Technologies, USA). The G3BP1 levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) used as the reference gene for normalization. Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8)

3T3-L1 adipocytes at a density of 5 × 104/mL were seeded into 96-well plates, and the medium was refreshed. Viability was monitored at 0, 24, and 48 h post-transfection using the CCK-8 kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at 37°C for 2 h. The absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microtiter plate reader (Thermofisher, MA, USA).

5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) imaging assay

Preliminarily, after being incubated with 100 μL of 50 μM EdU Reagent A (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 2 h, 3T3-L1 cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, Hefei, China) for 30 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA) for 5 min. After 30 min of exposure to the 1X Apollo staining reaction solution (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) in the dark at room temperature, the nuclei were stained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA) for 30 min. The cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Flow cytometry

After digestion and centrifugation, the resulting pellet was stained with the PI/RNase staining solution (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) in the dark for half an hour. The cell cycle was analysed with a Cytomics FC500 Flow Cytometer (BeckmanCoulter, USA) and processed using CXP Software ver.2.3.

Scratch assay

The cells were seeded in 6-well plates (1 × 104 cells/well) until 95% confluence. The cell monolayer was scraped with a 200-µl sterile pipette tip. The wells were washed, and representative images were captured under a light microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Transwell assay

Serum-free medium containing 5 × 104 cells was added into the upper chamber of 24-well transwell plates (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) pre-coated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). Complete medium supplemented with 10% FBS for chemotaxis was added to the lower chamber. Cells that did not invade through the membrane in the upper chamber were removed. The cells that had invaded into the lower chamber were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet and captured under a light microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Immunofluorescent staining

After differentiation, the 3T3-L1 cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, Hefei, China). After being blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 1 h, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies targeting FABP4 (12802–1-AP, 1/100, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) and PPARγ (16643–1-AP, 1/100, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) at 4°C overnight and with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with 5 µg/ml DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA) for 10 min at room temperature and subsequently sealed with glycerine-buffered saline (Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA). Cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Oil red O staining

After being fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, Hefei, China) for 10 min, the adipocyte tissues or 3T3-L1 cells were stained with 0.6% Oil Red O solution (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 37°C for 30 min and rinsed with 60% isopropyl alcohol. The samples were observed under a light microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

The adipocyte tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, Hefei, China), embedded in paraffin and trimmed. The samples were then dewaxed and dehydrated for staining with haematoxylin (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) for 5 min and eosin (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) for 1 min. The sections were subsequently sealed with neutral resin, covered with a coverslip and observed under a light microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Lipid quantification

After homogenization, the subcutaneous fat tissues and liver samples were centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 5 min. Triacylglycerol (TG) content was determined using a commercially available kit (Absin, Shanghai, China). The protein concentration was determined using the Bicinchoninate Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) the absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 550 nm using a microtiter plate reader (Thermofisher, MA, USA).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

After homogenization, the subcutaneous fat tissues and liver samples were centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 5 min. The adiponectin and resistin concentrations were measured using the corresponding ELISA kits (Elabscience, Wuhan, China). The readings were recorded at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermofisher, MA, USA).

Luciferase activity assay

After co-transfection of the reporter plasmids encoding TOPFlash or FOPFlash (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) with Lymphoid Enhancer Factor/T-Cell Factor (LEF/TCF) DNA-binding sites, the control plasmid pTK-RL, si-G3BP1, or si-NC, the luciferase activity was measured using a Dual-Glo luciferase assay kit (Promega, USA). Firefly luciferase expression levels were normalized against Renilla luciferase activity.

Data analysis

Graphs and corresponding statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism V.8 software (GraphPad Software, USA). The results of three replicates were recorded as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The data were tested for normal distribution using a Shapiro–Wilk test. The means of the two groups were compared using Student’s t-test. Differences in values across multiple groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

G3BP1 silencing inhibited the proliferation and induced cell cycle arrest in 3T3-L1 preadipocytes

Through the Adipose Tissue Knowledge Portal database, it was discovered that G3BP1 was highly expressed in mature adipocytes, and its expression gradually increased during the adipogenic process (Fig. S1A). In human fat tissues, G3BP1 transcription level was positively correlated with circulating CRP and fat cell volume, and negatively correlated with isoproterenol-stimulated lipolysis. G3BP1 protein level was positively correlated with circulating CRP (Fig. S1B). After the transfection with si-G3BP1, G3BP1 expression was decreased in 3T3-L1 preadipocytes (Figure 1(A)). At designated time points, when G3BP1 was expressed at low levels, the viability of 3T3-L1 preadipocytes was reduced (Figure 1(B)). The number of positive cells declined with deficiency of G3BP1, as demonstrated by EdU staining (Figure 1(C)). After knockdown of G3BP1, both PCNA and CDK2 protein expression levels declined (Figure 1(D)). Flow cytometry demonstrated that, after interference with G3BP1, the proportion of cells in G0/G1 phase increased, whereas the proportions of cells in S and G2/M phases decreased (Figure 1(E)). In addition, downregulation of G3BP1 resulted in decreased CCNB1 and CCND2 protein expression (Figure 1(F)).

G3BP1 silencing inhibited the proliferation and induced cell cycle arrest in 3T3-L1 preadipocytes. A. The transfection efficacy of si-G3BP1 at the protein level. B. Cell viability curve applying CCK-8 method. C. Microscopic observation of EdU-labelled cells. D. PCNA and CDK2 protein expression. E. Cell cycle stages from flow cytometry. F. CCNB1 and CCND2 protein expression. n = 3 biological and technical replicates.

G3BP1 silencing inhibited the migration and invasion in 3T3-L1 preadipocytes

Upon silencing of G3BP1, the rate of healing of 3T3-L1 preadipocytes decreased (Figure 2(A)). Coincidentally, G3BP1 deficiency weakened the ability of 3T3-L1 preadipocytes to invade (Figure 2(B)). The expression of MMP9, MMP7, and MMP2 decreased when G3BP1 was downregulated (Figure 2(C,D)).

G3BP1 silencing inhibited migration and invasion in 3T3-L1 preadipocytes. A. Microscopic observation of wound‑healing rate. B. Microscopic observation of invaded cells from transwell assay. C. MMP9, MMP7 and MMP2 mRNA expression. D. MMP9, MMP7 and MMP2 protein expression. n = 3 biological and technical replicates.

G3BP1 positively regulates adipogenesis

Oil Red O staining showed that lipid droplet formation was respectively suppressed and promoted by silencing and up-regulation of G3BP1 (Figure 3(A), Figure S2A). In addition, downregulation of G3BP1 lowered PPARγ, C/EBPα, ADIPOR1, FABP4, LEPR, FAS, ATGL, LPL, and FTO expression in relation to adipogenesis and fat metabolism (Figure 3(B,C)). However, elevation of G3BP1 led to an increase in PPARγ expression (Fig. S2B). Immunofluorescence staining also demonstrated weakened fluorescence intensity for FABP4 and PPARγ in G3BP1-silencing adipocytes (Figure 3(D)).

G3BP1 silencing inhibited the differentiation of 3T3-L1 preadipocytes. A. Microscopic images of lipid droplets. B. PPARγ, C/EBPα, ADIPOR1, FABP4, LEPR, FAS, ATGL, LPL and FTO mRNA expression. C. PPARγ, C/EBPα, ADIPOR1, FABP4, LEPR, FAS, ATGL, LPL and FTO protein expression. D. Immunofluorescent staining of PPARγ and FABP4. n = 3 biological and technical replicates.

G3BP1 deficiency blocked Wnt/β-catenin pathway

In the TOPFlash/FOPFlash reporter assay, β-catenin activity was reduced after G3BP1 was knocked down (Figure 4(A)), whereas the opposite trend was observed after G3BP1 was overexpressed (Figure 4(B)). Moreover, silencing of G3BP1 repressed β-catenin, cyclin D1, c-Myc, and Axin2 expression (Figure 4(C,D)).

G3BP1 deficiency blocked Wnt/β-catenin pathway. A-B. Evaluation of β-catenin activity in TOPFlash/FOPFlash reporter assays. C. Cyclin D1, c-Myc and Axin2 mRNA expression. D. β-catenin, cyclin D1, c-Myc and Axin2 protein expression. n = 3 biological and technical replicates.

G3BP1 promoted 3T3-L1 preadipocyte invasion via PI3K/AKT pathway

G3BP1 upregulation increased both p-PI3K and p-AKT protein expression, which was reversed by LY294002 (PI3K inhibitor) (Figure 5(A)). In contrast, SC3036 (a PI3K activator) increased both p-PI3K and p-AKT protein expression in G3BP1-silencing 3T3-L1 preadipocytes (Figure 5(B)). In the meantime, G3BP1 overexpression or interference accelerated or inhibited the invasion of 3T3-L1 preadipocytes, and these effects were neutralized by LY294002 or SC3036 (Figure 5(C,D)).

G3BP1 promoted 3T3-L1 preadipocyte invasion via the PI3K/AKT pathway. A-B. Protein expression of PI3K, p-PI3K, AKT, and p-AKT C-D. Microscopic observation of invaded cells in the transwell assay. n = 3 biological and technical replicates.

G3BP1 knockdown mitigated adipogenesis and fat metabolism disorder in HFD-fed obese mice

After injection of si-G3BP1, G3BP1 expression was significantly decreased in epididymal adipose tissue (eWAT) and inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT) (Figure 6(A,B)). In obese mice, G3BP1 deficiency markedly reduced the body weight gain (Figure 6(C)). Through recording food intake, we found insignificant difference in normal diet and high-fat diet between si-NC group and si-G3BP1 group (Fig. S3A). ELISA results showed that resistin concentration was reduced while adiponectin level was increased in subcutaneous fat tissues and liver samples when G3BP1 was downregulated (Figure 6(D,E)). Similarly, G3BP1 deficiency decreased serum resistin concentration (Fig. S3B) and resistin expression in both eWAT and iWAT (Fig. S3C). Additionally, the TG content was reduced by interference with G3BP1 in subcutaneous fat tissues and liver samples of obese mice (Figure 6(F)). As shown in Figure 6(G,H), Oil red O staining and H&E staining demonstrated that G3BP1 down-regulation suppressed fat accumulation in both eWAT and iWAT. In both subcutaneous fat tissues and liver samples of G3BP1-silenced obese mice, C/EBPα, SREBP-1, and ACSL1 protein expression was decreased (Figure 6(I,J)). In vivo, the expression of p-AKT and adipogenic marker PPARγ which is a downstream target of PI3K/AKT signalling were both down-regulated in eWAT and iWAT after G3BP1 was silenced (Figure 6(K,L)).

Discussion

Considering the decisive role of SGs in the progression of obesity-associated pancreatic cancer [28], G3BP1, the central protein within the stress‑granule RNA-protein network that contributes to the establishment of the percolation threshold for stress granule assembly, has been considered a crucial factor in various carcinomas, viral infections, nervous system diseases, and cardiovascular diseases [29]. In our study, G3BP1 accelerated adipocyte differentiation, adipogenesis, and fat metabolism disorder in HFD-induced obesity.

Although G3BP1 has been reported to regulate adipogenesis in 3T3-L1 preadipocytes [30], the novelty of our study lies in several aspects. Beyond adipogenesis, we demonstrated that G3BP1 silencing inhibited preadipocyte proliferation, migration and invasion. We further provided in vivo evidence that G3BP1 downregulation alleviated HFD-induced body weight gain, lipid deposition and metabolic abnormalities. Moreover, we identified Wnt/β-catenin and PI3K/AKT signalling as downstream mechanisms, and revealed depot-specific G3BP1 expression in VAT versus SAT, supporting a context-dependent role. These findings distinguish our work from previous studies and strengthen its broader impact.

In line with the context-dependent role of G3BP1, G3BP1 is overexpressed in the liver tissues of mice fed a HFD [15]. G3BP1 was also highly expressed during 3T3-L1 preadipocyte proliferation, and its knockdown suppressed cell viability and proliferation. This was accompanied by G0/G1 cell cycle arrest and reduced expression of key cell cycle regulators, including PCNA, CDK2, CCNB1 and CCND2, consistent with the reported role of miR-129-5p in targeting G3BP1 to inhibit preadipocyte proliferation [30–32]. Furthermore, G3BP1 knockdown attenuated preadipocyte migration and invasion, with decreased expression of metastasis-associated MMP9, MMP7 and MMP2.

The 3T3-L1 cell line, isolated from a mouse embryo, is a fibroblast-like preadipocyte whose uncontrolled differentiation into insulin-responsive lipid-loaded mature adipocytes in the white adipose tissue is called adipogenesis, which remains a primary metabolic event that causes obesity [33]. In agreement with this, analysis of the Adipose Tissue Knowledge Portal database revealed that G3BP1 was highly expressed in mature adipocytes and its expression gradually increased during adipogenesis. In human fat tissues, G3BP1 transcription level was positively correlated with circulating CRP and fat cell volume, and negatively correlated with isoproterenol-stimulated lipolysis, whereas G3BP1 protein level was positively correlated with circulating CRP. These human data link G3BP1 to lipid storage, inflammation and lipolysis, supporting a pro-adipogenic and clinically relevant role of G3BP1 in adipose tissues. Functionally, silencing G3BP1 exaggerates liver lipid deposition and insulin resistance in obese mice [15]. The coordination of essential transcription factor PPARγ with C/EBPα is known as a vital determinant of adipocyte fate, which further activates the expression of a variety of lipogenesis- and adipogenesis-associated genes [34]. Moreover, SREBP-1c can activate PPARγ through endogenous ligand production and control the expression of downstream fatty acid synthases [35]. ACSL1 is a long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase responsible for activating fatty acids for mitochondrial import [36]. Our data showed that G3BP1 deficiency suppressed lipid deposition and the expression of multiple adipogenic and lipogenic markers, including PPARγ, CEBPα, ADIPOR1, FABP4, LEPR, FAS, ATGL, LPL and FTO in adipocytes, while G3BP1 overexpression exerted the opposite effects. However, we acknowledge that these data do not fully exclude a contribution of general cellular stress to the knockdown phenotype, which needs to be determined in further study. In vivo, G3BP1 downregulation reduced body weight, TG level, and CEBPα, SREBP-1 and ACSL1 expression while increasing adiponectin level in HFD mice. It also lowered resistin levels in serum, subcutaneous fat, liver and adipose tissues. Since resistin is predominantly secreted by adipose tissue and is generally linked to insulin resistance, its reduction in G3BP1-knockdown mice is likely a consequence of suppressed adipogenesis and reduced adipose tissue mass. This decrease may partly contribute to the systemic metabolic changes observed in these mice, rather than directly driving metabolic deterioration. In contrast to our findings, Liu et al. [15] have reported that G3BP1-defective mice exhibit aggravated obesity and metabolic abnormalities under HFD, and that G3BP1 deficiency exacerbated hepatic lipid deposition and insulin resistance. This discrepancy may be explained, at least in part, by differences in the experimental models and target tissues. Our study used an acute systemic siRNA knockdown model, whereas Liu et al. used heterozygous G3BP1 mice generated through breeding, representing a chronic genetic model. In addition, Liu et al. primarily focused on the liver, whereas our study focused on adipose tissue and adipogenesis. However, the Adipose Tissue Knowledge Portal database showed high heterogeneity (I2 = 78%) between obese and non-obese individuals and G3BP1 mRNA was lower in visceral adipose tissue (VAT) than in subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) while protein showed the opposite pattern (Fig. S4), indicating depot-specific post-transcriptional or translational regulation. These observations suggest that the function of G3BP1 is context-dependent and may be influenced by factors such as the source of fat stores, the degree of inflammation, the metabolic state, and the duration or mode of G3BP1 perturbation, rather than being solely driven by body mass index (BMI). However, because our in vivo experiments used systemic acute G3BP1 knockdown, we cannot exclude contributions from extra-adipose tissues such as liver, muscle, immune cells, or the central nervous system. Therefore, our data do not establish a cell-autonomous role of G3BP1 in adipocytes in vivo. The apparent inconsistency with Liu et al. does not by itself invalidate either study, but it highlights the need for tissue-specific and time-resolved models. Future studies using adipocyte-specific G3BP1 knockout or overexpression, paired adipose depot analyses, and metabolic studies are required to define the cell-intrinsic and systemic contributions of G3BP1 to obesity. Accordingly, our conclusion should be limited to the context of acute systemic G3BP1 knockdown, and the adipose-intrinsic mechanism remains to be established.

Previous studies have shown that G3BP1 can activate Wnt/β-catenin signalling in oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma [37], colon cancer [38], and breast cancer [39]. As a key nuclear effector of canonical Wnt signalling, β-catenin binds to the intranuclear transcription factor LEF/TCF to affect tumour formation and progression via downstream target genes such as c-Myc and cyclin D1 [40]. Numerous studies have demonstrated the therapeutic promise of targeting the Wnt/β-catenin signalling pathway in obesity [41,42]. In our study, G3BP1 silencing inhibited Wnt/β-catenin signalling, as evidenced by reduced β-catenin activity and decreased expression of its downstream targets cyclin D1, c-Myc and Axin2 in 3T3-L1 cells.

Interestingly, G3BP1 has also been proposed to stimulate PI3K/AKT signalling in bladder cancer [25], oesophageal cancer [26] and non-small cell lung cancer [27]. In our study, G3BP1 silencing suppressed PI3K/AKT signalling in vitro and in vivo, and the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 or activator SC3036 reversed the effects of G3BP1 on preadipocyte invasion. As a significant regulator of glucose, lipid, and protein metabolism, the PI3K/AKT pathway is integral to the process of obesity [23,24] and mediates the proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis of 3T3-L1 cells [21,43–45].

Conclusions

In conclusion, G3BP1 may aggravate lipid accumulation and fat metabolism disorder in vitro and exert a pro-obesity effect in vivo. In addition, the impact of G3BP1 on preadipocyte invasion was likely the result of activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway, which might provide new insights for further research on G3BP1 as a new intervention target for metabolic disorders.
