A preparation program for psychological safety of hospitalized adolescents
Authors: Zahra Hadian Shirazi, Neda Jamalimoghadam, Parvin Ghaemghaemi, Mitra Daraei, Azadeh Amiri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe study aimed to investigate the impact of an information-based preparation program on the psychological safety of adolescents admitted to pediatric wards, emphasizing the importance of enhancing patient safety.MethodsThis quasi-experimental study was conducted among 98 adolescents admitted to pediatric wards at Namazi Hospital, managed by Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, in 2021. The participants were randomly assigned to either an intervention group or a control group using an electronic randomization table. The intervention group received an information-based preparation program, while the control group followed routine care. Adolescents completed the Psychological Safety Questionnaire after admission and at discharge. Data were analyzed using SPSS (Version 22), with a significance level of 0.05.ResultsThe mean psychological safety scores before the intervention were 136.73 ± 17.30 in the control group and 141.03 ± 16.34 in the intervention group, with no significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.20). After the intervention, the mean scores were 136.65 ± 19.01 in the control group and 145.50 ± 14.05 in the intervention group. A comparison of the mean psychological safety scores showed a significant difference between the two groups after the intervention (p = 0.01).ConclusionsThe findings of this study indicate that the information-based preparation program positively affected the psychological safety of hospitalized adolescents. Therefore, it is recommended that nurses incorporate this method into therapeutic programs for hospitalized adolescents to enhance their psychological safety effectively.

Introduction

Adolescence is the transitional stage from childhood to adulthood, during which significant changes occur in biological, cognitive, intellectual, and psychosocial dimensions [1]. Currently, adolescents (aged 10–19 years) make up 16% of the global population, totaling approximately 1.2 billion individuals, according to a recent publication by the World Health Organization (WHO) [2]. This period is critical for mental development, as it encompasses the formation of body image, self-concept, relationships with others, autonomy, independence, and life orientation [3].

Hospitalization can be a distressing experience for adolescents and their parents, causing significant stress [4]. This stress results from factors such as an unfamiliar environment, lack of privacy, and feelings of insecurity [5–7], along with anxiety related to separation and fear of injury or pain. Physical symptoms of anxiety disorders in adolescents include diaphoresis, palpitations, chest tightness, and nausea, often linked to autonomic nervous system activation. Panic attacks can occur, and behavioral signs include avoidance of certain activities or people [8–10].

On the other hand, hospitalization can be a threatening and stressful experience for children [11]. Unfamiliarity with the hospital environment, medical procedures, and a lack of information about the reasons for hospitalization can result in feelings of anger, insecurity, anxiety, and helplessness in adolescents [12]. High levels of anxiety can negatively affect children’s physiological and psychological health, hinder their ability to cope with therapies, increase negative emotions, and reduce cooperation with healthcare professionals [11].

Freitas et al. (2018) found that the hospitalization rate for adolescents aged 15–19 years was approximately 57.52 per 1,000 individuals [13]. According to Maslow’s theory, adolescence is a critical period for physical, cognitive, and psychosocial development, and a lack of safety can significantly hinder this development [14]. Hospitalization can cause adverse effects on adolescents and creates problems in various aspects such as physical and mental health [3]. Exposure to a stressful environment, such as a hospital, along with limited family presence especially from parents and peers can exacerbate feelings of insecurity among hospitalized adolescents [15].

In recent years, the promotion of safety culture in healthcare has gained momentum, with health organizations increasingly emphasizing the need to change organizational culture to enhance patient safety [16]. Safety is a lifelong necessity that humans strive to achieve, providing peace of mind and alleviating fear and anxiety. It is generally categorized into internal (mental) and external types, with internal safety being a fundamental aspect of human life that cannot be overlooked [17]. Psychological safety is a state in which mental and emotional needs are met. A perceived lack of safety can disrupt the body’s metabolism, potentially leading to mental and physical illness if it persists [18]. Psychological safety involves a mental assessment of one’s ability to control their environment and manage anxiety related to potential harms and threats [19, 20]. Moreover, invasive therapies and diagnostic procedures are significant stressors for hospitalized adolescents, intensifying their fears, worries, and misconceptions about hospitalization [21].

Invasive therapies and diagnostic methods are significant sources of stress for adolescents [22], often exacerbating their fears and worries during hospitalization. The loss of autonomy in this setting contributes to anxiety and a disturbance in psychological safety. One effective approach to managing anxiety for patients undergoing invasive procedures is psychological preparation. Techniques for psychological preparation can lead to reduced hospital stays, decreased use of sedatives, improved mood, shorter recovery times after surgery, and a notable reduction in fear and anxiety [23].

Studies indicate that patients need honest information about their therapies and health conditions to feel relaxed and engage in self-care [23, 24]. Psychological preparation has been shown to reduce symptom severity and anxiety [23]. Key elements of effective preparation include providing clear information about medical procedures, encouraging emotional expression, and fostering trust between healthcare professionals and patients [25–27]. Hospitalized adolescents should understand their illness and treatment processes, such as nutrition and disease management [28]. Additionally, nurses must be aware of adolescents’ needs, as effective communication between healthcare staff and adolescents enhances their understanding of their conditions, reduces stress, and promotes better treatment outcomes [5]. Pediatric nurses are essential for ensuring optimal healthcare outcomes for children by addressing their unique physical and emotional needs. Given children’s physiological and psychological differences from adults, these nurses provide specialized, age-appropriate care, accurate assessments, and tailored interventions to meet each child’s specific requirements [29].

The studied children expressed a strong desire to voice their opinions, ask questions, and receive information about their care and procedures [30]. Maor found that hospitalized adolescents require emotional, educational, and social support to reduce feelings of isolation, anxiety, and stress, noting their insufficient involvement in the therapy process [31]. These studies highlight that healthcare professionals significantly influence children’s experiences; weaker interactions lead to increased fear, poor follow-up, and potentially worse clinical outcomes [32].

Given that the stress of hospitalization negatively affects the psychological safety of adolescents, one significant factor contributing to this stress is their lack of knowledge about their illness, treatment processes, and medical procedures. Consequently, the researchers aimed to investigate the effects of an information-based preparation program on the psychological safety of adolescents admitted to pediatric wards. Notably, no similar studies have been conducted in Iran. Thus, this research seeks to explore how an information provision program can enhance the psychological safety of hospitalized adolescents.

Methods

Study type and setting

The aim of this study was to investigate the impact of an information-based training program on the psychological safety of hospitalized adolescents. This quasi-experimental study involved 89 adolescents aged 12–18 years admitted to various wards (Infectious Diseases, Cardiology, Endocrinology, and Internal Medicine) at Namazi Hospital in Shiraz, Iran. Namazi Hospital serves as a general, specialist, and subspecialist teaching facility for Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, providing diagnostic and therapeutic services while training medical and paramedical students. Established in 1956, it was once the best-equipped hospital in the Middle East. After obtaining permission from the Vice Chancellor for Research at Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, the researcher approached the selected wards to enroll adolescents meeting the following inclusion criteria: aged 12–18 years, parental consent to participate, adequate literacy to read and write, no prior hospitalizations or participation in other educational programs, no diagnosed mental illnesses, and no medications affecting mental health, as reported based on self- expression.

Sample size and sampling

Initially, 50 participants were allocated to each group. However, the final number of participants, 98 in total, was determined after excluding some individuals who did not meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria. These participants were selected using a convenience sampling method and electronically randomized. To ensure balance and minimize bias, the permuted block randomization method was employed. This method was used to maintain an approximately equal number of participants in both the control and intervention groups. It helped to ensure a relative balance in the baseline characteristics of the two groups.

Data collection

After completing the consent form by adolescents and parents, they were asked to complete demographic and Hospitalized Adolescents Psychological safety questionnaire in the presence of researcher. She explained how to fill them out and guided them to complete them in details. The adolescents were excluded from the study if they were transferred to another ward or hospital, passed away, disabled to answer the questionnaires due to any reason at any stage of the study. The questionnaires were completed at first day before education and at the discharge day after education.

Instrument

Demographic and Hospitalized Adolescents Psychological safety questionnaire, a researcher made one, were used in present investigation. Demographic questionnaire’s items included age, adolescent’s education level, parents’ occupation, the number of siblings and family history of the disease. This questionnaire was confirmed by a number of professors in the pediatric nursing department of Hazrate Fatemeh School of Nursing and Midwifery in Shiraz. Hospitalized Adolescents Psychological safety questionnaire includes six subscales (physical-professional support, psycho-spiritual support, relaxing environment, supportive presence, Strive for adaptation and respect dignity). It has 35 items with a five-point Likert scale according to the scores obtained from strongly agree (score 5) to strongly disagree (1). To determine the validity of this questionnaire, face validity, content validity and construct validity were used. The reliability of this questionnaire was determined by two methods including internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha coefficient and the Split Half) and stability (the test-re-test) were used. Content Validity Ratio (CVR) was reported 0.98 and desirable. Cronbach’s alpha was calculated (0.9) [33].

This questionnaire is scored based on a five-point Likert scale ranging from “Always” to “Never”: Positive Items: “Always” scores 5 and “Never” scores 1.Negative Items: “Never” scores 5 and “Always” scores 1.Total Questionnaire Score derived from the sum of the scores of all items. Time Required to Complete was approximately 10 to 15 min. The minimum score is 35 and the maximum score is 115. The score between 0 and 33.3 was weak. The score between 33.4 and 66.6 was medium and the score between 66.7 and 100 was strong. The intervention was performed in two first days of admission and also in the discharge day. The control group was cared based on the routine of ward.

The first day: The researcher provided information to the participants in intervention group at the first day of working with regard to the following items: physical environment of the ward, hospital regulations, ward’s routines (meals intervals, appointment hours, frequencies of taking vital signs, medication frequencies, etc.), how to use devices such as alarm lights, bed, light switches, TV, etc., the medical staff (ward nurses, their own nurse, etc.) and the episodes of changing nurses’ shifts. After providing information, the researcher accompanied the patient to the bed.

The second day; The following items were presented to the participants in the second day: the disease and its therapy process, laboratory tests and invasive measures, risk factors of disease, benefits and barriers associated with meal intake, exercise, preventive and control measures related to disease and focusing on the situation and also individual needs. Each patient’s personal beliefs and learning ability were assessed using oral explanations, pamphlets, and patient education booklets. Then, contact numbers and the times during which they could call were provided in order to answer adolescents’ and parents’ questions if the researcher was absent.

Finally, the researcher was present in the ward at the day of discharge and provided the necessary pre-discharge trainings to the adolescent and parents the following items: the process of home cares, the ways to prevent from recurrence, daily activities, dangerous signs and symptoms, social support, and necessary skills in solving health-related problems. The researcher also presented issues related to disease control, follow-ups, nutrition and other important points orally and in the form of pamphlets or educational booklets. When training was completed by the researcher, she helped the studied adolescents complete the questionnaires in both groups again. The questionnaires were completed at first day before education and at the discharge day after education.

Data analysis

Sampling was done based on the same study [23] by considering the first type error equal to 0.05, test power equal to 0.8 and using SPSS software version22. Descriptive statistics, such as means and standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, were used for all demographic variables. Two independent sample T-Test and paired sample t-test were utilized to analyze the mean scores of different variables between the two groups at two different time points respectively.

Ethical consideration

In order to conduct this research, Research Deputy and Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences obtained the permission to implement the project with the following code (18983-08-01-97). After obtaining the necessary permissions from all related departments, sampling was done. Prior to conducting the study, the necessary explanations about the confidentiality of the achieved information and its neutral interference in therapy process, discharge and follow-ups was reassured. Then, parents and their children completed consent forms. At the day of discharge, educational pamphlets and booklets were given to control group for respecting ethical principles.

Results

In this study, 50 people in each group were investigated in 2021. Finally, 98 participants were selected by convenient sampling through electronically randomized method (Permuted block randomization method). The participants were divided into control and intervention groups (49 participants in each group).

The mean age of adolescents was reported 14.31 ± 1.63 and 14.65 ± 1.79 in control and intervention groups respectively. It was found that adolescents in the control and intervention groups did not differ significantly in age (P = 0.32). The demographic variables of gender, hospital wards, father’s occupation, mother’s occupation, father’s education, mother’s education, and family history of disease were assessed between the intervention and control groups using the Chi-square test. The results indicated that the distribution of individuals across different variables did not show any statistically significant differences between the groups. In other words, the distribution of data across various variables was consistent between the two groups. It is important to note that there was no classification of the mother’s occupation into the categories of retired and worker. Discussion

The aim of this study was to determine the impact of information-based preparation program on psychological safety of adolescents admitted in pediatric wards of Shiraz Namazi Hospital. It was shown that performing information-based preparation program had significant effect on increasing psychological safety of hospitalized adolescents. The comparison of the mean scores of psychological safety between control and intervention groups demonstrated that there was no statistically significant difference between two groups before the intervention. Nevertheless, the psychological safety score of the intervention group improved after the intervention and the difference between two groups was statistically significant.

The comparison of the findings demonstrated that the mean scores of psychological safety did not differ significantly before intervention between intervention and control groups. Nevertheless, the mean score of psychological safety was statistically significant in the intervention group after intervention. These results were consistent with the findings of the study conducted by Behrouzian et al. in 2017 showed the impact of psychological preparation before upper gastrointestinal endoscopy on patients’ anxiety levels. Before the intervention was carried out, there was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of the rate of state and trait anxiety. After the intervention, the rate of state and trait anxiety decreased significantly in experimental group. The experimental group was psychologically prepared for endoscopy (given information about endoscopy and behavioral intervention), and then both groups were asked to complete STAI again an hour before endoscopy [23].

In addition, the investigation conducted by Majzoubi et al. revealed the impact of psychological readiness to reduce the symptoms of surgical anxiety in children [34]. Fernandes et al. in 2014 achieved the similar results related to the impact of providing preoperative information on reducing preoperative anxiety in children undergoing surgery [35]. Therefore, psychological preparation can be effective as a supportive way in making correct children’s misconceptions, reducing their anxiety and increasing their psychological safety. The comparison of the findings demonstrated that the mean scores of psychological safety’s subscales did not differ significantly before intervention between intervention and control groups. However, the mean scores of psychological safety subscales revealed that the mean scores of psychological safety’s subscales including physical-professional support and dignity differed significantly after intervention between intervention and control groups. The comparison of the achieved results showed that the mean scores of psychological safety’s subscales did not differ significantly before and after intervention in control group. However, the mean scores of physical- professional support, psycho- spiritual and dignity subscales were considerably improved in intervention group due to implementation of preparation programs based on information provision. In addition, the comparison of the achieved results showed that although the mean scores of psychological safety’s subscales did not differ significantly before and after intervention in control group, the provision of information had a significant effect on increasing mean scores of psychological safety of three subscales including physical- professional support, psycho-spiritual support and dignity in the intervention group.

The evidence shows that hospitalized adolescents have basic needs such as emotional, educational needs and also they require to receive social support to help them experience less isolation, anxiety and stress [31]. The findings of the study conducted by Nayeri et al. (2011) showed the importance of understanding and respecting privacy and interaction between nurses and adolescent patients [36]. Lerwick’s focused, in his study, on some principles of medical therapies including informing the patients and families, recognizing the children’s fears and also providing emotional support in order to reduce their anxiety and psychological disturbance caused by health to promote their psychological safety [37].

These studies address three important factors affecting mental safety, including physical-professional support, psycho-spiritual support and respecting dignity of hospitalized adolescents. They seem to be comparable to the subscales of present study. On the other hand, the findings of the present study revealed that the mean scores of psychological safety decreased over time in the control group in terms of some subscales including physical-professional support, psycho-spiritual support and relaxing environment As recent studies have shown, health care professionals have major impact on the experiences of children and adolescents so that weaker interactions can lead to fear, poor follow-ups and potentially worse clinical outcomes [32].

Since unavoidable conditions have been occurred recently in the wards due to COVID-19 pandemics and some pediatric wards have been allocated for COVID-19 patients, the fear and anxiety of COVID-19 transmission and reciprocal isolation and physical limitations performed after pandemics have led to reduction of health care staff interactions with patients. Therefore, the decrease in the mean scores of subscales of physical-professional support, psycho-spiritual support and relaxing environment is not unexpected in the control group. The findings of Salari et al.’s investigation has shown that COVID-19 not only causes physical health concerns, but also it has brought about a number of psychological disorders and mental health problems in different communities. The researcher emphasized the need to maintain people’s mental health and design psychological interventions which can improve mental health of vulnerable groups during this pandemics [38]. Psychological safety is the belief that it is safe to freely voice concerns and ideas, with little or no fear of reprimand or belittlement. A psychologically safe environment is necessary for people to learn from errors and engage in a culture of continuous reflection and improvement [39].

Limitations

One of the limitations of this research was poor cooperation of some adolescents or their parents, which lead to exclude them from the study. Other limitations of the study included critical conditions made by the prevalence of COVID-19 disease and the isolation of some parts of the research environment leading to reduce sampling. The researcher (forth author) tried to explain about COVID-19 disease and its protection ways to increase participant’s cooperation. However, this study was able to improve the psychological safety of hospitalized adolescents despite the critical situation and provide valuable evidence to improve the psychological safety of adolescents.

Conclusion

Given the fact that numerous stressors in the hospital may lead to short-term or even long-term psychological consequences in adolescents and they are forced to endure isolation, anxiety and stress in pediatric wards, it is essential to pay more attention to the principles of supportive care. It is very important to provide psychological training in pediatric wards. According to the results of this study, the positive effects of the training program based on providing information on the psychological safety in admitted adolescents is recommended as a simple, practical and effective way to improve the mental health and even physical health of adolescents. However, more research is needed to be conducted in this field due to different achieved results related to studied subscale of psychological safety in present investigation.
