Intracerebral arterial blood pressure in the vasculature distal to large vessel occlusions in patients with ischemic stroke: correlation with clinical and imaging parameters
Authors: Moritz Umhau, Niclas Schmitt, Jessica Jesser, Tim Hilgenfeld, Min Chen, Peter A Ringleb, Markus A Möhlenbruch, Michael O Breckwoldt, Martin Bendszus, Dominik F Vollherbst
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionMechanical thrombectomy is a highly effective treatment for acute ischemic stroke (AIS) caused by large vessel occlusions (LVO). However, our understanding of the pathophysiology of AIS is still limited, particularly regarding the ischemic microenvironment distal to the occlusion.AimTo investigate the relationship between the intracerebral blood pressure (BP) distal to an LVO and clinical and imaging parameters.MethodsIn this single-center prospective study, intracerebral BPs proximal and distal to the occluding clot were measured during thrombectomy in patients with AIS of the anterior circulation caused by LVO. BPs were correlated with imaging parameters and clinical data using Spearman’s rank correlation and linear regression.Results25 patients were included. A significant correlation was found between the mean arterial pressure (MAP) distal to the occlusion and the baseline Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (Spearman r=0.503, P=0.014) and baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score (r=−0.439, P=0.036). No significant correlation was observed between the MAP distal to the occlusion and collateral scores (eg, MAP vs American Society of Interventional and Therapeutic Neuroradiology/Society of Interventional Radiology score (r=0.307, P=0.165) or modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score (pre-mRS vs 90-day mRS; r=0.013, P=0.952)). Systemic MAPs did not correlate with the MAP distal to the clot (eg, R2=0.029, P=0.593).ConclusionThe intracerebral BP measured in the ischemic vasculature distal to the LVO correlates with the extent of the ischemic core and the clinical severity at baseline, but not with collateral scores, systemic BPs, or functional outcome. These results shed light on the pathophysiology of BP mechanisms in AIS and can be the basis for further research in this field.

Introduction

Ischemic stroke is among the most common causes of death worldwide and is the main reason for acquired disabilities in adults.1 Since 2015, mechanical thrombectomy (MT) has been established as a highly effective treatment for acute ischemic stroke (AIS) caused by large vessel occlusions (LVO).2 3 However, not all patients benefit from this treatment, despite clinically and radiologically similar baseline characteristics.

The blood pressure (BP) and collaterals supplying the ischemic core and tissue at risk (also called the penumbra), which are thought to be linked to each other, play a key role in the development of the resulting infarction and thus in the outcome of patients with AIS.46 Patent collaterals are associated with a good outcome, while this effect is particularly pronounced in patients who do not undergo thrombectomy.7 8 However, regarding the association between BP and collateral status, the current data are inconsistent.914 In some studies the admission BP of patients with AIS showed a U-shaped relationship with risks of mortality, dependency, and recurrent stroke.911 Other studies did not observe this relationship but suggest that higher BP is linked to poorer neurological recovery and functional outcome.12 Also, regarding the association between BP and collaterals, current data are inconclusive. While Hong et al found that higher baseline BP was associated with better collateral flow,13 Wufuer and colleagues showed that a moderately decreased BP is associated with good integrity of the collateral circulation.14 Another study found that raised initial BP was associated with poor cerebral collateral flow and more severe symptoms in patients with cardioembolic stroke but not in those with AIS caused by large artery atherosclerosis, suggesting differential effects of initial BP elevation on collateral flow depending on stroke subtypes.15

As a major limitation, all previous studies investigated the systemic BP of patients with AIS. However, the systemic BP might not adequately reflect the local BP in the vasculature of the ischemic tissue at risk. This ischemic brain parenchyma, which is supplied by the vessel segments distal to the clot, is clearly being affected by hypoperfusion and is one of the main denominators of stroke outcome. Therefore, the ischemic tissue with its microenvironment can be regarded as a ‘black box’ which has scarcely been investigated.

The aim of this work was therefore to invasively measure the arterial BP in the vasculature of the brain parenchyma distal to the occluding clot in patients with AIS undergoing MT. We focused on the assessment of patient-specific clinical and imaging parameters and correlated these parameters with this pressure to explore its value, and thus to draw possible conclusions about pathophysiological processes.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a single-center prospective interventional trial which was performed from October 2022 to April 2024 with approval of the local ethics committee (reference number: S234-2022). Informed consent was waived by the institutional review board. The study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study inclusion criteria were: AIS (onset <24 hours), initial complete or near-complete occlusion (expanded Treatment in Cerebral Infarction (eTICI) score 0 or 1) of a large brain-supplying vessel of the anterior circulation (carotid-T or M1 segment of the middle cerebral artery), decision for MT and MT using a stent-retriever as first-line approach. The exclusion criteria were: very late time window (≥24 hours), incomplete occlusion (eTICI score >1), distal occlusion (M2 segment of the middle cerebral artery or anterior cerebral artery) or occlusion in the posterior circulation, and first-line direct aspiration thrombectomy (without crossing the clot with a microcatheter). There were no restrictions regarding baseline National Institute of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) or modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores.

Clinical parameters

Time to onset of symptoms or last seen well (LSW), baseline NIHSS, NIHSS at 24 hours, pre-stroke mRS and mRS at 90 days were assessed by a board-certified neurologist. Deterioration in the mRS score (comparing the pre-stroke mRS and the mRS at 90 days) was calculated and defined as ΔmRS. The patients’ sex, age, baseline antithrombotic medication, and etiology of the stroke were also recorded.

Imaging parameters

All enrolled patients underwent the same in-hospital clinical standard of care treatments: patients received non-contrast CT imaging followed by CT angiography. CT perfusion was only performed in the late time window (>6 hours). Baseline Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (ASPECTS) and ASPECTS at 24 hours were rated independently by two readers (DFV with 8 years and NS with 5 years of experience in neuroradiology). In case of discrepancy, a consensus reading was performed (by MAM with 16 years of experience).

In the angiogram prior to MT, the eTICI score as well as two different collateral scores (the American Society of Interventional and Therapeutic Neuroradiology/Society of Interventional Radiology (ASITN/SIR) score16 and the Christoforidis score17) were assessed independently by two readers (DFV and MOB with 4 years in neuroradiology). In case of discrepancy, a consensus was made (by MAM).

Mechanical thrombectomy (MT)

MT was performed according to institutional standards. All MTs were performed under conscious sedation. After placing an 8 French sheath into the common femoral artery, an 8 French balloon guide catheter or a standard 8 French guide catheter was placed in the internal carotid artery (ICA). After digital subtraction angiography (DSA), BP measurement in the ICA was performed via the guiding catheter. A 5 or 6 French aspiration catheter was then coaxially positioned within the distal ICA and, with the aid of a 0.014 inch microwire, a 0.021 inch microcatheter was positioned distal to the clot in the distal M2 segment of the middle cerebral artery. The microcatheter was then cleared by aspiration of the flushing solution until the aspiration of blood using a 2.5 mL syringe. Under X-ray control injecting contrast agent, correct positioning of the microcatheter was verified. After BP measurement, a stent retriever was inserted and deployed and MT was performed under continuous aspiration at the aspiration and guiding catheter and under proximal balloon occlusion (in case a balloon catheter was used) according to the BADDASS technique.18 In case of incomplete recanalization, additional MT maneuvers were performed using the same technique or using direct aspiration or, in case of unsuccessful MT, rescue stenting. The degree of recanalization was assessed according to the eTICI score.

Blood pressure measurements

The procedure and positions of BP measurement are shown in figure 1.

Illustration of the different locations and time points of blood pressure measurement. Top: The invasive blood pressure measurement is shown. An initial measurement was taken in the internal carotid artery (ICA) proximal to the occlusion (i-BP-PTO). After navigating a microcatheter distal to the clot, the blood pressure distal to the occlusion was measured (i-BP-DTO). Both measurement methods were recorded via a central monitor.

Intra-arterial BPs (systolic, diastolic, and mean arterial pressure (MAP)) were assessed in the cervical ICA proximal to the occlusion (i-BP-PTO) via the guiding catheter before the first recanalization attempt, distal to the occlusion (i-BP-DTO) via the microcatheter (before insertion of the stent retriever), and at the end of the procedure after recanalization in the ICA via the guiding catheter, positioned in the ICA (i-BP-REC). In case of carotid-T occlusions with pseudo-occlusion of the ICA, BP measurement was performed in the proximal ICA adjacent to the carotid bifurcation. At the same time points, non-invasive systemic BPs were measured and defined as ni-BP-1, ni-BP-2 and ni-BP-3.

To measure the invasive arterial BP, a special study set-up was established: a tube system for invasive BP measurement was prepared on the operating table and flushed via a pressure infusion system. The system was then connected to a pressure transducer at the level of the heart and connected to the monitor provided by the neurointensivist or anesthesiologist. i-BPs were then measured by connecting the measurement tubes to the hubs of the catheters. ni-BPs were measured using a conventional BP cuff at the patient’s left arm.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as median (first quartile; third quartile), as absolute number (relative frequency in %), or as mean±SD. For the analysis of the BPs, the MAP was calculated (MAP = diastolic BP + 1/3 × (systolic BP – diastolic BP)). MAPs at the different locations were statistically compared using the Friedman test with post-hoc Dunn’s test. To analyze the association between invasive MAPs at the three different locations and the respective non-invasive systemic MAPs, simple linear regression analysis was performed. The correlation between the MAP distal to the occlusion (i-BP-DTO) and clinical (time to onset or LSW, baseline NIHSS, NIHSS at 24 hours, mRS at 90 days, ΔmRS (mRS at 90 days – pre-stroke mRS)) and imaging parameters (baseline ASPECTS, ASPECTS at 24 hours, ASITN/SIR score, Christoforidis score and eTICI score) was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation. To investigate the influence of the time since stroke onset or LSW and the MAP distal to the occlusion with the neurological outcome (ΔmRS (initial mRS vs mRS at 90 days) and NIHSS change (initial NIHSS vs NIHSS at 24 hours)), patients were dichotomized into those with a long/short time window (≥4 hours/>4 hours) and those with a high/low MAP (≥45 mmHg/>45 mmHg). These groups were then compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test with post-hoc Dunn’s test. All tests were two-tailed and considered significant at P<0.05. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism Software (Prism Version 10.2.1).

Results

Twenty-five patients treated between October 2022 and April 2024 who underwent thrombectomies at our center were included in this study.

Patient characteristics, clinical and imaging parameters

The median age was 79 (71; 86) years and most patients (76.0%) were female. The most frequent location of the occlusion was the M1 segment of the middle cerebral artery (68.0%), followed by the carotid-T (32.0%). The time from stroke onset or LSW was 210 min (154 min; 378 min).

Intravenous thrombolysis was administered in 12 patients (48.0%). Eighteen patients (72.0%) had received antithrombotic medication prior to the stroke, most frequently acetylsalicylic acid 100 mg daily. The etiology of stroke was identified as cardioembolic in 13 cases (52.0%), large artery atherosclerosis in two cases (8.0%), paraneoplastic thrombophilia was detected in two cases (8.0%), and paradox embolism in one case (4.0%). In seven cases (28.0%) the etiology was unknown.

The NIHSS score at baseline and at 24 hours was 16 (12; 21) and 15 (8; 23), respectively. The pre-stroke mRS score was 0 in 32.0%, 1 in 32.0%, 2 in 4.0%, 3 in 24.0%, 4 in 4.0%, 5 in 0%, and unknown in 4.0%. The mRS score at 90 days was 0 in 4.0%, 1 in 20.0%, 2 in 4.0%, 3 in 24.0%, 4 in 20.0%, 5 in 8.0%, and 6 in 20.0%.

The ASPECTS score at baseline was 8 (7; 9.5), while all patients had an ASPECTS of ≥4. At 24 hours the ASPECTS score was 7 (7; 9.5). The ASITN/SIR collateral score was 1 (0; 2) and the Christoforidis collateral score was 4 (3; 5).

MT procedures

The median number of MT maneuvers per treatment was 3 (1; 4). Complete or near-complete recanalization (eTICI 2c or 3) after one pass was achieved in eight patients (32.0%). In six patients (24.0%) the MT technique was switched from using a stent retriever to direct aspiration. Final eTICI was 2b50 in one case (4.0%), 2b67 in four cases (16.0%), eTICI 2c in 10 cases (40.0%), and eTICI 3 in 10 cases (40.0%). In four cases (three patients with M1 occlusions and one patient with a carotid-T occlusion), rescue stenting was performed to achieve recanalization. In one case, after successful MT a direct fistula between the carotid artery and cavernous sinus was observed. The fistula was occluded 2 weeks after the MT by parent vessel occlusion using coils without any persistent sequelae. No further periprocedural complications were observed.

Correlation of the BP distal to the occlusion with clinical and imaging parameters

The results of the Spearman’s rank correlation analysis are summarized in table 2 and graphically illustrated in figure 3. Statistical analysis showed a significant correlation between the i-BP-DTO and the baseline ASPECTS score (P=0.014) and the baseline NIHSS score (P=0.036): the lower the BP distal to the occlusion, the lower the ASPECTS and the higher the NIHSS. For example, patients with i-BP-DTO <60 mmHg had a median ASPECTS of 7, while patients with a i-BP-DTO of ≥60 mmHg had a median ASPECTS of 10. Regarding NIHSS, the median NIHSS score was 18 for patients with i-BP-DTO of <60 mmHg and 12 for patients with i-BP-DTO of ≥60 mmHg. However, this was only the case for the baseline ASPECTS and NIHSS scores. At 24 hours, ASPECTS and NIHSS scores did not correlate with the BP in the vasculature distal to the clot (P=0.465 and P=0.104, respectively). Also, for the collateral scores and for ΔmRS and mRS at 90 days, no statistically significant correlation was identified.

Discussion

Despite extensive clinical research in AIS caused by LVO, our understanding of the pathophysiology of AIS is in some parts still limited, particularly regarding the pathophysiology of the hypoperfused brain parenchyma distal to the clot. In this prospective clinical-experimental study, the microenvironment in the ‘black box’, the ischemic tissue distal to the occlusion in AIS, was investigated in patients undergoing MT for anterior circulation stroke due to LVO. Research on the relationship between BP, collateral status, and neurological outcome of patients with AIS has so far been based on systemic, mostly non-invasive, BP measurements. Here we report the first study on invasive BP measurement distal to the occluded vessel.

The main novel finding of this study is that the MAP in the vessels distal to the occlusion correlates with the extent of the ischemic core, represented by the baseline ASPECTS, and with the clinical severity of the stroke, represented by the baseline NIHSS score. Thus, higher BPs in the vasculature distal to the occlusion correlate with higher ASPECT scores and a correspondingly smaller ischemic core, and higher BPs distal to the occlusion correlate with lower NIHSS scores and correspondingly less severe symptoms at presentation. The correlation between BP and infarct extent suggests that the BP distal to an LVO may play an important role in the pathophysiology of infarct growth. The inverse correlation of the BP in the tissue distal to the occlusion with the NIHSS score shows the clinical relevance of these findings.

The second main finding of this work is that the BP measured distal to the occlusion does not correlate with the systemic BP, which was measured non-invasively at the same time, while invasively-measured BPs proximal to the occlusion and after recanalization do correlate with the systemic BP. This is a somewhat surprising finding which highlights that the perfusion pressure in the vasculature distal to the occlusion might be individual and linked to additional factors other than just systemic BP. Possible explanations for this finding are the reduction in the BP by the LVO and impairment of the autoregulation of the ischemic brain in AIS. It is known that cerebral blood flow is directly related to the BP and inversely related to the resistance of the vasculature.19 20 The brain vessels can normally adapt to acute changes by adjusting resistance, thereby ensuring metabolic supply. Autoregulation mechanisms enable the cerebral vasculature to maintain a stable blood flow within perfusion pressures ranging from 50 to 150 mmHg.20 Smooth muscle cells in the precapillary arterioles respond to pressure-dependent stimuli, detected through changes in wall tension, to modulate resistance accordingly.20 In the setting of AIS, cerebral autoregulation can be substantially impaired. This is thought to occur due to the maximal dilatation of arterioles in the context of acute ischemia.21 In the presence of ischemia, however, the brain vessels can fail to alter their vasoconstrictive response and thus be unable to adjust the resistance of the vasculature.19 Furthermore, due to the proximal LVO, even if autoregulatory mechanisms apply, they may not be enough to achieve a sufficiently high BP in the vasculature distal to the occluding clot.

There is a general notion that good collaterals are associated with a favorable neurological outcome.7 8 Good collaterals are associated with a decreased infarct growth22 23 and, at the same time, the infarct core as a whole is reduced in size.24 We hypothesize that good collaterals lead to a higher BP in the ischemic tissue which they supply. However, in this study we did not observe a correlation between the MAP in the ischemic vasculature and two different well-established collateral scores. This phenomenon might also be explained by the impaired autoregulation in the infarcted area. In this regard, a limitation of our study has to be considered: the BP distal to the occlusion was only measured in one specific position, which was most frequently the distal M2 segment of the middle cerebral artery. BPs may vary within the vasculature distal to an LVO and might be substantially higher at the outer boundaries of the tissue-at-risk, the location where collaterals deliver their blood into the territory of the occluded artery.

The time since the event of the vascular occlusion and the recanalization of a vessel plays a crucial role in the treatment of AIS. To explore the influence of this time and the BP distal to the occlusion on the neurological outcome, further analyses were performed in this study. No conclusive differences were found, but a tendency towards better outcomes was observed, especially for patients treated in the early time window. This may indicate that the BP in the occluded vasculature is particularly important in the first hours after the occurrence of the LVO.

In this study we aimed to explore the pathophysiology of AIS in patients treated with MT, focusing on the BP in the vasculature distal to an LVO. However, it should be emphasized that, currently, these BP measurements are only of scientific value and are not suitable to influence treatment decisions or estimate the prognosis of patients.

Our work has several limitations. The number of included patients was rather low, primarily due to the complexity of the BP measurement set-up in the emergency setting of thrombectomy, which required extensive work-up and additional personnel in addition to the acute stroke team. This is also the reason why not every eligible consecutive stroke patient could be included into this study during the study period. Additionally, different catheters were used for the BP measurement (eg, guiding catheters for measurements in the ICA and microcatheters for the measurements distal to the clot), which might have influenced the results of this study. Furthermore, as CT perfusion (CTP) was not performed in all patients, CTP parameters were not part of this study. The correlation between CTP and BP measurements should be addressed in future studies. Another limitation is that BP measurement was not technically successful in all cases. However, the rate of successful BP measurement of 89% in this study can be considered as relatively high. In four patients, rescue stenting was performed because of unsuccessful or incomplete recanalization. An underlying stenosis was not explicitly observed in these patients; however, an underlying intracranial atherosclerotic disease might lead to different BPs as BP physiology and dynamics might be different in this subgroup.

Conclusion

Invasive BP measurement using the catheters which are being used for MT in patients with AIS with LVO is technically feasible. The intracerebral BP measured in the ischemic tissue distal to the LVO correlates with the extent of the ischemic core (ie, ASPECTS) and with the clinical severity of the stroke (ie, NIHSS) at presentation, but not with collateral scores or systemic BPs. These results shed light on the pathophysiology of the BP mechanisms in AIS. Future studies with larger patient numbers should be performed to further investigate the microenvironment in the ischemic tissue distal to the occlusion in AIS.
