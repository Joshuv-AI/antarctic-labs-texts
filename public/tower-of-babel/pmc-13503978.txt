Efficacy and Safety of Mechanical Insufflation–Exsufflation in Invasively Ventilated Critically Ill Adults: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials
Authors: Zhiqun Liu, Guiling Liao, Hui Wen, Xiaotong Han, Maiying Fan, Shuzhen Mao, Xiaohua Liu, Huan Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMechanical insufflation–exsufflation (MI–E) is increasingly used in invasively ventilated adults in the intensive care unit (ICU), yet its therapeutic efficacy and safety remain uncertain due to inconsistent evidence.AimTo synthesize evidence on the clinical efficacy and safety of MI–E in this population and to examine methodological and clinical heterogeneity underlying reported outcomes.Study DesignA systematic review and meta‐analysis of randomized studies (including RCTs and randomized crossover trials), conducted following PRISMA guidelines, with risk of bias assessed using the Cochrane risk‐of‐bias tool.ResultsFive randomized controlled trials involving 310 patients were included. Introduction

Mechanical ventilation is a critical life‐support intervention for patients in the intensive care unit (ICU) [1]. However, due to impaired cough function and the presence of artificial airways, mechanically ventilated patients often experience increased airway resistance and decreased lung compliance [2]. These physiological changes promote the retention of pulmonary secretions, which in turn may lead to pneumonia, atelectasis and other pulmonary infections, ultimately prolonging the duration of mechanical ventilation and hospital stay [3]. Therefore, effective airway management is essential to facilitate early weaning and extubation, reduce ICU length of stay and lower healthcare costs. The current standard approach primarily consists of manual aspiration combined with chest physiotherapy (e.g., percussion, vibration and postural drainage) [4, 5]. Nevertheless, conventional methods fail to significantly improve cough function and may be associated with patient discomfort or airway trauma [6, 7]. Consequently, a more efficacious adjunctive therapy is needed.

Mechanical insufflation–exsufflation (MI–E) is a non‐invasive technique designed to clear respiratory secretions. By applying alternating positive and negative pressure to the airway, MI–E simulates the physiological cough mechanism, thereby enhancing peak expiratory flow (PEF) rates [8, 9]. This modality facilitates airway secretion clearance, improves patient oxygenation and increases lung compliance [10]. Compared with conventional open suctioning, MI–E has been shown to avoid pain and agitation in intubated patients while demonstrating a comparable safety profile in terms of adverse events [11]. MI–E has been safely and effectively implemented across a wide range of patients with impaired cough function, particularly those with neuromuscular disorders [12, 13, 14].

Based on this success, MI–E has been increasingly adopted in the ICU for invasively ventilated adults who retain an artificial airway (endotracheal tube or tracheostomy). From a theoretical perspective, MI–E holds significant potential for assisting mechanically ventilated patients with an artificial airway in the ICU to clear respiratory secretions. This theoretical advantage is supported by clinical evidence from a retrospective cohort study conducted in a Japanese ICU, which reported that the incidence of ventilator‐associated pneumonia (VAP) was significantly lower in patients receiving MI–E compared with those receiving conventional chest physical therapy (26.4% vs. 84.2%, p = 0.011), and multivariate analysis confirmed MI–E as an independent protective factor against VAP [15].

Although MI–E technology has significant theoretical advantages and is well established in patients with neuromuscular diseases, its application in the specific population of mechanically ventilated critically ill adults remains exploratory, and the underlying clinical evidence is still weak. Most existing relevant studies are small‐sample randomized crossover trials, feasibility studies or retrospective analyses.

Several randomized controlled trials have evaluated the effects of MIE in invasively ventilated adults, including both parallel‐group and crossover designs. In a randomized crossover trial, Martínez‐Alejos et al. found that adding MIE to expiratory rib cage compressions significantly increased the volume of cleared sputum compared with compressions alone, without clinically important haemodynamic changes [16]. In another crossover trial, Nunes et al. demonstrated that MIE at higher pressures (±50 cmH2O) improved static compliance and reduced airway resistance, while removing a greater amount of secretions than lower pressures or isolated suctioning [17]. In a parallel‐group RCT, Ferreira de Camillis et al. reported that MIE combined with standard physiotherapy resulted in a larger weight of aspirated mucus and a greater improvement in lung compliance compared with physiotherapy alone [18]. In contrast, other trials have failed to demonstrate superiority over conventional care. In a crossover trial, Coutinho et al. observed no significant differences in secretion volume, respiratory mechanics or haemodynamics between MI–E and conventional endotracheal suctioning [19]. Similarly, in a parallel‐group RCT, Kubota et al. found that the use of MI–E did not significantly increase ventilator‐free days at day 28 compared with conventional care in patients with high sputum retention [20]. Furthermore, safety concerns have been raised across studies, including transient desaturation, bradycardia and haemodynamic instability, although the incidence and clinical significance of these events remain poorly quantified [16].

However, the current literature lacks systematic reviews based on the highest level of evidence (randomized controlled trials) and offers no in‐depth analysis of the ‘implementation heterogeneity’ present across existing studies. Such analysis of heterogeneity is essential for translating evidence into actionable clinical guidelines. This study aims to conduct a rigorous systematic review integrating all current randomized evidence to systematically evaluate the efficacy and safety of MI–E in invasively ventilated adult patients; critically analyse the heterogeneity in implementation protocols and outcomes among existing randomized trials; and, based on these analyses, provide clearer references for clinical decision‐making while promoting standardization of future research in this field.

Methods

Systematic Review Registration

The systematic review was conducted in adherence to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses) guidelines. A protocol was developed according to the PRISMA‐P (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis Protocols) framework and prospectively registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; Registration Number: CRD42023403299).

Search Strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted across MEDLINE, EMBASE, PubMed, CINAHL, CNKI and the Wanfang Database, encompassing all publications available through January 1, 2026, without language restrictions. The search strategy integrated subject headings, keywords and Boolean logical operators, augmented by manual reference screening. The core concepts covered invasive mechanical ventilation, MI–E and adult ICU patients. Detailed search strings for each database are presented in Document S1.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

This review critically analysed and compared the clinical effects of MI–E versus conventional airway clearance techniques in invasively mechanically ventilated patients. The eligibility criteria were defined according to the PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome) framework. Inclusion criteria: (1) Population: adult patients (≥ 18 years) receiving invasive mechanical ventilation via an endotracheal tube (orotracheal or nasotrachea; tracheostomy excluded) in the intensive care unit (ICU), regardless of underlying aetiology, (2) Intervention: airway clearance using mechanical insufflation–exsufflation (MI–E) technology administered directly through the endotracheal tube, with explicit description of implementation parameters (e.g., pressure settings, cycle frequency, timing), (3) Comparison: conventional airway clearance techniques (e.g., manual aspiration, chest physiotherapy, closed suctioning), (4) Outcomes: at least one pre‐defined efficacy or safety outcome. Efficacy outcomes include sputum clearance (volume or weight), oxygenation status (SpO2, PaO2/FiO2 ratio), respiratory mechanics (airway resistance, lung compliance), duration of mechanical ventilation, and ICU length of stay. Safety outcomes refer to documented adverse events (e.g., haemodynamic changes, desaturation, barotrauma), and (5) Study design: randomized controlled trials (RCTs), including both parallel‐group and crossover designs. Exclusion criteria: (1) studies conducted exclusively in home care or non‐ICU settings (as they do not address the target population of critically ill invasively ventilated adults), (2) use of in‐line mechanical insufflation–exsufflation technology, (3) MI‐E applied via tracheostomy tube or mask interface, and (4) observational studies, reviews, editorials, conference proceedings, commentaries, abstract‐only publications or non‐human studies.

Data Extraction

Two investigators independently conducted comprehensive literature screening utilizing EndNote. The reference lists of included articles were also screened to identify additional eligible studies. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer to achieve consensus. When study eligibility remained unclear or insufficient data were reported, corresponding authors were contacted for clarification.

Extracted information encompassed: study design, purpose, hypothesis, patient characteristics, inclusion/exclusion criteria, recruitment and study completion rates, intervention and control measures; and outcomes (including assessment methods, specific results and conclusions). Data pertaining to methods, patient demographics, interventions, control interventions and outcomes were systematically extracted.

Quality Assessment

The methodological quality of the included randomized controlled trials was assessed independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane Collaboration's Risk of Bias tool. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. The following domains were evaluated: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting and other sources of bias. Each domain was rated as ‘low risk’, ‘unclear risk’ or ‘high risk’ of bias.

Data Synthesis and Statistical Analysis

Meta‐analyses were performed for outcomes reported by at least two randomized controlled trials with extractable data using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.4. Due to anticipated clinical heterogeneity in patient populations, MI–E pressure settings and comparator interventions, a random effects model was employed for all pooled analyses.

For sputum volume, studies reported results as either volume (mL) or weight (g). Assuming the density of respiratory secretions approximates 1.0 g/mL, both measures were considered to reflect the same physiological construct. To accommodate the different measurement units, the standardized mean difference (SMD) with Hedges' g correction for small sample bias was calculated with 95% confidence intervals (CI). For oxygenation (SpO2/SaO2), both measures were expressed as percentages and were considered clinically equivalent; therefore, the mean difference (MD) was used as the effect measure.

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I 2 statistic, with values of 25%, 50% and 75% interpreted as low, moderate and high heterogeneity, respectively. For the primary outcome of sputum volume, a sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding one study [19] that reported substantially higher secretion volumes than the other trials, suggesting potential differences in measurement protocol. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Quality of Evidence Assessment (GRADE)

The quality of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) system. Evidence quality started as ‘high’ and was downgraded based on five factors: ① risk of bias (assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool), ②inconsistency (heterogeneity, judged by I 2 statistic: I 2 ≥ 50% with p < 0.10 was considered serious [−1], I 2 ≥ 75% very serious [−2]), ③ indirectness (extent to which population, intervention, comparator and outcome matched the PICO question), ④ imprecision (small sample size or 95% confidence interval crossing the clinical decision threshold), and ⑤ publication bias (small‐study effects or funnel plot asymmetry). Each factor was downgraded by one level for serious and by two levels for very serious concerns. Final quality was rated as high (⊕⊕⊕⊕), moderate (⊕⊕⊕○), low (⊕⊕○○) or very low (⊕○○○). Heterogeneity (inconsistency) was emphasized as a key downgrading factor to address variability across available studies.

Results

Literature Selection Process

A systematic search of the databases yielded 146 records. After removing 12 duplicates, 134 records were screened by title and abstract, of which 92 were excluded as clearly irrelevant. The remaining 42 full‐text articles were assessed for eligibility. Based on the pre‐specified PICO criteria, 37 articles were excluded for the following reasons: (1) population not meeting eligibility criteria (n = 4), (2) ineligible study design (n = 24), and (3) intervention or application not meeting the protocol (n = 9). Consequently, five studies [16, 17, 18, 19, 20] met all eligibility criteria and were included in the systematic review and meta‐analysis. Flowchart of the selection process for the included studies.

Risk of Bias

Risk of bias assessments for the five included trials are summarized in Figure 2. All studies demonstrated low risk in random sequence generation, incomplete outcome data, and selective reporting. Allocation concealment was low risk in two studies and unclear in three. Blinding of participants and personnel was high risk in four studies, reflecting the non‐blinded nature of physiotherapy interventions. Blinding of outcome assessment was low risk in four studies and unclear in one. Other bias was unclear in two cross‐over trials due to lack of first‐period data reporting.

Risk of bias of the included randomized controlled trials.

Overall, one study [18] was judged as low risk across all domains. Three studies [16, 17, 20] were rated as having some concerns, primarily related to allocation concealment or cross‐over design limitations. One study [19] was assessed as high risk due to unclear allocation concealment, unclear outcome assessor blinding and cross‐over analytical shortcomings.

Inclusion of Study Characteristics and Implementation Heterogeneity Analysis

The two randomized controlled trials and three randomized crossover trials all enrolled adult patients. The included studies encompassed ICU populations from diverse countries and regions (France, Brazil and Japan). Patients presenting with pneumothorax, emphysema, empyema with fistula, haemoptysis or haemodynamic instability were uniformly excluded, as these are well‐documented contraindications.

The included studies did not report baseline comorbidities or disease severity comparisons between intervention and control groups. Concerning the timing of MI–E initiation for mechanically ventilated ICU patients, three studies commenced intervention after > 24 h of ventilation, whereas the other two involved patients ventilated for > 48 h.

With the exception of one study [17] utilizing two distinct pressure parameters, all other investigations employed pressures of ±40 cmH2O, specifying the MI–E device model used. Variations were observed in specific intervention parameters across studies, including the number of cycles, pause duration and inhalation time ratio during MI–E application. Primary outcome measures in four studies [16, 17, 18, 19] were sputum volume and respiratory mechanics. The remaining randomized controlled trial [20] defined ventilator‐free days at day 28 as its primary endpoint; secondary endpoints included ventilator days, ICU length of stay, mortality, and tracheostomy rates among survivors.

Heterogeneity existed in outcome measurement methodologies. For quantifying secretion clearance, some studies measured aspirated sputum volume [17, 18, 19], while others employed gravimetric assessment [18]. The timing of outcome assessments also varied, encompassing measurements: pre‐intervention, immediately post‐intervention, following post‐intervention endotracheal suctioning, and at 10, 15, 30 and 60 min post‐intervention [16, 17, 18, 19] (see Table 1 for details).

Physiological Indicators

Sputum Volume/Weight.

Sputum Volume/Weight.

Sputum Volume/Weight.

Four studies [16, 17, 18, 19] provided quantitative data on sputum clearance suitable for meta‐analysis. Because one study [16] reported sputum volume in millilitres (mL) and the others reported weight in grams (g), the standardized mean difference (SMD) was calculated using a random effects model. The pooled analysis of all four studies showed a significant increase in sputum clearance favouring mechanical insufflation–exsufflation (MI–E) compared with control interventions (SMD = 0.63, 95% CI, 0.32–0.93; p < 0.0001; I 2 = 38%; Figure 3).

Forest plot of sputum volume/weight meta‐analysis.

One study [19] reported markedly higher absolute secretion weights (mean > 8 g) than the other three trials, suggestive of differences in measurement protocol or patient population. A pre‐specified sensitivity analysis excluding this study yielded a consistent and slightly stronger pooled effect with reduced heterogeneity (SMD = 0.75, 95% CI, 0.39–1.10; I 2 = 32%; Figure 4).

Sensitivity analysis of sputum volume/weight (excluding Coutinho et al.).

Among the included studies, Nunes et al. additionally compared different MI–E pressure settings in a crossover design. The high‐pressure protocol (−50/+50 cmH2O) was associated with the greatest volume of cleared secretions compared with low‐pressure (−30/+30 cmH2O) and isolated suctioning (p = 0.003).

2SpO2/SaO2.

SpO2/SaO2.

Three studies [16, 17, 19] reported oxygenation outcomes. Two provided data suitable for meta‐analysis: Coutinho et al. [19] reported SpO2 at 30 min post‐intervention, and Martínez‐Alejos et al. [16] reported SaO2 at 1 h post‐intervention. Data from Nunes et al. [17] were presented exclusively in graphical format without numerical mean ± SD values and could not be reliably extracted for quantitative synthesis.

Pooling these studies using mean difference (MD) with a random effects model showed no significant effect of MI–E on oxygenation (MD = 0.28, 95% CI, −0.53 to 1.09; p = 0.50; I 2 = 9%; Figure 5). However, this pooled estimate should be interpreted cautiously given the difference in assessment timing (30 min vs. 1 h).

3Airway Resistance.

3Airway Resistance.

Airway Resistance.

Four studies [16, 17, 18, 19] reported airway resistance outcomes, but meta‐analysis was not feasible. Ferreira de Camillis et al. [18] reported only the change in airway resistance (ΔRaw) rather than absolute post‐intervention values, precluding pooling. Coutinho et al. [19] and Nunes et al. [17] presented data only graphically without extractable numerical values. Thus, only Martínez‐Alejos et al. [16] provided absolute post‐intervention data, leaving a single study available for this outcome.

Narratively, most studies found no significant or clinically relevant changes in airway resistance with MI–E. Martínez‐Alejos et al. [16] observed non‐significant fluctuations across time points. Ferreira de Camillis et al. [18] reported no significant difference in ΔRaw between groups (0 ± 3.48 vs. 0.22 ± 3.25 cmH2O/L/s; p = 0.59). Coutinho et al. [19] similarly noted no differences.

In contrast, Nunes et al. [17] found that high‐pressure MI–E (−50/+50 cmH2O) significantly and sustainedly reduced airway resistance, whereas isolated suctioning transiently increased it (p < 0.05). 4Lung Compliance.

4Lung Compliance.

Lung Compliance.

Four studies [16, 17, 18, 19] reported pulmonary compliance outcomes, but meta‐analysis was not feasible. Ferreira de Camillis et al. [18] reported only the change in static compliance (ΔC L) rather than absolute post‐intervention values, precluding pooling. Coutinho et al. [19] and Nunes et al. [17] presented compliance data only graphically without extractable numerical values. Thus, only Martínez‐Alejos et al. [16] provided absolute post‐intervention data, leaving a single study available for this outcome.

Narratively, Martínez‐Alejos et al. [16] observed non‐significant fluctuations in compliance across time points with both interventions. Coutinho et al. [19] similarly reported no significant differences, though numerical data were not provided.

In contrast, Ferreira de Camillis et al. [18] found a significantly greater improvement in ΔC L in the MI–E group compared with control (1.76 ± 4.90 vs. −0.57 ± 4.85 mL/cmH2O; p < 0.001). Nunes et al. [17] observed that only the high‐pressure MIE protocol (−50/+50 cmH2O) significantly increased static compliance ‐immediately post‐intervention (p < 0.05), whereas low‐pressure MIE (−30/+30 cmH2O) and isolated suctioning produced no significant change.

Clinical Endpoints

Mechanical Ventilation Duration.

Mechanical Ventilation Duration.

Mechanical Ventilation Duration.

Only one study [20] reported mechanical ventilation duration as an outcome. Consequently, meta‐analysis was not feasible for this endpoint. Kubota et al. [20] defined the primary outcome as ventilator‐free days (VFDs) at day 28 and also assessed total duration of mechanical ventilation up to day 28. No significant differences were observed between the MI–E and control groups in median VFDs (21 days, IQR 13–24 vs. 18 days, IQR 0–23; p = 0.38) or median ventilation duration (6 days, IQR 3–9 vs. 8 days, IQR 4–14; p = 0.19). 2ICU Length of Stay.

2ICU Length of Stay.

ICU Length of Stay.

Only one study reported ICU length of stay [20], precluding meta‐analysis. Kubota et al. [20] found no significant difference in median ICU stay between the MI–E and control groups (10 days, IQR 7–12 vs. 12 days, IQR 9–15; p = 0.31).

Safety Outcomes

The safety outcomes of MI–E in ventilated patients are presented in Table 4. Definitions of adverse events varied across the included studies. Martínez‐Alejos et al. [16] pre‐specified safety stopping criteria as a sustained decrease in SpO2 to < 85% or a > 10% drop from baseline, a > 20% change in heart rate or a > 20% change in systolic or diastolic blood pressure. Ferreira de Camillis et al. [18] defined haemodynamic adverse events as a decrease in SpO2 of ≥ 3% or a systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg. Kubota et al. [20] monitored for haemodynamic instability, arrhythmias, barotrauma, atelectasis, oxygen desaturation and mucus plugging. The remaining two studies [17, 19] did not provide explicit definitions of adverse events.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesized evidence from five randomized controlled trials involving 310 invasively ventilated critically ill adults to evaluate the efficacy and safety of mechanical insufflation–exsufflation. Meta‐analysis demonstrated that MI–E significantly increases sputum clearance compared with control interventions (SMD = 0.63, 95% CI 0.32–0.93; p < 0.0001; I 2 = 38%). This finding aligns with the established physiological mechanism of MI‐E, whereby alternating positive and negative pressures generate high peak expiratory flow, promoting cephalad movement of secretions from the main airways [21]. The effect remained robust in sensitivity analyses excluding a study with markedly higher secretion weights [19], which reduced heterogeneity to I 2 = 32%. However, it should be noted that the data from Nunes et al. [17] included in the meta‐analysis derive from the high pressure (−50/+50 cmH2O) protocol, whereas the other studies employed standard pressures (+40/−40 cmH2O); therefore, the contribution of this study should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

In contrast, MI–E produced no significant effect on oxygenation (MD = 0.28%, 95% CI, −0.53 to 1.09; p = 0.50; I 2 = 9%). Although one study reported a modest increase in SaO2 at 1 h post‐intervention [16], the pooled estimate confirms that any oxygenation benefit is neither consistent nor clinically meaningful in the immediate to short‐term period.

For airway resistance and pulmonary compliance, meta‐analysis was not feasible due to incompatible data formats. Ferreira de Camillis et al. [18] reported only change scores (ΔRaw and ΔC_L) rather than absolute post‐intervention values. Nunes et al. [17] and Coutinho et al. [19] presented these outcomes graphically without extractable numerical values. Nunes et al. [17] observed that high‐pressure MI–E (±50 cmH2O) significantly and sustainedly reduced airway resistance and improved compliance, whereas low‐pressure MI–E (±30 cmH2O) and isolated suctioning produced no significant improvement. In studies employing ±40 cmH2O, no significant changes in airway resistance or compliance were observed [16]. These divergent findings suggest a potential pressure‐dependent effect warranting further investigation.

Regarding long‐term clinical outcomes, only one parallel‐group trial reported ventilator‐free days and ICU length of stay [20]. Kubota et al. [20] found no significant differences between MI–E and control groups in median ventilator‐free days at day 28 (21 vs. 18 days; p = 0.38) or ICU stay (10 vs. 12 days; p = 0.31). The absence of benefit on these endpoints may reflect the multifactorial determinants of ICU outcomes, wherein a single adjunctive intervention targeting secretion clearance is insufficient to alter disease trajectory.

Methodological considerations. This review included both crossover and parallel RCTs. Three crossover trials [16, 17, 19] did not report first‐period data or paired analyses. In the absence of such data, we conservatively treated crossover comparisons as independent groups for meta‐analysis. This approach may underestimate standard errors and represents a limitation of the evidence synthesis. Future crossover trials should report paired data or first‐period results to enable more precise pooling.

Safety outcomes. Definitions of adverse events varied across studies. Martínez‐Alejos et al. [16] applied explicit stopping criteria (SpO2 < 85% or > 10% drop; heart rate or blood pressure change > 20%) and reported transient desaturations or haemodynamic variations in 10 of 23 MI–E treatments, none requiring intervention discontinuation. Ferreira de Camillis et al. [18] and Kubota et al. [20] reported zero adverse events according to their respective definitions. Overall, no serious adverse events attributable to MI–E were reported across the five trials. The transient physiological fluctuations observed were self‐limiting and clinically inconsequential.

We systematically assessed the quality of evidence from the included randomized controlled trials using the GRADE system, with particular attention to heterogeneity (inconsistency) as a key downgrading factor. The results showed that only sputum clearance reached moderate quality evidence; all other outcomes (oxygenation, airway resistance, lung compliance, clinical endpoints and safety) were rated as low or very low quality. This suggests that although MI‐E demonstrated a statistically significant benefit in sputum clearance (SMD = 0.63, I 2 = 38%), the overall certainty of the evidence remains limited. Importantly, heterogeneity (inconsistency) did not substantially downgrade the evidence for sputum clearance (I 2 = 38%, stable after sensitivity analysis), indicating acceptable consistency across studies for this outcome. For most other outcomes, high risk of bias, small sample sizes, inconsistent outcome definitions or narrative synthesis precluded reliable quantitative assessment of heterogeneity or indirectly weakened confidence in the findings. For instance, differences in measurement timing (30 min vs. 1 h) and type (SpO2 vs. SaO2) for oxygenation introduced indirectness, which also contributes to heterogeneity.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, the limited number of included studies (n = 5) and small overall sample size constrain the certainty of conclusions. Second, meta‐analysis was not feasible for several clinically relevant outcomes due to incompatible data formats and insufficient extractable numerical data. Third, publication bias cannot be excluded, although we searched multiple databases without language restrictions. Fourth, the inability to obtain paired data from crossover trials may have introduced unit‐of‐analysis error; we have conservatively treated these studies as parallel groups in the meta‐analysis and discussed the implications.

Implications for Practice

Current evidence supports MI‐E as an effective adjunctive therapy for enhancing sputum clearance in invasively ventilated critically ill adults, particularly when secretion retention is a predominant clinical concern. Implementation should include structured assessment of cough strength (e.g., peak cough flow < 160 L/min as a reference threshold) and careful exclusion of contraindications such as undrained pneumothorax, severe hemodynamic instability, or elevated intracranial pressure. Parameter titration should follow the principle of “start low, go slow,” with initial settings of ±20 cmH2O and gradual escalation guided by individual response. Continuous monitoring of vital signs during treatment remains essential for safe implementation. Given the current limitations, future research should prioritise large‐scale, multicentre RCTs employing standardised protocols. These studies should evaluate not only immediate physiological outcomes but also patient‐centred endpoints such as ventilator‐free days and long‐term quality of life. Additionally, research investigating optimal MI‐E parameters—including pressure settings, cycle frequency, and daily treatment sessions—would provide valuable guidance for evidence‐based practice development. Core outcome sets and detailed intervention reporting are urgently needed to reduce heterogeneity.

Conclusion

Based on a systematic review of available randomized controlled trials and GRADE evidence grading, this study concludes that mechanical insufflation–exsufflation (MI‐E) significantly increases sputum clearance (moderate quality evidence) compared with conventional airway clearance techniques in ICU patients. However, the effects on oxygenation, airway resistance, lung compliance, mechanical ventilation duration, ICU length of stay and safety remain uncertain, with low to very low quality evidence. Heterogeneity (inconsistency) did not substantially affect the evidence quality for the primary outcome (sputum clearance), but it may influence other outcomes due to variations in study design and limited sample sizes. Current evidence does not support routine widespread use of MI‐E in ICU settings, and more high‐quality, low‐heterogeneity studies are urgently needed.
