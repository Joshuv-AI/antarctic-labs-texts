Impact of Concomitant Cervical Anterolisthesis on Surgical Outcomes in Cervical Spondylotic Myelopathy: A Multicenter Prospective Study
Authors: Reo Shibata, Narihito Nagoshi, Junichi Yamane, Toshiki Okubo, Hitoshi Kono, Yoshiomi Kobayashi, Takahiro Kitagawa, Kanehiro Fujiyoshi, Yoshiyuki Yato, Tatsuya Yamamoto, Takeshi Ikegami, Takahito Iga, Kazuki Takeda, Satoshi Suzuki, Masahiro Ozaki, Morio Matsumoto, Masaya Nakamura, Kota Watanabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy DesignMulticenter prospective cohort study.ObjectivesThis study aimed to determine whether concomitant cervical anterolisthesis influences surgical outcomes in patients with cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM).MethodsA total of 691 CSM patients were enrolled and classified into those with anterolisthesis ≥ 2 mm (Slip group, n=95) and those without measurable slip (Non-slip group, n=596). Patients underwent surgical treatment as indicated and were followed for 2 years. Cervical alignment, C2–7 ROM, JOA scores, JOACMEQ domains, and SF-36 scores were assessed. Clinical outcomes were compared using multivariate analyses adjusted for baseline factors. A subgroup analysis evaluated high-grade slip (≥4 mm).ResultsThe Slip group was older and had more kyphosis and worse baseline lordosis and JOA score. Fusion was performed more frequently in the Slip group, which also showed a greater reduction in cervical ROM at 2 years. Adjusted postoperative JOA score, JOACMEQ, and SF-36 outcomes were comparable between groups postoperatively. Within the Slip group, high-grade slip (≥4 mm) was associated with less JOA improvement in an exploratory subgroup analysis, whereas JOACMEQ and SF-36 scores were not influenced by slip severity.ConclusionsNeutral-radiograph-defined cervical anterolisthesis ≥2 mm was not independently associated with worse adjusted 2-year neurological, functional, or quality-of-life outcomes. High-grade slip (≥4 mm), however, may limit neurological recovery, underscoring the importance of evaluating segmental stability when substantial slippage is present.Keywords: cervical spondylolisthesis, multicenter prospective study, cervical spondylotic myelopathy, neurological recovery, JOA score, JOACMEQ, SF-36

Introduction

Cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM) is a leading cause of spinal cord dysfunction in the aging population and the most common nontraumatic myelopathy worldwide. 1 It arises from degenerative alterations of the cervical spine that result in spinal cord compression. Among these conditions, cervical anterolisthesis has received relatively little attention in the past, in contrast to the extensively studied lumbar spondylolisthesis. 2 Emerging evidence suggests that degenerative cervical spondylolisthesis is more prevalent than previously recognized and frequently coexists with CSM.2,3 A recent systematic review reported that approximately 16–17% of patients with CSM exhibit radiographic cervical spondylolisthesis. 4 From a biomechanical prospective, spondylolisthesis contributes to dynamic spinal cord compression, particularly during cervical flexion or extension. 5 Such instability further compromises the spinal canal during motion, raising concerns that concomitant spondylolisthesis adversely affects surgical outcomes in patients with CSM.

Despite these concerns, clinical studies examining surgical outcomes in the setting of cervical spondylolisthesis remain limited, and the available retrospective evidence is conflicting. For instance, Oichi et al identified cervical anterolisthesis as a significant risk factor for poor neurological improvement following cervical laminoplasty, 6 suggesting that unaddressed spinal instability in the absence of stabilization may compromise postoperative improvement. In contrast, Ninomiya et al reported that patients with anterior cervical spondylolisthesis who underwent posterior decompression achieved postoperative JOA improvements comparable to those without spondylolisthesis, provided that global cervical alignment was preserved. 7 Collectively, these findings highlight the uncertainty regarding whether cervical spondylolisthesis itself adversely influences long-term surgical outcomes, or whether previously reported differences reflect confounding factors such as advanced age, baseline disease severity, or surgical selection bias.

In light of the limited and heterogenous evidence, the present study aims to clarify the impact of cervical spondylolisthesis on postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing surgery for CSM. Using a multicenter prospective cohort, we systematically compared clinical and radiographic outcomes between patients with and without cervical spondylolisthesis. We hypothesize that the presence of cervical spondylolisthesis is associated with poorer postoperative recovery, underscoring its potential relevance in preoperative risk stratification.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

This multi-institutional investigation of cervical spine surgeries was conducted by 10 high-volume centers in Japan. A total of 1,482 patients with cervical spinal disorders were prospectively enrolled between February 2019 and December 2022. 8 Among these, 870 patients diagnosed with CSM were included in the present study.CSM was defined as cervical spinal cord dysfunction attributable to degenerative cervical spondylosis. The diagnosis was made by the treating spine surgeon based on a combination of myelopathic symptoms and/or neurological signs and radiographic evidence of cervical spinal cord compression on preoperative imaging. After exclusion of 38 patients with prior cervical spine surgery and 141 without available 2-year follow-up data, 691 patients were included in the final analysis.

Cervical spondylolisthesis was defined as anterior slippage of ≥2 mm on neutral lateral radiographs, in accordance with previous reports.2,9-11 Flexion-extension instability was not incorporated into group classification because dynamic translation data were not collected in a standardized manner across participating centers. Based on this definition, patients were classified into a spondylolisthesis group (Slip group, n=95) and a non-spondylolisthesis group (Non-slip group, n=596).

To further explore the potential confounding effect of procedure selection, an additional subgroup analysis was performed in patients treated with decompression without fusion. In this decompression-only subgroup, postoperative outcomes were compared between the Slip and Non-slip groups using the same analytic framework as in the primary analysis.

For the subgroup analysis of Slip group, a threshold of 4 mm was secondarily applied to distinguish high-grade from low-grade spondylolisthesis. Within the Slip group, exploratory threshold analyses using 3-, 4-, 5-, and 6-mm cutoffs were performed. Because 4 mm showed the clearest separation in JOA recovery while preserving analyzable subgroup size, slip width ≥4 mm was used as an exploratory operational threshold for larger-magnitude slip. A patient flow diagram summarizing study enrollment, exclusions, follow-up, and subgroup classification is shown in Figure 1. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board at each participating institution (approval number: 20180045), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to surgery.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data collected included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, comorbidities, duration of symptoms, operative time, estimated blood loss (EBL) and length of hospital stay. Patients were evaluated preoperatively and at 6, 12, and 24 months postoperatively using the cervical Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score, 12 the Japanese Orthopaedic Association Cervical Myelopathy Evaluation Questionnaire (JOACMEQ) 13 , and the Short Form-36 (SF-36) 14 . The 24-month postoperative outcomes were used as the primary endpoint, and the 6- and 12-month outcomes were analyzed to assess interval recovery. The differences between postoperative and preoperative values for the JOA score and SF-36 were calculated as Δ values at 24 months. The minimum clinically important difference (MCID) was defined as 2.5 points for the JOA score 15 . For the JOACMEQ, the effective rate was assessed according to the previous study 16 as follows; (1) a postoperative score ≥20 points above the preoperative score or (2) a preoperative score <90 and a postoperative score ≥90. Patients with both pretreatment and posttreatment scores ≥90 were counted but excluded from further analysis. A group’s effective rate was calculated as [(number of patients judged “effective”)/((total number of patients in the group) − (number of patients with pretreatment and posttreatment scores ≥90))].

Radiological assessments were conducted using plain lateral radiographs. The C2–C7 Cobb angle was measured in the neutral position of the cervical spine as the angle formed by lines drawn along the inferior endplates of C2 and C7. Cervical range of motion (ROM) was defined as the difference in the C2–C7 angle between extension and flexion positions. Cervical spondylolisthesis was assessed on neutral lateral radiographs, and slip width, representing the degree of anterior slippage, was measured as the horizontal distance between a vertical line drawn from the posteroinferior corner of the cranial vertebral body and a vertical line drawn from the posterosuperior corner of the caudal vertebral body. Cervical spondylolisthesis was defined as an anterior slip width of ≥2 mm. Cervical kyphosis was defined as a C2–C7 angle of <−5° in the neutral position, according to a previous study. The measurement methods for the C2–C7 Cobb angle and slip width are illustrated in Supplementary Figure S1.

Surgical Treatment

All patients underwent surgical treatment for CSM, with the specific procedure chosen by the treating spine surgeon based on each patient’s pathology and alignment. The surgical approaches included posterior decompression (either selective laminectomy or laminoplasty) with or without instrumented fusion, as well as anterior cervical decompression and fusion (ACDF) for appropriate cases.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were compared between Slip vs. Non-slip groups using Student’s t-tests for normally distributed data and Wilcoxon rank-sum test for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics were compared between groups to identify potential confounders. Covariates in subsequent analyses were selected based on clinical relevance and prior literature, rather than solely on statistical significance at baseline. To evaluate the independent association between cervical spondylolisthesis and postoperative outcomes, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed. Postoperative clinical scores at 6, 12, and 24 months were analyzed, with group (Slip vs. Non-slip) as the independent factor, adjusting for baseline age, sex, BMI, symptom duration, preoperative C2–C7 angle, kyphosis, surgical procedure, and the corresponding preoperative value of each outcome measure. The 24-month outcomes were considered the primary postoperative outcomes. To account for the multicenter design, sensitivity analyses were performed for the main postoperative outcomes using two alternative model specifications: models including center as a fixed effect and linear mixed-effects models with a random intercept for center. These analyses were performed for the postoperative JOA score, JOACMEQ domains, and SF-36 PCS and MCS, using the same covariates as the primary models. Adjusted differences were calculated as the estimated difference between the Slip and Non-slip groups. Group effects are reported using F-statistics and corresponding p values. All analyses were performed using SPSS v27.

Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of both groups. By design, there were no prior cervical surgeries in either group. The Slip group was significantly older on average (72.8 ± 9.0 vs. 67.2 ± 12.0 years, p<0.001) and had a higher proportion of females (47.3 vs. 29.4%, p<0.001). They were also lower BMI on average (23.1 ± 3.5 vs. 24.9 ± 4.0, p<0.001). Symptom duration before surgery was similar between groups. There was no significant difference in the prevalence of comorbidities between the two groups, except for cardiac disease (14.7 vs. 6.7%, p=0.007).

Radiographic Findings

Radiographically, preoperative C2–C7 Cobb angle in the neutral position was significantly smaller in the Slip group (7.1 ± 14.4 vs. 10.1 ± 11.8 °, p=0.020), whereas cervical ROM was comparable between the two groups (35.9 ± 15.1 vs. 35.4 ± 13.7 °, p=0.445) (Table 1). A significantly higher proportion of patients in the Slip group exhibited preoperative cervical kyphosis (15.8 vs. 8.5%, p=0.026). Within the Slip group, the mean slip width was 3.9 ± 2.1 mm, and C4 was the most commonly affected level. Postoperatively, there were no significant differences between both groups in C2–C7 Cobb angle in the neutral position or prevalence of cervical kyphosis. Postoperative cervical ROM was significantly smaller in Slip group (28.0 ± 12.3 vs. 29.3 ± 12.5 °, p=0.025).

Preoperative Clinical Outcomes

Table 2 presents clinical outcomes adjusted for confounding factors using ANCOVA. The preoperative JOA score was significantly lower in the Slip group (10.6, 95% CI: 10.0 to 11.3) than the Non-slip group (11.3, 95% CI: 10.9 to 11.7, p=0.019). In the preoperative JOACMEQ, upper extremity function (63.4, 95% CI: 58.1 to 68.7) and lower extremity function (48.1, 95% CI: 41.3 to 55.0) were significantly lower in the Slip group than the Non-slip group (upper extremity function; 73.3, 95% CI: 69.9 to 76.8, p<0.001, lower extremity function; 55.8, 95% CI: 51.3 to 60.2, p=0.018), and the other scores were comparable. No significant differences were observed in SF-36 scores between the two groups.

Surgical Findings

As shown in Table 2, anterior or posterior fusion was selected significantly more frequently in the Slip group (p<0.001). The average operative time was significantly longer in the Slip group (118.7 ± 60.4 vs. 104.0 ± 50.9 minutes, p<0.001), whereas the mean intraoperative estimated blood loss and the length of postoperative hospital stay did not differ significantly. There was no significant difference in all complication rates between the two groups.

Postoperative Clinical Outcomes

Table 3 and Figure 2 present postoperative clinical scores adjusted using ANCOVA for potential confounders. Table 3 presents adjusted postoperative clinical outcomes at 6, 12, and 24 months after surgery. The adjusted JOA score was significantly lower in the Slip group than in the Non-slip group at 6 months after surgery (13.3, 95% CI: 12.8 to13.8 vs. 13.8, 95% CI: 13.5 to 14.1, p = 0.024), but this difference was no longer significant at 1 year (13.9, 95% CI: 13.4 to 14.4 vs. 14.0, 95% CI: 13.7 to 14.4, p = 0.471) or 2 years (13.7, 95% CI: 13.2 to 14.2 vs. 14.0, 95% CI: 13.6–14.3, p = 0.209). No significant differences were observed between the groups in the JOACMEQ domains or SF-36 PCS and MCS at 6, 12, or 24 months. At 2 years, ΔJOA, ΔPCS, ΔMCS, JOA MCID achievement, and JOACMEQ effective rates were also comparable between the groups.

Sensitivity analyses accounting for the multicenter design are shown in Supplementary Table S1. Cervical spondylolisthesis was not associated with worse 2-year postoperative outcomes in either the center fixed-effects models or the mixed-effects models with a random intercept for center. The adjusted JOACMEQ cervical spine function and quality-of-life scores were significantly higher in the Slip group, whereas postoperative JOA score, the other JOACMEQ domains, and SF-36 PCS and MCS did not differ significantly between the groups. Overall, these findings support the robustness of the primary interpretation after accounting for center-level effects.

In the decompression-only subgroup, there were 61 patients in the Slip group and 552 in the Non-slip group. Adjusted 2-year postoperative JOA scores were not significantly different between the groups (14.0, 95% CI: 13.6 to 14.3 vs. 13.6, 95% CI: 13.0 to 14.2, p = 0.188). Likewise, no significant differences were observed between the groups in the JOACMEQ domains or SF-36 PCS and MCS (Supplementary Table S2).

To examine whether postoperative outcomes varied according to slip magnitude within the Slip group, exploratory threshold analyses were performed using candidate cutoffs of 3, 4, 5, and 6 mm (Supplementary Table S3). Among these thresholds, the 4-mm cutoff showed the clearest separation in JOA recovery while preserving an analyzable subgroup size; therefore, slip ≥4 mm was used as an exploratory operational threshold for larger-magnitude slip. Patients were then stratified into low-grade slip (2 to <4 mm) and high-grade slip (≥4 mm) groups (Table 4). There were no significant differences between the two groups in postoperative scores or changes (Δ) in the JOACMEQ and SF-36. The JOACMEQ effective rates were also comparable between the groups. In contrast, postoperative JOA score was lower in the ≥4-mm group than in the 2 to <4-mm group (11.6, 95% CI: 10.4 to 12.8 vs. 12.9, 95% CI: 11.8 to 14.0; p = 0.005), and ΔJOA was smaller in the ≥4-mm group (0.99, 95% CI: −0.21 to 2.19 vs. 2.31, 95% CI: 1.21 to 3.42; p = 0.005). The JOA MCID achievement rate was also lower in the ≥4-mm group than in the 2 to <4-mm group (24.3% vs. 62.6%; p = 0.017).

Multivariable Linear Regression Analyses for Each Outcome

To identify factors associated with clinical outcomes, multivariable linear regression analyses were performed for each outcome. The results of the analysis for clinical outcomes are presented in Table 5. Across all models, postoperative clinical scores were significantly associated with their respective preoperative values. In multivariable regression analyses, greater slip width was associated with a lower postoperative JOA score (standardized β = −0.194, P = 0.049) and a smaller ΔJOA score (standardized β = −0.214, P = 0.049), whereas no significant associations were observed for JOACMEQ or SF-36 outcomes. In the JOACMEQ domains, upper extremity function, lower limb function, and QOL were significantly correlated with age. Additionally, the MCS domain of SF-36 was significantly associated with symptom duration.

Discussion

In this study, concomitant cervical spondylolisthesis did not adversely affect 2-year surgical outcomes in patients with CSM. After adjustment for baseline characteristics, cervical anterolisthesis was not associated with worse neurological or patient-reported outcomes at 2 years. The primary analyses showed no significant between-group differences in the JOA score, JOACMEQ domains, or SF-36 scores, and the center-adjusted sensitivity analyses likewise did not indicate inferior outcomes in the Slip group. However, greater slip severity was associated with less neurological improvement, as patients with larger slip magnitudes showed significantly smaller gains in JOA scores on multivariable regression analysis. In contrast, slip width was not significantly associated with JOACMEQ or SF-36 outcomes. Taken together, these findings suggest that cervical spondylolisthesis per se should not preclude surgical intervention for CSM, although careful consideration of slip severity is warranted when predicting the extent of neurological recovery.

A key finding of the present study is that, after adjusting for baseline characteristics, no significant differences were observed between the Slip and Non-slip groups in 2-year surgical outcomes. This indicates that the degree of surgical benefit is fundamentally comparable regardless of the presence of concomitant cervical spondylolisthesis. The results confirm and extend prior evidence, which has largely been based on JOA- or mJOA-centered evaluations. For example, Gondar et al performed a meta-analysis of three studies and noted that while patients with cervical spondylolisthesis had worse preoperative neurological status, their postoperative mJOA recovery ratio was only marginally—and not significantly in a random-effects model—lower than that of patients without spondylolisthesis. 4 Similarly, Suzuki et al demonstrated comparable JOA score recovery rates between slip and non-slip groups, 17 and Shigematsu et al found that small degenerative spondylolisthesis did not adversely affect laminoplasty outcomes in elderly patients at 3-year follow-up. 18 Importantly, the present study advances these JOA-based observations by incorporating validated patient-reported outcome measures. The absence of worse JOACMEQ and SF-36 outcomes in the Slip group indicates that, from the patient’s perspective, the presence of cervical anterolisthesis did not compromise functional recovery or health-related quality of life after surgery. By extending outcome assessment beyond neurological scores alone, our study provides a more comprehensive and robust evaluation of surgical efficacy, thereby strengthening the clinical relevance and reliability of these findings.

One plausible explanation for the discrepancy between our findings and earlier reports suggesting inferior outcomes in patients with cervical spondylolisthesis relates to differences in surgical strategy. Oichi et al exclusively evaluated patients treated with laminoplasty without fusion and identified anterolisthesis greater than 3 mm as a significant risk factor for poor neurological recovery, thereby concluding that laminoplasty alone may be suboptimal in such cases. 66 Similarly, Funaba et al reported that, among patients with cervical spondylolisthesis managed by laminoplasty without fusion, postoperative JOA recovery rates were significantly lower despite comparable preoperative neurological status. 2 19 In contrast, in the present cohort, patients with cervical spondylolisthesis were more likely to undergo instrumented fusion in conjunction with decompression, a strategy that may mitigate the adverse effects of segmental instability on spinal cord decompression. Therefore, the comparable outcomes observed in the primary analysis may reflect, at least in part, real-world surgical selection rather than the complete neutrality of cervical spondylolisthesis itself. To further address this issue, we performed an additional decompression-only subgroup analysis after excluding all fusion cases. In this subgroup, no significant differences were observed between the Slip and Non-slip groups in postoperative JOA, JOACMEQ, or SF-36 outcomes. This finding suggests that preferential fusion selection alone does not explain the primary findings. However, this subgroup analysis does not eliminate residual selection bias, because patients who underwent decompression alone were still selected nonrandomly and may have represented a clinically less unstable subset. In addition, the smaller number of Slip cases in this subgroup limited statistical power; therefore, these data should be interpreted as supportive sensitivity findings rather than definitive evidence of equivalence. When laminoplasty or decompression alone is performed without stabilization, anterior vertebral translation may persist postoperatively, potentially resulting in ongoing dynamic cord indentation and suboptimal neurological recovery in selected patients. 77 This hypothesis is supported by both our radiographic findings and prior biomechanical investigations demonstrating that the combination of cervical spondylolisthesis and reduced lordosis is associated with increased dynamic spinal cord compression during flexion-extension movements. 55 Notably, Sakamoto et al showed that a smaller cervical lordotic angle in the presence of anterolisthesis was associated with greater dynamic cord compression during neck flexion, which correlated with more severe preoperative lower extremity dysfunction. 55 These observations suggest that stabilization of the involved motion segment may attenuate dynamic cord tethering and thereby facilitate neurological recovery in patients with cervical spondylolisthesis who exhibit radiographic features of instability. Conversely, in patients who lack evidence of instability or kyphosis, posterior decompression without fusion—such as laminoplasty or laminectomy alone—can still yield favorable outcomes. 1 20

Although cervical spondylolisthesis has been increasingly recognized, data specifically addressing high-grade disease remain limited. Previous studies have reported that spondylolisthesis of ≥4 mm occurs in only 0.6–2.2% of surgically treated patients with CSM.3,7 In contrast, high-grade spondylolisthesis (≥4 mm) was identified in 5.9% of cases in the present cohort, representing a somewhat higher prevalence than previously described. Subgroup analysis suggested that patients with high-grade spondylolisthesis experienced significantly less neurological recovery. Furthermore, multivariable regression analysis revealed a significant association between increasing slip magnitude and lower postoperative JOA scores, indicating that a greater degree of slippage was independently associated with poor neurological outcomes. Therefore, because high-grade spondylolisthesis may be associated with limited neurological improvement even after appropriate treatment, these exploratory findings warrant further investigation to determine whether larger slip magnitude has prognostic value and whether treatment timing influences neurological recovery.

An additional interpretive consideration is that our group classification was based on anterior translation on neutral lateral radiographs rather than on standardized assessment of dynamic instability. Therefore, the present findings should not be interpreted as indicating that motion-dependent instability is clinically unimportant. Rather, they indicate that neutral-radiograph-defined anterolisthesis of ≥2 mm was not independently associated with worse adjusted 2-year outcomes in this multicenter cohort. This classification scheme may have included biologically distinct phenotypes, such as patients with visible but relatively fixed slip and patients with clinically relevant dynamic instability that was not apparent on neutral radiographs. Such heterogeneity could have attenuated true between-group differences. At the same time, the observed association between greater slip magnitude and poorer JOA recovery suggests that the severity of static translation may still carry prognostic relevance, even though dynamic instability itself was not directly measured.

This study has several limitations. First, its observational and nonrandomized design meant that surgical strategies were determined at the discretion of the treating surgeons, which may have introduced selection bias, particularly given that fusion procedures were more likely to be selected for patients with more severe or unstable pathology. Although the decompression-only subgroup analysis showed no significant between-group differences, this analysis should also be interpreted cautiously because decompression-only treatment was not randomly assigned and the Slip subgroup was smaller, limiting statistical power. Second, defining cervical spondylolisthesis as ≥2 mm on neutral lateral radiographs may have caused misclassification because dynamic instability was not systematically assessed. Patients with occult motion-dependent instability may have been classified into the Non-slip group, whereas the Slip group may have included patients with relatively fixed translation and different biomechanical characteristics. This limitation is important because the proposed adverse neurological effects are related to dynamic cord compression during motion. Therefore, our findings apply to neutral-radiograph-defined spondylolisthesis rather than dynamic instability per se. The smaller size and heterogeneity of the Slip group may also have reduced statistical power and attenuated between-group differences. Future studies using standardized dynamic imaging are needed to clarify the prognostic significance of cervical instability.

Third, the ≥4-mm subgroup analysis was exploratory and should be interpreted cautiously because the threshold was data-informed and the subgroup size was limited. Thus, the observed association between slip ≥4 mm and reduced JOA recovery should be considered hypothesis-generating and requires validation in future studies.

And finally, the 2-year follow-up period may be insufficient to capture longer-term outcomes, such as adjacent segment disease or late sagittal alignment changes. Despite these limitations, this study represents the largest prospectively enrolled cohort to date evaluating surgical outcomes in patients with cervical spondylotic myelopathy complicated by cervical spondylolisthesis. By incorporating validated patient-reported outcome measures alongside physician-reported JOA scores, we provided a comprehensive assessment of treatment effectiveness. These findings enhance current understanding of the clinical impact of cervical spondylolisthesis and may inform surgical decision-making and patient counseling regarding postoperative recovery and prognosis.

In conclusion, this multicenter prospective cohort study compared surgical outcomes between patients with cervical anterolisthesis ≥2 mm and those without measurable slip. After adjustment for baseline factors, 2-year postoperative neurological, functional, and quality-of-life outcomes were comparable between groups, although patients with anterolisthesis underwent fusion more frequently and exhibited greater loss of cervical range of motion. The decompression-only subgroup analysis also showed no significant differences between the Slip and Non-slip groups, suggesting that preferential fusion selection alone is unlikely to fully explain the primary findings, although residual treatment-selection bias cannot be excluded. However, within the anterolisthesis group, high-grade slip (≥4 mm) was associated with poorer neurological recovery, suggesting that substantial slippage may limit postoperative improvement despite overall favorable outcomes. Given these findings, treatment decisions should recognize that cervical anterolisthesis alone was not independently associated with worse postoperative outcomes in this cohort, whereas larger slip magnitude was associated with less favorable neurological recovery. However, because dynamic instability was not directly assessed, the prognostic impact of motion-dependent spondylolisthesis remains to be clarified.
