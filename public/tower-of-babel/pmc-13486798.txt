Balloon-assisted endovascular embolization of a subcutaneous penile arteriovenous fistula using a quadruple coaxial system: A case report
Authors: Yuki Sudo, Noritaka Kamei, Norio Hongo, Ryuichi Shimada, Yoshiki Asayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn arteriovenous fistula (AVF) is an abnormal vascular communication that allows blood to shunt directly from an artery to a vein. AVFs may be congenital or acquired, including traumatic or iatrogenic causes. Acquired AVFs typically involve the peripheral vascular system, whereas AVFs arising from the subcutaneous tissue of the penis have not been previously reported. We describe a rare subcutaneous penile AVF that was treated with endovascular therapy in a patient with a history of childhood surgery. The patient was a 49-year-old man with congenital hemophilia A and a history of circumcision during childhood. Gradual swelling in the left inguinal region had been noted for several years. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed abnormal vessels extending from the left inguinal region to the left penile subcutaneous tissue, raising suspicion of an AVF. Subsequent angiography confirmed a focal direct AVF supplied by branches of the left superficial femoral artery, with early venous drainage into the left femoral vein and no diffuse nidus-like vascular network. Endovascular treatment was performed using a quadruple coaxial system incorporating a small-caliber balloon catheter. Under balloon-assisted flow control, embolization was conducted from the distal draining vein to the proximal feeding artery using a mixture of N-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate, lipiodol, and ethanol. This case suggests that endovascular treatment may be a useful option for selected superficial penile AVFs.Keywords: Arteriovenous fistula, Penis, Endovascular embolization, Balloon-assisted embolization

Introduction

An arteriovenous fistula (AVF) is an abnormal vascular communication that allows blood to shunt directly from an artery to a vein [1]. AVFs may be congenital or acquired, including traumatic or iatrogenic causes. Acquired AVFs most commonly occur in the femoral vessels [2], and AVFs of the head and neck have also been reported [3].

Involvement of the male genital region is rare. Previous reports have described penile AVFs involving the corpus spongiosum or corpus cavernosum treated with coil or microsphere embolization [4,5]. However, to the best of our knowledge, AVFs arising from the subcutaneous tissue of the penis have not yet been reported.

Herein, we report a rare case of a subcutaneous penile AVF successfully treated with balloon-assisted embolization using an N-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate–lipiodol–ethanol (NLE) mixture.

Case presentation

The patient was a 49-year-old man with a medical history of circumcision at the age of 7 years. He had congenital hemophilia A and experienced difficulties with hemostasis during the procedure. Over several years, gradual swelling developed in the left inguinal region, prompting an initial evaluation at a local clinic. An inguinal hernia was suspected, and the patient was subsequently referred to the Department of Gastrointestinal Surgery at our institution. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) performed for further evaluation suggested the presence of an AVF, leading to referral to our department for endovascular treatment. The patient reported no associated symptoms, including erectile dysfunction, dysuria, or hematuria.

Physical examination revealed swelling in the left inguinal region, with suspected dilated vascular structures on the left side of the penis. Laboratory examination demonstrated a decreased factor VIII level of 20.2 IU/dL, with a negative result for factor VIII inhibitors, confirming a diagnosis of mild congenital hemophilia A.

Preoperative contrast-enhanced CT (Fig. 1) revealed well-developed abnormal vessels extending from the left inguinal region to the left penile subcutaneous tissue. Early opacification of the left femoral vein was observed, suggesting a high-flow arteriovenous shunt. The lesion was confined to the penile subcutaneous tissue, without evidence of cavernosal, urethral, or deeper pelvic involvement. No obvious internal iliac arterial supply was suggested on CT.

The lesion is confined to the penile subcutaneous tissue, without obvious involvement of the corpus cavernosum, urethra, or deeper pelvic structures. Volume-rendered image (E) demonstrates the feeding artery (red vessel, arrows), focal shunt point in the penile subcutaneous tissue (dashed circle), and dilated draining vein (blue vessel, arrowheads), consistent with a focal direct AVF.

Surgical ligation was initially considered. However, due to the patient’s hemophilia A, the associated bleeding risk, and concerns regarding AVF recurrence, a less invasive endovascular approach was selected to minimize the procedural and postoperative risks. Accordingly, coagulation factor replacement therapy was initiated. A total of 4000 units was administered—2000 units on the day before the procedure and 2000 units on the morning of the procedure.

Selective angiography of branches of the left superficial femoral artery (Fig. 2A) confirmed a high-flow arteriovenous shunt with a focal shunt zone in the superficial penile tissue and direct early drainage into the left femoral vein. The feeding artery was dilated and tortuous, whereas no diffuse nidus-like vascular network was identified. These angiographic findings supported the diagnosis of a focal direct AVF rather than an AVM. Selective angiography of the left internal iliac artery showed no contribution to the lesion.

Angiography and endovascular embolization. Digital subtraction angiography (A) of a branch of the left superficial femoral artery demonstrates a focal direct AVF in the penile subcutaneous tissue, with early venous drainage into the left femoral vein through the shunt point (dashed circle). Schematic illustration overlaid on the angiographic image (B) demonstrates the configuration of the quadruple coaxial system. The system consists of a 5-F guiding sheath, a 5.3-F guiding catheter, a 3.8-F balloon catheter, and a 1.6-F microcatheter, providing stable distal access to the shunt point. Postinjection fluoroscopic image (C) demonstrates a continuous NLE cast extending across the shunt point (dashed circle) from the distal draining vein to the proximal feeding artery (arrows). Postembolization DSA (D) shows minimal residual flow in the feeding artery (arrow); however, the shunt point and draining vein are no longer visualized.

Based on these angiographic findings, transarterial embolization targeting the focal shunt zone was planned. A transarterial approach via the left superficial femoral artery was employed to achieve embolization adjacent to the arteriovenous shunt. A 5-F guiding sheath (Ansel, Cook Medical, Bloomington, IN) was inserted via the right femoral artery and positioned in the left superficial femoral artery. Subsequently, a 5.3-F guiding catheter (Slim Guide, Medikit, Tokyo, Japan) was advanced coaxially. A quadruple coaxial system was then constructed, consisting of a 5.3-F guiding catheter, a 3.8-F balloon catheter (Pinnacle Blue, Tokai Medical Products, Aichi, Japan), and a 1.6-F microcatheter (Carnelian Marvel, Tokai Medical Products, Aichi, Japan). The balloon catheter was incrementally advanced over the microcatheter, followed by stepwise telescoping advancement toward the target vessel. The microcatheter was positioned adjacent to the shunt (Fig. 2B). Under balloon-assisted flow control, 0.21 mL of NLE (NBCA, Lipiodol, and ethanol were mixed at a ratio of 3:6:1) was injected, achieving embolization from the distal draining vein to the proximal feeding artery across the shunt (Fig. 2C). No migration into the femoral vein was observed. Post-embolization angiography of the left superficial femoral artery demonstrated minimal residual flow in the feeding artery; however, no residual filling of the shunt point or visualization of the draining vein was observed. These findings indicated angiographic obliteration of the AVF rather than simple flow stasis. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 4 without complications. Contrast-enhanced CT at 4 months after embolization (Fig. 3) showed no evidence of recurrence and further regression of the feeding artery. On clinical examination, no skin ulceration, necrosis, discoloration, erectile dysfunction, dysuria, or hematuria was observed.

Follow-up contrast-enhanced computed tomography (arterial phase) at 4 months after embolization. Axial images (A-C) and coronal maximum intensity projection (MIP) (D) show mild residual enhancement of a portion of the feeding artery (arrow); however, the feeding artery has markedly decreased in caliber, and the draining vein has also decreased in size and become thrombosed (arrowhead). A retained NLE cast is seen near the shunt point (dashed circle), without recurrent opacification of the shunt point or early opacification of the left femoral vein.

Discussion

We report a rare subcutaneous penile AVF treated with balloon-assisted endovascular embolization using a quadruple coaxial system and NLE. This case highlights the importance of imaging-based differentiation between AVF and AVM, selective embolization of the focal shunt, and preservation of superficial penile tissue perfusion, particularly in the setting of coexisting hemophilia.

AVFs arising in the male genital region are uncommon. Although several patients who developed scrotal AVFs have been reported [5], only a limited number of patients with penile AVFs—such as penile urethral AVFs and cavernosal arteriovenous fistulas—have been described. To the best of our knowledge, AVFs originating in the subcutaneous tissue of the penis have not been previously reported. In this patient, the history of childhood circumcision raised the possibility that postsurgical vascular changes may have contributed to lesion development.

The imaging-based differential diagnosis included AVM, venous malformation, and post-traumatic or postsurgical vascular ectasia. AVM was initially considered because CT showed abnormal vessels and early venous opacification; however, the absence of a diffuse nidus-like vascular network and the presence of a focal direct arteriovenous communication favored direct AVF. Venous malformation was considered unlikely because the lesion showed high-flow features, including arterial dilatation and early venous drainage. Post-traumatic or postsurgical vascular ectasia was also considered, given the history of childhood circumcision; however, the discrete shunt point and arterial-phase early venous opacification supported the diagnosis of AVF.

The exact timing of AVF development was unclear in this case. A previous retrospective study reported that the mean interval between trauma and AVF diagnosis was approximately 400 days [6]. However, delayed presentations have also been described, including cases diagnosed 23 years [7] and 52 years [8] after trauma. These reports suggest that acquired AVFs can remain clinically silent for long periods. In the present case, the absence of severe symptoms or cardiac complications may explain why the lesion remained undetected for several decades.

As no established treatment strategy exists for penile AVFs, management must be individualized. In previously reported penile AVFs involving the corpus cavernosum or corpus spongiosum, coil or microsphere embolization has been used. In the present case, preoperative CT showed that the abnormal vessels were confined to the subcutaneous penile tissue, without arterial supply to the corpus cavernosum, urethra, or deeper pelvic structures. Therefore, embolization of the focal subcutaneous shunt was considered feasible. However, because the lesion was located immediately beneath the penile skin, coil embolization was considered less suitable due to concerns regarding recurrence, coil migration, and local skin complications such as ulceration or necrosis. NLE was selected to achieve controlled embolization across the relatively long shunt segment while reducing the risk of nontarget embolization.

In the treatment of high-flow arteriovenous shunts, proximal embolization is associated with a high risk of recurrence and may complicate subsequent re-intervention; therefore, advancing the catheter as close as possible to the shunt point is essential. Despite the long and highly tortuous feeding artery, the quadruple coaxial system provided sufficient backup support. The incorporation of a small-caliber balloon catheter enabled stable positioning of the microcatheter adjacent to the shunt. Intermittent balloon inflation further enhanced support by anchoring the balloon catheter against the vessel wall and facilitating flow-assisted catheter advancement. Collectively, this approach allowed reliable distal access while maintaining catheter stability.

NLE, a low-adhesion embolic agent composed of NBCA, Lipiodol, and ethanol, has been reported to exert milder effects on the vascular wall and surrounding tissues compared with NBCA alone. This is likely attributable to its lower polymerization temperature compared with NBCA, which generates heat of approximately 80°C-90°C during polymerization [9]. These properties render NLE suitable for superficially located AVFs. Because NBCA-based embolic agents carry a risk of nontarget embolization, additional flow control is important. Manual compression of the venous outflow was considered; however, adequate compression was not feasible because of the lesion location. Therefore, balloon-assisted flow control was used to reduce shunt flow and allow controlled NLE injection. The embolization endpoint was designed to occlude the focal shunt while avoiding unnecessary occlusion of adjacent superficial arterial branches. Although minimal residual flow remained in the feeding artery, no residual filling of the shunt point or draining vein was observed. This endpoint was considered important for preserving perfusion of the surrounding penile skin and superficial tissues.

Previous studies have suggested that a moderate-concentration NLE, prepared with NBCA:Lipiodol:ethanol ratios of 2:7:1 or 3:6:1 under balloon occlusion, can achieve controlled embolization across the shunt while preventing excessive venous outflow [9]. In addition, NLE markedly prolongs solidification time, enabling effective embolization even when the catheter tip is positioned at a certain distance from the target lesion [10]. In this case, although a slight distance remained between the catheter tip and the shunt, the prolonged solidification time of the NLE permitted slow injection and adequate filling from the proximal feeding artery to the distal draining vein, resulting in satisfactory embolization without catheter adhesion or skin injury.

This report has several limitations. Although follow-up CT at 4 months showed no evidence of recurrence, longer-term imaging follow-up is needed because acquired AVFs and high-flow arteriovenous shunts may recur after apparent early occlusion. In addition, this report describes a single case, and further accumulation of similar cases is needed to evaluate the generalizability of this technique.

Conclusion

In conclusion, balloon-assisted endovascular embolization using a quadruple coaxial system and NLE may be a useful treatment option for selected superficial penile AVFs.
