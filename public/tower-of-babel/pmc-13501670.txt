Differentiated peak pathways and driving factors of industrial CO2 emissions at the provincial level in China
Authors: Zheng Wang, Changnan Kang, Huiyan Ge, Chenjun Du, Kangkang Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe industrial sector is the primary source of anthropogenic carbon emissions. Research on industrial carbon peaking and its drivers play a fundamental role in formulating emission reduction measures. The provincial industrial CO2 emissions were calculated from 2000 to 2021 in China. The Logarithmic Mean Divisia Index (LMDI) method was employed to quantitatively measure the contributions of industrial output, industrial structure, energy intensity, and energy structure to the changes in emissions. The results show that: (1) During the research period, the industrial CO2 emissions of various provinces experienced differentiated growth processes. Among those 11 provinces have already achieved industrial CO2 emission peak. (2) Industrial output value and energy intensity are the essential driving forces affecting industrial CO2 emissions, while the contributions of industrial structure and energy structure to CO2 emissions are relatively low. Spatio-Temporal differences exist in the contributions of various influencing factors. (3) 9 provinces have proactively peaked their emissions by improving energy efficiency, optimizing structure in energy use and industrial structure. While 2 passively emission declined provinces reduce emissions through a decline in industrial output value. (4) The provinces that have not yet reached their peak emissions are mainly industrial provinces, energy based provinces and developing central-western provinces. Their industrial output value has been steadily increasing, and the inhibitory effect of energy intensity and energy structure on CO2 is relatively low. Only under the low-carbon scenario can they almost achieve carbon peaking by 2030. The paper discusses industrial emission reduction strategies by controlling “super emitters” provinces, formulating differentiated emission reduction measures based on influencing factors, and practicing a low-carbon development path.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13021-026-00434-4.Keywords: Industrial CO2 emissions, Peaking pathways, Actively peaked, Passively peaked, Scenario simulation

Introduction

To tackle climate change, global governments and organizations have set carbon neutrality timetables. The European Union has set a goal to achieve carbon neutrality by 2050 [1, 2]. Similar to this, the United States was committed to achieving a “net-zero” greenhouse gas emissions plan and carbon neutrality by 2050 [3]. As the world’s largest carbon emitter, the Chinese government has committed to reaching the peak of CO2 emissions by 2030 and attaining carbon neutrality by 2060. The industrial, transportation and construction sectors have been identified as the world’s top three carbon emission sources and are facing severe challenges in reducing emissions [4]. The IPCC assessment indicates that the global industrial sector needs to reduce emissions by 25–30% before 2030 to achieve the 1.5 °C temperature control target [5]. Industry is a key sector for achieving carbon peaking and carbon neutrality [6].

China has experienced a rapid process of industrialization and urbanization since reform and opening-up in 1978 [7], Urban development, infrastructure construction, and improving in residents’ lifestyles all promote industry development [8]. As the major energy consumer in China, industry sector accounts for approximately 70% of the country’s total energy consumption [9]. This massive energy consumption inevitably leads to a large amount of CO2 emissions. Research shows that industry accounts for about 70% of the country’s carbon emissions [10, 11]. However, the added value of industry only accounts for 31.7% of the national GDP [9]. The contribution of industry to carbon emissions is substantial, and the industrial emissions intensity is too high. The huge amount of carbon emissions makes industry the main domain for reducing carbon emissions and achieving the carbon peak by 2030. Meanwhile, the high carbon emission intensity of industry makes it the main area for reducing carbon emission intensity and achieving a 60% to 65% reduction by 2030 compared to 2005 [12](Liu et al., 2019). Therefore, regulating industrial CO2 emissions is undoubtedly an important approach to carbon reduction efforts. Nevertheless, China is a vast country with significant spatial heterogeneity in terms of resource endowment, industrial structure, and technological level [11]. Previous studies have mostly focused on the carbon peak paths of individual provincial units [13], and the research on the differentiated peak paths of different provinces needs to be further deepened. More importantly, the provincial administrative units, as the key hubs for implementing national strategies and achieving specific indicators, are also the substantive entities for decomposing carbon emission indicators, and implementing environmental regulations. Therefore, conducting in-depth research at the provincial level on the spatio-temporal evolution characteristics and driving mechanisms of industrial carbon emissions is of irreplaceable practical significance for formulating precise regional emission reduction policies.

In recent years, the government has promoted the low-carbon transformation of industry through policy measures such as industrial structure adjustment, energy efficiency improvement, and clean energy substitution. The growth rate of industrial CO2 emissions has shown a trend of deceleration. Meanwhile, due to regional development differences, regional variations exist in industrial scale, industrial structure, and industrial technique level among different provinces in China. Thus, the industrial emissions of each provincial unit exhibited their own features and lead to different carbon peak path. However, the provincial CO2 emissions peak schedule and drivers is less clear. To fill the research gap, we conducted a research that revealed the emissions grow path and analyzed influence factors at provincial level in China from 2000 to 2021. Basing on estimated emission amounts, we revealed provincial emission grow path, identified peaked units, analyzed the influencing factors of CO2 emissions in different provinces, and further discussed formulating differentiated emission reduction strategies (Fig. 1).

Research framework

Literature review

Industrial CO2 emissions peaking path

Industrial CO2 emissions have drawn increasing attention due to large scale. Since China entered the 21 st century, especially with its accession to the WTO in 2001, the process of industrialization has accelerated significantly, promoting rapid economic development. In 2013, the economy entered a new normality, and the growth of industrial CO2 emissions began to slow down. In 2017, carbon emissions rebounded [14]. China’s industrial CO2 emissions presented a spatial pattern of “high in the east and low in the west”, but the growth rate in the central and western regions was faster [15]. Further research has found that industrial transfer has led to a shift of some CO2 emissions from the developed eastern regions to the central and western regions [16].

Facing the dual carbon goals, the carbon peaking paths of different sectors are important research contents. The major carbon-emitting, industry, transportation, construction, and power sectors are recognized as key domain for efforts to realize carbon peak. In the ‘baseline scenario’ development model, the transportation industry is projected to reach its peak in 2032 [17]. Under the ‘low-carbon scenario’, the peak is expected in 2027, while the peak times in eastern, central, and western regions may vary and be delayed sequentially [18]. In the baseline scenario, China’s construction industry is expected to reach its carbon peak in 2045, with a peak of 50,935,390 tons. Under the “low-carbon” scenario, the construction industry could peak in 2030, with emissions reaching 31,685,580 tons. Meanwhile, the timing of the peak varies across provinces [19]. As for the power industry, under the medium-term policy control scenario of slower macroeconomic growth, the CO2 emissions from the power industry could peak by 2030 [20].

Researchers have not yet reached a consensus on the path to peaking industrial carbon emissions. Some scholars predict that CO2 emissions in the industrial sector will peak as early as 2025 [21]. Others argue that the industry may lag behind this target and instead reach its peak around 2031 [14]. The demand for economic recovery during the pandemic has introduced new challenges to carbon peaking in the industrial sector [22, 23]. Research on the pathways to peaking industrial emissions suggests that it is feasible for China’s industrial CO2 emissions to peak before 2030 through industrial structure upgrading, energy revolution, and technological innovation [24]. Predictions for urban industries further indicate that under a green development and environmental protection scenario, carbon emissions could peak as early as 2030 [25]. However, due to the differences in regional industrial development, the timelines for peaking industrial CO2 emissions vary significantly across China. Certain regions have already achieved their carbon peak ahead of schedule [26].

Regional Differences in Industrial CO2 Emissions

The industrial sector consists of various sub-sectors. Identifying super-emitters among them, has become key research areas [27]. The Chinese government classified six energy-intensive industries, namely petroleum processing and coking, raw chemical materials and chemical products, non-metal minerals mining and dressing, ferrous metals mining and dressing, nonferrous metals mining and dressing, and production and supply of electricity and heat in 2010 [28]. Above six industries have attracted considerable academic attention due to their significant contributions to carbon emissions [29, 30]. When it comes to industry sector, researchers have particularly focused on sub-sectors such as steel production [31, 32], chemical engineering [33, 34], papermaking [35], and textile and apparel manufacturing [36].

Regional resource endowments and uneven development levels have resulted in significant differences in industrial technology levels and industrial structures across regions. Therefore, industry emissions are sensitive to regional heterogeneity. specific regions such as large emitter regions were focused on to conduct research.

For instance, studies have investigated the CO2 emission characteristics and influencing factors of the industry in regions such as economically developed areas [37, 38], major industrial manufacturing provinces [39], developing regions [40, 41], energy bases [42]. Such research is critical for formulating region-specific carbon reduction strategies and achieving balanced industrial development.

Influencing Factors of Industry CO2 emissions

Studies on carbon emissions aim to identify key influencing factors, and analyze the influencing mechanisms, thereby laying the foundation for low-carbon development strategies. Typically, STIPAT (Stochastic Impacts by Regression on Population, Affluence, and Technology) model, Kaya identity, and the Logarithmic Mean Divisia Index (LMDI) decomposition method were employed to quantitatively analyze carbon emissions growth. Scholars have analyzed the influencing factors of carbon emissions from the national, regional scale and industry levels. Findings highlight that economic level, energy consumption, and population, are the primary drivers of carbon emissions [37, 43]. These insights provide critical guidance for designing targeted carbon reduction policies and advancing sustainable development.

Economic factors including per capita GDP, industrial scale, and industrial structure, play a significant role in influencing carbon emissions. Industrial scale, exhibits a complex relationship with carbon emissions. It is generally believed that economic growth and industrial expansion are the major driving forces of carbon emissions [44]. However, when economic growth is accompanied by technological advancements and structural optimization, carbon emission intensity tends to decline [45]. Industrial structure, which refers to the composition and proportional relationship of various sectors within the national economy, significantly affects carbon emissions. For instance, transitioning from an industrial-based economy to a service-oriented economy can effectively reduce overall carbon emission intensity [46]. Energy factors, such as energy consumption, energy intensity, and energy structure, also critically influence carbon emissions. Energy structure pertains to the proportional use of different energy sources and their associated carbon emission coefficients. These factors vary significantly across industries and regions. Optimizing the energy structure, particularly by increasing the share of clean energy, can significantly lower amortized carbon emissions [47]. Energy intensity, defined as energy consumption per unit of GDP, serves as a vital indicator of energy utilization efficiency. A decline in energy intensity, coupled with the substitution of clean energy, is widely recognized as a key strategy for reducing carbon emissions [25]. In the context of economic development, steadily increasing the share of clean energy and improving energy efficiency are critical strategies for reducing CO₂ emissions. However, their effectiveness is significantly constrained by technological advancements and the availability of energy infrastructure.

Data and methods

Data

The industrial output value and industrial sub-sector output value data (measured in 108 yuan) for 30 provinces are obtained from the statistical yearbooks of each province. Additionally, based on the National Economic Industry Classification (GB/T 4754 − 2017), the industrial sector is divided into heavy industry, light industry and high-tech industry. The ratio of heavy industry output value to total industrial output value is used as a proxy indicator for the industrial structure of each province. Industrial CO2 emissions (measured in Mt CO₂) are calculated based on energy consumption data, following the IPCC inventory algorithm. The proportion of coal and oil in total energy consumption represents the energy consumption structure, while provincial energy consumption data for the period 2000–2021 are sourced from the China’s energy consumption balance table, measured in 10⁴ tons of standard coal equivalent (tce). Additionally, the standard coal conversion coefficients are adopted from the IPCC Guidelines for National Greenhouse Gas Inventories.

This paper uses the scenario analysis method to predict the industrial carbon dioxide emissions of each provincial unit from 2021 to 2040. To more comprehensively predict the time when the industrial carbon peak occurs in each province, three differentiated scenarios - the baseline scenario, the low-carbon scenario, and the high-carbon lock-in scenario - were constructed [17, 56, 57]. The Baseline Scenario follows a ‘Policy Inertia’ model, extrapolating the average rates of change from the 14th FYP period; The Low-Carbon Scenario embodies an ‘Enhanced Mitigation’ trajectory, assuming accelerated decarbonization driven by stringent policy enforcement; Conversely, the High-Carbon Lock-in Scenario simulates a potential regression where relaxed environmental regulations precipitate a resurgence of energy-intensive sectors, leading to stagnated improvements in energy efficiency and structural optimization (Table S1).

(1) Industrial output value forecast

Based primarily on the 14th Five-Year Plans and the 2035 Long-Term Plans of various provinces, together with relevant industry planning and forecast parameters, under the baseline scenario, the growth rates of industrial output value scale from 2021 to 2025, 2026 to 2030, 2031 to 2035, and 2036 to 2040 are 6%, 5.5%, 5.0%, and 4.5%, respectively. Among them, according to the 14th Five-Year Industrial Development Plans released by Jiangxi, Heilongjiang, and Guangxi, the growth rates of their industrial output value scale in each period are assumed to increase by 0.5% under the baseline scenario. With reference to previous studies [17], in the high-carbon scenario, the growth rate of industrial output value scale in each period is assumed to be increased by 0.3% compared to the baseline scenario, while under the low-carbon scenario, it is decreased by 0.3% compared to the baseline scenario.

(2) Energy Structure Forecast

In the scenario design, the provincial “2030 Carbon Peak Action Guidelines”, together with the coal consumption constraints or non-fossil energy proportion targets proposed in energy development plans, were primarily referenced. At the national level, China’s “14th Five-Year Plan” (2021–2025) set energy targets whereby the proportion of non-fossil energy consumption would reach approximately 20% and approximately 25% by 2030. Based on these national targets and combined with historical provincial trends, scenario-based projections were developed. Under the baseline scenario, the rates of energy structure change during 2021–2025, 2026–2030, 2031–2035, and 2036–2040 were − 2.0%, −1.6%, −1.3%, and 1.0%, respectively. Among the provinces considered, Inner Mongolia, Jiangsu, Shandong, Hebei, Shaanxi, and Guangdong have explicit targets for non-fossil energy shares or coal consumption proportions by 2025, therefore, the rates of energy structure change for these provinces were specified separately. With reference to previous studies [17], under the high-carbon scenario, the rate of energy structure change in each period was assumed to be 0.5% higher that of the baseline scenario, whereas under the low-carbon scenario, it was set to be 0.5% lower accordingly.

(3) Energy intensity prediction

In this study, the projection of energy intensity parameters was primarily based on the binding targets of national and provincial energy intensity constraints, with calibration conducted by incorporating the historical energy intensity change characteristics of each province. During China’s “14th Five-Year Plan” period (2021–2025), national energy policy sets a binding target of a 13.5% reduction in energy intensity per unit of GDP. Based on existing policies and observed energy intensity trends, under the baseline scenario, the rates of energy intensity change during 2021–2025, 2026–2030, 2031–2035, and 2036–2040 were − 2.5%, − 2.0%, − 1.5%, and 1.0%, respectively. Among the provinces considered, Anhui, Inner Mongolia, Jiangsu, Shandong, Liaoning, and Hebei have explicit energy intensity reduction targets by 2025; therefore, the energy intensity change rates for these provinces were specified separately. With reference to previous studies [17], under the high-carbon scenario, the energy intensity change rates in each period were assumed to be 0.2% higher than those under the baseline scenario, whereas under the low-carbon scenario, they were assumed to be 0.2% lower accordingly.

(4) Industrial Structure Forecast

At present, no explicit policy documents have been issued at the provincial or municipal level regarding the rate of change in the share of heavy industry. However, all provinces and municipalities have released policy documents aimed at promoting green industrial development and fostering the growth of emerging industries. Overall, the share of heavy industry within the industrial structure exhibits a declining trend. Based on historical trends in the share of heavy industry across provinces and municipalities, combined with recent economic development dynamics at both the national and provincial levels, under the baseline scenario, the rates of change in the industrial structure during 2021–2025, 2026–2030, 2031–2035, and 2036–2040 were − 1.5%, − 1.4%, − 1.3%, and − 1.2%, respectively. With reference to previous studies [17], under the high-carbon scenario, the rate of change in the industrial structure for each period was assumed to be 0.2% higher than that under the baseline scenario, whereas under the low-carbon scenario, it was assumed to be 0.2% lower accordingly.

Results and analysis

Spatio-temporal evolution of industrial emissions

From 2000 to 2021, provincial CO2 emissions growth in China exhibited two stage characteristics. Almost all provincial units’ emissions grew rapidly (Fig. S1). From 2000 to 2012, among those, Inner Mongolia, Ningxia, Xinjiang, Hainan, Qinghai, Fujian, and Shandong recorded the highest growth rates, each exceeding 300%. From 2012 to 2021, the growth of industrial CO2 emissions slowed nationwide, with emissions declining in several provinces. Beijing, Tianjin, Jilin, Shanghai, Henan, Hubei, Hunan, Chongqing and Sichuan exhibited clear downward trend. Nevertheless, energy-producing and economically less-developed provinces such as Xinjiang, Ningxia, Inner Mongolia, Shaanxi, Jiangxi and Guangxi still had significant growth rates, all above 40%. Notably, Xinjiang’s growth rate was approximately 130%.

According to the growth characteristic of CO₂ emissions, 30 provinces can be broadly categorized into three types: uninterruptedly increase, initial increase followed by decrease, and initial increase followed by lag (Table 1). Provinces exhibiting continuous increase include Hebei, Shanxi, Inner Mongolia, Liaoning, Heilongjiang, Jiangsu, Anhui, Jiangxi, Shandong, Guangdong, Guangxi, Guizhou, Shaanxi, Ningxia and Xinjiang. The second type includes Beijing, Tianjin, Jilin, Shanghai, Henan, Hubei, Chongqing, Sichuan, Yunnan and Qinghai, where emissions first increased and then declined. Industrial CO2 emissions in Zhejiang, Hunan, Hainan and Gansu first increased and then stagnated. Among these, provinces that experienced an increase followed by a declined have already reached their industrial CO₂ emissions peaks, provided that emissions do not rebound. Similarly, provinces characterized by an increase followed by a lag are also likely to have reached their emission peaks, as their emissions are expected to decline steadily in the coming years.

Analysis of the driving factors behind emissions

The LMDI method was employed to quantify the contributions of industrial output, industrial structure, energy intensity, and energy structure to changes in provincial industrial CO2 emissions during 2000–2004, 2004–2008, 2008–2012, 2012–2016, and 2016–2021. The results indicate that changes in industrial output and energy intensity are the primary drivers of provincial industrial CO2 emissions changes. In contrast, the contributions of industrial structure and energy structure are relatively minor. Additionally, the contributions of these four factors vary across temporal evolution and inter-province (Fig. 3).

Decomposition plot of industrial emission drivers based on Kaya's identity

From the perspective of industrial scale and industrial structure, both factors constitute the primary drivers of industrial carbon emissions growth, although their influence exhibits pronounced periodic fluctuations. From 2000 to 2012, the expansion of industrial scale was the dominant positive driving force, with a contribution significantly exceeding that of industrial structure, thereby continuously promoting industrial carbon emissions across most provinces. Industrial structure exerted a promoting effect between 2000 and 2008 but gradually shifted toward an inhibitory role after 2008, particularly in Heilongjiang and Liaoning Provinces. During the period 2012–2016, the positive effects of both industrial scale and industrial structure weakened substantially, and in some provinces they even became inhibitory factors. From 2016 to 2021, the positive contributions of both factors rebounded, accompanied by a further widening of interprovincial disparities.

From the perspective of energy structure and energy intensity, both generally exert a suppressive effect on industrial carbon emissions, although their mechanisms and evolutionary trajectories differ markedly. Energy intensity has consistently been a critical determinant of emission reduction, with its inhibitory effect peaking during 2008–2012 before gradually weakening. During 2012–2016, energy intensity even exerted a positive promoting effect in certain provinces, such as Heilongjiang, Qinghai, and Ningxia, indicating diminishing marginal returns to energy efficiency improvements and widening regional disparities. By contrast, the inhibitory effect of energy structure has been more stable, with its emission-reduction impact strengthening throughout the study period. Especially after 2016, the effect of energy structure surpassed that of energy intensity, emerging as a key long-term driver of industrial low-carbon transformation.

Overall, the driving mechanism of industrial carbon emissions demonstrates significant temporal evolution and regional heterogeneity. Throughout the study period, the “expansion effect” associated with industrial scale and industrial structure and the “constraint effect” associated with energy structure and energy intensity alternately dominated changes in industrial carbon emissions. The period 2012–2016 represents a critical transition stage, during which both the direction and magnitude of these effects shifted markedly. Not only did the combined influence of the four factors change from promoting to significantly inhibiting emissions, but provincial disparities also became most pronounced, exerting a profound influence on subsequent industrial carbon emission trajectories.

Prediction of industrial emissions peak timing

Under the baseline scenario, Hebei, Shanxi, and Anhui provinces will reach their peak carbon emissions in 2030, which is basically in line with China’s plan to achieve the overall national carbon peak in 2030 (Fig. 4). However, under the condition of maintaining the current industrial structure, economic growth rate, provinces such as Heilongjiang, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Fujian, Shandong, Guangdong, Guangxi, Shaanxi, Gansu, and Xinjiang will reach the peak of industrial CO2 emissions between 2030 and 2035. Meanwhile, provinces including Inner Mongolia, Liaoning, Hainan, and Guizhou still exhibit considerable potential for CO2 emissions reduction, making it challenging for them to reach the peak before 2035, and delay to 2035–2040. It is worth noting that Jiangxi and Ningxia provinces failed to reach their peak levels within the study period, but the increase of their industrial CO2 emissions has significantly weakened. Under the low-carbon scenario, Zhejiang, Gansu, and Xinjiang will reach their peaks between 2025 and 2030, while Inner Mongolia and Liaoning will also reach their peaks between 2030 and 2035. Under the high-carbon lock-in scenario, industrial CO2 emissions in provinces such as Inner Mongolia, Liaoning, Jiangxi, Hainan and Ningxia continue to grow slowly, and it is difficult to achieve carbon peaking before 2040. These findings are consistent with previous studies [58, 59]. To achieve the industrial carbon peaking in all provinces by 2030, it is necessary to develop industries under a low-carbon scenario.

Discussion

Differentiated peak paths

The Chinese industrial sector has developed rapidly due to large-scale foreign investment flowing into China since China’s accession to the WTO, and leading to a significant rise in industrial CO₂ emissions during the same period [60]. Since 2013, under the “new normal” of China’s economy, the Chinese government has launched the Belt and Road Initiative, promoted industrial transfer. As a result, the growth rate of industrial CO₂ emissions has slowed, and in some provinces, emissions have even stagnated or declined. These findings are consistent with Guan et al. (2018) [61].

During the research period, industrial CO₂ emissions of Beijing, Tianjin, Jilin, Shanghai, Henan, Hunan, Hubei, Chongqing, Sichuan, Yunnan and Qinghai provinces showed a continuous decline from 2016 to 2021 (Fig.S2). These provinces were the first to achieve peak industrial CO2 emissions. Although industrial CO2 emissions in other provinces declined in 2016, they rebounded by 2021. Additionally, industrial CO2 emissions in several provinces showed an overall upward fluctuation, and these provinces play a critical role in reducing CO₂ emissions and achieving industrial carbon peaking.

The 11 provinces have achieved carbon peaking, which can be categorized into passive and active carbon peaking modes based on the contributions of influencing factors (Table 2). Tianjin and Jilin are classified as passively peaked provinces, which achieve emission decrease primarily by declines in industrial output value. As traditional industry units, industrial upgrading has been implemented in recent years, and outdated industrial enterprises have been gradually phased out. Moreover, the development of high-tech industries can help reduce carbon dioxide emissions. These results are similar to other findings [62–64]. In contrast, other 9 proactively peaked units achieved carbon peaking mainly by improving energy utilization efficiency, optimizing energy structure, and adjusting industrial structure, allowing sustained growth in industrial output alongside emissions reductions. Shanghai, Henan, Hubei, Chongqing, Sichuan, and Yunnan mainly relied on the inhibitory effects of reduced energy intensity and optimized energy structure to curb CO2 emissions. Beijing and Hunan achieved emissions peaking mainly through the coordinated optimization of energy intensity, energy structure, and industrial structure. Qinghai adopted the CO2 emission reduction pathway driven by the optimization of energy structure.

Emission peaking driven by the effects of energy intensity and energy structure

Technological progress is a critical factor in reducing CO2 emissions. However, the decline in energy intensity has a dual effect. While reduced energy intensity curbs the growth of CO2 emissions, improvements in energy utilization efficiency lower energy costs, thereby encouraging enterprises to expand production. Consequently, the inhibitory effect of declining energy intensity is often offset by scale-driven efficiency gains. Therefore, although the energy intensity of most provincial units has declined rapidly (Fig. 5a), industrial CO2 emissions continue to rise in certain provinces.

Box plots of energy structure (a) and energy intensity (b) evolution from 2000 to 2021

In the early stages, improvements in energy utilization efficiency, driven by technological upgrades, significantly reduced industrial CO₂ emissions. Hence, the contribution of energy intensity to emission reduction was outstanding in most provinces during 2000–2012, which is consistent with other research [65–67]. Nevertheless, as technology improvement space became smaller, the marginal cost of further enhancing energy utilization efficiency rose, leading to a gradual weakening of the inhibitory effect of energy intensity on industrial CO2 emissions [68]. Meanwhile, due to the uneven technological development, the inhibitory effect of energy intensity exhibits obvious regional heterogeneity. In eastern regions, industrial enterprises generally adopt advanced automated production lines and energy-efficient equipment. In contrast, enterprises in the central and western regions often face challenges in upgrading technology due to constraints in financial resources and technical expertise. This further limits inhibitory effect of energy intensity on industrial CO2 emissions. The inhibitory effect of energy intensity has gradually weakened since 2012. Moreover, energy intensity in provinces such as Shanxi, Inner Mongolia, Liaoning, Heilongjiang, Xinjiang, and Ningxia became a primary contributor to the increase in industrial CO2 emissions during 2012–2016.

Under the economic “new normal”, several provinces have accelerated the upgrading of their industrial structure. The proportion of industry in total economic output has steadily declined, and the growth rate of industrial scale has slowed down. Meanwhile, the rising share of renewable energy sources, such as solar, wind, and hydropower, alongside the decarbonization and electrification of the energy system, has continuously optimized the energy structure (Fig. 5b). This optimization has played an increasingly significant role in curbing CO₂ emissions, emerging as a key driver of carbon peaking and aligning emissions reduction with declining energy intensity. Since 2012, the role of energy structure in reducing emissions has strengthened further. From 2016 to 2021, with the exception of Hainan Province, the optimization of energy structure has had a measurable inhibitory effect on CO2 emissions across all provinces. Among them, the energy structure in Hebei, Inner Mongolia, Liaoning, Heilongjiang, Zhejiang, Anhui, Shandong, Guangdong, Qinghai, and Ningxia has become the most important inhibitory factor. In provincial units such as Henan, Hubei, Chongqing, Sichuan, Yunnan and Shanghai, the decline in CO2 emissions is mainly driven by reductions in energy intensity and the optimization of the energy structure.

Rebound in industrial emissions and industrial intensification

The industrial production scale has experienced significant growth in energy-intensive regions (e.g., Shaanxi, Gansu, Ningxia, Xinjiang), industrial provinces (e.g., Hebei, Shanxi, Liaoning), economically underdeveloped provinces (e.g., Anhui, Jiangxi, Guizhou, Yunnan), and major manufacturing provinces (e.g., Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Fujian, Guangdong), while industrial CO2 emissions have also shown a rebound trend (Fig.S3). This rebound may be due to the impact of the China-US trade war since 2017, which prompted the revitalization of industrial activities and a subsequent resurgence in industrial production. Additionally, the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 temporarily disrupted the growth of CO2 emissions; however, CO2 emissions rebounded following the relaxation of regulatory policies. This trend aligns with the other research findings [69].

The industrial scale of economically underdeveloped provinces has rebounded, accompanied by an increasing trend of industrial intensification and expansion. This phenomenon is particularly evident in central and western provinces such as Anhui, Jiangxi, Henan, Hubei, Shaanxi, Gansu, Ningxia, and Qinghai, as well as in traditional industrial powerhouses and energy-intensive regions like Hebei, Shandong, Inner Mongolia, and Xinjiang. The proportion of heavy industry in these areas has increased significantly during the same period. This trend may be attributed to the transfer of traditional industries from economically developed coastal provinces to central and western provinces, which is consistent with the pollution heaven hypothesis for industrial CO₂ emissions. This observation aligns with previous research finding [43]. Among them, the energy intensity in Hebei, Ningxia, and Inner Mongolia has rebounded, and regional industrial development has entered a carbon lock-in mode, heavily reliant on the growth of CO2 emissions. Consequently, although the energy intensity of the above provinces has declined or the energy structure has been optimized, the emission reduction effects of technological progress and decarbonization of the energy structure have been offset by the promoting effect of industrial scale growth, resulting in a continuous upward trend in CO2 emissions.

Study limitations and future works

This study investigates the spatiotemporal dynamics of industrial CO2 emissions across China’s provincial-level regions, elucidating distinct peaking trajectories and their driving mechanisms. While multi-scenario simulations successfully quantify emission reduction potentials under diverse policy frameworks, several limitations remain addressed. Firstly, in modeling future scenarios for carbon peaking and low-carbon transition, the study emphasizes technological feasibility and policy stringency but stops short of providing a quantitative evaluation of the associated macroeconomic implications, specifically regarding capital investment and operational costs. Secondly, constrained by data and model complexities, the analysis of driving factors was confined to local energy consumption and industrial structure, inadvertently overlooking the significant influence of “embodied carbon” flows inherent in inter-provincial and international trade. Consequently, future inquiries should incorporate multi-regional input-output analyses and techno-economic assessment models. Such an approach is essential to trade off mitigation benefits against economic costs, ultimately determining optimal pathways that harmonize environmental sustainability with economic resilience.

Policy recommendations and conclusions

Policy recommendations

(1) Identification of key provincial units for industrial emission reduction. Provinces with significant growth trends in industrial CO2 emissions, such as Inner Mongolia, Liaoning, Jiangxi, Hainan, and Ningxia, should be prioritized as key regions for targeted industrial emission reduction efforts. These provinces need to formulate stricter CO2 emission control policies, intensify efforts to optimize the industrial structure, and promote the adoption of low-carbon technologies and clean energy to effectively reduce industrial CO2 emissions. In addition, major industrial provinces such as Shandong and Jiangsu, due to their large industrial scale and relatively high CO2 emissions, should also be regarded as priority provinces for industrial emission reduction. They should actively explore low-carbon development pathways and contribute to the realization of the national industrial carbon peaking targets.

(2) Propose region-specific strategies for provincial units based on local conditions. Considering the diverse factors influencing emissions across provincial units, tailored strategies are proposed for different regions. For eastern coastal provinces, such as Shandong, Jiangsu, and Zhejiang, efforts should focus on optimizing the energy structure, fostering the development of emerging technology-intensive industries, and promoting industrial upgrading to achieve sustainable growth. In the central regions, such as Anhui and Hubei, the adoption of low-carbon technologies and equipment should be prioritized. Optimizing the energy structure is critical, along with leveraging the potential for energy intensity reduction during the process of industrial transfer. In the western regions, such as Inner Mongolia and Xinjiang, policies should accelerate the deployment of wind and solar power within the energy consumption structure. Increasing the share of clean energy in the energy mix and reducing energy intensity are vital for achieving emission reduction goals. For traditional industrial provinces, such as Liaoning and Hebei, specific efforts should aim at improving energy utilization efficiency and advancing technological innovation to address their unique challenges. As for the southwest region, where abundant hydropower resources exist, it is essential to fully exploit these advantages. Strategies should include expanding hydropower capacity, increasing the share of clean energy in the energy structure, and further optimizing the overall energy structure to support regional sustainability goals.

(3) Low-carbon development pathways for industrial carbon peaking. Under the benchmark scenario, most provinces are unlikely to achieve their industrial carbon peaking targets by 2030. Therefore, provinces that have not yet reached the peak should follow the development path under the low-carbon scenario, further strengthen policy constraints and technological innovation, and achieve CO2 emissions reduction through structural optimization and improvements in energy efficiency. In addition, successful experiences from provinces that have already achieved emission peaking should be systematically introduced. For instance, Chongqing has achieved a stable decline in industrial CO2 emissions through enhanced energy management practices and improved energy utilization efficiency. Other provinces can adapt these best practices to their local conditions, thereby formulating more targeted and effective strategies for achieving their industrial carbon peaking objective.

Conclusion

Industrial CO2 emissions of provincial units in China from 2000 to 2021 were calculated based on the IPCC inventory methodology. The LMDI decomposition method was applied to quantitatively analyze the contributions of industrial scale, industrial structure, energy intensity, and energy structure to changes in CO2 emissions. The main findings were as follows:

(1) During the research period, Chinese industrial CO2 emissions exhibited two distinct stages: a rapid growth stage from 2000 to 2012, and a fluctuating decline stage from 2013 to 2021. The emissions grow differently across provincial units, 31 provinces’ emissions could be classified into three types: uninterruptedly increase, initial increase followed by lag, and initial increase followed by decrease.

(2) Industrial output and energy intensity primarily drove emission changes, while the effect of industrial structure and energy structure on emissions growth was relatively low. The contribution of different factors to emissions varied across temporal evolution and inter-province.

(3) Differentiated carbon peaking paths have emerged for provincial units. The 11 provincial units have achieved emission peaking. Among those, 2 passively peaked units achieved emission declined at the cost of industrial output reduction, 9 units proactively peaked emissions mainly through advancements in energy technologies, optimization of energy structure, and industrial upgrading.

(4) From 2016 to 2021, industrial CO₂ emissions rebounded in several provinces, driven by factors such as the resurgence of traditional industries. In particular, provinces in the central and western regions experienced intensified industrialization, leading to a sustained increase in their CO₂ emissions. These provinces face considerable challenges in achieving industrial carbon peaking by 2030.

(5) The benchmark scenario, low-carbon scenario and high-carbon lock-in scenario predictions indicate that only under the low-carbon development scenario can almost all provinces achieve carbon peaking, whereas some energy-oriented provinces still face considerable pressure to reach carbon peaking before 2030.
