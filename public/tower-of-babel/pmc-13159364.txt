Compliance to intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factors for treatment of retinal diseases in Nigerians: a retrospective multicenter study
Authors: Ogugua Ndubuisi Okonkwo, Adekunle Olubola Hassan, Darlingtess Abies Oronsaye, Chioma Emelumadu, Toyin Akanbi, Chineze Agweye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLittle is known about compliance with intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) therapy for the treatment of macular and retinovascular diseases among Nigerians and Africans. The objective of this study is to measure compliance to 3 or more and 6 or more intravitreal anti-VEGF injections for common macular and retinovascular diseases in Nigerian clinics and evaluate the impact on visual outcomes.MethodRetrospective multicenter chart review of 622 eyes/ 528 patients diagnosed with neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD), diabetic macular edema (DME), retinal vein occlusions (RVOs), including branch, central, and hemiretinal (BRVO, CRVO, and HRVO), and non-AMD Choroidal Neovascular Membrane (CNVM) from five clinics (urban, semi-urban, and rural), collecting demographics, diagnosis, injection type/number, pre-/post-BCVA (converted to LogMAR), and follow-up. Treatments were intravitreal Bevacizumab (Avastin), Ranibizumab (Patizra), and Aflibercept (Eylea). Definitions of compliance: compliant; ≥3 injections (standard loading), also ≥ 6 injections.ResultsFor all 622 eyes, presenting BCVA: 1.21 ± 0.84 and the final BCVA: 0.91 ± 0.80 (P = < 0.001). Overall compliance with ≥ 3 injections was 47.5%, and with ≥ 6 injections, 10.1%. Compliance to ≥ 3 injections by diagnosis was as follows: AMD 50.4%, Non-AMD CNVM 58.2%, BRVO 44.8%, CRVO 44.0%, HRVO 46.7%, DME 40.6%. Age (P = 0.264) and sex (P = 0.870) did not affect compliance to ≥ 3 injections. Clinic location significantly influenced compliance with ≥ 3 injections (P = 0.000), but not with 6 injections (P = 0.173). The highest rates of compliance with ≥ 3 injections were observed in urban tertiary centers. Injection type and cost were not significant factors (P = 0.36). Eyes with ≥ 3 injections achieved better vision (≥ 6/18) across all diagnoses; the most notable improvements were in non-AMD CNVM (+ 41.4%) and BRVO (+ 35%). Statistically significant LogMAR improvements were seen in CRVO (p = 0.049) and DME (p = 0.043). Postoperative endophthalmitis occurred in 2/622 eyes (0.0032) (both Avastin); no other serious adverse event was recorded.ConclusionsReal-world compliance is significantly lower than ideal. Urban and tertiary clinics show better adherence. Receiving the recommended loading doses is associated with improved visual outcomes for most diagnoses. Understanding the reasons for non-compliance, using a prospective approach, and addressing them will improve treatment outcomes for more Nigerian and presumably African patients receiving anti-VEGF drugs.Keywords: Intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factors, Age-related macular degeneration, Diabetic macular edema, Retinal vein occlusion, Choroidal neovascular membrane, Patient-compliance

Background

Intravitreal injection (IVI) of anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) agents has become popular for treating several retinal diseases because of their superior anatomical and visual outcomes compared to other therapies, such as retinal laser photocoagulation [1–3]. It is well known that a drawback of IVI anti-VEGF therapy is its frequent administration [4], which burdens patients, health systems, and treating physicians, making it difficult to follow the prescribed regimen recommended by the approving studies. For this reason, undertreatment is often reported in real-world use of these drugs since compliance with prescribed treatment protocol is lower than necessary, leading to inferior outcomes to those reported in the pivotal studies [5]. This situation has become a serious concern, prompting the search for more durable drugs that offer extended treatment intervals and less burden for patients and physicians [6, 7]. Compliance with IVI of anti-VEGF therapy has been studied, and researchers have identified factors contributing to poor adherence in the treatment of common retinal diseases. Still, none have done so from a purely African perspective [5]. It is essential to determine the extent of compliance and non-compliance in already health-disadvantaged populations, where poorer outcomes can be expected.

Because of the proven effectiveness of intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy, it has been widely adopted in low-income economies, including sub-Saharan Africa, with strained resources and health system deficits [8–10]. The logistical deficits and economic realities in these countries pose an even greater risk of undertreatment with intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy. Compliance with the use of intravitreal anti-VEGF drugs, fundamental to the success of IVI therapy, needs to be examined in the context of such low-income countries, where more severe forms of disease are seen (because of delays in presentation), and out-of-pocket payment is typical [11–13]. Understanding the rates of compliance introduces the magnitude of the problem, and investigating the factors affecting compliance will greatly benefit patients, physicians, policymakers, healthcare managers, governments, and other stakeholders. Furthermore, researching compliance with intravitreal anti-VEGF treatment and the duration of treatment provides a deeper understanding of the number of injections performed over a specific period, thereby enabling accurate compliance ratings and enhancing the ability to plan and implement strategies to improve access. Compliance with intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy has not been previously studied in a multicenter African setting, including a mix of urban and rural patients.

Therefore, we conducted a multicenter study to examine compliance with IVI of anti-VEGF therapy in a Nigerian population diagnosed with common macular and retinovascular diseases. We also assessed the impact of compliance on the visual outcomes of treated eyes.

Methods

Study aim

To determine the proportion of eyes compliant with 3 or more, and 6 or more anti-VEGF injections. We hypothesize that diagnosis, anti-VEGF drug type and cost, clinic location, and demographics could affect compliance with IVI of anti-VEGF.

Design

We performed a retrospective chart review of clinical data and studied the number of eyes that received 3 monthly IVI of anti-VEGF and 6 or more IVIs. Consecutive patients diagnosed with one of six macular or retinovascular diseases and treated with anti-VEGF constituted the study sample. There was no exclusion. From clinical records, data were obtained.

Data collection

Relevant information was obtained from the patient’s record by a trained data officer. Data collected includes the patients’ age and gender, symptom duration, indication/diagnosis for anti-VEGF injection, laterality, type of anti-VEGF injection, number of IVI, pre- and post-IVI Snellen best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), and duration of follow-up. The Snellen BCVA was converted to LogMAR. To determine the effect of anti-VEGF treatment on visual acuity across different diseases, the change between mean preoperative and postoperative LogMAR BCVA was assessed. Also, the rate of 6/18 or greater vision was used to assess the visual outcome of treated eyes.

Study setting

Five clinics participated in this multicenter study. Three of the clinics were in urban locations, and one each in a semi-urban and a rural location. This selection of clinics helped determine how the impact of clinic location, the cosmopolitan constitution, and patient distribution affected the number of IVI of anti-VEGF received and compliance. Clinics 1 and 2 had dedicated retina clinics and received referrals for retinal care from other regions of the country. There were vitreoretinal consultants in attendance in clinics 1 and 2, which served as tertiary referral centers for vitreoretinal cases. Clinics 3, 4, and 5 did not have dedicated retina clinics, but ophthalmologists who could diagnose and treat patients with macular and retinovascular diseases requiring intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy. Clinics 3, 4, and 5 were in urban, semi-urban, and rural locations, respectively. Considering all five clinics, only approximately 18% to 20% have medical insurance coverage for health care. The majority are required to pay for private care out of pocket. The three anti-VEGFs available at the time of the study to the five clinics are Bevacizumab (Avastin, Roche, 1.25 mg/0.05mL), which is used off-label for treating ocular diseases, Ranibizumab (Patizra, Novartis, 0.5 mg/0.05mL), and Aflibercept (Eylea, Bayer, 2.0 mg/0.05mL). All intravitreal anti-VEGF injections were administered in the operating room. The intravitreal anti-VEGF injections sampled were given between February 2017 and February 2022.

The consultant ophthalmologist or retinal specialist diagnosed macular or retinovascular disease, and a treatment plan was outlined after a clinical examination that included Snellen visual acuity testing, slit lamp biomicroscopy, and fundus examination with a 90D lens or binocular indirect ophthalmoscopy with a 20D lens. Ocular investigations could consist of one or more of the following: fundus photography, fundus fluorescein angiography, optical coherence tomography (OCT), and OCT angiography (OCTA), depending on the equipment available at the clinic. In some cases, visual acuity and findings on fundus examination were used to diagnose and monitor treatment response to intravitreal anti-VEGF when pre- or post-injection OCT could not be performed due to financial constraints. At the monthly clinic visits, the clinical examination as outlined initially would be repeated. At least three monthly loading doses of intravitreal anti-VEGF injections would be prescribed for the treatment of common macular and retinovascular diseases, including neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD), diabetic macular edema (DME), Retinal Vein Occlusions (RVOs), including Branch, Central, and Hemiretinal (BRVO, CRVO, and HRVO), and non-AMD Choroidal Neovascular Membrane (CNVM).

To clarify, we define neovascular age-related macular degeneration as the wet (neovascular or exudative) form, characterized by abnormal new blood vessel growth (neovascularization) that leaks fluid or blood beneath the macula. This “active” leakage appears as intraretinal fluid on OCT and causes rapid central vision loss. Diabetic macular edema involves fluid and lipid (hard exudate) buildup in the macula, resulting from vascular damage associated with long-term diabetes complications. Central retinal vein occlusion occurs when the main retinal vein posterior to the lamina cribrosa of the optic nerve is blocked, usually by thrombosis. Branch retinal vein occlusion refers to the obstruction of a branch of the retinal vein at an arteriovenous crossing. Hemiretinal vein occlusion (HRVO), a subtype, happens at the optic disc, blocking drainage in either the superior or inferior retina. HRVO acts as an intermediate between CRVO and BRVO. All RVO cases in this study showed retinal hemorrhages (distributed according to venous drainage), retinal and macular edema. Non-AMD CNVM was most often due to myopic choroidal neovascularization (80%) and idiopathic choroidal neovascularization (20%).

Because local compounding pharmacies are not used in Nigeria, most clinicians will compound aliquot intravitreal injections for patients directly from a 100 mg/4 ml vial of Bevacizumab. Ranibizumab and Aflibercept are available in single-dose packaged vials. The drug cost to the patient varies and is a significant determinant in choosing an anti-VEGF agent. Bevacizumab costs between $50 and $70 USD in different clinics across Nigeria, while Ranibizumab costs approximately $500, and Aflibercept costs between $500 to $700 USD at the time of reporting this study.

Study procedures

Secondary outcome

Secondary objectives include determining the effect of compliance on visual outcomes and predictors of adherence.

Definition of compliance to IVI of anti-VEGF

To evaluate adherence to intravitreal anti-VEGF treatment, we categorized eyes as compliant (three or more doses) or noncompliant (fewer than three doses) for six macular and retinovascular diseases. We also counted the number of eyes that received six or more intravitreal anti-VEGF injections. Treatment of nAMD, DME, and RVO at the participating hospitals follows a standard loading-dose regimen, with a minimum of 3 monthly intravitreal injections. After the initial three-monthly loading dose, an OCT scan is performed, and the injection interval can be extended by 4 weeks if macular edema resolves (as indicated by a reduction in central foveal thickness to normal). In some cases, patients may choose to be treated at their convenience (PRN-Pro Re Nata), when they attend clinics, and their OCT shows the presence of macular fluid or vision is poorer than previously. In summary, the treatment plan involves administering intravitreal anti-VEGFs monthly for three initial doses across six evaluated retinal diseases. If OCT results at the three-month mark indicate the need for ongoing treatment, monthly injections can continue; if not, extending the treatment interval is advised. This extension involves adding four weeks to the current interval. In this study, compliance was measured by counting the eyes that received three monthly anti-VEGF injections after the initial 3 months.

According to our treatment protocol for the six retina diseases, each eye is expected to receive the first three monthly injections within the first 3 months, followed by a 4-week extension of the treatment interval, at least 3 more injections every 8 weeks over the next 9 months, making a total of 6 injections over 12 months. Estimating how many eyes adhere to this provides evidence of the rate of compliance with treatment during the first 12 months. Depending on individual response to treatment, the number of injections could be higher.

We evaluated the impact of compliance on visual outcomes by measuring the change in LogMAR vision between pre-injection and final BCVA after treatment. Additionally, we assessed the proportion of eyes with 6/18 or better vision before treatment and after treatment in both the compliant and non-compliant groups.

Data analysis

Data were initially collected in Google Sheets and then exported to IBM SPSS (version 22, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for analysis. Continuous variables, including visual acuity measured in logarithms of the minimum angle of resolution (LogMAR), were summarized as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, which indicated non-normality.

Visual acuity at presentation (entry VA) and at the final visit (final VA) were analyzed as continuous variables. To assess changes in visual acuity over time across the six diseases, a repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, with time (presentation vs. final visit) as the within-subject factor and diagnosis as the between-subject factor. This approach appropriately accounted for the paired nature of repeated measurements within the same patients while allowing comparison across independent diagnostic groups. The interaction between time and diagnosis was evaluated to determine whether the magnitude of visual improvement differed across diagnostic categories.

Change in visual acuity (ΔVA) was calculated as the difference between presentation and final visual acuity (entry VA–final VA), with positive values indicating improvement.

To further explore differences within individual diagnostic groups, subgroup analyses were conducted by stratifying the dataset by diagnosis. Within each subgroup, independent-samples t-tests were used to compare mean changes in visual acuity between compliant and non-compliant patients. When normality assumptions were not met, the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test was applied.

Associations between categorical variables (unpaired), including diagnosis, gender, clinic location, injection type, and compliance status, were assessed using the Chi-square test of independence. Analyses were conducted on independent groups, as each patient contributed a single observation to each variable and no repeated or matched measurements were involved; therefore, methods for paired categorical data were not applicable. When expected cell counts were less than 5, Fisher’s exact test or Monte Carlo estimation was used as appropriate.

A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Ethics statement

The Eye Foundation Hospital Research Ethics Committee granted a waiver of ethical approval for this study because it involved only a review of the patient’s records, Reference Number EFH-HREC/2022/35. This research was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration, and patient identities were masked to ensure anonymity and confidentiality.

Discussion

While numerous studies on anti-VEGF treatment patterns exist from other continents, there are very few African studies to date. Therefore, this work contributes significantly to the understanding of global treatment patterns.

This multicenter retrospective study of 622 eyes treated with IVI of anti-VEGF in Nigerians reveals significant real-world gaps between recommended regimens and delivered treatments, with measurable effects on visual outcomes. This study also demonstrates the efficacy of IVI of anti-VEGF with recorded gains in vision across all six diagnoses after significant periods of follow-up. In general, significant gains in LogMAR vision were observed at final assessment (Tables 2 and 3). Key findings are that overall adherence was low; 47.5% of eyes received ≥ 3 injections (the standard loading dose), and only 10.1% received ≥ 6 injections over the recommended 12-month treatment interval. Compliance varied by diagnosis: highest ≥ 3 compliance in non-AMD CNVM (58.2%) and lowest in DME (40.6%); ≥6 compliance was highest in nAMD (20.8%) and very low for DME (3.8%) and HRVO (0%). Clinic location strongly influenced receipt of ≥ 3 injections (P = 0.00) (with urban tertiary centers showing higher compliance), but did not significantly affect ≥ 6 injections (P = 0.173). The type of anti-VEGF (Avastin, Ranibizumab, Aflibercept) did not significantly affect compliance for either of the two thresholds.

Visual outcomes favored eyes that completed ≥ 3 injections, and a higher proportion achieved 6/18 or better post-treatment across diagnoses (notably non-AMD CNVM + 41.4% and BRVO + 35%). Though all eye categories, regardless of disease or compliance status, showed improvement in LogMAR vision, this was statistically significant for CRVO (p = 0.049) and DME (p = 0.043), as shown in Table 6.

Our study is the first study in Nigeria and sub-Saharan Africa, according to our search, exploring compliance with intravitreal anti-VEGF and finds that undertreatment is common. The reason for this is likely multifactorial. Low rates of ≥ 3 and ≥ 6 injections indicate substantial deviation from the recommended pivotal trial regimens, and this finding is consistent with the global “real-world” experience, but more pronounced in our study [14–16]. Studies on compliance or adherence to recommended intravitreal injections include the report by Sobolewska et al., which found that non-adherence increased with increasing injection duration [5]. Our findings agree with theirs as rates of adherence to IV injection dropped sharply with time, signified by much lower rates of 6 injections (given over a longer length of time) compared to 3 injections. Radhakrishnan’s study from India reported that younger age and shorter duration of diabetes positively influenced compliance [17]. Polat from Turkey reported 78% of patients completed three monthly loading doses of anti-VEGF [18]. From Jordan is a report of 86% compliance [19]. These reports are superior to our finding of 47.5% and can be explained by the disparity in socioeconomic and health care indices between the countries.

Economic barriers are a key factor and likely influence the adoption of goods and services, including health-related services. Also, most of our patients pay out of pocket, and prices vary greatly—Avastin costs $50–70, whereas Ranibizumab and Aflibercept range from $500 to $700. As a result, Avastin should be preferred over the more expensive options. The fact that injection type did not significantly predict compliance suggests that cost interacts with other factors, such as access, referral patterns, perceived need, and treatment efficacy. Most patients received Avastin because of its more affordable pricing, but discontinued before completing loading doses, with compliance to Avastin being 45% for three or more injections and 13% for six injections. In some clinical scenarios, while post-treatment anatomic changes are observed on OCT, the visual changes lag behind or do not meet the patient’s expectations, prompting the patient to abandon treatment due to perceived lack of effect. Revealing the anatomic benefits of anti-VEGF therapy, as evident on OCT imaging, will encourage adherence to the recommended multiple-dose regimen of anti-VEGF injections. The challenge with this is the low utilization of OCT for the treatment of retinovascular disease, a situation that has been reported in Nigerian clinics [20]. Therefore, access to the OCT facility should be prioritized for the treatment of retinal diseases requiring anti-VEGF injections in low-income economies.

Habib et al., using a health belief model questionnaire, examined 343 Egyptian DME patients and identified the following as significant factors associated with non-adherence to scheduled clinic visits and anti-VEGF injections: unilateral injection, lack of funding, higher perceived barriers, lower perceived benefits, lower self-efficacy, and lower perceived susceptibility [21]. Our study was retrospective, did not use a health-related questionnaire, and did not consider any of the factors identified by Habib. However, perceived barriers and the absence of funding are general themes likely to be important factors to consider in the African context and will be explored in future prospective studies. Our study reveals that health system factors matter, as higher adherence in urban tertiary clinics (clinics 1–3) suggests better infrastructure, retina specialists, referral networks, or patient socioeconomic status improves compliance with prescribed anti-VEGF treatment. This finding is also supported by Rocha et al., who found that hospitals with higher organizational levels are associated with higher anti-VEGF treatment rates. Rocha also observed that more anti-VEGF treatments occurred in metropolitan areas, a finding supported by ours [22].

Despite significant and widespread anti-VEGF undertreatment, there was a good clinical effect on vision (Tables 2 and 3), as eyes that received ≥ 3 injections more often reached functional vision thresholds (6/18), and significant LogMAR gains in CRVO and DME indicate that completing early loading doses has a measurable benefit for vision. The beneficial effect, at least in the short term, has been reported by other studies on IV anti-VEGF adherence [17, 23]. Therefore, treatment for retinovascular diseases should aim for early loading doses of IVI of anti-VEGF and compliance with standard recommended treatment that sustains visual improvement. Recent research using a large database revealed that, in the real world, an average of 7.2 injections in the first year and 5.6 in the second year was achieved in order to sustain the gained vision [24]. Patients need to be educated on the required number of intravitreal anti-VEGF injections from the beginning of their first injection. Clearly, low adherence to IV anti-VEGF injections results in declining vision [24].

UNCOVER was a large, multicenter study classifying Ranibizumab-treated nAMD patients into three reimbursement groups: partially reimbursed, fully reimbursed, and self-paid [25]. It found patients had an average vision loss of -0.7 letters and a central retinal thickness (CRT) decrease of -44.4 μm. The self-paid group had the greatest CRT change. Our study was not stratified by insurance, but it showed a significant improvement in LogMAR vision for all diseases (grouped together, Table 2) and each disease (Tables 4 and 6). Data in Table 6 suggest that, over time, most diseases’ vision improved or stabilized, but nAMD didn’t show significant improvement, partly aligning with UNCOVER’s finding of poor vision outcome for nAMD, indicating that nAMD might be uniquely disadvantaged [25].

We investigated factors affecting compliance. Demographics were not a significant factor in our study, contrary to Wykoff’s report, which showed that older age and male gender were risk factors for vision loss at year 1, and this was tied to adherence [24]. Our study did not involve direct patient interaction. A study from India similar to ours reported an approximate 50% loss to follow-up, with non-affordability and no improvement in vision as factors [26]. These factors may likely interplay with others in the Nigerian setting. In another Palestinian study, 60% non-adherence was reported. A broader range of factors were determined to affect adherence, including the planned number of injections (fewer injections were more adherent), cost (financial barriers were a significant issue), indication for injections, sex (females were more adherent), need for physical assistance, and presence of mobility problems, which negatively influenced adherence [27]. Studies in Nigeria and other African states that adopt a patient-interview approach will yield more information on the direct causes of non-compliance in our specific population. This will help tailor interventions to improve compliance with intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy among Nigerians.

Two studies from Africa highlight IVI of anti-VEGF use in an African context. Oluleye et al. in Ibadan, Nigeria, conducted an audit of 182 eyes that received 330 IVI of Bevacizumab over three years. Most eyes (51.7%) received only 1 injection, while smaller percentages received 3 (27.5%) or 4 (2.7%) [28]. Despite this, they found that overall vision improved from 1.67 ± 0.91 LogMAR at baseline to 1.50 ± 1.27 LogMAR after one year. Our study included more injections and a longer follow-up period. The rate of patients receiving more than 3 injections (30.2%) was lower in Oluleye’s study compared to ours. Both African studies show a similar trend: a low number of IVIs administered, with only minimal to modest improvement in vision.

Again, Kabunga et al. prospectively reported on a small series of Ugandan eyes with macular edema treated with intravitreal bevacizumab, with significant improvements in vision and reduction in central macular thickness [29]. Mean BCVA improved from 0.70 ± 0.38 at baseline to 0.38 ± 0.36 LogMAR. The improvement in central macular thickness (CMT) and BCVA was more marked in patients with DME than in those with other causes. The findings of improved vision align with the general theme, including ours, on the effectiveness of IVI of anti-VEGF therapy for retinovascular diseases. Our study also supports the marked improvements in DME compared to AMD, as found by Kabunga.

Strengths and limitations relevant to our study include a multicenter design spanning urban, semi-urban, and rural clinics that captures real-world variability and involves a reasonably large sample of 622 eyes. Limitations affecting interpretation include its retrospective chart review nature; lack of standardized criteria for continuing or stopping therapy (some centers relied on visual acuity and fundus examination when OCT was not affordable); potential selection bias toward tertiary referral centers; limited data on reasons for non-compliance (financial, transportation, or patient beliefs); and short or variable follow-up in some subgroups. These factors restrict causal inference about why compliance is low and limit the ability to adjust for confounders. Although the retrospective design of our study could introduce selection bias, as discussed earlier, nonetheless, real-world data on African eyes clearly reveal insufficient anti-VEGF use in clinics, indicating opportunities for improvement. This represents a novel contribution in the African context, as such findings have not been previously documented in this way.

There were 2 cases of acute post-injection endophthalmitis during the study period, for a rate of 2/622 eyes (0.0032). Both cases involved intravitreal Avastin. There were no cases of retinal detachment, vitreous hemorrhage, occlusive vasculitis, or any other severe adverse events.

The following are three important implications of our study findings: (1) Clinical practice: Emphasizes the need to prioritize early adherence support through education, financial counseling, and scheduling, particularly for intravitreal anti-VEGF patients who will benefit from completing loading doses. (2) Health policy: Data support policies aimed at reducing out-of-pocket costs—such as insurance expansion, negotiated pricing, and perhaps using low-cost Avastin with safe compounding practices—and strengthening retinal services outside tertiary centers to improve access. (3) Future research: Prospective studies are needed to understand patient-level reasons for non-adherence, conduct cost-effectiveness analyses of subsidized therapy, and carry out interventional trials that focus on adherence strategies such as reminders and bundled pricing. We did not consider the effect of insurance coverage (a potentially important determinant of compliance) in the current study. Nonetheless, a multicenter prospective study starting soon will explore real-world intravitreal anti-VEGF use and outcomes in Nigerians. The study will, amongst other things, investigate how health insurance coverage influences adherence. The study will also investigate the reasons behind missed scheduled clinic visits among patients who do not attend.

From a community ophthalmology perspective, research on adherence to anti-VEGF injections in African populations should move beyond simply focusing on the “non-compliant patient”. Instead, it should explore the systems, logistics, and sociocultural factors that hinder adherence. For example, examining how out-of-pocket payments, healthcare financing, and cultural beliefs affect healthcare and disease management can identify potential intervention targets.

Conclusion: This study reveals low adherence to recommended intravitreal anti-VEGF regimens in Nigeria, with patterns influenced by clinic location and diagnosis. It also shows that receiving the recommended loading doses is associated with better functional outcomes across several anti-VEGF treated retinal diseases. Addressing financial and access barriers and tailoring adherence strategies for high-risk groups (including patients attending semi-urban and rural clinics) could improve the real-world effectiveness of anti-VEGF therapy in similar settings across Africa and other developing economies.
