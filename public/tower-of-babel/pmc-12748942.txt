Active learning-guided catalyst design for selective acetate production in CO electroreduction
Authors: Yuting Xu, Hefei Li, Jiaqi Yang, Yilang Liu, Wanyu Deng, Ahryeon Lee, Feng Jiao, Fanglin Che
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Acetic acid is an essential chemical with industrial and consumer relevance, and global demand is expected to reach 24.5 million tonnes by 2025. Electrochemical CO reduction reaction (CORR) over Cu offers a sustainable route to acetate from waste carbon, but identifying catalysts with high CO-to-acetate selectivity remains challenging due to limited mechanistic understanding. Here, we establish an artificial intelligence (AI)-driven multi-scale simulation framework integrating grand-canonical density functional theory (GC-DFT), microkinetic modeling (MKM), and active learning to elucidate the CORR mechanism and guide catalyst discovery. The DFT-based MKM reveals that acetate forms via CO-CH coupling, with CH* binding energy identified as the key descriptor governing selectivity of acetate production from CORR. Active learning optimization predicts Cu/Pd (2:1) and Cu/Ag (3:1) as the most selective catalysts. Zero-gap electrolyzer experiments confirm their effective performance, achieving acetate Faradaic efficiencies of 50% and 47%, respectively, compared to 21% for pure Cu. This study demonstrates a data-driven catalyst design strategy for advancing selective electrocatalysis.

Introduction

Acetic acid is an essential chemical reagent with broad industrial applications, including its use in textiles, food production, and pharmaceuticals. Its global demand has risen sharply, increasing from 17.3 million tonnes in 2019 to an estimated 24.5 million tonnes by 20251. Beyond traditional industrial uses, acetic acid has gained increasing attention for its role in sustainable biomanufacturing2. A notable example is the work by Hann et al., they have developed a hybrid inorganic-biological system integrating electrocatalysis with microbial conversion. In this system, the CO2-derived acetate serves as both a carbon and energy source for food production, highlighting its potential in sustainable biomanufacturing processes3.

With the growing demand for acetic acid, developing energy-efficient and sustainable production methods is crucial. Conventional methanol carbonylation is energy-intensive, fossil fuel-dependent, and relies on toxic catalysts and hazardous CO feedstocks, operating in a corrosive environment with limited feedstock flexibility4. In contrast, electrocatalytic approaches offer a more sustainable alternative. CO reduction reaction (CORR) consistently achieves higher acetate selectivity than CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR), with increased CO partial pressure further enhancing acetate formation5. To improve efficiency, CORR in a membrane electrode assembly (MEA) or zero-gap configuration has emerged as a promising strategy for acetate synthesis6,7. A tandem approach utilizing CORR in an MEA system has been reported to achieve higher energy efficiency and selectivity toward C2+ products compared to direct CO2RR8. Under alkaline conditions, CO2RR typically yields acetate selectivity below 10%, whereas CORR can exceed ~45%9,10. Moreover, CORR eliminates carbonate formation, a major challenge in alkaline CO2RR, making it a more practical and scalable approach for acetate production11.

Cu is the most extensively studied catalyst for CO-to-acetate conversion12,13. However, the selectivity of acetate on Cu is still limited. Various strategies to enhance the selectivity of Cu catalyst, including nano structuring, fluorinated polymer coatings, and bimetallic systems, have been developed9,14. Beyond catalyst design, acetate formation is also influenced by electrochemical parameters such as applied potential, pH, and CO partial pressure15. However, the optimal conditions for maximizing acetate selectivity and the rates of the rate-determining step (RDS) relative to C2 species remain unclear. Current optimization depends on trial-and-error methods because of limited mechanistic understanding. Enhancing this understanding is essential for rational catalyst design, leading to improved selectivity, efficiency, and industrial viability16.

Previous computational studies have explored acetate formation mechanism by analyzing reaction energies and barriers of selective CORR elementary steps using density functional theory (DFT) at the atomic scale9,17. Luc et al. investigated the reaction mechanism of CORR to acetate in comparison to ethylene and ethanol over Cu through DFT simulations. Their calculations identified CO-CH* as the key intermediate for acetate formation rather than ethylene9. However, their study exclusively considered CO dimerization as the pathway for C2 species formation, without comprehensively incorporating alternative C-C coupling mechanisms involving protonation reactions, such as CO-COH, CO-CHO, or CO-CHx pathways.

To investigate a broader range of C2 formation pathways after CO activation, Zhang et al. examined C-C coupling reactions where CO* undergoes protonation to form COH* or CHO*18. Their study identified CO-COH coupling as the most favorable pathway for C2 precursor formation, followed by successive hydrogenation reactions leading to acetate production. However, this work did not consider asymmetrical CO-CHx coupling, nor did it comprehensively evaluate the complex hydrogenation steps required for final product formation. Instead, it relied on simplified pathways, limiting its ability to identify all the possibilities of the selectivity-determining steps for acetate formation relative to other products.

Furthermore, the absence of solvation effects in the above approach reduces the accuracy of simulating real electrochemical electrode/electrolyte interfaces19–21. To address this, Santatiwongchai et al. introduced an explicit solvation model with three water layers to study the reaction mechanism of CO electroreduction to C2 species22. Unlike conventional DFT simulations in vacuum, their results demonstrated that solvation effects facilitate protonation reactions, enhancing the conversion of CO* to COH* and CHO*. This results in a thermodynamically preferred CO-COH/CHO coupling pathway, which is more favorable than CO dimerization. These findings highlight the substantial role of solvent interactions in promoting C-C coupling and subsequent protonation steps, underscoring the critical influence of solvation effects in electrochemical reaction mechanisms.

However, DFT-calculated energetics alone cannot directly quantify and benchmark the Faradaic efficiencies (FE) observed in electrochemical experiments, as they do not account for the influence of operating conditions. To quantitatively link DFT-derived energetics with CORR-to-acetate selectivity under operating conditions, microkinetic modeling (MKM) is a powerful approach21. Xiang et al. developed an MKM framework combined with DFT simulations to examine the effects of electrode potential and solution pH on CO2 electroreduction23. Their integrated model demonstrated that operating conditions regulate product selectivity, where formic acid (HCOOH) formation is thermodynamically controlled at low overpotentials, while CO production is kinetically controlled, with higher overpotentials favoring its formation. Focusing on C2 product formation, Liu et al. constructed an MKM based on a highly simplified reaction mechanism to identify the RDSs in CO2RR24. Their findings revealed that C2 product selectivity relative to C1 species is dictated by CO surface coverage, with higher CO coverage enhancing C-C coupling, thereby improving C2 selectivity.

While most current theoretical studies primarily aim to rationalize experimental observations, they are still limited in constructing comprehensive reaction networks and performing predictive multi-scale simulations that closely reflect real-world operating conditions. However, such a multi-scale approach is essential for achieving a deeper mechanistic understanding of reaction kinetics, accurately identifying RDSs, and uncovering key descriptors that predict and govern product selectivity. To bridge this gap, we developed an integrated multi-scale simulation framework that combines grand-canonical (GC) DFT with MKM. Our results highlight CH* binding energy as a critical descriptor for acetate selectivity, providing a mechanistic foundation for catalyst optimization. Building on the identified reaction mechanism, we have further developed an active learning model to optimize Cu-based catalysts and electrochemical conditions, largely reducing computational costs while maximizing acetate selectivity. Our predictions show that Cu/Pd (2:1) and Cu/Ag (3:1) alloys exhibit higher Faradaic efficiencies for CO reduction to acetate compared to pure Cu. The zero-gap electrochemical CORR experiments validated our multi-scale simulation framework for Cu-based catalysts and confirmed that AI-guided catalysts enhance acetate selectivity, demonstrating the practical applicability of our approach. The AI-guided simulation framework can also be extended to other catalytic reactions, such as CO2 electroreduction, as well as to other metal-based systems, including Pd-based catalysts for CORR. This work provides valuable data-driven approaches for catalyst design and electrochemical environment optimization, particularly offering a pathway toward improved acetate production for practical applications.

Results and discussion

CORR mechanism understanding at the atomic-scale

To elucidate the reaction mechanisms of CORR, a comprehensive reaction network covering the formation of both C1 and C2 products has been constructed, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Within this network, C-C coupling reactions are categorized into two possible pathways, based on insights from prior studies21,25–29.Fig. 2The proposed reaction mechanisms for CORR.The targeted products include ethylene, ethanol, acetate, and methane, along with hydrogen (H2) formation from the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER). The orange, purple, red, and green arrows indicate the most favorable reaction pathways for ethylene, ethanol, acetate, and methane formation, respectively, as determined by the subsequent microkinetic model and sensitivity analysis.

The proposed reaction mechanisms for CORR.

The targeted products include ethylene, ethanol, acetate, and methane, along with hydrogen (H2) formation from the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER). The orange, purple, red, and green arrows indicate the most favorable reaction pathways for ethylene, ethanol, acetate, and methane formation, respectively, as determined by the subsequent microkinetic model and sensitivity analysis.

One scenario involves C-C coupling via CO dimerization or the coupling of CO with COH or CHO. These pathways are highlighted in the upper color box in Fig. 2. Following this initial coupling, a series of protonation reactions lead to the formation of ethanol and ethylene. However, under this scenario, selective acetate formation requires preventing the complete protonation of one CO molecule to preserve the intermediate necessary for acetate production.

Another scenario involves C-C coupling via CO-CHx intermediates, highlighted in the lower color box in Fig. 2. Here, one CO is reduced to CHx species, including methylidyne (CH), methylene (CH2), and methyl (CH3). Subsequent C-C coupling determines the formation of CO-CH3 intermediates, which ultimately yield acetate. In this pathway, acetate formation competes with methane production, since both share common CHx intermediates. Ethanol and ethylene can also arise as competing products within this mechanism. The detailed reaction mechanism for each elementary step has been shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. For each elementary step, the energetic information, including reaction energies and activation barriers, were calculated using GC-DFT simulations.

To account for the influence of the applied potential on reaction energetics and activation barriers, the constant electrode potential (CEP) model via GC-DFT was employed21,30,31. In this approach, extra electrons were introduced into the system to simulate a fixed electrochemical potential, ensuring a realistic representation of potential-dependent energetics. The CEP model allows for a more accurate description of charge transfer and field effects at the electrode interface, making it particularly suitable for electrocatalytic reactions. Further details on the implementation of the CEP model are provided in the Supporting Information Section 1 Supplementary Figs. 2–5. The reaction energies and activation barriers for each elementary step have been listed in Supplementary Table 1. Implicit solvation was included in all calculations, and two explicit water molecules were added to capture hydrogen-bond interactions. To benchmark this approach, ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations with multiple water layers were performed, and the results (Supplementary Figs. 6–8) confirm that the two-water model provides sufficient accuracy for representing hydrogen-bonding effects. The GC-DFT and AIMD geometry files are available in Supplementary Data 1.

CORR mechanism understanding at the micro-scale under operating conditions

Building on the GC-DFT-calculated energetics, a key next step is to quantitatively assess how electrochemical operating conditions influence CORR performance (e.g., FE). To bridge this gap between atomic-scale energetics and experimentally measurable metrics, MKM at the micro-scale was employed to enable efficient, quantitative evaluation of reaction kinetics under realistic conditions.

Using GC-DFT calculation from the previous section, we determined potential-dependent Gibbs free energies, activation barriers, and vibrational partition functions to compute equilibrium and rate constants for each elementary step (Fig. 2) via transition state theory32,33. We then implemented an in-house Python-based solver to numerically solve ordinary differential equations (ODEs), modeling the system as a plug flow reactor, to develop an MKM framework for CORR over Cu(100) under varying experimental conditions. Further details on the MKM are provided in the SI Appendix Section 2. The MKM code is available in Supplementary Data 2.

Based on our established MKM, the FE of CORR and HER products over the Cu(100) surface, as a function of CO partial pressure, are depicted in Fig. 3a. In this model, CO concentration near the cathode has been assumed in equilibrium with the gaseous CO, following Henry’s law, while neglecting mass transfer resistances. This approximation effectively simulates the thermodynamic solubility equilibrium of CO in the MEA and/or zero-gap electrochemical systems34–36. Focused on HER profiles from model prediction in Fig. 3a, it can be indicated that increasing the partial pressure of CO under a fixed applied potential (−0.5 VRHE) suppresses HER, reducing its FE from ~25% at a lower CO pressure of 0.5 atm to around 8% at 1 atm. This suggests that higher CO pressure favors CORR over HER. Focusing on trends in C2 product formation from CORR, ethylene initially increases with rising CO partial pressure but declines after reaching a peak FE of ~54% when PCO exceeds ~0.6 atm. In contrast, oxygenate formation (ethanol and acetate) is promoted at higher CO pressures, as shown by the purple line in Fig. 3a. To validate the GC-DFT-based MKM prediction, CORR experiments in a three-compartment flow electrolyzer were conducted on commercial µm Cu as a function of CO pressures under an applied potential of −0.5 VRHE (Supplementary Fig. 9). Experiment results show that the trends of the FEs for HER and C2 products from CORR (Fig. 3b) align well with the MKM observations in Fig. 3a. At high CO pressure, the Faradaic efficiency of hydrogen stabilizes at ~10%. Additionally, experiments also show that ethylene formation is suppressed as CO pressure increases, while oxygenate formation is enhanced, reinforcing the competitive relationship between ethylene and oxygenates, consistent with MKM findings.

In addition to the influence of CO pressure, we further investigated the effect of applied potentials on product selectivity, ranging from −0.30 VRHE to −1.20 VRHE at a constant CO pressure of 1 atm. As shown in Fig. 3c, FE profiles reveal distinct volcano-type trends for C2 product formation. Ethylene and ethanol exhibit peak selectivity at relatively low potentials (~−0.60 VRHE and −0.40 VRHE, respectively), beyond which their FEs decline with increasingly negative potentials. In contrast, acetate production becomes dominant at more negative potentials, reaching its highest FE around -0.80 VRHE.

The trends observed in experimental performance tests for CORR over Cu (Fig. 3d) show strong agreement with our MKM predictions (Fig. 3c). The experimental data present the FEs of ethanol, ethylene, and acetate as functions of the applied potential over Cu electrode (Supplementary Fig. 10)37. Both experimental and MKM results indicate that ethylene and ethanol achieve their peak FEs at relatively lower potentials, with experimental values of −0.66 VRHE, while MKM predicts peak values at ~−0.5 VRHE for ethylene and ethanol. Similarly, acetate formation consistently appears at higher overpotentials, with its peak FE occurring at approximately −0.73 VRHE in experiments, closely matching the MKM-predicted peak at ~-0.8 VRHE. The strong correlation between experimental and theoretical trends further supports the proposed reaction mechanisms, reinforcing the conclusion that acetate formation follows a distinct C-C coupling pathway compared to ethylene and ethanol, leading to its optimal formation at more negative potentials.

To capture electrolyte effects, we also extended the MKM framework to explicitly include pH as a variable. The simulations predict that higher pH enhances acetate selectivity and shifts its optimal potential toward more negative values, while under each specific pH condition, acetate formation still follows a volcano-type trend with potential (Supplementary Fig. 11a), consistent with experimental observations(Supplementary Fig. 11b)37.

To illustrate the global trends of each C2 product, 3D plots depicting the evolution of ethylene, ethanol, and acetate as functions of the applied potential (0 to −1.2 VRHE) and CO pressure (0 to 1 atm) at pH of 13 are presented in Fig. 4, respectively. Ethylene reaches its highest FE of ~61% at an applied potential of approximately −0.42 VRHE and a CO pressure of 0.42 atm (Fig. 4a). As CO pressure increases beyond this point, ethylene formation declines while ethanol production is promoted. Ethanol, in competition with ethylene, benefits from higher CO partial pressure and achieves its peak FE of ~43% at ~−0.43 VRHE under the highest CO pressure, as shown in Fig. 4b. By doing so, we seek to uncover the key factors to control the acetate formation. Post-analysis of MKM surface coverages shows that CH* reaches surface coverage of 6.5 × 10-5 monolayer (ML) at -0.8 VRHE, whereas CHO*, COH*, CH2* and CH3* remain nearly five or six orders of magnitude lower. This large disparity demonstrates that CH* is the most promising intermediate present in sufficient abundance to feasibly couple with CO*, thereby establishing CO-CH coupling for CORR-to-acetate reaction (Supplementary Fig. 13). Notably, recent experimental studies provide indirect evidence for CO-CHx coupling where isotope-labeling and probe-assisted electroreduction experiments demonstrate that CO-CHx coupling can produce oxygenates, including acetate, under CORR conditions (see SI Section 2 for more details)43,44.

Methane, being a competitive product that shares the CHx pathway with acetate formation, was also analyzed through sensitivity analysis. Notably, the C-C coupling between CO and CH3 is an inhibitor for methane formation, whereas the subsequent reduction of CH3 to CH4 contributes positively to methane production, seen in Fig. 5c. Accordingly, the methane formation pathway is marked in green in the simplified reaction mechanism network in Fig. 5a.

To further distinguish the formation pathways of individual CORR products, sensitivity analyses were conducted for ethylene and ethanol. As shown in Fig. 5d, e, the CHO* formation step and the subsequent CO-CHO coupling reaction play a crucial role in promoting ethanol and ethylene production. Unlike acetate, which mainly follows the CO-CH coupling mechanism, ethylene and ethanol are formed through the CO-CHO coupling pathway, as highlighted in the purple pathway in Fig. 5a. This finding is strongly supported by previously published operando and in-situ studies, where ATR-SEIRAS measurements revealed clear CO-CHO peaks. This suggests that CO-CHO coupling plays a crucial role in ethylene and ethanol formation45.

This mechanistic difference provides insight into the volcano-like trends observed in Fig. 3b, where ethylene and ethanol reach their peak efficiencies at less negative potentials compared to acetate. The earlier peak for ethylene and ethanol is primarily attributed to the CO-CHO coupling step occurring at an earlier stage of CORR, particularly when CO is abundantly available on the catalytic surface. In contrast, acetate formation requires the prior generation of CH* species before undergoing C-C coupling with CO, which demands a more negative potential. This fundamental difference in C-C coupling pathways explains why acetate formation occurs at higher overpotentials compared to ethylene and ethanol.

Delving deeper into the competitive reaction pathways between ethylene and ethanol, ethanol formation likely proceeds via the reduction of CH2CHOH to CH2CH2OH, followed by further reduction to C2H5OH, as illustrated in Fig. 5d. Starting from CH2CH2OH on the surface, the pathway leading to C2H4 formation inhibits ethanol production, seen in Supplementary Fig. 14. However, this same reaction step promotes ethylene formation instead, as shown in Fig. 5d. Furthermore, the C2H5OH formation step is found to inhibit ethylene formation. These findings suggest that, once CH2CH2OH is formed on the surface, two possible branches emerge. In the ethanol pathway, CH2CH2OH undergoes proton–electron transfer step to form ethanol, as noted in the purple line in Fig. 5e. Alternatively, ethylene formation is favored through the loss of a water molecule from CH2CH2OH, as indicated by the orange line in Fig. 5e.

CH* Binding Strength as a Descriptor for Acetate Selectivity Control

According to sensitivity analysis results, CH* formation is a key factor that promotes acetate production, while little effect is observed for ethanol or ethylene formation. This highlights a key mechanistic role of CH* in acetate formation and suggests that CH* binding strength serves as an effective descriptor for tuning its selectivity. To further explore this hypothesis, we systematically varied the CH* binding strength either stronger or weaker than that on the pure Cu(100) surface and assessed its impact on product distribution using microkinetic modeling, as shown in Fig. 6. Notably, the binding energies of related radical species such as C*, CH2*, and CH3* exhibit linear correlations with CH* binding energy, consistent with previous reports46–48. In contrast, the FE for acetate exhibits a distinct volcano-type relationship with CH* binding strength, seen in Fig. 6b. A moderate enhancement in CH* binding, highlighted by the orange region, leads to a substantial increase in acetate selectivity. This pronounced and selective response positions CH* binding strength as a powerful and predictive descriptor, specifically for guiding catalyst design toward optimized acetate production.

Active learning-guided catalyst design to enhance acetate selectivity

Having identified CH* as a viable descriptor for tuning acetate selectivity without greatly influencing ethanol or ethylene formation, the next step is to design improved catalysts to overcome the limitations of pure Cu. Based on the promotion region highlighted in Fig. 6b (orange box), the ideal catalysts should exhibit moderately stronger CH* binding energies (∆ECH, with reference to the CH* binding over Cu) in the range of 0 to −0.45 eV, with an optimal value around -0.20 eV.

CH* relative binding energies (∆ECH) were calculated for several Cu-based bimetallic systems with low dopant ratios and plotted on the volcano trend in Fig. 6b. Systems with dopants that bind CH* weaker than pure Cu, such as Cu/Ga and Cu/Zn, reduce the FE of acetate formation. Conversely, systems with overly strong CH* binding, such as Cu/Co, Cu/Ru, and Cu/V, also hinder acetate formation. Only dopants that provide slightly stronger CH* binding, such as Co, La, Pt, Mo, Fe and W, effectively enhance acetate selectivity. These findings demonstrate the potential of CH*-binding optimization to guide the design of efficient catalysts for acetate production.

To design bimetallic systems within the promotion region for acetate formation, it is necessary to explore a wide range of transition metal dopants with varying ratios and test different adsorption sites for CH*. Achieving this goal requires extensive computational simulations and experimental efforts for synthesis and characterization, leading to a high workload. To address this challenge, active learning offers a promising solution for predicting CH* binding strength efficiently49,50. Active learning is a machine-learning framework that collaborates interactively with human users by identifying and prioritizing points for evaluation based on their maximum uncertainty. By focusing on areas of the data space with the highest uncertainty, each experimental or computational test provides useful information and helps speed up the exploration process. Unlike conventional supervised machine learning, which requires a large dataset collected prior to training, active learning can substantially reduce the amount of training data required, in some cases by several orders of magnitude, while still achieving accurate predictions51.

Figure 7a presents the overall framework for optimizing acetate selectivity in bimetallic Cu-based systems by integrating active learning with DFT simulations. The designed bimetallic alloy space explores various metal dopants in both surface and subsurface of the Cu(100) surface. A complete list of metals of interest is provided in Supplementary Table 2. The process begins with the selection of 23 different metals as dopants in the top layer of the Cu(100) surface, generating bimetallic systems with varying doping ratios. For each configuration, CH* adsorption structures are optimized, and the corresponding adsorption energies are calculated. Each entry in the database is characterized by 76 kinds of features, encompassing dopant species, dopant composition, lattice distortion, and adsorption site information. A full list of features is provided in Supplementary Table 3. To systematically describe CH* adsorption behavior, two categories of descriptive features are introduced: adsorption site characteristics (seen in Supplementary Fig. 15) and electronic properties. Initially, a dataset comprising 55 Cu/M (Cu-dopant metal) systems is generated to train the first iteration of the model (Model 1). The dataset is then divided into three subsets: 60% for training, 15% for testing, and 25% for validation, which are used to train the machine learning algorithm, evaluate the model performance, and tune the hyperparameters of the algorithm, respectively. For iterative model training, a random forest algorithm is selected as the initial testing algorithm due to its robustness and lower hyperparameter sensitivity compared to other machine learning methods52. The selection test of different algorithms is performed in next step.

In each iteration, the model predicts 15 CH* binding strengths for unexplored bimetallic systems based on the current training set. DFT calculations are subsequently performed on the predicted structures to validate model accuracy. If the discrepancy between model predictions and DFT results exceeds a predefined error threshold, 10 additional DFT data points are added to the training set and 2 to the testing set, maintaining the 80%:20% split ratio. The added data points are selected based on the following criteria: (1) those that exhibit poor predictive performance in the current model, exceeding a predefined error threshold, and (2) those containing dopant species or compositions not yet represented in the existing dataset. This iterative process continues until the model converges, ensuring accurate and reliable predictions of CH* binding energies across the compositional space.

Figure 7b illustrates the evolution of the model’s MAE across training, testing, and validation datasets over multiple iterations using the XGBoost algorithm53. By the third iteration, the training MAE decreases substantially from 0.05 eV to 0.03 eV, while the testing and validation MAEs drop from 0.15 eV to 0.12 eV and from 0.24 eV to 0.16 eV, respectively. Beyond this point, the reduction in MAE becomes more gradual as the expanding dataset enhances prediction accuracy. By the 10th iteration, the model stabilizes with MAEs of ~0.004 eV, 0.04 eV, and 0.10 eV for training, testing, and validation, respectively, demonstrating the framework’s effectiveness in refining CH* adsorption energy predictions. To further enhance predictive accuracy and robustness, various machine learning algorithms were evaluated for the final optimized model (Model 10) (Supplementary Figs. 16, 17). Among these, XGBoost exhibited the highest accuracy, consistently achieving lower MAEs and demonstrating competitive predictive performance. With Model 10 exhibiting strong predictive performance for CH* adsorption energies, the complete training, testing, and validation datasets, comprising CH* binding energies and all associated input features, are summarized in Supplementary Tables 4–6.

This active-learning framework facilitates efficient exploration of the design space by iteratively refining predictions of CH* binding energy, a key descriptor for acetate formation selectivity. The process involves continuous interaction between a machine-learning model and DFT calculations to systematically improve accuracy while minimizing computational cost. Using this optimized model, Cu/Pd (Cu:Pd=2:1) and Cu/Ag (Cu:Ag=3:1) were identified as promising catalytic candidates, exhibiting CH* binding energies within the promotional region, indicative of enhanced acetate formation selectivity compared to pure Cu. The thermodynamic stability of these two candidate surfaces was confirmed through DFT-calculated surface formation energies. The results indicate that both Cu/Pd and Cu/Ag surfaces are as stable as pure Cu, with details provided in Supplementary Fig. 18 of the Supplementary Information. The active-learning code is provided in Supplementary Data 2. These results offer a data-driven approach to catalyst design, facilitating the development of electrocatalytic systems with improved efficiency and selectivity for acetate production.

Experimental validation

To validate the active learning predictions, Cu/Ag (3:1) and Cu/Pd (2:1) catalysts, identified as promising candidates for enhanced acetate selectivity, were synthesized and characterized. Cu/Ag bimetallic catalyst was synthesized via galvanic displacement followed by H2 heat treatment. X-ray Diffraction (XRD) shows only metallic Cu and Ag phases without detectable oxides (Supplementary Fig. 19a). High-Resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (HRTEM) with Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) analysis reveals well-defined (200) lattice fringes in the bimetallic particles (Supplementary Fig. 20). Complementary quantification by Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS, bulk) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, surface) confirms the designed Cu/Ag composition (Supplementary Fig. 21, Supplementary Tables 7, 8). The surface Ag content is higher than the bulk, which agrees with Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS) mapping results (Supplementary Fig. 22).

Cu/Pd bimetallic catalyst was synthesized via a solvothermal method. XRD shows only reflections of face centered cubic (FCC) Cu/Pd alloy with pronounced peak broadening, indicating nanoscale crystallite size (Supplementary Fig. 19b). TEM further confirms the presence of small Cu/Pd nanoparticles uniformly distributed on the carbon support with an average diameter of 3.2 nm (Supplementary Fig. 23). HRTEM with FFT analysis reveals well-defined (200) lattice fringes in the Cu/Pd nanoparticles (Supplementary Fig. 24). Elemental mapping by Electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) verifies that Cu and Pd are homogeneously distributed throughout the carbon support (Supplementary Fig. 25). The Cu and Pd valence states were characterized by XPS, with representative spectra provided in Supplementary Fig. 26. In the Cu 2p region, the absence of shake-up satellites at 940-945 eV indicates negligible Cu2+. The Pd 3 d envelope shows metallic Pd0 with 3d5/2 and 3d3/2 at ~335.0 and ~340.3 eV, respectively, and no oxide-related features are detected. Quantification by ICP-MS (bulk) and XPS (surface) confirms the designed Cu/Pd composition (Supplementary Tables 7, 8).

Although Ag and Pd nanoparticles are widely studied for CO/CO2 reduction, we did not include them as standalone catalysts because their catalytic behaviors are intrinsically unfavorable for selective acetate formation: pure Ag binds CO too weakly to facilitate C-C coupling, while pure Pd binds CO so strongly that the surface becomes poisoned by CO*, leading both metals to primarily form C1 products with negligible acetate selectivity54–59. Instead, incorporating Ag or Pd into Cu creates bimetallic systems that retain the favorable C-C coupling properties of Cu while tuning CH* binding, thereby offering potential selectivity enhancement. Accordingly, the performances of Cu/Ag and Cu/Pd were directly compared with pure Cu to evaluate the predicted improvements.

The performance of Cu/Ag (3:1) and Cu/Pd (2:1) were evaluated in CO electrolysis under a CO partial pressure of 1 atm (seen in Fig. 8a and Fig. 8b) and compared with that of commercial 40-60 nm Cu (Fig. 8c). Both Cu/Ag and Cu/Pd exhibited much higher acetate Faradaic efficiency (50%) and (47%) compared to pure Cu (21%). In terms of carbon product distribution, the Cu/Ag and Cu/Pd bimetallic catalysts exhibited acetate selectivity of 72% and 78%, respectively, higher than the 42% observed on pure Cu. (Supplementary Figs. 27, 28). To further validate our model, we compared the potential-dependent formation trends of C2 products on active-learning-predicted Cu/Ag and Cu/Pd catalysts with experiments (Supplementary Fig. 29). Theoretical Faradaic efficiency predictions show good agreement with experimental zero-gap MEA electrolyzer measurements (Supplementary Fig. 30), revealing volcano-type trends for CORR-to-acetate with peak values similar to theory. These results confirm the predictive power of the multi-scale simulation and active learning framework, and more importantly, validate the CH* binding strength as a reliable descriptor for tuning acetate selectivity. The active learning accelerated multiscale simulation workflow can also be applied to Pd-based bimetallic catalysts. For Pd-based catalytic systems, GC-DFT energetics were computed and integrated into the MKM. The previously established design principle that CH* binding energy serves as a descriptor for acetate selectivity in CORR remains valid. Additional details are provided in SI Section 5 (Supplementary Figs. 31–33, and Supplementary Table 9). Moreover, the simulation framework can also be extended to other electrocatalytic reactions, such as CO2 reduction, as discussed in the SI Section 6 (Supplementary Fig. 34), highlighting its broad applicability and future potential.

In this study, a comprehensive reaction network for CORR was investigated using GC-DFT simulations, incorporating hybrid solvation and applied potential effects on energetics of intermediate-catalyst interactions at the atomic-scale. Via integrating GC-DFT at atomic-scale, MKM at macro-scale and zero-gap electrochemical experimental performance validation, we quantitatively described product formation from CORR under various operating conditions, including applied potentials and CO pressure.

Through sensitivity analysis of each CORR product, the most favorable reaction mechanism for acetate formation was elucidated, identifying the CO-CH coupling pathway as critical, with CH* serving as the key intermediate. Inspired by this mechanistic understanding, CH* was proposed as a descriptor to guide the tuning of acetate selectivity. Via active machine learning techniques, promising bimetallic systems, such as Cu/Pd and Cu/Ag, were identified and further validated by experiments with their superior acetate selectivity.

This closed research loop combining GC-DFT simulations, microkinetic modeling, active learning, and experimental studies, provides mechanistic insights into the complex reaction mechanisms underlying CORR. Furthermore, it offers an efficient strategy for predicting high-performance catalysts for acetate formation while minimizing computational and experimental efforts. This work is promising to contribute valuable references for advancing electrocatalysis applications.

Methods

DFT calculations

Grand-canonical density functional theory (GC-DFT) calculations were performed with Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) to study reaction energetics and activation barriers30,60,61. The revised Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (RPBE) functional within GGA was used, with adsorbates and the top two metal layers relaxed until forces were below 0.03 eV/Å and energies converged to 10-5 eV62,63. A 3 × 3 × 1 k-point grid and a 450 eV plane-wave cutoff were applied. Solvation was modeled using VASPsol with a dielectric constant of 78.421,30,64. We have also tested two explicit solvation models, one is with two water molecules for hydrogen bonding, and a Debye length of 3.0 Å (1.0 M electrolyte) and the other one is multiple-layer explicit water solvation model, with the water volume density of 0.83 g/cm3. The vacuum thickness was ~33 Å to ensure proper charge decay. Transition states were located using the CI-NEB method. Electrode potentials were defined using work function with the constant electrode potential (CEP) model31. More details are given in the SI Section 1.

Electrochemical measurements

Synthesis of Cu/Ag catalyst

Cu/Ag bimetallic catalyst was synthesized via a galvanic displacement method followed by hydrogen annealing. In a typical procedure, 500 mg of commercial Cu nanoparticles (25 nm, Sigma-Aldrich) were dispersed in a mixed solvent of 100 mL deionized (DI) water and 50 mL isopropanol (IPA). Separately, 375 mg of AgNO3 (99.9%, Fisher Scientific International) was dissolved in 20 mL DI water and then slowly added to the Cu dispersion. The mixture was stirred overnight to allow for the galvanic displacement reaction. The resulting product was collected by centrifugation, washed thoroughly with DI water and IPA, and dried under vacuum. Finally, the powder was subjected to a reductive treatment under 5% H2/N2 at 300 °C for 3 h.

Synthesis of Cu/Pd catalyst

Pd(acac)2 (15.2 mg) (99%, Sigma-Aldrich), Cu(acac)2 (26.6 mg) (99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich) and L-ascorbic acid (71.2 mg) (99%, Sigma-Aldrich) were dissolved in 15 mL oleylamine (Sigma-Aldrich) and sonicated for 1 h. XC-72R carbon (40 mg) (Fuel Cell Store) was then added, and the mixture was sonicated for 1 h. The suspension was transferred to a 23 mL stainless-steel autoclave and heated at 200 °C for 12 h. The product was collected by centrifugation, washed with acetone, and dried.

Electrode preparation

Catalyst inks were prepared by dispersing the synthesized Cu/Ag or Cu/Pd powders with Nafion ionomer (D520, Fuel Cell Store) in IPA at a 10:1 mass ratio. The mixtures were sonicated for 1 h and painted onto Sigracet 39 BB gas diffusion layers (Fuel Cell Store) with a catalyst loading of 1.0 mg cm-2. The same procedure was applied for preparing µm Cu ( ~ 625 mesh, 0.5–1.5 µm, 99%, Alfa Aesar), nm Cu (40–60 nm, Sigma-Aldrich), and Cu/Pd electrodes. NiFe anodes were synthesized following a previous method: 8.9 g Ni(NO3)3·6H2O (Sigma-Aldrich) and 1.2 g FeSO4·7H2O (Sigma-Aldrich) were separately dissolved in 150 mL IPA and 50 mL deionized water. The solutions were combined with stirring, and a pre-washed Ni foam (5 × 5 cm, 1.6 mm thick, MTI Corporation) was immersed at 25 °C for 24 h. Afterward, the foam was rinsed with water and IPA and dried at 60 °C.

CO electrolysis in a three-compartment flow electrolyzer

Pressure- and potential-dependent CO electrolysis was carried out at ambient temperature (25 °C) in a home-made three-compartment flow electrolyzer with an electrode area of 1 cm2 (Supplementary Fig. 35). Commercial µm Cu working electrode, Ag/AgCl reference electrode and Ir-black-coated Ti felt (Fuel Cell Store) counter electrode were used, with a Nafion 117 membrane (Fuel Cell Store) separating the cathodic and anodic electrolyte chambers. Fresh 1 M KOH was used as the electrolyte. The flow rates of the catholyte and anolyte were controlled by peristaltic pumps at 5 ml min−1. CO was fed at 25 mL min−1 via a mass-flow controller and diluted with Ar to achieve CO partial pressures of 0.2–0.8 bar while keeping the total gas flow at 25 mL min−1. The potential was controlled by an electrochemical workstation (PGSTAT 204 with 10.0 A booster). Prior to each performance test, solution resistance was measured by potentiostatic electrochemical impedance spectroscopy at open-circuit potential (10 mV amplitude, 0.1 Hz-100 kHz).
