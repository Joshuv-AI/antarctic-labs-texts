LibMOON: A Gradient-based MultiObjective OptimizatioN Library in PyTorch
Xiaoyuan Zhang, Liang Zhao, Yingying Yu, Xi Lin, Yifan Chen, Han Zhao, Qingfu Zhang

Abstract
Multiobjective optimization problems (MOPs) are prevalent in machine learning, with applications in multi-task learning, learning under fairness or robustness constraints, etc. Instead of reducing multiple objective functions into a scalar objective, MOPs aim to optimize for the so-called Pareto optimality or Pareto set learning, which involves optimizing more than one objective function simultaneously, over models with thousands / millions of parameters. Existing benchmark libraries for MOPs mainly focus on evolutionary algorithms, most of which are zeroth-order / meta-heuristic methods that do not effectively utilize higher-order information from objectives and cannot scale to large-scale models with thousands / millions of parameters. In light of the above gap, this paper introduces LibMOON , the first multiobjective optimization library that supports state-of-the-art gradient-based methods, provides a fair benchmark, and is open-sourced for the community.

Related works

2.1
Gradient-based multiobjective optimization

Gradient-based MOO has a long-standing research history. For example, the well-known convex optimization
[
20
]
[Chap 4] book outlines how linear scalarization can transform an MOO problem into a single-objective optimization (SOO) problem. However, for much of the past few decades, gradient-based methods have not been the primary approach for MOO, with MOEAs gaining more prominence due to their population-based approach, which is well-suited for approximating the PS and avoiding local optima. In recent years, however, gradient-based MOO has experienced a resurgence, particularly in (deep) machine learning, where these methods scale better with the number of decision variables. A pivotal contribution in this area is the MGDA-UB
[
12
]
, which introduced MOO techniques into deep learning by casting multi-task learning (MTL) as an MOO problem. Since then, many approaches have followed, including EPO
[
16

, Pareto Multi-Task Learning (PMTL)
[
13
]
, MOO with Stein Variational Gradient Descent (MOO-SVGD)
[
17
]
, and methods for learning the entire PS
[
19
,
18
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
. To make a fair comparison and for the ease of developing new methods, this paper implements them following a standardized manner.

3.2
MOO solvers

In this paper, we specially mean MOO solvers are solvers to find a set of Pareto solutions. The most simple and commonly used method is convert the MOO problem to a single objective optimization problem through some aggregation functions.

Aggregation-based methods.

A straightforward way is to use some aggregation functions
g_{\bm{\lambda}}():\mathbb{R}^{m}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
to convert a MOP to a single objective optimization problem. The reason that optimizing this converted single objective optimization problem will yield Pareto optimal solutions is due to the following two theorems.

Theorem 1

(Adapted from Theorem 2.6.2
[
36
]
)
.

If
g_{\bm{\lambda}}(\cdot)
is
strictly decreasing
w.r.t vector
{\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}})
, i.e.,
g_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}}^{(a)}))<g_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}}^{(b)}))
when
{\bm{L}}_{i}({\bm{\theta}}^{(a)})\preceq_{\mathrm{strict}}{\bm{L}}_{i}({\bm{\theta}}^{(b)})
, then the optimal solution
\theta^{*}
of
g_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}}))
serves as a
Pareto optimal
solution for the original MOP.

Proof.
See Mitten’s book
[
36
]
, Page 22. Similarly, for only decreasing aggregation functions, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

If
g_{\bm{\lambda}}()
is
decreasing
w.r.t. vector
{\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}})
(i.e.,
g_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}}^{(a)}))\leq g_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}}^{(b)}))
when
{\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}}^{(a)})\preceq_{\mathrm{strict}}{\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}}^{(b)})
, then the optimal solution
\theta^{*}
of
g_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}}))
serves as a
weakly Pareto optimal
solution for the original MOP.

Proof.
Similar to the proof of Mitten’s book
[
36
]
, Page 22.

In
Theorem

2
,
weakly Pareto optimality
means that for a solution
{\bm{\theta}}^{(a)}
, no other solutions
{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}
can strictly dominate it, i.e.,
{\bm{L}}_{i}({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime})<{\bm{L}}_{i}({\bm{\theta}}^{(a)})
for all
i\in[m]
.
Some common aggregation functions include the linear scalarization (LS) function, where
g_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\mathrm{LS}}({\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}}))=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}L_{i}({\bm{\theta}})
, the Tchebycheff function, where
g_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\mathrm{Tche}}({\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}}))=\max_{i\in[m]}\lambda_{i}\cdot(L_{i}({\bm{\theta}})-z_{i})
(
{\bm{z}}
is a reference point), Penalty-Based Intersection (PBI) function
[
29
]
, and COSMOS function
[
33
]
. For expressions and other aggregation functions, please refer to
Section

A.2
.

For a preference vector
{\bm{\lambda}}\succ\bm{0}
,
g_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\mathrm{LS}}(\cdot)
is a
strict decreasing function
meaning that directly optimizing
g_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\mathrm{LS}}(\cdot)
yields
Pareto optimal solutions
(by
Theorem

1
). However, for any
{\bm{\lambda}}\in{\bm{\Delta}}_{m}
, optimizing
g_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\mathrm{Tche}}(\cdot)
only yields weakly
Pareto optimal solutions
(by
Theorem

2
). For an improper setting of the weight factor
\mu
(see
Section

A.2
item 1 and 6), the optimal solution of
g_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\mathrm{PBI}}(\cdot)
or
g_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\mathrm{COSMOS}}(\cdot)
can be non-(weakly) Pareto optimal solutions of the original MOP.

An aggregation function is optimized by gradient descent in
LibMOON
via backpropagation, i.e,

{\bm{\theta}}_{k+1}={\bm{\theta}}_{k}-\eta{\bm{d}}_{k}={\bm{\theta}}-\eta\frac{\partial g_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}}))}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}}|_{{\bm{\theta}}_{k}},

where
{\bm{d}}_{k}
is called the updating direction at the
k
th iteration.

PSL Architecture.
Pareto models vary in structure. For synthetic problems, the simplest model is a fully-connected neural network that takes a preference as input and outputs the corresponding Pareto solution. In multitask learning, the input is a pair
({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
from dataset

{\bm{\theta}}
represents the target network’s parameter, with
\phi
as the hypernetwork’s parameter
[
46
]

{\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}})=\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\sim\mathcal{D}}(\ell(\bm{t}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{y}}))
, where
\ell
is a basic loss function like cross-entropy or mean square error. Structures of these two models are illustrated by
Figure

LibMOON

[

PSL Training.
Goal of PSL is to find a model with parameter
{\bm{\phi}}
optimizing the PSL loss
\ell_{\mathrm{psl}}

\min_{\bm{\phi}}\ell_{\mathrm{psl}}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\lambda}}\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\bm{p})}\tilde{g}_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}}_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{\lambda}}))),

where
\mathrm{Dir}(\bm{p})
is a Dirichlet distribution with hyperparameter
\bm{p}
.
\tilde{g}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\cdot)
can either be a generalized aggregation function as introduced in the previous section or a normal aggregation function. The gradient of
\ell_{\mathrm{psl}}
can be estimated by the chain rule:

\underbrace{\frac{\partial\ell_{\mathrm{psl}}}{\partial{\bm{\phi}}}}_{1\times D}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\lambda}}\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\bm{p})}\underbrace{\frac{\partial\tilde{g}_{\bm{\lambda}}}{\partial{\bm{L}}}}_{\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}:(1\times m)}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\partial{\bm{L}}}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}}}_{{\bm{B}}:(m\times n)}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\partial{\bm{\theta}}}{\partial{\bm{\phi}}}}_{{\bm{C}}:(n\times D)}.

(2)

Empirically, the expectation involved in
Equation

2
is estimated using a mini-batch of
K
preferences. The gradient vector
\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}=(\tilde{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\alpha}_{m})
is computed by MOO solvers as introduced in the previous section. The gradient matrix
{\bm{B}}
can be calculated either via backpropagation (when gradients are easily obtained) or using a zero-order method such as ES.
{\bm{C}}
can always be estimated through backward propagation, since
{\bm{\theta}}
is a continuous vector function of
{\bm{\phi}}
. Gradient calculations in existing PSL methods is summarized in
Table

4
. Parameter
{\bm{\phi}}
is iteratively updated by gradient descent:
{\bm{\phi}}\xleftarrow{}{\bm{\phi}}-\eta\frac{\partial\ell_{\mathrm{psl}}}{\partial{\bm{\phi}}}
.

3.4
Multiobjective Bayesian optimization solvers

When the evaluation of objective functions is costly, multiobjective Bayesian optimization (MOBO) is often the preferred methodology for tackling such challenges. While there are several existing libraries, such as BoTorch
[
47
]
and HEBO
[
48
]
, that facilities MOBO, they largely overlook algorithms that leverage decomposition techniques like PSL. To bridge this gap,
LibMOON
also includes three decomposition-based MOBO algorithms, including PSL-MOBO
[
23
]
, PSL-DirHVEI
[
23
,
49
]
, and DirHV-EGO
[
49
]
. In each iteration, these methods build Gaussian process (GP) models for each objectives and generate a batch of query points for true function evaluations.

PSL-MOBO is an extension of PSL method for expensive MOPs. It optimizes the preference-conditional acquisition function (AF)

PMTL, being a two-stage method, may fail when solutions fall outside the sector due to stochastic factors. MOO-SVGD optimizes both convergence and diversity but is generally unstable based on our tests.

Conclusion, limitations, and further works

Conclusion.

We introduced the
first
modern gradient-based MOO framework called
LibMOON
in PyTorch for the research community’s convenience.

LibMOON
supports more than 20 mainstream gradient-based MOO methods;
the modular design of
LibMOON
further allows the library to address various MOPs via various methods in a plug-and-play manner.

LibMOON
can thus be leveraged to quickly yet robustly test new MOO ideas.

Limitations

include: (1) rapid developments of gradient-based MOO methods makes it hard to incorporate all methods, so some effective methods may be missing; (2) gradient-based solvers may fail for problems with a number of local optimas.

Future Work

includes (1) maintaining a user and development community to address issues and (2) adding newly published methods as quickly as possible.

A.2
Aggregation functions

Aggregation function convert an MOP into a single-objective optimization problem under a specific preference vector
{\bm{\lambda}}
. Some popular aggregation functions are:

1.

COSMOS
:

g^{\mathrm{cosmos}}_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{\theta}})={\bm{\lambda}}^{\top}{\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}})-\mu\frac{{\bm{\lambda}}^{\top}{\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}})}{\|{\bm{\lambda}}\|\|{\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}})\|},

(3)

where
\mu
is a positive weight factor to align the objective vector
{\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}})
with the preference vector
{\bm{\lambda}}
.

2.

Linear scalarization (LS)
:

g^{\mathrm{LS}}_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{\theta}})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}L_{i}({\bm{\theta}}).

(4)

3.

Tchebycheff (Tche)
:

g^{\mathrm{Tche}}_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{\theta}})=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\left\{\lambda_{i}(L_{i}({\bm{\theta}})-z_{i})\right\},

(5)

where
{\bm{z}}
is a reference point, usually set as the nadir point the minimal value in each objectives.

4.

Modified Tchebycheff (mTche)
:

g^{\mathrm{mTche}}_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{\theta}})=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\left\{\frac{L_{i}({\bm{\theta}})-z_{i}}{\lambda_{i}}\right\},

(6)

where
{\bm{z}}
is a reference point, the same as the point used in the Tchebycheff scalarization function.

5.

Smooth Tchebycheff (STche)
:

g^{\mathrm{STche}}_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{\theta}})=\frac{1}{h}\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\exp(h\cdot\lambda_{i}(L_{i}({\bm{\theta}})-z_{i}))\right).

(7)

The Smooth Tchebycheff function uses a relaxed Smooth
\max
operator. The advantage of this approach is that
g^{\mathrm{STche}}_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{\theta}})
becomes a Smooth function if each objective function
L_{i}({\bm{\theta}})
is Smooth, unlike the non-Smooth
g^{\mathrm{Tche}}_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{\theta}})
. Smooth functions generally have faster convergence compared to non-Smooth ones. Similarly, we can define the Smooth modified Tchebycheff function.

6.

Penalty-Based Intersection (PBI):

g^{\mathrm{PBI}}_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{\theta}})=\underbrace{\frac{1}{\left\lVert{\bm{\lambda}}\right\rVert}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}L_{i}({\bm{\theta}})}_{d_{1}}+\mu\underbrace{\left\lVert{\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}})-\frac{d_{1}}{\left\lVert{\bm{\lambda}}\right\rVert}\cdot{\bm{\lambda}}\right\rVert}_{d_{2}},

(8)

where
\mu
is a positive weight factor that encourage a objective to align with a preference vector
{\bm{\lambda}}
.

7.

p
-norm
:

g^{\mathrm{pnorm}}_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{\theta}})={\left\lVert{\bm{\lambda}}\odot{\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}})-{\bm{z}}\right\rVert_{p}}.

(9)

The symbol
\odot
denotes the element-wise product between two vectors.

8.

Augmented Achievement Scalarization Function (AASF)
:

g^{\mathrm{AASF}}_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{\theta}})=g^{\mathrm{mTche}}_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{\theta}})+\rho g^{\mathrm{LS}}_{\bm{\lambda}}({\bm{\theta}}),

(10)

where
\rho
is small positive coefficient, which is usually set as 0.1. Contour curves for this functions for a bi-objective case can be found in the
LibMOON Doc

5
5

5

https://libmoondocs.readthedocs.io/en/latest/gallery/aggfuns.html
.

A.3
Metrics

Metrics used in
LibMOON
can be categorized into two groups. The first group evaluates the quality of a
set
of solutions
{\mathbb{Y}}=\{{\bm{y}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bm{y}}^{(N)}\}
, with specific metrics such as IGD and FD relying on the known Pareto front for accuracy.
The second group of metrics assesses the quality of
individual
solutions
{\bm{y}}
when a preference vector
{\bm{\lambda}}
is provided.

Group 1: Metrics for a set of solutions.

1.

Hypervolume (HV) (
\uparrow
)
[
53
]
: This metric evaluates both the convergence to the Pareto Front (PF) and the diversity of solutions. A low HV value indicates poor convergence, while high HV values imply better performance. The hypervolume is calculated as the volume dominated by at least one solution in the set
{\mathbb{S}}
with respect to a reference point
{\bm{r}}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{HV}_{\bm{r}}({\mathbb{S}})=\mathrm{Vol}({{\bm{y}}\;|\;\exists{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{S}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\preceq{\bm{y}}\preceq{\bm{r}}}).

2.

Inverted Generational Distance (IGD)
[
54
]
: IGD measures the average distance between points in a reference set
{\mathbb{Z}}
and the nearest solutions in the set
{\mathbb{S}}
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{IGD}({\mathbb{S}})=\frac{1}{|{\mathbb{Z}}|}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{|{\mathbb{Z}}|}\min_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{S}}}\rho({\bm{y}}^{(i)},{\bm{y}}^{\prime})^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

3.

Fill Distance (FD)
[
55
]
: This metric calculates the covering radius of a set of solutions
{\mathbb{S}}
, defined as the maximum minimum distance from any point in the reference set
{\mathbb{Z}}
to the nearest solution in
{\mathbb{Z}}
:

\mathrm{FD}({\mathbb{S}})=\max_{{\bm{y}}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\min_{{\bm{y}}\in{\mathbb{S}}}\rho({\bm{y}},{\bm{y}}^{\prime}).

(11)

4.

Minimal Distance (
\mathrm{l}_{\min}
): This metric captures the smallest pairwise distance among all objectives:

\displaystyle\mathrm{l}_{\min}=\min_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\rho({\bm{y}}^{(i)},{\bm{y}}^{(j)})

where
\rho()
denotes the Euclidean distance.

5.

Smooth Minimal Distance (
\mathrm{sl}_{\min}
): This metric is a “smooth-min" version of the minimal distance function, defined as:

\mathrm{sl}_{\min}=-\frac{1}{h\cdot k(k-1)}\log\left(\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\exp\left(-h\rho\left({\bm{y}}^{(i)},{\bm{y}}^{(j)}\right)\right)\right).

(12)

6.

Spacing: This metric measures the standard deviation of the minimal distances from one solution to others, with lower values indicating a more uniform distribution of objective vectors:

\mathrm{spacing}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(d_{i}-\bar{d})^{2},\qquad\bar{d}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{i},\qquad d_{i}=\min_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\rho({\bm{y}}^{(i)},{\bm{y}}^{(j)}).

(13)

7.

Span: This metric evaluates the range (span) of solutions in their minimal dimension, defined as:

\mathrm{Span}=\min_{1\leq i\leq m}\max_{1\leq k<l\leq N}|y_{i}^{(k)}-y_{i}^{(l)}|.

(14)

Group 2: Metrics for individual solutions.

1.

Penalty-based Intersection (PBI): This metric is a weighted sum of two distance functions
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
, given by
\mathrm{PBI}=d_{1}+\mu d_{2}
, where

d_{1}=\frac{\langle{\bm{y}}-{\bm{z}},{\bm{\lambda}}\rangle}{\left\lVert{\bm{\lambda}}\right\rVert},\qquad d_{2}=\left\lVert{\bm{y}}-(d_{1}{\bm{\lambda}}+{\bm{z}})\right\rVert.

(15)

2.

Inner Product (IP): This metric measures the alignment of the objective vector
{\bm{y}}
with the preference vector
{\bm{\lambda}}
:

\mathrm{IP}=\langle{\bm{y}},{\bm{\lambda}}\rangle.

(16)

3.

Cross Angle (
\vartheta
): For bi-objective problems, this metric measures the angular difference between the objective vector and the preference vector:

\vartheta=\left\lVert\arctan(y_{2}/y_{1})-\arctan(\lambda_{2}/\lambda_{1})\right\rVert.

(17)

Those metrics are summarized in
Table

12
and also can be found in the
LibMOON
document
.
