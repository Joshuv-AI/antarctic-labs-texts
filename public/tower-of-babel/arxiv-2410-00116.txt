Bayesian Calibration for prediction in a multi-output transposition context
Josselin Garnier, Baptiste Kerleguer, Cédric Durantin, Gilles Defaux, Guillaume Perrin

Abstract
Numerical simulations are widely used to predict the behavior of physical systems, with Bayesian approaches being particularly well suited for this purpose. However, experimental observations are necessary to calibrate certain simulator parameters for the prediction. In this work, we use a multi-output simulator to predict all its outputs, including those that have never been experimentally observed. This situation is referred to as the transposition context. To accurately quantify the discrepancy between model outputs and real data in this context, conventional methods cannot be applied, and the Bayesian calibration must be augmented by incorporating a joint model error across all outputs. To achieve this, the proposed method is to consider additional input parameters within a hierarchical Bayesian model, which includes hyperparameters for the prior distribution of the calibration variables. This approach is applied to a computer code with three outputs that models the Taylor cylinder impact test with a small number of observations. The outputs are considered as the observed variables one at a time, to work with three different transposition situations. The proposed method is compared with other approaches that embed model errors to demonstrate the significance of the hierarchical formulation.

Introduction

Numerical simulations are increasingly used to understand and optimize complex physical systems, as they enable the prediction of behavior in previously unobserved scenarios.
For accurate predictions, the input parameters of these simulations must be carefully specified.
Some of the simulation inputs, known as control variables, replicate experimental conditions chosen by the experimenter and are denoted by
\mathbf{x}
, while others are unknown parameters specific to the computer code, denoted by
\bm{\lambda}
.
These parameters typically have a physical significance and must be calibrated to ensure the code predictions closely match the actual system behavior, observed from limited experimental data.
The parameters
\bm{\lambda}
can also be numerical parameters that are needed to run the code. The simplest approach involves performing an optimization scheme on
\bm{\lambda}
to minimize a given distance between observed data and model outputs, see
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, then using the optimal values as if they were known. However, this method assumes no uncertainty in the observed data (no measurement error) and in the code prediction (no model error), which is unrealistic in practice.

A Bayesian methodology that considers noisy observations and treats the unknown parameters as a random vector
\mathbf{\Lambda}
can be adopted. The prior distribution of
\mathbf{\Lambda}
and the observations are used to build the posterior distribution of
\mathbf{\Lambda}
, which captures the uncertainty and allows for a predictive distribution of the output.
This framework is thoroughly presented in
[
4
]
and further studied in numerous works, for example
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
.

Nevertheless, predicting the behavior of physical systems using numerical models is never perfect, even with well-identified parameter values. There is always a discrepancy between the model outputs and the observed data, which must be accounted for as a model error, as highlighted in various works, see
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
.
Typically, as detailed in
[
4
]
, the model error term can be explicitly represented as an additive Gaussian process, whose distribution hyperparameters are estimated from the observed data. This approach is particularly useful but cannot be implemented for prediction in some situations due to the impossible estimation of the hyperparameters. These situations arise when the output of interest does not directly correspond to the experimental data, such as when calibration must be performed based on different experiments not directly suited to the objective.
The framework introduced in this paper will be called transposition. Four cases can be identified:

1.

Change of scale: The calibration domain is different from the domain of interest of the code.

2.

New inputs: The code is calibrated with fewer inputs than it will use.

3.

New code: The calibration parameters will be used in another code.

4.

Unobserved outputs: The calibration and prediction will be carried out by observing some outputs, but not all of them.

In this paper, only the case of predicting unobserved outputs will be considered, although the presented method is general enough to be adapted to many transposition scenarios.

A collection of
T
different output variables of interest is identified and denoted by
\bm{y}
. It depends on control variables
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, with
\mathcal{X}
a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{s},s\in\mathbb{N}^{\star},
such that:

\bm{y}(\mathbf{x})=(y_{t}(\mathbf{x}))_{t=1}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}.

Let
\bm{f}
be a numerical code to compute this
T
-dimensional output. Its input variables are
\mathbf{x}
and
p
unknown physical calibration parameters
\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{i})_{i=1}^{p}\in\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and

\bm{f}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\lambda})=(f_{t}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\lambda}))^{T}_{t=1}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}.

The experimental phase is assumed to provide
n
observations but only for the variable
t^{\prime}
, where
1\leq t^{\prime}\leq T.
They form the vector
\bm{y}_{\bm{t^{\prime}},\textbf{obs}}=(y_{t^{\prime},\text{obs},j})_{j=1}^{n}
which we consider as realizations of noisy measurements of
y_{t^{\prime}}
at design points
\mathbb{X}=(\mathbf{x}_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
with output measurement error
\bm{E}=(E_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
:

\bm{Y}_{\bm{t^{\prime}},\textbf{obs}}=(Y_{t^{\prime},\text{obs},j})_{j=1}^{n}=(y_{t^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x}_{j})+E_{j})_{j=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(1)

The probability density function (pdf) of the measurement error is known and is denoted by
p_{\bm{E}}
.
The transposition comes from the fact that only the variable
y_{t^{\prime}}
is measured in the experimental phase, while the objective is to predict
y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
for
1\leq t\leq T
, and
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathbb{X}
. In the following, and without loss of generality, we assume that the variable
y_{1}
is measured (i.e.
t^{\prime}=1
) considering the random vector
\bm{Y_{1,\text{obs}}}=(Y_{1,\text{obs},j})_{j=1}^{n}=(y_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{j})+E_{j})_{j=1}^{n}.
Note that in the proposed framework, the number of experiments
n
is assumed to be small (e.g.,
n=10
in the application presented in Section
3
). This limited sample size makes prediction at
\mathbf{x}_{0}
more challenging. However, this constraint is often unavoidable due to the financial, material, or time-related costs associated with each physical experiment.

The common additive discrepancy to represent the model error would be introduced as follows, by modeling the output variables of interest as

\bm{Y}(\mathbf{x})=(Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}))_{t=1}^{T}=\bm{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\Lambda})+\bm{\Delta}(\mathbf{x})
, with
\mathbf{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
a random vector of pdf
p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}
and
\bm{\Delta}
a
T
-variate Gaussian process with prior information depending on some hyperparameters
\phi
. In our transposition context, some of these hyperparameters are impossible to estimate. Indeed, let us consider for instance
\bm{\Delta}=(\Delta_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
pairwise independent Gaussian processes with respective mean
\mu_{t}(.)
and covariance
c_{t}(.,.)
that depends on hyperparameters
\phi_{t}
. It quickly appears that for every
t
, experimental measurments of
y_{t}
are needed to estimate
\phi_{t}
, but they are not available in this context.

Therefore, the objective is to propose a model error for the prediction of
y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
with hyperparameters that can be estimated from the observations
\bm{Y_{1,\text{obs}}}
. As presented in
[
12
]
, embedded errors can be employed to build a mathematical relationship between the observations and the variables of interest. A similar approach is described in
[
13
]
, where an embedding
Y_{t}(\mathbf{x})=f_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\Lambda}+\bm{\Delta})
is considered with
\bm{\Delta}
being a random discrepancy whose parameters require calibration, as detailed in
C
. In our work, an original embedding is proposed by increasing the variability in the calibration variables
\mathbf{\Lambda}
through the inclusion of
q-p
additional parameters to represent the model error, and then work with a
q
-dimensional vector
\mathbf{\Lambda}
. These
q-p
parameters encompass all parameters that influence the relationship between the controlled inputs and all the
T
outputs but are not classically intended to be calibrated. These could include, for example, mesh parameters, variables related to the simulation duration, or known physical law parameters.
As this study is conducted in the context of a very limited number of observations, prior assumptions play a crucial role. This is why a hierarchical approach is proposed for these additional parameters, allowing for the estimation of the hyperparameters of their prior distribution
[
14
]
, thereby refining it to better account for model error.

The main contribution of this work lies in this embedded model error for prediction in transposition contexts. A key challenge is the treatment of hyperparameters within the hierarchical framework.
Note that this method cannot be effective if the variations in the output due to the fluctuations of the additional parameters are not sufficiently large to capture the model error. This point will be further discussed in
Section

3
and
B
.

The article is organized as follows:
Section

2
outlines the Bayesian methodology tailored to the transposition context and introduces the surrogate model used.
Section

3
describes the application test case and the performance metrics examined, followed by numerical results with a comparison of various approaches, including the method from
[
13
]
. Finally,
Section

4
summarizes the work and proposes possible extensions.

2
Building an adapted Bayesian framework

2.1
Bayesian calibration

In the Bayesian framework, the idea is to obtain the posterior distribution (i.e. conditioned by the experimental data) of parameters, that balances prior knowledge with observed data, see
[
15
]
. More precisely, we consider

\left\{\begin{aligned} Y_{t}(\mathbf{x})&=f_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\Lambda}),\penalty\ \>\penalty\ \>\penalty\ \>\penalty\ \>\penalty\ \>\>\>1\leq t\leq T,\penalty\ \mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}\\
Y_{1,\text{obs},j}&=Y_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{j})+E_{j},\penalty\ \>\penalty\ \>\>1\leq j\leq n,\end{aligned}\right.

(2)

with
\mathbf{\Lambda}
of prior pdf
p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}
,
\bm{E}
of known pdf
p_{\bm{E}}
, and a small
n.

As explained in
Section

1
, here we consider
\mathbf{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
, that is made of the
p
unknown physical parameters and the
q-p
additional parameters included to emulate the model error. Obviously, the following formulas are valid when considering the classical situation
\mathbf{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

The goal is to predict
y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
, which requires investigating the posterior distribution of
Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
, i.e., the distribution of
Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\mid\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}
. This involves estimating
\mathbb{E}(\psi(Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))\mid\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}})
for all functions
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathbb{E}(\psi(Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))\mid\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}})
is well-defined. In particular, the estimation of
\mathbb{E}(Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\mid\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}})
and
\mathbb{E}(Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0})^{2}\mid\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}})
are required to determine the predictive mean and standard deviation. In situations where extreme events are studied, functions of the form
\Psi(y):y\to\mathbf{1}_{[b,+\infty[}
, with
b\in\mathbb{R}
, are considered.

The expectation given the observations can be expressed:

\mathbb{E}(\psi(Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))\mid\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}\psi(f_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\bm{\lambda}))p(\bm{\lambda}\mid\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}})d\bm{\lambda},

(3)

with
p(\bm{\lambda}\mid\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}})
the pdf of the posterior distribution of
\mathbf{\Lambda}
.
Bayes’ theorem, see
[
16
]
, gives
p(\bm{\lambda}\mid\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}})=\frac{p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\lambda})p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\lambda})}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})d\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}}
, where
p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\lambda})=p_{\bm{E}}\left(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}-(f_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\lambda}))_{j=1}^{n}\right)
is the likelihood of
\bm{\lambda}
. A consistent estimator of
\mathbb{E}(\psi(Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\mid\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}})
can be

\hat{E}_{M}\Big(\psi(Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))\Big)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\psi(f_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{\Lambda}_{k})),

(4)

where
(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{k})_{k=1}^{M}
is sampled with pdf

p(\bm{\lambda}\mid\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}})\propto p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\lambda})p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\lambda})
. However, the normalizing term
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})d\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}
is known to be intractable in practice, making it impossible to sample directly from
p(\bm{\lambda}\mid\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}})
with classical Monte Carlo schemes. Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods are then required here to work with posterior samples
(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{k})_{k=1}^{M}
with pdf proportional to
p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\cdot)p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}.
In our study, these MCMC samples are obtained with the Delayed Rejection Adaptive Metropolis (DRAM) algorithm detailed in
[
17
]
, but other algorithms such as Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC,
[
18
]
) could be used, in particular if
q
is large.

Note that here
p_{\bm{E}}
will be considered known in all the methods we implement, to ensure a fair comparison between them and focus on the model error. However, in some situations, inferring the parameters of
p_{\bm{E}}
is crucial. For instance, if
\bm{E}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}I)
with unknown
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
, then a solution is to consider
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
as a hyperparameter to be inferred together with
\bm{\lambda}
, and then work with the augmented set
\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}=(\bm{\lambda},\sigma_{\varepsilon})
of likelihood
p(\tilde{\bm{\lambda}}\mid\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}})=p_{\bm{E}(\sigma_{\varepsilon})}\left(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}-(f_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\lambda}))_{j=1}^{n}\right)
with
p_{\bm{E}(\sigma_{\varepsilon})}
the density of
\bm{E}(\sigma_{\varepsilon})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}I).

2.2
Hierarchical model

In the following,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{cal}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the domain of physical parameters to calibrate, and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{err}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{q-p}
is the domain of the additional parameters considered to emulate the model error.
Then
\bm{\lambda}\in\mathcal{L}
is introduced with
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{cal}}\times\mathcal{L}_{\text{err}}
.
In our study, the
q-p
parameters in
\mathcal{L}_{\text{err}}
are integrated with a hierarchical description, see
[
19
]
and
[
20
]
, to better target their correct distribution and account for the model error. This is particularly effective even in contexts where measurement noise is significant. The hierarchical description assumes that the a priori distribution of the additional parameters depends on hyperparameters
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{R}^{r}
.
The pdf of
\mathbf{\Lambda}
given
\bm{\alpha}
is denoted
p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(.\mid\bm{\alpha}),
and each
\bm{\alpha}
drives these parameters for the model error. In a hierarchical description, the objective is to perform Bayesian inference on the hyperparameters
\bm{\alpha}
as well, and then consider them as a random variable
\mathbf{A}\in\mathcal{A}
with prior distribution
p_{\mathbf{A}}
. Note that, to avoid possible confusion,
\mathbf{A}
is simply the upper case notation of
\bm{\alpha}
here, as lower-case letters represent deterministic variables, while the upper-case letters are associated with random variables throughout this article. The same convention applies to
\bm{\lambda}
and
\mathbf{\Lambda}
for instance.

As for
Equation

3
, Bayes’ Theorem provides that
p(\bm{\alpha}\mid\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}})
, the posterior pdf of
\mathbf{A}
evaluated at
\bm{\alpha}
, is proportional to
p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\alpha})p_{\mathbf{A}}(\bm{\alpha})
, where
p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\alpha})
is the likelihood of
\bm{\alpha}
. This likelihood can be expressed as

\displaystyle p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\lambda},\bm{\alpha})p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\lambda}\mid\bm{\alpha})d\bm{\lambda}

(5)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\lambda})p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\lambda}\mid\bm{\alpha})d\bm{\lambda}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ as given }\bm{\lambda},\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\text{ does not depend on }\alpha

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\mathbf{\Lambda})\mid\bm{\alpha}\right]

which is the expectation of
p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\mathbf{\Lambda})
when
\mathbf{\Lambda}
has pdf
p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(.\mid\bm{\alpha}).

Thus, the most natural way to estimate
p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\alpha})
for a given
\bm{\alpha}
is to compute the Monte Carlo estimator

\hat{P}_{L}(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\alpha})=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k=1}^{L}p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\prime}_{k})

(6)

with
(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\prime}_{k})_{k=1}^{L}
i.i.d. with pdf
p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(.\mid\bm{\alpha}).

With this approach, it would lead to
L\times n
runs of the computer code for the estimation of the likelihood of a single
\bm{\alpha}
, which is not feasible in practice.
To save significant computation time, the idea here is to use an Importance Sampling formulation, presented in
[
21
]
, based on the following result:

\displaystyle\forall(\bm{\alpha},\bm{\alpha}^{\star})\text{ such that }\text{supp}(p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(.\mid\bm{\alpha}))\subset\text{supp}(p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(.\mid\bm{\alpha}^{\star})),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\mathbf{\Lambda})\mid\bm{\alpha}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\mathbf{\Lambda})\frac{p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\mathbf{\Lambda}\mid\bm{\alpha})}{p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\mathbf{\Lambda}\mid\bm{\alpha}^{\star})}\mid\bm{\alpha}^{\star}\right]

Then, for all
\bm{\alpha}
, we can use the following Importance Sampling estimator of
p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\alpha})
, based on sampling associated with a vector of hyperparameters
\bm{\alpha}^{\star}
:

\hat{P}^{\bm{\alpha}^{\star}}_{L}(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\alpha})=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k=1}^{L}p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\prime}_{k})\frac{p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\prime}_{k}\mid\bm{\alpha})}{p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\prime}_{k}\mid\bm{\alpha}^{\star})},

(7)

with
(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\prime}_{k})_{k=1}^{L}
i.i.d. with pdf
p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(.\mid\bm{\alpha}^{\star}).
This estimator is consistent and asymptotically normal, as detailed in
Section

2.3
.
Here, the same samples are used for all
\bm{\alpha}
. Then, with only
L\times n
runs of the computer code we can estimate the likelihood
p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\alpha})
for all
\bm{\alpha}
, rather than just for a single
\bm{\alpha}
with the Monte Carlo estimation. This allows us to conduct a plug-in strategy by maximizing the posterior density of
\mathbf{A}
and working with the maximum a posteriori
\bm{\alpha}_{\text{MAP}}=\underset{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}}{\text{argmax}}\>p(\bm{\alpha}\mid\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}})
. Another strategy is to adopt a full-Bayesian approach and consider the entire posterior distribution of
\mathbf{A}
with MCMC methods.

2.3
Investigating
\bm{\alpha}_{\text{MAP}}

This section details the estimation of
\bm{\alpha}_{\text{MAP}}
which can then be used as a plug-in value for the hyperparameters to compute
\hat{E}_{M}\Big(\psi(Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))\Big)
with the prior distribution
p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(.\mid\bm{\alpha}_{\text{MAP}})
for
\mathbf{\Lambda}.
The idea is to consider
\hat{P}^{\bm{\alpha}^{\star}}_{L}(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\alpha})
as the estimator of the likelihood
p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\alpha})
of
\bm{\alpha},
for a relevant
\bm{\alpha}^{\star}
as detailed in the following.

2.3.1
Estimation of
\bm{\alpha}_{\text{MAP}}
.

Theoretically, the maximization of the posterior density on
\mathcal{A}
can be performed by selecting a given
\bm{\alpha}^{\star}
and evaluating
\hat{P}^{\bm{\alpha}^{\star}}_{L}(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\alpha})p_{\mathbf{A}}(\bm{\alpha})
for every
\bm{\alpha}
. However, the ratios
\frac{p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\prime}_{k}\mid\bm{\alpha})}{p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\prime}_{k}\mid\bm{\alpha}^{\star})}
can be highly fluctuating when
\bm{\alpha}
is far from
\bm{\alpha}^{\star}
, leading to estimators with a high variance. To overcome this issue, the proposed solution is work with
\bm{\alpha}^{\star}
close to
\bm{\alpha}_{\text{MAP}}
, using an iterative strategy in
\bm{\alpha}^{\star}
until reaching a fixed point, as detailed in
Algorithm

1
.
The inputs of the algorithm are
\mathbf{A}^{\star}_{0}
, the initial value of
\bm{\alpha}^{\star}
and
\tau
, the stop threshold. The value of
\tau
depends on the interpretation of
\bm{\alpha}
, and will be briefly discussed in Section
3.4
.
Note that in this work, the optimization
\mathbf{A}^{\star}_{\ell+1}=\underset{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}}{\text{argmax}}\>\hat{P}^{\mathbf{A}^{\star}_{\ell}}_{L}(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\alpha})p_{\mathbf{A}}(\bm{\alpha})
is performed with L-BFGS-B optimizer
[
22
]
. In the following, we will work with the optimal vector
\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell},
which is the approximation of
\bm{\alpha}_{\text{MAP}}
obtained at the end of the algorithm.

Algorithm 1

Iterative estimation of
\bm{\alpha}_{\text{MAP}}

Input:

\mathbf{A}^{\star}_{0}
,
\tau

\ell\leftarrow 0
,
\nu\leftarrow+\infty

while

\nu>\tau

do

Sample
(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\prime}_{k})_{k=1}^{L}
i.i.d. with pdf
p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(.\mid\mathbf{A}^{\star}_{\ell})

\mathbf{A}^{\star}_{\ell+1}\leftarrow\underset{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}}{\text{argmax}}\>\hat{P}^{\mathbf{A}^{\star}_{\ell}}_{L}(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\alpha})p_{\mathbf{A}}(\bm{\alpha})

\nu\leftarrow\lVert\mathbf{A}^{\star}_{\ell}-\mathbf{A}^{\star}_{\ell+1}\lVert

end while

2.3.2
Confidence in the estimation.

Although this is not required in the proposed method, it is possible to check that
p(\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell}\mid\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}})
, the posterior pdf at
\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell},
is close to the maximum a posteriori density across all
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}
.
As detailed in
A
, with a statistical test, we propose a confidence level
\gamma(\bm{\alpha})
associated with
p(\bm{\alpha}\mid\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}})<\beta p(\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell}\mid\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}})
for a given
\bm{\alpha}
, where
\beta
represents an acceptable margin of error for this estimation, set by the user. For example, in the application,
\beta=1.05
is used, see
Section

3.5
, corresponding to a tolerance of
5\%
on the posterior density. This confidence level will be computed for
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}
, as illustrated in
Figure

8(b)
. A threshold
\zeta
can be introduced, to ensure that
\forall\bm{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A},\gamma(\bm{\alpha})\geq\zeta,
with for instance
\zeta=0.95.
To estimate these confidence levels and similarly to the approach detailed in
Section

2.4
, we use
\hat{P}^{\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell}}_{L}(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\alpha})
as the estimator of
p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\alpha}).
More precisely, for this specific study, a number
L^{\prime}
of samples is considered, with
L^{\prime}
very large, to work with
\hat{P}^{\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell}}_{L^{\prime}}(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\alpha})
and investigate the convergence, when
L^{\prime}
goes to
+\infty
, of

\displaystyle\hat{P}^{\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell}}_{L^{\prime}}(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\alpha})p_{\mathbf{A}}(\bm{\alpha})

\displaystyle-\beta\hat{P}^{\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell}}_{L^{\prime}}(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell})p_{\mathbf{A}}(\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell})

(8)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L^{\prime}}\sum_{k=1}^{L^{\prime}}\left(p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\prime}_{k})\frac{p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\prime}_{k}\mid\bm{\alpha})p_{\mathbf{A}}(\bm{\alpha})-\beta p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\prime}_{k}\mid\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell})p_{\mathbf{A}}(\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell})}{p_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\prime}_{k}\mid\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell})}\right),

with
(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\prime}_{k})_{k=1}^{L^{\prime}}
i.i.d. with pdf

\displaystyle S_{L^{\prime}}(\bm{\alpha})^{2}=

2.5
Surrogate modeling

In all of the above, we considered that we can perform runs of the simulator
\bm{f}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\lambda})
to compute the estimators
\hat{E}_{M}\Big(\psi(Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))\Big)
,
\hat{P}_{L}(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\alpha})
or
\hat{E}_{N,M}^{\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell}}\Big(\psi(Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))\Big)
. It is important to note that in the approach presented here, the computation of the likelihood
p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\lambda})=p_{\bm{E}}\left(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}-(f_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\lambda}))_{j=1}^{n}\right)
requires
n
runs for a single
\bm{\lambda}.

It leads to the following numbers of calls to the simulator:

•

L\times n\times\ell
runs for the estimation of
\bm{\alpha}_{\text{MAP}}
, where
\ell
is the number of iterations before convergence of
Algorithm

1
.

•

L\times n+M\times(n+1)
runs for the estimation of
\hat{E}_{N,M}^{\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell}}\Big(\psi(Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))\Big)
. The factor
n+1
comes from the fact that simulations must be run at
(\mathbf{x}_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
and at the new point
\mathbf{x}_{0}.
If
\hat{p}^{\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell-1}}_{L}(\bm{\alpha}\mid\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}})
is used for the likelihood approximation, then only
M\times(n+1)
runs are needed.

In practice, these computations are not feasible when the physical simulations are costly (for instance, several hours long each) and then surrogate models are required. First, surrogate models are required for the approximation of
\left(f_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\lambda})\right)_{j=1}^{n}
to compute the likelihood
p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\lambda})=p_{\bm{E}}\left(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}-(f_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\lambda}))_{j=1}^{n}\right)
for the MCMC sampling in the posterior distribution of
\mathbf{\Lambda}
and the investigation of
\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell}
(see
Algorithm

1
). Additionally, a surrogate model is needed for
f_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\bm{\lambda})
to compute the prediction estimator at the new point
\mathbf{x}_{0}
for the unobserved output
f_{t}.
Therefore, for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{X}\cup\{\mathbf{x}_{0}\}
and
1\leq t\leq T
, we propose to build a surrogate model for
f_{t,\mathbf{x}}\colon\bm{\lambda}\longrightarrow f_{t}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\lambda})
.

Gaussian processes (GP) are used here as a surrogate model as they make it possible to quantify the uncertainties, as presented in
[
24
]
. Let us assume that
f_{t,\mathbf{x}}
is a realization of a Gaussian process
Z_{t,\mathbf{x}}(\bm{\lambda})
with prior mean
\mu_{t,\mathbf{x}}(\bm{\lambda})
and prior covariance kernel
k_{t,\mathbf{x}}(\bm{\lambda},\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})
.

Z_{t,\mathbf{x}}
and
Z_{t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}
are considered independent if
(t,\mathbf{x})\neq(t^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
. The kernel models the correlation structure between
f_{t,\mathbf{x}}(\bm{\lambda})
and
f_{t,\mathbf{x}}(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})
. In our study, the stationary Matérn 5/2 kernel, see
[
25
]
, is used. This choice of independency between the surrogate models implies that the Gaussian process used for the prediction at
\mathbf{x}_{0}
is independent of the processes associated with the observation points
\mathbf{x}_{j}
.

We consider a design of experiments
\mathbb{L}_{\mathrm{train}}=(\bm{\lambda}_{1},\dots,\bm{\lambda}_{n_{\mathrm{train}}})
and the observations

Z_{t,\mathbf{x}}^{\mathrm{train}}=(f_{t,\mathbf{x}}(\bm{\lambda}_{i}))_{i\in I_{\mathrm{train}}}
with
I_{\mathrm{train}}=\{1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{train}}\}
.
f_{t,\mathbf{x}}
can be approximated using the distribution of
Z_{t,\mathbf{x}}(\bm{\lambda})\mid Z(\mathbb{L}_{\mathrm{train}})=Z_{t,\mathbf{x}}^{\mathrm{train}}
,
which is a Gaussian process with the following mean and covariance:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \hat{f}_{t}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\lambda})&=\mu_{t,\mathbf{x}}(\bm{\lambda})+k_{t,\mathbf{x}}(\bm{\lambda},\mathbb{L}_{\mathrm{train}})k_{t,\mathbf{x}}(\mathbb{L}_{\mathrm{train}},\mathbb{L}_{\mathrm{train}})^{-1}(Z_{t,\mathbf{x}}^{\mathrm{train}}-\mu_{t,\mathbf{x}}(\mathbb{L}_{\mathrm{train}}))\\
c_{t}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\lambda},\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})&=k_{t,\mathbf{x}}(\bm{\lambda},\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})-k_{t,\mathbf{x}}(\bm{\lambda},\mathbb{L}_{\mathrm{train}})k_{t,\mathbf{x}}(\mathbb{L}_{\mathrm{train}},\mathbb{L}_{\mathrm{train}})^{-1}k_{t,\mathbf{x}}(\bm{\lambda}^{\prime},\mathbb{L}_{\mathrm{train}})^{T}\end{aligned}\right.,

where

k(\mathbb{L}_{\mathrm{train}},\mathbb{L}_{\mathrm{train}})=[k_{t,\mathbf{x}}(\bm{\lambda}_{i},\bm{\lambda}_{j})]_{i,j\in I_{\mathrm{train}}}
is the covariance matrix and

k_{t,\mathbf{x}}(\bm{\lambda},\mathbb{L}_{\mathrm{train}})=[k_{t,\mathbf{x}}(\bm{\lambda},\bm{\lambda}_{i})]_{i\in I_{\mathrm{train}}}
.

The variance is denoted
\varv_{t}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\lambda})=c_{t}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\lambda},\bm{\lambda})
. The model presented in
Equation

2
then becomes:

\left\{\begin{aligned} Y_{t}(\mathbf{x})&=\hat{f}_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\Lambda})+\sqrt{\varv_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\Lambda})}E^{f}(\mathbf{x}),\penalty\ \>\penalty\ \>\penalty\ \>\penalty\ \>\penalty\ \>\>\>\>1\leq t\leq T,\penalty\ \mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}\\
Y_{1,\text{obs},j}&=Y_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{j})+E_{j},\penalty\ \>\penalty\ \>\penalty\ \>\penalty\ \>\penalty\ \>\penalty\ \>\>\penalty\ \>\penalty\ \>\penalty\ \>\penalty\ \>\penalty\ \>\>\penalty\ \>\penalty\ \>\>\>\>\penalty\ \>\penalty\ \>\>\penalty\ \>\penalty\ \>1\leq j\leq n\end{aligned}\right.,

(17)

with
E^{f}(\mathbf{x})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,
E^{f}(\mathbf{x})\perp\!\!\!\!\perp E^{f}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
if
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, and
\bm{E}=(E_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
of pdf
p_{\bm{E}}.

The pdf of the likelihood of
\bm{\lambda}
becomes

p_{E^{\mathrm{tot}}(\bm{\lambda})}\left((y_{1,\text{obs},j}-\hat{f}_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\lambda}))_{j=1}^{n}\right)
with

E^{\mathrm{tot}}(\bm{\lambda})=\left(E_{j}+\sqrt{\varv_{1}\left(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\lambda}\right)}E^{f}(\mathbf{x}_{j})\right)_{j=1}^{n}.

(18)

Incorporating the variance of the Gaussian process into the likelihood computation ensures that its uncertainty is accounted for during sampling.
Particularly, in our study we consider that
p_{\bm{E}}
is the pdf of a Gaussian white noise vector, i.e.
\bm{E}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}I)
. This gives
E^{\mathrm{tot}}(\bm{\lambda})\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}I+\text{diag}\Big(\varv_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\lambda})\Big)_{j=1}^{n}\right),

and the estimators of the prediction mean and variance (from the law of total variance, see
[
26
]
) are

\begin{aligned} \hat{E}_{N,M}^{\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell}}\Big(Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\Big)&=\hat{E}_{N,M}^{\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell}}\Big(\hat{f}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{\Lambda})\Big)\\
\hat{E}_{N,M}^{\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell}}\Big(Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0})^{2}\Big)-\hat{E}_{N,M}^{\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell}}\Big(Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\Big)^{2}&=\hat{E}_{N,M}^{\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell}}\Big(\hat{f}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{\Lambda})^{2}+\varv_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{\Lambda})\Big)-\hat{E}_{N,M}^{\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell}}\Big(\hat{f}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{\Lambda})\Big)^{2}\end{aligned}.

(19)

This approach, which uses a separate Gaussian process (GP) for each
\mathbf{x}
, is well-suited to the current context, as it focuses on situations with a very limited number of observations
n
and a small number of predictions. Note that it is particularly appropriate in other cases where the control variables
\mathbf{x}_{j}
are categorical instead of numerical.
However, other implementations are possible, especially with higher values of
n
, by considering a Gaussian process
Z_{t}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\lambda})
in the joint space
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{L}
, with covariance
c_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\bm{\lambda},\bm{\lambda}^{\prime}).
In this case, the computation of the likelihood of
\bm{\lambda}
would be modified, as the distribution of
E^{\mathrm{tot}}(\bm{\lambda})
is now given by
E^{\mathrm{tot}}(\bm{\lambda})\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}I+\big(c_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{j_{1}},\mathbf{x}_{j_{2}},\bm{\lambda},\bm{\lambda})\big)_{j_{1},j_{2}=1}^{n}\right)

3.2
Model error

Unfortunately, no real experimental data were available for this study. However, one can easily generate virtual measurements and incorporate a custom model error to work within the previously described framework. This custom model error is introduced through a perturbation of the control variables, ensuring that the synthetic data are generated using a method whose error structure does not exactly replicate that of the inference approach we aim to apply.
Since our hierarchical methods rely on fluctuations of
Y_{0}
and
Y_{M}
, the model error must not be based on these parameters. Therefore, we choose to perturb the control variables instead.

For a given triplet
\mathbf{x}=(\ell_{0},r_{0},v_{0})
, we define
\omega(\mathbf{x})=(\ell_{0},r_{0},v_{0}+\Delta V)
, where
\Delta V
is fixed to a constant in our work.
For
1\leq t\leq T
, the true value is introduced as
y_{t}(\mathbf{x})=f_{t}(\omega(\mathbf{x}),\bm{\lambda}_{0})

for a given
\bm{\lambda}_{0}\in\mathcal{L}
, leading to a model error as there is no specific value of
\bm{\lambda}
that verifies that
\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X},f_{t}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\lambda})=y_{t}(\mathbf{x}).

Then the protocol is the following:

•

Select
n
points
\mathbb{X}=(\mathbf{x}_{j})_{j=1}^{n}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
with a Maximin Latin Hypercube Sampling
[
31
]
.
n
is small to work with a limited number of observations.

•

Compute
(y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{j}))_{j=1}^{n}=(f_{t}(\omega(\mathbf{x}_{j}),\bm{\lambda}_{0}))_{j=1}^{n}
,

•

Add a realization
\bm{\mathcal{E}}=(\mathcal{E}_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
of the vector
\bm{E}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}I)
as the measurement error for the observed variable.

Finally it provides the vector of experimental data,

\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}=\left(y_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{j})+\mathcal{E}_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{n}.

3.3
Performance metrics

Beyond the measurements of the first variable
\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}=(y_{1,\text{obs},j})_{j=1}^{n}=(y_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{j})+\mathcal{E}_{j})_{j=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

here we also have access to the true values
y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{j})
for
1\leq t\leq T,1\leq j\leq n.

A Leave-One-Out (LOO) approach is used, to predict
y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{j})
for
1\leq j\leq n
based on the noisy observations
\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}^{-j}}=(y_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{j^{\prime}})+E_{j^{\prime}})_{\underset{j^{\prime}\neq j}{j^{\prime}=1}}^{n}
.
This makes it possible to assess the performance of the method with a small dataset. Then, a specific
\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell}
is identified for every
j
, that will be denoted
\bm{\alpha}^{j\star}_{\ell}.

More precisely, for
j=1,\dots,n
and
t=1,\dots,T,
we have a random prediction
Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{j})\mid\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}^{-j}}
. Its distribution is called the predictive distribution, that will be compared to the true value
y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{j}).
In order to have a direct comparison of the different investigated methods, the scoring rule introduced in Equation 25 of
[
32
]
, based on the mean and the covariance of the predictive distribution, is selected here.
In the general case, it is expressed as

s(\bm{Y},\bm{y})=-\text{log}(\text{det}(\bm{\Sigma}))-(\bm{y}-\bm{\mu})^{T}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}(\bm{y}-\bm{\mu}),\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{with}

(20)

•

\bm{Y}
a random prediction vector of mean
\bm{\mu}
and covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}

•

\bm{y}
the vector of true values of the phenomenon.

Here, in the LOO scheme, the predictions
\left(Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{j})\mid\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}^{-j}}\right)_{j=1}^{n}
are independent. The estimated mean and variance of the prediction
Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{j})\mid\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}^{-j}}
are defined as
\hat{\mu}_{t,N,M}(\mathbf{x}_{j})
and
\hat{\mathrm{v}}_{t,N,M}(\mathbf{x}_{j})
, computed using the estimators
\hat{E}_{N,M}^{\bm{\alpha}^{j\star}_{\ell}}\Big(Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{j})\Big)
and
\hat{E}_{N,M}^{\bm{\alpha}^{j\star}_{\ell}}\Big(Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{j})^{2}\Big)-\hat{E}_{N,M}^{\bm{\alpha}^{\star}_{\ell}}\Big(Y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{j})\Big)^{2}
from
Equation

19
.

For
j=1,\dots,n
, the score is introduced as

s_{t,N,M}(\mathbf{x}_{j})=-\text{log}(\hat{\mathrm{v}}_{t,N,M}(\mathbf{x}_{j}))-\frac{(y_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{j})-\hat{\mu}_{t,N,M}(\mathbf{x}_{j}))^{2}}{\hat{\mathrm{v}}_{t,N,M}(\mathbf{x}_{j})}

and we finally investigate the overall prediction score

s_{t,N,M}(\mathbb{X})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}s_{t,N,M}(\mathbf{x}_{j}),

which corresponds to the score in
Equation

20
under the assumption of independent predictions, i.e., with
\bm{\Sigma}
as a diagonal matrix.
This score provides a meaningful balance between prediction accuracy and uncertainty quantification, making it particularly well-suited to our application.
The score is computed for all the investigated methods in our study, and the results are compared in
Section

3.5
, with higher scores indicating better predictive performance.

4
Summary and perspectives

This article addresses the transposition problem, which occurs when observed experimental data do not correspond directly to the data of interest. It examines a scenario with multiple outputs, where only one outputis experimentally observed through a limited set of observations, while the goal is to predict all outputs. The main idea is to propose a representation of the model error that is adapted to this situation, and thus needs to be embedded in the parameters
\mathbf{\Lambda}
to establish a relationship between the observations and the unobserved outputs. Specifically, we explore the inclusion in
\mathbf{\Lambda}
of additional parameters, typically numerical or known physical ones, that were not originally intended for calibration. These parameters are incorporated to account for model error through a hierarchical representation, directing this error into specific zones by introducing hyperparameters
\bm{\alpha}
for the prior distribution. The estimation of these hyperparameters is then conducted using Bayesian inference, either by identifying the maximum a posteriori
\bm{\alpha}_{\text{MAP}}
or by sampling from the posterior distribution, with an importance sampling scheme to significanlty reduce the computational cost. This study is conducted in the context of expensive simulations, with Gaussian process regression as a surrogate model.

The application test case of the article models the Taylor cylinder impact test, which involves three outputs considered as observed variables one at a time, leading to three different sets of results. The performance of our method is compared to the approach proposed by
[
13
]
, which introduces an
Embedded Discrepancy
method relevant to our scenario. It is important to keep in mind that the results of these Bayesian approaches are highly dependent on the specific application case.

The results obtained here highlight the significant impact of the hierarchical model, as evidenced by the scores computed to assess the predictive distribution in relation to the true values.
This impact is somewhat reduced when a small noise measurement variance is considered, although the hierarchical approach remains well-suited. In contrast, the
Embedded Discrepancy
method from
[
13
]
performs less effectively in our context, partly due to the fact that the most likely values of
\mathbf{\Lambda}
are located near the boundaries. Additionally, the Legendre-Uniform Polynomial Chaos (Legendre-Uniform PC) representation used in this study could be further modified to potentially enhance these results.

Although the study presented here is comprehensive, further developments should be considered. As explained in
Section

3.5
, the influence of the prior on
\mathbf{\Lambda}
is reduced when the noise measurement variance is small, although the results of the hierarchical model remain promising in this situation, as shown in
E
. An in-depth study of the relationship between the impact of the hierarchical representation and the noise measurement could be valuable, potentially identifying a threshold on the noise variance below which the likelihood on
\mathbf{\Lambda}
becomes too concentrated, resulting in a non-influential prior.

Another important aspect of the work is the surrogate model implemented with Gaussian process regression. Here, as explained in
Section

2.5
, a different Gaussian process is considered for every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and
1\leq t\leq T
, and they are all considered independent, which is possible with a small number of observations. Other implementations are possible. For instance, one could consider a Gaussian process
Z_{t}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\lambda})
in the joint space
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{L}
, or even multi-output Gaussian processes
[
35
,
36
,
37
]
to account for the dependencies between the outputs of the computer code
(f_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
.

Even though the framework presented in this article is general, it has only been tested in the context of a transposition situation involving unobserved outputs. Other transposition cases should be explored, such as situations involving a change of scale where predictions are made at a point
\mathbf{x}_{0}
far from the observation points
(\mathbf{x}_{j})_{j=1}^{n}.
Note that the transposition situations can be combined as well, with a change of scale for
\mathbf{x}
and the prediction of an unobserved output
y_{t}.
The method with the hierarchical model and the different performance metrics remain valid in these contexts.

Finally, as previously mentioned, the results of the methods are highly influenced by the application test case. In particular, a computer code without constraints on the calibration variables would be valuable, as it would allow for the consideration of untruncated prior distributions for instance.

Appendix B
Prior fluctuations of the outputs

As mentioned in
Section

1
, the fluctuations of the additional parameters must introduce sufficient variations in the outputs to adequately capture the model error. The objective here is to investigate, for our application test case, the amplitude of the variations in the three outputs when the calibration parameters
\mathbf{\Lambda}
vary according to their prior distribution. This analysis aims to confirm the applicability of our strategy in this context and to provide insights into the appropriate standard deviation for the prior distribution.

In Figure
6
, these variations are analyzed for prior distributions with different standard deviations. The idea is to visualize the distribution of
f_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\Lambda})
where
\mathbf{\Lambda}
has density
p^{\sigma}_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\lambda})=\mathbb{1}_{[0,1]^{p}}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{p})\times f_{\mathcal{N}_{t}(0.5,\sigma^{2},0,1)}(\lambda_{p+1},\dots,\lambda_{q})
for different values of
\sigma
, where
\mathcal{N}_{t}(0.5,\sigma^{2},0,1)
is a Gaussian distribution with mean
0.5
and standard deviation
\sigma
, truncated between
0
and
1
. More precisely, i.i.d. samples
\left(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{k}\right)_{k=1}^{10^{4}}
are generated with density
p^{\sigma}_{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\bm{\lambda})
, and the interval
[y_{t,\sigma}^{0.025}(\mathbf{x}),y_{t,\sigma}^{0.975}(\mathbf{x})]
is displayed to study the fluctuations of the outputs, where
y_{t,\sigma}^{0.025}(\mathbf{x})
and
y_{t,\sigma}^{0.975}(\mathbf{x})
are the empirical quantiles of level
0.025
and
0.975
of the sample
\left(f_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{\Lambda}_{k})\right)_{k=1}^{10^{4}}
.

The objective is to identify standard deviations that are too small to capture the model discrepancy when using centered prior distributions. In Figure
6
, the case
\sigma=+\infty
corresponds to uniform sampling in
[0,1]^{q}
, which is naturally the most exploratory scenario. This confirms that our strategy is applicable here, as the variations in the outputs are large enough to account for the model discrepancy.

Moreover, the exploration achieved with uniform sampling appears nearly identical to that obtained with prior distributions using
\sigma=0.75
,
\sigma=0.60
, and
\sigma=0.45
. In contrast, smaller values such as
\sigma=0.30
and

Appendix C
Embedded discrepancy

This section provides a brief summary of the work by
[
13
]
, who proposed another type of embedded error applicable in our transposition context. Since this method has been implemented in our work for comparison with our numerical results, these details are essential for understanding the fundamentals of their approach.

Their idea is to consider an embedded random discrepancy
\delta
, parameterized by
\bm{\alpha}^{2}
. In the following, this discrepancy is denoted
\delta(\bm{\alpha}^{2},\bm{\Xi})
, where
\bm{\Xi}
highlights explicitly the stochastic dimension.

This formulation leads to considering the random output
f_{t}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\alpha}^{1}+\delta(\bm{\alpha}^{2},\bm{\Xi}))
, where
\mathbf{\Lambda}=\bm{\alpha}^{1}+\delta(\bm{\alpha}^{2},\bm{\Xi})
is a random variable parametrized by
\bm{\alpha}^{1}
and
\bm{\alpha}^{2}
. The problem is treated as a Bayesian estimation of the parameters
\bm{\alpha}^{1}
and
\bm{\alpha}^{2}
, that are parameters of a pdf. In the following, we denote
\bm{\alpha}=(\bm{\alpha}^{1},\bm{\alpha}^{2})
the set of augmented parameters. Then, we consider the GP formulation

Y_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\hat{f}_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{A}^{1}+\delta(\mathbf{A}^{2},\bm{\Xi}))+\sqrt{\varv_{1}\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{A}^{1}+\delta(\mathbf{A}^{2},\bm{\Xi})\right)}E^{f},

with
\mathbf{A}^{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{q},\>\mathbf{A}^{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}.

C.1
Likelihood estimation.

We need to investigate
p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\alpha})
the likelihood of
\bm{\alpha}.
We first define the random vectors

\begin{cases}\hat{\mathbf{F}}(\bm{\alpha})=\left(\hat{f}_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\alpha}^{1}+\delta(\bm{\alpha}^{2},\bm{\Xi}))\right)_{j=1}^{n}\\
\mathbf{H}(\bm{\alpha})=\left(\hat{f}_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\alpha}^{1}+\delta(\bm{\alpha}^{2},\bm{\Xi}))+\sqrt{\varv_{1}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\alpha}^{1}+\delta(\bm{\alpha}^{2},\bm{\Xi}))}E^{f}+E_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{n}\end{cases}

Then, we have

p(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}}\mid\bm{\alpha})=\pi_{\mathbf{H}(\bm{\alpha})}(\bm{y_{1,\text{obs}}})

with
\pi_{\mathbf{H}(\bm{\alpha})}
the pdf of
\mathbf{H}(\bm{\alpha})
. One challenge is to evaluate
\pi_{\mathbf{H}(\bm{\alpha})}
for every
\bm{\alpha}
. A high dimensional Kernel Density Estimation (KDE,
[
39
]
) would provide a precise computation but is too costly in our context. Several likelihood approximations are proposed in
[
13
]
, and we have decided to work with an independent normal approximation here, for simplicity reasons. The idea is to get
R
samples
(\bm{\xi}_{r})_{r=1}^{R}
that are i.i.d. realizations of the random variable
\bm{\Xi}
, and then compute for
1\leq j\leq n

\begin{cases}\hat{\mu}_{j}&=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\hat{f}_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\alpha}^{1}+\delta(\bm{\alpha}^{2},\bm{\xi}_{r}))\\
\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{2}&=\frac{1}{R-1}\sum_{r=1}^{R}(\hat{f}_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\alpha}^{1}+\delta(\bm{\alpha}^{2},\bm{\xi}_{r}))-\hat{\mu}_{j})^{2}+\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\varv_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{j},\bm{\alpha}^{1}+\delta(\bm{\alpha}^{2},\bm{\xi}_{r}))+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\end{cases}

Finally, with the independent normal approximation, the pdf
\pi_{\mathbf{H}(\bm{\alpha})}
is defined as

\pi_{\mathbf{H}(\bm{\alpha})}(\mathbf{y})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\hat{\sigma}_{j}}exp(-\frac{(y_{j}-\hat{\mu}_{j})^{2}}{2\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{2}})\penalty\ .

C.2
Polynomial chaos representation.

The random discrepancy
\delta(\bm{\alpha}^{2},\bm{\Xi})
needs to be parametrized. As presented in
[
13
]
, we have opted for a Polynomial Chaos (PC,
[
40
]
) representation, and more precisely Legendre-Uniform PC that can respect the support of the calibration parameters. It gives

\bm{\alpha}^{1}+\delta(\bm{\alpha}^{2},\bm{\Xi})=\bm{\alpha}^{1}+\text{diag}(\bm{\alpha}^{2}\bm{\Xi}^{T})

with
\bm{\Xi}_{i}\sim\mathcal{U}(-1,1),\penalty\ 1\leq i\leq q
and
\bm{\Xi}_{i}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp\bm{\Xi}_{j}
if
i\neq j.

C.3
Prior construction.

The choice of the prior is here driven by the respect of the support of the parameters once again. We have opted for the uniform prior that respects the constraints
\bm{\alpha}^{1}_{i}-\bm{\alpha}^{2}_{i}\geq 0
and
\bm{\alpha}^{1}_{i}+\bm{\alpha}^{2}_{i}\leq 1,
and
\bm{\alpha}^{2}_{i}\geq 0
as well to avoid bimodal distributions.
We then have

p_{(\mathbf{A}^{1},\mathbf{A}^{2})}(\bm{\alpha}^{1},\bm{\alpha}^{2})\propto\prod_{i=1}^{q}\mathbb{1}_{\bm{\alpha}_{i}^{2}>0}\mathbb{1}_{\bm{\alpha}_{i}^{1}-\bm{\alpha}_{i}^{2}>0}\mathbb{1}_{\bm{\alpha}_{i}^{1}+\bm{\alpha}_{i}^{2}<1}.

C.4
Computation cost.

This strategy is relevant in our context but involves a high computation cost compared to the method presented in this article. Indeed, computing the likelihood for a single
\bm{\alpha}
requires
R\times n
calls to the simulator (or the surrogate model), compared to
n
calls in our method. This is a major difference as
R
is associated with sampling in dimension
q
and thus must be large.
