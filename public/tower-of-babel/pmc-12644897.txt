Structural insights into a rhombic Penrose tiling variant abundant in five-petaled flower motifs
Authors: Nobuhisa Fujita, Komajiro Niizeki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present a novel variant of a planar quasiperiodic tiling with ten-fold symmetry, utilising thick and thin rhombuses akin to the well-known rhombic Penrose tiling. It is shown that this new tiling shares several key characteristics with its predecessor, including similar vertex environments, polygonal acceptance domains exhibiting five-fold symmetry and a self-similarity tied to the golden mean, which serves as the fundamental scaling ratio. Nonetheless, it presents a fundamentally distinct structure. The new tiling introduces additional complexity through an increased number of prototiles and a dual grid pattern that incorporates both folded and straight lines. Most notably, it is characterized by a high density of two-tiered, five-petaled flower motifs, which occupy a significant portion of the tiling. Based on this prominent characteristic, we refer to the new tiling as the “Pentas tiling”. Introduction

Despite the recent discoveries of monotile tilings which utilise a single prototile1,2, aperiodic tilings typically rely on two or more shapes as their prototiles. A prominent example is the pentagonal Penrose tiling (P1), which employs six polygonal shapes: three types of regular pentagons, along with thin rhombuses, crowns and pentacles, which fill gaps between the pentagons3–5. These six prototiles adhere to specific matching constraints, often represented by bumps and dents on their edges, which forbid any periodic arrangements thereby enforcing aperiodicity. The number of prototiles in P1 can be effectively reduced to two by reassembling the six tile shapes, into pieces and recomposing them. This reworking has led to the discovery of two pairs of prototiles: kites and darts (P2) and thick and thin rhombuses (P3). Both sets incorporate matching rules that ensure aperiodicity4–6. These three Penrose tilings have garnered considerable attention for their contributions to our understanding of the structure of quasicrystals7–9.

In this study, we show that the thick and thin rhombuses of the P3 tiling can assemble into a new tiling, which we term the P3* tiling. The P3* tiling can be readily derived from the P1 tiling and hence belongs to the same mutual-local-derivability class as the P3 tiling. Although at first glance the P3 and P3* tilings may appear somewhat similar, the two structures are fundamentally different, as revealed by the shapes of their acceptance domains and dominant local structural motifs. Our findings also imply that the significance of the P3* tiling in the structural modelling of decagonal quasicrystals may well rival that of the P3 tiling.

We illustrate all the fundamental properties of the P3* tiling, including allowed vertex environments, acceptance domains, six types of prototiles and its inflation/deflation symmetry represented by scaling with the golden mean, . In addition, we show that each prototile of the P3* tiling can be uniquely decorated with Ammann bars. In a perfect P3* tiling, these bars align with the Ammann bar grid used in the P3 tiling, showcasing the local derivability between the two tilings. Finally, an assessment of hyperuniform order reveals that the P3* tiling exhibits slightly enhanced hyperuniformity compared to the P3 tiling.

Preliminaries

Aperiodic tilings with rhombuses

Due to their simplicity and versatility in representing a wide range of structures, rhombuses with constant edge length are often employed as prototiles in aperiodic tilings. Many of these tilings can be constructed using the dual-grid method10,11, originally developed by N.G. de Bruijn to create the P3 tiling via pentagrids, equally spaced line grids arranged in five symmetry-related orientations12. This method introduces continuous degrees of freedom through parallel shifts of the line grids, resulting in a continuous family of tilings composed of the same rhombic prototiles13,14. An attempt has been made to apply these generalized Penrose tilings to the structural analysis of decagonal quasicrystals15. Previous studies have revealed contrasting properties in the two-point correlations of generalised Penrose tilings with pentagonal or decagonal symmetry obtained through this method16,17, leading to a diverse range of physical behaviours18. The technique also demonstrates that the resulting tilings are quasiperiodic, as their vertices are achieved by orthogonally projecting lattice points cut from a high-dimensional hypercubic lattice11,12.

In addition to generalised Penrose tilings13–18, the thick and thin rhombuses can form a variety of further aperiodic tilings. The literature extensively discusses quasiperiodic binary tilings with either polygonal19–21 or fractal acceptance domains22. In addition, a non-Pisot substitution rule has been proposed to generate an aperiodic binary tiling that extends beyond quasiperiodicity23.

Classification concepts

Although the full range of aperiodic tilings remains largely unexplored24, understanding how individual tilings relate to one another is of significant interest. In addition to point symmetry, we often use two key equivalence concepts to define the relationships between aperiodic tilings or patterns. These concepts, referred to as local isomorphism and mutual local derivability, are briefly described as follows.

(i)Local isomorphism (LI): Two tilings are said to be locally isomorphic if every finite patch in one tiling appears in the other, up to translation, and vice versa25,26. Since the distinction between individual members of an LI class is physically insignificant, the point group symmetry of a given tiling is often defined by that of the entire LI class. A symmetry operation, in this context, is understood to transform a tiling into another that is locally isomorphic to it. It is also worth noting that the term ‘P3 tiling’, for example, refers not to a single specific realization, but to the entire LI class. The same holds true for other types of tilings discussed in the literature.

(ii)Mutual local derivability (MLD): Two tilings are said to be mutually locally derivable if one can be obtained from the other through local transformation, with each element in the former determined by a finite region of the latter and vice versa26. The MLD relationship between two tilings naturally establishes an MLD relationship between their respective LI classes. Notably, all three versions of the Penrose tiling, that is, P1, P2 and P3, are equivalent in terms of MLD. In general, all tilings that are MLD with each other form an MLD class of tilings.

Planar tilings with ten-fold rotational symmetry (more specifically, those with the planar point group ) are essential for modelling the structure of decagonal quasicrystals, which represent one of the primary symmetry classes observed in intermetallic systems. These materials are characterized by a periodic stacking of layers, within which atoms are arranged quasiperiodically. The MLD classification provides the most basic distinction among planar tilings within the same symmetry class. However, each MLD class contains an infinite number of tilings (or, more precisely, an infinite number of LI classes of tilings) that exhibit distinct local geometries. Consequently, in the structural modelling of quasicrystals, it is just as important to identify the most suitable tiling within an MLD class to represent the underlying geometry as it is to determine the appropriate MLD class itself.

P3* tiling

We next introduce the P3* tiling, which belongs to the same MLD class as the other three Penrose tilings, P1, P2 and P3. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the P3* tiling is derived locally from the P1 tiling by replacing each edge of the P1 tiling with a thin rhombus, with the acute corners aligned at the ends of the edge. The remaining gaps are uniquely filled with three distinct configurations, comprising one, three and five thick rhombuses, respectively, connected by shared edges. By contrast, the P1 tiling can be locally derived from the P3* tiling, where the edges of the former correspond to the long diagonals of all the thin rhombuses in the latter. It is surprising that such a straightforward modification of the standard Penrose tiling appears to have been previously overlooked.

P3* tiling composed of thick (shaded) and thin (white) rhombuses. The derivation of this tiling from the P1 tiling (orange solid lines) is shown on the left.

The arrangement of rhombuses in the P3* tiling differs from that in the P3 tiling. Nevertheless, it is equally aesthetically pleasing, with simple local patterns extending across the entire plane. An important observation common to both the P3 and P3* tilings is that no thin rhombus can be adjacent to more than one other thin rhombus through a shared edge; thus, thin rhombuses occur either in isolation or in pairs. Consequently, thick rhombuses are distributed uniformly throughout the structure, making both tilings look visually uniform.

Figure 2a illustrates the various vertex environments found in the P3 and P3* tilings, labelled according to Fig. 5 of Ref.14. Five of these environments, designated as D, Q, K, J and S, with coordination numbers of 3, 4 or 5 are common to both tilings. By contrast, the remaining environments with coordination numbers of 6 and 7 are unique to each tiling: T and V are exclusive to P3, and U and W are exclusive to P3*. Both tilings also feature the 5-coordinated environment labelled S, which appears in two distinct forms, namely, S1 and S2, as shown in Fig. 2b. These two forms differ in the arrangements of thin rhombuses within their second coordination shells. Notably, the S1 environment corresponds to the centre of a two-tiered, five-petaled flower motif, which occupies a significant portion of the P3* tiling, as depicted in Fig. 1. Based on this prominent characteristic, we propose referring to the P3* tiling as the “Pentas tiling”.

Dual grid of the P3* tiling

Each of the two prototiles of P3 can be uniquely decorated with straight line segments, known as Ammann bars, named after their inventor, Robert Ammann30. When the matching rules are fully adhered to throughout the tiling, these bars align seamlessly across tile edges, creating continuous straight lines oriented along the five edge directions of a regular pentagon. Collectively, these lines form what is referred to as the Ammann bar grid. Within this grid, each set of parallel lines forms a Fibonacci quasilattice, with long () and short () intervals between adjacent lines. Notably, each of the six prototile rhombuses is decorated with a unique pattern of line segments, which seamlessly extends into similar patterns on adjacent rhombuses, thereby establishing the matching rules of the P3* tiling.

Analogous Ammann bar grids with non-crystallographic point symmetries are known to serve as frameworks for producing quasiperiodic tilings, which correspond to the geometric duals of these grids. Several examples, particularly rhombic quasiperiodic tilings with eight-, ten- and 12-fold rotational symmetries, have been associated with Ammann bar grids34–38. As a primary example, the Ammann bar grid discussed earlier can be dualised to create the P3 tiling, more specifically, a once-deflated version with unit edge length, in contrast to the version shown in Fig. 7, which has an edge length of .

Discussion

The Pentas (P3*) tiling may have an advantage over the P3 tiling due to its greater density of type-S1 vertices. These vertices correspond to the centres of the two-tiered, five-petaled decagonal motifs illustrated in Fig. 2b (left), enhancing the overall visual uniformity. Importantly, the only areas not covered by these decagons are occupied solely by thick rhombuses of type c. Consequently, the total area covered by the decagons amounts to .

The visual characteristics of the Pentas (P3*) tiling can be further clarified by examining its hyperuniformity, a term that describes spatial patterns exhibiting vanishing density fluctuations at infinite wavelengths39. In d-dimensional space, a point pattern is termed hyperuniform, or said to exhibit hyperuniform order, if its local density fluctuations, quantified by the variance of the number of points within a disc-shaped observation window of radius R, grow more slowly than the mean number of points in the window as R increases. In other words, asymptotically behaves as Rα with α < d. More specifically, the pattern is said to be strongly hyperuniform if BRd−1. Previous studies have shown that the vertex sets of quasiperiodic tilings are often strongly hyperuniform16,17,40–43. In these cases, different quasiperiodic tilings can be contrasted by the asymptotic coefficient B, which serves as a quantitative metric for the degree of hyperuniform order39.

We next compare the P3 and P3* tilings in terms of the hyperuniform order metric B for their vertex sets. A large disc-shaped patch was generated for each tiling and the number variance (R) was numerically computed using observation windows centred at 62,500 randomly sampled points within a disc of radius 250 (measured in units of the rhombic edge length) located at the centre of the patch. The order metric B for each tiling was determined by fitting the data for Λ(R) ≔ (R)/R within 10 < R < 240 to the asymptotic functional form Λ(R) ≈ B + C/R using the linear least squares method (see Supplementary Fig. 4 for further details). The normalised order metric B, where and is the number density of points, was found to be 0.5914 and 0.5903 for the P3 and P3* tilings, respectively. This result for the P3 tiling aligns with previous estimates16,17,40,43. These values also suggest that the hyperuniform order of the P3* tiling is marginally higher than that of the P3 tiling, with the order metric showing a reduction of approximately 0.2%. Notably, the P3 tiling has previously been reported to exhibit the highest hyperuniform order among the generalised rhombic Penrose tilings16,17.

In summary, we have investigated the fundamental properties of the Pentas (P3*) tiling, a novel variant of Penrose tilings constructed from thick and thin rhombuses. These properties include its local vertex environments, ADs, MLD relationships with standard Penrose tilings, inflation rules and the associated dual grid. Notably, the P3* tiling shares several key features with its predecessor, the P3 tiling, such as high visual uniformity, similar vertex environments, polygonal ADs exhibiting five-fold symmetry and the characteristic -scaling property. Nevertheless, the two structures are fundamentally different, as evidenced by differences in the sizes and shapes of their ADs and dominant local structural motifs. It has also been shown that the P3* tiling introduces additional complexities, including an increased number of prototiles and a dual grid that incorporates both folded and ordinary straight lines. We further examined the visual uniformity and overall structural coherence of the P3* tiling in terms of the high density of two-tiered, five-petaled decagonal motifs exhibiting local five-fold symmetry, as well as a numerical evaluation of the hyperuniform order metric. Our findings indicate that the P3* tiling demonstrates slightly higher hyperuniform order compared to the P3 tiling.
