Developmental origins of exceptional health and survival: a four-generation family cohort study
Authors: Matthew Thomas Keys, Søren Netra, Dorthe Almind Pedersen, Pernille Stemann Larsen, Alexander Kulminski, Mary F. Feitosa, Mary Wojczynski, Michael Province, Kaare Christensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Descendants of longevity-enriched sibships demonstrate a broad health and survival advantage throughout the life course. However, little is known about manifestations during very early life. Here we show a pattern of lower risk of adverse early-life outcomes in third-generation grandchildren (N = 5637) of Danish longevity-enriched sibships compared to the general population, including infant mortality (Hazard Ratio = 0.53, 95% CI [0.36, 0.77]) and a range of neonatal health indicators. These associations in fourth-generation great-grandchildren (N = 14,908) were attenuated and less consistent (e.g., infant mortality, Hazard Ratio = 0.90, [0.70, 1.17]). Dilatory patterns across successive generations were independent of stable advantages in select socioeconomic and behavioural indicators (e.g., parental education, income and maternal smoking), maternal and paternal lines of transmission, as well as secular trends in the background population. However, our socioeconomic and behavioral indicators were limited in both range and granularity. Our findings suggest that the familial aggregation of exceptional health and survival has early life developmental components.

Introduction

The study of long-lived persons is of key importance for understanding the mechanisms that confer protection against age-related morbidity in late life1. Replicating their example more broadly, if possible, would have the potential to bring powerful social and economic benefits2,3. However, the diversity of ageing trajectories within and across populations reflects the broad spectrum of their environmental and genetic determinants4. Studies assessing the combined impact of behavioural risk factors in mid- and late-life estimate that these alone may account for up to 10 to 14 years of life expectancy5–8. Studies of twins suggest a relatively modest contribution of genetic factors to variation in overall lifespan9. However, these factors likely become increasingly important in survival at advanced ages, which is further evidenced by the familial aggregation of exceptional longevity10,11. Studies of families selected for their aggregation of longevity are expected to play a key role in advancing our understanding of genetic and environmental determinants of exceptional health and survival12–14.

One area that has received less attention is the impact of life-course history prior to midlife, and especially the early developmental periods15–18. The idea that environmental conditions in early life can influence health and functioning throughout the life-course is the basis of the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) framework19. Many prospective birth cohort studies have demonstrated associations between early life health outcomes and chronic disease in midlife20. However, life-course studies in the context of exceptional longevity are impractical due to the required length of follow-up, limited historical data, as well as methodological issues caused by attrition21–25. From a different perspective, studies of historical cohorts have been used to assess the impact of intrauterine exposure to environmental shocks (e.g., famines and economic recessions) in the 19th and early 20th centuries, on health and functioning in late life26–28. However, the relevance and external validity of these mechanisms to longevity, outside of extreme environmental shocks, is currently unknown.

Descendants of long-lived persons and longevity-enriched sibships often display similar health and metabolic profiles to their ancestors and can serve as a model for healthy ageing29–31. Multigenerational family studies, in particular, provide unique opportunities to circumvent several methodological challenges in studies of longevity. First, studies of familial aggregation are less likely to include ‘sporadically long-lived’ persons, which otherwise dilute mechanistic insight32. For example, the familial aggregation of longevity predicts greater survival even after age 100, when compared to sporadic centenarians33. Second, family studies allow for the characterisation of health over several generations and provide a greater scope to explore the underlying epidemiological mechanisms of transmission11,14,34. Lastly, by piecing together data over several generations, an indication of the life-course perspective of longevity-enriched families and their health advantages can be obtained35.

Previous findings by our group have demonstrated marked health and survival advantages across three generations of longevity-enriched families36–39. Offspring of Danish longevity-enriched sibships exhibit 52% reduced mortality between ages 20–69, whereas grandchildren exhibit 30% lower mortality between ages 0–50, compared to the background population38. A general lowering of disease risk was also present in both generations, with similar patterns of dilution as reported for survival38. The most marked advantages were observed in hospitalisation for mental and behavioural disorders, implicating behavioural mechanisms in the familial aggregation of exceptional health and survival38. Interestingly, we also observed preliminary indications that the survival advantage may manifest earlier in life than previously thought, suggesting a possible developmental component to the familial aggregation of exceptional health and survival38. However, its broader health manifestations in terms of neonatal and maternal outcomes, underlying epidemiological mechanisms, as well as transmission across successive generations, are currently unclear.

In this study, we utilize a multigenerational cohort of longevity-enriched sibships and their descendants in Denmark to broadly assess early developmental health characteristics associated with the familial aggregation of longevity in two successive generations. Administrative registers covering all residents of Denmark facilitated the linkage of our cohort to its fourth-generation descendants and comparisons with the general population. Clinical registries established in the 1970s permitted measurement of a range of neonatal, infant, and maternal health phenotypes in third-generation (G3) grandchildren, where we have previously observed an exceptional adult phenotype, and fourth-generation (G4) great-grandchildren of our cohort. We compare these to demographically matched births from the general population, assess the transmission of developmental effects over multiple generations, and explore mediating roles of maternal and paternal lines of transmission, as well as socioeconomic and behavioural factors. Lastly, we interpret our findings in the context of a broader literature examining the relationship between early-life health characteristics and adult and late-life health phenotypes. Our findings suggest that the familial aggregation of exceptional health and survival has early-life developmental components.

Results

Study population

Our source population included the entire population living in Denmark since 1968. After extending our cohort of longevity-enriched families to the great-grandchildren using the CPR register, we identified a total of 10,623 third-generation (G3) grandchildren (born between 1950 and 2010) and 16,586 fourth-generation (G4) great-grandchildren (born between 1973 and 2018). After taking into account demographic factors (birth year, sex, birth season, maternal birth year, parity, and mother's place of birth) by matching, G3 grandchildren had approximately half the mortality of controls (HR = 0.53, 95% CI [0.36, 0.77]), and no evidence of differential effects dependent on longevity-enriched family status of the mother or father (Interaction = 1.06, 95% CI [0.50, 2.28]). G4 great-grandchildren had a weaker association (HR = 0.90, 95% CI [0.70, 1.17]) and similarly, no evidence of an interaction depending on maternal or paternal lines of transmission (HR = 1.08, 95% CI [0.64, 1.82]). All estimates were robust to further adjustment for parental socioeconomic factors, suggesting a lack of mediation of the total effect through these epidemiological mechanisms.

The descriptive data from the Kaplan Meier survival curves in Fig. 2 suggest that infant survival was approximately equal in G3 grandchildren and G4 great-grandchildren, but that controls matched to G4 had higher survival than controls matched to G3. However, these descriptive data do not take into account secular trends in infant mortality over successive generations, which would affect all groups. To more appropriately test for dilatory effects over successive generations, we utilised the period of overlapping birth years between the two generations to perform an analysis directly comparing G3 grandchildren and G4 great-grandchildren (see ‘Direct Comparison’ in Fig. 3). Essentially, this analysis controlled for a potential ‘catch-up’ effect where the matched control group of the G4 great-grandchildren may have improved their survival more than those from longevity-enriched families due to changes in neonatal and maternal care throughout the study period. Here, we still observed a substantial reduction in mortality in G3 grandchildren compared to G4 great-grandchildren (HR = 0.32, 95% CI [0.16, 0.64]), independent of secular trends in the background population. Overall, this suggests a real and strong dilutionary effect on the infant survival advantage, likely reflecting a loss of protective factors otherwise present in previous generations. Table S23 of the Supplementary Material describes a range of negative control sensitivity analyses that support this inference.

In G3 grandchildren, notable reductions were observed for preterm birth (OR = 0.82, 95% CI [0.72, 0.93], RD = −1.81%), small for gestational age (OR = 0.83, 95% CI [0.76, 0.90], RD = −2.51%), neonatal respiratory disorders (OR = 0.77, 95% CI [0.66, 0.88], RD = −1.36%), other neonatal morbidity (OR = 0.87, 95% CI [0.79, 0.96], RD = −1.34%), assisted delivery (OR = 0.87, 95% CI [0.79, 0.96], RD = −1.34%), and hemorrhage (OR = 0.87, 95% CI [0.77, 0.98], RD = −0.96%). However, all other associations were also negative in effect, implying a general trend towards reduced risk across a wide range of adverse birth outcomes. In G4 great-grandchildren, signals of risk reduction were weaker in magnitude and less consistent. Still, they were notable in the case of small for gestational age (OR = 0.90, 95% CI [0.85, 0.94], RD = −1.17%), neonatal respiratory disorders (OR = 0.92, 95% CI [0.87, 0.98], RD = −0.61%), and other neonatal morbidity (OR = 0.91, 95% CI [0.86, 0.96], RD = −0.82%). Minimal changes were observed after adjusting for parental socioeconomic factors in both generations, suggesting a lack of mediation of the total effect through these mechanisms, as in the previous analyses of infant mortality.

After adjustment for multiple testing (noutcomes = 14) and applying a 5% significance threshold, statistically significant differences were observed for G3 in preterm birth (p = 0.0170), small for gestational age (p = 0.0003) and neonatal respiratory disorders (p = 0.0026). For G4, significant differences were observed in small for gestational age (p = 0.0002) and other neonatal morbidity (p = 0.0071). Tables S5, S6 and S21 of the Supplementary Material describe estimates, p-values, confidence intervals, adjusted p-values, and risk differences for all these analyses.

To assess general trends in our secondary outcomes of morbidity, we analysed composite outcomes comprising all neonatal and maternal outcomes, respectively, excluding the less well-defined ‘other morbidity’ outcomes. For the composite of neonatal outcomes (n = 7), we found reduced risk in G3 (OR = 0.89, 95% CI [0.84, 0.93], RD = −2.38%) and a smaller advantage in G4 (OR = 0.96, 95% CI [0.94, 0.99], RD = −1.07%). For the composite of maternal outcomes (n = 5), we found reduced risk in G3 (OR = 0.90, 95% CI [0.85, 0.96], RD = −1.48%) and a smaller advantage in G4 (OR = 0.95, 95% CI [0.93, 0.98], RD = −1.19%). Similar findings were obtained when assessing composites, which included ‘other neonatal morbidity’ and ‘other maternal morbidity’ outcomes. As in previous analyses, minimal changes were observed after adjusting for parental socioeconomic factors in both generations. Items S9–S12 of the Supplementary Material fully describe our analyses of composite outcomes of neonatal and maternal morbidity. Table S21 describes the risk differences for all outcomes.

Maternal smoking and parental education

Figure 5 describes the longitudinal completeness of data measuring maternal smoking behaviour in the Medical Birth Registry, and analyses comparing exposure to maternal smoking in mothers of G3 grandchildren and G44 great-grandchildren compared to the general population. Data on maternal smoking were only available since 1991, but were also uniquely missing in 1997. The prevalence of maternal smoking was consistently lower in mothers of G3 grandchildren (OR = 0.80, 95% CI [0.69, 0.92], RD = −4.24%) and G4 great-grandchildren (OR = 0.75, 95% CI [0.71, 0.79], RD = −4.20%) of longevity-enriched sibships. Sensitivity analyses

Our results were robust to further statistical adjustment for paternal country of birth and paternal age. Our findings on congenital anomalies were unchanged when considering longer diagnostic windows of detection. Analyses of continuous outcomes and more granular categorisations of key birth indicators (e.g., very/extremely small for gestational age) supported our primary study findings. There was a trend for increasing advantage when assessing more extreme phenotypes of size for gestational age, whereas estimates remained more stable when assessing birth weight directly, albeit with limited statistical power. Several outcomes with non-random missingness of data were detected and predisposed to a bias that dilutes the estimated protective effect against adverse outcomes in longevity-enriched families. For example, the Apgar score measured after 5 min was disproportionately missing in those with an early neonatal death. Consistent findings were observed across analyses of composite neonatal and maternal morbidities, with various definitions including or excluding ‘other morbidity’ categories. Our sensitivity analyses using penalised regression methods with 10-fold cross-validation demonstrated that our primary models were not susceptible to overfitting.

Our main findings were not changed when appending previously unmatched study participants to our data with controls matched on weaker criteria (Survival HRG3 = 0.55, 95% CI [0.38, 0.80]; HRG4 = 0.89 [0.69, 1.15]). Our inferences were robust to several definitions of our main socioeconomic covariates, including variations based on length of education, gross versus net income, breadwinner versus total income, and specific maternal and paternal measurements, suggesting that these alternative definitions did not pick up on residual bias not captured by our primary covariates. Our results were also unchanged when adjusting for additional covariates not included in our main analyses, specifically municipal urban density and parental occupation. To further address residual bias not captured by the socioeconomic factors employed in our study, we performed an E-value analysis. For the G3 survival advantage, unmeasured variables would have to increase the risk of infant mortality by 3.07 through pathways independent of those captured by our socioeconomic covariates, and be 3.07 as prevalent in the matched controls, to fully explain away the observed advantage. Combined with the stability of our estimates before and after adjusting for our observed factors, this may suggest that socioeconomic constructs in general are unlikely to be driving the exceptional infant phenotype observed in our study.

Lastly, negative control analyses of our ‘direct comparison’ methodology demonstrated that our results were not biased by secular improvements in neonatal outcomes and infant mortality when directly comparing generations G3 and G4. This supported our interpretation that the exceptional infant phenotype is attenuated over successive generations in longevity-enriched families, and that this likely reflects a dilution of protective factors otherwise present in previous generations. Full reporting on our sensitivity analyses is available in sections S2, S3 of the Supplementary Material.

Discussion

Previous research has shown that the survival advantage in descendants of longevity-enriched sibships may manifest as early as the first year of life38. However, little is known about what factors underlie this observation, other health manifestations beyond survival, or its transmission across generations. In this study, we set out to explore the early-life health characteristics of descendants of longevity-enriched sibships, utilising a multigenerational cohort and national birth registries in Denmark. We analysed patterns of infant survival and adverse birth outcomes in third-generation (G3) grandchildren and fourth-generation (G4) great-grandchildren descending from a proband generation of longevity-enriched sibships (G1). Compared to matched live births from the general population, G3 grandchildren demonstrated reductions in the risk of infant mortality and a range of adverse birth outcomes, independent of demographic and socioeconomic factors. After correction for multiple testing, statistically significant effects were observed for lowered risks of being small for gestational age, preterm birth, and neonatal respiratory disorders. However, these effects were attenuated in G4 great-grandchildren with weaker and less consistent effects across most outcomes studied. However, G4 great-grandchildren still manifested a small advantage when assessing neonatal and maternal morbidities overall, as suggested by our analyses of composite outcomes.

Maternal and prenatal care in Denmark has changed considerably over the observation period included in our study41. Such changes may have affected families differently, depending on their baseline risk for adverse birth outcomes, e.g., with policies targeted towards at-risk populations. For this reason, the lack of differences in survival of G4 great-grandchildren may reflect a ‘catchup’ effect of the general population, rather than a ‘dilution’ of the protective effect of being a descendent of a longevity-enriched sibship. The balance of these two competing effects has substantial implications for the interpretation of our study. To address this issue, we utilised overlapping birth cohorts between G3 and G4 descendants to directly assess patterns of intergenerational transmission of the survival advantage, without reference to the general population. The results of these analyses demonstrated a strong dilution of exceptional survival between G3 and G4 descendants of longevity-enriched siblings, even when keeping trends in infant mortality and other demographic effects constant over time. This methodology was also able to accurately capture expected patterns of survival when applied in a range of negative control comparisons involving G3 and G4 matched controls (Supplementary Materials S22, S23). The dilution of the exceptional infant survival observed in our cohort of longevity-enriched families was thus independent of secular trends in the background population and likely reflected a partial loss of protective factors otherwise present in previous generations, rather than a “catch-up” effect in the general population.

Maternal smoking is a well-established risk factor for adverse birth outcomes in offspring, as well as pathological sequelae in later life42. In our cohorts, we observed a lower rate of smoking during pregnancy in mothers of both G3 grandchildren and G4 great-grandchildren. This finding supports previous research by our group implicating behavioural factors in the epidemiological mechanisms underlying the familial aggregation of exceptional health and survival, including reduced risk of tobacco-related cancers in G2 offspring and G3 grandchildren37,38. Interestingly, the smoking advantage was present to the same degree in both generations after adjustment for parental education levels, suggesting the stability of this behavioural trait over successive generations. Furthermore, we observed advantages in both mothers who were descendants of a longevity-enriched sibship, as well as those who married into such families, suggesting a role for shared environmental effects, assortative mating, and/or indirect genetic effects in the aggregation of positive behavioural traits in longevity-enriched families43,44.

Spouses marrying into our cohort of longevity-enriched families exhibit survival that is less exceptional than their partners, but greater than the general population45. This relatively smaller advantage likely contributes to the dilution of exceptional health and survival across successive generations14,31,46. Thus, we tested the relative importance of cumulative maternal versus paternal factors in the transmission of the exceptional infant phenotype by comparing children with mothers versus fathers descending from a longevity-enriched family47. Remarkably similar patterns of infant survival were observed in both groups. This finding, interpreted in isolation, may suggest either a negligible effect of parental-level environment factors relative to heritable factors, or an approximately equal role of both paternal and maternal environmental factors. Due to mixed evidence across generations, we could not rule out modest differences in the importance of maternal and paternal environmental factors in transmission for other neonatal outcomes (see Supplementary Materials S9–S12).

Infant health outcomes have a proximal and limited range of determinants and depend less on complex interactions between factors later on in the life course, such as behaviour48. These proximal factors include strictly biological genetic factors directly transmitted from parent to offspring, as well as environmentally mediated factors relating to fetal nutrition, maternal behaviour and health during pregnancy, and the early postnatal environment. In our cohort, we observed an infant health and survival advantage declining across successive generations with increasing distance from the longevity-enriched proband sibships. As described previously, this pattern likely reflected a partial loss of protective factors otherwise present in previous generations. We also observed remarkably consistent advantages in behavioural and socioeconomic indicators in the parents of both G3 grandchildren and G4 great-grandchildren. Various sensitivity analyses suggested that maternal smoking, parental socioeconomic factors, as well as other factors proxied by their measurement, were unlikely to be the primary drivers of the exceptional infant phenotype observed in G3 grandchildren. Lastly, we observed similarity in maternal and paternal lines of transmission of exceptional infant survival, despite the known vulnerability of the fetus to maternal nutrition and physiology47.

The idea that environmental conditions in early life can directly influence health and functioning later in life has been subject to investigation and embraced by a range of scientific disciplines. Studies of prospective cohorts, experimental manipulation in model organisms, and quasi-random environmental shocks in early life (e.g., famine, business cycles, and natural disasters) have coalesced to form the framework for the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) hypothesis19,20. Maladaptive responses to developmental cues, generally meant to preserve genotypic variation in the face of transitory environmental changes, are likely central to this phenomenon49. Developmental effects have been implicated in a range of adult diseases, including cardiovascular and metabolic disease, cancer, and neuropsychiatric conditions20. In this literature, epigenetic mechanisms are suspected to play an important role in observations of multigenerational and transgenerational effects arising from developmental exposures50. These include altered DNA methylation, histone modifications and non-coding RNAs, which may also be relevant to paternal lines of transmission51–53. However, due to the difficulty of teasing apart patterns of epigenetic effects and inheritance across the life-course, there are ongoing uncertainties about their relevance to disease variation in humans54. Although recent progress has been made in developing experimental models to demonstrate epigenetic inheritance in mammals, further research is still needed55.

Results from the present study and previous research by our group point to a highly favourable profile of parental risk factors, which are commonly implicated in the developmental mechanisms of intergenerational disease transmission38. For example, G2 offspring and G3 grandchildren in our cohort exhibit lowered risk of disease incidence and mortality due to mental and behavioural disorders, cardiovascular and endocrine diseases, and infections during adulthood38. Social stress, substance abuse, infections, and cardiometabolic disease have all been established as probable causative factors within the DOHaD framework56–59. Interestingly, measurable changes in common birth outcomes are not necessary for long-term physiological changes in response to adverse prenatal and early postnatal conditions60. From these perspectives, our findings suggest that the familial aggregation of exceptional longevity may have developmental origins as early as the perinatal period.

It is important to note that the socioeconomic and behavioural measures employed in our study are but a limited set of potentially relevant factors compared to the multi-dimensional constructs they represent. However, given the stability of our estimates after adjustment for the factors we can observe, which have well-documented importance for infant health outcomes, it may be unlikely that further gains from more granular measures of socioeconomic status and behaviour, operating through independent pathways, could fully explain our findings61–63. From this perspective, proximal socioeconomic and behavioural factors may only play a minor mediating role in the epidemiological mechanisms underlying the exceptional infant phenotype observed in our cohort. Instead, these patterns could triangulate to implicate direct effects of maternal and paternal health factors, or heritable biological factors, such as additive genetic effects. However, this does not constitute direct evidence; our inferences here may be subject to residual biases, and so further research is necessary. Moreover, this would not preclude an increasingly important role for socioeconomic and behavioural factors manifesting later in childhood and beyond.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively assess early-life health characteristics associated with familial aggregation of longevity15–18. Such efforts have previously not been feasible due to a lack of prospective cohorts or clinical birth registries with sufficient history of follow-up and familial linkage21. In our view, the patterns of transmission between generations G3 and G4 observed in our study may be informative about trends in early-life health characteristics of previous generations, which we were unable to directly observe. It is notable that the advantage reported in our study was still evident after at least three generations, and this phenotype likely represents a conservative portrayal of the G2 offspring and G1 probands in their unobserved perinatal and infant periods. In this way, extrapolations of our findings can aid in developing our scientific knowledge of early-life health trajectories more directly associated with phenotypic longevity. However, it remains unknown to what extent such exceptional infant health phenotypes predict familial aggregation of longevity within a single generation, and this would be an important avenue for future research, once methodologically feasible to do so.

This study had several important limitations that were not emphasized elsewhere. First, data on maternal smoking during pregnancy were missing for a large portion of the G3 generation. Thus, we could not adjust for this variable in our models and perform analyses assessing its mediating role across a variety of statistically important differences. Our inferences from these data were instead based on its use as an outcome, where a similar behavioural advantage in maternal smoking was observed in both generations, despite the observed dilution in the exceptional infant phenotype in G4. Moreover, smoking data for both generations were only available with binary classification, and therefore, our measure may have missed more granular differences, for example, in smoking duration and intensity, and trajectories of smoking cessation across the trimesters of pregnancy. Similar limitations apply to our socioeconomic measures, which represent but a limited set of potentially relevant factors and may be subject to residual bias, as described previously. We were also unable to assess mediating relationships between specific maternal and neonatal outcomes due to insufficient sample sizes, so our results at this level are only descriptive.

Although we carefully assessed the role of unobserved factors in the dilution of exceptional infant survival across successive generations (i.e., direct comparison analysis), the methodological approach we employed may be susceptible to residual biases. Lastly, we have previously shown potent health advantages across adulthood in G3, which provided the basis for our discussion of the exceptional G3 infant health phenotype observed here. However, no such data exists yet for the G4 great-grandchildren, due to their relatively young age and a lack of follow-up. Therefore, our discussion is limited with respect to the implications of the diluted G4 early-life phenotype for later health and survival across adulthood in this generation. We were also unable to assess other developmental milestones, and ultimately, the scope of our study was limited to early-life health characteristics in the first year of life, specifically. Both of these areas would be highly important avenues for future research on the familial aggregation of exceptional health and survival, once data and follow-up are available.

Methods

Ethical approval

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in the original in-person survey of longevity-enriched families, in which familial linkage was initially established. For register-based studies, data are fully de-identified by Statistics Denmark. The Danish Data Protection Act allows for the processing of personal data, including de-identified register data, without informed consent if carried out solely for the purpose of conducting statistical or scientific studies of societal importance (https://www.datatilsynet.dk/regler-og-vejledning/forskning-og-statistik). Therefore, no further informed consent beyond that obtained in the original survey was required for the present register-based follow-up study. The study has been approved by The Regional Scientific Ethical Committees for Southern Denmark (S-VF-20030227), The Danish Data Protection Agency (J.nr. 2008-41-1753), and University of Southern Denmark, Research & Innovation Organization (J.nr. 10.635).

Danish longevity-enriched families

In this study, we utilised a Danish cohort of 659 families selected for their familial aggregation of exceptional longevity38. The cohort was comprised of three consecutive sub-studies, including the Danish Oldest Siblings pilot study, the Genetics of Healthy Aging study, and the Danish participants of the National Institute on Aging’s Long Life Family Study (LLFS)13. These sibships were recruited on having two or more siblings that reached 88 ≥ years of age and were alive at the time of recruitment between 2004–2009, although 99.5% of these initial sibling pairs eventually reached at least 90 years of age. At least two siblings from each sibship were then interviewed to identify the names and birth dates of their parents (G0), other siblings (G1), offspring (G2), and siblings’ offspring (G2). These additional family members were then linked within the Civil Registration System (CRS) conditional on them being alive and living in Denmark at some point on or after April 2nd 1968. Interviews were essential for establishing familial linkage to the offspring generation (G2), due to scarce familial linkage in the Civil Registration System (CRS) for those born before 195364.

The 3rd generation grandchildren of the proband generation were ascertained solely through existing familial linkage within the CRS, which could be performed with minimal attrition38,65. In the present study, we extended the cohort through the same process to include the 4th generation (G4) great-grandchildren (See Fig. 1), using parental linkage with the G3 grandchildren. In collaboration with the LLFS, this larger cohort of 659 Danish families (78 of whom participate directly in LLFS) has been employed as an ancillary resource to further study epidemiological characteristics of exceptional ageing. Specifically, the cohort facilitates population-based follow-up of longevity-enriched families as well as comparisons with the general population through the system of national administrative, socioeconomic and clinical registries in Denmark.

Study design

We performed a matched cohort study in the Danish population between 1st January 1973 and 31st December 2018. The Danish Medical Birth Registry was used to identify and extract information on live births in Denmark since 197340. The Danish National Patient Registry was used to ascertain additional conditions associated with childbirth, based on diagnoses listed on inpatient hospitalisations since 197766. The Civil Registration System (CRS) was used to determine status across follow-up, including death and emigration65. Data on parental education history were obtained from the Population Education Register, with coverage exceeding 90% of the population67. Education levels were defined according to the 2011 International Standard Classification of Education and coded as the highest attained level between the mother and father in our analyses. Data on parental income were attained from the Danish Income and Tax Register since 1980, and were defined in our analyses as the mean gross annual income between ages 30–45 and then summed across linked parents in the Civil Registration System65.

Our source population included all live births in Denmark between 1st January 1973 and 31st December 2018 with complete data on parity, maternal birth date, and maternal country of birth. Live births from Danish longevity-enriched families were compared to demographically matched live births from the general population at a 1:2 ratio. Matching criteria included sex, birth year, birth season, maternal birth year, maternal parity and whether the mother was born in Denmark or abroad. Participants who could not be matched to controls on these criteria at the desired ratio were removed (see Sensitivity Analyses).

Outcomes

Our primary outcome was infant mortality, defined as death within the first 365 days of life observed in the Civil Registration System. Secondary neonatal outcomes included preterm birth, small for gestational age, large for gestational age, low Apgar score, birth trauma, neonatal respiratory disorders, congenital malformations, and other neonatal morbidity. We also assessed several maternal outcomes, including assisted delivery, cesarean section delivery, preeclampsia and eclampsia, placental disorders, hemorrhage, and other maternal morbidity. Outcomes for ‘other neonatal morbidity’ and ‘other maternal morbidity’ were included in our study to provide the broadest scope for assessing health differences and included all adverse conditions related to the pregnancy, delivery and health of the neonate outside of those listed above (see Supplementary Material Table S2).

Preterm birth was defined as delivery prior to the end of the 37th week of gestation. Small and large for gestational age were defined as belonging to below the 10th and above the 90th percentiles, respectively, of birth weights for gestational age calculated from intrauterine growth curves based on ultrasonically estimated fetal weights68. Low Apgar score was defined as a value of below 7, measured after 5 min following delivery. Outcomes based on gestational age, weight for gestational age, and Apgar scores were available since the initiation of the Medical Birth Registry in 197340. All remaining neonatal and maternal outcomes were based on inpatient diagnoses observed in the National Patient Registry and were only available since 1977. Neonatal events were defined as those occurring in the first 28 days of life, and maternal events as occurring at any point during pregnancy, delivery, or the puerperium (up to 6 weeks following delivery). Full ICD-8 (1973–1992) and ICD-10 (1993–2018) codes were used to define our outcomes, and further explanations are presented in the Supplementary Material (Table S2). ICD-9 was not used in our study due to a direct transition in Denmark from a modified version of ICD-8 to ICD-10 in 1993.

We also assessed differences in maternal smoking and parental education levels between longevity-enriched families and the general population, as indicators of behavioural and socioeconomic characteristics. Maternal smoking during pregnancy is likely a causal determinant of a range of adverse neonatal and infant health outcomes, and may also serve as a proxy for other behavioural traits associated with poor health42,69. Data on maternal smoking were only available since 1991 in the Medical Birth Registry and were classified as a binary variable indicating any amount of smoking in any of the trimesters of pregnancy, regardless of later cessation. Parental education data were obtained from the Population Education Register and coded with the following categories: ‘Primary or Lower Secondary’, ‘Upper Secondary’, ‘Short Cycle Tertiary’, ‘Bachelor or Equivalent’, ‘Master, Doctoral or Equivalent’.

Statistical analyses

Differences in outcomes between G3 grandchildren and G4 great-grandchildren of longevity-enriched sibships and matched controls were assessed by Cox proportional hazard, conditional logistic, multinomial and ordinal logistic regression models.

For analyses of infant mortality, matched sets were followed up until whichever came first out of death, emigration, 365 days after birth, or the end of the observed data and analysed by Cox regression models. For neonatal and maternal morbidities, as well as maternal smoking, outcomes were treated as binary and analysed by conditional logistic regression models. For parental education level, the outcome was treated as categorical and was analysed by multinomial and ordinal logistic regression models. All models accounted for the matching variables via stratification, and estimated robust standard errors clustered on maternal ID to account for several births involving the same mother. Multicollinearity in our models was assessed by estimating variance inflation factors (VIFs) and assessing correlations between all included covariates. For analyses of neonatal and maternal morbidities as secondary outcomes, we conducted two-sided Wald hypothesis tests adjusted for multiple testing via the Hommel method, which assumes that outcomes are non-negatively correlated (noutcomes = 14)70,71.

Differences in maternal or paternal lines of transmission of infant phenotypes were assessed via interaction models. Separate estimates were provided for subgroups where the mother or father was a descendant of a longevity-enriched sibship compared to a marry-in. In the presence of no interactions, this would suggest an equal role for maternal and paternal factors in the transmission of exceptional survival and health in the neonatal and infant periods. We apply this methodology to analyses of infant survival and maternal smoking, and also to analyses of overall neonatal and maternal morbidities in the Supplementary Material (see Sensitivity Analyses).

Where applicable, we also calculated separate estimates with further adjustment for socioeconomic factors, including the highest parental education level, total gross income and marital status, which are epidemiologically well-documented for being strong socioeconomic determinants of infant and later-life health outcomes61–63. In our context, adjusting for these variables was a test of the mediating role of socioeconomic factors in the effect of being a descendant of a longevity-enriched sibship. Therefore, our adjusted estimates represent the ‘direct effect’ of being a descendant of a longevity-enriched sibship, which is not otherwise mediated by differences in socioeconomic factors. Since income data were not available before 1980, differential measurement windows over time were controlled for in our analyses by previously matching on birth year and maternal age in stratified regression models, ensuring measurement windows were consistent within matched pairs.

Patterns of transmission of health and survival advantages across generations in longevity-enriched families were assessed by directly comparing G3 grandchildren and G4 grandchildren in calendar periods where these successive generations have overlapping birth cohorts (approx. 1973–2010). We performed regression analyses with statistical adjustment for the same covariates used as matching criteria when selecting general population controls. Since this included birth year, these analyses were robust to changes in secular trends in the background population over the same period. A range of supplementary analyses were conducted to assess the suitability and robustness of this approach (see Sensitivity Analyses).

All statistical analyses were conducted in the R Statistical Software (v4.2.3; R Core Team 2023). All confidence intervals and two-sided hypothesis tests were provided at 95% and 5% levels, respectively. In addition to relative measures, absolute differences (AD) in unadjusted risks were presented where possible, such as in measures of cumulative survival at the end of follow-up, and analyses based on logistic regression models.

Sensitivity analyses

We performed several sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of our results. First, we assessed the impact of including children who were unable to be matched initially, by weaking their matching criteria and then appending them to our fully matched data and repeating key analyses. We then assessed longer windows of opportunity for the diagnosis of congenital malformations. We also examined mean differences in continuous rather than dichotomized outcomes where possible. Outcomes with incomplete data were analysed to determine if their observations were missing at random and, if so, the direction of potential biases. We assessed robustness to adjustment for paternal country of birth and age at conception. For outcomes based on birth weight, we evaluated more granular subgroups, for example, very and extremely small for gestational age and low birth weight in general. To capture broad patterns in neonatal and maternal morbidities, we assessed health advantages based on various composite scores comprised of the individual neonatal and maternal endpoints included in our study. We also assessed the robustness of our results to adjustment for additional socioeconomic measures, including municipal urban density, parental occupation, and length of education. In analyses adjusting for income, we also compared measurements based on gross versus net income, as well as breadwinner and total income. When adjusting for parental income and education, we also individually assessed the effect of adjusting for specific paternal and maternal measurements of these covariates. To assess the risk of overfitting in our models, we repeated key analyses using penalised Cox proportional hazard regression models with 10-fold cross-validation.

Lastly, we assessed the robustness of our ‘direct comparison’ methodology via the use of several negative control analyses. These analyses tested whether our results were biased by the known secular trends of improved neonatal outcomes and infant survival throughout the study period. First, as described in the statistical analyses section, we directly compared G3 grandchildren to G4 great-grandchildren in calendar periods where these successive generations have overlapping birth cohorts (approx. 1973–2010). These analyses statistically adjusted for the same criteria used in our previous matched analyses, including birth year, birth season, sex, maternal age and parity. We then compared G3 matched controls to G4 matched controls using the same models, where, if they adequately adjust for the confounding induced by matching (e.g., different distributions of maternal age and parity), no differences in survival should be observed. Several other comparisons were also conducted for completeness. More information on all these analyses can be found in Section 3 of the Supplementary Material.
