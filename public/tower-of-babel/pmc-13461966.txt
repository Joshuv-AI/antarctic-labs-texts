Successful oocyte transport through the oviduct does not depend on the longitudinal alignment of oviduct epithelial folds
Authors: Fumiko Matsukawa Usami, Masaki Arata, Kagayaki Kato, Dongbo Shi, Sanae Oka, Yingzi Yang, Toshihiko Fujimori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceLongitudinal folds of the oviduct epithelium are conserved across vertebrates and are thought to promote cilia-driven oocyte transport, yet their functional requirement is untested. Here, we found that oocytes reach the uterus in Vangl1 mutant oviducts in which longitudinal fold alignment is disrupted, whereas ciliary orientation is largely preserved, indicating that straight fold alignment is not strictly required for uterine-directed transport. Moreover, our results suggest that ciliary orientation can remain aligned with the ovary–uterus axis through mechanisms that are at least partly independent of canonical core planar cell polarity (PCP) protein polarity. These findings disentangle the roles of longitudinal fold alignment and ciliary orientation in oocyte transport and offer a perspective on how these polarities are coordinated within a single epithelium.

Tubular structures are fundamental architectural motifs in various organs, enabling transport of fluids, gases, or solid substances to specific locations. The topology of luminal surfaces of these organs varies widely and is linked to specific organ functions, such as mixing and absorption of nutrients in the intestine (1).

The oviduct is one such tubular organ that connects to the uterus and opens near the ovary. Ovulated oocytes enter the oviduct through the opening end and are subsequently transported toward the uterus (2). The luminal surface of the distal region of the oviduct, including the infundibulum and ampulla, is lined by an epithelium that exhibits folds running along the ovary–uterus (O–U) axis (3, 4). These longitudinal folds are conserved across a broad range of vertebrate species, including mammals, amphibians, and birds (5–9). However, physiological roles of these folds remain unclear.

The epithelium of the oviductal infundibulum is composed of multiciliated cells (MCCs) and secretory cells (SCCs). Each MCC forms approximately 200 motile cilia on its apical surface (10), and the beating direction of individual cilia is uniformly aligned along the O–U axis (3, 10, 11). Loss of MCCs or disrupted ciliary beating orientation is associated with female infertility (3, 12, 13), indicating that unidirectional ciliary beating provides a driving force for transporting oocytes to the uterus.

One proposed function of oviduct longitudinal folds is that they increase the probability of contact between oocytes and the ciliated epithelial surface, thereby facilitating efficient oocyte transport (14, 15). In addition, the longitudinal orientation of the folds may provide hydrodynamic advantages for oocyte transport. Since the oviduct is filled with luminal fluid, oocyte movement toward the uterus can transiently elevate intraluminal pressure, which would tend to push oocytes back toward the ovary. Such pressure buildup may be mitigated by interfold spaces that allow luminal fluid to be redistributed (15). However, in the chicken uterus, a domain of the oviduct, the direction of particle/egg transport is rather perpendicular to the fold orientation (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and A’ and Movies S1 and S2), raising the possibility that longitudinal folds are not universally required to direct oocyte or zygote transport.

Planar cell polarity (PCP) is a unidirectional alignment of cellular asymmetry within the plane of an epithelial sheet (16–22), exemplified by ciliary beating and cell elongation along the O–U axis in the oviduct. Core PCP proteins including the atypical cadherin CELSR1, the four-pass transmembrane protein VANGL1, and the WNT receptor FZD6 are enriched at circumferential cell–cell boundaries in the oviduct (3, 23). Such polarized localization of core proteins is a prerequisite for PCP establishment in many developing epithelia across species (16, 19, 24, 25). We previously found that mice lacking a seven-pass transmembrane atypical cadherin CELSR1 are infertile and epithelial folds of their oviducts are less straight and severely branched compared with wild-type oviducts (3). However, because Celsr1 mutant oviducts exhibited misoriented ciliary beating and discontinuities in tubal architecture (3, 10), either of which could impair oocyte transport (3, 10), we could not judge whether longitudinal folds are required for oocyte transport to the uterus.

Although CELSR1 is required for establishing multiple polarities in the oviduct (3), molecular mechanisms and causal relationships among these polarities are unresolved, limiting direct tests of the physiological roles of longitudinal folds. In this study, we searched for manipulations that specifically affect fold structures and found that in Vangl1 mutant mice, longitudinal folds were disorganized, while ciliary beating orientations and tubal architecture remained largely intact. Since the female mouse with the mutant allele is fertile, these observations suggest that longitudinal fold alignment is not strictly required for the successful oocyte transport to the uterus. Taking advantage of this genetic uncoupling between ciliary orientation, fold structures, and tubal architecture, we further examined the mechanisms underlying fold morphogenesis. Three-dimensional cell-shape analysis revealed a loss of O–U–oriented cell elongation in Vangl1 mutant oviducts, providing in vivo support for previous theoretical prediction that cell elongation promotes longitudinal fold formation. In addition, the polarized distribution of core proteins was largely maintained in Vangl1 mutant oviducts, but their polarity axes were not consistent with ciliary beating orientation, fold orientation, or the O–U axis. Together, these findings suggest that fold orientation and ciliary orientation are differentially regulated and that cilia can be oriented along the O–U axis by mechanisms that are at least partly independent of canonical core PCP polarity.

Results

Epithelial Folds Are Disorganized While Ciliary Beating Orientation Remains Intact in Vangl1gt/gt Oviducts.

The Vangl1gt/gt mouse strain is a gene trap mutant that markedly reduces VANGL1 protein levels (25, 26). To determine how VANGL1 loss affects planar organization in the oviductal infundibulum (hereafter referred to as the “oviduct”), we first examined epithelial fold architecture. In contrast to the straight longitudinal folds in wild-type oviducts (Fig. 1A), folds in Vangl1gt/gt oviducts failed to extend straight along the longitudinal axis of the tube and instead showed extensive branching (Fig. 1B), reminiscent of Celsr1 mutant oviducts (3). We quantitatively evaluated epithelial tissue structures using the Fiji plug-in, OrientationJ (27). OrientationJ calculates the local intensity gradient around each pixel and returns orientation and coherency indices, which represent the dominant structure direction and the degree of anisotropy, respectively. Coherency ranges from 0 to 1, which becomes low around nonunidirectional features such as branch points and fold inflections. In Vangl1gt/gt oviducts, orientation values were more variable (Fig. 1 A′, B′, C, and D′), and coherency was lower than in wild-type (Fig. 1E), indicating that straight longitudinal folds were disrupted in the mutant.

We next examined ciliary beating orientations. Since ciliary beating was misoriented in Celsr1 mutant oviducts (3, 10), we expected a similar defect in Vangl1gt/gt oviducts. However, high-speed imaging revealed that ciliary beating orientations were largely aligned with the O–U axis in Vangl1gt/gt oviducts, even in regions where folds were oriented circumferentially (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and B′ and Movies S3 and S4). To rigorously validate this observation, we next quantified ciliary orientation in individual MCCs by measuring the relative position of γ-TUBULIN and CEP128 immunofluorescent signals. Ciliary beating orientation corresponds to the relative position of the basal body and its laterally associated structure, the basal foot, both localized at the base of each cilium [Fig. 2 A and A′; (11, 28, 29)]. CEP128 and γ-TUBULIN mark the base and tip of the basal foot, respectively [Fig. 2A′; (30)]. The positional shifts between CEP128 and γ-TUBULIN signals were quantified using PIV (particle image velocimetry)-based image analysis and visualized as arrows (PIV vectors) in Fig. 2A, whose lengths and angles correspond to distance and relative orientation between the two signals (see Materials and Methods for details). The mean and CV of these PIV vector angles for each cell were used as indices of ciliary orientation and intracellular coordination, respectively. We confirmed that this PIV-based quantification closely matched manual measurements (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 C and C′). The CVs of PIV vectors in Vangl1gt/gt cells were comparable to those in wild-type cells, indicating that cilia orientations were normally coordinated within individual Vangl1gt/gt MCCs (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B). Although the mean PIV vector angles of individual MCCs were more broadly distributed in Vangl1gt/gt than in wild-type (P < 0.01, Watson-Wheeler test), they remained biased toward the O–U axis (Fig. 2 C–F). Moreover, ciliary orientation did not correlate with epithelial fold orientation (Fig. 2G).

In summary, in Vangl1gt/gt oviducts, the O–U–oriented ciliary beating was maintained, whereas folding patterns were disorganized. This prompted us to test whether longitudinal folds are required for the oviduct’s uterine-directed transport ability.

Oocyte Transport Is Successful in Vangl1gt/gt mutant Oviducts.

As previously reported (26), Vangl1gt/gt mice were able to produce pups under our breeding conditions regardless of their age (Fig. 3A), indicating that oocyte/embryo transport to the uterus can occur despite disrupted longitudinal fold alignment. To directly assess effects of longitudinal folds on uterine-directed transport, we applied fluorescent beads to the exposed lumen of longitudinally opened oviducts and compared their movements between wild-type and Vangl1gt/gt. Bead trajectories in Vangl1gt/gt oviducts were less straight along the O–U axis than in wild-type, but remained oriented toward the uterus (Fig. 3 B and B′ and, Movies S5 and S6). To quantitatively evaluate bead trajectories, we measured bead velocity and movement angles between consecutive movie frames. Bead velocity was lower in Vangl1gt/gt than in wild-type (Fig. 3C), and bead movement angles were more broadly distributed (Fig. 3 D and D′; P < 0.01, Watson-Wheeler test). Nevertheless, angles of bead movement remained biased toward the uterus in both genotypes (Fig. 3 D and D′). These results suggest that uterine-directed transport can occur even when fold alignment is disrupted.

Cell Elongation Was Abrogated in Vangl1gt/gt Oviducts.

In contrast to the largely intact alignment of ciliary beating orientation, epithelial folds were markedly disorganized in Vangl1gt/gt oviducts. This phenotype suggests that ciliary orientation and epithelial folding are regulated by distinct mechanisms, and that the Vangl1gt/gt mutant provides a useful system to dissect how epithelial fold structures and ciliary orientation are controlled.

A previous theoretical study proposed that cell shape elongation along the O–U axis generates tissue tension anisotropy, thereby aligning epithelial folds along the O–U axis (31). Unlike Celsr1 mutants, Vangl1gt/gt mutants did not exhibit defects in overall tubal architecture (SI Appendix, Fig. S3), allowing us to investigate mechanisms underlying longitudinal fold formation independently of gross structural defects. We therefore quantified cell shapes in Vangl1gt/gt oviducts.

In our earlier study, cell shape in the oviduct was analyzed using two-dimensional projections of apical cell-boundary signals (3). However, because the oviduct epithelium is inherently three-dimensional, such projections may hinder accurate evaluation of cell morphology, particularly when comparing samples that differ substantially in epithelial geometry. To overcome this limitation, we analyzed cell shapes in three dimensions. ZO-1–labeled apical cell edges (Fig. 4 A and A′ and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and A′) were segmented in three-dimensional datasets, and the aspect ratio and long-axis orientation of each cell were measured in wild-type and Vangl1gt/gt oviducts. To this end, we applied principal component analysis (PCA) to the voxel coordinates of each cell’s apical edge. We defined the plane spanned by the first and second principal components as the cell’s apical plane, and we used the first and second principal components as the cell’s long and short axes, respectively. The aspect ratio was defined as the length of the long axis divided by that of the short axis. In Vangl1gt/gt oviducts, the aspect ratio was smaller than in wild-type oviducts (Fig. 4B), indicating reduced elongation. Moreover, long-axis orientations were not aligned with the O–U axis in Vangl1gt/gt (Fig. 4 C and C′), suggesting that cell-shape PCP was lost. Taken together, these results support—though do not by themselves prove—the theoretical prediction that cell elongation contributes to epithelial fold alignment along the O–U axis.

Core PCP Proteins Remain Polarized but Are Misaligned With Ciliary Orientation in Vangl1gt/gt Oviducts.

Although cell-shape PCP was lost, ciliary beating orientation remained largely intact in Vangl1gt/gt oviducts. One possible explanation for this phenotype is that remaining core PCP proteins retain polarized distributions and provide a directional cue for ciliary orientation, but are insufficient to drive cell elongation along the O–U axis. To test this, we immunostained oviducts for CELSR1 and FZD6 and compared their distributions between wild-type and Vangl1gt/gt oviducts. In wild-type cells, CELSR1 and FZD6 were asymmetrically distributed at cell boundaries, with polarization biased along the O–U axis (Fig. 5 A and A′ and SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). In contrast, in Vangl1gt/gt cells, both proteins remained polarized at cell boundaries, but their polarity axes were not consistently biased along the O–U axis, although polarity directions appeared to be coordinated locally between adjacent cells (Fig. 5 B and B′ and SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). To further assess whether core PCP proteins still contribute to coordinating cilia orientation in Vangl1gt/gt oviducts, we analyzed the relationship between the direction of CELSR1 enrichment and ciliary orientation in individual MCCs. Contrary to our expectation, in Vangl1gt/gt oviducts, the cilia of most MCCs were oriented toward the uterus, whereas CELSR1-enriched cell boundaries displayed more variable orientation (representative data are shown in Fig. 6 A–C). We found that the direction of CELSR1 enrichment did not consistently coincide with ciliary orientation (Fig. 6 B–F), the long axis of a cell or the orientation of epithelial folds (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Together, these results indicate that although polarized distributions of core PCP proteins are maintained in Vangl1gt/gt oviducts, they neither provide a directional cue for ciliary orientation nor drive cell elongation. These findings further suggest that cilia orientation might be redundantly regulated by both core-protein–dependent and core-protein–independent mechanisms, whereas cell elongation depends primarily on core PCP proteins.

Discussion

The oviduct is an essential organ in animal reproduction, where oocytes and zygotes are transported toward the uterus. Because O–U–oriented epithelial folds in the infundibulum of the oviduct are conserved across animal species, it has been speculated that they contribute to the successful transport of oocytes to the uterus. However, this possibility has been difficult to test directly because manipulations that preferentially disrupt epithelial folds have been lacking. Here, by analyzing Vangl1gt/gt oviducts in which longitudinal folds are disorganized while O–U–oriented ciliary beating remains largely intact, we provide genetic evidence that straight longitudinal folds are not strictly required for successful uterine-directed transport.

Although uterine-directed transport can occur despite disrupted fold alignment, our bead-transport assay suggests that it contributes to transport efficiency. Fluorescent beads on the luminal surface of Vangl1gt/gt oviducts exhibited less straight trajectories and reduced velocities compared with wild-type. Two nonmutually exclusive factors may underlie this decrease. First, although ciliary beating was coordinated within individual MCCs, ciliary orientations across MCCs were more broadly distributed in Vangl1gt/gt oviducts, which could reduce net, tissue-scale transport. Second, the branched and locally misoriented folds in Vangl1gt/gt epithelia can act as physical obstacles: beads were occasionally trapped at branch points, consistent with disrupted fold geometry hindering bead movement along the O–U axis. One possible explanation for the apparent discrepancy between local bead trapping and the ability of Vangl1gt/gt females to produce offspring is the difference in size between fluorescent beads and the cumulus–oocyte complex (COC) transported in the oviduct. The 15-µm beads which we used in the experiments may be more susceptible to local trapping at fold branch points or regions with locally misoriented ciliary flow. By contrast, COCs are much larger and may receive ciliary forces over a broader epithelial surface, thereby reducing the impact of small local defects in fold geometry or ciliary orientation. However, we cannot exclude more subtle effects on transport efficiency, embryo passage timing, or reproductive performance over the full reproductive lifespan.

Along the O–U axis, the oviduct is regionalized into the infundibulum, ampulla, and isthmus, with prominent longitudinal folds in the infundibulum and ampulla, and predominantly circumferential folds in the isthmus (4). The circumferential orientation of folds in the isthmus may be related to the distinct transport environment of this region, where smooth muscle activity is thought to substantially contribute to transport (12, 14). However, the functional significance of this fold orientation remains to be tested. Beyond transport, epithelial architecture is likely to influence tissue homeostasis by shaping local microenvironments for stem cell maintenance and epithelial turnover (32). Previous lineage-tracing and label-retention studies have revealed that epithelial turnover in the mouse oviduct is not uniform along the O–U axis (33–36). Label-retaining cells persist specifically in the distal/upstream regions [infundibular/ampullary, (35, 36)], suggesting that epithelial architecture is maintained under distinct turnover regimes in different oviduct regions. Vangl1gt/gt mice, which disrupt fold organization, provide an opportunity to test how fold geometry contributes to epithelial maintenance and renewal in vivo.

Our results also help disentangle the mechanisms that establish PCP in the oviduct. It has been proposed that, during PCP establishment, a global cue generates a weak bias in cell-edge distributions of core proteins along the body axis, and that this bias is amplified by feedback interactions among core proteins (16, 20). In contrast to a typical observation that a loss of a core protein abolishes the biased distribution of the remaining core proteins at cell edges (3), the cell-edge distribution of CELSR1 remained polarized in Vangl1gt/gt oviducts. Moreover, the orientation of CELSR1 polarity appeared consistent between neighboring cells, yet it was not aligned with the O–U axis, reminiscent of tissue lacking a global cue (37). Because mice have VANGL2, a paralog of VANGL1, residual VANGL2 may be sufficient for sustaining feedback interaction and assembling the asymmetric complex. The loss of global coordination of core protein polarity may indicate that VANGL1 or a threshold level of VANGL proteins is required to sense, transmit, and/or generate global cues. Alternatively, defects in tissue architecture and cell geometry may prevent the rectification of core protein distributions (38). Although we focused on the oviduct, PCP-related genes have also been implicated in the morphogenesis of other tubular organs, including the lung. Core PCP proteins have been reported to regulate lung branching morphogenesis, and recent tissue-specific analyses suggest that mesenchymal VANGL1/2 contributes to airway elongation and distal sacculation (39–41). Thus, PCP components may regulate tubular architecture in multiple organs, although the tissue layers and mechanisms involved may differ from those controlling longitudinal fold alignment in the oviduct.

In wild-type oviducts, the orientations of cell elongation and cilia are consistent with the axis of planar-polarized core protein distribution. Because loss of CELSR1 disrupts both cell elongation and ciliary orientation, it has been proposed that polarized distributions of core proteins provide directional cues for elongating cells and orienting cilia, although underlying molecular mechanisms remain unclear. In Vangl1gt/gt oviducts, cell elongation was reduced and the long axis of cells was not aligned with core-protein enriched cell edges, suggesting that core proteins require VANGL1 to drive cell elongation. Likewise, ciliary orientation did not coincide with the orientation of core-protein-enriched cell boundaries. Nevertheless, ciliary orientation remained coordinated within each cell and aligned with the O–U axis in the mutant. These observations suggest that core-protein-dependent mechanisms require VANGL1 to coordinate ciliary orientation, and that a core-protein-independent mechanism might act redundantly to orient cilia along the O–U axis. One candidate core-protein-independent mechanism is luminal fluid flow: in adult mouse oviducts, luminal fluid flows from the uterus toward the ovary (42), and flow has been shown to coordinate ciliary orientation in cultured airway epithelia (43, 44), ependymal cells (45), and Xenopus skin (46). Notably, Celsr1 mutant oviducts often exhibit discontinuities in tubal structure (3), which could disrupt organ-scale flow and thereby compromise flow-based alignment mechanisms. Together, these observations support a model in which core-protein–dependent and flow-dependent mechanisms act in parallel to establish and/or maintain O–U–oriented ciliary PCP in the oviduct.

Our results suggest that oocyte transport can be achieved even when fold alignment is compromised, provided that O–U–oriented ciliary PCP is maintained. Ciliary PCP appears to be regulated by partially redundant mechanisms, but the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Developing experimental strategies to independently manipulate luminal flow and core PCP protein activity with minimal perturbation to tissue architecture will be useful for elucidating the mechanisms that regulate ciliary orientation and for determining how these parallel inputs coordinate ciliary beating to drive uterine-directed transport. More broadly, these approaches should extend to other ciliated epithelia, where organ-scale transport must be maintained despite variability in tissue geometry.

Materials and Methods

Mouse.

Female mice (Vangl1gt/gt and Slc; ICR, Japan SLC) were used. Details of Vangl1gt/gt mice are described in (26). Animal care and experiments were conducted following the Guidelines of Animal Experimentation of National Institutes for Natural Sciences. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Research Committee of National Institutes for Natural Sciences. Animals were maintained in a light- and temperature-controlled room using a 12 h:12 h light:dark cycle at 23 ± 2 °C.

Immunohistochemistry of Mouse Oviduct.

Oviducts were dissected and opened longitudinally along the O–U axis. The opened oviducts were fixed either in 4% PFA in PBS (–) at 4 °C overnight or in methanol at –30 °C for 45 min and then in acetone at –30 °C for 1 min. After washing, samples were incubated with Blocking One (Nacalai Tesque, 03953-95) at room temperature for 30 min. Samples were then incubated with primary antibodies (see SI Appendix, Table S1) at 4 °C overnight. Following primary antibodies were purchased; anti-γ-Tubulin (Sigma-Aldrich, T6557) and anti-FZD6 antibodies (R&D systems, AF1526). Anti-CEP128 was a gift from Dr. Chiba (47), Anti-E-CADHERIN was a gift from Dr. Takeichi (48). Anti-ZO-1 was a gift from Dr. Itoh and Dr. Furuse (49). After washing, samples were incubated with secondary antibodies (1/500, see SI Appendix, Table S1), phalloidin (1/500, see SI Appendix, Table S1), and/or Hoechst 33258 (1/25,000; Molecular Probes; H3569), diluted in Blocking One (Nacalai Tesque, 03953-95) at 4 °C overnight. After a final series of washes, samples were mounted in Fluoromount G (SouthernBiotech, 0100-01) or in ProLong™ Glass Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen, P36982) for cilia orientation analysis. All washing steps consisted of three brief rinses followed by three 10-min washes with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS (–) at room temperature. Images were acquired using a Nikon ECLIPSE Ti2 microscope equipped with CSU-W1 SoRa superresolution spinning disk confocal unit (Yokogawa).

Quantification of Epithelial Fold Structures.

Images of oviducts stained for ZO-1 were acquired at 2 μm Z intervals and then background signals were subtracted using “Subtract background” function in Fiji (rolling radius = 50 pixels). Subsequently, consecutive 24 images in each Z stack were selected and reconstructed into two-dimensional data by max intensity projection (MIP). These MIP images were denoised with gaussian filter using Gaussian Blur function in Fiji (σ = 1 pixel) and then processed with OrientationJ (27) in Fiji with Cubic Spline gradient and a local window of σ = 20 pixels, which approximately corresponds to fold widths. OrientationJ calculates the orientation, coherency, and energy of the intensity gradient in a local window centered around each pixel of an image. The orientation represents the dominant direction of structures within a local window. The coherency, which is a measure of the anisotropy of the gradient, takes values from 0 to 1. When the coherency is high, structures are well aligned in a specific orientation within a local window.

Since the image contained background or interfold pixels, we excluded these pixels based on their energy values, which represents the magnitude of intensity gradient. Specifically, within each manually defined region of interest (ROI), we selected pixels with energy values above the 25th percentile. ROIs were drawn to avoid areas in which epithelial structures appeared to be affected by dissection. Orientation values were then corrected by the angle of the manually defined O–U axis within each ROI. The resulting orientation and coherency values of the selected pixels were compared between wild-type and Vangl1gt/gt oviducts.

Analysis of Ciliary Beating Activities.

Green fluorescence beads (FluoSpheres Polystyrene Microspheres, 15 µm; Invitrogen, no. F21010) were applied to longitudinally opened oviducts in PBS, and movies were captured using a CCD camera (DP72; Olympus) with a stereomicroscope (MZFLIII; Leica for mouse samples and M165FC; Leica for chicken samples) at 1-s intervals. Beads’ movements on wild-type and Vangl1gt/gt oviducts were recorded at the same magnification. The trajectory of each bead was tracked using the Fiji plugin, TrackMate (50). Bead movement angles and velocity were calculated by measuring the displacement of each bead between two consecutive frames of the movie. We excluded beads’ movements slower than 1 pixel per frame from the analysis shown in Fig. 3 C, D, and D′, because these movements may yield unreliable measurements.

Chicken tissues were purchased from the NAGOYA UNIVERSITY through the National Bio-Resource Project of the MEXT, Japan.

Analysis of Ciliary Orientations.

To capture ciliary beating angles, oviducts were opened longitudinally and mounted on a glass bottom dish (MatTek; P35G-1.5-10-C) with a small volume of PBS. Movies were acquired at 400 frames/s using an upright microscope (BX70 Olympus) equipped with a digital high-speed camera (HAS-220; Ditect). The camera was loaded to the microscope using Olympus U-TV01X-2 and U-CMAD3 adaptors and imaging was performed with an Olympus UPLSAPO40XS objective.

Immunofluorescence images of γ-TUBULIN, CEP128, and E-CADHERIN were used to measure cilia orientations. Immunostained oviducts were mounted on slides with ProLong™ Glass Antifade Mountant (ThermoFisher Scientific, P36982) and imaged using a superresolution spinning disk confocal unit (CSU-W1 SoRa, Yokogawa) and Nikon Plan Apo VC 60x/1.40 Oil ∞/0.17 WD 0.13 DIC N2 objective. Captured γ-TUBULIN and CEP128 signals were deconvoluted using Nikon NIS elements and then γ-TUBULIN, CEP128 signals, and E-CADHERIN signals were two-dimensionally projected using Local Z Projector in Fiji (51). E-CADHERIN signals were processed with Cellpose2 to segment individual cells, and γ-TUBULIN and CEP128 signals from each MCC were extracted. Displacements between γ-TUBULIN and CEP128 signals were then analyzed using the iterative PIV plugin in Fiji, treating CEP128 and γ-TUBULIN images as if they were sequential time points.

Cell Shape Analysis in 3D.

Image preprocessing and cell-boundary extraction.

ZO-1–stained channel images were first subjected to an eight-neighbor median filter to reduce noise. Subsequently, Gaussian smoothing with a SD of (σ = 1) was applied to suppress high-frequency noise while preserving fine structural features. To compensate for anisotropic resolution along the z-axis, the acquired volumetric data were interpolated in the z direction using the Lanczos-5 method, resulting in a three-dimensional image with isotropic voxel size. Next, contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE) was applied to enhance the visibility of cell boundaries. This procedure corrected for uneven illumination and intensity inhomogeneity while enhancing the local contrast of ZO-1 signals originating from cell–cell junctions. Following preprocessing, the images were binarized using an automatically determined threshold based on Otsu’s method, and cell-boundary regions were extracted.

Identification of the cell-boundary network.

The extracted cell-boundary regions were subjected to three-dimensional skeletonization, converting the boundary structures into one-voxel-wide line representations. From the resulting skeletonized images, points at which multiple cell boundaries intersected were identified as vertices. The detected vertices and their connectivity were represented as a graph structure. Individual cell boundaries were then identified by applying Dijkstra’s shortest-path algorithm to this graph. This approach enabled the unique extraction of closed contours corresponding to individual cells from the entire cell-boundary network.

Definition and quantification of cell-shape features.

To quantitatively characterize cell shape, PCA was applied to the three-dimensional voxel coordinates of each cell boundary extracted from ZO-1–labeled apical cell junctions. Specifically, for each cell i, a set of voxel coordinates defining the cell boundary was obtained, and PCA was performed on this point cloud. The first principal component (PC1) was defined as the long axis of the cell shape, whereas the second principal component (PC2) was defined as the short axis. Cell elongation was quantified using an aspect ratio, defined as the ratio of the length of the PC1 axis to that of the PC2 axis (PC1/PC2). Larger values of this ratio indicate more elongated cell shapes.

In addition, the spatial orientation of the PC1 vector was used to determine the orientation of the cell’s long axis. This orientation was expressed as the angle relative to the O–U axis, which was defined by visual inspection. Using this metric, both the directionality of cell elongation and its alignment at the tissue level were evaluated.

Image processing and quantitative analyses were performed using custom-written programs implemented in C.

Quantitative Analysis of Polarity in Core Protein Distributions At Cell Edges.

To segment cell edges, F-ACTIN or E- CADHERIN signals were sequentially processed using Local Z Projector in Fiji (51), Cellpose2 (52), and custom Fiji macros. The segmented cell edges were then skeletonized using the “Skeletonize” function in Fiji. Together with the skeletonized cell edges, CELSR1 immunofluorescent images were processed using QuantifyPolarity v2.1 (53) to quantitatively evaluate CELSR1 distributions along cell edges. The PCA method provided by QuantifyPolarity was used to determine the angle of polarity, which represents the orientation of CELSR1-enriched cell edges within each cell.

Statistical Analysis.

CVs, means, and correlation coefficients for angular variables were calculated using the circular package (version 0.5.0) in R. Differences in angular distributions were assessed using the Watson–Wheeler test with the watson.wheeler.test function in the circular package (version 0.5.0) in R. Anderson–Darling test was performed using the ad_test function in the twosamples package (version 2.0.1) in R. Wilcoxon rank-sum test was performed using the wilcox.exact function in the exactRankTests (version 0.8.35) in R.
