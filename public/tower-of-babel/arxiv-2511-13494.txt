Language-Guided Invariance Probing of Vision–Language Models Journal: Nuclear Physics B
Jae Joong Lee

Abstract
Recent vision–language models (VLMs) such as CLIP, OpenCLIP, EVA02-CLIP and SigLIP achieve strong zero-shot performance, but it is unclear how reliably they respond to controlled linguistic perturbations. We introduce Language-Guided Invariance Probing (LGIP), a benchmark that measures (i) invariance to meaning-preserving paraphrases and (ii) sensitivity to meaning-changing semantic flips in image–text matching. Using 40k MS COCO images with five human captions each, we automatically generate paraphrases and rule-based flips that alter object category, color or count, and summarize model behavior with an invariance error, a semantic sensitivity gap and a positive-rate statistic. Across nine VLMs, EVA02-CLIP and large OpenCLIP variants lie on a favorable invariance–sensitivity frontier, combining low paraphrase-induced variance with consistently higher scores for original captions than for their flipped counterparts. In contrast, SigLIP and SigLIP 2 show much larger invariance error and often prefer flipped captions to the human descriptions, especially for object and color edits. These failures are largely invisible to standard retrieval metrics, indicating that LGIP provides a model-agnostic diagnostic for the linguistic robustness of VLMs beyond conventional accuracy scores.

Introduction

Vision–language models (VLMs) such as CLIP
[
6
]
, OpenCLIP
[
1
]
, EVA02-CLIP
[
8
]
and SigLIP
[
12
]
have become the standard backbone for zero-shot recognition, retrieval and many downstream multimodal tasks. By aligning images and texts in a shared embedding space, they deliver strong performance on conventional benchmarks without task-specific fine-tuning. Yet these evaluations say little about a basic question: how does a VLM behave when we
only
change the wording of the text, while keeping the image fixed?

From a pattern recognition perspective, two properties are important. A robust image–text encoder should exhibit
linguistic invariance
, remaining stable under meaning-preserving paraphrases, and
semantic sensitivity
, strongly penalizing edits that change key attributes such as object category, color or count. In practice, however, most benchmarks collapse these aspects into a single retrieval or classification score dominated by coarse category alignment. As a result, it is hard to tell whether a model is overfitting to surface form, under-reacting to semantic edits or failing on specific perturbation types.

We address this gap with
Language-Guided Invariance Probing
(LGIP), a simple diagnostic protocol for the linguistic robustness of VLMs. LGIP is used MS COCO
[
2
]
, using 40k images with five human-written captions each. For every image–caption pair, we automatically generate (1) paraphrases that preserve the core semantics while varying style and framing, and (2) semantic flips that systematically alter object, color or count. We then further study a frozen VLM with the image and all text variants, and summarize its behavior with an
invariance error
that captures similarity variance under paraphrasing and a
semantic sensitivity
score and positive rate that capture how reliably original captions outrank their flipped counterparts.

A comparative study of nine VLMs reveals a sharp split in this LGIP space. CLIP and large OpenCLIP models, and in particular EVA02-CLIP, trace a favorable frontier: they show low paraphrase-induced variance while consistently down-weighting flipped captions across object, color and count edits. In contrast, SigLIP-family models, which are a SOTA for zero-shot classification, show much
higher invariance error
and often assign higher similarity to flipped captions than to the original human descriptions, especially for attribute-level changes. These limitations remain largely invisible under standard zero-shot benchmarks, yet they are critical for applications that rely on prompts, captions or instructions with diverse but semantically equivalent phrasing.

LGIP is intentionally lightweight. It uses only existing caption corpora and rule-based perturbations, requires no access to model internals and applies uniformly across architectures and training recipes. Despite this simplicity, it exposes systematic, model-specific weaknesses in linguistic invariance and semantic grounding.

Our contributions are threefold:

1.

We propose
Language-Guided Invariance Probing
(LGIP), a benchmark and pipeline to evaluate VLM robustness to meaning-preserving paraphrases and meaning-changing semantic flips in image–text similarity space.

2.

We apply LGIP to MS-COCO, generating paraphrases and attribute-based flips (object, color and count) automatically, and define simple metrics that disentangle invariance error from semantic sensitivity and positive rate.

3.

We provide an empirical analysis of nine popular VLMs, showing that EVA02-CLIP and large OpenCLIP variants achieve a favorable invariance–sensitivity trade-off, while SigLIP-family models shows attribute-level failures that
standard benchmarks do not capture.

2
Related Work

Vision–Language Pretraining

Large-scale vision–language models (VLMs) such as CLIP learn joint image–text representations by contrastive pretraining on web-scale image–caption pairs
[
6
]
. Subsequent work has explored scaling trends and open-source training recipes, notably OpenCLIP, which studies reproducible scaling laws on LAION-5B and releases a family of public CLIP models
[
1
]
. EVA-CLIP further improves the CLIP training recipe with stronger image/text towers and optimized augmentation and optimization strategies, achieving state-of-the-art zero-shot performance at a given compute budget
[
8
]
. Beyond the standard softmax-based contrastive loss, SigLIP replaces the normalized softmax with a pairwise sigmoid loss, decoupling the objective from global batch statistics and enabling both larger batch sizes and more efficient training
[
12
]
. SigLIP 2 extends this recipe with captioning-based pretraining, self-supervised losses, and online data curation, yielding multilingual encoders with improved localization and dense features
[
10
]
. Our study takes these pretrained VLMs as fixed backbones and focuses on probing their behavior rather than on improving pretraining recipes.

Evaluation and Robustness of VLMs

As VLMs become widely deployed, there is growing interest in understanding their robustness and evaluation blind spots.
5
systematically benchmark robustness of multimodal image–text models under distribution shift by applying a broad set of image and text perturbations across multiple downstream tasks and introducing dedicated robustness metrics
[
5
]
.
11
design zero-shot recognition benchmarks that stress-test VLMs along semantic granularity and textual specificity, showing that models often favor moderately fine-grained labels and struggle with subtle textual differences
[
11
]
. Winoground proposes a probing dataset for visio-linguistic compositionality, where two images and two captions share identical bag-of-words but differ in word order; current VLMs perform near chance, revealing severe compositional failures
[
9
]
. More recently,
3
construct ViLP, a benchmark that disentangles visual language priors from true visual reasoning using carefully synthesized out-of-distribution image–question pairs, and show that many VLMs over-rely on language priors even when visual evidence is available
[
3
]
. Compared to these benchmarks, our language-guided invariance probing focuses specifically on
how CLIP-style encoders respond to controlled, caption-only perturbations while the image is held fixed
, yielding fine-grained invariance and sensitivity scores that are easy to interpret and compare across architectures.

Behavioral Testing and Textual Perturbations

Our methodology is inspired by behavioral testing in NLP. CheckList introduced a task-agnostic testing matrix of linguistic capabilities and test types, emphasizing contrastive cases that probe invariance and directional sensitivity rather than relying solely on aggregate accuracy
[
7
]
. TextAttack provides a modular framework for adversarial attacks and data augmentation, including character-, word-, and sentence-level perturbations that expose brittleness in NLP models
[
4
]
. In contrast to these works, which primarily target text-only models, we adapt the spirit of capability-based testing to the vision–language setting: we design semantic-preserving paraphrases and semantic-flip caption edits grounded in real MS COCO captions
[
2
]
, and we measure how a fixed image encoder’s similarity scores react to these controlled perturbations. Our framework explicitly separates (i)
invariance errors
, where models change their similarity for paraphrases that should be equivalent, and (ii)
sensitivity failures
, where models fail to down-weight captions that intentionally contradict the image.

Positioning of Our Benchmark

Where prior work often aggregates robustness into task-level accuracy drops under perturbations
[
5
,
11
]
or focuses on specialized multimodal reasoning setups
[
9
,
3
]
, our benchmark operates directly at the embedding-similarity level of image–text encoders. This has two advantages: (i) it isolates representation-level invariance and sensitivity from confounding decoder or prompt-engineering choices, and (ii) it produces model-agnostic, quantitative metrics that can be applied uniformly across CLIP, OpenCLIP, EVA-CLIP, SigLIP, SigLIP 2, and future architectures. In this sense, our work complements existing multimodal robustness benchmarks by offering a simple, reproducible, and diagnostic probe of how VLMs balance invariance to benign textual paraphrases against sensitivity to semantically flipped captions.

3.1
Problem Setup

We consider a vision–language model with an image encoder
f_{\text{img}}
and a text encoder
f_{\text{text}}
that map an image
I
and a caption
c
into a shared feature space. Image–text similarity is

s(I,c)=\operatorname{sim}\bigl(f_{\text{img}}(I),f_{\text{text}}(c)\bigr),

(1)

where
\operatorname{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)
is cosine similarity between
\ell_{2}
normalized embeddings.

Let
\mathcal{D}=\{(I_{i},\{c_{i}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N})\}_{i=1}^{M}
be a caption dataset with
M
images and
N
human captions per image (in our experiments,
M=40{,}000
and
N=5
on MS COCO). For each base pair
(I,c)
we construct two sets of textual perturbations:

1.

a
paraphrase set

\mathcal{P}(I,c)
of captions
c^{\prime}
that preserve the core semantics of
c
,

2.

a
semantic flip set

\mathcal{F}(I,c)
of captions
c^{\dagger}
that intentionally contradict at least one key attribute in
c
.

Paraphrases change style and framing while keeping meaning, whereas flips modify object category, color or count so that the caption becomes partially or fully inconsistent with the image.

LGIP quantifies two properties of a fixed model:
linguistic invariance
, where
s(I,c)
should remain stable under paraphrases, and
semantic sensitivity
, where
s(I,c)
should decrease when
c
is replaced by a flipped caption
c^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{F}(I,c)
as we shown in
fig.

1
.

3.2
Perturbation Construction

LGIP operates on top of existing human captions using simple rule-based text edits; images are never modified.

Paraphrases

For each base caption
c
, we generate a small set of paraphrases by wrapping
c
in natural language templates and prefixes, for example

\text{``a photo of }c\text{''},\quad\text{``this image shows }c\text{''},

(2)

and short markers such as “in this picture” or “in the scene”. These operations keep the content words of
c
intact and mainly vary stylistic context. We deduplicate outputs, discard extremely short strings and cap the number of paraphrases per caption at
K_{\text{same}}
.

Semantic flips

To construct semantic flips we apply targeted lexical substitutions that change one attribute at a time. We maintain small vocabularies of frequent COCO nouns (for object category flips), color words (for color flips) and number words (for count flips). When a caption
c
contains one of these tokens, we substitute it with another token from the same list, for example turning “a brown dog laying on the ground” into “a brown person laying on the ground” or “two people standing in a room” into “five people standing in a room”. We perform at most one substitution per flip, detect matches with regular expressions, discard edits that collapse to the original caption and deduplicate outputs. For each
c
we obtain up to
K_{\text{diff}}
flipped captions and record their type, yielding disjoint subsets

\mathcal{F}_{\text{obj}}(I,c),\quad\mathcal{F}_{\text{col}}(I,c),\quad\mathcal{F}_{\text{num}}(I,c),

(3)

for object, color and count flips respectively.

3.3
Metrics

LGIP defines one metric for paraphrase robustness and two for flip sensitivity.

Invariance error

For each image
I
and base caption
c
, we compare
s(I,c)
with
s(I,c^{\prime})
for paraphrases
c^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(I,c)
. The invariance error is

\mathcal{E}_{\text{inv}}=\mathbb{E}_{(I,c)}\mathbb{E}_{c^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(I,c)}\bigl[\lvert s(I,c)-s(I,c^{\prime})\rvert\bigr],

(4)

estimated as an empirical average over all image–caption pairs and their paraphrases. Lower values indicate greater stability to meaning-preserving edits.

Semantic sensitivity and positive rate

For a flipped caption
c^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{F}(I,c)
we define the gap

g(I,c,c^{\dagger})=s(I,c)-s(I,c^{\dagger}).

(5)

The average semantic sensitivity is

\mathcal{E}_{\text{sens}}=\mathbb{E}_{(I,c)}\mathbb{E}_{c^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{F}(I,c)}\bigl[g(I,c,c^{\dagger})\bigr],

(6)

which measures how strongly the model separates original captions from their flips.

To capture ordering, we define an indicator

\mathbf{1}_{\text{pos}}(I,c,c^{\dagger})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }s(I,c)>s(I,c^{\dagger}),\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(7)

and the positive rate

\text{PR}=\mathbb{E}_{(I,c)}\mathbb{E}_{c^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{F}(I,c)}\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{\text{pos}}(I,c,c^{\dagger})\bigr].

(8)

A model that behaves like random guessing yields
\text{PR}\approx 0.5
; values well above 0.5 indicate reliable sensitivity, whereas values near or below 0.5 reveal a failure to consistently down-weight contradictory captions.

All metrics can be restricted to a flip type
t\in\{\text{obj},\text{col},\text{num}\}
by averaging only over
c^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}(I,c)
, which provides a fine-grained view of how a model handles object, color and count edits.

Experiments

4.1
Experimental Setup

Dataset

We apply LGIP to MS COCO
[
2
]
. Following Section
3
, we sample
M=40{,}000
images from the training split and use all five human captions per image. For each base caption, we generate up to

K_{\text{diff}}=6

3.2

1.2

8.1\times 10^{4}
flip pairs per model (80632 valid flip triples after filtering). We report aggregate metrics over the full subset, using only the automatic deduplication and minimum length checks baked into the perturbation pipeline.

Models

We evaluate nine vision–language encoders that follow the CLIP-style dual tower architecture. The CLIP family includes ViT-B/16 and ViT-L/14 released by OpenAI
[
6
]
. The OpenCLIP family includes ViT-L/14 and ViT-H/14 variants trained on LAION-2B
[
1
]
. EVA02-CLIP is a stronger CLIP variant with an improved vision backbone and training recipe
[
8

. For sigmoid-loss models we include SigLIP base at 224 and 384 resolution, a SigLIP large model at 384 resolution
[
12
]
, and the SigLIP 2 base model which augments the objective with captioning and self-supervision
[
10
]
. All models are used as frozen encoders; LGIP only perturbs the text while keeping the image fixed.

For all models we obtain
\ell_{2}

We use the official preprocessing pipelines of each family (OpenCLIP preprocessors for CLIP, EVA and OpenCLIP, and the HuggingFace processor for SigLIP and SigLIP 2). Metrics are computed in a single pass, and all reported numbers are averages over valid image–caption–flip triples. We report invariance error

Within the CLIP and OpenCLIP families we observe a consistent pattern: larger models improve semantic sensitivity with only a mild increase in invariance error. CLIP ViT-B/16 already has low invariance error and moderate sensitivity; scaling to ViT-L/14 and then to OpenCLIP ViT-L/14 and ViT-H/14 increases the semantic gap and positive rate while keeping paraphrase variance small. EVA02-CLIP occupies an especially favorable point on this frontier, achieving the lowest invariance error among all models while maintaining sensitivity comparable to the strongest OpenCLIP models. In other words, the CLIP and EVA families can be moved along a smooth invariance–sensitivity frontier by scaling capacity and pretraining, and EVA02-CLIP sits near the Pareto front.

SigLIP-family models behave very differently. Base SigLIP variants exhibit invariance errors that are nearly an order of magnitude larger than CLIP, indicating that their similarity scores fluctuate substantially even for simple template-based paraphrases. More critically, their global semantic sensitivity is negative and the positive rate falls below 0.5, meaning that flipped captions often receive higher similarity than the original human descriptions. The large SigLIP model improves these numbers slightly but remains close to random ordering, and SigLIP 2 recovers a clearly positive gap and positive rate but still lags behind CLIP and EVA in both dimensions. Overall, LGIP shows that strong zero-shot accuracy
does not guarantee
robust behavior under controlled linguistic perturbations, and that training choices that improve retrieval can still harm invariance and sensitivity.

For CLIP and EVA, all three flip types yield positive gaps and high positive rates. 5
Discussion and Conclusion

We introduced
Language-Guided Invariance Probing
(LGIP), a benchmark for analyzing how vision–language models respond to controlled textual perturbations when the image is held fixed. By separating meaning-preserving paraphrases from meaning-changing semantic flips, LGIP explicitly measures two properties, which are linguistic invariance and semantic sensitivity.

Our study of nine VLMs shows that
these properties are not guaranteed by scale or strong zero-shot accuracy
. EVA02-CLIP and large OpenCLIP models occupy a favorable region of the LGIP space, combining low paraphrase-induced variance with large gaps and high positive rates on object, color and count flips. In contrast, SigLIP-family models exhibit substantially higher invariance error and often prefer flipped captions over human descriptions, revealing systematic attribute-level failures that remain hidden under conventional benchmarks.

Moreover, LGIP is model agnostic that it uses existing caption datasets, rule-based perturbations and frozen encoders, yet exposes clear, recipe-dependent differences in linguistic robustness. Using LGIP not only as an evaluation tool but also as a regularization signal that encourages low paraphrase variance and large margins to flipped captions is another promising direction.

Overall, LGIP provides a compact but informative probe of how VLMs trade off invariance and sensitivity on the language side.
By revealing counterintuitive behaviors
in widely used encoders, it argues for integrating targeted behavioral diagnostics alongside traditional accuracy metrics when designing and selecting future vision–language models.
