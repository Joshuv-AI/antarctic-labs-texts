Remarkable adsorptive denitrogenation of indole and quinoline from model fuel oil by CuCl/UiO-66 metal-organic framework
Authors: Amin Alamdari, Abbas Aghaeinejad-Meybodi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnvironmental pollution, driven by the increasing consumption of fossil fuels, is a critical concern, mainly due to the harmful effects of nitrogen-containing compounds (NCCs). NCCs must be deleted from model fuel because of negative results on the stability of the catalyst. A novel adsorbent was developed by reducing CuCl2 on a zirconium-based metal organic framework (MOF) with sodium sulfite under ambient conditions to produce CuCl(x)/UiO-66(Zr). This material was then applied for the first time to remove harmful NCCs from model fossil fuels. This study found that CuCl improved adsorptive denitrogenation (ADN) effectiveness through π-complexation, defining a crucial role for the Cu(I) species. The adsorbents analyzed using XRD, FTIR, FE-SEM, EDX, BET, and TGA techniques. Characterization analyses showed that CuCl was well distributed on UiO-66. Although the adsorbent porosity decreased after modification, however, the adsorption of NCCs enhanced. Adsorption results showed that CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 removed 200 ppm of quinoline (QUI) and indole (IND) in 360 min at 0.05 g adsorbent dose, 5 ml solution, 25 °C, and pH 8. Also, it was found that the adsorption of NCCs by CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 had a sufficient fit for IND and QUI with the maximum adsorption capacities of 227.27 and 188.68 mg/g, respectively, by the Langmuir isotherm. Also, the pseudo-second-order model showed the proper fit for QUI and IND with linear regression (R2) of 0.9997 and 0.9997, respectively, over CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66. The adsorbent used after five cycles still had a good performance in removing NCCs. The improved removal of NCCs by CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 may be explained by a π-complexation mechanism, where copper(I) ions form selective complexes with the adsorbates. The newly presented adsorption process can be performed as a method for refining of fuel, which is drastically beneficial, environmentally friendly, and cost-effective.Keywords: Denitrogenation, Adsorption, Fuel, UiO-66, Copper ionSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Fossil fuels supply about 90% of the world’s energy, but their consumption is increasing with the global population1. This makes optimizing their use and ensuring environmental sustainability a significant challenge2,3. A critical aspect of this is removing the significant pollutants found in fossil fuels before combustion, as they can harm human health1. During combustion, sulfur and nitrogen compounds (SCCs and NCCs) in fuels produce harmful SOx and NOx emissions4,5. Removing NCCs is particularly critical because they poison the catalysts used in the hydrodesulfurization (HDS) process6,7. In industrial adsorption, NCCs and SCCs compete for active sites, which hinder the desulfurization process8,9. Research confirms that even trace amounts of NCCs can make the production of low-sulfur fuels very challenging10,11. Fossil fuels typically contain two categories of NCCs: basic and neutral. While basic NCCs such as aniline (ANI) and quinoline (QUI) can be readily removed via acid refining, the elimination of neutral compounds like indole (IND), pyrrole (PRL), and carbazole (CBZ) remains a significant challenge12. In compliance with environmental policies, the denitrogenation of fuel oils is a critical refining process. NCCs are detrimental because they deactivate catalysts, corrode equipment, and hinder refining efficiency13. A primary hazard is their conversion during combustion into nitrogen oxides (NOx), which are key precursors to fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and ground-level ozone. These pollutants cause severe respiratory and cardiovascular diseases, increased cancer risk, and premature death in humans. Environmentally, they lead to acid rain, ecosystem damage, aquatic dead zones from eutrophication, and contribute to climate change via nitrous oxide (N2O)14.

Hydrodenitrogenation (HDN) is usually used in industry to decrease the NCCs in fuels15,16. HDN is a costly process and consumes a high amount of energy. Therefore, another process is needed to decrease the amount of NCCs to an acceptable level. In response to the limitations of conventional HDN, advanced deep denitrogenation techniques have been developed as promising alternatives for producing cleaner fuels. These include Adsorptive Denitrogenation (ADN), which captures NCCs via physisorption or chemisorption, evaluated on its capacity, selectivity, and regenerability; Oxidative Denitrogenation (ODN), a cost-effective method that operates under mild conditions but is challenged by the stability of NCCs and the need for selective catalysts; Extractive Denitrogenation (EDN), a simple separation process based on solubility that utilizes solvents like ionic liquids; and Biodenitrogenation (BDN), which employs microbes to break down NCCs, though research is focused on engineering strains that remove nitrogen without reducing the fuel’s energy content5,17–19.

The adsorption process has been proposed as a suitable process and plays an important role in removing undesirable compounds, including toxic pollutants20–23. The adsorption process offers distinct advantages—including speed, cost-effectiveness, low hazard, simple construction, easy recyclability, and wide applicability—making it a preferred choice over treatment methods9. This has spurred considerable research focused on creating low-cost and sustainable adsorbents24. The ADN has been investigated in different research to reduce NCCs from fuels. In addition, in the ADN process, the temperature and pressure are not high, unlike the HDN process; it does not require expensive hydrogen, and the cost of the adsorbent in this process is usually lower than the cost of the catalyst in the HDN process25. Cu(I)-Y zeolites, activated carbons, microporous carbons, silica-alumina containing HCl, activated alumina, Ni-based adsorbents, ion-exchange resins, meso-silica, NiMOs, and Ti-HMS are all adsorbents that have been used to remove NCCs from fuel2,3,26,27. Mondol et al. highlighted that despite extensive research, ADN has not yet been implemented industrially. For successful translation, future work must move beyond simply maximizing adsorption capacity and instead focus on the rational design of adsorbents with high selectivity (especially amongst other aromatics), easy recyclability, simple preparation, and overall cost-effectiveness for treating real fuels with low nitrogen concentrations. The authors identify MOFs as up-and-coming candidates to replace traditional materials like zeolites and activated carbon due to their superior stability and reusability, and they recommend future efforts prioritize investigating shaped MOFs in fixed-bed systems to advance the field toward practical application28.

MOFs are one of the important categories of microporous compounds that have attracted much attention. These materials have attractive features, including adjustable surface properties29,30. MOFs are a combination of metal centers and organic ligands that form a three-dimensional structure with porous surfaces18. MOFs based on metal centers are classified into different categories, among which the frameworks based on zirconium metal (such as UiO-66(Zr)) have attracted the attention of manufacturers and researchers31. These structures enable diverse applications in the petrochemical industry29, optics, separation, ion exchange, storage, catalysis23, sequestering aqueous pollutants32, and adsorbing and detecting pesticides33.

Among different types of MOFs, Zr-based UiO-66 has emerged as a particularly prominent material, making substantial contributions to both scientific research and industrial applications23. The Zr-based UiO-66 MOF is widely applicable due to its exceptional stability and porous architecture, which consists of inorganic Zr6O4(OH)4 clusters connected by terephthalic acid linkers to form a robust crystalline skeleton25,27. While other tetravalent metals can form similar structures, the zirconium variant is particularly notable26. The performance of UiO-66 can be enhanced through strategic modifications; introducing functionalized ionic species or transition metals can create surface defects that alter its physicochemical properties and improve its thermal stability and framework performance29. Furthermore, the inherent Lewis acidity of the unsaturated Zr4+ sites in non-defected crystals makes these MOFs promising for applications such as fuel oil desulfurization and denitrogenation31. The practical use of MOFs is often limited by their insufficient chemical and mechanical stability, making the development of stabilization strategies essential22. A promising method involves creating MOF-based composites by integrating MOFs with other functional materials22. A key issue is the underutilization of their inherent high porosity, as the large void spaces often lack strong adsorptive forces for small molecules22.

Extensive research and applications of MOFs have been conducted on modifying various MOFs to enhance their performance in ADN. For Zr-based UiO-66, functionalization with diverse groups like amines (-NH2)29, carboxylic acid (-COOH)30, and sulfonic acid (-SO3H)31, as well as the incorporation of metals, created surface defects that improved thermal stability and adsorption performance34–36. In Ti-based MIL-125, modifications with amine and oxychloride groups demonstrated that oxychloride functionalization provided active sites for hydrogen bonding, boosting performance37, while oxygen-rich vacancies also promoted adsorption38. For Cr-based MOFs like MIL-101, optimization of synthesis conditions was a key parameter39,40. Its performance was significantly enhanced through encapsulation with phytic acid41 and functionalization with amino groups, the latter showing a 9.3-fold improvement over activated carbon42. Fluorination to create unsaturated metal sites also proved sufficient performance43,44. Zn-based MOFs such as TMU-34 were shown to be suitable for basic compounds like QUI via specific interactions with framework sites45. Finally, MOF-derived carbons and composites, synthesized through pyrolysis and activation, demonstrated vastly superior adsorption capacity (5–6 times better) compared to conventional adsorbents46,47.

UiO-66 is a highly investigated MOF for ADN, valued for its stability, tunability, and straightforward synthesis. Research indicates that incorporating transition metals or ionic species can create beneficial surface defects, which enhance their thermal stability and catalytic performance. However, for MOF-based adsorption to become a commercially viable industrial process, key challenges must be overcome. These include developing easily separable adsorbents, precisely tailoring composite composition, ensuring stability in acidic/alkaline environments, and achieving a sufficient operational lifespan. Strategic design to address these hurdles is essential to transition this technology from laboratory success to large-scale industrial implementation12.

Porous materials incorporating Cu+ ions can effectively remove NCCs from fuel via π-complexation48,49. However, traditional synthesis methods for adsorbents like Cu+-exchanged zeolites often require high-temperature calcination, which is energy-intensive and costly50–52. This is a significant limitation for many MOFs, which possess low thermal stability and cannot withstand such harsh conditions53,54. Consequently, to effectively utilize MOFs for adsorption, it is crucial to develop gentle, easy, and economical synthesis methods that are compatible with their stability limitations53,54. The enhanced adsorption of NCCs on CuCl-modified adsorbent is frequently attributed to π-complexation with Cu+ ions. This mechanism underscores the critical importance of the copper oxidation state for effective adsorption. Among the limited number of transition metal ions capable of π-complexation, Cu+ is especially prominent due to its high efficacy, natural abundance, and low cost, making it a focal point in recent adsorbent development for NCC removal2,48,49.

Environmental pollution, driven by the increasing consumption of fossil fuels, is a critical concern, mainly due to the harmful effects of NCCs. A novel adsorbent was developed by reducing CuCl2 on a zirconium-based MOF with sodium sulfite under ambient conditions to produce CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66(Zr). This study found that CuCl improved ADN effectiveness through π-complexation, defining a crucial role for the Cu(I) species. The improved removal of NCCs by CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 may be explained by a π-complexation mechanism, where copper(I) ions form selective complexes with the adsorbates. To our knowledge, this is the first report on the application of a UiO-66 MOF modified with CuCl for ADN. The incorporation of CuCl can enhance the adsorption capacity of MOFs by acting as a physical barrier. This barrier limits linker access during synthesis, leading to a higher concentration of unsaturated metal sites within the CuCl@MOF composite compared to the pure MOF. These additional metallic centers provide more locations for strong interactions with adsorbate molecules, thereby improving the composite’s overall adsorption performance12. The characteristics of the synthesized MOFs were analyzed by FT-IR, XRD, FE-SEM, EDX, TGA, and BET analyses. Various process parameters, including the effect of contact time, CuCl2 content in synthesis, reaction temperature, initial concentration of NCCs, adsorbent dosage, and solution pH, were studied in the ADN process. Also, the performance of the regenerated adsorbent, the adsorption isotherm, and the kinetics of the process were studied.

Experimental

Materials

Dimethylformamide (DMF, 99%), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 35%), terephthalic acid (TPA, 99%), Zirconium chloride (ZrCl4, 99.5%), sodium sulfite (Na2SO3, 99%), n-heptane (C7H16, 97%), indole (IND, 98%), quinoline (QUI, 98%), and, cupric chloride (CuCl2, 99%), were purchased from Merck.

Preparation of adsorbents

UiO-66 was obtained by conventional methods55. HCl (1.009 g), ZrCl4 (1.129 g), DMF (27.04 g), and TPA (1.626 g) were added together and stirred for 15 min. After that, the solution was introduced to a 50 ml Teflon-lined autoclave. The Teflon-lined autoclave was heated for 24 h at 180 °C in an oven. The final slurry was filtered by filter paper and then rinsed with DMF solvent. 50 ml of solvent was introduced to one gram of MOF and heated in an electric oven at 150 °C for 5 h. Then, the sample was dried in a vacuum electric oven for 12 h, at 150 °C. 1 g of UiO-66 was transferred to a glass vial including 0.5, 1, 2, 3, and 4 ml of aqueous solution of 0.2 M CuCl2 and was mixed at 25 °C for 40 min with a magnetic stirrer. Then, an equal amount (0.5, 1, 2, 3, and, 4 ml) of aqueous solution of 0.1 M Na2SO3 was added drop-wise, while the CuCl2 solution including UiO-66 was stirred. The solution was stirred for 20 min, and after that, 1 ml of H2SO3 was introduced. H2SO3 aqueous solution was obtained by adding 25 ml of 0.25 mM Na2SO3 to 3 ml of 0.2 M HCl under stirring. Then, the solution including CuCl2 and UiO-66 was separated with filter paper and rinsed with H2SO3. Consequently, the wet samples were inserted into a vacuum oven and the samples were dried for 12 h, at 100 °C. The samples were labeled CuCl(x)/UiO-66, where x represents the volume of cupric chloride solution used in the preparation of adsorbents.

Characterization of the adsorbents

X-ray diffraction patterns (XRD) were utilized to study the crystalline property of the prepared MOF manufactured by Shimadzu Company (XRD-6000, Japan) in the 2θ range of 5–80º with λ = 1.5406 Å (Cu-Kα radiation) at 9 KW and a scanning rate of 10º/min. Fourier-Transform Infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) was employed following the KBr disc technique to check the properties of the sample molecules by the JASCO FTIR-4100 model (Japan) in a range of 400–4000 cm− 1. The sample’s morphology was analyzed using Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE-SEM, HITACHI S4160). Its elemental composition was determined by X-ray Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS, HITACHI S4160). The N2 adsorption and desorption analysis was used to investigate the surface characteristics of the MOF and Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) specific surface using the Belsorp mini device (Japan) by drying the adsorbents under vacuum for 6 h at 393 K and degassing for 12 h at 453 K. Thermal Gravimetric Analysis (TGA) (STA 504 instrument) was utilized to investigate of the thermal stability of adsorbents in air flow with 2°Cmin− 1 heating rate.

Procedure of adsorption experiments

Initial solutions (10000 ppm) of the fuel containing IND and QUI were prepared by dissolving the IND and QUI in n-heptane. The NCCs concentration in the n-heptane was diluted to 200 ppm to study the adsorption capacity of the MOF at different times of the process. To determine the adsorption capacity of QUI and IND, the model fuel was diluted to 50–350 ppm in the same n-heptane solvent. During the ADN experiments, a specific content of adsorbent (5 mg) was introduced to the n-heptane containing QUI or IND nitrogenous compound (5 ml), and the temperature was kept constant at 25 °C. The mixture was stirred in a beaker containing a magnetic stirrer for 5 to 360 min. After adsorptive denitrogenation, the MOF was filtered from the solution with a PTFE syringe filter (0.5 μm), and the final concentrations of the adsorbed compound were determined using a UV spectrometer (Spectrum, China). Evaluation of the regenerated adsorbent

The interaction among the adsorbents and the products of the ADN process leads to significant complications in the reusability of the adsorbent. For adsorbent reusability tests, 0.06 g of used adsorbent was introduced to 50 ml of ethanol and subjected to ultrasound for 3 h. Then, the samples were filtered and rinsed with a mixture of ethanol/water (a volume ratio of one to one) six times, and then dried at 60 °C overnight. The regenerated adsorbent was regenerated for adsorption performance in the new cycle under optimal conditions.

Results and discussion

Characterization of synthesized adsorbents

Figure 1A presents the XRD diffraction patterns for the pristine UiO-66 and CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 patterns. The zirconium-benzene terephthalate units in UiO-66, which are located in the face-centered crystal lattice, show important diffraction peaks at angles of 7.4º, 8.8º, 14.7º, 17.4º, 25.7º, 31.1º, 43.6º attributing to (111), (200), (222), (400), (600), (731), and (664) crystal planes63. The XRD diffraction patterns for the prepared MOFs are very similar and in agreement with the calculated pattern for UiO-66, which indicates that both MOFs have the same crystal structure. The CuCl index peaks are at 24.3 º and 43.1 º, and for CuCl2, the index peaks are mainly at 16 º, 26.3 º, 31.2 º, 38.5 º, and 49.2º. Due to the proper reduction of CuCl2, no diffraction patterns were observed. However, the intensity of the peaks in the CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 is lower than that of UiO-66, which suggests that this framework has less crystallinity compared to pristine UiO-66. The absence of a diffraction pattern for CuCl indicates uniform distribution, proper distribution, and a low amount of CuCl in UiO-66 cavities. FTIR spectrum drastically investigates the functional groups of the synthesized adsorbents. FTIR patterns for UiO-66 and CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 are shown in Fig. 1B. The characteristic peaks are identical for the two adsorbents. The absorption bands around 3000–3600 cm− 1 are related to the stretching frequency of hydrogen in the hydroxyl group, whereas the bands in the range of 1200 cm− 1 and 1100 cm− 1 are characteristic frequencies of inorganic ions64. The bands at 1580 cm− 1 and 1400 cm− 1 are related to the stretching vibration of the carbon-oxygen group. In both samples, the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of O-C-O bonds of terephthalic grafting appear at 1573 cm− 1 and 1410 cm− 165. The bands at 1500 cm− 1 and 763 cm− 1 are related to the C = C stretching vibration of the benzene ring, both of which are characteristic of ligands66,67. Bands corresponding to zirconium-oxygen metal stretching are observed in the range of 580 –550 cm− 1. The band at 1656 cm− 1 is ascribed to the C = O bond, which is attributed to the low content of additional Zr solvent created in the preparation of the MOF due to the non-exchanged ligands, activation processes, and order and charge preservation. The absorption band at 823 cm− 1 is ascribed to the hydroxyl group present in pristine MOF, while the bands at 688 and 746 cm− 1 indicate the stretching of µ3-O from Zr-(oC)68. The ranges below 1000 cm− 1 are related to the nature of metal-ligand bonds69. As can be seen, the initial UiO-66 stretching vibrations decrease with the addition of CuCl, which indicates the stretching states of the functional groups on the pristine MOF surface have some overlap with metal oxides70. Bands in the range of 400 to 700 cm− 1 indicate the interaction between metal-oxygen. The peaks at 651, 605, and 551 cm− 1 indicate coordination, bonds of metal-oxygen in [Metal(µ-O)] dimers, and the existence of resonance interaction of vibrational states of metal-oxygen-metal, and it is a suitable balance of metal oxide with MOF69.

The thermal stability of UiO-66 and its modified counterpart, CuCl(2)/UiO-66, was assessed via thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and DGT (Fig. 5A and B). Both materials exhibited a multiple-step weight loss profile. The first significant event, centered on 100 °C, is attributed to the desorption of water molecules from the pores. CuCl(2)/UiO-66 showed a greater weight loss here, indicating enhanced hydrophilicity imparted by its polar amine groups. A subsequent, smaller weight loss around 200 °C is assigned to the removal of trapped DMF solvent. The final decomposition step, marking the framework’s collapse, corresponds to the thermal decomposition of their respective terephthalic acid linkers into gaseous products and ZrO272.

Adsorption performance evaluation

To better study the role of CuCl content on the modification of UiO-66(Zr) in the ADN process of NCCs from n-heptane, various CuCl(x)/UiO-66s were synthesized. The optimal content of impregnated CuCl in the UiO-66(Zr) microporous adsorbent was investigated before different adsorption tests to gain the benefit of the adsorption process with CuCl(x)/UiO-66(Zr). The results for NCCs removal are shown in Fig. 6A. Based on this Figure, at the beginning of the process, the adsorption capacity of NCCs over CuCl(x)/UiO-66(Zr) increases with increasing the content of CuCl2 loaded up to a specific volume (from 0.5 to 2 ml) and then decreases. The adsorption of IND is mainly due to H-bonding and for QUI is mainly due to van der Waals interaction and π-complex interaction between the aromatic rings of the MOF and QUI. For both IND and QUI compounds, CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 (Zr) showed a better adsorption capacity at 6 h. Also, CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 (Zr) had a proper adsorption capacity for IND compared to QUI. At a higher amount of CuCl2, the pore blocking of the MOF during the modification stage occurred. Nevertheless, the optimal amount was 2 ml of CuCl2 aqueous solution per 0.005 g of pristine UiO-66(Zr). Therefore, the pristine MOF and CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 (Zr) would be considered as the selected adsorbents in further studies. It should be noted that there is a conflict between the valuable effect of impregnation with CuCl and the reduction of porosity due to the increase in CuCl content. Therefore, the volume of CuCl cannot be increased to any extent because the porosity of the MOF decreases.

To investigate the role of process time on the adsorptive removal process of IND and QUI from n-heptane fuel, the ADN process was performed for 360 min at 25 °C and 0.005 g of the MOF in 5 mL model fuel. An extended contact time leads to the removal of the blockage of NCCs in the adsorbent. One of the advantages of extended contact time is the increase of π-π reaction and electron density73. As shown in Fig. 6B, the removal of NCCs of IND and QUI with UiO-66 and synthesized CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 increases steadily and reaches more than 90%, within 120 min. Then, it becomes stable with increasing time of the adsorption process. After 360 min, it seems that a balance is established in ADN, which can be due to the existence of active sites in the outer layer of synthesized UiO-66 and CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 at the beginning of the ADN process38. The high adsorption rate of NCCs can occur due to the very high negative adsorption enthalpies of these two compounds51. The adsorption kinetics was high-speed, due to the size of the relatively large pores of the adsorbents. In general, the adsorption rate in different adsorbents did not differ much, and the adsorption rate was high-speed, which confirms the favorable and efficient adsorption for NCCs around MOFs compared to other adsorbents. A similar behavior was observed for the NCCs of IND and QUI, but the adsorption capacity of various NCCs was in the order of QUI < IND. From Fig. 6B, it can be seen that for a duration time of 360 min, the adsorption capacity for IND over UiO-66 and CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 was 175.89 and 204.40 mg/g, respectively, and for QUI over UiO-66 and CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 was 150 and 159.94 mg/g, respectively.

The trend of changes in adsorption capacity of NCCs from n-heptane using UiO-66 and CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 at different temperatures between 25 °C and 40 °C in a duration time of 360 min and 0.05 g of adsorbent is presented in Fig. 7A. The adsorption of NCCs of IND and QUI decreases with increasing temperature, which is due to the exothermic process for the adsorption of NCCs. Higher temperature has the opposite effect on the adsorption process38. According to Fig. 7A, over UiO-66 adsorbent at 25 °C, the adsorption capacity for IND and QUI was 175.89 mg/g and 150 mg/g, respectively. Also, over CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 adsorbent at a temperature of 25 °C, the adsorption capacity for IND and QUI was 204.40 and 159.94 mg/g, respectively. However, at 40 °C for the UiO-66, the adsorption capacity for IND and QUI was 152.90 and 140 mg/g, respectively. Also, over CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 adsorbent at a temperature of 40 °C, the capacity for IND and QUI is 196.12 and 152.90 mg/g, respectively. The higher efficiency of modified UiO-66 rather than UiO-66 can be related to the better synergy between the primary framework and CuCl in removing NCCs. Low temperatures can reduce process costs and increase safety on an industrial scale.

The adsorbent dosage is a crucial factor in the ADN. The adsorption rate increases with the active sites74. Figure 7B presents the role of adsorbent dosage in the process of ADN at 25 °C for 360 min and different amounts of adsorbent. As presented in Fig. 7B, the elimination of IND and QUI over pristine UiO-66 and CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 continuously enhances with the dosage of adsorbent. For both IND and QUI compounds, with the increase of the UiO-66 adsorbent dose from 2.5 to 10 mg, the capacity for IND increases from 154.95 to 177 mg/g and for QUI from 122 to 152 mg/g. Also, by increasing the CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 adsorbent dose from 2.5 to 10 mg, the adsorption capacity for IND increases from 173.36 to 206 mg/g and for QUI from 122 to 161 mg/g, and for both compounds at a dose of 5 mg, the adsorbent approximately becomes saturated. In addition, the interaction between the IND nitrogenous compound and the MOFs was greater than that of QUI. For the adsorption of the IND compound compared to the QUI compound, a lower adsorbent dose is needed. The enhancement in adsorption activity by increasing of dosage is due to the accessibility of more amounts of sites of adsorbents for the adsorption process75.

Figure 8 presents the role of the NCCs’ initial concentration in the range of 50 to 350 ppm in the adsorption process. For the QUI nitrogenous compound in the range of low concentrations (less than 200 ppm), with the increase in concentration of the QUI compound, the capacity increases. At higher concentrations (more than 200 ppm), the adsorption capacity becomes stable. For the IND nitrogenous compound, it enhances continuously by increasing concentration up to 150 ppm, and after that, the rate of increase decreases. In general, the adsorption process of both NCCs increased with the initial concentration of NCCs. This increase can be attributed to the concentration gradient. However, at high initial concentrations of QUI (above 200 ppm), the increase in adsorption rate decreased due to the interaction of QUI molecules with the UiO-66 and CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 adsorption sites76,77. This trend can be attributed to the steric hindrance between the QUI nitrogenous compounds due to the reduction of the pore space31. For QUI and IND, the maximum adsorption capacity at the adsorbent dose of 0.05 g was 157 and 187.84 mg/g, respectively for UiO-66, and 166.96 and 211.76 mg/g, respectively for CuCl(2.0)//UiO-66. Using the same amount of adsorbent, the adsorption capacity of neutral compounds (IND) is much higher compared to the basic compounds (QUI). Therefore, it is more difficult to remove basic NCCs than that of neutral compounds. Also, the adsorption capacity for CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 is greater than that of UiO-66.

The adsorption of IND and QUI onto UiO-66 and CuCl(2)/UiO-66 was significantly influenced by solution pH, a factor that alters the adsorbent’s surface charge. As depicted in Fig. 9A, the adsorption capacity for IND over CuCl(2)/UiO-66 increased from 185 mg/g at pH 2 to a maximum of 204.4 mg/g at pH 8, then declined gradually to 188 mg/g at pH 10 before a sharp decrease to 143 mg/g at pH 12. Also, the adsorption capacity for QUI over CuCl(2)/UiO-66 increased from 148 mg/g at pH 2 to a maximum of 159.94 mg/g at pH 8, then declined gradually to 155 mg/g at pH 10 before a sharp decrease to 114 mg/g at pH 12. For NCCs, adsorption capacity typically increases with higher pH levels because the adsorbent’s surface becomes more negatively charged, enhancing electrostatic attraction to the positive NCC molecules. This occurs as the increasing pH neutralizes positive charges on the adsorbent surface. Consequently, the NCCs’ negative sites have a greater affinity for the adsorbent’s active sites, promoting adsorption. When the pH exceeds 8 the adsorption declines under more alkaline conditions. This reduction is attributed to decreased protonation of the NCCs and increased electrostatic repulsion between the negatively charged adsorbent surface (due to adsorbed OH⁻ ions) and the ionized NCC molecules, which hinders diffusion and adsorption78.

(A) Effects of pH values on the adsorption of IND and QUI by UiO-66 and CuCl(2)/UiO-66, (B) and pHPZC of CuCl(2)/UiO-66.

The point of zero charge (pHzpc) for the CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 was determined to be 6.4 (Fig. 9B). At pH levels below this value, the adsorbent’s surface carries a positive charge, while at higher pH levels, the surface becomes negatively charged. This negative surface above pH 6.4 favors the adsorption of the NCCs through electrostatic forces, explaining the improved adsorption rates as the pH increased from 6 to 8. The decrease in adsorption at pH values greater than 8 is due to the dominance of hydrogen bonding between the NCCs and the MOF, which becomes a less effective mechanism for adsorption under these conditions compared to the electrostatic interaction that is optimal at lower alkaline levels79.

Kinetics study.

The kinetics was studied to investigate the separation process of IND and QUI nitrogenous compounds, which controls the adsorption retention time at the interface of fuel and adsorbent. To calculate the kinetics of the process on both adsorbents, two kinetics models were utilized to investigate the experimental data. The effect of duration time on adsorption capacity is presented in Fig. 6B. From Fig. 6B, during 6 h, the adsorption increases slowly with time. It was found that between 240 and 360 min, the adsorption process was autonomous. So, UiO-66(Zr) and CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66(Zr) can no longer adsorb IND and QUI because the pores of both adsorbents are fully occupied with NCCs. A graph of log(qe-qt) vs. t (min) is presented in Fig. 11A for the first model. For the second model, a plot of t/qt vs. t (min) is sketched in Fig. 11B. It was found that the process using both adsorbents follows the pseudo-second-order kinetics model. The parameters were determined by nonlinear curve fitting, and the results are illustrated in Fig. 11A and B. The adsorption kinetics data fit properly with the second model. The R2 values for IND in the first model were 0.9518 and 0.9961 over UiO-66(Zr) and CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66(Zr), respectively. Reusability study.

Figure 12 presents the adsorption regeneration studies of QUI and IND over both adsorbents. The capacity of IND and QUI were in the order of CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66(Zr) > pristine UiO-66(Zr). The adsorptive removal of NCCs over both adsorbents was investigated for reusability. The utilized adsorbent was regenerated by the simple solvent regeneration technique. The proper amount of ethanol was added to UiO-66(Zr) and CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66(Zr) for 20 min under stirring at 25 °C. The adsorbent was separated from the solution with filter paper and dried at 80 °C in an electric oven. The prepared adsorbents were used for the adsorptive removal process from simulated fuel. Reusability investigations showed that after five regeneration cycle, the UiO-66(Zr) adsorption capacity slowly declined from 157 to 149.15 mg/g for QUI and from 187 to 177.65 mg/g for IND, respectively. Also, after five regeneration cycles, the CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66(Zr) adsorption capacity slowly declined from 166.96 to 158.61 mg/g for QUI, and from 211.76 to 201.17 mg/g for IND, respectively. CuCl has π-complexion, which makes CuCl-impregnated UiO-66(Zr) high capability of adsorption rather than pristine UiO-66(Zr).

Comparison of AND and ODN

A series of comparative tests was performed to evaluate the materials’ effectiveness. An initial experiment, conducted without an oxidant, measured only the adsorption of IND and QUI onto the adsorbent surfaces. Under optimum conditions (360 min process time, 25 °C, 5 mg adsorbent, 200 ppm NCC, and solution pH 8), adsorption equilibrium was reached after 360 min, saturating all the samples. The maximum IND removal rates via adsorption were as follows: CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66(IND) (100%) > UiO-66(IND) (87.94%) > CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66(QUI) (79.7%) > UiO-66(QUI) (75%) (Fig. 15). Subsequently, the catalytic activity in oxidative denitrogenation (ODN) was assessed by introducing hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) as an oxidant (O/N = 4) and methanol as an extraction solvent. Under these conditions, UiO-66 and CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 achieved IND removal rates of 100% and 95%, respectively. Also, UiO-66 and CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 achieved QUI removal of 88.3% and 82.5%, respectively. This significant enhancement is because IND, acting as a Lewis base, interacts with Brønsted acid sites provided by the CuCl groups, which serve as active catalytic sites.

Economic appraisal

The laboratory-scale production cost for the adsorbent is estimated at approximately 1.3 $ per gram. This figure is derived from raw material costs, including ZrCl4 ($ 30/kg) and ancillary chemicals such as HCl ($ 11/kg), DMF ($ 11/L), TPA ($ 44/kg), Na2SO3 ($ 22/kg), and CuCl2 ($ 15/kg), which are utilized in small quantities, combined with expenses for transport and electrical energy. The overall cost-effectiveness is enhanced by the material’s capacity for chemical regeneration and reuse. Conclusion

In this research, copper (I) chloride (CuCl) was loaded on the UiO-66 MOF by the wet impregnation method, and CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66(Zr) was used for the denitrogenation of model fuel and compared to the pristine MOF, CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66(Zr) had a higher removal efficiency than the pristine UiO-66 framework. The higher removal percentage can be attributed to the synergistic effect between CuCl(2.0) and MOF with QUI and IND. Different parameters, including time, CuCl content, temperature, adsorbent dose, initial concentration of IND or QUI, and solution pH were investigated in the denitrogenation process. Contact time is an important and influential factor for the ADN process. The CuCl(2.0)/UiO-66 adsorbent showed maximum adsorption capacities of 188.68 and 227.27 mg/g for QUI and IND, respectively. In general, the performance of the adsorbent increases by enhancing the adsorbent dosage, which is ascribed to the enhancement in the active sites created for surface adsorption. The modified UiO-66 has a higher adsorption rate than UiO-66 in lower concentrations (less than 200 ppm). In the isotherms of adsorption study, according to the values ​​of R2, the degree of conformity of the experimental results with Langmuir and Freundlich adsorption models was suitable, and the order of squared errors was as Langmuir > Freundlich. Therefore, it can be assumed that the separation is homogeneous and single-layer so that the adsorbed molecules do not have any interaction with each other. Also, it was found that the pseudo-second-order kinetics model is more sufficient for describing the kinetic behavior. The newly presented adsorption system can be considered as an alternative method for fuel refining, which is fast, highly efficient, environmentally friendly, and cost-effective.
