Bridging the gap: overcoming anatomical challenges in kidney transplantation with prosthetic grafts
Authors: Hyung Sub Park
Published: 2026

Kidney transplantation remains the definitive treatment for patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [1]. Despite the increasing prevalence of ESRD, the disparity between the number of patients awaiting transplantation and the available organ pool (both living and deceased) remains a significant global health issue. Transplant surgeons are faced with challenging situations during the transplant procedure, which may include anatomical challenges such as short renal vessels, multiple renal vessels, short ureters, or suboptimal kidney status, among others. Some of these challenges can be predicted before the surgery, but in many cases, they are unpredictable and demand rapid, decisive actions to mitigate the ischemic time of the donor organ.

As elucidated by Cho et al. [2] in this issue of Annals of Surgical Treatment and Research, short renal veins impose a major challenge during kidney transplantation. This is especially the case when the right kidney must be used during living donation, given the anatomical shortness of the right renal vein relative to the inferior vena cava (IVC). This is the reason why during deceased donation, the right kidney is procured with the right renal vein and the IVC so that the renal vein can be lengthened across the IVC. Similar challenges arise when the recipient's iliac vein is positioned deep within the pelvic cavity. While maneuvers such as extensive iliac dissection or ligation of the internal iliac vein can facilitate a more superficial anastomosis, these techniques may not always eliminate the risk of tension—a primary precursor to venous thrombosis. Furthermore, surgeon-related factors, such as suboptimal procurement length or inadvertent injury (especially during deceased donor procurement), can necessitate additional venous reconstruction.

In such circumstances, where tension-free anastomosis is unfeasible, a conduit is required to bridge the gap. While autologous veins are the gold standard, they are often cumbersome to harvest, and the size mismatch between the harvested vein and the renal vein may require laborious maneuvers such as splicing or spiraling to match the calibers, which can further prolong ischemic times. Conversely, cryopreserved vascular allografts are often limited by high costs and restricted availability in many centers. Consequently, prosthetic grafts—with their standardized sizing and immediate accessibility—present a viable, though historically debated, alternative due to concerns regarding thrombosis and infection.

The article by Cho et al. [2] provides compelling, longterm evidence for the use of Dacron vascular grafts as a reliable and effective alternative for renal vein extension. Through a retrospective review of 11 cases conducted at Seoul National University Hospital from 2016 to 2024, the authors demonstrated that interposition with Dacron grafts—customized to the specific anatomical requirements of each case—facilitates tension-free alignment and ensures optimal hemodynamics. The study cohort experienced 100% vascular patency throughout a median follow-up of 46 months, with no incidence of thrombosis or graft-related infection, a success attributed in part to the meticulous surgical technique and a consistent postoperative protocol involving lifelong single antiplatelet therapy. By eliminating the need for complex tissue harvesting, this approach also maintained cold ischemic times within an acceptable range, which is critical for reducing the risk of delayed graft function.

These results are particularly striking given the historically lower patency rates of prosthetic grafts in the venous system compared to the arterial system—a discrepancy driven by lower venous pressure and flow dynamics. This is especially so from reports on oncovascular surgery, where extensive reconstruction of venous defects after radical cancer surgery using prosthetic grafts showed poor patency and, in many cases, was not always necessary [3,4]. While the study is limited by its retrospective design and small sample size, it serves as a robust call for surgeons to reconsider the utility of synthetic conduits in complex kidney transplantation. It demonstrates that when used with appropriate patient selection and adherence to prophylactic medication, Dacron grafts can be a safe and durable solution for challenging venous reconstructions.

As transplant surgery continues to advance, the ability to address anatomical hurdles with efficient, safe, and readily available tools is essential. The work by Cho et al. [2] contributes significantly to this goal, offering a practical pathway to optimize surgical outcomes and expand the surgical repertoire for complex transplantation cases. Future prospective, multicenter trials will be invaluable to further validate these findings and firmly establish the role of synthetic grafts in modern kidney transplantation.
