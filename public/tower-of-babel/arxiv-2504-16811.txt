A novel method for measuring the attenuation length and the group velocity of transparent liquids in a variable length cavity Journal: Nuclear Inst. and Methods in Physics Research, A
Jessica Eck, Dhanushka Bandara, Gina Grünauer, Tobias Heinz, Tobias Lachenmaier

Abstract
The transparency of liquid scintillators or water is an important parameter for many detectors in particle and astroparticle physics. In this work, the CELLPALS ( CELLPALS ) method for the determination of the attenuation length is presented for the first time. The method is based on an experimental setup similar to a Fabry-Pérot interferometer but adding up multiple-reflected intensities of a modulated light source. CELLPALS was developed to measure the attenuation length of highly transparent liquids ( > 10 ​ m >10\,\mathrm{m} ) with significantly lower uncertainties than with UV-Vis spectroscopy, which is a standard method for determining the attenuation length. In addition to the attenuation length, the group velocity of light in the sample can also be derived from the free spectral range of the cavity, which is not provided by any conventional method for determining the attenuation length. In this work, the CELLPALS method, its achievable precision and an experimental setup to demonstrate its feasibility are discussed. The attenuation lengths and group velocities of several transparent liquids were measured at wavelengths between 420 ​ nm 420\,\mathrm{nm} and 435 ​ nm 435\,\mathrm{nm} . In addition, the attenuation length and group velocity of LAB ( LAB ) samples after different purification stages and a purified LAB -based LS were measured at a wavelength of 425 ​ nm 425\,\mathrm{nm} . The results confirmed the potential of CELLPALS to determine the attenuation length with an uncertainty of ∼ 2 % \sim 2\,\mathrm{\%} . The group velocity of light in the sample can be determined with an uncertainty of ∼ 0.03 % \sim 0.03\,\mathrm{\%} .

The transparency of a medium is described by its attenuation length

I(L)=I_{0}\exp\left(-\frac{L}{L_{\text{att}}}\right)

(1)

denotes the distance
L

One method for determining the attenuation length of liquids is UV–Vis spectroscopy by measuring absorption spectra in the ultraviolet and visible light range
[

\sim 7\,\mathrm{\%}

In order to determine high attenuation lengths more precisely, a number of other methods have been developed, which are mainly based on increasing the light path. These devices typically use a vertical tube with a length
of
\sim(1-3)\,\mathrm{m}

12
,
13

14

15

The
CELLPALS
method allows the determination of the attenuation length of transparent liquids with an uncertainty comparable to these methods and with a significantly lower uncertainty than the UV–Vis method. Due to its compact design, the
CELLPALS
method requires only a small sample volume (
\sim 1\,\mathrm{litre}
) and is very straightforward in terms of cleaning and changing the sample. The location of the
CELLPALS
setup can also be easily changed, making it ideal as a direct monitoring system at the purification plant. Similar to the UV–Vis method,
CELLPALS
also compares the intensities of a reference beam and a sample beam, but the fundamental difference of
CELLPALS
is the extension of the effective light path by using an optical cavity in combination with an amplitude-modulated laser as the light source. The
CELLPALS
method is based on Fabry-Pérot interferometry, where it is not the wavelength of the light that interferes, but the sinusoidally modulated part of the intensity (cf.
2.1
). In

2
The CELLPALS principle

The
CELLPALS
principle is based on the attenuation of the intensity according to Beers’ Law (cf. Eq.
1
) and uses the principle of Fabry-Pérot interferometry. An optical resonator of length

L
is used to increase the effective light path
L_{\text{eff}}=\kappa\cdot L
through the sample by the factor
\kappa=\left(1-R\right)^{-1}

[
16
]
.
This factor depends on the reflectivity
R
of the two opposing semi-transparent concave mirrors
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
, which define the cavity resonator containing the sample (cf. Fig.
1
).
After entering the resonator through
M_{1}
, the light is reflected back and forth between the two mirrors. The total intensity
I_{\text{out}}(t)
leaving the cavity resonator behind mirror
M_{2}
is detected as a signal.

. The rays have been attenuated to different degrees (indicated by the brightness of the rays) and have different phase shifts in their modulation depending on their number of round trips
n
.

2.2
Frequency-dependent analysis of the intensities

To compare the initial and the attenuated intensity, the laser beam is split into a reference beam with the intensity
I_{\text{ref}}(t)
and a cavity beam with the intensity
I_{\text{cav}}(t)
, which are both detected by an
APD
(
APD
) (cf. Fig.
2
). The modulated intensity of the reference beam

I_{\text{ref}}(t)=I_{\text{mod}}(t)\,\xi_{\text{ref}}\exp(-i\phi_{\text{ref}}(\nu))

(7)

is equivalent to the modulated intensity
I_{\text{mod}}(t)
(cf. Eq.
2
) modulo an additional frequency-dependent phase
\phi_{\text{ref}}(\nu)=2\pi(x_{\text{ref}}/v_{g,\text{air}})\nu
that occurs due to the path
x_{\text{ref}}
from the beam splitter to the reference
APD
, where
v_{g,\text{air}}
denotes the group velocity of light in air.
The constant factor
\xi_{\text{ref}}
represents all additional attenuation factors due to optical elements, such as the beam splitter or the deflection mirror (cf. Fig.
2
), which of course depend on the wavelength but do not change with different modulation frequencies
\nu
of the intensity.

Similarly, the intensity of the cavity beam

I_{\text{cav}}(t)=I_{\text{out}}(t)\,\xi_{\text{cav}}\exp(-i\phi_{\text{cav}}(\nu))\quad

(8)

corresponds to the intensity
I_{\text{out}}(t)
(cf. Eq.
6
) modulo a linear phase
\phi_{\text{cav}}(\nu)=2\pi(x_{\text{cav}}/v_{g,\text{air}})\cdot\nu
and the frequency-independent attenuating factor
\xi_{\text{cav}}

By measuring the intensities
I_{\text{ref}}(t)
and
I_{\text{cav}}(t)
for different modulation frequencies
\nu
, the amplitudes and the phases of both can be derived and compared to each other. On the one hand, this provides the amplitude ratio

\mathcal{A}(\nu)=\frac{\left\lvert I_{\text{cav}}(t)\right\rvert}{\left\lvert I_{\text{ref}}(t)\right\rvert}=\frac{b_{1}}{\sqrt{A_{\text{eff}}^{2}-2A_{\text{eff}}\cos\left(2\pi\frac{\nu}{\Delta\nu}\right)+1}}

(9)

for different modulation frequencies
\nu
, taking into account the frequency-independent parameter
b_{1}=({\xi_{\text{cav}}}/{\xi_{\text{ref}}})\,T^{2}\exp(-{L}/{L_{\text{att}}})
. On the other hand, it provides the frequency-dependent phase shift

\displaystyle\Delta\varphi(\nu)

\displaystyle=\arg\left(I_{\text{ref}}(t)\right)-\arg\left(I_{\text{cav}}(t)\right)

\displaystyle=\arctan\left(\frac{A_{\text{eff}}\sin\left(2\pi\frac{\nu}{\Delta\nu}\right)}{1-A_{\text{eff}}\cos\left(2\pi\frac{\nu}{\Delta\nu}\right)}\right)+b_{2}\cdot\nu

(10)

between the modulation of the cavity and the reference beam. The linear part of the phase shift
b_{2}\cdot\nu={\pi\tau\nu}+[\phi_{\text{cav}}(\nu)-\phi_{\text{ref}}(\nu)]
reflects the effect of the path differences between the beam splitter and the corresponding
APD
s and therefore does not contain any physical information about the sample.
Both the amplitude ratio
\mathcal{A}(\nu)
and the phase shift
\Delta\varphi(\nu)
depend on the free spectral range

\Delta\nu=\frac{v_{g}}{2L}\,,

(11)

which is defined by the ratio of the group velocity
v_{g}
and the cavity length
L
and corresponds to the inverse of the round trip time
\tau
(cf. Eq.
5
), and the attenuation parameter

A_{\text{eff}}=R^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{2L}{L_{\text{att}}}\right)<1\,,

(12)

which takes into account the reflectivity
R
of the mirrors and the attenuation length
L_{\text{att}}
of the sample.

By determining the free spectral range
\Delta\nu
and the attenuation parameter
A_{\text{eff}}
from the frequency-dependent measurements of the amplitude ratio
\mathcal{A}(\nu)
or the phase shift
\Delta\varphi(\nu)
, the attenuation length
L_{\text{att}}
and the group velocity
v_{g}
of the sample in the cavity can be derived.

3
Measurement method and achievable precision

In the
CELLPALS
method, the intensity of the laser diode is sinusoidally modulated with modulation frequencies between
\sim 0.5\,\mathrm{MHz}
and
\sim 350\,\mathrm{MHz}
for a selected resonator length
L
, and the amplitudes and phases of the reference and sample beam are determined from intensity measurements for each modulation frequency
\nu
. In this way, the frequency-dependent amplitude ratio and phase shift between the two signals are derived, which can be fitted by the Eqs.
9
and
10
using the least squares method to estimate three parameters (
\Delta\nu
,
A_{\text{eff}}
and
b_{1}
respectively
b_{2}
) for both fit functions.

3.2
Achievable precision of the group velocity and the cavity length

The group velocity
v_{g}
in the sample can be determined with
CELLPALS
according to

v_{g}=\frac{\Delta\nu}{\Delta\nu_{\text{air}}}\cdot v_{g,\text{air}}

(13)

by measuring the free spectral width in air
\Delta\nu_{\text{air}}
and in the sample
\Delta\nu
for a chosen resonator length
L
(cf. Eq.
11
). The literature value of the group velocity in air

v_{g,\text{air}}^{\text{lit}}(\lambda=430\,\mathrm{nm})=(0.999700\pm 0.000005)\,\mathrm{c}

(14)

is known very precisely and can be derived from the chromatic dispersion (
c
is the speed of light in vacuum)
[
18
]
. Since this value is known about 60 times more precisely than the relative uncertainty
of the free spectral width
\Delta\nu
(cf.
3.1
), the achievable precision of
CELLPALS
to measure the group velocity
v_{g}
in a sample is limited by this uncertainty of
\sim 0.03\,\mathrm{\%}
.

On the other hand, according to
11
, the cavity length

L=\frac{v_{g,\text{air}}^{\text{lit}}}{2\cdot\Delta\nu_{\text{air}}}

(15)

can also be determined in air with the same accuracy of
0.03\,\mathrm{\%}
if the group velocity is known.

3.3
Achievable precision of the attenuation length

In order to determine the attenuation length
L_{\text{att}}
with only one cavity, the reflectivity
R
of the mirrors must be known. The manufacturer specifies the reflectivity in air as
R=0.95\pm 0.01
, but this value
changes in contact with different liquids
[
19
]
. If this uncertainty is taken into account for a cavity length of
L=0.5\,\mathrm{m}
, the systematic uncertainty
\text{d}L_{\text{att}}^{\text{syst}}/L_{\text{att}}=\left\lvert L_{\text{att}}L^{-1}\cdot\text{d}R^{\text{syst}}R^{-1}\right\rvert
for media with an attenuation length

L_{\text{att}}>10\,\mathrm{m}
is more than
20\,\mathrm{\%}
. The uncertainty of the reflectivity provides the largest systematic error when determining the attenuation length with only one cavity length, therefore it should be eliminated. This
can be achieved by determining the attenuation parameter
A_{\text{eff}}(L)
for more than one resonator length. A variable length cavity design is used in
CELLPALS
(cf.
4
), which provides any resonator
length
L
on a continuous scale. The maximum length
L_{\text{max}}=2r
is limited by the stability criterion in
16
, which depends on the radii of curvature
r
of the concave
resonator mirrors. The minimum length
L_{\text{min}}=v_{g}/(2\Delta\nu_{\text{max}})
corresponds to the maximal detectable frequency of the free spectral range
\Delta\nu_{\text{max}}\sim 350\,\mathrm{MHz}
which is limited by the hardware components of the setup and depends on the group
velocity of the sample (cf.
4
).

In order to determine
A_{\text{eff}}(L)
, however, the chosen cavity length
L
must be known for each measurement. For this purpose, a reference length
L_{0}
is determined according to
15
by measuring the free spectral width in air
\Delta\nu_{0,\text{air}}
. After that, the sample is filled into the cavity and the new free spectral width
\Delta\nu_{0}
is measured at the same resonator length
L_{0}
. Any new length
L_{i}=\Delta\nu_{0}/\Delta\nu_{i}\cdot L_{0}
chosen can now be determined by the new free spectral width
\Delta\nu_{i}
. The attenuation length
L_{\text{att}}
is determined from the measured attenuation parameters
A_{\text{eff}}(L)
, using the least squares fitting method with Eq.
12
as the fit function.

The achievable precision of the attenuation length measurement depends on the number of resonator lengths used, as demonstrated in Fig.

With this measurement method, the systematic uncertainty due to the lack of precise knowledge of the mirror reflectivity
R
is eliminated. This method can even be used to determine the reflectivity of the mirrors in different media with a higher precision than the manufacturer’s specifications.

3.4
Systematic effects of incomplete collimation of the cavity beam

Due to the beam cross-section of the laser light and the small sensitive area of the
APD
(cf.
4
), it has to be ensured that the total intensity of the cavity beam is detected. In order to estimate
the effect of incomplete collimation on the measurements, the expected beam alignment for a certain cavity length was calculated by using Gaussian beam optics and ray transfer matrix
[
19
]
.

The blue dots show the measured signal of a
L=80\,\mathrm{cm}

As the cavity meets the stability criterion

0\leq g^{2}\leq 1\quad\text{with}\quad g=1+\frac{L}{r}\,,

(16)

which depends on the cavity length
L
and on the radii of curvature
r
of the concave resonator mirrors, the rays do not leave the cavity. If the outgoing intensity is not fully collimated, there will be an infinite number of individual rays
I_{n}(t)
which, according to
6
, no longer contribute to the total outgoing intensity
I_{\text{out}}(t)
.

In Fig.
7
, the blue dots show the fully collimated measurements of the amplitude ratio (upper plot) and the phase shift (lower plot) of an
80\,\mathrm{cm}
cavity filled with
LAB
. In this case, the data is in agreement with the analytical fit functions in Eqs.
9
and
10
, which is not the case for incomplete collimation (yellow dots). To estimate the effect of incomplete collimation, the expected signal for the same cavity was calculated if the rays indicated by
\mathcal{Q}_{\tilde{B}}
(cf. calculated beam alignment in the upper left subplot in Fig.
7
) do not contribute to the total intensity (cf. yellow line). This result was experimentally confirmed by covering the respective rays in
\mathcal{Q}_{\tilde{B}}
with a piece of paper (cf. yellow dots). Several analyses of this kind show that an incomplete collimation of the outgoing intensity always results in characteristic deviations, which occur correlated in the amplitude ratio and in the phase shift and are also reflected in some measurements. These characteristic deviations provide a strong but simple criterion for incomplete collimation and allow us to dismiss such measurements.

4.2
Optical design and the variable-length cavity design

The optical design used in
CELLPALS
is illustrated in Fig.
2
. The light emitted by the

is initially split into a reference beam with
10\,\mathrm{\%}
and a cavity beam with
90\,\mathrm{\%}

LDs

APD

APD

A variable-length cavity was designed, made of stainless steel and coated with adcoat®32/2889
PTFE
(
PTFE
) by the Adelhelm company to avoid possible reactions between the stainless steel and the sample (cf. Fig.
9

5
UV-Vis method for determining the attenuation length

A common method for determining the transparency of liquids is UV–Vis spectroscopy, which uses a cuvette filled with the sample. The attenuation of the intensity caused by the sample is analysed by comparing the original
intensity
I_{0}
with the intensity
I(L)
after traversing the cuvette of length
L
. Unlike
CELLPALS
, however, the UV–Vis method does not use an optical resonator, so the effective light path
is limited to
L=10\,\mathrm{cm}
with conventional devices. The UV–Vis method is based on a wavelength-dependent measurement of the absorbance
\mathcal{A}(L)

[
27
]
which can be transferred into the attenuation length

L_{\text{att}}(\mathcal{A})
:

\mathcal{A}(L)=\log_{10}\left(\frac{I_{0}}{I(L)}\right)\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad L_{\text{att}}(\mathcal{A})=\frac{L\cdot\log_{10}(e)}{\mathcal{A}{}(L)}\,.

(17)

The absorbance of a cuvette filled with a sample can be described by

\mathcal{A}_{\text{full}}(\lambda)=2\mathcal{A}_{R_{CA}}(\lambda)+2\mathcal{A}_{R_{CS}}(\lambda)+2\mathcal{A}_{\text{w}}(\lambda)+\mathcal{A}_{S}(\lambda)\,,

(18)

where

1.

\mathcal{A}_{R_{CA}}(\lambda)=-\log_{10}(1-R_{CA}(\lambda))
describes the reflection at the surface between cuvette and air with the reflection coefficient
R_{CA}(\lambda)

2.

\mathcal{A}_{R_{CS}}(\lambda)=-\log_{10}(1-R_{CS}(\lambda))
describes the reflection at the surface between the cuvette and the sample

3.

\mathcal{A}_{\text{w}}(\lambda)=d_{C}/L_{\text{att,C}}(\lambda)\cdot\log_{10}(e)
describes the attenuation by passing the glass of the cuvette

4.

\mathcal{A}_{S}(\lambda)=L/L_{\text{att}}(\lambda)\cdot\log_{10}(e)
describes the absorbance by the sample and can be used to determine the attenuation length
L_{\text{att}}(\lambda)
.

Since
\mathcal{A}_{R_{CA}}(430\,\mathrm{nm})\approx 1.6\cdot 10^{-2}

[
28
,
29
]
is significantly larger than the attenuation by the sample
\mathcal{A}_{S}(L_{\text{att}}>10\,\mathrm{m})<4.3\cdot 10^{-3}
, the absorbance of the empty cuvette

\mathcal{A}_{\text{empty}}(\lambda)=4\mathcal{A}_{R_{CA}}(\lambda)+2\mathcal{A}_{\text{w}}(\lambda)

(19)

is also measured in the UV–Vis method. With

\mathcal{A}_{\text{sub}}(\lambda)=\mathcal{A}_{\text{full}}(\lambda)-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{A}_{\text{empty}}(\lambda)\geq 0\,,

(20)

\mathcal{A}_{R_{CA}}(\lambda)
can be eliminated. According to Eqs.
18
,
19
and
20
, the absorbance of the sample can now be determined by

\mathcal{A}_{S}(\lambda)=\mathcal{A}_{\text{sub}}(\lambda)-\mathcal{A}_{R_{CS}}(\lambda)-\mathcal{A}_{\text{w}}(\lambda)\,.

(21)

Since
\mathcal{A}_{R_{CS}}(\lambda)
and
\mathcal{A}_{\text{w}}(\lambda)
are not known exactly, the following assumptions are made: first,
\mathcal{A}_{R_{CS}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\text{w}}
are assumed to be wavelength-independent in the measured range and, furthermore, an infinite transparency
\mathcal{A}_{S}(\lambda_{\text{min}})=0
of the sample is assumed for the minimum absorbance at the wavelength
\lambda_{\text{min}}
. Thus
\mathcal{A}_{\text{min}}=\mathcal{A}_{\text{sub}}(\lambda_{\text{min}})
is associated with
\mathcal{A}_{\text{min}}=\mathcal{A}_{R_{CS}}+\mathcal{A}_{\text{w}}
and the absorbance of the sample is determined by

\mathcal{A}_{S}(\lambda)=\mathcal{A}_{\text{sub}}(\lambda)-\mathcal{A}_{\text{min}}

(22)

and is then converted to the attenuation length according to
17
.

).

shows the measured absorbance of the full and empty cuvette

and

for a typical sample. In addition, also

is inserted into the plot, which illustrates that the subtracted absorbance
\mathcal{A}_{\text{sub}}
in
20
, contrary to the theoretical consideration, provides negative values for the minimum absorbance. By setting this minimum value to
\mathcal{A}_{\text{sub}}(\lambda_{\text{min}})=0
, the absorbance is shifted towards higher values, which leads to a systematic underestimation of the attenuation length for highly transparent media. Since this effect cannot be explained by the theoretical considerations, it is difficult to estimate the extent of the resulting systematic uncertainty.

7
Conclusion and Outlook

CELLPALS
was developed as a method for precise attenuation length measurements for highly transparent media, that does not require a complex setup with a long sample holder. In direct comparison to the UV–Vis method, the
CELLPALS
measurements with three fixed cavity lengths provided significantly more precise results with an average relative uncertainty of
\sim 7\,\mathrm{\%}
(cf.
Tab.
1
). The achievable precision of the attenuation length with the
CELLPALS
method can be reduced to
\sim 2\,\mathrm{\%}
if more resonator lengths are used with a variable-length cavity (cf.
3.3
). In addition, the UV–Vis method reveals previously unknown effects that lead to a systematic underestimation of the attenuation length for very transparent media (cf.
6.1
). This is also confirmed by the comparison with the results obtained with the
CELLPALS
method in
Tab.
1
.

In terms of precision, the performance of
CELLPALS
is comparable to the methods using a vertical tube
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
which is around
\sim 2.5\,\mathrm{\%}
. However, due to its
compact design,
CELLPALS
is more flexible in terms of exchanging samples and its installation location and requires less than
1\,\mathrm{litre}
of sample for one measurement. In addition, the
CELLPALS
method enables
the determination of the group velocity with a relative uncertainty of
\sim 0.03\,\mathrm{\%}
(cf.
3.2
), which is not provided by any other method for determining the attenuation length.

CELLPALS
supports the measurement of the attenuation length and the group velocity of any transparent liquid (like
LS
or water) for a user-defined wavelength of the laser diode. The only requirement is an amplitude-modulated intensity up to
\sim 350\,\mathrm{MHz}
to determine the free spectral width of the resonator. For strong deviations from
\lambda=430\,\mathrm{nm}
, individual optical components such as the resonator mirrors or the beam splitter may have to be replaced.

The
CELLPALS
method still poses some challenges, as the data for some resonator lengths show deviations that are larger than the corresponding fit errors (see Fig.
11
), which are probably related to the collimation of the measured intensity behind the cavity. As discussed in
3.1
incomplete collimation leads to characteristic deviations in the amplitude ratio
\mathcal{A}(\nu)
and the phase shift
\Delta\varphi(\nu)
(cf. Fig.
7
) and can thus be identified very clearly. To avoid such deviations, a larger sensitive area of the
APD
s used would be useful in the future.

Intensity inside the cavity

By substituting
J=IT^{2}\exp(-{L}/{L_{\text{att}}})
and
\tilde{t}(t)=t-\tau/2
and by defining the attenuation parameter

A_{\text{eff}}\equiv e^{a}\quad\text{with}\quad a=\ln(R^{2})-\frac{2L}{L_{\text{att}}}\,,

(A.1)

the total intensity in
3
(cf.
2.1
) is reduced to

I_{\text{out}}(t)=Je^{i\omega\tilde{t}}\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(e^{a-i\omega\tau}\right)^{n}\,.

(A.2)

With
z=a-i\omega\tau~\in~\mathbb{C}
the sum corresponds to a geometric series with the solution

Z=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(e^{z}\right)^{n}=\frac{1}{1-e^{z}}~\in~\mathbb{C}\quad\text{if}\quad\left\lvert e^{z}\right\rvert<1\,,

which is true due to
A_{\text{eff}}<1
(cf. Eq.
A.1
). Therefore, Eq.
A.2
can be calculated to

I_{\text{out}}(t)=Je^{i\omega\tilde{t}}~Z\quad\text{with}\quad Z=\frac{1}{1-A_{\text{eff}}\cdot e^{-i\omega\tau}}~\in~\mathbb{C}\,.

(A.3)

By using the polar form
Z=|Z|\exp(i\phi)~\in~\mathbb{C}
and by substituting
J
and
\tilde{t}(t)
back, the total intensity
I_{\text{out}}(t)
in
A.3
can be reduced to a simple oscillation

I_{\text{out}}(t)\equiv\tilde{I}(\nu)\exp(i\left[2\pi\nu t-\Delta\phi(\nu)\right])

\tilde{I}(\nu)=\frac{IT^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{L}{L_{\text{att}}}\right)}{\sqrt{A_{\text{eff}}^{2}-2A_{\text{eff}}\cos(2\pi\nu\tau)+1}}~\in~\mathbb{R}

(A.4)

and the real phase shift

\Delta\phi(\omega)=\arctan\left(\frac{A_{\text{eff}}\sin(2\pi\nu\tau)}{1-A_{\text{eff}}\cos(2\pi\nu\tau)}\right)+\pi\nu\tau~\in~\mathbb{R}\,.
