Copper coating of magnesium alloy AZ31 using conventional electrode EDM for enhanced corrosion resistance
Authors: Kumail Hussain, Sadaqat Ali, Ammar Tariq, Waqas Alam, Mohammed Jameel, Jana Petrů, Muhammad Nasir Bashir, Niaz B Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMagnesium alloys such as AZ31 are promising for biomedical applications; however, their rapid corrosion in physiological environments limits their use. Many surface modification techniques for improving corrosion resistance, including powder-mixed electrical discharge machining (EDM), involve added process complexity and limited reproducibility. In this study, copper surface modification of AZ31 magnesium alloy was achieved using conventional electrode EDM without the use of external powders. A Taguchi L9 experimental design was employed to investigate the influence of peak current, pulse-on time, pulse-off time, and gap voltage on surface integrity, mechanical response, and corrosion behaviour. Surface and phase characterization were conducted using SEM, EDS, and XRD, while corrosion performance was assessed through potentiodynamic polarization testing in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The results show that EDM processing produced a continuous copper-coated surface layer (with a thickness of approximately 50–60 μm) with reduced surface roughness and increased surface hardness under optimized conditions. Electrochemical testing indicated a reduction in corrosion current density from 9.49 to 2.42 µA/cm², corresponding to a decrease in corrosion rate from 0.111 mm/year to 0.028 mm/year; an improvement of approximately 75%. These findings demonstrate that conventional electrode EDM is a practical approach for modifying the surface characteristics and improving the corrosion behavior of AZ31 magnesium alloy, with potential relevance for biodegradable implant-related applications.Keywords: Magnesium alloy AZ31, Copper, EDM, Conventional electrode, Coating, Biocompatibility, Biodegradable, Corrosion resistanceSubject terms: Chemistry, Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Orthopedic fixation devices are commonly used to support bone healing in load-bearing or displaced fractures when natural regeneration is insufficient1. Among other metals, magnesium alloys, particularly AZ31, have attracted considerable attention for temporary biomedical applications due to their low density, and favorable mechanical properties that resemble those of natural bone. These attributes make magnesium alloys promising candidates for orthopedic and cardiovascular components that are intended to provide temporary mechanical support during healing2,3. However, a major limitation of magnesium alloys is their high corrosion rate under physiological conditions, which can lead to premature loss of mechanical integrity and restrict their long-term in vivo stability.

To address this challenge, surface modification techniques have been extensively investigated to improve the corrosion resistance and surface integrity of magnesium alloys while preserving their bulk properties. Among these techniques, electrical discharge machining (EDM)4,5 has evolved beyond a conventional material removal process into a viable method for surface modification and coating through a mechanism commonly referred to as electrical discharge coating (EDC)6,7. During EDC, material from the tool electrode is transferred to the workpiece surface under high-energy electrical discharges, forming a modified surface layer with altered composition and properties8.

Recent studies have demonstrated that conventional solid electrodes can serve as sacrificial coating sources in EDM processes without the need for externally added powders. This approach offers a simpler and more reproducible alternative to powder-mixed EDM (PMEDM), which often suffers from challenges related to powder dispersion, agglomeration, and process instability9,10. In particular, copper has received attention as an electrode material due to its electrical conductivity, corrosion resistance, and reported antibacterial activity, which may be advantageous from a surface-engineering perspective for biomedical-related applications11. Consequently, the use of conventional copper electrodes in EDM presents a practical route for modifying the surface characteristics of magnesium alloys without the complexity associated with powder-assisted techniques.

The feasibility of EDM-based surface modification has been demonstrated across a range of metallic systems. For example, electrode material transfer during EDM has been shown to enhance surface hardness and modify microstructure through the incorporation of tool and dielectric elements12. Surface alloying using copper-based powder metallurgy electrodes has also been reported to improve mechanical performance in tool steels13. Although PMEDM has been widely explored for such purposes14, its scalability and reproducibility remain limited due to additional equipment requirements and process variability. In contrast, EDC using solid copper electrodes offers a more straightforward and controllable approach, with studies reporting consistent deposition behavior and improved surface characteristics15,16.

AZ31 is one of the most extensively studied magnesium alloys due to its high strength-to-weight ratio and biodegradability17. Nevertheless, its susceptibility to rapid corrosion, particularly in chloride-containing environments such as phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and simulated body fluid (SBF), remains a significant concern18,19. Various surface modification strategies including sol-gel coatings20,21, plasma electrolytic oxidation22,23, and EDM-based coating approaches24 have been employed to mitigate this issue. Despite these efforts, relatively limited work has focused on copper surface modification of AZ31 using conventional EDM electrodes, particularly with an emphasis on corrosion behavior in physiologically relevant environments.

Copper-based surface modification via EDM has been reported to improve surface integrity and functional performance in several studies. Uniform copper deposition has been shown to enhance surface hardness and reduce microcrack formation on steel substrates25. In related work, ceramic-particle-assisted EDM coatings on magnesium alloys have resulted in significant improvements in surface hardness, wear resistance, and frictional behavior26,27. Surface properties such as microhardness, surface roughness, and recast layer characteristics are especially important in applications where surface integrity influences mechanical durability and corrosion response28. From a surface-engineering standpoint, copper modification of AZ31 via EDM may lead to the formation of a dense surface layer capable of restricting the ingress of corrosive ions, thereby improving corrosion performance and surface stability26,29.

Although EDM-based surface modification and coating strategies have been explored for magnesium alloys, most reported studies rely on powder-mixed EDM or ceramic particle incorporation to achieve surface enhancement14,23,24,26,27. Systematic investigations focusing on copper surface modification of AZ31 using conventional solid EDM electrodes, particularly with an emphasis on correlating process parameters with surface integrity and corrosion behavior in physiologically relevant environments, remain limited.

In this context, the present study investigates copper surface modification of AZ31 magnesium alloy using conventional electrode EDM. Rather than introducing external powders or complex hybrid processes, this work focuses on utilizing a solid copper electrode to achieve surface modification through controlled EDM parameters. The influence of peak current, pulse-on time, pulse-off time, and gap voltage on surface roughness, microhardness, and electrochemical corrosion behavior in phosphate-buffered saline is systematically evaluated using a Taguchi L9 experimental design. Surface morphology, composition, and phase constitution are examined using SEM, EDS, and XRD. The novelty of this work lies in providing a systematic assessment of copper surface modification of AZ31 via conventional EDM and correlating process parameters with surface integrity and corrosion behavior, offering insights relevant to surface-engineering strategies for biodegradable magnesium alloys with potential biomedical relevance.

Materials and methods

This section outlines the materials preparation, experimental setup for EDM, and characterization techniques employed to investigate copper surface modification of AZ31 magnesium alloy. A structured experimental methodology based on a Taguchi L9 orthogonal array was adopted to evaluate the influence of EDM parameters on surface roughness, microhardness, and corrosion behaviour. The process was carried out in three phases: Phase I involved sample preparation and copper coating using die-sinking EDM; Phase II included surface and mechanical characterization through SEM, EDS, XRD, surface roughness, hardness, and corrosion testing; and Phase III focused on parametric optimization using Minitab to analyze the influence of input variables on surface roughness and hardness.

Materials procurement and sample preparation

AZ31 magnesium alloy plates and high-purity copper cylindrical rods were procured from Wuxi Eternal Bliss Alloy Casting and Forging Co. Ltd, China. The AZ31 plates had a thickness of 6 mm and were used as the substrate material due to their low density and relevance for surface modification studies of biodegradable magnesium alloys2. The plates were sectioned into specimens with dimensions of 10 mm × 10 mm × 6 mm using wire-cut electrical discharge machining (Model DK77, Jiangsu Sanxing Machinery Manufacture Co. Ltd, China). These dimensions were selected to ensure consistent coating conditions and suitability for surface and corrosion characterization30. High-purity copper cylindrical rods with a diameter of 10 mm were used as the tool electrode. The flat circular end of the copper rod acted as the active discharge surface during EDM processing. The electrode geometry and diameter were kept constant throughout all experiments to maintain comparable discharge conditions and deposition behaviour. Copper was selected due to its electrical conductivity and suitability for EDM-based surface modification Kerosene oil, obtained from NUST MRC, was used as the dielectric fluid during the EDM process. Experimental setup

Copper surface modification of AZ31 magnesium alloy was performed using a die-sinking EDM machine, EDM-71 series (Model EDM 7132, Beijing Sinofound Co. Ltd, China). During processing, both the copper electrode and AZ31 workpiece were fully submerged in a kerosene-based dielectric fluid to maintain discharge stability and debris removal. Controlled electrical discharges between the electrode and the workpiece enabled localized melting and material transfer from the copper electrode to the substrate surface. Four EDM input parameters including peak current, pulse-on time, pulse-off time, and gap voltage were varied at three levels each, as listed in Table 1. A total of nine experimental runs were conducted according to the Taguchi L9 orthogonal array to systematically evaluate the influence of these parameters on surface characteristics.

Design of experiment

The experimental design followed a Taguchi L9 orthogonal array implemented using Minitab 19 software. This approach enabled the evaluation of the main effects of four process parameters at three levels while minimizing the total number of experimental trials. The Taguchi method was selected to efficiently identify dominant process parameters influencing surface roughness and microhardness without requiring a full factorial design, which would significantly increase experimental complexity. Copper surface modification via EDM

Copper surface modification was achieved by employing the copper electrode as a sacrificial material source during EDM processing. Material transfer from the electrode to the AZ31 substrate occurred during electrical discharges, resulting in a copper-coated surface layer. After processing, all coated specimens were subjected to surface, mechanical, and electrochemical characterization to assess coating quality and surface performance.

Surface and microstructural characterization

Surface Characterization was conducted to evaluate the quality, integrity, and corrosion performance of the copper-coated AZ31 magnesium alloy samples. All characterization tests were performed on three specimens per processing condition to ensure repeatability and reduce experimental variability.

For detailed SEM, EDS, XRD, and corrosion characterization, three Cu-coated AZ31 specimens were selected based on the combined evaluation of surface roughness and microhardness results. Sample 1, processed at 4.5 A current, 90 µs pulse-on time, 90 µs pulse-off time, and 40 V, was selected because it produced the lowest surface roughness value of 0.689 μm. Sample 3, processed at 4.5 A current, 300 µs pulse-on time, 200 µs pulse-off time, and 70 V, was selected because it showed the highest microhardness value of 780.82 HV while maintaining a low surface roughness value of 0.924 μm. Sample 4, processed at 9.0 A current, 90 µs pulse-on time, 150 µs pulse-off time, and 70 V, was selected because it showed good combined performance, with a surface roughness value of 0.953 μm and a microhardness value of 600.20 HV. These selected specimens were further analyzed, and average values were reported where applicable to minimize experimental variation. The selection was made because lower surface roughness, higher hardness, and a relatively uniform copper-modified surface layer are important indicators of improved surface integrity and corrosion performance. Uncoated AZ31 specimens were also tested as reference samples for comparison.

Surface roughness measurement

Surface roughness and optical microstructural observations were performed using an Olympus DSX-1000 digital microscope [Olympus Corporation, Japan], as it is designed for high-precision surface and microstructure analysis. Surface roughness was measured under brightfield illumination. For each coated specimen, roughness measurements were taken at three different locations on the treated surface, and the average value was reported. The surface profile was recorded using a pitch size/resolution of 0.794 μm/pixel. The average surface roughness, Ra, was calculated as the arithmetic mean deviation of the measured surface profile from the mean line to analyze the effect of EDM parameters on surface quality.

Micro-hardness test

The microhardness was measured using a Micro-Vickers hardness tester (Model 401/402 MVD, WOLPERT Measuring Equipment Co. Ltd, Shanghai). A load of 500 g was applied with a dwell time of 12 s for each indentation. Measurements were taken at three different locations on each sample surface, and the average value was reported to minimize localized variation effects.

Surface morphology and coating thickness (SEM)

SEM analysis was performed using Scanning Electron Microscope (JEOL Model JSM-6490LV, Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV, images were captured at different magnifications, including ×60 and ×150 and a spot size setting of 53. Secondary electron imaging was used to observe surface morphology and coating features. It was used to examine the surface morphology and cross-sectional thickness of copper-coated AZ31 samples. Images were captured at high magnification, and coating thickness was determined by averaging measurements at three different points.

Elemental composition (EDS)

Elemental composition of the coated surface was determined using Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) (JEOL Model JSM-6490LV, Japan). EDS analysis was conducted using the attached EDS detector Potentiostat to identify the elemental composition of the copper-modified AZ31 surface.

Phase analysis via XRD

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed using an XPERT-PRO diffractometer equipped with a PW3050/60 theta/theta goniometer. The analysis was conducted using Cu radiation with Kα1 wavelength of 1.54060 Å and Kα2 wavelength of 1.54443 Å. The generator settings were 40 kV and 30 mA. The diffraction patterns were recorded in continuous scan mode over a 2θ range of 10.0125° to 79.9875°, with a step size of 0.0250° 2θ and a scan step time of 0.6000 s. A fixed divergence slit of 1.0000° and receiving slit size of 0.1000 mm were used. The measurement was performed at 25 °C. The obtained diffraction peaks were used to identify the crystalline phases present on the copper-coated AZ31 magnesium alloy surface.

Corrosion resistance evaluation

Electrochemical corrosion testing was performed using a potentiostat (Model CS350M, CorrTest Instruments, China) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), which was purchased from Bioworld, at 37 °C to simulate a physiological environment for evaluating the corrosion behavior of AZ31 magnesium alloy3. For each condition, three samples were tested, and the average values were reported, and the potentiodynamic polarization scan was conducted at a scan rate of 10 mV/s. The current limit of this Potentiostat is 2 A while Potential limit is 2.5 V. Initial potential was − 2.0 V while final potential was 1.0 V.

Potentiodynamic polarization testing was carried out using a conventional three-electrode electrochemical cell. Ag/AgCl (3 M KCl) was used as the reference electrode, platinum wire was used for the counter electrode, and the working electrode consisted of the substrate material, namely Sample 1 that was uncoated magnesium alloy AZ31 and Sample 2 which was Cu-coated magnesium alloy AZ31. This configuration was used to obtain the electrochemical response of both uncoated and copper-coated specimens under identical testing conditions.

The polarization results were used to determine corrosion potential (Ecorr) and corrosion current density (Icorr). These values were then used to evaluate the corrosion resistance of the copper-coated AZ31 sample relative to the uncoated AZ31 reference sample.

Results and discussion

This section presents the effects of copper surface modification of AZ31 magnesium alloy achieved through conventional electrode EDM. Surface roughness, microhardness, microstructural features, phase constitution, and electrochemical corrosion behaviour were evaluated to assess the influence of EDM parameters and the resulting surface integrity.

Surface roughness analysis and optimization

Surface roughness plays a critical role in determining surface integrity and corrosion behavior of EDM-processed materials. The average surface roughness (Ra) values obtained from the Taguchi L9 experimental array are summarized in Table 3, ranging from 0.689 μm to 2.045 μm. The lowest Ra value was obtained at lower current and shorter pulse duration, while higher current and longer pulse-on time led to increased roughness. This behavior can be explained based on the discharge energy associated with the EDM process, which is governed by peak current and pulse duration. Each electrical discharge generates a localized high-temperature plasma channel, resulting in melting and partial vaporization of the material. At lower energy input, the size of the molten pool is reduced, leading to the formation of smaller and shallower craters upon resolidification. This results in a more uniform surface morphology with lower Ra values. In contrast, higher peak current and longer pulse-on time increase the discharge energy, producing larger molten pools and deeper craters. The subsequent rapid solidification leads to the formation of irregular recast layers and surface asperities, thereby increasing surface roughness.

Microhardness evaluation and optimization

The microhardness of copper-coated AZ31 surfaces exhibited significant variation across different EDM conditions (Table 5), with values ranging from 250.04 HV to 780.82 HV. The hardness profile shown in Fig. 2 (b) indicates that the maximum hardness was obtained for Sample 03, which was processed at the lowest current (4.5 A) and longest pulse-on time (300 µs), suggesting that this increase in hardness may be attributed to controlled thermal input during EDM, which promotes rapid solidification and the formation of a refined recast layer, leading to improved surface hardness5,9. On the other hand, Sample 09, machined at the highest current (12 A) and longest pulse durations, exhibited the lowest hardness, indicating excessive discharge energy may reduce hardness by producing non-uniform deposition, coating defects, or thermal softening of the recast layer5,6,9.

Phase analysis (XRD)

X-ray diffraction analysis was conducted to identify crystalline phases present on the modified surface. Figure 3(a) shows the XRD pattern of uncoated AZ31 indicating characteristic peaks corresponding to the hexagonal close-packed magnesium matrix. In contrast, the copper-coated sample shown in Fig. 3(b) exhibits additional diffraction peaks corresponding to face-centered cubic (FCC) metallic copper, while magnesium peaks remain detectable.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of AZ31 magnesium alloy showing (a) diffraction pattern of uncoated AZ31, (b) diffraction pattern of copper-coated AZ31 after EDM processing, and (c) comparative XRD patterns highlighting the presence of metallic copper phases and the retention of the magnesium substrate peaks.

The comparative XRD pattern as shown in Fig. 3(c) highlights the emergence of crystalline metallic copper on the AZ31 surface. No distinct diffraction peaks attributable to crystalline copper oxide phases were detected within the resolution of the measurement. This suggests that copper deposition during EDM primarily occurs in metallic form, while any oxide species, if present, are likely amorphous or below the detection limit of XRD. The absence of some expected phases may be due to their low volume fraction, amorphous nature, or concentration below the detection limit of XRD31.

Surface morphology and elemental composition (SEM/EDS)

Surface morphology and cross-sectional characteristics of the copper-coated AZ31 magnesium alloy were examined using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) and Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS).

Figure 4 shows cross-sectional SEM image of the copper coated AZ31 magnesium alloy indicating a distinct interface between the deposited copper coating and the AZ31 substrate. The coating is uniform and well-bonded, without signs of delamination. The enhanced view shown in Fig. 4 highlights the copper coating thickness ranging between 55 and 60 microns, indicating consistent deposition during the EDM process. This thickness lies within the optimal range for surface protection without altering the dimensional accuracy of components, especially in biomedical applications.

Cross-sectional SEM image of the copper-coated surface layer and coating thickness measurement.

The 3D surface profile in Fig. 5 and optical microscopy image in Fig. 5 reveal a cratered texture typical of EDM processed surfaces. This topography results from rapid melting and solidification of the copper electrode during spark erosion, leading to the formation of recast layers with micro voids and globules. A uniform and compact modified surface is beneficial for implant-related applications because surface topography and coating integrity can influence adhesion, corrosion behavior, and biological response2,4,5.

Corrosion behaviour in simulated body fluid (PBS)

The electrochemical corrosion resistance of the copper-coated AZ31 magnesium alloy was evaluated via potentiodynamic polarization testing in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), simulating a physiological environment. The corrosion parameters were extracted by applying Tafel extrapolation to the anodic and cathodic branches of the polarization curves. Derived values of corrosion potential (Ecorr), corrosion current density (Icorr), Tafel slopes (Ba, Bc), and corrosion rate are summarized in 8, while the corresponding polarization curves are presented in Fig. 7.

Potentiodynamic polarization curves of uncoated and copper-modified AZ31 in PBS.

The uncoated AZ31 alloy showed a corrosion potential of − 1347.5 mV and a relatively high corrosion current density of 9.49 µA/cm2, corresponding to a corrosion rate of 0.11131 mm/year. In contrast, the copper-coated AZ31 sample displayed a lower Ecorr of − 1256.1 mV and a significantly lower Icorr of 2.42 µA/cm2, resulting in a substantially reduced corrosion rate of 0.028344 mm/year. These values indicate that the copper coating markedly enhanced the corrosion resistance of the AZ31 substrate3,10.

The polarization behavior, as shown in Table 8, reveal a reduction in both anodic and cathodic current densities for the coated sample, indicating suppression of both metal dissolution and cathodic reactions. The shift in Ecorr towards less negative values further suggests enhanced surface stability in the PBS environment.

The improved corrosion behavior can be attributed to the formation of a relatively continuous and adherent copper-modified surface layer, as confirmed by SEM analysis. This layer acts as a physical barrier, limiting direct exposure of the magnesium substrate to the electrolyte and thereby reducing the rate of anodic dissolution. In addition, the copper-rich recast layer formed during EDM may partially fill surface irregularities, reducing the number of active corrosion sites.

It is also important to consider the potential galvanic interaction between copper and magnesium, as copper is more noble than magnesium. In cases of discontinuous or porous coatings, such coupling may accelerate localized corrosion. However, the relatively uniform surface coverage observed in this study likely minimizes such galvanic effects, resulting in an overall improvement in corrosion resistance.

The potentiodynamic polarization curves in Fig. 7 show the relationship between potential and current density for the uncoated AZ31 and Cu-coated AZ31 samples in PBS at 37 °C. Both curves show typical cathodic and anodic regions around the corrosion potential. The cathodic branch appears at lower potentials, while the anodic branch appears as the potential increases.

The Cu-coated AZ31 curve is shifted slightly toward a less negative potential compared with the uncoated AZ31 curve. This shift indicates that the coated sample requires a higher potential to initiate active corrosion, suggesting improved electrochemical stability. Near the corrosion potential region, the Cu-coated sample also shows a lower current density response than the uncoated AZ31, which reflects reduced corrosion activity.

In the cathodic branch, the current density gradually decreases toward the corrosion potential, showing the reduction reaction behavior in PBS. In the anodic branch, the current density increases as the potential becomes more positive, indicating the onset of metal dissolution. However, the Cu-coated sample shows a comparatively lower current density over the active corrosion region, confirming that the EDM-produced copper-modified surface layer improves the polarization response of AZ31 magnesium alloy.

Conclusion

This study examined copper surface modification of AZ31 magnesium alloy using conventional electrode electrical discharge machining (EDM) and evaluated the influence of key process parameters on surface roughness, microhardness, and electrochemical behavior. The results show that discharge energy, governed primarily by peak current and pulse-on time, strongly influences surface characteristics.

Lower peak current and shorter pulse-on time were found to produce smoother surfaces with lower surface roughness, while higher energy input resulted in increased roughness due to the formation of deeper craters and irregular recast layers. In contrast, microhardness was enhanced due to the formation of a hardened recast layer under controlled processing conditions.

Based on the Taguchi analysis, the optimal parameter combination for achieving improved surface integrity and corrosion resistance corresponds to lower peak current and moderate pulse-on time (4.5 A peak current, 90 µs pulse-on time, 90 µs pulse-off time, and 40 V), yielding the lowest Ra value of 0.689 μm. In comparison, the maximum microhardness (780 HV) was obtained at 4.5 A, 300 µs pulse-on time, 200 µs pulse-off time, and 70 V, indicating that longer pulse duration promotes recast layer hardening.

Electrochemical testing confirmed a significant reduction in corrosion current density and corrosion rate for the coated samples compared to the uncoated AZ31 alloy. The improved corrosion resistance is attributed to the formation of a continuous copper-modified surface layer (50–60 μm), which acts as a barrier to electrolyte interaction.

These findings demonstrate that conventional electrode EDM is an effective strategy for improving the surface properties of AZ31 magnesium alloy. The selection of optimal parameters depends on the targeted performance objective, with lower discharge energy favoring surface smoothness and controlled higher pulse durations enhancing surface hardness, both contributing to improved corrosion behavior in biodegradable implant-related applications.
