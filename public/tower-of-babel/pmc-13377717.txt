Persistence of mechanoreceptors in ACL remnants: a histological and immunohistochemical analysis of 128 patients
Authors: Cahit Ancar, Nilüfer Dönmezdil, Fırat Aşır
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnterior cruciate ligament (ACL) remnants may retain sensory neural elements after rupture, potentially contributing to proprioceptive function and graft reinnervation following reconstruction. However, the persistence of mechanoreceptors in remnant tissues and the factors influencing their preservation remain incompletely understood. This study aimed to evaluate the presence of neural structures in ACL remnants using histological and immunohistochemical methods and to determine the association between mechanoreceptor preservation, injury duration, and remnant attachment pattern.MethodsACL remnant tissues were obtained from 128 patients undergoing arthroscopic ACL reconstruction. Specimens were examined using hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemistry with S100, myelin basic protein (MBP), and β-III-tubulin antibodies to identify neural elements. Patients were classified according to injury duration as subacute (≤ 3 months), chronic (3–12 months), and late chronic (> 12 months). Remnants were also categorized based on arthroscopic appearance as PCL–femur attachment or tibial stump. Mechanoreceptor presence and distribution were analyzed, and multivariate logistic regression was performed to evaluate factors associated with mechanoreceptor preservation.ResultsMechanoreceptors or neural structures were identified in 81 of 128 specimens (63.3%). Free nerve endings were the most frequently observed neural elements, whereas structured receptors such as Ruffini or Golgi-like endings were less common and Pacinian corpuscles were rare. Mechanoreceptor prevalence differed significantly according to injury duration, being highest in the subacute group (84.8%) and lower in the chronic (58.7%) and late chronic (53.1%) groups (p = 0.01). The remnant attachment pattern did not show a significant association with mechanoreceptor presence (p = 0.23). Logistic regression analysis demonstrated that longer injury duration was independently associated with reduced odds of mechanoreceptor detection.ConclusionsACL remnants frequently retain neural structures, particularly during the early post-injury period, but the prevalence of mechanoreceptors decreases with increasing injury duration. Remnant attachment pattern appears to have a limited influence on neural preservation. These findings support the biological rationale for remnant-preserving reconstruction strategies when technically feasible and highlight the potential importance of early surgical intervention for maintaining proprioceptive tissue integrity.Keywords: Anterior cruciate ligament, ACL remnant, Mechanoreceptors, Proprioception, Remnant-preserving reconstruction, Injury chronicity, Sensory fibers

Introduction

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries are among the most frequent causes of knee instability in young and physically active individuals, with annual incidence rates reaching up to 60–70 per 100,000 person-years [1, 2]. Beyond its mechanical role as a primary restraint to anterior tibial translation, the ACL contains a rich network of sensory nerve fibers and mechanoreceptors that contribute substantially to proprioception and neuromuscular control [3, 4]. Four major mechanoreceptor types have been described within the native ACL: Ruffini endings, Pacinian corpuscles, Golgi-like receptors, and free nerve endings, each playing a distinct role in joint position sense and reflexive stabilization [5, 6].

Following ligament rupture, these sensory structures undergo varying degrees of degeneration depending on injury chronicity, vascular supply, and mechanical integrity of the remnant tissue [7–9]. Increasing evidence suggests that the residual tibial stump may retain viable neural elements capable of supporting reinnervation of the graft after ACL reconstruction [10, 11]. Remnant-preserving surgical techniques have therefore gained attention, with proposed benefits including enhanced graft maturation, improved proprioceptive recovery, and potentially superior functional outcomes [12]. However, the persistence, distribution, and clinical relevance of mechanoreceptors in the ACL remnant remain incompletely understood, and available studies report heterogeneous findings [13, 14].

A key knowledge gap involves how remnant characteristics such as injury-to-surgery interval and attachment pattern (e.g., adherence to the posterior cruciate ligament or femoral notch vs. isolated tibial stump) influence the preservation of neural elements [15, 16]. Understanding these relationships is essential for determining when remnant preservation may provide true biological and functional benefit [17].

The present study aims to investigate the presence and distribution of mechanoreceptors in tibial ACL remnants using hematoxylin-eosin (H&E), S100, and myelin basic protein (MBP) immunohistochemistry in a large patient cohort. We further analyze whether mechanoreceptor persistence is associated with injury duration or remnant attachment pattern. By clarifying the sensory potential of ACL remnants, this study provides histological evidence that may guide the selection and optimization of remnant-preserving reconstruction strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This prospective observational study included patients undergoing arthroscopic ACL reconstruction at our institution. All procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and institutional ethical standards. Ethical approval was obtained from the Health Sciences University Gazi Yaşargil Training and Research Hospital Ethics Committee (Approval No: 244). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to surgery.

Patient selection and clinical data collection

Patients aged 18–50 years who were diagnosed with complete ACL rupture and from whom sufficient tibial remnant tissue could be harvested during arthroscopy were included. Exclusion criteria were previous knee surgery, systemic neurological or rheumatological diseases, inadequately preserved remnant tissue, and samples with histological artifacts. Demographic and clinical variables (age, sex, affected side, mechanism of injury, and time from injury to surgery) were recorded. Injury chronicity was categorized into three groups: subacute (3 weeks–3 months), chronic (4–12 months), and late chronic (> 12 months). This classification was selected to reflect the progressive biological changes occurring in ACL remnants after rupture, including early inflammatory responses, intermediate remodeling and fibrosis, and late-stage tissue degeneration. Similar time-based categorizations have been used in previous histological studies evaluating mechanoreceptor preservation in ACL remnants [16, 18].

Based on arthroscopic appearance, ACL remnants were categorized into two groups (Figs. 1 and 2). The PCL–femur attachment group comprised remnants adherent to the femoral notch or the posterior cruciate ligament, whereas the tibial stump group included remnants attached solely to the tibial footprint without superior adhesion.

Histological processing

Remnant specimens were immediately fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, processed using routine paraffin embedding, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Selected sections were stained with H&E for assessment of tissue architecture, vascularity, fibrosis, and potential mechanoreceptor-like structures.

Immunohistochemistry

Sections designated for immunohistochemical analysis underwent deparaffinization in xylene, rehydration through graded ethanol, and heat-induced antigen retrieval. S100 protein (rabbit polyclonal anti-S100, catalog no: ab52642; Abcam, US, dilution 1:200), myelin basic protein (MBP, rabbit polyclonal anti-MBP, catalog no: ab40390; Abcam, US, dilution 1:200) and β-III-tubulin (rabbit polyclonal anti-β-III-tubulin, catalog no: ab18207; Abcam, US, dilution 1:200) primary antibodies were used. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched using 3% hydrogen peroxide. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by species-appropriate biotinylated secondary antibodies and visualization using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB). Negative controls were prepared by replacing the primary antibody with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). All slides were counterstained with hematoxylin. Microscopic evaluation was performed using a Zeiss Imager A2 microscope (Zeiss Systems, Germany) [19, 20].

Evaluation of Mechanoreceptors and Nerve Fibers

Two blinded histologists independently assessed all H&E and immunostained sections. Discrepancies were resolved by joint review. Mechanoreceptors were identified morphologically as free nerve endings (fine, non-encapsulated terminal fibers, S100-positive), Ruffini endings (spindle-shaped, thinly encapsulated structures), Golgi-like endings (fusiform receptors near ligament insertions), and Pacinian corpuscles (concentric lamellar “onion-like” encapsulated structures). S100 staining was used to detect Schwann cells, whereas MBP staining identified myelinated fibers. Immunopositivity was recorded as present/absent and semi-quantitatively scored as mild, moderate, or strong. A subset of sections was examined in serial cuts to confirm that S100-positive fibers corresponded to MBP-positive myelinated axons.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (min–max), and categorical variables as frequency and percentage. Comparisons of continuous variables among groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Comparisons of mechanoreceptor presence across injury-duration groups and remnant types were conducted using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests where appropriate. A multivariable binary logistic regression model was constructed to identify independent predictors of mechanoreceptor presence. Independent variables included injury-duration category and remnant type. Results were presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient characteristics and injury profile

Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of the patients was 28.56 ± 8.09 years, and the cohort was predominantly male (91.4%). The mean body mass index was 24.5 ± 3.0 kg/m². Regarding injury laterality, 60.2% of cases involved the right knee and 39.8% involved the left knee. The most common mechanism of injury was sports-related trauma (87.5%), followed by simple falls (8.6%), high falls (2.3%), and traffic accidents (1.6%).

Mechanoreceptor presence according to remnant attachment pattern

Mechanoreceptor presence according to remnant type and injury duration is summarized in Table 3. In the PCL–Femur attachment group, mechanoreceptors were detected in 25 cases (86.2%) in the subacute group, 19 cases (61.3%) in the chronic group, and 15 cases (60.0%) in the late chronic group. Overall, early surgery favored preservation of neural elements, whereas prolonged injury duration significantly reduced mechanoreceptor detectability.

Mechanoreceptor presence according to remnant attachment pattern

Injury duration emerged as an independent predictor of mechanoreceptor loss. Compared with the subacute group, the odds of mechanoreceptor detection were significantly lower in both the chronic group (OR ≈ 0.28, 95% CI: 0.09–0.89, p = 0.027) and the late chronic group (OR ≈ 0.24, 95% CI: 0.08–0.78, p = 0.014). In contrast, remnant attachment pattern (PCL–femur attachment vs. tibial stump) was not independently associated with mechanoreceptor presence (OR ≈ 0.61, p = 0.23).

Distribution of remnant attachment types

The distribution of mechanoreceptor detection according to injury duration is presented in Table 5. Overall, mechanoreceptors or nerve structures were identified in 81 of 128 specimens (63.3%). Mechanoreceptor prevalence differed significantly among injury-duration groups. Positive neural structures were detected in 84.8% of subacute cases (28/33), 58.7% of chronic cases (27/46), and 53.1% of late chronic cases (26/49) (p = 0.01). Free nerve endings represented the most frequently observed neural structures across all groups, whereas encapsulated receptors such as Ruffini and Golgi-like endings were detected only occasionally. A Pacinian corpuscle was identified in a single specimen.

Immunohistochemical identification of neural structures

Histological examination of ACL remnant tissues revealed disrupted collagen bundles with varying degrees of fibrosis, vascular proliferation, and cellular infiltration (Fig. 3A–B). H&E staining demonstrated irregular ligament architecture, with areas of subsynovial vascularity and connective tissue remodeling. Within this altered ligament matrix, occasional sensory corpuscle–like structures were observed. In rare cases, a lamellated Pacinian corpuscle–like structure was detected, characterized by concentric lamellar architecture surrounding a central axon. However, definitive identification of neural elements required immunohistochemical confirmation. Immunohistochemical staining with S100 protein revealed abundant neural elements within the ACL remnants (Fig. 3C–D). S100-positive nerve fibers were predominantly distributed within the subsynovial layer and interfascicular connective tissue. These fibers typically appeared as thin, branching structures consistent with free nerve endings, which represented the most frequently observed neural morphology in the examined specimens. MBP immunostaining demonstrated the presence of myelinated nerve fibers within the remnant tissue (Fig. 3E–F). MBP-positive structures appeared as thicker, linear axonal profiles, indicating myelinated nerve bundles. In contrast, many of the delicate branching terminals identified with S100 staining lacked MBP expression, suggesting that a substantial proportion of neural elements consisted of unmyelinated or minimally myelinated fibers. Additional confirmation of neuronal structures was obtained using β-III-tubulin immunostaining (Fig. 3G–H). β-III-tubulin–positive fibers were observed within the ligament matrix and subsynovial connective tissue, appearing as fine neural processes and small axonal bundles. This staining highlighted the neuronal cytoskeleton and provided complementary evidence for the presence of neural elements within the ACL remnants. Together, these immunohistochemical findings confirm the persistence of both myelinated and unmyelinated neural fibers within ACL remnant tissues.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that a substantial proportion of ACL remnants retain sensory neural structures, particularly in patients who undergo reconstruction within the subacute period [21, 22]. Using S100, MBP, and β-III-tubulin immunohistochemistry in a cohort of 128 patients, we observed that free nerve endings represented the most frequently identified neural structures, whereas organized receptors such as Ruffini-like or Golgi-like endings were less common and Pacinian corpuscles were rare. Mechanoreceptor prevalence decreased with increasing injury-to-surgery interval, suggesting progressive neural degeneration over time [23, 24].

These findings are consistent with earlier studies indicating that ACL remnants may retain elements of sensory innervation after rupture. Dhillon et al. [25] and Zhang et al. [11] reported mechanoreceptor persistence in approximately 50–70% of remnant tissues, particularly when the interval from injury was relatively short. Subsequent investigations have similarly noted higher detection rates of neural elements in earlier injury phases [26, 27]. Our immunohistochemical findings also demonstrated S100-positive Schwann cell processes and MBP-positive myelinated fibers, while β-III-tubulin staining enabled more direct visualization of neuronal fibers within the ligament remnants.

The potential biological implications of these observations are noteworthy. Residual neural structures within the remnant tissue may contribute to reinnervation processes following ACL reconstruction through axonal sprouting from surviving nerve fibers [28, 29]. Experimental and clinical studies have suggested that remnant-preserving techniques may facilitate restoration of proprioceptive signaling and neuromuscular control [30–32]. However, as functional outcomes were not evaluated in the present study, the clinical relevance of the observed neural preservation should be interpreted with caution.

Remnant attachment pattern demonstrated a descriptive trend toward higher mechanoreceptor detection in remnants attached to the PCL or femoral notch [33, 34]. However, multivariable analysis indicated that this factor was not independently associated with mechanoreceptor presence once injury duration was considered. These findings suggest that the time interval following injury may play a more prominent role in neural preservation than remnant attachment morphology alone [35, 36]. Nevertheless, organized remnants may still offer mechanical or vascular advantages independent of their sensory components [37, 38].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Although immunohistochemical staining was performed using S100, MBP, and β-III-tubulin, the evaluation of mechanoreceptors remained primarily morphological and semi-quantitative. Furthermore, while neuronal markers facilitate visualization of axons, routine histological methods remain limited in their ability to reliably distinguish specific mechanoreceptor subtypes. In addition, the difference observed between the chronic and late chronic groups was relatively small, and their clinical significance should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Finally, the absence of functional outcome measures (e.g., IKDC or Lysholm scores) prevent direct correlation between neural preservation and postoperative function. Despite these limitations, the relatively large sample size and the combined histological and immunohistochemical approach strengthen the reliability of the observations.

Conclusion

Sensory neural structures can be detected in a considerable proportion of ACL remnants, particularly in the earlier phases following ligament injury. The present findings indicate that mechanoreceptor presence tends to decrease as the interval between injury and reconstruction increases, whereas the remnant attachment pattern appears to have a more limited association with neural preservation. These observations provide histological evidence that ACL remnants may retain sensory elements for a certain period after rupture. However, the functional implications of this neural preservation remain uncertain. Further studies incorporating quantitative histological analyses and clinical outcome measures are required to clarify the potential role of remnant tissue in proprioceptive recovery after ACL reconstruction.
