Fine-Tuning Causal LLMs for Text Classification: Embedding-Based vs. Instruction-Based Approaches
Amirhossein Yousefiramandi, Ciarán Cooney

Abstract
We explore efficient strategies to fine-tune decoder-only Large Language Models (LLMs) for downstream text classification under resource constraints. Two approaches are investigated: (1) attaching a classification head to a pre-trained causal LLM and fine-tuning on the task (using the LLM’s final token embedding as a sequence representation), and (2) instruction-tuning the LLM in a prompt→response format for classification. To enable single-GPU fine-tuning of models up to 8B parameters, we combine 4-bit model quantization with Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) for parameter-efficient training. Experiments on two datasets – a proprietary single-label dataset and the public WIPO-Alpha patent dataset (extreme multi-label classification) – show that the embedding-based method significantly outperforms the instruction-tuned method in F1-score, and is very competitive with - even surpassing - fine-tuned domain-specific models (e.g. BERT) on the same tasks. These results demonstrate that directly leveraging the internal representations of causal LLMs, along with efficient fine-tuning techniques, yields impressive classification performance under limited computational resources. We discuss the advantages of each approach while outlining practical guidelines and future directions for optimizing LLM fine-tuning in classification scenarios.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) with billions of parameters have shown remarkable capabilities in natural language generation and understanding in recent years
(
Brown et al. 2020
;
Wei et al. 2022
;
Ji et al. 2023
;
Chowdhery et al. 2023
)
. Traditionally, text classification tasks have been tackled by fine-tuning encoder-based transformers like BERT or RoBERTa on labeled data
(
Devlin et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2019
)
. In such models, a special classification token (e.g. BERT’s [CLS]) is used as an aggregate sequence representation, and a linear classifier is trained on its final hidden state. In contrast, decoder-only (causal) LLMs are pre-trained for next-word prediction with a left-to-right (causal) attention mask, so each token can only attend to preceding tokens and has no visibility of future tokens. As a result, they both lack an explicit classification token and do not provide a single token with bidirectional visibility over the entire input. However, they do have billions of parameters trained on trillions of tokens . This raises the question: Can we effectively fine-tune large causal LLMs for classification, and would they perform better than traditional domain-specific models on downstream tasks?

Fine-tuning LLMs is challenging due to their size
(
Ding et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2024
)
, as updating all parameters of a multi-billion-parameter model is often infeasible on a single GPU. Recently, advances in parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) have begun to offer solutions to this problem
(
Ding et al. 2023
;
Fu et al. 2023
;
Lialin et al. 2023
)
. One such technique is Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), which freezes the original model weights and injects small trainable weight matrices in each layer
(
Hu et al. 2021
)
. LoRA drastically reduces the number of trainable parameters (by orders of magnitude) while maintaining model performance, as demonstrated on GPT-3 and other models
(
Hu et al. 2021
)
. Another complementary advance is model quantization. 4-bit quantization, as implemented in the BitsAndBytes library
1
1

1

https://pypi.org/project/bitsandbytes/
, allows models to be loaded at lower precision, cutting memory requirements dramatically with minimal impact on accuracy
(
Zheng et al. 2024
)
.
Dettmers et al. 2023
introduced QLoRA, which combines 4-bit quantization with LoRA-based fine-tuning, enabling even a 65B-parameter model to be fine-tuned on a single GPU without losing performance.

In this work, we leverage these innovations to fine-tune several open-source causal LLMs – including variants of LLaMA
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
(1B, 3B, 8B parameters), as well as other recent models such as Mistral-7B
(
Jiang et al. 2023
)
, Qwen
(
Bai et al. 2023
)
, Gemma
(
Team et al. 2024
)
, and Microsoft’s Phi
(
Abdin et al. 2024
)
– for text classification tasks in the patent domain. We compare two methodologies: (1) we fine-tune the LLMs by adding a classification head on top of their embeddings (embedding-based fine-tuning), and (2) we reformulate the classification task as an instruction-following problem (instruction-based fine-tuning). In Approach 1, the model is treated as a feature extractor: after the input text is processed by the LLM, the hidden state of the final token - which implicitly attends to the entire sequence - is taken as a representation of the whole input, analogous to the [CLS] token in BERT. A feed-forward classification head is then trained on top of this representation. In Approach 2, the model is fine-tuned to generate the correct label given a prompted input —essentially learning to follow an instruction that asks for the class of the input. This approach aligns with the idea of instruction tuning, where an LLM is fine-tuned on prompt-response pairs to better follow task instructions
(
Wei et al. 2021
;
Sanh et al. 2021
)
.

We empirically demonstrate that a quantized LoRA fine-tuning of causal LLMs via the embedding-based approach consistently performs competitively with both the instruction-tuned LLM and our fine-tuned BERT baselines
(
Yousefiramandi and Cooney 2025
)
in terms of classification F1-score, despite vastly fewer trainable parameters. Notably, even relatively small LLMs (e.g. 3B parameters) when fine-tuned in this manner can compete with, or even exceed, the accuracy of traditional transformer models, echoing observations by other researchers
(
Bouchiha et al. 2025
)
. Our paper makes the following contributions:

•

Decoder-only Classification:

We demonstrate an effective strategy for using decoder-only LLMs in classification by harnessing their internal sequence representations.

•

Resource-efficient fine-tuning:
We
report SOTA results on classification tasks achieved with single-GPU friendly methods (LoRA + 4-bit quantization).

•

Classification vs Instruction:
We present a comparative analysis highlighting why the direct embedding approach is superior to instruction-based fine-tuning for classification scenarios.

•

Practical Guidelines:
We report on lessons learned from our experiments and make implementation suggestions for use by researchers in this area.

Methodology

We describe the two fine-tuning approaches in detail, along with the techniques used to make training feasible on limited hardware. Specifically, all experiments were run on a
Databricks
cluster using runtime 16.4 LTS (Apache Spark 3.5.2, Scala 2.12), on a
g6.2xlarge
node equipped with a single
NVIDIA L4
GPU and
32 GB
host memory. Aggressive quantization and PEFT techniques were essential to make training feasible under these constraints.

2.1
Approach 1: Classification Head on LLM Embeddings

Our first approach treats the causal LLM as an encoder that produces a fixed-size representation of the input, which we then feed to a small classification head. Specifically, given an input text sequence, we append the end-of-sequence token (if not already present) and obtain the hidden state of the final token from the LLM’s last layer. This final token’s embedding serves as a holistic representation of the entire sequence (analogous to BERT’s [CLS] embedding which captures an “aggregate” of the input). We attach a simple linear layer (or two-layer feed-forward network) on top of this embedding to predict the class label. Because causal LLMs process input autoregressively, the last token’s state inherently attends to all previous tokens in the sequence. Thus, using that state as a summary vector allows the classifier to consider the full context of the input.

During fine-tuning, only the classifier parameters and a small subset of LLM parameters (introduced via LoRA) are updated, while the majority of the LLM’s original weights remain frozen. To implement this efficiently, we adopt quantized LoRA fine-tuning
(
Dettmers et al. 2023
)
. The base LLM weights are loaded in 4-bit precision using the
BitsAndBytes
library (normal float 4 or NF4 quantization). This compresses model memory usage to roughly one-eighth of the 32-bit size, allowing even 8B parameter models to fit in GPU memory. We keep these quantized weights frozen during training, and backpropagate gradients only through them into the inserted LoRA adapters (as done in QLoRA).

The LoRA technique injects trainable low-rank matrices into key weight matrices of the model (we target the attention projection matrices – query, key, value, output – and the feed-forward layers’ projections)
(
Hu et al. 2021
)
. We set the LoRA rank
r=16
and apply a 5% dropout on the adapter updates for stability, following the original LoRA formulation and subsequent practice showing that small ranks are sufficient for effective task adaptation
(
Hu et al. 2022
;
Dettmers et al. 2023
)
. (Typical settings are
r\in\{8,16,32\}
with
\alpha\approx 2r
for stable, effective adaptation
(
Dettmers et al. 2023
;
Raschka 2023
;
Unsloth 2025
)
).

In practice, our LoRA fine-tuning modifies only a tiny fraction of the model’s parameters (typically a few million even for the largest models), which substantially reduces GPU memory and optimization overhead
(
Hu et al. 2022
;
Dettmers et al. 2023
)
. We further use 8-bit optimizers via
bitsandbytes
’ AdamW to decrease optimizer-state memory with negligible accuracy loss
(
Dettmers et al. 2021
)
. Dynamic padding is applied with a
DataCollatorWithPadding

so each batch is padded only to the longest sequence present, avoiding unnecessary compute on padding tokens
(
Wolf et al. 2020
)
. Finally, we employ gradient accumulation with Accelerate

https://huggingface.co/docs/accelerate/en/usage_guides/gradient_accumulation

Multi-Label Extension.

For multi-label classification tasks, we modify the classification head to output a probability for each possible label (using a sigmoid activation for each label). We train with a binary cross-entropy loss that encourages the model to assign appropriate probabilities to each label independently. At inference time, a threshold (e.g. 0.5) is applied to decide which labels are predicted for a given instance. This extension allows Approach 1 to handle scenarios where each input may belong to multiple classes.

Training details.

We fine-tune each model for a fixed number of epochs (or until validation loss plateaus) using a moderate learning rate (on the order of
1\text{-}2\times 10^{-4}
) suitable for LoRA parameters. The classification head is trained from scratch while the LoRA adapters adjust the model’s representations. We found that this approach leads to fast convergence and robust performance on the classification tasks. Notably, by leveraging the pretrained LLM’s rich features, this method often outperforms full fine-tuning of a much smaller model (like BERT) on the same task – a trend also reported by others
(
Bouchiha et al. 2025
)
. The resulting model generates its prediction by doing a forward pass to get the final token embedding and then a lightweight classification layer computation, incurring negligible additional latency at inference time.

2.2
Approach 2: Instruction-Tuning for Classification

Our second approach frames the classification problem as a form of natural language instruction following recent research
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Fatemi et al. 2024
;
Yin et al. 2024
)
. Instead of extracting embeddings, we convert each training example into a prompt for the LLM, and the label into a target response. For example, given an input text and a label, we might construct a prompt like:
“Text: <input text>\nQuestion: What is the category of the above text?\nAnswer:”
and train the LLM to output the correct category in the answer portion. The model is fine-tuned on many such
prompt
\to
answer
pairs so that it learns to produce the appropriate label when given a query prompt. This instruction-tuning approach follows the paradigm of recent multi-task fine-tuning efforts (e.g. FLAN, T0) where models are trained on a variety of natural language prompts to improve their ability to follow human instructions
(
Wei et al. 2021
;
Sanh et al. 2021
)
. In our case, the “instructions” are simply requests to classify the given text, effectively requiring the model to generate the correct label token, which for a limited label set might be a more indirect way to perform classification.

We fine-tune the LLM on these
prompt
\to
answer
pairs using supervised learning (standard next-token prediction loss on the label tokens). Similar to Approach 1, we apply 4-bit quantization and LoRA adapters (
r
=64) to reduce memory usage. LoRA target layers are the same as described above, and we again freeze the main weights and train only the LoRA parameters and final linear layers. The key difference is that now the model’s entire forward pass is used to produce an output sequence (the label text), and loss is computed over the output tokens.

During inference, the instruction-tuned model is given the classification prompt for a new input and we decode its output (usually constrained to just a few tokens) to retrieve the predicted label. This approach effectively turns the classification task into a text generation task conditioned on an instructional context. While powerful, it can be less straightforward for the model – especially if the model sometimes produces extra explanatory text or if the prompt is slightly ambiguous. We mitigate these issues by keeping prompts concise and consistent with those seen in training.

For multi-label classification in the instruction-tuned format, we adjust the prompting strategy: the model is asked to list all applicable labels for the input (e.g. “List all relevant categories for the above text.”). The training targets in such cases are the correct set of labels joined by a delimiter (for instance, “Label A, Label B”). This allows the instruction-tuned model to handle multiple labels, though in practice we found it more challenging for the model to learn the exact formatting and separation of labels.

Efficiency considerations.

The instruction-tuning method, due to the sequential decoding nature of generation, can be slower at inference time (the model must output tokens one-by-one for the label). However, since class labels are typically short (often a single word or a few words), this overhead is minor in practice. Training is slightly more involved than Approach 1 because the model has to learn to align its generated text exactly with the desired labels, essentially doing a form of conditional generation. We still leverage PEFT and quantization to keep the training feasible on one GPU, as in Approach 1.

Results in Table

Embedding-Based vs Instruction-Based Performance

Following direct comparison between embedding-based and instruction-based finetuning approaches in Section
3.1
, we broaden our investigation to a comprehensive set of LLM experiments spanning additional language models, parameter configurations, and both single-label and multi-label classification tasks. Table

Consistent with the trends noted earlier, Approach 1 continues to demonstrate robust classification performance, always competitive and often surpassing both instruction-tuned models and domain-specific BERT baselines—often with a fraction of the trainable parameters. For instance, Phi-3-mini-4k-instruct demonstrates remarkable performance despite only requiring updates to 7.6M-15.2M parameters. This is most visible in Figure
1
where the model F1 score is comparable to those with many more trainable parameters, suggesting efficient adaptation of internal LLM representations for this task. Key clusters in the bubble plots reinforce that increasing trainable parameters beyond a moderate threshold delivers diminishing returns; some compact models compete closely with much larger counterparts.

Moreover, the single-label and multi-label experiments reveal distinct requirements and model behaviors. The extension of the embedding-based method for multi-label tasks using sigmoid outputs proves highly reliable and stable, whereas instruction-tuned models sometimes struggled with label formatting or completeness in multi-label settings. The embedding approach’s focus on calibrated, class-wise probability distributions yields robust, interpretable outputs, whereas the need for precise prompt engineering in the instruction-based approach introduces complexity and occasional brittleness, as reflected in prediction errors and bubble plot variability (Figure
2
). Despite this, it is also clear that above a certain threshold the number of trainable parameters is important in the multilabel scenario.

Despite the ultimate performance of the very large Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 using approach 2 (Table

This flow of findings demonstrates that leveraging efficient finetuning strategies not only matches but consistently surpasses established encoder models on diverse tasks, aligning with the resource-efficient principles outlined at the outset and the comparative insights developed in Section

3.3
LLMs or BERT for domain-specific classification?

Both of our proposed methods offer an alternative to the established encoder-based approach to text classification tasks. However, the question remains open as to which of these is presently the preferred approach. A careful examination of the experimental results in Tables
2
,
3
, and
4
reveals a nuanced but consistent advantage for embedding-based fine-tuning of decoder-only LLMs over both traditional domain-specific BERT baselines and instruction-tuned LLMs across our single-label and multi-label patent classification tasks. Notably, the embedding-based approach achieves F1 scores that are not only competitive with, but in many cases exceed, those reported for fine-tuned BERT models—often with an order of magnitude fewer trainable parameters. For example, a 3.2B parameter Llama model with rank-8 LoRA achieves an F1 of 0.86 on the CLV dataset, outstripping the best BERT baseline (PatentBERT at 0.854) while updating only ~12M parameters compared to 346M for PatentBERT.

Throughput results (Tables
3
&
6
) emphasize the practical utility of Approach 1 in comparison with Approach 2: models trained with embedding-based fine-tuning exhibit substantially higher samples-per-second rates during both training and inference, facilitating faster experimentation and deployment with limited computational resources. This corroborates studies showing that resource-lean LoRA and QLoRA strategies preserve nearly full-precision accuracy while slashing memory and time costs
(
Dettmers et al. 2023
;
Hu et al. 2021
)
. However, our findings do not fully mirror recent work by
Bouchiha et al. 2025
, who report that a fine-tuned GPT-2 can outpace BERT-based models in hierarchical classification. Table
3
shows dramatically higher throughput rates for BERT-based methods.

It is important to note that while instruction fine-tuning (Approach 2) achieves parity or even slight improvements for certain large models and heavily resourced settings (e.g., Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 achieving F1 of 0.853 on CLV and 0.846 on WIPO), this comes at vastly higher computational and implementation complexity—often requiring over 150M trainable parameters and more careful prompt engineering. Recent investigations
(
Ghosh et al. 2024
)
have tempered initial enthusiasm for instruction tuning as a general recipe for classification, identifying stability and calibration issues that were also reflected in our own experiments.

Taken together, these results robustly support embedding-based fine-tuning of causal LLMs, augmented with LoRA and quantization, as the current state-of-the-art approach for efficient, high-performance domain-specific classification. This strategy yields strong performance across both single-label and multi-label tasks, with practical benefits in compute efficiency and reliability—thereby presenting a compelling alternative to both conventional encoder-based methods and instruction-tuned paradigms for applied text classification projects.

3.4
Practical Implications for Practitioners: Robust Fine-Tuning of LLMs for Classification

Partial ablation studies and error analyses offer several practical takeaways for engineers and practitioners seeking reliable, efficient LLM pipelines for text classification tasks. While resource constraints prevented exhaustive ablations across all models and hyperparameters, the experiments conducted illuminate key design choices that have direct consequences on deployment, scalability, and model robustness.

LoRA and Lightweight PEFT Are Essential:

Allowing the model to adapt internal representations with LoRA adapters, even with a very small number of trainable parameters (e.g., 8M–24M out of billions), produced a substantial boost in F1 over “frozen” LLMs where only the classifier head is fine-tuned. This confirms that adapting deeper layers, not just leveraging static pretrained embeddings, is necessary for strong performance—especially on complex or domain-specific tasks.

Adapter Rank and Tuning:

We found that increasing the LoRA rank from 8 to 16 provides a small but stable benefit, especially for highly multi-label tasks (like WIPO). Lower ranks may suffice for simpler datasets or where compute is the tightest bottleneck, but practitioners should not hesitate to test slightly larger LoRA ranks if throughput and memory permit, as the resource tradeoff remains minimal compared to classical full-model fine-tuning.

Headless Prompting Is Inadequate:

Experiments with zero-shot and few-shot instructional prompts—without any parameter updates—performed markedly worse than even small-scale LoRA fine-tuning. For practitioners, this means that genuine supervised adaptation is needed for best-in-class accuracy, and that prompt engineering alone cannot match the benefits of even lightweight, PEFT-based updates.

Model Calibration and Output Structure:

The embedding-based approach consistently produced more calibrated probability outputs, with clear confidence levels that correlated well with prediction correctness. This has practical significance for risk-sensitive applications and hierarchical labeling—thresholding or prioritizing outputs works out-of-the box, in contrast to instruction-based methods that may require custom post-processing and validation pipelines.

Error Sources and Engineering Overheads:

Instruction-based models were more brittle with respect to small changes in prompt phrasing or formatting, leading to label extraction errors and reduced performance. When models did not reproduce the expected verbalization template exactly, result parsing became nontrivial. Engineers deploying such models should budget time for extra evaluation and safeguards if instruction-tuned models are used in production.

Resource-Efficient Choices:

Overall, resource-lean, quantized LLMs with LoRA adapters offer a substantial reduction in training and inference latency—enabling rapid prototyping and frequent updates even on moderate hardware. Embedding-based designs proved “plug-and-play”—easy to implement, robust to input variations, and requiring less custom post-processing, which is critical for maintaining software simplicity and reducing operational costs.

4
Limitations

Despite our assumption that the results reported here will generalize, a limitation of the study is the single-domain focus on patents. Related to this is the subject of the task we addressed: a narrow focus on text classification.

Resource constraints are considered a feature of the study as we attempted to demonstrate that LLMs could be used in contexts in which BERT has dominated. For this reason models were only ever trained on single GPUs, but we acknowledge that more power compute can extend the scope and performance of LLMs.

5
Future Work

Future work includes exploring hybrid fine-tuning pipelines (combining embedding and instruction paradigms), scaling to even larger models and more challenging multi-label or hierarchical datasets, and conducting in-depth representation analyses to further understand the internal dynamics that drive the success of embedding-based tuning. Extensions to regression and other structured tasks also offer promising directions.

We also would like to further investigate the potential of reasoning models for classification tasks. In this work, we reported results using OpenReasoning-Nemotron-1.5B with instruction tuning but the standard prompt we used may not be optimal for fully leveraging thinking models like this one.

An interesting direction for future work is to compare the embeddings produced by decoder-based and BERT-based models using t-SNE or UMAP, and to quantify class separability via cluster-quality metrics (e.g., silhouette, Davies–Bouldin) and representation-similarity analyses (e.g., CKA). These analyses would indicate how well each model separates classes in the embedding space and which yields more distinct, interpretable clusters. In parallel, because decoder-only LLMs are substantially larger and exhibit lower training/inference throughput than encoder-only BERT-style models in this setting, a teacher–student (knowledge distillation) approach is a natural avenue: treat the decoder model as a teacher and train a compact BERT-style student with temperature-scaled soft targets and, where feasible, intermediate feature/attention matching. For hierarchical, multi-label settings, sequence-level and contrastive distillation objectives aligned with the label taxonomy are promising directions. The objective is to retain most of the teacher’s accuracy while recovering the throughput and memory efficiency characteristic of BERT-style models.

6
Conclusion

This study systematically compared two strategies for adapting large decoder-only LLMs to patent text classification: an embedding-based approach using a lightweight classification head and an instruction-based approach leveraging
prompt-response
supervised generation, both fine-tuned via LoRA and quantization. Through comprehensive experiments across both single-label and multi-label patent datasets, the results reveal a clear, consistent advantage for embedding-based fine-tuning. This method not only competes with or surpasses instruction-tuned LLMs in classification F1-score, but also performs well against established domain-specific BERT baselines—achieving strong results with far fewer trainable parameters and substantially lower memory and compute requirements. However, BERT-based models retain a signifcant advantage in terms of ultimate throughput for both training and inference.

In particular, the embedding approach delivers highly competitive or superior F1 performance (e.g., Llama-3.2-3B reaching 0.86 on CLV and 0.785 on WIPO), outpacing larger instruction-tuned models and BERT variants, while maintaining practicality for single-GPU environments. This finding is corroborated by reduced training and inference latency, as shown in our throughput results, and is consistent with emerging academic literature highlighting the effectiveness of parameter-efficient techniques such as LoRA and QLoRA in large model adaptation.

Analysis of failure modes and ablations further highlights the embedding-based approach’s strengths. Its probability outputs are more reliably calibrated, and it proves more robust to format and prompt challenges than instruction-based methods, which can suffer from prompt fragility and error propagation in text generation. Our results show that even models with moderate parameter counts can match or exceed the performance of both domain-tuned encoders and instruction-tuned decoders, demonstrating that resource-efficient LLM fine-tuning is not just viable, but optimal for domain text classification scenarios.

From a broader perspective, these results lower the practical barriers to deploying powerful LLMs for specialized NLP tasks under real-world compute constraints. Practitioners and researchers can now consider embedding-based, LoRA-finetuned causal LLMs as a preferred option for technical or domain-specific classification, securing strong performance with rapid iteration cycles and modest hardware.

In summary, this work provides empirical and practical evidence that parameter-efficient, embedding-based fine-tuning of LLMs is an effective, scalable, and high-performing alternative to both conventional BERT-style models and instruction-tuned LLMs for text classification—paving the way for broader and more resource-accessible adoption of advanced language models in specialized domains.
