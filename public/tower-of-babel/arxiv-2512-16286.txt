Low-Mach-number limit for multiphase flows
Cassandre Lebot

Abstract
This paper is devoted to the study of the low-Mach-number limit for solutions of the compressible Navier-Stokes or Euler equations for different types of fluids. We first review the different results obtained in the case of flows consisting of one phase. Then, we focus on the low-Mach-number limit for two-phase flows, considering different types of systems: with an algebraic closure or a PDE closure for the pressure, with one single or two different velocities, without or with entropy.



1
Introduction

The aim of this paper is to introduce the concept of low-Mach-number limit for compressible multiphase flows. The Mach number is a dimensionless quantity that represents the ratio of the flow velocity to the speed of sound in the fluid. A low Mach number is a criterion for incompressible flow. In the past, several authors have worked on the passage from compressible equations to an incompressible system when the Mach number tends to zero. The motivations are several. First, we better understand incompressible equations and we may use the passage to the limit and comparison to understand more properties on compressible equations. Moreover, low-Mach-number equations have several advantages in numerical analysis: simplification of the algorithms, smaller time step for the numerical integration. For more details on the numerical challenges for low-Mach-number systems, we refer the reader to
[
4
]
.

However, this question of the zero-Mach-number limit for multiphase flows is still to be examined. To the best of our knowledge,
[
43
]
and
[
36
]
are two of the very few articles that studied this question. The present contribution aims at giving an overview of this subject by discussing the formal derivation of the zero-Mach-number limit for several types of multiphase flow models.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we give a brief review of the low-Mach-number limit for one-phase flow. In Section
3
, we focus on two-phase flows system with the same velocity for both phases and derive the low-Mach-number equations for two-phase systems considering two types of pressure closure: an algebraic closure and a PDE closure. We discuss first the isentropic case and then the non-isentropic case. In Section
4
, we derive the low-Mach-number equations for two-phase flows system with two distinct velocities. Finally, Section
5
contains some open problems and remarks about topics that we have not considered in this paper.

2
Low-Mach number limit for one-phase flow

We give here a very short review of some results on the low Mach number limit for compressible flows. We refer the interested reader for more detailed reviews to
[
40
]
,
[
22
]
or
[
3
]
.

2.1
The isentropic case

In this part, we restrict ourselves to the case of isentropic fluids, caracterized by a constant entropy, and we assume that the flow evolves in the domain
\Omega
(that we will precise later) from the
d
-dimensional space
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, with
d\geqslant 2
.

We write
t\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
for the time variable and
x\in\Omega
denotes the space variable. We consider the following governing equations of a compressible flow:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\rho+\operatorname{div}(\rho u)=0,\\
&\partial_{t}(\rho u)+\operatorname{div}(\rho u\otimes u)+\nabla p(\rho)-\mu\Delta u-(\lambda+\mu)\nabla\operatorname{div}u=0\end{aligned}\right.

(2.1)

with initial conditions

\displaystyle\rho_{\lvert t=0}=\rho_{0}\geqslant 0\quad\text{and}\quad u_{\lvert t=0}=u_{0}

(2.2)

and where
\rho=\rho(t,x)\geqslant 0
represents the mass density,
u=u(t,x)
the velocity vector,
p=p(\rho(t,x))
the pressure. The viscosity coefficients
\lambda
and
\mu
satisfy
\mu>0
and
\lambda+2\mu>0
and are assumed to be constant (this can be relaxed). We consider a barotropic fluid with a
\gamma
pressure law:

p(\rho)=\rho^{\gamma}\text{, with }\gamma>1.

There are several ways on how to introduce the Mach number in the compressible flow equations. First, we can consider a varying equations of state and also varying transport coefficients, like viscosity coefficients for viscous fluids (see
[
18
]
,
[
39
]
). In that approach, a particular fluid is viewed as “nearly incompressible”.

The alternative way to derive the compressible Navier-Stokes equations in the small Mach number regime is to consider flows on varying time or spatial scale. This amounts to use a dimensional analysis, by rewriting the equations in their dimensionless form. One way to do it is by scaling each variable by its characteristic value (see
[
30
]
). The other way is to use a change of independent variables (see
[
2
]
,
[
31
]
). This is the approach we take in the following development (for more details, see the introduction in
[
32
]
).

We denote by
\varepsilon
the Mach number. The incompressible limit is the limit where the speed of the fluid becomes negligible compared to the speed of sound. Thus we reset the flow speed to the scale by replacing
u
with
\varepsilon u^{\varepsilon}
. A particle will move a distance of order
1/\varepsilon
during a time of order
1/\varepsilon^{2}
, meaning that the relevant time scale is
1/\varepsilon
. This is why we can use a change of variable like
t^{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{2}t
and
x^{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon x
, with
t^{\varepsilon}
and
x^{\varepsilon}
of order
1
(see
[
15
]
). Thus, we scale
u
and
\rho
in the following way

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\varepsilon u^{\varepsilon}(t^{\varepsilon},x^{\varepsilon}),\quad\rho(t,x)=\rho^{\varepsilon}(t^{\varepsilon},x^{\varepsilon}).

(2.3)

We can rewrite System (
2.1
):

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\rho^{\varepsilon}+\operatorname{div}(\rho^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon})=0,\\
&\partial_{t}(\rho^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon})+\operatorname{div}(\rho^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}\otimes u^{\varepsilon})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla p(\rho^{\varepsilon})-\mu^{\varepsilon}\Delta u^{\varepsilon}-(\lambda^{\varepsilon}+\mu^{\varepsilon})\nabla\operatorname{div}u^{\varepsilon}=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.4)

The initial data are
\rho^{\varepsilon}_{\lvert t=0}=\rho^{\varepsilon}_{0}
and
u^{\varepsilon}_{\lvert t=0}=u^{\varepsilon}_{0}
. We will see that it can be relevant to use initial data that depend on
\varepsilon
.

Formally, when we take the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
in System (
2.4
), we cancel the terms with the highest factor in
\varepsilon
in the equations. Therefore from the momentum equation we obtain when
\varepsilon
goes to zero

\displaystyle\nabla p(\rho^{\varepsilon})\to 0.

(2.5)

Let’s call
C
a constant in space such that the limit density
\rho^{0}
satisfies, because of (
2.5
):

\displaystyle p(\rho^{0})=C(t),

\displaystyle\rho^{0}=C(t)^{1/\gamma}.

From the mass equation we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{C^{\prime}(t)}{C(t)}+\operatorname{div}u^{0}=0.

We now integrate in space over
\Omega
:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{C^{\prime}(t)}{C(t)}=0.

In the bounded or in the periodic case, we deduce that
C^{\prime}(t)=0
and thus that
\rho^{\varepsilon}
must converge to a positive constant. In the case of the whole space, this depends on the condition that we use on
\rho^{0}
at infinity.

Now passing to the limit in the mass equation we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}u^{\varepsilon}\to 0.

(2.6)

Finally, coming back to the momentum equation, we find that the limit velocity
u^{0}
must satisfy the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\operatorname{div}u^{0}=0,\\
&\partial_{t}(\rho^{0}u^{0})+\operatorname{div}(\rho^{0}u^{0}\otimes u^{0})-\mu\Delta u^{0}+\nabla\pi=0.\end{aligned}\right.

with
\pi
being the “limit” of
\frac{(\rho^{\varepsilon})^{\gamma}-\bar{\rho}^{\gamma}}{\varepsilon^{2}}
.

Remark 2.1
.

We can also rewrite the momentum equation as

\rho^{0}\Big(\partial_{t}u^{0}+(u^{0}\cdot\nabla)u^{0}\Big)-\mu\Delta u^{0}+\nabla\pi=0.

We end up with a compatibility condition on the initial data: it is more or less difficult to pass to the limit in System (
2.4
) if the initial data verifies itself the conditions (
2.5
)-(
2.6
) or not. Clearly, it is easier to pass to the limit if it does. Hence, we introduce the notion of “well–prepared” initial data.

Definition 2.2
.

We say that the initial data
(u_{0}^{\varepsilon},\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon})
are well-prepared if they satisfy

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}u_{0}^{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon),\quad\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}=\bar{\rho_{0}}

with
\bar{\rho_{0}}
a constant.

Otherwise, we say that the initial data are “ill-prepared” or we call them “general” initial data. In that case, it actually means that sounds waves are generated initially and propagate with high speed in the domain (speed of order
1/\varepsilon
). In that paper, we will not consider this case and focus only on problems with well-prepared initial data.

Historically, the study of the low-Mach number limit in the compressible Navier-Stokes equations began with the case of well-prepared data. The first general proof of the transition from the equations of compressible fluids to the equations of incompressible fluids comes from
[
18
]
. Then
[
28
]
,
[
29
]
also studied the existence time of the solutions of System (
2.4
) (its uniformity in
\varepsilon
) and the passing to the limit, using symmetric hyperbolic system theory in the whole space and in the periodic case respectively. We can also note the work of
[
39
]
in a bounded domain with periodic boundary conditions that studies the convergence using filtering techniques.

Later, more works have dropped the assumption of well-prepared data. Considering the whole space,
[
42
]
gives the first proof of the transition from compressible Navier-Stokes equations to incompressible Navier-Stokes equations when the initial data is more general. However the uniform convergence does not hold in
t=0
because of the development of an initial layer (see also
[
25
]
,
[
27
]
). With periodic boundary conditions, several authors have studied the convergence using the filtering method (
[
14
]
,
[
24
]
,
[
21
]
).

P.-L. Lions, B. Desjardins, E. Grenier and N. Masmoudi also show in several works that we can pass to the limit when
\varepsilon
goes to zero using uniform estimates coming from the energy inequality. We remind the reader the energy inequality for Navier-Stokes equation, as it will be interesting to compare it later to the ones of two-phase flows systems:

\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\rho^{\varepsilon}\lvert u^{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}(t)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}(\gamma-1)}(\rho^{\varepsilon})^{\gamma}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\int\mu^{\varepsilon}\lvert\nabla u^{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}(s)+\lambda^{\varepsilon}(\operatorname{div}u^{\varepsilon})^{2}(s)ds\\
\leqslant\int\frac{1}{2}\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}\lvert u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}(\gamma-1)}(\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon})^{\gamma}.

(2.7)

We refer to Subsection
3.1
for more details on the computations of this energy.

They give a proof in
[
31
]
for the case of periodic boundary conditions, and in
[
17
]
for weak solutions for isentropic fluids in the whole space. In the latter, they improve the convergence result using Strichartz’ estimates. For the case of a bounded domain with homogeneous Dirichlet conditions,
[
16
]
show that the convergence is strong because of acoustic waves absorbed in boundary (using a geometric assumption on the domain). Let us note also the work of
[
41
]
that shows the weak convergence to solutions of incompressible flows using the antisymmetry of the acoustic operator when the condition
u\cdot n=0
is set in the boundary (and the convergence is shown to be strong in the case of well-prepared data). For a new approach to the low-Mach-number limit using dissipative measure-valued solutions, we refer to
[
19
]
.

2.2
The non-isentropic case

We now consider non-isentropic fluids and take into account the transport of entropy. For the sake of simplicity, we study the Euler system for inhomogeneous fluids (we do not consider viscosity):

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\rho+\operatorname{div}(\rho u)=0,\\
&\partial_{t}(\rho u)+\operatorname{div}(\rho u\otimes u)+\nabla p(\rho,S)=0,\\
&\partial_{t}S+u\cdot\nabla S=0,\end{aligned}\right.

(2.8)

where
S
is the entropy and the pressure
p
now depends on the density
\rho
and the entropy. By convention, we write
\rho=R(p,S)
. We may for instance consider the pressure state law
p(\rho,S)=\rho^{\gamma}e^{S}
,
\gamma>1
. Let us note that the momentum equation can be rewritten as

\rho\Big(\partial_{t}u+(u\cdot\nabla)u\Big)+\nabla p(\rho,S)=0.

By making the same change of variables than in the isentropic case (
2.3
) and scaling the entropy as
S(t,x)=S^{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon^{2}t,\varepsilon x)
, a factor
1/\varepsilon^{2}
appears in front of the gradient of the pressure. Then, we follow the presentation of
[
35
]
and make an adequate change of variable defining
q
as
p(t,x)=\bar{p}e^{\varepsilon q^{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon^{2}t,\varepsilon x)}
to obtain

\left\{\begin{aligned} &a^{\varepsilon}(\partial_{t}q^{\varepsilon}+u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla q^{\varepsilon})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\operatorname{div}u^{\varepsilon}=0,\\
&r^{\varepsilon}(\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}+u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\nabla q^{\varepsilon}=0,\\
&\partial_{t}S^{\varepsilon}+u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla S^{\varepsilon}=0,\end{aligned}\right.

where
a^{\varepsilon}
and
r^{\varepsilon}
are functions of
\rho^{\varepsilon}
and
S^{\varepsilon}
(see
[
35
]
for more details). The initial data are
\rho^{\varepsilon}_{\lvert t=0}=\rho^{\varepsilon}_{0}
,
u^{\varepsilon}_{\lvert t=0}=u^{\varepsilon}_{0}
and
S^{\varepsilon}_{\lvert t=0}=S^{\varepsilon}_{0}
. We refer the interested reader to
[
34
]
for a proof of the existence of solutions of this system on a fixed time interval with uniform bounds in
\varepsilon
.

Formally if we take the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, we obtain directly

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}u^{\varepsilon}\to 0,

\displaystyle\nabla q^{\varepsilon}\to 0.

Therefore the pressures
q^{\varepsilon}
and thus
p
are constant. The limit of the pressure state law gives the limit density
\bar{\rho}
as a function of the limit entropy
S
:
\bar{\rho}=R(\bar{p},\bar{S})
. Setting
\nabla\Pi
as the limit of the pressure gradient, we obtain formally in the limit the Euler system for inhomogeneous incompressible fluids

\left\{\begin{aligned} &r^{0}(\partial_{t}u^{0}+u^{0}\cdot\nabla u^{0})+\nabla\Pi=0,\\
&\partial_{t}S^{0}+u^{0}\cdot\nabla S^{0}=0,\\
&\operatorname{div}u^{0}=0.\end{aligned}\right.

The incompressible limit of non-isentropic Euler equations has been studied in the periodic case in
[
34
]
and in the whole space in
[
33
]
. We also refer to
[
39
]
in the case of a bounded domain with well-prepared data and to
[
1
]
in the general case.
[
9
]
also presents a multidimensional formal derivation of the low-Mach-number limit in an ill-prepared periodic case. For more details on the non-isentropic case, we refer the interested reader to the part of the non-isentropic equations presented in
[
22
]
or to the presentation
[
35
]
. For studies of the low-Mach-number limit in different types of compressible systems, we refer to
[
20
]
(in particular for the Navier-Stokes-Fourier system).

3
Low-Mach number limit for two-phase flows with one single velocity

3.1
Two-phase isentropic flows

This subsection briefly reviews two-phase flows models (for more details, see the book
[
26
]
or the review in
[
12
]
for instance).

First, one may mention as in
[
23
]
that there is no consensus on what constitutes a good two-phase flows model. Nevertheless, the majority of the models rely on the Baer–Nunziato equations, which describe compressible two-phase flows. One main difficulty is to understand and model both small scale and large scale effects and in particular the effects of small scale on larger scales. At an intermediate scale level, one may consider a two-phase flow as a flow composed of two one-phase areas separated by interfaces. Then, assuming some homogeneity at this mesoscale level and using tools as homogenization techniques, we can derive an averaged two-phase system that describes the flow at the macroscopic level. The interested reader may consult
[
10
]
,
[
5
]
or
[
6
]
to understand how averaged two-phase flow models may be obtained with no closure assumptions using homogenization techniques. Note that for the moment, the mathematical justification needs some restrictive assumptions like using the same velocity for both phases and only one dimension. Relaxing these assumptions is still a challenge. In the rest of this section, we will also consider only one velocity for both phases.

Two-phase systems with pressure algebraic closure.

We now consider a fluid of two phases,
+
and
-
, in a bounded or periodic domain
\Omega
with appropriate boundary conditions. We denote
\rho_{i}
,
p_{i}
,
\alpha_{i}
the density, pressure and volume fraction of phase
i
. The single velocity is denoted by
u
and any body force acting upon the fluid by
f
. Let
\mu_{i}
,
\lambda_{i}
be the viscous coefficients of phase
i
and
D(u)
is the viscous tensor defined as

D(u)=\frac{\nabla u+(\nabla u)^{t}}{2}.

Different authors, such as
[
37
]
, have studied a two-phase system with an algebraic closure for the pressure and only one velocity field taking the following form:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\alpha_{+}+\alpha_{-}=1,\\
&\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}u)=0,\\
&\partial_{t}(\alpha_{-}\rho_{-})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{-}\rho_{-}u)=0,\\
&\partial_{t}((\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}+\alpha_{-}\rho_{-})u)+\operatorname{div}((\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}+\alpha_{-}\rho_{-})u\otimes u)\\
&\quad\quad+\nabla(\alpha_{+}p_{+}(\rho_{+})+\alpha_{-}p_{-}(\rho_{-}))-2\operatorname{div}\Big((\alpha_{+}\mu_{+}(\rho_{+})+\alpha_{-}\mu_{-}(\rho_{-}))D(u)\Big)\\
&\quad\quad-\nabla\Big((\alpha_{+}\lambda_{+}+\alpha_{-}\lambda_{-})\operatorname{div}u\Big)=(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}+\alpha_{-}\rho_{-})f,\\
&p_{+}(\rho_{+})=p_{-}(\rho_{-}).\end{aligned}\right.

(3.1)

Remark 3.1
.

Note that the pressure term in the momentum equation can also be written as
\nabla(p_{+}(\rho_{+}))
or
\alpha_{+}\nabla p_{+}(\rho_{+})+\alpha_{-}\nabla p_{-}(\rho_{-})
.

Remark 3.2
.

Note the link between
\operatorname{div}D(u)
and
\Delta u
:

2\mu\operatorname{div}D(u)=\mu\Delta u+\mu\nabla\operatorname{div}u.

Therefore, taking
\alpha_{+}=1
,
\mu_{+}(\rho_{+})=\mu
,
\lambda_{+}=\lambda
, we recover System (
2.1
) for one-phase flow.

The associated energy estimate reads for all
t\in[0,T]
:

\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}+\alpha_{-}\rho_{-}\right)|u|^{2}(t)dx+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{+}(\rho_{+})^{\gamma_{+}}}{(\gamma_{+}-1)}(t)dx+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{-}(\rho_{-})^{\gamma_{-}}}{(\gamma_{-}-1)}(t)dx\\
+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big((\alpha_{+}\mu_{+}(\rho_{+})+\alpha_{-}\mu_{-}(\rho_{-}))\lvert D(u)\rvert^{2}+(\alpha_{+}\lambda_{+}+\alpha_{-}\lambda_{-})\lvert\operatorname{div}u\rvert^{2}\Big)\\
\quad\quad\leqslant\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}+\alpha_{-}\rho_{-}\right)|u|^{2}(0)dx+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{+}(\rho_{+})^{\gamma_{+}}}{(\gamma_{+}-1)}(0)dx+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{-}(\rho_{-})^{\gamma_{-}}}{(\gamma_{-}-1)}(0)dx\\
\quad\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}+\alpha_{-}\rho_{-})f\cdot u.

(3.2)

We provide for the reader’s convenience the details of the computation of the energy estimates. We start by taking the scalar product with
u
of the momentum equation. Let’s consider the first term:

\displaystyle u\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+})

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}|u|^{2})-\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}u\partial_{t}u,

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}|u|^{2})-u\Big(\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}u)-u\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+})\Big).

Therefore we get:

\displaystyle u\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+})=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}|u|^{2})+\frac{1}{2}|u|^{2}\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}).

(3.3)

In a similar way, we compute for the divergence term:

\displaystyle u\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}u\otimes u)=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}u\otimes u)+\frac{1}{2}|u|^{2}\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}u).

(3.4)

Now adding (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) and using the mass equation for phase
+
we obtain:

\displaystyle u\Big(\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}u\otimes u)\Big)=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}|u|^{2})+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}u\otimes u).

(3.5)

Similarly we have for phase
-
:

\displaystyle u\Big(\partial_{t}(\alpha_{-}\rho_{-})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{-}\rho_{-}u\otimes u)\Big)=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}(\alpha_{-}\rho_{-}|u|^{2})+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{-}\rho_{-}u\otimes u).

(3.6)

For the terms in
D(u)
and
\operatorname{div}u
, we integrate by parts to obtain:

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{div}\Big((\alpha_{+}\mu_{+}(\rho_{+})+\alpha_{-}\mu_{-}(\rho_{-}))D(u)\Big)\cdot u-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\Big((\alpha_{+}\lambda_{+}+\alpha_{-}\lambda_{-})\operatorname{div}u\Big)\cdot u

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\Big((\alpha_{+}\mu_{+}(\rho_{+})+\alpha_{-}\mu_{-}(\rho_{-}))\lvert D(u)\rvert^{2}+(\alpha_{+}\lambda_{+}+\alpha_{-}\lambda_{-})\lvert\operatorname{div}u\rvert^{2}\Big)

(3.7)

Now we add (
3.5
), (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) and integrate on the domain
\Omega
the scalar product with
u
of the momentum equation to obtain:

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\left(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}+\alpha_{-}\rho_{-}\right)|u|^{2}+\\
\int_{\Omega}\Big((\alpha_{+}\mu_{+}(\rho_{+})+\alpha_{-}\mu_{-}(\rho_{-}))\lvert D(u)\rvert^{2}+(\alpha_{+}\lambda_{+}+\alpha_{-}\lambda_{-})\lvert\operatorname{div}u\rvert^{2}\Big)\\
+\int_{\Omega}\alpha_{+}\nabla p_{+}(\rho_{+})\cdot u+\int\alpha_{-}\nabla p_{-}(\rho_{-})\cdot u=\int_{\Omega}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}+\alpha_{-}\rho_{-})f\cdot u.

The main novelty compared to the one-phase compressible flows is that there are two terms involving the pressures but also the volume fractions of each phase. We detail here the computations. Let’s focus on the
+
phase and set in this computation
\rho=\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}
:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\alpha_{+}\nabla p_{+}(\rho_{+})\cdot u

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho}{\rho_{+}}\nabla\rho_{+}^{\gamma_{+}}\cdot u

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\gamma_{+}}{\gamma_{+}-1}\nabla\rho_{+}^{\gamma_{+}-1}\cdot\rho u

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}-\frac{\gamma_{+}}{\gamma_{+}-1}\rho_{+}^{\gamma_{+}-1}\operatorname{div}(\rho u)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\gamma_{+}}{\gamma_{+}-1}\rho_{+}^{\gamma_{+}-1}\partial_{t}\rho

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\gamma_{+}}{\gamma_{+}-1}\rho_{+}^{\gamma_{+}}\partial_{t}\alpha_{+}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\gamma_{+}}{\gamma_{+}-1}\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}^{\gamma_{+}-1}\partial_{t}\rho_{+}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\gamma_{+}}{\gamma_{+}-1}\rho_{+}^{\gamma_{+}}\partial_{t}\alpha_{+}+\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\gamma_{+}-1}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}^{\gamma_{+}})-\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\gamma_{+}-1}\rho_{+}^{\gamma_{+}}\partial_{t}\alpha_{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\gamma_{+}-1}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}^{\gamma_{+}})+\int_{\Omega}\rho_{+}^{\gamma_{+}}\partial_{t}\alpha_{+}.

(3.8)

Similarly, we obtain for phase
-

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\alpha_{-}\nabla p_{-}(\rho_{-})\cdot u=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\gamma_{-}-1}(\alpha_{-}\rho_{-}^{\gamma_{-}})+\int_{\Omega}\rho_{-}^{\gamma_{-}}\partial_{t}\alpha_{-}.

(3.9)

We add (
3.8
) and (
3.9
) using
\alpha_{+}+\alpha_{-}=1
and
\rho_{+}^{\gamma_{+}}=\rho_{-}^{\gamma_{-}}
we obtain the pressure terms in the energy inequality

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}^{\gamma_{+}}}{\gamma_{+}-1}+\frac{\alpha_{-}\rho_{-}^{\gamma_{-}}}{\gamma_{-}-1}.

There are few works concerning the well-posedness of such two-phase systems. We refer in particular to
[
37
]
for the global existence of weak solution for averaged system with algebraic closure (when
\mu_{+}=\mu_{-}=\mu
and
\lambda_{+}=\lambda_{-}=\lambda
).

Remark 3.3
.

The energy (
2.7
) for one-phase systems is a particular case of this one, taking one volume fraction equal to 1 and the other to 0.

Two-phase systems with pressure PDE closure.

Following
[
12
]
we can consider a two-phase system with a PDE closure for the pressure and only one velocity field of the form:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\alpha_{+}+\alpha_{-}=1,\\
&\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}u)=0,\\
&\partial_{t}(\alpha_{-}\rho_{-})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{-}u)=0,\\
&\partial_{t}((\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}+\alpha_{-}\rho_{-})u)+\operatorname{div}((\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}+\alpha_{-}\rho_{-})u\otimes u)\\
&\quad\quad+\nabla(\alpha_{+}p_{+}(\rho_{+})+\alpha_{-}p_{-}(\rho_{-})-\operatorname{div}\Big((\alpha_{+}\mu_{+}(\rho_{+})+\alpha_{-}\mu_{-}(\rho_{-}))D(u)\Big)\\
&\quad\quad-\nabla\Big((\alpha_{+}\lambda_{+}+\alpha_{-}\lambda_{-})\operatorname{div}u\Big)=(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}+\alpha_{-}\rho_{-})f,\\
&\partial_{t}\alpha_{+}+u\cdot\nabla\alpha_{+}=\frac{1}{\mu}\alpha_{+}\alpha_{-}(p_{+}(\rho_{+})-p_{-}(\rho_{-})).\end{aligned}\right.

(3.10)

The associated energy estimate reads for all
t\in[0,T]
:

For the first term we can use the PDE for the volume fraction and for the second term we can use (

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla(\alpha_{+}p_{+}(\rho_{+}))\cdot u=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}p_{+}(\rho_{+})\partial_{t}\alpha_{+}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\mu}\alpha_{+}\alpha_{-}p_{+}(\rho_{+})\Big(p_{+}(\rho_{+})-p_{-}(\rho_{-})\Big)

\displaystyle+\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\gamma_{+}-1}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}^{\gamma_{+}})+\int_{\Omega}p_{+}(\rho_{+})\partial_{t}\alpha_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\mu}\alpha_{+}\alpha_{-}p_{+}(\rho_{+})\Big(p_{+}(\rho_{+})-p_{-}(\rho_{-})\Big)+\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\gamma_{+}-1}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}^{\gamma_{+}}).

+\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}^{\gamma_{+}}}{\gamma_{+}-1}+\frac{\alpha_{-}\rho_{-}^{\gamma_{-}}}{\gamma_{-}-1}.

In this part, we consider the following system with a PDE closure for the pressure:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon})=0,\\

\quad\quad-\nabla(\alpha_{+}\lambda_{+}(\rho_{+})\operatorname{div}u_{+})+\alpha_{+}\nabla(p_{+}(\rho_{+}))+p_{\mathrm{int},+}\nabla\alpha_{+}=\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}f+F_{\mathrm{int},+},\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{-}\rho_{-}u_{-})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{-}\rho_{-}u_{-}\otimes u_{-})-2\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{-}\mu_{-}(\rho_{-})D(u_{-}))\\
\quad\quad-\nabla(\alpha_{-}\lambda_{-}(\rho_{-})\operatorname{div}u_{-})+\alpha_{-}\nabla(p_{-}(\rho_{-}))+p_{\mathrm{int},-}\nabla\alpha_{-}=\alpha_{-}\rho_{-}f+F_{\mathrm{int},-},\\
p=p_{+}(\rho_{+})=p_{-}(\rho_{-}),\\
(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+})\rvert_{t=0}=R_{+,0},\quad(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}u_{+})\rvert_{t=0}=m_{+,0},\\
(\alpha_{-}\rho_{-})\rvert_{t=0}=R_{-,0},\quad(\alpha_{-}\rho_{-}u_{-})\rvert_{t=0}=m_{-,0}.\end{array}\right.

with
p_{\mathrm{int},i}
and
F_{\mathrm{int},i}
respectively the pressure and the drag term of phase
i=+,-
at the interface.

Remark 4.1
.

We refer the interested reader to
[
8
]
for a discussion on the term
p_{\mathrm{int},i}
. Note that it is an important choice for the model because it ensures the hyperbolicity of the system. Here we will assume that
p_{\mathrm{int},+}=p_{\mathrm{int},-}=p_{\mathrm{int}}
.

Remark 4.2
.

We can specify the drag terms as

F_{\mathrm{int},\pm}=\frac{1}{\eta}\lvert u_{+}-u_{-}\rvert(u_{\pm}-u_{\mp}).

Note that if we take the limit
\eta\to 0
, we get
u_{+}=u_{-}
and by adding the two momentum equations we recover System (
3.1
).

There are few works on the well-posedness of such system. We note in particular the work of
[
11
]
that shows the global existence of weak solution in the one-dimensional case and under some assumptions on the viscosity coefficients and the pressure laws. In the multidimensional case we refer to
[
7
]
(that includes capillarity in the system).

In the following, for the sake of simplicity, we do not consider any viscosity effects nor surface tension and we consider only the interfacial pressure. We do the adimensional analysis of the system using the same Mach number for both phases, with the same change of variable (
2.3
). It leads to a factor of
1/\varepsilon^{2}
in front of the pressure terms in both momentum equations.

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}+\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}=1,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}u_{+}^{\varepsilon})=0,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon}u_{-}^{\varepsilon})=0,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}u_{+}^{\varepsilon})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}u_{+}^{\varepsilon}\otimes u_{+}^{\varepsilon})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\nabla p_{+}(\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon})+p_{\mathrm{int}}^{\varepsilon}\nabla\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}=0,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon}u_{-}^{\varepsilon})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon}u_{-}^{\varepsilon}\otimes u_{-}^{\varepsilon})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}\nabla p_{-}(\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon})+p_{\mathrm{int}}^{\varepsilon}\nabla\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}=0,\\
p_{+}(\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon})=p_{-}(\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon}).\end{array}\right.

In the following, we are going to give a formal proof of the following proposition.

Proposition 4.3
.

Taking the limit as
\varepsilon
goes to zero in the system (
4.1
), we get formally the following limit system:

\begin{cases}\rho_{+}^{0}=C^{\frac{1}{\gamma_{+}}},\\
\rho_{-}^{0}=C^{\frac{1}{\gamma_{-}}},\\
\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{0}u_{+}^{0}+\alpha_{-}^{0}u_{-}^{0})=0,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}^{0}\rho_{+}^{0}u_{+}^{0})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{0}\rho_{+}^{0}u_{+}^{0}\otimes u_{+}^{0})+\alpha_{+}^{0}\nabla\pi+p_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}\nabla\alpha_{+}^{0}=0,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{-}^{0}\rho_{-}^{0}u_{-}^{0})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{-}^{0}\rho_{-}^{0}u_{-}^{0}\otimes u_{-}^{0})+\alpha_{-}^{0}\nabla\pi+p_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}\nabla\alpha_{-}^{0}=0,\\
\partial_{t}\alpha_{\pm}^{0}+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{\pm}^{0}u_{\pm}^{0})=0,\\
\alpha_{+}^{0}+\alpha_{-}^{0}=1.\\
\end{cases}

(4.17)

with
\pi
the limit gradient pressure.

Remark 4.4
.

In the limit system, both phases are homogeneous (
\rho_{\pm}
are constants), but the divergence free condition is now on the averaged velocity
u=\alpha_{+}u_{+}+\alpha_{-}u_{-}
.

Remark 4.5
.

The system (
4.17
) has a similar form than the two-layers shallow-water system between rigid lids when we reformulate using
U=\alpha_{+}u_{+}+\alpha_{-}u_{-}
with
\operatorname{div}U=0
,
h_{+}+h_{-}=\text{cst}
,
p_{\mathrm{int}}=g
the gravity and with a term
\text{cst}\nabla\alpha_{+}
in the momentum equation of phase
+
(see
[
13
]
).

Formal proof.

The formal proof is very similar to the one in Part
3.1.1
for the two-phase system with one velocity and a pressure algebraic closure. At order
\varepsilon^{-2}
the momentum equations yield

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla p_{+}(\rho_{+}^{0})=0,\\
\nabla p_{-}(\rho_{-}^{0})=0.\end{array}\right.

Following the same development than in the proof of Part
3.1.1
, because we also have
p_{+}(\rho_{+}^{0})=p_{-}(\rho_{-}^{0})
from the equality of pressures, we obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{+}^{0}=C_{0}^{1/\gamma_{+}},

\displaystyle\rho_{-}^{0}=C_{0}^{1/\gamma_{-}}.

But now the mass equations at order 1 read

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\alpha_{\pm}^{0}+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{\pm}^{0}u_{\pm}^{0})=0.

Adding these two equations and using
\alpha_{+}^{0}+\alpha_{-}^{0}=1
we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{0}u_{+}^{0}+\alpha_{-}^{0}u_{-}^{0})=0.

From the algebraic closure at order
\varepsilon^{2}
, we know that
p_{+}^{\prime}(\rho_{+}^{0})\rho_{+}^{1}=p_{-}^{\prime}(\rho_{-}^{0})\rho_{-}^{1}
. We set
\pi^{1}:=p_{+}^{\prime}(\rho_{+}^{0})\rho_{+}^{1}
. Therefore, the momentum equations at order 1 read

\displaystyle\rho_{+}^{0}\alpha_{+}^{0}(\partial_{t}u_{+}^{0}+u_{+}^{0}\cdot\nabla u_{+}^{0})+\alpha_{+}^{0}\nabla\pi^{1}+p_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}\nabla\alpha_{+}^{0}=0,

\displaystyle\rho_{-}^{0}\alpha_{-}^{0}(\partial_{t}u_{-}^{0}+u_{-}^{0}\cdot\nabla u_{-}^{0})+\alpha_{-}^{0}\nabla\pi^{1}+p_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}\nabla\alpha_{-}^{0}=0.

Note that the terms
\alpha_{\pm}^{1}\nabla p_{\pm}(\rho_{\pm}^{0})
are cancelled because we have
\nabla p_{\pm}(\rho_{\pm}^{0})=0
thanks to the momentum equations at order
\varepsilon^{-1}
.
∎

4.2
The non-isentropic case

4.2.1
With algebraic closure

We consider a non-isentropic two-phase flow system, where each phase has its own entropy
S_{\pm}
. As in the previous section, we neglect the effects of viscosity and surface tension and consider only an interfacial pressure term
p_{\mathrm{int}}\nabla\alpha_{+}
. We consider the following system with an algebraic closure for the pressure:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\alpha_{+}+\alpha_{-}=1,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}u_{+})=0,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{-}\rho_{-})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{-}u_{-})=0,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}u_{+})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}u_{+}\otimes u_{+})+\alpha_{+}\nabla p_{+}(\rho_{+},S_{+})+p_{\mathrm{int}}\nabla\alpha_{+}=0,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{-}\rho_{-}u_{-})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{-}\rho_{-}u_{-}\otimes u_{-})+\alpha_{-}\nabla p_{-}(\rho_{-},S_{-})+p_{\mathrm{int}}\nabla\alpha_{-}=0,\\
\partial_{t}S_{\pm}+u_{\pm}\cdot\nabla S_{\pm}=0,\\
p_{+}(\rho_{+},S_{+})=p_{-}(\rho_{-},S_{-}).\end{array}\right.

For simplicity, we consider
p_{\pm}(\rho_{\pm},S_{\pm})=\rho_{\pm}^{\gamma_{\pm}}e^{S_{\pm}}
for the state pressure laws. To the best of our knowledge, there are no works on the global existence of weak solutions for this system.

We consider the following adimensional system:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}+\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}=1,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{+})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}u_{+}^{\varepsilon})=0,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon}u_{-}^{\varepsilon})=0,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}u_{+}^{\varepsilon})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}u_{+}^{\varepsilon}\otimes u_{+}^{\varepsilon})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\nabla p_{+}(\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon},S_{+}^{\varepsilon})+p_{\mathrm{int}}\nabla\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}=0,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon}u_{-}^{\varepsilon})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon}u_{-}^{\varepsilon}\otimes u_{-}^{\varepsilon})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}\nabla p_{-}(\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon},S_{-}^{\varepsilon})+p_{\mathrm{int}}\nabla\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}=0,\\
\partial_{t}S_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}+u_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla S_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}=0,\\
p_{+}(\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon},S_{+}^{\varepsilon})=p_{-}(\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon},S_{-}^{\varepsilon}).\end{array}\right.

In this subsection we are going to give a formal proof of the following proposition.

Proposition 4.6
.

Taking the limit as
\varepsilon
goes to zero in the system (
4.2.1
), we get formally the following limit system:

\begin{cases}\rho_{+}^{0}=C^{\frac{1}{\gamma_{+}}}e^{-S_{+}^{0}/\gamma_{+}},\\
\rho_{-}^{0}=C^{\frac{1}{\gamma_{-}}}e^{-S_{-}^{0}/\gamma_{-}},\\
\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{0}u_{+}^{0}+\alpha_{-}^{0}u_{-}^{0})=0,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}^{0}\rho_{+}^{0}u_{+}^{0})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{0}\rho_{+}^{0}u_{+}^{0}\otimes u_{+}^{0})+\alpha_{+}^{0}\nabla\pi^{1}+p_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}\nabla\alpha_{+}^{0}=0,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{-}^{0}\rho_{-}^{0}u_{-}^{0})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{-}^{0}\rho_{-}^{0}u_{-}^{0}\otimes u_{-}^{0})+\alpha_{-}^{0}\nabla\pi^{1}+p_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}\nabla\alpha_{-}^{0}=0,\\
\partial_{t}S_{\pm}^{0}+u_{\pm}^{0}\cdot\nabla S_{\pm}^{0}=0,\\
\partial_{t}\alpha_{+}^{0}+u_{+}^{0}\cdot\nabla\alpha_{+}^{0}=0,\\
\alpha_{+}^{0}+\alpha_{-}^{0}=1,\\
\partial_{t}\alpha_{+}^{0}+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{0}u_{+}^{0})=0.\end{cases}

(4.32)

with
\pi^{1}
the limit pressure (of both phases).

Formal proof.

This formal proof is very similar to the one in the non-isentropic two-phase one-velocity case and we use the same expansion for the pressure (
3.43
). We obtain at order 1:

\displaystyle\alpha_{+}^{0}+\alpha_{-}^{0}=1,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}S_{\pm}^{0}+u_{\pm}^{0}\cdot\nabla S_{\pm}^{0}=0.

and the algebraic closure reads

\displaystyle p_{+}(\rho_{+}^{0},S_{+}^{0})=p_{-}(\rho_{-}^{0},S_{+}^{0}).

At order
\varepsilon^{-2}
the momentum equations yield

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla p_{+}(\rho_{+}^{0})=0,\\
\nabla p_{-}(\rho_{-}^{0})=0.\end{array}\right.

Thus, we can set
C(t)
a constant in space such that

\displaystyle p_{+}(\rho_{+}^{0},S_{+}^{0})=p_{-}(\rho_{-}^{0},S_{+}^{0})=C(t),

Adding the two mass equations using the entropy equations and the state laws, we now obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{0}u_{+}^{0}+\alpha_{-}^{0}u_{-}^{0})+\Big(\frac{\alpha_{+}^{0}}{\gamma_{+}}+\frac{\alpha_{-}^{0}}{\gamma_{-}}\Big)\frac{C^{\prime}(t)}{C(t)}=0.

(4.33)

Now we integrate in space to get

\displaystyle\frac{C^{\prime}(t)}{C(t)}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\frac{\alpha_{+}^{0}}{\gamma_{+}}+\frac{\alpha_{-}^{0}}{\gamma_{-}}\Big)dx=0.

Thus
C^{\prime}(t)=0
and
C
is a constant in time and space. Coming back to (
4.33
) we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{0}u_{+}^{0}+\alpha_{-}^{0}u_{-}^{0})=0.

The mass equations at order
1
now read

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\alpha_{\pm}^{0}+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{\pm}^{0}u_{\pm}^{0})=0.

Setting
\pi_{i}^{1}=p_{i}(\rho_{i}^{0})\Big(\gamma_{i}\frac{\rho_{i}^{1}}{\rho_{i}^{0}}+S_{i}^{1}\Big)
for
i=+,-
, we get from the order
\varepsilon^{2}
of the algebraic closure:

\displaystyle\partial_{\rho}p_{+}(\rho_{+}^{0},S_{+}^{0})\rho_{+}^{1}+\partial_{S}p_{+}S_{+}^{1}

\displaystyle=(\partial_{\rho}p_{-}\rho_{-}^{1}+\partial_{S}p_{-}S_{-}^{1}),

\displaystyle\pi_{+}^{1}

\displaystyle=\pi_{-}^{1}:=\pi^{1}.

Finally, the order 1 momentum equations read

\displaystyle\rho_{+}^{0}\alpha_{+}^{0}(\partial_{t}u_{+}^{0}+u_{+}^{0}\nabla\cdot u_{+}^{0})+\alpha_{+}^{0}\nabla\pi^{1}+p_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}\nabla\alpha_{+}^{0}=0,

\displaystyle\rho_{-}^{0}\alpha_{-}^{0}(\partial_{t}u_{-}^{0}+u_{-}^{0}\nabla\cdot u_{-}^{0})+\alpha_{-}^{0}\nabla\pi^{1}+p_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}\nabla\alpha_{-}^{0}=0.

∎

4.2.2
With PDE closure

We now consider a non-isentropic two-phase flow system with a PDE closure for the pressure:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\alpha_{+}+\alpha_{-}=1,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}u_{+})=0,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{-}\rho_{-})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{-}u_{-})=0,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}u_{+})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}\rho_{+}u_{+}\otimes u_{+})+\alpha_{+}\nabla p_{+}(\rho_{+},S_{+})+p_{\mathrm{int}}\nabla\alpha_{+}=0,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{-}\rho_{-}u_{-})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{-}\rho_{-}u_{-}\otimes u_{-})+\alpha_{-}\nabla p_{-}(\rho_{-},S_{-})+p_{\mathrm{int}}\nabla\alpha_{-}=0,\\
\partial_{t}S_{\pm}+u_{\pm}\cdot\nabla S_{\pm}=0,\\
\partial_{t}\alpha_{+}+u_{I}\cdot\nabla\alpha_{+}=\alpha_{+}\alpha_{-}(p_{+}(\rho_{+},S_{+})-p_{-}(\rho_{-},S_{-})),\end{array}\right.

with
u_{I}
the interface velocity that needs to be specified according to the physical context. We assume here that
u_{I}=u_{+}
(see
[
36
]
).

We consider the following adimensional system:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}+\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}=1,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{+})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}u_{+}^{\varepsilon})=0,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon}u_{-}^{\varepsilon})=0,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}u_{+}^{\varepsilon})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon}u_{+}^{\varepsilon}\otimes u_{+}^{\varepsilon})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\nabla p_{+}(\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon},S_{+}^{\varepsilon})+p_{\mathrm{int}}\nabla\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}=0,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon}u_{-}^{\varepsilon})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon}u_{-}^{\varepsilon}\otimes u_{-}^{\varepsilon})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}\nabla p_{-}(\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon},S_{-}^{\varepsilon})+p_{\mathrm{int}}\nabla\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}=0,\\
\partial_{t}S_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}+u_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla S_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}=0,\\
\partial_{t}\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}+u_{+}^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}=\alpha_{+}^{\varepsilon}\alpha_{-}^{\varepsilon}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big(p_{+}(\rho_{+}^{\varepsilon},S_{+}^{\varepsilon})-p_{-}(\rho_{-}^{\varepsilon},S_{-}^{\varepsilon})\Big).\end{array}\right.

In this subsection we are going to give a formal proof of the following proposition.

Proposition 4.7
.

Taking the limit as
\varepsilon
goes to zero in the system (
4.2.2
), we get formally the following limit system:

\begin{cases}\rho_{+}^{0}=C^{\frac{1}{\gamma_{+}}}e^{-S_{+}^{0}/\gamma_{+}},\\
\rho_{-}^{0}=C^{\frac{1}{\gamma_{-}}}e^{-S_{-}^{0}/\gamma_{-}},\\
\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{0}u_{+}^{0}+\alpha_{-}^{0}u_{-}^{0})=0,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{+}^{0}\rho_{+}^{0}u_{+}^{0})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{0}\rho_{+}^{0}u_{+}^{0}\otimes u_{+}^{0})+\alpha_{+}^{0}\nabla\pi_{+}^{1}+p_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}\nabla\alpha_{+}^{0}=0,\\
\partial_{t}(\alpha_{-}^{0}\rho_{-}^{0}u_{-}^{0})+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{-}^{0}\rho_{-}^{0}u_{-}^{0}\otimes u_{-}^{0})+\alpha_{-}^{0}\nabla\pi_{-}^{1}+p_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}\nabla\alpha_{-}^{0}=0,\\
\rho_{+}^{0}\alpha_{+}^{0}(\partial_{t}u_{+}^{0}+u_{+}^{0}\nabla\cdot u_{+}^{0})+\alpha_{+}^{0}\nabla\pi^{1}+p_{\mathrm{int}}^{0},\\
\partial_{t}S_{\pm}^{0}+u_{\pm}^{0}\cdot\nabla S_{\pm}^{0}=0,\\
\alpha_{+}^{0}\operatorname{div}(u_{+}^{0})=\alpha_{+}^{0}\alpha_{-}^{0}(\pi_{-}^{1}-\pi_{+}^{1}),\\
\alpha_{+}^{0}+\alpha_{-}^{0}=1,\\
\partial_{t}\alpha_{+}^{0}+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{0}u_{+}^{0})=0.\end{cases}

(4.48)

with
\pi_{i}^{1}
the limit pressure of phase
i
,
i=+,-
.

Remark 4.8
.

Like in
[
36
]
, we do not get the equality of the limit pressures, but a relation between them involving
\operatorname{div}u_{+}^{0}
, in the form:

\alpha_{+}^{0}\operatorname{div}(u_{+}^{0})=\alpha_{+}^{0}\alpha_{-}^{0}(\pi_{-}^{1}-\pi_{+}^{1}).

This allows to consider only one of the pressures as a variable, the other being given by this constraint.

Formal proof.

This formal proof is very similar to the one in the non-isentropic two-phase two-velocity case with algebraic closure. In the same way, we obtain

\displaystyle p_{+}(\rho_{+}^{0},S_{+}^{0})=p_{-}(\rho_{-}^{0},S_{+}^{0})=C,

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{+}^{0}u_{+}^{0}+\alpha_{-}^{0}u_{-}^{0})=0.

Note that the PDE closure at order
\varepsilon^{-2}
reads as the algebraic closure

\displaystyle p_{+}(\rho_{+}^{0},S_{+}^{0})=p_{-}(\rho_{-}^{0},S_{+}^{0}).

The mass equations at order
1
now read

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\alpha_{\pm}^{0}+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{\pm}^{0}u_{\pm}^{0})=0.

Setting
\pi_{i}^{1}=p_{i}(\rho_{i}^{0})\Big(\gamma_{i}\frac{\rho_{i}^{1}}{\rho_{i}^{0}}+S_{i}^{1}\Big)
for
i=+,-
, we get from the order
1
of the PDE closure:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\alpha_{+}^{0}+u_{+}^{0}\cdot\nabla\alpha_{+}^{0}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{+}^{0}\alpha_{-}^{0}\Big(\partial_{\rho}p_{+}(\rho_{+}^{0},S_{+}^{0})\rho_{+}^{1}+\partial_{S}p_{+}S_{+}^{1}-(\partial_{\rho}p_{-}\rho_{-}^{1}+\partial_{S}p_{-}S_{-}^{1})\Big),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\alpha_{+}^{0}+u_{+}^{0}\cdot\operatorname{div}\alpha_{+}^{0}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{+}^{0}\alpha_{-}^{0}(\pi_{+}^{1}-\pi_{-}^{1}.).

Using the mass equation to replace
\partial_{t}\alpha_{+}^{0}
, we get

\displaystyle-\alpha_{+}^{0}\operatorname{div}(u_{+}^{0})=\alpha_{+}^{0}\alpha_{-}^{0}(\pi_{+}^{1}-\pi_{-}^{1}).

Finally, the order 1 momentum equations read

\displaystyle\rho_{+}^{0}\alpha_{+}^{0}(\partial_{t}u_{+}^{0}+u_{+}^{0}\nabla\cdot u_{+}^{0})+\alpha_{+}^{0}\nabla\pi_{+}^{1}+p_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}\nabla\alpha_{+}^{0}=0,

\displaystyle\rho_{-}^{0}\alpha_{-}^{0}(\partial_{t}u_{-}^{0}+u_{-}^{0}\nabla\cdot u_{-}^{0})+\alpha_{-}^{0}\nabla\pi_{-}^{1}+p_{\mathrm{int}}^{0}\nabla\alpha_{-}^{0}=0.

∎

5
Some open problems

A non-isentropic case with more complicated thermodynamics.
In this paper, we consider a system of equations for non-isentropic flows with the simplest entropy equations. This allows for an easier formal analysis of the low Mach number limit. One could improve the physical basis of the system by adding thermodynamics, using temperatures for each phase to get additional energy equations. This is done by
[
43
]
that generalize the low-Mach-number asymptotics for two-phase flows of granular materials. After non-dimensionalizing the system with respect to a reference thermodynamic state, the Mach number appears in the energy equations. Considering a PDE closure for the pressure that is an equation on the volume fraction that depends on both pressure difference and phasial temperatures difference, they get in the limit equality of both pressures and temperatures. Then only one energy equation is needed in the system, and the other can be used as an equation for the volume fraction. This is particularly advantageous in a numerical point of view.

A different Mach number for each phase.
In this paper we have always considered the same Mach number for both phases. To get closer to the physics, one would like to consider the low-Mach-number limit of only one Mach number, the one of the phase with the smallest speed of sound. The asymptotic calculation is done by
[
36
]
, where the authors use a system of equations governing two compressible gas phases, following respectively a perfect gas law and a stiff gas pressure state law, and perform a low-Mach-number limit with respect to the second phase. They obtain in the limit a constant density for the second phase, but not anymore equality of pressures or of temperatures nor the free divergence of the velocity of the second phase.

General initial data in more general domains.
In this paper, we only presented some results for the low Mach number limit for multiphase flows in the case of well-prepared data, in a bounded domain. As far as we know, nothing has been done in the case of more general initial data in other types of domains.
