A rare case of isolated pulmonary metastasis from a colorectal tubulovillous adenoma with focal carcinoma
Authors: Guicheng Pan, Junliang Ma, Yongxiang Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Isolated pulmonary metastasis originating from an early-stage colorectal adenoma with only focal carcinomatous transformation is exceedingly rare and poses significant diagnostic challenges.Case presentation:A 68-year-old man was found to have a solitary pulmonary nodule on routine chest computed tomography, radiologically highly suggestive of primary lung cancer. The patient underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, during which frozen-section analysis indicated invasive adenocarcinoma, prompting a lobectomy with systematic lymph node dissection. However, definitive histopathology, combined with immunohistochemistry, demonstrated that the lung lesion was metastatic adenocarcinoma of colorectal origin. Subsequent colonoscopy identified tubulovillous adenomas in the ascending colon and a tubulovillous adenoma with high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia and focal adenocarcinoma in the sigmoid colon.Conclusion:This case demonstrates that even a colorectal tubulovillous adenoma with only focal carcinomatous transformation can give rise to isolated pulmonary metastasis – a phenomenon that is underrecognized. When a solitary pulmonary nodule exhibits classic primary lung cancer features on imaging, the possibility of metastasis from an occult colorectal primary must still be considered. Integrated morphological and immunohistochemical evaluation, along with appropriate gastrointestinal follow-up, is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and to guide correct staging and management.

Introduction

Colorectal adenomas are well-recognized precursor lesions in the adenoma–carcinoma sequence, a multistep process that typically spans several years[1]. While distant metastasis is common in advanced colorectal carcinoma, particularly to the liver via portal venous drainage, isolated pulmonary metastasis is usually associated with deeply invasive or advanced primary tumors[2,3]. Pulmonary metastasis arising from an early colorectal lesion with only focal carcinoma is therefore exceptionally uncommon[4].

Here, we report a rare case in which a solitary pulmonary nodule was the initial manifestation of the disease and was initially misdiagnosed as primary lung cancer. A definitive diagnosis was established only after postoperative pathological and immunohistochemical analyses, underscoring the indispensable role of pathology in differentiating primary lung adenocarcinoma from metastatic colorectal cancer.

Case presentation

A 68-year-old male underwent a routine physical examination, during which a contrast-enhanced chest computed tomography scan revealed a solitary nodule measuring 11.8 × 11.6 mm in the right upper lobe. The lesion demonstrated spiculated margins, lobulation, pleural traction, and homogeneous enhancement, features that were highly suggestive of a primary lung malignancy (Fig. 1). The patient had a 5-year history of hypertension controlled with nifedipine and a surgical history of cholecystectomy and renal cyst resection. There was no family history of malignancy. Pathological findings

Lung specimen

Definitive histopathological examination of the lung nodule revealed infiltrating adenocarcinoma with a glandular growth pattern. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated diffuse, strong positivity for pan-cytokeratin (CK), CDX2 (nuclear), SATB2 (nuclear), Villin (membranous/cytoplasmic), CEA, and CK20 (cytoplasmic), with negativity for CK7, TTF-1, and Napsin-A. The Ki-67 proliferation index was approximately 60%. Colonoscopic and colonic pathology findings

To identify the primary tumor, a colonoscopy was performed postoperatively. Two pedunculated polyps (0.8 and 1.0 cm) were identified in the ascending colon, and a 1.2 cm intraluminal lesion with a central depression and a friable surface was observed in the sigmoid colon (Fig. 4A and B). Histopathological evaluation showed that the ascending colon lesions were tubulovillous adenomas. The sigmoid colon lesion was diagnosed as a tubulovillous adenoma with high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia and a focal area of tubular adenocarcinoma. No definite lymphovascular invasion was identified in the available specimens.

Discussion

This case is notable for several atypical features. First, the pulmonary lesion was the initial and dominant clinical manifestation, whereas the primary colorectal lesion was small and demonstrated only focal invasive carcinoma. Second, the pulmonary nodule exhibited classic radiological characteristics of primary lung cancer, making preoperative differentiation highly challenging.

Although distant metastasis from early colorectal neoplasia is rare, it is not biologically implausible[5]. One possible explanation is that a deeply invasive focus of carcinoma may not have been fully captured in the endoscopically resected specimen. Such a focus could have gained access to the systemic circulation, potentially via the inferior rectal venous plexus, thereby bypassing portal venous drainage and seeding the lung. Similar mechanisms have been postulated in rare reports of distant metastasis from early colorectal cancer[6].

From a pathological perspective, this case also illustrates a well-known interpretive pitfall: distinguishing true early stromal invasion from pseudoinvasion in colorectal adenomas. Pseudoinvasion, caused by prolapse of adenomatous epithelium into the stalk or submucosa, can closely mimic invasive carcinoma on routine hematoxylin and eosin staining[1]. Misinterpretation may lead to overdiagnosis of malignancy or, conversely, underestimation of genuine invasive foci. In the present case, careful morphological assessment combined with the typical colorectal immunophenotype (CDX2+/SATB2+/CK7−) allowed definitive identification of true carcinoma both in the primary sigmoid lesion and in the pulmonary metastasis. This underscores the importance of integrating morphology with a panel of lineage-specific immunomarkers when evaluating suspicious glandular lesions in the colon and their potential metastases.

This case also emphasizes the decisive role of immunohistochemistry in determining tumor origin. The combined positivity for CDX2 and SATB2, together with negativity for lung markers such as CK7 and TTF-1, is highly specific for a colorectal origin and is critical in the evaluation of metastatic adenocarcinoma presenting as a solitary pulmonary nodule. The additional positivity for CK20, a marker of hindgut-derived carcinomas, further reinforces the colorectal origin of the metastasis.

Clinically, this case carries important implications. For pathologists and pulmonologists, reliance on imaging alone is insufficient to distinguish primary lung cancer from metastatic disease. For gastroenterologists and oncologists, even early colorectal lesions with high-grade dysplasia or focal carcinoma should not be assumed to carry no metastatic risk, highlighting the need for meticulous pathological assessment and appropriate postoperative surveillance. This case report adheres to the SCARE guidelines; the completed SCARE checklist is provided as supplementary material. Although this is a single case, it highlights a potential diagnostic and biological pitfall that warrants further investigation in larger cohorts.

Conclusion

This case conveys three clinically relevant messages. First, colorectal adenomas harboring only a microscopic focus of invasive carcinoma – as seen in this case – may still possess metastatic potential, warranting systematic postoperative surveillance even after complete endoscopic resection. Second, a solitary pulmonary nodule with spiculated margins and pleural traction, the classic radiological hallmarks of primary lung cancer, can represent metastatic disease from an occult colorectal primary; reliance on imaging features alone carries a risk of misdiagnosis. Third, in such diagnostically challenging scenarios, a panel of immunohistochemical markers (CDX2, SATB2, Villin, combined with CK7 and TTF-1 negativity) is decisive in confirming colorectal origin. Pathologists should also remain vigilant for pseudoinvasion in adenomas, which may mimic true stromal invasion and confound staging. Heightened awareness of these diagnostic pitfalls can facilitate accurate diagnosis and appropriate clinical management.
