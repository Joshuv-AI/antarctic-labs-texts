Development and validation of a model to predict full thickness rotator cuff tear
Authors: Tianci Wang, Qiuping Tian, Bo Lu, Jialiang Guo, Bing Yin, Yanbin Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFull-thickness rotator cuff tear (FTRCT) often requires surgical treatment. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the diagnostic gold standard; however, it is costly and resource-intensive. We aimed to develop and validate a clinical prediction model for FTRCT in patients with RCI, with the goal of identifying those at high risk at the initial visit.MethodsThis prospective study consecutively enrolled patients clinically diagnosed with RCI from May 2025 to September 2025. All patients underwent shoulder MRI to confirm the presence or absence of FTRCT. Potential predictors involving demographic characteristics, clinical history, and physical examination findings, were collected. Predictor selection was performed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, followed by multivariable logistic regression to construct a risk prediction model, and then a nomogram was developed.Results357 patients were included, MRI confirmed FTRCT in 166 patients (46.5%). Patients were randomly divided into a training set (n = 249) and a test set (n = 108). LASSO regression identified 10 candidate predictors, and multivariable logistic regression identified physical labor, history of trauma, age, daily pain score, pain duration, and physical examination shoulder strength as independent predictors of FTRCT. The nomogram achieved an AUC of 0.910 (95% CI, 0.88–0.95), with 88% sensitivity, 82% specificity, and a C-index of 0.890 (95% CI, 0.83–0.95). Calibration and decision curve analyses supported its accuracy and clinical utility.Conclusions This study developed a preliminary clinical nomogram for predicting FTRCT in patients with RCI, which showed good performance in internal validation and may help support early risk assessment and MRI triage. Introduction

Rotator cuff injury (RCI) is one of the most common causes of shoulder pain and dysfunction and represents a substantial health burden worldwide [1]. Its prevalence increases markedly with age, affecting approximately 40%–50% of individuals older than 60 years [2–6]. Given its high frequency and its impact on daily activities, work capacity, and quality of life, RCI imposes considerable personal and socioeconomic costs.

Management of RCIs depends largely on the severity of the structural damage. Full thickness rotator cuff tears (FTRCTs) generally require surgical repair [7, 8], whereas partial-thickness tears and tendinopathy are usually managed conservatively at the initial stage [9]. Although RCIs can often be suspected on the basis of history and specialized physical examination, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) remains the reference standard for confirming the presence and severity of tendon injury and for guiding treatment decisions [7]. However, routine MRI for every patient with suspected RCI is neither practical nor efficient. MRI is costly, time- consuming, and often limited by appointment availability, which may increase the burden on both patients and healthcare systems. At the same time, failure to obtain MRI in patients with FTRCI may delay surgical referral, potentially resulting in poor outcomes [8, 10]. Delayed surgery for full-thickness tears has been associated with inferior outcomes, as untreated tears may progress in size and fatty infiltration over time [8, 11]. Therefore, clinicians face a common dilemma at the initial visit: how to identify patients who are more likely to harbor FTRCT and should be prioritized for MRI evaluation. Previous studies on the severity assessment of RCIs have mainly focused on identifying individual risk factors associated with RCT [12–14]. In addition, imaging-based parameters such as acromial spur morphology, acromiohumeral interval, and acromioclavicular angle have also been explored as predictors of rotator cuff tears [15–18]. However, risk factors alone do not provide clinicians with an intuitive or quantitative estimate of an individual patient’s probability of having a full-thickness tear. A practical clinical prediction tool may therefore be useful for early risk stratification and more selective MRI utilization.

Accordingly, this study was to develop and validate a clinical prediction model for estimating the probability of FTRCT in patients with RCI using information that is readily available at the initial clinical visit. We sought to provide a simple and clinically applicable tool to support early decision-making, facilitate more appropriate MRI referral, and improve the efficiency of care for patients with suspected rotator cuff injury.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient population

This was a prospective study conducted at our shoulder joint specialty clinic. From May 2025 to September 2025, we consecutively enrolled patients clinically diagnosed with a rotator cuff injury (RCI) by a senior shoulder specialist based on a comprehensive physical examination. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) clinical diagnosis of RCI confirmed by a senior shoulder specialist through standardized physical examination maneuvers (including the Jobe test, external rotation resistance test, and abdominal compression test); (3) willingness to participate and provide written informed consent. The exclusion criteria were: (1) a history of shoulder surgery on the affected side; (2) combined with other conditions causing shoulder pain (e.g., shoulder fracture, infection, rheumatoid arthritis); (3) neurological disorders affecting shoulder strength (e.g., cervical radiculopathy, stroke sequelae); (4) inability to undergo MRI (e.g., claustrophobia, non-MRI compatible implants). All procedures followed the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection and predictor variables

Demographic and clinical history include: age, sex, dominant or non-dominant side, whether a direct trauma leading to shoulder pain or not, engagement in physical labor (PL), history of hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, hyperlipidemia, and smoking. PL was further classified as heavy (e.g., construction workers, miners, farmers without automation) or mild (e.g., photographers, musicians, nurses, teachers) [19]. Daily pain score (DPS) was the average shoulder pain level of daily activity, Pain intensity was measured using a 10-cm visual analog scale (VAS), ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst imaginable pain). Patients were asked to rate their average shoulder pain during daily activities [20]. Pain time (PT, in months) was defined as the period from the first occurrence of shoulder pain (including intermittent pain with transient relief) to the clinic visit. The duration was recorded in months.

Specialized physical examination was performed by an experienced shoulder specialist. Standardized tests included the jobe test (supraspinatus assessment) [21, 22], abdominal compression test (subscapularis assessment) [23], and external rotation resistance (infraspinatus/teres minor assessment) [24, 25]. Results of the above tests are classified as positive or negative, for more precise quantification of specialized physical examinations, we introduced two quantitative indicators for each specialized test: physical examination shoulder strength (PESS) and physical examination pain score (PEPS). For each of the three specialized test, the patient was asked to rate the intensity of pain using VAS, which was described previously. PEPS was defined as the highest VAS score recorded across the three specialized tests. PESS was assessed by a shoulder specialist during physical examination, who compared the strength of the affected side with the healthy contralateral side. For each of the three standardized physical examination maneuvers, PESS was defined as the strength of the affected shoulder expressed as a percentage of that of the contralateral shoulder. The patient-level PESS used for analysis was defined as the lowest percentage among the three test-specific measurements. All PESS measurements were completed and recorded before MRI results were available and were therefore independent of the subsequent MRI findings.

All patients underwent standardized shoulder MRI using a 3.0 Tesla Siemens scanner with a dedicated shoulder coil. The protocol included T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and proton density-weighted fat-suppressed sequences in oblique coronal, oblique sagittal, and axial planes, slice thickness was 3 mm with a 0.3 mm interslice gap. A FTRCT was defined as complete discontinuity of tendon fibers from the articular to the bursal surface [26]. MRI images were independently reviewed by two experienced shoulder specialists (with > 5 years of musculoskeletal MRI experience) blinded to clinical data. Disagreements were resolved by a third specialist, with the majority opinion adopted as final.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (Version 4.4.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR) as appropriate; categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages (%).

Variable selection was performed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. Variables with non-zero coefficients were selected as candidate predictors and further analysed by univariable and multivariable logistic regression to identify independent risk factors for FTRCT. According to guideline recommendations for clinical prediction model development [27], the required sample size is at least ten times the number of predictor variables. With 15 candidate variables, the minimum required sample size was 150, our sample of 357 patients was therefore adequate. A nomogram was then constructed based on the selected predictors. Model performance was evaluated by the concordance index (C-index), receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, calibration plot, and decision curve analysis (DCA). The C-index and area under the ROC curve (AUC) ranged from 0.5 to 1.0, with higher values indicating better discriminative ability. Calibration plots assessed agreement between predicted and observed probabilities. DCA evaluated clinical utility based on net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To further evaluate the internal validity and stability of the model, bootstrap resampling with 1,000 replications was performed. The optimism-corrected AUC was calculated to assess potential overfitting.

Nomogram development and performance

A nomogram incorporating the 10 candidate predictors was constructed (Fig. 3). In the training set, the model achieved an AUC of 0.910 (95% CI: 0.88–0.95), with a sensitivity of 88% and specificity of 82% (Fig. 4), indicating excellent discriminative ability. The C-index was 0.890 (95% CI: 0.83–0.95). Bootstrap internal validation with 1,000 resamples yielded an apparent AUC of 0.907 (95% CI: 0.878–0.937) and an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.892 (95% CI: 0.861–0.921), indicating good internal validity with minimal overfitting (Fig. 5). The calibration plot showed good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities (Fig. 6a, b). Decision curve analysis demonstrated a positive net benefit over a wide range of threshold probabilities (Fig. 7a, b). Similar performance was observed in the test set, confirming internal validity.

Nomogram usage

As shown in Fig. 3, the value of each predictor is located on its corresponding axis, and a vertical line is drawn to the “Points” axis to obtain the score for that predictor (e.g., sex: 0 for male, 2.5 for female). The individual scores are summed to obtain a total point value, which is then projected onto the “Risk of FTRCT” axis to obtain the estimated probability of FTRCT.

Discussion

In this prospective cohort of patients with clinically suspected RCI, we developed and internally validated a clinical prediction model for estimating the probability of full-thickness rotator cuff tear. The model incorporated six independent predictors—age, physical labor, trauma history, daily pain score, pain duration, and physical examination shoulder strength—and showed good discrimination, calibration, and potential clinical utility. Among these, reduced shoulder strength during specialized physical examination (quantitative evaluation using PESS) was the most informative predictor. These results indicate that routinely available clinical information may provide a useful basis for early risk stratification and more selective use of MRI in this population.

The clinical rationale for developing this prediction model is supported by evidence that early surgical repair for full-thickness rotator cuff tears yields better outcomes than delayed surgery after failed conservative treatment [8]. Furthermore, untreated full-thickness tears tend to progress over time—with a reported progression rate of 54.9% at approximately 3-year follow-up [11]—and progressive fatty infiltration may compromise the success of later repair [28, 29]. These findings underscore the importance of identifying patients at high risk for full-thickness tears at an early stage, so that timely MRI and surgical referral can be considered.

Previous studies applied machine learning to predict rotator cuff tears using imaging-derived anatomical parameters [15–18]. In contrast, our model specifically predicts full-thickness tears using clinical variables available at the initial visit, addressing a distinct decision point in the clinical pathway. Previous studies evaluating the performance of specialized physical examination tests have uniformly classified results as positive or negative based on the presence of pain or subjective weakness [22–26]. In our study, we quantify specialized physical examination findings as continuous variables, and we demonstrate its dose-response relationship with FTRCT risk. This initiative could provide a reference for future studies require RCI quantification, clinicians should move beyond binary “weak/normal” assessment and adopt a graded evaluation. Notably, PESS emerged as the strongest predictor of FTRCT (OR = 3.5 per 20% decrease), outperforming other clinical variables. The strong predictive value of PESS may be explained by the fact that weakness on resisted testing reflects more severe tendon compromise, which is characteristic of full-thickness tears [30]. In contrast, PEPS showed poor predictive ability. This discrepancy likely arises because: (1) pain is less specific, whereas profound weakness is more specific to complete tendon discontinuity; (2) PEPS is more susceptible to transient confounders such as analgesic use, anxiety, or pain tolerance; (3) true mechanical weakness may outweigh pain as the dominant signal for FTRCT. Clinically, a patient with severe pain but near-normal strength (high PEPS, high PESS) is unlikely to have an FTRCT, whereas disproportionate weakness relative to pain (low PESS, moderate PEPS) should raise suspicion. Our findings suggest that quantifying strength deficits, not pain severity, should be prioritized during physical examination for FTRCT triage.

DPS and PT reflect the severity of RCI, and were independently associated with 2.2-and 1.5-fold increased risk of FTRCT respectively, in the present study. DPS captured the patient’s average pain level during daily activities, and patients with higher DPS experience greater mechanical irritation and inflammatory response around the torn tendon [31], therefore representing more severe tendon lesion. PT adds a temporal dimension to severity assessment. Longer symptom duration may indicate delayed care-seeking, failed natural healing, and continuous tendon degradation, allowing a partial tear to progress to full-thickness. Our findings showed that each 6-month increase in PT was associated with a 1.5-fold increase in the odds of FTRCT, underscoring the need to avoid prolonged “watchful waiting” beyond several months without diagnostic imaging, particularly in patients with additional risk factors.

Age, trauma, and heavy physical labor are well-established risk factors for rotator cuff tears [13, 14, 31–33]. Our study confirmed each as an independent predictor of FTRCT, with effect sizes comparable to previous reports: age (OR = 1.9 per 10-year increase vs. OR = 1.76–5.07 in prior meta-analyses [32]), trauma (OR = 3.1 vs. OR = 2.74–5.20 [31, 32]), and heavy physical labor (OR = 2.4 vs. OR = 1.57–2.51 [32]). Notably, we further distinguished heavy from mild physical labor, demonstrating a graded relationship between occupational workload and FTRCT risk. These findings reinforce the multifactorial nature of rotator cuff tears, wherein biological degeneration (age), acute overload (trauma), and chronic microtrauma (heavy labor) converge to compromise tendon integrity.

The primary clinical challenge in patients with suspected RCI is determining whether immediate MRI is warranted or whether an initial trial of conservative management is appropriate. The nomogram developed in this study provides a quantitative, individualized estimate of FTRCT probability based on clinical variables and demonstrated good discrimination.To facilitate clinical translation, we defined risk thresholds based on the nomogram’s predicted probability: low-risk (< 20%), intermediate-risk (20%–70%), and high-risk (> 70%). These cutoffs were chosen to balance sensitivity and specificity based on the ROC analysis. In the high-risk group (> 70%), where model specificity is relatively high, early MRI evaluation and timely surgical referral may be considered, given the potential impact of delayed intervention on outcomes [8, 10]. In the intermediate-risk group (20%–70%), the model provides modest net benefit, and clinical decision-making may be guided by patient preferences, symptom severity, and functional impairment. In the low-risk group (< 20%), where the sensitivity is relatively high (88%), an initial period (often 8–12 weeks) of conservative management without immediate MRI may be reasonable, with imaging reconsidered if symptoms persist or worsen. Overall, this risk-stratified approach may help support more selective use of MRI and improve resource allocation, while complementing—rather than replacing—clinical judgment.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the model was developed and internally validated using a single-centre cohort of 357 patients. Although internal validation showed promising performance, external validation in diverse populations and healthcare settings is necessary to assess generalizability. Second, the model’s sensitivity (88%) and specificity (82%) are not perfect; a small number of FTRCTs may be missed (false negatives), and some patients without FTRCT may be referred for MRI unnecessarily (false positives). Therefore, the nomogram should be used as a decision support tool, not as a replacement for clinical judgment. Third, the study did not assess inter-rater reliability of physical examination findings, which may vary among clinicians. Future large-scale, multi-centre prospective studies are needed to validate and refine the model. Fourth, the high prevalence of FTRCT in this cohort (46.5%) reflects the enriched patient population at our institution, which is a regional orthopedic referral center. Some patients seen at this clinic are typically referred with more advanced pathology, representing the target population for this prediction model. Thus, the model’s findings are most applicable to specialist settings, validation in lower-prevalence primary-care populations is needed before broader use. Fifth, PESS was assessed as a semi-quantitative percentage of contralateral strength by a single examiner, without formal inter- or intra-rater reliability testing. While the use of a single experienced specialist reduced variability, the reproducibility of this measure across different examiners remains unestablished and should be addressed in future studies.

Finally, while our nomogram provides a quantitative estimate of FTRCT risk, decisions regarding MRI referral and surgery should remain individualized, taking into account patient symptoms, functional demands, age, activity level, tear chronicity, and patient preferences. The model is intended to support, not replace, clinical judgment.

Conclusion

We developed and internally validated a nomogram that accurately predicts the probability of FTRCT in patients with RCI using simple, readily available clinical information. This tool may assist clinicians in identifying high-risk patients who should be prioritized for MRI, while supporting initial conservative management in low-risk patients, thereby facilitating more efficient and cost-effective use of MRI resources. External validation is required before widespread clinical adoption.
