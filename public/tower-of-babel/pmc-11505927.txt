Endovascular treatment for aggressive vertebrobasilar stenosis due to giant cell arteritis: illustrative cases
Authors: Ryosuke Mizuta, Shoji Saito, Keita Kawabe, Hiroki Seto, Tomoaki Suzuki, Masakazu Sano, Hitoshi Hasegawa, Makoto Oishi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDGiant cell arteritis (GCA) infrequently presents with progressive symptomatic vertebrobasilar stenosis. Vertebrobasilar GCA is often refractory to medical treatments and can lead to short-term ischemic stroke recurrence, which is associated with a poor prognosis. Endovascular treatment (EVT) is a therapeutic option; however, the optimal timing and indications for its application remain unclear.OBSERVATIONSThis study reports two patients with vertebrobasilar GCA who exhibited repeated ischemic strokes in the vertebrobasilar territory, along with progressive severe stenosis and occlusion of the bilateral vertebral arteries, despite receiving medical therapy. They were successfully treated with balloon angioplasty, and there were no subsequent occurrences of stroke or restenosis.A review was conducted of six cases of vertebrobasilar GCA treated with EVT. All patients had bilateral lesions and experienced recurrent strokes within 30 days. Angiography suggested ischemic complications in vertebrobasilar GCA resulting from hemodynamic ischemia caused by stenosis rather than intradural vasculitis. The improved blood flow through EVT alleviated patient symptoms and prevented recurrent strokes.LESSONSSome patients with vertebrobasilar GCA exhibit rapid stenosis progression and repeated hemodynamic ischemia despite medical therapy. EVT is a potential strategy for treating medically refractory vertebrobasilar GCA. Performing EVT prior to recurrent infarctions can lead to favorable outcomes. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE24404

Giant cell arteritis (GCA) is an autoimmune vasculitis that predominantly targets large- and medium-sized extracranial arteries and occasionally affects intracranial arteries. Ischemic stroke is a rare complication of GCA, occurring in 3%–7% of cases, mainly in the vertebrobasilar territory.1, 2 Treatments including corticosteroids and antiplatelet therapies are often ineffective, leading to a poor prognosis with a mortality rate of 28%–36%.1, 2 Bilateral stenosis or occlusion of the vertebral artery (VA) is associated with a mortality rate of 75%.3 While endovascular treatment (EVT) is a therapeutic option, its optimal timing and specific indications remain unclear.4 In this study, we present two cases of progressive symptomatic vertebrobasilar GCA resistant to medication that were successfully managed with EVT. Additionally, we reviewed six similar cases documented in the literature.

Illustrative Cases

Case 1

A 71-year-old male with a history of hypertension, dyslipidemia, type 2 diabetes, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, and polymyalgia rheumatica presented with sudden dizziness and dysarthria. Neurological examination showed right ptosis, limb dysmetria, and truncal ataxia. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed bilateral cerebellar and pons infarcts, right VA occlusion, and left VA severe stenosis (Fig. 1A and B). Laboratory test results showed low inflammation with a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 0.31 mg/dL. Antithrombotic therapy with aspirin 100 mg/day, clopidogrel 75 mg/day, and argatroban infusion was started immediately. Despite prompt initiation of antithrombotic therapy, the patient experienced recurrent vertebrobasilar insufficiency, resulting in worsening symptoms and a decrease in consciousness on day 22. Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) revealed recurrent cerebellar and pons infarcts and progression of left VA stenosis (Fig. 1C). Arterial spin labeling indicated low blood flow in the vertebrobasilar territory (Fig. 1D). Emergency angiography revealed severe stenosis (warfarin-aspirin symptomatic intracranial disease [WASID] 77%) in the left V3–4 segment, with delayed antegrade contrast filling and right VA occlusion (Fig. 2A). EVT was performed on day 23 with the patient under general anesthesia. A 6-Fr Envoy guide catheter (Johnson & Johnson) was positioned at the origin of the left VA. We inflated the Gateway percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) balloon catheter (2.0 mm × 9 mm, Boston Scientific Corp.) six times along the stenotic segment, proceeding from proximal to distal. The balloon was inflated at a nominal pressure of 6 atm for 30 seconds. Balloon angioplasty resulted in the improvement of stenosis (WASID 35%) and contrast filling with no complications or recoil (Fig. 2B, Video 1). The patient’s symptoms improved. Postprocedure, a temporal artery biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of GCA. We continued dual antiplatelet therapy and initiated methylprednisolone pulse therapy followed by oral prednisone. After tapering corticosteroids, the patient was transferred to a rehabilitation hospital on day 85 with truncal ataxia and a modified Rankin Scale score of 3. Case 2

An 86-year-old female with a history of polymyalgia rheumatica, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and a lacunar infarct presented with sudden dizziness and vomiting. The patient had been prescribed prednisolone 2 mg/day and aspirin 100 mg/day for the past 3 months. Physical examination showed truncal ataxia and superficial temporal artery hardening. MRI revealed bilateral cerebellar and left occipital lobe infarcts, right VA occlusion, and severe left VA stenosis, which had not been observed on MRI 3 months earlier (Fig. 3A–C). Laboratory test results showed low inflammation with an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 46 mm/hr and CRP of 0.57 mg/dL. Given the history of polymyalgia rheumatica, temporal artery hardening, and rapid progression of VA stenosis, VA vasculitis associated with GCA was suspected. Antithrombotic therapy with aspirin 100 mg/day, clopidogrel 75 mg/day, and argatroban infusion was started immediately. On day 2, a temporal artery biopsy was conducted to confirm GCA, and methylprednisolone pulse therapy was initiated. Despite early diagnosis and prompt initiation of methylprednisolone pulse therapy, the patient experienced recurrent vertebrobasilar insufficiency. MRI revealed gadolinium enhancement of the vertebrobasilar territory and progression of left VA stenosis (Fig. 3D). EVT was performed on day 23 with the patient under general anesthesia. Angiography revealed right VA occlusion and left V3–4 junction severe stenosis (WASID 86%), with delayed antegrade contrast filling (Fig. 4A). A 4-Fr ASAHI FUBUKI Dilator Kit (ASAHI INTECC CO., LTD.) was positioned at the origin of the left VA. Balloon angioplasty was performed twice using the Gateway PTA balloon catheter, sized 1.5 mm × 9 mm and 2.5 mm × 12 mm (Boston Scientific Corp). The balloon was inflated at a nominal pressure of 6 atm for 30 seconds. Balloon angioplasty resulted in the improvement of stenosis (WASID 55%) and contrast filling with no complications or recoil (Fig. 4B, Video 1). The patient's symptoms markedly improved, leading to discharge on day 34 with no neurological deficit and a modified Rankin Scale score of 1. The patient continued oral prednisone. Discussion

Observations

These two cases of vertebrobasilar GCA demonstrated rapid, progressive stenosis in the V3–4 region and repeated hemodynamic ischemia despite low inflammation levels and medical therapy. Both cases were successfully treated with balloon angioplasty.

GCA rarely causes severe intracranial artery stenosis, with 73%–84% of cases affecting the vertebrobasilar territory.1, 2 Typical clinical GCA symptoms, including headache and elevated inflammatory markers, are often absent in these patients, making the differentiation between vertebrobasilar GCA and atherosclerosis a diagnostic challenge.1, 2 Beuker et al. described a distinctive pattern of VA stenosis where stenosis occurred at V3–4 proximal to the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) in GCA, contrasting with V4 distal to the PICA in atherosclerosis.2 This pattern aligns with the findings in our two cases.

Vessel wall imaging with contrast-enhanced MRI is useful in diagnosing vertebrobasilar GCA.4 However, current evidence regarding the role of vessel wall imaging in predicting outcomes, as well as in monitoring disease activity and damage in GCA, is limited. Although a treatment response of vessel wall alterations was observed, neither baseline imaging findings nor those during follow-up were clearly associated with a disease relapse or other outcomes.4 In case 2, vessel wall enhancement extended from the VA to the basilar artery, yet stenosis was confined to a portion of the VA.

High-dose glucocorticoids and adjunctive immunosuppressive agents are standard treatments for GCA and should be promptly initiated.5 However, the conditions of some patients with intracranial GCA are refractory to these therapies. In our case 2, despite prompt steroid pulse therapy, contrast enhancement persisted in the affected artery, and stenosis progressed. In two retrospective studies, 28%–38% of patients with intracranial GCA experienced short-term recurrent ischemic strokes despite medical therapy.1, 2 Beuker et al. identified that the expression of interleukin-6 and interleukin-17 in the vessel wall was significantly increased in patients with a rapid progressive course.2

The 2021 American College of Rheumatology guidelines conditionally recommend antiplatelet therapy for patients with flow-limiting vertebral or carotid artery involvement.5The guidelines recommend surgical intervention for patients exhibiting progressive infarction.5 However, the optimal timing and indications for this intervention are unclear. These patients presented with stenosis in the V3–4 proximal to the PICA, and seven had lesions in both VAs. Prior to EVT, all patients received glucocorticoids or antiplatelet therapy, and two received additional immunosuppressants. Despite these treatments, they had rapid progressive stenosis and recurrent strokes within 30 days. Balloon angioplasty was performed in all cases, with stent placement performed in two cases. One case reported procedure-related arterial dissection with no new neurological deficits.11 One patient died due to extensive infarction of the cerebellum and brainstem.7

Angiographic assessment indicated slow flow in the vertebrobasilar territory, suggesting that ischemic complications resulted from hemodynamic ischemia due to stenosis rather than intradural vasculitis. Improved blood flow alleviated the patient's symptoms and prevented recurrent strokes.

The safety and efficacy of EVT for inflamed blood vessels have not been thoroughly investigated. While the occurrence of treatment-related complications was minimal in this study, performing PTA on inflamed vessels carries the risk of dissection; therefore, the balloon inflation pressure and frequency should be minimized. For the same reason, stent placement should be avoided if possible, but it should be considered in cases of recoil, dissection, or restenosis. Previous reports have documented cases that require multiple PTAs due to restenosis, necessitating short-term imaging follow-up.11, 12

The outcomes of EVT in this study demonstrated a more favorable prognosis than in previous reports on medical therapy.1–3 Recurrent infarctions in the vertebrobasilar region result in serious sequelae. In cases with bilateral lesions, EVT prior to the development of recurrent infarctions can lead to favorable outcomes, as observed for the patients in this study.

Lessons

Patients with vertebrobasilar GCA can experience short-term repeated ischemic strokes due to rapid stenosis progression despite medical therapy. Bilateral VA stenosis can lead to severe hemodynamic infarctions in the vertebrobasilar region, leading to poor outcomes. EVT prior to the development of recurrent infarctions can improve patient prognosis. For patients experiencing recurrent ischemic events, physicians should consider EVT as part of the therapeutic approach.
