Developmental timing of auditory deprivation influences spatial memory and hippocampal plasticity in rats
Authors: Marjan Mirsalehi, Alimohamad Asghari, Masoud Nasiri Nasab Rafsanjani, Akram Pourbakht, Maryam Balali, Sayedali Ahmadi, Zeinab Akbarnejad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAge-dependent sensory deprivation may differentially affect hippocampal integrity, making it essential to investigate how the timing of exposure influences neurodevelopmental outcomes. This study aimed to examine how peripheral auditory deprivation at various ages impacts cognitive functions in rats. Transcanal cochlear ablation was performed at postnatal days (PND) 14, 21, 28, and 60. At four months of age, spatial learning, memory, neurogenesis, and molecular markers associated with synaptic plasticity were evaluated. Compared to controls, all experimental groups—except those ablated at PND 14—exhibited spatial memory deficits, with the most pronounced impairment observed in animals ablated at PND 28 and 60. Correspondingly, hippocampal neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity were significantly reduced in these groups. These changes were age-dependent, with early-life auditory sensory deprivation causing less significant impairment in adulthood. Our study demonstrated an age-dependent effects of auditory deprivation on hippocampal function. The more pronounced reduction in hippocampal function developed in hearing-deprived animals at older ages compared to animals with early-life cochlear ablation. This highlights the importance of age in understanding the cognitive effects of auditory deprivation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26704-8.Keywords: Cochlear ablation, Hearing loss, Spatial learning and memory, Synaptic plasticity, Neurogenesis, Developmental ageSubject terms: Neuroscience, Auditory system, Cognitive neuroscience

Introduction

Hearing loss (HL) has been associated with a wide range of health outcomes, including dementia, depression, social isolation, and cognitive decline1. There is considerable evidence that HL is a potential risk factor for cognitive impairment and synaptic loss in the hippocampus2–5. The hippocampus, a key area of the limbic system, is crucial for spatial memory, a type of memory essential for learning, remembering, and navigating specific locations. Multiple studies have demonstrated that the hippocampus is crucial in processing auditory signals6. The integration of sensory processing, which involves visual, olfactory, and auditory information, is fundamental to learning, memory, and cognition7–9. However, the exact mechanisms linking HL to hippocampal dysfunction remain unclear.

Potential mechanisms may include impaired hippocampal neurogenesis, altered synaptic plasticity, disrupted multisensory integration, activation of stress-related pathways, and neuroinflammatory responses10,11. It is crucial to investigate the brain’s functional adaptations to the initial loss of auditory input to elucidate the causal mechanisms linking HL and cognitive decline. Auditory sensory deprivation refers to the experimental reduction or elimination of auditory input, while cochlear ablation is defined as a surgical procedure that destroy the cochlea to abolish auditory signaling12.

Various forms of HL, such as conductive hearing loss, hair cell ablation, noise-induced hearing loss, and age-related hearing loss, have been shown to negatively affect non-auditory parts of the brain, including the hippocampus13–17. This highlights the significant link between HL and impaired hippocampal function, which can lead to subsequent cognitive and memory decline. However, in most related studies, HL was induced in adulthood.

The hippocampus is a critical brain region involved in spatial learning and memory tasks18. It can employ auditory information to form spatial learning and memory8. Several studies have evaluated the effect of HL on spatial learning and memory using the Morris water maze (MWM) test in rodents, with most focusing on noise-induced hearing loss and reporting impaired spatial learning and memory15,16,19,20. To our knowledge, no study has investigated the impact of permanent hearing loss on adult cognition across different developmental stages, from infancy to young adulthood. Studying the effects of HL at different developmental ages is particularly important because the brain undergoes dynamic developmental stages during which sensory input strongly influences hippocampal circuit formation and cognitive outcomes. Identifying how auditory deprivation at distinct stages—from infancy to young adulthood—affects hippocampal neurogenesis and memory may provide valuable insights into age-dependent vulnerabilities. Through illuminating how hearing deprivation affects the brain at the different developmental stages, this research can help design and time clinical interventions for childhood hearing loss, contributing to a broader understanding of the brain’s developmental plasticity.

We hypothesized that auditory deprivation at later developmental stages would result in more pronounced impairments in spatial memory and hippocampal function compared to early-life deprivation. The current study, with its potential to significantly advance our understanding of the effects across different ages of auditory deprivation on cognition, aimed to evaluate the effects of auditory sensory deprivation induced by cochlear ablation at different developmental ages, including early life at postnatal day (PND14), the juvenile stage (PND21), pre-adolescence (PND28) and young adult-hood (PND60), on spatial learning and memory, hippocampal neurogenesis, and hippocampal synaptic plasticity at four months of age.

Results

Auditory brainstem response (ABR) recordings and establishment of hearing deprivation model

Baseline ABR recordings were performed in the cochlear ablation (CA) and control groups before cochlear ablation. The results showed that the baseline hearing thresholds of the CA-14 group were in the range of 30 to 50 dB sound pressure level (SPL), a normal range for their age. As ABRs become apparent in neonatal rats between PND 12 and PND 14 and reach adult levels by PND 2221, the baseline hearing thresholds in the CA-14 group were, therefore, within the expected range. Baseline hearing thresholds in the other groups ranged between 10- and 20-dB SPL, which is considered normal in rats (Fig. S1). Immediately after cochlear ablation, hearing thresholds were not detectable in all experimental groups, even with stimuli as high as 90 dB intensity. If the ABR was not discernible with the maximum output of the system (90 dB SPL), the ABR threshold was considered 90 dB. The hearing thresholds remained unchanged in the control group. At 4 months of age, ABR thresholds were undetectable at 90 dB in all experimental groups (CA-14, CA-21, CA-28, and CA-60). These values confirmed the establishment of the auditory deprivation model and its stability over time (Fig. S1). The ABR thresholds remained 10 to 20 dB SPL in the control group (Fig. S1). To rule out the role of vestibular dysfunction as a confounding factor in spatial learning and memory22, behavioral tests were performed to evaluate vestibular function. Animals exhibiting any signs of vestibular dysfunction were excluded from the study.

Spatial learning and memory assessments using the Morris water maze

During the acquisition phase, traveled distance and escape latency were assessed as indicators of spatial learning. Compared with the control group, the CA-21, CA-28, and CA-60 groups exhibited significantly greater traveled distances and escape latencies (all P < 0.05; Fig. 1A–D), whereas the CA-14 group did not differ from control (P > 0.05). Post-hoc analysis (Tukey’s test) further revealed that CA-14 and CA-21 rats performed better than CA-60 rats, showing shorter traveled distances and escape latencies (P < 0.001). No significant differences were detected between CA-28 and CA-60 groups (P > 0.05).

Two-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of both Time and Group on behavioral performance. Specifically, the main effect of Time was significant for traveled distance (F (3, 105) = 63.86, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.65) and escape latency (F (3, 105) = 101.50, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.74), indicating that all groups improved across training days. The main effect of Group was also significant for traveled distance (F (4, 35) = 18.84, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.68) and escape latency (F (4, 35) = 19.49, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.69), confirming overall differences among experimental groups. Moreover, a significant Group × Time interaction was observed for both traveled distance (F (12, 105) = 2.71, p = 0.003, partial η2 = 0.23) and escape latency (F (12, 105) = 4.38, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.33), suggesting that the rate and pattern of improvement varied depending on the age at which cochlear ablation was performed.

The probe test was performed on the fifth day after the acquisition phase. The average time spent in the target quadrant was analyzed as an index of spatial memory. A univariate ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of group on probe performance, F (4, 35) = 37.91, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.81, indicating a large effect size. Multiple comparison analysis revealed that rats with cochlear ablation at days 21, 28, and 60 spent significantly less time in the target quadrant than control (P < 0.001; Fig. 1E). Post-hoc tests further indicated that CA-60 animals showed the greatest memory impairment, spending less time in the target quadrant than CA-14 and CA-21 rats (P < 0.001), while CA-21 and CA-28 groups did not differ significantly.

The comparison of mean swimming speeds between the experimental and control groups showed no statistically significant differences, F (4, 15) = 0.842, p = 0.520, partial η2 = 0.183, indicating no significant motor function differences between animals (Fig. 1F).

Western blot analysis

Statistical analysis revealed a significant main effect of group on BDNF expression (F (4, 10) = 22.98, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.90). Compared with control, BDNF levels were significantly reduced in the CA-21 (p = 0.012), CA-28 (p = 0.001), and CA-60 (p < 0.001) groups, whereas no difference was observed in CA-14 (p = 0.694). Discussion

The current study showed that auditory sensory deprivation induced by cochlear ablation impaired spatial learning and memory, as evaluated by the MWM test. The severity of the impairment varied depending on the developmental stage at which deprivation occurred. Specifically, rats that underwent cochlear ablation at PND 28 and PND 60 (CA-28, CA-60 groups) exhibited more pronounced deficits in spatial learning and memory compared with those ablated at PND 14 and PND 21 (CA-14, CA-21 groups). These findings suggest that the impact of auditory deprivation on spatial learning and memory is age-dependent, with greater impairments observed when HL occurs at later developmental stages.Previous studies have applied several methods to establish auditory sensory deprivation in vivo, including prolonged noise exposure, administration of ototoxic agents (e.g., aminoglycosides and cisplatin), and surgical cochlear ablation. Noise exposure can model progressive, frequency-specific hearing loss; however, the extent of cochlear damage varies depending on exposure parameters and species-specific susceptibility, and may partially recover23,24.Ototoxic pharmaceuticals have the capacity to target selectively the cochlear hair cells and the spiral ganglion neurons; nevertheless, the efficacy of these drugs is influenced by systemic metabolism, the permeability of the blood-labyrinth barrier, and potential off-target toxicity 25,26. In contrast, cochlear ablation produces an immediate, complete, and irreversible cessation of peripheral auditory input27. This enables precise control over the timing of deprivation and reduces variability between subjects28. In the current study, bilateral cochlear ablation was chosen to ensure definitive elimination of peripheral auditory activity on a pre-determined experimental day. ABR confirmed complete auditory deprivation after surgery. To eliminate the possibility of vestibular dysfunction influencing spatial learning and memory performance22, we evaluated vestibular function using a semi-quantitative behavioral test battery comprising postural asymmetry scoring, head roll tilt observation, and the tail elevation reflex test–a validated, objective method for detecting vestibular deficits in rodents29. This methodological choice enabled us to isolate the effects of loss of auditory input from other sensory or motor influences, thereby strengthening the interpretability of our findings.

Recent human neuroimaging studies have demonstrated that HL is associated with structural brain changes, particularly in the hippocampus. Individuals with HL tend to exhibit reduced hippocampal volumes30, and more severe HL has been linked to greater degrees of hippocampal atrophy31. These findings suggest that hearing deprivation may affect hippocampal structure as well as function, providing further foundation for interpreting behavioral results in animal models of HL. Given the known anatomical and functional connections between the auditory cortex and the hippocampus, cortical changes may contribute to the observed hippocampal deficits. While our study did not directly assess the auditory cortex, previous research has shown its connection to the hippocampus13.

Guo et al.32 studied the link between HL and cognitive function in mice. They found that mice with HL had impaired social memory but no significant differences in other cognitive tests32. This supports our research indicating minimal effects on spatial learning and memory in adulthood when auditory input is removed early in life. Another study by Qian et al. provides evidence of cochlear hair cell ablation effects on spatial learning and memory. Qian et al. investigated sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) induced by diphtheria toxin injection at postnatal day 30 (PND30) or 60 (PND60) and assessed spatial learning and memory using a radial 8-arm maze at PND90–120. Their results showed that mice with SNHL exhibited more working memory errors and reference memory errors than wild-type controls, confirming impaired cognitive function. The severity of impairment persisted beyond two months post-auditory insult, with older-onset hearing loss (P60) associated with more pronounced spatial memory deficits compared to younger-onset SNHL (PND30), suggesting the age at which HL occurs influences the severity of central deficits14.

The development and organization of the auditory cortex depend heavily on stimulus-driven learning and have limited developmental windows, referred to as “sensitive periods,” during which the capacity for plasticity is maximal33. In rodent models, these windows coincide with early postnatal stages, such as PND14, when auditory pathways and auditory-hippocampal connections are still maturing and synaptic plasticity is high. At this stage reduction or elimination of auditory input can be partially compensated for by other sensory modalities, such as visual or olfactory input. with aging (e.g., from PND28 to PND60), these pathways become stabilized. Sudden deprivation of auditory input leads to reduced sensory stimulation of the hippocampus, disruption of place cell activity, and reduced synaptic plasticity. Behavioral evidence suggests that auditory deprivation at a younger age is associated with adaptive reorganization and partial recovery of function. By contrast, deprivation at a later age leads to permanent impairments in functions34. These findings indicate that the closure of the sensitive period is accompanied by reduced flexibility and limited compensatory capacity. The results demonstrated that age plays a crucial role in the effects of auditory input deprivation. Deprivation at an older age (PND60) exerts a more pronounced impairment of spatial learning and memory than deprivation at a younger age (PND14). This difference can be attributable to the greater plasticity present at earlier developmental stages, which may facilitate compensatory mechanisms for reduced auditory sensory input. Synaptic plasticity is a multifaceted process involving numerous molecular and functional components. In the present study, we focused on synaptophysin, PSD-95, and BDNF because of their well-established and complementary roles in hippocampal synaptic architecture. Synaptophysin is a presynaptic vesicle membrane protein essential for neurotransmitter release and synaptic vesicle cycling, serving as a reliable marker of presynaptic integrity35. PSD-95 (postsynaptic density protein 95) is a major scaffolding protein that anchors NMDA and AMPA receptors at excitatory synapses, thereby stabilizing postsynaptic signaling complexes and regulating synaptic strength36,37. BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) is a neurotrophin critical for neuronal survival, dendritic growth, and activity-dependent synaptic modulation, and is a key mediator of experience-dependent plasticity38,39. By assessing these markers, we captured both pre- and postsynaptic elements as well as neurotrophin-mediated signaling, providing a mechanistic framework for interpreting the observed age-dependent behavioral deficits. In a previous experimental model, temporary conductive hearing loss (TCHL) induced at PND 14 and assessed at PND 42 was associated with pronounced deficits in hippocampal long-term potentiation and attenuated NMDA receptor–mediated synaptic transmission13.The observed discrepancies between their findings and those presented in this study can be due to the nature and extent of HL as in the present study, ablation of the cochlea on PND 14 resulted in complete and irreversible sensorineural hearing loss, causing immediate and complete elimination of auditory input. In contrast, the study by Zhao et al. performed tympanic membrane perforation, which causes partial conductive hearing loss. Another possible explanation is that incomplete hearing loss may result in distorted auditory input, which can be more damaging to neural development than complete silence40.Thus, the brain may struggle to interpret the distorted signals, resulting in maladaptive plasticity. Distorted auditory input during development can lead to maladaptive neural reorganization, even more than complete deprivation. Duration of auditory deprivation may also have been influential; previous research has shown that auditory deprivation, can induce molecular and cellular changes in the hippocampus within 7 to 14 days. These changes include altered expression of genes associated with neuroplasticity (e.g., BDNF, Arc), changes in synaptic receptor composition (AMPA/NMDA ratios), and increased microglial activation and oxidative stress. However, structural remodeling in the hippocampus—characterized by changes in dendritic architecture, volume reduction, and persistent impairments in neurogenesis—usually appears over a period of one to three months10,12. In the study performed by Zhao et al., TCHL effects were assessed at PND 42 following 28 days of auditory deprivation. In contrast, in the present study, outcomes were evaluated at four months of age, after a more extended deprivation period of 2 to 3.5 months (depending on the experimental groups), indicating a much longer period of deprivation. Therefore, in our study, the extended duration of auditory deprivation likely provided a broader window for structural remodeling and compensatory neuroplasticity.

Beckmann et al.11 investigated the effects of gradual sensory hearing loss on synaptic plasticity in the hippocampus. They used the C57BL/6 mice model, which exhibits progressive, hereditary hearing loss beginning in early adulthood, to investigate how cumulative deafness affects the function of the hippocampus. Immunohistochemistry and electrophysiology evaluations were conducted to determine alterations in synaptic plasticity and the expression levels of associated receptors in two- and four-month-old mice. They found that four-month-old mice exhibited more significant synaptic plasticity deterioration compared to two-month-old mice, resulting in severe memory and learning deficits. Their results also showed profound changes in hippocampal synaptic plasticity, spatial memory, and neuroreceptor expression, including alterations in NMDA receptor subunit composition (increased GluN2A and decreased GluN2B levels), and changes in metabotropic glutamate receptors. These results suggest that progressive age-related hearing loss leads to significant spatial memory impairment in later life, possibly mediated by receptor-level changes and disrupted synaptic plasticity within the hippocampus11. Studies have shown that the mammalian brain exhibits remarkable adaptability to sensory deprivation41. After sensory loss, cortical and subcortical reorganization initially impairs cognitive processes42. On the other hand, studies have shown that cognitive recovery can occur after prolonged vision loss43,44. This raises the possibility that a similar recovery may occur over a prolonged period after HL.

Additionally, research indicates that decreased auditory stimulation can lead to a decline in neurogenesis in the hippocampus. Kurioka et al.12 examined how unilateral or bilateral conductive hearing loss, induced by occluding the external ear canal, affects hippocampal neurogenesis in a mouse model. They found that the number of doublecortin-positive (DCX) and Ki-67-positive cells in the hippocampus decreased significantly in mice with unilateral or bilateral hearing loss12. Neurogenesis in the hippocampus plays a crucial role in spatial learning and memory. In fact, several studies have shown that reduced adult hippocampal neurogenesis negatively affects the spatial learning and memory abilities of rodents45–48. The results of the current study revealed that decreased neurogenesis is associated with significant impairments in spatial learning and memory. Additionally, the results showed that eliminating auditory input at an early age had a minimal effect on spatial learning and memory. This can be attributed to neural networks in the brain, especially during the critical early developmental period and at younger ages, that exhibit a high degree of neuroplasticity, a fascinating aspect of brain function that allows it to reorganize itself with age. The hippocampus of younger rats seems to be less dependent on auditory perception and, thus, more easily adapts to HL. In addition, the hippocampus of younger rats may inherently have greater neuroplasticity than that of adult rats, increasing their ability to adapt to reduced sensory input. A combination of these factors may be responsible for the differences observed. Further investigations and studies are needed in this area.

The current study has some limitations. First, although the primary aim of this study was to investigate the age-dependent effects of peripheral auditory deprivation on spatial learning and memory in adult rats, the underlying biological mechanisms were not examined in detail. In particular, we did not assess changes in the auditory cortex that might contribute to hippocampal changes following HL. This limits our ability to determine whether cortical reorganization mediates the observed cognitive deficits. Second, molecular analyses were limited to hippocampal markers of neurogenesis and plasticity. A broader investigation including synaptic and neurotransmitter-related molecules—such as NMDA receptor subunits, GABAergic components, and metabotropic glutamate receptors—could provide deeper insight into the cellular basis of cognitive impairment. Another limitation of the present study is that only male rats were used. This choice was made because spatial learning and memory are strongly influenced by sex hormones and estrous cycle–related variability, and restricting the study to males could reduce hormonal confounding. Nevertheless, this limits the generalizability of the findings across sexes.

In conclusion, peripheral auditory deprivation induced by transcanal cochlear ablation impaired spatial learning and memory, disrupted hippocampal neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity in rats, and had effects that were strongly dependent on the developmental stage. Animals deprived at later stages showed the most pronounced spatial memory deficits and concomitant reductions in hippocampal plasticity, whereas deprivation early in life resulted in minimal long-term impairment. These findings reveal the mechanistic link between auditory input and hippocampal function, showing that the timing of auditory deprivation critically shapes cognitive outcomes. Further research is needed to clarify the role of cortical changes, unravel the molecular mechanisms underlying developmental stage–specific sensitivity, and evaluate potential interventions to mitigate the long-term cognitive consequences of auditory deprivation.

Materials and methods

Animals and experimental groups

Healthy pregnant albino Wistar rats were obtained from the Royan Institute and monitored daily for delivery. A total of 14 pregnant rats were used, yielding 148 pups. The day of birth was designated as PND0. The pups were weaned at PND21, and every four male pups were placed in separate cages and housed under standard conditions: 12-h light/dark cycles and controlled temperatures (22–24 °C), with access to food and water ad libitum. To minimize litter effects, pups were randomly assigned to five experimental groups using a litter-balanced protocol, ensuring that each group included animals from multiple litters and avoided overrepresentation from any single litter. The rats were randomly divided into five groups: the control group received no intervention. In contrast, the CA-14 group underwent cochlear ablation on PND14, and the CA-21, CA-28, and CA-60 groups underwent cochlear ablation on PND21, PND28, and PND60, respectively (Fig. 5). To minimize potential bias, behavioral evaluations and data processing were conducted by researchers who were unaware of the group assignments. Identification codes were used to mask group identity throughout the assessment and analysis stages. All rats were euthanized with an overdose of pentobarbital sodium (100–150 mg/kg, intraperitoneally), at the end of the fourth month of life, following established veterinary guidelines to minimize pain and distress. After confirming deep anesthesia, decapitation was performed, and brain tissue samples were collected for subsequent analyses. Before this, the rats underwent several procedures, including auditory evaluations, behavioral assessments, and tissue sample collection for Western blot and immunofluorescent analyses. Only male rats were used in this study to minimize hormonal confounding. Spatial learning and memory, as well as hippocampal neurogenesis, are known to be influenced by sex hormones and estrous cycle–related variability in females, which can increase data variance. Therefore, restricting the study to males allowed us to reduce hormonal variability for the present mechanistic investigation49–52. This animal study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Iran University of Medical Sciences for the use and care of laboratory animals (approval ID: IR.IUMS.FMD.REC.1399.875). The study protocol strictly adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines and complied with all relevant national laws and institutional regulations governing animal welfare and ethical research practices.

Hearing assessment with ABR

Hearing thresholds were assessed by recording the ABR in response to click stimuli using the “Audiology Lab” (Otoconsult, Frankfurt am Main, Germany) system. The ABR recordings were performed in three stages: before the cochlear ablation (to confirm the normal hearing status), immediately after the intervention (to confirm the establishment of HL), and at four months of age (to show the persistence of HL). The animals were anesthetized with ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) intraperitoneally and placed on a heating pad in a soundproof chamber for ABR recordings. The subdermal needle electrodes were positioned at the vertex (reference electrode), behind the pinna (active electrode), and behind the pinna of the contralateral ear (ground electrode). The click stimuli (3 ms duration) were presented to the external auditory canal via a calibrated loudspeaker (DT48, BeyerDynamics, Heilbronn, Germany) connected to a plastic cone. The responses were averaged from 128 presentations, with sound levels ranging from 90 to 10 dB SPL in 10 dB steps. The recordings were analyzed using a custom-written algorithm in the MATLAB environment (The MathWorks Inc., USA). The lowest stimulus intensity that evoked a detectable wave II was defined as the hearing threshold.

Cochlear ablation

Auditory deprivation was induced by bilateral cochlear ablation at PND 14, 21, 28, and 60 in groups CA-14, CA-21, CA-28, and CA-60, respectively. Cochlear ablation was performed using a transcanal approach described by Mostafapour et al.53 with some modifications. Briefly, after anesthesia, the tympanic membrane was visualized and removed (Fig. 5A, B). The malleus and incus were also removed (Fig. 5C). The basal turn of the cochlea was identified and penetrated using a 23-gauge needle. The cochlear contents were then aspirated, and the modiolus was destroyed (Fig. 5D). Postoperative analgesia was provided using meloxicam (1–2 mg/kg, subcutaneously), administered once daily for two consecutive days in accordance with veterinary guidelines to minimize pain and distress. The establishment of the model was confirmed by the ABR test immediately after the procedure. Rats were returned to their cages after two hours.

Behavioral tests for vestibular function evaluation

To exclude vestibular trauma during cochlear ablation, behavioral vestibular tests were performed one day after surgery and one day before the MWM test, as previously described54–56. In brief, the behavioral tests for vestibular deficits consisted of three components: postural asymmetry, head roll tilt, and the tail elevation test. Each component had a maximum score of 10 (Table. S1). Animals were excluded from the study if they exhibited any deficits during vestibular function screening; as a result, four animals were removed from the experimental groups.

Behavioral tests for spatial learning and memory

MWM was performed to evaluate the spatial learning and memory of the rats as previously described57,58. The MWM is a black-walled, circular pool filled with water at 22 ± 2 °C. Special cues in each quadrant surrounded the pool. A transparent platform was placed 1 cm below the water surface in one quadrant. The acquisition phase consisted of one block of four trials, each day for four consecutive days. In each trial, the rat was randomly placed in one of the four quadrants to swim and find the platform. If the animal could not find the platform in 90 s, it was guided to the platform. The trial was repeated after 30 s of rest. The probe test was conducted on the fifth day: the platform was removed, and the rat was allowed to swim for 60 s. A video camera was placed above the pool to record the animal’s behavior. The distance traveled to find the platform (traveled distance), the time spent finding the platform (escape latency), the swimming speed, and the time spent in the target quadrant (for probe tests) were calculated by the EthoVision tracking system (Noldus Information Technology, Wageningen, the Netherlands).

Western blot analysis

Western blot analyses were conducted with modifications to the methods used in previous studies59. For this purpose, protein samples were extracted from the entire right hippocampus, which has been predominantly implicated in spatial learning and memory functions60. This homogenized tissue (including DG, CA1, and CA3) was analyzed as a commonly applied approach in studies of synaptic plasticity61. The hippocampal tissues were harvested and homogenized in a cold buffer and then centrifuged (14,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C). The supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration was determined using the Bradford protein assay62. The proteins (15 µg per lane of each sample) were loaded on a 12.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide (SDS-PAGE) gel, electrophoresed, and then transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membranes were blocked using bovine serum albumin (BSA) in 0.1% Tween 20 for 1 h. Then, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4ºC with specific primary antibodies: rabbit anti- BDNF antibody (orb10181, Biorbyt, Wuhan, China, 1:300), rabbit Anti-PSD95 antibody (orb500730, Biorbyt, Wuhan, China, 1:1000), rabbit anti-Synaptophysin antibody (orb704356, Biorbyt, Wuhan, China, 1:600), and rabbit anti-beta Actin antibody (GTX109639, Genetex, USA, 1:1000). Following this, the membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with secondary antibodies: goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (BA1054, Boster, Wuhan, China,1:10,000). Next, the membranes were incubated with Enhanced Chemiluminescence (ECL) for 1–2 min. Densitometry of protein bands was performed using Gel Analyzer software (version 2010a, NIH, USA). The protein expression was normalized to β-actin. Blots were visualized using chemiluminescence and X-ray film. The physical edges of the PVDF membranes were not visible because X-ray film only records the chemiluminescent signal. The membranes were kept intact and not cut before antibody incubation. All of the original blot images for each replicate are in the Fig. S2 Information.

Immunofluorescence staining

Tissue samples used for immunofluorescence analysis were selectively obtained from the dentate gyrus (DG) of the right hippocampus, since this subregion is recognized as a well‑established site of adult hippocampal neurogenesis63. No staining was performed in CA1 or CA3.

Following dissection, the samples were fixed in a 10% formalin solution and embedded in paraffin after tissue processing. Tissues were sectioned at 5 µm. Paraffin-embedded brain sections were deparaffinized in xylene and dehydrated in graded ethanol. In a microwave, they were placed in 1X TBST solution (5912-T, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). After reaching the boiling point, the microwave was turned off, and the samples remained in the solution for 20 min. The samples were washed with PBS (4417P, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) three times, with 5-min intervals. Subsequently, 0.3% Triton (T8787, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added for a period of 30 min to permeabilize the cell membranes. The samples were rewashed with PBS, and 10% goat serum (G9023, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added for 45 min to block nonspecific binding of the secondary antibody. The diluted primary antibody of Ki-67 (orb389335, Biorbyt, Wuhan, China, 1:100) and DCX (orb382549, Biorbyt, Wuhan, China, 1:100) in PBS was applied to the samples, which were then incubated in a humid environment at 2 to 8 °C for 24 h. After that, the tissue was washed four times with PBS, each time for 5 min. The secondary antibody (orb688925, Biorbyt, Wuhan, China, 1:150) was then added and incubated for 1 h and 30 min at 37 °C in a dark incubator (AriaTeb, Tehran, Iran). The samples were transferred from the incubator to a dark room, and after three washes, DAPI (9542 D, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added. After 20 min, the samples were washed with PBS, and a PBS solution was applied to the samples. The slides were examined under a fluorescence microscope to confirm the presence of the markers.

Statistical analysis

The means ± standard errors of means (SEM) were used to represent the data. The data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA) and IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normal distribution of the data was evaluated using normality plots (Q-Q plots) and the Shapiro–Wilk’s test. Levene’s test was used to check for homogeneity of variances. The parameters of MWM during the acquisition phase (traveled distances, escape latency, and swimming speed) were compared using a two-way analysis of variance (by considering the day of the acquisition phase as a within factor and animal group as a between factor). The one-way analysis of variance was used to compare the time spent in the target quadrant during the probe test of MWM, protein expression in Western blot analysis, and immunofluorescence expression of proteins. Tukey correction was used for post hoc multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than 0.05.
