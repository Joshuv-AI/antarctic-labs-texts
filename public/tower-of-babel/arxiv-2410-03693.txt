Linear Independence of Generalized Neurons and Related Functions
Leyang Zhang

Abstract
The linear independence of neurons plays a significant role in theoretical analysis of neural networks. Specifically, given neurons H 1 , … , H n : ℝ N × ℝ d → ℝ H_{1},...,H_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R} , we are interested in the following question: when are { H 1 ​ ( θ 1 , ⋅ ) , … , H n ​ ( θ n , ⋅ ) } \{H_{1}(\theta_{1},\cdot),...,H_{n}(\theta_{n},\cdot)\} are linearly independent as the parameters θ 1 , … , θ n \theta_{1},...,\theta_{n} of these functions vary over ℝ N \mathbb{R}^{N} . Previous works give a complete characterization of two-layer neurons without bias, for generic smooth activation functions. In this paper, we study the problem for neurons with arbitrary layers and widths, giving a simple but complete characterization for generic analytic activation functions.

Introduction

The study of linear independence of neurons has played an important part in not only the detailed analysis of shallow neural networks (NN) – e.g., two-layer networks
[
Zhang et al., 2024
,
Luo et al., 2023
]
, but also some analysis of deep NN, such as Emebdding Principle proposed by
Zhang et al. [2021]
,
Li et al. [2022]
. In this paper we study the following problem:

Given parameter space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and input space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, under what conditions of parameters
\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
are
H(\theta_{1},\cdot),...,H(\theta_{m},\cdot)
linearly dependent?

The simplest possible cases (1) are as follows:

(a)

When
H(\theta_{k},\cdot)
is constant zero for some
1\leq k\leq m
.

(b)

When
H(\theta_{k},\cdot),H(\theta_{j},\cdot)
are both constant for some distinct
k,j\in\{1,...,m\}
.

(c)

When
H(\theta_{k},\cdot)=H(\theta_{j},\cdot)
for some distinct
k,j\in\{1,...,n\}
.

For two-layer neurons of the form
\sigma(wx)
,
w,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it has been shown that for generic activations, these are the
only
cases in which they linearly dependent
[
Fukumizu and Amari, 2000
,
Simsek et al., 2021
,
Sun et al., 2020
,
Luo et al., 2023
]
. However, due to the non-linearity of activation function, it becomes challenging to analyze neurons of arbitrary width and depth. In this paper, we demonstrate that the same result for two-layer neurons holds for neurons with arbitrary width and depth, given generic activation functions.

Traditionally, the linear independence of a set of functions is often proved using their Wronskian (provided that these functions are sufficiently smooth). This is exactly the idea behind the mainstream methods for showing linear independence of two-layer neurons without bias, see e.g.
Simsek et al. [2021]
,
Ito [1996]
. Unfortunately, due to the great complexity of deep NN, this method is almost impossible to perform on neurons with more layers – in fact, it is even not obvious how it works for two-layer neurons with bias. Thus, we use alternative ways to solve this problem.

In this work we investigate the linearity of neurons in both general and specific settings. For the general case, we introduce the concept of generalized neurons and generalized neural networks (Definition
2.1
) which includes usual neurons and neural networks. For such objects, we characterize their linear independence for specific activation functions based on their growth rates (Proposition
3.2
and
3.3
). Then we parametrically deform the activation function to deduce the generality of such characterizations, with the help of analytic bump functions (Section
3.3
). By detailly studying the minimal zero set
\mathcal{Z}
(Definition
4.1
) of a neural network, we show that on any bounded subset of parameter space, for “most” analytic activations, Embedding Principle fully characterize the linear independence. For the specific case, we study two-layer neurons (with and without bias) and three-layer neurons. We identify several classes of activations, which include commonly used activations (Sigmoid, Tanh, Swich, etc.) where, again, the linear independence is fully characterized by Embedding Principle.

Notations and Assumptions

In this section we define the notations and assumptions which will be used later. Let
\mathbb{N}:=\{1,2,3,...\}
and let
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
. We consider
\mathbb{F}
-valued functions defined on
\mathbb{F}^{d}:=\Pi_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{F}
. Such a function

is called

if it is real analytic on an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or complex analytic on an open subset of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Given a multi-index
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
for some
s\in\mathbb{N}
, the
\alpha
-th derivative of
f
is denoted by
\partial^{\alpha}f
or
\partial_{z}^{\alpha}f(z)
if we want to emphasize the variable
z
; in particular, the set of partial derivatives of
f
can be written as

\{\partial^{\alpha}f:\alpha\in\cup_{s=1}^{\infty}\{1,...,d\}^{s}\}.

For a
curve
we mean a smooth function
\gamma
from an interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{F}^{N}
for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e. a smooth function
\gamma:I\to\mathbb{F}^{N}
. We also use
\gamma
to denote the image of this function. So for example we will simply write
\gamma\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{N}
instead of
\gamma(I)\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{N}
. Then, given
I
of the form
[\alpha,\beta]
or
I=[\alpha,\infty)
we define

l_{\gamma}(t):=\int_{\alpha}^{t}|\gamma^{\prime}(s)|ds

as the length function of
\gamma
. Specifically, when
I=[\alpha,\infty)
we write
l_{\gamma}(\infty)=\int_{\alpha}^{\infty}|\gamma^{\prime}(s)|ds
as the total length of
\gamma
.

Given an open set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{F}
, we write
\sigma:\Omega\to\mathbb{F}
for our activation function, unless specified explicitly. When
\Omega
is not specified,
\sigma
is defined on the whole real line, and, whenever possible, on the whole
\mathbb{C}
. For parameterized functions, we mean functions of the form
H:\Omega\times\mathbb{F}^{d}\to\mathbb{F}
, where
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{N}
is open. Any
\theta\in\Omega
is called a parameter of
H
, and any
z\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
the input of
H
. Two class of parameterized functions we are interested in are the generalized neurons and generalized neural networks (NN).

Definition 2.1

(Generalized neurons and generalized NN)
.

Let
d,m,N\in\mathbb{N}
and
O\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{d}
be open. Given parametrized functions
f_{1},...,f_{m}:O\times\Omega\to\mathbb{F}^{d}
. A generalized neuron is a parametrized function having the form

\sigma\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}f_{k}(\theta^{\prime},x)+b\right)

or

\partial_{\theta}^{\alpha}\sigma\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}f_{k}(\theta^{\prime},x)+b\right),\quad\alpha\in\cup_{s=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{N}^{s}

or

\partial_{\theta^{\prime}}^{\alpha}\sigma\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}f_{k}(\theta^{\prime},x)+b\right),\quad\alpha\in\cup_{s=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{N}^{s},

where its parameter is
\theta:=(w_{1},...,w_{m},b)\in\mathbb{F}^{m+1}
and
\theta^{\prime}
, provided that
\theta
is chosen such that it is well-defined. Any linear combination of generalized neurons is called an generalized neural networks, or generalized NN.

Remark 2.1
.

Note that while we consider parametrized functions
f_{1},...,f_{m}
, the requirement actually includes the usual functions. Indeed, given any function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{F}
, it induces a trivial parametrized function
\tilde{f}:O\times\Omega\to\mathbb{F}
defined by
\tilde{f}(\theta^{\prime},x)=f(x)
for all
\theta^{\prime}\in O
. In this case,
\partial_{\theta^{\prime}}^{\alpha}\tilde{f}
’s are all constant-zero functions, which are clearly linear combinations of generalized neurons, whence generalized NN.

The generalized NN form a large class of objects to study. In fact, as we show in the examples below, they include the “classical” fully-connected neural networks (see Definition
2.2
) as well as linear combinations of the partial derivatives of them. In this paper we focus on the generalized NN of the form

\sigma\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}f_{k}(z)+b\right)

and their linear combinations. Brief discussion will be made about how to extend our results to the generalized NN throughout the article.

Definition 2.2

(fully-connected neural network)
.

Given
L\in\mathbb{N}
,
d=:m_{0},m_{1},...,m_{L}=1\in\mathbb{N}
and a function
\sigma:\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
, an
L
-layer fully-connected neural network with input dimension
d
, widths
\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
and activation
\sigma
is a parameterized function
H:\mathbb{R}^{\sum_{l=1}^{L}m_{l}(m_{l-1}+1)}\times\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
defined inductively on

as follows:

\displaystyle H^{(0)}:\mathbb{R}^{\sum_{l=1}^{L}m_{l}\times(m_{l-1}+1)}\times\mathbb{F}^{d}\to\mathbb{F}^{d},\,\,H^{(0)}(\theta,z)=z

(1)

\displaystyle H^{(l)}:\mathbb{R}^{\sum_{l=1}^{L}m_{l}\times(m_{l-1}+1)}\times\mathbb{F}^{d}\to\mathbb{F}^{m_{l}},\,\,H^{(l)}(\theta,z)=\left(\sigma\left(w_{k}^{(l)}H^{(l-1)}(\theta,z)+b_{k}^{(l)}\right)\right)_{k=1}^{m_{1}}

\displaystyle H^{(L)}:\mathbb{R}^{\sum_{l=1}^{L}m_{l}\times(m_{l-1}+1)}\times\mathbb{F}^{d}\to\mathbb{F},\,H^{(L)}(\theta,z)=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{L-1}}a_{j}H^{(L-1)}(\theta,z)+b

\displaystyle H(\theta,z)=H^{(L)}(\theta,z).

Here
\theta:=(\theta^{(l)})_{l=1}^{L}
, where

\displaystyle\theta^{(L)}

\displaystyle=\left((a_{j})_{j=1}^{m_{L}},b\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{L}}\times\mathbb{R}\approx\mathbb{R}^{m_{L}+1},

\displaystyle\theta^{(l)}

\displaystyle=\left((w_{k}^{(l)})_{k=1}^{m_{l}},(b_{k}^{(l)})_{k=1}^{m_{l}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{l}m_{l-1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{l}}\approx\mathbb{R}^{m_{l}(m_{l-1}+1)},\quad 1\leq l<L.

For simplicity, we say
H
is a (fully-connected) NN with network structure
\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
and activation
\sigma
. Furthermore, we make the following definitions:

(a)

We call
\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
the network structure of
H
, and for simplicity we denote
N:=\sum_{l=1}^{L}m_{l}\times(m_{l-1}+1)
whenever we deal with a fully-connected NN.

(b)

The
w_{k}^{(l)}
’s are often called weights, and the
b
and
b_{k}^{(l)}
’s are often called biases.

(b)

When
b
and all the
b_{k}^{(l)}
’s are forced to be always zero, we say
H
is an NN without bias. In this case, we will still write
\theta:=(\theta^{(L)},...,\theta^{(1)})
, but

\displaystyle\theta^{(L)}

\displaystyle=(a_{j})_{j=1}^{m_{L}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{L}},

\displaystyle\theta^{(l)}

\displaystyle=(w_{k}^{(l)})_{k=1}^{m_{l}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{l}m_{l-1}},\quad 1\leq l<L

To distinguish it from a general fully-connected NN, we may sometimes call the latter one NN with bias.

Remark 2.2
.

For each
0\leq l\leq L
,
H^{(l)}
is independent of
\theta^{(l^{\prime})}
’s for all
l^{\prime}>l
. Thus, as an abuse of notation we may also view it as a map
\mathbb{R}^{\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{l}m_{l^{\prime}}\times(m_{l^{\prime}-1}+1)}\times\mathbb{F}^{d}\to\mathbb{F}^{m_{l}}
but still write
H^{(l)}(\theta^{(l)},...,\theta^{(1)},z)=H^{(l)}(\theta,z)
as in the definition. Second, when we emphasize the dependence of
H
on activation
\sigma
, we may write
H_{\sigma}
or
H(\sigma,\cdot,\cdot)
, namely, given a set
\mathcal{F}
of functions from
\mathbb{F}
to
\mathbb{F}
, we can view it as a map

H:\mathcal{F}\times\mathbb{R}^{\sum_{l=1}^{L}m_{l}(m_{l-1}+1)}\times\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}.

(2)

The simplest non-trivial example of fully-connected NNs are perhaps two-layer ones. Using the notations in Definition
2.2
, such networks take the form

H_{\sigma}:\mathbb{R}^{(m_{1}+1)+m_{1}(d+1)}\times\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F},\quad H_{\sigma}(\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^{m_{1}}a_{k}\sigma\left(w_{k}^{(1)}z+b_{k}^{(1)}\right)+b,

and a two-layer NN without bias takes the form

H_{\sigma}:\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}+m_{1}d}\times\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F},\quad H_{\sigma}(\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^{m_{1}}a_{k}\sigma\left(w_{k}^{(1)}z\right).\\

Then we give some important examples to illustrate how the concept of generalized NN and classical neural networks (including fully-connected NNs) are related to one another.

(a)

A generalized neuron is a generalized NN.

(b)

A fully-connected two layer NN is a generalized NN. Indeed, given
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\theta=(w,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}=\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, a generalized neuron is an element in the set

\{\sigma(wx+b),\sigma^{(s)}(wx+b),\sigma^{(s)}(wx+b)\Pi_{l=1}^{s}x_{i_{l}}\}_{s=1}^{\infty}.

Therefore, using our notations in Definition
2.1
, we can set
m=d
and
f_{t}(\theta^{\prime},x)=x_{t}
, the trivially induced
t
-th coordinate map on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
1\leq t\leq d
. Then any two-layer NN is just

\sum_{k=1}^{m^{\prime}}a_{k}\sigma\left(\sum_{t=1}^{d}(w_{k})_{t}f_{t}(\theta^{\prime},x)+b_{k}\right)

for some
(w_{1},b_{1}),...,(w_{m},b_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.

(c)

More generally, for any
L\in\mathbb{N}
, any fully connected
L
-layer neuron is a generalized neuron. Indeed, we may set
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
m=\text{its $(l-1)$-th layer width}
, and each
f_{k}
to be an
(l-1)
-layer neuron of it. Similarly, the partial derivatives of the parameters of
L
-layer neurons are generalized neurons. Therefore, any linear combination of
L
-layer neurons, as well as the partial derivatives of its parameters, are generalized NN.

(d)

A Res-Net neural network is also an generalized NN, because it can be viewed as a linear combination of an
L
-layer network and its lower-level layers.

Given any
m\in\mathbb{N}
and generalized neurons
H_{1},...,H_{m}
, our goal in this paper is to investigate what parameters
\theta_{1},...,\theta_{m}
will guarantee that
H_{1}(\theta_{1},\cdot),...,H_{m}(\theta_{m},\cdot)
are linearly dependent/independent. Obviously, the answer to this questions depends on
\sigma
. For example, when
\sigma
is constant, then such
\theta
does not exist at all; on the other hand, as shown by
Simsek et al. [2021]
,
Zhang et al. [2024]
, for generic analytic
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
H_{k}(\theta_{k},x)=\sigma(\theta_{k}x)
where
\theta_{k},x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, this holds if and only if
\theta_{1},...,\theta_{m}
are distinct. We will establish a similar result for generalized neurons with generic activations
\sigma
. To do this we will first construct special activations, then carefully deform it to other ones. The construction relies on the growth of functions which we define below. First we introduce the standard notations on function asymptotics.

Definition 2.3

(Function asymptotics)
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{F}
be open and
\gamma:[0,\infty)\to\Omega
a curve. Given
f,g:\Omega\to\mathbb{F}
, we denote

(a)

f(\gamma(t))=O(g(\gamma(t))
as
t\to\infty
if there is a
C>0
with
|f(\gamma(t))|\leq C|g(\gamma(t))|
;

(b)

f(\gamma(t))=o(\gamma(t))
as
t\to\infty
if there for any
C>0
we have
|f(\gamma(t))|\leq C|g(\gamma(t))|
;

(c)

f(\gamma(t))=\Omega(g(\gamma(t))
as
t\to\infty
if there is a
C>0
with
|f(\gamma(t))|\geq C|g(\gamma(t))|
;

(d)

f(\gamma(t))=\Theta(g(\gamma(t))
as
t\to\infty
if
f(\gamma(t))=O(g(\gamma(t)))
and
f(\gamma(t))=\Omega(g(\gamma(t))
.

Specifically, for
f,g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{F}
, we write
f(x)=O(g(x))
,
f(x)=o(g(x))
,
f(x)=\Omega(g(x))
and
f(x)=\Theta(g(x))
as
x\to\infty\,(-\infty,\pm\infty)
when the curve is just
[0,\infty)
(or
(-\infty,0]
or
\mathbb{R}
).

Definition 2.4

(Hyper-polynomial growth)
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{F}
be open and
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{F}
. Given a curve
\gamma:[0,\infty)\to\Omega
, we say
\lim_{t\to\infty}\re{f(\gamma(t))}=\infty(-\infty)
at hyper-polynomial rate (along
\gamma
), or simply
f
grows at hyper-polynomial rate (along
\gamma
) if
\re f(\gamma(t_{1}))=o(\re f(\gamma(t_{2})))
whenever

(a)

\frac{1}{l_{\gamma}(t_{2})-l_{\gamma}(\infty)}-\frac{1}{l_{\gamma}(t_{1})-l_{\gamma}(\infty)}\to\infty
as
t_{1},t_{2}\to\infty
if
l_{\gamma}(\infty)
is finite, or

(b)

l_{\gamma}(t_{2})-l_{\gamma}(t_{1})\to\infty
as
t_{1},t_{2}\to\infty
if
l_{\gamma}(\infty)
is infinite.

Definition 2.5

(Hyper-exponential growth)
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{F}
be open and
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{F}
. Given a curve
\gamma:[0,\infty)\to\Omega
, we say
\lim_{t\to\infty}\re{f(\gamma(t))}=\infty(-\infty)
at hyper-exponential rate (along
\gamma
), or simply
f
grows at hyper-exponential rate (along
\gamma
) if
\re f(\gamma(t_{1}))=o(\re f(\gamma(t_{2}))
whenever
t_{1},t_{2}\to\infty
such that
l_{\gamma}(t_{2})-l_{\gamma}(t_{1})
bounded below by a strictly positive number for all large
t_{1},t_{2}
.

Below are examples and counter-examples of functions which grow at hyper-polynomial or hyper-exponential rates.

(a)

f(z)=e^{z}
and
\gamma(t)=t+ic
for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
with
\cos(b)\neq 0
. Indeed,
\lim_{t\to\infty}|\re e^{t+ic}|=|\cos(b)|e^{t}=\infty
and we have

\frac{\re e^{t_{1}+ic}}{\re e^{t_{2}+ic}}=e^{-(t_{2}-t_{1})}\to 0

as
t_{2}-t_{1}\to\infty
. Thus,
e^{t_{1}+ic}=o(e^{t_{2}+ic})
as
t_{1},t_{2},(t_{2}-t-1)\to\infty
at the same time. Thus,
\lim_{t\to\infty}\re f(\gamma(t))=\infty
at hyper-polynomial rate. However, since

\frac{\re e^{t+ic}}{\re e^{t+b+ic}}=e^{-b}

for any
b\in\mathbb{R}
, the exponential function does not grow at hyper-exponential rate along
\gamma
.

(c)

f(x)=e^{x^{2}}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
\gamma(t)=t
. Then
\lim_{t\to\infty}f(\gamma(t))=e^{t^{2}}=\infty
and we have

\frac{e^{t^{2}}}{e^{(t+b)^{2}}}=e^{-(2bt+b^{2})}\to 0

as
t\to\infty
, for any
b>0
. Thus,
\lim_{t\to\infty}f(\gamma(t))=\infty
at hyper-exponential rate.

(d)

If
\lim_{t\to\infty}\re f(\gamma(t))=\infty
at hyper-exponential rate then
\lim_{t\to\infty}\re f(\gamma(t))=\infty
at hyper-polynomial rate. To illustrate this let’s consider the case for
l_{\gamma}(\infty)=\infty
. Since
f
grows at hyper-exponential rate, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we can find
t,t^{\prime}\to\infty
such that
\re f(\gamma(t))=o(\re f(\gamma(t^{\prime})))
and
l_{\gamma}(t^{\prime}{}^{(n)})\geq l_{\gamma}(t)+n
. Here
t^{\prime}{}^{(n)}
can be viewed as a function in
t
. Thus, we can find a sequence
\{\delta_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
decreasing to 0, and a sequence
\{M_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
increasing to
\infty
, such that for any
n
,

|\re f(\gamma(t))|\leq\delta_{n}|\re f(\gamma(t^{\prime}{}^{(n)}))|

whenever
t\geq M_{n}
. Now define
t_{1},t_{2}
(as functions in
t
) by
t_{1}=t
and
t_{2}=t^{\prime}{}^{(n)}
for
t\in[M_{n},M_{n+1})
. Then it is clear that
t_{1},t_{2}\to\infty
,
l_{\gamma}(t_{2})-l_{\gamma}(t_{1})\to\infty
, and
\re f(\gamma(t_{1}))=o(\re f(\gamma(t_{2})))
.

(e)

When
|\dot{\gamma}|
is bounded below over
[0,\infty)
(so in particular
l_{\gamma}(\infty)=\infty
), then any function
f
which grows faster than any polynomial grows at hyper-polynomial rate along
\gamma
, i.e., if for any
s\in\mathbb{N}
there is some
T>0
such that
f(\gamma(t))>l_{\gamma}(t)^{s}
whenever
t\geq T
, then
f
grows at hyper-polynomial rate.

Definition 2.6

(Ordered growth)
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{F}
be open and
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to\Omega
a curve. Given a finite collection of functions
f_{1},...,f_{m}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, we say
f_{1},...,f_{m}
have ordered growth (along
\gamma
) if there is a permutation
\pi\in S_{m}
such that, as
t\to\infty
we have
f_{\pi(k)}(\gamma(t))=o(f_{\pi(k-1)}(\gamma(t)))
for all
2\leq k\leq m
.

Below are examples of functions which have ordered growth.

(a)

Given
w_{1},...,w_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
w_{1}>...>w_{m}
, then
e^{w_{1}x},...,e^{w_{m}x}
have ordered growth along
\gamma(t)=t
.

(b)

More generally, given any function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{F}
and a curve
\varphi:[0,\infty)\to\Omega
such that
f
grows at hyper-polynomial rate along
\varphi
. For any
m\in\mathbb{N}
and any
\tau_{1},...,\tau_{m}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\tau_{k}(t)-\tau_{k-1}(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
, the functions

f_{k}:=f\circ\varphi\circ\tau_{k},\quad 1\leq k\leq m

have ordered growth along
\gamma(t)=t
. This follows from the definition of Hyper-polynomial growth of a function.

3
Preparing Lemmas and Propositions

This section is the main part of preparation of our results, which can be found in Section
4
. An important part of our general theory is to order the growth of a finite collection of functions. To obtain such results, we will apply the preparations in
3.1
, where various results to help inductively establish ordered growth of generalized neurons are presented. Another part is about approximating some given functions, which relies on the preparations in
3.2
and
3.3
. In
3.2
, we study how the asymptotics of a parameterized function depends on its parameters. In
3.3
, we introduce a class of analytic functions similar to bump functions; this will be used in the constructions in Section
4
. But first, we show that the study of linear independence of generalized neurons of arbitrary input dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
can be reduced to input dimension
d=1
.

Lemma 3.1

(dimension reduction)
.

Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Given distinct
w_{1},...,w_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then there is a
v\in\partial B(0,1)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\langle w_{1},v\rangle,...,\langle w_{m},v\rangle
are distinct. Moreover, if
w_{k},w_{j}
are multiples to one another, then for any
v\in\partial B(0,1)
,
\langle w_{k},v\rangle,\langle w_{j},v\rangle
are multiples to one another.

3.1
Functions of Ordered Growth

Now we consider functions of ordered growth. One important result is that these functions are linearly independent. This is exactly presented in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1

(functions of ordered growth are linearly independent)
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{F}
be open. Suppose that
f_{1},...,f_{m}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
have ordered growth along a curve
\gamma:[0,\infty)\to\Omega
and none of
f_{k}\circ\gamma
is eventually constant zero, i.e.,
\sup\{t:f_{k}(\gamma(t))\neq 0\}=\infty
. Then

(a)

f_{1},...,f_{m}
are linearly independent.

(b)

\frac{1}{f_{1}},...,\frac{1}{f_{m}}
are linearly independent.

Thus, if
\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}f_{k}
or
\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\frac{1}{f_{k}}
is constant zero, then
a_{1}=...=a_{m}=0
.

Proof.

By rearranging the indices if necessary, we can assume that in the proof for (a) and (b) below,
f_{k}(\gamma(t))=o(f_{k-1}(\gamma(t)))
as
t\to\infty
, for all
2\leq k\leq m
.

(a)

Let
a_{1},...,a_{m}\in\mathbb{F}
be constants such that
\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}f_{k}\equiv 0
. By our assumption, there is a sequence
\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
diverging to
\infty
such that
f_{1}(\gamma(t_{j}))\neq 0
. Moreover, we have
f_{k}(\gamma(t_{j}))=o(f_{1}(\gamma(t_{j})))
as
j\to\infty
, for all
2\leq k\leq m
. It follows that

a_{1}=-\lim_{j\to\infty}\sum_{k=2}^{m}a_{k}\frac{f_{k}(\gamma(t_{j}))}{f_{1}(\gamma(t_{j}))}=0.

This means we can instead consider
\sum_{k=2}^{m}a_{k}f_{k}
, which is also constant zero. By repeating this argument, we will eventually show that
a_{k}=0
for all
k
, thus proving the linear independence of the functions
f_{1},...,f_{m}
.

(b)

The proof is similar to (a). Let
a_{1},...,a_{m}\in\mathbb{F}
be constants such that
\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{a_{k}}{f_{k}}\equiv 0
. Let
\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
diverging to
\infty
be such that
f_{m}(\gamma(t_{j}))\neq 0
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
. It follows that

\displaystyle a_{m}

\displaystyle=-\lim_{j\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}a_{k}\frac{1/f_{k}(\gamma(t_{j})}{1/f_{m}(\gamma(t_{j})}

\displaystyle=-\lim_{j\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}a_{k}\frac{f_{m}(\gamma(t_{j}))}{f_{k}(\gamma(t_{j}))}=0.

This means we can instead consider
\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\frac{a_{k}}{f_{k}}
, which is also constant zero. By repeating this argument we will eventually show that
a_{k}=0
for all
k
, thus proving the linear independence of the functions
\frac{1}{f_{1}},...,\frac{1}{f_{m}}
.

∎

Remark 3.1
.

In fact, our proof for (a) implies that as long as
f_{k}(\gamma(t))=o(f_{j}(\gamma(t)))
as
t\to\infty
for all
k\neq j
, then the constant-zero function
\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}f_{k}
must give
a_{j}=0
. This does not fully use the fact that
f_{1},...,f_{m}
have ordered growth. The ordered growth property simply helps us restrict the functions to a single curve
\gamma
to establish their linear independence. A similar remark holds for (b).

The next proposition works for
f_{1},...,f_{m}
’s with ordered growth. They will be used to deduce complete characterizations of linear independence of multi-layer neurons with certain types of activations.

Proposition 3.2

(Functions of ordered growth – I)
.

Let
f_{1},...,f_{m}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be functions of ordered growth along a curve
\gamma:[0,\infty)\to\Omega
, such that
\lim_{t\to\infty}f_{k}(\gamma(t))=\infty
for all
1\leq k\leq m
. Suppose that
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies

(a)

\lim_{z\to\infty}\sigma(z)=\infty
and
\lim_{z\to-\infty}\sigma(z)=\infty
, both at hyper-polynomial rate.

(b)

For all
w>0
and
\tilde{w}<0
, either
\sigma(\tilde{w}f_{\pi(1)}(\gamma(t)))=o(\sigma(wf_{\pi(m)}(\gamma(t))))
or
\sigma(wf_{\pi(1)}(\gamma(t)))=o(\sigma(\tilde{w}f_{\pi(m)}(\gamma(t))))
as
t\to\infty
. Here
\pi
is the permutation defined as in Definition
2.6
.

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
and parameters
\{(w_{jk})_{k=1}^{m}\}_{j=1}^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Define for each
j
a function

H_{j}(z):=\sigma\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{jk}f_{k}(z)\right).

(3)

Then
H_{1},...,H_{n}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
have ordered growth (along
\gamma
) and
\lim_{t\to\infty}H_{j}(\gamma(t))=\infty
for all
1\leq j\leq n
if and only if all the
w_{j}:=(w_{jk})_{k=1}^{m}
are distinct and
\neq 0
.

Proof.

It is clear that if
w_{j_{1}}=w_{j_{2}}
, then
H_{1},...,H_{m}
cannot have ordered growth along
\gamma
, and if
w_{j}=0
for some
j
, then
\lim_{t\to\infty}H_{j}(\gamma(t))=\sigma(0)<\infty
. So it remains to deal with the other direction. By rearranging the indices of
f_{1},...,f_{m}
, we may assume that
f_{k}(\gamma(t))=o(f_{k-1}(\gamma(t)))
as
t\to\infty
for all
2\leq k\leq m
.

First, let’s prove that
\lim_{t\to\infty}H_{j}(\gamma(t))=\infty
whenever
w_{j}\neq 0
. So fix any
j
. Because
w_{j}\neq 0
, there is a smallest
k\in\{1,...,m\}
such that
w_{jk}\neq 0
. Then

\lim_{t\to\infty}\left|w_{jk}f_{k}(\gamma(t))\right|=|w_{jk}|\lim_{t\to\infty}f_{k}(\gamma(t))=\infty.

Moreover, our assumption implies that
f_{k^{\prime}}(\gamma(t))=o(f_{k}(\gamma(t)))
for all
k^{\prime}>k
. This, together with the fact that
w_{j1}=...=w_{j(k-1)}=0
, yields

\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{m}w_{jk^{\prime}}f_{k^{\prime}}(\gamma(t))\sim w_{jk}f_{k}(\gamma(t))

(4)

as
t\to\infty
. In particular,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\left|\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{m}w_{jk^{\prime}}f_{k^{\prime}}(\gamma(t))\right|=\infty.

It follows from property (a) of
\sigma
that
\lim_{t\to\infty}H_{j}(\gamma(t))=\infty
.

Then we investigate the ordered growth property these
H_{j}
’s. For this we assume without loss of generality that
\sigma(\tilde{w}f_{1}(\gamma(t)))=o(\sigma(wf_{m}(\gamma(t))))
for any
w>0
and
\tilde{w}<0
. We claim that this implies
\sigma(\tilde{w}f_{k}(\gamma(t)))=o(\sigma(wf_{j}(\gamma(t))))
for all
k,j\in\{1,...,m\}
, whenever
w>0
and
\tilde{w}<0
. Indeed, given
k\neq 1
and/or
j\neq m
, we have

\displaystyle f_{k}(\gamma(t))=o(f_{1}(\gamma(t))),\text{and/or }f_{m}(\gamma(t))=o(f_{j}(\gamma(t))).

Thus,

\displaystyle\tilde{w}f_{k}(\gamma(t))=o(f_{1}(\gamma(t))),\text{and/or }wf_{m}(\gamma(t))=o(f_{j}(\gamma(t))).

(5)

Since
w
and
\tilde{w}
are non-zero, we also have

\displaystyle\tilde{w}f_{k}(\gamma(t)),\tilde{w}f_{1}(\gamma(t))

\displaystyle\to-\infty,

\displaystyle wf_{m}(\gamma(t)),wf_{j}(\gamma(t))

\displaystyle\to\infty

as
t\to\infty
. Combining these with property (a) of
\sigma
, we can easily see that

\sigma(\tilde{w}f_{k}(\gamma(t))\ll\sigma(\tilde{w}f_{k}(\gamma(t)))\ll\sigma(wf_{m}(\gamma(t))))\ll\sigma(wf_{j}(\gamma(t))).

Similarly, property (a) of
\sigma
also implies that
\sigma(wf_{k}(\gamma(t))=o(\sigma(\tilde{w}f_{j}(\gamma(t))))
whenever
k>j
and
w,\tilde{w}
are both (strictly) positive/negative. Moreover, note that for any distinct
j_{1},j_{2}
, because
w_{j_{1}}\neq w_{j_{2}}
, there is a smallest
k\in\{1,...,m\}
with
w_{j_{1}k}\neq w_{j_{2}k}
. By our proof above, this implies that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\left|\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{m}(w_{j_{1}k^{\prime}}-w_{j_{2}k^{\prime}})f_{k^{\prime}}(\gamma(t))\right|=\lim_{t\to\infty}|w_{j_{1}k}-w_{j_{2}k}|f_{k}(\gamma(t))=\infty.

Now fix distinct
j_{1},j_{2}\in\{1,...,n\}
. Let
k_{1},k_{2}\in\{1,...,m\}
be the smallest numbers with
w_{j_{1}k_{1}},w_{j_{2}k_{2}}\neq 0
, respectively, and let
k_{1}^{\prime},k_{2}^{\prime}\in\{1,...,m\}
be the smallest numbers with
w_{j_{1}k_{1}^{\prime}}\neq w_{j_{2}k_{2}^{\prime}}
. Consider the following possible cases. If
w_{j_{1}k_{1}}>0
and
w_{j_{2}k_{2}}<0
, then by (
5
) above, we have
\sigma(w_{j_{2}k_{2}}f_{k_{2}}(\gamma(t)))=o(\sigma(w_{j_{1}k_{1}}f_{k_{1}}(\gamma(t)))
as
t\to\infty
. By (
4
),

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{j_{2}k}f_{k}(\gamma(t))

\displaystyle\sim w_{j_{2}k_{2}}f_{k_{2}}(\gamma(t)),

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{j_{1}k}f_{k}(\gamma(t))

\displaystyle\sim w_{j_{1}k}f_{k}(\gamma(t)).

It follows that
H_{j_{2}}(\gamma(t))=o(H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t)))
. Similarly, if
w_{j_{1}k_{1}}<0
and
w_{j_{2}k_{2}}>0
we can show that
H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t))=o(H_{j_{2}}(\gamma(t)))
. Then suppose
w_{j_{1}k_{1}}
and
w_{j_{2}k_{2}}
are both positive or negative. This means
\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{j_{1}k}f_{k}(\gamma(t))
and
\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{j_{2}k}f_{k}(\gamma(t))
both diverge to
\infty
or
-\infty
. Moreover, if
w_{j_{1}k_{1}^{\prime}}-w_{j_{2}k_{2}^{\prime}}>0
then
\sum_{k=1}^{m}(w_{j_{1}k}-w_{j_{2}k})f_{k}(\gamma(t))\to\infty
, while
w_{j_{1}k_{1}^{\prime}}-w_{j_{2}k_{2}^{\prime}}<0
implies
\sum_{k=1}^{m}(w_{j_{1}k}-w_{j_{2}k})f_{k}(\gamma(t))\to-\infty
. Using property (a) of
\sigma
, for the first case, we have
H_{j_{2}}(\gamma(t))=o(H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t))
, while for the second case
H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t))=o(H_{j_{2}}(\gamma(t)))
.

By dealing with the three cases above, we show that for any distinct
j_{1},j_{2}
we must have
H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t))=o(H_{j_{2}}(\gamma(t)))
or vice versa. The proof is completed by noting that this result is equivalent to
H_{1},...,H_{n}
having ordered growth along
\gamma
.
∎

Proposition 3.3

(Functions of ordered growth – II)
.

Let
f_{1},...,f_{m}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be functions of ordered growth along a curve
\gamma:[0,\infty)\to\Omega
, such that
\lim_{t\to\infty}f_{k}(\gamma(t))=\infty
for all
1\leq k\leq m
. Suppose that
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies

(a)

\lim_{z\to\infty}\sigma(z)=\infty
and
\lim_{z\to-\infty}\sigma(z)=\infty
, both at hyper-exponential rate.

(b)

For all
w>0
and
\tilde{w}<0
, either
\sigma(\tilde{w}f_{\pi(1)}(\gamma(t)))=o(\sigma(wf_{\pi(m)}(\gamma(t))))
or
\sigma(wf_{\pi(1)}(\gamma(t)))=o(\sigma(\tilde{w}f_{\pi(m)}(\gamma(t))))
as
t\to\infty
. Here
\pi
is the permutation defined as in Definition
2.6
.

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
and parameters
\left\{\left((w_{jk})_{k=1}^{m},b_{j}\right)\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
. Define for each
j
a function

H_{j}(z):=\sigma\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{jk}f_{k}(z)+b_{j}\right).

(6)

Then
H_{1},...,H_{n}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
have ordered growth (along
\gamma
) and
\lim_{t\to\infty}H_{j}(\gamma(t))=\infty
for all
1\leq j\leq n
if and only if all the
(w_{j},b_{j})
’s are distinct and none of
w_{j}=0
.

Proof.

It is clear that if
w_{j_{1}}=w_{j_{2}}
, then
H_{1},...,H_{m}
cannot have ordered growth along
\gamma
, and if
w_{j}=0
for some
j
, then
\lim_{t\to\infty}H_{j}(\gamma(t))=\sigma(b_{j})<\infty
. So it remains to prove the other direction. Again, we will show that for any distinct
j_{1},j_{2}\in\{1,...,m\}
, either
H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t))=o(H_{j_{2}}(\gamma(t)))
or vice versa, as
t\to\infty
. The proof is identical to the one for Proposition
3.2
above, except that we need to deal with one more case:
w_{j_{1}}=w_{j_{2}}
and
b_{j_{1}}\neq b_{j_{2}}
for some
j_{1},j_{2}\in\{1,...,n\}
. Note that as
t\to\infty
,

\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{j_{1}k}f_{k}(\gamma(t))+b_{j_{1}},\,\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{j_{2}k}f_{k}(\gamma(t))+b_{j_{2}}\to\infty(-\infty)

simultaneously; in particular, they have the same signs. Thus, by property (a) of
\sigma
, as
t\to\infty
:

(a)

If they are both positive (diverge to
\infty
) and
b_{j_{1}}>b_{j_{2}}
, then
H_{j_{2}}(\gamma(t))=o(H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t)))
.

(b)

If they are both positive (diverge to
\infty
) and
b_{j_{1}}<b_{j_{2}}
, then
H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t))=o(H_{j_{2}}(\gamma(t)))
.

(c)

If they are both negative (diverge to
-\infty
) and
b_{j_{1}}>b_{j_{2}}
, then
H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t))=o(H_{j_{2}}(\gamma(t)))
.

(d)

If they are both positive (diverge to
-\infty
) and
b_{j_{1}}<b_{j_{2}}
, then
H_{j_{2}}(\gamma(t))=o(H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t)))
.

This completes the proof.
∎

Below we give some examples about
\sigma
’s which make Proposition
3.2
and/or
3.3
hold. The idea is to construct
\sigma
as
\sigma(z)=a(z)+\tilde{a}(-z)
, where
a,\tilde{a}
grows at different orders and
\lim_{z\to-\infty}a(z)
,
\lim_{z\to-\infty}\tilde{a}(z)
are bounded. For simplicity, consider
m=2
and
f_{1}(x)=x^{2}
,
f_{2}(x)=x
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Clearly,
\lim_{x\to\infty}f_{1}(x)=\lim_{t\to\infty}f_{2}(x)=\infty
and
f_{2}(x)=o(f_{1}(x))
as
x\to\infty
, i.e., they have ordered growth.

(a)

For Proposition
3.2
, define
\sigma(z)=e^{z^{3}}+e^{-z}
. Then for any
w>0
and
\tilde{w}<0
, we have
\sigma(\tilde{w}f_{1}(x))\sim e^{\tilde{w}x^{2}}
, while
\sigma(wf_{2}(x))\sim e^{w^{3}x^{3}}
. Thus, the hypotheses in Proposition
3.2
are satisfied.

(b)

For proposition
3.3
, define
\sigma(z)=e^{z^{7}}+e^{-z^{3}}
. In the same way as (a) we can check that the hypotheses in Proposition
3.3
are satisfied. Clearly this
\sigma
also satisfies the hypotheses in Proposition
3.2
.

As we can see from these examples, the “local behavior” of
\sigma
is not important – we care about the behavior of
\sigma(z)
as
z\to\pm\infty
. In fact, the idea is to play with its growth. Note that this depends on the
f_{k}
’s. In general, if we are dealing with
L
-layer generalized neurons, we need to take every layer into consideration. Also note that the
\sigma
we construct differs much from what is used in practice. However, as we shall see in Section
4
, we can carefully deform it to approximate a commonly used activation function very well.

Remark 3.2
.

Proposition
3.2
and Proposition
3.3
both deal with generalized neurons of the form
\sigma\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}f_{k}+b_{k}\right)
(a.k.a. the
H_{j}
’s defined in (
6
)). Obviously they focus on neurons of multiple layers. However, we can apply the idea of them to deal with more kinds of generalized neurons. The result below is an example about how to extend them. It deals with
H_{j}
’s and their partial derivatives against parameters.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
f_{1},...,f_{m}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be functions of ordered growth along a curve
\gamma:[0,\infty)\to\Omega
, such that
\lim_{t\to\infty}f_{k}(\gamma(t))=\infty
for all
1\leq k\leq m
. Suppose that
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies

(a)

\lim_{z\to\infty}\sigma(z),\sigma^{\prime}(z)=\infty
and
\lim_{z\to-\infty}\sigma(z),\sigma^{\prime}(z)=\infty
, such that
\sigma(z)=o(\sigma^{\prime}(z))
as
z\to\pm\infty
.

(b)

We have
\sigma^{\prime}(wf_{\pi(k)})f_{\pi(1)}(\gamma(t))=o(\sigma(\tilde{w}f_{\pi(k-1)}(\gamma(t))))
whenever
w,\tilde{w}
are both strictly positive or negative.

(c)

For all
w>0
and
\tilde{w}<0
, either
\sigma^{\prime}(\tilde{w}f_{\pi(1)}(\gamma(t)))f_{\pi(1)}(\gamma(t))=o(\sigma(wf_{\pi(m)}(\gamma(t))))
, or

\sigma^{\prime}(wf_{\pi(1)}(\gamma(t)))f_{\pi(1)}(\gamma(t))=o(\sigma(\tilde{w}f_{\pi(m)}(\gamma(t))))
as
t\to\infty
. Here
\pi
is the permutation defined as in Definition
2.6
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
define
H_{j}
as in formula (
6
). Then, if and only if the
(w_{j},b_{j})
’s are distinct and
w_{j}\neq 0
for all

, the functions

\left\{H_{j},\partial_{w_{jk}}H_{j},\partial_{b_{j}}H_{j}\right\}_{j,k=1}^{n,m}

have ordered growth along
\gamma
, and

\lim_{t\to\infty}H_{j}(\gamma(t))=\lim_{t\to\infty}\partial_{w_{jk}}H_{j}(\gamma(t))=\lim_{t\to\infty}\partial_{b_{j}}H_{j}(\gamma(t))=\infty

for every
1\leq j\leq n
and
1\leq k\leq m
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
f_{k}(\gamma(t))=o(f_{k-1}(\gamma(t)))
for all
2\leq k\leq m
and
\sigma^{\prime}(\tilde{w}f_{1}(\gamma(t)))f_{1}(\gamma(t))=o(\sigma(wf_{m}(\gamma(t))))
as
t\to\infty
, for all
w>0
and
\tilde{w}<0
. Similar as in the proof for Proposition
3.2
, for any
k,j\in\{1,...,m\}
and any
w>0
and
\tilde{w}<0
, we have

\sigma^{\prime}(\tilde{w}f_{k}(\gamma(t)))f_{k}(\gamma(t))=o(\sigma(wf_{j}(\gamma(t))))

as
t\to\infty
. Combining this with property (a) and (b) of
\sigma
, we conclude that for any
w_{1},...,w_{m}>0
and
\tilde{w}_{1},...,\tilde{w}_{m}<0
, we have the following ordering:

\displaystyle\sigma(\tilde{w}_{m}f_{m}(\gamma(t))

\displaystyle\ll...

(7)

\displaystyle\ll\sigma(\tilde{w}_{1}f_{1}(\gamma(t)))\ll\sigma^{\prime}(\tilde{w}_{1}f_{1}(\gamma(t)))\ll\sigma^{\prime}(\tilde{w}_{1}f_{1}(\gamma(t)))f_{1}(\gamma(t))

\displaystyle\ll\sigma(w_{m}f_{m}(\gamma(t)))\ll\sigma^{\prime}(w_{m}f_{m}(\gamma(t)))\ll\sigma^{\prime}(w_{m}f_{m}(\gamma(t)))f_{1}(\gamma(t))

\displaystyle\ll...

\displaystyle\ll\sigma^{\prime}(w_{1}f_{1}(\gamma(t)))f_{1}(\gamma(t)).

Given
1\leq j\leq n
, by our definition of
H_{j}
we have

\displaystyle\partial_{w_{jk}}H_{j}(z)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\prime}\left(\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{m}w_{jk^{\prime}}f_{k^{\prime}}(z)+b_{k^{\prime}}\right)f_{k}(z)

\displaystyle\partial_{b_{j}}H_{j}(z)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\prime}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{jk}f_{k}(z)+b_{k}\right).

These are clearly generalized neurons. As mentioned in previous proofs, for each
j
there is some
k(j)\in\{1,...,m\}
such that

\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{jk}f_{k}(\gamma(t))\sim w_{jk(j)}f_{k(j)}(\gamma(t))

as
t\to\infty
. Fix
j_{1},j_{2}\in\{1,...,n\}
. We would like to establish an ordering of the functions
\left\{H_{j_{i}},\partial_{w_{j_{i}k}},\partial_{b_{j_{i}}}H_{j_{i}}\right\}_{k,i=1}^{m,2}
. Consider the following cases. Note that in (

), the
w_{j}
and

’s are arbitrary.

(a)

Suppose that
w_{j_{1}k(j_{1})}

),

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\,\,H_{j_{2}}(\gamma(t))\ll\partial_{b_{j_{2}}}H_{j_{2}}(\gamma(t))\ll\partial_{w_{j_{2}1}}H_{j_{2}}(\gamma(t))

\displaystyle\ll H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t))\ll\partial_{b_{j_{1}}}H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t))\ll\partial_{w_{j_{1}1}}H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t)).

Moreover, using the ordered growth of

(recall the formulas for

\partial_{b_{j_{2}}}H_{j_{2}}(\gamma(t))\ll\partial_{w_{j_{2}m}}H_{j_{2}}(\gamma(t))\ll...\ll\partial_{w_{j_{2}1}}H_{j_{2}}(\gamma(t))

and a similar inequality holds for

. Thus, we establish an ordering for all the functions.

(b)

Suppose that
w_{j_{1}k(j_{1})}
and

are both positive or negative. Let

is the smallest number such that

\sum_{k=1}^{m}(w_{j_{1}k}-w_{j_{2}k})f_{k}(\gamma(t))\sim(w_{j_{1}k^{\prime}}-w_{j_{2}k^{\prime}})f_{k}(\gamma(t))

as

. If

, then by property (a) and (b) of
\sigma

), we have

\displaystyle H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t))

\displaystyle\ll\partial_{b_{j_{1}}}H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t))\ll\partial_{w_{j_{1}1}}H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t))

\displaystyle\ll\partial_{b_{j_{2}}}H_{j_{2}}(\gamma(t))\ll\partial_{w_{j_{2}1}}H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t)).

If
w_{j_{2}k^{\prime}}<w_{j_{1}k^{\prime}}
we simply argue in the same way to obtain

\displaystyle H_{j_{2}}(\gamma(t))

\displaystyle\ll\partial_{b_{j_{2}}}H_{j_{2}}(\gamma(t))\ll\partial_{w_{j_{2}1}}H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t))

\displaystyle\ll\partial_{b_{j_{1}}}H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t))\ll\partial_{w_{j_{1}1}}H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t)).

Finally, using the ordered growth of
f_{1},...,f_{m}
we also have

\partial_{b_{j_{1}}}H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t))\ll\partial_{w_{j_{1}m}}H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t))\ll...\ll\partial_{w_{j_{1}1}}H_{j_{1}}(\gamma(t))

and similarly for
j_{2}
. Thus, we can also establish an ordering for all the functions.

∎

Let’s consider the example (b) above again. We claim that the activation function
\sigma(z)=e^{z^{7}}+e^{-z^{3}}
satisfies the hypotheses in Proposition
3.4
. First note that
\sigma^{\prime}(z)=7z^{6}e^{z^{7}}-3z^{2}e^{-z^{3}}
, whence
\sigma^{\prime}(z)\sim 7z^{6}e^{z^{7}}
as
z\to\infty
, and
\sigma^{\prime}(z)\sim-3z^{2}e^{-z^{3}}
as
z\to-\infty
. Thus,
\sigma
clearly have property (a). For (b), given
w>0
and
\tilde{w}<0
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}(\tilde{w}x^{2})\sim-3\tilde{w}^{2}x^{4}e^{-\tilde{w}^{3}x^{6}},\quad\sigma^{\prime}(wx)\sim 7w^{6}x^{6}e^{x^{7}}

as
t\to\infty
. Finally, (c) is mentioned in Proposition
3.3
, so we have proved that (c) holds as well. This shows that
\sigma
satisfies the hypotheses in Proposition
3.4
.

Remark 3.3
.

In the same way we can formalize and prove a similar result concerning finitely many
L
-layer neurons and all the partial derivatives against parameters of them.

3.2
Local Asymptotics of Parameterized Functions

Consider a parameterized function
H:\mathbb{F}^{N}\times\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
, where
\theta\in\mathbb{F}^{N}
is called the parameters of
H
and
z\in\mathbb{F}
the input of
H
(see Section
2
). The level sets, the asymptotics of
H(\theta,\cdot)
near any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{F}
all depend on
\theta
: for example, when
H
is analytic, we have
H(\theta,x)\sim c(x-x_{0})^{s}
for some
c
and
s
determined by
\theta
. Below we study how these properties depend on
\theta
. We will first prove the most general case. Then we will focus specifically on definable functions (Defintion
3.3
) which gives us a stronger result.

Before we present the results, let’s first make the following definition.

Definition 3.1
.

Define
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{F})
as the set of real analytic functions
f:\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
. For each
S\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
define
C^{S}(\mathbb{F})
the set of functions
f:\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
whose
s
-th derivatives are all continuous,
0\leq s\leq S
. Given such a function space
\mathcal{F}:=\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{F})
or
C^{S}(\mathbb{F})
, define the following topologies on it:

(a)

(Uniform convergence) Given
E\subseteq\mathbb{F}
, let
\left\|f\right\|_{\infty,E}:=\sup_{x\in E}|f(x)|
for
f\in\mathcal{F}
. Specifically, we write
\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}
when
E=\mathbb{F}
.

(b)

(
S
-order uniform convergence). Given
E\subseteq\mathbb{F}
, let
\left\|f\right\|_{S,E}:=\sup_{x\in E}\sum_{s=0}^{S}|f^{(s)}(x)|
for
f\in\mathcal{F}
, where
f^{(s)}
is the
s
-th derivative of
f
. Specifically, we write
\left\|f\right\|_{S}
when
E=\mathbb{F}
.

(b)

(local
S
-order uniform convergence) An open ball around some
f\in\mathcal{F}
has the form

\left\{g\in\mathcal{F}:\sum_{s=0}^{S}\left\|g^{(s)}-f^{(s)}\right\|_{\infty,\overline{B(z^{*},r)}}<\delta\right\}

where
\overline{B(z^{*},r)}:=\{z\in\mathbb{F}:|z-z^{*}|\leq r\}
.

Remark 3.4
.

When
S=0
, we write
C(\mathbb{F})
for
C^{0}(F)
; this is just the set of continuous functions on
\mathbb{F}
. Also note that for
S,S^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
, the
S^{\prime}
-order and local
S^{\prime}
-order uniform convergence topologies on
C^{S}(\mathbb{F})
are well-defined only when
S^{\prime}\leq S
.

The following lemma is so straightforward that we omit the proof for it.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
E
be a subset of
\mathbb{F}^{N}
. Suppose that
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{F})\times\mathbb{F}^{N}\to[0,\infty)
is a function and there are constants
L,R>0
such that for any
\sigma,\tilde{\sigma}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{F})
, we have
\sup_{\theta\in E}\left\|\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}-\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{\sigma}}\right\|<L\left\|\sigma-\tilde{\sigma}\right\|_{\infty,[-R,R]}
. Then for any
\sigma,\tilde{\sigma}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{F})
,
\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{\sigma}}^{-1}(0)
is contained in
\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}^{-1}\left[0,L\left\|\sigma-\tilde{\sigma}\right\|_{\infty,[-R,R]}\right)
.

Another way to state this lemma is that, given
\sigma
and
\varepsilon>0
, for any
\tilde{\sigma}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{F})
with
\left\|\sigma-\tilde{\sigma}\right\|_{\infty,[-R,R]}<\frac{\varepsilon}{L}
we have
\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{\sigma}}^{-1}(0)\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}^{-1}[0,\varepsilon)
, so locally
\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{\sigma}}^{-1}(0)
and
\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}^{-1}(0)
“looks” similar. We would like to first find a
\sigma
and a function
\mathcal{L}
with good properties, then carefully change
\sigma
to
\tilde{\sigma}
. This operation is contained in the proof for Proposition
4.1
. Then we apply Lemma
3.2
to show that
\mathcal{L}_{\sigma}^{-1}(0)
and
\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{\sigma}}^{-1}(0)
“looks similar”, at least locally.

Under the setting of neural networks, this simple result says more. For example, consider
\mathcal{L}(\sigma,\theta):=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\sigma(w_{k}x)\right)^{2}dx
. By the existing results linear independence of (unbiased) two-layer neurons
Simsek et al. [2021]
, when
\sigma
is a non-polynomial analytic function whose derivatives are all not odd or even, then
\mathcal{L}(\sigma,\theta)=0
if and only if
\theta=(a_{k},w_{k})_{k=1}^{m}
satisfies:

(a)

the
w_{k}
’s are not all distinct,

(b)

the sum of
a_{k}
’s whose corresponding
w_{k}
’s are the same equals zero.

Then Lemma
3.2
implies that for
\tilde{\sigma}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
sufficiently close to
\sigma
,
\mathcal{L}(\tilde{\sigma},\theta)\neq 0
whenever the
w_{k}
’s have a “gap” to one another, i.e., for any
k\neq j
we have
|w_{k}-w_{j}|>\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
. Since
\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\tilde{\sigma}(w_{k}x)
is analytic, these neurons
\{\tilde{\sigma}(w_{k}x)\}_{k=1}^{m}
are linearly independent.

The next result we will use frequently is Corollary
3.1
. To prove it we need Lemma
3.4
and Lemma
3.5
, which can be deduced from Lemma
3.3
. Note that it is a summary of Theorem 6.3.3 in
Krantz and Parks [2002]
. The original theorem gives a more detailed characterization of the zero set of an analytic function, but we do not need that much.

Lemma 3.3

(stratification of zero set)
.

Given
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{N}
be open. Then for any analytic function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{F}
,
f^{-1}(0)
is a locally finite union of analytic submanifolds of
\Omega
(or
\mathbb{F}^{N}
), i.e., for any compact
K\subseteq\Omega
,
f^{-1}(0)\cap K
is a union of finitely many manifolds.

To prove Lemma
3.4
and Lemma
3.5
, we first introduce the idea of o-minimal sets and definable functions
[
Ghorbani, 2021
,
Wilkie, 1996
]
.

Definition 3.2

(o-minimal structure, rephrased from
Wilkie [1996]
)
.

We define a structure
\mathcal{S}
on
\mathbb{R}
to be a sequence
\{S_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
satisfying the following axioms for each
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.

(a)

S_{n}
is an algebra of subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(b)

If
A\in S_{n}
, both
A\times\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{R}\times A\in S_{n+1}
.

(c)

All semi-algebraic sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are in
S_{n}
.

(d)

For any
E\subseteq S_{n+1}
,
Pr_{n}(E)\subseteq S_{n}
where
Pr_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the projection map onto the first
n
coordinates of elements in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

For any
n
and any
E\subseteq S_{n}
, we call
E
a definable set. A structure is said to be o-minimal if the definable sets in
S_{1}
are exactly finite unions of points and intervals.

Definition 3.3

(definable functions, Definition 3.2 from
Ghorbani [2021]
)
.

Given definable sets
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A function
f:A\to B
is called definable, if its graph
{(x,f(x)):x\in A}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
is definable.

In
Ghorbani [2021]
, it is said that Wilkie proved that the smallest structure containing the graph of
\exp(\cdot)
is an o-minimal structure, whence the algebraic operations on
\exp(\cdot)
are all definable functions. In particular, this include

(a)

All commonly seen analytic activations, such as Sigmoid, Tanh, Softmax and Swish activations.

(b)

All functions of the form
\sigma(x)=e^{x^{\alpha}}+e^{x^{\beta}}
, where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}
.

(c)

The generalized neurons and neural networks with activations in (a) and/or (b).

(d)

The derivatives (against both input and parameter) of functions in (c).

An important result about definable functions is as follows. It is useful to us in Section
4.1
and in Corollary
3.1
.

Theorem 3.1
.

The zero set of a definable function has finitely many components; in particular the zero sets of functions in the examples (a), (b), (c) and (d) above all have finitely many connected components.

Proof.

By
Ghorbani [2021]
, every definable set has finitely many connected components. Thus, it suffices to show that the zero set of a definable function
f:A\to B
is a definable set, where
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
for some
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
. This is because the functions in examples (a), (b), (c) and (d) are all definable. By Definition
3.3
, the graph of
f
,
\{(x,f(x)):x\in A\}
is definable. Let
p:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
be the polynomial
p(x,y)=|y|^{2}
. Since

\displaystyle f^{-1}(0)=\{(x,f(x)):x\in A\}\cap\{(x,0):x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\}=\{(x,f(x)):x\in A\}\cap p^{-1}(0),

f^{-1}(0)
is definable by Definition
3.2
.

∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Given
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\{c_{s}\}_{s=0}^{\infty}
are (real or complex) analytic functions defined on an open subset
\Omega
of
\mathbb{F}^{N}
. Denote their common zero set by
\mathcal{Z}
. Given a bounded open subset
\Omega^{\prime}
of
\Omega\setminus\mathcal{Z}
, there is some
S\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
\theta\in\Omega^{\prime}
, we can find some
s\in\{0,1,...,S\}
with
c_{s}(\theta)\neq 0
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that when
\mathcal{Z}\neq\mathbb{F}^{N}
, there is some
S\in\mathbb{N}
with
\left(\cap_{s=0}^{S}c_{s}^{-1}(0)\right)\cap B=\mathcal{Z}\cap B
, where
B
is any compact ball of
\mathbb{F}^{N}
. For this we argue in an inductive way. First fix any
\theta_{1}\in B\setminus\mathcal{Z}
. By hypothesis there is some
s_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
with
c_{s_{1}}(\theta_{1})\neq 0
. Thus, Lemma
3.3
implies that
c_{s_{1}}^{-1}(0)\subseteq B
is contained in
\mathcal{Z}
union a countable, locally finite collection of connected analytic submanifolds of
\mathbb{F}^{N}
, each one (except
\mathcal{Z}
) having dimension at most
N-1
; in particular, finitely many of them intersect
B
.

Suppose that we have found integers
s_{1},...,s_{l(k)}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
c_{s_{1}}^{-1}(0)\cap...\cap c_{s_{l(k)}}^{-1}(0)
is
\mathcal{Z}
union a countable collection of connected analytic submanifolds of
\mathbb{F}^{N}
having dimension at most
N-k
, and finitely many of them intersect
B
, say

c_{s_{1}}^{-1}(0)\cap...\cap c_{s_{l(k)}}^{-1}(0)\cap B\subseteq\mathcal{Z}\cup\left(\mathcal{M}_{1}\cup...\cup\mathcal{M}_{n(k)}\right)

Here
n(k)
and
l(k)
are integers depending on
k
. We may assume that
\mathcal{M}_{j}\neq\mathcal{Z}
for all
1\leq j\leq n(k)
. For any
\mathcal{M}_{j}
and any
\theta\in\mathcal{M}_{j}\setminus\mathcal{Z}
, there is a
s_{l(k)+j}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
with
c_{s_{l(k)+j}}(\theta)\neq 0
. Since
\mathcal{M}_{j}
is an analytic manifold, this implies that
c_{s_{l(k)+j}}^{-1}(0)\cap\mathcal{M}_{j}
is contained in a countable union of submanifolds of
\mathcal{M}_{j}
, each one having dimension
N-(k+1)
. Do this for each
1\leq j\leq n(k)
and set
l(k+1)=l(k)+n(k)
. Then clearly
c_{s_{1}}^{-1}(0)\cap...\cap c_{s_{l(k+1)}}^{-1}(0)
is contained in
\mathcal{Z}
union a countable collection of analytic submanifolds of
\mathbb{F}^{N}
having dimension at most
N-(k+1)
. Define
f(\theta)=\sum_{j=1}^{l(k+1)}c_{s_{j}}(\theta)
, then

f^{-1}(0)=c_{s_{1}}^{-1}(0)\cap...\cap c_{s_{l(k+1)}}^{-1}(0).

By Lemma
3.3
,
f^{-1}(0)
is a locally finite union of analytic submanifolds of
\mathbb{F}^{N}
, whence our proof above simply says that each such manifold (except
\mathcal{Z}
) in the union has dimension at most
N-(k+1)
, or finitely many intersect
B
. This completes the induction step.
∎

This result can be strengthened when
\{c_{s}\}_{s=0}^{\infty}
are definable functions.

Lemma 3.5
.

Given
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\{c_{s}\}_{s=0}^{\infty}
are (real) definable functions on
\mathbb{F}
. Denote their common zero set by
\mathcal{Z}
. Then there is some
S\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{Z}
, we can find some
s\in\{1,...,S\}
with
c_{s}(\theta)\neq 0
.

Proof.

We first note there is some
S^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
c_{0}^{-1}(0)\cap...\cap c_{S^{\prime}}^{-1}(0)\setminus\mathcal{Z}
is a (possibly) empty collection of discrete points. Indeed, if this is not true then for any
S^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
,
c_{0}^{-1}(0)\cap...\cap c_{S^{\prime}}^{-1}(0)\setminus\mathcal{Z}
contains an analytic set
\mathcal{M}
of dimension at least 1. In other words,
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\cap_{s=0}^{\infty}c_{s}^{-1}(0)
, which implies that
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
, a contradiction.

Fix this
S^{\prime}
. Define
c:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
by
c(\theta)=\sum_{s=0}^{S^{\prime}}|c_{s}(\theta)|^{2}
. Since
c
is obtained by composing a definable function with definable functions, it is also definable, and clearly

c^{-1}(0)=c_{0}^{-1}(0)\cap...\cap c_{S^{\prime}}^{-1}(0).

Thus, by Theorem
3.1
,
c^{-1}(0)
has finitely many components, implying that
c_{0}^{-1}(0)\cap c_{S^{\prime}}^{-1}(0)\setminus\mathcal{Z}
is a finite set which we may denote by
\{p_{1},...,p_{l}\}
. For any
1\leq i\leq l
, there is some
S_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
with
c_{S_{i}}(p_{i})\neq 0
. Thus, by setting

S:=\max\{S^{\prime},S_{1},...,S_{l}\},

we can see that
\left(\cap_{s=0}^{S}c_{s}^{-1}(0)\right)\setminus\mathcal{Z}=\emptyset
.
∎

This result has many immediate corollaries about analytic parameterized functions, which we list below. They will be used in the proof for Proposition
4.2
.

Corollary 3.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{F}^{N}\times\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
be an analytic function which is not constant zero. Let
\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{N}
denote the set of parameters
\theta\in\mathbb{F}^{N}
such that
f(\theta,\cdot)\equiv 0
. Then any point
\theta^{*}\in\mathbb{F}^{N}
has a neighborhood
U(\theta^{*})\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{N}
and a
S\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
\theta\in U(\theta^{*})\setminus\mathcal{Z}
, there are some
s\in\{0,1,...,S\}
and
c_{s}(\theta)\neq 0
with
f(\theta,x)\sim c_{s}(\theta)x^{s}
as
x\to 0
. In particular, for any bounded open subset
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{N}
we can find some
S\in\mathbb{N}
making the statement hold, and if
f
is a definable function such that its derivatives are all definable, this holds for
\Omega=\mathbb{F}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{Z}
.

Proof.

Fix
\theta^{*}\in\mathbb{F}^{N}
. Note that on a bounded product neighborhood
U(\theta^{*})\times V(\theta^{*})\in\mathbb{F}^{N}\times\mathbb{F}
of
(\theta^{*},0)
we can write
f(\theta,x)
in its series expansion in
x
, namely

f(\theta,x):=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}c_{s}(\theta)x^{s},\quad(\theta,x)\in U(\theta^{*})\times V(\theta^{*}).

Here the
c_{s}
’s are coefficients in the series expansion, each one being analytic in
\theta
. If
\theta\in\mathcal{Z}
,
c_{s}(\theta)=0
for all
s\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
; if
\theta\notin\mathcal{Z}
, since
f(\theta,\cdot)
is not constant zero, there must be some
s\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
with
c_{s}(\theta)\neq 0
. Therefore, the common zero set (restricted to
U(\theta^{*})
) of
c_{0},c_{1},...
is just
\mathcal{Z}\cap U(\theta^{*})
. By Lemma
3.4
above, we have some
S\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
\theta\in U(\theta^{*})\setminus\mathcal{Z}
, there is a smallest
s\in\{0,1,...,S\}
with
c_{s}(\theta)\neq 0
. Then
f(\theta,x)\sim c_{s}(\theta)x^{s}
as
x\to 0
. This proves the first part of the corollary.

Now fix a bounded open
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{N}
. Note that
\overline{\Omega}
is compact. For every point
\theta^{*}\in\overline{\Omega}
, we can apply the first part of this corollary to find a
U(\theta^{*})
and
S(\theta^{*})
. Since finitely many
U(\theta^{*})
’s cover
\overline{\Omega}
(and thus
\Omega
), say
\overline{\Omega}\subseteq U(\theta_{1}^{*})\cup...\cup U(\theta_{n}^{*})
. Set
S:=\max\{S(\theta_{1}^{*}),...,S(\theta_{n}^{*})\}
. Given
\theta\in\Omega
,
\theta\in U(\theta_{j}^{*})
for some
1\leq j\leq n
. Then by definition of
S(\theta_{j}^{*})
, there are some
s\leq S(\theta_{j}^{*})\leq S
and
c_{s}(\theta)\neq 0
with
f(\theta,x)\sim c_{s}(\theta)x^{s}
as
x\to 0
.

Finally, assume that
f
and its derivatives are all definable functions. Define
c_{s}(\theta):=\frac{\partial^{s}f}{\partial x^{s}}(\theta,x)|_{x=0}
. Then
\{c_{s}\}_{s=1}^{\infty}
are definable functions. Therefore, by Lemma
3.5
there is some
S\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{Z}
, we can find some
s\in\{1,...,S\}
with
c_{s}(\theta)\neq 0
. But then we obtain again
f(\theta,x)\sim c_{s}(\theta)x^{s}
as
x\to 0
.

∎

3.3
Analytic Bump Functions

Recall that a bump function
\xi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
is a function such that
\xi=\text{const.}
on an interval
[a,b]
and
\xi=0
on
\mathbb{R}\setminus[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]
, where
[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]
is some interval containing
[a,b]
properly. Such functions can be made smooth
Lee [2013]
, but never analytic. Our goal in this part is to construct a
\xi
such that
\xi\approx 1
on
[a,b]
and
\xi\approx 0
on
\mathbb{R}\setminus[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]
, where the approximation can be arbitrarily precise. The key to this construction is the following lemma.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\rho:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
be a function which is continuous, non-decreasing and has finite intersection with any linear function on
\mathbb{R}
. then there are
\lambda>0
and an interval
I\subseteq[0,\infty)
containing the origin, such that the sequence of functions

\left\{f_{n}:f_{1}=1+\lambda\rho,f_{n}=1+\lambda\rho(f_{n-1})\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

satisfies the following properties:

(a)

\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x)
exists for every
x\in I
, and this limit is strictly greater than
1
. Moreover, if
\rho
is strictly convex and continuously differentiable, there is some
L>1
such that
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
converges uniformly to the constant function
x\mapsto L
on
I
.

(b)

Conversely, assume that
x=o(\rho(x))
as
x\to\infty
. Then
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x)=\infty
for all
x\in[0,\infty)\setminus I
. Moreover, if
\rho
is strictly increasing on
[0,\infty)
,
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
diverges to
\infty
uniformly on
[0,\infty)\setminus I^{\prime}
, where
I^{\prime}
is any interval properly containing
I
.

Proof.

We will first find intervals for (a) and (b) separately. As the intervals are the same, the desired result follows.

(a)

Fix
x^{*}\geq 0
. Suppose that the sequence
\{f_{n}(x*)\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
converges, then using
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n+1}(x^{*})=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x^{*})
and the continuity of
\rho
, we obtain

1+\lambda\rho(\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x^{*}))=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x^{*}).

It follows that

\lambda=\frac{\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x^{*})-1}{\rho(\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x^{*}))}.

Thus, our goal is to find a
\lambda>0
and then a closed interval
I
satisfying (a). Since
\rho
is positive on
[0,\infty)
, the map
x\mapsto\frac{x-1}{\rho(x)}
is well-defined on
[0,\infty)
, negative when
x<1
and positive when
x>1
. For any positive number
\lambda<\sup_{x\geq 0}\frac{x-1}{\rho(x)}
we have an equation in
x

\frac{x-1}{\rho(x)}=\lambda\Longleftrightarrow\frac{1}{\lambda}(x-1)=\rho(x).

(8)

Since
\rho
has finite intersection with any linear function on
\mathbb{R}
, there are finitely many roots for the equation above, say
L_{1}<....<L_{n}
. Clearly,
L_{k}>1
for all
k
. We would like to show that
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x^{*})
exists whenever
x^{*}\in[0,L_{n}]
. For this, consider the following cases:

i)

x^{*}\in[L_{k},L_{k+1}]
for some
1\leq k<n
. If
\frac{x^{*}-1}{\rho(x^{*})}\leq\lambda
, then rearranging this inequality we obtain
x^{*}\leq 1+\lambda\rho(x^{*})
. Moreover, since
\rho
is non-decreasing,
\rho(x^{*})\leq\rho(L_{k+1})
and thus (because
L_{k+1}>1
)

1+\lambda\rho(x^{*})=1+\frac{L_{k+1}-1}{\rho(L_{k+1})}\rho(x^{*})\leq L_{k+1}.

Similarly, if
\frac{x^{*}-1}{\rho(x^{*})}\geq\lambda
, then
x^{*}\geq 1+\lambda\rho(x^{*})
and
1+\lambda\rho(x^{*})\geq L_{k}
. By repeating this argument, we can immediately see that the sequence
\{f_{n}(x^{*})\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is monotonic and thus converges. In fact, when
x^{*}\in(L_{k},L_{k+1})
, our definition of
L_{k}
’s implies that all the inequalities above are strict, and in particular

\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x^{*})=\left\{\begin{aligned} &L_{k+1},&\frac{x^{*}-1}{\rho(x^{*})}<\lambda\\
&L_{k},&\frac{x^{*}-1}{\rho(x^{*})}>\lambda\end{aligned}\right..

ii)

x^{*}\in(1,L_{1})
. Then by definition of
L_{1}
we must have
\frac{x^{*}-1}{\rho(x^{*})}<\lambda
. Argue in the same way as in i) we obtain
x^{*}<1+\lambda\rho(x^{*})
and
1+\lambda\rho(x^{*})<L_{1}
. Thus, by repeating this we see that
\{f_{n}(x^{*})\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subseteq(1,L_{1})
and is increasing. Therefore, it converges (to
L_{1}
).

iii)

x^{*}\in[0,1]
. In ii) we have shown that
\{f_{n}(x^{*})\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
converges whenever
x\in(1,L_{1}]
. Therefore, as
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is defined inductively, it is enough to show
1+\lambda\rho(x^{*})\in(1,L_{1}]
. Since
\lambda>0
and
\rho(x^{*})>0
, we clearly have
1+\lambda\rho(x^{*})>1
. On the other hand, since
\rho
is increasing,
\rho(x^{*})\leq\rho(L_{1})
and thus

1+\lambda\rho(x^{*})=1+\frac{L_{1}-1}{\rho(L_{1})}\rho(x^{*})\leq L_{1}.

This completes the proof.

Now further assume that
\rho
is strictly convex and continuously differentiable. Since
\rho^{\prime}
is strictly increasing and thus for some sufficiently small
\lambda
, the graph of
\frac{1}{\lambda}(x-1)
is tangent to the graph of
\rho
. For this
\lambda
, equation (
8
) has precisely one root, say
L
. Then by ii) and iii) above, for any
x^{*}\in[0,L]
, we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x^{*})=L
. We then show that
f_{n}(0)\leq f_{n}(x)
for all
x\in[0,L]
, so that the convergence of
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is uniform. This is clear for
n=1
. Assume it holds for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then

\displaystyle f_{n+1}(x)

\displaystyle=1+\lambda\rho(x)

\displaystyle\geq 1+\lambda\rho(L^{\prime})

\displaystyle=f_{n+1}(L^{\prime}).

This completes the induction step.

Therefore, given
\lambda
such that equation (
8
) has roots, the interval
I
for (a) can be
I=[0,L]
, where
L>1
is the largest root of (
3.6
).

(b)

Let
\lambda
and
L>1
be defined as in the last paragraph of the proof for (a). We would like to show that for any
x^{*}>L
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x^{*})=\infty
. Since
x=o(\rho(x))
as
x\to\infty
and since
L
is the largest root of equation (
8
), we must have
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x-1}{\rho(x)}=0
, which implies that
\frac{x-1}{\rho(x)}<\lambda
for all
x>L
. Equivalently, similar as in (a)-i),

1+\lambda\rho(x^{*})>x^{*}.

By repeating this argument we can see that
\{f_{n}(x^{*})\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is strictly increasing. Assume that its supremum is
c\in(L,\infty)
. Let

\delta:=\frac{L-1}{\rho(L)}-\sup_{x\geq(L+c)/2}\frac{x-1}{\rho(x)}

and let

\varepsilon:=\inf_{x\geq 0}\rho(x).

By the continuity of
\rho
, both
\delta
and
\varepsilon
are positive. For any large
n
with
f_{n}(x^{*})>\frac{L+c}{2}
and
|f_{n}(x^{*})-c|<\delta\varepsilon
we have

\displaystyle f_{n+1}(x^{*})-f_{n}(x^{*})

\displaystyle=1+\lambda\rho(f_{n}(x^{*}))-f_{n}(x^{*})

\displaystyle=\frac{L-1}{\rho(L)}\rho(f_{n}(x^{*}))-(f_{n}(x^{*})-1)

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{L-1}{\rho(L)}-\frac{f_{n}(x^{*})-1}{\rho(f_{n}(x^{*}))}\right]\rho(f_{n}(x^{*}))

\displaystyle\geq\delta\varepsilon,

It follows that
f_{n+1}(x^{*})>c
, a contradiction. Therefore,
\{f_{n}(x^{*})\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
must diverge to
\infty
as
n\to\infty
.

Now further assume that
\rho
is increasing on
[0,\infty)
.
Arguing in the same way as in (a), we can see that for any
L^{\prime}>L
,
f_{n}(x)\geq f_{n}(L^{\prime})
for all
x\geq L^{\prime}
. Since
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(L^{\prime})=\infty
,
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
must diverge to
\infty
uniformly on
[L^{\prime},\infty)
.

Therefore, the interval
I=[0,L]
(for prescribed
\lambda
) in (a) also works for (b).

∎

Corollary 3.2

(Analytic bump function)
.

Given any
\varepsilon>0
and
a,a^{\prime},b,b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
with
a^{\prime}<a<b<b^{\prime}
, there is a positive
\xi\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\left\|\xi-1\right\|_{\infty,[a,b]}<\varepsilon
,
\left\|\xi\right\|_{\infty,\mathbb{R}\setminus[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]}<\varepsilon
and
\left\|\xi\right\|_{\infty}<1+\varepsilon
. Moreover, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\xi
can be chosen such that

\xi^{(s)}(x)=o\left(\underbrace{\exp(-\exp(...\exp(|x|)...))}_{\text{$n$ ``$\exp$"'s}}\right)

as
x\to\pm\infty
, and

\left\|\xi^{(s)}\right\|_{\infty,[a,b]}<\varepsilon

for all
0\leq s\leq S
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.6
and the remark above, there is some
\rho:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
which is analytic, non-decreasing, strictly convex, and
x=o(\rho(x))
as
x\to\infty
. For this
\rho
there are some
\lambda
and some
L>1
such that

(a)

\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}=L
uniformly on
[-L,L]
.

(b)

\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x)=\infty
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-L,L]
, and
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
diverges to
\infty
uniformly on
\mathbb{R}\setminus I^{\prime}
, where
I^{\prime}
is any interval properly containing
[-L,L]
.

(c)

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
f_{n}
is bounded below by
f_{n}(0)
.

Here the sequence
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is defined as in Lemma
3.6
:

f_{1}(x)=1+\lambda\rho(x),\quad f_{n+1}(x)=1+\lambda\rho(f_{n}(x))\,\,\,\,\forall\,n\in\mathbb{N}.

Define a sequence of functions

g_{n}(x):=\frac{1}{L}f_{n}\left(\frac{b-a}{2L}x+a\right),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}

and then a sequence of functions

\xi_{n}(x):=\frac{1}{g_{n}(x)},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Clearly, each
g_{n}
is just obtained using a translation and a stretch from
f_{n}
. Thus, for
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
we have

(a)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\xi_{n}=1
uniformly on
[a,b]
.

(b)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\xi_{n}(x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[a,b]
, and
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
converges uniformly on
\mathbb{R}\setminus[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]
.

(c)

\xi_{n}>0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

This means for sufficiently large
n
we have, by setting
\xi:=\xi_{n}
, both
\left\|\xi-1\right\|_{\infty,[a,b]}<\varepsilon
and
\left\|\xi\right\|_{\infty,\mathbb{R}\setminus[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]}<\varepsilon
, proving the first part of the statement.

For the second part, we simply set
\rho(x)=e^{x^{2}}
. Then it is clear that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
s\in\mathbb{N}
,

\xi_{n}^{(s)}(x)=o\left(\underbrace{\exp(-\exp(...\exp(|x|)...))}_{\text{$n$ ``$\exp$"'s}}\right)

as
x\to\pm\infty
, and by Lemma
3.6
(b),
\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|\xi_{n}^{(s)}\right\|_{\infty,\mathbb{R}\setminus[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]}=0
. Then we prove that for any
s\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|\xi_{n}^{(s)}\right\|_{\infty,[a,b]}=0
. By looking at our construction, it suffices to prove that (see also example (b) above)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|f_{n}^{(s)}\right\|_{\infty,[-\tilde{x}^{*},\tilde{x}^{*}]}=0,\quad\tilde{x}^{*}\in[0,x^{*}],

where
x^{*}=\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{2}
. To illustrate this we only prove the case for
s=1
; the general case can be proved in a similar way. Note that each
f_{n}^{\prime}
is odd and increasing on
[0,\infty)
, because each
f_{n}
is even and

f_{n}^{\prime}=\lambda\rho^{\prime}(f_{n-1})\cdot...\cdot\lambda\rho^{\prime}.

Fix
\tilde{x}^{*}\in[0,x^{*}]
and assume that there is a sequence of indices
\{n_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
such that
\lim_{k\to\infty}f_{n_{k}}^{\prime}(\tilde{x}^{*})\geq C
for some
C>0
. Since each
f_{n_{k}}^{\prime}
is increasing on
[0,\infty)
, for any
x\in[\tilde{x}^{*},x^{*})
we must have
\lim_{k\to\infty}f_{n_{k}}^{\prime}(x)\geq C
as well. But then

\displaystyle\varliminf_{k\to\infty}f_{n_{k}}(x^{*})

\displaystyle\geq\varliminf_{k\to\infty}f_{n_{k}}(\tilde{x}^{*})+\varliminf_{k\to\infty}\int_{\tilde{x}^{*}}^{x^{*}}f_{n_{k}}^{\prime}(x)dx

\displaystyle=L+C(x^{*}-\tilde{x}^{*})>L,

contradicting
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x^{*})=L
by Lemma
3.6
. Therefore,
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}^{\prime}(\tilde{x}^{*})=0
. Since each
f_{n}^{\prime}
is odd and increasing on
[0,\infty)
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|f_{n}^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty,[-\tilde{x}^{*},\tilde{x}^{*}]}=0
and thus for sufficiently large
n
,
\left\|\xi_{n}^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty,[a,b]\cup\mathbb{R}\setminus[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]}<\varepsilon
must hold.

∎

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\sigma,\sigma_{0}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
such that there are some
S,n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
with

\sigma^{(s)}(x),\sigma_{0}^{(s)}(x)=o\left(\underbrace{\exp(\exp(...\exp(|x|)...))}_{\text{$n$ ``$\exp$"'s}}\right)

for all
s\in\{0,1,...,S\}
, as
x\to\pm\infty
. Given real numbers
a,a^{\prime},b,b^{\prime}
with
a^{\prime}<a<b<b^{\prime}
, for any
\varepsilon>0
there is an analytic function
\tilde{\sigma}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
having the following properties:

(a)

\left\|\tilde{\sigma}-\sigma\right\|_{S[a,b]}<\varepsilon
and
\left\|\tilde{\sigma}-\sigma_{0}\right\|_{S,\mathbb{R}\setminus[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]}<\varepsilon
for all
s\in\{0,1,...,S\}
.

(b)

Fix
s\in\{0,1,...,S\}
. If we further assume that
\left\|\sigma-\sigma_{0}\right\|_{S,[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]}<\varepsilon
, then there is some
C_{s}>0
such that
\left\|\sigma-\sigma_{0}\right\|_{S}<C_{s}\varepsilon
.

Proof.

(a)

By Corollary
3.2
, there is an analytic bump function
\xi\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\left\|\xi-1\right\|_{\infty,[a,b]}<\varepsilon^{\prime}
,
\left\|\xi\right\|_{\infty,\mathbb{R}\setminus[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]}<\varepsilon^{\prime}
and for any
s\in\{0,1,...,S\}
,
\left\|\xi^{(s)}\right\|_{\infty,[a,b]\cup\mathbb{R}\setminus[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]}<\varepsilon^{\prime}
and

\xi^{(s)}(x)=o\left(\underbrace{\exp(-\exp(...\exp(|x|)...))}_{\text{$n+1$ ``$\exp$"'s}}\right),

where
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
is a small number to be determined. Define
\tilde{\sigma}
by

\tilde{\sigma}(x):=\xi(x)\sigma(x)+(1-\xi(x))\sigma_{0}(x).

(9)

Fix
s\in\{0,1,...,S\}
. By inductively applying Leibniz rule for differentiation we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}^{(s)}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{s}\binom{s}{k}\xi^{(k)}(x)\sigma^{(s-k)}(x)+\sum_{k=1}^{s}\binom{s}{k}(1-\xi(x))^{(k)}\sigma_{0}^{(s-k)}(x)

\displaystyle=\xi(x)\sigma^{(s)}(x)+(1-\xi(x))\sigma_{0}^{(s)}(x)+\sum_{k=1}^{s}\binom{s}{k}\xi^{(k)}(x)\left[\sigma^{(s-k)}(x)-\sigma_{0}^{(s-k)}(x)\right].

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle|\tilde{\sigma}^{(s)}(x)-\sigma^{(s)}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq|1-\xi(x)||\sigma^{(s)}(x)-\sigma_{0}^{(s)}(x)|+\sum_{k=1}^{s}\binom{s}{k}\xi^{(k)}(x)|\sigma^{(s-k)}(x)-\sigma_{0}^{(s-k)}(x)|

\displaystyle|\tilde{\sigma}^{(s)}(x)-\sigma_{0}^{(s)}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\xi(x)|\sigma^{(s)}(x)-\sigma_{0}^{(s)}(x)|+\sum_{k=1}^{s}\binom{s}{k}\xi^{(k)}(x)|\sigma^{(s-k)}(x)-\sigma_{0}^{(s-k)}(x)|.

Therefore, by our construction of
\xi
, we have, for any
x\in[a,b]

\displaystyle|\tilde{\sigma}^{(s)}(x)-\sigma^{(s)}(x)|

\displaystyle<\varepsilon^{\prime}|\sigma^{(s)}(x)-\sigma_{0}^{(s)}(x)|+\varepsilon^{\prime}\sum_{k=1}^{s}\binom{s}{k}|\sigma^{(s-k)}(x)-\sigma_{0}^{(s-k)}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}\left\|\sigma-\sigma_{0}\right\|_{S,[a,b]}+\varepsilon^{\prime}2^{S}\left\|\sigma-\sigma_{0}\right\|_{S,[a,b]}.

Second, by hypothesis of
\sigma^{(s)},\sigma_{0}^{(s)}
and our construction of
\xi
there is some
R>0
such that whenever
|x|>R
,

\displaystyle|\tilde{\sigma}^{(s)}(x)-\sigma_{0}^{(s)}(x)|

\displaystyle<e^{-u}u+\sum_{k=1}^{s}\binom{s}{k}e^{-u}u

\displaystyle\leq(1+2^{S})e^{-u}u

\displaystyle<\varepsilon,

where

u:=\underbrace{\exp(\exp(...\exp(|x|)...))}_{\text{$n$ ``$\exp$"'s}},

and for any

,

\displaystyle|\tilde{\sigma}^{(s)}(x)-\sigma_{0}^{(s)}(x)|

\displaystyle<\varepsilon^{\prime}\left\|\sigma-\sigma_{0}\right\|_{S,[-R,R]}+\varepsilon^{\prime}2^{S}\left\|\sigma-\sigma_{0}\right\|_{S,[-R,R]}.

Thus, by setting
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon\left(S(1+2^{S})\left\|\sigma-\sigma\right\|_{S,[a,b]\cup[-R,R]}\right)^{-1}
, we obtain the desired estimates for both
\left\|\tilde{\sigma}-\sigma\right\|_{S,[a,b]}
and
\left\|\tilde{\sigma}-\sigma_{0}\right\|_{S,\mathbb{R}\setminus[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]}
.

(b)

Let
\xi
be an analytic bump function defined in (a) and
\tilde{\sigma}(x):=\xi(x)\sigma(x)+(1-\xi(x))\sigma_{0}(x)
. Fix
s\in\{0,1,...,S\}
. By (a), we need only investigate

for

\displaystyle|\sigma^{(s)}-\sigma_{0}^{(s)}|

\displaystyle\leq(1+2^{S})\left\|\xi\right\|_{S,[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]}\varepsilon.

This completes the proof.

4
Theory of General Neurons

This section investigates the linear independence / linear dependence of neurons with general structures, i.e., arbitrary layer and widths. As mentioned in Section
1
, this depends on both the parameters and choice of activation functions. We will first show the existence of activation function for which the set of parameters (which we denote by
\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}
, see Definition
4.1
) making an NN constant zero is “minimal”. In Corollary
4.1
, we analyze the geometric structure of
\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}
for given
\sigma
and network structure. In general, this set varies as the activation function
\sigma
and network structure changes; however, they still admit some geometries which we characterize. in Corollary
4.1
. We also notice that the requirement
\sigma(0)=0
offers us extra good structure of
\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}
, so we further discuss networks with such activation functions in Section
4.1
.

Recall that a fully-connected neural network
H
with network structure
\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
has the form

\displaystyle H(\theta,z)=H^{(L)}(\theta,z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{L-1}}a_{j}H^{(L-1)}(\theta,z)+b

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{L-1}}a_{j}\sigma\left(w_{j}^{(L-1)}H^{(L-2)}(\theta,z)+b_{j}^{(L-1)}\right)+b.

If we further assume that for some
m\leq m_{L-2}
,
H_{1}^{(L-2)}(\theta,\cdot),...,H_{m}^{(L-2)}(\theta,\cdot)
are the non-constant components of
H^{(L-2)}
, then we can rewrite each component of
H^{(L-1)}
as

H_{j}^{(L-1)}(\theta,z)=\sigma\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{jk}^{(L-1)}H_{k}^{(L-2)}(\theta,z)+\left[\sum_{k>m}w_{jk}^{(L-1)}H_{k}^{(L-2)}(\theta,z)+b_{j}^{(L-1)}\right]\right).

(10)

For more notations and definitions about neural networks, see Section
2
.

Theorem 4.1

(Linear independence of fully-connected neurons)
.

There is a
\sigma\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
with the following property: given
d,L\in\mathbb{N}
and
m_{1},...,m_{L}\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the fully-connected NN,
H
, with network structure
\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
. Then the generalized neurons
\{H_{k}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot)\}_{k=1}^{m_{L-1}}
are linearly independent if and only if

(a)

The vectors

\left(\left(w_{jk}^{(L-1)}\right)_{k=1}^{m},\sum_{k>m}w_{jk}^{(L-1)}H_{k}^{(L-2)}(\theta,z)+b_{j}^{(L-1)}\right),\quad 1\leq j\leq m_{L-1}

are distinct, and

(b)

We have
\left(w_{jk}^{(L-1)}\right)_{k=1}^{m}=0
for at most one
j\in\{1,...,m_{L-1}\}
. Moreover, for this
j
,
H_{j}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot)
is not constant zero.

Furthermore, the same result holds for NN without bias.

Proof.

For simplicity, for each
1\leq j\leq m_{L-1}
we introduce the notations

\tilde{w}_{j}=(\tilde{w}_{jk})_{k=1}^{m}:=\left(w_{jk}^{(L-1)}\right)_{k=1}^{m},\quad\tilde{b}_{j}:=\sum_{k>m}w_{jk}^{(L-1)}H_{k}^{(L-2)}(\theta,z)+b_{j}^{(L-1)}.

With these notations, we can write each generalized neuron
H_{j}^{(L-2)}
as

H_{j}^{(L-1)}(\theta,z)=\sigma\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}\tilde{w}_{jk}H_{k}^{(L-2)}(\theta,z)+\tilde{b}_{j}\right).

This clearly relates us to the theory about functions of ordered growth. In fact, we will use Proposition
3.3
to complete the proof, arguing in an inductive way. For this, consider any activation
\sigma\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
with
\sigma(x)=\Theta\left(\exp(e^{x^{5}})\right)
as
x\to\infty
and
\sigma(x)=\Theta\left(\exp(e^{-x^{3}})\right)
as
x\to-\infty
. Notice that
\sigma
has the following properties:

i)

\lim_{z\to\infty}\sigma(z)=\lim_{z\to-\infty}\sigma(z)=\infty
, both at hyper-exponential rate. Moreover, we also have
\lim_{z\to\infty}\log\sigma(z)=\lim_{z\to-\infty}\log\sigma(z)=\infty
.

ii)

Given
f_{1},f_{2}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
diverging to
\infty
of ordered growth such that
f_{2}(x)=o(f_{1}(x))
as
x\to\infty
, we have, for any
w>0
and
\tilde{w}<0
,
\sigma\left(we^{f_{2}(x)}\right)=o\left(\sigma\left(\tilde{w}e^{f_{1}(x)}\right)\right)
as
x\to\infty
. Indeed, by the construction of
\sigma
, there are
C_{1}^{+},C_{2}^{+},C_{1}^{-}C_{2}^{-}>0
such that

\displaystyle C_{1}^{+}\exp(e^{z^{5}})\leq

\displaystyle\,\sigma(z)\leq C_{2}^{+}\exp(e^{z^{5}})

\displaystyle C_{1}^{-}\exp(e^{-z^{3}})\leq

\displaystyle\,\sigma(z)\leq C_{2}^{-}\exp(e^{-z^{3}})

as
z\to\infty
and
z\to-\infty
, respectively. Therefore, as
x\to\infty

\displaystyle\sigma\left(we^{f_{2}(x)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}^{+}\exp\left(\exp\left(w^{5}e^{5f_{2}(x)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\ll C_{1}^{-}\exp\left(\exp\left(-\tilde{w}^{3}e^{3f_{1}(x)}\right)\right)\leq\sigma\left(\tilde{w}e^{f_{1}(x)}\right),

where we used
\lim_{x\to\infty}(3f_{1}(x)-5f_{2}(x))=\infty
and (a) to do the approximations.

First we show that given
(\tilde{w}_{j},\tilde{b}_{j})_{j=1}^{m_{L-1}}
satisfying (a) and (b) of this theorem, the generalized neurons
H_{1}^{(L-1)},...,H_{m_{L-1}}^{(L-1)}
are linearly independent. Without loss of generality, assume that
d=1
. The case for
L=1
is trivial. For
L=2
, given distinct
(w_{k},b_{k})_{k=1}^{m_{1}}
such that at most one of the
w_{k}
is
0
, say
w_{m_{1}}=0
. Then
\{\sigma(w_{k}x+b_{k})\}_{k=1}^{m_{1}-1}
all diverge to
\infty
and have ordered growth (along the real line), while
\sigma(w_{m_{1}}x+b_{m_{1}})\equiv\sigma(b_{m_{1}})
. To see this, fix any distinct
k_{1},k_{2}\in\{1,...,m_{1}-1\}
and consider the following cases:

i)

w_{k_{1}},w_{k_{2}}
are both positive or negative. If
w_{k_{1}}>w_{k_{2}}>0
then

\displaystyle\sigma(w_{k_{2}}x+b_{k_{2}})

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}^{+}\exp\left(e^{(w_{k_{2}}x+b_{k_{2}})^{5}}\right)

\displaystyle\ll C_{1}^{+}\exp\left(e^{(w_{k_{1}}x+b_{k_{1}})^{5}}\right)\leq\sigma(w_{k_{1}}x+b_{k_{1}}),

or
\sigma(w_{k_{2}}x+b_{k_{2}})=o\left(\sigma(w_{k_{1}}x+b_{k_{1}})\right)
as
x\to\infty
. If
w_{k_{1}}<w_{k_{2}}<0
, then

\displaystyle\sigma(w_{k_{2}}x+b_{k_{2}})

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}^{-}\exp\left(e^{-(w_{k_{2}}x+b_{k_{2}})^{3}}\right)

\displaystyle\ll C_{1}^{-}\exp\left(e^{-(w_{k_{1}}x+b_{k_{1}})^{3}}\right)\leq\sigma(w_{k_{1}}x+b_{k_{1}}),

or
\sigma(w_{k_{2}}x+b_{k_{2}})=o\left(\sigma(w_{k_{1}}x+b_{k_{1}})\right)
as
x\to\infty
. The same argument works for
w_{k_{2}}>w_{k_{1}}>0
and
w_{k_{2}}<w_{k_{1}}<0
.

ii)

w_{k_{1}},w_{k_{2}}
have different signs. If
w_{k_{1}}>0
and
w_{k_{2}}<0
, then

\displaystyle\sigma(w_{k_{2}}x+b_{k_{2}})

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}^{-}\exp\left(e^{-(w_{k_{2}}x+b_{k_{2}})^{3}}\right)

\displaystyle\ll C_{1}^{+}\exp\left(e^{(w_{k_{1}}x+b_{k_{1}})^{5}}\right)\leq\sigma(w_{k_{1}}x+b_{k_{1}}),

or
\sigma(w_{k_{2}}x+b_{k_{2}})=o\left(\sigma(w_{k_{1}}x+b_{k_{1}})\right)
as
x\to\infty
. The same argument works for
w_{k_{1}}<0
and
w_{k_{2}}>0
.

iii)

w_{k_{1}}=w_{k_{2}}
. Then we must have
b_{k_{1}}\neq b_{k_{2}}
. Again, using property (a) of
\sigma
above, we can easily deduce that these two neurons have ordered growth.

This proves the case for
L=1
-layer neurons. Moreover, using the same argument we can show that the functions
\{\log\left(\sigma(w_{k}x+b_{k})\right)\}_{k=1}^{m_{1}-1}
all diverge to
\infty
and have ordered growth.

Now consider
L\geq 3
. Suppose that
\log H_{1}^{(L-2)}(\theta,\cdot),...,\log H_{m}^{(L-2)}(\theta,\cdot)
all diverge to
\infty
and have ordered growth such that
\log H_{k}^{(L-2)}(\theta,\cdot)=o(\log H_{k-1}^{(L-2)}(\theta,\cdot))
for all
2\leq k\leq m
, and the functions
\{\log H_{k}^{(L-2)}(\theta,\cdot)\}_{k=m+1}^{m_{L-2}}
are constant. Assume that
(\tilde{w}_{j},\tilde{b}_{j})
’s satisfy requirement (a) and (b) above, in particular,
(\tilde{w}_{j},\tilde{b}_{j})
’s are distinct.

If
\tilde{w}_{j}
’s are all non-zero, then by applying property (b) of
\sigma
to
f_{1}=\log H_{1}^{(L-2)}(\theta,\cdot)
and
f_{2}=\log H_{m}^{(L-2)}(\theta,\cdot)
, we can see that both requirements (a) and (b) in Proposition
3.3
are satisfied, thus the
H_{j}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot)
’s,
1\leq j\leq m_{L-1}
all diverge to
\infty
and have ordered growth, and so are the
\log H_{j}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot)
’s. If there is one
\tilde{w}_{j}=0
, we may, by rearranging the indices if necessary, assume that
\tilde{w}_{m_{L-1}}=0
. Then
H_{m_{L-1}}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot)
and
\log H_{m_{L-1}}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot)
are constant functions, while by applying Proposition
3.3
we see that the
H_{j}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot)
’s,
1\leq j\leq m_{L-1}-1
all diverge to
\infty
and have ordered growth, and so are the
\log H_{j}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot)
’s. Then it follows from Proposition
3.1
(and the fact that at most one
H_{j}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot)
is constant) that
H_{j}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot),...,H_{m_{L-1}-1}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot)
are linearly independent.

Conversely, if
(\tilde{w}_{j},\tilde{b}_{j})_{j=1}^{m_{L-1}}
are not distinct, we clearly have two identical generalized neurons, and if there are distinct
j_{1},j_{2}\in\{1,...,m_{L-1}\}
such that
\tilde{w}_{j_{1}}=\tilde{w}_{j_{2}}=0

and

are both constant functions. Neither cases give linearly independent neurons. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

Several remarks on this result. First, note that if
\left(w_{j}^{(L-1)},b_{j}^{(L-1)}\right)_{j=1}^{m_{L-1}}
does not satisfy (a) or (b), then the
H_{j}^{(L-1)}
’s are
linearly dependent for all
\sigma
. Second, the choice of activation function can be extended to any
\sigma
with

\sigma(z)=\Theta\left(\exp(e^{z^{p}})\right),\quad\sigma(z)=\Theta\left(\exp(e^{-z^{q}})\right)

as
z\to\infty
and
z\to-\infty
, respectively, where
p,q>1
are distinct odd numbers. Finally, this theorem actually characterizes the parameters which yield linearly independent neurons in an inductive way. As we can see, requirement (a) and (b) of
w_{j}^{(L-1)}
’s and
b_{j}^{(L-1)}
’s depend on
m
, which is determined by the parameters of
H^{(L-2}
. Thus, to fully characterize the set of parameters which yield linearly independent neurons, we must then work with
H^{(L-2)}
, then
H^{(L-3)}
, …, by applying this theorem for
L-1
times. It is then clear that for both NN with and without bias, the structure of this set of parameters depends on activation function
\sigma
and network structure
\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
. One important and special case is
\sigma(0)=0
, as the set stabilizes over generic choices of such activations. To further our investigation of these topics, we introduce some notations below.

Definition 4.1

(Minimal zero set)
.

Given
d,L\in\mathbb{N}
and
m_{1},...,m_{L}\in\mathbb{N}
. Consider
H
(with or without bias) with network structure
\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
. Define a subset
\mathcal{Z}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L},\sigma)
of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that a point
\theta=((a_{j},b)_{j=1}^{m_{L-1}},\theta^{(L-1)},...,\theta^{(1)})\in\mathcal{Z}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L},\sigma)
if and only if

(a)

For any
1\leq l\leq L-2
,
\theta^{(l)}
is arbitrary.

(b)

For
l=L-1
, either
H_{k}^{(L-2)}(\theta,\cdot)=\text{const.}
for all
1\leq k\leq m_{L-2}
, or
\theta^{(L-1)}=(w_{j}^{(L-1)},b_{j}^{(L-1)})_{j=1}^{m_{L-1}}
does not satisfy requirement (a) or (b) of Theorem
4.1
.

(c)

By rearranging the indices of
H_{j}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot)
’s if necessary, we can find
0=n_{0}<n_{1}<...<n_{r}\leq m_{L-1}
and group them as

\displaystyle H_{n_{j-1}+1}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot)=...=H_{n_{j}}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot)\neq\text{const.},

\displaystyle\forall\,1\leq j\leq r,

\displaystyle H_{n_{j}}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot)\neq H_{n_{j+1}}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot),

\displaystyle\forall\,1\leq j<r,

\displaystyle H_{n_{r}+1}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot),...,H_{m_{L-1}}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot)=\text{const.}.

Then, after rearrangement of indices,
\sum_{t=n_{j-1}+1}^{n_{j}}a_{t}=0
for all
1\leq j\leq r
, and if
H
is an NN with bias we require

\sum_{t=n_{r}+1}^{m_{L-1}}a_{t}H_{t}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot)+b=0;

if
H
is an NN without bias we require

\sum_{t=n_{r}+1}^{m_{L-1}}a_{t}H_{t}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot)=0.

When the network structure or/and activation function is clear from context, then we simply write
\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma},\mathcal{Z}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L})
or just
\mathcal{Z}
, respectively.

Definition 4.2
.

Given
d,L\in\mathbb{N}
and
m_{1},...,m_{L}\in\mathbb{N}
. Consider
H
(with or without bias) with network structure
\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
. Define
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L})
as the set of parameters
\theta
such that
H(\theta,\cdot)\equiv 0
, i.e.,

\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L})=\bigcap_{z\in\mathbb{F}}H(\cdot,z)^{-1}(0).

For simplicity, we will write
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{\sigma}
or just
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
when the network structure or/and activation function is clear from context.

Remark 4.2
.

\mathcal{Z}
is “minimal” in the sense that for any
\sigma
and any
\theta\in\mathcal{Z}
,
H_{\sigma}(\theta,\cdot)
must be constant zero. Thus,
\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
. Theorem
4.1
says that there is some
\sigma\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\mathcal{Z}=\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
.

Corollary 4.1

(Structure of
\mathcal{Z}
)
.

Given
d,L\in\mathbb{N}
and
m_{1},...,m_{L}\in\mathbb{N}
. Consider any NN with network structure
\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
and activation
\sigma\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
. Given
\bar{\theta}^{(L-2)},...,\bar{\theta}^{(1)}
, the intersection of the set
E
with
\mathcal{Z}
, where

E:=\left\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\theta^{(l)}=\bar{\theta}^{(l)}\,\forall\,1\leq l\leq L-2\right\},

is a finite union of sets of the form
\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{V}
. Here
\mathcal{V}
is a finite union of linear spaces and
\mathcal{M}
a finite union of analytic submanifolds of
\mathbb{R}^{m_{L-1}+1}
. In particular,
\mathcal{Z}
is closed. The same holds for NN without bias, except that we may have countably many union of such sets.

Proof.

Given
\bar{\theta}^{(L-2)},...,\bar{\theta}^{(1)}
, there is some
0\leq m\leq L-2
such that
H_{k}^{(L-2)}(\theta,\cdot)
is non-constant for all
1\leq k\leq m
and
H_{k}^{(L-2)}(\theta,\cdot)
is constant for all
m<k\leq m_{L-2}
. Fix any
\theta=(\theta^{(l)})_{l=1}^{L}\in E\cap\mathcal{Z}
. For
\theta^{(L-1)}
we must be able to find distinct
j_{1},j_{2}\in\{1,...,m_{L-1}\}
such that

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\left(\left(w_{j_{1}k}^{(L-1)}\right)_{k=1}^{m},\sum_{k>m}w_{j_{1}k}^{(L-1)}H_{k}^{(L-2)}(\theta,z)+b_{j_{1}}^{(L-1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\left(w_{j_{2}k}^{(L-1)}\right)_{k=1}^{m},\sum_{k>m}w_{j_{2}k}^{(L-1)}H_{k}^{(L-2)}(\theta,z)+b_{j_{2}}^{(L-1)}\right)

or

\left(w_{j_{1}k}^{(L-1)}\right)_{k=1}^{m}=\left(w_{j_{2}k}^{(L-1)}\right)_{k=1}^{m}=0,

Therefore,
\theta^{(L-1)}=(w_{j}^{(L-1)},b_{j}^{(L-1)})_{j=1}^{m_{L-1}}
must be taken from the union of
2\binom{m_{L-1}}{2}
linear subspaces:

\displaystyle E^{\prime}=

\displaystyle\,\bigcup_{j_{1}\neq j_{2}}\left[\left\{\theta^{(L-1)}:\left(w_{j_{1}k}^{(L-1)}-w_{j_{1}k}^{(L-1)}\right)_{k=1}^{m}=0,\sum_{k>m}c_{k}(w_{j_{1}k}^{(L-1)}-w_{j_{2}k}^{(L-1)})+b_{j_{1}}^{(L-1)}-b_{j_{2}}^{(L-1)}=0\right\}\right.

\displaystyle\,\left.\cup\left\{\theta^{(L-1)}:(w_{j_{1}k})_{k=1}^{m}=(w_{j_{2}k})_{k=1}^{m}=0\right\}\right],

where
c_{k}=H_{k}^{(L-2)}(\theta,0)
(or actually
H_{k}^{(L-2)}(\theta,x)
for any
x
, because the neuron is constant) for every
m<k\leq m_{L-2}
. So in particular,
\theta^{(L-1)}\in\cup_{m=0}^{m_{L-2}}E^{\prime}
. Conversely, any
\theta
with
\theta^{(L-1)}\in\cup_{m=0}^{m_{L-2}}E^{\prime}
must be in
\mathcal{Z}
.

Now consider
l=L
. By Definition
4.1
, for any
\theta
with
\theta^{(L-1)}\in E^{\prime}
, there must be a partition
0=n_{0}<n_{1}<...<n_{r}\leq m_{L-1}
and a permutation
\pi
on
\{1,...,m_{L-1}\}
such that

\sum_{t=n_{j-1}+1}^{n_{j}}a_{\pi(t)}=0,\quad\forall\,1\leq j\leq r

(11)

and

b=b\left((a_{t})_{t=1}^{m_{L-1}},\theta^{(l)}\right)=-\sum_{t=n_{r}+1}^{m_{L-1}}c_{t}\left(\theta^{(l)}\right)a_{\pi(t)}

(12)

for some functions
\{c_{t}\}_{t=n_{r}+1}^{m_{L-1}}
determined by constant neurons among
\{H_{j}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot)\}_{j=1}^{m_{L-1}}
. Since the
c_{t}
’s are analytic on each linear subspaces in
E
, it follows that (for fixed
r
) equation (
12
) consists of a finite union
\mathcal{M}_{r}
of submanifolds in
\mathbb{R}^{m_{L-1}+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{L-1}(m_{L-2}+1)}
, by taking union over all permutations
\pi
and all appropriate (subsets of) linear spaces in
E^{\prime}
. Meanwhile, equations (
11
) implies that
(a_{j})_{j=1}^{n_{r}}
consists of a finite union
\mathcal{V}_{r}
of linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n_{r}}
, by taking union over all possible
(n_{0},...,n_{r})
’s. As we can see,
\mathcal{M}_{r}
and
\mathcal{V}_{r}
both depend only on
r
, whence

E\cap\mathcal{Z}=\cup_{r}\mathcal{M}_{r}\times\mathcal{V}_{r},

completing the proof for NN with bias.

The proof for NN with structure
\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
without bias is quite similar. Indeed, using the notations above, we now have

(a)

The set
E^{\prime}
is still a finite union of linear spaces, as

\displaystyle E^{\prime}=

\displaystyle\,\bigcup_{j_{1}\neq j_{2}}\left[\left\{\theta^{(L-1)}:\left(w_{j_{1}k}^{(L-1)}-w_{j_{1}k}^{(L-1)}\right)_{k=1}^{m}=0,\sum_{k>m}c_{k}(w_{j_{1}k}^{(L-1)}-w_{j_{2}k}^{(L-1)})=0\right\}\right.

\displaystyle\,\left.\cup\left\{\theta^{(L-1)}:(w_{j_{1}k})_{k=1}^{m}=(w_{j_{2}k})_{k=1}^{m}=0\right\}\right].

(b)

Equations (
11
) does not change.

(c)

Equation (
12
) becomes
\sum_{t=n_{r}+1}^{m_{L-1}}c_{t}\left(\theta^{(l)}\right)a_{t}=0
.

Suggested by the proof for NN with bias, our proof for NN without bias would be completed if we can show that
((a_{j},b)_{j=n_{r}+1}^{m_{L-1}},\theta^{(l)})
satisfying (c) consists of a countable union of analytic submanifolds in
\mathbb{R}^{m_{L-1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{L-1}m_{L-2}}
. By (c), the set of these parameters lies in the zero set of the analytic function
\sum_{t=n_{r}+1}^{m_{L-1}}c_{t}\left(\theta^{(l)}\right)a_{t}
, which, by Lemma
3.3
, is a countable union of analytic submanifolds in its domain, so we are done.

∎

To illustrate the structure of
\mathcal{Z}
, consider a three-neuron two-layer NN
H(\theta,x)=\sum_{k=1}^{3}a_{k}\sigma(w_{k}x+b_{k})+b
. With the notations in Corollary
4.1
, we have
E^{\prime}=E_{1}^{\prime}\cup E_{2}^{\prime}
, where

\displaystyle E_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\{(w_{1},b_{1})=(w_{2},b_{2})\}\cup\{(w_{1},b_{1})=(w_{3},b_{3})\}\cup\{(w_{2},b_{2})=(w_{3},b_{3})\}

\displaystyle E_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\{w_{1}=w_{2}=0\}\cup\{w_{1}=w_{3}=0\}\cup\{w_{2}=w_{3}=0\}.

Therefore,
E_{1}^{\prime},E_{2}^{\prime}
and thus
E^{\prime}
are finite union of linear spaces. If
\theta\in\mathcal{Z}
and
\theta^{(1)}=(w_{k},b_{k})_{k=1}^{3}\in\{(w_{1},b_{1})=(w_{2},b_{2})\}\setminus E_{2}^{\prime}
then we must have

a_{1}+a_{2}=0,\quad a_{3}=0,\quad b=0.

If
\theta^{(1)}\in\{w_{1}=w_{2}=0\}
then we must have

b=-a_{1}\sigma(b_{1})-a_{2}\sigma(b_{2}),\quad a_{3}=0

when
w_{3}\neq 0
, and

b=-a_{1}\sigma(b_{1})-a_{2}\sigma(b_{2})-a_{3}\sigma(b_{3})

when
w_{3}=0
. The other cases can be analyzed in a similar way. It follows that the
\mathcal{Z}
for this network is a finite union of sets of the form
\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{V}
.

The following result is just a restatement of Embedding Principle
Zhang et al. [2021]
,
Li et al. [2022]
using our notations. It gives a hierarchical description (in terms of NN width) of the parameter space, including
\mathcal{Z}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
.

Theorem 4.2

(Embedding Principle)
.

Given
d,L\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
m_{1},m_{1}^{\prime},...,m_{L},m_{L}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
m_{l}^{\prime}\leq m_{l}
for all
1\leq l\leq L
. Denote
N=\sum_{l=1}^{L}m_{l}m_{l-1}
and
N^{\prime}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}m_{l}^{\prime}m_{l-1}^{\prime}
. Denote
H,H^{\prime}
as the NN with network structure
\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
and
\{m_{l}^{\prime}\}_{l=1}^{L}
, respectively. Then there are
M:=\Pi_{l=1}^{L}\left[\binom{m_{l}-1}{m_{l}^{\prime}-1}!\right]
full-rank linear maps
\varphi_{1},...,\varphi_{M}
from
\mathbb{R}^{N^{\prime}}
to
\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that for any
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and any
1\leq i\leq M
,
H_{\sigma}^{\prime}=H_{\sigma}\circ(\varphi_{i},id)
. In particular,
\varphi_{i}(\mathcal{Z}(\{m_{l}^{\prime}\}_{l=1}^{L}))\subseteq\varphi_{i}(\mathcal{Z}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}))
and
\varphi_{i}(\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}(\{m_{l}^{\prime}\}_{l=1}^{L}))\subseteq\varphi_{i}(\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}(\{m_{l}\})_{l=1}^{L}))
.

Next we investigate the set of activation functions which do not satisfy
\mathcal{Z}=\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be real vector space of functions from
\mathbb{F}
to
\mathbb{F}
. Let
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{F})
be a subset of
\mathcal{F}
such that
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}
if and only if there is some
\theta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}
with
H_{\sigma}(\theta^{*},\cdot)\equiv 0
.

Proposition 4.1
.

The following results hold for
\mathcal{C}
as a subset of
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
.

(a)

For any
S\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
,
\overline{\mathcal{C}}\neq\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
, i.e.,
\mathcal{C}
is not dense in
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
, under the norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{S}
.

(b)

For any
S\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
,
\mathcal{C}
does not have an interior in
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
, under the local
S
-order uniform convergence.

(c)

Given
\sigma_{0}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
such that

\sigma_{0}(x)=o\left(\underbrace{\exp(\exp(...\exp(|x|)...))}_{\text{finite ``$\exp$"'s}}\right)

as
x\to\pm\infty
. For any
S\in\mathbb{N}
and any
\varepsilon,R>0
, there is a
\tilde{\sigma}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying
\left\|\tilde{\sigma}-\sigma_{0}\right\|_{S}<\varepsilon
and
H_{\tilde{\sigma}}(\theta,\cdot)
is not constant zero whenever
\theta\in B(0,R)
satisfies
\text{dist}\left({\theta},{\mathcal{Z}_{\tilde{\sigma}}}\right)\geq\varepsilon
.

Similar results hold for
\mathcal{C}
as a subset of
C^{S}(\mathbb{R})
with any
S\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0,\infty\}
.

Proof.

(a)

This follows immediately from the proof of Theorem
4.1
: for any activation
\sigma\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
with
\sigma(x)=\Theta\left(\exp(e^{x^{5}})\right)
as
x\to\infty
and
\sigma(x)=\Theta\left(\exp(e^{-x^{3}})\right)
as
x\to-\infty
,
H_{\sigma}(\theta,\cdot)
is constant zero if and only if
\theta\in\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}
.

(b)

Fix
\sigma_{0}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
. By Proposition
3.5
, for any
\varepsilon>0
and any
a,a^{\prime},b,b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
with
a^{\prime}<a<b<b^{\prime}
, there is a
\tilde{\sigma}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\left\|\tilde{\sigma}-\sigma_{0}\right\|_{S,[a,b]}<\varepsilon
and
\left\|\tilde{\sigma}-(\exp(e^{x^{5}})+\exp(e^{-x^{3}}))\right\|_{S,\mathbb{R}\setminus[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]}<\varepsilon
. In particular,
\tilde{\sigma}(x)=\Theta\left(\exp(e^{x^{5}})\right)
as
x\to\infty
and
\tilde{\sigma}(x)=\Theta\left(\exp(e^{-x^{3}})\right)
as
x\to-\infty
. Thus, by Theorem
4.1
,
H_{\sigma}(\theta,\cdot)
is constant zero if and only if
\theta\in\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}
. Equivalently,
\tilde{\sigma}\notin\mathcal{C}
. Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, the desired result follows.

(c)

Let
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
be a small number to be determined. By Proposition
3.5
, there is some
\sigma\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\left\|\sigma-\sigma_{0}\right\|_{\infty,[0,2]}<\varepsilon^{\prime}
and
\sigma(x)=\Theta(\exp(e^{x^{5}})+\exp(e^{-x^{3}}))
as
x\to\pm\infty
. By Theorem
4.1
,
H_{\sigma}(\theta,\cdot)
is constant zero if and only if
\theta\in\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}
. Fix this
\sigma
. By Proposition
3.5
again, by making
\left\|\sigma-\sigma_{0}\right\|_{\infty,[0,2]}
to be sufficiently small, there is some
\tilde{\sigma}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\left\|\tilde{\sigma}-\sigma\right\|_{S,[0,1]}<\varepsilon^{\prime}
and
\left\|\tilde{\sigma}-\sigma_{0}\right\|_{S}<\varepsilon^{\prime}
. In particular,
\left\|\tilde{\sigma}-\sigma\right\|_{\infty,[0,1]}<\varepsilon^{\prime}
. Now consider the integral function
\tau:\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
defined by

\tau(f,\theta):=\int_{0}^{1}H_{f}^{2}(\theta,x)dx.

Clearly,
\tau
is continuous in
\theta
, non-negative and
H_{f}(\theta,\cdot)\equiv 0
if and only if
\tau(f,\theta)=0
. Define

L:=\inf\left\{\tau(\sigma,\theta):\theta\in\overline{B(0,R)},\text{dist}\left({\theta},{\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}}\right)\geq\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon\right\}.

Similarly,
\mathcal{Z}_{f}
varies continuously with respect to
f
in the sense that for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\text{dist}\left({\mathcal{Z}_{f}},{\mathcal{Z}_{\tilde{f}}}\right):=\sup\inf_{\theta\in\mathcal{Z}_{f},\tilde{\theta}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\tilde{f}}}|\theta-\tilde{\theta}|<\varepsilon

when
\left\|f-\tilde{f}\right\|_{\infty,[0,1]}
is sufficiently small. Thus, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon^{\prime}\in(0,\varepsilon)
, we have both

\inf\left\{\tau(\tilde{\sigma},\theta):\theta\in\overline{B(0,R)},\text{dist}\left({\theta},{\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}}\right)\geq\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon\right\}\geq\frac{L}{2}>0

and
\text{dist}\left({\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}},{\mathcal{Z}_{\tilde{\sigma}}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon
.

We now show that this
\tilde{\sigma}
satisfies the requirements of (c). By construction, we already have
\left\|\tilde{\sigma}-\sigma_{0}\right\|_{S}<\varepsilon
. Let
\theta\in B(0,R)
be such that
\text{dist}\left({\theta},{\mathcal{Z}_{\tilde{\sigma}}}\right)\geq\varepsilon
. Assume that
\tau(\tilde{\sigma},\theta)=0
, then we must have
\text{dist}\left({\theta},{\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon
. Thus, there is some
\theta^{*}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}
with
|\theta-\theta^{*}|\leq\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon
. Fix this
\theta^{*}
, since
\text{dist}\left({\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}},{\mathcal{Z}_{\tilde{\sigma}}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon
, there is some
\tilde{\theta}^{*}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\tilde{\sigma}}
with
|\theta^{*}-\tilde{\theta}^{*}|\leq\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon
. But then

\text{dist}\left({\theta},{\mathcal{Z}_{\tilde{\sigma}}}\right)\leq|\theta-\tilde{\theta}^{*}|\leq|\theta-\theta^{*}|+|\theta^{*}-\tilde{\theta}^{*}|\leq\frac{2}{3}\varepsilon,

contradicting our assumption that
\text{dist}\left({\theta},{\mathcal{Z}_{\tilde{\sigma}}}\right)\geq\varepsilon
. It follows that
\tau(\tilde{\sigma},\theta)=0
.

∎

4.1
\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}
for Activations Vanishing at Origin

In this part we study
\mathcal{Z}
for NN without bias and with activation
\sigma\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\sigma(0)=0
. First we show that for definable activation functions with respect to the o-minimal structure generated by
\exp(\cdot)
(and vanishing at the origin),
\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}
is just a finite union of linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. This can be seen both as a generalization of linear independence of two-layer neurons and as a converse to Embedding Principle (Theorem
4.2
). Then we show in Proposition
4.2
that up to arbitrarily small perturbation of any
\sigma\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
with
\sigma(0)=0
, we have
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{\sigma}\cap\overline{B(0,R)}=\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}\cap\overline{B(0,R)}
, and furthermore
\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}\cap\overline{B(0,R)}
is just a finite union of linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
intersecting
\overline{B(0,R)}
. Therefore, by these two results, we can see that the requirement
\sigma(0)=0
generically yields the simplest possible geometry of
\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}
(and
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{\sigma}
), and thus gives the simplest possible characterization of parameters
\theta
such that the neurons are linearly independent.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
H
be an NN with network structure
\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
and without bias. For
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\sigma(0)=0
, we have
H_{\sigma}^{(l)}(\theta,0)=0
for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. In particular,
H_{\sigma}^{(l)}(\theta,\cdot)
is constant if and only if it is constant zero (note that in the formulas above we are abusing the notation
H^{(l)}(\theta^{(l)},...,\theta^{(1)},\cdot)=H^{(l)}(\theta,\cdot)
, see remark
2.2
).

Proof.

For the first part, we prove by induction. Indeed, this is obvious for
l=1
. Let
l\geq 2
and assume that this holds for
H_{1}^{(l-1)}(\theta,\cdot),...,H_{m_{l-1}}^{(l-1)}(\theta,\cdot)
. Then

H_{k}^{(l)}(\theta,0)=\sigma\left(w_{k}^{(l)}H^{(l-1)}(\theta,0)\right)=\sigma(0)=0,

completing the induction step. It follows that for any
1\leq l\leq L-1
and any
1\leq k\leq m_{l}
,
H_{k}^{(l)}
is constant if and only if it is constant zero.

Now assume that
H_{\sigma}^{(l)}(\theta,\cdot)
is constant. Then for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
H_{\sigma}^{(l)}(\theta,x)=H_{\sigma}^{(l)}(\theta,0)=0
. Therefore,
H_{\sigma}^{(l)}(\theta,\cdot)
is constant if and only if it is constant zero.

∎

Lemma 4.2
.

There is some
\sigma\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
such that the following results hold.

(a)

\sigma(0)=0
.

(b)

Consider an NN with network structure
\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
without bias. For any parameter
\theta=(m_{l})_{l=1}^{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
\{H_{k}^{(L-2)}(\theta,\cdot)\}_{k=1}^{m_{L-2}}
are distinct and not constant zero, then
\{H^{(L-1)}_{j}(\theta,\cdot)\}_{j=1}^{m_{L-1}}
are linearly independent if and only if
w_{j}^{(L-1)}\neq 0
for all
j\in\{1,...,m\}
, and
w_{j_{1}}^{(L-1)}\neq w_{j_{2}}^{(L-1)}
for all distinct
j_{1},j_{2}\in\{1,...,m_{L-1}\}
.

(c)

Consider
H
with network structure
\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
without bias. For any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, there are
\tilde{m}_{1},...,\tilde{m}_{L}\in\mathbb{N}
, each
\tilde{m}_{l}\leq m_{l}
, together with a unique
\tilde{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{\sum_{l=1}^{L}\tilde{m}_{l}\tilde{m}_{l-1}}
, such that
\tilde{H}^{(l)}(\tilde{\theta},\cdot)=H^{(l)}(\tilde{\theta},\cdot)
for all
l\in\{1,...,L\}
.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{\sigma}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
satisfy the requirements in Theorem
4.1
. Define

\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\quad\sigma(z)=\tilde{\sigma}(z)-\sigma(0).

Then clearly
\sigma(0)=0
and
\sigma(z)=\Theta(\tilde{\sigma}(z))
as
z\to\pm\infty
. In particular,
\lim_{z\to\infty}\sigma(z)=\lim_{z\to-\infty}\sigma(z)=\infty
at hyper-exponential rate (actually hyper-polynomial rate growth is enough, but hyper-exponential rate growth implies hyper-polynomial rate growth),
\sigma(z)=\Theta(\exp(e^{z^{5}}))
as
z\to\infty
and
\sigma(z)=\Theta(\exp(e^{-z^{3}}))
as
z\to-\infty
.

First we prove (b). Note that (with the notation in Theorem
4.1
)
m=m_{L-2}
, because Lemma
4.1
says
H_{k}^{(L-2)}(\theta,\cdot)
’s are non-zero implies that they are not constant. By the proof of Theorem
4.1
,
\{H^{(L-1)}_{j}(\theta,\cdot)\}_{j=1}^{m_{L-1}}
are linearly independent if and only if

(a)

The vectors
w_{j}^{(L-1)}
,
1\leq j\leq m_{L-1}
are distinct.

(b)

We have
w_{j}^{(L-1)}=0
for at most one
j
. Moreover, for this
j
,
H_{j}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot)
is not constant zero.

However, when
w_{j}^{(L-1)}=0
,
H_{j}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot)
is constant; by Lemma
4.1
it must be constant zero. Thus,
\{H^{(L-1)}_{j}(\theta,\cdot)\}_{j=1}^{m_{L-1}}
are linearly independent if and only if
w_{j}^{(L-1)}
are distinct and non-zero.
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

There is some
\sigma\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
such that for any NN without bias, with network structure
\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
and activation
\sigma
, the minimal zero set coincides with
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{Z}=\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{Z}
is a finite union of linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma
be any activation satisfying the requirements in Lemma
4.2
. In particular,
\sigma(0)=0
,
\lim_{z\to\infty}\sigma(z)=\lim_{z\to-\infty}\sigma(z)=\infty
at hyper-exponential rate,
\sigma(z)=\Theta(\exp(e^{z^{5}}))
as
z\to\infty
and
\sigma(z)=\Theta(\exp(e^{-z^{3}}))
as
z\to-\infty
. Since
\sigma
satisfies Theorem
4.1
, we immediately have
\mathcal{Z}=\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
.

Then we show that for any
1\leq l\leq L-1
and
0=n_{0}<n_{1}<...<n_{r}\leq m_{L-1}
, the set of parameters
\theta^{(l)},...,\theta^{(1)}
such that up to a rearrangement of the indices,

\displaystyle H_{n_{j-1}+1}^{(l)}(\theta,\cdot)=...=H_{n_{j}}^{(l)}(\theta,\cdot)\neq\text{const.}

\displaystyle\forall\,1\leq j\leq r,

(13)

\displaystyle H_{n_{j}}^{(l)}(\theta,\cdot)\neq H_{n_{j}+1}^{(l)}(\theta,\cdot)

\displaystyle\forall\,1\leq j<r,

\displaystyle H_{n_{r}+1}^{(l)}(\theta,\cdot)=...=H_{m_{l-1}}^{(l)}(\theta,\cdot)\equiv 0

is a finite union of linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{l}m_{l^{\prime}}m_{l^{\prime}-1}}
. To prove this, first recall that by Theorem
4.1
and our proof above, the neurons
\{\sigma(w_{k}^{(1)}x)\}_{k=1}^{m_{1}}
are linearly independent if and only if the
w_{k}^{(1)}
’s are distinct and non-zero. Thus, they can be classified as in (
13
) if and only if there are exactly
r
distinct, non-zero weights
w_{n_{1}}^{(1)},...,w_{n_{r}}^{(1)}
, each one repeating for a fixed number of times, and there are exactly
m_{1}-n_{r}
weights equal to zero. Thus, for fixed
0=n_{0}<n_{1}<...<n_{r}\leq m_{1}
, the closure of the set of
\theta^{(1)}=(w_{k}^{(1)})_{k=1}^{m_{1}}
satisfying (
13
) is a finite union of linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}d}
.

Now consider
2\leq l\leq L-1
. Assume that this holds for
l-1
. Fix
\theta^{(l-1)},...,\theta^{(1)}
. Let
0=n_{0}<...<n_{r}\leq m_{l-1}
work for the
H_{k}^{(l-1)}(\theta,\cdot)
’s. Up to a rearrangement of indices, we can write each
H_{j}^{(l)}
as

H_{j}^{(l)}(\theta,z)=\sigma\left(\sum_{t=1}^{r}\left(\sum_{k=n_{t-1}+1}^{n_{t}}w_{jk}^{(l)}\right)H_{n_{k}}^{(l-1)}(\theta,z)\right).

By Theorem
4.1
again, these generalized neurons can be classified as in (
13
) if and only if for some
r^{\prime}\leq m_{l}
, there are exactly
r^{\prime}
distinct, non-zero “combined weights”

\left(\sum_{k=n_{t-1}+1}^{n_{t}}w_{n_{1}^{\prime}k}^{(l)}\right)_{t=1}^{r},...,\left(\sum_{k=n_{t-1}+1}^{n_{t}}w_{n_{r^{\prime}}^{\prime}k}^{(l)}\right)_{t=1}^{r},

each one repeating for a fixed number of times, and there are exactly
m_{l}-n_{r^{\prime}}^{\prime}
“combined weights” equal to zero. Thus, for fixed
0=n_{0}^{\prime}<n_{1}^{\prime}<...<n_{r^{\prime}}^{\prime}\leq m_{1}
, the closure of the set of
\theta^{(l)}=(w_{j}^{(l)})_{j=1}^{m_{l}}
satisfying (
13
) is a finite union of linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{m_{l}m_{l-1}}
. Finally, by Definition
4.1
, given
\theta^{(L-1)},...,\theta^{(1)}
, the closure of the set of
\theta^{(L)}=(a_{j})_{j=1}^{m_{L-1}}
is a finite union of linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{m_{L-1}}
.

From this we can see that
\mathcal{Z}
is a finite union of some sets
V_{1},...,V_{n}
whose closure are linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Since
\mathcal{Z}
is closed, it follows that

\mathcal{Z}=\overline{\mathcal{Z}}=\overline{V_{1}}\cup...\overline{V_{n}},

so that
\mathcal{Z}
is a finite union of linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

As we can see from the proof of Theorem
4.3
, when
\sigma
satisfies the hypotheses in this theorem,
\mathcal{Z}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L},\sigma)
is independent of
\sigma
– it depends only on the network structure
\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
. This is different from the general case proved in Theorem
4.1
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Consider
L\geq 2
and the network structure
\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
. Given
S\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon,M>0
. For any
\sigma_{0}\in C^{S}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\sigma_{0}(0)=0
and

\sigma_{0}(x)=o\left(\underbrace{\exp(\exp(...\exp(|x|)...))}_{\text{finite ``$\exp$"'s}}\right)

as
x\to\pm\infty
, there is some
\tilde{\sigma}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
with
\left\|\tilde{\sigma}-\sigma_{0}\right\|_{S}<\varepsilon
, and
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{\tilde{\sigma}}=\mathcal{Z}_{\tilde{\sigma}}
.

Proof.

Let
\mu>S
be an integer to be determined later. Given
\sigma\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
with the following two properties:

(a)

It satisfies the hypotheses in Theorem
4.3
.

(b)

It is obtained by algebraic operations of exponential function and polynomials.

Therefore, we can apply Proposition
3.5
and/or
4.1
to find some
\tilde{\sigma}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\left\|\tilde{\sigma}-\sigma\right\|_{\mu,[0,1]}<\varepsilon
,
\left\|\tilde{\sigma}-\sigma\right\|_{\mu}<\varepsilon
and
\tilde{\sigma}(x)-\sigma(x)=O(x^{\mu})
as
x\to 0
. We will prove that for sufficiently large
\mu
, we have
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{\tilde{\sigma}}\cap\overline{B(0,R)}=\mathcal{Z}_{\tilde{\sigma}}\cap\overline{B(0,R)}=\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}\cap\overline{B(0,R)}
.

By property (b) of
\sigma
,
\sigma
and its derivatives
\sigma
, and thus
H_{\sigma}
and its derivatives are all definable functions. By Corollary
3.1
, for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}
, there is some
M\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{Z}
, there are some
s\in\{0,1,...,M\}
and
c_{s}(\theta)\neq 0
with
H_{\sigma}(\theta,x)\sim c_{s}(\theta)x^{s}
as
x\to 0
. Thus, if
\theta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}
satisfies
H_{\tilde{\sigma}}(\theta^{*},\cdot)\equiv 0
, we must have

\displaystyle|H_{\tilde{\sigma}}(\theta^{*},x)-H_{\sigma}(\theta^{*},x)|

\displaystyle=|H_{\sigma}(\theta^{*},x)|

(14)

\displaystyle\sim|c_{s}(\theta^{*})||x|^{s}

\displaystyle=\Omega(x^{M}),

where
s\in\{0,1,...,M\}
and
c_{s}(\theta^{*})\neq 0
.

We then show by induction that under our construction of
\tilde{\sigma}
, we have
|H_{\tilde{\sigma}}(\theta,x)-H_{\sigma}(\theta,x)|=O(x^{\mu})
as
x\to 0
. Indeed, for any
w\in\mathbb{R}
we have

|\tilde{\sigma}(wx)-\sigma(wx)|\leq C|wx|^{\mu}=C|w|^{\mu}|x|^{\mu},

where
C\geq 0
is determined by
\tilde{\sigma}
,
\sigma
, but not
w
, because we can always make
x
small enough so that
wx
is bounded by a given constant, say
|wx|\in[0,1]
. Thus, for
L=2
we obtain the following estimate for
\theta=(a_{k},w_{k})_{k=1}^{m_{1}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)m_{1}}
:

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{k=1}^{m_{1}}a_{k}\tilde{\sigma}(w_{k}x)-\sum_{k=1}^{m_{1}}a_{k}\sigma(w_{k}x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m_{1}}|a_{k}||\tilde{\sigma}(w_{k}x)-\sigma(w_{k}x)|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m_{1}}|a_{k}||w_{k}|^{\mu}\right)|x|^{\mu}

\displaystyle\leq Cm_{1}R^{\mu+1}|x|^{\mu}.

Thus, the desired result holds for two-layer networks
H_{\tilde{\sigma}}^{(2)}
and
H_{\sigma}^{(2)}
. Now suppose that the result holds for each component of
H_{\tilde{\sigma}}^{(L-2)}
and
H_{\sigma}^{(L-2)}
, so given
\theta
there is some
C^{\prime}>0
with
|H_{\tilde{\sigma}}^{(L-2)}(\theta,x)-H_{\sigma}^{(L-2)}(\theta,x)|\leq C^{\prime}|x|^{\mu}
for all sufficiently small
x\in\mathbb{R}
. For the components of
H_{\tilde{\sigma}}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot)
and
H_{\sigma}^{(L-1)}(\theta,\cdot)
we do the following estimate:

\displaystyle|H_{\tilde{\sigma},j}^{(L-1)}(\theta,x)-H_{\sigma,j}^{(L-1)}(\theta,x)|

\displaystyle=\left|\tilde{\sigma}\left(w_{j}^{(L-1)}H_{\tilde{\sigma}}^{(L-2)}(\theta,x)\right)-\sigma\left(w_{j}^{(L-1)}H_{\sigma}^{(L-2)}(\theta,x)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\tilde{\sigma}\left(w_{j}^{(L-1)}H_{\tilde{\sigma}}^{(L-2)}(\theta,x)\right)-\tilde{\sigma}\left(w_{j}^{(L-1)}H_{\sigma}^{(L-2)}(\theta,x)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\,\,+\left|\tilde{\sigma}\left(w_{j}^{(L-1)}H_{\sigma}^{(L-2)}(\theta,x)\right)-\sigma\left(w_{j}^{(L-1)}H_{\sigma}^{(L-2)}(\theta,x)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\tilde{\sigma}^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty,[0,1]}|w_{j}^{(L-1)}||H_{\tilde{\sigma}}^{(L-2)}(\theta,x)-H_{\sigma}^{(L-2)}(\theta,x)|

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\,\,+C|w_{j}^{(L-1)}|^{\mu}|H_{\sigma}^{(L-2)}(\theta,x)|^{\mu}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\tilde{\sigma}^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty,[0,1]}RC^{\prime}|x|^{\mu}+CR\sum_{k=1}^{m_{L-2}}\left\|\frac{d}{dx}H_{\sigma,k}^{(L-2)}(\theta,\cdot)\right\|_{\infty,[0,1]}^{\mu}|x|^{\mu}.

Here
x
is so small that it satisfies

x\in[0,1],\quad w_{j}^{(L-1)}H_{\tilde{\sigma}}^{(L-2)}(\theta,x)\in[0,1],\quad w_{j}^{(L-1)}H_{\sigma}^{(L-2)}(\theta,x)\in[0,1].

Moreover, in the estimate above, for the second inequality we use the induction hypothesis on
H_{\tilde{\sigma}}^{(L-2)}
and
H_{\sigma}^{(L-2)}
, and for the second and third inequality we use Lipschitz property of
\sigma
and the
H_{\sigma,k}^{(L-2)}
’s, respectively. Thus, we have
|H_{\tilde{\sigma},j}^{(L-1)}(\theta,x)-H_{\sigma,j}^{(L-1)}(\theta,x)|=O(x^{\mu})
as
x\to 0
for every
1\leq j\leq m_{L-1}
. It follows that

\left|H_{\tilde{\sigma}}(\theta,x)-H_{\sigma}(\theta,x)\right|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m_{L-1}}|a_{k}||H_{\tilde{\sigma},j}^{(L-1)}(\theta,x)-H_{\sigma,j}^{(L-1)}(\theta,x)|=O(x^{\mu}).

as
x\to 0
, showing the claim above.

Combining this estimate with (
14
), we can see that

\Omega(x^{M})=|H_{\tilde{\sigma}}(\theta^{*},x)-H_{\sigma}(\theta^{*},x)|=O(x^{\mu})

as
x\to 0
. But this gives a contradiction whenever
\mu>M
. Thus,
H_{\tilde{\sigma}}(\theta^{*},\cdot)
cannot be constant zero. Since this holds for any
\theta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}
, we must have
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{\tilde{\sigma}}=\mathcal{Z}_{\tilde{\sigma}}=\mathcal{Z}_{\sigma}
, completing the proof.

∎

5
Misc

The purpose of this section is to discuss linear independence of neurons with special structures, especially those two- and three-layer neurons. As the methods we use here are often different from that for proving general cases in Section
4
, one may read this part by skipping Section
4
.

The structure for this section is as follows. First, in Section
5.1
, we show that while generic activation functions (see Definition
5.1
) implies that for two-layer neurons without bias,
\mathcal{Z}
is completely characterized by the simplest possible cases (
1
) in Section
1
. In particular, for any
m,d\in\mathbb{N}
,

\{\sigma(w_{k}x):w_{k},x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}_{k=1}^{m}

(15)

are linearly independent if and only if
w_{k}
’s are distinct. Unfortunately, it does not provide such simple structure of
\mathcal{Z}
for general neurons (e.g., multi-layer ones and even two-layer neurons with bias). Thus, it is natural to ask for what choice of
\sigma
do we have this, or at least similar results. For neurons without bias, this is addressed by perturbing any
\sigma\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
with
\sigma(0)=0
, as shown in Section
4.1
. Section
5.2
discusses this problem for two-layer neurons with bias. In particular, we identify three cases in which
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
is completely characterized by the simplest possible cases (
1
).

Finally, we discuss the linear independence of neurons with Sigmoid and Tanh activations for three-layer neurons with bias, i.e., activation functions
\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}
or
\tanh(z)=\frac{e^{x}-e^{-x}}{1+e^{-x}}
.

5.1
Generic Activations are not Enough

Recall the definition of generic activations often used in the analysis of two-layer neural networks.

Definition 5.1

(Generic activation)
.

A generic activation
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function (
\sigma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
) such that
\sigma(0)\neq 0
and its derivatives satisfy
\sigma^{(s)}(0)\neq 0
for infinitely many even and odd numbers
s\in\mathbb{N}
.

The definition of generic activation differs slightly among different works. The definition here follows Simsek’s version
[
Simsek et al., 2021
]
. Alternatively, some works further require that
\sigma
is analytic, such as
Luo et al. [2023]
,
Sun et al. [2020]
. However, their proof still only uses
C^{\infty}
property; the analyticity is mainly used in the analysis of loss landscapes.

The following results give a complete characterization of two-layer neurons without bias and with generic activation. A proof can be found in
Luo et al. [2023]
.

Proposition 5.1

(Corollary 3.1 from
Luo et al. [2023]
)
.

Let
m,d\in\mathbb{N}
. Given a generic activation
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, then for any
w_{1},...,w_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the (two-layer) neurons
\{\sigma(w_{k}z)\}_{k=1}^{m}
are linearly independent if and only if
w_{j}\neq w_{k}
for all distinct
j,k\in\{1,...,m\}
.

Example.
We now give examples to show that with generic activations,
\mathcal{Z}
for either two-layer neurons with bias or for multi-layer neurons without bias could still have very complicated structures. In the following example,
\sigma
always denotes a generic activation.

(a)

(two-layer neurons with bias) Consider
\sigma(z)=e^{z}
. Then for any
w\in\mathbb{R}
and
b,b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma(wz+b)
and
\sigma(wz+b^{\prime})
are linearly dependent.

(b)

(two-layer neurons with bias) Consider
\sigma(z)=\tanh(z+1)
. Then for any
w\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma(wz-1)
and
\sigma(-wz-1)
are linearly dependent.

(c)

(two-layer neurons with bias) Consider
\sigma(z)=\cos(z)+\sin(z)
. Then for any
w\in\mathbb{R}
and any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\sigma(wz)
and
\sigma(wz+2k\pi)
are linearly dependent. Similarly, it is not difficult to construct a smooth function
\sigma
such that
\sigma(wz)=\sigma(wz+b)+b^{\prime}
for given
w,b,b^{\prime}\neq 0
and all
z\in\mathbb{R}
, but then
\sigma(wz)-\sigma(wz+b)-b^{\prime}
is constant zero.

(d)

(multi-layer neurons without bias) Consider
\sigma(z)=\tanh(z+1)
again. Then for real numbers
w_{1}^{(2)},w_{2}^{(2)}
and
w^{(1)}
, the three-layer neurons

\displaystyle\sigma\left(w_{1}^{(2)}\sigma(w^{(1)}z)+w_{2}^{(2)}\sigma(0)\right),\sigma\left(-w_{1}^{(2)}\sigma(w^{(1)}z)+w_{2}^{(2)}\sigma(0)\right)

are linearly dependent provided that
w_{2}^{(2)}=-\frac{1}{\tanh(1)}
. Using the same idea we can construct similar examples for general multi-layer neural networks without bias, when
\sigma(z)=\tanh(z+1)
.

5.2
Two-layer Neurons With Bias

Definition 5.2

(Fourier transform)
.

Let
\mathscr{F}
denote the Schwartz class of functions on
\mathbb{R}
, namely,
f\in\mathscr{F}
if for any
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
we have
\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}|x^{s^{\prime}}f^{(s)}(z)|<\infty
. For any
f\in\mathscr{F}
the Fourier transform of
f
is defined as

\hat{f}(\xi)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(z)e^{-i\xi z}dz.

In particular, we will use
\xi
to denote the frequency-domain variable.

Given
w_{1},...,w_{m}>0
, the following result can be used to distinguish Schwartz functions
\{f(w_{k}z)\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
by investigating the decay rates of their Fourier transforms.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
f\in\mathscr{F}
be non-constant zero. Then its Fourier transform
\hat{f}
has the property that for any
0<\tilde{w}<w
,
\hat{f}(w\xi)=o(\hat{f}(\tilde{w}\xi))
as
\xi\to\infty
.

Proof.

Note that
f\in\mathscr{F}
implies
\left\|\xi^{s}f^{\hat{}}\right\|_{\infty}<\infty
for all
s\in\mathbb{N}
(
cite Proposition 2.2.11 in Grafakos’ book
). In other words, for any
s\in\mathbb{N}
, there is some
T>0
such that
|f^{\hat{}}(\xi)|=O(\xi^{-s})
as
\xi\to\infty
. We then show that this implies

\frac{1}{\hat{f}(\tilde{w}\xi)}=o\left(\frac{1}{\hat{f}(w\xi)}\right),

(16)

so that the desired result follows.

For simplicity, let
g:=\frac{1}{\hat{f}}
and by a substitution of variables if necessary, we can assume that
\tilde{w}=1
and
w>1
. Assume that (
16
) does not hold. Then there is a sequence
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}\xi_{n}=\infty
, and there is some
M>0
such that
0<|g(w\xi_{n})|\leq M|g(\xi_{n})|
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. It follows that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
\xi_{n}\in[w^{k-1},w^{k})
for some
k
we have

\displaystyle 0<|g(w\xi_{n})|\leq M|g(\xi_{n})|\leq...\leq M^{k}\left|g\left(w^{-k}\xi_{n}\right)\right|.

Note that
w^{-k}\xi_{n}\in[1,w)
, whence

\displaystyle|g(w\xi_{n})|

\displaystyle\leq M^{k}\left\|g\right\|_{\infty,[1,w]}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|g\right\|_{\infty,[1,w]}(w^{\log_{w}M})^{k}

\displaystyle=\left\|g\right\|_{\infty,[1,w]}(w^{k})^{\log_{w}M}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|g\right\|_{\infty,[1,w]}(w\xi_{n})^{\log_{w}M},

Let
s:=\log_{w}M
. Then for any
s^{\prime}>s
,

\displaystyle\left|\hat{f}(w\xi_{n})(w\xi_{n})^{s^{\prime}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{(w\xi_{n})^{s+(s^{\prime}-s)}}{g(w\xi_{n})}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{|w^{k}|^{s}}{\left\|g\right\|}_{\infty,[1,w]}|w\xi_{n}|^{s^{\prime}-s}

\displaystyle=\frac{M^{k}}{\left\|g(w\xi_{n})\right\|_{\infty,[1,w]}}|w\xi_{n}|^{s^{\prime}-s},

and thus
\lim_{n\to\infty}|\hat{f}(w\xi_{n})(w\xi_{n})^{s^{\prime}}|=\infty
, contradicting the fact that
\left\|x^{s^{\prime}}\hat{f}\right\|_{\infty}<\infty
.
∎

Remark 5.1
.

As a result, given
m\in\mathbb{N}
and (strictly) positive numbers
w_{1},...,w_{m}
, the functions
\{\hat{f}(w_{k}\xi)\}_{k=1}^{m}
have ordered growth if and only if
w_{k}\neq w_{j}
for all distinct
k,j\in\{1,...,m\}
. This result will be used in Proposition
5.2
(c) to deduce the linear independence of two-layer neurons with bias.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Given distinct
b_{1},...,b_{m}>0
and non-zero constants
a_{1},...,a_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\varlimsup_{z\to\infty}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}e^{ib_{k}z}\right|>0.

Proof.

Let
f(z):=\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\cos(b_{k}z)
. It suffices to show that
\varlimsup_{z\to\infty}|f(z)|>0
. Note that for any
s\in\mathbb{N}
,
\left\|f^{(s)}\right\|_{\infty}<\infty
because

|f^{(s)}(z)|=\left|\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}b_{k}^{s}\cos(b_{k}z)\right|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}|a_{k}||b_{k}|^{s}.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
b_{1}>b_{2}>...>b_{m}
. Then there are
S\in\mathbb{N}
,
\delta_{S}>0
and a sequence
\{z_{n}^{(S)}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}z_{n}^{(S)}=\infty
, such that for any
z\in(z_{n},z_{n}+\delta_{S})
,
|f^{(S)}(z)|\geq\frac{|a_{1}||b_{1}|^{S}}{2}
. Fix this
S
. We would like to show that for any
0\leq s\leq S
, there are
\delta_{s}>0
and a sequence
\{z_{n}^{(s)}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}z_{n}^{(s)}=\infty
, such that
|f^{(s)}|
is bounded below by some
C_{s}>0
on
(z_{n}^{(s)},z_{n}^{(s)}+\delta_{s})
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This immediately gives us the desired result.

Indeed, our proof above implies that this holds for
s=S
. Suppose it holds for some
s\in\{1,...,S\}
. Let
\delta_{s},\{z_{n}^{(s)}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
and
C_{s}
be defined as above. If
\varlimsup_{z\to\infty}|f^{(s-1)}(z)|=0
, then there is some
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
|f^{(s-1)}(z_{n}^{(s)})|\leq\frac{C_{s}\delta_{s}}{3}
. But this gives a contradiction, because

\displaystyle|f^{(s-1)}(z_{n}^{(s)})|

\displaystyle=\left|f^{(s-1)}(z_{n}^{(s)})+\int_{z_{n}^{(s)}}^{z_{n}^{(s)}+\delta_{s}}f^{(s)}(z)dz\right|

\displaystyle\geq\left|\int_{z_{n}^{(s)}}^{z_{n}^{(s)}+\delta_{s}}f^{(s)}(z)dz\right|-\left|f^{(s-1)}(z_{n}^{(s)})\right|

\displaystyle\geq C_{s}\delta_{s}-\frac{C_{s}\delta_{s}}{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{2C_{s}\delta_{s}}{3}.

Therefore, we can set
C_{s-1}:=\frac{1}{2}\varlimsup_{z\to\infty}|f^{(s-1)}(z)|
, which is strictly positive according to our proof above. Now
f^{(s)}
is (uniformly) bounded, so there must be some
\delta_{s-1}>0
and a sequence
\{z_{n}^{(s-1)}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
diverging to
\infty
, such that
|f^{(s-1)}(z)|>C_{s-1}
whenever
z\in(z_{n}^{(s-1)},z_{n}^{(s-1)}+\delta_{s-1})
. This completes the induction step.

∎

Proposition 5.2
.

Given
d,m\in\mathbb{N}
and a continuous
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. The following results hold.

(a)

Suppose there are some
s\in\mathbb{N}
,
p\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
with
\sigma^{(s)}=f^{p}+c
, where
\lim_{z\to\infty}f(z)=\lim_{z\to-\infty}f(z)=\infty
both at hyper-exponential rate, and for any
w>0
and
\tilde{w}<0
either
f(wz)=o(f(\tilde{w}z))
as
z\to\infty
or
f(\tilde{w}z)=o(f(wz))
as
z\to\infty
. Then
\{\sigma(w_{k}z+b_{k})\}_{k=1}^{m}
are linearly independent if and only if
(w_{k},b_{k})\neq(w_{j},b_{j})
for all distinct
k,j\in\{1,...,m\}
.

(b)

Suppose there are some
s\in\mathbb{N}
,
p\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
with
\sigma^{(s)}=f^{p}+c
, where
\lim_{z\to\infty}f(z)=\lim_{z\to-\infty}f(z)=\infty
both at hyper-exponential rate, and for a sequence
\{z_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}z_{n}=\infty
we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(z_{n})}{f(-z_{n})}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. Then
\{\sigma(w_{k}z+b_{k})\}_{k=1}^{m}
are linearly independent if
(w_{k},b_{k})\pm(w_{j},b_{j})\neq 0
for all distinct
k,j\in\{1,...,m\}
.

(c)

Suppose there is some
s\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\sigma^{(s)}
is an even function with no zeros,
\sigma^{(s)}\in\mathscr{F}
, and its Fourier transform has countably many zeros. Then
\{\sigma(w_{k}z+b_{k})\}_{k=1}^{m}
are linearly independent if and only if
(w_{k},b_{k})\pm(w_{j},b_{j})\neq 0
for all distinct
k,j\in\{1,...,m\}
, and when
\sigma(0)\neq 0
, we have
w_{k}=0
for at most one
k
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
, it suffices to work with
d=1
. Moreover, when
d=1
, if
a_{1},...,a_{m}
are constants such that
\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\sigma(w_{k}z+b_{k})\equiv 0
, then clearly
\sum_{k=1}^{m}[a_{k}w_{k}^{s}]\sigma^{(s)}(w_{k}z+b_{k})\equiv 0
. Thus, we only need to show that
\{\sigma^{(s)}(w_{k}z+b_{k})\}_{k=1}^{m}
with
d=1
are linearly independent.

(a)

Let
\{(w_{k},b_{k})\}_{k=1}^{m}
be distinct. First assume that
p>0
. Then
\sigma^{(s)}(z)=\Theta(f^{p}(z))
as
z\to\pm\infty
. It follows that

\lim_{z\to\infty}\sigma^{(s)}(z)=\lim_{z\to-\infty}\sigma^{(s)}(z)=\infty

at hyper-exponential rate and for any
w>0
and
\tilde{w}<0
, either
\sigma^{(s)}(wz)=o(\sigma^{(s)}(\tilde{w}z))
or
\sigma^{(s)}(\tilde{w}z)=o(\sigma^{(s)}(wz))
as
z\to\infty
. Thus, applying Proposition
3.3
to
\sigma^{(s)}
and the identity map, we can see that
\{\sigma^{(s)}(w_{k}z+b_{k})\}_{k=1}^{m}
have ordered growth, whence by Proposition
3.1
, they are linearly independent.

Then assume that
p<0
. So

\lim_{z\to\infty}\sigma^{(s)}(z)=\lim_{z\to-\infty}\sigma^{(s)}(z)=c

and
\frac{1}{\sigma^{(s)}(z)-c}=\Theta(f^{-p})
as
z\to\pm\infty
(the hyper-exponential growth of
f
implies that
\frac{1}{\sigma^{(s)}(z)-c}
is well-defined whenever
|z|
is large). Again, by Proposition
3.1
,
\{\sigma(w_{k}z+b_{k})-c\}_{k=1}^{m}
are linearly independent. On the other hand, if
a_{1},...,a_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
are constants such that
\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\sigma^{(s)}(w_{k}z+b_{k})\equiv 0
, then

\lim_{z\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\sigma^{(s)}(w_{k}z+b_{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}c
and thus we must have

\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\left(\sigma^{(s)}(w_{k}z+b_{k})-c\right)\equiv 0.

By linear independence of
\{\sigma^{(s)}(w_{k}z+b_{k})\}_{k=1}^{m}
,
a_{1}=...=a_{m}=0
. It follows that the functions
\{\sigma^{(s)}(w_{k}z+b_{k})\}_{k=1}^{m}
are linearly independent.

Conversely, if
(w_{k},b_{j})=(w_{j},b_{j})
for some distinct
k,j\in\{1,...,m\}
, then
\sigma(w_{k}z+b_{k})=\sigma(w_{j}z+b_{j})
, so
\{\sigma(w_{k}z+b_{k})\}_{k=1}^{m}
cannot be linearly independent.

(b)

The proof is almost identical to that for (a); so we only show the case for
p>0
. Let
\{(w_{k},b_{k})\}_{k=1}^{m}
be distinct. Again, we have
\sigma^{(s)}(z)=\Theta(f^{p}(z))
as
z\to\pm\infty
, whence

\lim_{z\to\infty}\sigma^{(s)}(z)=\lim_{z\to-\infty}\sigma^{(s)}(z)=\infty

at hyper-exponential rate and

L:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sigma^{(s)}(z_{n})}{\sigma^{(s)}(-z_{n})}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}

Fix distinct
(w,b),(\tilde{w},\tilde{b})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Consider the following cases.

i)

w,\tilde{w}
are both positive or negative. Then clearly
wz+b
and
\tilde{w}z+\tilde{b}
diverge to
\infty
or
-\infty
simultaneously. If
|w|>|\tilde{w}|
, then
\lim_{z\to\infty}|(wz+b)-(\tilde{w}z+\tilde{b})|=\infty
. Therefore,
\sigma^{(s)}(\tilde{w}z+\tilde{b})=o(\sigma^{(s)}(wz+b))
as
z\to\infty
. Similarly, if
|w|<|\tilde{w}|
then we have
\sigma^{(s)}(wz+b)=o(\sigma^{(s)}(\tilde{w}z+\tilde{b}))
as
z\to\infty
. If
w=\tilde{w}
then we must have
b\neq\tilde{b}
, which means
|(wz+b)-(\tilde{w}z+\tilde{b})|=|b-\tilde{b}|
for all (large)
z
. Again,
\sigma^{(s)}(wz+b)
and
\sigma^{(s)}(\tilde{w}z+\tilde{b})
have ordered growth.

ii)

w>0
and
\tilde{w}<0
. By i), the functions
\sigma^{(s)}(wz+b)
and
\sigma^{(s)}(-\tilde{w}z-\tilde{b})
have ordered growth, say
\sigma^{(s)}(-\tilde{w}z-\tilde{b})=o(\sigma^{(s)}(wz+b))
as
z\to\infty
. Define a sequence
\{\tilde{z}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
-\tilde{w}\tilde{z}_{n}-\tilde{b}=z_{n}
. Then
\lim_{n\to\infty}\tilde{z}_{n}=\infty
and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sigma^{(s)}(-\tilde{w}\tilde{z}_{n}-\tilde{b})}{\sigma^{(s)}(\tilde{w}\tilde{z}_{n}+\tilde{b})}=L.

This, together with the ordered growth of
\sigma^{(s)}(-\tilde{w}z-\tilde{b})
and
\sigma^{(s)}(wz+b)
, we can see that for sufficiently large
n
,

\left|\sigma^{(s)}(\tilde{w}\tilde{z}_{n}+\tilde{b})\right|\leq\frac{1}{L}\left|\sigma^{(s)}(-\tilde{w}\tilde{z}_{n}-\tilde{b})\right|=o\left(\sigma^{(s)}(w\tilde{z}_{n}+b)\right)

as
n\to\infty
(or equivalently, as
z_{n}\to\infty
).

iii)

w<0
and
\tilde{w}>0
. Argue in the same way we can show that
\sigma^{(s)}(wz+b)
and
\sigma^{(s)}(\tilde{w}z+\tilde{b})
have ordered growth.

Since
(w,b)
and
(\tilde{w},\tilde{b})
are arbitrary, the functions
\{\sigma^{(s)}(w_{k}z+b_{k})\}_{k=1}^{m}
have ordered growth. Therefore, by Proposition
3.1
, they are linearly independent. Conversely, if
(w_{k},b_{j})=(w_{j},b_{j})
for some distinct
k,j\in\{1,...,m\}
, then
\sigma(w_{k}z+b_{k})=\sigma(w_{j}z+b_{j})
, so the functions
\{\sigma(w_{k}z+b_{k})\}_{k=1}^{m}
cannot be linearly independent.

(c)

We will use a different way to prove this result. First, if
(w_{k},b_{k})\pm(w_{j},b_{j})=0
for some distinct
k,j\in\{1,...,m\}
, then because
\sigma^{(s)}
is even, we must have
\sigma^{(s)}(w_{k}z+b_{k})=\sigma^{(s)}(w_{j}z+b_{j})
. Similarly, if
w_{k}=w_{j}=0
for some distinct
k,j\in\{1,...,m\}
, we obtain two constant functions
\sigma^{(s)}(b_{k})
and
\sigma^{(s)}(b_{j})
. In either case, the functions are not linearly independent.

For the converse, assume that we are given such
(w_{k},b_{k})
’s. Since
\sigma^{(s)}
is an even function,
\sigma^{(s)}(wz+b)=\sigma^{(s)}(-wz-b)
for all
(w,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, thus we only need to consider the
(w_{k},b_{k})
’s with the following structures:

i)

There are
0=m_{0}<m_{1}<...<m_{r}=m
such that
w_{m_{t}}>w_{m_{t+1}}>0
for any
1\leq t<r
and
w_{k}=w_{m_{t}}
for any
m_{t-1}<k\leq m_{t}
.

ii)

We have
m_{r}=m
or
m_{r}=m-1
.

iii)

For any
1\leq t\leq r
,
b_{m_{t-1}+1}>...>b_{m_{t}}
.

Since
\sigma^{(s)}\in\mathscr{F}
, its Fourier transform is well-defined. In particular, for any
1\leq k\leq m
with
w_{k}\neq 0
the Fourier transform of
\sigma^{(s)}(w_{k}z+b_{k})
can be computed by

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sigma^{(s)}(w_{k}z+b_{k})e^{-i\xi z}dz=e^{i\frac{b_{k}}{w_{k}}\xi}\left(\sigma^{(s)}\right)\hat{}\left(\frac{\xi}{w_{k}}\right).

Let
f_{k}(\xi):=\left(\sigma^{(s)}\right)\hat{}\left(\frac{\xi}{w_{k}}\right)
for each
1\leq k\leq m
. We now show that
\sum_{k=1}^{m_{r}}a_{k}\sigma^{(s)}(w_{k}z+b_{k})
is constant (which must be zero from the proof of Lemma
5.1
) if and only
a_{1}=...=a_{m_{r}}=0
. In this case, its Fourier transform takes the form

\sum_{t=1}^{r}\left(\sum_{k=m_{t-1}+1}^{m_{t}}a_{k}e^{i\frac{b_{k}}{w_{m_{t}}}\xi}\right)f_{k}(\xi)\equiv 0.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
a_{1},...,a_{m_{r}}
are non-zero (otherwise simply reduce the number of terms). By ii) above, for any
1\leq t\leq r
,

\frac{b_{m_{t-1}+1}}{w_{m_{t}}},...,\frac{b_{m_{t}}}{w_{m_{t}}}

are distinct. By hypothesis on
\sigma^{(s)}
,
f_{m_{r}}^{-1}(0)
is countable. These, together with Lemma
5.2
, give us some constant
C>0
and a sequence
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}\xi_{n}=\infty
,
f_{m_{r}}(\xi_{n})\neq 0
, and

\left|\sum_{k=m_{r-1}+1}^{m_{r}}a_{k}e^{i\frac{b_{k}}{w_{m_{r}}}\xi_{n}}\right|\geq C\quad\forall\,n\in\mathbb{N}.

By Lemma
5.1
, the functions
f_{m_{1}},...,f_{m_{r}}
satisfy
f_{m_{t}}(\xi)=o(f_{m_{r}}(\xi))
as
\xi\to\infty
, for all
1\leq t<r
. But then

a_{m_{r}}=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{t=1}^{r-1}\left(\frac{\sum_{k=m_{t-1}+1}^{m_{t}}a_{k}e^{i\frac{b_{k}}{w_{m_{t}}}\xi_{n}}}{\sum_{k=m_{r-1}+1}^{m_{r}}a_{k}e^{i\frac{b_{k}}{w_{m_{t}}}\xi_{n}}}\right)\frac{f_{m_{t}}(\xi_{n})}{f_{m_{r}}(\xi_{n})}=0,

contradicting
a_{1},...,a_{m_{r}}\neq 0
.

By our proof above, if
m_{r}=m
, we immediately see that
\{\sigma^{(s)}(w_{k}z+b_{k})\}_{k=1}^{m}
are linearly independent. If
m_{r}=m-1
, then
w_{m}=0
and we have two cases. First, if
\sigma(w_{m}z-b_{m})\equiv 0
then the neurons are trivially linearly dependent. Otherwise, assume there are
a_{1},...,a_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
with
\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\sigma(w_{k}z+b_{k})\equiv 0
. Then
\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\sigma^{(s)}(w_{k}z+b_{k})\equiv 0
and thus
\sum_{k=1}^{m_{r}}a_{k}\sigma^{(s)}(w_{k}z+b_{k})
must be constant, whence
a_{1}=...=a_{m_{r}}=0
by our proof above. But then
a_{m}=0
as well, showing that
\{\sigma(w_{k}z+b_{k})\}_{k=1}^{m}
are linearly independent.

∎

Below we give examples of activation functions that satisfy the requirements of Proposition
5.2
.

(a)

Consider
f(z)=e^{z^{q}}+e^{-z^{r}}
for some distinct odd numbers
q,r\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}
. It is clear that
\lim_{z\to\infty}f(z)=\lim_{z\to-\infty}f(z)=\infty
at hyper-exponential rate. Thus, by Proposition
5.2
(a),
\{f(w_{k}z+b_{k})\}_{k=1}^{m}
are linearly independent if and only if
(w_{k},b_{k})\neq(w_{j},b_{j})
for all distinct
k,j\in\{1,...,m\}
. By Proposition
5.2
(a) again, this also holds for
\sigma(z)=f^{p}+c
, where
p\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

(b)

Consider
f(z)=e^{z^{r}}
for some even number
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Clearly,
\lim_{z\to\infty}f(z)=\lim_{z\to-\infty}f(z)=\infty
at hyper-exponential rate. Since
f
is an even function and has no real roots,
\frac{f(z)}{f(-z)}=1
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus, by Proposition
5.2
(b),
\{f(w_{k}z+b_{k})\}_{k=1}^{m}
are linearly independent if
(w_{k},b_{k})\pm(w_{j},b_{j})\neq 0
for all distinct
k,j\in\{1,...,m\}
(in fact this is also a necessary condition, as
f(wz+b)=f(-wz-b)
). By Proposition
5.2
(b) again, this also holds for
\sigma(z)=f^{p}+c
, where
p\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

(c)

Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})
and there is some
s\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\sigma^{(s)}
is an even function,
\sigma^{(s)}\in\mathscr{F}
and
\sigma^{(s)}(z)=O(e^{-\lambda|z|})
as
z\to\pm\infty
for some
\lambda>0
. Then for any
\xi,\xi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
with
|\xi-\xi_{0}|<\lambda
, we have

\displaystyle|\hat{\sigma^{(s)}}(\xi)|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sigma^{(s)}(z)e^{-i(\xi-\xi_{0})z}e^{-i\xi_{0}z}dz\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sigma^{(s)}(z)\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s!}(-i(\xi-\xi_{0})z)^{s}e^{-i\xi_{0}z}dz\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s!}|\xi-\xi_{0}|^{s}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\sigma^{(s)}(z)||z|^{s}dz

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s!}|\xi-\xi_{0}|^{s}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda|z|}|z|^{s}dz

\displaystyle=C\lambda\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{s!}(\lambda^{s}s!)|\xi-\xi_{0}|^{s}

\displaystyle<\infty.

Here
C>0
is any constant such that
|\sigma^{(s)}(z)|\leq Ce^{-\lambda|z|}
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus, for any
\xi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\hat{\sigma^{(s)}}
has a series expansion at
\xi_{0}
given by

\hat{\sigma^{(s)}}(\xi)=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\xi-\xi_{0})^{s}}{s!}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sigma^{(s)}(z)(-iz)^{s}e^{-i\xi_{0}z}dz,\quad\xi\in\left(\xi_{0}-\frac{1}{\lambda},\xi_{0}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\right).

It follows that
\hat{\sigma^{(s)}}
is analytic, so it has countable zeros and thus
\sigma
satisfies the requirements in Proposition
5.2
(c).

(d)

We now use (c) to show that a class of commonly used activation functions all satisfy the requirements in Proposition
5.2
(c), whence the results in (c) holds. Indeed, if
f
is Sigmoid function, then
f^{\prime}=f(1-f)
is an even function,
f^{\prime}\in\mathscr{F}
and
f^{\prime}(z)=O(e^{-|z|})
as
z\to\pm\infty
; in fact,
f^{(s)}(z)=O(e^{-|z|})
for all
s\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, any “
f^{\prime}
-related” activation functions satisfies the requirements for Proposition
5.2
. This include:
\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}
(Sigmoid),
\sigma(z)=\frac{e^{x}-e^{-x}}{e^{x}+e^{-x}}
(Tanh),
\sigma(x)=\log\left(1+e^{x}\right)
,
\sigma(x)=\frac{x}{1+e^{-x}}
(Swish). Indeed, in the first three examples we have
\sigma^{\prime}=f^{\prime}
or
\sigma^{\prime\prime}=f^{\prime}
, and in the last example we have

\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}\left(\frac{x}{1+e^{-x}}\right)=xf^{\prime\prime}+f^{\prime},

so that, by properties of
f^{\prime}
, we also have
\sigma^{\prime\prime}
is even,
\sigma^{\prime\prime}\in\mathscr{F}
and
\sigma^{\prime\prime}(z)=O(e^{-|z|})
as
z\to\pm\infty
.

5.3
Three-layer Neurons with Sigmoid and Tanh Activation

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\sigma
be Sigmoid activation. Given
m\in\mathbb{N}
and dstinct
\{(w_{k},b_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\}_{k=1}^{m}
such that
w_{1}\geq...\geq w_{m}>0
, there are curves
\gamma_{1}^{+},...,\gamma_{m}^{+}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{C}
with the following properties.

(a)

For each
1\leq k\leq m
,
\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma_{k}^{+}(t)\in\mathbb{C}
.

(b)

For each
1\leq k\leq m
,
\sigma\left(w_{k}\gamma_{k}^{+}(t)+b_{k}\right)\in\mathbb{R}
for all
t\geq 0
.

(c)

For each
1\leq k\leq m
, we have
\lim_{t\to\infty}\sigma\left(w_{k}\gamma_{k}^{+}(t)+b_{k}\right)=\infty
and
\sigma\left(w_{k^{\prime}}z+b_{k^{\prime}}\right)
is analytic on a neighborhood of
\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma^{+}(t)
whenever
k^{\prime}>k
.

Similarly, there are curves
\gamma_{1}^{-},...,\gamma_{m}^{-}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{C}
with properties (a), (b), and
\lim_{t\to\infty}\sigma(w_{k}\gamma_{k}^{-}(t)+b_{k})=-\infty
and
\sigma\left(w_{k^{\prime}}z+b_{k^{\prime}}\right)
is analytic on a neighborhood of
\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma^{-}(t)
whenever
k^{\prime}>k
.

Proof.

We only show how to find
\gamma_{1}^{+}
and
\gamma_{1}^{-}
; the construction of the other
\gamma_{k}^{+},\gamma_{k}^{-}
’s are similar. Note that we can view
\sigma
as a meromorphic function on
\mathbb{C}
; in particular, it has (simple) poles at
\{i(2q+1)\pi:q\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. This means for each
1\leq k\leq m
, the neuron
\sigma(w_{k}z+b_{k})
is also meromorphic on
\mathbb{C}
and has poles at
\{\frac{i(2q+1)\pi-b_{k}}{w_{k}}:q\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.

Since
w_{1}\geq w_{k}
for all
k
,
\im\frac{i\pi}{w_{1}}
achieves the minimum of the set
\{\im\frac{i(2q+1)\pi}{w_{k}}:q\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\},1\leq k\leq m\}
. Meanwhile, if
w_{1}=w_{k}
for some
k
, we must have
b_{1}\neq b_{k}
(because
(w_{1},b_{1})
and
(w_{k},b_{k})
are distinct); thus the real parts of the poles
\frac{i\pi-b_{1}}{w_{1}}
and
\frac{i(2q+1)\pi-b_{1}}{w_{1}}
are distinct. Consider the curve

\tilde{\gamma}_{1}^{+}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{C},\quad\tilde{\gamma}_{1}^{+}(t)=\frac{1}{w_{1}}\left(-\frac{1}{t+1}-b_{1}+i\pi\right)

and the curve

\tilde{\gamma}_{1}^{-}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{C},\quad\tilde{\gamma}_{1}^{-}(t)=\frac{1}{w_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{t+1}-b_{1}+i\pi\right)

It is not difficult to deduce that

(a)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\tilde{\gamma}_{1}^{+}(t)=\frac{i\pi-b_{1}}{w_{1}}\in\mathbb{C}
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}\tilde{\gamma}_{1}^{-}(t)=\frac{i\pi-b_{1}}{w_{1}}\in\mathbb{C}

(b)

\sigma(w_{1}\tilde{\gamma}_{1}^{+}(t)+b_{1})=\frac{1}{1-e^{-1/(t+1)}}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma(w_{1}\tilde{\gamma}_{1}^{-}(t)+b_{1})=\frac{1}{1-e^{1/(t+1)}}\in\mathbb{R}
.

(c)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\sigma(w_{1}\tilde{\gamma}_{1}^{+}(t)+b_{1})=\infty
, and by the minimality of
\im\frac{i\pi}{w_{1}}
mentioned above, there is some
T>0
such that for any
k>1
,
\sigma(w_{k}\tilde{\gamma}_{1}^{+}(t)+b_{k})
is analytic on the open ball

B\left(\frac{i\pi-b_{1}}{w_{1}},\frac{1}{T}\right)\subseteq\mathbb{C}.

Similarly,
\lim_{t\to\infty}\sigma(w_{1}\tilde{\gamma}_{1}^{-}(t)+b_{1})=\infty
and there is some
T>0
such that for any
k>1
,
\sigma(w_{k}\tilde{\gamma}_{1}^{+}(t)+b_{k})
is analytic on this open ball.

It follows that we can define
\gamma_{1}^{+},\gamma_{1}^{-}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{C}
by
\gamma_{1}^{+}(t)=\tilde{\gamma}_{1}^{+}(t+T)
and
\gamma_{1}^{-}(t)=\tilde{\gamma}_{1}^{-}(t+T)
.

∎

Note that while we can use Proposition
5.2
to deduce the linear independence of
\{\frac{1}{1+e^{w_{k}z+b_{k}}}\}
, we can also directly use Lemma
5.3
to prove it, using the growth orders of these functions along
\gamma_{k}^{+}
and
\gamma_{k}^{-}
. We omit the proof here.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{F}
be open and
f_{1},...,f_{m}:\Omega\to\mathbb{F}
be meromorphic functions. Let
\gamma:[0,\infty)\to\Omega
be a curve such that for
z^{*}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma(t)
, we have
f_{1}(\gamma(t))\geq C|\gamma(t)-z^{*}|^{-q}
for some
C,q>0
and
f_{2},...,f_{m}
are analytic on a neighborhood of
\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma(t)
. Then for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any
w_{1},...,w_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, if
\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\exp(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{jk}f_{k}(z))
is constant zero, the functions

\sum_{j^{\prime}:w_{j^{\prime}1}=w_{j1}}a_{j^{\prime}}\exp\left(\sum_{k=2}^{m}w_{j^{\prime}k}f_{k}(z)\right),\quad 1\leq j\leq n

must all be constant zero.

Proof.

By rearranging the indices (in
j
) and multiplying
\exp(-(\min_{1\leq j\leq n}w_{j1})f_{1})
to each term in the sum, we can assume that

w_{11}\geq w_{12}\geq...\geq w_{1n}=0.

Also, denote
z^{*}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma(t)
. We shall prove the desired result by induction on number of different
w_{j1}
’s. The result clearly holds when
w_{11}=...=w_{1n}
. Now assume that there is some
n^{\prime}\in\{1,...,n\}
with
w_{11}=...=w_{1n^{\prime}}>w_{1(n^{\prime}+1)}\geq...\geq w_{1n}=0
. Rewrite the sum as

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{jk}f_{k}(z)\right)

(17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}a_{j}\exp\left(\sum_{k=2}^{m}w_{jk}f_{k}(z)\right)\right]e^{w_{11}f_{1}(z)}+\sum_{j>n^{\prime}}a_{j}\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{jk}f_{k}(z)\right).

Assume that
\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}a_{j}\exp\left(\sum_{k=2}^{m}w_{jk}f_{k}(z)\right)
is not constant zero. Since
z^{*}\in\Omega
and
f_{2},...,f_{m}
are analytic near
z^{*}
, this sum is also analytic near
z^{*}
. Thus, there is some
s\in\mathbb{N}
with

\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}a_{j}\exp\left(\sum_{k=2}^{m}w_{jk}f_{k}(z)\right)=\Theta((z-z^{*})^{s})

as
z\to z^{*}
, whence

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}a_{j}\exp\left(\sum_{k=2}^{m}w_{jk}f_{k}(\gamma(t))\right)\right]e^{w_{11}f_{1}(\gamma(t))}

\displaystyle=\Theta\left((\gamma(t)-z^{*})^{s}e^{w_{11}f_{1}(\gamma(t))}\right)

\displaystyle=\Omega\left((\gamma(t)-z^{*})^{s}\exp\left(\frac{2w_{11}+w_{(n^{\prime}+1)1}}{3}f_{1}(\gamma(t))\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\Omega\left(\exp\left(\frac{w_{11}+w_{(n^{\prime}+1)1}}{2}f_{1}(\gamma(t))\right)\right)

as
t\to\infty
. Note that in the estimation above, the third equality holds because
(\gamma(t)-z^{*})^{s}=o(\exp(wf_{1}(\gamma(t))))
as
t\to\infty
for any
w>0
, which can be seen from

\delta(t)^{s}\ll e^{\frac{w}{\delta(t)^{p}}}=O\left(e^{wf_{1}(\gamma(t))}\right),\quad\delta(t)=|\gamma(t)-z^{*}|

as
t\to\infty
. On the other hand, since
w_{j1}<w_{11}
for all
j>n^{\prime}
, we also have

\displaystyle\sum_{j>n^{\prime}}a_{j}\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{jk}f_{k}(\gamma(t))\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\sum_{j>n^{\prime}}|a_{j}|e^{w_{j1}f_{1}(\gamma(t))}\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\exp\left(w_{(n^{\prime}+1)1}f_{1}(\gamma(t))\right)\right)

\displaystyle=o\left(\exp\left(\frac{w_{11}+w_{(n^{\prime}+1)1}}{2}f_{1}(\gamma(t))\right)\right)

as
t\to\infty
. Combining the estimates for the two parts, we can see from equation (
17
) that
\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\exp(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{jk}f_{k}(\gamma(t)))\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
, contradicting the hypothesis that it is constant zero. Therefore, we must have

\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}a_{j}\exp\left(\sum_{k=2}^{m}w_{jk}f_{k}(z)\right)\equiv 0.

Then equation (
17
) becomes

\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{jk}f_{k}(z)\right)=\sum_{j>n^{\prime}}a_{j}\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{jk}f_{k}(z)\right).

Thus, we can perform an induction to derive the final result.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

Suppose we have
m=1
, and
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{F}
and
\gamma:[0,\infty)\to\Omega
are given as in Lemma
5.4
. Then the functions
\{e^{w_{j}f(z)}\}_{j=1}^{n}
are linearly independent if and only if
w_{1},...,w_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
are distinct. Now assume that there are
\gamma_{1},...,\gamma_{m}:[0,\infty)\to\Omega
such that for each
k
,
f_{k}(\gamma_{k}(t))\geq C|\gamma_{k}(t)-\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma_{k}(t)|^{-q_{k}}
for some
C,q_{k}>0
, and
f_{k^{\prime}}
’s are analytic on a neighborhood of
\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma_{k}(t)
for all
k^{\prime}\neq k
. By arguing inductively using Lemma
5.4
, we can see that the function
\{\exp(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{jk}f_{k}(z))\}_{j=1}^{n}
are linearly independent if and only if the vectors
\{w_{j}=(w_{j1},...,w_{jm})\}_{j=1}^{n}
are distinct.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{F}
be open. Let
f,\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{n}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be meromorphic functions such that there are two curves
\gamma^{+},\gamma^{-}:[0,\infty)\to\Omega
with the following properties:

(a)

z_{+}^{*}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma^{+}(t),z_{-}^{*}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma^{-}(t)\in\Omega
. Moreover, there are constants
q_{+},q_{-}>0
such that
f(\gamma^{+}(t))=\Omega(|\gamma^{+}(t)-z_{+}^{*}|^{-q_{+}})
and
f(\gamma^{-}(t))=\Omega(|\gamma^{-}(t)-z_{-}^{*}|^{-q_{-}})
as
t\to\infty
.

(b)

\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{n}
are analytic, non-zero near
\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma^{+}(t)
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma^{-}(t)
, and the functions
\{\Pi_{j^{\prime}\neq j}\lambda_{j^{\prime}}\}_{j=1}^{n}
are linearly independent.

Then for any
w\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, the function

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{a_{j}}{1+\lambda_{j}(z)\exp(wf(z))}

(18)

satisfies

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{a_{j}}{1+\lambda_{j}(\gamma(t))\exp(wf(\gamma(t)))}=o\left(\exp(-|\tilde{w}f(\gamma(t))|\right)

for some
\gamma\in\{\gamma^{+},\gamma^{-}\}
depending only on
w
and any
|\tilde{w}|<|w|
.

Proof.

The result obviously holds for
a_{1}=...=a_{n}=0
. So suppose that the
a_{j}
’s are not all zero. Let
\gamma\in\{\gamma^{+},\gamma^{-}\}
be such that
w\gamma(t)\to\infty
; then let
z^{*}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\gamma(t)
, so in particular
z^{*}\in\{z_{+}^{*},z_{-}^{*}\}
. Rewrite (
18
) as

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{a_{j}}{1+\lambda_{j}(z)\exp(wf(z))}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\Pi_{j^{\prime}\neq j}\left(1+\lambda_{j^{\prime}}(z)e^{wf(z)}\right)}{\Pi_{j=1}^{n}\left(1+\lambda_{j}(z)e^{wf(z)}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\Pi_{j^{\prime}\neq j}\lambda_{j^{\prime}}(z)\right]e^{(n-1)wf(z)}+g_{1}(z)}{\left[\Pi_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}(z)\right]e^{nwf(z)}+g_{2}(z)}.

where
g_{1},g_{2}:\Omega\to\mathbb{F}
are functions which satisfy
g_{1}(\gamma(t))=O(e^{(n-2)wf(\gamma(t)}))
and
g_{2}(\gamma(t))=O(e^{(n-1)wf(\gamma(t))})
as
t\to\infty
. Since none of the
\lambda_{j}
’s vanish near
z^{*}
, so is
\Pi_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}
. Since the functions
\{\Pi_{j^{\prime}\neq j}\lambda_{j^{\prime}}\}_{j=1}^{n}
are linearly independent and since the
a_{j}
’s are not all zero, there is some
s\in\mathbb{N}
with

\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\Pi_{j^{\prime}\neq j}\lambda_{j^{\prime}}(z)=\Theta((z-z^{*})^{s})

as
z\to z^{*}
. Meanwhile, by hypothesis (a), for any
\tilde{w}\in\mathbb{R}
with
|\tilde{w}|<|w|
we have, as
t\to\infty
,
|\gamma(t)-z^{*}|^{s}\ll e^{(|w|-|\tilde{w}|)|\gamma(t)-z^{*}|^{-q}}
and thus

|\gamma(t)-z^{*}|^{s}=o\left(e^{(|w|-|\tilde{w}|)|f(\gamma(t))|}\right).

It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{a_{j}}{1+\lambda_{j}(\gamma(t))\exp(wf(z))}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Theta((\gamma(t)-z^{*})^{s})e^{(n-1)wf(\gamma(t))}+O(e^{(n-2)wf(\gamma(t))})}{\Theta(e^{nwf(\gamma(t))})+O(e^{(n-1)wf(\gamma(t))})}

\displaystyle=\Theta\left(\frac{\Theta((\gamma(t)-z^{*})^{s})e^{(n-1)wf(\gamma(t))}}{\Theta(e^{nwf(\gamma(t))})}\right)

\displaystyle=\Theta\left(\frac{o\left(e^{(|w|-|\tilde{w}|)|f(\gamma(t))|}\right)}{\Theta(e^{|w||f(\gamma(t))|}}\right)

\displaystyle=o\left(e^{-|\tilde{w}||f(\gamma(t)|}\right)=o\left(e^{-|\tilde{w}f(\gamma(t))|}\right),

where the last equality holds because by our construction of
\gamma
,
|w||f(\gamma(t))|=|wf(\gamma(t))|
as
t\to\infty
.

∎

Proposition 5.3
.

Given
d,m,n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\{(w_{k}^{(1)},b_{k}^{(1)})\}_{k=1}^{m}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{md+m}
be such that
(w_{k_{1}}^{(1)},b_{k_{1}}^{(1)})\pm(w_{k_{2}}^{(1)},b_{k_{2}}^{(1)})\neq 0
for all distinct
k_{1},k_{2}\in\{1,...,m\}
and
w_{k}^{(1)}\neq 0
for all
k\in\{1,...,m\}
. Let
\{(w_{j}^{(2)},b_{j}^{(2)})\}_{j=1}^{m}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{nm+n}
be such that
(w_{j_{1}}^{(2)},b_{j_{1}}^{(2)})\pm(w_{j_{2}}^{(2)},b_{j_{2}}^{(2)})\neq 0
for all distinct
j_{1},j_{2}\in\{1,...,n\}
and
w_{j}^{(2)}\neq 0
for all
j\in\{1,...,n\}
. Then for
\sigma
being a Sigmoid or Tanh activation function, the three-layer neurons

\left\{\sigma\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{jk}^{(2)}\sigma\left(w_{k}^{(1)}z+b_{k}^{(1)}\right)+b_{j}^{(2)}\right)\right\}_{j=1}^{n}

are linearly independent.

Proof.

Because
\tanh(z)=\frac{2}{1+e^{-2z}}-1
, we only need to prove the desired result for Sigmoid activation function. By Lemma
3.1
, assume that
\{w_{k}^{(1)}\}_{k=1}^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. Define for each
1\leq k\leq m

f_{k}(z):=\sigma\left(w_{k}^{(1)}z+b_{k}^{(1)}\right)

and for each
1\leq j\leq n

\displaystyle H(\theta_{j},z)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{jk}^{(2)}f_{k}(z)+b_{j}^{(2)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{jk}^{(2)}f_{k}(z)+b_{j}^{(2)}\right)},\quad\theta_{j}=(w_{j}^{(2)},b_{j}^{(2)}).

By rearranging the indices of the
w_{j1}
’s, we may assume that
w_{11}^{(2)}\geq...\geq w_{n1}^{(2)}
. Let
\gamma_{1}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{C}
be a curve with limit
z^{*}\in\mathbb{C}
such that the requirements (a), (b) and (c) in Lemma
5.3
, and moreover
\lim_{t\to\infty}w_{11}^{(2)}\gamma_{1}(t)=\infty
.

With these notations and assumptions, we then show that
\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}H(\theta_{j},\cdot)\equiv 0
implies
a_{1}=...=a_{n}=0
, thus showing that
\{H(\theta_{j},\cdot)\}_{j=1}^{n}
are linearly independent. First consider
n=1
and any
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Since

\lim_{t\to\infty}\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{1k}^{(2)}f_{k}(\gamma(t))+b_{1}^{(2)}\right)=\exp\left(\sum_{k=2}^{m}w_{1k}^{(2)}f_{k}(z^{*})+b_{1}^{(2)}\right)\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{w_{11}^{(2)}f_{1}(\gamma(t))}=\infty,

H(\theta_{1},\cdot)
is clearly not a constant (zero) function. Fix
n\geq 2
. Suppose that the desired result holds for all
n^{\prime}<n
and all
m\in\mathbb{N}
. There is a (unique)
n^{\prime}\in\{1,...,n\}
with
w_{11}^{(2)}=...=w_{n^{\prime}1}^{(2)}>w_{(n^{\prime}+1)1}^{(2)}\geq...\geq w_{n1}^{(2)}
. Thus, we can rewrite
\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}H(\theta_{j},z)
as

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}H(\theta_{j},z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}\frac{a_{j}}{1+\exp\left(\sum_{k=2}^{m}w_{jk}^{(2)}f_{k}(z)+b_{j}^{(2)}\right)e^{w_{j1}^{(2)}f_{1}(z)}}+\sum_{j>n^{\prime}}a_{j}H(\theta_{j},z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}\frac{a_{j}}{1+\lambda_{j}(z)e^{w_{j1}^{(2)}f_{1}(z)}}+\sum_{j>n^{\prime}}a_{j}H(\theta_{j},z),

where we define
\lambda_{j}(z)=\exp(\sum_{k=2}^{m}w_{jk}^{(2)}f_{k}(z)+b_{j}^{(2)})
for each
1\leq j\leq n^{\prime}
. By Lemma
5.4
and its remark, the functions
\{\Pi_{j^{\prime}\neq j}\lambda_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
are linearly independent. Thus, when
a_{1},...,a_{n^{\prime}}
are not all-zero we can use Lemma
5.5
to obtain

\sum_{j=1}^{n^{\prime}}\frac{a_{j}}{1+\lambda_{j}(\gamma(t))e^{w_{j1}^{(2)}f_{1}(\gamma(t))}}=o\left(e^{-|\tilde{w}f_{1}(\gamma(t))|}\right)

as
t\to\infty
, where
\tilde{w}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfies
\min_{n^{\prime}<j\leq n}|w_{j1}^{(2)}|<|\tilde{w}|<|w_{11}^{(2)}|
. On the other hand, we have

\sum_{j>n^{\prime}}a_{j}H(\theta_{j},\gamma(t))=\Omega\left(e^{-|\tilde{w}f_{1}(\gamma(t))|}\right)

as
t\to\infty
. It follows that when
a_{1},...,a_{n^{\prime}}
are not all-zero, the function
\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}H(\theta_{j},\cdot)
cannot be constant zero. Thus,
a_{1}=...=a_{n^{\prime}}=0
and we can rewrite
\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}H(\theta_{j},z)=\sum_{j>n^{\prime}}a_{j}H(\theta_{j},z)=0
for all
z
. By induction hypothesis,
a_{n^{\prime}+1}=...=a_{n}=0
, showing that
\{H(\theta_{j},\cdot)\}_{j=1}^{n}
are linearly independent.

∎

Corollary 5.1

(Necessary and sufficient conditions for three-layer Tanh neurons)
.

Given
d,m,n\in\mathbb{N}
. Fix any
(w_{k}^{(1)})_{k=1}^{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{dm+m}
. When
\sigma
is Tanh activation, the three-layer neurons without bias

\left\{\sigma\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{jk}^{(2)}\sigma\left(w_{k}^{(1)}z\right)\right)\right\}_{j=1}^{n},

are linearly independent if and only if for all distinct
j_{1},j_{2}\in\{1,...,n\}
,

\left(\sum_{k^{\prime}:w_{k^{\prime}}^{(1)}\pm w_{k}^{(1)}=0}\text{Sgn}\left(\frac{w_{k^{\prime}}}{w_{k}}\right)w_{j_{1}k^{\prime}}^{(2)}\right)_{k=1}^{m}\pm\left(\sum_{k^{\prime}:w_{k^{\prime}}^{(1)}\pm w_{k}^{(1)}=0}\text{Sgn}\left(\frac{w_{k^{\prime}}}{w_{k}}\right)w_{j_{2}k^{\prime}}^{(2)}\right)_{k=1}^{m}\neq 0

and for all
j\in\{1,...,n\}
,

\left(\sum_{k^{\prime}:w_{k^{\prime}}^{(1)}\pm w_{k}^{(1)}=0}\text{Sgn}\left(\frac{w_{k^{\prime}}}{w_{k}}\right)w_{jk^{\prime}}^{(2)}\right)_{k=1}^{m}\neq 0.

Here we define
\text{Sgn}(u)=1
if
u>0
and
\text{Sgn}(u)=-1
otherwise, and for two vectors
w,\tilde{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\text{span}\{w\}=\text{span}\{\tilde{w}\}
we define
\frac{w}{\tilde{w}}
as the number
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
with
w=\lambda\tilde{w}
.

6
Discussion and Conclusion

The article delves into the intricate relationship between the linear independence of neurons and their parameters, offering insightful contributions that shed light on this fundamental aspect. We make a comprehensive exploration of neurons with diverse structures. First, in Section
4
we study the structure of the set
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L})
of parameters making them linearly dependent, and in particular, to what extent can Embedding Principle characterize it. Specifically, we find specific activation function for which
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}(\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L})
is minimal, and Embedding Principle fully characterize it. Based on this, we extend our investigation to neurons without bias, demonstrating that for any activation
\sigma
vanishing at the origin, we can slightly “perturb it” so that the linear independence of corresponding neurons can still be locally described by the Embedding Principle. This nuanced analysis highlights the adaptability and versatility of neural networks in accommodating different activation functions while maintaining linear independence. Further research may focus on extending this result to the whole parameter space.

Besides, in Section
5
we provide a thorough treatment of the linear independence for both two-layer and three-layer neurons. For two-layer neurons, we identify three classes of activation function and fully describe how the parameters of these neurons affect their linear independence. These classes of activations include commonly used ones such as Sigmoid, Tanh, etc. Meanwhile, we show by example that for two-layer neurons with bias or three-layer neurons, and with generic activations (Definition
5.1
), the relationship between linear independence of neurons and parameters could be very complicated, contrary to the previous studies about such activations. For three-layer neurons, we focus on “Sigmoid-type” activation and also establish complete description of the relationship between linear independence of neurons and parameters.

Finally, we note that our paper introduces innovative methodologies to deep learning research, leveraging tools from analytic function theory, complex analysis, and Fourier analysis and developing novel, systematic proof strategies. We hope that such tools and ideas would give fresh insights and advancements for future studies of neural networks (and in general other machine learning models).
