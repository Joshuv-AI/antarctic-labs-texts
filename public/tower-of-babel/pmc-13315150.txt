Barriers for Secondary Hypertension Screening in the United States: A National Physician Survey
Authors: Adina F. Turcu, Zara Salman, Sonja M. Kromm, Lei Zhang, Lili Zhao, Z. Tuba Suzer‐Gurtekin, Gina Vincent, Maria Papaleontiou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertension affects almost half of US adults and is a major cause of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Although expert guidelines recommend screening for secondary hypertension in patients with treatment‐resistant hypertension and other high‐risk groups, screening is conducted in <2% of candidates. This study aimed to determine barriers for secondary hypertension screening across US practices.MethodsPrimary care physicians, cardiologists, nephrologists, and endocrinologists, randomly selected from active members of the American Medical Association, were surveyed on barriers for secondary hypertension screening.ResultsResponse rate was 67% (425 of 633 response‐eligible physicians). The leading reported barriers preventing secondary hypertension screening included visit time constraints (43.5%), poor ancillary support (29.4%), and testing‐related logistics (27.5%). Primary care physicians were 10‐ to 17‐fold less likely to be familiar with testing interpretation and subsequent steps than nephrologists and endocrinologists. Physicians in practices covered largely by Medicare were twice more likely to report poor ancillary support than physicians in practices covered primarily by private health insurance. Private practice physicians were more likely to report reimbursement concerns (adjusted odds ratio, 3.4 [95% CI, 1.6–7.7]) and poor ancillary support (adjusted odds ratio, 2.1 [95% CI, 1.3–3.6]) but also more likely to have access to specialists (adjusted odds ratio, 4.0 [95% CI, 1.2–13.5]) than physicians practicing in large medical groups.ConclusionsOur findings call for measures to address critical barriers in secondary hypertension screening. Strategies needed to facilitate personalized hypertension care include optimization of clinic visit duration, ancillary support, education initiatives, and timely access to specialists.

Hypertension is the leading cause of cardiovascular morbidity and premature death worldwide. 1 In the United States, hypertension affects almost 1 in 2 adults, a prevalence that exceeds that of cigarette smoking, dyslipidemia, or diabetes, other major risk factors for cardiovascular disease. 2 Despite the availability of a large array of antihypertensive agents, the rates of hypertension control have declined over the past decade across all age and race groups. 3 , 4

Secondary hypertension is thought to account for at least 10% of hypertension cases, and a much higher prevalence of secondary hypertension is projected among patients with apparent treatment‐resistant hypertension (hypertension that is above goal while treated with 3 antihypertensive agents at maximum tolerated doses, including a diuretic, or controlled hypertension on ≥4 antihypertensive agents). 5 When tested for systematically, secondary hypertension can be identified in up to 50% of patients with apparent treatment‐resistant hypertension 6 , 7 and in over a third of patients with early‐onset hypertension. 8 Screening for secondary hypertension has been consistently endorsed by expert guidelines among populations at risk, including those with treatment‐resistant hypertension. 9 , 10 Nevertheless, secondary hypertension is seldom screened for, 11 , 12 , 13 contributing to the high rates of uncontrolled hypertension. Early detection of secondary causes of hypertension offers opportunities for effective personalized therapy and prevention of cardiovascular and kidney complications.

This survey study was designed to identify potentially modifiable barriers to secondary hypertension screening in US practices.

METHODS

Data Availability

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population and Data Collection

Internists, family practice physicians, cardiologists, nephrologists, and endocrinologists were randomly selected from active members of the American Medical Association. An introductory letter describing the project, the survey instrument, a monetary gift ($25), and a self‐addressed stamped envelope were sent in a package to the selected physicians in May 2021. Following a modified Dillman method, nonresponders were sent postcard reminders 3 weeks later, followed, as needed, by a second survey instrument with a postage‐paid return envelope another 3 weeks later. Due to the growing rates of remote and hybrid work during the COVID‐19 pandemic, the survey was subsequently sent via email to all participants who were not reached via mail or did not respond. The database was closed on April 26, 2022.

The study was reviewed by the University of Michigan Institutional Review Board (HUM00177696) and was granted exemption. All participants provided informed consent to participate in the study. Data from the survey were deidentified and logged using a double entry method to ensure <1% error.

Measurements

Information Collected From Respondent Physicians

Physicians were asked to provide information on their basic demographic characteristics, primary clinical setting, primary specialty, average number of patients seen in half‐day blocks of outpatient clinics and during a typical week, insurance coverage of most treated patients, most influential source for hypertension management decisions, academic meetings most often attended, clinical guidelines read, and number of years in practice (Table 1 and Data S1). Only physicians who have cared for patients with hypertension over the past year were included in the study.

Respondent Characteristics

Perceived Barriers to Screening for Secondary Hypertension

Physicians were asked to select all barriers to screening for secondary hypertension from the following: clinic visit time constraints; poor ancillary support to assist patients with setting up for tests; not familiar enough with the results interpretation or with the next steps for evaluation; no specialists in proximity to help with the subsequent steps; insufficient time to keep up to date with guidelines; concerns that the testing‐related logistics might be too complex for the patient; worry that preparation and testing might lead to severe complications; worry about patient satisfaction, reimbursement concerns; secondary hypertension is rare; and other.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using R‐4.1.0 (the R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Descriptive data are reported as nonweighted frequencies and percentages. Univariate logistic regression was conducted to determine associations between barriers for secondary hypertension screening and physicians' and clinical practice characteristics. Separate multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to determine barriers for secondary hypertension screening while adjusting for physician sex, clinical setting, primary specialty, and major health insurance payor for their practice. For logistic regression, categories were grouped based on similarity and distribution, to facilitate clinical interpretation, and results are shown as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and 95% CI. We defined statistical significance as a 2‐tailed P<0.05.

RESULTS

Sample Characteristics

Of 633 response‐eligible physicians, 425 (67%) completed the survey (Figure 1). Respondents included 230 (55.2%) men, 271 (63%) White, 101 (23.8%) Asian, and 24 (5.6%) Black physicians (Table). Respondents were practicing in family practice (32.4%), internal medicine (26.9%), cardiology (13.2%), endocrinology (12.2%), and nephrology (11.8%). Most physicians were in private practice (38.1%), and large medical groups or staff‐model health maintenance organizations (20.6%). The mean number of years in practice since completing clinical training was 24.49 (±13.64) years. On average, male physicians had been in practice longer than female physicians (means±SD: 19.60±11.62 years versus 15.68±9.25 years, P<0.001). Almost half of eligible respondents cited “recently published clinical guidelines” (49.5%) as the source most influential in decisions on treatment of patients with hypertension, and 25.4% relied on residency/fellowship training. Of the 4 expert guidelines on hypertension provided, 62.1% of respondents selected the 2017 American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association Guideline for the Prevention, Detection, Evaluation, and Management of High Blood Pressure in Adults (Table). 9 Of respondents, 57.0% reported seeing a maximum of 10 patients with secondary hypertension over the past year, and 11.1% reported seeing >50 patients with secondary hypertension over the previous year (Table S1). Of all patients with hypertension seen over the previous year, most physicians (71.6%) suspected secondary hypertension in ≤10%, and 10.2% of physicians suspected secondary hypertension in >25% of their patients with hypertension.

Reported Barriers for Secondary Hypertension Screening

Physician‐reported barriers for screening for secondary hypertension included clinic visit time constraints (43.5%), poor ancillary support (29.4%), and concerns that the testing‐related logistics might be too complex for the patient (27.5%) (Figure 2). In addition, 18.8% of physicians reported that they believed secondary hypertension was rare, and 14.8% reported not being sufficiently familiar with testing.

Physician‐reported barriers to screening for secondary hypertension.

Primary care physicians (internists and family practitioners) were more likely than endocrinologists (aOR, 16.67 [95% CI, 2.04–100.00]) and nephrologists (aOR, 10.00 [95% CI, 1.32–100.00]) to be unfamiliar with testing interpretation and subsequent steps (Figure 3 and Table S2). Conversely, endocrinologists and nephrologists were more likely than primary care physicians to report concerns for testing‐related complications (aOR, 9.45 [95% CI, 3.20–27.93] and 7.29 [95% CI, 2.29–23.20], respectively), regardless of clinical setting, physician sex, or major health insurance payor.

After adjusting for the same covariates, physicians in practices covered primarily by Medicare were more likely to report poor ancillary support (aOR, 2.02 [95% CI, 1.16–3.53]) and lack of access to specialists (aOR, 4.96 [95% CI, 1.04–23.52]) as compared with physicians in practices covered primarily by private health insurance.

Physicians in private practice were more likely to report reimbursement concerns (aOR, 3.45 [95% CI, 1.60–7.69]) and poor ancillary support (aOR, 2.13 [95% CI, 1.26–3.57]) but were more likely to have access to specialists (aOR, 4.01 [95% CI, 1.19–13.47]) than physicians practicing in large medical groups or staff‐model health maintenance organizations.

After adjusting for clinical setting, primary specialty, and patients' health insurance, women were more likely than men to report concerns for testing‐related complications (aOR, 3.26 [95% CI, 1.31–8.08]), concerns that testing for secondary hypertension might present complexity for the patient (aOR, 2.14 [95% CI, 1.35–3.39]), as well as lack of familiarity with testing (aOR, 2.78 [95% CI, 1.52–5.09]).

We found no associations between reported barriers for secondary hypertension screening and years in practice or US region (data not shown).

DISCUSSION

In this nationwide survey of US physicians at the frontline of hypertension care, we uncovered several critical barriers to secondary hypertension screening. Of these, clinic visit time constraints (43.5%) and poor ancillary support (29.4%) were the leading physician‐reported barriers. Physician 14 , 15 , 16 and patient dissatisfaction with allotted visit time 17 , 18 , 19 has been reported for decades, yet, there has been a paucity of solutions with broad implementation. The evolution of health care to electronic medical records has increased requirements for clinical documentation and physician burnout. 20 , 21 , 22 Interventions to optimize the duration, efficiency, and quality of outpatient visits are crucial to delivering personalized hypertension care, particularly since the hypertensive population is of overwhelming magnitude in most practices.

The economic burden of hypertension is profound, with annual US costs estimated at $48.6 billion, including both direct health care expenditures and indirect costs from lost productivity; notably, these estimates likely underestimate the true burden as they do not fully capture downstream complications. 23 Primary aldosteronism is associated with disproportionately higher risks of adverse cardiovascular and renal outcomes, including stroke, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and chronic kidney disease, independently of blood pressure severity alone. 24 , 25 Failure to detect secondary hypertension causes represents missed opportunities for personalized therapy, including potential surgical cure. Recognizing that earlier detection and targeted management strategies may improve clinical outcomes while reducing long‐term health care expenditures, contemporary practice guidelines for hypertension propose improved and pragmatic screening strategies, by lowering barriers to case detection. 26 , 27 , 28 Such approaches have the potential to improve blood pressure control, reduce cardiovascular events, and generate downstream cost savings through prevention of high‐cost complications and more efficient resource use.

Ancillary support remains critical for assistance with medical documentation and for guiding patients through complex instructions, such as test preparations. Physicians in practices primarily covered by Medicare reported lack of ancillary support twice more often than those serving patients with private insurance. Furthermore, physicians in Medicare‐covered practices reported lack of access to specialists 5‐fold more often than those in practices covered by private insurance, although the number of physicians reporting this concern was small, reducing the generalizability of this finding. Inequities in blood pressure control related to race, access to health care, education, or rural versus urban setting have been previously recognized. 13 , 29 Our data further emphasize health insurance as an important contributor to inequities in hypertension care.

Compared with specialists in secondary hypertension, such as endocrinologists or nephrologists, primary care physicians and internists were 10 to 16 times less likely to be familiar with testing interpretation and adequate next steps. Limited access to specialists further discourages testing. Simplified algorithms for secondary hypertension screening and rapid access to expert opinion via electronic medical records would facilitate secondary hypertension screening by general practitioners, who see the highest volume of patients with hypertension. Physicians also reported testing‐related concerns, including complexity of logistics and risk of severe complications. Such concerns were higher among endocrinologists and nephrologists, who often see patients with secondary hypertension in advanced stages, after comorbidities have developed. 11 Initiatives to increase awareness, encourage early testing for secondary hypertension, and to simplify testing algorithms, as proposed by the recent Endocrine Society practice guidelines for primary aldosteronism 26 might facilitate personalized hypertension therapy and reduce cardiovascular morbidity, which, for now, remains the leading cause of death in the United States. 30 Notably, updated practice guidelines published since this study was conducted have shifted toward encouraging initial testing for primary aldosteronism without stopping interfering medications, in order to not miss screening opportunities. 23 , 26 , 27 Medication adjustment should be considered whenever feasible if results are ambiguous or incongruent with the clinical suspicion.

When adjusting for clinical setting, specialty, and major health insurance payor, female physicians were more likely than male physicians to report concerns with testing complexity, testing‐related complications, as well as low familiarity with testing results and subsequent testing. Some reports suggest that women might be less confident than men, 31 , 32 although female physicians perform as well or better on standardized examinations 33 and provide equivalent or better patient care compared with their male counterparts. 34 , 35 Among responders to this survey, male physicians had been in practice longer than female physicians (19.60±11.62 years versus 15.68±9.25 years, P<0.001). Overall, however, we found no associations between reported barriers for secondary hypertension screening and years in practice. Other factors, such as degree of empathy, perceived challenges with testing, previous training, established patterns of practice, and other difficult‐to‐quantify factors might contribute to the differences in approach to secondary hypertension screening reported by men versus women.

We cannot exclude self‐report bias and nonresponse bias, although our response rate was similar to other surveys of American Medical Association members 36 , 37 and the sample characteristics were similar to those of US physician databases. 38 In addition, when stratified by variables of interest, some numbers were small, leading to large CIs, limited generalization.

CONCLUSIONS

Our findings call for urgent measures to address major barriers to secondary hypertension screening, including optimizing clinic visit duration, increasing awareness of secondary hypertension, and facilitating ancillary support and timely access to specialists. Electronic medical records tools that assist with identifying screening candidates and guide testing interpretation can further enhance the yield of secondary hypertension screening. Such initiatives are crucial to accelerate the identification and personalized therapy of underlying causes of hypertension in millions of Americans.
