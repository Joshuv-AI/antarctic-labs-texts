D-amino acid oxidase suppresses hepatocellular carcinoma via oxidizing D-amino acids
Authors: Zishuai Li, Chao Fu, Yifan Chen, Letian Fang, Wenbin Liu, Jun Zhao, Jiayi Zhao, Ran Meng, Rui Pu, Yiwei Shi, Hongsen Chen, Yida He, Yi Jin, Yibo Ding, Guangwen Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatient-derived organoids provide a powerful platform for elucidating mechanisms of drug resistance and tumor evolution in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and identifying novel therapeutic targets.MethodsTranscriptomic sequencing was used to compare gene expression patterns between organoid-forming and non-forming HCC tissues, as well as between sorafenib-resistant organoids and sorafenib-sensitive counterparts. The TCGA-liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) cohort was used to screen for the key molecular drivers of HCC evolution from the overlapping differentially expressed genes. The effects of D-amino acid oxidase (DAO) on the growth, migration, invasion, apoptosis, and generation of H2O2 were evaluated in HepG2 and SK-Hep-1 cell lines and human HCC organoids. The therapeutic efficacy of DAO against HCC growth and drug resistance was validated with xenograft mouse model and organoids, respectively.ResultsExpression level of DAO was significantly downregulated in HCC tissues that successfully formed organoids compared to those that failed, as well as in sorafenib-resistant organoids versus their parental counterparts. In the TCGA-LIHC cohort, DAO expression was significantly reduced in advanced-stage HCC tissues and was inversely correlated with stemness- and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related molecules. Lower DAO expression was associated with poor overall survival in patients with HCC. In HepG2 cells, DAO knockdown significantly enhanced cell proliferation. Ectopic DAO expression suppressed proliferation, migration, and invasion in HepG2 and SK-Hep-1 cells. D-alanine (D-Ala) supplementation further enhanced the anti-proliferative effect of overexpressed DAO, but did not significantly alter the DAO-mediated suppression of migration or invasion. Ectopic expression of DAO induced apoptosis via the generation of H2O2 upon simultaneous supplementation of D-Ala into the culture medium; the addition of catalase, an H₂O₂-degrading enzyme, significantly reversed the D-Ala-induced effects. In BALB/c nude mouse models, HCC cells overexpressing DAO formed significantly smaller tumors than the control cells (P = 0.010), and this tumor-suppressive effect was further enhanced by D-alanine supplementation. Ectopic DAO expression restored sorafenib sensitivity in resistant organoids.ConclusionDAO appears to be a novel endogenous stemness repressor. The reduction in DAO is a critical molecular event in the evolution of HCC. Therapeutically, combined DAO and D-amino acid supplementation is a promising strategy for HCC treatment, particularly for reversing sorafenib resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07399-x.

Background

Primary liver cancer (PLC) is the third leading cause of cancer–related mortality worldwide [1]. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounts for 75%–80% of PLC cases globally and 93% in China [1, 2]. Chronic infection with hepatitis B virus remains to be the most common cause of HCC worldwide [3]. The prognosis for HCC remains poor, with a five-year survival of 10–19% in most countries [4]. In China, males aged from 45 to 49 years had the highest incidence and mortality rates of HCC [5]. Notably, most HCC cases are already at an advanced stage at the time of diagnosis. For advanced HCC, multi-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), including sorafenib and lenvatinib, are used to prolong patient survival, but the survival benefit is limited [6]. HCC patients treated with TKIs inevitably develop drug resistance within a few months, leading to further progression and metastasis [6, 7]. Mechanistically, sorafenib resistance in HCC cells is acquired through adaptive evolution, with metabolic reprogramming and upregulation of stemness genes [8, 9]. Targeted therapies against these resistance-associated genes can optimize treatment regimens and improve therapeutic approaches for TKIs treatment [10]. Thus, identifying key molecular events during the HCC evolution under therapeutic pressure may facilitate the development of novel strategies to enhance the sensitivity of targeted therapy.

Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) can fully recapitulate histopathological and molecular features of the original tumors, thus predicting patient treatment responses [11–13]. Two-dimensional cell lines lack the ability to mimic tumor structural characteristics, whereas genetically engineered mouse models and patient-derived xenografts are both time-consuming and economically burdensome. PDOs preserve the original tumor heterogeneity of the patient and allow long-term culture to model the clonal evolution of resistance under sustained drug pressure. This makes them a superior model for functionally recapitulating the clinical trajectory of drug resistance and for identifying clinically translatable resistance mechanisms [11]. For liver cancer, PDOs have emerged as excellent models for studying drug resistance and cancer evolution in vitro [14–16]. However, PDOs also have several limitations, such as low and variable success rate of PDOs establishment, lack of standardization, and incomplete simulation of the tumor microenvironment. The success rate of liver cancer organoid construction is approximately 26%-37.5% [14–16]. The successful establishment of PDOs is influenced by the evolutionary stage of cancerous tissues [17]. Most HCC organoids develop from poorly differentiated or highly proliferative HCC tissue [18]. In our previous study, the successful establishment of HCC organoids was associated with the upregulation of stemness–related genes, and the stemness of HCC organoids was further elevated under the pressure of sorafenib [16]. However, most studies focused exclusively on successfully established PDOs, while the molecular characteristics of tissues that fail to form organoids are largely ignored. It remains unclear whether a molecule acts throughout the entire evolutionary trajectory of HCC. To address this gap, we performed comparative transcriptomic analyses between HCC tissues capable versus incapable of forming organoids, as well as between sorafenib-resistant organoids and their drug-sensitive parental counterparts. Key molecules involved in HCC evolution were identified from the differentially expressed genes across both contrasts. The expression of D-amino acid oxidase (DAO) was consistently downregulated in HCC tissues that successfully formed organoids and in sorafenib-resistant organoids. Moreover, ectopic expression of DAO enhanced the sensitivity of HCC to sorafenib, highlighting DAO as a novel and promising therapeutic target for overcoming sorafenib resistance in HCC and warranting further translational exploration.

Methods

Patients and surgically removed tumor specimens

Patients who underwent curative resection performed by Prof. Zhao Jun’s team at the Eastern Hepatobiliary Surgery Hospital (Shanghai, China) were enrolled. Their clinicopathological characteristics was reported [16]. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Eastern Hepatobiliary Surgery Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from all the participants. Fresh tumor tissues were transported at 4 °C and processed immediately after resection, followed by standardized pathological evaluation, PDOs generation, and snap-freezing in liquid nitrogen for RNA extraction.

Culture of HCC organoids and establishment of acquired sorafenib-resistant HCC organoids

Freshly resected HCC tissues were minced into 1–2 mm³ fragments and digested for 30–60 min at 37 °C in a digestion solution containing 0.125 mg/mL collagenase IV (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) and 0.1 mg/mL DNase I (Sigma). The suspension was filtered through a 100 μm nylon cell strainer and centrifuged at 450 rpm for 3 min. The pellet was washed with pre-cooled advanced DMEM/F12 (GIBCO, Billings, MT, USA) and resuspended in Matrigel (Corning, Tewksbury, MA, USA). Subsequently, 3–5 × 10³ cells were seeded into pre-warmed 24-well plates. After 15–20 min of polymerization, the complete organoid culture medium was added to each well. The organoid medium contained advanced DMEM/F12 with 1% penicillin-streptomycin (GIBCO), 1% glutamax (GIBCO), 10 mM HEPES (GIBCO), 1:50 B27 supplement (GIBCO), 1:100 N2 supplement (GIBCO), 1.25 mM N-acetyl-L-cysteine (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), 10 mM nicotinamide (Sigma), 50 ng/ml recombinant human epidermal growth factor (Peprotech, Cranbury, NJ, USA), 100 ng/ml recombinant human fibroblast growth factor 10 (Peprotech), 25 ng/ml recombinant human hepatic growth factor (HGF) (Peprotech), 10 µM forskolin (Selleckchemicals, Houston, TX, USA), and 5 µM A8301 (Selleckchemicals) [14]. The culture medium was refreshed twice a week.

Four randomly selected HCC organoids (HCC-10, HCC-25, HCC-52, and HCC-118) were exposed to escalating sorafenib (1.2–10 µM, 20% increments per passage) for 3–5 months. Resistance was confirmed by 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2, 5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT)-derived IC50 assays, and sorafenib-resistant organoids were maintained under identical culture conditions with the addition of 4 µM sorafenib (Selleck Chemicals).

RNA sequencing (RNA‑Seq)

Total RNA was extracted from human HCC tissues or organoids using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). RNA concentration and integrity were evaluated using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), respectively. Purified RNA was preserved at − 80 °C. The libraries were constructed using TruSeq Stranded Total RNA with Ribo-Zero Gold (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and then sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

RNA-Seq data of liver cancer and adjacent normal samples (TCGA-LIHC) were downloaded from UCSC Xena (https://xenabrowser.net/). NetworkAnalyst (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/NetworkAnalyst/home.xhtml) and R (version 4.2.3, DESeq2 R package) were used to perform differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis, with a differential threshold of |Fold Change| ≥ 2 and a false discovery rate < 0.05 or P < 0.05. GEPIA2 (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#index) was used for the prognostic analysis of the TCGA database. TIMER2.0 (http://timer.comp-genomics.org/timer/) was used for the gene correlation analysis.

Culture of cell lines

SK-Hep-1, HepG2, and 293T cells were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (BasalMedia, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (GIBCO) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Bio-light, Shanghai, China).

Construction of lentivirus and transfection of small interfering RNA (siRNA)

A DAO-overexpressing plasmid containing the human DAO coding sequence in the pSLenti–EF1–EGFP–P2A–Puro–CMV–MCS–3xFLAG–WPRE expression vector was purchased from Obio Technology (Shanghai, China). The DAO–overexpressing and empty control plasmids were transfected into 293T cells with lentiviral packaging plasmids (pLP1, pLP2, and pLP/VSVG) (Obio Technology) using a Lipofectamine 3000 kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). After 48 h, lentiviral particle–containing supernatants were harvested and concentrated using a Lenti-X Concentrator (Clontech, Mountain View, CA, USA). Cells (SK-Hep-1 and HepG2) and sorafenib-resistant HCC organoids (HCC25-SR and HCC118-SR) were transduced with lentivirus for 24 h and subsequently screened using 4 µg/mL puromycin dihydrochloride (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Stable DAO-overexpressing cell lines were established via lentiviral transduction: SK-Hep-1-DAO and HepG2-DAO. Stable DAO-overexpressing sorafenib-resistant HCC organoids were also established via lentiviral transduction: HCC25-SR-DAO and HCC118-SR-DAO. Lentiviruses carrying the empty vector served as negative controls, generating SK-Hep-1-Control, HepG2-Control, HCC25-SR-Control, and HCC118-SR-Control, respectively.

HepG2 cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells/well in 6-well plates. The following day, cells were transfected with 30 pmol of either siRNA targeting DAO (siDAO) or a negative control siRNA (siControl) using 9 µL of Lipofectamine® RNAiMAX reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The siRNAs were synthesized by Obio Technology (Shanghai, China), and their sequences were listed in Table S1. Cells were collected for further experiments 24 h after transfection.

Quantitative RT-PCR and Western blot

Quantitative RT-PCR and Western blotting were performed as previous described [19]. Primers used in this study were listed in Table S1. GAPDH was used as internal control. The primary antibodies used were anti-DAO (dilution 1:1000, DF7266, Affinity Biosciences, Jiangsu, China) and anti-GAPDH (dilution 1:2000, sc-25778, Santa Cruz, Dallas, TX, USA) rabbit polyclonal antibodies. GAPDH was used as a loading and normalization control.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK8) and MTT assays

CCK8 kit (Dojindo, Osaka, Japan) was used to assess cell proliferation. Cells were seeded into 96-well plates (Corning, Kennebunk, ME, USA) at a density of 2000 cells per well. At 24-h intervals, 10 µL of CCK8 was added to each well and incubated for 2 h before measuring the optical density values at 450 nm in a microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). For HCC organoids, the MTT assay (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used to assess cell proliferation. HCC organoids were seeded into 96–well plates (Corning) at 2000 cells per well with 7 µL Matrigel (Corning). Microscopic images were captured on days 1, 3, 7, 10, and 14 using a microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Then, 100 µL of serum-free Advanced DMEM/F12 medium containing 500 µg/mL MTT was added to each well. After incubation for 2 h, the supernatant was discarded, and 20 µL of 2% SDS solution was added to each well. Following another 2 h incubation, 100 µL of DMSO was added to each well for 1 h, and the absorbance value at 490 nm was detected using a microplate reader (BioTek).

Cell cycle and apoptosis assays

A Beckman Coulter CytoFlEX S flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Miami, FL, USA) was used to measure cell cycle and apoptosis. For each sample, a total of 1 × 106 cells were collected and fixed at − 20 °C in 75% ethanol for 24 h. Cells were resuspended in 250 µL Propidium iodide/RNase staining buffer (BD, San Diego, CA, USA) and the cell cycle distribution was measured. ModFit LT software (Verity Software House, Topsham, ME, USA) was applied to analyze the raw data. D-alanine (D-Ala, Sigma), D-proline (D-Pro, Targetmol, Boston, MA, USA), D-aspartate (D-Asp, Targetmol), and D-serine (D-Ser, Targetmol) were diluted to 1 mM using culture medium and added to the appropriate well plates to culture cells or organoids [20]. A 1 mg/mL stock solution of catalase (MedChemExpress, NJ, USA) was prepared by dissolving it in PBS with gentle rotation at room temperature for 30 min. For the experiments, this stock solution was diluted to a final concentration of 5 µg/mL in fresh differentiation medium and applied to the co-culture system for an incubation period of 48 h. For cell apoptosis, 0.25% trypsin without EDTA (GIBCO) was used to collect cells whereas Cultrex organoid harvesting solution (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) and TrypLE Express (GIBCO) were used to harvest HCC organoids. Cells and organoids were resuspended in 500 µL binding buffer (BD Pharmingen, San Diego, CA, USA), stained with Annexin V-APC and 7-AAD (BD Pharmingen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the proportion of apoptotic cells was measured.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Transwell inserts (Corning) were used to measure the cell migration and invasion. For invasion, the membrane of the upper chamber was coated with Matrigel (Corning), and the residual liquid was aspirated after incubation for 6 h. The migration assay did not require Matrigel (Corning). A total of 5 × 105 cells were resuspended in 100 µL DMEM containing 0.1% bovine serum albumin (Bio-light) and seeded into the upper chamber, whereas the lower chamber was filled with 500 µL DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum. Specifically, for the D-Ala supplementation groups, we supplemented the culture media of cells with 1mM D-Ala into both the upper and lower chamber. After 48 h, the upper chamber was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Bio-light) for 15 min and stained within a crystalline violet staining solution (Bio-light) for 20 min. Five fields were randomly selected and photographed using a microscope (Leica). ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/software/imagej/) was used to measure cell numbers.

Detection of the concentration of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) in culture medium supernatant

The culture supernatants were collected from each experimental group. The Hydrogen Peroxide Assay Kit (Beyotime) was thawed on ice. The culture supernatant was added to 96-well plates at a volume of 50 µL per well, and 100 µL of hydrogen peroxide detection reagent was added to each well. After 30 min, the absorbance at 560 nm was immediately measured using a microplate reader (BioTek).

Animal research

Forty female mice (BALB/c-nude) were purchased from Cavens Laboratory Animal (Changzhou, China) to examine the tumorigenicity. Forty mice were randomly assigned to one of the four groups. SK-Hep-1-DAO cells (5 × 106) or SK-Hep-1-Control cells (5 × 106) were mixed with Matrigel and subcutaneously injected into 20 mice. SK-Hep-1-DAO nude mice (mice injected with SK-Hep-1-DAO cells) were dichotomized into SK-Hep-1-DAO group (drinking water) and SK-Hep-1-DAO + D-Ala group (drinking water containing 20 g/L D-Ala) [21]. SK-Hep-1-Control nude mice (mice injected with SK-Hep-1-Control cells) were also dichotomized into SK-Hep-1-Control and SK-Hep-1-Control + D-Ala groups in the same manner. The tumor volumes were measured twice a week and calculated according to the formula: V = 0.5 × length × width2. After 38 days, the mice were sacrificed, and the weights of the xenograft tumors were measured. The tumors were harvested for H&E staining. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Military Medical University.

Sorafenib sensitivity

HCC organoids were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2000 cells per well, along with 7 µL of Matrigel. The concentration gradient of sorafenib was set to 0, 0.625, 1.25, 2.5, 5, 10, 20, and 40 µM, with six replicate wells for each concentration [22]. After six days of initial culture, sorafenib was added to the medium according to the indicated concentration gradient. After an additional six days, 100 µL of serum-free Advanced DMEM/F12 medium containing 500 µg/mL MTT was added to each well. After 2 h of incubation, the supernatant was removed, and 20 µL of 2% SDS solution was added to each well for 2 h. Subsequently, 100 µL of DMSO was added to each well for 1 h, and the absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek). The half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) was calculated using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.3.0).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and graphing were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.3.0, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For two independent samples that conformed to a normal distribution, Student’s t-test was used, while those that did not conform to a normal distribution were subjected to nonparametric tests such as the Mann-Whitney U test. Sorafenib sensitivity analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software. One-way ANOVA was applied to evaluate between-group and within-group variance. Two-way ANOVA was used to analyze the effect of two independent variables on one dependent variable. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant difference.

Results

Identification of key molecules related to organoid formation and sorafenib resistance

HCC organoids were constructed using surgically removed HCC tissues from 10 patients between January 2018 and January 2019, and we successfully constructed five organoids. Four HCC organoids (HCC-118, HCC-25, HCC-10, and HCC-52) were randomly selected to construct sorafenib-resistant HCC organoids. The expression levels of 27 genes were simultaneously upregulated or downregulated in HCC tissues from which organoids were successfully established (compared to those that failed) and in sorafenib-resistant organoids (compared to their sensitive counterparts) (Fig. 1a). Survival analysis revealed that the downregulation of DAO, ATP binding cassette subfamily A member 6 (ABCA6), cytochrome P450 family 3 subfamily A member 4 (CYP3A4), and betaine-homocysteine S-methyltransferase (BHMT) was associated with a poor prognosis in HCC (Fig. 1b–e). Conversely, the higher expression of microfibril associated protein 2 (MFAP2) was associated with a poor prognosis in HCC (Fig. 1f). Genes that were not significantly associated with survival were shown in Figure S1. The expression of DAO, ABCA6, CYP3A4, and BHMT was downregulated, while the expression of MFAP2 was upregulated in HCC tissues with successful organoid establishment (Fig. 1g) and sorafenib-resistant human HCC organoids (Fig. 1h), compared to their respective controls. The effects of MFAP2, BHMT, CYP3A4, and ABCA6 on HCC development have been previously reported [23–26]. However, the effect of DAO on HCC has not been elucidated. In the TCGA database, it was found that the expression level of DAO was significantly lower in tumor tissues than in adjacent liver tissues of patients with cancer, including HCC, cholangiocarcinoma, colon cancer, and renal clear cell carcinoma (Figure S2a). In HCC tissues, the expression level of DAO was inversely correlated with advanced stages (Fig. 1i). In HCC tissues, the expression of DAO was inversely associated with that of stemness markers, including cluster of differentiation 44 (CD44), epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM), and prominin-1 (PROM1), as well as EMT-related molecules, including Vimentin, Twist family bHLH transcription factor 1 (Twist1), and Snail family transcriptional repressor 1 (SNAI1) (Fig. 1j–o).

The effect of DAO on liver cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion

To clarify the role of DAO in HCC, we investigated the effect of DAO on the growth, migration, and invasion of HepG2 and SK-Hep-1 cell lines as well as on the growth of HCC118-SR and HCC25-SR human HCC organoids in vitro. Quantitative RT-PCR and Western blot analysis confirmed the ectopic expression and knockdown of DAO following the transfection (Figure S2b–g). Compared with the control group (HepG2-siControl), the knockdown of DAO (HepG2-siDAO) significantly enhanced cell proliferation (P < 0.0001). Moreover, when D-Ala was supplemented to the HepG2-siDAO cells (HepG2-siDAO + D-Ala), no significant change was observed in the proliferation rate compared to the HepG2-siDAO alone (P = 0.28) (Fig. 2a). The CCK8 assay showed that cell proliferation was significantly decreased 72 h after ectopic expression of DAO in HepG2 and SK-Hep-1 cell lines, and this inhibitory effect was further potentiated by supplementation with D-Ala. (Figure 2b and c). The MTT assay showed that cell proliferation was significantly decreased by ectopic expression of DAO in HCC118-SR and HCC25-SR organoids (Fig. 2d and e). Flow cytometry indicated that ectopic expression of DAO in the HepG2 and SK-Hep-1 cell lines significantly reduced the percentage of cells in the S phase. This result further indicated that DAO inhibited HCC cell proliferation (Fig. 2f and g). Transwell assays showed that compared to parental controls (HepG2-Control and SK-Hep-1-Control cells), ectopic expression of DAO (HepG2-DAO and SK-Hep-1-DAO) significantly decreased the migration and invasion of HepG2 and SK-Hep-1 cell lines (Fig. 3). Supplementation with D-Ala did not significantly suppress the migration and invasion of either control or DAO-overexpressing cells (Fig. 3).

DAO induced apoptosis of HCC by oxidizing D-amino acids to generate H2O2

Flow cytometry showed that the percentage of apoptotic cells was not significantly altered by ectopic expression of DAO (Fig. 4a, b, d, f, and h). It has been demonstrated that DAO acts as a D-amino acid oxidase that oxidizes D-amino acids to produce peroxides that induce apoptosis in several cancers, such as pancreatic cancer, colorectal cancer, and glioblastoma [27]. Therefore, we supplemented the culture medium of HCC cells with 1 mM D-alanine (D-Ala) as a substrate for DAO. Interestingly, after supplementation, the proportion of apoptotic cells significantly increased in HCC cells transfected with DAO (Fig. 4a, b, d, f, and h). The concentration of H2O2 in the supernatant significantly increased only in the culture of HCC cells with DAO overexpression and D-Ala supplementation (Fig. 4c, e, g, and i). Ectopic expression of DAO enhanced the sensitivity of sorafenib to HCC

MTT experiments showed that the IC50 values for HCC25-SR-DAO (P < 0.0001) and HCC118-SR-DAO (P < 0.05) were lower than those of their respective controls (Fig. 7). For the HCC25 organoid line, the IC50 values were 2.52 ± 0.05 µM for parental cells, 4.72 ± 0.03 µM for their sorafenib-resistant counterparts (HCC25-SR), and 3.14 ± 0.07 µM for the DAO-expressing resistant organoids (HCC25-SR-DAO) (Fig. 7b and c). Similarly, for the HCC118 line, the corresponding IC50 values were 1.37 ± 0.01 µM (parental), 6.32 ± 0.02 µM (HCC118-SR), and 5.62 ± 0.31 µM (HCC118-SR-DAO) (Fig. 7d and e). Moreover, compared with their corresponding sorafenib-resistant organoids, the expression level of DAO was significantly downregulated (Figure S2d, S2e, and S2g). These results indicated that ectopic expression of DAO enhanced the sensitivity of sorafenib-resistant organoids to the drug, as shown by their decreased IC50 values.

Discussion

In this study, DAO was identified as a repressor of both organoid formation and drug resistance. Ectopic expression of DAO inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HCC cells. Reduced DAO expression in tumor tissues was associated with poor prognosis in patients with HCC. DAO is a flavin adenine dinucleotide-dependent oxidase that catalyzes the oxidative deamination of neutral and polar D-amino acids, generating α-keto acids, ammonia, and reactive oxygen species as metabolic byproducts [28]. Unlike L-amino acids, D-amino acids exist at trace levels in mammalian tissues and participate in diverse physiological processes as potential regulatory molecules [29]. Notably, a population-based metabolomic study demonstrated elevated circulating levels of DAO substrates in HCC patients compared with those in chronic hepatitis B controls [30]. This clinical observation aligns with our experimental findings, which suggest that DAO deficiency drives metabolic reprogramming and promotes HCC progression.

The vulnerability of cancer cells to oxidative stress imbalance creates a therapeutic window for localized H₂O₂ amplification [31]. Numerous chemotherapeutic agents mediate their antitumor effects through free radical generation, inducing irreversible oxidative damage in malignant cells [32]. In this study, we found that DAO induces apoptosis in HCC cells by oxidizing D-Ala to produce H2O2 and increasing oxidative stress. However, DAO-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) kills cancer cells and may also promote adaptive resistance in the long run, because of the dual roles of ROS in cancer evolution. While acute, high levels of ROS are cytotoxic, chronic sub-lethal exposure can lead to therapeutic resistance by activating adaptive signaling, such as the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)/Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1)-mediated antioxidant response and pro-survival pathways, such as nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) and hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α) [33–37]. Furthermore, cellular senescence is characterized by both D-amino acid accumulation and concomitant upregulation of DAO expression [21, 38]. Cellular senescence is a permanent cell cycle arrest induced by various stresses, such as persistent DNA damage and oxidative stress. DAO promotes senescence by producing ROS as a byproduct of substrate oxidation [39]. DAO-generated H₂O₂ can trigger cell cycle arrest and senescence through the upregulation of p53 and p21 proteins, which are key regulators of cell cycle progression and genomic stability [40]. These findings support our results that DAO-induced oxidative stress disrupts the redox balance in cancer cells and leads to cell cycle arrest and apoptosis.

The combination of DAO-induced oxidative stress and glycolysis inhibitors synergistically depleted ATP and elevates H₂O₂ production in cancer cells. The efficacy of this oxidative stress-mediated energy depletion therapy has been validated in glioblastoma treatment [41]. Polyethylene glycol-conjugated DAO (PEG-DAO) combined with D-proline showed a significant antitumor effect by inducing H2O2 in mouse tumor models [42]. The combination of the choline binding domain of the amidase N-acetylmuramoyl-L-alanine (CLytA)-DAO and D-Ala induces apoptosis in several cancer cell lines, including pancreatic cancer cells, colorectal cancer cells, and glioblastoma cells [27]. Supplementation with D-Ala induced cytotoxic oxidative stress in cancer cell line transduced with recombinant retroviruses containing DAO cDNA [43]. This study indicated that combined DAO and D-Ala supplementation have significant therapeutic potential for HCC treatment and reversal of sorafenib resistance. The DAO and D-Ala therapeutic strategies validated in other malignancies may be adaptable for HCC management. However, the potential toxicity of systemic H₂O₂ generation limits its clinical application. High H₂O₂ levels cause severe cellular damage, including DNA damage, lipid peroxidation, and protein inactivation, which can contribute to a range of pathologies [33, 44]. These detrimental effects are well-documented and have been linked to neurodegeneration, atherosclerosis, insulin resistance, β-cell damage, and chronic kidney disease [45, 46]. Therefore, to enhance the clinical potential of DAO, future work should focus on developing tumor-specific delivery systems, advancing enzyme engineering, like advancing enzyme engineering [47]. In addition, ROS are central to inflammation and immunity in cancer [48, 49]. While our xenograft studies in immunodeficient mice were able to assess the direct anti-tumor effects of DAO with D-alanine supplementation, the underlying mechanisms by which DAO-generated ROS interact with the tumor-immune microenvironment require further investigation.

Sorafenib resistance is intrinsically linked to the upregulation of stem-like properties [8]. This is supported by our previous findings on HCC organoids [16]. Our current study indicated a significant inverse correlation between DAO expression and key stemness markers in the TCGA database, suggesting that the downregulation of DAO maybe a molecular feature of this sorafenib resistant, stem-like phenotype. DAO was significantly downregulated at both the mRNA (Figure S2d and S2e) and protein (Figure S2g) levels in the sorafenib-resistant HCC organoids compared to their parental counterparts, and these organoids have been previously characterized as having elevated stemness features [16]. Hence, we hypothesized that DAO enhances sorafenib sensitivity by inhibiting the cancer subpopulation with stem-like features that emerges under the selective pressure of sorafenib.

Although this study identified promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the in vivo experiments were conducted with immunodeficient mice, which inherently lack a functional immune system and thus cannot fully recapitulate tumor-immune microenvironment interactions. The underlying mechanisms by which DAO interacts with the tumor microenvironment require further investigation. Second, DAO was identified from the transcriptomic data of ten samples and validated as a tumor suppressor in HCC using both in vivo and in vitro evidence. Although its association with overall survival was confirmed through analysis of the TCGA-LIHC dataset (364 cases), potential biases may exist due to the lack of consideration for biological factors such as immunity and genetic variations. Therefore, further validation through prospective studies is needed to clarify the impact of DAO expression and its metabolites in HCC.

Conclusions

While previous work has suggested a general tumor-suppressive role for DAO in other cancers, its specific function in HCC remains unknown. In HCC, we found that reduced DAO expression represents a fundamental molecular mechanism underlying HCC progression and acquired sorafenib resistance. DAO is negatively associated with stemness, and its overexpression enhances sorafenib sensitivity. Ectopic expression of DAO inhibits the proliferation, migration and invasion of HCC cells and induces apoptosis by oxidizing and metabolizing D-amino acids to generate H2O2, thus inhibiting the progression of HCC. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that DAO combined with D-amino acids as a promising therapeutic strategy for HCC treatment.
