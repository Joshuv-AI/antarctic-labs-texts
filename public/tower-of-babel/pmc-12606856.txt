Actual macronutrient composition of the Mediterranean diet and its association with adiposity and inflammation: a cross-sectional real-life study
Authors: Claudia Vetrani, Evelyn Frias-Toral, Annamaria Di Martino, Federica Cantelli, Chiara Graziadio, Giuseppe Annunziata, Ludovica Verde, Giovanna Muscogiuri, Antonia Tricopoulou, Annamaria Colao, Daniel Simancas-Racines, Luigi Barrea
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesThe Mediterranean diet (MD) relies on a specific food consumption pattern that has been given pleiotropic effects on human health. However, even foods typically considered beneficial within the MD framework may vary in macronutrient composition, potentially leading to imbalanced or unhealthy dietary profiles despite overall adherence. To date, no study has evaluated the total energy intake and percentage macronutrient composition in a cohort of adult individuals stratified by their adherence to MD. We aimed to investigate the total energy intake and macronutrient composition among individuals stratified by adherence to MD, and its association with indices of adiposity and inflammation.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in 1342 volunteers. Participants underwent clinical examination (anthropometry, body composition, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein assessment), and nutritional data collection, including adherence to MD (PREDIMED questionnaire) and habitual diet composition (7-day food records). The associations between PREDIMED score, macronutrient composition, and clinical outcomes were explored by Pearson’s correlation. Linear regression analysis was used to assess the macronutrient that best predicted adherence to MD.ResultsParticipants with high adherence to MD (H-MD) presented lower intake of carbohydrate, while higher protein and fat amounts than individuals with medium (M-HD) or low (L-MD) adherence to MD. The H-MD group showed lower adiposity (body mass index, waist circumference, and fat mass) and C-reactive protein concentrations than individuals in the M-MD and L-MD groups p < 0.001 for all comparisons). Linear regression analysis showed low carbohydrate intake as the best predictor of high PREDIMED score (β: -0.875; p < 0.001).ConclusionH-MD is characterised by lower carbohydrate, higher protein and fat intakes than those usually reported in nutritional recommendations. This profile was significantly associated with lower adiposity and inflammation. A low carbohydrate amount was the best predictor of H-MD, suggesting that dietary carbohydrate should be on the lower side of the recommended range.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07109-7.

Introduction

The Mediterranean diet (MD) is a generic term referring to dietary patterns based on traditional eating habits in countries bordering the Mediterranean Sea. MD is characterized by a wide variety of plant foods (i.e., vegetables, fruits, legumes, nuts, and whole-grain cereals), the use of extra-virgin olive oil as the main source of fat, and moderate consumption of animal protein and fat (preferring fish, eggs, and low-fat dairies), and restricted intake of sweets and processed foods [1]. Accordingly, MD provides a specific nutritional profile that has been given pleiotropic effects on human health [2]. More in detail, energy intake is provided by non-refined carbohydrate (55–60%), 30–35% from fat, and ~ 15% from protein. As for fat quality, monounsaturated fatty ac-ids (MUFA, 10–15%) are higher than saturated fatty acids (SFA < 10%) and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA, 5–10%). Sugar intake is less than 10% by limiting sweets and sugar-sweetened beverages [3].

MD is considered a healthy diet relying on accumulating evidence that consistently supports the relationship between low adherence to the MD and increased prevalence and/or incidence of chronic non-communicable diseases (NCDs, i.e. type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular and neurodegenerative diseases, and some cancers) [4–7].

Moreover, several studies have been conducted to highlight the mechanisms behind the beneficial effects of short- and long-term MD-based nutrition interventions. The most reported mechanisms involve: (a) protection against oxidative stress and inflammation, (b) blood lipid and glucose-lowering effect, (c) improvement of hormones and growth factors secretion, (d) gut microbiota-modulation towards metabolite-producing bacteria influencing metabolic health [8–11].

Nevertheless, it is poorly known whether the nutritional profile of MD in larger populations reflects adherence to the recommended macronutrient distribution. Indeed, the definition of the MD is mainly based on food groups and their optimal consumption frequency.

Consequently, several serving-based questionnaires have been developed to assess MD adherence [12]. These questionnaires are considered feasible and reliable tools for scoring adherence to MD and are widely used in epidemiological studies to highlight associations with risk factors for several diseases. Although these questionnaires provide an optimal stratification of individuals according to their adherence to MD, no information on dietary composition can be derived from these tools. Therefore, it can be hypothesised that even individuals presenting high adherence to MD (H-MD) by questionnaire could not meet optimal macronutrient thresholds according to nutritional recommendations. That would require educational interventions to correct deviations from healthy dietary composition.

The clinical relevance of optimal macronutrient intake arises from several studies demonstrating a strong association between poor dietary habits and a range of risk factors for chronic diseases. In addition, the Global Burden of Disease Study reported that dietary risks explain almost 11 million deaths (regardless of age, sex, and sociodemographic development of their place of residence), suggesting the importance of improving overall dietary composition [13–15].

Nevertheless, systematic reviews highlight the inconsistency and scarcity of nutrient reporting in adherence to MD studies, with only about one‑half to one‑third of studies providing detailed macronutrient intake data and marked between‑study variability. In detail, Abdelhamid and colleagues [16] reviewed 369 studies and found that few evidence reports nutrient intakes across the range of the Mediterranean dietary pattern in adults, underscoring the considerable heterogeneity and limited detail in macronutrient reporting. This gap has practical significance: optimising dietary therapy requires not only promoting adherence to the MD pattern but also tailoring its quantitative macronutrient profile, particularly the balance of carbohydrates, proteins, and total and specific types of lipids. A clear understanding of the macronutrient signature underpinning H-MD adherence could refine clinical nutrition guidance, enabling the adjustment of dietary prescriptions not only to support adherence but also to optimise metabolic outcomes and therapeutic precision.

Against this background, the present study aimed to evaluate: (1) the actual macronutrient composition among individuals stratified by adherence to MD, and (2) the prevalence of obesity and inflammatory status in the study cohort, as MD has been appointed as an anti-obesity and anti-inflammatory pattern.

Material and methods

Participants

All individuals and their relatives attending the Italian Centre for the Care and Well-Being of Patients with Obesity (C.I.B.O) of Federico II University Hospital (Naples, Italy) were screened for eligibility. Eligible participants were men and women aged > 18 years while exclusion criteria were: pregnant and breast-feeding women, history of high alcohol intake (more than 30 ml/day for men and 20 ml/day for women), any other acute/chronic disease severely affecting health status, i.e., impaired renal function (estimated glomerular filtration rate calculated by chronic kidney disease epidemiology collaboration equation < 90 mL/min/1.73 m2), liver diseases (A, B, or C virus or autoimmune hepatitis, history of toxins or drugs known to induce hepatitis), cardiovascular disease (previous cardiovascular events, atherosclerosis, coronary artery or peripheral vascular disease) or other physical conditions that can significantly influence weight gain and the usual eating habits. All participants who reported adherence to medical nutrition therapies other than the Mediterranean diet (such as the ketogenic diet and others), as well as those taking specific nutraceuticals that could interfere with inflammation levels, were excluded from the study.

For the use of her/his data, each participant gave informed consent following the approval of the Ethical Committee of the Federico II University.

Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted in compliance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement checklist [17].

All individuals meeting the inclusion criteria were consecutively enrolled in this study and underwent an interviewer-administered questionnaire and a physical examination.

Anamnestic data (family and personal medical history, pharmacological therapy or supplement use, etc.) and lifestyle habits (smoking, alcohol use, and physical activity) were collected according to standard procedures as in previous studies [10, 18]. Individuals who smoked at least one cigarette/day (YES/NO) were defined as current smokers, and participants habitually engaged in at least 30 min/day of aerobic exercise (YES/NO) were defined as physically active. Alcohol use was identified with a threshold of more than 20 ml/day.

Nutritional information included adherence to MD and the assessment of habitual diet composition.

The adherence to MD was assessed using the Prevention with Mediterranean Diet questionnaire (Prevención con Dieta Mediterránea, PREDIMED) [19], consisting of 14 items (intake and amount of extra-virgin olive oil, frequency of fruit, vegetables, nuts, legumes, red meat, poultry, fish, animal fat, sweetened beverages, sweets, and ‘’sofrito’’). A skilled nutritionist administered this questionnaire with the same face-to-face interview used in previous studies [18]. PREDIMED score was calculated by assigning a score of 1 and 0 for each item. According to the PREDIMED score, participants were classified as follows: 0–5, low adherence; 6–9, average adherence; and ≥ 10, high adherence [19].

In addition, all participants were asked to fill in a 7-day food record to collect information about eating habits and dietary composition, as previously reported [20]. In brief, all participants were given descriptive information on the proper identification of consumed foodstuffs and the calculation of portion sizes. All foods and beverages consumed (including dressings) were recorded. Portions were reported by household measures (cups, spoons) or weight, providing as much detail as possible (i.e., cooking methods, brand names). A skilled nutritionist discussed the received food records with participants to check for potential mistakes and missing information. In addition, 7-day food records with a daily food intake < 800 kcal (3350 kJ) and/or incomplete compilation were excluded from the analyses (Supplementary Fig. 1). Total energy intake and macronutrient composition were calculated using the MetaDieta software (Meteda, Ascoli-Piceno, Italy). Adequate macronutrient intake was assessed according to reference values for the general population recommended by national [21] and international nutrition societies [22, 23]. Briefly, a healthy macronutrient distribution for adults is typically 45–65% of daily energy from carbohydrates, 20–35% from fat, and 10–35% from protein. Consuming percentages outside these ranges may have detrimental effects on overall health [24].

Methods

Anthropometric measures were collected by a single skilled nutritionist at each visit in the morning (8–10 a.m.). Weight, height, and waist circumference were detected in participants wearing light clothing and no shoes, after an overnight fast, according to standard procedures [25]. Weight and height were used to calculate body mass index (BMI, kg/m2) [26]. BMI was classified according to the WHO criteria [26]: normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m2); Overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m2); Obesity class I (30.0–34.9 kg/m2); Obesity class II (35.0–39.9 kg/m2); Obesity class III (≥ 40.0 kg/m2). All measurements were taken while the participant was standing upright with the feet together and the arms hanging closely by the sides, with the participant standing and breathing normally, as previously reported [27].

Body composition was evaluated by bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA). BIA was performed by a phase-sensitive BIA system (an 800 A current with a frequency of 50 kHz BIA 101 RJL, Akern Bioresearch, Florence, Italy) [28, 29] with BIATRODES electrodes (Akern Srl; Florence, Italy), according to the standard procedures of the European Society of Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition (ESPEN) [30]. All measurements were performed under strictly standardised conditions by the same certified skilled nutritionist and with the same device to avoid inter-observer and inter-device variability as reported in previous studies [25, 27]. Briefly, the device was routinely checked with resistors and capacitors of known values. Reliability for intraday and interday measurements by the same observer was < 2% for resistance (R), < 2.5% for reactance (Xc), and < 3.3% for R, < 2.8% for Xc, respectively. The coefficients of variation (CVs) of repeated measurements of R and Xc at 50 kHz were determined in 10 females by the same observer: CVs were 1.4% for R and 1.3% for Xc.

As for the assessment of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), fasting blood samples were collected in the morning (8–10 a.m.), and stored at − 80 °C until processing. Serum hs-CRP concentrations were analysed by CardioPhase® (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics, Marburg, Germany), based on particle-enhanced immunonephelometry, according to the manufacturer’s standard procedure. The CV of intra-and interassay was < 7% [27]. All participants were further stratified into three cardiovascular risk categories based on hs-CRP levels: low cardiovascular risk (< 1.0 mg/L), intermediate cardiovascular risk (1.0–3.0 mg/L), and high cardiovascular risk (≥ 3.0 mg/L), in accordance with the guidelines of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the American Heart Association (AHA) [31].

Statistical analyses

The data distribution was evaluated by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and variables not normally distributed were normalised by logarithmic transformation. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas categorical variables were reported as numbers and percentages (%). Differences between groups were assessed by analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) and post hoc analyses for multiple comparisons (Bonferroni). Chi-squared (χ2) test for independence was used to assess the association between outcomes and categorical variables.

The associations between the PREDIMED score, the main clinical parameters, and dietary composition in the whole population were explored by bivariate associations using Pearson’s correlation. Moreover, a linear regression analysis, performed with a stepwise model, was used to assess nutrients that best predicted the PREDIMED score (used as the dependent variable).

All parameters were adjusted for potential confounding factors (age, sex, and body mass index) using a general linear model (GLM) for univariate analysis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical analysis was performed according to standard methods using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences software 26.0 (SPSS/PC; SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA).

As for anthropometric measures, A-MD and H-MD showed significantly lower BMI and waist circumference than L-MD, with H-MD presenting the lowest values. In addition, H-MD had lower BMI and waist circumference than A-MD (p < 0.001). This translated into a significant difference for the prevalence of BMI class among the three groups, with H-MD presenting the highest prevalence of lean individuals, while L-MD included most participants with grade II and grade III obesity. In addition, H-MD presented the lowest percentage of fat mass and the highest percentage of muscle mass as compared with the other two groups (Table 1).

As for inflammation, hs-CRP concentrations were two-fold lower in A-MD and H-MD than in L-MD, with H-MD presenting the lowest values (p < 0.001). In addition, more than half individuals in L-MD presented hs-CRP concentrations ≥ 3.0 mg/L (p < 0.001, Table 1).

Participants in the H-MD showed a high prevalence of physical activity and alcohol intake (reporting only red wine consumption), whereas less smoking habits than the other two groups (p < 0.001 for all comparisons, Table 1).

Correlation and regression analyses

The bivariate association between dietary composition and clinical parameters are reported in Figs. 1 and 2. BMI, WC and FM% are directly associated with carbohydrate (CHO) and simple sugars, whereas inversely associated with protein, fat, and MUFA. The same trends were observed for hs-CRP, even though the power of association was lower than BMI and WC.

Conversely, MM% and PhA were directly associated with protein, fat, and MUFA, whereas inversely associated with carbohydrate and simple sugars.

The PREDIMED score was significantly associated with total energy (p = 0.001) and macronutrient intake (p < 0.001), except for the amount of SFA (Supplementary Table 1).

None of the significant associations detected with the bivariate correlation changed when adjusted for age, sex, and BMI.

The results of the linear regression analysis (Supplementary Table 2), performed with a stepwise model, showed that the percentage of CHO was the main predictor of the PREDIMED score (β: -0.875; p < 0.001, Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

This is the first study to evaluate the actual macronutrient composition in adult individuals stratified according to the PREDIMED score in a real-life setting. In brief, high adherence to MD is characterised by proportions of carbohydrate slightly below those usually reported in nutritional recommendations (50–60%), with protein and fat slightly above the traditional MD macronutrient distribution range. Indeed, in our study, total energy intake consisted of carbohydrate ranging from 46 to 49%, protein 19–22%, and fat 31–33%, with no major difference between women and men.

Previous studies investigated the relationship between MD-adherence, food choices, and dietary composition in specific populations (i.e., elderly [32], students [33], postmenopausal women [34]); our data refer to a wide adult population that might increase the transferability of results.

The clinical relevance of our results relies on accumulating evidence reporting that high-carbohydrate diets (namely added sugars and refined grains) are associated with a higher risk of total mortality [35], type 2 diabetes [36] and some cancers [37, 38]. Moreover, additional studies have suggested that complex carbohydrates are associated with decreased cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk, whereas plant-based diets with more refined foods have been associated with increased CVD risk [39, 40]. In line with this evidence, our findings suggested that a lower amount of carbohydrates (still within the recommended range) also drives a reduction of sugar intake, ensuring two fundamental principles of an optimal and healthy diet.

Furthermore, the present study showed that high adherence to MD was associated with a lower adiposity (BMI, WC and FM%) and inflammation (hs-CRP).

As for the association between MD and anthropometric parameters, our findings are in line with previous studies demonstrating that MD (among plant-based diets) is associated with body weight control and improved body composition (lower FM% and higher MM%) [41]. These effects could be related to the low energy density of MD-based meals as well as the increased satiation related to the high consumption of vegetables.

In our study, the low prevalence of overweight/obesity in the H-MD group could also be explained by the higher amount of protein intake. Indeed, several studies have shown that increasing dietary protein might enhance satiety and energy expenditure, thus favouring the maintenance of an adequate body weight [42].

In addition, higher adherence to MD is associated with lower serum concentrations of inflammatory biomarkers (including hs-CRP) [43]. The anti-inflammatory effect of MD is not novel, but in our study, it was independent of BMI, suggesting the direct role of MD on CRP concentrations. Unfortunately, we did not analyse other inflammatory markers, but CRP is a reliable inflammatory biomarker in different clinical settings [44].

Focusing on the adherence to MD but also the specific macronutrient composition it entails is of critical importance in the context of precision nutrition. While H-MD adherence has been consistently associated with favourable health outcomes, including reduced risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and certain cancers, this classification alone does not necessarily reflect the optimal distribution of macronutrients (carbohydrates, proteins, and fats) required to meet individual metabolic needs. Quantifying the macronutrient profile of a Mediterranean-style diet provides a more comprehensive nutritional assessment that can substantially enhance dietary personalisation. Personalised dietary planning based on both adherence level and macronutrient composition can improve the management and prevention of overweight and obesity by aligning caloric intake and nutrient balance with an individual’s physiological and metabolic characteristics. Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that the chronic low-grade inflammation underlying non-communicable diseases is modifiable through targeted nutritional interventions [43]. The MD rich in anti-inflammatory components (e.g. monounsaturated fats) may not exert its full therapeutic potential if the macronutrient ratios are imbalanced, such as excessive carbohydrate intake or insufficient protein, which can independently contribute to metabolic dysregulation and systemic inflammation. Therefore, assessing both adherence to MD and its associated macronutrient distribution enables a more accurate appraisal of its metabolic impact. This dual approach supports a precision nutrition model aimed at reducing inflammation, preventing chronic diseases, and optimising long-term health outcomes. High adherence alone does not guarantee the most advantageous nutritional profile; it is the integration of qualitative (food choices) and quantitative (macronutrient proportions) parameters that holds the key to effective dietary interventions.

The strength of this study is the use of the weighted 7-day food records that represent the gold standard to assess eating habits at an individual level [45]. In addition, this study was performed in a large population in a real-life setting.

The present study also had weaknesses. All questionnaires used in this study were translated into Italian from the English version with no forward-backward translation method to guarantee the conceptual equivalence [46]. Nevertheless, the participants were interviewed by expert operators, thus increasing the reliability of the collected data.

Given the exploratory nature of the study and in the absence of prior data available in the literature, sample size calculation was not performed. In addition, the cross-sectional design of the study did not allow for demonstrating a cause-and-effect relationship but only an association that deserves further investigation. Moreover, information on micronutrient and non-nutrient (i.e., fibre, polyphenol, phytochemicals, etc.) intake is missing since the exact quality of some foodstuffs (i.e., refined vs. whole foods) was not reported by some participants, as well as cooking methods, which could have affected results reliability.

Finally, the single-centre recruitment with potential selection bias and potentially less transferability of these results to other populations. Indeed, the results refer to a cohort living in Italy, characterised by specific nutritional habits. Although further studies in different populations are warranted, the present study refers to a macronutrient profile that can be achieved by a MD-based dietary pattern worldwide.

Conclusion

The findings of this study highlight the importance of concurrently assessing adherence to MD and the actual macronutrient composition of the diet in clinical practice.

Conventional scoring systems (i.e., the PREDIMED score) refer to the consumption of specific foodstuffs included in the MD, and it does not necessarily reflect an optimal macronutrient intake. Our results suggest that individuals classified as intermediate MD adherence may still follow a diet with unfavourable macronutrient distribution, which may be associated with increased cardiometabolic risk and chronic low-grade inflammation. Therefore, a comprehensive dietary assessment that includes both the quality of food choices and their macronutrient profile is essential to ensure that the MD’s well-documented health benefits can be truly achieved. This approach can help clinicians to identify patients who, despite reporting fair adherence to MD, may require targeted nutritional counselling to correct potential imbalances and reduce long-term health risks.

In addition, referring to macronutrient composition rather than specific MD foodstuffs can help to spread the health benefits related to the MD-nutritional profile worldwide, sharply increasing the adherence to nutritional recommendations [2].

In conclusion, high adherence to MD is characterised by proportions of carbohydrate slightly below those usually reported in nutritional recommendations, with protein and fat slightly above the traditional MD macronutrient distribution range. This macronutrient profile was associated with lower adiposity and inflammation in a real-life setting. In addition, low carbohydrate amount was the best predictor of high adherence to MD, suggesting that carbohydrate intake should be on the lower side of the recommended range.
