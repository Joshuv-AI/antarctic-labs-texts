FedAK: a semi-supervised one-shot framework for heterogeneous federated learning via feature-level attention-based knowledge distillation
Authors: Hassan Salman, Jean-François Pradat-Peyre, Sonia Guehis, Nour Charara, Chamseddine Zaki, Abbass Nasser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Federated learning (FL) enables multiple devices to collaboratively train a shared model while keeping their data localized, thereby preserving privacy. Despite its promise, FL continues to face key challenges such as model heterogeneity, high communication overhead, and performance degradation under non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) data. This paper introduces FedAK, a semi-supervised one-shot FL framework that integrates feature-level attention with knowledge distillation to support efficient global learning while reducing data exposure. In FedAK, each client independently trains its local model in a fully supervised manner on private labeled data and transmits only the feature representations of a shared public dataset, greatly reducing communication costs. On the server side, a semi-supervised aggregation strategy is adopted, where an attention-based aggregation module is trained on a small labeled subset to generate informative ensemble features and soft pseudo-labels for a larger unlabeled subset. These pseudo-labels guide a global student model through knowledge distillation, eliminating the need for direct access to client data or logits. Extensive experiments across four benchmark datasets demonstrate that FedAK consistently outperforms four state-of-the-art one-shot FL methods, confirming its effectiveness under heterogeneous and non-IID settings.

Introduction

Federated learning (FL) has emerged as a transformative paradigm in distributed machine learning, enabling multiple devices to collaboratively train a shared model while preserving the privacy of their local data1. Unlike centralized approaches, FL allows model parameters to be updated through decentralized optimization across a network of clients and a central server. This approach is particularly advantageous for privacy-sensitive applications such as healthcare, finance, and mobile intelligence. In its conventional form, FL operates through multiple communication rounds in which clients train local models on private data and send their updates to the server for aggregation, typically using the Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm2. The global model is then redistributed for further refinement until convergence.

Despite its success, FL remains challenged by heterogeneity, communication inefficiency, and non-IID data distributions. The non-IID nature of data across clients often leads to inconsistent local updates, causing global model divergence and degrading generalization performance. Studies have demonstrated that FedAvg simple averaging strategy struggles under such heterogeneity, resulting in suboptimal model performance3,4. Consequently, numerous efforts have sought to mitigate these limitations through server-side aggregation optimization, client personalization, or adaptive participation mechanisms. For example, aggregation-based methods such as FedFTG3, FedAMP5, and Ditto6 assign dynamic weights or personalized layers to better reflect local data characteristics. Local optimization methods like FedProx7, FedBN8, and MOON9 improve stability and adaptability under skewed data distributions. Other strategies, such as Astraea10 and FedCS11, enhance training efficiency through informed client selection. While effective to some extent, these multi-round techniques still incur substantial communication costs and remain susceptible to privacy leakage from repeated model exchanges.

To alleviate the communication burden, one-shot FL has recently gained attention. One-shot FL restricts communication between clients and the server to a single round, significantly reducing overhead and minimizing the risk of privacy exposure12–14. Although this paradigm promises scalability and efficiency, it faces critical challenges under heterogeneity scenarios, where clients possess diverse model architectures or imbalanced data distributions15–18. To cope with such issues, several one-shot frameworks have been proposed. Methods like FedDF19 and FedMD20 employ auxiliary public datasets for server-side distillation, while DENSE21 and FEDCVAE-based frameworks22 introduce data-free approaches. However, these solutions either fail to jointly address model and data heterogeneity or rely on logit-sharing mechanisms that risk information leakage.

To better understand the impact of heterogeneity on one-shot FL performance, we distinguish between its data and model dimensions. Data heterogeneity can be further categorized as label heterogeneity (clients own disjoint label distributions) or size heterogeneity (unequal local dataset volumes)23,24. Model heterogeneity refers to clients using distinct network architectures or capacities8. These variations collectively degrade global convergence, with label imbalance skewing predictions toward dominant classes and size heterogeneity leading to over-representation of large clients. In addition, most one-shot FL frameworks rely on fully labeled public data, which limits scalability and realism in privacy-preserving scenarios. This challenge has motivated the rise of semi-supervised FL (FSSL), where limited labeled data and abundant unlabeled data are collaboratively exploited across clients. Recent advances in FSSL have demonstrated that leveraging unlabeled data can substantially enhance global performance and reduce annotation costs. For instance, SemiFL25 introduces consistency regularization for clients lacking labels, DS-FL26 employs distillation on shared public data to mitigate communication overhead, SemiPFL27 personalizes semi-supervised aggregation using hypernetworks and Semi-HFL28 addresses resource heterogeneity through sub-model partitioning. Further improvements, such as FedCR29 using contrastive objectives, SAGE30 correcting pseudo-label mismatches via confidence discrepancy, and (FL)231 applying sharpness-aware consistency, collectively reveal that FSSL can enhance robustness in unlabeled settings. Moreover, FedMox32 adapts semi-supervised strategies for low-resource environments, while comprehensive surveys28,33 have systematized these approaches and highlighted the remaining limitations. Nevertheless, most FSSL frameworks still depend on iterative communication rounds or assume homogeneous architectures making them unsuitable for one-shot heterogeneous FL scenarios.

Motivated by these observations, we propose FedAK, a one-shot FL framework that addresses both model and data heterogeneity by combining feature-level attention with knowledge distillation (KD). In this setting, KD serves as a mechanism for transferring the collective knowledge of diverse client models into a single global model. Through feature-level attention, FedAK adaptively weights the heterogeneous feature representations produced by different client architectures, while distillation enables these aggregated representations to guide the learning of a unified global model. In FedAK, clients independently train heterogeneous models on private labeled datasets in a fully supervised manner and transmit only feature representations extracted from a shared public dataset, rather than raw data or logits, thus preserving privacy and significantly minimizing communication overhead compared with traditional FL methods that repeatedly exchange full model parameters across many communication rounds. On the server side, a semi-supervised aggregation strategy is adopted, where an attention-based mechanism is trained on a labeled subset of the public data to generate informative ensemble feature representations. The trained attention module then produces soft pseudo-labels for an unlabeled subset, enabling a global student model to learn through KD and feature matching without requiring any real labels during training.

Through this dual-stage design, FedAK effectively decouples the supervision process, using labeled public data exclusively to train the attention mechanism while performing global model learning on unlabeled data, ensuring robust generalization even under highly heterogeneous client settings. It is important to clarify how FedAK differs from prior representation-based distillation approaches in federated settings. While existing one-shot FL methods perform ensemble distillation at the logit level, either by averaging client predictions on a shared public dataset or by exchanging logit vectors directly, FedAK fundamentally shifts the communication substrate to intermediate feature representations. Crucially, this is not merely a substitution of logits with features. The core novelty lies in the combination of three design choices that, to the best of our knowledge, have not been jointly addressed in prior one-shot FL literature: (i) heterogeneity-aware per-image attention aggregation, where a learned attention module assigns per-client importance weights for each individual public sample rather than applying global or uniform client weighting, (ii) label-efficient server-side supervision, where only a small labeled subset is used exclusively to train the attention module, while the global student is trained entirely on unlabeled data via pseudo-labels, decoupling the supervision of aggregation from the supervision of distillation, and (iii) dual-objective distillation, combining soft pseudo-label KD with feature matching to enforce representational consistency beyond predictive alignment alone. Taken together, these choices constitute a unified framework that cannot be reduced to applying attention on top of existing logit-level ensemble methods, nor to wrapping standard distillation pipelines with a semi-supervised layer, as each component is architecturally and functionally entangled with the others.

Related work

FL has emerged as a compelling solution for privacy-preserving collaborative intelligence, enabling distributed clients to train a global model without sharing raw data. However, practical deployment of FL remains challenged by heterogeneous client models, non-IID data, and communication inefficiency, which jointly impede convergence and generalization. Traditional FL frameworks, such as FedAvg2, rely on iterative parameter aggregation across multiple communication rounds. Although approaches like FedProx7, FedAMP5, and MOON9 have introduced adaptive or personalized objectives to improve robustness under non-IID conditions, they still suffer from high communication costs and privacy exposure due to repeated model exchanges.

To alleviate these limitations, researchers have increasingly adopted KD as an alternative for knowledge transfer in federated settings34. KD enables the server to aggregate informative outputs logits or intermediate features from multiple heterogeneous clients instead of model parameters, facilitating more efficient and privacy-preserving collaboration. For instance, Hinton et al.35 introduced the foundational distillation framework where a compact student learns from a stronger teacher soft predictions. This idea was later adapted to FL to handle statistical and architectural heterogeneity among clients. FedGen36 utilized a generative model to produce representative samples for distillation, while FedDF19 proposed ensemble distillation on a shared public dataset to fuse heterogeneous client knowledge. Similarly, FedGEMS37 combined multiple teacher models to transfer complementary information, and FedZKT38 achieved zero-shot transfer through synthetic gradient generation without public data. These techniques significantly reduce communication overhead and enhance aggregation flexibility. Nonetheless, most existing KD-based frameworks still depend on logit sharing, which can leak task-specific information and increase transmission load when the number of classes is large. Beyond logit and parameter sharing, recent works have further examined privacy risks from the perspective of transmitted representation content, including identity-preserving covert communication strategies that demonstrate how the information content of shared signals depends critically on the data they are computed from39, and data-aware deduplication approaches that highlight the role of representation design in limiting unintended information exposure40. Independently, research on robust heterogeneous information fusion has demonstrated that adaptively weighting contributions from diverse sources, rather than treating them uniformly, is essential for achieving generalizable and robust representations under source variability41,42. These findings collectively motivate a shift from logit-level uniform aggregation toward feature-level adaptive fusion strategies in federated knowledge transfer.

The emergence of one-shot FL has further advanced the goal of communication-efficient collaboration. Unlike conventional FL that iteratively exchanges updates, one-shot approaches constrain the communication process to a single round, making them particularly suitable for cross-silo or bandwidth-limited environments22. Early methods explored direct model ensembling, where the server aggregated complete local models to approximate a global solution43. Later, KD-based strategies became dominant, as seen in FedDF19 and FedMD20 where each client predicts on a shared public dataset, and the server averages their outputs to train a student model. These approaches maintain high performance under moderate heterogeneity and provide strong baselines for one-shot knowledge transfer. However, their reliance on public labeled datasets and logit-level communication raises both scalability and privacy concerns. More recently, FedOL44 proposed a one-shot FL approach where clients transmit prediction outputs on an unlabeled public dataset under heterogeneous architectures, introducing an iterative pseudo-label refinement objective to mitigate the impact of skewed local distributions on server-side learning. Recent work on federated neural nonparametric point processes45 has further highlighted that modeling heterogeneous client contributions through principled probabilistic frameworks can substantially improve aggregation robustness, underscoring the broader need for methods that go beyond uniform client weighting in one-shot federated settings. In a related direction, communication-efficient federated online distillation has been shown to handle non-IID data distributions effectively while maintaining low communication overhead, further validating the shift toward distillation-based aggregation strategies in heterogeneous federated environments46.

To address the lack of public data availability, data-free distillation frameworks were introduced. DENSE21 pioneered a two-stage scheme that first synthesizes surrogate data through a generator and then conducts distillation over the ensemble of client models, achieving effective knowledge fusion without direct data sharing. Building on similar principles, FedFTG3 and DFDG47 employed adversarial and dual-generator training to expand the diversity of generated samples and enhance robustness under non-IID conditions. More recently, Co-Boosting48 proposed a coupled optimization framework in which synthesized data and the ensemble model mutually enhance each other progressively, achieving state-of-the-art performance in data-free one-shot settings. Similarly, FedHydra49 introduced a data-free one-shot FL framework for heterogeneous architectures that combines synthetic data generation with progressive server-side distillation to improve aggregation robustness under non-IID conditions. While these solutions effectively reduce the need for real data, they involve complex optimization procedures and still rely on synthetic data transmission, which can be computationally demanding and less stable under severe heterogeneity.

Despite the notable progress achieved by KD and generator-based one-shot frameworks, two fundamental challenges remain unresolved: (i) the inability to handle joint model and data heterogeneity in a unified manner, and (ii) the persistent privacy risk associated with logit or synthetic data transmission. Existing methods often treat client contributions equally or fuse them through simple averaging, ignoring the varying reliability of local feature representations. Furthermore, the global model learning is typically supervised by the same data used for aggregation, limiting its adaptability when labeled data are scarce.

Proposed method

The proposed FedAK framework introduces a one-shot FL paradigm with a server-side semi-supervised aggregation strategy that combines feature-level attention aggregation and KD to overcome both model and data heterogeneity across clients. Unlike traditional multi-round FL schemes such as FedAvg or FedProx, which repeatedly exchange model parameters over multiple communication cycles, FedAK completes the collaborative training process within a single round of communication. This design achieves exceptional efficiency and robustness while ensuring data privacy.

As illustrated in Fig. 3, the overall workflow of FedAK can be divided into three main phases: (i) client-side local training and feature extraction, (ii) server-side attention-based aggregation and pseudo-label generation, and (iii) global student model learning via distillation and feature matching. Clients train independently on private data and send only feature representations extracted from a shared public dataset to the server, rather than raw data or model weights. The figure highlights this flow clearly by showing local model training and feature extraction as the initial steps, followed by the transmission of 128-dimensional public-data features to the server, and concluding with the global student training stage that synthesizes knowledge from all clients without compromising privacy.

Once the client features arrive at the server, the public dataset is partitioned into a labeled subset referred to as Subset A and an unlabeled subset referred to as Subset B. The server employs the labeled subset to train an additive attention mechanism that learns to assign meaningful importance weights to the heterogeneous feature representations contributed by different clients. After this training stage, the attention module produces aggregated 128-dimensional feature vectors together with soft pseudo-labels for the unlabeled subset, providing coherent and reliable supervisory signals for the student model. This architecture constitutes the core knowledge aggregation strategy of FedAK and enables the system to leverage client diversity effectively without exposing any private data.

Attention mechanism for adaptive feature aggregation

The attention module constitutes the cornerstone of our framework, serving as the bridge between heterogeneous client knowledge and the formation of an informative global representation. Its principal function is to adaptively weight the feature embeddings extracted from multiple clients, allowing the server to emphasize the most relevant and discriminative information during the aggregation process. This mechanism not only mitigates the effects of data and model heterogeneity but also ensures that the global model benefits from each client unique learning perspective.

To compute the relative importance of each client feature, the attention mechanism follows a two-stage process: first generating latent relevance scores and then normalizing them into attention weights. where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{W}_{a}\in\:{R}^{h\times\:d}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{b}_{a}\in\:{R}^{h}$$\end{document} denote the trainable weight matrix and bias vector of the attention layer, respectively. This transformation projects client features into a latent subspace where their contribution to the global context can be assessed more effectively.

A trainable context vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{a}\in\:{R}^{h}$$\end{document} is then used to measure the alignment between each intermediate representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{u}_{i}$$\end{document}​ and the global attention context. The resulting compatibility scores are converted into normalized attention coefficients through a softmax operation:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\alpha\:}_{i}=\frac{\mathrm{e}\mathrm{x}\mathrm{p}\left({u}_{i}^{\mathbf{\top\:}}{w}_{a}\right)}{{\sum\:}_{k=1}^{K}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{x}\mathrm{p}\left({u}_{k}^{\mathbf{\top\:}}{w}_{a}\right)},\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:i=\mathrm{1,2},\dots\:,K$$\end{document}

The coefficients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\alpha\:}_{i}\in\:\left[\mathrm{0,1}\right]$$\end{document} quantify the relative importance of each client contribution for a given input. A higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\alpha\:}_{i}$$\end{document}​ indicates that the corresponding client \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{i}$$\end{document}​ has produced a more informative or generalizable representation for that specific data instance. Collectively, the vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:=[{\alpha\:}_{1},{\alpha\:}_{2},\dots\:,{\alpha\:}_{K}]$$\end{document} satisfies \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sum\:}_{i}{\alpha\:}_{i}=1$$\end{document}, ensuring a balanced weighting scheme.

Finally, the aggregated feature representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{agg}$$\end{document}​ is computed as a weighted combination of the client features:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{agg}=\sum\:_{i=1}^{K}{\alpha\:}_{i}{z}_{i}$$\end{document}

This aggregated feature captures the most relevant characteristics of the input data by integrating multi-source knowledge while discarding redundant or biased information. Unlike traditional averaging-based fusion, which treats all clients equally regardless of their representational quality, the additive attention mechanism dynamically learns to emphasize the most informative and reliable client features while down-weighting less useful ones. The selection of additive attention, rather than multiplicative or dot-product variants, is motivated by its robustness to feature scaling and superior interpretability, particularly when fusing representations from heterogeneous sources. As demonstrated in50, additive attention employs a feedforward alignment function that computes relevance scores through learnable transformations, offering greater flexibility and numerical stability compared to multiplicative attention, which relies on inner-product operations that can become unstable when feature magnitudes differ significantly across models. In the context of FL, where client embeddings may vary in scale and statistical distribution, this stability ensures consistent weighting and improves the aggregation quality. Beyond numerical stability, the additive attention mechanism carries a principled interpretation that is particularly meaningful in federated settings. The context vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{a}$$\end{document} acts as a global reference point that encodes what an informative and generalizable feature representation should look like, learned from the labeled public subset. The compatibility score between each client feature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{u}_{i}$$\end{document} and this context vector can therefore be interpreted as a measure of how closely a client learned representation aligns with the global discriminative structure, effectively capturing the reliability of that client contribution for a given sample. Clients whose local data distributions are severely skewed due to non-IID conditions will produce feature embeddings that deviate from this global context, and the softmax normalization will naturally assign them lower attention weights. In this sense, the attention mechanism implicitly performs distribution-aware client reliability estimation without requiring any explicit knowledge of local data distributions, making it a principled rather than heuristic aggregation strategy under heterogeneous federated conditions. Consequently, FedAK adopts additive attention as a principled mechanism for adaptive feature fusion, enabling the server to integrate knowledge effectively under model and data heterogeneity.

The attention parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{W}_{a}$$\end{document}​,\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{b}_{a},$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{a}$$\end{document}​ are learned during supervised training on the labeled subset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{A}\:$$\end{document}of the public data. Through backpropagation, the attention network jointly optimizes the weighting strategy and the classifier parameters, ensuring that the aggregated features contribute effectively to class discrimination. Once trained, the attention module generalizes its learned weighting behavior to the unlabeled subset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{B}$$\end{document}​, where it generates soft pseudo-labels to guide the semi-supervised learning of the global student model.

Within the broader FedAK workflow, this attention mechanism functions as the core aggregation engine transforming a diverse set of client-derived representations into a unified and discriminative feature space. By learning which clients provide the most trustworthy information for each input sample, the attention mechanism enables the global model to benefit adaptively from all participants, establishing a more balanced and intelligent fusion process than conventional averaging or fixed-weight schemes.

Global student learning via KD

In the final stage of the FedAK framework, the central server initiates the training of a global student model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:G(x;\psi\:$$\end{document}) to consolidate the collective knowledge transferred from all participating clients. This phase is executed exclusively on the server side, utilizing only the unlabeled subset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{B}$$\end{document}​ of the shared public dataset. No further interaction with the clients occurs after this point, thereby preserving the one-shot communication property of the framework. The knowledge distilled in the previous stage encoded through the aggregated features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{agg}$$\end{document}​ and their corresponding pseudo-labels \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\stackrel{\sim}{y}$$\end{document} ​which serves as the supervisory signal for training the global model.

The student model receives the unlabeled public samples and learns to approximate the behavior of the attention-aggregated ensemble. For each data instance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:x\in\:{D}_{B}$$\end{document}​, the student generates a probability distribution over classes, denoted as:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{p}_{s}\left(x\right)=softmax\left(G\right(x;\frac{\psi\:}{T})$$\end{document}

where \:T represents a temperature parameter used to control the sharpness of the output distribution. The softmax function converts the student model output logits into a normalized probability distribution, and dividing the logits by the temperature \:T controls how smooth or sharp this distribution becomes. A higher temperature produces softer, more diffuse probabilities that emphasize inter-class relationships, while a lower temperature yields sharper predictions. The resulting distribution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{p}_{s}\left(x\right)$$\end{document} represents the student model estimated class probabilities for the input \:x.The attention module provides the server with the pseudo-labels \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\stackrel{\sim}{y}\left(x\right)$$\end{document}, which are treated as soft targets representing the consensus of multiple clients. The KD loss is employed to minimize the divergence between the student output distribution and the attention-based pseudo-labels:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{L}}_{KD}={T}^{2}\sum\:_{x\in\:{D}_{B}}KL({p}_{s}\left(x\right),\stackrel{\sim}{y}\left(x\right))$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:KL(\bullet\:)$$\end{document} denotes the Kullback–Leibler divergence. This objective encourages the global student to align its predictions with the aggregated knowledge derived from the attention mechanism, enabling it to replicate the global decision patterns distilled from heterogeneous clients.

To ensure consistency at the representation level, FedAK further incorporates a feature matching loss, which constrains the feature space of the student model to align with the attention-fused feature space \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{agg}$$\end{document}. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{G}_{f}\left(x\right)$$\end{document} represent the intermediate feature embedding generated by the student model for a sample \:x. The FM loss is defined as:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{L}}_{FM}=\frac{1}{\left|{D}_{B}\right|}\sum\:_{x\in\:{D}_{B}}|\left|{G}_{f}\left(x\right)-{F}_{agg}\left(x\right)\right|{|}_{2}^{2}$$\end{document}

This constraint ensures that the global model not only replicates the predictive behavior of the ensemble but also inherits its internal feature structure, thereby improving the coherence and robustness of the learned representations.

The overall training objective for the global student combines both the distillation and feature-matching losses as a weighted sum:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{L}}_{student}=\:\alpha\:{\mathcal{L}}_{KD}+\gamma\:{\mathcal{L}}_{FM}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\gamma\:$$\end{document} are balancing coefficients that control the contribution of each loss term. These hyperparameters are empirically chosen to ensure an optimal trade-off between predictive fidelity and representational alignment.

During this training stage, the server optimizes the global student model parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\psi\:$$\end{document} using without requiring any ground-truth labels or additional input from the clients. This design allows the entire learning process to be completed independently on the server side, maintaining privacy and communication efficiency.

Upon convergence, the global student model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:G(x;{\psi\:}^{*})$$\end{document} encapsulates the collective knowledge distilled from all heterogeneous clients in a compact, unified representation. This final model serves as the global federated model of the FedAK framework. It can be directly evaluated on a test dataset or redistributed to the clients for further local adaptation if desired.

Clients model specifications

To realistically emulate model heterogeneity across clients, FedAK deploys a diverse set of architectures varying in depth, parameter count, and representational capacity. Specifically, five heterogeneous client models are employed to reflect the non-uniform computational resources typical of federated environments as illustrated in Fig. 5. Client 1 adopts a ResNet-18, offering a moderate-depth residual network that facilitates stable gradient propagation through shortcut connections51. Client 2 uses a deeper ResNet-50, capable of learning more complex semantic hierarchies through its bottleneck design. Client 3 employs a VGG-11 with Batch Normalization, providing a high-capacity convolutional backbone that emphasizes spatial consistency52. Client 4 is configured with a custom lightweight CNN, specifically designed for resource-constrained devices, consisting of four convolutional layers (with 64, 128, 256, and 512 filters respectively), each followed by batch normalization, ReLU activation, and max pooling, culminating in two fully connected layers that map to the feature dimension of 128. Finally, Client 5 utilizes a ResNet-20, often used in compact federated setups to maintain computational feasibility while preserving strong accuracy on small-scale datasets like CIFAR and SVHN. This deliberate diversity among client models allows FedAK to evaluate its robustness against architectural inconsistency, ensuring that the attention-based aggregation mechanism can effectively integrate features extracted from networks with varying representational strengths and depths.

Global model specifications

For the student model, we create a simple effective CNN. It has three convolutional layers, each paired with batch normalization (BN), max pooling, and the ReLU activation function to enhance performance. As is standard in CNN design, the number of channels increases at each layer, starting from 3 and expanding to 32, then 64, and finally 128, while the image size gradually shrinks. Once the model extracts important features, they are flattened and passed through a fully connected (FC) layer with 128 size, followed by a dropout layer and ReLU activation. We evaluate FedAK using four widely adopted datasets and analyze the results in detail. Finally, we compare FedAK performance with other state-of-the-art methods to highlight its effectiveness.

Experimental setup

All experiments were implemented in PyTorch 2.3.1 with CUDA 12.1 support, running under Python 3.11.3 on an NVIDIA Quadro RTX 6000 GPU. This configuration ensures both computational efficiency and reproducibility across heterogeneous models. All random seeds were fixed to maintain consistency across multiple runs. The main experiments are conducted with \:K=5 participating clients, each equipped with a distinct heterogeneous architecture as described in Sect. 3. We configured the feature embedding size to 128 dimensions, meaning that each client projects its public-data representation into a 128-dimensional feature vector before transmission. This setting was selected to balance representational expressiveness, attention-module complexity, and communication efficiency. The entire FedAK pipeline including client-side training, attention-based aggregation, and global distillation was executed in a single communication round, consistent with the one-shot learning paradigm.

Baselines

Our proposed method concentrates on tackling diversity challenges in one-shot FL, hence we omit specific comparative methodologies. We excluded methods necessitating further model downloads or datasets such as FedBE55 and FedGen36, as well as regularization techniques FedProx7, FedNova56, Scaffold17, as their underlying optimization frameworks are incompatible with the single communication round constraint. FedDCVAE22 is excluded due to its client-side data embedding and upload mechanism, which fundamentally contrasts with our methodology. FedAvg2 is excluded from the main one-shot comparison due to its lack of support for model heterogeneity, but is included alongside FedProx as a reference comparison in Sect. 4.6 to contextualize FedAK performance within the broader multi-round FL landscape. Additionally, Co-Boosting48 and FedHydra49 are excluded as both rely on server-side optimization procedures that scale with the number of distillation iterations, making direct comparison under a fixed computational budget infeasible without either underrepresenting their performance or granting them a disproportionately larger optimization budget. To facilitate a meaningful comparison, we select four benchmark methods that illustrate the dominant approaches to one-shot federated knowledge transfer: FedDF19, FedMD20, DENSE21 and FedOL44.

Training configuration

To ensure a fair and consistent evaluation across heterogeneous clients and datasets, all training processes in FedAK were carefully standardized while preserving the framework one-shot communication principle. The training configuration is organized into three distinct phases: client-side local training, server-side attention aggregation, and global student distillation. Each stage is optimized independently within the same experimental environment to maintain reproducibility and stability.

Client-side local training.

Client-side local training.

Each client performed local training for 50 epochs using Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with a momentum of \:0.9 and an initial learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:1{e}^{-2}$$\end{document}. The batch size was set to 64, and a cosine annealing scheduler was applied to smoothly reduce the learning rate over time. This schedule yielded better convergence and generalization compared to exponential decay. After local convergence, clients froze their model parameters and extracted 128-dimensional feature embeddings for all samples in the shared public dataset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{pub}$$\end{document}​. These features were transmitted once to the central server for aggregation.

(b) Server-side attention aggregation.

At the server, the additive attention module and its classifier head were trained on the labeled subset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{A}$$\end{document}​ of the public data for 50 epochs, using SGD with a learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:1{e}^{-1}$$\end{document} and momentum \:0.9. The attention module was implemented as a two-layer network with tanh activation and a learned context vector, following the additive attention formulation50. This design dynamically weighted the contributions of heterogeneous client features, enhancing their fusion effectiveness. The model was trained using a supervised cross-entropy objective combined with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{2}$$\end{document}​ regularization to improve generalization and prevent overfitting to small labeled subsets.

(c) Global student distillation.

The global student model was trained exclusively on the unlabeled subset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{B}$$\end{document}​ for 50 epochs under the supervision of pseudo-labels and aggregated features generated by the trained attention module. The optimization employed SGD with a learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:1{e}^{-2}$$\end{document} and momentum \:0.9, while the learning rate followed a cosine annealing schedule. The KD temperature was set to \:T=3, and the relative loss weighting parameters were \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:=0.5$$\end{document} for the KD term and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\gamma\:=0.5$$\end{document} for the feature-matching term. No ground-truth labels were used in this phase, maintaining the semi-supervised design of FedAK and ensuring that global training remained entirely privacy-preserving.

Evaluation

This section presents an in-depth evaluation of our proposed FedAK framework across multiple benchmark datasets and heterogeneity conditions. The experiments aim to validate its robustness, convergence stability, and performance advantages over state-of-the-art one-shot federated baselines.

Performance under non-IID heterogeneity

Across all datasets and heterogeneity settings, FedAK consistently delivers the highest performance and maintains a clear advantage over all one-shot FL baselines. As illustrated in Fig. 6, which reports the CIFAR-10 accuracy trajectories under varying non-IID strengths, FedAK demonstrates strong resilience, rising rapidly and stabilizing well above competing approaches. Under severe heterogeneity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:(\alpha\:=0.1)$$\end{document}, it converges to an accuracy of 59.26%, while FedDF, FedMD, and FedOL plateau at 47.96%, 45.09%, and 45.48% respectively, with DENSE remaining considerably lower at 40.18%. As the data distribution becomes more balanced \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:(\alpha\:=0.3)$$\end{document}, the performance of all methods improves. However, FedAK sustains its lead with 63.04%, maintaining a consistent advantage of approximately 8% over FedDF, FedMD, and FedOL. Even under milder conditions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:(\alpha\:=0.5)$$\end{document}, FedAK maintains its dominance with a stable accuracy of 66.74%, while FedDF, FedMD, and FedOL reach 59.58%, 59.45%, and 58.79% respectively, and DENSE trails behind at 57.49%. Notably, FedOL achieves competitive performance against FedDF and FedMD under moderate heterogeneity settings, yet consistently falls short of FedAK across all non-IID levels, demonstrating the advantage of feature-level attention aggregation over logit-level pseudo-label refinement.

A similar trend is observed on SVHN, as depicted in Fig. 7, where the relative performance gaps become even more pronounced in the highly heterogeneous case. At \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:=0.1$$\end{document}, FedAK achieves 66.57%, standing well above FedMD (56.12%), FedDF (54.45%), FedOL (57.87%), and DENSE (45.35%). As heterogeneity decreases, all methods improve, but FedAK consistently preserves its top position achieving 84.43% accuracy at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:=0.3$$\end{document} and 87.32% at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:=0.5$$\end{document}, while FedDF and FedMD remain slightly lower, FedOL shows slower convergence particularly under severe non-IID conditions, and DENSE continues to lag considerably. Moreover, FedAK learning curves display smoother and more stable convergence compared to all baselines throughout the training process.

For FashionMNIST, FedAK improvement is especially evident under the most challenging conditions. At \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:=0.1$$\end{document}, it achieves 61.84%, whereas FedDF, FedMD, and FedOL reach only 55.13%, 59.91%, and 60.25% respectively, with DENSE struggling to exceed 48.81%. As the data distribution becomes less skewed, FedAK continues to outperform its counterparts, achieving 87.75% at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:=0.3$$\end{document} and 90.74% at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:=0.5$$\end{document}, maintaining a consistent advantage of several percentage points over all baselines including FedOL which achieves 84.44% and 86.91% under the same settings.

On MNIST, which serves as the upper-bound benchmark for model performance, FedAK nearly saturates the achievable accuracy, recording 96.75%, 97.49%, and 98.30% for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:=\:$$\end{document}0.1, 0.3, and 0.5 respectively. FedOL achieves competitive results on this relatively simpler dataset, outperforming FedDF and FedMD across all heterogeneity levels, yet remaining consistently below FedAK. DENSE consistently lags behind, improving gradually from strong non-IID conditions to more balanced data distributions.

Overall, these findings clearly illustrate the strength of FedAK design in handling diverse and uneven data distributions. The additive attention mechanism serves as the core enabler, effectively identifying and emphasizing informative feature contributions from each client while down-weighting less useful or biased representations. This adaptive aggregation ensures that even clients with limited data or weaker models contribute meaningfully to the global representation. Meanwhile, the integration of KD and feature matching allows the global model to inherit this collective knowledge efficiently, learning from both labeled and unlabeled public data in a single communication round. Time complexity analysis

To better understand the computational efficiency of FedAK, we compare its overall time cost with existing one-shot FL baselines by examining the load on the clients, the server, and the global student model. Discussion

The experimental results presented in the previous section demonstrate the effectiveness of FedAK across multiple datasets and heterogeneity levels. This section complements the empirical evaluation by addressing four analytical dimensions: the privacy and leakage properties of feature-level transmission, the dependence of the framework on public dataset characteristics including quality and domain alignment, the theoretical justification for the chosen feature embedding dimension and its trade-offs and the connection between FedAK core design principles and broader uncertainty-aware and reliability-driven FL paradigms.

Privacy and leakage considerations

A central claim of FedAK is that transmitting intermediate feature representations extracted from public samples, rather than raw data, logits, gradients, or model parameters, can reduce privacy exposure and limit the attack surface. However, feature representations are not inherently privacy-free, and it is important to characterize the nature and limitations of this protection.

Regarding the risk of data reconstruction, transmitting raw pixels or gradient updates exposes clients to well-established inversion and membership inference attacks. Logit sharing, while less direct, still leaks task-specific decision boundaries and has been shown to enable model inversion under certain conditions12,13. Defense mechanisms against such attacks, including gradient inversion defenses and differential privacy applied to generative models, have been explored extensively in recent FL literature57,58. In FedAK, clients transmit only the 128-dimensional feature embeddings extracted from the shared public dataset, not from their private training data. Since these features are computed on public samples that the server already has access to, they do not carry private data statistics in the same sense as gradients or private-data logits. The transmitted features encode how each client model responds to public inputs, not what the client private data looks like.

Regarding distributional leakage, it is acknowledged that feature representations can implicitly reflect properties of the local training distribution, as a model trained on a skewed distribution will produce systematically different embeddings than one trained on a balanced distribution. This concern is particularly relevant in cross-device federated scenarios where clients operate under heterogeneous and non-IID conditions59. However, in FedAK this risk is structurally mitigated: the features transmitted are responses to fixed public inputs, meaning that any distributional signal they contain is indirect and entangled with the public data own characteristics, making it considerably harder to isolate private distribution information compared to transmitting features of private samples directly.

Furthermore, the one-shot communication constraint limits the attack surface considerably. Unlike multi-round FL where repeated gradient or feature exchanges enable temporal correlation attacks, FedAK clients communicate exactly once, providing an adversary with a single static snapshot that cannot be exploited across rounds.

Overall, while FedAK does not provide formal differential privacy guarantees, its design offers a practically meaningful privacy advantage over logit-sharing and parameter-sharing baselines by restricting the transmitted information to public-data responses within a single communication round. Integrating formal privacy mechanisms such as local differential privacy or feature perturbation into the FedAK pipeline remains a promising direction for future work.

Public dataset dependence and domain alignment

The performance of FedAK is inherently tied to the availability and quality of the shared public dataset, as it serves dual roles: providing labeled samples for attention module training and unlabeled samples for global student distillation. Three key characteristics of the public dataset warrant theoretical analysis: its size, quality, and domain alignment with client data.

Public dataset size. The attention module in FedAK is a relatively lightweight component, consisting of a linear projection layer and a trainable context vector, and is therefore less sensitive to the size of the labeled subset than a full classifier would be. Nevertheless, if the labeled subset were reduced substantially below 1,000 samples, the attention module may fail to learn discriminative weighting patterns across clients, particularly under severe non-IID conditions where the feature space is highly fragmented. The global student distillation benefits from a larger unlabeled subset, as a greater number of pseudo-labeled samples provides a richer supervisory signal and reduces the variance of the distillation objective. This theoretical analysis is empirically supported by the ablation study presented in Sect. 4.7, where increasing the public dataset size from 3,000 to 8,000 samples yields a consistent improvement in student test accuracy from 57.83% to 70.54% on CIFAR-10 under \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:=0.5$$\end{document}, confirming that public dataset size is a key factor governing the effectiveness of the distillation objective in FedAK.

Public dataset quality. The quality of pseudo-labels generated by the attention module directly determines the reliability of the supervisory signal for student distillation. When the public dataset contains noisy or ambiguous samples, the attention module may produce overconfident or inconsistent pseudo-labels, which in turn degrades the distillation objective. This risk is partially mitigated by the temperature scaling applied during pseudo-label generation, which softens the label distributions and reduces the sensitivity of the distillation loss to individual misclassifications. Furthermore, since the attention module is trained on labeled samples with ground truth supervision, its weighting behavior is anchored to true class boundaries rather than being entirely dependent on the unlabeled data distribution, providing an additional layer of robustness against noisy public samples.

Domain alignment. FedAK assumes that the public dataset is drawn from a distribution sufficiently related to the client data to produce meaningful feature representations. When the public dataset originates from a substantially different domain, the features extracted by client models on public samples may not faithfully reflect the knowledge encoded during private training, weakening the informativeness of the transmitted features. This is a general limitation shared by all public-data-dependent federated distillation methods. The sensitivity to domain shift naturally decreases as client feature extractors become more general, which is encouraged by the diverse heterogeneous architectures employed in FedAK, ranging from lightweight custom CNNs to deeper ResNet variants. In scenarios where a significant domain gap exists between the public dataset and client data, feature alignment techniques or self-supervised contrastive learning strategies could be adopted to bridge this gap. Specifically, contrastive objectives such as SimCLR60 or MoCo61 applied to the public dataset prior to feature extraction could encourage client models to produce more domain-invariant representations, reducing the sensitivity of the aggregated features to distribution mismatch and improving the reliability of the distillation process.

Feature-dimension trade-off

The empirical ablation study presented in Sect. 4.7.3 confirms that 128 dimensions represent an optimal trade-off between representational expressiveness and communication efficiency in FedAK. This choice is further supported by three complementary theoretical arguments. First, by the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma62, a 128-dimensional projection faithfully preserves pairwise distances between high-dimensional points with high probability, providing a theoretical basis for its adequacy in the classification tasks evaluated. Second, while logit transmission requires only one value per output class per sample, it encodes solely the final decision boundary information and discards the rich intermediate representational structure captured by the feature embedding, making the 128-dimensional feature vector a more informationally dense communication substrate. Third, the attention module projection matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{W}_{a}$$\end{document} maps from the feature dimension to the latent subspace, and a larger embedding dimension would increase the attention parameter count and require a proportionally larger labeled subset for reliable training, as empirically confirmed by the marginal gain observed when moving from 128 to 256 dimensions despite a doubled communication cost. Furthermore, the 128-dimensional projection layer serves as a shared interface across all heterogeneous client architectures, decoupling the feature dimension from each client model internal capacity and enabling consistent attention-based aggregation across architecturally diverse participants.

Reliability-aware and uncertainty-aware feature fusion

While FedAK is presented as a framework for one-shot heterogeneous FL, its core design principles align with broader research directions in uncertainty-aware and reliability-driven collaborative learning, extending its relevance beyond the specific federated setting.

The attention-based feature aggregation in FedAK can be interpreted as an implicit form of reliability estimation under distributional uncertainty. The context vector of the attention module learns to assign lower weights to client representations that deviate from the globally discriminative feature space, which naturally arises when clients operate under severe non-IID conditions. This behavior is conceptually related to uncertainty-guided fusion strategies explored in multimodal and distributed settings, where source reliability is estimated adaptively to improve robustness under incomplete or noisy observations63,64. In FedAK, client heterogeneity plays a role analogous to source uncertainty: just as unreliable sources are down-weighted in multimodal fusion to prevent degradation of the joint representation, unreliable client features are suppressed by the attention mechanism to protect the quality of the aggregated embedding.

This connection extends naturally to the broader paradigm of principled aggregation under heterogeneous source reliability. Recent works on federated uncertainty quantification, such as FedSI65 and FedKT66, demonstrate that accounting for client-level uncertainty during aggregation leads to more stable and generalizable global models. FedAK contributes to this direction by providing a lightweight, data-driven mechanism for reliability-aware aggregation that does not require explicit probabilistic modeling or Bayesian inference, making it practically applicable in one-shot settings where iterative uncertainty estimation is infeasible.

Furthermore, the pseudo-label generation stage of FedAK addresses distribution-level modeling under partial supervision. The attention module, trained on a small labeled public subset, generalizes its weighting behavior to the unlabeled subset, producing soft supervisory signals that reflect the collective reliability of client representations. This is related to approaches that handle partially labeled settings through adaptive representation weighting, as explored in uncertainty-aware perception frameworks64 and generative modality dropout strategies63, where incomplete supervision is compensated through learned source reliability. The client-server interaction design in FedAK also resonates with recent works on behavioral state assessment in client-server recognition systems67, which similarly address the challenge of aggregating heterogeneous client-side observations into a coherent server-side inference. Together, these connections demonstrate that FedAK design, while motivated by the specific constraints of one-shot FL, participates in a broader scientific conversation about reliable and efficient knowledge aggregation from heterogeneous, partially observable distributed sources.

Conclusion and future work

This work introduced FedAK, a one-shot FL framework with a server-side semi-supervised aggregation design, enabling effective collaboration among heterogeneous clients operating under strict communication and privacy constraints. Rather than relying on repeated parameter exchange or logit sharing, FedAK builds its global representation directly from client-extracted features, enabling the server to aggregate multi-source knowledge while avoiding the vulnerabilities typically associated with conventional one-shot aggregation. At the core of the method lies an additive attention mechanism that learns to weigh client contributions based on their reliability, expressiveness, and consistency. This design allows FedAK to adapt naturally to differences in data distributions and model capacities, providing a level of robustness that existing one-shot FL methods struggle to maintain. Our experiments across different datasets show that FedAK is able to consistently outperform four strong one-shot FL baselines across all evaluated datasets and non-IID settings. The framework tends to converge faster and with fewer oscillations, and it holds up well even when client distributions are heavily skewed. This suggests that combining feature-based aggregation with a lightweight distillation step is a promising direction for scenarios where communication needs to be minimized.

Despite these encouraging results, several directions remain open for further investigation. Evaluating FedAK in settings where public data is extremely scarce, noisy, or weakly aligned with client domains would provide a more complete understanding of its practical applicability. Additionally, exploring more adaptive variants of the attention mechanism, including context aware or meta learned weighting strategies, may further enhance its stability. Finally, while the present work focuses on a strictly one-shot learning, incorporating a small number of refinement rounds could be investigated to determine whether modest additional communication can yield further performance gains.
