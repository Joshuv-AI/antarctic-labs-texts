Extend-and-forward relaying through wireless frequency extension for sub-terahertz communication
Authors: Eray Güven, Louis Thivierge, Jean-François Frigon, Güneş Karabulut Kurt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study introduces extended-and-forward relaying for sub-terahertz (sub-THz) extended fronthaul link (EFL) via jointly implemented frequency division multiplexing at the fronthaul and wireless frequency extension for sub-THz relaying. EFL transmission and reception, phase noise (PN) induced symbol error probability, and operable frequency regions are modeled. Aggregated PN significance over distributedly synchronized (DS) and non-synchronized (NS) EFLs is compared. 180 GHz EFL on cascaded software-defined radio (SDR)-THz testbed is demonstrated with off-the-shelf SDRs, frequency extenders, and various radio frequency measurement tools. DS demonstration achieves 0 bit error rate over transmitted bits per user at measured signal-to-noise ratios between 11 and 13 dB, whereas NS shows 0.01 bit error floor with software controllable SDR clock aggregated 0.384 radian PN deviation at most. A digital post correction is proposed to suppress PN-related clock offset by dynamic phase correction, which achieves up to 0.088 BER decrement in NS, with the improvement varying by user and link distances.Keywords: Sub-terahertz communication, Frequency extension, Frequency division multiplexing, Software-defined radiosSubject terms: Engineering, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

In wireless communication, there is a growing demand for less congested fronthaul and wideband wireless backhaul links. To address these versatile demands, sub-terahertz (sub-THz) bands not only enable faster communication with wide spectrum availability, but also present segregable sub-bands that minimize the interference in fronthaul. An overview of the recent advancements in THz communication, use cases, challenges, and advancements is given in1. Other than macro-scale deployment challenges such as power inefficiency, high attenuation, and mobility, it has recently been shown that sub-THz communication faces design and link-level challenges, such as data rate scarcity, frequency sparsity, and hardware impairments2.

Several sub-THz and THz applications have been investigated in the literature, including long-distance backhaul links3 and indoor radio systems4–6. In7 and8, a phase noise (PN) resilient photonic-based 300 GHz wireless backhaul and fronthaul link is introduced. Multi-channel sub-THz and THz backhaul demonstrations in9,10 at 130 GHz and 220 GHz over distances exceeding 1 km. Recently, PN and peak-to-average-power ratio in sub-THz with orthogonal frequency division multiplexing (OFDM)11, where the modeling of PN is proposed with a Gaussian distribution in12. In the same monolithic scenario, the potential use of sub-THz targeting multi-users with leaky wave antennas in13 for downlink. Similarly, several mm-wave and sub-THz software-defined radio (SDR) demonstrations are gaining attention, such as14,]15.

SDRs provide low-cost and flexible real-time DSP capability while also offering reprogrammable FPGA-based RF front-end architectures. To overcome the data rate sufficiency challenge, two recent studies have deployed RF-SoC-based SDR implementations5,6. Another study develops the high-throughput SDR for mmWave beam management16. In6,17, a frequency multiplexing technique for ultrabroadband signal generation via SDR is demonstrated. As a low profile SDR unit, USRP-B200 provides futuristic applications based on SDR, such as CubeSats design, mobile communication, spectroscopy, and scientific observations18.

In contrast to the existing monolithic sub-THz use cases, we take advantage of a wireless frequency multiplexing technique19 with RF upconversion in the fronthaul, without using dedicated multiplexing and signal-separation hardware, such as multiplexers, demultiplexers, or diplexers. We refer to this architecture as extend-and-forward (EF) relaying. In this approach, a frequency extender (FEX) captures the sub-6 GHz multi-user frequency division multiplexing (FDM) signal over the air, upconverts it to the sub-THz band, and forwards it toward the BS, where downconversion is performed. This approach allows existing sub-6 GHz infrastructure to leverage the sub-THz spectrum without requiring additional resources. As a solution for ultrabroadband data generation from a monolithic source, EF relaying exploits the integration of independent users to aggregate sub-bands and achieve ultrabroadband transmission in sub-THz. An extended fronthaul link (EFL) between EF relay and BS, not only eliminates the self-interference, but also enables mobile users to benefit from sub-THz communication without mobility challenges20. This potential benefit serves at the main motivation of this work where we aimed its experimental demonstration.

Our work differs from recent single-link and single-transmitter sub-THz demonstrations, such as the D-band SDR link in21, the multi-subchannel digital subcarrier multiplexing scheme in22, and the single-source wideband OFDM demonstration in23, none of which perform wireless relaying of independently generated, frequency-multiplexed transmitters. EF relaying offers advantages over traditional THz relaying methods. Unlike amplify-and-forward techniques24, it inherently avoids amplifying interference to co-channel bands. Additionally, it achieves signal privacy benefits without the computational overhead associated with decode-and-forward relaying approaches25. Furthermore, unlike existing SDR-based sub-THz frequency extension studies that primarily focus on single-user link-level transmission, the proposed architecture performs real-time wireless frequency extension of aggregated frequency-division-multiplexed users. This enables multi-user sub-THz relaying without signal regeneration, extending frequency-extension techniques beyond conventional point-to-point operation. Wireless sub-THz multiplexing analysis

In this section, the electronics-based sub-THz up and downconversion is analyzed for 2 stages of cascaded EFL. In the first stage S(1), N users, each equipped with a single antenna and operating over a narrowband channel, are assigned sub-6 GHz uplink frequencies. Uplink user signals are multiplexed in frequency by a single antenna-equipped BS. In the second stage of S(2), the multiplexed signal is extended into the sub-THz band to be transmitted to a BS over EFL.

Each user is represented by a radio (by an SDR in experimental validation in Section “Experimental design”). BS is represented by a FEX-1 to perform joint multiplexing and frequency upconversion, and the CN is represented by the FEX-2 integrated with an SDR to perform sub-THz downconversion and message decoding. Instrumentation

The EF relaying experiment was conducted in an indoor lab environment with the SDR-THz testbed in the Poly-Grames Research Center30. Three independent Universal Serial Radio Peripheral (USRP) B200 (SDR-1, SDR-2, and SDR-3) were used to emit signals. They were each emitting over the air a predetermined message at different assigned carrier frequencies. Transmitter SDRs emit with an omnidirectional swivel dipole antenna of maximum 5 dBi gain. Sub-THz up and downconversion is performed by Virginia Diodes Inc. (VDI) frequency extension modules (FEX) with WR-5.1 input port flange. FEXs are referred to as FEX-1 and FEX-2 in upconversion and downconversion, respectively. FEX-1 and FEX-2 are used to perform the upconversion and the down conversion respectively with an integrated M=6 multiplier (a wideband doubler and trippler) which ends up with M+1 harmonics as explained in Section “Wireless sub-THz multiplexing analysis”. The selection of M=6 is determined by the architecture of the employed FEX modules. While the same carrier frequency may be generated using higher multiplication factors and lower source frequencies, larger multiplication factors increase harmonic generation, insertion loss, and phase-noise amplification. Among the available operating frequencies which are compatibale with system, 180 GHz was selected as a mid-band operating point sufficiently far from the cutoff regions of the front-end hardware. Both FEX-1 and FEX-2 are equipped with directive horn antennas, with a gain of 11 dBi and a 3 dB beamwidth of on the E-plane and on the H-plane. A detailed radiation analysis for the front-end in31. Digital design details

All SDRs data and power connections are done over USB 3.0 via UHD 4.7. IF bandwidth in S(1) is proportional with 2 baseband sampling rate and the oversampling ratio of 4. Targeting the 400 kHz IF bandwidth for each user SDR (with usable bandwidth due to root raised cosine (RRC) pulse shaping and built-in half band filter), 32 MHz master clock rate with 32 interpolation factor and decimation. In the reception on SDR-4, a matched filter to the transceiver’s RRC taps mitigates inter-symbol interference (ISI) and downsamples afterwards. Adaptive filter algorithm of decision-directed (DD) least mean square (LMS) equalizer blindly equalizes the channel while CFO is estimated and compensated by digital phase-locked loop (DPLL)38.

Timing synchronization39 tunes the sampling time to maximize the correlation of transmitted and detected receieved symbols, while algorithm40 estimates the SNR by the kurtosis of the signal and the noise for 100 sample interval. IQ symbols are collected and analyzed over SDR-4 and SA. z(t) on SDR-4 is windowed with Blackman-Harris before the discrete Fourier transform (DFT), and the power is normalized with maximum power spectrum density (PSD). SDR-4 utilizes built-in bandpass filters to eliminate undesired users. Lastly, each 100 symbols are packed as 1 frame, where the instantaneous BER for each frame is calculated and illustrated in Figs. 5 and 7. The digital design is further enhanced through the post-processing DPC algorithm, which is detailed in the following subsection. The decision boundary π /M and the PN-resilience criterion supports the CFO and SNR estimation blocks.

Digital post correction (DPC)

Based on the knowledge of aggregated SCO due to the phase jitter, several steps of the DPC algorithm aim to break down the phase reliance on each DSP step at the receiver. The proposed DPC chain is applied exclusively to the NS receiver architecture. It consists of two main stages. First, an AGC-based compensation is applied to mitigate amplitude fluctuations and restore a nearly constant-envelope received signal. Second, several conventional synchronization blocks are replaced with alternative synchronization methods that are less dependent on carrier phase information, at the expense of reduced PLL tracking capability and slower convergence. After digital downconversion from IF to baseband, DPC decimates each sample and normalize the fast fluctuations with digital AGC41 with attack and decay rate of 1. Following this, the DD-based Mueller & Müller algorithm39 is replaced with Gardner42, which estimates timing error from midpoint samples. Figure 6 shows IQ symbols with DPC. Similarly, the DD adaptive filter is replaced with the constant modulus algorithm (CMA)43 with 0.001 convergence rate, where the linear equalization is placed based on the constant amplitude instead of the phase. Since CMA adaptation relies exclusively on signal magnitude, the proposed DPC constitutes a suboptimal solution relative to fully phase-aware synchronization schemes.

Overall, the six tested configurations demonstrate that synchronization architecture, rather than link distance alone, is the primary determinant of EFL performance, with DPC offering a practical middle ground between the non-synchronized and distributedly synchronized extremes. NS-6B and NS-17B are dominated by SCO-induced degradation, with average BER of 0.130 and 0.115, respectively, and SNR as low as -12.12 dB for the worst-case user. DS-6B is the strongest configuration, achieving zero measured bit errors at 11–13 dB SNR, while DS-17B trades SNR for distance (falling below 5.5 dB) yet avoids the persistent bit-error floor seen in the NS cases. Applying DPC to the NS cases lowers the average BER to 0.060 (NS-6B) and 0.070 (NS-17B), up to 0.088 for the best-case user, at the cost of a higher residual CFO. These results confirm that DS remains the strongest configuration, DPC offers a practical partial correction for NS deployments, and both BER and CFO are governed by the same PN/SCO mechanisms.

Conclusion

Extend-and-Forward (EF) relaying is introduced for sub-THz extended fronthaul links (EFL), toward integration with existing sub-6 GHz infrastructure. In this framework, frequency-division multiplexed (FDM) multi-user signals operating in the sub-6 GHz band are wirelessly extended to the sub-THz domain.

The backbone of EFL signal modeling is established, addressing the primary challenge of phase noise (PN) induced symbol error probability and identifying operable frequency regions. The impact of clock synchronization on sampling offset is demonstrated with real-time both in the signal and symbol domains using a 180 GHz EFL setup on an SDR-THz testbed. Compared to the non-synchronized case, distributed clock synchronization across sub-THz up and down-converters achieves lower bit error rates and higher SNR, even under low spectral power conditions. Our proposed DPC algorithm further reduces the bit error rate by up to 0.088, despite the presence of a residual clock offset that introduces a bit error floor, enhancing performance over non-synchronized cases. Prospective use cases are presented, including 1) EF repeaters for EFL to enable interference-free relaying, and 2) an ultrabroadband sub-THz backhaul link for multiple access.

We note that the present validation is a proof-of-concept and has limitations regarding the tested link distances, the number of simultaneous users, and system-level applicability toward expansion to a full multi-cellular or multi-hop deployment. Furthermore, the DS configuration, which achieves the best measured performance, relies on a shared local oscillator across FEX-1 and FEX-2, and therefore imposes precise synchronization requirements that are not readily scalable to distributed, geographically separated relay deployments. The future work directions below are aimed directly at addressing these four limitations: distance, user scale, system-level applicability, and synchronization.

As future work, we plan to investigate the impact of misalignment using a heterogeneous SDR-THz testbed that integrates SDR technologies of varying quality and data rates, aiming to explore the performance limits beyond the tested distances. From the implementation side, we aim to extend the current set-up by active RF components, transitioning from a conceptual study toward real-time applications, moving toward the system-level applicability noted above. This will provide a means to quantify the impact of the impairments, including the synchronization overhead inherent to the DS configuration. Other open issues include exploring the EFL setup in a multi-cellular scenario where a higher number of users are considered, addressing the multi-user scalability limitation discussed above.
