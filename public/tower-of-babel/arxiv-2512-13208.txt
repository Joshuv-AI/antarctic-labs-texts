A tropical version of Martens’ theorem for metric graphs Thanks: Both authors are members of the INDAM group GNSAGA. The second author is supported by Alexander von Humboldt Foundation.
Giusi Capobianco, Angelina Zheng

Abstract
. We study the conjecture stated by Jensen and Len on a tropical version on Martens’ theorem via the Brill–Noether rank of a tropical curve. We recall Coppens’ counterexample of Martens-special chain of cycles, and we generalize the construction defining another class of graphs, Martens-special trees of cycles, for which the conjecture does not hold in a similar setting. These are not the only counterexamples. However, we prove that the conjecture holds for all metric graphs with a stricter assumption on the degree in the Brill–Noether rank.



1.
Introduction

The classical Martens’ theorem provides insight into the structure and properties of algebraic curves characterizing hyperelliptic curves and refining the result obtained by Clifford
[
Cli78
]
,
[
ACGH85
, Chapter III]
, as it offers an understanding of the Brill–Noether loci.
This result determines their dimensions and plays a significant role in various geometric constructions and classifications in Brill–Noether theory.
Let us recall Martens’ theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

[
ACGH85
, Theorem 5.1]

Let
C
be a smooth curve of genus
g,
and
d,r
integers satisfying
0<2r\leq d<g.
Then

\operatorname{dim}W_{d}^{r}(C)\leq d-2r,
and equality holds precisely when
C
is hyperelliptic.

In tropical geometry, the Brill–Noether theory has been adapted to study the combinatorial analogs of these algebraic objects. The tropical version of Brill–Noether theory translates problems about divisors on algebraic curves into problems about divisors on metric graphs.
The divisor theory on graphs and metric graphs was developed by Baker and Norine in
[
BN09
]
,
[
Bak08
]
and later studied by many other authors.
One of the most useful result is Baker’s specialization Lemma, extended by Caporaso
[
Cap12
]
, which gives a relation between the rank of a divisor and that of its tropicalization, namely the rank of a divisor can only increase under the specialization map.
This relation between curves and graphs provides alternative proofs for classical algebraic results, such as the Brill–Noether theorem (
[
CDPR12
]
).

In the past decades tropical analogs of well-known algebraic theorems such as the Riemann–Roch theorem and Clifford’s theorem, have been proved by various authors; see, for instance,
[
GK08
]
,
[
AC13
]
,
[
MZ08
]
,
[
Fac10
]
,
[
Cop16
]
.
However, it is not always true that the same statements hold in tropical geometry. An example is indeed Martens’ theorem: the naive tropical analog of the above theorem does not hold and the known statement for abstract tropical curves is the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

[
Len17
, Theorem A.1]

Let
\Gamma
be a metric graph of genus
g,
and
d,r
integers satisfying
0<2r\leq d<g.
Then

\operatorname{dim}W_{d}^{r}(\Gamma)\leq d-2r
and equality holds when
\Gamma
is hyperelliptic. Moreover, equality may also hold for
\Gamma
non-hyperelliptic.

In
1
we will present, for instance, a non-hyperelliptic metric graph for which
\operatorname{dim}W_{d}^{r}(\Gamma)=d-2r.

Jensen and Len suggested in
[
Len17
]
that, in order to have a tropical analog of Martens’ theorem, one should replace the dimension of the Brill–Noether locus with the
Brill–Noether rank
, first introduced in
[
LPP12
]
.
The Brill–Noether rank
w_{d}^{r}(\Gamma)
of a tropical curve
\Gamma
, contrary to the dimension of the Brill–Noether locus
\operatorname{dim}W_{d}^{r}(\Gamma)
, varies upper semi-continuously on the moduli space of tropical curves, see
[
Len14
, Theorem 5.4]
and
[
LPP12
, Theorem 1.2, 1.6 and 1.7]
. Therefore, it might be a good candidate for the analog of Martens’ theorem for tropical curves.

Proposition 1.3
.

[
Len17
, Propostion A.3]

Let
\Gamma
be a metric graph of genus
g
, and let
r,d
be as in the conditions of Martens’ theorem. Then

w_{d}^{r}(\Gamma)\leq d-2r.

Moreover, for hyperelliptic graphs,
w_{d}^{r}(\Gamma)=d-2r.

Furthermore, Jensen and Len have conjectured that equality holds only for hyperelliptic tropical curves, suggesting the following tropical version of Martens’ theorem.

Conjecture 1
.

[
Len17
]

Let
\Gamma
be a metric graph of genus
g,
and
d,r
as above. Then

w_{d}^{r}(\Gamma)\leq d-2r,
and equality holds precisely when
\Gamma
is hyperelliptic.

Recently, Coppens has proved that the conjecture holds for chains of cycles of genus
g
for rank
r
and degree
d
such that
1\leq r\leq g-2
and
2r\leq d\leq g-3+r
.

Theorem 1.4
.

[
Cop25
, Theorem A]

Let
\Gamma
be a chain of cycles of genus
g
. Let
r
be an integer with
1\leq r\leq g-2
and let
d
be an integer with
2r\leq d\leq g-3+r
. Then
w^{r}_{d}(\Gamma)=d-2r
implies
\Gamma
hyperelliptic.

However, Conjecture
1
is not true in its full generality. In
[
Cop25
]
, the author provides counterexamples to such a conjecture proving the existence of graphs of genus
g\geq 2r+3
called
Martens-special
chains of cycles which are not hyperelliptic and satisfy the equality
w^{r}_{g-2+r}(\Gamma)=g-2-r
.

Theorem 1.5
.

[
Cop25
, Theorem B]

A chain of cycles
\Gamma
satisfies
w^{r}_{g-2+r}(\Gamma)=g-2-r
for some integer
1\leq r\leq g-2
if and only if either
\Gamma
is hyperelliptic or
\Gamma
is a Martens-special chain of cycles of rank
r
.

We further investigate this conjecture by first generalizing Coppens’ counterexample of Martens-special chains of cycles to a class of graphs that we will call
Martens-special trees of cycles
.
More precisely, for these graphs, we first prove the analog statements of Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
.
Moreover, we will show that these are not the only graphs for which Conjecture
1
fails.
Indeed, we will see that also graphs that are obtained by changing the length of some edge in a hyperelliptic graph represent also a counterexample.
However, if we impose a stricter bound on the degree then Conjecture
1
is true.
The precise statement of our main theorem is the following.

Theorem A
.

Let
\Gamma
be a metric graph of genus
g,
and
d,r
integers with
0<2r\leq d\leq g-3+r
. Then

w_{d}^{r}(\Gamma)\leq d-2r.
Equality holds precisely when
\Gamma
is hyperelliptic.

This in particular provides an alternative proof of the tropical Clifford’s theorem when
d,r,g
are positive integers satisfying
2r\leq d\leq g-3+r
.

We will first recall in Section
2
the notions and definitions of divisor theory on metric graphs.
In Section
3
, we will present Coppens’ counterexample for Conjecture
1
and construct new classes of graphs for which the conjecture is again not true. Finally, in Section
4
we will prove that, restricting to
d\leq g-3+r
, the conjecture holds.

2.
Divisor theory on metric graphs

In this section, we recall the basic definitions and properties of divisor theory on metric graphs that we need throughout the article. For more details, see
[
BN09
]
,
[
BF11
]
,
[
Bak08
]
, and
[
Len25
]
.

A
metric graph
is a metric space
\Gamma
such that there exist a graph
G
and a length function on its set of edges
l\colon E(G)\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that
\Gamma
is obtained from
(G,l)
by gluing intervals
[0,l(e)]
for
e\in E(G)
at their endpoints, as prescribed by the combinatorial data of
G
.
We say that
(G,l)
is a
model
for
\Gamma
and the
genus
of
\Gamma
is the first Betti number of
G
. Notice that the genus is independent of the choice of the model.
We will only consider finite, connected and weightless graphs.

The
distance

d(x,y)
between two points
x,y\in\Gamma
is the length of the shortest path between them.
For any point
x
, its
valence
, denoted
\operatorname{val}(x)
, is the number of connected components in
U_{x}\setminus\{x\}
, where
U_{x}
is an arbitrarily small open neighborhood containing
x
.

The
canonical model

(G_{0},l_{0})
of
\Gamma
is the model obtained by taking as its set of vertices

V(G_{0})=\{x\in\Gamma|\,\mathrm{val}(x)\geq 3\}.

Unless otherwise specified, we will work with the canonical model of a metric graph. In particular, we say that
G_{0}
is the
underlying combinatorial graph
of
\Gamma
.

Moreover, let us recall that a metric graph is said to be
k
-edge connected
if the removal of any subset of
l\leq k-1
edges in its canonical model does not disconnect the graph. A
k
-edge cut
is a set of
k
edges, in the canonical model, whose removal disconnects the graph. All incident edges to a vertex clearly form a
k
-edge cut, this will be called
trivial
.

A
divisor

D
is a formal sum
D=\sum_{p\in\Gamma}a_{p}p,
with
a_{p}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
a_{p}\neq 0
for finitely many
p\in\Gamma
.
Its
degree
is
\deg(D)=\sum_{p\in\Gamma}a_{p}
and the
group of divisors
of
\Gamma
is
\mathrm{Div}(\Gamma),

and it decomposes as

\operatorname{Div}(\Gamma)=\cup_{d\in\mathbb{Z}}\operatorname{Div}^{d}(\Gamma),

where
\operatorname{Div}^{d}(\Gamma)
is the group of divisors of degree
d
.
A divisor
D=\sum_{p\in\Gamma}a_{p}p
is called
effective
, and we write
D\geq 0,
if

a_{p}\geq 0
, for all
p\in\Gamma
.
We also define the
support
of
D
as the set
\operatorname{Supp}(D)=\{p\in\Gamma|a_{p}\neq 0\}.

A
rational function
on
\Gamma
is a continuous, piecewise linear function
f\colon\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
whose slopes are all integers.
The
principal divisor
associated to the rational function
f
is

\ddiv(f)=\sum_{P\in\Gamma}\sigma_{p}(f)p,

where
\sigma_{p}(f)
is the sum of the slopes of
\Gamma
in all directions emanating from
p
.
Two divisor
D_{1},D_{2}
are
linearly equivalent
, and we write
D_{1}\sim D_{2}
, if their difference is a principal divisor.

The (
Baker and Norine
)
rank
of a divisor
D
on
\Gamma
is

\displaystyle\operatorname{r}(D)=\max\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:\forall\,E\in\mathrm{Div}^{k}(\Gamma);E\geq 0,\exists E^{\prime}\geq 0\text{ such that }D-E\sim E^{\prime}\},

or
\operatorname{r}(D)=-1
if the above set is empty.

From
[
BN09
, Corollary]
, the contraction of bridges, i.e.
1
-edge cuts, does not change the rank. From now on we will only consider metric graphs without points of valence
1
(for metric graphs with leaves, results on their Brill–Noether loci are the same).

Let us recall the Riemann–Roch theorem for metric graphs, first proved for combinatorial graphs in
[
BN07
]
, that will be used throughout the paper.

Theorem 2.1
.

[
GK08
,
MZ08
]

Let
D\in\operatorname{Div}(\Gamma),
and
K
be the canonical divisor of
\Gamma.
Then

\operatorname{r}(D)-\operatorname{r}(K-D)=\operatorname{deg}(D)+1-\operatorname{g}(\Gamma).

It is an immediate consequence of the Riemann–Roch theorem that if
\operatorname{deg}(D)\geq\operatorname{g}(\Gamma)+1
, then
\operatorname{r}(D)\geq 1
.

In order to determine if the rank of a given divisor is greater or equal than
1,
we will also make extensive use of Dhar’s burning algorithm,
[
Dha90
]
.
Given an effective divisor
D\in\operatorname{Div}(\Gamma)
and a point
w\in\Gamma
, Dhar’s burning algorithm consists in starting a fire at
w,
which spreads along each edge incident to
w
. Any
x\in\Gamma
with
D(x)>0
can control the fire in
D(x)
directions: if
D(x)
is bigger or equal than the number of burnt edges incident to
x
then the fire stops at
x
, otherwise it burns
x
and continues to spread along all remaining edges incident to
x
.
Following
[
BS13
]
, if the entire graph burns, then
D-w
is a
w
-reduced divisor and in particular
\operatorname{r}(D-w)=-1
, meaning that it cannot be linearly equivalent to an effective divisor, therefore
\operatorname{r}(D)<1
.

Definition 1
.

For non-negative integers
r
and
d
, the
Brill–Noether locus

W^{r}_{d}(\Gamma)
is the set of divisor classes of degree
d
and rank at least
r
on the metric graph
\Gamma
.

As already mentioned in the introduction, by
[
Len17
, Theorem A.1]
, unlike the algebraic case, it is not true that
\operatorname{dim}W_{d}^{r}=d-2r
only for hyperelliptic graphs. Here is an example.

Example 1
.

Figure
2.1
shows a non-hyperelliptic metric graph with two divisors
x+y+z_{i}\in W_{3}^{1}(\Gamma),
for
i=1,2.

Moreover, one can check that any
x+y+z
;
z\in\overline{z_{1}z_{2}}
, is a divisor of degree
3
and rank
1
, therefore
\operatorname{dim}W_{3}^{1}(\Gamma)=3-2\cdot 1.

x
y
z_{1}
x
y
z_{2}

Figure 2.1
.
A metric graph
\Gamma
with two non-equivalent divisors of degree 3 and rank 1. Here
\operatorname{dim}W^{1}_{3}(\Gamma)=1.

The Brill–Noether rank is defined as follows.

Definition 2
.

[
Len17
, Definition A.2]
The
Brill–Noether rank
of a metric graph
\Gamma
is the integer

w_{d}^{r}(\Gamma):=\operatorname{max}\{\rho;\text{ for any }E\in Div_{+}^{r+\rho}(\Gamma);\exists\,D\in W_{d}^{r}(\Gamma)\text{ such that }\operatorname{Supp}(E)\subseteq\operatorname{Supp}(D)\}.

3.
Counterexamples for Jensen–Len’s conjecture

In this section we first recall the metric graphs studied by Coppens in
[
Cop25
]
and
[
Cop24
]
, namely
Martens-special chains of cycles
.
Coppens proved that for such graphs Conjecture
1
does not always hold.
Later, we will also provide other counterexamples, first by slightly modifying such graphs.

3.1.
Martens-special chains of cycles

Let us first recall the definition of Martens-special chains of cycles, as defined by Coppens.
Let
\Gamma
be a chain of cycles of genus
g.
Then
\Gamma
has
g
cycles
C_{1},\dots,C_{g},
where
C_{1},C_{g}
are loops and the cycles
C_{2},\dots,C_{g-1}
have vertices
v_{2},w_{2},\dots,v_{g-1},w_{g-1}
as in Figure
3.1
.

\dots
C_{1}
C_{2}
C_{3}
C_{g}
v_{2}
w_{2}
v_{3}
w_{3}

Figure 3.1
.
A chain of cycles of genus
g
.

Recall that contraction of bridges does not change the rank, so we will assume that the bridges have arbitrary lengths (also zero).

For
i=2\dots,g-1,
denote by
l_{i}
the length of the
i
-th cycle and define the integers

(1)

m_{i}=\begin{cases}\frac{l_{i}}{d(v_{i},w_{i})}&\text{ if }\frac{l_{i}}{d(v_{i},w_{i})}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Let us observe that
m_{i}=2
for any
i=2\dots,g-1,
if and only if the chain of cycle is hyperelliptic,
[
Cha13
, Section 3]
. Moreover, we will simply write
hyperelliptic cycle
when referring to a
cycle
C_{1},C_{g}
or
C_{i}
with
m_{i}=2.
We will say that it is non-hyperelliptic otherwise. In what follows, we will consider non-hyperelliptic chains of cycles. We refer to
[
Cop25
, Section 4]
for more details.

Definition 3
.

If
\Gamma
is a chain of cycles of genus
g\geq 2r+3
then it is
Martens-special
of rank
r
and type
k\geq 1
if there exist integers
j_{1},\dots j_{k}
such that

(1)

r+1<j_{1}<\dots<j_{k}<g-r;

(2)

j_{i+1}-j_{i}\geq r+1
for each
1\leq i\leq k-1
;

(3)

m_{s}\neq 2
if
s=j_{1},\dots,j_{k}
and
m_{s}=2
if
s\neq j_{1},\dots,j_{k}
.

From the definition, one can think of a Martens-special chain of cycle of rank
r
and type
k
as a chain of cycles with
k
non-hyperelliptic cycles, such that at least the first
r+1
and the last
r+1
cycles are hyperelliptic and between any two non-hyperelliptic cycles, there are at least
r
hyperelliptic ones.
Let us notice, moreover, that a Martens-special chain of rank
r,
then satisfies the above conditions for any
r^{\prime},
with
1\leq r^{\prime}\leq r.

Coppens proved that on such graphs Conjecture
1
holds for
d\leq g-3+r
while they are counterexamples when
d=g-2
and
g\geq 2r+3
(see Theorems
1.4
,
1.5
). For chains of cycles of genus
g
there is a necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of a divisor
of degree
d
and rank
r
, which is the existence of a function on a rectangle with

g-d+r
columns and
r+1
rows
called
\underline{m}
-displacement tableau, where
\underline{m}
is the vector defined by (
1
). For more details see
[
CDPR12
]
,
[
Cop25
, Theorem 1]
.

We now provide an explicit example of a Martens-special chain of cycles and prove that the equality in Theorem
1.5
holds for
r=1
.
We first notice that, given a divisor
D
of degree
d\leq g
on a chain of cycles we can always characterize it as follows.

Definition 4
.

We say that an effective divisor
D
on a chain of cycles
\Gamma
is
cycle-reduced
if
\operatorname{deg}(D|_{\gamma})\leq 1
for any cycle
\gamma\in\Gamma
and
\operatorname{deg}(D|_{b})=0
for any bridge
b
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
D
be a divisor of degree
d\leq g
on a chain of cycles of genus
g.
Then there exist a cycle-reduced divisor
D^{\prime}
such that
D^{\prime}\sim D.

Proof.

First, we may always assume that none of the points in the support of
D
lies on a bridge.
If two points are in the same cycle, one could take the linearly equivalent divisor where one of the two points is moved to the closest vertex and then again the linearly equivalent one obtained by moving such a point to the adjacent cycle through the bridge. Because of the condition on the degree, for any pairs of points in each cycle, there is always an empty one where we can move one of the two points.
∎

.

We consider a chain of cycle of genus
5,
with
m_{3}=3
and
m_{2}=m_{4}=2
, as in Figure
3.2
.
One can check from Definition
3
that it is Martens-special of rank and type
1.

Let us show that
w^{1}_{4}=2,
according to Theorem
1.5
.
By definition of Brill–Noether rank, this is equivalent to show that for any effective
E\in\operatorname{Div}^{3}(\Gamma)
, there exists
p\in\Gamma
such that
E+p\in W_{4}^{1}(\Gamma).

Let
E=\sum_{i=1}^{3}x_{i}
for some
x_{i}\in\Gamma,
and by Lemma
3.1
we may assume that it is cycle-reduced. We have two possibilities.

(1)

If
\operatorname{deg}(E|_{C_{3}})=0
, we consider the point
x_{i}
in the component of
\Gamma\setminus C_{3}
where the degree of the divisor is 1 (
x_{1}
in the Figure

). Then we choose
p
as the image of

Then
E+p\sim 2v_{3}+x_{j}+x_{k}
(or

.
The divisor

. These linear equivalences are shown in Figure

is

3.2.
Martens-special trees of cycles

Let us first recall some definitions and notations from
[
BM08
, 5.2]
that we will use to define a new class of graphs.

A
block
of a graph is a connected subgraph with no separating vertices and it is maximal with respect
to this property.
Moreover, from
[
BM08
, 5.2]
, the blocks of a connected graph
G
form a decomposition and fit together in a treelike structure.
We associate to
G
a bipartite graph
T_{G}
with bipartition
(B,S)
, where
B
is the set of blocks of
G
and
S
the set of separating vertices of
G
. A block
B
and a separating vertex
v
are adjacent in
T_{G}
if and only if
B
contains

v
.
The resulting graph
T_{G}
is therefore a tree, called the
block tree
of
G.

With abuse of notation, we will use the same terms when referring to metric graphs.

Definition 5
.

A
tree of cycles
is a metric graph
\Gamma
whose block-tree
T_{\Gamma}
is such that

(1)

any subgraph corresponding to a path in
T_{\Gamma}
is a chain of cycles, up to contraction of bridges corresponding to the endpoints of the path and

(2)

any component in the block decomposition of
\Gamma
corresponding to a vertex in
T_{\Gamma}
of valence at least
3
is a vertex of the same valence in
\Gamma
.

Notice that from the above definition the graph in Figure
3.5
is not a tree of cycles, while the one in Figure
3.7
is a tree of cycles. Indeed, in Figure
3.5
, the block tree has a unique vertex of valence
3
, which corresponds to the cycle defined by three edges, hence not a vertex.

Remark 1
.

Let us observe that Lemma
3.1
naturally extends to trees of cycles: given an effective divisor of degree equal or smaller than the genus of a metric tree of cycles we can always find a linearly equivalent divisor that is cycle-reduced.

We will prove that the statement in Theorem
1.4
generalizes to trees of cycles. Let
us first show that any non-hyperelliptic metric graph that satisfies Conjecture
1
for
r=1
, also satisfies it for higher rank.
Then it is sufficient to consider

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\Gamma

Proof.

By definition,

\operatorname{r}(E+p)=0

Assume by contradiction that

The assumption

For example

Then, adding

Choose

Then

In other words, there exists

Definition 6
.

Let
\Gamma
be a tree of cycles whose vertices corresponding to vertices of valence at least 3 in its block tree

are denoted by
v_{1},\dots,v_{t}.

A tree of cycles

(P1)

It is hyperelliptic.

(P2)

It is Martens-special of rank

or

(P3)

It is a chain of cycles that becomes Martens-special of rank
r^{\prime}
and type

by adding at some

a hyperelliptic chain of cycles of genus

.

Here,

is the number of non-hyperelliptic cycles.

.

In the above definition, when we consider the subgraph

This is because, from Definition

r
.

In the above definition, if the block tree

is a path we recover the definition of a Martens-special chain of cycles of rank

and type

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\Gamma
be a Martens–special tree of cycles of genus
g
and rank
r\leq g-2
.
Then
w_{g-2+r}^{r}(\Gamma)=g-2-r.

Proof.

Let us assume that
\Gamma
has a block-tree
T_{\Gamma}
with precisely a vertex
v
of valence
3
: the complement of a chain of cycles corresponding to a maximal path is again a chain of cycles.
The proof for the general case can be obtained inductively by gluing chains of cycles to
\Gamma
.

Let
E
be any cycle-reduced divisor of degree
g-2
. We need to show that there exist
p_{1},\dots,p_{r}
such that
E+\sum_{i=1}^{r}p_{r}
has rank
r
.

We consider a maximal chain
\Gamma_{1}
such that
\operatorname{deg}(E_{1})=g(\Gamma_{1})-2,
where
E_{1}:=E|_{\Gamma_{1}}.

Let us assume that
\Gamma_{1}
satisfies property (P3) in Definition
6
, then we replace the vertex
v
with a hyperelliptic chain
H
of genus
r
and obtain a Martens-special chain
\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}
of rank
r
. This is clear in the case
r=1.
If
r>1
this is also true because by definition there is at least a Martens-special chain of cycles contained in
\Gamma
of rank (at least)
r
and the two subgraphs in
\Gamma_{1}
obtained by removing
v
must be contained in Martens-special chains of rank higher or equal than
r
.

Then by Theorem
1.5
if we consider the effective divisor
E_{1}+q_{1}+\dots+q_{r}
on
\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}
, with
q_{i}
in the interior of distinct edges, each in a distinct cycle of
H
, then there exist
q^{\prime}_{i}
such that
\operatorname{r}(E_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}(q_{i}+q_{i}^{\prime}))\geq r
on
\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}.

If
q_{i}^{\prime}\not\in H,
for any
i
, then we take
p_{i}=q_{i}^{\prime}
and
\operatorname{r}(E_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}p_{i})\geq r
on
\Gamma_{1}.
Therefore
E_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}\sim r\cdot v+E_{1}^{\prime}
with
\operatorname{Supp}E_{1}^{\prime}\subset\Gamma_{1}
. Thus
E+\sum_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}\sim r\cdot v+E|_{\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{1}}+E_{1}^{\prime}
which restricted to
\overline{\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{1}}
has also rank (at least)
r
by Riemann–Roch.

If
q_{i}^{\prime}\in H,
for some
i\in\{1,\dots,r\}
, then for each of such
i
we have two possibilities: either
E_{1}+q_{i}+q_{i}^{\prime}\sim E_{1}+2w,
with
w
one of the two points in the boundary
\partial H
in
\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}
, or
E_{1}+q_{i}+q_{i}^{\prime}\sim E_{1}+w+w^{\prime},
with
w^{\prime}\in H,
not a vertex (up to relabeling the points). In the latter case, we just repeat the above argument and then
w\notin H
behaves as
q_{i}^{\prime}\not\in H
.

Otherwise we may assume
E_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}(q_{i}+q_{i}^{\prime})\sim E_{1}+(2s)w+\sum_{j=s+1}^{r}(q_{j}+q_{j}^{\prime}),
for the first
s
pairs of points, with
s;\,1\leq s\leq r.

Then we have

(3)

\operatorname{r}(E_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}(q_{i}+q_{i}^{\prime}))=\operatorname{r}(E_{1}+(2s)w+\sum_{j=s+1}^{r}(q_{j}+q_{j}^{\prime}))\geq r\qquad\text{ on }\Gamma^{\prime}_{1}.

Notice that since
H
is hyperelliptic and
q_{j}^{\prime}\notin H
for
j=s+1,\dots,r,
then

(4)

\operatorname{r}(E_{1}+(2s)v+\sum_{j=s+1}^{r}q_{j}^{\prime})\geq r\qquad\text{ on }\Gamma_{1}.

Moreover, removing
s
points cannot decrease the rank more than
s
, then from (
3
) we have

(5)

\operatorname{r}(E_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}q_{i}+\sum_{j=s+1}^{r}q_{j}^{\prime})\geq r-s\qquad\text{ on }\Gamma^{\prime}_{1}.

Again, if we restict to
\Gamma_{1},
then

(6)

\operatorname{r}(E_{1}+\sum_{j=s+1}^{r}q_{j}^{\prime})\geq r-s\qquad\text{ on }\Gamma_{1}.

Then we take
p_{j}=q_{j}^{\prime}
for
j=s+1,\dots,r
and for
i=1,\dots,s,
let
p_{i}
be a point in the
i
-th closest cycle in
{\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{1}}
to
v
at the same distance from
v
as the point already in the support of
E
.
Notice that we have already observed that when
r>1
the subgraph in
{\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{1}}
incident
v
has to contain (at least)
r\geq s
consecutive hyperelliptic cycles.
Then

(7)

E+\sum_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}\sim E_{1}+(2s)v+\sum_{j=s+1}^{r}p_{j}+R,

for some effective divisor
R
with
\operatorname{Supp}(R)\subset\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{1}
and degree
g(\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{1})-s
.
Such a divisor has rank (at least)
r
on
\Gamma_{1}
from (
4
).
Moreover from (
6
), we can write
E_{1}+\sum_{j=s+1}^{r}p_{j}\sim(r-s)v+R^{\prime},
with
\operatorname{Supp}(R^{\prime})\subset\Gamma_{1}.
Then (
7
) rewrites as

(8)

E+\sum_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}\sim E_{1}+(2s)v+\sum_{j=s+1}^{r}p_{j}+R\sim(r+s)v+R^{\prime}+R.

Its restriction on
\overline{\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{1}}
is
(r+s)v+R
, which has rank (at least)
r
by Riemann-Roch.

If
\Gamma_{1}
is hyperelliptic or Martens-special of rank
r
we just consider
\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}=\Gamma_{1}
without adding the points
q_{i}
and the claim follows more easily.
Repeating the argument for any
E
concludes the proof.
∎

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\Gamma
be a tree of cycles of genus

Then
w_{g-2+r}^{r}(\Gamma)=g-2-r
for some integer

if and only if
\Gamma
is hyperelliptic or it is a Martens-special tree of cycles of rank at most

Proof.

From Propositions
1.3

3.4

\Gamma
is non-hyperelliptic such that
w_{g-2+r}^{r}(\Gamma)=g-2-r
, then it is Martens-special of rank at most
r
.
As in the previous proof, we assume that
\Gamma
has a block-tree

with precisely a vertex

of valence

Let
\Gamma_{1}
be one of the chains corresponding to a maximal path in

and
consider
E_{1}
an effective divisor on
\Gamma_{1}
of degree
g_{1}-2

g_{1}=g(\Gamma_{1})

over
\Gamma.
We choose the points
\{q_{j}\}_{j=1,\dots,g-g_{1}}

\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{1},

Then, from the equality
w_{g-2+r}^{r}(\Gamma)=g-2-r

p_{1},\dots,p_{r}
such that

(9)

\operatorname{r}(E_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{g-g_{1}}q_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}p_{i})\geq r.

Notice that among the points
p_{1},\dots,p_{r}
, some might be contained in
\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{1}.
For instance, if
p_{1}\in\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{1}
, there exists

such that
E_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{g-g_{1}}q_{j}+p_{1}\sim E_{1}+\sum_{j\neq\hat{j}}q_{j}+2v
(otherwise we would have

\Gamma_{1}

Therefore on
