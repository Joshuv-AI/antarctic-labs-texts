Prognostic comparison of ablation and laser excision in a population-based cohort of stage III–IV pulmonary malignant tumors
Authors: Guo-Sheng Li, Xiu-Shun Xiang, Zhan-Yu Xu, Yue Li, Jun Liu, Hua-Jian Peng, Xiang Gao, Chang-Qian Li, Dong-Sheng Lu, Ya-Nan Zhang, Hua-Fu Zhou, Jian-Ji Guo, Nuo Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAblation (AL) and laser excision (LE) have shown broad application prospects in treating pulmonary malignant tumor (PMT). This study is the first to directly explore the differences in outcomes between AL and LE in treating patients with PMT at III–IV stages.MethodsThe study included 157 PMT patients from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to minimize the impact of clinical feature imbalances on confounding factors between the AL and LE groups. Kaplan-Meier curves, cumulative incidence function, univariable Cox regression analysis, and multivariable Cox regression analysis were used to explore prognostic factors for overall survival (OS)/cancer-specific survival (CSS).ResultsBefore PSM, a trend was observed indicating poorer OS, crude CSS, and net CSS in the AL group compared to the LE group (p = 0.019–0.071). After PSM, the LE group demonstrated significantly longer OS (median: 18.00 months) than the AL group (median: 13.00 months; p = 0.048). Similarly, crude CSS (p = 0.017) and net CSS (p = 0.028) were superior in the LE group. In the post-PSM multivariable analysis, LE remained associated with improved OS (adjusted HR = 0.52 [95% CI 0.32–0.86]; p = 0.011). The findings were robust across multiple sensitivity analyses: the change-in-estimate analysis showed ≤ 6.3% variation in the treatment HR upon omission of any single covariate; a 60-day landmark analysis yielded a consistent HR (0.56 [95% CI 0.34–0.93]; p = 0.025); and, in the full cohort, IPTW and propensity-score stratification produced comparable HRs (0.63 [95% CI 0.39–1.02], p = 0.060; and 0.53 [95% CI 0.32–0.89], p = 0.016). Stage IV remained an independent predictor of worse OS compared to stage III (HR = 3.35 [95% CI 1.99–5.65]; p < 0.001).ConclusionLE may offer a better prognosis than AL in treating patients with advanced-stage PMTs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-026-04008-w.Keywords: Ablation, Laser excision, Lung cancer, Propensity score matching, Cox regression analysis

Introduction

Pulmonary malignant tumor (PMT) is one of the most significant threats to human health globally, with lung cancer (LC) being the most common form of this disease. For example, there are over 2.4 million new cases and 1.8 million deaths attributed to LC annually [1, 2]. According to the latest data released by the World Health Organization in 2024, LC currently has the highest incidence and mortality rates among all types of cancer [2]. Pathologically, LC is categorized into small cell lung cancer and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), with the latter comprising over 80% of cases and consisting mainly of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) [3–6]. Studying the pathological subtypes is beneficial for enhancing the clinical management of PMT.

Although significant advances have been made in targeted therapies, immunotherapies, and radiotherapy for lung cancer [7, 8], video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) remains the primary treatment modality for early-stage PMT [9], which typically includes lobectomy and segmentectomy [10–12]. However, for patients who have contraindications to standard surgical resection (such as distant metastasis) or reject standard therapy, alternative approaches, such as ablation (AL) and laser excision (LE), may be considered [13–15]. In recent years, both AL and LE have garnered growing interest among clinicians and researchers in the practical application of surgical approaches for PMT patients [16–18].

While traditional thoracoscopic surgeries (e.g., lobectomy and segmentectomy) are currently commonly employed in PMT treatment [19, 20], there has been relatively less exploration of the use of AL and LE within the PMT domain. Several reports have suggested important prognostic value for specific PMT patients undergoing AL and LE [21, 22]. Nevertheless, no studies have directly explored the prognostic differences between PMT patients undergoing AL and LE. Therefore, conducting research in this area could contribute to a better understanding of the application value of these therapies in PMT and potentially provide clinical guidance. This study aims to explore the prognostic differences between PMT patients undergoing AL and LE using data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database [23]. Potential baseline characteristic imbalances between the groups may lead to substantial confounding effects on the analysis results. Hence, this study utilizes propensity score matching (PSM) to rigorously match the AL group and the LE group, aiming to minimize the impact of clinical feature imbalances on confounding factors and obtain a more objective comparison of treatment efficacy between AL and LE for PMT patients.

Materials and methods

Case selection and data collection

This study adheres to the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected Data (RECORD) guidelines [24] (Supplementary Material 1). The data used in this study were extracted from the SEER database, which encompasses approximately 28% of the United States population. The specific dataset employed for this research is “Incidence - SEER Research Data, 17 Registries, Nov 2024 Sub (2000–2022)” [25], with data collection taking place in October 2025. The data used in this study have been anonymized in compliance with SEER data usage requirements [23]; thus, ethical committee approval is not required. The AL group underwent laser AL or cryoablation (treatment code 12 in SEER), while the LE group underwent laser excision (treatment code 24 in SEER). Notably, the SEER database does not specify the imaging modality used for AL. However, previous studies indicate that AL is typically performed under CT guidance, although a small percentage is conducted using fluoroscopy, MRI, or ultrasound [26]. By contrast, LE is generally performed in conjunction with either VATS or open thoracotomy to remove lesions using lasers but not other tools such as staplers [27, 28].

The inclusion criteria for cases were as follows: (1) Patients diagnosed with PMT between 2018 and 2022, including LUAD, LUSC, and other subtypes of PMTs (ICD-O-3 codes: 8012, 8013, 8033, 8041, 8070, 8071, 8072, 8083, 8140, 8200, 8240, 8246, 8249, 8250, 8253, 8255, 8430, 8480, 8551, 8560, 8800, and 9041); (2) Patients aged 18 years or older and younger than 90 years; (3) Patients with clearly documented information on gender, race, and marital status; (4) Patients with pathologically confirmed PMT as the first malignant tumor; (5) Patients with clearly documented information on PMT subtypes, lesion details (laterality and site), and SEER’s eighth version of the Extent of Disease (EOD) staging; (6) Patients with advanced PMT stages (EOD III–IV stages); and (7) Patients with recorded information on the type of surgery and chemotherapy. The exclusion criteria were: (1) Unclear EOD staging; (2) missing or indeterminate data for overall survival (OS) or lung cancer–specific survival (CSS); and (3) OS recorded as 0 months (indicating immediate loss to follow-up or death after diagnosis). The collected data included pathological type, gender, race, marital status, tumor lesion information, EOD stages, treatment, and time to treat (days from diagnosis to AL or LE). All independent variables were recorded as categorical variables, except for age and time to treat, which were treated as continuous variables. The primary endpoint was OS, defined as the interval from diagnosis to death from any cause; patients alive at last contact were censored. CSS was assessed in two complementary ways: (1) crude PMT-specific mortality, estimated using the cumulative incidence function (CIF) with non-PMT death as a competing event (between-group comparisons by Gray’s test); and (2) net PMT-specific survival, estimated by Kaplan–Meier with non-PMT deaths censored [29]. All patients were retained regardless of cause of death; no cases were excluded due to competing events.

PSM parameter settings

PSM was employed to generate well-matched groups, thereby reducing the impact of confounding factors [30–33]. Variables were selected for PSM if they were deemed clinically relevant based on prior evidence (e.g., stage and primary lesion site) [34, 35] or if they exhibited significant baseline imbalance between the AL and LE groups (statistical differences or absolute standardized mean difference [SMD] ≥ 0.10). Matching was performed at a 2:1 ratio using the “optimal” method [31–33].

Statistical analysis

The SEER*Stat software (Version 9.0.42.0) [36] was utilized for data extraction in this study, while the Tableone package [37] was used for feature organization. Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests were conducted to compare the baseline characteristics between the AL and LE groups.

OS and net CSS were compared between the AL and LE groups using Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank tests. Crude PMT–specific mortality was estimated with CIFs, treating non–PMT deaths as competing events; between-group differences were assessed with Gray’s test. Univariable Cox regression analysis was employed to explore prognostic factors for OS before and after PSM. Subsequently, multivariable Cox regression analysis was also performed to identify the independent prognostic factors for PMT. Proportional hazards assumption was evaluated prior to Cox analyses using the Grambsch–Therneau χ2 test based on scaled Schoenfeld residuals, confirming feasibility of Cox analyses in both the pre- and post-PSM datasets.

Three sensitivity analyses were undertaken to complement the primary Cox model for OS. First, a change-in-estimate procedure was applied: the multivariable model was repeatedly refit after omitting one baseline covariate at a time; the adjustment set comprised age, gender, race, marital status, histologic subtype, laterality, tumor site, and time from diagnosis to treatment. For each refit, the adjusted hazard ratio (HR) for LE versus AL with its 95% confidence interval (CI) was recorded to assess the stability of the treatment effect. Second, to mitigate immortal time bias arising from delays between diagnosis and procedure, a 60-day landmark analysis was performed. Only patients alive at 60 days were retained, time zero was reset to that landmark, and follow-up accrued thereafter; post-landmark OS was compared using Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank tests, and the treatment effect was re-estimated with a covariate-adjusted Cox model using the same adjustment set. Third, robustness to alternative confounding control in the full pre-matched cohort was evaluated by estimating propensity scores via logistic regression with the same covariates, followed by inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) using average-treatment-effect weights with robust variance, and propensity-score stratification by quintiles entered as strata in a Cox model.

All computational analyses in this study were performed using R (Version 4.5.1) and several packages [32, 37–40], with a p < 0.050 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The study comprised a total of 157 patients, 113 patients who underwent AL and 44 patients who underwent LE (Table 1). Of these patients, 93 were male and 64 were female, with 37 diagnosed with LUAD, 96 with LUSC, and 24 with other pathological subtypes. Baseline information prior to PSM indicated no statistical differences between the AL and LE groups regarding age, gender, race, marital status, pathological subtypes, tumor lesion laterality, lesion sites, EOD stages, and time to treat (all p > 0.050; Table 1). However, the absolute SMD values for race, marital status, subtype, and tumor lesion laterality were all greater than 0.1 (Supplementary Material 2), indicating an imbalance in these variables between the AL and LE groups.

Based on the baseline characteristics exhibiting absolute SMD values of ≥ 0.10 (namely, race, marital status, subtype, and tumor lesion laterality), as well as clinically relevant factors identified from prior evidence and practice (stage and primary lesion site) [34, 35], PSM was conducted. Comparison of prognosis between the AL group and the LE group before PSM

Before PSM, survival patterns differed between groups. The LE group showed longer OS than the ablation group (median survival 18.00 months vs. 12.00 months), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.071; Supplementary Material 3A). The cumulative incidence of crude PMT-specific mortality was higher in the AL group than in the LE group, and this difference was statistically significant (p = 0.019; Supplementary Material 3B). Consistently, net CSS was better in the LE group than in the AL group (median survival 23.00 months vs. 13.00 months), with a significant between-group difference (p = 0.034; Supplementary Material 3C).

The proportional hazards assumption was assessed in both the pre- and post-PSM datasets; no significant violations were detected (all p > 0.050, Supplementary Material 4). Therefore, a Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was considered appropriate. Univariable Cox regression analysis suggested that EOD stage before PSM could act as a prognostic risk factor for patients with PMT (HR = 2.47 [95% CI: 1.64–3.73]; p < 0.001; Supplementary Material 5A). In the multivariable Cox regression analysis, stage IV disease was identified as a prognostic risk factor compared with stage III disease (HR = 3.24 [95% CI: 2.00–5.25], p < 0.001, Supplementary Material 5B). After adjustment for covariates, the LE group showed a more favorable prognosis than the AL group (HR = 0.57 [95% CI: 0.35–0.94], p = 0.026, Supplementary Material 5B).

Comparison of prognosis between the AL group and the LE group After PSM

The analysis results prior to PSM may be biased due to imbalances in covariates between groups. Therefore, further analyses were conducted on the post-PSM data to obtain more reliable conclusions. After PSM, the data indicated that the OS was longer in the LE group (median OS of 18.00 months) compared to the AL group (median OS of 13.00 months) (p = 0.048, Fig. 2A). The LE group consistently indicated higher survival probabilities over 12 (56.8% versus 51.0%), 24 (42.6% versus 35.3%), and 36 months (39.3% versus 28.2%) compared to the AL group (Fig. 2A). Similarly, the cumulative incidence of crude PMT–specific mortality was higher in the AL group than in the LE group, and this difference was statistically significant (p = 0.017; Fig. 2B). Consistent with this finding, net PMT–specific survival was better in the LE group than in the AL group, with a significant between-group difference (p = 0.028; Fig. 2C).

Univariable Cox regression analysis suggested that the OS of PMT patients might be influenced by marital status (HR = 0.64 [95% CI: 0.41–0.98]; p = 0.041) and EOD stage (HR = 2.28 [95% CI: 1.46–3.54]; p < 0.001) (Fig. 3A). In this univariable Cox model, the association between treatment (AL/LE) group and OS was directionally consistent with the Kaplan–Meier analysis (Fig. 2A) but did not reach nominal statistical significance (HR = 0.62 [95% CI: 0.39–1.01]; p = 0.053; Fig. 3A), likely reflecting residual confounding. After adjustment for all factors (age, gender, race, marital status, histology subtype, laterality, tumor site, stage, and time to treatment), LE was independently associated with improved prognosis compared with AL (multivariable Cox regression, HR = 0.52 [95% CI: 0.32–0.86]; p = 0.011; Fig. 3B). Additionally, stage IV disease remained independently associated with worse OS (HR = 3.35 [95% CI: 1.99–5.65]; p < 0.001; Fig. 3B).

Sensitivity analysis

To assess whether the observed survival benefit of LE depended on any single baseline covariate, we conducted a change-in-estimate analysis on the post-match cohort by re-fitting the prespecified full Cox model while dropping one covariate at a time. Notably, EOD stage was consistently retained in the model because of its strong prognostic relevance and its potential influence on the results. Treatment HRs for LE versus AL remained consistently < 1.0 with limited variation (maximum relative change 6.3% when omitting Subtype; all others < 3.6%) and remained statistically significant (range of p = 0.009–0.019) (Supplementary Material 6), supporting that the LE advantage does not hinge on any single adjustment variable.

A 60-day landmark analysis was also performed to mitigate immortal time bias. This analysis was restricted to patients alive at 60 days (representing > 94% of the cohort), at which point follow-up time was reset. Post-landmark Kaplan–Meier curves suggested a trend toward improved OS with LE versus AL (p = 0.087; Supplementary Material 7A). In the corresponding multivariable Cox analysis, LE remained associated with improved OS (HR = 0.56 [95% CI: 0.34–0.93]; p = 0.025), whereas stage IV disease was associated with worse prognosis than stage III (HR = 3.20 [95% CI: 1.86–5.49]; p < 0.001) (Supplementary Material 7B).

In the full cohort without PSM, IPTW and propensity score stratification were used. Using IPTW, the direction of effect matched the primary analysis: LE was associated with a lower risk of death than AL in both the univariable analysis (HR = 0.66 [95% CI: 0.41–1.04]; p = 0.075) and the multivariable analysis (HR = 0.63 [95% CI: 0.39–1.02]; p = 0.060). With propensity score stratification (quintiles), LE remained protective (univariable HR = 0.66 [95% CI: 0.41–1.04]; p = 0.075; multivariable HR = 0.53 [95% CI: 0.32–0.89]; p = 0.016) (Supplementary Material 8).

Discussion

In recent years, thanks to the widespread use of high-resolution computed tomography, PMTs are more likely to be detected at an early stage [41]. Standard surgical treatment remains the primary therapeutic strategy for early-stage PMT patients, including lobectomy, segmentectomy, wedge resection, and sleeve resection [13]. However, for certain PMT patients (e.g., those with pulmonary metastases or severe cardiopulmonary comorbidities), alternative treatment modalities, such as AL and LE, have demonstrated promising efficacy and safety profiles [21, 22, 27, 42]. To date, there has been no direct comparative investigation between AL and LE. To our knowledge, this study is the first to directly explore the differences in outcomes between AL and LE in treating PMT patients at III–IV stages. By employing PSM to reduce confounding factors and utilizing various statistical methods (Kaplan–Meier curve, univariable Cox regression analysis, and multivariable Cox regression analysis), our study achieved reliable conclusions. The results indicate that patients receiving AL have a poorer prognosis compared to those undergoing LE, providing some reference value for the clinical adoption of these two methods in treating PMT patients at advanced stages.

Ablation and LE have shown broad application prospects in treating PMT. The clinical use of AL includes various techniques, such as laser AL, cryoablation, radiofrequency AL, and microwave AL [16]. Laser AL, a less frequently reported AL technique [16], has been demonstrated in an earlier study by Rosenberg et al. [17] to achieve a five-year survival rate of 27% for PMT patients. Moore et al. [18] conducted cryoablation on inoperable stage I NSCLC patients, achieving a five-year OS rate, CSS rate, and progression-free survival rate of 67.8%, 56.6%, and 87.9%, respectively. Furthermore, a study by Wang et al. [21] found no significant difference in OS and disease-free survival between stage I NSCLC patients treated with microwave AL and those undergoing VATS lobectomy. Zeng et al. [22] compared thermal AL and wedge resection for treating stage I NSCLC patients and found no significant difference in OS. AL also has some advantages. On the one hand, it is suitable for patients who refuse surgery or have significant comorbidities affecting cardiopulmonary function. On the other hand, for patients with pulmonary metastases, AL can be a viable alternative, though this requires evaluation based on the size, location, and number of metastatic lesions [14]. Similarly, LE has been used for treating PMT patients. Baier et al. [43] performed pulmonary LE on patients with multiple lung metastases from advanced renal cell carcinoma, finding that patients who achieved R0 resection had a five-year OS rate of 54% and a median survival time of 69 months. Therefore, both AL and LE show promising efficacy profiles for treating PMT. However, there has been no direct comparison of outcomes between AL and LE in treating PMT patients to date; this study aims to fill that gap.

In our study, the LE group had better OS and CSS than the AL group. Previous studies have identified advanced stages as risk factors for prognosis in PMT patients [44, 45], a phenomenon also observed in our analysis among patients undergoing AL or LE. The Kaplan–Meier curve for the LE group indicated longer OS/CSS than for the AL group, a conclusion supported by the multivariable Cox regression analysis. Moreover, this direction of effect persisted in prespecified robustness checks, including a change-in-estimate procedure, a 60-day landmark analysis to mitigate immortal time, and two alternative confounding controls in the full cohort (IPTW and propensity-score stratification). Compared with AL, LE may achieve more complete endobronchial tumor clearance, better restore airway patency, and permit immediate visual assessment of margins [46], which could translate into improved disease control. The association favoring LE over AL may reflect differences in debulking efficacy and airway restoration [46, 47]; however, the present dataset lacks procedural details (e.g., completeness of resection, margin status, operator experience, device settings), and this interpretation requires validation in future studies. In stage III–IV cases treated with AL or LE, PMT stage was independently associated with prognosis, underscoring that tumor burden and dissemination remain the dominant determinants of long-term outcomes irrespective of local technique. Taken together, when both approaches are technically feasible, LE may be the preferential option for achieving superior OS/CSS, while treatment selection should remain individualized to stage, lesion location, comorbidity profile, and procedural risk.

Our study has certain limitations. First, the data source is the SEER database, which does not record patients’ comorbidities and cardiopulmonary function information, potentially leading to selection bias. Second, systemic therapy was not included because SEER typically records only ever/never receipt, without modality, initiation date, sequencing, or time-varying exposure. Third, although immortal-time bias was mitigated with a prespecified 60-day landmark, a full target-trial emulation was not feasible using SEER data, which lack the longitudinal detail required to repeatedly redefine eligibility and to model dynamic treatment assignment (e.g., evolving operability or performance status). Fourth, the AL group only included patients undergoing laser AL and cryoablation; future research is needed to explore outcome differences between other AL techniques, such as radiofrequency AL and microwave AL. Fifth, the sample size post PSM is relatively small. Sixth, this study is retrospective, necessitating further prospective studies to validate our findings.

Conclusions

Our study suggests that LE may offer a better prognosis than AL in treating patients with advanced-stage PMTs. The current findings may provide insights for the clinical management of PMT patients and future research design.
