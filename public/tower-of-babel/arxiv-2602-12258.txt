Post-measurement states are (very) useful for measurement discrimination
Charbel Eid, Marco Túlio Quintino

Abstract
The standard approach to quantum measurement discrimination is to perform the given unknown measurement on a probe state, possibly entangled with an auxiliary system, and make a decision based on the measurement outcome obtained. In this work, we go beyond the standard aforementioned scenarios by considering not only the classical outcome of a measurement, but also its post-measurement quantum state. More specifically, instead of considering only the positive-operator valued measure (POVM) operators, we consider their associated Lüders instrument. We prove that, when the post-measurement quantum states are available, the task of discriminating two qubit projective measurements is equivalent to discriminating two copies of quantum states associated to each projector pair, extending previous results known for the case where probe states are separable. Then, we proceed by showing that the advantage of considering post-measurement states in measurement discrimination can be large. We formalize this claim by presenting a family of pairs of measurements where the ratio between the discrimination bias of the measurement discrimination task with and without post-measurement states can be arbitrarily large. This shows that, while the post-measurement state was neglected in most of the previous literature, its use can significantly improve the performance of quantum measurement discrimination.



I
Introduction

In classical physics and information theory, erroneous identification of a system or object is necessarily the consequence of either incomplete access to the system we are observing or an incomplete knowledge of what we are identifying it as. This is not the case in quantum theory, where one may be provided with complete access to a system along with a guarantee that it is one of two possible systems and complete descriptions of both, yet still be unable to correctly identify which of the two they are observing. The tasks of discriminating quantum objects thus form a fundamental problem, with impact and applications in various branches of quantum information and computation
Bae and Kwek 2015
;
Barnett and Croke 2009
;
Chiribella et al. 2008
;
Pereira et al. 2020
;
Blahut 1974

Chiribella 2012
;
Ohst et al. 2026
;
Lewandowska et al. 2023
;
Skotiniotis et al. 2024
;
Sentís et al. 2016
.

The discrimination of quantum states has been the first and most widely studied case, and seminal results such as the Helstrom bound were developed before the 70s
Helstrom 1969
. State discrimination opened the door for widespread interest in other variations of the problem as well as providing a large amount of tools and methods for approaching them. A related fundamental task we consider in this work is the discrimination of quantum measurements. While measurements are at the core of quantum theory, and significant progress has been made
Sedlák and Ziman 2014
;
Puchała et al. 2018
;
Ziman et al. 2009
;
Miková et al. 2014
;
Ji et al. 2006
;
Puchała et al. 2021
;
Manna et al. 2025
;
Krawiec et al. 2020
;
Sudarsanan Ragini and Ziman 2024
;
Krawiec et al. 2024
;
Ziman and Heinosaari 2008
, our understanding of measurement discrimination remains limited when compared to state discrimination.
In particular, apart from the recent work done in
Manna et al. 2025
, previous works on measurement discrimination typically focus on POVMs and classical outcomes, without analyzing the role of the associated post-measurement states.

In this work, we investigate the role of post-measurement quantum states in the task of minimum-error measurement discrimination. We introduce the problem of Lüders instrument discrimination, in which the post-measurement state associated with each POVM element is accessible, and formulate it within the framework of quantum channel discrimination. We first prove that in the dichotomic case, for qubit projective measurements, Lüders instrument discrimination is equivalent to the discrimination of two-copy pure states, thereby extending previous results that considered strategies without entanglement
Manna et al. 2025
and showing that access to post-measurement states strictly enhances discrimination performance. We further demonstrate that this enhancement can be arbitrarily large: even for qubit measurements, we construct explicit families of POVMs for which the ratio between the optimal discrimination biases with and without post-measurement states diverges. Finally, we complement our analytical results with a semidefinite-programming based numerical investigation of more general scenarios, illustrating the operational relevance of post-measurement states beyond analytically tractable cases.

III
Quantum Instruments: measurements with post-measurement states

In quantum theory, it is not necessarily the case that quantum measurements destroy the quantum system. There are cases where, in addition to a classical outcome, one has access to a post-measurement quantum state. For instance, after performing a projective measurement on some orthonormal basis
\{\phi_{a}\}_{a}
, when the outcome
a
is obtained, one may have access to a post-measurement state given by
\ket{\phi_a}
. More generally, after performing a quantum measurement with the POVM
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{a}\}_{a=1}^{n}
on the state
\rho
, one may have access to classical outcomes
a
and post-measurement states

\displaystyle\rho_{a}:=\frac{\sqrt{M_{a}}\rho\sqrt{M_{a}}^{\dagger}}{\text{Tr}\left(M_{a}\rho\right)},

(3)

where
\sqrt{M_{a}}
are the unique positive semidefinite square roots of
M_{a}
, often called the measurement operators, or the Kraus operators of the quantum measurement
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
.

This more general way to define quantum measurements can be formalized by quantum instruments. A quantum instrument
Heinosaari and Ziman 2011
;
Davies and Lewis 1970
;
Watrous 2018
is a set of trace non-increasing, completely positive (CP) linear maps
\mathcal{I}:=\{\widetilde{C}_{a}\}_{a=1}^{n}
, such that their sum
\sum_{a=1}^{n}\widetilde{C}_{a}
is a quantum channel, that is, a completely positive trace preserving map (CPTP). Quantum instruments represent probabilistic quantum transformations and, thus, are the right object to model the transformation a measurement applies to a state. Given an instrument
\mathcal{I}
and an input state
\rho
, the probability of map
\widetilde{C}_{a}
being applied is
p(a|\rho,\mathcal{I})=\text{Tr}\left(\widetilde{C}_{a}(\rho)\right)
, with
\sum_{a}^{n}\text{Tr}\left(\widetilde{C}_{a}(\rho)\right)=1
by construction. Each instrument corresponds to a unique POVM, which can be proven to have its elements given by
M_{a}=\widetilde{C_{a}}^{\dagger}(\mathbb{1})
, where
\widetilde{C_{a}}^{\dagger}
is the adjoint map of
\widetilde{C_{a}}
. On the other hand, for each measurement
\mathcal{M}
, there are various sets of instruments
\widetilde{C_{a}}
such that
\text{Tr}\left(\widetilde{C}_{a}(\rho)\right)=\text{Tr}\left(M_{a}\rho\right)\,\,\forall\rho,a
.
Heinosaari and Ziman 2011

The instrument corresponding to
Eq.

3
is known as Lüders’ instrument, and it has various physical and mathematical properties
Heinosaari and Ziman 2011
. The Lüders instrument of a POVM
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{a}\}_{a=1}^{n}
is
\mathcal{L}:=\{\widetilde{L}_{a}\}_{a=1}^{n}
,
\widetilde{L}_{a}(\rho)=\sqrt{M_{a}}\rho\sqrt{M_{a}}
. The probability of a map
\widetilde{L}_{a}
being applied here is simply
p(a|\rho,\mathcal{L})=\text{Tr}\left(\widetilde{L}_{a}(\rho)\right)=\text{Tr}\left(M_{a}\rho\right)
, correctly reproducing the probability distribution given by the measurement. The quantum channel associated with each of the linear maps
\widetilde{C}_{a}
of a quantum instrument
\mathcal{I}
is
\rho_{a}=\widetilde{C}_{a}(\rho)/p(a|\rho,\mathcal{I})
or for Lüders’ instruments
\widetilde{L}_{a}(\rho)/p(a|\rho,\mathcal{L})
.

Due to their practical and mathematical relevance
Heinosaari and Ziman 2011
, in this work, we will restrict our analysis to Lüders’ instruments. We notice that, if we consider the task of measurement discrimination with arbitrary instruments, one can artificially make this task trivial by choosing some specific instruments. For instance, for any set of POVMs
\{M_{a|x}\}_{a,x}
, we may define the instruments
\widetilde{C}_{a|x}(\rho)=\text{Tr}\left(M_{a|x}\rho\right)\outerproduct{x}{x}
, which performs the measurement
x
and output “orthogonal flag-states”
\outerproduct{x}{x}
, which allow us to trivially identify which measurement was performed.

V
Methods: quantum testers and channel discrimination

In order to simplify our analysis, it is convenient to represent quantum measurements and quantum instruments as quantum channels, as done in
Sedlák and Ziman 2014
. In this way, the problem of quantum instrument discrimination is reduced to a quantum channel discrimination task. The problem of minimum-error one shot channel discrimination is the following
Watrous 2018
: Alice is given an unknown quantum channel (CPTP map)
\widetilde{C}_{x}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{in}})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{out}})
, drawn from an ensemble
\mathcal{E}=\{p_{x},\widetilde{C}_{x}\}_{x=1}^{N}
that is known to her. After being allowed to use the channel
\widetilde{C}_{x}
once, her task is to determine which channel she received, by performing operations on it and guessing the value of
x\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.

In order to analyze discrimination between quantum channels, it is convenient to represent channels via the Choi-Jamiołkowski isomorphism
Choi 1975
;
Jamiołkowski 1972
. The Choi operator of a linear map
\widetilde{C}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{in}})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{out}})
is defined as
C:=(\mathbb{1}\otimes\widetilde{C})(|\Phi^{+}\rangle\langle\Phi^{+}|)\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{in}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\text{out}})
, where
\ket{\Phi^+}=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\ket{j}\otimes\ket{j}
is a unnormalized maximally entangled state. A linear map
\widetilde{C}
is completely positive (CP) if and only if
C\geq 0
, and trace preserving (TP) if and only if
\Tr_{\text{out}}(C)=\mathbb{1}_{\text{in}}
. We now thus have a one-to-one mapping between linear maps and Choi operators as well as strict conditions for identifying Choi operators that correspond to quantum channels.

Being able to now treat quantum channels as matrices, we introduce process POVMs (PPOVMs)
Ziman 2008
, also known as quantum testers
Chiribella et al. 2009
. Testers are the analogous of POVMs to quantum channels, and may be viewed as a higher-order version of POVMs
Taranto et al. 2025
, since they act as measurement devices for quantum channels. A tester is a set of linear operators
\mathcal{T}:=\{T_{b}\}_{b=1}^{N}
,
T_{b}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{in}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\text{out}})
, satisfying,
T_{b}\geq 0\,\forall b
and
\sum_{b}^{N}T_{b}=\sigma\otimes\mathbb{1}_{{out}}
, where
\sigma\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{in}})
is a quantum state. It holds that
p(b|\widetilde{C},\mathcal{T})=\Tr(T_b C)
is the probability of obtaining outcome
b
for a channel discrimination task where we use a strategy with corresponding tester
\mathcal{T}
, knowing the correct channel is
\widetilde{C}
. Moreover, one can prove that every tester
\mathcal{T}
admits a physical realization as an admissible channel discrimination strategy, as well as that every possible strategy admits a valid tester, making the study of channel discrimination extensively analyzable through the lens of testers without loss of either realizability or attainability
Ziman 2008
.

Main result 1: analytic solution for projective qubit instruments and optimal strategies

VI.1
Success probability

We now present our first main result which considers the simple instance of a pair of projective qubit Lüders instruments. We give a closed formula which takes the from of a Helstrom bound for the discrimination of two-copy pure states.

Theorem 1
.

In the context of one-shot minimum-error discrimination, the task of discriminating two projective qubit Lüders instruments characterized by POVMs
\{|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|,|\psi^{\perp}\rangle\langle\psi^{\perp}|\}

1

1

.

\mathcal{Z}=\{|0\rangle\langle 0|,|1\rangle\langle 1|\}
and
\mathcal{X}=\{|+\rangle\langle+|,|-\rangle\langle-|\}
, occurring with equal probabilities, and where
\ket{+}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0}+\ket{1}\right)
. With no access to the post-measurement state, this is equivalent to the discrimination of
\ket{0}
and
\ket{+}
and has maximum success probability
p=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\sqrt{1-|\bra{0}+\rangle|^{2}}\right)=\cos^{2}(\pi/8)\approx 0.853
, while with access to the post-measurement state, the success probability becomes
p^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\sqrt{1-|\bra{00}++\rangle|^{2}}\right)\approx 0.933
, which constitutes a significant increase.

Below, we present the core of the proof of
Theorem

1
. The proof that optimal performance is attainable without entanglement is presented in
Section

VI.3
.

Proof.

Consider two projective qubit measurements
\mathcal{M}_{1}=\{M_{1|1},M_{2|1}\}=\{|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|,|\psi^{\perp}\rangle\langle\psi^{\perp}|\}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}=\{M_{1|2},M_{2|2}\}=\{|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|,|\phi^{\perp}\rangle\langle\phi^{\perp}|\}
, and the task of optimally discriminating their respective Lüders’ Instruments
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
, occurring with probabilities
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
. Using a tester
\mathcal{T}
to accomplish this task, we can write the probability of successful discrimination:

\displaystyle p_{\text{succ}}

\displaystyle=p_{1}p(1|\widetilde{C_{L}}_{1},\mathcal{T})+p_{2}p(2|\widetilde{C_{L}}_{2},\mathcal{T})

(13)

\displaystyle=p_{1}\text{Tr}\left({C_{L}}_{1}T_{1}\right)+p_{2}\text{Tr}\left({C_{L}}_{2}T_{2}\right),

(14)

where
\widetilde{C_{L}}_{x}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{in}})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{out1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\text{out2}})
,
{C_{L}}_{x}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{in}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\text{out1}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\text{out2}})
are the corresponding channels and their Choi operators:

\displaystyle{C_{L}}_{x}=\sum_{a=1}^{2}M_{a|x}^{T}\otimes M_{a|x}\otimes|a\rangle\langle a|

(15)

where
.^{T}
stands for transposition in the computational basis.

Let us introduce the projectors
\pi_{a}=\mathbb{1}\otimes\mathbb{1}\otimes|a\rangle\langle a|
, for which we have the identity
\sum_{a}\pi_{a}{C_{L}}_{x}\pi_{a}={C_{L}}_{x}
. From this follows that:

\displaystyle p(b,\widetilde{C_{L}}_{x}|\mathcal{T})

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}\left({C_{L}}_{x}T_{b}\right)=\sum_{a}\text{Tr}\left(\pi_{a}{C_{L}}_{x}\pi_{a}T_{b}\right)

(16)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\text{Tr}\left({C_{L}}_{x}\pi_{a}T_{b}\pi_{a}\right).

(17)

In this way, for all
\widetilde{C_{L}}_{x}
, given a tester
\mathcal{T}\neq\pi(\mathcal{T})
, we can always use
\pi(\mathcal{T})
instead, while preserving all conditional probabilities, and since
\pi(\pi(\mathcal{T}))=\pi(\mathcal{T})
, all testers we now consider satisfy the relation
\mathcal{T}=\pi(\mathcal{T})
. The most general form for
\mathcal{T}
we will be considering is thus:

\displaystyle T_{b}=\sum_{a}\sigma_{a|b}\otimes|a\rangle\langle a|,

(18)

with operators
\sigma_{a|b}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{in}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\text{out1}})
,
\sigma_{a|b}\geq 0
and, following from the normalization condition on
\{T_{b}\}_{b}
,
\forall a
, we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{b}\sigma_{a|b}=\rho\otimes\mathbb{1},

(19)

where
\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{in}})
is a quantum state.

Furthermore, we introduce the transformations
\Gamma\otimes\Gamma\otimes\hat{X}(.)
where
\hat{X}(.)
is conjugation by the Pauli X operator and
\Gamma(A)=\frac{\text{Tr}\left(A\right)\mathbb{1}-A}{\dim(A)-1}
is the universal NOT transformation, which is a positive and trace-preserving linear map
Bužek et al. 1999
;
Rungta et al. 2001
. For the dimension of
A
,
\dim(A)=2
, we have:

\displaystyle T^{\prime}_{b}=\Gamma\otimes\Gamma\otimes\hat{X}(T_{b})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\Gamma\otimes\Gamma(\sigma_{a|b})\otimes\hat{X}|a\rangle\langle a|\hat{X}

(20)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\Gamma\otimes\Gamma(\sigma_{a+1|b})\otimes|a\rangle\langle a|,

(21)

then:

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle(b,\widetilde{C_{L}}_{x}|\mathcal{T}^{\prime})=\text{Tr}({C_{L}}_{x}T^{\prime}_{b})

(22)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\text{Tr}(\Gamma\otimes\Gamma(\sigma_{a+1|b})(M_{a|x}^{T}\otimes M_{a|x}))

(23)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\text{Tr}((\sigma_{a+1|b})\Gamma\otimes\Gamma(M_{a|x}^{T}\otimes M_{a|x}))

(24)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\text{Tr}(\sigma_{a+1|b}(M_{a+1|x}^{T}\otimes M_{a+1|x}))

(25)

\displaystyle=p(b,\widetilde{C_{L}}_{x}|\mathcal{T}),

(26)

where we used the properties
\text{Tr}(\Gamma\otimes\Gamma(A)B)=\text{Tr}(\Gamma\otimes\Gamma(B)A)
and
\Gamma(M_{a|x})=M_{a+1|x}
. Using the same argument as earlier, for any tester
\mathcal{T}
that does not satisfy
\mathcal{T}=\Gamma\otimes\Gamma\otimes\hat{X}(\mathcal{T})
, we can use the tester
\Gamma\otimes\Gamma\otimes\hat{X}(\mathcal{T})
instead, which does while preserving all conditional probabilities. Thus we limit the set of testers we optimize over to ones that satisfy this property and therefore also meet:

\displaystyle\Gamma\otimes\Gamma(\sigma_{a+1|b})=\sigma_{a|b}

(27)

Using this last equation, we can further restrict the normalization of
\{\sigma_{a|b}\}_{b}
, since:

\displaystyle\sum_{b}\sigma_{a|b}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}\Gamma\otimes\Gamma(\sigma_{a+1|b})

(28)

\displaystyle=\rho\otimes\mathbb{1}=\Gamma\otimes\Gamma(\rho\otimes\mathbb{1})

(29)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{1}\otimes\mathbb{1},

(30)

given that
\rho=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{1}
is the only solution to
\rho=\Gamma(\rho)
. With the properties that
\Gamma\otimes\Gamma
in
\dim(A)=2
is self-adjoint and self-inverse, we can show that:

\displaystyle p(b,\widetilde{C_{L}}_{x}|\mathcal{T})

(31)

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}((M_{1|x}^{T}\otimes M_{1|x})\sigma_{1|b}+(M_{2|x}^{T}\otimes M_{2|x})\sigma_{2|b})

(32)

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}((M_{1|x}^{T}\otimes M_{1|x})\sigma_{1|b}+(M_{1|x}^{T}\otimes M_{1|x})\Gamma(\sigma_{2|b}))

(33)

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}(2\sigma_{1|b}(M_{1|x}^{T}\otimes M_{1|x}))

(34)

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}(N_{b}(M_{1|x}^{T}\otimes M_{1|x})),

(35)

where the operators
N_{b}=2\sigma_{1|b}
obey
N_{b}\geq 0
and
\sum_{b}N_{b}=\mathbb{1}
form by definition a POVM. And, for the discrimination of two projective qubit instruments, we now have:

\begin{split}p_{\text{succ}}&=p_{1}\text{Tr}\left(N_{1}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\otimes|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|^{T}\right)\\
&+p_{2}\text{Tr}\left(N_{2}|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|\otimes|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|^{T}\right)\end{split},

(36)

where
p_{\text{succ}}
is the success probability of discriminating states
|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\otimes|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|^{T}
and
|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|\otimes|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|^{T}
occurring with respective probabilities
p_{1}
,
p_{2}
, and using POVM
\{N_{b}\}_{b}
. For pure qubits, as is the case here, this is in turn equivalent to the discrimination of states
|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|^{\otimes 2}
and
|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|^{\otimes 2}
.
∎

VI.2
Attaining maximal performance with entanglement

In this subsection, we show how to attain the optimal performance presented in
Theorem

1
with entangled states, and in the following subsection, we show how entanglement is not required to attain the optimal performance.

An entanglement based strategy can be derived using elements from the proof of the theorem. This is done by first writing the normalization condition given in
Eq.

28
as a normalization of the tester elements:

\displaystyle\sum_{b}T_{b}=\sum_{a,b}\sigma_{a|b}\otimes|a\rangle\langle a|=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{1}\otimes\mathbb{1}\otimes\mathbb{1}.

(37)

We then use the equation
Ziman 2008
;
Chiribella et al. 2009
:

\displaystyle\sum_{b}T_{b}=\text{Tr}_{\text{aux}}\left(\rho\right)\otimes\mathbb{1}\otimes\mathbb{1},

(38)

where
\rho
is the bipartite input state and
\text{Tr}_{\text{aux}}
is the partial trace over the auxiliary space, to write:

\displaystyle\text{Tr}_{\text{aux}}\left(\rho\right)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{1}

(39)

To reiterate the argument (see
Theorem

1
): For any tester that does not satisfy the above conditions, there exists one that achieves the same success probability, yet does. We can thus use
Eq.

39
, to find the input state
\rho
of the optimal tester, which has to be a maximally entangled state as its partial trace is
\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{1}
. If one inputs the maximally entangled state
|\phi_{2}^{+}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{00}+\ket{11}\right)
, into a Lüders instrument (see
Fig.

2
), then for a measurement element
|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
, direct calculation shows that we get the unnormalized total output state:

\displaystyle\left(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\otimes\mathbb{1}\right)|\phi_{2}^{+}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{\psi}\otimes\overline{\ket{\psi}},

(40)

where
\overline{\ket{\psi}}
is the complex conjugate of
\ket{\psi}
in the computational basis. One way to prove the identity of
Eq.

40
is to use the fact that for any
A:\mathcal{H}_{\text{in}}\to\mathcal{H}_{\text{out}}
, we have that

\displaystyle A\otimes\mathbb{1}_{\text{in}}\ket{\phi^+_{d_\text{in}}}=\sqrt{\frac{d_{\text{in}}}{d_{\text{out}}}}\mathbb{1}_{\text{out}}\otimes A^{T}\ket{\phi^+_{d_\text{out}}}.

(41)

In this way, if we view
|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
as the composition of the operator
\ket{\psi}:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{1}
with
\bra{\psi}:\mathbb{C}^{1}\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
, and note that
\bra{\psi}^{T}=\overline{\ket{\psi}}
, we obtain
Eq.

40
.

At this point, we recognise that the problem of discriminating Lüders instruments characterized by POVMs
\{|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|,|\psi^{\perp}\rangle\langle\psi^{\perp}|\}
and
\{|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|,|\phi^{\perp}\rangle\langle\phi^{\perp}|\}
is equivalent to discriminating the states
\ket{\psi}\overline{\ket{\psi}}
from
\ket{\psi^\perp}\overline{\ket{\psi^\perp}}
. Now, we notice that the operator

U:=\overline{\ket{\psi}}\bra{\psi}+\overline{\ket{\psi^\perp}}\bra{\psi^\perp}
is unitary. Hence, the pair of states
\{\ket{\psi},\ket{\psi^\perp}\}
is related to the pair of states
\{\overline{\ket{\psi}},\overline{\ket{\psi^\perp}}\}
via the unitary operator
U
, hence both sets of states are unitarily equivalent, and discriminating
\ket{\psi}\overline{\ket{\psi}}
from
\ket{\psi^\perp}\overline{\ket{\psi^\perp}}
is equivalent to discriminating
\ket{\psi}{\ket{\psi}}
from
\ket{\psi^\perp}\ket{{\psi^\perp}}
.

VI.3
Attaining maximal performance without entanglement

We now show how to attain the maximum discrimination success probability without the use of entanglement. For that, we use a flipping symmetry argument similarly to
Theorem

1
to show that in the dichotomic case, ‘projective qubit instruments discrimination with no access to entanglement’ is equivalent to ‘two-copy pure state discrimination with no access to entangling measurements’. Then, use the fact that optimal two-copy pure state discrimination is attainable without access to entangling measurements
Acín et al. 2005
.

The most general strategy that uses no entanglement will simply be to input a state
\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{in}})
into the Lüders instrument
\mathcal{L}_{x}
, then observe the classical outcome
a
and apply a corresponding measurement
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{a}=\{M_{b|a}\}_{b=1}^{n_{a}^{\prime}}
to the post-measurement state. The maximum success probability for the discrimination of two Lüders instruments generated by two projective qubit measurements
\mathcal{M}_{x\in\{0,1\}}=\{|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|_{a|x}\}_{a,x\in\{0,1\}}
, with prior probabilities
p_{a\in\{0,1\}}
can be written as follows:

\begin{split}\max_{\rho,\{M_{b|a}\}}&p_{s}=\\
\max_{\rho,\{M_{b|a}\}}&p_{0}\text{Tr}\left(\rho|\psi_{0|0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0|0}|\right)\text{Tr}\left(|\psi_{0|0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0|0}|M^{\prime}_{0|0}\right)\\
+&p_{0}\text{Tr}\left(\rho|\psi_{1|0}\rangle\langle\psi_{1|0}|\right)\text{Tr}\left(|\psi_{1|0}\rangle\langle\psi_{1|0}|M^{\prime}_{0|1}\right)\\
+&p_{1}\text{Tr}\left(\rho|\psi_{0|1}\rangle\langle\psi_{0|1}|\right)\text{Tr}\left(|\psi_{0|1}\rangle\langle\psi_{0|1}|M^{\prime}_{1|0}\right)\\
+&p_{1}\text{Tr}\left(\rho|\psi_{1|1}\rangle\langle\psi_{1|1}|\right)\text{Tr}\left(|\psi_{1|1}\rangle\langle\psi_{1|1}|M^{\prime}_{1|1}\right)\\
\end{split}

(42)

Using the property
\text{Tr}\left(\Gamma(A)B\right)=\text{Tr}\left(A\,\Gamma(B)\right)
for
A,B
2 dimensional projectors, we can then rewrite the above expression:

\begin{split}\max_{\rho,\{M_{b|a}\}}&p_{s}=\\
\max_{\rho,\{M_{b|a}\}}&p_{0}\text{Tr}\left(\rho|\psi_{0|0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0|0}|\right)\text{Tr}\left(|\psi_{0|0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0|0}|M^{\prime}_{0|0}\right)\\
+&p_{0}\text{Tr}\left(\Gamma(\rho)|\psi_{0|0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0|0}|\right)\text{Tr}\left(|\psi_{0|0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0|0}|M^{\prime}_{1|1}\right)\\
+&p_{1}\text{Tr}\left(\rho|\psi_{0|1}\rangle\langle\psi_{0|1}|\right)\text{Tr}\left(|\psi_{0|1}\rangle\langle\psi_{0|1}|M^{\prime}_{1|0}\right)\\
+&p_{1}\text{Tr}\left(\Gamma(\rho)|\psi_{0|1}\rangle\langle\psi_{0|1}|\right)\text{Tr}\left(|\psi_{0|1}\rangle\langle\psi_{0|1}|M^{\prime}_{0|1}\right)\\
\end{split}

(43)

One can then verify that the above expression is the same as the maximum success probability of sequentially discriminating pure states
\ket{\psi_{0|0}\psi_{0|0}}
and
\ket{\psi_{0|1}\psi_{0|1}}
, with prior probabilities
p_{0},p_{1}
, by sequentially using measurement
\{\rho,\Gamma(\rho)\}
on the first copy then
\{M^{\prime}_{0|0},M^{\prime}_{1|0}\}
or
\{M^{\prime}_{1|1},M^{\prime}_{0|1}\}
on the second copy. (Notice here that we have flipped the indices of the measurement
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{1}
.)

The strategy that achieves this success probability, for the equal distribution case, will be to first input the optimal state for the discrimination of the two measurements without access to the post-measurement state and without entanglement. Explicitly, the pure state whose Bloch vector is the bisector of the two vectors corresponding to states
\ket{\psi_{0|0}}
and
\ket{\psi_{1|1}}
. If the output is
0
, then apply the optimal measurement for discriminating
\ket{\psi_{0|0}}
and
\ket{\psi_{0|1}}
occurring with respective probabilities
p_{M}
and
1-p_{M}
. If the the output is
1
, then apply the optimal measurement for discriminating
\ket{\psi_{1|1}}
and
\ket{\psi_{1|0}}
occurring with respective probabilities
p_{M}
and
1-p_{M}
. We call
p_{M}
, the optimal success probability of discriminating measurements
\mathcal{M}_{0}
and
\mathcal{M}_{1}
, with no access to the post-measurement state, as given in
Sedlák and Ziman 2014
.

VII
Main result 2: Large advantage of using the post-measurement state

As proven in the previous section, having access to a post-measurement state allows us to outperform the strategies that do not take it into account, for the special case of projective qubit measurements. A natural question to ask is then, how large can this advantage be in the general case? In order to compare the performance of strategies that do consider the post measurement state to those that do not, we define the
instrument advantage bias
as the ratio of the bias between the two optimal strategies’ success probabilities.

\displaystyle\Delta(\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2}):=

\displaystyle\frac{p_{s,\max}^{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2})-0.5}{p_{s,\max}^{\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2})-0.5}

(44)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d_{L}(\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2})}{d_{M}(\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2})},

(45)

where:

•

p_{s,\max}^{\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2})
denotes the optimal success probability for discriminating between the POVMs
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, occurring with equal probabilities.

•

p_{s,\max}^{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2})
denotes the optimal success probability for discriminating between the Lüders instruments
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
, occurring with equal probabilities.

and
d_{L}(.,.)
and
d_{M}(.,.)
are respectively the Lüders’ distance and the post-measurement agnostic distance as defined in
Eq.

8
and
Eq.

12
.

Using
Theorem

1
, we see that for a pair of projective qubit measurements occurring with equal probabilities, the advantage can be written:

\displaystyle\Delta(\mathcal{M}_{\psi},\mathcal{M}_{\phi})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{1-|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|^{4}}}{\sqrt{1-|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|^{2}}}

(46)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1+|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|^{2}}.

(47)

Hence, we see that,
\Delta(\mathcal{M}_{\psi},\mathcal{M}_{\phi})\leq\sqrt{2}
, is an upper bound which is attained in the limit where
\innerproduct{\psi}{\phi}=1
, that is, when the two measurements are the same.

In the following, we prove that, when the pair of discriminated measurements are not restricted to sets of two rank-1 projectors, the instrument advantage bias can get arbitrarily large.

Theorem 2
.

Even when considering qubit measurements, the instrument advantage bias (see
Eq.

44
)
\Delta(\mathcal{M}_{\psi},\mathcal{M}_{\phi})
can be arbitrarily large. That is, for any positive real number
L\in\mathbb{R}
, there exist POVMs
\mathcal{M}_{\psi},\mathcal{M}_{\phi}
such that
\Delta(\mathcal{M}_{\psi},\mathcal{M}_{\phi})>L
.

The proof of
Theorem

2
is constructive. For that, we consider the POVM
\mathcal{Z}=\{|0\rangle\langle 0|,|1\rangle\langle 1|\}
, and the family of POVMs

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{p}:=\{|0\rangle\langle 0|+p|1\rangle\langle 1|,(1-p)|1\rangle\langle 1|\},

(48)

where
p\in[0,1]
. The POVM
W^{p}
can be seen, as a noisy version of
\mathcal{Z}
, where for
p=0
,
W^{p=0}=\mathcal{Z}
, and when
p=1
,
W^{p=1}
is a measurement that outputs
0
regardless of the input state. We will prove that the instrument advantage bias
\Delta(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{W}^{p})
gets arbitrarily large as
p\rightarrow 0
.
It is also noteworthy that all POVM elements of the pair of measurements
\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathcal{W}^{p}
are diagonal in the computational basis, hence they may be viewed as classical measurements. This shows that post-measurement states also enables large advantages in the classical regime.

Proof.

Let us denote
\mathcal{W}^{p}:=\{|0\rangle\langle 0|+p|1\rangle\langle 1|,(1-p)|1\rangle\langle 1|\}
, which defines a measurement for all
p\in[0,1]
. Consider now the discrimination of
\mathcal{W}^{p}
and
\mathcal{Z}
occurring with equal probabilities. We can derive the optimal probability of discriminating these measurement without access to the post-measurement state by first writing each of the measurements as a quantum channel and then solving for the corresponding channel discrimination task. For a measurement
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{a}\}_{a=1}^{n}
, we will represent the corresponding quantum channel as follows:

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}(\rho)=\sum_{a=1}^{n}\text{Tr}\left(M_{a}\rho\right)|a\rangle\langle a|

(49)

We will write the success probability of the channel discrimination strategy where we input state
\rho\in\mathcal{L}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\text{aux}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\text{in}}\right)
,
\rho=\sum_{ijkl}\rho_{ikjl}\outerproduct{i}{j}\otimes\outerproduct{k}{l}
and apply the optimal measurement for the given input state at the end, as the success probability of optimally discriminating states
\mathbb{1}\otimes\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}}\left(\rho\right)
and
\mathbb{1}\otimes\widetilde{\mathcal{W}^{p}}\left(\rho\right)
. We then have:

p_{s,\rho}^{\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{W}^{p})=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\norm{( \id\otimes\map{\Wcal^p})(\rho)- (\id\otimes\map{\Zcal}) (\rho) }_{1},

(50)

where
\norm{.}_{1}
represents the trace norm or 1-norm. After replacing all terms with their corresponding expressions, the equation simplifies to:

\displaystyle p_{s,\rho}^{\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{W}^{p})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\norm{\sum_{i,j} p \rho_{i1j1} \ket{i}\bra{j} \otimes\hat{Z} }_{1}

(51)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{p}{2}\norm{\sum_{i,j}\rho_{i1j1} \ket{i}\bra{j}}_{1},

(52)

where
\hat{Z}=|0\rangle\langle 0|-|1\rangle\langle 1|
is the Pauli Z matrix. One can here see, that
\max_{\rho}p_{s,\rho}^{\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{W}^{p})
is achieved for any coherent superposition of input states
|01\rangle\langle 01|
and
|11\rangle\langle 11|
, with
\max_{\rho}\norm{\sum_{i,j}\rho_{i1j1} \ket{i}\bra{j}}_{1}=1
, and thus:

\max_{\rho}p_{s,\rho}^{\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{W}^{p})=p_{s,\max}^{\mathcal{M}}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{W}^{p})=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{p}{2}

(53)

We now derive a lower bound on the optimal probability of discriminating
\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathcal{W}^{p}
provided access to the post-measurement state by discriminating between the quantum channel representations
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathcal{Z}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathcal{W}^{p}}
of their corresponding Lüders’ instruments
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{Z}}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{W}^{p}}
. We can write the probability of optimally discriminating the two channel with no access to entanglement and for an input state
\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{in}})
,
\rho=\sum_{i,j}\rho_{ij}\ket{i}\bra{j}
by applying the same method we did earlier:

p_{s,\text{LOCC}\,\rho}^{\mathcal{I}}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{W}^{p})=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\norm{\map\Ical_{\Wcal^p}(\rho)- \map\Ical_\Zcal(\rho) }_{1},

(54)

We replace again all terms with their corresponding expressions and get the following expression:

\begin{split}p_{s,\text{LOCC}\,\rho}^{\mathcal{I}}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{W}^{p})=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}||p\rho_{11}|10\rangle\langle 10|+\sqrt{p}\rho_{01}|00\rangle\langle 10|\\
+\sqrt{p}\rho_{10}|10\rangle\langle 00|-p\rho_{11}|11\rangle\langle 11|\,||_{1},\end{split}

(55)

The operator inside the trace norm being hermitian (self-adjoint), we can compute it by summing over the absolute eigenvalues,
\norm{A}_{1}=\sum_{i}|\lambda_{i}|
, which in our case are:

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}

\displaystyle=0

(56)

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{p}}{2}\left(\rho_{11}-\sqrt{4\rho_{01}\rho_{10}+\rho_{11}^{2}}\right)

(57)

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{p}}{2}\left(\rho_{11}+\sqrt{4\rho_{01}\rho_{10}+\rho_{11}^{2}}\right)

(58)

\displaystyle\lambda_{3}

\displaystyle=-p\rho_{11}

(59)

Thus:

p_{s,\text{LOCC}\,\rho}^{\mathcal{I}}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{W}^{p})=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left(\sqrt{p}\sqrt{4\rho_{01}\rho_{10}+\rho_{11}^{2}}+p\rho_{11}\right)

(60)

By imputing the state
|1\rangle\langle 1|
, we can set a lower bound
p_{s,\text{LOCC}\,|1\rangle\langle 1|}^{\mathcal{I}}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{W}^{p})
on the maximum value of
p_{s,\text{LOCC}\,\rho}^{\mathcal{I}}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{W}^{p})
:

p_{s,\text{LOCC}\,|1\rangle\langle 1|}^{\mathcal{I}}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{W}^{p})=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left(p+\sqrt{p}\right).

(61)

We then have:

\Delta(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{W}^{p})\geq\frac{p+\sqrt{p}}{2p}

(62)

Where for
p\rightarrow 0
,
\Delta\rightarrow\infty
, thus proving that for any
L\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists
\epsilon
, for which
\Delta(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{W}^{\epsilon})>L.

∎

IX
Discussion

Motivated by the potential usefulness of having access to post-measurement quantum states when discriminating quantum measurements, a resource omitted by most of previous literature, we proposed the task of Lüders instrument discrimination, a measurement discrimination where the post-measurement is taken into account. We then investigate the problem of measurement discrimination with access to the post-measurement state, and adapt tools developed for quantum channels to investigate Lüders instrument discrimination from an analytical and computational perspective.

We start by showing that, in the simplest form of the problem, namely dichotomic qubit projective Lüders instrument discrimination, the problem is equivalent to two-copy pure state discrimination, extending the results from
Manna et al. 2025
. This may be contrasted to the measurement discrimination counterpart that is equivalent to one-copy pure state discrimination
Sedlák and Ziman 2014
, thus concluding that having access to the post-measurement state provides a significant advantage for the task. In addition to bounding the success probability, we discuss the possible strategies one can implement to optimally approach the problem, also showing that the maximum success probability can be achieved without the use of entanglement and proposing an optimal strategy that does not require it.

Then, we quantify and formalize the advantage gained in a dichotomic measurement discrimination task from having access to the post-measurement state. We do so by comparing the diamond distances between the pair of quantum channels associated to the measurement devices and the pair of channels corresponding to their Lüders instruments. We define the ‘Lüders Advantage Bias’ as the ratio of these two quantities, and present an explicit qubit example where the advantage bias can get arbitrarily large for certain measurements, showcasing the significance of considering the post-measurement state when discriminating quantum measurements.
Finally we employ the numerical tools developed for quantum channel discrimination to analyze the differences between measurement discrimination and Lüders discrimination tasks in scenarios that are harder to deal with analytically. Our computational code is openly available at
cha
.
