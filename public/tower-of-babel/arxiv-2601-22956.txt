Abstract
. Large language model (LLM) research in software engineering has largely focused on tasks such as code generation and bug repair. In practice, teams often draft multiple candidate proposals for fixing an issue and then deliberate on one golden proposal for implementation. This selection requires not only assessing the issue’s scope, impact, and urgency, but also a clear understanding of each proposal’s strengths and weaknesses. A good selection could make issue resolution more reliable reducing regression and operational risk, whereas a poor choice can increase risk and even cause unpredictable failures. We first conduct a manual study of real-world issues to characterize the rationales maintainers use when selecting among competing proposals. Motivated by these findings, we introduce SWE-Manager, a joint selection-and-synthesis approach that selects the best proposal and synthesizes a golden proposal. SWE-Manager yields an 8B model trained via reinforcement learning (RL) to compare proposals, justify its choice, and synthesize a golden proposal for implementation. We consider that proposal selection is a reasoning task, mirroring how technical managers review competing proposals by weighing issue context and each proposal’s solution, without executing code or running tests. On the SWE-Lancer Manager benchmark, SWE-Manager achieves 53.21% selection accuracy and 57.75% earned reward ($152,750), outperforming strong baselines including GPT-5. To further evaluate the effectiveness of SWE-Manager in real-world issue resolution, we design the P2A framework, which simulates the real-world workflow where multiple proposals are drafted, reviewed, and a golden proposal is decided for implementation. On the SWE-Lancer IC benchmark, P2A with SWE-Manager achieves a 55.6% pass rate matching the performance of P2A with GPT-5 and improves end-to-end resolution over the baseline by 7.1%. These results highlight proposal selection as a critical yet underexplored capability for LLM-based software engineering systems.

2.
MANUAL STUDY

To uncover the underlying principles of proposal selection, we follow the data collection protocol used for the SWE-Lancer Manager benchmark
(
22
)
and curate a dataset containing 2,852 GitHub issues and corresponding 7,500 proposals. Each issue includes at least two candidate proposals. The issues include publicly available maintainer discussions that document the selection process, and in all cases the decision is made before the final code changes are implemented. This setting enables us to analyze, from the maintainers’ perspective, why a particular proposal is ultimately selected.

2.1.
Study Design

Similar to prior studies
(
9
;
6
)
, we determine the required sample size using standard statistical techniques
(
15
)
. Specifically, the sample size is computed as a function of the desired margin of error, confidence level, and population size. In our study, the population comprises 2,852 issues. Setting the margin of error to
\pm 5\%
and the confidence level to
95\%
, we obtain a required sample size of 339.
To ensure the reliability of our qualitative analysis, the first and second authors independently reviewed the sampled issues and cross-checked the extracted rationales for proposal selection. Specifically, for each issue, we identified the selected proposal and traced the corresponding discussion to collect explicit evidence (e.g., maintainer comments, review feedback, and stated requirements) that justified the decision. The first two authors then compared their annotations, reconciled disagreements through discussion, and consolidated a final set of reasons. Based on these cross-validated annotations, we analyze why a particular proposal is chosen, synthesizing recurring decision factors and trade-offs reflected in repository owners’ deliberations.

Risk and Safety.

This theme captures selection decisions that prioritize safe deployment and low regression risk. The most frequent single criterion is choosing a
minimal, verifiable change with low regression risk
(86/339, 25.4%), indicating that maintainers often prefer proposals that are easier to validate and less likely to introduce unintended side effects. Related rationales include selecting proposals with
controlled fix scope and high executability
(32/339, 9.4%) and
better robustness in edge cases
(20/339, 5.9%). A smaller portion of decisions explicitly cite urgency and opt for
time-critical mitigation
(7/339, 2.1%). Collectively, these rationales suggest that proposal selection frequently functions as a risk-filtering process: among plausible fixes, maintainers prefer the one that is most predictable to ship.

Fix Depth.

Fix depth reflects how maintainers distinguish superficial patches from deeper remedies. The dominant rationale is
root-cause elimination
(68/339, 20.1%), where proposals are favored for correcting the underlying failure mechanism rather than masking symptoms. In addition, maintainers sometimes prefer
Systematic remediation
(31/339, 9.1%), favoring solutions that mitigate not only the reported failure but also other instances of the same failure pattern. Notably, fix-depth rationales often coexist with risk considerations in discussion: deeper fixes are attractive, but are evaluated against their scope and verification burden.

Maintainability.

Maintainability rationales reflect the need to keep the codebase coherent under project-specific constraints. A substantial share of decisions favor proposals that
conform to repository engineering constraints
(42/339, 12.4%), such as architectural boundaries, reuse of existing abstractions, consistency conventions, or component unification. Another portion favors proposals that are
extensible for future requirements or variants
(13/339, 3.8%), where maintainers prefer designs that accommodate foreseeable evolution. These criteria are often context-dependent and can appear non-obvious without the surrounding discussion; in such cases, citing a short discussion snippet can help clarify what a repository constraint or an extensibility concern concretely means.

Others.

Finally, we observe rationales that arise from product semantics and pragmatic workflow constraints.
Product/design expectations
(26/339, 7.7%) capture decisions where maintainers select proposals that better match intended user-facing behavior even when alternatives are technically plausible.
Tie-breaking by delivery speed
(14/339, 4.1%) occurs when candidates are assessed as similarly acceptable and the decision is made based on implementation or review efficiency. These rationales highlight that proposal selection is not purely technical optimization, but is shaped by broader socio-technical considerations.

Findings.

Our manual study leads to several practical findings.
(1)
Safety-first dominates selection:
nearly half of rationales (42.8%) prioritize verifiability, controlled scope, and regression avoidance, and the single most common criterion is minimal, verifiable change (25.4%).
(2)
Maintainers value depth, but under constraints:
fix-depth rationales are the second largest group (29.2%), suggesting that maintainers actively distinguish root-cause fixes from superficial patches, yet such choices are frequently weighed against the risk and validation burden captured by the dominant safety theme.
(3)
Repository fit is a recurring deciding factor:
maintainability rationales (16.2%), especially conformance to repository engineering constraints (12.4%), indicate that “fit” with project conventions and architectural expectations is a recurring deciding factor among otherwise plausible proposals.
(4)
Socio-technical factors are non-negligible:
product semantics and delivery pragmatics together account for 11.8%, demonstrating that real-world selections can hinge on user-facing expectations or process efficiency when technical considerations do not uniquely determine a winner.

METHOD

Figure 2
.
SWE-Manager methodology overview.
We formulate proposal selection as taking an issue and its candidate proposals as input, and producing a reasoning trace, a selected proposal ID, a selection justification, and a synthesized golden proposal as output. Then, we curate GitHub issues paired with multiple candidate proposals and a maintainer-selected best-proposal ID. Conditioned on the issue, proposals, and the selected ID, we prompt GPT-5 to generate supervision targets including the reasoning trace, justification, and golden proposal. Starting from Qwen3-8B, we first apply supervised fine-tuning on these targets to teach the model how to reason about proposal selection. We then optimize it with DAPO using weighted rewards for reasoning quality, selection correctness, justification, and golden-proposal synthesis, yielding a model that both selects the best proposal and produces an actionable consolidated plan.

3.1.
SWE-Manager

Motivated by the findings of our manual study, we seek to
scale
proposal selection beyond case-by-case human deliberation by learning a decision policy from real-world issue discussions. This goal is inspired by how technical managers operate in practice, that when presented with multiple proposals from engineers, they weigh factors such as technical feasibility, risk, maintainability, and alignment with project goals before committing to a solution.
Our curated dataset provides rich evidence of such deliberation, capturing how maintainers and contributors negotiate proposal choices in the open.
Building on this dataset and the extracted decision strategies, we introduce SWE-Manager-8B, a reasoning-based model that selects the most suitable proposal from multiple candidates, trained via rule-guided reinforcement learning (RL).

3.1.1.
Task Formulation

Given a software engineering issue
I

N

P=\{p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{N}\}
, SWE-Manager is tasked with selecting the best proposal and synthesizing a golden proposal. Specifically, the model generates an token sequence that contains: (1) a structured reasoning trace that compares candidates and key trade-offs, (2) the
ID
of the selected best proposal
\hat{p}\in P
, (3) a concise explanation that justifies the selection based on evidence from the issue context, and (4) a

\tilde{p}
that consolidates the strengths of the candidates into a refined plan.
Requiring SWE-Manager to synthesize a
golden proposal
is intended to strengthen proposal comparison rather than to merely produce an additional artifact. By constructing a consolidated plan that selectively integrates the strongest components from competing candidates, the model is forced to contrast proposals along their key differences, surface trade-offs, and reconcile inconsistencies. This process promotes a more deliberate selection behavior and, in turn, supports more accurate identification of the best proposal than directly predicting an index.

3.1.2.
Data Preparation.

Following the data collection procedure of SWE-Lancer
(
22
)
, we curate a dataset from GitHub consisting of 2,852 issues and 7,500 associated proposals, where each issue has at least two competing proposals. Each instance includes the issue context, the candidate proposal set, and a ground-truth proposal ID selected by maintainers.
To obtain structured supervision beyond the discrete selection label, we prompt GPT-5
(
23
)
with the issue, the candidate proposals, and the ground-truth selection. GPT-5 is required to produce a reasoning trace, a concise justification for why the selected proposal is preferable, and a synthesized
golden proposal
that consolidates useful elements across candidates by contrasting their strengths and weaknesses and removing incorrect or risky steps.
Because the generation is anchored to real maintainer decisions, the resulting annotations provide decision-consistent training signals for both selection and consolidation.

3.1.3.
SFT

As supervised fine-tuning has made great success in teaching model how to think in math and other reasoning task, we follow such practice for LLM alignment
(
1
;
25
;
5
)
initializing the model with supervised fine-tuning.
In this stage, the input to the model consists of (i) the issue description, including the problem statement and relevant context, and (ii) a set of candidate proposals for that issue.
The output is a single structured sequence with four fields: (1) a
think
section that performs explicit comparative reasoning over the candidates under the issue constraints, (2) a
selected_id
that identifies the chosen proposal, (3) a
justification
that concisely explains why this proposal is preferred over alternatives, and (4) a
golden_proposal
that consolidates the most useful elements across proposals into an executable plan.
We include SFT as a necessary initialization step because SFT supplies dense token-level guidance on the desired thinking process and output structure, which stabilizes subsequent RL training and improves sample efficiency. This design choice is also supported by our ablation results, where SFT followed by DAPO yields the strongest performance compared with applying DAPO directly.
Starting from Qwen3-8B
(
42
)
, we train on these structured targets so that the model learns both the desired response format and a reasoning prior for proposal selection, providing a stable initialization for subsequent DAPO optimization.

3.1.4.
DAPO Optimization.

After SFT, we further optimize the policy with reinforcement learning using DAPO (Decoupled Clip and Dynamic sAmpling Policy Optimization)
(
45
)
. The input is identical to SFT: the model takes the issue and candidate proposals as input and generates a structured sequence containing
think
,
selected_id
,
justification
, and
golden_proposal
. We apply RL because proposal selection is ultimately a
decision
problem, where improvements should be driven by outcome-aligned signals rather than only imitating model-written rationales. In particular, RL allows us to directly optimize for correct selection while encouraging the model to maintain high-quality reasoning and produce an actionable consolidated plan.
We adopt DAPO instead of standard GRPO
(
29
)
training because DAPO improves stability and sample efficiency when optimizing long-form generation with mixed reward signals. Concretely, DAPO decouples clipping from policy optimization and uses dynamic sampling to maintain informative training signals across batches, which helps avoid overly conservative updates and reduces sensitivity to reward noise. This is well-suited to our setting, where the reward combines a verifiable selection signal with multiple softer, similarity-based components.
Concretely, we use a weighted reward that promotes (i) correct proposal-index selection, (ii) high-quality reasoning, (iii) faithful justifications that reflect proposal trade-offs, and (iv) effective golden-proposal synthesis that consolidates complementary strengths into an actionable plan:

(1)

r\;=\;0.4\,r_{\text{sel}}\;+\;0.2\,r_{\text{think}}\;+\;0.2\,r_{\text{justi}}\;+\;0.2\,r_{\text{gold}},

where the selection reward is a verifiable indicator

(2)

r_{\text{sel}}=\mathbb{I}\!\left[\hat{p}=p^{*}\right],

and
r_{\text{think}},r_{\text{justi}},r_{\text{gold}}\in[0,1]
are normalized similarity-based scores for the
think
content, the
justification
, and the synthesized golden proposal, respectively.

3.1.5.
Training Settings

In the supervised fine-tuning stage, we use full-parameter fine-tuning with a learning rate of
5\times 10^{-6}
, a warmup ratio of 0.1, and a batch size of 48.
Based on the insights from recent DAPO reports, we set the clip ratio to a high value of 0.28 and do not employ a KL loss in the objective function. With a batch size of 48, we train Qwen3-8B for 3 epochs. Throughout training, the temperature is maintained at 1.0, and the learning rate is started at
1\times 10^{-6}
. The training process utilizes the MS-Swift
(
47
)
framework with the vLLM
(
17
)
engine for rollout. All the training is distributed across 12 A100 GPUs, leveraging the Zero3
(
27
)
algorithm.

3.2.
P2A

To evaluate SWE-Manager ’s performance in a real-world issue resolution scenario, we design the P2A framework. P2A is intended to simulate a real-world workflow in which multiple proposals are drafted, reviewed, and a proposal for implementation, which is common in real-world software engineering, where solution ideas are first discussed and reviewed before any code is modified.
Specifically, P2A decomposes the end-to-end issue resolve process into three collaborating roles: (1) a
Proposal Agent
that reads the issue context and generates candidate proposals, each including a restatement of the problem in issue, a root cause analyze of the issue, and the proposed method to solve the issue; (2) a
Technical Manager
will review all candidate proposals under the issue constraints and synthesize the golden proposal for implementation;
and (3) an
Implementation Agent
that executes the golden proposal by producing the corresponding code changes to resolve the issue.
In this framework, SWE-Manager plays the role of a Technical Manager, generating a token sequence that includes the selected best proposal and a synthesized golden proposal.

In practice, we use the synthesized
golden proposal
for implementation rather than the selected proposal.
We aim to reflect the model’s understanding of the issue and its analysis of candidate proposals in downstream issue resolution through the P2A framework.
Unlike the SWE-Lancer Manager benchmark, where one candidate is guaranteed to correspond to the eventual ground-truth solution, proposals generated on the fly by the Proposal Agent may all be incorrect or incomplete.
In this case, forcing the model to choose one proposal for implementation will inevitably yield an incorrect patch, because downstream execution must follow a flawed proposal. As a result, even when the model can identify that all candidates are incorrect or inadequate, it has no channel to express this judgment in the downstream outcome.
Using the synthesized golden proposal gives the model an opportunity to fully demonstrate its ability to reason over competing proposals by cross-analyzing candidates, extracting useful elements, and composing a synthesized proposal that guides the downstream Implementation Agent.

Both the

and the

are adapted from mini-SWE-agent
(
43

, a lightweight bash-driven software engineering agent that interacts with the repository solely through shell commands.
As shown in Figure
3
, we first provide an issue description to the Proposal Agent. The issue typically describes (i) steps to reproduce the bug, (ii) the expected behavior, (iii) the observed behavior, and (iv) the affected platform or environment. The Proposal Agent analyzes the issue description and interacts with the codebase to understand the surrounding context and identify potential causes. It then generates the candidate proposals, each containing a restatement of the problem, a root-cause analysis, and a concrete method for resolving the bug.
Next, SWE-Manager reasons over the candidate proposals by systematically comparing their strengths and weaknesses (e.g., feasibility, risk of regression, maintainability, and consistency with existing design). Based on this comparison, it selects the best proposal, provides an explicit justification for the choice, and synthesizes a
golden proposal
that consolidates the advantages of the candidates.
Finally, the Implementation Agent follows the golden proposal as guidance to implement the required changes and produce the corresponding code diff to resolve the issue.
This role-based decomposition allows us to isolate and rigorously assess the contribution of proposal selection, while also measuring its downstream impact on the final implementation quality.

4.
EXPERIMENT

In this section, SWE-Manager is evaluated for selecting the best proposal from competing candidates. Its impact is then validated in a realistic workflow, testing whether proposal reasoning and selection prior to coding improves end-to-end issue resolution. Finally, ablations and analyses identify which training and modeling components contribute most to SWE-Manager’s proposal-selection gains.

4.1.
Benchmarks

We evaluate SWE-Manager on the SWE-Lancer benchmark suite released by OpenAI
(
22
)
, which comprises two benchmarks: Manager and IC (Individual Contributor).
SWE-Lancer Manager benchmarks proposal selection: given an issue description and multiple candidate proposals, the goal is to select the maintainer-selected (ground-truth) best proposal. It contains 265 instances with a total reward budget of $264,500. Each instance includes a user-reported issue (typically with reproduction steps, observed and expected behavior, and environment details) and structured candidate proposals (e.g., problem restatement, root-cause analysis, and a fix strategy).
SWE-Manager is further validated on SWE-Lancer IC within the P2A framework to assess whether the synthesized golden proposal improves downstream implementation. SWE-Lancer IC measures end-to-end issue resolution via test-based verification of the generated patch and contains 198 instances with a total reward budget of $189,300.

4.3.
Metrics

4.3.1.
SWE-Lancer Manager (
Match (%), #Match, Earned (%), $Earned
).

We evaluate proposal selection by checking whether the model-selected proposal matches the ground-truth proposal for each issue. We report Match (%), the percentage of issues for which the selected proposal ID matches the ground-truth proposal ID. We additionally report #Match, the absolute number of issues for which the selected proposal matches the ground truth. Following SWE-Lancer’s scoring scheme, we report Earned (%), the percentage of the total available reward earned by the model on the test set, and $Earned, the total dollar amount earned by correct selections.

4.3.2.
SWE-Lancer IC (
Pass (%), #Pass, Earned (%), $Earned
).

We evaluate downstream issue resolution using the benchmark’s execution-based criterion. We report Pass (%), defined as the percentage of issues for which the produced code patch passes the provided verification and resolves the issue. We additionally report #Pass, the absolute number of issues successfully resolved. We also report Earned (%), defined as the percentage of the total available reward that is earned by successful patches on the test set, and $Earned, defined as the total dollar amount earned by successful patches.

5.
DISCUSSION

5.1.
Proposal Guidance for Implementation Agent

In P2A, we evaluate the synthesized golden proposal via test-based patch success on the SWE-Lancer IC benchmark. Although this criterion directly reflects the downstream objective, it couples the model’s proposal-synthesis quality with the implementation agent’s ability to implement the plan, which can confound attribution. To strengthen internal validity, we further analyze solved-instance overlap. Importantly, the downstream Implementation Agent differs from the mini-SWE-agent baseline only in that it is required to follow the provided golden proposal; all other prompts, tools, and inference settings are held constant.
In particular, we compare P2A’s successes against the mini-SWE-agent baseline and the
union
of instances solved by any mini-SWE-agent powered by the proposal-source backbones.
We find that 31.82% of P2A’s successful cases fall outside the baseline, and 14.55% fall outside the union, indicating that, with golden-proposal guidance, the Implementation Agent can succeed on a non-trivial set of instances that are not recovered by the baseline or any single proposal source. This overlap pattern supports the interpretation that a key portion of P2A’s gains is attributable to the golden proposal as an actionable, higher-quality plan that steers downstream patch generation toward correct fixes.

5.2.
Threats to Validity

5.2.1.
Data Leakage and Randomness

To reduce the impact of randomness, we set the temperature to 0 for SWE-Manager-8B during evaluation, ensuring deterministic outputs and making results comparable across runs and baselines.
We also mitigate potential data leakage and contamination effects. When constructing the training set, we explicitly remove all instances from the SWE-Lancer Manager and SWE-Lancer IC benchmarks. Cross-issue leakage is also unlikely because candidate proposals are written in a structured natural-language format that mainly includes a problem restatement, root-cause analysis, and a proposed solution, and they contain little to no concrete code. As a result, proposals from later issues are unlikely to encode implementation details that would reveal the ground-truth best proposal for earlier benchmark issues.

5.2.2.
Generalizability and Robustness

A key concern is whether SWE-Manager-8B can generalize to proposal-selection problems that it has not encountered during training. In the P2A framework, SWE-Manager-8B serves as a selector over proposals generated on the fly by proposal agents. Because these proposals are synthesized at inference time and are not part of the training corpus, P2A evaluates selection under genuinely unseen proposal sets, reducing the likelihood that performance is driven by memorizing specific training instances.

However, our results also reveal a limitation in robustness under larger proposal pools. The training distribution is concentrated on instances with two to three proposals, whereas practical settings may involve richer candidate sets. When the number of proposals exceeds three, the input length increases sharply and SWE-Manager-8B is evaluated in a regime that is relatively under-represented during training. This distribution shift can degrade comparative judgment under longer and denser inputs, and it partially explains why SWE-Manager-8B trails GPT-5 when selecting among larger proposal sets.

5.2.3.
Conclusion Reliability

Manual study of selection rationales may be subject to researcher bias and category ambiguity. We mitigate this threat via cross-checking and adjudication, and we provide the taxonomy definitions and representative examples for transparency. End-to-end IC evaluation is computationally expensive, and the behavior of closed-source models may change over time. We therefore document inference settings and model versions, and we release prompts and scripts when possible to facilitate replication.

6.
LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

Our study highlights two key limitations that also suggest concrete future directions.
First, our current SWE-Manager operates without code access. While SWE-Manager achieves stronger overall proposal-selection performance than the agent baseline powered by GPT-5, it remains slightly behind on high-value instances. Notably, for GPT-5, the agentic method yields only modest gains in overall accuracy compared with direct selection (47.2% vs. 44.15%), yet it may still help recover expensive cases. A promising next step is to train a tool-augmented variant of SWE-Manager that can selectively query repository context during review, while preserving the same decision-oriented interface to enable fair comparison and controllable cost.

Second, while in the P2A framework proposals are generated on the fly, SWE-Manager has demonstrated generalizability to previously unseen proposal sets. However, its robustness can still be challenged as the proposal pool grows and the input context becomes substantially longer. Future work will rebalance training toward larger proposal pools and longer contexts, and expand coverage with synthesized multi-proposal data, for example, by sampling diverse candidate sets and using curriculum training that gradually increases proposal count and context length.

9.
CONCLUSION

Proposal selection is a critical step in software engineering workflows. We conduct a manual study of maintainer discussions to characterize why certain proposals are chosen, and use these insights to train SWE-Manager-8B, an 8B selector via decision-aligned reinforcement learning that selects the best proposal and synthesizes a golden proposal. SWE-Manager-8B achieves strong performance on the SWE-Lancer Manager benchmark and surpasses GPT-5 in both accuracy and reward.
We further introduce P2A to evaluate SWE-Manager-8B in an end-to-end workflow in which multiple proposals are drafted, reviewed, and a final proposal for implementation. On SWE-Lancer IC, P2A with SWE-Manager-8B matches P2A with GPT-5, and improves patch success over baselines by 7.1%. These results support proposal selection as a scalable capability and highlight proposal consolidation as a practical mechanism for improving downstream issue resolution.
