Oculocardiac reflex causing bradycardia in a trauma patient following firearm injury
Authors: Jennifer E Geller, Saba Daneshpooy, Sophia Block, Meera Kohli, Megan Lundy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe oculocardiac reflex is a vagal response to pressure on the globe or periorbital structures resulting in a reduction in heart rate greater than 20 %. Here, we describe the case of a patient who sustained extensive craniofacial and orbital trauma from a firearm injury whose otherwise unexplained bradycardia resolved following surgical decompression.Keywords: Firearm injury, Oculocardiac reflex, Vagal response, Trauma

Introduction

The oculocardiac reflex, also known as the Aschner reflex or the trigeminovagal reflex is defined as a reduction in heart rate by greater than 20 % secondary to pressure on the ocular globe or traction of the extraocular muscles [1]. This physiologic phenomenon results from stimulation of the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve, which connects with the central nervous system via its sensory nucleus in the pons. Nerve fibers connect to the motor nucleus of the vagus nerve which cause vagal stimulation to the heart resulting in bradycardia (Fig. 1).

A pictoral representation of the anatomy involved in the oculocardiac reflex.

The reflex itself has many triggers, two of which result from trauma to the face. The first is by direct pressure from the trauma or hematoma formation that subsequently increases pressure. The second is by intentional anesthetic nerve blocks [[2], [3], [4]]. This reflex is well described as an intraoperative complication in facial surgeries, however there is limited discussion of its occurrence in the non-operative setting. In this case report, we will discuss a patient presenting to an urban, academic, level 1 trauma center after a firearm injury to the face where this reflex was identified during the hospitalization.

Case presentation

A 63-year-old female presented as a transfer from a referring facility after a self-inflected firearm injury to the face resulting in an intraparenchymal right frontal and temporal lobe hemorrhage, scattered subarachnoid hemorrhages, bilateral posterior subdural hematomas, and comminuted fractures of the bilateral orbits extending from one side to the other side traversing through the globes, ethmoid air cells, and inferior frontal lobes. On initial presentation, her primary survey was intact other than diminished GCS to 12 which was due to the inability to open her eyes. She was intubated for airway protection and taken for CT scans. This revealed the aforementioned injuries. She was transferred to our facility intubated and sedated. Of note, she was hypertensive with systolic blood pressures up to the 200 in normal sinus rhythm which improved with increased sedation.

She was admitted to the Surgical Intensive Care Unit (SICU). Neurosurgery, ophthalmology, and oral and maxillofacial surgery teams were consulted for operative planning. Given her adequate mental status prior to intubation, ability to follow commands with spontaneous awakening trials, and isolated injuries to her upper face without airway involvement, there was consideration of extubation her while awaiting her surgical intervention. As her sedation and ventilation wean trials were attempted, however, she began experiencing bradycardia with normotension. Initially, there was concern for herniation, as it is a more commonly described cause of bradycardia trauma patients, however, she was never hypertensive with these episodes, had no change in GCS, and had neuroimaging without signs of this pathology. In addition, she did not have increased airway pressures during these episodes or other signs of increased intraabdominal pressure to suggest that she had other sources of vagal stimulation, such as Valsalva mechanism. Given her ocular pathology, it was thought that her increased alertness with sedation pauses resulted in increased ocular pressure subsequently triggering the oculocardiac reflex. Cardiology was consulted and agreed that vagal stimulation form her ocular pathology was a likely contributor to her bradycardia consistent with the oculocardiac reflex. On hospital day seven, she proceeded to the operating room with oculoplastic surgery and neurosurgery who performed a bicoronal frontal craniotomy for anterior skull repair with right frontal extraventricular drain placement. After decompression, her sinus bradycardia resolved.

She continued on to make a significant recovery. She was weaned off of sedation and passed spontaneous breathing trials. She was extubated to room air and downgraded from the SICU. She was transferred back to the referring facility for rehabilitation and pending safe discharge to a psychiatric facility.

Discussion

This case is a unique example of a physiologic phenomenon not well studied in the literature nor frequently reported in trauma patients. Most of the literature on this topic are isolated case reports, many of which involve pediatric anesthesia from the standpoint of either post-ophthalmic nerve blocks or pediatric trauma with a few studies on the pre-operative management of the reflex [5,6]. One trauma case describes a 16-year-old male in the United Kingdom who presented for alleged assault with increased swelling of the left eye and found to have a trapdoor fracture with subsequent resolution with decompression.

Physiologically, the reflex stems from increased pressure on the periocular nerves that signal the afferent limb of the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve. Nerve fibers connect with the central nervous system through the sensory nucleus of the trigeminal nerve which is back in the brainstem spanning from the midbrain to the upper cervical spinal cord. Additional nerve fibers connect to the motor nucleus of the Vagus nerve in the medulla oblongata which, via the efferent limb of this process, cause vagal stimulation of the heart and resultant bradycardia. While sinus bradycardia is the most common, associations with reduced arterial pressure, arrhythmia, asystole, and —in the worst-case scenario — cardiac arrest have been reported [1]. Additional non-cardiac manifestations include syncope, nausea, emesis and hypotension given the vagal nature of the response.

While definitive treatment of the reflex requires addressing the underlying cause, there are certain agents that can help in the pre-operative period for stability. While glycopyrrolate was used in this case, ketamine has been noted to counteract vagal stimulation by enhancing sympathetic activity. A study from 2015 noted that ketamine was superior to atropine, an anticholinergic that is commonly used for bradycardia [7]. A much older study from 1984 that looked at 99 patients undergoing strabismus surgery during which all patients were given atropine or glycopyrrolate 45 min before induction with anticipation of traction on the extraocular muscles and resultant oculocardiac reflex. They noted glycopyrrolate to be twice as potent as atropine in dosing the medication [8]. Our patient saw improvement with glycopyrrolate, but ultimately improved with surgical decompression. After her operation as described above, her bradycardia permanently subsided.

Conclusion

While not many studies exist investigating the oculocardiac reflex in the adult trauma setting, it should be considered in cases where there is facial trauma and persistent or intermittent bradycardia. Early recognition can help guide surgical decompression and management.
