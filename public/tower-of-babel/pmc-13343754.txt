Pharmacological treatment for Charcot neuroarthropathy: a systematic review
Authors: Liang Liu, Jun Lu, Jie Wang, Qiong Wang, Hongmou Zhao, Yan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCharcot neuroarthropathy (CN) is a debilitating joint disorder that predominantly affects patients with neuropathy, particularly those with diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN). CN causes painless, rapid joint destruction and often leads to foot deformity, ulceration, osteomyelitis, and, in severe cases, amputation. Its pathogenesis involves repetitive microtrauma due to loss of protective sensation, triggering an inflammatory cascade that activates osteoclasts (OCs) disproportionately relative to osteoblasts (OBs) via the RANKL–RANK–OPG pathway, resulting in progressive bone loss and joint destruction. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the efficacy of anti-resorptive agents in promoting bone remodeling and alleviating clinical symptoms in patients with active or stable CN.MethodsFollowing Cochrane Collaboration guidelines, we searched MEDLINE, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing anti-resorptive agents, such as bisphosphonates, denosumab, calcitonin, and parathyroid hormone analogues, with placebo or no treatment in patients with Charcot neuroarthropathy. Two independent reviewers performed data extraction and risk-of-bias assessment using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool. Primary outcomes were bone mineral density (BMD), bone turnover markers (BTMs), time to remission, change in foot temperature, and adverse events. Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata 18, with random-effects models used to pool results.ResultsWe identified 936 records and nine reports describing seven RCTs met the inclusion criteria. The meta-analysis showed no significant difference in BMD between anti-resorptive agents and control groups. However, anti-resorptive therapy significantly reduced bone resorption markers. Clinical outcomes, including foot temperature change and time to remission, did not differ significantly between groups. Adverse events were similar between the intervention and control groups.ConclusionsAlthough anti-resorptive agents reduce bone resorption markers in patients with Charcot neuroarthropathy, current evidence does not support their efficacy in improving BMD or providing clinically meaningful symptom relief beyond standard offloading. High-quality clinical trials and mechanistic studies are needed to define the role of these agents in CN management.

Introduction

Charcot neuroarthropathy (CN) is a painless, destructive joint disorder that occurs in patients with neuropathy. Jean-Martin Charcot first described CN as a complication of tabes dorsalis in 1868 [1]. Today, however, most cases arise from non-syphilitic neuropathies, particularly diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN), as global diabetes prevalence rises [2]. In patients with diabetes, DPN increases the incidence of CN from 0.1–0.4% [3] to approximately 35% [4]. This subset is termed diabetic Charcot arthropathy (DCA). Among affected joints, the foot and ankle are the most commonly involved sites. Due to its painless onset and rapid, progressive destruction, an uncontrolled CN course often leads to foot deformity, ulceration, or osteomyelitis, and in severe cases, amputation and increased mortality [5]. Consequently, CN is increasingly recognized as one of the most devastating complications of diabetic peripheral neuropathy.

Loss of protective sensation renders the affected joint vulnerable to repetitive microtrauma [6, 7]. Accumulated microdamage triggers joint injury and, most critically, initiates a cascade of inflammatory activity [8, 9]. Sustained inflammation drives excessive activation of the receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL)–receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB (RANK)–osteoprotegerin (OPG) pathway, leading to disproportionate osteoclast (OC) activation relative to osteoblast (OB) activity. This imbalance results in progressive bone loss and joint destruction [10, 11] which a mechanism widely referred to as the injury–inflammation hypothesis of CN pathogenesis [6].

Given that RANKL-RANK-OPG signaling is central to OC-mediated bone collapse in CN, anti-resorptive agents have emerged as adjunctive therapies to standard offloading like total contact casting (TCC) [12]. Bisphosphonates [13], the anti-RANKL monoclonal antibody denosumab [14], calcitonin [15], and parathyroid hormone analogues [16] have been evaluated in different CN stages. While some studies report encouraging clinical and biochemical responses, the evidence base remains limited and findings are inconsistent [12]. A 2021 systematic review (search conducted in 2019) evaluating both anti-resorptive and anti-inflammatory pharmacotherapies for acute CN concluded that existing data do not support routine use of these agents [17]. That review included only five studies including one non-randomized controlled trial and pooled just three for meta-analysis.

To address these gaps, the present meta-analysis evaluates the anti-bone-destructive effects of anti-resorptive agents in patients with active or stable CN, based on randomized controlled trial (RCT) evidence. We also assess clinically relevant outcomes including time to remission, foot temperature change, and adverse events to determine whether these agents provide meaningful symptom relief. Our hypothesis is that anti-resorptive therapy increases bone mass and alleviates symptoms, particularly during the active phase of CN. We specifically sought to answer two questions:1) Do anti-resorptive agents improve bone remodeling in CN, as reflected by changes in bone mineral density (BMD) or bone turnover markers (BTMs)? 2) In the acute phase of CN, do anti-resorptive agents reduce time to remission and foot temperature, and are they associated with acceptable safety?

Materials and methods

Research design and search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with Cochrane Collaboration methodology. We developed a prospective protocol and registered it in PROSPERO (CRD42023416025). Electronic bibliographic databases including MEDLINE, EMBASE, the Cochrane Library, Scopus, Web of Science, and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched on 15 April 2025, with no language restrictions. Given the limited number of published randomized controlled trials on this topic, our search strategy focused specifically on the Population and Intervention components of the PICO framework. Population terms: “neuro-osteoarthropathy”, “Charcot neuroarthropathy”, and “Charcot” and intervention terms: “bisphosphonates”, “pharmac*”, “drug therapy”, “anti-resorptive”, “pamidronate”, “alendronate”, “zoledronate”, “parathyroid hormone”, “calcitonin”, “denosumab”, and “RANKL antibody”. Search strings combined these terms using Boolean operators, adapted to the syntax of each database.

Study selection

According to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [18], the literature search was independently conducted by two reviewers (QW and JW), and the third author (ZHM) cross-checked the results and resolved discrepancies through discussion. After excluding duplicate and irrelevant reports by screening titles and abstracts, we performed full-text review and manual reference checking of all potentially eligible papers and recent systematic reviews. All studies meeting the following selection criteria were included: (1) participants had a confirmed diagnosis of Charcot neuroarthropathy in the acute or quiescent phase. (2) studies compared any anti-resorptive agent, such as bisphosphonates, denosumab, calcitonin, or parathyroid hormone analogues with placebo or no treatment. (3) the study design was a RCT. (4) Outcomes included at least one prespecified primary endpoint. We excluded studies in which participants had active foot infection or severe renal impairment.

Data extraction and quality assessment

The details of participants, interventions and comparators, outcomes, study design, and the events number as well as the effect measurements of the main and additional outcomes in each selected study were extracted and summarized. Two authors (ZY and WQ) independently extracted outcome data. The third author (LL) would take part if disagreements arise. The missing data were handled by searching other reports of the study and contacting the investigator to request the data. For no response from the authors about the missing data, extrapolations or estimates would be implemented by data transformation advised in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews. GetData Graph Digitizer (http://getdata-graph-digitizer.com) was used to extract the data only in figures. The data were recorded in an Excel extracting spreadsheet.

Bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported results were assessed by version 2 of the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) [19] at the study level. Two authors evaluated the bias with the tools of ROB2 developed by Cochrane independently. And the disagreements will be settled by discussing with the third author (ZHM).

Study outcome and statistical analysis

The primary outcomes of this meta-analysis were BMD or bone mineral content (BMC) of the lower extremity, measured by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. Data from the foot, hip, and region of interest within these sites were included. Another primary outcome was BTMs, measured by biochemical assays. All reported BTMs were included, and values from the last follow-up were pooled. The secondary outcomes were time to remission, defined as the period until remission, which was indicated by a temperature difference of less than 2 °C between the two feet. Foot temperature change from baseline to the last follow-up was measured using a clinical thermometer. Adverse events included all reported adverse events.

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). For continuous outcomes, we calculated the mean difference (MD) for time to remission and foot temperature change, or the standardized mean difference (SMD) for BMD and BTMs, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochrane Q test p-value and I² statistic. A random-effects model was used to pool results. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study Characteristics of Included Trials

A total of 936 reports were identified according to our search strategy, and the distribution of records across each database is shown in Fig. 1. Duplicate reports were removed automatically, leaving 617 records for title and abstract screening. The study selection process, including reasons for exclusion at each stage, is illustrated in Fig. 1. Overall, nine reports describing seven RCTs met eligibility criteria for inclusion in the meta-analysis. The characteristics of these studies are presented in Table 1. Briefly, all included trials were RCTs involving 234 participants, all of whom were diagnosed with Charcot neuroarthropathy according to the prespecified selection criteria. Interventions evaluated in these trials included anti-resorptive agents such as pamidronate [20], alendronate [21], calcitonin [15], zoledronic acid [22, 23], teriparatide [24], and parathyroid hormone analogues [16]. Except for the studies by Pitocco et al. [25] and Bem et al. [15], which used no treatment as control, all other trials employed saline as a placebo control, and offloading was applied in both intervention and control groups. Reported outcomes included foot temperature, time to remission, BMD, and bone turnover markers or a subset thereof. Further details on the included studies are provided in Table 1.

Risk of bias

Figure 2 illustrates the summary of the risk of bias for each study according to the Revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2). Two studies showed low bias risk [20, 24] while the others showed high bias risk (5 studies [15, 16, 25–27]). The overall high-risk ratio is beyond 70%. Among the high-risk studies, 3 studies failed to describe the randomized processes [15, 25, 26], and 2 studies [15, 25] failed to avoid awareness of participants’ assignment due to no placebo being set. The main issue for the overall high-risk ratio was no information for their missing outcome data among the high-risk studies. Because the included outcomes were objective values, the included studies exhibited low risk in terms of their “measurement of the outcome” and “selection of the reported result” items.

Bone remodeling

Bone mineral density

Three studies provided BMD values, as shown in Fig. 3a, which illustrates the pooled effect of anti-resorptive agents on BMD across the included trials. No statistically significant difference was observed between the intervention and control groups (SMD = 1.23, 95% CI: −0.47 to 2.00, P = 0.17). Substantial heterogeneity was present among the studies (P < 0.10), with an I² value of 91.52%, indicating high heterogeneity. A sensitivity analysis excluding the study by Pakarinen et al. 2013 [26], identified as the study contributing most to heterogeneity, was conducted. This resulted in a non-significant heterogeneity test (P = 0.17) and an I² value of 46.72%, suggesting moderate heterogeneity between the remaining two studies [24, 27]. Consistent with the overall analysis, no significant difference in BMD was found after excluding this study (SMD = 0.42, 95% CI: −0.37 to 1.21, P = 0.17).

Bone-formation markers

Four studies reported bone formation markers [15, 20, 24, 27]. No statistically significant effect was observed across these trials (SMD = 0.94, 95% CI: −1.55 to 3.43, P = 0.46). Substantial heterogeneity was present among the studies (P < 0.10, I² = 97.31%). Rastogi et al. 2019 [24] was identified as the primary source of heterogeneity. A sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the impact of excluding this study, resulting in no residual heterogeneity (P = 0.51, I² = 0%). However, the pooled effect remained non-significant (SMD = − 0.32, 95% CI: −0.71 to − 0.08, P = 0.12) 9 (Fig. 4).

Bone-resorption markers

Five studies reported bone-resorption markers [15, 20, 21, 24, 27]. The overall pooled results showed the significant effect of the intervention to reduce the bone resorption markers while the heterogeneity was high (SMD=-0.66, CI:-1.22 to -0.09, P = 0.02, I2 = 62.55%). After adjusting by isolating the possible source of the heterogeneity (the outlier of Jude et al. 2001 [20] and the opposite side of Rastogi et al. 2019 [24]), the effect size was still significant and laid the same side (SMD=-0.58, CI:-1.03 to -0.14, P = 0.01, I2 = 0) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

The cornerstone of conservative management for CN is suppression of inflammation and promotion of bone repair, achieved primarily through total contact casting and offloading [28]. In recent years, the central role of RANKL/OPG signaling in CN pathogenesis has been increasingly recognized, prompting clinical exploration of pharmacological interventions targeting this pathway and related effectors [29]. Although the precise mechanisms underlying CN remain incompletely understood, current evidence indicates that inflammatory-driven disruption of the RANKL/OPG balance is a key driver of disease progression [12]. In affected patients, RANKL-mediated osteolysis is excessively activated, while expression of OPG is relatively downregulated. This RANKL upregulation promotes osteoclast (OC) differentiation and contributes directly to bone destruction [30]. Selby et al. [13] first reported improvements in patient symptoms and reductions in CN activity following alkaline phosphatase treatment. Jude et al. [20] reported that pamidronate, a bisphosphonate (BP), reduced disease activity and bone destruction in active CN in their randomized controlled trial. However, high-level clinical evidence remains limited.

A systematic review of medical therapy for acute Charcot neuroarthropathy was published by Rastogi et al. [17]. That review included a limited number of studies, and one of which was a non-randomized controlled trial. Further, the review included a multi-arm study and analyzed its arms as independent comparisons [23], which may have compromised the conclusions due to the introduction of bias. Anther limitation was the inclusion of methylprednisolone, a glucocorticoid. Glucocorticoids such as methylprednisolone may counteract the effects of anti-resorptive agents, potentially influencing pooled results through clinical heterogeneity [31]. To address these limitations, we conducted this systematic review to evaluate the clinical and bone remodeling effects of anti-resorptive agents specifically.

In this systematic review, we included seven RCTs according to our prespecified inclusion and exclusion criteria, all evaluating anti-resorptive agents. To directly assess the biological effects of these agents, bone resorption markers, not just BMD, were prespecified as primary outcomes.

Anti-resorptive agents are widely used in osteoporosis to inhibit bone resorption and increase BMD. In our analysis, only Pakarinen et al. [26] reported a significant hip BMD increase with anti-resorptive therapy versus comparator. The other two trials [24, 27] showed no significant difference, and the pooled effect for total BMD remained non-significant (Fig. 3a). Heterogeneity was high, likely arising from methodological differences: Das et al. [27] reported BMC, whereas Rastogi et al. [24] and Pakarinen et al. [26] reported BMD. Thus SMD was used to evaluate the effect size. Sensitivity analysis excluding Pakarinen et al. [26] reduced heterogeneity (I² = 46.72%; P = 0.17), indicating moderate heterogeneity persisted, potentially due to differences in baseline BMD, measurement methods, anatomical sites, and reporting units. The pooled effect remained non-significant aftering excluding, consistent with the overall result. Therefore, our findings do not support using anti-resorptive agents to enhance BMD in patients with Charcot neuroarthropathy. They also underscore the need for standardized BMD measurement protocols in CN research.

Another objective of this review was to evaluate BTMs which a group of biomarkers reflecting osteoblast and osteoclast activity [32]. A prior meta-analysis could not pool BTMs because included studies measured different markers [17]. BTMs fall into two categories: bone formation markers and bone resorption markers. Formation markers include serum bone-specific alkaline phosphatase, osteocalcin, and procollagen type I N-terminal propeptide. Resorption markers include serum C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen, tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase 5b, and urinary N-telopeptide of type I collagen [33]. Among our included studies, four reported formation markers and five reported resorption markers. Given their distinct biological roles, we analyzed these two categories separately. The SMD was used to harmonize measurement units across studies. Rastogi et al. [24] reported significant improvements in formation markers and the remaining studies found no significant effects.

The most notable finding concerned bone resorption markers. Except for Jude et al. [20], no individual study reported a statistically significant effect. However, pooling resorption markers, after excluding studies contributing to heterogeneity (Jude et al. [20] and Rastogi et al. [24]), revealed a statistically significant reduction. This supports the biological activity of anti-resorptive agents in inhibiting bone resorption in patients with CN.

The second set of outcomes comprised clinical measures, specifically foot temperature and time to remission, key clinical indicators of disease activity [34]. We pooled the change in these parameters from baseline to last follow-up. Four studies reported foot temperature and all enrolled patients with active Charcot neuroarthropathy. The pooled analysis showed no significant reduction in foot temperature following anti-resorptive therapy, with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 46.72%). Excluding the study by Jude et al. [20], which contributed most to heterogeneity, reduced I² to 0% and eliminated heterogeneity, yet the pooled effect remained non-significant. These findings do not provide sufficient evidence to support superior efficacy of anti-resorptive agents over offloading alone for reducing foot temperature in active CN.

Assessment of time to remission is clinically crucial for evaluating CN treatment outcomes. Consensus criteria for defining CN resolution remain lacking [35]. In clinical practice, a reduction of ≥ 2 °C in foot temperature is commonly used to indicate resolution of the acute phase [6]. In this review, Das et al. [23] and Petrova et al. [16] defined resolution using this ≥ 2 °C threshold, whereas Pakarinen et al. [22] used a stricter criterion of ≥ 1 °C. The primary goal of anti-resorptive agents is to alleviate clinical symptoms and reduce the duration of offloading [35]. Pooled results showed a mean difference of − 2.44 weeks with anti-resorptive therapy versus comparator, but this was not statistically significant. Notably, Pakarinen et al. [22] reported prolonged time to remission, contrary to the direction of effect in other trials, which may reflect their more stringent definition of resolution and may indicate that the anti-resorptive agents may prolonged the acurated phase. Overall, our findings do not provide sufficient evidence to support using anti-resorptive agents to accelerate remission in active CN. They further highlight the need for standardized, prospectively validated criteria to assess resolution in future studies.

Side effects were also a concerning outcome of this review. We pooled complication rates using binary outcomes. Overall, no significant difference was observed between anti-resorptive agents and TCC. These findings suggest that the safety profile of anti-resorptive agents in Charcot neuroarthropathy is comparable to their established safety profile in osteoporosis management [36].

Our findings indicate no significant effects of anti-resorptive agents on BMD or clinical outcomes in the Charcot neuroarthropathy, suggesting that the underlying inflammatory process may not be effectively suppressed by these agents. Current evidence remains insufficient to support the routine use of anti-resorptive agents in CN beyond standard offloading. However, a promising finding was the significant reduction in bone resorption markers following anti-resorptive intervention. Bone remodeling is a coupled process involving both resorption and formation. Inhibiting osteoclast induction may therefore impede subsequent osteoblast recruitment [37]. In osteoporosis treatment, anti-resorptive agents fail to increase BMD in patients with very low baseline BMD [38] that partially explains the absence of BMD improvement in our analysis. These results suggest that future studies should examine the coupled dynamics of osteoclast and osteoblast activity in CN.

This review has several limitations. First, the number of included studies was relatively insufficient to generate robust clinical evidence. A key reason is that CN is relatively rare compared with osteoporosis, which limiting the availability of primary studies, particularlyRCTs. Second, a high risk of bias was present across the included studies. According to RoB 2 assessments, most studies did not adequately address missing outcome data and did not report details of random assignment. Due to the limited number of included studies, we did not perform a funnel plot to assess publication bias, as advised in the Cochrane Handbook [39], though descriptive analysis suggests the possibility of publication bias. Third, substantial heterogeneity was present among the included studies, arising from differences in baseline characteristics, types of anti-resorptive agents used, and bone measurement methods. These limitations inevitably affect the robustness of the pooled conclusions. Future studies should aim to minimize bias and heterogeneity through rigorous design, transparent reporting, and standardized outcome assessment.

The current review was still have some limatations. First, the amont included studies were too few to settle the strong evidence for clinical. One of the impotant reason was that comparing the osteoporosis, CN has an raletively rare prevalence. Consequently, few primary studies, particularly RCTs have evaluated anti-osteoporotic agents in CN. Second, high risk of bias were existed in the current review. In results of the RoB 2 evaluation, most of the included studies failed to handel the missing data appropriately and lacked the reporting of the random assignment. Due to the limite inclusion numbers of studies, we did not perform the funnel plot to evaluated publication bias to avoid the misleading results as adviced in Corcrane handbook [39], however, according to describly analysis, we could assume the existance of the published bias. Thirdly, obvious heterogeneitys were existed in the including studies, the sources of these heterogeneity were from the population baseline, intervention agents types, and measurement methods. The above limitation inevitably impacts the robustness of pooled conclusions in the current review. The designing of future studies should avoid the exist bias and heterogeneitys.

In conclusion, although this meta-analysis, based on a limited number of studies, provides evidence that anti-resorptive agents reduce bone resorption markers, evidence for effects on BMD and clinical outcomes remains insufficient to support their use beyond standard offloading in CN. Mechanistic studies in CN are urgently needed, and further high-level clinical trials are required to define the role of anti-resorptive agents in CN management.
