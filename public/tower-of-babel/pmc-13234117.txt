Advantages of magnetic compression in rat colonic anastomosis: a comparative study with a transcriptomic perspective
Authors: Shuqin Xu, Long Liu, Linbiao Xiang, Jiaru Xu, Yuning Shao, Chentao Li, Yuebing Han, Yaoxing Ren, Miaomiao Zhang, Nan Li, Juanjuan Wang, Xiaopeng Yan, Yi Lyu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal anastomosis is a common procedure in colorectal cancer surgery, but traditional suturing is technically demanding and prone to complications. Magnetic compression anastomosis (MCA) offers a simpler, minimally invasive alternative with favorable outcomes. This study compares the healing effects of MCA and conventional suturing in rat colonic anastomosis and investigates the molecular mechanisms involved. Twenty male Sprague–Dawley rats (200–230 g) were randomly divided into an MCA group and a suture (control) group. The survival rates, and histological and transcriptomic profiles of the anastomotic sites were compared. The survival rate of the MCA group (100%) was significantly higher than that of the Control group (70%). Histological examination showed improved tissue alignment and more continuous mucosal surfaces in the MCA group. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that differentially expressed genes between the MCA and Control groups were highly enriched in immune-inflammatory pathways. PPI network analysis identified IL-1β as a key molecule, with significantly elevated expression in the MCA group. MCA is a simple, low-complication method for colonic anastomosis in rats. Transcriptomic analysis suggests that IL-1β is identified as a key candidate molecule associated with the enhanced anastomotic healing observed in MCA.Keywords: IL-1β, Immune-inflammatory response, Gene expression profiling, Intestinal healingSubject terms: Cancer, Gastroenterology, Oncology

Introduction

Colorectal anastomosis is a critical surgical technique widely applied in the treatment of colorectal cancer, offering significant clinical value as one of the primary approaches for gastrointestinal reconstruction. Utilizing rats as an animal model for colorectal anastomosis has become a widely adopted method in experimental research1–4. Among these studies, conventional manual suture anastomosis remains the most frequently used technique. In recent years, various new methods have been developed to address the challenges associated with colorectal anastomosis5,6. However, due to the thin rectal wall and narrow lumen in rats, each method faces technical limitations. The effectiveness of traditional suturing largely depends on the surgeon’s clinical skill, the procedure is time-intensive, and there is a high risk of complications, such as anastomotic leakage and luminal stricture1,2.

Our team has been exploring the potential of magnetic compression anastomosis (MCA)7 as an alternative approach for gastrointestinal anastomosis.. While still largely in the experimental and early clinical stages, we have investigated this technique in selected cases, such as esophageal atresia8, vascular anastomosis9, as well as rectal and colonic strictures10,11. Preliminary findings suggest this technique may offer potential advantages of simplicity and minimal invasiveness.

Transcriptomics offers a comprehensive view of gene expression according to genomic RNA transcripts under specific conditions12. From the perspective of molecular biology, transcriptomics enables researchers to understand the dynamic changes of gene regulation, identify differentially expressed genes, and investigate complex biological processes such as disease progression and cellular responses to external stimuli. Compared to traditional methods focused on individual gene or specific pathway, transcriptomics allows for evaluating thousands of genes, providing a holistic perspective on the molecular mechanisms driving biological functions13. With the development of high-throughput sequencing technologies, transcriptomics is gradually becoming an indispensable tool in elucidating the molecular basis of various physiological and pathological processes.

Our previous research has demonstrated that, compared to suture-based colorectal anastomosis14, magnetic compression anastomosis results in a visibly smoother and more even anastomotic site. HE and Masson staining show a continuous mucosal layer, less collagen in the muscular layer, and reduced granuloma formation. However, the reasons behind these differences warrant further investigation. Therefore, we performed rectal anastomosis and compared the anastomotic sites of the magnetic compression and Control groups. Through macroscopic examination, histopathology, transcriptomics, and immunohistochemistry, we comprehensively analyzed and explained the underlying reasons for the superior outcomes observed in the magnetic compression group.

Methods

Ethical statement

This protocol was approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University (Approval No.: XJTUAE20-2419). The study was conducted in strict accordance with the guidelines for the management and use of experimental animals at Xi’an Jiaotong University Medical Center. All animal experiments complied with the ARRIVE guidelines and were carried out in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Animals

Twenty male Sprague–Dawley rats, weighing 200–230 g (8–10 weeks old), were obtained from the Animal Experiment Center of Xi’an Jiaotong University School of Medicine.

Magnetic rings

The device used in this experiment included a pair of magnetic rings made of sintered neodymium-iron-boron (NdFeB, N45) material with a nickel coating to enhance corrosion resistance. The rings have an outer diameter of 6 mm (Fig. 1A), an inner diameter of 3 mm, and a wall thickness of 3 mm (Fig. 1B) (Northwest Institute for Nonferrous Metal Research, Xi’an, China). Each ring weighs 0.22 g.

Device specifications for magnetic compression anastomosis. (A) The diameter of the magnetic compression anastomosis (MCA) ring. (B) The thickness of the MCA ring.

Study design

The 20 rats were randomly divided into the MCA group and the Control group. Rats in the Control group underwent traditional manual interrupted colonic anastomosis using 6–0 absorbable sutures (Jin Huan, Shanghai, China). The rats in the MCA group received colonic anastomosis using magnetic compression.

Surgical procedure

The rats were fasted for one day and weighed, then anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital (1.5 ml/kg). Upon confirmation of successful anesthesia, the animals were positioned supine on the operating table, and hair on the lower abdomen was shaved. Sterile surgical instruments were used throughout the procedure, adhering strictly to aseptic surgical protocols. To access the abdominal cavity, a midline incision approximately 3 cm in length was made in the lower abdomen to expose the proximal and distal colon. The proximal and distal segments of the colon were transected, followed by end-to-end anastomosis. To minimize technical bias, all surgical procedures for both groups were performed by the same senior surgeon with over 10 years of experience in animal microsurgery. In the control group, a standardized, full-thickness, interrupted manual suturing technique was strictly applied to perform the end-to-end anastomosis using 6–0 absorbable sutures (Jin Huan, Shanghai, China). Approximately 8 to 10 symmetric stitches were placed for each anastomosis, maintaining a uniform bite size of 1.5–2.0 mm from the wound edge and a spacing of 1.5–2.0 mm between adjacent stitches. Tension was carefully controlled during knot tying to ensure adequate tissue approximation without causing local ischemia. For the MCA group, as in our previous studies, an 8F gastric tube was inserted through the anus to the proximal colon, with a magnetic ring threaded onto the tube and positioned in the proximal colon. The proximal and distal colon stumps were gently tied around the tube using 4-0 non-absorbable sutures (Aipu, Hangzhou, China). A second magnetic ring was inserted via the anus to the distal colon stump. The colonic ends were compressed together between the magnets, and the tube was then removed (Fig. 2A). After anastomosis, the abdominal incision was closed in layers with 4-0 non-absorbable sutures.

Postoperative care

After recovery from anesthesia, rats were returned to individual cages and monitored until full awakening. During the first three postoperative days, animals were provided with liquid diet and free access to water, after which they resumed normal chow. Rats were observed twice daily for general activity, appetite, grooming, stool passage, and signs of discomfort. The surgical incision was checked daily for redness, swelling, or dehiscence.

To minimize gastrointestinal burden and ensure normal transit, daily abdominal X-ray imaging was performed to confirm the position and progression of the magnetic rings through the gastrointestinal tract until natural expulsion. No additional interventions were required, and all animals received supportive care as needed.

Postoperative monitoring, fluid resuscitation, pain management, and general supportive care were strictly identical for both groups to eliminate confounding factors. For any animals that died prematurely, prompt necropsies were performed. Objective criteria for diagnosing complications included: (1) Anastomotic leakage: visible gross dehiscence at the anastomotic suture line, the presence of uncontained fecal matter in the abdominal cavity, or severe generalized purulent peritonitis; (2) Anastomotic stricture: visible fibrotic narrowing of the anastomotic ring leading to proximal bowel dilation (> 1.5 times the normal diameter) accompanied by severe fecal impaction.

Pain management and humane endpoints

To reduce postoperative discomfort, all rats received meloxicam (1 mg/kg, subcutaneously) immediately after the procedure and once daily for two consecutive days. Animals were monitored closely for signs of pain or distress, including piloerection, hunched posture, decreased mobility, abnormal respiration, and reduced food intake. Humane endpoints were predefined according to institutional guidelines. Animals were euthanized prematurely if any of the following occurred: (1) Body weight loss exceeding 20% of baseline, (2) Failure to eat or drink for > 24 h, (3) Severe lethargy, inability to ambulate, or recumbency. (4) Marked abdominal distension or signs of peritonitis, (5) Respiratory distress or persistent bleeding, (6) Self-mutilation or unrelieved pain despite analgesia. All rats were followed for 14 days after surgery unless meeting humane endpoints. No animals reached humane endpoints in the MCA group.

Euthanasia

At the designated experimental endpoint (14 days after surgery), rats were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg), consistent with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020). Death was confirmed by the absence of heartbeat and respiratory movement, followed by cervical dislocation to ensure complete euthanasia. The colon, including the anastomotic site, was then harvested for subsequent analysis.

Burst pressure measurement

Two weeks post-surgery, the rats were euthanized, and an 8 cm segment of colon, including the anastomotic site and adjacent bowel, was excised. One end of the anastomotic specimen was clamped with a hemostat, while a catheter was inserted into the other end and secured with monofilament sutures. The entire anastomotic segment was immersed in 0.9% saline, and intraluminal pressure was gradually increased. The pressure at which the first air bubble appeared was recorded as the burst pressure.

Histological analysis

The sutured or magnetically compressed colorectal anastomotic site was excised and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h. The colonic segment was then embedded in paraffin, and 4 μm-thick sections were cut at the anastomotic site. The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and Masson’s trichrome stain and examined for histological evaluation under a light microscope.

IL-1β immunohistochemical staining analysis

The sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated through a series of xylene and graded alcohols. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) for 10 min at room temperature. Next, the sections were subjected to antigen retrieval by immersion in a sodium citrate buffer (10 mmol/L, pH 6.0), followed by heat treatment for 3 min under saturated conditions. The sections were then blocked with normal goat serum for 30 min at room temperature to reduce non-specific binding. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against IL-1β (Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China). The following day, the sections were incubated with secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) at 37 °C for 1 h, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Afterward, the sections were developed using diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen. The sections were then counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted with a coverslip. All the sections were independently observed by 3 pathologists under a Leica light microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Image analysis was performed using Image J 2.9.0 software to measure the percentage of positive cells in three high-power fields for comparison.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 17.0 statistical software, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Specimens collection and preservation

A total of six samples were obtained from rat model, including three Control group and three MCA group. The Control group was rectum anastomosis end to end using absorbable surgical sutures (6–0), while the MCA group was treated by two magnetic rings. After two weeks tissue anastomosis were resected and specimens were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Then, these samples were stored at − 80 °C, RNA extraction, and High-throughput RNA sequencing. Except for anastomotic methods, all technique employed and feeding were consistent with established surgical protocols.

High-throughput RNA sequencing

The High-throughput RNA sequencing was conducted by Annoroad Gene Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). The total RNAs were isolated from six anastomotic tissue by TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The RNA quality was tested using NanoDrop One (NanoDrop, Wilmington, DE, USA). A total of 2 μg RNA per sample was employed as input material for the preparations. According to manufacturer’s recommendations, sequencing libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA). The libraries were sequenced on DNBSEQ-T7 platform. The RNA sequencing raw count data were converted to transcripts per million format and further log-2 transformed.

Data processing and bioinformatic analysis

The high-throughput RNA sequencing data was processed into Log2 (TPM) to enhance data comparability and results reliability. According to Log2 (TPM) expression value, distribution and density were exhibited across Control group and MCA group. The Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was usually used to reduce the dimensionality of the dataset and identify the principal components. Spearman’s correlation analysis was utilized to evaluate the monotonic relationships between two sets of data. Based on gene expression, the internal links were assessed between Control group and MCA group using correlation analysis. The limma R package was performed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) following the criterion: P-value < 0.05 and Log2 (Fold change) > 2.

Biological function enrichment

To decipher potential biological functions between Control group and MCA group, the clusterProfiler R package was used to perform Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses. A false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant. For further exploring the specific biological characteristics of two groups, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) algorithm was conducted, which presented biological pathway activities using gene rank information. DEGs identified with the limma package were ranked in accordance with descending Log2 (Fold change) value. GO and KEGG gene sets were generated from the MSigDB dataset. The P value was corrected by the Benjamin-Hochberg approach and the adjusted P-value (FDR) < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Immune infiltration analysis

The immune infiltration analysis was performed based on ssGSEA of R package GSVA (https://www.bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/GSVA.html). The genesets of each immune cell type was obtained from published resources15 and homologous gene conversion from human to rat was performed based on the R package homologene (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/homologene/index.html).

PPI network construction and key molecule screening

The STRING database serves as a search tool for assessing interacting genes/proteins, including all known and predicted proteins (https://cn.string-db.org/). PPI network was constructed from identified DEGs according to STRING interactome. The medium confidence scores were set to over 0.4 and PPI network interactions file was downloaded. Cystoscope software was used to explore and visualize network biology analysis. Molecular complex detection (MCODE), a plugin in Cytoscape, is usually employed to identify the finest clusters and explore important hub genes.

Results

Magnet expulsion time

The average expulsion time for the magnets was 4 ± 1.05 days. Careful examination of the expelled magnets (Fig. 2D–E) revealed partial corrosion and detachment of the nickel coating on the magnet’s surface due to exposure to the digestive tract, while the core structure of the magnet remained intact.

Survival rate and postoperative complications

The survival rate in the MCA group was 100%, significantly higher than the 70% survival rate in the Control group. No postoperative complications were observed in the MCA group. In the Control group, three rats died: one from anastomotic stricture and obstruction, and two from anastomotic leakage.

Burst pressure

Two weeks post-surgery, the average burst pressure in the MCA group was 174.1 mmHg, compared to 160.9 mmHg in the Control group (Fig. 2F), indicating that the anastomosis in the MCA group was more robust and durable.

Macroscopic appearance of anastomosis

Two weeks post-surgery, the macroscopic appearance of the anastomotic sites differed between the MCA group and the Control group (Fig. 3). In comparison, Fig. 3A,C show that the MCA anastomosis had minimal scarring, and the anastomotic site exhibited no significant protrusion into the lumen. In contrast, the traditional suture anastomosis showed notable outward protrusion of the lumen. From the intraluminal perspective (Fig. 3B,D), the MCA anastomotic site appeared relatively smooth and even, with a more continuous mucosal surface. In the Control group, remnants of suture knots from the hand-sewn anastomosis were visible within the mucosa, along with a more pronounced depression at the anastomotic site.

Histological appearance of the anastomosis

Two weeks post-surgery, as shown in Fig. 3E–H, histological examination of the MCA anastomotic site revealed continuous mucosal coverage, with well-restored epithelial migration and a relatively smooth surface. In contrast, the Control group exhibited incomplete mucosal and submucosal layers, with thicker scar tissue and increased collagen fiber proliferation.

The connection and difference of distinct intervening measure

Based on gross tissue and pathological tissue, the anastomosis effect of magnetic press is better than that of traditional suture. To further explore biological mechanism, RNA sequencing and transcriptomics were performed. The comparability and reliability of data were promoted by standardized processing. The landscape of gene expression was exhibited in Fig. 4A,B. PCA analysis suggested two separate groups were classified according to distinct intervening measure, which further confirmed the difference was existed at molecular level (Fig. 4C). The correlation analysis proved the links were close with intra-group while the relationships were weak with inter-group (Fig. 4D). The difference of gene expression usually maps onto biological characteristics, generating distinct clinical outcome.

The identification of key biological pathway

Using the limma R package, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between the magnetic anastomosis and Control groups (Fig. 5A). A total of 209 DEGs were detected, including 124 upregulated and 85 downregulated genes (Fig. 5B,C). Functional enrichment analysis was performed based on these DEGs to explore potential alterations in biological processes. KEGG analysis indicated that immune-inflammatory pathways were significantly enriched in the MCA group, such as the IL-7 signaling pathway, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and TNF signaling pathway (Fig. 5D). GO analysis further confirmed enhanced activation of immune cell recruitment, including granulocyte migration, myeloid leukocyte migration, and neutrophil migration (Fig. 5E). Additionally, the volcano plot revealed high expression levels of matrix metalloproteinases (MMP3, MMP8, MMP13, and MMP9), which likely play a critical role in tissue remodeling, wound healing, and maintaining ECM integrity. Overall, the enhanced immunoinflammatory activity, chemotaxis of inflammatory cells, and associated factors may be key drivers of differences in anastomotic healing.

Correlative analysis of the immunoinflammatory response in anastomotic healing

To verify the importance of immune inflammation in anastomotic healing, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed between the Control group and the magnetic anastomosis (MCA) group. The GSEA results indicated that the chemotaxis of inflammatory cells and factors played a crucial role in the anastomotic healing process (Figs. 6A,B). Focusing on immune inflammation, immune cell enrichment scoring revealed that the MCA group showed elevated scores across 28 immune cell types to varying degrees, suggesting a close relationship between MCA stimulation, immune cell activation, and gastrointestinal regeneration (Fig. 6C). Molecules are known to play a critical role in regulating biological processes. PPI network analysis further identified key inflammatory hub genes, including IL-1β, CXCL1, and CXCL2 (Fig. 6D). To determine the primary candidate for further validation, we evaluated these hub genes based on quantitative criteria. IL-1β was selected because it exhibited the highest degree of connectivity (centrality) within the PPI network and demonstrated one of the most statistically significant P-values in the differential expression analysis (Fig. 5A). Therefore, based on our correlative analysis, IL-1β was identified as a primary candidate molecule associated with improved anastomotic healing. Compared to the Control group, the expression levels of these inflammatory molecules were higher in the MCA group (Fig. 6E). Additionally, immunohistochemical staining for IL-1β was performed on anastomotic tissue. As shown in Fig. 6F, the MCA group exhibited a larger positive area for IL-1β, with statistically significant differences. Thus, immune-inflammatory activity is closely associated with anastomotic healing, and key inflammatory molecules like IL-1β may participate in regulating this process as candidate mediators.

Discussion

Magnetic compression anastomosis (MCA) is an emerging, experimental surgical technique within the field of magnetic surgery. Although it has shown promise in various animal models and selected clinical case reports, it is important to acknowledge that MCA has not yet been widely adopted in routine clinical practice, nor is it included in standard guidelines for colorectal surgery. Currently, it remains primarily an exploratory approach. Hand-sewn suturing remains a classic and widely applied method in colorectal anastomosis. This study compares the healing effects of magnetic compression and traditional suture techniques in rat colonic anastomosis. Results demonstrate that the MCA group exhibited higher survival rates, fewer postoperative complications, and greater bursting pressure tolerance at the anastomotic site. Macroscopically, the MCA anastomoses displayed smoother mucosal surfaces and shallower depressions at the anastomotic sites. Histologically, MCA anastomoses showed a more complete mucosal migration, continuous submucosal layers, and reduced inflammatory cell infiltration. Given the histological and clinical advantages observed in the MCA group, we conducted high-throughput transcriptome sequencing on anastomotic tissues, performing quality control, differential gene analysis, and pathway enrichment of differentially expressed genes. We found that immune-inflammatory pathways were highly enriched in the MCA group at the transcriptome level. Finally, PPI network analysis identified IL-1β as a key molecule, suggesting its significant role in accelerating anastomotic healing.

In our previous studies, we demonstrated numerous advantages of magnetic compression anastomosis (MCA) in gastrointestinal surgeries compared to traditional suturing techniques. Specifically, we confirmed the superior outcomes of MCA over sutures in esophageal16, gastro-intestinal17, and end-to-end small intestinal anastomoses18. Both our prior research and current study indicate that MCA results in enhanced anastomotic healing, characterized by more complete mucosal migration and continuity in the submucosal, muscular, and serosal layers. We preliminarily attribute these benefits to the unique mechanism of MCA: tissues at the anastomotic site experience evenly distributed compression forces, unlike hand-sewn anastomosis, where each suture point subjects the tissue to varying degrees of longitudinal traction, potentially impairing the anastomotic integrity. However, recent studies have increasingly focused on the impact of magnetic fields on wound healing and tissue regeneration19–22, suggesting that MCA’s superior healing outcomes may involve more complex mechanisms beyond mechanical compression. To explore these underlying mechanisms, we conducted high-throughput RNA sequencing on tissues from the anastomotic sites, aiming to uncover the molecular pathways that may contribute to MCA’s accelerated healing effects.

Transcriptomic analysis revealed differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with both upregulated and downregulated expressions in the MCA group compared to the control. Functional enrichment analysis showed that these DEGs are primarily associated with immune-inflammatory signaling pathways, including the IL-17, NF-kappa B, and TNF pathways. In addition, we identified pathways significantly involved in tissue regeneration and vascular remodeling, such as the Jak-STAT pathway, which supports tissue regeneration23, and the fluid shear stress and atherosclerosis pathway, which promotes vascular regeneration24. Given the prominent enrichment of immune-inflammatory pathways, we assessed immune cell infiltration and found elevated immune cell activity in the MCA group. Notably, the activation of macrophages is well-documented to accelerate wound healing and tissue regeneration25, suggesting that enhanced immune responses may contribute to the improved healing observed with MCA.

Subsequently, we performed a PPI functional enrichment analysis, which highlighted IL-1β as a candidate molecule. Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed higher IL-1β expression in the MCA group. IL-1β is known for its roles in immune modulation and inflammation promotion, primarily secreted by macrophages to activate and proliferate T and B cells, thereby enhancing immune responses26. It activates immune cells such as macrophages and neutrophils, increasing their phagocytic and bactericidal abilities. Consequently, we hypothesize that the elevated IL-1β expression observed in the MCA group is strongly correlated with the enhanced anastomotic healing. It should be noted that our current transcriptomic and immunohistochemical data primarily demonstrate an association rather than direct causality. This effect is particularly relevant in the colorectal region, the most pathogen-dense section of the gastrointestinal tract, where a heightened immune-inflammatory response at the anastomotic site could facilitate the clearance of pathogens and debris, accelerating wound healing. Moreover, IL-1β may promote wound healing and tissue regeneration through additional mechanisms. Given the complex microbiota within the gastrointestinal tract, Wang et al. previously reported that bacterial involvement can induce tissue repair through IL-1β27, suggesting that the gut microbiota might also participate in digestive tract healing. Additionally, Yosuke et al. demonstrated that IL-1β directly activates the PI3K/AKT and MEK/ERK pathways, promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) induction28. These findings underscore IL-1β’s potential positive role in enhancing digestive tract healing in our study.

Moreover, the direct effects of the magnetic field should not be overlooked. Numerous studies have reported that magnetic fields promote tissue repair and wound healing19–22,29. For instance, Zhang Xin29 demonstrated that magnetic fields improved alcoholic liver disease by regulating oxidative stress and cellular proliferation in hepatocytes. Consistently, our study also found activation of oxidative stress and proliferation pathways in the magnetic field group. Additionally, Laranjeira et al.30 confirmed that magnetic fields promote bone tissue regeneration through angiogenesis, indicating considerable potential for application in bone tissue engineering, which aligns with our findings.

Therefore, the favorable outcomes of magnetic compression anastomosis (MCA) techniques are not solely due to the uniform distribution of pressure; they are likely driven by a complex interplay of underlying mechanisms. Understanding these physiological mechanisms not only enhances the effective application of MCA but also supports risk mitigation and further refinement of magnetic surgical approaches. Additionally, MCA holds promise not only for routine gastrointestinal reconstruction but also for challenging cases, such as managing postoperative colorectal anastomotic strictures. By applying MCA to re-compress stenotic segments, this technique may offer a unique advantage in digestive tract reconstruction, providing superior anastomotic healing and a less technically demanding procedure. However, extensive, well-designed clinical trials are still required to establish its long-term safety, efficacy, and standardize the procedure before it can be considered for broader clinical application.

Despite the promising findings, this study has several limitations. First, our transcriptomic analysis identified IL-1β as a key molecule associated with MCA based on correlative data; however, functional validation experiments (e.g., gene knockout or neutralization) are lacking, meaning causality cannot be definitively established at this stage. Second, the sample size for RNA-sequencing was relatively small (n = 3 per group). Third, the analyses were conducted at a single postoperative time point, which may not capture the full dynamic process of healing. Finally, as an animal model study, these findings require further validation in clinical human samples to confirm their translational relevance.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that MCA is a simple, feasible, and low-complication method for colonic anastomosis in rats, offering superior postoperative outcomes compared to traditional hand-sewn sutures. Transcriptomic analysis identifies IL-1β as a key candidate molecule associated with the improved anastomotic healing in MCA, though further functional studies are needed to establish definitive causality.
