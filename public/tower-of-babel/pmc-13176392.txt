Ontogeny of Placental FcRn Protein During Human and Animal Pregnancy to Inform Developmental Toxicity Testing
Authors: Christopher J Bowman, William J Breslin, Connie Chen, Warren Foster, Julia Hui, Beata Kusmider, Susan Laffan, Matt Myers, Lakshmi Sivaraman, Belen Tornesi, Susan Westmoreland, John M DeSesso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionSpecies differences exist in Fc domain‐containing molecule transfer/biodistribution to the embryo‐fetus during pregnancy. Placentation was reviewed and placental neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) quantitation in the chorioallantoic placenta (CAP) and/or inverted yolk sac placenta (InvYSP) was investigated across species throughout gestation to further understand and inform developmental toxicity testing of Fc domain‐containing molecules.MethodsQuantitative mass spectrometry was used to quantify FcRn protein in CAP and/or InvYSP throughout gestation in mouse, rat, guinea pig, rabbit, and nonhuman primate (NHP). In human placental tissues, immunohistochemistry was used to localize FcRn distribution during each trimester.ResultsFcRn protein was detected and concentration increased throughout gestation in mouse, rat, guinea pig, and rabbit; with InvYSP concentration markedly higher compared with CAP at all gestational ages. In contrast, NHP FcRn protein concentration in the CAP remained constant throughout gestation. In humans, during 1st trimester, FcRn occurred in decidua, maternal endothelium, and maternal and fetal macrophages. During 2nd trimester, fetal FcRn increased in syncytiotrophoblasts and fetal chorionic vessels. FcRn expression decreased in maternal endothelium near term.ConclusionsFcRn protein was detected throughout gestation (including organogenesis) in placental tissue of all animal species. Regardless of FcRn location, these data support that Fc domain‐containing molecules can access the embryo‐fetus throughout gestation in all species, with the extent of transfer likely depending on FcRn concentration and size of the placenta. This information on placental FcRn ontogeny will help improve study design and translation of nonclinical developmental toxicity data to human safety assessment of Fc domain‐containing molecules.Keywords: FcRn, ontogeny, placenta, placental transfer

Introduction

Large biological molecules including an Fc domain, such as monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) and immunoglobulin G (IgG)‐like molecules, are being developed at a rapid pace for diseases that occur in both sexes and span all life stages, inclusive of the period of reproductive activity. The efficacy of many vaccines is dependent upon an immune response, which may involve placental transfer of maternal IgGs (Etti et al. 2022). Because offspring can benefit from such therapies or be at risk of exposure to Fc domain‐containing biopharmaceuticals indicated for females of reproductive potential, it is important to understand the likelihood and extent of placental transfer of such molecules. Consequently, transport mechanisms must be investigated to ascertain whether, to what extent, and when during gestation maternal IgGs and Fc domain‐containing biopharmaceuticals (henceforth referred to as Fc‐containing molecules) traverse the placenta.

The present work first reviews placental development, structure, and modes of transfer of Fc‐containing molecules. Additional details of the comparative anatomy and development of placentae among species used in safety assessments have been described elsewhere (DeSesso 2012; DeSesso et al. 2012; Kaufmann and Burton 1994; Ramsey 1982). Therefore, the present work will focus on similarities and differences in the timing, location, and mechanisms whereby Fc‐containing molecules are transported from the maternal system to the offspring. Then, new information will be shared on FcRn protein concentration in the inverted yolk sac placenta (InvYSP) and chorioallantoic placenta (CAP) of test species used in developmental toxicity testing and humans. All this information will facilitate a better understanding of how these different test species can be used in developmental toxicity testing to inform human risk assessment.

The Health and Environmental Sciences Institute Developmental and Reproductive Toxicology (HESI DART) Technical Committee undertook studies to fill gaps in the understanding of the placental transfer of IgGs and Fc‐containing biopharmaceuticals through collating information from the available scientific literature and research. The Committee identified several key gaps:

Defining developmental age of onset for placental FcRn expression. In particular, it was paramount to correlate the gestational milestones (placental maturation and function vs. embryo‐fetal development milestones) occurring in concert with the onset of FcRn expression for each species. Such comparisons are important in considering the relevance to humans of findings in animal species used in developmental toxicity testing.

The Committee recognized that a project to accomplish a detailed, cross‐species survey of FcRn expression during development is not a trivial activity. Consequently, a workgroup of the HESI DART Committee was formed with the primary objectives of (1) establishing the state of the science at the outset of the project; (2) determining the ontogeny of FcRn concentration in CAP and InvYSP throughout gestation across species and (3) establishing the location of FcRn within placental and yolk sac tissues during gestation.

Please also refer to the symposium titled “Cross‐species FcRn Ontogeny and Impact on Placental Transfer of Monoclonal Antibodies and Other Fc‐containing Biotherapeutics” hosted at the 56th Annual Meeting of the Teratology Society in June 2016 (Birth Defects Research (A) 106: 373–374).

Early Development, Fetal Membranes, and Formation of Placenta

Ovulation separates the meiotic ovum from the readily available nutrient supplies of the maternal ovary. Surrounded by a modest cloud of supporting cells, the ovum is released into a space that is technically or physically outside the female's body. If fertilization occurs, meiosis is completed, and a newly formed diploid organism (the zygote) initiates a burst of metabolic and cellular activities characterized by a series of rapid mitotic cellular divisions. The survival of the zygote depends on an efficient supply of oxygen and nutrients and a means for removal of metabolic wastes. In the case of viviparous animals (animals that give birth to live young), this survival challenge is met by the establishment of a temporary organ, the placenta, which serves as an interface between the mother and offspring for the exchange of nutrients, gases, metabolic waste, and xenobiotics during gestation.

For the mammalian species considered in this paper, fertilization occurs in the distal uterine tube and the zygote undergoes mitotic cleavage divisions to form a mass of cells that navigates the uterine tube and enters the lumen of the uterus. With continued development, this mass of cells rearranges into a sphere with a small inner cell mass (the embryo proper) and an outer rim of cells (the trophoblast) that adheres to and then invades the wall of the uterus. Tissues associated with the trophoblast of the conceptus differentiate into fetal membranes that become intimately apposed to the uterine endometrium to form a placenta.

The placenta and fetal membranes are transient organs that form early in gestation and exist only during in utero life. They serve as organs of nutrition and respiration, provide a mechanism to eliminate metabolic waste, protect the embryo/fetus, and assist in establishment of embryonic vascularity. The four mammalian fetal membranes include the chorion, yolk sac, amnion, and allantois. The chorion is composed of trophoblast plus underlying mesenchyme. The yolk sac is a ventral endodermal protrusion of the midgut, surrounded by mesenchyme. The amnion, which surrounds the embryo, is derived from the epiblast and is continuous with the ectoderm of the skin at the umbilicus. The allantois is an evagination of hindgut endoderm that is associated with development of the cloaca/urinary bladder and is important in establishing the embryonic vasculature of the placenta.

The maternal portion of the placenta is typically the wall of the uterus (endometrium) and the fetal portion consists of one or more of the fetal membranes. The types of placentae can be classified according to the fetal membranes that contribute to apposition of fetal‐to‐maternal tissues and to those involved in vascularizing the placenta. In general, the mammals typically used in developmental toxicity assessments have a definitive chorioallantoic placenta (CAP), which is the same type of placenta present in humans. Additionally, all species considered herein have hemochorial placentae, which means that maternal blood directly contacts the chorionic epithelium. The chorionic epithelium of the species considered herein possesses the same specialized structures, cells, and processes (bidirectional transcytosis and endosomal protection) as nonhuman primate (NHP) and humans. One difference is that NHP and human placentae exhibit villi to increase surface area for physiologic exchange, whereas rodents and rabbits have labyrinthine placentae. The labyrinthine arrangement results in a more efficient exchange of molecules than a villous placenta.

Inverted Yolk Sac Placenta

The sequence of gestational milestones among mammals is consistent across species (Figure 1), although the timing of the events differs dramatically (Table 1). Efficient transfer of materials to and from the embryo by means of the placenta requires the establishment of a functioning embryonic cardiovascular system, which takes time. In short‐gestation species (like rodents) much of organogenesis is completed before the embryonic cardiovascular system and CAP are fully functioning, beginning at approximately the 20‐somite stage (Table 1; yellow arrow in Figure 1). However, the brevity of the gestational periods of mouse, rats, and rabbits (19–32 days) requires that the embryos of these species develop quickly. Rapid development demands the flow of nutrients from the maternal system. To fulfill the needs of their swiftly growing embryos, these species develop an early placenta that takes advantage of their large yolk sacs (Jollie 1990). This early placenta, which is not present in animals with longer gestation periods including NHP and humans, is called an “inverted” yolk sac placenta (InvYSP) because portions of the parietal yolk sac membranes attenuate and become discontinuous at the plane of apposition to the uterine wall, leaving the epithelium of the visceral yolk sac membrane in virtual contact with the uterine lumen and epithelium (Figure 2). The InvYSP transports nutrients to the embryo by a “histotrophic” process (Figure 3). This entails endocytosis of maternally derived macromolecules in the uterine secretions by the visceral yolk sac endoderm and the subsequent enzymatic lysis of those macromolecules within lysosomal vacuoles, followed by diffusion into the embryo. Once the CAP becomes functional, the nutritive importance of the rodent InvYSP is greatly reduced. Nevertheless, the InvYSP remains functional throughout gestation in rodents (Beck and Lloyd 1977; Ornoy and Miller 2023) and plays an important role in the establishment of passive immunity as described below.

Development of the rodent inverted yolk sac placenta. In rodents, an early, interim placenta that is composed of the epithelium of the yolk sac abutted to the chorion develops during the middle of the second gestational week. This occurs when the yolk sac expands rapidly (A) to fill the chorionic cavity (exocoelom). This expansion causes the yolk sac epithelium to contact the chorion (B); subsequently, the lumen of the yolk sac “deflates”, such that the outer (parietal) yolk sac membrane remains in contact with the chorion and the visceral yolk sac membrane approaches the parietal yolk sac membrane (C). With continued development, both the chorion and the parietal yolk sac membrane degenerate, leaving the visceral yolk sac membrane in contact with the secretions from the uterine glands (C inset), which can be taken up into endocytic vacuoles. The vacuoles merge with lysozymes and their contents are digested by lytic enzymes. The digestant is then transferred to the embryo (see Figure 3 for further description). The allantois joins to the chorion 24–72 h later, establishing the chorioallantoic placenta (D) (DeSesso et al. 2012) with permission.

This diagram depicts the steps involved during uptake of material from the uterine secretions and transfer to the rodent embryo by means of the inverted yolk sac placenta. Maternal tissues are at the bottom of the diagram; embryonic tissues are in the upper region. The discontinuous tissue interposed between maternal and embryonic tissues is the area where the parietal yolk sac endoderm and the chorion have degenerated, allowing access of the visceral yolk sac endoderm to the secretions of the uterine glands. It is this organization (with the visceral yolk sac abutting the uterine lumen) that leads to the descriptive term of “inversion”. (2) The membrane‐bound vacuoles move to a supranuclear position, where they are approached by lysosomes. (3) Lysosomes merge with the membrane‐bound vacuoles to form secondary lysosomes. The contents of the secondary lysosomes undergo enzymatic degradation. (4) The digested material diffuses from the secondary lysosomes to the inside of the endodermal cell (below the tight junctions), where it exits to the extracellular space and diffuses into embryonic capillaries of the vitelline vasculature. Mechanisms of Transfer

A diverse suite of mechanisms exists to transport molecules across the placenta, including passive diffusion and carrier‐mediated mechanisms (Carney et al. 2004; Miller et al. 1976; van der Aa et al. 1998). Small molecules, including urea, oxygen, carbon dioxide, and many xenobiotics weighing less than 600 Da, cross the placenta by diffusion.

Most small molecule drugs and chemicals cross the placenta by passive diffusion. This leads to the rational assumption that the embryo/fetal blood concentration of the substance in question will be proportional to its concentration in maternal blood. In contrast, biopharmaceuticals (e.g., oligonucleotides and peptides) are orders of magnitude larger than small molecule drugs and their size impedes efficient passive transfer across the placenta. The immunoglobulins IgA, IgM, IgE, IgD, and other antibody fragments that lack the Fc domain are not (or are poorly) transported across the placenta (Gitlin et al. 1964; Miller et al. 2003; Wakefield et al. 2011). In contrast, IgG antibodies gain access to the developing offspring by special transfer mechanisms. IgG antibodies are naturally occurring proteins in healthy maternal animals that protect against many diseases in an infant's first vulnerable days‐to‐months of life. Fc‐containing molecules such as IgG antibodies cross the placenta in a molecule‐, species‐ and gestational stage‐specific manner using a receptor‐mediated mechanism. This transfer mechanism involves the recognition of a fragment crystallizable (Fc) domain of IgG by the FcRn receptor. Antibody‐like biopharmaceuticals (such as mAbs) also possess an Fc domain and can take advantage of this transfer mechanism. Consequently, developmental toxicity safety assessment of Fc‐containing biopharmaceuticals and vaccine‐specific induced maternal IgG may vary from small molecules because they may have a different biodistribution profile.

Fc‐containing biopharmaceuticals are the fastest growing class of new drugs in terms of approvals by regulatory agencies (Beck et al. 2010). Not surprisingly, there are numerous candidate biopharmaceuticals in various stages of drug development. Antibody‐like biopharmaceuticals (e.g., monoclonal antibodies, bi‐ and trispecific antibodies, antibody‐drug conjugates, Fc‐fusion proteins) have several desirable advantages over small molecule pharmaceuticals including high target specificity, low off‐target problems, and relatively long half‐lives. As noted above, however, safety testing of these biopharmaceuticals may require a case‐by‐case approach (ICH_S6(R1) 2011; Subramanyam et al. 2008). Biopharmaceuticals should be tested in a species wherein the biopharmaceutical is pharmacologically active (i.e., the pharmacological target must be present in the test species) and toxicologically relevant. In turn, this requirement often translates to challenges identifying a relevant nonclinical species used for toxicity testing as described below.

Carrier‐mediated mechanisms may (active transport) or may not (facilitated diffusion) require energy to cross the placenta. While small molecules can take advantage of the first three mechanisms and can be assumed to undergo transplacental transport during virtually all stages of gestation, transplacental transfer of Fc‐containing molecules is impacted by such factors as gestational age at the time of exposure and whether the receptor molecules are expressed on the absorptive surface.

Immunoglobulin Structure and State of Knowledge Concerning Placental Transfer

While immunoglobulins are important for mediating passive immunity, fetal synthesis of these molecules is negligible, and they must be obtained from the mother. Further, IgG is the predominant fetal immunoglobulin and the only one that is transferred across the placenta to any large extent (Malek 2003; Malek et al. 1994).

IgGs are large molecules, weighing approximately 150 kDa. They are “Y”‐shaped molecules comprising two identical heavy polypeptide chains and two identical light polypeptide chains. The stem of the “Y” (the Fc region) is a constant structure that binds to Fc receptors (FcR). The antibody binding portion of the molecule (the branches of the “Y”) has variable structures. The large size of IgG molecules impedes their transport across intact epithelia via diffusion; however, because the Fc region of IgG and other Fc‐containing molecules remain constant, they can undergo receptor mediated endocytosis via binding to the FcR, the most important of which during gestation is the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) (DeSesso et al. 2012; Pentsuk and van der Laan 2009; Roopenian and Sun 2010).

Neonatal Fc Receptor (FcRn)

FcRn binds all subclasses of IgG monomers and other Fc‐containing molecules with rather high affinity (Baker et al. 2009; Firan et al. 2001; Kristoffersen and Matre 1996; Roopenian and Akilesh 2007; Simister and Story 1997). Under steady state conditions in polarized cells, most FcRn accumulates in subapical vesicles (Baker et al. 2009; Simister et al. 1996; Story et al. 1994), where it participates in bi‐directional transcytosis. Upon completion of the transcytosis of a Fc‐containing molecule, FcRn can be reinternalized for reuse, indicating that FcRn operates as shuttles (Antohe et al. 2001; Praetor et al. 1999).

During pregnancy, FcRn is localized in the human term CAP within intracellular vesicles of syncytiotrophoblasts (Figure 5) (Antohe et al. 2001; Leach et al. 1996; Saji et al. 1999; Simister et al. 1996), but not on the surface of apical cell membranes (Kristoffersen and Matre 1996). FcRn binding to its ligand is pH‐dependent, such that binding occurs at pH ≤ 6.5, but not at pH ≥ 7.0–7.5 (Baker et al. 2009; Ghetie and Ward 1997; Simister et al. 1996; Story et al. 1994). This means that FcRn binds to its ligand (Fc containing molecule) only after the ligand has been internalized into an FcRn‐containing endosome that has become acidified.

Route for transcytosis of IgG molecules into primate embryos. In humans and nonhuman primates, IgG that is present in maternal blood crosses into the offspring by means of FcRn receptors found in the syncytiotrophoblast of the chorioallantoic placenta. (FcRn receptors are not present in the syncytiotrophoblast of rodents.) In humans and primates, maternal IgG is found floating in the plasma of maternal blood, which is in contact with the syncytiotrophoblast layer of the embryonic portion of the placenta. IgG molecules are imbibed in endocytic vacuoles of the syncytiotrophoblast (1 and 2). When the vacuoles coalesce with lysosomes, the released lysosomal enzymes acidify the fluid in the vacuole (3). The acid milieu promotes the binding of IgG to FcRn receptors co‐located in the vacuoles. The FcRn‐bound IgG molecules are protected from attack by the lytic enzymes (4). The vacuoles traverse the cell and merge with the basal cell membrane to disgorge their contents into the pH neutral environment of the embryonic tissue space. Under conditions of pH neutrality, the IgG is released from the FcRn receptor and diffuses into the capillaries of the embryonic portion of the placenta (5). CAP: chorioallantoic placenta (DeSesso et al. 2012) with permission.

Rodents, on the other hand, primarily express FcRn in the InvYSP endodermal cells rather than the CAP (Kim et al. 2009). Irrespective of the location of the FcRn receptors, the mechanism of transcytosis across the visceral endodermal membrane of the InvYSP is similar to that described above, being dependent on the pH of the endosome (Figure 6). Thus, in NHP and humans, gestational transfer of Fc‐containing molecules occurs primarily across the human CAP, whereas in rodents gestational transfer happens across the InvYSP.

Route for transcytosis of IgG molecules into rodent and rabbit embryos. In rodents and rabbits, IgG gains access to the embryo by means of the inverted yolk sac placenta. IgG (originally in maternal blood) is found in uterine secretions from sources that include secretions from uterine glands and, possibly, from intercellular leakage between uterine epithelial cells. The IgG is picked up along with the uterine secretions that are endocytosed (1) in vacuoles by embryonic visceral yolk sac endodermal cells as described in Figure 3. When the lysosomes merge with the vacuoles and release their cocktail of lytic enzymes (2), the fluid of the vacuole becomes acidified. The acidification promotes the binding of IgG to FcRn receptors that are found within the vacuoles (3). Binding with the FcRn protects the IgG from degradation by the lytic enzymes. When the vacuole merges with the basal cell membrane of the visceral yolk sac endodermal cells, the contents of the vacuole are disgorged into the pH neutral milieu of the embryonic connective space. In the presence of neutral pH, IgG is released from the FcRn receptor and diffuses into the embryonal/fetal capillaries for delivery to the embryo (4) (DeSesso et al. 2012) with permission.

The high affinity of Fc‐containing molecules to FcRn helps explain their relatively long systemic half‐lives compared with most small molecule therapeutics. Drug developers have exploited FcRn binding affinity by using Fc mutations to optimize desired half‐life and other characteristics. In addition, there appears to be a relationship between FcRn binding characteristics and maternal‐fetal transfer because high‐affinity FcRn binding has been shown to increase transcytosis of Fc‐containing molecules and ablation of FcRn binding prohibits transfer (Thorn et al. 2012; Zikos et al. 2024); however, binding kinetics and other factors influencing IgG disposition and elimination can alter this relationship (Datta‐Mannan, Witcher, Tang, Watkins, Jiang, and Wroblewski 2007; Datta‐Mannan, Witcher, Tang, Watkins, and Wroblewski 2007).

Timing of Appearance of FcRs and Role in Placental Transfer of IgG

The gestational periods and the extent of maternofetal transfer of Fc‐containing molecules vary across species (Figure 7) (Bowman et al. 2013) and this is thought to be affected by the site of transfer, as described above. Although FcRn mRNA expression has been reported in preimplantation embryos as early in development as the 1‐cell stage and at higher levels in 2‐cell and 8‐cell stage mouse embryos (Warner and Paschetto 2000), it is not clear how or if this embryo expression results in the transfer of any maternal Fc‐containing molecules at this stage. In rodents, the InvYSP is established relatively early in gestation and begins to transfer Fc‐containing molecules by approximately gestation day (GD) 9.5 in rat (Roberts et al. 1990) and approximately GD 7.5 in mouse (Jaffe et al. 1990; Kim et al. 2009). This means that a gestation exposure pathway exists in both rodent species commonly used for developmental toxicity testing during most of the period of organogenesis. Binding and transcytosis of IgG by guinea pig FcRn in vitro has also been shown alongside increased maternal‐fetal transfer of IgGs in guinea pig (Xu et al. 2015). The predominant pathway for maternofetal transfer of IgG in rabbits is also across the InvYSP, which is established GD 9–13 (Carney et al. 2004). Transfer in the rabbit begins around GD 15, and the rate of transfer increases as gestation proceeds (DeSesso et al. 2012). However, in contrast to rodents as well as NHP and humans, early transfer of Fc‐containing molecules in rabbit also occurs during a brief period prior to GD 8, which is prior to implantation of the rabbit conceptus (Table 1) and may occur by diffusion through incomplete, or dehisced, tight junctions, similar to what occurs in the small intestine (Pappenheimer 1990; Pappenheimer and Reiss 1987).

The timing and extent of transfer of Fc‐containing molecules is similar to that of endogenous maternal IgGs in NHP (Bowman et al. 2013; Pentsuk and van der Laan 2009). Studies that have investigated the presence of FcRn and IgG in NHP embryos have detected them at all timepoints evaluated, with the earliest tested times to date being GD 35 (Catlin et al. 2020; Moffat et al. 2014). Because the period of major organogenesis in NHP ends at approximately GD 45 (Table 1), these data demonstrate exposure to the embryo during the last half of this critical period of organ development. Previous estimates regarding the earliest time of embryonic exposure were limited due to the absence of data (Bowman et al. 2013) rather than the absence of transfer or inadequacy of detection limits. Future studies may investigate younger embryos and may thereby detect exposure even earlier in gestation. Based on these limited data, there remains much to be learned about maternofetal transfer of these molecules.

In humans, FcRn expression has not been detected before gestational week 14 (Israel et al. 1993). Consistent with this, available information on human fetal‐to‐maternal IgG ratios has shown them to be 5%–10% as early as gestational week 17–22. However, Jauniaux et al. (1995) reported fetal‐to‐maternal IgG ratios of approximately 3.5% in coelomic fluid during gestational weeks 6–12 and Dancis et al. (1961) found a ratio of 2.8% at 12 weeks. Based on the totality of existing (limited) data, including data from NHP, materno‐fetal transfer of Fc‐containing molecules would appear to begin during the latter half of organogenesis in the human.

Importantly, (mini)pigs are poor models for investigating the mechanisms of placental transport in general, and IgG transport specifically, because pigs have multiple maternal tissue layers within the placenta and they fail to transfer IgG across the placenta (Hey et al. 2020; Jacobsen et al. 2016). The porcine placenta is epitheliochorial (Ramsey 1982), which means maternal blood does not contact fetal tissue and, while there are extra layers of tissue between maternal and fetal blood, the pig lacks a syncytiotrophoblast layer (DeSesso 2024). Despite the presence of FcRn in both the porcine cytotrophoblast and small intestinal epithelium (which could allow fetal access to IgG via swallowed amniotic fluid), maternal IgG has not been detected in fetal tissues (Jacobsen et al. 2016).

Collection and Handling of Animal Tissues

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of individual organizations and conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. At each facility, approved and standard animal care, housing, and environmental conditions (controlled) were implemented. An enriched environment, such as paired housing, chew toys, etc. was provided for all animals.

Tissues were collected from time‐mated CD‐1 mice (n = 25), Sprague–Dawley rats (n = 10), and New Zealand white rabbits (n = 12) at GSK (formerly GlaxoSmithKline), Collegeville, PA, from Hartley guinea pigs (n = 10) at Bristol Myers Squibb Company, New Brunswick, NJ, and from cynomolgus monkeys (n = 3) at Charles River Labs, Reno, NV. Animals were humanely euthanized on various days during gestation, beginning shortly after implantation and ending shortly before expected parturition. Immediately following euthanasia, embryos or fetuses were dissected from the uterus. The InvYSP and CAP were collected, weighed, and placed in separate sample vials (tissue samples were weighed but, in some cases, these were not whole tissues, for example, InvYSP). For younger embryos, tissues were pooled at different nominal amounts dependent on tissue size (e.g., GD 13 rabbit InvYSPs were pooled with 3 or 4 per vial, GD 10 rabbit with 8 to 10 per vial). If tissues were pooled, the number of source embryos was recorded. Tissue samples were rinsed in PBS, frozen in a dry ice/ethanol bath immediately after collection and stored in a freezer set to maintain −70°C until analyzed. Samples were shipped on dry ice if analysis was conducted at a site other than that at which it was collected.

Analysis of FcRn Protein in Animals

Mouse, Rat, and Rabbit

Frozen InvYSP and/or CAP tissue samples were thawed, and two tissue samples from the same dam were weighed and pooled prior to homogenization. Homogenate hydrophobic membrane fractions were enriched, digested, and spiked with heavy isotope‐labeled surrogate FcRn peptides. These peptides from the FcRn protein were selected based on uniqueness, chemical stability, absence of analytical interference, and consistent detection and quantification. Samples were subjected to nano‐liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) for quantitation of endogenous FcRn peptides and the isotope labeled surrogates. Samples were measured in singlicate. Rat results are based on average concentrations of three peptides, mouse from two peptides, and rabbit from a single peptide.

Guinea Pig

Previously washed and weighed (after collection and prior to storage at ≤ 70°C) InvYSP and/or CAP tissue samples were homogenized, digested, and peptides extracted, spiked with stable isotope labeled surrogate FcRn peptides for quantitation, and subjected to LC–MS/MS. The peptide from the FcRn protein was selected based on uniqueness, chemical stability, absence of analytical interference, and consistent detection and quantification. Sample analysis was conducted in duplicate. Results are based on a single peptide.

Nonhuman Primate

After thawing, sections that were approximately 50 mg wet weight were taken (this was a different wet weight in each but is normalized to 50 mg). It should be noted that gross morphological differences in the placenta made it difficult to ensure equivalent pieces were used; one tissue sample per NHP was analyzed in analytic triplicates. Hydrophobic membrane fractions were enriched, digested, and spiked with heavy isotope‐labeled surrogate FcRn peptides. These peptides from the FcRn protein were selected based on uniqueness, chemical stability, absence of analytical interference, and consistent detection and quantification. Samples were subjected to LC–MS/MS for quantitation of endogenous FcRn peptides and the isotope labeled surrogates. Results are based on average concentrations of four peptides.

Collection, Handling, and FcRn Analysis in Human Placenta

Human placental tissues were provided by Hamilton Health Sciences, Hamilton, ON. All procedures were conducted according to approved research ethics protocol (0269‐T) provided by the Hamilton Health Sciences integrated Research Ethics Board. First (n = 6) and second (n = 5) trimester placental tissues from elective pregnancy terminations were obtained as paraffin‐embedded blocks. Near or full‐term (third trimester) placental samples (n = 6) were provided as tissues. All specimens were fixed in 10% NBF, paraffin embedded (also referred to as FFPE tissues) and processed for standard immunohistochemical staining. Each specimen consisted of a large portion of tissue dissected from a human placenta and considered representative of the whole tissue. Single full‐size, large sections of each human placenta were evaluated and considered representative of each specimen (see Figure S1 for representative sections at different trimesters). Specimens were uniquely identified, and all patient characteristics were blinded.

FFPE tissue blocks were sectioned at 5 μm, mounted onto pre‐washed and subbed (APES) glass slides, and baked for immunohistochemistry (IHC). Antibodies for human cellular proteins and markers used to analyze FcRn expression and cellular localization included FcRn (Sigma, HPA 012122, rabbit polyclonal IgG, 0.5 mg/mL), CD31/PECAM1 (Santa Cruz, M‐20 goat polyclonal, 0.5 mg/mL), IBA1 (Wako, Cat# 019‐19741, rabbit polyclonal, 0.05 mg/mL), and human IgG (Leica Microsystems, Novocastra, RWP49, 0.3 mg/mL). Slides were digitally scanned, and images were captured and stored as high‐resolution tiff files. FcRn expression was evaluated by a pathologist based on the presence or absence of immunohistochemical staining within specific cell types within the placenta. The binary scoring for each cell type was calculated as a percentage of the total number for samples for each trimester. Data were compiled and graphed using GraphPad Prism 7.

In the mouse (Figure 8A), FcRn concentration in the InvYSP increased dramatically (380×) from GD 8 to 10. Subsequently, the InvYSP FcRn concentrations increased at 1.8×, 2.1×, 2.7×, and 2.7× on GD 12, 14, 16 and 18, respectively, compared with GD 10. For the mouse CAP, there was a 7× increase from GD 8 to 10, but then generally no change in FcRn concentration thereafter; levels varied from 0.58× to 0.81× compared to GD 10.

In the rat (Figure 8B), the InvYSP FcRn had consistent and dramatic increases in concentration as gestation progressed; increasing 6×, 21×, 38×, and 37× on GD 13, 16, 19, and 21, respectively, compared to GD 10 levels. Conversely, CAP concentrations increased initially as the placenta was developing up to GD 13 but then remained generally steady thereafter; increasing 37× from GD 10 to GD 13 but then varying slightly from 0.60× to 0.98× from GD 16 to 21. There were no remarkable differences in either tissue concentration between GD 19 and 21.

In the rabbit (Figure 9A), there was a steady increase in InvYSP FcRn concentrations from GD 10 throughout organogenesis followed by a more dramatic increase after organogenesis; during organogenesis concentrations increased 5×, 9×, and 22× on GD 13, 16, and 19, respectively, and then 50× and 71× on GD 24 and 29, respectively, compared to GD 10. The concentration of FcRn was 10‐times higher post organogenesis than during organogenesis. For the rabbit CAP concentrations, there were very slight variations at all measured timepoints, with a slight trend for increasing as gestation progressed; concentrations were 0.67×, 1.1×, 1.9×, 2.3×, and 2.0× of GD 10 level on GD 13, 16, 19, 24, and 29, respectively.

In the guinea pig (Figure 9B), early organogenesis was not captured in the sample collection, with the first timepoint on GD 15. For FcRn concentration in the InvYSP in the guinea pig there was a clear trend of increasing concentration, 8.6×, 24×, 62×, 40× on GD 21, 30, 45 and 60, respectively compared with GD 15. For the guinea pig CAP concentration, there was a steady, but much less dramatic, increase throughout gestation; concentrations increased 2.5×, 6.4×, 10× and 13× on GD 21, 30, 45 and 60 compared to GD 15.

In the NHP (Figure 9C), the first sample collections were in the middle of organogenesis on GD 35 and only CAP was collected. There were no notable differences in FcRn concentration in CAP in the NHP at any of the measured timepoints from mid‐organogenesis to near the end of gestation on GD 135; compared to GD 35, concentrations were 0.94×, 1.3×, 0.85×, and 1.0× on GD 50, 70, 100, and 135, respectively.

Overall, FcRn protein was detected throughout gestation (including organogenesis) in placental tissue (InvYSP and/or CAP) of all animal species with a trend of increasing concentration as gestation progressed, except for NHP. FcRn protein was detectable in all InvYSP tissues that could be collected, even at the earliest timepoints, and there were much higher FcRn protein levels per mg of tissue in the InvYSP compared to the CAP in all species (InvYSP not present in NHP). The concentrations in CAP were low in all species tested (including NHP) and remained rather unchanged at all gestational ages. This is particularly notable for the rabbit, guinea pig, and NHP for which gestation extends well beyond the period of organogenesis.

Discussion

Animal FcRn Protein Concentrations in InvYSP and CAP

Understanding the ontogeny of FcRn concentration in the InvYSP and CAP through gestation across species compared with humans is critical to understanding embryo‐fetal exposure of Fc‐containing biopharmaceuticals and human risk assessment. With roadmaps to reduce animal testing in preclinical safety studies announced by both US and European health authorities, the data generated herein may ultimately facilitate the use of in silico tools/computational modeling of placental transfer of Fc‐containing molecules using FcRn expression levels and binding affinity of these molecules to the receptor.

In the mouse, the higher concentration of FcRn in the InvYSP compared to the CAP is quite pronounced, averaging ~25× (ranging from 9× to 41×) throughout gestation. This is consistent with the presence of FcRn by in situ hybridization (Jaffe et al. 1990) and IHC (Latvala et al. 2017) in the mouse epithelial cells of the InvYSP endoderm. In the rat there were markedly higher concentrations of FcRn protein in the InvYSP compared to the CAP (averaging approximately 10× more, except on GD 13; Figure 8B). This is aligned with reports of FcRn detection via IHC with intense and dramatic staining in the endoderm layer of the InvYSP of the cotton rat, and much less intense staining in the labyrinth and chorionic plate on GD 15 (Martinez et al. 2020). This FcRn detection in the endoderm and in the endothelial cells of fetal vessels in the labyrinth zone of the CAP has been reported elsewhere and is consistent with the mouse (Latvala et al. 2017). The endoderm layer of the InvYSP directly faces the fetal circulation. This endodermal expression of FcRn is likely responsible for transporting maternal IgG across the yolk sac wall and into the fetal circulation. In addition to placental FcRn for maternal‐fetal transfer of Fc‐containing molecules prenatally, rodents share an additional route for postnatal transfer, likely due to the short gestation period. At birth, the rodent gastrointestinal tract is immature (DeSesso 2022) with neutral gastric secretions (pH 7–7.5) that lack digestive enzymes until weaning (Walthall et al. 2005). The neonatal intestinal epithelium differs both morphologically and functionally from mature, adult epithelium (Carlile and Beck 1983; Picut and Parker 2017) and relies mainly on pinocytosis for absorption. Absorption of IgG and other Fc‐containing molecules is mediated by FcRn that occurs only in the epithelial cells of the immature proximal small intestine (Arevalo Sureda et al. 2016; Cooper et al. 2014; Israel et al. 1997). Nonselective pinocytosis of nutrients and other macromolecules occurs in epithelial cells of the ileum, which lack the FcRn receptor.

In the rabbit, FcRn concentration was negligible in the CAP during organogenesis and relatively low later in gestation. FcRn concentration in the InvYSP was detectable starting on GD 13 and increased 13× from GD 13 to 29 during and after organogenesis to the end of gestation (Figure 9A). Although rabbit FcRn in the InvYSP had previously been reported at the end of gestation (Cobbs Jr. et al. 1980; Liteplo et al. 1982), this is the first report of detectable FcRn during organogenesis and is consistent with placental transfer of IgG following the period of yolk sac inversion (GD 9–13) when Reichert's membrane degradation is completed and the InvYSP membrane is in close contact with the uterine epithelium (Carney et al. 2004; Smith and Schechtman 1962). Available data on FcRn binding IgG are consistent with the role of FcRn in maternal‐fetal transfer of IgG in the rabbit (Catunda Lemos et al. 2012; Szikora et al. 2017) as in other species; and therefore, supports the relevance of this species in evaluation of Fc‐containing biopharmaceuticals in safety assessment in pregnancy.

In the guinea pig InvYSP, FcRn concentration was markedly higher than in the CAP with InvYSP increasing approximately 40× from GD 15 to GD 60 and CAP increasing 13× during the same period (Figure 9B). FcRn expression on GD 15 suggests that some mAb transfer to the embryo is likely occurring in the early period of organogenesis and via both the InvYSP and CAP. FcRn expression in the CAP and InvYSP is consistent with placental transfer of IgG and Fc‐containing molecules at all time points (Barnes 1959; Leissring and Anderson 1961; Ma et al. 2014; Struble et al. 2012; Xu et al. 2015).

Unlike mouse, rat, rabbit, and guinea pig, overall placental FcRn protein concentration in NHP did not appear to increase during gestation (NHP do not have InvYSP). However, similar to these other species, FcRn protein was detected in the CAP at the earliest tested timepoint (GD 35 for NHP) which is during the period of major organogenesis, but not as early in organogenesis as the other species were assessed. Nevertheless, FcRn protein concentration remained consistent throughout gestation (Figure 9C). This is consistent with detection of FcRn in NHP CAP as early as GD 35 and similar expression throughout gestation via Western Blot (Catlin et al. 2020) and aligns with FcRn detected in the NHP CAP on GD 150 via IHC staining in the syncytiotrophoblasts and in endothelial cells of fetal vessels of the placental villi (Latvala et al. 2017).

Previously, Latvala et al. (2017) published IHC data that delineated the presence of placental FcRn across mouse, rat, and NHP at the end of organogenesis (mouse and rat) or later (NHP), but the amounts were not quantified. To our knowledge, data from the current study are the first to quantify the concentration of FcRn in the InvYSP and/or CAP during early gestation in these species. The data have some limitations in that direct comparison across species cannot be made due to slightly different approaches used to quantify FcRn peptides. Nevertheless, comparisons within a species between CAP and InvYSP FcRn concentrations are quantitative. Even with this limitation, clear themes arise from the data across species with InvYSP being the primary contributor to FcRn concentrations in mouse, rat, rabbit, and guinea pig, and with concentration increasing as gestation progresses across all non‐primate species. In the current study, FcRn protein was detected throughout gestation (including organogenesis) in placental tissue (InvYSP and/or CAP) of all animal species with a trend of increasing concentration as gestation progressed, except for NHP. FcRn protein was detectable in all InvYSP tissues that could be collected, even at the earliest timepoints, and there were much higher FcRn protein levels per mg of tissue in the InvYSP compared to the CAP in all species (InvYSP not present in NHP). This phenomenon of higher InvYSP FcRn protein concentration persisted even as gestation progresses and the CAP becomes more functional through all gestational ages assessed. The concentrations in CAP were low in all species tested (including NHP) and remained rather unchanged at all gestational ages. This is particularly notable for the rabbit, guinea pig, and NHP, which have longer gestational periods beyond the period of organogenesis. These data suggest that the InvYSP remains an important tissue for FcRn mediated transfer throughout all of gestation in rat and mouse, rabbit, and guinea pig.

In the present study, the concentration of FcRn protein was measured, not total FcRn protein or functional transcytosis of Fc‐containing molecules via the FcRn (as presence of protein may not directly translate to capability of IgG maternal fetal transfer). In addition, the CAP and InvYSP FcRn concentration data reported were in some cases based on pooled tissue (e.g., InvYSP in early gestation rodents) or a representative portion (CAP in late gestation in NHPs) and normalization to whole tissue weight was not possible. Despite this, it is known that the placenta rapidly increases in size during gestation. Based on this, it is reasonable to expect that the total FcRn protein would increase throughout gestation (even in the CAP where FcRn protein concentration was largely unchanged throughout gestation). Consequently, the capacity to transfer proportionally more Fc‐containing molecules would also be expected to increase throughout gestation along with increased embryo‐fetal exposure as the pregnancy progresses.

The correlation of FcRn protein expression to Fc‐containing molecules transfer and exposure to the embryo may not be straightforward in some cases. There are data suggesting that the expression pattern, and likely the level, of FcRn within the InvYSP placenta is altered when the receptor gene is controlled by different promoters, as shown in transgenic mice expressing human FcRn (Latvala et al. 2017). Other characteristics of Fc‐containing proteins such as their quaternary structure (Brinkhaus et al. 2022), glycosylation pattern (Jennewein et al. 2019; Saunders 2019; Volkov et al. 2023), point mutation, IgG subclass and species (Szikora et al. 2017; Vaccaro et al. 2006) can also impact their binding affinity to FcRn and therefore the transplacental transport.

Human Placenta FcRn

In human placenta, FcRn was consistently expressed in fetal macrophages (Hofbauer cells). FcRn expression in other cells within the fetal portion of the placenta increased in the second trimester; FcRn expression in syncytiotrophoblasts was rarer in the first trimester and increased dramatically in the second and third trimesters (Figure 12). FcRn expression in endothelial cells in fetal vessels was infrequent in the first trimester but increased in the second trimester in both small and large fetal vessels and remained high in the third trimester (Figure 12).

In the maternal portion of the placenta, FcRn endothelial expression peaked in the second trimester and dropped in the third trimester. Decidua and maternal placental macrophages, like fetal macrophages, expressed FcRn throughout gestation.

These data demonstrate a marked increase of FcRn expression in syncytiotrophoblasts and fetal vasculature in the second trimester, which facilitates transfer of maternal IgG as well as Fc‐containing molecules to the developing fetus. The observed increase in FcRn vascular expression lends support to published reports that IgG exposures to the embryo are lower during the period of major organogenesis (first trimester) compared to the second trimester. An interesting observation was the abundance of FcRn on fetal and maternal macrophages throughout gestation. These fetal macrophages (Hofbauer cells) also express other Fc receptors including FcγRI, FcγRII, FcγRIII (Jensen and Matre 1995; Kiskova et al. 2019; Saji et al. 1999). While beyond the scope of this study, fetal capillaries express FcγRIIb, which may also play a role in the transfer of Fc‐containing molecules.

Previous studies investigating the localization of the FcRn in the human placenta reported varying results. Across all trimesters, FcRn has been consistently detected in syncytiotrophoblasts (Kiskova et al. 2019; Latvala et al. 2017; Leach et al. 1996; Simister et al. 1996) and in the endothelial cells of fetal blood vessels (Kiskova et al. 2019; Latvala et al. 2017; Leach et al. 1996; Radulescu et al. 2004). Signal consistent with expression in macrophages was noted in two studies examining term placentas (Kiskova et al. 2019; Leach et al. 1996). Notably, cytotrophoblastic expression was not observed in the only study assessing second trimester placentas (Latvala et al. 2017). To our knowledge, the present study is the first to systematically analyze FcRn expression in both fetal and maternal placental tissues across all trimesters.

FcRn and Placental Transfer of Fc‐Containing Molecules

To date, the available information on placental transfer of Fc‐containing molecules has relied on measuring the concentration of these molecules in the embryo‐fetus. These data can vary widely within and across species including mouse, rat, rabbit, NHP, and human; particularly in the context of maternal concentrations (Bowman et al. 2013). Species‐specific data include mouse (Brambell 1970; Kim et al. 2009), rat (Bowman et al. 2012; Coder et al. 2013; Halliday 1955; Thorn et al. 2012), rabbit (Brambell 1970; Carney et al. 2004), guinea pig (Struble et al. 2012; Xu et al. 2015), NHP (Catlin et al. 2020; Moffat et al. 2014; Wang et al. 2016), and human (Dancis et al. 1961; Jauniaux and Gulbis 2000; Jauniaux et al. 1995; Malek et al. 1996). These data across species are very limited early in gestation during organogenesis, likely due to challenges with sample collection. To improve our understanding of placental transfer of Fc‐containing molecules throughout gestation, the orthogonal approach of evaluating placental FcRn ontogeny across species was evaluated. Based on data in the present study, FcRn protein concentrations in mouse, rat, rabbit, guinea pig, and NHP are generally consistent with available data on placental transfer of Fc‐containing molecules (Bowman et al. 2013), in that the concentrations of FcRn protein increase as gestation progresses in all animal species tested except NHP, although it is anticipated that total placental FcRn increases in all species during gestation as the size of placenta increases. Also, the presence of FcRn concentration during organogenesis is consistent with limited data available indicating transfer of Fc‐containing molecules during organogenesis, even if only at very small amounts. Our data demonstrate that the InvYSP has FcRn protein available in measurable quantities during organogenesis in mouse, rat, rabbit, and guinea pig. The observation that the FcRn protein concentration increases as gestation progresses is a strong indicator that InvYSP contributes to the FcRn‐mediated placental transfer of Fc‐containing molecules throughout pregnancy in the mouse, rat, rabbit, and guinea pig. In addition, the presence of FcRn in NHP and human CAP throughout gestation, including first trimester, supports similar hypotheses that Fc‐containing molecules are capable of maternal‐fetal transfer early. As previously indicated, while FcRn concentrations may not increase throughout gestation in NHP and humans, the rapid growth of the primate CAP is consistent with the observation of increasing placental transfer of Fc‐containing molecules as gestation progresses.

FcRn—Not the Whole Story

The importance of FcRn in the transport of IgG is undeniable as knockout mice that lack FcRn cannot transfer IgG (Kim et al. 2009) and Fab fragments (that lack the Fc portion) or IgG with Fc fragments mutated so that they do not bind FcRn and do not cross the placenta in rats (Thorn et al. 2012; Wakefield et al. 2011). While these data and other information presented and discussed above indicate partial understanding of the phenomenon of maternofetal transfer of some large biomolecules, our current knowledge of the entire repertoire of transporters and kinetics remains rudimentary but continues to evolve (Albrecht et al. 2019; Ilekis et al. 2016). Certainly, large (> 500 Da) non‐IgG based molecules such as bilirubin (585 Da) and vancomycin (1449 Da) cross the placenta. There are other placental proteins, including placental alkaline phosphatase and annexin II that bind Fc‐containing molecules, although they have not yet been determined to play a role in placental transfer (Kristoffersen 1996; Simister and Story 1997).

It is becoming clear that FcRn is the predominant mechanism for maternofetal transfer of large biomolecules, but it is likely not a lone actor. Interesting data have been presented on the development and validation of an in silico placental model for use in optimization of vaccine schedules in pregnancy (Wessel and Dolatshahi 2023). They note the controversy regarding the roles of FcRn on syncytiotrophoblasts and the role of Fc‐γ‐III receptors on endothelial cells. Fc‐γ receptors have been described in placenta (Kameda et al. 1991) and others have suggested they might be useful in enhancing Fc‐molecule transplacental transport (Jennewein et al. 2019). A recent study also suggests that an additional Fcγ receptor might be required to complete placental transfer of IgGs in rhesus macaques (Rosenberg et al. 2023). Elsewhere, it was demonstrated that although placental transport was driven by FcRn, glycosylation of the Fc‐γ‐III receptors might modulate the rate of transport (Borghi et al. 2020).

Other routes and modes of transfer need to be investigated, including unidentified transporters and the possible existence of gaps within the syncytial plate. Further, one might ask if there are pores or other openings that might appear transiently in the syncytiotrophoblast. Understanding the timing of maternofetal transfer of Fc‐containing molecules and identifying alternate routes to cross the placenta should continue to be a focus of research into the safety of these molecules.

Implications for Risk Assessment

While it is important to recognize that the primary site of Fc‐containing molecule transfer differs between rodents and rabbits (InvYSP) versus primates (CAP), from a risk assessment standpoint it is important to appreciate that based on available data in the literature and the placental FcRn expression data presented here, Fc‐containing molecules have access to rodent, rabbit, and primate embryos during critical periods of gestation. FcRn is considered the predominant mechanism to transfer Fc‐containing molecules to offspring, so the presence of FcRn expression in all species at all stages of pregnancy (including organogenesis) supports the relevance of nonclinical models for hazard identification of Fc‐containing biotherapeutics (Bowman et al. 2013). As mentioned earlier, there may be other receptors or mechanisms of transfer, as well as other modifying/regulatory factors, so a direct 1‐to‐1 relationship between FcRn expression and maternal‐fetal transfer of Fc‐containing molecules may not always be expected. For example, it has been observed that Fc‐fusion proteins have reduced affinity to human FcRn and shorter half‐life relative to mAbs (Berthelsen et al. 2010; Suzuki et al. 2010), this may be expected to have lower placental transfer (Murashima et al. 2009). Consequently, while further research is necessary to complete our understanding of how and when Fc‐containing biotherapeutics gain access to the developing conceptus, the available nonclinical animal models provide meaningful data to inform human risk assessment.

To improve use of animal data for human risk assessment, any relevant species‐specific differences should be considered. One of the species‐specific characteristics is FcRn binding properties in animal species compared with human (Ober et al. 2001); differences can be important when evaluating human risk of Fc‐containing therapeutics based on animal data. For example, mouse FcRn unexpectedly has a much broader binding specificity relative to human FcRn (Rodewald 1976; Zhou et al. 2003). In addition, mice models have significant differences in murine FcRn binding specificity compared to human FcRn (Vaccaro et al. 2006). As described earlier, unlike species with longer gestation periods, both mouse and rat also transfer Fc‐containing molecules using FcRn during lactation from maternal milk to offspring via intestinal absorption. Like in other animal species described above, the guinea pig is a good model because the guinea pig FcRn immobilized on a solid surface can bind both human IgG and guinea pig IgG, making the guinea pig a relevant model to humans to assess placental transfer of Fc‐containing molecules. All engineered human Fc isotype‐specific constructs are internalized by cells expressing guinea pig FcRn in vitro (Xu et al. 2015).

Conclusions

We reviewed comparative placentation among relevant laboratory species and humans and investigated the location, ontogeny, and quantitation of placental FcRn concentrations to provide more context to maternal IgG and Fc‐containing biopharmaceuticals transfer/biodistribution to offspring during pregnancy and subsequently inform developmental toxicity testing. The presence and/or increasing concentration of FcRn in the InvYSP and CAP (with expected increasing placental surface area) as pregnancy progresses suggest that the embryo and fetus can potentially be exposed to Fc‐containing molecules throughout pregnancy, including during the period of major organogenesis. In short gestation species (e.g., mouse, rat, guinea pig, and rabbit), FcRn occurred predominantly in the InvYSP rather than the CAP; whereas in NHP and human (which do not develop InvYSP) FcRn was present only in CAP. Regardless of FcRn location, Fc‐containing molecules can access the embryo throughout gestation in all species, although the extent of transfer depends on changes in both the extent of FcRn expression and size of the placenta. Thus, while the location of FcRn differs across species, exposure of embryos to Fc‐containing molecules during gestation occurs in all species. Despite this growing body of interspecies information regarding placental FcRn and biodistribution of Fc‐containing molecules, the general pharmacokinetics and dynamics of this process are not fully understood and there may be molecule‐specific safety and efficacy profiles in pregnant women and fetuses. Understanding and addressing these gaps may also facilitate better modeling of placental transfer of Fc‐containing molecules. Addressing these gaps in the future should lead to better clinical practices and therapeutic strategies to treat pregnancy‐related diseases.
