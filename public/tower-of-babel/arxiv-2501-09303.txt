General detectability measure
Masahito Hayashi

Abstract
Distinguishing resource states from resource-free states is a fundamental task in quantum information. We have approached the state detection problem through a hypothesis testing framework, with the alternative hypothesis set comprising resource-free states in a general context. Consequently, we derived the optimal exponential decay rate of the failure probability for detecting a given n n -tensor product state when the resource-free states are separable states, positive partial transpose (PPT) states, or the convex hull of the set of stabilizer states. This optimal exponential decay rate is determined by the minimum of the reverse relative entropy, indicating that this minimum value serves as the general detectability measure. The key technique of this paper is a quantum version of empirical distribution.

II.1
Formulation

Figure 1
:
Our testing problem

In this paper, we study the problem of quantum hypothesis testing
to distinguish a given state
\sigma
from a set
{\cal S}_{0}

of resource-free states.
In the standard framework of quantum hypothesis testing, two quantum states,

\rho
and
\sigma
, on a given Hilbert space
{\cal H}
represent the two simple hypotheses.
As illustrated in Fig.
1
,
one is the alternative hypothesis
H_{1}
, which is composed of
a state
\sigma
,
the other is the null hypothesis
H_{0}
, which is composed of
a state
\rho
.
The aim of the hypothesis testing is
to accept the alternative hypothesis
H_{1}
by rejecting
the null hypothesis
H_{0}
with a fixed precision level.
To keep this fixed precision level for the rejection of
H_{0}
,
we impose the probability for incorrectly rejecting
H_{0}
to be upper bounded by a threshold
\epsilon>0
.
This probability
\epsilon
is called the significance level of our test.

That is, we impose the following condition for our test operator
\Pi

as

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{\epsilon,\rho}\coloneqq\Big\{\Pi\Big|0\leq\Pi\leq I,\Tr[(I-\Pi)\rho]\leq\epsilon\Big\},

(1)

where
I
denotes the identity operator.
Then, we optimize our test under this condition as follows.

\displaystyle\beta_{\epsilon}(\rho\|\sigma)\coloneqq\min_{\Pi\in{\cal D}_{\epsilon,\rho}}\Tr[\Pi\sigma].

(2)

Since our aim is
to distinguish a given state
\sigma
from a compact set

{\cal S}_{0}
of resource-free states,
we impose the following condition instead of (
1
).

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{\epsilon,{\cal S}_{0}}\coloneqq\Big\{\Pi\Big|0\leq\Pi\leq I,\max_{\rho\in{\cal S}_{0}}\Tr[(I-\Pi)\rho]\leq\epsilon\Big\}.

(3)

A hypothesis characterized by a set of states is known as a “composite hypothesis”.
Our optimization problem is formulated as

\displaystyle\beta_{\epsilon}\left({\cal S}_{0}\middle\|\sigma\right)\coloneqq\min_{\Pi\in{\cal D}_{\epsilon,{\cal S}_{0}}}\Tr[\Pi\sigma].

(4)

In statistics, the probability
1-\beta_{\epsilon}\left({\cal S}_{0}\middle\|\sigma\right)

is called the detection probability of
\sigma

under the null hypothesis
{\cal S}_{0}

at a significance level
\epsilon

[
30
]
.
When we make the detection by rejecting the null hypothesis

{\cal S}_{0}
in statistics,
we need to fix a significance level
\epsilon
.
Hence, we can consider that the quantity

\beta_{\epsilon}\left({\cal S}_{0}\middle\|\sigma\right)

is linked to the detectability of the state
\sigma

under the null hypothesis
{\cal S}_{0}

rather than

\beta_{\epsilon}\left(\sigma\middle\|{\cal S}_{0}\right)

because

1-\beta_{\epsilon}\left(\sigma\middle\|{\cal S}_{0}\right)

shows the probability to correctly accept the null hypothesis
{\cal S}_{0}
.
Thus, when
a sequence of subsets
{\cal F}_{n}\subset{\cal S}({\cal H}^{\otimes n})

is given as our null hypothesis,
the exponent

\frac{-1}{n}\log\beta_{\epsilon}\left({\cal F}_{n}\middle\|\sigma^{\otimes n}\right)
shows
the strength of the detection power of the state
\sigma

under the null hypothesis
{\cal F}_{n}

at a significance level
\epsilon
.
Therefore, when the limit

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{-1}{n}\log\beta_{\epsilon}\left({\cal F}_{n}\middle\|\sigma^{\otimes n}\right)
exists,
it can be considered as
the detectability measure.
The aim of this paper is to characterize
the detectability measure under a general setting.

This quantity can be generalized by using another compact set of states
\mathcal{S}_{1}
as

\displaystyle\beta_{\epsilon}\left({\cal S}_{0}\middle\|\mathcal{S}_{1}\right)\coloneqq\min_{\Pi\in{\cal D}_{\epsilon,{\cal S}_{0}}}\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{1}}\Tr[\Pi\sigma].

(5)

For convenience, we introduce the following auxiliary quantities:

\displaystyle\beta_{\epsilon}(\rho\|{\cal S}_{2})\coloneqq\min_{0\leq\Pi\leq I}\Big\{\max_{\sigma\in{\cal S}_{2}}\Tr[\sigma\Pi]\Big|\Tr[(I-\Pi)\rho]\leq\epsilon\Big\}.

(6)

We also employ the relative entropy and the sandwiched Rényi relative entropy
[
31
,
32
]
, defined as

\displaystyle D(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Tr\rho(\log\rho-\log\sigma),

(7)

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\Tr\left(\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\rho\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha}.

(8)

Specifically,
D_{1}(\rho\|\sigma)
is equivalent to the relative entropy
D(\rho\|\sigma)
.

In preparation for the following discussion, we define the notations:

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}({\cal S}_{0}\|\sigma)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min_{\rho\in{\cal S}_{0}}D_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma),

(9)

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\rho\|\mathcal{S}_{1})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{1}}D_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma),

(10)

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}({\cal S}_{0}\|\mathcal{S}_{1})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min_{\rho\in{\cal S}_{0},\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{1}}D_{\alpha}(\rho\|\sigma).

(11)

In addition, in this paper,

\displaystyle R_{n,\alpha,r}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=

\displaystyle\frac{-\frac{1-\alpha}{n}D_{\alpha}({\cal F}_{n}\|\sigma^{\otimes n})-r}{1-\alpha}.

(85)

We define

\displaystyle{}_{n}(\sigma,r)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\bigcup_{\rho_{n}\in{\cal F}_{n}}{}_{n}(\sigma,\rho_{n},R_{n,\alpha,r}).

(86)

Since all states
\rho_{n}\in{\cal F}_{n}
and
\sigma^{\otimes n}

are permutation-invariant,
it is sufficient to discuss whether

{\cal T}_{n,p}
is contained in

{}_{n}(\sigma,r)

for each
p\in{\cal T}_{n}
.

When
{\cal T}_{n,p}
is contained in

{}_{n}(\sigma,r)
,
there exists
a state
\rho_{n,p}\in{\cal F}_{n}
such that

{\cal T}_{n,p}\subset{}_{n}(\sigma,\rho_{n,p},R_{n,\alpha,r})
.
When
{\cal T}_{n,p}
is not contained in

{}_{n}(\sigma,r)
,
the intersection
{\cal T}_{n,p}\cap{}_{n}(\sigma,r)
is an empty set.

The above statement can be shown as follows.
When
{\cal T}_{n,p}
is contained in
{}_{n}(\sigma,r)
,
there exists a state
\rho_{n,p}\in{\cal F}_{n}
such that

{\cal T}_{n,p}\cap{}_{n}(\sigma,\rho_{n,p},R_{n,\alpha,r})\neq\emptyset
.
We choose an element

x^{n}\in{\cal T}_{n,p}\cap{}_{n}(\sigma,\rho_{n,p},R_{n,\alpha,r})
.
Given a permutation
g
on
\{1,\ldots,n\}

we define
g(x^{n})=(x_{g(1)},\ldots,x_{g(n)})
.
Hence, any element of
{\cal T}_{n,p}
is written as a form

.
Also, since the set
{}_{n}(\sigma,\rho_{n,p},R_{n,\alpha,r})
is permutation invariant,

g(x^{n})
belongs to
{}_{n}(\sigma,\rho_{n,p},R_{n,\alpha,r})
.
Hence,

{\cal T}_{n,p}\subset{}_{n}(\sigma,\rho_{n,p},R_{n,\alpha,r})
.

Assume that
{\cal T}_{n,p}
is not contained in

{}_{n}(\sigma,r)
.
If there exists an element
x^{n}\in{\cal T}_{n,p}\cap{}_{n}(\sigma,r)
,
there exists a state
\rho_{n,p}\in{\cal F}_{n}
such that

x^{n}\in{\cal T}_{n,p}\cap{}_{n}(\sigma,\rho_{n,p},R_{n,\alpha,r})
.
Using the above discussion, we find that

{\cal T}_{n,p}\subset{}_{n}(\sigma,\rho_{n,p},R_{n,\alpha,r})
, which contradicts the above assumption.
Hence, there exists no element
x^{n}\in{\cal T}_{n,p}\cap{}_{n}(\sigma,r)
, i.e.,
the intersection
{\cal T}_{n,p}\cap{}_{n}(\sigma,r)
is an empty set.

In summary, for any
p\in{\cal T}_{n}
, there exists
a state
\rho_{n,p}\in{\cal F}_{n}
such that

\displaystyle{\cal T}_{n,p}\cap{}_{n}(\sigma,r)={}_{n}(\sigma,\rho_{n,p},R_{n,\alpha,r}).

(87)

Therefore,

\displaystyle{}_{n}(\sigma,r)=\bigcup_{p\in{\cal T}_{n}}{}_{n}(\sigma,\rho_{n,p},R_{n,\alpha,r}).

(88)

Then,
\rho_{n}\in{\cal F}_{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle\Tr(\rho_n (I-1_{\Omega_{n}(\sigma,r)}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\Tr(\rho_n (I-1_{\Omega_{n}(\sigma,\rho_n,R_{n,\alpha,r})}))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\Tr( (\rho_n)^{\alpha} (\sigma^{\otimes n})^{1-\alpha})e^{n(1-\alpha)R_{n,\alpha,r}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{(\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}({\cal F}_{n}\|\sigma^{\otimes n})}e^{n(1-\alpha)R_{n,\alpha,r}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{=}}e^{-nr},

(89)

where
(a)
,
(b)
and
(c)
follow from (
88

(
84
), and (
85
), respectively.

VI.1
Notations for quantum empirical distribution

To set the stage for our results in the quantum domain,
we review a quantum version of Sanov’s theorem proposed by
[
25
]
,
which is based on the concept of quantum empirical distribution and differs from the hypothesis-testing approach
used in the quantum Sanov theorem by the paper
[
6
,
5
]
.
The extension by the paper
[
25
]
has a structure similar to that of the classical Sanov theorem
[
45
,
27
]
,
relying on empirical distributions rather than hypothesis testing.
We now introduce the necessary notation to present the quantum Sanov theorem for quantum empirical distribution.

Consider a basis
\mathcal{B}=\{|v_{j}\rangle\}_{j=1}^{d}
for the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
The space
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}
has a standard basis given by

|v[x^{n}]\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=|v_{x_{1}},\ldots,v_{x_{n}}\rangle
for
x^{n}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.
When performing a measurement on
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}
in basis
\mathcal{B}
,
the outcome is represented by the sequence
x^{n}
.
The empirical state associated with
x^{n}
under basis
\mathcal{B}
is

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}|v_{x_{j}}\rangle\langle v_{x_{j}}|
,
denoted as
e_{\mathcal{B}}(x^{n})
.
The pinching map
{\cal E}_{{\cal B}}
is defined as

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{{\cal B}}(\sigma)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{x}|v_{x}\rangle\langle v_{x}|\sigma|v_{x}\rangle\langle v_{x}|.

(115)

For the
n
-fold tensor product space,
the set of diagonal states relative to
\mathcal{B}
is denoted by
\mathcal{S}[\mathcal{B}]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}[\mathcal{B}]

\displaystyle=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}|v_{j}\rangle\langle v_{j}|\;\middle|\;\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}=1,\quad p_{j}\geq 0\right\},

(116)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{n}[\mathcal{B}]

\displaystyle=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}|v_{x_{j}}\rangle\langle v_{x_{j}}|\;\middle|\;(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{X}^{n}\right\}.

(117)

For any empirical state
\rho\in\mathcal{S}_{n}[\mathcal{B}]
,
the projection operator is defined as

\displaystyle 1_{\rho,\mathcal{B}}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{x^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}e_{\mathcal{B}}(x^{n})=\rho}|v[x^{n}]\rangle\langle v[x^{n}]|.

(118)

If
\rho\notin\mathcal{S}_{n}[\mathcal{B}]
, we set
1_{\rho,\mathcal{B}}^{n}=0
.

Then, the original classical Sanov theorem is stated as follows.

Proposition 26
.

[
27
, Theorem 2.1.10 and Eq. (2.1.12)]

A pair of a state
\rho\in\mathcal{S}[\mathcal{B}]

and a subset
{\cal S}\subset\mathcal{S}[\mathcal{B}]

satisfy the following inequality

\displaystyle\Tr\rho^{\otimes n}\left(\sum_{\rho^{\prime}\in{\cal S}\cap\mathcal{S}_{n}[\mathcal{B}]}1_{\rho^{\prime},\mathcal{B}}^{n}\right)\leq(n+1)^{d-1}2^{-n\inf_{\rho^{\prime}\in{\cal S}}D(\rho^{\prime}\|\rho)}.

(119)

In addition, when the closure of the interior of
{\cal S}

equals to the closure of
{\cal S}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{-1}{n}\log\Tr\rho^{\otimes n}\left(\sum_{\rho^{\prime}\in{\cal S}\cap\mathcal{S}_{n}[\mathcal{B}]}1_{\rho^{\prime},\mathcal{B}}^{n}\right)=\inf_{\rho^{\prime}\in{\cal S}}D(\rho^{\prime}\|\rho).

(120)

We now apply Schur duality for
{\cal H}^{\otimes n}
,
as used in
[
46
,
7
,
47
,

,

,

,

,

,
6
,

.
A sequence of non-negative integers
\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})
,
ordered in non-decreasing form, is known as a Young index.
Although other references
[
37
,
54
,
55
]
order Young indices in decreasing form,
we adopt the opposite convention for convenience.
The set of Young indices
\bm{\lambda}
with
\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}=n
is denoted by
Y_{d}^{n}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{P}_{d}
the set of probability distributions
p=(p_{j})_{j=1}^{d}

satisfying
p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq\ldots\leq p_{d}
.
For any density matrix

on

represents the eigenvalues of

.
The map
\overline{\cal E}_{\cal B}
is defined as

\displaystyle\overline{\cal E}_{\cal B}(\sigma)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(p(\sigma),{\cal E}_{\cal B}(\sigma)).

(121)

The set

consists of elements of

where each

is an integer multiple of

.
The majorization relation
\succ

and

is defined as follows: for

,

if

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}^{\prime}\quad\text{for}\quad k=1,\ldots,d-1.

(122)

This condition also defines the majorization relation on

.

VI.2
Quantum Sanov theorem based on quantum empirical distribution

We define the TP-CP map
{\cal E}_{n,{\cal B}}
as

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{n,{\cal B}}(\sigma)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{(p,\rho)\in\mathcal{R}_{n}[\mathcal{B}]}(\Tr 1_{p,\rho,\mathcal{B}}^{n}\sigma)\rho_{n,{\cal B}}(p,\rho).

(135)

For a state
\rho\in\mathcal{S}[\mathcal{B}]
and a pair
(p^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\in\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{B}]
, we define

\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{B}}((p^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\|\rho)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=D(\rho^{\prime}\|\rho)+S(\rho^{\prime})-S(p^{\prime}),

(136)

where
S(\rho)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=-\Tr(\rho\log\rho)
.
The following subset of
\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{B}]
plays a significant role in the quantum Sanov theorem for quantum empirical distributions.
For a state
\rho\in\mathcal{S}[\mathcal{B}]
and a positive value
r>0
, we define

\displaystyle S_{\rho,r}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\left\{(p^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\in\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{B}]\;\middle|\;D_{\mathcal{B}}((p^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\|\rho)\leq r\right\},

(137)

with
S_{\rho,r}^{c}
representing its complement.
The properties of the set
S_{\rho,r}
are described in the following result. Readers may refer to
[
25
]
for the proof.

Proposition 27

(
[
25
, Lemma 1]
)
.

For
\rho\in\mathcal{S}[\mathcal{B}]
and
r>0
, the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle\Tr\rho^{\otimes n}\left(\sum_{(p^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\in S_{\rho,r}^{c}\cap\mathcal{R}_{n}[\mathcal{B}]}1_{p^{\prime},\rho^{\prime},\mathcal{B}}^{n}\right)\leq(n+1)^{\frac{(d+4)(d-1)}{2}}2^{-nr}.

(138)

Additionally, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\Tr\rho^{\otimes n}\left(\sum_{(p^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\in S_{\rho,r}^{c}\cap\mathcal{R}_{n}[\mathcal{B}]}1_{p^{\prime},\rho^{\prime},\mathcal{B}}^{n}\right)=r.

(139)

Lemma 28
.

A permutation-invariant
state
\rho_{n}
on
{\cal H}^{\otimes n}
satisfies the relation

\displaystyle\rho_{n}\leq\overline{d}_{n}{\cal E}_{n,{\cal B}}(\rho_{n}).

(140)

Proof.

We diagonalize the matrix
1_{p,\rho,\mathcal{B}}^{n}\rho_{n}1_{p,\rho,\mathcal{B}}^{n}

as
\sum_{j}c_{p,\rho,j}|x(p,\rho,j)\rangle\langle x(p,\rho,j)|\otimes 1_{\mathcal{V}_{\bm{\lambda}}}
, where

1_{\mathcal{V}_{\bm{\lambda}}}
is the identity operator on

\mathcal{V}_{\bm{\lambda}}
.
Hence,
the subspace
{\cal U}_{np,\rho}

is spanned by
\{|x(p,\rho,j)\rangle\}_{j}
.
We choose a basis

\{|y(p,\rho,j)\rangle\}_{j}

mutually unbiased to
\{|x(p,\rho,j)\rangle\}_{j}

on the subspace
{\cal U}_{np,\rho}
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\langle y(p,\rho,j)|\Big(\sum_{k}c_{p,\rho,k}|x(p,\rho,k)\rangle\langle x(p,\rho,k)|\Big)|y(p,\rho,j)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle y(p,\rho,j^{\prime})|\Big(\sum_{k}c_{p,\rho,k}|x(p,\rho,k)\rangle\langle x(p,\rho,k)|\Big)|y(p,\rho,j^{\prime})\rangle.

(141)

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\langle y(p,\rho,j)|\Big(\sum_{k}c_{p,\rho,k}|x(p,\rho,k)\rangle\langle x(p,\rho,k)|\Big)|y(p,\rho,j)\rangle

\displaystyle\cdot|y(p,\rho,j)\rangle\langle y(p,\rho,j)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr\Big(\sum_{k} c_{p,\rho,k} |x(p,\rho,k)\rangle\langle x(p,\rho,k))|\Big)

\displaystyle\cdot\frac{1}{\dim{\cal U}_{np,\rho}}\sum_{j}|y(p,\rho,j)\rangle\langle y(p,\rho,j)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr\Big(\sum_{k} c_{p,\rho,k} |x(p,\rho,k)\rangle\langle x(p,\rho,k)|
\Big)\frac{1}{\dim{\cal U}_{np,\rho}}1_{{\cal U}_{np,\rho}}.

(142)

Thus, using
P_{p,\rho,j}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=|y(p,\rho,j)\rangle\langle y(p,\rho,j)|\otimes 1_{\mathcal{V}_{\bm{\lambda}}}
,
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j}P_{p,\rho,j}\rho_{n}P_{p,\rho,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{j}\langle y(p,\rho,j)|\Big(\sum_{k}c_{p,\rho,k}|x(p,\rho,k)\rangle\langle x(p,\rho,k)|\Big)|y(p,\rho,j)\rangle

\displaystyle\cdot|y(p,\rho,j)\rangle\langle y(p,\rho,j)|\Big)\otimes 1_{\mathcal{V}_{\bm{\lambda}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr\Big(\sum_{k} c_{p,\rho,k} |x(p,\rho,k)\rangle\langle x(p,\rho,k)|\Big)

\displaystyle\cdot\frac{1}{\dim{\cal U}_{np,\rho}}(1_{{\cal U}_{np,\rho}}\otimes 1_{\mathcal{V}_{\bm{\lambda}}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr(\rho_n 1_{p,\rho,\mathcal{B}}^n )\rho_{n,{\cal B}}(p,\rho).

(143)

Now, we define the pinching map

{\cal E}_{n,{\cal B},\rho_{n}}
as

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{n,{\cal B},\rho_{n}}(\sigma)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{p,\rho,j}P_{p,\rho,j}\sigma P_{p,\rho,j}.

(144)

Then, we have

\displaystyle\overline{d}_{n}{\cal E}_{n,{\cal B}}(\rho_{n})=\overline{d}_{n}\sum_{(p,\rho)\in\mathcal{R}_{n}[\mathcal{B}]}(\Tr 1_{p,\rho,\mathcal{B}}^{n}\rho_{n})\rho_{n,{\cal B}}(p,\rho)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\overline{d}_{n}{\cal E}_{n,{\cal B},\rho_{n}}(\rho_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\geq}}\rho_{n},

(145)

where

(a)
follows from (
143
)
and

(b)
follows from the pinching inequality
[
7
, Lemma 9]
.
∎

Lemma 29
.

A permutation-invariant
state
\rho_{n}
on
{\cal H}^{\otimes n}

and an element

(146)

for

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}({\cal E}_{n,{\cal B}}(\rho_{n})\|\sigma^{\otimes n})+\log\overline{d}_{n}\geq D_{\alpha}(\rho_{n}\|\sigma^{\otimes n}).

defined in (

\displaystyle e^{(\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}({\cal E}_{n,{\cal B}}(\rho_{n})\|\rho_{n,{\cal B}}(\eta))}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}e^{(\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}({\cal E}_{n,{\cal B},\rho_{n}}(\rho_{n})\|\rho_{n,{\cal B}}(\eta))}

\displaystyle\Tr\Big(\rho_{n,{\cal B}}(\eta)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}
({\cal E}_{n,{\cal B},\rho_n}(\rho_n))
\rho_{n,{\cal B}}(\eta)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}
\Big)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\Tr\Big(
{\cal E}_{n,{\cal B},\rho_n}
\big(\rho_{n,{\cal B}}(\eta)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}
\rho_n
\rho_{n,{\cal B}}(\eta)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\big)
\Big)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr\Big(

\big(\rho_{n,{\cal B}}(\eta)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}

\rho_{n,{\cal B}}(\eta)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\big)
\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr\Big(
\rho_{n,{\cal B}}(\eta)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}
\rho_n
\rho_{n,{\cal B}}(\eta)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}
\Big)

\displaystyle\cdot\Big({\cal E}_{n,{\cal B},\rho_{n}}\big(\rho_{n,{\cal B}}(\eta)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\rho_{n}\rho_{n,{\cal B}}(\eta)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\big)\Big)^{\alpha-1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\overline{d}_{n}^{1-\alpha}\Tr\Big(\rho_{n,{\cal B}}(\eta)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}
\rho_n
\rho_{n,{\cal B}}(\eta)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}
\Big)

\displaystyle\cdot\Big(\rho_{n,{\cal B}}(\eta)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\rho_{n}\rho_{n,{\cal B}}(\eta)^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\Big)^{\alpha-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{d}_{n}^{1-\alpha}e^{(\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}(\rho_{n}\|\rho_{n,{\cal B}}(\eta))},

(148)

where

(a)
follows from
(a)
of (
145
),

(b)
follows from the relation

and
(c)
follows from (
140
).
Hence, we obtain (
146
).

Since
\sigma^{\otimes n}

Lemma 30
.

For
(p,\rho)\in\mathcal{R}_{n}[\mathcal{B}]
,
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu({\cal S}_{{\cal B}}(p,\rho,\epsilon))}\int_{{\cal S}_{{\cal B}}(p,\rho,\epsilon)}\sigma^{\otimes n}\mu(d\sigma)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle c^{n}(p,\rho,\epsilon)\rho_{n,{\cal B}}(p,\rho),

(152)

where

\displaystyle c^{n}(p,\rho,\epsilon)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=

\displaystyle\frac{d_{np,\rho}}{d_{np}\mu({\cal S}_{{\cal B}}(p,\rho,\epsilon))}\Big((n+d)^{-\frac{(d+2)(d-1)}{2}}-d_{np}e^{-n\epsilon}\Big).

(153)

Proof.

As shown in
[
54
, (6.23)]
,
for
\sigma\in{\cal S}_{{\cal B}}(p)
, we have

\displaystyle(n+d)^{-\frac{(d+2)(d-1)}{2}}\leq\Tr(\sigma^{\otimes n}
1_{\mathcal{U}_{np} \otimes\mathcal{V}_{np}}).

(154)

Hence,

\displaystyle(n+d)^{-\frac{(d+2)(d-1)}{2}}1_{\mathcal{U}_{np}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{np}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1_{\mathcal{U}_{np}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{np}}\Tr\Big(
1_{\mathcal{U}_{np} \otimes\mathcal{V}_{np}}
\int_{{\cal S}_{{\cal B}}(p)}
\sigma^{\otimes n}
\mu(d \sigma) \Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{np}d_{np}^{\prime}1_{\mathcal{U}_{np}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{np}}\Big(\int_{{\cal S}_{{\cal B}}(p)}\sigma^{\otimes n}\mu(d\sigma)\Big)1_{\mathcal{U}_{np}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{np}}.

(155)

Since

126
), we have

\displaystyle(n+d)^{-\frac{(d+2)(d-1)}{2}}1_{p,\rho,\mathcal{B}}^{n}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1_{p,\rho,\mathcal{B}}^{n}\Tr\Big(

\int_{{\cal S}_{{\cal B}}(p)}
\sigma^{\otimes n}
\mu(d \sigma) \Big)

\displaystyle d_{np}d_{np}^{\prime}1_{p,\rho,\mathcal{B}}^{n}\Big(\int_{{\cal S}_{{\cal B}}(p)}\sigma^{\otimes n}\mu(d\sigma)\Big)1_{p,\rho,\mathcal{B}}^{n}

\displaystyle 1_{\mathcal{U}_{np,\rho}}\otimes 1_{{\cal V}_{np}}\Tr(1_{p,\rho,{\cal B}}^n
\int_{{\cal S}^c(p,\rho,\epsilon)}
\sigma^{\otimes n}
\mu(d \sigma) )

\sigma^{\otimes n}
\mu(d \sigma) )

VIII
Conclusion

We have presented a generalization of the quantum Sanov theorem
for hypothesis testing under simpler assumptions
than those used in
[
8
,
9
]
,
and have shown that
our condition is weaker than that used in
[
8
]
.
The measurement used here is the same as
the measurement used in the old papers

[
46
,
7
]
.
This result shows that the minimum value
D({\cal F}_{1}\|\sigma)
works as a general detectability measure.
Section
III
has introduced both the classical and quantum formulations,
stated in Theorems
12
and
13
.
While the method in
[
8
]

addressed the case where
{\cal F}_{n}
is the set of separable states,
it do not demonstrate applicability to the cases
where
{\cal F}_{n}
is the set of positive partial transpose (PPT) states
or the convex hull of stabilizer states.
In addition, the paper
[
9
]
clarifies that the approach by
[
9
]

does not cover the PPT state case.
In contrast, our method covers all three cases: separable states, PPT states, and the convex hull of stabilizer states, demonstrating
the broader applicability and generality of our approach.
The method in
[
8
]
utilizes the blurring map,
while
[
9
]
employs polar sets.
Our central technique, however, is the quantum empirical distribution,
which depends on the choice of basis.
This method was originally introduced
to establish a quantum analog of the Sanov theorem
using empirical distributions,
as the original classical Sanov theorem describes the asymptotic behavior
of empirical distributions.

In this paper,
we first have developed two classical extensions of the quantum Sanov theorem
for hypothesis testing, detailed in Sections
IV
and
V
.
Section
IV
has presented a classical generalization
(Theorem
14
) using empirical distributions,
while Section
V
has provided another extension (Theorem
21
).
Both results, which include non-single-letterized forms,
can be regarded as meta-theorems.
Lemma
17
is essential for deriving Theorem
12
,
and both approaches are necessary to obtain our main result, Theorem
13
.
Next, in Subsection
VI.3
, we have derived several useful formulas
for quantum empirical distributions.
In Section
VII
,
we have established a quantum generalization (Theorem
31
),
which also qualifies as a meta-theorem due to its non-single-letterized structure.
Combining Theorem
31
, the formulas from Subsection
VI.3
,
and the quantum Sanov theorem for quantum empirical distributions by the paper
[
25
]
,
we have derived our final result, Theorem
13
.

This paper did not discuss the infinite-dimensional case.
In the multi-mode Bosonic system, we can consider various examples for the set of the resource-free states, e.g.,
the set of Gaussian mixture of coherent states,
the convex hull of the set of coherent states,
the set of Gaussian states,
and the set of non-negative Wigner function states, etc

[
56
]
.
These examples satisfy the conditions (A2)–(A5) and the convexity and closedness conditions.
Also, to satisfy the condition (B3) for these examples,
we can consider using the heterodyne measurement, i.e., the POVM given by the resolution of the identity by the coherent states.
However, it is needed to extend the obtained result to the
multi-mode Bosonic system to apply it to these examples.
This is an interesting future study.

(\log\rho_{n_k}-\log\sigma_{n_k}))

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n_{k}}\Tr(\rho_{n_k} (Q_{n_k}(b_{\epsilon})-Q_{n_k}(b))
(\log\rho_{n_k}-\log\sigma_{n_k}) )

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n_{k}}\Tr( \rho_{n_k} Q_{n_k}(b)
(\log\rho_{n_k}-\log\sigma_{n_k}) )

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle b_{\epsilon}\Tr(\rho_{n_k} (I-Q_{n_k}(b_{\epsilon})))+b\Tr(\rho_{n_k} (Q_{n_k}(b_{\epsilon})-Q_{n_k}(b)))

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n_{k}}\Tr(\rho_{n_k} Q_{n_k}(b)
\log\Tr\sigma_{n_k}Q_{n_k}(b)).

(244)

Taking the limit
k\to\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{n_{k}}D(\rho_{n_{k}}\|\sigma_{n_{k}})=b_{\epsilon}(1-\epsilon)+b\epsilon.

(245)

Taking the limit

b_{\epsilon}\to D^{\dagger}(\epsilon|\vec{\rho}\|\vec{\sigma})

and
b\to\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}D(\rho_{n}\|\sigma_{n})
,
we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}D(\rho_{n}\|\sigma_{n})\geq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{n_{k}}D(\rho_{n_{k}}\|\sigma_{n_{k}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D^{\dagger}(\epsilon|\vec{\rho}\|\vec{\sigma})(1-\epsilon)+\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}D(\rho_{n}\|\sigma_{n})\epsilon

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle D^{\dagger}(\epsilon|\vec{\rho}\|\vec{\sigma})(1-\epsilon)+\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{n_{k}}D(\rho_{n_{k}}\|\sigma_{n_{k}})\epsilon,

(246)

which implies

\displaystyle D(0|\vec{\rho}\|\vec{\sigma})

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}D(\rho_{n}\|\sigma_{n})\geq D^{\dagger}(\epsilon|\vec{\rho}\|\vec{\sigma})

(247)

\displaystyle D(0|\vec{\rho}\|\vec{\sigma})

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{n_{k}}D(\rho_{n_{k}}\|\sigma_{n_{k}})\geq D^{\dagger}(\epsilon|\vec{\rho}\|\vec{\sigma}).

(248)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle D(0|\vec{\rho}\|\vec{\sigma})=D^{\dagger}(\epsilon|\vec{\rho}\|\vec{\sigma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}D(\rho_{n}\|\sigma_{n})=\liminf_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{n_{k}}D(\rho_{n_{k}}\|\sigma_{n_{k}}).

(249)

\square
