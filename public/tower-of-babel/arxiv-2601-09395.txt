Maximum number of one-element commutation classes of a permutation
Ricardo Mamede, José Luis Santos, Diogo Soares

Abstract
In this paper, we provide an upper bound for the number of one-element commutation classes of a permutation, that is, the number of reduced words in which no commutation can be applied. Using this upper bound, we prove a conjecture that relates the number of reduced words with the number of commutation classes of a permutation.



1
Introduction

Given a positive integer
n\geq 1
, let
\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
denote the
symmetric group
on
n+1
elements formed by all permutations of the set
[n+1]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n+1\}
with composition (read from the right) as group operation. To denote a permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
, we will use the
one-line notation

\sigma:=(\sigma(1),\sigma(2),\ldots,\sigma(n+1))
, as well as the classic
cycle notation
, where cycles are written in round brackets and the images separated by spaces. For instance,
\sigma=(3,4,1,5,2)\in\mathfrak{S}_{5}
can be expressed in cycle notation as
\sigma=(1\ 3)(2\ 4\ 5)
.

The symmetric group is a famous example of a more general family of groups called
Coxeter groups
, which are groups generated by a set of involutions (elements of order 2) subject to some specific relations (see
2
;
8
). In this case, the group
\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
is generated by the involutions
\{s_{i}:i\in[n]\}
, where
s_{i}:=(1,\ldots,i+1,i,\ldots,n+1)=(i\ i+1)
, which satisfy the following relations:

\displaystyle s_{i}s_{j}

\displaystyle=s_{j}s_{i},\;\text{if }|i-j|>1

(1)

\displaystyle s_{i}s_{i+1}s_{i}

\displaystyle=s_{i+1}s_{i}s_{i+1},\;i\in[n-1],

(2)

with (
1
) being called a
commutation
, and (
2
) a
braid relation
.

Every permutation
\sigma
can be written as a product
s_{i_{1}}s_{i_{2}}\cdots s_{i_{l}}
of generators, with
i_{j}\in[n]
for all
j\in[l]
. When
l
is minimal, we say that
s_{i_{1}}s_{i_{2}}\cdots s_{i_{l}}
is a
reduced decomposition
, and
i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{l}
a
reduced word
of
\sigma
. The integer
l:=\ell(\sigma)
is the
length
of
\sigma
, and it corresponds to the number of
inversion pairs
of
\sigma
, that is,

\ell(\sigma)=|\{(\sigma(i),\sigma(j)):i<j,\sigma(i)>\sigma(j)\}|.

We denote by
R(\sigma)
the set of reduced words of
\sigma
. As an example, if
\sigma=(3,4,2,1)
, then
\ell(\sigma)=5
and
R(\sigma)=~\{12132,12312,21232,21323,23123\}
, which corresponds to all possible sequences of indices of the generators for a reduced decomposition of
\sigma
.
A well-known result of Tits
15
states that two reduced words for the same permutation differ by a finite sequence of commutations and/or braid relations.

We can define an equivalence relation
\sim
on the set
R(\sigma)
by setting
\textbf{a}\sim\textbf{b}
if these words differ by a sequence of commutations. The equivalence classes generated by
\sim
are the
commutation classes
of
\sigma
, being the commutation class of
\textbf{a}\in R(\sigma)
denoted by
[\textbf{a}]
. The set of all commutation classes of
\sigma
is denoted by
C(\sigma)
. For
\sigma=(3,4,2,1)
, we have three distinct commutation classes:
[12132]=\{12132,12312\}
,
[21232]=\{21232\}
and
[21323]=\{21323,23123\}
. These objects were already considered by some authors and are related to other topics in Discrete Mathematics such as Higher Bruhat orders, pseudoline arrangements and rhombic tilings of polygons (see
7
;
4
;
3
;
1
;
9
;
5
).

In general, a commutation class may contain several reduced words. For instance, the number of reduced words inside the commutation class of the word
1\cdot 21\cdot 321\cdots n(n-1)\cdots 1
is equinumerous with the number of shifted standard staircase tableaux
11
, all of these words being reduced words for the longest permutation
w_{0}:=(n+1,n,\ldots,1)
. However, it can happen an extreme situation in which a reduced word is its entire commutation class, that is, a word
\textbf{a}\in R(\sigma)
satisfying
[\textbf{a}]=\{\textbf{a}\}
. A reduced word with such a property is called
one-element commutation class
, and we denote by
R_{\bullet}(\sigma)
the set of one-element commutation classes of
\sigma
. For instance, the word
\textbf{a}=21232
is the only one-element commutation class of
\sigma=(3,4,2,1)
. In the case of the longest element, we have
|R_{\bullet}(w_{0})|=4
for all
n\geq 3
, a result first obtained in
6
and then recovered by Tenner
14
. This enumeration was later generalized for an arbitrary involution by the authors
10
, showing that the number of one-element commutation classes of an involution is either
0
,
1
,
2
or
4
. An immediate consequence is that an involution cannot have more than four one-element commutation classes. Could it be possible for a general permutation to have more than four such words? The main result of this paper states that such a permutation cannot exist.

Theorem A

(Theorem
3.9
)
.

Let
n\geq 1
. Then, for all
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
we have
|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|\leq 4
.

This result is interesting just by itself as it shows that the number of words for which we can’t apply any commutation is very limited. Moreover, it can also be used to prove a conjecture proposed in
3
that relates the cardinalities of
R(\sigma)
and
C(\sigma)
.

Theorem B

(Theorem
4.2
)
.

For all
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
, we have
\displaystyle|C(\sigma)|\leq\frac{1}{2}|R(\sigma)|+1
.

This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the terminology from
14
;
10
relevant to one-element commutation classes, along with auxiliary results. In Section 3, we prove Theorem
A
. The key idea is to partition the set of permutations into two disjoint subsets according to a property of their one-element commutation classes, and then apply an inductive argument. Using the upper bound for
|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|
, Section 4 establishes Theorem
B
. Finally, Section 5 presents some open problems and possible directions for future research.

Preliminaries

Given a set
S
, denote by
S^{*}
the free monoid generated by
S
consisting of all finite words with letters in
S
, with concatenation as multiplication and the empty word as identity element. For our purposes,
S
will be either the set
[n]
or
[i,j]:=\{i,i+1,\ldots j\}

i\leq j
positive integers. By convention, we consider
[i,j]
to be the empty set if
i>j

subword
of a word
\textbf{a}=i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{l}
is a word obtained from

by deleting some of its letters, while a
factor
is a subword formed by consecutive letters in

For a reduced word to be a one-element commutation class, some properties must be satisfied. A basic requirement follows from the fact that such words contain no available commutations. Since we can commute a pair of letters in a reduced word of a permutation whenever there is a factor
ij
with
|i-j|>1
, we have that
R_{\bullet}(\sigma)
is the subset of
R(\sigma)
formed by the reduced words where every factor of length 2 is of the form
i(i+1)
or
(i+1)i
. A word with this property is called a
word formed by consecutive integers
. For instance, the word
\textbf{a}=2345432123456765434
is one such example. Additionally, one-element commutation classes must satisfy some technical properties, which will be important throughout the paper.

The characterization of words formed by consecutive integers that are reduced can be given in the following way.

Theorem 2.2

(
10
)
.

Let
\textbf{a}\in[n]^{*}
be a word formed by consecutive integers and let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
be the corresponding permutation. Then,
\textbf{a}\in R_{\bullet}(\sigma)
if and only if there is no factor of

Theorems

and

will be important to prove the main result of this paper. We end this section with some more auxiliary results.

Lemma 2.3

(
13
)
.

Fix a permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
. The following statements are equivalent:

1.

There is a word
\textbf{a}\in R(\sigma)
containing a letter

.

2.

Every word
\textbf{a}\in R(\sigma)
contains a letter
i

3.

\{\sigma(1),\ldots,\sigma(i)\}\neq\{1,\ldots,i\}
.

4.

\{\sigma(i+1),\ldots,\sigma(n+1)\}\neq\{i+1,\ldots,n+1\}
.

We also recall the classical definition of fixed point of a permutation.

Definition 2.6
.

For

and

is a

of

Every

and

The proof of the following result can be found in

Lemma 2.4

(
10
)
.

Let

a reduced word for some

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}

\textbf{a}=\textbf{p}\,\widehat{j}\,\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{4.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{5.50002pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}\,\widehat{M}\,\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{i}}$}}}\hss}{i}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{i}}$}}}\hss}{i}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{4.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{i}}$}}}\hss}{i}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{5.50002pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{i}}$}}}\hss}{i}}\,\textbf{q}
and
\textbf{b}=\textbf{p}^{\prime}\,\widehat{j}\,\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{4.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{5.50002pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}\,\widehat{M}\,\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{i}}$}}}\hss}{i}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{i}}$}}}\hss}{i}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{4.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{i}}$}}}\hss}{i}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{5.50002pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{i}}$}}}\hss}{i}}\textbf{q}^{\prime}
, for some words
\textbf{p},\textbf{p}^{\prime}\in[m+1,j-1]^{*}
and
\textbf{q},\textbf{q}^{\prime}\in[i+1,M-1]^{*}
. Since
\textbf{a}\neq\textbf{b}
we have either
\textbf{p}\neq\textbf{p}^{\prime}
or
\textbf{q}\neq\textbf{q}^{\prime}
. Given the restrictions of the words
\textbf{p},\textbf{p}^{\prime},\textbf{q}
and
\textbf{q}^{\prime}
, we have
\varphi(\textbf{a})\neq\varphi(\textbf{b})
, showing that
\varphi
is injective. Therefore,
|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|\leq|R_{\bullet}(\sigma\gamma^{-1})|
.
∎

We have now all the ingredients to prove the main result of this paper.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
n\geq 1
. Then, for all
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
we have
|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|\leq 4
.

Proof.

We use induction on
\ell(\sigma)
. If
\ell(\sigma)=1
, then
\sigma
contains only one reduced word and the result follows.

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
and suppose that
|R_{\bullet}(\pi)|\leq 4
for every
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
satisfying
\ell(\pi)<\ell(\sigma)
. We may assume that
|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|>0
, otherwise the result is trivially satisfied. Consider
m
and
M+1
to be the minimum and maximum non-fixed points of
\sigma
, respectively. If
R_{\bullet}(\sigma)
contains an oscillation, then
|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|\leq 4
by Theorem
3.5
and we are done. Otherwise, if
\textbf{a}\in R_{\bullet}(\sigma)
and
a
is not an oscillation, then there are words
\textbf{p}\in[m+1,j-1]^{*}
,
\textbf{q}\in[i+1,M-1]^{*}
and integers
i\neq m,j\neq M
such that

\textbf{a}=\textbf{p}\,\widehat{j}\,\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{4.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{5.50002pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}\,\widehat{M}\,\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{i}}$}}}\hss}{i}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{i}}$}}}\hss}{i}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{4.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{i}}$}}}\hss}{i}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{5.50002pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{i}}$}}}\hss}{i}}\,\textbf{q}

or

\textbf{a}=\textbf{q}\,\,\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{i}}$}}}\hss}{i}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{i}}$}}}\hss}{i}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{4.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{i}}$}}}\hss}{i}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{5.50002pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{i}}$}}}\hss}{i}}\,\widehat{M}\,\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{4.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{5.50002pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}\,\widehat{j}\,\textbf{p},

by Lemma
3.6
. It is enough to consider the first case, since the second case follows from the first one applying the reverse word. If
j<i
, then
|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|=1<4
, by Proposition
3.7
. If
j\geq i
, then there is
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
such that
|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|\leq|R_{\bullet}(\pi)|
with
\ell(\pi)<\ell(\sigma)
, by Proposition
3.8
. Using the induction hypothesis,
|R_{\bullet}(\pi)|\leq 4
and therefore
|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|\leq 4
.
∎

4
An application

The bound obtained in Theorem
3.9
will help us prove a conjecture proposed in
3
that states the following.

Conjecture 4.1

(Conjecture 7.2 of
3
)
.

For all
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
,
\displaystyle 0\leq|C(\sigma)|\leq\frac{1}{2}|R(\sigma)|+1
.

Note that
C(\sigma)
is the disjoint union of the set of commutation classes with more than one reduced word and the set of one-element commutation classes, that is,

\displaystyle C(\sigma)=\{[\textbf{a}]\in C(\sigma):|[\textbf{a}]|>1\}\cup\{[\textbf{a}]\in C(\sigma):|[\textbf{a}]|=1\}.

(3)

Clearly,
|\{[\textbf{a}]\in C(\sigma):|[\textbf{a}]|=1\}|=|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|
. Since the set
C(\sigma)
partitions
R(\sigma)
, from (
3
) we have at least
2|C(\sigma)\setminus R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|+|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|
different reduced words, implying that

2|C(\sigma)|-|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|\leq|R(\sigma)|,

which simplifies to

\displaystyle|C(\sigma)|\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(|R(\sigma)|+|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|\right).

(4)

From Theorem
3.9
,
|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|\leq 4
and we get

|C(\sigma)|\leq\frac{1}{2}|R(\sigma)|+2,

which almost proves Conjecture
4.2
. For us to have a “+1” instead, we need the following.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
with
m
and
M+1
the minimum and maximum non-fixed points of
\sigma
, respectively. Suppose that
|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|>0
and
\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{4.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{5.50002pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}\,\widehat{M},\widehat{M}\,\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{4.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{5.50002pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}\notin R_{\bullet}(\sigma)
. If
M-m\geq 4
, then there is
[\textbf{a}]\in C(\sigma)
such that
|[\textbf{a}]|\geq 4
.

Proof.

Since
|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|>0
, Lemma
3.1
implies that either
\sigma(m)=M+1
or
\sigma(M+1)=m
. Assume the latter holds (the other case is analogous), and let
\textbf{a}\in R_{\bullet}(\sigma)
. Again from Lemma
3.1
, we can write
a
as

\textbf{a}=\textbf{p}\,\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{4.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{5.50002pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}\,\widehat{M}\,\textbf{q}

for some words
\textbf{p}\in[m+1,M]^{*}
and
\textbf{q}=[m,M-1]^{*}
. By hypothesis,
\textbf{a}\neq\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{4.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{5.50002pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}\,\widehat{M}
, and thus at least one of the words
p
or
q
is not empty. If
p
is not empty (the other case is similar), the fact that
M-m\geq 4
implies that

\textbf{a}=\textbf{p}^{\prime}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}(m+1)\ m(m+1)}(m+2)(m+3)(m+4)\cdots M\ \textbf{q},

for some word
\textbf{p}^{\prime}
. Performing a braid relation using the red factor of
a
, we obtain the word

\textbf{a}^{\prime}=\textbf{p}^{\prime}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}m\ (m+1)m}(m+2)(m+3)(m+4)\cdots M\ \textbf{q}.

But then, the rightmost letter
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}m}
can commute with the factor
(m+2)(m+3)(m+4)
in
\textbf{a}^{\prime}
, generating three additional reduced words that belong to
[\textbf{a}^{\prime}]
. These can be obtained from
\textbf{a}^{\prime}
in the following way:

\displaystyle\textbf{a}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\textbf{p}^{\prime}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}m\ (m+1)m}(m+2)(m+3)(m+4)\cdots M\ \textbf{q}

\displaystyle\sim\textbf{p}^{\prime}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}m\ (m+1)}(m+2){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}m}(m+3)(m+4)\cdots M\ \textbf{q}

\displaystyle\sim\textbf{p}^{\prime}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}m\ (m+1)}(m+2)(m+3){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}m}(m+4)\cdots M\ \textbf{q}

\displaystyle\sim\textbf{p}^{\prime}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}m\ (m+1)}(m+2)(m+3)(m+4){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}m}\cdots M\ \textbf{q}.

Therefore,
|[\textbf{a}^{\prime}]|\geq 4
and we have the result.
∎

Before proceeding to the proof of Conjecture
4.1
, we need the following definition.

Definition 4.1

(
12
)
.

Let
\textbf{a}=i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{l}\in R(\sigma)
. For
k\geq 1
a positive integer, the

of

\textbf{a}^{k}:=(i_{1}+k)(i_{2}+k)\cdots(i_{l}+k)\in R((1,\ldots,k,\sigma(1)+k,\sigma(2)+k,\ldots,\sigma(n+1)+k)).

Letting

, we have that the map

is a bijection between

and

, which also extends naturally to a bijection between

.

Theorem 4.2
.

For all
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}
, we have
\displaystyle 0\leq|C(\sigma)|\leq\frac{1}{2}|R(\sigma)|+1
.

Proof.

If
|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|=0
, then every commutation class for
\sigma
has at least two distinct elements, and thus
\displaystyle|C(\sigma)|\leq\frac{1}{2}|R(\sigma)|<\frac{1}{2}|R(\sigma)|+1
, by (
4
).

Suppose that
|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|>0
and let
m
and
M+1
be the minimum and maximum non-fixed points of
\sigma
, respectively. If either
\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{4.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{5.50002pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}\,\widehat{M}\in R_{\bullet}(\sigma)
or
\widehat{M}\,\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{4.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{5.50002pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}\in R_{\bullet}(\sigma)
, then there is only one reduced word for
\sigma
(that is,
|R(\sigma)|=1
) which will be one of previous, and consequently only one commutation class, implying that
\displaystyle|C(\sigma)|=|R(\sigma)|\leq\frac{1}{2}|R(\sigma)|+1
. Otherwise, if
\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{4.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{5.50002pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}\,\widehat{M}\notin R_{\bullet}(\sigma)
and
\widehat{M}\,\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\displaystyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{3.77779pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\textstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{4.81111pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{5.50002pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\widehat{\hphantom{m}}$}}}\hss}{m}}\notin R_{\bullet}(\sigma)
, then we need to consider two separate cases.

Case 1
:
M-m\geq 4

From the previous proposition, there is
[\textbf{a}]\in C(\sigma)
such that
|[\textbf{a}]|\geq 4
. Then, the set
C(\sigma)
can be written as the disjoint union

C(\sigma)=\{[\textbf{b}]\in C(\sigma):|[\textbf{b}]|>1,[\textbf{b}]\neq[\textbf{a}]\}\cup\{[\textbf{a}]\}\cup\{[\textbf{b}]\in C(\sigma):|[\textbf{b}]|=1\}.

Since
C(\sigma)
partitions the set
R(\sigma)
, we have at least
2|C(\sigma)\setminus(\{[\textbf{a}]\}\cup R_{\bullet}(\sigma))|+4+|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|
different reduced words. Thus,

\displaystyle 2(|C(\sigma)|-1-|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|)+4+|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|\leq|R(\sigma)|\Leftrightarrow 2|C(\sigma)|-|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|+2\leq|R(\sigma)|,

implying that

2|C(\sigma)|\leq|R(\sigma)|+|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|-2.

Since
|R_{\bullet}(\sigma)|\leq 4
by Theorem
3.9
, we have
2|C(\sigma)|\leq|R(\sigma)|+2
which implies the result.

Case 2
:
M-m<4

Consider
\pi=(\sigma(m)-m+1,\sigma(m+1)-m+1,\ldots,\sigma(M+1)-m+1)
. Given the fact that
m
and
M+1
are minimum and maximum non-fixed points of
\sigma
, we have
\{\sigma(m),\sigma(m+1),\ldots,\sigma(M+1)\}=[m,M+1]
, and so
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{M-m+2}
. Then, the map
\textbf{a}\mapsto\textbf{a}^{m-1}
is a bijection between
R(\pi)
and
R(\sigma)
, which extends naturally to a bijection between
C(\pi)
and
C(\sigma)
. Since
M-m<4
, we have that
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
with
k<6
. Therefore, the conjecture holds in this case if it holds for all permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{k}
with
k<6
, which was checked computationally.
∎
