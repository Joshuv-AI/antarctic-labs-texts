Changing Bases with Pipe Dream Combinatorics
Anna Weigandt

Abstract
. Lascoux and Schützenberger introduced Schubert and Grothendieck polynomials to study the cohomology and K-theory of the complete flag variety. We present explicit combinatorial rules for expressing Grothendieck polynomials in the basis of Schubert polynomials, and vice versa, using the bumpless pipe dreams (BPDs) of Lam, Lee, and Shimozono. A key advantage of BPDs is that they are naturally back stable, which allows us to give a combinatorial formula for expanding back stable Grothendieck polynomials in terms of back stable Schubert polynomials. We also provide pipe dream interpretations for the rules originally given by Lenart (Grothendieck to Schubert) and Lascoux (Schubert to Grothendieck), which were previously formulated in terms of binary triangular arrays. We give new proofs of these results, relying on Knutson’s co-transition recurrences. As a consequence, we obtain a formula for expanding Grothendieck polynomials into Schubert polynomials using chains in Bruhat order. The key connection between the pipe dream and BPD change of basis formulas is the canonical bijection of Gao and Huang. We show that co-permutations are preserved by this map.

1.
Introduction

The
complete flag variety

\mathsf{Flags}(n)
is the space of nested sequences of vector subspaces of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
of the form

V_{1}\subset V_{2}\subset\cdots\subset V_{n}=\mathbb{C}^{n},

where
\dim(V_{i})=i
for all
i
.
The flag variety has distinguished subvarieties called
Schubert varieties
, which are indexed by permutations in the
symmetric group

S_{n}
.
Each Schubert variety determines a class
\sigma_{w}\in\mathrm{H}^{*}(\mathsf{Flags}(n))
in the cohomology ring of
\mathsf{Flags}(n)
.
These
Schubert classes
form a linear basis for
\mathrm{H}^{*}(\mathsf{Flags}(n))
.
A central problem in Schubert calculus is to find a combinatorial rule for the structure constants
c_{u,v}^{w}
in the product

\sigma_{u}\cdot\sigma_{v}=\sum_{w\in S_{n}}c_{u,v}^{w}\sigma_{w}.

The Borel isomorphism identifies
\mathrm{H}^{*}(\mathsf{Flags}(n))
with
\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/I
, where
I
is the ideal generated by nonconstant elementary symmetric polynomials.
Lascoux and Schützenberger
[
LS82a
]
introduced
Schubert polynomials

\mathfrak{S}_{w}
, which are representatives for the Schubert classes.
There is an analogous story in K-theory.
Here,
Grothendieck polynomials

\mathfrak{G}_{w}
serve as representatives for the classes of structure sheaves in the K-theory of
\mathsf{Flags}(n)

[
LS82b
]
.

The purpose of this article is to present combinatorial formulas for expanding Grothendieck polynomials in the basis of Schubert polynomials, and vice versa. We give formulas for changing bases between Schubert and Grothendieck polynomials as sums over the
bumpless pipe dreams (BPDs)
of Lam, Lee, and Shimozono
[
LLS21
]
. We also translate the change of basis formulas of Lenart
[
Len99
]
and Lascoux
[
Las04
]
, originally stated in terms of binary triangular arrays, into sums over
pipe dreams
.
Pipe dreams and BPDs are certain tilings of the
n\times n
grid, both of which are used in formulas for computing the monomial expansions of Schubert and Grothendieck polynomials
[
BJS93
,
BB93
,
FS94
,
FK94
,
FK96
,
LLS21
,
Wei21
]
.
Pipe dreams have greatly contributed to our combinatorial understanding of Schubert calculus, see, for instance,
[
KM05a
,
KM05b
,
AS17
,
Knu22
]
.
BPDs were originally developed to study back stable Schubert calculus
[
LLS21
,
LLS23
]
.
Since then, BPDs have become an important tool in Schubert calculus see, e.g.,
[
BS22
,
Hua23a
,
Hua23b
,
HS24
,
LOT+25
]
.

Certain properties of Schubert and Grothendieck polynomials appear more transparently in terms of pipe dreams or bumpless pipe dreams.
For instance, the transition recurrence on Schubert and Grothendieck polynomials has a simple bijective explanation in terms of BPDs (see
[
Las02
,
Wei21
]
), whereas the co-transition recurrence of Knutson
[
Knu22
]
is compatible with pipe dreams.
Similarly, pipe dreams index components in antidiagonal Gröbner degenerations of
matrix Schubert varieties

[
KM05a
]
, while BPDs govern certain diagonal degenerations
[
KMY09
,
HPW22
,
Kle23
,
KW23
]
.
We show that the BPD and pipe dream change of basis formulas are closely connected via the canonical bijection from pipe dreams to bumpless pipe dreams of Gao and Huang
[
GH23
]
.

Our first main theorem states that we may expand the Grothendieck polynomial

as a signed sum over Schubert polynomials, indexed by permutations associated to the reduced co-BPDs arising from BPDs of

Theorem 1.1
.

Given
w\in S_{n}
, we have

\mathfrak{G}_{w}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{B}\in\overline{\sf BPD}(w)\\
\check{\mathcal{B}}\text{ is reduced}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\ell(\delta(\check{\mathcal{B}}))-\ell(w)}\mathfrak{S}_{\delta(\check{\mathcal{B}})}.

Although this formula involves signs, they alternate with degree, and the monomial expansion is cancellation free.

We also show that each Schubert polynomial
\mathfrak{S}_{w}
expands as a positive sum of Grothendieck polynomials, indexed by permutations arising from the co-BPDs associated to the reduced BPDs of
w
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Given
w\in S_{n}
, we have

\mathfrak{S}_{w}=\sum_{\mathcal{B}\in{\sf BPD}(w)}\mathfrak{G}_{\delta(\check{\mathcal{B}})}.

At the level of monomials, this formula is not generally cancellation free.

We present new proofs of
1.5
and
1.6
.
Our approach relies on the combinatorial co-transition recurrence of
[
Knu22
]
.
One advantage of these new proofs is that they lead to a method for constructing the pipe dreams for
w
that have reduced co-pipe dreams recursively in terms of certain chains in Bruhat order (see
Section

6.3
).
This chain theoretic construction is similar in spirit to the climbing chains of
[
BS02
]
and
[
LRS06
]
, which were used to give formulas for Schubert and Grothendieck polynomials, respectively.
Though we still sum over a subset of these chains, there are typically fewer chains to check than there are pipe dreams for
w
.

Our new results on BPDs follow from transferring properties from pipe dreams to BPDs.
We use the canonical bijection between these objects, given by Gao and Huang
[
GH23
]
.
Our key observation is the following:

Theorem 1.9
.

Suppose
\mathcal{P}\in{\sf Pipes}_{n}
maps to
\mathcal{B}\in{\sf BPD}_{n}
under the column-weight preserving canonical bijection of Gao–Huang.
Then
\delta(\check{\mathcal{P}})=\delta(\check{\mathcal{B}})
.

In other words,
1.9
states that the canonical bijection preserves
co-permutations
.
This statement provides evidence that, despite pipe dreams and BPDs exhibiting a number of different properties, there is underlying structure governing aspects of their behavior in parallel.
We prove
1.9
by analyzing the co-transition recurrence on both pipe dreams and BPDs, focusing on how co-transition modifies the associated co-objects.

1.2.
Connections to the literature

In addition to the binary triangular array formulas of
[
Len99
,
Las04
]
for changing bases between Schubert and Grothendieck polynomials, Lenart
[
Len00
]
also gave a tableau formula for the special case of symmetric Grothendieck polynomials and
Schur polynomials
.
The
stable Grothendieck polynomials
of
[
FK94
]
arise as specializations of back stable Grothendieck polynomials.
The results of Lenart
[
Len00
]
naturally extend to give expansions of stable Grothendieck polynomials indexed by partition shapes into
Schur functions
, and vice versa.
Chan and Pflueger
[
CP21
]
generalized this result to provide analogous expansions for
skew stable Grothendieck polynomials
into
skew Schur functions
, and vice versa.
Skew stable Grothendieck polynomials (and likewise skew Schur functions) are not linearly independent, thus the resulting expansions are not canonical.
It would be interesting to compare the result of specializing
Theorem

8.1
with the work of Chan–Pflueger, but we do not pursue this direction here.

The expansion of Grothendieck polynomials into the Schubert basis has been a useful tool for studying related algebraic and geometric questions.
For instance, Monical, Tokcan, and Yong conjectured that Grothendieck polynomials have saturated Newton polytopes
[
MTY19
]
. In the special case of symmetric Grothendieck polynomials, Escobar and Yong
[
EY17
]
proved the conjecture using Lenart’s tableau change of basis formula.
Although there has been additional progress
[
MS20
,
CCMM24
]
, the full conjecture remains open. Lenart’s formula was also used to prove a combinatorial rule for computing the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity of Grassmannian matrix Schubert varieties
[
RRR+21
]
.
A number of open questions about the monomial support of Grothendieck polynomials remain, see, for example,
[
MSS24
]
.
Additional understanding of the Grothendieck to Schubert expansion may be useful for studying these problems.

2.
Background

Write
\mathbb{Z}_{+}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
. Given
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we write
[m]=\{i\in\mathbb{Z}:1\leq i\leq m\}
and
[m,n]=\{i\in\mathbb{Z}:m\leq i\leq n\}
.
Many of our objects are tilings of an
n\times n
grid.
We use matrix coordinates to refer to the cells in the grid: the cell
(i,j)
sits in the
i
th row from the top of the grid and the
j
th column from the left.

2.1.
The symmetric group

In this section, we recall basic facts about the
symmetric group
.
We refer the reader to
[
Man01
]
for background.

We write
S_{n}
for the
symmetric group
on
n
letters, i.e., the set of bijections from
[n]
to itself, with multiplication defined by composition of functions.
We often represent
w\in S_{n}
using one-line notation as
w=w_{1}\,w_{2}\,\cdots\,w_{n}
, where
w_{i}=w(i)
for all
i\in[n]
.

The
permutation matrix
of
w\in S_{n}
is the
n\times n
matrix with ones in positions
(i,w(i))
for all
i\in[n]
, and zeros elsewhere.
When convenient, we conflate
w
with its permutation matrix.
We sometimes represent permutation matrices visually by drawing an
n\times n
grid with dots in cells
(i,w(i))
for all
i\in[n]
. Call this the
graph
of a permutation.
We may strike out all cells that lie weakly to the right and weakly below each dot; the set of coordinates of the remaining cells is the
Rothe diagram
of
w
, which we denote by
{\rm D}(w)
.

Example 2.1
.

Let
w=52413
.

By striking out cells to the right and below each dot in the grid pictured above, we see that

{\rm D}(w)=\{(1,1),(1,2),(1,3),(1,4),(2,1),(3,1),(3,3)\}.\qed

Given
w\in S_{n}
, we define
c_{w}(i)=\#\{j\in[n]:i<j\text{ and }w(i)>w(j)\}
. Equivalently,
c_{w}(i)
is the number of cells in row
i
of the Rothe diagram of
w
.
The
Lehmer code
of
w
is the tuple
c_{w}=(c_{w}(1),\ldots,c_{w}(n))
.
The map from permutations to their Lehmer codes is a bijection from
S_{n}
to
\{(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n}):c_{i}\leq n-i\text{ for all }i\in[n]\}.

Let
s_{i}=(i\,i+1)\in S_{n}
denote the simple transposition that exchanges
i
and
i+1
.
Also, write
t_{i,j}=(i\,j)\in S_{n}
for the transposition that exchanges
i
and
j
.
Given a tuple of positive integers
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
, we say that
\mathbf{a}
is a
word
for
w
if
s_{a_{1}}\cdots s_{a_{k}}=w
, and a
reduced word
for
w
if
\mathbf{a}
is a minimum length word for
w
.
If
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
is a reduced word for
w
, then
k
is the
Coxeter length
of
w
, and we write
\ell(w)=k
.

The
Bruhat order
on the symmetric group is the poset that is the transitive closure of the covering relations
u<v
if there exist
i,j
such that
ut_{i,j}=v
and
\ell(u)+1=\ell(v)
.
Given
w\in S_{n}
and
i\in[n-1]
, we say that
i
is a
descent
of
w
if
ws_{i}<w
.
Write
\mathrm{des}(w)=\{i:ws_{i}<w\}
.
We say that
i\in[n-1]
is an
ascent
of
w
if
ws_{i}>w
.

To each permutation
w\in S_{n}
, we associate a
rank function

\mathrm{rk}_{w}:[0,n]\times[0,n]\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
defined by

\mathrm{rk}_{w}(a,b)=\#\{i:i\in[a]\text{ and }w(i)\in[b]\}.

The map from permutations to their rank functions is bijective.
Given
u,v\in S_{n}
, if there exists a least upper bound of
\{u,v\}
in
S_{n}
, we denote it by
u\vee v
and call it the
join
of
u
and
v
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
u,v,w\in S_{n}
.

(1)

u\leq v
if and only if
\mathrm{rk}_{u}(a,b)\geq\mathrm{rk}_{v}(a,b)
for all
(a,b)\in[0,n]\times[0,n]
.

(2)

If
\mathrm{rk}_{w}(a,b)=\min(\mathrm{rk}_{u}(a,b),\mathrm{rk}_{v}(a,b))
for all
(a,b)\in[0,n]\times[0,n]
, then
w=u\vee v
.

Proof.

(1) This is the Ehresmann criterion for Bruhat order; see, e.g.,
[
BB05
, Theorem 2.1.5]
.

(2) Suppose
\mathrm{rk}_{w}(a,b)=\min(\mathrm{rk}_{u}(a,b),\mathrm{rk}_{v}(a,b))
for all
(a,b)\in[0,n]\times[0,n]
. By Part (1), this implies
w
is an upper bound of both
u
and
v
.

Suppose that
w^{\prime}\leq w
is an upper bound of
u
and
v
. Then, again by Part (1), we have

\mathrm{rk}_{w^{\prime}}(a,b)\leq\mathrm{rk}_{u}(a,b),\mathrm{rk}_{v}(a,b)
which implies
\mathrm{rk}_{w^{\prime}}(a,b)\leq\min(\mathrm{rk}_{u}(a,b),\mathrm{rk}_{v}(a,b))
for all
(a,b)\in[0,n]\times[0,n]
.

Because

\min(\mathrm{rk}_{u}(a,b),\mathrm{rk}_{v}(a,b))=\mathrm{rk}_{w}(a,b)\leq\mathrm{rk}_{w^{\prime}}(a,b)\leq\min(\mathrm{rk}_{u}(a,b),\mathrm{rk}_{v}(a,b)),

we conclude that
\mathrm{rk}_{w^{\prime}}(a,b)=\mathrm{rk}_{w}(a,b)
for all
(a,b)\in[0,n]\times[0,n]
, and therefore
w=w^{\prime}
.
∎

It will sometimes be useful to consider permutations as elements of
S_{+}
, the set of bijections from
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
to itself such that all but finitely many elements are fixed points.
There is a natural embedding of
S_{n}
into
S_{+}
via the map
w\mapsto\tilde{w}
, where

\tilde{w}(i)=\begin{cases}w(i),&\text{if }i\in[n],\\
i,&\text{if }i>n.\end{cases}

Conversely, each
w\in S_{+}
may be represented by some permutation in
S_{N}
, for
N
sufficiently large.
In this case, we write
w\in S_{N}
.
The definitions of Bruhat order, reduced words, Coxeter length, Lehmer code, rank function, etc., all extend naturally to
S_{+}
.

2.2.
Hecke actions

The
0
-Hecke monoid

\mathcal{H}_{n}
is the free monoid on generators
\{\tau_{i}:i\in[n-1]\}
, subject to the relations:

(1)

\tau_{i}^{2}=\tau_{i}
,

(2)

\tau_{i}\tau_{j}=\tau_{j}\tau_{i}
if
|i-j|>1
, and

(3)

\tau_{i}\tau_{i+1}\tau_{i}=\tau_{i+1}\tau_{i}\tau_{i+1}
.

There is an action
*
of
\mathcal{H}_{n}
on
S_{n}
defined by

w*\tau_{k}=\begin{cases}w&\text{if }ws_{k}<w,\\
ws_{k}&\text{if }ws_{k}>w.\end{cases}

The
Demazure product
of a word is defined recursively as follows:
\delta(\emptyset)=\mathrm{id}
, and
\delta((a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}))=\delta((a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1}))*\tau_{a_{k}}
.
Note that if
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
is a reduced word, then
\delta(\mathbf{a})=s_{a_{1}}s_{a_{2}}\cdots s_{a_{k}}
.

Also, given
w\in S_{n}
and
k\in[n-1]
, we define

w\square\tau_{k}=\begin{cases}w&\text{if }ws_{k}>w,\\
ws_{k}&\text{if }ws_{k}<w.\end{cases}

The operator
\square
is closely related to
*
.
Write
w_{0}
for the
longest permutation
in
S_{n}
, that is,
w_{0}=n\,n-1\,\ldots\,1
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
w\in S_{n}
and
i\in[n-1]
.
Then the following hold:

(1)

(w*\tau_{i})w_{0}=(ww_{0})\square\tau_{n-i}
.

(2)

(w\square\tau_{i})w_{0}=(ww_{0})*\tau_{n-i}
.

(3)

w_{0}(w*\tau_{i})=(w_{0}w)\square\tau_{i}
.

(4)

w_{0}(w\square\tau_{i})=(w_{0}w)*\tau_{i}
.

Proof.

In one-line notation,
ww_{0}
is
w_{n}w_{n-1}\cdots w_{1}
, i.e., right multiplication by
w_{0}
reverses the string.
In particular, we have
w_{i}=(ww_{0})_{n-i+1}
and
w_{i+1}=(ww_{0})_{n-i}
.
Thus,
w_{i}<w_{i+1}
if and only if
(ww_{0})_{n-i}>(ww_{0})_{n-i+1}
.
Thus, parts (1) and (2) follow directly from this observation and the definitions of
*
and
\square
.

The second two statements follow from observing that
w
has a descent at
i
if and only if
w_{0}w
has an ascent at
i
.
∎

We record another lemma for later use.

Lemma 2.4
.

Fix a word
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k})
with
a_{i}\in[n-1]
for all
i
.
Let
\mathbf{b}=(n-a_{1},n-a_{2},\ldots,n-a_{k})
. Then
\delta(\mathbf{a})=w_{0}\delta(\mathbf{b})w_{0}
, where
w_{0}\in S_{n}
is the longest permutation.

Proof.

We proceed by induction. The statement is trivially true for the empty word.
Now let
\mathbf{a^{\prime}}=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k-1})
and
\mathbf{b^{\prime}}=(n-a_{1},n-a_{2},\ldots,n-a_{k-1})
.
Assume that
\delta(\mathbf{a^{\prime}})=w_{0}\delta(\mathbf{b^{\prime}})w_{0}
.
Then by applying
Lemma

2.3
, we have

\displaystyle\delta(\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle=\delta(\mathbf{a^{\prime}})*\tau_{a_{k}}

\displaystyle=(w_{0}\delta(\mathbf{b^{\prime}})w_{0})*\tau_{a_{k}}

\displaystyle=((w_{0}\delta(\mathbf{b^{\prime}}))\square\tau_{n-a_{k}})w_{0}

\displaystyle=w_{0}(\delta(\mathbf{b^{\prime}})*\tau_{n-a_{k}})w_{0}

\displaystyle=w_{0}\delta(\mathbf{b})w_{0},

as desired.
∎

2.3.
Schubert polynomials and Grothendieck polynomials

The symmetric group
S_{n}
acts on the ring
\mathbb{Z}[\beta][x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
by permuting the indices of the
x_{i}
’s. Specifically, given
w\in S_{n}
and
f\in\mathbb{Z}[\beta][x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, we define

w\cdot f=f(x_{w_{1}},x_{w_{2}},\ldots,x_{w_{n}}).

Given
i\in[n-1]
, we define the
Newton divided difference operator

N_{i}
by
N_{i}(f)=\frac{f-s_{i}\cdot f}{x_{i}-x_{i+1}}
.
We also define the
K-theoretic divided difference operator

\overline{N}^{(\beta)}_{i}
by

\overline{N}^{(\beta)}_{i}(f)=N_{i}\left((1+\beta x_{i+1})f\right).

Note that
N_{i}=\overline{N}^{(0)}_{i}
.

We use these operators to recursively define two families of polynomials:
Schubert polynomials

\{\mathfrak{S}_{w}:w\in S_{n}\}
and
\beta
-Grothendieck polynomials

\{\mathfrak{G}^{(\beta)}_{w}:w\in S_{n}\}
.
Both families start with the same initial condition:

\mathfrak{S}_{w_{0}}=\mathfrak{G}^{(\beta)}_{w_{0}}=x_{1}^{n-1}x_{2}^{n-2}\cdots x_{n-1}.

If
w_{i}<w_{i+1}
, then we define

\mathfrak{S}_{ws_{i}}=N_{i}(\mathfrak{S}_{w})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{G}^{(\beta)}_{ws_{i}}=\overline{N}^{(\beta)}_{i}(\mathfrak{G}^{(\beta)}_{w}).

Note that
\mathfrak{S}_{w}=\mathfrak{G}^{(0)}_{w}
.

The divided difference and K-theoretic divided difference operators satisfy the same braid and commutation relations as the simple reflections.
Hence, Schubert and
\beta
-Grothendieck polynomials are well-defined.
Furthermore,
\left(\overline{N}_{i}^{(\beta)}\right)^{2}=-\beta\overline{N}_{i}^{(\beta)}
and
N_{i}^{2}=0
.

As such,

(2.1)

N_{i}(\mathfrak{S}_{w})=\begin{cases}\mathfrak{S}_{ws_{i}}&\text{if }w>ws_{i},\\
0&\text{if }w<ws_{i}\end{cases}

and

(2.2)

\overline{N}^{(\beta)}_{i}(\mathfrak{G}^{(\beta)}_{w})=\begin{cases}\mathfrak{G}^{(\beta)}_{ws_{i}}&\text{if }w>ws_{i},\\
-\beta\mathfrak{G}^{(\beta)}_{w}&\text{if }w<ws_{i}.\end{cases}

There is a natural inclusion
\iota:S_{n}\hookrightarrow S_{n+1}
defined by

\iota(w)(k)=\begin{cases}w(k),&\text{if }1\leq k\leq n,\\
n+1,&\text{if }k=n+1.\end{cases}

Schubert and
\beta
-Grothendieck polynomials are stable with respect to this inclusion, that is,

\mathfrak{S}_{\iota(w)}=\mathfrak{S}_{w}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{G}^{(\beta)}_{\iota(w)}=\mathfrak{G}^{(\beta)}_{w}.

Because of this stability, we may regard these families of polynomials as being indexed by permutations
w\in S_{+}
.

We will often be interested in the specialization
\beta=-1
.
For convenience, we define
\overline{N}_{i}=\overline{N}^{(-1)}_{i}
and
\mathfrak{G}_{w}=\mathfrak{G}^{(-1)}_{w}
.
We call the polynomials
\mathfrak{G}_{w}

Grothendieck polynomials
, omitting the
\beta
.
In this case, we may write
Equation

2.2
more compactly as

(2.3)

\overline{N}_{i}(\mathfrak{G}_{w})=\mathfrak{G}_{w\square\tau_{i}}.

Note that
\mathfrak{G}_{w}(-\beta x_{1},-\beta x_{2},\ldots,-\beta x_{n})=(-\beta)^{\ell(w)}\mathfrak{G}_{w}^{(\beta)}
. Thus, there is no generality lost by working with Grothendieck polynomials as opposed to
\beta
-Grothendieck polynomials.

The sets of Schubert polynomials and Grothendieck polynomials each form a
\mathbb{Z}
-linear basis for
\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots]
.
As such, any Schubert polynomial can be expressed as a
\mathbb{Z}
-linear combination of Grothendieck polynomials, and vice versa.

3.
Planar histories

The main combinatorial objects in this paper are pipe dreams and bumpless pipe dreams. Both are special cases of
planar histories
.
For historical reasons, we draw pipe dreams so that pipes start at the top of the
n\times n
grid, and exit at the left edge.
Pipes in bumpless pipe dreams, on the other hand, start at the bottom of the grid and exit at the right.
We also introduce co-pipe dreams, in which pipes start at the bottom and exit on the left, and co-bumpless pipe dreams, in which pipes start at the top and exit on the right.
Despite the variety of orientations among these diagrams, they share certain fundamental similarities as planar histories.

We state our main definitions and facts in terms of
northeast planar histories
. We will also discuss
southeast
,
northwest
, and
southwest
planar histories, by modifying the definitions and results for NE planar histories as needed.

3.1.
Preliminaries on planar histories

A
pseudoline
is a piecewise linear curve in the plane.
A
northeast (NE) planar history

\mathcal{P}
is a collection of pseudolines in a rectangular region such that:

(1)

each pseudoline starts at the bottom edge of the rectangle,

(2)

each pseudoline ends at the right edge of the rectangle,

(3)

each pseudoline travels only north or east at all times, and

(4)

distinct pseudolines do not travel concurrently.

We may modify the conditions above as appropriate to define southeast (SE), northwest (NW), and southwest (SW) planar histories.

To align with the terminology for our primary objects of study (BPDs and pipe dreams), we refer to pseudolines as
pipes
from this point on.
We say that a planar history is
reduced
if each pair of pipes crosses at most one time.
We draw sharp turns in pipes as rounded bends.
This allows us to visually distinguish between the case when two pipes meet at a point but do not cross from the case where they do cross.
When two pipes meet but do not cross, this is sometimes known as an
osculating point
in the literature. See
[
Beh08
]
.

.

The map

Let

.

(2)

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}\in\overline{\sf Pipes}(w)
.

(1)

If
\mathcal{P}\mapsto\mathcal{Q}
is a ladder move or a K-theoretic ladder move, then
\mathcal{Q}\in\overline{\sf Pipes}(w)
.

(2)

The pipe dream
\mathcal{P}
can be obtained from the bottom pipe dream of

by a sequence of ladder moves and K-theoretic ladder moves.

Proof.

(1) Let
w\in S_{n}
and
\mathcal{P}\in\overline{\sf Pipes}(w)
. Suppose
\mathcal{P}\mapsto\mathcal{Q}
is a ladder move or K-theoretic ladder move. A (K-theoretic) ladder move may be obtained by applying a sequence of the moves described in

. Thus
w=\delta(\mathcal{P})=\delta(\mathcal{Q})
, and therefore
\mathcal{Q}\in\overline{\sf Pipes}(w)
.

(2) Let
\mathcal{P}\in\overline{\sf Pipes}(w)
.
If the crossings of
\mathcal{P}
are left-justified, then
\mathcal{P}
must be the bottom pipe dream of
w
.
Otherwise, there exists some
(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}
with
j>1
such that
(i,j-1)\notin\mathcal{P}
.
Choose
i
to be maximal with this property. Now let

be the smallest index such that
(i^{\prime},j)\notin\mathcal{P}
.
Then we have
[i+1,i^{\prime}-1]\times[j-1,j]\subseteq\mathcal{P}
.
If
(i^{\prime},j-1)\in\mathcal{P}

\mathcal{P}
by removing the crossing at position
(i,j)
.
If instead
(i^{\prime},j-1)\notin\mathcal{P}
, then we may apply an inverse ladder move to
\mathcal{P}
by removing the crossing at
(i,j)
and adding a crossing at
(i^{\prime},j-1)
.

We may iterate this process until we reach a pipe dream that is left-justified. At each step, we either delete a crossing or move a crossing down and to the left. Because
\overline{\sf Pipes}_{n}
is finite, the process terminates. By the first part, each intermediate pipe dream lies in
\overline{\sf Pipes}(w)
. Since the final pipe dream is left-justified, it must be the bottom pipe dream of

.

5.2.
Dominant parts of diagrams

A
partition
is a tuple of positive integers
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})
with
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{k}
.
Given a partition
\lambda
, we define the
Young diagram
of
\lambda
to be
{\mathbb{Y}}_{\lambda}=\{(i,j):j\in[\lambda_{i}],i\in[k]\}
.
An
addable cell
of
{\mathbb{Y}}_{\lambda}
is a cell
(i,j)
that satisfies all of the following conditions:

(1)

(i,j)\notin{\mathbb{Y}}_{\lambda}
,

(2)

if
i>1
, then
(i-1,j)\in{\mathbb{Y}}_{\lambda}
, and

(3)

if
j>1
, then
(i,j-1)\in{\mathbb{Y}}_{\lambda}
.

Intuitively,
(i,j)
is an addable cell of
{\mathbb{Y}}_{\lambda}
if adding it to the Young diagram of
\lambda
produces the Young diagram corresponding to a larger partition
\lambda^{\prime}
.

Given
U\subseteq[n]\times[n]
, the
dominant part
of
U
, denoted
\mathrm{dom}(U)
, is the largest set of the form
{\mathbb{Y}}_{\lambda}
such that
{\mathbb{Y}}_{\lambda}\subseteq U
(under the containment order). Define
\mathrm{dom}(w)=\mathrm{dom}({\rm D}(w))
.

has three addable cells,
(1,5),(2,2),
and
(4,1)
. Note that the addable cells are of the form
(i,w(i))
with
i\in\{1,2,4\}
.
∎

In the next lemma, we show that
\mathrm{dom}(\mathcal{P})
is invariant among elements
\mathcal{P}\in\overline{\sf Pipes}(w)
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
w\in S_{+}
.
The following statements hold.

(1)

If
(i,j)
is an addable cell of
\mathrm{dom}(w)
, then
j=w(i)
.

(2)

\mathrm{dom}(w)=\mathrm{dom}(\mathcal{P})
for all
\mathcal{P}\in\overline{\sf Pipes}(w)
.

(3)

If
(i,j)
is an addable cell of
\mathrm{dom}(w)
, then
(i,j)\notin\mathcal{P}
for all
\mathcal{P}\in\overline{\sf Pipes}(w)
.

Proof.

(1) Suppose
(i,j)
is an addable cell of
\mathrm{dom}(w)
. Because
(i,j)\notin\mathrm{dom}(w)
, but is adjacent to
\mathrm{dom}(w)
, we must have
(i,j)\notin{\rm D}(w)
.
Thus, in the Rothe diagram, the cell
(i,j)
must have a dot weakly above it or weakly to its left. Since the Rothe diagram is formed by removing all cells weakly to the right and below each dot in the graph of
w
, such a dot must occur at
(i,w(i))
, and hence
(i,j)=(i,w(i))
.

(2) Note first, if
\mathcal{P}\mapsto\mathcal{Q}
is a ladder move or K-theoretic ladder move, then
\mathrm{dom}(\mathcal{P})=\mathrm{dom}(\mathcal{Q})
. By
Lemma

5.2
, any
\mathcal{P}\in\overline{\sf Pipes}(w)
can be reached from the bottom pipe dream by such moves, so it suffices to verify the claim for the bottom pipe dream.

If
\mathcal{P}
is the bottom pipe dream of
w
, then by construction we have
\mathrm{dom}(w)\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(\mathcal{P})
. From Part (1) and the fact that for every
(i,j)=(i,w(i))
that is an addable cell of
{\rm D}(w)
, we have
(i,w(i))
is not a crossing in the bottom pipe dream of
w
, we conclude that
\mathrm{dom}(\mathcal{P})
cannot contain any partition shape larger than
\mathrm{dom}(w)
. Thus,
\mathrm{dom}(\mathcal{P})=\mathrm{dom}(w)
.

(3) Let
\mathcal{P}\in\overline{\sf Pipes}(w)
and suppose for contradiction that
(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}
.
From Part (2), we know that
\mathrm{dom}(\mathcal{P})=\mathrm{dom}(w)
, and that
(i,j)
is an addable cell for this partition.

By
Lemma

5.2
, there exists a sequence of inverse (K-theoretic) ladder moves taking
\mathcal{P}
to the bottom pipe dream of
w
.
Since
(i,j)
is not present in the bottom pipe dream, there must exist an intermediate pipe dream
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\in\overline{\sf Pipes}(w)
such that
(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
but
(i,j-1)\notin\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
.

But then
(i,j)
would not be an addable cell for
\mathrm{dom}(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})=\mathrm{dom}(w)
, contradicting our assumption.
∎

The next lemma follows readily from the interpretation of pipe dreams as subwords of an ambient word. We refer the reader to

for background.

Lemma 5.5

Suppose

If

(2)

(1) This follows directly from

(2) By

5.3.
Statements of the co-transition recurrences

In this section we state the algebraic and combinatorial co-transition recurrences of Knutson
[
Knu22
]
.
Given
w\in S_{+}
and a cell
(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\times\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, we say that
(a,b)
is a
neighbor
of
(i,j)
in
w
if all of the following conditions hold:

(1)

b=w(a)
,

(2)

i\leq a
and
j\leq b
,

(3)

(i,j)\neq(a,b)
, and

(4)

for all
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in[i,a]\times[j,b]-\{(i,j),(a,b)\}
, we have
b^{\prime}\neq w(a^{\prime})
.

Given
w\in S_{+}
, we define

(5.1)

\phi_{i}(w)=\{j:(j,w(j))\text{ is a neighbor of }(i,w(i))\},

and

(5.2)

\Phi_{i}(w)=\{wt_{i,j}\in S_{+}:j\in\phi_{i}(w)\}.

It follows directly from the definition of neighbor and
Equation

5.2
that

\Phi_{i}(w)=\{wt_{i,j}\in S_{+}:\ell(wt_{i,j})=\ell(w)+1\}.

We also define

\overline{\Phi}_{i}(w)=\{wt_{i,i_{k}}t_{i,i_{k-1}}\cdots t_{i,i_{1}}:\emptyset\neq\{i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\}\subseteq\phi_{i}(w)\}.

Observe that each nonempty subset of
\phi_{i}(w)
uniquely determines an element of
\overline{\Phi}_{i}(w)
.
For convenience, given
U\subseteq\phi_{i}(w)
with
U=\{i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\}
, we define

w_{U,i}=wt_{i,i_{k}}t_{i,i_{k-1}}\cdots t_{i,i_{1}}
. We also set
w_{\emptyset,i}=w
. It is immediate from the definitions that

(5.3)

\{w\}\cup\overline{\Phi}_{i}(w)=\{w_{U,i}:U\subseteq\phi_{i}(w)\}.

Note also that
\ell(w_{U,i})=\ell(w)+\#U
.

We now recall the combinatorial co-transition recurrence on pipe dreams from
[
Knu22
]
.
We state both the cohomological and K-theoretic versions.
Knutson proved the cohomological statement and sketched a proof of the K-theoretic recurrence
[
Knu22
]
.
We include additional details for completeness.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
w\in S_{+}
and suppose
(i,j)
is an addable cell of
\mathrm{dom}(w)
.

(1)

The map
\mathcal{P}\mapsto\mathcal{P}\cup\{(i,j)\}
defines a bijection from
\overline{\sf Pipes}(w)
to
\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{u\in\overline{\Phi}_{i}(w)}\overline{\sf Pipes}(u)
.

(2)

The map
\mathcal{P}\mapsto\mathcal{P}\cup\{(i,j)\}
defines a bijection from
{\sf Pipes}(w)
to
\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{u\in\Phi_{i}(w)}{\sf Pipes}(u)
.

We will prove
Proposition

5.6
in
Section

5.4
.
As an immediate consequence, we obtain the algebraic co-transition recurrences for Grothendieck and Schubert polynomials.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
w\in S_{+}
. Suppose
(i,j)
is an addable cell of
\mathrm{dom}(w)
.

(1)

\displaystyle x_{i}\mathfrak{G}_{w}=\sum_{u\in\overline{\Phi}_{i}(w)}(-1)^{\ell(u)-\ell(w)+1}\mathfrak{G}_{u}
.

(2)

x_{i}\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{w}=\sum_{u\in\Phi_{i}(w)}\mathfrak{S}_{u}
.

Proof.

This is immediate from
Theorem

4.3
and
Proposition

5.6
.
∎

We note that the first part
Proposition

5.7
is a special case of
[
Len03
, Theorem 3.1]
.
The second part can be derived from Monk’s rule.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let

and fix
U=\{i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\}\subseteq\phi_{i}(w)
with

.
Then
\mathrm{rk}_{w}(a,b)-\mathrm{rk}_{w_{U,i}}(a,b)=1

The
Boolean lattice

B_{n}
is the poset whose elements are the subsets of
[n]
, ordered by inclusion.
As the name suggests,
B_{n}
is also a lattice; join is given by taking unions of subsets, and meet by taking intersections.
If a lattice is isomorphic to
B_{n}
, for some
n
, we also refer to it as a Boolean lattice.

Given
u,v\in S_{+}
, let
[u,v]_{S_{+}}=\{w\in S_{+}:u\leq w\leq v\}
.
We now describe certain intervals in Bruhat order that govern the K-theoretic co-transition recurrences, and show that they are Boolean lattices.

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
w\in S_{+}
.

(1)

Given
U,U^{\prime}\subseteq\phi_{i}(w)
, we have
w_{U,i}\leq w_{U^{\prime},i}
if and only if
U\subseteq U^{\prime}
. As such, the set
\{w\}\cup\overline{\Phi}_{i}(w)
, considered as a subposet of the Bruhat order on
S_{+}
, is a Boolean lattice.

(2)

[w,\vee\Phi_{i}(w)]_{S_{+}}=\{w\}\cup\overline{\Phi}_{i}(w)
.

Proof.

(1) This follows directly from
Lemma

2.2
and
Lemma

5.9
.

(2) Let
v=\vee\Phi_{i}(w)
. Suppose that
u\in S_{+}
with
w\leq u\leq v
. If
u\in\{w\}\cup\overline{\Phi}_{i}(w)
we are done, so assume not.

Choose
n
large enough so that
\{w\}\cup\overline{\Phi}_{i}(w)\subseteq S_{n}
.
By
Lemma

2.2
, we have

\mathrm{rk}_{w}(a,b)\geq\mathrm{rk}_{u}(a,b)\geq\mathrm{rk}_{v}(a,b)\text{ for all }(a,b)\in[0,n]\times[0,n].

Thus,

(5.4)

0\leq\mathrm{rk}_{w}(a,b)-\mathrm{rk}_{u}(a,b)\leq\mathrm{rk}_{w}(a,b)-\mathrm{rk}_{v}(a,b)\text{ for all }(a,b)\in[0,n]\times[0,n].

By
Lemma

5.9
,
\mathrm{rk}_{w}(a,b)-\mathrm{rk}_{v}(a,b)
equals
1
if
(a,b)\in\bigcup_{h\in\phi_{i}(w)}[i,i_{h}-1]\times[w(i),w(i_{h})-1]
and is
0
otherwise.

Now let
U\subseteq\phi_{i}(w)
be a subset of maximal size such that
w_{U,i}\leq u
.
Because
w_{U,i}\leq u
, there exists indices
j<k
so that
w_{U,i}t_{j,k}\leq u
and
\ell(w_{U,i}t_{j,k})=\ell(w_{U,i})+1
. Because of
Equation

5.4
, we must have

(j,w_{U,i}(j))\in\bigcup_{h\in\phi_{i}(w)}[i,i_{h}-1]\times[w(i),w(i_{h})-1].

But then by
Lemma

5.8
, we must be in the situation where
w_{U,i}t_{j,k}=w_{U\cup\{k\},i}
with
j\in U
and
k\in\phi_{i}(w)-U
, which contradicts the maximality of the set
U
. Hence, no such
u\notin\{w\}\cup\overline{\Phi}_{i}(w)
can exist in the interval
[w,\vee\Phi_{i}(w)]_{S_{+}}
, and the claim follows.
∎

The next lemma describes how removing a cross from the dominant part of a pipe dream modifies the corresponding permutations.

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
u\in S_{+}
and
(i,j)\in\mathrm{dom}(u)
.
Let
i<i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}=u^{-1}(j)
be the row indices of the neighbors of
(i,j)
in
u
.
Let
w=ut_{i,i_{1}}t_{i,i_{2}}\cdots t_{i,i_{k}}
.
The following hold:

(1)

u\in\overline{\Phi}_{i}(w)
.

(2)

If
\mathcal{Q}\in\overline{\sf Pipes}(u)
, then
\mathcal{Q}-\{(i,j)\}\in\overline{\sf Pipes}(w)
.

Proof.

(1) By construction,
w(i)=j
. Thus,
(i,j)\notin\mathrm{dom}(w)
.
In particular,
(i,j)
is a northwest most dot in the graph of
w
, hence
(i,j)
is an addable cell for
\mathrm{dom}(w)
. Furthermore,
u=w_{U,i}
where
U=\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\}
, thus
u\in\overline{\Phi}_{i}(w)
.

(2)
We have
w\leq u
and hence, by
Lemma

5.5
, there exists
\mathcal{P}\in\overline{\sf Pipes}(w)
such that
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
.
Because
(i,j)
is an addable cell for
\mathrm{dom}(w)
, by
Lemma

5.4
,
(i,j)\notin\mathcal{P}
.
Therefore,
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}-\{(i,j)\}
.
Applying
Lemma

5.5
, we see that
\mathcal{Q}-\{(i,j)\}\in\overline{\sf Pipes}(v)
for some
v\in S_{n}
with
w\leq v\leq u
.

By
Lemma

5.10
, we have
v\in\{w\}\cup\overline{\Phi}_{i}(w)
.
By
Lemma

5.4
, because
(i,j)\notin\mathrm{dom}(\mathcal{Q})
, it follows that
(i,j)\notin\mathrm{dom}(v)
.
Because
(i,j)\in\mathrm{dom}(\pi)
for all
\pi\in\overline{\Phi}_{i}(w)
, we have
v=w
.
∎

We now are ready to prove the co-transition recurrences on pipe dreams.

Proof of
Proposition

5.6
.

(1) Let
\mathcal{P}\in\overline{\sf Pipes}(w)
.
Since
(i,j)
is an addable cell of
\mathrm{dom}(w)
, by
Lemma

5.4
, we know that
(i,j)\notin\mathcal{P}
.
Hence, the given map is injective.

Let
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{P}\cup\{(i,j)\}
.
We have
\mathcal{Q}\in\overline{\sf Pipes}(u)
for some
u\in S_{+}
.
Furthermore, by
Lemma

5.4
,
\mathrm{dom}(\mathcal{P})=\mathrm{dom}(w)
and hence
(i,j)
is an addable cell of
\mathrm{dom}(\mathcal{P})
. Therefore,
(i,j)\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathcal{Q})=\mathrm{dom}(u)
.
By
Lemma

5.11
, since
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{Q}-\{(i,j)\}\in\overline{\sf Pipes}(w)
, we have
u\in\overline{\Phi}_{i}(w)
.
Hence, the map is well-defined.

To show surjectivity, take some
u\in\overline{\Phi}_{i}(w)
and let
\mathcal{Q}\in\overline{\sf Pipes}(u)
. We can write
u=w_{U,i}
for some
U\subseteq\phi_{i}(w)
with
U\neq\emptyset
.
Let
i<i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}=u^{-1}(j)
be the row indices of the neighbors of
(i,j)
in
u
.
By construction, and the definition of neighbors, it must be that
U=\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\}
.
Furthermore,
w=ut_{i,i_{1}}t_{i,i_{2}}\cdots t_{i,i_{k}}
. Thus, by

5.11
, we have
\mathcal{P}\in\overline{\sf Pipes}(w)
.

(2) If
\pi\in S_{n}
and
\mathcal{P}\in{\sf Pipes}(\pi)

.
Furthermore, if
\mathcal{Q}\in\overline{\sf Pipes}(\pi)-{\sf Pipes}(\pi)

.

6.
Changing bases with pipe dreams

In this section, we give new proofs of Lenart’s rule
[
Len99
]
for expanding Grothendieck polynomials as sums of Schubert polynomials, as well as Lascoux’s rule
[
Las04
]
for expanding Schubert polynomials as sums of Grothendieck polynomials.
We have rephrased both rules in terms of pipe dreams and co-pipe dreams; up to reindexing the underlying triangular arrays, the statements are equivalent.
Lenart and Lascoux used noncommutative Schubert calculus in their arguments.
We give new proofs of both rules using the combinatorial co-transition recurrence on pipe dreams.
The supporting framework we develop for these proofs also yields a recursive method to generate the set of pipe dreams for
w\in S_{n}
whose co-pipe dreams are reduced (see
Section

6.3
).

6.1.

w\in S_{n}

j

(i,j)

\mathrm{dom}(w)

\alpha(w)=j

\alpha(w)

.

Let

(4) First we will show that the map is well-defined.

The following lemma describes how the the co-transition recurrence modifies co-permutations of pipe dreams.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let

with
w\neq w_{0}
.
Suppose
(i,j)
is an addable cell of
\mathrm{dom}(w)
with

and define

Proof.

Because we have chosen

to be the leftmost addable cell of
\mathrm{dom}(w)
with

, it follows that we obtain

from

by removing the last entry.
Furthermore, this entry is
n-i
. This is because when we map
\mathcal{P}
to
\check{\mathcal{P}}
, the bump at
(i,j)
in

becomes a crossing in

at

. When we flip vertically to get the word

, the crossing in

moves to
(n-(i+j)+1,j)
, which has associated letter

.
Thus,
\delta({\mathbf{a}}_{\check{\mathcal{P}}})=\delta({\mathbf{a}}_{\check{\mathcal{Q}}})*\tau_{n-i}
, and so,

\displaystyle\delta(\check{\mathcal{P}})

\displaystyle=\delta({\mathbf{a}}_{\check{\mathcal{P}}})w_{0}

(by
Lemma

4.4
)

\displaystyle=(\delta({\mathbf{a}}_{\check{\mathcal{Q}}})*\tau_{n-i})w_{0}

\displaystyle=(\delta({\mathbf{a}}_{\check{\mathcal{Q}}})w_{0})\square\tau_{i}

(by
Lemma

2.3
)

\displaystyle=\delta(\check{\mathcal{Q}})\square\tau_{i}

(by

4.4
)

as desired.

The next lemma provides a recursive method to construct pipe dreams for

, if

7.3.
The Canonical bijection

Gao and Huang
[
GH23
]
introduced a bijection between reduced pipe dreams and reduced bumpless pipe dreams.
Their bijection is canonical in the sense that it intertwines with certain natural bijective maps on pipe dreams and bumpless pipe dreams that provide combinatorial explanations for Monk’s rule. Furthermore, Gao and Huang showed that any bijection with this property is the canonical bijection.
We will use the column-weight preserving version of their map, which differs from the original only by transposition of diagrams.
Let
\kappa:{\sf Pipes}_{n}\rightarrow{\sf BPD}_{n}
denote the column-weight preserving canonical bijection of Gao–Huang. Column-weight preserving means that

\#\{i:(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}\}=\#\{i:(i,j)\in{\rm D}(\kappa(\mathcal{P}))\}

for all
j\in[n]
.
The map
\kappa
is also permutation preserving, that is,
\delta(\mathcal{P})=\delta(\kappa(\mathcal{P}))
.
For a detailed definition of this map, we refer the reader to
[
GH23
]
.

For our purposes, the following lemma is enough.

Lemma 7.8
.

Let
w\in S_{n}
with
w\neq w_{0}
. Set
j=\alpha(w)
and
i=w^{-1}(j)
.
Fix
\mathcal{B}\in{\sf BPD}(w)
.
Suppose
\mathcal{B}\mapsto\mathcal{B}^{\prime}

8.
Expansion of back stable Grothendieck polynomials into back stable Schubert polynomials

1.1
extends naturally to the setting of back stable Schubert calculus.
We refer the reader to
[
LLS21
,
LLS23
]
for background.

8.1.
The ring of back symmetric formal power series

Write
S_{\mathbb{Z}}
for the set of permutations of
\mathbb{Z}
that fix all but finitely many elements.
The set
S_{\mathbb{Z}}
is a group under composition of functions and is generated by the set of simple reflections
\{s_{i}=(i\,i+1):i\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.

Given
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}
with
p\leq q
, we say that
w\in S_{\mathbb{Z}}
is
supported on
[p,q]
if
w(i)=i
for all
i\notin[p,q]
.
Whenever
w\in S_{\mathbb{Z}}
is supported on
[p,q]
, we may regard
w
as an element of
S_{[p,q]}
, the group of permutations of the set
[p,q]=\{p,p+1,\ldots,q\}
.
In particular, if
w
is supported on
[1,n]
, we may identify it with its restriction to the symmetric group
S_{n}
.

Our setting involves certain formal power series in the variables
\{x_{i}:i\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.
We say
such a power series
f
has
bounded degree
if there exists
M\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that the total degree of each monomial in
f
is at most
M
.
Also,
f
has
bounded support
if there exists
N\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
x_{i}
does not appear in
f
for all
i>N
.
Let
R
denote the set of formal power series that have bounded degree and bounded support.

An element
f\in R
is
back symmetric
if there exists some integer
c
so that
s_{i}\cdot f=f
for all
i<c
.
We write
\overleftarrow{R}\subsetneq R
for the subset of
back symmetric formal power series
.
Lam, Lee, and Shimozono showed that

\overleftarrow{R}=\Lambda\otimes\mathbb{Q}[\ldots,x_{-1},x_{0},x_{1},\ldots],

where
\Lambda
denotes the ring of symmetric functions in the variables
\{\ldots,x_{-2},x_{-1},x_{0}\}

[
LLS21
, Proposition 3.1]
.

8.2.
Back stable Schubert and Grothendieck polynomials

Let
w\in S_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Write
{\sf red}(w)
for the set of reduced words for
w
, and let

{\sf hecke}(w)=\{\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{L}):\delta(\mathbf{a})=w\}

denote the set of
Hecke words
for
w
.
By
[
LLS21
, Theorem 3.2]
, the
back stable Schubert polynomial
has the monomial expansion

(8.1)

\overleftarrow{\mathfrak{S}}_{w}=\sum_{(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{\ell(w)})\in{\sf red}(w)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{1}\leq b_{2}\leq\cdots\leq b_{\ell(w)}\\
a_{i}\leq a_{i+1}\Rightarrow b_{i}<b_{i+1}\\
b_{i}\leq a_{i}\end{subarray}}x_{b_{1}}x_{b_{2}}\cdots x_{b_{\ell(w)}}.

We take this expansion as a definition here, although
\overleftarrow{\mathfrak{S}}_{w}
is equivalently the limit of certain shifts of Schubert polynomials.
Lam, Lee, and Shimozono showed that back stable Schubert polynomials form a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear basis for
\overleftarrow{R}

[
LLS21
, Theorem 3.5]
.

Similarly, by
[
LLS23
, Proposition 4.4]
, the
back stable Grothendieck polynomial
has the monomial expansion

(8.2)

\overleftarrow{\mathfrak{G}}_{w}=\sum_{(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{L})\in{\sf hecke}(w)}(-1)^{L-\ell(w)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{1}\leq b_{2}\leq\cdots\leq b_{L}\\
a_{i}\leq a_{i+1}\Rightarrow b_{i}<b_{i+1}\\
b_{i}\leq a_{i}\end{subarray}}x_{b_{1}}x_{b_{2}}\cdots x_{b_{L}}.

Again,
\overleftarrow{\mathfrak{G}}_{w}
is the limit of certain shifts of Grothendieck polynomials.
Note that for
w\neq\mathrm{id}
,
\overleftarrow{\mathfrak{G}}_{w}
is not an element of
R
, since it is not of bounded degree.
However, the degree
d
component of
\overleftarrow{\mathfrak{G}}_{w}
is a back symmetric formal power series, and it expands as a finite sum of back stable Schubert polynomials.
See
[
LLS23
, Section 4.1]
for a description of the back stable ring in which
\overleftarrow{\mathfrak{G}}_{w}
resides.

Given integers
p\leq q
, we define

\mathfrak{S}^{[p,q]}_{w}=\sum_{(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{\ell(w)})\in{\sf red}(w)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq b_{1}\leq b_{2}\leq\cdots\leq b_{\ell(w)}\leq q\\
a_{i}\leq a_{i+1}\Rightarrow b_{i}<b_{i+1}\\
b_{i}\leq a_{i}\end{subarray}}x_{b_{1}}x_{b_{2}}\cdots x_{b_{\ell(w)}}

and

\mathfrak{G}^{[p,q]}_{w}=\sum_{(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{L})\in{\sf hecke}(w)}(-1)^{L-\ell(w)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq b_{1}\leq b_{2}\leq\cdots\leq b_{L}\leq q\\
a_{i}\leq a_{i+1}\Rightarrow b_{i}<b_{i+1}\\
b_{i}\leq a_{i}\end{subarray}}x_{b_{1}}x_{b_{2}}\cdots x_{b_{L}}.

Given
w\in\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{Z}}
, fix
q
so that
w(i)=i
for all
i>q
.
Then
\mathfrak{S}^{[p,q]}_{w}
and
\mathfrak{G}^{[p,q]}_{w}
are nonzero if and only if
w
is supported on
[p,q]
.
Furthermore, in this case, these polynomials are simply evaluations of ordinary Schubert and Grothendieck polynomials on the alphabet
x_{p},x_{p+1},\ldots,x_{q}
, for an appropriate shift of
w
.
Namely, if
w^{\prime}\in S_{[p,q]}
is defined by
w^{\prime}(i)=w(i+p-1)-p+1
, then

\mathfrak{S}_{w}^{[p,q]}=\mathfrak{S}_{w^{\prime}}(x_{p},x_{p+1},\ldots,x_{q})

and

\mathfrak{G}_{w}^{[p,q]}=\mathfrak{G}_{w^{\prime}}(x_{p},x_{p+1},\ldots,x_{q}).

In particular, when
p=1
and
q=n
, we recover the usual compatible sequence definitions of the ordinary Schubert and Grothendieck polynomials, as given in
[
BJS93
]
and
[
FK94
]
, respectively.

8.3.

We now recall the back stable version of BPDs from
[

,
LLS23
]
.
An

BPD
is a tiling of
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
with the six tiles from
Equation

4.1
, forming a network of pipes labeled by
\mathbb{Z}
such that:

(1)

pipes do not start or end within the grid,

(2)

for each column
c

r_{c}
such that the tile at position
(r,c)
is a
for all
r>r_{c}
,

(3)

for each row
r
, there exists a column
c_{r}
such that the tile at position
(r,c)
is a
for all
c>c_{r}
, and

there exist integers
p\leq q
such that for all

is a

, we say that the
S_{\mathbb{Z}}
BPD is
supported
on
[p,q]
.
An

We may assign a permutation to an

\mathcal{B}
by labeling each pipe with the index of the column in which it is eventually vertical, and propagating labels across crossings using the local rules for NE planar histories, as in
Equation

3.1
.
We obtain an associated permutation

by setting

if the pipe labeled
j
is eventually horizontal in row

.

\delta(\mathcal{B})=w
.

Given
w\in S_{\mathbb{Z}}
, write
\overleftarrow{\overline{\sf BPD}}(w)
for the set of

, and
\overleftarrow{{\sf BPD}}(w)
for the set of reduced

.

is supported on
[p,q]
, then

is also supported on
[p,q]
.
We write
\overline{\sf BPD}^{[p,q]}(w)\subseteq\overleftarrow{\overline{\sf BPD}}(w)
and

for the subsets of

[p,q]

It is immediate from the definitions that restricting an element of
\overline{\sf BPD}^{[p,q]}(w)
to the rectangle

defines a bijection from

(2)

(3)

for each row

, the tile at position

is a

8.5.
The back stable Grothendieck to back stable Schubert expansion

We now state and prove the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 8.1
.

Given
w\in S_{\mathbb{Z}}
, we have

\overleftarrow{\mathfrak{G}}_{w}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{B}\in\overleftarrow{\overline{\sf BPD}}(w)\\
\check{\mathcal{B}}\text{ is reduced}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\ell(\delta(\check{\mathcal{B}}))-\ell(w)}\overleftarrow{\mathfrak{S}}_{\delta(\check{\mathcal{B}})}.

Proof.

Because each homogeneous component of
\overleftarrow{\mathfrak{G}}_{w}
is back symmetric, by
[
LLS21
, Theorem 3.5]
, we may write

(8.3)

\overleftarrow{\mathfrak{G}}_{w}=\sum_{v\in S_{\mathbb{Z}}}(-1)^{\ell(v)-\ell(w)}d_{w,v}\overleftarrow{\mathfrak{S}}_{v},

so that in each degree, we have a finite linear combination of back stable Schubert polynomials.
In other words, for each
k
, the set

\{v\in S_{\mathbb{Z}}:\ell(v)=k\text{ and }d_{w,v}\neq 0\}

is finite.

Given
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, choose integers
p\leq q
so that
w
is supported on
[p,q]
, and also
v
is supported on
[p,q]
for all
v\in S_{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
\ell(v)=k
and
d_{w,v}\neq 0
. We can do this because the set of such
v
is finite.
Now specialize both sides of
Equation

8.3
, setting
x_{i}=0
for all
i\notin[p,q]
, which yields

\mathfrak{G}_{w}^{[p,q]}=\sum_{v\in S_{\mathbb{Z}}}(-1)^{\ell(v)-\ell(w)}d_{w,v}\mathfrak{S}^{[p,q]}_{v}.

On the other hand, as an immediate consequence of
1.1
, we also have

\mathfrak{G}_{w}^{[p,q]}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{B}\in\overline{\sf BPD}^{[p,q]}(w)\\
\check{\mathcal{B}}\text{ is reduced}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\ell(\delta(\check{\mathcal{B}}))-\ell(w)}\mathfrak{S}^{[p,q]}_{\delta(\check{\mathcal{B}})}.

Since the set
\{\mathfrak{S}_{v}^{[p,q]}:v\in S_{\mathbb{Z}}\text{ is supported on }[p,q]\}
is linearly independent, it follows that if
\ell(v)=k
, then

d_{w,v}=\#\{\mathcal{B}\in\overline{\sf BPD}^{[p,q]}(w):\check{\mathcal{B}}\text{ is reduced and }\delta(\check{\mathcal{B}})=v\}.

In particular, if
\mathfrak{S}^{[p,q]}_{v}\neq 0
, then
\mathfrak{S}^{[p^{\prime},q]}_{v}\neq 0
for all
p^{\prime}<p
. Thus,

d_{w,v}=\#\{\mathcal{B}\in\overline{\sf BPD}^{[p^{\prime},q]}(w):\check{\mathcal{B}}\text{ is reduced and }\delta(\check{\mathcal{B}})=v\}

for all
p^{\prime}<p
and so

d_{w,v}=\#\{\mathcal{B}\in\overleftarrow{\overline{\sf BPD}}(w):\check{\mathcal{B}}\text{ is reduced and }\delta(\check{\mathcal{B}})=v\}.

Since the choice of
k
was arbitrary, the theorem follows.
∎
