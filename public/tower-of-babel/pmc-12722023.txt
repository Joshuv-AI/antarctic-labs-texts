Targeted proteomics reveals immune protein patterns in serum from patients with calcific aortic stenosis
Authors: Alice Mallia, Andrea Binatti, Diego Sangiorgi, Elisa Mikus, Giulia G. Papaianni, Stefania Ghilardi, Erica Gianazza, Francesco Vieceli Dalla Sega, Elena Tremoli, Cristina Banfi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryCalcific aortic valve disease (CAVD) is a progressive condition with poor prognosis once symptomatic. The disease’s progression is driven by a combination of mechanical stress, lipid infiltration, and immune responses, which involve both innate and adaptive immune systems. To explore the role of immune-related proteins in CAVD, we applied a targeted proteomic approach based on the proximity extension assay technique. Ten proteins were downregulated in patients with CAVD with respect to control subjects, indicating disrupted biological processes such as redox homeostasis, immune response, and cell motility. A protein-protein interaction network revealed connections between these proteins, with notable clusters associated with immune regulation and redox homeostasis. Further analysis identified two distinct patient subgroups, suggesting heterogeneity among patients with CAVD, which could help with patient stratification. The study identified proteins that may serve as candidate biomarkers for early detection of CAVD and emphasizes the role of immune-driven mechanisms in CAVD.

Introduction

Calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD) is the most common valvular disease in the developed world, and there are currently no effective pharmacological treatments available. When patients become symptomatic, the prognosis is grim; untreated individuals have a survival rate of 50% at 2 years and 25% at 5 years.1 The impact of CAVD on healthcare is substantial, and this burden is expected to grow in the coming decades due to the aging population.2,3

Several mechanistic pathways have been elucidated in CAVD. Studies conducted on animal models4,5 as well as analyses of human aortic valve tissue,6,7 indicate that factors such as inflammation, neurohormonal activation, lipid oxidation, biomineralization, and activation of the innate immune system play significant roles in the progression of valve fibrosis, calcification, and stenosis. The initiation of CAVD is now understood to be influenced by a combination of mechanical stress and lipid infiltration, which trigger an innate immune response.8 In turn, immune cells release pro-inflammatory cytokines, which not only propagate inflammation but also enhance oxidative stress, leading to the activation of valvular interstitial cells (VICs).9 Moreover, the adaptive immune system contributes significantly to disease progression. Specifically, T helper 1 and T helper 17 cells release key cytokines such as interferon-gamma and interleukin (IL-17), both of which further exacerbate chronic inflammation and promote osteogenic differentiation in VICs.8

Concurrently, B cells are implicated in the disease progression by producing autoantibodies targeting oxidized lipids and apoptotic cell debris, thus contributing to immune complex deposition and persistent immune activation.10 Moreover, recent studies have proposed emerging immune-mediated mechanisms, e.g., trained immunity and clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential, as possible factors that could lead to the sustained activation of immune cells, potentially contributing to the onset of CAVD.11,12 Collectively, these immune-driven processes facilitate the fibro-calcific remodeling of the aortic valve, underscoring the critical roles of both innate and adaptive immunity in CAVD progression.9,10,13,14

The protein signature in aortic stenosis has been explored in aortic valve tissue15,16,17 and in plasma.18,19,20,21,22 It should be noted, however, that data obtained by proteomics of valve aortic tissue may present specific limitations due to the presence of severe valve calcification. In contrast, plasma or serum is more readily accessible, facilitating a comprehensive investigation of biological processes during the early stages of aortic stenosis. Plasma/serum proteomics offers a robust approach for identifying unpredictable biomarkers by enabling detailed profiling of proteins involved in key pathogenic processes such as inflammation and immune regulation. Recent research has underscored the efficacy of this approach, demonstrating that certain circulating proteins related to inflammation and immune regulation, namely members of the tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily and trefoil factor-3, were differentially expressed in patients with aortic stenosis who exhibited reduced left ventricular global longitudinal strain. This finding was also associated with adverse clinical outcomes.22

Building on prior findings, our objective was to employ a targeted proteomic approach to acquire new insights into the involvement of immune-related proteins as candidate biomarkers for CAVD in comparison to control subjects.

Circulating proteins linked to aortic valve calcification

Serum samples were analyzed using a targeted proteomic approach based on the proximity extension assay (PEA) principle, which enabled the detection of relative changes in protein levels according to the group. Figure S1 shows the distribution profile of the panel of 92 proteins analyzed in both the control and patient groups. Table S1 enumerates the protein data, organized by distinct groups of subjects. Differences in the proteomic profile between patients and controls were evaluated using Limma method, which identified 10 significantly different proteins (Figure 1; Table 2). These proteins include: peroxiredoxin 1 (PRDX1), peroxiredoxin 5 (PRDX5), amphiregulin (AREG), beta-galactosidase (GLB1), tripartite motif-containing protein 5 (TRIM5), E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase (TRIM21), Plexin-A4 (PLXNA4), islet cell autoantigen 1 (ICA1), butyrophilin subfamily 3 member A2 (BTN3A2), and C-type lectin domain family 4 member C (CLEC4C). In addition, as 56.4% of patients used statins, we have evaluated its potential impact on protein levels only within the patient group by two complementary approaches. First, a differential abundance analysis comparing statin users to non-users revealed no statistically significant differences for any of the 83 investigated proteins. Second, Boruta algorithm assessed the importance of statin use for 5 proteins (CCL11, DPP10, IL10, NTF4, and PRDX3), but it is not selected as an important factor for any of the 10 candidate biomarker proteins identified in the comparison between patient group to controls (Figure S2).

Protein-protein interaction network reveals crosstalk between redox homeostasis, immune response, and cell motility pathways

A protein-protein interaction network was constructed using differentially expressed proteins and their first interactors. The resulting network included 43 proteins and 90 interactions (Figure 2). GLB1, ICA1, and BTN3A2 were excluded because they were either isolated nodes or formed modules not connected to the main network. Markov clustering (MCL) of the network identified three interconnected subnetworks, each associated with distinct biological functions. The core subnetwork is linked to cell redox homeostasis and detoxification of reactive oxygen species (ROS), featuring PRDX1 and PRDX5 as central proteins that interact directly with each other. Through NFE2L2 interactions with SQSTM1 and RPS27A, this subnetwork is connected to the largest module, which contains 20 proteins involved in the regulation of innate immune response to cytosolic DNA and protein tagging via ubiquitination. Within this cluster, AREG and TRIM5 are present, with TRIM21 occupying a central position. The third module, involved in axon guidance and SEMA3A-Plexin repulsion signaling by inhibiting integrin adhesion, has PLXNA4 as a central hub and includes CLEC4C. This module is connected to the redox homeostasis subnetwork through the interaction between CDK5 and FYN. Analysis conducted using the GTEx Portal revealed distinct RNA expression profiles of the differentially expressed proteins across various human tissues under normal conditions (Figure 4). PRDX1 and PRDX5 displayed widespread expression across most tissues and cell types, underscoring their potential roles in mitigating oxidative stress in multiple organs. In vascular tissues—including aorta, tibial artery, and coronary artery—several proteins, such as TRIM5, GLB1, TRIM21, and BTN3A2, showed comparable expression levels without substantial differences. Similarly, ICA1 was moderately expressed in cardiac tissues such as the left ventricle and atrial appendage, in line with other proteins including GLB1 and AREG. PLXNA4 expression in the aorta was detectable but not higher than that of TRIM21 or GLB1. AREG, GBL1, TRIM21, and BTN3A2 display moderate expression levels in whole blood, indicating their involvement in the regulation of immune cells. BTN3A2 also shows high expression in the spleen, reinforcing its potential role in immune system activation. Hierarchical clustering reveals two distinct patient subgroups based on differential protein levels

Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analysis of the abundance patterns of the 10 significant proteins distinguished most patients who had undergone aortic valve surgery from controls (Figure 5A). Additionally, within the group of patients with aortic valve calcification, two distinct clusters were identified: cluster 2 and cluster 3. Hierarchical clustering clearly illustrated this separation (Figure 5B). Specifically, cluster 2 consisted of 14 valve surgery patients and two control subjects, all of whom showed intermediate downregulation of the 10 proteins compared to the control group (Figure 5C). In contrast, cluster 3 included the remaining 25 patients, who exhibited a more significant downregulation of the same proteins. This clustering pattern reveals the heterogeneity in the molecular profiles of patients with aortic valve pathology, which may have important implications for patient stratification. Table 3 presents an overview of the clinical characteristics of patients in cluster 2 and cluster 3. Platelet counts, surfactant protein-D (SP-D), and left ventricular mass differed significantly between the two clusters. Specifically, patients in cluster 3 exhibited lower platelet counts, along with higher SP-D levels and greater left ventricular mass. Furthermore, while cluster 3 demonstrated a more distinctly altered proteomic profile compared to controls, the median age of these patients was lower (76 years) than that of cluster 2 (80.5 years). Pharmacological targetability of CAVD-associated proteins

To explore the translational potential of our findings, we interrogated public drug target databases, including Open Targets and DrugBank, to assess whether any of the differentially expressed proteins in CAVD are currently targeted by approved or investigational compounds. This analysis revealed that several proteins have known pharmacological associations. GLB1 is the target of PBGM01, a gene therapy in development for GM1 gangliosidosis. CLEC4C is inhibited by litifilimab, a monoclonal antibody under investigation for cutaneous and systemic lupus erythematosus. PRDX1 interacts with artenimol, an antimalarial drug. Both PRDX1 and PRDX5 are upregulated by polaprezinc, a gastroprotective agent. In addition, PRDX5 is modulated by auranofin, an anti-rheumatic drug, and by motexafin gadolinium, a small molecule used in cancer and HIV-related indications. Lastly, AREG is associated with nitroglycerin, a widely used nitrate vasodilator. These findings support the relevance of our proteomic signature and suggest that some of the identified proteins may represent potential targets for therapeutic modulation in CAVD.

Discussion

This study utilized targeted proteomics with PEA technology, enabling the identification of a panel of ten immune-related circulating proteins that are differentially abundant in patients with CAVD compared to healthy controls. These findings offer important insights into the molecular mechanisms underpinning the disease and highlight potential biomarkers that may support disease monitoring and risk stratification. All ten proteins, PRDX1, GLB1, AREG, TRIM5, TRIM21, BTN3A2, ICA1, CLEC4C, PLXNA4, and PRDX5, were less abundant in patients with CAVD. The robustness of our findings is further supported by the orthogonal validation performed with quantitative ELISA assays, which confirmed the significantly lower levels of PLXNA4 and AREG in patients compared to controls.

PRDX1 and PRDX5 are thiol-specific peroxidases essential for detoxifying ROS. Over the last decade, a wealth of evidence has emerged, underscoring the role of peroxiredoxins in immune responses, especially in the context of innate immunity and inflammation. Their reduced abundance in patients with CAVD may reflect diminished antioxidant defenses and increased susceptibility to oxidative damage, which is an established contributor to calcification and chronic inflammation in aortic valve disease.23

The tripartite motif-containing proteins TRIM5 and TRIM21 are part of a family of over 70 E3 ubiquitin ligases in humans.24,25,26 They play roles in viral restriction, immune regulation, cell stress responses, proliferation, apoptosis, differentiation, transcription, and DNA repair.27,28,29 TRIM proteins can enhance or inhibit innate immune signaling in pathways such as the type I interferon response and nuclear factor κB activation. Recent studies indicate that variants of TRIM5 and TRIM21 are linked to changes in lipid metabolism and increased inflammation in macrophages, raising the risk of cardiovascular diseases like coronary artery disease, atherosclerosis, and cardiac remodeling.30 A decrease in TRIM5 and TRIM21 levels may lead to an inflammatory shift in macrophages, contributing to aortic valve calcification.31,32

PLXNA4 is a transmembrane glycoprotein that primarily acts as a receptor for semaphorins.33 It plays a crucial role in macrophage Toll-like receptor (TLR)-mediated signaling and cytokine production during sepsis,34 although it does not participate in phagocytosis or antigen presentation. Plxna4−/− cells exhibit reduced cytokine production when activated by TLR agonists or bacterial stimuli, highlighting its importance in Rac1 activation for TLR signaling.34 Although there is a solid understanding of the roles of plexins and semaphorins in immunity, their broader implications are often overlooked.35 Interestingly, in the cardiovascular system, the downregulation of endothelial PLXNA4 has been linked to atherosclerosis through its influence on the RhoA/ROCK pathway,36 which is also recognized as playing a role in aortic valve calcification.37

ICA69, encoded by the ICA1 gene, has a limited distribution across cells and tissues. It has mainly been studied for its role in organ-specific autoimmune diseases, especially type 1 diabetes, where it was identified as an autoantigen by Pietropaolo et al. in 1993.38 ICA69 is crucial for the trafficking of insulin granules, working alongside PICK1. Mice deficient in either ICA69 or PICK1 develop diabetes-like conditions characterized by glucose intolerance due to impaired insulin secretion.39 Additionally, ICA69 is associated with autism spectrum disorder and various autoimmune diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis and primary Sjögren’s syndrome, all of which are associated with a deficiency in immune tolerance toward ICA69.40,41 Notably, chronic inflammation related to immune responses in Sjögren’s syndrome may exacerbate the degeneration of valve tissue.42 Furthermore, the thymic deletion of ICA69 expression has been shown to instigate inflammatory events in multiple organs.43 In summary, while ICA1’s role in cardiovascular diseases remains largely unclear, its involvement in autoimmunity may reflect the consequences of chronic inflammation.

BTN3A2 is primarily expressed by antigen-presenting cells and plays a key role in regulating the activation of human γδ T cells through T cell receptor-dependent recognition of phosphoantigens.44 CLEC4C is expressed in plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), which are key producers of type I interferons and significantly contribute to the host’s initial defense mechanisms. Abnormalities in the quantity and function of pDCs have been linked to a range of immune-related health conditions.45 Notably, aortic pDCs have been shown to provide protection against atherosclerosis by promoting the induction of regulatory T cells.46 A similar protective mechanism may also occur in the aortic valve.

AREG, a member of the epidermal growth factor family, plays a crucial role in immune response, tissue repair, and cellular proliferation.47 Reduced levels of AREG can impair tissue regeneration and disrupt valve homeostasis, potentially worsening degenerative changes. In cardiovascular diseases, AREG is known to protect cardiomyocytes by triggering hypertrophy, which helps them adapt to pressure overload and defend against ischemia-reperfusion injury.48,49,50 It provides benefits by activating the EGFR/phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway and enhancing lysosomal acidity, which facilitates the clearance of autophagosomes, thereby preventing excessive autophagosome formation and apoptosis in cardiomyocytes and ultimately mitigating ventricular dysfunction.50 While the role of AREG in aortic valve calcification remains unclear, altered levels of AREG may be linked to ongoing ventricular compensatory adaptations that arise from pressure overload.51

GLB1 is a crucial lysosomal enzyme that clears damaged macromolecules by hydrolyzing β-linked galactose from glycoconjugates.52 Reduced GLB1 activity leads to the accumulation of GM1 ganglioside and its derivative, GA1, particularly in neuronal lysosomes, contributing to severe neurological gangliosidosis (as reviewed in Koeppen et al.53). GLB1 deficiency is associated with Morquio B disease, a condition that primarily impacts trabecular bone and ligament stability, and it is often linked to aortic valve defects.54 Our findings suggest a potential connection between decreased GLB1 levels and susceptibility to CAVD, although we currently lack genetic data to determine if this is due to mutations in the GLB1 gene.

As a confirmatory finding, the network analysis revealed that the proteins differently expressed in patients with CAVD primarily participate in pathways related to cell redox homeostasis and the detoxification of ROS, which are key factors in the pathophysiology of CAVD.55 Additionally, other proteins are predicted to be involved in regulating the innate immune response to cytosolic DNA, protein tagging via ubiquitination, axon guidance, and SEMA3A-Plexin repulsion signaling by inhibiting integrin adhesion. While the roles of these pathways in the context of CAVD remain unclear, they may indicate a crosstalk between redox regulation, immune response, and cell motility pathways. Interestingly, among the significant nodes identified in the network analysis, the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor (Nfr2, NFE2L2) plays a crucial role in linking oxidative stress and immune response. This aligns with the recognized function of Nfr2 in CAVD as a protective factor against calcification processes, as it inhibits the osteogenic transition of valvular interstitial cells.56 Another crucial node is constituted by CDK5, which may play a significant yet unexplored role in CAVD. CDK5 promotes T cell survival and motility,57 indicating its potential involvement in immune cell infiltration, a known contributor to CAVD pathology. Additionally, CDK5 enhances the production of IL-2,58 which may further amplify immune responses in CAVD. Notably, its interaction with eNOS and its regulation of nitric oxide production,59 which is impaired in CAVD, suggest that CDK5 may play a role in endothelial dysfunction and vascular calcification.

To examine the impact of biomarker changes on CAVD, we utilized hierarchical clustering. This approach allowed us to identify two distinct clusters among patients with CAVD, highlighting the heterogeneity in the molecular profiles linked to aortic valve pathology. This variation may have important implications for patient stratification.

Our analysis revealed that patients with a more pronounced decrease in the levels of the newly identified 10 proteins were younger, with an average age of 76.0 years compared to 80.5 years (p = 0.019). These patients also exhibited a greater left ventricular mass, measuring 249 g versus 184 g (p = 0.044), along with a lower left ventricular ejection fraction of 60.13% compared to 66.0% (p = 0.103), though the latter only approached statistical significance. Conversely, our cohort included only patients with severe stenosis, and we did not observe differences in severity as assessed by mean aortic gradient or aortic valve area (AVA) among clusters. Moreover, these patients demonstrated significantly higher levels of SP-D, with values of 234.22 ng/mL compared to 87.30 ng/mL (p = 0.004). SP-D is a protein primarily found in the lungs and belongs to the collagenous collectin family of lectins. While it is predominantly expressed in pulmonary tissues, SP-D is also detected in various non-pulmonary tissues, including the glandular, reproductive, gastrointestinal, and cardiovascular systems.60 In the cardiovascular system, SP-D is expressed in both endothelial and smooth muscle cells, where it plays a significant role in modulating inflammatory signaling61,62,63; candidate biomarkers may help identify patients with CAVD with left ventricular impairment, possibly due to prolonged pressure overload or individual differences in ventricular remodeling, thereby being instrumental in the stratification of patients at higher risk.

Interestingly, despite comparable hemodynamic severity of aortic stenosis, as reflected by similar mean transvalvular gradients and AVA, patients in cluster 3 exhibited greater left ventricular mass and a higher proportion of NYHA class III–IV symptoms. This suggests that factors beyond instantaneous valve hemodynamics—such as the chronicity of pressure overload, differences in ventricular adaptive mechanisms, or myocardial tissue remodeling—may influence disease manifestation and symptom burden. The lack of significant differences in comorbidities (e.g., hypertension) and antihypertensive therapy between clusters further supports the hypothesis of interindividual variability in hypertrophic response. While speculative, these observations underline the importance of integrated phenotyping that includes both molecular and structural cardiac features. Future studies incorporating advanced imaging techniques or myocardial tissue profiling may help clarify the mechanisms underlying such divergent remodeling patterns within a homogeneously defined cohort of patients with severe CAVD.

Lastly, we assessed the capacity of the identified proteins to distinguish between control subjects and patients with CAVD. While the ROC analysis indicated favorable levels of sensitivity and specificity in this pilot cohort, these results are preliminary and require external validation before the biomarkers can be considered for clinical use.

Beyond biological interpretation, we also explored the translational potential of our findings by querying drug target databases. Our cross-referencing of differentially expressed proteins with established drug target databases revealed that approved or investigational compounds, including anti-inflammatory agents, vasodilators, and modulators of oxidative stress, already target several of the identified proteins. For instance, CLEC4C, PRDX1, and PRDX5 are modulated by drugs currently used in autoimmune, infectious, and neoplastic conditions. These associations underscore the translational relevance of our proteomic findings and suggest potential avenues for drug repurposing or targeted therapeutic development in CAVD. While these observations are preliminary and require functional validation, they offer a promising framework to bridge molecular insights with actionable strategies aimed at addressing the current lack of effective pharmacological treatments for this disease.

Beyond their biological interpretation, these immune-related proteins may also hold translational potential as circulating biomarkers. In clinical practice, they could be applied in two complementary contexts: risk stratification, to refine prognostic assessment among patients with severe CAVD, especially in relation to ventricular remodeling and symptom burden; and disease monitoring, to support longitudinal follow-up and evaluate the effects of medical or interventional therapies. Although these applications remain exploratory, articulating their possible integration into the clinical pathway underscores the potential utility of immune protein signatures for improving patient management in CAVD.

Limitations of the study

It is essential to acknowledge several limitations of this study. First, no formal sample size calculation was performed prior to study initiation, as the primary objective was exploratory and aimed at identifying potential immune-related biomarkers in CAVD. The findings, derived from a single cohort with a relatively small sample size, should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger, independent populations.

Second, although we adjusted for key confounders such as age and sex, the limited sample size did not allow for robust multivariable adjustments or stratified analyses based on additional covariates such as comorbidities and therapeutic treatments. These factors may influence circulating protein levels and contribute to variability in the expression profiles observed. As such, the biomarker signatures identified should be interpreted with caution, and their specificity for CAVD must be further validated in more heterogeneous cohorts with broader clinical characteristics.

Third, the cross-sectional design and absence of longitudinal follow-up preclude the assessment of temporal changes in biomarker levels and their potential association with disease progression or clinical outcomes. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal sampling and include individuals across the full spectrum of CAVD severity (i.e., mild, moderate, and severe) to evaluate the prognostic value of candidate biomarkers for risk stratification and therapeutic monitoring. Finally, although our data suggest a possible association between certain biomarker profiles and declining left ventricular function, this relationship remains preliminary and warrants further investigation. The inclusion of patients at earlier stages of aortic valve disease may provide additional insights into the dynamic evolution of immune-related biomarkers during disease progression. Another important limitation of this study concerns the control group. In particular, no clinical or imaging-based assessments (e.g., echocardiography) were performed to exclude subclinical cardiovascular conditions, such as aortic valve sclerosis or stenosis. The classification of control participants was based solely on self-reported medical history obtained through structured interviews, without objective diagnostic confirmation. While this strategy is commonly used in exploratory studies and facilitated participant recruitment, it does not fully exclude the presence of asymptomatic or subclinical cardiovascular disease, especially in older individuals. Moreover, due to the lack of access to individual-level clinical and demographic data for these controls, a detailed comparison with the patient group was not feasible. As a consequence, we were unable to assess potential differences in baseline characteristics or adjust for confounding variables, which may limit the interpretation of group-specific protein expression patterns.

A key technical limitation of this study is the use of a targeted proteomics approach restricted to the 92 proteins included in the Olink Immune Response panel. While this platform enables high-sensitivity and high-specificity quantification of circulating immune-related proteins, it inherently limits the scope of discovery by excluding molecular pathways not covered by the panel. As such, important biological processes involved in CAVD pathogenesis—including bone metabolism, extracellular matrix remodeling, and calcification signaling—may not be adequately captured. To broaden the molecular landscape and strengthen mechanistic insights, future research may benefit from more comprehensive proteomic strategies, including untargeted mass spectrometry-based analyses or expanded targeted panels encompassing a wider range of disease-relevant pathways.

Conclusions

Inflammation and immune activation play a crucial role in CAVD development. Various immune cell types infiltrate the tissue of the aortic valve, driving inflammatory responses that contribute to the calcification process. Biomolecules circulating in the bloodstream linked to these processes may be valuable for early detection, assessing disease severity, and guiding clinical decisions.

In this study, we identified a panel of immune-related biomarkers that are expressed differently in patients with CAVD. These findings require validation in independent cohorts before these biomarkers can be reliably proposed for diagnosis or monitoring of disease progression. Currently, B-type natriuretic peptide is the only biomarker recommended by existing guidelines for guiding aortic valve replacement in asymptomatic patients with severe aortic stenosis.64 Our findings emphasize the need for additional biomarkers to enhance risk stratification and clinical management of CAVD. Taken together, these results support the hypothesis that immune-related proteins may complement existing clinical parameters for risk stratification and disease monitoring, once validated in prospective cohorts.

Experimental model and study participant details

The study was conducted at Maria Cecilia Hospital, Cotignola (RA), Italy. Thirty-nine patients diagnosed with severe calcific aortic stenosis were enrolled between September 2018 and May 2022. All patients were candidates for aortic valve replacement. The inclusion criteria were as follows: age >65, calcific severe CAVD, and hospitalization for aortic valve replacement. The exclusion criteria were as follows: infective endocarditis, rheumatic valve disease, end-stage kidney disease, hematological disorders, hepatic dysfunction (bilirubin >20 mmol/L, prothrombin >2.0 International Normalised ratio), severe renal failure (glomerular filtration rate <30 mL/min/1 · 73 m2), severe cognitive impairment (Short Portable Mental Status Questionnaire (SPMSQ) < 4), life expectancy <12 months for non-cardiac causes, and bioprosthetic degeneration caused by endocarditis. Sixteen participants (11 females and 5 males, with an average age of 50.9 ± 10.6 years) were recruited from the general population. They underwent a structured interview to exclude individuals with relevant medical conditions, ensuring that none had a history of cardiovascular disease or inflammatory disorders. Additionally, none of the participants had taken any medication in the week prior to the blood draw. This group served as the control group. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of “Romagna” (approval code: 590/2017) and was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent at the time of enrollment. No formal sample size calculation was performed, as this was an exploratory pilot study designed to identify potential immune-related biomarkers in CAVD.

This study was approved by the institutional Ethics Committee and it was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent at the time of enrolment.

Method details

Sample preparation

Blood samples were allowed to clot at room temperature for 2 h, and serum was obtained after centrifugation at 1500 x g for 15 min. The serum samples were stored at −80°C until analysis.

Circulating proteome profiling and analysis

The samples were analyzed using the Proximity Extension Assay (PEA) with the Olink Target 96 Immune Response Panel (Olink Proteomics, Uppsala, Sweden). This technique involves the specific binding of target proteins to antibody probes that are labeled with double oligonucleotides. Following this, microfluidic real-time PCR is utilized to amplify the oligonucleotide sequence, allowing for quantitative DNA detection. Quality control and normalization procedures were applied to the threshold cycle (Ct) data obtained from both internal and external controls. A comprehensive list of the analyzed proteins, including their respective acronyms and UniProt codes, is provided in Table S1. Protein levels were quantified on a relative scale and expressed as normalized protein expression (NPX) units in log2 scale, with higher NPX values indicating higher protein concentrations.

Validation by ELISA assays

To validate proteomic results, serum levels of Amphiregulin (AREG) and Plexin-A4 (PLXNA4) were quantified using commercial ELISA kits, following the manufacturers’ protocols. Amphiregulin was measured with the Quantikine Human Amphiregulin Immunoassay (R&D Systems, Catalog #DAR00), a 4.5-h quantitative sandwich ELISA with a sensitivity of 1.43 pg/mL. Plexin-A4 was assessed using the Human Plexin A4 ELISA Kit (Antibodies.com, Catalog #A312006), a sandwich ELISA with a sensitivity of 4.47 ng/L and a measurement range of 75–1200 ng/L. All samples were analyzed in duplicate, and concentrations were calculated from standard curves generated by four-parameter logistic regression.

Bioinformatic analyses

Proteins with values below the limit of detection (LOD) and with missing values in more than 50% of samples in both groups were excluded from further analysis. This filtering step resulted in the exclusion of 9 out of 92 proteins, leaving a final dataset of 83 proteins across 55 samples (totalling 4565 data points). Within the filtered dataset, only 4 individual values (0.088%) remained missing. These missing values were imputed by the Random Forest method, as implemented in the missForest R package. Due to this extremely low level of missingness, we did not perform validation procedures such as masking or bootstrapping, as these procedures would not be statistically meaningful. The negligible extent of imputation supports the robustness of downstream analyses. Values below the LOD were replaced by LOD/√ 2. This is a common and accepted approach for handling left-censored data in Olink datasets, as it offers a reasonable compromise in mean estimations without introducing excessive bias.65,66 Moreover, the remaining 83 proteins used for differential amount analysis had only 4.2% and 8.8% of values, on average, below LOD in controls and patients’ groups, respectively. Given this low rate, this strategy represents a pragmatic choice with a negligible effect on the subsequent statistical analysis. The Boruta method was employed to assess the importance of age and sex on protein serum levels in all samples and to confirm the non-importance of statins use only within patients’ group. Differential protein abundance analyses were subsequently performed using the limma method, incorporating the effect of age and sex into the model. Limma is a statistical method that employs an empirical Bayes approach to improve variance estimation by shrinking protein-specific variances toward a pooled estimate derived from all proteins in the dataset. This regularisation is particularly advantageous for studies with small sample sizes, such as this one, where individual variance estimates can be unstable. After modeling protein levels using linear models, limma applies moderated t-tests, enhancing sensitivity while controlling false discovery rate. The framework is implemented in the R/Bioconductor limma package. Although originally developed for transcriptomic data, limma is well suited for proteomic analyses—particularly with log2-transformed and normalized data such as the NPX values provided by the Olink platform. This approach enhances statistical power and allows for the inclusion of covariates (e.g., age and sex) in the model. The method has been increasingly adopted in both Olink-based proteomics studies.65,67,68 Proteins were considered differentially abundant if they had a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted-pValue below 0 · 05 and a B-statistics greater than −1. Significantly differentially abundant proteins were subsequently used as independent variables for unsupervised clustering of the 55 samples. Hierarchical clustering was performed using Ward’s method with Euclidean distance. The number of clusters was determined by GAP statistics and confirmed by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP, umap R package) based on the quantification of the differentially abundant proteins.

The expression of differentially expressed proteins across various human tissues was explored using the GTEx portal database (https://gtexportal.org).

A protein-protein network was constructed using data from the STRING database (https://string-db.org/), considering only physical interactions with a minimum required interaction score of 0 · 7 and limiting the maximum number of interactors per protein to 80. To identify subclusters within the network, Markov Clustering algorithm (MCL) was applied with an inflation parameter of 1 · 4. Associations between the identified clusters and biological functions were performed with the Clusters analysis implemented in String. The resulting network and its clustering data were imported into Cytoscape software for reconstruction and analysis, where the CytoHubba app was used to identify the shortest path, considering first-stage nodes that connect the proteins of interest.

To assess the therapeutic relevance of the proteins identified in the study, we queried publicly available drug target databases, including Open Targets Platform (https://platform.opentargets.org/) and DrugBank (https://go.drugbank.com/), to determine whether any of the differentially expressed proteins are known targets of approved or investigational compounds. The search included both direct protein–drug interactions and indirect regulatory associations.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Clinical continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and compared in Table 3 using the Mann-Whitney test. Categorical variables were expressed as absolute numbers and frequencies, and comparisons in Table 3 were made using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. No imputation was performed for missing data in clinical variables. The handling of missing and below LOD values for Olink proteomics data is described in bioinformatic analyses section.

The relationships between variables were analyzed using the Spearman’s rank correlation test. Given the limited sample size, only correlations with an absolute Rho value >0.6 and p < 0.05 were considered significant. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to evaluate the ability of circulating protein biomarkers to distinguish between conditions (using the pROC R package), while multivariate modeling was performed using LASSO-regularized logistic regression with leave-one-out corss-validation (LOOCV) by glmnet R package.

All statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using R version 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), except for ELISA data, which were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). All tests were two-tailed, and p-values <0 · 05 were considered statistically significant. Graphical represention and network analyses were generated using Cytoscape software.

Complete statistical details for each experiment, including the type of test and the definition of center and dispersion, are provided in the corresponding figure legends, tables, and results section. In Tables 1 and 3, n refers to the number of individual subjects. Statistical significance in Figure 3 is indicated by asterisks, as detailed in the corresponding legend, where the applied statistical test and the p-value thresholds are reported.
