Recent advances in TMEM16F: structural plasticity, functional versatility, and implications for human diseases
Authors: Li Chen, Zhigang Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTMEM16F is a calcium-activated membrane protein that functions as both a non-selective ion channel and a phospholipid scramblase, linking intracellular Ca²⁺ signaling to dynamic regulation of plasma membrane lipid asymmetry. Recent studies have substantially advanced our understanding of TMEM16F structure and regulation, revealing pronounced conformational heterogeneity and allosteric mechanisms through which Ca²⁺, pH and membrane context coordinate its dual functions. Cell- and tissue-specific analyses indicate that TMEM16F-mediated membrane remodeling contributes to diverse physiological processes, including blood coagulation, cell–cell fusion, regulated cell death, immune responses, and neurodevelopment. Dysregulation of TMEM16F activity has been associated with a broad spectrum of pathological conditions, ranging from bleeding disorders and thromboinflammation to neurodegeneration, infection-associated tissue damage, and cancer. This review integrates recent structural, cellular and pathological insights to provide a comprehensive overview of TMEM16F biology, highlighting mechanisms underlying its dual functions and summarizing emerging evidence for its roles in health and disease.Keywords: TMEM16F, Phospholipid scramblase, Ion channel, Structural mechanisms, Blood coagulation, Neurodegenerative diseases, Tumor immunity, Inflammation, Cell fusion

Introduction

Calcium-dependent regulation of plasma membrane lipid asymmetry is fundamental to diverse cellular processes, including signal transduction, membrane trafficking and cell–cell communication. TMEM16F (also known as ANO6) is a calcium-activated membrane protein distinguished by its dual function as a phospholipid scramblase and a non-selective ion channel, directly linking intracellular Ca²⁺ signals to rapid remodeling of membrane surface properties.

TMEM16F plays critical roles across a variety of physiological systems, including blood coagulation, cell–cell fusion, skeletal development and immune regulation. Accumulating evidence links dysregulated TMEM16F activity to diverse pathological conditions, such as hereditary bleeding disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, infection-associated inflammation, and tumor progression [1–3]. Despite extensive characterization of TMEM16F activity across experimental systems, a central challenge remains unresolved: how does a single membrane protein, mediating an apparently conserved biochemical activity, give rise to such divergent cellular and disease-associated outcomes?

Recent advances in structural biology, molecular dynamics simulations, and cell-type-specific functional analyses indicate that TMEM16F does not act as a simple binary switch. Instead, its activity is shaped by pronounced conformational heterogeneity and by integration of multiple contextual cues, including local Ca²⁺ microdomains, intracellular pH, membrane composition and mechanical forces. These factors collectively modulate the extent, duration and spatial confinement of phospholipid scrambling and ion flux [1, 2, 4]. Accordingly, the biological consequences of TMEM16F activation are determined not solely by its molecular activity per se, but by how TMEM16F-mediated membrane remodeling is interpreted within distinct cellular and tissue contexts.

In this review, we synthesize recent progress in understanding the structural basis, regulatory mechanisms and context-dependent functions of TMEM16F. Rather than presenting isolated observations, we aim to highlight unifying principles that explain how TMEM16F integrates intracellular signals to regulate membrane dynamics and contributes to diverse biological outcomes.

Structural heterogeneity and conformational plasticity

TMEM16F exhibits substantial structural heterogeneity, which is closely linked to its dual functions as a calcium-activated ion channel and phospholipid scramblase, as well as its diverse physiological roles [5–7]. This heterogeneity manifests at multiple levels, including dimer conformations, transmembrane helix rearrangements, and dynamic responses to intracellular calcium (Ca2+) and regulatory ligands, and has been extensively characterized by cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), atomic force microscopy (AFM), and functional assays [8–10].

Recent structural analyses, integrating cryo-EM with AFM under near-physiological conditions, have revealed substantial variability in TMEM16F dimer interfaces and protomer arrangements [11]. These observations suggest that dimer asymmetry and flexible inter-subunit coupling are intrinsic features of TMEM16F, rather than experimental artifacts, and may underlie its ability to switch between ion-conductive and lipid-scrambling modes.

Ca²⁺ binding triggers stepwise conformational rearrangements within the transmembrane core of TMEM16F, particularly involving helices TM3, TM4 and TM6, which together shape the permeation pathway. These rearrangements progressively alter helix flexibility and pore hydration, thereby facilitating transitions from closed states to conformations permissive for ion conduction and lipid scrambling [11]. Cryo-EM studies of activating mutants further support this mechanism by showing major rearrangements that expose hydrophilic patches to the membrane, thereby facilitating lipid diffusion and simultaneously opening a pore for ion conduction within the same conformation [8]. This dual gating mechanism highlights how TMEM16F integrates Ca2+ signals to coordinate its bifunctional roles [5, 6].

Molecular dynamics simulations have provided complementary insights into the allosteric pathways that govern TMEM16F’s conformational transitions. These simulations reveal that the opening of the lipid-conductive groove is linked to destabilization of Ca2+ bound at a distal site near the dimer interface, which triggers dynamic changes in specific protein regions to produce an open-groove state competent for lipid scrambling [12]. Such an allosteric mechanism underscores the complexity of TMEM16F regulation, where distal Ca2+ binding sites modulate the structural landscape to enable functional versatility.

Further structural analyses have identified a third Ca2+ binding site in TMEM16 family members, including TMEM16F, which allosterically controls channel activation and gating [5, 13]. This additional site contributes to the fine-tuning of TMEM16F’s activity, suggesting that multiple Ca2+ binding events orchestrate a coordinated conformational response. The interplay between these sites and the dynamic dimer interface likely facilitates the protein’s ability to respond to varying intracellular Ca2+ concentrations and other regulatory factors such as pH [1].

The structural heterogeneity of TMEM16F also extends to its drug-binding properties. Cryo-EM structures have revealed that pharmacological agents like niclosamide and 1PBC bind to a conserved pocket located along the lipid scrambling groove, modulating TMEM16F’s ion and lipid transport functions [7, 14]. Mutagenesis studies demonstrate that alterations in this pocket can selectively affect ion conduction or lipid scrambling, further emphasizing the functional compartmentalization within the protein’s heterogeneous conformational states.

Across multiple lines of evidence, these findings support a model in which structural heterogeneity and Ca²⁺-dependent allosteric regulation are central to TMEM16F function. By sampling a continuum of conformational states with distinct permeation properties, TMEM16F integrates intracellular Ca²⁺ signals and membrane environment cues to coordinate ion transport and phospholipid scrambling. This conformational plasticity provides a unifying mechanistic framework for understanding TMEM16F’s diverse physiological roles and its involvement in disease [7, 8, 11, 12, 14].

Coupling mechanisms between ion channel activity and phospholipid scrambling

TMEM16F is unique among membrane proteins in its ability to mediate both non-selective ion conduction and Ca²⁺-dependent phospholipid scrambling, raising fundamental questions about how these two activities are mechanistically coordinated within a single molecular framework.

The coupling between its ion channel activity and phospholipid scrambling is tightly linked through its subunit cavity, which serves as a shared conduit for both ions and phospholipids. Structural and functional studies have revealed that conformational changes associated with ion channel gating directly facilitate the bidirectional translocation of phospholipids across the plasma membrane, indicating a mechanistic interdependence and reciprocal regulation of these two functions. Molecular dynamics simulations and mutational analyses have provided strong support for the hypothesis that the subunit cavity acts as a common permeation pathway. For instance, charged mutations at the inner activation gate residues (F518, Y563, and I612) induce spontaneous pore opening in the calcium-bound state, increasing pore hydration and enabling lipid scrambling, thus demonstrating the structural basis for the coupling of ion and lipid permeation [4]. This open state resembles the activated conformations observed in fungal scramblases, suggesting a conserved mechanism across species. Furthermore, the in-groove phospholipid and ion transduction pathways overlap intracellularly up to the inner gate but diverge extracellularly, implying a sophisticated gating mechanism that coordinates ion conduction with lipid translocation [4].

Experimental evidence also indicates that TMEM16F preferentially scrambles phosphatidylserine (PS) and phosphatidylcholine over phosphatidylethanolamine, highlighting a selective lipid scrambling activity that may be modulated by ion channel gating [15]. Mutagenesis studies targeting the transmembrane helices 4 (TM4) and 6 underscore the critical role of TM4 in controlling both ion and lipid permeation, further supporting the concept of a shared permeation pathway within the subunit cavity [16]. Additionally, the allosteric mechanism leading to an open-groove lipid conductive state involves destabilization of calcium ions at distal binding sites, which triggers conformational dynamics facilitating the opening of the groove for lipid scrambling, reinforcing the coupling between ion binding, channel gating, and phospholipid translocation [12].

Taken together, these findings delineate a model in which TMEM16F’s ion channel activity and phospholipid scrambling are structurally and functionally intertwined via the subunit cavity, with conformational gating changes enabling the bidirectional movement of phospholipids and ions. This tunable coupling mechanism provides a structural basis for how TMEM16F integrates ionic and lipid-mediated signals, enabling flexible modulation of membrane properties in response to diverse cellular demands.

Transcriptional regulatory mechanism of TMEM16F

Emerging evidence indicates that TMEM16F expression is finely tuned by a multilayered regulatory network involving microRNAs (miRNAs) and transcription factors, enabling cell–type–specific and context-dependent control of its function.

One well-characterized regulatory axis centers on miR-223-3p, the most abundant miRNA in platelets and megakaryocytes (MKs). miR-223-3p directly targets TMEM16F mRNA, as demonstrated by luciferase reporter assays confirming its binding to the 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR). Functional studies reveal an inverse relationship between miR-223-3p abundance and TMEM16F expression: miR-223-3p overexpression markedly suppresses TMEM16F mRNA levels, whereas its inhibition leads to a substantial increase in TMEM16F expression. Importantly, this post-transcriptional regulation translates into functional consequences at the cellular level. Elevated miR-223-3p attenuates TMEM16F-dependent phosphatidylserine (PS) exposure, reducing the formation of procoagulant platelets and impairing platelet-supported thrombin generation, whereas miR-223-3p downregulation enhances procoagulant platelet formation. These findings establish miR-223-3p as a key negative regulator of TMEM16F-driven platelet procoagulant activity [17].

In addition, a distinct regulatory axis consists of NRF2 and miR-17-5p, linking inflammatory signaling to TMEM16F-mediated coagulation in sepsis. NRF2 enhances TMEM16F expression in endothelial cells through dual mechanisms: direct binding to the TMEM16F promoter and indirect suppression of miR-17-5p, a miRNA that negatively regulates TMEM16F mRNA. This coordinated regulation results in increased TMEM16F expression under inflammatory and thrombotic conditions, such as lipopolysaccharide-induced sepsis. Functionally, elevated TMEM16F promotes phospholipid scrambling and PS exposure on the endothelial surface, processes that amplify microvascular thrombosis and inflammatory responses [18].

Overall, these findings reveal that TMEM16F is regulated by a multi-layered transcriptional network, with miR-223-3p governing platelet procoagulant activity and the NRF2/miR-17-5p axis mediating inflammation-associated coagulation. Such regulatory complexity underscores TMEM16F’s central role in integrating miRNA- and transcription factor-dependent pathways to modulate coagulation and inflammation in physiological and pathological contexts.

Regulation of TMEM16F activity

Intracellular Ca2+ signaling: functional coupling with upstream channels and TMEM16F activation

Intracellular Ca2+ signaling plays a pivotal role in the activation of TMEM16F, which mediates PS externalization and ion conductance on the plasma membrane [19, 20]. Activation of TMEM16F requires relatively high local concentrations of intracellular Ca²⁺ (EC₅₀ ≈ 10 µM), which are typically achieved through spatially restricted microdomains rather than global cytosolic Ca²⁺ elevation [21, 22]. These microdomains are generated by Ca²⁺ influx via specific plasma membrane channels, such as TRPV4 and PIEZO1, which act as key upstream activators.

Further supporting the importance of intracellular Ca2+ in TMEM16F regulation, studies have shown that Ca2+ influx through channels such as TRPV4 activates TMEM16F in human trophoblasts, facilitating cell fusion events critical for placental development. This functional coupling emphasizes that local Ca2+ microdomains and their associated pH environments are key determinants of TMEM16F activity in physiological contexts [19]. This pathway contributes to syncytiotrophoblast formation, a process essential for placental development [19, 23]. Additionally, electrophysiological analyses reveal that both intracellular and extracellular Ca2+ concentrations modulate TMEM16F channel kinetics and activity, with intracellular Ca2+ elevation doubling channel activity, while extracellular Ca2+ enhances channel opening frequency without altering ion permeability [24]. These findings suggest that Ca2+ regulation of TMEM16F is multifaceted, involving both direct binding and modulation of channel gating properties.

In red blood cells (RBCs), which lack intracellular Ca²⁺ stores and canonical signaling organelles, the mechanosensitive channel PIEZO1 constitutes the primary route for Ca²⁺ entry. Mechanical deformation or membrane stretch activates PIEZO1, generating local Ca²⁺ microdomains that are sufficient to trigger TMEM16F-dependent phospholipid scrambling [20, 23]. Genetic deletion of TMEM16F in murine erythrocytes completely abolishes Ca²⁺-induced PS exposure, confirming TMEM16F as the sole CaPLSase in this system [20].

Beyond erythroid and placental systems, studies in HEK293 cells demonstrate that local Ca²⁺ entry through TRPC1 channels can activate endogenous calcium-activated chloride channels (CaCCs) with biophysical properties characteristic of TMEM16F, including strong outward rectification, voltage-dependent single-channel conductance (3–60 pS), and insensitivity to Ba²⁺ [25]. The inability of the fast Ca²⁺ chelator BAPTA to fully suppress channel activity suggests nanodomain coupling (< 10 nm) between TRPC1 and these CaCCs. Although the molecular identity was not definitively confirmed due to co-expression of multiple anoctamin isoforms in HEK293 cells, the functional profile strongly implicates TMEM16F or a closely related family member [25].

Recent studies further refine the regulatory landscape of TMEM16F. A conserved N-terminal domain may function as an auxiliary Ca²⁺ reservoir that modulates sensitivity to cytosolic Ca²⁺, potentially lowering the activation threshold under conditions such as increased membrane curvature or elevated temperature [22]. Additionally, BAPTA has been shown to exert Ca²⁺-independent inhibitory effects on endogenous ANO6-like currents, possibly through membrane partitioning, underscoring the need for caution in interpreting chelator-based experiments [21].

Collectively, these findings support a model in which TMEM16F acts as a localized effector of stimulus-specific Ca²⁺ signals—whether mechanical (via PIEZO1), osmotic/thermal (via TRPV4), or receptor-mediated (via TRPC1)—thereby linking diverse extracellular cues to rapid, spatially constrained phospholipid scrambling. This channel-scramblase/ion channel coupling may influence diverse physiological and pathological processes ranging from placental development to thromboinflammatory disorders [26, 27].

pH-dependent regulation of TMEM16F activity

TMEM16F’s dual-function is intricately regulated by intracellular pH (pHi) and Ca2+ concentration, exhibiting a complex interplay that reflects the dynamic cellular environment.

Recent studies have demonstrated that intracellular pH (pHi​) modulates the Ca2+ sensitivity of TMEM16F through proton competition at the primary Ca2+-binding sites (TM6-TM8), leading to pH-dependent shifts in the equilibrium between closed and activated conformations [1]. At physiological Ca2+ concentrations, low pHi​ stabilizes closed states by reducing Ca2+ binding affinity, while high pHi​ promotes activation; this regulation exhibits a bell-shaped relationship with intracellular Ca2+ concentrations [Ca2+]i, peaking at ~ 15 µM [Ca2+]i [1]. The physiological relevance of this pH-dependent modulation is highlighted by the association of TMEM16F with diseases characterized by dysregulated intracellular pH, such as cancer, where altered pHi may influence TMEM16F-mediated processes including coagulation, cell fusion, and immune responses [1].

Intriguingly, the use of calcium chelators such as BAPTA has revealed calcium-independent effects on TMEM16F channels, indicating that experimental conditions can influence observed channel behavior and highlighting the complexity of Ca2+ regulation [21]. Moreover, the interplay between Ca2+ and pH regulation is further complicated by the distinct ion selectivity and gating mechanisms of TMEM16F, which can vary depending on recording configurations and cellular context [28].

Taken together, these regulatory layers indicate that TMEM16F functions as a sensor–integrator of local microenvironmental signals, translating combined Ca²⁺, pH and membrane-derived inputs into finely tuned ion–lipid transport responses. Such multilayered regulation is likely essential for restricting TMEM16F activation in space and time, thereby preventing inappropriate or chronic phospholipid scrambling that could otherwise be misinterpreted as a cell death or immune clearance signal [1, 19, 21, 24, 28]. Having outlined the regulatory mechanisms governing TMEM16F activity, we next discuss its major physiological functions.

Procoagulant activity in platelets and endothelial cells

TMEM16F functions as a central molecular link between intracellular Ca²⁺ signaling and procoagulant membrane remodeling. In platelets, Ca²⁺-dependent activation of TMEM16F drives rapid phospholipid scrambling and externalization of PS, thereby generating a negatively charged membrane surface that supports the assembly of coagulation enzyme complexes and efficient thrombin generation [29].

Beyond platelets, TMEM16F-dependent phospholipid scrambling also operates in vascular endothelial cells, where it contributes to the formation of procoagulant membrane domains. Endothelial TMEM16F activity is proposed to cooperate with platelet-derived PS-exposing surfaces, collectively sustaining localized hemostatic responses at sites of vascular injury. This coordinated mechanism highlights TMEM16F as a shared executor of Ca²⁺-dependent membrane remodeling across distinct vascular cell types [29, 30].

At the regulatory level, TMEM16F activity is tightly coupled to Ca²⁺ influx and is modulated by the lipid composition of the plasma membrane. Alterations in membrane sphingomyelin content affect the distribution of TMEM16F within membrane microdomains, thereby influencing its sensitivity to Ca²⁺ and the extent of PS externalization. These observations indicate that membrane lipid composition serves as an important determinant of TMEM16F-mediated procoagulant activity [31]. The pathological relevance of TMEM16F-mediated PS exposure extends to traumatic brain injury (TBI)-induced coagulopathy, where hypercoagulability correlates with increased platelet PS exposure and scramblase activity. Treatment with benzbromarone, a uric acid-lowering agent, reduces TMEM16F expression and PS externalization on platelets, thereby ameliorating hypercoagulability and improving neurological outcomes post-TBI [32].

Collectively, available evidence supports a mechanistic framework in which TMEM16F integrates Ca²⁺ signaling with membrane lipid asymmetry to control the generation of procoagulant surfaces in platelets and endothelial cells. Through this role, TMEM16F coordinates cellular contributions to coagulation by regulating when and where PS exposure occurs within the vascular compartment.

Regulation of spinal motoneuron excitability and motor endurance

TMEM16F plays a pivotal role in modulating the function of spinal α-motoneurons, particularly in the context of high-demand motor performance. Specifically expressed in choline acetyltransferase-positive (ChAT⁺) α-motoneurons within the ventral horn of the spinal cord, TMEM16F localizes as synaptic clusters at the postsynaptic membrane opposing cholinergic C-boutons—specialized synapses critical for fine-tuning motoneuron excitability during strenuous motor tasks. As a CaCC, TMEM16F mediates a Ca²⁺-dependent Cl⁻ conductance that directly regulates the recruitment threshold of fast α-motoneurons, with a selective impact on the fast-fatigable (FF) subtype, which exhibits the highest activation threshold among motoneuron populations [33].

Functional deletion of TMEM16F (in Tmem16f ⁻/⁻ mice) leads to significant increases in the rheobase (threshold current) of FF α-motoneurons, while exerting minimal effects on slow-reacting (Type 1) or fatigue-resistant (FR, Type 2) α-motoneurons. This elevation in recruitment threshold impairs the ability of FF α-motoneurons to be efficiently activated during strenuous motor activities that require rapid, high-force muscle contractions. Consistently, behavioral assessments demonstrate that Tmem16f ⁻/⁻ mice exhibit reduced treadmill endurance, including a 17% decrease in maximal running speed and shortened running distance/duration at 60% of maximal speed compared to wild-type littermates. Overall, these findings establish TMEM16F as an essential regulator of motor resistance, whereby its modulation of FF α-motoneuron excitability enables the recruitment of high-threshold motor units necessary for sustaining intense or prolonged motor performance [33].

Regulation of cell-cell fusion

Cell–cell fusion is a fundamental biological process in multicellular development and tissue organization. Trophoblast fusion during placental development requires coordinated reorganization of plasma membrane architecture, including dynamic regulation of lipid asymmetry at sites of cell–cell contact. Regulated externalization of PS has emerged as a conserved mechanistic step in this process [34–37].

TMEM16F, a Ca²⁺-activated phospholipid scramblase, mediates transbilayer PS movement from the inner to the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane. TMEM16F-dependent lipid scrambling disrupts membrane asymmetry and is proposed to generate a fusion-permissive membrane environment by altering membrane lipid organization [38]. Notably, PS exposure during trophoblast fusion occurs independently of apoptotic signaling, indicating a regulated physiological role for TMEM16F-mediated membrane remodeling.

Activation of TMEM16F depends on localized intracellular Ca²⁺ microdomains rather than global cytosolic Ca²⁺ elevations. In trophoblasts, the Ca²⁺-permeable channel TRPV4 provides spatially restricted Ca²⁺ influx that efficiently triggers TMEM16F-dependent phospholipid scrambling, supporting a tight functional coupling between Ca²⁺ entry and lipid remodeling [19].

In addition to chemical cues, mechanical signals also contribute to the regulation of trophoblast fusion. The mechanosensitive ion channel PIEZO1 is highly expressed in placental trophoblasts and plays a critical role in syncytiotrophoblast formation. Mechanical activation of PIEZO1 induces Ca²⁺ influx, which in turn activates TMEM16F and promotes PS externalization. Placental deletion of PIEZO1 results in defective syncytiotrophoblast formation and embryonic lethality, closely phenocopying TMEM16F deficiency and further underscoring the central importance of Ca²⁺-dependent membrane remodeling in trophoblast fusion [23].

Together, these findings support a unified mechanistic framework in which TMEM16F acts as a central executor of Ca²⁺-dependent phospholipid scrambling during trophoblast fusion, with ion channel–derived Ca²⁺ microdomains providing spatial and temporal control over membrane lipid reorganization.

Involvement in plasma membrane repair and tension homeostasis

Plasma membrane integrity is fundamental for cellular homeostasis, and its breach by external insults, such as pore-forming toxins (PFTs), constitutes a direct threat to cell survival. To counteract this, eukaryotic cells have evolved an intrinsic, calcium (Ca²⁺)-dependent plasma membrane repair (PMR) machinery. Recent work has established the lipid scramblase TMEM16F as a critical and non-redundant component of this protective response against PFT-mediated injury.

PMR is a Ca²⁺-dependent process essential for cell survival following injury, such as that caused by bacterial PFTs. TMEM16F has emerged as a critical mediator of this response. Upon PFT-induced Ca²⁺ influx, TMEM16F is rapidly activated, thereby driving PS externalization. Rather than merely signaling apoptosis, this PS exposure actively promotes membrane repair by enhancing local membrane fluidity and facilitating the formation of blebs and extracellular vesicles (EVs) that shed damaged membrane regions [39, 40].

Beyond lesion removal, TMEM16F also regulates plasma membrane surface area through its functional coupling with dynamins, large GTPases involved in membrane remodeling. Recent studies have identified a Ca²⁺-responsive membrane reservoir system composed of plasma membrane invaginations stabilized by dynamin oligomers. Activation of TMEM16F induces bidirectional phospholipid scrambling, which alters the lipid composition and biophysical properties of the membrane, leading to relaxation of dynamin-mediated constriction and opening of these membrane reservoirs [41].Release of membrane reservoirs enables rapid lateral expansion of the plasma membrane, contributing to the regulation of membrane tension and facilitating adaptive morphological responses [41].

Taken together, these findings support a mechanistic framework in which TMEM16F functions as a Ca²⁺-activated lipid remodeling switch that coordinates membrane repair, surface area regulation, and tension homeostasis through dynamic interactions with dynamin-dependent membrane structures.

Modulation of angiogenesis via endothelial junction dynamics

TMEM16F regulates endothelial junction dynamics by coupling Ca²⁺-dependent phospholipid scrambling to intracellular signaling pathways that control adherens junction stability. Consistent with this role, angiogenesis defects have been observed in zebrafish embryos following TMEM16F knockdown [42]. In endothelial cells, vascular endothelial (VE)-cadherin is a core component of adherens junctions and a key regulator of vascular integrity and angiogenic remodeling [26, 30, 43, 44].

TMEM16F mediates the externalization of PS and other anionic phospholipids, generating localized membrane environments that influence kinase recruitment and activation. Evidence indicates that TMEM16F activity modulates Src family kinase phosphorylation at endothelial junctions. Src-dependent phosphorylation of VE-cadherin promotes its internalization and turnover, thereby facilitating dynamic remodeling of cell–cell adhesions during angiogenesis.

Mechanistically, TMEM16F-dependent lipid scrambling is proposed to regulate Src signaling by altering membrane charge and lipid composition, which affects Src localization and activity. Through this mechanism, TMEM16F indirectly controls VE-cadherin stability and junctional organization, linking membrane lipid asymmetry to endothelial permeability and sprouting behavior. Consistent with this model, loss or inhibition of TMEM16F is associated with altered Src phosphorylation and reduced VE-cadherin abundance, supporting a role for TMEM16F in coordinating lipid remodeling with adherens junction regulation during angiogenesis [26, 43, 44].

Context-dependent regulation of immune cell signaling

Lipids are not only structural components that form the physical structure of cell membranes and maintain cellular integrity but also directly participate in signal transduction processes. Acidic phospholipids, such as PS, phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), and inositol phosphate (PI), are preferentially localized to the inner leaflet of the cell membrane, and their negative charges provide specific docking sites for membrane-associated proteins and cytoplasmic proteins containing polybasic amino acid fragments. Studies have confirmed that TMEM16F is involved in regulating immune cell activation, but its specific functions are context-dependent and even involve seemingly contradictory reports [45–47].

In natural killer (NK) cells, activation triggers TMEM16F-dependent phospholipid scrambling, leading to PS exposure on the plasma membrane. This lipid redistribution is not a marker of apoptosis but functions as a negative feedback mechanism: it reduces surface expression of activating receptors (e.g., 2B4) and impairs the association of Src family kinases (such as Fyn and Lck) with the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane, thereby attenuating NK cell signaling and cytotoxic function [45].

Similarly, in CD8⁺ T cells during chronic viral infection, T cell receptor (TCR) engagement induces Ca²⁺ influx, which activates TMEM16F and drives phospholipid scrambling. This process has been shown to limit excessive T cell activation and prevent T cell exhaustion—likely by promoting the formation of multivesicular bodies (MVBs), which sequester activated TCRs and associated signaling molecules and deliver them to lysosomes for degradation, thereby terminating TCR signaling [46].

In contrast, a study in Jurkat T cells revealed a potentiating role for TMEM16F. Upon TCR stimulation, TMEM16F-mediated lipid scrambling alters the electrostatic properties of the inner plasma membrane leaflet, causing dissociation of non-engaged (“bystander”) TCR-CD3 complexes from the membrane. This bystander dissociation reduces background signaling noise, thereby enhancing the sensitivity and strength of antigen-specific TCR signaling and ultimately amplifying T cell activation [47].

Collectively, these studies demonstrate that TMEM16F-mediated phospholipid scrambling can exert dual—and opposing—effects on immune cell function: it may either dampen activation (as in NK cells and exhausted T cells) or enhance antigen-specific responses (as in Jurkat T cells), depending on the cellular context and activation conditions.

Platelet-centric mechanism: defective PS externalization

Scott syndrome is a rare hereditary bleeding disorder defined by a profound defect in PS externalization on activated platelets, a process that is indispensable for normal blood coagulation. TMEM16F has been identified as the causative gene underlying this syndrome and encodes a Ca²⁺-activated phospholipid scramblase responsible for bidirectional transbilayer movement of phospholipids, including PS. Loss-of-function mutations in TMEM16F result in defective scrambling activity, leading to impaired exposure of PS on the platelet surface during activation, which is critical for assembling coagulation factor complexes and promoting thrombin generation. This defect disrupts the procoagulant surface of platelets, thereby causing bleeding diathesis in affected individuals.

Functional studies have demonstrated that TMEM16F mediates both ion channel activity and phospholipid transport through a shared subunit cavity, and that conformational changes linked to channel gating are required for scramblase function. Mutations impair these processes, resulting in defective phospholipid scrambling and coagulation failure [48]. Furthermore, the pathological mechanism extends to altered platelet membrane microdomains, where TMEM16F distribution and activation are modulated by lipid composition, such as sphingomyelin content. For instance, deficiency of sphingomyelin synthase 1 (SMS1) enhances Ca²⁺ influx and TMEM16F-mediated PS externalization, promoting aberrant platelet clearance and thrombocytopenia, which secondarily aggravate hemostatic dysfunction [31]. These findings elucidate the molecular basis by which TMEM16F dysfunction in Scott syndrome compromises platelet procoagulant activity and leads to bleeding disorders.

Endothelial cell involvement: impaired vascular hemostasis

Beyond platelets, TMEM16F also contributes to vascular hemostasis through its expression in endothelial cells (ECs), thereby extending the pathophysiological framework of Scott syndrome beyond a platelet-centric model. Although ECs normally present an anticoagulant surface, they can acquire a procoagulant phenotype under pathological conditions by externalizing PS at the vessel wall. Genetic ablation of TMEM16F specifically in ECs results in marked hemostatic impairment in vivo. Endothelial cell–specific TMEM16F knockout mice exhibit significantly prolonged tail bleeding times and reduced venous thrombus formation following inferior vena cava stenosis, indicating defective clot formation capacity [29]. Mechanistically, endothelial TMEM16F mediates Ca²⁺-dependent PS exposure required for efficient thrombin generation and fibrin deposition during thrombosis. Pharmacological inhibition of TMEM16F scramblase activity in ECs similarly attenuates PS externalization and endothelial procoagulant activity. Notably, TMEM16F cooperates with its paralog TMEM16E to spatially concentrate PS exposure at the vessel wall rather than on platelets during thrombus formation [43]. Overall, these observations establish TMEM16F loss-of-function as a unifying mechanism underlying defective procoagulant surface generation across multiple cellular compartments, culminating in bleeding disorders.

Role of TMEM16F in hematologic disorders

In contrast to bleeding disorders caused by insufficient TMEM16F activity, excessive or dysregulated activation of TMEM16F contributes to a distinct spectrum of hemolytic and thromboinflammatory diseases. In mature RBCs, which lack nuclei, mitochondria, and internal Ca²⁺ stores, TMEM16F operates within a minimal mechanotransduction module formed by functional coupling with the mechanosensitive Ca²⁺ channel PIEZO1. PIEZO1-TMEM16F axis enables RBCs to convert mechanical stimuli—such as shear stress, membrane deformation, or sickling-induced distortion—into biochemical responses through a defined sequence: PIEZO1 opening, localized Ca²⁺ influx, TMEM16F activation, and subsequent PS externalization [20, 23].

Dysregulation of this pathway plays a central role in hereditary xerocytosis, where gain-of-function mutations in PIEZO1 cause chronic Ca²⁺ influx. Sustained Ca²⁺ entry leads to persistent activation of TMEM16F, continuous PS exposure, and secondary activation of the Ca²⁺-sensitive Gardos channel (KCNN4). The resulting K⁺ efflux drives cellular dehydration, increased membrane fragility, and hemolysis, establishing a direct mechanistic link between PIEZO1 hyperactivity, TMEM16F overactivation, and red cell pathology [20].

In sickle cell disease (SCD), deoxygenation triggers polymerization of mutant hemoglobin S, inducing RBC sickling and membrane distortion. This mechanical stress potently activates PIEZO1, initiating a cascade of Ca²⁺ entry, TMEM16F-dependent PS externalization, and downstream prothrombotic events. Exposed PS serves as a catalytic surface for tenase and prothrombinase complexes, accelerating thrombin generation. It also promotes the release of procoagulant microparticles and enhances RBC adhesion to ECs via PS-recognizing receptors (e.g., TIM-4, stabilin-2), collectively exacerbating vaso-occlusion and thromboinflammation [23, 27].

Multiple lines of evidence validate this axis as a therapeutic target. TMEM16F KO murine RBCs are completely resistant to Ca²⁺-induced PS exposure [20]. In human SCD RBCs, pharmacological inhibition of PIEZO1 with benzbromarone or the peptide toxin GsMTx4 suppresses Ca²⁺ influx, PS externalization, microparticle release, and endothelial adhesion [23, 27]. Moreover, TMEM16F-specific affibody molecules—engineered binding proteins that selectively inhibit TMEM16F without affecting Ca²⁺ entry—effectively block PS exposure, confirming TMEM16F as the indispensable downstream effector [49].

Taken together, these findings delineate a disease context in which TMEM16F hyperactivation, driven by mechanical stress and aberrant Ca²⁺ signaling, promotes PS exposure on RBCs, thereby endowing them with enhanced procoagulant and pro-adhesive properties. Elucidation of this pathway not only clarifies the molecular basis of thrombotic complications in hemoglobinopathies and related hemolytic disorders but also provides a rationale for therapeutic strategies targeting either PIEZO1-mediated Ca²⁺ entry or TMEM16F-dependent phospholipid scrambling [20, 23, 27].

Role of TMEM16F in neurodegenerative diseases

TMEM16F has emerged as an important regulator of pathological processes in the central nervous system (CNS), particularly in neurodegenerative diseases characterized by progressive neuronal loss, protein aggregation, and chronic neuroinflammation. A convergent theme across multiple disease models is the aberrant activation of TMEM16F-mediated PS externalization, which profoundly influences neuron–microglia interactions and innate immune responses in the brain (Fig. 4).

Under physiological conditions, PS is confined to the inner leaflet of the neuronal plasma membrane, whereas its externalization serves as an “eat-me” signal that promotes microglial recognition and phagocytosis. In pathological settings, TMEM16F-dependent PS exposure can be aberrantly induced in stressed but viable neurons, thereby enhancing microglial activation and neuroinflammation. This mechanism has been implicated in multiple models of neurodegeneration, positioning TMEM16F as a key molecular switch that links neuronal stress to microglial-mediated neuronal loss [50, 51].

Involvement in tauopathies and Alzheimer’s disease

In tauopathies, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), TMEM16F-mediated PS exposure is aberrantly activated in neurons burdened with pathological phosphorylated tau. In the PS19 mouse model expressing mutant human tau (P301S), genetic deletion of TMEM16F results in a marked reduction in tau pathology and microgliosis. Notably, neuron-specific ablation of TMEM16F, but not microglia-specific deletion, recapitulates these protective effects, indicating that neuronal TMEM16F is the primary driver of tau-associated neurodegeneration [50]. These findings suggest that TMEM16F facilitates tau-driven pathology by promoting neuronal PS exposure, which in turn enhances microglial activation and phagocytic responses.

TMEM16F also contributes to neuroinflammation and disease progression in amyloid-driven AD models. Elevated TMEM16F expression has been observed in APP/PS1 transgenic mice, and knockdown of TMEM16F leads to improved spatial memory, reduced amyloid-β (Aβ) plaque burden, and decreased neuronal apoptosis. Mechanistically, TMEM16F modulates microglial polarization by favoring a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype while suppressing the transition to the anti-inflammatory M2 state. TMEM16F knockdown attenuates activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and reduces the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α, thereby dampening neuroinflammatory cascades that exacerbate neuronal injury [52].

Contribution to ischemic neuronal injury

Beyond chronic neurodegeneration, TMEM16F-mediated PS exposure also contributes to neuronal loss in acute brain injury. In models of cerebral ischemia–reperfusion, TMEM16F expression is upregulated, leading to reversible PS exposure on stressed yet viable neurons. This aberrant PS signaling promotes microglial phagocytosis and exacerbates neuronal loss. Knockdown of TMEM16F reduces PS externalization, limits microglial engulfment of neurons, decreases infarct volume, and improves functional recovery, highlighting a neuroprotective effect of TMEM16F inhibition in ischemic injury [51].

Regulation of α-synuclein propagation in Parkinson’s disease

TMEM16F has further been implicated in synucleinopathies such as Parkinson’s disease (PD), where it regulates the secretion and propagation of pathological α-synuclein (α-syn). TMEM16F deficiency reduces the release and cell-to-cell spread of α-syn aggregates in neuronal cultures and in vivo mouse models. Consistently, a TMEM16F variant with enhanced scramblase activity has been identified in PD patients and correlates with altered α-syn secretion, linking TMEM16F function to disease susceptibility and progression [53]. These findings suggest that TMEM16F facilitates the dissemination of neurotoxic protein species, thereby contributing to the spread of pathology in PD.

Role in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)

Beyond its roles in cortical neurodegeneration, accumulating evidence highlights a critical involvement of TMEM16F in spinal motoneuron function and in the pathogenesis of ALS, a devastating neurodegenerative disorder primarily affecting motor systems [33]. In the SOD1G93A mouse model, which recapitulates key features of human ALS, genetic ablation of TMEM16F delays disease onset and attenuates motor decline in a sex-dependent manner, with pronounced effects observed in males [33].

Mechanistically, TMEM16F functions as a CaCC in spinal α-motoneurons, where its activation generates an outward current that contributes to the regulation of neuronal excitability and recruitment thresholds. Loss of TMEM16F increases the activation threshold of fast α-motoneurons—particularly those with high recruitment thresholds—thereby limiting excessive neuronal firing. This shift in excitability is consistent with a reduction in activity-dependent intracellular Ca²⁺ stress, as reflected by decreased ATF3 expression, a transcriptional marker induced by elevated Ca²⁺ levels. Importantly, ALS is characterized by the selective vulnerability of high-threshold, fast-fatigable (FF) motoneurons. In this context, TMEM16F-dependent modulation of excitability may preferentially impact these degeneration-prone neuronal populations, linking intrinsic electrophysiological properties to disease susceptibility [33].

Collectively, current evidence establishes TMEM16F as a context-dependent regulator of neurodegenerative pathology with divergent functions across disease entities and neuronal populations. In cortical neurodegenerative conditions, including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and ischemic brain injury, aberrant TMEM16F-mediated PS externalization emerges as a central mechanism linking neuronal stress to microglial activation, maladaptive phagocytosis, and chronic neuroinflammation. In contrast, in ALS, TMEM16F primarily regulates membrane excitability through its Ca²⁺-activated Cl⁻ conductance, thereby contributing to the differential vulnerability of fast-fatigable motoneuron subtypes. This functional divergence underscores the pleiotropic and cell-type–specific roles of TMEM16F in the nervous system and highlights the need for disease- and context-selective therapeutic strategies. While recent advances, including the development of TMEM16F-specific affibodies, demonstrate the feasibility of selectively modulating TMEM16F activity, a deeper understanding of its molecular regulation, temporal dynamics, and safety profiles in the CNS will be essential before TMEM16F-targeted interventions can be translated into effective therapies for neurodegenerative diseases [49].

Role of TMEM16F in infectious diseases

TMEM16F has emerged as an important regulator of host-pathogen interactions, exerting context-dependent functions in infectious diseases through its role in membrane lipid scrambling, plasma membrane integrity, and thromboinflammatory signaling. Rather than acting as a classical antimicrobial effector, TMEM16F primarily modulates host cell resilience and inflammatory responses, thereby shaping disease outcomes during bacterial and viral infections.

TMEM16F preserves Kupffer cell integrity in bacterial infection

A critical protective role of TMEM16F has been demonstrated in host defense against Listeria monocytogenes (Lm) infection. TMEM16F expression in liver-resident Kupffer cells is indispensable for maintaining hepatic immune homeostasis during bacterial challenge. Mechanistically, TMEM16F-mediated phospholipid scrambling alters plasma membrane lipid composition and increases membrane fluidity, which is essential for repairing membrane damage induced by the pore-forming toxin listeriolysin O (LLO). In the absence of TMEM16F, Kupffer cells exhibit plasma membrane rupture and fragmentation, leading to lytic cell death.

This loss of Kupffer cell integrity results in uncontrolled release of danger-associated molecular patterns, excessive liver inflammation, dysregulated lipid metabolism, and increased bacterial burden. Importantly, TMEM16F does not directly enhance bacterial clearance; instead, it preserves Kupffer cell viability and prevents immunopathology by safeguarding plasma membrane integrity under toxin-induced stress. Cell type–specific knockout studies further demonstrate that deletion of TMEM16F in Kupffer cells—but not in T or B lymphocytes—recapitulates these pathological features, underscoring a specialized and nonredundant role for TMEM16F in tissue-resident macrophages during bacterial infection [40] .

TMEM16F links inflammatory signaling to immunothrombosis in sepsis

Beyond localized bacterial defense, TMEM16F also participates in systemic inflammatory responses during sepsis. Sepsis is defined by dysregulated host immunity to infection, where uncontrolled crosstalk between inflammation and coagulation drives endothelial injury, microvascular thrombosis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and multiorgan failure [54].

TMEM16F has emerged as a critical mediator linking innate immune sensing of bacterial endotoxin (lipopolysaccharide, LPS) to coagulation cascade activation during sepsis [55, 56]. Upon cytosolic LPS recognition, caspase-11 cleaves and activates gasdermin D (GSDMD), which forms membrane pores to trigger calcium influx. This rise in intracellular Ca²⁺ subsequently activates TMEM16F, leading to PS externalization on the cell surface independently of pyroptosis. Exposed PS markedly enhances tissue factor (TF) activity, initiating excessive coagulation, fibrin deposition, microvascular thrombosis, and eventually life-threatening DIC in endotoxemia and sepsis. Genetic silencing or pharmacological inhibition of TMEM16F attenuates PS exposure, TF activation, and coagulopathy, supporting a role for TMEM16F as a downstream effector of the caspase-11–GSDMD pathway in sepsis-associated immunothrombosis [55].

Recent mechanistic studies in cellular, murine, and clinical models have uncovered a NRF2/miR-17-5p/TMEM16F regulatory axis that couples inflammatory signaling to prothrombotic dysfunction in sepsis [18].TMEM16F, a Ca²⁺-activated phospholipid scramblase highly expressed in endothelial cells (ECs), platelets, and macrophages, mediates PS externalization and microvesicle (MV) release, thereby providing catalytic surfaces for tenase and prothrombinase complexes that accelerate thrombin generation. In LPS-induced septic mice and cultured ECs, TMEM16F expression is markedly upregulated, whereas its genetic deletion reduces microthrombus formation, proinflammatory cytokine secretion (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α), lung and liver damage, and mortality. These findings support a contributory role of TMEM16F in driving immunothrombotic responses in sepsis [18].

Clinically, circulating TMEM16F and miR-17-5p levels correlate tightly with disease severity. In septic patients, plasma TMEM16F is positively associated with markers of coagulation activation (PAI-1, D-dimer), inflammation (CRP, IL-6), and DIC scores, whereas miR-17-5p shows inverse correlations. Notably, non-survivors display higher TMEM16F and lower miR-17-5p compared with survivors, supporting their potential as prognostic biomarkers [18].

Collectively, these observations position TMEM16F as a molecular link between inflammation and thrombosis in sepsis and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target for modulating immunothrombotic pathology.

Viral exploitation of TMEM16F in membrane fusion and pathogenesis

In contrast to its protective role in bacterial infection, TMEM16F can be exploited by viral pathogens to facilitate pathological membrane fusion events [57]. Several viral fusogens, including the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, have been shown to activate or depend on host TMEM16F activity to induce PS externalization and promote cell–cell fusion and syncytia formation [58, 59]. These fusion events enable viral spread and immune evasion but also contribute to tissue damage and chronic inflammation. Alterations in TMEM16F function or expression could therefore influence the degree of local inflammation and tissue damage, as observed in the differential symptomatology of COVID-19 variants [40, 60].

Emerging evidence further links TMEM16F-mediated membrane fusion to virus-associated neuropathology. TMEM16F-dependent fusion events have been implicated in the spread of pathological tau species and the formation of multinucleated microglia, which are associated with neuroinflammation and neuronal loss. Pharmacological inhibition of TMEM16F, including the use of specific blockers or senolytic strategies, has been proposed to limit pathological cell fusion, cellular senescence, and neurodegenerative progression in the context of viral infection [61].

In summary, these findings establish TMEM16F as a pivotal regulator at the intersection of infection, inflammation, and membrane dynamics. In bacterial infection, TMEM16F preserves tissue-resident immune cell integrity and prevents excessive immunopathology, whereas in viral infection, it can be co-opted to drive detrimental membrane fusion and inflammatory damage. This functional duality underscores the context-dependent roles of TMEM16F in infectious diseases and highlights its potential as a therapeutic target—either to be enhanced for host protection or inhibited to mitigate pathogen-driven pathology.

Role of TMEM16F in tumor immunity

TMEM16F functions as a pivotal node linking plasma membrane lipid dynamics, regulated cell death and tumor immune evasion [62–65]. TMEM16F is activated during the execution phase of ferroptosis by elevated intracellular Ca²⁺ to mediate phospholipid scrambling—redistributing phosphatidylethanolamine, PS and other phospholipids across the plasma membrane bilayer. This activity locally remodels lipid architecture at sites of peroxidation, diluting oxidized phospholipid clusters and reducing membrane tension—thereby preserving membrane integrity and suppressing lytic cell death. In the absence of TMEM16F, ferroptotic cells undergo rapid plasma membrane rupture and release damage-associated molecular patterns, resulting in immunogenic cell death that drives adaptive immune-dependent antitumor immunity [62].

Critically, TMEM16F also promotes an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment through reversible PS exposure on stressed but viable tumor cells, a process distinct from the irreversible PS externalization mediated by Xkr8 during apoptosis [63, 65]. PS externalization engages TAM (Tyro3, Axl, MerTK) and TIM family receptors on immune cells, thereby reinforcing immunosuppressive signaling [66, 67]. Consistently, Genetic ablation of TMEM16F attenuates tumor growth in immunocompetent, but not immunodeficient mice, confirming its immune-dependent pro-tumorigenic role [63]. Mechanistically, TMEM16F-mediated PS exposure establishes an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment through regulation of MFGE8 and MMP9, thereby promoting tumor growth in an immune-dependent manner.

The clinical relevance of TMEM16F is underscored by its context‑dependent expression across malignancies. It is overexpressed in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, gastric cancer, and glioma, where it promotes metastasis and correlates with poor prognosis, partly through ERK pathway activation. Conversely, reduced TMEM16F levels are observed in breast, prostate, cervical and ovarian cancers, and associate with adverse outcomes—potentially due to increased susceptibility to ferroptosis. Moreover, TMEM16F expression is associated with altered macrophage polarization and T cell infiltration in multiple cancers, suggesting that it acts not only as a cell-intrinsic survival factor but also as a potential extrinsic modulator of antitumor immunity [64].

Therapeutically, inhibition of TMEM16F synergizes with PD-1 blockade to induce durable tumor rejection in preclinical models, suggesting that targeting phospholipid scrambling may overcome resistance to immune checkpoint therapy [62]. Collectively, these findings establish TMEM16F as a multifaceted regulator that integrates lipid metabolism, cell death execution and immune surveillance—highlighting the need for cancer type-specific strategies in its therapeutic exploitation [49, 62].

Therapeutic targeting of TMEM16F

Biologic agents: TMEM16F-specific affibodies

Recent advances have enabled the development of TMEM16F-specific affibodies as selective molecular tools for targeting this multifunctional membrane protein, which has long lacked specific modulators. Using phage display screening against purified human TMEM16F, high-affinity affibodies were successfully isolated and validated by multiple biophysical assays, demonstrating the feasibility of selectively targeting TMEM16F [2].

Beyond high-affinity binding, TMEM16F-specific affibodies exhibit functional activity in disease-relevant cellular models. Several candidates attenuate glutamate-induced neuronal death and suppress microglial phagocytosis, indicating a neuroprotective and immunomodulatory effect. These actions are consistent with interference in TMEM16F-mediated PS exposure and membrane remodeling, processes that govern both neuronal vulnerability and microglia–neuron interactions [49].

Given TMEM16F’s involvement in platelet procoagulant activity, cancer progression, and neurodegeneration, affibodies offer dual diagnostic and therapeutic potential. Their high specificity enables precise detection of TMEM16F expression in pathological tissues, while functional modulation of TMEM16F activity may mitigate aberrant PS exposure, excessive microglial engulfment, or pathological coagulation. In summary, TMEM16F-targeting affibodies represent a promising biologic strategy to probe and therapeutically modulate TMEM16F-dependent pathways, warranting further in vivo validation in disease models [2, 49].

Small-molecule inhibitors: pharmacological modulation of TMEM16F functions

Pharmacological targeting of TMEM16F has been advanced by the identification of small-molecule inhibitors that suppress its ion channel and lipid scramblase activities. Structural and functional studies have revealed a conserved drug-binding pocket within TMEM16F that can be occupied by compounds such as niclosamide and 1PBC. Cryo-electron microscopy and mutagenesis analyses demonstrate that binding at this site inhibits Ca²⁺ influx and/or PS externalization, indicating that TMEM16F’s ion conduction and scrambling functions can be differentially modulated [7, 14].

Niclosamide, an FDA-approved anthelmintic drug, has attracted particular interest due to its ability to inhibit TMEM16F-dependent syncytia formation induced by the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein. By suppressing Ca²⁺ signaling, membrane conductance, and PS exposure, niclosamide attenuates viral cell–cell fusion, thrombosis, and inflammation, implicating TMEM16F blockade as a potential strategy for mitigating severe COVID-19 pathology [68–70]. Niclosamide analogues with improved pharmacokinetic properties further support the therapeutic viability of this approach [71] .

TMEM16F inhibition also shows promise in non-viral disease contexts characterized by pathological PS exposure. Benzbromarone suppresses PIEZO1–TMEM16F coupling in sickle RBCs, reducing PS exposure, microparticle release, and thrombin generation, thereby alleviating hypercoagulability [27]. Similarly, TMEM16F blockade attenuates platelet procoagulant responses and reduces PS-mediated pathology in traumatic brain injury models [32, 69].

Importantly, not all reported TMEM16F inhibitors are specific; compounds such as tannic acid and epigallocatechin gallate have been shown to produce false-positive effects through fluorescence quenching rather than true enzymatic inhibition, underscoring the need for rigorous electrophysiological and imaging validation [72]. Nevertheless, structure-guided drug development highlights the possibility of achieving functional selectivity—targeting either ion channel activity or lipid scrambling—an especially valuable feature given TMEM16F’s diverse physiological roles, including coagulation, cell fusion, immune regulation, and ferroptosis suppression [7].

Together, affibody-based biologics and small-molecule inhibitors provide complementary strategies for targeting TMEM16F in disease. While affibodies offer high specificity and mechanistic precision, small molecules such as niclosamide enable pharmacological modulation with translational potential. Continued efforts to enhance selectivity, bioavailability, and functional bias will be essential for translating TMEM16F-targeted therapies into clinical applications.

Conclusions and future perspectives

Over the past three years, substantial progress has been made in elucidating the structural features, regulatory mechanisms, and biological functions of TMEM16F. Accumulating evidence indicates that TMEM16F serves as a Ca²⁺-activated membrane protein whose dual activities in phospholipid scrambling and ion conduction are tightly regulated by intracellular Ca²⁺ signals, membrane properties, and cellular context. These features provide a mechanistic basis for the broad involvement of TMEM16F in diverse physiological processes and disease states.

Despite these advances, several fundamental questions remain unresolved. A key challenge is to understand how TMEM16F selectively engages its scramblase and channel functions under distinct signaling conditions, and how different sources and spatiotemporal patterns of Ca²⁺ entry shape TMEM16F activity in vivo. In addition, the relative contributions of altered TMEM16F expression versus aberrant functional activation to disease progression remain poorly defined and are likely to be context dependent.

Future studies integrating high-resolution structural analyses, cell type–specific models, and in vivo disease systems will be essential to clarify these issues. In particular, dissecting the regulatory networks that control TMEM16F activation, functional bias, and tissue-specific roles may provide critical insights into its pathophysiological relevance. Such efforts will also inform the development of strategies aimed at selectively modulating TMEM16F activity while preserving its essential physiological functions.
