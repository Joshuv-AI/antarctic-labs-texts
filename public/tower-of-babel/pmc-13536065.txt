Comparison of cumulative opioid consumption between variable-rate feedback infusion and fixed-rate basal infusion modes of intravenous patient-controlled analgesia following mixed surgery: protocol for a prospective randomised controlled trial
Authors: Yan Xing, Minying Liu, Yanyuan Sun, Xinjun Mao, Jifeng Li, Zhanjun Zhang, Hu Sun, Fang Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInadequately managed postoperative pain remains a significant clinical challenge, often leading to delayed mobilisation and increased complications. While intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) is a mainstay of treatment, conventional fixed-rate basal infusion modes may not align with the fluctuating biphasic nature of postoperative pain. This often results in either insufficient analgesia or unnecessary opioid overexposure. Advanced network-integrated PCA pumps now allow for ‘variable-rate feedback infusion,’ which dynamically adjusts the background rate based on real-time patient demand. This study aims to determine whether a variable-rate feedback infusion mode reduces total cumulative opioid consumption at 48 hours postoperatively while providing non-inferior analgesic efficacy compared with a fixed-rate basal infusion mode.Methods and analysisThis is a multicentre, randomised, double-blind, controlled trial to be conducted at three hospitals in China. A total of 1170 patients (aged 18–65 years, American Society of Anaesthesiologists I-III) undergoing elective mixed surgery under general anaesthesia, including thoracic, abdominal, spinal, orthopaedic and cranial procedures performed using either minimally invasive or open approaches, will be recruited and randomised in a 1:1 ratio to either the fixed-rate basal infusion group or the variable-rate feedback infusion group. Both groups will receive a standardised PCA solution of sufentanil. In the variable-rate feedback infusion group, the background infusion rate will automatically increase by 0.5 mL/hour on demand boluses during lockout intervals and decrease by 0.5 mL/hour after 1 hour of inactivity. The primary outcome is the total cumulative opioid consumption at 48 hours postoperatively, including sufentanil delivered via PCA and opioid-equivalent rescue analgesics. Key secondary outcomes include resting and movement pain scores, cumulative PCA volume consumption, number and dosage of additional rescue analgesia and postoperative quality of recovery. Exploratory outcomes include sleep quality, sedation and agitation levels, and the degree of nausea and vomiting, patient’s satisfaction with PCA. These outcomes are assessed at predefined postoperative time points.Ethics and disseminationThe study has been approved by the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Tiantan Hospital (KY2025-387-02) and the Affiliated Hospital of Youjiang Medical University for Nationalities (YYFY-LL-2026-006), and the Second Affiliated Hospital of Hainan Medical University (2026-K140-01). The study will be conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants before enrolment. Findings from this study will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals and at scientific conferences. Introduction

Effective postoperative pain management is a critical component of surgical care, yet a substantial proportion of patients, reportedly approximately 80%, experience moderate to severe pain following surgery.1 2 Inadequately controlled pain can impede early mobilisation and increase the incidence of various complications, including pulmonary atelectasis, pulmonary infections, myocardial ischaemia, stress ulcers, anxiety and depression, thereby prolonging hospitalisation and elevating the risk of readmissions.3 Consequently, optimising postoperative analgesia is paramount for minimising such complications and facilitating patient recovery.

Intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) has emerged as a mainstay for delivering opioids in the postoperative setting, allowing patients to self-control analgesia according to their individual needs. The evolution of PCA pump technology can be broadly categorised into three stages: elastomeric pumps, electronic pumps, and more recently, network-integrated pumps. Elastomeric pumps offer the advantage of being lightweight, portable, disposable, and simple to manipulate, and they do not require a power source and are not susceptible to programming errors. However, a significant limitation is their inflexibility, as the infusion rate typically cannot be altered, and precise monitoring of the administered medication volume is often not feasible. Electronic PCA pumps, while enabling programmable on-demand opioid delivery, can present challenges due to complex programming interfaces that may be prone to operational errors and cannot transmit administered medication information outward.4 Network-integrated pumps represent a further advancement, offering wireless connectivity that supports central monitoring and integration with electronic medical record systems. While the network-integrated pumps can provide advanced control, the related infusion modes of PCA pumps may require further optimisation to reduce opioid consumption and strengthen pain management.

Since demand-only PCA without basal infusion struggles to manage breakthrough pain triggered by patient movement or external stimuli and its reliance on patient activation fails to address the pain during sleep, PCA using a demand dose with a basal infusion at a constant rate has been widely used. Nevertheless, conventional PCA with a fixed-rate basal infusion may not align optimally with the biphasic pattern of postoperative pain. It is more intense than anticipated immediately after surgery and less intense from the day after surgery.5 Patients may thus encounter insufficient analgesia in the acute postoperative period, necessitating frequent rescue analgesics due to lockout intervals and the fixed-rate basal infusion, which heightens the risk of opioid-related adverse effects such as respiratory depression, sedation, nausea and vomiting, potentially prolonging hospital stays and increasing healthcare costs.6 Therefore, a PCA system capable of dynamically adjusting the background infusion rate in response to real-time analgesic needs could offer improved pain control while minimising cumulative opioid consumption. Furthermore, minimising unnecessary opioid exposure is a key strategy in reducing the risks associated with prolonged postoperative opioid use. Therefore, refining PCA modalities to optimise the balance between effective analgesia and minimised opioid consumption is an essential goal in postoperative pain management, necessitating rigorous investigation into different infusion strategies.

A prospective, randomised, double-blind study involving patients undergoing laparoscopic radical cervical cancer surgery suggested that a time-scheduled decremental continuous infusion of oxycodone led to lower cumulative PCA doses than a fixed bolus-only regimen.7 Yet, numerical rating scale (NRS) scores did not differ significantly, likely because the decremental infusion still adhered to a predetermined dosing schedule rather than patient-specific feedback. Another prospective, randomised, controlled, double-blind trial in patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy found that a variable-rate feedback infusion mode where the background injection rate varies depending on the patient’s bolus demand reduced PCA volume and opioid consumption compared with a fixed-rate basal infusion mode, although no significant difference was observed in NRS pain score at 6 hours postoperatively, possibly due to limited statistical power.8 Furthermore, a randomised controlled trial comparing PCA modes after spinal fusion surgery noted fewer demand requests and reduced fentanyl consumption in a variable-rate feedback infusion group compared with the fixed-rate basal infusion group 48 hours postoperatively.9 These findings suggest that a variable-rate feedback infusion mode may reduce cumulative opioid consumption compared with a fixed-rate basal infusion. However, the broader clinical applicability of this mode across diverse surgical populations and its efficacy in multicentre settings remains untested, with limited robust evidence supporting its widespread adoption.

Therefore, we hypothesise that a variable-rate feedback infusion mode will reduce total cumulative opioid consumption at 48 hours while providing non-inferior analgesia compared with a fixed-rate basal infusion mode. To investigate this, we propose a prospective, multicentre, randomised, double-blind study in patients undergoing mixed surgery. This study extends previous single-centre work by incorporating a larger sample size, a broader surgical population and objective data capture using network-integrated PCA pumps. Previous studies were limited by small sample sizes that may have been insufficient to detect clinically meaningful differences in pain scores and other patient-centred outcomes. Pain intensity will therefore be assessed as a key secondary outcome and has been considered in the sample size estimation to ensure adequate statistical power.

Methods and analysis

The multicentre, randomised, controlled, double-blind study will be conducted at Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Affiliated Hospital of Youjiang Medical University for Nationalities, and Second Affiliated Hospital of Hainan Medical University. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Tiantan Hospital (KY2025-387-02). The trial was prospectively registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov (NCT07361822) on 22 December 2025 prior to patient enrolment. The study will adhere to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent will be obtained from all participants before enrolment. Recruitment and informed consent

Participants will be recruited from the Department of Anaesthesiology at the participating centres. Eligible patients scheduled for elective mixed surgery will be identified and approached by trained investigators or attending clinicians during the preoperative assessment period. All eligible patients will receive detailed verbal and written information about the study at least 24 hours prior to enrolment. This information will include the study objectives, procedures, potential risks and benefits, and alternative analgesic options. Participants will be given sufficient time to consider participation and will have the opportunity to ask questions before providing consent.

Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants or their legally authorised representatives prior to the initiation of any study-specific procedures. Participants will be informed that their participation is voluntary and that they may withdraw from the study at any time without any impact on their ongoing medical care. No financial or material incentives will be provided for participation in this study. A model informed consent form is provided in online supplemental file 1. The ethics-approved study protocol is included as online supplemental file 2.

Randomisation and intervention

After enrolment, the patients will be randomly assigned to either the fixed-rate basal infusion mode group or the variable-rate feedback infusion mode group in a 1:1 ratio. Randomisation will be stratified by trial site and surgery type (including thoracic, abdominal, spinal, orthopaedic and cranial procedures), with computer-generated block sequence with a block size of 4 or 6 implemented through the PROC PLAN procedure of SAS 9.2 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). Allocation concealment will be ensured through a central web-based randomisation system accessible only to authorised study personnel responsible for treatment allocation. Investigators, outcome assessors and patients will remain blinded to group assignment until completion of statistical analyses. The distribution of surgical types is expected to reflect routine clinical practice at participating centres, and recruitment will be monitored to minimise imbalance.

All participants will receive a standardised PCA solution containing sufentanil (100 µg), ondansetron (16 mg) and normal saline with a total volume of 100 mL. In the conventional fixed-rate basal infusion mode group, the PCA is set to administer a bolus of 2 mL with a lockout interval of 15 min and background infusion rate of 2 mL/h. In the variable-rate feedback infusion mode group, a bolus of 2 mL with a lockout interval of 15 min and background infusion rate of 2 mL/h, which increases to 2.5 mL/h, is used whenever the demand dose is administered within the lockout interval. The background infusion rate is automatically decreased by 0.5 mL/h when the bolus button is not pressed for 1 hour. The background infusion rate is limited to a maximum of 4.0 mL/h and a minimum of 1 mL/h, corresponding to sufentanil infusion rates of 4 µg/h and 1 µg/h, respectively.8–10 The specific variable-rate feedback infusion mode setting was based on previous research using similar feedback-controlled PCA algorithms.8 The protocol parameters were further reviewed through internal clinical assessment, as well as by participating investigators and device specialists, to ensure patient safety and to provide effective analgesia. If the PCA reservoir is exhausted during the observation period, a new PCA solution with identical settings will be prepared and connected according to the assigned treatment allocation.

At the completion of surgery, patients will be awakened and transferred to the post-anaesthesia care unit. Instructions on the use of the PCA device will be repeated. Postoperative assessments in the post-anaesthesia care unit and ward will be conducted by an independent investigator blinded to group allocation, intraoperative anaesthetic management and PCA setting. The clinician will collect all the data throughout the study period. Pain intensity will be quantified using the NRS (0, no pain and 10, worst pain possible). When patients experience pain with the NRS≥4, the post-anaesthesia care unit nurse or the patient will push the PCA button for administration of a bolus dose. When patients require additional rescue analgesics within the lockout interval, the attending physician can administer oxycodone and acetaminophen, a combination of 5 mg oxycodone hydrochloride and 325 mg paracetamol per tablet six hourly as rescue analgesics in the ward to treat pain of >4 points on the NRS. The maximum dose will be limited to four tablets per day. Rescue analgesia administration will not be restricted by the PCA device lockout mechanism and will be determined clinically by the attending physician when pain remains inadequately controlled despite PCA use.

Standard anaesthesia protocol

In the pre-anaesthetic area, all enrolled patients will receive standardised instructions on pain assessment using the NRS and operation of the PCA system. All participating centres will use the same Intelligent Network Pump (TUOREN Medical, Henan Province, China) with identical hardware, software configuration and PCA parameter settings to ensure consistency in data collection across study sites.

All patients will adhere to the standard anaesthesia protocol. Preoperative sedation will be prohibited when patients are transported to the operation room. On entering the operating room, all patients will undergo continuous monitoring of blood pressure, pulse oximetry, electrocardiography, bispectral index, end-tidal carbon dioxide, minimum alveolar concentration of the inhalation agent and urine output. Anaesthesia induction involves propofol (1.5 to 2.5 mg/kg) or ciprofol (0.3 mg/kg to 0.4 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.3 to 0.4 µg/kg) and mivacurium (0.2 mg/kg) or cisatracurium (0.2 mg/kg) followed by endotracheal intubation or double-lumen endobronchial intubation for thoracoscopic surgery. Mechanical ventilation will be maintained with tidal volume of 6 to 8 mL/kg, respiratory rate of 12 to 15 /min, inspiratory/expiratory ratio of 1:2, 40% to 60% inspired oxygen fraction at a flow rate of 1 to 2 L/min, targeting end-tidal carbon dioxide between 35 and 45 mmHg. Remifentanil (0.05 to 0.2 µg/kg/min) and sevoflurane or desflurane (0.7 to 1.2 minimum alveolar concentration) will be combined to maintain bispectral index 40 to 50 and haemodynamic parameters within ±20% of baseline. Supplemental sufentanil (0.1 to 0.2 µg/kg) will be administered for noxious stimuli response. Intraoperative fluid consisted of crystalloid at 5~10 mL/kg/h. No regional analgesic techniques or local anaesthetic infiltration will be permitted during the perioperative period, including the postoperative phase. All drug doses and fluid volume will be calculated using ideal body weight.

Data collection and measurement

All data will be collected prospectively by a research assistant blinded to group allocation. After obtaining informed consent, we will collect baseline information 1 day before surgery, including gender, age, height, weight, medical history, medication history and ASA physical status. Perioperative assessment includes type of surgery, approach of surgery, the size of the surgical incision, the type of intubation, duration of anaesthesia and operation, anaesthesia maintenance management, administered fluid intake, blood loss, urine output and dose of administered intraoperative sufentanil and remifentanil. Data collected during the use of PCA device, including cumulative opioid consumption, cumulative PCA volume consumption, postoperative pain score, bolus demand count and background infusion rate, will be directly downloaded using the device’s built-in mobile internet networking system. The number of patients who received additional rescue analgesics will be assessed systematically within 48 hours after surgery. Postoperative quality of recovery, quality of sleep, functional activity and overall satisfaction with PCA will also be recorded. Safety outcomes will include the incidence and severity of opioid-related adverse effects, primarily sedation, nausea and vomiting. During PCA use, patients will be routinely monitored in the ward, with regular assessment of vital signs, including oxygen saturation and respiratory status, in accordance with standard clinical practice.11 In addition, to assess the adequacy of blinding, patients and outcome assessors will be asked to guess the allocated intervention group at the completion of the study period.

Data management and quality control

Study data will be collected by trained investigators using protocol-specific standardised Case Report Forms. To uphold high standards of data integrity and accuracy, all study information will be documented in a centralised electronic data management system (EpiData Manager, Version 4.6.0.6; EpiData Association, Denmark). A rigorous, independent double-data entry procedure will be executed for data validation. Any discrepancies identified between the two sets of entries will be verified and resolved through cross-referencing with the original source documents. Automated range checks and validation rules will be implemented within the electronic database to identify implausible or inconsistent values, including impossible PCA infusion rates, opioid doses, lockout settings and cumulative opioid consumption values. PCA-related data directly imported from network-integrated PCA devices will be cross-checked against source records before database locking.

An independent Data and Safety Monitoring Committee will provide oversight of trial conduct and participant safety. The committee will operate according to a predefined charter outlining its roles, responsibilities, meeting schedule and decision-making procedures. The committee will convene biannually to review cumulative safety data, protocol adherence, data quality and overall trial conduct. One formal interim analysis is planned after approximately 50% of participants have completed the 48-hour follow-up assessment. The interim analysis will be conducted for safety and feasibility assessment only, without formal efficacy stopping rules. Therefore, no alpha-spending adjustment is planned, and the final analysis will use a two-sided significance level of 0.05. Recommendations regarding trial continuation, protocol modification, temporary suspension or early termination for safety concerns will be made by the Data and Safety Monitoring Committee based on predefined considerations of participant safety, data quality and overall trial conduct.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not directly engaged in the conceptualisation of the research question, the design of the study protocol or the selection of clinical outcome measures. Participant recruitment will be undertaken via physician referrals at the participating multicentre clinical sites.

On completion of the study, the findings will be disseminated to the global scientific community through oral or poster presentations at international academic conferences and publication in peer-reviewed journals. Additionally, a plain-language summary of the trial results will be provided to study participants on request following the completion of the final data analysis.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

The primary endpoint is the total cumulative opioid consumption at 48 hours postoperatively, including sufentanil delivered via PCA and opioid-equivalent rescue analgesics. Based on published equianalgesic opioid conversion ratios,12–14 1 mg oral oxycodone is equivalent to 1.5 mg oral morphine, 3 mg oral morphine is equivalent to 1 mg intravenous morphine and 1 mg intravenous morphine is approximately equivalent to 1 µg sufentanil. Therefore, each rescue tablet containing 5 mg oxycodone hydrochloride will be estimated as roughly 2.5 µg sufentanil equivalent.

Sample size

Sample size calculations were performed using PASS 11.0 software (NCSS, LLC, USA). The sample size calculation was based on both the primary endpoint and key secondary analgesic endpoint.

For the primary endpoint, total cumulative opioid consumption at 48 hours postoperatively, previous randomised controlled trials provided the basis for our assumptions. One study comparing variable-rate feedback infusion with fixed-rate basal infusion modes of intravenous PCA following spinal fusion surgery reported mean fentanyl consumption delivered at 48 hours after surgery was 7.26±5.56 µg/kg and 11.9±6.82 µg/kg, respectively.9 Accordingly, the mean weight was 60.1±8.6 kg and 63.2±12.6 kg, respectively, and the cumulative fentanyl consumption can be calculated as 436.3±334.2 µg and 752.1±431 µg, respectively. Another study suggested the mean sufentanil consumption in group variable-rate feedback infusion mode with 169.1±35.9 µg was lower than in group fixed-rate basal infusion mode with 181.8±34.8 µg.8 According to the conversion, 1 µg sufentanil is approximately equivalent to 10 µg fentanyl in terms of equivalent dosage. Therefore, we hypothesised that the total cumulative opioid consumption at 48 hours after surgery in the fixed-rate basal infusion mode group was 80.00±25.00 µg and 75.00±25.00 µg in the variable-rate feedback infusion mode group. Notably, a 5 µg reduction in sufentanil consumption approximately corresponds to a 5 mg reduction in morphine consumption, which is comparable to the minimal important difference suggested in a recent anchor-based postoperative opioid consumption study.18 This corresponds to a standardised effect size (Cohen’s d) of approximately 0.2, representing a relatively small effect size. As no universally accepted minimal clinically important difference for postoperative opioid consumption has been established, the clinical relevance of any observed opioid reduction will be interpreted together with analgesic efficacy, rescue analgesic use and opioid-related adverse effects. As these assumptions were derived from previous studies involving different surgical populations and opioid regimens, and considering the anticipated heterogeneity in opioid requirements across different surgical categories in this multicentre mixed-surgery population, a more conservative SD of 25 µg was adopted for the sample size calculation. In addition, stratified randomisation and adjustment for study centre are expected to improve statistical precision and partially account for centre-level variability. To achieve 90% power with a two-sided α-value of 0.05, a sample size of 526 per group is required. Given the short postoperative follow-up period of 48 hours, we anticipate minimal loss to follow-up. Assuming a conservative dropout rate of 10%, 585 patients will be recruited in each group.

For the key secondary analgesic endpoint, movement-evoked NRS pain score at 24 hours postoperatively, a non-inferiority margin of 1 point on the NRS was considered clinically acceptable.19 Assuming a true between-group difference of 0, a common SD of 2.0, a one-sided α level of 0.025 and 90% power, 86 patients per group would be required to assess non-inferiority. Since the sample size required for the primary endpoint was larger, the final planned sample size was determined by the primary endpoint. Therefore, a total of 1170 patients, with 585 patients in each group, will be enrolled.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis will be conducted by IBM SPSS Statistics (V. 27.0) and R software (V. 4.4.1) where appropriate. The primary endpoint, total cumulative opioid consumption at 48 hours postoperatively, will be analysed primarily according to the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle. The ITT population will include all randomised participants analysed according to their assigned groups regardless of protocol deviations or treatment adherence. The PP population will include participants without major protocol violations who receive the assigned PCA mode for at least 24 hours postoperatively. The ITT analysis will be considered primary, and the PP analysis supportive.

Descriptive statistics will be presented as means with SD for normally distributed data, medians with IQRs for skewed continuous data, and counts (percentages) for categorical data. Unadjusted comparisons between groups for normally distributed data will be performed using Student’s t-test, while the Mann-Whitney U test will be used for skewed data. Categorical data will be assessed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test.

The primary outcome, total cumulative opioid consumption at 48 hours postoperatively, will be analysed using an analysis of covariance or adjusted linear regression model, with treatment group as the main factor. Predefined covariates will include study centre, type of surgery, approach of surgery, age, gender and ASA physical status. The treatment effect will be reported as an adjusted mean difference with 95% CIs. If the distribution of the primary outcome is markedly skewed, logarithmic transformation or robust regression will be considered. An unadjusted between-group comparison using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate, will be performed as a sensitivity analysis. Anaesthesia maintenance variables, including the choice of intravenous and inhalational agents, may additionally be adjusted for in sensitivity analyses if clinically relevant imbalances between groups are identified. Predefined subgroup analyses will be conducted according to age, gender, ASA physical status, study centre, surgery type and surgical approach. Treatment effect modification will be explored by including interaction terms (eg, treatment×subgroup variable) in the regression models. These subgroup analyses will be considered exploratory, as the study is not specifically powered for definitive interaction testing.

Repeated continuous secondary outcomes, including pain scores, PCA volume consumption, sedation scores and other longitudinal measurements, will be analysed using linear mixed-effects models with fixed effects for treatment group, time and treatment-by-time interaction, and random intercepts for participants. Study centre may additionally be included as a random effect where appropriate. These models allow inclusion of incomplete repeated measurements under the missing-at-random assumption without requiring last observation carried forward imputation. Ordinal secondary outcomes, including patient satisfaction and nausea/vomiting scores, will be analysed using proportional odds models or other appropriate regression approaches. Sensitivity analyses using alternative modelling strategies may also be performed where appropriate. Secondary outcome analyses will primarily be considered exploratory and interpreted cautiously with emphasis on effect sizes and 95% CIs rather than solely on p-values. The intervention will be considered clinically favourable if the variable-rate feedback infusion mode demonstrates both superiority in reducing total cumulative opioid consumption and non-inferiority in postoperative analgesia compared with the fixed-rate basal infusion mode.

Missing data for the primary outcome will initially be assessed using complete-case analysis. If the extent of missing data is substantial, sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation under the missing-at-random assumption will be performed. The imputation model will include treatment group, baseline characteristics and available postoperative outcome measurements. Worst-case scenario analyses may additionally be conducted as robustness analyses.

Discussion

The proposed multicentre randomised controlled trial aims to evaluate whether a variable-rate feedback infusion mode in intravenous PCA can reduce total cumulative opioid consumption while providing non-inferior analgesia compared with the conventional fixed-rate basal infusion mode. Given the high prevalence of postoperative pain and the ongoing global effort to implement opioid-sparing strategies, optimising PCA delivery systems is a critical clinical priority.

Our study focuses on total cumulative opioid consumption at 48 hours as the primary endpoint. Reducing total opioid intake is intended not merely as dose reduction but as a strategy that may minimise opioid-related adverse effects such as respiratory depression, postoperative nausea and vomiting, and excessive sedation. By using the feedback mechanism, we anticipate that patients in the variable-rate group will achieve effective analgesia with a lower total dose, thereby improving safety profiles and potentially accelerating functional recovery—a key goal of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery protocols.

Unlike previous single-centre studies that focused on specific surgical procedures, this trial includes a heterogeneous population undergoing mixed surgeries and is conducted across multiple centres. This design enhances the external validity and generalisability of the results and allows evaluation of whether the advantages of variable-rate feedback infusion are consistent across different surgical types and clinical settings. In addition, the use of network-integrated PCA pumps enables precise and objective collection of analgesic usage data, strengthening the reliability of outcome measurements.

Nevertheless, several potential limitations should be considered. The variability in surgical trauma, postoperative pain intensity, and anaesthetic management among different types of surgery may introduce heterogeneity that could influence analgesic requirements and opioid consumption. Although stratified randomisation is employed to mitigate this effect, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. In addition, the inclusion of a mixed surgical population may limit the interpretability of procedure-specific effects. Moreover, while total cumulative opioid consumption is an objective and clinically meaningful endpoint, patient-reported outcomes such as pain intensity and satisfaction are inherently subjective and may be influenced by psychological and cultural factors. The exclusion of patients with chronic pain conditions or significant psychiatric disorders may further limit generalisability to more complex postoperative populations commonly encountered in routine clinical practice. The restriction of enrolment to patients aged 18–65 years may limit generalisability to older populations, who have higher risks of opioid-related adverse effects and different pain responses. In addition, as this is a multicentre study conducted exclusively in China, differences in perioperative practice patterns, healthcare systems and cultural perceptions of pain may affect the applicability of the findings to other regions and populations. Finally, secondary outcomes are categorised as key and exploratory outcomes. Given the multiplicity of secondary outcomes, the analyses will be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution.

In summary, this study is expected to provide multicentre evidence regarding whether a variable-rate feedback infusion mode can reduce postoperative opioid consumption while providing non-inferior analgesia. The findings may also help inform future investigations of patient-centred recovery outcomes associated with different PCA infusion strategies. The present trial also represents one component of a broader multicentre research programme evaluating multiple optimisation strategies for postoperative PCA management.
