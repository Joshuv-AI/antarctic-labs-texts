Seroprevalence and Associated Risk Factors of Foot‐and‐Mouth Disease in Livestock in Ethiopia: Findings From a National Surveillance (2016–2023)
Authors: Daniel Gizaw, Hagos Asgdom, Ayelech Muluneh, Ayenalem Fentie, Demessa Negessu, Genet Bogale, Takele Worku, Ashebir Abebe, Getnet Abie Mekonnen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundFoot‐and‐Mouth Disease (FMD) is a highly contagious viral disease that poses a severe threat to global livestock production and food security. In Ethiopia, the disease is endemic, with multiple circulating serotypes (O, A, SAT1, and SAT2) significantly impacting the agricultural economy.ObjectiveThis study aimed to determine the seroprevalence of FMD across livestock populations in Ethiopia and identify associated risk factors to inform effective, evidence‐based control strategies.MethodsA cross‐sectional surveillance study was conducted between 2016 and 2023. A total of 19,699 serum samples were collected from cattle, sheep, and goats across all regional states using a systematic sampling design. Antibodies against the FMD virus 3ABC protein were detected using Non‐Structural Protein (NSP) ELISA. Data were analysed using descriptive statistics, univariable analysis, and multivariable logistic regression.ResultsThe overall FMD seroprevalence was 22% (4,341/19,699). Prevalence varied significantly by region, with the highest in Gambella (56.8%), followed by SNNPR (25.6%) and Oromia (23.7%), and the lowest in Amhara (14.6%). By species, cattle (23.9%) and sheep (22.3%) exhibited higher infection rates compared to goats (5.6%). Regional location (specifically Gambella) was identified as a significant risk factor (OR: 1.31; 95% CI: 0.36–0.49).ConclusionsThese findings confirm the endemic nature of FMD across all Ethiopian regional states and production systems. Cattle serve as the primary maintenance species, with age, species, and regional movement patterns acting as key drivers of viral circulation.RecommendationsThere is an urgent need for risk‐based surveillance and targeted vaccination strategies, particularly along major livestock trade routes and in districts with intensive inter‐herd mixing. Implementing these spatially and epidemiologically informed control measures is essential to reducing the burden of FMD, protecting pastoralist livelihoods, and enhancing national food security and livestock export potential.

Introduction

Ethiopia is thought to have the largest livestock population in Africa, with in‐country estimates of more than 60 million cattle, 31 million sheep and 32 million goats (CSA 2016). Livestock contributes 37–87% of household incomes, 35–49% of the agricultural Gross Domestic Product (GDP) and 15–17% of the national GDP (Endalew and Ayalew 2016). In addition to its economic contribution, livestock farming provides multiple benefits, including the consumption of meat and milk, transportation, draught power for cultivation, manure for soil fertilisation, social status, as well as earnings from the export of live animals, meat, skins, and hides (Endalew and Ayalew 2016). The FMD virus consists of seven serologically distinct serotypes: O, A, C, SAT1, SAT2, SAT3 and Asia 1. To date, only serotypes O, A, SAT1 and SAT2 have been reported in Ethiopia, whereas Asia 1 and SAT3 have not been detected. FMD virus has a high mutation rate because the viral RNA‐dependent RNA polymerase is incapable of proofreading (Knowles 2020). Ethiopia, along with several East African countries, belongs to regional pool 4 of FMD viruses (Gizaw et al. 2020; Sulayeman et al. 2018; Di Nardo et al. 2011).

All cloven‐hoofed animals are susceptible to FMD infection. The severity of clinical effects varies depending on the species, breed of animal, viral strain, and epidemiological circumstances (Knowles et al. 2005). FMD is endemic to Ethiopia and distributed widely across the country. The prevalence of FMD varies across different farming systems and agro‐ecological areas. The disease is particularly prevalent in pastoral areas. Extensive livestock movement and high rates of animal contact at markets, grazing areas, and watering points contribute to the spread of FMD. Several risk factors were identified for the spread of FMD in Ethiopia, which include, production system, geographic location, age of animals, contact with wildlife and season of the year (Megersa et al. 2009; Jemberu et al. 2016). Jemberu et al. (2016) showed the association of herd size, difference in geographical location (district) and age of the animals with seropositivity of FMD. There was also a wide difference in animals that had contact with wildlife compared to herds that rarely had contact with wildlife (Molla et al. 2010). Sulayman et al. (2018) indicate cattle are affected more than small ruminants. Variation was also indicated between different age groups, where younger animals were affected more than older animals (Ayelet et al. 2012; Rufael et al. 2008; Gelaye et al. 2009).

Factors such as extensive livestock movement, both legal and illegal trade, weak border control, insufficient animal traceability systems and high rates of animal contact at commercial markets, communal grazing areas, and watering points contribute to the spread of FMD in Ethiopia and are undoubtedly the main risk factors of FMD spread (Zewdie et al. 2023; Abdela 2017). In Ethiopia, FMD outbreaks have been reported in all regions of the country. The most dominant serotype is O, followed by SAT2. Serotype C has not been reported since 1983 (Gizaw et al. 2020; Gelaye et al. 2009; Legesse et al. 2013).

Recognising the seroprevalence of FMD and implementing an effective control plan can unlock numerous benefits for producers and the private sector. Access to lucrative markets, increased livestock product exports, higher domestic milk sales, improved livestock health and productivity, and strengthened biosecurity measures are among the advantages that can contribute to the economic growth and sustainability of the livestock industry.

The objective of this surveillance was to generate the essential epidemiological data concerning FMD seroprevalence, regional distribution, and associated risk factors across all production systems in Ethiopia. This information is critical for characterising the country's status as a trade‐sensitive FMD endemic area and forms the scientific basis for developing and prioritising strategic control interventions needed to enhance livestock health, secure access to higher‐value export markets, and contribute toward national economic development objectives such as lemat turfat’.

Materials and Methods

Surveillance Design, Data Sources and Sampling Strategy

This study is a retrospective analysis of secondary surveillance data obtained from the official national FMD records of the Animal Health Institute (AHI). These data were collected by regional veterinary services during routine diagnostic and surveillance activities as part of the national Surveillance of Trade‐Sensitive Diseases (STSD) project between 2016 and 2023. This project systematically monitored high‐impact pathogens, including FMD, Contagious Bovine Pleuropneumonia (CBPP), Rift Valley Fever (RVF), and Peste des Petits Ruminants (PPR), using a cross‐sectional study design across multiple regions and production systems.

The surveillance utilised data from passive and active surveillance efforts conducted in Ethiopia to investigate the seroprevalence and serotype distribution of FMD in susceptible livestock populations (cattle, sheep, and goats). These data were compiled from multiple cross‐sectional sampling events, providing a retrospective analysis of FMD dynamics over the study period.

Beginning in 2016, the initial sampling framework was established by randomly selecting 300 kebeles (the smallest administrative unit in Ethiopia) from all regional states. This random selection ensured initial broad geographical distribution, intentionally incorporating diverse livestock populations from both the mixed agriculture highlands and the lowland pastoralist areas. Following this baseline setup, the subsequent collection of cumulative surveillance data over multiple years integrated both active focal investigations and passive veterinary reporting structures. Within the selected administrative framework, sampling intensity varied according to clinical suspicion, livestock density, and active outbreak alerts; consequently, kebeles and districts experiencing higher reporting volumes contributed more cumulative records.

Herds within the targeted areas were selected through a combination of active tracing and convenience sampling during outbreak investigations, veterinary clinic visits, and monitoring at communal watering or grazing points. Within each selected herd, individual animals aged six months or older were systematically or conveniently sampled to ensure they had sufficient time to develop antibodies after potential exposure to the FMDV. Livestock species included in the study comprised bovines (cattle), sheep, and goats. Because this study involved the analysis of existing surveillance records and did not involve direct animal handling or primary blood collection by the authors, it was exempt from institutional animal‐use ethical review. Authorisation to access and utilise these surveillance data for research purposes was granted by the Animal Health Institute.

Vaccination Status

Information regarding the individual vaccination status of sampled animals was not systematically collected, as routine FMD vaccination in the sampled regions is currently neither mandatory nor widely implemented, except in specific intensive dairy production systems. Consequently, our seroprevalence findings are based on the detection of antibodies against the Non‐Structural Proteins (NSP) of the FMDV. Since NSP antibodies are typically elicited by natural infection rather than by standard inactivated vaccines, these results predominantly reflect past field exposure to the virus, allowing for a clearer differentiation between natural infection and potential vaccine‐induced immunity in our study population.

Sample Collection and Laboratory Analysis

Approximately 7 mL of blood was collected from each animal's jugular vein using a plain vacutainer tube. The tubes were then kept slant and protected from direct sunlight until the blood clotted and the serum separated. The separated serum was transferred to sterile cryovials, stored on ice in the field, and later transferred to a refrigerator at −20°C at the Animal Health Institute. Finally, the samples were tested using the 3ABC NSP ELISA ID VET kit to detect FMD antibodies, following the manufacturer's instructions.

Risk Factors Assessment

In conjunction with serological testing, a comprehensive range of potential risk factors for FMD seropositivity were documented during the study period. These factors covered host characteristics, including the animal's age, sex, and breed, and management factors related to the specific production systems (e.g., pastoralist vs. mixed crop‐livestock farming). The geographical location of the animals, encompassing their region and administrative zone, was also meticulously recorded. These documented variables were subsequently subjected to statistical analysis (as described in the Data Analysis section) to quantitatively identify their association with FMD seropositivity within the studied livestock populations

Serological Test

A non‐structural protein Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (NSP FMD 3ABC ELISA) was used to detect antibodies against non‐structural proteins (NSPs) of the FMD virus. Unlike the structural proteins of the virus, which are involved in the formation of viral particles, the NSPs are produced during active viral replication and are not present in the viral particle itself. Therefore, detecting antibodies against NSPs can indicate exposure to the FMD virus, even in the absence of viral particles. The NSP FMD 3ABC ELISA test is commonly used for surveillance, monitoring and control of FMD. It helps in identifying infected animals in the early stages of the disease, as well as in identifying carriers or animals that have previously been exposed to FMD. Antigen‐coated plate preparation, dilution of serum, incubation, washing and addition of conjugate and the substrate were all followed according to the manufacturer's protocols (Idvet Innovative France).

Data Analysis

In conjunction with serological testing, a comprehensive range of potential risk factors for FMD seropositivity were documented during the study period, including host characteristics (age, sex, and species), production systems, and geographical location (region and administrative zone). The collected data were subjected to statistical analysis using R software (version 4.5.2). To identify associations between these characteristics and FMD seropositivity, a multivariable logistic regression model was employed. The model included Age as a continuous variable, while Sex, Region, Zone and Species were included as categorical variables. All study variables were included in the multivariable model to adjust for potential confounding, and multicollinearity was assessed to ensure model stability. Odds ratios (OR) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated by exponentiating the model coefficients. The reference categories for the categorical predictors were defined as ‘0’ for Sex, ‘Afar’ for Region, ‘Afder’ for Zone, and ‘Caprine’ for Species. To ensure robust and actionable epidemiological findings, data analysis and risk factor reporting were conducted at the district level. Statistical significance was set at a p‐value of < 0.05.

Results

A total of 19,699 serum samples collected from susceptible livestock across all regions of Ethiopia between 2016 and 2023 were subjected to serological testing for FMD antibodies. The overall seroprevalence across the entire surveillance period was determined to be 22.0% (4341/19,699), with a 95% Confidence Interval (CI) of 21.5–22.6%.

The FMD seroprevalence demonstrated significant regional variation (Table 1). The highest recorded seroprevalence was documented in Gambella Regional State at 56.8% (134/236; 95% CI: 50.5%–63.1%), which was markedly higher than the national average. Following this, the highest rates were found in the South Nation Nationalities and Peoples' Region (SNNPR) at 25.6% (898/3504; 95% CI: 24.2–27.17%) and the Oromia Regional State at 23.7% (2287/9662; 95% CI: 22.8%–24.5%). In contrast, the lowest prevalence was documented in the Amhara Regional State at 14.6% (426/2913; 95% CI: 13.3–15.9%).

Animal species was a highly significant factor (P < 0.001), with Bovine (23.9%) and Ovine (22.3%) populations showing substantially higher risk compared to Caprine (5.4%) species. Furthermore, age and sex were significant predictors of exposure: adult animals exhibited higher seropositivity (23.4%) compared to young animals (16.7%), and female livestock demonstrated higher seroprevalence (23.5%) than their male counterparts (19.1%). These findings collectively highlight the multifactorial nature of FMDV transmission, confirming that host demographics and spatial distribution are central to the disease's persistence in Ethiopia.

Discussion

The overall FMD seroprevalence of 22.0% (4341/19,699) documented across Ethiopia during the 2016–2023 surveillance period confirms the endemic nature and wide circulation of the FMD virus throughout the national livestock population. This high prevalence rate indicates that a substantial proportion of the country's livestock has been exposed to circulating FMD virus serotypes (O, A, SAT1 and SAT2). Such sustained high levels of seropositivity underscore the significant threat FMD poses to livestock health and productivity, validating its classification as a major trade‐sensitive disease in the region.

Information regarding the individual vaccination status of sampled animals was not systematically collected, as routine FMD vaccination in the sampled regions is currently neither mandatory nor widely implemented, except in specific intensive dairy production systems. Consequently, our seroprevalence findings are based on the detection of antibodies against the Non‐Structural Proteins (NSP) of the FMDV. Since NSP antibodies are typically elicited by natural infection rather than by standard inactivated vaccines, these results predominantly reflect past field exposure to the virus, allowing for a clearer differentiation between natural infection and potential vaccine‐induced immunity in our study population.

In the present study, the FMD seroprevalence in cattle was estimated at 23.9% (4,103/17,150). This high rate confirms that cattle serve as the primary maintenance and indicator species for FMDV in Ethiopia. The significant proportion of exposed cattle is consistent with their central role in traditional mixed crop‐livestock and pastoralist systems, which inherently involve high levels of animal movement and frequent co‐mingling at markets and communal grazing areas, thereby facilitating widespread virus transmission. Furthermore, our multivariable analysis identified Age, Sex, Species and Region as significant risk factors for seropositivity. These findings align with previous epidemiological studies, which have consistently highlighted the multifactorial nature of FMDV transmission in the region. The observed associations likely reflect cumulative exposure, where older animals have a greater probability of encountering the virus and variations in management practices, such as the movement of draft cattle versus the more localised grazing of other species. Ultimately, these results underscore that FMDV dynamics in Ethiopia are driven by a combination of host susceptibility and ecological factors related to livestock husbandry and mobility.

Seroprevalence in sheep was also high at 22.3% (132/591). This finding is comparable to the rate in cattle and suggests that sheep populations are subject to a similar high level of exposure. While sheep often show milder clinical signs compared to cattle (acting as “silent carriers”), this serological evidence highlights their important role in the epidemiology and persistence of the virus within mixed herds.

In contrast, the seroprevalence in goats was significantly lower, at 5.4% (106/1958). This suggests that a considerably smaller fraction of the goat population was exposed to FMD. The lower rate could be attributed to factors such as a genuinely lower biological susceptibility to infection, differences in herding and management practices (e.g., less frequent movement to large communal markets), or differences in the duration or magnitude of viral shedding compared to cattle.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis revealed that Age, Species, Sex, Region, and Zone were statistically significant risk factors for FMDV seropositivity (p < 0.05). The observed regional (χ2 = 352.85, P < 0.05) and zonal (χ2 = 350.51, df = 2, p‐value < 2.2e‐16). Variations highlight the geographic heterogeneity of viral circulation, likely reflecting differences in livestock density, trade routes, and the intensity of animal movement across administrative boundaries.

Regarding host‐specific factors, age was positively associated with seropositivity. This is consistent with the concept of cumulative exposure; as animals remain in the herd longer, they encounter more opportunities for infection through communal grazing, market interactions, and contact with transient herds, thereby increasing the likelihood of seroconversion over time. Furthermore, the variation observed between species—with higher seroprevalence in cattle—can be explained by distinct management practices. In the study area, cattle are frequently moved for agricultural purposes such as ploughing and threshing, which facilitates extensive intermingling. In contrast, small ruminants are often kept under more localised grazing management, limiting their exposure to the virus. These findings suggest that FMDV transmission in Ethiopia is driven by a complex interplay between the natural susceptibility of different species and the human‐mediated movement of livestock, which dictates the frequency and scale of viral transmission.

The findings of this surveillance study have several implications. Understanding the seroprevalence of FMD in different regions of Ethiopia provides valuable information for disease control and prevention measures. It helps to identify areas with high FMD prevalence, enabling targeted vaccination campaigns and biosecurity interventions. Additionally, analysing risk factors such as age, sex, breed, and production systems can aid in developing tailored strategies to minimise FMD transmission and its impact on livestock populations. Tadesse et al. (2019) reported seroprevalence rates of 6.38%, 3.67%, and 2.17% in 389 cattle, sheep and goats, respectively. These findings are considerably lower than those observed in the present surveillance study. A seroprevalence study in the country reported the prevalence ranges from 5.6% to 42.7% in cattle (Sulayeman et al. 2018; Megersa et al. 2009; Tesfaye et al. 2016; Beyene et al. 2015; Belina et al. 2016; Melkamsew 2018). The prevalence in small ruminants ranges from 4% to 11% (Beyene et al. 2015). The seroprevalence report of FMD in Ethiopia ranges from 4% to 11% in small ruminants and 30% in ungulate wildlife (Melkamsew 2018; Sahle et al. 2007).

The observed peaks in seroprevalence during 2019 and 2022 likely reflect an interplay between intensified surveillance, as the Animal Health Institute (AHI), prioritised diagnostic support during active outbreaks, and management‐related drivers. These fluctuations may also be attributed to increased livestock movement and trade during holiday markets, coupled with climatic impacts on pastoralist migration patterns, which enhanced viral transmission risks. Additionally, temporal shifts in the timing and regional coverage of vaccination programmes likely contributed to these variations.

The seroprevalence of the disease varies spatially, and previous studies have not covered all regions of the country. Prior research indicates that FMD poses a major threat across many parts of Ethiopia, causing considerable economic losses through morbidity, mortality, and trade restrictions (Abdela 2017).

The observed regional variations and identified risk factors in this study underscore the complex epidemiology of FMDV in Ethiopia. FMDV infection showed significant associations with production systems, species, age and sex; notably, pastoral areas such as Afar and Gambella were found to be at higher risk compared to other regions. The substantially higher seroprevalence observed in Gambella, in particular, likely stems from complex drivers including frequent cross‐border livestock movement and trade, which facilitate viral introduction, alongside high‐intensity wildlife‐livestock interactions at shared grazing and water points. Furthermore, regional differences in livestock density and the consistency of vaccination coverage create varying levels of herd susceptibility, directly impacting the endemic circulation and maintenance of the virus across different administrative zones of Ethiopia. Consistent with these findings, our study revealed that cattle are the most affected species compared to caprine and ovine, while susceptibility is significantly higher in adult animals and females, observations that align with previously reported patterns.

Finally, this study is subject to temporal fluctuations in surveillance intensity, evidenced by the substantial variation in annual sample sizes (ranging from 962 in 2023 to 10,538 in 2016). This variability likely reflects inconsistencies in resource allocation, regional reporting challenges, and shifts in surveillance priorities over the study period. Such imbalance may impact the statistical precision of our estimates; consequently, observed annual trends should be interpreted with caution regarding these variations in sampling effort. While the use of multivariate analysis accounts for potential confounding, the reduced sample sizes in certain years may limit the robustness of our findings for those specific intervals. Future research should prioritise consistent sampling protocols or the application of post‐stratification weighting to mitigate the impact of such sampling imbalances.

Conclusion

This study confirms that FMD is endemic throughout Ethiopia, with an overall seroprevalence of 22% among the livestock population surveyed. Our findings indicate a significant geographical variation in seroprevalence, with the highest rates observed in the Gambella, SNNPR and Oromia regions. Furthermore, the study identified key differences in seroprevalence among species, with cattle showing a notably higher rate than small ruminants. These results highlight the widespread nature of the disease and the need for a targeted approach to control. Based on our findings, we recommend the following measures to enhance FMD control in Ethiopia: Increase investment in veterinary services and diagnostic capabilities to improve the timeliness and accuracy of FMD detection.
