Radiomics and radiogenomics in ovarian cancer: a review with a focus on ultrasound applications
Authors: Yiyang Huang, Wangrui Peng, Huai Yang, Zichao Liu, Tingyu Zhang, Linyuan Jin, Wen He, Meng Du, Zhiyi Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ovarian cancer (OC) is a leading cause of gynecologic cancer mortality, poses significant diagnostic challenges due to its inter- and intra-tumoral heterogeneity. Conventional qualitative imaging often fails to capture such complexity, whereas radiomics is specifically designed to address it. By integrating imaging features with clinical, genomic, or proteomic data, these approaches reveal sub-visual heterogeneity and enhance diagnostic accuracy. Ultrasound-based radiomics and radiogenomics further extend its clinical utility. Virtual biopsy techniques, such as radiomic habitat maps fused with ultrasound, enable real-time, multi-site sampling to predict prognosis, peritoneal metastasis, and recurrence. This review synthesizes recent advancements in ultrasound-based radiomics and radiogenomics for OC, focusing on their clinical utility in improving diagnostic accuracy, prognostic stratification, and personalized therapeutic strategies. Despite progress, challenges such as insufficient standardization and limited model interpretability persist. Future efforts should prioritize AI-augmented analytical pipelines, multicenter prospective validation, and biomarker discovery to bridge imaging phenotypes with precision oncology framework in OC management.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) is the sixth most common female malignancy, demonstrating significant geographical variations in survival outcomes due to disparities in early detection and treatment access [1, 2]. Traditional imaging remains the primary approach for ovarian tumor detection [3]; however, its effectiveness heavily depends on the physician’s experience [4], and certain subtle tumor-specific imaging features are difficult to discern with the naked eye, limiting diagnostic accuracy.

Compounding these limitations, increasing evidence highlights the considerable spatial and temporal heterogeneity of ovarian tumors. Recent advances in single-cell sequencing have revealed substantial genetic and epigenetic differences across tumor regions, including gene mutations, somatic copy number variations, chromatin states, and DNA methylation [5]. Such heterogeneity can lead to inconsistent therapeutic responses and complicates treatment planning. A single biopsy may also fail to capture the full molecular landscape of the tumor, posing challenges to precise diagnosis and personalized therapy. In response to these challenges, radiomics and radiogenomics have emerged as promising technologies. Powered by artificial intelligence, these methods can automatically extract high-dimensional features from medical images and integrate them with genomic data to characterize tumor heterogeneity. This approach reduces interobserver variability, improves reproducibility, and facilitates more informed clinical decision-making by enabling comprehensive, noninvasive tumor profiling [6, 7].

In this study, we systematically reviewed the conceptual frameworks of radiomics and radiogenomics, critically analyzed their technical advancements in OC management. Furthermore, we delineated unresolved challenges in clinical translation, proposing standardized pipelines for future validation.

Radiomics and radiogenomics

Radiomics and radiogenomics are rapidly growing interdisciplinary field that integrates medical imaging, computer science, bioinformatics, and molecular biology. Radiomics is widely used for disease detection, diagnosis, prognosis assessment, and treatment response prediction [8], especially in revealing tumor heterogeneity [9]. Radiogenomics focuses on combining imaging features with molecular information to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of tumors [10]. Although radiomics and radiogenomics have their own research focuses, they are highly compatible in terms of technological processes and application concepts, both relying on AI techniques to process high-dimensional image data [11] and sharing image feature extraction processes [12]. Indeed, radiomics provides rich information on imaging phenotypes, while radiogenomics reveals their underlying biological significance through multi-omics data [13]. The synergistic development of the two jointly promotes the goal of individualized diagnosis and treatment by non-invasive means (Fig. 1).

Radiomics: from image characterization to precision medicine

The term “radiomics” was initially introduced by Lambin in 2012, referring to the process of extracting a vast array of quantitative features from medical images, particularly within defined regions of interest [7]. Gillies et al. further state that radiomics is the process of transforming routine digital images into analyzable high-throughput data designed to enhance clinical decision-making [14]. The core concept is that medical images contain implicit information about histopathology that can be systematically extracted and interpreted by quantitative image analysis [15].

Radiomics can be categorized into traditional handcrafted radiomics and deep learning-based radiomics according to the feature extraction method. The former relies on manual predefinition and selection of image features, while the latter automatically learns deep features in images with the help of deep neural networks, and in recent years, deep learning-based radiomics has become a hot research topic [16]. By combining clinical, genetic, and pathological data, radiomics can construct predictive models to achieve individualized medical goals [17–19]. Additionally, radiomics analysis can be applied to tumor subregions, metastatic lesions, and normal tissues [20].

The radiomics pipeline generally includes the following steps: (i) acquire images, (ii) identify volumes of interest (i.e., those volumes likely to contain prognostic value), (iii) segment volumes (i.e., delineate volume boundaries using computer-aided contouring), (iv) extract and qualify descriptive features from the volumes, (v) populate a searchable database with these features, and (vi) mine the data to develop classifier models [21]. Random Forest (RF), LASSO, Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Support Vector Machines (SVM), and Minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance (mRMR) are commonly used methods in feature selection and machine learning modeling [22–26]. The research on radiomics has developed significantly for ten years ago and the advancement of hardware and software facilities has made quantitative imaging of tissue pathophysiology possible.

Radiogenomics: bridging the image and molecular levels

The value of radiogenomics stems from the fact that although nearly all cancer patients undergo imaging at some point, often multiple times during their treatment, not all patients have their genomic maps of disease. Furthermore, when genomic profiling is performed, it is performed at one location only once and is susceptible to sampling error. Radiogenomics allows non-invasive monitoring of the tumor as a whole or even at multiple sites, overcoming the limitations of biopsy and thus enhancing the accuracy of disease diagnosis, treatment prediction, and efficacy assessment [14, 21].

Mining radiomics data to detect correlations with genomic patterns is called radiogenomics, which primarily encompasses two aspects: (i) exploring the relationship between germline genotypic variations and clinical variability as reflected in radiation therapy response [14, 27]; and (ii) investigating the link between specific imaging features and specific gene expression patterns that determine the underlying cellular pathophysiology [28]. The concept, which is rapidly developing with the rise of precision medicine, is considered to be a product of the combination of imaging and genomics [29], with the goal of establishing a multidimensional link between medical imaging data and genomic data [30]. This field has grown tremendously over the past few years, showing great potential for developing non-invasive prognostic and diagnostic methods for identifying therapeutic biomarkers in cancer research by combining quantitative imaging features of tumor phenotypes with genomic features [31].

In recent years, radiogenomics has shown another trend, which is to combine radiomics features with proteomics features and biological parameters other than genomics, such as metabolomics, to form a broader connotation [32] (Table 1). With the continuous improvement of molecular characterization of various types of cancers, as well as the advancement of texture analysis and machine learning technologies, radiogenomics is expected to drive cancer diagnosis towards the field of personalized medicine [33].

Applications of radiomics and radiogenomics in ovarian cancer

With the continuous advancement of imaging technology, radiomics and radiogenomics have gradually become indispensable tools in the early diagnosis, prognostic assessment, and individualized treatment plan development of OC. Imaging genomics can reveal the internal heterogeneity of tumors by extracting high-dimensional features from images, while imaging genomics further improves the accuracy and sensitivity of imaging diagnosis by combining image features with molecular genetic data. Next, we will discuss in detail the specific applications of imaging genomics and imaging genomics in OC.

Applications of radiomics in ovarian cancer

Studies demonstrate that early detection of OC metastasis significantly improves patient prognosis, treatment efficacy, and survival [34]. CT, a routine imaging modality, offers high spatial resolution and rapid acquisition, enabling the extraction of high-dimensional features (e.g., tumor heterogeneity, texture, shape) to predict lymph node/peritoneal metastasis and survival outcomes [35, 36]. In contrast, MRI provides richer biological data through multiparametric sequences (e.g., T2WI, DWI, DCE-MRI), enhancing soft-tissue characterization, whereas PET/CT detects distant metastases (e.g., lymph node/bone) via FDG uptake but has limited sensitivity for small lesions.

Several studies highlight the predictive power of radiomics. Song et al. integrated MRI-based radiomics features (T2WI, fat-suppressed T2WI, DWI) with pelvic effusion and CA125 levels, achieving AUCs of 0.963 (training) and 0.928 (validation) [37]. Similarly, Yu et al. combined multiparametric MRI (FS-T2WI, DWI, DCE-MRI) with clinical data to predict peritoneal carcinomatosis (AUC: 0.902) [38]. CT-based models have also shown good performance (AUC: 0.86), but direct cross-modality comparisons are inappropriate due to differences in acquisition and feature extraction. Overall, these studies demonstrate that combining radiomics features with clinical indicators improves predictive performance [39].

Beyond clinical indicators, radiomics has also been combined with biomarker information. For example, Wan et al. focused on CCR5 [40]. Although it did not have a significant effect on overall survival, its expression level was positively correlated with lymphocytic infiltration. A radiomics model constructed based on CCR5 expression has certain prognostic predictive ability, and a column-line diagram tool was established to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates by combining imaging and clinical features, showing good prospects for clinical application.

Extending this line of work to metabolic imaging, PET/CT-based radiomics has been applied to capture intratumoral and metabolic heterogeneity. In a recent study, the performance of radiomics signatures extracted separately from PET and CT components of PET/CT images by Wang’s group and combined with clinical features to predict PFS in patients with advanced HGSOC (progression- free survival). The predictive performance of the model integrating clinical features and PET image RS was better than simply using clinical features. Compared with clinical parameters alone or in combination with CT- radiomics signatures, PET- radiomics signatures can improve diagnostic accuracy and provide complementary prognostic information [41].

However, although CT can rapidly acquire images and extract morphological and textural features, its sensitivity is limited for lesions smaller than 5 mm and for complex regions such as the lesser omental bursa and mesentery [42]. MRI combined with diffusion-weighted imaging can improve lesion detectability, but remains constrained by high cost, long acquisition time, and susceptibility to motion artifacts [43]. PET-CT has limited spatial resolution for small lesions, and variations in tracer uptake across histological subtypes, along with a high rate of false positives [44], restrict its broader application in radiomics research [45]. In contrast, ultrasound offers advantages of being radiation-free, low-cost, and real-time repeatable, demonstrating greater clinical accessibility and potential in radiomics analysis.

Applications of radiogenomics in ovarian cancer

From prognostic stratification to therapy optimization

Radiogenomics combines imaging features with genetic signatures to predict prognosis and guide clinical management in OC. It has been confirmed that approximately 50% of HGSOCs exhibit homologous recombination deficiency, often linked to “BRCA1/2” mutations, which influence platinum chemotherapy sensitivity and PARP inhibitor response [46, 47]. While radiomic features correlate with HGSOC prognosis, they poorly discriminate BRCA mutation status [48]. For example, Li et al. developed 2D/3D radiomic models (AUCs: 0.78–0.91) for mutation prediction, though performance differences among models were insignificant [49].

Radiogenomics also associates imaging features with molecular subtypes. For instance, peritoneal involvement and mesenteric infiltration on CT correlate with CLOVAR genomic subtypes, which reflect transcriptional profiles and prognosis [50, 51]. Additionally, radiogenomics aids therapy monitoring. While recurrent tumors show genomic divergence from primary lesions, homologous recombination deficiency status often remains stable [52]. Post-chemotherapy residual tumors, however, may exhibit homologous recombination deficiency shifts, highlighting their role in resistance mechanisms [53]. CT-derived radiomic features further predict time-to-progression (C-index: 0.658) and mesenchymal subtype scores (P = 0.043) [54], though these findings require validation. As neoadjuvant chemotherapy gains traction, radiogenomics offers a non-invasive tool to stratify treatment response and prognosis, enabling personalized therapeutic strategies.

Virtual biopsies

A key challenge in radiomics is tumor heterogeneity, where distinct imaging “habitats” within a single tumor reflect varying biological behaviors. In HGSOC, higher CT-derived habitat heterogeneity correlates with worse prognosis [55]. Further studies link habitat heterogeneity to phylogenetic evolution and T-cell infiltration, though sample sizes remain limited due to technical complexity [56, 57]. Radiogenomics bridges imaging features with molecular biology, providing biological validation for radiomics biomarkers and supporting its largest potential application—virtual biopsy [58].

This non-invasive paradigm integrates multimodal imaging (CT, MRI, PET) with genomic data to infer tumor molecular characteristics, minimizing reliance on invasive tissue sampling [59]. To overcome spatial alignment challenges, 3D printing technology enables precise coregistration of imaging, histopathology, and genomic datasets. For instance, Weigelt et al. utilized 3D-printed molds to guide multisampling of HGSOC, demonstrating spatial concordance between radiomic habitats and genetic subclonal architecture [56]. Building on this, Maria et al. developed an intraoperative image-guided automated sampling system that preserves surgical workflow integrity [60]. Such innovations may identify therapy-resistant subclones localized to specific tumor regions, informing preemptive strategies against resistance [61]. CT, MRI, and PET modalities have made significant progress in radiogenomics; however, their static imaging nature limits the capture of dynamic molecular changes within tumors. Ultrasound, with its high temporal resolution and operational flexibility, offers new possibilities for achieving real-time virtual biopsy and precision therapy [62].

Applications of ultrasound-based radiomics and radiogenomics in ovarian cancer

Ultrasound imaging, a cornerstone in evaluating the female reproductive system, holds significant potential for advancing OC management through radiomics and radiogenomics. Despite their nascent stage in OC research, these fields benefit from the versatility of multimodal ultrasound—including B-mode, Doppler, contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS), and elastography, which enables multidimensional assessment of structural, hemodynamic, and biomechanical properties. The integration of such modalities enriches radiomic feature extraction, providing complementary data to enhance machine/deep learning models for improved diagnostic and prognostic accuracy [63]. Furthermore, correlating ultrasound-derived features with clinical, genomic, and proteomic profiles offers critical insights into tumor biology, facilitating personalized risk stratification and therapeutic strategies.

As a noninvasive, cost-effective, and scalable tool, ultrasound-based radiogenomics has emerged as a pivotal area in precision oncology. Current efforts focus on leveraging its capabilities to refine OC diagnosis, predict treatment responses, and optimize therapeutic decision-making (Table 2). Standardization of imaging protocols and analytical workflows, coupled with technological advancements, will be essential to translate these innovations into clinical practice, ultimately positioning ultrasound radiogenomics as an integral component of precision medicine for OC.

Ultrasound-based radiomics in ovarian cancer

Radiomics and AI-enhanced ultrasound analysis have significantly expanded the diagnostic and prognostic capabilities in ovarian cancer. From accurately differentiating benign and malignant tumors, refining O-RADS risk stratification, predicting histologic subtypes, to assessing metastatic risk, these advanced techniques integrate imaging features with clinical data to provide high-performance, non-invasive tools that support personalized management and improve clinical outcomes (Fig. 2).

Applications of ultrasound-based radiomics in ovarian cancer management. The major clinical applications of ultrasound-based radiomics in ovarian cancer, including tumor differentiation, AI-assisted O-RADS risk stratification, subtype prediction, metastatic risk assessment, and virtual biopsy. By integrating imaging features with artificial intelligence and clinical data, these approaches enhance diagnostic accuracy, automate risk classification, and support individualized treatment planning

Differentiation of benign and malignant ovarian tumors

Radiomics and deep learning have shown promise in distinguishing benign from malignant ovarian tumors. Early ultrasound texture analysis using traditional machine learning achieved moderate accuracy (0.62–0.77) [64], later matching mid-level physician performance (69% sensitivity, 86% specificity) but still below expert levels [65]. Multi-feature fusion improved accuracy to over 90%, nearing expert performance [66]. Deep learning has further advanced classification. A CNN-based model with a denoising autoencoder reached 97.2% accuracy in tumor detection and 90.12% in malignancy discrimination [67]. Combining ultrasound with clinical data (e.g., menopausal status, CA125) increased AUC from 0.957 (ultrasound alone) to 0.984 [68]. Moreover, for borderline ovarian tumors (BOTs), radiomics improved differentiation. Nomograms combining radiomic and clinical features outperformed senior sonographers [69], while deep learning models with handcrafted and deep features achieved AUCs >0.83 for all tumor types, enhancing BOT recognition [70]. These advances provide high-accuracy tools to support clinical decisions.

AI-assisted O-RADS risk stratification

However, in actual clinical practice, physicians often need more nuanced risk level judgments to guide management decisions, such as whether or not to operate. In recent years, the O-RADS system proposed by the American College of Radiology has provided a uniform standard for risk stratification of ovarian tumors [71]. To improve its objectivity and operability, several studies have attempted to introduce artificial intelligence and radiomics technologies into the field.

Liu et al. constructed an ultrasound image-based nomogram model that incorporates clinical information, traditional radiomics features, and deep learning features, and the AUC of the model reaches 0.930, which is significantly better than the performance of any single modality, and can effectively improve the interpretation accuracy of young doctors [72]. Du et al. constructed a DLR_Nomogram model that performed well in both O-RADS 4/5 high-risk groups, significantly improved the ability to discriminate border-ambiguous lesions, and outperformed the traditional O-RADS system in risk re-stratification and clinical adjunctive judgment [73]. Liu et al. achieved automatic segmentation of adnexal masses with O-RADS risk class discrimination by constructing a machine learning model based on ultrasound radiomics [74]. Among them, the segmentation model (FCN ResNet101) achieved high accuracy (Dice = 89.1%) in the test set, and the AUC reached 0.966 after combining with LightGBM classification. In addition, Liu et al. conducted the first automated grading study of O-RADS using the ConvNeXt-Tiny network, which achieved an accuracy of 86.4% with an AUC of 0.964 in an independent test set, which significantly shortened the time of interpretation and improved the diagnostic accuracy of low-residency physicians [75]. These AI-enhanced radiomics approaches demonstrate robust potential to standardize O-RADS risk stratification, improve diagnostic consistency across skill levels, and optimize clinical decision-making for ovarian tumor management.

Prediction of ovarian cancer subtypes

Type I and type II epithelial OC differ significantly in treatment strategies and prognosis [76], and radiomics has shown high potential for histologic subtype differentiation. Yao et al. developed a nomogram model for predicting type I and type II EOC by combining the radiographic features extracted from ultrasound images with clinical indicators such as CA125 and CA199 for the first time. The AUC of the model reached 0.83 and 0.82 in the training set and test set, respectively, showing good discrimination ability [77]. On this basis, Tang et al. further constructed a model containing 7 radiomic features and integrated more clinical variables (e.g., menopausal status, ROMA index, etc.) by using a multicenter sample, which improved the AUC of the fusion model in the test set to 0.886. Although CA125 is commonly used as a tumor marker for epithelial OC (EOC), about 20% of patients with EOC have normal levels of CA125, making it easy to miss the diagnosis [78]. To address this challenge, Chen et al. developed a joint model combining 12 imaging features with 2 clinical indicators (age, uric acid), which achieved good results even when traditional markers failed [79]. Radiomics-based multimodal models enhance subtype-specific diagnosis in epithelial ovarian cancer by complementing traditional biomarkers and integrating clinical variables, demonstrating improved accuracy.

Prediction of metastatic risks

Early detection of metastases is critical for accurate staging of OC, which not only helps to improve patient prognosis but may also optimize treatment options and improve overall survival [34]. In recent years, the application of radiomics in OC has evolved to achieve high sensitivity and specificity in the prediction of metastasis, and this progress is expected to further guide clinical decision-making.

Zhou et al. conducted a multicenter study to construct a multimodal prediction model for assessing the risk of peritoneal implantation metastasis (PM) by combining transvaginal ultrasound image features, O-RADS descriptions, and tumor markers (e.g., HE4 and D-dimer) [80]. The model demonstrated better than single-modality prediction ability in the training set, internal validation set, and external test set, especially in the external hospital data, which still maintained high generalization ability, proving its robustness. In contrast, Qi et al. focused on the problem of predicting pelvic lymph node metastasis (LNM) in patients with high-grade plasmacytoid ovarian cancer (HGSOC), and constructed a columnar line graph model for preoperative evaluation by extracting radiomic features using three preoperative ultrasound images and combining CA125 levels with ultrasound-suggested peritoneal thickening information [81]. The model achieved an AUC of 0.881 in the external test set, which was significantly better than the radiomics model alone (0.855) and the clinical model (0.735), and achieved a good balance between sensitivity and specificity.

It was shown that radiomics-based multimodal models advance metastatic risk prediction in ovarian cancer, achieving high diagnostic accuracy and clinical utility for personalized surgical planning and therapeutic decision optimization.

Ultrasound-based radiogenomics in ovarian cancer

Ultrasound-based radiogenomics, an emerging branch of radiomics, has been extensively explored in breast and thyroid cancers but remains understudied in OC [82]. Recent efforts demonstrate its potential as a cost-effective and scalable screening tool. For instance, Camilla et al. developed an ovarian ultrasound radiogenomics model to predict germline *BRCA1/2* mutation status in high-risk women [83]. While this model shows promise for stratifying patients who may avoid unnecessary genetic testing, its negative predictive value currently precludes clinical utility as a standalone screening method. The authors propose that refined versions could eventually aid in identifying “BRCA1/2” pathogenic variant carriers and optimizing genetic testing workflows.

However, this study is limited by its retrospective single-center design, potential selection bias, and restricted focus on “BRCA1/2” genes without addressing broader homologous recombination repair pathways. The model’s applicability remains confined to high-risk populations. Nevertheless, it highlights the feasibility of ultrasound radiogenomics for precision oncology. Multicenter validation, expanded genetic panels, and prospective trials are needed to advance clinical translation.

While radiomic habitat maps are typically defined on CT/MRI, ultrasound remains the primary modality for real-time biopsy guidance. Multimodal image fusion technology, such as integrating ultrasound with CT/MRI at matching scan planes, combines the real-time operability of ultrasound with the complementary strengths of other modalities (Fig. 3). This approach enables ultrasound-guided habitat biopsies by fusing virtual biopsy maps from CT/MRI with live ultrasound imaging [58].

Lucian Beer et al. demonstrated this concept in six HGSOC patients. They co-registered CT-derived radiomic habitat maps with ultrasound via landmark-based rigid registration, achieving a mean dice similarity coefficient (DSC) of 0.78 for pelvic lesions [84]. However, smaller omentum metastases showed poor registration (DSC: 0.37–0.53), attributed to probe-induced anatomical displacement. To balance accuracy and safety, habitats were thresholded at 3 cm³, with ≤ 3 habitats per lesion. Despite demonstrating the feasibility of CT/ultrasound fusion for habitat-guided biopsies, this study is constrained by temporal mismatches between imaging and biopsy, reliance on 2D validation, technical variability across CT protocols, and a small cohort (n = 6). While these factors limit immediate clinical generalizability, the approach establishes a novel framework to integrate radiomics with real-time sampling, advancing research into tumor heterogeneity and precision radiogenomics. Further validation in larger, standardized cohorts is essential.

Critics and challenges

Since Lambin et al. first introduced the term radiomics in 2012 [7], over a decade of research has focused on extracting high-dimensional quantitative features from medical imaging data. These features are refined into clinically actionable biomarkers through advanced analytics [85]. While radiomics is predominantly applied to tumor characterization, including histological subtyping, treatment response prediction, and personalized therapeutic planning. Notably, radiomics also extends to ovarian physiological evaluation. Studies suggest that normal ovarian functions are tightly linked to oncogenesis, where dysregulation may drive malignant transformation [86].

Chen’s team has pioneered AI-driven assessment of ovarian reserve function. Their work includes automated ovarian and follicular boundary segmentation, antral follicle counting, and follicular volumetry [87–90]. They proposed dominant follicle volume as a novel ultrasound-based radiomic biomarker to evaluate oocyte maturity, predict oocyte retrieval outcomes, and optimize the timing of hCG-triggered ovulation. In this decade, the research of radiomics and radiogenomics has involved tumors of different organs in various systems of the human body, from glioma in the brain [91], to lung cancer [92], to tumors in various systems and organs of the abdomen [93, 94]. These findings indicate that radiomics and radiogenomics can support the analysis of OC gene phenotype expression [40], generate habitat maps based on intratumoral heterogeneity, virtual biopsy guide clinical biopsy, and predict recurrence and metastasis [95], predicting patient prognosis, and supporting the formulation of clinical strategies have shown great potential [96].

Despite extensive literature on radiomics, its translation into clinical practice remains limited due to multifaceted challenges. For instance, Park et al. evaluated radiomics studies using the Radiomics Quality Score (RQS) and the TRIPOD (Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis) checklist [97]. Their analysis revealed suboptimal quality, with average RQS scores of 26.1% and TRIPOD compliance rates of 56.8%, underscoring deficiencies in methodological rigor and reporting standards. To address this, mandatory adherence to structured frameworks [98, 99] could enhance reproducibility (Table 3). Key barriers to clinical translation of radiomics include variability in feature reproducibility across imaging protocols [100, 101], lack of standardized methodologies for feature selection and model development [102], and the “black-box” nature of many algorithms, which undermines clinical trust and raises ethical concerns [103]. To address these limitations, advancing interpretable AI frameworks, implementing multicenter prospective validation, and establishing consensus-driven standardized workflows are critical steps to enhance robustness and clinical applicability [104].

While radiomics holds promise for improving clinical outcomes in OC, its clinical adoption demands rigorous validation to conclusively demonstrate clinical utility [100]. Ultrasound-based radiomics and radiogenomics, while sharing common methodological challenges with other imaging modalities, exhibit unique trade-offs in OC applications. A primary limitation lies in operator dependency, as variability in scanning planes and probe handling across clinicians introduces heterogeneity in radiomic feature extraction. This issue may be mitigated by adopting 3D volumetric ultrasound to standardize spatial reconstruction. Conversely, ultrasound offers multimodal real-time capabilities, integrating structural, functional, and molecular imaging biomarkers during a single examination, thereby enabling dynamic tumor characterization. Furthermore, emerging therapeutic technologies like high-intensity focused ultrasound exemplify diagnostic-therapeutic synergy, where real-time imaging feedback during ablation enhances targeting precision and therapeutic monitoring [105]. These dual roles position ultrasound as a versatile tool for advancing precision oncology.

Conclusion

Despite persistent challenges, radiomics represents a paradigm-shifting innovation in oncology. Ultrasound-based radiomics/radiogenomics, in particular, demonstrate unique translational potential owing to the modality’s cost-effectiveness, procedural simplicity, and global availability. While still in its nascent stages, this approach is emerging as a frontier for innovation in OC management. By bridging multimodal imaging biomarkers with therapeutic guidance, ultrasound radiomics not only refines diagnostic precision but also lays the groundwork for closed-loop systems integrating real-time diagnosis, targeted biopsy, and therapy monitoring. Sustained progress will depend on multidisciplinary collaboration, technical refinement, and robust clinical validation to unlock its full clinical promise.
