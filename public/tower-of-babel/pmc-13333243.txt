Respiratory Microbiome Remodeling in Aging: Implications for Immunosenescence and Therapeutic Intervention
Authors: Seong-Mook Jung, Wonkeon Sunwoo, Young Min Son
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aging involves progressive declines in lung structure and immune function, increasing the incidence and severity of respiratory diseases and reducing vaccine responsiveness. Meanwhile, the respiratory tract harbors a dynamic microbial ecosystem that contributes to immune homeostasis and colonization resistance. Growing evidence indicates that aging disrupts this host-microbe balance within the respiratory tract; however, the mechanisms and therapeutic implications remain incompletely integrated. This review summarizes age-related remodeling of the respiratory microbiome. Beyond compositional shifts, aging alters microbial functions, including metabolic output and resilience to perturbation, exhibiting downstream effects on epithelial barriers, mucus clearance, and immune priming. Furthermore, as the microbiome-immune axis is modifiable, microbiome-targeted therapies represent key opportunities to restore respiratory homeostasis during aging. These interventions, combined with senescence- and cytokine-directed immunomodulation and vaccine optimization using adjuvants and mucosal immune-informed designs, may reduce infection burden and chronic lung disease progression in older populations. Together, this review highlights that a deeper understanding of age-related respiratory microbiome remodeling and its interplay with immunosenescence will be essential for the rational design of microbiome-informed therapies.

INTRODUCTION

Aging is a complex and multifaceted process that results in physiological and functional declines across various organ systems, including the lungs and the immune system. With aging, even otherwise healthy individuals experience a gradual decline in lung function, driven by structural alterations that compromise gas exchange and immune changes that increase susceptibility to pulmonary infection and chronic respiratory diseases (12). Across multiple respiratory conditions—including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), lung cancer, and viral pneumonias, such as coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19)—epidemiological data consistently reveal substantially higher incidence, severity, and mortality in older adults than in younger individuals (345). These age-associated pulmonary diseases are increasingly recognized to be closely linked to dysbiosis of the respiratory microbiome, with disease-specific shifts in microbial composition and reduced diversity noted in human and animal models (6).

The respiratory tract, which was once considered a sterile environment, is now recognized as a dynamic microbial ecosystem that consists of bacteria, viruses, fungi, and archaea (7). These microbial communities are collectively referred to as the microbiome, which encompasses the total collection of microorganisms and their genetic material in a specific environment. In the lungs, these communities interact with the host immune system to maintain homeostasis, regulate inflammatory responses, and protect against the invasion of pathogens (8).

Aging disrupts this delicate balance, resulting in microbial dysbiosis—a condition characterized by a decline in beneficial bacteria and an expansion of pathogenic microbes (7). This dysbiosis, in combination with immunosenescence—the progressive decline in immune function associated with aging—results in increased susceptibility to respiratory infections, chronic inflammation, and various pulmonary diseases, including COPD, pneumonia, asthma, and pulmonary fibrosis (910). Unlike the immune systems of young or middle-aged adults, the aging immune system exhibits diminished innate responses, reduced T and B cell diversity, and impaired resolution of inflammation, compromising effective pathogen clearance and tissue repair (11).

In this review, we examine the age-related changes in the composition and function of the respiratory microbiome that occur with aging and explore how these microbial alterations interact with immunosenescence to shape susceptibility to infection, chronic lung disease, and impaired vaccine responsiveness. In particular, we focus on several major microbiome-immune axes, including microbial metabolites, epithelial barrier and mucus function, colonization resistance and inflammatory tone, and age-associated dysfunction in innate and adaptive immunity. We also discuss how these interconnected changes may inform microbiome-based and immune-directed therapeutic strategies. Overall, this review highlights that a deeper understanding of age-related respiratory microbiome remodeling and its interplay with immunosenescence is essential for the rational design of microbiome-informed therapies in older adults.

THE AGING RESPIRATORY MICROBIOME

Age-related compositional alterations in the respiratory microbiome

The respiratory microbiome is a complex and dynamic microbial community residing in the lungs, trachea, and upper respiratory tract. It plays a crucial role in maintaining immune homeostasis, regulating inflammatory responses, and protecting against respiratory infections (8). However, aging leads to significant alterations in both the composition and function of the respiratory microbiome. In line with this, an age-related study of healthy adults reported clear age-dependent shifts in sputum-based airway microbiome composition, with older individuals exhibiting an increased relative abundance of Firmicutes and a decreased abundance of Proteobacteria; these changes were associated with reduced lung function (9). Such age-related remodeling of the respiratory microbiome is associated with altered immune function and respiratory health and may contribute to increased susceptibility to infections, chronic inflammation, and age-related lung diseases (12).

Aging results in a progressive decline in microbial diversity and alterations in community structure, which increases the risk of microbial imbalance, also known as dysbiosis. Compared with healthy younger adults, healthy older adults and patients with chronic lung diseases frequently exhibit reduced bacterial α-diversity and prevalence of commensal bacterial genera, such as Prevotella, in the upper airway including nasopharyngeal, oropharyngeal, and oral samples (1213). Because microbial diversity is essential for maintaining immune balance and restraining pathological inflammation, this age-related loss of diversity and commensal bacteria is thought to make the respiratory system more vulnerable to microbial imbalance and inflammation-mediated disease.

As microbial diversity declines with age, opportunistic pathogens, which can cause infection under conditions of impaired host defense, such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus, and Haemophilus influenzae, become more dominant in respiratory samples, including nasal and oropharyngeal swabs and sputum (1415). Enrichment of these pathogens is associated with increased airway inflammation and age-related respiratory diseases, such as COPD, pneumonia, and bronchiectasis.

Among these pathogens, P. aeruginosa is a Gram-negative opportunistic bacterium that is frequently detected in patients with COPD and significantly contributes to disease exacerbations (16). Another major pathogen, H. influenzae, becomes more prevalent with age and is one of the leading causes of community-acquired pneumonia in older adults (17). Its presence is associated with frequent hospitalizations and high mortality rates. Additionally, S. aureus, especially methicillin-resistant S. aureus, is further enriched in the respiratory tract of high-risk older cohorts, such as nursing home residents, where its prevalence is likely influenced by healthcare exposure and frequent antibiotic use (18). The presence of S. aureus increases the risk of secondary bacterial infections, particularly in individuals recovering from viral infections such as influenza or COVID-19 (1920).

Although these findings are derived predominantly from observational human studies, they collectively support the concept that aging is accompanied by a characteristic respiratory dysbiosis, in which the loss of microbial diversity and commensal microbes is coupled with the selective enrichment of bacterial pathogens. This age-related remodeling of the respiratory ecosystem not only reflects the cumulative impact of immunosenescence, chronic lung disease, and medical interventions but also establishes a proinflammatory milieu that favors persistent colonization and recurrent infections in older adults. These compositional alterations lay the foundation for the functional changes in microbial metabolism, epithelial barrier integrity, and immune regulation that are increasingly recognized as key determinants of respiratory outcomes in the elderly.

Functional consequences of age-related dysbiosis

Beyond compositional changes, the respiratory microbiome also undergoes functional alterations with aging, including shifts in metabolic output and loss of resilience to environmental perturbations (21). These functional declines contribute to immune dysregulation and increased susceptibility to respiratory diseases. For example, in a gut-lung axis study, aged mice with age-related intestinal dysbiosis and enrichment of gut-derived bacteria in the lung have been reported to exhibit exaggerated neutrophilic inflammation, impaired bacterial clearance, and increased mortality after Streptococcus pneumoniae-induced pneumonia compared with young mice (22). Microbial communities across the gut-lung axis produce metabolites that regulate immune responses and help maintain lung homeostasis. Microbial metabolites: short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and antimicrobial peptides (AMPs)

One of the most important classes of microbiota-derived metabolites, SCFAs such as butyrate and propionate, are known for their role in regulating inflammatory pathways and maintaining epithelial barrier integrity through the activation of G-protein-coupled receptors and inhibition of histone deacetylases (23). Across both animal and human studies, aging is accompanied by shifts in gut microbial composition with reduced SCFA-producing bacteria and fecal SCFA concentrations, which correlate with altered expression of SCFA-associated receptors and markers of low-grade systemic inflammation (2425). This reduction in SCFA production is thought to contribute to inflammaging, characterized by chronic, low-grade activation of innate and adaptive immune pathways in older individuals. Most evidence linking altered SCFA availability to pulmonary immune outcomes comes from gut-lung axis studies, in which aging- or infection-associated gut dysbiosis and reduced SCFA production have been associated with increased susceptibility to respiratory infection (222627). By contrast, within the respiratory tract itself, SCFAs have been detected in airway samples; however, direct evidence that SCFAs are produced locally by the respiratory microbiome remains limited (2829). In contrast to the generally beneficial effects of SCFAs, in clinically stable cystic fibrosis, airway anaerobes are detected more frequently with increasing age and produce SCFAs, such as acetate in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), which correlates with increased epithelial IL-8 production, inducing neutrophilic inflammation (29). However, these findings should be interpreted in the context of cystic fibrosis and not as direct evidence of SCFA remodeling during normal aging.

AMPs produced by epithelial cells and shaped by commensal microbiota constitute a key arm of colonization resistance at mucosal barrier surfaces (3031). In the intestine, for example, symbiotic bacteria induce epithelial expression of bactericidal lectins, such as RegIIIγ, which can spatially segregate the microbiota from the epithelium and limit bacterial translocation (31). In addition, human aging is associated with altered AMP profiles in barrier tissues, including reduced Paneth cell α-defensin HD5 levels in intestine and accompanying shifts in gut microbiota composition in older adults (32). In the respiratory tract, age-related changes in β-defensin responses have also been reported in the nasopharynx during severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection (33). Although longitudinal data on AMP expression in the aging lung are still limited, these studies, together with the well-established role of defensins and cathelicidin in airway host defense (30), support the concept that age-related dysbiosis and epithelial aging converge to dysregulate AMP-mediated colonization resistance and thereby increase susceptibility to respiratory infection in older adults.

Epithelial barrier and mucus functions

Dysbiosis and aging-related changes in the gut-lung axis affect epithelial barrier function, with experimental models showing increased gut permeability, translocation of gut-derived bacteria to the lung, and exaggerated neutrophilic inflammation during pneumonia in aged hosts (22). In parallel, aging of the respiratory tract itself is associated with impaired mucociliary clearance and altered mucus properties, which favor mucus retention and airway obstruction—key features of chronic lung diseases, such as COPD and asthma (2134). Together, these findings suggest that age-related dysbiosis and epithelial barrier dysfunction cooperate to create a microenvironment characterized by impaired pathogen clearance, persistent inflammation, and structural remodeling of the airways.

Impact on colonization resistance and inflammatory tone

A healthy microbiome plays an essential role in maintaining colonization resistance and shaping local inflammatory tone in the respiratory tract (35). However, with age, these protective functions become increasingly disrupted. In older adults with influenza-like illness (ILI), the nasopharyngeal communities characterized by low abundance of the commensal genera Corynebacterium and Dolosigranulum during acute illness were enriched for Haemophilus and other potential pathobionts, that is, resident microbes that can become harmful under dysbiotic or inflammatory conditions, and exhibited greater phylogenetic restructuring (36). Participants whose core commensals were relatively preserved during acute ILI exhibited more stable microbiota profiles during recovery. This pattern may suggest that the loss of commensal upper airway bacteria compromises colonization resistance and alters antigen exposure and local cytokine environments during infection, thereby favoring pathogen persistence and dysregulated host responses.

Moreover, dysbiosis contributes to excessive activation of inflammatory pathways, leading to lung tissue damage and fibrosis. For example, among patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), a disease that predominantly affects older adults, those with lower lung microbiome diversity and higher bacterial burden in BALF exhibited faster declines in forced vital capacity and worse survival (37). These patients also exhibited higher neutrophil counts and elevated inflammatory mediators in the lung, suggesting that lung dysbiosis may amplify inflammatory signaling cascades and accelerate fibrotic remodeling of the parenchyma.

Taken together, these studies indicate that age-related dysbiosis reshapes not only the microbial composition but also the metabolic output, barrier function, and ecological stability of the respiratory microbiome. These functional perturbations provide a mechanistic framework linking aging-related microbial changes to the increased burden and altered clinical course of respiratory diseases in the elderly.

Clinical implications of age-related respiratory dysbiosis

These microbiome changes have important clinical implications for disease susceptibility, immune regulation, and treatment responses in older adults (38).

Pneumonia

One of the most concerning implications of age-related microbial dysbiosis is the heightened risk of pneumonia. Older adults experience a loss of protective commensals in the respiratory tract, together with increased colonization by opportunistic pathogens such as S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, and P. aeruginosa in oropharyngeal samples or sputum (1314). More broadly, aging-associated declines in immune surveillance and pathogen clearance increase susceptibility to both bacterial and viral pneumonia in older adults (22239). Community-acquired pneumonia also imposes a greater clinical burden in later life, with higher hospitalization and case-fatality rates (240). In addition to these broader aging-related changes, frail older adults—particularly those with dysphagia or poor oral hygiene—represent a distinct clinical context in which microbiome alterations along the oral-lung axis may further promote aspiration of pathogenic bacteria into the lower respiratory tract and increase the risk of aspiration pneumonia (4142). Thus, frailty-associated aspiration-related microbiome changes should be distinguished from the more general aging-associated shifts in the respiratory microbiome, although both may contribute to pneumonia susceptibility in later life.

Chronic lung diseases: COPD, asthma, and long coronavirus disease (LC)

Dysbiosis of the aging respiratory microbiome is strongly associated with the worsening of chronic lung diseases, such as COPD, asthma, and LC. Older adults with COPD often exhibit a shift of the upper airway and sputum microbiome characterized by an increased presence of pathogenic bacteria, which exacerbates inflammation and airway remodeling (38). Importantly, decreased diversity and increased Moraxella abundance have been found to correlate with higher levels of IL-17A, IL-5, and IL-22 in sputum, linking dysbiosis to a proinflammatory milieu that likely promotes exacerbations and structural airway changes. The presence of P. aeruginosa in patients with COPD has been linked to frequent disease exacerbations and an increased likelihood of hospitalization (16). Similarly, in adults with asthma and COPD, including many older patients, airway microbiome patterns enriched with potentially pathogenic genera such as Moraxella and Haemophilus in sputum have been associated with neutrophilic and proinflammatory airway responses (43).

In addition to these classical chronic lung diseases, LC has emerged as a postviral chronic condition with associated prior SARS-CoV-2 infection (44). A recent meta-transcriptomic study of sputum and upper-airway swab samples from older adults with LC (mean age≈65 years) exhibited reduced microbial α-diversity and distinct β-diversity patterns across the upper and lower respiratory tracts, together with multikingdom remodeling of the respiratory ecosystem—disrupted abundance of pathogenic bacteria and fungal microbes, such as Candida, and increased detection of respiratory viruses, such as herpes simplex virus 1 and human coronavirus 229E—compared with age-matched non-LC controls, supporting an association between persistent respiratory dysbiosis and chronic respiratory and systemic symptoms in LC (45).

Respiratory microbiome influences on vaccine responsiveness and protection

The aging respiratory microbiome influences the host immune responses in ways that may affect both vaccine responsiveness and the level of protection achieved after vaccination. In older adults, nasopharyngeal microbiome profiles are shaped by both age and vaccination status; unvaccinated elderly individuals show enrichment of pneumonia-associated taxa, such as S. aureus and specific Streptococcus species compared with vaccinated or younger individuals, suggesting that age-related respiratory dysbiosis can modulate mucosal immune protection and the risk of secondary bacterial complications following influenza infection (3646). Furthermore, in a longitudinal intranasal live-attenuated influenza vaccine study, anterior nares swabs from healthy individuals collected before and after vaccination showed a significant increase in nasal microbial richness (47). Moreover, the presence of specific taxa was significantly associated with influenza-specific mucosal IgA titers, indicating that vaccine-induced shifts in the nasal microbiome correlate with inter-individual variations in mucosal Ab responses.

As these clinical implications highlight, understanding the interplay between the respiratory microbiome and the immune system in aging individuals is crucial for developing targeted microbiome-based therapies. Strategies aimed at restoring microbial balance may hold promise in preventing and treating respiratory diseases in older adults.

AGING AND MICROBIOME-IMMUNE INTERACTIONS IN THE RESPIRATORY TRACT

Aging is associated with profound changes in immune system function, collectively referred to as immunosenescence (48). These alterations affect both innate and adaptive immunity, resulting in increased susceptibility to infections, chronic inflammation, and impaired tissue repair.

Furthermore, the respiratory microbiome and the immune system maintain a bidirectional relationship, in which the microbiome influences immune function, and the immune system, in turn, shapes microbial composition. In aging individuals, disruption of this crosstalk promotes chronic inflammation, impaired pathogen defense, and increased disease susceptibility through altered microbial metabolites, host signaling pathways, and immune-cell activation.

Innate immune alterations

The innate immune system provides the first line of defense against inhaled pathogens and environmental insults. However, with aging, multiple aspects of innate immunity become dysfunctional, compromising early pathogen control and promoting chronic inflammatory states in the lung (1). For instance, after intratracheal challenge with Gram-positive cell wall components, aged rats exhibited exaggerated neutrophil influx, increased protein leakage, and higher levels of the proinflammatory mediator macrophage migration inhibitory factor in BALF, indicating that the aged innate response is dysregulated and shifts from efficient early pathogen control toward prolonged, tissue-damaging inflammation (49).

Macrophages

Macrophages play a central role in clearing pathogens and apoptotic cells, modulating inflammation, and orchestrating downstream immune responses (50). In aging lungs, macrophages exhibit several functional impairments. Aged macrophages exhibit a diminished capacity to engulf and destroy bacteria and apoptotic neutrophils, leading to delayed clearance of infections and cellular debris (51). Aging also favors the proinflammatory polarization of M1 macrophages, characterized by the production of proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-1β in response to LPS exposure (52). This chronic low-grade inflammatory state contributes to persistent airway inflammation and tissue damage (49).

Simultaneously, pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), including TLRs, become less responsive with age, resulting in delayed or blunted activation upon microbial encounter and ineffective pathogen clearance (52). In addition, aging is associated with impaired phagocytic and efferocytic functions of pulmonary macrophages, which may weaken microbial control in the lung (5253). Supporting this possibility, a recent study using a lung interstitial macrophage-focused Il10ra-deficient model showed that impaired IL-10 sensing in these cells was associated with commensal dysbiosis-driven pulmonary inflammation, accompanied by an approximately 10-fold increase in lung bacterial abundance and expansion of Delftia acidovorans and Rhodococcus erythropolis as potential inflammation-associated taxa in the lower airways (54). Although this was not a physiological aging model, these findings suggest that when macrophage regulatory or bactericidal functions decline, dysbiosis-associated microbial exposure may persist in the lung and promote inflammatory remodeling. In addition, aging-related gut dysbiosis and increased intestinal permeability may further amplify this process by increasing exposure to gut-derived microbial products and, potentially, gut-associated microbial signals (2255). Collectively, these observations raise the possibility that age-associated impairment of macrophage function may contribute to sustained low-grade pulmonary inflammation through persistent microbial stimulation, thereby increasing susceptibility to lung tissue damage and chronic lung disease.

Neutrophils

Neutrophils are the most abundant circulating leukocytes that act as first-line effectors for the rapid elimination of respiratory pathogens (56). Aging is associated with multiple functional defects in neutrophils. In aged hosts, impaired neutrophil chemotaxis not only delays arrival at sites of pulmonary infection but also leads to inappropriate retention within inflamed tissues, amplifying bystander damage (57). The antimicrobial functions of neutrophils are also compromised in aged hosts; oxidative burst activity and phagocytic killing are diminished, weakening their overall bactericidal capacity in aged hosts (58). Furthermore, aging dysregulates neutrophil apoptosis, and aged neutrophils are less prone to undergo programmed cell death, favoring their persistence at inflammatory sites and contributing to chronic tissue injury (59). This accumulation increases oxidative stress and protease release, which can exacerbate inflammation and structural damage in the airways and parenchyma (60).

In the setting of an aging, dysbiotic respiratory microbiome that drives repeated or misdirected neutrophil recruitment, these defects favor tissue destruction over effective host defense, setting the stage for chronic airway injury and remodeling. For example, in patients with COPD, sputum microbiota and airway cytokine profiling in a cohort enriched for older adults revealed that Haemophilus- and Moraxella-dominated, low-diversity clusters were associated with heightened airway inflammation and more frequent exacerbations (61). Consistent with these findings, other studies have revealed that in predominantly older adults with COPD, both increased airway bacterial burden and Proteobacteria-dominated, low-diversity sputum microbiome profiles are associated with higher sputum neutrophil counts, more frequent exacerbations, greater declines in lung function, neutrophil-related inflammatory proteomic signatures, and increased mortality (6263). When these systemic defects are superimposed on an aged respiratory tract already colonized by a dysbiotic microbiome, as seen in COPD, it is biologically plausible that repeated but inefficient neutrophil recruitment promotes chronic oxidative and protease-mediated tissue damage rather than efficient pathogen clearance (5658).

Dendritic cells (DCs)

DCs act as professional antigen-presenting cells that bridge innate and adaptive immunity. With aging, DCs exhibit several functional deficiencies that compromise their ability to prime effective adaptive responses. Aged DCs show reduced antigen uptake and processing, resulting in weaker activation of naïve T cells (64). Although antigen uptake by conventional DCs in aged mice is largely preserved, these cells showed impaired antigen degradation and cross-presentation on MHC class I, resulting in markedly reduced proliferation and IFN-γ production by naïve CD8+ T cells (65). Furthermore, DC migration to draining lymph nodes (LNs) is also impaired by reduced C-C motif chemokine receptor 7 expression on respiratory DCs, delaying the initiation of adaptive immune responses and increasing the risk of uncontrolled infection (66). Similar to macrophages, DCs from older individuals tend to secrete higher levels of inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, and CXCL-10), which contribute to systemic and local inflammaging (67).

In mouse models of respiratory influenza infection, orally antibiotic-treated or germ-free mice lacking commensal microbiota exhibited impaired activation and migration of respiratory DCs to draining LNs and reduced antiviral T cell responses, indicating that commensal-derived tonic microbial signals precondition DCs in the airways (26). However, because these models involve global microbiota depletion or systemic dysbiosis rather than selective perturbation of the respiratory microbiome, these findings cannot be interpreted as evidence specific to airway-resident microbial communities.

Adaptive immune alterations

The adaptive immune system is responsible for antigen-specific responses and long-term immunological memory. Aging leads to profound changes in both T and B cell compartments, resulting in weakened immune surveillance, impaired control of respiratory infections, and reduced vaccine responsiveness.

T cell compartment

T cells are crucial for coordinating immune responses and eliminating virus-infected or transformed cells. With aging, multiple quantitative and qualitative defects arise in T cell populations (68). A primary hallmark is the decline in naïve T cell production due to thymic involution, which progressively limits the repertoire of T cells that can recognize novel antigens (69). This reduction increases susceptibility to emerging or newly encountered respiratory pathogens. Concurrently, memory T cells accumulate with age, skewing the immune system toward responses against previously encountered antigens rather than new infections (70). Chronic antigen exposure—arising from persistent viral infections and environmental antigens—can also drive T cell exhaustion, characterized by reduced proliferative capacity and diminished cytokine production (71). For example, in aged mice that have recovered from influenza pneumonia, chronically stimulated lung CD8+ tissue-resident memory T cells (TRM) acquire a dysfunctional, exhaustion-like phenotype and actively sustain chronic lung inflammation and fibrosis rather than providing efficient antiviral protection (72). Although direct evidence remains limited, age-associated dysregulated microbial communities may contribute to chronic antigenic and inflammatory pressure that favors such dysfunctional T cell states (2173). These exhausted T cells show impaired effector functions and are less effective in clearing respiratory infections in aged hosts, contributing to more severe, prolonged, or relapsing disease courses.

In human lung tissue, ex vivo infection experiments revealed that the frequency of lung CD8+ memory T cells, particularly the lung CD8+ TRM subset, declines with donor age, and this attrition is associated with a blunted type I IFN and IFN-γ response to influenza virus (74). In contrast, in certain mouse models, during the memory phase following influenza virus infection, aged mice can harbor comparable or even higher numbers of lung CD8+ TRM cells than young mice, and these expanded TRM populations are associated with more severe and prolonged lung inflammation after heterosubtypic challenge (7075). Together, these studies indicate that aging can both erode the pool and antiviral quality of lung CD8+ TRM cells, reducing early protection against respiratory viruses. In some settings, it can favor the accumulation of dysregulated TRM populations that are linked to sustained inflammation and tissue damage rather than those that are linked to balanced tissue repair.

These changes are underpinned by classical hallmarks of T cell immunosenescence, including telomere erosion and epigenetic reprogramming that stabilizes terminal effector states at the expense of plasticity (7677). In addition, aging is associated with the accumulation of T cell subsets expressing exhaustion, inhibitory, or senescence markers, such as KLRG1 and PD-1 (7879). Collectively, aging reshapes pulmonary T cell networks into a state that is less capable of mounting de novo responses to novel respiratory pathogens while being more prone to driving bystander tissue damage and chronic lung pathology.

SCFAs, including butyrate, acetate, and propionate, are key microbial metabolites that promote the differentiation and function of Treg, which suppress inflammation (2380). In aged hosts, reduced SCFA production has been linked to enhanced systemic and mucosal inflammatory tone, and oral SCFA supplementation can dampen inflammaging and protect against acute lung injury in mice (81). Furthermore, age-associated quantitative and, more importantly, functional alterations in Treg can impair immune tolerance, favoring excessive inflammation and exacerbating chronic lung diseases such as pulmonary fibrosis (82). These age-related alterations in Treg function are likely exacerbated by an age-related decline in SCFA production, which reduces Treg-supporting and type 2-suppressive signals, thereby favoring Th2-driven airway inflammation and increasing the risk of asthma exacerbations.

COPD is often considered a disease of accelerated pulmonary aging and predominantly affects older adults (83). In a human study of COPD, analysis of both lung tissue and peripheral blood showed an increased Th17/Treg ratio that correlates with impaired lung function (84). In a sputum-based airway microbiome study of patients with COPD, lower-airway dysbiosis, characterized by loss of commensal taxa and enrichment of potentially pathogenic Proteobacteria, was associated with Th1/Th17- and pro-inflammatory sputum mediator profiles (85). Together, these human studies link aging-associated COPD to changes in both T cell balance and the respiratory microbiome, although the relationship between them remains observational rather than causal.

B cell compartment

B cells produce Abs that neutralize pathogens and provide long-term humoral protection. Aging affects multiple aspects of B cell biology. Older individuals generate a less diverse Ab repertoire, which limits their ability to mount robust responses to antigenically novel pathogens (86). Class-switch recombination (CSR) and affinity maturation become less efficient, resulting in lower titers and lower affinity Abs following infection or vaccination (8788). At the cellular level, B cells from older adults exhibit reduced induction of activation-induced cytidine deaminase and the transcription factor E47 upon stimulation, which directly impairs CSR and germinal center reactions (89). Consistent with these cellular defects, elderly individuals often show a smaller increase in influenza-specific Ab titers after vaccination, whereas in pneumococcal vaccination, age-related impairment may be more apparent in Ab functional activity than in total IgG level (9091). These alterations contribute to the reduced effectiveness of vaccines, including those targeting influenza viruses and pneumococci.

In the elderly, the breakdown of B cell tolerance and expansion of age-associated B cells (ABCs) are accompanied by higher basal levels of autoreactive Abs and subclinical autoimmunity, even in the absence of overt autoimmune disease (92). A key cellular correlate of this phenomenon is the expansion of ABCs, a CD11c+T-bet+ memory-like subset that accumulates with age and is programmed by chronic TLR7/9 signaling, together with inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ and IL-21 (93). These ABCs exhibit an intrinsically skewed, often autoreactive B cell receptor repertoire and preferentially generate class-switched IgG autoantibodies and pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby linking B cell immunosenescence to pathogenic humoral responses (94).

At respiratory mucosal surfaces, the density and composition of resident microbial communities are tightly coupled to secretory Ab responses. In the nasal cavity, higher levels of secretory IgA constrain bacterial biomass, and studies of the upper airway indicate that microbiota-host interactions shape the production of mucosal IgA, innate effector molecules, such as secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor, and susceptibility to airway inflammation (95).

Older adults are particularly vulnerable to malnutrition and suboptimal dietary intake because of multifactorial, often modifiable constraints (e.g., reduced appetite, swallowing/dental problems, and functional or social limitations), and inadequate nutrition is a modifiable driver of age-associated microbiome remodeling along the gut-lung axis (21). Such nutrition-linked microbiome remodeling is expected to contribute to dysbiosis and reduced community resilience across the gut-lung axis, thereby altering host immune tone and mucosal defense capacity. Consistent with functional relevance, poor nutritional status in older adults in nursing homes has been associated with weaker serological responses to influenza vaccination, and malnutrition has been frequently observed among poor responders (96). Together with dysbiosis and loss of resilience along the gut-lung axis in malnourished older adults, these age-related changes in respiratory and intestinal microbiota are hypothesized to interact with B cell immunosenescence to impair the quality and durability of mucosal IgA and IgG responses to respiratory viruses and pneumococcal vaccines in the elderly. Defects in B cell function and Ab production further reduce vaccine efficacy, often necessitating booster doses, higher antigen contents, or enhanced adjuvants to achieve sufficient protection in older individuals.

Inflammaging and cellular senescence

A defining feature of the aging immune system is the emergence of persistent, chronic low-grade systemic inflammation, often referred to as inflammaging that develops with aging, driven by cumulative immune dysregulation, cellular senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), and persistent sterile or microbe-derived stimuli, and it contributes to impaired tissue homeostasis and increased vulnerability to infection and chronic disease (97). This state arises from the convergence of several processes, including the increased production of pro-inflammatory mediators, such as IL-6, TNF-α, and C-reactive protein (98). With aging, the epigenome—comprising chemical and structural marks that regulate gene expression without altering the DNA sequence, such as DNA methylation, histone modifications, and chromatin accessibility—undergoes remodeling, and these epigenetic changes can directly shape the induction and persistence of the SASP by sustaining inflammatory gene programs (99). The accumulation of senescent immune and stromal cells amplifies this response through the SASP, characterized by prolonged secretion of cytokines, chemokines, proteases, and growth factors that can maintain chronic immune activation even in the absence of overt infection (97).

In the respiratory tract, age-associated alterations in microbiome-immune crosstalk are increasingly associated with inflammaging, as impaired immune regulation permits pathobiont expansion while persistent microbial products and metabolites sustain innate inflammatory signaling (1213100). For example, aging-related chronic inflammation and TLR dysfunction can impair bacterial sensing/clearance—aged mice show reduced lung TLR1/2/4 expression and blunted cytokine responses during S. pneumoniae challenge despite higher bacterial burdens—while clinical pneumonia cohorts similarly exhibit that more severe disease is accompanied by pathogen-dominant sputum communities, illustrating how expanded pathobionts can sustain innate immune stimulation and further reinforce dysregulated inflammation (101102).

Notably, age-associated upper airway community states feature a higher prevalence of Gram-negative taxa, such as Moraxella, and inflammatory airway ecosystems often shift toward Gram-negative Gammaproteobacteria, together providing a plausible source of increased mucosal exposure to endotoxins, such as LPS (103104). In parallel, increased persistent exposure to proinflammatory microbial components, such as LPS, engages PRRs, particularly TLR4, and sustains a tonic inflammatory tone in the aging airway and parenchyma (105). Thus, age-related changes in microbial function and host immune sensing together maintain a chronic and low-grade inflammaging state rather than an appropriately terminated response.

At the level of innate immune sensing, aging lungs exhibit a paradoxical combination of diminished protective responses and low-grade inflammation. The expression and signaling capacity of PRRs, such as TLRs, decrease, compromising the early detection and clearance of respiratory pathogens and contributing to increased susceptibility to infections such as pneumonia and influenza virus infection (39102). This impaired clearance is expected to permit the persistence and relative expansion of pathobionts, increasing the local burden of microbial ligands in the airway and parenchyma. Moreover, low-dose but continuous exposure to dysbiosis-associated microbial ligands results in persistent engagement of TLR4 and related pathways, promoting ongoing low-level production of inflammatory mediators (105). Importantly, as these inputs are chronic and often subthreshold, the response is maintained as low-grade, persistent inflammation rather than a self-limited, fully resolved acute reaction. In parallel, the dysregulation of inflammasome activity, including impaired NLRP3 function and reduced IL-1β secretion, further weakens antimicrobial defenses and contributes to suboptimal vaccine responses in older individuals (106). Together, these alterations shift host-microbe interactions from tightly regulated, self-limiting inflammation toward a state in which inadequate acute defense coexists with sustained inflammatory signaling.

Accumulating evidence indicates that senescent cell accumulation, microbiota-driven inflammaging, and altered PRR signaling in the aging lung converge to favor fibrotic remodeling of the respiratory tissue. Persistent inflammaging, repeated epithelial injury, and aberrant repair responses promote fibroblast activation, extracellular matrix deposition, and airway structural changes (107108). These processes contribute to the development and progression of chronic respiratory conditions, including COPD, asthma, and fibrotic interstitial lung diseases, such as IPF (6109). Thus, age-related alterations in immune function and the respiratory microbiome not only increase infection risk but also create a self-perpetuating inflammatory milieu that drives irreversible loss of lung function. Understanding how senescence, inflammaging, and microbiome-immune dysregulation intersect in the aging respiratory tract is essential for designing interventions to restore homeostasis, improve vaccine responsiveness, and reduce the burden of chronic lung disease in older populations.

THERAPEUTIC APPROACHES FOR TARGETING MICROBIOME-IMMUNE INTERACTIONS IN THE AGING RESPIRATORY TRACT

Given the profound impact of aging on the respiratory microbiome and immune system, there is a growing interest in therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring microbial balance and enhancing immune function. Although direct therapeutic targeting of the respiratory microbiome remains limited, relevant approaches include microbiome-directed interventions, immunomodulatory therapies, and precision medicine applications aimed at reducing inflammation and mitigating age-related respiratory dysfunction.

This section discusses current and emerging microbiome-based therapies, immune-modulating strategies, and potential clinical applications for mitigating respiratory dysfunction in aging populations (Fig. 3).

Microbiome-targeted therapeutic strategies

Therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating the respiratory microbiome can restore microbial balance, suppress inflammation, and enhance immune defenses. These strategies include probiotics, prebiotics, microbiota transplantation, and engineered microbiome therapeutics.

Probiotics

Probiotics have been widely studied for their beneficial effects on the gut microbiome, and emerging evidence suggests that they may also influence pulmonary immunity, although most current data still derive from oral administration rather than direct respiratory delivery. In human studies, oral probiotic administration has been associated with reduced severity of severe pneumonia in elderly patients and fewer COPD exacerbations in a pilot trial designed to modulate the respiratory microbiome (110111). In older adults, daily intake of specific probiotics has also been associated with enhanced influenza-related protection, including higher Ab responses induced by Lactobacillus casei DN-114 001 and lower incidence of influenza and fever accompanied by increased NK cell and neutrophil activity induced by Bifidobacterium longum BB536 (112113). Evidence for upper-airway colonization resistance by Streptococcus salivarius is currently derived from a randomized controlled trial in physically active individuals rather than elderly respiratory cohorts (114). By contrast, mechanistic evidence that probiotics directly modulate pulmonary immune responses comes mainly from experimental or cell-based studies, including respiratory superinfection models using lactobacilli and studies of cigarette smoke-activated human macrophages treated with Lactobacillus rhamnosus or Bifidobacterium breve (115116). Nonetheless, most of these studies used gut-targeted oral probiotics and did not systematically characterize the respiratory microbiome. Thus, whether the clinical benefits are primarily mediated through modulation of the lung microbiota, the gut-lung axis, or systemic immune pathways remains to be fully elucidated.

Prebiotics

Prebiotics, which are non-digestible fibers that stimulate the growth of beneficial bacteria, also show promise in modulating immune function (117). In prefrail older and healthy adults, galacto-oligosaccharides supplementation increased fecal Bifidobacterium abundance, although it did not improve immune function or oxidative stress in that study (118). Evidence suggesting improved vaccine responsiveness with probiotics or prebiotics comes mainly from broader adult meta-analytic data rather than respiratory-focused interventional studies conducted specifically in older adults (119). Thus, although prebiotics remain biologically plausible modulators of the microbiome-immune axis, direct evidence that they reshape the respiratory microbiome or improve respiratory outcomes in aging populations remains limited.

Microbiota transplantation

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) is the most clinically established microbiota-transfer approach, but its established use in humans is currently confined to gastrointestinal indications, particularly recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection (120). While FMT has been primarily used to treat gut disorders, emerging research suggests that it may also be beneficial for lung diseases via the gut-lung axis. Its relevance to respiratory disease remains indirect and is currently supported mainly by preclinical studies showing that restoration of the gut microbiota can improve pulmonary immune function and reduce inflammation in experimental models of emphysema and COPD (121122). Similarly, evidence that gut microbiota restoration can influence vaccine responsiveness currently comes from preclinical animal models, including mice and non-murine systems, rather than from dedicated human respiratory trials (123124). Thus, although FMT provides a useful translational framework for microbiota manipulation, dedicated clinical trials using FMT to enhance vaccine responses or prevent respiratory infections in patients, especially older adults, are still lacking.

By contrast, respiratory microbiota transplantation (RMT), conceptually analogous to FMT, remains an early translational concept rather than an established clinical intervention. Mechanistic support currently comes from murine models, in which transfer of upper respiratory tract lavage together with fecal suspensions from untreated donors into antibiotic-depleted mice restored GM-CSF- and IL-17A-dependent antibacterial immunity in the lung, indicating that upper airway commensals can causally re-program alveolar macrophage function and improve clearance of bacterial pneumonia (125). In humans, evidence is currently limited to early pilot studies and case-series level experience in refractory chronic rhinosinusitis using donor nasal secretions or sinus mucus after antibiotic preconditioning (126127). Although RMT remains in its infancy compared with FMT, these early studies provide proof-of-concept that targeted manipulation of airway microbial communities may reshape local immune responses and warrant further investigation in respiratory disease settings relevant to aging.

Engineered microbiome therapeutics

Recent advances in synthetic biology have enabled the development of engineered probiotics, which are genetically modified bacteria designed to provide targeted therapeutic effects (128). However, engineered microbiome therapeutics are currently supported largely by review-level, conceptual, and preclinical evidence rather than by human respiratory trials. Current examples include engineered probiotics designed to secrete anti-inflammatory metabolites, which have so far been demonstrated mainly in gut-focused experimental models such as colitis (129). Conceptually, similar synthetic-biology strategies could be extended to lung-resident microbial chassis or inhaled live biotherapeutic products (LBPs), enabling the in situ production of immunomodulatory metabolites within the respiratory tract to provide localized control of age-related chronic inflammation. Direct respiratory applications, however, remain preclinical; for example, engineered live bacteria suppressing P. aeruginosa infection and biofilm formation have been demonstrated in mouse lung models rather than in older human patients (130). Likewise, evidence that engineered probiotics can enhance vaccine responses is currently derived mainly from conceptual reviews and preclinical studies, including mouse influenza models, rather than clinical studies in older adults (131). Thus, engineered microbiome therapeutics should currently be viewed as emerging preclinical platforms for precision intervention rather than near-term clinical options for aging respiratory disease.

Immunomodulatory strategies

Immunomodulatory strategies aim to counteract immunosenescence and restore effective immune responses in aging individuals. These strategies include senolytic agents, cytokine-targeting drugs, and immune-boosting interventions. Although they do not primarily target the respiratory microbiome itself, they may still intersect with microbiome-immune interactions by modifying the inflammatory and immune context in which dysbiosis develops and persists. Given that direct microbiome-based therapies for the aging respiratory tract remain limited, these host-directed strategies are discussed here as complementary approaches for improving respiratory immune resilience in older adults.

Senolytic therapies

Senolytic drugs selectively induce apoptosis in senescent immune cells, which play a role in chronic inflammation and inflammaging (132). These drugs have shown promise in restoring immune function and reducing inflammation in aging individuals. Among senolytic strategies, the most direct human evidence currently comes from a small first-in-human open-label pilot study of dasatinib plus quercetin in patients with IPF, in which preliminary benefit was reported for age-related phenotypes (133). However, the ability of these drugs to specifically deplete senescent T cells and enhance adaptive immune responses in elderly individuals has not yet been clearly demonstrated and remains an active area of investigation. In contrast, evidence that senolytic approaches restore lung immune function or reduce virus-associated tissue injury currently comes from preclinical models. In aged-lung experimental models, senolytic targeting has been associated with reduced inflammatory responses, lower senescence burden, and attenuation of virus-induced lung injury (134). Senolytic therapy is therefore being explored for age-related lung diseases, but much of the respiratory evidence remains preclinical.

Senolytic strategies may intersect with microbiome dynamics indirectly. In aged mice, dasatinib plus quercetin alleviated intestinal senescence and inflammation while modulating gut microbiome composition (135). More broadly, links between senolytics and microbiome dynamics are currently supported mainly by aged-animal studies and review-level discussions rather than respiratory intervention studies in older adults (135136). Therefore, the observed association suggests a potentially relevant link between senolytics and microbiome-immune interactions, but further studies are needed to define its biological role.

Cytokine-targeted therapies

Targeting inflammatory cytokines has emerged as a promising strategy to counteract chronic lung inflammation and immune dysfunction associated with aging. Evidence for cytokine-targeted therapies currently comes mainly from human proof-of-concept studies in non-aging-specific inflammatory airway disease settings. For example, IL-6 receptor blockade has been evaluated in mild asthma trials in humans, where pharmacodynamic target engagement was confirmed but robust improvement in allergen-induced airway responses was not demonstrated (137). Similarly, inhaled IFN-β1a has phase 2 human trial data in viral respiratory infection settings, although its specific relevance to aging-associated microbiome-immune dysfunction remains uncertain (138). Thus, while cytokine-targeted strategies are clinically more advanced than several microbiome-directed approaches, their application as microbiome-relevant interventions for aging-related respiratory dysfunction remains largely exploratory.

Cytokine-targeted therapies may also intersect with microbiome dynamics, as changes in gut microbiome composition have been reported in human intestinal inflammatory disease settings during anti-cytokine treatment (139). However, these findings are derived from non-respiratory human disease settings and cannot yet be directly extrapolated to age-associated intestinal or respiratory disease. Thus, whether cytokine-targeted therapies meaningfully influence microbiome-immune interactions in aging-related respiratory disease remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

Strategies to improve vaccine responses

Aging is associated with poor vaccine responses due to immunosenescence. To address this challenge, new strategies have been developed to enhance vaccine efficacy in older individuals. Among the interventions discussed in this section, vaccine optimization is one of the most clinically advanced. Adjuvanted vaccine strategies are supported by human data in older adults, including MF59-adjuvanted influenza vaccines and phase 1/2 evaluation of RSV prefusion F vaccines (140141). TLR agonists are also being investigated as compounds that can enhance antigen presentation, but support for their use in this context currently comes mainly from broader mechanistic and preclinical literature rather than from aging-specific respiratory intervention trials (142). Likewise, IL-7-based enhancement of respiratory vaccine responses remains preclinical, with support derived from intranasal mouse influenza models rather than clinical studies in older adults (143).

The microbiome may also shape respiratory vaccine responsiveness. Evidence that the microbiome influences respiratory vaccine responses currently comes mainly from animal studies, including microbiota depletion models and oral-bacteria-based mucosal adjuvant approaches (26144). In humans, support is more indirect and is currently limited to oral probiotic studies or meta-analytic data suggesting modest improvement in vaccine responses in older adults, without direct demonstration of respiratory microbiome remodeling (145). Nonetheless, direct evidence that microbiome-targeted approaches can consistently enhance respiratory vaccine efficacy in older individuals remains limited, and studies directly examining how the respiratory microbiome influences vaccine responsiveness in older adults are still needed.

Taken together, these microbiome-targeted and immunomodulatory interventions suggest that the aging respiratory tract is not simply an irreversibly declining system, but a plastic ecosystem that may be therapeutically reconditioned. However, the current evidence remains uneven, with much of the support for microbiome-directed mechanisms still deriving from preclinical models and gut-focused studies, while direct evidence in older adults remains limited. Clinical interpretation is further complicated by the heterogeneity of the available human data, which vary substantially across age groups, underlying diseases, intervention routes, and study endpoints. A major translational limitation is that many studies infer respiratory benefit without directly assessing respiratory-focused endpoints, such as respiratory microbiome composition, lower-airway inflammation, pneumonia incidence, exacerbation frequency, hospitalization, or mucosal immune responses. Given that many of these approaches remain at an early stage of development, especially for direct respiratory application, future studies in older adults will first need to establish feasibility and safety while considering heterogeneity in frailty, comorbidity, and baseline microbiome-immune status. Early clinical studies should therefore prioritize feasibility, safety, and respiratory-relevant outcome assessment before larger efficacy-oriented trials are pursued. Where feasible, incorporation of respiratory-relevant outcomes will also be important to clarify whether observed benefits reflect modulation of the respiratory microbiome itself or broader systemic effects. Future studies combining longitudinal microbiome profiling, immune phenotyping, and age-aware clinical investigation will be important to define causality and to support the development of safe, personalized interventions for older adults with lung disease or suboptimal respiratory vaccine responses.

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES

Aging induces extensive changes in the respiratory microbiome and the immune system, creating a self-reinforcing cycle that contributes to chronic respiratory diseases, increased infection susceptibility, and diminished immune function (Fig. 4). Disruptions in the gut-lung axis, including gut dysbiosis, increased intestinal permeability, and altered microbial metabolite signaling, may further exacerbate pulmonary inflammation and weaken epithelial repair and immune tolerance. Understanding how microbial and immune pathways converge during aging will be essential for identifying targets that can restore tissue homeostasis and strengthen host defense.

Future research should prioritize therapeutic approaches that can directly modulate the microbiome to enhance immune resilience. Prebiotics, probiotics, and microbial transplantation represent promising strategies to correct dysbiosis and improve pulmonary immunity. Metabolite-based interventions, such as the delivery of SCFAs or other immunoregulatory microbial metabolites, may help reestablish balanced immune responses and reduce age-associated pulmonary inflammation. Engineered bacterial strains and LBPs offer opportunities for the precise manipulation of microbial communities, suppression of pathogenic overgrowth, and reinforcement of epithelial and immune repair mechanisms in aging lungs.

An important translational consideration is that microbiome-targeted therapies may pose distinct safety and efficacy challenges in frail older adults. In this population, immunosenescence, multimorbidity, impaired barrier integrity, and frequent antibiotic exposure may increase vulnerability to adverse effects and contribute to variable treatment responses. For live microbial interventions, including probiotics, FMT, RMT, and LBPs, potential risks include microbial translocation or infection, whereas therapeutic efficacy may also be limited by colonization failure or inconsistent engraftment within a pre-existing dysbiotic ecosystem. In addition, FMT raises concerns regarding donor screening and transmission of infectious or multidrug-resistant organisms, RMT poses unresolved challenges related to donor airway sample standardization, delivery, and engraftment durability, and engineered LBPs introduce additional issues of persistence, biocontainment, and unintended immune activation. To address these challenges, future studies should incorporate rigorous donor and product standardization, recipient stratification based on clinical and microbiome features, longitudinal monitoring of engraftment and safety, and, where possible, more defined or controllable formulations to improve predictability and translational feasibility in frail older adults.

Advances in vaccine science may further support respiratory health in older adults. Next-generation vaccines that incorporate microbiome-derived adjuvants or that intentionally modulate mucosal immunity could help overcome immunosenescence-related reductions in vaccine responsiveness. By enhancing mucosal immune activation and improving antigen presentation, microbiome-informed vaccines may provide superior protection against respiratory infections in aging populations.

Overall, advancements in respiratory medicine will require an integrated strategy that combines microbiome-targeted therapies, metabolite-based interventions, and next-generation immunotherapies. More fundamentally, age-related respiratory microbiome remodeling should be understood not only as a compositional shift, but as a functional reorganization that interacts with immunosenescence to shape inflammation, host defense, and vaccine responsiveness. A clearer understanding of this microbiome-immune axis will be essential for developing microbiome-informed interventions that improve respiratory health in older adults.
