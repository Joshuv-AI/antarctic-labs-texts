Language Models as Artificial Learners: Investigating Crosslinguistic Influence
Abderrahmane Issam, Yusuf Can Semerci, Jan Scholtes, Gerasimos Spanakis

Abstract
Despite the centrality of crosslinguistic influence (CLI) to bilingualism research, human studies often yield conflicting results due to inherent experimental variance. We address these inconsistencies by using language models (LMs) as controlled statistical learners to systematically simulate CLI and isolate its underlying drivers. Specifically, we study the effect of varying the L1 language dominance and the L2 language proficiency, which we manipulate by controlling the L2 age of exposure—defined as the training step at which the L2 is introduced. Furthermore, we investigate the impact of pretraining on L1 languages with varying syntactic distance from the L2. Using crosslinguistic priming, we analyze how activating L1 structures impacts L2 processing. Our results align with evidence from psycholinguistic studies, confirming that language dominance and proficiency are strong predictors of CLI. We further find that while priming of grammatical structures is bidirectional, the priming of ungrammatical structures is sensitive to language dominance. Finally, we provide mechanistic evidence of CLI in LMs, demonstrating that the L1 is co-activated during L2 processing and directly influences the neural circuitry recruited for the L2. More broadly, our work demonstrates that LMs can serve as a computational framework to inform theories of human CLI.

Introduction

The study of crosslinguistic influence (CLI) is central to understanding how two languages coexist and interact within bilingual speakers
33
;
34
. Despite its prominence, the underlying mechanisms of CLI remain debated. Evidence from crosslinguistic priming—in which processing a structure in one language facilitates the production of the same structure in another—points to the existence of shared syntactic representations between languages
17
;
16
;
12
. However, while priming is a well-attested phenomenon, the number of variables that can affect human language acquisition leaves many questions unanswered. Previous research have explored various predictors of CLI; yet, findings remain inconclusive due to significant variance in how these predictors are operationalized across studies
37
.

Language models (LMs) can simulate human-like grammatical judgments from unstructured data
40
;
44
. Despite significant differences between LMs and human learning, they can still contribute to the debate on human language acquisition, especially when their learning environment is deliberately made plausible
39
. Their greatest potential lies in the ability to ablate the variables that affect CLI. For instance, we can vary the L1 and L2 language exposure ratios and study the effect on crosslinguistic priming. This degree of control is rarely possible in psycholinguistic studies, which often rely on non-standardized linguistic tests of language dominance, leading to contradictory conclusions
37
. Furthermore, while human studies are often limited in their scalability, artificial learners facilitate the broad evaluation of linguistic phenomena, and the extension to languages with few non-native speakers, offering a comprehensive quantitative account of CLI.

While LMs cannot simulate the full human learning experience, we focus on studying the effects of the L1 language dominance and L2 language proficiency, which are straightforward to manipulate in LMs, and were shown to be predictive of CLI and crosslinguistic priming effects
37
;
16
. As shown in Figure
1
, to manipulate dominance and proficiency, we vary the age of exposure, which is the training step at which we introduce the L2 language. For instance, introducing the L2 language at step=32K means the model was trained exclusively on the L1 language for 32K steps, leading it to be more entrenched in the model’s parameters, therefore increasing its dominance. To maintain the L1 influence and prevent catastrophic forgetting, we train on interleaved L1/L2 sequences to maintain the L1 language influence; consequently, late L2 introduction also results in lower L2 exposure, directly affecting its proficiency.

Our hypothesis is that a distant L1 language will lead to more negative or less positive CLI effects compared to a less distant one. As shown in Figure
1
, we study the CLI effects of 5 languages that are increasingly distant from the L2 language. We measure CLI effects using the Benchmark of Linguistic Minimal Pairs for English (BLiMP)
40
—a dataset of 67k pairs of grammatical and ungrammatical sentences. More specifically, we measure the difference in BLiMP accuracy between the bilingual LM, and a monolingual counterpart. While studies of crosslinguistic priming have found that it affects both grammatical
6
;
16
and ungrammatical
36
;
5
structures, we similarly prime the model with the L1 translation of the grammatical target sentence, and we study its effect on BLiMP accuracy.

Beyond grammatical accuracy, we investigate whether bilingual LMs exhibit preference for the writing of non-native speakers that share the same L1. Using the First Certificate in English (FCE) dataset
43
—which contains articles written by different L1 non-native speakers along with their corrections—we study whether LMs internalize L1 specific CLI effects. This analysis provides evidence that the nuances of human CLI can be modeled and differentiated computationally. Finally, we provide mechanistic evidence of L1 influence on L2 processing and learning. We use LogitLens
28
to decode LMs hidden states and quantify the L1 language presence during L2 processing. Furthermore, we investigate the effect of the L1 language on learning the L2 by correlating the L2 language neuron overlap between the L1 languages with their syntactic distance.

In summary, this work provides a comprehensive investigation of the computational modeling of CLI effects. Our contributions are as follows:

•

We propose a methodology to manipulate the L1 language dominance and the L2 language proficiency by varying the age of exposure and training on interleaved L1/L2 sequences.

•

We perform a quantitative study and ablation of CLI and crosslinguistic priming on the BLiMP benchmark.

•

Using the FCE dataset, we investigate whether LMs internalize L1-specific nuances based on their preference of non-native text from the same L1.

•

We provide mechanistic evidence of L1 influence using LogitLens and language neuron analysis.

Related Works

Crosslinguistic Influence

The study of how two languages interact is a prominent research focus of both human bilinguals
20
;
32
;
37
;
34
and artificial learners
30
;
29
;
42
;
3
. While studies of language models (LMs) consistently show that syntactic distance negatively impacts target language performance, it remains unclear whether this effect stems from shared grammatical representations or alternative factors, such as interference resulting from limited model capacity
9
. In human CLI research, there is a stronger consensus that representations are either fully shared or separate-but-connected
16
;
2
. Most of the evidence for this comes from studies of crosslinguistic priming
7
. We similarly employ crosslinguistic priming to study CLI in artificial models, discussing the implications for both artificial and human bilinguals.

Crosslinguistic priming

Crosslinguistic priming, first formulated by
7
, is a promising methodology for validating hypotheses regarding CLI
33
. For instance,
17
;
16
proposed the the shared-syntax hypothesis, using crosslinguistic priming as evidence. Specifically, the hypothesis is that if a structure is shared between two languages, then priming will lead to its activation, subsequently affecting processing and production in the other language. This hypothesis implies that priming within and across languages should be similar, and inherently bidirectional
31
. However, other studies have questioned these implications
38
;
2
, suggesting that representations are separate-but-connected rather than fully shared. Furthermore, studies on priming of ungrammatical structures support the connectedness hypothesis
36
;
5
, where even structures that do not exist in the target language can be primed. We contribute to this debate by applying crosslinguistic priming to LMs as controlled statistical learners, and we discuss our findings in light of these hypotheses.

L2 acquisition in Language Models

Previous work have studied the effect of multilinguality and bilingual training on individual language acquisition
14
;
13
;
9
;
3
. However, these studies often overlook the establishment of an explicit L1-L2 status between languages.
29
and
42
addressed this by training Second Language Acquisition (SLA) LMs to study transfer effects, finding that greater linguistic distance correlates with increased negative transfer. While these studies prioritize cognitive plausibility by either training on child directed speech
42
or on human-scale linguistic input
29
, we argue that for studying CLI, the plausibility of the bilingual training regime is more critical than restricted data. Because models trained on human-scale data achieve sub-human performance, we prioritize approaching human-like grammatical competence to ensure that observed CLI effects are representative of mature bilingual processing. Other studies have trained SLA LMs to study different L2 acquisition phenomenon; for instance,
11
trained SLA models to study the critical period (CP) effects
26
. Most similar to our approach,
4
used crosslinguistic priming to track the development of shared grammatical representations. However, our focus is on CLI as a potential product of these shared representations. We specifically employ crosslinguistic priming to study CLI effects and to ablate conditions that are necessary for simulating human-like CLI.

Methodology

Our methodology comprises four parts: the bilingual training framework (§
3.1
), language selection and syntactic distance (§
3.2
), the CLI and priming evaluation protocol (§
3.3
), and mechanistic interpretability methods used to investigate internal representations (§
4.3
).

3.1
Bilingual Model Training

Our bilingual training setup is designed to mirror key constraints of human acquisition. Bilingual humans typically receive less input per language than monolinguals, as acquiring multiple languages does not increase total linguistic exposure
19
. Furthermore, variations in the age of exposure affect both the total volume of L2 input and the learner’s cognitive plasticity, which typically declines with age
26
. We simulate these dynamics by training LMs on a fixed amount of training steps while varying the onset of L2 introduction. To reflect the decline of learning ability, we use a linear learning rate scheduler that decreases the learning rate linearly as a function of the training steps. During L2 introduction, we maintain exposure to the L1 language to prevent catastrophic forgetting
11
;
4
, a phenomenon that humans generally do not experience due to critical period (CP) effects
24
;
8
. While this setup does not capture the full complexity of human learning, it provides a principled approach to establishing a clear language dominance hierarchy. Within this framework, increasing the age of exposure simultaneously increases L1 dominance and decreases L2 proficiency—two primary predictors of CLI and priming effects
37
;
16
.

Following previous work
10
;
4
, we train bilingual GPT2 models with balanced and imbalanced L1 and L2 exposure. For each language, we use 128M lines of de-duplicated OSCAR corpus
1
and train a SentencePiece tokenizer
23
for each language pair on 100K lines. To ensure a balanced vocabulary despite imbalanced exposure, we sample tokenizer training lines equally from both languages. We create sequences of 256 tokens, shuffle them, then sample 1B tokens for training per language, and 1M tokens for evaluation. During monolingual training, all sequences in the batch are of the same language, while during bilingual training, the batch contains interleaved L1 and L2 sequences. Models are trained for a total of 64K steps with a linear learning rate scheduler, and we vary the age of exposure by initiating the bilingual training at steps 0, 16K, 32K, 48K without resetting the learning rate. Detailed hyperparameters are reported in Appendix
A
.

3.3
Linguistic Evaluation

3.3.1
Correlation between Syntactic Distance and L2 Knowledge

We investigate the correlation between L1-L2 syntactic distance and L2 grammatical proficiency as a primary proxy for CLI. To quantify L2 knowledge, we use the BLiMP benchmark
40
, which consists of 67,000 minimal pairs of acceptable and unacceptable sentences across 67 linguistic categories. We evaluate the model’s performance based on sentence-level surprisal. For a given pair (
s_{acc}
,
s_{unacc}
), the model is considered correct if it assigns a higher pseudo-log-likelihood to the acceptable sentence:

\log P(s_{acc})>\log P(s_{unacc})

(1)

where the probability is normalized by the number of tokens to account for variations in sequence length. Our hypothesis is that if the L1 significantly influences the L2, then L2 accuracy on BLiMP will exhibit a negative correlation with the syntactic distance between the language pair.

Because our tokenizers are trained on bilingual data unique to each L1-L2 pair, the resulting vocabularies vary across models. To isolate the effects of CLI from tokenizer differences, we train a monolingual L2 baseline for each pair using the same bilingual tokenizer. This ensures that any performance difference is attributable to training data composition rather than vocabulary bias. Following
3
, we quantify the CLI effect as the relative change in accuracy:

\small CLI_{L1\to L2}=\frac{\text{Acc}{L2}-\text{Acc}{L1,L2}}{\text{Acc}_{L2}}

(2)

where
Acc_{L2}
represents the monolingual baseline accuracy and
Acc_{L1,L2}
represents the bilingual model accuracy. Under this metric, a positive value indicates interference (negative transfer), while a negative value indicates facilitation (positive transfer) from the L1 to the L2.

3.3.2
Preference for Non-native Constructions

CLI effects extend beyond binary grammatical accuracy to influence the frequency and distribution of specific constructions. For instance, while both English and French allow prenominal adjectives, French-English bilinguals may produce them more frequently than monolinguals due to crosslinguistic transfer
27
. To capture these subtle "interlanguage" patterns, we evaluate whether bilingual LMs exhibit a preference for the specific constructions used by non-native speakers of the same L1.

We use the First Certificate in English (FCE) dataset
43
, a Grammatical Error Correction (GEC) corpus annotated with learner L1s. We convert the dataset into a minimal-pair acceptability task, filtering for grammatical errors while excluding spelling and punctuation mistakes. Since some L1 languages exhibit significantly higher number of data points than others, we undersample the dataset using 5 random seeds to prevent them from biasing the results.

Our core assumption is that a model that "prefers" specific L1 constructions will exhibit a smaller surprisal gap between acceptable and unacceptable sentences. Because raw surprisal values vary across LMs, we define a normalized surprisal difference,
\Delta_{S}
, to compare preferences across models:

\small\Delta_{S}(M_{L1^{i}})=\frac{\log P(s^{i}_{acc})-\log P(s^{i}_{unacc})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}[\log P(s^{j}_{acc})-\log P(s^{j}_{unacc})]}

(3)

where
M_{L1^{i}}
is a model trained with
L1_{i}
as its primary language and
s_{j}
represents sentences from learners with
L1^{j}
and
N
is the number of languages. A lower
\Delta_{S}
indicates that the model finds the errors of its corresponding L1 less "surprising" relative to errors from other L1 backgrounds, signaling a computational preference for specific L1 CLI effects. The aforementioned undersampling ensures that the denominator is not biased by high frequency languages.

3.3.3
Crosslinguistic Priming

To investigate the influence of shared or connected representations on L2 processing, we employ a crosslinguistic priming paradigm similar to those used in psycholinguistic studies
16
;
36
. Following the approach of
4
, we evaluate whether the presence of an L1 prime affects the model’s preference for L2 grammatical vs. ungrammatical structures. Since BLiMP benchmark lacks parallel translations, we generate L1 primes by translating the acceptable sentence of each minimal pair into the corresponding L1 using the NLLB-200-distilled-600M model
1
1

1

https://huggingface.co/facebook/nllb-200-distilled-600M
. To measure the priming effect, we prepend the L1 translation as context and calculate the length-normalized negative log-likelihood (surprisal) of the subsequent L2 acceptable and unacceptable sentences. Formally, we evaluate the conditional log-probability:
\log P(X|\text{Prime})
, where
X\in{s_{acc},s_{unacc}}
and the Prime is the L1 translation of
s_{acc}
. By comparing
CLI_{L1\to L2}
before and after priming, we quantify the degree to which L1 structures facilitate or interfere with L2 processing.

3.4
Mechanistic Evidence

To corroborate our behavioral findings, we investigate the internal mechanisms of CLI by analyzing the L1 language presence during L2 processing
3.4.1
and L1 effect on the L2 language neurons
3.4.2
.

3.4.1
Logit Lens

LogitLens
28
projects intermediate hidden states into the output vocabulary space by applying the model’s unembedding matrix. This allows for the observation of how internal representations converge toward a final prediction across layers. Recent work has demonstrated that multilingual LLMs often utilize English as an intermediate "pivot" when processing other languages
41
. We employ LogitLens to detect whether our bilingual LMs similarly utilize the L1 as an intermediate representation during L2 processing, as a direct indicator of L1 influence on the L2 hidden states.

Our approach identifies the top 10 predicted tokens per hidden state. To quantify language presence, we categorize these tokens using a pre-constructed dictionary. A token is classified as L1- or L2-specific if its relative frequency in the respective training corpus is at least twice that of the other language (
\text{count}(L1)/\text{count}(L2)>2
). By tracking the distribution of these classified tokens across layers, we can measure the L1 presence during L2 inference.

3.4.2
Language Neurons identification

We identify language neurons as those consistently activated by sequences of a specific language. Using 10M held-out tokens, we isolate attention and Feed-Forward Network (FFN) neurons using the Parallel Language-specific Neuron Detection (PLND) method
45
. Following this framework, we define language neurons as those consistently ranking in the top 25% of activations. For our GPT2 architecture, this corresponds to 192 neurons per attention layer and 768 neurons per FFN layer.

To assess L1 influence on L2 structural representations, we identify L2-specific neurons across different L1 models and calculate their intersectional overlap. We hypothesize that if the L1 affects the development of L2 circuitry, then L1 languages with greater syntactic similarity will yield a higher degree of L2 neuron overlap. We test this by correlating the number of shared L2 neurons with the syntactic distance of the respective L1 pairs.

The introduction of crosslinguistic priming further polarizes these effects. For syntactically similar pairs, priming facilitates processing, resulting in positive CLI (i.e., accuracy exceeding the monolingual baseline). In contrast, priming distant L1s leads to further degradation in L2 accuracy. While increasing L1 dominance (moving from Step=0 to Step=16K) amplifies the overall priming magnitude, this effect begins to wane with higher ages of exposure (Step=48K). We attribute this decline to the model’s reduced L2 proficiency, aligning with psycholinguistic evidence that L2 competence modulates the strength of priming effect

36

Analysis of the 13 individual linguistic phenomena in BLiMP (Appendix
B.1
) reveals that the number of phenomena that benefit from positive priming effects is negatively correlated with the L1 syntactic distance. Furthermore, ablation of the translation model (Appendix
B.2
) and permutation tests (Appendix
B.3
) confirm that the results are not explained by translation quality, lexical overlap or the L1 language activation. Specifically, neither shuffling the prime tokens nor sampling a random prime leads to effects consistent with syntactic distance, suggesting that observed CLI is driven by specific L1 structure activation that leads to either positive or negative CLI depending on whether it is shared between the languages or not.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that both L1 dominance and L2 proficiency, operationalized here via the age of exposure, are robust predictors of CLI strength, corroborating findings from human bilingual studies

;
25
;
5

Based on the observation that Greek and Korean are the only two languages with a different script than English, we conducted an experiment of romanizing both languages using uroman

This suggests that orthographic overlap serves as a critical facilitator for CLI, eliciting a stronger co-activation of shared syntactic representations between the L1 and L2.

In conclusion, our SLA models exhibit a higher preference for the "interlanguage" patterns of linguistically similar L1s, particularly when prompted with an L1 prime. While we observe significant variance across different ages of exposure, a consistent pattern emerges: linguistic distance positively correlates with surprisal difference. This indicates that as syntactic distance increases, the model’s "familiarity" with L1-specific non-native constructions diminishes, providing behavioral evidence that LMs internalize L1-specific patterns similar to those observed in human bilinguals.

4.3
Mechanistic Analysis

4.3.1
LogitLens: L1 Co-activation during L2 Processing

Figure 5
:
The ratio of L1 by L2 tokens during L2 processing on BLiMP dataset. The bold lines show the average over the 5 languages.

Figure
5
illustrates the ratio of L1 to L2 tokens decoded from the model’s hidden states via LogitLens. We observe a clear increase in the L1/L2 ratio corresponding to an increased age of exposure, providing a mechanistic explanation for the intensified CLI effects observed in models with late age of exposure. This ratio serves as a direct proxy for L1 dominance and entrenchment; notably, the balanced bilingual model (Step=0) maintains a ratio near zero, suggesting that imbalanced training is a prerequisite for establishing a dominant L1 language.

Across languages, the volume of L1 tokens in the hidden states appears independent of syntactic distance. Surprisingly, L1 priming does not uniformly increase L1 token density; rather, the effect is language-specific. For example, priming significantly elevates the L1/L2 ratio in Turkish-L1 models while decreasing it in Greek-L1 models. This suggests that the impact of priming on performance (as seen in §
4.1
) is likely driven by the activation of specific grammatical representations rather than a simple increase in general L1 signal.

4.3.2
Neuron Overlap: How the L1 Language Affects The L2 Language Neurons

We hypothesize that if the L1 serves as a structural prior, syntactically similar L1s will result in higher overlap among L2-activated neurons compared to distant L1s. Figure
6
illustrates the number of shared neurons of each L1 language with the rest of the languages shown on the x-axis based on their syntactic distance. Across all models, we observe a consistent negative correlation: as syntactic distance increases, the number of shared L2 neurons significantly decreases. This trend confirms that the L1 influence is not merely a behavioral phenomenon but is rooted in the model’s internal architecture. Notably, German as the language most similar to English, exhibits a shallower slope and wider confidence interval, suggesting it leads to less distinct neuron recruitement due to its similarity with the L2.

5
Ablations

5.1
Early Imbalanced LMs do not witness similar effects

Models trained with a high age of exposure are inherently subjected to two variables: an imbalance in total training volume (more L1 data, less L2 data) and the sequential nature of acquisition (L1 entrenchment prior to L2 introduction). To isolate these effects and verify if data imbalance alone is sufficient to establish L1 dominance, we trained a series of early imbalanced LMs. These models were exposed to both languages starting at Step 0, but with L1/L2 ratios specifically calibrated to match the total token counts of the 16K and 32K age of exposure models.

The results in Figure
7
demonstrate that early imbalanced training is insufficient to produce effects consistent with linguistic distance on the BLiMP benchmark. While priming does yield some positive transfer for similar languages (German, Spanish) and negative transfer for distant ones, the magnitude and gradient of these effects do not scale linearly with syntactic distance. This leads us to conclude that prior L1 entrenchment is necessary for consistent CLI effects.

5.2
Asymmetry of CLI effects

We ablate the asymmetry of CLI effects by training on English as the L1 and the other languages as L2. Figure
8
shows the CLI effects on BLiMP of two ages of exposure (i.e. 16K and 32K). The results show that before priming, the L2 languages—regardless of their syntactic distance—lead to negligible effects on the L1’s language grammatical accuracy. Furthermore, the priming effects only affect the 3 most similar languages positively, while distant languages (i.e. Korean and Turkish) show minimal effects.

These results suggest that while the priming of grammatical structures is largely bidirectional, the priming of ungrammatical constructions is not. Because grammatical structures are likely shared or highly integrated between languages, they remain resilient to shifts in language dominance, supporting the shared-syntax hypothesis
17
. Conversely, ungrammatical transfer appears to require a more entrenched L1 structure to successfully "override" L2 processing.

The asymmetry of CLI has been discussed extensively in psycholinguistic research, with evidence often showing that priming with the more dominant L1 language leads to larger priming effects than priming with the L2 language
21
;
38
;
31
. While prior work in NLP found no inherent asymmetry in LMs, suggesting instead that such effects may be due to specific crosslinguistic differences rather than L1/L2 status
4
, our findings offer a more nuanced view. We demonstrate that the asymmetry of priming effects is fundamentally constrained by syntactic overlap. Even when English is the target language, priming effects only manifest bidirectionally across syntactically similar pairs, indicating that language status and linguistic distance interact to modulate the strength of CLI.

:
CLI effects on the L1 language before and after priming with the L2. Before priming, the difference across languages is almost negligible, however, priming leads to only positive effects on similar languages.

5.3
Smaller LMs Show Weaker Crosslinguistic Priming Effect

We further investigate the role of model scale in mediating CLI effects. Smaller models, due to their limited parameter count, are often hypothesized to share more representational capacity across languages, potentially amplifying CLI. To test this, we ablate model size by training "Small" and "Tiny" variants of GPT-2 with an age of exposure of 32K. As shown in Figure
9
, smaller models exhibit a more distinct relationship between syntactic distance and CLI before priming compared to the base model. However, the effect of priming is inconsistent with syntactic distance, especially for the "Tiny" model, where all languages regardless of their distance lead to negative effects. These findings suggest that while smaller models may better capture CLI effects due to forced parameter sharing, they lack the necessary capacity to respond to crosslinguistic priming.

6
Discussion

L1 Dominance and L2 Proficiency

Consistent with human SLA research, L1 dominance significantly modulates CLI and crosslinguistic priming in LMs. Our results demonstrate that varying the age of exposure effectively manipulates language dominance: increased L1 entrenchment correlates with stronger CLI on BLiMP (§
4.1
) and more pronounced mechanistic signals in LogitLens (§
4.3.1
) and neuron overlap (§
4.3.2
). However, a trade-off exists: as the age of exposure increases, L2 proficiency declines, eventually attenuating priming effects (Figure
2
). Notably, syntactically similar languages like German and Spanish exhibit persistent priming effects even at Step=48K. This suggests that shared syntactic features are acquired more rapidly or resiliently in the L2, allowing facilitation to manifest in similar languages while distant ones remain inhibited by low proficiency.

Word Order Overlap

In human psycholinguistics, the necessity of overlapping word order for the development of shared syntax remains a subject of active debate. Some studies found priming effects in the absence of word order overlap
15
;
35
, while others have not
6
;
22
. Our results in language models indicate that while word order impacts the magnitude of CLI, it is not a strict necessity for its occurrence. Specifically, we observe that priming with languages that diverge in word order still significantly affects English. However, priming effects are most stable in languages sharing a dominant word order with English (e.g., German, Spanish, and Greek), particularly in our L1-English ablation (§
5.2
). This suggests that word order overlap is not a binary prerequisite for CLI, though syntactic alignment substantially reinforces the strength and stability of crosslinguistic influence.

Shared vs Connected Account

The debate between "shared" and "separate-but-connected" syntax remains central to understanding the bilingual mind. Proponents of the shared-syntax hypothesis argue that a single underlying representation governs equivalent structures across languages, citing the existence of crosslinguistic priming as primary evidence
17
;
16
. Conversely, observed asymmetries—where CLI is significantly stronger from the dominant L1 to the non-dominant L2—suggest that syntactic structures may be separate but connected by varying degrees of associative strength
38
;
37
.

Our findings provide a potential resolution to this dichotomy by demonstrating that both accounts may be true depending on the structural context. Our crosslinguistic priming results indicate that shared structures (i.e., those that are grammatically acceptable in both languages) indeed exhibit bidirectional priming. However, the priming of non-shared structures is markedly asymmetrical; these effects are only robust when moving from the dominant L1 to the L2. This suggests that while core grammatical commonalities are processed through shared representations, the transfer of language-specific or ungrammatical structures is constrained by language dominance and entrenchment, favoring the connected account for non-overlapping structures.

7
Conclusion

While fundamental differences exist between how LMs and humans acquire language, the emergence of human-like linguistic capabilities in LMs trained on unstructured data suggests that these models can serve as informative "artificial learners" for psycholinguistic theory. In this work, we utilized bilingual LMs to simulate and analyze crosslinguistic influence (CLI) through a systematic ablation of language dominance and proficiency. We provide behavioral evidence showing that L1 dominance and L2 proficiency significantly modulate CLI and crosslinguistic priming, alongside mechanistic evidence demonstrating how the L1 physically affects L2 hidden states and neuron recruitment.

Central to the debate on whether CLI stems from shared representations, our findings suggest a hybrid account: structures common to both languages appear to be shared, as evidenced by bidirectional facilitation. However, the observed non-bidirectionality of priming for distant languages suggests that non-overlapping structures remain separate-but-connected. Ultimately, this work highlights the utility of LMs in bridging the gap between behavioral patterns and internal mechanisms, providing a level of granular control and interpretability that is currently unattainable in human subjects.
