Determinants of HIV, Hepatitis B, and Hepatitis C Infections Among Preoperative Patients in a Tertiary Hospital in the Eastern Democratic Republic of Congo
Authors: Tony Akilimali Shindano, Pacifique Mufungizi Cinamula, Fabrice Gulimwentuga Cikomola, David Mwenebitu Lupande
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHuman immunodeficiency virus (HIV), hepatitis B virus (HBV), and hepatitis C virus (HCV) are three of the most common occupational infections due to blood-borne infectious diseases. Surgeons are particularly at risk, especially in countries with high endemicity, such as the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC). Assessing the burden of these viruses among preoperative patients could help evaluate the level of risk.MethodsThis was a retrospective cross-sectional study carried out in the departments of Surgery and Gynecology of a tertiary care hospital in Bukavu, in eastern DRC. Standard enzyme immunoassays were used to determine the serology for HIV (anti-HIV Ab), HBV (HBsAg), and HCV (anti-HCV Ab) as a part of the routine minimum preoperative assessment. The frequency and risk factors of positive tests were evaluated, as well as factors for non-performance of these tests.ResultsOf patients who underwent surgery (2092), only 1871 performed at least one serological test (89.4%). A positive HIV serology test was observed in 39/1861 patients (2.1%), while 69/1870 and 43/1871 patients had reactivity to HBsAg (3.37%) and anti-HCV (2.29%), respectively. The female/male ratio showed statistically significant differences for these three viruses, respectively. Among the risk factors studied, age and male gender appear to influence HBsAg and anti-HCV reactivity, whereas a history of transfusion or tattooing was found to be statistically significant in patients with positive HBsAg serology, and previous surgery has been more commonly observed in cases of anti-HCV reactivity.ConclusionThe rate of HIV, HBV, and HCV infection in this patient population was similar to that of the national level, although higher rates are observed among some subgroups. In any case, surgeons should maintain systematic universal protective measures since the cost-effectiveness of screening based on the presence of risk factors has not yet been clearly demonstrated.

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), hepatitis B (HBV), and hepatitis C (HCV) infections represent three of the most widespread viral infections worldwide. These three viruses share broadly the same transmission routes (blood, percutaneous, sexual, and mother-to-child) and are associated with significant morbidity and mortality. Despite the recent decline in the global prevalence of HIV infection due to the decrease of new infections, it nevertheless remains a real health problem in affected populations. Globally, 40.8 million people were living with HIV in 2024, and around 630,000 of them died from HIV-related causes.1 On the other hand, according to World Health Organization (WHO) estimates for 2022, approximately 254 and 50 million people were living with chronic hepatitis B and hepatitis C infection, respectively.2,3 In addition to their high frequency, these two viral diseases are also the leading causes of liver diseases.4

Surgeons are particularly at risk of these infections from needlestick injuries, with a reported rate of 2–10 injuries/100 procedures.5–7 The risk of infection following a needlestick injury varies widely depending on the microorganism, with a rate of approximately 0.3, 3, and 30% for HIV, HCV, and HBV, respectively.8,9 For this reason, some medical institutions have introduced a preoperative screening test for HIV and hepatitis for medical safety.

This risk is even greater in some countries, such as the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), classified as one of the highly endemic regions of these viruses and, above all, which has a high level of surgical activity due to the chronic war that is raging there. For safe practice, identifying seropositive patients is therefore important in this context, especially if they are asymptomatic or non-self-reported patients.

Although this population group is not among those particularly at risk, determining the frequency of these infections could be useful in enabling safety precautions in the operating room for prevention of spread. These measures are crucial, especially since recent data from the region noticed a low level of awareness and prevention of blood-borne diseases.10–12

This study aimed to evaluate the burden of anti-HIV antibodies, hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) and anti-HCV positive identified during the preoperative screening, as well as determinants for the disease.

Methodology

Study Design and Participants

This was a hospital-based retrospective cross-sectional study carried out in a tertiary care hospital in Bukavu, one of the largest towns in eastern DRC. We used a consecutive sampling of all patients undergoing elective or emergency surgery regardless of age, gender indication, and nature of operations over a 6-month period, from January 1 to July 1, 2023. The participants came from the departments of Surgery and Gynecology. Data were collected on the patients’ clinical history and from the laboratory. During the study period, standard enzyme immunoassays were used to determine the serology for HIV antibody (Determine® HIV-1/2, Abbott Laboratories, USA) and Uni-Gold™ HIV Rapid Test (Trinity Biotech plc, Bray, Ireland), HBV antigen (Determine® and ELISA Dialab® HBsAg Sensitive, Eastleigh, United Kingdom), and HCV antibody (Dialab® HCV Ab Sensitive ELISA Kit, Eastleigh, United Kingdom). These serological tests are part of the routine minimum preoperative assessment at the “Hopital Provincial General de Reference de Bukavu.”

Study Endpoints

The primary endpoint of the study was the number adults tested for HIV, HBV and HCV infections during pre-operative assessment as well as the frequency of positive tests.

Secondary endpoints included the proportion and factors for non-performance of these tests as well as some risk factors for the presence of positive serology.

Statistical Analysis and Ethics

Descriptive statistics of socio-demographic variables were computed and presented as proportions, means, or medians. For age, patients were categorized based on five age groups, including groups <20 years, between 21–30 years, 31–40 years, 41–50 years, and >50 years. Surgical procedures have been classified as major and minor. A major surgical procedure has been defined as one that may present technical difficulties, a risk of bleeding, or performed on a patient in a precarious condition or with specific characteristics (ie: neurosurgery, lung surgery, tumor removal, bowel resection,). It should be noted that the classification of procedure was determined through chart review or clinical judgment without formal validation.

The findings data were analyzed using the SPSS 20.0 software for Windows (SPSS Inc. Chicago, IL, USA). To compare means and medians, we used Student’s t-test and the Mann–Whitney test, respectively. To compare proportions, Pearson’s chi-square test or Fischer’s exact test were used, depending on the context. Statistical significance was set at a p-value < 0.05. To prevent type I errors that may occur in analyses involving multiple tests, the significance threshold was adjusted using the Bonferroni correction method.

Our study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration. For all seropositive patients, surgeries were done under safety protocol and they were regularly referred to an infectiologist or hepatologist for confirmation and further management, as appropriate. This procedure is a part of the routine clinical practice protocol in place in our institution prior to the study.

Of these surgical procedures, 85.9% were major surgeries, and 46.4% were performed on an emergency basis. The mean age of patients was 35.6±13 years, and 58.9% of them were male. Discussion

Although recent data show that the epidemic of the three viruses appears to be declining in the DRC, they remain public health problems. The DRC is currently considered a country of intermediate endemicity for HBV and HCV infections, with estimated prevalence rates around 5% and 3%, respectively.2,13–15 The HIV rate is around 0.7%.16,17

A comparison of prevalence rates shows higher rates of HBV and HCV infections in the Surgery Department of whereas HIV appears to be more common in the Gynecology Department (Table 2). This could be related to the clinical and demographic differences in the two Departments and, consequently, to the underlying risk factors. Furthermore, the higher HIV prevalence within the gynecology population may also be related to a possible selection bias, as high-risk patients are typically referred to major specialist centers for better monitoring. This could result in an over-representation of patients who test positive for HIV.

Practicing the art of healing in such an environment exposes healthcare professionals to a potential risk of transmission of these viruses from infected patients. Therefore, screening for HIV, HBV, and HCV as a part of routine preoperative investigations has become common practice in many institutions, although there are no formal recommendations. Mastery of one’s working environment can enable appropriate precautions during surgery but also ensures the use of adequate personal protective equipment. That is why this screening has become virtually systematic before any surgical procedure performed in our institution. In accordance with current legislation in the DRC, only HIV testing is subject to informed consent. However, the data from our study shows that this screening was not carried out systematically. It appears that it is more frequently performed in cases of major surgery, emergency, weekend interventions, or when the primary surgeon is a specialist (p<0.05). Some situations, such as major or emergency surgery, explain this systematic screening because they pose the greatest risk due to their complexity. Conversely, assistants request these evaluations less often when they are at the forefront of the procedure, either to minimize risks or simply because they are most often assigned to minor procedures. However, universal measures for the prevention of blood-borne diseases should remain systematic, given that prevalence rates for the three viruses remain quite high.

Although the study population is not homogeneous, the profile observed for these three viruses appears to mirror that of the general population, with average endemicity for hepatitis B and C and a feminization of HIV infection.16 However, the prevalence of the later infection appears to be higher than the latest national data, especially in the gynecology department. Although they are not particularly at risk, this population has been pre-selected because of their active pathology and also because of their extensive medical and surgical history (15.8% have a history of previous surgery, and 36.4% have had a transfusion). The classic risk factors are found in large numbers among our patients, highlighting the need to strengthen preventive measures. These include systematic screening of blood products, systematic vaccination of at-risk groups, safer surgical practices, and some socio-cultural practices such as ritual scarification. Our data seem to show that screening in this patient group could also be a means of detecting asymptomatic patients, enabling early treatment and prevention of complications. This is all the more important given that we are in the era of direct-acting antivirals (against HCV) and highly effective antiretroviral treatment of HIV infection, where the “test-to-treat” strategy is encouraged. In relation to hepatitis B, it could be one of the means of achieving the goal of its elimination by 2030.18,19 On the other hand, the risk of percutaneous injuries could be limited due to the additional measures taken for contaminated patients, especially since previous data from the region showed that occupational accidents related to blood products were very numerous. Unfortunately, screening is often only requested in cases of jaundice, proven liver disease, or any other situation suggestive of possible immunosuppression. Nevertheless, it remains important to maintain systematic universal protective measures despite negative test results, as some patients may be in the serological window period or have occult infections, especially since the test used is only serologically based.

The major question is whether this systematic screening is cost-effective. Contradictory results were found in several studies depending on their design or the country of study.20–23 Our study cannot formally confirm this either, especially since in the DRC, patients largely have to pay for the costs of care out of their own pocket. Nevertheless, this activity could be a key factor in detecting new patients as part of the “test and treat” strategy recommended for the three viruses.18,24–26

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the data pertain only to HBsAg, while information on anti-HBc, anti-HBe antibodies, and viral load was unavailable. This limitation prevented a more detailed characterization of HBV infections. Second, the findings related to HCV are based solely on serological results. Moreover, as the data were collected from a single hospital, this may have introduced selection bias and limits the generalizability of the findings. On the other hand, although this could theoretically be corrected by the Bonferroni method, it remains, however, possible that some significant findings may represent chance observations because of multiple comparisons. Finally, the reasons why preoperative screening was not performed in some patients were not documented in the medical records. It is therefore unclear whether this was due to a deliberate refusal by the patients or to missing or incomplete information in their medical files.

Conclusion

The risk of acquiring HIV, hepatitis B, or hepatitis C infection during surgical procedures is not negligible. Systematic preoperative screening for these viruses reveals an overall seroprevalence comparable to that of the general population, although higher rates are observed among some high-risk subgroups. Such screening is essential not only in the clinical practice of surgeons but also may facilitate identification of previously undiagnosed infections and create opportunities for improving therapeutic coverage of infected individuals. Nevertheless, the cost-effectiveness of systematic screening compared with targeted approaches focusing on high-risk groups has yet to be clearly established. It would be interesting to assess, at a later stage, the benefits achieved in relation to the number of newly treated patients, as well as the long-term clinical benefits.
