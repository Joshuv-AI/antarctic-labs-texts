Utilization and associated factors of second dose measles vaccine among mothers having a child less than two years old in Enderta District, South Eastern Tigray, Ethiopia
Authors: Rigbe Teweldebrhan Gebrekidan, Abreham Habtemariam Weldu, Shishay Wahdey Teklemedhin, Haftamu Ebuy Teka, Haftom Gebrehiwot Misgna, Gebretsadik Kiros Lema, Mussie Alemayehu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to assess the utilization and associated factors of second-dose measles vaccine among mothers with children less than two years old in Enderta district, South Eastern Tigray.MethodsA mixed community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in Enderta district, South Eastern Tigray from January- to March 2020. The sample size of this study was 410 mothers, 10 in-depth interviews, and 2 focus group discussions. Multistage sampling technique for the quantitative data and purposive sampling for the qualitative data was used. Quantitative data were collected by the interviewer-administered questionnaires and entered and analyzed using Epi-data-3.1 and SPSS-20, respectively. Binary logistic regression analysis was done and adjusted odds ratios measured the strength of statistical association at 95% confidence interval. Variables with a P-value < 0.2 in the bivariate analysis were entered into multivariable analysis statistical significance was declared at P-value < 0.05. Thematic analysis was employed for the qualitative data. After the qualitative data was coded, themes were developed. The result of the qualitative data was presented and discussed by triangulating.ResultUtilization of second dose measles-containing vaccine was 32.4%. Top reasons for not vaccinating second dose measles-containing vaccines to their children were: lack of awareness about the necessity of the vaccine, missing the appointment date, mother being too busy and absence of vaccine supply. In the multivariable analysis; children aged 18–23 months (AOR 0.5; CI (0.3–0.8)), number of children greater than five per family (AOR 2.3; CI (1.3–4.3)), type of health facility (AOR 1.7; CI (1.05–2.9)), children with a history of completed basic vaccines at 12 months (AOR 2.6; CI (1.5–4.3)) and knowledgeable mothers (AOR 1.67; CI (1.04–2.7)) were significantly associated with the taking of second dose measles vaccine of their last child.ConclusionDespite the slight improvement from the regional and national Ethiopian Demographic Health Survey reports, utilization of second dose measles vaccine in this study was low.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06436-4.Keywords: Measles containing vaccine, Children less than two years old, Associated factors, Tigray, Ethiopia

Background

Measles remains a significant global health concern, resulting in an estimated 207,500 deaths worldwide in 2019, with over 70% occurring in African countries, disproportionately impacting low-resource settings [1]. The highly contagious measles virus, a paramyxovirus, spreads via respiratory droplets [2].

Despite the availability of a live attenuated vaccine since 1963, global measles burden persists, exacerbated by vaccine hesitancy and suboptimal immunization coverage, leading to outbreaks even in developed nations [3]. While measles mortality has declined significantly since the introduction of the vaccine, it remains a deadly disease, particularly in developing countries [4]. Achieving and maintaining high measles vaccination coverage is crucial for preventing outbreaks. Measles incidence and transmission are effectively prevented by vaccinating at least 95% of the population with two doses of measles vaccine, establishing herd immunity and protecting even those who cannot be immunized [5, 6]. Suboptimal vaccination coverage continues to pose a significant public health challenge, leading to outbreaks and highlighting the need for sustained efforts to reach and maintain the required immunization threshold [1].

In Ethiopia, measles is endemic, with recurring outbreaks [7]. Although measles infections have decreased substantially due to improved government preparedness and response to outbreaks, including measles supplementary immunization activities (SIAs), such as the one conducted in 2017, sub-national outbreaks continue to occur [7].

In April 2017, the second-dose measles vaccine introduction policy was updated, recommending that countries integrate the vaccine into their national immunization programs within 6 to 12 months of introducing any new vaccine, regardless of existing measles-containing vaccine first dose (MCV1) coverage [8].

WHO guidelines recommend routine administration of the second measles vaccine dose at 15 to 18 months of age in settings with ongoing measles transmission and MCV1 administration at 9 months, with a minimum one-month interval between MCV1 and the second dose [9]. Routine administration of the second dose in the second year of life reduces the risk of outbreaks and the accumulation of susceptible children [9].

A multi-year plan aimed for 90% MCV2 coverage by 2020 [10]. However, the 2019 Ethiopia Mini Demographic and Health Survey (EMDHS) reported only 9% MCV2 coverage nationally, with higher coverage in the Tigray region (17%), both far below the 90% target [11]. This falls significantly short of both the planned target and the average African coverage of 23% [7, 11]. The aim of this study was to assess MCV2 utilization and identify factors influencing uptake in the study area.

Methods

Study area, period, and population

The study was conducted in Enderta district, one of the districts in South Eastern Zone of Tigray Regional State. Enderta district is located in the surrounding area of Mekelle Zone and it is composed of 17 Kebelles. The total population of the district was around 92,573; in which 22.3% of them were reproductive age group mothers. In addition to that there were around 5300 under two years’ children. Moreover, 7 health centers, 11 health posts and 33 Health Extension Workers (HEWs) were providing health service for the population of the district (Tigray Regional Health Bureau: Enderta woreda maternal and child health demographic report, unpublished). The study was conducted from January 2020 - March 2020.

Study design and participants

A mixed community based cross-sectional study design was conducted to answer the objective of the study. Exploratory sequential approach was used to collect the data. First, the qualitative data was collected which had a 25% weight. Second, the quantitative data was collected by considering a 75% weight of the study. The source populations for the quantitative data were all mothers having a child less than two years old and living in Enderta district.

Sample size calculation

The sample size for the qualitative data was decided by information saturation and accordingly two focus group discussions (FGDs) and 10 in depth interview (IDI) were collected. To determine the sample size for quantitative part of this particular study, a single proportion sample size formula was used by considering the following assumptions; 95% confidence level 5% marginal error, 17% proportion of MCV2 utilization from Tigray according to EMDHS [9, 11]. Sample size for the first objective (n) was 217, to calculate the sample size for the second objective P was used from a Kenyan study [12]. So, the sample size was 248, this sample size was greater than the sample size calculated for the first objective and it was taken as a final sample size. Since, two steps were used to select the participants, the sample size was supposed to be multiplied by a design effect of 1.5 which provided a sample size (n) of 372. Finally, a thirty eight (10%) of non-response rate was considered and gives a total sample size (n) of 410. For the qualitative data, IDI and FGD members were selected purposively. Individual FGDs were composed of 6 mothers. The members of the IDIs were; HEW (Vaccinators) and immunization focal persons. Whereas for the quantitative data, Enderta district was selected purposely then, a multistage sampling technique was used to approach the study participants. Enderta district had a total of 17 Kebelles, in which eight (8) of them were selected randomly to participate in this study. In addition to that, each Kebelles were made up of four (4) Villages in which two Villages from each previously selected Kebelles were included by lottery method. The total sample size was allocated proportionally to the size of the children in each Village. Finally, participants were selected by simple random sampling from the family folders of the respective Villages. Enderta Woreda was selected purposely. Then, a multistage sampling technique was used to approach the study participants. The total sample size was allocated proportionally to the size of the children in each Kushets. Finally, participants were selected by simple random sampling from the family folders of the respective Kushets (Fig. 1).

Study variables

Utilization of second dose measles vaccine is the dependent variable: it’s a dichotomous variable as Yes/No.

Socio-economic and demographic variables, health service variables, maternal and reproductive variables, and knowledge about second dose measles vaccine were the predictor variables.

Data collection tools, procedures, and quality control

The purpose of the qualitative data was to explore the feelings and opinions of the participants about second dose measles vaccine and to incorporate in the tool development for quantitative data. When we assess the utilization of MCV2, we checked their vaccination card/any health related card to document their MCV2 status. But, for those children who lost their vaccination card we asked their mothers/care-givers to recall the child’s MCV2 status.

FGDs and IDIs were conducted by a two trained data collectors and semi-structured interview questionnaires were used to collect the data by radio recorder and notebooks. The principal investigator was facilitated the FGD and IDI. The tools for the qualitative data were composed of issues related to vaccination status of the setting, knowledge of mothers about second dose measles vaccine, source of information, maternal and health professional related barriers to second dose measles vaccine, and utilization of second dose measles vaccine.

For the IDI, three immunization focal persons and seven vaccinators were selected purposely, and then a 30 min interview was conducted separately, while two homogenous groups of 6 mothers were assigned together and discussed on the issues for about 30–45 min. The data collectors were recorded the discussion and took important points to their notebook.

A primary data was collected by interviewer administered structured questionnaires prepared by reviewing different literatures [12–14]. This study was incorporated mainly issues related to socio-demographic data, health service factors, knowledge about second dose measles vaccine, maternal factors and utilization of second dose measles vaccine. Data were collected by 10 BSc holder junior nurses and two senior nurses were supervised the data collection process. The survey was conducted from January 2020 to March 2020.

To maintain consistency in the quality of qualitative data collected, the semi structure questionnaires were first prepared in English language, and then it was translated to Tigrigna language and back to English to check the effectiveness of the questionnaires. Questionnaires were pre-tested in 5% of the sample size in Gratsatsi Village, Debrekibei Kebelle, and a local area with a population having similar socio-demographic status and who was not included in the main study. After the questionnaires were pretested, correction of questionnaires was conducted. Collected data were edited and cleaned on a daily basis. Supervisors (2 senior Nurses) were followed for the correct data collection. The principal investigator was taken corrective measures for missing values, irregularities, and inconsistencies timely; to ensure data quality at each data collector level. Two days training was provided for interviewers, data collectors and supervisors.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis was employed for the qualitative data. The main ideas of the discussion were extracted from audio records and written documents. Main ideas were organized and thematic areas were developed. The IDI themes have been labeled as; “vaccination status of the setting”, “health professionals’ source of information to update about second dose measles vaccine”, “maternal and health Professionals’ behavior and barriers for second dose measles vaccine”. In addition to that, the FGD themes were labeled as; “purpose of second dose measles vaccine”, “challenges in vaccinating second dose measles vaccine”, “status of second dose measles vaccine” and “improving status of second dose measles vaccine”. The result of the qualitative data was presented and discussed by triangulating with the quantitative data.

The data were entered to Epi-data 3.1 and analyzed using SPSS version 20. Data were summarized by using tables and figures. Variance inflation factors (VIF) were examined to test for potential multi- collinearity among independent variables. The VIF ranged from 1.1 to 1.79, which were below the cut-off 5.00 and implied no significant problem related to multi-collinearity. Binary logistic regression analysis was done and the strength of statistical association was measured by adjusted odds ratios at 95% confidence interval. Variables with P < 0.2 in the bivariate analysis were entered in to multivariable analysis and the statistical significance was declared at P < 0.05. Result of descriptive study was presented in text, tables and charts.

Results

Results of qualitative data

In this study 10 health professionals in depth interview and 2 focus group discussions among mothers were conducted. The IDI and FGD were transcribed, coded, categorized and themed. The thematic analysis process which was applied to the transcript produced key concepts that were apparent in the data. These themes are noticed as essential in determining the understandings of all the participants.

The IDI themes have been labeled as; “Vaccination Status of the Setting”, “Maternal and Health Professionals’ Behavior and Barriers for MCV2”. In addition to that, the FGD themes were labeled as; “Purpose of MCV2”, “Community Perception about MCV2”, “Challenges in Vaccinating MCV2”, “Status of MCV2” and “Improving Status of MCV2”.

Vaccination status of the setting

This theme was defined as the vaccination service in each settings or health posts and health centers. When they asked about the vaccination service in their setting, all participants were expressed that the general vaccination of their setting was good, while the MCV2 vaccination is not good so far. One of the participants commented her experience of vaccination service as follows;

“I stayed in this health facility for about 4 years and the general vaccination service is good so far, however, the vaccination service was not good for Measles containing vaccine second dose. It may be because of the service was started recently just before 1 years.” [39 years old HEW, orthodox].

Maternal and health professionals’ behavior and barriers for MCV2-

Participants supported that most mothers do not have knowledge about Measles Containing Vaccine second dose regardless of their readiness and the training delivered them by health professionals at any opportunistic time. Moreover the interviewees declared that mothers have many challenges that hinder to vaccinate their child such as; traditional reasons (‘’kolo’’), fear of adverse effects, inconvenient date, missing of the appointment date, long distance to vaccination center and changing the appointment date by the facility. This was verbalized by one of the health professionals as follows;

“Most of the time mothers were faced with challenges like; change in appointment date due to the Vial does not opened for less than 10 children, mothers do not have adequate knowledge about the MCV2, missing their appointment date and fear of side effects. These reasons were obstacles not to vaccinating their child for MCV2” [35 years old Male, Orthodox].

This was also stated by one of the FGD participants as follows; “…When we are trying to vaccinate our children many problems were facing us like; transportation problem, vial not opened for few children, social issues make us to forget it (funeral, religious holidays), work burden at home, fear of vaccine side effect, missing the appointment date, lack of knowledge about the presence of the vaccine and ignorance about vaccination for mothers…” [40 years old, Orthodox follower mother].

Even though the health professionals as well as the community do not have any rumors or doubts about the MCV2, many challenges were available at the health facility. These challenges were; shortage of MCV2 supply, limited man power, vaccine vial not opened for few children and lack of training for HCW. One of the IDI participants stated that; “…As a health professional who is assigned as a vaccinator I faced common challenges like; vaccine vial not opened for few children and lack of training about the Vaccine. When MCV2 was introduced no one was trained me on how to vaccinate, even though, health professionals were supposed to be trained if new vaccine is introduced…” [33 years old, orthodox follower].

Purpose of MCV2 vaccination

This theme is defined by the participant’s ability to understand why their children should be vaccinated for MCV2. Majority of the participants in both FGD groups responded that the reasons of MCV2 vaccination were to prevent disease. One of the members of the FGD supported the purpose of MCV2 as follows; “…As to my understanding the reason for vaccinating MCV2 to our children is to avoid new measles infections after the first vaccination. Repeated vaccination prevents measles…” [38 years old mother, orthodox follower].

Knowledge about MCV2

This theme is about the participant’s awareness about MCV2 and their sources of information. The majority of the participants were not having any information about MCV2. One of the FGD participants stated that “…In my opinion, mothers might not have knowledge about MCV2, may be due to the vaccine was new. No one was informed the mothers about MCV2…” [30 years old, female, orthodox follower].

Community perception about MCV2

In this theme the participants assured that the community’s perception about MCV2 is positive. This was supported by their verbalization as follows; “…Our community’s perception on MCV2 is good because many children were saved from death due to the disease and it prevents the rashes (Shihir)that occur due to the disease…” [37 years old, mother, orthodox follower].

Coverage of MCV2 and the way forward

The participants reflected their understanding on the coverage of MCV2 in their setting. Majority of them reported that the coverage of MCV2 was “low”. Moreover, there were many mothers who did not know about the presence of the vaccine. This was articulated by one of the FGD members as follows; “I do not think this vaccination is started. No one vaccinated his/her children for MCV2 so far as to my knowledge.”[32 years old mother, Orthodox].

The interviewees strongly recommended that the MCV2 coverage should be improved by; supplying adequate MCV2, health education to mothers and WDA about MCV2, increasing man power in the vaccination center, capacity building for vaccinators, and changing the behavior of the community. One of the participants in the IDI recommended that “…To improve the coverage of MCV2 measures should be taken both in the facility and in the community. The following interventions should be done; adequate and continuous supply of vaccine, adequate number of vaccinator, awareness creation to the community about MCV2…” [35 years old, immunization focal person, orthodox follower].

Result of quantitative study

Moreover, the mean age of the children was 18.9 (±:2.7 SD) months. 85%, 351 (85.6%) of the mothers were from rural areas of the District. Out of the total study subjects; 384 (93.7%) were married. All study participants were followers of orthodox Christianity. About 90% of the participants were household wives. Among the husbands of the participants, 303 (78.9%) were farmers. Regarding the educational status 305 (74.4%) mothers were not educated and 319 (83.1%) of their husbands were not attended formal education. The average numbers of children per participants were 3.45(SD ±:1.8) (Table 1). In addition to that, 404 (98.5%) participants had a health facility near to their residence with a mean travel of 30 min.

Second dose measles vaccine utilization

Almost all participants were vaccinated their last child for OPV3, DPT3 and PCV3. In spite of the high utilization of these three vaccines, the uptake of vitamin A and history of completion for all basic vaccines at 12 months was 88.3% and 68.5% respectively (Table 3).

A health center was the nearest health facility of MCV service for about 228 (56.4%) of the participants. Immunization card was available in about 384 (93.7%) participants during the study, while the others were checked by the verbalization of the mothers and tracing back to their respective health facility registration.

About 394 (96.1%) children were vaccinated for MCV1 at nine months, though only 133 (32.4%) of them were vaccinated for MCV2 (Table 4). The low utilization of MCV2 was supported by both the IDI and FGD reports. It may be because of the service was started recently just before 1 years.” [39 years old HEW, orthodox]. The FGD were also supported this by saying “I do not think this vaccination is started. In the bivariate analysis; Place of residence, number ANC visit, place of delivery, age of last children, number of children, type of nearest health facility, distance to a nearest health facility, completion of basic vaccines at 12 month and knowledge of mothers about second dose measles vaccine were associated with the outcome variable.

However, in the multivariable analysis only; age of last children, number of children, type of nearest health facility, basic vaccines completed at age of 12 months and knowledge of mothers about the vaccine were statistically associated with the utilization of second dose measles vaccine.

Children aged 12–17 months were 50% less likely to vaccinate for MCV2 compared to younger children (AOR = 0.5, 95% CI: 0.3–0.8). Moreover, higher numbers of children per family were 2.3 more likely to be vaccinated for MCV2 compared to less number of children per family (AOR = 2.3, 95% CI: 1.3, 4.3).

Mothers who had a health post as their nearest type of health facility were 70% higher likelihood of second dose measles vaccination for their children compared to mothers with a health center near to them (AOR = 1.7, 95% CI:1.05, 2.9).

Children who completed all basic vaccines at 12 months were 2.6 more likely to be vaccinated for second dose measles vaccine compared to those who did not completed basic vaccines at 12 months (AOR = 2.6, 95% CI:1.5, 4.3). In addition to that, knowledgeable mothers about MCV2 were 67% higher likelihood of vaccinating their children for second dose measles vaccine compared to those who were not knowledgeable (AOR = 1.67, 95% CI:1.04, 2.7).

Discussion

This study was tried to assess the utilization of second dose measles vaccine and associated factors among mothers with less than 24 months children. The utilization of the second dose measles vaccine was 32.4%, 95%,CI(28–36.4.4) while children aged 12–17 months, number of children greater than five per family, type of nearest health facility, children with a history of completed basic vaccines at 12 months and knowledgeable mothers were statistically associated with the taking of second dose measles vaccine of their last child.

In this study the utilization of second dose measles vaccine was 32.4%,95% CI(28–36.4.4), which was higher than from a similar studies conducted in; Tigray (17%), Ethiopia (9%), Africa (26%), Ghana (23.9%) and Kenya (17.9%) [11, 12, 15]. There are different reasons for these variations. The reason for the higher utilization of second dose measles vaccine in this study compared to the EDHS/2019 report in Tigray and Ethiopia may be because of the earlier survey after the initiation of second dose measles vaccine at nationwide, while this study was conducted after more than one years of second dose measles vaccine inauguration. The others reasons for the higher utilization in this study may be evidenced by the efforts made to mobilize the community by the health extension program which is not practiced in Africa, Ghana and Kenya. This may implicate that as the proportion of second dose measles vaccinated children increases their susceptibility to measles virus would decrease [16].

Moreover, the utilization of second dose measles vaccine in this study was lower than from the findings of a study conducted in; America (82%), South East Asia (80%), Europe (91%), Eastern Mediterranean (74%) and Western Pacific (91%) [8]. The fact of these differences may be due to many of the studies conducted in the developed world were with a better supply of vaccines and child health policies. This finding was supported by the finding of the IDI and FGD. Both health professionals and mothers were reported that the utilization of second dose measles vaccine was low in the district, which is consistent with the quantitative data finding. In addition to that, the utilization of second dose measles vaccine was lower in this study compared to the findings of; Brazil (75.1%), China (93.9%), Pakistan (45%), Sudan (77.4%), Ghana (70%) and Tanzania (44.2%) [7, 9, 13, 15, 17–19]. The reasons may be the time for the introduction of second dose measles vaccine was earlier on these literatures which helped them to develop experiences and their developmental status, especially in China and Brazil. Another reason for the difference was the knowledge of mothers about second dose measles vaccine which was 47.8% in this study while, it was about 96% in Ghana. This may imply that many children may become susceptible for measles outbreak compared to countries with a higher proportion of measles vaccination [16].

The most common reasons for not vaccinating second dose measles vaccine among mothers were lack of awareness about the need of immunization, missing the appointment date, mother to busy, vaccine not available, ignorance of the use of vaccination, family problem, including illness of mother and vial not opened for small number of children. This in turn supported the FGD and IDI of the qualitative study and it is almost similar to the findings of a study conducted in Ghana and Kenya [12, 20]. The similarities may be due to the similar socio-demographic and economic status of the participants. The qualitative study also supported that the common reasons for the low utilization of second dose measles vaccine were; social reasons, mothers work burden, lack of transportation to the vaccination center, fear of vaccine side effect, missing the appointment date, lack of knowledge, ignorance about vaccination, shortage of vaccine supply, vial not opened for few children and absence of vaccinator [1].

One of the IDIs participant were supported that mothers had many challenges that hinder to vaccinate MCV2 their child by saying “….Most of the time mothers were faced with challenges like; change in appointment date due to the Vial does not opened for less than 10 children, mothers do not have adequate knowledge about the MCV2, missing their appointment date and fear of side effects. These reasons were obstacles in vaccinating their child for MCV2” [35 years old Male, Orthodox].

A 40 years old and orthodox follower mother from the FGD participants supported the main challenges faced to mothers in vaccinating MCV2 for their children by saying “…When we are trying to vaccinate our children many problems were facing us like; transportation problem, vial not opened for few children, social issues make us to forget it (funeral, religious holidays), work burden at home, fear of vaccine side effect, missing the appointment date, lack of knowledge about the presence of the vaccine and ignorance about vaccination for mothers”.

Knowledge about the vaccination was associated with second dose measles vaccine utilization which is consistent to the findings of study conducted in Kenya [12]. Mothers who knew about second dose measles vaccine were 67% more likely to vaccinate their children. This may be justified by the value of knowledge in improving the vaccination status of children. This implies relevant bodies should invest on improving the knowledge of mothers about second dose measles vaccine for a better utilization [5].

The history of complete vaccination at first year was significantly associated with the utilization of second dose measles vaccine among the study participants. This finding is congruent with result of a study conducted in Kenya [12]. This may be justified by the similar socio economic status of the participants as well as the same awareness or level of knowledge about vaccination. The impact of a competed history of basic vaccines at 12 months implied that higher utilization of second dose vaccine can be achieved [12].

In this study higher numbers of children were directly related to the utilization of second dose measles vaccine. This was consistent with finding of a study conducted in Austria [21]. This may be justified by the fact that mothers with a higher number of children could get experiences about the vaccination by vaccinating their older children [5].

In a study conducted in china; children delivered at home, younger mothers, low maternal education background, mothers with a fixed job, and low household income were associated with the delayed vaccination for the second dose of measles vaccine. However, none of these factors had an association with the utilization of second dose measles vaccine in this study. This may be determined by the reasons of; low home delivery, few younger mothers and few mothers with fixed jobs in this study setting [17].

Even though no literatures shown an association between the ages of the children and utilization of second dose measles vaccine, in this study age of the child was significantly associated with the utilization of second dose measles vaccine. This may be due to the mothers perception of older children may not need to be vaccinated more. In addition to that mothers may forget to return for additional vaccine as they stay longer after their last vaccination schedule. This finding implied that delivering second dose measles vaccine as early as possible after the first year of life would be helpful for improving the utilization [5].

In addition to that, mothers with a health post with a nearest facility were 70% more likely to vaccinate their children compared to the health center as a nearest health facility. However, no studies had statistical significance with the type of nearest health facility. This may be due to health post were easily accessible to mothers and maternal and child health service centered in it.

Limitation

This study may not reveal a cause and effect relationship since it was cross-sectional study design. Moreover, recall bias, selection bias and lack of generalizability especially for the urban areas were the limitations of the study.

Conclusion and recommendation

Despite the slight improvement from the regional and national EDHS report, the utilization of measles containing vaccine second dose in this study was low. In the final model; children aged 12–17 months, number of children greater than five per family, type health facility, children with a history of completed basic vaccines at 12 month and knowledgeable mothers were significantly associated with the taking of second dose measles vaccine of their last child. The top reasons for mothers that make them not to vaccinate their children were; lack of awareness about the necessity of the vaccine, missing of the appointment date, mother to busy and absence of second dose measles vaccine supply.

Tigray Regional Health Bureau should supply the second dose measles vaccine adequately and continuously. Moreover, the Enderta health district center has a duty to work on mothers how to get sufficient health education about second dose measles vaccine. Mothers should get a reminder about the vaccination schedule (may be by mobile call- to minimize the appointment date missing) through HEWs (Vaccinators) in Enderta district. Finally, a wider study must be conducted in all the other Districts in Tigray Region and also longitudinal study should be conducted to address the cause and effect.
