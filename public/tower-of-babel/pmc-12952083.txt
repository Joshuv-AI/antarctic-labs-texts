Targeting SERPINB3–MAPK axis-mediated cuproptosis resistance enhances the response to antitumor immunotherapy
Authors: Xiao-Xiao Huang, Cheng-Ke Xie, Yi-Chao Mo, Wei Li, Yong-Ding Wu, Zhi-Yuan Li, Hao-Xiang Zhang, Ge Li, Long Jin, Xin-Quan Lin, Jian-Fei Hu, Yin-Hao Chen, Hong-Yi Lin, Shun-Cang Zhu, Jin-Peng Lu, Hou-Juan Zhu, Wan-Wan Wang, Yi Huang, Zu-Wei Wang, Long Huang, Dan-Feng Wang, Yi-Feng Tian, Cheng-Yu Liao, Shi Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cuproptosis, a newly identified form of cell death, is closely linked to copper homeostasis and protein lipoylation. Using a multi-omics approach, we firstly reveal that SERPINB3 confers cuproptosis resistance in pancreatic cancer and functions as a theranostic biomarker. Mechanistically, SERPINB3 inhibits FDX1 transcription by activating the MAPK signaling pathway, thereby conferring cuproptosis resistance. We further demonstrated that SERPINB3 directly interacts with MEK1, impeding its chaperone-mediated autophagic degradation, which ultimately leads to sustained activation of the MAPK signaling pathway. Additionally, we found that SERPINB3 promotes pancreatic cancer immune evasion by upregulating PD-L1 expression on tumor cells. This phenomenon motivated the development of a triple-combination strategy consisting of MAPK inhibition, cuproptosis induction, and αPD-1 therapy for pancreatic cancer patients with high SERPINB3 expression. To this end, we further developed a metal-organic framework (MOF) loaded with both copper ions and MEK inhibitor, which significantly triggers tumor-specific cuproptosis and enhances antitumor immunity. In summary, SERPINB3 serves as a predictive biomarker to inform therapeutic strategies targeting cuproptosis, thereby establishing a novel paradigm for cancer immunotherapy that integrates metal biology, targeted therapy, and immune modulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12943-025-02529-x.

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a highly aggressive cancer with a poor prognosis and a 5-year survival rate of only 13% [1]. Existing chemotherapy and contemporary immune checkpoint therapies show limited success in treating advanced pancreatic cancer [2–7], highlighting the urgent need for innovative treatment strategies. In recent years, targeting tumor cell death resistance mechanisms has emerged as a novel therapeutic approach, representing a groundbreaking direction in pancreatic cancer research. Our previous work focused on cell death modalities, such as ferroptosis and necroptosis, and the development of multi-nanotherapeutic anticancer systems [8–11]. Cuproptosis, a form of cell death, is activated through the direct binding of overloaded intracellular copper ions to lipoylated proteins in the tricarboxylic acid cycle. In turn, this leads to their abnormal oligomerization and subsequent loss of iron-sulfur cluster proteins, ultimately triggering cell death [12]. However, the mechanisms underlying cuproptosis initiation and its downstream effects in tumors, particularly in PDAC, remain unclear.

Increasing intracellular copper levels is considered the most straightforward strategy to trigger cuproptosis. This concept has been experimentally validated in preclinical studies, such as gastric cancer cell models [13]. In this study, we found that pancreatic cancer cells contained markedly higher intracellular copper levels compared with non-tumor cells, indicative of a potential “copper-accumulated” phenotype. Clinical cohort analyses demonstrated that while PDAC cells exhibited increased susceptibility to cuproptosis, PDAC cells with higher copper concentration paradoxically developed higher aggressive features and enhanced cuproptosis resistance. The mechanism underlying this resistance remains elusive, and deciphering this key process is expected to provide novel therapeutic strategies for pancreatic cancer.

The MAPK signaling pathway, a critical pro-tumorigenic network that is frequently upregulated in pancreatic cancer [14], has been reported to be activated by copper ions through MEK1 stimulation [15–19]. We hypothesize that pancreatic cancer cells utilize the MAPK signaling pathway to develop cuproptosis resistance and facilitate tumor progression. Thus, targeting MAPK signaling could help restore cuproptosis sensitivity and suppress tumor progression.

Ferredoxin 1 (FDX1) is known to reduce Cu²⁺ to its more toxic form, Cu⁺, while regulating the lipoylation of dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT) [12], and downregulation of FDX1 can confer resistance to cuproptosis. In this study, we identified a highly conserved and potent cysteine protease inhibitor, SERPINB3, as a critical regulator of FDX1 in PDAC cells, which was correlated with downregulation of FDX1 and strongly linked to the MAPK signaling pathway. We hypothesize that SERPINB3 may be involved in cuproptosis resistance through the MAPK signaling pathway. Furthermore, because cell death is frequently associated with remodeling of the immune microenvironment, we hypothesize that inducing cuproptosis in pancreatic cancer cells may enhance responsiveness to immunotherapy, particularly to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 monoclonal antibodies.

In this study, we demonstrate that SERPINB3 mediates tumor progression through two molecular mechanisms: (1) sustained MAPK signaling pathway activation through the inhibition of chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA)-dependent MEK1 degradation, leading to the transcriptional suppression of FDX1; and (2) upregulation of PD-L1 expression to facilitate immune evasion. To address these therapeutic challenges, we developed a novel nanocrystalline cuproptosis inducer and combined it with MAPK pathway inhibitors and PD-1 immune checkpoint blockade. This combinatorial approach demonstrated potent and synergistic antitumor efficacy in SERPINB3-high PDAC models by simultaneously triggering cuproptosis, suppressing oncogenic MAPK signaling, and reshaping the tumor immune landscape to support immune-mediated tumor clearance.

Results

Pancreatic cancer cells exhibit cuproptosis resistance

Cuproptosis is triggered by intracellular copper overload. To understand its role in pancreatic cancer, we first analyzed the intracellular copper concentrations in 43 pairs of PDAC tissues and their adjacent normal tissues. Compared with that in normal pancreatic tissue, the copper concentrations in PDAC tissues were significantly elevated (Fig. 1A-B). To further confirm this, we used magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) on 10 freshly collected PDAC tumor samples to isolate EpCAM+ tumor cells and nontumor cells (Fig. 1C). Even within the tumor microenvironment, the intracellular copper ion concentration in tumor cells was significantly higher than that in nontumor cells (Fig. 1D), which implies the higher copper accumulation and suggests an increased susceptibility to cuproptosis. This observation supports the feasibility of inducing cuproptosis in PDAC cells through copper elevation. Consistently, in vitro cell viability assays demonstrated that treatment with the copper ionophore elesclomol-Cu (ES-Cu) preferentially induced cell death in PDAC cells over normal pancreatic epithelial cells. This effect was effectively reversed by the copper chelator tetrathiomolybdate (TTM), confirming the copper-dependence of the observed cell death (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Furthermore, we quantified the cuproptosis propensity in PDAC tissues by measuring the levels of lipoylated proteins and FDX1, which have been reported to be hallmarks of cuproptosis execution [12, 13, 20]. Compared with normal pancreatic tissue, PDAC tissues presented significantly higher levels of lipoylated proteins and FDX1 (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Fig. S1B), indicating an enhanced propensity for cuproptosis in malignant cells.

Based on the observed copper enrichment in PDAC cells, we next investigated whether this “copper-accumulated” state, along with the increased propensity for cuproptosis, was associated with patient prognosis. Specifically, we sought to determine whether tumors with higher copper dependence and cuproptotic potential might be associated with better clinical outcomes. Our results demonstrated that copper content was positively correlated with tumor American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) stage, tumor differentiation, lymph node metastasis, and distant metastasis (Fig. 1F-G, Supplementary Fig. S1C-S1D). Additionally, the copper content was negatively correlated with overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) in patients with PDAC (Fig. 1H), indicating that the copper content is correlated with tumor progression. The unexpected results lead us to suspect that even though PDAC cells are more prone to cuproptosis than non-tumor cells, PDAC cells may possess intrinsic resistance mechanisms to evade copper-induced cell death or create an immunosuppressive microenvironment, thereby facilitating tumor progression.

To investigate these hypotheses, we evaluated the expression levels of lipoylated proteins and FDX1 in PDAC tumors at different stages. We found that as the tumor stage increased, lipoylated proteins and FDX1 expression gradually decreased, suggesting that susceptibility to cuproptosis may diminish with advancing tumor stage (Fig. 1I-J). Furthermore, we constructed patient-derived organoids (PDOs) from the tumor tissues of 32 PDAC patients and evaluated their sensitivity to cuproptosis using live/dead staining after ES-Cu treatment (Fig. 1K). We found significantly elevated IC50 values for ES-Cu in PDAC with high AJCC stages (Fig. 1L), consistent with the development of cuproptosis resistance in advanced tumors. Next, we treated PDOs with a stage I IC50 concentration of ES-Cu for validation. At this concentration, ES-Cu failed to induce cuproptosis in stage III/IV PDAC (Supplementary Fig. S1E-S1F). Furthermore, we found that cuproptosis resistance was associated with poor tumor differentiation, distant metastasis, and reduced DFS (Supplementary Fig. S1G-S1K), further strengthening the link between cuproptosis resistance and advanced PDAC.

Next, we investigated whether further escalation of ES-Cu concentration could overcome resistance and effectively trigger cuproptosis in stage III/IV PDAC cells. While increasing the dose did enhance cytotoxicity in these advanced tumor cells, excessively high concentrations also produced toxic effects in normal pancreatic epithelial cells (Supplementary Fig. S1L), raising concerns about therapeutic selectivity. Taken together, these findings suggest that although PDAC cells display elevated copper accumulation and an intrinsically higher propensity for cuproptosis, resistance mechanisms in advanced disease may limit the efficacy of simply increasing copper levels. Surprisingly, advanced-stage PDAC displayed increased resistance to cuproptosis. Further investigation into tumor heterogeneity will be required to clarify the mechanisms underlying this resistance and to help the development of potential effective therapeutic strategies.

SERPINB3 acts as a key regulatory factor of cuproptosis resistance despite copper ion accumulation in PDAC cells

To further elucidate the mechanisms by which pancreatic cancer cells develop resistance to cuproptosis despite copper ion accumulation, we examined the clinical and transcriptomic data of PDAC patients from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Using a curated set of cuproptosis-related genes (CRGs: FDX1, LIAS, LIPT1, DLD, DLAT, DLST, PDHA1, PDHB, MTF1, GLS, CDKN2A, ATP7A, ATP7B, SLC31A1, DBT, and GCSH) [12, 21], we performed unsupervised clustering on the TCGA-PDAC dataset, which revealed three distinct patient subgroups characterized by differing cuproptosis risk scores (Supplementary Fig. S2A-S2B). Subgroup 1 (high-risk) and subgroup 3 (low-risk) patients showed significant survival differences (Supplementary Fig. S2C), and we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between these two groups (Supplementary Fig. S2D). Additionally, we used the LASSO regression algorithm to establish a novel risk model for pancreatic cancer patients (Supplementary Fig. S2E). This model effectively stratified them into high-risk and low-risk groups with significantly different prognoses (Supplementary Fig. S2F-S2G), allowing the identification of survival-related DEGs between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. S2H). To further substantiate the risk model for PDAC patient stratification introduced in our study, we performed additional validation using other public databases, which confirmed its robustness (Supplementary Fig. S2I-S2L). Furthermore, we analyzed cancer-related DEGs between PDAC and normal pancreatic tissues in four pairs of samples from the TCGA database (Supplementary Fig. S2M). Finally, based on live/dead staining results from patient-derived organoids (PDOs), we categorized eight pancreatic cancer patients, who underwent single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) at our center, into cuproptosis-sensitive and cuproptosis-resistant groups. We then identified DEGs associated with cuproptosis sensitivity between the tumor cells of these two groups (Supplementary Fig. S2N). Cross-analysis of the four gene sets revealed SERPINB3 as one of the overlapping candidate genes (Supplementary Fig. S2O), suggesting its role as a key mediator of cuproptosis resistance in pancreatic cancer cells.

Experimental validation via immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunoblotting confirmed the higher expression of SERPINB3 in PDAC tissues compared with normal tissues (Fig. 1M, Supplementary Fig. S3A-S3B). In our internal PDAC cohort, elevated SERPINB3 expression was significantly correlated with clinicopathological indicators of tumor aggressiveness, including tumor size, lymph node metastasis, distant metastasis, and advanced AJCC stage (Fig. 1M-N, Supplementary Fig. S3C), and reduced OS and DFS (Supplementary Fig. S3D-S3E), which is consistent with cuproptosis resistance (Fig. 1L, Supplementary Fig. S1G-S1K). Conversely, SERPINB3 is upregulated in cuproptosis-resistant PDAC. We found that while treated PDOs with a stage I IC50 concentration of ES-Cu, the susceptibility to cuproptosis in PDAC cells was negatively correlated with SERPINB3 expression (Supplementary Fig. S3F-S3G). These results suggest that high SERPINB3 expression may be a key factor underlying cuproptosis resistance and tumor progression in PDAC.

Further investigations were performed to explore whether an immunosuppressive microenvironment forms in cuproptosis-resistant PDAC. Immune profiling showed reduced lymphocyte infiltration in SERPINB3-high cuproptosis-resistant tumors, particularly effector T cells (Supplementary Fig. S3H-S3I), alongside upregulated CD274 (PD-L1) expression (Supplementary Fig. S3J). These findings indicate that high SERPINB3 expression may also induce an immunosuppressive microenvironment.

Overall, although pancreatic cancers typically present increased copper accumulation and an intrinsic susceptibility to cuproptosis, aggressive subtypes develop increased resistance to cuproptosis through a mechanism independent of intracellular copper levels. This resistance is directly linked to elevated expression of SERPINB3, which functions as a biomarker for both cuproptosis resistance and tumor aggressiveness.

SERPINB3 confers cuproptosis resistance and promotes tumor progression in pancreatic cancer cells

To elucidate the role of SERPINB3 in PDAC cuproptosis resistance, we examined the protein and mRNA expression levels of SERPINB3 in PDAC cell lines and human pancreatic ductal epithelial cells (Supplementary Fig. S4A-S4B). In addition, we successfully established ASPC-1-OE-SERPINB3 (SERPINB3-overexpressing) clones and BXPC-3-sh-SERPINB3 (SERPINB3 knockdown) clones (Supplementary Fig. S4C-S4F). Cell viability assays revealed that SERPINB3 knockdown significantly increased sensitivity to ES-Cu-induced cell death, which was reversed by TTM treatment. Conversely, SERPINB3 overexpression markedly enhanced resistance (Fig. 2A-C, Fig. S4G-S4I), suggesting that SERPINB3 inhibits the cuproptosis pathway. In vivo studies using a subcutaneous xenograft model in nude mice further validated that overexpression of SERPINB3 confers resistance to cuproptosis (Fig. 2D).

Given the central role of protein lipoylation and oligomerization in cuproptosis, we used a lipoic acid-specific antibody to detect the degree of DLAT lipoylation and confocal immunofluorescence to analyze ES-Cu-induced lipoylated protein oligomer formation. Western blotting (WB) and immunofluorescence analyses revealed that while SERPINB3 knockdown did not affect the basal expression of DLAT, it significantly increased the levels of lipoylated proteins and promoted ES-Cu-induced DLAT oligomer formation, while in contrast, SERPINB3 overexpression induced opposite effects (Fig. 2E-F). These findings confirm that SERPINB3 mediates cuproptosis resistance by modulation of the DLAT lipoylation pathway.

Notably, functional assays revealed that in the absence of ES-Cu, SERPINB3 overexpression directly promoted PDAC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, whereas SERPINB3 knockdown suppressed these malignant phenotypes (Fig. 2G-I). In vivo experiments suggested that SERPINB3 overexpression significantly increased the incidence of liver metastasis (Fig. 2J). These findings suggest that SERPINB3 has multiple roles, including promoting resistance to cuproptosis and malignant behavior in tumor cells. This makes SERPINB3 a promising target for developing novel therapeutic strategies to trigger cuproptosis in pancreatic cancer cells.

SERPINB3 confers resistance to cuproptosis by reducing FDX1 expression

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying SERPINB3-mediated cuproptosis resistance, we investigated its impact on intracellular copper homeostasis. SERPINB3 did not influence intracellular copper ion levels or alter the concentration of glutathione (GSH), an endogenous copper chelator known to suppress cuproptosis by lowering intracellular copper content (Fig. 3A-B). Clinical data analysis further confirmed the absence of correlation between SERPINB3 expression and the copper ion concentration (Supplementary Fig. S4J-S4K), suggesting that SERPINB3 suppresses cuproptosis independently of copper homeostasis regulation.

Next, we explored the relationships between SERPINB3 and upstream regulators of cuproptosis. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that SERPINB3 overexpression specifically suppressed FDX1 transcription, while its knockdown increased FDX1 expression, without affecting expression levels of LIAS, DLAT, DLST, or LIPT1 (Fig. 3C). These findings were confirmed at the protein level as both IHC and WB consistently revealed an inverse correlation between SERPINB3 and FDX1 expression (Fig. 3D-E).

We further evaluated whether SERPINB3 mediates cuproptosis resistance through FDX1 via rescue experiments. WB and confocal immunofluorescence assays demonstrated that FDX1 knockdown reversed the SERPINB3 depletion-induced increase in DLAT lipoylation and oligomer formation (Fig. 3F-H). Functional investigations, including CCK-8 assays and 3D spheroid models, further confirmed that FDX1 knockdown rescued the enhanced sensitivity to ES-Cu caused by SERPINB3 downregulation (Fig. 3I-J). However, the results of the rescue assays confirmed that SERPINB3 does not regulate tumor proliferation, invasion, and migration through the FDX1 pathway (Fig. 3K-P, Supplementary Fig. S4L-S4N). Together, these results identify FDX1 as a key downstream target of SERPINB3, specifically mediating its role in cuproptosis resistance, but not contributing to its effects on tumor progression.

To determine whether SERPINB3 regulates FDX1 expression and cuproptosis resistance through MAPK/ERK pathway activation, we observed that SERPINB3 did not affect total ERK protein levels in tissue samples but was positively correlated with ERK phosphorylation (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Fig. S5C-S5D). In pancreatic cancer cells without ES-Cu treatment, SERPINB3 overexpression upregulated the MAPK signaling pathway (as evidenced by increased p-ERK levels) and downregulated FDX1, effects reversible by MEK inhibitor (MEKi) treatment (Fig. 4E, Supplementary Fig. S5E). Accordingly, under ES-Cu-induced cuproptosis conditions, treatment with MEKi effectively reversed SERPINB3-mediated resistance, as evidenced by restored DLAT oligomerization, enhanced cell death (as shown by CCK-8 assays and 3D tumor spheroid live/dead staining), and reduced colony formation (Fig. 4E-J).

Considering the role of SERPINB3 in promoting tumor proliferation and metastasis, and resistance to cuproptosis, we hypothesized that its protumorigenic effects might also be driven by activation of the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway. In the absence of ES-Cu, MEKi treatment markedly abolished the enhanced proliferation and metastatic capacity of SERPINB3-overexpressing cells, as shown by colony formation, 3D proliferation, migration, and invasion assays (Fig. 4J, Supplementary Fig. S5F-S5H). These findings establish the MAPK/ERK pathway as a central mediator of SERPINB3-driven cuproptosis resistance and tumor progression and metastasis.

SERPINB3-mediated activation of the MAPK signaling pathway inhibits FDX1 transcription

We demonstrated the critical role of the MAPK signaling pathway in SERPINB3-mediated suppression of FDX1 RNA levels (Supplementary Fig. S5E). To further investigate this mechanism, actinomycin D chase assays were performed and revealed that SERPINB3 does not affect FDX1 mRNA stability (Fig. 5A-B), indicating transcriptional regulation via MAPK/ERK signaling. Through screening of ERK1/2-associated downstream transcription factors, we identified FOXO3 as a key regulator, whose knockdown led to a reduction in both FDX1 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 5C-D). Functional investigations further revealed that FOXO3 depletion rescued the increased sensitivity to ES-Cu treatment caused by SERPINB3 downregulation (Supplementary Fig. S6A-S6C) and attenuated the SERPINB3 knockdown-induced accumulation of lipoylated DLAT and DLAT oligomers (Fig. 5E-G). These findings identify FOXO3 as the key transcription factor through which SERPINB3 regulates FDX1 expression, thereby contributing to cuproptosis resistance.

Dual-luciferase reporter assays confirmed that FOXO3 increases FDX1 promoter activity to upregulate transcription (Fig. 5H). To identify FOXO3-binding regions, we designed 10 primer pairs covering all potential binding sites within the FDX1 promoter and conducted chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) analysis (Fig. 5I-J, Supplementary Fig. S6D). Based on JASPAR database predictions and ChIP assay results, we constructed mutant versions of the FDX1 promoter (Supplementary Fig. S6E). Luciferase assays using these constructs showed that mutation of the − 1215 to −1222 binding region significantly reduced FOXO3-mediated promoter activation (Fig. 5K).

Since MAPK/ERK activation promotes FOXO3 nuclear exclusion and ubiquitin-mediated degradation [22], we investigated the impact of SERPINB3-mediated MAPK/ERK activation on FOXO3. IHC and WB analyses demonstrated an inverse correlation between SERPINB3 and FOXO3 protein levels (Fig. 5L-N). WB and immunofluorescence further confirmed that SERPINB3 knockdown increased FOXO3 nuclear accumulation, whereas its overexpression reduced FOXO3 nuclear localization, an effect reversed by MEK inhibition (Fig. 5O-P, Supplementary Fig. S6F-S6H). Collectively, these data demonstrate that SERPINB3 activates the MAPK/ERK pathway to reduce nuclear FOXO3 availability, thereby suppressing FDX1 transcription and driving cuproptosis resistance.

These effects were abolished by MEK1 inhibition (Fig. 6B), indicating that SERPINB3 promotes MAPK signaling pathway activation via MEK1 upregulation.

Since SERPINB3 does not alter MEK1 mRNA levels, we investigated its effect on MEK1 protein regulation by assessing protein stability (Fig. 6C-D). The results showed that SERPINB3 significantly inhibited MEK1 protein degradation (Fig. 6E). In eukaryotic cells, the ubiquitin‒proteasome system and the autophagy‒lysosome pathway are the primary protein control systems. Using pathway-specific inhibitors, we demonstrated that both the lysosomal inhibitor bafilomycin A1 (BAF) and the proteasome inhibitor MG-132 blocked MEK1 degradation (Fig. 6F). However, only BAF was able to prevent the increase in MEK1 degradation induced by SERPINB3 knockdown (Fig. 6G). Consistently, immunofluorescence demonstrated that SERPINB3 overexpression suppressed MEK1 lysosomal localization, whereas SERPINB3 knockdown promoted it (Fig. 6H, Supplementary Fig. S7A), indicating that SERPINB3 stabilizes MEK1 primarily by suppressing lysosomal degradation rather than the ubiquitin‒proteasome system. Moreover, in ATG5-deficient BXPC-3 cells, where macroautophagy is impaired, SERPINB3 knockdown still promoted MEK1 degradation (Fig. 6I), confirming that SERPINB3 regulates MEK1 degradation through the lysosomal pathway rather than via proteasomal or macroautophagic mechanisms.

We next analyzed the other lysosomal degradation mode, CMA, a selective, lysosome-dependent pathway that targets proteins containing a KFERQ-like motif, recognized by the 70-kDa heat shock cognate protein (HSC70). The HSC70-substrate complex undergoes functional interaction with lysosome-associated membrane protein 2 A (LAMP2A), a critical receptor that facilitates substrate translocation into the lysosomal compartment for subsequent degradation [23–26]. Analysis of human MEK1 amino acid sequences revealed three canonical KFERQ-like motifs (Supplementary Fig. S7B), suggesting that MEK1 is a potential CMA substrate. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), mass spectrometry (MS), and proximity ligation assay (PLA) confirmed that MEK1 interacts with HSC70 (Fig. 6J and M, Supplementary Fig. S7C). Knockdown of HSC70 or LAMP2A rescued SERPINB3 knockdown-induced MEK1 degradation (Fig. 6K-L). Next, we investigated the impacts of SERPINB3 on the CMA-mediated MEK1 degradation process. The MS, Co-IP, and colocalization assays revealed that SERPINB3 binds to MEK1 (Supplementary Fig. S7D-S7G). SERPINB3 overexpression suppressed MEK1–HSC70 interaction, whereas SERPINB3 knockdown promoted this interaction (Fig. 6M-N). These data demonstrate that SERPINB3 inhibits CMA-mediated MEK1 degradation by disrupting MEK1–HSC70 binding, thereby stabilizing MEK1, activating the MAPK signaling pathway, which explains the SERPINB3-mediated cuproptosis resistance and tumor progression in pancreatic cancer.

Additionally, we established 6 PDOs from PDAC patients with differential SERPINB3 expression levels. The low SERPINB3 expression (SERPINB3low) group exhibited increased sensitivity to cuproptosis, with ES-Cu alone effectively inducing cancer cell death. In contrast, the high SERPINB3 expression (SERPINB3hi) group presented marked resistance to cuproptosis, which was reversed by MEKi treatment (Fig. 7B and D). We further established a subcutaneous xenograft model in nude mice and showed that SERPINB3 overexpression enhanced tumor growth and conferred resistance to ES-Cu treatment. However, MEKi therapy effectively suppressed both SERPINB3-induced tumor progression and its associated resistance to cuproptosis (Fig. 7E and G). In summary, these data establish MEKi as a strategy to restore cuproptosis sensitivity in SERPINB3hi pancreatic cancer.

A novel nanomaterial carrier effectively delivers copper ions and MEKis to orthotopic pancreatic tumors to suppress their growth

Interestingly, in a SERPINB3-overexpressing orthotopic nude mouse pancreatic cancer model, the combination of MEKi and ES-Cu failed to achieve the expected therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 7F and H). This observation aligns with prior studies showing that ES-Cu’s limited tumor tissue penetration, due to its short plasma half-life and apparent volume of distribution, restricts its bioavailability [27–32]. Similarly, while ES-Cu effectively elevated intracellular copper levels in vitro and in subcutaneous tumors, its impact on copper concentrations in orthotopic pancreatic tumors was minimal (Fig. 7I).

To overcome this limitation, we leveraged metal-organic framework (MOF) nanoparticles, an optimal drug delivery platform known for high payload capacity, stability, and biocompatibility. MOFs can be surface modified for targeted delivery and programmed to release drugs in acidic microenvironments [33–37]. We designed an MOF-based system to deliver both MEKi and copper ions. Briefly, the MEKi was mixed with Zn²⁺ and 2-methylimidazole to synthesize MOF-MEKi (MOF-M). MOF-M was then sequentially coordinated with Cu²⁺ and S²⁻ to form copper-integrated MOF-MEKi-CuSx (MOF-M-CuSx) (Fig. 7J). For comparative analysis, controls, including MOF-CuSx, were synthesized using analogous methods. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and dynamic light scattering (DLS) confirmed that MOF-M and MOF-M-CuSx were ~ 182 nm in size (Supplementary Fig. S8B-S8C). A UV‒vis-NIR spectrophotometer was used to examine the absorption spectra of MOF-M and MOF-M-CuSx (Supplementary Fig. S8D). The ζ potential values were + 13.0 mV and + 20.0 mV for MOF-M and MOF-M-CuSx, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S8E).

Next, we evaluated MOF-M-CuSx biodistribution in orthotopic tumor-bearing mice. Fluorescence/bioluminescence imaging 48 h post-tail vein injection revealed significant tumor accumulation of MOF and MOF-M, with MOF-M-CuSx showing enhanced uptake in orthotopic tumors due to the copper-avid phenotype of pancreatic cancer (Supplementary Fig. S8F-S8G). Copper concentration assays confirmed that compared to ES-Cu treatment, MOF-CuSx treatment led to elevated intracellular copper levels in orthotopic tumor models, indicating the improved performance of this treatment modality (Fig. 7I). As hypothesized, dual delivery of MEKi and copper via MOF-M-CuSx effectively induced cuproptosis in SERPINB3-overexpressing orthotopic tumors, significantly suppressing tumor progression (Fig. 7K) with no detectable toxicity to major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, brain) (Supplementary Fig. S8H).

In summary, the poor efficacy of elesclomol in orthotopic pancreatic tumors was overcome with an MOF-based nanoplatform for delivering both copper ions and MEKi. This strategy achieved potent tumor suppression in SERPINB3-high orthotopic models while demonstrating favorable safety profiles.

SERPINB3 induces immunosuppression by upregulating the expression of PD-L1 in tumor cells

To elucidate the association between SERPINB3 and the immunosuppressive microenvironment, we explored the relationship between SERPINB3 and immune cell infiltration here. We performed RNA sequencing analysis on the ASPC-1-EV and ASPC-1-OE-SERPINB3 cells to assess immune checkpoint ligand expression. The results revealed that PD-L1 (CD274) expression was significantly elevated upon SERPINB3 overexpression (Fig. 8A). WB and mIFS further confirmed that SERPINB3 expression was positively correlated with PD-L1 expression (Fig. 8B-E) and negatively correlated with CD8+ T-cell infiltration (Fig. 8C, D and F). External scRNA-seq data from pancreatic cancer patients also revealed that the SERPINB3hi group exhibited markedly increased CD274 (PD-L1) expression in tumor cells (Fig. 8G) and alteration of lymphoid immune cell infiltration, including decreased effector T cell infiltration and increased exhausted T cell infiltration (Supplementary Fig. S9A-S9D). These findings are consistent with our own clinical scRNA-seq datasets and confirm that SERPINB3 overexpression likely induces the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment.

It is well established that the PD-1/PD-L1 axis influences the infiltration and function of both exhausted and effector T cells. Given prior evidence that ERK promotes PD-1/PD-L1 expression through multiple pathways [38–42], we investigated whether SERPINB3 modulates tumor PD-L1 levels and T cell activity via the MAPK pathway. Our WB results demonstrated that MEKi treatment partially attenuated SERPINB3-driven PD-L1 upregulation (Fig. 8H), indicating that SERPINB3 upregulates PD-L1 via MAPK signaling, although other regulatory pathways may also be involved. This observation supports the rationale for combining PD-1 blockade with other methods. Allogeneic co-culture CD8+ T cells-mediated cytotoxicity assays revealed that SERPINB3 knockdown significantly promoted tumor cell killing by CD8+ T cells, whereas SERPINB3 overexpression suppressed this effect. MEKi treatment alone failed to fully rescue SERPINB3-driven immune suppression (Fig. 8I-J). Further analysis of interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and granzyme B (GZMB), key cytokines secreted by activated CD8+ T cells, revealed consistent trends (Fig. 8K-L).

These findings suggest that SERPINB3 facilitates immune evasion by activating MAPK signaling and through additional pathways that upregulate PD-L1. Although MEKi treatment alone was insufficient to fully reverse SERPINB3-driven immunosuppression, its combination with anti-PD-1 therapy effectively restored CD8⁺ T cell activation.

Cuproptosis induction in combination with anti-PD-1 therapy can further inhibit the progression of SERPINB3hi PDAC

Based on these findings, we propose that combining anti-PD-1 immunotherapy with MOF-M-CuSx treatment could provide synergistic therapeutic benefits for pancreatic cancer patients with high SERPINB3 expression. Using a C57BL/6 mouse orthotopic pancreatic tumor model with SERPINB3 overexpression (Fig. 9A), we observed that monotherapy with either an anti-PD-1 antibody or MOF-M-CuSx modestly inhibited tumor growth. However, combination therapy significantly increased antitumor efficacy, resulting in robust suppression of tumor progression (Fig. 9B-E).

Discussion

Changes in copper homeostasis can affect cell growth, invasiveness, and drug resistance [17, 43]. However, excessive intracellular copper can trigger cuproptosis, a process whose role and regulatory mechanisms in tumors are still not well understood [12]. Extensive research has revealed compared to normal tissues, tumor tissues exhibit a significantly increased demand for copper [44]. For instance, elevated copper levels in tumor tissues have been observed in patients with diverse cancers [45–49]. This surge in copper demand primarily stems from copper’s role as an essential component of various enzymes involved in cellular energy metabolism and antioxidant defense factors, which collectively meet the high energy demands of rapidly dividing tumor cells [50, 51]. In this study, we demonstrated that pancreatic cancer cells also exhibit a “copper-accumulated” phenotype. Compared with nontumor tissues, tumor cells exhibited greater copper accumulation and cuproptosis sensitivity, indicating that cuproptosis targeting could be a tumor-selective strategy with minimal toxicity to normal stromal/immune cells. We also identified SERPINB3 as a cuproptosis biomarker with a dual role: it sustains MAPK/ERK pathway activation by impairing CMA-dependent MEK1 degradation, thereby conferring cuproptosis resistance. Simultaneously, it promotes tumor cell proliferation and metastasis, as well as PD-L1-associated immunosuppression, making it an ideal therapeutic target.

SERPINB3, a highly conserved cysteine protease inhibitor, was previously shown to protect cells from lysosomal apoptosis by suppressing lysosomal protease activity [52, 53] and inducing antitumor immunosuppression via inflammatory mediator release [54, 55]. Here, we revealed its novel function in cuproptosis regulation: SERPINB3 shields copper-accumulated pancreatic cancer cells from copper-induced death independently of its canonical cysteine protease inhibitory function. Importantly, cuproptosis sensitivity in these cells is independent of intracellular copper levels but rather is governed by SERPINB3 expression. As a clinically established tumor biomarker, SERPINB3 offers unparalleled practicality for predicting cuproptosis sensitivity independent of copper homeostasis.

While previous studies showed copper’s ability to activate MAPK signaling, its relationship with cuproptosis remains unclear. Our work is the first to demonstrate that MAPK plays an upstream regulatory role in resisting cuproptosis. We found that FOXO3 is a key transcription factor for FDX1. SERPINB3 interferes with CMA-mediated MEK1 degradation by competitively blocking HSC70–MEK1 interactions, leading to sustained MAPK activation. This cascade causes FOXO3 to exit the nucleus and silences FDX1 transcription, creating a “molecular barrier” against cuproptosis. Importantly, without copper stress, SERPINB3–MAPK/ERK activation independently promotes tumor cell growth and spread. Therefore, combining a MEKi with a cuproptosis inducer not only makes SERPINB3hi tumors more sensitive to copper-induced cell death but also reduces MAPK-driven cancer progression, as confirmed in PDOs and subcutaneous tumor models.

Elesclomol, a copper ionophore, is a common cuproptosis inducer. However, its pharmacokinetic limitations hinder effective copper delivery in orthotopic pancreatic tumors. To address this, we engineered MOF-M-CuSx, an MOF nanoplatform that can deliver both MEKi and copper ions. This system achieved robust intratumoral copper accumulation and increased cuproptosis sensitivity in orthotopic models, significantly suppressing tumor progression with minimal systemic toxicity.

The tumor microenvironment of PDAC is characterized by limited infiltration of CD8+ T cells, thereby conferring intrinsic resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors [14, 56–58]. As cuproptosis was reported as an immunogenic cell death (ICD) [29, 30, 59–63]. We investigated the role of SERPINB3 in shaping the immune microenvironment of PDAC. We found that SERPINB3 plays roles in both cuproptosis resistance and immune evasion. Specifically, SERPINB3hi tumors presented elevated PD-L1 expression and reduced effector T-cell infiltration, mirroring clinical observations. While an increase in PD-L1 expression mediated by ERK pathway activation was partially reversed by MEK inhibition, residual PD-L1 elevation and CD8+ T-cell dysfunction persisted, implying the involvement of additional regulatory pathways. This rationale drove our triple-combination strategy (MEKi + cuproptosis inducer + anti-PD-1). In SERPINB3hi orthotopic models, MEKi overcomes cuproptosis resistance and restores CD8+ T-cell infiltration, and the addition of anti-PD-1 therapy further improved tumor control and survival by attenuating PD-L1-mediated T-cell exhaustion.

Based on these findings, SERPINB3 expression status has become a vital biomarker for guiding cuproptosis-related therapy and immunotherapy in pancreatic cancer patients. For SERPINB3low tumors, copper ionophores alone may be sufficient. For SERPINB3hi tumors, we suggest a “cuproptosis sensitization-immunotherapy awakening” strategy: MOF-based co-delivery of Cu²⁺ and a MEKi to overcome the pharmacokinetic limitations of ES-Cu, inhibit SERPINB3–MAPK signaling, restore FDX1 expression, enhance cuproptosis effectiveness, and work synergistically with PD-1 blockade to reverse immune suppression. Notably, this combination showed strong effectiveness in validation studies within orthotopic models.

Given the current suboptimal efficacy of chemotherapy for PDAC, overcoming chemoresistance in this malignancy has become a critical issue. A growing body of evidence suggests that activation of the ERK signaling pathway plays a key role in gemcitabine resistance in PDAC [64, 65]. Based on the role of SERPINB3 in activating the ERK pathway, we hypothesize that SERPINB3 expression may also serve as a biomarker for predicting chemosensitivity in PDAC. Furthermore, it would be valuable to investigate whether combining chemotherapy with our proposed “cuproptosis sensitization-immunotherapy awakening” strategy could yield improved therapeutic outcomes in PDAC with high SERPINB3 expression, which warrants further study.

However, this study has certain limitations that warrant consideration. While promising, the best cutoff value for SERPINB3 expression in predicting cuproptosis resistance needs to be further refined. Additionally, the safety, dosing, and schedule of triple-combination therapy in humans necessitate rigorous clinical evaluation.

Conclusion

This study highlights the dual function of SERPINB3 in pancreatic cancer, where it contributes both to resistance against cuproptosis and to immune evasion. These findings identify SERPINB3 as a potential predictive biomarker for determining tumor sensitivity to copper-mediated cytotoxicity. Importantly, for patients demonstrating high SERPINB3 expression, a combination therapy involving MEK inhibitors, copper ionophores, and PD-1 immune checkpoint blockade represents a promising and potentially transformative treatment strategy.

Methods

Cell lines

Human PDAC cells (ASPC-1, BXPC-3) were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and were cultured in RPMI-1640 containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotics (50 U/mL penicillin and 50 mg/L streptomycin) in a humidified atmosphere at 37 ℃ with 5% CO₂ according to the ATCC recommendations. All cell lines were tested to confirm that they were free of mycoplasma and authenticated by short-tandem repeat analysis.

Cell transfection

The human SERPINB3 lentivirus or plasmid was purchased from GeneChem (Shanghai, China). Lentiviral transfection was performed by GeneChem (Shanghai, China), and the plasmids were transfected into cells via Roche transfection reagents (Roche, Basel, Switzerland).

Culture and expansion of human pancreatic cancer organoids

Pancreatic cancer organoids were established from surgically resected PDAC tumor specimens following an optimized protocol. Briefly, fresh tumor tissues underwent sequential processing: (1) Triple-wash with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Gibco, 10010023) to remove blood contaminants; (2) Mechanical dissociation into 2–3 mm³ fragments using sterile surgical blades; (3) Enzymatic digestion employing a two-step protocol: initial incubation with MasterAim Tissue Enzyme Solution I (AIMINGMED, 100 − 050) in Advanced DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, 12634010) at 37 °C for 1 h with vortex mixing every 15 min, followed by secondary digestion with MasterAim Tissue Enzyme Solution II (AIMINGMED, 100 − 051). Digestion was stopped by adding Advanced DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS).

For organoid culture, 500 viable cells were resuspended in 20 µL growth factor-reduced Matrigel (Corning, 356231) and plated in 48-well culture plates. Followed by 5min polymerization at 37 °C. The cells were then overlaid with either basal medium (Advanced DMEM/F12, 10 mM HEPES, 1× GlutaMAX-I, 100 µg/mL primocin, 1× penicillin/streptomycin solution) or complete medium (Advanced DMEM/F12, 10 mM HEPES, 1× GlutaMAX-I, 100 µg/mL primocin, 1×penicillin/streptomycin solution, 500 nM A83-01, 10 µM Y-27632, 1.56 mM N-acetylcysteine, 10 mM nicotinamide, 10 ng/mL FGF10, 1× B27 supplement, 10 µM forskolin, 30% Wnt3A-conditioned medium, 2% R-spondin-conditioned medium, 4% Noggin-conditioned medium). Medium replacement was performed every 72 h with fresh pre-warmed solutions.

Cell sorting

Using the Tumor Dissociation Kit (Xinbio, 92-01−0127), fresh PDAC tissue samples were dissociated into single-cell suspensions according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were resuspended in buffer and passed through preseparation filters (30 μm, Miltenyi, 130-041−407) to remove cell clumps that might clog the column. Dead cells were removed via a Dead Cell Removal Kit (Miltenyi, 130-090−101) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The cell count was adjusted to 1 × 10⁷ cells. The cell pellet was resuspended in 60 µL of buffer, followed by the addition of 20 µL of FcR blocking reagent (Miltenyi, 130-059−901) and CD326 (EpCAM) microbeads (Miltenyi, 130-061−101). The mixture was thoroughly mixed and incubated at 2–8 °C for 30 min. The cells were washed by adding 1–2 mL of buffer and centrifuged at 300×g for 10 min. The supernatant was completely aspirated. The column was placed in the magnetic field of a MACS Separator. The negative control cells (nontumor cells) were collected, and EpCAM+ tumor cells were retained on the column. This separation process was repeated three times. After removing the column from the magnetic field, EpCAM+ tumor cells were eluted by flushing the column with buffer. The sorted cells were then subjected to further analysis.

Copper microplate assay

The Copper Microplate Assay Kit was purchased from Absin Bioscience (Shanghai, China, abs580140). The experiment was performed according to the user manual.

GSH assay

The intracellular GSH concentration was quantified using a commercial GSH Assay Kit (Solarbio, BC1175) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cell pellets were collected by centrifugation and homogenized in protein precipitation reagent. Cellular membranes were disrupted through three cycles of rapid freeze-thaw treatment using liquid nitrogen and a 37 °C water bath. After centrifugation at 8,000× g for 10 min to remove denatured proteins, the supernatant was collected for analysis. The reduced GSH content was determined spectrophotometrically by measuring the absorbance of reaction products at 412 nm using a microplate reader. Final GSH concentrations were normalized to total protein content measured by BCA assay and expressed as µmol/mg protein.

Live/dead staining of PDOs

PDOs were pulsed treated with DMSO or ES-Cu (ratio = 1:1) for 2 h, 48 h later, the PDOs were stained via a Calcein/PI Cell Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and imaged via an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Generation of 3D-tumor spheroids and growth assay

Tumor cell monolayers were washed twice with PBS (Gibco, 10010023), followed by the addition of a cell dissociation enzyme (Gibco, 25200072) to generate single-cell suspensions devoid of clusters. Subsequently, cell counts were performed using a hemocytometer, and the suspensions were adjusted to concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 2 × 103 cells/mL, with the optimal density determined for each specific cell line. The prepared cell suspensions were then transferred into sterile reservoirs, and a multichannel pipette was used to dispense 200 µL per well into ultra-low attachment (ULA) 96-well round-bottom plates (2000 cells per well). These plates were incubated at 37℃ with 5% CO2 and 95% humidity to facilitate spheroid formation. Spheroid size changes were monitored over three consecutive days using a high-content quantitative imaging analysis system.

Generation and live/dead staining of 3D tumor spheroids

As above, after 2 days of culture, the formation of 3D-tumor spheroids was visually confirmed prior to initiating the live/dead staining. The spheroids were treated with DMSO or ES-Cu (40nM, ratio = 1:1) for 2 h, 48 h later, the PDOs were stained via a Calcein/PI Cell Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and imaged via an inverted fluorescence microscope.

3D- tumor spheroid invasion assay

As above, after 2 days of culture, the formation of 3D-tumor spheroids was visually confirmed prior to initiating the invasion assay. Samples of the 2-day-old spheroids were transferred to ULA 96-well plates on ice. Next, 100 µL of growth medium was carefully aspirated from each well and replaced with 100 µL of basement membrane-like matrix (BMM, Matrigel, Corning), which was gently dispersed into the U-bottom wells. The plate was then incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to allow matrix solidification. Following this, 100 µL of complete growth medium was added to each well. Images were captured continuously from 0 h to 72 h.

3D-tumor spheroid migration assay

As above, after 2 days of culture, the formation of 3D-tumor spheroids was visually confirmed prior to initiating the migration assay. Subsequently, the spheroids were carefully transferred to a 96-well gelatin-precoated migration plate and incubated with complete growth medium. The spheroids were allowed to adhere to the coated surface for 60 min before image acquisition commenced at 0 h and continued up to 72 h.

Colony formation assay

Cells were plated in six-well plates (800 cells/well) and cultured for two weeks in complete culture medium. Subsequently, the cell colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. The percentage and intensity of the colony area were quantified by the ImageJ plugin ‘‘ColonyArea’’.

Immunofluorescence

Cells cultured on glass-bottom confocal dishes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 30 min at 4 °C, followed by permeabilization using 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min at room temperature. Following three PBS washes, samples were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h at 37 °C, then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After three additional PBS washes, cells were exposed to fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h in the dark at room temperature. Subsequent to final PBS washes, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (10 µg/mL) for 5 min. Fluorescent images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope.

Actinomycin D assay

The cells were treated with 2 µg/mL actinomycin D (Sigma, USA) for 0, 3, 6 h. The expression levels of FDX1 were determined via qRT-PCR analysis.

Immunoprecipitation and IP-MS analysis

Protein-protein interactions were captured using the Pierce Crosslink Magnetic IP/Co-IP Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with protocol optimization. In brief, cells were lysed in ice-cold IP lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 1 mM EDTA) supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitors. Pre-cleared lysates were incubated with antibody-conjugated magnetic beads under rotation at 4 °C for 12 h. Subsequently, bead complexes were isolated via magnetic separation and washed three times with chilled wash buffer. Specifically bound proteins were eluted with low-pH elution buffer (0.1 M glycine, pH 2.5) and neutralized with 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.5). Eluates were divided for parallel analysis: (1) WB with specific antibodies; (2) IP-MS sample preparation involving SDS-PAGE separation, in-gel tryptic digestion, and LC-MS/MS analysis.

Luciferase reporter gene assay

A 2,000-bp human FDX1 promoter fragment (−2000 to + 1 relative to TSS) was PCR-amplified from genomic DNA and cloned into a pGL3-Basic vector. For normalization, the pRL-TK vector expressing Renilla luciferase was co-transfected. Transient transfection was performed with Lipofectamine 3000 at a 3:1 ratio of firefly: Renilla constructs. 48 h post-transfection, dual luciferase activities were quantified using a dual luciferase system according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Firefly luciferase signals were normalized to Renilla values for data standardization.

CD8+ T-cell isolation and in vitro expansion

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were obtained from healthy donor blood samples via Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation (Fcmacs Biotechnology, China) following standardized protocols. CD8+ T lymphocytes were subsequently purified through negative MACS using a commercial isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany). Freshly purified CD8+ T cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 culture medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 10 ng/mL recombinant human IL-2 (PeproTech, USA) on tissue culture plates precoated with 3.5 µg/mL human anti-CD3 (BioLegend, USA) and 1 µg/mL anti-CD28 (BioLegend) antibodies. Fresh media containing IL-2 were replenished every 2 days, and the cells were maintained in culture for a period of 5 to 7 days.

CD8+ T-cell-mediated tumor cell killing assay

Pretransfected BXPC-3 or ASPC-1 cells (5 × 10⁴ cells/well) were plated in 24-well plates and pretreated with trametinib (MCE, HY-10999, 100 nM) or vehicle control (DMSO ≤ 0.1%) for 24 h. Activated CD8+ T cells (5 × 10⁵ cells/well) were cocultured with target cells at a 10:1 effector: target (E: T) ratio in complete RPMI-1640 medium. The experimental groups were supplemented with an anti-human PD-1 mAb (pembrolizumab biosimilar, Bio X Cell, BE0189, 20 µg/mL), whereas the control groups received isotype-matched human IgG4 (Bio X Cell, CP148, 20 µg/mL). After 16 h of incubation under standardized conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂), the cell culture supernatants were harvested and clarified by centrifugation (400×g, 5 min, 4 °C). Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release was measured using the LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime, C0016) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance at 490 was determined nm using a microplate reader.

PLA

According to the manufacturer’s instructions, PLA was performed with some modifications using the Duolink in situ kit. PDAC cells were harvested after 4 days of culture and then fixed with 2% formaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min at room temperature. The fixed cells were stained with mouse anti-HSC70 and rabbit anti-MEK1 antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. After staining, the cells were washed and then incubated with secondary mouse PLUS and rabbit secondary antibody MINUS for 30 min at room temperature. The cells were subsequently washed with TBS (0.01 M sodium phosphate, 0.15 M NaCl) containing 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA), followed by a 30-minute ligation reaction at 37 °C and a 100-minute amplification step at 37 °C. Cells were washed again with TBS containing 0.5% BSA. Observations were made under a confocal microscope.

Mouse experiments

Six- to eight-week-old nude and wild-type C57BL/6 mice were procured from Anbiorui Biochemistry Laboratory (Fuzhou, China). Age-matched littermates of both sexes were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at Anbiorui BD Laboratory (Fuzhou, China). The cohort sizes for individual experiments are detailed in the corresponding figure legends. All procedures strictly adhered to the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Fujian Provincial Hospital (Protocol No. IACUC-FPH-SL-20240530[0312]). Animals were prospectively randomized into experimental groups by an independent investigator, with strict segregation maintained between the treatment and control groups. Humane endpoints were defined on the basis of the following institutional ethical standards: (1) tumor burden ≤ 2.0 cm in maximal diameter; (2) impairment of essential functions (feeding, elimination, or ambulation); or (3) severe deterioration of body condition. Euthanasia was performed via cervical dislocation under anesthesia. The tumor dimensions were rigorously monitored to maintain a maximal diameter < 2 cm throughout the study period. The experimental protocols and termination criteria align with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 for preclinical studies.

For the subcutaneous model, six- to eight-week-old nude mice received a subcutaneous injection of 1 × 106 genetically modified ASPC-1 (OE-SERPINB3 or control) or BXPC-3 (sh-SERPINB3 or control) cells. Tumors were harvested at three weeks post-injection for assessment of tumor growth. The treatment methods are described in the corresponding legends.

For the hepatic metastasis model, six- to eight-week-old nude mice received intrasplenic injections of 1 × 106 genetically modified ASPC-1 (OE-SERPINB3 or control) or BXPC-3 (sh-SERPINB3 or control) cells. Metastatic progression was assessed four weeks after the procedure.

For the orthotopic tumor model, six- to eight-week-old nude or C57BL/6 mice received intrapancreatic injections of 1 × 106 OE-SERPINB3 or control luciferase-expressing KPC or ASPC-1 cells; tumor growth was evaluated by bioluminescence imaging post-implantation, and the tumors were harvested for further analysis at the above-described end points. The treatment methods are described in the corresponding legends.

Human tissues

Human PDAC tissue samples were obtained from Fujian Provincial Hospital and Fujian Medical University Union Hospital. These samples were utilized for IHC, PDOs generation, mIFS, and scRNA-seq. Technical support for mIFS was provided by Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., LTD. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Provincial Hospital (IRB No. K2024-06–027).

ScRNA-seq

Four pancreatic cancer tissue samples sensitive to cuproptosis and four pancreatic cancer tissue samples resistant to cuproptosis were collected for analysis. The tissues were enzymatically digested at 37 °C for 30 minutes in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 0.25% collagenase II and IV and 0.05% DNase I. Following digestion, the cells were counted, and 1.5 × 104 primary cancer cells from each sample were loaded onto the 10X Genomics Chromium platform. Single-cell libraries were prepared using the Single-cell 3’ v2 Reagent Kit (10X Genomics) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Gel bead-in-emulsion (GEM) generation, barcoding, GEM-RT purification, cDNA amplification, and library preparation were performed as per standard procedures. Library quantification was performed using a Qubit fluorometer, and pooled libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system with 150 bp paired-end reads. The raw sequencing data were aligned to the human reference genome (mm10-3.0.0) using CellRanger (version 3.1.0). External validation datasets were obtained from publicly available repositories (CRA001160, GSE154778, GSE155698, GSE202740, and GSE212966). Downstream analyses were conducted in R (version 4.0.3) using the Seurat package (version 2.2). Quality control filtering excluded genes expressed in fewer than two cells and retained cells expressing more than 200 genes, whose mitochondrial gene content was less than 10%. The coefficient of variation for genes was calculated using Seurat. Dimensionality reduction was performed via principal component analysis (PCA) on the top 2000 most variable genes. A k-nearest neighbor graph was constructed on the basis of Euclidean distances in the space of the first 10 principal components. Cell clustering was achieved using the Louvain modularity optimization algorithm, and the results were visualized via uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). Hemoglobin-expressing cells were excluded from further analysis. Differential gene expression analysis for each cluster was performed using the ‘bimod’ test implemented in Seurat’s FindMarkers function. Marker genes were defined as those with a log2-fold change > 0.585 and p < 0.05. The cell clusters were annotated using canonical markers from the CellMarker-2.082 database. Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment analysis was conducted, with significantly enriched terms selected on the basis of a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of ≤ 0.05. The scRNA-seq experiments were supported by Singleron Biotechnologies Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9.1.2. Data are expressed as mean ± SD throughout the study unless otherwise indicated. For comparisons between two groups, continuous variables were analyzed using two-tailed Student’s t-test for normally distributed data or Mann-Whitney U test for nonparametric distributions. Categorical variables were assessed with two-tailed Pearson’s χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on expected frequency counts. A two-way ANOVA was used for multiple comparisons, followed by post hoc analysis. Survival analyses were performed by constructing Kaplan-Meier curves, with intergroup differences evaluated using log-rank tests. A probability threshold of P < 0.05 was predefined as statistically significant for all comparisons.
