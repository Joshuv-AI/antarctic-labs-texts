Certified Decisions
Isaiah Andrews, Jiafeng Chen

Abstract
. Hypothesis tests and confidence sets are ubiquitous in empirical research, yet their connection to subsequent decision-making is often unclear. We view statistical inference as pairing recommended decisions with guarantees of their quality—or certificates —and develop a theory of certified decisions . Specifically, we attach P-certificates—upper bounds on loss that hold with probability at least 1 − α 1-\alpha —to recommended actions. We prove that it is without loss to limit attention to P-certificates generated by minimax decisions over confidence sets, or what Manski (2021) terms “as-if decisions with a set estimate.” We further show that such certificates allow safe adoption decisions for ambiguity-averse downstream decision-makers. A parallel argument applies to E-certified decisions obtained from e-values in settings with unbounded loss. We illustrate this view of statistical inference through an application to economic mobility and one to AI model routing.

Introduction

Inferential tools, such as hypothesis tests and confidence intervals, are ubiquitous in
empirical practice. At the same time, many empirical papers aim, directly or indirectly,
to improve decision-making by potential consumers of research, such as policymakers,
firms, or households. As highlighted by
Manski (2021)
, the connection
between inference and decision-making is unclear: outside of special cases,
optimal decisions in the tradition of
Wald (1949)
rarely depend on the data
through the results of conventional inference procedures.

With more structure on the decision problem, we further show that some optimality results for inference translate to optimality for P-certificates. In particular, if
the parameter is scalar and the loss function prefers high parameter values (e.g., implementing

confidence lower bounds for

Next, to connect P-certified decisions to standard decision-theoretic notions such as risk and
expected utility, we study an ambiguity-averse decision-maker with limited capacity for
data analysis who delegates that analysis to an analyst. The analyst provides the
decision-maker with a P-certified decision. The decision-maker then needs to decide whether to adopt the action recommended
by the analyst, or to default to a safe action with known
(but potentially large) loss. This decision-maker believes that the analyst’s certificate
R(Y)
contains information about the loss of the recommended action, but has limited
knowledge of the data-generating process and wishes to protect themselves against a large
increase in risk uniformly over a potentially large class of data-generating processes.

P-certificates are useful to such a decision-maker. Specifically, when losses lie in

[0,1]
and the default action costs
C\in[0,1)
, any rule
that adopts the recommended action
\delta(Y)
only when
R(Y)\leq C
yields expected loss
no greater than
C+\alpha(1-C)
, meaning that the decision-maker faces a risk increase
of at most
\alpha(1-C).
Moreover, we characterize optimal adoption decisions for
decision-makers who balance subjective and worst-case risk. We find that an optimal adoption
rule either never adopts or interpolates between the threshold rule
\mathbbm{1}(R(Y)\leq C)
and
the Bayes rule that adopts whenever subjective expected loss is below
C
.

We then illustrate how this perspective—viewing inference as providing certificates for
decisions—helps interpret empirical findings and construct recommendations in two
applications. First, we revisit Creating Moves to Opportunity

(
Bergman et al., 2024
, CMTO,)
, an experiment where housing voucher holders are
recommended putatively high-economic-mobility Census tracts. Since these Census tracts are
selected based on noisy estimates of their quality, there is concern about whether the voucher
holders were indeed induced to move to higher-quality places—especially with additional
ambiguity over their previous locations and their location choices.

Andrews et al. (2024)
and
Mogstad et al. (2024)
report inference results with
starkly different qualitative conclusions on this question:
Andrews et al. (2024)
report that targeted tracts have significantly higher average mobility than non-targeted tracts, while
Mogstad et al. (2024)
report that the tract-level mobility ranking is highly uncertain. We show that their inference
results are relevant for different decision problems, with different ambiguity sets over
movers’ relocation patterns, and introduce a family of decision problems that interpolates
between them. We then construct certified decisions for this spectrum of decision problems. This exercise quantifies how much robustness to relocation patterns can be accommodated while still certifying positive mobility gains.

Our second application considers routing queries to large language models (LLMs) using
noisy estimates of model quality. We view different inference-on-winner-type procedures

(
Zrnic and Fithian, 2025
;
Zrnic and Fithian, 2024
;
Andrews et al., 2024
;
Chernozhukov et al., 2026
)

as providing both recommended actions and resulting certificates. The tightness of the
resulting certificate can be thought of as a notion of decision-relevant power for a given
procedure, which we thus compare across procedures. We find cases in
which recommending a model with a lower estimated payoff but greater precision yields a
more favorable guarantee than certifying the model with the highest estimated payoff. This
illustrates how choosing actions in a certificate-aware way—as
Chernozhukov et al. (2026)
advocate—can be beneficial.

Finally, we extend our analysis to certified decisions that correspond to an alternative
approach to frequentist inference. A rapidly growing literature proposes

as alternatives to p-values and confidence sets
(see

such that
R(\cdot)\leq\tilde{R}(\cdot)
almost surely.
Such decisions imply risk bounds for downstream decision-makers even in contexts with unbounded loss functions. We illustrate these certificates in the LLM routing application.

Kiyani et al. (2025)

2.
P-certified decisions

Consider a statistical decision problem characterized by a loss function
L(a,\theta):\mathcal{A}\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
for an action
a\in\mathcal{A}
and an unknown
parameter
\theta\in\Theta
.
Data
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
is drawn from some distribution
P
. To model uncertainty, following

Andrews et al. (2025)
, we assume that Nature may choose any
(\theta,P)
pairs from
a set
\mathcal{M}
. For instance, if
(\theta,P)
satisfy a statistical model indexed
by
\theta
, then
\mathcal{M}=\left\{{(\theta,P_{\theta}):\theta\in\Theta}\right\}
. More generally, we allow that
\theta
may fail to fully describe
P,
for
instance due to nuisance parameters or model incompleteness. This formulation also does
not impose point-identification: we may have
\left\{{(\theta_{1},P),(\theta_{2},P)}\right\}\subset\mathcal{M}
for
\theta_{1}\neq\theta_{2}.
Throughout, we refer to
L
as a loss function, but it is possible
to choose
L(a,\theta)=\tilde{L}(a,\theta)-\inf_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\tilde{L}(a^{\prime},\theta)
so that
L
represents regret for some primitive loss function
\tilde{L}

instead.

We start by defining
P-certified decisions
and establishing some basic properties.

Definition 1

(P-certified decisions)
.

For some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the pair
(\delta(\cdot),R(\cdot)):\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{A}\times\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is called a
1-\alpha

P-certified decision
if, for
every
(\theta,P)\in\mathcal{M}
,

P\left({L(\delta(Y),\theta)\leq R(Y)}\right)\geq 1-\alpha.

When this holds,
R(Y)
is called a
(1-\alpha)

P-certificate
for
\delta(Y)
.

The guarantee (
1
) is familiar—demanding that
R(Y)
be a

(1-\alpha)
-confidence upper bound for the random quantity
L(\delta(Y),\theta)
. This
guarantee is a frequentist one: it holds ex ante over repeated draws of the data
Y
for
fixed
(\theta,P)
, uniformly over the relevant econometric model
\mathcal{M}
. A realization of
a low
R(Y)
certifies that the corresponding action
\delta(Y)
is attractive,
in the sense that either its loss is low (
L(\delta(Y),\theta)\leq R(Y)
), or something
improbable—specifically, an event with probability at most
\alpha
—has occurred.

We next study procedures that (optimally) generate P-certified decisions. We
then present a stylized delegation model in which P-certified decisions, generated by an
analyst recommending a decision, allow subsequent risk bounds for an ambiguity-averse
decision-maker who decides whether or not to accept analyst recommendations.

First, P-certified decisions are readily generated by as-if optimization with set
estimates
(
Goldfarb and Iyengar, 2003
;
Henry and Onatski, 2012
;
Manski, 2021
;
Lim and Park, 2026
)
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\hat{\Theta}(Y)
be a confidence set:
P(\hat{\Theta}(Y)\ni\theta)\geq 1-\alpha
for all
(\theta,P)\in\mathcal{M}
. For any
\delta(Y)
, let
R(Y)=\sup_{\theta\in\hat{\Theta}(Y)}L(\delta(Y),\theta)
. Then
(\delta,R)
is a
(1-\alpha)
P-certified decision. In
particular, if the minimum
\delta^{*}(Y):=\argmin_{a}\sup_{\theta\in\hat{\Theta}(Y)}L(a,\theta)

exists, then the corresponding
R^{*}(Y):=\sup_{\theta\in\hat{\Theta}(Y)}L(\delta^{*}(Y),\theta)\leq\sup_{\theta\in\hat{\Theta}(Y)}L(\delta(Y),\theta)
for any other
\delta
.
4
4

4

Throughout, the supremum over an empty set is equal to
-\infty
.

Proof.

Fix
(\theta,P)\in\mathcal{M}
.
If
\theta\in\hat{\Theta}(Y)
then
L(\delta(Y),\theta)\leq R(Y)
by definition. Thus

1-\alpha\leq P(\theta\in\hat{\Theta}(Y))\leq P\left\{{L(\delta(Y),\theta)\leq R(Y)}\right\}
and

(\delta,R)
is a P-certified decision.
For the second part, observe that
R^{*}(Y)=\min_{a}\sup_{\theta\in\hat{\Theta}(Y)}L(a,\theta)\leq\sup_{\theta\in\hat{\Theta}(Y)}L(\delta(Y),\theta)
.∎

Proposition

1
shows that the worst-case loss of any decision rule over a given confidence
set is automatically a P-certificate of that decision rule. Correspondingly, by

optimizing
this worst-case loss over a confidence set—exactly as in as-if
optimization—we obtain the tightest certificate among those constructed
this way. In

Section

2.1
, we shall see that the
converse is also
essentially true: Any reasonable P-certified decision
(\delta,R)
is (weakly) beaten by
as-if optimization using

Example 1

(Inference on winners)

We consider an example based on the recent literature on inference on winners

(
Benjamini et al., 2019

Andrews et al., 2024

Zrnic and Fithian, 2025
;
Zrnic and Fithian, 2024

Suppose we are interested in potentially implementing an action

from a finite set

For instance, we could be interested in whether to implement a program, as

Bergman et al. (2024)

a\in\mathcal{A}
could
index a potential set of “recommended” neighborhoods.

Our goal may be to maximize some outcome, for instance the average household income rank
in adulthood (relative to the adult income distribution) for children in the targeted

\theta(a)\in[0,1]
the (unknown) true outcome associated
with selection
a,
we would like to choose actions

that maximize
\theta(a)
. To represent this preference, we can consider the loss function
L(a,\theta)=1-\theta(a)
.

Data (e.g. Census-tract level measures for economic opportunity together with a model

Bergman et al. 2024

X(a)
for the

along with an associated standard error

\sigma(a)
, which for simplicity we treat as known. For
a\in\mathcal{A}
, suppose that

\frac{X(a)-\theta(a)}{\sigma(a)}\sim Z(a)
, for some random variables
\left\{{Z(a)}\right\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}

One reasonable decision rule is empirical welfare maximization, which takes
\delta(Y)=\argmax_{a\in\mathcal{A}}{X(a)}
. This decision rule corresponds to as-if optimization against a
joint confidence set for
\theta
based on an unstudentized statistic
X(a)-\theta(a)=Z(a)\sigma(a)

\hat{\Theta}(Y)=\bigtimes_{a\in\mathcal{A}}[X(a)-c_{1-\alpha},\infty),\text{ where }P\left({\sup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Z(a)\sigma(a)\leq c_{1-\alpha}}\right)=1-\alpha

This confidence set
corresponds to an unstudentized version of what

call the
projection approach, which was suggested in the supplementary materials of

which inflates the estimated loss by

2.1.
Optimal provision of P-certificates

In settings where P-certificates are useful, it seems reasonable to prefer tighter bounds
on the loss. That is, given the choice between level-
(1-\alpha)
P-certificates
R(Y)
and

\tilde{R}(Y)
, if
R(Y)\leq\tilde{R}(Y)
for all
Y,
it is natural to prefer
R

to
\tilde{R}
. More generally, if we consider any loss function
B(R,\theta)
that is
increasing in
R
for all
\theta,
then

\mathbb{E}_{P}[B(R(Y),\theta)]\leq\mathbb{E}_{P}[B(\tilde{R}(Y),\theta)]
for all
(P,\theta),
so
R
yields
a lower expected loss than
\tilde{R}
.

When
R
and
\tilde{R}
are ordered almost surely, or more generally in the sense of
first-order stochastic dominance, we say
R
weakly dominates
\tilde{R}
.

Definition 2
.

We say that a
1-\alpha
P-certified decision
(\delta,R)

weakly dominates
another
1-\alpha
P-certified decision
(\tilde{\delta},\tilde{R})
if

P(R(Y)\geq r)\leq P(\tilde{R}(Y)\geq r)

for all
r
and all
(\theta,P)\in\mathcal{M}
.

We next show that the class of P-certified decisions from as-if optimization against a
confidence set, à la
Proposition

1

essentially complete
class with respect to
dominance. That is, any P-certified decision rule
(\tilde{\delta},\tilde{R})
is weakly dominated by some decision rule
(\delta,R)
formed by as-if optimization. As an implication, these decision
rules are also essentially complete with respect to any risk function of the form
\mathbb{E}_{P}[B(R,\theta)]
, for any weakly increasing
B(\cdot,\theta)
.

To state this result, denote by
\mathcal{C}_{P,\alpha,\epsilon}
the class of decision
rules formed by as-if optimization against a confidence set
\hat{\Theta}(Y)
in the
following fashion: For
\epsilon>0
, consider
(\delta(Y),R(Y))
such that (i)

\hat{\Theta}
is a
1-\alpha
confidence set under
\mathcal{M}
and (ii)

(6)

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\hat{\Theta}(Y)}L(\delta(Y),\theta)

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sup_{\theta\in\hat{\Theta}(Y)}L(a,\theta)+\epsilon\quad\text{ and }\quad R(Y)=\sup_{\theta\in\hat{\Theta}(Y)}L(\delta(Y),\theta)

The constant
\epsilon

accounts for cases where the minimax loss over
\hat{\Theta}(Y)
is not achieved by any element of

; our result holds for any

Theorem 1
.

Assume that

\inf_{a\in\mathcal{A}}L(a,\theta)>-\infty
for every
\theta\in\Theta
.
For any P-certified decision
(\tilde{\delta},\tilde{R})
and any

\epsilon>0
, there exists some
(\delta,R)\in\mathcal{C}_{P,\alpha,\epsilon}
that weakly dominates

Theorem

1
shows a sense in which it is without loss to limit attention to
P-certificates derived from as-if optimization with confidence sets. Thus, not only is
as-if optimization a convenient way to generate P-certified decisions, but there is, in a

Theorem

1
follows from the simple observation that inverting the P-certification property yields a valid confidence set,

\tilde{\Theta}(Y):=\left\{{\theta:L(\tilde{\delta}(Y),\theta)\leq\tilde{R}(Y)}\right\},\quad P(\theta\in\tilde{\Theta}(Y))=P(L(\tilde{\delta}(Y),\theta)\leq\tilde{R}(Y))\geq 1-\alpha.

As-if optimizing against this confidence set can only weakly improve the resulting certificate.

Remark 2

(Properties of as-if optimization)
.

Since restricting to as-if optimization
over confidence sets is, for the purpose of generating P-certified decisions, without
loss, we highlight a few additional features of these decision rules. First,
confidence sets allow for decision-makers with different decision problems—including ones unknown to the analyst—to derive
their own P-certificates by optimizing (
6
), making confidence sets
attractive for communicating information to heterogeneous decision-makers. Second, in
addition to generating P-certificates, as-if decisions with confidence sets are
dynamically consistent in the sense axiomatized by

Lim and Park (2026)

Lastly, we comment on set-identification of
\theta

its guarantees of P-certification, and its essential completeness do not qualitatively
depend on whether
\theta
is point- or set-identified.

5

changes optimal decision rules in their setting. By contrast, so long as we consider decision problems for
which

2.2.
Benefits to a downstream ambiguity-averse decision-maker

The guarantee
(
1
) is arguably intuitive and may be imposed by fiat as a
prerequisite for decisions—much like how confidence sets have become standard
implementations of statistical inference. Indeed, recent work by
Galeotti et al. (2024, Definition 1)
imposes exactly such a notion. Yet P-certification is not immediately
connected to standard decision-theoretic targets such as risk or expected utility.
Towards establishing such a connection, this subsection analyzes how P-certified
decisions may provide value to a downstream, ambiguity-averse decision-maker who
delegates data analysis to an analyst while hoping to minimize expected loss (risk). The
analyst provides the decision-maker with a P-certified decision, and
the decision-maker must choose whether to accept the recommendation or to instead
default to some safe action.

For this subsection, we assume that the loss function is bounded, and without further loss
of generality assume
L(a,\theta)\in[0,1]
(correspondingly, we may assume certificates are also bounded
R(Y)\in[0,1]
). The decision-maker has access to a
default action
a_{0}\not\in\mathcal{A}
, which yields a loss
C\in[0,1]
which is known to the
decision-maker and does not depend on
\theta.
Our decision-maker decides whether to
adopt the recommended action
\delta(Y)
—incurring an uncertain loss of
L(\delta(Y),\theta)
—or to implement the default action
a_{0}
and incur loss
C
. We model the decision-maker as unfamiliar with the details of the relevant statistical decision problem. Correspondingly, the decision-maker would like to delegate data analysis to an analyst, while requiring worst-case assurances for the performance of the resulting decision.

Let
Q\in\{0,1\}
be an indicator for whether the decision-maker adopts the analyst’s recommendation. A simple adoption rule is
Q:=\mathbbm{1}(R(Y)\leq C)
—adopting whenever the reported certificate is lower than the default cost. The
corresponding loss is
QL(\delta(Y),\theta)+(1-Q)C
. For such adoption rules,
P-certified decisions provide a worst-case risk bound: the decision-maker incurs no more
than
u\alpha(1-C)
excess risk if they never adopt when
R>C
and adopt with
probability at most
u
when
R\leq C
.

Proposition 2
.

For
u\in[0,1]
and any
(\theta,P)\in\mathcal{M}
, any adoption decision
Q
with
\mathbb{E}[Q\mid Y]\leq u\mathbbm{1}(R(Y)\leq C)
has bounded risk

\mathbb{E}_{P}[QL(\delta(Y),\theta)+(1-Q)C]\leq C+u\alpha(1-C).

Proposition

2
shows one way in which P-certified decisions provide value to
ambiguity-averse decision makers: they ensure that the decision-maker does not have
to tolerate much excess risk so long as they adopt
\delta(Y)
only when
R(Y)
suggests that this is preferable to the default action.
10
10

10

Let
\delta_{Q}(Y)
denote the recommendation when
Q=1

and the default action otherwise.
Since
P\{L(\delta_{Q}(Y),\theta)>QR(Y)+(1-Q)C\}\leq\alpha
and
L(\delta_{Q}(Y),\theta)\leq 1
, we also obtain the
following
P

R(Y)
can be much smaller
than

\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[{L(\delta_{Q}(Y),\theta)}\right]\leq\alpha+\mathbb{E}_{P}[QR(Y)+(1-Q)C].

If
Q=\mathbbm{1}(R(Y)\leq C)
, this bound simplifies to
\alpha+\mathbb{E}_{P}[\min(R(Y),C)]
.
To further control the excess risk, the decision-maker may either adopt
less, in the sense of choosing a smaller

, or ask that the analyst provide
recommendations with higher confidence, requesting a larger

Proposition

2
applies to all P-certified decisions, including those derived

.

Of course, a decision-maker solely concerned with worst-case risk and facing
sufficient ambiguity would always default to the safe action. We next consider optimal
adoption for a particular form of ambiguity-averse preferences.

Consider a decision-maker who is ambiguity averse and uncertain of the precise loss function, but believes the P-certification property. The decision maker observes the recommended action and the certificate,
(\delta(Y),R(Y))
, drawn from a distribution
G
. The true value of
G
is the law of
(\delta,R)
induced by the true distribution
P
and the analyst’s choices, which in turn depend on the loss
L
. Let
\ell:\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
denote a generic loss function, which may lie outside the class
\left\{L(\cdot,\theta):\theta\in\Theta\right\}
induced by the true

. Our next result considers
a decision-maker who entertains
all
pairs
(\ell(\cdot),G)
satisfying
P-certification:

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{DM}}^{(P,\alpha)}:=\left\{{(\ell(\cdot),G):G(\ell(\delta)\leq R)\geq 1-\alpha,G(\mathcal{A}\times[0,1])=1}\right\}.

This model encodes that P-certification is essentially the only thing that the
decision-maker knows.
11
11

11

To prove that

is a P-certified decision, when the loss function

is
money-metric the analyst can in principle offer insurance policies that pay out

\alpha

before seeing the data. If
(\delta(\cdot),R(\cdot))
is indeed a P-certificate,

Neyman (1937)
writes, “The theoretical statistician constructing [a

gambler has a certain range of possibilities to choose from while, whatever he actually
chooses, the probability of his winning and thus the probability of the bank losing has

Relative to this model, the decision-maker’s

A decision rule for this problem is an adoption probability function

prescribing the probability of adoption as a function of recommended action

and

. The corresponding risk function is then

By

2

.
12
12

12

Note that this model rationalizes the simple threshold decision
\mathbbm{1}(R(Y)\leq C)

as itself as-if optimization under the confidence set

, valid under all

\ell(\delta(Y))

We suppose that the decision-maker balances between subjective Bayes risk minimization and

\min_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\left\{{(1-\omega)\mathbb{E}_{(\ell,G)\sim\pi}\left[{R_{\text{adopt}}(q(\cdot,\cdot);\ell(\cdot),C,G)}\right]+\omega\sup_{(\ell,G)\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{DM}}^{(P,\alpha)}}R_{\text{adopt}}(q(\cdot,\cdot);\ell(\cdot),C,G)}\right\}

Equation (
2.2

and subjective expected utility maximization.

The next proposition characterizes optimal adoption decisions for such preferences. In
particular, among adoption decision rules that solely depend on the reported certificate,
the optimal adoption decision is either to never adopt, or to adopt with a smoothed

\mathbbm{1}(R(Y)\leq C)

\pi

Proposition 3
.

Assume:

(1)

C\in(0,1)
and
\mathcal{A}
contains at least two elements.

(2)

The class of decision rules
\mathcal{Q}=\left\{{q:\mathcal{A}\times[0,1]\to[0,1]:q(a,r)=q(r)}\right\}

consists of rules that do not depend on the reported action
\delta(Y)
.

(3)

The prior
\pi

2.2

m(r):=\mathbb{E}[\ell(\delta(Y))\mid R(Y)=r]\text{ is weakly increasing and }m(C)<C

for (

{q_{\omega}^{*}(r)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{ if }r\leq C\\

Proposition

3
shows that the optimal adoption decision is a compromise
between the simple threshold rule
q(r)=\mathbbm{1}(r\leq C)
and the Bayes decision

the ambiguity-averse preferences then reduces adoption when the certificate

\omega

the pessimistic part increases, adoption in this “ambiguous” region

\omega
increases, the pessimism completely dominates and

Together,

Note that

3.
Illustrations of P-certified decisions

We next illustrate how the certified decisions perspective helps interpret empirical findings and construct recommendations in two applications.

3.1.
Creating Moves to Opportunity

In an experiment known as Creating Moves to Opportunity (CMTO),
Bergman et al. (2024)

offered low-income Seattle families assistance to move to neighborhoods designated as

high-mobility
. These high-mobility tracts are roughly the top third of census
tracts in the area, ranked by noisy estimates of upward mobility from the Opportunity
Atlas
(
Chetty et al., 2026
)
.
Bergman et al. (2024)
, in selecting these Census
tracts, face the challenge of only observing noisy estimates of tract mobility. Different
statistical inference analyses can thus reach different inferential conclusions about the
extent to which the data support designating the selected tracts as
high-mobility.

In one analysis,
Andrews et al. (2024)
compare the
average mobility
of the
selected tracts against the average mobility of tracts where the voucher holders currently
reside. They find evidence that, indeed, the select tracts are higher mobility—in the
sense that confidence sets for this difference-of-averages exclude zero. In
another analysis, however,
Mogstad et al. (2024)
instead ask which tracts can be confidently
placed in the true top third. They find that, for the 397 tracts in Seattle and
King County,
every tract
is included in the 95% confidence set for the true
top-third (and also in the confidence set for the bottom-third) of tracts. Thus, at conventional levels, there is insufficient
evidence to support that any given tract—even the one with the highest mobility
estimate—truly belongs to the top third.

Of course,
Andrews et al. (2024)
and
Mogstad et al. (2024)
consider
different inferential questions, which are in turn relevant for different decision problems. In particular, we show in this section that each inference result corresponds to a P-certificate for an appropriately formulated problem.
The key distinction between these decision problems is the ambiguity set assumed for relocation patterns—that is, for a given selection of target tracts, where will voucher households move from, and where will they move to?
If prospective movers
are selected at random from the population of housing voucher holders with children and choose
targets uniformly at random among recommended tracts, then the analysis of

Andrews et al. (2024)
is directly reassuring. Instead, if movers could
systematically move from the best unselected tract to the worst selected tract, then
the CMTO program might not actually improve economic mobility at all, corresponding to the conclusion of
Mogstad et al. (2024)
. Consequently, as

Mogstad et al. (2024)
write, “Assumptions on location choices indeed are needed to
be statistically confident […] that CMTO
would have a positive impact” (p.514).

To formalize this connection, consider an analyst who recommends a set
S\subset\left\{{1,\ldots,n}\right\}
of
m:=|S|=\lfloor n/3\rfloor
target tracts. We let
\theta_{i}
be the true economic mobility of
tract
i
, collected in a vector
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Suppose the status-quo locations of prospective movers are summarized by a probability vector
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
, while conditional on
S
movers choose new locations according to a probability vector
q
supported
on
S
. The average change in economic mobility for such a population of movers is then

(q-p)^{\prime}\theta
. For a given class
W(S)\in\Delta^{n-1}
of
p
and
V(S)\in\Delta^{n-1}

of
q
—to be specified—containing the plausible set of movement patterns, we will consider the worst-case
mobility gain over
W(S)
and
V(S)
:

U(S,\theta;W,V):=\min_{p\in W(S),q\in V(S)}(q-p)^{\prime}\theta.

This then generates a large menu of decision problems indexed by
W(\cdot),V(\cdot)
.

We winnow down this menu by choosing some parametrizations for
W(\cdot),V(\cdot)
that
vary in how “spread out” movers’ current and future locations are. One intuitive
parametrization for plausible targets
V(\cdot)
is one where movers spread over at
least a fraction
\rho\in[0,1]
of the selected tracts:

V_{\rho}(S):=\left\{{q\in\Delta^{n-1}\text{ supported on $S$}:q_{i}\leq\frac{1}{1\vee\lceil\rho m\rceil}}\right\}.

With
\rho=0
,
V_{\rho=0}(S)
allows all movers to settle in the same tract; with
\rho=1
,

V_{\rho=1}(S)
only contains the uniform distribution on
S
. We also consider two
specifications for allowable distributions
W(\cdot)
of where movers come from.
The first specification fixes
W(S)=\left\{{b}\right\}
, where
b_{i}
is the share of housing-voucher
households with children living in tract
i
in 2018, the year CMTO began.
13
13

13

We follow
Andrews et al. (2024)
in choosing

.
The second specification
mirrors

and considers adversarial choices of

W_{\rho}(S):=\left\{{p\in\Delta^{n-1}\text{ supported on $S^{C}$}:p_{i}\leq\frac{1}{1\vee\lceil\rho(n-m)\rceil}}\right\},

where we assume that in the status quo, movers are spread out across a
fraction

, respectively.
14
14

14

To give a little intuition, in the

A back-of-the-envelope upper bound for

.
Separately, for each CZ we can compute an estimate of

4.
E-certified decisions

We have shown that P-certified decisions offer a natural route to combine
(frequentist) statistical inference with decision-making. However, high-probability
guarantees, as appearing in confidence intervals and hypothesis tests, are not the only
type of frequentist guarantee available. A recent literature in statistics proposes
e-values
as an alternative and a complement to traditional statistical inference
(see
Ramdas and Wang, 2024
, for a review)
. An
e-variable
against the parameter
value
\theta_{0}
is a nonnegative random variable
E(Y,\theta_{0})

such that

\mathbb{E}_{P}[E(Y,\theta_{0})]\leq 1\text{ for all $P$ such that $(\theta_{0},P)\in\mathcal{M}$}.

One can interpret
E(\cdot,\theta_{0})
as the realized dollar payoff of a bet,
priced at $1, against the hypothesis
H_{0}:\theta=\theta_{0}
. Since the expected payoff
under
H_{0}
is less than $1, this bet is (at most) fair. Correspondingly, a large realization of
E(Y,\theta_{0})
can be interpreted as evidence against
H_{0}
—in the sense that it is an
unlikely event under
H_{0}
—much like a small p-value is evidence against
H_{0}
.

As with confidence sets, we can use e-variables to certify the quality of decisions.

Conclusion

This paper considers combining statistical inference with statistical decisions. In two
leading modes of frequentist inference, statistical inference translates to
certified decisions
—decisions paired with certificates of their performance.
We show that it is without loss to base certified decisions on statistical inference procedures, and thus that inferential and decision goals are, at the very least, partially
aligned.
We also
show that these certified decisions provide ambiguity-averse downstream decision-makers
with useful risk guarantees and simple adoption decision rules—which may be implemented
and understood without statistical sophistication on the part of the decision-maker.

Details of as-if optimization with e-variables

In particular, with the choice of e-variables in
Section

4.1
, the as-if
optimization following
Grünwald (2023)
simplifies considerably due to the
observation that

\sup_{\theta}\frac{L(a,\theta)}{E(Y,\theta)}=\max_{k\neq a}\sqrt{\sigma_{a}^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}}\sup_{t\geq\max_{j\neq k}\frac{Y_{j}-Y_{k}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{j}^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}}}}\frac{t-\frac{Y_{a}-Y_{k}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{a}^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}}}}{G_{k}(t)}

for a known non-decreasing function
G_{k}(\cdot)
.

This follows from two observations. First, if
k
is the best arm under
\theta
, then
E(Y,\theta)=G_{k}(\max_{j\neq k}t_{j}(Y,\theta))
for some non-decreasing
G_{k}(\cdot)
. Second, for a fixed
k
, let
t(\theta,k)=\max_{j\neq k}t_{j}(Y,\theta)
. We have that

\Theta=\bigcup_{k\in[n]}\bigcup_{t\geq\max_{j\neq k}\frac{Y_{j}-Y_{k}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{j}^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}}}}\left\{{\theta:t(\theta,k)=t,k(\theta)=k}\right\}.

Maximizing
L(a,\theta)/E(Y,\theta)
over the inner set of
\theta
, applying the
first observation, yields the closed form
G_{k}(t)^{-1}\sqrt{\sigma_{a}^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}}\left({t-\frac{Y_{a}-Y_{k}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{a}^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}}}}\right)
.
