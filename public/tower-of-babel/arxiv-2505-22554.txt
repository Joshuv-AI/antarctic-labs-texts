Can Copulas Be Used for Feature Selection? A Machine Learning Study on Diabetes Risk Prediction
Agnideep Aich, Md Monzur Murshed, Amanda Mayeaux and Sameera Hewage

Abstract
Accurate diabetes risk prediction relies on identifying key features from complex health datasets, but conventional methods like mutual information (MI) filters and genetic algorithms (GAs) often overlook extreme dependencies critical for high-risk subpopulations. In this study we introduce a feature-selection framework using the upper-tail dependence coefficient ( λ U \lambda_{U} ) of the novel A2 copula, which quantifies how often extreme higher values of a predictor co-occur with diabetes diagnoses (targer variable). Applied to the CDC Diabetes Health Indicators dataset ( n = 253,680 n=253{,}680 ), our method prioritizes five predictors (self‐reported general health, high blood pressure, body mass index, mobility limitations and high cholesterol levels) based on the upper tail dependencies. These features match or outperform MI and GA selected subsets across four classifiers (Random Forest, XGBoost, Logistic Regression, Gradient Boosting), achieving accuracy up to 86.5% (in case of XGBoost) and AUC up to 0.806 (in case of Gradient Boosting), rivaling the full 21-feature model. Permutation importance confirms the clinical relevance of these predictors, with BMI and GenHlth driving accuracy. To our knowledge, this is the first work to apply a copula’s upper-tail dependence for supervised feature selection , bridging extreme-value theory and machine learning to deliver a practical toolkit for diabetes prevention.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus affects over 500 million adults globally, with projections estimating a 50% rise in prevalence by 2045. This escalating burden underscores the urgent need for tools that balance predictive accuracy with interpretability, enabling clinicians to prioritize high-risk individuals for targeted interventions. While machine learning has revolutionized risk modeling, existing methods often struggle to isolate features that signal
extreme
risk, the very cases where early action could avert irreversible complications.

Current feature-selection
[
11
]
techniques, such as mutual information (MI)
[
16
]
filters and genetic-algorithm (GA)
[
12
]
wrappers, excel at identifying broadly relevant predictors but fail to prioritize variables whose
extreme values
co-occur with adverse outcomes. For example, MI quantifies general dependency but cannot distinguish whether a high BMI percentile correlates more strongly with diabetes than a low one. Similarly, GAs optimize for overall accuracy rather than extreme-risk performance. This gap limits their utility in public health contexts, where resource constraints demand tools that flag the most vulnerable subpopulations.

Copulas, statistical tools for modeling dependencies between variables, offer a promising solution. Introduced by Sklar
[
17
]
in 1959 and subsequent advances by Nelsen
[
14
]
, in 2006 copulas separate marginal distributions from dependency structures, enabling nuanced analysis of non-Gaussian relationships. However, classical copulas like Gaussian or Clayton treat dependencies symmetrically, making them ill-suited for isolating tail events.Recently, Aich et al.
[
1
]
introduced a novel Archimedean copula, originally called the B copula and hereafter referred to as the A2 copula, to capture dual tail dependencies effectively. In this work, we explicitly quantify its
upper‐tail dependence
coefficient
[
13
]
,
\lambda_{U}
, which measures the probability that two variables simultaneously occupy their highest percentiles. This property aligns perfectly with diabetes risk prediction, where extreme values of predictors like BMI or blood pressure often indicate presence of the disease.

In this work, we bridge extreme-value theory
[
15
]
and machine learning by proposing the first feature-selection framework based on
\lambda_{U}
. Using the CDC Diabetes Health Indicators dataset (
n=253{,}680
), we rank 21 predictors by their tail dependence with diabetes status and validate the top five against MI and GA-selected subsets. Our results demonstrate that models trained on these features namely GenHlth, HighBP, BMI, DiffWalk and HighChol achieving accuracy up to
86.5%
(in case of XGBoost) and AUC up to
0.806
(in case of Gradient Boosting) while maintaining interpretability. Crucially, permutation importance analysis confirms that these predictors drive performance precisely because their
extreme values
correlate with diabetes, offering clinicians a transparent, evidence-based tool for risk stratification.

Related Work

Feature selection methods range from straightforward filters to sophisticated wrappers and embedded approaches. One of the earliest and most enduring filter techniques relies on
mutual information
(MI), a measure Shannon
[
16
]
introduced in his 1948 landmark paper on information theory. MI tells us how much knowing a feature
X
reduces our uncertainty about an outcome
Y
, and Cover and Thomas
[
6
]
later distilled these ideas into a definitive textbook on information theory. MI‐based filters remain popular in practice because they can uncover complex, non‐linear relationships without the overhead of training a full predictive model.

Wrapper methods take a different tack by folding feature selection directly into the model‐building process. Genetic algorithms (GAs), first proposed by Holland
[
12
]
in 1975 and made widely accessible by Goldberg
[
10
]
in 1989, are a classic example. GAs treat each candidate subset of features as an individual in a population, then use mechanisms inspired by natural evolution (selection, crossover, mutation) to evolve high‐performing feature combinations. Other wrappers rely on greedy stepwise searches or heuristic hill‐climbing, while embedded approaches such as LASSO
[
18
]
integrate feature penalization into the model’s own objective function.

Copulas, long a staple in fields like finance and environmental risk assessment for modeling dependencies and extreme‐value behavior, have seen relatively little use in supervised learning. A handful of recent studies leverage copula mixtures or vine copulas to capture non‐Gaussian joint distributions, but none, as far as we know, focus on a copula’s upper‐tail dependence coefficient as a direct criterion for feature screening.

That omission is exactly what we address. By using the A2 copula’s upper‐tail dependence coefficient, which quantifies the probability that two variables simultaneously fall into their highest percentiles, we propose a truly tail‐focused feature‐selection strategy. To our knowledge, this is the first work to apply a copula’s upper‐tail dependence in a classification setting. We benchmark our A2‐based ranking against both MI filters and a streamlined GA wrapper, and then validate all three feature subsets across four classifiers: Random Forest
[
3
]
, XGBoost
[
5
]
, Logistic Regression
[
7
]
, and Gradient Boosting
[
8
]
. The outcome is an interpretable workflow that combines extreme‐value theory with standard machine‐learning pipelines, yielding compact feature sets that deliver top‐tier predictive performance.

Preliminaries

Copulas and Dependency Modeling

Copulas are statistical tools that model dependency structures between random variables, independent of their marginal distributions. Introduced by Sklar
[
17
]
(1959), they provide a unified approach to capturing joint dependencies. Archimedean copulas
[
9
]
, known for their simplicity and flexibility, are defined using a generator function, making them particularly effective for modeling bivariate and multivariate dependencies.

A2 Copula

The A2 copula extends the Archimedean copula framework to capture both upper and lower tail dependencies more effectively, which makes it ideal for understanding relationships in critical scenarios, such as the risk factors leading to diabetes. The generator function for the A2 copula is given by:

\phi_{A2}(t;\theta)=\left(t^{-\frac{1}{\theta}}+t^{\frac{1}{\theta}}-2\right)^{\theta},\quad\theta\geq 1.

(1)

Its inverse generator function is derived as:

\phi_{A2}^{-1}(t)=\left[\frac{t^{1/\theta}+2-\sqrt{\Bigl(t^{1/\theta}+2\Bigr)^{2}-4}}{2}\right]^{\theta}.

The A2 copula is parameterized by
\theta
, which governs the strength of dependency. A2’s upper tail dependence coefficient is given by:

\lambda_{U}=2-2^{\frac{1}{2\theta}}.

Mutual Information (MI)

Mutual Information, introduced by Shannon
[
16
]
in 1948, quantifies the non-linear dependence between two variables by measuring the reduction in uncertainty of one variable given knowledge of the other. For feature selection, MI is computed as:

I(X;Y)=\sum_{x\in X}\sum_{y\in Y}p(x,y)\log\left(\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}\right),

where
p(x,y)
is the joint distribution and
p(x),p(y)
are marginal distributions. MI is widely used in feature selection due to its ability to capture complex dependencies beyond linear correlations (Cover et al.
[
6
]
).

Genetic Algorithms (GA)

Genetic Algorithms, pioneered by Holland
[
12
]
in 1975, are optimization techniques inspired by natural selection. In feature selection, GAs evolve subsets of features through iterative processes of selection, crossover, and mutation. Key components include:

•

Fitness Function
: Typically classification accuracy (e.g., via cross-validation).

•

Crossover
: Combines subsets (e.g., single-point crossover or uniform exchange of feature subsets).

•

Mutation
: Introduces randomness to avoid local optima.

GAs are particularly effective for high-dimensional problems where exhaustive search is infeasible (Goldberg (1989)).

Upper Tail Dependence

Upper tail dependence
[
13
]
measures the strength of association between extreme high values of two variables. In other words, if one variable takes on a high value, there is a greater likelihood that the other variable will also be high. This concept is particularly useful in feature screening, as it allows us to identify predictors where extreme increases are strongly linked with higher diabetes risk. By focusing on features with high upper tail dependence, we can pinpoint variables that significantly contribute to the risk of diabetes when they exhibit elevated levels. In this work, we leverage the upper tail dependence property of the novel A2 Copula for this purpose.

Permutation Importance

Permutation importance
[
3
]
is a widely used, model-agnostic technique to quantify the contribution of each feature to a model’s predictive performance. It operates by measuring the increase in prediction error when the values of a single feature are randomly shuffled, thereby disrupting its relationship with the target variable. Features that lead to greater drops in performance are considered more influential.

Formally, for a trained classifier
f
and test data
(\mathbf{X}_{\text{test}},\mathbf{y}_{\text{test}})
, the permutation importance
I_{j}
of feature
j
is defined as:

I_{j}=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(f(\mathbf{X}_{\text{test}}),\mathbf{y}_{\text{test}})\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(f(\mathbf{X}_{\text{test}}^{(j)}),\mathbf{y}_{\text{test}})\right]

where
\mathbf{X}_{\text{test}}^{(j)}
represents the test set with the
j
-th feature column permuted, and
\mathcal{L}
is a loss function (e.g., cross-entropy or 1 - accuracy). This approach requires no internal access to the model and is compatible with any black-box classifier, making it particularly valuable in interdisciplinary applications where transparency and interpretability are essential.

In this study, we use permutation importance to validate the practical relevance of features selected via A2 copula upper-tail dependence. Specifically, we evaluate how much each of the five A2-selected features contributes to a trained Random Forest classifier’s accuracy by measuring the average performance degradation over multiple permutations. This analysis helps bridge statistical tail dependence with real-world model behavior, ensuring that selected features are not only theoretically meaningful but also practically predictive of diabetes risk.

Methodology

In this study, we integrate copula‐based dependency modeling with standard machine‐learning workflows to identify and validate diabetes risk factors. We begin by transforming all variables into pseudo‐observations, estimate upper‐tail dependence using the A2 copula, and compare its feature rankings against mutual information (MI) and a genetic‐algorithm wrapper (GA). Finally, we evaluate predictive performance and interpretability across four classifiers.

Data Preprocessing and Pseudo‐Observations

We used the CDC Diabetes Health Indicators dataset (21 predictors). Each feature
x_{i}
and the binary target
y
were mapped to the unit interval via their empirical cumulative distribution functions:

u_{i}=\frac{\mathrm{rank}(x_{i})}{n+1},\quad v=\frac{\mathrm{rank}(y)}{n+1}.

This pseudo‐observation transformation preserves dependency structures while ensuring uniform marginals for copula analysis.

To quantify how higher values of the predictor is related to the higher values of the target variable, we employ the upper‐tail dependence coefficient. The parameter
\theta
of the A2 copula is estimated using a neural network mentioned in Aich et al. (2025) (Under review). For each feature target pair, we:

1.
Transform to pseudo‐observations
(U,V)
.

2.
Obtain
\hat{\theta}
.

3.
Compute
\lambda_{U}\;=\;2\;-\;2^{\tfrac{1}{2\hat{\theta}}}
. We then rank features by descending
\lambda_{U}
and select the top five for classification.

Alternative Feature‐Selection Baselines

Mutual Information (MI)

We compute
\mathrm{MI}(X;Y)
for each predictor using five‐fold cross‐validation and select the five features with highest MI scores.

Genetic-Algorithm Wrapper (GA)

We initialize 10 random subsets of size five and evolve them over five generations using:

1.
Tournament selection (size 2),

2.
Crossover combining the first half of one parent with the second half of another,

3.
Random repair to maintain subset size.

Classification and Evaluation

We compare four classifiers, Random Forest, XGBoost, Logistic Regression, and Gradient Boosting trained on:

•

All 21 predictors.

•

The five A2‐selected features.

•

The five MI‐selected features.

•

The five GA‐selected features.

For each train/test split (80/20, stratified), we standardize features only for Logistic Regression; tree‐based models use raw values. Performance is assessed via accuracy, precision, recall, F
1
‐score, and AUC.

Interpretability via Permutation Importance

To validate the practical importance of A2‐selected features, we train a Random Forest on those five predictors and compute permutation importances on the held‐out test set (20 repeats). Mean accuracy decreases upon shuffling each feature provide an interpretable ranking, which we compare directly to the
\lambda_{U}
ordering.

Medical and Public Health Implications

Our framework centers on the A2 copula’s
upper‐tail dependence coefficient

\lambda_{U}
, which directly measures how often a risk factor
X
and diabetes
Y
both land in their highest percentiles. In plain terms, a high
\lambda_{U}
means that when
X
is extreme, diabetes is almost always extreme as well. In our analysis, ranking by A2 copula’s
\lambda_{U}
yields the following top five predictors (in descending order of tail‐co‐occurrence):

\text{GenHlth},\;\text{HighBP},\;\text{BMI},\;\text{DiffWalk},\;\text{HighChol}.

These are the variables that spike alongside diabetes most consistently at the very highest values.

We then used permutation importance on our Random Forest model to see which features the model actually relies on most (Figure
1
). Shuffling each feature in turn caused the greatest drop in accuracy for:

\text{BMI},\;\text{GenHlth},\;\text{HighChol},\;\text{HighBP},\;\text{DiffWalk}.

Although the exact ordering differs,
\lambda_{U}
places general health first, while the model gives top billing to BMI, both analyses highlight the same five features. This divergence is expected as
\lambda_{U}
captures a purely statistical tail‐dependency property, whereas permutation importance reflects how the trained model leverages each feature for prediction.

Despite this slight reordering, the agreement on the core five predictors namely self‐reported general health, high blood pressure, body mass index, mobility limitations and high cholesterol levels reinforce their dual importance both as extreme‐value risk markers and as practical drivers of model accuracy. Focusing on upper‐tail dependence rather than average correlations allows us to zero in on patients who fall into the highest‐risk strata. From a public health standpoint, this suggests:

Targeted Screening:
Prioritize outreach and screening for individuals with extreme BMI or poor self‐reported health, since they occupy the diabetic tail with highest probability.

Aggressive Risk Management:
Emphasize blood‐pressure and cholesterol control in patients whose readings lie in the upper tail, where intervention has the greatest impact on preventing severe diabetes outcomes.

Mobility Support:
Offer physical‐therapy and mobility improvement programs to those with walking difficulties, as severe functional impairment co‐occurs with diabetes at markedly elevated rates.

By combining statistical tail‐dependency ranking with model‐driven importance, we deliver a robust, interpretable strategy for identifying and intervening on the very highest diabetes risks using just five features for maximum clarity and efficiency.

Conclusion and Future Work

Our study introduces a novel feature-selection framework leveraging the upper-tail dependence coefficient (
\lambda_{U}
) of the novel A2 copula to identify predictors whose extreme values signal diabetes risk. By focusing on tail dependencies, the probability that a feature and diabetes diagnosis simultaneously reach their highest percentiles, we prioritize variables like GenHlth, HighBP, BMI, DiffWalk and HighChol) that are clinically actionable for high-risk subpopulations. Evaluated on the CDC Diabetes Health Indicators dataset (
n=253{,}680
), models trained on just five A2-selected features achieve accuracy upto 86.5% (in case of XGBoost) and AUC up to 0.806 (in case of Gradient Boosting), pretty much outperforming mutual information (MI) and genetic algorithm (GA) baselines. Permutation importance validates the clinical relevance of these predictors, confirming that extreme values drive performance. To our knowledge, this is the first application of a copula’s tail dependence for supervised feature ranking, offering a transparent, evidence-based tool for diabetes prevention. Future work should extend this framework in three key directions:

Clinical Translation:
Prospective studies tracking individuals flagged by high
\lambda_{U}
features (e.g., BMI
\geq 40
) could validate our method’s real-world utility. Integrating the framework into electronic health records (EHRs) would enable dynamic risk alerts for clinicians, while federated learning implementations could ensure privacy-preserving deployment across healthcare systems.

Methodological Enhancements:
Multivariate extensions using vine copulas could model interactions among predictors (e.g., how extreme BMI
and
hypertension amplify risk). Hybrid strategies combining
\lambda_{U}
with MI or GA might further optimize feature subsets in datasets with nonlinear dependencies. Embedding tail dependencies into neural architectures (e.g., attention-based transformers) could improve pattern detection in unstructured data like medical imaging or genomics.

Generalizability:
Testing the framework on other diseases with extreme-risk profiles (e.g., cardiovascular events, chronic kidney disease) would assess its broader applicability. Open-source tools for tail-dependent feature selection, paired with detailed tutorials, would democratize access for researchers and policymakers.

These steps would solidify the role of extreme-value theory in precision public health, ensuring our method remains scalable, interpretable, and actionable for global diabetes prevention efforts.
