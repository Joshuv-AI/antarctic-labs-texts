Chromosome-level genome assembly of the hemiparasitic Taxillus sutchuenensis (Loranthaceae)
Authors: Fen Yuan, Rui Rao, Rong Chen, Ruozhu Lin, Liang-Liang Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTaxillus sutchuenensis, an ecologically and medicinally important hemiparasitic plant that parasitizes diverse woody hosts, was sequenced to generate a high-quality chromosome-level genome assembly. PacBio HiFi long reads, RNA-seq transcriptome data, and Hi-C data were used to assemble a 406.32 Mb genome anchored onto nine pseudo-chromosomes, with a scaffold N50 of 45.59 Mb. The assembly showed high completeness and accuracy, supported by BUSCO (93.6%) and Merqury QV (70.6) assessments. The LTR Assembly Index (LAI) of 13.98 indicated excellent continuity. A total of 21,795 protein-coding genes were predicted, with 94.46% functionally annotated. Repetitive sequences accounted for 50.05% of the genome, primarily LTR retrotransposons. This genome provides a valuable resource for investigating the evolution, functional genomics, and parasitic mechanisms of hemiparasitic plants.

Background & Summary

Despite Darwin’s opinion on “the highest importance to gain a clear insight into the means of modification and coadaptation” 166 years later since 1859, we are still struggling in adversity in understanding the interrelationships between hosts and parasitic. The ecological interpretation framework of systems constructed by non-photosynthetic parasitic plants and their hosts has been partly reconstructed at the molecular level1–6. In contrast, systems formed by green (photosynthetic) parasitic plants and their hosts are likely to involve additional layers of complexity, particularly in processes such as host recognition7,8, immune evasion9,10, water utilization11, and coevolution, especially in the absence of genomic resources. In fact, we remain insufficiently informed about regarding many green parasitic plants due to the lack of genomic background information, with only 12 of genomes of Santalales6,12–15, and only one genome record from Loranthaceae, reaching our field of view.

A high-quality genome dataset, particularly with detailed annotations, is valuable for understanding the mechanisms of communication and coevolution between parasites and their hosts, as well as among parasites themselves, thereby facilitating the exploration of previously overlooked biological questions. One recent study16 showed that multiple parasitic plants on the same host could influence one another, possibly through the shared host, leading to a reduced overall impact on the host, an intriguing case of a pattern that can be described as “1 + 1 < 2” in terms of host damage17–19, which supporting a long-standing guess of mine since 2005 the first time observing a parasite plant of Macrosolen in Xishuangbanna Tropical Botanical Garden. To further explore this idea, a well-assembled and well-annotated genome would provide essential data for investigating the genetic basis of such interactions and for assessing whether this and related phenomena reflect adaptive evolutionary mechanisms or arise from ecological constraints.

A search of GenBank reveals only one whole genome record for Taxillus chinensis, Loranthaceae20, a species mainly distributed in southern China not accessible for sampling. A population of T. sutchuenensis was identified growing on the Osmanthus fragrans, in our campus, for which a reference genome is available21. The finding provided us with an opportunity to test the aforementioned hypothesis by sequencing and assembling the genome of T. sutchuenensis which is also nearly the most widely distributed species in the Taxillus genus22.

Taxillus sutchuenensis is a taxonomically complex species first described in 1915 and initially placed in the genus Loranthus before it was treated under the genus Taxillus23. It is widely distributed south of the Qinling-Huaihe Line, as well as in the southern part of Korean Peninsula, and southern Japan, and is known to parasitize wide a range of host plants22. This broad host range to parasitize a wide variety of host plants is relatively uncommon among parasitic species and warrants further investigation at both the genomic and transcriptomic levels. The range of T. sutchuenensis overlaps with several other Taxillus species, raising the possibility of hybridization or reticulate evolution events such as lineage fusion. Genomic data are therefore essential to support future studies on these evolutionary processes.

Methods

Sample collection and genome sequencing

Samples of healthy Taxillus sutchuenensis plants were collected on March 17, 2025, from the campus of Southwest Forestry University (102.755543° E, 25.05731° N) (Fig. 1). To obtain high-quality genomic and transcriptomic data, four types of fresh tissues, including old leaves, young leaves, flowers, and stems, were collected and immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. To ensure traceability and future verification, a voucher specimen (Voucher No. 2025031701) was prepared and deposited in the Herbarium of Southwest Forestry University (SWFC).

Taxillus sutchuenensis. (a) living habitat on host tree, (b) close-up of parasitic shoots attached to host branches.

For high-quality genome sequencing, high-molecular-weight DNA was extracted from T. sutchuenensis using a modified CTAB method. Sequencing was performed by Pacific Biosciences on the PacBio Revio platform, which utilizes Single-Molecule Real-Time (SMRT) technology. A PCR-free circular library was constructed using the SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit and sequenced in CCS mode to generate high-fidelity (HiFi) long reads. In total, 1,177,719 HiFi reads were obtained, yielding 22.65 Gb of data with an average read length of 19.23 kb, a read N50 of 18.16 kb, and a maximum read length of 63.39 kb. The median read quality was Q31.3, with 91% of reads exceeding 15 kb in length, indicating a highly reliable library (Table 1).

To achieve a chromosome-level genome assembly, a Hi-C chromatin conformation capture library was constructed using the Phase Genomics Proximo Hi-C (Plant) Prep Kit and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq. 6000 platform with 150 bp paired-end reads. Approximately 135.81 Gb of high-quality Hi-C data were obtained (≈202× genome coverage), with Q20 and Q30 rates of 99.15% and 97.39%, respectively, and a GC content of 42.27% (Table 1).

In addition, to facilitate accurate gene annotation of T. sutchuenensis, total RNA was extracted from different tissues, including old leaves, young leaves, stems, and flowers, and sequenced on the MGISEQ-2000 platform with 150 bp paired-end reads. The RNA-seq data were used to support gene structure prediction and functional annotation (Table 1).

Genome size estimation and k-mer analysis

To assess the genomic characteristics of T. sutchuenensis, a genome survey was performed using high-fidelity long-read (HiFi) sequencing data. First, k-mer counting was conducted on the HiFi reads using Jellyfish24 v2.1.3 with a k-mer size of 25 to generate the initial k-mer frequency distribution. The resulting k-mer database was then processed with jellyfish stats to summarize overall k-mer frequency characteristics, followed by jellyfish histo to produce the k-mer histogram. Subsequently, GenomeScope v2.025 was used to fit the k-mer distribution, resulting in estimated genome sizes of 346 Mb, with a heterozygosity levels of 0.51%, the sequencing error rate was only 0.145%, and repeat contents of 44% for HiFi data (Fig. 2).

Genome assembly and scaffolding

To ensure the accuracy and continuity of the genome assembly, we first performed a comprehensive quality assessment of the raw PacBio HiFi reads using LongQC v1.2.0c26, including metrics such as read length distribution, base quality distribution, and GC content. The results indicated that the sequencing data were of high quality and uniform, suitable for downstream assembly analysis. Subsequently, HiFi reads were assembled de novo using Hifiasm v0.25.027. Hifiasm generated two phased haplotype assemblies (hap1 and hap2) along with a primary assembly. Potential contamination in the genome assembly was assessed using BlobTools v1.1.128 and DIAMOND v2.1.8 BLASTx28, with comparisons against the UniProt reference proteomes database (Fig. 3).The primary assembly was used for redundancy removal, scaffolding, and subsequent analyses. To reduce redundancy caused by heterozygous regions, the preliminary assembly was further processed with pseudohaploid and Purge_Dups v1.2.529 to remove redundant sequences, resulting in a more continuous and representative haploid genome assembly. After redundancy removal, the final assembly had a total size of 412.98 Mb with 579 contigs and a contig N50 of 1.94 Mb (Table 2).

Based on this assembly, contigs were aligned using minimap2 v2.2630. The Long Reads Scaffolder (LRScaf v1.1.12)31 was then applied to improve draft genome assembly at the scaffold level. Chromosome-level scaffolding was then performed using ALLHiC v0.9.832 in combination with Hi-C data, enabling anchoring and orientation of the genome sequences. To further enhance the accuracy of the chromosome assembly, the Hi-C scaffolds were visually inspected and manually corrected using Juicebox v1.11.0833, rectifying potential errors in chromosome order and orientation. The final chromosome-level assembly had a total length of 406.32 Mb, representing sequences anchored and oriented onto nine pseudochromosomes. Compared with the pre-Hi-C scaffold assembly (406.83 Mb), 99.88% of the assembled sequences were successfully anchored onto chromosomes. The scaffold reached N50 of 45.59 Mb (Fig. 4, Table 2), demonstrating high data quality suitable for highly continuous assembly. Overall, these results are consistent with previously reported genomes of other parasitic plants, such as Taxillus chinensis, Cuscuta campestris and Santalum species12,34,35.

To systematically evaluate the completeness and accuracy of the Taxillus sutchuenensis genome assembly, a multi-level assessment strategy was employed. First, basic statistical analyses were performed on the assembly, including the number of sequences, N50 length, and GC content, to provide an overall reflection of assembly continuity and uniformity. Second, to verify assembly coverage and accuracy, PacBio HiFi reads were aligned to the assembled genome using Minimap230, and alignment rate and genome-wide coverage were calculated with Samtools36. The results showed that the read coverage of the main pseudochromosomes exceeded 99%, with an average sequencing depth of approximately 40–50 × . Both base quality and alignment quality were high, indicating that the assembly represents nearly all genomic regions.

In addition, genome-wide quality values (QV) and consensus accuracy were estimated using Merqury v1.3, while the integrity and continuity of long terminal repeat (LTR) sequences were assessed using the LTR Assembly Index (LAI). The results revealed that the overall QV of the T. sutchuenensis genome reached 70.6, and the LAI value was 13.98 (Table 3). The k-mer completeness was 95.61%, indicating that most sequencing-derived k-mers were successfully captured in the final assembly. The Merqury k-mer spectra analysis further supported the high quality of the assembly (Fig. 5). Based on Merqury k-mer analysis, the T. sutchuenensis genome exhibited a high proportion of repetitive sequences (with unique regions accounting for approximately 55%) and an estimated genome size of about 346 Mb, slightly smaller than the final assembly size of 406 Mb. This difference is mainly attributed to the collapsing effect of repetitive regions, reflecting a genome characterized by high repetitiveness and low heterozygosity37,38.

Furthermore, genome-wide completeness was evaluated using BUSCO v5.4.739 (database: viridiplantae_odb10), showing a completeness rate of 93.6%, further confirming the accuracy and completeness of the T. sutchuenensis genome assembly and gene prediction.

Annotation of repetitive elements

Repetitive elements in the Taxillus sutchuenensis genome were annotated using a combination of de novo prediction and homology-based approaches. A species-specific repeat library was first constructed with RepeatModeler40 v2.0, followed by genome annotation using RepeatMasker v4.1.5 based on the custom library. In total, approximately 203.37 Mb of repetitive sequences were identified, accounting for 50.05% of the assembled genome. Among them, long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons were the most abundant type (28.99%), primarily consisting of Copia (11.51%) and Gypsy (14.52%) families. SINEs and LINEs accounted for 0.38% and 1.03%, respectively. DNA transposons represented 3.18%, dominated by the hobo-Activator (1.77%) and MULE-MuDR (0.67%) families. Unclassified repeats accounted for 16.25% of the genome, while tandem repeats (simple and low-complexity repeats) comprised less than 0.05% (Fig. 6, Table 4).

Gene prediction and functional annotation

Gene annotation of Taxillus sutchuenensis was performed by integrating transcriptome evidence, homology-based annotation, and de novo gene prediction. RNA-Seq data from four tissue types (old leaves, young leaves, flower leaves, and stems) were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 v2.2.141, and the resulting alignments were used for genome-guided transcriptome assembly with Trinity v2.8.542 and StringTie v2.2.143. The assembled transcripts were further optimized and UTRs updated using PASA v2.4.144.

For homology-based annotation, protein sequences from seven closely related species (Arabidopsis thaliana, Vitis vinifera, Olea europaea, Solanum lycopersicum, Rosa chinensis, Striga asiatica, and Cuscuta campestris) were aligned to the T. sutchuenensis genome using Miniprot v0.1245. De novo prediction was performed with AUGUSTUS v3.546 trained with both RNA-Seq hints and protein homology evidence.

All evidence sources (transcripts, homology-based predictions, and ab initio predictions) were integrated using EVidenceModeler(EVM) v1.1.147 to generate the final consensus gene set, followed by UTR extension. The longest isoform of each gene was retained as the representative sequence.

In total, 21,795 protein-coding genes and 24,568 transcripts were annotated, with an average gene length of 5,899 bp. These 21,795 gene models represent the initial consensus gene set generated by EVM. After integration, refinement, and redundancy filtering, a final non-redundant set of 15,709 representative protein-coding genes was retained for downstream analyses. Across all annotated transcripts, a total of 181,739 CDSs and 195,938 exons were identified, reflecting the complexity of gene structure. Gene annotation completeness was assessed using universal single-copy orthologs (BUSCOs) from BUSCO v5.4.7 with the eudicots_odb10 dataset. All 1,440 conserved plant genes were present, including 1,351 single-copy and 56 duplicated genes, indicating that the annotated gene set is largely complete. OMArk evaluation further indicated a gene set consistency of 89.47%, supporting the overall reliability of the predicted gene models. This result is consistent with expectations for hemiparasitic plants, which may have lost some lineage-specific genes but still retain most core plant genes34,48. In addition to BUSCO assessment, we evaluated the structural properties of the predicted gene models using GFF-derived summary statistics. The annotation comprises 15,709 protein-coding genes and 27,758 transcripts (1.77 transcripts per gene), with a mean CDS length of 1,364.76 bp and an average of 7.06 exons per transcript. The predicted proteome has a mean length of 454.92 amino acids. Moreover, 14,839 representative genes (94.46%) received functional annotation in at least one public database, indicating broad functional support for the predicted gene models.

Functional annotation of protein-coding genes in Taxillus sutchuenensis was performed using multiple approaches. Protein sequences were aligned to NR, Swiss-Prot (sprot), and TrEMBL databases using BLASTP (e-value ≤ 1e-5)49 to identify homologs. Protein domains and family information were annotated via InterProScan50. Functional categories including Gene Ontology (GO), Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG), and KEGG pathways were assigned through eggNOG-mapper based on the eggNOG database. To avoid annotation redundancy caused by multiple transcript isoforms, only the longest isoform of each gene was used as the representative protein sequence for functional annotation, resulting in approximately 15,700 protein sequences. Overall, the majority of predicted proteins (14,839 genes, 94.46%) received functional annotations in at least one database. InterPro showed the highest coverage (93.14%), while KEGG coverage was lower (29.06%), consistent with typical plant genome annotation patterns, this Venn diagram shows the overlap of gene functional annotations across multiple databases, highlighting both shared and uniquely annotated genes (Table 5, Fig. 7).

To comprehensively identify non-coding RNA (ncRNA) genes in the Taxillus sutchuenensis genome, a systematic prediction of rRNA, tRNA, miRNA, snRNA, and other ncRNAs was performed. rRNA genes were predicted using Barrnap v0.9 under the eukaryotic mode, resulting in the identification of 412 rRNA sequences tRNA genes were identified by tRNAscan-SE v2.0.1251 under the eukaryotic model, yielding 318 tRNA genes, of which 302 encode the standard 20 amino acids. Furthermore, Infernal v1.1.452 combined with the Rfam (v14.9)53 database was used to predict other ncRNAs, identifying 63 miRNAs, 1,864 snRNAs, and 1,954 other ncRNA genes.

Technical Validation

To ensure the quality and validity of our data, we implemented rigorous quality control measures during sequencing, assembly, and annotation.

PacBio HiFi sequencing generated 22.65 Gb of long-read data, with an average read length of 19.23 kb. The median read quality reached Q31.3, and 91% of reads exceeded 15 kb, indicating high read accuracy and continuity. Hi-C sequencing produced 135.81 Gb of clean data, with Q20 and Q30 values of 99.15% and 97.39%, respectively, demonstrating high sequencing quality and consistency. In addition, RNA-seq sequencing generated 35.42 Gb of high-quality transcriptomic data from multiple tissues, providing robust support for gene model construction.

The final genome assembly had a total size of 406.32 Mb and was anchored onto nine pseudo-chromosomes, with a scaffold N50 of 45.59 Mb, demonstrating high assembly continuity. BUSCO analysis (embryophyta_odb10) identified 93.6% complete conserved genes (91.0% single-copy and 2.6% duplicated), indicating high completeness. Merqury indicated a k-mer completeness of 95.61% and an overall consensus quality value (QV) of 70.6, with chromosome-level QV values ranging from 66.51 to 74.90, reflecting high base-level accuracy. The LTR Assembly Index (LAI) reached 13.98, supporting strong continuity within repetitive regions. Mapping of PacBio HiFi reads to the assembly showed a mapping rate exceeding 99% across pseudochromosomes, with an average sequencing depth of 40–50 × . Collectively, these results demonstrate that the Taxillus sutchuenensis genome assembly is highly complete, continuous, and accurate.

To further evaluate chromosome-scale structural accuracy, Hi-C interaction statistics were summarized from the filtered BAM file. A total of 202,979,001 Hi-C reads (approximately 101.49 million read pairs) were retained for Hi-C–based chromosome-level structural analysis. Among these, 113,384,856 reads (55.86%) were classified as intra-chromosomal (cis) interactions, whereas 89,594,145 reads (44.14%) corresponded to inter-chromosomal (trans) interactions. Following Hi-C–based chromosome-level analysis, 99.88% of the assembled sequences were successfully anchored and oriented onto nine pseudochromosomes, indicating strong genome-wide structural support from Hi-C data.
