Effectiveness of multi-stage diabetes screening in the dental care setting: a scoping literature review
Authors: M. K. Sandhu, I. L. C. Chapple, R. Al-Falaki, N. Barker, Z. Yonel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetes is of great interest globally, given the associated health and economic costs. It may impact long-term systemic and oral health with unfavourable outcomes when undetected or poorly controlled, leading to various oral manifestations, including premature tooth loss and reduced quality of life. Early detection can enable early diagnosis, intervention and improve treatment and health outcomes.AimsTo synthesise evidence on risk‑targeted screening and early case‑detection protocols for Type 2 Diabetes (T2D) that incorporate HbA1c or glucose point-of-care testing (POCT) using finger‑stick (FSB) or gingival crevicular blood (GCB) with a clinical risk assessment, to ascertain the value and potential impact of chair-side testing if implemented in primary care dental settings.MethodsElectronic databases were searched using a pre-defined strategy, identifying 21 eligible studies; nine studies focused primarily on identifying at-risk individuals through finger-stick testing and twelve through gingival crevicular blood testing. Returned articles and grey literature were screened and used as supportive material for later discussion. A descriptive synthesis of 21 articles was undertaken, with focus of the review on multi-stage chair-side diabetes screening protocols incorporating risk questionnaires and periodontal examinations and HbA1c or glucose POCT testing.ResultsChair-side HbA1c-or glucose POCT may identify potentially new T2D cases. Identification rates were shown to range between 20-66%.ConclusionWhen a POCT is added as part of a two-staged screening protocol, it enhances the ability to identify more cases correctly and is easily integrated into the dental appointment for chair-side screening. Gingival crevicular blood may be used as an alternative blood source; however, the screening method has limitations. Therefore, the most promising solution seems to be a multi-staged predictive model involving tandem risk questionnaires, periodontal examination, and capillary testing with a chair-side POCT.

Background

Diabetes has become a growing global public health concern over the years, driven primarily by rising levels of obesity, sedentary lifestyles and an ageing population [1, 2]. Its prevalence has been described as one of the most challenging health problems of the 21st century [1]. Prevalence figures have been progressively increasing, with an estimated 537 million adults said to be living with the disease. The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) reported that 4.2 million adults aged 20–79 were estimated to have died due to diabetes and its complications in 2019 [3] and 6.7 million in 2021 [2].

Recent projections suggest that more than 1.31 billion people could be living with the disease by 2050 worldwide, with the global rise driven predominantly by increasing rates of T2D [4]. Among the various forms of diabetes, Type 2 (T2D) is by far the most common, accounting for approximately 90% of cases in the UK with the current cost of managing it is said to have reached 10% of the annual health budget [5]. Estimates suggest that 921,100 are unaware that they have T2D [2]. Diabetes is often asymptomatic in its initial stages, non-diabetic hyperglycaemia (NDH) and can remain undiagnosed for many years. Evidence suggests that without intervention, a number of patients will develop diabetes within five years [6]. Those affected are more susceptible to diabetes-related complications, particularly those with metabolic syndrome [7], which in turn negatively impacts the future burden on healthcare services [8]. Prevention is critical to interrupt the progression of NDH to T2D and its early complications, including periodontitis to reduce the long-term burden on healthcare services.

The inter-relationship between periodontitis and diabetes has been long established [7, 9–12], with a greater understanding that prevention and treatment require the involvement of both medical and oral healthcare providers. Recent developments have seen the medical profession recognise the need to close the gap between oral and general health [13–17]. This growing emphasis on preventive care, disease management, and closer interprofessional collaboration has strengthened the drive for oral healthcare clinicians to screen for diabetes risk at the chair-side [18, 19].

Dental professionals screen and assist patients daily with oral health, positively impacting their overall health [20–24]. They may also be well-placed to risk-assess patients for NDH/T2D. Evidence from studies of immune-senescence and inflammaging suggest, that hyperglycaemia induces inflammaging and diabetes-related tissue lesions, including the inflammatory bone loss seen in periodontitis [25–28]. Therefore, risk-targeted screening may support careful and close monitoring of patients in our care and offer an opportunity for early identification of those who are asymptomatic or unaware of their risk of developing future disease, potentially enabling timely intervention and stabilisation of both diseases.

Preliminary screening protocols generally involve a standalone risk questionnaire, a two-stage model that integrates the questionnaire with a periodontal examination, or a multi-stage protocol that concludes with HbA1c-POCT [29–32]. Early detection of abnormal blood glucose levels currently relies primarily on estimating glucose concentration through capillary blood glucose testing [33]. The mechanistic links between periodontitis and diabetes often results in individuals with periodontitis exhibiting gingival inflammation and increased bleeding on probing, reflecting underlying systemic dysregulation [34, 35]. Given that a recognised method of screening for diabetes is from capillary blood collected via a finger-stick test, there may be scope for collecting blood from the gingival tissues for diabetes testing in the dental setting, therefore, making screening at the chair-side using finger-stick blood (FSB) or gingival crevicular blood (GCB) of great interest and relevance to the review as it is pertinent to use in general practice.

Thus, this scoping literature review aims to synthesise the evidence on preliminary screening for T2D using HbA1c or glucometric readings in conjunction with a clinical dental protocol to ascertain the value and potential impact of chair-side POCT if implemented in the primary care dental setting. The literature reviewed had a particular focus on the number of cases identified through a chair-side screening protocol when an HbA1c or glucose POCT was added to the screening protocol and whether blood elicited from the gingival sulcus upon periodontal probing can be used as an alternative source to identify dysglycemia at the chair-side.

Methodology

The pre-specified search strategy was implemented for publications between 1998 and 2023. An initial search using EBSCOhost was conducted for publications between 1998 and 2018 (including PubMed, CINAHL) and Google Scholar and other electronic databases and webpages between 1998 and 2023. The preliminary Boolean search ranged from 1998 to 2018 and resulted in 74,595 papers when (MeSH) terms “Diabetes AND Screening AND Dental” were used and 21,701 when MeSH terms Diabetes Screening AND Periodontal Disease were used.

The MeSH terms such as “Diabetes Screening” AND “Periodontal Disease” OR “Diabetes Screening” AND “Periodontitis” OR “Diabetes Mellitus Screening” AND “Periodontal Disease” OR “Diabetes Mellitus Screening” AND “Periodontitis” were searched, filters were added with limiters set on search dates from 1998 to 2018, English language which further resulted in 3013 papers (Table 1). The titles and abstracts of the returned articles were assessed against pre-specified eligibility criteria to ensure that those selected were best suited for comprehensive analysis. Additional searches included manual /electronic searches of websites and a manual search of journals and literature reference lists. Duplicates were eliminated from the set, and those that were dissimilar in the screening method or which did not meet the eligibility criteria. Returned articles and grey literature were screened and used as supportive material for later discussion.

Overall, the search strategy produced a wide range of published articles which were reported according to the “Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses” (PRISMA) guidelines (Fig. 1). The articles that met inclusion were those which undertook a prior risk assessment for NDH/T2D; used HbA1c or glucose POCT in screening protocol; based in a clinical dental setting; the subjects were adults ≥ 18 years of age; and those written in English. Articles that did not undertake a prior risk assessment; did not use blood sample for POCT, non-dental setting; and of non-English language were excluded from the set.

Eligible papers were assessed and analysed using the “Critical Appraisal Skills Programme” (CASP)- critical appraisal tool and further synthesised and consolidated towards this review. Nine studies that primarily focused on identifying at-risk individuals through a capillary blood sample elicited through finger puncture and 12 studies that tested blood from the gingival sulcus are included in this review.

Results

Twenty-one studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the review (Fig. 1). The characteristics of the included studies are summarised in Table 2. The results are presented in the synthesis tables (Tables 3 and 4), organised thematically.

Nine studies reported FSB testing of which six used a protocol which consisted of a tandem risk questionnaire, periodontal examination and a capillary finger-stick measurement [29, 30, 36, 38, 39, 42]. Three studies used a two-step protocol excluding the periodontal examination, of which one used periodontal disease risk questions in a self-reported risk questionnaire [40] and two excluded periodontal parameters altogether [37, 41]. Across all nine studies, FSB samples were collected chair-side in the dental setting for the POCT-based screening [29, 30, 36–42]. To evaluate sensitivity and specificity, five studies in the FSB group [29, 30, 37, 38, 42] also obtained venous blood (VB) samples for definitive laboratory analysis.

All twelve GCB studies used a protocol that consisted of a periodontal examination and a POCT test with GCB and FSB or VB samples [20, 33, 43–52]. Ten of the twelve GCB studies compared GCB to FSB samples [20, 33, 43–46, 49–52] while two compared GCB to VB [47, 48]. In all twelve studies, GCB samples were collected chair-side in the dental setting for the POCT-based screening [20, 33, 43–52]. In addition, two studies, within the GCB group [45, 51] also acquired VB samples for definitive laboratory confirmation.

The combined samples recruited amongst the selected studies in the FSB group were 4465 of which 3594 subjects (80.4%) were found to be at potential risk and put forward for testing [29, 30, 36–42] and 1299 subjects in the GCB test group [20, 33, 43–52].

The identification rates across the FSB group ranged between 20.4 and 65.6% of the studies assessing the HbA1c or glucose POCT screening protocol [30, 36–42].

Across the correlation studies (shown in Table 4), statistically significant positive correlations were identified between the comparators of GCB to FSB and GCB to VB. Eight studies reported highly significant correlations when GCB was compared with FSB samples [20, 33, 43, 44, 49–52]. In addition, a highly significant correlation was observed between GCB and VB samples [47]. One study also reported a strong and significant correlation between GCB and FSB [46]. Two of the twelve studies showed a near-perfect correlation [44, 49]. Three studies showed high sensitivity and specificity of the GCB sample measurements obtained by glucometer when a confirmatory laboratory test was conducted [20, 45, 48]. There was no significant difference between the two blood sources when used for chair-side testing to identify potential prediabetes and diabetes cases, irrespective of whether the capillary blood is sourced from the finger or gingival sulcus [33, 43, 46, 49]; anterior cubital vein or gingival sulcus upon analysis [47, 48].

Discussion

The reviewed literature was consistent with the six existing systematic reviews [53–58] and, more recently, a large study in a German population which reported significantly positive predictive values when a dentally focused, targeted-risk protocol was used to identify those at risk of NDH or undiagnosed diabetes [59].

Chair-side screening could help healthcare professionals intervene in early case detection of NDH/ T2D, enabling close monitoring and tighter glucose control of the periodontally compromised patient living with NDH/ T2D. Intervention studies have shown that the treatment of periodontitis results in a greater reduction of systemic inflammatory markers and improves glycaemic control of periodontitis patients with diabetes, which may significantly improve health outcomes [60]. Given the close correlation between the two diseases, chair-side screening may offer an opportunity for early detection of individuals at risk and enable timely and appropriate treatment and stabilisation of both diseases.

The concept of screening for diabetes in a dental setting using predictive models and a multi-disciplinary approach to care has gained interest in recent years. Various screening methods have been explored over the years for diabetes screening/case detection in dental care settings. Many of these have been too complex, creating challenges relating to implementation and integration into the dental appointment or not being conducive to testing in primary care dental settings, for example, fasting plasma glucose testing [29, 37], saliva testing [61–63] or urine testing [64]. Alternative, and more suitable chair-side tests have been studied further, particularly the HbA1c or glucose POCT.

The present literature reviewed searched for a multi-staged predictive model in tandem with a HbA1c or glucose POCT. The predictive model consisted of a risk questionnaire and a periodontal examination to stratify those in greatest need, followed by an HbA1c or glucose POCT for those exceeding the model threshold. This approach seemingly balances risk and cost, with only those at greatest risk (determined by risk questionnaires) receiving the more expensive POCT. It is reported that for diabetes-dental screening, ‘population-based screening’ where all would receive a POCT is time-consuming and uneconomical [65, 66]; therefore, population-based screening is not advocated for dental settings, but rather a two-phased risk-targeted approach is preferred.

The literature, however, highlighted the benefits of chair-side screening and demonstrated how a multi-staged risk assessment process integrated with a simple POCT using an HbA1c monitor could enhance the predictability of risk assessments in dental settings [20, 29, 30, 33, 36–52]. Although HbA1c or glucose POCT monitors can be used alone, when added to such a screening protocol, they were shown to enhance the identification of cases [29, 30, 36–42]. Several authors support this, including Barasch et al. [67], Vadakkekuttical et al. [68] and Lalla and colleagues, who found sensitivity of the testing process to increase from 72% to 92% when the HbA1c-POCT was added to the protocol compared to risk questionnaires alone or of protocols combined with a risk questionnaire and a periodontal examination [29, 30]. The literature demonstrated that self-monitoring devices achieved identification rates ranging from 20 to 66% (mean 42%) [29, 30, 36–42]. Similarly, and consistent with this Parker et al. [69] and Friman et al. [70] found identification rates of 40%, which also successfully correlated with definitive medical testing.

Overall, the literature demonstrated the HbA1c-glucose monitors as sensitive and reliable tools that provide consistent results that correlate well with laboratory methods [29, 30, 36–38, 42, 43, 46–49, 51, 71]. They are more suitable and easier to integrate into dental appointments than other tests, lending further to their credibility for diabetes-dental screening.

Whilst the efficacy has been established, it has been reported that patients can often be psychologically opposed to receiving finger-stick POCT, reporting finger-prick anxiety, general anxiety and stress [33, 72]. Studies suggest patients can be apprehensive about the pain associated with the lancet used for the finger puncture and, therefore, more amenable to less invasive techniques [20, 43, 46, 47, 73]. Consequently, alternative, minimally invasive strategies for sourcing blood for chair-side screening have been explored, notably utilising blood from the gingival crevice.

The literature reviewed demonstrated that GCB can be used for testing blood glucose during periodontal examination [33, 45, 46, 51, 52]. The profuse bleeding elicited from the gingival sulcus when undertaking periodontal examination in those with active periodontitis, may provide a ‘good source’ of blood for use in chair-side testing [33, 43–45, 47–52]. Also, GCB is an acceptable [20, 44], reliable source to screen for diabetes in periodontal disease populations [48], described by several authors as an excellent source of blood for glucometric analysis [43, 46, 47, 50].

The technique for GCB collection during diagnostic periodontal examination is described in the literature as a convenient screening tool, with the ability to perform a test at the chair-side that is safe, easy to perform, and comfortable for the patient [51] and more acceptable than alternative invasive methods [43, 46]. Its rapidity and extensive availability provided greater patient acceptance, as the collection was quick and easy, as demonstrated by Rosedale and Strauss [73] and Sande and colleagues [74]. Its simplicity, cheapness, and minimum disturbance for individuals [33, 43, 46] make it a suitable alternative source for HbA1c or glucometric measurement. Nevertheless, this phenomenon has not been without controversy.

Previous studies found that although GCB was a convenient source for glucometric testing in the initial phase, it was less suitable for the monitoring phase as tissue improvements seen following periodontal therapy meant adequate blood samples from the gingival sulcus would not be available, potentially impeding the glucometric monitoring, observed by Strauss and colleagues [20].

However, literature has since found that with developments in glucose monitors, second generation monitors are now more sensitive and able to elicit results with much smaller blood quantities [43, 45, 47, 51]. In fact, Yared et al. [75] found that most new glucose monitors achieve glucose level estimation with as little as 1–3 µL GCB, as opposed to 10–15µL of blood volumes, found in studies with first generation monitors [69, 75].

Further, Parker and colleagues suggested laboratory glucose values obtained by measuring venous blood to be closer to capillary blood values taken from the gingival sulcus rather than blood taken from the finger [69]. They explained that as blood travels from the capillary to the venous circulation, the blood glucose values reduce by approximately 3.5 mg/dL due to glycolysis bringing finger blood glucose values down, further validating its efficacy in detecting dysglycemia.

Understandably, this method has some limitations, such as the risk of blood sample contamination, as seen in studies by Khader et al. [44] and Strauss et al. [20]. Khader and colleagues reported lower readings in the GCB group, attributing this to the potential contamination of gingival blood samples with plaque and dilution from saliva or gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) [44]. Although an increase in inflammation in the periodontal tissues is often seen in those with dysglycemia, evidence suggests an increase in GCF levels caused by the inflammatory changes are seen to be greater when compared to non-diabetics with the same level of periodontal disease [51]. Hence, contamination of the blood sample by GCF would have been attributed to the sample being diluted with increased levels of GCF at the collection site.

Similarly, Strauss et al. [20] reported that contamination occurred when samples contacted oral tissues, debris, saliva, bacteria, or remnants of a pre-procedural mouth rinse. The risk increased when GCB was collected from posterior or mandibular sites due to insufficient bleeding in the routine anterior maxillary area. Such challenges observed with insufficient blood volumes can impede intra-oral blood sample collection at the chair-side, particularly in cases with diabetic patients with good glucose control or those without periodontitis who are unlikely to have sufficient crevicular bleeding. In patients undergoing maintenance therapy, where there has been successful resolution of gingival and periodontal inflammation, blood sources are less likely to exhibit bleeding, similar to those with a positive smoking history. Hence, it was noted by Strauss et al. [20], who included subjects under maintenance therapy, that some participants could not provide adequate bleeding for the GCB sample, with only 1/3rd of the tip on the collection card reported being covered and found by the laboratory to be inadequate for testing .

Further limitations include the risk of potential damage to the test strip. Shetty et al. [46] found that rubbing or direct wiping of intra-oral blood from the gingival tissues onto the test strip to cause damage to the strip’s chemical indicator surface.

Furthermore, several studies used random blood glucose measurements with glucometers for blood glucose testing [33, 43–52]. Glucometers do not measure HbA1c and, therefore, only provide a measure of blood glucose at a given point in time rather than the 3-month period that HbA1c monitors reflect. However, it is noteworthy that the POCT was intended for screening rather than diagnosis, with those found at true risk being sent for confirmatory testing.

Including a POCT may improve the specificity of testing, ensuring those referred to the medical practitioner for definitive diagnostic testing are accurately identified and at true risk [76].

Risk-targeted screening could be a successful and cost-effective method for identifying disease prevalence, provided there is closer interprofessional working and two-way feedback addressing the patient’s metabolic control and periodontal health status [22, 31, 32, 77–79]. Evidence suggests that screening for diabetes by oral healthcare providers can only be effective if those requiring medical evaluation follow-up with diagnostic testing with the medical practitioner [39]. Several studies highlighted a lack of patient-physician follow-up when a confirmatory test was required [29, 30, 36–39, 41].

Nevertheless, the reviewed literature showed the positive impact of early identification of hyperglycaemia in the dental setting through chair-side screening when an HbA1c or glucose POCT is added to a screening protocol.

Conclusion

The evidence reviewed indicates that while there are some limitations, dysglycemia in periodontally compromised patients can be identified through chair-side HbA1c or glucose screening protocols. The evidence demonstrates the benefits and challenges of chair-side screening. However, the cost saving of screening and early intervention compared to the long-term costs associated with managing diabetes-related complications would seem to outweigh the limitations [5]. Thus, the most promising solution seems to be a multi-staged predictive model involving tandem risk questionnaires, periodontal examination, and capillary testing. Of course, more robust, and larger-scale studies employing large sample sizes, externally validated questionnaires, and subsequent capillary HbA1c or glucose POCT for those identified as being at high-risk are essential if cost-effective care pathways are to be developed in the future. Nevertheless, to date, the literature seems promising.
