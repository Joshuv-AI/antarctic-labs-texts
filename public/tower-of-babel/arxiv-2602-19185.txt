Some effective operators for graphene monolayer superlattices, from variational perturbation theory
Louis Garrigue

Abstract
. Our goal is to provide precise effective operators for monolayer graphene at Fermi energy. We consider the microscopic potential created by a lattice, and add a macroscopic potential with the same periodicity but varying at a scale ε − 1 ∈ ℕ \varepsilon^{-1}\in\mathbb{N} , creating a superlattice. Our approach consists in coupling the variational approximation, perturbation theory together with a multiscale method. At the effective level the usual massless Dirac operator is replaced by other operators, and we provide simulations in the case of graphene.



1.
Introduction

In this document, we consider semiclassical Schrödinger operators of the form

(1)

\displaystyle h^{\varepsilon}:=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(-i\nabla+\varepsilon A(\varepsilon x)\right)^{2}+v(x)+\varepsilon V(\varepsilon x),

We will be interested in the states at the interface between filled and unfilled bands, because they are the main ones determining the physical behavior of quantum systems. In particular, this applies to graphene considered as a one-body model like in DFT.

In

2.
Introduction of the setting

In this section we introduce the objects that will be manipulated, most importantly we present the scaled exact operator.

2.1.
The lattice

We consider the two-dimensional space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
instead of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
in which graphene is embedded, but everything can be extended to the
\mathbb{R}^{3}
situation. Let us consider
a_{0}>0
,
k_{\rm D}:=\frac{4\pi}{3a_{0}}
, and the graphene lattice vectors

(2)

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle:=a_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\\
-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad

\displaystyle a_{2}:=-a_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\\
\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\end{pmatrix},

(3)

\displaystyle a_{1}^{*}

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{3}k_{\rm D}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\\
-\frac{1}{2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad

\displaystyle a_{2}^{*}:=-\sqrt{3}k_{\rm D}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\\
\frac{1}{2}\end{pmatrix}.

We define the action of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
on
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
as
na:=n_{1}a_{1}+n_{2}a_{2}
and similarly
ma^{*}:=m_{1}a^{*}_{1}+m_{2}a^{*}_{2}
.
The direct lattice is
\mathbb{L}:=\{na\;|\;n\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\}
, the dual one is thus
\mathbb{L}^{*}:=\{ma^{*}\;|\;m\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\}
. The fundamental cell is
\Omega:=\{xa\;|\;x\in[0,1]^{2}\}
and we denote by
\mathcal{B}
the first Brillouin zone. We will consider the Dirac point
K:=-\tfrac{1}{3}(a_{1}^{*}+a_{2}^{*})\in\mathcal{B}
, a high-symmetry point.

2.2.
Scaling

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any
q\in[1,+\infty[
, we define the set of periodic operators

\displaystyle L^{q}_{\rm per}(n\Omega):=\left\{f\in L^{q}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\;|\;\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\forall y\in\mathbb{L},f(x+ny)=f(x)\right\}.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any function
f,g\in L^{2}_{\rm per}(\varepsilon^{-1}\Omega)
, we define
\left<f,g\right>_{L_{\rm per}^{2}(n\Omega)}:=\int_{n\Omega}\overline{f}g
. Moreover, we will use mainly

\displaystyle\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>:=\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{L^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega)}.

We define the scaling operator
Q
on functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle(Qf)(x):=f\left(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}\right),

its inverse is
\left(Q^{-1}f\right)(x)=f(\varepsilon x)
. We have
Q:L^{2}_{\rm per}(\varepsilon^{-1}\Omega)\rightarrow L^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
and for
\varepsilon\in 1/\mathbb{N}:=\{1/n,n\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{0\}\}
,
\left<Qf,Qg\right>_{L_{\rm per}^{2}(\Omega)}=\varepsilon^{2}\left<f,g\right>_{L_{\rm per}^{2}(\varepsilon^{-1}\Omega)}
so in those spaces,
Q^{*}=\varepsilon^{2}Q^{-1}
.

2.3.
Symmetries of the potentials

Let us define the rotation matrix

\displaystyle R_{\theta}:=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},

and its corresponding action
(\mathcal{R}_{\theta}f)(x):=f(R_{-\theta}x)
on functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. We consider potentials with honeycomb symmetry, which is defined in
[
36
, Definition 2.1]
. They are potentials
v\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, with
p>1
, such that

(4)

\displaystyle v\in L^{p}_{\rm per}(\Omega),\qquad v(-x)=v(x),\qquad\mathcal{R}_{\frac{2\pi}{3}}v=v,\qquad v(x_{1},-x_{2})=v(x_{1},x_{2}).

As for the potentials
V
and
A
, we only assume that
V,A\in L^{p}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
but for simulations of Section
5
we will choose
A=0
and
V
respecting the symmetries (
4
).

2.4.
The scaled exact operator

2.4.1.
The microscopic exact problem

Let us denote by
(E^{q}_{m},u^{q}_{m})\in\mathbb{R}\times H^{1}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
the Bloch eigenpairs of

\displaystyle h_{q}:=\tfrac{1}{2}(-i\nabla_{q})^{2}+v,\qquad\mathbb{L}-\text{periodic},

which is bounded from below, for
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
q\in\mathcal{B}
, sorted such that
E^{q}_{m}
is increasing with
m
. Hence

(5)

\displaystyle h_{q}u^{q}_{m}=E^{q}_{m}u^{q}_{m}.

We assume that there is
m_{\rm F}\in\mathbb{N}
such that the dispersion relation of
k\mapsto h_{k}
has a conical intersection at the Dirac point
k=K
at the level
m_{\rm F}
. This situation is generic, as proved in
[
36
, Theorem 5.1]
. In particular, this implies that
E^{K}_{m_{\rm F}}=E^{K}_{m_{\rm F}+1}=:E_{\rm F}
, which we call the Fermi level. We also assume that this level is exactly 2-fold degenerate, so
E^{K}_{m_{\rm F}-1}<E^{K}_{m_{\rm F}}=E^{K}_{m_{\rm F}+1}<E^{K}_{m_{\rm F}+2}
.

Let us define

(6)

\displaystyle w_{1}:=u^{K}_{m_{\rm F}},\qquad\qquad w_{2}:=u^{K}_{m_{\rm F}+1}

such that
\left|\!\left|w_{1}\right|\!\right|_{L^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega)}=\left|\!\left|w_{2}\right|\!\right|_{L^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega)}=1
and such that with

(7)

\displaystyle\phi_{a}(x):=e^{iKx}w_{a}(x),

we have

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\frac{2\pi}{3}}\phi_{a}=\omega^{a}\phi_{a},\qquad\qquad\text{for all }a\in\{1,2\},

where
\omega:=e^{i\frac{2\pi}{3}}
, see
[
36
]
. The functions
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
are the Bloch states at the Dirac point and Fermi level, see
[
36
]
for more details about the symmetries of those Bloch states.

2.4.2.
The exact operator

We want to study the operator
h^{\varepsilon}=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+v+\varepsilon Q^{-1}V
defined in (
1
), which is
\varepsilon^{-1}\mathbb{L}
-periodic. For any
q\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we define
\nabla_{q}:=e^{-iqx}\nabla e^{iqx}
where
e^{iqx}
is considered as a multiplication operator, so
\nabla_{q}=\nabla+iq
and
-i\nabla_{q}=-i\nabla+q
. Instead of
h^{\varepsilon}
, we work on its Bloch transform, acting on
L^{2}_{\rm per}(\varepsilon^{-1}\Omega)
and given by

(9)

\displaystyle h^{\varepsilon}_{q}=e^{-iqx}h^{\varepsilon}e^{iqx}=\tfrac{1}{2}(-i\nabla_{q}+\varepsilon Q^{-1}A)^{2}+v+\varepsilon Q^{-1}V.

We have
\nabla_{q}Q=\varepsilon^{-1}Q\nabla_{\varepsilon q}
so
\varepsilon Q^{-1}\nabla_{\varepsilon^{-1}q}=\nabla_{q}Q^{-1}
,
(-i\nabla_{q}+Q^{-1}A)^{2}Q^{-1}=\varepsilon^{2}Q^{-1}\left(-i\nabla_{\varepsilon^{-1}q}+A\right)^{2}
and instead of
h^{\varepsilon}_{q}
we work on its rescaled (and hence semiclassical) version
Qh^{\varepsilon}_{q}Q^{-1}
. We center the study of
h^{\varepsilon}
around the Fermi level, which we assume to be at a the Dirac point
K
, presenting a conical intersection in the dispersion relation, see
[
36
]
for a precise definition. Hence we work on

(10)

\displaystyle H^{\varepsilon}_{k}:=Q\left(h^{\varepsilon}_{K+\varepsilon k}-E_{\rm F}\right)Q^{-1}=\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\left(-i\nabla_{\varepsilon^{-1}K+k}+A\right)^{2}+Qv+\varepsilon V-E_{\rm F},

which acts on
L^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
. The two operations “scaling” and “Bloch transform” commute, hence the order in which we apply them does not matter.

2.4.3.
Exact eigenvectors when
V=0

Let us denote by
H^{\varepsilon,V=0}_{k}
the operator
H^{\varepsilon}_{k}
with
V=0
. In this case, its band diagram is simply a folding of the band diagram of
-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+v
, (see
[
55
,
33
,
62
,
73
]
for the folding/unfolding operations) and the eigenmodes of
H^{\varepsilon,V=0}_{k}
can be deduced from the ones of
-\frac{1}{2}(-i\nabla_{p})+v
. Let use analyze the branches starting at the Dirac point. We compute that
H^{\varepsilon,V=0}_{k}Qu^{K+\varepsilon k}_{m}=(E^{K+\varepsilon k}_{m}-E_{\rm F})Qu^{K+\varepsilon k}_{m}
, so
\left(E^{K+\varepsilon k}_{m}-E_{\rm F},Qu^{K+\varepsilon k}_{m}\right)_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
are eigenmodes for
H^{\varepsilon,V=0}_{k}
. Moreover if
V
is considered as a perturbation, the eigenmodes of
H^{\varepsilon,V=0}_{k}
are smoothly deformed to the ones of
H^{\varepsilon}_{k}
.

3.
The general effective operators

In this section we present effective operators for two-scales graphene, in a general way. More specific choices will be made in Section
4
.

3.1.
Two-scales reduced basis

Let us consider a family
\mathcal{F}
of
M
linearly independent and periodic functions
\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{M}\in H^{1}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
,

(11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}:=\left(\psi_{j}\right)_{1\leqslant j\leqslant M}.

This family will impose the behaviors of eigenfunctions of the reduced space at the microscopic scale. They potentially depend on the momentum
k
, but not on
\varepsilon
. As in
[
21
]
, we then build the reduced vector space

(12)

\displaystyle\left\{\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{M}\alpha_{j}(x)\psi_{j}\left(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\;|\;\alpha_{j}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{\rm per}(\Omega),j\in\{1,\dots,M\}\right\},

which is the two-scale space “spanned” by the space of microscopic functions of
\mathcal{F}
when the macroscopic behavior is let free. Efficient families will

•

have
M
small so the resulting effective model will be “computable”,

•

choose
\psi_{j}
in a way so that the resulting effective operator will be accurate.

We then define the operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}:\hskip 14.22636pt\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbb{C}^{M}\otimes\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{\rm per}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})&\longrightarrow&H^{1}_{\rm per}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})\\
\alpha&\longmapsto&\sum_{j=1}^{M}\alpha_{j}(x)\psi_{j}\left(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}\right),\end{array}

which enables to go from envelop functions to real-space functions.

3.2.
Effective operator

We study the action of the exact operator
H^{\varepsilon}_{k}
in the projected space (
12
). We want to associate it a low-dimensional model which will reproduce the Fermi eigenmodes with precision. Let us define

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle:=\left(\left<\psi_{a},\left(h_{k}-E_{\rm F}\right)\psi_{b}\right>\right)_{1\leqslant a,b\leqslant M},\qquad\qquad\mathcal{S}:=\left(\left<\psi_{a},\psi_{b}\right>\right)_{1\leqslant a,b\leqslant M}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\mathcal{L}:=\left(\left<\psi_{a},(-i\nabla_{K})\psi_{b}\right>\right)_{1\leqslant a,b\leqslant M},

where the coefficients of
\mathcal{L}
belong to
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Those
M\times M
matrices are all hermitian,
\mathcal{S}
is a Gram matrix, so since
\mathcal{F}
is composed of linearly independent functions,
\mathcal{S}
is strictly positive. Then an effective model for
H^{\varepsilon}_{k}
is

(13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{\varepsilon}_{k}:=\varepsilon^{-1}\mathcal{M}\otimes{\mathds{1}}+\mathcal{L}\cdot\otimes(-i\nabla_{k}+A)+\mathcal{S}\otimes\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon(-i\nabla_{k}+A)^{2}+V\right)

as an operator of
\mathbb{C}^{M}\otimes\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
, where the only spatial dependence exists via
V
and
A
. A formal derivation of (
13
) is provided by the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1

(Weak convergence)
.

Take
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}^{M}\otimes\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
, and
V,A\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
. For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
C_{N}>0
such that for any
\varepsilon\in]0,1[\cap(1/\mathbb{N})
,

\displaystyle\left|\varepsilon^{-1}\left|\Omega\right|\left<\mathcal{J}\alpha,H^{\varepsilon}_{k}\mathcal{J}\beta\right>_{L^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})}-\left<\alpha,\mathbb{H}^{\varepsilon}_{k}\beta\right>_{\mathbb{C}^{M}\otimes L^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})}\right|\leqslant C_{N}\varepsilon^{N}.

A proof is given in Section
6
. The eigenvalue equation for
\mathbb{H}^{\varepsilon}_{k}
is then

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{\varepsilon}_{k}\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{k}=\mathbb{E}^{\varepsilon}_{k}\mathcal{S}\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{k},

where the generalized eigenpairs of
\mathbb{H}^{\varepsilon}_{k}
will be denoted by
\left(\mathbb{E}^{\varepsilon}_{k},\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{k}\right)
. When
\mathcal{F}
is well chosen, the operator
\mathbb{H}^{\varepsilon}_{k}
spectrally well approximates the exact operator
H^{\varepsilon}_{k}
in the following sense : if
V
and
A
are small enough and if
\left(\mathbb{E}^{\varepsilon}_{k},\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{k}\right)
is a generalized eigenpair of
\mathbb{H}^{\varepsilon}_{k}
, then there exists an eigenpair
\left(\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}_{k},\Phi^{\varepsilon}_{k}\right)
of
H^{\varepsilon}_{k}
such that

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-1}\left(\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}_{k}-\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}_{0}\right)\simeq\mathbb{E}^{\varepsilon}_{k}-\mathbb{E}^{\varepsilon}_{0},\qquad\qquad\Phi^{\varepsilon}_{k}\simeq\mathcal{J}\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{k}\textup{ up to a phase factor}.

The maps
(k,V)\mapsto\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}_{k}
and
(k,V)\mapsto\mathbb{E}^{\varepsilon}_{k}
are locally smooth away from eigenvalue crossings. So to detect which level of the effective model corresponds to which one of the exact model, we start by identifying the eigenmodes of
H^{\varepsilon,V=0}_{0}
and
\mathbb{H}^{\varepsilon,V=0}_{0}
(see Section
2.4.3
), which eigenvalues then vanish, and then we let
k
and
V
change smoothly to see the deformations of eigenvalues and keep track of them even at crossings.

Remark 3.2

(Undo the Bloch transform)
.

When the coefficient matrices
\mathcal{M},\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{S}
do not depend on
k
, the operator in real space can be written as the inverse of the Bloch transform
\mathbb{H}^{\varepsilon}=e^{ikx}\mathbb{H}^{\varepsilon}_{k}e^{-ikx}
and does not depend on
k
.

Remark 3.3

(Effective operators are defined for any
\varepsilon>0
)
.

While the exact operator
H^{\varepsilon}_{k}
is only defined for
\varepsilon\in 1/\mathbb{N}
, the effective operators
\mathbb{H}^{\varepsilon}_{k}
are defined for any
\varepsilon>0
. Moreover, the computation cost of the effective operators is essentially constant in
\varepsilon
while it was exponentially increasing with
1/\varepsilon
in the exact model.

3.3.
First simplification

We recall the Pauli matrices

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{2}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{3}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

In our choices of
\mathcal{F}
, for any
k
we will always have
\psi_{a}=w_{a}
for
a\in\{1,2\}
, where
w_{a}
are the Bloch states at the point
K
, defined in Section
2.4.1
. We will also always have
w_{a}\perp\psi_{b}
for
a\in\{1,2\}
and
b\in\{3,\dots,M\}
. Hence the operators
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{S}
can be simplified as

(14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&M\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathcal{L}=\begin{pmatrix}v_{\rm F}\sigma&T\\
T^{*}&L\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathcal{S}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathds{1}}&0\\
0&S\end{pmatrix}.

where

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle:=\left(\left<\psi_{a+2},\psi_{b+2}\right>\right)_{1\leqslant a,b\leqslant M-2},\quad M:=\left(\left<\psi_{a+2},\left(h_{k}-E_{\rm F}\right)\psi_{b+2}\right>\right)_{1\leqslant a,b\leqslant M-2}

(15)

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle:=\left(\left<\psi_{a},(-i\nabla_{K})\psi_{b+2}\right>\right)_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a\leqslant 2\\
1\leqslant b\leqslant M-2\end{subarray}},L:=\left(\left<\psi_{a+2},(-i\nabla_{K})\psi_{b+2}\right>\right)_{1\leqslant a,b\leqslant M-2}.

Moreover,
v_{\rm F}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is defined via
\left<w_{1},(-i\nabla_{K})w_{2}\right>=v_{\rm F}\left(1,-i\right)^{T}
, see
[
21
, Appendix I]
.

From now on we take

4.1.
Preliminary information about the unscaled system

In this section, we present particular choices for the families

4.1.1.
A family of Bloch eigenmodes with special symmetry

From

,
10
]
, the symmetries of

presented in Section

imply that

(16)

\displaystyle\left<w_{a},(-i\nabla_{K})w_{a}\right>=0,\;a\in\{1,2\},\quad\qquad\left<w_{1},(-i\nabla_{K})w_{2}\right>=v_{\rm F}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
-i\end{smallmatrix}\right).

Those computations are enough to derive the effective massless Dirac operator
v_{\rm F}\sigma\cdot(-i\nabla)
from
-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+v
where

has honeycomb symmetry.

4.1.2.
Pseudo-inverse

Let us denote by
P
the orthogonal projector onto the eigenspace
\Ker\left(h_{K}-E_{\rm F}\right)=\Span\left(w_{1},w_{2}\right)
, and
P_{\perp}:=1-P
. An important quantity which we will need is the pseudo-inverse

\displaystyle R:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left((E_{\rm F}-h_{K})_{\mkern 1.0mu\vrule height=6.02777pt\mkern 2.0muP_{\perp}L^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega)\rightarrow P_{\perp}L^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega)}\right)^{-1}&\mbox{on }P_{\perp}L^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega)\\
0&\mbox{on }PL^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega)\end{array}\right.

extended on
L^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
by linearity, so
(E_{\rm F}-h_{K})R=R(E_{\rm F}-h_{K})=P_{\perp}
.

4.2.
Variational perturbation theory : Dirac modes and their derivatives up to order
\ell

The first way of building families
\mathcal{F}
comes from variational perturbation theory
[
66
,
39
]
, which was analyzed mathematically in
[
40
]
. It consists in building a variational space using the derivatives of the eigenfunctions. Here we build
\mathcal{F}
using
u^{q}_{m_{\rm F}}
and their derivatives with respect to
q
. In Appendix
B
we present how one can differentiate those eigenstates with respect to
q

4.9.
Error and limit of high
M

Let us denote by
P_{\mathcal{J}}
the orthogonal projection onto
\im\mathcal{J}
, and
P_{\mathcal{J}}^{\perp}:=1-P_{\mathcal{J}}
. We recall that we denoted by
\Phi^{\varepsilon}_{k}
the eigenvector of the exact operator
H^{\varepsilon}_{k}
defined in (
10
). As presented in
[
2
, Chapter II, Section 8, (8.46)]
or in
[
40
, Proposition 3.2]
, the error between
\Phi^{\varepsilon}_{k}
and the aproximation
\mathcal{J}\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{k}
(produced by our effective operators) is controlled by
P_{\mathcal{J}}^{\perp}\Phi^{\varepsilon}_{k}
. Hence, usually, the larger
\im\mathcal{J}
the smaller
P_{\mathcal{J}}^{\perp}\Phi^{\varepsilon}_{k}
and the smaller the error
\left|\!\left|\Phi^{\varepsilon}_{k}-\mathcal{J}\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{k}\right|\!\right|_{L^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega)}
. So making
\mathcal{F}
larger improves the approximation but produces effective operators that are more expensive to deal with.

When
M
is large, one can expect that formally,
\im\mathcal{J}
becomes close to

\displaystyle\left\{\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{M}\alpha_{j}(x)\varphi_{j}\left(\tfrac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\;|\;\alpha_{j}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{\rm per}(\Omega),\varphi_{j}\in H^{1}_{\rm per}(\Omega),j\in\{1,\dots,M\}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{\rm per}(\Omega)\otimes H^{1}_{\rm per}(\Omega),

and all approximations become equivalent. Hence in the larger
M
limit, using excited states or derivatives no longer matters.

4.10.
Schur reduction to a
2\times 2
matrix-valued operator

We recall the formalism of Schur’s reduction in Appendix
C
. We formally apply it to the effective operator
\mathbb{H}^{\varepsilon}_{k}
, where the Schur projection
P_{2}
is the projection onto the first two components of
\mathbb{C}^{M}
, the computations are detailed in Section
C.1
. We define the
2\times 2
matrix-valued operator

(28)

\mathcal{H}^{\varepsilon}_{k}:={\mathds{1}}\otimes V+v_{\rm F}\sigma\otimes\cdot(-i\nabla_{k})\\
+\varepsilon{\mathds{1}}\otimes\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(-i\nabla_{k})^{2}-T\cdot(-i\nabla_{k})(M^{-1}T^{*})\cdot(-i\nabla_{k})\right),

acting on
\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes L^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
, where more precisely, for any
i\in\{1,2\}
and any
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes L^{2}_{\rm per}(\Omega)
,

\left(T\cdot(-i\nabla_{k})(M^{-1}T^{*})\cdot(-i\nabla_{k})\psi\right)_{i}\\
=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a,b,j\leqslant 2\end{subarray}}\left(T^{a}M^{-1}\big(T^{b}\big)^{*}\right)_{ij}\left(-i\nabla_{k}\right)_{a}\left(-i\nabla_{k}\right)_{b}\psi_{j}.

The operator
\mathcal{H}^{\varepsilon}_{k}
is similar to the one derived in
[
21
]
, but is has a corrected second-order differential operator. It is the operator
\mathbb{H}^{\varepsilon}_{k}
on which we applied a Schur reduction, as explained in Appendix
C
. Hence we “reduced” the operator
\mathbb{H}^{\varepsilon}_{k}
, which had divergences, to
\mathcal{H}^{\varepsilon}_{k}
, which has no divergence. A drawback of the Schur reductions is that one needs to apply an operator to the eigenvectors of
\mathcal{H}^{\varepsilon}_{k}
to obtain an approximation of the exact eigenvectors, see Appendix
C
.

5.7.
Conclusion

The goal of this document was to obtain accurate effective operators for the Bloch eigenmodes of the two-scales exact Schrödinger operator (
1
). The derivation of effective operators by using variational perturbation theory led to several models, depending on the perturbation order and on the momentum dependence.

Here are the main conclusions brought by the simulations.

•

The precision given by the massless Dirac operator can be significantly improved to approach the eigenmodes of the exact Bloch operator, as we saw on Figures
4
and
5
;

•

The cut-off
\nu
needs to be well-chosen to avoid spectral pollution of the effective models. We can in general choose
\nu=2
, as explained in Section
5.2
;

•

We are not retaining the
k

\mathcal{F}_{1,k}
and
\mathcal{F}_{2,k}
because of non-smooth behaviors and lack of accuracy seen in Figures
4
,
5
and
8
;

•

As seen in Section
5.6
, the effective operator
\mathcal{F}_{1}
built with perturbative (with respect to the momentum) vectors at first order has most of the time better precision than the effective operator
\mathcal{F}^{6}
coming from adding eigenstates at
K
, and hence we recover the same kind of conclusions as in
[
40
, Section 6.3]
.

We believe that the use of the proposed effective operators could be of interest in some applications to model low-energy Dirac fermions, when precision is needed.

Avoiding the use of degenerate perturbation theory with eigenstates, we can use perturbation theory with density matrices, which is much simpler when the goal is only to generate the space spanned by perturbative eigenvectors. See

Appendix C
Schur reduction

In this section, we quickly recall the principle of the Schur reduction
[
79
,
78
]
, at a formal level. Consider two self-adjoint operators
H
and
Y
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, take an orthogonal projection
\mathbb{P}
onto
\mathcal{H}
, define
\mathbb{P}^{\perp}:=1-\mathbb{P}
, and assume that
\mathbb{P}Y\mathbb{P}^{\perp}=0
. Let us denote by
(E,\phi)
a non-degenerate solution of the generalized eigenvalue problem associated to
H
and
Y
, so
H\phi=EY\phi
, and
Y
is called the mass operator. Then

(70)

\displaystyle(H-EY)\mathbb{P}\phi+(H-EY)\mathbb{P}^{\perp}\phi=0.

Applying
\mathbb{P}^{\perp}
gives
\mathbb{P}^{\perp}(EY-H)\mathbb{P}^{\perp}\phi=\mathbb{P}^{\perp}H\mathbb{P}\phi
. Assume that there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\displaystyle\sigma\left(\left(\mathbb{P}^{\perp}(EY-H)\mathbb{P}^{\perp}\right)_{\mkern 1.0mu\vrule height=6.02777pt\mkern 2.0mu\mathbb{P}^{\perp}\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{\perp}\mathcal{H}}\right)\cap\;]-\varepsilon,\varepsilon[=\varnothing,

and define the partial inverse

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mbox{on }\mathbb{P}\mathcal{H},\\
\left(\left(\mathbb{P}^{\perp}(EY-H)\mathbb{P}^{\perp}\right)_{\mkern 1.0mu\vrule height=6.02777pt\mkern 2.0mu\mathbb{P}^{\perp}\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{\perp}\mathcal{H}}\right)^{-1}_{\perp}&\mbox{on }\mathbb{P}^{\perp}\mathcal{H},\end{array}\right.

extended by linearity on all of
\mathcal{H}
. We have
r\mathbb{P}^{\perp}(EY-H)\mathbb{P}^{\perp}=\mathbb{P}^{\perp}
so
\mathbb{P}^{\perp}\phi=rH\mathbb{P}\phi
. Applying
\mathbb{P}
to (
70
) and using
1=\mathbb{P}+\mathbb{P}^{\perp}
gives

(71)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(H+HrH-EY\right)\mathbb{P}\phi=0,\qquad\qquad\phi=(1+rH)\mathbb{P}\phi.

This set of equations has to be seen in the following way : one of the generalized eigenmodes of the effective operator
\mathbb{P}\left(H+HrH\right)\mathbb{P}
(with mass operator
Y
) is
(E,\mathbb{P}\phi)
, so once one has obtained it, one can obtain
\phi
by applying
1+rH
to
\mathbb{P}\phi
. Thus, solving
\mathbb{P}\left(H+HrH\right)\mathbb{P}
with mass operator
Y
leads to solve the exact operator
H
with mass operator
Y
.
