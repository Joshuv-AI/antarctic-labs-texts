Background

Oculocutaneous albinism (OCA) is a group of rare, autosomal recessive disorders caused by congenital melanin deficiency, which are characterized by the complete absence or reduction of melanin in the skin, hair, and eye. The main manifestations of the eyes are congenital nystagmus, hypopigmentation of the iris and fundus, and macular fovea dysplasia [1–4]. Currently, there are few reports on OCA combined with secondary angle closure, and no case reports about the genetic testing for these patients. This study includes a Chinese family with OCA , in which the subject of this case study showed OCA and secondary angle closure. We aim to analyze the genotype and phenotype of the patient and review the recent relevant literature to enhance our understanding of the disease. In addition, we hope to provide some insights and experiences to assist other ophthalmologists in managing similar patients.

Case presentation

Clinical findings

A 53-year-old Chinese male patient presented to ophthalmic clinic with decreased visual acuity in both eyes for 6 months and worsened during 3 months. Owing to insufficient understanding of the disease, the patient had not received regular treatment before. He had a family history of albinism for over 50 years and was the sixth child of nonconsanguineous parents. Among his five siblings, one sister also had albinism, while the other four siblings did not. Their parents had passed away, and the patient complained that they had no clinical manifestations of albinism (the family tree is presented in Fig. 1).

The family tree. This family included two patients (II:2 and II:6). Patient II:6 is the proband

Through general physical examinations, we found that the proband had normal intelligence and developmental history. He had the typical manifestation of albinism, which was white skin and hair over the whole body. His neck was symmetrical in appearance and normal in movement, the thyroid gland was not palpable, and there were no obvious positive signs in the heart, lungs, abdomen, surrounding blood vessels, musculoskeletal system, reproductive system, and nervous system.

The ophthalmologic examinations showed his visual acuity was light perception (LP) in the right eye and 0.08 in the left eye. Following a week of the administration of carteolol drops at the initial visit in our hospital, the intraocular pressure (IOP) was 22.3 mmHg in the right and 24.4 mmHg in the left with an air puff tonometer. Slit lamp examination showed the white palpebral skin and eyelashes with nystagmus in both eyes. The ocular alignment was orthotropic and the eye movement was normal. There was no obvious congestion or edema in the conjunctiva of both eyes. The cornea of the right eye was transparent, with a central anterior chamber depth of about 3 corneal thickness (CT) and a peripheral anterior chamber depth of about one-fourth of the CT. The pupil was round, with a diameter of about 3 mm and sensitive to light reflection. The iris was widely depigmented, and the lens appeared opaque and swollen, leading to poor visibility of the fundus. The cornea of the left eye was transparent with a normal anterior chamber depth and slightly cloudy lens. The fundus in the left eye showed widespread depigmentation of the retina and choroid, and the choroidal blood vessels could be seen (Fig. 2). Optical coherence tomography (OCT) showed the refractive interstitial opacity in the right eye and no signal in the macular area and macular fovea hypoplasia in the left eye (Fig. 3). Ultrasonic biological microscope (UBM) showed the chamber angle was narrow, and the anterior chamber was shallow in the right eye, with the central anterior chamber depth of 1.81 mm and central corneal thickness of 520 μm, while the chamber angle of the left eye was open in all quadrants, and the depth of central anterior chamber was 2.71 mm and central corneal thickness of 525 μm (Fig. 4). The axial length was 23.85 mm in the right eye and 26.51 mm in the left eye, and the thickness of the lens was 5.62 mm in the right eye and 3.88 mm in the left eye measured by B-scan ultrasonography. Spotted and clustered echoes can be seen in the vitreouses of both eyes, with high activity. No obvious abnormalities observed in the retina and orbit (Fig. 5). Gonioscopy was not performed.

Genetic findings

To further investigate the genetic pathogenesis of the family, the whole blood samples were collected from three subjects (patients II:2, II:6, and healthy member III:2), after obtaining written informed consent from all subjects. The genomic DNA for whole-exome sequencing (WES) was extracted, and then, bioinformatics analysis was performed on the sequencing results. On the basis of the ACMG genetic variation classification standards and guidelines, suspicious pathogenic variants were screened and validated by Sanger sequencing.

The study found the two patients, II:2 and II:6, harbored compound heterozygous variants c.230G > A (p. Arg77Gln) and c. 832G > A (p. Arg278*) in the TYR gene. Both of these variants were a heterozygous variant. The healthy member III:2 carried the heterozygous variant c.230G > A (p. Arg77Gln) in TYR gene. The Sanger sequencing results are shown in Fig. 6. The genetic pattern of this family was consistent with the autosomal recessive inheritance pattern.

Diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up results

On the basis of the above genotypic and phenotypic results, the proband was diagnosed with OCA, senile mature cataract, secondary angle closure in the right eye, and ocular hypertension in the left eye, as well as bilateral nystagmus. At the initial visit, the patient was prescribed carteolol eye drops twice daily to control IOP. Medication was adjusted based on follow-up IOP conditions. Simultaneously, relevant preoperative examinations were conducted to exclude any surgical contraindications. Under local anesthesia, the patient underwent phacoemulsification and intraocular lens implantation surgery for the right eye.

The patient underwent regular follow-up after surgery and demonstrated good adherence to the treatment plan. On the first postoperative day, the visual acuity improved to 0.08 with an IOP of 18.3 mmHg in the right eye, while the visual acuity was 0.08 with an IOP of 24.7 mmHg in the left eye. We prescribed him bromonidine eye drops twice daily in combination with carteolol eye drops twice daily to control the IOP of the left eye. Slit lamp examination showed mild conjunctival congestion, transparent cornea, normal anterior chamber depth, round pupil with a diameter of approximately 3 mm and sensitive to light reflection, widespread depigmentation of the iris, and well-positioned intraocular lens. Fundus examination revealed widespread depigmentation of the retina and choroid and the choroidal blood vessels. One week after surgery, the visual acuity was 0.1, and the IOP was 19.6 mmHg in the right eye and 18.5 mmHg in the left eye. One month after surgery, the visual acuity was 0.12, and the IOP was 18.1 mmHg in the right eye and 19.1 mmHg in the left eye; the anterior segment and fundus examination findings remained consistent with the previous assessment.

Discussion

OCA is an autosomal recessive inherited disorder characterized by reduced or complete absence of melanin synthesis in the skin, hair, and eyes. The prevalence is around 1 in 20,000 to 1 in 10,000 individuals [1–4]. The main clinical features are characterized by infantile-onset nystagmus, photophobia, visual impairment, hypopigmentation of the iris and fundus, dysplastic macular fovea, refractive errors, and sometimes a degree of color vision impairment, as well as depigmentation of the skin and hair [1]. There are limited reports on the coexistence of OCA and ocular hypertension or angle closure, most of these articles focused on the treatment of these patients. For example, R.A. Catalano described an infant with oculocutaneous albinism and congenital glaucoma [5]. A.D. Dotson reported a case of unsuccessful transscleral cyclophotocoagulation in an oculocutaneous albinism patient [6]. D. Pásztor reported the management of a patient with oculocutaneous albinism, keratoconus, total cataract, and glaucoma. These articles all did not report the genetic results of these patients [7]. In this study, we collected a Chinese OCA pedigree with two affected members, focused on the clinical and genetic examination results of the patient, and provided recommendations for ophthalmologists to treat patients with OCA in their clinical practice. The study findings indicate that the proband showed secondary angle closure, and genetic testing revealed he harbored compound heterozygous variants in the TYR gene.

The detailed ophthalmic and systemic examinations showed the proband had the depigmentation of the skin, hair, iris, and fundus, accompanied with photophobia, decreased visual acuity, elevated IOP, nystagmus, dysplastic macular fovea, and cataract; these clinical manifestations were similar to previously reported patients with OCA[8]. However, the proband presented with secondary angle closure due to the opacity and swelling of lens, and there were few related reports about this. OCA can be associated with two types of angle-closure glaucoma: primary angle-closure glaucoma and secondary angle-closure glaucoma [5, 7]. Primary angle-closure glaucoma primarily occurs in patients with OCA with anterior segment dysgenesis (ASD) [9], which involves congenital maldevelopment in various anterior segment structures such as the cornea, iris, lens, and anterior chamber angle, and owing to the dysplasia of the aqueous outflow structures originating from neural crest cells, approximately 50% of these patients develop glaucoma. Astigmatism was the most common visually significant refractive error in albinism [10], and the proportion of hyperopia and myopia were similar [11]. Secondary angle-closure glaucoma often occurs in patients with OCA with hyperopia and astigmatism [10, 12]. Patients with hyperopia commonly present with a shorter axial length, and, as they age, the thickness of the lens increases. This combination can result in a shallower anterior chamber and a narrower angle, ultimately leading to the development of angle-closure glaucoma. In the study, the patient had an axial length of 23.85 mm in the right eye and 26.51 mm in the left eye. The thickness of the lens was measured as 5.62 mm in the right eye and 3.88 mm in the left eye. These findings indicate that the pathogenic mechanism of angle closure in the right eye of this patient was primarily due to lens thickening, resulting in an increased lens-to-eye volume ratio. This, in turn, led to crowding of the anterior segment, resulting in angle narrowing and, subsequently, developing into secondary angle closure. Therefore, we performed phacoemulsification combined with intraocular lens implantation. After the surgery, the patient’s anterior chamber deepened, the angle opened up, and IOP was well controlled. This case highlights the importance for ophthalmologists to carefully examine various structures in the anterior segment, including the anterior chamber and angle, when evaluating patients with OCA. Detailed ophthalmic examination, especially examination of the anterior segment, can identify the cause of angle closure and contribute to make appropriate and proper treatment measures. For patients above the age of 40 years, it is crucial to be vigilant about the possibility of secondary angle closure caused by lens opacity and thickening. In terms of treatment, it is necessary to identify the underlying cause of angle closure to provide appropriate and effective management.

OCA is a genetically heterogeneous disorder with autosomal recessive inheritance. It presents with a variety of clinical manifestations and can be classified into syndromic and nonsyndromic types based on the involvement of other organ systems. Syndromic OCA is often associated with systemic abnormalities including immunodeficiency, pulmonary fibrosis, hematological abnormalities, neurological impairments, and so on. Two examples of syndromic OCA are Hermansky–Pudlak syndrome (HPS) and Chediak–Higashi syndrome (CHS) [4]. HPS encompasses 11 subtypes, namely HPS1-11, which are caused by variants in the HPS1, AP3B1, HPS3, HPS4, HPS5, HPS6, DTNBP1, BLOC1S3, BLOC1S6, AP3D1, and BLOC1S5 genes [13–17], and the pathogenic gene for CHS is LYST [18, 19]. Patients with HPS and CHS can exhibit similar clinical manifestations, therefore, genetic testing can contribute to an accurate diagnosis. Nonsyndromic OCA can be further classified into seven types, which are caused by variants in the TYR, OCA2, TYRP1, SLC45A2, SLC24A5, LRMDA, and DCT genes, respectively [20–24]. Among these subtypes, OCA1 is the most common subtype and can be divided into OCA1A and OCA1B. Different subtypes have some overlap or similar phenotypes. The overlap of phenotypes makes it difficult to accurately classify and diagnose solely on the basis of clinical manifestations [1, 25]. For instance, patients with OCA1A showed a complete absence of pigment in the hair, eyes, and skin. The phenotypes of OCA1B and OCA2 are typically somewhat less severe than OCA1A, and the two subtypes show considerable clinical overlap [26]. The above findings emphasize the importance of molecular diagnosis in accurate diagnosis and classification. Furthermore, identifying pathogenic genes can provide better genetic counseling for patients. Carrier detection and prenatal diagnosis are possible when the disease-causing mutations have been identified in the family.

Through WES and bioinformatics analysis, we found that the two patients harbored compound heterozygous variants c.230G > A (p. Arg77Gln) and c. 832G > A (p. Arg278*) in the TYR gene. One healthy member carried the heterozygous variant c.230G > A (p. Arg77Gln) in TYR gene. These results were consistent with the autosomal recessive inheritance pattern and further confirmed the diagnosis of the patients was OCA1. These findings showed genetic testing can be used for precise diagnosis of genetically heterogeneous disorder with overlapping clinical manifestations, which can enable clinicians to provide precise genetic counseling, prognostic information, and personalized treatment strategies for patients. A limitation of this study is that owing to the deceased parents and geographical distance, it was not possible to obtain phenotypic and genotypic information of the proband’s parents and siblings.

Conclusion

We report a patient with OCA combined with cataract and secondary angle-closure, and WES suggested that he harbored TYR gene variants. Comprehensive examinations were important for identifying the causes of angle-closure and making proper treatment strategies. Genetic testing enabled precise diagnosis and genetic counseling. Additionally, genetic screening contributes to the accurate diagnosis, follow-up, and genetic counseling.
