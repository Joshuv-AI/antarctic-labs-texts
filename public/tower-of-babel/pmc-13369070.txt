Surgeon Perceptions and Experiences with Postoperative Acute Pain Management: A Mixed-Methods Study
Authors: Vinod Dasa, Scott J. Keating, Sushanth Jeyakumar, K. D. Jacobs, Lauren A. Crowder, Sloan Rucker, Alexandra J. Feld, Moeen Panni, Ann M. Menzie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAlthough prior studies have examined healthcare providers’ perspectives on opioid prescribing for postoperative pain, limited research exists on surgeons’ experiences managing acute postoperative pain in adults. This study evaluated surgeons’ perceptions and experiences to better understand challenges in managing acute pain and assess perceived impacts of inadequately managed pain on patients.MethodsA sequential mixed methods study was conducted using concept elicitation interviews and a descriptive quantitative survey, with a convenience sample of United States (US)-based general, orthopedic, and plastic surgeons. Interview findings identified key themes related to surgeons’ perceptions of challenges and barriers associated with managing postoperative acute pain. These themes informed the development of a survey to quantify surgeons’ unmet needs, challenges, and experiences regarding postoperative pain management.ResultsKey themes from the interviews (N = 30) included challenges with effective pain management, advantages and disadvantages of commonly used pain medications, and difficulties managing pain in special populations. Among surveyed surgeons (N = 80), the most frequently reported challenges were managing patient pain management expectations (72.5%) and balancing adequate analgesic effect with risk of adverse events (71.3%). Approximately half (53.8%) were “moderately satisfied” with pain medications available during the study period, identifying the most important medication characteristics as the degree and duration of analgesic effect and time to onset. Most surgeons (80.0%) reported that concerns about opioid addiction or dependency “moderately” to “extremely impacted” their prescribing patterns. Additionally, 65% of surgeons indicated that inadequately managed postoperative pain has a moderate to extreme impact on patient recovery.ConclusionsSurgeon experiences with postoperative pain management emphasize challenges and unmet needs surrounding acute pain medications and highlight surgeons’ perceptions of the negative impacts of inadequately managed acute pain on patients.

Introduction

Approximately 64 million surgical procedures are conducted in the United States (US) annually [1]. Postoperative acute pain is common, with nearly 86% of patients reporting postoperative pain and up to 75% of whom experienced moderate-to-severe pain [2]. A 2023 meta-analysis further reported that as many as 58% of postoperative patients experience moderate-to-severe acute pain lasting at least 1–2 weeks following hospital discharge [3]. While some degree of discomfort is expected during recovery, postoperative acute pain is frequently suboptimally controlled [4]. Inadequately managed postoperative pain, defined as persistent moderate-to-extreme pain following surgery that is not effectively controlled by treatment, may be associated with prolonged opioid use, increased morbidity, delayed recovery, decreased quality of life (QoL), and greater healthcare resource utilization and costs [4].

Postoperative acute pain is managed using a range of pharmacologic and non-pharmacologic approaches. Common pharmacologic treatments include opioids, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), acetaminophen, and gabapentinoids [5]. Interventional and procedural approaches such as regional anesthesia, peripheral nerve blocks, epidural techniques, and trigger point injections may also be used. In addition, non-pharmacologic strategies, including physical modalities (e.g., heat or ice therapy), rehabilitation, and complementary approaches, are often incorporated into care plans. These diverse strategies are frequently integrated within multimodal analgesia frameworks, which combine treatments with differing mechanisms of action to optimize pain control while minimizing medication-related risks [6]. Multimodal analgesia is a cornerstone of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols, which aim to reduce opioid exposure, accelerate recovery, shorten hospital length of stay, and improve postoperative outcomes [5].

Despite advances in multimodal care, managing postoperative acute pain remains complex. Opioid and non-opioid analgesics are associated with adverse events (AEs), such as nausea, vomiting, ileus, and respiratory depression, while opioids uniquely carry risks of misuse, addiction, and dependence [7–9]. A US-based survey reported that 79% of patients treated for postoperative pain experienced at least one medication-related AE [2]. Clinicians may therefore face challenges balancing analgesic effectiveness with safety considerations [4–6].

Inadequately controlled postoperative acute pain has been associated with delayed recovery, impaired physical and mental functioning, sleep disturbances, reduced productivity, and diminished QoL [4, 11–13]. Poor pain control may also be associated with prolonged opioid use and increased risk of dependence and opioid use disorder, as well as the development of chronic postoperative pain [4, 14, 15]. In addition, inadequately managed pain may contribute to extended hospital stays, increased readmissions, and higher healthcare costs [6, 7].

Postoperative acute pain management is inherently multidisciplinary and may involve surgeons, anesthesiologists, regional anesthesia teams, pain specialists, nurses, rehabilitation providers, and other clinicians. However, surgeons play a central role across the perioperative continuum, including setting expectations with patients, prescribing discharge analgesics, monitoring recovery-related pain concerns, and addressing pain-related barriers to rehabilitation and return to function. Despite this important role, limited quantitative evidence exists describing surgeons’ contemporary experiences and perspectives regarding postoperative acute pain management.

This study aimed to explore surgeons' perspectives and experiences regarding postoperative acute pain management. Specifically, the objectives were to (1) characterize surgeons’ self-reported approaches to postoperative acute pain management and the challenges and barriers they encounter, and (2) identify surgeon-perceived unmet needs and patient impacts associated with inadequately managed postoperative acute pain using qualitative and quantitative methods.

Methods

This exploratory sequential mixed methods study integrated qualitative and quantitative approaches to provide both depth and breadth of insight into surgeon experiences [16, 17]. Semi-structured qualitative interviews were first conducted to elicit themes related to postoperative acute pain management. Findings from the qualitative phase informed the development of a quantitative survey designed to measure the frequency and distribution of surgeon-reported experiences and perceptions.

Surgeon Recruitment

Eligible participants specialized in general, orthopedic, or plastic surgery, were actively involved in managing patients’ postoperative acute pain, and regularly performed major surgical procedures in an operating room setting. Surgeons were required to spend ≥ 50% of working hours in a clinical setting and/or performing clinical tasks to ensure inclusion of primarily patient-facing clinicians. Additional eligibility criteria included an active US medical license, ≥ 3 years of surgical experience following completion of a medical residency or fellowship program, an average caseload comprising ≥ 50% adult patients, and fluency in English.

Surgeons were recruited through established physician panels and databases. The qualitative phase used purposive sampling to recruit up to 30 surgeons (ten per specialty) to ensure diversity of perspectives [18]. The quantitative phase aimed to recruit up to 80 surgeons with representation across specialties (i.e., general, orthopedic, and plastic surgeons; n = 20–30 per group) and soft demographic quotas for sex (≤ 80% male) and race and/or ethnicity (≤ 80% white) aligned with population-based demographic estimates [19–22]. Due to the sequential study design, recruitment efforts for the two phases were performed separately.

Study Procedures

Qualitative interviews were conducted in January and February 2024, and the quantitative survey was administered in August 2024.

All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standard of the WCG Institutional Review Board (IRB) and the Helsinki Declaration of the World Medical Association. The qualitative study was reviewed by SCG IRB and determined exempt (#1365464). The quantitative study was reviewed and approved by WCG IRB (#1377343).

Qualitative participants were provided with a study information sheet and gave verbal informed consent, including consent to audio recording and publication of de-identified findings, prior to interview initiation. Quantitative participants provided electronic informed consent prior to survey participation.

Data from both phases were de-identified to protect participant privacy and confidentiality.

Qualitative Phase

Sixty-minute semi-structured interviews explored surgeons’ clinical background and experience (e.g., types and volume of surgical procedures and typical patient population), pain management approaches, perceived challenges, and perceptions of patient impacts associated with inadequate pain control. Open-ended questions allowed additional probing into different aspects of surgeons’ responses to encourage both elaboration and clarification when necessary. Audio-recordings of interviews were transcribed verbatim and coded using deductive and inductive approaches, addressing pre-identified concepts while capturing new concepts emerging from the data [23]. Two trained researchers independently coded an initial subset of transcripts to assess coding consistency. Discrepancies were reviewed and resolved through discussion until a consensus was reached. Concept saturation was evaluated by grouping interviews chronologically; no new major concepts emerged after the fourth of six sets, indicating adequate thematic coverage [24] and that the sample size was sufficient to meet the study objectives.

Quantitative Phase

Survey items were developed using themes identified in the qualitative phase. The instrument underwent internal multidisciplinary review to assess clinical relevance, clarity, and programming functionality prior to fielding. Formal pilot testing and cognitive interviews were not conducted.

The online survey contained 50 items to quantify surgeons’ clinical practice characteristics, challenges in postoperative acute pain management, perceived patient impacts, and attitudes toward analgesic therapies. Surgeons were also asked to rank the importance of medication characteristics from “most important” (a value of 1) to “least important” (a value of 7). For other survey items, surgeons were presented with a specific medication type (e.g., NSAID, opioid) and asked to select their level of either concern around medication-related AEs or comfort prescribing the medication to specific comorbid patient populations. Response options consisted of the following Likert-scale: “not at all” (a value of 1); “slightly” (a value of 2); “moderately” (a value of 3); “very” (a value of 4); and “extremely” (a value of 5). Additional demographic and clinical characteristics were captured via eligibility screening to characterize surgical specialization, sex, and race/ethnicity. Survey responses were collected through a secure electronic data capture system. All survey items were required. Two respondents initiated but did not complete the survey and were excluded from the analysis. No item-level missing data were present among completed surveys.

Analyses

Qualitative interview data used thematic analysis to identify patterns in surgeon responses concerning the relevant and important elements of managing patients’ acute pain, including surgeons’ experiences managing acute pain in adults and perceptions of patient-experienced impacts related to inadequate pain management. Quantitative survey data were analyzed using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were summarized using means, standard deviations (SD), medians, interquartile ranges (IQR), and ranges. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Given that the study was not powered for statistical comparisons, inferential analyses, including statistical comparisons between subgroups, were not performed. Qualitative data were coded and analyzed using NVivo 14 (Lumivero, LLC., Denver, CO, USA), and quantitative analyses were conducted using SAS® (Version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Advantages and Disadvantages of Acute Pain Medications

Surgeons outlined their perceptions of the advantages and disadvantages of commonly used prescription acute pain medications available during the study period, including opioids, NSAIDs, and acetaminophen (Table 3). The most frequently reported advantage of opioid medications in postoperative acute pain management was analgesic effectiveness. In contrast, the most commonly reported disadvantage of opioids was the potential for AEs, which was spontaneously reported by 24 (80.0%) surgeons. AEs of concern included constipation or paralytic ileus, drowsiness, sedation or altered cognition, nausea, vomiting, and respiratory depression or overdose. When describing NSAIDs, the most identified advantage was reduced pain and inflammation, and surgeons valued NSAIDs as a non-opioid, non-addictive treatment option. Risk of potential AEs was a commonly reported disadvantage of NSAIDs, most often including gastrointestinal (GI) upset, gastric bleeding, renal failure, and increased bleed risk. Surgeon-Perceived Impacts of Inadequate Postoperative Pain Management

Nearly all surgeons described patient-related impacts associated with inadequately managed postoperative acute pain. Based on their clinical experience, participants indicated that insufficient pain control may negatively affect postoperative recovery, including delays in ambulation and slower recovery.

Surgeons also described perceived effects on patients’ physical and emotional functioning, including sleep disruption, reduced ability to perform activities of daily living, mood changes, and difficulty returning to work or normal productivity. These findings reflect surgeon-reported perceptions and clinical experiences rather than objectively measured patient outcomes.

Quantitative Phase

Surgeon Characteristics

A total of 80 surgeons completed the survey, with comparable proportions specializing in general (n = 29, 36.3%), orthopedic (n = 27, 33.8%), and plastic surgery (n = 24, 30.0%). Most surgeons were male (n = 64, 80.0%) and white (n = 58, 72.5%). Surgeons were similarly distributed across US Census regions; the largest proportion reported practicing medicine in the South (n = 29, 36.3%). Following completion of a residency or fellowship, surgeons had a median of 17.0 years (range 3.0–40.0 years) of experience performing surgical procedures in an operating room-based setting (Table 1).

Nearly two-thirds of surgeons (n = 52, 65.0%) reported perceiving “moderate” to “extreme” patient-related impacts associated with inadequately managed postoperative acute pain. Discussion

This mixed-methods study provides surgeon-reported perspectives on the challenges of managing postoperative acute pain across diverse surgical specialties. Surgeons described postoperative pain management as a complex clinical responsibility requiring careful balancing of analgesic effectiveness, safety risks, patient expectations, and administrative demands. Across both qualitative and quantitative phases, participants reported frequent use of multimodal analgesia strategies, reflecting contemporary efforts to reduce reliance on opioids while optimizing pain control.

Medication-Related Challenges and Trade-offs

Surgeons identified substantial concerns regarding AEs associated with commonly used analgesics. Consistent with existing literature, opioids were valued for their analgesic effectiveness but raised significant safety concerns. In a systematic review by Wheeler et al. (2002), up to 30% of postoperative patients experienced GI AEs (e.g., nausea or vomiting), while central nervous system effects (e.g., somnolence, dizziness) and pruritus were also common [9]. Notably, less than 1% of patients experienced respiratory depression, yet this remained the most frequently cited concern among surgeons, with 66.3% reporting being “very” or “extremely concerned” about this AE. This disconnect between statistical frequency and clinical concern may reflect the severity and potential lethality of respiratory depression, influencing prescribing behavior even when the absolute risk is low.

NSAIDs were similarly recognized for their anti-inflammatory and analgesic properties, yet prescribing was often limited by safety concerns in at-risk populations. Evidence indicates that NSAIDs are associated with upper GI complications and acute renal failure, particularly among patients with comorbidities [25–27]. Surgeons in this study reported being “not at all comfortable” prescribing NSAIDs to patients with GI bleeding or ulcers (41.3%), kidney or liver disease (21.3%), or bleeding disorders (18.8%). Although acetaminophen is commonly used and generally well tolerated, it has been found to provide only moderate analgesic efficacy compared with NSAIDs [28]. These findings underscore the challenges surgeons face in weighing the clinical benefits of available analgesics against the risks of potential AEs, particularly in medically complex patients.

Multimodal Analgesia: Adoption and Limitations

The widespread adoption of multimodal analgesia strategies, reported by 85.0% of surveyed surgeons as “often” or “always” used, reflects efforts to optimize pain control while minimizing reliance on any single medication class. Multimodal approaches are intended to limit opioid consumption and reduce opioid-related AEs while achieving adequate analgesia [10]. However, our findings suggest that even within multimodal frameworks, surgeons perceive limitations. Over half (53.8%) reported being only “moderately satisfied” with available prescription pain medications, and 71.3% identified balancing adequate analgesic effect with AE risk as a primary challenge.

It is important to acknowledge that multimodal analgesia encompasses more than oral prescription medications. Regional anesthesia techniques, nerve blocks, and structured ERAS pathways represent critical components of comprehensive pain management strategies. While some surgeons in this study mentioned these modalities, they were not the primary focus of participant responses. The present study’s focus on oral prescription analgesics reflects the scope of our research and does not diminish the importance of technique-based and systems-level approaches to postoperative pain management.

Potential complications associated with multimodal therapy, such as polypharmacy risks, drug-drug interactions, or the challenge of tailoring regimens to individual patient profiles, may contribute to suboptimal outcomes. Successfully implementing multimodal analgesia requires coordination across care teams, individualized patient assessments, and ongoing monitoring.

Opioid-Specific Concerns: Addiction Risk and Administrative Burden

Eighty percent of surveyed surgeons reported that their opioid prescribing preferences were “moderately” to “extremely impacted” by concerns about addiction or dependency, and 23.8% indicated being “not at all comfortable” prescribing opioids to patients with current or prior substance use behaviors. These findings reflect heightened awareness of the opioid crisis and the potential for even short-term prescriptions to contribute to long-term opioid use. A US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) analysis by Shah et al. demonstrated that even brief opioid prescriptions are associated with increased risk of long-term use, underscoring the clinical rationale for cautious prescribing [29].

However, efforts to mitigate addiction risk through restrictive prescribing must be balanced against the need to provide adequate postoperative analgesia. Under-prescribing or premature discontinuation of opioids may result in inadequately managed pain, which carries risks for delayed recovery, chronic pain development, and diminished QoL [4].

Surgeons also described substantial administrative burdens associated with opioid prescribing, including compliance with PDMPs, drug enforcement agency oversight, electronic prescribing requirements, and two-factor authentication. Surveyed surgeons reported spending a median of 5.0 min per prescription on such tasks. With a median of ten opioid prescriptions per week, this equates to approximately 50 min weekly, or an estimated 43.3 h annually per surgeon. While this reflects individual-level burden, the cumulative effect across surgical departments and healthcare systems, where advanced practice providers and other prescribers also manage opioid prescriptions, may substantially reduce productivity and divert resources from direct patient care.

It is important to note that these administrative challenges are largely specific to the US healthcare system. Regulatory frameworks, insurance policies, and prescribing monitoring requirements vary widely across countries, and the generalizability of these findings to non-US settings may be limited.

Surgeon-Perceived Impacts of Inadequately Managed Pain

Sixty-five percent of surgeons indicated that inadequately managed postoperative pain has a moderate to extreme impact on the recovery process, with delays in ambulation, extended hospitalization, and limited participation in physical therapy identified as key consequences. It is important to emphasize that these findings reflect surgeon perceptions rather than objective patient outcome data. Nevertheless, they align with evidence from the broader literature indicating that poorly controlled postoperative pain is associated with negative impacts on physical functioning, recovery trajectories, and health-related quality of life [4, 30].

Approximately half of surgeons (47.5%) also perceived that inadequately managed pain negatively impacts patients’ sleep, mental health, and ability to return to work. These multidimensional consequences underscore the clinical significance of effective pain management not only for immediate postoperative comfort but also for longer-term functional and psychosocial outcomes.

The alignment between surgeon-reported perceptions and published evidence suggests that clinicians are attuned to the real-world impacts of pain on their patients, even in the absence of formal outcome measurements within routine clinical practice. However, these findings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of causal relationships or generalized inadequacy of current pain management strategies. Rather, they highlight areas where surgeons perceive room for improvement and where targeted interventions, whether pharmacologic, procedural, organizational, or educational, may enhance patient outcomes.

Patient Communication and Expectation Management

Surgeons identified addressing patient expectations as a leading challenge (72.5%) and one of the most burdensome aspects of postoperative pain management (25.0%). Qualitative interviews revealed that some patients expect complete pain relief, while clinical reality often involves managing pain to tolerable levels rather than eliminating it entirely. Misalignment between patient expectations and achievable outcomes can lead to dissatisfaction and potentially increased analgesic demands.

Effective preoperative counseling and shared decision-making are essential to establishing realistic expectations. However, time constraints, communication barriers, and variability in patient health literacy may limit the effectiveness of these conversations. Additionally, surgeons described challenges in assessing pain in patients whose self-reported pain scores appeared incongruent with clinical examination findings or observed behaviors. These complexities highlight the need for improved communication strategies, patient education tools, and potentially interdisciplinary involvement (e.g., pain specialists, psychologists) to support both patients and surgeons in navigating postoperative pain management.

Heterogeneity Among Surgeons and the Need for Further Research

While this study provides valuable insights into surgeon perspectives, the findings may not capture the full spectrum of variability in clinical practice. Surgeons varied by specialty (general, orthopedic, and plastic surgery), years of experience (range 3–45 years post-training), and geographic region; however, the descriptive analysis precluded formal subgroup comparisons. Surgical specialty, patient populations, institutional resources, and prescribing philosophies may meaningfully influence pain management approaches. For example, orthopedic surgeons managing major joint replacements may have different concerns regarding NSAIDs and regional anesthesia compared to plastic surgeons performing elective procedures. Similarly, surgeons in academic centers with dedicated acute pain services may have different experiences than those in community settings with fewer resources. Future research should explore these sources of heterogeneity through stratified analyses to better understand how context shapes pain management practices and to identify targeted strategies for addressing specialty-specific and setting-specific challenges.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, participation was voluntary and may reflect selection bias, as surgeons with a greater interest in pain management may have been more likely to enroll. Second, the sample was predominantly male and white, which may limit generalizability, although demographic distributions were broadly consistent with the US surgical workforce.

All findings were based on self-reported perceptions and are subject to recall and response biases. The study did not include patient-level clinical outcomes; therefore, findings cannot determine the objective adequacy of pain management practices. However, the mixed-methods design enabled triangulation of in-depth qualitative insights with broader quantitative patterns. The study used descriptive analyses and was not powered to detect differences across specialties, regions, or practice settings. Postoperative pain care is multidisciplinary, yet this study focused exclusively on surgeons to address an identified evidence gap; perspectives from anesthesiologists, pain specialists, nurses, and rehabilitation providers were not captured. Finally, several identified challenges, particularly administrative burdens, are specific to the US healthcare system and may not generalize to international populations. Despite these limitations, the mixed-methods approach provides a comprehensive and contemporary characterization of surgeon-reported experiences in postoperative pain management.

Conclusions

This mixed-methods study reveals challenges that surgeons face when managing postoperative acute pain despite widespread adoption of multimodal analgesia. Key difficulties include balancing analgesic effectiveness with safety risks, aligning treatment with patient expectations, and navigating administrative requirements related to opioid prescribing. Surgeons perceive that inadequately controlled pain adversely affects recovery, mobility, participation in rehabilitation activities, sleep, and return to normal functioning, including work. Although perception-based, these findings align with broader clinical evidence highlighting the consequences of suboptimal pain control. Addressing these challenges will likely require multifaceted strategies, including the development of analgesics with improved safety and efficacy profiles, expanded access to regional anesthesia and ERAS-based approaches, streamlining prescribing workflows, and stronger multidisciplinary coordination. Continued efforts to optimize postoperative pain management may improve recovery trajectories and reduce the functional burden associated with acute postoperative pain.
