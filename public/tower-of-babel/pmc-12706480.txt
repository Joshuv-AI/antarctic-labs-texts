Extensive peritruncal subarachnoid hemorrhage: a risk factor for the development of spinal arachnoid cysts. Illustrative cases
Authors: Laura Krismer, Sameer Nazeeruddin, Theo Demerath, Christian Scheiwe, Christine Julia Gizaw, Jürgen Beck, Roland Roelz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDSpinal arachnoid cysts (SACs) are a rare, delayed complication after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH), potentially leading to spinal cord injury. Pathogenesis and preventive strategies remain poorly understood, and management is often frustrating. The authors reviewed their institutional 19-year aSAH patient registry to explore SAC prevalence and potential risk factors.OBSERVATIONSAmong 1470 aSAH admissions (2005–2023), 1098 of whom were 6-month survivors, 5 patients (0.46%) developed symptomatic, imaging-confirmed SAC. Notably, all SAC cases occurred in patients who did not receive active blood clearance (3 before October 2015 and 2 who were not eligible), resulting in a prevalence of 15.2%. The median time to myelopathy onset was 28 months (range 5–76 months). Secondary spinal arachnoid cysts (SACs) and syringomyelia, while less common than primary spinal cystic pathologies, present substantial therapeutic challenges, often precluding complete symptomatic recovery for many patients.1,2 Underlying etiologies leading to secondary spinal arachnopathy might be iatrogenic after surgical procedures, trauma, infection, or subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH).1 Aneurysmal SAH (aSAH) is an exceptionally rare cause of SACs with fewer than 30 cases reported in the literature.2,3

SACs after aSAH typically affect the thoracic spine, potentially due to the kyphotic curvature causing blood accumulation during bed rest.4,5 Various mechanisms for SAC development have been suggested, including hemolysis and subsequent inflammation, which might impede CSF resorption and also lead to chronic hydrocephalus.6–8 An initial inflammatory phase may be followed by adhesive arachnoiditis with leptomeningeal scarring and nerve root tethering that obstructs normal CSF flow.1,2,9 This obstruction can trap CSF in cystic spaces, known as the “ball-valve mechanism.”1 Syrinx formation occurs when CSF shifts from the spinal subarachnoid space into the central canal, resulting in syringomyelia.10 Additionally, the blockage of spinal cord blood vessels may cause focal ischemia, leading to spinal cord injury.5,11,12

The current literature on clinical features and management of this pathology is limited. Common treatment options for intradural cysts or syringomyelia include surgical decompression through laminectomy, adhesion microlysis, cyst fenestration, and shunt placement.1,2,4,9

Since only individual case reports have been published so far, there is a lack of information on prevalence and risk factors of aSAH-associated SACs. In this study, we present a review of the literature and perform a structured analysis of our institutional 19-year registry of aSAH patients for documented cases of SACs. On this basis, we report the population at risk for SACs and discuss the preventive potential of active blood clearance.

Illustrative Cases

Study Objective

The aim of this study was to assess the prevalence and identify potential risk factors for the development of SACs following aSAH. To this end, we conducted a systematic literature review and screened our institutional 19-year registry of aSAH patients for documented cases of SACs. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the ethics committee of the University Medical Center Freiburg.

Literature Review

A systematic electronic search was conducted using the PubMed database to identify published cases of SACs following SAH. The search strategy included the keywords “subarachnoid hemorrhage” AND “arachnoid cyst” OR “syringomyelia” OR “syrinx.” Publications from January 1990 through July 2024 were considered. Findings were categorized as aneurysmal and nonaneurysmal SAH. Cases with spinal SAH, traumatic SAH, or cerebral arachnoid cysts were excluded. Only articles published in the English language were included. The final search was conducted on July 29, 2024.

Twenty-three published cases with SACs after aSAH (Table 1) and 2 cases following perimesencephalic nonaneurysmal SAH (PNSAH) (Table 2) were identified. In the aSAH group, the mean age at diagnosis of SACs was 54.3 ± 12.8 years, with a female predominance (74%). Most aneurysms originated from the posterior circulation, particularly the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA; 43%) and vertebral artery (VA; 26%), while 17% were located in the anterior circulation. Aneurysm treatment included coil embolization (n = 15) and microsurgical clipping (n = 8). The majority of cases presented with Fisher grade 3 or 4 hemorrhages, often involving the ventricles (n = 9), basal cisterns (n = 6), or posterior fossa (n = 5), and were graded as Hunt and Hess (H&H) grades 3–5. Arachnopathy predominantly affected the thoracic spine, and syringomyelia was present in 14 cases. The median time to diagnosis was 12 months (range 0.5–144 months). Almost all cases were treated surgically, with 11 patients receiving a cyst shunt. The 2 PNSAH cases were both females who developed SACs at 4.5 and 10 months, respectively. Both patients underwent surgical decompression, one also with cyst shunt insertion.

Institutional Database Screening

All consecutive patients diagnosed with aSAH and admitted to the Department of Neurosurgery at the University Medical Center Freiburg during a 19-year period (January 2005 to December 2023) were included in our institutional PRISMA registry (DRKS ID: 00016532). The registry was maintained retrospectively until January 2019 and prospectively thereafter. Patients who died within 6 months post-aSAH (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] score 6) and patients lost to follow-up were excluded from the analysis. To identify patients who developed symptomatic SACs, all available imaging studies were screened for spinal imaging. Spinal imaging, particularly spinal MRI studies, were then evaluated by a neuroradiologist and a neurosurgeon. In cases with imaging findings suspicious for SACs, clinical records were examined to correlate findings with symptoms. The extent of subarachnoid blood was quantified using the Hijdra score.13 Aneurysmal SAH therapy in our institution is divided into two periods—before versus after the implementation of active blood clearance—which was introduced in October 2015. Ever since, 33% of aSAH patients were selected for methods for active blood clearance by intrathecal fibrinolytic irrigation therapy; this factor was accounted for in the analysis (Fig. 1).14–16

Flowchart of institutional SAC cases after aSAH.

Among 1470 consecutive aSAH patients admitted between 2005 and 2023, 1098 survived ≥ 6 months and were screened. Five patients (0.46%) had imaging-confirmed symptomatic SAC, all with posterior circulation aneurysms and extensive subarachnoid blood (Fisher grade 4, Hijdra cistern score > 18). In the subgroup of patients with a posterior circulation aneurysm and extensive subarachnoid blood (n = 44), the SAC prevalence was 11.4%. All 5 patients received an external ventricular drain (EVD). Four underwent endovascular coiling, 1 of whom also received clipping of an additional middle cerebral artery aneurysm. The remaining patient was treated primarily with microsurgical clipping. Cerebral vasospasm occurred in 4 cases, with 3 receiving endovascular rescue therapy. Four patients developed hydrocephalus requiring ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt placement. Lumbar puncture (LP) was performed in 3 cases—1 due to meningitis—with 1 requiring lumbar drainage (LD).

As a special feature of this registry, we introduced a fundamental change in the treatment of aSAH patients from October 2015 onward.: After this date, approximately 33% of patients were enrolled in a randomized controlled trial (SPLASH), which compared active blood clearance by fibrinolytic irrigation via a catheter implanted into the prepontine cistern under stereotactic guidance with standard of care (SoC).16 Patients with a large amount of blood were selected, for whom we assumed a high risk of vasospasm. Notably, all 5 SAC cases occurred in patients not treated with active blood clearance (n = 33), resulting in a prevalence of 15.2%. Three cases occurred before October 2015, and 2 patients were enrolled in the SPLASH trial and randomized to SoC. Accordingly, these patient were not eligible for active blood clearance (Fig. 1).

The 5 institutional SAC patients (mean age 57.6 ± 4.7 years; 2 males, 3 females) were symptomatic, with a median time to onset of 28 months (range 5–76 months). Thoracic arachnopathy was present in all patients, with syringomyelia in 4. Treatment was surgical in 4 cases. Despite intervention, neurological deficits persisted. At the last follow-up, 1 patient was able to walk independently, 3 were ambulating with assistance, and 1 was nonambulating (Fig. 2).

Case 1

A 44-year-old woman experienced an H&H grade 4 SAH with perimesencephalic bleeding from a dissecting right VA aneurysm. After endovascular coiling, severe cerebral vasospasms caused transient right-sided hemiparesis, which required treatment with spasmolytic agents. She was discharged without neurological deficits and later received a VP shunt for posthemorrhagic hydrocephalus. After 10 years of stability, she developed progressive gait instability and urinary dysfunction. Neurological examination revealed bilateral proximal paraparesis and mild left foot weakness. Spinal MRI showed multilevel spinal cord tethering, with contrast stops at T2–3, T6–7, and T9–10 on myelography. She underwent surgical adhesiolysis at these levels. At the 6-week postoperative follow-up, bladder function improved, but proximal lower limb strength worsened.

Case 2

A 48-year-old female patient experienced severe SAH (H&H grade 5) from a ruptured basilar artery aneurysm. A significant volume of hemorrhage was located in the peritruncal region and within the basal cisterns. After coil embolization and vasospasmolysis, she was discharged without deficits. Four months later, she developed gait instability, with CT showing progressive ventricular dilation unresponsive to repeated LP. MRI revealed multiple arachnoid cysts, myelopathy, and early syringomyelia. For further diagnostics, the patient underwent invasive intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring and regulated CSF drainage with a combined EVD/ICP probe. Despite normal ICP, symptoms worsened. After a 10-year gap in follow-up, the patient presented with spastic immobilizing paraparesis. MRI showed thoracic syringomyelia and spinal cord compression, with contrast stops at T9–10 on myelography. Suboccipital myelography revealed delayed contrast flow at T3–4. Therefore, microsurgical decompression and detethering at T3–4 and T9–10 with subarachnoid shunting at the upper level was performed. Postoperatively, symptoms persisted, and 3 months later she lost the ability to walk. MRI still showed multiple cysts and syringohydromyelia.

Case 3

A 56-year-old man presented with SAH (H&H grade 2) from a ruptured left VA aneurysm, treated with coiling. His ICU course was complicated by severe vasospasm, meningitis, diabetes insipidus, and posthemorrhagic hydrocephalus, necessitating the placement of multiple LP and VP shunts. Discharged to rehabilitation after 6 weeks, he was unable to walk independently. A month later, he was readmitted with right-sided hemiparesis and incontinence; the VP shunt was revised due to suspected infection. Two years later, he developed mild left-sided weakness and sensory deficits. Meanwhile, he was able to ambulate with walking sticks. MRI revealed extensive syringomyelia with fourth ventricle communication. Symptoms fluctuated, including sensorimotor tetraparesis, pain, and neurogenic bladder/bowel dysfunction. Despite several shunt revisions, he never regained independent walking. Eight years post-SAH, he had paraparesis, distal upper limb weakness, and a T8 sensory level, remaining ambulatory with a walker.

Case 4

A 56-year old man experienced an H&H grade 5 SAH from either a basilar or right middle cerebral artery aneurysm with extensive hemorrhage. The aneurysms were treated with coiling and clipping, respectively. Enrolled in the SPLASH trial, he was randomized to SoC and received an LD. His course was fraught with complications such as vasospasms, terson syndrome, cerebral salt-wasting syndrome, and stress-induced cardiomyopathy. Discharged with an mRS score of 3 and no deficits, he later developed hydrocephalus requiring a VP shunt. Three months post-SAH, he had lower limb hypesthesia and ataxia but returned to work. Two and a half years later, he was readmitted with progressive gait instability, proximal paraparesis, and bladder dysfunction. MRI revealed arachnoid cysts from C7 to L2 and myelopathy at T7–12. Myelography showed tethering with contrast stop at T11. Consequently, he underwent T8 and T11 hemilaminectomies, adhesiolysis, and subarachnoid drainage, with partial symptom improvement. Suboccipital myelography still demonstrated a contrast stop at T9, leading to further surgery at this level. Postoperatively, epidural hematoseroma caused new right-dominant paraparesis, requiring urgent evacuation, which improved deficits. At the 3-month follow-up, new syringomyelia at T2 was noted, but the patient could walk with a stick despite residual gait instability.

Case 5

A 59-year-old woman presented with an SAH (H&H grade 4) resulting from a fusiform aneurysm of the left PICA. The hemorrhage was extensive, involving the suboccipital region and the craniocervical junction. Emergency surgical hematoma evacuation via a suboccipital craniotomy and aneurysm clipping with occlusion of the affected PICA was performed. After enrollment in the SPLASH trial, the patient was randomized to SoC. Throughout her clinical course, no vasospasms were observed. However, she developed posthemorrhagic hydrocephalus, necessitating VP shunt implantation. The patient was discharged after 1 month without any neurological deficits. Nearly four years later, the patient presented with progressive coordination difficulties in the lower extremities, an unsteady gait, and diffuse dysesthesia in the right leg, without clinically apparent motor weakness. Spinal MRI revealed arachnoid cysts at levels T4–6, causing spinal cord compression and a myelopathy signal. Myelography demonstrated a marked delay in contrast flow at the T5–6 level. The patient underwent a T4–6 hemilaminectomy with adhesiolysis, cyst fenestration, and placement of a subarachnoid drain. At the time of discharge, her gait had modestly improved, although the right leg dysesthesia persisted.

Discussion

The development of SAC and syringomyelia represents a rare but severe delayed complication following aSAH, often associated with unsatisfactory clinical outcomes despite maximal therapeutic efforts. To date, only few cases have been published, and both risk factors and preventive strategies remain poorly understood.

While early and late complications of aSAH are well documented, such as rebleeding, acute hydrocephalus, elevated ICP, neurogenic pulmonary edema, cardiac complications, and electrolyte disturbances in the early phase (hours to days),17 and cerebral vasospasms with delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI), chronic hydrocephalus requiring VP shunt placement, and seizures in the late phase (days to months),18–20 posthemorrhagic spinal arachnoiditis and SAC formation remain exceptional and are likely underdiagnosed.

Previous reports have suggested certain predisposing factors, such as female sex and ruptured posterior circulation aneurysms, as contributing to SAC development.2 Our findings, based on both literature review and institutional data, corroborate these associations, with a female predominance and posterior circulation aneurysms being the most frequent origin of SAH. A likely explanation is the extensive subarachnoid blood distribution in the basal cisterns and posterior fossa, which may facilitate blood migration into the spinal subarachnoid space.4

The underlying pathophysiology remains incompletely understood. Potentially, blood breakdown products trigger inflammatory responses, impairing CSF resorption. Similarly, in the spinal compartment, inflammation may promote leptomeningeal scarring, arachnoid adhesions, and cystic CSF entrapment, ultimately leading to spinal cord tethering and syrinx formation.1,6,7,9,10

In our series, case 3 developed meningitis during the ICU stay, which may have contributed to SAC formation. Inflammatory conditions such as infectious meningitis and spinal adhesive arachnoiditis are recognized risk factors for SAC.21–23 These findings support the hypothesis that inflammation, whether triggered by hemolysis of subarachnoid blood or driven by primary infection, plays a critical role in its pathogenesis.

In keeping with the pertinent literature, surgical treatment of SAC led to only partial improvement, and significant neurological impairment persisted in all patients, although the ability to walk with assistance was maintained in 3 of 5 cases. One was still independently mobile.

Since October 2015, our center has utilized active cisternal blood clearance in selected aSAH patients at risk for DCI using either of the following personalized catheter applications: intraoperative placement of a cisterno-ventricular catheter in patients with clipping of the ruptured aneurysm if the chiasmatic region is entered, ventriculo-lumbar irrigation in patients with stable ICP, and stereotactic catheter ventriculocisternostomy if LD is considered unsafe. These catheter applications enable irrigation therapy using urokinase for clot lysis and on-demand nimodipine for spasmolysis. While the primary goal of this treatment is the prevention of delayed cerebral infarction, active blood clearance may also reduce the risk for other blood-induced complications, including hydrocephalus, superficial siderosis, and SAC formation.15

Although introducing foreign materials into the ventricular or spinal compartments (e.g., lumbar drains, puncture needles) may increase infection risk and potentially contribute to arachnoiditis, evidence remains mixed. The EARLYDRAIN trial reported improved outcomes with lumbar CSF drainage in aSAH patients, without a significantly higher infection rate, although its broad definition of infection (including fever, pneumonia, and meningitis, among others) limits interpretation.24 A systematic review by Palermo et al. found CSF infection rates up to 31.6% with combined EVD and LD use.25 Hulou et al. reported meningitis rates of 13.9% (EVD), 4.7% (LD), and 6.7% (no drainage).26 Liang et al. observed significantly more CSF infections in LD use compared with serial LP.27 Additionally, Machida et al. reported a case of tonsillar herniation and syringomyelia after LD overdrainage in aSAH, highlighting the potential risk of LD in the acute phase, particularly when there is extensive blood in the posterior fossa.28 In our series, all 5 patients received an EVD. Repeated LPs were performed in case 2 (for hydrocephalus) and case 3 (for meningitis), while case 4 received an LD.

Despite a manageable infection risk, enhancing CSF clearance via active lavage may help to reduce the risk of vasospasms. If extensive blood in the basal cisterns and posterior fossa contributes to SAC pathogenesis, then early cisternal lavage may limit the caudal migration of blood into the spinal compartment and the subsequent inflammatory cascade. Previous work has shown that intracranial blood clearance can reduce the severity of systemic inflammatory responses, providing further biological plausibility for this hypothesis.29

Supporting this, all SAC cases in our cohort occurred in patients who did not receive active blood clearance, including 3 before and 2 after the introduction of active blood clearance (the latter were not eligible due to participation in a randomized controlled trial).

Observations

This review of our institutional aSAH registry including all aSAH cases of the last 19 years revealed a striking finding: the overall prevalence of SAC was low (0.46%). However, all SAC cases occurred in patients with a ruptured posterior circulation aneurysm, an extensive peritruncal blood distribution, and no active blood clearance. In this patient population, we observed a 15.2% SAC prevalence. This is the first systematic report that offers preliminary observations aiming to outline the population at risk and provide an initial estimate of the prevalence of SAC after aSAH.

Limitations

In the literature review, complete clinical or imaging data, including modified Fisher grade, were sometimes unavailable. In our institutional registry, loss to follow-up or external care may have led to underreporting, and the true prevalence of SAC may even be higher. Moreover, this study focused exclusively on SAC after aSAH. SAC cases following non-aSAH are even rarer and were not considered in this analysis. Prospective multicenter studies will be needed to validate potential risk factors.

Lessons

This study provides the first prevalence estimate of SAC following aSAH, identifying a rate of 11.4% within a high-risk subgroup characterized by posterior circulation aneurysms and extensive peritruncal blood distribution. While no SAC cases were observed among patients who underwent active cisternal blood clearance, the small sample size limits definitive conclusions. Nonetheless, this observation underscores the potential role of active blood clearance as a preventive strategy to reduce the risk of this rare but debilitating complication.
