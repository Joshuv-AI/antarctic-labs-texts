Downscaling to small‐volume storage bags supports platelet quality and efficient use of cold‐stored platelets for preclinical studies
Authors: Janhavi Mahajan, Matthew P. Padula, Denese C. Marks, Lacey Johnson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo maximize the utilization of cold‐stored platelets (CSPs) in preclinical research, the use of small‐volume storage bags (mini‐bags) or tubes would allow multiple treatment groups to be investigated from a single component. It is necessary to determine the effect of using downscaled storage formats on CSP quality and function.MethodsMini‐bags were prepared by vacuum sealing platelet storage bags to ¼ the standard size. Apheresis platelets (n = 8) in 40% plasma/60% PAS‐E (SSP+) were aseptically aliquoted into mini‐bags (30 mL) or polypropylene tubes (15 mL; CSP‐tubes), and the remaining volume (~190 mL) was retained in the original bag (control). Platelets were refrigerated (2–6°C), and in vitro quality parameters were assessed over 21 days.ResultsMetabolic capacity was altered for CSP‐tubes, with a significant decline in pH, mitochondrial membrane depolarization, and rapid glycolysis. CSP‐tubes had greater storage‐related activation, as evidenced by a higher proportion of annexin‐V‐positive platelets and a greater number of CD61+/annexin‐V+ extracellular vesicles (EVs). Similarly, platelet functional capacity in CSP‐tubes was compromised, with reduced clot strength compared to controls. CSPs stored in mini‐bags remained comparable to controls, with the exception of a greater proportion of annexin‐V‐positive platelets and EVs at day 21. Importantly, despite these changes, no functional deficits were observed in mini‐bag components.ConclusionsStorage in tubes markedly impairs the metabolic, structural, and functional quality of CSPs, indicating that this approach is unsuitable. In comparison, mini‐bag storage preserves key in vitro parameters, supporting a scalable strategy that maximizes the utilization of CSPs for preclinical evaluation.

INTRODUCTION

Platelets for transfusion are conventionally stored at room temperature (RT; 20–24°C), which limits their shelf life to 5–7 days, depending on institutional regulations. 1 These guidelines have been established due to the increased risk of bacterial proliferation and the progressive changes in platelet quality that occur during RT storage, as a result of ongoing metabolic activity. 2 In recent years, cold‐stored platelets (CSPs; stored at 2–6°C) have regained interest, largely due to the inhibition of bacterial growth, 3 and the preservation of metabolic capacity, aggregatory properties, and clot formation and resolution functions over extended storage, 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 allowing for an extension of the shelf life to at least 14 days. 12 The recognized advantages offered by CSPs have driven ongoing efforts to optimize platelet quality, function, and in vivo survival, to maximize product efficacy and shelf life. Accordingly, multiple strategies are being investigated, including the evaluation of protective and inhibitory compounds to supplement platelet storage solutions, and the exploration of altered storage practices, such as shelving style and timing of refrigeration. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16

Platelet components are commonly stored in polyvinylchloride (PVC) blood bags plasticized with butyryl trihexyl citrate (BTHC). The specified volume for platelet storage bags is 100–400 mL, in order to maintain an optimal surface area‐to‐volume ratio and facilitate sufficient gas exchange for platelet metabolism. 1 Alternative storage parameters, including changes to the component volume, platelet content, storage container, and container orientation, may compromise platelet quality, structure, and/or function, by impairing metabolic capacity. 17 , 18 However, as the metabolic rate of platelets is reduced and/or altered during cold storage, 4 , 5 , 6 greater flexibility in storage containers may be possible. Storage in tubes, as used in many historical studies, 19 , 20 , 21 or small‐volume storage bags would maximize the utilization of CSPs for preclinical research, allowing the comparison of multiple treatment groups from a single platelet component. As such, the aim of this study was to assess the impact of downscaling storage formats, using tubes and small‐scale storage bags, on the metabolic, structural, and functional quality of CSPs stored for 21 days.

METHODS

Collection and storage of platelet components

Ethics approval was obtained from the Australian Red Cross Lifeblood Human Ethics Committee. Platelet donations were collected from eligible volunteer donors.

Apheresis platelet components (n = 8) were collected using the TRIMA Accel platform (Terumo BCT, Lakewood, CO, USA) and suspended in 40% plasma/60% platelet additive solution (PAS‐E, SSP+; MacoPharma, Mouvaux, France). Platelets were rested for 1 h, transported to the processing center, and stored at RT, on a platelet agitator (Helmer Inc., Noblesville, IN, USA) overnight.

On day 1 post‐collection, each platelet component was split to obtain three matched groups, as shown in Figure 1. For storage in tubes (CSP‐tubes), 15 mL of platelets were aseptically transferred into 15‐mL polypropylene tubes in a biosafety cabinet and stored upright in a metal rack during the storage period (Figure 1A). Small‐scale storage bags (mini‐bags) were prepared using a semiprofessional vacuum sealer (MagicVac Maxima 2; Flaem Medical Devices, Desenzano del Garda, Italy), to horizontally heat seal standard platelet storage bags (PVC with BTHC; Terumo BCT) to ¼ the size. The integrity of the seals was checked by visual inspection and applying gentle manual pressure to confirm a secure weld prior to transferring platelets. No leaks, sealing defects, or unintended volume losses were observed for any mini‐bag components during the storage period. Platelets (30 mL) were aseptically transferred to mini‐bags (Figure 1B), which represent a scaled‐down storage format at approximately 25% capacity of the minimum volume requirement for platelet components (>100 mL). The remaining volume (190 ± 10 mL) was retained in the original bag (control), maintaining the volume specification for full‐size storage bags (Figure 1C). All platelet storage formats were refrigerated at 2–6°C. Bags were stored on solid, flat trays without agitation for 21 days. In vitro testing was performed at selected timepoints throughout storage (day 1, 7, 14, and 21). During storage, platelets settled uniformly onto the plastic surface of both standard‐size storage bags and mini‐bags. No specific accumulation of platelets was observed along the horizontal heat‐sealed seam, or in the square corners of mini‐bags that were introduced by the heat seal. On the day of testing, control and mini‐bag components were massaged in a circular motion for 10 s prior to sampling to ensure a homogenous mixture of platelets. For controls and mini‐bags, a 5‐mL sample was removed by sterile connection of a sampling bag, or by aseptic transfer using a syringe and needle‐free spike (OriGen Biomedical Inc., Austin, TX, USA) in a biological safety cabinet, respectively. CSP‐tubes were gently inverted 4–5 times, and 5 mL was removed with a transfer pipette in a biological safety cabinet. Platelet components were returned to their respective storage conditions immediately after sample removal.

Fresh frozen plasma (FFP) was used to perform platelet dilutions for functional assays. Four units of ABO‐matched FFP were pooled, aliquoted, and stored at −40°C. At the beginning of each testing day, an aliquot of FFP was thawed and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min, and used for the relevant dilutions.

In vitro quality analysis

All platelet components were examined for the presence of swirl and visible aggregates by holding storage containers up to a light source. The platelet concentration was measured using an automated hematology analyzer (CellDYN Emerald; Abbott Core Laboratory, Abbott Park, IL, USA). The pH was measured at RT using a pH meter (SevenCompact; Mettler‐Toledo GmBH, Greifensee, Switzerland).

The platelet phenotype was assessed by flow cytometry (FACS Canto II; Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Platelet populations were identified with gating based on forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) properties, and a total of 10,000 platelet events were recorded. Platelet glycoproteins and activation markers were measured using the following antibodies: CD61‐FITC, CD42b‐PE, GPVI‐eFluor660, and CD62P‐PE, as previously described. 22 Platelets were stained with annexin‐V‐FITC to measure phosphatidylserine (PS) externalization. 22 The median fluorescence intensity (MFI) or proportion of platelets staining positive for the respective markers was reported. Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using a fluorescent dye, tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester (TMRE), and the MFI was reported, as previously published. 23 The absolute number of platelet extracellular vesicles (EVs) were enumerated using TruCount tubes (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) with 10,000 bead events collected. 22 Platelet EVs were defined as events ≤1.0 μm in size, staining positive for both CD61‐APC and annexin‐V‐FITC.

Platelet supernatants were prepared by double centrifugation as previously published 24 and stored at −80°C until analysis. Glucose and lactate were measured from the platelet supernatant using a biochemistry analyzer (Vitros XT3400; Ortho‐Clinical Diagnostics Inc., Rochester, NY, USA). For supernatants collected later in storage (day 14–21), when the glucose concentration approached the lower limit of the biochemistry analyzer (1.1 mmol/L), a glucose hexokinase assay kit was used, as per the manufacturer's instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Vantaa, Finland). The acetate concentration in the supernatant was measured using an acetic acid kit (acetate kinase format), as per the manufacturer's instructions (Megazyme, Wicklow, Ireland). The concentration of soluble P‐selectin in the supernatant was quantified using a commercially available enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for CD62P (R&D Systems Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA). The concentration of supernatant CD62P was normalized to the platelet content of the respective storage containers. All samples for plate‐based assays (glucose, acetate, and CD62P) were measured in triplicate against the respective standard curve.

Clot formation was measured by viscoelastic testing (ClotPro, Haemoview Diagnostics, enicor GmbH, Munich, Germany), using the IN‐test, which activates clot formation via the intrinsic pathway. Platelet samples (diluted to 200 × 109/L in FFP) were prepared and tested as previously described. 25 The clotting time (CT; time taken to initiate clot formation) and maximum clot firmness (MCF; overall clot strength) were reported.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Significance was determined using a two‐way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare the effect of downscaled storage formats on CSP quality over the storage period. Post hoc Bonferroni's multiple comparisons tests were used to determine differences between storage groups at specific timepoints. A p‐value less than .05 was considered statistically significant.

Cold storage of platelets induces an initial morphological shift within 24 h, which is maintained over 21 days of storage. 5 , 22 The loss of platelet swirl in all refrigerated components was indicative of this change. FSC, which measures the platelet size, and SSC, which measures internal cell complexity or granularity, were significantly lower in CSP‐tubes compared to controls from day 7 of storage (Table 1).

Platelets are known to have significant metabolic flexibility during RT storage, switching between glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation to meet energy demands under different circumstances. 26 , 27 , 28 The glucose concentration decreased over 21 days, accompanied by an increase in lactate concentration, in all groups regardless of storage format (Figure 2A,B). Of note, CSP‐tubes demonstrated a trend toward increased glycolysis, with lower glucose and higher lactate concentrations over storage. When assessing the daily rates of glucose consumption and lactate production in relation to the platelet count, platelets in CSP‐tubes consumed glucose more rapidly, with a corresponding increase in the lactate production rate compared to controls during short‐term storage (day 1–7; Figure 2C,D). Rapid glycolysis was associated with acidification of the component (Figure 2E) and mitochondrial dysfunction, as evidenced by the decrease in TMRE fluorescence from day 7 (Figure 2F). By day 14 of storage, glucose was depleted in CSP‐tubes (0.3 ± 0.1 mmol/L), compared to controls (1.7 ± 1.5 mmol/L), resulting in minimal glucose consumption and lactate production during the remainder of the storage period, accompanied by plateauing of the pH. Despite glucose depletion in CSP‐tubes, there were no changes in the consumption of acetate between storage groups (data not shown), indicating that this was not readily used as a metabolic substrate during extended cold storage. In comparison, no significant changes were observed for CSPs stored in mini‐bags compared to controls, indicating that metabolic capacity was preserved throughout storage.

Cold storage induces known changes to the platelet surface, with a loss of key glycoproteins and an increase in activation markers, particularly as storage progresses. 29 The abundance of GPIIIa remained similar, regardless of storage format (Figure 3A). In contrast, platelets in CSP‐tubes showed a significant decrease in the abundance of GPIbα and GPVI from day 14 (Figure 3B,C), representing a markedly greater decline than typically observed under standard cold storage conditions. Greater platelet degranulation was observed in CSP‐tubes during short‐term storage (day 1–7), with an increase in the proportion of CD62P‐positive platelets on day 7, followed by a decrease during extended storage (Figure 3D). This shift was likely due to shedding of P‐selectin from the platelet surface, which is reflected in the higher concentration of supernatant CD62P in CSP‐tubes from day 14 onward (Figure 3E). Significant storage‐related activation in CSP‐tubes was further evidenced by a higher proportion of annexin‐V‐positive platelets throughout storage (Figure 3F), and a greater number of CD61+/annexin‐V+ EVs (Figure 3G). Taken together, the loss of functional glycoproteins and increase in activation markers demonstrates more pronounced cold storage lesion‐related changes in CSP‐tubes, compared to controls, over the storage period. In contrast, while no changes were observed in the abundance of platelet glycoproteins or degranulation, platelets in mini‐bags showed a small but significant increase in the proportion of annexin‐V‐positive platelets and a greater number of EVs compared to controls; however, this change was only observed at day 21.

Clot formation was assessed by viscoelastometry, which provides a comprehensive assessment of coagulation factors, platelets, and their interaction. 30 CSP‐tubes showed significantly lower clot strength (MCF) during extended storage (Figure 4A), which was accompanied by a trend toward prolongation of the CT (day 21 control: 184 ± 11 s, CSP‐tubes: 198 ± 28 s; Figure 4B). Taken together, the functional capacity of CSP‐tubes was impaired compared to standard CSPs, particularly as storage progressed beyond 14 days. Conversely, storage in mini‐bags did not alter platelet function, with no differences to clot formation parameters compared to controls throughout the storage period.

DISCUSSION

CSPs provide a number of benefits compared to conventionally stored platelets, including reduced metabolic requirements and the preservation of platelet quality over a significantly longer storage duration. However, as the use of CSPs becomes more widely considered, several challenges have been highlighted, such as the lack of guidelines surrounding optimal storage practices and reduced in vivo survival. 31 As such, research priorities directed toward addressing these issues are required to facilitate broader integration into clinical practice. To investigate these strategies, the use of downscaled storage formats may support the efficient use of CSPs in preclinical research. Our study is the first to show that downscaled storage in polypropylene tubes impairs the metabolic, structural, and functional quality of CSPs, while storage in mini‐bags preserves key in vitro parameters over 21 days.

During RT storage, platelet energy requirements are reliant on both anaerobic glycolysis and oxygen‐dependent oxidative phosphorylation to generate ATP for energy, with the flexibility of switching energy pathways depending on the availability of metabolic substrates. 26 , 27 , 28 It is likely that similar metabolic flexibility is maintained during cold storage. While glycolysis is preferentially used during ex vivo storage of platelets, depletion of glucose may initiate a shift toward oxidative phosphorylation, which requires viable, structurally intact mitochondria to sustain ATP production. This shift involves the utilization of alternative metabolic substrates, such as acetate, which is present in the formulation of most platelet additive solutions. 32 The metabolism of acetate results in the consumption of hydrogen ions and production of bicarbonate, which contribute to a more alkaline pH. 5 The importance of gas‐permeable plastics for platelet storage is well established, 33 , 34 to support mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and enable oxygen and carbon dioxide to readily diffuse in and out of the storage environment. Mini‐bags used in our study were prepared using the same material specifications as full‐size platelet bags, ensuring that critical parameters influencing gas exchange, including the material composition, plasticizer content, and plastic film thickness, were matched with standard storage bags. Comparatively, it is likely that the reduced gas permeability of polypropylene, as well as the difference in material thickness between the wall of the polypropylene tubes (approx. 0.8 mm) and the PVC film in storage bags (approx. 0.3–0.4 mm), are contributing to the development of a hypoxic storage environment in CSP‐tubes.

Reduced oxygen permeability in storage containers promotes a metabolic shift toward anaerobic respiration, leading to rapid glycolysis and the resulting accumulation of lactic acid. 34 Our findings are consistent with these changes, with platelets in CSP‐tubes demonstrating a decline in pH, accompanied by a trend toward higher lactate concentration. While the rate of glucose consumption and lactate production in CSP‐tubes appears to slow as storage progresses, this was likely due to glucose exhaustion during later storage, as a result of rapid consumption between day 1–7. Accordingly, lactate production was highest in CSP‐tubes during short‐term storage, after which this stalled in parallel with reduced glucose consumption. While the depletion of glucose favors the utilization of acetate as a fuel source, given the significant mitochondrial dysfunction and hypoxic storage environment in CSP‐tubes, it is unlikely that oxidative phosphorylation could proceed effectively. Incomplete acetate metabolism, alongside glucose depletion and the consequent decrease in lactate production, may account for the plateau in pH observed between day 14–21. Of note, while a significant drop in pH was observed for CSP‐tubes compared to controls, the pH remained above the minimum regulatory specification (>6.4), 1 despite a marked deterioration in platelet quality parameters. These findings align with the increasing recognition that pH may not be the most appropriate quality control indicator for platelets in additive solution, 35 particularly toward the end of storage, or for CSPs. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that despite the reduced metabolism of CSPs, 4 , 5 , 6 the choice of storage material remains critical during extended cold storage.

Although several studies report the use of small‐scale storage formats for CSPs, 15 , 36 , 37 there is a lack of literature demonstrating that findings from downscaled components are translatable to standard‐size CSPs. While storage in tubes has been historically presumed to have minimal impact on CSPs due to their reduced metabolic activity, our findings challenge this assumption. Importantly, many earlier studies using tubes for cold storage of platelets were performed prior to the 1970s, 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 and significant changes in platelet collection, processing, and manufacturing practices have occurred since this time. Further, these studies were conducted before the metabolic and structural requirements of CSPs were fully characterized, and our data suggest that this format may be less well suited to the contemporary expectations for CSP quality over longer storage periods.

As a consequence of storage‐related stressors, platelets may display an increase in activation markers, including PS externalization, EV release, mitochondrial membrane depolarization and receptor shedding. 42 , 43 , 44 While most cold storage lesion‐related changes occur later during storage (day 14–21), 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 significant mitochondrial dysfunction and PS externalization were observed as early as day 7 in CSP‐tubes, likely due to the hypoxic storage environment. Mitochondrial membrane depolarization is often considered the central marker of metabolic stress but is closely associated with other deleterious changes, including platelet shrinkage and fragmentation to form platelet‐derived EVs, which have been reported as indicators of impaired structural integrity and reduced functional capacity. 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 Consistent with this, platelets in CSP‐tubes showed a decrease in FSC, accompanied by a greater release of EVs from day 14–21, suggesting progressive structural deterioration. GPIbα and GPVI are critical for collagen‐mediated platelet adhesion, activation and aggregation during clot formation. 42 As such, the loss of these receptors, alongside the accumulation of other storage lesion‐related changes, may be contributing to the reduction in MCF and loss of clot formation capability observed during extended storage in CSP‐tubes. Of note, while mitochondrial dysfunction coincided with an overall decline in platelet quality in CSP‐tubes, the temporal sequence of these events remains unclear, specifically if one is preceding or driving the other. Comparatively, while CSPs stored in mini‐bags had higher PS externalization and EV release than standard‐size CSPs, this did not translate to a reduction in hemostatic function, as was observed in CSP‐tubes. Of note, the increase in annexin‐V‐positive platelets was comparatively smaller in mini‐bags relative to CSP‐tubes, and the reduced magnitude of this effect, together with the preservation of mitochondrial membrane potential over storage, suggests that not all activation signatures are functionally deleterious, particularly when metabolic capacity is maintained. These findings suggest that differential mechanisms may be contributing to the increase in PS and EV release observed in mini‐bags versus CSP‐tubes.

Downscaled storage of platelet components presents a range of advantages in relation to preclinical research efficiency and resource utilization. While these benefits have resulted in several studies using mini‐bags, 15 , 36 , 37 , 49 , 50 these investigations have either been limited to RT storage or presented other methodological constraints, including the evaluation of CSPs over short‐term storage (< 10 days) or the absence of paired comparisons with full‐size storage bags. Our findings support the use of mini‐bags as a scalable storage format that can be translated to standard CSP components, with comparable in vitro parameters over 21 days. The proportional reduction of platelet volumes in mini‐bags enables simultaneous evaluation of multiple treatment conditions, streamlining data collection while promoting more cost‐effective and sustainable use of reagents. This approach is particularly advantageous for studies involving resource‐intensive modifications, such as the supplementation of platelet storage solutions with additive compounds, as it enables high‐throughput screening of compounds that may be limited in availability and/or costly if required in large quantities.

Although this study supports the use of mini‐bags as an option for the preclinical screening of CSPs, some limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, in‐house mini‐bags were utilized for this investigation to ensure consistency with our standard platelet components in terms of bag material, as manufacturing smaller storage bags with matching plastic specifications was not feasible. However, for routine preclinical work, it would be ideal for small‐scale storage bags to be commercially available. Alternatively, pediatric storage bags may be a suitable option, although a greater volume (40–60 mL) would be required to meet minimum specifications. However, this approach would enable the standardization of storage bags and negate the requirement for a vacuum sealer. Further, our study investigated one type of platelet bag material and tube configuration, and variations in polymer composition, plasticizer content, and geometric profiles may yield different outcomes. While platelet storage bags made from several plastics and plasticizers are commercially available, the optimal material specifications for CSPs remain poorly defined, as material requirements may differ according to the collection, manufacturing and processing method used. Further work is required to determine the best combination of plastics and plasticizers that provide sufficient gas permeability while remaining cost‐effective. It is also becoming increasingly more apparent that differences in platelet storage solutions and component processing may alter the in vitro quality and functional properties of CSPs, particularly as storage progresses, 9 , 10 , 51 and it is unclear if our findings can be applied to the extended storage of CSPs in 100% plasma, other additive solutions, or CSPs treated with pathogen reduction treatments.

Our findings carry immediate operational relevance, presenting a practical and efficient strategy for the rapid screening of CSPs in preclinical studies that require evaluation of multiple experimental conditions, such as novel additive compounds, supplementation strategies, storage temperatures, pathogen reduction treatments, or agitation regimes. The implementation of such multifactorial study designs is typically constrained by the volume available from a single donor component or requires pooling platelet components from multiple donors. Our data support the use of mini‐bags as a fit‐for‐purpose storage format for CSPs, enabling matched, multi‐arm study designs with reduced donor variability and accelerated data collection.
