Impact of sweet drink on pituitary response and subject comfort during insulin tolerance test
Authors: Tiphaine Carton, Coralie Thiry, Fleur Wolff, Bernard Corvilain, Agnès Burniat
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe insulin tolerance test (ITT) is the gold standard for the diagnosis of cortisol and growth hormone (GH) deficiencies. Once hypoglycemia is detected whether patients should receive carbohydrates to recover faster from hypoglycemia is controversial. The aim of our study was to assess the impact of shortening the duration of hypoglycemia by providing a sugar drink on patient comfort and hormonal responses. This prospective, single-center, crossover, single-blind study enrolled 15 healthy participants. Each subject performed two ITT: one with normal Cola and the other with Cola Zero. Glucose and hormone levels were measured at baseline and after the induction of insulin-induced hypoglycemia. Patient tolerance to ITT was evaluated using reported hypoglycemia side effects, visual analog scale scores, and quality-of-life questionnaire. Normal Cola shortened the period of hypoglycemia (plasma glucose < 40 mg/dl) from 30 to 15 min (p = 0.005), allowing a higher proportion of participants to recover from potentially dangerous values after 45 min (12 vs. 5; p = 0.008). Cola also improved patient comfort, as evaluated using a visual analog scale (VAS score 2.6 vs 3.7, p = 0.016). Sugar did not significantly change the median cortisol and GH peaks. Body mass index was the only factor determining the cortisol peak, independent of the injected insulin dose. The time to reach hormone peaks was similar between the two protocols. Cortisol and GH values were significantly higher at 120 min in the Cola Zero group, suggesting the prolongation of hypothalamic-pituitary stress in the absence of sugar. Our study confirmed that rapid correction of hypoglycemia during ITT improves subject comfort, reduces the duration of hypoglycemia and associated symptoms, while guaranteeing unchanged cortisol and GH peaks.Keywords: Sweet drink, Insulin tolerance test, Cortisol, Growth hormone, StressSubject terms: Adrenal gland diseases, Multihormonal system disorders

Introduction

The insulin tolerance test (ITT) is considered the gold standard for diagnosing central adrenal insufficiency (AI) and growth hormone (GH) deficiency1–3. The ITT consists of injecting rapid insulin intravenously to lower blood glucose levels and assessing the cortisol and GH response to the induced stress. A plasma cortisol peak ≥ 500 nmol/L and GH peak > 3 µg/L excludes the diagnosis of AI and severe GH deficiency, respectively, in adults1–3. Once hypoglycemia is reached, whether patients should be administered carbohydrates to recover faster from hypoglycemia is controversial4–6. In some centers the patient’s blood glucose rises spontaneously and an ampoule of 30% or 50% glucose solution is administrated only in cases of severe symptomatic hypoglycemia. In other centers, a sweet food and/or drink is given to the patient as soon as hypoglycemia is achieved. However, there is no recommendation on this subject. Some centers prefer the first option because a spontaneous slow, delayed rise in blood glucose is an indirect indicator of a cortisol deficit. However, prolonged hypoglycemia is uncomfortable for patients and is not without risks, particularly in older and frail patients (risk of stroke, acute coronary syndrome, seizure). Therefore, the question is whether shortening the duration of hypoglycemia by supplying sugar to the patient alters the pituitary response and overdiagnoses cortisol and/or GH deficiency. Few studies have addressed this issue. Borm et al.7 evaluated the GH and cortisol levels in response to ITT in healthy adults based on whether patients received an intravenous low-dose glucose infusion for recovery. They observed comparable pituitary responses and reduced hypoglycemic symptoms in the glucose infusion protocol. In contrast, Yeste et al.8 showed a significant reduction in GH and cortisol responses to insulin hypoglycemia when taking oral glucose in a pediatric population with growth delay.

The aim of our study was to assess the impact of shortening the duration of insulin-induced hypoglycemia by providing a sugar drink on patient’s comfort and hormonal responses.

Our secondary objective was to discuss the cortisol threshold regarding recent studies suggesting that, thanks to the improved specificity of new immunoassays, the cortisol cutoff of 500 nmol/L still in the guidelines should be lowered. Raverot et al.9 proposed a threshold of 374 nmol/L based on more than 100 stimulation tests (88 ACTH stimulating and 21 ITT) and Javorsky et al.10 proposed a threshold of 402 nmol/L after ACTH stimulation testing, using the same cortisol assay (Elecsys®Cortisol II assay).

Materials and methods

Subjects

Fifteen participants were selected from among the doctors in the hospital's Endocrinology Department and medical students at the university. The inclusion criteria were: being in good health, aged between 18 and 45 years, and having a BMI between 18 and 28 kg/m2. The exclusion criteria included: pregnancy, epilepsy, psychiatric disorders, cardiovascular pathology, diabetes, glucocorticoid medication and oral contraceptives. Patients who smoked had to be nonsmokers for at least 12 h before the test.

Study design

Each subject underwent two ITT at intervals of 1–3 weeks. One test was performed with 150 ml of normal Cola, and the other with 150 ml of Cola Zero. This beverage was chosen for several reasons. First, 150 ml of Cola, providing 15 g of carbohydrates, is a well-known sweetened beverage recognized for its rapid and effective correction of hypoglycemia in diabetic patients. Second, it is a simple and easily reproducible method for administering glucose to patients in other centers. Finally, it enabled the establishment of a valid control group, participants in the control group receiving 150 ml of Cola Zero, a diet variant containing no carbohydrates. To maintain the study's rigor, participants were blinded to the type of Cola administered—whether regular or diet—minimizing potential biases related to their perceptions and reports of side effects. The precise measurement of 150 ml was ensured using a calibrated measuring cup. The sequence of the two tests was randomized through a draw conducted by the supervising physician, who also prepared the drink. Randomization led to seven subjects starting with the ITT followed by regular Cola, and eight starting with the ITT followed by Cola Zero.

Protocol

A continuous interstitial glucose measurement system (CGMS; Medtronic CGMS Guardian™Connect) was placed on one of the subjects’ arms from 24 to 48 h before the test until at least 24 h after the test. The ITT was started between 8:00 and 8:30 a.m., with the patient fasting for 12 h. Anthropometric parameters, blood pressure, and heart rate were measured before the start of the test and were controlled at the end of the test.

An intravenous cannula was inserted with an infusion of isotonic NaCl solution (0.9%) 30 min before the insulin injection. The subjects received a bolus of rapid insulin of 0.15 IU/kg body weight (ActrapidTM, NovoNordisk, Denmark) (time 0).

Blood samples were collected at -30, 0, 20, 30, 45, 60, 75, 90, and 120-min. Plasma cortisol was measured at each time point, plasma ACTH and GH were measured at every time point except at 20, 45, and 75 min, and serum glucose was determined at each time point with additional measurements at 15 and 25 min.

During the protocol, blood glucose was regularly checked with an Accu-Check® Performa meter (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) at the following times: − 30, 0, 15, 20, 25, 30, 45, 60, 75, 90, 120 min.

A 150 ml of Cola (normal or Zero) was given to the subject as soon as the blood glucose level fell below 40 mg/dl on the Accu-Check® Performa meter, and this value was checked and confirmed using another glucometer (Contour Next®, Ascensia Diabetes Care Inc. Canada). An ampoule of 30% glucose should only be injected in case of severe symptomatic hypoglycemia.

Side effects (warm feeling, sweating, fatigue, dizziness, nausea, palpitations, cold extremities, blurred vision, and tremors) and their onset and duration were recorded for each patient during the test.

At the end of each ITT, the subjects were asked to evaluate their degree of tolerance to the test using a visual analog scale (VAS) with 100 mm bars (from 0 = completely tolerable to 100 mm = not at all tolerable). The nurse in charge was also asked to estimate her degree of stress during the ITT using the VAS (from 0 = not at all stressed to 100 mm = maximum imaginable stress). At the end of the second ITT, the subject was asked to reevaluate the VAS score of the first ITT (revised VAS) to reduce the bias related to the stress of the unknown of the first test, which was by definition, no longer present during the second.

A quality-of-life questionnaire containing 10 questions based on the SF12 questionnaire was also sent to the participants the day after the test (questionnaire in supplemental data). They were asked about their general state of health and the physical and emotional limitations observed in the 36 h following the test. Each participant was asked to perform the same type of physical activity and eat the same type of meal (particularly in terms of carbohydrate content) for 24 h following the two tests.

Assays

All biological analyses were performed in the Department of Clinical Chemistry, LHUB-ULB, Université Libre de Bruxelles (Brussels, Belgium). Serum cortisol and ACTH concentrations were determined by the Cobas® e801 analytical unit, based on the "ECLIA" technology (ElectroChemiLuminescence ImmunoAssay; Elecsys® Cortisol II for cortisol, Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany). Plasma glucose levels were measured using an in vitro enzymatic assay for quantitative determination of glucose on a c702 Roche automated clinical chemistry analyzer. GH concentrations were quantified using a chemiluminescence immunoassay (CLIA) on a Liaison XL analyzer (DiaSorin, Saluggia, Italy).

Capillary blood glucose levels were measured using the Accu-Check® Performa meter (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany), and hypoglycemia was confirmed using the Contour® Next meter (Ascensia Diabetes Care Inc. Canada).

Interstitial glucose levels were measured using the Medtronic Guardian™Connect sensor in place on the subject's arm from 24 to 48 h prior to the ITT (to allow for necessary calibrations). A threshold of 70 mg/dl was set for hypoglycemia on CGMS for data analysis because of the lower sensitivity of this technique for glycemic assessment, with an otherwise measurable minimum threshold of 40 mg/dl.

In response to hypoglycemia, a peak plasma cortisol level ≥ 500 nmol/l excluded the diagnosis of adrenal insufficiency according to the guidelines still in place. However, the data were also analyzed with regard to new cortisol thresholds, which have been revised following recent publications, using the Elecsys® Cortisol II assay (9,10). A GH peak > 3 µg/l (3) allowed the exclusion of a severe growth hormone deficiency.

Statistics

Sample size calculation: we aimed to detect a 5% difference in peak cortisol levels with appropriate statistical power and an alpha error rate of 5%. Given the pilot nature of our study and the need to balance feasibility with statistical rigor, we chose a 70% power level and accounted for an intra-group correlation of 0.70, resulting in a sample size requirement of 15 participants. Intragroup correlation is justified as each participant serves as their own control, thereby reducing interindividual variability. A 70% power level was chosen to maximize available resources and participant involvement while acknowledging potential limitations in statistical power.

All descriptive values are expressed as medians and confidence intervals (CI: 25th percentile (p25), 75th percentile (p75)), unless otherwise stated. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for the glucose, ACTH, cortisol, and GH levels from 0 to 120 min. Data between tests with and without sweet beverages were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (the values were asymmetrically distributed). Correlation analyses between different data were performed using the Spearman’s correlation coefficient. A p-value < 0.05 was considered as significant. Multivariate analyses were performed using multiple linear regression. Statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics 28. Graphs were constructed using Stata SE version 17.

Ethics approval

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Erasme-ULB Hospital (Brussels, Belgium) under the reference P2021/102/CCB B4062021000048 and registered in the Ethics Committee Application Form. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant after receiving a complete explanation of the purpose and nature of the study. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Results

Population

The 15 participants that were included (seven women, eight men) had a median age of 27 years (CI 24–32), median weight of 66 kg (CI 58–77) and median BMI of 21.9 kg/m2 (CI 20.1–23.1). All participants were in good health.

Glycemic course

Hypoglycemia (plasma glucose < 40 mg/dl) was reached after a median time of 15 min (CI 15–20) after insulin injection in both protocols (Table 1). Symptoms of hypoglycemia were experienced by most subjects 5 min after the onset of plasma hypoglycemia. All patients achieved symptomatic hypoglycemia with comparable nadir plasma glucose levels between the two protocols (17 mg/dl [normal Cola] vs. 16 mg/dl [Cola Zero], p = 0.141) (Fig. 1; Table 1).

The median time spent in hypoglycemia (plasma glucose < 40 mg/dl) was significantly shorter in the normal Cola group (median 15 min [normal Cola] vs. 30 min [Cola Zero]; p = 0.005) (Table 1). Sweet drinks also significantly shortened the median time spent in hypoglycemia, when calculated from the time of drinking, i.e., from the capillary measurement, of a plasma glucose level below 40 mg/dl (10 min [normal Cola] vs. 20 min [Cola Zero]; p = 0.003) (Table 1). Thus, 45 min after insulin injection, only five out of 15 participants recovered from hypoglycemia in the Cola Zero protocol compared with 12 out of 15 in the normal Cola protocol (χ2 = 7; p = 0.008) (Table 1).

When analyzing interstitial glucose using CGMS, the median time spent in hypoglycemia (glucose < 70 mg/dl) was 30 min for the group with normal Cola compared to 60 min for the group with Cola Zero, but this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.126) (Table 1).

Plasma cortisol, ACTH, and GH

Most subjects showed small differences in cortisol and GH peak responses between the two tests. Ten participants had a lower cortisol peak with regular Cola compared to Cola Zero, while five subjects had a higher peak with regular Cola. However, the median peak cortisol level was equivalent between the two protocols, with a value of 552 nmol/L (CI 527–637) in the Cola Zero group compared to 549 nmol/L (CI 495–584) in the normal Cola (p = 0.053) (Fig. 2A; Table 1). Similarly, the delta (relative and absolute cortisol increments) and AUC of plasma cortisol did not differ significantly between the two protocols (Table 1). Only the plasma cortisol measured at 120 min was significantly higher in the Cola Zero group (487 mmol/L [Cola Zero] vs. 377 nmol/L [normal Cola]; p = 0.002) (Fig. 2B; Table 1).

Eleven participants had a lower GH peak with normal Cola compared to Cola Zero, while four exhibited a higher peak with normal Cola. However, as with cortisol, GH peaks, as well as the AUC of GH concentration, were comparable in both protocols (Fig. 3A, Table 1). The results were also comparable for peak and AUC of ACTH (Table 1, supplemental Fig. 1A). Only the GH level at time 120 min was significantly higher in the Cola Zero protocol (8.23 µg/L vs 4.28 µg/L, p = 0.02) (Fig. 3B; Table 1).

The cortisol peak was achieved at the 75th minute in most cases. There was no significant difference in the time to peak between the two protocols: 75 min (CI 60–75) in the normal Cola group vs. 75 min (CI 60–90) in the Cola Zero group (p = 0.272) (Table 1). The time to reach the peak cortisol level was significantly correlated with the age of the subject (Rs = 0.475, p = 0008) (Table 1).

GH and ACTH peaks appeared in most participants at the 60th minute (Fig. 3B; supplemental Fig. 1B). The time to reach the GH peak was comparable between the two groups: 60 min (CI 60–75) in the normal Cola group vs. 60 min (CI 45–90) in the Cola Zero group (p = 0.78) (Table 1).

There was a positive and significant correlation between the value of GH and cortisol peaks (Rs = 0.371, p = 0.043), but no significant correlation between the time spent in hypoglycemia and the cortisol peak or the AUC of cortisol.

Multiple linear regression evaluating the factors determining the cortisol peak showed BMI to be the only predictive factor (coefficient B = 17.9; T = 2.766; p = 0.011) (Supplementary Table 1). The correlation between BMI and cortisol peak was found to be independent of the insulin dose injected.

The GH peak was significantly correlated with the duration of hypoglycemia (< 40 mg/dl) (Rs = 0.410, p = 0.024). However, the global p-value of the multiple linear regression evaluating the factors determining the GH peak was not significant (overall R = 0.413; p = 0.324), excluding any predictive factor.

Cortisol and GH thresholds

The minimum peak of plasma cortisol was 390 nmol/L in the normal Cola protocol, and 449 nmol/L in the Cola Zero protocol (Fig. 2A, Table 1). Four subjects showed a cortisol peak lower than 500 nmol/L: one subject during his two tests and three subjects only during their test with normal Cola (390 nmol/L vs. 569 nmol/L, 416 nmol/L vs. 541 nmol/L, and 489 nmol/L vs. 527 nmol/L without sugar). However, all cortisol peaks were still above 374 nmol/L, which is the threshold proposed by Raverot et al.9. All participants also showed a normal GH response with a minimum peak of 5.78 µg/L (Fig. 3A).

Side effects and tolerance to the test

The main symptoms reported in both ITT were fatigue, sweating, hunger, and a feeling of heat (Fig. 4). The median duration of side effects (unaccounted for hunger) was 35 min in the normal Cola group vs. 50 min in the Cola Zero group. However, this difference was not significant (p = 0.102) (Table 1).

Clinical side effects. Mean duration (min) of side effects according to the type of Cola (withe bars: normal Cola; black bars: Cola Zero).

Similarly, a better tolerance to the test was noted when drinking normal Cola: VAS score of 2.5 with normal Cola vs. 3 with Cola Zero (p = 0.036). This difference was even more significant when considering the revised VAS: VAS score 2.6 versus 3.7 (p = 0.016) (Table 1). However, this discomfort did not seem to continue for long after the ITT, as the QoL scores assessed by each participant on the day after the test were comparable between the two protocols (p = 0.262).

There was no significant difference in the mean blood pressure or heart rate at the beginning and end of the test between the two protocols (data not shown).

Multiple linear regression of the determinants of VAS showed that BMI (coefficient B = 0.30; T = 2.37; p = 0.026), duration of hypoglycemia (blood glucose < 40 mg/dl) (coefficient B = 0.07; T = 2.62; p = 0.015), and GH at time 120 min (coefficient B = 0.10; T = 2.29; p = 0.031) were significant predicting factors (supplemental Table 2).

Glycemic variability: CGMS data

Based on the CGMS data, there was significantly greater glycemic variability estimated by the coefficient of variation in the hours following hypoglycemia (from hypoglycemia release to midnight) in all participants compared to the night before the test (p = 0.009) (Table 2, supplemental Fig. 2A and Fig. 2B). However, there was no significant difference in the coefficients of variation between the two protocols (normal Cola versus Cola Zero) (p = 0.776) (Table 1). Post-ITT glycemic variability and postprandial glucose peak (meal following the test) were correlated with the duration of hypoglycemia, regardless of the protocol used (considering plasma hypoglycemia < 40 mg/dl: Rs = 0,372, p = 0,043 and Rs = 0,392, p = 0,035; for glycemic variability and glycemic peak, respectively) (Table 2).

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate whether the pituitary response to insulin-induced hypoglycemia was comparable, depending on whether the subject was administered sugar after hypoglycemia. To our knowledge, only one study by K. Borm et al., published in 20057, also studied the influence of glucose administration during ITT in healthy adults (administration of a 500 ml infusion of 5% glucose over 30 min vs. normal saline). In our study, all participants exhibited symptomatic hypoglycemia with blood glucose values < 40 mg/dl, validating all tests performed. None of the participants experienced severe symptomatic hypoglycemia requiring intravenous glucose infusion. The nadir blood glucose levels were equivalent between the two protocols. Similar to Borm et al.7, we observed a decrease of 50% in the time spent in hypoglycemia during the tests followed by oral glucose solution (normal Cola), allowing a higher proportion of subjects to come out of potentially dangerous values 45 min after insulin injection. This observation constitutes an argument in favor of oral glucose intake, especially in populations at risk of complications, such as fragile patients, and patients with coronary heart disease. The duration of hypoglycemia was not correlated with the weight, BMI, or age of the participants.

Normal Cola did not significantly change the median cortisol and GH peaks induced by hypoglycemia. Similarly, the AUC of plasma cortisol and delta cortisol levels were comparable between the two groups. The time to achieve the cortisol and GH peaks was also similar, but the cortisol and GH values were significantly higher at 120 min in the Cola Zero group, demonstrating the prolongation of hypothalamic-pituitary stress in the absence of sugar. The significant positive correlation between cortisol and GH peaks illustrates the link between adrenal and somatotroph axis sensitivity to hypoglycemia.

These results are broadly comparable to those observed by Borm et al.7, who also observed an adequate and comparable GH and cortisol responses in the protocol with glucose infusion (cortisol 634 nmol/L with glucose solution vs 690 nmol/L with saline solution; non-significant difference). The cortisol values were globally higher in the study by Borm et al., owing to a different assay method with lower specificity (first-generation immunoassays).

In addition to reducing the time spent in hypoglycemia, the normal Cola protocol improved participants’ comfort during the test. The VAS at the end of the test showed a better score in the case of glucose intake. Similarly, normal Cola resulted in almost a 30% reduction in the duration of hypoglycemia-related symptoms (35 min vs. 50 min), although the difference was not significant (large interquartile ranges).

The reduction in time spent in plasma hypoglycemia was not observed by CGMS analyses. However, it is well known that interstitial glucose measurement lacks accuracy, especially for low glucose levels. Nonetheless, this tool remains useful and valid for continuous glucose measurement, trend analysis, and detecting glycemic variability. In this regard, CGMS analysis showed that the ITT resulted in a higher postprandial glucose peak and greater variability in blood glucose levels in the hours following the test for all participants. This variability was significantly correlated with the duration of hypoglycemia but not with the nature of the drink. Increased glycemic variability after hypoglycemia has been previously described in patients with diabetes11,12. Some studies have demonstrated the negative impact of glycemic variability on several clinical outcomes, especially cardiac complications, in patients with and without diabetes11,13,14. This constitutes an additional argument for shortening the duration of hypoglycemia in fragile patients, particularly those at risk of coronary heart disease.

Consistent with Borm et al.7, no significant correlation between the duration of hypoglycemia and cortisol or GH peak was observed, unlike other studies such as that of Nye et al.15, which showed a positive correlation between the depth and duration of hypoglycemia and cortisol response (ITT healthy subjects). Although guidelines define the threshold for hypoglycemia, there are no recommendations on the duration needed to ensure a reliable cortisol (or GH) response. Our study found that prolonged hypoglycemia resulted in a slower normalization of hormone levels (higher cortisol and GH levels at the 120th minute in the group Cola Zero). This suggests that the pituitary response may be triggered by reaching a certain blood glucose threshold (an on–off effect), but that the duration of hypoglycemia does not necessarily enhance the response; rather, it prolongs it.

In our study, BMI was the only factor determining cortisol but not GH peak, independent of the insulin dose injected. Obesity and insulin resistance are known to be associated with hyperactivity of the hypothalamus–pituitary–adrenal axis. However, there are very few studies on the influence of body mass index (BMI) on cortisol response in stimulation tests, with conflicting results16–19. Conversely, the link between growth hormone (GH) peak and BMI is well-established, with specific cutoffs for overweight or obese patients20. Nevertheless, the BMI range in our cohort was too narrow to draw definitive conclusions.

Our secondary objective was to re-discuss the thresholds of stimulated plasma cortisol levels. If we refer to the plasma cortisol thresholds still used in most guidelines, i.e., 500 nmol/L, four participants would have been diagnosed with an insufficient cortisol response, three of whom only received normal Cola. However, when the threshold of 374 nmol/L proposed by Raverot et al.9 was used as a reference, all participants showed an adequate adrenal response (minimum 390 nmol/L).

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, the participants were young (median age of 27 years) and lean (median BMI of 21.9 kg/m2), whereas the majority of patients with secondary adrenal insufficiency are older and have higher BMI, especially when other pituitary deficiencies are present21–23. Age and weight can influence cortisol levels and the degree of insulin resistance24.

Secondly, we did not collect data on the menstrual cycle phase of female participants at the time of testing, which can affect insulin sensitivity25. However, this factor is less significant than weight and all participants received weight-based doses of rapid-acting insulin as recommended in the standard protocol. They all reached a plasma glucose level below 40 mg/dL and the glucose nadir achieved was comparable between the two groups.

Thirdly, we cannot exclude a stimulating effect on cortisol secretion from the caffeine contained in Cola. However, the caffeine content in both types of Cola is equivalent and low (15 mg per 150 ml). According to the literature, the expected stimulating effect for such a dose is likely negligible, particularly when compared to the much stronger stress response induced by hypoglycemia during the ITT26.

Fourthly, the tests were conducted on healthy individuals without adrenal insufficiency. Therefore, we cannot rule out the possibility that shortening the duration of stress, might fail to detect partial insufficiency.

Finally, our cohort size was limited, though comparable to that in the study by Borm et al.

The insulin tolerance test remains an unpleasant and potentially dangerous procedure, limiting the possibility of a large volunteer recruitment, particularly when each participant must undergo two tests.

Nevertheless, our results should be confirmed in a larger cohort of patients with suspected adrenal insufficiency.

In conclusion, our study confirms that the rapid correction of hypoglycemia does not change hormonal peaks after ITT. It improves participants’ comfort and reduces the duration of hypoglycemia and associated symptoms, while guaranteeing unchanged hormonal peaks (plasma cortisol and GH). The only differences in hormonal responses observed after normal Cola were related to lower cortisol and GH values at 120 min, reflecting the shortening of hypothalamic-pituitary stress. Our findings support the notion that the initial hypoglycemic trigger is critical for cortisol and GH peaks, and thus for pituitary insufficiency diagnosis, whereas the duration of hypoglycemia appears to prolong the response rather than enhance it.

Although our results suggest there is no valid reason to withhold sugar from patients during ITT, it is crucial to emphasize that these findings need to be replicated in a population with pituitary insufficiency before any changes to diagnostic procedures are implemented. Our study also confirmed the need to lower plasma cortisol thresholds when using the new cortisol assays, but further studies in appropriate patient populations are necessary to validate this adjustment.
