Every finitely generated abelian group is the class group of a generalized cluster algebra Thanks: This work was partly supported by the Austrian Science Fund (FWF), project 10.55776/DOC-183-N.
Mara Pompili

Abstract
. We determine the class group of those generalized cluster algebras that are Krull domains. In particular, this provides a criterion for determining whether or not a generalized cluster algebra is a UFD. In fact, any finitely generated abelian group can be realized as the class group of a generalized cluster algebra. Additionally, we show that generalized cluster algebras are FF-domains and that their cluster variables are strong atoms. Finally, we examine the factorization and ring-theoretic properties of Laurent phenomenon algebras.3

1.
Introduction

The
class group
of a
Dedekind domain
(a domain where every non-zero ideal factors uniquely into prime ideals) is an invariant that measures the failure of unique factorization. In a unique factorization domain (UFD), every element can be uniquely factored into atoms (also called irreducibles), up to order and units. However, not all domains exhibit unique factorization, and class groups are a tool that captures this phenomenon.
If a Dedekind domain allows non-unique factorizations of its elements into atoms, the class group provides a way of classifying the different types of factorizations.
Even if the class group is typically defined for Dedekind domains, the definition can be extended to
Krull domains,
a higher dimensional generalization of Dedekind domains. The class group of a Krull domain
A
is the abelian group of the equivalence classes of divisorial ideals of
A
, where two ideals are equivalent if they differ by a principal ideal. The structure of a Krull domain can be fully described (up to units) by its class group and the distribution of prime divisors within each class. Claborn’s Realization Theorem
[
Cla66
, Theorem 7]
states that every abelian group is isomorphic to the
class group of a Dedekind domain. Similar realization theorems, yielding commutative Dedekind domains which are more geometric, respectively
number theoretic, in nature, were obtained by Leedham-Green
[
LG72
]
and Rosen
[
Ros73
,
Ros76
]
. For more recent research on this direction see for instance
[
CG24
,
Cha22
,
Cla09
,
Per23
,
Sme17
]
.
One of the goals of this paper is to show that for any finitely generated abelian group
G
, there exists a
generalized cluster algebra
that has
G
as its class group.

Generalized cluster algebras were introduced by Chekhov and Shapiro
[
CS13
]
in 2014. Their idea was to generalize the construction of cluster algebras introduced by Fomin and Zelevinsky in their four-part series of foundational papers
[
FZ02
,
FZ03
,
BFZ05
,
FZ07
]
(the paper
[
BFZ05
]
is in cooperation with Berenstein). Generalized cluster algebras were introduced to study Teichmüller spaces of Riemann surfaces with orbifold points.
Unlike the classical setting, where exchange relations are binomial, generalized cluster algebras allow multinomial exchange relations. In
[
CS13
, Theorem 2.5 and Theorem 2.7]
the authors show that generalized cluster algebras also possess the Laurent phenomenon and their finite type classification coincides with the one of cluster algebras.
Gekhtman, Shapiro, and Vainshtein
[
GSV18
, Theorem 4.1]
prove that a generalized upper cluster algebra coincides with its upper
bounds under coprimality conditions. In Section
4
we provide a proof of this fact using a more elementary argument.
Later, Bai, Chen, Ding, and Xu
[
BCDX20
, Theorem 3.10]
prove that
the conditions of acyclicity and coprimality close the gap between lower bounds and
upper bounds associated to generalized cluster algebras, and thus obtaining the
standard monomial bases of these algebras.

Despite these and other similarities between cluster algebras and generalized cluster algebras, our study reveals a significant difference in their factorization properties, particularly in terms of their class groups.
The class groups of cluster algebras have been previously studied in
[
GELS19
,
Pom25
]
. In
[
GELS19
]
, the authors show that the class group of a cluster algebra that is a Krull domain is always of the form
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
. Furthermore, in the case of acyclic cluster algebras, they provide an explicit formula for

r
based on the initial data. In
[
Pom25
]
, similar results are obtained for upper cluster algebras that are Krull domains. Specifically, the authors present an explicit calculation of the rank of the class group of full rank upper cluster algebras. Generalized cluster algebras have a more complex behavior: their class groups are still finitely generated abelian groups (see Theorem
4.1
), but they can have torsion (Example
4.11
). In fact, Theorem
4.14
shows that every finitely generated abelian group can be realized as the class group of a generalized cluster algebra. In addition, every class contains infinitely many prime divisors.

For a classical cluster algebra that is a Krull domain only two scenarios are possible: either the algebra is a UFD, or all arithmetic invariants are infinite and any non-empty finite subset
L\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
can be
realized as a set of lengths of some element. This dichotomy is not true anymore for a generalized cluster algebra that is a Krull domain, since its class group can be finite, and hence arithmetical invariants can be finite too. For instance, we provide an example of a generalized cluster algebra that is not a UFD, but it is half-factorial, that is there exists an element of the algebra that has at least two different factorizations but the lengths of the factorizations of each element are always the same.

In addition to the study of the class groups of generalized cluster algebras, in Section
2
we show that generalized cluster algebras are FF-domains (domains in which every element has only finitely many divisors) and that cluster variables are strong atoms (elements such that their powers factor uniquely into atoms), as was done for cluster algebras in
[
Pom25
, Section 2]
. Strong atoms have been the focus of numerous recent studies; see, for instance,
[
Ang22
,
FFN+24
,
GK24
,
RW21
]
.

Moreover, Section
5
is dedicated to the study of some ring-theoretical properties of Laurent phenomenon algebras, a class of rings introduced by Lam and Pylyavskyy
[
LP16
]
, another generalization of cluster algebras.

Notations

We denote by
\mathbb{N}
the set of positive integers, and by
\mathbb{N}_{0}
the set of non-negative integers. If
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
, we let
[a,b]=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}\mid a\leq x\leq b\}
be the (discrete) interval from
a
to
b
. A
domain

A
is a non-zero commutative ring with identity without non-zero zero divisors. We denote by
A^{\bullet}
the set of non-zero elements of
A
, by
A^{\times}
the set of units (or invertible elements) of
A
, and by
\mathbf{q}(A)
its quotient field. We say that two elements
a,b\in A
are associated if
a=\varepsilon b
for some
\varepsilon\in A^{\times}
.

2.
Generalized cluster algebras

For some reference about cluster algebras and generalized cluster algebras, see
[
BCDX20
,
Kel10
,
Wil14
]
.

Let
n,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
n+m>0
, and let
R
be the ring of integers
\mathbb{Z}
or a field of characteristic 0. We will refer to the rational function field
\cal{F}
in
n+m
independent variables over
{\mathbf{q}}(R)
as the
ambient field
.
A
cluster
is a set
{\mathbf{x}}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},x_{n+1},\ldots,x_{n+m}\}\in\mathcal{F}
of algebraically independent variables over
R
, and an
extended cluster
is a cluster with a bipartition of its element. We will denote by
L_{\mathbf{x}}
the Laurent polynomial ring
R[x_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,x_{n+m}^{\pm 1}]
.

Definition 2.1

(Exchange matrix)
.

A matrix
B=(b_{ij})\in\mathcal{M}_{n\times n}(\mathbb{Z})
is
skew-symmetrizable
if there exists a diagonal matrix
D\in\mathcal{M}_{n\times n}(\mathbb{N})
such that
DB
is skew-symmetric.
An
(n+m)\times n
integer matrix is an
exchange matrix
if the submatrix supported on the first
n
rows is skew-symmetrizable.

Let
B
be a
(n+m)\times n
exchange matrix. We denote by
{\mathbf{d}}
a
n
-tuple
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
, where
d_{i}
is a positive integer such that
b_{ji}/d_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
for every
i\in[1,n]
and
j\in[1,n+m]
. We will write
\beta_{ji}:=b_{ji}/d_{i}.

Definition 2.2

(Generalized seed)
.

A
generalized seed
of rank
n
in
\cal{F}
is a tuple
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
where

•

{\mathbf{x}}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\ldots,x_{n+m}\}
is an extended cluster: the elements
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
are called
cluster variables
, and the elements of
\{x_{n+1},\ldots,x_{n+m}\}
are called
frozen variables
;

•

B=(b_{ij})\in\mathcal{M}_{(n+m)\times n}(\mathbb{Z})
is an exchange matrix;

•

\rho=\{\rho_{i}\mid i\in[1,n]\}
is the set of
strings
, where
\rho_{i}=\{\rho_{i,0},\ldots,\rho_{i,d_{i}}\}
with
\rho_{i,0}=\rho_{i,d_{i}}=1
, and
\rho_{ij}
is a monomial in
R[x_{n+1},\cdots,x_{n+m}]
for every
j\in[1,d_{i}-1]
.

Let
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
be a generalized seed. The
directed graph

\Gamma({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
is the graph whose vertices are all
i\in[1,n]
and such that there is an edge
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces j}
if and only if
b_{ij}>0
.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}
, define the function
[x]_{+}:=x
if
x\geq 0
, and
[x]_{+}=0
otherwise.

Definition 2.3

(Mutation of generalized seeds)
.

For
i\in[1,n]
the
mutation
of a generalized seed
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
in direction
i
is a triple
\mu_{i}({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)=({\mathbf{x}}_{i},\rho^{\prime},B_{i})
with

•

{\mathbf{x}}_{i}=({\mathbf{x}}\setminus\{x_{i}\})\cup\{x_{i}^{\prime}\}
where

x_{i}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{x_{i}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{d_{i}}\rho_{i,j}\prod_{k=1}^{n+m}x_{k}^{j[\beta_{ki}]_{+}+(d_{i}-j)[-\beta_{ki}]_{+}}\right)\in\mathcal{F};

•

\rho^{\prime}=(\rho\setminus\{\rho_{i}\})\cup\{\rho_{i}^{\prime}\},
where
\rho^{\prime}_{ij}=\rho_{i,d_{i}-j}
for
j\in[0,d_{i}]
;

•

B_{i}=(b^{\prime}_{kl})
with

b_{kl}^{\prime}=\begin{cases}-b_{kl}&\text{if $k=i$ or $l=i$;}\\
b_{kl}+([b_{il}]_{+}b_{ki}+b_{il}[-b_{ki}]_{+})&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The polynomials

f_{i}:=\sum_{j=0}^{d_{i}}\rho_{i,j}\prod_{k=1}^{n+m}x_{k}^{j[\beta_{ki}]_{+}+(d_{i}-j)[-\beta_{ki}]_{+}}\in R[{\mathbf{x}}]

are called the
exchange polynomials
(associated to the seed
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
).

Definition 2.4
.

A generalized seed
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
is

(1)

coprime
if the exchange polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
are pairwise coprime in
R[{\mathbf{x}}]
;

(2)

acyclic
if the directed graph
\Gamma({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
does not contain directed cycles.

Notice that, since
d_{i}
divides
b_{ji}
for every
j\in[1,n+m]
, each
d_{i}
divides
b^{\prime}_{ji}
for every
j\in[1,n+m]
. Moreover, if
d_{i}=1
for every
i\in[1,n]
we get the classical mutation of a classical seed, as in
[
FZ02
]
.

Example 2.5
.

Let

B=\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\
-3&0\\
0&-4\end{pmatrix},\quad{\mathbf{x}}=\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\},\quad(d_{1},d_{2})=(3,2),\quad\rho_{1}=\{1,3,3,1\},\quad\rho_{2}=\{1,2,1\},

so that
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
is a generalized seed. Notice that in this case
x_{3}
is a frozen variable, thus we can mutate only in direction
1
and
2
. Therefore, mutations only change the sign of the principal part of the matrix, i.e. the submatrix of
B
supported on the first 2 rows. Recall that we denote by
\beta_{ji}
the integers
b_{ji}/d_{i}
. The exchange relations are given by

\begin{split}f_{1}=x_{1}x_{1}^{\prime}&=\prod_{k=1}^{3}x_{k}^{3[-\beta_{k1}]_{+}}+3\prod_{k=1}^{3}x_{k}^{[\beta_{k1}]_{+}+2[-\beta_{k1}]_{+}}+3\prod_{k=1}^{3}x_{k}^{2[\beta_{k1}]_{+}+[-\beta_{k1}]_{+}}+\prod_{k=1}^{3}x_{k}^{3[\beta_{k1}]_{+}}\\
&=x_{1}^{3}+3x_{1}^{2}+3x_{1}+1,\end{split}

and

f_{2}=x_{2}x_{2}^{\prime}=\prod_{k=1}^{3}x_{k}^{2[-\beta_{k2}]_{+}}+2\prod_{k=1}^{3}x_{k}^{[\beta_{k2}]_{+}+[-\beta_{k2}]_{+}}+\prod_{k=1}^{3}x_{k}^{3[\beta_{k2}]_{+}}=x_{2}^{2}+2x_{2}x_{3}^{2}+x_{3}^{4}.

Examples 2.6
.

(1)

The seed in Example
2.5
is coprime (
f_{1},
and
f_{2}
do not share any non-trivial factor), and acyclic, since
\Gamma({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)=2\overset{2}{\rightarrow}1
and it does not contain directed cycles.

(2)

Let

B=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
-1&0&1\\
0&-1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad{\mathbf{x}}=\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}

be a seed. Then

f_{1}=x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2},\quad f_{2}=x_{1}^{2}+x_{3}^{2},\quad f_{3}=x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}.

Then the seed
({\mathbf{x}},B)
is not coprime (
f_{1}=f_{3}
), but it is acyclic (
\Gamma({\mathbf{x}},B)=1\rightarrow 2\rightarrow 3
).

B=\begin{pmatrix}0&2&-2\\

f_{1}=x_{2}+1,\quad f_{2}=x_{1}+x_{3},\quad f_{3}=x_{2}+1.

2.1.
Factorization properties of generalized cluster algebras

Let us recall some definitions. For more details about factorization theory, see for instance
[
GHK06
]
.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
A
be a domain.

(1)

A non-unit
u\in A^{\bullet}
is an
atom
, or an
irreducible element
, if
u=ab
with
a,b\in A
implies
a\in A^{\times}
or
b\in A^{\times}
.

(2)

An atom
u\in A^{\bullet}
is a
strong atom
, or an
absolutely irreducible element
, if for all positive integers
n\geq 1
the only factorization (up to associates) of
u
is
u^{n}=u\cdots u.

(3)

A non-unit
p\in A^{\bullet}
is a
prime element
if
p
dividing
ab
with
a,b\in A
implies that
p
divides one of
a
and
b
.

A domain
A
is
atomic
if every non-zero non-unit element of
A
can be written as a finite product of atoms. It is an
FF-domain
(finite factorization domain) if it is atomic and every non-zero non-unit factors into atoms in only finitely many ways up to order and associates. Finally,
A
is a
factorial
domain (or a
UFD
) if it is atomic and every non-unit of
A
factors in a unique way up to order and associates.

As for cluster algebras, the following properties hold.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
be a generalized seed, and
\cal{A}
,
\cal{U}
be the generalized cluster algebra, and the generalized upper cluster algebra associated to
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B),
respectively. Then:

(1)

The units of
\cal{A}
and
\cal{U}
are the set
\{\varepsilon x_{n+1}^{a_{n+1}}\cdots x_{n+m}^{n+m}\mid\varepsilon\in R^{\times},a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

(2)

Every cluster variable is a strong atom of
\cal{A}
and
\cal{U}.

(3)

Two cluster variables
x
and
y
are associate if and only if
x=y
.

(4)

The following statements are equivalent

(a)

\cal{A}

(
resp.
\cal{U}
)
is factorial;

(b)

every cluster variable is prime in
\cal{A}

(
resp.,
\cal{U}
)
;

(c)

the cluster variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
are prime in
\cal{A}

(
resp.,
\cal{U}
)
.

(5)

Assume that
\cal{A}

(
resp.,
\cal{U}
)
is factorial, then

(i)

the exchange polynomials associated to a cluster are pairwise distinct;

(ii)

all exchange polynomials are irreducible.

Proof.

For (1), (3), (5) repeat the arguments in
[
GLS13
]
, for (2), repeat the argument in
[
Pom25
, Corollary 2.5]
, and for (4) repeat the argument in
[

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
be a generalized seed, and
A
be either the generalized cluster algebra
\cal{A}({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
or the generalized upper cluster algebra
\cal{U}({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
. Then
A
is an FF-domain. In particular, it is atomic.

Proof.

To prove the statement it suffices to show that every non-zero element of
A
has finitely many non-associated divisors
[
GHK06
, Proposition 1.5.5]
. Suppose then for sake of contradiction that there is a non-zero element
a\in A
with infinitely many non-associated divisors. Let
{\mathbf{x}}
be a cluster and
P_{\mathbf{x}}
the starfish product associated to it. Since every divisor of
a
in
A
is a divisor of
a
in
L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}
for every
i\in[0,n]
, we have that, if
b
divides
a
in
A
, then
(b,\ldots,b)
divides
(a,\ldots,a)
in
P_{\mathbf{x}}
. The ring
P_{\mathbf{x}}
is factorial, so in particular the element
(a,\ldots,a)
has only finitely many non-associated divisors in
P_{\mathbf{x}}
, hence there must exist a sequence
(b_{k})_{k\geq 1}
consisting of non-associated divisors of
a
in
A
such that

(b_{k}\ldots,b_{k})P_{\mathbf{x}}=(b_{l},\ldots,b_{l})P_{\mathbf{x}}\quad\text{for every}\,k,l\geq 1.

This implies that, for every
k,l\geq 1
, the elements
b_{k}
and
b_{l}
are associated in
{L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}}
for every
i\in[0,n]
, in particular, for every pair
(j,i)\in[1,n+m]\times[0,n]
there exist
a_{j,i}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\varepsilon_{i}\in R^{\times}
such that

\varepsilon_{i}x_{1}^{a_{1,i}}\cdots{x_{i}^{\prime}}^{a_{i,i}}\cdots x_{n+m}^{a_{{n+m},i}}b_{k}=b_{l}.

Then for every
i\in[1,n]
we have

\varepsilon_{i}x_{1}^{a_{1,i}}\cdots{x_{i}^{\prime}}^{a_{i,i}}\cdots x_{n+m}^{a_{{n+m},i}}b_{k}=\varepsilon_{0}x_{1}^{a_{1,0}}\cdots{x_{i}}^{a_{i,0}}\cdots x_{n+m}^{a_{{n+m},0}}b_{k},

whence
a_{j,i}=a_{j,0}
and
a_{i,i}=a_{i,0}=0
, and
\varepsilon_{i}=\varepsilon_{0}
for every
(j,i)\in[1,n+m]\times[1,n]
. This implies that
b_{k}=\varepsilon_{0}x_{n+1}^{a_{n+1,0}}\cdots x_{n+m}^{a_{n+m,0}}b_{l},
and this is a contradiction because we assumed that
b_{k}
and
b_{l}
are not associated in
P_{\mathbf{x}}
.
∎

3.
Starfish Lemma

This section aims to prove the Starfish Lemma for generalized cluster algebras. This has been already done in
[
GSV18
]
for a more general setting, but here we present a more straightforward proof that follows the original proof of the Starfish Lemma for classical cluster algebras, as in
[
BFZ05
]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

[
BCDX20
, Lemma 3.9]

Let
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
be a coprime generalized seed. Then

S_{\mathbf{x}}=\bigcap_{i=2}^{n}R[x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\pm 1},\cdots,x_{i-1}^{\pm 1},x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime},x_{i+1}^{\pm 1},\cdots,x_{n+m}^{\pm 1}]

(3.1)

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
be a generalized seed. Let
x_{2}^{\prime}
and
x_{2}^{\prime\prime}
be the cluster variables exchanged with
x_{2}
in the generalized seeds
\mu_{2}({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
and
\mu_{2}\mu_{1}({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
, respectively, then

R[x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2},x_{2}^{\prime},x_{3}^{\pm 1},\ldots,x_{n+m}^{\pm 1}]=R[x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2},x_{2}^{\prime\prime},x_{3}^{\pm 1},\ldots,x_{n+m}^{\pm 1}]

(3.2)

Before proceeding with the proof, we present an example to help the reader navigate the technical details of the lemma.

Example 3.3
.

Let

B=\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\
-3&0\\
0&-4\end{pmatrix},\quad{\mathbf{x}}=\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\},\quad\rho_{1}=\{1,3,3,1\},\quad\rho_{2}=\{1,2,1\},

as in Example

in the generalized seeds
\mu_{2}({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)

\mu_{2}\mu_{1}({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
, respectively. Observe that

Therefore we have

\displaystyle x_{1}x_{1}^{\prime}=x_{2}^{3}+3x_{2}^{2}+3x_{2}+1

\displaystyle x_{2}x_{2}^{\prime}=x_{1}^{2}+2x_{1}x_{3}^{2}+{x_{3}^{4}},

\displaystyle x_{2}x_{2}^{\prime\prime}={x_{1}^{\prime}}^{2}x_{3}^{4}+2{x_{1}^{\prime}}x_{3}^{2}+1.

Then if we replace

we get

\begin{split}x_{2}x_{2}^{\prime\prime}&={x_{1}^{\prime}}^{2}x_{3}^{4}+2{x_{1}^{\prime}}x_{3}^{2}+1\\
&=(x_{2}x_{2}^{\prime}-x_{1}^{2}-2x_{1}x_{3}^{2}){x_{1}^{\prime}}^{2}+2{x_{1}^{\prime}}x_{3}^{2}+1\\
&=x_{2}x_{2}^{\prime}{x_{1}^{\prime}}^{2}-(x_{2}^{3}+3x_{2}^{2}+3x_{2}+1)^{2}-2x_{3}^{2}x_{1}^{\prime}(x_{2}^{3}+3x_{2}^{2}+3x_{2}+1)+2{x_{1}^{\prime}}x_{3}^{2}+1\\
&=x_{2}x_{2}^{\prime}{x_{1}^{\prime}}^{2}-(x_{2}^{3}+3x_{2}^{2}+3x_{2})^{2}-2x_{2}^{3}-6x_{2}^{2}-6x_{2}+2x_{3}^{2}x_{1}^{\prime}(x_{2}^{3}+3x_{2}^{2}+3x_{2})\end{split}

and in particular

&=x_{2}x_{2}^{\prime\prime}x_{1}^{2}-x_{3}^{4}(x_{2}^{3}+3x_{2}^{2}+3x_{2})^{2}-2x_{3}^{4}(x_{2}^{3}+3x_{2}^{2}+3x_{2})+x_{3}^{2}x_{1}(x_{2}^{3}+3x_{2}^{2}+3x_{2})\end{split}

and therefore

(3.4)

Observe that

\begin{split}\frac{q_{3}g\left(x_{1}\right){x^{\prime}_{1}}^{b}}{r_{2}}&=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d_{2}}\frac{g_{k}q_{3}}{r_{2}}x_{1}^{k\beta_{12}}\right){x^{\prime}_{1}}^{b}=\sum_{k=1}^{d_{2}}\frac{g_{k}q_{3}}{r_{2}}(x_{1}x_{1}^{\prime})^{k\beta_{12}}{x^{\prime}_{1}}^{(d_{2}-k)\beta_{12}}\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{d_{2}}\frac{g_{k}q_{3}}{r_{2}}(f(x_{2})+r_{1})^{k\beta_{12}}{x^{\prime}_{1}}^{(d_{2}-k)\beta_{12}}.\end{split}

(3.5)

The element
\frac{q_{2}q_{3}}{r_{2}}(f(x_{2})+r_{1})^{b}
can we rewritten as

x_{2}P_{d_{2}}+\frac{q_{2}q_{3}r_{1}^{b}}{r_{2}}=x_{2}P_{d_{2}}+r_{3},

(3.6)

where
P_{d_{2}}
is a polynomial in
x_{2}
. In addition, for every
i\in[1,d_{2}-1]
we have

\begin{split}\frac{g_{k}q_{3}}{r_{2}}(f(x_{2})+r_{1})^{k\beta_{12}}{x^{\prime}_{1}}^{(d_{2}-k)\beta_{12}}=x_{2}P_{k}+\frac{g_{k}q_{3}r_{1}^{k\beta_{12}}}{r_{2}}{x^{\prime}_{1}}^{(d_{2}-k)\beta_{12}}=x_{2}P_{k}+h_{k}{x^{\prime}_{1}}^{(d_{2}-k)\beta_{12}},\end{split}

(3.7)

where
P_{r}
is a polynomial in
x_{2}
. Combining (
3.5
) with (
3.6
) and (
3.7
), we get

\begin{split}\frac{q_{3}g\left(x_{1}\right){x^{\prime}_{1}}^{b}}{r_{2}}&=\sum_{k=1}^{d_{2}}\frac{g_{k}q_{3}}{r_{2}}(f(x_{2})+r_{1})^{k\beta_{12}}{x^{\prime}_{1}}^{(d_{2}-k)\beta_{12}}\\
&=x_{2}P_{d_{2}}+r_{3}+\sum_{k=1}^{d_{2}-1}(x_{2}P_{k}+h_{k}{x^{\prime}_{1}}^{(d_{2}-k)\beta_{12}}).\end{split}

Thus, (
3.4
) can be rewritten as

x_{2}x_{2}^{\prime\prime}=x_{2}\left(\frac{q_{3}x_{2}^{\prime}{x^{\prime}_{1}}^{b}}{r_{2}}\right)+x_{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d_{2}}P_{k}.

Hence
x_{2}^{\prime\prime}\in R[x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2},x_{2}^{\prime}]
.

Similarly, one can prove that
x_{2}^{\prime}\in R[x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2},x_{2}^{\prime\prime}]
. ∎

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
be a generalized seed, and
({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},\rho^{\prime},B^{\prime})
be the seed obtained mutating
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
in direction
k\in[1,n]
. If the two seeds are both coprime, then
S_{\mathbf{x}}=S_{{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

The theorem is a direct consequence of Proposition
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
.
∎

Corollary 3.5
.

Assume that all the generalized seeds mutation-equivalent to a generalized seed
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
are coprime. Then the upper bound
S_{\mathbf{x}}
is independent of the choice of generalized seeds mutation-equivalent to
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
, and so is equal to the generalized upper cluster algebra
\cal{U}({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B).

A classical seed
({\mathbf{x}},B)
is coprime if and only if no two
columns of
B
are proportional to each other with the proportionality coefficient being
a ratio of two odd integers
[
BFZ05
, Lemma 3.1]
.
For generalized seeds, this is not true anymore as the following example shows.

Example 3.6
.

Let
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
be the generalized seed given by

•

{\mathbf{x}}=\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}
,

•

B=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\
1&0&-3\\
0&3&0\end{pmatrix},

•

\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}=\{1,1\}
and
\rho_{3}=\{1,1,4,1\}.

Observe that the first and the last column of
B
are proportional to each other with
3
as a proportionality coefficient. On the other hand, the exchange polynomials are given by

f_{1}=1+x_{2},\quad f_{2}=x_{1}+x_{3}^{3},\quad f_{3}=x_{2}^{3}+x_{2}^{2}+4x_{2}+1,

hence they are coprime.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
be a generalized seed. If no two columns of
B
are proportional to each other, then the seed is coprime.

Proof.

For every
f=\sum_{k}c_{k}{\mathbf{x}}^{{\mathbf{a}}_{k}}\in R[{\mathbf{x}}]
, we denote by
N(f)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
the Newton polytope of
f
, that is

N(f)=\left\{\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}{\mathbf{a}}_{k}\mid\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}=1,\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\right\}.

Note that the Newton polytope
N(fg)
of a product is equal to the Minkowski sum
N(f)+N(g)
. Denote by
B(j)
the
j
-th column of
B
.

Let
i\in[1,n]
and
f_{i}
be an exchange polynomial. By definition, we can write
f_{i}=\sum_{r=0}^{d_{i}}\rho_{r,i}{\mathbf{x}}^{{\mathbf{a}}_{r,i}}.
Observe that, if
r\in[1,d_{i}-1]
, then

{\mathbf{a}}_{r,i}=\frac{r}{d_{i}}{\mathbf{a}}_{0,i}+\frac{d_{i}-r}{d_{i}}{\mathbf{a}}_{d_{i},i},

that is,
{\mathbf{a}}_{r,i}
belongs to the line segment with endpoints
{\mathbf{a}}_{0,i}
and
{\mathbf{a}}_{d_{i},i}
, and hence
N(f_{i})
is a line segment. Furthermore,
N(f_{i})
is parallel to
B(i)
. It follows that, for every non-trivial factor
g
of
f_{i}
, the Newton polytope
N(g)
is also a line segment parallel to
N(f_{i})
. Suppose now that, by contradiction, there exist
i\neq j
such that
f_{i}
and
f_{j}
have a non-trivial common factor. Then
N(f_{i})
and
N(f_{j})
are collinear and so
pB(i)=qB(j)
, for some
p,q\in\mathbb{Q}.
Hence a contradiction.∎

Combining Lemma
3.7
with the fact that matrix mutations preserve the rank of the matrix (
[
BFZ05
, Lemma 3.2]
), one can prove the following proposition.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
be a generalized seed. If
B
has full rank, then all seeds are coprime. In particular, we have that
\mathcal{U}=S_{{\mathbf{y}}}
for every cluster
{\mathbf{y}}
.

We have shown that if a seed
(\mathbf{x},\rho,B)
is coprime, then the generalized upper cluster algebra
\mathcal{U}(\mathbf{x},\rho,B)
coincides with its upper bound
S_{\mathbf{x}}
. In
[
BCDX20
]
the authors proved that if
(\mathbf{x},\rho,B)
is acyclic and coprime, then the upper bound
S_{\mathbf{x}}
coincide with its lower bound, that is the
R
-algebra generated by the elements
\{x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{n},x_{n}^{\prime},x_{n+1},\ldots,x_{n+m}\}.
More precisely,

Theorem 3.9

(
[
BCDX20
, Theorem 3.10]
)
.

Let
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
be a generalized seed. If the seed is acyclic and coprime, then

4.
Generalized cluster algebras and their class group

4.1.
The class group of a generalized cluster algebra

Let
A
be a domain and
\mathfrak{X}(A)
be the set of its height-1 prime ideals. The domain
A
is a
Krull domain
if the localization
A_{\mathfrak{p}}
at every height-1 prime ideal
{\mathfrak{p}}
is a discrete valuation domain,
A
is the intersection of all these DVRs and every non-zero element
a\in A
is contained in at most a finite number of height-1 prime ideals of
A
.
The
class group

\cal{C}(A)
of a Krull domain
A
is

\cal{C}(A)=\cal{F}({\mathfrak{X}}(A))/\cal{H}(A)

where
\cal{F}({\mathfrak{X}}(A))
is the free abelian group over the set of height-1 prime ideals and
\cal{H}(A)=\{a{\mathbf{q}}(A)\mid a\in{\mathbf{q}}(A)^{\bullet}\}
is the subgroup of principal ideals of the quotient field. A Krull domain
A
is factorial if and only if its class group
\cal{C}(A)
is trivial. For more details, see
[
Fos73
,
GHK06
]
. Locally acyclic cluster algebras (
[
GELS19
]
) and full rank upper cluster algebras (
[
Pom25
]
) are Krull domains. It is immediate to see that this is also true for locally acyclic generalized cluster algebras (for the definition see
[
BCDX20
]
), and for full rank generalized upper cluster algebras. The Markov cluster algebra is the only known example of a cluster algebra that is not a Krull domain (
[
GELS19
, Section 6]
). Nevertheless, the question of whether or not a (generalized) upper cluster algebra fails to be a Krull domain is still open.

Theorem 4.1

(
[
GELS19
, Theorems 3.1 and 3.2]
)
.

Let
A
be a Krull domain, and let
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in A
be such that
A[x_{1}^{-1},\dots,x_{n}^{-1}]=D[x_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]
is a factorial Laurent polynomial ring for some subring
D
of
A
. Let
\mathfrak{p}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{p}_{t}
be the pairwise distinct height-1 prime ideals of
A
containing one of the elements
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. Suppose that

x_{i}A={\sideset{}{\mkern-2.0mu\raisebox{2.04439pt}{$\vphantom{\prod}{}^{v}$}}{\prod}_{l=1}^{t}}\mathfrak{p}_{j}^{a_{ij}},

with
\mathbf{a}_{i}=(a_{ij})_{j=1}^{t}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{t}.
Then
\mathcal{C}(A)\cong\mathbb{Z}^{t}/\langle\mathbf{a}_{i}\mid i\in[1,n]\rangle
and
\cal{C}(A)
is generated by
[\mathfrak{p}_{1}],\dots,[\mathfrak{p}_{t}]
.

Suppose in addition that
D
is infinite and either
n\geq 2
or
n=1
and
D
has at least
|D|
height-1 prime ideals. Then every class of
\mathcal{C}(A)
contains precisely
|D|
height-1 prime ideals.

As a direct application of the previous theorem we obtain the following result.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
be a generalized seed, and
A
be either the generalized cluster algebra
\cal{A}({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
or the generalized upper cluster algebra
\cal{U}({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
. Assume that
A
is a Krull domain. Let
{\mathfrak{p}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathfrak{p}}_{r}
be the pairwise distinct height-1 prime ideals of
A
containing one of the elements
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. Suppose that

x_{i}A={\sideset{}{\mkern-2.0mu\raisebox{2.04439pt}{$\vphantom{\prod}{}^{v}$}}{\prod}_{l=1}^{r}}\mathfrak{p}_{j}^{a_{ij}},

with
\mathbf{a}_{i}=(a_{ij})_{j=1}^{r}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r}.
Then
\mathcal{C}(A)\cong\mathbb{Z}^{r}/\langle\mathbf{a}_{i}\mid i\in[1,n]\rangle
and
\cal{C}(A)
is generated by
[\mathfrak{p}_{1}],\dots,[\mathfrak{p}_{r}]
.

In addition, every class of
\cal{C}(A)
contains precisely
|R|
height-1 prime ideals.

Proof.

The theorem is a direct application of Theorem
4.1
, since
A[{\mathbf{x}}^{-1}]=R[{\mathbf{x}}^{\pm 1}]
, and
R[{\mathbf{x}}^{\pm 1}]
is a factorial Laurent polynomial ring.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

The algebraic structure of a Krull domain is completely determined (up to units) by its class group and the distribution of prime divisors in each class, see
[
GHK06
, Theorem 2.5.4]
.
Moreover, Theorem
4.1
has important consequences in understanding factorization properties of a (generalized) cluster algebra. Let
\cal{A}
be a (generalized) cluster algebra or a (generalized) upper cluster algebra, and
\cal{C}(\cal{A})
its class group. If
\cal{C}(\cal{A})
is trivial, then
\cal{A}
is factorial. If the class group
\cal{C}(\cal{A})
is infinite, then for every non-empty finite subset
L=\{l_{1},\ldots,l_{k}\}
of
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
there exists
a\in\cal{A}
such that

a=u_{1,1}\cdots u_{1,l_{1}}=\cdots=u_{l_{1},1}\cdots u_{l_{1},l_{1}},

where
u_{ij}
are atoms of
\cal{A}
. This follows from a more general result of Kainrath on infinite class groups, see
[
Kai99
]
or
[
GHK06
, Theorem 7.4.1]
. If the class group
\cal{C}(\cal{A})
is a finite group, then factorization properties of
\cal{A}
can be studied via some arithmetic invariants, like the elasticity or the catenary degree. For more details, see the survey
[
Sch16
]
.

Examples 4.4
.

The previous theorems describe the structure of the class groups of certain (generalized) cluster algebras that are Krull domains; however, without explicitly identifying the height-1 prime ideals containing a cluster variable, an explicit computation of the class group is not possible. Here are two classes of examples where such computations have been carried out.

(1)

Let
\cal{A}=\cal{A}({\mathbf{x}},B)
be an acyclic cluster algebra of rank
n
over the ring of integer
\mathbb{Z},
without isolated vertices. We say that two indices
i,j\in[1,n]
are
partner
if the corresponding exchange polynomials
f_{i}=x_{i}x_{i}^{\prime},f_{j}=x_{j}x_{j}^{\prime}
do not share a non-trivial common factor. Partnership is an equivalence relation on
[1,n]
and the equivalence classes are called partner sets. For a partner set
V\subseteq[1,n]
and an positive integer
d\in\mathbb{N}
, denote by
\mathsf{c}(V,d)
the number of
i\in V
for which
d
divides
d_{i}:=\mathrm{gcd}\{b_{ji}\mid j\in[1,n]\}
the greatest common divisor of the
i
-th column of
B
. Then the class group of
\cal{A}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
with

r=\sum_{V}\sum_{d\in\mathbb{N},\text{ odd}}(2^{\mathsf{c}(V,d)}-1)-|V|,

where the outer sum is taken over all partner sets
V\subseteq[1,n]
, see
[
GELS19
, Theorem B]
. A more general formula for cluster algebras over arbitrary fields can be found in
[
GELS19
, Theorem 5.5, Remark 5.6]
. See
[
GELS19
, Section 5]
for a long list of examples where the class group is explicitely computed, including class groups of algebras of finite type.

(2)

Let
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{x}},B)
be a full rank upper cluster algebra of rank
n
over a factorial domain
R
. For
i\in[1,n]
, let
l_{i}
be the number of irreducible factors of the exchange polynomial
f_{i}=x_{i}x_{i}^{\prime}
in
R[{\mathbf{x}}].
Then the class group of
\cal{U}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
, where
r=\sum_{i}l_{i}-n
. For a proof and examples see
[
Pom25
, Theorem 3.8]
.

Remark 4.5
.

Recall that a domain is Krull if and only if it is completely integrally closed and satisfies the ACC on divisorial ideals. (Generalized) upper cluster algebras are intersections of factorial domains by construction, hence they are completely integrally closed. Therefore, if they fail to be Krull domains, they must not satisfy the ACC on divisorial ideals.

Classical (upper) cluster algebras have free class groups.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
({\mathbf{x}},B)
be a (classical) seed, and
A
be either the cluster algebra
\cal{A}({\mathbf{x}},B)
or the upper cluster algebra
\cal{U}({\mathbf{x}},B)
. Assume that
A
is a Krull domain., and let
r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
denote the number of height-1 prime ideals that contain one of the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. Then the class group
\cal{C}(A)
of
A
is free abelian of rank
r-n
.

Proof.

For the proof see
[
GELS19
, Theorem A]
and
[
Pom25
, Theorem 3.4]
for the case of cluster algebras and upper cluster algebras, respecively.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

Theorem
4.6
and Theorem
4.1
show us the different behavior of cluster algebras and generalized cluster algebras. The class group of a cluster algebra and the class group of a generalized cluster algebra are always finitely generated and abelian. Exchange polynomials of a cluster algebra do not have repeated factors
[
GELS19
, Proposition 2.3]
, and this fact implies that the class group is torsion-free. In general, this is not the case for generalized cluster algebra, see Example
4.8
for an exchange polynomial with a square factor. Moreover, in Example
4.11
we show an example of a generalized cluster algebra with a torsion class group.

Example 4.8
.

Consider the generalized seed
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
given by

•

{\mathbf{x}}=\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
;

•

B=\begin{pmatrix}0&-2\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
;

•

\rho_{1}=\{1,1\},

\rho_{2}=\{1,2,1\}
.

The exchange polynomials are

f_{1}=x_{2}+1,\quad f_{2}=x_{1}^{2}+2x_{1}+1.

Thus,
f_{2}=(x_{1}+1)^{2}
has a square factor.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
be a generalized seed, and
\cal{U}=\cal{U}({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
the generalized upper cluster algebra. Assume that
\cal{U}
is a Krull domain. Then for every height-1 prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
of
\cal{U}
there exists a cluster
{\mathbf{y}}
such that
\mathfrak{p}L_{{\mathbf{y}}}\in\mathfrak{X}(L_{{\mathbf{y}}}).

Furthermore, if
\cal{U}=S_{\mathbf{y}}
for some cluster
{\mathbf{y}}
, then for every height-1 prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
of
\cal{U}
there exists
k\in[0,n]
such that
\mathfrak{p}L_{{\mathbf{y}}_{k}}\in\mathfrak{X}(L_{{\mathbf{y}}_{k}}).

Proof.

For the proof, one can repeat the same argument in
[
Pom25
, Theorem 3.6]
.
∎

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
be a generalized seed and suppose that
\cal{U}({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)=S_{\mathbf{x}}
. Let
i\in[1,n]
, and consider the exchange polynomial
f_{i}=x_{i}x_{i}^{\prime}
. Let
r_{1},\ldots,r_{t}\in R[{\mathbf{x}}]
be the distinct and non-associated irreducible polynomials that divide
f_{i}
. Then

\{r_{j}L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}\cap\cal{U}\mid j\in[1,t]\}

is the set of all the height-1 prime ideals of
\cal{U}
that contain
x_{i}
.

Proof.

Let
r_{j}
be an irreducible element of
f_{i}
in
R[{\mathbf{x}}]
. Since no monomial in
{\mathbf{x}}
divides the exchange polynomials,
r_{j}
is also irreducible, and hence prime, in
L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}
. Denote by
{\mathfrak{p}}^{\prime}=r_{j}L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}
the height-1 prime ideal of
L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}
generated by
r_{j}
. Since
L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}=\cal{U}[{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{-1}]
, we have that
{\mathfrak{p}}^{\prime}\cap\cal{U}
is a height-1 prime ideal of
\cal{U}
that contains
x_{i}
.

Vice versa, let
{\mathfrak{p}}
be a height-1 prime ideal of
\cal{U}
that contains the cluster variable
x_{i}
. By Proposition
4.9
, the ideal
{\mathfrak{p}}L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{k}}
is a height-1 prime ideal of
L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{k}}
for some
k\in[1,n]
. Notice that
x_{i}
is a unit in all the Laurent polynomials rings
L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{j}}
with
j\neq i
, so the only possibility is that
{\mathfrak{p}}L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}
is a height-1 prime ideal of
L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}.
In particular,
f_{i}=x_{i}x_{i}^{\prime}\in{\mathfrak{p}}L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}
, hence there exists an irreducible factor
r_{j}
of
f_{i}
such that
r_{j}\in{\mathfrak{p}}L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}
. The element
r_{j}
is irreducible also in
L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}
, that is factorial, therefore
r_{j}L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}={\mathfrak{p}}L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}
, and
{\mathfrak{p}}=r_{j}L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}\cap\cal{U}
.

∎

This theorem allows us to get more information about height-1 prime ideals that contain a cluster variable and hence more information on the class group. In the next example we show how to compute the class group of a generalized cluster algebra.

Example 4.11
.

Consider the seed
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
given in Example
4.8
. The directed graph
\Gamma({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
is

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 2}

and it is acyclic. In addition,
\mathrm{det}(B)=2
, so
B
has full rank and the seed is coprime. Theorem
3.9
then implies that

\cal{A}({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)=\cal{U}({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)=S_{\mathbf{x}}.

In particular, Corollary
4.10
implies that
{\mathfrak{p}}_{1}=(x_{2}+1)L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}\cap\cal{U}
is the only height-1 prime ideal that contains
x_{1}
and that
{\mathfrak{p}}_{2}=(x_{1}+1)L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{2}}\cap\cal{U}
is the only height-1 prime ideal that contains
x_{2}
.
Then by Corollary
4.2
we have

\cal{C}(\cal{U})\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}/\langle{\mathbf{a}}_{1},{\mathbf{a}}_{2}\rangle,

where

x_{1}\cal{U}={\mathfrak{p}}_{1}^{a_{11}}\cdot_{v}{\mathfrak{p}}_{2}^{a_{12}},\quad\text{and}\quad x_{2}\cal{U}={\mathfrak{p}}_{1}^{a_{21}}\cdot_{v}{\mathfrak{p}}_{2}^{a_{22}}.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
be a generalized seed, and
\cal{U}=\cal{U}({\mathbf{x}},\rho,B)
the generalized upper cluster algebra associated to it. Assume that
\cal{U}=S_{\mathbf{y}}
for some cluster
{\mathbf{y}}
. Then
\cal{U}
is factorial if and only if the exchange polynomials
f_{i}=y_{i}y_{i}^{\prime}\in R[{\mathbf{y}}]
are irreducible.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.12
we only need to prove that if the exchange polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
are irreducible, then
\cal{U}
is factorial. By Theorem
4.2
, the class group
\cal{C}(\cal{U})
of
\cal{U}
is
\mathbb{Z}^{r}/\langle{\mathbf{a}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{a}}_{n}\rangle
, where
{\mathfrak{p}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathfrak{p}}_{r}
are the height-1 prime ideals that contain one of the variables
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
and
{\mathbf{a}}_{i}=(a_{ij})_{j=1}^{r}
is such that

y_{i}\cal{U}={\mathfrak{p}}_{1}^{a_{i1}}\cdot_{v}\cdots\cdot_{v}{\mathfrak{p}}_{r}^{a_{ir}}.

Corollary
4.10
implies that
\{f_{i}L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}\cap\cal{A}\mid i\in[1,n]\}
are the only height-1 prime ideals that contain
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, hence
r=n
and
{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}=f_{i}L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}\cap\cal{U}
for every

, whence
a_{ij}=0
for every
j\neq i
and
a_{ii}=1
. Therefore
\langle{\mathbf{a}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{a}}_{n}\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
\cal{C}(\cal{U})=0
, so
\cal{U}
is factorial.
∎

4.2.
Every finitely generated abelian group is the class group of a generalized cluster algebra

Throughout all this section, the ground ring
R
is an algebraically closed field.

In this section, we show that every finitely generated abelian group can be realized as the class group of a generalized cluster algebra. We proceed showing some examples first.

Examples 4.13
.

(1)

Let
G=\mathbb{Z}^{n},
with
n\geq 1.
We want to construct a cluster algebra whose class group is isomorphic to
G
. Let

B=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\

5.
LP algebras

This section is dedicated to Laurent phenomenon algebras (or LP algebras). They were introduced by Lam and Pylyavskyy in
[
LP16
]
in order to get a general definition of those algebras for which the Laurent phenomenon holds. The aim of this section is to outline, step by step, that their behaviour is quite similar to the one of cluster algebras. A cluster algebra is an LP algebra under the condition of primitivity (the greatest common divisors of each column of any seed is 1) and coprimality (see Proposition
5.12
). We show that the Markov cluster algebra is an LP algebra (see Example
5.15
), even if the Markov seed is neither primitive nor coprime. In general an LP algebra and a cluster algebra coming from the same seed do not coincide (see Example
5.13
).

Definition 5.1

(LP seed)
.

A
Laurent phenomenon seed
(in short
LP seed
) of rank
n
in
\cal{F}
is a pair
({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{F}})
such that

(i)

{\mathbf{x}}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
is a cluster,

(ii)

{\mathbf{F}}=\{F_{1},\ldots,F_{n}\}
is a collection of irreducible elements of
R[{\mathbf{x}}]
such that for each
i,j\in[1,n]
,
x_{j}\nmid F_{i}
, and
F_{i}
does not depend on
x_{i}
. The polynomials
F_{1},\ldots,F_{n}
are called the
exchange polynomials
(associated to
({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{F}})
).

Example 5.2
.

Suppose that
R=\mathbb{Z}
or
R=\mathbb{Q}
. Let
({\mathbf{x}},B)
be a classical seed of rank
n
, and set
d_{i}=\mathrm{gcd}\{b_{ji}\mid j\in[1,n]\}
. Let
{\mathbf{F}}=\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\}
be the exchange polynomials associated to
({\mathbf{x}},B).
Then
({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{F}})
is a LP seed if and only if
d_{i}
is a power of
2
for every
i\in[1,n]
. If instead
R
is an algebraically closed field, then
({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{F}})
is a LP seed if and only if
d_{i}=1
for every
i\in[1,n].

[
GELS19
, Proposition 2.3]

Remark 5.3
.

By common convention, a polynomial
f\in R[{\mathbf{x}}]
is an
irreducible polynomial
if it is a non-constant polynomial such that cannot be factored into the product of two non-constant polynomials. In contrast, a polynomial
f\in R[{\mathbf{x}}]
is an
irreducible element
of
R[{\mathbf{x}}]
if it is not a unit and it is not a product of two non-units. For instance, the polynomial
2\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
is an irreducible element of
\mathbb{Z}[x]
, but it is not an irreducible polynomial of
\mathbb{Z}[x]
, and the polynomial
2x-4
is an irreducible polynomial of
\mathbb{Z}[x]
but it is not an irreducible element of
\mathbb{Z}[x]
. In the original definition of an LP algebra, Lam and Pylyavskyy
[
LP16
]
assumed that the exchange polynomial
F_{i}
is an
irreducible element of

R[{\mathbf{x}}]
. This implies that the polynomials
F_{i}
cannot be invertible. On the other hand, in the proof of
[
DL22
, Lemma 4.6]
it is implicitly assumed that if the exchange polynomial is constant, then it is invertible.

Let
F,G\in R(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
be two rational functions. Denote by
F|_{x_{i}\leftarrow G}
the expression obtained by substituting
G
for
x_{i}
in
F
. If
x_{i}
appears in
F
, we write
x_{i}\in F
. Otherwise, we write
x_{i}\notin F
.

Definition 5.4

(Exchange Laurent polynomials)
.

Let
({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{F}})
be an LP seed. For each
F_{j}\in{\mathbf{F}}
, define a Laurent polynomial
\hat{F}_{j}=\frac{F_{j}}{x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{j-1}^{a_{j-1}}x_{j+1}^{a_{j+1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}}}
, where
a_{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is maximal such that
F_{k}^{a_{k}}
divides
F_{j}|_{x_{k}\leftarrow F_{k}/x}
in
R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1},{x}^{-1},x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. The Laurent polynomials
{\mathbf{\hat{F}}}=\{\hat{F}_{1},\ldots,\hat{F}_{n}\}
are called the
exchange Laurent polynomials
(associated to
({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{F}})
).

Example 5.5

(
[

.

Let

,
{\mathbf{x}}=\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\},

Then

and

since

and
x_{2}\in F_{1},F_{3}.
For the same reason,

does not appear in the denominator of

, since

. So it remains to compute the exponent of

in the denominator of

In order to that, we replace in

by

Since

Definition 5.6

(Mutation of LP seed)
.

Let
({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{F}})
be an LP seed, and
k\in[1,n]
. Define a new pair

\mu_{k}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{F}}):=(\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{n}\},\{F_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,F_{k},\ldots,F_{n}^{\prime}\}),

where

and the

(1)

, then

;

(2)

If

(i)

Define
G_{i}:=F_{i}|_{x_{k}\leftarrow N_{k}}
, where

(ii)

Define
H_{i}
to be

with all common factors between

and
\hat{F}_{k}|_{x_{i}\leftarrow 0}
removed. Notice that
H_{i}
is defined only up to units.

(iii)

Let

be a Laurent monomial in
\{x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{n}^{\prime}\}
such that

and it is not divisible by any variable in
{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}
.

Then we say that the new pair
\mu_{k}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{F}})
is obtained from the LP seed
({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{F}})
by the

in direction
k
.

One can prove that
\mu_{k}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{F}})
is also an LP seed, and that
\mu_{k}
is an involution. See
[
DL22
]
for more details.

Example 5.15

(Markov Seed)
.

Let
({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{F}})
be the LP seed given by
{\mathbf{F}}=\{x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2},x_{1}^{2}+x_{3}^{2},x_{2}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}\}
. By
[
LP16
, Lemma 2.7]
,
{\mathbf{F}}=\hat{{\mathbf{F}}}
. If
({\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{G}})
is a seed mutation-equivalent to
({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{F}})
, then
{\mathbf{G}}=\{y_{2}^{2}+y_{3}^{2},y_{1}^{2}+y_{3}^{2},y_{2}^{2}+y_{1}^{2}\}
and hence
\hat{{\mathbf{G}}}={\mathbf{G}}
. This implies that the (upper) LP algebra associated to
({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{F}})
is the Markov (upper) cluster algebra. In particular,
\cal{A}_{LP}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{F}})
is not factorial. In addition, this example shows that the conditions in Proposition
5.12
are not necessary conditions.

An LP seed
({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{F}})
is
sign-skew symmetric
if
x_{i}\in F_{j}
if and only if
x_{j}\in F_{i}
. Note that if a seed is sign-skew symmetric, then any mutations of it is sign-skew symmetric as well.

(

DL22
, Corollary 4.14]

Let

for every

Theorem 5.19
.

Let
\cal{U}
be an upper LP algebra. If
\cal{U}=\bigcap_{k=0}^{n}L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{k}}
for some cluster
{\mathbf{x}}
, then
\cal{U}
is factorial.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.14
, it is enough to show that
x_{k}
is prime in
\cal{U}
for every
k\in[1,n]
. We claim that it suffices to show that
x_{k}
is prime in
L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{k}}
. Assume that
x_{k}
is prime in
L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{k}}
, and suppose by contradiction that there exist
a,b\in\cal{U}
such that
x_{k}\mid_{U}ab
, but
x_{k}
does not divide
a
and
b
in
\cal{U}
. Since
x_{k}
is a unit in
L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}
for every
i\in[1,n]\setminus\{k\}
, then
x_{k}
does not divide
a,b
in
L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{k}}
. Therefore we obtain a contradiction, because clearly
x_{k}\mid_{L_{{\mathbf{x}}_{k}}}ab.
Now, the primeness of
x_{k}
follows from the exchange relation and the irreducibility of
F_{k}
.
∎

Remark 5.20
.

A necessary condition for a (upper) cluster algebra to be factorial is that exchange polynomials must be pairwise distinct and irreducible elements. From one hand exchange Laurent polynomials of an LP seed are irreducible by construction. Moreover, the authors did not find examples of an LP seed with two equal exchange Laurent polynomials.
