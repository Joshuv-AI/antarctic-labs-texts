Ecofriendly algal extracellular polymeric substance-based nanobiocomposite for photodegradation of bisphenol A
Authors: Bikramjit Bhattacharya, Abhrajit Debroy, Dhivya Viswanathan, Feeba Nissi Anandraj, KrishnaRao Eswar Neerugatti, Amitava Mukherjee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBisphenol A (BPA), a common plasticizer and an emerging pollutant is found in the water bodies due to the degradation of microplastics. The present work studies the efficiency of algal extracellular polymeric substance (EPS)-based zeolite/nanoceria nanobiocomposite for the degradation of BPA. Zeolite was heat/acid activated for deposition of photoactive CeO2 nanoparticles. The nanobiocomposite was characterized by X-ray diffractometer (XRD), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) analysis. The removal of BPA was studied with different parameters, i.e., BPA concentration (20–60 mg/L) and pH (6–10). 31% BPA got adsorbed under dark conditions. Nanobiocomposite under ultraviolet A irradiation increased the BPA removal to 81% after 120 min of the photocatalytic reaction. The BPA removal mechanism was elucidated using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis. Biosafety assessment revealed 96% and 89% cell viability in Chlorella sp. and Allium sativum with the degraded solution. Furthermore, the biosafety evaluation for the nanobiocomposite also revealed that EPS enables synergism as both green and efficient approach for BPA breakdown.

Introduction

Bisphenol A (BPA) is a white, crystalline solid plasticizer used in 70% of polycarbonate plastic production, including food storage containers1 and reusable plastic water bottles,2 renowned for their durability, transparency, and heat resistance. Approximately 30% of BPA production is allocated for the manufacturing of epoxy resins, renowned for their strong adhesiveness and chemical resistance.3 These resins are used to make protective coatings for the commonly used beverage cans.4 Besides being utilized as a plasticizer, BPA also acts as an agonist that mimics estradiol, a form of estrogen that binds to the ERα and ERβ receptors in various tissues of the body.5 Though the binding energy of BPA is lower than estradiol, at higher concentrations, it may result in reproductive problems,6 developmental disorders,7 and cancer.8

A 2022 study reported that the annual production of BPA may significantly exceed 10 million tons with a steep rise in production between 2023 and 2028 due to increased global demand for polycarbonate and epoxy plastics.9 BPA monomer units leaches out in soil,10 water, and air (dust)11 from these commonly used polycarbonate commercial plastics by the hydrolysis of the ester bonds.12 Physical factors like elevated temperatures, mechanical stress along with chemical factors like acidic or basic environments, lead to the release of free BPA molecules in the food served or stored in the containers.13 Other sources of BPA release include thermal paper used for tickets, labels, and receipts.14 These not only release BPA in landfills but also lead to dermal absorption of BPA during handling.15 The major concern is the environmental persistence of BPA since its half-life varies significantly depending on the various environmental factors such as pH, temperature, and microbial activity.16 Studies indicate that the median concentration of BPA in wastewater influent and final effluent is 400 and 150 ng/L, respectively, with removal efficiencies ranging from 1% to 77%.17 A study on the efficiency of sunlight vs. UV-A photodegradation utilizing Fe3O4/SiO2/TiO2 magnetic nanocomposite for the removal of BPA resulted in 74% and 50% removal of BPA, respectively.18 Other comparative studies have been listed in Table 1.Table 1Comparative table of different photocatalysts with the algal EPS-based nanobiocompositeSl no.PhotocatalystLight sourceReaction time (min)Concentration of BPA (mg/L)Removal of BPA (%)Reference1Fe3O4/SiO2/TiO2 magnetic nanocompositeSunlight2205074Aghel et al.182TiO2–MoS2-reduced graphene oxideUV-C3005062.40Luo et al.193C-doped ZnO catalystUV-C1,44050100Bechambi et al.204N,Co-codoped TiO2Sunlight1405040Garg et al.215Algal EPS-based zeolite/nanoceria nanobiocompositeUV-A1204081Current study

Zeolites are white, crystalline material having a tetrahedral aluminosilicate structure, with a porous three-dimensional network and cations like Mg2+ and Ca2+.22,23 Zeolite-based nanocomposites have been studied for the removal of heavy metal (cadmium),24 toxic dye (methylene blue),25 as well as gases (SO2)26 from water. Photocatalytic degradation of the contaminants by embedding a photoactive metal oxide within a zeolite matrix generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) when activated by light, which effectively degrades the contaminant by advanced oxidation processes. Nanoceria (CeO2)-based photocatalysts have drawn attention due to the fluorite structure of nanoceria, stable crystal framework, and the ability to change the redox trivalent (Ce3+) and tetravalent (Ce4+) states.27 Since this rare earth metal oxide lacks an independent application due to its limited surface area, high electron-hole pair recombination, studies have employed it by fabricating it over a supportive matrix or other nanomaterial composites.

Adsorption, chemical oxidation, and biological treatment are some of the conventional methods for the removal of toxic phenolic compounds like BPA. The re-release of contaminants due to desorption, high operation costs, and additional treatment requirements for the disposal of concentrated waste are some of the potential drawbacks of the studies performed previously.10 Metal oxide nanocomposites (MONPs) can be an effective solution for the removal of environmental contaminants like BPA due to their adsorptive and photocatalytic properties, yet they may pose a threat of metal oxide leaching, resulting in biological toxicity.28 In the aquatic system, the MONPs may partially be dissolved from the nanocomposite, releasing free metal ions (Ce3+/Ce4+). These leached species induce oxidative stress and generate ROS in algae, fishes, and other organisms, disrupting the food chains and affecting human health through water contamination.29

To address the above limitation, algal extracellular polymeric substances (algal EPS) may be considered as a solution where EPS could be coated onto the MONP. Marine and freshwater algal species produce two types of EPS, viz. loosely bound and tightly bound EPS (LB-EPS/TB-EPS).30 Algal EPS, particularly TB-EPS is rich in polysaccharides, proteins, and humic acid (HA) like compounds capable of chelating metal ions by adhering to the surface of MO nanocomposite. Recent studies have revealed that algal EPS is biocompatible and its degradation does not pose any secondary toxicity risks.31 To the best of our knowledge, a biosafe algal EPS-based zeolite/nanoceria nanobiocomposite for the removal of BPA has not been explored.

The present study focuses on the development of an algal EPS-based zeolite/nanoceria nanobiocomposite for the photocatalytic removal of BPA from aqueous medium, resulting in non-toxic byproducts and ultimately mineralizing the plasticizer into CO2 and water. The schematic representation of the current work is given in the Figure 1. The photocatalytic approach involves generation of ROS by the nanobiocomposite for the breakdown of BPA, as indicated by the cell viability and low oxidative stress of freshwater Chlorella sp. algal strain and Allium sativum, respectively. Furthermore, another crucial aspect of the biosafety assessment was also performed for zeolite/nanoceria nanocomposites with and without EPS to confirm that algal EPS, not only mitigates the toxic effects of nanoceria but also opens new horizons for next-generation green-nano bioremediation strategies.Figure 1Synthesis of algal EPS-based nanobiocomposite

FTIR spectra

Figure 2B depicts the Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra of pristine zeolite, nanoceria, algal TB-EPS along with the nanobiocomposite constituting these components. The FTIR spectra of zeolite show an intense characteristic peak at 967.32 cm−1 corresponding to the asymmetric Si–O–Al bond stretching within the aluminosilicate network of zeolite.32 The less defined multiple peaks shown below 800 cm−1 may be attributed to the framework vibrations. Nanoceria shows characteristic peak at 450 cm−1 which is an indicator of low frequency vibrations between metal and oxygen (Ce–O).33 The absence of any other organic functional groups confirms the inorganic nature of nanoceria. The FTIR spectra of algal EPS show a weak peak at 1699 cm−1 denoting the C=O stretching of the amide-I, a distinct marker for the presence of proteins. Additionally, a sharp peak in the fingerprint region at 1056 cm−1 further confirms the presence of C–O and C–O–C from polysaccharides.34 The nanobiocomposite shows intense peaks at 453 cm−1 (Ce–O), 978 cm−1 (Si–O–Al), 1647 cm−1 (C=O and C=C), and 2942 cm−1 (C–H), confirming the presence of its three constituents. An intense peak at 978 cm−1 was found to be broader and deeper than the peak of zeolite confirming the presence of polysaccharide peak (1056 cm−1). Lastly, the wide peak in the range of 3600–3000 cm−1 confirmed the presence of hydroxyl groups in the nanobiocomposite.

FE-SEM and EDX analysis

Figure 2C shows the morphology of the nanobiocomposite using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) analysis. The image reveals a regular distribution of large cuboidal-shaped crystals (zeolite) with rough surface decorated with minute spherical particles (nanoceria) on the surface of the cuboidal crystals. This indicates the successful embedding of nanoceria onto the zeolite which act as an adsorbent matrix for the contaminant.

Figure 2D depicts the EDX spectra of the nanobiocomposite, confirming its elemental composition of the same. The major elements identified from the elemental-colored profiles were Ce, O, C, Al, Si, and Na. The detection of Al and Si confirmed the presence of zeolite without any structural deformities, acting as the foundational backbone of the nanobiocomposite. The detection of Ce and O confirms the presence of nanoceria in the nanobiocomposite.

Photodegradation studies for removal of BPA

Ratio of the nanobiocomposite

Based on the previous studies from our group on caffeine degradation, the nanoceria/zeolite nanocomposite was varied between 1:1, 1:2, and 2:1 ratios wherein the maximal removal of caffeine observed for 1:1 ratio. Zeolite is an effective adsorbent owing to its regular microporous framework structure and nanoceria functions during UV irradiation by generating ROS species to degrade the contaminant through advanced oxidation processes (AOPs). Figure S1A shows the comparison between 1:1:1, 1:1:0.5, and 1:1:0.1 ratios of the nanobiocomposite. As per our finding, 1:1:0.1 ratio has shown 35% adsorption of BPA which is more than 1:1:1 and 1:1:0.5 ratios, but falls behind in the degradation percentage by UV-A illumination at 47%. In comparison between 1:1:1 and 1:1:0.5, the overall removal% is similar (81%), hence 1:1:0.5 was chosen as the optimized ratio to avoid the usage of excess EPS which is not essential. This also ensured equal concentrations of zeolite and nanoceria to enable optimal deposition of nanoceria on the zeolite as shown in Figure 2B. The equal ratio of zeolite and nanoceria was chosen as the optimal ratio as per our previous findings.35

The algal EPS percentage was reduced to half that of the other two to enable the efficient activity of both zeolite and nanoceria in the nanobiocomposite. Figure S1B shows the role of the pristine components of the nanobiocomposite in the removal of BPA. The role of zeolite was pronounced in dark conditions in comparison to nanoceria and EPS, thereby acting as the primary adsorbent. Similarly, the role of nanoceria was pronounced during UV-A illumination because of its photocatalytic activity. Lastly, the algal EPS showed a limited adsorption as compared to zeolite/nanoceria and more activity during the UV-A illumination. This is due to photolysis of BPA as shown in Figure 3C. The photolysis of BPA was found to be 66% whereas, the EPS showed an overall removal of 70.5%. A slight jump in the removal was facilitated by the initial limited adsorption of BPA by the EPS.Figure 3Photodegradation studies of BPA(A) Effect of concentration of BPA.(B) Effect of pH.(C) Photolysis vs. photocatalysis.(D) Effect of humic acid.

Effect of concentration of BPA

Figure 3A shows the influence of contaminant concentration (BPA) on the photocatalytic degradation process. The photocatalysis of BPA was conducted at concentrations ranging from 20 to 60 mg/L over a duration of 120 min. As shown in the figure, there is a time-dependent increase in the removal percentage with 93%, 81%, and 79% removal using 20 mg/L, 40 mg/L, and 60 mg/L BPA at 120 min, respectively. At 20 mg/L concentration, BPA shows 83% adsorption on the surface of zeolite under dark conditions within 60 min. Though it shows the highest removal at the end of the reaction (dark + UV-A), the role of UV-A irradiation in the breakdown of BPA is much lesser than what is shown at concentrations of 40 and 60 mg/L BPA, respectively. Due to low concentration, BPA was rapidly adsorbed within an hour due to the low concentration and the availability of free active sites on zeolite. Since, a major amount of BPA was adsorbed onto the zeolite, the effect of UV-A irradiation was minimal.

At 40 mg/L BPA, the adsorption reached 31% after 60 min, whereas the overall removal percentage increased to 81% at the end of the total reaction at 120 min. Almost a 50% increase in the removal was achieved due to UV-A irradiation for the same. At 60 mg/L BPA, the adsorption was 28% after 60 min; however, after 120 min of the reaction, the removal percentage increased to 79%. An almost similar increase of 51% removal of 60 mg/L BPA was achieved with UV-A irradiation. As the concentration of BPA was increased 2- to 3-fold, low adsorption can be attributed to the adsorptive active sites of zeolite being supersaturated by excess BPA.36 The adsorption and total removal percentage of BPA were nearly same for 40 and 60 mg/L concentrations. Therefore, 40 mg/L BPA was identified as the optimal concentration for remediation.

Effect of pH

Figure 3B shows the effect of pH on the photocatalytic breakdown of BPA at the optimized concentration of 40 mg/L. The effect of pH on the photocatalytic breakdown of 40 mg/L BPA was analyzed across a pH range of 6–10. At pH 6, 7.8, and 10, the removal percentage BPA are 79%, 81%, and 81%, respectively. A pH range of 6–10 was chosen to mimic a freshwater ecosystem, as its pH often fall under this range.37

The photocatalytic degradation within this pH range remained almost same with minimal variations in the adsorption, indicating that maximum removal of BPA is possible within this range using the nanobiocomposite.38 The pKa1 and pKa2 values of BPA is approximately 9.6 and 10.2, respectively.

BPA assumes anionic form at pH levels exceeding 10.2 and hence it is predominantly in cationic form within the pH range studied in this work. The zeta potential of the zeolite acting as the adsorbent was found to be −12.20 ± 5.59. The negative charge on the zeolite facilitated the adsorption of the positively charged BPA by 25–31%. At, 40 mg/L, the adsorption of BPA could not increase further due to the saturation of the active sites on the zeolite as discussed earlier. Such a low adsorption favored the UV-A photocatalytic degradation to up to 50% of the excess BPA present in the solution. The total removal percentages at pH 7.8 and 10 are similar and approximately 81%. Therefore, to remain closer to neutral conditions, pH 7.8 was identified as the optimal pH for the removal of 40 mg/L BPA.39

Figure S2 shows the degradation kinetics of the reaction for the removal of 40 mg/L BPA by 1:1:0.5 ratio of the nanobiocomposite at pH 7.8. As per the plot, there is a linear relationship between the logarithm of concentration and time (min) of BPA with a reaction rate of 0.02032 min−1 whereas, the catalyst does not reduce over time suggesting the order to be pseudo-first order reaction with R2 value of 0.98 exhibiting an excellent fit into this kinetic model.

Figure 3C presents the comparative effect of photolysis (without nanobiocomposite; only UV-A) versus the photocatalysis (with nanobiocomposite and UV-A). The total removal percentage via photolysis and photocatalysis of 40 mg/L BPA at pH 7.8 were 66% and 81% respectively. As discussed earlier, in photocatalysis, the breakdown of BPA was predominantly led by the influence of the UV-A irradiation on the nanoceria in the nanobiocomposite, in addition to the adsorption by zeolite, enhancing removal efficiency unlike in photolysis. A reusability test with the same algal EPS-based nanobiocomposite was performed which showed a removal efficiency of 66.04%, till five consecutive cycles in Figure S3. Finally, to understand any possible changes in the nanobiocomposite after repeated usage till five cycles, FTIR was used to compare the before/after changes in the nanobiocomposite. The spectra show that the characteristic peaks of the nanobiocomposite are largely preserved, with only minor changes in intensity and position. This demonstrated that the nanobiocomposite is efficient and maintains a stable removal efficiency even after multiple cycles.

Effect of humic acid

HA is a dark-colored organic compound which is a mixture of complex macromolecules. HA interference is studied in photocatalytic studies since it is a major compound found in soil and water which might interfere in the removal process. Figure 3D presents the effect of HA on the photocatalytic degradation of BPA, studied under optimized conditions of 40 mg/L BPA and pH 7.8, with a concentration range of 0–10 mg/L. The removal percentage of BPA in the presence of HA (0–10 mg/L) was found to be 81%, 73%, and 77.1%, respectively. At 5 mg/L HA concentration, the adsorption of BPA after 60 min decreased to 13%, as compared to 31% at 0 mg/L of HA. Similarly, the photodegradation using UV-A irradiation was also hindered to 73% at 5 mg/L from 81% at 0 mg/L. This could be attributed to the fact that in the presence of 5 mg/L HA, HA and BPA competes to acquire the active sites of the zeolite.40 The exchangeable cations inside the zeolite framework might have possibly bridged the HA to the zeolite surface instead of BPA.41

However, after 60 min with 10 mg/L HA concentration, the adsorption increased to 37% surpassing both 0 and 5 mg/L. Yet, the final removal percentage remained marginally lower than 0 mg/L but higher than 5 mg/L concentrations. The adsorption of BPA was more favorable in the presence of HA compared to the solution without HA, indicating to the bridging effect of HA. HA can form larger complexes with the contaminant by providing fresh active sites resulting into higher adsorption.42 Finally, the slight hinderance in the breakdown activity of UV-A irradiation maybe attributed to the intrinsic ability of HA to absorb UV-A light (inner filter effect) which was meant to activate the photocatalyst’s surface. The color of the solution darkens with increasing HA concentration, significantly impacting the light penetration that affects photodegradation.43

Mineralization and the fate of BPA

Figure 4A shows the mineralization of BPA, estimated using total organic carbon (TOC) analysis to understand the removal of BPA by the nanobiocomposite. After 120 min, the TOC removal from BPA increased to 81% and corresponds with the photodegradation percentage as discussed earlier, and further increased to 96.15% after 150 min, indicating the complete breakdown of BPA in the solution. The ROS responsible for the removal of BPA was studied using radical scavenging activity. Previous studies have reported the ability of cerium ions to transit between Ce4+ and Ce3+ states. The UV-A irradiation causes Ce4+ ions to undergo reduction and form Ce3+ ions through the generation of superoxide anions.44 This reaction could be reverted back, allowing the Ce3+ ions to be oxidized to Ce4+.45 Figure 4B presents the effect of various radical scavengers like sodium azide (1O2), isopropanol (OH.), p-benzoquinone (O2−), and ammonium oxalate (h+) on the removal of BPA.46 The nanobiocomposite showed similar removal percentage with 40 mg/L BPA at pH 7.8 maintaining its activity except in the case of p-benzoquinone. A drastic drop with just 17.4% removal was seen when tested in the presence of p-benzoquinone denoting superoxide (O2−) radical as the predominant ROS species in action. Upon exposing ceria to UV-A radiation, electrons in the valence band of ceria are excited to the conduction band, resulting in the formation of positively charged holes. The photogenerated electrons subsequently reacts with the molecular oxygen (O2) adsorbed on the surface of the nanobiocomposite to produce O2− radical.Figure 4Fate of BPA(A) TOC removal % of BPA.(B) Radical scavenging activity.

Furthermore, to confirm the removal efficiency of the algal EPS-based nanobiocomposite, the degradation intermediates were identified and analyzed using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). The C18 HPLC column was used for the LC run, equipped with Shimadzu LabSolutions software. The intermediates were detected using the following parameters: scan method (100–2000 m/z)/ selected ion monitoring (SIM) method; column oven temperature: 40°C, injection volume: 10 μL, flow rate: 0.8 mL/min, run time: 30 min. Figure 5 shows the degradation intermediates detected by the LC-MS which were 4-isopropenylphenol (m/z: 133), 4-hydroxyacetophenone (m/z:135), and phenol (m/z: 93) (Figure S4). Due to the great electron density on the C–C single bonding joining the phenyl group and the isopropyl carbon, it is more susceptible to the attack of O2− resulting in the β-scission. With time, the O2− abstracts protonic species from BPA to form phenoxy BPA radicals which undergo successive hydroxylation, dehydrogenation, alkylic-oxidation, radical coupling, and bond cleavage to form products such as 4-isopropenylphenol and phenol.47 Such degradation intermediates can also be correlated with previous reports.48 However, the amount of phenol produced seems to be minimal based on the MS analysis. Previous studies have also shown 4-isopropenylphenol to degrade further to quinones (hydro/benzo) or any other organic acids but both of these compounds were not detected through LC-MS likely due to their low concentrations beyond the detectable limit.49,50,51,52 Further degradation may result in the aromatic ring-opening products, leading to the formation of CO2 and H2O.Figure 5BPA degradation pathway showing intermediates identified by LC-MS analysis

Biosafety assessment using Chlorella sp. and Allium sativum

Effects on cell viability

The nanocomposite prepared with EPS exhibited reduced toxicity compared to the former prepared without EPS. In terms of toxicity, the nanocomposite at a concentration of 10 mg/L has showed 77% of cell viability in the presence of EPS. Whereas the nanocomposite without EPS showed only 60%, showing a significant 17% difference in the cell viability. Meanwhile, the toxicity with 100 mg/L of nanocomposite, the cell viability has reached 53% and 43% in the presence and absence of EPS, respectively. In addition, for the samples degraded with the nanobiocomposite (Figure 6C), the cell viability was observed to be 72% at 0 min (untreated), increasing to 89%, and reaching approximately 96% at 60 (adsorbed), and 150 min (UV-A treated), respectively. The ecotoxicity study (cell viability and total ROS generation) was also performed using Allium sativum as a plant model to check the effect of nanocomposite (with and without EPS) toxicity as well as toxicity of BPA degradation byproducts with nanobiocomposite. Upon exposure to the synthesized nanocomposite at 10 and 100 mg/L (in the absence of EPS), the cell viability was observed to be approximately 52% and 49%, respectively (Figure 6B). Whereas, in the presence of EPS, a significant increase (p < 0.001) in the cell viability was noted, approximately 89% and 83%, respectively, for the plant cells exposed to 10 and 100 mg/L of the nanocomposite. The comparative study for the cell viability of both algae and plant shows that EPS is playing a pivotal role in mitigating the toxic effects of the nanocomposite by the formation of an eco-corona as per previous studies.30 EPS is a bulky substance which may develop steric hindrance or electrostatic repulsion, thereby altering the ability of ceria NPs to interact and get internalized in the algal and plant cells. Without the presence of EPS, the ceria NPs could leach out into the solution due to the reaction conditions resulting in reduced cell viability in both cases.53 Nanoceria can easily undergo transition between Ce4+ and Ce3+ states through the utilization of oxygen vacancies. When Ce4+ gains an electron, it generates a vacancy, whereas the reverse process removes it. Ceria photocatalysis depends on Ce3+ states or oxygen vacancy sites that act as a trap for electrons that enables radical formation. The irreversible conversion of Ce3+ to Ce4+ following the reaction indicates a depletion of active vacancies and potential leaching of Ce. Figure S5 shows the Ce4+/Ce3+ X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) plots for the EPS non-coated and coated nanocomposite for before and after reaction. The plots clearly show the Ce4+ and Ce3+ peaks across all the samples showing spin orbital splitting ranges between 17 and 19 eV for Ce 3d. In samples without EPS, an increase in the Ce4+/Ce3+ ratio shows that Ce3+ sites have undergone oxidation and subsequently decreased during photocatalysis, implying instability in which Ce3+ centers are depleted more rapidly than they are restored. This leads to poor durability and subsequent surface degradation. Meanwhile, the Ce4+/Ce3+ ratio exhibits a negligible increase after reaction for EPS-coated samples (Figure S6). Thus, the EPS-coated samples mitigate redox cycling, thereby preserving Ce3+ sites and minimizing excessive oxidation and leaching. Moreover, this stability in the redox ratio maintains Ce3+ states, mitigating cerium toxicity.

Similarly, as shown in Figure 6D upon interaction with the untreated sample (0 min), a significant drop in the cell viability was observed (∼35%, p < 0.001). Whereas, in the exposure to BPA solution that has undergone only adsorption (60 min) and the UV-A treated sample (150 min), the cell viability was observed to be approximately 56% and 89%, respectively. A toxicity reduction of 53% was noted when the plant cells were exposed to the treated sample when compared to the untreated sample. This demonstrates the significant reduction in toxicity due to the conversion of BPA into safer byproducts which may further get degraded to CO2 and H2O as discussed in the degradation mechanism earlier in Figure 5.

They are the byproducts of the normal cellular metabolism, but excessive ROS production can lead to the oxidative stress damaging cellular components, and disrupt the normal cell function.54 Figure 7 depicts the effects of ROS on the algal cells and plant model. Whereas, at 100 mg/L of nanocomposite concentration, the ROS production in the algal cells has increased by approximately 1.9- and 4-fold compared to the control, for the nanocomposites with and without EPS, respectively. Figure 7B reveals the ROS production in the plant cells interacting with the nanocomposite synthesized in the presence and absence of EPS. At 10 mg/L nanocomposite concentration, in the presence and absence of EPS, the produced ROS has approximately increased between 1- and 1.2-fold, respectively, in comparison with the control cells. Whereas, with the exposure of 100 mg/L of nanocomposite to the plant cells, the ROS production has increased by 1.1- and 1.3-fold when compared to the control, for the nanocomposites with and without EPS, respectively. The plant cells exposed to 10 mg/L of the nanocomposite exhibited a decrease in ROS generation by ∼14% while the 100 mg/L treatment exhibited a significant decrease (∼18%, p < 0.05) in the presence of EPS when compared to the treatments without EPS. It is interesting to note that the increase in the total ROS in the presence of EPS is much lower than without EPS for both 10 and 100 mg/L nanocomposite. The cell viability results indicate that the algal cells and plant cells treated with nanobiocomposite (with EPS) showed more cell viability than without EPS for both the concentrations tested earlier. The interaction of the nanobiocomposite may have generated ROS, although it may also reflect the active cellular processes wherein the cell is effectively mitigating the ROS by generating the antioxidants without getting overwhelmed.55 A higher cell viability with the nanocomposite with EPS suggests that the antioxidant response of both components was robust enough to sustain the cellular life processes. Figure 7D shows that a significant decrease (∼17%, p < 0.001) in ROS generation between the plants exposed to untreated and treated samples, analogous to the trend observed in the cell viability assay, i.e., 0 min > 60 min > 150 min, where 0 min showed highest level of total ROS generation. At 0 min, BPA was pristine, while at 60 min, it was just adsorbed by zeolite which does not account for lowering the toxicity for both algae and plant due to the obvious desorption of BPA into the solution. Ultimately, the role of UV-A irradiation activating the nanoceria showed the maximum breakdown, producing fewer toxic byproducts eventually leading to formation of CO2 and water.

BPA was removed by the nanobiocomposite consisting of zeolite, the adsorbent and matrix on which nanoceria was deposited. This composite was coated with algal EPS to make it biocompatible and less toxic. As per our findings, three ratios of nanobiocomposite were tested for the removal of BPA out of which 1:1:0.5 ratio was found to be optimum. The adsorption played a minimal role in the 1:1:0.5 ratio of the nanobiocomposite, rather the UV-A irradiation contributed to about 50% of the total removal at 81%. The removal of BPA was almost similar in the pH ranging from acidic to basic and worked best in close to neutral pH. A reusability test revealed removal efficiency of 66.04%, till five consecutive cycles demonstrating its stable removal efficiency without major changes in the structure even after multiple cycles. The ROS (O2−) was the chief contributor in the breakdown of BPA into the reaction intermediates. The degraded products showed less toxicity when compared to the undegraded sample of BPA. As mentioned earlier, without contributing to further toxicity by the leaching of nanoceria, the algal EPS-based nanobiocomposite was proven to be non-toxic for the environmental usage. Overall, the synthesized nanobiocomposite is effective and eco-friendly unlike other nanocatalysts and maybe used in the future in large-scale nanobioremediation of water bodies by the action of UV-A from sunlight.

Limitations of the study

The production of algal EPS is dependent on the culture used and has to be replenished for the synthesis of the nanobiocomposite after each photocatalytic reaction. Lastly, the impact of heavy metals on the degradation efficiency may be studied in the future.

Method details

Extraction of algal EPS

For the extraction of algal EPS, the marine microalgae, Chlorella sp. was utilized. The microalgae were grown in sterile and membrane filtered natural sea water (NSW) for 15-20 days. The algal cells were harvested on reaching exponential growth phase at 7000 rpm at 4°C for 10 minutes. To generate stress and production of EPS by the microalgae, the algal cell pellet was resuspended in autoclaved artificial sea water medium having a pH of 7.8. The composition of ASW is listed in the supplemental information (Table S1). The algal cell suspension was kept under Philips TL-D Super 80, linear fluorescent lamp, India at 3000 lux constant illuminated condition for a period of 24 hrs. Following this, the algal suspension was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm at 4°C for 15 min. The supernatant thus obtained is termed as LB-EPS (loosely bound extrapolymeric substances). The residual algal cell pellets left after the removal of the supernatant were treated with 0.1 M of 200 mL phosphate buffer (pH 7.2). This resuspended algal-PBS mixture was subjected to water bath for 20 min at 70 °C. The suspension was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm at 4°C for 15 min, filtered using syringe filter of 0.1 μm size and was labelled as TB-EPS (tightly bound exopolysaccharides). This TB-EPS solution was lyophilized for 48 hr to obtain an amorphous, white EPS powder which was used for the synthesis of the nanobiocomposite.

Synthesis of the nanobiocomposite

A stoichiometric amount of ammonium cerium nitrate (4.9 g) and glycine (1.8 g) based on oxidizer to fuel ratio was dissolved in 60 mL of deionized water thoroughly and sonicated for 10 minutes. Later, the solution was kept in a pyrex dish for combustion at 400 °C for 15 minutes.56,57 The obtained powder was finely grounded using mortar and pestle and washed with deionized water. Later the pure ceria was sintered at 600 °C for 1 hr. The sintered nano ceria was utilized for composite preparation and employed for all the catalytic experiments.

Zeolite 13X type was heat activated at 150 °C by using a muffle furnace following 0.1 mM hydrochloric acid treatment. The HCL-zeolite powder was dried in a hot air oven at 70°C for 6 hours.

The nanobiocomposite was prepared in two ratios 1:1:1 and 1:1:0.5 of which the concentration of zeolite was 50 mg/ 50 mL, TB-EPS 50 mg/25 mg/ 50 mL, and nanoceria (CeO2) 50 mg/ 50 mL in Milli-Q water. The solution was stirred overnight at room temperature at 400 rpm followed by centrifugation at 6500 rpm for 20 minutes. The supernatant was discarded and the nanobiocomposite was washed with ethanol and Milli-Q water respectively. After the washing steps, the supernatant was discarded and the pellet was dried at 70 °C for six hours in the hot air oven, as depicted in Figure 1.

Characterization

Powder X-ray Diffraction (XRD, Malvern Panalytical X’Pert3 Powder, Netherlands) was utilized to examine the phase and crystalline structure of the Zeolite:nanoceria:EPS nanocomposite. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy was utilized to study the various functional groups on the surface of the nanobiocomposite, zeolite, cerium (IV) oxide and TB-EPS (FT-IR, IR-Spirit Shimadzu; Japan). To understand the morphology and elemental composition, scanning electron microscopy and EDX spectroscopy were performed. The mineralization of BPA (contaminant) was analysed using the total organic carbon analyser (TOC analyser, Shimadzu; Japan). The BPA degradation products were detected by utilizing liquid chromatograph-mass spectrophotometry (LC-MS 8045RX, Shimadzu; Japan).

Photocatalytic batch study of BPA degradation using the nanobiocomposite

A stock BPA solution of 1000 mg/L was prepared in acetone which was further diluted in Milli-Q water to make the working solution of 50 mL. In the photocatalytic batch study, 1:1:1 and 1:1:0.5 and 1:1:0.1 ratio dosage of the Zeolite:nanoceria:EPS nanobiocomposite was studied. 50 mg/50 mL (1000 mg/L) of the dried nanobiocomposite powder was introduced in 20, 40 and 60 mg/L BPA solution respectively. The effect of pH was studied between pH 6 to 9 by adjusting the pH of the solution 0.1 M HCl or NaOH. The optimization studies were performed between 0 min and 120 min. The nanobiocomposite and BPA solution was subjected to adsorption/desorption cycle for the initial 60 min after which it was subjected to UV-A irradiation (365 nm) at room temperature (26°C) in a closed multi-lamp photoreactor fitted with 10 W UV lamps acting as the source of illumination. The intensity of the UV-A light source is 14.5 W/m2. The removal of BPA at a constant wavelength of 265 nm by using UV-Visible spectrophotometer (UV-2600 Spectrophotometer, Shimadzu, Japan). The efficiency of the nanobiocomposite to remove BPA was calculated using the expression(Equation 1)R%=1−(C/C0)×100where R% represent the removal percentage and C0 and C represent the initial concentration and the final concentration respectively of BPA.

Biosafety assessment

The contaminant was diluted for 4 times and interacted with the biological indicator like, freshwater microalgae Chlorella sp., and terrestrial plant model Allium sativum.

Evaluation of cell viability

For algal risk assessment analysis, healthy algal cells were taken from the exponential phase, followed by centrifugation and resuspension of the cells in autoclaved lakewater. The algal cells were adjusted to 0.5 OD (optical density) referred from our previous study.58 After a 72 h incubation period, for the algal toxicity study on freshwater microalgae, Chlorella sp., the number of viable cells was counted using a hemocytometer under an optical microscope. Healthy, viable cells with distinguished morphology (larger cells) were then differentiated from dead cells (as clusters). The cells with a higher level of damage were also considered dead cells. The cell counts for the treatment groups were represented with respect to the control.59 The impact of the contaminants, BPA, and the nanoceria-based synthesized nanocomposites for the current work was assessed for the cell viability study.

Allium sativum bulbs were purchased from the local market and were allowed to germinate in Hoagland nutrient solution.60 Details of the various chemicals used for the preparation of Hoagland nutrient solution are provided in the supplemental information (Table S2). The germinated garlic cloves with 4-5 cm long roots were used for toxicity experiments. The cloves were submerged in interaction vials containing the contaminants for 4h. After a 4h interaction period, the cytotoxicity study was performed using Evans blue dye 0.25% (w/v). The treated roots measuring 1-2 cm were excised and immersed in Evans blue for 15 min, followed by washing them with distilled water for 30 min. The roots were then carefully transferred to 4 mL of N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and were incubated in the dark condition for 1h to extract the dye. The absorbance of the extracted dye was measured to obtain the percentage of cell death using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (UV-220, Thermofisher Scientific, USA) at 600 nm. The cell viability percentage was calculated by subtracting the cell death from 100, and the results were compared with the viability of the untreated cells as a reference point.61

Assessment of reactive oxygen species

After a 72 h incubation period, algal cell suspension (5mL) was incubated under dark condition with 50 μl of 100 mM DCFH-DA (cell permeable non-fluorescent dye) for 30 min.62 After incubation, the samples were loaded onto a 96-well plate to record the fluorescence intensity using a spectrofluorometer at excitation (485 nm) and emission (530nm) wavelengths, respectively. The amount of ROS produced was calculated and represented with respect to the control.

After 4h of exposure to the contaminants, 10 roots from each treatment were homogenized in a chilled mortar and pestle using 1X PBS and the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. 2′,7′-dichloro-fluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) (100 mol L-1) was added to the obtained supernatant, and the samples were kept in a dark condition for 30 min. The fluorescence of the samples was measured using a Fluorescent Spectrophotometer (Cary Eclipse fluorescence spectrophotometer, Model – G9800A, Agilent Technologies, USA) with excitation and emission wavelengths set at 485 and 530 nm, respectively. The obtained results were represented with respect to the control.63

Quantification and statistical analysis

Two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post-test was performed by using GraphPad-Prism-8 to evaluate the statistical significance.
