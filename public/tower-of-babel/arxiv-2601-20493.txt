Regularity of Lipschitz free boundaries for weak solutions of Alt-Caffarelli type problems
Joan Domingo-Pasarin, Xavier Ros-Oton

Abstract
. Motivated by the Serrin problem, we study weak solutions of the generalised Alt-Caffarelli problem − Δ ​ u = f -\Delta u=f in Ω \Omega , u = 0 u=0 on ∂ Ω \partial\Omega , ∂ ν u = Q \partial_{\nu}u=Q on ∂ Ω \partial\Omega . Our main result establishes that if Ω \Omega is Lipschitz, then it is actually C ∞ C^{\infty} (provided that f f and Q Q are smooth). This was known before only for viscosity solutions. As a corollary, we obtain an alternative solution of Serrin’s problem in the case of Lipschitz domains. We also discuss the characterisation of the regularity of Lipschitz domains in terms of their Poisson kernel.

1.
Introduction

Free boundary problems have been a major line of research in the field of PDE in the
last decades; see for instance
[
9
,
27
,
33
]
. Among them,
Serrin’s overdetermined problem has seen important developments since its
introduction in
[
31
]
; we refer the reader to the surveys

[
30
,
26
]
for a comprehensive overview of the contributions
to this problem. Given a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, Serrin’s problem consists in
proving that if
\Omega
admits a solution of

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta u&=1\quad&&\text{in }\Omega\\
u&=0\quad&&\text{on }\partial\Omega\\
\partial_{\nu}u&=c\quad&&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

then
\Omega
is a ball
B_{R}
of radius
R=R(n,c)
. Without the Neumann condition
(
1.1
) already has a unique solution. Therefore, the Neumann
condition makes the problem overdetermined and turns it into a free boundary problem.
Serrin’s problem can thus be understood as showing that the overdetermined nature of
(
1.1
) forces
\Omega
to be a ball.

In his celebrated paper
[
31
]
, Serrin solved this problem for
C^{2}

bounded domains under the assumption
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
. Observe that whenever

\Omega
has finite
(n-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure and

u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, (
1.1
) has a natural weak formulation:

(1.2)

-\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta dx=c\int_{\partial\Omega}\eta d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{\Omega}\eta dx\qquad\forall\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Hence, after Serrin’s result it was natural to ask whether one could assume lower
regularity of
\Omega
and
u
and still obtain the same result. This proved to be a
challenging question which did not see any progress initially. In 1992, Vogel

[
34
]
achieved a breakthrough when he was able to show that
C^{1}

regularity combined with some additional hypothesis on
u
were enough to prove
Serrin’s theorem. Six years later, in 1998, Parjapat proved in
[
28
]
that
Serrin’s theorem still holds for domains which are
C^{2}
everywhere except at a
potential corner. This raised the question of whether the regularity of
\Omega
could
be lowered even further to Lipschitz regularity. Such question was left unanswered
for 27 years until the very recent paper by Figalli and Zhang

[
19
]
, where it has been solved. In it, the authors establish
Serrin’s theorem for a wider class of sets of finite perimeter which includes
Lipschitz domains.

Of particular interest to us is Remark 1.5 in
[
19
]
, where Figalli
and Zhang highlight the connection between Serrin’s problem and the Alt-Caffarelli
problem. More precisely, they point out the fact that one could think of using the
regularity theory of the latter to solve the former. Unfortunately, at the time this
regularity theory was mainly developed for viscosity-type solutions or minimizers of
the problem’s energy functional. Thus, Figalli and Zhang conclude that it is unclear
how one would apply said regularity theory to the weak formulation
(
1.2
). Consequently,
[
19
]
developed a
different approach based on geometric measure theory techniques.

Motivated by the remark of Figalli and Zhang, in this paper we study weak solutions
of the Alt-Caffarelli problem whose free boundary is Lipschitz. Introduced in the
seminal paper
[
1
]
, the classical Alt-Caffarelli problem (or one-phase
Bernoulli problem) consists in studying for which bounded domains
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
(with

0\in\partial\Omega
) there exists a non-negative function
u:B_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
solving

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \Delta u&=0\quad&&\text{in }\Omega\cap B_{1}\\
u&=0\quad&&\text{on }\partial\Omega\cap B_{1}\\
\partial_{\nu}u&=1\quad&&\text{on }\partial\Omega\cap B_{1}.\end{aligned}\right.

Here,
\nu
denotes the inward normal vector to
\partial\Omega\cap B_{1}
. As in Serrin’s
problem, the overdetermined nature of (
1.3
) leads one to expect
some restriction on the choice of
\Omega
. In particular, one may think of using the
additional boundary condition
\partial_{\nu}u=1
to prove higher regularity of the free
boundary
\partial\Omega\cap B_{1}
. This topic was already studied in
[
1
]

extensively. However, even though most results in
[
1
]
were stated for a
weak notion of solution, the literature for this problem has been mostly focused on
viscosity solutions and energy minimizers; see for instance
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
5
,
11
,
13
,
20
,
32
,
15
,
14
,
18
,
17
]
.
In particular, for such solutions it is known that Lipschitz free boundaries are
smooth (we refer to
[
9
]
for more details). However, as Figalli and Zhang
observed, there is no analogous result for weak solutions.

Remark 1.1
.

The recent paper
[
22
]
provides a deep understanding of so-called
stationary solutions of (
1.3
). This class is different from the
weak solutions that we study here. Actually, as explained in more detail below, the
key point of our work consists in showing that, if the free boundary is Lipschitz,
then weak solutions are stationary (and even viscosity) solutions.

1.1.
Main results

In this paper we prove the smoothness of Lipschitz free boundaries for weak solutions
of the one-phase Bernoulli problem with right hand side

(1.4)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta u&=f\quad&&\text{in }\Omega\cap B_{1}\\
u&=0\quad&&\text{on }\partial\Omega\cap B_{1}\\
\partial_{\nu}u&=Q\quad&&\text{on }\partial\Omega\cap B_{1}.\end{aligned}\right.

We assume
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
to be a bounded Lipschitz domain with

0\in\partial\Omega
and both
f
and
Q
to be smooth functions such that

f\geq 0
and
Q>0
. Our main theorem is as follows:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain such that

0\in\partial\Omega
and assume
u
solves (
1.4
) in the following
weak sense:
u\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(B_{1})
,
u\geq 0
,
u=0
on

B_{1}\setminus\Omega
and

-\int_{B_{1}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta dx=\int_{\partial\Omega\cap B_{1}}Q\eta d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{\Omega\cap B_{1}}f\eta dx,\qquad\forall\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(B_{1}).

If
f,Q\in C^{\infty}(B_{1})
,
f\geq 0
and
Q>0
, then

\partial\Omega\cap B_{1}
is locally a smooth surface in
B_{1}
.

It is important to highlight the fact that the Lipschitz regularity of
\Omega
in
Theorem
1.2
is optimal. Namely, it is not possible to prove smoothness of the
free boundary (for weak solutions) if we assume lower regularity of
\Omega
. Indeed, in

[
24
,
25
]
Lewis and Vogel showed that it is possible to construct a
domain
\Omega
whose boundary is
C^{1-\varepsilon}
for every

\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and for which there exists a function
u
solving
(
1.4
) in the weak sense of Theorem
1.2
with

f\equiv 0
and
Q\equiv 1
.

This, in particular, shows that weak solutions could exhibit a more delicate behavior
at the free boundary
\partial\Omega\cap B_{1}
than viscosity solutions or energy minimizers.
Actually, a blow-up procedure at the free boundary does not preserve the weak notion
of solution in general. In fact, this only happens for those solutions for which the
set
B_{1}\setminus\Omega
has positive density at the free boundary. Moreover, even with this
assumption, the improvement of flatness from
[
23
]
is still needed to show
that the blow-up limit is a weak solution. Compare this with the setting of viscosity
solutions and energy minimizers, which pass to the limit without the need for any
major result or assumption. In a similar fashion, we also need
[
23
]
to
prove that weak solutions have zero first inner variation, which is then used in the
proof of the 1-homogeneity of blow-ups.

As we see, the improvement of flatness from
[
23
]
is of utmost importance
when dealing with weak solutions of (
1.4
) and proving
Theorem
1.2
. Unfortunately, even though
[
23
]
deals with the even
more general setting of the
p(x)
-Laplacian, we cannot use directly their results
due to the fact that their weak notion of solution is more restrictive than ours.
Showing that our definition of weak solution fulfills the hypothesis in

[
23
]
is far from trivial and constitutes one of the main steps of the
proof of Theorem
1.2
.

As an immediate consequence of Theorem
1.2
, we are able to give an
alternative proof of
[
19
, Theorem 1.3]
for the case of Lipschitz
domains.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. Then
\Omega
admits a solution

u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
to (
1.2
) if and only if
\Omega
is a ball.

1.2.
Poisson kernel

The regularity theory for the one-phase Bernoulli problem
(
1.4
) can also be used to characterize the regularity of
Lipschitz domains in terms of their Poisson kernel.

Given a bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, consider the Green function

G_{x}
for
\Omega
with pole at
x
. Then,
G_{x}
is a positive harmonic function
in
\Omega\setminus\{x\}
which is continuous up to the boundary of
\Omega
and has zero
boundary value on
\partial\Omega
. Moreover, by Dahlberg’s theorem
[
29
, Theorem
13.10]
, the Poisson kernel
P_{x}=\partial_{\nu}G_{x}
, where
\nu
is the
inward normal vector to
\Omega
, exists
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-a.e. on
\partial\Omega
and is positive.

The regularity of
P_{x}
is deeply linked to the regularity of the domain

\Omega
. On the one hand, if
\Omega
is smooth, then

G_{x}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{x\})
and
P_{x}
is smooth for any

x\in\Omega
. Thus, the smoothness of
\Omega
implies the smoothness of

P_{x}
. On the other hand, one may wonder if the converse is also true. This has
been widely studied in the last decades (see e.g.
[
21
,
2
,
4
,
3
]
) and is now well understood. In particular, the following
result has been known for a long time.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain and
x\in\Omega
. Assume that the
Poisson kernel
P_{x}
for
\Omega
with pole at
x
equals

Q|_{\partial\Omega}
for some
Q\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then
\partial\Omega
is
C^{\infty}
.

Although this result is usually attributed to
[
6
]
(see, e.g.

[
21
]
), the results in
[
6
]
are for viscosity solutions and it
is not obvious how to apply them to deduce Theorem
1.4
. Our Theorem

1.2
clarifies this issue, as it applies directly to weak solutions.

Indeed, in this context, one can show that the Green function
G_{x}
is a weak
solution of (
1.4
) (with
f\equiv 0
). For this, by
[
29
, Lemma
7.6]

-\int_{\Omega}\nabla G_{x}\cdot\nabla\eta=-\eta(x)+\int\eta d\omega^{x}\qquad\forall\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

where
\omega^{x}
is the harmonic measure for
\Omega
with pole at
x
. Since

d\omega^{x}=P_{x}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
by Dahlberg’s theorem, and localizing in a ball
B_{r}
such
that
x\not\in B_{r}
(we assume
0\in\partial\Omega
), we have

(1.5)

-\int_{B_{r}}\nabla G_{x}\cdot\nabla\eta=\int_{\partial\Omega\cap B_{r}}P_{x}\eta d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\qquad\forall\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(B_{r}).

That is,
G_{x}
(extended by zero in
B_{r}\setminus\Omega
) solves
(
1.4
) in the weak sense with
Q=P_{x}
. This allows us to
apply Theorem
1.2
to
G_{x}
to recover Theorem
1.4
.

Remark 1.5
.

Theorems
1.2
and
1.4
still hold if we assume less regularity of

f
and
Q
. Precisely, the results from
[
10
]
imply
that if
f\in C^{k-1,\alpha}(B_{1})
and
Q\in C^{k,\alpha}(B_{1})
with

k\geq 2
and
\alpha>0
, then
\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}
and

\partial\Omega
are
C^{k+1,\alpha}
respectively.

Remark 1.6
.

Even though Theorem
1.4
only needs
P_{x}
to be smooth for one point

x\in\Omega
, once we know
\partial\Omega
is smooth it follows that

P_{x}
will be smooth for all
x\in\Omega
.

Lastly, let us expand a bit more on the domains constructed in
[
24
,
25
]
mentioned previously. In
[
24
]
, Lewis and Vogel were able to
construct a non-trivial domain
\Omega
with boundary homeomorphic to the unit sphere,
and such that the Poisson kernel for
\Omega
at
0\in\Omega
is given by a constant

a
. Moreover, they showed that both the homeomorphism and its inverse can be taken
to be
C^{1-\varepsilon}
for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Thus, even though the Green function

G_{0}
for this domain is a weak solution of (
1.4
) near

\partial\Omega
with
Q\equiv a\in C^{\infty}
, the boundary of
\Omega
is not smooth. In other words,

[
24
,
25
]
show that the Lipschitz regularity of
\Omega
in Theorem

1.4
is optimal in terms of proving smoothness of
\partial\Omega
.

1.3.
Strategy of the proof

As we mentioned previously, an important ingredient we use in the proof of Theorem

1.2
is the improvement of flatness from
[
23
]
. To be able to apply
it, we need to generalize some results from
[
1
]
to show that our
definition of weak solution fulfills the more restrictive hypothesis in

[
23
]
.

Once we have an improvement of flatness result, we obtain as a consequence the fact
that blow-up limits preserve the notion of weak solution. Importantly, this only
holds if we also assume that the set
B_{1}\setminus\Omega
has positive density at free
boundary points. This assumption, which is always satisfied by Lipschitz free
boundaries, is needed to rule out degenerate limits such as
|x\cdot e_{n}|
which, a
priori, can happen without the density condition. Also as a consequence of the
improvement of flatness we obtain that the first inner variation of weak solutions
vanishes. We then use this result to prove by means of a Weiss-type monotonicity
formula the 1-homogeneity of blow-up limits.

At this point we have all the main ingredients of the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
The last step of the proof can be done in two different ways, both using all the
results mentioned above. The first one consists in classifying the 1-homogenous weak
solutions obtained as blow-up limits at free boundary points. To do so, we follow the
ideas of
[
12
]
to prove that said limits are flat. From there, the
improvement of flatness allows us to conclude the proof. Alternatively, we can use
the already known regularity theory for viscosity solutions proven by De Silva in

[
13
]
. Assuming once more the density condition of the set

B_{1}\setminus\Omega
, we are able to show that weak solutions of
(
1.4
) are also viscosity solutions. Thus, by

[
13
]
we obtain the smoothness of the free boundary.

1.4.
Organization of the paper

The paper is organised as follows. In Section

2
we define the notion of weak solution for
(
1.4
) and prove some its basic properties. In Section

3
we show that our definition of weak solution satisfies the
required hypothesis to apply the improvement of flatness result from

[
23
]
. In Section
4
we study the blow-up sequences
of weak solutions at the free boundary. In Section
5
we
prove the 1-homogeneity of blow-up limits. In Section
6
we
show the smoothness of Lipschitz free boundaries with smooth data
f,Q
. Lastly, in
Section
7
we prove Theorem
1.2
and its consequences
Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
.

2.
Weak solutions and basic properties

To study (
1.4
) we consider the following equivalent
formulation:

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta u&=f\quad&&\text{in }\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}\\
u&=0\quad&&\text{on }\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}\\
\partial_{\nu}u&=Q\quad&&\text{on }\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}.\end{aligned}\right.

Here,
u:B_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
is non-negative and
f
and
Q
are given smooth functions
in
B_{1}
. As in
[
1
]
, throughout the paper we assume

0<Q_{\mathrm{min}}\leq Q\leq Q_{\mathrm{max}}<\infty,

and we consider the case
f\geq 0
. Observe that
f\equiv 0
corresponds to the
classical homogeneous case studied by Alt and Caffarelli in
[
1
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

A function
u
is a weak solution of (
2.1
) in

B_{1}
if it satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

u\in C(B_{1})
,
u\geq 0
and
-\Delta u=f
in the open set

\{u>0\}
.

(2)

For any open set
D\subset\subset B_{1}
there exist constants

0<c_{D}\leq C_{D}<\infty
such that for any ball
B_{r}(x)\subset D
with

x\in\partial\{u>0\}
we have

c_{D}\leq\frac{1}{r}\fint_{\partial B_{r}(x)}ud\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq C_{D}.

(3)

u
solves the equation

\Delta u=Q\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mspace{3mu}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{5.1903pt}{\begin{picture}\roundcap\roundjoin\polyline(0,1)(0,0)(1,0)
\end{picture}}}{\raisebox{5.1903pt}{\begin{picture}\roundcap\roundjoin\polyline(0,1)(0,0)(1,0)
\end{picture}}}{\raisebox{5.1903pt}{\begin{picture}\roundcap\roundjoin\polyline(0,1)(0,0)(1,0)
\end{picture}}}{\raisebox{5.1903pt}{\begin{picture}\roundcap\roundjoin\polyline(0,1)(0,0)(1,0)
\end{picture}}}}\mspace{3mu}\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\}-f\chi_{\{u>0\}}

in
B_{1}
in the distributional sense, that is,

-\int_{B_{1}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta dx=\int_{\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}}Q\eta d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{B_{1}}f\chi_{\{u>0\}}\eta dx\quad\text{for any }\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(B_{1}).

Remark 2.2
.

If
u:B_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
fulfils condition
item 1
of Definition
2.1
,
then
u\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(B_{1})
. This follows from a similar argument to

[
1
, Remark 4.2]
, adjusting for the fact that
-\Delta u=f
.

Remark 2.3
.

The second condition in Definition
2.1
is a regularity and non-degeneracy
condition. Indeed, let
u:B_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function satisfying conditions

item 1
and
item 2
. Consider an open set

D\subset\subset B_{1}
and let
B_{r}(x)\subset D
be any ball with

x\in\partial\{u>0\}
. Integrating in spherical coordinates in
item 2
yields

c\leq\frac{1}{r}\fint_{B_{r}(x)}udx\leq C

for some constants
c
,
C
depending on
D
. In particular,

c\leq\frac{1}{r}\fint_{B_{r}(x)}udx\leq\frac{1}{r}\sup_{B_{r}(x)}u,

and we see that the lower bound in
item 2
corresponds to a non-degeneracy
condition for
u
. Moreover, as we will see in Proposition
2.6
, the
upper bound in
item 2
implies that
u
is locally Lipschitz in
B_{1}
.
Thus, we can write

\tilde{c}\leq\frac{1}{r}\sup_{B_{r}(x)}u\leq\tilde{C},

where
\tilde{c}
and
\tilde{C}
are positive constants depending on

D
.

Remark 2.4
.

By Remark
2.3
, if
u
satisfies the first two conditions of Definition

2.1
, then
u
satisfies (1) and (2) of
[
23
, Definition 2.2]
.
Thus,
[
23
, Theorem 2.1, Remark 2.3]
imply that the set
\{u>0\}

has locally finite perimeter in
\Omega
. Hence, the reduced boundary

\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\}
in
item 3
is well defined.

Remark 2.5
.

As one would expect, any weak solution with smooth free boundary is a
classical solution. Indeed, if
\partial\{u>0\}
is regular enough (say

C^{1,\alpha}
), then

\partial\{u>0\}=\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\}
and by Schauder estimates

u\in C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\{u>0\}}\cap B_{1})
. Thus, given

\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(B_{1})
, an integration by parts yields

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}}\eta\nabla u\cdot\nu d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{\partial\{u>0\}\cap\partial B_{1}}\eta\nabla u\cdot\nu d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{\{u>0\}}\eta\Delta udx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}}\eta\partial_{\nu}ud\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\int_{B_{1}}f\chi_{\{u>0\}}\eta dx,

where
\nu
is the inward normal vector of
\{u>0\}
. Consequently, condition

item 3
implies that

\int_{\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}}(Q-\partial_{\nu}u)\eta d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=0\quad\text{for all }\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(B_{1})

and therefore
|\nabla u|=\partial_{\nu}u=Q
on
\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
u:B_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function satisfying condition
item 1
and the
upper bound of condition
item 2
of Definition
2.1
. Then
u
is
locally Lipschitz in
B_{1}
.

Proof.

We will prove that for each small
\delta>0
there is a constant
C_{\delta}
such that

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1-\delta})}\leq C_{\delta}
. Since
\nabla u=0
in the interior of

\{u=0\}\cap B_{1-\delta}
, it is enough to estimate the gradient of
u
in

\{u>0\}\cap B_{1-\delta}
. Let
x_{0}\in\{u>0\}\cap B_{1-\delta}
. We follow the proof of

[
33
, Lemma 3.5]
and consider two cases:

•

If
\dist(x_{0},\partial\{u>0\})\geq\delta/6
, then
-\Delta u=f
in the ball

B_{\delta/6}(x_{0})
and so, by classical Schauder estimates we have

\displaystyle|\nabla u(x_{0})|

\displaystyle\leq C_{n}\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\delta/6}(x_{0}))}+\delta\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\delta/6}(x_{0}))}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{n}\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1-\frac{\delta}{2}})}+\delta\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1-\frac{\delta}{2}})}\right).

Here we have used that

B_{\delta/6}(x_{0})\subset B_{1-\frac{5}{6}\delta}\subset B_{1-\frac{\delta}{2}}
since
x_{0}\in B_{1-\delta}
.

•

If
\dist(x_{0},\partial\{u>0\})<\delta/6
, then we let

y_{0}\in\partial\{u>0\}
be the point that realizes the distance to the free boundary
and set

r\coloneqq\dist(x_{0},\partial\{u>0\})=|x_{0}-y_{0}|.

Since
-\Delta u=f
in
B_{r}(x_{0})
, we can use interior estimates once more to
obtain

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r/2}(x_{0}))}\leq C_{n}\left(\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\|u\|_{L^{1}(B_{r}(x_{0}))}+r\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r}(x_{0}))}\right).

Hence,

\displaystyle|\nabla u(x_{0})|

\displaystyle\leq C_{n}\left(\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\|u\|_{L^{1}(B_{r}(x_{0}))}+r\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r}(x_{0}))}\right)

\displaystyle=C_{n}\left(\frac{1}{r}\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}udx+r\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r}(x_{0}))}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{n}\left(\frac{2^{n}}{r}\fint_{B_{2r}(y_{0})}udx+\frac{\delta}{6}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1-\frac{5}{6}\delta})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{n}\left(2^{n+1}C_{B_{1-\frac{\delta}{2}}}+\frac{\delta}{6}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1-\frac{\delta}{2}})}\right).

For the last inequality we have used the fact that for any open set

D\subset\subset B_{1}
, any
x\in\partial\{u>0\}\cap D
and any ball
B_{r}(x)\subset D
we have

\frac{1}{r}\fint_{B_{r}(x)}udx\leq C_{D}

for some constant
C_{D}
depending on
D
. This is an immediate consequence of
integrating in spherical coordinates in
item 2
.

∎

Remark 2.7
.

In the classical Alt-Caffarelli problem
f\equiv 0
,
Q\equiv 1
, weak solutions are
stationary solutions (see Definition
5.1
) by
[
35
, Theorem
5.1]
. Also, minimizers of
\mathcal{F}
are both viscosity and weak solutions by

[
33
,
1
]
respectively. In other words, we have the following
relations between the different notions of solutions:

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\text{Minimizer}&\implies&\text{Weak sol.}&\implies&\text{Stationary sol.}\\
\big\Downarrow&&&&\\
\text{Viscosity sol.}&&&&\end{array}

Note that weak solutions are a proper subclass of stationary solutions. Indeed, it
is easy to check that the function
u(x)=|x_{n}|
is a stationary solution in

\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
f\equiv 0
and
Q\equiv 1
. However,
u
is not a weak solution since

\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\}=\emptyset
and condition
item 3
would imply that

u
is harmonic in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. One of the main results of this paper is to show
that for the more general case
f\geq 0
, weak solutions are stationary solutions
(see Proposition
5.2
) and, assuming an additional condition on the free
boundary, viscosity solutions (see Proposition
6.9
).

Lemma 2.8
.

Assume that
u:B_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
is a function satisfying conditions
item 1
and

item 2
of Definition
2.1
. Let
B_{r_{k}}(x_{k})\subset B_{1}
be a
sequence of balls with
r_{k}\to 0
,
x_{k}\to x_{0}\in B_{1}
and
u(x_{k})=0
.
Consider the blow-up sequence

u_{r_{k}}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{r_{k}}u(x_{k}+r_{k}x).

Then there exists a Lipschitz continuous blow-up limit
u_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

such that, up to subsequence,

(1)

u_{r_{k}}\rightarrow u_{0}
in
C^{0,\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for every

0<\alpha<1
,

(2)

u_{r_{k}}\rightarrow u_{0}
in
H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

(3)

\chi_{\{u_{r_{k}}>0\}}\rightarrow\chi_{\{u_{0}>0\}}
in

L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

(4)

\partial\{u_{r_{k}}>0\}\rightarrow\partial\{u_{0}>0\}
locally in Hausdorff distance in

\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(5)

If
x_{k}\in\partial\{u>0\}
, then
0\in\partial\{u_{0}>0\}
,

(6)

\Delta u_{0}=0
in
\{u_{0}>0\}
.

Moreover,
u_{0}
satisfies condition
item 2
of Definition
2.1

with the same constants as
u
.

Proof.

The proof of 4.7 in
[
1
]
can be applied to our case with small
modifications to account for the fact that
-\Delta u=f
. Thus, we obtain the
existence of a Lipschitz continuous blow-up limit
u_{0}
satisfying

item 1
,
item 3
,
item 4
and
item 5
.
Moreover, we also have that
\nabla u_{r_{k}}\to\nabla u_{0}
weakly star in

L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Observe that
u_{r_{k}}
is a solution of
-\Delta u_{r_{k}}=f_{r_{k}}
in

\{u_{r_{k}}>0\}
with
f_{r_{k}}(x)=r_{k}f(x_{k}+r_{k}x)
and, since
f
is
smooth,
f_{r_{k}}\to 0
in
C^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Consider any ball

B_{r}(x)\subset\{u_{0}>0\}
. Then, for
k
large enough

B_{r}(x)\subset\{u_{r_{k}}>0\}
and

\|u_{r_{k}}-u_{r_{l}}\|_{C^{2,\alpha}(B_{r/2}(x))}\leq C(\|u_{r_{k}}-u_{r_{l}}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r}(x))}+\|f_{r_{k}}-f_{r_{l}}\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{r}(x))}).

It follows that
u_{r_{k}}
is a Cauchy sequence in
C^{2,\alpha}(K)
for any compact
subset
K\subset\{u_{0}>0\}
, and therefore
u_{r_{k}}\to u_{0}
in

C^{2,\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\{u_{0}>0\})
. In particular,

\Delta u_{0}=\lim_{r_{k}\to 0}\Delta u_{r_{k}}=-\lim_{r_{k}\to 0}f_{r_{k}}=0

which proves
item 6
.

Let us prove
item 2
. First we claim that

\nabla u_{r_{k}}\to\nabla u_{0}
pointwise a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. To see this, observe that since

u_{r_{k}}\to u_{0}
in
C^{2,\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\{u_{0}>0\})
, in particular

\nabla u_{r_{k}}\to\nabla u_{0}\quad\text{uniformly in compact subsets of }\{u_{0}>0\}.

Thus, we only need to show that

(2.2)

\nabla u_{r_{k}}\to\nabla u_{0}\quad\text{a.e. in }\{u_{0}=0\}.

In the contact set
\{u_{0}=0\}
a.a. points have density 1 by the Lebesgue
differentiation theorem. Let
S
be the set of such points. If
x\in S
, then

(2.3)

u_{0}(x+y)=o(|y|).

Indeed, otherwise we could find a sequence
y_{k}\in B_{\rho_{k}}(x)
with

\rho_{k}\to 0
such that
u_{0}(y_{k})>\gamma\rho_{k}
for some
\gamma>0
. Then by the
Lipschitz continuity of
u_{0}
we would have

u_{0}>\frac{\gamma}{2}\rho_{k}\quad\text{in }B_{c\gamma\rho_{k}}(y_{k})

for some small constant
c>0
depending only on the Lipschitz constant
of
u_{0}
. This would imply that
\{u_{0}>0\}
has positive density at

x
, contradicting the fact that
x\in S
. From (
2.3
) we have that
for any
\varepsilon>0
,

u_{0}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\rho\quad\text{in }B_{\rho}(x)\text{ for small }\rho.

Using that
u_{r_{k}}\to u_{0}
uniformly in
B_{\rho}(x)
we then see that

u_{r_{k}}<u_{0}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\rho<\varepsilon\rho

in
B_{\rho}(x)
if
k
is large enough, say
k\geq k_{0}(\varepsilon,\rho)
. Thus,

\frac{u_{r_{k}}}{\rho}<\varepsilon\quad\text{in }B_{\rho}(x)

and by non-degeneracy it then follows that
u_{r_{k}}=0
in
B_{\rho/2}(x)
.
Consequently,
u_{0}=0
in
B_{\rho/2}(x)
and therefore
S
is an open set.
Moreover, this argument also shows that
u_{r_{k}}=u_{0}=0
in any ball

B_{\delta}(x)\subset S
with
\delta
small enough and
k
sufficiently large. Hence, the
same conclusion also holds for any compact subset of
S
and this completes the
proof of (
2.2
).

Now, since
\nabla u_{r_{k}}\to\nabla u_{0}
weakly star in

L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the sequence
\nabla u_{r_{k}}
is bounded in

L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Hence, by the a.e. pointwise convergence showed
above and the dominated convergence theorem we have

\nabla u_{r_{k}}\to\nabla u_{0}
in
L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Also, the local uniform
convergence of
u_{r_{k}}
implies that
u_{r_{k}}\to u_{0}
in

L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Thus,
u_{r_{k}}\to u_{0}
in

H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Lastly, the fact that
u_{0}
satisfies condition
item 2
of Definition

2.1
is an immediate consequence of the local uniform convergence

u_{r_{k}}\to u_{0}
and can be proven in the same way as 4.7 in
[
1
]
.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

Using Lemma
2.8
and the same arguments of
[
1
, Lemma 5.3]
we
obtain that for any weak solution
u
,

(1)

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\{u>0\}\setminus\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\})=0
,

(2)

|\{u=0\}\cap D|>0
for every open set
D\subset B_{1}
containing a point of

\{u=0\}
.

3.
Improvement of flatness for weak solutions

The aim of this section is to obtain a regularity result for flat free boundaries in
the spirit of
[
1
, Theorem 8.1]
, but for weak solutions of our
inhomogeneous one-phase problem instead. Such a result was proven in
[
23
]

in a more general setting but using a more restrictive notion of weak solution. Thus,
our goal will be to show that any weak solution
u:B_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
in the sense of
Definition
2.1
is also a weak solution in the sense of
[
23
, Definition
2.2]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
u
is a weak solution in
B_{1}
, then for
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-a.e.

x_{0}\in\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}
we have the following asymptotic
development for
u
and
x\to 0
:

u(x_{0}+x)=Q(x_{0})(x\cdot\nu_{u}(x_{0}))_{+}+o(x),

where
\nu_{u}(x_{0})
is the interior normal vector of
\{u>0\}
at
x_{0}
.

Proof.

The result can be proven with the same arguments found in the proof of

[
1
, Theorem 4.8, Remark 4.9]
. For the convergence of blow-up sequences
we can use Lemma
2.8
, and instead of the representation theorem of

[
1
]
we use the definition of weak solution directly.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
u
be a weak solution in
B_{1}
and let

x_{0}\in\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}
. If there is a ball
B\subset\{u=0\}
touching the
boundary
\partial\{u>0\}
at
x_{0}
, then

\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\to x_{0}\\
u(x)>0\end{subarray}}\frac{u(x)}{\dist(x,B)}\geq Q(x_{0}).

Proof.

The proof can be carried out exactly as
[
1
, Lemma 4.10]
with the only
modification being that instead of using the representation theorem of

[
1
]
, we use condition
item 3
from the definition of weak
solution. For the convergence of blow-up sequences we use Lemma
2.8
.
∎

One of the conditions of
[
23
, Definition 2.2]
consists on an upper bound
on
|\nabla u|
at free boundary points. Precisely, for

x_{0}\in\partial\{u>0\}\cap\Omega
we need to prove that

\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\to x_{0}\\
u(x)>0\end{subarray}}|\nabla u(x)|\leq Q(x_{0}).

To show this, we will adapt
[
1
, Theorem 6.3]
to our inhomogeneous
setting, which gives an upper bound of
|\nabla u|
near the free boundary in terms of

Q
and some error terms. To prove this result we will need the following two
lemmas.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\delta,\kappa>0
be small constants and assume
u
is a weak solution such
that

|u(x)-Q(x_{0})((x-x_{0})\cdot\nu_{u}(x_{0}))_{+}|\leq\delta r

for
x\in B_{r/\kappa}(x_{0})\subset B_{1}
with

x_{0}\in\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\}
. Then, there exist constants
C_{1}
and

C_{2}
depending only on
n
and
\|f\|_{\infty}
such that

|\nabla u(x)-Q(x_{0})\nu_{u}(x_{0})|\leq C_{1}Q(x_{0})(\delta+\kappa)

for
x\in B_{r/2\kappa}(x_{0})\cap\{u=r\}
and

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(B_{r/2\kappa}(x_{0})\cap\{u=r\})\leq C_{2}\left(\frac{r}{\kappa}\right)^{n-1}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may assume
r=\kappa
,
\nu_{u}(x_{0})=e_{n}
,

Q(x_{0})=1
and
x_{0}=0
. By hypothesis,
u
is positive in

B_{1}\cap\{x_{n}>\delta\kappa\}
, and thus, the function

v(x)\coloneqq u(x)-x_{n}
solves
-\Delta v=f
in this region and satisfies

|v|\leq\delta\kappa
. Hence, if
\delta
and
\kappa
are small enough, the estimate

(3.1)

|\nabla v|\leq C_{n}\left(\frac{1}{\kappa}\|v\|_{\infty}+\kappa\|f\|_{\infty}\right)\leq C(\delta+\kappa)\quad\text{in}\quad B_{1/2}\cap\left\{x_{n}>\frac{\kappa}{2}\right\}

holds. In particular, since
u(x)=\kappa
implies

x_{n}\geq(1-\delta)\kappa>\frac{\kappa}{2}
, we obtain

(3.2)

|\partial_{n}u-1|\leq C(\delta+\kappa)\quad\text{on}\quad B_{1/2}\cap\{u=\kappa\}

and deduce that
\partial_{n}u>0
on
B_{1/2}\cap\{u=\kappa\}
provided

\delta
and
\kappa
are small enough. It follows by the implicit function theorem that

B_{1/2}\cap\{u=\kappa\}
is a graph, that is, there exists a function

g:B_{1/2}^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
u(x^{\prime},g(x^{\prime}))=\kappa
for
x^{\prime}\in B_{1/2}^{\prime}
, where

x=(x^{\prime},x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
and
B_{1/2}^{\prime}
is the ball of radius

1/2
in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. Moreover,

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(B_{1/2}\cap\{u=\kappa\})=\int_{B_{1/2}^{\prime}}\sqrt{1+|\nabla g|^{2}}dx^{\prime}

and

\nabla g=-\frac{1}{\partial_{n}u}(\partial_{1}u,\ldots,\partial_{n-1}u)
. Now, using
(
3.1
) and (
3.2
) it is easy to see
that
|\nabla g|^{2}\leq C
and therefore

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(B_{1/2}\cap\{u=\kappa\})\leq C|B_{1/2}^{\prime}|

which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
u
be a weak solution in
B_{1}
and let
D\subset\subset B_{1}
be a connected domain
containing a free boundary point. There exists a constant

C=C(n,D,c_{D},C_{D},\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(D)})<\infty
such that for

\varepsilon>0
and
B_{r}\subset D
with
r\leq 1

\int_{B_{r}\cap\{u=\varepsilon\}}|\nabla u|d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq Cr^{n-1}.

Proof.

Since
\nabla u
is bounded by Proposition
2.6
, for almost all
r

we have

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}\cap\{u=\varepsilon\}}|\nabla u|d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{r}\cap\partial\{u>\varepsilon\}}\nabla u\cdot\nu d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{r}\cap\{u>\varepsilon\}}\nabla u\cdot\nu d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\int_{B_{r}\cap\{u>\varepsilon\}}fdx

\displaystyle\leq c\int_{\partial B_{r}}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}\int_{B_{r}}dx.

Thus, using that
r\leq 1
we see that

\int_{B_{r}\cap\{u=\varepsilon\}}|\nabla u|d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq C(r^{n-1}+r^{n})\leq Cr^{n-1}.

∎

Proposition 3.5
.

If
u
is a weak solution in
B_{1}
with
f\geq 0
, then for every open set

D\subset\subset B_{1}
there are constants
C_{1}=C_{1}(n,D,c_{D},C_{D})
and

C_{2}=C_{2}(n,D,\|\nabla f\|_{\infty})
such that

(3.3)

\sup_{B_{r}(x_{0})}|\nabla u|\leq\sup_{B_{R}(x_{0})}Q+C_{1}\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{\alpha}+C_{2}R^{2}

for balls
B_{r}(x_{0})\subset B_{R}(x_{0})\subset D
, provided
r
and
R
are
sufficiently small.

Proof.

The proof of this result is for the most part the same as the proof of

[
1
, Theorem 6.3]
, with the exception of some small modifications to
account for the fact that
-\Delta u=f
in
\{u>0\}
. Below we mainly highlight
this modifications. For more details on the rest of the steps we refer the reader
to
[
1
]
.

For any domain
D\subset\subset B_{1}
with smooth boundary define

U\coloneqq(|\nabla u|-Q_{D})_{+}\quad\text{with}\quad Q_{D}\geq\sup_{D}Q.

Then
U
is bounded and an elementary computation shows that
U
is a
subsolution in
\{u>0\}
of the equation
-\Delta v=g
with

g=-\frac{\nabla u}{|\nabla u|}\cdot\nabla f
, that is,
-\Delta U\leq g
in

\{u>0\}
. Since we expect
U
to have zero boundary value on

\partial\{u>0\}\cap D
, first we prove the representation formula

U(x)=\int_{\{u>0\}\cap\partial D}U\partial_{\nu}G_{x}^{0}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{\{u>0\}\cap D}G_{x}^{0}d\lambda-\int_{\{u>0\}\cap D}gG_{x}^{0}dy

for
x\in\{u>0\}\cap D
, where
\lambda\coloneqq\Delta U+g
is a Radon measure,

G_{x}^{0}
is the Green function of the domain
\{u>0\}\cap D
and
\nu
is the
inward normal vector to
D
.

Step 1.

Given
x\in D
with
u(x)>\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon>0
small, define the Green
function
G_{x}^{\varepsilon}
as follows:

\displaystyle G_{x}^{\varepsilon}-\Gamma_{x}\in H^{1}(D)\quad\text{where }\Gamma_{x}\text{ is the fundamental solution,}

\displaystyle G_{x}^{\varepsilon}=0\quad\text{on }\partial D\text{ and }\{u\leq\varepsilon\}\cap D,\qquad\int_{D}\nabla G_{x}^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\zeta=\zeta(x)

for every
\zeta\in H^{1}(D)
that is Lipschitz continuous near
x
and zero on

\partial D
and
\{u\leq\varepsilon\}\cap D
. Proceeding as in
[
1
]
, we see that

G_{x}^{\varepsilon}
is a positive function in
(\{u>\varepsilon\}\cap D)\setminus\{x\}
satisfying that

G_{x}^{\varepsilon}\leq G_{x}^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\quad\text{for}\quad 0<\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq\varepsilon,

and
G_{x}^{0}\leq G_{x}
where
G_{x}
is the Green function with respect to the
entire domain
D
. Now, choose
\varepsilon_{x}
,
d_{x}
and
C_{x}
such that

G_{x}^{0}\leq C_{x}\frac{u(x)}{2}\text{ on }\partial B_{d_{x}}(x),\quad\text{and}\quad u-\varepsilon_{x}\geq\frac{u(x)}{2}\text{ in }B_{d_{x}}(x).

Then, for
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{x}
we can define the test function

\zeta
as
\zeta=\max(G_{x}^{\varepsilon}-C_{x}(u-\varepsilon)_{+},0)
in

D\setminus B_{d_{x}}(x)
and
\zeta=0
in
D\cap B_{d_{x}}(x)
. Using

\zeta
as test function for
G_{x}^{\varepsilon}
we find

\int_{D}\nabla G_{x}^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\zeta=\zeta(x)=0.

Also, since
\zeta=0
in
\{u\leq\varepsilon\}\cap D
by construction, testing

u
against
\zeta
we see that

\int_{D}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\zeta=\int_{D}f\zeta.

Combining the two equalities and using that
f,\zeta\geq 0
yields

\int_{D\setminus B_{d_{x}}(x)}\nabla(G_{x}^{\varepsilon}-C_{x}(u-\varepsilon))\cdot\nabla\zeta=-C_{x}\int_{D}f\zeta\leq 0.

Hence, if we set

A=\{y\in D\setminus B_{d_{x}}(x)\mid G_{x}^{\varepsilon}-C_{x}(u-\varepsilon)_{+}>0\}
, then

A\subset\{u>\varepsilon\}
and so

\int_{A}|\nabla(G_{x}^{\varepsilon}-C_{x}(u-\varepsilon))|^{2}=\int_{D\setminus B_{d_{x}}(x)}\nabla(G_{x}^{\varepsilon}-C_{x}(u-\varepsilon))\cdot\nabla\zeta\leq 0.

This would imply that
G_{x}^{\varepsilon}-C_{x}(u-\varepsilon)_{+}=0
in
A
, contradicting the
definition of
A
. Thus, we conclude that
A
is empty, that is,

G_{x}^{\varepsilon}\leq C_{x}(u-\varepsilon)_{+}\quad\text{in}\quad D\setminus B_{d_{x}}(x).

From this point the rest of this step can be carried out in the same way as

[
1
]
.

Step 2.

As in
[
1
]
, we get the same representation formula for

\varepsilon>0
but with an additional term:

U(x)=\int_{\{u>\varepsilon\}\cap\partial D}U\partial_{\nu}G_{x}^{\varepsilon}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{\{u>\varepsilon\}\cap D}G_{x}^{\varepsilon}d\lambda+\int_{\{u>\varepsilon\}\cap D}gG_{x}^{\varepsilon}dy+\int_{\{u=\varepsilon\}\cap\overline{D}}q_{x}^{\varepsilon}Ud\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

The first and second integrands converge to the desired limit as in

[
1
]
. Also, since

\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}\leq\|\nabla f\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}<\infty
, the third integrand converges to

\int_{\{u>0\}\cap D}gG_{x}^{0}dy

as wanted. Thus, we have to show that

\int_{\{u=\varepsilon\}\cap\overline{D}}Ud\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\to 0

as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Step 3.

Let
\kappa>0
be a small constant and for
\delta>0
choose an open set

D_{\delta}
such that

\overline{D}\subset D_{\delta}\subset\subset\Omega\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\{u>0\}\cap(D_{\delta}\setminus\overline{D}))\leq\delta.

Also, for
\varepsilon>0
let
K_{\varepsilon}
be the set of points

x_{0}\in\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\}\cap\overline{D}
such that

|u(x_{0}+x)-Q(x_{0})(x\cdot\nu_{u}(x_{0}))_{+}|\leq\delta\kappa|x|\quad\text{for }x\in B_{\varepsilon/\kappa}.

The sets
K_{\varepsilon}
are monotone in
\varepsilon
and by Proposition
3.1
they
form a covering of the reduced free boundary up to a set of
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
measure
zero. Hence by Remark
2.9
we can choose

\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\delta)
small enough such that

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\{u>0\}\cap(\overline{D}\setminus K_{\varepsilon}))\leq\delta\quad\text{and}\quad B_{\varepsilon/\kappa}(\overline{D})\subset D_{\delta},

where

B_{\varepsilon/\kappa}(\overline{D})=\bigcup_{x\in\overline{D}}B_{\varepsilon/\kappa}(x)
. Now, choose a finite
covering of
K_{\varepsilon}
with balls
B_{\varepsilon}^{j}
of radius
\varepsilon
such that

\chi_{K_{\varepsilon}}\leq\sum_{j}\chi_{B_{2\varepsilon}^{j}}\leq C(n),

and let
x_{j}\in B_{\varepsilon}^{j}\cap K_{\varepsilon}
. As in
[
1
]
, we can use Lemma

3.3
and
[
23
, Theorem 2.1]
to obtain the estimate

\sum_{j}\int_{B_{\varepsilon/(2\kappa)}(x_{j})\cap\{u=\varepsilon\}}Ud\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq C(\kappa)\delta\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\{u>0\}\cap D_{\delta})

and conclude that the left hand side tends to zero as
\delta\to 0
. Thus, it remains
to estimate

\int_{L_{\varepsilon}}Ud\mathcal{H}^{n-1},\quad\text{where }L_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq(\overline{D}\cap\{u=\varepsilon\})\setminus\bigcup_{j}B_{\varepsilon/(2\kappa)}(x_{j}).

For
x\in L_{\varepsilon}
with
U(x)>0
we have

|\nabla u(x)|\geq c>0
. Then for the maximum ball
B_{r}(x)
in

\{u>0\}
we can use the following approximate mean value formula for
u

u(x)=\fint_{\partial B_{r}(x)}ud\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{B_{r}(x)}f(y)G_{B_{r}(x)}(x,y)dy,\quad\text{($G_{B_{r}(x)}$ is the Green function of $B_{r}(x)$)}

to obtain the estimate

\displaystyle c\leq|\nabla u(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{n}}{r}\fint_{\partial B_{r}(x)}ud\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+C_{n}r\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r}(x))}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{n}}{r}\left(u(x)+\int_{B_{r}(x)}f(y)G_{B_{r}(x)}(x,y)dy\right)+C_{n}r\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r}(x))}.

Since

\int_{B_{r}(x)}f(y)G_{B_{r}(x)}(x,y)dy\leq\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r}(x))}\int_{B_{r}(x)}G_{B_{r}(x)}(x,y)dy=C_{n}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r}(x))}r^{2},

it follows that

(3.4)

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{n}}{r}\left(u(x)+\int_{B_{r}(x)}f(y)G_{B_{r}(x)}(x,y)dy\right)+C_{n}r\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r}(x))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{n}\varepsilon}{r}+C_{n}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}r.

Suppose
C_{n}\|f\|_{\infty}r\geq\frac{C_{n}\varepsilon}{r}
. Then

c\leq 2C_{n}\|f\|_{\infty}r
and therefore
r\geq c_{1}>0
. Set

v\coloneqq-\frac{\nabla u(x)}{|\nabla u(x)|}
and write

u(x+tv)-u(x)=\int_{0}^{t}\nabla u(x+sv)\cdot vds.

Since
-\Delta u=f
in
B_{c_{1}}(x)\subset B_{r}(x)
, by Schauder estimates

\displaystyle\|\nabla u\|_{C^{0,\alpha}(B_{c_{1}/2}(x))}

\displaystyle\leq C_{n}\left(\frac{1}{c_{1}}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{c_{1}}(x))}+c_{1}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{c_{1}}(x))}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{n}\left(\frac{1}{c_{1}}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}+c_{1}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}\right).

Thus, the function
h(s)\coloneqq\nabla u(x+sv)\cdot v
is Hölder continuous for

0\leq s<\frac{c_{1}}{2}
and its Hölder constant is independent of
x
.
Hence, since
h(0)=-|\nabla u(x)|\leq-c
, there exists some
s_{0}
independent of

x
such that
h(s)\leq-\frac{c}{2}
for
s\in[0,s_{0})
. It follows that for

t<s_{0}
we have

u(x+tv)-u(x)=\int_{0}^{t}\nabla u(x+sv)\cdot vds\leq-\frac{c}{2}t.

Consequently, taking
\varepsilon
small enough we can guarantee the existence of some

t
such that
2\varepsilon/c<t<s_{0}<c_{1}
and

u(x+tv)\leq\varepsilon-\frac{c}{2}t<0.

Since this contradicts the non-negativity of
u
, we deduce that

C_{n}\|f\|_{\infty}r\leq\frac{C_{n}\varepsilon}{r}
. Inserting this estimate back in
(
3.4
) we obtain

c\leq\frac{2C_{n}\varepsilon}{r},

which means that, for a small constant
\tau
independent of
\varepsilon
,

\kappa
and
x
, the ball
B_{\varepsilon/\tau}(x)
intersects the free boundary. From this
point the remaining part of this step can be finished as in
[
1
]
using
Lemma
3.4
and
[
23
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Step 4.

This step is for the most part the same as
[
1
]
. Let
x_{0}
be a
free boundary point and set
D=B_{r}(x_{0})
. Let
\nu
be the inward normal
vector to
B_{r}(x_{0})
. We will prove the following estimate for the Poisson
kernel
\partial_{\nu}G_{x}^{0}
:

\int_{\{u>0\}\cap\partial B_{r}(x_{0})}\partial_{\nu}G_{x}^{0}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq 2^{-\alpha}

for
x\in B_{r/2}(x_{0})
and some
\alpha>0
, provided
r
is small enough. By
the same computations in
[
1
]
we have

\int_{\partial B_{r}(x_{0})\cap\{u>\varepsilon\}}\partial_{\nu}G_{x}^{\varepsilon}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq 1-\limsup_{\delta\to 0}\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{\partial B_{r-\delta}(x_{0})}v_{x}^{\varepsilon}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\eqqcolon 1-s_{\varepsilon},

and we want to estimate
s_{\varepsilon}
from below. Define

u_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq(u-\varepsilon)_{+},\qquad\Lambda_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq B_{r/2}(x_{0})\cap\{u\leq\varepsilon\},

and let
w_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}(B_{r}(x_{0}))
be the solution of

\begin{cases}\Delta w_{\varepsilon}=0\quad&\text{in }B_{r}(x_{0})\setminus\Lambda_{\varepsilon},\\
w_{\varepsilon}=1\quad&\text{on }\partial B_{r}(x_{0}),\\
w_{\varepsilon}=0\quad&\text{a.e. in }\Lambda_{\varepsilon}.\end{cases}

Proceeding as in
[
1
]
we find that

s_{\varepsilon}\geq cr^{2-n}\int_{\partial B_{r}(x_{0})}\partial_{-\nu}w_{\varepsilon}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\geq cr^{2-n}\lambda_{w_{\varepsilon}}(B_{r/2}(x_{0})),

where
\lambda_{w_{\varepsilon}}\coloneqq\Delta w_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
is a Radon measure. Moreover, we also
see that
u_{\varepsilon}\leq Crw_{\varepsilon}
in
B_{r}(x_{0})
. Therefore, if we choose a sequence

\varepsilon\to 0
such that
\{u=\varepsilon\}\cap B_{r/2}(x_{0})
are smooth surfaces, then

w_{\varepsilon}
is continuous in
B_{r/2}(x_{0})
and zero in

\Lambda_{\varepsilon}
. Also,

\Delta(Crw_{\varepsilon}-u_{\varepsilon})=f
in

B_{r/2}(x_{0})\setminus\Lambda_{\varepsilon}
and we can apply Lemma 2.1 of
[
23
]
to
conclude that

\lambda_{Crw_{\varepsilon}-u_{\varepsilon}}\coloneqq\Delta(Crw_{\varepsilon}-u_{\varepsilon})-f\chi_{B_{r/2}(x_{0})\setminus\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}

is a Radon measure in
B_{r/2}(x_{0})
. Hence,

Cr\lambda_{w_{\varepsilon}}-\lambda_{u_{\varepsilon}}=\lambda_{Crw_{\varepsilon}-u_{\varepsilon}}\geq 0

and we obtain

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lambda_{w_{\varepsilon}}(B_{r/2}(x_{0}))\geq\frac{c}{r}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lambda_{u_{\varepsilon}}(B_{r/2}(x_{0}))\geq\frac{c}{r}\lambda_{u}(B_{r/2}(x_{0})).

Using Remark
2.3
,
[
23
, Lemma 2.4]
implies that

\lambda_{u}(B_{r/2}(x_{0}))\geq cr^{n-1}

for
r
small enough. Thus,

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}s_{\varepsilon}\geq cr^{2-n}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lambda_{w_{\varepsilon}}(B_{r/2}(x_{0}))\geq cr^{1-n}\lambda_{u}(B_{r/2}(x_{0}))\geq c

and this finishes the estimate for the Poisson kernel.

Step 5.

As in the previous step, set
D=B_{r}(x_{0})
with
r\leq R
and
define

\phi(r)\coloneqq\sup_{B_{r}(x_{0})}U(x),\qquad Q_{D}\coloneqq\sup_{B_{R}(x_{0})}Q(x)\geq\sup_{B_{r}(x_{0})}Q(x).

Then for
x\in B_{r/2}(x_{0})
we get, provided
r
is small enough,

\displaystyle U(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\{u>0\}\cap\partial B_{r}(x_{0})}U\partial_{\nu}G_{x}^{0}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{\{u>0\}\cap B_{r}(x_{0})}G_{x}^{0}d\lambda+\int_{\{u>0\}\cap B_{r}(x_{0})}gG_{x}^{0}dy

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sup_{B_{r}(x_{0})}U(x)\right)\int_{\{u>0\}\cap\partial B_{r}(x_{0})}\partial_{\nu}G_{x}^{0}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r}(x_{0}))}\int_{\{u>0\}\cap B_{r}(x_{0})}G_{x}^{0}dy

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-\alpha}\phi(r)+\|\nabla f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r}(x_{0}))}\int_{\{u>0\}\cap B_{r}(x_{0})}G_{x}^{0}dy

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-\alpha}\phi(r)+Cr^{2}.

Here we have used: in the first step, the representation formula we have
proved for
U
; in the second step, the fact that

\int_{\{u>0\}\cap B_{r}(x_{0})}G_{x}^{0}d\lambda
is non-negative; in the third step,
the estimate for the Poisson kernel; and in the last step, the fact
that

\int_{\{u>0\}\cap B_{r}(x_{0})}G_{x}^{0}dy\leq\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}G_{x}dy\leq C_{n}r^{2}.

Thus, we have obtained

\phi\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)\leq 2^{-\alpha}\phi(r)+Cr^{2},

and an iteration of this estimate yields

\phi\left(\frac{r}{2^{k}}\right)\leq 2^{-k\alpha}\phi(r)+Cr^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{4^{k-j}}=2^{-k\alpha}\phi(r)+C\left(1-\frac{1}{4^{k}}\right)r^{2}\leq 2^{-k\alpha}\phi(r)+Cr^{2}

for any
r\leq R
small enough. In particular, if
R
is also
sufficiently small we can apply this estimate with
r=R
to obtain

\phi\left(\frac{R}{2^{k}}\right)\leq 2^{-k\alpha}\phi(R)+CR^{2}.

Now, given
r\leq\frac{R}{2}
, choose
k
such that

\frac{R}{2^{k+1}}\leq r\leq\frac{R}{2^{k}}.

Then, since
\phi(r)
is monotone in
r
,

\phi(r)\leq\phi\left(\frac{R}{2^{k}}\right)\leq 2^{-k\alpha}\phi(R)+CR^{2}\leq\left(\frac{2r}{R}\right)^{\alpha}\phi(R)+CR^{2}.

Therefore,

\sup_{B_{r}(x_{0})}|\nabla u|-\sup_{B_{R}(x_{0})}Q\leq\phi(r)\leq\phi(R)\left(\frac{2r}{R}\right)^{\alpha}+CR^{2}

and the proof is finished.∎

.

Let
u
be a weak solution. Then

\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\to x_{0}\\
u(x)>0\end{subarray}}|\nabla u(x)|\leq Q(x_{0})

for every
x_{0}\in\partial\{u>0\}\cap\Omega
.

Proof.

The result follows immediately from Proposition
3.5
by taking the limits

r\to 0
and
R\to 0
in (
3.3
).

.

Let

.

Corollary 3.10
.

Let
u
be a weak solution in
B_{1}
and let

x_{0}\in\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}
be a regular point. Then,

\partial\{u>0\}
is a
C^{\infty}
-surface in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
.

Proof.

Replacing
u
with
Q(x_{0})^{-1}u
we may assume that
Q(x_{0})=1
. Let

u_{r_{k}}
be a blow-up sequence at
x_{0}
converging (in the sense of
Lemma
2.8
) to
u_{0}(x)=(x\cdot\nu)_{+}
for some unit vector

\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
u_{r_{k}}\to u_{0}
locally uniformly in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

\partial\{u_{r_{k}}>0\}\to\partial\{u_{0}>0\}
locally in Hausdorff distance, given any
small
\varepsilon>0
we have

(3.5)

\displaystyle\|u_{r_{k}}-u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\leq\varepsilon,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\partial\{u_{r_{k}}>0\}\subset\{-\varepsilon\leq x\cdot\nu\leq\varepsilon\}\cap B_{1},

for

large enough. On the one hand, it follows from (

) that

(x\cdot\nu)_{+}-\varepsilon\leq u_{r_{k}}(x)\leq(x\cdot\nu)_{+}+\varepsilon\quad\text{in }B_{1}

and therefore

u_{r_{k}}>0\quad\text{in }\{x\cdot\nu>\varepsilon\}\cap B_{1},\qquad u_{r_{k}}\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{in }\{x\cdot\nu<-\varepsilon\}\cap B_{1}.

On the other hand, by (
3.6
) we know that in

\{x\cdot\nu<-\varepsilon\}\cap B_{1}

u_{r_{k}}=0
or

u_{r_{k}}>0
. Fix any ball

. If
u_{r_{k}}>0
in

, then by the non-degeneracy of

we can find a point

. Hence,

cr\leq u_{r_{k}}(y)\leq\varepsilon

and taking

In particular

. Hence, taking

to conclude that

is a

-surface for some

and some small

. Thus,

\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\}\cap B_{\rho}(x_{0})=\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{\rho}(x_{0})

and

4.
Blow-up limits of weak solutions

The blow-up procedure we saw in Lemma
2.8
applies, in particular, to
weak solutions. However, the blow-up limit
u_{0}
is not guaranteed to be a
weak solution. In fact, without any additional assumptions the limit of a
sequence of weak solutions need not be a weak solution. Indeed, the function

u_{\varepsilon}(x)\coloneqq\max(|x_{n}|,\varepsilon)-\varepsilon

is a weak solution for
f\equiv 0
,
Q\equiv 1
for all
\varepsilon>0
. However,

u_{\varepsilon}
converges to
u_{0}(x)=|x_{n}|
in
H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as

\varepsilon\to 0
, and
u_{0}
is not a weak solution as we saw in Remark
2.7
. The
issue is that the
n
-dimensional Lebesgue measure of the contact set

\{u_{\varepsilon}=0\}
vanishes in the limit.

In the proof of
[
35
, Theorem 5.1]
it was shown that, for the classical
Alt-Caffarelli problem (
f\equiv 0
,
Q\equiv\text{const.}
), a positive density
condition on the contact set
\{u=0\}
guarantees that the blow-up limit of Lemma

2.8
is a weak solution. We now extend this result to the more general setting
(
2.1
). The proof follows the steps of

[
35
]
with minor adjustments.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
u
be a weak solution in
B_{1}
and assume
u
satisfies the following
uniform positive density condition: for every open subset
D\subset\subset B_{1}
, there
exists a constant
c_{D}>0
such that

(4.1)

\frac{|\{u=0\}\cap B_{r}(x)|}{|B_{r}|}\geq c_{D}>0

for any ball
B_{r}(x)\subset D
with
x\in\partial\{u>0\}\cap D
. Then, for any

x_{0}\in\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}
and any blow-up sequence at
x_{0}

u_{r_{k}}(x)=\frac{1}{r_{k}}u(x_{0}+r_{k}x),\quad r_{k}\to 0,

there exists a Lipschitz continuous blow-up limit
u_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
such
that, up to subsequence,

(1)

u_{r_{k}}\to u_{0}
in the sense of Lemma
2.8
,

(2)

u_{0}
is a weak solution in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
f\equiv 0
and

Q\equiv Q(x_{0})
.

Proof.

Given a blow-up sequence
u_{r_{k}}
, Lemma
2.8
implies the existence of a
blow-up limit
u_{0}\in C^{0,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
u_{r_{k}}\to u_{0}
(in the sense of
Lemma
2.8
). Moreover, we also know from Lemma
2.8
that
u_{0}

satisfies conditions
item 1
and
item 2
of Definition

2.1
with
f\equiv 0
. Let us prove that
u_{0}
satisfies condition

item 3
.

Step 1.

First we claim that (
4.1
) also holds for the blow-up limit

u_{0}
. Let
D\subset\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
B_{r}(x)\subset D
be a ball with

x\in\partial\{u_{0}>0\}
. Since

\partial\{u_{r_{k}}>0\}\to\partial\{u_{0}>0\}
locally in Hausdorff distance, we can find a
sequence of points
x_{k}\in\partial\{u_{r_{k}}>0\}
such that
x_{k}\to x
. Notice that
for any
k

\displaystyle|\{u_{r_{k}}=0\}\cap B_{r}(x_{k})|

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{r}(x_{k})}\chi_{\{u_{r_{k}}=0\}}(y)dy=\frac{1}{r_{k}^{n}}\int_{B_{rr_{k}}(x_{0}+r_{k}x_{k})}\chi_{\{u=0\}}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r_{k}^{n}}|\{u=0\}\cap B_{rr_{k}}(x_{0}+r_{k}x_{k})|.

Since
B_{r}(x_{k})\subset D
for
k
large enough, there exists an open set

D^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
(depending on
D
) such that

B_{rr_{k}}(x_{0}+r_{k}x_{k})\subset x_{0}+r_{k}D\subset D^{\prime}
. Moreover,

x_{k}\in\partial\{u_{r_{k}}>0\}
implies that

x_{0}+r_{k}x_{k}\in\partial\{u>0\}
. Hence, by the positive density of
u
at

x_{0}+r_{k}x_{k}
there is a constant
c>0
depending on
D^{\prime}
(and therefore
on
D
) such that

\frac{1}{r_{k}^{n}}|\{u=0\}\cap B_{rr_{k}}(x_{0}+r_{k}x_{k})|\geq\frac{1}{r_{k}^{n}}|B_{rr_{k}}|c=|B_{r}|c.

Thus,

\frac{|\{u_{r_{k}}=0\}\cap B_{r}(x_{k})|}{|B_{r}|}\geq c>0

and the convergence
\chi_{\{u_{r_{k}}>0\}}\to\chi_{\{u_{0}>0\}}
in

L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
implies that

\frac{|\{u_{0}=0\}\cap B_{r}(x)|}{|B_{r}|}\geq c

with
c=c(D)
.

Step 2.

Next we claim that

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\{u_{0}>0\}\setminus\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u_{0}>0\})=0
. Assume by
contradiction that the claim does not hold. Then by
[
16
, 4.5.6 (3)]
,

s^{1-n}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u_{0}>0\}\cap B_{s}(x_{1}))\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad s\to 0

for
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-a.a. points

x_{1}\in\partial\{u_{0}>0\}\setminus\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u_{0}>0\}
. Let

x_{1}
be such a point and consider a second blow-up sequence

u_{0s_{l}}(x)\coloneqq(u_{0})_{s_{l}}(x)=\frac{1}{s_{l}}u_{0}(x_{1}+s_{l}x).

By Lemma
2.8
,
u_{0s_{l}}
converges to a blow-up limit
u_{00}
.
Moreover, the fact that
\{u_{0}>0\}
has locally finite perimeter in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

(recall Remark
2.4
) implies that for
R\in(0,\infty)
and

\phi\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(B_{R};\mathbb{R}^{n})

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}\chi_{\{u_{0s_{l}}>0\}}\Div\phi

\displaystyle=s_{l}^{1-n}\int_{B_{Rs_{l}}(x_{1})}\chi_{\{u_{0}>0\}}\Div\left(\phi\left(\frac{x-x_{1}}{s_{l}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=s_{l}^{1-n}\int_{\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u_{0}>0\}\cap B_{Rs_{l}}(x_{1})}\phi\left(\frac{x-x_{1}}{s_{l}}\right)\cdot\nu d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

\displaystyle\leq\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}s_{l}^{1-n}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u_{0}>0\}\cap B_{Rs_{l}}(x_{1})).

Taking the limit as
l\to\infty
we obtain that

\int_{B_{R}}\chi_{\{u_{00}>0\}}\Div\phi=0,\quad\forall\phi\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(B_{R};\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Therefore,
\chi_{\{u_{00}>0\}}
is a function of bounded variation which
is constant a.e. in
B_{R}
. Since
u
satisfies (
4.1
),
so do
u_{0}
and
u_{00}
by the previous step. Thus,
u_{00}=0
in

B_{R}
which contradicts the non-degeneracy of
u_{0}
at
x_{1}
.

Step 3.

Our next step is to prove that
\partial\{u_{0}>0\}
is a

C^{1,\alpha}
-surface in a neighborhood of any point of the reduced boundary

\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u_{0}>0\}
. Let

x_{1}\in\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u_{0}>0\}
and once again let

u_{00}
be a blow-up limit of
u_{0}
at
x_{1}
. Then,

\{u_{0s_{l}}=0\}\longrightarrow\{x\cdot\nu<0\}\quad\text{in }L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}},

where
\nu=\nu_{u_{0}}(x_{1})
denotes the inward unit normal vector to

\{u_{0}>0\}
at
x_{1}
. Since

\chi_{\{u_{0s_{l}}>0\}}\longrightarrow\chi_{\{u_{00}>0\}}\quad\text{in }L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

as well, we infer that
\{u_{00}=0\}=\{x\cdot\nu<0\}
almost everywhere. By the
continuity and the uniform density estimate of
u_{00}
it follows that

\{u_{00}=0\}=\{x\cdot\nu\leq 0\}\text{ everywhere}.

Thus,
\partial\{u_{00}>0\}=\{x\cdot\nu=0\}
and the local convergence

\partial\{u_{0s_{l}}>0\}\to\partial\{u_{00}>0\}
in Hausdorff distance implies that for
any
\varepsilon>0

\|u_{0s_{l}}-u_{00}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\leq\varepsilon,\qquad\partial\{u_{0s_{l}}>0\}\cap B_{1}\subset\{-\varepsilon\leq x\cdot\nu\leq\varepsilon\}\cap B_{1},

for
l
large enough. In particular, in

\{x\cdot\nu<-\varepsilon\}\cap B_{1}
either
u_{0s_{l}}>0
or

u_{0s_{l}}=0
. Moreover,
u_{0s_{l}}\leq\varepsilon
in

\{x\cdot\nu<-\varepsilon\}\cap B_{1}
since
u_{00}=0
. Now fix

B_{\rho}(x)\subset\{x\cdot\nu<-\varepsilon\}\cap B_{1}
and suppose

u_{0s_{l}}
is positive in
B_{\rho}(x)
. Then, by the non-degeneracy of
u_{0}

there exists a point
y\in B_{\rho}(x)
such that

c\rho\leq u_{0s_{l}}(y)\leq\varepsilon

and taking
\varepsilon
small enough we reach a contradiction. Therefore we conclude
that
u_{0s_{l}}=0
in
\{x\cdot\nu<-\varepsilon\}\cap B_{1}
. In other words,

u_{0s_{l}}\in F(1,\varepsilon;\infty)
in
B_{1}
in the direction
\nu
, or equivalently,

u_{0}\in F(1,\varepsilon;\infty)
in
B_{s_{l}}(x_{1})
in the direction
\nu
. Repeating this
argument with the first blow-up sequence
u_{r_{k}}
we obtain that

u_{r_{k}}\in F(1,2\varepsilon;\infty)
in
B_{s_{l}}(x_{1})
in the direction

\nu
. Hence, taking
\varepsilon
and
s_{l}
small enough we can apply Theorem

3.8
to deduce that
\partial\{u_{r_{k}}>0\}\cap B_{\rho}(x_{1})
is a

C^{1,\alpha}
-surface for some small radius
\rho
. Lastly, since the surfaces

\partial\{u_{r_{k}}>0\}\cap B_{\rho}(x_{1})
are uniformly bounded in

C^{1,\alpha}
(also by Theorem
3.8
), we conclude that

\partial\{u_{0}>0\}
must be a
C^{1,\alpha}
-surface in
B_{\rho}(x_{1})
.

Step 4.

Let
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Since

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\{u_{0}>0\}\setminus\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u_{0}>0\})=0
and

\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u_{0}>0\}
is open relative to

\partial\{u_{0}>0\}
, we can find a finite covering

\supp\eta\cap(\partial\{u_{0}>0\}\setminus\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u_{0}>0\})\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}B_{t_{i}}(y_{i})

such that

\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}t_{i}^{n-1}\leq\delta.

Split the left hand side of condition
item 3
as follows:

\displaystyle\int\nabla u_{0}\cdot\nabla\eta=\int_{\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}B_{t_{i}}(y_{i})}\nabla u_{0}\cdot\nabla\eta+\int_{\{u>0\}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}B_{t_{i}}(y_{i})}\nabla u_{0}\cdot\nabla\eta\eqqcolon I_{1}+I_{2}.

For the first integral we have

|I_{1}|\leq C_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}t_{i}^{n}\leq C_{1}\delta^{\frac{n}{n-1}}

and therefore
I_{1}\to 0
as
\delta\to 0
. For the second integral,

\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u_{0}>0\}
being a
C^{1,\alpha}
-surface implies that

u_{0}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\{u_{0}>0\}}\cap(\supp\eta\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}B_{t_{i}}(y_{i})))
by classical Schauder regularity estimates. Also, by

[
23
, Theorem 4.4]
we know
\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u_{r_{k}}>0\}
is a

C^{1,\alpha}
-surface for every
k
as well. In particular,
u_{r_{k}}
is a weak
solution for
f_{r_{k}}=r_{k}f(x_{0}+r_{k}x)
and

Q_{r_{k}}(x)\coloneqq Q(x_{0}+r_{k}x)
satisfying

\partial_{\nu}u_{r_{k}}=Q_{r_{k}}
on
\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u_{r_{k}}>0\}
in the
classical sense by Remark
2.5
. Moreover, the smoothness of
Q
implies
that
Q_{r_{k}}\to Q(x_{0})
locally uniformly. Thus, since

\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u_{r_{k}}>0\}
are
C^{1,\alpha}
-surfaces converging to

\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u_{0}>0\}
in the
C^{1,\alpha}
norm, we deduce that

\partial_{\nu}u_{0}=Q(x_{0})
on
\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u_{0}>0\}
in the classical sense.
Integrating by parts and using that
\Delta u_{0}=0
in
\{u_{0}>0\}
we obtain

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\{u_{0}>0\}\cap\partial(\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}B_{t_{i}}(y_{i}))}\eta\partial_{\nu}u_{0}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{\partial\{u_{0}>0\}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}B_{t_{i}}(y_{i})}\eta\partial_{\nu}u_{0}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\{u_{0}>0\}\cap\partial(\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}B_{t_{i}}(y_{i}))}\eta\partial_{\nu}u_{0}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u_{0}>0\}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}B_{t_{i}}(y_{i})}Q(x_{0})\eta d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Since

\left|\int_{\{u_{0}>0\}\cap\partial(\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}B_{t_{i}}(y_{i}))}\eta\partial_{\nu}u_{0}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\right|\leq C_{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}t_{i}^{n-1}\leq C_{2}\delta,

letting
\delta\to 0
we see that

I_{2}=-\int_{\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u_{0}>0\}}Q(x_{0})\eta d\mathcal{H}^{n-1},

and therefore

\int\nabla u_{0}\cdot\nabla\eta=-\int_{\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u_{0}>0\}}Q(x_{0})\eta d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Thus,
u_{0}
satisfies condition
item 3
with
f\equiv 0
and

Q\equiv Q(x_{0})
and the proof is complete.∎

The uniform density condition stated in Proposition
4.1
does not hold in
general for all weak solutions. Consider, for instance, the following example: let

u(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}-|x-\frac{1}{2}e_{n}|^{2})\quad&\text{if }x\in B_{1/2}(\frac{1}{2}e_{n}),\\
\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}-|x+\frac{1}{2}e_{n}|^{2})\quad&\text{if }x\in B_{1/2}(-\frac{1}{2}e_{n}),\\
0\quad&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then
u
is a weak solution in
B_{1}
for
f\equiv n
,
Q\equiv 1
(in fact,

u
is a classical solution outside the origin) and

\partial\{u>0\}=\{0\}\cup\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\}.

However, the contact set
\{u=0\}
has density zero at the origin. Thus,
despite being a weak solution,
u
fails to satisfy (
4.1
).

This example also shows that even for blow-up sequences, a very specific kind
of sequence, the blow-up limit need not be a weak solution. Indeed, if

u_{r_{k}}
is a blow-up sequence of
u
at
0
, then the blow-up limit

u_{0}
is

u_{0}(x)=|x\cdot e_{n}|.

In particular,

\{u_{0}=0\}=\{x_{n}=0\},

and consequently
|\{u_{0}=0\}|=0
. Thus, we infer that
u_{0}
cannot be
a weak solution (see Remark
2.9
).

For our purposes, however, the Lipschitz regularity of the free boundary
guarantees the density estimate required by Proposition
4.1
. Hence,
we have the following corollary.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
u
be a weak solution in
\Omega
such that
\partial\{u>0\}
is Lipschitz.
For any point
x_{0}\in\partial\{u>0\}\cap\Omega
and any blow-up sequence

u_{r_{k}}
at
x_{0}
, there exists a weak solution

u_{0}\in C^{0,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
f\equiv 0
and

Q\equiv Q(x_{0})
such that, up to subsequence,
u_{r_{k}}\to u_{0}
in the sense
of Lemma
2.8
.

Proof.

The result is an immediate consequence of Proposition
4.1
and the fact
that Lipschitz boundaries satisfy (
4.1
).
∎

5.
Homogeneity of blow-up limits

In this section we prove the 1-homogeneity of blow-up limits by means of a Weiss
monotonicity formula.

5.1.
Stationary solutions

To prove the monotonicity formula we first show the important fact that weak
solutions are also stationary solutions.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
u\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(B_{1})
be a non-negative function such that

u\in C(B_{1})\cap C^{2}(\{u>0\})
. We say
u
is a stationary solution of
(
2.1
) if the inner variation of the functional

\mathcal{F}_{f,Q}(v)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{1}}\left(|\nabla v|^{2}+Q^{2}\chi_{\{v>0\}}\right)dx-\int_{B_{1}}fvdx

vanishes at
u
, that is, for any
\xi\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})
we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\delta\mathcal{F}_{f,Q}(u_{\varepsilon},B_{1})[\xi]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{1}}\left(|\nabla u|^{2}\Div\xi-2\nabla u\cdot D\xi\nabla u+\Div(Q^{2}\xi)\chi_{\{u>0\}}\right)dx-\int_{B_{1}}\Div(f\xi)udx,

where
u_{\varepsilon}=u\circ\tau_{\varepsilon}^{-1}
and

\tau_{\varepsilon}
is the diffeomorphism defined by

\tau_{\varepsilon}(x)=x+\varepsilon\xi(x)
for
x\in B_{1}
and
\varepsilon
small.

Proposition 5.2
.

Every weak solution is a stationary solution.

Proof.

We follow the ideas of the proof of
[
35
, Theorem 5.1]
. Let
u
be a
weak solution in
B_{1}
. We will use the facts that

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\{u>0\}\setminus\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\})=0
, that

\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\}
is open relative to
\partial\{u>0\}
and that

\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\}
is locally a
C^{\infty}
-surface in
B_{1}
(see

[
23
, Corollary 4.1]
).

Let
\xi\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(B_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})
. For any

\delta>0
we can find a covering

\supp\xi\cap(\partial\{u>0\}\setminus\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\})\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}B_{r_{i}}(x_{i})

such that
x_{i}\in\partial\{u>0\}\setminus\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}r_{i}^{n-1}\leq\delta
. Since
\partial\{u>0\}\setminus\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\}
is closed,

\supp\xi\cap(\partial\{u>0\}\setminus\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\})
is compact and we may
pass to a finite subcovering

\supp\xi\cap(\partial\{u>0\}\setminus\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\})\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}B_{r_{i}}(x_{i}).

Denote
B_{i}\coloneqq B_{r_{i}}(x_{i})
and split the inner variation as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\Div\xi-\nabla u\cdot D\xi\nabla u+\frac{1}{2}\Div(Q^{2}\xi)\chi_{\{u>0\}}-\Div(f\xi)u\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\{u>0\}\cap\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}B_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\Div\xi-\nabla u\cdot D\xi\nabla u+\frac{1}{2}\Div(Q^{2}\xi)-\Div(f\xi)u\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\{u>0\}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}B_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\Div\xi-\nabla u\cdot D\xi\nabla u+\frac{1}{2}\Div(Q^{2}\xi)-\Div(f\xi)u\right)

\displaystyle\eqqcolon I_{1}+I_{2}.

For the first integral we have that

(5.1)

|I_{1}|\leq C_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}r_{i}^{n}\leq C_{1}\delta^{\frac{n}{n-1}}

using the fact that

r_{k}^{n-1}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}r_{i}^{n-1}\leq\delta
for all
k
. On the other hand, by
Remark
2.5
we also know

u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\{u>0\}}\cap(\supp\xi\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}B_{r_{i}}(x_{i})))
and
the fact that
u
fulfills the boundary condition

|\nabla u|=\partial_{\nu}u=Q
on

\partial\{u>0\}\cap(\supp\xi\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}B_{r_{i}}(x_{i}))
in the classical sense.
Moreover, in
\{u>0\}
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\Div\xi

\displaystyle-\nabla u\cdot D\xi\nabla u=

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{i}u)^{2}\partial_{j}\xi_{j}-\partial_{j}u\partial_{i}\xi_{j}\partial_{i}u

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{i}u)^{2}\partial_{j}\xi_{j}-\partial_{i}(\partial_{j}u\,\xi_{j}\,\partial_{i}u)+\partial_{ij}u\,\xi_{j}\,\partial_{i}u+\partial_{j}u\,\xi_{j}\,\partial_{ii}u

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{i}u)^{2}\partial_{j}\xi_{j}-\partial_{i}(\partial_{j}u\,\xi_{j}\,\partial_{i}u)+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{j}(\partial_{i}u\,\xi_{j}\,\partial_{i}u)-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{i}u)^{2}\partial_{j}\xi_{j}+\partial_{j}u\,\xi_{j}\,\partial_{ii}u

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}-\partial_{i}(\partial_{j}u\xi_{j}\partial_{i}u)+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{j}(\partial_{i}u\,\xi_{j}\,\partial_{i}u)+\partial_{j}u\,\xi_{j}\,\partial_{ii}u

\displaystyle=\Div\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\xi-(\nabla u\cdot\xi)\nabla u\right)-f\nabla u\cdot\xi,

and therefore

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\Div\xi

\displaystyle-\nabla u\cdot D\xi\nabla u-\Div(f\xi)u=

\displaystyle=\Div\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\xi-(\nabla u\cdot\xi)\nabla u\right)-f\nabla u\cdot\xi-u\Div(f\xi)

\displaystyle=\Div\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\xi-(\nabla u\cdot\xi)\nabla u\right)-\Div(uf\xi).

Thus, since

\supp\xi\cap\left(\{u>0\}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}B_{i}\right)
is a piecewise

C^{1}
domain, we can apply the divergence theorem to
I_{2}
to obtain

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\partial\left(\{u>0\}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}B_{i}\right)}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\xi\cdot\nu-\nabla u\cdot\xi\nabla u\cdot\nu+\frac{1}{2}Q^{2}\xi\cdot\nu-uf\xi\cdot\nu\right)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\partial\{u>0\}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}B_{i}}\frac{1}{2}\left(Q^{2}-|\nabla u|^{2}\right)\xi\cdot\nu d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\{u>0\}\cap\partial\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}B_{i}\right)}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\xi\cdot\nu-\nabla u\cdot\xi\nabla u\cdot\nu+\frac{1}{2}Q^{2}\xi\cdot\nu-uf\xi\cdot\nu\right)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\{u>0\}\cap\partial\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}B_{i}\right)}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\xi\cdot\nu-\nabla u\cdot\xi\nabla u\cdot\nu+\frac{1}{2}Q^{2}\xi\cdot\nu-uf\xi\cdot\nu\right)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Hence, we see that

(5.2)

|I_{2}|\leq C_{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\delta}}r_{i}^{n-1}\leq C_{2}\delta.

Combining (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) yields

\left|\int_{B_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}\Div\xi-\nabla u\cdot D\xi\nabla u+\frac{1}{2}\Div(Q^{2}\xi)\chi_{\{u>0\}}+\Div(f\xi)u\right)\right|\leq C_{1}\delta^{\frac{n}{n-1}}+C_{2}\delta

and letting
\delta\to 0
gives the desired result.
∎

5.2.
Weiss monotonicity formula

For a given boundary data
Q
and a non-negative function
u\in H^{1}(B_{1})
, we
define the modified Weiss function

W_{Q}(u)\coloneqq\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx+Q(0)^{2}|\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}|-\int_{\partial B_{1}}u^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Then, for any
u\in H^{1}(B_{r}(x_{0}))
,
u\geq 0
, the scaling

u_{r}(x)=\frac{1}{r}u(x_{0}+rx),\qquad Q_{r}(x)=Q(x_{0}+rx),

satisfies that
u_{r}\in H^{1}(B_{1})
and

\displaystyle W_{Q}(u_{r})

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla u_{r}|^{2}dx+Q_{r}(0)^{2}|\{u_{r}>0\}\cap B_{1}|-\int_{\partial B_{1}}u_{r}^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r^{n}}\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}|\nabla u|^{2}dx+\frac{Q(x_{0})^{2}}{r^{n}}|\{u>0\}\cap B_{r}(x_{0})|-\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\int_{\partial B_{r}(x_{0})}u^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

By
[
33
, Lemmas 9.1, Lemma 9.2]
the function
r\mapsto W_{Q}(u_{r})
is
continuous and a.e. differentiable for any
u\in H^{1}(B_{\delta}(x_{0}))
and

r\in(0,\delta)
. Moreover, if
z_{r}(x)=|x|u_{r}(\frac{x}{|x|})
is the 1-homogeneous
extension of
u_{r}
from
\partial B_{1}
to
B_{1}
, then
[
33
, Lemma 9.2]
also
gives an explicit expression for the derivative of
W_{Q}(u_{r})
, namely

\frac{\partial}{\partial r}W_{Q}(u_{r})=\frac{n}{r}\left(W_{Q}(z_{r})-W_{Q}(u_{r})\right)+\frac{1}{r}\int_{\partial B_{1}}|\nabla u_{r}\cdot x-u_{r}|^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
u
be a weak solution in
B_{1}
. Then, for every
x_{0}\in B_{1}
and every

0<r<r_{0}
with
r_{0}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\dist(x_{0},\partial B_{1})
, we have

W_{Q}(z_{r})-W_{Q}(u_{r})\geq\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial B_{1}}|\nabla u_{r}\cdot x-u_{r}|^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-Cr,

where
C
is a positive constant that depends on
n
,

\|u\|_{\infty}
,
\|f\|_{\infty}
,
\|\nabla f\|_{\infty}
,
\|Q\|_{\infty}
and
\|\nabla Q\|_{\infty}
.

Proof.

We follow the ideas of
[
33
, Lemma 9.8]
. Without loss of generality
assume
x_{0}=0
. For every
\varepsilon>0
, consider the function

\phi_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(B_{r})
such that

\phi_{\varepsilon}=1\quad\text{in }B_{(1-\varepsilon)r},\qquad\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)=-\frac{1}{r\varepsilon}\frac{x}{|x|}+o(\varepsilon)\quad\text{in }B_{r}\setminus B_{(1-\varepsilon)r},

and define the test vector field
\xi_{\varepsilon}(x)=\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)x
. Since

u
is a stationary solution in
B_{1}
by Proposition
5.2
, testing

u
against
\xi_{\varepsilon}
we obtain

\displaystyle 0=\delta\mathcal{F}_{f,Q}(u)[\xi_{\varepsilon}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\left(|\nabla u|^{2}\Div\xi_{\varepsilon}-2\nabla u\cdot D\xi_{\varepsilon}\nabla u+(Q^{2}\Div\xi_{\varepsilon}+\nabla Q^{2}\cdot\xi_{\varepsilon})\chi_{\{u>0\}}\right)dx

\displaystyle\quad-\int(\nabla f\cdot\xi_{\varepsilon}+f\Div\xi_{\varepsilon})udx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\left(-2\phi_{\varepsilon}|\nabla u|^{2}-2(\nabla u\cdot x)(\nabla u\cdot\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon})\right)dx

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int\left((n\phi_{\varepsilon}+\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}\cdot x)(|\nabla u|^{2}+Q^{2}\chi_{\{u>0\}})+\phi_{\varepsilon}\nabla Q^{2}\cdot x\chi_{\{u>0\}}\right)dx

\displaystyle\quad-\int\left((n\phi_{\varepsilon}+\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}\cdot x)uf+\phi_{\varepsilon}u\nabla f\cdot x\right)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{r}}\left((n-2)|\nabla u|^{2}+(nQ^{2}+\nabla Q^{2}\cdot x)\chi_{\{u>0\}}\right)\phi_{\varepsilon}dx

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2r\varepsilon}\int_{B_{r}\setminus B_{(1-\varepsilon)r}}\left(2\left(\nabla u\cdot\frac{x}{|x|}\right)^{2}-|\nabla u|^{2}-Q^{2}\chi_{\{u>0\}}\right)|x|dx

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{B_{r}}\left(nuf+u\nabla f\cdot x\right)\phi_{\varepsilon}dx+\frac{1}{r\varepsilon}\int_{B_{r}\setminus B_{(1-\varepsilon)r}}uf|x|dx+o(\varepsilon).

Now we can let
\varepsilon\to 0
and multiply everything by 2 to get

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{r}}\left((n-2)|\nabla u|^{2}+(nQ^{2}+\nabla Q^{2}\cdot x)\chi_{\{u>0\}}\right)dx

\displaystyle\quad+r\int_{\partial B_{r}}\left(2\left(\nabla u\cdot\frac{x}{|x|}\right)^{2}-|\nabla u|^{2}-Q^{2}\chi_{\{u>0\}}\right)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

\displaystyle\quad-2\int_{B_{r}}\left(nuf+u\nabla f\cdot x\right)dx+2r\int_{\partial B_{r}}ufd\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Using that
-\Delta u=f
in
\{u>0\}
we can compute

\int_{B_{r}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx=\int_{B_{r}}\Div(u\nabla u)dx+\int_{B_{r}}ufdx=\int_{\partial B_{r}}u\nabla u\cdot\frac{x}{|x|}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\int_{B_{r}}ufdx.

Inserting this computation in the term
-2|\nabla u|^{2}
of the first integral
and rescaling everything to
B_{1}
we find that

(5.3)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{1}}\left(n|\nabla u_{r}|^{2}+(nQ_{r}^{2}+\nabla Q_{r}^{2}\cdot x)\chi_{\{u_{r}>0\}}\right)dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial B_{1}}\left(-2u_{r}\nabla u_{r}\cdot x+2(\nabla u_{r}\cdot x)^{2}-|\nabla u_{r}|^{2}-Q_{r}^{2}\chi_{\{u_{r}>0\}}\right)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

\displaystyle-2\int_{B_{1}}\left((n+1)u_{r}f_{r}+u_{r}\nabla f_{r}\cdot x\right)dx+2\int_{\partial B_{1}}u_{r}f_{r}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

With this we see that

\displaystyle W_{Q}(z_{r})

\displaystyle-W_{Q}(u_{r})=

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla z_{r}|^{2}dx+Q_{r}(0)^{2}|\{z_{r}>0\}\cap B_{1}|-\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla u_{r}|^{2}dx-Q_{r}(0)^{2}|\{u_{r}>0\}\cap B_{1}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial B_{1}}\left(|\nabla u_{r}|^{2}-|\nabla u_{r}\cdot x|^{2}\right)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial B_{1}}u_{r}^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{n}Q_{r}(0)^{2}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\{u_{r}>0\}\cap\partial B_{1})-\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla u_{r}|^{2}dx-Q_{r}(0)^{2}|\{u_{r}>0\}\cap B_{1}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial B_{1}}|\nabla u_{r}\cdot x-u_{r}|^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial B_{1}}\left(|\nabla u_{r}|^{2}-2|\nabla u_{r}\cdot x|^{2}\right)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2}{n}\int_{\partial B_{1}}u_{r}\nabla u_{r}\cdot xd\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\frac{1}{n}Q_{r}(0)^{2}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\{u_{r}>0\}\cap\partial B_{1})

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla u_{r}|^{2}dx-Q_{r}(0)^{2}|\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}|

(5.4)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial B_{1}}|\nabla u_{r}\cdot x-u_{r}|^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\sum_{i=1}^{6}\text{Error}_{i}.

Here we have used: in the first step, the fact that
u_{r}\equiv z_{r}
on

\partial B_{1}
; in the second step, explicit expressions for integrals involving

z_{r}
by integration in polar coordinates (see
[
33
, (9.5)]
); in the
third step, we add and subtract

\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial B_{1}}|\nabla u_{r}\cdot x-u_{r}|^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
. In the last step, we have
introduced the terms in (
5.3
) (divided by
n
) which
leaves us with the following error terms:

\displaystyle\text{Error}_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\left(Q_{r}(0)^{2}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\{u_{r}>0\}\cap\partial B_{1})-\int_{\partial B_{1}}Q_{r}^{2}\chi_{\{u_{r}>0\}}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\right),

\displaystyle\text{Error}_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\left(Q_{r}(0)^{2}|\{u_{r}>0\}\cap B_{1}|-\int_{B_{1}}Q_{r}^{2}\chi_{\{u_{r}>0\}}dx\right),

\displaystyle\text{Error}_{3}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\int_{B_{1}}\nabla Q_{r}^{2}\cdot x\chi_{\{u_{r}>0\}}dx,\hskip 30.00005pt\text{Error}_{4}\coloneqq-\frac{2}{n}\int_{B_{1}}u_{r}\nabla f_{r}\cdot xdx,

\displaystyle\text{Error}_{5}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\frac{2(n+1)}{n}\int_{B_{1}}u_{r}f_{r}dx,\hskip 39.25008pt\text{Error}_{6}\coloneqq\frac{2}{n}\int_{\partial B_{1}}u_{r}f_{r}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

For the first two error terms we have

\displaystyle|\text{Error}_{1}|,\,|\text{Error}_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq C_{n}\|Q_{r}(0)+Q_{r}\|_{\infty}\|Q_{r}(0)-Q_{r}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{n}\|Q\|_{\infty}\|\nabla Q\|_{\infty}r

\displaystyle=O(r).

For the third term we use that

\nabla Q_{r}^{2}(x)=2Q_{r}(x)\nabla Q_{r}(x)=2rQ_{r}(x)\nabla Q(rx)
to obtain

|\text{Error}_{3}|\leq C_{n}r\int_{B_{1}}|Q_{r}(x)\nabla Q(rx)|d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq C_{n}\|Q\|_{\infty}\|\nabla Q\|_{\infty}r=O(r).

For the fourth term we use the boundedness of
u
to obtain

|\text{Error}_{4}|\leq C_{n}\|u\|_{\infty}\|\nabla f\|_{\infty}r=O(r).

For the last two error terms we have

\displaystyle|\text{Error}_{5}|

\displaystyle\leq C_{n}\|f\|_{\infty}\int_{B_{1}}u(rx)dx=O(r),

\displaystyle|\text{Error}_{6}|

\displaystyle\leq C_{n}\|f\|_{\infty}\int_{\partial B_{1}}u(rx)dx=O(r),

since
u
is Lipschitz in
B_{r_{0}}
. Inserting this estimates in
(
5.2
) yields

W_{Q}(z_{r})-W_{Q}(u_{r})\geq\frac{1}{n}\int_{\partial B_{1}}|\nabla u_{r}\cdot x-u_{r}|^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-Cr

as claimed.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
u
be a weak solution in
B_{1}
and let

x_{0}\in\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}
be any free boundary point. For any blow-up sequence

u_{r_{k}}
of
u
at
x_{0}
, the blow-up limit
u_{0}
(see Lemma
2.8
)
is a 1-homogeneous function.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.3
, for all
0<r<r_{0}
with

r_{0}\coloneqq\dist(x_{0},\partial\Omega)
we have

(5.5)

\frac{\partial}{\partial r}W_{Q}(u_{r})\geq\frac{2}{r}\int_{\partial B_{1}}|\nabla u_{r}\cdot x-u_{r}|^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-C\geq-C.

Since
u
is Lipschitz in
B_{r_{0}}(x_{0})
, we also have
|u_{r}(x)|\leq L|x|
for
all
0<r<r_{0}
and therefore

W_{Q}(u_{r})\geq-\int_{\partial B_{1}}u_{r}^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\geq-L^{2}.

Hence,
r\mapsto W_{Q}(u_{r})
is a continuous and a.e. differentiable function in
the interval
(0,r_{0})
which is bounded below and whose derivative is also
bounded below in
(0,r_{0})
. It follows that the limit

(5.6)

l=\lim_{r\to 0}W_{Q}(u_{r})

exists and is finite.

Fix
0<S<R<\infty
. Integrating (
5.5
) from

Sr_{k}
to
Rr_{k}
gives us

W_{Q}(u_{Rr_{k}})-W_{Q}(u_{Sr_{k}})=\int_{Sr_{k}}^{Rr_{k}}\frac{2}{r}\int_{\partial B_{1}}|\nabla u_{r}\cdot x-u_{r}|^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}dr-C(Rr_{k}-Sr_{k}).

We can rewrite the integral term as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{Sr_{k}}^{Rr_{k}}\frac{2}{r}\int_{\partial B_{1}}|\nabla u_{r}\cdot x-u_{r}|^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}dr

\displaystyle=\int_{S}^{R}\frac{2}{r}\int_{\partial B_{1}}|\nabla u_{r_{k}r}\cdot x-u_{r_{k}r}|^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}dr

\displaystyle=\int_{S}^{R}\frac{2}{r^{n}}\int_{\partial B_{r}}\left|\nabla u_{r_{k}}\cdot\frac{x}{r}-\frac{u_{r_{k}}}{r}\right|^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}dr

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}\setminus B_{S}}2|x|^{-n-2}|\nabla u_{r_{k}}\cdot x-u_{r_{k}}|^{2}dx.

Inserting this back into the equation yields

W_{Q}(u_{Rr_{k}})-W_{Q}(u_{Sr_{k}})=\int_{B_{R}\setminus B_{S}}2|x|^{-n-2}|\nabla u_{r_{k}}\cdot x-u_{r_{k}}|^{2}dx-C(Rr_{k}-Sr_{k}).

Thus, letting
r_{k}\to 0
and using the fact that
u_{r_{k}}\to u_{0}
in

H^{1}(B_{R})
we find that

\int_{B_{R}\setminus B_{S}}2|x|^{-n-2}|\nabla u_{0}\cdot x-u_{0}|^{2}dx=0.

It follows that
\nabla u_{0}\cdot x-u_{0}=0
a.e. in

B_{R}\setminus B_{S}
. Since this holds for any
0<S<R<\infty
, we conclude that

\nabla u_{0}\cdot x-u_{0}=0
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and therefore
u_{0}
is
1-homogeneous as claimed.
∎

6.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

At this point we have all the main ingredients for the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
As already explained in the introduction, the last part of the proof can be carried
out either by classifying the blow-up limits at free boundary points, or by
leveraging the known regularity of free boundaries of viscosity solutions. However,
before we start with this last step we prove an auxiliary result concerning the
definition of weak solution in the case of Lipschitz domains.

Generally, one would expect a weak notion of solution to only ask for condition

item 3
in Definition
2.1
. Without any additional assumptions, none
of the three conditions in Definition
2.1
can be used to deduce the other.
Nevertheless, in the case of Lipschitz domains we can prove that
item 3
is
a sufficient condition to be a weak solution.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain such that

0\in\partial\Omega
. Assume
u\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(B_{1})
is a non-negative function such
that
u=0
in
B_{1}\setminus\Omega
and

(6.1)

-\int_{B_{1}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta=\int_{\partial\Omega\cap B_{1}}Q\eta d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{\Omega\cap B_{1}}f\eta,\qquad\forall\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(B_{1}).

Then
u
is a weak solution in
B_{1}
in the sense of Definition
2.1
.
Moreover,
\{u>0\}=\Omega\cap B_{1}
.

Proof.

Taking
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(\Omega\cap B_{1})
in (
6.1
) we see that

\int_{\Omega\cap B_{1}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta=\int_{\Omega\cap B_{1}}f\eta,\qquad\forall\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(\Omega\cap B_{1}).

Hence,
u
solves
-\Delta u=f
in
\Omega\cap B_{1}
in the weak sense. By the
smoothness of
f
and the fact that
\Omega
is Lipschitz we deduce that

u\in C(\overline{\Omega\cap B_{1}})\cap C^{\infty}(\Omega\cap B_{1})
. In particular,

u
solves
-\Delta u=f
in the classical sense in
\Omega\cap B_{1}
. Also, since

u=0
on
\partial\Omega\cap B_{1}
, we infer that
u\in C(B_{1})
. Now, the fact that

-\Delta u=f\geq 0
in
\Omega\cap B_{1}
implies that
u
is superharmonic in

\Omega\cap B_{1}
, and by the non-negativity of
u
we conclude that
u>0
in

\Omega\cap B_{1}
.

We have just proved that
\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}=\Omega\cap B_{1}
and therefore

\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}=\partial\Omega\cap B_{1}
as well. Thus, we can rewrite
(
6.1
) as

(6.2)

-\int_{B_{1}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta=\int_{\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}}Q\eta d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{\{u>0\}}f\eta,\qquad\forall\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(B_{1}).

Since
\Omega
is Lipschitz,
\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\Omega=\partial\Omega

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-a.e. and the equation above implies that
u
satisfies

item 3
. Also,
u\in C(B_{1})
,
u\geq 0
and
-\Delta u=f
in

\Omega\cap B_{1}=\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}
. In other words,
u
also fulfills condition

item 1
.

It remains to check that
u
satisfies
item 2
. Let

D\subset\subset B_{1}
be an open set and choose
B_{r}(x)\subset D
with

x\in\partial\{u>0\}
. By density, (
6.2
) holds for

\eta\in H^{1}_{0}(B_{1})
as well. Define

\zeta(y)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2n}(r^{2}-|y-x|^{2})\in H^{1}_{0}(B_{1}).

Then

\displaystyle-\int_{B_{r}(x)}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\zeta

\displaystyle=-\int_{\partial B_{r}(x)}u\nabla\zeta\cdot\nu d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\int_{B_{r}(x)}u\Delta\zeta

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{n}\int_{\partial B_{r}(x)}ud\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{B_{r}(x)}u,

and by (
6.2
)

(6.3)

\displaystyle\fint_{\partial B_{r}(x)}ud\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

\displaystyle=\fint_{B_{r}(x)}u-\frac{1}{|B_{r}|}\int_{B_{r}(x)}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\zeta

\displaystyle=\fint_{B_{r}(x)}u+\frac{1}{|B_{r}|}\int_{\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{r}(x)}Q\zeta d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\frac{1}{|B_{r}|}\int_{\{u>0\}\cap B_{r}(x)}f\zeta.

Observe that since
\partial\{u>0\}=\partial\Omega\cap B_{1}
is Lipschitz,

(6.4)

cr^{n-1}\leq\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{r}(x))\leq Cr^{n-1}

for some constants
c,C>0
depending on
D
. Thus,
(
6.3
) yields

\displaystyle\fint_{\partial B_{r}(x)}ud\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{Q_{\mathrm{min}}}{|B_{r}|}\int_{\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{r/2}(x)}\zeta d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\frac{\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}}{|B_{r}|}\int_{\{u>0\}\cap B_{r}(x)}\zeta

\displaystyle\geq c_{n}Q_{\mathrm{min}}\frac{r^{2}}{|B_{r}|}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{r/2}(x))-c_{n}\|f\|_{\infty}r^{2}

\displaystyle\geq cr-c_{n}\|f\|_{\infty}r^{2}.

It follows that if
r
is smaller than some constant
c_{0}
depending on
n
,

Q_{\mathrm{min}}
and
\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}
, then

\fint_{\partial B_{r}(x)}ud\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\geq cr.

Hence, for
r\leq c_{0}
the lower bound in
item 2
holds. Given that for

r\geq c_{0}
,

\frac{1}{r}\fint_{\partial B_{r}(x)}ud\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\geq\left(\frac{c_{0}}{r}\right)^{n+1}\frac{1}{c_{0}}\fint_{\partial B_{c_{0}}(x)}ud\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\geq\left(\frac{c_{0}}{\diam(D)}\right)^{n+1}c,

we conclude that
u
satisfies the lower bound in
item 2
for any ball

B_{r}(x)\subset D
.

Lastly, let us prove the upper bound of condition
item 2
. First, since

\{u>0\}=\Omega\cap B_{1}
and
\Omega
is a Lipschitz domain, by Schauder estimates

u\in C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\{u>0\}})
for some small
0<\alpha<1
. Therefore

u\in C^{0,\alpha}(B_{1})
. Now define

\phi(r)\coloneqq\frac{1}{r^{\alpha}}\fint_{\partial B_{r}(x)}ud\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

By the Hölder continuity of
u
we know that
\phi
is bounded. Moreover, we can
rewrite (
6.3
) in terms of
\phi
as

\displaystyle\phi(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{r^{n+\sigma}}\int_{0}^{r}s^{n+\alpha-1}\phi(s)ds+\frac{1}{r^{\alpha}|B_{r}|}\int_{\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{r}(x)}Q\zeta d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\frac{1}{r^{\alpha}|B_{r}|}\int_{\{u>0\}\cap B_{r}(x)}f\zeta

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{n+\alpha}\sup_{(0,r)}\phi+C_{n}Q_{\mathrm{max}}\frac{r^{2}}{r^{\alpha}|B_{r}|}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{r}(x))+C_{n}\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}\frac{r^{2}}{r^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{n+\alpha}\sup_{(0,r)}\phi+Cr^{1-\alpha},

where we have used (
6.4
) to bound the second term, and the
fact that
r\leq\diam(D)
to estimate the last term. Consequently,

\left(1-\frac{n}{n+\alpha}\right)\sup_{(0,r)}\phi\leq Cr^{1-\alpha},

or equivalently,

\sup_{(0,r)}\phi\leq C\frac{n+\alpha}{\alpha}r^{1-\alpha}.

Hence,

\fint_{\partial B_{r}(x)}ud\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq Cr

and
u
fulfills condition
item 2
.
∎

6.1.
Lipschitz cones

The aim of this section is to prove Theorem
1.2
by means of classifying the
blow-up limits at free boundary points of a weak solution with Lipschitz free
boundary. Observe that by Corollary
4.2
such blow-ups are weak solutions of
the classical one-phase problem with constant boundary condition. Therefore,
throughout this subsection we will work in the particular case
f\equiv 0
,

Q\equiv\text{const}
.

Remark 6.2
.

By
[
1
, Remark 4.2]
, if a function
u
satisfies condition

item 1
of Definition
2.1
with
f\equiv 0
, then its Laplacian is
non-negative in the distributional sense. Thus,
\lambda=\Delta u
is a (positive) Radon
measure such that

-\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi dx=\int_{\Omega}\varphi d\lambda,\quad\forall\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(\Omega).

Moreover, by
[
1
, Theorem 4.3]
the following two conditions are
equivalent:

•

For any open set
D\subset\subset B_{1}
there exist constants

0<c_{D}\leq C_{D}<\infty
such that for any ball
B_{r}(x)\subset D
with

x\in\partial\{u>0\}
we have

c_{D}\leq\frac{1}{r}\fint_{\partial B_{r}(x)}ud\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq C_{D}.

•

For any open set
D\subset\subset B_{1}
there exist constants

0<c_{D}\leq C_{D}<\infty
such that for any ball
B_{r}(x)\subset D
with

x\in\partial\{u>0\}
we have

c_{D}r^{n-1}\leq\int_{B_{r}(x)}d\lambda\leq C_{D}r^{n-1}.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
Q_{0}>0
be a positive constant and let
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a function which
is constant in the
e_{n}
direction, that is,

u(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}).

Then
u
is a weak solution in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
f\equiv 0
and
Q\equiv Q_{0}
if and only
if
v
is a weak solution in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
for
f\equiv 0
and
Q\equiv Q_{0}
.

Proof.

We use the notation
x=(x^{\prime},x_{n})
for points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
B_{r}^{\prime}
for balls
in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. In this notation we have that
u(x)=v(x^{\prime})
and

\nabla u(x)=(\nabla v(x^{\prime}),0)
. Since
u(x)=v(x^{\prime})
, it is immediate to see that
condition
item 1
holds for
u
if and only if it holds for
v
.

Let us now check condition
item 2
. If
u
is a weak solution, then both

u
and
v
satisfy
item 1
, and by Remark
6.2
it is enough to
prove that
v
satisfies the following: for every bounded open set

D^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
and every ball
B_{r}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\subset D^{\prime}
with
x^{\prime}\in\partial\{v>0\}
,

c_{D^{\prime}}r^{n-2}\leq\int_{B_{r}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})}d\lambda^{\prime}\leq C_{D^{\prime}}r^{n-2},

where
\lambda^{\prime}
is the Radon measure given by the distributional Laplacian of

v
. Observe that for any
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

(6.5)

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)dx&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{i}v(x^{\prime})\partial_{i}\varphi(x)dx\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\partial_{i}v(x^{\prime})\int_{\mathbb{R}}\partial_{i}\varphi(x)dx_{n}dx^{\prime}\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\partial_{i}v(x^{\prime})\partial_{i}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x^{\prime},x_{n})dx_{n}\right)dx^{\prime}\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\nabla v(x^{\prime})\cdot\nabla\psi(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}\end{split}

where
\psi(x^{\prime})\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x^{\prime},x_{n})dx_{n}
. Therefore, since

\psi\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi d\lambda=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi dx=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\nabla v\cdot\nabla\psi dx^{\prime}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\psi d\lambda^{\prime}

and we see that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi d\lambda=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\psi d\lambda^{\prime}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x^{\prime},x_{n})dx_{n}d\lambda^{\prime}(x^{\prime})

for every
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Now, given any ball

B_{r}(x)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
we can take
\varphi
such that

\chi_{B_{r}(x)}\leq\varphi\leq\chi_{B_{2r}(x)}
and obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}(x)}d\lambda

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi d\lambda=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi dy_{n}d\lambda^{\prime}(y^{\prime})\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi_{B_{2r}(x)}dy_{n}d\lambda^{\prime}(y^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\int_{x_{n}-2r}^{x_{n}+2r}\chi_{B_{2r}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})}dy_{n}d\lambda^{\prime}(y^{\prime})=4r\int_{B_{2r}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})}d\lambda^{\prime}.

Similarly, taking
\varphi
such that

we get

r\int_{B_{r/2}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})}d\lambda^{\prime}\leq\int_{B_{r}(x)}d\lambda.

Combining the two estimates we see that for any ball
B_{r}(x)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(6.6)

r\int_{B_{r/2}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})}d\lambda^{\prime}\leq\int_{B_{r}(x)}d\lambda\leq 4r\int_{B_{2r}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})}d\lambda^{\prime}.

Let
D^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
be a bounded open set and let

be a
bounded open set large enough so that

and

B_{2r}((x^{\prime},0))\subset D
for all

. Since

is a weak
solution by hypothesis, there exist constants

such that

(6.7)

c_{D}r^{n-1}\leq\int_{B_{r}(x)}d\lambda\leq C_{D}r^{n-1},

for any
B_{r}(x)\subset D
with
x\in\partial\{u>0\}
. Hence, combining (

) and

) we conclude that

for any

with

, that is,

satisfies
condition
item 2
. Conversely, if

is a weak solution, then once

and

. Therefore we can repeat the

)

.

Lastly, let us check condition

. Assume

Then

and therefore

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta(x_{n})\nabla v(x^{\prime})\cdot\nabla\psi(x^{\prime})dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\nabla v(x^{\prime})\cdot\nabla\psi(x^{\prime})\int_{\mathbb{R}}\eta(x_{n})dx_{n}dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\nabla v(x^{\prime})\cdot\nabla\psi(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}.

Since

\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\}\cap\{x_{n}=t\}=\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{v>0\}\times\{t\}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we also have

\displaystyle\int_{\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\}}\varphi(x)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\}\cap\{x_{n}=t\}}\psi(x^{\prime})\eta(t)d\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(x^{\prime})dt

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\eta(t)\int_{\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{v>0\}}\psi(x^{\prime})d\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(x^{\prime})dt

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{v>0\}}\psi(x^{\prime})d\mathcal{H}^{n-2}.

Thus, using that
u
is a weak solution we conclude that

-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\nabla v\cdot\nabla\psi dx^{\prime}=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi dx=Q_{0}\int_{\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\}}\varphi d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=Q_{0}\int_{\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{v>0\}}\psi d\mathcal{H}^{n-2}.

For the converse, assume
v
is a weak solution in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. By
(
6.5
), for any function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\nabla v(x^{\prime})\cdot\nabla\psi(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime},

where
\psi=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi dx_{n}
. On the other hand,

\displaystyle\int_{\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\}}\varphi(x)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\}\cap\{x_{n}=t\}}\varphi(x^{\prime},t)d\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(x^{\prime})dt

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{v>0\}}\varphi(x^{\prime},t)d\mathcal{H}^{n-2}(x^{\prime})dt

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{v>0\}}\psi(x^{\prime})d\mathcal{H}^{n-2},

and therefore

-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi dx=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\nabla v\cdot\nabla\psi dx^{\prime}=Q_{0}\int_{\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{v>0\}}\psi d\mathcal{H}^{n-2}=Q_{0}\int_{\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\}}\varphi d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

∎

Definition 6.4
.

We say that
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a Lipschitz cone if
u
is a 1-homogeneous
weak solution with Lipschitz free boundary,
f\equiv 0
and

Q\equiv Q_{0}
for some constant
Q_{0}>0
. A Lipschitz cone is said to be
trivial if it’s free boundary is a hyperplane, that is, there exists a unit
vector
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
u(x)=Q_{0}(x\cdot\nu)_{+}
.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
u
be a Lipschitz cone in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

x_{0}=e_{n}\in\partial\{u>0\}
. Then, any blow-up sequence

u_{r}(x)=\frac{1}{r}u(x_{0}+rx)

has a subsequence
u_{r_{k}}
,
r_{k}\rightarrow 0
which converges locally uniformly
to

u_{0}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}),

with
v
a Lipschitz cone in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. Moreover, if
x_{0}
is a
singular point for
\partial\{u>0\}
, then
v
is a non-trivial cone.

Proof.

We follow closely the proof of
[
12
, Lemma 5.4]
. Let
Q_{0}>0
be the
boundary condition of
u
. By Corollary
4.2
and Proposition
5.4
,
we can find a subsequence
u_{r_{k}}
with
r_{k}\rightarrow 0
converging locally uniformly
to a 1-homogeneous weak solution
u_{0}
with the same constant boundary data

Q_{0}
. Moreover, for any fixed
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we can take
r
small enough so that

1+tr\geq 0
. Therefore, using that
u
is 1-homogeneous we see that

\displaystyle u_{r}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r(1+tr)}u((1+tr)(x_{0}+rx))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+tr}u_{r}(tx_{0}+(1+tr)x),

and letting
r=r_{k}\rightarrow 0
we obtain that

u_{0}(x)=u_{0}(tx_{0}+x),\quad\text{for all }t\in\mathbb{R}.

Thus,
u_{0}
is constant in the
x_{0}=e_{n}
direction. By Proposition

6.3
it follows that
v(x^{\prime})=u_{0}(x^{\prime},x_{n})
is a weak weak solution
in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
with
f\equiv 0
,
Q\equiv Q_{0}
and Lipschitz free boundary.
Moreover,
v
is 1-homogeneous since

v(\lambda x^{\prime})=u_{0}(\lambda x^{\prime},x_{n+1})=u_{0}(\lambda x^{\prime},\lambda x_{n+1})=\lambda u_{0}(x^{\prime},x_{n+1})=\lambda v(x^{\prime})

for any
\lambda>0
. Hence,
v
is a Lipschitz cone in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.

For the final statement, assume
v
is a trivial cone, that is,

v(x^{\prime})=Q_{0}(x^{\prime}\cdot\nu^{\prime})_{+}
for some unit vector
\nu^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. Then

u_{0}(x)=v(x^{\prime})=Q_{0}(x^{\prime}\cdot\nu^{\prime})_{+}=Q_{0}(x\cdot\nu)_{+}

where
\nu\coloneqq(\nu^{\prime},0)
. Thus,
u_{0}
is a trivial cone in

\mathbb{R}^{n}
and therefore
x_{0}
is a regular point.
∎

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
v\geq 0
be a non-negative function defined in
B_{1}
such that

v
is harmonic in
\{v>0\}
,
0\in\partial\{v>0\}\cap B_{1}
and

\partial\{v>0\}\cap B_{1}
is a Lipschitz graph in the
e_{n}
direction with
Lipschitz constant
L
. Then there exists
\delta=\delta(n,L)>0
such that

v
is monotone in the
e_{n}
direction in
B_{\delta}
.

Proof.

See
[
9
, Lemma 11.12]
.
∎

Proposition 6.7
.

All Lipschitz cones are trivial.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the dimension
n
. Assume
n=2
and let

u:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a Lipschitz cone. Since
\partial\{u>0\}
is Lipschitz,

u
satisfies the density estimate (
4.1
) and we can apply

[
35
, Theorem 5.1]
to obtain that

\partial\{u>0\}=\partial_{\mathrm{red}}\{u>0\}
. Hence,
[
1
, Theorem 8.2]
and

[
10
, Corollary 1.6]
imply that
\partial\{u>0\}
is a

C^{\infty}
-curve locally in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and by the 1-homogeneity of
u
we conclude

u
is trivial.

Assume now that the statement holds for dimension
n-1
and let
u
be a
Lipschitz cone in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with boundary condition
Q_{0}
. Since all Lipschitz
cones in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
are trivial by induction hypothesis, Lemma
6.5

implies that
\partial\{u>0\}\setminus\{0\}
consists only of regular points. Thus,

\partial\{u>0\}
is smooth outside the origin by Corollary
3.10
. Moreover,
without loss of generality we may assume that
\partial\{u>0\}
is a Lipschitz graph
in the
e_{n}
direction in
B_{1}
with Lipschitz constant
L
. Hence, given any
direction
\sigma
of the open cone

\mathcal{C}\coloneqq\{\xi=(\xi^{\prime},\xi_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\xi_{n}>L|\xi^{\prime}|\},

there exists
\delta(\sigma)
such that
\partial_{\sigma}u\geq 0
in

B_{\delta(\sigma)}
by Lemma
6.6
. Since
u
is 1-homogeneous,

\partial_{\sigma}u
is 0-homogeneous and therefore
\partial_{\sigma}u\geq 0
in

\{u>0\}
for any
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}
. Moreover, by continuity of the derivatives of

u
in
\{u>0\}
, the same conclusion must hold for

\sigma\in\partial\mathcal{C}
. Thus, there exists a direction

\tau\in\partial\mathcal{C}
,
|\tau|=1
such that
\tau
is tangent to

\partial\{u>0\}
at some point
x_{0}\in\partial\{u>0\}\setminus\{0\}
and

\partial_{\tau}u\geq 0\quad\text{in }\{u>0\}.

If
\partial_{\tau}u=0
at some point in
\{u>0\}
, then

\partial_{\tau}u\equiv 0
by the maximum principle. Hence,
u
is constant in the

\tau
direction and by Proposition
6.3
we can reduce the problem to

n-1
dimensions. Then by induction hypothesis
u
is trivial and we
are done.

Otherwise
\partial_{\tau}u>0
in
\{u>0\}
. Since
x_{0}\neq 0
, the free boundary is
smooth in a neighborhood
B_{\rho}(x_{0})
of
x_{0}
. Thus, Remark
2.5

implies that
u
is a classical solution in
B_{\rho}(x_{0})
and

u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\{u>0\}}\cap B_{\rho}(x_{0}))
. In particular, if

\nu
denotes the inward normal vector to
\partial\{u>0\}
, then

\partial_{\nu}u=Q_{0}
on
\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{\rho}(x_{0})
. Therefore, the fact that

\tau
is tangent to
\partial\{u>0\}
at
x_{0}
yields

\partial_{\nu}\partial_{\tau}u(x_{0})=0.

On the other hand,
\partial_{\tau}u
is a positive harmonic function in

\{u>0\}\cap B_{\rho}(x_{0})
(also smooth up to the boundary) and

\partial_{\tau}u\geq 0
on
\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{\rho}(x_{0})
by continuity of

\partial_{\tau}u
. Since
\tau\in\partial\mathcal{C}
is tangent to

\partial\{u>0\}
at
x_{0}
, we also have that
\partial_{\tau}u(x_{0})=0
. Thus, we reach a
contradiction because the Hopf lemma implies that

\partial_{\nu}\partial_{\tau}u(x_{0})>0.

∎

With Proposition
6.7
we are now able to give the first proof of Theorem

1.2
.

First proof of Theorem
1.2
.

By Lemma
6.1

u
is a weak solution in
B_{1}
in the sense of Definition

2.1
and its free boundary

is Lipschitz. Let

be a free boundary point. By Corollary

and

, for any blow–up sequence

there exists a 1-homogeneous weak solution
u_{0}
with

and

6.2.
Proof using viscosity solutions

In general, a weak solution
u
need not be a viscosity solution. However, if the
density of
\{u=0\}
at the free boundary is positive, then we show that
u
is
a viscosity solution. This allows us to prove the improved regularity of Lipschitz
free boundaries using the known regularity results for viscosity solutions.

Definition 6.8
.

A non-negative function
u\in C(B_{1})
is a viscosity solution of
(
2.1
) if

(1)

-\Delta u=f
in
\{u>0\}
in the viscosity sense.

(2)

u
satisfies the boundary condition
|\nabla u|=\partial_{\nu}u=Q
in the
following sense: for any point
x_{0}\in\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}
and any smooth function

\varphi\in C^{\infty}(B_{1})
,

•

If
\varphi
touches
u
from below at
x_{0}
, then

|\nabla\varphi(x_{0})|\leq Q(x_{0})
.

•

If
\varphi_{+}
touches
u
from above at
x_{0}
, then

|\nabla\varphi(x_{0})|\geq Q(x_{0})
.

Proposition 6.9
.

Let
u
be a weak solution of (
2.1
) in

B_{1}
such that

(6.8)

\liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{|\{u=0\}\cap B_{r}(x)|}{|B_{r}|}>0

for every point
x\in\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}
. Then
u
is a viscosity solution in

B_{1}
.

Proof.

We follow the proof of
[
33
, Proposition 9.18]
. We need to check that

u
satisfies the boundary condition
\partial_{\nu}u=Q
in the viscosity sense. To
this end, let
x_{0}\in\partial\{u>0\}\cap B_{1}
and let
\varphi
be a smooth function
touching
u
from below at
x_{0}
. Consider the blow-up sequences

u_{r_{k}}(x)=\frac{1}{r_{n}}u(x_{0}+r_{k}x),\qquad\varphi_{r_{k}}(x)=\frac{1}{r_{k}}\varphi(x_{0}+r_{k}x).

By (
6.8
) we can apply Proposition
4.1
to obtain the
existence a weak solution
u_{0}\in C^{0,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
f\equiv 0
and

Q\equiv Q(x_{0})
such that
u_{r_{k}}\to u_{0}
locally uniformly in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover,

u_{0}
is harmonic in
\{u_{0}>0\}
and by Proposition
5.4
it is
1-homogeneous.

Notice that by the smoothness of
\varphi
we have that

\varphi_{r_{k}}\to\varphi_{0}
locally uniformly in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with

\varphi_{0}(x)=\nabla\varphi(x_{0})\cdot x
. Without loss of generality we may assume that

\nabla\varphi(x_{0})=ae_{n}
so that

\varphi_{0}(x)=ax_{n}.

Moreover, we can assume that
a>0
since otherwise the inequality

|\nabla\varphi(x_{0})|\leq Q(x_{0})
holds trivially.

Given the fact that
u\geq\varphi
near
x_{0}
, we see that

u_{0}\geq\varphi_{0}
and therefore
u_{0}>0
on the set
\{x_{n}>0\}
. Thus,

u_{0}
is a 1-homogeneous harmonic function on
\{u_{0}>0\}\supset\{x_{n}>0\}
and
we deduce (see
[
33
, Lemma 9.16]
) that

\text{either}\quad u_{0}(x)=\alpha x_{n}^{+}\quad\text{or}\quad u_{0}(x)=\alpha x_{n}^{+}+\beta x_{n}^{-}.

The second case can be discarded since it contradicts
(
6.8
). Hence,

u_{0}(x)=\alpha x_{n}^{+}

and Remark
2.5
implies that
\alpha=Q(x_{0})
. Now, the inequality

u_{0}\geq\varphi_{0}
implies that

|\nabla\varphi(x_{0})|=a\leq Q(x_{0}).

Suppose now that
\varphi
touches
u
from above at
x_{0}
. We repeat the same
blow-up procedure and assume
\varphi_{0}
is of the form
\varphi_{0}(x)=ax_{n}
with

a=|\nabla\varphi(x_{0})|
. Again, since
u\leq\varphi
near
x_{0}
, we obtain that

u_{0}\leq\varphi_{0}
. Now, the non-degeneracy of
u
implies that

u_{0}\not\equiv 0
and therefore
a>0
. Moreover, the inequality

u_{0}\leq\varphi_{0}
also implies
\{u_{0}>0\}\subset\{x_{n}>0\}
. Hence, by the
1-homogeneity of
u_{0}
and
[
33
, Lemma 9.15]
we must have

\{u_{0}>0\}=\{x_{n}>0\}
and

u_{0}(x)=\alpha x_{n}^{+}.

Thus, by Remark
2.5
we get that
\alpha=Q(x_{0})
and the inequality

u_{0}\leq\varphi_{0}
implies

|\nabla\varphi(x_{0})|=a\geq Q(x_{0}).

∎

By Lemma

7.1.
Serrin’s overdetermined problem

Recall that for a bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a function

u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
is said to be a weak solution of Serrin’s problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta u&=1\quad&&\text{in }\Omega\\
u&=0\quad&&\text{on }\partial\Omega\\
\partial_{\nu}u&=c>0\quad&&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

if it satisfies the equation

(7.1)

-\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta=c\int_{\partial\Omega}\eta d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{\Omega}\eta,\qquad\forall\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Without loss of generality we may assume
0\in\partial\Omega
and

\Omega\subset\subset B_{1}
. Note that (
7.1
) implies

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta=\int_{\Omega}\eta\geq 0,\qquad\forall\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(\Omega),\,\eta\geq 0.

Thus,
u
is a weakly superharmonic function in
\Omega
with zero boundary values.
By the maximum principle,
u>0
in
\Omega
. After extending
u
by zero in

B_{1}\setminus\Omega
, we can take
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(B_{1})
in
(
7.1
) to obtain

-\int_{B_{1}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta=c\int_{\partial\Omega\cap B_{1}}\eta d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{\Omega\cap B_{1}}\eta,\qquad\forall\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(B_{1}).

Hence, by Theorem
1.2

\partial\Omega\cap B_{1}
is locally smooth in

B_{1}
, so
\partial\Omega\cap B_{1/2}=\partial\Omega
is smooth. Now, by Schauder estimates

u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
and therefore
u
solves (
1.1
) in
the classical sense. Thus,
[
31
]
implies
\Omega=B_{R}
.
∎

7.2.
Poisson kernel regularity

Recall that if
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded Lipschitz domain and
G_{x}
denotes the
Green function for
\Omega
with pole at
x\in\Omega
, then
G_{x}
is a positive harmonic
function in
\Omega\setminus\{x\}
,

G_{x}\in C(\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{x\})
and
G_{x}=0
on

\partial\Omega
. Moreover, the Poisson kernel
P_{x}=\partial_{\nu}G_{x}
is a positive function
defined
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-a.e. on
\partial\Omega
and satisfies the equation

-\int_{\Omega}\nabla G_{x}\cdot\nabla\eta=-\eta(x)+\int_{\partial\Omega}P_{x}\eta d\mathcal{H}^{n-1},\qquad\forall\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
G_{x}
be the Green function for
\Omega
with pole at
x
. Also, let

y\in\partial\Omega
be any boundary point and choose
r
sufficiently small so that

x\not\in B_{r}(y)
. After a translation and a rescaling we may assume
y=0
and

r=1
. Then, by the discussion above

-\int_{B_{1}}\nabla G_{x}\cdot\nabla\eta=\int_{\partial\Omega\cap B_{1}}P_{x}\eta d\mathcal{H}^{n-1},\qquad\forall\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(B_{1}),

where
P_{x}
is smooth by assumption and positive on
\partial\Omega
. Thus, extending

G_{x}
by zero in
B_{1}\setminus\Omega
we can apply Theorem
1.2
to deduce that

\partial\Omega
is a smooth surface in, say,
B_{1/2}
. Since

y\in\partial\Omega
is arbitrary, we conclude that
\partial\Omega
is a smooth surface.
∎
