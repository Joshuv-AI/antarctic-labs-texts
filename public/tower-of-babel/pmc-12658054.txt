Diel study reveals increased nighttime bacterial activity and its connection to organic compounds in the ocean
Authors: Shuting Liu, Krista Longnecker, Kevin Vergin, Luis M Bolaños, Stephen Giovannoni, Keri Opalk, Elisa Halewood, Melissa C Kido Soule, Gretchen J Swarr, Rachel Parsons, Amy E Maas, Hannah Gossner, Leocadio Blanco-Bercial, Fabian Wittmers, Alexandra Z Worden, Elizabeth B Kujawinski, Ruth Curry, Rod Johnson, Craig A Carlson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe linkages between dissolved organic matter (DOM) dynamics and bacterial activity (BAct) are key to regulating carbon fluxes in marine food webs. While transcriptional activities have shown diel patterns, the connections between BAct and DOM on the diel timescale remain unclear. This study explored how bacterioplankton transform DOM over diel cycles in the euphotic and upper twilight zones (to 300 m) of the North Atlantic subtropical gyre. BAct peaked at night in the euphotic zone, following daytime maxima in chlorophyll fluorescence, suggesting a delayed bacterial response to photosynthetic activity. Our data show nighttime BAct exceeded daytime rates 34-47% under dark incubations. Total dissolved amino acids (TDAA) concentrations were lowest at night in the upper twilight waters, indicating nighttime DOM consumption by bacterioplankton. Additionally, we observed that diel vertical migrators could contribute to the oscillation of labile DOM, such as TDAA, although their irregularity requires more detailed studies. BAct shifted DOM composition from fresher to more degraded forms, likely driven by bacterioplankton lineages such as SAR11, Rhodospirillaceae, and SAR202. Our findings show that daytime photosynthesis drives DOM production, while enhanced nighttime heterotrophic BAct facilitates its consumption and transformation, highlighting notable fluctuations in microbial processes and carbon cycling on a diel scale.Subject terms: Microbiology, Ecology

Introduction

Many compounds within the bulk of dissolved organic matter (DOM) are essential energy and carbon sources for the microbial oceanic food web, connecting multiple biological processes1,2. Phytoplankton extracellular release, zooplankton excretion, and viral lysis are significant sources of DOM production, while heterotrophic bacterioplankton uptake is a major DOM sink2. DOM in seawater is composed of a myriad of diverse compounds with varying chemical compositions and reactivities3. Amino acids are some of the most labile, and therefore biochemically “desirable”, components in the DOM pool4. Total dissolved amino acids (TDAA), comprised of dissolved free amino acids (DFAA) and dissolved combined amino acids (DCAA), are commonly measured after proteins, peptides, and other amino acid-containing polymers are subjected to acid hydrolysis5,6. In the oceanic water column, TDAA concentrations range from 0.05 to 4 μmol L−1, accounting for 0.4–7% of dissolved organic carbon (DOC) and 1.4–15% of dissolved organic nitrogen (DON)3,7,8. Microbes preferentially degrade amino acids, which can be used as diagenetic indicators of DOM despite being a small fraction of DOM standing stock9,10. The dissolved metabolome includes small biomolecules (less than ~800 Daltons) that can provide insight into the movement of labile DOM. Labile dissolved metabolites in the DOM pool, such as aldoses, osmolytes, and amino acids, are actively cycled by microbial processes in the ocean11. One example highlighting the dynamism of this process is the production of organosulfur metabolites by phytoplankton that change the regulation of biochemical pathways, enhancing quorum sensing, promoting organic nitrogen uptake and triggering differential metabolite production by heterotrophic bacterioplankton12. Identifying and measuring dissolved metabolites are important steps in unraveling interaction networks transacted by microorganisms via this free market of dissolved organic compounds.

The quantity and quality of DOM often serve as bottom-up drivers that influence bacterioplankton growth, metabolism, and community structure13,14. For instance, freshly produced phytoplankton DOM is typically enriched with nitrogen-containing compounds such as proteins, oligopeptides, and amino acids, meets bacterioplankton nutrition demands and promotes rapid bacterioplankton production over short time scales15–17. While some studies show a weak correlation between bacterioplankton community structure and metabolic function due to functional redundancy18,19, other studies have revealed that different bacterioplankton taxa exploit DOM resources along a continuum of varying quantity, quality and lability20–22.

The metabolic control of autotrophic response to light and coupling between autotrophs and heterotrophs have been studied throughout diel cycles, focusing on photosynthetic rates, primary production, and heterotrophic bacterial activity23,24. While some studies reported diel cycles of bacterial activity tightly coupled to primary production25,26, others revealed that bacterial activity and photosynthesis were out of phase, or exhibited no consistent diel pattern23,27,28. Diel dynamics of DOM biogeochemistry and its linkage with microbial activities remain poorly understood. Here, we targeted stations in the North Atlantic subtropical gyre (NASG; northwestern Sargasso Sea in particular), specifically in the vicinity of the Bermuda Atlantic Time-series Study (BATS) site and Hydrostation S (HS), to investigate the temporal phasing of heterotrophic bacterial activity (BAct, dark 3H-Leucine incorporation), changes in bacterioplankton community structure, and the microbial transformation and fate of DOM. We used TDAA as indices of DOM diagenetic quality, quantified a targeted suite of dissolved metabolites, and analyzed diel-scale changes in BAct, bacterioplankton abundance (BA), and bacterioplankton community structure for two summer research cruises. Our study’s aim was to capture the rapid and consistent microbial responses to DOM flux in the oligotrophic ocean, offering insights into diel DOM-microbe interactions in their natural context.

Results and discussion

To identify whether a variable exhibited a diel pattern, we applied two approaches: (1) fitting observed data with a sine wave model to assess consistent diel oscillation over the sampling period; and (2) conducting cross-correlation function (CCF) analysis to evaluate the strength and timing (lag) between photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) and observed variables. Only variables that met both criteria—sine wave fitting with consistent day-night cycles and significant CCF relationship with PAR—were classified as exhibiting diel patterns. In the following, diel patterns in microbial activity, DOM compounds, and bacterioplankton taxa will be discussed.

Diel changes in microbial activity and biomass

In the NASG, over two (July 2018) and three days (July 2019), there were recurring diel oscillations of chlorophyll fluorescence and BAct measured as 3H-leucine incorporation rate in the sunlit euphotic zone, defined as the zone of the water column extending from surface to the depth where chlorophyll fluorescence was larger than 35% of the maximum chlorophyll fluorescence value (typically around 120 m). There were significant cross-correlations between both depth-integrated chlorophyll fluorescence and BAct and PAR in both years (Fig. 1, p < 0.05). The deep chlorophyll maximum (DCM) developed between ~80–95 m, and a sine wave model (r2 = 0.82–0.90) identified chlorophyll reaching a daytime maximum between 10:00 and 13:00 local time, as observed in other oligotrophic ocean systems29. Inorganic nitrogen and phosphorus within the euphotic zone are typically below detection limits in the stratified summer period, with inorganic nitrogen and phosphorus increasing to up to 2 μmol L−1 and 0.04 μmol L−1, respectively, in the upper twilight zone (from the base of the euphotic zone to 300 m, Supplementary Figs. 1a, b and 2a, b). The nutrient-limited euphotic zone was dominated by Prochlorococcus and Synechococcus, accounting for 24–50% and 4–17% of the phytoplankton carbon, respectively, at BATS30. Among the dominant phytoplankton amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), specifically the top ten ranked by relative abundance, members of Prochlorococcus low-light-adapted ecotype I (LLI), Prochlorococcus LLII, Pelagomonas calceolata, Ostreococcus clade II, and Bathycoccus all showed significant cross-correlations with chlorophyll fluorescence (Supplementary Fig. 3, p < 0.05). Consistent with previous findings that DNA replication and cell division often lag photosynthesis31, we observed that the peak relative abundance of these ASVs occurred approximately 7–10 h (lag = −1) after the fluorescence maxima. In the summer period over the two years, BAct in the euphotic zone ranged from 2.5 to 31.4 pmol L−1 h−1 of leucine incorporation during the daytime and from 9.1 to 41.5 pmol L−1 h−1 at night (Fig. 1). BAct was most pronounced between 40 and 80 m and reached a diel maximum between 21:00 and 01:00 (local time) in the euphotic zone (sine model fitting r2 = 0.97–0.98), lagging chlorophyll fluorescence maximum by 10–15 h each day (Fig. 1). BAct was assessed via 3H-leucine incorporation, a sensitive method that reflects an instantaneous protein synthesis rate. In contrast to BAct, the maximum of BA was observed between 40 and 60 m; however, no significant diel patterns (CCF p > 0.05) were observed for BA in either the euphotic or upper twilight zones (Supplementary Fig. 5). Unbalanced growth between protein synthesis and cell division can lead to an uncoupling between BAct and BA32,33. The mismatch between BAct and BA may also be attributed to the large background of slow-growing or inactive bacterioplankton cells, as well as loss due to bacterivory and viral lysis34. No significant diel patterns were observed for the other measured environmental variables (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).

To emphasize BAct signals in the upper twilight zone and avoid skewing by high BAct values in the euphotic zone, BAct at each time was subtracted from the mean and divided by the standard deviation of BAct across the same depth, yielding a z-score. Plots of BAct mean z-score across the euphotic and upper twilight depths and a fitted sine wave model showed recurring diel variability of BAct peaking at night in the upper twilight zone in July 2018 (Supplementary Fig. 6a). However, in July 2019, unlike the consistent nighttime peak of BAct in the euphotic zone, BAct in the upper twilight zone reached its peak during the daytime (08:00–14:00 local time). This deviation resulted in a non-significant cross-correlation of mean z-score of BAct to PAR and no consistent diel pattern for BAct across euphotic to upper mesopelagic waters in July 2019 (Supplementary Fig. 6b).

The dark 3H-leucine incorporation assay is a widely used protocol for assessing heterotrophic bacterial production33,35,36. However, prior studies have shown that conducting 3H-leucine incubations in the light can increase uptake rates 1.5-fold compared to dark incubations37, indicating that results from dark incubation requires careful interpretation and further evaluation. In our direct comparisons of dark 3H-leucine incubations performed over multiple days, we observed a pronounced diel pattern in dark uptake rates. How this diel trend is affected by incubation under diel variability in light intensity remains an open question and a topic of further investigation. In our study, the observation that BAct reached a maximum rate at night (37% over the daytime rates on average) in the euphotic zone provided key insight into the dynamics of microbial activity in oligotrophic systems. If the pattern is represented more broadly in ocean systems, then traditional bacterial production measurements using 3H-leucine incorporation assay conducted exclusively during the day (as they are often coordinated with primary production sampling), could underestimate heterotrophic bacterial activity (production) by as much as 34–47%. This systematic bias of using data from dark 3H-leucine incubations conducted during the day would lead to the underestimation of the microbial loop’s role in recycling freshly-produced organic matter and mediating the carbon flux between microbes and labile organic matter in the euphotic zone. Diel variability of heterotrophic bacterial activity has been described in previous studies28,38 and has been shown to be greater at night than during daytime at several oligotrophic sites27,37,38. Yet contrasting reports of higher BAct in the day with maximum at noon or late afternoon24,39, or weak and inconsistent diel changes for eutrophic environments23,40, indicate that varying drivers appear to control BAct in differing trophic states.

Factors that may explain the recurring nocturnal increase of BAct observed in our study include a lagged response to photosynthetic release of DOM, a response to grazer-induced DOM production, photoinhibition of BAct, and competition for inorganic nutrients with phytoplankton38. Primary production is a major source of DOM in the marine system2. The nighttime BAct maximum and its temporal lag relative to daytime chlorophyll fluorescence maximum may reflect the time required for the production and release of photosynthate, coupled with the delay associated with bacterial synthesis and activation of hydrolytic and transport proteins required to take up photosynthate41. Similarly, a lag between BAct and primary production has been observed elsewhere26,27. Herbivorous grazing on bacteria, cyanobacteria, or other phytoplankton has been shown to peak at night in regions such as the East China Sea and Mediterranean Sea28,38, leading to the release of fresh DOM through excretion and sloppy feeding, thereby triggering a rapid heterotrophic bacterial response to grazing-derived DOM. A negative correlation between predatory protist lineages and bacterioplankton lineages (Supplementary Table 1) is consistent with the potential bioavailable DOM release via protistan grazing. Inhibitory effects in the day, like solar radiation42 and enhanced competition for limiting nutrients43 between heterotrophic bacteria and phytoplankton, can suppress bacterial activity in the daytime, resulting in higher BAct at night. While these factors may contribute to the diel pattern of BAct in the euphotic zone, diel oscillation of BAct below the euphotic zone where the light was limited and inorganic nutrients were replete (Supplementary Figs.1a, b, 2a, b, and 6) indicates other factors influence the diel pattern in the upper twilight water column.

Bacterial activity correlated with diel variations in concentrations of DOM compounds

Studies examining the diel-scale variability of specific DOM compounds are even more scarce than those focused on microbial activities25,44. Here, we investigated how BAct may both respond to and affect bulk DOM concentrations and specific DOM compounds over diel cycles. While bulk DOC and DON concentrations did not show significant diel variability in July 2018 (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b), both showed diel oscillations in the euphotic zone in July 2019, with a significant cross-correlation with PAR (p < 0.05). DOC and DON concentrations reached maxima during the day when chlorophyll fluorescence was highest and BAct was lowest (Supplementary Fig. 8a–d), suggesting net DOM production by autotrophs during daytime in the euphotic zone followed by net consumption by heterotrophic bacterioplankton in the evening in 2019. It is possible that the diel variability of a small, highly labile fraction of the bulk DOM is obscured by the much larger fraction of surface DOM (>70%), which is composed of recalcitrant material with lower bioavailability2,45. However, examining the temporal dynamics of specific compounds known to be labile can provide useful insight into the diel variability of DOM and its link to BAct.

TDAA is a bioavailable component of DOM and serves as a sensitive indicator of DOM diagenetic status46. Previous studies have shown that the decoupling between the dynamics of bulk DOC and the variability in TDAA concentrations may be due to the rapid turnover rates of TDAA components in the presence of a larger fraction of recalcitrant DOC in natural seawater47. While diel patterns in bulk DOC were only resolved in the euphotic zone in 2019, temporal variability of TDAA carbon concentrations (TDAA-C) in the upper twilight zone displayed diel patterns in both years, as well as in the euphotic water in 2019, with nighttime minima in TDAA-C concentrations (Fig. 2a, b and Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). When pooling data from both years, the depth-integrated (both euphotic and upper twilight) TDAA-C anomaly (calculated as the deviation from each year’s mean divided by the mean) showed significantly lower values at night (Fig. 2c, Wilcoxon test, p < 0.0001). TDAA yield normalized to DOC also showed consistent diel patterns as TDAA-C (Supplementary Figs. 7c and 8e), indicating DOM quality changed between day and night.

Diel variability in DFAA rather than TDAA has been examined in previous studies in both coastal and open ocean waters. Mopper and Lindroth48 and Carlucci et al.49 reported DFAA maxima occurred in the late evening with minima occurring early in the day or late afternoon. In those studies, DFAA dynamics were related to minimal microbial metabolism (incorporation and respiration) of amino acids per cell or exudate release from grazing activities at night. In contrast, Lu et al.50 found no significant difference in DFAA concentrations between day and night. In the Sargasso Sea, DFAA concentrations are <10 nM and account for less than 5% of TDAA (DFAA + DCAA) concentrations51. We expect that DFAA and TDAA would display different temporal patterns. Zhang et al.52 found that DFAA concentrations were higher at night. In contrast, TDAA in the surface seawater tracked diel change of chlorophyll, showing greater average concentrations in the daytime than at night, which was similar to the TDAA pattern observed in our study. In contrast, Shen et al.53 observed TDAA “hotspots” between dusk and dawn and attributed their production to zooplankton grazing release at night in a highly productive coastal margin of the Gulf of Mexico. Hence, there is no single pattern of DFAA and TDAA variability that appears to be ubiquitous across different study regions, as suggested by previous research: our study contributes one example of a diel TDAA pattern among this variation. Differences in daily patterns of TDAA and DFAA may also be related to the timing of hydrolytic enzyme production by bacterioplankton. Bacterioplankton use extracellular hydrolysis and ABC transporters to take up TDAA, and hydrolytic enzyme activities can vary by 34–48% at fine time scales over hours to days and can change up to fourfold over a day54,55.

The TDAA dynamics we observed reflect the net result of competing processes of TDAA production and removal. Extracellular release from phytoplankton is an important source of DOM, and the percentage of extracellular release of net primary production ranges from 5 to 42% in the oligotrophic oceans2,56. Phytoplankton DOM exudates may contain 10–30% TDAA44,57,58, contributing to amino acid accumulation in the euphotic zone during the daytime in 2019 (Fig. 2b). In marine environments, amino acids can support up to 40% of bacterioplankton production and account for 10–20% and 5–50% of bacterioplankton carbon demand and bacterioplankton nitrogen demand, respectively51,59. The imbalance between DOM production and consumption leads to short-term accumulation or dissipation of TDAA. The greatest BAct rates were observed between local times of 21:00–01:00 in the upper twilight in 2018 and euphotic in 2019, and corresponded to the lowest concentration of TDAA-C and TDAA yield. The negative correlation between BAct and TDAA-C during the cruises in both years (Supplementary Fig. 9, sine model fitting r2 = 0.58–0.98) further reinforces the diel pattern in bacterial activity and DOM composition. We interpret the data to indicate that TDAA consumption exceeded its production and rapid bacterioplankton degradation of labile DOM left behind a more diagenetically-altered DOM. Meanwhile, a decrease in BAct during daytime allowed the temporal accumulation of fresher, less diagenetically altered DOM. The abundance of specific copiotrophic bacterioplankton, such as members of the Rhodospirillaceae family, were negatively correlated with the concentration of TDAA-C in the upper twilight zone in 2019 (Supplementary Fig. 10, CCF p < 0.05, see more details in the next section). This suggests that individual bacterioplankton lineages consuming TDAA had an impact on the diel variability of TDAA at depth.

The differences in the TDAA dynamics between the euphotic and upper twilight zones in 2018 may reflect distinct sources of TDAA in these depth layers. For example, surface ocean DCAA are mostly identified as proteins from phytoplankton sources51. On the other hand, mesozooplankton (0.2 to >20 mm) can serve as a significant source of TDAA in both surface and deeper waters, as vertically migrating zooplankton release TDAA and metabolites into the surrounding environment60–62. The data from the Multiple Opening/Closing Net and Environmental Sensing System (MOCNESS) shows that there was generally more zooplankton biovolume in the euphotic zone at night compared to during the day. Conversely, there was more biovolume in the upper twilight zone during the day compared to at night in both years (Fig. 3). Although our study did not directly demonstrate TDAA production by zooplankton, enrichment of taurine (Tau), an amino acid-like metabolite known to be produced by zooplankton61, was observed in the euphotic zone between midnight and dawn in 2018 (Supplementary Fig. 11a, b, sine model fitting r2 = 0.97). This timing coincided with the diel migratory pattern of mesozooplankton at BATS, which graze on daily primary production in the surface water at night and migrate to depth during the day to avoid predation63. The production of labile DOM by diel migrators at the surface at night in July 2018, combined with daytime photosynthetic DOM production, likely obfuscated the diel variability of TDAA in the euphotic zone. However, diel migrators may have contributed to the diel oscillation of TDAA at depth where concentrations were higher during the day. It is important to note that Tau production is not exclusive to zooplankton; it can originate from phytoplankton, invertebrates, and fish64–66. In July 2019, Tau dynamics differed, with concentrations peaking during the day within the euphotic zone rather than at night (Supplementary Fig. 11c, d, since wave model fitting r2 = 0.77). This daytime peak aligns with elevated concentrations of labile TDAA measured in the surface waters and suggests other biological processes, such as phytoplankton-derived TDAA production, were greater than zooplankton TDAA production in the euphotic zone in 2019. Alternatively, interannual differences may reflect zooplankton patchiness and metabolic activity occurring at spatial or temporal scales finer than our sampling resolution67,68. Zooplankton have a wide range of migratory distances in the day, many of which fall in the lower twilight zone (300–700 m)67 can spread out the distribution of Tau, leading to diluted and unresolvable diel pattern in the upper twilight water (Supplementary Fig. 11a, c) where resident and non-migratory zooplankton make up a substantial proportion of the zooplankton biomass69.

Principal component analyses (PCA) and subsequent analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) demonstrate that TDAA composition was significantly different between nighttime and daytime samples in both years (Fig. 4, ANOSIM p < 0.05). At night, TDAA composition shifted towards more negative values in the PC1 axis of the PCA biplot in 2018 and the upper left quadrant of the PCA biplot in 2019. Both years showed common patterns of higher proportions of β-alanine (BAla), glycine (Gly), and alanine (Ala) at night (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Tables 2, 3). BAla is a non-protein amino acid that accumulates with increasing DOM diagenetic alteration9,70; therefore, its higher proportion at night suggests DOM degradation occurred over the diel timescales at this site during a summer stratified period. Gly and Ala are generally more resistant to bacterioplankton degradation than other amino acids and tend to increase in mol% after DOM decomposition10,71,72, further indicating heterotrophic microbial transformation of DOM composition at night. In addition to biological processes, physical variabilities like internal waves, isopycnal uplift, or downwelling may also lead to temporal variability of TDAA concentration and composition at any given depth. Physical data collected by autonomous gliders before, during, and after our sampling period indicated that vertical displacement of isopycnals was limited to 10–24 m due to slow currents and minimal mesoscale interference at the beginning of July 2018 and July 2019 (Supplementary Fig. 12). These observations suggest that physical perturbation was minimal during the study periods. As such, it is unlikely that ephemeral physical perturbations explain the observed diel changes in specific amino acids, particularly under such stable conditions73. Overall, the diel changes of TDAA composition observed in the NASG were most likely related to biological processes and most clearly resolved when the velocity field was minimal.

Targeted dissolved metabolites, including organic acids, vitamins, organosulfur compounds, nucleosides, oligosaccharides, and free amino acids, also revealed diel patterns in the euphotic zone that were significantly cross-correlated with PAR in one or both years (Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 13). Previous studies of diel periodicity have primarily focused on particulate metabolites74–76. In contrast, the present study provides insights into microbial metabolism and interaction over depth at fine temporal resolution from the dissolved metabolites’ perspectives. Benzoic acids 2,3-dihydroxybenzoic acid and 4-hydroxybenzoic acid, and the organosulfur compound glutathione showed higher concentrations in the daytime (Supplementary Fig. 13). The only dissolved metabolite that displayed diel patterns in both years was glutathione. Other dissolved metabolites with diel variability only displayed this pattern in one of the two years. The variability of bacterioplankton community structure and unique interactions among different lineages between the two years help to explain these discrepancies (Supplementary Table 4).

Glutathione is an organosulfur compound. A significant cross-correlation between glutathione and SAR11 ASVs in both years (Supplementary Fig. 14, CCF p < 0.05) suggests glutathione facilitated the growth of SAR11, although delayed response to glutathione and the slow-growing lifestyle of SAR11 may have led to delayed negative correlations between the SAR11 cell abundance (ASV relative abundance times total BA) and glutathione concentration in 2018 or affected the expected negative correlation in 2019. This result is consistent with SAR11 lacking genes to assimilate sulfate and requiring the incorporation of reduced sulfur to fulfill cellular demands77. The elevated daytime concentration of 4-hydroxybenzoic acid likely reflects a decoupling between daytime metabolite production driven by high photosynthetic rates and nighttime heterotrophic BAct maxima. This pattern is consistent with cyanobacteria being a potential source of this metabolite, as has been shown in culture78,79. Concentrations of organosulfur compounds 3-mercaptopropionic acid and 5′-methylthioadenosine (MTA), oligosaccharide chitotriose, vitamins folic acid and riboflavin, and the amino acid tryptophan reached maximum concentrations at night (Supplementary Fig. 13). MTA was positively correlated to picoeukaryotes and nanoeukaryotes in the North Atlantic Ocean80 and may be related to protist growth activity at night (Supplementary Fig. 15). Chitotriose is a hydrolytic byproduct of chitin found in the cell walls of marine organisms such as mesozooplankton, fungi and other crustacean shells81 and may result from enhanced bacterial activity at night. Some vitamins are photosensitive and display diel patterns of production at night, followed by photodegradation during the day82. For instance, high light intensity during the day coincides with low riboflavin concentrations in the euphotic zone, which may be partly attributed to photodegradation, particularly in surface water83. Folic acid from zooplankton excreta62 is at its highest concentration in the surface water at night, corresponding to the vertical migration of zooplankton (Fig. 3).

DOM compounds and other mechanisms linked to diel changes of bacterioplankton taxa

Both bacterioplankton community composition and the activity of specific groups exhibit variability on diel timescales84. In addition to the diel oscillation of photoautotrophic Prochlorococcus abundance in the euphotic zone, multiple bacterioplankton members of the copiotrophs Rhodospirillaceae, Flavobacteriales, and Opitutae (belonging to Verrucomicrobia), and oligotrophs SAR11 clade, SAR86 clade, Acidimicrobiales (belonging to Actinobacteria), SAR116 clade, SAR406 clade, SAR202 clade, SAR324 clade, and Salinisphaeraceae revealed diel patterns in their abundance in the euphotic and/or upper twilight zone with significant cross-correlation with PAR (CCF p < 0.05, sine model fitting r2 = 0.64–0.89, selected examples in Fig. 5, full list in Supplementary Figs. 16, 17, 18, and Supplementary Table 4). Members of Flavobacteriaceae NS4 marine group, Balneola (belonging to Cytophagia), SAR92 clade (belonging to Cellvibrionales), Enterobacteriaceae, and Nitrospina peaked in the day, while Flavobacteriaceae NS5 marine group, Chloroflexi S085, Anaerolineaceae, Defluviicoccus (belonging to Rhodospirillaceae), Roseospira thiosulfatophia, and Thermosipho atlanticus had greater abundance at night (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 18). Some members belonging to SAR11 clade, SAR86 clade, SAR202 clade, SAR116 clade, Puniceicoccaceae (belonging to Verrucomicrobiales), SAR324 clade, Salinisphaeraceae ZD0417 marine group, SAR406 clade, Rhodospirillaceae AEGEAN 169 marine group, Acidimicrobiales Sva0996 marine group, had ASVs that peaked at both times, suggesting different cell growth strategies among ecotypes. Only seven common bacterioplankton ASVs had diel patterns in both years, with the majority of ASVs demonstrating diel periodicity being unique in one year or the other, potentially due to inter-annual variability of DOM substrates, including metabolites (Supplementary Fig. 13). More investigation on finer temporal sampling schemes over a longer time is needed.

Several mechanisms could explain the diel pattern of specific bacterioplankton ASVs, including immediate response to DOM, lagged response to DOM production, protistan grazing, viral lysis, and rhodopsin-supported growth. Diel oscillations in the growth of specific bacterioplankton groups, including Rhodospirillaceae and SAR11 clades, were consistent with diel periodicity of TDAA (Supplementary Fig. 10) or dissolved metabolites such as glutathione (Supplementary Fig. 14), suggesting these groups respond to DOM production. The Flavobacteriaceae clade is frequently associated with the phycosphere surrounding the phytoplankton cell and can respond to phytoplankton blooms85,86. Verrucomicrobiales are enriched in particle-associated communities87,88. Diel changes of Flavobacteriaceae and Verrucomicrobiales abundance are consistent with a rapid response to photosynthate release or particle dissolution.

Other slow-growing ASVs, including members of the SAR11, SAR406, SAR86, SAR202, SAR92 clades, as well as Acidimicrobiales, exhibited lagged response to photosynthetically derived or transformed DOM89–91. Their diel variability may reflect delayed responses to earlier signals with oscillations extending beyond the duration of our sampling window. Members of the SAR11, SAR86, SAR406, and SAR116 clades have been shown to display periodically expressed transcripts related to functions such as oxidative phosphorylation, amino acid metabolism, and translation, indicating intrinsic cellular control over diel cycles75,92–94. Diel production of virus and viral-host interactions could control bacterioplankton taxa in oligotrophic oceans95. A significant negative correlation between bacterioplankton ASVs and heterotrophic and mixotrophic protist ASVs (Supplementary Fig. 15 and Supplementary Table 1), indicating that top-down control by protistan grazing may contribute to the diel oscillation of specific taxa. However, more investigation is needed to assess direct links between these variables.

Alternatively, proteorhodopsins, light-harvesting pigments found in SAR11, SAR86, Actinobacteria, and Flavobacteriaceae clades96–99 may contribute to diel patterns in abundances. For instance, the abundance of proteorhodopsin-containing SAR11 and Flavobacteria genes almost doubled under a dark-light cycle over five days and three days, respectively, but declined under constant darkness99. Higher rhodopsin expression during the day and rhodopsin-triggered cell division may partially explain the higher daytime abundance of those groups in our study92,99,100. However, the effect of proteorhodopsin expression on growth likely varies by bacterioplankton taxa. For example, members of the Flavobacteriaceae NS5 marine group showed no obvious increase in cell abundance production in the day while members of SAR11, SAR86, Acidimicrobiales, and Flavobacteriaceae NS4 marine group do increase (Supplementary Fig. 16). Although the exact mechanism leading to the diel variability of bacterioplankton ASVs in this study needs further investigation, the observations of diel oscillations of bacterioplankton ASV abundance indicate how bacterial activity and DOM substrates intricately control bacterioplankton biomass, consistent with microscale model simulation of bacterioplankton growth and behavior on the diel scale101.

Conclusion

In conclusion, diel oscillations in phytoplankton biomass, BAct, DOM compound concentrations and composition, and abundances of specific bacterioplankton lineages were observed in a relatively quiescent stratified water column in the NASG. The patterns are summarized in the conceptual diagram (Fig. 6), which highlights diel changes in euphotic and upper twilight zones. Daytime photosynthetic DOM production by phytoplankton followed by nighttime heterotrophic bacterioplankton consumption drove pronounced diel periodicity in the measured concentrations of DOC, TDAA, and other dissolved metabolites such as glutathione within the euphotic zone.

Variables appear in the figure at corresponding depths (euphotic vs. upper twilight zones). Variables on the left side indicated peak values in the daytime, while the right side indicated peak values at night. Variables with dashed borders showed a diel pattern in only one of the two years, while others showed a consistent diel pattern in both years of this study. Zooplankton vertical migration likely contributed to TDAA supply at depth during the day in the upper twilight zone and the euphotic zone at night. The temporal decoupling of BAct and bacterioplankton abundance resulted in diel variability in distinct bacterioplankton ASVs, with some peaking in the day and others at night throughout the upper 300 m of the water columns. Notably, BAct consistently reached maximum rates at night in the euphotic zone, exceeding daytime rates by as much as 34–47% when measured as dark-incubated 3H-leucine incorporation rates.

Together with prior studies on diel microbial dynamics, these findings linked diel changes in DOM molecular composition with microbial metabolic responses, thus advancing our understanding of the fine-scale coupling between microbial community structure and DOM biogeochemistry.

Methods

Contextual regional surveys

A spatial survey conducted prior to the cruise helped to identify a quiescent water mass in the northwestern Sargasso Sea with a low horizontal velocity of <0.1–0.2 m sec−1. A Slocum Seaglider was deployed in the vicinity of BATS (31°40’N, 64°10’W) from June 2018 to July 2018 and near HS (32°10’N, 64°13’W) from July 5th, 2019 to July 11th, 2019. The glider acquired measurements from the surface to 500 m or 900 m at intervals of 1–1.5 h and was equipped with a Seabird pumped conductivity, temperature, and depth (CTD) device, Aanderaa optode (model 4831), and Wet Labs ECO chlorophyll fluorometer and backscatter optical sensors. Pre-mission and post-mission calibrations and corrections were applied to the sensors according to established procedures102,103. CTD data were corrected for pressure bias, sensor lag, thermal mass inertia104, spikes, and conductivity offset/drift (by comparison to collocated ship-based CTD casts). The oxygen data were corrected for sensor response time105, pressure, temperature, salinity effects106, and offset (by comparison to ship-based CTD casts). Chlorophyll fluorescence in the daytime was corrected for non-photochemical quenching by using backscatter data and comparing daytime profiles to adjacent nighttime profiles to determine the depth of quenching following established protocols107,108. To produce relative fluorescence units (RFU) and relative backscatter units (RBU), factory-determined scaling factors were applied to both fluorescence and backscatter measurements. Profiles were evaluated and corrected for linear drift within each mission. The mission data were intercalibrated by aligning the profile minima onto common RFU and RBU values, respectively.

Seawater sampling at sea

Onboard the R/V Atlantic Explorer, diel sampling of seawater at stations (31°40’N, 64°10’W in July 2018 and 32°10’N, 64°13’W in July 2019) chosen to reduce the effect of horizontal advection was conducted approximately every 7 h over 2 days in July 2018 and every 7 to 10 h over 3 days in July 2019. Seawater from 7 to 10 depths (1, 20, 40, 60, 80, 95, 120, 160, 200, 250, 300 m) over the surface 300 m was collected from Niskin bottles (Ocean Test Equipment Inc.) mounted on a CTD profiling rosette. Incident PAR data were collected every two seconds from the air throughout the cruises.

Duplicate samples for DOC and total dissolved nitrogen (TDN), and a single sample for TDAA from each depth were filtered in-line through 47 mm GF/F filters packed in polycarbonate filter holders. They were collected into 40 mL precombusted (at 450 °C) glass vials with polytetrafluoroethylene coated silicone septa and then acidified to a pH of approximately 3 with 4 N HCl, as described in Halewood et al. (2022)109. Four liters of seawater were filtered through 0.2 μm Omnipore filters (EMD Millipore) and acidified with HCl (Optima grade) for dissolved metabolite analysis according to Dittmar et al.110 Inorganic nitrogen (nitrate + nitrite) and phosphate single samples were collected unfiltered into 20 mL high-density polyethylene (HDPE) vials and stored at −20 °C. Particulate organic matter (POM) was concentrated onto 25 mm GF/F filters by filtering 2–3 L of seawater. Bacterioplankton abundance (BA, single samples) was sampled by collecting 10–40 mL into sterile tubes (Falcon™), then fixed with 0.2 μm filtered formalin (1% final concentration) and stored at 4 °C to be processed within 48 h or stored at −80 °C for microscopic analyses, as detailed in Porter and Feig (1980)111. Seawater for bacterial activity (BAct) analysis, measured as 3H-leucine incorporation, was collected in 50 mL polycarbonate tubes and incubated in microtubes (1.6 mL) at in situ seawater temperature as described below. Two to three liters of seawater were filtered onto 0.2 μm Sterivex filters (polyethersulfone membrane, Millipore) and stored in sucrose lysis buffer at −80 °C for DNA amplicon sequencing.

Chemical analyses

DOC was analyzed using the high temperature combustion (HTC) method on a modified TOC-V or TOC-L analyzer (Shimadzu) at the University of California, Santa Barbara (UCSB)109. TDN was measured on a TNM-L chemiluminescence analyzer (Shimadzu) after HTC109. The precision of DOC analysis is ~1 μmol L−1 or CV of 2–3%, and the precision of TDN analysis is ~0.5 μmol L−1 or CV of 5–10%. Daily reference water was calibrated with consensus reference material from D. Hansell (University of Miami). Inorganic nutrients (nitrate + nitrite, phosphate) were measured at the Marine Science Institute analytical lab in UCSB through flow injection analysis following established protocols112,113. Since ammonium measurements from frozen samples are not reliable, DON was calculated as TDN minus nitrate + nitrite only. As ammonium concentrations are usually more than one order of magnitude lower than DON concentrations in the top 300 m of the Sargasso Sea114, DON overestimation due to omitting ammonium concentrations is inconsequential at the resolution of TDN at this site.

For amino acids, triplicate TDAA samples were hydrolyzed in 6 N HCl (added with 1% 12 mmol L−1 ascorbic acid to prevent oxidation by nitrate) under nitrogen at 110 °C for 20 h. Hydrolyzed samples were then neutralized through nitrogen evaporation. Amino acids were derivatized with o-phthaldialdehyde and analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Dionex ICS 5000+) equipped with a fluorescence detector (Ex = 330 nm, Em = 418 nm) following an established protocol7,115 with modifications21. As a dissolved excreta from zooplankton61, taurine (Tau, an amino acid-like compound) was analyzed together with TDAA using the same method. Analyzed amino acids or amino acid-like compounds included: aspartic acid (Asp), glutamic acid (Glu), histidine (His), serine (Ser), arginine (Arg), threonine (Thr), glycine (Gly), Tau, β-alanine (BAla), tyrosine (Tyr), alanine (Ala), γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), methionine (Met), valine (Val), phenylalanine (Phe), isoleucine (Ile), leucine (Leu), and lysine (Lys). Replicates of TDAA measurements have a CV of ~10–20%. The detection limit of individual amino acids is 2 nmol L[−1 115. TDAA carbon (TDAA-C) was calculated as the sum of all individual amino acid concentrations in carbon units and TDAA yield was calculated as the percentage of TDAA-C in DOC concentrations.

For metabolites, the acidified filtrate was passed through Agilent Bond Elut PPL cartridges to extract dissolved metabolites as previously described110,116. Methanol extracts were evaporated and reconstituted in 250 µL of 95:5 (v/v) water/acetonitrile with isotopically labeled standards. Samples were analyzed using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (Accela Open Autosampler and Accela 1250 Pump, Thermo Scientific) coupled to a heated electrospray ionization source (H-ESI) and a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (TSQ Vantage, Thermo Scientific) operated under selected reaction monitoring (SRM) mode117. Separation of sample peaks was performed on a reverse phase column (Waters Aquity HSS T3, 2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) at 40 °C and with mobile phase A as 0.1% formic acid in water and mobile phase B as 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The gradient program with the flow rate of 0.5 mL min−1 follows: 1% B for 1 min, increased to 15% B from 1 to 3 min, increased to 50% B from 3 to 6 min, and then increased to 95% B from 6 to 9 min, maintained at 95% B until 10 min, then decreased to 1% B from 10 to 10.2 min, and then held at 1% B until 12 min. Samples with a 5 μL injection were run in both positive and negative ion modes. Raw data files were converted to mzML format and then processed with MAVEN118,119 for peak identification and integration. Peaks were identified by the combination of their unique retention time, precursor m/z, and product m/z. Two SRM transitions per target compound were monitored for quantification and confirmation. Metabolite concentrations were calculated using a standard curve of at least five points and were corrected for the extraction efficiency of the PPL cartridge according to Johnson et al.120 and compounds not included in Johnson et al.120 (Supplementary Table 5). A set of 100 metabolites was included in the analysis, and 42 metabolites were above the detection limit (<1 pmol L−1 to 2 nmol L−1) and passed the quality control in this study. Peak area reproducibility for metabolites analyzed with this method has an average CV of 23%117.

Particulate organic carbon (POC) and particulate organic nitrogen (PON) were measured using the Dumas combustion method in an automated organic elemental analyzer at the Marine Science Institute analytical lab in UCSB after filters were fume acidified overnight to remove inorganic carbon121. The detection limit for carbon is 2–7 μg, and for nitrogen is 1–2 μg. The precision of POM analysis is ~0.3%.

Bacterial activity experiments

BAct was analyzed using the 3H-leucine incorporation method with <5 pmol L−1 h−1 detection limit122,123. 4,5-3H(N)-Leu at 20 nM (specific activity 50.2–52.6 Ci mmol–1; Perkin Elmer, Boston, MA) was spiked in triplicate 1.6 mL seawater in microcentrifuge tubes. Samples were incubated in the dark at in situ temperatures for 3–4 h and then terminated by 100% trichloroacetic acid (TCA, 6% final concentration). After termination, samples were extracted with 5% TCA and 80% ethanol via the modified microcentrifuge method35,124. A killed control was added with 100% TCA (6% final concentration) and followed the same incubation and extraction steps as before. After extraction, samples were mixed with 1.5 mL Ultima Gold (Perkin Elmer LLC), left for at least 2 h, and then measured in a liquid scintillation counter. The CV of BAct analysis is <10%.

Bacterioplankton abundance counts

Single BA samples were filtered onto 0.2 μm polycarbonate filters stained with Irgalan Black under a gentle vacuum (~100 mm Hg). Cells on filters were then stained with 5 μg mL−1 4’,6’-diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI, Sigma-Aldrich). Filters were mounted onto slides with Resolve immersion oil (high viscosity), and cells were enumerated under ultraviolet excitation using an epifluorescence microscope (Olympus BX51) at ×1000 magnification111. For each filter, at least 10 fields or 500 cells were counted, and the CV was within 5–10%.

DNA extraction, 16S rRNA gene amplicon PCR, sequencing, and analysis

DNA on Sterivex filters was extracted using the phenol, isoamyl alcohol, and chloroform (PIC) protocol125. 16S rRNA genes were amplified with V1-V2 primer 27F (5’-AGAGTTTGATCNTGGCTCAG-3’) and 338RPL (5’-GCWGCCWCCCGTAGGWGT-3’) linked to “general” Illumina overhang adapters. Polymerase chain reactions (PCR) amplification and purification followed the previous method126,127. Libraries were purified following the 16S amplicon library preparation protocol of the Illumina MiSeq sequencer, pooled in equimolar concentrations, and sequenced using 2 × 250 Pair-End lanes with a MiSeq Reagent Kit v2 at the Center for Quantitative Life Sciences (CQLS) at Oregon State University. 16S amplicon sequences were trimmed, dereplicated, excluded for chimeras, and assigned to ASV phylogeny using the DADA2 R package, version 1.2128 and Silva data (version 123). Unassigned ASV sequences were BLAST searched against a non-redundant 16S rRNA sequence database and then against the full non-redundant database to be assigned to the top BLAST hit129–131. For fine-scale phylogenetic assignment of SAR11 and SAR202 clades, their ASVs were further run through the PhyloAssigner pipeline126 using customized phylogenetic trees132. The relative abundance of an ASV was calculated by normalizing its reads to the total reads of all ASVs, excluding plastids and non-bacteria ASVs in the same sample. The cell abundance of an ASV was estimated by multiplying its relative abundance by the total BA.

16S rRNA amplicons from phytoplankton ASVs, including plastid and cyanobacteria ASVs were identified within V1-V2 16S rRNA gene ASVs through a rewritten version of PhyloAssigner133, relying on a maximum likelihood approach and a series of reference phylogenies and alignments. First, all representative ASVs were classified using a global 16S rRNA gene reference126. All ASVs classified as cyanobacterial or plastid were further classified with a better-resolved cyanobacteria and plastid reference phylogeny134. Then, cyanobacteria and plastid ASVs were further classified using designated databases previously published for Prochlorococcus, Viridiplantae133, Dictyochophyceae, and Pelagophyceae135. PhyloAssigner and the V1V2-specific reference databases used herein are available through GitHub (https://github.com/BIOS-SCOPE/PhyloAssigner_python_UCSB). Only the top 10 phytoplankton ASVs and their relative abundance (%) among all cyanobacteria, plastids, and heterotrophic bacteria were used for this study due to the limited sequencing depth.

18S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing

18S rRNA genes of DNA were amplified with V4 primer 5-CCAGAC[GC]C[CT]GCGGTAATTCC-3 and 5-ACTTTCGTTCTTGAT[CT][AG]A-3. PCR amplification and library preparation followed established protocols136. Amplicon sequencing was conducted on an Illumina MiSeq using 2 × 250 PE V2 chemistry at Oregon State University CQLS. Quality control and read merging was conducted with MeFiT137 and assembled reads was assigned to taxonomies in MOTHUR based on PR2 database138 following Blanco-Bercial et al.139. After the exclusion of metazoans and unclassified eukaryotes, ASV counts in each sample were rarefied to 20,000 and the top 20 ASVs within each phylum were the focus of this study. To assess the grazing effects of protists on bacterioplankton, parasite Syndiniales were excluded and sequencing reads of heterotrophic or mixotrophic (function profiles identified based on literature) protist ASVs139,140 were used for data analysis.

MOCNESS sampling and zooplankton biovolumes

To assess the potential effects of zooplankton on DOM over the diel scale, zooplankton biovolume was measured from discrete depth layers using a closing net and laboratory imaging system as described previously69. Briefly, a 1-m MOCNESS141 equipped with 150 µm nets was deployed during the mid-day (9 am–1 pm local time) and mid-evening (11 pm–4 am local time) down to 1000 m. Upon retrieval, the catch from each of the eight vertically discrete nets was divided into two splits. Half were preserved in 95% undenatured ethanol and the remainder were preserved in buffered 4% formalin in seawater. Aliquots of the formalin-preserved samples were imaged using a ZooSCAN ver. 3 at 4800 dpi following the methods of Gorsky et al.142 and Vandromme et al.143, images were processed in ZooProcess, and then fed into Ecotaxa for machine-assisted identification. Individual biovolumes were calculated for all “live” particle images (i.e., excluding threads, detritus, bubbles, etc.) using the ellipsoidal calculation based on measured minor and major axes of the image, summed, and scaled to biovolume per volume of water (mm3 m−3) based on the number of splits and volume filtered for each net.

Statistics and reproducibility

Contour plots of depth profiles for each variable over sample time were plotted using Ocean Data View144. To enlarge the signal in the upper twilight zone and set it on the same scale as that in the euphotic zone, z-score (standardized parameter) was calculated as:

where zi is the z-score of the variable at target depth i, Xi is the original variable value, and AVE Xi and STD Xi are the mean and standard deviation of all values over the same target depths in the dataset.

Water column depths were divided into the euphotic zone and upper twilight zone, where the base of the euphotic zone was defined as the bottom depth of the layer where chlorophyll fluorescence was larger than 35% of the maximum chlorophyll fluorescence value and the upper twilight zone was from the base of the euphotic zone to 300 m145. If the sampling depth did not coincide with the interface depth between the euphotic zone and upper twilight zone, variable data was interpolated using “na.approx” function in R146. To evaluate the diel pattern, depth-integrated data (using “integrateTrapezoid” function in R147) were first fitted with a sine wave model (a*sin(b*x + c)+dx+f, where a, b, c, d, f were parameters generated from model fitting and x was sampling time point; dx term was included to account for potential variations in sine wave amplitude across diel sampling time) using “nls” function in R148, r2 for goodness of nonlinear model fitting was calculated as 1-(sum of squared residuals from the model)/(sum of squared differences from the mean of the response variable), and then a CCF analysis was conducted between surface water PAR data and depth-integrated observed data (n = 7–8)149. CCF analysis is a correlation analysis between two time-series datasets, providing not only the correlation but also time lag between two variables. Two metrics were used to define a significant diel pattern: (1) CCF analysis results showed significant correlation between variables and PAR with p < 0.05; (2) sine model fitting with consistent day vs. night difference; both metrics had to be significant to define a variable as having a diel pattern.

To examine the diel pattern of TDAA-C, 2 years of euphotic and upper twilight zone data were pooled together and TDAA-C anomalies were calculated as the deviation from the mean divided by the mean (i.e., (observed-mean)/mean). Variable anomalies were grouped into day and night, and a significant difference between day and night groups in boxplots was assessed using a Wilcoxon signed rank test (one-tail). PCA was conducted on the molar percentage of amino acids after standardization (deviation from the mean divided by standard deviation) to avoid skew by the dominant amino acids150. The significant difference between day and night TDAA compositions was assessed by ANOSIM using the R vegan package151.

To identify major phytoplankton taxa contributing to the chlorophyll fluorescence signal, CCF analysis between the mean relative abundance of the top 10 phytoplankton ASVs in the euphotic zone and euphotic zone depth-integrated chlorophyll fluorescence was conducted. To assess the potential grazing effects of protists on bacterioplankton, depth-integrated read counts of protist 18S ASV that were negatively and significantly (p < 0.05) correlated with depth-integrated 16S ASV abundance were selected.
