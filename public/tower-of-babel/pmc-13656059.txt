Beyond diagnosis and antibiotic stewardship: a hypothesized additional role for procalcitonin in patients with suspected sepsis
Authors: Guillaume Monneret, Morgane Gossez, Fabienne Venet
Published: 2026

Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection (1,2). According to international estimates, sepsis affects approximately 49 million people worldwide each year and is associated with around 11 million deaths annually, accounting for nearly 20% of all global deaths. This burden exceeds that of all cancers combined, highlighting sepsis as one of the leading causes of death worldwide (3). Moreover, the true scale of sepsis is likely underestimated owing to heterogeneity in diagnostic practices, unequal access to healthcare resources, and limitations in epidemiological surveillance. While historically associated mainly with severe community-acquired infections, sepsis increasingly occurs in older adults, patients with multiple comorbidities, immunocompromised individuals, and hospitalized patients exposed to invasive procedures. Mortality remains substantial despite advances in antimicrobial therapy, intensive care, and supportive management (2,3). Therefore, sepsis continues to represent a major public health challenge, placing a substantial burden on healthcare systems worldwide. From a pathophysiology standpoint, recent evidence shows that sepsis is characterized by a complex and dynamic immune response involving both an early hyper-inflammatory phase and the activation of counter-regulatory mechanisms aimed at limiting potentially deleterious inflammation (4). Thus, sepsis should be viewed not only as an acute infectious emergency but also as a complex syndrome involving profound interactions among pathogens, inflammation, and host immunity (5). This complexity explains why improving outcomes requires both rapid recognition and increasingly personalized therapeutic strategies (6).

Since its first description as a potential sepsis marker in 1993 (7), procalcitonin (PCT) has generated an enormous body of scientific literature, with more than 4,500 articles indexed in PubMed retrieved by combining the terms “sepsis” and “procalcitonin”. Over the past three decades, PCT has become one of the reference biomarkers in the field of infection and sepsis management. Initially described as a diagnostic marker for bacterial infection and sepsis, PCT was then proposed as a tool to support clinical decision-making, particularly for identifying patients in whom antibiotic therapy should be initiated (8). Unlike traditional inflammatory markers, PCT shows relatively good sensitivity and specificity for bacterial infections, as its production is strongly induced by systemic bacterial inflammatory responses and is less affected by many non-infectious inflammatory conditions (9). Beyond diagnosis, PCT has been extensively investigated as a dynamic biomarker for monitoring response to antimicrobial therapy (10). Because PCT has a relatively short biological half-life (approximately 20 hours), effective control of the infectious process is usually associated with a rapid decline in circulating PCT concentrations. Conversely, persistent elevation or insufficient decrease may suggest ongoing infection, inadequate source control, or treatment failure. This kinetic approach has led to the development of PCT-guided antibiotic stewardship strategies, particularly aiming to reduce unnecessary antibiotic exposure by supporting earlier discontinuation of therapy when clinical improvement is observed. Several randomized clinical trials and meta-analyses have suggested that PCT-guided strategies can reduce antibiotic duration without compromising patient safety, especially in respiratory infections and intensive care settings (11,12).

That said, the optimal integration of PCT into clinical pathways continues to evolve. Early diagnosis of sepsis in the emergency department remains particularly challenging because clinical manifestations are often non-specific and there is no single diagnostic gold standard. As a result, many patients initially suspected of having sepsis do not ultimately have a bacterial infection. This creates a major clinical dilemma: while delayed antibiotic administration may increase mortality in patients with true sepsis, excessive antibiotic use contributes to antimicrobial resistance, adverse drug effects, and unnecessary healthcare costs. To further evaluate the potential role of PCT in addressing this unresolved challenge, the PRONTO study investigated whether combining PCT testing with clinical assessment could improve sepsis recognition, optimize antibiotic decision-making, and safely reduce antibiotic prescriptions without negatively affecting patient outcomes (13).

The PRONTO study was a multicenter, randomized, controlled, open-label, phase 3 trial conducted in 20 emergency departments in England and Wales. Between November 2020 and November 2023, adults presenting with suspected sepsis were randomly assigned to receive either standard care guided by National Early Warning Score 2 (NEWS2; a physiology-based scoring system) alone or NEWS2 combined with a PCT-guided algorithm. The primary analysis included more than 5,400 patients. The trial had two co-primary outcomes: (I) to improve the identification of patients requiring intravenous antibiotic therapy while reducing unnecessary antibiotic exposure, and (II) to demonstrate that this strategy could be implemented without increasing 28-day mortality. Overall, the PRONTO study evaluated whether incorporating rapid PCT measurement into routine clinical assessment could optimize antibiotic stewardship while maintaining patient safety.

The first finding was the absence of a reduction in early antibiotic initiation. The addition of PCT did not decrease the proportion of patients receiving intravenous antibiotics within 3 hours of presentation. Surprisingly, despite having no major impact on early antibiotic initiation, the PCT-guided strategy was associated with a significant reduction in mortality. Twenty-eight-day mortality was 13.6% in the PCT-guided group compared with 16.6% in the usual care group, and this benefit persisted at 90 days (20.0% vs. 23.8%). This was the most striking and unexpected finding of the study, raising an important question: how could the addition of a diagnostic biomarker reduce mortality without altering antibiotic use? The authors acknowledged that the mechanisms underlying this benefit remained uncertain, but several potential explanations were proposed. One hypothesis is that integrating PCT with routine clinical assessment improved overall clinical decision-making. By combining physiological deterioration (NEWS2) with PCT, clinicians may have obtained a more accurate assessment of disease severity, leading to closer monitoring, earlier recognition of clinical deterioration, and more timely escalation of care. Consequently, the observed survival benefit may reflect a broader improvement in patient management rather than an effect on the timing of antibiotic administration alone. In summary, PRONTO raises the possibility that a PCT-informed clinical strategy may have effects beyond antibiotic stewardship, although the underlying mechanisms remain unknown.

Consequently, the PRONTO trial moves beyond the conventional use of PCT as an antibiotic stewardship guide, opening up new avenues in sepsis management. It raises the hypothesis that this biomarker may improve patient outcomes by enhancing overall clinical decision-making, rather than simply optimizing antibiotic use. This hypothesis, which remains to be validated, is consistent with recent studies showing that integrating routinely available clinical variables and biomarkers at hospital admission can help identify biologically distinct sepsis subphenotypes (14). Using combinations of clinical parameters and biomarkers (e.g., C-reactive protein, ferritin, IL-6, IL-8, lactate), investigators have consistently identified two major phenotypes, broadly described as hyper-inflammatory and hypo-inflammatory (14). Importantly, retrospective analyses suggested that anti-inflammatory therapies (including corticosteroids, anakinra, tocilizumab, and activated protein C) improved survival in patients with the hyper-inflammatory phenotype but were ineffective or even harmful in those with the hypo-inflammatory phenotype, thereby providing a potential explanation for the neutral results observed in the original unselected clinical trials. More specifically, one of these studies developed a parsimonious machine-learning model based on only three biomarkers selected from an initial panel of 36. The final model comprised PCT, IL-6, and sTREM-1 and identified patients most likely to benefit from hydrocortisone therapy (15). Likewise, a recent study combining soluble and cellular biomarkers retained PCT as a key variable for clustering patients presenting to the emergency department with community-acquired pneumonia (16). It should be acknowledged that these perspectives are speculative and go beyond a strict interpretation of the PRONTO study, which was not designed to address these questions. These hypotheses therefore remain to be explored and validated in well-designed future studies. Collectively, these considerations suggest a potential role for PCT that extends beyond its traditional applications in diagnosing bacterial infection and guiding antibiotic therapy. Alongside clinical evaluation, PCT, as part of a broader panel of biomarkers, may contribute to improved overall clinical decision-making. Furthermore, once fully validated, this approach to biological subphenotyping and risk stratification holds promise for the early identification of high-risk patients, paving the way for precision clinical management and targeted immunomodulatory interventions.

Ultimately, beyond its strict findings, the PRONTO study provides a basis for generating hypotheses regarding an additional role for PCT in sepsis. These hypotheses are consistent with the recent evolution of the literature toward biological phenotyping and more individualized approaches to the treatment of septic patients. This is particularly encouraging in a field where sepsis has been described as “the quintessential medical disorder of the 21st century” (17).
