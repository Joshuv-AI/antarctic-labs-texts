1
Introduction

Diffusion-based text-to-image generators have become increasingly popular in recent years due to their ease of training, leveraging simple and efficient loss functions, and their enhanced stability during training. Despite these advantages, they still face challenges in generating images that faithfully adhere to textual prompts. Common issues include miscounting, missing objects, and errors in attribute binding.

To address these limitations, CONFORM
(
Meral et al., 2023
)
employs Contrastive Learning as a solution. However, problems persist (interactions are missing or misinterpreted) when it comes to generating images involving interactions between objects
(see Fig.
6
/
7
/
8
/
9
).

In this project, we aim to tackle the issue of infidelity in text-to-image generation, focusing particularly on actions involving multiple objects. Building upon the foundations of CONFORM
(
Meral et al., 2023
)
and InteractDiffusion
(
Hoe et al., 2024
)
, our primary goal is to generalize the contrastive learning approach in CONFORM
(
Meral et al., 2023
)
to better support actions and potentially other complex interactions. Furthermore, we explore the integration of concepts from InteractDiffusion
(
Hoe et al., 2024
)
to enhance interaction embeddings within the model, promoting more accurate and contextually appropriate visualizations of object interactions.

In the end, our method shows promising results even with verbs that Stable Diffusion
(
Rombach et al., 2021b
)
understands mediocrely (c.f.
3.2
for a thorough analysis).

2.1
Semantically hypergraphic contrastive adjacency learning

Figure 1:
Semantic hypergraph of “A pigeon carrying a letter is playing with a white dog and a cat in a forest”

A
Semantic Hypergraph
is constructed from a prompt: noun groups as vertices
V
,
and hyperedges
E=\{a\text{ action of }m\text{ parties }v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\in V\}
, as in Fig.
1
.

Inspired by
Meral et al. (2023)
, we first perform
contrastive adjacency learning

on all vertices to reduce the overlapping of their attention regions.

For each hyperedge (
a
,
v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}
), we perform
contrastive adjacency learning
: we pose a loss on the attention so that the vertices have different attention regions, but the region borders are close to each other.

In addition, we apply
another contrastive learning
between the action with its associated parties and the rest of the components.
Note that in practice, for this contrastive learning,
we need to use detached PyTorch tensor values of the objects.
This results in zero gradient on the latent variables of the objects on this learning part,
so that the action is drawn to its associated objects
and not the opposite.

One simplistic visual example to demonstrate our method is in Figure
2
and a more detailed description of the loss function is in the Appendix
A.1
.

Figure 2:
Contrast means that the attention regions are separated and link means that the regions are close; the diagram shows the concept and the plots
show an example (from SHYI method) of attention maps of tokens where “dog”
and “cat” have distinct yet connected attention regions, and
the attention region “plays” is forced onto “dog” and “cat” by contrasting with “forest”

Iterative Loss Selection
Since this is a multiobjective optimization,
simply adding the losses together
and optimizing by gradient descent does not
perform well. To mitigate this effect,
we alternatively select different loss terms of different
objectives at each
gradient descent iteration.

When a constrastive group has only one token, it pairs
to no one and produces no loss for the network to learn.
To produce a loss value for this token and also to enhance consistency
across timesteps,
we add the attention maps of the same tokens of step
t+1

into their contrastive groups. This is performed by
Meral et al. (2023)

as well.

Finally, the latent representation is updated at each iteration as

z_{t}^{\prime}=z_{t}-\alpha_{t}\nabla_{z_{t}}\mathcal{L}

for the selected loss
\mathcal{L}
.

2.2
Enhancement by InteractDiffusion

Diffusion models often struggle with accurately generating images from prompts involving interactions between multiple objects. These challenges primarily come from the models’ inability to properly understand relationships such as subject-action-object and maintain spatial fidelity in intricate scenes. To address these issues, we integrated components of InteractDiffusion
(
Hoe et al., 2024
)
into our framework to enhance action understanding.

Leveraging InteractDiffusion Weights

InteractDiffusion
(
Hoe et al., 2024
)
incorporates object interaction-aware attention mechanisms and role-specific embeddings (e.g., subject, object, action) into its fine-tuning process. To benefit from that, we imported the
pre-trained weights
of InteractDiffusion, which were trained to improve modeling of object interactions.

While InteractDiffusion typically includes bounding box annotations to guide the model during training and inference, we decided not to include it as our method requires
only on the prompts itself. Instead, the improvements achieved (see Figure
3
and Section
3
in general) came solely from the interaction-specific prior knowledge embedded in the weights.

Integration into the pipeline

In practice, to integrate InteractDiffusion, we replaced the U-Net
2
2

2

https://huggingface.co/interactdiffusion/diffusers-v1-2/tree/main/unet
component in the pipeline with that of InteractDiffusion. Then we made adjustments in the codebase
to make it compatible with this new U-Net architecture and included other components
of the InteractDiffusion model with which the U-Net was trained (although those components
were not trained by the InteractDiffuion team).

2.3
Related Work

Previous work has already addressed the infidelity of text-to-image generation for multiple objects with different attributes through contrastive learning (CONFORM
(
Meral et al., 2023
)
) or segregation loss (A-star
(
Agarwal et al., 2023
)
), ensuring attribute binding consistency (Divide & Bind
(
Li et al., 2023
)
) and maximizing attention for all objects in prompts (Attend-and-Excite
(
Chefer et al., 2023
)
).

These methods typically work on top of diffusion-based text-to-image generation models like Stable Diffusion
(
Rombach et al., 2021a
)
or Imagen
(
Saharia et al., 2022
)
.
InteractDiffusion
(
Hoe et al., 2024
)
improves text-to-image generation on prompts that include interaction with multiple objects but relies on region marking.

Although these methods are good at handling separate objects and their attributes, they face challenges when it comes to dealing with interactions as they cannot be assigned simply to one object.

3
Results

Due to the limitation of the relatively small diffusion
models that we use, image generation with
prompts with more than two interacting objects
is catastrophic for all the pipelines. Hence, we stick to
prompts of two interacting objects.

We evaluate our methods through qualitative and quantitative experiments.
A user study is conducted to compare image generation methods (details in
3.2
).

Quantitatively, we use TIFA
(
Hu et al., 2023
)
, which performs text-to-image faithfulness evaluations with question answering. The question-answer pairs for the TIFA scores are generated using GPT-3.5turbo and GPT-4o. In addition, we evaluate our results using CLIP
(
Radford et al., 2021
)
: compare the cosine similarity of text and image embeddings.

3.3
User Study

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of our method, we conducted a user study focusing on qualitative assessments of image generation.

The primary aim of this study was to address the limitations of automated metrics such as TIFA and CLIP, which fail to reliably capture action fidelity and object interactions, as noted in Subsection
3.1
.

Participants were asked to compare images generated by our pipeline (SHYI) with those generated by the baseline model (CONFORM). They evaluated the images based on:

1.

Prompt Accuracy: Which image encapsulates the prompt better?

2.

Interaction Quality: Which image shows more interaction between the two objects?

An example of this evaluation is shown in Fig.
10
.

The study included around 30 participants.
In total, each participant reviewed

6\text{ prompts}\times 3\text{ seeds}\times 2\text{ questions}=36\text{ comparisons}
.

The study was designed to ensure balanced and comprehensive feedback across a diverse set of prompts. The prompts are selected by first eliminating the prompts that have poor image quality for both of the pipelines and then by choosing randomly. We do this selection to keep the survey form short for participants.

4
Discussion

We find that the results depend very much on the ability of Stable Diffusion (addressed as SD in the following) to understanding the action verbs.

When SD understands the verb very well, CONFORM and SHYI have comparable fidelity (see Figure
2
/
6
/
12
).

SD tends to interpret actions in the sense of human being. In this case, SHYI further fosters this effect. Effectively, in Table
3
(“Dog carrying stick over bridge”), the dogs are carrying sticks by hand as humans do. In this sense, SHYI outperforms CONFORM. This problem may be solved by training the diffusion model with more animal interactions. When the action is indeed done by human (and when Stable Diffusion interprets this verb not poorly), SHYI clearly outperforms CONFORM as in Table
4
(“Fisherman catching fish near river”).

When SD does not understand the action (or does not interpret it as a verb) at all, both pipelines perform poorly. For example, in Table
10
(“Robot painting canvas in studio”), SD does not understand “painting” as a verb, perhaps due to its strong prior of it being a noun. Still, one of SHYI’s result (seed 4914) shows a brush held by the robot (in the painting though).

In one case CONFORM clearly outperforms SHYI, see Table
9
(“Bird building nest on cliff”). We think that this is because our link loss is too strong in modifying the latent variables, so that it maximizes the border connection between the two objects. This should be mitigated by decreasing the weight of link loss or making it less often to be selected (in iterative loss selection c.f.
2.1
).

Of course, apart from the understanding of actions of SD, other coefficients also influence image quality and fidelity. In Table
15
(“Cat chasing mouse under tree”), SHYI shows fushioned cats and mouses. The reason is that the environment (“tree”) has too large attention region so “cat” and “mouse” are forced to have the same attention region. Nevertheless, SHYI with seed 4914 has the only image output where the cat is chasing.

5
Summary

In general, our method SHYI shows promising yet not perfect results in high-fidelity text-to-image generation with object interactions by aiding the attention assignment with semantically hypergraphic contrastive adjacency learning.

To further improve our method, we list here some future directions:

•

Hyperparameter tuning
: to scale to more complexe prompts, our method naturally introduces more losses. So how to balance those losses should be studied and tested.

•

Spatial relationship of objects
: how to induce the correct spatial relationship of objects without manually entering the boxes as in InteractDiffusion
(
Hoe et al., 2024
)
to replace the link loss.

•

Improve the understanding of actions
of Stable Diffusion:
for example, how to force Stable Diffusion to understand “paint” as a verb instead of noun.

A.1
Loss function formulas

We define:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{Contrast}}=-\log\frac{\exp\left(\operatorname{sim}\left(A^{j},A^{j^{+}}\right)/\tau_{1}\right)}{\sum_{n\in\{j^{+},j_{1}^{-},\ldots j_{N}^{-}\}}\exp\left(\operatorname{sim}\left(A^{j},A^{n}\right)/\tau_{1}\right)}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{Link}}=-\log\frac{\exp\left(\operatorname{sim}\left(\tilde{A}^{j},\tilde{A}^{j^{-}}\right)/\tau_{2}\right)}{\sum_{n\in\{j^{-},j_{1}^{+},\ldots j_{N}^{+}\}}\exp\left({\operatorname{sim}\left(\tilde{A}^{j},\tilde{A}^{n}\right)/\tau_{2}}\right)}

where
\tilde{A}
is the Gaussian smoothed version of the attention map,
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
are temperature parameters, and
(j,j^{+})
and
(j,j^{-})
are positive and negative pairs.

The intuition behind using Gaussian smoothing is that it adds weights to the outside borders of attention regions, and the overlapping of outside borders is desired. The effect of the weights of attention regions in link loss, which is unwanted, is hopefully mostly counter-effected by contrast loss. In practice, we use Gaussian smoothing also for
A^{j}

but with smaller kernel and variance compared to
\tilde{A}^{j}

For contrast loss, given a token (
j
) in a noun group, we positively pair it with tokens in the same noun group (
j^{+}
), and negatively pair it with tokens in different noun groups (
j^{-}
). Given an action token (
j
), we positively pair it with tokens of the interacting parties (
j^{+}
), and negatively pair it with tokens of other actions and other noun groups (
j^{-}
). We denote
\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{Contrast}}
as the average contrast loss of all positive pairs.

For link loss, we set the positive pairs as tokens in the same noun group and negative pairs as nouns in different noun groups but linked by a hyperedge (action). We denote
\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{Link}}
as the average link loss of all negative pairs.

The final loss function is then the weighted average of
\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{Contrast}}
and
\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{Link}}
. The weights are controlled by iterative loss selection (0 or 1 on different
terms in the losses to perform iterative loss selection, c.f. Subsection
2.1
)
