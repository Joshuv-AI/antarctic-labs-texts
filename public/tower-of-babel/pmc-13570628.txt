Effects of a Nurse-Led Diabetes Perception Intervention Delivered via a Mobile Messaging Platform (LINE) on Glycemic Control and Patient Outcomes Among Adults with Diabetes and Poorly Controlled Glucose: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Varinthon Jantaramanee, Aurawamon Sriyuktasuth, Sirada Kesornsri, Pratana Satitvipawee, Sathit Niramitmahapanya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuboptimal glycemic control remains a major challenge among adults with type 2 diabetes (T2D). Illness perceptions may influence diabetes self-management behaviors and glycemic control.PurposeTo evaluate the effects of a nurse-led diabetes perception intervention delivered via a mobile messaging platform (LINE) on glycemic control, illness perceptions, and patient satisfaction among adults with T2D and poorly controlled glucose.MethodsA randomized controlled trial was conducted among 102 adults aged 30–59 years with T2D and poorly controlled glucose [HbA1c 7.5–9.0% or FPG ≥130 mg/dL]. Participants were randomly assigned to either a nurse-led diabetes perception intervention delivered via LINE plus standard care (n = 51) or standard care alone (n = 51). HbA1c, FPG, and patient satisfaction were assessed at baseline and week 13, and illness perceptions at baseline, week 6, and week 13. Data were analyzed using analysis of covariance and repeated-measures analysis of variance.ResultsBaseline characteristics were comparable between groups. At week 13, after adjustment for baseline values, the intervention group demonstrated significantly lower HbA1c (7.26% vs 7.91%; mean difference = −0.65%, 95% CI: −1.14 to −0.17; p = 0.008), FPG (123.12 vs 164.36 mg/dL; mean difference = −41.24 mg/dL, 95% CI: −57.37 to −25.11; p <0.001), and illness perception scores (31.90 vs 38.39; mean difference = −6.49, 95% CI: −9.58 to −3.40; p <0.001), together with higher patient satisfaction scores (63.90 vs 58.63; mean difference = 5.27, 95% CI: 2.38 to 8.17; p <0.001), compared with the control group.ConclusionThe nurse-led diabetes perception intervention delivered via LINE significantly improved glycemic control, illness perceptions, and patient satisfaction among adults with T2D and poorly controlled glucose. These findings support the potential integration of this accessible and feasible nursing intervention into diabetes self-management support in clinical practice.Keywords: digital health, glycemic control, patient outcome, type 2 diabetes mellitus

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D) is a growing global health concern, currently affecting an estimated 589 million adults, with projections increasing to 853 million by 2050.1,2 In Thailand, the prevalence of T2D continues to rise, particularly among adults with T2D and poorly controlled glucose.3 On average, individuals diagnosed with diabetes incur healthcare expenditures that are approximately 2.6 times higher than those without the condition.4,5 Poor glycemic control in individuals with T2D is associated with an increased risk of serious complications, reduced quality of life, and substantial healthcare costs.6–8 Achieving optimal glycemic control, typically defined as a glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level below 7%, requires not only pharmacologic therapy but also sustained lifestyle modifications and effective self-management behaviors.9

Misconceptions about diabetes—such as perceiving limited control over their condition—can undermine self-care efforts and adversely affect glycemic outcomes. Consequently, adults with T2D and poorly controlled glucose often require structured support to modify their illness perceptions and adopt healthier dietary and physical activity behaviors.10–12 Dietary control plays a central role in glycemic management,13 yet limited diabetes knowledge, competing work demands, and time constraints frequently lead to inappropriate eating patterns.14–16 Carbohydrate counting is often perceived as complex and disruptive to daily routines.17 In Thailand, consumption of high-glycemic rice and sugar-sweetened beverages further contributes to excessive carbohydrate intake and suboptimal glycemic control.18,19

Physical activity is essential for enhancing insulin sensitivity and reducing cardiovascular risk.20,21 However, adults with T2D and poorly controlled glucose commonly encounter barriers such as work fatigue, limited motivation, and diabetes-related symptoms.22–25 Insufficient physical activity remains a key factor contributing to poor glycemic outcomes in this population.

Illness perceptions have been shown to significantly influence diabetes self-care behaviors and long-term outcomes.26,27 Guided by the Common-Sense Model of Self-Regulation (CSM)28, individuals’ beliefs about their illness affect coping strategies, lifestyle choices, and adherence to treatment recommendations. Adults with T2D and poorly controlled glucose frequently hold inaccurate or negative beliefs that hinder self-management, leading to poor dietary control, reduced physical activity, and inadequate glycemic regulation. Addressing and reshaping these perceptions is essential to support behavior changes and improve disease management.29,30

Mobile phone–based interventions show promise in supporting diabetes self-management by providing convenient access to guidance on diet, physical activity, medication adherence, and monitoring.31,32 In addition, previous studies have reported improvements in biochemical outcomes, particularly HbA1c and other metabolic parameters, following digital and mobile health interventions among people with diabetes.33,34 However, their adoption is often hindered by limited digital literacy, preference for free and simple applications, unclear instructions, and time-consuming data entry.35,36 LINE, a widely used mobile messaging platform in Thailand and several Asian countries, provides a practical channel for delivering health interventions. Its widespread use, free access, and familiarity among users may facilitate communication between healthcare providers and patients. In addition, features such as text messaging, video calls, reminders, and multimedia sharing enable the delivery of tailored education and ongoing support, making the platform well suited for implementing nurse-led behavioral interventions.

Interventions addressing illness perceptions alongside dietary and physical activity behaviors have demonstrated effectiveness in improving diabetes outcomes.37,38 Nevertheless, many of these interventions rely on face-to-face delivery with limited follow-up, posing challenges related to time, staffing, and cost.39–43 Given the increasing availability of digital communication platforms, nurse-led interventions that integrate motivational strategies with personalized education may offer a scalable and feasible approach to improving diabetes self-management.

Informed by the Common-Sense Model of Self-Regulation, this study implemented a nurse-led diabetes perception intervention delivered via a mobile messaging platform (LINE) designed to improve illness perceptions, promote behavior change, and enhance glycemic outcomes among adults with T2D and poorly controlled glucose in an urban setting. The intervention incorporated dietary and physical activity guidance as well as motivational support to facilitate sustained self-management.

Delivered through the LINE platform, the intervention provided structured behavioral guidance and ongoing motivational support. This study adds to the growing evidence on the effects of nurse-led diabetes perception interventions delivered via LINE in improving both glycemic control and patient-reported outcomes among adults with T2D and poorly controlled glucose.

Materials and Methods

Research Design

This study employed a two-arm, parallel randomized controlled trial to examine the effects of a nurse-led diabetes perception intervention delivered via LINE on glycemic control, illness perceptions, and patient satisfaction with nursing care. The intervention focused on modifying illness perceptions and supporting behavioral change through personalized dietary and physical activity guidance combined with motivational strategies. The trial was registered with the Thailand Clinical Trial Registry and conducted in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines44 (Figure 1).

Setting

This study was conducted in the diabetes clinic of the medical outpatient department at a 1200-bed tertiary care hospital located in a metropolitan area of Bangkok, Thailand. The clinic provides routine diabetes assessment, medical follow-up, lifestyle counseling, and laboratory monitoring, serving a large and diverse population of adults with T2D. The clinical environment allowed for consistent delivery of standard care and facilitated the integration of the nurse-led LINE-based intervention within existing service workflows.

Participants

Adults with T2D and poorly controlled glucose were eligible to participate if they met the following criteria: (a) aged 30–59 years; (b) diagnosed with T2D and poorly controlled glucose, defined as HbA1c 7.5–9.0% or fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥130 mg/dL; (c) no changes in insulin or oral glucose-lowering medications for at least 3 months prior to enrollment, with no restrictions on specific drug classes; (d) able to communicate in Thai; and (e) able to use a smartphone with the LINE platform.

Participants with severe medical conditions that could interfere with participation were excluded according to the study protocol, including those with active malignancy; chronic kidney disease stage 4–5 (estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <30 mL/min/1.73 m2); unstable medical conditions; recent acute cardiac events within 6 months (eg, myocardial infarction or hospitalization for heart failure); severe hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥180 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥110 mmHg); or severe psychiatric disorders affecting adherence. Participants were withdrawn if they experienced any of the following during the study period: (a) adjustment of glucose-lowering medications; (b) hospitalization that interfered with study participation; or (c) development of severe conditions such as stroke, severe hypertension, or diabetic ketoacidosis.

All participants continued to receive standard diabetes care under routine clinical supervision. No changes in glucose-lowering medications occurred during follow-up, reducing the likelihood that observed HbA1c changes were attributable to pharmacological adjustments.

Potential participants attending routine follow-up visits at the diabetes clinic were approached by the research team, provided with study information, and invited to participate. The first participant was recruited on 20 July 2023, and recruitment continued until 5 October 2023 at the hospital.

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size was calculated based on a previous study by Boonjun and Pongpatanawut,45 which examined the effects of a carbohydrate-counting program among adults with T2D and poorly controlled glucose. In that study, the mean HbA1c levels in the intervention and control groups were 7.62 (SD = 0.9) and 8.31 (SD = 1.3), respectively. Rosner’s formula46 for comparing two independent means was applied to estimate the required sample size, as follows:

n = (Z1–α/2 + Z1–β)2 (σ12 + σ22)/(μ1 – μ2)2

Where:

μ1, μ2 = means of the two groups σ12, σ22 = variances of the two groups

Z1–α/2 = 1.96 for a two-sided test

Z(1–β) = 0.842 for 80% power

Substitution into the formula produced an estimated sample size of 41.75 participants per group. Based on a significance level of 0.05 and power of 0.80, the minimum required sample was 84 participants. To account for an anticipated 20% dropout rate, the final target sample size was increased to 102 participants, with 51 allocated to the intervention group and 51 to the control group using block randomization.

Randomization and Blinding

Randomization

Adults with T2D and poorly controlled glucose attending routine follow-up visits were invited to participate after receiving detailed information about the study, and written informed consent was obtained prior to baseline assessment. Participants were randomly allocated using a parallel-group design with a 1:1 allocation ratio. A computer-generated randomization sequence with a block size of four was used to ensure balanced group assignment and reduce allocation bias.

Participants were assigned to either the intervention group or the control group.

Allocation concealment was maintained using sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes prepared according to the predetermined randomization sequence. After completion of baseline assessments, participants opened the envelope to reveal their group assignment. Recruitment and randomization continued until the target sample size of 102 participants was achieved, with 51 participants allocated to each group.

Blinding

Due to the nature of the behavioral and educational intervention, blinding of participants and the intervention-delivering nurse was not feasible. However, to minimize potential assessment bias, an outcome assessor was blinded to group allocation throughout the data collection process. Statistical analyses were conducted using coded group labels to further reduce the risk of analytical bias.

Intervention

Nurse-Led Diabetes Perception Intervention Delivered via LINE

The nurse-led diabetes perception intervention delivered via LINE was developed to modify illness perceptions and promote sustainable behavior change through personalized education and motivational strategies, thereby improving glycemic control among adults with T2D and poorly controlled glucose. The intervention consisted of four structured educational sessions delivered during weeks 1–4 via face-to-face and video-based communication, followed by two telephone follow-up sessions at weeks 7 and 11 for reinforcement and support. The LINE platform was used to facilitate communication, deliver educational materials, provide reminders, and enable ongoing interaction between nurses and participants. Participants received the intervention during routine clinic visits. Supplementary self-management information and reinforcement materials were subsequently delivered via LINE, allowing participants to access the information repeatedly as needed. Participants could also communicate with the nurse researcher through LINE during working hours for additional questions or clarification.

The weekly structure, core contents, duration, and mode of delivery of the nurse-led diabetes perception intervention via LINE are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Intervention Adherence

Intervention adherence was assessed using attendance records for the educational sessions, completion of follow-up telephone calls, and LINE-based activity logs, including message interaction and participant engagement throughout the intervention period. Participants in the intervention group were encouraged to attend all four educational sessions and complete scheduled follow-up telephone calls. Those who missed intervention activities were contacted by the researcher to promote continued participation and minimize attrition.

Control Group

Participants in the control group received standard diabetes care, which included routine medical treatment, laboratory testing, regular endocrinologist follow-up visits, and individualized diabetes education provided by the healthcare team at the diabetes clinic.

Measurements

The following questionnaires were used for data collection in this study.

Demographic and Health Information Form

A demographic and health information form was used to collect participants’ personal and clinical characteristics, including age, gender, educational level, employment status, duration of diabetes, and relevant health-related characteristics.

Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (BIPQ)

The Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (BIPQ) was used to assess diabetes-related illness perceptions.47 The instrument evaluates patients’ cognitive and emotional representations of illness across key domains, including consequences, timeline, personal control, treatment control, identity, concern, and emotional response. The BIPQ consists of eight scored items rated on an 11-point rating scale ranging from 0 (“never/rarely”) to 10 (“very severely”), as well as one open-ended item assessing perceived causes of diabetes. Scores for items 1, 2, 5, 6, and 7 are summed, while items 3 and 4 are reverse scored, yielding a total score range of 0 to 80. Higher scores indicate more negative illness perceptions associated with maladaptive coping, whereas lower scores reflect more accurate illness perceptions and adaptive coping behaviors.

The BIPQ was translated into Thai, followed by back-translation and cultural adaptation by Sowattanangoon.48 The Thai version employs an 11-point rating scale with a right-triangle visual layout to enhance ease of use. The content validity index was appropriately established among adults with T2D and poorly controlled glucose, demonstrating good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.75 in the original version47 and 0.95 in the current study.

Patient Satisfaction with Nursing Care Questionnaire

Patient Satisfaction with Nursing Care Questionnaire developed by Suwisith and Hanucharurnkul49 was used to assess patient satisfaction with nursing care in this study. This self-administered instrument evaluates satisfaction across three domains: empathy, service quality, and professional competence. It consisted of 15 items rated on a five-point rating scale ranging from 1 (“highly dissatisfied”) to 5 (“highly satisfied”), with total scores ranging from 15 to 75. Higher scores indicate greater satisfaction with nursing care. The questionnaire demonstrated strong content validity among adults with T2D and poorly controlled glucose and high internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.94 in the original version49 and 0.95 in the present study.

Quality Control and Safety

The nurse-led diabetes perception intervention delivered via LINE was piloted with five individuals resembling the target population of adults with T2D and poorly controlled glucose to assess feasibility, including intervention procedures, assessment processes, duration, and timing. Feedback from the pilot phase was used to refine the intervention content and delivery procedures prior to study implementation.

To ensure intervention quality and consistency, the principal investigator (PI) was the sole provider of nurse-led diabetes perception intervention delivered via LINE according to a standardized protocol. This approach minimized variability in intervention delivery and supported intervention fidelity throughout the study. Data were collected using validated instruments and standardized procedures to ensure data quality and reliability. The intervention was considered low risk. Participants were encouraged to engage in physical activity appropriate to their individual health status, and guidance emphasized safe exercise practices. Participants were monitored for any concerns related to intervention participation, and no adverse events were reported.

Several measures were implemented to ensure participant privacy, confidentiality, and appropriate communication throughout the intervention period. Participants used their own password-protected mobile phones to communicate with the researcher through individual LINE accounts, and no group chats were used. Communication was conducted only between the researcher and participants during working hours. No highly sensitive personal health information or detailed medical records were transmitted through the application. All study data were stored separately in password-protected research files accessible only to authorized research personnel.

Data Collection

Eligibility screening was conducted based on predefined inclusion criteria, and written informed consent was obtained from eligible participants prior to data collection. Baseline assessments were then completed by a research assistant who was blinded to group allocation. At baseline, demographic and health information, illness perceptions, and patient satisfaction with nursing care were assessed in both the intervention and control groups before the intervention commenced. A total of 102 participants were enrolled and randomized, with 51 participants assigned to the intervention group and 51 to the control group. Participants in the intervention group received the nurse-led diabetes perception intervention delivered via LINE in addition to standard diabetes care, whereas participants in the control group received standard diabetes care alone.

At week 6, illness perceptions were reassessed using an online questionnaire administered via Google Forms and distributed to participants through LINE by a research assistant blinded to group allocation. At week 13, participants again completed the online illness perception assessment. At the same time point, glycemic outcomes, including HbA1c and FPG, were extracted from medical records. Patient satisfaction with nursing care was also assessed at week 13.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Statistical significance was set at a p value of <0.05. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and health-related characteristics, including frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables, as appropriate.

Baseline characteristics between the intervention and control groups were compared using independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U-tests for continuous variables, and Chi-square tests for categorical variables, depending on data distribution. Changes in patient satisfaction with nursing care, HbA1c, and FPG from baseline to week 13 were compared between groups using independent t-tests. As HbA1c and FPG were non-normally distributed, changes in these variables were compared using Mann–Whitney U tests.

Changes in illness perceptions over time (baseline, week 6, and week 13) were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) to examine time effects, group effects, and time-by-group interaction effects. In addition, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed to determine the intervention effect on outcome variables at follow-up while adjusting for baseline scores as covariates. All relevant statistical assumptions for the applied analyses were examined and met.

Results

Participants were aged 30–59 years, with no significant differences in age distribution between the intervention and control groups. Gender distribution, educational attainment, and employment status were comparable across groups, with most participants holding a bachelor’s degree or higher and being employed in non-government positions. The mean duration of T2D was also similar between groups.

Retention rates were 98.0% in the intervention group and 96.1% in the control group. One participant in the intervention group and two participants in the control group were lost to follow-up at the 3-month visit; therefore, outcome data were not available for these participants.

Baseline anthropometric and glycemic measures, including waist circumference, waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), HbA1c, and FPG, did not differ significantly between the intervention and control groups. Overall, baseline demographic and health-related characteristics were comparable between groups; however, patient satisfaction with nursing care differed significantly at baseline.

After completion of the intervention, at week 13, the intervention group demonstrated a significantly lower mean HbA1c level (7.19 ± 0.9) compared with the control group (7.98 ± 1.5; p <0.05). Similarly, the mean FPG level was significantly lower in the intervention group (121.71 ± 28.6) than in the control group (165.84 ± 45.5; p <0.001). When baseline values were adjusted using ANCOVA, significant between-group differences remained at week 13. The intervention group demonstrated lower adjusted HbA1c levels than the control group (adjusted mean ± SE: 7.26 ± 0.17 [95% CI, 6.92–7.59] vs 7.91 ± 0.17 [95% CI, 7.57–8.25]). Regarding PSNQ scores, the intervention group demonstrated higher adjusted scores than the control group (63.90 ± 1.01 [95% CI, 61.90–65.91] vs 58.63 ± 1.02 [95% CI, 56.60–60.66]). The adjusted mean difference was 5.27 points (95% CI, 2.38–8.17). This difference was statistically significant (F(1,96) = 13.08, p <0.001, ηp2 = 0.12). No significant between-group differences were observed in waist circumference or waist-to-height ratio at week 13, as presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that the nurse-led diabetes perception intervention delivered via LINE grounded in the Common-Sense Model (CSM)28 effectively improved illness perceptions and glycemic outcomes among adults with T2D and poorly controlled glucose. The intervention was designed to modify patients’ cognitive and emotional representations of diabetes, thereby promoting adaptive self-management behaviors and improving clinical outcomes. These findings are consistent with previous evidence indicating that theory-driven, technology-enabled self-management interventions can enhance patient engagement, strengthen self-management behaviors, and improve glycemic outcomes among adults with T2D.50,51

To our knowledge, few randomized controlled trials have specifically targeted illness perceptions as a primary mechanism of behavioral change while simultaneously evaluating glycemic outcomes and patient satisfaction using a mobile messaging platform.

By week 13, participants in the intervention group showed significant improvements in illness perception scores compared with the control group, whereas no significant differences were observed at week 6. A transient improvement in the control group at week 6 may be explained by a Hawthorne effect or increased self-awareness from repeated questionnaire completion.

However, without sustained intervention exposure, this effect appeared to diminish by week 13, suggesting that continued engagement is required to modify cognitive and emotional representations of chronic illness. According to the CSM, changes in illness perceptions influence coping strategies and subsequently health outcomes.28 The delayed emergence of between-group differences may reflect the time required for cognitive restructuring and behavioral adaptation.

Improvements in illness perceptions may have served as an intermediate mechanism linking the intervention to behavioral change. Participants with more adaptive perceptions may have been more likely to engage in dietary regulation and physical activity. The individualized education, correction of misconceptions, and motivational support delivered via LINE likely enhanced self-efficacy and treatment adherence. Prior studies indicate that personalized digital motivational interventions improve physical activity and glycemic outcomes in individuals with diabetes.52,53 Nurse-led interventions have similarly been associated with improved chronic disease outcomes.

The nurse’s role was central to intervention effectiveness. Nurses are well positioned to assess illness perceptions, provide tailored education, and deliver motivational support addressing cognitive and emotional aspects of diabetes management. The combination of clinical expertise and sustained digital communication via LINE may have strengthened therapeutic relationships and facilitated continuous engagement.

These perception-driven and behavior-related changes were accompanied by clinically meaningful improvements in glycemic control. By week 13, median HbA1c decreased by 0.81 percentage points in the intervention group compared with 0.02 percentage points in the control group (between-group difference: 0.79 percentage points). Similarly, median FPG decreased by 29.79 mg/dL in the intervention group but increased by 7.84 mg/dL in the control group (between-group difference: 37.63 mg/dL).

These findings align with evidence that mobile health–based interventions incorporating individualized support and behavioral strategies improve glycemic outcomes.33,54 A 1% reduction in HbA1c is associated with reduced risk of diabetes-related complications, suggesting potential clinical relevance, although longer follow-up is required to confirm sustainability.55 No significant changes were observed in waist circumference or WHtR. As shown in prior systematic reviews, anthropometric outcomes often require longer intervention durations (6–12 months) to demonstrate measurable change.56–58 In addition, this intervention primarily targeted illness perceptions and self-management rather than weight reduction.

Participants in the intervention group reported higher satisfaction with nursing care, particularly in domains such as empathy, accessibility, and professional competence. These findings are consistent with prior evidence that personalized nursing interventions enhance patient satisfaction and may improve clinical outcomes.59–61 The LINE-based communication likely supported timely interaction and strengthened therapeutic relationships.

The intervention required additional nursing time for individualized education, follow-up calls, and digital support via LINE. However, these gains may be acceptable given the observed clinical and psychosocial benefits. Each educational session required approximately 20–30 minutes, and follow-up calls lasted 5–10 minutes per participant. As the intervention was embedded within routine care and delivered through a widely available mobile messaging platform with standardized educational materials and automated reminder messages, additional administrative and infrastructural demands were relatively limited compared with conventional face-to-face education.

From an implementation perspective, this approach may enhance continuity of care, patient engagement, and scalability in routine diabetes management. Future implementation may benefit from nurse training in motivational communication and partial automation of reminders to improve efficiency and reduce workload.

The present findings are consistent with prior studies demonstrating beneficial effects of mobile health interventions on diabetes self-management and glycemic outcomes.51,62 Previous research has also reported improvements in broader metabolic indicators, including lipid profiles and insulin sensitivity, suggesting multidimensional benefits of digital health interventions.63,64 This study has several limitations. It was conducted in a single urban setting in Thailand, which may limit generalizability. The relatively short follow-up period may not capture long-term sustainability of behavioral and metabolic changes. In addition, participants required access to smartphones and LINE, potentially excluding individuals with limited digital literacy. Although HbA1c and FPG are standard indicators of glycemic control, HbA1c may be influenced by conditions affecting erythrocyte turnover. While individuals with severe or unstable conditions were excluded, relevant metabolic confounders were not systematically assessed. Future studies should include longer follow-up periods, more diverse populations, additional glycemic biomarkers, and broader cardiometabolic outcomes to strengthen evidence of long-term effectiveness and scalability.

Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of individualized, theory-driven, nurse-led digital interventions in improving illness perceptions, glycemic outcomes, and patient experiences among adults with T2D and poorly controlled glucose.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a nurse-led diabetes perception intervention delivered via LINE and grounded in the Common-Sense Model can improve illness perceptions, glycemic control, and patient satisfaction among adults with T2D and poorly controlled glucose. The findings provide evidence that a theory-driven digital nursing intervention can positively influence both clinical and patient-reported outcomes.

These results highlight the potential of integrating mobile messaging platforms into routine diabetes care to enhance patient engagement, continuity of support, and self-management. Strengthening digital competencies within multidisciplinary healthcare teams may facilitate implementation of patient-centered chronic disease management. Future multi-site studies with longer follow-up periods and more diverse populations, incorporating broader clinical and cardiometabolic outcomes, are warranted to evaluate the sustainability, scalability, and generalizability of this approach.
