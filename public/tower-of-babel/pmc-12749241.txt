Synergistic structural performance of green self-compacting concrete enhanced by nano-clay and waste glass powder
Authors: Sheelan Mahmoud Hama, Ibrahim A. AL-Jumaily, Elaf Khalid Dahamm, Mohamed Ghalla, Md. Habibur Rahman Sobuz, Sani Aliyu Abubakar, George Wardeh, M. Z. A. M. Zahid, Taher A. Tawfik, W. Zaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study presents a novel investigation into the performance of structural for reinforced self-compacting concrete (SCC) beams including waste glass powder (WGP) and nano-clay (NC) as sustainable partial replacements for cement. Unlike prior studies that focus on individual effects, this research evaluates the combined influence of NC plus WGP blends on full-scale beam behavior using advanced Digital Image Correlation (DIC) techniques. Ten SCC mixtures were designed across three series: NC-only, WGP-only, and hybrid NC–WGP mixes (constant 15% total binder replacement). Reinforced beams (100 × 150 × 1200 mm) were tested under four-point bending to assess flexural strength, stiffness, ductility, crack width, and strain behavior. The results showed that the 2.5% NC + 12.5% WGP mix achieved superior compressive strength (80.6 MPa) and flexural performance. DIC analysis confirmed enhanced crack control, curvature, and energy absorption in hybrid beams. In contrast, high NC content (10%) caused strength loss and embrittlement due to agglomeration. This study is the first to integrate NC and WGP in SCC beams and assess their synergistic effects using full-field DIC analysis, providing new insight into the design of high-performance, eco-efficient structural concrete.

Introduction

Concrete remains the most widely used construction material due to its availability, versatility, and mechanical performance. The environmental cost of cement production particularly its contribution to global CO₂ emissions poses a critical sustainability challenge. It is estimated that concrete-related activities account for nearly 10% of global CO₂ emissions1, primarily due to cement manufacturing. Addressing this issue requires alternative, eco-friendly binders that reduce cement content without compromising structural integrity.

WGP is a non-biodegradable byproduct rich in amorphous silica, has emerged as a promising supplementary cementitious material (SCM). Its incorporation in concrete helps divert significant amounts of glass from landfills while offering mechanical and durability benefits through pozzolanic reactions2,3. Several studies report that partial cement replacement with WGP (typically up to 15%) can improve compressive strength, modulus of rupture, and durability, while also mitigating alkali-silica reactions4,5.

In addition, a study found that replacing 10–15% of cement with WGP resulted in increases of 22–26% in compressive strength, 23–28% in tensile splitting strength, 10–17% in modulus of rupture and 4–6% in bond strength8. Recent research on beams containing WGP showed that replacing 15% of the cement resulted in significant improvements in flexural strength6. While replacing cement with WGP improves the load-bearing capacity of reinforced concrete (RC) beams, it can lead to brittle failure, suggesting that the incorporation of fibers could improve ductility7. Comparative studies have shown that RC beams with WG and plastic waste exhibit significant improvements in maximum load, mid-span deflection, ductility and total energy compared to conventional RC beams8.

Recent studies have highlighted the role of advanced materials and reinforcement strategies in improving the structural performance of concrete. For instance, UHPC reinforced with polyethylene fibers9, FRP bars10, and prefabricated UHPC shells11 have demonstrated enhanced mechanical behavior, bond performance, and fatigue resistance. While these studies focus on specialized high-performance systems, they underscore the broader importance of material innovation for structural enhancement. Building on this principle, the present study investigates the synergistic incorporation of nano-clay and waste glass powder in green SCC to improve mechanical performance and deformation characteristics, analyzed using digital image correlation.

Nano clay can be considered as nanomaterial with a high specific surface area and reactive alumino-silicate content has shown the potential to enhance concrete performance at low dosages. By filling micro-pores and accelerating C-S-H gel formation, NC refines the interfacial transition zones (ITZs), densifies the microstructure, and improves both strength and durability12. The strength gain is highly depend on NC content, with excessive NC content leading to particle agglomeration and strength loss13,14.

The high pozzolanic reactivity of NC and its unique elongated structure contribute to these improvements through a needle action mechanism10. Although extensive research has attempted to determine the optimum NC dosage for SCC, the results remain inconsistent. For instance, studies have shown that replacing 0.75-1% of cement with NC significantly enhances mechanical properties11,12, while others report similar improvements with 6-7.5% NC13,14. Moreover, substituting NC for 10% of the cement increased compressive and tensile strength by 63.1% and 46.8%, respectively, compared to the reference sample15. Optimum performance in compressive and flexural strength has been observed with a 5% NC replacement16, attributed to the fine particles, large surface area and amorphous nature of NC that quickly create a secondary C-S-H gel when hydrated with free lime, filling the capillary pores within the SCC matrix. Studies on the application of NC in slab structures have shown that the optimum NC dosage is around 8% of the cement weight17.

Recent research into the synergistic effects of WGP and NC in SCC showed that replacing cement with 2.5% NC and 12.5% WGP resulted in a 30% increase in compressive strength and a 33% improvement in modulus of elasticity compared to control samples18.

While many studies have independently examined the effects of WGP and NC on concrete properties, research investigating their combined influence on the reinforced concrete (RC) beams structural behavior particularly in SCC systems remains limited. Even fewer studies have employed advanced full-field measurement techniques to capture detailed strain and deformation patterns under loading.

This study introduces a novel approach by combining WGP and NC in SCC mixtures and analyzing the flexural performance of RC beams using DIC. DIC serves as an optical method that operates without any physical contact, which provides high-resolution, displacement and full-field strain data, enabling precise visualization of crack initiation, propagation, and curvature distribution19–21. Unlike conventional strain gauges or LVDTs, which offer only point-based measurements, DIC delivers comprehensive insight into material behavior under load, including localized failure mechanisms and strain concentrations.

This research aims to evaluate the synergistic effects of WGP and NC as partial cement replacements in SCC on the reinforced beams structural behavior, with DIC employed as a state-of-the-art diagnostic tool. The outcomes are expected to advance sustainable construction practices by optimizing binder composition while also demonstrating the value of modern monitoring technologies in structural concrete assessment.

The novelty of this work lies in combining NC and WGP in self-compacting concrete beams and evaluating their synergistic structural behavior using DIC, which has not been reported previously. The findings demonstrate how hybrid nano–pozzolanic systems can improve both mechanical and deformation characteristics, contributing to the design framework of high-performance green SCC.

Experimental work

Raw materials

OPC (Ordinary Portland cement) with a fineness of 330 m2/kg was utilized for all concrete mixes that have been prepared in this work. The cement meets the restrictions of IQS (Iraqi specifications) No. 5/201922.

Broken and discarded window glass was collected from glass shops in Ramadi. Glass powder was prepared in several stages, which were carried out by washing the glass and cleaning it of dirt and dust, then placing the glass in special mills to ground the broken glass to powder as shown in Fig. 1. The particle size distribution of the glass powder used in this study met the general requirements outlined in ASTM C61823 for Class N pozzolans and ASTM C124024 for silica fume, which are commonly used as comparative benchmarks for evaluating alternative pozzolanic materials. Although these standards are not specific to waste glass powder, they are widely referenced in the literature6–9 for assessing the pozzolanic activity and fineness of WGP used in cementitious systems. According to Table 1, the relatively high Na₂O + K₂O content confirms that the WGP is a soda-lime type glass, consistent with window glass used in architectural applications. These alkali levels are typical of benign glasses with low alkali–silica reactivity when the particles are finely ground below 75 μm. The particle-size distribution (Fig. 2a) showed a median diameter (D₅₀) ≈ 18 μm, with 90% of the particles finer than 45 μm. This fineness satisfies ASTM C618 and C1240 limits for pozzolanic materials, ensuring high reactivity and favorable packing with cement grains. The high amorphous-silica content and fine particle size contribute to a strong pozzolanic reaction with Ca(OH)₂, forming additional C–S–H gel and refining the pore structure of the SCC matrix.

Nano-clay used in this study was a montmorillonite-based smectite that widely recognized for its high surface area and layered aluminosilicate structure. The NC had an average platelet thickness of 20–30 nm and a maximum particle size below 100 nm, as confirmed by the particle size distribution curve shown in Fig. 2(a). Figure 2(a) shows the cumulative particle size distribution for NC reaching 100% just below the 100 μm mark (which is 102 nm on the graph’s x-axis, consistent with the definition of a nano-material). The specific surface area about 30.8 m²/g and specific gravity about 2.5. The nano-scale size (< 100 nm) ensures homogeneous dispersion when properly mixed, enabling effective interaction with cement hydration products and improved densification of ITZs. The chemical composition of NC are listed in Table 1.

The pozzolanic activity index of both WGP and NC was assessed according to ASTM C311/C311M25, which measures the strength activity index as an indicator of pozzolanic behavior. Mortar cubes (50 mm) were prepared with a standard mix of cement, standard sand, and the pozzolanic material (replacing 20% of cement by weight), and cured for 7 and 28 days. Compressive strength was then measured to be put in comparison of control mortar specimens composed of 100% cement. The pozzolanic reactivity index was calculated as the ratio (in percentage) of the compressive strength of the blended mortar to that of the control mortar. The results indicated that WGP reached 94.4% at 7 days and 100.86% at 28 days, while NC achieved 103.76% and 106.46%, respectively, indicating strong pozzolanic activity for both materials. The particle size analysis confirms that both NC and WGP possess adequate fineness and reactivity for use as pozzolanic additives in SCC. The NC particles were below 100 μm, enhancing the filler effect and matrix cohesion, while the WGP exhibited a median size of ≈ 18 μm with 90% finer than 45 μm, meeting ASTM C618 and C1240 limits.

Description of reinforced SCC beams and fundamentals of DIC

Ten reinforced SCC beams (100 × 150 × 1200 mm) were cast and tested under four-point bending. Longitudinal reinforcement included two 10 mm bars in both tension and compression zones, while 6 mm stirrups were spaced at 55 mm to prevent premature shear failure (Fig. 3).

Fig. 3Details of used RSCC beams.

Details of used RSCC beams.

High-resolution images were captured using a Canon EOS 5D Mark IV digital camera (30.4 MP; 6720 × 4480 pixels) and analyzed using GOM Correlate 2022 software. The DIC setup followed the best-practice recommendations outlined by Hairong et al.19, Sarvari et al.20, and LePage et al.21. The side face of each beam was coated with a high-contrast black base layer, over which a stochastic white speckle pattern was applied. The speckles were approximately 3–5 pixels in diameter, ensuring a contrast ratio of at least 5:1 and sufficient surface texture for accurate correlation. The camera was positioned 1.8 m from the specimen, capturing the full 1000 × 150 mm region of interest at an effective spatial resolution of approximately 0.1 mm/pixel. Images were recorded at a frame rate of 1 Hz (one frame per second) to balance temporal resolution and data size, consistent with prior DIC studies on concrete members34. Calibration of the optical system was performed using a precision grid before each test. The DIC derived displacement and strain data were validated against LVDT measurements, showing a maximum deviation below 3%, confirming the reliability of the adopted setup. Rigid body mechanism theory served as the foundation for the DIC approach. During testing, sequential surface images of each beam were captured before and throughout deformation, and processed using correlation algorithms to obtain full-field displacement and strain maps34.

At 56 days, all beams were tested under a four-point bending configuration at a loading rate of 0.5 MPa/s using a universal testing machine. Three LVDTs were placed at the mid-span to record deflection for verification of DIC results. The procedures are illustrated in Fig. 4.

The slump flow diameters ranged between 680 and 740 mm, confirming that all mixtures met the EFNARC requirement (650–800 mm). The reference mix achieved a slump flow of 730 mm, whereas increasing NC content progressively reduced the flow diameter to 685 mm at 10% NC. This reduction is attributed to the high surface area and layered structure of NC, which increase water adsorption and internal friction35–38. The T500 time, representing viscosity and flow rate, increased from 2.4 s (reference) to 3.5 s (10% NC), reflecting the thixotropic effect of NC addition. Similar observations were reported by Hosseini et al.11 and Divekar et al.40, who noted that NC particles act as nucleation centers, slightly increasing yield stress while improving cohesiveness.

In contrast, the 15% WGP mix displayed a slump flow of 740 mm and a T500 of 2.3 s, indicating improved flowability compared with NC mixes. This improvement stems from the smooth, inert nature of glass powder, which reduces internal friction and enhances lubrication in the cement paste39. The hybrid mixtures (NC + WGP) exhibited intermediate behavior: the 2.5% NC + 12.5% WGP mix maintained a flow diameter of 725 mm and T500 of 2.6 s, confirming that WGP effectively counterbalanced the viscosity increase induced by NC.

The V-funnel flow times ranged from 6.8 to 9.3 s, all within EFNARC limits (≤ 12 s). The reference mix recorded 7.1 s, while increasing NC content raised the time to 9.1 s at 10% NC due to the higher water demand of the nano-particles and their tendency to form a denser internal network. Conversely, WGP slightly decreased the V-funnel time to 6.8 s, consistent with its spherical morphology and filler effect that enhance flow continuity. The hybrid 2.5% NC + 12.5% WGP mix exhibited a balanced flow time (7.2 s), indicating optimized viscosity and internal stability suitable for SCC placement without segregation or bleeding.

The L-box ratios (H₂/H₁) varied between 0.81 and 0.90, demonstrating adequate passing ability for all mixtures (EFNARC recommends ≥ 0.8). The reference and WGP-only mixtures achieved the highest ratios (0.89–0.90), while increasing NC content slightly reduced passing ability to 0.82 at 10% NC. This reduction is linked to increased cohesiveness and lower paste fluidity, which limit aggregate mobility through reinforcement bars. Nevertheless, all hybrid mixes maintained L-box ratios above 0.85, confirming that the combined use of WGP and moderate NC levels does not hinder self-compaction capability.

These results confirm that both WGP and NC can be successfully used in SCC without compromising its self-compacting behavior when properly dosed. WGP improves flowability through its filler and lubricating effects, whereas NC increases cohesiveness and viscosity, enhancing segregation resistance. The 2.5% NC + 12.5% WGP hybrid mix achieved the most balanced performance—exhibiting high flowability, adequate viscosity, and strong passing ability—thus satisfying the essential attributes of SCC: filling ability, passing ability, and resistance to segregation. These findings align with the observations of Kong et al.35 and Dahamm et al.36, who reported similar rheological trends in SCC incorporating nano-additives and glass powders.

Mechanical properties

Figure 5a, b, and c present the compressive strength results of all SCC mixes incorporating various proportions of NC and WGP. The hybrid mix with 2.5% NC and 12.5% WGP exhibited the highest compressive strength (80.6 MPa), significantly outperforming the reference mix (62.0 MPa). All mixes showed strength enhancement relative to the control, except for those with 10% NC (58.8 MPa) and 10% NC + 5% WGP (60.28 MPa), which exhibited strength reductions likely due to excessive NC content. Similarly, Fig. 5.b shows that the modulus of elasticity followed a comparable trend. The 2.5% NC + 12.5% WGP mix exhibited the highest stiffness, while the 10% NC mix recorded the lowest. These trends confirm that moderate replacement levels can enhance mechanical properties, whereas higher NC content leads to performance degradation. The superior performance of the 2.5% NC + 12.5% WGP mix can be attributed to a synergistic combination of physical densification and chemical reactivity mechanisms.

The fine particle sizes of both NC and WGP contribute to improved packing density. This reduces internal porosity, strengthens the cement paste matrix, and minimizes weak ITZs, which are typically responsible for early-age cracking and strength loss in cementitious composites36. WGP, rich in amorphous silica, reacts with calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂) released during cement hydration to produce secondary calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel, which enhances strength and durability37. Similarly, NC contributes to the formation of C–S–H through its high surface area and pozzolanic reactivity. Their combined use amplifies these secondary hydration reactions, leading to a denser, stronger matrix. The selected hybrid dosage (2.5% NC + 12.5% WGP) lies within the optimal range for both dispersion and reactivity. At this level, NC particles are well distributed, minimizing agglomeration, while WGP provides sufficient pozzolanic effect without excessively diluting the cementitious content. In contrast, higher nano content led to particle agglomeration, increased porosity, and weak zones, ultimately reducing mechanical performance38.

Liu et al.39 demonstrated the strength-enhancing role of WGP due to its filler effect and pozzolanic reactivity. Divekar et al.40 reported improvements in pore structure and C–S–H gel development with small additions of NC. Other researchers showed that nano additives improve mechanical performance only when uniformly dispersed, consistent with the observed strength reductions in high NC content mixes41.

Figure 5.c displays the splitting tensile strength values. The highest value (4.64 MPa) was achieved by the 5% NC mix, followed closely by the 2.5% NC + 12.5% WGP mix (4.6 MPa). The lowest tensile strength (2.8 MPa) was recorded for the 10% NC mix, further highlighting the negative impact of excessive nanoclay due to poor dispersion and weak interfacial bonding. Figure 5.d and e illustrate linear correlations between compressive strength and both splitting tensile strength and modulus of elasticity, with high coefficients of determination (R² = 0.84 and R² = 0.89, respectively). These strong correlations confirm that the incorporation of fine supplementary materials like NC and WGP consistently influences the mechanical performance of SCC. As detailed in Table 1, the high silica content in WGP promotes strong pozzolanic reactions, particularly when finely ground. NC not only contributes additional silica but also alumina (Al₂O₃) and trace oxides like Fe₂O₃ and CaO. Collectively, these chemical and physical enhancements contribute to improved compressive, tensile, and elastic behavior, reinforcing the effectiveness of this hybrid sustainable binder system.

Load-deflection of tested beams

All displacement or deflection calculations were performed using the GOM software. Figure 6 illustrates the load–deflection responses of the reinforced SCC beams tested under four-point bending. The behavior was analyzed across three groups representing different binder compositions: WGP-only (Group A), NC-only (Group B), and combined NC–WGP blends (Group C).

Group A—effect of WGP

Figure 6a compares the load–deflection curves of the reference beam (0% NC–0% WGP) and the beam containing 15% WGP. The inclusion of WGP increased the ultimate load capacity from 60 kN to 66 kN, a 10% enhancement. The corresponding mid-span deflection also increased slightly from 10.78 mm to 14.5 mm. This improvement aligns with previous findings8, which reported similar increases in ultimate strength due to WGP’s pozzolanic reactivity and micro-filling effects. The modified matrix likely exhibited improved internal cohesion, enabling better crack bridging and post-cracking behavior.

Group B—effect of NC

Figure 6b presents the load–deflection responses of beams incorporating nano-clay (NC) at varying replacement levels. Beams with 2.5% and 5% NC showed moderate improvements in flexural capacity. The 5% NC beam achieved a peak load of 70 kN, a 16.6% increase over the reference. The 2.5% NC beam followed closely with a 13.3% improvement.

The increasing NC content to 7.5% caused no substantial gain, but at 10%, a performance drop was observed. The 10% NC beam failed at a deflection of 6.85 mm and a reduced load of 40 kN, indicating brittle behavior. The degradation is attributed to excessive NC causing particle agglomeration, poor dispersion, and weakened ITZs, consistent with prior studies37.

Group C—combined effect of NC and WGP

Figure 6c depicts the load–deflection behavior of beams with combined NC and WGP replacements. The beam with 12.5% WGP and 2.5% NC recorded a deflection of 14.85 mm and the highest ultimate load of 74 kN, marking a 12% increase in strength over the 15% WGP-only beam. This suggests a synergistic interaction between the nano and pozzolanic additives, enhancing both strength and energy dissipation capacity.

While the increase in load was significant, the marginal rise in deflection suggests a slightly more brittle failure mode. This could be due to the stiffening effect of NC in the matrix, which redistributes internal stresses and modifies the post-cracking response. As NC content increased beyond 2.5%, a gradual reduction in performance was observed. The 10% NC + 5% WGP beam exhibited the lowest strength in Group C, confirming that high NC content, even in hybrid blends, compromises load-bearing capacity due to agglomeration-induced defects.

Across all groups, the load–deflection responses confirm that optimal flexural behavior was achieved with moderate NC and WGP combinations, particularly the 2.5% NC + 12.5% WGP mix. Excessive nano-clay content consistently led to reduced performance regardless of WGP presence, reinforcing the need for dosage optimization when using reactive nanomaterials in SCC systems.

Load- strain relations

Figure 7 presents the load–strain relationships at mid-span for all tested beams, capturing the behavior of both tensile and compressive zones. The strain data were obtained using DIC, with three virtual extensometers positioned on each beam. The strain values represent the average of these extensometers in both zones. Compressive strains are denoted as negative, while tensile strains are positive. In all cases, post-cracking behavior dominates the strain development.

Group A—effect of WGP

As shown in Fig. 7a, incorporating 15% WGP in the SCC matrix led to noticeable increases in strain. The maximum tensile strain increased by 12.2%, while the compressive strain increased by 2.7% compared to the reference beam.

This suggests that WGP improved the deformability of the concrete, primarily through microstructural densification and secondary pozzolanic reactions. The amorphous silica in finely ground WGP reacts with the Ca(OH)₂ released during cement hydration to form additional C–S–H gel3,39. A denser microstructure delays crack initiation and slows the coalescence of micro-cracks under tensile stress, enabling the material to accommodate higher strain before failure37,39.

Also, the fine particle size of WGP enhance the packing density and lubrication of the cement matrix, improving interfacial bonding between paste and aggregate. These effects collectively enhance energy absorption and load redistribution capacity during bending, as reflected in the higher tensile and compressive strain values recorded for the WGP mixture. Similar trends were reported by Cai et al.3, Liu et al.39, and Chu et al.5, who observed that WGP refines the ITZs and enhances micro-crack resistance through pozzolanic densification. Consequently, the improved deformability of the 15% WGP beam in this study can be attributed to these synergistic physical and chemical effects, which lead to a more ductile strain response and better crack control, as later confirmed by the DIC crack pattern analysis in Sect. 3.6.1.

Group B—effect of NC

The influence of nano-clay on strain behavior is illustrated in Fig. 7b. Moderate NC content (2.5% and 5%) significantly enhanced strain capacity. The 2.5% NC beam exhibited a 14.8% increase in compressive strain and a 38% increase in tensile strain, indicating improved energy absorption and post-cracking ductility. The 5% NC mix followed with 15.2% and 17% increases in compressive and tensile strains, respectively.

At 7.5% NC, strain gains diminished, and at 10% NC, a marked reduction in tensile strain (− 44%) was observed, returning compressive strain to values comparable with the reference. This reversal highlights the adverse effect of NC agglomeration at higher dosages, which impairs crack resistance and strain accommodation by introducing stiffness heterogeneity and micro-defects.

Group C—combined effect of NC and WGP

Figure 7c displays the strain responses of beams with combined NC and WGP replacements. The 2.5% NC + 12.5% WGP mix again exhibited the best performance, with a 10% increase in compressive strain and a 26% rise in tensile strain relative to the WGP-only beam. These enhancements reflect a synergistic effect between the finely dispersed NC and the pozzolanic WGP in densifying the matrix and distributing tensile stresses more effectively.

The 5% NC + 10% WGP, 7.5% NC + 7.5% WGP, and 10% NC + 5% WGP mixes showed reductions in compressive strain by 5.0%, 12.3%, and 37.3%, respectively, compared to the 15% WGP beam. Corresponding tensile strain reductions were 10.9%, 30.4%, and 38%, confirming that excessive nano-clay negatively impacts deformation capacity even in the presence of WGP.

In all cases, the load–strain curves remained nearly identical up to approximately 30 kN, indicating elastic behavior. At around 25% of each beam’s ultimate capacity, initial flexural cracks appeared. As the load increased to approximately 40% of the ultimate value, crack propagation stabilized, forming short vertical cracks beneath the neutral axis. The observed strain trends underscore that optimal NC dosages (2.5–5%) can effectively enhance concrete ductility, while higher levels (≥ 10%) reduce strain capacity due to structural stiffening and internal microcracking.

The particle size analysis confirms that both NC and WGP possess adequate fineness and reactivity for use as pozzolanic additives in self-compacting concrete. The NC particles were below 100 μm, enhancing the filler effect and matrix cohesion, while the WGP exhibited a median size of ≈ 18 μm with 90% finer than 45 μm, meeting ASTM C618 and C1240 limits. Their fine and amorphous nature promotes a strong pozzolanic reaction with Ca(OH)₂, forming additional C–S–H gel and refining the pore structure, which collectively enhance matrix densification and mechanical performance.

Mode of failure, Deflection profile, and moment-curvature relationship

Mode of failure

The mode of failure and crack propagation patterns of the tested beams were evaluated using DIC, which allowed a full-field visualization of strain and crack development. As shown in Fig. 10, most beams failed in a classic flexural mode, characterized by vertical cracks initiating at the mid-span and propagating upward toward the compression zone.

The reference beam (0% NC and 0% WGP) (Fig. 10a) exhibited typical flexural failure characterized by a dominant vertical crack at mid-span, indicating limited resistance to tensile micro-cracking.

Beams incorporating NC at 2.5%, 5%, and 7.5% (Fig. 10b–d) displayed enhanced crack distribution compared to the reference. The 2.5% NC mix showed multiple fine cracks with reduced width, indicating improved tensile strain capacity and microstructural densification due to pozzolanic activity. With increasing NC content to 7.5%, a more refined crack pattern was observed, though the cracks remained mostly vertical, suggesting that nano clay improved crack resistance without significantly altering the failure mechanism. While, at 10% NC (Fig. 10e), the crack pattern became less refined with localized wide cracks, which can be attributed to excessive NC content leading to reduced workability and poor dispersion, potentially creating weak zones.

The beam with 15% WGP (Fig. 10f) also demonstrated improved crack distribution compared to the reference, with smaller and more uniformly spaced cracks. This can be linked to the pozzolanic reactivity and filler effect of glass powder, which contribute to a denser matrix and better stress transfer. Nonetheless, some brittleness in crack growth was noted, indicating that while WGP enhances compressive performance, it may have limited benefits in controlling tensile crack width alone.

The blended mixes with combined NC and WGP contents (Fig. 10g–j) exhibited the most favorable crack patterns. For example, the 2.5% NC + 12.5% WGP mix (Fig. 10g) showed a highly dispersed microcrack network with minimized crack width and delayed propagation, indicating enhanced ductility and stress redistribution. This confirms a synergistic effect where nano clay enhances the interface bonding and WGP refines the pore structure. At moderate combinations (e.g., 5% NC + 10% WGP, Fig. 10h), the distribution remained favorable, though slightly less optimal than the 2.5% NC + 12.5% WGP blend. Higher NC content (e.g., 10% NC + 5% WGP, Fig. 10j) resulted in less effective crack control, likely due to agglomeration effects and reduced workability.

Overall, the DIC analysis confirms that: NC up to 7.5% improves microcracking behavior by densifying the matrix and enhancing tensile strain capacity. WGP enhances crack dispersion through its filler effect but may not alone control crack width effectively. Finally, optimal combinations, particularly 2.5% NC + 12.5% WGP, yield the most favorable failure mode with distributed fine cracks and delayed localization, indicative of improved flexural toughness and post-cracking behavior.

Locations with concentrated deflection may correspond to crack initiation or failure zones. Finally, differences help improve modeling assumptions or highlight the effects of material modifications (NC and WGP) as in presented study. The deflection profiles of the beams at ultimate load were constructed using DIC data recorded at mid-span and at positions under the applied loads. Deflection profile for tested beams.

The 15% WGP beam showed a 34.5% increase in maximum mid-span deflection compared to the reference, with a more symmetrical and uniform deflection shape, indicating improved deformability and balanced load transfer. Regarding to effect of NC, increasing NC up to 7.5% resulted in increased deflections at mid-span 30.6%, 29.3%, and 15.96% for 2.5%, 5.0%, and 7.5% NC, respectively relative to the control. Conclusions
