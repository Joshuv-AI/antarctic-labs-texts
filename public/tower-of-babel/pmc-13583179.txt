Evaluation of Ambient Air Particulate Matter, Urine Arsenic Levels and the Effect on Residents Health in an Artisanal Mining Community and an Incorporated Mining Community in Ghana From April 2024 to July 2024: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Nafisa Akua Assan, Eric N. Y. Nyarko, Justice Kumi, Emmanuel K. Ofori, Alberta Dzifa Dayie, Derrick N. D. Doodo, Maxwell Seyram Sunu, Aliu Issahaku, Isaac Kpabitey Lawer, Christian Obirikorang, Henry Asare‐Anane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimOver 200 million people in developing and industrialized countries are exposed to arsenic poisoning. Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) constitutes a significant air contaminant, especially in urban and industrial suburbs. A significant proportion of countries do not possess sufficient ambient air quality guidelines for particulate matter pollution, which has the potential for toxicity, including renal toxicity. The health risk of pollution to inhabitants of a mining area is of much concern. The Ghanaian mining sector plays a significant role in the economy, being the largest foreign exchange earner. This study assessed ambient air quality, general health status and biochemical indices of the kidney that might be due to poisoning from mining pollutants such as Arsenic.Materials and MethodsThe study recruited 65 participants, each from artisanal and incorporated mining companies, who have lived in the community for a minimum of 5 years since the mining activity began. The kidney function of the participants was assessed using urinary Albumin Creatinine Ratio (uACR), whiles the Particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10) in air was measured using the Modular Air Quality System (MAQS). Urine Arsenic levels were measured using an atomic absorption spectrophotometer (AAS). A line graph was used to illustrate the trend in PM levels whilst Chi‐square analysis was used to compare the general health status, and risk of kidney disease.Results and DiscussionPM2.5 levels in the artisanal mining community exceeded the WHO standard of 35 µg/m3, while PM10 levels in the incorporated mining community were above the WHO standard of 70 µg/m3. More individuals in the artisanal mining community were at risk of kidney disease (p = 0.006) compared to the incorporated mining community.ConclusionThe inhabitants of the artisanal mining community are at a higher risk of air pollution related disorders and kidney diseases whereas those in the incorporated mining community exhibit poorer general health status.

Introduction

Globally, PM air pollutants (especially PM2.5) are major public health hazards, causing an estimated 4.2 million premature deaths each year [1]. About 7.3 billion individuals are subjected annually to hazardous PM2.5 levels, predominantly in low‐ and middle‐income nations, including Ghana [2]. A study of air quality in Tarkwa (Western region of Ghana) showed values significantly exceeding permissible limits for human health [3]. Arsenic is employed in gold mining because it concentrates gold into deposits by binding with gold in mineral structures, making gold more easily extracted and processed.

Arsenic exposure to humans is related to a variety of negative health effects in mining areas [4, 5, 6]. Approximately 140 million individuals across at least 70 nations are exposed to high levels of arsenic poisoning exceeding the WHO provisional recommendation of 10 μg/L [7, 8]. In West Africa (Burkina Faso), a significant fraction of rural boreholes (14.6%) had arsenic levels that exceed the recommended limit [9]. The mineral‐rich terrain of Ghana is marked by substantial gold reserves, predominantly located in towns like Obuasi, Tarkwa, and Prestea [10]. Mining activities, whether artisanal or incorporated, are important contributors to global economic development [11, 12, 13]. However, it is also noted for presenting substantial environmental and public health hazards [14, 15, 16]. Mining activities can expose communities to harmful pollutants, including Arsenic (As), particulate matter 2.5 (PM2.5) and particulate matter 10 (PM10) pollutants. Particulate matter (PM) can penetrate deep into the lungs and bloodstream, causing a range of health problems [17, 18]. Arsenic enters the environment through crushing, smelting and disposal of arsenic‐rich ores (arsenopyrite), releasing it into air, water, and soil. Arsenic can be released as dust and fine particles (PM‐ bound As) which is carried away by the wind. Moreover, the release of As and other toxic heavy metals to the environment breeds an alarming health concern for both humans and animals [19, 20, 21].

Elevated levels of arsenic in humans can have serious implications, such as gastroenteritis, dermatological alterations, and an increased risk of cancer after prolonged exposure [22]. Studies indicate a dose‐response association between arsenic exposure and albuminuria; high arsenic levels are related to increased albuminuria [22]. Although the total death rate due to unintentional poisoning in Ghana is about 1.7 per 100,000 people [23], arsenic‐related ailments such as bronchitis, skin cancer, and other skin problems are on the rise. A study conducted by Dartey et al., (2013) in Ghana observed that urinary levels of As were higher in artisanal gold miners [24]. Epidemiologic studies also suggest that As is associated with chronic kidney diseases (CKD) [25, 26]. Women in Artisanal and Small‐Scale Gold Mining (ASGM) communities had relatively high urine As concentrations. Elevated concentrations correlated with heightened chances of low birth weight, preterm birth, and observable congenital anomalies in comparison to women residing in non‐ASGM communities, as indicated by a study conducted in Tanzania [27].

Although some studies [11, 12], have explored the negative impacts of ASGM on the environment and its inhabitants, few, if any have compared the different impacts of the major types of mining practices on the health of residents in these Ghanaian communities. Research has been conducted on heavy metal contamination of soil and water, along with associated health risks [28]; however, none has investigated ambient air quality and the health of residents in these two mining areas. (incorporated and artisanal). This study aims at identifying biological indices correlated with reported environmental and pathological findings among inhabitants in two mining towns. The novelty of this study is attributed to the lack of research reports that compare ecotoxicological and health indicators between residents of an incorporated mining community and those of an artisanal (illegal or ‘galamsey’) mining community in Ghana. In addition, studies on PM levels in the ambient air of these two mining communities, though necessary to their health, have not been explored. The findings will inform stakeholders about the health hazards that artisanal and incorporated mining communities confront, and support the development of intervention strategies.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

The study employed a comparative cross‐sectional quantitative study of two mining communities from April 2024 to July 2024. The two study sites were Abesewa Gyaman, located in the Wassa Amenfi East District of the Western Region (Figure 1A). The second district is Anyinam, situated in the Obuasi East district of the Ashanti Region (Figure 1) The study sites are in Ghana (Figure 1B). The two communities were chosen due to the active large‐scale mining in Anyinam and the Galamsey (artisanal mining) activities in Abesewa Gyaman in Ghana.

(A) Map Abesewa Gyaman, with coordinates 5° 49′ 7″ N, 2° 1′ 23″ W and Anyinam with coordinates 6° 10’ 53” N, 1° 40’ 18” W. (B) Map of Ghana.

The maps of the two communities were generated using QGIS Version 3.14.16‐Pi (QGIS Development Team, 2020. QGIS Geographic Information System. Open‐Source Geospatial Foundation Project. (http://qgis.org).

Study Population and Sampling Procedure

The two districts have a total population of 173,060, with 80,659 in Abesewa Gyaman and 92, 401 in Anyinam, respectively [29]. A total of 138 participants were recruited into the study by adopting the formula N = Z2 × P× (1‐P)/E2 as proposed by Pourhoseingholi [30]. Where N is the required sample size, Z is the z score at 1.96, P is 10% estimate of people exposed to Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) [31] and E is the margin of error at 5%. The 130 selected participants consisted of 65 from the artisanal mining community and 65 from the incorporated mining community. Eight [8] participants withdrew from the study.

Sampling

In Ghana, arsenic exposure contributes to chronic kidney disease (CKD), which affects 13.3% of the population. Additionally, air pollution and high particulate matter (PM) levels are primary risk factors for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), which impacts 10% of Ghanaians [30, 31]. For the minimum sample size, the 10% for the population likely exposed to COPD as a result of air pollution was chosen, considering also the cost of a larger sample size and the responsiveness of the inhabitants, a convenience sampling was used for each mining community depending on the participants location, willingness to participate and provide urine sample.

Participants aged 18 to 60 years who had lived in the mining community for at least 5 years (without travelling out to live elsewhere for more than 3 months) since the mining activities began were included. Exclusion criteria included participants who had spent a month or more nights consecutively outside the study sites, those who had consumed sea food within 72‐h prior to data/specimen collection and individuals with a known history of kidney dysfunction. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethical and Protocol Review Committee of the College of Health Sciences, University of Ghana.

Data and Specimen Collection

Participants data and demographic information were collected via a one‐on‐one interview using a standard structured questionnaire. Anthropometric data such as age, sex, weight and height were measured and recorded. Information on the consecutive number of days spent outside the mining communities was recorded to rule out any contribution of ambient air pollution from other communities after prolonged stay. Data on sea food intake for the past 72 h prior to the data collection was also recorded to rule out the contributions to arsenic intake through sea food organic arsenic. Thirty millilitres (30 mL) of spot urine was provided by each participant in two separate sterile containers. The urine sample for the albumin and creatinine analysis was stored between 2°C and 8°C until analysis while the sample for the urine arsenic analysis was stored under −20°C until analysed.

Ambient Air Particulate Matter Measurement

The 24‐h particulate matter (PM) levels in the air of the two mining communities were measured using the Modular Air Quality System for 7 weeks. Mean values for each week were estimated and recorded. The ambient air particle was measured by laser scattering based on Gustav Mie particle light scattering theory with Micro‐Photoelectric detection technology to produce a complete set of air particle distribution concentration measurement system, as described by Yu et al., (2017) [32].

Urine Arsenic Measurement

The urine arsenic concentration was determined by Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometry (AAS) [33]. A 0.5 mL of urine was measured and transferred into a digestion flask. A ternary mixture of analytical grade nitric acid (HNO3), perchloric acid (HClO4), and sulfuric acid (H2SO4) was added (10 mL; 1:1:2). The sample was then digested using a block digester (Behrotest® K8) at 150oC.

for 60 min. The sample solution was allowed to cool in a fume hood, transferred into a 100 mL volumetric flask, and diluted to the mark with distilled water. Blank analysis was done following the same procedure employed for sample digestion without test samples. Before sample analysis, the atomic absorption spectrometer was calibrated using a series of calibration standards; As (1, 5, and 10 ppb). Calibration standards were prepared from a working standard solution of 100 ppm derived from a 1000 ppm stock solution.

Levels of As in urine acid digestates were determined using acetylene gas (C2H2) as fuel and compressed air as an oxidant. The acid digestates were introduced into the spectrometer by suction into the AAS (Perkin Elmer®, PinAAcle 900 T), and the concentrations were measured and recorded. Analysis was done in triplicates, and the mean concentrations were expressed in units of µg/L.

Urine Albumin and Creatinine Measurement, and Calculation of Urine Albumin Creatinine Ratio (uACR)

Urine albumin and urine creatinine were analysed with the Bromocresol green method and the kinetic methods, respectively, as reported by [34, 35]. The Beer Lambert principle was used in calibrating the semi‐automated URIT810 chemistry analyzer (URIT, China 2020), allowing for the quantification of urine albumin. The Beer Lambert law is expressed as: Albumin(g/L)=AbsorbanceofTestAbsorbanceofStandard×55

To measure the urine creatinine concentration, a 1:50 dilution of each urine sample was prepared by adding 1 part of the urine sample to 49 parts of distilled water. The urine creatinine was measured with the Jafe's modified technique using the semi‐automated URIT810 Chemistry analyser (URIT, China, 2020). The urine creatinine concentration was calculated as follows: Creatinine concentration in urine(mg/dL)=ΔASampleΔStandard×100

The result obtained was then multiplied by the dilution factor of 50 to obtain the concentration of creatinine in the urine of the test participant.

The urine albumin creatinine ratio (uACR) was estimated as described by the National Kidney Foundation (US) using the formula Albumin(mg/dl)Creatinine(g/dl)×1000and categorized into low risk (< 30), moderate to high risk (30–300) and severe risk (> 300).

Statistical Analyses

SPSS version 27 and Microsoft Excel 2019 were used for data analysis. Chi‐square was used to compare the differences in the sociodemographic characteristics between the 2 groups. Using linear regression analysis, the average levels of air particulate matter over seven [7] weeks were illustrated with line graphs. Descriptive and inferential statistics (mean) were used to analyze the body mass index (BMI), Arsenic concentration, urine albumin and urine creatinine levels. The albumin creatinine ratio of the two communities was compared using Chi‐Square. The independent sample t‐test was used to compare the difference in the BMI, urinary albumin, urine creatinine, urine arsenice and the uACR of the two communities. Certain individuals who might contribute confounding factors due to their dietary or health status were excluded during the analysis of the data.

Results

PM Trends for the Two Communities

The averages of the weekly measurements were plotted using a line graph to study the trend of the PM values (Figures 2 and 3). In the Artisanal mining community, the PM 2.5 values were consistently above the WHO recommended limit of 35.0 µg/m3 for a 24‐h measurement. The highest value recorded at 42.3 µg/m3 and the lowest value at 37.2 µg/m3. PM10 values observed in the incorporated mining community over the 7‐week period were also above the WHO recommended value of 70.0 µg/m3. The highest 24‐h PM10 value measured in the incorporated mining community was 81.0 µg/m3 and the lowest was 73.6 µg/m3 (Figure 3).

Also, more individuals in the incorporated mining community reported blurred vision, (n = 22 vs 9, p = 0.007), diarrhoea (n = 8 vs 1, p = 0.016), muscle cramps, (13 vs 5, p0.042) and pain in the loins (10 vs 3, p = 0.041). There was no statistically significant difference in response rates in the number of people who experienced respiratory symptoms like cough, chest pain, nausea and vomiting, abdominal pain, shortness of breath and itching between the two communities (Table 2).

Discussions

This study sought to compare the ambient air particulate matter (PM), selected health indices of residents, urinary arsenic levels and albumin‐creatinine ratio of residents in an artisanal (galamsey) mining community and an incorporated (large) scale mining community in Ghana. The PM10 values observed in the artisanal mining community were within the WHO limits (≤ 35 µ/gm3), while those recorded in the incorporated mining community were above the upper WHO limit. These findings (i.e. PM10) are contrary to findings in artisanal community in Nigeria, where the average PM10 value measured was above 330 µ/gm3 [36]. Mining activities involved blasting, crushing and conveying of ores and rocks. This released dust and other fumes into the atmosphere which were the common sources of course particles (including PM10.0), and fine particles (such as PM2.5) [11, 34]. The PM2.5 levels in the artisanal mining community was comparatively higher than in the incorporated one, possibly because the active mining is farther from the residential areas of the community where these measurements were taken. Other driving forces of particulate matter pollution included agricultural practices, vehicular fumes, and weather variables such as temperature [37] – which were not assessed in this study. Due to their small size, the PM2.5 particles travel further compared to PM10. PM2.5 particles suspend in the air for longer periods, allowing winds to blow them even farther from their exuding territories. On the other hand, fine particulate matter in the air typically decreases because rainfall acts as a cleansing mechanism, and in this study, there were some rainfalls within the period of measurement. However, a study by Wang et al (2023) [38] reported that the air humidity threshold influences particulate matter levels. He further stated that, above a certain threshold, the concentration of particulate matter was reduced as humidity increased. However, below that threshold, the concentration of particulate matter increased with humidity [39]. The primary cause of inconsistency in estimating pollution levels when precipitation occurs is the wide range of variations in rainfall intensity. Fluctuating humidity, and other pollutant characteristics [39], could also be a possible factor influencing PM measurement recorded in the current study.

General Well‐Being of the Communities

From the current studies, more persons in the incorporated mining community were fatigued (n = 19 vs 3). The finding could be associated with their closeness to the active incorporated mining site, which increases their exposure to dust particles and pollutants more often as compared to the inhabitants of the artisanal mining community. Previous literature identified associations between air pollutants and chronic stress, stress resilience, depressive mood and fatigue [40, 41, 42]. Experts in mining ergonomics frequently mention poor illumination, heat, exposure to heavy metals, and other ergonomic concerns in mining communities [43, 44]. The number of people who had diarrhoea in the two mining communities was statistically significant (p = 0.001) with more people in the incorporated mining community having frequent diarrhoea. Consumption of untreated and non‐potable water might contribute to the resident's diarrhoea [45]. Another source of concern is the leaching of heavy metal oxides into water bodies in communities that rely on such resources for drinking [46]. Yeboah et al (2008), identified occurrences of diarrhoea among residents of mining settlements attributable to contaminated water [47]. The communities in the study area probably depended on polluted water from mining activities which resulted in an incident of diarrhoea cases [47].

Equally, participants in the incorporated mining community experienced muscle cramps often than those in the artisanal community (p = 0.042). Paraesthesia, pain and numbness in the soles of the feet due to reduced ability of neurons to detoxify reactive oxygen species. This causes reduced glutathione production commonly presenting as pain and numbness in the soles of the feet, a clinical manifestations of sensory nerve arsenic poisoning [48]. However, arsenic poisoning in our current study, reported urinary As levels, statistically the same in both communities (p > 0.05) (Table 2) The prevalence of blurred vision was significantly higher in the incorporated mining community than in the artisanal mining community (p = 0.007). This disparity may be attributed to frequent ocular exposure to atmospheric pollutants and th e deposition of particulate matter, including PM2.5 and PM10.0, on the eye surface [49, 50]. Several symptoms differed significantly between the communities; however, the number of individual symptom comparisons increases the possibility of chance findings (type I error). These findings should therefore be considered exploratory and interpreted cautiously, particularly because the study was cross‐sectional and urinary arsenic concentrations did not differ significantly between the groups.

Urine Biochemistry and Risk of Kidney Failure

Arsenic concentrations measured in the urine of participants from the two mining communities were below the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR) allowable levels of 50 µg/L. Contrary to our findings, a high level of urinary As concentration was found in people living around an abandoned mining site in India [51]. In addition, exposure, toxicological and health impact of mining activities, including the impact of heavy metals and mercury have been studied [52, 53] elsewhere. However, further studies should look not only in urine, but also in blood and other tissues, as inorganic arsenic can accumulate in vital organs in the body, reducing their clearance into urine. This study found that the uACR of the two communities were significantly different (p = 0.006). More persons in the artisanal community were at moderate‐high risk of kidney failure, compared with those in the incorporated mining community. Although no similar study was found to compare the risk of renal pathology among the two major types of mining – incorporated and artisanal, in Ghana, research by Okoye et al., (2022) [52], found an increased risk of Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) among a population living in and around oil and gas mining community. Similarly, a study by Rodriguez‐Villamizar et al., (2023) [53] revealed that populations in gold mining environments had a reduced estimated Glomerular filtration rates (eGFR), which equally represents a higher risk of kidney diseases, especially CKDs. Also, a systematic review by Rasking et al., (2023) [54], reported that 90% of communities with high PM2.5 levels have increased rates of renal dysfunction among their populations [55]. Other studies [56, 57] have found a direct association between prolonged air pollution (including PM and As) exposure and elevated risks of both acute renal dysfunction (ARD) and ARD‐related deaths. This is especially alarming, as the residents of these mining areas are constantly subjected to unsafe amounts of air pollution. The significant difference in uACR risk‐category distribution between the two communities indicates an observed difference in kidney‐risk markers; however, this finding does not establish causality. In particular, urinary arsenic concentrations were not significantly different between the communities. Because the study was cross‐sectional, temporality between mining‐related exposures and kidney outcomes cannot be established. Other environmental, occupational, behavioral or demographic factors may also contribute to the observed difference.

Limitations

Our current study is limited by the acute period of monitoring the air PM values, non‐seasonal monitoring and the lack of a control (non‐mining) residential community due to lack of funding.

Conclusion

In conclusion, PM2.5 levels were higher in the residential area of the artisanal mining community, while PM10 concentrations were comparatively higher in the incorporated mining community. Also, residents in the artisanal mining community faced greater risk of kidney failure than those in the incorporated mining community who had generally poorer health indices.
