Attention-aware differential learning for predicting peptide-MHC class I binding and T cell receptor recognition
Authors: Rui Niu, Jingwei Wang, Yanli Li, Jiren Zhou, Yang Guo, Xuequn Shang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe identification of neoantigens is crucial for advancing vaccines, diagnostics, and immunotherapies. Despite this importance, a fundamental question remains: how to model the presentation of neoantigens by major histocompatibility complex class I molecules and the recognition of the peptide-MHC-I (pMHC-I) complex by T cell receptors (TCRs). Accurate prediction of pMHC-I binding and TCR recognition remains a significant computational challenge in immunology due to intricate binding motifs and the long-tail distribution of known binding pairs in public databases. Here, we propose an attention-aware framework comprising TranspMHC for pMHC-I binding prediction and TransTCR for TCR-pMHC-I recognition prediction. Leveraging the attention mechanism, TranspMHC surpasses existing algorithms on independent datasets at both pan-specific and allele-specific levels. For TCR-pMHC-I recognition, TransTCR incorporates transfer learning and a differential learning strategy, demonstrating superior performance and enhanced generalization on independent datasets compared to existing methods. Furthermore, we identify key amino acids associated with binding motifs of peptides and TCRs that facilitate pMHC-I and TCR-pMHC-I binding, indicating the potential interpretability of our proposed framework.Keywords: attention mechanism, differential learning, T cell receptor, neoantigen, pMHC-I binding, TCR-pMHC-I recognition

Introduction

Neoantigens are endogenous tumor peptides presented by major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) molecules on the cell surface, eliciting immune responses from CD8+ T cells through recognition by their T cell receptors (TCRs) [1]. Neoantigens are selectively expressed in tumor cells and minimally expressed in normal cells, enabling them to induce immune responses unaffected by central and peripheral tolerance. This makes neoantigens ideal targets for personalized cancer immunotherapies and potential prognostic markers for tumor survival [2, 3]. Advances in cancer immunotherapies have led to clinical applications of adoptive T cell transfer therapies, such as tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) therapy and TCR-engineered T cells (TCR-Ts) therapy, improving patient survival rates for aggressive and previously untreatable cancers [4–6]. Primary methods for neoantigen selection, such as using liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) to identify presented peptides and employing tetramer-associated magnetic enrichment to validate TCR recognition of known peptide-MHC-I (pMHC-I) complexes, require abundant cellular material and are time-consuming and labor-intensive. Therefore, improving neoantigen prediction through computational methods would significantly enhance the effectiveness and reduce the cost of targeted immunotherapies.

Various computational approaches have been developed to predict antigen presentation and recognition processes, facilitating advancements in experimental applications [7]. Notably, these machine learning models were trained and tested on general pMHC-I datasets, rather than neoantigen-MHC-I datasets, due to the current lack of neoantigen-specific datasets. However, given that neoantigens are a subset of antigenic peptides, pMHC-I datasets may still provide valuable insights into neoantigen-related binding characteristics. These models primarily fall into two categories: allele- and pan-specific prediction. Allele-specific methods train models based on known peptides that bind to the same MHC-I allele or TCRs that recognize peptides presented by the same allele [8–10]. These trained models are then used to predict the binding likelihood of new pMHC-I or TCR-peptide pairs. Although these allele-specific methods can address some aspects of neoantigen presentation and recognition, their performance is limited by the extensive polymorphism of MHC-I alleles. Training and applying these models is complex, demanding high-quality binding data for each allele. As a result, developing models for alleles with sparse or absent binding data remains a significant challenge.

In contrast, pan-specific methods aim to predict pMHC-I and TCR-pMHC-I binding across diverse MHC-I alleles. For example, in pMHC-I binding prediction, NetMHCpan-4.0 utilizes a three-layer neural network to extract binding features from pMHC-I pairs and predict the binding affinity of novel pairs at a pan-specific level [11]. Similarly, ACME employs a deep neural network (DNN) with an attention module to improve binding predictions [12], while CapsNet-MHC leverages a capsule neural network to capture subtle local features of pMHC-I pairs [13]. MHCnuggets, using an long short-term memory (LSTM) architecture, initially trains on a single allele and then transfers this knowledge to rare alleles through transfer learning [14]. In the same vein, BigMHC uses bidirectional LSTM architectures to learn pMHC-I binding patterns [15]. Despite these advances, all of these methods primarily focus on local sequence patterns due to the inherent limitations of their model architectures, neglecting the influence of long-range dependencies within sequences. Recent transformer-based methods, such as TransPHLA and STMHCpan, have addressed these limitations by incorporating mechanisms that capture long-range dependencies more effectively [16, 17].

In TCR-pMHC-I recognition prediction, statistical approaches have advanced to cluster TCRs based on sequence similarity, identifying antigen-specific recognition patterns. Methods such as TCRdist and iSMART utilize pairwise Smith-Waterman alignment of TCR sequences to achieve greater clustering specificity, although scalability remains a challenge [18, 19]. To address this, algorithms like GIANA enable rapid clustering of large TCR cohorts [20]. However, due to the long-tail distribution of datasets, clustering methods tend to emphasize the majority class, often relegating less frequent TCRs to marginal categories and resulting in significant data loss. Recent advancements in TCR-pMHC-I recognition prediction underscore the importance of sequence dependencies. For example, ERGO and ERGO2, use parallel encoders composed of LSTM networks or autoencoders (AEs) to generate sequence embeddings and predict TCR-peptide binding through a multilayer perceptron (MLP) [21, 22]. DeepTCR utilizes a DNN-based variational AE to obtain TCR representations and can predict the binding of a TCR to specific pMHC-I complexes [23]. However, these approaches have limitations when dealing with rare or unseen samples. PanPep employs a meta-learning approach, first training a neural Turing machine-based meta-learner, which is then fine-tuned on a few TCR support sets for few-shot prediction [24]. Additionally, it applies disentanglement distillation for zero-shot prediction, which has proven highly effective for handling rare or novel samples. In contrast, pMTnet integrates a transfer learning strategy, combining a pre-trained AE and LSTM network to generate embeddings for TCR and pMHC-I sequences, using differential learning to further refine the model [25]. However, relying solely on differential learning can sometimes cause the model to focus too heavily on inter-sample differences, potentially at the expense of overall classification accuracy. While ERGO and PanPep operate independently of MHC-I alleles, focusing solely on peptide and TCR sequences, pMTnet includes specific MHC-I sequences as part of its input, enhancing its contextual understanding of the interactions.

Although the rapid development of these models has significantly advanced neoantigen identification research, several challenges need to be further focused. First, capturing binding motif information related to position and content is challenging. Peptide binding motifs are short amino acid patterns that enable binding to specific MHC-I types, while TCR binding motifs are critical amino acid sequences for binding specific pMHC-I. As illustrated in Fig. 1, we generated consensus-binding motifs for three types of MHC-I molecules and their pMHC-I complexes using the pLogo program [26]. These motifs were derived from pMHC-I and TCR-pMHC-I binding pairs curated from public datasets. In terms of the same length, ligands binding to the same MHC-I molecule or pMHC-I complex exhibit position-related consensus-binding motifs. However, models based on recurrence, convolution, or traditional attention mechanisms often struggle to capture complex dependencies within and between binding pairs, limiting their ability to fully understand binding motifs and learn robust sequence representations. Second, accurate prediction of TCR-pMHC-I binding remains a challenge due to the long-tail distribution of the training dataset collected from public datasets. In this dataset, a few pMHC-I complexes bind to a large set of TCRs, while most have limited binding TCRs (Fig. 2). An imbalanced dataset can result in a biased model that favors the majority class and performs poorly for the minority class. Given the high diversity of neoantigens, experimentally validated TCR-pMHC-I binding pairs are often underrepresented in the public datasets (Fig. 2). Consequently, enhancing the model’s generalization ability to predict TCRs binding to less common or novel pMHC-I complexes is essential for accurate TCR-pMHC-I binding prediction.

In this paper, we present an attention-aware framework consisting of TranspMHC for pMHC-I binding prediction and TransTCR for TCR-pMHC-I recognition. The core idea behind TranspMHC and TransTCR is to apply self-attention to pMHC-I and TCR-pMHC-I pairs, enabling the model to learn the content and positional characteristics of each immune sequence while capturing binding motif information across sequence pairs. In TransTCR, we integrate the pre-trained pMHC-I encoder from TranspMHC for feature embedding. Additionally, TransTCR employs a differential learning strategy at the pMHC-I-specific level by constructing two identical, weight-sharing models: one processes the binding TCR, while the other processes the non-binding TCR for the same pMHC-I complex. This strategy aims to distinguish between binding and non-binding TCRs for the same pMHC-I complex through comparison. We performed extensive experiments to demonstrate that differential learning significantly improves the predictive performance of TCR-pMHC-I binding by minimizing the influence of the long-tail distribution in the training dataset. Performance comparisons conducted on two independent, experimentally validated datasets verified that TranspMHC and TransTCR achieved better results than other existing models. Moreover, we showed that self-attention helps to learn important binding motifs and their positional preferences, providing interpretability for our proposed models.

Materials and methods

TranspMHC and TransTCR are staged models designed to recognize binding motifs of pMHC-I and TCR-pMHC-I pairs (Fig. 3). TranspMHC, trained with a weighted cross-entropy loss function, effectively captures binding motif information. TransTCR incorporates the pre-trained pMHC-I encoder from TranspMHC for feature embedding. To account for the long-tail distribution of the TCR-pMHC-I dataset and reliably capture binding motifs of binding TCRs, a differential learning strategy is applied at the pMHC-I-specific level to distinguish binding from non-binding TCRs. Following this approach, TransTCR outputs the binding percentile rank of TCR-pMHC-I pairs rather than direct predictions.

Architecture of TranspMHC and TransTCR for accurate pMHC-I binding and TCR-pMHC-I recognition prediction. The model integrates content embedding with positional embedding to generate comprehensive representations of raw immune sequences. In TranspMHC, a single-layer encoder extracts features specific to each immune data type, while an integrative encoder refines concatenated representations to capture binding motif information, which is then processed by a MLP classifier for final prediction. In TransTCR, pre-trained peptide, MHC-I, and integrative encoders are transferred from TranspMHC, allowing the concatenation of pMHC-I embeddings with TCR embeddings obtained from a single-layer encoder. These representations are further refined through an integrative encoder and classified with an MLP. TransTCR also incorporates differential learning to enhance training and employs percentile rank to produce the final TCR-pMHC-I binding recognition scores.

Datasets

For the pMHC-I datasets, we compiled a training dataset from the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB), accessed on 12 December 2023. IEDB compiles a comprehensive collection T cell epitopes across multiple species in the context of infectious disease, allergy, autoimmunity, and cancer. The independent testing dataset was collected from the in-house data of Mei et al. [9]. To guarantee the reliability of the datasets, the raw data for both the training and testing datasets underwent the following preprocessing steps. (1) Data selection: we focused exclusively on pMHC-I pairs from human species associated with cancer. (2) Data filtering: we limited the dataset to MHC-I sequences of up to 34 amino acids and peptides no longer than 20 amino acids. We also excluded sequences with lowercase or non-amino acid characters and removed duplicates, keeping only unique pMHC-I pairs. Finally, the training dataset includes a total of 193 952 pairs ( = 120,694, = 73,258), while the testing dataset includes 19 504 data pairs ( = 9,407, = 10,097).

For the TCR-pMHC-I datasets, the training dataset was curated from three well-known databases: McPAS-TCR, IEDB, and VDJdb [27–29]. An independent testing dataset was constructed by collecting 665 experimentally validated TCR-pMHC-I binding pairs (Supplementary Information). To ensure reliable datasets, the following preprocessing steps were applied to refine the raw data for both the training and testing datasets. (1) Data selection: we focused exclusively on TCR-pMHC-I binding pairs from human species associated with cancer. For the TCR sequences, we used the CDR3 region of the TCR chain, which is a key determinant of specificity in neoantigen recognition [30]. (2) Data filtering: we restricted the dataset to CDR3 sequences up to 27 amino acids in length and peptides no longer than 20 amino acids, as these represent the most biologically relevant scenarios. Additionally, we excluded TCR-pMHC-I sequences that contained lowercase letters or non-amino acid characters, and removed duplicates by retaining only unique TCR-pMHC-I pairs. (3) Non-binding data generation: Only a few dozen non-binding TCR-pMHC-I pairs could be collected from the databases, which is far from sufficient to meet the needs of model training. To create negative samples of non-binding pairs, we compiled a non-binding TCR set comprising 60 333 379 unactivated TCRs from 587 healthy donors [31]. Following our differential learning approach, for each TCR-pMHC-I binding pair, we generated three non-binding pairs by randomly selecting three TCRs from the non-binding TCR set for the corresponding pMHC-I. As a result, the training dataset consists of 132 860 data pairs ( = 33,215, = 99,645), while the testing dataset comprises 2660 data pairs ( = 665, = 1,995). Details on these data are shown in the Supplementary Table 1.

MLP classifier

Both TranspMHC and TransTCR employ an MLP classifier with two hidden layers to generate the model output. The numerical vectors derived from the integrative encoder are first passed through a layer of 256 neurons with ReLU activation and batch normalization. This is followed by a fully connected layer with 64 neurons, also activated by ReLU. The final layer consists of two neurons that apply a linear transformation, producing a two-dimensional output tensor representing the probabilities of binding and non-binding, respectively.

Results

Model training and parameter setting

Both TranspMHC and TransTCR models are implemented using the PyTorch library. In our experiments, we set the batch size to 1024 and trained the models for 20 epochs. The TranspMHC model was trained with the weighted cross-entropy loss function. For the TransTCR model with a differential learning approach, the margin and the regularization parameter for the differential loss function were set to 0.8 and 0.03, respectively. The contribution parameters and for the differential fusion loss function were both set to 1. We used the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001 to adjust the learning rates during training. To mitigate bias from dataset partitioning, we employed a five-fold cross-validation strategy throughout model evaluation. Specifically, the training dataset was randomly partitioned into five equal folds, with four-fifths serving as the training set and one-fifth as the validation set to assess the predictive performance of the TranspMHC and TransTCR models. Then, we used the best-performing model from the cross-validation to test the independent testing dataset. We evaluated the model performance using six common metrics: area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC), accuracy, F1-score, balanced accuracy, and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC). We further examined how the number of encoder layers, ranging from 1 to 3, affects model performance (Supplementary Figure 1). Our results revealed a non-linear relationship between layer number and performance. Given the increased training depth and computational cost with additional layers, we recommend a single-layer encoder as it offers an optimal balance between computational efficiency and performance.

Performance comparison of TranspMHC with other pMHC-I prediction models

To assess the performance of the proposed TranspMHC model, we conducted a comparison against seven widely used pan-specific models. These included the baseline model NetMHCpan-4.0 [11], recommended by IEDB, the attention-based ACME model [12], the LSTM-driven BigMHC model [15] and pMTnet [25], the capsule neural network-powered CapsNet-MHC [13], as well as two transformer-based models, TransPHLA [16] and STMHCpan [17]. It is important to note that not all methods are adaptable with peptides of every length. For instance, NetMHCpan, CapsNet-MHC, and STMHCpan are limited to peptides with a maximum length of 15 amino acids. In our experiments, we first conducted five-fold cross-validation for each model to minimize bias caused by randomness in the training dataset and to ensure robust model evaluation. After identifying the best-performing model through cross-validation, we then tested it on an independent testing dataset to accurately assess its true performance and generalization ability.

To demonstrate the impact of training dataset distribution on model performance, we visualized the five-fold cross-validation results in Fig. 4. The TranspMHC model consistently outperformed the seven comparison methods with a tighter distribution across six metrics, displaying stable performance across metrics. While BigMHC showed comparable results to TranspMHC on AUPRC, its performance declined across the other five metrics. As summarized in Table 1, the best-performing models from cross-validation were further evaluated on an independent testing set. TranspMHC achieved the highest performance in five metrics, with its balanced accuracy exceeding STMHCpan by 1.4%, despite a slight 0.2% decrease in accuracy. Compared to the baseline model NetMHCpan-4.0, TranspMHC improved AUROC by 6.1% and AUPRC by 7.0%. Additionally, it outperformed the next best model by 2.3% in AUROC, 1.5% in AUPRC, 1.4% in balanced accuracy, 0.7% in F1-score, and 1.5% in MCC. These results highlight TranspMHC’s ability to capture sequence content and positional dependencies, allowing it to more accurately identify binding motifs and enhance pMHC-I binding prediction on unseen datasets.

Performance comparison of TransTCR with other TCR-pMHC-I prediction models

To comprehensively assess the pan-specific performance of the TransTCR model, we compared it to four widely-used models and four custom-built models. These comprised the baseline models ERGO-AE and ERGO-LSTM [21], which utilize fundamental LSTM and AE architectures, the differential learning-based approach pMTnet [25], and the state-of-the-art model PanPep [24]. Notably, certain models, such as ERGO and PanPep, are designed specifically for TCR-peptide binding prediction and do not incorporate MHC-I molecule input, and overlook the role of MHC-I molecules that present the peptide. To facilitate a detailed comparison, we constructed four additional models. These included a transformer-based model, a NearMiss-based model, and a Cluster Centroid-based model, each mirroring a single branch architecture of TransTCR but without a pre-trained pMHC-I encoder. The NearMiss-based model applies the Near Miss undersampling technique to balance class distributions during training, while the Cluster Centroid-based model employs clustering of head pairs, using centroids to represent these pairs and achieve balanced distributions. Furthermore, a model with identical architecture to TransTCR, yet lacking the pre-trained pMHC-I encoder, was included to evaluate the impact of transfer learning on model performance. We conducted five-fold cross-validation for each model to reduce training dataset randomness and ensure robust evaluation. The best-performing model was then tested on an independent testing dataset to accurately assess its performance and generalization.

The five-fold cross-validation results are presented in Fig. 5, where the TransTCR model consistently outperformed all eight comparison models, demonstrating a tighter metric distribution and stable performance across six evaluation metrics. Although PanPep achieved comparable balanced accuracy to TransTCR, its performance declined on the other five metrics to varying degrees. Following cross-validation, the top-performing models were evaluated on an independent testing set, as summarized in Table 2. TransTCR surpassed baseline models ERGO-LSTM and ERGO-AE across all six metrics. Compared with the state-of-the-art PanPep, TransTCR achieved a 3% improvement in AUROC, 2.3% in accuracy, 6.4% in balanced accuracy, 5.3% in F1-score, and 4% in MCC, with only a minor 0.6% decrease in AUPRC. Additionally, TransTCR outperformed the next best model by 0.4% in AUROC, 2.2% in accuracy, 2.3% in balanced accuracy, 2.8% in F1-score, and 1.5% in MCC. These findings highlight the effectiveness of integrating self-attention mechanisms, transfer learning, and differential learning, which collectively enhance the model’s generalization in identifying binding motifs, thereby improving TCR-pMHC-I binding predictions on new datasets.

TranspMHC enables more accurate pMHC-I binding prediction at MHC-I allele-specific level

Current T cell therapy is MHC-dependent immunotherapy, and a practical limitation is that TCR-transfected T cells can only target peptides presented by specific MHC-I alleles [6]. After demonstrating the effectiveness of TranspMHC in predicting pMHC-I binding at a pan-specific level, we next compared its allele-specific performance against other models. We stratified the test dataset by MHC-I alleles, including HLA-A*02:01, HLA-A*01:01, HLA-A*24:02, HLA-B*08:01, HLA-B*18:01. In addition to the seven pan-specific models evaluated in Table 1, we included MHCnuggets [14], an allele-specific model using an LSTM architecture that transfers learning from common alleles to rare, less-studied ones. Paired t-tests were performed to assess the statistical significance of performance differences.

The performance comparisons for the five MHC-I alleles are summarized in Fig. 6. TranspMHC achieved the highest mean AUROC (0.868) and AUPRC (0.864) across models, significantly outperforming others in AUROC, except for CapsNet-MHC ( = 0.071). In terms of AUPRC, TranspMHC was superior to six models, with non-significant differences observed only for ACME ( = 0.097) and BigMHC ( = 0.060), both of which showed significant differences in AUROC. Notably, all models performed well on HLA-A*24:02 and HLA-B*18:01, while performance varied for HLA-A*01:01 and HLA-B*08:01. This variation correlates with allele frequency in the training dataset, where HLA-A*24:02 and HLA-B*18:01 were more common. These findings suggest that TranspMHC leverages binding motif information in addition to sequence data, maintaining robust performance across diverse MHC-I alleles.

Differential learning enhances generalizability of TransTCR

The TCR-pMHC-I training dataset displays a pronounced long-tail distribution, while the experimentally validated testing dataset often differs significantly from the majority class in the training set (Fig. 2). To evaluate the effectiveness of the differential learning strategy, we compared three categories of models: differential learning-based models, undersampling models, and a baseline model. The differential learning-based models included TransTCR and a similar model without the pre-trained pMHC-I encoder but otherwise sharing TransTCR’s architecture and training strategy. The undersampling models and the baseline model, which share the same architecture as a branch of TransTCR but lack a pre-trained pMHC-I encoder, were trained on the dataset using weighted cross-entropy loss without applying the differential learning strategy. As illustrated in Fig. 7, differential learning-based models outperformed both undersampling and baseline models across four key metrics. Specifically, the NearMiss model improved AUROC and AUPRC by 2.7% and 2.4%, respectively, while the Cluster Centroid model showed gains of 2.1% and 1.5% over the baseline. While undersampling techniques helped mitigate the long-tail distribution to some extent, performance was limited by the inherent data loss. In contrast, the differential learning strategy provided average improvements of 4.7% in AUROC and 4.2% in AUPRC over the baseline. TransTCR further enhanced AUROC by 2.7% through its pre-trained pMHC-I encoder. In summary, the differential learning strategy effectively captures binding motif-specific features, and the pre-trained pMHC-I encoder further refines feature embedding at the pMHC-I level, strengthening the differential learning approach for improved TCR-pMHC-I binding predictions.

TranspMHC and TransTCR capture key binding motif features of pMHC-I and TCR-pMHC-I pairs

We performed consensus-binding motif analyses to identify key binding motifs of peptide and TCR residues that facilitate pMHC-I and TCR-pMHC-I binding. For pMHC-I binding, three MHC-I alleles were selected from training dataset: HLA-A*02:01, HLA-A*24:02, and HLA-B*18:01 (Fig. 1A–I). The pMHC-I pairs corresponding to these alleles were selected from the testing dataset. TranspMHC was utilized to predict the binding likelihood of these pMHC-I pairs, preserving those with probabilities greater than 0.8 as binding pairs. These pairs were divided by MHC-I type and peptide length, and the pLogo program was used to visualize the binding motifs for each specific allele (Fig. 8A–I). For TCR-pMHC-I binding, we selected three pMHC-I complexes from training dataset, including HLA-A*03:01 (KLGGALQAK), HLA-B*07:02 (LPRRSGAAGA), and HLA-A*02:01 (GILGFVFTL) (Fig. 1J-R). However, due to the limited number of TCR-pMHC-I pairs in the testing dataset, generating consensus binding motifs was challenging. Therefore, we additionally collected a TCR cohort from the 10 Genomics immune profiling data, which includes 72 349 binding and non-binding TCRs [32]. For each pMHC-I complex, we combined it with the entire TCR cohort to construct TCR-pMHC-I set, which was then fed into TransTCR for binding prediction. The TCR-pMHC-I pairs with predicted binding percentile ranks less than 2% were preserved as binding pairs. The pLogo program was then used to visualize the binding motifs of these TCRs for each pMHC-I complex based on pMHC-I type and TCR length (Fig. 8J–R).

Predicted binding motifs and residue specificity for three MHC-I alleles and three pMHC-I complexes: (A–C) HLA-A*02:01 (8, 9, and 10 mers), (D–F) HLA-A*24:02 (8, 9, and 10 mers), (G–I) HLA-B*18:01 (8, 9, and 10 mers), (J–L) HLA-A*03:01 KLGGALQAK (13, 14, and 15 mers), (M–O) HLA-B*07:02 LPRRSGAAGA (13, 14, and 15 mers), and (P–R) HLA-A*02:01 GILGFVFTL (13, 14, and 15 mers). In each pLogo, the -axis represents log-odds binomial probability. Residues above the threshold line are statistically significant (), with the height indicating significance level. Overrepresented residues are shown relative to the human background.

As anticipated, the residues at both ends of the peptides, which made closer contact with the antigen-binding cleft of MHC-I molecules, were more likely to exhibit binding motif preferences. Specifically, peptides binding to HLA-A02:01 preferred Leu at position 1 (P1) or P2, and Leu/Val at the C-terminal (Figs 1A–C and 6A–C). In Fig. 8(A), the frequency of Leu at P1 was 65.3%, while Val and Leu at the C-terminal occurred at frequencies of 44.3% and 25.5%, respectively. For HLA-A24:02, the frequency of Tyr at P2 surpassed 70.0% (Figs 1D–F and 6D–F), with a preference for Phe at the C-terminal. For HLA-B*18:01, peptides preferred Asp at P1, Glu at P2, and Tyr/Phe at the C-terminal (Figs 1G–I and 8G–I). Our predicted amino acid preferences closely matched the actual data. Comparing Figs 1(G) and 8(G), the frequency of Asp at P1 was 32.3% and 24.5%, respectively, while the frequency of Glu at P2 was 96.4% and 86.5%, respectively. Additionally, the frequency of both Tyr and Phe at the C-terminal exceeded 20.0%.

For TCRs binding to pMHC-I complexes, as shown in Fig. 8J–R) the first four positions (Cys, Ala, Ser, Ser) and the last four positions (Glu/Thr, Gln, Tyr/Phe, Phe) of the TCR CDR3s exhibited high conservation scores, indicating the potential structural function of these segments. Additionally, residues in the middle segments of TCR CDR3s showed binding motif preferences, such as Gly at P6/P7/P8 and Asn at P8/P9. As shown in Fig. 8(K), the frequency of Gly at P7 was 38.8%, while that of Asn was P9 is 16.9%. This was consistent with prior knowledge that the middle segments of CDR3 bulged out and were in closer contact with pMHC-Is [25]. Furthermore, for the 13-mer TCRs binding to HLA-A*02:01 (GILGFVFTL), a binding motif preference for Arg at P6 and Ser at P7 was observed in the training dataset (Fig. 1P) with frequencies of 58.7% and 39.3%, respectively, which was also evident in Fig. 8(P) with frequencies of 33.4% and 31.2%. This illustrated the importance of this binding motif in predicting the binding between 13-mer TCRs and the HLA-A*02:01 (GILGFVFTL) complex. However, this preference for Arg and Ser in the middle segments is unique to 13-mer TCRs and was not observed in 14- and 15-mer TCRs that bound to the same pMHC-I complex. The results show that TranspMHC and TransTCR effectively captures binding and non-binding features of pMHC-I and TCR-pMHC-I pairs, transferring this knowledge to unseen datasets and identifying key binding motifs. This enables early-stage optimization of T cell-based immunotherapy by filtering peptides and TCRs that cannot be presented or recognized in vivo.

Discussion and conclusion

Understanding peptide presentation by MHC-I molecules and the binding of TCRs to pMHC-I complexes is fundamental for advancing T cell immunotherapies. In this study, we introduced TranspMHC, which utilizes a self-attention mechanism to predict pMHC-I binding, and based on this pre-trained pMHC-I encoder, we developed TransTCR for predicting TCR-pMHC-I recognition. TransTCR is specifically designed to capture distinctions between binding and non-binding TCRs at the pMHC-I-specific level through a differential training strategy. To our knowledge, TransTCR is the first model to integrate self-attention and differential learning to address pan-specific TCR-pMHC-I recognition.

Our evaluations reveal that both TranspMHC and TransTCR outperform existing methods in predicting pMHC-I and TCR-pMHC-I binding probabilities, especially on independent test datasets. Despite its pan-specific design, TranspMHC maintains robust performance at the allele-specific level. Ablation studies further confirm the effectiveness of the differential and transfer learning strategies in TransTCR: differential learning markedly enhances performance by distinguishing binding from non-binding TCRs, while transfer learning provides modest additional improvements. Consensus-binding motif analyses on external datasets show that predicted binding motifs align well with those in the training data, supporting the reliability of these models for predicting pMHC-I and TCR-pMHC-I binding.

The effectiveness of TranspMHC and TransTCR relies on their transformer-based immune sequence encoder and differential learning strategy, which collectively capture both intrinsic sequence information and position-dependent binding motifs during training. This framework is modular, with the potential for adaptation to other sequence binding-related bioinformatics challenges, particularly for identifying key binding motifs, thus offering a valuable platform for advancing immunotherapy design and optimization.
