Evaluating the relationship between standing height, body mass index, body fat percentage with risk of inguinal hernia: a Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Yuting Li, Baihui Li, Fanlei Meng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Observational studies have suggested links between standing height, BMI, body fat percentage, and inguinal hernia risk, but with inconsistent results. We conducted Mendelian randomization (MR) method to investigate their causal relationships. Independent SNPs associated with standing height, BMI, and body fat percentage were extracted from GWAS data as instrumental variables (IVs). Two-sample MR initially explored causal relationships between these factors and inguinal hernia risk. Multivariable Mendelian randomization (MVMR) assessed the direct causal effects of exposures on inguinal hernia. Positive findings from the MVMR analysis were independently validated. The inverse-variance weighted Methods (IVW), weighted median(WM), and MR-Egger were used for the MR estimates. Sensitivity analyses were used to examine the stability and reliability of the results. Two-sample MR analysis revealed a suggestive causal association between height and inguinal hernia (OR = 1.091, 95% CI = 1.003–1.186, P = 0.042). BMI (OR = 0.846, 95% CI = 0.774–0.924, P < 0.001) and body fat percentage (OR = 0.793, 95% CI = 0.690–0.911, P = 0.001) were significantly protective factors. After MVMR adjustment, BMI (OR = 0.746,95%CI = 0.638–0.872, P < 0.001) remained protective. Independent validation yielded consistent result. Genetically predicted BMI is a significant protective factor for inguinal hernia, providing valuable guidance for individualized prevention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-78122-x.

Introduction

Inguinal hernia is a prevalent condition, constituting approximately 75% of abdominal wall hernias. The incidence rate is higher in males than females, with a lifetime risk reaching up to 27%1. Clinical presentations of inguinal hernia include a palpable mass in the inguinal region, which may subside when intra-abdominal pressure decreases or in a supine position. Most individuals experience mild to moderate discomfort, and symptoms tend to worsen with increased physical activity. Inguinal hernia has resulted in significant healthcare resource consumption. According to statistics, approximately 20 million inguinal hernia repair surgeries are performed worldwide each year, with varying surgical rates across different countries ranging from 100 to 300 surgeries per 100,000 individuals. In the United Kingdom, around 100,000 cases of inguinal hernia patients undergo surgery annually2,3.Moreover, it has been estimated that hernias in the United States result in significant activity limitation for approximately 400,000 individuals, surpassing the number of lost workdays compared to any other chronic digestive condition4,5. Complications of inguinal hernia encompass incarceration, intestinal obstruction, and strangulation, posing potential life-threatening risks to health.

Previous research indicates that risk factors for inguinal hernia include age, gender, smoking, family history, increased intra-abdominal pressure, and alterations in the metabolism of collagen connective tissue and extracellular matrix6,7. Obesity is also considered a risk factor for inguinal hernia. In theory, obesity can lead to increased intra-abdominal pressure, thereby elevating the risk of developing inguinal hernia8–10. However, some observational studies5,11–14 have reached the opposite conclusion, suggesting an inverse association between obesity and the risk of developing inguinal hernia. The calculation of Body Mass Index (BMI) involves dividing weight (in kilograms) by the square of height (in meters), expressed as weight (kg)/height^2 (m), and is commonly used as an indicator to assess obesity. Contrary to expectations, the aforementioned studies suggest that as BMI increases, the risk of developing inguinal hernia decreases. Body fat percentage refers to the proportion of fat mass in relation to total body weight and is used as an indicator of obesity based on body composition. Acquired generalized lipodystrophy (AGL) is a rare condition characterized by a gradual reduction in fat over time, becoming systemic. A study regarding AGL indicates that it increases the risk of developing umbilical hernia15. Conversely, another study also suggest an increased risk of hernia associated with sarcopenic obesity (SO)16.The association between height and inguinal hernia remains uncertain. A study on risk factors for inguinal hernia in American adults suggests that an increase in height is linked to a higher risk of inguinal hernia in females5. Some research suggests that height may be a risk factor for abdominal incisional hernia17. However, an epidemiological and genetic analysis of height and the risk of 50 diseases indicates a negative correlation between height and the incidence of diaphragmatic hernia, with no observed association with the risk of inguinal hernia18.

The aforementioned studies are all traditional observational epidemiological studies. Such studies are subject to limitations arising from sample size, confounding factors, and the potential for reverse causation, introducing certain constraints in making causal inferences. Mendelian randomization (MR) studies are a commonly employed method in epidemiology for assessing the relationship between exposure factors and disease risk19. This is because genetic alleles are randomly assigned during pregnancy, effectively mitigating the influence of confounding factors or reverse causation in observational studies, thus yielding more reliable research outcomes20 .This study aims to investigate the causal associations between standing height, BMI, body fat percentage, and the risk of inguinal hernia.

Study design and methods

Study design

MR analysis is a genetic epidemiological method that utilizes genetic variations as instrumental variables (IVs) to investigate the causal effects of exposures on disease outcomes21. There are three critical assumptions underlying MR analysis (Fig. 1)22. The current study utilized publicly available summary-level data obtained from large genome-wide association studies (GWASs) and consortia. Employing MR methodology, the research delved into the genetic perspective of causal relationships between standing height, BMI, body fat percentage, and the risk of inguinal hernia. Initially, separate two-sample MR analyses were conducted to investigate the causal associations of standing height, BMI, and body fat percentage with the risk of inguinal hernia. Subsequently, a Multivariable Mendelian Randomization (MVMR) analysis was conducted to evaluate the direct causal effects of exposures on the risk of inguinal hernia. Finally, independent validation was carried out on positive results obtained from the MVMR analysis. The study’s design process is depicted in Fig. 2. Data sources

In the two-sample MR and MVMR analyses, GWAS summary statistics for standing height, BMI, and body fat percentage were obtained from the UK Biobank. Summary statistics for standing height, with a sample size of 461,950 individuals, are available for download at https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/datasets/ukb-b-10787/. For BMI, summary statistics, based on a sample size of 461,460 individuals, can be accessed at https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/datasets/ukb-b-19953/. Similarly, summary statistics for body fat percentage, with a sample size of 454,633 individuals, are downloadable from https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/datasets/ukb-b-8909/. Inguinal hernia summary statistics were obtained from FinnGen, encompassing 207,653 individuals, including 17,096 cases and 190,557 controls. The dataset is accessible at https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/datasets/finn-b-K11_HERING/.

In the independent validation of positive results for MVMR, GWAS summary data related to BMI were derived from a GWAS meta-analysis23, accessible at https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/datasets/ebi-a-GCST002783/, with a sample size of 236,781. GWAS summary data for inguinal hernia were obtained from the UK Biobank, accessible at https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/datasets/ukb-b-5369/, with a sample size of 463,010, including 11,789 cases of inguinal hernia and 451,221 controls.

The UK Biobank project, a prospective cohort study, gathers extensive genetic and phenotypic data from about 500,000 individuals aged 40 to 69 across the United Kingdom. Each participant offers a diverse range of health-related information. Genome-wide genotype data from all participants present numerous opportunities for uncovering new genetic associations and understanding complex traits. FinnGen, a collaborative public-private research effort, integrates imputed genotype data from both newly acquired and archived samples from Finnish biobanks, along with digital health record data from Finnish health registries, to offer fresh insights into disease genetics. Due to the exposure and outcome data originating from different databases, there is no sample overlap. All the aforementioned data are sourced from European populations. Instrumental variables selection and data harmonization

Initially, we identified SNPs significantly associated with the exposure from the GWAS dataset, employing a filtering criterion of P < 5 × 10− 8 to designate them as Instrumental Variables (IVs). Subsequently, to obtain independent SNPs, we applied a linkage disequilibrium (LD) r2 threshold, selecting SNPs with r2 < 0.001 and kb = 10,000, based on European ancestry reference data obtained from the 1,000 Genomes Project24. Following this, all SNPs were harmonized between the exposure and outcome datasets through allele alignment, ensuring effect consistency, and removing SNPs with palindromic structures. The strength of IVs was evaluated using the F-statistic, where an F > 10 indicates the absence of weak instrument variables. We calculated the F-statistics using the following formula: F=[(N-K-1)/K]*[R2/(1-R2)], where N represents the sample size of exposure data, and R2 represents the proportion of variance in exposure data explained by each selected SNP25.The calculation of R2 used the formula: R2=∑[2*(1-MAF)*MAF*β2/(SE2*N)], where MAF is the minor allele frequency, β is the effect size of the allele, and SE is the standard error26.

Statistical analysis

In the two-sample MR analyses, three MR analytical methods were conducted to assess the causal effects of exposures on inguinal hernia in this study. The primary MR analysis was conducted by the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method, which combines the Wald ratio estimates of each SNP on the outcome to gain a pooled causal estimate and provides the highest statistical power. If heterogeneity is present, a random-effects IVW model is applied; conversely, in the absence of heterogeneity, a fixed-effects model is employed27. Furthermore, another two MR analytical methods, weighted median (WM) and MR-Egger, were implemented as complements to detect the causality. The WM method can generate unbiased causal estimates on the condition that at least 50% of the weight comes from valid instrumental variables28. The MR-Egger method provides consistent estimates accounting for pleiotropy on the condition that all the instruments are invalid, although with the lowest power20.The estimation of the risk for inguinal hernia is presented in terms of Odds Ratio (OR) and a 95% Confidence Interval (CI).To account for multiple testing adjustments, the Bonferroni-corrected p-value threshold was set at 0.017 (p = 0.05/3)29.A p-value less than 0.017 is considered to exhibit a significant causal association, while one with a p-value between 0.017 and 0.05 is deemed to indicate a suggestive causal association. A p-value greater than 0.05 is considered not statistically significant.

Considering the potential multicollinearity among correlated exposures, we applied LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) regression to address multicollinearity prior to performing the MVMR analysis. In the MVMR analysis, the IVW method was employed as the statistical approach, and a p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. When independently validating positive results from MVMR analysis, the primary statistical method remains the IVW approach. The MR-Egger method and WM method are employed as supplementary to the IVW method. A p-value less than 0.05 is considered statistically significant for all tests mentioned above, and all tests are conducted as two-sided tests.

To ensure the stability and reliability of our results, we conducted comprehensive sensitivity analyses, comprising the following: (1)Heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran’s Q test, with significance (p < 0.05) indicating the presence of heterogeneity30.(2)Horizontal pleiotropy was examined by estimating the intercept term from MR-Egger regression, where a non-zero intercept suggests potential bias20.(3)Leave-one-out (LOO) analysis was performed to identify any pleiotropy driven by individual SNPs31.(4)The symmetry of the funnel plot was visually inspected to evaluate horizontal pleiotropy.

All these MR analyses were performed using the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.5.7) in R Version 4.3.1.

Results

Two sample mendelian randomization analyses

Instrumental variables selection

We excluded 30 palindromic SNPs, leaving 714 SNPs as valid instrumental variables for standing height. Additionally, after removing 25 palindromic SNPs, 417 SNPs were retained as valid instrumental variables for BMI. Moreover, 19 palindromic SNPs were excluded, leading to 367 SNPs being included as valid instrumental variables for body fat percentage. All IVs exhibited F-values greater than 10, indicating minimal susceptibility to weak instrumental variable bias. Comprehensive SNP information can be found in Supplementary Table 1.

Causal effects of exposures on inguinal hernia

The results obtained through the IVW method indicate a suggestive causal association between standing height and inguinal hernia (OR = 1.091, 95% CI = 1.003–1.186, P = 0.042). Nevertheless, the results from the MR-Egger method and WM method do not reach statistical significance. Both IVW (OR = 0.846, 95% CI = 0.774–0.924,P < 0.001) and MR-Egger (OR = 0.713, 95% CI = 0.562–0.904, P = 0.005) consistently suggest that BMI serves as a significant protective factor against inguinal hernia. The findings from the WM method (OR = 0.852, 95% CI = 0.733–0.991, P = 0.038) imply a suggestive causal relationship between BMI and inguinal hernia. Moreover, both IVW (OR = 0.793, 95% CI = 0.690–0.911, P = 0.001) and WM method (OR = 0.754, 95% CI = 0.620–0.917, P = 0.005) results indicate that body fat percentage acts as a significant protective factor against inguinal hernia. However, the results from the MR-Egger method do not reach statistical significance. Nonetheless, a consistent overall effect direction is observed across all three methods, as evidenced by examination of the OR values and the scatter plot (Supplementary Fig. 1A,1 C,1E). Sensitivity analyses

The results of the Cochran’s Q test indicate the presence of heterogeneity. The MR-Egger intercept test reveals the absence of horizontal pleiotropy, and the funnel plot (Supplementary Fig. 1B,1D,1 F) displays bilateral symmetry, further suggesting no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy in the MR analysis. Additionally, the LOO analysis (Supplementary Figs. 2–4) indicates that the causal relationship is unlikely to be influenced by the effects of individual SNPs. The results of the sensitivity analysis can be found in Supplementary Table 2.

MVMR analysis

Before performing MVMR, we applied LASSO regression to screen the exposure factors and address multicollinearity. Following the LASSO regression, body fat percentage was excluded. Finally, BMI and height were included in the MVMR analysis. The results revealed that BMI remains a significant protective factor for inguinal hernia ( OR = 0.746, 95% CI = 0.638–0.872, P<0.001), while the causal effects of height on inguinal hernia was not statistically significant(OR = 1.076, 95% CI = 0.987–1.172, P = 0.096). Detailed results of the MVMR analysis are presented in Fig. 3.

Independent validation

Instrumental variables selection

After adjusting through MVMR analysis, we conclude that BMI is a significant protective factor for inguinal hernia. To validate the reliability of our findings, we employed GWAS data from different sources (BMI data sourced from a GWAS meta-analysis23, while inguinal hernia data originated from the UK Biobank database) for independent verification. Following the exclusion of 3 palindromic SNPs, a total of 75 SNPs were included as effective instrumental variables for BMI. The F-statistics for all instrumental variables exceeded 10, indicating a relatively low likelihood of weak instrument bias. Detailed information regarding the SNPs can be found in Supplementary Table 1.

Causal effects of BMI on inguinal hernia

The results from the IVW method (OR = 0.995, 95% CI = 0.992–0.999, P = 0.007) and WM method (OR = 0.994, 95% CI = 0.989–0.999, P = 0.014) demonstrate significant statistical significance as indicated by their respective P values. However, the result from the MR-Egger method lacks statistical significance. Nevertheless, based on the OR values and scatter plot (Supplementary Fig. 1G), the overall effect values for the three methods align in direction. While the OR values from the IVW and WM methods are close to 1, they also suggest a protective trend of BMI against inguinal hernia development. Discussion

This study employed the method of two-sample MR and MVMR analyses to investigate the causal relationships between standing height, BMI, body fat percentage, and inguinal hernia from a genetic perspective. Two-sample MR analysis revealed that BMI and body fat percentage are significant protective factors against inguinal hernia, while height demonstrated suggestive causal associations with inguinal hernia. Following adjustment in MVMR analysis, BMI remained a significant protective factor.

It is intuitively believed that obesity may lead to hernia occurrence due to elevated intra-abdominal pressure, thereby increasing the risk of inguinal hernia. Previous studies have indicated that conditions or behaviors leading to an increase in intra-abdominal pressure, such as constipation or prostate enlargement, can contribute to the development of inguinal hernia. An observational study by Kartal et al. suggested that chronic constipation may be a significant etiological factor for the onset of inguinal hernia32.A study on the incidence factors of adult inguinal hernia in the Taiwanese population revealed that benign prostatic hyperplasia increases the risk of developing inguinal hernia33.However, numerous studies5,11–14,34have reached the opposite conclusion, suggesting that obesity serves as a protective factor against inguinal hernia.

Most of the existing research on the relationship between obesity and inguinal hernia consists of traditional observational studies, which are prone to confounding factors and reverse causality. In contrast, our study investigates the associations between two obesity indicators: BMI and body fat percentage, and the risk of inguinal hernia from a genetic perspective. BMI is the most widely used measure for assessing obesity, and previous observational studies have predominantly relied on this metric. Our two-sample MR analysis revealed that genetically predicted BMI is significantly associated with a reduced risk of inguinal hernia. Body fat percentage, which represents the proportion of body weight composed of fat, offers an alternative perspective on obesity from a body composition standpoint. To date, no observational studies have utilized body fat percentage to evaluate its relationship with inguinal hernia risk. By using body fat percentage as an exposure variable, our study aims to explore obesity and inguinal hernia risk from a different angle. The results of the two-sample MR analysis were consistent with those for BMI, showing that body fat percentage is also a significant protective factor against inguinal hernia. These findings reinforce the protective role of obesity against inguinal hernia from multiple perspectives.

An observational study on risk factors for inguinal hernia in U.S. adults found that greater height was associated with an increased risk of inguinal hernia in adult women5. In contrast, an epidemiological and genetic analysis of height and the risk of 50 diseases found no association between height and inguinal hernia, but a link with diaphragmatic hernia18. These two studies reached conflicting conclusions. In our study, two-sample MR analysis suggested that genetically predicted height might increase the risk of inguinal hernia, though this causal association was only suggestive. Prior to MVMR analysis, we corrected for multicollinearity among exposure variables using LASSO regression, during which body fat percentage was excluded. After MVMR analysis, the association between height and inguinal hernia became statistically nonsignificant, providing further evidence against a genetic link between height and inguinal hernia. Meanwhile, BMI remained a significant protective factor.

From the perspective of our study, we consider the conclusion that BMI provides a protective effect against inguinal hernia to be robust. BMI accounts for both height and weight, and we believe it serves not only as a measure of obesity but also as an indicator of body proportion. For a taller individual to maintain the same BMI, they would need to have a higher body weight, which may offer protection against inguinal hernia. Among two individuals of the same height, the one with a higher weight may have greater protection against inguinal hernia. However, increasing body weight also raises other health risks, highlighting the importance of using BMI as a tool for individualized prevention, rather than focusing solely on weight. Although BMI has limitations in assessing obesity, particularly for individuals with high muscle mass, such as fitness enthusiasts or bodybuilders, it remains a useful indicator for the majority of the population. For these highly muscular individuals, BMI may not perfectly reflect their obesity status, but given that they constitute a minority, BMI is still applicable as a measure for most people.

The present study demonstrates several strengths: (1) By employing an MR research design, it significantly reduces confounding factors and the potential for reverse causation often present in observational studies. Previous research has suggested a lower incidence of inguinal hernia in obese patients, possibly due to reduced opportunities for detecting inguinal hernias during physical examinations34. However, this study effectively mitigates observational biases, thereby enhancing the reliability of the conclusions drawn. (2) The use of LASSO regression eliminated the impact of multicollinearity among exposure variables, thereby improving the credibility of the MVMR statistical results. (3) Furthermore, the study underwent independent validation, adding greater persuasiveness to the results.

This study also exhibits certain limitations: (1) Sensitivity analysis revealed heterogeneity within the study. However, this was addressed by employing a random-effects model using the IVW method to assess causal relationships, ensuring the credibility of the results. (2) This study did not account for other known risk factors, such as smoking, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or family history, focusing solely on height, BMI, and body fat percentage. Nevertheless, sensitivity analysis indicated no presence of horizontal pleiotropy, affirming the robustness of the causal effect. (3) Since the GWAS summary data utilized in this study are derived solely from individuals of European descent, caution should be exercised in generalizing the study results to other ethnic populations. (4) Inguinal hernias are classified into direct and indirect types. This study considered inguinal hernias as a whole without distinguishing between the two types, due to data limitations. Future research could explore a more detailed analysis by hernia subtype.

In summary, this study provides genetic-level causal evidence, concluding that BMI is a significant protective factor for inguinal hernia, offering some guidance for individualized prevention strategies.
