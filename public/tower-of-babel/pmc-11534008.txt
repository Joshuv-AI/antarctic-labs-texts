Band Diagram Insights into the Kinetic and Thermodynamic Engineering of Tandem Photocatalytic Cells
Authors: Aastha Goyal, Yuanhui Zhu, Kirk H Bevan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this work, we theoretically investigate the impact of kinetic and thermodynamic properties on the performance of photocatalytic cells operating in an unassisted tandem configuration, including electron affinity and ionization energies, recombination rates, and reaction rates. To this end, we present general rules and metrics for identifying and isolating the origin of an observed shift in the onset potential at either the photoanode or the photocathode of these devices. The correlation between kinetic and thermodynamic shifts in the onset potential is demonstrated through the use of band diagrams and key comparable features within readily accessible characterization tools: current–voltage plots are taken both under illumination and in the dark and further coupled with Mott–Schottky plots. To illustrate this conceptual framework, a model system comprised of a p-type doped BiVO4 photocathode and an n-type doped BiVO4 photoanode is employed. By varying each of the aforementioned kinetic and thermodynamic parameters in isolation, the manner in which these various mechanisms shift the onset potential is demonstrated. This work intends to showcase how kinetic and thermodynamic effects are distinctly manifested in these commonly used characterization tools and further proposes thermodynamic band-edge engineering as a potentially useful and largely unexplored avenue for possibly improving tandem cell performance, in addition to the conventional approach of optimizing kinetics.

Introduction

As the world continues to move toward more sustainable energy sources and storage solutions, there is increased interest in the utilization of photocatalysis. Unassisted photocatalysis via photoelectrochemical (PEC) cells has garnered intensive research interest with respect to hydrogen fuel production through water splitting and CO2 scrubbing.1−3 The basic operating principle of PEC cells consists of a semiconductor–liquid interface in which an electron–hole pair is generated within the semiconductor region upon the absorption of light.4−6 These generated carriers can then participate in redox reactions within the liquid. However, this process is quite complex and highly nonequilibrium.7−9 As such, the operation of PEC cells is still not entirely understood and the efficiency of these devices often remains low.10−12 Most studies of PEC cells consist of characterizing a single working electrode with respect to a counter electrode (e.g., in a half-cell configuration). Nevertheless, an efficient tandem configuration, wherein each half-reaction is driven by a light-absorbing photoanode and photocathode, remains a long-standing goal in the domain of unassisted photocatalytic reactions.13,14

While the literature is replete with band diagram sketches depicting the operation of tandem cells dating back to Nozik’s landmark photochemical diode proposal,10,13−22 very little (if any) work has been done to actually compute such band diagrams and understand what insights they might provide to improve the operation of tandem cells. Despite the long-standing stature of photocatalysis as a field,23−26 it is only in the last ∼10 years or so that numerical band diagram methods have begun to take root in the photocatalysis literature.27−33 These have primarily focused on understanding photoanodes, understandably due to their more sluggish reaction properties,34−36 and so the combined treatment of photoanodes and photocathodes has not received as much analysis in this direction.37−39 It is the purpose of this study to further fill this conceptual gap, in terms of more accurate device calculations, on tandem operation.

To begin with, let us consider the criteria concerning the operation of such a photocatalytic cell as illustrated in Figure 1a. For the sake of a general introductory discussion, here we have sketched a band diagram for both a photoanode (orange-red) and photocathode (green) in tandem operation. Directly computed band diagrams will be discussed later. Representative current–voltage curves are shown below in Figure 1b with the same coloring assignments. When a positive bias is applied with respect to a reference electrode the photoanode current (JPAlight red in Figure 1b) rises and then plateaus when the semiconductor space charge region (SCR) absorbs all the incoming light and band bending sufficiently suppresses carrier recombination.6,40−43 The same principles apply to the photocathode current though in the opposite bias direction (JPClight green in Figure 1b). The important point is that through the principle of current conservation, a tandem cell operates in an unassisted manner only where these two current–voltage curves intersect, as indicated by VOP and JOP in Figure 1b. For the vast majority of systems reported in the literature, this operating point is very low and inefficient, even as a fraction of the maximum light absorption (shown as yellow waved arrows in Figure 1a) efficiency of the semiconducting materials employed.44,45 Often performance is less than 10% of the theoretical limit of a material system.46 Problematically, to operate the system at higher currents when the unassisted intersection is poor, one must apply an external bias. This is shown by black dots and arrows in Figure 1b and externally in the upper part of Figure 1a. Of course, an additional bias defeats the purpose of unassisted operation, and thus, the question becomes how does one shift over the photoanode or photocathode operating curves so as to intersect at a higher operating current? Ultimately, this question is nearly the same as asking how one can shift the onset potential, where ∼1% of the final saturation current is reached, of the photoanode or photocathode in their respective directions—denoted as VPAonset and VPConset in Figure 1b. From this, the second question alluded to above: What readily available experimental signatures might aid in determining the physical origin of an observed onset shift?

In this work, we aim to comprehensively demonstrate that directly computed band diagrams can offer insights into the kinetic and thermodynamic factors that can drive such a shift, as well as illustrate the hazards associated with false positives in this regard. Our study is split into several parts considering both kinetic and thermodynamic factors that can contribute to shifting the onset. First, we investigate how competing recombination and carrier transfer kinetic properties determine the placement of VPAonset and VPConset. The process of recombination is indicated by the carrier lifetime variable τ in Figure 1a, and can be viewed as a kinetic process internal to the semiconductor carrier dynamics.47 Correspondingly, the interfacial carrier transfer rates determined by chemical reaction kinetics are indicated by kp and kn in the photoanode and photocathode (see also Figure 1a). Subsequently, we demonstrate how thermodynamic properties impact the placement of VPAonset and VPConset. Within this thermodynamic context, we first show how by tuning the electron ionization or affinity energies (EIE and EEA) one may alter the respective onsets of photoanodes and photocathodes. Subsequently, for the sake of completeness, we also explain the manner in which pH can provide “false positives” in the shifting of VPAonset and VPConset (see Figure 1b). While the discussion of competing kinetic factors is not new to the literature,48−50 the discussion of material thermodynamic properties is almost entirely unexplored to our knowledge—pH properties aside.4,5 However, the purpose of this paper is not only to delve into possible novelty but more importantly to provide a comprehensive examination of all the competing material and chemical properties that can be used to tune the operation of tandem configurations toward high efficiencies.51 In this regard, we seek to employ computed band diagrams to clearly show the impact of each property and how they can be identified through three combined key experimental tools: light current plots, dark current plots, and Mott–Schottky plots. From this emerges a general design framework for experimentally extracting the factors determining the onset of photoanodes and photocathodes, at least for monolithic materials. Thus, the deeper aim of this work is to help point out how the kinetic or thermodynamic factors contributing to an onset shift can be identified experimentally and how one may strengthen their physical conceptualization through the use of computed band diagrams.

Kinetic Properties

As mentioned, without changing the actual thermodynamic material properties, one can only improve the operating intersection by tuning kinetic factors. This is, after all, the conventional approach in electrocatalysis.70−74 In this respect, there are three kinetic components that can lead to particularly poor tandem performance, as per the model example in Figure 3. First, the majority of carriers are not able to easily transport out of the photon-absorbing band bending region. This is reflected by marked curvature in their quasi-Fermi levels in the SCR that arises from setting χ = 0.5 in Figure 3. A higher majority carrier concentration in the SCR (when 0 < χ < 1) leads to more carrier recombination and thus a reduced concentration of minority carriers available to drive a given photocatalytic reaction. Likewise, when the carrier recombination lifetime (τ) is shorter there will be less minority carriers available to participate in a given reaction (see Figure 1a)—we can consider this to be the second kinetic reason for poor tandem cell performance. In Figure 3 we assume a rather low carrier lifetime of 10–11 s.42,48,75,76 We choose this low τ value to merely emphasize the impact that reduced recombination can have on the onset.48 Lastly, the third and most commonly addressed kinetic factor concerns the actual carrier transfer rates77−79 (kp and kn) in the reaction process as illustrated earlier in Figure 1a.

General Kinetic Contributions to the Onset

Let us begin by considering the impact of tuning τ and χ together, with respect to the onsets of JPAlight and JPClight. Within Figure 4a,4b the carrier lifetime (τ) is varied from 10–12 s–1 through to 10–8 s–1 at a fixed value of χ = 0.5. We see a clear beneficial shift in both the photoanode and photocathode onset potential as τ is increased; again we are using the flat band electrochemical potential of the photoanode as our reference. Earlier combined experimental and theoretical work has shown the importance of enhancing the carrier lifetime to improve the photocurrent onset.48 In Figure 4c,4d one can further see that the degree of shift per decade of change in τ differs depending on the relative value of χ. When the magnitude of τ is varied to longer lifetimes (e.g., by reducing the number of recombination centers48), one can see that the beneficial shift in onset increases further for even larger values of χ. This can be seen for both the photoanode and photocathode in Figure 4c,4d, respectively. However, for the same lifetime (τ) and a smaller value of χ the onset point always worsens. This is because recombination increases and becomes harder to suppress through band bending when the majority carrier concentration is increased in the SCR (labeled in Figure 1a) by lowering χ.6,49 A lower value of χ provides a curved majority carrier quasi-Fermi level in the SCR more closely following the band banding profile as shown in Figure 3b for χ = 0.5. For the same reason lowering χ reduces the fill factor between the dark current and light current curves, as can be seen in Figure 4c,4d at a given fixed value of τ. However, it does not have as dramatic an impact on lowering the fill factor as surface states can through pinning of the minority carrier quasi-Fermi level splitting.41,80 Ultimately, the separation between the light and dark current curves is determined by the degree of quasi-Fermi splitting between the minority and majority carriers.41It is also interesting to note that the Mott–Schottky intercept is not modified in either case by τ (see Figure 4e,4f)—an important point we shall return to at the end of Section 3. These equations contain some important insights. First, they show that the onset should shift 2.3 kBT/χ per decade change in τ and to some degree similarly for each decade change in the respective rate term (kp0 and kn0) so long as all of the potential drop occurs in the semiconductor depletion region. We see this trend with respect to τ in Figure 4c,4d, where at T = 300 K there is an approximately 59 meV per decade shift when χ = 1 and this per decade shift increases as χ is lowered. However, when kp0 and kn0 are changed the variation remains more or less the same for the range of χ explored (see Figure 5c,5d). This is because kp0 and kn0 are not the entirety of kp and kn as can be seen in eqs 15 and 23. Rather, there is a band-edge unpinning impact on kp and kn through Vint and the corresponding Tafel term (γ). Under high light intensities, as one lowers kp0 or kn0 the band-edge unpinning (Vint) will need to increase to reach the same saturation current qΦ (or even the same onset current). With greater values of Vint, less of the total potential drop occurs in the SCR of the semiconductor and the more delayed onset becomes (see Figures 1 and 3). Meaning, Vint must increase to provide the same level of current with lower values of kp0 and kn0—since an onset shift is measured in terms of the bias change required to reach the same current. When Vint increases the total fraction of the applied potential drop that occurs across the semiconductor (Vbb,PA and Vbb,PC) decreases. Thus, per eqs 30 and 31 the impact of the χ becomes less pronounced in the onset shift as less of a potential drop is occurring inside the semiconductor region for the same applied bias when Vint captures a larger fraction of this total potential drop. This coupling effect between band-edge unpinning and the onset can be ameliorated by going to much lower illumination intensities (Φ), where a Tafel contribution is not needed to reach the saturation current.42 This is shown in Figures S1 and S2 within the Supporting Information where the 2.3 kBT/χ trends are fully recovered. However, in a realistic system (under 1 Sun or so of illumination) band-edge unpinning effects are likely to be present as emphasized in Figures 4 and 5.42,50

Band Diagrams and Kinetics

Considering that we began with a band diagram description in Figure 3, and set it as a core goal of this study in Section 1, at first glance, it seems rather puzzling that further provided band diagram plots have not been detailed regarding the trends in Figures 4 and 5. However, deeper reflection shows that regardless of which of these kinetic factors one tunes (kp0, kn0, τ, or even χ) the total potential drop across the tandem junction in Figure 3b will remain the same! This is because, as mentioned earlier, the tandem operation is satisfied when EFp,PC = EFn,PA at the electrode contact of each terminal. Since the electrochemical potentials of each terminal will always merge at the electrode contact (see Figure 3b), their total electrostatic displacement (e.g., via band bending) with respect to one another will always be the same regardless of how one sets the recombination or carrier transfer rates! Meaning, their total displacement from a flat band offset of electrochemical potentials (see Figure S3) to a short-circuit configuration (see Figure 3b) will always be the same.

This is not to say that their individual degree of displacement will always be the same but that their total electrostatic displacement will be the same (including any possible band-edge unpinning contributions). Their degree of band bending displacement will remain the same only when the kinetic factors on both sides are altered to the same degree. To illustrate this, in Figure S4 we present the band diagram plots where the carrier transfer rates are held fixed at 0.1 s–1 and the lifetime is increased to τ = 10–8 s for both terminals—see the Supporting Information. Here it can be seen that the only difference with respect to Figure 3b is that the quasi-Fermi level splitting is allowed to proceed further (under the same degree of band bending) and thus the current increases through an increased minority carrier density at the surface; less is lost to recombination. On the other hand in Figure S5 we have held the recombination term at τ = 10–11 s and increased the transfer rates to 102 s–1. Here it can be seen that with the same degree of quasi-Fermi level splitting as in Figure 3b one attains a much higher intersection of JOP, simply because less minority carriers are needed to approach the saturation condition where JOP ≈ qΦ per eqs 15 and 23.

Before closing our kinetic discussion, it is also worthwhile to further consider the Mott–Schottky results in Figures 4 and 5. The Mott–Schottky intercept of the photoanode in Figures 4e and 5e intersects with its flat band potential at 0.5 V versus NHE and has a positive slope as expected. However, in Figures 4f and 5f the Mott–Schottky of the photocathode is negatively sloped, being p-type, and is shifted toward 2.6 V versus NHE. This offset between the two Mott–Schottky intercepts, of the n-type photoanode and p-type photocathdoe, basically reflects the difference between the Fermi levels of the two terminals when they are both in the flat band configuration when the same reference electrochemical potential is used for each. That is, the Mott–Schottky difference between intercepts represents their thermodynamic offset prior to the application of any band bending (evolution of the potential drop at VOP in Figure 3). This flat band separation of Fermi levels is illustrated in Figure S3 and brings us to our next focus.

Thermodynamic Properties

Now let us consider how thermodynamic properties of a tandem system might be employed to shift the onset of the illuminated photocurrent and presumably seek to increase JOP in Figures 1 and 3. As briefly outlined in Section 1, there are two ways to do this: (1) through band alignment engineering; and (2) through pH variation. The former can be employed to beneficially increase JOP (at least relative to the maximum absorbed photon flux), while the latter cannot be employed and is prone to false positives with respect to the goals outlined in Figure 1b. Both are now discussed for the sake of completeness.

Thermodynamic Contributions from the Material

First the plots in Figures 6 and 7 are examined where all other material parameters are held fixed save for the band-edge positions (full parameters are given in Tables S1 and S2 of the Supporting Information). In both cases, we are employing thermodynamic properties of the semiconductor material to shift the onset. In Figure 6 we are tailoring the electron affinity (EEA) of the model system in Figure 3 while keeping its electron ionization energy (EIE) fixed. We do not speculate on how this band-edge engineering might be achieved, for example, through some form of metal cation stoichiometric substitution in an oxide, but to merely point out that if we could alter the material in this manner what would happen to the onset in the form of a thought experiment. Certainly, the construction of such a compound would involve a degree of variation in both EEA and EIE (or, similarly, EC and EV).84,85 However, in the case of a photoanode, only EEA shifting is beneficial (see Figure 6), where we can see that for each eV change in EEA there is a correspondingly equivalent shift in the onset. This is manifest subtly in Figure 6a, but is most evident in the reference electrode plot in Figure 6b–all other parameters held fixed. In Figure 6a we are plotting with respect to the material flat band potential, and as EEA is varied so will its flat band potential, which leads to the more apparent trends versus NHE in Figure 6b and also the Mott–Schottky shifting trends in Figure 6g. Similarly, in Figure 7 one can see that by tuning EIE (while holding EEA fixed), a beneficial shift is obtained for a photocathode as required in the opposite direction. Again we do not speculate on how this might be achieved, merely to state that tuning EIE can allow one to tune the illumination onset for photocathodes. This is once more most evident in the reference electrode plot in Figure 7b on an NHE scale, but is also apparent when set against the flat band potential of the photocathode in Figure 7a—note this is not the same as the photoanode potential used in Figures 3–5. Also, for a photocathode, the key telling feature of such a thermodynamic contribution to the illumination onset shift is in the Mott–Schottky intercept, as shown in Figure 7g.

More insight can be obtained from the illuminated and dark band diagram plots in Figures 6c–f and 7c–f. These band diagram plots are taken where JPA = qΦ/2 or JPC = qΦ/2 as marked by a dashed line in Figures 6a,b and 7a,b. The goal is to understand what thermodynamic factors contribute to achieving the same total current but with a shift in the onset. The voltages where this current condition is reached are noted at the top of the band diagram plots in Figures 6c–f and 7c–f. In the case of photoanode onset tuning in Figure 6c–f, we see that as EEA is increased and EIE is held fixed, more bias must be applied from the flat band configuration to achieve the same dark current intensity. This explains the trends in Figure 6a. However, since the valence band edge is not moving (EIE is fixed) from a static reference electrode perspective (NHE) no further overpotential is needed in the dark to reach the same current, thus leading to the trends in Figure 6b. By contrast, when the same degree of overpotential is applied relative to NHE when a material has a raised EEA and EIE fixed (see Figure 6c–f), one is able to arrive at more band bending at the same NHE overpotential (or total bias relative to the flat band) and the illuminated onset is instead seen to shift as given in Figure 6b. Effectively, the additional thermodynamic shift in EEA (with EIE fixed) increases the effective potential drop on the illuminated photoanode relative to the same NHE overpotential. Correspondingly, in the case of photocathode onset tuning in Figure 7c–f, we see that as EIE is increased and EEA is held fixed, more bias must be applied (in the direction of band bending) from the photoanode flat band configuration to achieve the same dark current intensity. This, similarly, explains the trends in Figure 7a. Now, since the conduction band edge is not moving (EEA is held fixed relative to vacuum) once more from a static reference electrode perspective (NHE) no further overpotential is needed in the dark to reach the same current, thus leading to the trends in Figure 7b. By contrast, when the same degree of overpotential is applied relative to NHE when a material has a raised EIE and EEA fixed (see Figure 7c–f), one is able to arrive at more band bending at the same NHE overpotential (or total bias relative to the flat band of the photocathode) and the illuminated onset is instead seen to shift as given in Figure 7b. This occurs in a mirror opposite manner to what was just discussed for a photoanode; the additional thermodynamic shift in EIE (with EEA fixed) increases the effective potential drop on the illuminated photocathode relative to the same NHE overpotential.

Clearly, band-edge engineering can have an onset benefit when applied appropriately to either a photoanode or a photocathode. However, this always requires that the band gap be increased to some degree. One never obtains something for nothing; there is always a compromise. In this case, each shift in the onset reduces photon absorption to some degree and thus the total value of Φ in a practical solar situation. This means that the maximum saturation photocurrent will correspondingly decrease. This is why we chose to move only one band edge in a beneficial manner. We do not provide a detailed exploration of these band gap tuning aspects in this work but instead point the reader to the excellent discussion on this compromise in ref (81). Nevertheless, a brief summary of the solar photoabsorption loss with increasing band gap size is provided in the Supporting Information. Moreover, in a realistic materials engineering situation one needs to consider how both the conduction band and valence band edges may shift relative to vacuum (in the form of EIE and EEA)— mentioned above.86 We are only pointing out that there can be thermodynamic contributions that beneficially, to some degree, shift the illumination onset. We are not pointing out exactly how to carry this out for a given material system that is set aside for future work. However, it is interesting to note that some experimental findings seem to suggest this material composition tuning approach may be beneficial for the reasons just discussed (see, for example, ref (87)).

Thermodynamic Contributions at the Interface

Next, the final “missing piece” in our thermodynamic discussion arises from possible pH contributions (or more broadly that of charged species adsorption)68 to the onset shift of either a photoanode or photocathode. After all, pH always brings to mind a Pourbaix diagram and the thermodynamic properties of electrochemical systems.88 To start with, we shall simply state that pH tuning has no markedly beneficial impact on the operation of a tandem cell as is pointed out briefly in ref (5). However, it is important to explain “why” in a little more depth, since misconceptions can arise when considering only a photoanode or photocathode in isolation, i.e., a half-cell.

One can formulate various models of pH-dependent adsorption of H+ and OH– groups on the surface of a material, with full consideration of buffering capacity and so forth.89−92 However, doing so does not really add much to the end message that we are trying to arrive at here, so let us take the simplest (or most idealized) model of pH dependence. This model holds that a fixed interfacial potential (VH) within the Helmholtz layer should form due to H+ and OH– group attachment and detachment kinetics that depend on the pH as in eq 32

where pHPZC is the pH at which net zero charge adsorption is achieved and VH is the potential seen by positive charges as in eq 1. In our model system in Figure 3 we assume a pHPZC = 7. For a detailed discussion on eq 32 we refer to ref (5). However, for the sake of completeness, we also include an overview in the Supporting Information of this work. Now let us put this theory into practice.

Consider the plot in Figure 8a where we vary the pH for the model system in Figure 3. For both the photoanode and photocathode, there is a shift of 2.3 kBT per decade in the VOP to the left as the pH is successively varied from 5 through to 9. The important point is that the photoanode and photocathode curves shift in the same direction with respect to NHE and thus JOP does not improve with respect to pH. If one were to view the photoanode or photocathode in isolation, as a half-cell, one might say that by tuning the pH appropriately, one can obtain a desired onset shift. However, this of course has no impact because both terminals (when composed of the same material and surface chemistry) should respond to pH changes in the same manner as shown in eq 32. However, some complexities might arise when the surface carrier concentrations are driven far from equilibrium. The nonbeneficial nature of varying the pH is similarly summarized by the dark current plots for each terminal in Figure 8b, where the overpotential difference between the two terminals does not vary at all with respect to pH, as one would expect from a Pourbaix diagram.88 However, the signature of a pH-induced shift in the onset is evident within Mott–Schottky plots, such as those given in Figure 8c,d. Note, however, that the intercept separation between the photoanode and photocathode (corresponding to the flat band separation of their bulk Fermi levels, as discussed earlier) remains the same with respect to pH variation. As stated earlier, when the Mott–Schottky intercepts change a thermodynamic effect is present in shifting the onset.

To understand why varying the pH has no tangible impact on JOP let us again return to the band diagram technique as given in Figure 8e. This plot is much the same as that in Figure 3b, with the exception that we included more information about the liquid electrolyte and interface region. Within the electrolyte region, the contribution of eq 32 is shown as an exaggerated potential step (in length for plotting clarity purposes) directly at the edge of the liquid electrolyte region for both terminals. Next, we have the NHE electrochemical potential situated at −4.44 eV below the vacuum of the liquid region.93,94 The key point is that this NHE position does not vary with respect to vacuum in the liquid (as shown in Figure 8e), but does vary with respect to the band edges of each terminal as VH acts with varying degrees at various values of pH. Moreover, the band diagram that is shown in Figure 8e is always that obtained at each JOP in Figure 8a. Meaning, it is taken where the two terminals are short-circuited in precisely the same manner. Notably, their electrochemical point of operation which is indicated by the dotted horizontal line in Figure 8e is shifting relative to NHE but their precise properties of this short-circuit operation are identical as the pH is changed. This is why we see VOP change with respect to pH but do not extract any real useful contributions from it in terms of improving the JOP intersection. One can make the same argument for any thermodynamically driven adsorption process that is the same on both terminals.68 Of course, one can counter argue that we have assumed identical materials and that different materials might have different pH responses, in terms of buffering capacity, and so forth, and the argument might not be exactly valid in all situations. However, it is unlikely that any considerable practical benefit could be gleaned from such pH contributions even if the materials were to have different pH responses.5 There is no “free lunch” and band diagrams help make this abundantly clear, as shown in Figure 8e.

Experimentally Relevant Takeaways

When the onset current shifts in a beneficial manner (as emphasized first in Figure 1a), one would like to know exactly what possible contribution drove this shift. For monolithic cells, meaning those composed of a single material on either the photoanode or the photocathode (but not necessarily the same material on each), we can use the findings in Figures 3 through 8 to partially guide our analysis in this respect. Toward this end, there are three bedrock experimental characteristics from Figures 4–8 with important experimental relevance to uncovering the underlying mechanisms: (1) the illuminated system current–voltage response; (2) the dark system current–voltage response; and (3) the Mott–Schottky plot.

A decision tree concerning these three tools is given in Figure 9. First one needs to determine if the onset shift is driven by thermodynamic or kinetic factors. The tool for this is the Mott–Schottky plot. If there is a thermodynamic factor contributing then there will be a shift in the Mott–Schottky plot (for either a photoanode or a photocathode)—see for example Figures 6g, 7g, and 8c,d. However, if there is a purely kinetic contribution to the onset shift then there should be no change in the Mott–Schottky intercept as shown in Figures 4e,f and 5e,f. This is the first branch in the decision tree in Figure 9. Now let us consider the light and dark current responses for each scenario, that can be used to further extract out the driving term in the onset shift. If we take the thermodynamic branch in Figure 9, there are two further possibilities: (1) the shift is due to a material thermodynamic contribution, and/or (2) the shift is due to a pH contribution. In the case of a pH contribution, there will be an equivalent shift in both the light and dark current responses. In the case of a band-edge thermodynamic driven shift, there will “only” be an offset in the illuminated current curve (assuming a standard reference electrode such as NHE is applied), so long as only EEA is altered in the photoanode and only EIE is altered in the photocathode. If both are altered together then there can be an onset shift in both the light and dark currents (vs NHE) to the degree both band-edge positions are changed, but there should be a similar shift in the Mott–Schottky plot as there is in the illuminated current for such a thermodynamic band-edge effect. Now, let us return to the kinetic branch in Figure 9, which has the key distinguishing feature of no Mott–Schottky shift. If the hole and electron transfer rates (kp0 and kn0, respectively) for the photoanode or photocathode are responsible, then both the light and dark current will experience a similar shift as shown in Figure 5a,5b. However, if the carrier lifetime is the “only” factor contributing then only the illuminated current should shift as shown in Figure 4a,4b. Hence, from these three tools, we have an emerging methodology for understanding what factors are contributing to a given change observed in the onset. Of course, if all these factors are acting together, it can be complicated to extract the key onset impacting mechanism. Further tools may be needed, including band gap measurements and electron affinity and ionization energy characterization.99,100 Mott–Schottky plots have their own particular difficulties80,101,102 and one may need to use other mechanisms to try to estimate the flat band potential.68 Additional established impedance and transient techniques may also be useful for determining the change in kinetic and other factors.50,103 We leave these more complex scenarios to future investigations. However, the main message is that one should at least try to obtain these three plots as a bedrock starting point to begin to uncover the relevant contributing factors per Figures 4–8—more advanced characterization tools should certainly be applied to provide proper rigor.95−98

Other characterization methods should also be applied to verify such interpretations.95−98 This simplified decision tree is not always sufficient, particularly when each of these kinetic and thermodynamic factors is acting together and must be disentangled.

Conclusions and Summary

In this article, we have investigated kinetic and thermodynamic factors that when optimized, can lead to higher photocatalytic efficiency of PEC cells in a tandem configuration. In terms of kinetic effects, the impact of decreasing recombination by increasing carrier lifetimes (τ) or by improving majority carrier movement out of the depletion region by increasing χ, as well as the impact of minority carrier transfer into the liquid by increasing kp and kn was investigated. To this end, it was found that a 10-fold increase in carrier lifetime results in a 2.3 kBT/χ favorable shift of the onset potential, with a favorable onset for the photoanode being a negative shift toward lower biases and for the photocathode, a positive shift toward higher biases. Similarly, for a 10-fold increase in carrier transfer rates, one can expect at least a 2.3 kBT/χ favorable shift of the onset potential. In reality, these increases are not easily obtained. For example, for oxidation of water at the photoanode, the carrier lifetimes are often on the order of 10–11 s and carrier transfer rates in the range of 1 → 10 s–1.42,48,104 Both parameters are difficult to improve due to the sluggish nature of the multistep reactions at the photoanode as well as the presence of many trap states that promote recombination within the semiconductor region. If these parameters could be improved, perhaps through the use of cocatalysts and through improved semiconductor purity, a 5-fold increase in each of τ and kp and kn as well as a 0.1 increase in χ would theoretically improve efficiency from 4% as seen in Figure 3 to >90% of the energy captured by the system. However, in this article, we present an alternative approach through thermodynamic band-edge engineering. Here, when holding the valence band fixed while raising the conduction band for the photoanode (decreasing electron affinity) or holding the conduction band fixed while lowering the valence band of the photocathode (increasing the ionization energy), a favorable onset shift of the same magnitude as the change in band gap is observed. As such, a 0.25 eV change in both the photoanode and photocathode as compared to the 2.4 eV band gap in Figure 3, would result in an efficiency of >90% of the energy captured by this example system (with respect to the total photon flux with an energy equal to or greater than the band gap). Of course, increasing the band gap means that less incoming sunlight has enough energy to surpass the band gap and generate electron–hole pairs to begin with, thus the overall energy captured by the system decreases. However, larger band gap materials are generally more stable against photocorrosion, which may add more favorability to such a trade-off. Finally, it was also further emphasized that altering the pH of the electrolyte has no net effect on the efficiency of tandem cells. All of these findings were summarized in a systematic approach involving the combined use of illuminated current–voltage plots, dark current–voltage plots, and Mott–Schottky plots to gain preliminary insight into the cause of a suspected onset shift.

Further work along this avenue may involve considerations beyond simply monolithic materials, such as those with complex heterostructures in the semiconductor region, which may yield further benefits. In addition, one may consider photocorrosion and other such effects that were not included in this work. Moreover, a dedicated study providing direct comparison between the experimental application of the onset shifting strategies suggested herein and such a modeling approach would be an extremely fruitful undertaking in future work.48 It is also possible to utilize more complex recombination descriptions, including surface states,48,80,101 and quasi-Fermi level simulations in light and dark for further accuracy. One may consider solving the full set of continuity equations rather than the modified set as proposed here, but doing so is computationally expensive and less easily amenable to rapid insights.40
