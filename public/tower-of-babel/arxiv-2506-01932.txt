Nonlocal pseudo-symmetries and Bäcklund transformations as 𝒞 \mathcal{C} -morphisms
Diego Catalano Ferraioli, Tarcísio Castro Silva

Abstract
In this paper, we show how factorization with respect to nonlocal pseudosymmetries allows one to obtain Bäcklund transformations, interpreted as nonlocal 𝒞 \mathcal{C} -morphisms of differential equations. This approach is illustrated through several examples.

1
Introduction

Bäcklund transformations, although originally arising within the realm
of classical differential geometry, have come to play a significant
role in the modern analysis of nonlinear phenomena. Indeed, this type
of transformation is named after the mathematician traditionally associated
with a theorem that studies the transformation of surfaces with constant
negative curvature in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, realizing them as focal surfaces
of a pseudospherical congruence of lines. In practice, starting from
a surface with constant negative Gaussian curvature, Bäcklund’s theorem
allows for the construction of a one-parameter family of surfaces
by integrating only a system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs).
Since such surfaces correspond, from an analytical point of view,
to solutions of the sine-Gordon equation
z_{xt}=\sin z
, Bäcklund’s
theorem provides a method for generating new solutions of the sine-Gordon
equation from a given one, again through the integration of a system
of ODEs. In doing so, this transformation acts as an automorphism
of the sine-Gordon solution space — a property that justifies the
term auto-Bäcklund transformation.

In its modern formulation, an auto-Bäcklund transformation for a differential
equation
\mathcal{E}
refers to a map that associates, to any given
solution of
\mathcal{E}
, a family of new solutions of
\mathcal{E}
,
typically by integrating a system of ODEs. Then, more generally, a
Bäcklund transformation is understood as a transformation that, via
the integration of a system of ordinary differential equations, maps
solutions of one differential equation
\mathcal{E}
to solutions
of another equation
\mathcal{Y}
.

Given a nonlinear differential equation, the problem of determining
the existence of its auto-Bäcklund transformations—or, more generally,
of Bäcklund transformations between this equation and other equations
of interest—is a significant problem that has attracted the attention
of many mathematicians (see, for instance
[
4
,
24
,
25
,
29
,
33
,
35
,
37
,
45
]

and references therein). However, it is a difficult problem which
is still open in its full generality, despite having been studied
for a long time and, in recent years, interesting progress having
been made in some particular cases (see, for instance, the works
[
2
,
3
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
42
,
43
]
).

On the other hand, a notion closely related to that of a Bäcklund
transformation is that of a differential substitution. In fact, a
differential substitution from an equation
\mathcal{E}
to an equation

\mathcal{Y}
is a transformation that maps solutions of
\mathcal{E}

to solutions of
\mathcal{Y}
, without requiring the integration
of a system of ordinary differential equations. Classical examples
of differential substitutions include the Cole–Hopf transformation,
which maps solutions of the heat equation to solutions of Burgers
equation, and the Miura transformation, which maps solutions of the
modified Korteweg-de Vries (mKdV) equation to solutions of the Korteweg-de
Vries (KdV) equation.

As with Bäcklund transformations, given a differential equation
\mathcal{E}
,
it is often of great interest to know whether a differential substitution
exists that maps its solutions into those of another equation
\mathcal{Y}
.
Regarding this issue, in the context of evolutionary equations, Sokolov
took a significant step forward in
[
38
]
, highlighting a
fundamental relationship between factorization via pseudosymmetries
and differential substitutions. However, although Sokolov underscored
the importance of pseudosymmetries through this discovery, these have
been surprisingly underexplored in the literature. Indeed, aside from
some further contributions by Sokolov himself, to the best of our
knowledge this notion has been explicitly addressed only in few papers
(e.g.,
[
14
,
15
,
29
]
), and, in simplified forms, in studies
concerning mainly the reduction of ordinary differential equations
(see, for instance,
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
32
]

and the references therein).

As in the case of common symmetries, we can refer to pseudosymmetries
of a covering
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
of an equation
\mathcal{E}

as nonlocal pseudosymmetries of
\mathcal{E}
. This further type
of extension of the notion of pseudosymmetry plays a fundamental role
in this paper, together with the notion of
\mathcal{C}
-morphism
and the factorization of an equation by means of pseudosymmetries.
Indeed, in the present paper we show how the factorization by nonlocal
pseudosymmetries allows us to obtain Bäcklund transformations between
differential equations.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 is a section of preliminaries,
devoted to some general aspects of the geometry of differential equations,
viewed as submanifolds of jet spaces, and a discussion of more specific
notions that will play a particularly significant role in the remainder
of the paper. This section is divided into the following four subsections:
the first is devoted to a brief introduction to differential equations
as submanifolds of jet spaces; the second deals with
\mathcal{C}
-morphisms;
the third, after a short review of the notion of symmetry, provides
a discussion of the notion of pseudosymmetry; and finally the fourth
subsection reviews the theory of differential coverings. In particular,
while discussing the notion of pseudosymmetry, a description of the
factorization process through pseudosymmetries is also provided. Furthermore,
some significant examples are included in this section to illustrate
the central notions discussed here. Next, in Section 3, we demonstrate
how starting from zero-curvature representations of an equation
\mathcal{E}
,
we can determine Riccati-type differential coverings for
\mathcal{E}
,
together with nonlocal conservation laws. This type of covering, with
the related conservation laws, is particularly useful for determining
possible nonlocal pseudosymmetries of
\mathcal{E}
. Finally, Section
4 shows how factorization using nonlocal pseudosymmetries of an equation

\mathcal{E}
can provide Bäcklund transformations for
\mathcal{E}
.
This approach to determining Bäcklund transformations is illustrated
here through a series of examples.

-

and

of a fiber

(in particular for total derivatives,
Cartan distributions, prolongations, symmetries, horizontal and vertical

and differential coverings) we will use the notations and conventions
revised in Subsections
2.1
,
2.3.1

and
2.4
, which follow references like
[
8
,
22
,
28
,
34
,
40
]
;

-

(x_{i},u_{\sigma}^{j})
usually denote the
canonical
coordinates
in a
k
-order jet bundle
J^{k}(\pi)
;

-

by considering a system of differential equations
\mathcal{E}\subset J^{k}(\pi)
,
we often describe it as
\mathcal{E}=\{F^{1}(x,u_{\sigma})=0,...,F^{r}(x,u_{\sigma})=0\}

except when a differential covering of
\mathcal{E}
is involved
in the discussion;

-

when
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{Y}\subset J^{k}(\pi)
are such
that
\mathcal{Y}
defines a
q
-dimensional differentiable covering
(or extension) of
\mathcal{E}
, we will usually distinguish the
fiber coordinates
\{u^{1},...,u^{m}\}
into the two subsets
\{z^{1},...,z^{m-q}\}

(also referred to as
local variables
) and
\{v^{1},...,v^{q}\}

(also referred to as
nonlocal variables
), such that
\mathcal{E}=\{F^{1}(x,z_{\sigma})=0,...,F^{r}(x,z_{\sigma})=0\}

and
\mathcal{Y}=\{F^{h}(x,z_{\sigma})=0,\,v_{i}^{s}=X_{i}^{s}(x,z_{\sigma},v)\}
,
where
h=1,...,r
,
s=1,...q
and
i
enumerates the independent
variables
x_{1},...,x_{n}
;

-

for differentiable covering of Riccati type, we will usually
denote nonlocal variables by
\{\rho^{j}\}
;

-

by a “basic system of invariants” we mean a system of invariants
that generates all invariants through prolongations and functional
combinations, while by a “complete system of invariants” we mean
a system of invariants that generates a particular class of invariants
(e.g., those of a specific order) through functional combinations.

,

]
and
[

,

,

,

,

2.1
Differential equations as submanifolds of jet spaces

Let
\pi:E\rightarrow M
be a fiber bundle, with
dim\,M=n
and

dim\,E=n+m
. For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
we denote by
J^{k}(\pi)

the manifold of
k
-th order jets of sections of
\pi
and by
\pi_{k}:J^{k}(\pi)\rightarrow M

the
k
-order jet bundle
of sections of
\pi
. By definition,
a point
\theta
of
J^{k}(\pi)
is an equivalence class
\theta=[s]_{a}^{k}

of smooth sections
s
of
\pi
whose graphs at
a\in M
pass through
the same point
s(a)
, where they have the same contact up to order

k
. Hence, any section
s
of
\pi
togheter with its derivatives
up to order
k
, determines a section
j_{k}(s)
of
\pi_{k}
which
is called the
k
-th order prolongation of

s
. For
h>k

we denote by
\pi_{h,k}:J^{h}(\pi)\rightarrow J^{k}(\pi)
the
natural
projection
of
J^{h}(\pi)
onto
J^{k}(\pi)
, given by
[s]_{a}^{h}\rightarrow[s]_{a}^{k}
.

Denoting by
\{x_{1},...,x_{n}\}
local coordinates on
M
and by

\{u^{1},...,u^{m}\}
local fiber coordinates of
\pi
, the induced

canonical coordinates
on
J^{k}(\pi)
will be
\{x_{i},u_{\sigma}^{j}\}
,
where
i\in\{1,...,n\}
,
j\in\{1,...,m\}
and
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{n})

is a multi-index of order
|\sigma|=\sigma_{1}+...+\sigma_{n}
such
that
0\leq|\sigma|\leq k
. By definition, if
\theta=[s]_{a}^{k}
,
then
x_{i}(\theta):=x_{i}(a)
and
u_{\sigma}^{j}(\theta):=\frac{\partial^{|\sigma|}s^{j}}{\partial x_{1}^{\sigma_{1}}...\partial x_{n}^{\sigma_{n}}}(a)
.
Throughout the paper,
C^{\infty}(M)
will denote the algebra of
smooth functions on
M
whereas
\mathcal{F}_{k}(\pi)
will denote
the algebra of
smooth functions on

J^{k}(\pi)
. In some
cases, to emphasize that a function
f\in\mathcal{F}_{k}(\pi)
depends
on
(x_{i},u_{\sigma}^{j})
with
|\sigma|\leq k
, it is used the
notation
f=f(x,u^{(k)})
. Moreover, for lower order
k
, notations
like
u_{x_{i}}^{j},u_{x_{i}x_{j}}^{j},...
will be used instead
of multi-index notation.

The
k
-th order jet space
J^{k}(\pi)
is naturally equipped with
the
Cartan distribution

\mathcal{C}^{k}(\pi)
, which is
spanned by tangent planes to graphs of
k
-th order jet prolongations
of sections of
\pi
. In coordinates
\mathcal{C}^{k}(\pi)
can
be described either as the annihilator of the Pfaffian system
\{\omega_{\sigma}^{j}=du_{\sigma}^{j}-\sum_{i}u_{\sigma+1_{i}}^{j}dx_{i}:\;0\leq|\sigma|\leq k-1,\;j=1,...,m\}
,
or as the distribution generated by the vector fields
\{\partial_{u_{\sigma+1_{i}}^{j}},\,D_{i}^{(k)}:\;0\leq|\sigma|\leq k-1,\;j=1,...,m\}
,
with
D_{i}^{(k)}
denoting the
k
-th order
truncated total
derivative operators
, i.e.,
D_{i}^{(k)}:=\partial_{x_{i}}+\sum_{|\sigma|\leq k-1}{\displaystyle u_{\sigma+1_{i}}^{j}\partial_{u_{\sigma}^{j}}}.

It is easy to show that integral manifolds
\Sigma
of
\mathcal{C}^{k}(\pi)

with
independence condition

\text{$\Omega$=}dx_{1}\wedge...\wedge dx_{n}\neq 0

(i.e., such that
\Omega|_{\Sigma}\neq 0
) are
k
-th order prolongations
of sections of
\pi
(see for instance
[
40
]
).

A
k
-th order system of differential equations
\mathcal{E}
, for
the sections of
\pi
, can be geometrically interpreted as a submanifold

\mathcal{E}\subset J^{k}(\pi)
and its solutions are just sections

s
of
\pi
whose
k
-order prolongations
j_{k}(s)
lay on
\mathcal{E}
.
Hence, solutions of
\mathcal{E}
are sections of
\pi
whose
k
-order
prolongation are integral manifolds of the
induced Cartan
distribution

\mathcal{C}^{k}(\mathcal{E}):=\mathcal{C}^{k}(\pi)\cap T\mathcal{E}

over
\mathcal{E}
.

The
infinite jet space

J^{\infty}(\pi)
is the inverse
limit of the sequence of surjections
M\overset{\pi}{\longleftarrow}J^{0}(\pi)\overset{\pi_{1,0}}{\longleftarrow}...\overset{\pi_{k,k-1}}{\longleftarrow}J^{k}(\pi)\overset{\pi_{k+1,k}}{\longleftarrow}...\;.

By definition, any
\theta\in J^{\infty}(\pi)
is a sequence
\theta=\{\theta_{r}\}

of points
\theta_{r}\in J^{r}(\pi)
such that
\pi_{h,k}(\theta_{h})=\theta_{k}
,
for all
h>k
. Of course
J^{\infty}(\pi)
is not a finite dimensional
manifold, nevertheless one may introduce a differential calculus on

J^{\infty}(\pi)
by making use of standard constructions of differential
calculus over commutative algebras
[
8
,
22
]
. Indeed, by defining
the exterior algebra
\Lambda^{*}(\pi)
of
differential forms
on

J^{\infty}(\pi)
as the direct limit of the sequences of embeddings

\Lambda^{*}\left(J^{k}(\pi)\right)\overset{\pi_{k+1,k}^{*}}{\longrightarrow}\Lambda^{*}\left(J^{k+1}(\pi)\right)\overset{\pi_{k+2,k+1}^{*}}{\longrightarrow}....\;
,
one also defines the commutative algebra of
smooth functions
on

J^{\infty}(\pi)
as
\mathcal{F}(\pi):=\Lambda^{0}(\pi)
. Since

\Lambda^{*}\left(J^{k}(\pi)\right)\subset\Lambda^{*}\left(J^{k+1}(\pi)\right)\subset...\;,

then
\Lambda^{*}(\pi)
is a filtered algebra and one may think of
any
h
-form over
J^{\infty}(\pi)
as an
h
-form on some finite
order jet space; this is true in particular for
\mathcal{F}(\pi)
.
It follows that the
exterior differential

d
naturally
extends to the exterior algebra
\Lambda^{*}(\pi)
and defines the
de Rham complex of
J^{\infty}(\pi)
. Then, the
\mathcal{F}(\pi)
-module

\mathcal{D}(\pi)
of
vector fields
on
J^{\infty}(\pi)

is by definition the module of all derivations of
\mathcal{F}(\pi)

which preserve the natural filtration
\mathcal{F}_{k}(\pi)\subset\mathcal{F}_{h}(\pi)\subset...

of
\mathcal{F}(\pi)
, i.e., any
Z\in\mathcal{D}(\pi)
has an associated

filtration degree

l\in\mathbb{N}
such that
Z(\mathcal{F}_{k}(\pi))\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{k+l}(\pi)
,

\forall k\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence, in coordinates, a vector field
Z

can be identified with a formal series
Z=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}+\sum_{\sigma}\sum_{j}\beta_{\sigma}^{j}\partial_{u_{\sigma}^{j}}
,
with
\alpha_{i},\beta_{\sigma}^{j}\in\mathcal{F}(\pi)
. It follows
that vector fields on
J^{\infty}(\pi)
, contrary to the finite dimensional
case, do not have in general an associated flow. However, any
Z

with zero filtration degree is the inverse limit of an inverse sequence

\{Z^{(k)}\}
of vector fields on finite order jet spaces, hence
one may define the flow of
Z
as being the inverse limit of a sequence
of flows on finite order jet spaces. Moreover, one can define
Lie
derivative
of functions, vector fields or forms on
J^{\infty}(\pi)

in a completely algebraic way. For instance, the Lie derivative of
a function
G\in\mathcal{F}(\pi)
along a vector field
Z\in\mathcal{D}(\pi)

is
L_{Z}(G):=Z(G)
, and the Lie derivative of
Y\in\mathcal{D}(\pi)

along
Z
is
L_{Z}Y:=\left[Z,Y\right]
. Whereas, the Lie derivative
of a form
\omega\in\Lambda^{*}(\pi)
along
Z
is defined as
L_{Z}\omega:=i_{Z}(d\omega)+d(i_{Z}\omega)
,
where
i_{Z}
denotes the
inner product
operation
i_{Z}:\Lambda^{h}(\pi)\longrightarrow\Lambda^{h-1}(\pi)
.

Also
J^{\infty}(\pi)
is naturally equipped with a Cartan distribution
denoted by
\mathcal{C}(\pi)
and defined as the inverse limit of
the sequence of surjections
\mathcal{C}^{1}(\pi)\overset{\pi_{1,0*}}{\longleftarrow}\mathcal{C}^{2}(\pi)\overset{\pi_{2,1*}}{\longleftarrow}...\overset{\pi_{k,k-1*}}{\longleftarrow}\mathcal{C}^{k}(\pi)\overset{\pi_{k+1,k*}}{\longleftarrow}...\;.

In coordinates
\mathcal{C}(\pi)
can be described either as the
annihilator of the Pfaffian system
\{\omega_{\sigma}^{j}=du_{\sigma}^{j}-\sum_{i}u_{\sigma+1_{i}}^{j}dx_{i}:\;|\sigma|\geq 0,\;j=1,...,m\}
,
or as the distribution generated by the totality of vector fields

\{\partial_{u_{\sigma+1_{i}}^{j}},\,D_{i}:\;|\sigma|\geq 0,\;j=1,...,m\}
,
where

D_{i}:=\partial_{x_{i}}+{\displaystyle\sum_{|\rho|\geq 0}\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{\rho+1_{i}}^{j}\partial_{u_{\rho}^{j}}}

denote the
total derivative operators
.

Given a
k
-th order equation (or system)
\mathcal{E}=\{\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0}\}\subset J^{k}(\pi)
,
under regularity conditions one may consider the
r
-
th order
prolongation

\mathcal{E}^{(r)}=\{D_{\mu}\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0}:\;0\leq|\mu|\leq r\}
.
Equation
\mathcal{E}
will be called
formally integrable

if, and only if,
\mathcal{E}^{(r)}
are submanifolds of
J^{k+r}(\pi)

and the maps
\pi_{k+r+1,k+r}:\mathcal{E}^{(r+1)}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}^{(r)}

are smooth fiber bundles, for any
r\geq 0
.

By definition the
infinite prolongation

\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}=\{D_{\mu}\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0}:\;|\mu|\geq 0\}
,
of a formally integrable equation
\mathcal{E}
, is the inverse limit
of the sequence of fiber bundles
\pi_{k+r+1,k+r}:\mathcal{E}^{(r+1)}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}^{(r)}
.
By restricting
\Lambda^{*}(\pi)
to
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
one
gets the exterior algebra
\Lambda^{*}(\mathcal{E})
of
differential
forms on

\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
and in particular the algebra

\mathcal{F}(\pi)
of
smooth functions on

\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
.
Moreover, since any prolongation
\mathcal{E}^{(r)}
is naturally
equipped with an induced Cartan distribution
\mathcal{C}^{k+r}(\mathcal{E}^{(r)})
,
also
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
is naturally equipped with an induced

Cartan distribution
denoted by
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{E})

and defined as the inverse limit of the sequence of surjections
\mathcal{C}^{k}(\mathcal{E})\overset{\pi_{k+1,k*}}{\longleftarrow}\mathcal{C}^{k+1}(\mathcal{E}^{(1)})\overset{\pi_{k+2,k+1*}}{\longleftarrow}...\;
.
In coordinates, one has that
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{E})=\left\langle\bar{D}_{1},...,\bar{D}_{n}\right\rangle=\text{Ann}\left\{\bar{\omega}_{\sigma}^{j}:\,j=1,...,m,\quad|\sigma|\geq 0\right\}
,
where
\bar{D}_{i}
and
\bar{\omega}_{\sigma}^{j}
are the restrictions
to
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
of the total derivatives and Cartan forms,
respectively.

Now, since
\mathcal{C}(\pi)
is totally horizontal with respect
to the mapping
\pi_{\infty}:J^{\infty}(\pi)\rightarrow M
, the tangent
bundle
\mathcal{T}\left(\pi\right)
on
J^{\infty}(\pi)
decomposes
as
\mathcal{T}\left(\pi\right)=\mathcal{V}(\pi)\oplus\mathcal{C}(\pi)
,
where
\mathcal{V}(\pi):=Ker\left(\pi_{\infty}\right)_{*}
. Dually
one has
\Lambda^{1}(\pi)=\Lambda^{(1,0)}(\pi)\oplus\Lambda^{(0,1)}(\pi)
,
where
\Lambda^{(1,0)}(\pi):=Ann\left(\mathcal{V}(\pi)\right)
and

\Lambda^{(0,1)}(\mathcal{\pi}):=Ann\left(\mathcal{C}(\pi)\right)

are the
\mathcal{F}(\pi)
-modules of horizontal and vertical
1
-forms
on
J^{\infty}(\pi)
locally generated by
\{dx_{i}\}
and Cartan
forms
\left\{\omega_{\sigma}^{j}\right\}
, respectively. More
in general, by considering
\Lambda^{(p,q)}(\pi)=\left(\bigwedge^{p}\Lambda^{(1,0)}(\pi)\right)\bigwedge\left(\bigwedge^{q}\Lambda^{(0,1)}(\pi)\right)
,
the
\mathcal{F}(\pi)
-module of
r
-forms on
J^{\infty}(\pi)

decomposes as
\Lambda^{r}\left(\pi\right)=\bigoplus_{p+q=r}\Lambda^{(p,q)}\left(\pi\right)
.
By definition we set
\mathcal{F}(\pi)=\Lambda^{(0,0)}(\pi)
. Accordingly,
the exterior differential splits into the sum
d=d_{H}+d_{V}
of
the horizontal and vertical differentials
d_{H}:\Lambda^{(p,q)}(\mathcal{\pi})\rightarrow\Lambda^{(p+1,q)}(\pi)

and
d_{V}:\Lambda^{(p,q)}(\pi)\rightarrow\Lambda^{(p,q+1)}(\pi)
,
satisfying
d_{H}^{2}=d_{V}^{2}=0
and
d_{H}\circ d_{V}=-d_{V}\circ d_{H}
.
In coordinates, horizontal and vertical differentials can be easily
computed, since on any function
f\in\mathcal{F}(\pi)
they act as
graded derivations on
\Lambda^{*}(\pi)
such that
d_{H}f:=\sum_{i}D_{i}(f)dx_{i}

and
d_{V}f:=\sum_{\sigma}\sum_{j}\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_{\sigma}^{j}}\omega_{\sigma}^{j}
.

Analogously, given a formally integrable equation
\mathcal{E}
,
since
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{E})
is totally horizontal with respect
to the mapping
\bar{\pi}_{\infty}:\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}\rightarrow M
,
the tangent bundle
\mathcal{T}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)
on
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}

decomposes as
\mathcal{T}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)=\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{E})\oplus\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{E})
,
where
\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{E}):=Ker\left(\bar{\pi}_{\infty*}\right)

is the vertical bundle on
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
. Hence the
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{E})
-modules

\Lambda^{(1,0)}(\mathcal{E})
and
\Lambda^{(0,1)}(\mathcal{E})

of horizontal and vertical
1
-forms on
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
,
locally generated by
\{dx^{i}\}
and restricted Cartan forms
\left\{\bar{\omega}_{\sigma}^{j}:=\left.\omega_{\sigma}^{j}\right|_{\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}}\right\}
,
can be used to decompose the
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{E})
-module of

r
-forms on
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
as
\Lambda^{r}\left(\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}\right)=\bigoplus_{p+q=r}\Lambda^{(p,q)}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)
.
By definition we set
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{E})=\Lambda^{(0,0)}(\mathcal{E})
.
Accordingly, on
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
the exterior differential

d
(still denoted by
d
, for ease of notation) splits into the
sum
d=\bar{d}_{H}+\bar{d}_{V}
of the horizontal and vertical differentials

\bar{d}_{H}:\Lambda^{(p,q)}(\mathcal{E})\rightarrow\Lambda^{(p+1,q)}(\mathcal{E})

and
\bar{d}_{V}:\Lambda^{(p,q)}(\mathcal{E})\rightarrow\Lambda^{(p,q+1)}(\mathcal{E})
,
which satisfy
\bar{d}_{H}^{2}=\bar{d}_{V}^{2}=0
and
\bar{d}_{H}\circ\bar{d}_{V}=-\bar{d}_{V}\circ\bar{d}_{H}
.
In coordinates,
\bar{d}_{H}
and
\bar{d}_{V}
can be easily computed,
since they act as graded derivations on
\Lambda^{*}(\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)})

and for any function
f\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{E})
they are such
that
\bar{d}_{H}f:=\sum_{i}\bar{D}_{i}(f)dx_{i}
and
\bar{d}_{V}f:=\sum_{\sigma}\sum_{j}\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_{\sigma}^{j}}\bar{\omega}_{\sigma}^{j}
,
where
\bar{D}_{i}
denote the total derivatives restricted to
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
.
For ease of notation, we will denote
\Lambda^{(p,0)}(\pi)
and
\Lambda^{(p,0)}(\mathcal{E})

by
\bar{\Lambda}^{p}(\pi)
and
\bar{\Lambda}^{p}(\mathcal{E})
,
respectively.

Starting from these algebraic structures in the algebra of differential
forms on
\mathcal{E}
, new constructions and new notions can be
introduced further. For instance, using the horizontal differentials

\bar{d}_{H}:\overline{\Lambda}^{p}(\mathcal{E})\rightarrow\overline{\Lambda}^{p+1}(\mathcal{E})
,
one can consider the notion of
conservation law
for
\mathcal{E}
,
which is a closed horizontal form
\mu\in\overline{\Lambda}^{n-1}(\mathcal{E})
,
i.e., a horizontal
(n-1)
-form on
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
satisfying

\bar{d}_{H}\mu=0
. On the other hand, since an exact horizontal
form is trivially closed, one can naturally limit itself to consider
closed horizontal
(n-1)
-forms up to exact horizontal forms. Thus,
a conservation law can also be understood as a cohomology class
[\mu]\in\overline{H}^{n-1}(\mathcal{E})
,
i.e.,
[\mu]=\left\{\mu+\bar{d}_{H}\rho:\,\rho\in\overline{\Lambda}^{n-2}(\mathcal{E})\right\}
.
For instance, when
n=2
, a conservation law is locally described
by an horizontal
1
-form
Adx_{1}+Bdx_{2}
on
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}

such that
\bar{D}_{2}A-\bar{D}_{1}B=0
, i.e.,
D_{2}A-D_{1}B=0

on
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
.

Moreover, given a matrix Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, one may consider
the exterior algebras
\mathfrak{g}\otimes\Lambda^{*}(\pi)
and
\mathfrak{g}\otimes\Lambda^{*}(\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)})

of
\mathfrak{g}
-valued forms on
J^{\infty}(\pi)
and
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
,
respectively. The graded algebra of
\mathfrak{g}
-valued horizontal
forms on
J^{\infty}(\pi)
and
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
, will be
denoted by
\mathfrak{g}\otimes\bar{\Lambda}^{*}\left(\pi\right)=\bigoplus_{p}\mathfrak{g}\otimes\bar{\Lambda}^{p}(\pi)

and
\mathfrak{g}\otimes\bar{\Lambda}^{*}\left({\cal E}\right)=\bigoplus_{p}\mathfrak{g}\otimes\bar{\Lambda}^{p}\left({\cal E}\right)
,
respectively. By definition,
\mathfrak{g}
-valued horizontal
p
-forms
on
J^{\infty}(\pi)
(resp.,
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
) are generated
by
\mathfrak{g}
-valued
p
-forms
A\omega
, with
A

\mathfrak{g}
-valued
functions on
J^{\infty}(\pi)
(resp.,
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
).
Then, one may define a skew-symmetric product
[\;,\;]
by linearly
extending the product
\left[A_{1}\omega_{1},A_{2}\omega_{2}\right]:=\left[A_{1},A_{2}\right]\omega_{1}\wedge\omega_{2}
,
between generators. One can check that
[\;,\;]
satisfies the following
properties: (i)
[\rho,\sigma]=-(-1)^{rs}[\sigma,\rho]
; (ii)
(-1)^{rt}[\rho,[\sigma,\tau]]+(-1)^{sr}[\sigma,[\tau,\rho]]+(-1)^{ts}[\tau,[\rho,\sigma]]=0
;
(iii)
d_{H}[\rho,\sigma]=[d_{H}\rho,\sigma]+(-1)^{r}[\rho,d_{H}\sigma]
,
analogously for
\bar{d}_{H}
. Where
r,s
and
t
are the degrees
of
\rho,\sigma
and
\tau
, respectively. Also, one may define
an exterior product
\wedge
on
\mathfrak{g}\otimes\bar{\Lambda}^{*}\left(\pi\right)
,
or
\mathfrak{g}\otimes\bar{\Lambda}^{*}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)
,
by linearly extending the product
A_{1}\omega_{1}\wedge A_{2}\omega_{2}=A_{1}A_{2}\omega_{1}\wedge\omega_{1}
.

This allows one to introduce the notion of
zero-curvature
representation
(ZCR) of
{\cal E}
, that is a
\mathfrak{g}
-valued
non-vanishing
1
-form
\alpha\in\mathfrak{g}\otimes\bar{\Lambda}^{1}\left({\cal E}\right)

such that

\bar{d}_{H}\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\left[\alpha,\alpha\right]=0.

(1)

This is a very important notion in the theory of integrable equations
with
2
independent variables
[
1
,
41
,
44
]
.
In such a case, by taking
\alpha=A\,dx_{1}+Bdx_{2}
, condition (
1
)
reads
D_{2}A-D_{1}B+[A,B]=0
on
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
. Here
we notice that (
1
) can also be equivalently written as

\bar{d}_{H}\alpha-\alpha\wedge\alpha=0
. Moreover, since
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}\subset J^{\infty}(\pi)
,
any element of
\mathfrak{g}\otimes\bar{\Lambda}^{1}(\mathcal{E})

can be identified with an element of
\mathfrak{g}\otimes\bar{\Lambda}^{1}(\pi)
.
Hence, in the outer geometry, (
1
) can also be rewritten
as
d_{H}\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\left[\alpha,\alpha\right]=0\;\mbox{mod}\,\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
.
In particular, when
\mathcal{E}=\{F^{j}=0,\,j=1,...,h\}
, under
regularity assumptions (i.e., if any prolongation
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
,

h\geq 0
, is totally non-degenerating
[
34
]
) equation (
1
)
can also be rewritten in “characteristic” form
d_{H}\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\left[\alpha,\alpha\right]=\sum D_{\sigma}(F^{j})\omega_{j}^{\sigma}
,
where
\omega_{j}^{\sigma}\in\mathfrak{g}\otimes\bar{\Lambda}^{2}(\pi)
.
Hence in general (
1
) holds only modulo some differential
consequences of
\mathcal{E}
, thus in general (
1
) is
not equivalent to
\{F^{j}=0,\,j=1,...,h\}
.

2.2
\mathcal{C}
-morphisms (or Lie-Bäcklund maps)

In this subsection
J^{\infty}(\pi)
and
J^{\infty}(\pi^{\prime})
will
denote the infinite jet spaces of sections of two fiber bundles
\pi:E\rightarrow M

and
\pi^{\prime}:E^{\prime}\rightarrow M^{\prime}
with
canonical coordinates

\{x_{i},u_{\sigma}^{j}\}

and
\{x^{\prime}_{i},u^{\prime}{}_{\sigma}^{j}\}
, respectively. In particular we
assume that
n=\dim M=\dim M^{\prime}
, since in the paper we are mainly
concerned with this case.

A
\mathcal{C}
-morphism, also referred to as a Lie-Bäcklund transformations

[
4
,
22
]
, is a smooth map
\mathcal{B}:J^{\infty}(\pi)\rightarrow J^{\infty}(\pi^{\prime})
,
such that
\mathcal{B}_{*}\mathcal{C}_{\theta}(\pi)\subset\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{B}(\theta)}(\pi^{\prime})
,
for any
\theta\in J^{\infty}(\pi)
, i.e.,

\mathcal{B}_{*}\mathcal{C}(\pi)\subset\mathcal{C}(\pi^{\prime}).

(2)

Notice that
\mathcal{B}_{*}\mathcal{C}(\pi)\subset\mathcal{C}(\pi^{\prime})

is equivalent to
\mathcal{B}^{*}\left(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}(\pi^{\prime})}\right)\subset\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}(\pi)}
,
where
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}(\pi)}
is the EDS generated by Cartan
(or multi-contact) forms
\omega_{\sigma}^{j}=du_{\sigma}^{j}-\sum_{i}u_{\sigma+1_{i}}^{j}dx_{i}

on
J^{\infty}(\pi)
, analogously
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}(\pi^{\prime})}
.

Notice that in general these transformations are generalizations of
Lie transformations (i.e., point and contact transformations) that
need not to be diffeomorphisms. Indeed, as shown by Bäcklund
[
6
]

(see also
[
4
]
), only Lie transformations are invertible.
Moreover, by
\mathcal{B}^{*}\left(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}(\pi^{\prime})}\right)\subset\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}(\pi)}

it follows that under such a transformation the image of an integral
manifolds of Cartan distribution is still an integral manifold.

In canonical coordinates, a
\mathcal{C}
-morphism (Lie-Bäcklund
transformation) has the form

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}x^{\prime}_{i}=\xi^{i}\left(x,u^{(k)}\right),\\
u^{\prime}{}^{j}=\nu^{j}\left(x,u^{(k)}\right),\\
\vdots\\
u^{\prime}{}_{\sigma}^{j}=\nu_{\sigma}^{j}\left(x,u^{(k+|\sigma|)}\right),\qquad|\sigma|\geq 0\end{array}\right.

(3)

where in view of
\mathcal{B}^{*}\left(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}(\pi^{\prime})}\right)\subset\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}(\pi)}

the functions
\xi^{i}
and
\nu_{\sigma}^{j}
are such that
d\nu_{\sigma}^{j}-\sum_{i}\nu_{\sigma+1_{i}}^{j}d\xi^{i}=0\mod{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}}}
.

Like in the finite order case, integral manifolds
\Sigma
of
\mathcal{C}(\pi)

with independence condition
\text{$\Omega$=}dx_{1}\wedge...\wedge dx_{n}\neq 0

(i.e., such that
\Omega|_{\Sigma}\neq 0
) are
\infty
-th order
prolongations of sections of
\pi
. One has the following

Proposition 1
.

A
\mathcal{C}
-morphism maps an integral
manifold
\Sigma
of
\mathcal{C}(\pi)
with independence condition

dx_{1}\wedge...\wedge dx_{n}\neq 0
to an integral manifold
\Sigma^{\prime}

of
\mathcal{C}(\pi^{\prime})
with independence condition
dx^{\prime}_{1}\wedge...\wedge dx^{\prime}_{n}\neq 0

whenever the regularity assumption

\det\left(D_{s}\xi^{i}\right)\neq 0

(4)

is satisfied on
\Sigma.

Proof.

Indeed, being
\Sigma
the infinite prolongation of a section
\mathfrak{s}

locally described as
\mathfrak{s}(x)=(x,u(x))
, one has
\left(\mathcal{B}\circ\mathfrak{s}^{(\infty)}\right)^{*}(\omega^{\prime}{}_{\sigma}^{j})=\allowbreak\left(\mathfrak{s}^{(\infty)}\right)^{*}\circ\mathcal{B}^{*}\left(\omega^{\prime}{}_{\sigma}^{j}\right)\allowbreak=0\mod{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}(\pi)}}
,
since the graph of
\mathfrak{s}^{(\infty)}
is an integral manifold
of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}(\pi)}
. Thus the graph
\Sigma^{\prime}
of

\mathcal{B}\circ\mathfrak{s}^{(\infty)}
describes another integral
manifold of
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}(\pi^{\prime})}
. On the other hand
whenever
\det\left(D_{s}\xi^{i}\right)\neq 0
along the graph of

\mathfrak{s}^{(k)}
(i.e.,
\mathfrak{s}^{(k)}{}^{*}\left(\det\left(D_{s}\xi^{i}\right)\right)\neq 0
),
the first
n
-equations
\{x^{\prime}_{i}=\xi^{i}(x,u^{(k)}(x)),\,i=1,...,n\}

of (
3
) restricted to
\mathfrak{s}^{(k)}
can be
locally solved with respect to
(x_{1},...,x_{n})
. Thus, there exists
a local diffeomorphism
x^{\prime}=\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{s}}(x)=\left(\xi\circ\mathfrak{s}^{(k)}\right)(x)

that allows one to pass from the parametrization
\left(\mathcal{B}\circ\mathfrak{s}^{(\infty)}\right)(x)

of
\Sigma^{\prime}
to the new parametrization
\left(\mathcal{B}\circ\mathfrak{s}^{(\infty)}\circ\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{s}}^{-1}\right)(x^{\prime})
.

Then, since

\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(\mathcal{B}\circ\mathfrak{s}^{(\infty)}\circ\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{s}}^{-1}\right)^{*}\left(dx^{\prime}_{1}\wedge...\wedge dx^{\prime}_{n}\right)&=\left(\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{s}}^{-1}\right)^{*}\circ s^{(\infty)\,*}\circ\mathcal{B}^{*}\left(dx^{\prime}_{1}\wedge...\wedge dx^{\prime}_{n}\right)\\
&=\left(\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{s}}^{-1}\right)^{*}\circ s^{(\infty)\,*}\circ\left(d\left(\xi^{1}(x,u^{(k)})\right)\wedge...\wedge d\left(\xi^{n}\left(x,u^{(k)}\right)\right)\right)\\
&=\left(\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{s}}^{-1}\right)^{*}\circ\left(d\left(\xi^{1}\circ s^{(k)}(x)\right)\wedge...\wedge d\left(\xi^{n}\circ s^{(k)}(x)\right)\right)\\
&=dx_{1}\wedge...\wedge dx_{n},\end{array}

it turns out that
\left(\mathcal{B}\circ\mathfrak{s}^{(\infty)}\circ\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{s}}^{-1}\right)(x^{\prime})

is the infinite prolongation of the section of
\pi^{\prime}

\mathfrak{s}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})=\left(\mathcal{B}^{(0)}\circ\mathfrak{s}^{(k)}\circ\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{s}}^{-1}\right)(x^{\prime})=\left(x^{\prime},\nu\left(x,u^{(k)}\left(\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{s}}^{-1}(x^{\prime})\right)\right)\right).

∎

Remark 2
.

Proposition
1
entails
that a
\mathcal{C}
-morphism sends infinite prolongations of (local)
sections to infinite prolongations of (local) sections, whenever (
4
)
is satisfied. Indeed, it is noteworthy to stress that the action of
such a
\mathcal{C}
-morphism
\mathcal{B}
on the infinite prolongation

\mathfrak{s}^{(\infty)}(x)
of a (local) section
\mathfrak{s}(x)

is not necessarily defined for any
x
, because
\det\left(D_{s}\xi^{i}\right)(\mathfrak{s}^{(\infty)}(x))

could be zero at some points. Also, we stress that (
4
)
entails that the push-forward
\mathcal{B}_{*\theta}:\mathcal{C}_{\theta}(\pi)\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{B}(\theta)}(\pi^{\prime})

establishes an isomorphism for any
\theta\in J^{\infty}(\pi)
.

We will refer to a
\mathcal{C}
-morphism (
3
) satisfying
regularity assumption (
4
) as a
regular
\mathcal{C}
-morphism
.
One also have the following

Proposition 3
.

A regular
\mathcal{C}
-morphism (
3
)
is completely determined by its lower components

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}x^{\prime}_{i}=\xi^{i}\left(x,u^{(k)}\right),\\
u^{\prime}{}^{j}=\nu^{j}\left(x,u^{(k)}\right),\end{array}\right.

through the prolongation formulas

\left(\begin{array}[]{lll}\nu_{\sigma+1_{1}}^{1}&\cdots&\nu_{\sigma+1_{1}}^{m}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\nu_{\sigma+1_{n}}^{1}&\cdots&\nu_{\sigma+1_{n}}^{m}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{lll}D_{1}\xi^{1}&\cdots&D_{1}\xi^{n}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
D_{n}\xi^{1}&\cdots&D_{n}\xi^{n}\end{array}\right)^{-1}\cdot\left(\begin{array}[]{lll}D_{1}\nu_{\sigma}^{1}&\cdots&D_{1}\nu_{\sigma}^{m}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
D_{n}\nu_{\sigma}^{1}&\cdots&D_{n}\nu_{\sigma}^{m}\end{array}\right).

(5)

Proof.

Indeed, in view of the decomposition
d=d_{H}+d_{V},
one readily
gets that

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{B}^{*}\left(\omega^{\prime}{}_{\sigma}^{j}\right)&=\mathcal{B}^{*}\left(du^{\prime}{}_{\sigma}^{j}-\sum_{i}u^{\prime}{}_{\sigma+1_{i}}^{j}dx^{\prime}_{i}\right)=d\nu_{\sigma}^{j}-\sum_{i}\nu_{\sigma+1_{i}}^{j}d\xi^{i}\\
&=d_{H}\nu_{\sigma}^{j}-\sum_{i}\nu_{\sigma+1_{i}}^{j}d_{H}\xi^{i}+d_{V}\nu_{\sigma}^{j}-\sum_{i}\nu_{\sigma+1_{i}}^{j}d_{V}\xi^{i}\\
&=\sum_{s}\left(D_{s}\nu_{\sigma}^{j}-\sum_{i}\nu_{\sigma+1_{i}}^{j}D_{s}\xi^{i}\right)dx_{s}\mod{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}(\pi)},}\end{array}

in view of the identity
d_{V}\nu_{\sigma}^{j}-\sum_{i}\nu_{\sigma+1_{i}}^{j}d_{V}\xi^{i}=0\mod{\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}(\pi)}}
.
Thus
\mathcal{B}^{*}\left(\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}(\pi^{\prime})}\right)\subset\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{C}(\pi)}

if and only if
D_{s}\nu_{\sigma}^{j}-\sum_{i}\nu_{\sigma+1_{i}}^{j}D_{s}\xi^{i}=0
,
that is

\left(\begin{array}[]{lll}D_{1}\xi^{1}&\cdots&D_{1}\xi^{n}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
D_{n}\xi^{1}&\cdots&D_{n}\xi^{n}\end{array}\right)\cdot\left(\begin{array}[]{lll}\nu_{\sigma+1_{1}}^{1}&\cdots&\nu_{\sigma+1_{1}}^{m}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\nu_{\sigma+1_{n}}^{1}&\cdots&\nu_{\sigma+1_{n}}^{m}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{lll}D_{1}\nu_{\sigma}^{1}&\cdots&D_{1}\nu_{\sigma}^{m}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
D_{n}\nu_{\sigma}^{1}&\cdots&D_{n}\nu_{\sigma}^{m}\end{array}\right).

Hence, in view of (
4
) one gets (
5
).
∎

Moreover, in view of (
2
), one also has the following

Lemma 4
.

For any
\mathcal{C}
-morphism
\mathcal{B}:J^{\infty}(\pi)\rightarrow J^{\infty}(\pi^{\prime})

one has

D_{i}\circ\mathcal{B}^{*}=\sum_{j}\alpha_{ij}\mathcal{B}^{*}\circ D_{j}^{\prime},

(6)

for some smooth functions
\alpha_{ij}
on
J^{\infty}(\pi)
. In
particular, if
\mathcal{B}
is regular, one has
\det\left(\alpha_{ij}\right)\neq 0

and

\mathcal{B}^{*}\circ D_{j}^{\prime}=\sum_{i}\alpha^{ij}D_{i}\circ\mathcal{B}^{*},

(7)

with
(\alpha^{ij})=(\alpha_{ij})^{-1}
.

Proof.

The invariance condition (
2
) is equivalent to say that
for any
\theta\in J^{\infty}(\pi)
one has
\mathcal{B}_{*}(\left.D_{i}\right|_{\theta})=\sum_{j}a_{ij}\left.D_{j}\right|_{\mathcal{B}(\theta)}
,
where
a_{ij}
are some constants. Hence, for any function
f
on

J^{\infty}(\pi^{\prime})
one has that
D_{i}\left(\mathcal{B}^{*}(f)\right)(\theta)=\sum_{j}a_{ij}D_{j}(f)(\mathcal{B}(\theta))=\sum_{j}a_{ij}\mathcal{B}^{*}\left(D_{j}(f)\right)(\theta)
.
Then, since this holds for any
\theta
one readily gets (
6
).

Now, applying (
6
) to
f=x^{\prime}_{s}
and using the fact
that
\mathcal{B}^{*}\left(x^{\prime}_{s}\right)=\xi^{s}\left(x,u^{(k)}\right)

and
\mathcal{B}^{*}\left(D_{j}^{\prime}\left(x^{\prime}_{s}\right)\right)=\mathcal{B}^{*}\delta_{js}=\delta_{js}
,
one readily gets
D_{i}\xi^{s}=\alpha_{is}
and hence (
7
)
readily follows by (
4
) and (
6
).
∎

We will use the following

Definition 5
.

Let
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}{}^{(\infty)}
be
the infinite prolongations of two formally integrable equations
\mathcal{E}\subset J^{k}(\pi)

and
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\subset J^{l}(\pi^{\prime})
, respectively. By a (regular)

\mathcal{C}
-morphism from
\mathcal{E}
to
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
we
mean a regular
\mathcal{C}
-morphism
\mathcal{B}:J^{\infty}(\pi)\rightarrow J^{\infty}(\pi^{\prime})

such that
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)})\subseteq\mathcal{E}^{\prime}{}^{(\infty)}
.

In view of Proposition
1
and Remark
2
,
a regular
\mathcal{C}
-morphism from
\mathcal{E}
to
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}

transforms solutions of
\mathcal{E}
to solutions of
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
.
Moreover, one has the following

Proposition 6
.

A regular
\mathcal{C}
-morphism
\mathcal{B}:J^{\infty}(\pi)\rightarrow J^{\infty}(\pi^{\prime})

is a
\mathcal{C}
-morphism from
\mathcal{E}=\{\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0}\}

to
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=\{\mathbf{F^{\prime}}=\mathbf{0}\}
if and only if

\mathcal{B}^{*}(\mathbf{F^{\prime}})=0\mod{\{D{}_{\sigma}\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0}:|\sigma|\geq 0\}}.

(8)

.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}=\{D{}_{\sigma}\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0}:|\sigma|\geq 0\}

and
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}{}^{(\infty)}=\{D{}_{\rho}^{\prime}\mathbf{F}^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}:|\rho|\geq 0\}
,
the condition
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)})\subseteq\mathcal{E}^{\prime}{}^{(\infty)}

is equivalent to
\mathcal{B}^{*}\left(D^{\prime}_{\rho}\left(\mathbf{F}^{\prime}\right)\right)=0\mod{\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}}
,
for any multi-index
\rho
. Hence, in view of (
7
),

\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)})\subseteq\mathcal{E}^{\prime}{}^{(\infty)}

is equivalent to

D_{\mu}\mathcal{B}^{*}\left(\mathbf{F}^{\prime}\right)=0\mod{\{D{}_{\sigma}\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0}:|\sigma|\geq 0\}},

(9)

for any multi-index
\mu
. Thus (
9
) implies
\mathcal{B}^{*}(\mathbf{F^{\prime}})=0\mod{\{D{}_{\sigma}\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0}:|\sigma|\geq 0\}}
,
for
\mu=0
. Conversely, by totally deriving
\mathcal{B}^{*}(\mathbf{F^{\prime}})=0\mod{\{D{}_{\sigma}\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0}:|\sigma|\geq 0\}}

one gets (
9
).
∎

It is noteworthy to remark that, being the order of
\mathcal{B}^{*}(\mathbf{F^{\prime}})

always finite, the analysis of condition (
8
) in practice
only requires the consideration of a finite number of differential
consequences of
\mathbf{F=0}
.

Example 7
.

An example of regular
\mathcal{C}
-morphism
between two equations is the Cole-Hopf transformation from the heat
equation
\mathcal{E}=\{u_{t}-u_{xx}=0\}
to the Burgers equation

\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=\{u^{\prime}_{t}-u^{\prime}_{xx}-2u^{\prime}u^{\prime}_{x}=0\}
, where
u=u(x,t)

and
u^{\prime}=u^{\prime}(x,t)
. Indeed the Cole-Hopf transformation is defined
by
u^{\prime}=u_{x}/u
, at the points where
u\neq 0
. In this case, by
repeatedly applying (
5
), one can readily check that

u^{\prime}_{x}=\frac{u_{xx}}{u}-\left(\frac{u_{x}}{u}\right)^{2},\qquad u^{\prime}_{xx}=\frac{u_{xxx}}{u}-3\left(\frac{u_{x}}{u}\right)\,\left(\frac{u_{xx}}{u}\right)+2\left(\frac{u_{x}}{u}\right)^{3},\quad\ldots\;.

On the other hand

u^{\prime}_{t}=\frac{u_{xt}-u_{t}\,u^{\prime}}{u}=\frac{u_{xxx}}{u}-\left(\frac{u_{xx}}{u}\right)\,\left(\frac{u_{x}}{u}\right)\mod{\mathcal{E}^{(1)}},

hence
u^{\prime}_{t}-u^{\prime}_{xx}-2u^{\prime}u^{\prime}_{x}=0\mod{\mathcal{E}^{(1)}}
.

Example 8
.

Another example of regular
\mathcal{C}
-morphism
between two equations is the Miura transformation from the mKdV equation

\mathcal{E}=\{u_{t}-u_{xxx}+6u^{2}u_{x}=0\}
to the KdV equation

\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=\{u^{\prime}_{t}-u^{\prime}_{xxx}-6u^{\prime}u^{\prime}_{x}=0\}
, where
u=u(x,t)

and
u^{\prime}=u^{\prime}(x,t)
. For ease of comparison, here and in Example
14
,
it will be used the form of KdV adopted in
[
38
]
where the
Miura transformation is defined by
u^{\prime}=u_{x}-u^{2}
. In this case,
by repeatedly applying (
5
), one can readily check that

u^{\prime}_{x}=-2\,uu_{x}+u_{xx},\qquad u^{\prime}_{xx}=-2\,u_{x}^{2}-2\,uu_{xx}+u_{xxx},\qquad u^{\prime}_{xxx}=-6\,u_{x}u_{xx}-2\,uu_{xxx}+u_{xxxx},\quad\ldots\;.

On the other hand

u^{\prime}_{t}=u_{xt}-2\,uu_{t}=u_{xxxx}-2\,uu_{xxx}-6\,u^{2}u_{xx}-12\,u^{3}u_{x}-12\,uu_{x}^{2}\mod{\mathcal{E}^{(1)}},

hence
u^{\prime}_{t}-u^{\prime}_{xxx}-6u^{\prime}u^{\prime}_{x}=0\mod{\mathcal{E}^{(1)}}
.

2.3
Symmetries and pseudosymmetries

In this subsection, after reviewing the notion of symmetry of a differential
equation
[
8
,
22
,
34
]
, we will give an introduction to
pseudosymmetries, one of its possible generalizations proposed by
Sokolov in the paper
[
38
]
, together with some of its key
properties, which will be particularly important in the forthcoming
parts of the paper.

2.3.1
Symmetries of differential equations

Finite symmetries of a smooth distribution
\mathcal{D}

on a manifold
N
are diffeomorphisms
\psi:N\rightarrow N
such
that
\psi_{*}\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
. Analogously, from
the infinitesimal point of view, infinitesimal symmetries of a smooth
distribution
\mathcal{D}
on a manifold
N

are smooth vector fields
Y
on
N
such that
L_{Y}\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
.
For instance, if
\mathcal{D}
is generated by a system of vector
fields, i.e.,
\mathcal{D}=<X_{1},...,X_{n}>
, the symmetry condition
is equivalent to
[Y,X_{i}]=\sum_{s}\alpha_{i}^{s}X_{s}
, for any

i\in\{1,...,n\}
and some smooth functions
\alpha_{i}^{s}
.

Now, given a
k
-th order equation (or system)
\mathcal{E}=\{\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0}\}\subset J^{k}(\pi)
,
the
classical finite symmetries
of
\mathcal{E}
are finite
symmetries of the distribution
\mathcal{C}^{k}(\pi)
which leave
invariant the submanifold
\mathcal{E}
. Analogously,
classical
infinitesimal symmetries
of
\mathcal{E}
are vector fields on
J^{k}(\pi)

which are infinitesimal symmetries of
\mathcal{C}^{k}(\pi)
and
are tangent to
\mathcal{E}
. Hence, by definition, the flow of an
infinitesimal symmetry of
\mathcal{E}
is a
1
-parameter local
group of local diffeomorphisms which are finite symmetries of
\mathcal{E}
.
A finite symmetry
\psi
is called
projectable
if
\psi^{*}(C^{\infty}(M))\subset C^{\infty}(M)
.
Analogously, an infinitesimal symmetry
X
is called projectable
if
X(C^{\infty}(M))\subset C^{\infty}(M)
.

It can be seen (see
[
8
]
for details) that infinitesimal
symmetries
X
of
\mathcal{C}^{k}(\pi)
are completely described
by a
generating function

\varphi=(\varphi^{j})
, defined
as
\varphi^{j}:=X\,\lrcorner\,\omega^{j}
. This function is always
of first order (i.e., only depend on
x_{i},u^{j},u_{i}^{j}
) and,
whenever
m>1
, it is always of the form
\varphi^{a}=a_{i}(x_{i},u^{j})u_{i}^{a}+b^{a}(x_{i},u^{j})
.
In particular, in canonical coordinates
\{x_{i},u_{\sigma}^{j}\}
,
one has

X=-\sum_{i}\frac{\partial\varphi^{a}}{\partial u_{i}^{a}}D_{i}^{(k)}+\sum_{|\sigma|=0}^{k-1}\,\sum_{j=1}^{m}D_{\sigma}^{(k)}(\varphi^{j})\partial_{u_{\sigma}^{j}},

(10)

where
D_{\sigma}^{(k)}:=\left(D_{1}^{(k)}\right)^{\sigma_{1}}\circ...\circ\left(D_{n}^{(k)}\right)^{\sigma_{n}}

and
a
is any fixed integer in
\{1,...,m\}
.

Thus, in view of (
10
), the higher order components
of an infinitesimal symmetry can be obtained from lower order ones
by means of a recurrence formula. In this sense one says that the
infinitesimal symmetries of
\mathcal{C}^{k}(\pi)
are
prolongations

of lower order vector fields. For instance, when
m>1
any infinitesimal
symmetry
X
of
\mathcal{C}^{k}(\pi)
is the prolongation of a
vector field on
J^{0}(\pi)
; in such a case,
X
is usually referred
to as a
point infinitesimal symmetry
. On the other hand,
when
m=1
an infinitesimal symmetry
X
of
\mathcal{C}^{k}(\pi)

is in general the prolongation of a symmetry of
\mathcal{C}^{1}(\pi)
,
that is not necessarily the prolongation of a vector field on
J^{0}(\pi)
.
When
m=1
,
X
is usually referred to as a
contact infinitesimal
symmetry
whenever it is not a point symmetry.

In practice, computing infinitesimal classical symmetries
X
of
an equation
\mathcal{E}\subset J^{k}(\pi)
consists in the search
of generating functions
\varphi=(\varphi^{j})=(X\,\lrcorner\,\omega^{j})

such that
X
is tangent to
\mathcal{E}
. This tangency condition
leads to a linear system of PDEs for the function
\varphi
, which
is usually overdetermined and hence can be algorithmically studied
by taking into consideration the full set of compatibility conditions.
The analysis of this kind of system is in general more feasible if
one makes use of a symbolic manipulation package like those written
for Maple.

On the other hand, when
\mathcal{E}
is formally integrable, a noteworthy
extension of the notion of symmetry is possible. Indeed, one can say
that a vector field
Z\in\mathcal{D}(\pi)
is a symmetry of
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}

if, and only if,
Z
is a symmetry of
\mathcal{C}(\pi)
which is
tangent to
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
. However, since
\mathcal{C}(\pi)

is Frobenius, the Lie algebra
\mathcal{DC}(\pi)
of vector fields
inscribed in
\mathcal{C}(\pi)
is an ideal of trivial (or characteristic)
symmetries, that one wants preliminarily gauge out from symmetries
of
\mathcal{C}(\pi)
. Thus, denoting by
\text{Sym}(\pi)
the full
Lie algebra of symmetries of
\mathcal{C}(\pi)
, one can define the
Lie algebra of
generalized (or higher) symmetries of

\mathcal{C}(\pi)

as the quotient algebra
\text{sym}(\pi)=\text{Sym}(\pi)/\mathcal{DC}(\pi)
.
Hence, since any field of
\mathcal{DC}(\pi)
is already tangent
to
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
, all elements of a coset
\left[X\right]\in\text{sym}(\pi)

(i.e.,
\left[X\right]=X\,\mod{\mathcal{DC}(\pi)},
with
X\in\text{Sym}(\pi)
)
are tangent to
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
, or neither are they. Thus,
since it make sense to say that an element of
\text{sym}(\pi)
is
tangent or not to
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
, one defines the Lie algebra
of
generalized (or higher) symmetries of

\mathcal{E}
as
the sub-algebra
\text{sym}(\mathcal{E})\subset\text{sym}(\pi)
of
generalized symmetries of
\mathcal{C}(\pi)
that are tangent to

\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
.

In coordinates, one can see that any
X\in\text{Sym}(\pi)
has the
form

X=\sum_{i}f_{i}D_{i}+\sum_{|\sigma|\geq 0}\sum_{j=1}^{m}D_{\sigma}(\varphi^{j})\partial_{u_{\sigma}^{j}},

(11)

where
\varphi=(\varphi^{j})=(X\,\lrcorner\,\omega^{j})
and
f_{i}

are some differentiable functions. Thus, any higher symmetry
\left[X\right]

can be identified with its vertical (or evolutionary) representative

\re_{\varphi}=\sum_{|\sigma|\geq 0}\sum_{j=1}^{m}D_{\sigma}(\varphi^{j})\partial_{u_{\sigma}^{j}},

(12)

with generating function
\varphi=(\varphi^{a})
. Moreover,
\re_{\varphi}\in\text{sym}(\mathcal{E})

whenever
\re_{\varphi}
is tangent to
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
,
i.e.,
\varphi
satisfies the system of linear differential equations

\sum_{|\sigma|\geq 0}\sum_{j=1}^{m}D_{\sigma}(\varphi^{j})\partial_{u_{\sigma}^{j}}\mathbf{F}=0\mod{\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}}.

(13)

For any fixed
\varphi
, this is typically an overdetermined system
for
\varphi
that involves only a finite number of differential
consequences of
\mathcal{E}
and can be explicitly solved by considering
its integrability conditions.

In particular, among the generalized symmetries of
\mathcal{E}

one has the
classical generalized symmetries
, which are the
vertical representatives of infinite prolongations of classical symmetries
of
\mathcal{E}
.

For further details on the theory of classical and generalized symmetries,
as well as for examples and explicit computations, see
[
8
,
34
]
.

2.3.2
Pseudosymmetries of differential equations

Pseudosymmetries were introduced by Sokolov in
[
38
]
as
a generalization of infinitesimal symmetries.

In general, given a smooth distribution
\mathcal{D}
, a pseudosymmetry
of
\mathcal{D}
can be understood as a smooth vector field
Y
such
that
L_{Y}\mathcal{D}\subseteq\,<Y>+\mathcal{D}
. For instance,
for a distribution
\mathcal{D}=<X_{1},...,X_{n}>
, the pseudosymmetry
condition is equivalent to say that for any
i\in\{1,...,n\}
one
has
[Y,X_{i}]=\alpha_{i}Y+\sum_{s}\beta_{i}^{s}X_{s}
, for some
smooth functions
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}^{s}
.

Sokolov introduced also the more general notion of an
r
-pseudosymmetry
of
\mathcal{D}
, that can be seen as a system
\{Y_{1},...,Y_{r}\}

of vector fields such that
L_{Y_{h}}\mathcal{D}\subseteq\,<Y_{1},...,Y_{r}>+\mathcal{D}
,
for any
h=1,...,r
. Hence, for a distribution
\mathcal{D}=<X_{1},...,X_{n}>
,
one has an
r
-pseudosymmetry
\{Y_{1},...,Y_{r}\}
if for any
i\in\{1,...,n\}

the
r\times 1
matrix
\mathbb{Y}=\left[Y_{1},...,Y_{r}\right]^{T}

is such that

\left[\mathbb{Y},X_{i}\right]=\mathbb{U}_{i}\mathbb{Y}+\mathbb{V}_{i}\mathbb{X},

(14)

where
\left[\mathbb{Y},X_{i}\right]:=\left[\left[Y_{1},X_{i}\right],...,\left[Y_{r},X_{i}\right]\right]^{T}

and
\mathbb{X}=\left[X_{1},...,X_{n}\right]^{T}
, whereas
\mathbb{U}_{i}

and
\mathbb{V}_{i}
are some
r\times r
and
r\times n
matrix-valued
functions, respectively.

It is well known that the flow of an infinitesimal symmetry always
transforms an integral manifolds of
\mathcal{D}
to another (possibly
different) integral manifolds. Also, in view of
[Y,X_{i}]=\sum_{s}\alpha_{i}^{s}X_{s}
,
it is readily seen that an infinitesimal symmetry of
\mathcal{D}

sends an invariant (or first integral)
I
of
\mathcal{D}
to another
invariant
Y(I)
. These properties are in general not true for pseudosymmetries.
However, pseudosymmetries share with symmetries another important
property described by the following

Proposition 9
.

Let
\left\{\xi^{1},...,\xi^{n},\nu^{1},...,\nu^{m}\right\}
be
invariants of a pseudosymmetry
Y
(or, an
r
-pseudosymmetry
\{Y_{1},...,Y_{r}\}
)
of
\mathcal{D}=<X_{1},...,X_{n}>
, such that

\det\left(\begin{array}[]{lll}X_{1}\left(\xi^{1}\right)&\cdots&X_{1}\left(\xi^{n}\right)\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
X_{n}\left(\xi^{1}\right)&\cdots&X_{n}\left(\xi^{n}\right)\end{array}\right)\neq 0.

Then the functions
\nu_{i}^{j}
, defined by

\left(\begin{array}[]{lll}\nu_{1}^{1}&\cdots&\nu_{1}^{m}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\nu_{n}^{1}&\cdots&\nu_{n}^{m}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{lll}X_{1}\left(\xi^{1}\right)&\cdots&X_{1}\left(\xi^{n}\right)\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
X_{n}\left(\xi^{1}\right)&\cdots&X_{n}\left(\xi^{n}\right)\end{array}\right)^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{lll}X_{1}\nu^{1}&\cdots&X_{1}\nu^{m}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
X_{n}\nu^{1}&\cdots&X_{n}\nu^{m}\end{array}\right),

(15)

are invariants of
Y
(resp.,
\{Y_{1},...,Y_{r}\}
).

Proof.

Consider the matrix identity

\left(\begin{array}[]{lll}X_{1}\left(\xi^{1}\right)&\cdots&X_{1}\left(\xi^{n}\right)\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
X_{n}\left(\xi^{1}\right)&\cdots&X_{n}\left(\xi^{n}\right)\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{lll}\nu_{1}^{1}&\cdots&\nu_{1}^{m}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\nu_{n}^{1}&\cdots&\nu_{n}^{m}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{lll}X_{1}\nu^{1}&\cdots&X_{1}\nu^{m}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
X_{n}\nu^{1}&\cdots&X_{n}\nu^{m}\end{array}\right).

In order to save space and avoid ambiguity, since we have the parentheses
due to the derivatives along the field X, we will rewrite last identity
as
\left[X_{i}(\xi^{h})\right]\left[\nu_{h}^{j}\right]=\left[X_{i}(\nu^{j})\right]
,
instead of
\left(X_{i}(\xi^{h})\right)\left(\nu_{h}^{j}\right)=\left(X_{i}(\nu^{j})\right)
.
The same will be done for the other matrix identities that follow.
Thus, by taking the Lie derivative of this identity with respect to

Y
on gets

\left[Y\left(X_{i}(\xi^{h})\right)\right]\left[\nu_{h}^{j}\right]+\left[X_{i}(\xi^{h})\right]\left[Y(\nu_{h}^{j})\right]=\left[Y\left(X_{i}(\nu^{j})\right)\right].

On the other hand, in view of
[Y,X_{i}]=\sum_{s}\alpha_{i}^{s}X_{s}+\beta_{i}Y

and the fact that
Y(\xi^{h})=Y(\nu^{j})=0
, last identity reduces
to

\left[\sum_{s}\alpha_{i}^{s}X_{s}(\xi^{h})\right]\left[\nu_{h}^{j}\right]+\left[X_{i}(\xi^{h})\right]\left[Y(\nu_{h}^{j})\right]=\left[\sum_{s}\alpha_{i}^{s}X_{s}(\nu^{j})\right],

or equivalently

\left[\alpha_{i}^{s}\right]\left[X_{s}(\xi^{h})\right]\left[\nu_{h}^{j}\right]+\left[X_{i}(\xi^{h})\right]\left[Y(\nu_{h}^{j})\right]=\left[\alpha_{i}^{s}\right]\left[X_{s}(\nu^{j})\right].

Then by
\left[X_{s}(\xi^{h})\right]\left[\nu_{h}^{j}\right]=\left[X_{s}(\nu^{j})\right]

one readily gets
\left[X_{i}(\xi^{h})\right]\left[Y(\nu_{h}^{j})\right]=0
.
The result follows from non-degeneracy condition
\det\left[X_{i}(\xi^{h})\right]\neq 0
.
An analogous proof holds in the case of an
r
-pseudosymmetry
\{Y_{1},...,Y_{r}\}
,
since for any
X_{i}
and
Y_{h}
one has
[Y_{h},X_{i}]=\sum_{s}\alpha_{hi}^{s}X_{s}+\sum_{k}\beta_{hi}^{k}Y_{k}
,
for some functions
a_{hi}^{s}
and
\beta_{hi}^{k}
.
∎

In general one has no guarantee that the “derived” system of invariants

\{\xi^{i},\nu^{j},\nu_{i}^{j}\}
is functionally independent, even
when
\{\xi^{i},\nu^{j}\}
is a functionally independent system.
The scenario, however, changes in the case of the Cartan distribution

\mathcal{C}(\pi)
on the infinite jet space. Indeed, in such a case
the generators
X_{i}
of
\mathcal{C}(\pi)
are the total derivatives,
hence the jet-degree of
\left[\nu_{h}^{j}\right]=\left[X_{i}(\xi^{h})\right]^{-1}\left[X_{i}(\nu^{j})\right]

(which is (
15
)) is higher than that of
\{\xi^{i},\nu^{j}\}
.
Thus, if
\left\{\xi^{i},\nu^{j}\right\}
are functionally independent
invariants of a pseudosymmetry
Y
(or, an
r
-pseudosymmetry
\{Y_{1},...,Y_{r}\}
)
of
\mathcal{\mathcal{C}}(\pi)=<D_{1},...,D_{n}>
satisfying
\det\left(D_{s}\xi^{i}\right)\neq 0
,
the prolongation formulas (
5
) provide an infinite sequence

\{x_{i}^{\prime}=\xi^{i},\,u^{\prime}{}_{\mu}^{j}=\nu_{\mu}^{j}\}
of invariants
of
Y
(resp.,
\{Y_{1},...,Y_{r}\}
) that defines a regular
\mathcal{C}
-morphism
in
J^{\infty}(\pi)
, in view of Proposition
3
.

On the other hand, given a formally integrable equation
\mathcal{E}=\{\mathbf{F}=0\}\subset J^{k}(\pi)
,
if
\left\{\xi^{i},\nu^{j}\right\}
are functionally independent
invariants of a pseudosymmetry
\bar{Y}
(or, an
r
-pseudosymmetry

\{\bar{Y}_{1},...,\bar{Y}_{r}\}
) of
\mathcal{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{E})=<\bar{D}_{1},...,\bar{D}_{n}>

on
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
satisfying
\det\left(\bar{D}_{s}\xi^{i}\right)\neq 0
,
then prolongation formulas (
15
) allow one
to define a sequence
\{\xi^{i},\,\nu_{\mu}^{j}\}
of invariants
of
\bar{Y}
(resp.,
\{\bar{Y}_{1},...,\bar{Y}_{r}\}
) that are
functionally dependent, due to the constraints imposed by
\mathbf{F}=0

and its differential consequences. Indeed, assuming for instance that

\{\xi^{i},\,\nu_{\mu}^{j}\}
are
\ell
-th order invariants of

\bar{Y}
, the cardinality of
\{\xi^{i},\,\nu_{\mu}^{j}\}
grows
with
|\mu|
exactly as the dimension of
J^{|\mu|}(\pi)
, that
in its turn grows faster than the dimension of the prolongation
\mathcal{E}^{(\ell+|\mu|-k)}
.
Therefore, for sufficiently large
|\mu|
the system
\{\xi^{i},\,\nu_{\mu}^{j}\}

is necessarily functionally dependent, in view of the fact that for
a nontrivial
\bar{Y}
there are at most
\dim\mathcal{E}^{(\ell+|\mu|-k)}-1

functionally independent invariants of order
\ell+|\mu|
, as one
can see by considering the projections of
\bar{Y}
on the prolongations
of
\mathcal{E}
. Indeed, looking at vector fields as derivations,
the projection
\left.\bar{Y}\right|_{\mathcal{E}}
of
\bar{Y}

on
\mathcal{E}
is defined as the restriction of
\bar{Y}
to the
algebra of smooth functions on
\mathcal{E}
. Analogously, for
\mathcal{q}>k
,
the projection
\left.\bar{Y}\right|_{\mathcal{E}^{(\mathcal{q}-k)}}

of
\bar{Y}
on
\mathcal{E}^{(\mathcal{q}-k)}
is defined as the
restriction of
\bar{Y}
to the algebra of smooth functions on
\mathcal{E}^{(\mathcal{q}-k)}
.
When the projection of
\bar{Y}
to
\mathcal{E}^{(\mathcal{q}-k)}

(or
\mathcal{E}
) is a derivation, hence a vector field on
\mathcal{E}^{(\mathcal{q}-k)}

(or
\mathcal{E}
, resp.), one has that invariants of the projection
are also invariants of
\bar{Y}
. On the contrary, when the projection
of
\bar{Y}
to
\mathcal{E}^{(\mathcal{q}-k)}
(or
\mathcal{E}
)
is not a derivation, hence it is a relative vector field on
\mathcal{E}^{(\mathcal{q}-k)}

(or
\mathcal{E}
, resp.), one has that invariants of the projection
are not necessarily also invariants of
\bar{Y}
. Thus, in any case,
the number of functionally independent invariants of order
\mathcal{q}

(or
k
, resp.) admitted by
\bar{Y}
is at most
\dim(\mathcal{E}^{(\mathcal{q}-k)})-1

(or
\dim(\mathcal{E})-1
, resp.). Analogous considerations hold
when
\{\xi^{i},\,\nu_{\mu}^{j}\}
are invariants of an
r
-pseudosymmetry.

Therefore, since
\mathbf{F}=0
and its differential consequences
entail that for sufficiently large
|\mu|
the system
\{\xi^{i},\,\nu_{\mu}^{j}\}

is functionally dependent on
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
, it follows
that the invariants
\{\nu{}_{\sigma}^{j}\}
satisfy an associated
system of partial differential equations
\mathbf{Q}=0
(together
with their prolongations), modulo
\mathbf{F}=0
and its differential
consequences. This way one can look at
\{x^{\prime}_{i}=\xi^{i},\,u^{\prime}{}_{\mu}^{j}=\nu_{\mu}^{j}\}

as the components of a
\mathcal{C}
-morphism from
\mathcal{E}=\{\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0}\}

to
\mathcal{Q}=\{\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{0}\}
. Of course, if one chooses
another system of invariants
\{\bar{x}^{\prime}_{i}=\bar{\xi}^{i},\,\bar{u}^{\prime}{}^{j}=\bar{\nu}^{j}\}

equivalent to
\{\xi^{i},\,\nu^{j}\}
, i.e., such that
\{\bar{\xi}^{i}=A^{i}(\xi^{i},\nu^{j}),\,\bar{\nu}^{j}=B^{j}(\xi^{i},\nu^{j})\}

and
\det\left(D_{s}\bar{\xi}^{i}\right)\neq 0
, one would obtain
a system
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
which is point equivalent to
\mathcal{Q}
,
through the point transformation
\{\bar{x}^{\prime}_{i}=A^{i}(x^{\prime},u^{\prime}),\,\bar{u}^{\prime}{}^{j}=B^{j}(x^{\prime},u^{\prime})\}
.
In particular, one can choose
\left\{\xi^{i},\nu^{j}\right\}

in such a way that none of its element is the prolongation of another
one and, in addition to that,
\{\xi^{i},\,\nu_{\mu}^{j}\}
is a
complete system of invariants, i.e., a system that generates invariants
of all possible orders. In such a case, according to the common terminology
used in the particular case of symmetries (see for instance
[
22
,
38
,
39
]
),
one can look at
\mathcal{Q}
as the factorization (or the quotient)
of
\mathcal{E}
by
\bar{Y}
.

Examples
13
and
14
below will
provide a simple illustration of above factorization procedure. Other
examples, describing Bäcklund transformations, will be discussed in
Section
4
.

In view of these considerations, we are particularly interested here
to the determination of pseudosymmetries or
r
-pseudosymmetries
of
\mathcal{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{E})
on
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
.
Using the outer geometry of
J^{\infty}(\pi)
, we will consider pseudosymmetries
and
r
-pseudosymmetries of
\mathcal{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{E})

that are obtained by restricting to
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
suitable
vector fields on
J^{\infty}(\pi)
. Thus, it is convenient to adopt
the following

Definition 10
.

Let
\mathcal{E}=\{\mathbf{F=0}\}\subset J^{k}(\pi)
be a formally
integrable differential equation. A
pseudosymmetry
of
\mathcal{E}

is a vector field
Y
on
J^{\infty}(\pi)
which is tangent to
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}

and restricts to a pseudosymmetry
\bar{Y}:=\left.Y\right|_{\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}}

of
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{E})
. Analogously, an

r
-pseudosymmetry

of
\mathcal{E}
is a system of vector fields
\{Y_{1},...,Y_{r}\}

on
J^{\infty}(\pi)
which are tangent to
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}

and restrict to an
r
-pseudosymmetry
\{\bar{Y}_{1}:=\left.Y_{1}\right|_{\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}},...,\bar{Y}_{r}:=\left.Y_{r}\right|_{\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}}\}

of
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{E})
.

In his paper
[
38
]
, Sokolov discussed a method for calculating
the pseudosymmetries of
\mathcal{E}
. We will describe this method
here using the following proposition, along with a short proof for
the convenience of our readers.

Proposition 11
.

Let
\mathcal{E}=\{\mathbf{F=0}\}\subset J^{k}(\pi)
,
with
\text{$\mathbf{F}$}=(F^{1},...,F^{p})
, be a formally
integrable differential equation and
\mu=U_{1}dx^{1}+...+U_{n}dx^{n}

be a conservation law of
\mathcal{E}
, i.e., an horizontal
1
-form
such that

[D_{i}+U_{i},D_{j}+U_{j}]=0\mod{\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}}.

(16)

Then, the vector field on
J^{\infty}(\pi)

Y=\sum_{i}a_{i}D_{i}+\sum_{|\sigma|\geq 0}\sum_{j}\left(D+U\right)_{\sigma}(\varphi^{j})\partial_{u_{\sigma}^{j}},

(17)

where

\left(D+U\right)_{\sigma}:=\left(D_{1}+U_{1}\right)^{\sigma_{1}}\circ...\circ\left(D_{n}+U_{n}\right)^{\sigma_{n}},

is a pseudosymmetry of
\mathcal{E}
whenever
Y(F^{h})=0\mod{\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}}
,
i.e.,

\sum_{|\sigma|\geq 0}\sum_{j}\partial_{u_{\sigma}^{j}}\left(F^{h}\right)\left(D+U\right)_{\sigma}(\varphi^{j})=0\mod{\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}},

(18)

for any
h=1,...,p
, which is equivalent to the tangency condition
of
Y
to
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
.

Proof.

In view of (
17
) one gets (for ease of notation,
we use here the Einstein’s summation convention)

\begin{array}[]{ll}\left[D_{s},Y\right]&=\left[D_{s},a_{i}D_{i}\right]+\left[D_{s},\left(D+U\right)_{\sigma}(\varphi^{j})\partial_{u_{\sigma}^{j}}\right]=D_{s}(a_{i})D_{i}+\left[D_{s}+U_{s},\left(D+U\right)_{\sigma}(\varphi^{j})\partial_{u_{\sigma}^{j}}\right]-\left[U_{s},\left(D+U\right)_{\sigma}(\varphi^{j})\partial_{u_{\sigma}^{j}}\right].\end{array}

On the other hand, by (
16
) one has

\left(D_{s}+U_{s}\right)\circ\left(D+U\right)_{\sigma}=\left(D+U\right)_{\sigma+1_{s}}\mod{\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}}.

Hence, above identity can be rewritten as

\begin{array}[]{ll}\left[D_{s},Y\right]&=D_{s}(a_{i})D_{i}+\left(D+U\right)_{\sigma+1_{s}}(\varphi^{j})\partial_{u_{\sigma}^{j}}-\left(D+U\right)_{\sigma}(\varphi^{j})\partial_{u_{\sigma-1_{s}}^{j}}-\left(D+U\right)_{\sigma}(\varphi^{j})\partial_{u_{\sigma}^{j}}\circ U_{s}+\left(D+U\right)_{\sigma}(\varphi^{j})\partial_{u_{\sigma}^{j}}\circ U_{s}\\
&\qquad-U_{s}\circ(Y-a_{i}D_{i})\mod{\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}},\end{array}

which reduces to

[D_{s},Y]=\left(D_{s}(a_{i})+a_{i}U_{s}\right)D_{i}-U_{s}Y\mod{\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}},

(19)

in view of arbitrariness of
\sigma
. On the other hand (
19
)
entails that

D_{s}\left(Y(D_{\sigma}(F^{h}))\right)-Y\left(D_{s}(D_{\sigma}(F^{h}))\right)=\left(D_{s}(a_{i})+a_{i}U_{s}\right)D_{i}\left(D_{\sigma}(F^{h})\right)-U_{s}Y(D_{\sigma}(F^{h}))=-U_{s}Y(D_{\sigma}(F^{h}))\mod{\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}},

i.e.,

Y\left(D_{\sigma+1_{s}}(F^{h})\right)=U_{s}Y(D_{\sigma}(F^{h}))+D_{s}\left(Y(D_{\sigma}(F^{h}))\right)\mod{\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}},

for any
\sigma
and for any
h
. Thus
Y(F^{h})=0\mod{\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}}
,

h=1,...,p
, guarantee that
Y\left(D_{\sigma}(F^{h})\right)=0\mod{\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}}
,
i.e., that
Y
is tangent to
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
. Therefore,
in view of (
19
), one gets that
Y
restricts on
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}

to a pseudosymmetry of
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{E})
.
∎

Some remarks are in order here. First, in view of (
17
),
one has recurrence formulas for the components of a pseudosymmetry
that allow one to obtain higher order components from lower order
ones. To distinguish the case of pseudosymmetries from that of symmetries,
we will say that (
17
) is the
pseudo-prolongation
,
relatively to the conservation law
\mu
,
of the (possibly
relative) vector field

on

the pseudo-prolongation
reduces to standard prolongation and the pseudosymmetry is just a

to observe that in practice the determination of pseudosymmetries

a finite number of differential consequences of
\mathcal{E}
.

For

.

Let

For

Example 13
.

The heat equation
\mathcal{E}=\{u_{t}-u_{xx}=0\}
,
where
u=u(x,t)
, admits the classical infinitesimal symmetry (which
is a particular pseudosymmetry of
\mathcal{E}
with
\mu=0
)
Y=u\,\partial_{u}+u_{x}\partial_{u_{x}}+u_{t}\partial_{u_{t}}+\,...

provided by the prolongation of
u\,\partial_{u}
; indeed it is readily
seen that
Y(u_{t}-u_{xx})=u_{t}-u_{xx}=0\mod{\mathcal{E}}
. In this
case, since
Y
is a classical symmetry of the ambient Cartan distribution,
it projects to a vector field on any finite order jet space
J^{k}(\pi)

(where
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2},\;(x,t,u)\mapsto(x,t)
).
On the other hand, being also tangent to
\mathcal{E}
(hence a symmetry
of
\mathcal{E}
), its restriction
\bar{Y}:=\left.Y\right|_{\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}}

projects to a vector field on
\mathcal{E}
as well as any of its
prolongations
\mathcal{E}^{(\ell)}
. From now one we will limit
ourself to domains where
u
and its derivatives do not vanish, hence
above projections of
Y
are always nontrivial. Thus, being
\mathcal{E}

a
7
-dimensional submanifold of
J^{2}(\pi)
,
\bar{Y}
admits

6=\dim\mathcal{E}-1
functionally independent invariants of order

2
,
8=\dim\mathcal{E}^{(1)}-1
functionally independent invariants
of order
3
, and so on. These invariants can be obtained by means
of prolongations from the following basic system of invariants
\{\xi^{1}=x,\,\xi^{2}=t,\,\nu^{1}=u_{x}/u,\,\nu^{2}=u_{t}/u\}

of
\bar{Y}
, that define a
\mathcal{C}
-morphism
\mathcal{B}:J^{\infty}(\pi)\rightarrow J^{\infty}(\pi^{\prime})
,
where
\pi^{\prime}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2},\;(x,t,u^{1}{}^{\prime},u^{2}{}^{\prime})\mapsto(x,t)
.
Indeed, non-degeneracy condition (
4
) is satisfied
and a first prolongation provides

u^{1}{}^{\prime}_{x}=\frac{u_{xx}}{u}-\left(\frac{u_{x}}{u}\right)^{2},\qquad u^{1}{}^{\prime}_{t}=u^{2}{}^{\prime}_{x}=\frac{u_{xt}}{u}-\left(\frac{u_{x}}{u}\right)\left(\frac{u_{t}}{u}\right),\qquad u^{2}{}^{\prime}_{t}=\frac{u_{tt}}{u}-\left(\frac{u_{t}}{u}\right)^{2}.

Hence, a complete system of second order functionally independent
invariants of
\bar{Y}
is given by
\{x,\,t,\,u^{1}{}^{\prime},\,u^{2}{}^{\prime},\,u^{2}{}^{\prime}_{x},\,u^{2}{}^{\prime}_{t}\}
,
since the other two invariants
u^{1}{}^{\prime}_{x},u^{1}{}^{\prime}_{t}
satisfy
the relations

u^{1}{}^{\prime}_{x}=u^{2}{}^{\prime}-\left(u^{1}{}^{\prime}\right)^{2},\qquad u^{1}{}^{\prime}_{t}=u^{2}{}^{\prime}_{x}\qquad\mod{\{u_{xx}=u_{t}\}}.

This means that
\mathcal{B}
is a
\mathcal{C}
-morphism from the
heat equation
\mathcal{E}=\{u_{t}-u_{xx}=0\}
to the system
\mathcal{Q}=\{u^{1}{}^{\prime}_{x}-u^{2}{}^{\prime}+\left(u^{1}{}^{\prime}\right)^{2}=0,\,u^{1}{}^{\prime}_{t}-u^{2}{}^{\prime}_{x}=0\}
,
that one can interpret as the factorization of
\mathcal{E}
by
Y
.
Hence,
\left\{u^{1}{}^{\prime}=u_{x}/u,\,u^{2}{}^{\prime}=u_{t}/u\right\}
transforms
solutions of
\mathcal{E}
to solutions of
\mathcal{Q}
, as well
as of any of its differential consequences. In particular, since the
Burgers equation
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=\{u^{\prime}_{t}-u^{\prime}_{xx}-2u^{\prime}u^{\prime}_{x}=0\}
with

u^{\prime}:=u^{1}{}^{\prime}
is a differential consequence of
\mathcal{Q}
, one
obtains the Cole-Hopf transformation
u^{\prime}=u_{x}/u
as a by-product
of above transformation.

Example 14
.

The mKdV equation
\mathcal{E}=\{u_{t}-u_{xxx}+6u^{2}u_{x}=0\}
,
can be written in conservation law form as

]
,
obtained from the vector field

2.4
Differentiable coverings and Bäcklund transformations as nonlocal

\mathcal{C}
-morphisms

Let
\mathcal{E}\subset J^{k}(\pi)
be a formally integrable equation,
with
\pi:E\rightarrow M
,
\dim M=n
. Throughout the paper it will
be adopted the following

Definition 15
.

A
differentiable covering
of a formally integrable equation

\mathcal{E}
is a fiber bundle
\tau:\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}

with the following properties:

1.

\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
is equipped with a
n
-dimensional involutive
distribution
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
;

2.

for any
\theta\in\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
the push-forward
\tau_{*\,\theta}:\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\theta}\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}_{\tau(\theta)}(\mathcal{E})

is an isomorphism.

According to above definition (see
[
8
]
), when
\tau
has
finite fiber dimension
q
,
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
is locally diffeomorphic
to the product
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}\times W
, where
W\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{q}

is an open set. Thus, locally identifying
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
with

\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}\times W
,
\tau
can be locally realized
as the natural projection
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}\times W\rightarrow\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
.
Then, using standard coordinates
v^{1},...,v^{q}
in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
,
the generators
\tilde{D}_{i}
of the involutive distribution
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}

on
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}\times W
can be locally written as
\tilde{D}_{i}=\bar{D}_{i}+\sum_{s}X_{i}^{s}\partial_{v^{s}}

where
\bar{D}_{i}
are the total derivatives on
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}

and
X_{i}^{s}
are smooth functions such that
\left[\tilde{D}_{i},\tilde{D}_{j}\right]=0
,
i.e.,
\tilde{D}_{i}\left(X_{j}^{s}\right)=\tilde{D}_{j}\left(X_{i}^{s}\right)
,
in view of
[\bar{D}_{i},\bar{D}_{j}]=0
.

In this paper we will consider only the case when
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}

is the infinite prolongation of a formally integrable system
\mathcal{Y}\subset J^{l}(\pi)
.
Thus, in view of above considerations, the fiber coordinates
\{u^{1},...,u^{m}\}

of
\pi
will be separated in two subsets
\{z^{1},...,z^{m-q}\}

and
\{v^{1},...,v^{q}\}
, such that
\mathcal{E}=\left\{\mathbf{F}(x,z_{\sigma})=0\right\}

and
\mathcal{Y}^{(\infty)}
is the infinite prolongation of a system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}F^{h}(x,z_{\sigma})=0,\\
v_{i}^{s}=X_{i}^{s}(x,z_{\sigma},v).\end{array}\right.

(23)

It is noteworthy to observe that, in view of the definition of a differentiable
covering
\tau:\mathcal{Y}^{(\infty)}\longrightarrow\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
,
any solution of
\mathcal{Y}
can be projected by
\tau
to a solution
of
\mathcal{E}
.

A system
\mathcal{Y}
like (
23
) will be called
a
differentiable extension
of
\mathcal{E}=\left\{\mathbf{F}=0\right\}
.
Moreover, given a differentiable extension
\mathcal{Y}
of
\mathcal{E}
,
it is customary to call
v^{1},...,v^{q}
the nonlocal variables
of the given extension (or the corresponding differentiable covering).
Also, non-local symmetries of
\mathcal{E}
are the symmetries of
a differentiable extension
\mathcal{Y}
of
\mathcal{E}
.

Example 16
.

It is well known
[
31
]

(see also
[
26
,
13
,
30
,
41
]
and references therein)
that KdV equation
\mathcal{E}=\{z_{t}=-6zz_{x}-z_{xxx}\}
, where

z=z(x,t)
, is the integrability condition of the system (Lax pair)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\phi_{xx}=-z\phi-\lambda\phi,\\
\phi_{t}=2(2\lambda-z)\phi_{x}+z_{x}\phi,\end{array}\right.

(24)

where
\phi=\phi(x,t)
and
\lambda
is a real parameter usually
referred to as the spectral parameter. Geometrically this means that
(
24
) defines a differentiable covering of KdV. Indeed,
by setting
v^{1}:=\phi
and
v^{2}:=\phi_{x}
, one can rewrite
(
24
) as a first order system

\left(\begin{array}[]{l}v_{x}^{1}\\
v_{x}^{2}\end{array}\right)=X\left(\begin{array}[]{l}v^{1}\\
v^{2}\end{array}\right),\qquad\left(\begin{array}[]{l}v_{t}^{1}\\
v_{t}^{2}\end{array}\right)=T\left(\begin{array}[]{l}v^{1}\\
v^{2}\end{array}\right),

with

X=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}0&1\\
-z-\lambda&0\end{array}\right),\qquad T=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}z_{x}&4\lambda-2z\\
z_{xx}+2z^{2}-2\lambda z-4\lambda^{2}&-z_{x}\end{array}\right)

usually referred to as a matrix Lax pair for the KdV.

Thus, by introducing the fiber bundle
\mathbb{R}^{4}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

(x,t,z,v^{1},v^{2})\mapsto(x,t)
, it is easy to check that the infinite
prolongation of the system
\mathcal{V}

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}v_{x}^{1}=v^{2},\\
v_{x}^{2}=-(z+\lambda)v^{1},\\
v_{t}^{1}=z_{x}v^{1}+\left(4\lambda-2z\right)v^{2},\\
v_{t}^{2}=\left(z_{xx}+2z^{2}-2\lambda z-4\lambda^{2}\right)v^{1}-z_{x}v^{2},\\
z_{t}=-6zz_{x}-z_{xxx}\end{array}\right.

(25)

defines a
2
-dimensional differentiable covering
\mathcal{V}^{(\infty)}\longrightarrow\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}

of KdV.

It is interesting to notice here that this covering is an extension
of a
1
-dimensional covering, by means of the potential of a conservation
law. Indeed, by introducing
\rho:=v^{2}/v^{1}
one can readily see
that (
25
) reduces to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\left(\ln v^{1}\right)_{x}=\rho,\\
\left(\ln v^{1}\right)_{t}=z_{x}+\left(4\lambda-2z\right)\rho,\\
\rho_{x}=-\rho^{2}-z-\lambda,\\
\rho_{t}=-2(2\lambda-z)\rho^{2}-2z_{x}\rho+z_{xx}+2z^{2}-2\lambda z-4\lambda^{2},\\
z_{t}=-6zz_{x}-z_{xxx}.\end{array}\right.

It follows that above covering extends the
1
-dimensional differentiable
covering
\tau:\mathcal{Y}^{(\infty)}\longrightarrow\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
,
defined by the system
\mathcal{Y}

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\rho_{x}=-\rho^{2}-z-\lambda,\\
\rho_{t}=-2(2\lambda-z)\rho^{2}-2z_{x}\rho+z_{xx}+2z^{2}-2\lambda z-4\lambda^{2},\\
z_{t}=-6zz_{x}-z_{xxx}.\end{array}\right.

(26)

In particular,
\mu:=\rho\,dx+\left(z_{x}+\left(4\lambda-2z\right)\rho\right)dt

is a nonlocal conservation law (depending on the nonlocal function

\rho
defined in
\tau
). Thus, the
2
-dimensional covering can
be seen as an extension of
\tau
by means of the equations for the
potential
\ln v^{1}
of
\Lambda
. We also notice that, in terms
of the Lax “potential”
\phi
, the nonlocal variable of
\tau

reads
\rho=\phi_{x}/\phi
.

For further details on the theory of differential coverings, as well
as for examples and explicit computations, see
[
8
]
and references
therein.

Definition 17
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\subset J^{k}(\pi)
and
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\subset J^{k^{\prime}}(\pi^{\prime})

be two formally integrable equations, with
\pi:E\rightarrow M
and

\pi^{\prime}:E^{\prime}\rightarrow M^{\prime}
such that
\dim M=\dim M^{\prime}
. A Bäcklund transformation
(BT) from
\mathcal{E}
to
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
is a regular
\mathcal{C}
-morphism

\mathcal{B}:J^{\infty}(\pi)\rightarrow J^{\infty}(\pi^{\prime})
from
\mathcal{Y}

to
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
, for some differentiable covering
\tau:\mathcal{Y}^{(\infty)}\longrightarrow\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
,
with
\mathcal{Y}\subset J^{l}(\pi)
. In particular
\mathcal{B}

is an auto-Bäcklund transformation (ABT) whenever
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}

is a copy of
\mathcal{E}
.

According to this definition, a BT can be seen as a pair of
\mathcal{C}
-morphisms

\begin{array}[]{ccc}&\mathcal{Y}^{(\infty)}\\
\qquad\tau\swarrow&&\searrow\mathcal{B}\\
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}&&\qquad\mathcal{E}^{\prime}{}^{(\infty)}\end{array}

which is analogous to the point of view followed by Krasil’shchik
and Vinogradov in
[
23
]
. However, in this article, we will
not limit ourselves to considering Bäcklund transformations that keep
the independent variables fixed.

,

defined by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\rho^{\prime}_{x}=-\rho^{\prime}{}^{2}-z^{\prime}-\lambda,\\
\rho^{\prime}_{t}=-2(2\lambda-z^{\prime})\rho^{\prime}{}^{2}-2z^{\prime}_{x}\rho^{\prime}+z^{\prime}_{xx}+2z^{\prime}{}^{2}-2\lambda z^{\prime}-4\lambda^{2},\\
z^{\prime}_{t}=-6z^{\prime}z^{\prime}_{x}-z^{\prime}_{xxx}.\end{array}\right.

In this case, to obtain a new solution
z^{\prime}
of KdV out of a given
one
z=f_{0}
, the first step is to compute the corresponding solution

\rho=g_{0}
of the Riccati system, with arbitrary
\lambda
. Then,
since
g_{0}
depends on
\lambda
, one gets another instance
\hat{g}_{0}

of that function, by substituting
\lambda
with
\hat{\lambda}\neq\lambda
.
This way (
29
) provides the new pair of functions

z^{\prime}=-f_{0}-2\hat{g}_{0}^{2}-2\hat{\lambda},\qquad\rho^{\prime}=-\frac{\hat{\lambda}-\lambda}{\hat{g}_{0}-g}-\hat{g}_{0}

that satisfy

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\rho_{x}=-\rho^{2}-z-\lambda,\\
\rho_{t}=-2(2\lambda-z)\rho^{2}-2z_{x}\rho+z_{xx}+2z^{2}-2\lambda z-4\lambda^{2},\\
z_{t}=-6zz_{x}-z_{xxx}.\end{array}\right.

The peculiarity of this transformation, in comparison with that of
previous example, is that it provides the new solution
z^{\prime}
together
with the corresponding solution
\rho^{\prime}
of the Riccati system, with
arbitrary
\lambda
. Thus, further applications of the transformations
only require derivations and algebraic operations.

It is noteworthy to remark here that, passing to the Lax potential

\phi
, the transformation (
29
) takes the form (with

\phi
and
\hat{\phi}
solutions of Lax equations with parameters

\lambda
and
\hat{\lambda}
, respectively)

z^{\prime}=z+2\left(\ln\hat{\phi}\right)_{xx},\qquad\phi^{\prime}=\phi_{x}-\phi\left(\ln\hat{\phi}\right)_{x},

which is known in literature as the Darboux transformation of KdV
(see for instance
[
19
,
30
]
). Thus, the notion of BT
described by Definition
17
allows one to regard also
Darboux transformations as examples of BT.

Example 21
.

Consider the covering
\tau:\mathcal{Y}^{(\infty)}\longrightarrow\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}

of the short-pulse equation
\mathcal{E}=\{z_{xt}=\frac{1}{2}z^{2}z_{xx}+zz_{x}^{2}+z\}\subset J^{3}(\pi)
,
defined by the system
\mathcal{Y}

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\rho_{x}=-{\displaystyle\frac{z_{x}}{4\eta}}\rho^{2}+{\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\eta}}\,\rho+{\displaystyle\frac{z_{x}}{4\eta}},\\
\rho_{t}=-{\displaystyle\frac{z\left(zz_{x}+4\,\eta\right)}{8\eta}}\rho^{2}+{\displaystyle\frac{\left(8\,\eta^{2}+z^{2}\right)}{4\eta}}\rho-{\displaystyle\frac{z\left(-zz_{x}+4\,\eta\right)}{8\eta}},\\

(30)

where
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{4}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
(x,t,z,\rho)\mapsto(x,t)
.
One can check that the transformation

x^{\prime}=x+8\frac{\eta}{\rho^{2}+1},\qquad t^{\prime}=t,\qquad z^{\prime}=-z+8\eta\frac{\rho}{\rho^{2}+1}.

(31)

determines an auto-Bäcklund transformation, i.e., a BT from

3
Riccati-type differentiable coverings determined by ZCRs

Let
\alpha\in\mathfrak{g}\otimes\bar{\Lambda}^{1}\left({\cal E}\right)

be a ZCR of the form
\alpha=X\,dx+T\,dt
, for a formally integrable
equation
\mathcal{E}:=\left\{\mathbf{F=0}\right\}
with two independent
variables
\left(x,t\right)
. We assume here that
X
and
T
are

\ell\times\ell
matrices, with
\ell\geq 2
.

One can readily check that (
1
) is equivalent to the integrability
condition

\bar{D}_{t}X-\bar{D}_{x}T+\left[X,T\right]=0

(32)

of a linear system of the form

V_{x}=XV,\qquad V_{t}=TV,

(33)

where
V=(v^{1},...,v^{\ell})^{T}
is an auxiliary vector valued
function of
(x,t)
.

This means that, by suitably enlarging the space of dependent variables,

\alpha
defines a differentiable covering
\tau:\mathcal{Y}^{(\infty)}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
,
with
\mathcal{Y}:=\left\{\mathbf{F=0},\,\bar{D}_{x}V=XV,\,\bar{D}_{t}V=TV\right\}
.
In such an enlarged space, (
32
) can be also understood
as the integrability condition of an equation of the form

\bar{d}_{H}V=XV\,dx+TV\,dt,

(34)

where
\bar{d}_{H}V=\bar{D}_{x}V\,dx+\bar{D}_{t}V\,dt
. Notice that

V_{x}=\tilde{D}_{x}V
and
V_{t}=\tilde{D}_{t}V
, in terms of the
total derivatives
\tilde{D}_{x}
and
\tilde{D}_{t}
in
\mathcal{Y}^{(\infty)}
.

Now, it is noteworthy to observe that linear transformations
V\mapsto V^{S}:=SV
,
by a nonsingular matrix valued function
S
on
\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}
,
naturally determine a
\mathcal{C}
-morphism of
\mathcal{Y}
. Under
such transformations, (
33
) transforms as

V_{x}^{S}=X^{S}V^{S},\qquad V_{t}^{S}=T^{S}V^{S}

with

X^{S}:=\bar{D}_{x}\left(S\right)S^{-1}+SXS^{-1},\qquad T^{S}:=\bar{D}_{t}\left(S\right)S^{-1}+STS^{-1}.

Accordingly, (
34
) transforms as
\bar{d}_{H}V^{S}=X^{S}V^{S}\,dx+T^{S}V^{S}\,dt
.
These transformations are usually referred to as
gauge transformations
.

We have the following

Lemma 22
.

For any fixed
h\in\{1,...,\ell\}
, under the gauge transformation

V\mapsto V^{\prime}=\frac{1}{v^{h}}V
equation (
34
) rewrites as

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\bar{d}_{H}\left(\ln v^{h}\right)=\left[\alpha\right]^{h}V^{\prime},\\
\bar{d}_{H}V^{\prime}=\alpha V^{\prime}-\left(\left[\alpha\right]^{h}V^{\prime}\right)V^{\prime}\end{array}\right.

where
\left[\alpha\right]^{h}
denotes the
h
-th row of
\alpha
.
Moreover, the first order system (
33
) is equivalent
to the following one

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{\displaystyle\frac{v_{x}^{h}}{v^{h}}}=\left[X\right]^{h}V^{\prime},\\
{\displaystyle\frac{v_{t}^{h}}{v^{h}}}=\left[T\right]^{h}V^{\prime},\\
V^{\prime}_{x}=XV^{\prime}-\left(\left[X\right]^{h}V^{\prime}\right)V^{\prime},\\
V^{\prime}_{t}=TV^{\prime}-\left(\left[T\right]^{h}V^{\prime}\right)V^{\prime},\end{array}\right.

where
\left[X\right]^{h}
and
\left[T\right]^{h}
denote the
h
-th
rows of
X
and
T
, respectively.

Proof.

The result follows by substituting
V=v^{h}V^{\prime}
(with
h
fixed)
in (
34
).
∎

Since
V^{\prime}=\frac{1}{v^{h}}V
, it is natural to write
V^{\prime}
as

V^{\prime}=(\rho^{1},...,\rho^{h-1},1,\rho^{h},...,\rho^{\ell-1})^{T},

where

\rho^{j}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}v^{j}/v^{h},&j<h,\\
v^{j+1}/v^{h},&j>h.\end{array}\right.

It follows that the differentiable covering
\tau:\mathcal{Y}^{(\infty)}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}^{(\infty)}

is the extension, by means of the potential of a nonlocal conservation
law, of a differentiable covering defined by a system of
\ell-1

Riccati equations.

For instance, by taking
h=\ell
for ease of notations, one has the
following

Proposition 23
.

To any ZCR
\alpha=X\,dx+T\,dt
of
\mathcal{E}:=\left\{\mathbf{F=0}\right\}
,
with
\ell\times\ell
matrices
X
and
T
, it is associated the

(\ell-1)
-dimensional differentiable covering defined by the system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\rho_{x}^{j}=\sum_{s=1}^{\ell-1}\left(-X_{\ell s}\rho^{s}\rho^{j}+X_{js}\rho^{s}\right)-X_{\ell\ell}\rho^{j}+X_{j\ell},\qquad j=1,...,\ell-1\\
\rho_{t}^{j}=\sum_{s=1}^{\ell-1}\left(-T_{\ell s}\rho^{s}\rho^{j}+T_{js}\rho^{s}\right)-T_{\ell\ell}\rho^{j}+T_{j\ell},\qquad j=1,...,\ell-1\\
\mathbf{F=0}.\end{array}\right.

In particular, with respect to this covering,
\mathcal{E}
admits
the following nonlocal conservation law

\mu=\left(\sum_{s=1}^{\ell-1}X_{\ell s}\rho^{s}+X_{\ell\ell}\right)dx+\left(\sum_{s=1}^{\ell-1}T_{\ell s}\rho^{s}+T_{\ell\ell}\right)dt.

Analogous results hold for any other
h=1,2,...,\ell-1
, with the
corresponding nonlocal variables
\bar{\rho}_{1},\bar{\rho}_{2},...,\bar{\rho}_{\ell-1}

reciprocally related, for any choice of
h
, to the
\rho_{1},\rho_{2},...,\rho_{\ell-1}

above. However, it is noteworthy to remark that, despite in any case
the obtained Riccati systems are equivalent under the transformations

(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},...,\rho_{\ell-1})\mapsto\left(\bar{\rho}_{1},\bar{\rho}_{2},...,\bar{\rho}_{\ell-1}\right)
,
the corresponding conservation laws are in general not equivalent.

4
Nonlocal pseudo-symmetries and Bäcklund transformations

As discussed in Subsection
2.4
, BT are
\mathcal{C}
-morphisms
involving differentiable coverings of equations. Our aim here is to
illustrate the relationship of BT with nonlocal pseudosymmetries.
To this end, let
\mathcal{Y}
be a system of the form (
23
)
that defines a differentiable covering of
\mathcal{E}
. As discussed
in Subsection
2.3.2
, assuming that
\mathcal{Y}

admits a pseudosymmetry
Y
(or, an
r
-pseudosymmetry
\{Y_{1},...,Y_{r}\}
),
for any system of functionally independent invariants
S=\{x^{\prime}_{i}=\xi^{i},\,z^{\prime}{}^{j}=\nu^{j}\}
,
satisfying (
4
), one can consider the corresponding
system
\mathcal{Q}
, satisfied by the prolonged invariants
\nu_{\sigma}^{j}

modulo
\mathcal{E}
and its consequences. Then, since the form of

\mathcal{Q}
depends on the choice of the system
S
, one might
ask whether it is possible to choose
S
such that
\mathcal{Q}

is just a copy of
\mathcal{E}
(or, another equation
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}

of interest) or includes it among its differential consequences. With
such a choice one would obtain a
\mathcal{C}
-morphism from
\mathcal{Y}

to
\mathcal{E}
(or
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
, respectively), that describes
an ABT (or BT, resp.).

On the other hand, to calculate pseudosymmetries (or,
r
-pseudosymmetries)
of
\mathcal{Y}
, one needs to know its conservation
laws
\mu
(or, horizontal
1
-forms
\gamma
satisfying the zero-curvature
condition) first. Therefore, for the practical application of the
procedure described above, it is necessary first to determine not
only differential coverings but also some
\mu
or
\gamma
, should
one be interested to
r
-pseudosymmetries. Based on the results of
the previous section, it is therefore clear that in the case of an
equation with two independent variables (
n=2
) a particularly advantageous
situation is that of equations admitting zero-curvature representations:
indeed, starting from these representations, the Riccati coverings
together with associated nonlocal conservation laws
\mu
remain
automatically determined and one can directly pass to search for corresponding
pseudosymmetries. Instead, we do not yet have a result analogous to
that of Proposition
23
that can help us construct
the horizontal
1
-forms
\gamma
needed to compute
r
-pseudosymmetries.
Hence, calculating the possible
r
-pseudosymmetries admitted by
the Riccati coverings, associated with ZCRs, remains rather complicated,
as is already the case with the
r
-pseudosymmetries of an equation

\mathcal{E}
, without considering any differentiable
covering at all, so much so that for these
r
-pseudosymmetries,
it is not easy to find significant examples in the literature. On
the other hand, in the context of this work, we think that
r
-pseudosymmetries
should play an important role when the Riccati covering has dimension
greater than
1
, because in this case the covering equations themselves
impose non-trivial compatibility conditions for the invariants of
a possible pseudosymmetry, thus indicating the existence in this case
of strong restrictions on the possibility of having pseudosymmetries.
Therefore, starting from a
\ell\times\ell
matrix-valued ZCR, with

\ell>2
, looking for
(\ell-1)
-pseudosymmetries is more natural.
This is what we did, for instance, in Example
34
,
where we used a
\mathfrak{sl}_{3}(\mathbb{R})
-valued ZCR of the
Tzitzeica equation to show that the ABT found in
[
9
]
is also
obtainable by following the approach described above, by using a
2
-pseudosymmetry
of the corresponding Riccati covering. Thus we believe that the question
of the computation of these
r
-pseudosymmetries deserves a further
detailed study. The same is true for the more general question on
the role that pseudosymmetries and
r
-pseudosymmetries can have
in the integrability property of an equation. We also believe that
pseudosymmetries can play a fundamental role in studying Bäcklund
transformations in higher dimensions. These and other questions will
be addressed in future works.
