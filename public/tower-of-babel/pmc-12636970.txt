Long-term effects of psychotherapy in children and adolescents: protocol for database development and a systematic review in the LaKiJu META project
Authors: Sarah K Schäfer, Clara F Burmeister, Eva Lottermoser, Christian G Schäfer, Lorena Cascant Ortolano, Jutta Stoffers-Winterling, Tabea Flasinksi, Monika Equit, Silvia Schneider, Klaus Lieb, Anke de Haan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionApproximately one in every six children and adolescents is affected by mental disorders, which impose significant costs on patients, their families and societies. Psychotherapy is the first-line treatment for many of these disorders, and systematic reviews of post-intervention effects show small to moderate favourable outcomes compared with control groups. However, the long-term effects of psychotherapy remain less well understood.Methods and analysisThe LaKiJu META project aims to address this gap by developing an open-access database, which will subsequently be used for data synthesis. This database will be established through literature searches in nine databases for (cluster) randomised controlled trials (RCTs) investigating the long-term effects (≥6 months) of any type of psychotherapy in school-aged children and adolescents (ages 6;00 to 17;11 years) with mental disorders. Outcomes will be prioritised based on their relevance to patients, caregivers and clinicians and will encompass a broad range of measures, including symptom changes, response rates and reliable changes. Syntheses will use multilevel meta-analyses to compare intervention and control groups at follow-up assessments, across both transdiagnostic and disorder-specific symptom outcomes. In secondary analyses, we will examine changes within intervention groups over time. Moderator analyses will focus on the effects of study-, intervention- and patient-level characteristics.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval for public involvement was obtained from the ethics committee of the Faculty of Psychology of the Ruhr University Bochum. For dissemination, we will employ tailored strategies to reach researchers, clinicians, patients and their caregivers, with all groups involved in the development of dissemination plans.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251003208 (preregistered on 10 March 2025).

Introduction

Mental disorders are prevalent among children and adolescents, with 8.8–15.5% of young individuals diagnosed with at least one disorder.13 These conditions are associated with significant costs4 and have a substantial negative impact on the quality of life of both patients and their families.57 Children and adolescents with mental disorders face an increased risk of adverse educational and economic outcomes in later life.8 9 Without treatment, mental disorders have a high risk of persisting and evolving into chronic conditions in adulthood.1013 Clinical guidelines recommend psychotherapy as the first-line treatment for many mental disorders in children and adolescents.1418

Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) is the most extensively studied type of psychotherapy.19 However, several other psychotherapeutic approaches—including psychodynamic and psychoanalytic therapies, play therapy, person-centred therapy, systemic and family therapy, and ‘third-wave’ CBT interventions such as schema therapy and acceptance and commitment therapy—have also been investigated in clinical trials.20 Many of these psychotherapies have demonstrated favourable efficacy and effectiveness compared with (predominantly passive) control groups at post-intervention assessments.

For the most common mental disorders in children and adolescents, systematic reviews and meta-analyses have summarised the effects of psychotherapy, generally reporting small to moderate favourable effects compared with (often passive) control groups. These findings include anxiety or fear-related disorders,21 22 depressive disorders,2325 attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)26 27 and disruptive disorders (ie, oppositional defiant disorder and conduct-dissocial disorder).2830

Given the long-term consequences of mental disorders in children and adolescents10 11 and the extended period of life following treatment, understanding the long-term effects of psychotherapy is particularly crucial in this age group. However, these effects have been less frequently investigated in clinical trials, and comprehensive systematic reviews and meta-analyses on long-term outcomes remain lacking for many mental disorders and types of psychotherapy. Previous systematic reviews on long-term effects have often encompassed highly heterogeneous psychosocial interventions,31 32 been limited to qualitative syntheses,3335 focused on specific psychotherapy types (eg, CBT, family therapy),203436 examined only single disorders,3739 or targeted specific mental symptoms (eg, depressive and anxiety symptoms),32 40 and outcome types (eg, reductions in symptom distress).31 41 Consequently, many of these reviews were based on a limited number of studies, limiting their ability to conduct in-depth quantitative analyses of long-term effects, temporal dynamics and potential moderators.

For instance, a recent meta-analysis examining long-term effects of interventions for depression and anxiety disorders found a small reduction in depressive symptoms but no significant effect on anxiety symptoms after 2 years.32 These findings align with another review on psychotherapy delivered in routine settings for internalising symptoms.41 However, these reviews included heterogeneous interventions ranging from prevention to treatment32 or were restricted to specific settings (eg, outpatient treatment41). Additionally, both focused primarily on symptom reduction, which may not fully capture the most important outcomes for patients.42

None of the existing systematic reviews have adopted a broader approach that simultaneously examines multiple patient- and clinician-relevant outcome measures, such as individual goal attainment, functioning, positive mental health, psychosocial resources and family-related outcomes. Yet, these factors are essential for sustaining positive treatment effects over time.43 44 While the MetaPsy platform (https://www.metapsy.org) provides a unique open-access database on psychotherapy outcomes, it primarily covers short-term effects in adults with common mental disorders. Currently, there is no comprehensive database on the long-term effects of psychotherapy in children and adolescents. Such a resource would be highly valuable for clinicians, clinical guideline teams and researchers interested in conducting (disorder-specific) reviews on the long-term effects of psychotherapy.

Objectives

The LaKiJu META project (English project title: Multidimensional long-term effects of psychotherapy in children and adolescents—a systematic review and meta-analysis) aims to address this research gap by developing an open-access database and conducting a systematic review on a highly patient-relevant yet underexplored topic—that is, the long-term effects of psychotherapy in children and adolescents.

The project aims to answer the following research questions, selected for their relevance to patients, caregivers and clinical practice:

What are the long-term effects of psychotherapy in children and adolescents (≥6 months post-intervention) on transdiagnostic symptom categories, specifically internalising and externalising symptoms?

To achieve these objectives, the project integrates clinical and methodological expertise with the lived experiences of patients and caregivers. Ultimately, we aim to enhance the development and updating of clinical guidelines, thereby improving clinical care and supporting informed decision-making for patients and their caregivers.

Methods and analysis

Preregistration and reporting

The methodological approach for the planned systematic review follows the standards of the Cochrane Collaboration.45 This protocol adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P,46 see online supplemental material SM1) and PRISMA guidelines for reporting literature searches (PRISMA-S,47 see online supplemental material SM2). The final review report will comply with the PRISMA statement48 and PRISMA guidelines for reporting harms in systematic reviews.49 The review project was prospectively preregistered via PROSPERO (ID: CRD420251003208) on 10 March 2025. In line with principles of full transparency, any significant changes or updates to the process will be publicly documented via the Open Science Framework (OSF) project (https://osf.io/rcgma/).

Eligibility criteria

Population

Eligible studies include school-aged patients (6;00–17;11 years50) with any mental disorder as defined by established international classification systems, such as the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) or the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM).51 Mental disorders are defined according to Chapter 6 of the ICD-11,52 excluding neurodevelopmental disorders (except for ADHD and primary tics/tic disorders) and mental or behavioural disorders associated with pregnancy, childbirth or the puerperium. Studies with heterogeneous diagnoses or multiple comorbidities are eligible. Populations qualify if diagnoses are assessed at the individual level (eg, by validated means of assessment such as structured clinical interviews) or if participants are selected based on elevated symptom distress (eg, meeting a clinical cut-off on a validated self-report questionnaire for the mental disorder that is targeted by the treatment). To maximise generalisability, samples are eligible regardless of other than age-related sociodemographic characteristics (eg, sex/gender, nationality, ethnicity) and somatic comorbidities. For mixed samples including participants younger than 6 years or older than 17 years, 11 months, corresponding authors will be contacted for subgroup data. If subgroup data are unavailable, studies will be included if the mean/median sample age falls within the target age range.

Intervention

This review includes all types of psychotherapy aimed at treating mental disorders or reducing their burden in children and adolescents through psychological methods that primarily rely on verbal and non-verbal communication.53 Psychotherapy will be considered regardless of its theoretical background, encompassing cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), psychodynamic/psychoanalytic therapy, play therapy, person-centred therapy, systemic/family therapy and eclectic therapies designed for children and adolescents. Most of these treatments are delivered individually or in groups, but they vary in methods and techniques, duration and intensity. The review will include all treatment settings (inpatient and outpatient) and delivery modes (in-person and digital). Concomitant pharmacotherapy is allowed if it is administered similarly across intervention and control groups. Trials exclusively investigating non-psychotherapy psychosocial interventions (eg, health promotion programmes, prevention measures, relaxation techniques, patient education programmes) or stand-alone self-help programmes are not eligible. Non-psychotherapy psychosocial interventions are defined as follows: Health promotion interventions aim to enhance and optimise mental health.54 55 Prevention interventions seek to reduce the risk of mental disorders at the individual or population level or manage their consequences.56 Relaxation techniques include any method designed to induce relaxation and reduce arousal or stress (eg, progressive relaxation, autogenic training, guided imagery).57 Patient education programmes involve interventions designed to modify patients’ cognitions, beliefs, emotions and behaviours through information delivery (eg, psychoeducation groups, psychoeducational materials).58 Relaxation techniques and psychoeducation are eligible only when integrated into psychotherapy but not as stand-alone interventions.

Control groups

We include both active and passive control groups which are drawn from the same population and randomly allocated to the groups. Passive control groups consist of no intervention, treatment/care as usual (TAU/CAU) or waitlist. Active control groups include enhanced treatment/care as usual (ETAU/ECAU) as well as comparators in which patients engage in specific activities during the intervention period (eg, supportive counselling, psychoeducation, self-help programmes).

We acknowledge that the inclusion of passive control groups—particularly waitlist controls—may lead to inflated effect size estimates.235961 However, such control groups are highly prevalent in research on treatment outcomes for children and adolescents; for example, they accounted for 61% of control groups in a systematic review of CBT for depression in this population.62 Excluding these studies would therefore risk presenting an incomplete picture of the available evidence, especially for less frequently examined mental disorders. Consequently, waitlist controls will be eligible for inclusion; nevertheless, results will be interpreted with caution, explicitly acknowledging their limitations.

Outcomes

The outcomes of interest will include disorder-specific symptom measures (eg, anxiety, depressive symptoms, conduct problems), assessed through self-report, proxy/other-report or clinician ratings. For the purposes of our analyses, these disorder-specific symptoms will be categorised within broader transdiagnostic frameworks of internalising and externalising symptoms.63 Additionally, categorical outcomes (ie, response and remission rates) will be derived from disorder-specific measures, following clinical guidelines and recommendations, as previously conducted in systematic reviews focused on specific disorders.64 To comprehensively assess the potential negative effects and risks associated with psychotherapy, we will examine adverse treatment effects (eg, symptom deterioration, adverse events, side effects). Furthermore, we will consider clinician-rated diagnoses, dropout rates as an indicator of treatment acceptance, and various measures of functioning (eg, social and academic functioning). We will also evaluate positive mental health, well-being, psychosocial resources and resilience factors (eg, self-efficacy), as well as family-related outcomes (eg, family functioning). For inclusion, outcomes must be assessed using validated measures (eg, standardised self-reports) at long-term follow-up, which should occur at least 6 months after the completion of the intervention. The full list of primary and secondary outcomes for this systematic review will be developed collaboratively with patients, caregivers and clinicians through a structured patient involvement process (see Patient and public involvement for further details). On completion of this process, a finalised list of primary and secondary outcomes, along with their importance ratings derived from stakeholder groups, will be uploaded to the OSF project (https://osf.io/rcgma/).

Study design

We will include (cluster) randomised controlled trials (RCTs) as the gold standard for efficacy and effectiveness research,65 provided that each trial includes a minimum of 10 participants per arm and at least one long-term follow-up assessment (≥ 6 months post-intervention). Head-to-head comparisons refer to studies comparing two types of treatment supposed to be effective by primary study authors66 (eg, CBT vs systemic/family therapy, CBT vs psychopharmacotherapy). Those studies will be included in analyses of within-group changes over time but will be excluded from analyses on overall intervention effects if no eligible active or passive control group is available. Dismantling studies assess those features of psychotherapies that are either the active mechanisms of change or identify the degree to which specific components add to the magnitude of change67 are not eligible for inclusion. Non-randomised controlled trials, single-arm trials and qualitative studies will be excluded.

Information sources and search strategy

The search strategy for this systematic review was developed in close collaboration with an information specialist (LCO) experienced in designing search strategies for clinical guidelines, adhering to the standards outlined by the Cochrane Collaboration.45 The strategy focuses on primary studies. Following prospective preregistration, a systematic search was conducted on 10 March 2025, with no specified start date, across the following nine electronic databases: Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL) and PsycINFO via EBSCO, MEDLINE via PubMed, Web of Science Core Collection via Clarivate, Embase and Scopus via Elsevier, Epistemonikos, and PTSDPubs. Search updates will be performed regularly, with a final update occurring 2 months before the completion of the review. The full search strategies for all databases, including details of the search filters used, are provided in online supplemental material SM3.

The search strategy consists of four clusters of search terms related to (1) age group, (2) population characteristics, (3) intervention type and (4) study design. Terms within each cluster are linked using the Boolean operator OR, while clusters are linked with the operator AND.

Additionally, we will search for trial registrations and preprints on platforms such as clinicaltrials.gov, the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP), the EU Clinical Trials Register (EUCTR), as well as medRxiv, PsyArXiv and OSF Preprints. The MetaPsy website (https://www.metapsy.org) will be searched for RCTs with eligible follow-up lengths. We will also check the reference lists of systematic reviews identified in the related overview of systematic reviews project (https://osf.io/scjmq/) and references in clinical guidelines.1518 Unpublished data will be sought through grey literature databases (eg, OpenGrey, ProQuest Dissertations and Theses), and posts on scientific social networks (eg, ResearchGate, LinkedIn). To maximise sensitivity, experts in the field and authors of large-scale trials will be contacted to request unpublished data on long-term follow-ups.

Study selection

Records were collected using Zotero68 and deduplicated using the Deduplicator.69 Study selection will be conducted in Rayyan.70 Two members of the review team (including at least one psychologist with a minimum of a master’s degree and at most one student team member) will independently screen titles and abstracts of the identified records to assess their eligibility. Irrelevant studies will be excluded immediately at the title and abstract stage. Eligibility will also be assessed independently and in duplicate at the full-text level by two team members (same formal qualification as above). Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion or by consulting a third, supervisory team member (AdH, CGS, SKS). Automated strategies will not be employed to ensure maximal precision. Inter-rater reliability for both title/abstract and full-text screening will be calculated and reported using Cohen’s kappa. The screening process will be documented using a PRISMA flow diagram.48

Data extraction

Data extraction will be performed by one member of the review team (psychologist with a minimum of a master’s degree) and subsequently verified by a second member (trained student team member) using a customised electronic data collection form, developed based on the former Effective Practice and Organisation of Care Review Group (EPOC) data collection form71 and similar projects conducted by review team members.7274 Any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion or by consulting a third, supervisory review team member (AdH, CGS, SKS). Information will be coded for the following domains: general information (eg, citation, country, open science criteria), sample characteristics (eg, mental health status, age, gender distribution), methodological study characteristics (eg, inclusion and exclusion criteria, study design details), intervention description (eg, type of psychotherapy, dose), comparator description (eg, type of comparator, duration), treatment outcomes (eg, outcome types, outcome assessments, data for calculation of effect estimates, adverse effects), and information required for quality appraisal. In cases of uncertainty, primary study authors will be contacted for clarification. A preliminary list of the information to be extracted is provided in online supplemental material SM4 and the full list will be uploaded to the OSF project (https://osf.io/rcgma/) prior to the commencement of the coding process.

Quality appraisal

Risk of bias

The risk of bias will be independently assessed by two members of the review team (including at least one psychologist with a minimum of a master’s degree and at most one student team member) using the Revised Tool for Assessing Bias in Randomised Trials (RoB2) of the Cochrane Collaboration.75 For RCTs, the following bias domains will be evaluated: (1) randomisation process, (2) deviations from intended interventions, (3) missing outcome data, (4) outcome assessment and (5) selective reporting of results. For cluster randomised controlled trials (cRCTs), RoB2 also includes an assessment of bias arising from the identification or recruitment of participants. Judgements can be ‘low’ or ‘high’ or express ‘some concerns’. For the LaKiJu META project, we will use an adapted version of RoB275 developed as part of the MetaPsy project (V1.0–0, February 2025; https://metapsy.org/rob/#v1.0–0), which is specifically tailored to trials examining psychological interventions. Moreover, standard quality assessment tools will be adapted to account for sources of bias relevant to the current review question (eg, bias related to the availability of post-intervention booster sessions). For our analyses of changes over time within the treatment group, we will adapt our quality assessment to focus only on the aspects most relevant to this research question—namely, deviations from the intended intervention, outcome assessment, missing outcome data and selective reporting of results.

We acknowledge that the inclusion of a large proportion of trials rated as having a high risk of bias can substantially distort review results, potentially leading to overestimated effect sizes and, in turn, invalid conclusions.64 76 77 Whenever possible, we will restrict our analyses to subsets of studies with an overall low risk of bias or an overall rating of some concerns. However, for some mental disorders, such analyses may not yet be feasible because of the limited number of primary studies examining long-term effects and the scarcity of trials rated with some concerns or low risk. In these instances, the results of syntheses that include studies with an elevated risk of bias will be interpreted with caution, discussed in light of their limitations, and explicitly labelled as preliminary due to the inclusion of high-risk-of-bias studies.

Publication bias

The potential impact of publication bias will be assessed through visual inspections of contour-enhanced funnel plots,78 regression-based tests for multilevel meta-analyses79 and worst-case meta-analyses.80

Database development

One of the primary objectives of the LaKiJu META project is the development and launch of a sustainable open-access database that will provide information on the long-term effects of psychotherapy in children and adolescents. This database will be a valuable resource for researchers, clinical guideline teams and clinicians. It will serve as the foundation for the data synthesis planned within this project while also enabling researchers worldwide to use the data collected to explore their own research questions. The database will allow researchers to select subsets of data relevant to specific research questions based on trial characteristics. For example, it will be possible to focus on trials at low risk of bias that examine the effects of CBT on conduct disorder. In this way, we aim to facilitate and promote high-quality evidence syntheses on the long-term effects of psychotherapy across a broad range of mental disorders in children and adolescents.

To ensure the database meets the needs of its users, we will survey potential user groups (ie, researchers, clinical guideline teams and clinicians) during its development. Pilot versions of the database will be tested with these user groups through small feasibility studies, and the feedback will be used to refine the final version. The database will be made publicly accessible via an easy-to-use platform, such as the Open Science Framework or through the infrastructure of the German Center for Mental Health (DZPG). It will adhere to open science standards and be reported in accordance with the FAIR principles (ie, data will be findable, accessible, interoperable and reusable).81 On availability of sufficient financial resources, we plan to perform regular updates of the database beyond the anticipated completion date of the LaKiJu META project (December 2026).

Data synthesis

Studies will be summarised both narratively and in tabular form (eg, sample characteristics, intervention types). Primary studies will be grouped based on various characteristics (eg, type of psychotherapy, mental disorders, outcome types, intervention dose, length of follow-up period, age groups) for subsequent qualitative and quantitative analyses. If quantitative synthesis is not feasible for specific mental disorders, psychotherapies or outcome types, we will employ a combination of statistical methods (ie, vote counting based on the direction of effects) and visual synthesis (ie, effect direction plots), in accordance with the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis guidelines of the Cochrane Collaboration.82 Quantitative synthesis will be conducted if more than five studies are available per intended analysis.

All steps of the quantitative synthesis will follow recommendations of the Cochrane Collaboration.45 Statistical analyses will be performed in R V.4.4.3 (or later)83 using the R packages metafor,84 clubSandwich85 and PublicationBias,86 with additional packages being added based on modelling requirements.

Calculation of effect estimates

Effect estimates will be calculated to compare intervention and control groups at follow-up time points occurring 6 months or more after the end of the intervention. If data are available for multiple time points, effect estimates will be calculated separately for each time point. Where possible, intent-to-treat (ITT) data will be used to adopt a more conservative approach. For all analyses, multiple outcome types will be employed to assess long-term effects from various perspectives, including standardised mean differences/changes (for continuous outcomes), and relative risks (for categorical outcomes such as response rates, remission rates, dropout rates, reliable changes and symptom deteriorations).64 This approach is employed not only to assess statistically significant differences in symptom distress but also to explore a broad range of patient- and clinician-relevant treatment outcomes, allowing for comparisons across different outcome types. If data for specific outcome measures are not available from primary studies, we will contact corresponding authors for further information, sending up to two reminder emails after 4 and 8 weeks. In our database, we will document as many outcomes as possible to maximise flexibility for future users. Analyses conducted using the database will employ appropriate methods to address the challenges of multiplicity, such as the interdependence of effect estimates derived from a single study.87

Planned analyses

First, we will examine the long-term effects of psychotherapy across multiple outcome types (eg, standardised mean differences, response rates, reliable changes), within the broader transdiagnostic categories of internalising and externalising symptoms, aiming for a maximal comprehensive analysis (see73 74 for similar approaches using larger symptom categories). This analysis will address the question of whether different types of psychotherapy have an overall effect on mental symptoms in children and adolescents. For this purpose, we will perform pairwise multilevel meta-analyses comparing intervention and control groups at follow-up assessments (and post-intervention assessments for comparisons of between-group differences at post-intervention and later assessments). All analyses will employ random-effects models, nesting effect estimates within primary studies and outcome types to account for study and outcome dependencies, along with cluster-robust estimates to adjust for dependent effect estimates.88 Using this approach, we will account for similarities of effect estimates derived from a single study (eg, depressive and anxiety symptoms assessed within one study) and reporting on the same outcome types (eg, depressive symptoms assessed in different studies). Subsequently, we will conduct in-depth analyses on specific mental disorders (eg, anxiety or fear-related disorders, depressive disorders, ADHD) and symptom types (eg, anxiety symptoms, depressive symptoms, conduct problems) using meta-regression models, with disorders and outcome types being included as moderators. Given the well-documented risk that waitlist controls may inflate treatment effect estimates,23 59 60 overall treatment-versus-control estimates will be reported only if moderator analyses reveal no evidence of such inflation. Should evidence emerge, results will be presented separately by type of control condition.

Second, within the multilevel framework of our analyses, we will examine within-group longitudinal symptom changes from pre-intervention to post-intervention and, more importantly, from post-intervention to follow-up assessments, as well as between earlier and later follow-up time points. While our main analyses will concentrate on between-group differences at follow-up assessments, these analyses will focus on the effect of time at the treatment-group level and will evaluate the length of follow-up (ie, weeks since the post-intervention assessment) as a moderator in the meta-regression models. We acknowledge that such analyses could also be conducted using cohort studies on the long-term outcomes of psychotherapy. However, these studies are not eligible for inclusion in the current review because preliminary literature searches indicated lower study quality and reporting standards for such studies. Moreover, our resources for study selection, data extraction and data synthesis are limited, which further necessitated restricting the scope of the review.

Assessment of heterogeneity

To assess between-study and between-outcome heterogeneity, we will visually inspect forest plots, calculate I² statistics and conduct Cochran’s Q tests.89 To account for uncertainty in the meta-analytical models, we will calculate 95% prediction intervals when more than five studies are available.45 90 91

Moderator and subgroup analyses

In the presence of substantial heterogeneity, we will perform moderator analyses through subgroup analyses and meta-regression to explore whether specific study, intervention or patient characteristics are associated with more favourable long-term effects. We plan to examine the effects of the type of psychotherapy (eg, theoretical background, disorder-specific vs transdiagnostic focus), intervention dose, setting, caregiver involvement, type of control group, initial symptom distress, patient age (children vs adolescents), and gender balance within the sample. Moderator analyses will be conducted for the broader categories of internalising and externalising problems as well as for disorder- and symptom-specific analyses separately. Furthermore, based on a sufficiently large database, we will explore interactions between follow-up length and these moderators. Additional moderators may be introduced during the review process based on information from primary studies as well as patient and public involvement.

Stability of treatment effects

On an exploratory basis, we will examine the stability of effect estimates (ie, size and rank) from post-intervention to follow-up assessments, as well as between earlier and later follow-up time points, using intra-class correlations.73 74

Sensitivity analyses

We will perform sensitivity analyses to assess the impact of using ITT vs complete data, unpublished data, self- vs proxy-/other- vs clinical reports, low- vs high-intensity active control groups, and study quality (low risk of bias vs some concerns vs high risk of bias). Moreover, some trials will include highly heterogeneous samples (eg, patients with diverse disorders) or treatment approaches (eg, various types of psychotherapy), and for some trials, such information may not be available from the publication or through personal contact with the primary study authors. These trials will be included in the overall analyses (eg, on mental health symptoms), but their impact on the results will be assessed through sensitivity analyses by reanalysing the data with these studies excluded.

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of the evidence will be evaluated by two independent raters (at least one psychologist with a minimum of a master’s degree) using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) system.92 GRADE specifies four levels of the certainty for a given outcome: high, moderate, low and very low.

Patient and public involvement

The LaKiJu META project aims to involve patients and the public at all stages of the review process. Our project proposal was developed through discussions with clinical experts and members of clinical guideline committees.

The review process will continuously involve three key stakeholder groups: children and adolescents who have received or are currently receiving psychotherapy for mental disorders, caregivers of these patients and clinicians involved in the treatment of mental disorders in children and adolescents. To engage these groups, we will organise focus groups at the outpatient units of Technische Universität Braunschweig (JuKiPsy) and the Mental Health Research and Treatment Center (FBZ) of the Ruhr University Bochum. Each focus group will consist of 5 participants, with each group meeting for five sessions. The sessions will be held in person for children, adolescents and caregivers, and online via Zoom for clinicians. In total, we will conduct 10 focus groups with children and adolescents (separated by age groups and gender), four focus groups with caregivers, and four focus groups with clinicians. Polls via Mentimeter (https://www.mentimeter.com) will be used to collect data in focus groups with adolescents, caregivers and clinicians. For children, we will use non-digital methods for data collection (eg, paper and pencil rankings).

Session 1 will serve as an introductory meeting to allow participants to get to know each other and to present the LaKiJu META project (see table 1 for details). Session 2 will focus on identifying important outcomes, irrespective of those outcomes typically reported in clinical trials. In Session 3, focus groups will prioritise the outcomes identified by the focus group in Session 2 as well as the outcomes that are regularly reported in clinical trials. Session 4, which will take place after the completion of the data synthesis, will involve presenting and discussing the review results. Finally, Session 5 will be dedicated to developing and discussing strategies for disseminating the review results to each target group.

Details on the focus group process across stakeholder groups

Note. Focus groups will be held in the outpatient units of Technische Universität Braunschweig and Ruhr University Bochum, with both cities representing socioeconomically heterogeneous regions in Germany.

The results from these focus groups will directly inform the development of the review project. Specifically, the final selection of primary and secondary outcomes will be based on the priorities identified by patients, caregivers and clinicians. A report detailing the focus group process and its outcomes will be published as an example of patient involvement at younger ages in a peer-reviewed international journal.

Additionally, we will reach out to teams currently working on clinical guidelines for mental disorders in children and adolescents through an online survey to assess their information needs and explore the potential use of our database in the development and updating of clinical guidelines. The responses will guide the development of our database. Furthermore, we will invite interested researchers and clinical guideline team members to evaluate pilot versions of the newly developed database.

Ethics and dissemination

As a systematic review of published clinical trials that does not rely on individual patient data, this review does not require ethical approval. However, we have obtained ethical approval for the involvement of patients, their caregivers and clinicians from the ethics committee of the Faculty of Psychology of the Ruhr University Bochum (ID: 981).

The dissemination of the project will target three main groups: researchers, clinicians involved in the treatment of mental disorders in children and adolescents, and the public, including children and adolescents affected by mental disorders and their caregivers. Researchers will be informed about the start of the project and the planned review process through the PROSPERO preregistration (PROSPERO-ID: CRD420251003208), the related OSF project (https://osf.io/rcgma/), and the publication of this review protocol in an international peer-reviewed journal. Review materials (eg, screening guidance, data extraction sheets) will be continuously uploaded via OSF. Data extracted from primary studies will be made available to the scientific community following quality checks by the review team, and the R code used for analyses will be uploaded to ensure full reproducibility of our results. Data documentation will adhere to FAIR principles81 at all stages. For the publication of our results, we will use open science badges from OSF to indicate that the project was preregistered and has open materials and data. Additionally, we have registered a project site on LinkedIn (https://www.linkedin.com/company/lakiju-meta) to engage with the scientific community and provide regular updates on the project’s progress.

Clinicians involved in the treatment of mental disorders in children and adolescents will be engaged through presentations at well-established conferences focused on clinical care (eg, International Congress of the European Society for Children and Adolescent Psychiatry, Deutscher Psychotherapie Kongress), and by making the review results and the open-access database available to clinical guideline teams (eg, National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, Arbeitsgemeinschaft der Wissenschaftlichen Medizinischen Fachgesellschaften e.V.). We also aim to publish brief versions of our findings in journals that are frequently read by clinicians. Further strategies for dissemination to clinicians will be developed collaboratively with clinicians in Session 5 of the focus groups.

Children and adolescents with mental disorders, along with their caregivers, will be reached through patient organisations, primarily those focused on specific disorders. We will provide these organisations with plain language summaries of our results for particular mental disorders. Additionally, we will use our institutional websites, as well as institutional and personal social media channels, to advertise the project results. Additional dissemination strategies for this group will be discussed in Session 5 of the focus groups with children, adolescents and caregivers.
