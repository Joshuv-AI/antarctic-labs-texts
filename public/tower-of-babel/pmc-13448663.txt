Trametinib for multiple non-ossifying fibromas due to KRAS mosaic mutations: two case reports
Authors: Marie Vincent, Soizic Tiriau, Marine Fouillet-Desjonqueres, Alicia Besson, Wassim Ouchetto, Sébastien Barbarot, Bertrand Isidor, Aymeric Rouchaud, Geneviève Baujat, Pierre Vabres, Patrick Edery, Marion Delous, Massimiliano Rossi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKRAS mosaic activating variants are the main cause of non-ossifying fibromas (NOFs), the most common benign lesion of the growing skeleton. Multifocal NOFs cause bone fragility, and no specific treatment is currently available.MethodsWe report two children, carrying mosaic KRAS variants (p.G13D and p.A146T), presenting with oculoectodermal syndrome and recurrent fractures due to progressive polyostotic NOFs. To assess the impact of these mutations on KRAS function, we conducted transient KRAS overexpression in HEK293 cells and then tested the effect of the MEK-inhibitor trametinib at the cellular level.ResultsWe show that trametinib yields, in vitro, significant reduction of RAS-pathway hyperactivation induced by the two KRAS variants and, in vivo, remarkable clinical and radiological improvement with no recurrence of fractures and reossification of NOFs under treatment; resurgence of lesions is observed one year after stopping treatment.ConclusionsHence, trametinib constitutes a promising precision therapeutic approach for severe KRAS-related NOFs.Subject terms: Medical genetics, Diseases, Drug discovery

Introduction

Non-ossifying fibromas (NOFs) are the most common benign lesions of the growing skeleton, typically presenting during the first two decades of life1. The incidence of NOF is not known, but it has been suggested that 30–40% of children develop NOFs during growth1. Histologically, these lesions are characterized by the presence of fibroblast proliferation and osteoclast-like multinucleated giant cells. NOFs are usually isolated, but they can occur multifocally. Clinically, they can be asymptomatic or cause compression on adjacent tissues, pain, and fractures with motor impairment2,3. Activating mutations in genes of the RAS/MAPK signaling pathway have been found in 81.4% of isolated NOFs, KRAS being the main involved gene (64.4%)3. These mutations are detected in a mosaic state in NOFs, indicating that they are confined to specific tissues as a result of post-zygotic events. Treatment of isolated NOFs is usually conservative, but surgery can be required in case of bone fragility2. Nevertheless, multifocal NOFs causing recurrent fractures can lead to severe motor impairment and no specific treatment is available to date.

Trametinib is an inhibitor of the MEK protein, developed in oncology for the treatment of tumors harboring the BRAF V600 mutation4,5. Here, we report two children affected by oculoectodermal syndrome (OES) carrying mosaic KRAS variants (NM_004985 KRAS c.38G>A, p.G13D and c.436G>A, p.A146T) and presenting with multiple NOFs causing recurrent fractures and severe motor impairment, despite multiple orthopedic interventions. We show that trametinib yields, in vitro, a dose-dependent reduction of the RAS pathway hyperactivation induced by the two KRAS variants and, in vivo, a striking clinical and radiological improvement including reossification of NOFs.

Patients and methods

Patients

Patient 1 was a 9-year-old boy diagnosed shortly after birth with OES. He carried the NM_004985 KRAS c.38G>A (p.G13D) pathogenic variant in 25% of alleles of DNA extracted from fresh skin biopsy (absent in lymphocytes and cultured fibroblasts). He presented with multiple cerebral arteriovenous malformations requiring embolizations; severe ocular malformations including microphthalmia, sclerocornea, upper eyelid coloboma, and choristomas; scalp aplasia cutis congenita, bitemporal lipomas and hyperpigmented skin lesions following Blaschko lines (Fig. 1A). He underwent surgery for aortic coarctation at nine months of age, complicated by recurrent pleural and pericardial effusions resolved after three months’ treatment with sirolimus. At that time, the mutation had not yet been identified and there were no bone lesions, sirolimus was prescribed as a last-line treatment for the chylothorax. He had mild psychomotor delay and attention deficit disorder requiring special schooling. He developed polyostotic progressive NOFs of long bones at the age of three years, leading to multiple fractures, chronic bone pain and lower limb length discrepancy (Fig. 1B). Orthopedic interventions did not prevent new fractures and he needed a wheelchair by the age of five years. He also developed a central giant cell granuloma (CGCG) of the jaw at five years old, surgically treated.

Patient 2 presented at birth with bilateral eye choristomas requiring surgery, left eyelid coloboma, localized scalp aplasia cutis congenital, hyperpigmented spots and ventricular septal defect (Fig. 1C). He progressively developed facial and thoracic sebaceous hamartoma following Blaschko lines and scalp nevus psiloliparus. Brain angio-MRI and CT-scan showed mild dilatation of temporal subarachnoid spaces, small bilateral lipomas in front of cerebellar peduncles, no vascular malformations, and bilateral parietal skull defects. OES was confirmed by exome sequencing on fresh skin biopsy showing a KRAS somatic mosaic heterozygous pathogenic variant (NM_004985 KRAS c.436G>A, p.A146T) in 24.9% of alleles (absent in lymphocytes and cultured fibroblasts). Since the age of three years, he developed multiple NOFs of right femur and tibia (Fig. 1D) and right clavicle causing multiple fractures with right femoral bending, lower limbs length discrepancy and significant gait limitation, despite multiple orthopedic interventions.

Written informed consent to participate in the study, including the publication of clinical details and photographs, was obtained from the parents of each participant.

This study was approved by the Human Protection Committee of Nantes University Hospital (CPP Ouest 4) and by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Hospices Civils de Lyon (IRB 00013204, Opinion No. 26-091, CNIL Register No. 26-5091) and received agreement from health authorities (ANSM, French National Agency for Drug Safety).

Plasmids

To transiently express human KRAS, we used the plasmid JDW 836 (pCS2-V5-mScarlet-I-hsKRAS4A-WT) kindly given by Joshua Wythe6 (Addgene plasmid # 156410; http://n2t.net/addgene:156411; RRID:Addgene_156411). p.G13D or p.A146T variants were inserted using Q5 Site Directed Mutagenesis Kit (New England BioLabs, #E0552S) and the following primers: hsKRAS-G13D_F, 5’-GGAGCTGGTGaCGTAGGCAAG-3’, hsKRAS-G13D_R, 5’-AACTACCACAAGTTTATATTCAGTCATTTAC-3’, and hsKRAS-A146T_F, 5’-TGAAACATCAaCAAAGACAAG-3’, hsKRAS-A146T_R, 5’-TAAAAGGAATTCCATAACTTC-3’.

Cell culture and assay conditions

HEK293 cells (ATCC, CRL-1573) were cultivated in DMEM GlutaMAX (Gibco) plus 10% fetal calf serum (FCS) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin. Cells were plated in 6-well plates at a seeding density of 1.2 ×106 cells/well. Twenty-four hours later, after reaching 60–80% confluence, cells were transfected with 4 µg of pCS2-V5-mScarlet-I-hsKRAS4A plasmid, carrying the wild type (WT) or mutated forms of KRAS cDNA (p.G12V used as a constitutively active form, p.G13D or p.A146T) under the CMV promoter, using LTX Lipofectamine with PLUS reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific, #15338100). After transfection, cells were maintained in DMEM + 10% FCS for around 30 h, during which transfection rate was assessed using mScarlet fluorescence. Then, cells were washed with PBS and starved in FCS-free DMEM for 18 h prior to be treated with trametinib (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc-364639) for one hour. Cells were then washed with ice-cold PBS and cytoplasmic proteins were extracted using 1% Triton lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl; 1% Triton X100; 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, #11697498001) and anti-phosphatases (Roche, #4906845001).

Western blot

Protein quantification was performed using BCA protein assay kit (Pierce), and 25 µg of proteins were denatured at 95 °C for 5 min in 2X Laemmli Sample buffer (Sigma-Aldrich), and loaded into 10% Mini-PROTEAN TGX Precast Gels (Biorad), then blotted on PVDF membrane (Millipore). Blocking (1 h at room temperature (RT)) and antibody incubation (overnight at 4 °C) were performed using TTBS + 5% non-fat milk. Primary antibodies used were: pERK (phospho p44/42 ERK1/2, Cell signaling Technology #4370, 1/2000e), total ERK (p44/42 ERK1/2, Cell Signaling Technology #9102, 1/1000e), and dsRed (Living Colors DsRed Polyclonal Rabbit Antibody, Ozyme #632496, 1/100e). Goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody HRP (Invitrogen, 1/10000e) was incubated for 1 h at RT, and enhanced chemiluminescence was performed using SuperSignal West Pico PLUS or West Atto Ultimate substrates (Thermo Scientific) depending of the signal intensity with the iBright imaging system (Invitrogen).

Results

Trametinib treatment in two OES patients with severe NOFs

According to previous reports showing the efficacy of trametinib in patients with RAS pathway mutations (see discussion), and following multidisciplinary discussions and agreement from health authorities (ANSM, French National Agency for Drug Safety) for compassionate access, trametinib was initiated at a dose of 0.02 mg/kg/day in oral suspension. Patient 1 started treatment at the age of six years and continued for three years with a gradual decrease over the last two years, while patient 2 began at the age of 8.5 years (1.5 years follow-up).

A complete workup was performed on both patients before treatment. This included clinical examination; blood tests (liver and renal function tests; creatine kinase; calcium, phosphate, parathyroid hormone, 25-hydroxy-vitamine D; FGF23, P1NP and CTX as bone turnover markers; alkaline phosphatase and osteocalcin as bone formation markers); ophthalmologic evaluation; cardiac and renal ultrasound; standard skeletal X-rays; dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and bone scintigraphy.

Patients were reviewed at baseline, month 1, month 6 and, then, every 6 months. Radiographies of long bones and blood tests were performed every six months; ophthalmological evaluation and other imaging exams were performed every 12 months.

A significant clinical improvement was noted in both patients since the first months of treatment including the absence of new fracture, disappearance of pain, and motor improvement. Indeed, patient 1 used a wheelchair at M0 and at M6 he started walking again without limitation; and 6-minute walking test performed in patient 2 showed 44% of normal predicted exercise capacity at M0, 63% at M12 and 83% at M18. Radiologically, reossification of all NOFs was noted after six months of treatment in both patients, which was confirmed in all subsequent evaluations (Fig. 2A, C).

Calcium, phosphate, parathyroid hormone, 25-hydroxy-vitamin D blood levels were normal. Markers of bone formation and reabsorption (total and bone alkaline phosphatase, osteocalcin, CTX, P1NP, FGF23) did not show clear trends.

Mild cutaneous side effects were observed, including heterogeneous hair discoloration, acne-like lesions, delayed wound healing in patient 1 and xerosis in both patients, improved by topical treatments. Biologically, a moderate elevation of CPK without clinical impact was noted in both patients (3.5 X upper normal limit in patient 1; 1.5 X in patient 2). For patient 1, trametinib doses were reduced gradually after one year and completely stopped two years later. He developed 15 months after stopping treatment a recurrence of CGCG, and radiographs showed a resurgence of NOFs (Fig. 2B). Given the absence of pain and new fracture, we have not resumed treatment for the moment and we have planned close multidisciplinary follow-up. Patient 2 is still under treatment.

p.G13D and p.A146T are gain-of-function variants, and trametinib inhibits KRAS-induced activation of MAPK pathway

To evaluate the impact of KRAS p.G13D and p.A146T variants on the protein function, we performed overexpression experiments in HEK293 cells by using dsRed-tagged KRAS construct, and assessed the phosphorylation of the downstream effector of KRAS, the kinase Erk (Fig. 3A) by western blot. We compared the impact of the variants to that of the wild type (WT) and the constitutively active (CA) forms of KRAS, and showed that the variants strongly stimulate Erk phosphorylation as the CA form does, compared to the WT form (Fig. 3B, C). Hence, we concluded that both variants identified in patients 1 and 2 have a gain-of-function effect. Next, we treated transfected cells with trametinib and observed that it had little or no effect at the lowest dose (1 nM), but led to a strong reduction in Erk activation at higher doses (10 and 100 nM) (Fig. 3B, C), thus indicating that trametinib treatment is efficient for neutralizing the gain-of-function effect of patients’ KRAS variants.

Discussion

Pathogenic variants in genes of the RAS/MAPK pathway have been reported in 81.4% isolated NOFs, KRAS being the main involved gene (64.4%)3,7. Interestingly, the KRAS variants found in patients 1 and 2 (p.G13D and p.A146T) have also been reported in isolated NOFs3, thus suggesting a continuum spectrum encompassing isolated and syndromic polyostotic NOFs.

The clinical phenotypes caused by mosaic mutations in the RAS/MAPK pathway is highly variable depending on the tissues involved by the mosaicism, including OES (OMIM #600268), encephalocraniocutaneous lipomatosis (ECCL; OMIM #613001); Schimmelpenning-Feuerstein-Mims syndrome (SFMS; OMIM #163200); and various vascular malformations (OMIM #108010)8. The prevalence of NOFs in mosaic RASopathies is currently not known.

Among diseases due to germline mutations in genes involved in the RAS/MAPK pathway, multiple NOFs can be observed in the NF1 phenotypic spectrum and Osteoglophonic dysplasia (OMIM #166250). Giant cell lesions of the jaw including CGCG, presenting histological similarities with NOFs, have been reported in Noonan syndrome and other related RASopathies9,10.

OES is the severe end of the spectrum of conditions dues to mosaic KRAS mutations. To our knowledge, 25 OES patients have been reported to date11–16. OES clinical phenotype is characterized by multiple congenital abnormalities including ocular features, dermatological lesions, cardiovascular and possible neurological involvement11–14. Skeletal lesions are frequent and typically appear during the first five years of life, including NOFs of long bones causing recurrent fractures, and CGCG of the jaw13,14,17. Even if NOFs in one OES adult patient spontaneously resolved following skeletal maturity14, bone lesions usually progress causing recurrent long bone fractures in spite of orthopedic treatments, as observed in patients 1 and 214. This evolution contrasts with isolated NOFs, rarely documented at the molecular level, which tend to regress spontaneously over time7. This might be explained by the different degree of mosaic severity.

Trametinib, an inhibitor of the MEK protein of the RAS pathway, is used in oncology for tumors harboring the BRAF V600 mutation4,5, and recently for recurrent low grade serous ovarian cancer with RAS-RAF-MAPK mutations18. No long-term adverse effects have been reported to date after discontinuation of treatment.

In developmental disorders dues to germline or mosaic mutations in genes of the RAS/MAPK pathway, trametinib has led to significant improvement of lymphatic and cardiovascular abnormalities in 10 patients with Noonan syndrome19–25, and stabilization of a cerebral tumor in a patient with ECCL26. One patient affected by cutaneous skeletal hypophosphatemia syndrome, caused by somatic mosaic NRAS variant, was treated by trametinib and showed normalization of phosphatemia and phosphaturia, improvement of chylothorax and skin lesions27,28. We do not have information on the sustainability of the treatment effectiveness in these observations; the risk of relapse must be monitored.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study evaluating trametinib effects on NOFs.

Our in vitro data confirmed that the c.38G>A (p.G13D) and the c.436G>A (p.A146T) activate the RAS/MAPK signaling pathway through phosphorylation of extracellular signal-regulated kinases 1/2 (pErk1/2), thus fitting with previous report29.

In vivo, trametinib treatment caused an impressive clinical and radiological improvement in the two patients including absence of new fractures, significant motor improvement and striking reossification of lithic bone lesions. This improvement lasted over time, as long as the patient remained under treatment. We did not observe any significant changes in other features, such as the skin, eyes, heart or brain. Overall tolerance was good during follow-up, the observed side effects were minimal including mainly mild reversible skin lesions. After one-year discontinuation of treatment in patient 1, we observed recrudescence of the lytic lesions, thus confirming the unfavorable progression without treatment. We recommend to monitor closely patients after treatment discontinuation in order to detect possible recurrence. No long-term adverse effect was observed.

In conclusion, trametinib reduced the KRAS hyperactivation caused by p.G13D and p.A146T pathogenic variants in vitro, and in vivo, significantly improved bone fragility caused by severe polyostotic NOFs in the two children, with overall a good tolerance. Further studies are required to confirm these promising results in a larger patient cohort with syndromic and non-syndromic severe NOFs, and to assess long-term efficacy and tolerance of this precision therapeutic approach.
