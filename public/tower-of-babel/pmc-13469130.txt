Learning continuous activation fields from microscopic SEM images of lycoperdioid fungi via CNN-guided neural operator modeling
Authors: Eda Kumru, Özge Demir, Gülce Ediş, Fatih Ekinci, Koray Açıcı, Ayhan Aydın, Mustafa Sevindik, Mehmet Serdar Güzel, Ilgaz Akata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate interpretation of microscopic morphological features is critical for resolving taxonomic boundaries in morphologically similar fungal taxa, yet conventional image analysis approaches largely rely on discrete classification or pixel-level segmentation, which fail to capture the inherently continuous nature of biological microstructures. In this work, a continuous spatial representation learning framework is introduced for the analysis of scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of lycoperdioid fungal spores by integrating convolutional neural network (CNN)-based local feature encoding with a coordinate-aware neural operator architecture. Microscopic images are reformulated as functions defined over a two-dimensional spatial domain, and a Deep Operator Network (DeepONet) is employed to learn mappings from image functions to continuous activation fields under weak supervision. The proposed framework was evaluated on high-resolution SEM images from five lycoperdioid species, acquired under standardized imaging conditions. Quantitative analyses within the evaluated model configuration indicate that the operator-learning-based approach achieves a 28–35% reduction in mean squared error relative to proxy-based activation references and an 18–22% improvement over patch-level CNN representations without operator coupling. Residual norm and line-profile analyses further reveal a ~ 30% suppression of high-frequency spatial fluctuations, particularly in boundary-dense regions, indicating enhanced spatial regularization. Explainability analysis based on continuous activation fields shows that over 70% of high-response regions consistently align with diagnostically relevant micromorphological features, while same-class activation variance is reduced by 20–25%, confirming improved representational stability without over-smoothing. By moving beyond discrete decision-making toward operator-based continuous modeling, this study establishes a scalable and interpretable framework for microscopic SEM image analysis. The proposed methodology provides a transferable foundation for explainable artificial intelligence applications across fungal taxonomy, biomedical imaging, and other domains requiring high-resolution spatial interpretation of complex microstructures.Keywords: Scanning Electron Microscopy, Neural Operator Learning, DeepONet, Microscopic Image Analysis, Explainable Artificial IntelligenceSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Lycoperdioid fungi (puffballs) are gasteroid Basidiomycota that form globose to subglobose basidiomata and disperse spores passively through an apical opening in the peridium1. Their taxonomy, especially within Lycoperdon sensu lato, has relied on macromorphological traits such as basidiome shape, peridial surface, capillitium structure, and spore mass color and texture for over a century2. While practical for identification, these traits are limited by species overlap and morphological convergence across lineages3.

The practical implications of this phylogenetic framework are especially evident in species complexes with highly similar basidiomata, where traditional features offer weak differentiation3. For example, taxa such as Apioperdon pyriforme (Schaeff.) Vizzini, Lycoperdon atropurpureum Vittad., L. echinatum Pers., L. excipuliforme (Scop.) Pers., and L. perlatum Pers. often develop basidiomata of similar size and shape, differing only subtly in peridial ornamentation or surface texture1,3. Consequently, Apioperdon pyriforme was once classified within Lycoperdon, while other similar taxa remain in a more narrowly defined Lycoperdon sensu stricto, showing how convergence has repeatedly obscured true relationships2. This morphological similarity has also led to ongoing misidentifications, with inconsistent naming in field surveys and frequent mislabeling in fungarium collections2,4.

Reliable identification of lycoperdioid fungi increasingly depends on molecular evidence or on micromorphological characters, which offer greater taxonomic consistency than macroscopic features alone2. When DNA sequence data are unavailable, basidiospore characteristics such as size, shape, wall structure, and surface ornamentation provide a robust basis for species delimitation because they show stable differences among species and low variability within species3. Ornamentation patterns, including echinulate, verrucose, reticulate, or punctate surfaces, together with the presence and development of pedicels, function as diagnostic characters when examined in detail2. Detecting these features requires high magnification, and both advanced light microscopy and electron microscopy are essential for revealing ultrastructural traits that cannot be resolved using routine observational methods5. Consequently, an integrative approach that combines ribosomal internal transcribed spacer ITS-based molecular data with detailed analyses of spore micromorphology is critical for resolving species boundaries and improving identification accuracy within Lycoperdon and related genera2,4.

Building on this framework, using AI to analyze SEM images of basidiospores offers an innovative approach to classifying lycoperdioid fungi. SEM provides detailed insights into spore ornamentation, wall structures, and pedicel features, which are often subtle and complex. Integrating these images with machine learning algorithms enables consistent quantification, comparison, and classification, minimizing observer bias and enhancing differentiation among similar taxa. Automated analysis of SEM images can improve species identification and help distinguish closely related lineages within Lycoperdon and related genera. AI-supported spore micromorphology complements ITS molecular data, establishing a more objective and scalable system for lycoperdioid fungal taxonomy1–5.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence have enabled macroscopic and microscopic image analysis to move beyond discrete classification and segmentation toward learning spatially continuous and interpretable representations6–13. In this context, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are widely used to encode local texture, edge density, and fine-scale morphological patterns from high-resolution images, yet they remain inherently limited in capturing long-range spatial coherence when applied in isolation14,15. To address this limitation, operator-learning-based architectures have emerged as a powerful alternative by formulating images as functions defined over a spatial domain and learning mappings between function spaces rather than fixed-dimensional vectors16. Among these approaches, Deep Operator Networks (DeepONets) provide a principled framework for integrating local feature representations with explicit spatial coordinate information, enabling resolution-independent and spatially continuous outputs17. By coupling CNN-based local encoders with coordinate-aware neural operators, such frameworks allow microscopic structures to be modeled as continuous activation fields, offering improved numerical stability, enhanced interpretability, and a more faithful representation of the inherently continuous nature of biological microstructures18–20.

The novelty of this work lies in reformulating microscopic SEM image analysis from a discrete decision problem into a continuous operator-learning framework that explicitly models spatial activation as a function over the image domain. Unlike prevailing approaches in the literature that rely on classification or pixel-level segmentation, the proposed methodology introduces a CNN-guided neural operator paradigm capable of learning resolution-independent activation fields under proxy-guided supervision. This contribution extends current microscopic image analysis literature by demonstrating, for the first time in this context, that neural operator architectures such as DeepONet can generate spatially coherent, numerically stable, and interpretable representations from SEM data without requiring dense annotations. Beyond its methodological contribution, the framework provides a scalable and transferable foundation with broad impact potential, enabling consistent analysis across varying magnification levels and imaging modalities. As such, the proposed approach offers a generalizable pathway for explainable artificial intelligence in microscopic and ultrastructural image analysis, with implications extending to fungal taxonomy, biomedical imaging, materials science, and other domains where continuous spatial interpretation of high-resolution imagery is critical18–20.

Materials and methods

The experimental workflow was designed to integrate classical morphological assessment with high-resolution microscopic imaging under standardized and reproducible conditions. Specimen collection, preparation, imaging, and downstream analyses were conducted following a unified protocol to ensure consistency across samples and to minimize variability arising from environmental, instrumental, or operator-dependent factors. Particular emphasis was placed on preserving micromorphological integrity during sample handling and imaging, as subtle surface features and ultrastructural patterns form the basis for both taxonomic interpretation and subsequent computational analysis. This integrated methodological framework establishes a robust foundation for correlating morphological observations with quantitative image-based representations derived from scanning electron microscopy.

Morphological and SEM studies

Specimens were collected from the province of İstanbul, and detailed records of their macroscopic characteristics and ecological habitat data were obtained during field studies. The morphological features of the examined specimens are presented in Fig. 1 21.

The dataset used in this study consists of Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) images from five morphologically distinct Lycoperdoid species, with corresponding GenBank accession numbers provided. Apioperdon pyriforme (PX872887), Lycoperdon atropurpureum (PX872902), L. echinatum (PX872888), L. excipuliforme (PX872901), L. perlatum (PX872889), and the SEM micrograph dataset is publicly available via Zenodo22. The SEM analyses were performed using a QUANTA 400 F Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FE-SEM) with a maximum resolution of 1.2 nm, and the SEM micrographs were acquired at 40,000× magnification, calibrated based on the largest spore size observed in the studied fungus. A constant SEM spot size of 3 was used during image acquisition to maintain consistent beam conditions and to enable comparable visualization of fine spore surface ornamentation across all specimens. Each class included 160 high-resolution SEM micrographs acquired under standardized conditions at 300 dpi to ensure consistent magnification and structural clarity. Images were obtained from locally prepared fungal specimens under controlled laboratory lighting to minimize background artifacts. The dataset consisted of 800 images organized into species-specific directories and partitioned into training (70%), validation (15%), and testing (15%) subsets using a stratified random sampling strategy implemented in Python to preserve class distribution and ensure experimental reproducibility23,24. All images were stored in lossless TIFF format to minimize compression-induced degradation of fine morphological structures25.

The SEM micrographs were acquired from multiple spores and different microscopic surface regions belonging to morphologically distinct fungal specimens under standardized imaging conditions26. Dataset partitioning was performed at the image level using stratified random sampling in order to preserve class balance across subsets. The acquired images included variations in local surface topology, spore orientation, ornamentation density, and microscopic acquisition regions, thereby increasing intra-class diversity. Although the present framework focuses on continuous operator-based spatial representation learning rather than specimen memorization, the potential influence of specimen-level partitioning is acknowledged as a methodological consideration for future large-scale studies27.

The SEM dataset was intentionally constructed using a balanced class distribution in order to minimize potential class imbalance effects during operator-learning optimization28. Each of the five fungal species contributed 160 SEM micrographs acquired under standardized imaging conditions, resulting in a total dataset of 800 images with equal class representation. In addition, stratified random sampling was employed during dataset partitioning to preserve balanced class proportions across the training, validation, and testing subsets29. Therefore, additional imbalance mitigation strategies such as class weighting or oversampling were not required within the present framework.

Subsequent laboratory investigations involved detailed micromorphological examinations using light microscopy, with multiple measurements conducted in different chemical reagents. Ultrastructural features were further assessed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Representative SEM micrographs of the examined specimens are presented in Fig. 2a-e.

DNA extraction, PCR amplification, sanger sequencing (ITS) and phylogenetic analyses

Genomic DNA was isolated from voucher specimens preserved in the fungarium collection of the Ankara University Herbarium (ANK) using a cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB)-based extraction method adapted for fungal tissues containing high levels of polysaccharides and secondary metabolites30. The complete molecular workflow, including DNA extraction, ITS amplification, Sanger sequencing, sequence editing, phylogenetic analysis, and database deposition, is summarized schematically in Fig. 3.

Overview of the molecular workflow applied in this study. The workflow included CTAB-based DNA extraction from lycoperdoid fungal samples, PCR amplification of the ITS region, agarose gel electrophoresis, Sanger sequencing, sequence assembly, multiple sequence alignment, model-based phylogenetic inference, and phylogenetic tree reconstruction.

Approximately 20–30 mg of dried fungal tissue was mechanically disrupted and processed using CTAB extraction buffer. The extracted DNA was purified with chloroform–isoamyl alcohol, precipitated with cold isopropanol, washed with 70% ethanol, and resuspended in sterile distilled water. DNA quality was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis.

The nuclear ribosomal internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region was amplified using the universal primer pair ITS1 and ITS431. PCR amplification was performed using GoTaq DNA Polymerase under standard cycling conditions, and successful amplicons were checked by agarose gel electrophoresis before Sanger sequencing. Forward and reverse chromatograms were manually inspected, edited, and assembled into consensus sequences.

The ITS sequences generated in this study were used primarily to confirm the taxonomic identity of the specimens included in the SEM image dataset and to ensure the reliability of the species labels used for model development. Additional reference sequences were retrieved from the NCBI GenBank database, and multiple sequence alignment was performed using MUSCLE32,33. Phylogenetic relationships were inferred using the Maximum Likelihood method34 implemented in MEGA1235. The Tamura three-parameter model with a proportion of invariable sites (T92 + I) was selected as the nucleotide substitution model36. Branch support was evaluated using 1000 bootstrap replicates37, and bootstrap values below 70% were not shown on the phylogenetic tree. Agaricus duplocingulatoides was used as the outgroup taxon.

Deep learning-based classification framework

Microscopic image analysis differs from classical computer vision problems both conceptually and mathematically because of the highly complex, irregular, and multi-scale morphological characteristics of biological structures38. At the microscopic level, observed structures are rarely separated by sharp boundaries; instead, tissue density, granularity, microstructural organization, and texture characteristics vary continuously across space. Since this continuity is intrinsic to the nature of biological patterns, addressing the problem solely through classification approaches that produce discrete class labels or segmentation methods that impose hard pixel-level decisions inevitably leads to a loss of representational information39. In this study, the problem of microscopic image analysis is therefore reformulated as a continuous spatial representation learning problem rather than a task of discrete output generation.

The operator learning perspective is particularly important because the objective is not merely to generate outputs on a fixed pixel grid at a given resolution, but to obtain a spatially continuous representation that can be queried at arbitrary coordinates within the analyzed image domain42. This property is conceptually relevant to microscopic image analysis, where scale variations and different magnification levels are commonly encountered, although such cross-resolution generalization was not empirically evaluated in the present study.

Operator learning is a recently developed machine learning paradigm designed to learn mappings between entire function spaces rather than between fixed-dimensional vectors43. Unlike conventional deep learning models that typically process finite-dimensional inputs and produce discrete outputs, neural operator frameworks aim to model how spatially distributed functions evolve over a domain. In this context, the input image is interpreted not simply as a collection of pixels, but as a continuous spatial function defined over the image plane. The objective of the operator is therefore to learn how local spatial patterns are transformed into another continuous functional representation. This perspective is particularly suitable for microscopic SEM images because biological microstructures such as spore ornamentation, boundary irregularities, and ultrastructural surface transitions inherently exhibit continuous spatial organization rather than isolated discrete patterns44.

Among neural operator architectures, DeepONet provides a coordinate-aware framework composed of two complementary components: a branch network and a trunk network45. The branch network learns local morphological representations extracted from microscopic image patches, while the trunk network learns how these representations vary across spatial coordinates. The interaction between these two components enables the model to generate continuous activation fields that preserve both local micromorphological detail and global spatial continuity46. In the context of fungal SEM analysis, this formulation allows the framework to represent gradual changes in ornamentation density, echinulate protrusions, and surface topology more naturally than conventional classification-based approaches that compress the entire image into a single categorical decision45,46.

Accordingly, each microscopic image was decomposed into local patches using a fixed window size P, representing the patch size, and stride S, representing the spatial step between adjacent patches. The objective of this study is to generate a continuous activation field from microscopic images; however, reliable and objective ground truth for such a field is rarely available in microscopic datasets51. The role of the proxy field is not to serve as an exact representation of biological reality, but to provide a repeatable, automatically generated reference that guides the learning process spatially. This approach is consistent with proxy-guided learning principles, where learning is initiated using partial or noisy signals while aiming for more regularized representations through model generalization52. The important thing is that the aim of the model is not to exactly replicate the proxy signal, but to be a smooth and structured continuous field learning model regulated by the proxy signal53.

The proxy activation field was generated through a deterministic image-processing pipeline designed to emphasize structurally relevant spatial transitions within SEM images54. First, input images were converted into normalized grayscale representations in order to standardize local intensity distributions. Subsequently, gradient-sensitive filtering and edge-enhancement operations were applied to highlight high-frequency morphological regions associated with ornamentation boundaries, surface protrusions, and local texture transitions. Spatial smoothing was then performed to reduce isolated noise responses while preserving coherent structural regions. Finally, the resulting activation responses were normalized into a continuous spatial field defined over the image domain. Importantly, the proxy field was not intended to serve as an exact biological segmentation mask or definitive ground-truth annotation; rather, it functioned as a deterministic proxy-guidance signal supporting the operator-learning optimization process under limited biological supervision55.

Further assessment of the biological relevance of the learned activation fields was conducted through qualitative examination of representative SEM images from the test subset by experts experienced in fungal micromorphology. The evaluation focused on diagnostically relevant ultrastructural regions, including ornamentation boundaries, echinulate protrusions, pedicel-associated structures, and high-curvature surface transitions. High-response activation regions were compared with expert-identified morphological areas using an overlap based spatial consistency analysis under a fixed activation threshold56. The analysis aimed to determine whether the learned continuous activation fields corresponded with biologically meaningful micromorphological patterns rather than merely reflecting the proxy activation structure.

Data preprocessing plays a critical role in microscopic image analysis because SEM datasets frequently contain heterogeneous image formats, varying bit depths, and device-dependent intensity distributions57. Each input image was initially read in its native format and expanded to three channels when grayscale representations were encountered. Images stored in BGR or BGRA format were subsequently converted into RGB space to ensure color-space consistency during feature extraction. When the input datatype differed from uint8, intensity normalization was applied using min–max scaling in order to standardize dynamic ranges across acquisitions obtained from different microscopic systems58. A convolutional neural network-based encoder is employed to generate feature representations suitable for operator learning60. Unlike classical CNN-based studies, this encoder is not used for direct classification, but rather to generate latent representations that serve as inputs to the branch component of the operator architecture. Each patch is mapped to a low-dimensional vector, where d denotes the encoder output dimensionality. The role of the CNN here is to summarize local textural and morphological information into a feature vector. Given the importance of fine-scale patterns in microscopic data, aggressive pooling operations that would prematurely discard local details are avoided61. This design choice reflects the principle that while deep pooling may promote global invariance, it can suppress subtle local structures that are critical for constructing a continuous spatial field.

The operator itself is learned using the DeepONet architecture, which consists of a branch network encoding the input function samples and a trunk network encoding the coordinates at which the output is evaluated62. The CNN-based encoder used in the present framework was designed as a lightweight local feature extraction module for generating compact latent morphological representations from SEM image patches64. The encoder consisted of sequential convolutional layers with ReLU activation functions and fixed kernel sizes designed to preserve fine-scale micromorphological texture information while progressively reducing spatial dimensionality. The resulting local feature maps were transformed into a compact latent representation vector d, which was subsequently provided to the DeepONet branch network for coordinate-aware operator learning. The encoder architecture was intentionally kept computationally lightweight because the primary objective of the study was to investigate continuous operator-based spatial representation learning rather than CNN architecture optimization65.

During inference, the learned operator was evaluated densely across the image plane. Patch-level predictions were reshaped into a coarse spatial grid and subsequently interpolated to full image resolution, yielding a continuous activation field normalized to the range [0,1] for visualization and spatial analysis69. This inference strategy reflects one of the principal advantages of operator learning, namely the ability to generate resolution-independent and spatially continuous functional representations.

Importantly, within the proposed framework, the CNN encoder does not operate as a classifier and does not produce class probabilities or softmax-based categorical outputs. Its role is restricted to extracting local functional representations from SEM image patches, which are subsequently integrated with explicit spatial coordinates through the operator-learning structure. The combination of CNN-based local encoding and coordinate-aware operator learning therefore provides a mathematically consistent framework for generating continuous activation fields from microscopic images70.

From a biological perspective, the DeepONet framework can be interpreted as a system that learns how microscopic morphological patterns evolve continuously across the spore surface rather than assigning a single global decision to the entire image71. In SEM images of lycoperdioid fungi, diagnostically important features such as ornamentation density, echinulate protrusions, pedicel-associated regions, and surface-boundary irregularities often vary gradually across neighboring spatial regions. Conventional CNN-based classification approaches generally compress these local structures into a single categorical representation, potentially limiting spatial interpretability72. In contrast, the coordinate-aware DeepONet framework evaluates local morphological patterns jointly with their spatial coordinates, enabling the generation of a continuous activation field that reflects how biologically relevant micromorphological structures are distributed across the spore surface. This operator-learning strategy therefore provides a more spatially coherent and biologically interpretable representation of fungal spore ultrastructure71,72.

The results presented in this section detail the behavior of continuous spatial activation fields obtained through the combined use of patch-based sampling, convolutional neural network-based local representation extraction, and the coordinate-aware DeepONet architecture. The findings demonstrate that the proposed approach not only solves a regression problem minimizing proxy references but also learns a functional and ordered field representation by considering local morphological patterns in microscopic SEM images along with their spatial context. The patch grid presented in Fig. 5 clearly demonstrates how the input function is sampled in the context of operator learning.

Patch grid.

The decomposition of the SEM image into local patches with a fixed step size reveals a deliberate avoidance of treating the image as a single global tensor. Instead, local observations of the function defined on the image space, taken from specific points, are fed into the model. This approach enables the local-scale capture of high-frequency edges, protrusions, and micro-irregularities frequently observed on microscopic spore surfaces. The regular and deterministic structure of the patch grid ensures that the normalized coordinates fed into the trunk component also form a consistent spatial reference; this allows for clear visualization of where the output field is being spatially evaluated. The DeepONet-based continuous activation space presented in Fig. 6 clearly demonstrates that the model does not produce a discrete classification or segmentation output; instead, it learns a continuous response function defined on the image plane.

Continuous activation field.

The concentration of activation in the boundary regions of the spore structure and in areas where the surface morphology is more complex shows that the model can distinguish local textural cues and associate these cues with spatial location. In contrast, the low activation level in homogeneous background regions reveals that the model’s output is not a global response or a noise-based distribution; it is modulated in direct relation to the visual content. This behavior supports the idea that local representations extracted by the CNN encoder are integrated with coordinate information via the trunk component to create a functional output. At this point, the impact of the CNN-based encoder on the results should be considered in more detail. In this study, the CNN does not generate any class labels or decisions; it maps the local morphological information in each patch into a low-dimensional latent representation space suitable for operator learning. Each patch shown in Fig. 1 is processed by a CNN encoder and converted into a vector summarizing microscopic features such as surface texture, granularity, edge density, and local contrast. These latent vectors are fed as input to the DeepONet branch component and evaluated together with the coordinate information provided by the trunk component to produce a spatially continuous output. Therefore, the CNN alone does not provide spatial continuity of the output; however, it plays a critical role as a local information source within the operator learning architecture. The rapid decrease in the MSE value in the first epochs indicates that the CNN encoder can represent the basic local patterns defined by the proxy in the early stages. The slower and more stable decrease in the loss value in later epochs reveals that the encoder, branch, and trunk components are learning a more regular functional representation together. The absence of unstable oscillations during the training process shows that the optimization is numerically balanced and that the learned operator has reached a stable solution. Overall, these results demonstrate that the combination of patch-based sampling, CNN-based local representation extraction, and the coordinate-aware DeepONet architecture provides a consistent framework, both methodologically and practically, for generating continuous spatial activation fields in microscopic SEM images. The resulting activation fields exhibit more regular, spatially consistent, and class-contextual patterns compared to the proxy reference; This situation strongly demonstrates the potential of the operator learning approach to generate continuous representations instead of discrete decisions in microscopic image analysis.

Discussion

The ITS-based phylogenetic analysis presented in this study supports the species-level identification of specimens assigned to Lycoperdon and Apioperdon and is consistent with the current taxonomic concepts for these genera. The identification of clades that match morphologically (based on physical characteristics) and nomenclaturally recognized taxa indicates that the ITS region provides sufficient resolution for distinguishing species within Lycoperdaceae. This supports the widespread use of ITS as the primary DNA sequence used to identify fungal species, also known as the fungal DNA barcode73.

The clear separation of Apioperdon pyriforme from Lycoperdon species further supports the recognition of Apioperdon as a distinct genus, as proposed in earlier molecular and taxonomic studies. The recovery of Apioperdon as an independent lineage is consistent with previous phylogenetic analyses based on ribosomal DNA markers and demonstrates that ITS data are sufficient to recover established generic boundaries within Lycoperdaceae74.

This study presents a framework for the analysis of microscopic SEM images that goes beyond classical classification and pixel-level segmentation approaches, based on continuous spatial representation learning75,76. The inherently irregular, multi-scale, and continuous morphological features of biological microstructures are often modeled in the literature using discrete labels or hard boundaries; however, such approaches inevitably lead to a loss of a significant portion of the represented information77,78.

In this context, addressing microscopic images from a functional perspective offers an alternative that more naturally reflects the continuity of biological patterns. In the proposed method, microscopic images are modeled as input functions defined on a two-dimensional space, and the problem is reformulated as learning an operator that generates a continuous scalar field defined on the same space from these functions79. This approach conceptually aligns with the neural operator learning literature, which has been successfully used in recent years in the context of partial differential equations and physics-based systems80,81. The DeepONet architecture was selected because of its theoretical capacity to generalize across function spaces; however, broader empirical generalization beyond the present dataset remains to be validated.

The convolutional neural network used for the extraction of local visual patterns was used not to directly generate a decision or class probability, unlike classical CNN-based studies, but to generate latent function representations that provide input to the operator learning process. It is well known in the microscopic image analysis literature that CNNs can effectively encode features such as local texture, granularity, and microstructure organization82,83.

Transferring these local representations along with spatial coordinates to the DeepONet trunk component enabled the clear separation of visual content and location information and created a suitable structure for continuous field generation. The use of a proxy activation field in the training process offers a proxy-guided supervision strategy, given the lack of reliable pixel-level annotations84.

Treating the proxy field not as a direct biological location reality but as a regulatory signal guiding the learning process prevented the model from simply replicating a deterministic image processing output; Instead, it enabled the learning of a more spatially smooth activation domain within the present dataset. The steady reduction in MSE loss and the achievement of low error levels during training demonstrate that the proposed architecture can be stably optimized under this proxy-supervised training scenario. The resulting continuous activation domains are interpretable in a resolution-independent manner, unlike classical pixel-based outputs. This feature is directly related to the “grid-independent generalization” capability, which is highlighted in the literature as one of the fundamental advantages of neural operators75. Considering the need for consistent analysis across different magnification levels and imaging conditions in microscopic images, such a representation may have practical importance, although this broader applicability requires further validation.

The SEM-based activation fields obtained in this study should be regarded not only as computational outputs of the model, but also as visual representations of the micromorphological regions contributing to species-level discrimination. The fact that these activation fields were concentrated particularly on spore surface characteristics such as ornamentation density, surface roughness, verrucose or reticulate structures, and local topological differences indicates that these outputs can be associated with diagnostic characters traditionally used in fungal taxonomy3,5. In this respect, the model provides an additional interpretative layer that complements ITS-based phylogenetic identification and conventional morphological assessment. Particularly in taxa that are morphologically similar or exhibit overlapping microscopic characters, the activation maps may direct the taxonomist’s attention to subtle yet informative SEM surface features that might otherwise be overlooked. Therefore, this approach may contribute to making species delimitation more visible and interpretable, reducing the risk of misidentification, and enabling the practical use of SEM images as a supportive tool in integrative taxonomy85,86.

Although the proposed framework showed stable performance within the present SEM dataset, its generalization capacity should be interpreted with caution because the current evidence is based on a single dataset comprising five fungal species acquired under standardized imaging conditions. Therefore, broader transferability across different fungal groups, SEM devices, magnification settings, laboratories, and acquisition protocols has not yet been demonstrated. Future studies using larger multi-centre datasets, broader taxonomic sampling, cross-device validation, and heterogeneous imaging conditions are required to evaluate the robustness and general applicability of the proposed approach. Despite the promising results obtained in this study, several limitations of the proposed framework should be acknowledged. First, the current operator-learning architecture was evaluated under standardized SEM acquisition conditions and may exhibit sensitivity to substantial magnification variability, severe contamination artifacts, or highly overlapping spore structures not extensively represented in the present dataset. Second, the framework operates under weak spatial supervision using proxy-derived activation guidance rather than dense expert-defined biological annotations, which may limit direct biological interpretability in certain complex microscopic scenarios. In addition, the present study primarily serves as a proof-of-concept demonstration of continuous operator-based spatial representation learning for microscopic SEM image analysis rather than a fully optimized benchmark-oriented predictive framework. Future studies involving larger multi-center datasets, fully annotated biological reference maps, cross-magnification evaluations, and alternative neural operator architectures may further improve the robustness, scalability, and biological generalization capability of the proposed methodology.

The results of this study demonstrate clear quantitative differences among the evaluated modeling strategies, highlighting the advantages of the proposed CNN-guided DeepONet framework. Compared to the proxy-based activation approach, the operator-learning model achieved a 28–35% reduction in mean squared error (MSE), indicating a substantially improved approximation of continuous spatial activation fields. Relative to patch-level CNN representations without operator coupling, DeepONet further reduced spatial error by approximately 18–22%, reflecting its superior ability to integrate local features with global spatial context. Residual norm analysis showed that high-frequency discrepancies, particularly concentrated around boundary and curvature-dense regions, were suppressed by ~ 30% in the operator-based formulation, whereas both proxy-driven and CNN-only approaches preserved stronger local oscillations. In addition, line-profile variance across principal spatial axes decreased by ~ 32% under the DeepONet architecture, quantitatively confirming smoother functional transitions. Same-class consistency analysis revealed a 20–25% reduction in intra-class activation variance compared to CNN-only models, while maintaining inter-class separability. Collectively, these percentage-level improvements demonstrate that the DeepONet-based operator framework outperforms alternative algorithms in terms of numerical stability, spatial regularization, and consistency of learned representations, establishing it as the most robust modeling strategy evaluated in this study. However, these values should be interpreted as descriptive performance estimates rather than statistically generalized performance claims, because repeated independent runs, standard deviations, and confidence intervals were not included in the present study.

Conclusion

This study provides molecular data supporting the species-level identification of Lycoperdon and Apioperdon specimens based on ITS rDNA sequences. The phylogenetic analyses are consistent with current taxonomic treatments of these genera and demonstrate that ITS data are suitable for species-level identification within Lycoperdaceae. In addition, the successful use of dried fungarium material preserved in the Ankara University Herbarium (ANK) highlights the value of curated fungal collections for molecular systematic studies. Overall, the integration of fungarium specimens with molecular phylogenetic approaches contributes to a more robust and verifiable framework for fungal taxonomy.

This study demonstrates that microscopic SEM images of lycoperdioid fungal spores can be modeled more faithfully through continuous spatial representation learning rather than conventional discrete classification or segmentation paradigms. By integrating CNN-based local feature encoding with a coordinate-aware DeepONet architecture, the proposed framework consistently generated smooth, spatially coherent activation fields aligned with biologically meaningful microstructural regions. Quantitatively, the operator-learning-based approach achieved a ~ 28–35% reduction in mean squared error (MSE) relative to the proxy activation reference, indicating a substantial improvement in spatial regularity and functional consistency. Residual analyses further revealed that high-frequency fluctuations at spore boundaries were suppressed by approximately 30%, while preserving class-discriminative morphological cues. From an explainability perspective, activation-field-based XAI analysis showed that over 70% of the high-response regions overlapped with diagnostically relevant ornamentation and boundary structures, confirming that the learned representations are not only smoother but also biologically interpretable. Among the evaluated configurations, the CNN-guided DeepONet model consistently produced the most stable and class-consistent activation fields, outperforming proxy-driven and purely local representations in both numerical convergence and spatial coherence.

An additional practical advantage of the proposed framework arises from its localized spatial analysis strategy based on patch-coordinate operator learning. Because the model evaluates microscopic structures through fine-scale spatial representations rather than relying solely on global image appearance, the resulting activation fields are comparatively less sensitive to moderate medium impurities, localized SEM acquisition artifacts, and small variations in magnification conditions. The SEM images used in this study were acquired under standardized biologically appropriate magnification levels commonly employed by experts during fungal micromorphological examination, enabling the framework to focus primarily on diagnostically relevant ultrastructural patterns. Nevertheless, highly overlapping spores, severe contamination conditions, and substantial cross-magnification variability may still influence activation-field stability and should be investigated in future studies. Owing to its continuous spatial representation capability, the proposed operator-learning framework may also be transferable to other fungal groups, pollen morphology, diatom analysis, cellular microscopy, histopathological imaging, and broader biomedical image interpretation tasks.

Beyond the specific case of lycoperdioid fungal spores, the proposed framework is inherently extensible. Future studies should replicate this approach using alternative operator-learning architectures (e.g., Fourier Neural Operators, graph-based neural operators, or transformer-assisted operator models) to assess generalization across different functional priors. In addition, while this study focused on SEM imagery, the methodology can be directly transferred to other microscopic and imaging modalities, including light microscopy, confocal microscopy, histopathological whole-slide images, and even non-biological microstructural datasets. Applying the framework to multimodal image sources and cross-scale data would further validate its robustness and establish continuous operator learning as a general-purpose paradigm for explainable, high-resolution microscopic image analysis.
