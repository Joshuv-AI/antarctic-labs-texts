Clinical Outcomes of AO/OTA Type C Distal Radius Fractures Treated With Volar Locking Plate Versus Fragment-Specific Plating
Authors: Mallery Zeiman, Joshua E Lerman, Gabriela Almanzar, William A Civatte, John T Capo, Amish A Naik, Jaclyn M Jankowski, Frank A Liporace, Richard S Yoon, Nicole Montero-Lopez, Bishoy N Saad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeDistal radius fractures remain one of the most common adult fractures, with increasing operative fixation rates. Volar locking plates (VLPs) have been widely used because of their low complication rates and straightforward approach. Fragment-specific fixation (FSF) is often used for more complex fractures. Prior comparative studies have been limited because of smaller cohorts.MethodsA retrospective multicenter review was performed on patients who underwent surgical fixation with either VLPs or FSF from January 2018 to June 2024. Demographic, operative, and postoperative data were collected. Analysis used t test for continuous variables and Fisher exact test was used for categorical variables (P ≤ .05).ResultsIn total, 211 patients were included: 150 VLP and 61 FSF. The two groups were comparable in terms of demographics and comorbidities. The data set included individuals presenting with complete articular, type C, fractures in accordance with the Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen/Orthopaedic Trauma Association (AO/OTA) classification system. Compared with the VLP group, FSF was associated with longer operative times (133 vs 94 minutes) and greater removal of hardware rates (19.7% vs 7.3%). Intraoperative complications, postoperative complications, 90-day emergency department visits, and readmissions were comparable between the groups.ConclusionsFragment-specific fixation was associated with longer operative times and higher rates of reoperation for hardware removal but did not appear to increase rates of major adverse postoperative clinical outcomes when compared with VLP.Type of study/level of evidenceTherapeutic III.Key words: Distal radius fracture, Fragment-specific fixation (FSF), Volar locking plate (VLP)

Distal radius fractures (DRFs) are a common injury in adults and represent a significant source of functional impairment, despite shifts in their epidemiology. Although the incidence of wrist fractures has declined in the United States, the rate of operative fixation has increased over the past two decades.1, 2, 3, 4 Recent studies estimate DRFs account for 6.9% of all adult fractures, with a bimodal age distribution affecting both younger and older patients.2,4,5 These injuries impact school, work, and activities in younger patients, whereas the older population often experience loss of functional ability and independence.3 Although nonsurgical treatment is often associated with social costs related to prolonged immobilization and delayed return to function, operative treatment carries a higher economic burden.6 Given the increasing operative rates and an aging population, it is imperative to discuss optimal surgical management of DRFs.

Volar locking plates (VLPs) have become the most widely used fixation method because of their favorable functional outcomes and relatively low complication rates compared with other techniques.7 The use of a single volar incision and the one plate construct provides a practical and efficient surgical approach. However, VLPs are not universally applicable. Complex, comminuted intra-articular fractures may require alternative fixation methods.7, 8, 9, 10

Fragment-specific fixation (FSF) provides this alternative by stabilizing the fragments with multiple plates or pins based on the columnar anatomy of the distal forearm.11 Early reports show that FSF has demonstrated early mobility and good functional and radiographic results.11, 12, 13, 14 However, the FSF technique often requires multiple surgical approaches, which incurs a greater risk of surgical complications if not approached carefully.11

Multiple studies have compared VLPs with FSF, reporting generally acceptable outcomes for both, with lower complication rates associated with VLPs and less fragment displacement associated with FSF.9,15,16 However, these prior comparative studies are limited by small cohorts ranging from 12 to 100 patients (6–25 in the FSF group).9,15,16 This study aimed to address this limitation by presenting a large, multicenter retrospective comparison of VLP and FSF for DRFs, hypothesizing that VLP would be associated with similar clinical outcomes to FSF.

Materials and Methods

A retrospective multicenter database of DRFs was created after institutional review board approval. The group consisted of patients treated for DRFs between January 2018 and June 2024 from six participating orthopedic surgeons across three institutions. Current Procedural Terminology codes were used to identify patients and data were collected by three independent medical student researchers who were trained by a board-certified and fellowship-trained orthopedic trauma surgeon to classify based on Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen (AO) criteria.

For the current study, patients treated with VLP or FSF were identified. The study population was defined by the following inclusion criteria: skeletally mature patients who sustained a complete articular (AO/Orthopaedic Trauma Association [OTA] type C) fracture and underwent open reduction internal fixation using either FSF or a VLP with radiographic imaging on file. Exclusion criteria included the following: AO/OTA type A and B fractures, closed reduction only, surgery performed by nonparticipating surgeons, and lack of postoperative follow-up (minimum 2 weeks) at the included institutions.

Patients were stratified into a FSF or VLP cohort based on the fixation method used. Clinical and demographic data were collected through manual chart review. Extracted variables included: age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score, body mass index (BMI), AO/OTA classification, operative time, length of hospital stay (LOS), surgical complications, postoperative emergency department (ED) visits, readmissions, removal of hardware, and postoperative complications. Complications in this cohort are defined as prolonged stiffness, hardware failure, nonunion, malunion, and tendon injury. Statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel. Continuous variables were compared using paired t tests. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher exact test. Statistical significance was defined as a P ≤ .05.

Results

A total of 928 patients with DRFs were identified, 438 (47.2%) of which underwent operative fixation. After applying our inclusion/exclusion criteria, 211 were analyzed: 150 were treated with VLP and 61 with FSF. The mean age was 55.4 years, with 59.2%% women. There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of age, BMI, sex, CCI, or follow-up time (Table 1).

Discussion

This study represents a sizable cohort comparing VLP and FSF for DRFs with a sample size of 211 patients. Prior comparative studies have been limited by smaller sample sizes, with cohorts ranging from 46 to 99 patients.15, 16, 17 Baseline demographic characteristics such as age, BMI, gender, and CCI score were comparable between groups, which strengthens the internal validity.

Our findings align with the prior literature showing that FSF achieves comparable outcomes to VLP fixation. Sammer et al,16 Wei et al,17 and Landgren et al15 reported similar functional outcomes between VLP and FSF, however, they reported statistically significantly higher complications specifically related to reoperation/ROH in the FSF group. Our data reflect similar trends, demonstrating significantly higher operative time and ROH in the FSF group (Table 3). These findings are largely attributable to the nature of FSF, which often requires multiple plates, staged removal, and typically requires longer operative times because of the increased complexity of the fracture and the additional fixation.11 However, our study did not demonstrate significantly higher overall postoperative complication rates. Our study was unique because of its focus on solely AO/OTA Type C fractures in order to best eliminate selection bias because of the nature of FSF use in complex fracture patterns. This is an important distinction compared with prior literature that examined types A, B, and C.15, 16, 17 Compared with prior literature, our study provides definitions for the complications for clarity, which may provide additional guidance to surgeons considering FSF. ROH constitutes an additional operative burden, whether it is part of a planned outcome or because of hardware failure. This cohort saw significantly more cases of ROH in the FSF group. Although the majority of these were part of the planned staged treatment of these fractures, it necessitates another surgical event with its associated risks, additional anesthesia, and recovery time. Although ROH is not unique to FSF, the extra clinical burden demonstrated in this cohort is a factor that must be kept in consideration when comparing VLP treatment with FSF.

Our study showed that the surgeons elected for FSF in more complex fracture patterns (Table 2), which aligns with the prior literature.13,14 Despite the greater proportion of complex (AO/OTA class C3) fractures in the FSF group (47.5% vs 19.3%), major clinical outcomes such as 30-day ED visits, 90-day ED visits, readmissions, and postoperative complications were comparable between the 2 groups (Tables 3 and 4). However, the FSF group had nearly double the percent of 30-day ED visits compared with the VLP group even though there was no statistically significant difference. This is probably attributed to the relatively small sample size of these cohorts and that the majority of these ED visits were because of pain or wound checks. Although there was no significant difference in outcomes, the significantly longer operative time is an important point for surgeons to take into consideration when choosing treatment. Additionally, it is important to note that even though the mean LOS in both groups were not significantly different, they were elevated because of the inclusion of polytrauma patients in the cohorts. Overall, our findings suggest that FSF remains a safe option when indicated to stabilize complex fracture patterns. However, it may be prudent for surgeons to consider the hypothetical increased clinical burden of planned reoperation for ROH in the setting of FSF of certain patients.

Limitations

The primary limitations of this study are inherent because of its retrospective design and usage of an institutional database. We had access to the information documented within the chart and imaging included. Selection of fixation method was determined by individual surgeon preference which introduces potential selection and confounding biases. As discussed, differences in fracture patterns may influence grouping, as more complex fractures may require FSF constructs. Another possible limitation of our study may be the relative inexperience of our three medical student researchers classifying the fractures. This is also why we chose to forgo a more comprehensive analysis of radiographic measures. However, in regard to analysis of AO/OTA fracture classification, Dipnall et al18 found that there was no difference in inter-rate reliability across orthopedic training experience. A power analysis was not conducted prior to this study, which limits the precision of the results. A major limitation of this study is the lack of functional outcome measures. Our analysis relied solely on complication rates, ED visits, and readmissions, which does not fully capture the primary therapeutic goal of restoration of function after a distal radius fracture. Key functional measures, such as Disabilities of the Arm, Should, and Hand scores were not available in the medical records. As a result, comparisons based solely on adverse events provide an incomplete picture of patient recovery, and future studies are needed to compare functional and radiographic outcomes. Despite these limitations, our large sample size enhances the generalizability and strength of our findings.

Overall, FSF was associated with longer operative times, and increased rates of hardware removal compared with VLPs. Although FSF carries a higher procedural burden because of planned hardware removal, this does not correlate with elevated complication rates. Given the utility of FSF in complex DRFs, our findings support the use of FSF when indicated while acknowledging the additional procedural steps and follow-up interventions that may be required as part of the treatment course. Our study provides a large sample size and exclusively compares AO/OTA type C DRFs, providing a more comparable analysis.
