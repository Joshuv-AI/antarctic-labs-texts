Learnable Loss Geometries with Mirror Descent for Scalable and Convergent Meta-Learning
Yilang Zhang, Bingcong Li, Georgios B. Giannakis

Abstract
Utilizing task-invariant knowledge acquired from related tasks as prior information, meta-learning offers a principled approach to learning a new task with limited data records. Sample-efficient adaptation of this prior information is a major challenge facing meta-learning, and plays an important role because it facilitates training the sought task-specific model with just a few optimization steps. Past works deal with this challenge through preconditioning that speeds up convergence of the per-task training. Though effective in representing locally quadratic loss curvatures, simple linear preconditioning can be hardly potent with complex loss geometries. Instead of relying on a quadratic distance metric, the present contribution copes with complex loss metrics by learning a versatile distance-generating function, which induces a nonlinear mirror map to effectively capture and optimize a wide range of loss geometries. With suitable parameterization, this generating function is effected by an expressive neural network that is provably a valid distance. Analytical results establish convergence of not only the proposed method, but also all meta-learning approaches based on preconditioning. To attain gradient norm less than ϵ \epsilon , the convergence rate of 𝒪 ⁡ ( ϵ − 2 ) \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2}) is on par with standard gradient-based meta-learning methods. Numerical tests on few-shot learning datasets demonstrate the superior empirical performance of the novel algorithm, as well as its rapid per-task convergence, which markedly reduces the number of adaptation steps, hence also accommodating large-scale meta-learning models.



I
Introduction

The advent and advances of deep learning (DL) have led to documented success across a broad spectrum of fields, including computer vision
[
1
]
and nature language processing
[
2
]
. However, DL relies heavily on large-scale and high-capacity models, which necessitate extensive training using a vast number of labeled data. However, the data collection and annotation process in certain applications can be non-trivial and costly, requiring substantial human efforts and prohibitively high budget. As an example, the ResNet-50 neural network (NN) model, which is widely adopted in natural and medical image processing, contains over 23 million parameters
[
3
]
. In contrast, a medical image dataset such as BreastMNIST consists of merely 780 data
[
4
]
, given the imperative to uphold medical confidentiality by avoiding the disclosure of private patient information. Consequently, large models tend to overfit the given dataset, and lead to degraded generalization ability. This calls for a paradigm shift to reach the desired model capacity with manageable number of labeled data.

Interestingly, in comparison to DL models, humans can perform exceptionally well on complicated tasks such as object recognition or concept comprehension with minimal samples. For instance, a child can efficiently learn to recognize objects such as cars and planes after observing solely a couple of pictures
[
5
]
. How to integrate this data-efficient learning ability of humans into DL is both appealing and crucial, especially for applications with limited data records. Examples of such applications include drug molecule discovery
[
6
]
, minority language translation
[
7
]
, and catastrophic weather prediction
[
8
]
.

To cross-pollinate learning of humans to DL,
meta-learning
(aka
learning-to-learn
) has been recognized to offer a powerful framework for learning an unseen task from limited labeled data. Specifically, meta-learning seeks to accumulate
task-invariant prior
information from a collection of interrelated tasks, that can subsequently aid the learning of a novel, albeit related task. Although limited data may be available to learn this new sought task, the prior serves as a strong inductive bias that effectively transfers knowledge to aid its learning
[
9
]
. In image classification for instance, a feature extractor learned from a collection of given tasks can act as a common prior, thus benefiting a variety of other image classification tasks.

Depending on how this “data-limited learning” is performed per task, existing meta-learning algorithms can be categorized as either NN- or optimization-based ones. In the former, the learning of a task is modeled by an NN mapping from its training data to task-specific model parameters
[
10
,
5
,
11
]
. The prior information is encoded in the NN weights, which are shared and optimized across all tasks. With the effectiveness of NNs to approximate complex mappings granted, their black-box structure challenges their reliability and interpretability. In contrast, optimization-based meta-learning alternatives interpret “data-limited learning” as a cascade of a few optimization iterations (aka
adaptation
) over the model parameters. The prior here is captured by the shared hyperparameters of the iterative optimizer. A representative algorithm is model-agnostic meta-learning (MAML)
[
9
]
, which views the prior as a learnable task-invariant initialization of the optimizer. By starting from an informative initial point, the model parameters can rapidly converge to a stationary point within a few gradient descent (GD) steps. It has been pointed out that the initialization in MAML can be viewed as the mean of a multivariate Gaussian prior over the model parameters under a second-order Taylor approximation
[
12
]
. Building upon MAML, a series of variants have been proposed to learn different priors
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
.

While optimization-based meta-learning has been proven effective numerically, recent studies suggest that its stability heavily rely on the convergence of per-task optimization
[
13
,
18
]
. Hence, a straightforward improvement is to grow the number of descent iterations. However, this can be infeasible as the overall complexity of meta-learning scales linearly with the number of adaptation GD steps
[
13
]
. Moreover, using accelerated first-order optimizers, such as Nesterov’s algorithm
[
19
]
, introduces extra backpropagation complexity when optimizing the prior. To improve the per-task convergence without markedly adding to the computational overhead, another line of research focuses on second-order optimization using a learnable preconditioning matrix
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
. Essentially, this preconditioner captures the quadratic curvature of the training loss function, and linearly transforms the gradient based on the curvature. To acquire more expressive and generic forms of preconditioning, recent advances suggest replacing the linear matrix multiplication with a nonlinear NN transformation
[
25
]
, or, a singular value preconditioning
[
26
]
.

Unlike previous works that rely on preconditioning to model the prior of
quadratic loss curvature
, this contribution proposes to learn a distance metric that captures a
generic loss geometry
prior shared across tasks, thus accelerating the convergence of task adaptation. This generalization is effected by replacing the preconditioned GD (PGD) with the more generic mirror descent algorithm (MiDA)
[
27
]
. We thus term the resultant meta-learning algorithm MetaMiDA. All in all, our contribution is threefold.

C1.

An NN-based distance-generating function is developed to model generic loss geometries, with theoretical guarantees on the validity of the resultant mirror map.

C2.

Convergence analysis of not only MetaMiDA, but also all PGD-based meta-learning methods is established. The rate
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
matches GD-based meta-learning
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
, yet relying on fewer assumptions.

C3.

Extensive numerical tests showcase MetaMiDA’s improved empirical performance and accelerated adaptation, even with merely
one
optimization step.

This work extends our previous conference paper
[
31
]
in three key aspects. First, while the mirror map in
[
31
]
is restricted to have a triangular Jacobian, this contribution allows for more generic mirror maps by modeling alternatively the distance-generating function. Second, theoretical analysis is provided to guarantee the convergence of a broad family of meta-learning algorithms. Lastly, numerical experiments are vastly expanded to incorporate extensive datasets, large-scale models, complexity analysis, and more challenging setups such as cross-domain generalization.

Preliminaries

This section outlines the meta-learning formulation, followed by a recap of popular meta-learning approaches, and their limitations, especially that of scalability.

II-A

Problem setup

To enable learning a new task from limited data, meta-learning extracts task-invariant information from a collection of
T

\Pr(t=1)=\ldots=\Pr(t=T)={T}^{-1}

\{\mathbf{x}_{\star}^{n}\}_{n=1}^{N_{\star}^{\mathrm{tst}}}

\{y_{\star}^{n}\}_{n=1}^{N_{\star}^{\mathrm{tst}}}
are to be predicted.

The key premise of meta-learning is that the aforementioned
T

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
pertaining to each individual task

can be much smaller than
d

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}

\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{trn}}_{\star}

Letting

denote the parameter of the prior (aka meta-parameter), the meta-learning objective can be formulated as a bilevel optimization problem. The inner-level (task-level) trains each task-specific model by optimizing

and

provided by the outer-level (meta-level). The outer-level adjusts

by evaluating the optimized

on
\{\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{val}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
. The two levels depend on each other and yield the following nested bilevel objective

\displaystyle\;\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}}\ell^{\mathrm{trn}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t})+r(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t};\boldsymbol{\theta}),\;\;t=1,\ldots,T

III
Learning loss geometries with MetaMiDA

Instead of relying on quadratic approximations of the local loss induced by certain norms (e.g.,
\|\cdot\|_{2}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{P}}
), our fresh idea is to learn a data-driven distance metric that captures a broader spectrum of loss geometries. This will be accomplished by learning the so-termed distance generating function (DGF), which is introduced first. All the proofs in this section are delegated to the Appendices.

III-A

Distance metric reflecting loss geometry prior

Leveraging the idea of mirror descent
[
27
]
, one can replace
\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}^{k}\|_{2}^{2}
in (
5
) with a generic metric
D_{h}
to arrive at

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}^{k+1}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}}\text{lin}(\ell^{\mathrm{trn}}_{t},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}^{k})(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t})+\frac{1}{\alpha}D_{h}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}^{k}),\;\forall k

(6)

where
D_{h}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}^{k}):=h(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t})-\text{lin}(h(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}),\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}^{k})
is the Bregman divergence depicted in Figure
1
(b), and the associated DGF
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is strongly convex to ensure the existence and uniqueness of the minimizer. Then, applying the stationary point condition leads to the mirror descent update

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}^{k+1}=\nabla h^{*}\big(\nabla h(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}^{k})-\alpha\nabla\ell^{\mathrm{trn}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}^{k})\big),\;\forall k

(7)

where
h^{*}(\mathbf{z}):=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\top}\mathbf{z}-h(\boldsymbol{\phi})
is the Fenchel conjugate of
h
. Since
h
is strongly convex, it holds that
h^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is convex and Lipschitz-smooth
[
27
]
. As a special case, with

, it is easy to verify that (
7

5
) due to the self-duality of
\|\cdot\|_{2}
. Likewise, (
4
) can be recovered with
h(\cdot)=\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{P}}^{2}
, where
\nabla h
reduces to a linear mapping.

Function
h
reflects our prior knowledge about the geometry of
\ell^{\mathrm{trn}}_{t}
. Upon setting
h=\ell^{\mathrm{trn}}_{t}
(though
\ell^{\mathrm{trn}}_{t}
may not be strongly convex) in (

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}^{k+1}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}}\ell^{\mathrm{trn}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t})
, which recovers the original NLL minimization solved in (

h
would yield

(up to a constant) within a sufficiently large region around
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}^{k}
. If
h
is properly chosen for the geometry of the optimization landscape, mirror descent can outperform GD markedly in either convergence rate or constant factor

39
]

Different from past works with

h
(e.g.,
\|\cdot\|_{2}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbf{P}}
), we are here after a

h
that best fits the given tasks. Toward this end, we reformulate (

\mathbf{z}_{t}^{k+1}=\mathbf{z}_{t}^{k}-\alpha\nabla\ell^{\mathrm{trn}}_{t}(\nabla h^{*}(\mathbf{z}_{t}^{k})),\;\forall k

where the primal vector is recovered via
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}^{K}=\nabla h^{*}(\mathbf{z}_{t}^{K})
. It can be observed that the dual update (

, requiring merely the calculation of

and

\mathbf{z}_{t}^{0}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{z}:=\nabla h(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\phi}),\,\forall t
along with the conjugate

\|\cdot\|_{*}
and element-wise non-negative; and iii)
\{\mathbf{M}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{I}
are bounded wrt
\|\cdot\|_{*}
.

The term
\mathbf{M}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{z}
in (
9a
) implements a skip connection from the input to
i
-th layer, which improves NN expressiveness. The following remark demonstrates how to construct such an NN via proper parameterization.

Remark 1

(Valid NN parametrization)
.

The three conditions under Theorem
1
are mild, and they can be readily satisfied. For instance, choices of
\sigma
obeying i) include ELU
[
40
]
and Softplus
\sigma(\cdot)=\log(1+\exp(\cdot))
. In addition, conditions ii) and iii) can be attained via parameterization
\mathbf{W}_{i}:=\sigma_{W}(\check{\mathbf{W}}_{i})
and
\mathbf{M}_{i}:=\sigma_{M}(\check{\mathbf{M}}_{i})
, where
\sigma_{W}
is non-negative and bounded (e.g., sigmoid),
\sigma_{M}
is bounded (e.g.,
\tanh
), and parameter of
h^{*}
is defined as
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}:=\{\check{\mathbf{W}}_{i},\check{\mathbf{M}}_{i},\mathbf{b}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{I}
.

Using these choices, the next corollary asserts that the resultant NN has a universal Lipschitz-smoothness constant wrt
\mathbf{z}
regardless of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}
.

Corollary 2

(Universal Lipschitz-smoothness)
.

Suppose conditions in Theorem
1
hold, with weight bounds in conditions ii) and iii) fixed. For any
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}
, there exists a universal Lipschitz-smoothness constant for
h^{*}
.

In fact,
h^{*}
in Theorem
1
can be proved also Lipschitz-continuous wrt
\mathbf{z}
; see Appendix
A
. To enable modeling Lipschitz-smooth yet not Lipschitz-continuous functions, one can add an additional quadratic term to the output of the NN; that is,
h^{*}(\mathbf{z};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}):=\mathbf{a}_{I}(\mathbf{z};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{z}^{\top}\mathbf{P}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{P})\mathbf{z}
, where
\mathbf{P}\succeq 0
and
\|\mathbf{P}\|
is bounded, and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}
is augmented with
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{P}
. This not only satisfies the desired convexity and Lipschitz-smoothness requirements, but also enhances the expressiveness of
h^{*}
by explicitly incorporating the quadratic loss curvature model used in PGD.

III-C

Algorithm design with guaranteed convergence

Having modeled
h^{*}(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})
with an NN, here we develop the proposed approach dubbed meta-learning with MiDA (MetaMiDA). Utilizing the dual update (
8
) and
h^{*}
provided by Theorem
1
, our optimization objective is

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}}\;

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}\mathcal{L}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}):=\mathbb{E}_{t}\ell^{\mathrm{val}}_{t}(\nabla_{1}h^{*}(\mathbf{z}_{t}^{K}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h});\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}))

(10a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{t}^{0}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{z})=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{z},\;t=1,\ldots,T,

(10b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{t}^{k+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})=\mathbf{z}_{t}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})-

\displaystyle\quad\alpha\nabla\ell^{\mathrm{trn}}_{t}(\nabla_{1}h^{*}(\mathbf{z}_{t}^{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h});\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})),\,k=0,\ldots,K-1.

This can be solved using the standard alternating optimizer, where (
10a
) is optimized via mini-batch stochastic GD (SGD). The pseudo-codes of the resultant MetaMiDA approach are summarized in Algorithm

.

Next, we analyze the convergence of Algorithm
1
. While existing convergence results in meta-learning focus exclusively on GD-based approaches
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
, our approach here pertains to the broader family of MiDA-based methods, which also includes all the PGD-based ones. Comparing to (
2
), our objective (
10
) contains an extra parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}
, which thus complicates the analysis. As the optimization of (
10a
) is based on SGD, the analysis will apply Theorem
1
with
\|\cdot\|_{2}
norm. For notational simplicity, let
\ell^{\mathrm{set}}:=\mathbb{E}_{t}[\ell^{\mathrm{set}}_{t}]
denote the expected NLL with
\mathrm{set}\in\{\mathrm{trn},\mathrm{val}\}
, and meta-loss
\mathcal{L}:=\mathbb{E}_{t}\mathcal{L}_{t}
. Define
\boldsymbol{\theta}:=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}\}
,
h^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=h^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{z};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})
,
\nabla_{1}h^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\nabla_{1}h^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{z};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})
, and
\nabla_{2}h^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\nabla_{2}h^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{z};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})
. Likewise, define
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})
, and
\nabla_{1}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
respectively the partial derivatives wrt
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{z},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}
. Our analysis is built on top of the following three technical assumptions.

Assumption 1

(Loss functions)
.

For each
t=1,\ldots,T
and
\forall\boldsymbol{\phi},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, loss function
\ell^{\mathrm{set}}_{t},\,\mathrm{set}\in\{\mathrm{trn},\mathrm{val}\}
has

i) finite lower bound
\ell^{\mathrm{set}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\phi})>-\infty
;

ii) Lipschitz-continuous gradient
\|\nabla\ell^{\mathrm{set}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\phi})-\nabla\ell^{\mathrm{set}}_{t}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\|_{2}\allowbreak\leq G_{\ell}^{\mathrm{}}\|\boldsymbol{\phi}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\|_{2}
;

iii) Lipschitz-continuous Hessian
\|\nabla^{2}\ell^{\mathrm{set}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\phi})-\nabla^{2}\ell^{\mathrm{set}}_{t}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}})\|_{2}\leq H_{\ell}^{\mathrm{}}\|\boldsymbol{\phi}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\|_{2}
; and

iv) Lipschitz-smooth composition
\|\nabla(\ell^{\mathrm{val}}_{t}\circ\nabla_{1}h^{*})(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\nabla(\ell^{\mathrm{val}}_{t}\circ\nabla_{1}h^{*})(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2}\leq G_{\ell^{\mathrm{}}h}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2},\;\forall\boldsymbol{\theta},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
.

Assumption 2

(Bounded variance)
.

For each
t=1,\ldots,T
and
\forall\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it holds that
\mathbb{E}_{t}\|\nabla\ell^{\mathrm{val}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\phi})-\nabla\ell^{\mathrm{val}}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}
.

Assumption 3

(Inverse mirror map)
.

For
\forall\boldsymbol{\theta},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
,
h^{*}
has Lipschitz-continuous

i) partial gradient
\|\nabla_{1}h^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\nabla_{1}h^{*}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2}\leq G_{h}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}
; and

ii) mixed Jacobian
\|\nabla\nabla_{1}h^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\nabla\nabla_{1}h^{*}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2}\leq H_{h}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}
.

Remark 2

(Mild assumptions)
.

Assumptions
1
-
3
are mild and common in not only meta-learning
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
but also generic bilevel optimization
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
. Assumptions
1
and
3
merely assume Lipschitz-continuity of the gradients and Hessians of
\ell^{\mathrm{set}}_{t}
and
h^{*}
, without any premise on their own Lipschitz-continuity or the convexity of
\ell^{\mathrm{set}}_{t}
. It is also noteworthy that the Lipschitz-continuity of
\nabla_{1}h^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
wrt
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{z}
has already been proved in Corollary
2
. As
\|\nabla_{1}h^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\nabla_{1}h^{*}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2}\leq\|\nabla_{1}h^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\nabla_{1}h^{*}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{z};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})\|_{2}+\|\nabla_{1}h^{*}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{z};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})-\nabla_{1}h^{*}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2}
, a sufficient condition for Assumption
3
.i) to hold is the Lipschitz-continuity of
\nabla_{1}h^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
wrt
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}
, which is also mild. Assumption
2
is standard in stochastic optimization for analyzing convergence of SGD.

The key challenge in the convergence analysis is the unbounded smoothness of
\mathcal{L}
. This property is characterized in the next proposition.

Proposition 3

(Unbounded smoothness of meta-loss)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
3
hold. Define constants
\gamma:=1+\alpha G_{h}G_{\ell}^{\mathrm{}}
,
C_{G,1}:=\gamma^{K}(\gamma^{K}-1)(G_{h}\frac{H_{\ell}^{\mathrm{}}}{G_{\ell}^{\mathrm{}}}+\frac{H_{h}}{G_{h}})
,
C_{G,2}:=\gamma^{K-1}[(\gamma-1)(G_{h}\frac{H_{\ell}^{\mathrm{}}}{G_{\ell}^{\mathrm{}}}+\frac{H_{h}}{G_{h}})K+(\gamma^{K}-1-\alpha KG_{h}G_{\ell}^{\mathrm{}})(G_{h}\frac{H_{\ell}^{\mathrm{}}}{G_{\ell}^{\mathrm{}}}+\frac{H_{h}}{G_{h}})]
, and vector
\mathbf{g}_{t}^{K}:=\nabla_{1}(\ell^{\mathrm{val}}_{t}\circ\nabla_{1}h^{*})(\mathbf{z}_{t}^{K};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})
. It holds that

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{1}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\nabla_{1}\mathcal{L}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq G_{\mathcal{L},1}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2},

(11a)

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq G_{\mathcal{L},2}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{2}

(11b)

where
G_{\mathcal{L},j}:=C_{G,j}\mathbb{E}_{t}\|\mathbf{g}_{t}^{K}\|_{2}+\gamma^{2K}G_{\ell^{\mathrm{}}h},\;j=1,2
.

Proposition
3
points out that the smoothness constant
G_{\mathcal{L},j}
grows linearly with
\mathbb{E}_{t}\|\mathbf{g}_{t}^{K}\|_{2}
, and thus it can be unbounded. The standard remedy in SGD is to estimate
G_{\mathcal{L},j}
, and adjust
(\beta_{1}^{r},\beta_{2}^{r})
on the fly. This estimation relies on a mini-batch
\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{r}\subset\{1,2,\ldots,T\}
of cardinality
\hat{B}
that is sampled independently of
\mathcal{B}^{r}
, to yield the estimator

\hat{G}_{\mathcal{L},j}^{r}:=\frac{C_{G,j}}{\hat{B}}\sum_{t\in\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{r}}\|\mathbf{g}_{t}^{K}\|_{2}+\gamma^{2K}G_{\ell^{\mathrm{}}h},\;j=1,2.

(12)

It can be inferred from the definition (
12
) that
\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{r}}\hat{G}_{\mathcal{L},j}^{r}=G_{\mathcal{L},j}
is unbiased. Next, we analyze the complexity of this estimator, and compare it with existing approaches.

Remark 3

(Complexity of smoothness estimator)
.

Estimator (
12
) requires sampling an additional batch of tasks. To compute
\mathbf{g}_{t}^{K}
for each
t\in\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{r}
, one needs to acquire
\mathbf{z}_{t}^{K}
via
K
mirror descent steps. Fortunately, this will not increase time complexity since the mirror descent (
10b
) is computed
in parallel
across tasks in
\mathcal{B}^{r}\cup\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{r}
. Moreover, it is seen from lines 4-12 of Algorithm
1
that the computation of meta-gradient
\nabla\mathcal{L}=[\nabla_{1}\mathcal{L}^{\top},\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}^{\top}]^{\top}
relies solely on
\mathcal{B}^{r}
. Hence,
\mathbf{z}_{t}^{k}
can be updated
in place
for
t\in\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{r}
, without creating computation graphs and storing second-order information for backpropagation. Additionally, it will be shown soon in Corollary
7
that
\hat{B}\ll B
. As a result, this estimator only contributes to a marginal increase (
<2\%
in our numerical tests) of the overall space complexity.

Remark 4

(Comparison with existing methods)
.

The smoothness estimator (
12
) distinguishes this paper from current works by relying on
fewer assumptions
and providing
tighter bounds
.
Existing smoothness estimators
[
28
,
29
]
are linear functions of
\|\nabla\ell^{\mathrm{val}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}^{0})\|_{2}
instead of
\|\mathbf{g}_{t}^{K}\|_{2}
. Although these estimators can be computed at the initialization
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}^{0}
, they rely on the extra assumption that
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{trn}}_{t}
is similar enough to
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{val}}_{t}
so
\|\nabla\ell^{\mathrm{trn}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\phi})-\nabla\ell^{\mathrm{val}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\|_{2}
is bounded for
\forall t
and
\forall\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
To confirm the tightness of the proposed estimator, consider
\alpha=0
or
K=0
so that
\mathcal{L}=\ell^{\mathrm{val}}\circ h
has constant smoothness
G_{\ell^{\mathrm{}}h}
. In this case, taking
\alpha=0
or
K=0
in (
12
) yields coefficient
C_{G,j}=0
, and thus our estimator
\hat{G}_{\mathcal{L},j}=G_{\ell^{\mathrm{}}h}
is tight. With the same setup, the coefficient of
\|\nabla\ell^{\mathrm{val}}_{t}(\mathbf{z}_{t}^{0})\|_{2}
are yet greater than
0
; see e.g.,
[
29
, Proposition 7]
, hence resulting in a loose and even unbounded smoothness estimator.
Similar to
[
28
,
29
]
, calculation of (
12
) requires Lipschitz constants in Assumptions
1
and
3
, which are hard to estimate especially for large NNs. In practice, these two scalars can be viewed as hyperparameters and are obtained through grid search. As an alternative, if
\ell^{\mathrm{trn}}_{t}
is further assumed Lipschitz-continuous,
\hat{G}_{\mathcal{L},j}^{r}
will boil down to a constant, thus enabling a constant learning rate
[
30
]
.

An ideal choice is
\beta_{j}^{r}=1/G_{\mathcal{L},j}^{r}
, which is unfortunately infeasible to compute.
Intuitively, if
\hat{B}
is moderately large, (
12
) could provide an accurate estimation of
G_{\mathcal{L},j}^{r}
, and one can thus set
\beta_{j}^{r}\propto 1/\hat{G}_{\mathcal{L},j}^{r}
. The following proposition bounds the first- and second-order moments of this choice.

Proposition 4

(Moments of learning rate estimator)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
-
3
hold, and define
\zeta:=2\alpha+\frac{(\gamma^{K}-\gamma)(\sqrt{T}+1)}{G_{h}G_{\ell^{\mathrm{}}}}
. If
\hat{B}\geq\max_{j=1,2}\frac{2C_{G,j}^{2}[G_{\ell^{\mathrm{}}h}\zeta+G_{h}(1+\sqrt{T})]^{2}}{\gamma^{4K}G_{\ell^{\mathrm{}}h}^{2}\sqrt{T}}\sigma^{2}
, then

\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{r}}\bigg[\frac{1}{\hat{G}_{\mathcal{L},j}^{r}}\bigg]\geq\frac{1}{G_{\mathcal{L},j}},~\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{r}}\bigg[\frac{1}{(\hat{G}_{\mathcal{L},j}^{r})^{2}}\bigg]\leq\frac{\sqrt{T}+1}{G_{\mathcal{L},j}^{2}}.

It is worth noting that while
T
can be large (e.g.,
T=8,000

[
15
]
), its square root is relatively small (
\sqrt{8000}\approx 89
). Next, we account for the stochasticity of SGD by bounding the second-order moment of the meta-gradient norm.

Proposition 5

(Second-order moment of meta-gradient)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
-
3
hold, and let
C_{\mathcal{L},1}:=\frac{\gamma^{K}}{2-\gamma^{K}}
,
C_{\mathcal{L},2}:=G_{\ell^{\mathrm{}}h}\zeta+G_{h}\sqrt{T}
. If
\alpha<\frac{2^{1/K}-1}{G_{h}G_{\ell}^{\mathrm{}}}
, then it holds for any given
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla_{1}\mathcal{L}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathcal{L},1}\|\nabla_{1}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{2}+C_{\mathcal{L},1}C_{\mathcal{L},2}\sigma,

(13a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq(C_{\mathcal{L},1}-1)\|\nabla_{1}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{2}+

\displaystyle\qquad C_{\mathcal{L},1}C_{\mathcal{L},2}\sigma+\|\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|_{2}.

(13b)

Building upon Propositions
3
-
5
, we now establish the convergence of our MetaMiDA approach.

Theorem 6

(Convergence of MetaMiDA)
.

Consider Algorithm
1
with meta-learning rate
\beta_{j}^{r}=\frac{1}{C_{\beta}\hat{G}_{\mathcal{L},j}^{r}},\;j=1,2
, where
C_{\beta}>\frac{\sqrt{T}+1}{2}
. Suppose that Assumptions
1
-
3
, and the conditions on
\hat{B}
and
\alpha
in Propositions
4
and
5
hold. Define constants
C_{B,1}:=\frac{2C_{\beta}}{\sqrt{T}+1}
and
C_{B,2}:=2C_{\mathcal{L},1}^{2}-1+3(C_{\mathcal{L},1}-1)^{2}\max\{\frac{C_{G,1}}{C_{G,2}},1\}
. If
B\geq\frac{\max\{C_{B,2},2\}}{C_{B,1}-1}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{1}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\rho})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\eta_{1}}\Big(\frac{\Delta}{R}+\frac{\eta_{5}}{B}\Big)+

\displaystyle\qquad\sqrt{\frac{1}{4\eta_{1}^{2}}\Big(\frac{\Delta}{R}+\frac{\eta_{5}}{B}\Big)^{2}+\frac{\eta_{2}}{\eta_{1}}\Big(\frac{\Delta}{R}+\frac{\eta_{5}}{B}\Big)},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\rho})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{\eta_{3}}\big(\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{1}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\rho})\|_{2}+\eta_{4}\big)\Big(\frac{\Delta}{R}+\frac{\eta_{5}}{B}\Big)}.

where
\rho\in\{0,\ldots,R-1\}
is a discrete uniform random variable,
\Delta:=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{0})-\inf_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]
, and
\{\eta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{5}
are defined in (
E
).

By specifying the values of
\alpha
and
C_{\beta}
, the following corollary provides a simplified version of Theorem
6
.

Corollary 7

(Convergence rate)
.

Consider Theorem
6
with
\alpha=\frac{(1+T^{-1/2})^{1/K}-1}{G_{h}G_{\ell^{\mathrm{}}}}
and
C_{\beta}=\sqrt{T}
. If batch sizes satisfy
B=\Omega(\sigma^{2}\epsilon^{-2})
and
\hat{B}=\Omega(\sigma^{2})
, it takes
R=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
iterations to reach an
\epsilon
-stationary point; that is,
\mathbb{E}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\rho})\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
.

Although MetaMiDA relies on more complicated task-level updates (
10b
) and fewer assumptions, our analysis still guarantees the same convergence rate as for GD-based meta-learning
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
.

IV-C

Cross-domain generalization

We next assess MetaMiDA’s performance in a more challenging and practical scenario, dubbed cross-domain few-shot learning, in which meta-training and meta-testing tasks are from two different yet related domains. By deliberately increasing the domain gap, this test measures the overfitting of the learned prior to a specific domain (aka, meta-overfitting). In practice, such a behavior can be harmful in rapidly changing environments, preventing generalization of the learned prior to unseen fields.

The test setup follows from
[
53
]
. Specifically, the prior is meta-trained on miniImageNet with the
4
-block CNN, and meta-tested on tieredImageNet, CUB, and Cars datasets. In addition,

during the meta-training phase, and

in meta-testing to improve adaptation to new tasks from unseen domains. Table

lists the performance of GD-based, PGD-based, and our MiDA-based approaches. It is seen that MetaMiDA shows superior accuracies on all three datasets. The performance of MetaMiDA is also comparable to the computationally intensive GAP
[
26
]
that relies on SVD. This highlights MetaMiDA’s remarkable generalization ability despite the large domain gap. Essentially, the loss geometries are related to not only the task data distributions, but also the model structure shared across tasks. Although the domain gap could cause a significant data distribution shift, the learning model remains unchanged across different domains. As a result, domain-generic knowledge can be still captured by the learned loss geometries prior, thus facilitating the learning of cross-domain tasks.

IV-D

Enhanced performance via accelerated adaptation

Having confirmed the empirical performance gain of MetaMiDA on popular few-shot classification datasets, the next test analyzes the underlying reason for its superiority. Concluding remarks

This contribution has established that the task-level optimization of meta-learning can be enhanced through a versatile loss geometry prior, which captures and optimizes a wide spectrum of loss functions. The proposed MetaMiDA approach generalizes existing PGD-based meta-learning approaches, and offers provable convergence guarantees. Extensive numerical tests on various benchmark datasets illustrate MetaMiDA’s superiority in few-shot learning for data-limited applications, cross-domain generalization for rapidly changing environments, and accelerated adaptation with enhanced scalability.

Appendix A
Proof of Theorem
1
and Corollary
2

Proof.

The proof of Theorem
1
is inspired by
[
55
]
which studies the convexity of a multi-layer NN, while our analysis further establishes the Lipschitz-smoothness by introducing several additional mild constraints. For notational compactness, denote by
L_{\sigma}
and
G_{\sigma}
respectively the Lipschitz-continuity and smoothness constants of
\sigma
, and
\|\mathbf{W}_{i}\|_{*}\leq W,\;\forall i
and
\|\mathbf{M}_{i}\|_{*}\leq M,\;\forall i
the bounds of NN weights across all layers.

Theorem
1
and Corollary
2
can be established via mathematical induction on the total number
I
of NN layers. First, consider the base case where
I=1
. According to (
9
), the NN simplifies to
h^{*}(\mathbf{z};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})=\mathbf{a}_{1}=\sigma\big((\mathbf{W}_{1}+\mathbf{M}_{1})^{\top}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{b}_{1}\big)
, where
\mathbf{W}_{1},\mathbf{M}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
reduces to column vectors. Since
\sigma
is convex and non-decreasing, and pre-activation
f_{1}(\mathbf{z}):=(\mathbf{W}_{1}+\mathbf{M}_{1})^{\top}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{b}_{1}
is linear thus convex, their composition
h^{*}=\sigma\circ f_{1}
is thereby convex. Furthermore, it follows that

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{1}h^{*}(\mathbf{z};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})-\nabla_{1}h^{*}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})\|_{*}

\displaystyle=|\sigma^{\prime}\big(f_{1}(\mathbf{z})\big)-\sigma^{\prime}\big(f_{1}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}})\big)|\|\mathbf{W}_{1}+\mathbf{M}_{1}\|_{*}

\displaystyle\leq G_{\sigma}|f_{1}(\mathbf{z})-f_{1}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}})|\|\mathbf{W}_{1}+\mathbf{M}_{1}\|_{*}

\displaystyle=G_{\sigma}|(\mathbf{W}_{1}+\mathbf{M}_{1})(\mathbf{z}-\tilde{\mathbf{z}})|\|\mathbf{W}_{1}+\mathbf{M}_{1}\|_{*}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}G_{\sigma}\|\mathbf{W}_{1}+\mathbf{M}_{1}\|_{*}^{2}\|\mathbf{z}-\tilde{\mathbf{z}}\|

\displaystyle\leq G_{\sigma}(W+M)^{2}\|\mathbf{z}-\tilde{\mathbf{z}}\|

where
(a)
uses Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. In addition to Lipschitz-smoothness,
h^{*}
is also Lipschitz-continuous via

\displaystyle|h^{*}(\mathbf{z};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})-h^{*}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})|

\displaystyle=|\sigma\big(f_{1}(\mathbf{z})\big)-\sigma\big(f_{1}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}})\big)|

\displaystyle\leq L_{\sigma}|f_{1}(\mathbf{z})-f_{1}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}})|

\displaystyle=L_{\sigma}|(\mathbf{W}_{1}+\mathbf{M}_{1})(\mathbf{z}-\tilde{\mathbf{z}})|

\displaystyle\leq L_{\sigma}\|\mathbf{W}_{1}+\mathbf{M}_{1}\|_{*}\|\mathbf{z}-\tilde{\mathbf{z}}\|

\displaystyle\leq L_{\sigma}(W+M)\|\mathbf{z}-\tilde{\mathbf{z}}\|.

It is worth noting that the continuity and smoothness constants
L_{\sigma}(W+M)
and
G_{\sigma}(W+M)^{2}
neither rely on
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}
.

Now assume with any
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}
satisfying Theorem
1
,
h^{*}
is convex, Lipschitz-continuous, and Lipschitz-smooth wrt
\mathbf{z}
for
I=1,\ldots,I_{0}
. We next show that the same properties also hold for
I=I_{0}+1
. For the case
I=I_{0}+1
, (
9
) implies
h^{*}(\mathbf{z};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})=\mathbf{a}_{I_{0}+1}=\sigma(\mathbf{W}_{I_{0}+1}^{\top}\mathbf{a}_{I_{0}}+\mathbf{M}_{I_{0}+1}^{\top}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{b}_{I_{0}})
, where
\mathbf{W}_{I_{0}+1},\mathbf{M}_{I_{0}+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
boils down to vectors. As each entry
[\mathbf{a}_{I_{0}}]_{j},\forall j
is an
I_{0}
-layer NN defined by (
9
), the inductive hypothesis implies
[\mathbf{a}_{I_{0}}]_{j}
is convex, Lipschitz-continuous, and Lipschitz-smooth wrt
\mathbf{z}
regardless of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h}
. For later use, let
L_{h,I_{0}}
and
G_{h,I_{0}}
be the maximum Lipschitz-continuity and smoothness constants for
[\mathbf{a}_{I_{0}}]_{j}
across the index
j
. Notice that since
\mathbf{W}_{I_{0}+1}
is element-wise non-negative,
\mathbf{W}_{I_{0}+1}^{\top}\mathbf{a}_{I_{0}}
is a conical combination of functions convex in
\mathbf{z}
, and is thus also convex in
\mathbf{z}
. Then, it is straightforward to see that the pre-activation
f_{I_{0}+1}(\mathbf{z}):=\mathbf{W}_{I_{0}+1}^{\top}\mathbf{a}_{I_{0}}+\mathbf{M}_{I_{0}+1}^{\top}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{b}_{I_{0}}
is convex. Hence, the composition
h^{*}=\sigma\circ f_{I_{0}+1}
is convex.

Next, we conclude the induction by proving that
h^{*}
is Lipschitz-continuous and smooth with
I=I_{0}+1
. For
\forall\mathbf{z},\tilde{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{i}
be the hidden neurons of the
i
-th layer when
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}
serves as the input of
h^{*}
. On one hand,

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{1}h^{*}(\mathbf{z};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})-\nabla_{1}h^{*}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})\|_{*}

\displaystyle=\|\sigma^{\prime}(f_{I_{0}+1}(\mathbf{z}))\big(\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\mathbf{a}_{I_{0}}\mathbf{W}_{I_{0}+1}+\mathbf{M}_{I_{0}+1}\big)-

\displaystyle\qquad\sigma^{\prime}(f_{I_{0}+1}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}))\big(\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{I_{0}}\mathbf{W}_{I_{0}+1}+\mathbf{M}_{I_{0}+1}\big)\|_{*}

\displaystyle\leq\|\sigma^{\prime}(f_{I_{0}+1}(\mathbf{z}))\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\mathbf{a}_{I_{0}}-\sigma^{\prime}\big(f_{I_{0}+1}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}})\big)\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{I_{0}}\|_{*}\|\mathbf{W}_{I_{0}+1}\|_{*}+

\displaystyle\quad\;\,\|\sigma^{\prime}(f_{I_{0}+1}(\mathbf{z}))-\sigma^{\prime}\big(f_{I_{0}+1}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}})\big)\|_{*}\|\mathbf{M}_{I_{0}+1}\|_{*}

\displaystyle\leq W|\sigma^{\prime}(f_{I_{0}+1}(\mathbf{z}))|\times\|\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\mathbf{a}_{I_{0}}-\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{I_{0}}\|_{*}+

\displaystyle\quad\;\,W|\sigma^{\prime}(f_{I_{0}+1}(\mathbf{z}))-\sigma^{\prime}(f_{I_{0}+1}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}))|\times\|\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{I_{0}}\|+

\displaystyle\quad\;\,MG_{\sigma}|f_{I_{0}+1}(\mathbf{z})-f_{I_{0}+1}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}})|.

(14)

\displaystyle=|\mathbf{W}_{I_{0}+1}^{\top}(\mathbf{a}_{I_{0}}-\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{I_{0}})+\mathbf{M}_{I_{0}+1}^{\top}(\mathbf{z}-\tilde{\mathbf{z}})|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{W}_{I_{0}+1}\|_{*}\|\mathbf{a}_{I_{0}}-\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{I_{0}}\|+\|\mathbf{M}_{I_{0}+1}\|_{*}\|\mathbf{z}-\tilde{\mathbf{z}}\|_{2}

(15)

) renders the Lipschitz-smoothness

where

.
On the other hand, the Lipschitz-continuity holds via

\displaystyle|h^{*}(\mathbf{z};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})-h^{*}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{h})|

\displaystyle=|\sigma(f_{I_{0}+1}(\mathbf{z}))-\sigma(f_{I_{0}+1}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}))|

\displaystyle\leq L_{\sigma}|f_{I_{0}+1}(\mathbf{z})-f_{I_{0}+1}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}})|

\displaystyle:=L_{h,I_{0}+1}\|\mathbf{z}-\tilde{\mathbf{z}}\|

where
(a)

).

Finally, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{x_{2}+c}\left[\begin{matrix}1&-\frac{x_{1}}{x_{2}+c}\\
-\frac{x_{1}}{x_{2}+c}&\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{(x_{2}+c)^{2}}\end{matrix}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{x_{2}+c}\left[\begin{matrix}1&-\frac{x_{1}}{x_{2}+c}\end{matrix}\right]\left[\begin{matrix}1\\
-\frac{x_{1}}{x_{2}+c}\end{matrix}\right]

which has eigenvalues
\frac{2}{x_{2}+c}[1+\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{(x_{2}+c)^{2}}]>0
and
0
. As a result,
f
is convex and thus applying Jensen’s inequality on (

) results in

\frac{\eta_{3}\mathbb{E}^{2}\|\nabla_{2}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\rho})\|_{2}}{\mathbb{E}\|\nabla_{1}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\rho})\|_{2}+\eta_{4}}\leq\frac{\Delta}{R}+\frac{\eta_{5}}{B}.

Lemma 8
.

For random variables

It follows that
