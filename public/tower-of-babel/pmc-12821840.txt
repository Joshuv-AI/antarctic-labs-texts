Risk factors for proximal contact loss after implant-supported prosthetic restoration in adjacent natural teeth
Authors: Lei Liu, Yue Jiang, Hui Xu, Yu Zheng, Xiao-Jun Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProximal contact loss (PCL) between implant-supported restorations and adjacent natural teeth is a frequent clinical complication, potentially leading to food impaction, caries, and periodontal issues. However, the risk factors contributing to PCL remain incompletely understood.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included patients who received single implant-supported crowns adjacent to natural teeth between June 2015 and June 2025. Eligible cases met stringent inclusion criteria based on radiographic, clinical, and periodontal assessments. PCL was diagnosed using floss resistance and radiographic separation tests, and quantified via calibrated shim stock measurements. Data on demographic, prosthetic, anatomical, and periodontal variables were extracted. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were used to identify independent predictors of PCL.ResultsA total of 206 patients (395 contact points) were included. The incidence of PCL was 67.5% at the patient level and 40.3% at the contact-point level. Multivariate analysis revealed that clinical food impaction (HR 2.75; 95% CI, 1.70–4.58; P < 0.001), residual ridge height discrepancy (HR 2.51; 95% CI, 1.37–4.58; P = 0.003), and advanced alveolar bone loss in adjacent teeth (HR 5.83; 95% CI, 1.05–32.33; P = 0.044) were independently associated with PCL.ConclusionsPCL is a prevalent complication following implant-supported prosthetic restoration. Local anatomical and prosthetic factors—particularly food impaction, ridge discrepancy, and bone loss—are key predictors. Proactive identification and management of these risk factors may help preserve interproximal contact integrity over time.Keywords: Proximal contact loss (PCL), Implant-Supported restoration, Dental implants, Food impaction, Alveolar bone loss, Residual ridge discrepancy, Cox regression

Introduction

In recent decades, implant-supported prosthetic restoration has emerged as a predictable modality for the replacement of missing teeth, yielding high long‐term survival rates and patient satisfaction [1]. Despite these favorable outcomes, complications involving the interface between restorations and adjacent natural dentition continue to challenge clinical success [2]. Proximal contact loss (PCL), a condition characterized by the formation of an interproximal space between an implant‐supported crown and its neighboring natural tooth, has been reported in up to 50% of cases within five years after prosthesis placement [3, 4]. This phenomenon predisposes patients to food impaction, secondary caries, periodontitis, and marginal bone loss around both implant and natural tooth, ultimately compromising peri‐implant tissue health and prosthesis longevity [5]. Proximal contact integrity depends upon a complex interplay of prosthetic design, occlusal dynamics, and biologic adaptation of the surrounding periodontal ligament [6]. In natural dentition, continuous eruption and mesial drift maintain interproximal relationships; by contrast, osseointegrated implants lack periodontal proprioception and remodeling capacity, rendering prosthetic contacts susceptible to spontaneous opening [7]. Physiologic tooth movement is a lifelong process, occurring at an estimated rate of 20–30 μm per year in the mesial direction, and is driven by occlusal forces, periodontal ligament remodeling, and age-related migration [8]. Even such minute displacement can disrupt contact equilibrium when an ankylosed implant restoration is positioned adjacent to a mobile natural tooth, predisposing the site to gradual contact separation [9]. Factors implicated in contact loss include crown emergence profile, axial inclination of the implant, choice of restorative material, occlusal loading patterns, and timing of crown insertion relative to adjacent tooth movement [10, 11]. In addition, patient‐related variables such as age‐related tooth migration, parafunctional habits, and inconsistent oral hygiene practices may exacerbate contact deterioration over time.

Previous investigations have primarily addressed the prevalence and clinical sequelae of PCL, with fewer studies systematically evaluating potential risk indicators. A retrospective cohort study involving single-unit implants demonstrated that distal contacts adjacent to second molars were more prone to opening than mesial contacts near incisors, suggesting an anatomic influence of arch curvature and occlusal forces [12, 13]. Moreover, inter‐implant distance and angulation have been correlated with contact stability, underscoring the importance of precise three‐dimensional implant placement. However, existing evidence remains fragmented, often limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous prosthetic protocols, and short follow‐up intervals. To optimize clinical outcomes and inform evidence‐based treatment planning, comprehensive identification of modifiable and non‐modifiable risk factors for PCL is imperative. A deeper understanding of the relative contributions of prosthetic design parameters, such as crown contour, contact point location, and proximal emergence angles, as well as patient‐specific variables, including periodontal biotype, occlusal scheme, and interdental spacing, will facilitate targeted interventions [14, 15].

The present study aimed to investigate the incidence and risk factors associated with PCL between implant-supported prosthetic restorations, including single crowns, splinted crowns, and fixed bridges, and adjacent natural teeth. Using a retrospective cohort design and time-to-event analysis with Cox proportional hazards modeling, we comprehensively evaluated demographic, behavioral, prosthetic, occlusal, and local anatomic determinants of PCL. Particular attention was given to food impaction, residual ridge height discrepancy, and alveolar bone support as potentially modifiable clinical contributors to interproximal contact instability.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study enrolled patients who underwent implant‑supported prosthetic restoration at our institution between June 2015 and June 2025. Patients were eligible if they were ≥ 18 years of age at the time of prosthesis insertion; had received a single implant‑supported crown adjacent to at least one natural tooth, with definitive restoration placed; demonstrated osseointegration confirmed by primary stability at placement (insertion torque ≥ 35 N·cm) and absence of implant mobility at prosthesis delivery; and had high‑quality periapical radiographs and intraoral photographs available at baseline. Adjacent natural teeth were required to exhibit intact proximal contacts and periodontal health (probing depth ≤ 3 mm, no radiographic alveolar bone loss beyond physiologic levels). Complete clinical records, including implant position, abutment type, crown material, occlusal scheme, and patient demographics, were mandatory. Exclusion criteria comprised a history of moderate to severe periodontitis (Stage III/IV) or active periodontal therapy within six months prior to implant placement; adjacent tooth mobility (Grade II or greater) or interdental bone defects at baseline; systemic conditions or medications affecting bone metabolism or soft tissue healing; and current heavy smoking (≥ 10 cigarettes per day) or tobacco cessation within 12 months before prosthesis delivery. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians. The study was approved by the hospital’s ethics committee and conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. Data confidentiality was ensured, with all personal identifiers removed prior to analysis to protect participant privacy.

Diagnostic criteria for proximal contact loss

The diagnosis of PCL between an implant‑supported restoration and its adjacent natural tooth is most commonly based on the following consensus criteria:

Floss Resistance Test: A standard 25‑µm waxed dental floss or 8‑µm shim‑stock metal strip is inserted interproximally under gentle pressure. PCL is confirmed when the floss or shim‑stock passes through the contact point without perceptible resistance, or with an audible “snap,” in contrast to a firm, resistant engagement at baseline.

Floss Resistance Test: A standard 25‑µm waxed dental floss or 8‑µm shim‑stock metal strip is inserted interproximally under gentle pressure. PCL is confirmed when the floss or shim‑stock passes through the contact point without perceptible resistance, or with an audible “snap,” in contrast to a firm, resistant engagement at baseline.

Radiographic Separation Measurement: Comparison of standardized periapical radiographs taken at prosthesis delivery and at follow‑up, with measurement of interproximal space at the mid‑contact point. An increase of ≥ 0.2 mm in horizontal separation confirms PCL.

Quantification of proximal contact loss and alveolar bone support

PCL was quantified by sequential insertion of 50 μm–thick metal shim stock strips into the interproximal gap between the implant-supported crown and its adjacent natural tooth. Strips were advanced until light resistance was first encountered; the maximum number of shim stock pieces fully seated without resistance was recorded and multiplied by 50 μm to determine the PCL gap width. Bone support of the adjacent natural tooth was evaluated using standardized periapical radiographs obtained at each follow-up. It was defined as the ratio of alveolar bone height to total root length, calculated by dividing the distance from the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) to the alveolar crest by the distance from the CEJ to the root apex. This radiographic ratio reflects the extent of alveolar bone loss due to previous periodontal disease and was used to assess longitudinal changes in periodontal bone support adjacent to sites exhibiting PCL.

Data collection

Data for this study were obtained retrospectively from patient charts and radiographic archives and encompassed demographic, behavioral, clinical, prosthetic, occlusal, and periodontal parameters. Demographic and behavioral data included patient sex, age at implant placement, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and presence of bruxism, diabetes mellitus, or hypertension. Clinical and implant‑related variables comprised duration of edentulism before restoration, implant site (tooth position and arch), free‑end versus bounded span, adjunctive surgical procedures (e.g., bone grafting or sinus lift), abutment type, crown material, and pulp vitality of adjacent teeth. Occlusal and prosthetic measures included occlusal scheme, presence of food impaction, contact point location, interproximal gap width (measured using 50 μm shim stock), residual ridge height discrepancy at the contact region, and wear facets on the opposing dentition. Periodontal support of the natural adjacent tooth was assessed on standardized periapical radiographs by calculating the ratio of cementoenamel junction–crest to cementoenamel junction–apex distance and recording interproximal probing depths.

A plunger cusp was defined as a cusp tip on an opposing or adjacent tooth that projects or occludes into the interproximal embrasure during function, exerting compressive forces on the marginal ridge area and potentially leading to food impaction or proximal contact loss. Residual ridge height discrepancy was defined as a vertical difference greater than 1 mm between the alveolar crest level at the implant platform and that at the adjacent natural tooth on standardized periapical radiographs. This measurement reflects the discrepancy in residual ridge height between the implant and the neighboring tooth.

All measurements and data extraction were initially performed by the first investigator, a prosthodontist with over 10 years of clinical and radiographic experience, to ensure standardized identification of proximal contact loss and related parameters. A second examiner independently reviewed 20% of cases to confirm consistency, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus with a third investigator.

For statistical purposes, each contact point, defined as the interproximal interface between an implant-supported crown and its directly adjacent natural tooth, was treated as an independent observational unit (“contact-point level”). When a patient had more than one implant site, each contact point was analyzed separately.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical data are presented as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons for time‑to‑event outcomes were performed by univariate log‑rank tests; variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis were subsequently entered into a multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model to identify independent predictors of PCL.

Results

Patient characteristics and proximal contact loss incidence

A total of 206 patients were enrolled, including 86 males and 120 females, with ages ranging from 21 to 83 years (mean, 51 years). A total of 206 patients had 395 adjacent interproximal contact points in total. PCL occurred in 139 patients, yielding a patient‑level incidence of 67.5%. At the contact‑point level, 159 of 395 sites exhibited PCL, corresponding to an incidence of 40.3%.

Baseline distribution of interproximal contact points

A total of 395 interproximal contact points were analyzed. Of these, 253 (64.1%) were associated with female patients and 142 (35.9%) with male patients. The age distribution showed that 83 contact points (21.0%) were from patients aged 20–39 years, 192 (48.6%) from those aged 40–59 years, and 120 (30.4%) from patients aged 60 years or older. Food impaction was present in 159 (40.3%) contacts, while 236 (59.7%) showed no impaction. The edentulous span adjacent to the implant had been present for < 5 years in 256 contacts (64.8%) and ≥ 5 years in 139 (35.2%). Free‑end spans comprised 36.7% of contacts, while fixed restorations were predominantly single crowns (68.9%), followed by connected crowns (17.5%) and fixed bridges (13.7%). Morphologic assessments revealed that a residual ridge height discrepancy was present in 11.4% of contacts, and a plunger cusp was observed in 5.8%. Radiographically, the majority of contacts exhibited moderate bone support (77.2% with 1/8–2/8 of root length), with fewer demonstrating minimal (< 1/8, 6.6%) or advanced (> 3/8, 2.0%) bone loss. Finally, mesial contact locations predominated (68.1%) compared to distal sites (31.9%) (Table 1).

Univariate analysis of risk factors for proximal contact loss

Univariate analysis demonstrated that several prosthetic and anatomical variables were significantly associated with PCL. Contact points exhibiting clinical food impaction had a markedly higher PCL rate compared with those without impaction (89/159 vs. 27/236; χ² = 90.83, P < 0.001). Shorter edentulism duration (< 5 years) was also linked to increased PCL incidence (119/159 vs. 40/159; χ² = 9.13, P = 0.003). Similarly, mesial contact location showed a strong association with PCL (139/159 vs. 20/159; χ² = 38.00, P < 0.001), as did the presence of a residual ridge height discrepancy (33/159 vs. 126/159; χ² = 11.32, P < 0.001). Radiographic bone support influenced outcomes, with minimal support (< 1/8 of root length) sites exhibiting higher PCL rates than those with greater support (4/159 vs. 18/236; χ² = 12.51, P = 0.005). Finally, contacts with a plunger cusp were more prone to PCL than those without (22/159 vs. 11/236; χ² = 10.45, P = 0.001). In contrast, none of the patient demographic factors, including sex, age category, smoking, alcohol use, diabetes, hypertension, or bruxism, demonstrated significant univariate associations with PCL (all P > 0.05). Likewise, occlusal and surgical variables (occlusal stability, implant position, jaw arch, free‑end span, additional surgical procedures, and restoration format) as well as characteristics of adjacent and opposing dentition (pulp vitality, tooth type, wear patterns) were not significantly related to PCL (all P > 0.05) (Table 2).

Independent predictors of proximal contact loss

In the multivariate Cox proportional hazards model, three local anatomic and prosthetic factors (particularly food impaction, ridge discrepancy, and bone loss) emerged as independent predictors of PCL. After adjusting for potential confounders, contact points with clinical food impaction demonstrated a 2.75‑fold increased hazard of PCL compared with non‑impacted sites (HR 2.75; 95% CI, 1.70–4.58; P < 0.001). Similarly, a residual ridge height discrepancy doubled the risk of PCL (HR 2.51; 95% CI, 1.37–4.58; P = 0.003). Advanced alveolar bone support loss (> 3/8 of root length) was also significantly associated with PCL, conferring a nearly six‑fold hazard (HR 5.83; 95% CI, 1.05–32.33; P = 0.044). Other variables, including edentulism duration, distal contact location, and the presence of a plunger cusp, did not retain statistical significance in the adjusted analysis (all P > 0.05), indicating that their effects on contact stability may be mediated through or outweighed by the primary anatomic factors identified (Table 3).

Discussion

This study confirmed that PCL is a prevalent and clinically relevant complication following implant-supported prosthetic restoration adjacent to natural teeth. Among the 206 patients included, 67.5% exhibited at least one site of PCL, and on a contact-point level, 159 of 395 interproximal contacts were affected, resulting in a prevalence rate of 40.3%. This finding aligns with previously reported PCL rates, which range from 30% to 58%, particularly in long-term restorations. For example, Wong et al. reported a PCL incidence of 43% in 186 contacts over an average follow-up period, with a predominance of mesial contact loss in the mandibular arch [16]. Other studies with up to 10 years of follow-up have documented rates approaching 50%, suggesting that the 40.3% found in our study is consistent with the broader literature and reinforces the notion that PCL is a common and clinically important outcome [17].

Univariate analysis in this study demonstrated that PCL was primarily associated with local anatomical and restorative factors, while systemic and behavioral factors such as age, sex, smoking, and bruxism showed no significant correlation. This highlights the predominance of local biomechanical influences over systemic contributors in the etiology of PCL, a conclusion that has also been supported by systematic reviews. For instance, existing literature suggests that variables such as sex, jaw location (maxilla or mandible), type of prosthesis, type of opposing dentition, and pulp vitality of adjacent teeth are not predictive of PCL, underscoring the need for universal surveillance regardless of patient characteristics. Multivariate Cox regression analysis identified three independent risk factors significantly associated with PCL: presence of food impaction, discrepancy in residual ridge height between implant and adjacent teeth, and advanced bone loss of adjacent natural teeth. Notably, interproximal contacts with reported food impaction exhibited a 2.75-fold increased risk of PCL (HR = 2.75, P < 0.001). PCL and food impaction exhibit a bidirectional and self-perpetuating relationship. While food impaction is often regarded as a clinical sequela of PCL arising from mesial drift of adjacent teeth, it may also function as an aggravating factor that accelerates the deterioration of interproximal contact integrity. Once the tight contact is disrupted, masticatory forces can wedge food particles into the enlarged embrasure space, generating lateral mechanical stress on the adjacent tooth and implant restoration. This process may further widen the contact gap and contribute to occlusal wear or minor tooth displacement. Concurrently, the accumulation of food debris and plaque can induce localized inflammation and marginal bone remodeling, exacerbating soft tissue breakdown and promoting further contact loss. Therefore, although food impaction may initially result from early contact loosening, its persistence can potentiate the severity and recurrence of PCL. Recognizing this reciprocal interaction underscores the importance of managing both conditions concurrently to preserve interproximal stability and peri-implant health.

Residual ridge height discrepancy also emerged as a significant predictor (HR = 2.51, P = 0.003). Vertical misalignment between the implant site and the adjacent natural tooth can lead to abnormal restoration contours and high-positioned or narrow contact areas, compromising contact stability. This finding indicates the importance of restoring ideal alveolar ridge anatomy before or during prosthetic rehabilitation to reduce the risk of future contact loss. Additionally, the presence of severe bone loss in adjacent teeth (bone support loss >3/8 of the root length) was associated with a markedly elevated risk of PCL (HR = 5.83, P = 0.044). Reduced periodontal support may increase the mobility or mesial drift of natural teeth, facilitating the development of gaps between implant-supported crowns and adjacent teeth [18, 19]. These findings underscore the need for comprehensive periodontal evaluation and management of adjacent teeth both prior to and during follow-up of implant restorations. Mesial contacts were more susceptible to PCL than distal contacts. Although this variable did not retain statistical significance in the multivariate model, likely due to collinearity with other variables, 87% of PCL cases occurred at mesial sites. This is consistent with the physiological tendency of natural teeth to drift mesially over time, a process disrupted by the immobility of dental implants [20, 21]. Hence, the imbalance leads to progressive separation of contacts, predominantly on the mesial side. This common biomechanical mechanism supports the clinical necessity of paying closer attention to mesial contacts during follow-up.

The discrepancy between our findings and those of previous reports regarding the influence of contact location (mesial vs. distal) on PCL should be acknowledged. In this study, mesial contacts showed a significantly higher incidence of PCL, consistent with the physiological mesial drift of adjacent teeth near osseointegrated implants. In contrast, Byun et al. [12] found that contact location lost significance after multivariate adjustment, and Pappous et al. [13] reported inconsistent evidence across studies, likely due to heterogeneity in implant design, restoration type, and follow-up duration. These differences may also stem from methodological variation: our time-to-event Cox regression with calibrated radiographic assessment may have captured temporal changes more accurately than prior cross-sectional analyses. Additionally, the higher proportion of mandibular and free-end implants in our cohort could have accentuated mesial drift–related contact loss. Overall, while mesial contact loss appears to be the predominant clinical pattern, its magnitude relative to distal sites likely varies with cohort composition and study design, underscoring the need for further longitudinal validation. The overall PCL incidence in our study falls within mid-range values reported previously, 20–30% in early follow-ups and over 50% in long-term studies [22, 23], confirming that PCL is a progressive and prevalent event. The mesial predominance aligns with reports by Koori et al. and Pang et al. [24, 25], supporting mesial drift as a primary etiologic mechanism. Although some earlier studies suggested higher rates in the mandible, recent evidence, including ours, shows no consistent arch-related difference. Prosthetic variables had minimal impact on PCL, consistent with most prior research showing that restoration type, implant site, and opposing dentition do not significantly alter risk. Liang et al. [26]. reported greater susceptibility in bridge-type prostheses, though such effects often disappeared in multivariate analysis. Our identification of food impaction, ridge height discrepancy, and advanced bone loss as significant predictors aligns with Liang et al. and Pang et al. [25, 26]. The strong association of bone loss (HR = 5.83) underscores the influence of periodontal support on contact stability. Ridge height discrepancy, less discussed in previous literature, may represent an overlooked anatomic contributor, highlighting the need for preemptive ridge augmentation or prosthetic modification. Although bruxism was not significant here, prior studies indicate that anterior occlusal forces can induce mesial vector components, promoting contact breakdown and reinforcing the multifactorial etiology of PCL [27, 28].

The high incidence of PCL underscores the necessity for routine interproximal contact monitoring following implant restoration. Clinicians should conduct contact assessments at 3 months post-restoration and semiannually thereafter. Patients identified as high-risk, such as those with periodontal bone loss, residual ridge discrepancies, or signs of food impaction, should receive individualized preventive interventions. Prior to prosthetic placement, adjacent periodontal status should be optimized. In cases of vertical bone deficiency, bone augmentation or prosthetic modification should be considered to improve contact area configuration. Adjustments such as enameloplasty can be employed to establish broader, more stable contact surfaces. Patients with signs of food impaction or early PCL may benefit from occlusal splints or interproximal restorations to mitigate further deterioration. Proactive correction of plunger cusps and prompt intervention for open contacts can also reduce downstream complications. Clinicians should educate patients about the risk of contact loss and tailor follow-up schedules accordingly.

This study’s strengths include a relatively large sample size (206 patients, 395 contacts), comprehensive evaluation of both systemic and local risk factors, and the application of Cox regression modeling to capture time-to-event data. The inclusion of novel factors such as ridge height discrepancy broadens current understanding of PCL pathogenesis. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design may introduce information and measurement bias. Some early or mild PCL cases may have been underreported due to variability in documentation. Second, the single-center nature of the study limits generalizability. Third, variation in follow-up duration may have influenced observed PCL rates, although Cox modeling adjusted for time to some extent. Additionally, unmeasured variables such as occlusal force magnitude and three-dimensional contact morphology were not analyzed. PCL detection was primarily based on clinical probing and radiographic assessments, which may be less accurate than digital three-dimensional methods. Finally, while this study focused on risk identification, future prospective trials are needed to evaluate the effectiveness of specific prevention or management strategies for PCL.

Conclusions

This study confirms that PCL is a common complication following implant-supported prosthetic restoration adjacent to natural teeth. Food impaction, residual ridge height discrepancy, and severe alveolar bone loss in adjacent teeth were identified as significant risk factors. Early recognition and targeted intervention for high-risk cases are essential to maintain long-term interproximal stability.
