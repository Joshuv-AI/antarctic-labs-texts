Exploring vasopressin use in septic shock: a retrospective cohort study from Türkiye (VASCO-TURK)
Authors: Ahmet Oğuzhan Küçük, Aleyna Bektaş Hocek, Davut Aydın, Berfin Acar, Ezgi Pehlivanlar, Mehtap Pehlivanlar Küçük
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVasopressin is a peptide hormone and often added to reduce catecholamine dose and side effects according to recent guidelines. Despite increasing global interest in vasopressin use in septic shock, real-world data from low- to middle-income countries, including Türkiye, remain scarce.Material-methodThis study was conducted as a single-center, retrospective, observational study in a tertiary intensive care unit, with the diagnosis of septic shock according to Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines and who received norepinephrine alone and both norepinephrine with vasopressin as an adjunctive vasopressor.ResultsA total of 146 septic shock patients were included in the study, with 113 (77.4%) in the norepinephrine group and 33 (22.6%) in the patients receiving adjunctive vasopressin therapy. Overall survival: 24.2% in adjunctive vasopressin group vs. 24.8% in norepinephrine group (p = 1.000). Median norepinephrine dose decreased significantly from 0.55 mcg/kg/min (IQR: 0.44–0.84) at baseline to 0.43 mcg/kg/min (IQR: 0.27–0.72) by the 6th hour (p = 0.010), supporting a vasopressor-sparing effect of vasopressin. Adjunctive vasopressin initiation time was 14 h (IQR 5–25 h), and the initial norepinephrine dose at the time of vasopressin introduction was, 0.56 mcg/kg/min (IQR 0.445–0.880). Mean arterial pressure (MAP), improved from 60 mmHg (IQR: 54–66) at hour 0 to 70 mmHg (IQR: 63.08–75.75) at hour 2 and 74 mmHg (IQR: 61.75–83.5) at hour 6 (p < 0.001). Heart rate initially remained stable between baseline and the 2nd hour (110 vs. 110 bpm), but decreased significantly by the 6th hour to a median of 97.5 bpm (IQR: 88.75–120.5; p = 0.003).ConclusionThis single center study, with a limited number of patients, is valuable as it reflects the first national experience with vasopressin. In this study, no difference in mortality was observed between patients receiving norepinephrine alone and those receiving vasopressin in combination with norepinephrine. Vasopressin demonstrated a significant norepinephrine-sparing effect, improved heart rate control, and increased mean arterial pressure, suggesting potential hemodynamic stabilization.Trial registration The study is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06953297). Introduction

Septic shock is the leading cause of death among critically ill patients and is the main indication for the use of vasopressors. From a pathophysiological perspective, there is a diminished responsiveness of vascular smooth muscle to norepinephrine, accompanied by a relative insufficiency of vasopressin that is an endogenous peptide hormone [1]. To obtain a targeted mean arterial pressure (MAP) (≥ 65 mmHg) to ensure tissue perfusion, norepinephrine (NE) is currently recommended as the first-line vasoactive drug when fluid resuscitation fails. If MAP is still inadequate, the SSC guideline proposes to start a second-line vasopressor [2].It’s clearly known that high-dose catecholamines are associated with numerous side effects, particularly tachyarrhythmias; therefore, a second agent is often required to allow for dose reduction [3, 4]. Early on, multiple small studies indicated that the addition of vasopressin in septic shock patients led to a reduction in norepinephrine requirements, though it did not consistently translate into improved survival rates [5]– [6]. In 2010, Bauer et al. conducted the first study specifically examining vasopressor weaning. This retrospective observational study reported a greater incidence of hypotension in patients where vasopressin was the first agent to be tapered. Nevertheless, no significant differences were observed in outcomes such as hospital length of stay or mortality [7]. In contrast, Jun Xu et al. evaluated 1,817 patients diagnosed with septic shock according to Sepsis-3 criteria and found that initiating vasopressin when norepinephrine doses were low (NE < 0.25 µg/kg/min) was associated with improved 28-day mortality [8].

Vazopressins effectiveness has been defined by two trials (VASST and VANISH) that dealt with different populations and were underpowered to detect a realistic mortality effect. Both the Vasopressin and Septic Shock Trial (VASST) and the Vasopressin versus Norepinephrine as Initial Therapy in Septic Shock (VANISH) trial found that vasopressin treatment did not significantly alter 28-day or 90-day mortality [9, 10].

In a recent study The OVISS Reinforcement Learning Study’ ye göre among 14 453 critically ill patients with septic shock from 232 hospitals in 4 independent datasets, a reinforcement learning model recommended the initiation of vasopressin more frequently, sooner, at a lower norepinephrine dose, and at a lower organ failure score compared with the average observed actions of clinicians [11]. In light of these variable results the SSC 2021 guidelines issue a weak recommendation for starting vasopressin initiation when the dose of NE is in the range of 0.25–0.5 µg/kg/min [12].

When and whether a second agent, such as vasopressin, should be added is still controversial. Although positive studies on the effectiveness of vasopressin in septic shock have increased in recent years, there is still no clear consensus on the optimal timing for its initiation. This article was written to share the initial experiences regarding the use of vasopressin in Turkey following its availability.

Materyals and methods

Study design

The STROBE guideline was followed during the writing of article. This study was conducted as a single-center, retrospective, observational study in a tertiary intensive care unit, utilizing routine tests and clinical records of the patients. The study (2025-5) was approved by the ethical committee of Karadeniz Teknik Üniversitesi. Ethical approval number is 2025/5. Clinicaltrials study approval number is NCT06953297. URL: https://ctv.veeva.com/study/vasopressin-use-in-septic-shock-from-turkiye. Date: 23/04/2025.

Study population

We included all adult patients (aged >18 years) admitted to the ICU between 1 January 2024 and 15 December 2024 with a diagnosis of septic shock according to the Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines, who received either norepinephrine alone or norepinephrine in combination with vasopressin as an adjunctive vasopressor [13]. Since vasopressin has been available in our country for only a very short period of time (with covering this duration), patients who received vasopressor treatment with a diagnosis of septic shock in the last year were included in the study retrospectively. We excluded all patients admitted those who did not have a diagnosis of septic shock, patients transferred from another participating ICU and those whose electronic medical records were not retrievable.

Data and definitions

We collected hospital administrative and intensive care unit data, encompassing patient demographics, prescribed medications, vital sign measurements, laboratory results, and necessary treatments, in addition to patient-centered outcomes such as ICU and 28 day mortality and duration of ICU stay. The analysis included the source of infection and information regarding the administered antimicrobial treatment.

The Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score and the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score were recorded at diagnosis of septic shock. The worst hemodynamic parameters at the time of septic shock diagnosis were also documented. Septic shock was defined according to the Sepsis-3 consensus definition [14–16]. Patients were screened if vasopressin was infused for at least 4 h. Patients were excluded if they received epinephrine or dopamine either prior to or in addition to norepinephrine. The onset of septic shock was defined as the time of norepinephrine initiation. For patients receiving vasopressin, documentation of both the initiation and discontinuation times was required.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint of this study was the evolution of physiological parameters according to the timing of vasopressin commencement such as change in norepinephrine dose, arterial pH, creatinine, lactate and haemodynamic observations. The secondary endpoint was mortality.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Mac, Version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The distribution of continuous variables was evaluated using both the Shapiro–Wilk test and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, in addition to visual methods such as histograms and Q–Q plots. Variables that did not follow a normal distribution were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR: 25th–75th percentiles), whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. For comparisons between two independent groups (e.g., vasopressin vs. non-vasopressin), the Mann–Whitney U test was used for continuous non-normally distributed data. Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was applied for categorical variables, as appropriate. For repeated measurements over time (e.g., changes in vital signs and laboratory values following vasopressin initiation), Friedman’s two-way analysis of variance by ranks was used. Statistically significant differences were further explored using pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni adjustment, and results were annotated with superscript letters in the relevant tables and figures. Survival analyses were performed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed by the log-rank test (Mantel–Cox). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The Kaplan-Meier survival curves (Fig. 2) demonstrate an early divergence between groups, with a numerically lower survival probability in the vasopressin group during the first 10 days of ICU stay. However, this difference did not reach statistical significance. The log-rank test (Mantel-Cox) yielded a χ² value of 1.251 with a p-value of 0.263, indicating no significant difference in ICU survival between patients who received vasopressin and those who did not.

Kaplan-Meier analyses of mortality between groups

Following vasopressin initiation, a significant and clinically meaningful improvement was observed in multiple hemodynamic and metabolic parameters within the first six hours of treatment. The timing of adjunctive vasopressin initiation time was 14 h (IQR 5–25 h), and the initial norepinephrine dose at the time of vasopressin introduction was, 0.56 mcg/kg/min (IQR 0.445–0.880 mcg/kg/min). Systolic blood pressure increased from a baseline median of 80 mmHg (IQR: 70–92.5) to 90 mmHg at the 2nd hour and reached 95 mmHg at the 6th hour (p = 0.002). Similarly, diastolic blood pressure rose from 50 mmHg (IQR: 40–50) at baseline to 60 mmHg (IQR: 50–60) by the 6th hour (p = 0.001). These changes were accompanied by an increase in mean arterial pressure (MAP), which improved from 60 mmHg (IQR: 54–66) at hour 0 to 70 mmHg (IQR: 63.08–75.75) at hour 2 and 74 mmHg (IQR: 61.75–83.5) at hour 6 (p < 0.001). Heart rate initially remained stable between baseline and the 2nd hour (110 vs. 110 bpm), but decreased significantly by the 6th hour to a median of 97.5 bpm (IQR: 88.75–120.5; p = 0.003).

Concomitantly, serum lactate levels declined significantly over time, from a baseline median of 4.5 mmol/L (IQR: 3.55–7.05) to 4.0 mmol/L at the 2nd hour and 3.25 mmol/L at the 6th hour (p < 0.001). Although arterial pH values showed a slight upward trend, the change did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.07). In terms of vasopressor requirements, the median norepinephrine dose decreased significantly from 0.55 mcg/kg/min (IQR: 0.44–0.84) at baseline to 0.43 mcg/kg/min (IQR: 0.27–0.72) by the 6th hour (p = 0.010), supporting a vasopressor-sparing effect of vasopressin. Throughout this period, vasopressin dosing remained stable at a median of 0.02 U/min (p = 0.016) (Table 4 and Fig. 3).

Discussion

Vasopressin is a peptide hormone extracted from the pituitary gland. It regulates water balance by reducing the volume of urine in the body, and in the hypotensive conditions like septic shock, it stimulates V1a receptors in patients, causing vascular smooth muscle contraction and an increase in blood pressure. In the early stages of septic shock, high levels of vasopressin are produced to regulate blood pressure and tissue water balance. Over the next 72 h, it falls to very low levels and vasopressin stores are depleted. Vasopressin levels remain significantly low for 7 days in septic shock. The more severe the sepsis, the greater the vasopressin deficiency [17].

This single-center study with a limited number of patients is valuable as it reflects the first national experience with vasopressin use in septic shock. In this study, no difference in mortality was observed between patients receiving norepinephrine alone and those receiving vasopressin in combination with norepinephrine. In this cohort, experiencing the initial national use of vasopressin, the timing of adjunctive vasopressin initiation was relatively late, 14 (5–25) hours, and the initial norepinephrine dose at the time of vasopressin introduction was considerably higher, 0.56 (0.445–0.880) mcg/kg/min, compared to other studies. According to the limited available literature, not only the delayed initiation of vasopressin but also its administration after higher norepinephrine doses may have contributed to the observed mortality outcome [18, 19]. Furthermore, in our patients in the vasopressin group had higher APACHE and SOFA scores compared to those receiving norepinephrine alone, which may explain the absence of a significant difference in mortality outcomes between the groups. In the initial experiences, the fact that vasopressin was considered only when patients were hemodynamically more unstable with lower MAP levels and showing clinical deterioration may explain the poor clinical scores observed at that point. This situation suggested that, in our clinic, the use of vasopressin has not yet become a reflexive consideration by physicians in the early phase, before norepinephrine doses reach high levels.

Recurrent robust and large observational data shows that early initiation of vasopressin in patients with vasodilatory shock, in early shock states is associated with decreased mortality (20). In contrast, The VASST and VANISH trials, the only large, multicentre, double-blind randomised controlled trials of adjunctive vasopressin use in septic shock, found no significant difference in mortality.⁠ In the VASST trial, vasopressin was administered after an average delay of approximately 12 h, once patients had reached the norepinephrine inclusion threshold of 5 µg/min. At this point, the mean norepinephrine dose was 0.26 µg/kg/min, and the serum lactate concentration was 3.5 mmol/L (4). In this study, as we we have identified, low-dose vasopressin (0.01 to 0.03 U per minute) did not reduce mortality rates as compared with norepinephrine among patients with septic shock. In another remarkable study, the VANISH trial, initiated vasopressin at a median time of 3.5 h following shock onset, with a lower mean norepinephrine dose of 0.15 µg/kg/min and a serum lactate level of 2.3 mmol/L at randomisation [10]. This study was conducted with 409 patients and found no difference in mortality between the norepinephrine group and the early vasopressin group. In a more recent study conducted by White et al. across 12 centers and involving 2,747 patients; there was an earlier median vasopressin initiation time in the ‘early’ group at 1 h and a higher mean norepinephrine dose of 0.27 µg/kg/min and serum lactate of 5.5 mmol L. In this large, multicentre, retrospective observational study, they found that earlier adjunctive vasopressin initiation for patients with septic shock was independently associated with decreased hospital mortality [19]. Although studies on the optimal timing of vasopressin initiation are still ongoing, the latest sepsis guidelines emphasize that vasopressin should be considered once a norepinephrine dose of 0.25–0.5 µg/kg/min is reached [2]. In our study as well, vasopressin was initiated at a norepinephrine dose close to the upper limit of this sugession.

According to our findings, initiation of vasopressin was associated with a significant decrease in norepinephrine dose requirements. The initiation of vasopressin infusion was associated with an immediate norepinephrine sparing effect and a rapid reduction in, hyperlactatemia, tachycardia. The administration of vasopressin also resulted in a notable hemodynamic improvement, characterized by a significant rise in mean arterial pressure. The physiological changes seen after vasopressin initiation, as demonstrated in our study, are consistent with the literature. Open-label randomized controlled trials investigating adjunctive vasopressin have demonstrated comparable decreases in norepinephrine requirements, lactate levels, acidosis, and heart rate, consistent with the findings observed in our study [6, 17, 18, 19, 20]. In this cohort, among the physiological parameters that showed improvement, only the increase in blood pH was not statistically significant.

In this cohort the respiratory tract was the most common infection source in both groups (54.9% in norepinephrine and 66.7% in vasopressin group). Some experimental studies have suggested that vasopressin may influence respiratory regulation through mechanisms involving osmolality and V1a receptor activity. However, these findings are primarily derived from preclinical models, and the clinical significance of such effects remains uncertain. As our study did not evaluate respiratory parameters, we cannot comment on any potential respiratory impact of vasopressin use in septic shock. Future clinical research is warranted to explore this possible association [16]. This may be a reason why vasopressin should had a greater need with worse clinical state of the patients with septic shock caused by respiratory infections.

Limitations of this study need to be considered. Firstly, due to limitations in the availability of vasopressin in our country, our study population is limited. Secondly, the APACHE and SOFA scores, lactate level, at the time of vasopressin initiation were all higher in the vazopressin group, indicating a greater severity of illness in these patients. The lack of effect of vasopressin addition on mortality may be attributed not only to the small number of patients in the cohort, but also to its use in patients with more severe illness. Third, we do not have data to explain why patients commenced vasopressin predominantly late. This may be due to limited drug availability, the lack of an established protocol for vasopressin use within the unit, the absence of an established reflex among ICU physicians to initiate vasopressin as the second-line vasopressor. Fourth, over the years, advancements in technology and improvements in filter efficiency have led cytokine adsorption therapies to become a reflexive first-line option at high norepinephrine doses. This may act as a confounding factor in the timing and effectiveness assessment of vasopressin. The fact that it is a single-center study also represents a limitation.

Conclusion

This single center study with a limited number of patients is valuable as it reflects the first national experience with vasopressin use. In this study, no difference in mortality was observed between patients receiving norepinephrine alone and those receiving vasopressin in combination with norepinephrine. Adjunctive vasopressin use was associated with a reduction in norepinephrine requirements, accompanied by a decline in lactate levels, a decrease in heart rate, and an overall improvement in hemodynamic parameters, particularly a significant increase in mean arterial pressure.
