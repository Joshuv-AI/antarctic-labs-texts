Gravitational Holonomy in Sagnac Interferometry
Reza Javadinezhad, Ali Seraj

Abstract
We analyze the influence of gravitational waves on a Sagnac interferometer formed by the interference of two counter-propagating beams traversing a closed spatial loop. In addition to the well-known Sagnac phase shift, we identify an additional contribution originating from a relative rotation in the polarization vectors. We formulate this effect as a gravitational holonomy associated to the internal Lorentz group. The magnitude of both effects is computed due to gravitational waves generated by a localized source far from the detector, at leading order in the inverse distance. For freely falling observers, the phase shift is zero and the polarization rotation becomes the dominant effect.

Introduction

Consider two light beams propagating along the same spatial path in opposite directions. Assuming that the two beams are initially in phase, one could measure possible differences in the final phases, after completing one or several loops, by interfering the beams. Eikonal approximation and interference

The
eikonal
approximation provides a systematic framework for describing wave propagation, when the wavelength is much shorter than all other characteristic length scales in the problem. It is an important example of multiple-scale perturbation
[
27
]
. This approximation is widely used across diverse physical contexts, ranging from laser-beam propagation in fiber optics to gravitational lensing by compact astrophysical objects (see,
e.g.
,
[
47
]
for a textbook treatment). The central idea is that the waveform can be decomposed into a rapidly varying phase and a slowly varying amplitude. In the present work, we apply this framework to the propagation of EM waves on a dynamical curved spacetime that itself contains gravitational radiation.

Consider EM waves, described by a gauge field
A_{\mu}(x)
, propagating in a curved spacetime with metric
g_{{\mu\nu}}(x)
. We expand the gauge field, in the eikonal form, as

\displaystyle A_{\mu}(x)\;=\;\mathcal{R}e\left[a(x)\,f_{\mu}(x)\,e^{iS(x)/\epsilon}\right]\,,

(2)

where
\epsilon\ll 1
is a bookkeeping parameter,
S(x)
is the rapidly varying eikonal phase,
a(x)
is the slowly varying scalar amplitude, and
f_{\mu}(x)
is the slowly varying polarization vector with unit norm
f_{\mu}f^{\mu}=1
.
We then insert this ansatz into the Maxwell’s equations and solve the equations order by order in
\epsilon
. Define the wavevector
k_{\mu}\equiv\nabla_{\mu}S
as the normal to the wavefronts. At
O(\epsilon^{-2})
, Maxwell equations reduce to the so-called
eikonal equation

\displaystyle k^{\mu}k_{\mu}=0\,,\qquad k_{\mu}=\nabla_{\mu}S\,.

(3)

These two properties imply that
k^{\mu}
is geodesic as well

\displaystyle k^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}k_{\mu}=k^{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}k_{\nu}=\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\mu}(k^{\mu}k_{\mu})=0.

(4)

Therefore, the EM waves propagate along null geodesics of the spacetime.
At next order
O(\epsilon^{-1})
, one finds transport equations for the polarization and the amplitude

\displaystyle k^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}f_{\mu}

\displaystyle=0,\qquad\nabla_{\mu}(a^{2}k^{\mu})=0\,.

(5)

The former states that the polarization is parallel transported along the ray, while the latter is the conservation of photon number: the amplitude decreases when neighboring rays diverge, and increases when they converge.

Transverse polarization.

The gauge field has a gauge redundancy, which can be partly fixed by imposing the Lorenz gauge condition
\nabla^{\mu}A_{\mu}=0

k^{\mu}f_{\mu}=0

\displaystyle f_{\mu}\to f_{\mu}+\beta(x)k_{\mu}\,\qquad\beta=\frac{i\alpha}{a}\,.

(6)

The physical polarization can thus be defined as the equivalence class
[f_{\mu}]
with the equivalence relation
f_{\mu}\sim f_{\mu}+\beta k_{\mu}

2.1
Interference

In any interferometric experiment, an EM beam is split into two beams through a beam splitter. The beams travel along different spacetimes paths, say
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
. Finally the beams recombine (typically by the beam splitter itself) on a single path towards the detector’s screen.
What is measured by the detector is the total light intensity
I_{\text{tot}}
averaged over several cycles of the EM wave oscillation,

\displaystyle I_{\text{tot}}

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{S}_{\text{tot}}\cdot N\rangle\,,

(16)

where
\boldsymbol{S}=\boldsymbol{E}\times\boldsymbol{B}
, is the Poynting vector (not to be confused with the eikonal phase
S
), projected on the screen with normal vector
N
.

To describe each beam, we use the eikonal approximation (
2
) implying

\displaystyle F_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}e\left(\frac{i}{\epsilon}ae^{iS/\epsilon}(k_{\mu}f_{\nu}-k_{\nu}f_{\mu})\right)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{0})\,.

(17)

Imposing the Lorenz gauge
k^{\mu}f_{\mu}=0
implies
f_{0}=-{\hat{k}}^{i}f_{i}
, which can be used to write the electric field
E_{i}=F_{0i}=\hat{t}^{\mu}e_{i}{}^{\nu}F_{\mu\nu}
and magnetic field
B_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{ijk}F_{jk}=\frac{1}{2}\hat{t}^{\mu}e_{i}{}^{\nu}\epsilon_{{\mu\nu}\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta}
as

\displaystyle E_{i}=\mathcal{R}e\left(\frac{a\omega}{i\epsilon}e^{iS/\epsilon}p_{i}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{0})\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{B}=\hat{k}\times\boldsymbol{E}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{0})\,,

(18)

where
p_{i}=(\delta_{i}{}^{j}-{\hat{k}}_{i}{\hat{k}}^{j})f_{j}
is the transverse physical polarization that we introduced before in (
8
) and (
15
).
Accordingly, the Poynting vector reads
\boldsymbol{S}=\boldsymbol{E}\times({\hat{k}}\times\boldsymbol{E})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{0})=|\boldsymbol{E}|^{2}\,{\hat{k}}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{0}).

The two beams recombine towards the detector’s screen, which is normal to the superposed beam. The total intensity is therefore

\displaystyle I_{\text{tot}}

\displaystyle=\,\langle(\boldsymbol{E}_{1}^{2}+\boldsymbol{E}_{2}^{2}+2\boldsymbol{E}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}_{2})\rangle=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{int},

(19)

where
I=\langle\boldsymbol{E}^{2}\rangle
for each beam. The first two terms are the intensities of each of the beams. They can be measured independently using auxiliary monitoring detectors placed right before the two beams recombine at the beam splitter. The interference is then encoded in the
cross-correlation function

\gamma_{12}
defined as

\displaystyle\gamma_{12}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{(I_{\text{tot}}-I_{1}-I_{2})}{2\sqrt{I_{1}\,I_{2}}}=\frac{\langle\boldsymbol{E}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}_{2}\rangle}{\sqrt{\langle|\boldsymbol{E}_{1}|^{2}\rangle\langle|\boldsymbol{E}_{2}|^{2}\rangle}}\,.

(20)

We assume that the beams have linear polarization when interfered, implying that the polarization for the two beams are real valued. Accordingly, for each beam,

\displaystyle E_{i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{a\omega}{\epsilon}\sin(S/\epsilon)\,p_{i}\,,\qquad\sqrt{2I}=\tfrac{a\omega}{\epsilon}\,,

(21)

where in the last equality, we used
|p|=1
, thanks to (
10
).
As a result, the cross-correlation function becomes

\gamma_{12}=\cos\Delta S\,\cos\psi\,,\quad\Delta S\equiv S_{1}-S_{2}\,,\quad\cos\psi\equiv p_{1}\cdot p_{2}/,.

(22)

Therefore, the interferometric effect is purely written in terms of two angles: I) the difference
\Delta S
in the eikonal phases of the two beams , II) the angle
\psi=\cos^{-1}(p_{1}\cdot p_{2})
between the polarization of the beams when they interfere.
Note the disappearance of
\epsilon
in (
22
). This is because while each of
S_{1},S_{2}
are fast variables, the difference in phases
S_{1}-S_{2}
is a slow/adiabatic effect proportional to
\epsilon
which is cancelled by the
\epsilon^{-1}
in the eikonal exponent.

Initial conditions.

The interference described by (
22
) crucially depends on the initial phases and polarizations of the two beams. If the two beams are initially in phase and have identical polarization vectors, then both angles
\Delta S,\psi
are initially zero and get a small correction after completing the loop. As a result,

\displaystyle\gamma_{12}

\displaystyle\approx 1-\frac{1}{2}\big(\Delta S^{2}+\psi^{2}\big)\,.

Being quadratic in the small angles, the interferometric effect is highly suppressed and hence hard to detect. This suppression due to cosine function can be circumvented by changing the initial states. One can introduce an initial relative phase or an initial rotation of polarization by using a suitable optical device. In particular, it is wise to prepare the initial state of the beams to have either orthogonal phases or orthogonal polarization vectors. Let us discuss these separately.

•

\displaystyle\Delta S\big|_{t=0}=\pi/2,\qquad p_{1}\cdot p_{2}\big|_{t=0}=1,

(23)

In this case, the setup is sensitive to phase difference as the polarization effect is second order, and

\displaystyle\gamma_{12}

\displaystyle\approx\pi/2-\Delta S.

(24)

•

Aligned phase/Orthogonal polarization

\displaystyle\Delta S\big|_{t=0}=0,\qquad p_{1}\cdot p_{2}\big|_{t=0}=0\,.

(25)

In this case,

\displaystyle\gamma_{12}

\displaystyle=p_{1}\cdot p_{2}\approx\pi/2-\psi\,,

(26)

To conclude this section, let us stress on an observational distinction between the eikonal phase shift
\Delta S
and the polarization rotation
\psi
. Decomposing the EM field in the circular polarization (helicity) basis, one can see that the two effects correspond to a phase shift in each polarization. The point is, however, that the eikonal phase shift
\Delta S
rotates the two polarizations in the same way, while the phase induced by polarization rotation on right-handed photons is opposite that of left-handed ones.
This suggests an alternative way to empirically resolve the eikonal phase shift from the polarization rotation: one can split the light beams inside the Sagnac interferometer into its two polarization components and interfere them separately. Eq. (

\displaystyle\gamma_{12}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\cos(\Delta S-\psi)+\cos(\Delta S+\psi)\Big)\,.

Each of the two terms on the right-hand side is actually the contribution from one of the polarizations.

2.2
Gravitational holonomy

Evolution of the polarization.

It was shown in (
5
) that the polarization vector
f^{\mu}
is parallel transported along the null ray. This equation can be solved in a local orthonormal frame
e_{a}{}^{\mu}
and its inverse
e^{a}{}_{\mu}
as follows.

\displaystyle e_{a}{}^{\mu}\,:\qquad g_{{\mu\nu}}e_{a}{}^{\mu}e_{b}{}^{\nu}=\eta_{ab}\,,\qquad\eta_{ab}e^{a}{}_{\mu}e^{b}{}_{\nu}=g_{\mu\nu}.

(28)

Write
f^{\mu}=f^{a}e_{a}^{\mu}
and let the light trajectory be a worldline
\gamma:\{x^{\mu}(\lambda)\}
, where
\lambda
is a parameter on the null geodesic of the light ray. The parallel transport equation can be recast for
f^{a}
as

\displaystyle\dfrac{df^{a}}{d\lambda}=\Omega^{a}{}_{b}f^{b}\,,\qquad\Omega^{a}{}_{b}=-k^{\mu}\omega_{\mu}{}^{a}{}_{b},

(29)

where
\omega_{\mu}{}^{a}{}_{b}=e^{a}{}_{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}e_{b}{}^{\nu}
is the associated torsionless spin connection, which is real and antisymmetric under
a\leftrightarrow b
. The spin connection as a one-form is denoted as
\omega^{a}{}_{b}=\omega_{\mu}{}^{a}{}_{b}dx^{\mu}
is , and
\boldsymbol{\omega}
denotes the matrix valued one-form with elements
\omega^{a}{}_{b}\,
. Equation (
29
) is solved by a path ordered exponential

\displaystyle f^{a}(t)={U}^{a}{}_{b}(t)\,f_{0}^{b}\,,\qquad U(t)=\mathcal{P}\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\boldsymbol{\omega}\right).

(30)

where in integral is over the worldline
\gamma
of the light ray and
f_{0}=f(\lambda=0)
is the initial polarization. Note that the evolution matrix
U
is path-dependent, and to stress this, we sometimes denote it as
U_{\gamma}
. Since
\boldsymbol{\omega}
is a one-form, it can be unambiguously integrated on the worldline. The Dyson series expansion can be used to approximate the path-ordered exponential

\displaystyle U^{a}{}_{b}(t)=\delta^{a}{}_{b}-\int_{0}^{t}\omega^{a}{}_{b}(\lambda)d\lambda\,+\int_{0}^{t}\omega^{a}{}_{c}(\lambda)\int_{0}^{\lambda}\omega^{c}{}_{b}(\lambda^{\prime})d\lambda d\lambda^{\prime}+\cdots.

(31)

Holonomies have three important properties:

Inversion.

Since
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\mathrm{T}}=-\boldsymbol{\omega}
is antisymmetric,
U_{\gamma}^{\dagger}=U_{-\gamma}=U_{\gamma}^{-1}
, where
U_{-\gamma}
is the holonomy along the reversed path.

Composition.

Given two curves
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
such that the endpoint of
\gamma_{1}
coincides with the starting point of
\gamma_{2}
, then

\displaystyle U_{\gamma_{2}}U_{\gamma_{1}}=U_{\gamma_{1}\circ\gamma_{2}},

(32)

where
\gamma_{1}\circ\gamma_{2}
is the joint curve of
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
.

Gauge transformation.

Under a local Lorentz transformation
\Lambda(x)\in SO(3,1)
of the tetrad,
\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime}=\Lambda\,\boldsymbol{e}
, which implies
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}=\Lambda\boldsymbol{\omega}\Lambda^{-1}+\Lambda d(\Lambda^{-1})
. The holonomy
U_{\gamma}
with endpoints

then transforms nicely as

\displaystyle U^{\prime}_{\gamma}=\Lambda(x_{f})\,U_{\gamma}\,\Lambda^{-1}(x_{0})\,.

(33)

These properties can be proven either by the definition of the holonomy in terms of the path-ordered exponential, or its corresponding transport equation
(\frac{d}{d\lambda}-\Omega)U=0
with the initial condition
U=1
at
\lambda=0
.

\displaystyle p_{2}\cdot p_{1}

Moreover, in the absence of gravitational effects one has

3.1
Eikonal phase difference

The eikonal equation
k^{\mu}k_{\mu}=g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}S\partial_{\nu}S=0
can be solved perturbatively by using a metric perturbation around the flat metric,

g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}+\delta g_{\mu\nu}.

(36)

Accordingly we write the eikonal phase as
S=S^{(0)}+S^{(1)}
in the FNC. To leading order we find the equation for the flat space solution
S^{(0)}
,

\eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}S^{(0)}\partial_{\nu}S^{(0)}=0.

(37)

This equation admits a simple plane-wave solution of the form
S^{(0)}=q_{\mu}X^{\mu}
.
The four-vector
q_{\mu}=(-\omega,q_{i})
is null in flat spacetime, satisfying

q_{\mu}q_{\nu}\eta^{\mu\nu}=0
.
At first subleading order, the equation for
S^{(1)}
becomes,

q^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}S^{(1)}=-\frac{1}{2}q_{\mu}q_{\nu}\delta g^{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}q^{\mu}q^{\nu}\delta g_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}(\omega^{2}\delta g_{00}+\delta g_{ij}\,q^{i}\,q^{j})+\omega\,q^{i}\,g_{0i},

(38)

where we have used the elemantry relation
\delta g_{\mu\nu}=-\eta_{\mu\alpha}\eta_{\nu\beta}\delta g^{\alpha\beta}
. What we ultimately wish to compute is the phase difference between two counter-propagating beams in the spacetime with inverse metric
g^{\mu\nu}
.
This phase difference vanishes for the leading eikonal
S^{(0)}
but is nonzero at subleading order
S^{(1)}
.
One may extract it by explicitly solving the subleading equation (
38
), but this is unnecessary, since the same information can be obtained by directly integrating that equation.
Because the equation is already subleading, we may set
q^{\mu}=\tfrac{dx^{\mu}}{d\lambda}
and consistently neglect higher-order corrections.
Integrating over one complete revolution of the beam then yields,

\displaystyle\ S^{(1)}\Big|^{\lambda_{1}}_{0}=\int_{0}^{\lambda_{1}}d\lambda\frac{DS^{(1)}}{D\lambda}=\int_{0}^{\lambda_{1}}d\lambda\,q^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}S^{(1)}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\lambda_{1}}d\lambda(\omega^{2}\delta g_{00}+\delta g_{ij}\,q^{i}\,q^{j})+\omega\int dx^{i}g_{0i}.

(39)

Note that the beam traces a closed loop in space, so the last integral is in fact a line integral around the loop.
For later convenience, we define the vector
\mathcal{A}_{i}\equiv-\tfrac{1}{2}g_{0i}
.
In a Sagnac experiment the second beam propagates along the same loop but in the opposite direction, this effectively corresponds to

q_{i}\rightarrow-q_{i}
while
{\omega}\rightarrow{\omega}
. The first integral on the right hand side of (
39
) is even under this inversion and therefore cancels out when computing the phase difference between counter-orbiting beams. We are therefore left with

\Delta S=\ S_{1}^{(1)}\Big|^{\lambda_{1}}_{0}-{S}_{2}^{(1)}\Big|^{{\lambda}_{2}}_{0}=-4\omega\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{A}\,\,,

(40)

where in the integral is over the one-form
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{i}dx^{i}
.
In this derivation, one directly obtains the phase difference without referring to the time delay between the two beams
[
5
,
21
,
22
]
.

3.2
Sagnac polarization effect

Going back to Figure

\displaystyle=U_{\gamma_{1}}|f_{01}\rangle,\qquad|f_{2}\rangle=U_{\gamma_{2}}|f_{02}\rangle\,.

In the approximation discussed in (

\displaystyle\langle p_{2}|p_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle f_{02}|U_{\Gamma}|f_{01}\rangle+O(r^{-2}),\qquad U_{\Gamma}=\mathcal{P}\exp\big[-\oint_{\Gamma}\omega\big]\,,

(42)

where

and returns back along

An important property of the above result is that it is invariant under local Lorentz gauge transformations. This can be easily seen from the gauge transformation of the holonomy (
33

We conclude that local Lorentz transformations,
i.e.
the choice of the tetrad will not affect the relative polarization rotation, and we are free to do the calculation in any frame we wish.

A notable feature of the polarization rotation compared to the phase shift is its independence of the frequency of light. This can have a positive and a negative side. On the one hand, the phase shift is enhanced by increasing light’s frequency, while the polarization rotation is not. On the other hand, the frequency independence of the holonomy can be used as a signature to distinguish it from other effects, including noises in an actual experiment.

Adiabatic limit.

In typical situations, the frequency of the gravitational field is much smaller than the frequency of oscillation of the light beam inside the loop. For example, for typical stellar mass black hole binaries, the frequency
\nu_{\text{GW}}\sim 100
Hz, while the frequency of oscillation of the light beam inside a 1 meter loop is
\nu_{\text{loop}}\sim 10^{8}
Hz. As a result, the temporal extent of the loop in Figure

shrinks to zero and the holonomy can be approximated by

\displaystyle U_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\approx U_{2\mathcal{C}}=\mathcal{P}\exp\big[-2\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\omega\big]\,,

(43)

where
\mathcal{C}
is the spatial loop along which the light beam is orbiting.

4
Fermi normal coordinates

Fermi normal coordinate (FNC) system provides a natural framework to study gravitational effects in the laboratory of a local observer. The metric in this coordinate system is written naturally in terms of the acceleration and rotation of the observer, as well as tidal effects from the Riemann curvature of spacetime
[
31
,
35
,
36
]
.

FNC is constructed as follows. Let
x^{\mu}(\tau)
be the observer’s worldline (which might be a geodesic or not) and
\tau
its proper time. Its proper velocity and acceleration are defined as
\hat{t}^{\mu}=\tfrac{dx^{\mu}}{d\tau},\;a^{\mu}=\tfrac{d\hat{t}^{\mu}}{d\tau}=\hat{t}^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}\hat{t}^{\mu}
. Construct an orthonormal frame adapted to the observer as
e_{a}{}^{\mu}=(\hat{t}^{\mu},e_{i}{}^{\mu})
. The evolution of the frame along the worldline is characterized as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{D}e^{\mu}_{a}}{\mathrm{D}\tau}=-(a^{\mu}\hat{t}_{\nu}-a_{\nu}\hat{t}^{\mu}+\hat{t}_{\alpha}\omega_{\beta}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\mu}{}_{\nu})e^{\nu}_{a}.

(44)

The antisymmetry of the terms inside the parentheses ensures orthonormality. The frame’s evolution is therefore specified by
a_{i}=e_{i}{}^{\mu}a_{\mu}
and
{\omega}_{i}=e_{i}{}^{\mu}{\omega}_{\mu}
The latter measures the failure of spatial basis
e_{i}
from being Fermi-Walker transported along the worldline.

We define the Fermi normal coordinate
(T,X^{i})
of spacetime events as follows. If the event is on the worldline, we take
(T,X^{i})=(\tau,0)
. For a given spacetime event
p
off the worldline, there is a unique spacelike vector
X^{i}
whose exponential map defines a spatial geodesic
x^{\mu}(\lambda)
such that at
\lambda=0
intersects the observer’s worldline orthogonally at proper time
\tau
and reaches the event
p
at
\lambda=1
. The FNC of the point
p
is thus defined as
(T=\tau,X^{i})
. The spacetime metric in this coordinate system is given as an expansion in powers of
X^{i}
. To quadratic order
O(X^{2})
[
36
]
,

\displaystyle ds^{2}

\displaystyle=-dT^{2}\Big[1+2a_{j}X^{j}+\big(a_{i}a_{j}+{\omega}_{i}{\omega}_{j}-{\omega}_{k}{\omega}^{k}\delta_{ij}+R_{0i0j}\big)X^{i}X^{j}\Big]

(45)

\displaystyle\quad+2dTdX^{i}\Big[\epsilon_{ijk}\,\omega^{j}X^{k}-\frac{2}{3}R_{0nim}\,X^{n}X^{m}\Big]+dX^{i}dX^{j}\Big[\delta_{ij}-\frac{1}{3}R_{injm}\,X^{n}X^{m}\Big]+O(X^{3}).

It is convenient for both physical interpretation and subsequent calculations to recast the local FNC metric in the language of
gravito-electromagnetism
(GEM), see
e.g.

[
33
]
. In this viewpoint, we write the metric (
45
) in terms of effective scalar and vector potentials
\Phi,\mathcal{A}_{i}
and the tidal field
h_{ij}
as

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-(1-2\Phi)dT^{2}-4\mathcal{A}_{i}dTdX^{i}+(\delta_{ij}+h_{ij})dX^{i}dX^{j},

(46)

where

\displaystyle\Phi(X)=-\frac{1}{2}\Big[2a_{j}X^{j}+\big(a_{i}a_{j}+{\omega}_{i}{\omega}_{j}-{\omega}_{k}{\omega}^{k}\delta_{ij}+R_{0i0j}\big)X^{i}X^{j}\Big]+O(X^{3}),

(47a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{i}(X)=-\frac{1}{2}\Big[\epsilon_{ijk}\,\omega^{j}X^{k}-\frac{2}{3}R_{0lim}\,X^{l}X^{m}\Big]+O(X^{3}),

(47b)

\displaystyle h_{ij}(X)=-\frac{1}{3}R_{iljm}\,X^{l}X^{m}+O(X^{3}).

(47c)

Sagnac effect from gravitational waves

Asymptotically flat spacetimes near null infinity are conveniently described using
Bondi coordinates
(u,r,\theta^{A})

In Bondi gauge the metric takes the form

\displaystyle ds^{2}=

\displaystyle-(1-\frac{2m}{r})du^{2}-2du\,dr+r^{2}\Big(q_{AB}+\frac{C_{AB}}{r}\Big)d\theta^{A}d\theta^{B}+D^{A}C_{AB}\,du\,d\theta^{B}+\dots,

(48)

where

D_{A}q_{BC}=0

m(u,\theta^{A})
and the symmetric trace-free shear tensor
C_{AB}(u,\theta^{A})

N_{AB}=\dot{C}_{AB}
is the Bondi news tensor. Hereafter, overdot refers to derivative with respect to

In the following, we consider two asymptotic observers in Bondi coordinates, I) a
static
observer: an accelerated observer at constant spatial coordinates
(r,\theta^{A})
, II) a
freely falling
observer. We will use the results of Section
4
to construct a local FNC associated to these observers, and use them to compute the Sagnac effect for each observer.

To construct the Fermi coordinates associated to any of the above observers, what we need is an explicit identification of the observer’s worldline
x^{\mu}(\tau)
and frame
e_{a}{}^{\mu}
in terms of Bondi coordinates. From there, we can read off the relevant quantities appearing in the corresponding FNC metric (
45

\displaystyle a_{i}=e_{i}^{\mu}a_{\mu}\,,\qquad{\omega}_{i}=e_{i}^{\mu}{\omega}_{\mu}\,,\qquad R_{abcd}=e_{a}{}^{\mu}e_{b}{}^{\nu}e_{c}{}^{\alpha}e_{d}{}^{\beta}R_{{\mu\nu}\alpha\beta}\,.

(49)

The right hand sides of these equations are then computable in Bondi coordinates.

5.1
Hierarchy of scales.

The Fermi normal coordinates, discussed in Section
4
, can involve several length scales. In the problem at hand, there are three length scales: the size of the laboratory
|X|
, distance
r
of the observer to the source of GW and the wavelength
\lambda_{\rm g}
of GWs. A necessary condition for the validity of the FNC expansion is that
\frac{|X|}{r}\ll 1
and
\frac{|X|}{\lambda_{\rm g}}\ll 1
. However, we have another assumption here: that
\lambda_{\rm g}\ll r
. This hierarchy is well satisfied for astrophysical gravitational-wave sources: their distances are typically of the order of billions of light-years, whereas the relevant wavelengths are only a few thousand kilometers, corresponding to a frequency of order
\sim 100
Hz.

As we will see explicitly below, the leading behavior of the quantities appearing in the FNC metric are

\displaystyle|a_{i}|\sim\frac{|N_{AB}|}{r}\sim|{\omega}_{i}|\,,\qquad|R_{abcd}|\sim\frac{|\dot{N}_{AB}|}{r}.

(50)

Looking at (
45
), these imply consistency relations

\displaystyle a_{i}X^{i}\sim\frac{|X|}{r}\ll 1\,,\qquad\frac{R_{0i0j}X^{i}X^{j}}{a_{i}X^{i}}\sim\frac{|\dot{N}_{AB}|X}{|N_{AB}|}\sim\frac{X}{\lambda_{\rm g}}\ll 1.

(51)

Moreover,
the hierarchy
\lambda_{\rm g}\ll r
further implies that

\displaystyle\frac{(a_{i}X^{i})^{2}}{R_{0i0j}X^{i}X^{j}}\sim\frac{|N_{AB}|^{2}}{|\dot{N}_{AB}|r}\sim|N_{AB}|\frac{\lambda_{\rm g}}{r}\ll 1.

(52)

Therefore, we can drop quadratic terms in the acceleration and rotation vectors in (
45
). Terms that are not appearing in (
45
) are subleading with factors of
\frac{|X|}{r}
or
\frac{|X|}{\lambda_{\rm g}}
.

Tetrad and spin connections in the weak field

In this subsection, we construct a tetrad and the corresponding spin connections associated to the FNC metric (

In this approximation, there exists a natural tetrad adapted to GEM. The metric (

\displaystyle=(1-\Phi)dT+2\mathcal{A}_{i}dX^{i}+O(\tfrac{1}{r\,\lambda_{\rm g}})\,,\qquad e^{i}=dX^{i}+\frac{1}{2}h_{ij}dX^{j}+O(\tfrac{1}{r\,\lambda_{\rm g}}).

(53)

At the origin of FNC

which is expected by the construction of FNC.
The spin connection

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}_{ij}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{F}_{ij}dT+\partial_{[i}h_{j]k}dX^{k}+O(\tfrac{1}{r\,\lambda_{\rm g}}),

(54b)

where the GEM field strengths

\displaystyle\equiv-\partial_{i}\Phi-2\dot{\mathcal{A}}_{i}\,,\qquad\mathcal{F}_{ij}\equiv\partial_{i}\mathcal{A}_{j}-\partial_{j}\mathcal{A}_{i}\,,

(55)

together with the tidal effect

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i}

\displaystyle=a_{i}+\big(R_{0i0k}+\epsilon_{ijk}\dot{\omega}^{j}\big)X^{k}-\frac{2}{3}\dot{R}_{0jik}X^{j}X^{k}+O(\tfrac{1}{r\,\lambda_{\rm g}}),

(56a)

(56b)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}R_{ijkl}X^{l}+O(\tfrac{1}{r\,\lambda_{\rm g}})\,.

(56c)

The polarization rotation, however, is exactly the same expression from the static observer. This is obvious from the fact that the spatial metrics

are identical in both cases and the fact that polarization rotation is solely read from
h_{ij}
. We also make an assumption about the initial state of the polarizations similar to the static case, therefore we have

\displaystyle p_{1}\cdot p_{2}=\frac{1}{4r}\dot{N}_{IJ}\sigma_{K}\epsilon^{JK}(f_{01}^{I}f_{02}^{1}-f_{01}^{1}f_{02}^{I})+O(\frac{1}{r^{2}}).

(75)

5.4
Analysis

Let us now compare the magnitude of different effects. As explained in Table
2
, the Sagnac phase (
67
) and the holonomy angle (
70
) are, in principle, independently measurable. Since both effects appear at the same asymptotic order
O(1/r)
, it is therefore necessary to determine which of them provides the dominant contribution.

The relative magnitude of the two effects is controlled by the ratio
\frac{|\dot{N}_{AB}|}{{\omega}\,|N_{AB}|}
. Upon using
\dot{N}_{AB}\sim\omega_{\mathrm{\rm g}}N_{AB}
, we find that

\frac{p_{1}\cdot p_{2}}{\Delta S}\Big|_{\text{static}}\approx\frac{\omega_{\rm g}}{\omega}=\frac{\lambda_{\rm e}}{\lambda_{\rm g}}\approx 10^{-12}.

(76)

Therefore, the polarization rotation is typically much smaller than the Sagnac phase shift.

Another remark concerns the polarization rotation. We observe that the polarization rotation for the static and freely falling observers coincide at leading order. This is partly related to the gauge covariance of the holonomy (
33
), which implies the invariance of (
42
) under local Lorentz transformations. The Sagnac phase shift (
40
) does not enjoy such invariance.

Memory effects.

The result (
67
) is the Sagnac phase shift after one loop. However, the interference could happen after
N
loops. While the one-loop interference can be used to measure the instantaneous value of the news tensor, the accumulated phase after many loops captures GW memory effects. To see this, let
T
be the travel time of one loop. The
N
-loop effect involves

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N}N_{JK}(n\,T)\approx\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{t=NT}N_{JK}(u)du

(77)

Therefore, the net phase shift is

\displaystyle\Delta S^{\rm N-loop}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{r}\,\frac{4\pi\sigma_{I}}{\lambda_{\rm e}\,T}\,\epsilon^{IJ}D^{K}\Delta C_{JK}

(78)

where
\Delta C_{JK}=C_{JK}\big|_{t=0}^{t=NT}
. For a circular loop of radius
R
, the factor
\frac{4\pi|\sigma_{I}|}{\lambda_{\rm e}\,T}=\frac{2\pi R}{\lambda_{\rm e}}\sim 10^{6}
for the scales assumed in Figure
1
. Therefore, the static (accelerated) Sagnac interferometer is sensitive to memory effects, with a magnifying factor
\frac{|X|}{\lambda_{\rm e}}
.
On the other hand, the polarization rotation (
71
) is proportional to
\dot{N}_{AB}
. The extra time derivative makes it less memory-sensitive, because
N_{AB}
vanishes before and after the burst of GWs.

Another remark about the polarization rotation is its independence on the frequency of the EM beam. This could be a signature that distinguishes this effect from the eikonal phase shift, as well as several other noises in such systems. This could be of importance for realistic applications, see
[
19
,
32
]
.

6
Discussion

The framework implemented in this paper can be used to study various GW effects. While we focused on the effects of GWs on propagation of EM waves and in particular the Sagnac experiment, we believe that the setup can be very useful to study GW memory observables, such as those discussed in
[
38
,
37
,
50
,
49
,
23
]
.

Concerning the effect of gravitational waves on a Sagnac interferometer at large distance, we showed that the Sagnac phase shift and polarization rotation, both appear at
O(1/r)
, while the latter effect is typically smaller by a factor
\frac{\lambda_{\rm e}}{\lambda_{\rm g}}
. An important exception is the case of a freely falling laboratory, in which the Sagnac phase shift is zero and the leading effect is a polarization rotation. It is interesting to compare the polarization rotation effects discussed in this paper with those discussed recently in
[
19
,
32
]
.

We showed that in the particular case, where the Sagnac loop is tangent to the sphere (
i.e.
lies in the transverse plane of Figure
4
), then the effect is suppressed to
O(1/r^{2})
. However, we did not study such subleading effects carefully in this paper. One future direction is to include such subleading effects in our framework and provide a unified framework for subleading memory effects
[

,
50
,
49
,
48
]
. At subleading orders, there are several interesting effects showing up, including the gravitational spin-Hall effect
[
53

54

.

We showed that the Sagnac phase shift can be written as an integral over the gravitomagnetic gauge field. Partial results along that line was provided in

We provide the basis for extending our analysis to the propagation of light beams inside a medium with the application of optical fibers in Sagnac interferometers in mind. Appendix A
Polarization transport in optical fibers

In this paper, we focused on Sagnac configurations in which light propagates in vacuum and is guided by mirrors. An important question is how these results change when fiber optics are used instead. We refer the reader to recent relevant works
[
8
,
34
]
.
Practical optical fibers can have a wide variety of refractive–index profiles, we do not analyze specific fiber geometries here; instead, we refer the reader to the literature where such cases are treated in detail. For simplicity, we assume the fiber’s refractive-index profile is piecewise constant (e.g. single mode fiber). We solve Maxwell’s equations within each uniform segment and do not consider the matching conditions at interfaces between segments with different refractive indices. This presents no difficulty, the differential equations governing electromagnetic wave propagation inside the fiber are independent of the boundary conditions. The latter become relevant only when one seeks particular solutions of these equations.

In this appendix, we denote the
4d
spacetime’s metric and its covariant derivative as
{\tilde{g}}_{\mu\nu}
and
\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}
, and reserve
g_{\mu\nu},\nabla_{\mu}
for the
optical metric
(defined below) and its associated derivative
[
24
]
,

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}\equiv\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}+(1-\frac{1}{n^{2}})u^{\alpha}u^{\beta}\tilde{g}_{\nu\beta}\tilde{g}_{\mu\alpha}.

(79)

The inverse optical metric can be easily worked out,

\displaystyle g^{\mu\nu}=\tilde{g}^{\mu\nu}-(n^{2}-1)u^{\mu}u^{\nu}.

(80)

Let’s consider the Maxwell equations in an isotropic medium with constant refractive index
n
as measured by a congruence of observers
O
. This congruence is described by a unit timelike vector field
u^{\mu}
, satisfying
u^{\mu}u^{\nu}\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}=-1
. If the observers are at rest with respect to the medium, then we simply have

u^{\mu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-\tilde{g}_{00}}}(1,0,0,0).

The Maxwell equations in this medium can be written in the following form
[
24
]
,

\displaystyle\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}(g^{\mu\alpha}g^{\nu\beta}F_{\alpha\beta})=0,\qquad\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}(g^{\mu\nu}A_{\nu})=0,

(81)

Note that in (
81
), the covariant derivative is with respect to the spacetime metric. If we write the equations purely in terms of the spacetime metric and its covariant derivative, we find

\displaystyle\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}(\tilde{g}^{\mu\alpha}\tilde{g}^{\nu\beta}F_{\alpha\beta})=(n^{2}-1)\Big(\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}(\tilde{g}^{\nu\beta}u^{\mu}u^{\alpha}F_{\alpha\beta})+\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}(\tilde{g}^{\mu\alpha}u^{\nu}u^{\beta}F_{\alpha\beta})\Big),

(82a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}(\tilde{g}^{\mu\nu}A_{\nu})=(n^{2}-1)\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}(u^{\mu}u^{\nu}A_{\nu}).

(82b)

On the other hand, we may try to write the equations purely in terms of the optical metric and its associated derivative.

Defining
F^{\mu\nu}\equiv g^{\mu\alpha}g^{\nu\beta}F_{\alpha\beta}
and
A^{\mu}\equiv g^{\mu\nu}A_{\nu}
, we note that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}(F^{\mu\nu})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_{\mu}(\sqrt{-g}F^{\mu\nu}),\quad\nabla_{\mu}A^{\mu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_{\mu}(\sqrt{-g}A^{\mu}),\quad\det(g)=n^{2}\det(\tilde{g}).

(83)

Since
n
is constant, the above equations imply that
\nabla_{\mu}F^{\mu\nu}=\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}F^{\mu\nu}
and similarly
\nabla_{\mu}A^{\mu}=\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}A^{\mu}
, and therefore (
81
) implies Maxwell’s equations inside the medium, purely written in terms of the optical metric and its associated derivative

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}F^{\mu\nu}=0,\qquad\nabla_{\mu}A^{\mu}=0.

(84)

These equations should be compared with their more complicated spacetime counterparts in (
82
). We find that Maxwell’s equations inside the fiber, when written with respect to the optical metric, take exactly the same form as Maxwell’s equations in vacuum. This observation allows us to extend all results obtained in vacuum to the case in which light propagates inside a fiber. In particular, by adopting the eikonal ansatz for the gauge field
A_{\mu}=af_{\mu}e^{iS/\epsilon}
, one recovers precisely (
3
) and (
5
). For completeness and comparison, we also present their counterparts in spacetime, which look much more complicated,

\displaystyle\tilde{g}^{\mu\nu}k_{\mu}k_{\nu}=(n^{2}-1)(k_{\mu}u^{\mu})^{2},

(85a)

\displaystyle k_{\beta}(\tilde{g}^{\alpha\beta}-(n^{2}-1)u^{\alpha}u^{\beta})\tilde{\nabla}_{\beta}\Big((\tilde{g}^{\nu\gamma}-(n^{2}-1)u^{\nu}u^{\gamma})f_{\gamma}\Big)=-(n^{2}-1)f_{\alpha}k_{\beta}u^{\nu}\tilde{\nabla}^{\beta}u^{\alpha}.

(85b)

Appendix B
Derivation of the spin connections

To derive the spin connections corresponding to the GEM tetrad (
53

\displaystyle de^{a}+{\omega}^{a}{}_{b}\wedge e^{b}

\displaystyle=0,

(86)

Taking the exterior derivative of (

\displaystyle de^{0}=\mathcal{E}_{i}~dX^{i}\wedge dT+\mathcal{F}_{ij}~dX^{i}\wedge dX^{j}\,,

(87)

\displaystyle de^{i}=\frac{1}{2}(\dot{h}_{ij}dT+\partial_{k}h_{ij}dX^{k})\wedge dX^{j}\,.

(88)

On the other hand, it is convenient to exploit the algebraic property
\partial_{(i}h_{jk)}=0

\displaystyle de^{i}=\frac{1}{2}(\dot{h}_{ij}dT-(\partial_{i}h_{jk}+\partial_{j}h_{ki})dX^{i})\wedge dX^{j}.

Substituting

\displaystyle{\omega}_{0i}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{i}dT+\big(\mathcal{F}_{ij}+\alpha_{ij}\big)dX^{j}\,,

\displaystyle{\omega}_{ij}

\displaystyle=-\big(\mathcal{F}_{ij}+\alpha_{ij}+\frac{1}{2}\dot{h}_{ij}\big)dT+\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{i}h_{jk}+\partial_{j}h_{ki}+\beta_{ijk})dX^{k},
