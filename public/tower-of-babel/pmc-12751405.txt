The assessment of continuity of care and care coordination for Children and Youth with Special Health Care Needs (CYSHCN): a cross-sectional descriptive study in Northeastern Italy
Authors: Elisa Zanello, Adele Maggiore, Raffaella Dobrina, Chiara Barbiero, Enrica Causin, Elisa Cragnolin, Laura Novello, Sabrina Plet, Luca Ronfani, Paola Toscani, Marianela Urriza, Francesca Valencak, Manuela Giangreco
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildren and Youth with Special Health Care Needs (CYSHCN) represent a growing, though numerically limited, category of the paediatric population requiring care from multiple professionals across different settings. As such, they are at increased risk of experiencing fragmented care, lower quality of services, and unmet health needs. Continuity of care and care coordination are essential to ensure adequate assistance. This study aimed to assess the continuity and coordination of care for CYSHCN in a region of north-east Italy.MethodsOver 21 months (2018–2020), CYSHCN meeting the inclusion criteria were enrolled at a public tertiary university hospital as part of a cross-sectional study. Data about care activities performed over 12 months by Family Paediatricians (FPs), General Practitioners (GPs), and Community Healthcare Professionals (CHPs) were collected using a structured tool. After 9–12 months, parents’ perceptions of continuity of care were assessed using a 20-item questionnaire on a 5-point Likert scale. In 2020, families and professionals were also asked about perceived changes in care due to the COVID-19 pandemic.ResultsMost of the 51 children enrolled had oncological conditions (60.8%). FPs/GPs and CHPs reported 116 and 155 care activities respectively, with statistically significant differences in the type, setting, and intensity of care. Parents reported high levels of perceived continuity of care, though slightly lower ratings were observed for family paediatrician and care coordinator. Among 109 respondents (32 FPs/GPs, 20 CHPs, 32 parents), perceived changes in care during the pandemic were reported more frequently by professionals (58.4%) than by families (43.8%). However, most parents (85.7%) considered the care they received to be adequate.ConclusionsContinuity and coordination of care for CYSHCN were generally well maintained in the region, even during the pandemic. Nonetheless, gaps remain in the role of primary care providers and informational continuity. Strengthening care integration—especially through better communication systems and the use of ICT—may enhance coordination, particularly for children with complex needs and those in rural settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13052-025-02160-y.Keywords: Children with special healthcare needs, Continuity of care, Care coordination, COVID-19 pandemic, Family perspective, Health care professionals

Background

Children and Youth with Special Health Care Needs (CYSHCN) represent a growing, although numerically minor, category of paediatric population [1]. They can be defined as “those who have or are at increased risk for a chronic physical, developmental, behavioural, or emotional condition and who also require health and related services of a type or amount beyond that required by children generally” [2].

According to the United States data from the 2016–2019 National Survey of Children’s Health, the overall estimated prevalence of CYSHCN was 18.8% [3]. In Europe and Italy, information about the prevalence and incidence of special health care needs in the paediatric population is limited to specific health conditions, such as disability and diseases requiring palliative care. According to Eurostat (2024), in the European Union, 3.4% of children had moderate disability and 1.0% severe disability in 2021 [4]. In Italy, the prevalence of genetic diseases and complex disabilities in paediatric population (0–17 years) was estimated to be 0.5% [5], while Benini et al. estimated that 34–54 children per 100,000 inhabitants (of any age) required palliative care [6].

Similar to adult patients with chronic conditions, CYSHCN generally require long-term health care provided by an array of professionals and clinicians in multiple outpatient and inpatient settings and are at greater risk of unmet healthcare needs [7, 8]. According to the American Academy of Pediatrics [9, 10], two key elements in healthcare for CYSHCN are essential to improve quality and minimise risks: continuity of care and care coordination. “Continuity of care” has been defined as the degree to which patients experience their care over time as coherent and interconnected [11] and “care coordination” as the intentional organisation of care activities among providers and with the patient to facilitate the appropriate delivery of health care services [12]. Continuity of care has been found to be associated with lower Emergency Department utilisation and more frequent receipt of care coordination for Children with Medical Complexity (CMC) [13] and to be a key issue in the transition from hospital to home for legal guardians of children with chronic conditions and professionals [14] and for parents of CYSHCN [15]. In contrast, the lack of care coordination for CYSHCN has been associated with unmet healthcare needs [16, 17] representing an additional burden on families [18]. According to the conceptual framework developed by Gardner et al. [19], for these elements to function optimally, timely and accurate exchange of information among all providers at different levels is essential: different kinds of information can be used to support continuity at the patient level and to guide coordination across the whole system.

In Italy, CYSHCN receive health care by multiple providers, mainly through the “Servizio Sanitario Nazionale” (SSN) (i.e. National Health Service), which guarantees healthcare to all citizens through a public system where they can receive healthcare without costs or with a marginal participation in costs. In each region the local Health Authorities (HAs) provide community healthcare, home care, residential care, semi-residential long-term care, outpatient specialist care through Districts, and inpatient care in public hospitals and/or private accredited hospitals or clinics [20]. Family Paediatricians (FPs) and General Practitioners (GPs) provide primary care. FPs provide paediatric primary care to all children aged 0–14 years, and up to 16 years under certain circumstances (i.e. chronic conditions or special health needs). For children over 6 years, the family can choose a GP to care for their child. Local HA social-health services and/or Municipal Social Services respond to the social needs of families.

As suggested by Brooks et al. [21], the interaction between the child’s needs, family, professionals and services in a specific territory represents a complex system, in which the components influence each other; exploring different perspectives can help better understand the experiences of all involved. However, studies on the topic of continuity of care and coordination for complex needs in paediatric population are limited, and generally focus only on some parts of the system [22, 23]. To our knowledge, only one study in Italy investigated these aspects, namely the Special Needs Kids (SpeNK) Project, conducted in the Emilia Romagna region, which focused on the experiences of families [15, 24] and the role of FPs [25].

Inspired by the SpeNK project, our study aimed to address the issues of continuity of care and coordination of care for CYSHCN with a broader approach. We collected and compared information from different perspectives, including not only families and primary care providers (FPs and GPs), but also Community Healthcare Professionals (CHP) who take care of CYSHCN in the Friuli-Venezia Giulia region.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted in north-east Italy in the Friuli-Venezia Giulia region by the Institute for Maternal and Child Health “IRCCS Burlo Garofolo” in collaboration with the regional HAs, as collaborating centres. The Institute is a public referral university hospital and represents a highly specialised national centre for women’s and children’s health. The Continuity of Care Office (CCO) has been active since 2016, with a multidisciplinary team (nurses, psychologist, social worker), to support the implementation of integrated hospital-community care pathways for patients with specific health and/or psychosocial needs and their families. For these patients and families, the CCO, after assessing their needs, establishes an “integrated care network” with the relevant hospital department or outpatient clinic and the local health care services in the area of residence to coordinate care between providers (e.g., the District’s local services, the local hospital, the primary care physician/GP, social services, etc., as needed). A cross-sectional descriptive study was performed by recruiting the CYSHCN and their families, residents of the region, accessing to the study hospital from September 1, 2018 until June 30, 2020. The study then evaluated the activities provided to them by primary and community health professionals during a 12-months follow-up period after recruitment, and measured their families’ experience with the continuity of care received at the end of the follow-up period.

Participants

Children and families were recruited through criterion purposive sampling at the hospital during the study period, according to the following inclusion criteria: (1) child aged 0–17 years, (2) residence in the Friuli-Venezia Giulia region, (3) access to the maternal and child health hospital for inpatient and/or outpatient care in any hospital department/clinic, and referral to the hospital CCO for the activation of the “hospital-community integrated care network” for new or changed health care needs.

The exclusion criteria were the presence of complex psychosocial situations (i.e., involvement of Social Services and/or judicial authority).

The enrollment occurred at the hospital department or the outpatient clinic of access. After an exhaustive explanation of the study, parents were asked to sign an informed consent form to collect clinical data on the child, to contact them for a phone interview at the end of the follow-up period, and to collect information on the health care provided to the child by health professionals who were identified as referents for his/her care outside the hospital, i.e., the child’s FP/GP and a Community Health Professional (CHP) of the local health care service (usually a nurse or doctor). All collected data were recorded anonymously in an electronic protected database.

FPs, GPs, and CHPs identified as referents for the child’s care were contacted by the researchers and invited to participate in the study by providing information about the performed activities. Their involvement was arranged with their respective HA as collaborating centres. For all health professionals, participation was voluntary and researchers ascertained their willingness to participate at a first phone contact. No personal data about the health professionals were recorded.

Data collection and instruments

Data on children and families were collected at recruitment regarding the child’s age, gender, clinical condition, hospital ward/clinic of recruitment, residence, parental citizenship, and contact details of healthcare providers (i.e., GP/FP and CHP).

The FPs, GPs and CHPs attending the recruited children provided data about the activities performed for them during 12 months on a specific form. The form was developed based on the SpeNK-FP tool [25], derived from the Care Coordination Measurement Tool (CCMT©) by Antonelli et al. [26], and investigated the characteristics of the performed activities, e.g., kind, frequency, instruments used, other professionals’ involvement (see Additional File 1).

Families were interviewed through a phone administration of the SpeNK-Q after a maximum of 12 months from recruitment (see Additional File 2). The SpeNK-Q is an instrument designed to measure families’ perceptions about the continuity of care for CYWSHN [24]. It includes 20 items to be rated on a 5-points Likert scale, two open-ended questions asking to identify the person who takes care of all or most of child’s health problems, and who coordinates his/her health care, and two questions about most and least liked aspects of the care received by the child. The validation study was conducted on a sample of parents of preterm-born children ant the Principal Component Analysis showed a 5-factors structure explaining 60.2% of item variance: informational continuity; coordination of care; continuity of family–paediatrician relationship; family support; information on care plan.

Given the extraordinary public health emergency situation in 2020 due to the COVID-19 pandemic, we also asked the families and professionals who were interviewed during that period two closed-ended questions about their perception of a change in health care for CYSHCN and of its adequacy vs. inadequacy.

Data analyses

Our expected sample was of 50 patients. The size was calculated considering the level of continuity perceived by the parents as primary outcome (assessed by item 18 of the SpeNK-Q instrument) and hypothesising, according to the previous results of the SpeNK Project, a frequency of satisfaction of 85% and a precision of the estimate of 10%, setting alpha = 0.05.

Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages while continuous variables were described using the median and interquartile range. Fisher Exact or Chi-square tests were applied to evaluate the association between two categorical variables. The Shapiro-Wilk test was performed to evaluate test the Gaussian distribution of continuous variables. Non-parametric Wilcoxon Mann-Whitney or Kruskal-Wallis tests were used, as appropriate, to assess differences in the distribution of a continuous variable across categories of a qualitative variable.

A p-value < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. All the statistical analyses were performed using SAS software, Version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

A conventional content analysis approach was used to analyse the qualitative data collected through the open-ended questions [27]. One researcher (EZ) read all responses to identify recurring themes to develop thematic categories emerging from the collected data; the resulting categorization was compared and revised collaboratively with the research team (CB, SP) to ensure reflexivity and avoid excessive subjectivity [28].

During the study period, 51 children, their families, and the health professionals involved in their care were recruited.

The children presented a variety of conditions, with a majority of oncological diseases (60.8%). Almost half of the recruited families lived in the same area of the study hospital (41.2%). Activities reported by FPs/GPs and CHPs per characteristics

Forty-one FPs/GPs reported at least one activity out of a total of 116, and no activity in 6 cases. They assisted 1 to 3 children of the sample, and the most frequently reported activities involved contact with family members and other professionals (n = 49, 40.2%), prescribing and providing drugs and medical supplies (n = 29, 23.8%), and direct care (n = 21, 17.2%).

Twenty-four CHPs reported at least one activity, for a total of 155 activities, and none in 3 cases. They assisted 1 to 8 children of the sample and the most of reported activities involved direct care (n = 85, 53.8%).

Participation in the health care network meetings was reported 17 times (13.9%) by FPs/GPs and 15 times (9.5%) by CHPs; for both groups, it required the most time, i.e., half an hour per child.

FPs/GPs reported no contact with other professionals in 57.9%, while CHPs in 41.3% of cases. CHPs reported more frequent contact with the hospital than FPs/GPs (36.1% vs. 19.0%). FPs/GPs and CHPs reported contact with other services and/or professionals in 23.1% and 22.6% of cases.

The majority of reported activities included the use of phone, e-mail, and/or computer (n = 78, 63.9%) for FPs/GPs and access to patients/families’ homes (49.4%) for CHPs.

Focus on COVID-19 pandemic

One hundred nine respondents (32 parents, 32 FPs/GPs and 20 CHPs) answered questions about care provided during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020. A greater proportion of professionals (58.4%) reported changes in healthcare compared to parents (43.8%). However, despite the perceived changes, most parents (85.7%) still considered the assistance adequate.

Associations between sample characteristics, activities of FPs/GPs and CHPs and levels of continuity of care perceived by families

Our analysis of activities carried out by FPs/GPs and CHPs showed statistically significant differences in the method used, both by child’s pathology (p < 0.0001) and province of residence (p < 0.0001) and in contacts with others by pathology (p < 0.0001), (see Table 5). As for the pathology, for children with an oncological disease, compared to the others, the reported activities involved more often direct care (47.1% vs. 23.6%), were carried out by access at school/home/hospital (42.4 vs. 12.7), and contacts with others mainly were with the hospital (36.9% vs. 15.7%). Regarding the residence area, the performed activities for children living in Trieste province vs. other areas, involved more frequent contact with family members and other professionals (41.4% vs. 29%), prescriptions and provision of drugs and medical supplies (17.1% vs. 11.2%), and network meetings (14.4% vs. 9.5%). The activities were carried out mainly through in-person meetings at the clinic (39.6% vs. 18.9%) or by phone/e-mail/computer (39.6% vs. 38.5%). These interactions typically lasted for over 30 min (46% vs. 32.1%).

Discussion

Our study assessed the issues related to the continuity of care and care coordination for CYSHCN in the Friuli-Venezia Giulia region. We collected and compared information from a multidimensional perspective, considering children, family experiences, and the activities of primary care and community-based providers.

Our sample included children with a diverse range of “special health needs” and the majority were CMC, having oncological disesas and neurological conditions. CMC have severe chronic conditions, substantial functional limitations, increased health and other services’ needs, and increased healthcare costs [29]. Our study found a difference in care provided by FPs/GPs and CHPs to children with oncological diseases compared to others (i.e., more direct care, access at school/home/hospital and contact with the hospital). The difference can be partly explained by the long-standing, coordinated care pathway developed for oncology patients, often more recognized as vulnerable and disadvantaged [30]. At the study hospital, such patients have historically benefited from integrated care pathways, involving healthcare, social and educational services, which helped establish more systematic approaches for managing care coordination for other complex conditions.

Also, we found a difference in the activity performed by FPs/GPs and CHPs for children resident in Trieste province, which is an urban area, compared to the other territories of the region. The difference can be linked to geographic factors: urban areas have more accessible services, while rural areas face greater dispersion and distance from hospitals, requiring more movements on the territory for professionals and families. Accordingly, our results show that FPs/GPs and CHPs in Trieste urban area, compared to other areas, have more contacts with family members and other professionals, mainly through in-person meetings at the clinic. According to literature, this impacts the scope of their work and contribute to higher unmet needs for health care for children in rural areas due to challlanges like transportation barriers, provider shortages, and delayed diagnoses [31], especially for CMC [32, 33].

Moreover, our findings showed a difference in the activities carried out by FPs/GPs and CHPs, in terms of type of activity, time dedicated, methods used, and contacts with others. This finding can be explained by the differences in roles, functions, and settings of FPs/GPs and CHPs in the Italian healthcare system, with FPs/GPs providing primary care mostly in the clinics, and CHPs providing healthcare at community level, moving accross the territory [20].

Regarding families’ perceptions, we collected overall positive evaluations and found no significant association with the quantity and characteristics of activities carried out for the children by healthcare providers. Similar to the results of SpeNK Project [24], in our study parents of recruited children reported high levels of continuity of care. The overall positive responses of families may have been influenced by acquiescence bias, with respondents tending to give favorable answers due to the interviewer’s association with the hospital that treated their children. This aligns with evidence showing a tendency toward positive responses in patient satisfaction surveys and the known impact of response biases in Patient Reported Outcomes Measures, especially in telephone interviews compared to self-administered ones [34, 35].

Our study also enlighted the families’ perception of the essential role of the hospital in care coordination for CYSHCN, also after transition from hospital to home. This seems in contrast to recommendations on the potential role of FP in care coordination [26, 36]. However, we should consider that CMC, who represent the greater part of our sample, are more likely to require hospitalization, have more hospital accesses and need for specialist care [37–39]. Some studies suggest that hospital professionals, too, may play a pivotal role in promoting the continuity of care after hospital discharge [14].

According to our results, also informational continuity represents a potential area of improvement to parents of CYSHCN. It has been defined as the “use of information on past events and personal circumstances to make current care appropriate for each individual” [40]. Information flow and communication are key factors in parents’ experiences, especially for families of CYSHCN. Studies show that poor communication, lack of coordination, and fragmented information can cause confusion, increase parents’ stress and negatively impact on interaction with healthcare services [15, 23, 24, 41, 42]. This aligns with the Framework of Information Continuity, which highlights how information gaps can undermine care coordination and increase the burden on families [19]. About coordination and continuity of care, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends the use of Information and Communication Technology (ICT) for the “remote management of people and communities with various health care needs by supporting self-care and enabling electronic communication among health care professionals” [43]. According to the literature, tools as Electronic Health Records (EHRs), telehealth, and digital platforms, can support real-time communication and care coordination, especially for families in rural or underserved areas [44, 45]. In a recent international scoping review authors identified specialist personnel, digital technology, and seamless health system integration as essential elements supporting care coordination for CSHCN [23]. In the regional context, many ICT tools are already present (EHRs, telehealth, etc.), so our findings suggest the need to raise awareness, train, and persuade people to use what is already available, rather than developing new initiatives.

Finally, our study found that, during the COVID-19 pandemic, most professionals and families in our sample did not perceive major changes in healthcare, though professionals noticed changes more than parents. When parents did report changes, they generally found care to be adequate. This contrasts with a study from Brazil, where caregivers reported disrupted care [46]. The difference may be due to targeted initiatives adopted in our study area, such as home vaccinations and special hospital appointments for CYSHCN, that helped to maintain the continuity of care for children and families. These innovative measures likely contributed to healthcare system resilience during the pandemic [47].

Globally, our findings outline a geographic area where CYSHCN can benefit from an integrated, continuous, and coordinated healthcare pathway across multiple settings. In this context, the role of hospital CCO as promoter of the “integrated care network” should be underlined and its likely impact on healthcare across settings for complex needs should be explored, since it represents a “single point access” designed to help coordination and continuity of care from hospital to home, as recommended by WHO [43].

Our study has several limitations. First, the small sample size and the greater proportion of onclogical conditions does not allow us to generalize our findings to children with health conditions, such as chronic pathologies, neurodevelopmental disorders, rare diseases or congenital anomalies, that may have different care coordination needs. The partial overlap of the recruitment period with the COVID-19 pandemic, which impacted overall access to healthcare, may have influenced the composition of our sample. Second, the families’ perception of continuity of care was measured with SpeNK-Q Questionnaire, which was validated only on parents of preterm newborns and not specifically for CYSHCN. Also, the families’ responses, as discussed above, may have been positively influenced by acquiescence bias and the method used to administer the interview. Moreover, the lack of socio-demographic data on families prevented us from exploring the impact of certain variables that may influence their experiences and interactions with health services [48]. Finally, this study was conducted at a single tertiary referral hospital with a dedicated Continuity of Care Office. While this setting enabled focused data collection and organizational consistency, it may not reflect the structure, resources, or practices of other hospitals or regional healthcare systems. As such, the generalisability of the findings to broader or less specialized healthcare contexts may be limited.

The main strength of our study is the adoption of a multidimensional perspective. Our study is the first in Italy to examine the issues of care coordination and continuity of care for CYSHCN, considering the community and local health services’ activities together with the experiences of families.

Conclusions

Our study offers insights into the complex reality of care for CYSHCN and some suggestion for improvements. It reveals that both the coordination and the continuity of care for CYSHCN are performing well in the region, with families reporting positive experiences, even during the COVID-19 pandemic. It also highilights the need to strengthen the collaboration between healthcare providers’ and to support informational continuity to ensure care coordination, especially in rural areas.

These findings have global relevance and stress the importance of integrating care services and promoting the use of digital tools, such as telemedicine and mobile health services, to improve healthcare access and outcomes, particularly in underserved and low-income areas.

Further studies could explore, in a participatory fashon, the barriers and facilitators perceived by families’ and professionals about continuity and coordination of care for CYSHCN. Future research should also explore the characteristics and impact of the CCO, as hospital-based organizational model to promote care coordination for special health care needs in pediatric population.
