Reward design and hyperparameter tuning for generalizable deep reinforcement learning agents in autonomous racing
Authors: Naga Sai Shreya Kunda, Pranave Kc, Mayank Pandey, A. A. Nippun Kumaar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) is transforming autonomous racing by enabling agents to make real-time, high-stakes decisions with the least supervision. Yet, strong generalization over multiple varied tracks is a key bottleneck. Within this paper, a rigorous examination of the relationship between reward system design and hyperparameter tuning for autonomous racing agents using the AWS DeepRacer platform as a unified benchmark is conducted. A comprehensive comparison of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithms on two vastly different reward structures with the aid of an extensive tuning of batch size, learning rate, discount factor, and entropy is performed. The results identify that a well-engineered reward mechanism, under the optimized hyperparameter (batch size 128, learning rate 0.0003, discount factor 0.99, entropy 0.01), allows PPO to outperform standard benchmarks with an average lap time of 12.464 s on 21 unseen tracks. These results demonstrate not only enhanced performance but also improved generalization, enabling the models to perform effectively on previously unseen tracks. Additionally, significant emphasis was placed on reward shaping and analyzing hyperparameter sensitivity in large-scale DRL systems to ensure their practical applicability in autonomous scenarios.

Introduction

Autonomous racing has become one of the most challenging arenas for Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL)1, where agents make quick, accurate decisions in highly unpredictable and dynamic environments. In spite of recent technological developments in DRL methods like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)2 and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)3, having consistent and generalized performance across new environments has been a challenge. This requires the agent not only to have high speed navigation but also adaptability to unseen environments, false sensory information and dynamic obstacles, reflecting the real time situations. Reward system design and hyperparameter optimization in this scenario become imperative but under-researched factors that affect the adaptability of the agent, the efficiency of the race and real-world transferability.

AWS DeepRacer4 offers a realistic but controlled setting for training and testing DRL agents, including cloud-based simulation, reward function customization, and both virtual and real-world deployment. It is a useful testbed for testing autonomous navigation strategies under distinct levels of environment complexity. Its ability to support iterative training, hyperparameter optimization, and reward system modification is especially well-suited to the task of learning how design decisions affect agent performance and can provide insights that are portable to larger real-world problems.

Literature survey

In recent years, Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has been employed to approach the problems of autonomous racing. In this research agenda, the emphasis is placed on model development, performance maximization, and applicability of models in the real world. This section presents the literature survey. The areas of concern are DRL model development, reward system design, hyperparameter tuning and sim-to-real transfer in the domains of developed DRL models on autonomous navigation and AWS DeepRacer.

DRL applications in AWS DeepRacer

Most researchers employed AWS DeepRacer as a platform to train and test autonomous racing agents. Bromall et al.5 investigated the use of Reinforcement Learning models to optimize AI/ML learning results, observing that although lab sessions enhanced user comprehension significantly, engagement strategies remained wanting. Koparan et al.6 researched the effect of hyperparameters on the performance in racetracks and indicated that using Huber loss and an optimized batch size significantly improved the results. Garza-Coello et al.7 documented improvements in prediction accuracy for autonomous driving operations by means of optimizing local and cloud-based training, whereas Cota et al.8 placed great emphasis on practical advantage in terms of employing a combination of local and cloud training for skill development in AI and reinforcement learning. Zhu et al.9 then reduced lap times considerably further by integrating the VNARM framework into the AWS DeepRacer platform.

In their systematic study, Ahmed et al.10 demonstrated that sweeping the hyper parameters of the discount factors and learning rates could severely affect performance of vehicles. All of the works demonstrate the usefulness of AWS DeepRacer as a scalable algorithm in the field of Reinforcement Learning research but also the sensitivity of the model performance to tuning strategies.

Reward function and navigation strategy design

Reward-based shaping of behavior of agents has been a main area of interest in shaping up the behavior. The study of Song et al.11 introduced a multi-objective reward scheme that can be used together with bird-view inputs and a modified PPO algorithm to increase the navigation speed and collision avoidance performance of the autonomous robot in an urban environment. The seminal study by Hu et al.12 combined deep RL with Artificial Potential Fields in a bid to enhance path planning within dynamic environments; to this end, deeper convergence and obstacle avoidance were achieved by the hybrid approach.

In the AWS DeepRacer experiment, Li et al.13 fine-tuned both reward functions and hyperparameters to minimize lap times and the issue of performance indicators versus system limitations was considered. Shi et al.14 also improved the performance of the race with walled tracks in a very realistic high-speed racing environment, in which race completion success rates have increased significantly with the use of the scene graph extraction techniques to improve their environment knowledge recognition. These works testify to the high importance of well-planned reward frameworks in promoting the stability and performance of independently operating driving agents.

Comparative evaluations of DRL algorithms

Comparative analyses of DRL algorithms have provided insights into their suitability for autonomous systems. Muzahid et al.15 compared PPO and SAC for collision avoidance tasks in multi-vehicle environments, finding that both achieved high success rates, with SAC showing advantages in sample efficiency under certain conditions. Zhu et al.16 applied Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) for velocity control, reporting improvements in ride comfort, safety, and efficiency. Capo and Loiacono17 demonstrated the effectiveness of DDPG for trajectory planning in the TORCS simulation environment but noted susceptibility to low-level control issues. Weiss and Behl18 proposed AdmiralNet, a hybrid model combining CNNs, RNNs, and Bézier curves, which outperformed standard baselines for trajectory prediction under high-speed conditions. These comparative studies provide valuable perspectives on the strengths and limitations of DRL approaches in dynamic, competitive environments.

Sim-to-real transfer and real-world validation

Real-world applicability remains a crucial challenge in DRL research. Revell et al.19 investigated sim-to-real transfer issues in autonomous driving, identifying persistent performance gaps between simulated training and real-world deployment. Chu et al.20 similarly addressed sim-to-real transfer for miniature autonomous cars, emphasizing the importance of model simplifications and action-space refinements to bridge this gap.

Although AWS DeepRacer offers a partially physical environment for validation, these studies collectively suggest that ensuring real-world robustness requires further attention to model generalization and environment variability.

Insights into broader RL applications

Beyond AWS DeepRacer, recent work has sought the application of DRL in mobile robot navigation and energy management domains. Kumaar and Kochuvila21 demonstrated that structured state representations and reward functions significantly improve RL-based path planning for mobile service robots. Shivkumar and Nippun Kumaar22, and Ramesh et al.23, explored federated DRL approaches and comparative analyses of traditional RL algorithms (Q-learning, SARSA) with deep methods (DQN), showing improvements in decentralized environments and resource optimization tasks. Gückiran and Bolat24, and Cai et al.25, addressed autonomous racing in simulation environments, validating the feasibility of DRL for real-time navigation, while highlighting challenges related to learning stability and generalization. In robotic manipulation, a recent study by Shivakumar et al.26, applied DRL, specifically, Federated Deep Reinforcement Learning, relying on Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) to train automated movement for strong handling of a task—moving from a random starting point to a specific target. This approach uses federated learning to allow different robot systems to train together and still keep their data private, revealing the rising interest in privacy-preserving ways of learning robotics. Recent research has also highlighted the application of RL in large-scale traffic control systems. Li et al.27 presented a comprehensive survey of urban traffic control in mixed environments with connected automated vehicles, underscoring RL’s effectiveness in optimizing signal coordination and traffic flow. Su et al.28 introduced a hierarchical RL framework for stochastic network traffic, achieving improved stability and responsiveness in multi-intersection control. Building upon these advancements, Liang et al.29 proposed the OAM option-action RL framework, enabling scalable and adaptable control strategies for universal multi-intersection scenarios. These works demonstrate RL’s scalability and generalization potential in complex, real-world transportation networks.

Even though the field has advanced, there are still major issues, mainly related to working with big data, unforeseen situations and inefficient learning. However, learning from these case studies highlights the fact that strong learning, generalization and stability are necessary for using DRL in real situations, but it also illustrates how DRL fits well into areas such as robotics, racing and others.

Research gap

Despite the comprehensive experimental framework and extensive hyperparameter tuning employed in this study, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, all experiments were conducted in a simulated AWS DeepRacer environment, which, while enabling safe and rapid experimentation, cannot fully replicate real-world conditions such as sensor noise, lighting variations, and unpredictable track dynamics. Second, the study evaluated agent performance on a limited set of track layouts, which may restrict generalization to novel or more complex racing environments. Third, no real-world validation was performed, leaving uncertainties about the agents’ behavior under physical constraints or safety-critical scenarios. Finally, the focus on reward system design and hyperparameter optimization, while systematic, does not account for other influential factors such as network architecture variations, multi-agent interactions, or transfer learning, which could further affect agent performance and adaptability.

Proposed methodology

This section summarizes the experimental setup designed to investigate the impact of reward system structuring and hyperparameter optimization on autonomous racing performance. The problem within a standard reinforcement learning framework is first formalized, followed by a detailed description of the environment, agent, state and action spaces, and the reward strategies employed. It is followed by the discussion of the different DRL algorithms employed, the hyperparameter tuning process, and the evaluation step, based on the generalized data obtained from the agent’s performance on the various tracks, to assess the generalization.

Problem formulation

The aim of this work is to train an autonomous racing agent capable of navigating various diverse tracks efficiently and reliably. The agent must learn to complete laps with minimum time and minimal off-track events by optimizing its driving strategy through interaction with a simulated environment. The environment is identified by continuous state space having features such as track width, distance from the centerline, speed, steering angle, and others. The agent selects continuous actions consisting of throttle and steering angle adjustments. Detailed descriptions of the state and action spaces are provided in section C. The agent’s learning is guided by a scalar reward signal provided at each timestep. Two distinct reward strategies are designed in this study to evaluate their effect on learning stability and performance. The overall objective is to identify a policy π(a|s) that maximizes the expected sum of rewards over an episode.

Environment and agent setup

The Agent and the Environment interact within a standard Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework, where, at each timestep, the agent perceives its current state, selects an action, and obtains a corresponding reward. The agent’s goal is to maximize cumulative rewards over an episode. This process is illustrated schematically in Fig. 1.

Reward functions

The reward function provides feedback to the agent on the outcomes of its actions. For this study, two distinct reward systems have been used to compare and highlight the importance of reward systems. The two reward systems are defined as Reward System 1 (R1) and Reward System 2 (R2).

Both reward mechanisms, R1 and R2, reward the agent to drive to the center of the track, drive at the optimal speed, and make efficient progress. Maximum rewards are given when the car is within 10% of the track’s width, and rewards decrease as it gets further out. Speed is also rewarded, with higher speeds getting a multiplicative reward bonus and lower speeds getting a penalty. A progress reward also gives a bonus for sufficient forward progress per step, and a time management penalty prevents wasteful step use. The reward is only given when all wheels are on track, encouraging course conformity. While R1 maximizes speed with these simple principles, R2 adds additional constraints for smoother driving. It penalizes sharp and moderate turns to encourage stability, adds an off-track penalty to venture off track edges, and enforces controlled speed behavior by penalizing excessive speed with sharp steering angles. These improvements in R2 give a more balanced driving policy that balances priorities for speed and stability, leading to improved generalization to diverse track conditions.

Reward System 2 was constructed to explicitly balance speed, stability, and track adherence by combining complementary terms that encourage centerline tracking, efficient forward progress, and controlled steering behavior while penalizing unsafe or unstable actions. Each term was chosen to reflect a driving heuristic that maps directly to desirable agent behavior: proximity to the track center reduces the probability of boundary violations; a progress-per-step bonus encourages efficient forward motion and prevents dithering; multiplicative speed bonuses incentivize maintaining high but safe velocity; steering-dependent penalties discourage sharp steering at high speed and thus reduce rollouts and off-track events; and a small time-management penalty discourages unnecessarily long episodes. The off-track indicator applies to a strong negative signal to discourage leaving the track. Reward System 2 uses multiplicative and additive components together, so the agent learns trade-offs (e.g., moderate speed with stable steering is preferred over maximum speed with large steering corrections). This design explicitly encodes safety–speed trade-offs that standard single-term rewards (e.g., only centerline distance or only lap-time minimization) miss and therefore leads to smoother trajectories and improved generalization across unseen tracks.

Algorithms

This section discusses the algorithms employed in the study. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) have been chosen because of their proven effectiveness in continuous control environments30.

Proximal policy optimization (PPO)

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a reinforcement learning algorithm that builds on policy gradient methods. It was designed to address issues present in earlier algorithms such as Advantage Actor-Critic (A2C), particularly instability and sample inefficiency. The clipped objective function, which limits the update of the policies during training, can be considered the highlight of the PPO. Such a mechanism makes learning more stable and reliable since it is able to maintain a healthy balance between exploration and exploitation. The computational efficiency of the algorithm means that it will be well adapted towards real-time uses and other applications with low-dimensional continuous action space. PPO is an actor-critic algorithm and therefore consists of two modules, the: actor-To select actions according to the existence policy and the critic-To learn the value of the current states based on the policy in use. Through environmental feedback, PPO continuously updates the agent’s behavior. The advantage function helps the agent make better decisions by comparing actual outcomes with expected returns. The clipped objective prevents overfitting to specific actions and maintains training stability. Meanwhile, the critic component evaluates state values, offering essential guidance in dynamic scenarios such as autonomous racing. Soft actor-critic (SAC)

Soft Actor-Critic, or SAC, is a reinforcement learning technique that improves stability and exploration during training. It is built on the maximum entropy framework. SAC encourages a variety of action exploration by optimizing the policy’s entropy, which improves learning, particularly in complicated settings. Because SAC is an off-policy, model-free algorithm, it is more flexible because it does not need an environment model. It employs an actor-critic architecture in which the critic uses the Q-function to assess the value of state-action pairs, and the actor creates a probability distribution over potential actions. By employing two Q-networks, this Q-function reduces the overestimation bias and ensures more consistent learning, which is essential for value-based updates. Also, more stable networks are achieved by using target networks so that the volatility of an update can be lessened. The system architecture of SAC algorithm is described in Fig. 5.

The automated car at work is trained through SAC, applied with the focus on the best decisions and navigation. Effectively utilized by the racetrack, Although the critic evaluates the state-action values to help an agent decide how effective its action is, This is because, during such activities, the actor brings about the probability of what he can do, which controls the course of the vehicle. The emphasis that SAC has devoted to entropy has finally made it possible to test new strategies, which is important when you face unpredictable and changing positions of the race. By lowering overestimation bias and avoiding inconsistent updates, Q-networks and target networks aid in stabilizing the training process and allowing the agent to progressively enhance its performance. By adding these elements, SAC gives the agent greater control over its actions, which improves its track navigation skills, particularly in situations involving competitive, fast-paced racing.

When compared to Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), the SAC method may show some shortcomings in terms of stability and convergence consistency, despite its powerful exploration capabilities. SAC’s training dynamics are more complicated and sensitive to hyperparameter tuning by nature because it is an off-policy technique that depends on replay buffers and numerous value estimations. Entropy overemphasis can result from improper adjustment of the temperature parameter (α\alphaα) or an imbalance between the learning rates of the critic and the actor. This can lead to excessive exploration and inadequate refining of the policy. Multiple critic networks can add to the computational overhead and may cause value estimates to become unstable in the initial stages of training. On the other hand, PPO’s clipped goal function and on-policy formulation limit policy updates inside a trust region, which makes learning more consistent and seamless. Consequently, while SAC often achieves superior long-term performance and adaptability in stochastic environments, PPO generally demonstrates faster convergence, greater training stability, and reduced variance across runs, particularly in structured or less dynamic tasks such as autonomous racing tracks.

Hyperparameter tuning strategy

In this study, hyperparameter tuning was conducted systematically within the operational constraints of the AWS DeepRacer platform. Since AWS DeepRacer’s training console restricts hyperparameter tuning to a fixed set of predefined options, a combination was performed across all permissible values. Specifically, the gradient descent batch size was varied among {32, 64, 128, 256, 512}, the number of epochs per update was constrained to integer values between 3 and 10, and the learning rate was tuned within the range of 1e −8–1e −3. The discount factor (γ) was explored within 0.0 to 1.0, while the PPO-specific entropy regularization coefficient and SAC’s entropy coefficient (α) were confined to the 0.0–1.0 interval. Additionally, the loss function could only be chosen between Huber and Mean Squared Error (MSE), and the experience buffer size per update was limited to integer values between 5 and 100. Given these restrictions, a full Bayesian or k-fold validation approach was not feasible. Instead, Batch size was varied among (64, 128, 512), learning rates evaluated included (0.0003, 0.0009, 0.001), discount factors included the range (0.85, 0.90, 0.99), and entropy coefficients (0.005, 0.01, 0.02). Each configuration was evaluated over five independent trials, and the final combination—batch size = 128, learning rate = 0.0003, discount factor = 0.99, and entropy coefficient = 0.01—was selected for achieving optimal stability, convergence, and generalization on unseen tracks. This process ensured a reproducible and efficient exploration of the available hyperparameter space while adhering to platform limitations.

Training and evaluation

Training was performed for 2 hours for each algorithm and for each hyperparameter. Every hyperparameter-tuned model’s performance was compared based on their average lap times and average number of off-track events to determine the best combination. Performance of each model was evaluated through a number of trials in order to determine accurate results. In AWS DeepRacer, the service limits evaluation trials to a maximum of five runs for each model configuration. In the interest of fairness and evenness, the final inference of each model was determined by computing the mean lap time and off-track collisions in these five trials. This minimizes the effects of the occurrence of outlier laps where the agent might have been affected by unlucky breakdowns or experienced a lucky non-typical performance run as a result of environmental accidents. The results of the models were measured in two main parameters, i.e., the Average Lap Time (Seconds) and the Off-Track Events. Average Lap Time (seconds) indicates the time a single lap on the track takes the autonomous agent. Smaller lap time represents a better and optimum driving strategy. Off-Track Events are the number of times that an agent violates the track boundaries. Reduction in off track events means greater consistency and control. This comprehensive setup enables a rigorous evaluation of the interplay between reward design, hyperparameter sensitivity, and agent generalization performance.

Summary of results

The experimental results show that the design of the reward system and systematic hyperparameter tuning play pivotal roles in enhancing the performance of DRL agents in autonomous racing tasks. Among the evaluated models, PPO trained with Reward System 2 and optimized hyperparameters (batch size = 128, learning rate = 0.0003, discount factor = 0.99, entropy = 0.01) achieved the best performance, with an average lap time of 12.5948 seconds and a significant reduction in off-track events. Furthermore, generalization tests conducted across 21 unseen tracks confirmed the robustness and adaptability of the tuned model, achieving competitive lap times with minimal deviations. Although the tuned PPO-R2 model won in most of the tracks, it also came with a varied performance, extensively due to the length and complexity of few tracks, but guaranteeing a promising overall result. These findings emphasize that careful reward engineering and hyperparameter sensitivity analysis are critical for achieving scalable and transferable generalized DRL solutions in autonomous navigation domains.

Conclusion and future scope

This study systematically evaluated the effect of reward system design and hyperparameter optimization on autonomous racing performance within the AWS DeepRacer platform, comparing Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithms. The results demonstrated that PPO, when trained with optimized hyperparameters (batch size = 128, learning rate = 0.0003, discount factor = 0.99, and entropy = 0.01) and the carefully engineered Reward System 2, achieved superior performance by minimizing lap times (12.59 s) and off-track events (1 off-track event) on the Reinvent 2018 track. Furthermore, generalization tests across 21 unseen tracks confirmed the robustness of the tuned model, underscoring the importance of structured reward engineering and hyperparameter tuning in developing scalable Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) solutions for autonomous navigation.

Since the AWS DeepRacer framework currently provides only two built-in DRL algorithms—PPO and SAC, future work would aim to extend this study by integrating and evaluating additional algorithms such as Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3) and Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) to further enhance adaptability and performance. Moreover, a broader comparison of hyperparameters, including network architecture depth, target update rates, and exploration coefficients, could be conducted to better understand their impact on model stability and convergence. These research directions will help close the gap between simulated training and real-world deployment, advancing the broader domain of scalable, reliable, and transferable DRL applications in autonomous systems.
