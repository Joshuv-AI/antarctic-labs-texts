Comparative Efficacy of Semaglutide and Tirzepatide in Chinese Adults with Obesity without Diabetes: A Real-World Observational Study
Authors: Zhangwaner Yao, Arshman Ahmad, Faping Gong, Nan Zhang, Menghua Zeng, Qian Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo conduct a direct comparison of the effects of Semaglutide and Tirzepatide among adults with obesity but without diabetes.MethodsFrom January 2024 to February 2026, a total of 183 Chinese adults were enrolled from the obesity clinic at our hospital in a single-center, retrospective observational cohort study. All participants received either once-weekly Semaglutide (1.7mg) or Tirzepatide (5mg) for a duration of three months. The primary outcome was the change in body weight from baseline to month 3. Secondary outcomes included changes in visceral fat mass, appetite, and key metabolic parameters. Body weight, waist circumference, body fat composition, and metabolic parameters were assessed at baseline and at follow-up. Eating behaviors and appetite were evaluated using the Control of Eating Questionnaire (CoEQ) and the Simplified Nutritional Appetite Questionnaire (SNAQ).ResultsBoth treatments yielded clinically meaningful improvements in body weight, fat mass, and metabolic parameters. Tirzepatide was associated with greater reductions in body weight [−2.87 kg (95% CI, −4.41 to −1.32), p < 0.001)] and higher proportion of patients achieving more than 10% weight loss [21.50% (95% CI, 7.45 to 35.55), p = 0.003]. Moreover, Tirzepatide demonstrated superior efficacy in reducing total fat mass and visceral adiposity, achieving greater total weight loss (TWL%) and excess weight loss (EWL%) percentages, and ameliorating insulin resistance. By contrast, the effects on food cravings and appetite regulation were comparable between the two medications. The overall safety profiles were generally similar between groups.ConclusionTirzepatide demonstrates superior efficacy to Semaglutide in obesity management among Chinese adults. Larger-scale and longer-term studies based on real-world clinical practice are warranted to validate and generalize these findings.Keywords: obesity, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide receptor, observational study

Introduction

In recent years, obesity has been recognized as a chronic metabolic disease and has emerged as one of the most significant global public health challenges. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), over one billion individuals worldwide are affected by obesity, encompassing adults, children, and adolescents.1–3 In China, it is projected that over 70% of adults will become overweight/obese by 2030.4 Obesity is linked with various health complications and increases the risk of developing conditions such as type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and even certain types of cancer,5–8 largely due to increased insulin resistance and chronic low-grade inflammation.9 These complications often lead to higher medical expenses, which place a financial burden on families and create additional strain on healthcare systems.10 In addition, obesity reduces productivity and affects overall quality of life, resulting in both medical and economic challenges for families and society.11

The first-line approach to managing obesity involves lifestyle modification, including increased physical activity and dietary improvements. However, many patients fail to achieve clinically meaningful weight loss through lifestyle changes alone. Because of this, pharmacological therapy has become an important part of obesity treatment.12 Semaglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R) agonist, promotes satiety, suppresses appetite, and enhances metabolic control, leading to significant weight reduction.13–15 Tirzepatide, a dual GLP-1R and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide receptor (GIPR) agonist, has demonstrated even greater efficacy in promoting weight loss, as evidenced by the randomized controlled trial (RCT) SURMOUNT-5, which showed that Tirzepatide achieved a mean weight reduction of 20.2% compared to 13.7% with Semaglutide at 72 weeks.16 A recent narrative review also highlighted that Tirzepatide produces substantial weight loss and improvements in cardiometabolic parameters among individuals with obesity, supporting its growing role as a pharmacological option for weight management.17

Although large-scale RCTs, including the STEP and SURMOUNT series, have consistently demonstrated the effectiveness of both agent,13,18,19 direct comparative evidence between these two agents derived from real-world clinical settings remains limited—particularly among East Asian populations, who bear a disproportionately increasing burden of obesity and for whom more effective anti-obesity medications are urgently needed. Given the distinct genetic backgrounds, body composition and dietary patterns unique to East Asian individuals, existing Western-dominated data cannot be fully generalized to this cohort. This critical evidence gap necessitates further real-world comparative research to optimize pharmacological treatment strategies tailored to East Asian obese populations. Moreover, appetite suppression is a mechanistically critical pathway for weight reduction; however, the SURMOUNT-5 trial was not designed to directly compare the effects of the two medications on appetite regulation. Furthermore, current real-world studies have primarily focused on changes in body weight, leaving the effects on appetite regulation, fat distribution, and broader metabolic improvements uncertain.

Therefore, our study aimed to evaluate the therapeutic effects of Semaglutide and Tirzepatide in adults with obesity but without diabetes who received treatment in the obesity clinic at our hospital. We compared changes in body weight, appetite, and metabolic parameters following 3 months of treatment.

Material and Methods

Study Subjects

This is a single-center, retrospective observational cohort study conducted in accordance with the STROBE statement. Data were extracted from electronic medical records and prospectively collected clinical data obtained from the obesity clinic of our hospital. Between January 2024 and February 2026, a total of 183 Han Chinese patients with non-diabetic obesity were recruited. Obesity was defined as a body mass index (BMI) of ≥28 kg/m2, based on the Guidelines for Prevention and Control of Overweight and Obesity in Chinese Adults.20 Eligible participants met all of the following inclusion criteria: (i) aged 20–60 years; (ii) new users of Tirzepatide or Semaglutide, with no prior use of any GLP-1 receptor agonist; (iii) no use of other weight-loss medications or agents known to affect body weight (eg, corticosteroids, antipsychotics) during the preceding three months; and (iv) absence of diagnosed anxious depression as psychiatric conditions and their treatments may influence appetite, eating behaviors, and weight-related outcomes. Exclusion criteria included: (i) inability to maintain medication adherence for at least three consecutive months; (ii) moderate or severe renal or hepatic dysfunction; and (iii) abnormalities in the function of the pituitary-thyroid and adrenal axis.

Sample Size

The sample size was calculated prospectively using preliminary clinical data, specifically based on a two-sample independent t-test for superiority: This formula was calculated based on our preliminary study observed body weight loss data from treatments with Semaglutide or Tirzepatide. The standard deviation (SD) in each group was assumed to be 4 kg, and the expected difference in weight loss between the two groups (∆) was 2.5 kg (a clinical meaningful weight loss). A two-sided test was used with a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a statistical power of 85% (1−β = 0.85). The anticipated dropout rate (δ) was set at 20%. The estimated sample size was at least 55 participants for each group.

Treatment Allocation and Interventions

Treatment allocation was determined by physicians according to clinical guidelines, patient characteristics, and drug availability during the study period.

Subjects received either Semaglutide or Tirzepatide administered subcutaneously once weekly. The initial dose of Semaglutide was 0.5 mg for two weeks, followed by an increase to 1.0 mg for an additional two weeks. For Tirzepatide, the starting dose was 2.5 mg, maintained for one month. All subjects returned for a follow-up visit at one month after initiation of treatment, at which time the doses were escalated to 1.7 mg for Semaglutide and 5 mg for Tirzepatide, respectively, for a duration of two months. At three months after treatment initiation, subjects returned for a subsequent evaluation. Medication adherence and lifestyle intervention were assessed at the 1- and 3-month follow-up visits through face-to-face interviews and documented in the electronic medical record. During the treatment period, all subjects were required to adhere to a lifestyle intervention consisting of caloric restriction [daily intake < (height in cm − 105) × 20 kcal], balanced nutrition (carbohydrates: 40–55%, protein: 15–20%, and fat: 20–30% of total daily calories), and engagement in at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity physical activity per week.

Eating Behavior and Appetite Assessment

Eating behavior and appetite were assessed using the validated questionnaires: the Control of Eating Questionnaire (CoEQ), which contains items 1–20, and the Simplified Nutrition Appetite Questionnaire (SNAQ), respectively (see Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Questionnaires were administered prior to treatment initiation and at the 3-month follow-up visit. Response options, labeled from “a” to “e”, were assigned numerical scores ranging from 1 to 5. The total scores and individual item scores were calculated, with higher values reflecting poorer control over eating behavior and greater appetite. All questionnaires were completed by participants under the supervision of one trained nurse.

Clinical and Biochemical Measurements

The anthropometric measurements, including body weight, height, waist circumference, and body fat distribution, were obtained while participants wore light clothing and were barefoot, using a standardized body composition analyzer (IOI353, Jawon, Korea). All measurements were performed by a trained nurse following standardized, protocol-specified procedures. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m2). Blood pressure (BP) was measured on the non-dominant arm after participants had rested seated for 10 minutes, using an automated electronic sphygmomanometer (OMRON BP-203RVIIIC, Matsusaka, Japan).

Blood was drawn after overnight fast and biochemical parameters were checked using the standard laboratory methods at the Central Laboratory and Endocrine Laboratory of our hospital. The same equipment and laboratory protocols were used consistently throughout the study period. Insulin was measured by the electrochemiluminescence (Cobas e601, Roche, Germany). Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1C) was estimated by high-pressure liquid chromatography using an analyzer (Premier HB-9210, Primus Corporation, America). Serum lipids were measured as follows: total cholesterol (TC) using the cholesterol oxidase-HDAOS method (Wako, Osaka, Japan); triglycerides (TG) using the GPO-HDAOS Glycerol blanking method (Wako); high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL) using the immunoinhibition (direct) method (Wako) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL) using the selective protection enzymatic (direct) method (Wako). Glutamic-pyruvictransaminase (ALT), Glutamicoxalacetictransaminase (AST), Creatinine (Cr) and Uric acid (UA) were measured using enzymatic methods on the autoanalyzer (HITACHI 7180, Ichige, Japan). The Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated as fasting glucose (mmol/L) × fasting insulin (μIU/mL)/22.5, using paired fasting glucose and insulin measurements obtained from the same blood sample for each participant.

Safety Assessment

Safety status was assessed at the first and third months of follow-up. Adverse events (AEs) evaluated by physician, including gastrointestinal AEs (eg, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and constipation) and psychiatric AEs (eg, anxiety, depression, and insomnia), were recorded in electronic medical records through face-to-face interviews.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5). Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Prior to treatment, comparisons of means for continuous variables between groups were performed using the unpaired t test or Mann–Whitney test (when the data were not normally distributed). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variable comparisons were assessed using chi-square (χ2) tests. The changes in body weight and body composition from baseline between the two groups were compared using the unpaired t-test with confidence intervals (CIs). Following treatment, to avoid confounding by baseline values, the intergroup differences were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (two-way ANOVA) followed by post-hoc testing with adjusted p-values. Exact p-values are reported and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The Baseline Characteristics of Subjects

A total of 183 participants were enrolled—86 assigned to the Semaglutide group and 97 to the Tirzepatide group—according to their allocated treatment. As shown in Table 1, the baseline characteristics of the two subject groups were comparable. Specifically, mean age was 30.74 ± 8.52 years in the Semaglutide group versus 32.90 ± 7.71 years in the Tirzepatide group; mean BMI was 32.55 ± 3.40 kg/m2 versus 31.82 ± 3.52 kg/m2; and mean body weight was 88.52 ± 13.39 kg versus 86.77 ± 15.25 kg (all p > 0.05). No significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of demographic variables—including age, sex, body weight, and fat distribution—or metabolic parameters such as blood glucose levels, insulin resistance, renal function, liver function, and lipid profile. Furthermore, eating behavior and appetite status were similar in both groups.

The Outcomes Following Semaglutide and Tirzepatide Treatment

After three months of treatment, both Semaglutide and Tirzepatide led to clinically meaningful improvements in body weight, fat mass, and metabolism. The mean change in body weight from baseline (∆ body weight) was significantly different, with a reduction of 8.15 kg (95% CI, 7.23 to 9.07) in the Semaglutide group and a reduction of 11.02 kg (95% CI, 9.76 to 12.27) in the Tirzepatide group (Figure 1a). Therefore, Tirzepatide demonstrated a 2.87 kg (95% CI, 1.32 to 4.41, p < 0.001) greater reduction in body weight compared to Semaglutide, along with a 21.50% (95% CI, 7.45 to 35.55, p = 0.003) higher proportion of patients achieving more than 10% weight loss (Figure 1b). Moreover, Tirzepatide showed superior efficacy over Semaglutide in reducing waist circumference (−2.62 cm, 95% CI, −4.40 to −0.84, p = 0.004), total fat mass (−1.83 kg, 95% CI, −2.96 to −0.71, p = 0.002), fat mass percentage (−1.32%, 95% CI, −2.13 to −0.51, p = 0.002), visceral fat mass (−1.07 kg, 95% CI, −1.60 to −0.53, p < 0.001), and visceral fat area (−9.89 cm2, 95% CI, −16.24 to −3.53, p = 0.003) (Figure 2a–f). Furthermore, the total weight loss percentage (TWL%) and excess weight loss percentage (EWL%) were significantly greater in the Tirzepatide group compared with the Semaglutide group, indicating a superior therapeutic efficacy of Tirzepatide in the management of obesity.

Moreover, Semaglutide and Tirzepatide demonstrated comparable effects on modulating food cravings and appetite, as quantified by the Control of Eating Questionnaire (CoEQ) and the Simplified Nutritional Appetite Questionnaire (SNAQ) (Table 2).

Finally, no significant differences were observed in the risk of gastrointestinal AEs (17.40% vs 19.60%, p = 0.429) or psychiatric AEs (4.70% vs 3.10%, p = 0.433) between patients receiving Semaglutide and those receiving Tirzepatide. No serious AEs occurred during the treatment period in either group. No treatment discontinuations due to adverse events were reported in either group.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the efficacy of Semaglutide and Tirzepatide in patients with obesity and without diabetes treated at our obesity clinic. Our results demonstrated that Tirzepatide was superior to Semaglutide in promoting weight loss, improving insulin resistance, and achieving eating behavior control. Although the doses of Tirzepatide (5mg/week) administered in our study were lower than those of Semaglutide (1.7 mg/week) compared to other studies, our findings remain consistent with findings from the SURMOUNT-5 trial,16 where Tirzepatide (10–15 mg/week) demonstrated greater reductions in body weight, waist circumference, and insulin resistance compared to Semaglutide (1.7–2.4 mg/week).16 To the best of our knowledge, currently only four real-world studies conducted in the United states (US) or the United kingdom (UK) have directly compared the weight-loss effects of Semaglutide and Tirzepatide in non-diabetic individuals with overweight or obesity.21–24 However, the reported outcomes remain inconsistent, with one study showing comparable efficacy between the two agents, while other studies including ours indicate superior outcomes with Tirzepatide treatment.25–27 This discrepancy may be attributed to imbalanced dosing regimens between the groups. In the study that failed to detect significant differences, participants received 2.4 mg of Semaglutide versus variable doses of Tirzepatide (ranging from 5 mg to 15 mg).

In addition, consistent with SURMOUNT-5 trial, our study demonstrated a significantly greater reduction in total and visceral fat mass in the Tirzepatide treatment group compared to the Semaglutide group. This more substantial reduction may explain the greater decrease in body weight and insulin resistance observed in the Tirzepatide group, even when Tirzepatide is administered at a lower dose compared to Semaglutide. Our findings further confirm the functional actions of GIPR in human and mouse adipocytes. Tirzepatide is an imbalanced agonist favoring GIPR over GLP-1R activity. Its binding affinity for GIPR is comparable to that of native GIP, whereas its affinity for GLP-1R is approximately five-fold lower than that of native GLP-1.28 In contrast to GLP-1R, GIPR is abundantly expressed and functionally active on adipocytes. Studies have shown that GIPR differentially regulates adipocyte function in both fasting and fed states. In the fed state, GIPR agonism enhances dietary lipid clearance into adipocytes for storage by increasing lipoprotein lipase (LPL) expression and activity, without promoting adiposity or weight gain, thereby contributing to improved lipid metabolism and reduced insulin resistance. In the fasting state, GIPR agonism promotes lipid mobilization from adipocytes by upregulating key lipolytic enzymes, including hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL) and fatty acid binding protein 4 (FABP4). Similar to the findings obtained under fasting conditions, the combination of GIPR and GLP-1R agonism induced by Tirzepatide leads to a more pronounced enhancement of lipolysis compared to GIPR agonism alone. This is due to a greater suppression of food intake caused by the co-activation of GLP-1 and GIP receptors.29 Moreover, it has been demonstrated that GIPR agonism increased energy expenditure through futile calcium cycling in adipocytes.30 The mechanistic framework of GIPR activation provides a plausible explanation for the enhanced fat mass reduction observed with Tirzepatide.

However, a recent real-world study reported that Semaglutide demonstrated superior efficacy over Tirzepatide in reducing the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) among adults with overweight/obesity but without diabetes. Semaglutide was associated with a significant 29% and 22% reductions in the risk of 3-point MACE and 5-point MACE, respectively, compared with Tirzepatide.31 The superior cardiovascular benefits of Semaglutide are attributable to its high binding affinity for GLP-1R and the generation of metabolically stable fragments, including GLP-1(28–36)amide, that retain structural and functional similarity to nature GLP-1.32 Importantly, these effects are independent of the magnitude of weight loss.

From a clinical perspective, our findings suggest that Tirzepatide may be particularly suitable for patients with more severe visceral adiposity and insulin resistance. Furthermore, maintaining calorie restriction during treatment is recommended, as it may enhance therapeutic benefits through the mechanistic role of GIPR in the state of nutritional deprivation. However, Semaglutide remains a highly effective and widely used pharmacotherapy for obesity–particularly in individuals with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) or those at high risk for ASCVD.

Our study has several strengths, including the use of real-world patient data, comprehensive assessment of fat distribution, and application of validated instruments such as CoEQ and SNAQ. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective observational design introduces potential confounding and treatment allocation bias, as treatment assignment was not randomized but determined by physician discretion. Second, the relatively small sample size and short 3-month follow-up duration limit our ability to assess long-term efficacy and safety. Third, because this was a single-center study, the generalizability of our findings may be limited. Finally, we did not adjust for several potential confounders—including socioeconomic status and detailed dietary patterns. Accordingly, larger-scale, longer-term, and multicenter observational studies are needed to validate these findings in diverse populations. Additionally, targeted education to address obesity-related bias and promote a balanced evaluation of the benefits and risks of GLP-1 medications for weight management is warranted, particularly in regions where perceptions toward these therapies may influence their clinical adoption.33

Conclusions

In conclusion, our real-world study demonstrates that Tirzepatide exhibits superior efficacy compared with Semaglutide in the management of obesity, as reflected by significantly greater reductions in body weight, visceral adiposity, and insulin resistance. Our findings in the Chinese patient population may or may not be generalizable to the world at large.
