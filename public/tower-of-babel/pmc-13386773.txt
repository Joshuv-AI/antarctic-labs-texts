Personalized therapeutic platform: organoid models for hepatic malignancies
Authors: Ziyan Zhou, Tingting Ma, Ning Tang, Hongping Zheng, Lingting Wang, Siyuan Liu, Xiaotong Lu, Baorui Liu, Xinhua Zhu, Liang Qi, Jie Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient responses to therapeutic agents in hepatic malignancies are highly variable, yet the field lacks robust in vitro models for predicting individual drug responsiveness. Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) that faithfully recapitulate the biological characteristics of parental tumors offer a promising platform for personalized drug testing.MethodsWe established PDOs from fresh tumor tissues of 29 patients with hepatic malignancies, achieving a success rate of 79.31% (23/29). Histological staining confirmed high morphological and phenotypic consistency between PDOs and original tumor tissues. Drug sensitivity testing was performed using a panel of standard-of-care therapeutics, and model stability was assessed through serial passaging and cryopreservation. Clinical correlation was evaluated by comparing PDO-predicted sensitivity with observed treatment responses in 16 patients followed prospectively.ResultsThe established PDOs maintained structural and functional fidelity to parental tumors and demonstrated reproducible drug sensitivity profiles across serial passaging and cryopreservation. In a prospective clinical validation, patients whose treatment matched PDO-predicted sensitive agents showed significantly improved objective response rate (ORR: 36.36%, 95% CI: 10.93–69.21) and disease control rate (DCR: 90.91%, 95% CI: 58.72–99.77), compared to those receiving PDO-predicted resistant therapies (ORR: 0%, DCR: 20%).ConclusionThis study establishes a robust and clinically validated PDO platform for hepatic malignancies that accurately predicts patient-specific drug responses. The model demonstrates high reproducibility and stability, supporting its utility for guiding personalized therapy selection and improving outcomes in patients with liver cancer.Keywords: Hepatic malignancies, Organoids, Drug sensitivity, Personalized treatment

Background

Hepatic malignancies can be classified into primary liver cancer (PLC) and metastatic liver cancer. PLC itself comprises three distinct pathological types: Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), and combined hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma (cHCC-CCA/CHC). When formulating anti-tumor treatment plans for patients, clinicians typically refer to relevant guidelines or select therapeutic agents based on their own experience. However, the recommended regimens in guidelines are based on clinical studies with specific sample sizes. This traditional, non-personalized treatment model can result in a subset of patients not benefiting from the guideline-recommended regimens, which is a key obstacle hindering the development of precision and personalized medicine. If efficient drug screening models could be used to identify patient-specific sensitive drugs, it would not only improve treatment efficacy and reduce patient trial-and-error costs but also address the developmental challenges in precision therapy caused by tumor heterogeneity [1].

Currently, in vitro cancer research predominantly utilizes cell line models and mouse models. Cell line models are two-dimensional systems that can retain the genetic characteristics of parental tissue relatively well. But they often fail to recapitulate certain critical histological and morphological features that tumors acquire during development [2]. Mouse models can circumvent some limitations inherent to cell lines. However, they are hampered by species differences, which can lead to discrepancies between pre-clinical and clinical trial outcomes. Furthermore, their cumbersome modeling procedures and high costs are often criticized by researchers. In recent years, a novel research model has effectively addressed these issues: organoids.

Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) are three-dimensional in vitro structures. They form tissues not only highly similar in structure and function to the parental tissue but also exhibiting consistent histological and genetic characteristics [2]. Therefore, when derived from tumor tissue, the resulting PDOs will maintain high consistency with the original tumor and can thus function as representative in vitro models for drug sensitivity testing. Compared to traditional cell line and mouse models, PDOs not only retain the advantages of both but also offer benefits such as easier establishment, maintenance, and long-term preservation [3, 4]. In recent years, PDOs have been successfully established from a range of solid tumors, including liver cancer [2], pancreatic cancer [5], breast cancer [6], colorectal cancer [7], and prostate cancer [8]. These studies demonstrate that PDOs can highly preserve the morphological and genetic features of the parental tumor tissue and maintain stability during passaging culture. This reliability highlights the application value of PDOs as a novel in vitro model.

We aim to establish hepatic malignancy PDOs for drug sensitivity testing to identify potentially effective drugs. By subsequently correlating these results with patient clinical outcomes, we will verify the accuracy and feasibility of PDOs in predicting drug sensitivity, hoping to provide guidance for the personalized treatment of cancer patients.

Methods

Sample collection

Tumor tissue sample collection was performed after enrolled patients had completed required clinical assessments, including medical history collection, physical examination, and imaging studies (CT/MRI). For patients undergoing surgical tumor resection, tissue samples were obtained from both primary and metastatic sites whenever possible. Sampling preferentially targeted areas near the lesion margin with minimal necrosis. Collected tissues were sectioned into small pieces (approximate 2 mm diameter). For non-surgical patients, samples were obtained via core needle biopsy. Biopsies were performed ensuring patient safety, with a minimum of two tissue cores collected. The collected samples were rinsed and promptly placed in centrifuge tubes containing preservation solution, ensuring the solution fully submerged the samples. Tubes were stored at 0 ~ 8 °C, and PDO establishment was initiated as soon as possible. All samples were assigned identifiers following the format IHCC-XXX. The same identifier referred to both the original sample and its derived PDOs, with suffixes added when necessary for distinction.

Drug sensitivity testing in PDOs

PDOs were collected using Gibco 1× TrypLE and dissociated into small cell clusters. Subsequently, 50–200 cell clusters were seeded into a 384-well plate. After 48 h, serially diluted test drugs were added. Prior to the formal experiments, a pre-test was conducted on the PDOs. In this pre-test, a relatively high Maximum concentration (Cmax) was set, and a 10-fold serial dilution was used for the concentration gradients to screen for an appropriate concentration range. Based on the pre-test results, the Cmax for each drug in formal experiments was appropriately reduced, and the concentration gradients were set using 5-fold serial dilutions.Each drug was tested at six different concentrations (Table 1), with three technical replicates for each concentration, alongside blank control wells. The selection of drug concentrations considered the theoretical Peak Plasma Concentration (PPC), spanning an interval covering 3–4 orders of magnitude.

Drug concentration gradient

After 72h of drug exposure, cell optical density(OD) value in each well was measured using the Adenosine Triphosphate Bioluminescence-based Tumor Chemosensitivity Assay(ATP-TCA). The cell growth inhibition rate for each drug concentration group was calculated using the following formula: Inhibition Rate (%) = [(OD value of control group - OD value of drug-treated group) / OD value of control group] × 100%.

To normalize drug concentration values, the concentration (C) of each gradient was expressed as a ratio of its Cmax. A logarithmic transformation was then applied to visualize the relationship between drug concentration and the cell growth inhibition rate. Data were processed using Origin 2022 to generate dose-response fitting curves. The corresponding fitting equations were used to calculate the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) and the Area Under the Curve (AUC) score.

In this study, 25% of the PPC was set as the cutoff point. The drug sensitivity prediction result was classified as sensitive if the IC50 value was below the cutoff point, and resistant if the IC50 value exceeded the cutoff point.

Study flow

Patients participating in the study provided voluntary signed informed consent after fully understanding the project details. Tumor tissue samples were obtained from patients via surgery or biopsy, and PDOs were cultured and established in vitro. The following six drugs were used for drug sensitivity testing on the PDOs: Oxaliplatin, Irinotecan, 5-Fu, Gemcitabine, Cetuximab, and Lenvatinib. Concurrently, patients received standard anti-tumor therapy as guided by their clinicians and underwent regular imaging examinations to evaluate treatment response. The treatment process will be carried out simultaneously with the construction of PDOs and drug testing, and the two will not interfere with each other. Finally, the drug sensitivity results were compared with the clinical efficacy to assess the accuracy and feasibility of PDOs as a predictive model. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital (Approval Number: 2023-606-01). The specific workflow is shown in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of the study. Tumor samples were collected from 29 patients, and 23 PDO lines were successfully established. Among the patients corresponding to these 23 PDO lines, 16 received the prescribed therapy and underwent efficacy evaluation

Treatment and efficacy evaluation

All patients underwent baseline examinations prior to initiating treatment and received anti-tumor therapy under the guidance of clinicians as soon as possible after the completion of sampling. For patients receiving first-line therapy, drug selection and dosage followed clinical guideline recommendations. Treatment regimens for patients with multiple prior lines of therapy were determined based on guideline recommendations combined with the clinical experience of senior physicians. During the treatment period, patients underwent regular imaging reassessments as clinically required by their physicians. Treatment efficacy was evaluated according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 (RECIST v1.1). The efficacy evaluation endpoints in this study included: Complete Response (CR), Partial Response (PR), Stable Disease (SD), Progressive Disease (PD), Objective Response Rate (ORR), and Disease Control Rate (DCR).

Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

Collected tumor tissue samples and PDOs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, followed by standard processing through an ethanol gradient for dehydration, xylene clearing, and paraffin embedding. Continuous sections of 4–5 μm thickness were prepared using a microtome. At least seven sections were prepared from each paraffin block: one for H&E staining and the remainder for IHC staining. All sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series.

For H&E staining, sections were stained with Harris’s hematoxylin for 5 min, differentiated in 1% acid alcohol, blued in ammonia water, and counterstained with 1% Eosin Y solution for 1 min. Finally, sections were dehydrated through an ethanol gradient, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam.

For IHC staining, Heat-Induced Epitope Retrieval (HIER) was performed first to expose antigen epitopes. Two different retrieval buffers were used depending on the target antigen characteristics: EDTA buffer (pH 8.0) for CK7, CK19, Hept1, and GPC3 antigens, and citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for the Ki-67 antigen. After retrieval, sections were allowed to cool naturally to room temperature. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubating sections with 3% hydrogen peroxide solution at room temperature for 15 min. Non-specific binding sites were blocked with normal goat serum for 30 min. After blocking, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber with the following specific mouse-derived monoclonal primary antibodies: anti-CK7 (1:150, Zhongshan Golden Bridge, Beijing), anti-CK19 (1:150, Zhongshan Golden Bridge, Beijing), anti-Hept1 (1:150, Zhongshan Golden Bridge, Beijing), anti-GPC3 (1:200, Maixin, Fuzhou), and anti-Ki-67 (1:250, Dako). The next day, sections were warmed to room temperature, incubated with a biotin-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by incubation with Streptavidin-Biotin Complex (SABC) at 37 °C for 30 min. Between each step, sections were washed thoroughly three times for 5 min each with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). Color development was performed using a freshly prepared 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate working solution, monitored microscopically in real-time, and stopped by rinsing with distilled water. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin, differentiated in acid alcohol, and blued in ammonia water. Finally, sections were dehydrated through an ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and corresponding graph generation were performed using Prism 10. Categorical data were described as percentages (number of cases). The normality of all continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test (α = 0.05). As the number of viable cells in the successful versus failed PDO modeling groups did not follow a normal distribution, the Mann-Whitney test (α = 0.05) was used for intergroup comparison, and box plots were employed to identify outliers. For correlation analyses, all involved data met the normality assumption, hence Pearson correlation analysis (α = 0.05) was applied. All statistical tests mentioned above were two-sided, and P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Origin 2022 was used to process cell viability inhibition rate data, generate dose-response fitting curves, and calculate the IC50 and AUC Score based on the corresponding fitting equations.

Consistency comparison

Consistency evaluation is obtained by comparing the actual clinical efficacy of patients with the PDOs drug sensitivity prediction results. The cases we consider consistent include: the efficacy evaluation is CR, PR, or SD, and the PDOs drug sensitivity prediction result is sensitive; the efficacy evaluation is PD, and the PDOs drug sensitivity prediction result is resistant. Conversely, the clinical efficacy is inconsistent with the PDOs drug sensitivity prediction result.

Results

Establishment of a PDO library

To establish a PDO library, we collected tumor tissue samples from 29 patients and successfully generated 23 PDO lines, yielding a success rate of 79.31%. Among the patients corresponding to these 23 PDO lines, 16 received treatment as required and underwent efficacy evaluation. We categorized the patients according to enrollment, successful PDO generation, and follow-up, and compiled the corresponding clinical data for each group, including gender, pathological type, differentiation degree, and stage (Fig. 2).

Our established PDO library encompassed various hepatic malignancies, most of which were PLCs (ICC and HCC), accounting for 65.21% (15/23) of the lines. The library also included models derived from metastatic liver cancers, such as gallbladder carcinoma (GBC) and colorectal carcinoma (CRC) liver metastases. The six failed generation attempts spanned multiple subtypes (2 ICC, 3 GBC, 1 CRC), suggesting that modeling failure was due to factors other than the specific pathological subtype. The establishment of PDOs begins with the isolation and extraction of tumor cells from the sample, followed by their subsequent culture. To investigate potential reasons for culture failure, we quantified the number of tumor cells extracted from the samples. Among the six failed cases, two showed no cell residue after enzymatic digestion, precluding subsequent culture steps. Consequently, we speculate that the number of viable cells in the starting sample may be a critical factor influencing modeling success. This finding holds significant importance for our subsequent efforts to establish a larger-scale PDO library for hepatic malignancies.

Assessment of PDO quality

We documented the growth process of tumor cells in the organoid complete medium using bright-field imaging (Fig. 3B). As the culture duration increased, there was a significant increase in cell number, and the structures progressively formed into PDOs that exhibited a three-dimensional, spherical morphology with good translucency and no significant central black necrosis.

In addition to evaluating the microscopic morphology of the PDOs, we performed H&E staining and IHC staining on both the original tumor tissue samples and their corresponding PDOs (Fig. 3C) to further assess the quality of the established models. In H&E-stained sections, cells within the PDOs were arranged in structures that were nearly round and nest-like, with central lumens. In contrast, cells in the original tumor tissue samples exhibited a dense packing pattern, forming irregular nests, with some containing clear areas. The morphological characteristics between the two were highly similar. Concurrently, IHC staining demonstrated that the expression patterns of the five tumor markers (CK7, CK19, Hept1, GPC3, and Ki-67) in the PDOs were highly consistent with those observed in the original tumor tissues. This confirms that the key phenotypic features and proliferative activity of the original tumors were faithfully recapitulated in the corresponding PDOs. In summary, we conclude that the PDOs established in this study largely preserve the genetic heterogeneity of the parental tumor tissues and can replicate their morphological architecture and histological characteristics. This provides a robust foundation for utilizing these PDOs as predictive models for drug sensitivity testing.

Drug sensitivity testing

Following successful establishment, PDOs were dissociated into small cell clusters and seeded into plates for subsequent drug sensitivity testing (Day 0). After 48 h of culture, microscopic examination revealed that the cell clusters had reformed into PDOs of considerable size (Day 2). At this point, serially diluted test drugs were added to the plates. The morphological changes of the PDOs were observed, and OD values were measured 72 h later (Day 5).

By comparing bright-field images taken before (Day 2) and after (Day 5) drug exposure, we observed that PDOs from the same source exhibited varying degrees of volume reduction or overall shrinkage following treatment with different drugs. Conversely, some PDOs showed no significant morphological changes or even continued growth post-treatment (Fig. 3D). This indicates differential effects of various drugs on PDOs derived from the same patient, demonstrating the potential of PDOs as a model for drug sensitivity testing.

All experimental data were processed using Origin 2022 to generate dose-response fitting curves for the six drugs (Fig. 4A). The corresponding fitting equations were used to calculate the respective IC50 and AUC Score. Drug sensitivity prediction results were determined by comparing the IC50 values to the cutoff point (25% of the PPC). Additionally, the AUC Z-scores which obtained from normalizing the AUC Scores of all curves were visualized in the form of a heatmap (Fig. 4B). It should be noted that the dose-response curves for IHCC-161 and IHCC-163 were not included because the concentration gradients used in pre-tests differed from those in the formal experiments. Furthermore, due to insufficient PDO yield, IHCC-179, IHCC-171, and IHCC-181 were excluded from full dose-response analysis. Instead, they were tested at a reduced number of concentrations (three, two, and one, respectively). Consequently, dose-response curves could not be generated for these models, and their drug sensitivity results were assessed simply using inhibition rates at low concentration, high concentration, and overall PDO inhibition rate.

Analysis of Fig. 4 reveals that PDOs derived from the same patient exhibited different responses to various drugs. In contrast, PDOs from different patients showed varying inhibition rates when treated with the same drug. Even among PDOs derived from multiple patients with the same pathological type, drug sensitivity profiles demonstrated considerable variation. Given the substantial inter-patient and intra-patient variability observed in the drug sensitivity tests, we conclude that PDOs can serve as an effective drug screening model.

To further validate the stability of the PDOs, we performed serial passaging and cryopreservation on IHCC-180 and IHCC-189. Drug sensitivity testing was subsequently conducted on the passaged PDOs (IHCC-180-P5, IHCC-189-P4) and the revived PDOs (IHCC-180-R, IHCC-189-R). The resulting dose-response curves were compared with those of the primary PDOs (IHCC-180-P0, IHCC-189-P0) (Fig. 5A and B). The dose-response curves for IHCC-180-P5, IHCC-180-R, IHCC-189-P4, and IHCC-189-R highly overlapped with those of their respective primary PDOs (IHCC-180-P0, IHCC-189-P0). Subsequently, we performed Pearson correlation analysis on the AUC Scores (Fig. 5C). The Pearson correlation coefficient (r) between IHCC-180-P0 and IHCC-180-R was 0.9480 (P = 0.0040), and between IHCC-180-P0 and IHCC-180-P5 was 0.9558 (P = 0.0029). For IHCC-189, the Pearson r between IHCC-189-P0 and IHCC-189-R was 0.9904 (P = 0.0001), and between IHCC-189-P0 and IHCC-189-P4 was 0.9983 (P = 0.0001). The findings indicate that for PDOs originating from the same source, following either passaging or cryopreservation, the Pearson r values in comparison to the primary PDOs are all in close proximity to 1. This further substantiates the elevated degree of similarity observed in the dose-response curves. Therefore, we conclude that the PDOs established in this study possess a high degree of stability and are not affected by cell passaging or cryopreservation processes.

Clinical follow-up

To validate whether the drug sensitivity results from PDOs correlated with clinical outcomes, we tracked the treatment regimens of 16 patients and evaluated their therapeutic response according to RECIST v1.1 (Fig. 6A). Among these 16 patients, the clinical efficacy matched the drug sensitivity predictions from PDOs in 11 cases, accounting for 68.75% of the followed-up cohort. Within this matched cohort, 4 patients were assessed as achieving Partial Response (PR), 6 had Stable Disease (SD), and only 1 experienced Progressive Disease (PD) (ORR: 36.36%, 95% CI: 10.93–69.21%; DCR: 90.91%, 95% CI: 58.72–99.77%). In contrast, among the 5 patients in the mismatched cohort where clinical outcomes did not align with PDO predictions, 1 had SD and 4 had PD (ORR: 0%, 95% CI: 0-52.18%; DCR: 20%, 95% CI: 0.51–71.64%). Consequently, both the ORR and DCR were significantly superior in the matched cohort compared to the mismatched cohort (36.36% vs. 0%; 90.91% vs. 20%) (Fig. 6B). These results indicate that the utilization of PDOs in drug sensitivity testing can serve as an effective predictor of clinical efficacy, as evidenced by the observation of higher ORR and DCR in the matched cohort. In summary, we conclude that PDOs can serve as a valid predictive model for drug sensitivity.

Discussion

As a highly promising three-dimensional model, PDOs have been repeatedly validated at the histological and genomic levels for their ability to faithfully recapitulate the morphology, expression patterns, and genetic heterogeneity of primary tumors [9]. This makes them a highly personalized, human-derived disease model, holding significant importance for in vitro testing of anti-tumor drugs and providing a robust platform for developing individualized treatment strategies for different patients. Compared with cell lines, the most significant advantage of PDOs lies in their successful preservation of the three-dimensional structure of tumors. This three-dimensional structure affects the gradient distribution of nutrients and oxygen, the transmission of mechanical signals between cells, and the penetration of drugs, making the results of PDOs-based drug sensitivity tests theoretically more physiologically relevant [10]. Compared with the Patient-derived Tumor Xenograft model, the construction cycle of PDOs is significantly shortened, with higher success rates and expandability, and relatively controllable costs, enabling them to have the potential for medium-throughput screening and the construction of large-scale biological sample banks.

In this study, our team successfully established a PDO library predominantly derived from hepatic malignancies, achieving a high modeling success rate of 79.31%. We demonstrated that these models highly replicate the biological and genetic characteristics of the parental tumor tissues, including histomorphology, phenotypic markers, and proliferative activity. Furthermore, the stability of these models was confirmed through serial passaging and cryopreservation. It should be noted that although we have demonstrated the similarity between PDOs and parental tissues in terms of morphology and histology through H&E staining and IHC staining, we have not conducted further verification at the genomic and proteomic levels. This is a limitation of this study and also our future research direction.

Multiple studies have indicated that tumor tissues with high malignancy, strong proliferative activity, and low differentiation are more amenable to successful PDO culture compared to those with poor proliferative capacity and high differentiation [9, 11–13]. Among the 29 patients enrolled in our study, 89.6% were diagnosed with stage III or IV advanced cancer, whose tumor tissues typically exhibit higher malignancy and stronger proliferative activity. This patient demographic likely contributed to our high modeling success rate.

During PDO culture, we observed that defining uniform success criteria is challenging. Even PDOs cultured within the same laboratory exhibited variations in size, quantity, and morphological appearance. In our study, the successful culture was defined by the formation of three-dimensional, spherical structures with good translucency and absence of central black necrosis. No specific requirement for PDO quantity was identified. Consequently, among the 23 successfully cultured PDO lines, three yielded insufficient numbers for comprehensive testing across the full drug concentration gradient. For these three cases, drug sensitivity was assessed simply using inhibition rates at low and high concentrations, and overall PDO inhibition rate.

In our study design, assessing drug sensitivity in PDOs and determining the actual treatment regimen for patients were conducted independently. The PDO drug sensitivity results were compared with the clinical efficacy only after clinicians had evaluated the patient’s treatment response. Ultimately, among the 16 followed-up patients, our PDOs achieved a prediction accuracy of 68.75% for drug sensitivity. Notably, a study undertaken in 2024 by Professor Li Xiaoxing’s team at Sun Yat-sen University, which established gastric cancer PDOs for drug sensitivity testing, reported a remarkable 91.7% concordance between PDO predictions and clinical outcomes in 12 followed-up patients [12]. We speculate that the factors that may affect the accuracy of predictions include the following aspects. First, the biological characteristics of specific cancer types may influence the predictability of drug responses; tumors with higher intrinsic heterogeneity or complex microenvironments may be more difficult to model accurately. Second, differences in PDO culture protocols, including medium composition, Matrigel batches, and culture duration, may lead to biases in drug sensitivity results. Third, different studies have different definitions of drug sensitivity, which may result in different classification outcomes. Finally, patient population characteristics and the specific drugs tested may also contribute to variability.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this single-center study is still in its preliminary stage, and the sample size of the clinical validation cohort (n = 16) is relatively small, which has led to a certain degree of overlap in the confidence intervals between the matched and mismatched cohorts. Therefore, we need to continue expanding the sample size and narrowing the confidence intervals in subsequent studies. Second, there are two different pathological subtypes, HCC and ICC, in PLC. However, due to the small size of our sample, we were unable to conduct meaningful statistical analyses on each subgroup individually. This is also one of the issues that we need to address in the future after expanding the sample size. Third, the PDOs we constructed lack components of the immune microenvironment such as immune cells. This deficiency may lead to inaccuracies in the assessment of drug efficacy. In the future, developing a co-culture system containing autologous immune cells and stromal components will be crucial for addressing this limitation. Finally, this study only sampled each patient at a single time point. In subsequent studies, PDOs from different periods can be constructed by sampling the same patient at multiple time points, thereby enabling a more comprehensive analysis.

The future prospects for PDOs are considerable. Researchers have successfully established PDOs from various cancer types [14], and the scope of drug testing is gradually expanding from single agents to combination therapies [15]. Furthermore, several studies have already reported preliminary successes in exploring the utility of PDOs for predicting clinical efficacy of personalized treatment regimens, such as radiotherapy and combination therapies [14, 16, 17]. It is important to acknowledge, however, the inherent limitation of PDOs: the lack of a complete and systematic in vivo microenvironment. Since PDOs lack functional vascular systems, immune cells, nerves, and complex extracellular matrix components including fibroblasts, they cannot model systemic drug effects or immune responses [11]. Currently, researchers are actively exploring ways to co-culture PDOs with the immune system [18].

If PDOs technology can be applied clinically in the future, it will be beneficial for patients. For newly diagnosed patients, only a little more samples need to be collected on the basis of meeting the needs of pathological diagnosis, so that PDOs construction and drug sensitivity testing can be carried out simultaneously. This will enable both clear diagnosis and the formulation of effective treatment plans to be achieved at the same time. For patients with drug resistance after multiple lines of treatment, effective drug screening can also be achieved through PDOs drug sensitivity testing. However, several practical challenges must be addressed before this technology can be widely applied. First, cost is one of the barriers. PDO culture and drug testing require specialized reagents, equipment, and trained personnel. Second, differences among institutions in tissue processing, culture conditions, and detection protocols may lead to inconsistent results and hinder multi-center validation. Developing consensus guidelines, quality control indicators, and potential commercial kits is crucial for improving reproducibility and scalability. Third, the establishment of PDOs-immune cell co-culture systems is still under exploration and currently requires more data for support. Finally, the regulatory pathways for PDO-based treatment guidance remain unclear, and prospective clinical trials are needed to demonstrate its role in improving efficacy and cost-effectiveness to support regulatory approval and clinical application. Therefore, future efforts should focus on standardizing and quality-controlling the entire PDOs culture process, reducing the cost, shortening the time required for drug sensitivity testing, developing co-culture systems that integrate PDOs with autologous fibroblasts and immune cells, conducting multi-center, prospective clinical trials, and improving relevant declarations and approvals before the technology is put into practical application.

Conclusions

In summary, this study successfully established a robust and clinically validated biobank of PDOs from hepatic malignancies with a high success rate. We demonstrated that these PDOs faithfully recapitulate the histological, phenotypic, and genetic profiles of the original patient tumors. Importantly, the platform demonstrated functional utility: drug sensitivity profiles generated in vitro effectively predicted patient clinical responses, with significantly improved outcomes observed when treatment aligned with PDO predictions. Furthermore, the model exhibited remarkable stability across serial passaging and cryopreservation, supporting its practical application for biobanking. Collectively, our findings validate hepatic malignancy PDOs as a reliable, patient-specific tool for guiding personalized therapy selection. This platform addresses a critical need in precision oncology by providing a clinically predictive model to optimize treatment efficacy and minimize ineffective therapeutic exposure for patients with liver cancer.
