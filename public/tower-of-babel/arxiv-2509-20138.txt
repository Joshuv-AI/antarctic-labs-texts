Formal Verification of Minimax Algorithms
Wieger Wesselink, Kees Huizing, Huub van de Wetering

Abstract
Using the Dafny verification system, we formally verify a range of minimax search algorithms, including variations with alpha-beta pruning and transposition tables. For depth-limited search with transposition tables, we introduce a witness-based correctness criterion and apply it to two representative algorithms. All verification artifacts, including proofs and Python implementations, are publicly available.



1
Introduction

Game tree search algorithms, such as minimax and its optimization via alpha-beta pruning, compute optimal moves by recursively evaluating all possible outcomes under the assumption of perfect play. These algorithms assign values to game states based on the principle that one player seeks to maximize the evaluation, while the opponent seeks to minimize it.
The alpha-beta pruning technique, which may significantly improve the efficiency of minimax by pruning branches that cannot affect the final decision, was analyzed in detail by Knuth and Moore in their seminal work
[
5
]
. Various theoretical frameworks have been applied to minimax and related algorithms, including lattice theory
[
1
]
and AND/OR tree representations
[
9
]
.
Several variations of alpha-beta pruning exist. In
[
5
]
, the
fail-hard
variant of alpha-beta pruning is discussed, while Fishburn introduced
fail-soft
variants in
[
3
,
4
]
.
More recently, Nipkow et al. have verified implementations of several minimax variants in a functional programming setting using the Isabelle proof assistant
[
8
]
. Their work includes a verified transposition-table-based algorithm, but only in the setting of depth-unlimited search.
This setting avoids many of the subtleties that arise in depth-limited search, where partially explored subtrees and varying depths complicate the formulation and verification of correctness criteria for transposition table usage.
In this paper, we focus on the formal verification of minimax and negamax
[
9
]
algorithms in an imperative setting using Dafny, a programming language and verifier designed for writing provably correct programs
[
6
]
. Dafny is grounded in Floyd-Hoare logic and supports automated reasoning through constructs such as preconditions, postconditions, assertions, loop invariants, and lemmas.
Beyond basic minimax, negamax, and alpha-beta, we also verify depth-limited variants and explore extensions that incorporate transposition tables, a widely used optimization in practical game-playing engines. Transposition-table-based algorithms are known to be subtle and error-prone, and even small implementation differences can lead to incorrect behavior. Our work shows how formal verification can provide a robust foundation for understanding and reasoning about their correctness.

Definitions

A game tree is a rooted directed acyclic graph representing the possible evolutions of a two-player game. Each node corresponds to a game position, and each edge to a legal move. Nodes are colored by the player to move: white for the maximizer and gray for the minimizer, as illustrated in Figure
1
.
Formally, a node is defined as a record with three attributes:

\text{Node}=\textbf{record}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\textit{eval}&\text{: evaluation value}\\
\textit{color}&\text{: player to move ($\pm 1$)}\\
\textit{children}&\text{: ordered child list}\end{array}\right.

The
eval
field encodes an integer value of a game state: the maximizer (
color
=1) favors higher, while the minimizer (
color
=-1) favors lower values. Internal nodes use heuristic evaluations, while leaves reflect game-theoretic values.
The
children
list defines the successors of a node
u
in a fixed order, inducing edges
(u,v)
iff
v\in u.\textit{children}
. A fixed order is not strictly necessary, but it is commonly used and enables deterministic execution.
We assume acyclicity, as repetition rules in games like chess and draughts preclude infinite loops in practice. Nodes abstract from concrete game states: equality is structural, requiring identical
eval
,
color
, and isomorphic subtrees. This suffices for search correctness while avoiding full state representation.
Since a node
u
implicitly defines the subtree rooted at
u
, we use the terms node and game tree interchangeably. For a game tree
u
we recursively define
\textsf{minimax}(u)
as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u.\textit{eval}&\text{if $|u.\textit{children}|=0$}\\
\min\{\textsf{minimax}(v)\mid v\in u.\textit{children}\}&\text{if $|u.\textit{children}|>0$ and $u.\textit{color}=-1$}\\
\max\{\textsf{minimax}(v)\mid v\in u.\textit{children}\}&\text{if $|u.\textit{children}|>0$ and $u.\textit{color}=1$}\\
\end{cases}

(1)

and
\textsf{negamax}(u)
as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u.\textit{color}\cdot u.\textit{eval}&\text{if $|u.\textit{children}|=0$}\\
\max\{-\textsf{negamax}(v)\mid v\in u.\textit{children}\}&\text{if $|u.\textit{children}|>0$}.\\
\end{cases}

(2)

For minimax, we allow the same player to move consecutively. For negamax, the game must be
turn-based
, i.e., for each node
u
the following holds:

\displaystyle\forall v\in u.\textit{children}:v.\textit{color}=-u.\textit{color}.

(3)

Note that the two functions are the same up to a sign inversion.

\textsf{negamax}(u)=u.\textit{color}\cdot\textsf{minimax}(u).

(4)

In depth-limited search, we work with
truncated game trees
. For a game tree
u
,
\left\lfloor{u}\right\rfloor_{d}
denotes the subtree containing all nodes within distance
d
from the root.

2.1
Alpha-beta Pruning

Alpha-beta pruning is an optimization of minimax search that avoids exploring parts of the game tree which cannot affect the final decision. The algorithm maintains a window
(\alpha,\beta)
of values that are relevant to the outcome: values within this open interval have to be computed exactly, while values outside the window can be approximated without impacting the root decision. This allows the search to terminate early in branches that are irrelevant.
We introduce several predicates used in the verification of alpha-beta pruning:

\displaystyle\textsf{is-negamax-ab-result}(x,u,\alpha,\beta)=\textsf{is-ab-result}(x,\textsf{negamax}(u),\alpha,\beta)

(5)

\displaystyle\textsf{is-ab-result}(x,e,\alpha,\beta)=(e\leq x\leq\alpha)\;\lor\;(\alpha<e=x<\beta)\;\lor\;(\beta\leq x\leq e),

(6)

where the predicate
is-ab-result
(adapted from
[
4
]
) specifies when a value
x
is acceptable as an approximation of an exact minimax or negamax value
e
.
To support reasoning about partial computations, we also define

\textsf{is-partial-negamax-ab-result}(x,u,i,\alpha,\beta)=\textsf{is-ab-result}(x,\textsf{pnm}(u,i),\alpha,\beta),

(7)

where
\textsf{pnm}(u,i)=\max\{-\textsf{negamax}(u.\textit{children}[j])\mid 0\leq j<i\}
.

2.2
Transposition Tables

A transposition table maps game states to previously computed search results and is used to avoid repeated evaluations by storing entries of the form

\text{TableEntry}=\textbf{record}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\textit{value}&\text{ approximated minimax or negamax value.}\\
\textit{depth}&\text{ search depth at evaluation time.}\\
\textit{flag}&\text{ approximation classification (see below).}\end{array}\right.

The
flag
distinguishes between

\begin{cases}\textsf{exact}&\text{$value$ is an exact minimax value.}\\
\textsf{lowerbound}&\text{$value$ is a lower bound.}\\
\textsf{upperbound}&\text{$value$ is an upper bound.}\end{cases}

The formal semantics of transposition tables is often underspecified. The primary obstacle is the absence of precise semantics for stored entries. While the flag characterizes the relationship (lower bound, upper bound, or exact result) between the stored value and an exact minimax value, it is unclear which specific game tree this exact minimax value can be tied to.
Note that we do not store best moves in the table, as our focus is on the correctness of returned values rather than on search efficiency.

Formal Specification of Transposition Table Search

For depth-limited transposition-table-based algorithms
NegamaxTT[W,M]
, the returned results may depend on values computed earlier in unrelated parts of the game tree, potentially at greater depth than required at the current node. The algorithm effectively explores an
expansion
of the truncated depth-
d
tree by greedily reusing deeper search results via the transposition table. This behavior is illustrated in Figure
2
(r), where the primary search unfolds as a triangle-shaped subtree rooted at the current node. Upon a transposition table hit, the algorithm may reuse a value previously computed in a deeper search of a different subtree.
So not only the nodes in the truncated depth-
d
tree contribute to the search result, but also recursively the nodes in these (reused) subtrees.
This motivates our notion of a
witness
, a concrete expansion of the truncated tree that merges all such reused subtrees into a single game tree. Correctness is then characterized by the existence of a witness whose minimax (or negamax) value equals the value returned by the algorithm.

We now formalize this idea.
For any result returned by the algorithm on input tree
u
and depth-limit
d
, there must exist a game tree
u^{\prime}
, the witness, that justifies the returned value. The following conditions must hold:

1.

\left\lfloor{u}\right\rfloor_{d}\subseteq u^{\prime}\subseteq u
, meaning that
u^{\prime}
is an expansion of the truncated tree
\left\lfloor{u}\right\rfloor_{d}
.

2.

For every node
z
in
u^{\prime}
, either all the children of
z
in
u
are in
u^{\prime}
, or none.

3.

\textsc{NegamaxTT[W,M]}(u,\alpha,\beta,\textit{depth},T)=\textsf{negamax}(u^{\prime})
.

In Appendix
0.C
we
formalize conditions 1 and 2 in the predicate
is-aon-expansion
(
all-or-none expansion
)
and express the correctness of the algorithm as follows:

\begin{array}[]{l}\textsf{is-negamax-tt-result}(\textit{result},u,\alpha,\beta,\textit{depth})=\\[5.0pt]
~~\exists u^{\prime}:\;\textsf{is-aon-expansion}(u^{\prime},u,\textit{depth})\land\textsf{is-negamax-ab-result}(\textit{result},u^{\prime},\alpha,\beta).\end{array}

(8)

Moreover, for every entry
t
stored in the transposition table we require the existence of a witness, justifying that entry. This is formalized as follows:

\displaystyle\textsf{is-valid-table-entry}(t,u)=\exists u^{\prime}:\textsf{is-aon-expansion}(u^{\prime},u,t.\textit{depth})~\land

(9)

\displaystyle\quad\begin{cases}t.\textit{value}\geq\textsf{negamax}(u^{\prime}),&\text{if }t.\textit{flag}=\textsf{upperbound}\\
t.\textit{value}=\textsf{negamax}(u^{\prime}),&\text{if }t.\textit{flag}=\textsf{exact}\\
t.\textit{value}\leq\textsf{negamax}(u^{\prime}),&\text{if }t.\textit{flag}=\textsf{lowerbound}\end{cases}

We put no restrictions on the entries stored into the transposition table.
At every iteration of the algorithm, we allow the table’s contents to change arbitrarily, as long as the entries remain valid. This reflects practical implementations, where transposition tables are typically realized using hash tables: insertions may overwrite existing entries due to collisions or replacement strategies.

Verification of NegamaxTTW

We formally verified Algorithm
3
against the correctness criteria in equations (
8
) and (
9
). Dafny checked that the implementation satisfies its specification in all cases, leaving no unverified code paths. This provides a formal guarantee that the algorithm behaves correctly for any valid input. The Dafny code for this verification spans approximately 850 lines for this algorithm alone.
A major part of the effort also involved crafting suitable loop invariants, and managing Dafny’s interaction with the underlying SMT solver, Z3.
Proofs involving quantifiers are especially prone to excessive memory usage and solver timeouts.
A substantial amount of work went into splitting proofs into smaller lemmas, along with other techniques to reduce solver load and maintain proof stability. The Dafny code for the algorithm is included in Appendix
4
; the specifications and supporting lemmas, which constitute the bulk of the verification work, are available in a GitHub repository
[

]
.

The transposition table

According to our correctness criterion, the result of a depth-2 search at
v
must be justified by some expansion of
\left\lfloor{v}\right\rfloor_{2}
whose full negamax evaluation equals 2. However, among the four possible expansions of
\left\lfloor{v}\right\rfloor_{2}

The root cause appears to be the reuse of a lower bound (3) computed with alpha-beta window
(0,2)
in a search with a wider alpha-beta window
(0,5)
. This reuse leads to pruning of a child node with value 1, which, if unpruned, would yield a better result.

Notably, the full depth-6 search in call (a) also returns the value 2, even though the parent node of the child with value 2 has a child with value 1 that is within the search horizon.
According to minimax principles, a minimizing player is expected to prefer a child with value 1 over one with value 2.
Whether this outcome signals an undesirable behavior or points to the need for an alternative correctness notion remains an open question.

5
Results

Our first contribution is the introduction of a witness-based correctness criterion for depth-limited game tree search with transposition tables. This notion formalizes the intuitive requirement that the returned value must correspond to the exact evaluation of some fully explored subtree (the witness).

As a second contribution, we applied this correctness notion to two representative algorithms. We successfully verified the
NegamaxTTW
algorithm in Dafny, providing, to our knowledge, the first formal proof of correctness for a depth-limited transposition-table-based negamax search with alpha-beta pruning. In contrast, a variant by Marsland
[
7
]
(with best move tracking omitted) was shown to violate the criterion, based on a concrete counterexample.
Whether this outcome reflects a shortcoming in the Marsland variant or highlights the need for a different correctness notion remains an open question.

Beyond these case studies, we developed and verified over 15 variations of minimax and negamax algorithms, ranging from basic recursive definitions to depth-limited and alpha-beta optimized versions. All verification artifacts, including Dafny proofs and Python implementations with randomized testing, are available on GitHub
[
10
]
. The most complex case,
NegamaxTTW
, verifies in approximately 4 seconds with Dafny 4.10.0.

6
Conclusions

This work illustrates the potential of formal verification in reasoning about correctness properties of game tree search algorithms, particularly when optimizations such as transposition tables are involved.

Our main contribution is a general and intuitive witness-based correctness criterion, suitable for verifying depth-limited search algorithms with or without alpha-beta pruning. We showed that this criterion is sufficient to prove the correctness of the
NegamaxTTW
algorithm, but not of a variant by Marsland
[
7
]
, highlighting how small algorithmic changes can invalidate formal correctness arguments. These results demonstrate the importance of precise semantics in verifying optimized search techniques.
More broadly, our work showcases Dafny’s suitability for verifying recursive, stateful algorithms with complex invariants, as commonly found in AI for games.

We propose two directions for future work. A logical continuation of our work is to include the formalization of other search algorithms such as
SSS^{\ast}
and MTD(f). A second direction is the extension of correctness reasoning to quantum variants of game tree search. Recent advances in quantum program verification (e.g.,
[
12
]
) suggest a pathway toward bringing formal techniques to this emerging domain.

Appendix 0.B
The NegamaxTTM Algorithm

Algorithm
4
is a slightly simplified version of the transposition-table-based search algorithm by Marsland
[
7
]
. Special handling of the best move has been removed. This algorithm differs from the Wikipedia version in Algorithm
3
in four respects:

1.

Transposition Table Lookup
(lines
1
–
7
):
Stored bounds are used to narrow the alpha-beta window rather than terminate search immediately.

2.

Value propagation
(lines
12
–
13
): Fishburn’s fail-soft scheme
[
4
]
is used, which differs in how intermediate bounds are propagated during recursion.

3.

Flag Assignment
(line
15
): Results are classified against the current
\alpha
rather than the initial alpha-beta window.

4.

Depth-Preserving Updates
(line
17
): Existing entries are retained when their search depth exceeds that of the new result.

Verification of this algorithm was unsuccessful, as discussed in Section
4
. The failure stems from the first difference: narrowing the alpha-beta window based on a stored bound can lead to unexpected pruning. In contrast, differences 2, 3, and 4 can be safely incorporated into Algorithm
3
without compromising correctness.

One subtle issue arises with difference 3 when it is applied to Algorithm
3
. If the current value of
\alpha
is used, the three cases in the corresponding if statement may no longer be mutually exclusive. To restore correctness, the order of these cases must be adjusted: the lower bound case must precede the upper bound case. This requirement is non-obvious and difficult to detect without the aid of a formal verifier.

Appendix 0.C
Formal definition of all-or-none expansions

In Section
4
all-or-none expansions were introduced. The formal definition is given below. For a node
u
, let
C=u.\textit{children}
and
C^{\prime}=u^{\prime}.\textit{children}
. The predicate
is-aon-expansion
is defined as:

\displaystyle\textsf{is-aon-expansion}(u^{\prime},u,d)\;=\;u.\mathrm{eval}=u^{\prime}.\mathrm{eval}\;\land\;u.\mathrm{color}=u^{\prime}.\mathrm{color}\;\land

(11)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left(|C|=|C^{\prime}|\;\land\;\forall\,i\in[0,|v|):\;\textsf{is-aon-expansion}(C^{\prime}_{i},C_{i},d-1)\right),&\text{if }d>0\\[5.0pt]
\left(|C^{\prime}|=0\;\lor\;\left(|C|=|C^{\prime}|\;\land\;\forall\,i\in[0,|v|):\;\textsf{is-aon-expansion}(C^{\prime}_{i},C_{i},0)\right)\right),&\text{if }d=0\end{cases}

Note that every all-or-none expansion
