Targeting CB1 and TRPM8 receptors to counteract CD8+ T cell exhaustion
Authors: Adel Mohammadzadeh, Sahand Moazzendizzaji, Aliasghar Tabatabaei Mohammadi, Afsaneh Ahmadi, Rahim Mahmodlou, Ali Akbar Pourfathollah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe prolonged interaction between the immune system and tumor antigens can result in T cell exhaustion. Extensive research has been conducted on strategies to reactivate exhausted T cells within the tumor microenvironment. However the exact contribution of the endocannabinoid system (ECS) and nociceptors in regulating CD8+ T cells within the framework of cancer-related inflammation has not been thoroughly studied. This study investigated the use of a TRPM8 antagonist (RQ-00203078), a selective cannabinoid receptor 1 (CB1) antagonist (AM251), and alpelisib (BYL-719) to control CD8+ T cell exhaustion. Our findings showed that administration of the CB1 antagonist AM251, either alone or in combination with alpelisib, significantly reduced the expression of PD-1 and Lag-3 on CD8+ T cells. Interestingly, treatment with the TRPM8 antagonist led to a notable increase in PD-1 expression on CD8+ T cells. These findings suggest that the decreased expression of inhibitory receptors on CD8+ T cells after treatment with the CB1 antagonist whether alone or with alpelisib and TRPM8 highlights the potential of ECS as a promising therapeutic target in cancer treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46794-2.Keywords: Neuro-immune interaction, CD8+ T cell exhaustion, PD-1 and Lag-3, Alpelisib, CB1 and TRPM8 antagonists, NociceptorsSubject terms: Cancer, Drug discovery, Immunology

Introduction

CD8+ T cells play a crucial role in the immune response to tumors, making it essential to understand their role and potential interactions with the tumor microenvironment for effective therapeutic strategies1. The population of CD8+ T cells within tumor microenvironments is directly linked to overall survival rates in breast cancer patients, leading translational efforts to focus on these cells2. Previous studies have shown that tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells can recognize neoantigens in samples from patients with triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), suggesting their potential as viable candidates for tumor vaccine development3.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is characterized by various inhibitory signatures, including the presence of regulatory T cells (Tregs), acidic pH conditions, and inflammatory and hypoxic environments. These factors are associated with the gradual impairment of CD8+ tumor-infiltrating T cells, which markedly influences treatment results4. Research demonstrates that the tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a crucial role in regulating the anti-tumor immunity of CD8+ T cells. This regulation impacts various clinical outcomes, such as drug resistance, metastasis, and responses to immunotherapies5. Additionally, it has been observed that tumors are innervated by nerve cells and the intratumoral nerve play an important role in regulating tumor progression6. However, the specific effects of sensory receptors on immune cells, particularly regarding immune cell exhaustion in the context of tumor immunity, have been relatively underexplored in the literature7.

T cell exhaustion plays a critical role in tumor immunity8, highlighting the need for the development of effective therapeutic strategies to address this challenge and promote a comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms involved.

Among the T cells, CD8+ T cells are unique in their immune response against tumors9. However, the persistent inflammatory environment associated with cancer development can lead to a state known as exhaustion, compromising the capacity of T cells to effectively eradicate tumors10.

Recent studies have identified several distinguishing features of exhausted T cells, characterized by increased expression of inhibitory receptors such as PD-1, CTLA-4, Lag-3, TIM-3, BTLA and TIGIT. Additionally, these cells exhibit a reduced ability to produce essential cytokines and present elevated levels of various transcription factor profiles, such as TOX, TOX2, Bcl-6, BLIMP1, and Nr4A10.

One prominent area of research in immunotherapy for tumor treatment is the optimization of T cell exhaustion by reducing or blocking inhibitory receptors on these cells11. Experimental and clinical findings indicated that T cell exhaustion can be reversed through the modulation or inhibition of inhibitory receptors12. For example, monoclonal antibodies targeting immune checkpoints, such as ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4), nivolumab (anti-PD-1), and atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1), effectively restore T cell functionality13. Although immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy has yielded promising outcomes in treating various cancers, its effectiveness is inconsistent across different cancer types, and some patients may not respond positively to these treatment14. Furthermore, for numerous applications, including the rejuvenation of exhausted chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells, it is important to identify novel strategies to mitigate the effects of exhausted T cells15,16.

The transient receptor potential (TRP) channels are essential in the dynamic regulation of peripheral reactions to noxious stimuli17. Investigating TRP channels offers significant opportunities to enhance our comprehension of cancer biology and to develop novel therapeutic strategies18. This superfamily includes a wide array of polymodal ion channels that are essential for numerous cellular functions and have been linked to cancer development, making them appealing targets for additional investigation in cancer research and treatment18. TRP channels act as broadly distributed sensors for both chemical and physical stimuli, functioning as signal transducers by altering membrane potential or intracellular calcium (Ca2+) levels19. The cold thermo-receptor TRPM8, a subtype of transient receptor ion channel, is widely observed in mammalian sensory nerves and non-neuronal tissues20.

Endocannabinoids are a group of arachidonic acid derivatives synthesized by damaged and activated cells during inflammatory responses21. The endocannabinoid system includes endogenous compounds such as anandamide (AEA) and 2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG), as well as their cannabinoid receptors, CB1 and CB2, which play a crucial role in regulating various cellular functions22.

The CB1 receptor, a member of the G protein-coupled receptor family, is encoded by the CNR1 gene and is mainly expressed in the central nervous system (CNS), although it is rarely found on immune cells. Its expression levels can change under pathological conditions, including cancer23,24. This analgesic receptor is also present on both nociceptive and non-nociceptive sensory neurons in the dorsal root ganglia (DRG), within the immune system, and in the endometrium25,26.

Stimulation of the CB1 receptor by AEA leads to coupling with Gi/o proteins, which then inhibits adenylate cyclase, affecting the levels of cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) inside the cell and influencing ion channels and neurotransmitters, promoting synaptic plasticity and providing neuroprotection27.

Research has revealed a significant overexpression of TRPM8 and CB1 in certain cancer types, including breast and prostate cancer28,29. This enhanced expression not only aids in cancer cell metastasis but also functions as a promoting factor, establishing TRPM8 as a promising candidate for targeted cancer therapy30. Inhibiting CB1 signaling has been associated with apoptosis induction and angiogenesis inhibition in breast cancer23, indicating its potential as a valuable therapeutic target31.

The pharmacological treatment of various cancers, including breast cancer, currently relies heavily on chemotherapeutic agents such as anthracyclines, which target the biochemical pathways within cancer cells32. Recent studies have focused on reducing the side effects of these agents and preventing the development of drug resistance33. Additionally, a significant pathway associated with cell survival and various human cancers is the overexpression of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway34. Alpelisib (BYL-719), a potent PI3Kα-specific inhibitor, is an FDA-approved drug widely used to treat several breast cancer subtypes, including HR+/HER2−, HER2+, and triple-negative variants35.

To explore the integrative role of sensory receptors on CD8+ T cell function during chronic immune responses against tumors, we used a mouse model of breast cancer.

The aim of this study was to examine the relationship between the endocannabinoid system (ECS), the TRPM8 channel, and the regulation of CD8+ T cell exhaustion in the tumor microenvironment. This was achieved by administering the CB1 antagonist, AM251, and the TRPM8 antagonist, RQ-00203078.

Discussion

Chronic inflammation is a prominent feature of various tumors and can lead to T cell exhaustion38. However, despite being a recognized characteristic of tumors and being detected by various receptors, limited attention has been given to the specific roles of endocannabinoids and TRP channels in immune cells during tumor development39.

In this study, we focused on how nociceptors, particularly TRPM8, and the endocannabinoid receptor, CB1, influence CD8 T cell exhaustion during breast cancer treatment. Our findings reveal that the interaction between nociceptors, the endocannabinoid system, and immune cells during breast cancer progression offers new insights for developing therapeutic strategies targeting CB1 and TRPM8 receptors.

T cells are vital to the immune response against tumors, but evidence indicates that anti-tumor effector T cells frequently display high levels of inhibitory receptors, resulting in a dysfunctional state termed exhaustion40. The increased expression of inhibitory receptors on T cells, along with a reduced capacity for proliferation, is commonly observed in various types of cancers. A high prevalence of inhibitory receptors on immune cells is associated with a poor prognosis, failure of cancer immunotherapy such as chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy and lower survival rates41–43. Thus, addressing T-cell exhaustion is a critical goal in cancer immunotherapy44. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICBs) have received significant attention in recent years owing to their clinical effectiveness45. However, the effectiveness of these treatments is limited by their side effects, which include neurological and immune-related adverse effects (irAEs) in certain cancer patients46–48.

Our data indicate that treatment with a CB1 antagonist, either alone or in combination with alpelisib, significantly decreased the expression of PD-1 and Lag-3 on antitumor CD8+ T cells. This suggests that AM251 treatment could enhance antitumor immune responses by reducing inhibitory signaling pathways mediated by PD-1 and Lag-3 on CD8+ T cells49.

Nociceptors are another set of receptors that detect harmful stimuli, functioning as polymodal receptors that signal injury during inflammation50. The expression pattern of TRPM8 varies among different types of tumors, with a notable increase seen in breast cancer51. To study the impact of TRPM8 on the exhaustion of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, we observed a significant increase in PD-1 expression in a group treated solely with the TRPM8 antagonist, with levels significantly higher than those in the untreated group (P value < 0.0001). Conversely, administering the TRPM8 antagonist in combination with alpelisib led to a significant reduction in PD-1 levels on CD8+ T cells (P value < 0.001). These findings highlight the crucial role of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway in regulating various cellular processes, including the function of nociceptors in immune cells52,53.

PD-1 is transiently up-regulated during T-cell activation; however, elevated levels of PD-1 expression can impair the effector functions of T cells54. To validate the results from flow cytometry, we employed IHC to investigate PD-1 expression in cells within the tumor microenvironment and assess how these treatments affect immune cells. Our IHC data confirmed that the application of a CB1 antagonist leads to a reduction in the expression of PD-1 on immune cells within tumor tissues in groups treated with the CB1 antagonist, thereby enhancing the efficacy of this therapeutic approach and supporting our hypothesis.

Interestingly, the analysis of data obtained from flow cytometry and IHC revealed a significant decrease in PD-1 expression after concurrent treatment with alpelisib and a TRPM8 antagonist.

In contrast to the findings obtained through flow cytometry regarding the impact of alpelisib on PD-1 expression, the IHC results indicated a notable reduction in the alpelisib-treated groups. This discrepancy may be attributed to the more complex cellular populations or the heterogeneous nature of the cells examined in IHC compared to flow cytometry. Furthermore, it is possible that the mechanisms by which alpelisib exerts its effects on various immune cell populations might be distinct55.

To understand the molecular mechanisms driving these results, we conducted RT-PCR analysis to evaluate mRNA expression levels of transcription factors and exhaustion related genes. Our results revealed that compared with the TRPM8 antagonist-treated group, the CB1 antagonist-treated group had decreased levels of various co-inhibitory receptors, including PD-1, CTLA-4, Tim-3, Lag-3 and TIGIT.

Additionally, we examined the expression patterns of NFATc1 (Raptor) and NFATc2 (Ricror) in the experimental groups. The results indicated a significant decrease in NFATc1 levels and an increase in NFATc2 levels in both the CB1 antagonist-treated groups and those treated with a combination of alpelisib. Furthermore, our findings showed a significant increase in TCF-1 expression levels in the groups treated with CB1 and in those administered both CB1 and alpelisib.

The present study focused on sampling and analyzing whole tumor tissue to assess alterations in gene expression. However, these methods do not directly elucidate the role of these genes in CD8+ T cells. Therefore, an alternative approach involving the isolation of CD8+ T cells is necessary. Recent studies have highlighted the pivotal role of TCF-1 in modulating CD8+ T cell responses in cancer, suggesting that tumor-infiltrating TCF-1+ T cells, which are called stem-like CD8+ T cells, possess enhanced long-term persistence, greater ability to regulate tumor growth, and the unique capability to both self-renew and differentiate into terminally dysfunctional T cells56.

Our research suggests that targeting multiple signaling pathways may be a more effective strategy for cancer treatment. Specifically, we found that using alpelisib alone does not decrease the expression of PD-1 or Lag-3 on CD8+ T cells. However, the combination of a CB1 antagonist significantly improved the outcomes. A similar pattern was observed with the TRPM8 antagonist; the combination of this agent with alpelisib resulted in a significant reduction in PD-1 expression on CD8 T cells.

A variety of TRPM8 antagonists have been reported to possess significant anti-cancer effects, particularly in terms of reducing cancer cell proliferation and metastasis across several types, including prostate cancer, osteosarcoma, lung cancer and glioblastoma57. The application of a pharmacological antagonist has been demonstrated to reduce tumor growth in a mouse model of colon cancer58. Our results indicate that the administration of TRPM8 antagonist alone did not yield favorable results and may, in fact, increase the expression of Lag-3 and PD-1 on CD8+ T cells. To elucidate this empirical finding, it is important to clarify that the present study examines the impact of TRPM8 antagonists on immune cells, rather than on cancer cells themselves. Furthermore, the functionality of the TRPM8 channel may vary among different cancer cells or tumors depending on the specific TRPM8 antagonists used, which can complicate the interpretation of findings59. While our hypotheses about CB1 were statistically supported, it is important to acknowledge certain limitations that should be noted. Future studies should include research to determine if T cell exhaustion is linked to neuron-expressed markers, clarify the underlying mechanisms of this relationship, and examine additional phenotypic characteristics. This includes exploring exhaustion markers on CD8 T cells such as Tim-3 and TIGIT, as well as signaling pathways like PI3K/Akt/mTOR kinase. Additionally, it would be beneficial to conduct functional assays on T cell functionality, such as isolating CD8+ T cells, proliferation, INF-γ assays, and investigating the molecular mechanisms involving the roles of CB1 and TRPM8 receptors in T cell functionality.

Given that alpelisib is a potent inhibitor of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway, it is crucial to understand the pharmacological and regulatory functions of this pathway in modulating nociceptors, specifically TRPM8, in immune cell responses.

In conclusion, our study highlights the significant role of nociceptors and the endocannabinoid system as key contributors to T cell exhaustion during tumor progression. The reduction of inhibitory receptors on CD8+ T cells within the breast cancer mouse model following treatment with a CB1 antagonist indicates a key role of the endocannabinoid system and TRP channels that may serve as valuable targets for cancer therapeutic strategies.

Material and methods

Cell culture and breast cancer mouse model induction

The 4T1 murine mammary carcinoma cell line (ATCC Number: CRL-2539) was acquired from the Pasteur Institute in Tehran, Iran. The cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL of penicillin, and 100 μg/mL of streptomycin (Gibco, UK). Incubation took place at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 5% CO2.

In order to establish the 4T1-induced breast cancer model, female BALB/c mice aged between 6 to 8 weeks were obtained from the Pasteur Institute in Tehran, Iran. When the 4T1 cell lines reached the logarithmic growth phase, they were trypsinized (using Gibco), resuspended, and then a suspension containing 1 × 105 4T1 cells in 100 μL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was injected into the mammary fat pad of the mice. Eight days post-implantation, the mice were examined for tumor growth and were randomly divided into treatment and control groups.

Drug treatments protocol and tumor monitoring

In this investigation, mice were randomly selected based on their tumor volumes 8 days post-implantation. Randomization was performed in a stratified manner according to tumor burden. On day 8, all mice showing varying tumor sizes or no tumors were excluded from the study. Only mice with similar tumor sizes were included in the experiment. After selecting all animals based on tumor burden, those with comparable tumor sizes were randomly and blindly assigned to different cages or study groups. The treatment protocol was started on day 10 post-induction.

The drugs used were AM251, a selective CB1 antagonist, a TRPM8 antagonist, RQ-00203078 (a cold receptor antagonist), and alpelisib (BYL-719), a potent inhibitor of PI3Kα, all obtained from Medchemexpress, USA.

In the in vivo experiments, the compounds were formulated into stock solutions by dissolving them in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), PEG300, Tween-80, and saline, following the manufacturer’s guidelines (Medchemexpress, USA). Drug administration began on day 10 post-induction, with a dosage of 2 mg/kg, i.p of AM251 (Sigma), 1mg/kg of TRPM8 antagonist RQ-00203078, and 30mg/kg of alpelisib per mouse, either individually or in combination, every 2 to 3 days for 6 administrations. The control group was administered an equivalent volume (100µl) of DMSO, PEG300, Tween-80, and saline. After completing the treatment regimen, the mice underwent a two-day recovery period before being euthanized. On day 28 post-induction, the mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues were harvested for flow cytometry analysis, immunohistochemistry, and subsequent gene expression studies. Tumor progression was quantitatively assessed by measuring tumor size in cubic centimeters (cm3) using the diameter formula. This was calculated using the ellipsoid method, expressed as π/6 × D1 × D2 × D3, where D1, D2, and D3 denote the largest diameters of the tumor compartment measured in three-dimensional planes: axial, sagittal, and coronal reformations60 (Fig. 1D).

The overall health of the mice was assessed throughout the experiment, and body weight was measured at both the initial and final endpoints of the study, as depicted in Fig. 1B.

Sample preparation

Mice were humanely euthanized using a combination of ketamine and xylazine. Following euthanasia, tumor tissues were surgically removed. A portion of the tissues underwent enzymatic digestion with Collagenase IV (Gibco) and DNAse I (Sigma), along with mechanical disruption to facilitate cell release. After a 30-min incubation, the cell suspension was centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min to pellet the cells. The resulting cell pellet was then treated with a red blood cell lysis buffer for 10 min at room temperature to remove any remaining red blood cells. The processed cells were filtered through a 40µm cell strainer (SPL, Korea).

Flow cytometry

In this study, CD8+ T cells were identified as CD3+ CD8+ T cells. Within this population, the frequencies of PD-1 and Lag-3 positive cells were compared across different groups (Fig. 4). Tumor- derived immune cells underwent a staining protocol consistent with previously established methods. To assess the CD8+ T cell population, cells were washed with FACS buffer (phosphate-buffered saline supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum). Approximately 1 × 106 cells were then treated with fixation and permeabilization buffer (BD Biosciences, USA) and stained with a cocktail of antibodies. This cocktail included PE-Cy7-labeled anti-mouse CD3 (clone B312915; BioLegend), APC-labeled anti-mouse CD8 (Clone 53–6.7; BioLegend), FITC-labeled anti-CD4 (Clone GK 1.5; BioLegend), PerCP-Cy5.5-labeled anti-PD-1 (clone RMP1-30; BioLegend), and Brilliant Blue-labeled anti-LAG-3 (Clone C9B7W; BioLegend).

The staining process took 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. The stained cells were subsequently analyzed using a FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and FlowJo software (Tree Star, Ashland, OR, USA) for data analysis.

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and RT-PCR

Tumor tissue was used as the source of immune cells for RNA extraction. A portion of the tumor was minced and digested with 1% collagenase type I enzyme. After a 30-min incubation period, the cellular mixture was centrifuged at 300g for 5 min to separate the cells. The isolated cells were then treated with red blood cell (RBC) lysis buffer. After a 10-min incubation, the cells were centrifuged again at 300g. The pelleted cells were then used for reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) analyses with appropriate primer sets.

Total mRNA was extracted using the FAVORGEN kit from Taiwan following the manufacturer’s protocol. Approximately 1–5 µg of RNA was used for cDNA synthesis, which was performed with the Superscript cDNA Synthesis Kit from Gene All in Korea. Gene expression was measured using the SYBR Green method in real-time PCR, and the cycle threshold (Ct) values were analyzed for gene expression using the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method. Primers specific to mouse genes (listed in Table 1, supplementary) were obtained from Metabion in Germany. The fold changes among all experimental groups were calculated using the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method, with GAPDH used as the reference gene.

Histopathological analysis

To evaluate the expression of the PD-1 protein, tumor tissues from all mice were removed and fixed in 10% formalin for histological assessment. Tissue sections, sliced at a thickness of 4 µm, were air-dried for one hour at 60 °C and stored in the dark at 4 °C. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the PD-1 antibody (ab64261—Rabbit Specific HRP/DAB (ABC) Detection IHC Kit). The process involved deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval. Subsequently, the samples were blocked with 1% BSA in TBS and incubated overnight at 4 °C with monoclonal rabbit anti-mouse PD-1 antibody. After incubation, the tissues were washed three times with TBS containing 0.03% Triton X-100 and then incubated with a goat biotinylated polyvalent secondary antibody for 10 min at room temperature. Following another round of washing, the specimens were incubated with Streptavidin-HRP, followed by the addition of 1 µL of DAB chromogen and 20 µL of DAB substrate for color development. The samples were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Microscopic analysis was performed using a fluorescence microscope (Germany-AXIOM, BM-600 LED EPI FLUORESCENT), and image analysis, representing percentages of area, was conducted using ImageJ software.

Statistical analyses

Statistical significance was assessed using a one-way ANOVA conducted with Prism software (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA). A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; and ***P < 0.001).

Ethics statement

To establish the 4T1-induced breast cancer model, it was confirmed that all animal care and experimental procedures conducted in this study adhered to the ethical guidelines approved by Urmia University of Medical Sciences, in alignment with ARRIVE guidelines (ARRIVE 2.0)61. All authors acknowledge the ethical considerations relevant to this manuscript. Ethical approval for this research was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Urmia University of Medical Sciences under approval code [IR.UMSU.AEC.1401.004]. Mice were maintained under standard housing conditions with a controlled temperature of 22°C, following a 12-h light/dark cycle. Food and water were available at all times, and appropriate air conditioning was implemented to minimize potential harm or distress. All necessary measures and precautions were employed to ensure the well-being of the animals. At the conclusion of the experiment, in accordance with IACUC guidelines, all euthanasia procedures for the mice were conducted using carbon dioxide (CO2) gas at a flow rate of 3 L/min until respiration ceased62. Prior to this procedure, the animal received anesthesia to minimize distress, which involved administering a mixture of ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (5 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injection. Euthanasia was subsequently performed using CO₂ asphyxiation, followed by the cervical dislocation method, which involved placing a pen directly behind the ears at the base of the skull and, while lifting the base of the tail, dislocating the cervical vertebrae. In this study, female BALB/c mice, weighing between 21 and 23 g and aged 6–8 weeks, were obtained from the Pasteur Institute in Tehran, Iran. Upon reaching the logarithmic growth phase, 4T1 cell lines were trypsinized (using Gibco), resuspended, and a suspension containing 1 × 105 4T1 cells in 100 μL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was injected into the mammary fat pad of the mice. Eight days post-implantation, the mice were evaluated for tumor growth and were randomly assigned to treatment and control groups. The experimental groups consisted of eight categories: Control or Untreated, Alp (Alpelisib treated), TRPM8 ant (TRPM8 antagonist treated), CB1 ant (CB1 antagonist treated), Alp/CB1 ant (Alpelisib & CB1 antagonist treated), TRPM8/CB1 ant (TRPM8 & CB1 antagonist treated), Alp/TRPM8 ant (Alpelisib & TRPM8 antagonist treated), and Alp/CB1/TRPM8 ant (Alpelisib & CB1 & TRPM8 antagonist treated), with n = 5 mice in each group. In total, n = 50 mice were utilized for tumor induction, resulting in n = 4–5 mice in each experimental group. Mice that did not develop a tumor mass or exhibited a tumor mass significantly larger than the others by day 8 of induction were excluded from the study. Additionally, 4–5 mice were randomly allocated to each cage (Large cage Size (W × L × H), 7.5″ × 11.75″ × 5″) for each experimental group.
