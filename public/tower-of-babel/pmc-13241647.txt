Geranylgeraniol promotes osteoblast differentiation and inhibits osteoclastogenesis through MAPK and nuclear receptor signaling
Authors: Mineko Tomomura, Takao Tsukahara, Ryuichiro Suzuki, Hiroshi Sakagami, Kenjiro Bandow, Akito Tomomura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bone is regulated in a coordinated manner through the catabolic action of osteoclasts and the anabolic function of osteoblasts. Geranylgeraniol (GGOH) is a diterpenoid alcohol found in plant oils and is an intermediate in the mevalonate pathway. GGOH suppressed receptor activator of NF-κB ligand (RANKL)-induced tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase-positive multinuclear osteoclast formation via downregulation of nuclear factor of activated T-cells cytoplasmic 1 expression. Among the RANKL-activated mitogen-activated protein kinases in bone marrow cells, phosphorylation of c-Jun amino-terminal kinase was significantly suppressed following GGOH treatment. In contrast, GGOH stimulates alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity and mineralization in calvarial osteoblasts. GGOH increased the expression of osteoblast biomarkers such as collagen type 1 and ALP, and osteogenic genes, such as Msx2, Runx2, and Smad. Mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathway (c-Jun amino-terminal kinase, p38, extracellular signal-regulated kinase) and NF-κB were augmented by GGOH. GGOH-induced ALP expression was suppressed by a farnesoid X receptor (FXR) antagonist, whereas FXR agonist increased ALP activity in osteoblasts. In contrast, the suppressive effect of GGOH on RANKL-induced tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase activity in osteoclasts was attenuated by liver X receptor and FXR antagonists, and these agonists mimicked the effect of GGOH. GGOH restored bisphosphonate-suppressed osteoblast differentiation. GGOH also showed protective effects against lipopolysaccharide -induced bone resorption both in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, GGOH treatment improved bone loss in ovariectomized mice. These results suggest that GGOH is a potent osteogenic and osteoprotective factor that promotes osteoblast differentiation and suppresses osteoclast differentiation, at least through nuclear receptor activation.

Bone homeostasis is maintained by a balance between osteoblast-mediated bone formation and osteoclast-mediated bone resorption (1). Increased osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption can lead to osteoporosis, which is a major health problem in an aging population (2). Many clinical therapies, such as estrogen, bisphosphonates, vitamin D preparations, and anti- receptor activator of NF-κB ligand (RANKL) antibodies are used to treat osteoporosis; however, some negative side effects have been reported (3, 4, 5, 6). A dual-target approach that suppresses osteoclasts to reduce bone resorption and stimulates osteoblasts to promote bone formation would constitute a more efficacious therapeutic modality for bone loss diseases such as osteoporosis.

Normal bone remodeling is regulated by the differentiation, activation, and apoptosis of osteoblasts and osteoclasts by several cytokines, hormones, nutrients, vitamins, and cellular metabolites. Osteoclasts differentiate from the monocyte–macrophage lineage under the stimulation of macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) and RANKL, which is expressed by mature osteoblasts and stromal cells. RANKL and its receptor RANK signaling axis in pre-osteoclasts stimulate c-Fos and nuclear factor of activated T-cells cytoplasmic1 (NFATc1), leading to the expression of tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) and cathepsin K (CTSK), and the formation of large, multinucleated mature cells to resorb the bone matrix (7, 8, 9). Osteoblast progenitor cells arise from mesenchymal stem cells and differentiate into mature osteoblasts that express alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and secrete of extracellular matrix components, such as collagen type 1 (Col1). Finally, hydroxyapatite is formed within the extracellular matrix and mineralized. Bone morphogenetic protein activates Smad and runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), a master transcription factor for osteoblast differentiation, leading to increased expression of ALP and Col1 (10, 11, 12).

The nuclear receptor (NR) superfamily of transcription factors plays a key role in regulating the transcription of genes involved in osteoclast and osteoblast differentiation and functions in a complex and multifaceted manner. Depending on the ligands, these factors can form as homodimers or heterodimers. Estrogens, whose receptors are members of the NR superfamily, are important for bone metabolism, and estrogen deficiency causes osteoporosis (3). Furthermore, retinoid X receptor and its heterodimeric NR partners such as farnesoid X receptor (FXR), pregnane X receptor (PXR), and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) are expressed in osteoblast and osteoclast lineage and regulate bone metabolism. The absence of FXR or PXR in mice results in osteopenia (13, 14). PPARγ-deficient ES cells fail to differentiate into adipocytes but spontaneously differentiate into osteoblast (15).

Disruption of the orchestrated control of osteoblasts and osteoclasts under pathogenic conditions can lead to an imbalance in bone remodeling and bone disorders. Inflammation triggers osteoclast formation, as observed in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced periodontal disease and pro-inflammatory cytokine-induced rheumatoid arthritis (16, 17). To protect against bone disorders and maintain normal bone structure and function, it is important to target osteoclasts to suppress excessive bone resorption, and to target osteoblasts for bone formation.

We have previously demonstrated that rhinacanthin C, a natural compound extracted from the leaves of Euptelea polyandra Sieb et. Zucc inhibited RANKL-stimulated osteoclast formation (18). Other rhinacanthin compounds, which are naphthoquinone esters with varying side-chain structures, showed weak or no effects on osteoclast differentiation. Therefore, we assumed that the alkyl side-chain structure of rhinacanthin C is important for its anti-osteogenic activity. The structure of rhinacanthin C resembles that of vitamin K2 menaquinone-4 (MK-4), which consists of a naphthoquinone ester with a geranylgeranyl side chain. MK-4 is a well-known compound that is beneficial for bone health (19, 20, 21). In our initial experiments, we found that geranylgeraniol (GGOH) suppressed osteoclast differentiation, and enhanced osteoblast differentiation, suggesting that geranylgeraniol is a reciprocal modulator of bone metabolism. GGOH is an isoprenoid compound found in plant oils and is an intermediate in the biosynthesis of several important biomolecules in the mevalonate pathway (22).

In the present study, we investigated the reciprocal regulation of osteoclast and osteoblast differentiation by GGOH and demonstrated its efficacy in models of LPS-induced bone resorption in vitro and in vivo, as well as in osteoporosis in ovariectomized mice.

RANKL induces NFATc1, a master transcription factor for osteoclastogenesis, which is upregulated by the transcription factor c-Fos (23). Fos and Nfatc1 mRNA and protein levels in osteoclasts were increased by RANKL treatment but were suppressed by GGOH after 12 h of treatment (Fig. 2, F and G). In differentiated osteoclasts, activation of c-Src leads to the formation of actin rings for bone resorption, and secretion of proteases, such as Ctsk (24). Therefore, we investigated the expression of these molecules required for mature osteoclasts. GGOH suppressed RANKL-induced Ctsk and Dcstamp mRNA expression (Fig. 2F), as well as c-Src protein level (Fig. 2G). DC-STAMP (Dendritic cell-specific transmembrane protein) is required for cell-cell fusion and the formation of multinucleated osteoclasts. The expression of Tnfrsf11a/Rank, a receptor for RANKL, was reduced by GGOH after 48 h of treatment. We also confirmed the suppressive effect of GGOH on RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation in the macrophage cell line RAW264.7 cells (data not shown).

GGOH stimulates osteoblast differentiation

Next, we investigated the effects of GGOH on osteoblastogenesis. In contrast to its anti-osteoclastogenic effects, GGOH stimulated osteoblast differentiation in mouse calvarial osteoblast progenitors cultured in an osteogenic medium (see Experimental Procedures). Both ALP staining (Fig. 3A) and enzymatic activity (Fig. 3B) in calvarial osteoblasts were increased following GGOH treatment; however, this effect was not due to enhanced cell proliferation (Fig. 3C). On the other hand, geranylgeranyl acetone, a synthetic derivative of GGOH obtained by replacing the terminal hydroxyl group with an acetone moiety, did not enhance ALP levels during calvarial osteoblast differentiation under our culture conditions (Fig. S1). Mature osteoblasts form the bone nodules and deposit calcium. Fifteen days after confluent cells were transferred to an osteogenic medium containing GGOH, calcium deposition was significantly increased in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3D). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis of the mRNA expression of osteoblastic genes was performed (Fig. 3E). The expression of the osteoblast transcription factors, Msh Homeobox2, Smad1 and Runx2 in cultured calvarial osteoblasts increased in a time-dependent manner following GGOH treatment. Furthermore, the expression of Col1a1 and Alpl in the calvarial osteoblasts was significantly increased by the addition of GGOH. GGOH did not increase Dlx5 and Osx (Sp7) expression but rather suppressed Dlx5 expression. Distal-less homeobox 5 (Dlx5) and Osterix (Osx) are known as early markers of osteogenic differentiation. The expression of both genes decreased during differentiation. It is possible that the Dlx5-and Osx-dependent stages had already occurred under our culture conditions. We studied the effects of GGOH using cultures of bone marrow cells and calvarial osteoblast progenitor cells in each separate culture. Next, we used a co-culture system of bone marrow cells and osteoblast progenitor cells to further verify the dual functions of GGOH in osteogenesis and osteoclastogenesis (Fig. S2). Osteoblast cells participate in osteoclast formation through the Tnfsf11/Rankl expression induced by 1α,25(OH)2D3. The formation of TRAP-positive osteoclasts, including multinucleated cells, was observed in the co-culture (Fig. S2A). However, the number of osteoclasts decreased in a dose-dependent manner after GGOH treatment. In contrast, an increase in the number of ALP-positive cells was observed in the presence of GGOH. RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated that GGOH suppressed osteoclast markers (Dcstamp and Ctsk) while enhancing osteoblast markers (Alpl and Col1a1) (Fig. S2B). The expression of Tnfsf11/Rankl was suppressed 1 day after GGOH treatment, but Tnfrsf11a/Rank expression was not. Therefore, GGOH exerts its inhibitory effect on osteoclast formation through both indirect and direct mechanisms.

Cells derived from dental pulp tissue of the teeth possess multipotent differentiation capacity, including osteogenic potential. We examined the effects of GGOH on the osteogenic differentiation of dental pulp cells isolated from mouse dental pulp (Fig. S3). The expression of the osteogenic markers Dlx5, Osx/Sp7, Alpl, and Col1a1 tended to increase to varying degrees following GGOH treatment after 3 days of culture in osteogenic medium (Fig. S3A). Dspp (Dentin sialophosphoprotein), an odontoblast-specific marker, was significantly upregulated by GGOH. GGOH also stimulated mineralization as assessed by Alizarin Red S and von Kossa staining (Fig. S3, B‒D).

Adipocytes are also derived from mesenchymal stem cells, the common progenitors of both adipocytes and osteoblasts (25). We observed the appearance of Oil Red O-stained lipid droplet cells in calvarial cells cultured in an osteogenic medium containing charcoal-treated fetal bovine serum (ct-FBS). However, GGOH suppressed the generation of Oil Red O-stained lipid droplet cells (Fig. 4, A and B). GGOH only suppressed RANKL-induced JNK phosphorylation and activated RANKL-stimulated ERK and NF-κB signaling during osteoclastogenesis (Fig. 5A). GGOH suppresses osteoclastogenesis and enhances osteoblastogenesis through FXR and LXR

Next, we examined the involvement of NRs in the GGOH-mediated upregulation of ALP in osteoblast differentiation using NR agonists and antagonists (Fig. 6). When GGOH was co-treated with an antagonist of LXR (5CPPSS-50; CPPSS), FXR (guggulsterone; Guggul), or PXR (ketoconazole; Ketoco), GGOH-mediated ALP upregulation was suppressed to varying degrees (Fig. 6A). Among them, Guggul suppressed the effects of GGOH by approximately 60 to 70%. GW3965 suppressed RANKL-induced TRAP activity during osteoclast differentiation to a level similar to that observed with GGOH (Fig. 6C). GW4064 also suppressed RANKL-induced TRAP activity but to a lesser extent than GW3965. The PXR agonists pregnenolone 16α-carbonitrile, MK-4, and rifampicin did not suppress RANKL-induced TRAP activity in osteoclasts when co-administered with RANKL. Conversely, when the LXR and FXR antagonists CPPSS and Guggul, respectively, were co-administered with GGOH and RANKL in osteoclast cultures, they reversed the GGOH-mediated suppression of osteoclast differentiation (Fig. 6D). These results suggested that the effect of GGOH on osteoblast/osteoclast differentiation may occur, at least in part, via the LXR and FXR pathways. The FXR antagonist negated the positive effects of GGOH on osteoblastogenesis, but did not completely suppress them. Furthermore, its agonist stimulated osteoblast differentiation, but was less effective than GGOH (Fig. 6, A and B). We found that the geranylgeranyl transferase one inhibitor (GGTI-298) suppressed the effect of GGOH, and co-treatment with GGTI-298 and Guggul almost completely attenuated the effect of GGOH on osteoblastogenesis (Fig. S4). Thus, GGOH may stimulate osteoblastogenesis through protein prenylation in addition to nuclear receptor activation.

GGOH prevents bisphosphonate-induced suppression of osteoblastogenesis

We investigated the effects of zoledronic acid (ZA) on the differentiation of calvarial osteoblasts (Fig. 7). GGOH inhibits LPS-induced osteoclast formation and bone resorption in vitro and in vivo

To investigate the efficacy of GGOH in treating osteoporosis, we assessed its effects in in vitro and in vivo models of bone loss. LPS, a cell wall component of gram-negative bacteria, causes inflammation and induces bone resorption. GGOH completely suppressed LPS-induced multinuclear osteoclast formation in the co-culture system of bone marrow cells and calvarial osteoblast progenitor cells (Fig. 8A). We examined the inhibitory effects of GGOH on LPS-induced calvarial bone resorption in vivo (Fig. 8B). Subcutaneous injection of LPS into mouse calvaria significantly increased TRAP staining throughout the calvaria (Fig. 8B, upper panels). The number of TRAP-positive resorption pits increased with LPS treatment compared with the PBS-treated control (Fig. 8B, lower panels). However, the LPS-induced TRAP-stained bone resorption pits were significantly reduced in mice co-treated with LPS and GGOH. Reconstructed 3D images of microfocus-computed tomography of mouse cranial bone showed that cranial bone pit formation increased and sutures were enlarged in LPS-treated mice compared with control mice (Fig. 8C). The LPS-induced cranial pit formation and suture enlargement were reversed by co-administration with GGOH. Micro-CT analysis of the calvarial bone revealed that LPS treatment decreased the bone volume/tissue volume (BV/TV) (Fig. 8D), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) (Fig. 8F), and Euler number (Fig. 8H), a parameter used to quantify bone connectivity, while it increased the bone surface/bone volume ratio (BS/BV) (Fig. 8E), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) (Fig. 8G). However, GGOH co-administration to the LPS-treated mice improved such osteoporosis-related parameters. Discussion

The mevalonate pathway is involveed not only in the cholesterol biosynthesis, but also in the regulation of nonsteroidal bioactive molecules or intermediates that play important roles in biological activity and metabolic control (28, 29, 30). GGOH is a diterpene alcohol consisting of four isoprene units, and is a metabolite synthesized from geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate (GGPP) via the mevalonate pathway in humans (22). It can also be obtained from certain vegetables and fruits (31, 32, 33). GGOH functions as a bioactive compound with various health benefits, including improvement of glucose homeostasis, anticancer and anti-inflammatory effects, production of testosterone, and positive effects on cognitive function (34, 35, 36, 37, 38). Although diverse beneficial effects of GGOH on human health have been reported, its role in bone metabolism remains obscure.

In this study, we demonstrated the inhibitory effect of GGOH on bone resorption and stimulatory effect on bone formation in vitro. We found that GGOH has a contradictory dual function in bone metabolism: suppression of RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation from bone marrow-derived osteoclast precursor cells and stimulation of osteoblast differentiation from calvaria-derived osteoblast precursor cells in each separate culture (Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3) as well as in a co-culture system (Fig. S2). We also demonstrated the effects of GGOH on osteogenic differentiation from mouse dental pulp cells. Ultimately, GGOH significantly promoted mineralization in both cell types under the same osteogenic culture conditions. The effect of GGOH on osteogenic gene expression and mineralization levels varied slightly between the dental pulp cells and calvarial osteogenic progenitor cells. This variation may reflect intrinsic heterogeneity in cell origin, epigenetic status, and responsiveness to signaling pathways, in addition to culture-associated factors, such as passage number and cell density.

Our observations indicated that farnesol had a weak effect and GGOH had a strong effect on ALP induction, whereas geranylgeranyl acetone had no effect. This suggested that both the number of isoprene units and the presence of a polar terminal group are necessary for GGOH to promote osteoblastogenesis (Figs. 1 and S1). Similar to GGOH, geranylgeranoic acid, a natural compound generated by the oxidation of GGOH, promotes osteoblast formation and inhibits osteoclast formation (39). The relationship between the structure of isoprenoids and their functions in bone metabolism remains to be elucidated.

The molecular mechanisms through which GGOH enhances osteoblast differentiation while suppressing osteoclast formation remain incompletely understood. In particular, the specific signaling pathways mediating its reciprocal regulation of osteoclastogenesis and osteoblastogenesis have not yet been defined. One possible mechanism is that GGOH targets the JNK pathway. JNK signaling is important for osteoclast and osteoblast differentiation (40, 41, 42, 43). In this study, we demonstrate that GGOH suppresses and enhances JNK phosphorylation during the induction of osteoclastogenesis and osteoblastogenesis, respectively. These findings are consistent with the dual effects of GGOH on osteoclastogenesis and osteoblastogenesis. Interestingly, this reciprocal effect of GGOH was specific to JNK among the MAPK signaling pathways. GGOH suppressed RANKL-induced JNK phosphorylation, but not other signaling pathways including ERK, p38, and IκB (Fig. 5). The mechanism by which GGOH maintains JNK activity at low levels during RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis remains to be elucidated.

Another possibility is that GGOH can regulate nuclear receptors, as shown in Figure 6, by using nuclear receptor agonists and antagonists. GGOH-induced ALP activity was significantly suppressed by FXR antagonist; conversely, the FXR agonist stimulated ALP activity in a manner similar to GGOH. However, in addition to FXR, the LXR antagonist reversed the suppression of RANKL-induced TRAP activity by GGOH, and each agonist suppressed RANKL-induced TRAP activity, similar to GGOH (Fig. 6). These results suggest that GGOH may function through FXR nuclear receptor activation during osteoblastogenesis, whereas GGOH may suppress RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation through LXR and FXR. FXR plays a role in energy metabolism, inflammatory processes, and bone metabolism; thus, it is a potential drug discovery target for various diseases (44, 45, 46). FXR knockout led to an obvious decline in bone mineral density and bone formation rate, and an increase in osteoclast number (13). The FXR agonist, GW4046, has been reported to suppress JNK phosphorylation in liver carcinogenesis (47) and osteoclastogenesis in an osteoarthritic mouse model (48). Suppression of JNK phosphorylation by GGOH treatment in RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis may be partly caused by FXR activation by GGOH in osteoclasts. On the other hand, the LXR agonist (GW3965) ameliorates osteoblast-mediated osteoclast differentiation and completely protects against OVX-induced bone loss (49). In our study, LXR agonist strongly suppressed RANKL-induced osteoclasts compared to the FXR agonist, and its antagonist inhibited the GGOH suppression of RANKL-induced TRAP activity in a dose-dependent manner. Thus, GGOH may function through LXR, in addition to FXR, in osteoclasts. It is currently unclear whether GGOH acts as a ligand for these nuclear receptors and how FXR/LXR activation is mechanistically linked to downstream signaling pathways. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the dual functions of GGOH in osteoclastogenesis and osteoblastogenesis, further comprehensive studies, including analysis of signal pathways, transcriptional regulation, and post-transcriptional modification are required. Adipocytes and osteoblasts are differentially regulated in the mesenchymal stem cell pool (25, 26). In our study, GGOH suppressed adipogenesis in calvaria-derived cells grown in osteogenic medium containing cs-FCS. In agreement with our observation, GGOH downregulates Pparg expression and suppresses pre-adipocyte differentiation (50). However, it has been reported that GGOH increased the expression of Pparg in adipocyte-lineage cells, and enhances adipogenesis induced by PPARγ agonists (51). The effect of GGOH on adipogenesis may depend on environmental factors, including serum components, cell stage of differentiation, and the origin and lineage of the cells. Adipocytes accumulate in the bone marrow during aging and obesity, and are associated with bone health (52, 53, 54). In the future, it would be interesting to examine the effects of GGOH on bone marrow adiposity associated with bone loss.

The second goal of studying the effect of GGOH on bones was to investigate its efficacy in the pathological conditions of bone loss. BRONJ is osteonecrosis of the jaw that occurs in patients receiving long-term bisphosphonates (BP) such as ZA. Multifunctional factors such as impaired bone turnover, bacterial infection, and inflammation are considered causes of BRONJ (27). We demonstrated that ZA significantly suppressed osteoblast formation, whereas GGOH protected against zoledronate-induced suppression of osteoblast differentiation (Fig. 7). Recently, Jung reported that GGOH reverses BP-induced suppression of osteoblast differentiation in human cells (55). The molecular basis of the antagonistic effects of GGOH against BP treatment in bone cells has not yet been elucidated. BP partially inhibited the effect of GGOH by stimulating osteoblastogenesis. BP inhibits farnesyl diphosphate synthetase, leading to reduced farnesyl diphosphate and GGPP synthesis and subsequent inhibition of protein prenylation (56). We found that the geranylgeranyl transferase inhibitor GGTI-298 partially inhibited the effects of GGOH (Fig. S3), suggesting that GGOH may be converted to GGPP, which is a geranylgeranylation substrate that could be responsible for the action of GGOH. GGOH may help restore cellular metabolic processes that are impaired in long-term BP-treated cells. Thus, GGOH is a potential drug target for the treatment of BRONJ.

GGOH also suppressed LPS-induced osteoclast formation and bone resorption, both in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 8). LPS stimulates TLR4 signaling, which induces an inflammatory response and is associated with osteoporosis, periodontal disease, vascular damage, and Alzheimer's disease (16, 57, 58). The inhibitory effects of GGOH on the LPS-induced pro-inflammatory responses have been demonstrated in human macrophage-like THP-1 cells (59). The anti-inflammatory properties of GGOH may be worth investigating for the treatment of inflammatory bone diseases.

Finally, regarding the positive effects of GGOH on bone metabolism, we demonstrated its anti-osteoporotic effects using an ovariectomized mouse model that mimics estrogen-depleted bone loss seen in older women. GGOH ameliorated bone loss induced by ovariectomy (Fig. 9). GGOH may protect against bone loss through its anabolic and anticatabolic effects on bone metabolism, that is stimulation of osteoblast formation and inhibition of osteoclast formation.

Our current studies suggest that GGOH may be a protective factor against bone diseases, such as inflammatory bone loss and postmenopausal osteoporosis. To develop an effective treatment for GGOH in bone diseases, further studies are required to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms involved, including comprehensive metabolomic analyses before and after treatment.

In conclusion, GGOH has a dual function of inhibiting osteoclastogenesis and promoting osteoblastogenesis. GGOH exerts osteoprotective effects against LPS-induced inflammatory bone loss and OVX-induced osteoporosis. Thus, GGOH has the potential for the treatment of osteoporosis.

Osteoclast formation from bone marrow cells

Osteoclasts were differentiated from BMMs as previously described (60). Briefly, bone marrow cells collected from the femurs and tibias of 6-week-old ddY mice were cultured in α-MEM containing 10% FBS with M-CSF (10 ng/ml) for 3 days. Then, the BMMs were further cultured in M-CSF alone or M-CSF plus RANKL (10 ng/ml) with or without GGOH for three to 4 days. The mouse macrophage cell line RAW264.7 was utilized to form osteoclasts, as described previously (24). The cells were cultured in α-MEM containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin and stimulated by RANKL (10 ng/ml), either alone or in the presence of GGOH or the test sample. After three to 4 days of culture, TRAP activity in the medium was measured and TRAP staining was performed.

Primary culture of mouse calvarial osteoblasts

Primary mouse osteoblasts were cultured according to the method reported by Udagawa et al. (61). Briefly, excised calvarial bones from 1-day-old neonatal ddY mice were sequentially digested with 0.1% collagenase (type 1) (Fujifilm-Wako Pure Chemical) and 0.2% dispase II (Godo Shusei) in α-MEM containing 0.1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cells from the second to fifth collagenase/dispase-digested cell suspensions were resuspended in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Cells were cultured for 2 days in a 100 cm2 culture dish until reaching confluence. After treatment with trypsin (0.05%)/EDTA (0.02%) (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical), the cells were sub-cultured for 4 days and stored before use.

For differentiation of osteoblasts, cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells/cm2 and cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Confluent cells were further cultured in α-MEM containing 10% ct-FBS, 50 mg/ml L-ascorbic acid and 10 mM β-glycerophosphate (defined as differentiation medium). The medium was changed every 3 days.

Primary culture of mouse dental pulp cells

Mouse dental pulp stem cells were isolated as described by Nur Akmal et al. (62) with some modifications. Incisors from adult C57BL/6 mice were removed and the dental pulp was digested with 0.2% collagenase (type 1) and 0.4% dispase II in α-MEM containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C for 1 h (10 min × six digestions) with shaking. Subsequently, the digested tissue was sequentially pipetted using a 2 ml pipette, with blue and yellow tips to dissociate cell clumps and release the cells. The cells suspension was passed through at 40 μm cell strainer (FALCON 352350). After centrifugation at 200×g for 5 min, the cells were cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS. The medium was changed every 3 days. Confluent cells were further cultured in osteogenic medium with ct-FBS, and the medium was changed every 3 days.

Formation of mouse osteoclasts in coculture of mouse bone marrow cells and calvarial osteoblasts

Primary osteoblasts prepared from newborn mouse calvaria (1 × 104 cells/well) were plated with bone marrow cells (4 × 104 cells/well) in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS on 96 well plates (63). After 1 day of cocultures, cells were treated with or without GGOH (5‒20 μM) in the presence of dexamethasone (10−8 M) and 1α,25(OH)2D3 (10−8 M). For LPS treatment, LPS (100 ng/ml) was added 1 day after co-culture.

Cell viability

Cell viability was evaluated using a Cell Proliferation Assay Kit (Promega). At the end of the culture period, 1/10 volume of the reagent was added to the culture medium and incubated for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 492 nm.

TRAP assay

For the TRAP activity, 30 μl of appropriately diluted cell culture supernatant was incubated with 30 μl of TRAP reagent (10 mM L-ascorbic acid, 40 mM sodium tartrate dihydrate, 10 mM 4-nitrophenolphosphate Na, 0.2% Triton X-100, 4 mM EDTA, 40 mM NaCl, and 40 mM sodium acetate buffer, pH 5.5) for 30 min at 37 °C. The reaction was terminated using 30 μl of 300 mM NaOH and the absorbance was measured at 405 nm.

For TRAP staining, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS, stained for TRAP activity using a leukocyte acid phosphatase kit (Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and photographed.

ALP assay

Cells were fixed with 4% PFA in PBS and permeabilized with a cold methanol-acetone (1:1) solution. The cells were stained with 2% nitro-blue tetrazolium chloride and 1% 5-bromo-4-chloro-3′-indolylphosphate in 0.1 M Tris-buffered saline (pH 9.5) for 30 min at 37 °C. ALP staining was quantified by measuring the photometric absorbance at 545 nm after treatment with 20% acetic acid, 10% SDS, and 50% DMSO.

Bone resorption assay

The bone resorption activity of the osteoclasts was assessed by measuring resorption pit formation using a bone resorption assay kit purchased from PG Research. BMMs were cultured on calcium phosphate-coated plates with M-CSF (50 ng/ml) for 3 days. RANKL (50 ng/ml) was added in the presence or absence of GGOH, and the cells were further cultured. After 7 days, the cells were removed by washing with 5% sodium hypochlorite for three to 5 minutes, followed by washing with distilled water and air-drying. The absorbed areas were observed under a microscope (ECLIPSE TS100, Nikon), and the absorbed pit area was quantified using NSI Element Image software (Nikon, https://www.microscope.healthcare.nikon.com).

Mineralization assay

Calvarial osteoblasts and dental pulp cells were cultured in an osteogenic differentiation medium containing 10% ct-FBS for 15 to 30 days until bone nodules were detected. The cultured cells were rinsed with ice-cold PBS and fixed in 70% ethanol. They were then stained with 40 mM Alizarin Red S (pH 4.2) for 10 min. After washing with water, the samples were photographed. Cultured cells were also stained with the Von Kossa stain. The fixed cells were incubated in 1% silver nitrate solution under UV light for 30‒60 min until the solution turned cloudy. After rinsing with water, the cells were incubated for 5 min in a 5% sodium thiosulfate solution and then rinsed with water. Cells stained with Von Kossa were photographed.

Oil Red O staining

Cells were fixed in 4% PFA in PBS for 1h and stained with 0.5% Oil Red O in isopropanol for 1 h. After three washes with water, cells were photographed.

In vivo LPS-induced bone loss model

Eight-week-old male ddY mice (Sankyo Labo Service) were divided into three groups (n = 5/group): control, LPS-, and LPS plus GGOH-treated groups. The calvariae were dissected, fixed in 4% PFA, and stained for TRAP activity.

Ovariectomy induced osteoporosis model mouse

Six-week-old C57BL/6J female mice (Sankyo Labo Service) were divided into three groups (n = 5/group): sham, ovariectomy (OVX), and OVX with GGOH treatment group. The mice were anesthetized with intraperitoneal injection of medetomidine hydrochloride (Kyoritsu Seiyaku; 0.3 mg/kg B W.), midazolam (Astellas Pharma; 4 mg/kg B W.) and butorphanol tartrate (Meiji Animal Health; 5 mg/kg B W.) at 0.05 ml/10 g B W./mouse. Anesthetized mice were subjected to ovariectomy (OVX) or a sham operation. OVX was performed using a bilateral dorsal abdominal approach and the ovaries and oviducts were excised. A similar procedure was performed in the sham operation group, but no tissue excision was performed. After surgery, the OXX and GGOH groups received a 10 mg/ml GGOH solution, which was diluted with 1% Tween 80 (Sigma-Aldrich) in 0.9% saline (64), by peritoneal injection at 50 mg/kg B W. every other day for 3 weeks. Sham and OVX groups were intraperitoneally injected with control vehicle (1% Tween 80 in saline, 0.05 ml/10 g B W./mouse). After 3 weeks of treatment with GGOH or the control vehicle, the mice were sacrificed, and femurs were excised for bone morphology experiments. The success of the OVX surgery was confirmed at the end of the experiment by measuring uterine weight and atrophy.

Micro-CT imaging and measurement of bone morphometric parameters

Three-dimensional reconstruction images of calvarial bone were obtained by micro-focal computed tomography scanning (MCT-CB130MF; Hitachi Medical) at the Research Institute of Bone Structure Analysis. The X-ray tube was operated at an acceleration voltage of 60 kV and current of 100 μA. The geometry of the setup and the resulting resolution constraints yielded voxel size of 32 mm × 32 mm × 32 mm. The 3-D images were analyzed using Tri-3D Bon software (Ratoc System Engineering, https://www.ratoc.co.jp/ENG/index.html). TRI/3D-Bon Micro-focal computed tomography scans of femurs were obtained using Skyscan 1172 (Bruker) at 59 kV and 100 μA, and analyzed using Nrecon and CT-analyzer (Skyscan). For trabecular bone analysis, a region of interest was defined from 0.5 to 1.5 mm proximal to the growth plate. Representative 2D transverse images were selected from the starting point of this region of interest (0.5 mm) for consistency. Trabecular bone volume (BV, mm3), total tissue volume (TV, mm3), and bone volume fraction (BV/TV, %) were calculated in the 3D mode. Static bone morphometric parameters were trabecular number (Tb.N), Tb.Th and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp). The connectivity was calculated using the Euler method of Odgaard and Gundersen (Euler’s number).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using the EZR software (https://www.jichi.ac.jp/saitama-sct/SaitamaHP.files/statmedEN.html). Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and post hoc Tukey’s tests. Data are presented as means ± SD. Cell experiments were performed in triplicates within each group across at least two independent experiments. Animal experiments were performed using five mice per group. In all analyses, p < 0.05 was taken to indicate statistical significance.
