Low regularity symplectic schemes for stochastic NLS
Jacob Armstrong-Goodall 1, Yvain Bruned 2

Abstract
We introduce a class of symplectic resonance based schemes for Schrödinger’s equation in dimension one, building on the work in [ 1 ] wherein resonance based numerical schemes were developed in the context of dispersive PDE driven by time dependent, or space-time dependent, coloured noise. We work primarily with a cubic nonlinearity, advancing the approach introduced in [ 17 ] for deriving symplectic schemes in the deterministic setting. As an example of such a scheme we derive the resonance based midpoint rule for the Stochastic NLS and analyse its convergence properties.



1
Introduction

Symplectic numerical schemes are those that preserve certain geometrical structures inherent in the solution of a differential equation. Such integrators can be seen as a discrete process possessing the same qualitative properties as the associated Hamiltonian system. The geometrical structures in question often arise in physics representing physical quantities, such as mass or energy, that are preserved over the time evolution of a system. Symplecticity is closely related to integrability which is the study of preserved properties solutions to differential equations. Probably the most famous example of an integrable system from the PDE literature is the KdV equation. This equation preserves mass, momentum, energy and many other quantities. In this work we will study numerical schemes for the nonlinear Schrödinger equation driven by space-time white noise,

i\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}^{2}u=\lambda{\left|u\right|}^{2}u+\kappa u\circ\Phi\xi(t,x),

with solutions on the one dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}
. The schemes we introduce will preserve two important quantities, the first being the mass

\int_{\mathbb{T}}|u(s,x)|^{2}dx

(1)

and the second being the stochastic perturbed Hamiltonian system,

\displaystyle\dot{u}=-\frac{\partial H_{0}}{\partial\bar{u}}-\frac{\partial H_{1}}{\partial\bar{u}}\circ\Phi\xi\quad\dot{\bar{u}}=\frac{\partial H_{0}}{\partial u}+\frac{\partial H_{1}}{\partial u}\circ\Phi\xi

(2)

where

H_{0}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int{\left|\nabla u\right|}^{2}dx+\frac{\lambda}{4}\int{\left|u\right|}^{4}dx,\quad H_{1}(u)=\frac{\kappa}{2}\int{\left|u\right|}^{2}dx.

This can formally be thought of as the Hamiltonian of the deterministic system
H_{0}(u)=\frac{1}{2}{\left|{\left|\nabla u\right|}\right|}_{L^{2}}+\frac{\lambda}{4}{\left|{\left|u\right|}\right|}_{L^{4}}
, with the addition of the work done by the noise,
H_{1}\circ\Phi\xi
.

The study of numerical methods for Hamiltonian systems is a well developed field making use of various techniques including variational, symplectic and multi-symplectic methods, see
[
3
]
for a robust review article on the topic. More recently resonance based schemes for dispersive PDE have been introduced to deal with the low regularity regime, see
[
6
]
for high order local error analysis of a very general class of equations. In
[
5
]
, more general non-linearities are considered by avoiding use of the Fourier transform, and instead the analysis is performed via nested commutators. Symmetric-in-time resonance based schemes are introduced in
[
4
]
and play a pivotal role in the construction of symplectic schemes, while in
[
17
]
, Runge-Kutta methods are derived for KdV and Schrödinger’s equation and the error analysis of the midpoint rule is performed as an example.

In the case of Schrödinger’s equation the idea in
[
17
]
is to first use polynomial interpolation to give a low regularity, symplectic, discretisation of the kernel, leading to a map of the intial conditions to an approximation of the solution at a given time. Secondly, the S-stage Runge-Kutta method is applied to the Hamiltonian system describing the evolution of the equation. Combining these concepts gives rise to a class of implicit Runge-Kutta schemes which at low orders have desirable regularity properties. These are the first instance of symplectic schemes designed to deal with low regularity initial data. The resonance based schemes introduced in
[
6
]
were applied in the stochastic setting in
[
1
]
where strong and pathwise global error analysis was performed for schemes approximating solutions to equations of the form,

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}u(t,x)+\mathcal{L}{\left(\nabla\right)}u(t,x)=|\nabla|^{\alpha}p{\left(u(t,x),\overline{u}(t,x)\right)}

(3)

\displaystyle+|\nabla|^{\beta}f(u(t,x),\overline{u}(t,x))\Phi\xi(t,x),\quad u(0,x)=v(x).

where
\Phi\xi(t,x)
is a white noise in time and coloured in space. In the sequel, we will also use the notation
\Phi dW(t,x)
. We find that the fixed point convergence, and stability of the scheme both require a restrictive bound on the time-step which is random and proportionate to the norm of the initial conditions. Due to this obstacle we only study the local error of the scheme.

In
[
1
]
it is shown that one can obtain an order
\sqrt{t}
approximation to the SNLS whith initial data
v\in H^{1}
and
\Phi\xi(t,x)\in H^{2}
in space. At higher order it is shown that no improvement is offered over traditional schemes due to lack of path-wise integrability of the stochastic integrals arising in the iterations of Duhamel’s formula. In this work we note that symplecticity in the stochastic setting is only satisfied by the averaged energy. Perhaps for this reason, the study of stochastic Hamiltonian systems began only recently, for instance, schemes derived to preserve symplectic structures were proposed by Milstein, Repin and Tetryakov in
[
18
]
for SDE, these authors overcame the restriction on the time-step by using a truncated noise, this however does not lead to a strongly convergent scheme. In
[
14
]
, Debussche and Di Menza performed numerical experiments to explore the impact of the spatial covariance of the noise on the blow-up of the solutions to the focusing cubic SNLSE. For this they employ the stochastic midpoint, the low regularity version of which is derived in this paper. Debussche and de Bouard then study the discretisation used in these experiments in
[
2
]
, wherein they observe, as we do, that the smallness condition for stability is random and too restrictive. Interestingly, with regards to the simulations in
[
14
]
, they state that the restriction caused no apparent problems in the implementation of the scheme and we reproduce this result our implementation of the schemes. The same authors also prove the convergence in probability of a mass preserving scheme for SPDE with Itô type noise in
[
13
]
. Symplectic Runge-Kutta and mean square convergence analysis of midpoint rule for SNLSE is performed in
[
8
]
and in
[
15
]
where both symplectic and multi-symplectic methods are studied. Strong convergence for the SNLSE with random initial data is studied in
[
9
,
10
]
where space-time discretisation is considered for the Stratanovich product in the former, while in the latter the time discretisation is studied for the Itô noise and damping proportional to the covariance operator. Strong convergence of an operator splitting method for the cubic SNLS with space-time Stratonovich noise (coloured in space, white in time), on
\mathbb{R}
is performed in
[
11
]
. The works focusing on obtaining strong convergence results rely on exponential integrability conditions to perform the stability and error analysis. The exponential integrability of not only the scheme, but the solution itself, seems to be a key obstacle in obtaining results about strong convergence, especially in dimensions greater than one. When it comes to implicit schemes however the bound on the time-step is restrictive enough to imply the exponential integrability but is so restrictive that stability can never be ensured.

The overarching goal in this work is to derive a class of symplectic resonance based Runge-Kutta schemes for Schrödingers equation in one dimension. In contrast to
[
1
]
we will work with equations with noise of the Stratonovich type. This is because the symplectic discretisation of the nonlinear term is necessarily implicit and Itô type noises are inherently linked to explicit discretisations of the noise. Another difference is that for the deterministic integral arising in Duhamel’s formula, instead of straightforward Taylor expansion of the operator, we will employ techniques used in
[
17
]
via the symplectic low regularity approximation to the kernel

K
(s;k,k_1,k_2,k_3):=e^-2iskk_1 + e^2isk_2k_3 - 1.

For the stochastic convolution we will use a similar approximation but note that as observed in
[
1
]
, the integrals can not be studied in the pathwise sense, meaning that strongly convergent schemes can not be constructed this way. We will also employ novel iterations of Duhamel’s formula, first introduced in
[
17
]
as follows:

u(t)=e^{i(t-s)\Delta}u(s)-ie^{it\Delta}\int_{s}^{t}F(u(r),\bar{u}(r))dr-ie^{it\Delta}\int_{s}^{t}u\Phi\circ dW(x,r)

which, when used in conjunction with the standard mild form to derive a midpoint estimate to

, produces cancellations in the second iteration of the stochastic convolution. This implies the second iteration of the stochastic convolution in Duhamel’s formula is zero up to a term of order
t^{2}
, which allows us to construct low order schemes without this term. The above considerations lead to the following class of schemes for the SNLSE

\displaystyle u_{n+1}

\displaystyle=e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{n}+te^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}\sum_{\alpha\in D}b^{(0)}_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}+\sqrt{t}e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}\sum_{\alpha\in D}b^{(1)}_{\alpha}L_{\alpha}

\displaystyle U_{\alpha}(u_{n})

\displaystyle=u_{n}+t\sum_{\tilde{\alpha}\in D}a^{(0)}_{\alpha,\tilde{\alpha}}K_{\tilde{\alpha}}+\sqrt{t}\sum_{\tilde{\alpha}\in D}a^{(1)}_{\alpha,\tilde{\alpha}}L_{\tilde{\alpha}}

where
\alpha\in D={\left\{(p,q,r):0\leq p,q,r\leq S\right\}}
and

K_{\alpha}=\mathfrak{F}^{2d}_{p}{\left(t;c_{q};U_{\alpha}(u_{n})\right)},\quad L_{\alpha}=\mathfrak{P}^{2d}_{p}{\left(t;c_{q};U_{\alpha}(u_{n})\right)}.\

Here
K_{\alpha}
and
L_{\alpha}
represent the discretisation of the deterministic and stochastic integrals in Duhamel’s formulation and
\alpha=(p,q,r)
is the coefficient arising in the S-stage Runge-Kutta iteration of the Hamiltonian. We will prove that the mass, and the sample average of the energy, are preserved under the correct assumptions on the noise and the coefficients of the discretisation. As an example of such a scheme we will derive the stochastic resonance midpoint rule

S^{n+1}_{t}{\left(u_{0}\right)}=u_{n+1}=e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{n}+e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}{\left(t\mathfrak{F}_{0}{\left(t;1;u_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right)}+\sqrt{t}\mathfrak{P}_{0}{\left(t;1;u_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right)}\right)}

where

which is the resonance based version of the scheme in
[
8
,
14
]
. We prove the strong and pathwise convergence of the midpoint scheme and note that the regularity requirements are improved as a result of the careful treatment of the oscillatory integrals. It should be pointed out that these schemes can be applied to other dispersive equations such as the wave equation, Manakov system or KdV by deriving the corresponding dicretisations of the terms in Duhamel’s formula. The only limitation is to be able to perform the symplectic discretisation of a kernel of the type (

2
Hamiltonian Equations

Our focus will primarily be on the stochastic cubic Schrödinger equation on the one dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}.

Stochastic NLS Equation (SNLSE):

i\partial_{t}u(t,x)+\partial_{x}^{2}u(t,x)-\lambda{\left|u(t,x)\right|}^{2}u(t,x)=\kappa u(t,x)\circ\Phi\xi(t,x),\quad u(0,x)=v(x).

(4)

Here
\Phi:L^{2}(\mathbb{T})\rightarrow H^{s}(\mathbb{T})
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator. We denote
\xi(t,x)=\frac{\partial^{2}W(t,x)}{\partial t\partial x}
and impose that it satisfies the identity,

\mathbb{E}{\left[\xi(t,x)\xi(s,y)\right]}=\delta(t-s)\delta(x-y),

which is the definition of space-time white noise. The operator
\Phi\Phi^{*}
can be interpreted as the spatial covariance of
\xi(t,x)
, specifically, if
\Phi
is defined through a kernel
K
as follows:

\Phi u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}K(x,y)u(y)dy.

Then the correlation of the noise is given by

\mathbb{E}[\Phi\xi(t,x)\Phi\xi(s,y)]=c(x,y)\delta(t-s)

with

c(x,y)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}K(x,z)K(y,z)dz,

reflecting the fact that increasing the spatial covariance is equivalent to regularisation.

The mild formulation or the Duhamel formulation of the cubic SNLSE is

\displaystyle u(t)

\displaystyle=e^{it\Delta}v-i\lambda e^{it\Delta}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-is\Delta}u(s){\left|u(s)\right|}^{2}ds

(5)

\displaystyle-i\kappa e^{it\Delta}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-is\Delta}u(s)\circ\Phi dW(s).

Consider the infinite-dimensional phase space
M
with coordinates
\mu_{k}
and momenta
\bar{u}_{k}
, for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then the Hamiltonian is a function
H:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and the solution to the Hamiltonian system is a curve
{\left(u_{k}(t),\bar{u}_{k}(t)\right)}
in
M
which obeys the Hamiltonian equations of motion:

\dot{u}_{k}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial\bar{u}_{k}},\quad\dot{\bar{u}}_{k}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial u_{k}}.

(6)

When the Hamiltonian is time independent,
\partial H/\partial t=0
, the value of the function
H(u,\bar{u})
is conserved over the evolution of the solutions of the equation. This conservation law can also be expressed via the symplectic form,

\omega(u,\bar{u})=\int du(t,x)\wedge d\bar{u}(t,x)dx=\int dv(x)\wedge d\bar{v}(x)dx,\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(7)

The pair
(M,\omega)
is a symplectic manifold. This identity corresponds to the conservation of energy in physical systems.

2.0.1
The Deterministic Setting

First we consider the deterministic setting in which
\kappa=0
,

i\partial_{t}u(t,x)+\partial_{x}^{2}u(t,x)-\lambda{\left|u(t,x)\right|}^{2}u(t,x)=0,\quad u(0,x)=v(x).

(8)

When considering a periodic boundary, the equation (
8
) arises from the following Hamiltonian:

H(u)=\int{\left(\frac{1}{2}{\left|\partial_{x}u\right|}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{4}{\left|u\right|}^{4}\right)}dx,

(9)

which in physics is interpreted as the energy of the system, and in the case of the defocusing NLSE, is a conserved quantity.
In Fourier space, one has
\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}v)(k)=ik\hat{v}_{k}
, which gives

H(u,\bar{u})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}{\left|k\right|}^{2}u_{k}\bar{u}_{k}+\frac{\lambda}{4}\sum_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4}\in\mathbb{Z}}u_{k_{1}}\bar{u}_{k_{2}}u_{k_{3}}\bar{u}_{k_{4}}.

(10)

The solution of (
8

{\left(u_{k}(t),\bar{u}_{k}(t)\right)}
obeying (
6

8

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\int{\left|u\right|}^{2}dx=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}u_{k}\bar{u}_{k},

(11)

The Hamiltonian system is defined on the phase space
\mathcal{P}_{s}(\mathbb{R}):=l^{2}_{s}(\mathbb{R})\times l^{2}_{s}(\mathbb{R})
where for
s\in\mathbb{R}

l^{2}_{s}(\mathbb{R}):={\left\{(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}}:\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}k^{2s}{\left|a_{k}\right|}^{2}<\infty\right\}}

is a Hilbert space for the standard norm
{\left|{\left|a\right|}\right|}_{s}^{2}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}{\left|k\right|}^{2s}{\left|a_{k}\right|}^{2}
.

2.0.2
The SNLSE as a Hamiltonian System

We consider generic Hamiltonian systems in one dimension of the form

\dot{u}_{k}=\frac{\delta H}{\delta\bar{u}_{k}}+\frac{\delta\hat{H}}{\delta\bar{u}_{k}}\circ\Phi\xi,\quad\dot{\bar{u}}_{k}=-\frac{\delta H}{\delta u_{k}}-\frac{\delta\hat{H}}{\delta u_{k}}\circ\Phi\xi.

Here

(14)

As in (

\displaystyle\hat{H}(u,\bar{u})=\frac{\kappa}{2}\sum_{k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}}u_{k_{1}}\bar{u}_{k_{1}},\quad\Phi\xi=\Phi_{k_{2}}dW_{k_{2}}(t).

Symplectic Resonance-Based Runge-Kutta Scheme for Dispersive PDE

In this Section, we present the derivation of resonance-based Runge-Kutta methods in the deterministic case, following the treatment laid out in
[
17
]
. We first rewrite the Duhamel formulation (
5
) in Fourier space with only the deterministic part

\displaystyle u_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-itk^{2}}v_{k}-i\lambda\sum_{k=-k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}e^{-itk^{2}}

(21)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}e^{isk^{2}}\bar{u}_{k_{1}}(s)u_{k_{2}}(s)u_{k_{3}}(s)ds,

where the operator
e^{it\Delta}
is assigned in the Fourier space to
e^{-itk^{2}}
, and the point-wise product is mapped to the convolution product. Then, one observes the following decomposition.

u_{k}(t)=e^{-itk^{2}}v_{k}+R_{k}(t).

(22)

Here,
R_{k}(t)
is of order
\mathcal{O}(t)
. One substitutes (
22
) inside the integral of the right-hand side of (
21
), in order to obtain

u_{k}(t)=e^{-itk^{2}}v_{k}-i\lambda\sum_{k=-k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}e^{-itk^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{is(k^{2}+k_{1}^{2}-k_{2}^{2}-k_{3}^{2})}ds\bar{v}_{k_{1}}v_{k_{2}}v_{k_{3}}+\mathcal{O}(t^{2}).

The oscillatory integral in the expansion above can be rewritten as

-i\lambda\sum_{k+k_{1}=k_{2}+k_{3}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-2iskk_{1}+2isk_{2}k_{3}}ds.

(23)

In
[
17
]
, to ensure the symplecticity of their schemes, the authors introduce the following approximation.

\displaystyle e^{-2iskk_{1}+2isk_{2}k_{3}}

\displaystyle=e^{-2iskk_{1}}+e^{2isk_{2}k_{3}}-1+{\left(e^{-2iskk_{1}}-1\right)}{\left(e^{2isk_{2}k_{3}}-1\right)}

(24)

\displaystyle\approx e^{-2iskk_{1}}+e^{2isk_{2}k_{3}}-1:=\mathcal{K}(s;k,k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}).

The main idea is that, for obtaining a low-regularity scheme, one wants to avoid discretising the operator
e^{isP(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})}
with
P(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})=-2kk_{1}+2k_{2}k_{3}

via a straightforward Taylor expansion. This expansion would require more regularity on the initial datum
v
.
The original idea in
[
19
]
was to split the operator into a dominant part
P_{\text{\tiny{dom}}}
and a lower part
P_{\text{\tiny{low}}}
:

P=P_{\text{\tiny{dom}}}+P_{\text{\tiny{low}}},\quad P_{\text{\tiny{dom}}}=2k_{1}^{2},\quad P_{\text{\tiny{low}}}=-2k_{1}(k_{2}+k_{3})+2k_{2}k_{3}.

(25)

Then, one performs a Taylor expansion on the lower part and integrates the dominant part. The result of the integral can be mapped back to physical space as

\int^{t}_{0}e^{2isk_{1}^{2}}ds=\frac{e^{2itk_{1}^{2}}-1}{2ik_{1}^{2}}\rightarrow\varphi_{1}(-2i\Delta t),\quad\varphi_{1}(z)=\frac{e^{z}-1}{z}.

Such a naive splitting, (
25
), does not give a symplectic scheme. One has to change it into:

P=S_{\text{\tiny{dom}}}+S_{\text{\tiny{low}}},\quad S_{\text{\tiny{dom}}}=-2kk_{1},\quad S_{\text{\tiny{low}}}=2k_{2}k_{3}.

To preserve symmetries, one has to perform the same treatment for the dominant and the lower part, in the sense that they both need to be integrated exactly:

\int^{t}_{0}e^{-2iskk_{1}}ds=\varphi_{1}(-2itkk_{1}).

Then, using the fact that

-2kk_{1}=k^{2}+k_{1}^{2}-(k_{2}+k_{3})^{2},

one has

\frac{e^{-2itkk_{1}}}{kk_{1}}\bar{v}_{k_{1}}v_{k_{2}}v_{k_{3}}\mapsto\partial_{x}^{-1}e^{-it\Delta}{\left(\partial_{x}^{-1}e^{-it\Delta}\bar{v}e^{it\Delta}v^{2}\right)}.

One performs the same kind of integration on the lower part. This approach can be generalised to higher orders by introducing the operator
P_{d}[f]
, defined as follows. We first fix
d\in\mathbb{N}
distinct interpolation points
0\leq\gamma_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\gamma_{d}\leq 1
, then
P_{d}[f]
is the unique interpolation polynomial of degree
d-1
that matches the function values of
f
at the points
t\gamma_{j}
:

P_{d}[f](t\gamma_{j})=f(t\gamma_{j}).

(26)

A good approximation of the kernel
\mathop{\mathrm{exp}}\nolimits(-2iskk_{1}+2isk_{2}k_{3})
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{2d}(s;k,k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})

\displaystyle:=e^{-2iskk_{1}}P_{d}[e^{2i\cdot k_{2}k_{3}}](s)+e^{2isk_{2}k_{3}}P_{d}[e^{-2i\cdot kk_{1}}](s)

\displaystyle\quad-P_{d}[e^{2i\cdot k_{2}k_{3}}](s)P_{d}[e^{-2i\cdot kk_{1}}](s).

From
[
17
, Proposition 3.6]
, one has

\mathcal{K}_{2d}(s;k,k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})-e^{-2iskk_{1}+2isk_{2}k_{3}}=\mathcal{O}(t^{d+1}(kk_{1}k_{2}k_{3})^{d/2}).

(27)

Let us mention that the term
\frac{1}{kk_{1}}
can be mapped back to physical space only in dimension one. For higher dimensions, this is not true, and one must perform further approximations that require more regularity of the initial datum. The choice (
24
) guarantees the following symmetry, crucial for having a symplectic scheme:

\mathcal{K}_{2d}(s;k,k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})=\overline{\mathcal{K}_{2d}(s;k_{2},k_{3},k,k_{1})}.

(28)

This property ensures the preservation of the symplectic form, (
7
). We first rewrite an approximation of the Duhamel formulation;

\displaystyle u_{k}(t)

\displaystyle\approx e^{-itk^{2}}v_{k}-i\lambda\sum_{k=-k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}e^{-itk^{2}}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{K}_{2d}(s;k,k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})\bar{w}_{k_{1}}(s)w_{k_{2}}(s)w_{k_{3}}(s)ds+\mathcal{O}(t^{2}k_{1}k_{2})

where
w_{k_{1}}(s)=e^{-isk_{1}^{2}}v_{k_{1}}
. Then, we want to use a polynomial-type interpolation for the term involving the unknown:

w_{k_{i}}(s)\approx\sum_{p=0}^{S}\frac{s^{p}}{t^{p}}\sum_{q=0}a_{p,q}w_{k_{i}}(c_{q}t),

for some
S\in\mathbb{N}
,
0\leq c_{0}\leq c_{1}<\cdots<c_{S}\leq 1
,
a_{p,q}\in\mathbb{C}
.
Using this interpolation, one defines the map,

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathcal{F}^{2d}_{p}(t,c_{q};v)

\displaystyle:=-i\lambda\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{ixk}\sum_{k+k_{1}=k_{2}+k_{3}}\frac{1}{t^{p+1}}\int_{0}^{c_{q}t}\mathcal{K}_{2d}(s;k,k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})s^{p}ds\overline{v}_{k_{1}}v_{k_{2}}v_{k_{3}},

where
c_{q}\in[0,1]
.
This map, which describes the discretisation of the integral in (
23
), leads to the following scheme for the NLSE,

\displaystyle u_{n+1}

\displaystyle=e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{n}+te^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}\sum_{\alpha\in D}b^{\alpha}K_{\alpha},

(29)

\displaystyle K_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{F}^{2d}_{p}{\left(t;c_{q};u_{n}+t\sum_{\tilde{\alpha}\in D}a^{\tilde{\alpha}}_{\alpha}K_{\tilde{\alpha}}\right)},\quad D={\left\{(p,q,r):0\leq p,q,r\leq S\right\}}.

where
\alpha=(p,q,r)
and
\mathfrak{F}^{2d}_{p}
is the physical space map of
\mathcal{F}^{2d}_{p}
.
Under the correct constraints on the parameters
a^{\alpha}_{\tilde{\alpha}}
and
b^{\alpha}
, this class of schemes is capable of conserving the Hamiltonian and the
L^{2}
norm. We quote
[
17
, Theorem 4.1]
.

Theorem 3.1

Suppose that
u\in H^{r}
,
r>3/2
, and that the real-valued coefficients
b^{\alpha}
,
a^{\tilde{\alpha}}_{\alpha}

satisfy

b^{\tilde{\alpha}}b^{\alpha}=b^{\alpha}a^{\tilde{\alpha}}_{\alpha}+b^{\tilde{\alpha}}a^{\alpha}_{\tilde{\alpha}},\quad\forall\alpha,\tilde{\alpha}\in S,

Then the Runge–Kutta resonance-based schemes (
29
)
preserve the corresponding quadratic first integrals exactly.

4
Stochastic Symplectic Resonance-Based Runge-Kutta Method

We now explain how to derive the midpoint rule for the SNLSE; the idea is to adapt the general scheme given in
[
6
]
.
We recall the Duhamel formulation, given by (
4
) in physical space, and in Fourier space by,

\displaystyle u_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-itk^{2}}v_{k}-i\lambda\sum_{k=-k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}e^{-itk^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{isk^{2}}\bar{u}_{k_{1}}(s)u_{k_{2}}(s)u_{k_{3}}(s)ds

(30)

\displaystyle-i\kappa\sum_{k=k_{1}+k_{2}}e^{-itk^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{isk^{2}}u_{k_{1}}(s)\Phi_{k_{2}}\circ dW_{k_{2}}(s).

Iterating the Duhamel formula and truncating at order

\mathcal{O}(t^{\frac{3}{2}})
, one gets

\displaystyle u_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-itk^{2}}v_{k}-i\lambda\sum_{k=-k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}e^{-itk^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{is(k^{2}+k_{1}^{2}-k_{2}^{2}-k_{3}^{2})}ds\bar{v}_{k_{1}}v_{k_{2}}v_{k_{3}}

\displaystyle\quad-i\kappa\sum_{k=k_{1}+k_{2}}e^{-itk^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{isk^{2}}e^{-isk_{1}^{2}}v_{k_{1}}\Phi_{k_{2}}\circ dW_{k_{2}}(s)

\displaystyle\quad-\kappa^{2}\sum_{k=k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}e^{-itk^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{isk^{2}}

\displaystyle e^{-is(k_{1}+k_{2})^{2}}\int_{0}^{s}e^{ir(k_{1}+k_{2})^{2}}v_{k_{1}}\Phi_{k_{2}}\Phi_{k_{3}}\circ dW_{k_{2}}(r)\circ dW_{k_{3}}(s)+\mathcal{O}(t^{\frac{3}{2}}).

In the next subsection, we explain how to discretise the stochastic integral of order
\sqrt{t}
and show that the integral of order
t
disappears up to order
t^{2}
.

4.1
Discretisation of Stochastic Iterated Integrals

The first stochastic integral is given in Fourier space by

I_{1}=-i\kappa\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{ikx}\sum_{k=k_{1}+k_{2}}\Phi_{k_{2}}v_{k_{1}}e^{-it(k_{1}+k_{2})^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{is(kk_{2}+k_{1}k_{2})}\circ dW_{k_{2}}(s).

(31)

One way of approaching the discretisation of this integral is to perform integration by parts to rewrite the right-hand side as a random boundary term and a Riemann integral over a Brownian path. The Riemann integral can then be approximated via a quadrature rule. For instance, we could write

\int_{0}^{t}e^{isP(k)}dW(s)\;=\;e^{itP(k)}\,W(t)-iP(k)\int_{0}^{t}e^{isP(k)}\,W(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,

and approximate this by

\int_{0}^{t}e^{isP(k)}\,W(s)ds=\frac{t}{2}\Bigl(W(0)+e^{itP(k)}W(t)\Bigr)+\mathcal{O}\!\bigl(t^{3/2}\bigr).

It is not immediately obvious whether it offers any advantage or whether this discretisation will preserve relevant structures. For now, we proceed with a simple Taylor expansion of the operator, which suffices to obtain an improvement in the regularity of initial data,

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=-i\kappa\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{ikx}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
k=k_{1}+k_{2}\end{subarray}}\Phi_{k_{2}}v_{k_{1}}e^{-it(k_{1}+k_{2})^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{is(kk_{2}+k_{1}k_{2})}\circ dW_{k_{2}}(s)

(32)

\displaystyle\approx-i\kappa\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{ikx}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
k=k_{1}+k_{2}\end{subarray}}\Phi_{k_{2}}v_{k_{1}}e^{-it(k_{1}+k_{2})^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}(1+is(kk_{2}+k_{1}k_{2})

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}(s^{2}(kk_{2}+k_{1}k_{2})^{2}))\circ dW_{k_{2}}(s)

\displaystyle=-i\kappa\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{ikx}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
k=k_{1}+k_{2}\end{subarray}}\Phi_{k_{2}}v_{k_{1}}e^{-it(k_{1}+k_{2})^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\circ dW_{k_{2}}(s)

\displaystyle-\kappa\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{ikx}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
k=k_{1}+k_{2}\end{subarray}}\Phi_{k_{2}}v_{k_{1}}e^{-it(k_{1}+k_{2})^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}is(kk_{2}+k_{1}k_{2})\circ dW_{k_{2}}(s)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}{\left(\kappa\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
k=k_{1}+k_{2}\end{subarray}}k_{1}k_{2}^{2}t^{3/2}\right)}\quad a.s.

For a structure-preserving approximation, one has to replace
u_{k_{1}}
by a midpoint approximation:

v_{k_{1}}:=u_{k_{1}}(0)\rightarrow\frac{u_{k_{1}}(0)+e^{itk_{1}^{2}}u_{k_{1}}(t)}{2},

or a similar symmetric approximation at various points. In the end, one gets the following almost sure approximation:

I_{1}^{S}=-i\kappa\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{ikx}\sum_{k=k_{1}+k_{2}}\Phi_{k_{2}}\frac{u_{k_{1}}(0)+e^{itk_{1}^{2}}u_{k_{1}}(t)}{2}e^{-it(k_{1}+k_{2})^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\circ dW_{k_{2}}(s).

In a general setting, one can consider the map
\mathcal{P}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{p}^{2d}(t;c_{q};v)

\displaystyle=-i\kappa\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{ixk}\frac{1}{t^{p+\frac{1}{2}}}\sum_{k=k_{1}+k_{2}}v_{k_{1}}\Phi_{k_{2}}

(33)

\displaystyle\int^{c_{q}t}_{0}P_{d}[e^{i\cdot(kk_{2}+k_{1}k_{2})}](s)s^{p}\circ dW_{k_{2}}(s).

For the double-iterated stochastic integral
I_{2}
, one has

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=-\kappa^{2}\sum_{k=k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}e^{-itk^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{isk^{2}}

\displaystyle e^{-is(k_{1}+k_{2})^{2}}v_{k_{1}}\Phi_{k_{2}}\Phi_{k_{3}}\int_{0}^{s}e^{ir(k_{1}+k_{2})^{2}}e^{-irk_{1}^{2}}\circ dW_{k_{2}}(r)\circ dW_{k_{3}}(s).

We proceed by expansion of both dominant and lower terms to get the following discretisation:

I_{2}\approx-\kappa^{2}\sum_{k=k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}v_{k_{1}}\Phi_{k_{2}}\Phi_{k_{3}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\circ dW_{k_{2}}(r)\circ dW_{k_{3}}(s)+\mathcal{O}(k_{1}k^{2}_{2}k_{3}^{2}t^{\frac{3}{2}}).

This discretisation was proposed in
[
1
]
, but it will not preserve the structure. One has to make use of the key idea in
[
4
]
, which is to iterate the Duhamel formula symmetrically, for example, by using a midpoint rule. Then,
I_{2}
is transformed into

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=-\kappa^{2}\sum_{k=k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}e^{-itk^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{is(k^{2}-(k_{1}+k_{2})^{2})}{\left(\frac{1}{2}v_{k_{1}}\Phi_{k_{2}}\int_{0}^{s}e^{ir(k_{1}+k_{2})^{2}}\right.}

\displaystyle{\left.e^{-irk_{1}^{2}}\circ dW_{k_{2}}(r)+\frac{1}{2}v_{k_{1}}\Phi_{k_{2}}\Phi_{k_{3}}\int_{t}^{s}e^{ir(k_{1}+k_{2})^{2}}e^{-irk_{1}^{2}}\circ dW_{k_{2}}(r)\right)}\circ dW_{k_{3}}(s).

Then, proceeding with a full Taylor expansion on the various operators, one gets

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=-\kappa^{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{ikx}\sum_{k=k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}}e^{itk}

\displaystyle\quad v_{k_{1}}\Phi_{k_{2}}\Phi_{k_{3}}\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{s}\circ dW_{k_{2}}(r)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{t}^{s}\circ dW_{k_{2}}(r)\bigg)\circ dW_{k_{3}}(s)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathcal{O}(k_{1}k_{2}^{2}k_{3}^{2}t^{2})\quad a.s.

We observe that

\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{s}\circ dW_{k_{2}}(r)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{t}^{s}\circ dW_{k_{2}}(r)=W_{k_{2}}(s)-\frac{1}{2}W_{k_{2}}(t),

and so, integrating against
W_{k_{3}}(t)
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{t}{\left(W_{k_{2}}(s)-\frac{1}{2}W_{k_{2}}(t)\right)}\circ dW_{k_{3}}(s)=\int_{0}^{t}W_{k_{2}}(s)\circ dW_{k_{3}}(s)-\frac{1}{2}W_{k_{2}}(t)W_{k_{3}}(t).

But in Stratonovich theory, we have the following,

\int_{0}^{t}W_{k_{2}}(s)\circ dW_{k_{3}}(s)+\int_{0}^{t}W_{k_{3}}(s)\circ dW_{k_{2}}(s)=W_{k_{2}}(t)W_{k_{3}}(t),

which implies that

I_{2}=0+\mathcal{O}(k_{1}k_{2}^{2}k_{3}^{2}t^{2})\quad a.s.

(34)

In short, when constructing first-order schemes for (
4
) with Stratonovich-type noise, it makes sense to disregard the second iteration and to set the approximation of
I_{2}
to be zero. This simplification has been observed in
[
4
]
for double-iterated integrals in the deterministic setting.

4.2
Discretisation Maps Properties

When verifying that our scheme preserves certain structures, we will need several hypotheses. These are based on formal calculations that are performed when deriving the relevant properties.

Hypothesis 4.1

The smoothing, or covariance, operator of the noise is a self-adjoint Hilbert-Schmidt operator
\Phi:L^{2}\rightarrow H^{2}
, which is symmetric in its Fourier coefficients, i.e.

\Phi^{*}_{k}=\Phi_{k},\quad\Phi_{k}=\Phi_{-k}.

Remark 4.1

An example of

\frac{1}{k^{2}},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash{\left\{0\right\}},\end{cases}

We conclude by Hypothesis
4.2
and Lemma
5.5
that we have

d\bm{\mu}^{n+1}\wedge d\bm{\nu}^{n+1}-d\bm{\mu}^{n}\wedge d\bm{\nu}^{n}=0.

6
Convergence Analysis of the Midpoint Rule

In the previous sections, we have developed the theory for a class of
\mathcal{O}{\left(\sqrt{t}\right)}
resonance-based Runge-Kutta schemes for the non-linear Schrödinger equation. We will now look closely at the convergence of one particular scheme, specifically the one introduced in Example
1
. Recall that in this example, we derived the stochastic resonance-based midpoint rule, which is written for initial conditions
u_{0}
as follows:

S^{n+1}_{t}{\left(u_{0}\right)}=u_{n+1}=e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{n}+e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}{\left(t\mathfrak{F}_{0}{\left(t;1;u_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right)}+\sqrt{t}\mathfrak{P}_{0}{\left(t;1;u_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right)}\right)}

(40)

where

u_{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{u_{n}+e^{-it\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{n+1}}{2}.

and the random variables in the scheme are

\sqrt{t}W_{n}(x)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}{\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{ikx}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\circ dW(s)\right)}.

For conservation of mass and energy we require real-valued noise
W_{n}(x)
. This is equivalent to symmetry of frequencies in Fourier space, i.e, the condition that the Fourier coefficients satisfy

W_{n}(-k)=\overline{W_{n}(k)}=W_{n}(k).

(41)

Thus, when the noise is real, the modulus
|W_{n}(x)|
is simply the absolute value of the real-valued process
W_{n}(x)
.
If
W_{n}(x)
is complex,
W_{n}(x)\overline{W_{n}(x)}=|W_{n}(x)|^{2}
, hence, essentially the same notation extends to a complex valued process for which our scheme still converges, albeit without the conservation properties.

To perform fixed-point iterations, we will introduce an auxiliary function with fixed randomness. The stochastic discretisation map will be altered to

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{*}_{0}(t;1;v,X)

\displaystyle=-i\kappa\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{ixk}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\sum_{k=k_{1}+k_{2}}v_{k_{1}}\Phi_{k_{2}}X_{k_{2}},

(42)

where
X
is a fixed random variable. Now we define the auxiliary process

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{t}(w)

\displaystyle:=e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}u_{n}+te^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}\mathfrak{F}_{0}{\left(t;1;u_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{w}\right)}+\sqrt{t}e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}\mathfrak{P}^{*}_{0}{\left(t;1;u_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{w},W_{n}\right)},

where
W_{n}(t)
is a centred Gaussian vector with variance
t
, inherited from the
n^{\text{th}}
iteration of our scheme,
S^{n}_{t}(u_{0})
. To simplify the notation, we have defined:

u_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{w}=\frac{u_{n}+e^{-it\partial_{x}^{2}}w}{2}.

Note that iterations of
\mathfrak{S}_{t}(\cdot)
are not random, because the random variable is fixed from the beginning. In the next subsection, we will demonstrate that each iteration of Scheme (
40
) has an almost sure fixed point, provided the timestep is sufficiently small.

6.1
Existence and Stability

Because the methods discussed in this paper are implicit, we must employ a fixed-point iteration to solve the method at each time step; for this reason, we aim to demonstrate that such a process indeed converges to the correct solution. This can be captured in the following statement:

Theorem 6.1

(Convergence of Fixed Point Iterations)

Let
R_{n}>0
and
\alpha>1
. Then, for any
u_{n},v,w\in B_{R_{n}}(H^{\alpha})={\left\{u\in H^{\alpha}:{\left|{\left|u\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}<R_{n}\right\}}
we have that
\mathfrak{S}_{t}^{j}(u_{n})
converges to
u_{n+1}
almost surely, i.e.

\mathbb{P}{\left(\mathop{\mathrm{lim}}_{j\rightarrow\infty}\mathfrak{S}_{t}^{j}(u_{n})=u_{n+1}\right)}=1.

Proof.

We want to show that for sufficiently small, but positive
t
, that the iterates of
\mathfrak{S}_{t}(u_{n})
form a convergent sequence. We have

\displaystyle{\left|{\left|\mathfrak{F}_{0}(t;1;u_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{w})-\mathfrak{F}_{0}(t;1;u_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{v})\right|}\right|}^{2}_{H^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=t^{2}\frac{{\left|\mu\right|}^{2}}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\langle k\rangle^{2\alpha}\bigg|\sum_{k+k_{1}=k_{2}+k_{3}}{\left[\frac{e^{-2itkk_{1}}-1}{-2itkk_{1}}+\frac{e^{2itk_{2}k_{3}}-1}{-2itk_{2}k_{3}}-1\right]}

\displaystyle\bigg({\left(\bar{u}^{n}_{k_{1}}+\bar{w}_{k_{1}}\right)}{\left({u}^{n}_{k_{2}}+{w}_{k_{2}}\right)}{\left({u}^{n}_{k_{3}}+{w}_{k_{3}}\right)}-{\left({\bar{u}}^{n}_{k_{1}}+{\bar{v}}_{k_{1}}\right)}{\left({u}^{n}_{k_{2}}+{v}_{k_{2}}\right)}{\left({u}^{n}_{k_{3}}+{v}_{k_{3}}\right)}\bigg)\bigg|^{2}.

By applying the following factorisation to the non-linearity,

\displaystyle abc-def

\displaystyle=abc-bcd+bcd-edc+cde-efd

(43)

\displaystyle=(a-d)bc+(b-e)dc+(c-f)de,

and observing that the exponential functions in
k,k_{1},k_{2}
and
k_{3}
are uniformly bounded, we have, by the triangle inequality, that

t{\left|{\left|\mathfrak{F}_{0}(t;1;u^{v}_{n+\frac{1}{2}})-\mathfrak{F}_{0}(t;1;u^{w}_{n+\frac{1}{2}})\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}\leq tC_{R_{n}}{\left|{\left|v-w\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}.

Then, for the stochastic term, we have

\sqrt{t}{\left|{\left|\mathfrak{P}^{*}_{0}(t;1;u^{v}_{n+\frac{1}{2}},X)-\mathfrak{P}^{*}_{0}(t;1;u^{w}_{n+\frac{1}{2}},X)\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}\leq\sqrt{t}\Phi{\left|X\right|}{\left|{\left|v-w\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}.

This implies that for
v,w\in B_{R_{n}}(H^{\alpha})
, we have

{\left|{\left|\mathfrak{S}_{t}(w)-\mathfrak{S}_{t}(v)\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}\leq{\left(C_{R_{n}}t+C_{\Phi,X}\sqrt{t}\right)}{\left|{\left|v-w\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}.

It follows that

{\left|{\left|\mathfrak{S}_{t}(e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}w)-\mathfrak{S}_{t}(e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}v)\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}\leq{\left(C_{R_{n}}t+\sqrt{t}C_{\Phi,W_{n}}\right)}{\left|{\left|v-w\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}},

where
R_{n}
is the radius of the ball containing
u_{n}
, and
W_{n}
is the random variable introduced by
S^{n}_{t}(u_{0})
. Now, selecting
t_{n}
such that
t_{n}C_{R_{n}}+\sqrt{t_{n}}C_{\Phi,W_{n}}<1
, i.e.

0<t_{n}<\frac{C_{\Phi,W_{n}}^{2}+2C_{R_{n}}}{2C_{R_{n}}^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{4C_{R_{n}}C_{\Phi,W_{n}}^{2}+C_{\Phi,W_{n}}^{4}}{C_{R_{n}}^{4}}},

(44)

we apply the Banach fixed point theorem to prove the result. Note that this holds almost surely, as the random variable is infinite with negligible probability, but it is nonetheless possible.

Remark 6.2

As a result of the condition (
44
), the scheme can not be adapted and well-posed with probability
1
. To be well-posed, we require the fixed point iterations to converge and to make this probability
1
we need to select
t_{n}
based on the random variables in the
n^{\text{th}}
iteration, which is only
\mathcal{F}_{t_{n+1}}-
adapted.

Remark 6.3

(Problem concerning fixed point convergence)

We note that the bound (
44
) is very restrictive. In fact, it is clear that due to the randomness in equation (
4
), for any fixed time step
t
, the scheme will be ill-posed with non-negligible probability. This problem is observed in
[
2
]
, where convergence of a discretisation is proved as
t\rightarrow 0
. The scheme in
[
2
]
has been successfully applied in simulations of the SNLSE in
[
14
]
. To prove weak convergence of similar schemes, Milstein, Repin, and Tretyakov
[
18
]
use a truncated noise; this approach does not allow strong convergence, however, as the error between the truncated variable and the randomness driving (
4
) can be arbitrarily large.

In the next theorem, we will show that the stability of the scheme requires the same condition as the fixed-point convergence.

Theorem 6.4

(Stability)

Fix
\alpha>1
and let
S_{t}^{n}(u)=u_{n}
. For
0\leq n\leq N
, define
R_{n}=\mathop{\mathrm{min}}{\left\{R\in\mathbb{N}:v_{n},w_{n}\in B_{R}(H^{\alpha})\right\}}
and
R^{*}=\mathop{\mathrm{max}}{\left\{R_{n}<\infty:n\leq N\right\}}
. Define the stopping time
\tau=tn^{*}
, which occurs if
R_{n^{*}}=\infty
. Let
T=Nt
be the length of the simulation, and finally, let
m=\mathop{\mathrm{min}}{\left\{N,n^{*}\right\}}
. Then we have

\mathop{\mathrm{lim}}_{{\left|{\left|v-w\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}\rightarrow 0}\mathop{\mathrm{sup}}_{0\leq n\leq m}\mathbb{P}{\left({\left|{\left|S_{t}^{n}(v)-S^{n}_{t}(w)\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}\geq K\right)}=0.

Proof of Theorem
6.4
.

By the hypothesis of the theorem, we have
v,w\in B_{R_{0}}(H^{\alpha})
. Setting
\tilde{v}=\frac{v+e^{-it\partial_{x}^{2}}S_{t}(v)}{2}
and
\tilde{w}=\frac{w+e^{-it\partial_{x}^{2}}S_{t}(w)}{2}
, we find that

\displaystyle{\left|{\left|S_{t}(v)-S_{t}(w)\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq{\left|{\left|e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}(v-w)\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle+t{\left|{\left|e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}\mathfrak{F}_{0}{\left(c_{q};1;\tilde{v}\right)}-e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}\mathfrak{F}_{0}{\left(c_{q};1;\tilde{w}\right)}\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{t}{\left|{\left|e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}\mathfrak{P}_{0}{\left(c_{q};1;\tilde{v}\right)}-e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}\mathfrak{P}_{0}{\left(c_{q};1;\tilde{w}\right)}\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}.

Clearly the first term is bounded by a constant
C_{R_{0}}
since
e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}
is unitary on
H^{\alpha}
. For the second term, we follow the argument laid out in
[
17
]
, which we reproduce here for convenience. First, we note that

\displaystyle{\left|{\left|\tilde{v}-\tilde{w}\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle={\left|{\left|\frac{v+e^{-it\partial_{x}^{2}}S_{t}(v)}{2}-\frac{w+e^{-it\partial_{x}^{2}}S_{t}(w)}{2}\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}{\left({\left|{\left|v-w\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}+{\left|{\left|S_{t}(v)-S_{t}(w)\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}\right)}.

Then since
w_{n},v_{n},S_{t}(w_{n}),S_{t}(v_{n})\in B_{R^{*}}(H^{\alpha})
, we have

\displaystyle t{\left|{\left|e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}\mathfrak{F}{\left(t;1;v\right)}-e^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}\mathfrak{F}{\left(t;1;w\right)}\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}\leq tC_{R^{*}}{\left|{\left|{\left(v-w\right)}+S_{t}(v)-S_{t}(w)\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}.

For the stochastic term, we use the estimate

\displaystyle\sqrt{t}{\left|{\left|\mathfrak{P}_{0}(t;1;v_{n+\frac{1}{2}})-\mathfrak{P}_{0}(t;1;w_{n+\frac{1}{2}})\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{t}C_{\Phi,W_{n}}{\left({\left|{\left|v_{n}-w_{n}\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}+{\left|{\left|S_{t}(v_{n})-S_{t}(w_{n})\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}\right)}.

Putting these together, we obtain

\displaystyle{\left|{\left|S_{t}(v)-S_{t}(w)\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq{\left|{\left|v-w\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}+t\frac{C_{R^{*}}}{2}{\left({\left|{\left|v-w\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}+{\left|{\left|S_{t}(v)-S_{t}(w)\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}\right)}

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{t}\frac{|W|}{2}{\left({\left|{\left|v-w\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}+{\left|{\left|S_{t}(v)-S_{t}(w)\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}\right)},

where
W
is a standard Gaussian random variable. This implies the inequality

\displaystyle{\left|{\left|S_{t}(v)-S_{t}(w)\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+\frac{tC_{R}+\sqrt{t}|W|}{2}}{1-\frac{tC_{R}+\sqrt{t}|W|}{2}}{\left|{\left|v-w\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}.

(45)

Remark 6.5

Note that the function
\frac{1+x}{1-x}
blows up at
x=1
and is negative for values outside of
[0,1)
. Since the right side is positive, the inequality in (
45
) makes sense only if

tC_{R_{n}}+\sqrt{t}{\left|W_{n}\right|}<1.

This is the precise condition required on
t
for the fixed point convergence.

Next, we use the fact that

\frac{1+x/2}{1-x/2}\leq e^{3x/2},\quad\text{for }x<1,

and note that
e^{\frac{3}{2}{\left(tC_{R_{n}}+\sqrt{t}{\left|W_{n}\right|}\right)}}>1
. Iterating the scheme and applying inequality (
45
) at each step leads to the following inequality

{\left|{\left|S_{t}^{n}(v)-S_{t}^{n}(w)\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}\leq\mathop{\mathrm{exp}}\nolimits{\left(X_{n}\right)}{\left|{\left|v-w\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}},

where we define
X_{n}=\sum_{m=1}^{n}{\left(tC_{R_{m}}+\sqrt{t}{\left|W_{m}\right|}\right)}
. It follows that
e^{X_{n}}
has increasing expectation and is hence a sub-martingale in terms of
n
, and thus we may apply Markov’s inequality and Doob’s maximal inequality to obtain

\mathbb{P}{\left(\mathop{\mathrm{sup}}_{n\leq m}{\left|{\left|S_{t}^{n}(v)-S_{t}^{n}(w)\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}\geq K\right)}\leq\frac{2\mathbb{E}{\left[e^{X_{m}}\right]}{\left|{\left|v-w\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}}{K}\quad a.s.

6.2
Local Error

We have seen that the scheme is unstable with a non-negligible probability; however, we can prove the following result about local error.

Theorem 6.6

(Local Error)

Denote by
E_{t}(u(t_{n}))
the exact solution at time
t_{n}+t
from the initial datum
u(t_{n})
. Let
\alpha>1
and choose
R>0
such that

\mathop{\mathrm{sup}}_{s\in[0,t]}{\left|{\left|E_{s}(u(t_{n}))\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}\leq R,

and that

, then

{\left|{\left|S_{t}(u(t_{n}))-E_{t}(u(t_{n}))\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}\leq C_{1}t^{3/2},

where
C_{1}>0
is a random constant depending on
\Phi
,
\alpha
,
W_{1}
and
W_{2}^{2}
for

W_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\int_{0}^{t}\circ dW(s),\quad W_{2}=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{r}\circ dW(s)\circ dW(r).

Proof.

Note that by the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality and the assumption that
\Phi
is Hilbert-Schmidt, one can show that
\Phi W(t,x)
is almost surely bounded in
H^{s}
for each
t
. Specifically, for any
T>0

\mathbb{E}{\left[\mathop{\mathrm{sup}}_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\Phi W(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{s}}^{2}\right]}\lesssim T\|\Phi\|_{\text{HS}(L^{2},H^{s})}^{2}.

This implies that
\Phi W(t,x)
is almost surely in
H^{s}
for each
t
, which will hold for the remainder terms in the series expansions of our operators. Next, we introduce the shorthand notations

w_{t_{n}+\frac{1}{2}}(s)=e^{is\partial_{x}^{2}}\frac{u(t_{n})+e^{-it\partial_{x}^{2}}S_{t}(u(t_{n}))}{2},\quad\tilde{w}_{t_{n}+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{u(t_{n})+e^{-it\partial_{x}^{2}}S_{t}(u(t_{n}))}{2}.

Initially, we will look at the difference between the scheme in
4.1
and the Duhamel iteration of

, meaning we want to bound the
H^{\alpha}
norm of the following terms.

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}(u(t_{n}))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-s)\partial_{x}^{2}}|u(t_{n}+s)|^{2}\bar{u}(t_{n}+s)ds-t\mathfrak{F}_{0}{\left(t;1;\tilde{w}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right)},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2}(u(t_{n}))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-s)\partial_{x}^{2}}u(t_{n}+s)\circ\Phi dW(s)-\sqrt{t}\mathfrak{P}_{0}{\left(t;1;\tilde{w}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right)}.

The primary challenge in the analysis arises from the presence of midpoint approximations in the maps

and
\mathfrak{P}

\displaystyle{\left|{\left|S_{t}(u(t_{n}))-E_{t}(u(t_{n}))\right|}\right|}_{H_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle={\left|{\left|S_{t}(u(t_{n}))-E_{t}(w_{t_{n}+\frac{1}{2}})+E_{t}(w_{t_{n}+\frac{1}{2}})-E_{t}(u(t_{n}))\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq{\left|{\left|S_{t}(u(t_{n}))-E_{t}(w_{t_{n}+\frac{1}{2}})\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}}+{\left|{\left|E_{t}(w_{t_{n}+\frac{1}{2}})-E_{t}(u(t_{n}))\right|}\right|}_{H^{\alpha}},

\displaystyle E_{t}(w_{t_{n}+\frac{1}{2}}(s))

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1,k}(w_{t_{n}+\frac{1}{2}})

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2,k}(w_{t_{n}+\frac{1}{2}})

7
Numerical Experiments

We have implemented the numerical scheme
40
and demonstrate strong and pathwise convergence. For comparison we also implemented the stochastic Crank-Nicolson method studied in
[
14
]
.

We find that the Crank-Nicolson Scheme has generally better mass preservation in the stochastic case but only works for modestly discretised spatial mesh and small timesteps. On the other hand, the Resonance based method has improved conservation properties for a more refined spatial mesh, and good stability for larger timesteps. For example, our scheme converges for spatial mesh of size
2^{-13}
(8192 Fourier modes) with a timestep as large as
10^{-2}
. With the same timestep, however, the Crank-Nicolson scheme can converge with at maximum a mesh of the size
2^{-3}
(8 Fourier modes). This makes our scheme much better suited to the study of long time scales on refined spatial meshes. This scenario is applicable to many modern topics in dispersive (S)PDE such as energy cascading and ergodicity of solutions.

We note that in practice both schemes are stable and convergent, which in theory is not true as the schemes are almost surely unstable after a large enough period of time. On the other hand neither scheme are symplectic to machine precision, which
is
true in theory. In other words, we find substantial mismatch between theory and practice which are likely the result of finite computational resources.

8
Conclusion

We have derived a class of Runge-Kutta schemes for SPDEs that reduce the regularity requirements
on the initial conditions compared to traditional schemes. Focusing on the example of the cubic Schrödinger
equation, we have demonstrated that, in fact, due to their implicit nature, the existence and uniqueness of
the schemes rely on employing a very restrictive step size, (
44
). We have shown that the
schemes are symplectic, and for the stochastic midpoint rule, we have proven that the local error is of
order
t^{3/2}
. In
[
2
]
, de Bouard and Debussche remark that “this problem [of non-uniqueness] never caused any trouble in the implementation of the method”, referring to the experiments in
[
14
]
.
Likewise, we find that our schemes are stable and convergent in practice, even though they are almost surely unstable after a long enough period of time. This mismatch between theory and practice is likely the
result of finite computational resources, which prevent the schemes from diverging. The resonance scheme shows better stability properties than the Crank-Nicolson suggesting the timestep is less restrictive, probably due to a better contraction constant in the stability analysis. The conclusions of our experiments are
that traditional methods excel at low spatial resolution and small time steps, while the resonance-based methods excel at high spatial resolution and large time steps.
This comes at the cost of increased complexity in implementation and reduced conservation properties, which can be overcome by refining the spatial mesh – a fair trade if
ones intention is to study dispersive properties.
