AidAI: Automated Incident Diagnosis for AI Workloads in the Cloud
Yitao Yang, Yangtao Deng, Yifan Xiong, Baochun Li, Hong Xu, Peng Cheng

Abstract
AI workloads experience frequent incidents due to intensive hardware utilization and extended training times. The current incident management workflow is provider-centric, where customers report incidents and place the entire troubleshooting responsibility on the infrastructure provider. However, the inherent knowledge gap between customer and provider significantly impacts incident resolution efficiency. In AI infrastructure, incidents may take several days on average to mitigate, resulting in delays and productivity losses. To address these issues, we present AidAI, a customer-centric system that provides immediate incident diagnosis for customers and streamlines the creation of incident tickets for unresolved issues. The key idea of AidAI is to construct internal knowledge bases from historical on-call experiences during the offline phase and mimic the reasoning process of human experts to diagnose incidents through trial and error in the online phase. Evaluations using real-world incident records in Microsoft show that AidAI achieves an average Micro F1 score of 0.854 and Macro F1 score of 0.816 without significant overhead.

The design of AidAI comprises offline and online phases.
The offline phase (

(1) Historical Incident Database: AidAI constructs a vectorized incident database from historical records, organizing failure patterns for easy reference.
(2) Incident Taxonomy: AidAI uses an LLM to construct a hierarchical incident taxonomy that systematically organizes failure types. To further enhance its utility, AidAI extracts verification instructions from historical incident records and integrates them into the taxonomy.
(3) Domain-specific Knowledge Base: AidAI curates a knowledge base that includes tool usage, system configurations, and other information derived from on-call experience.
The online phase (
section

4.2

(1) Historical Data-Driven Diagnosis (
section

4.2.1

(2) Taxonomy-Guided Diagnosis (
section

4.2.2

4.2.3

If the issue remains unresolved, AidAI escalates the incident to the infrastructure support team for further investigation.

We implement a prototype of AidAI and evaluate its performance using one-year production incident records in Microsoft. The evaluation results show that AidAI achieves an average Micro F1 score of 0.854 and a Macro F1 score of 0.816, outperforming several baseline approaches.

L1: Inconsistency in Information Quality:

The effectiveness of provider-centric workflow heavily depends on the quality of initial incident reporting and subsequent customer communication.
However, we find that the incident description varies significantly among customers.
While some provide comprehensive documentation, including detailed error messages, logs, and reproduction steps, others submit minimal information such as basic error stacktraces.
To quantify the inconsistency in incident descriptions, we randomly selected ten incidents from each specific root cause category and analyzed their descriptions. Using cosine similarity-based clustering

As summarized in Table

This variation necessitates additional communication cycles and extended troubleshooting efforts, significantly impacting resolution times.

L2: Inefficiency in Handling Customer Errors:

The current workflow necessitates a full troubleshooting process for all reported incidents, regardless of their root cause and origin.
For incidents caused by customer-side misconfigurations or code errors, this leads to much wasted time and resources, as the provider’s support efforts are spent on handling issues outside their responsibility.
This also causes prolonged resolution times for customers in turn.

L3: Knowledge Gaps at Customers:

If customers were able to diagnose the incidents themselves and handle their own mistakes before contacting the provider, they could significantly reduce the time to resolution, and the OCEs’ workload could also be reduced.
Further, even for incidents caused by provider issues, customer’s initial investigation can provide valuable information and better incident tickets to the provider, facilitating faster resolution.
Yet, because customers possess varying levels of technical expertise and many lack the skills needed to troubleshoot complex issues, the current workflow is unable to leverage this potential and forces all burden on the provider’s side.

In light of the above limitations of provider-centric workflow, we propose to develop a

It serves the dual benefits of (1) identifying customer errors without having to unnecessarily rely on the provider, and (2) providing more comprehensive and useful initial investigations for the provider when the incident cannot be resolved by customers. Design Overview

To address the challenges outlined in
Section

2
, we build AidAI, a customer-centric system for automated incident diagnosis in AI workloads.
AidAI runs on the customer’s side and provides immediate incident diagnosis. In case AidAI fails to identify a root cause, it automatically creates a new ticket to engage the provider with comprehensive details of its investigation, including hypotheses it has tested and the corresponding verifications it has performed to rule it out.
AidAI’s design incorporates three key mechanisms.

Taxonomy-Guided Multiple-Step Diagnosis.

To address Challenge 1, we observe that OCEs typically diagnose incidents through multi-round hypothesis and verification, following a top-down approach. They first identify a potential root cause direction and then progressively narrow down their focus by executing specific diagnostic steps.
If the initial hypothesis fails, they refine the hypothesis and explore alternative directions iteratively until the root cause is identified.
Moreover, given the dense deployment of AI infrastructure, recurring hardware errors can be restricted to finite failure types (
section

2.1
).
Organizing these failures into a structured taxonomy enables AidAI to systematically capture domain knowledge and cover diverse error scenarios.

Based on these two insights, AidAI employs a taxonomy-guided diagnosis that systematically breaks down complex incident analysis into manageable steps to mimic the natural thought process of OCEs.
Specifically, inspired by the DFS strategy, we design an LLM-driven process that begins with the incident description as input and recursively investigates root causes within the taxonomy.
Unlike one-shot prediction, this recursive process lets AidAI iteratively refine hypotheses and explore alternatives, improving diagnosis accuracy and interpretability.

Hypotheses Validation Through Execution and Reflection.

To address challenge 2, we design AidAI with explicit evidence validation for each potential root cause hypothesis. The key insight is that each hypothesis can be validated through a series of steps to either confirm or reject the error for AI workload incidents. This design choice reduces false positives and makes diagnostic results more interpretable.

Specifically, AidAI employs an execution-reflection mechanism. For each hypothesis, we design an execution agent to interact with the physical environment and collect execution results. A reflection agent then validates the hypothesis based on the collected evidence. When validation fails, AidAI explores alternative diagnostic paths to generate new hypotheses.
This evidence-driven approach ensures diagnostic conclusions are well-grounded, thereby improving the overall diagnostic accuracy and trustworthiness.

Improving Diagnosis with Internal Knowledge.

Although LLMs possess general knowledge, they lack domain-specific diagnostic expertise, such as available verification tools that can be used and some internal incident categories that are not publicly available.
We found that the existing historical incident records and troubleshooting guides contain valuable domain-specific knowledge and expert insights from on-call engineers.
Based on this finding, we construct internal knowledge base comprising a historical incident database that captures failure patterns, a hierarchical incident taxonomy for comprehensive failure type coverage, and a domain-specific knowledge base from expert knowledge.
This integration allows AidAI to utilize a rich internal knowledge base, thereby enhancing its diagnostic capabilities.

4.1
Offline: Constructing Internal Knowledge Base

In the offline phase, AidAI builds knowledge bases from historical incidents, including a past incident database, an incident taxonomy, verification scripts, and a domain-specific knowledge base. These components are then used to guide the online diagnosis process.

Historical Incident Database Construction.

As mentioned in
section

2.1
, hardware-related incidents tend to recur. To facilitate the diagnosis of these incidents, we first construct a historical incident database. This database contains a collection of past incidents, including the incident descriptions and root cause labels. These data are stored in the vectorized format for efficient retrieval and comparison. When new incidents are reported, AidAI system can quickly search for similar incidents in the historical database to accelerate the diagnosis process through pattern matching.

Incident Taxonomy Construction.

As discussed in
section

3
, we organize failures into a structured taxonomy to provide comprehensive coverage of various error scenarios.
Beyond mere classification, the taxonomy serves as a guiding framework for the diagnosis process, enhancing the accuracy of root cause analysis.
Our taxonomy features the following characteristics:

\bullet
Automated Construction: Utilizing LLMs to analyze and categorize the historical incidents in the database.

\bullet
Hierarchical Structure: The taxonomy labels classify failures from general categories to specific incidents.

\bullet
Verification Support: Each label in the taxonomy is associated with a set of verification scripts to verify the root cause.

Figure
5
outlines the offline taxonomy construction process, which involves two passes.

Pass 1: Initial Labelling.
This process begins with the LLM analyzing original historical incident descriptions and OCEs discussions to generate hypotheses and corresponding root cause categories. We restrict the structural format of the output categories as
<main_category>.<sub_category>.<detailed_error_msg>
through prompt engineering. Since all incidents are processed simultaneously in this step, it may produce duplicate or inconsistent labels, necessitating further refinement.

Pass 2: Taxonomy Refinement and Expansion.
At this stage, the focus shifts to enhancing the taxonomy’s precision and coverage. Starting with an empty taxonomy, the LLM queries the existing taxonomy with the identified root cause label. This is to determine whether the category already exists.
If exists, the LLM retrieves its associated description and refines it for clarity and accuracy.
If is new, the taxonomy is updated with the new label and its associated root cause description.
We further enrich the taxonomy by incorporating labels from troubleshooting guide (TSG) documents, which are summarized by on-call engineers based on their expertise and experience.
The iterative nature of this process ensures that the taxonomy remains both comprehensive and adaptable to new incident patterns.
Finally, the refined taxonomy still needs a thorough review by human experts to ensure accuracy. We provide the visualization of the constructed taxonomy in Appendix
B
.

For internal nodes, we manually design more comprehensive verification scripts to cover a wider range of incidents. Domain-specific Knowledge Construction.

We also curate a domain-specific knowledge base from on-call experience and troubleshooting guides, including internal verification tools and details about system configuration.

4.2
Online: Tiered Diagnosis Pipelines

The online troubleshooting process in AidAI is powered by a multi-agent system comprising summarization, planning, execution, reflection, and conclusion agents. These agents work collaboratively to mimic the diagnostic process of human experts, as illustrated in Figure
4
.
The summarization agent extracts key information from the incident description provided by the customer.
The planning agent develops a diagnosis strategy by integrating incident details with external data.
The execution agent runs verification scripts and gathers results for analysis.
The reflection agent evaluates the results, validates the hypotheses, and refines the diagnosis plan.
Finally, the conclusion agent provides the final diagnosis report and assists in creating accurate incident tickets.
The core of AidAI is the planning agent, which incorporates domain-specific knowledge derived offline to guide the online diagnosis.
Figure
6
illustrates the detailed diagnostic pipeline design in the online phase.

4.2.1
Pipeline 1: Historical Data-Driven Diagnosis.

For a new incident, the planning agent first attempts to diagnose using the historical incidents database. This pipeline is designed to efficiently handle simple and recurring incidents.
The quick path in Figure
6
outlines this process.
It begins by vectorizing the new incident description and retrieving the most similar historical incidents from the database.
Then the planning agent selects the top two most similar incidents and their root causes as hypotheses. The execution agent runs the verification scripts to gather results for the hypotheses.
The reflection agent validates the hypotheses based on the gathered results.
For all confirmed hypotheses, the conclusion agent summarizes the diagnosis and provides a final report to the customer.
If neither hypothesis is validated, the planning agent transitions to the taxonomy-guided diagnosis pipeline for further investigation.

4.2.2
Pipeline 2: Taxonomy-Guided Diagnosis.

As the slow path shown in Figure
6
, the taxonomy-guided diagnosis pipeline uses an LLM-driven approach to recursively explore the taxonomy for root cause identification.
This diagnosis offers a structured and thorough approach to addressing complex incidents. Algorithm
1
outlines the detailed process, and Figure
7
illustrates the recursive search in action.

State Initialization.

The diagnosis begins with the incident description and the pre-built incident taxonomy. As described in Algorithm
1
(line 19-20)
, the planning agent initializes three key components:
(1) The current node in the taxonomy, starting from the root.
(2) The memory of the planning agent, which stores all diagnostic information during the process.
(3) The incident description, which provides the guiding context for the first iteration.
After initialization, the planning agent iteratively explores the taxonomy tree, progressively refining the search space to identify the most probable root causes.

State Expansion and Branch Pruning.

Path selection is a critical component in the taxonomy tree traversal. In AidAI design, this process is driven by the capabilities of LLMs and guided by the taxonomy structure. Specifically, at each searching step, the planning agent ranks potential root cause labels from all its subnodes, ordering them from most to least likely based on the incident description, past interactions, and environmental verification results.
Then it follows a depth-first search (DFS) strategy, starting with the most likely subnode and systematically progressing from high-level categories to specific nodes. Figure

Algorithm 1

Taxonomy-Guided Diagnosis

Environment Interactions.

For each hypothesis proposed by the planning agent, the execution agent interacts with the physical environment by executing verification scripts and gathering additional diagnostic information, as shown in Figure
7
.
The collected information will be stored in the AidAI’s memory to guide further diagnosis. Specifically, the planning agent relies on this information to refine its hypotheses and the reflection agent to validate current hypotheses. This dynamic interplay between taxonomy-driven logic and real-world observations ensures accurate and grounded diagnoses.

Backtracking.

If a detailed hypothesis (leaf node) is invalidated, the system retraces its steps to explore alternative branches. As shown in Figure
7
, red arrows at leaf nodes illustrate this process. When a hypothesis is rejected by the reflection agent, the planning agent returns to a previously explored node and reassesses other potential paths. This ensures that no viable hypothesis is prematurely dismissed.

Goal & Termination Condition.

The diagnostic process concludes only when all feasible branches have been fully explored to ensure diagnostic completeness. For these valid root causes identified during the exploration (e.g., the green node with a checkmark in Figure
7
), they are handed over to the conclusion agent for final summarization. If no root cause is identified after exhausting all branches, the system triggers the exploration pipeline to further investigate potential issues.

4.2.3
Pipeline 3: Root Cause Exploration.

When both quick and slow paths fail to identify the root cause, indicating a complex or unusual incident beyond conventional diagnostic patterns, the system transitions to a root cause exploration pipeline. This pipeline serves as a last resort for diagnosing challenging incidents, providing alternative potential root causes and diagnostic suggestions to customers.

In this pipeline, we leverage domain-specific knowledge (
section

4.1
) and historical interactions (
section

4.2.2
) to provide diagnostic suggestions.
When an incident enters the pipeline, the planning agent first queries this knowledge base to obtain relevant domain expertise.
The agent then integrates the retrieved domain-specific knowledge with interaction records from the pipeline #2, and the original incident description to generate other potential root causes.
For diagnostic suggestions, we use prompt engineering to include common troubleshooting instructions in AI infrastructure to enhance the planning agent’s capabilities. We also prompt the agent to dynamically generate additional custom instructions when necessary. However, these instructions are presented as actionable suggestions, but their execution still requires human intervention to reduce LLM hallucination risks and ensure accuracy.

The customers can provide their feedback and let the LLM regenerate other hypotheses and suggestions.
If all suggested solutions are still ineffective, the incident is escalated to the infrastructure support team for specialized investigation.

6.1
Experiment Setup

Incident Ticket Datasets.

We collect incident ticket content from production-scale GPU clusters in Microsoft, spanning from 2023-04 to 2024-03.
For focusing on AI workload incidents, we filtered the tickets using keywords such as

For the evaluation, approximately 25% of the incidents (208) were randomly set aside as the test set, while the remaining 75% were used to construct the historical incident database and taxonomy. The test set covers a diverse range of incident categories: 115 GPU-related incidents, 53 system software issues, 22 interconnect and networking problems, 16 user application incidents, and 2 framework and library-related incidents.

Baselines.
We comapre AidAI with the following four baselines:

•

[
37

•

Comprehensive Verification Diagnosis (CVD)

Metrics.

We evaluate AidAI using Precision, Recall, and Micro/Macro F1-scores, comparing its performance against baseline methods.
The Micro F1-score aggregates performance across all classes, weighting each sample equally, while the Macro F1-score evaluates performance on a per-class basis, treating all classes equally regardless of their size.
Additionally, we assess its efficiency in terms of verification time and LLM invocation overhead.

Fidelity of Incident Categories.

To ensure label accuracy, we perform a fidelity check by comparing LLM-generated labels with human expert annotations for 50 randomly selected test set tickets. The results show 96% accuracy, demonstrating the accuracy of the LLM-labeled incident tickets.

Overall Performance

Effectiveness of Diagnosis.

We evaluate the overall performance of AidAI and all the baseline methods using the Micro and Macro F1-scores.
The error bars represent the standard deviation of F1 scores across multiple experimental runs.

As shown in Figure

In terms of the Macro F1 score, the gap in AidAI (0.816) and remaining baseline methods is even more pronounced, with RCACopilot, DID-o1, and CVD scoring 0.380, 0.424, and 0.427, respectively.
The higher Macro F1 score by AidAI demonstrates its ability to perform well across all classes, despite class imbalance.

While, for Macro F1, the error bars of AidAI are slightly larger than RCACopilot, DID-o1, and CVD, this is due to certain categories having fewer samples, leading to higher variance in the evaluation results.
Overall, these results underscore the effectiveness of AidAI in accurately predicting root cause categories while maintaining stability under varying conditions.

We also measure the performance of AidAI across different incident categories to understand its effectiveness in handling various types of incidents. Figure

Discussions

AidAI and Existing Diagnostic Tools.

AidAI can integrate various diagnostic tools designed for customer-side usage, such as NVIDIA DCGM
[
39
]
and SuperBench
[
64
]
, to enhance its diagnostic capabilities. In contrast, tools like Pingmesh
[
18
]
and Deepview
[
70
]
, which are tailored for provider-side monitoring, are not applicable for customer-centric diagnosis and therefore cannot be utilized by AidAI.

Hallucination Risk in LLMs.

Open-ended generation in LLMs is known to generate hallucinated text
[
22
,
65
]
.
To address this, AidAI restricts the hypothesis to a predefined incident taxonomy using multiple-choice selection and ensures output validity through an execution-reflection loop
[
45
,
23
]
.

Mitigation Suggestions.

Currently, AidAI design just provides an initial diagnosis with root causes, it does not provide mitigation suggestions. When an incident happens, customers still need to wait for OCEs from cloud providers to mitigate these system errors. In the future, we plan to extend AidAI to provide mitigation suggestions to customers.

Infrastructure Software Errors.

AidAI is proposed as a customer-centric diagnosis system with limited visibility into the complex software stack of the underlying infrastructure. When customers trigger hidden errors/bugs in the software stack, AidAI only identifies the issue as a potential infrastructure software error (with no clear root cause) and escalates it to the infrastructure for further diagnosis.
Automating the diagnosis of such errors is particularly challenging, especially during software upgrades, maintenance, or new components rollout, as these processes frequently introduce unforeseen problems.

Conclusion

In this paper, we present AidAI, a customer-centric system that automates incident diagnosis for AI workloads.
During offline, AidAI constructs internal knowledge bases from historical on-call experiences.
For online diagnosis, AidAI employs sequential diagnostic pipelines that mimic the reasoning and diagnostic processes of human experts, providing immediate feedback to customers and facilitating the creation of accurate incident tickets for unresolved problems.
Extensive evaluations using real-world incidents demonstrate the effectiveness of AidAI against various baselines.

The error stack also indicates potential issues in the PyTorch’s TensorImpl destructor and the Python interpreter’s deallocation process for PyObjects.
