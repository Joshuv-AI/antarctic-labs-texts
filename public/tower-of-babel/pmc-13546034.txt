Bicornuate Uterus with Third Trimester Fetal Demise in a Non-Communicating Rudimentary Uterine Horn: A Case Report
Authors: Bwire Innocent, Fardowso Dahir Warsame, Ayan Hussein Hashi Snr, Fatima Abdallah Noor, Mohamed Abdi Deheyeh, Qabul Isse Omar Snr, Namujju Josephine, Okurut Emmanuel, Baruti Petrus, Theoneste Hakizimana, Adam Abdrahim Suliman Ebaid

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBicornuate uterus is a rare congenital uterine anomaly that occurs due to incomplete fusion of the mullerian ducts during fetal development, resulting in a single cervix and two uterine horns with partial to complete separation with or without communication between the cavities. Pregnancy in a rudimentary non-communicating uterine horn of a bicornuate uterus is an even rare occurrence, usually due to transperitoneal migration of sperms or zygote, and it is often associated with fetal demise and uterine rupture. We report a case of a bicornuate uterus unicollis with a third-trimester fetal demise in a non-communicating rudimentary uterine horn with focus on diagnosis, investigations and management in a low resource setting.Case PresentationThe patient was a 24-year-old Ugandan, gravida 2 para 0+1 at 29 weeks of gestation who presented with abdominal pain for 3 days. She had a history of dyspareunia, dysmenorrhea, and a first-trimester miscarriage four years ago. Laparotomy for an abdominal ectopic pregnancy revealed a bicornuate uterus with a gravid non-communicating rudimentary uterine horn containing a dead fetus. Introduction

The female reproductive system develops from the mullerian ducts between the 7th and 12th week of gestation. The uterus, fallopian tubes, cervix, and upper third of the vagina arise from two pairs of mullerian ducts. During the 8th week of gestation, the mullerian ducts fuse cranially to form the uterus and caudally to form the upper third of the vagina. A midline septum is present, which usually reabsorbs around 20th week of gestation.1

Mullerian Duct anomalies (MDA) result from defective formation, fusion, or reabsorption of mullerian ducts during fetal development and are present in 6.7% of women in the general population. Bicornuate uterus accounts for 39% of uterine anomalies due to incomplete fusion of the mullerian ducts resulting in a single uterine cervix and two uterine horns with partial to complete separation with or without communication between the cavities. Women with partial bicornuate uterus have a 60% chance of delivering a living child while complete bicornuate uterus has poor reproductive outcomes.2

The bicornuate uterus has a prevalence of 0.4% in the general population with two subtypes according to cervical canal involvement: bicornuate uterus bicollis with two cervical canals and bicornuate uterus unicollis with one cervical canal.3 Uterus Bicornuate Non-Communicant Rudimentary Horn (UBNCRH) is a rare mullerian duct anomaly when no anatomical communication exists between the two uterine horns of a bicornuate uterus.4 Pregnancy in a rudimentary uterine horn is very rare and occurs in about 1 in 100,000 to 140,000 pregnancies due to transperitoneal migration of the sperm or zygote during the implantation period, with a clinical picture of ectopic pregnancy.5

Complications associated with bicornuate uterus include infertility, dysmenorrhea, recurrent miscarriages, cervical incompetence, preterm birth, and uterine rupture. The maternal–fetal outcomes of an unrecognized rudimentary horn pregnancy are poor with a uterine rupture rate of up to 80% and a fetal survival rate of 6%.6,7

Pregnancy in a bicornuate uterus is considered a high risk as it is linked to poor reproductive outcomes such as cervical incompetence, recurrent miscarriages, ectopic pregnancy, preterm birth, fetal malpresentation, intrauterine growth restriction, fetal demise and uterine rupture (up to 35th week of gestation). Fertility is preserved if implantation of the zygote occurs in a well-developed uterine horn rather than a rudimentary horn, which is associated with hypoplastic endometrium, poor vasculature of arcuate arterioles as well as abnormalities of the endo-myometrial junction.8,9

In Africa, a literature search for UBNCRH with fetal demise noted a case in South Africa in which a 44-year-old gravida 4 para 2 with one miscarriage presented with a live fetus at 16 weeks with a fibroid-like structure on ultrasound scan interpreted as a fibroid uterus due to her advanced age. A follow-up scan after one month revealed second-trimester fetal demise at 21 weeks and induction with a misoprostol dose of 200 µg was initiated. After 5 misoprostol doses with no progress, this was followed by induction using a mechanical catheter bulb with no effect as well. Hysterotomy for failed induction revealed a bicornuate uterus with a failed pregnancy in the left rudimentary uterine horn. A classical incision was used to deliver the non-viable fetus and bilateral tubal ligation was also done.8

In Uganda, this is the first case report, and it highlights both human and diagnostic challenges in the management of pregnancy in a non-communicating rudimentary uterine horn of a bicornuate uterus in a low-resource settings.

Case Presentation

We report a case of a 24-year-old G2 P0+1 Ugandan woman at 29 weeks of gestation by last normal menstrual period who presented to Kampala International University Teaching Hospital with a 3-day history of lower abdominal pain of gradual onset, constant and more on the right side. The pain was not relieved by over-the-counter paracetamol and was associated with no fetal movements. She had a history of a first-trimester spontaneous miscarriage four years ago. She attended an antenatal clinic a month ago at a lower health facility where her booking vitals were normal and hematinics were started. No booking ultrasound scan was done. She also reported on‑and‑off occasional dyspareunia and dysmenorrhea since menarche at 14 years, and had no difficulty in conception. She was married and reported no known familial illness.

A general physical examination revealed no obvious external abnormalities with normal vital signs. The abdomen was asymmetrically distended, with moderate lower abdominal tenderness, a sympysio-fundal length of 29 cm, longitudinal lie, cephalic presentation, and no fetal heart tones heard on auscultation. A speculum examination revealed no external abnormalities and one visible closed uterine cervix.

Laboratory investigations included a full blood count with a hemoglobin level of 15.6 g/dl, a white blood cell count of 8×109 cells, and a platelet count of 224×109 cells. Her blood group was A- rhesus positive. The coagulation profile was normal. Initial trans-abdominal ultrasound findings were indicative of a bicornuate or didelphic uterus. A non-viable fetus was noted in the right uterine horn at 31 weeks of gestation and the left uterus was empty and normal, as shown in Figure 1 above.

First ultrasound scan indicative of bicornuate/didelphic uterus with left empty uterus and gravid right uterus containing a dead fetus.

An initial diagnosis of intrauterine fetal death with a bicornuate uterus was made. Induction of labour with vaginal misoprostol (50 µg) every four hours was initiated and after five misoprostol doses, there were adequate uterine contractions but no cervical changes. A second transabdominal ultrasound scan was performed, with findings indicative of a non-viable abdominal ectopic gestation, as shown in Figure 2 below.

Second ultrasound scan showing a fetal head outside an empty normal uterus suggestive of abdominal ectopic pregnancy.

A diagnosis of abdominal ectopic pregnancy and intrauterine fetal death was made. The patient was counseled and consented for an exploratory laparotomy due to an abdominal ectopic pregnancy. Intraoperatively two uterine horns were noted: a right gravid rudimentary uterine horn attached caudally to a left non-gravid uterine horn, as shown in Figure 3. Ovaries, fallopian tubes, and round ligaments were noted on either side.

Intra-operative view showing two uterine horns: a right gravid uterine horn with open transverse incision and a left non‑gravid uterine horn.

Through a transverse incision on the right gravid uterine horn, a macerated stillbirth, a male baby weighing 1.5 kg, and a placenta were delivered. Probing for communication between the two uterine horns with a concurrent vaginal examination, assisted by another assistant, revealed a continuous cervix with the left non-gravid uterine horn and no communication to the right horn, as shown in Figure 4.

Intraoperative view of two uterine horns with no obvious communication between the two horns noted.

Resection of the gravid right uterine horn and the fallopian tube was done, to prevent future obstetric complications, with the repair of the lateral uterine wall. The ovary on the right side was preserved. The resected uterine horn is shown in Figure 5 below.

Resected right uterine horn.

Post-operative recovery was uneventful, and she was discharged on day 3 after psychological support on analgesics, hematinics, antibiotics, and a follow-up ultrasound scan to be done at six weeks.

Discussion

Mullerian ducts undergo elongation, canalization, fusion, and resorption of the septum. Disruptions in any stage of this process result in Mullerian abnormalities. A bicornuate uterus is usually due to incomplete fusion of the paramesonephric ducts in the 10th week, resulting in a uterus divided into two wide-spaced uterine horns.10

These anomalies are classified by American Fertility Society in which class I is uterine agenesis or hypoplasia, class II is unicornuate uterus, class III is didelphys uterus, class IV is bicornuate uterus, class V is septate uterus, class VI is arcuate uterus, and class VII is diethylstilbestrol exposure related anomalies.11

Rudimentary uterine horns result from the arrested development of one of the mullerian ducts and are more predominant on the right side, as the left mullerian duct advances slightly ahead of the left. It is usually non-communicating in 83% of the cases. Varied terminologies such as bicornuate uterus with rudimentary horn, bicornuate uterus with atretic horn, and bicornuate uterus with accessory horn have been used in literature making the classification of UBNCRH challenging. Although rudimentary non-communicating horns are class IIB MDA, they can occur with the bicornuate uterus and are not always associated with the unicornuate uterus. Bicornuate uterus with hemi-obstruction has also been referred to in literature as rudimentary horns as well as class IV MDA.9,11

Diagnosis of bicornuate uterus in pregnancy is challenging as most cases remain undiagnosed until complications occur. Ninety percent of cases result in fetal demise or uterine rupture, with the need for further evaluation. The majority of patients present with severe lower abdominal pain but may have a history of miscarriages, dyspareunia, or dysmenorrhea. The imaging modality of choice for early diagnosis of uterine anomalies is three‑dimensional ultrasonography, especially in low-resource settings. However, Magnetic Resonance Imaging provides better resolution and can differentiate between uterine anomalies. However, the gold standard remains hysteroscopy and laparoscopy.5,8,12 In this case, the symptoms of dyspareunia and dysmenorrhea were present but not investigated further as well no specialist referral or review was done. No routine first-trimester ultrasound was performed, missing the opportunity to detect uterine anomalies early. Furthermore, the initial scan suggested bicornuate or didelphic uterus with intrauterine fetal death, and a repeat scan later revealed an abdominal ectopic pregnancy, showing how interpretation can be difficult without advanced imaging. Magnetic Resonance Imaging or high‑resolution three‑dimensional ultrasonography was unavailable leading to diagnostic uncertainty and delayed interventions. It should be noted that the symphysio-fundal height and lie appeared normal, masking the anomaly. The diagnosis was distinct from unicornuate uterus with a rudimentary horn as the two uterine horns were relatively developed, though one was non-communicating.

Although challenging, the management of pregnancy in UBNCRH with excision of the gravid rudimentary horn by laparotomy or laparoscopy has been widely practiced and is the recommended standard practice due to the risk of uterine rupture. Difficulties in early diagnosis have resulted in failed terminations of pregnancy by induction and uterine evacuations reported in several case reports. Although the use of misoprostol in this case did not result in uterine rupture, use in the second trimester has been associated with uterine rupture in some case reports. Nevertheless, the standard surgical correction procedure for partial bicornuate uterus is Strassman metroplasty, and should be considered in women with recurrent pregnancy loss and reproductive difficulties.2,12,13

Conclusions

Despite the rarity and difficulty in the diagnosis of uterine anomalies in low-resource settings, it is important for clinicians, especially obstetricians and gynecologists, to be aware of a bicornuate uterus with a non-communicating rudimentary horn pregnancy that can lead to fetal demise and uterine rupture. Early antenatal diagnosis by ultrasonography is crucial in prevention of future obstetric complications in low resource settings.
