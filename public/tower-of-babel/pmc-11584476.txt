Carbonic anhydrase inhibitor alleviates retinal barrier toxicity in paclitaxel-induced retinopathy and macular edema by inhibiting CAXIV
Authors: Ya-ting Ye, Ya-li Niu, Zi-yi Zhou, Yu Sun, Tian-fang Chang, Yu-tong Jing, Qian Bai, Zhao-jie Chu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate the mechanism of paclitaxel (PTX)-induced macular edema and the therapeutic effect of carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (CAI) on this condition.MethodsThe effect of PTX on cell morphology was detected by immunofluorescence. Cell barrier was measured by measuring cell resistance across the epithelium. Western blotting analysis and reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) were performed to investigate the effects of PTX or PTX + CAI on the expression of carbonic anhydrase XIV (CAXIV), aquaporin 4 (AQP4) and inflammatory factors. After intraperitoneal injection of PTX in vivo, retinal electrophysiology (ERG) was used to evaluate the effects of drugs on visual electrophysiology.ResultsPTX inhibited the proliferation of ARPE-19 and Müller cells, promoting their apoptosis, changing their morphology and cell cycle, reducing the transepithelial resistance of ARPE-19 cells and promoting the expression of inflammatory factors; This process was alleviated after temporary withdrawal. CAI inhibited the upregulation of inflammatory factors. Following treatment with PTX, the expression levels of AQP4 and CAXIV were higher than control group; nevertheless, the levels of ZO-1 and OCLN were lower than control group. In vivo, the ERG analysis showed that the light- and dark-adapted 3.0 ERG, and dark-adapted 3.0 oscillatory potentials decreased to different degrees following treatment with PTX.ConclusionPTX-induced macular edema is mainly due to Müller cell toxicity. The condition can be alleviated by regulating water channels and enhancing subretinal fluid absorption. Thus, CAI may provide a new therapeutic approach for PTX-induced macular edema.Keywords: Paclitaxel, Macular edema, Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (CAI), Carbonic anhydrase XIV (CAXIV), Aquaporin 4 (AQP4)

Introduction

Paclitaxel (PTX), the first member of the taxane family, is one of the most widely used antineoplastic agents with broad activity against several types of cancer (e.g., breast, ovarian, and cervical). [1, 2] Despite its efficacy, PTX is associated with the development of side effects (e.g., bone marrow suppression, allergic reaction, gastrointestinal reaction, numbness in hands and feet, and liver injury. In recent years, adverse ocular reactions caused by chemotherapy with a taxane, such as dry eye [3, 4], tear overflow [5], nasolacrimal passage obstruction [6, 7], conjunctivitis [8], glaucoma [9, 10], optic neuropathy [11–13], and macular edema [14–17], have also been reported. Among those, macular edema is a vision-threatening ocular illness with an incidence of 0.5%. [14] Nevertheless, the mechanism through which macular edema is induced by chemotherapy with PTX remains unclear. Currently, few studies (mainly case reports) are available, and there are no relevant guidelines for the clinical management of macular edema.

Macular edema is defined as an accumulation of fluid in the outer plexiform layer and the inner nuclear layer. The most common types of macular edema are diabetic macular edema, [18] macular edema secondary to retinal vein occlusion, and uveitis-associated macular edema. Their pathogenesis is complex and multi-factorial, and spontaneous regression is rare. Nonetheless, macular edema inevitably leads to the destruction of the blood-retinal barrier (BRB). Furthermore, inflammation and increased expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) play an important role in its occurrence. Therefore, anti-VEGF and anti-inflammatory therapies have been widely adopted in this setting [19]. Notably, PTX-induced macular edema appears to differ from other types of this condition. Clinical examination through fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) has revealed irregular leakage in most types of macular edema in the late phase. Interestingly, macular edema caused by taxanes is not characterized by obvious leakage on FFA imaging [20] More importantly, PTX-induced macular edema resolves spontaneously after drug withdrawal, and therapy with anti-VEGF drugs does not exert an obvious effect [21] These findings suggest that PTX induces macular edema through unique mechanisms.

It is well established that the destruction of the BRB is the pathological basis of macular edema. The BRB maintains the stability of the environment of ocular neurons and photoreceptors, ensuring their physiological functions. [22] It has been demonstrated that retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and Müller cells play an important role in BRB maintenance. Accordingly, few studies speculated that the mechanism underlying PTX-induced macular edema may be the PTX-induced toxicity of RPE and Müller cells [23, 24] However, the exact mechanism remains unclear. While medical treatment of macular edema with carbonic anhydrase inhibitors has been known for a long time [25]. The aim of this study was to investigate the possible mechanisms involved in PTX-induced toxicity and discuss the use of carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (CAI) as a therapeutic strategy.

Materials and methods

ARPE-19 and Müller cell culture and grouping

ARPE-19 cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). ARPE-19 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM)/F12 (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; HyClone, Logan, UT, USA), 100 mg/mL streptomycin, and 100 IU/mL penicillin (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), and were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The culture medium was replaced every 2 days. When the cells reached a 75–80% confluence, they were treated with 0.25% trypsin-0.02% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid solution and passaged. The number of cells was determined using an automated cell counter (Ruiyu, Shanghai, China).

Extraction, identification, and grouping of Müller cells

The eyes of newborn C57 mice (age: 6–8 days) were soaked in 10% DMEM for approximately 2 h. The eyeballs were digested at 37 ℃ for 45 min with digestive solution (0.5 mL DMEM, 1 mL 0.25% trypsin, and 1 mL collagenase I). The retina was removed, ground, centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min. After resuspended in 10%DMEM, pass through 100 um screen, seeded at a density of 6–9 eyes per 100 mm dish, and incubated at 37 ℃. After the cells adhered to the surface of the dish, the medium was changed, followed by another change 4–5 days later.

Müller cells were inoculated in a confocal dish; when the cells reached 70–80% confluence, they were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. Next, they were blocked using 1% bovine serum albumin and 0.5% Trition X-100 (phosphate-buffered saline [PBS] dilution) for 30 min. Primary antibodies—rabbit-derived aquaporin 4 (AQP4) and glutamine synthetase—were diluted with the above sealing fluid solution (1:200) and added to the cells, which were incubated overnight in a wet box at 4 ℃. Cells were incubated for 1 h with secondary antibodies (Donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L), Alexa Fluor Plus 594, Invitrogen, America) (1:200) at room temperature. After washing thrice in PBS, the nuclei were stained and sealed with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), and observed and photographed under a confocal microscope (FV1000, Nagoya, Olympus, Japan).

ARPE-19 and Müller cells were randomly divided into six groups: control; 0.005 mg/L PTX; 0.05 mg/L PTX; 0.5 mg/L PTX; 5 mg/L PTX; and PTX (0.05 mg/L) + CAI (1 mM). Acetazolamide, used in this work, was purchased from Selleck Chemicals (Batch No. S4506. Houston, United States).

Detection of cell viability

Cell counting kit‑8 (CCK‑8) assay

ARPE-19 and Müller cells were seeded into 96-well plates (density: 2 × 104 cells/mL). At 50% confluence, the medium was replaced with FBS-free medium, and culture was continued for 12 h. PTX (0, 0.005, 0.05, 0.05, and 5 mg/L) was added to the medium, and ARPE-19 and Müller cells were incubated for 12, 24, 36, 48, and 72 h at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. Subsequently, CCK-8 reagent (E-CK-A361; Elabscience, Wuhan, China) (10 μL) was added to each well, and the cells were cultured for 3 h. The absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (Bio Tek, Vermont, USA).

Cell apoptosis analysis by flow cytometry

Annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)/propidium iodide (PI) staining was used to detect apoptosis. According to the protocol, Müller cells were harvested after different treatments, and an Annexin V-FITC and PI (Sigma–Aldrich, Saint Louis, Missouri, USA) mixture (100 μL) was added to the cells. Thereafter, flow cytometry was used to detect fluorescent cells. The rate of apoptosis in Müller cells was determined using the BD FACSuite software (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA).

Cell cycle analysis by flow cytometry

The treated cells were washed with cold PBS and fixed with 70% alcohol for 4 h. Next, the cells were stained with PI for 10 min at 37 °C in darkness. Flow cytometry (BD Biosciences) was used to detect the percentage of cells in the G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases.

Detection of cell barrier function

Establishment and analysis of a cell barrier model

A Transwell plate was immersed in the culture medium for 1 h. Subsequently, ARPE-19 cells were inoculated (density: 5 × 104 cells/mL) at the bottom of the Transwell chamber (micropore diameter: 0.4 μm) and incubated at 37 ℃ in an incubator with 5% CO2. Cells without cell growth and only with the filtration membrance were sued as the blank group. Cell culture medium was added to all upper (500 μL) and lower (1,500 μL) chambers to maintain a constant hydrostatic pressure. The morphological alterations were observed with an inverted phase-contrast microscope (Shanxi Antai Group Co., Ltd., Shanxi, China; magnification: × 100). After reaching 100% confluence, the cells were cultured in the medium with low serum (1% FBS); the medium was changed every 2 days. Transepithelial cell resistance was measured as transepithelial resistance (TER) (Ω cm2); TER was measured at the same time each day. The TER was also measured in the blank group to provide the background resistance value. The TER value of the experimental group was calculated as follows: the measured resistance value minus the background resistance value multiplied by the filter membrane area. Measurements were obtained from three extraction points, and the resistance measurement of each hole was repeated at least thrice to derive the average value. Tight connection of cells into hexagons and a stable electrical resistance for 3 consecutive days denoted cell polarization. Following cell polarization, medium containing 0 mg/L and 0.05 mg/L PTX was added to the upper chamber of the Transwell plate through liquid exchange, and changes in resistance values were measured at 3, 6, 12, and 24 h after addition.

Immunofluorescence staining assay

Müller cells were cultured and treated in a confocal dish. After different treatments, cells were fixed, sealed, and incubated with the fluorescent dye (phalloidin; 1:200) at 4 °C overnight. The cell nuclei were stained with DAPI. Using a confocal scanning laser microscope (FV1000; Olympus, Nagoya, Japan), stained cells were observed and images were captured.

Mouse model

There-week-old of C57BL/6 J mice were classified into three groups: control, PTX, and PTX + CAI. Mice in the control group were intraperitoneally injected with 0.9% normal saline; mice in the PTX group were intraperitoneally injected with 5 mg/kg PTX; mice in the PTX + CAI group were injected with 25 mg/kg CAI 30 min after intraperitoneal injection of 5 mg/kg PTX, once weekly for 4 weeks. The mice had access to water and standard diet.

Retinal electrophysiology (ERG) data

ERG was performed because the vasculature in the retina of mice appeared normal. After dark adaptation overnight, the mice were deeply anesthetized, and the pupils of the tested eyes were dilated with tropicamide eye drops (Xingqi Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd, Shenyang, China). An active electrode was subsequently inserted into the cornea of the tested eye, the reference electrode was inserted beneath the cheek mucosa around the tested eye, and the ground electrode was inserted into the skin of the tail. Full-field (ganzfeld) stimulation and the RETI port system (Brandenburg, Germany) were used to document ERG data. In this study, scotopic 0.01 cd s/m2, 3.0 cd s/m2 (maximal), and 3.0 cd s/m2 oscillatory potential (OP) ERG, as well as photopic 3.0 cd s/m2 and 3.0 cd s/m2 flicker ERG were recorded.

RNA isolation and reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated with the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using a reverse transcription kit (Takara, Japan) according to the instructions provided by the manufacturers. The cDNA and specific primers were added to the reaction system (20 μL) according to the instructions provided with the PCR kit (SYBR Premix EX Taq; Takara). The expression levels of the target genes were analyzed using β-actin as an internal reference. The primer sequences used in this experiment are shown in Table 1.

Western blotting analysis

ARPE-19 cells, Müller cells, and C57 mouse retinas were harvested and lysed using cell lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid protein detection reagent (Beyotime). Proteins (30 μg/lane) were loaded on a 10% or 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gel for separation. Next, the separated proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. After blocking at room temperature for 1 h, the membranes were probed with the primary antibodies (anti-zona occludens 1 [anti-ZO-1], anti-occludin [anti-OCLN], anti-interleukin-6 [anti-IL-6], tumor necrosis factor-α [anti-TNF-α], anti-monocyte chemotactic protein 1 [anti-MCP-1], anti-VEGF, anti-CAXIV, anti-AQP4, anti-β-tubulin, and anti-β-actin) at 4 °C overnight. Following incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h, the protein bands were visualized via enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL; ZETA, Beijing, China). The relative expression levels of proteins were determined using a densitometer and expressed as absorbance units. Each experiment was repeated thrice. Statistical analysis

All quantitative results were presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean. Differences between the mean variables of two groups were determined using Student’s t-test. One-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s post hoc test was used for multiple group analyses. P-values < 0.05 denoted statistically significant differences. Statistical analysis was conducted using the GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

The survival status of Müller cells was changed by PTX

Müller cells were cultured for 48 h and were identified by immunofluorescence staining of its specific markers glutamine synthetase and AQP4 (Fig. 1a). PTX inhibited the proliferation of Müller cells and promoted their apoptosis in a dose- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 1b and c). and Müller cells (Fig. 1d) may be arrested in the G2/M phase after treatment with PTX. The concentration of 0.05 mg/mL for 24 h was selected as the optimal condition for the following experiments. Normal Müller cells exhibit spindle, stellated, and polygon shapes. The cell morphology was clear, and the cells were arranged in clusters or in parallel (Fig. 1e).

PTX upregulated CAXIV, promoted inflammation, and downregulated the expression of VEGF in ARPE-19 and Müller cells

It has been reported that inflammation and VEGF play important roles in macular edema. Moreover, it has been shown that 2% dorzolamide (a CAI) may resolve macular edema induced by taxanes in the early stage. In the physiological structure of the retina, transmembrane CAXIV is specifically distributed on the top and outside of the base of RPE cells and the end foot and non-end foot membranes on Müller cells. RT-qPCR showed that, in ARPE-19 cells, CAXIV was higher in the PTX group versus the control group, and the expression of VEGF was decreased (Fig. 2a). At the protein level, the expression of CAXIV and VEGF was consistent with the change observed at the mRNA level (Fig. 2b and c). Furthermore, the expression levels of CAXIV and VEGF in Müller cells were consistent with those recorded in ARPE-19 cells (Fig. 2d–f).

RT-qPCR showed that the mRNA levels of IL-6, MCP-1, and TNF-α were elevated in ARPE-19 and Müller cells. At the protein level, the expression of inflammatory factors was consistent with that noted at the mRNA level (Fig. 2g–l). Following treatment with PTX, ARPE-19 and Müller cells were maintained in normal medium for 24, 36, 48, and 72 h. The protein expression of inflammatory factors was decreased at 36–48 h. However, the expression of these factors was subsequently increased at 72 h (Fig. 2m–p).

PTX disrupted the barrier function of Müller and ARPE-19 cells

In the eyes, retinal Müller cells specifically express AQP4, which is essential for maintaining retinal water balance. PTX increased the expression of AQP4 in Müller cells (Fig. 3a and b). We next obtained ERG data for C57BL/6 mice from the PTX-treated and healthy control groups. The representative recordings provided a comparison of the b-wave amplitudes of the scotopic and photopic phases, b-wave maximal amplitudes, and O2 amplitudes of the OPS response in the eyes. It was observed that the light- and dark-adapted 3.0 ERG, and dark-adapted 3.0 OPS wave in the eyes of mice treated with PTX decreased to varying degrees. Moreover, the ERG amplitude was decreased in the PTX-treated group mice, and the most significant variation in the OPS wave was observed in these animals (Fig. 3c and d).

CAI alleviated the PTX-induced disturbance of ARPE-19 and Müller cells

In previously reported individual clinical cases, CAI were effective in relieving PTX-induced macular edema. Addition of CAI decreased the expression of AQP4 in Müller cells (Fig. 4a–c). In ARPE-19 and Müller cells, concurrent treatment with CAI and PTX decreased the mRNA expression of inflammatory factors compared with the PTX drug group (Fig. 4d–g). The expression of inflammatory factors at the protein and mRNA levels was consistent (Fig. 4e–i).

In vivo study confirmed the toxicity and possible molecular mechanism of PTX

After establishing the animal models, the retinal protein and mRNA of mice were obtained. Firstly, we verified in vivo that PTX increased the expression of CAXIV in the retina (Fig. 5a–c). Secondly, the mRNA levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and MCP-1 in the retina in the control, PTX, and PTX + CAI groups were measured by RT-qPCR. As expected, these levels were significantly higher in PTX-treated retinas versus control. However, injection of PTX + CAI decreased the expression of inflammatory factors (Fig. 5d). The protein levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and MCP-1 in the retinas of the three groups were also analyzed by western blotting; the findings were consistent with the RT-qPCR data (Fig. 5e–f). Finally, we confirmed that the expression of AQP4 at both the mRNA and protein levels was consistent with that measured in vitro (Fig. 5g–i).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study on the mechanism involved in PTX-induced macular edema. We investigated the mechanisms underlying the toxicity of PTX using two relevant types of cells. We demonstrated that PTX affected the survival status of ARPE-19 and Müller cells. Treatment of ARPE-19 and Müller cells with PTX resulted in transient upregulation of inflammatory factors, as well as the expression of CAXIV and AQP4. The in vivo study showed revealed a change in retinal electrophysiological Max 3.0, indicating possible damage in Müller cells. Surprisingly, CAI appeared to suppress PTX-induced toxicity in the retina. Through in vitro and in vivo studies, we confirmed that CAI regulated water conduction by alleviating inflammation, inhibiting AQP4, suppressing the activity of membrane-binding carbonic anhydrase, and enhancing subretinal fluid absorption.

According to previously published clinical cases and related literature, treatment with glucocorticoids and anti-VEGF drugs is ineffective in patients with PTX-induced macular edema. Meanwhile, our in vitro data showed that PTX induced the release of inflammatory factors and decreased VEGF expression in ARPE-19 and Müller cells. However, when treatment with PTX was discontinued, the expression of inflammatory factors was decreased compared with that detected in the PTX-treatment group. This is consistent with previous research findings [26]. These findings indicated that the PTX-induced upregulation of inflammatory factors in ARPE-19 and Müller cells was transient and could be reversed after the discontinuation of treatment. Therefore, PTX-induced macular edema may not depend on inflammation and the upregulation of VEGF. Therefore, PTX-induced macular edema may not depend on inflammation and upregulation of VEGF, and the exact mechanism requires further investigation.

The in vitro studies showed that the expression of AQP4 increased after treatment of Müller cells with PTX. As the most abundant osmotically driven transmembrane water channel protein, AQP4 is essential for normal retinal fluid and water homeostasis [27]. Under normal conditions, Müller cells transfer excess water from the retina to the blood through transcytotic water transport via AQP4 channels. [27] AQP4 is also involved in vasogenic and cytotoxic edema in the brain. [27] In the eyes, AQP4 expression is restricted to the retinal Müller cells and ciliary epithelium. In the presence of PTX, the dysfunction of Müller cells results in dysregulation of water movement through the glial-vascular interface. This process redistributes AQP4, thereby leading to an increase in the permeability of Müller cells. This effect is caused by cytotoxic edema. Glial cell swelling is considered the main cause of ischemic cerebral edema. [28] In the present study, we observed that PTX-induced macular edema may involve cytotoxic edema. In addition to cytotoxic edema characterized by Müller cell swelling, vasogenic edema may also be involved in the development of macular edema. In our previous studies, we also found that the polarization function of the RPE was affected by PTX [29].Moreover, the in vivo and ERG examination results showed that the OPS values were slightly decreased. These findings suggested limited impairment of the barrier function. Therefore, according to the present findings, we hypothesized that the PTX-induced macular edema was mainly due to Müller cell toxicity. However, clinical studies have found that after discontinuing the use of PTX medication, the condition of macular edema was reversed. This may be because that PTX-induced macular edema was significantly different from other types.The absence of retinal vascular leakage suggested that the BRB was not irreversibly damaged and that macular edema may be caused by a transient disfunction of Müller cells, which can be alleviated or reversed after discontinuation of treatment. [23]

CAI have been utilized in a few clinical cases of PTX-induced macular edema. [30, 31] In addition, it is widely used in the treatment of epilepsy, obesity, arthritis, and hypoxic tumors. [32] In ophthalmological practice, CAI are commonly used in the treatment of glaucoma and macular edema secondary to retinitis pigmentosa. [33, 34] In vitro, the addition of CAI in cultured Müller cells reduced the expression of AQP4. This indicates that CAI (e.g., acetazolamide) reversibly inhibit the water conduction of AQP4, thereby reducing the formation of edema. In addition, one study demonstrated that transgenic mice overexpressing carbonic anhydrase exhibited increased joint inflammation and tissue destruction. [35] Meanwhile, CA8 regulates inflammatory pain by inhibiting the inositol triphosphate receptor 1 (ITPR1)-cytoplasmic free calcium pathway. [36] Our study demonstrated that CAI alleviated inflammation. CAXIV was initially found in human retinas [37]; it is a membrane-associated enzyme in the retina, with a molecular weight of 54 KDa.[38] It has been reported that inhibition of RPE cell-associated membrane-bound CA is sufficient for enhancing subretinal fluid uptake and retinal adhesion in patients with macular edema and other retinal diseases. [37, 39, 40] CAXIV is the target of CA inhibitors that enhance subretinal fluid absorption in macular edema. In addition, CAXIV may facilitate CO2 removal from the neural retina and modulate photoreceptor function. The enrichment of CAXIV in specific membrane domains of glia cells and RPE suggests that it has a specialized buffering effect on the pH value and volume of retinal neurons and their surrounding extracellular gaps. [41] Thus, we hypothesized that the mechanisms through which CAI alleviate PTX-induced macular edema mainly include: (1) further regulation of water transport and alleviation of macular edema by downregulating the expression of AQP4 in Müller cells; and (2) inhibition of the activity of membrane-binding CAXIV, and enhancement of subretinal fluid absorption in patients with macular edema, thus alleviating edema.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the findings of this study suggest that PTX-induced macular edema is mainly due to Müller cell toxicity. CAI may relieve PTX-induced macular edema and facilitate recovery by regulating water channels and enhancing the absorption of subretinal fluid. This evidence provides a new potential therapeutic approach for PTX-induce macular edema in clinical practice.
