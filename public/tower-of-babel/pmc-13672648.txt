Dietary FODMAP Manipulation for Exercise-Associated Gastrointestinal Symptoms: Why Symptom Severity Should Guide Our Approach
Authors: Rachel Scrivin, Natália Vilela Silva Daniel, Gary Slater, Ricardo J S Costa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExercise-induced gastrointestinal syndrome (EIGS) is a well-defined term that describes disruptions to gastrointestinal integrity and function that occur during exercise. A commonly reported outcome of EIGS is exercise-associated gastrointestinal symptoms (Ex-GIS), including flatulence, lower abdominal bloating and pain, and urge to defecate, which can compromise comfort, training quality and race-day execution. Low-fermentable oligo-, di-, mono-saccharide and polyol (FODMAP) diets are effective at reducing gastrointestinal symptoms in individuals with irritable bowel syndrome and, owing to similarities in symptom presentation, have increasingly been used by athletes to better manage Ex-GIS. This Current Opinion article argues that FODMAP manipulation should be used strategically rather than as a routine dietary default. For athletes with severe Ex-GIS, a short-term low-FODMAP approach may help attenuate symptoms around key training sessions and/or competition. In contrast, athletes with mild or infrequent symptoms may benefit from a more liberal FODMAP intake that mitigates disruptions to gastrointestinal integrity and potentially EIGS outcomes while avoiding unnecessary dietary restriction. However, neither approach has consistently been shown to improve performance. Accordingly, any modification of FODMAP intake should be individualized and guided by likely underlying primary mechanisms, symptom severity, exercise demands, and the need to maintain overall dietary adequacy.

Key Points

A low-FODMAP diet appears effective in reducing Ex-GIS severity, particularly when used short-term around key training sessions and/or competition.Higher FODMAP intakes may mitigate EIGS pathophysiology with possible protective dietary influences; however, this may lead to greater nutrient malabsorption and Ex-GIS severity.Current evidence does not show consistent performance benefits from short-term FODMAP manipulation.Future research should incorporate standardized, validated symptomology assessment tools and full dietary provision in line with current best practice guidelines.

Introduction

Exercise-associated gastrointestinal symptoms (Ex-GIS) are commonly reported by athletes, with up to 80% of ultra-endurance runners reporting symptoms, including nausea, urge to regurgitate, belching, upper-abdominal pain, urge to defecate, and diarrhea [1]. Ex-GIS can interrupt or prevent training (18.6%) and/or competitions (11.6%) [2] or, in some cases, be a sign of something more serious and of clinical concern aligned with exercise-induced gastrointestinal syndrome (EIGS), e.g., gastroparesis, acute reversible colitis, and/or septic shock [1, 3–6]. Ex-GIS are an outcome of EIGS [1, 7], which warrants attention with respect to both prevention and management strategies [8].

EIGS describes the complex pathophysiological response of the gastrointestinal tract to exercise that disrupts gastrointestinal integrity and/or function. EIGS develops via two primary causal pathways, the circulatory– and/or neuroendocrine–gastrointestinal pathways [8]. Two additional proposed pathways requiring further investigation include mechanical strain of exercise (i.e., jolting, jarring, and/or tissue friction) on the peritoneal cavity and exaggerated metabolic responses to exercise (i.e., decreased pH, or hypoglycemia) [8]. The minimum threshold of exertional stress required to promote substantial acute gastrointestinal integrity changes has been observed in experimental exercise models that apply ≥ 2-h exertional-heat stress (EHS) or ≥ 3-h in temperate conditions [9, 10]. Exercise stress below the minimum threshold is unlikely to lead to any clinically relevant changes to gastrointestinal integrity or function [10]. Specific therapeutic interventions with mostly consistent beneficial outcomes on Ex-GIS and gastrointestinal functional issues include promoting euhydration (e.g., before and during exercise), gut training, individual feeding tolerance adjustments, or the implementation of a low- fermentable oligo-, di-, mono-saccharide and polyol (FODMAP) diet [8].

FODMAPs are short-chain fermentable carbohydrates that are not entirely digested and/or poorly absorbed by the intestines [11, 12], whereby, upon reaching the large intestine, they are rapidly fermented by the colonic microbiota [11, 13]. Bacterial fermentation increases colonic contents, including water and gases such as hydrogen (H2), methane (CH4), and carbon dioxide (CO2) [14, 15]. The end point of fermentation may lead to an increase in short-chain fatty acids (SCFA) production, e.g., acetate, butyrate, and propionate [16]. Lowering dietary FODMAP content has proven efficacy for reducing gastrointestinal symptoms and improving quality of life for bowel disorders of gut–brain interaction, such as irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) [17–19]. Many of the gastrointestinal symptoms reported by people with IBS are similar to those described by athletes during and/or shortly after exercise, i.e., Ex-GIS [7]. It is not surprising that many athletes self-experiment with dietary management strategies to mitigate Ex-GIS, such as dietary fiber reduction (15%), dairy avoidance (6%), and low-FODMAP diets (5%), commonly implemented with success [20]. The most frequently eliminated FODMAP food components include lactose (88%), fructose (24%), galacto-oligosaccharides (22%), polyols (6%) and fructo-oligosaccharides (5%), with nearly all athletes reporting an improvement in their symptoms after specific dietary FODMAP removal [21].

However, as dietary FODMAPs have protective mechanisms for maintaining epithelial integrity (i.e., acting as a prebiotic) [16, 22] it is possible that a high-FODMAP diet has merit for endurance athletes in protecting the integrity of the gastrointestinal tract in response to prolonged strenuous exercise [22, 23]. Therefore, in this Current Opinion article, we argue that dietary FODMAP manipulation is a clinically useful intervention but one that requires careful consideration. Specifically, we propose that low-FODMAP diets are most appropriate for athletes experiencing frequent, performance-limiting symptoms, whereas higher-FODMAP diets can be used to support epithelial integrity and SCFA production when symptoms are less severe. We use available experimental data to support this position and recommend practical guidance on when to apply a low-FODMAP diet with endurance athletes.

Study Selection

To inform this opinion, PubMed, SPORTDiscus, Ovid MEDLINE, CINAHL Ultimate, Embase, Cochrane reviews, and Web of Science databases were searched (July 2024) using terms related to endurance exercise, gastrointestinal symptoms, EIGS outcomes, and FODMAPs. We focused on experimental studies that applied a low-FODMAP diet before (e.g., 24–48-h) and/or during endurance exercise with either a high-FODMAP diet or a control, placebo, habitual or usual diet as a comparator. Studies excluded were animal models or in-vitro studies, and those that included athletes < 18 or > 55 years, pregnant and/or lactating females, sedentary individuals, or individuals with diagnosed gastrointestinal diseases or disorders (e.g., IBS and inflammatory bowel disease). We used these studies to illustrate how varied FODMAP loads may influence EIGS outcomes and/or Ex-GIS, and how this should inform practical recommendations aligned with current best-practice guidelines [8].

Dietary FODMAP Manipulation

The recommended low-FODMAP dietary cutoffs have been defined as < 0.5 g FODMAPs at each sitting (e.g., meal and/or snack) [24, 25], and the efficacy of these cutoffs has been tested in several studies [11, 24]; however, study participants were either individuals with IBS or healthy controls, not specifically endurance athletes. As endurance athletes have increased energy and carbohydrate requirements, it is likely that this leads to a higher FODMAP intake, e.g., up to 81 g per day (g·d−1) of total FODMAPs reported in an endurance athlete’s habitual diet [26, 27], compared with ~ 24 g·d−1 in a typical Australian diet [24]. It is reasonable to assume that owing to an endurance athlete’s increased energy and carbohydrate requirement, a more realistic low-FODMAP diet cutoff is < 5 g·d−1 total FODMAPs, which is recommended for exercise gastroenterology research [9].

When comparing the low-FODMAP dietary interventions, of the six included studies, only three studies provided adequate dietary control where all intervention food and drink were provided to promote dietary adherence, with dietary intake confirmed against food diaries (Table 2) [23, 28, 29]. The dietary controlled low- and high-FODMAP trials comprised 2.0 ± 0.7 g·d−1 versus 46.9 ± 26.2 g·d−1 [23], 3.0 ± 0.2 g·d−1 versus 54.8 ± 10.5 g·d−1 [28], 8.1 ± 3.5 g·d−1 versus 41.4 ± 7.9 g·d−1 [29] of total FODMAPs, respectively. Although the low-FODMAP trial in Lis et al. [29] exceeded the commonly recommended cutoff of < 5 g·d−1 total FODMAPs, the absolute difference between low- and high-FODMAP diets remained large enough to constitute clear dietary exposures, justifying their comparison within this context. [9]. Taken together, we believe the data support the pragmatic use of a short-term low-FODMAP intervention for athletes reporting substantial Ex-GIS, particularly when full dietary provision and adherence can be achieved. However, considering only two studies implemented robust dietary control aligned to best-practice guidelines (e.g., a randomized double-blind crossover protocol with full dietary provisions), this highlights that our current position rests on limited evidence, and that clinical decisions must therefore be individualized rather than protocol-driven. In advocating for low-FODMAP strategies, we acknowledge the potential for restrictive dietary practices to exacerbate disordered eating in susceptible athletes [30]. We view low-FODMAP manipulation as a time-limited, symptom-driven intervention that should be used with expert nutrition support and careful monitoring, allowing for adequate energy availability.

Effect of Dietary FODMAP Manipulation on Ex-GIS

To reduce the risk of bias when assessing Ex-GIS, the use of a validated visual analogue scale during exercise is advocated [7], alongside pretest familiarization [7, 9]. Despite this a range of symptom assessment tools have been used to measure and describe Ex-GIS. Three studies [23, 28, 31] used a validated visual analogue scale [7] recommended for exercise gastroenterology research [9], whereas the other studies used nonstandardized and nonvalidated scales [26, 29, 32]. Across studies, lower Ex-GIS severity during exercise was reported in most low-FODMAP trials (n = 5), compared with high-FODMAP trials (Table 3). Specifically, low-FODMAP trials were associated with less gut discomfort [23, 28], total GIS severity [23, 28, 32], upper GIS severity [23, 28], lower GIS severity [23], nausea, and dizziness [23]. Collectively, these findings support our positioning that low-FODMAP diets reduce Ex-GIS severity. In contrast, Lis et al. [29] reported no differences in Ex-GIS severity between trials; however, the exercise stress load was likely insufficient to result in substantial Ex-GIS, as demonstrated in other exercise models that reliably promoted Ex-GIS [33–36]. From a practical perspective, we interpret these findings as evidence that low-FODMAP diets reduce Ex-GIS severity rather than the incidence per se. That is, athletes may still experience Ex-GIS during exercise, but when they do, the symptom severity appears to be reduced when low-FODMAP strategies are implemented.

Effect of Dietary FODMAP Manipulation on Gastrointestinal Integrity

The circulatory–gastrointestinal pathway of EIGS describes a redistribution of blood flow away from the splanchnic area toward the exercising muscles and peripheral circulation, to aid skeletal muscle metabolism and thermoregulation during exercise [1, 8]. Reduced blood flow to the splanchnic area leads to perturbations of the intestinal epithelium, contributing to gastrointestinal dysfunction and symptom development [1, 8]. A key biomarker used to measure the magnitude of intestinal epithelial damage is plasma intestinal fatty acid-binding protein (I-FABP) concentrations [9]. I-FABP is solely present in the enterocytes of the intestinal epithelium and is a proposed surrogate marker indicative of enterocyte damage [37].

Only two studies that manipulated dietary FODMAP load have measured changes in plasma I-FABP concentrations at rest and in response to exercise (Table 4) [23, 28]. For clinical relevance, the minimal detectable change (MDC) of pre- to post-exercise plasma I-FABP concentrations is ≥ 1301 pg∙mL−1 [10]. Scrivin et al. [28] observed no significant differences in pre- to post-exercise plasma I-FABP concentration between dietary interventions (i.e., high-carbohydrate low-FODMAP ~ 601 pg·mL−1; high-carbohydrate high-FODMAP ~ 706 pg·mL−1). In this instance, it is likely that the 48-h high carbohydrate intake (i.e., 10–12 g·kg·d−1) pre-exercise, and modest carbohydrate (30 g·h−1) during the first 2 h of steady-state exercise, lessened epithelial injury, regardless of dietary FODMAP intake [28]. Similarly, higher carbohydrate intakes (i.e., 45 g·h−1) during exercise can ameliorate epithelial injury [38] and attenuate small intestine permeability [39]. These findings support the importance of carbohydrate ingestion to maintain carbohydrate availability to fuel exercise while also mitigating intestinal epithelial damage [38, 40, 41]. In contrast, Gaskell et al. [23] had participants running for 2 h at 60% of V˙ O2max in 35 °C heat without carbohydrate intake during exercise, which resulted in clinically relevant pre-to post-exercise changes in plasma I-FABP concentrations of ~ 1805 pg·mL−1 and ~ 1174 pg·mL−1 on low- and high-FODMAP, respectively. It is likely that lower plasma I-FABP concentrations observed on high-FODMAP are due to greater nutrient content along the intestinal lumen, as evidenced by higher breath H2 during the recovery period and/or greater fecal SCFA concentrations, which may stimulate epithelial microvascular blood flow, thereby mitigating intestinal epithelial injury [22, 23].

Supporting this rationale, secondary sample analysis by Gaskell et al. [22] demonstrated that a high-FODMAP diet (51 g·d−1) compared with a low-FODMAP diet (2 g·d−1) substantially increased total fecal SCFA (i.e., + 60%), including acetate, butyrate, and propionate concentrations, as well as modestly increasing plasma total SCFA (i.e., + 16%) and acetate concentrations. Scrivin et al. [42] reported similar findings, i.e., high-carbohydrate high-FODMAP diets were associated with significantly higher total fecal SCFA (i.e., + 48%), including acetate and butyrate concentrations compared with habitual diets. These findings support the rationale for SCFA contributing to reduced intestinal epithelial injury, despite a higher fermentative load [22].

Owing to perturbations in intestinal epithelial integrity experienced during exercise, it is likely that bacteria translocate into the systemic circulation, causing systemic inflammatory and/or anti-inflammatory cytokine responses [9]. However, only two studies investigated secondary follow-on systemic outcomes [23, 28]. Both Gaskell et al. [23] and Scrivin et al. [28] found no significant differences in systemic responses between low- and high-FODMAP trials (Table 5). It is important to note that laboratory models using exercise stress with or without carbohydrate intake may not be sufficient to elicit substantial systemic cytokine responses. Moderate increases in cytokine responses have only been observed in three laboratory studies [33, 35, 43], whereas field studies consistently report much larger increases [44, 45]. It is possible that no effect was observed in Gaskell et al. [23] and Scrivin et al. [28] as much greater exercise stress is required to truly detect intervention effects at the systemic cytokine level. However, as dietary FODMAPs maintain epithelial integrity (i.e., acting as a prebiotic and subsequent SCFA production) [16, 22], it is possible that a high-FODMAP diet has merit for endurance athletes in protecting the integrity of the gastrointestinal tract in response to prolonged strenuous exercise [22, 23]. We argue that low-FODMAP diets are best positioned as short-term strategies to reduce Ex-GIS severity. Once acute symptom control is achieved, practitioners should prioritize the reintroduction of FODMAP-containing foods where tolerated, particularly for endurance athletes with high training loads, to support gastrointestinal integrity and ensure overall dietary adequacy.

Effect of Dietary FODMAP Manipulation on Gastrointestinal Function

The development of EIGS via the neuroendocrine gastrointestinal pathway is due to an increase in sympathetic nervous system activity, which affects gastrointestinal functional capacity (i.e., impaired gastrointestinal motility and malabsorption) [1]. Gastrointestinal function can be assessed using various techniques to establish gastrointestinal emptying, transit, absorption, and smooth muscle activity [9]. Less-invasive techniques include a lactulose challenge, ingestion of a telemetry pill, measuring breath H2 and CH4, or electrogastrography [9]. To date, only two studies have investigated gastrointestinal function in the context of FODMAP manipulation during exercise, assessing either carbohydrate malabsorption [23] or orocecal transit time (OCTT) [28].

Carbohydrate malabsorption, typically determined by an increase in breath H2 responses > 10 ppm from baseline [46], is clinically relevant to Ex-GIS given its association with luminal gas production and an increase in osmotic fluid, which are potential triggers of Ex-GIS during exercise. Gaskell et al. [23] reported significantly greater area under the curve for breath H2 concentration during exercise and recovery following a high-FODMAP diet compared with a low-FODMAP diet (p = 0.031) (Table 4) [23]. This is unsurprising given FODMAPs are osmotically active and readily fermented, naturally producing more H2 [11, 24]. A high-FODMAP diet is likely to increase luminal contents, which may have further contributed to carbohydrate malabsorption [13]. Malabsorbed carbohydrate might activate the ileal brake, delaying gastrointestinal transit, and increasing nutrient exposure within the intestine during exercise, potentially mitigating intestinal hypoperfusion [47]. In contrast, Scrivin et al. [28] reported delayed OCTT in both high-FODMAP and low-FODMAP trials (~ 83 min and ~ 95 min post exercise, respectively), which was an unexpected finding given the lower fermentable loads in the low-FODMAP trial. As both trials included a 48-h high-carbohydrate loading period, it is plausible that increased total energy and food volume increased luminal contents, thereby influencing gastrointestinal motility equally, rather than the FODMAP load per se. This observation could have broader implications for endurance athletes adhering to carbohydrate-loading strategies independently of FODMAP intake. Given this limited and somewhat equivocal data on gastrointestinal transit and carbohydrate malabsorption, we do not believe current evidence supports using FODMAP manipulation primarily to ‘optimize’ gut function. However, reductions in carbohydrate malabsorption and fermentation provide a biologically plausible pathway by which low-FODMAP diets reduce Ex-GIS symptom burden during exercise. These mechanistic effects likely operate through reductions in luminal distention and gas production, rather than improvements in motility as such. We suggest that FODMAP manipulation should be viewed as a symptom targeted strategy that may benefit susceptible athletes, rather than a universal prescription, reinforcing the need for individualized case-by-case application.

Effect of Dietary FODMAP Manipulation on Performance Outcomes

While it might be assumed that any moderation of Ex-GIS severity and/or incidence would have the potential to result in favorable performance outcomes, only one experimental study [28] has explored the performance implications of low-FODMAP diets. Endurance athletes consumed a 48-h high-carbohydrate high-FODMAP or high-carbohydrate low-FODMAP diet with a standardized low-FODMAP breakfast before completing a 2-h steady-state run at 60% of V˙ O2max followed by a 1-h distance performance test in temperate ambient conditions. Despite greater Ex-GIS severity reported on the high-carbohydrate high-FODMAP trial, no significant difference was observed for the distance completed during the 1-h distance performance test [28]. In contrast, field research in events > 3-h has indicated self-reported performance improvements on a 48-h high-carbohydrate low-FODMAP diet [31]. In real-world settings, a higher carbohydrate diet before prolonged endurance events is advocated to optimize glycogen storage, which can enhance athletic performance and delay fatigue [48] Given that only one controlled trial and a small number of case-series indicate possible performance benefits when low-FODMAP strategies are used as part of a broader gastrointestinal management strategy, we do not consider it appropriate to advocate low-FODMAP diets as a performance-enhancing intervention in their own right. Rather, our position is that any performance gains are likely to be secondary to better symptom control in selected athletes and should be communicated as such to avoid unrealistic expectations.

Conclusions

In this Current Opinion article piece, we have argued that dietary FODMAP manipulation is a useful but nuanced tool for managing Ex-GIS in endurance athletes. Only a small number of well-controlled experimental trials suggest that 24–48-h low-FODMAP diets can reduce the severity of Ex-GIS during and after exercise, whereas high-FODMAP diets may better support epithelial integrity, likely via their prebiotic effects. We view this apparent paradox not as a contradiction but as an indication that FODMAP manipulation should be used strategically, rather than simplistically.

For athletes with frequent, severe, performance-limiting Ex-GIS, we advocate a short-term, practitioner-led low-FODMAP diet around key training sessions and/or events, accompanied by specific carbohydrate provision during exercise to mitigate epithelial injury. For athletes with mild or infrequent symptoms, we see greater merit in a high-FODMAP diet, which may have some protective effect on intestinal epithelial integrity, aligned with its prebiotic properties (i.e., increased fecal and/or SCFA concentrations) [22]. In all cases, we emphasize that low-FODMAP diets should not become a chronic default diet in endurance sport, especially for those with restrictive eating patterns or disordered eating [30]. Dietary FODMAP manipulation should be viewed as one component of a broader, individualized gastrointestinal management plan. This plan should be preceded by an appropriate assessment to identify likely causal pathways and implemented in collaboration with qualified practitioners. Future research using best practice methodological frameworks is required to test and refine this opinion, but we support the principles outlined, which provide a pragmatic basis for clinical decision-making.
