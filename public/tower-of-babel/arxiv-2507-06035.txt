Rethinking Pricing in Energy Markets: Pay-as-Bid vs Pay-as-Clear
Ioannis Caragiannis, Zhile Jiang, Stratis Skoulakis

Abstract
The design of energy markets is a subject of ongoing debate, particularly concerning the choice between the widely adopted Pay-as-Clear (PC) pricing mechanism and the alternative Pay-as-Bid (PB). These mechanisms determine how energy producers are compensated: under PC, all selected producers are paid the market-clearing price (i.e., the highest accepted bid), while under PB, each selected producer is paid their own submitted bid. The overarching objective is to meet the total demand for energy at minimal cost in the presence of strategic behavior. We present two key theoretical results. First, no mechanism can uniformly dominate PC or PB. This means that for any mechanism ℳ \mathcal{M} , there exists a market configuration and a mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium of PC (respectively for PB) that yields strictly lower total energy costs than under ℳ \mathcal{M} . Second, in terms of worst-case equilibrium outcomes, PB consistently outperforms PC: across all market instances, the highest possible equilibrium price under PB is strictly lower than that under PC. This suggests a structural robustness of PB to strategic manipulation. These theoretical insights are further supported by extensive simulations based on no-regret learning dynamics, which consistently yield lower average market prices in several energy market settings.

Introduction

In modern electricity markets, an Independent System Operator (ISO) coordinates energy production so that the overall production meets the energy demand
[
5
]
. In particular, the ISO performs a procurement auction in which each electricity producer submits a bid specifying their production cost per kilowatt-hour (kWh) together with the maximum energy they can supply. The ISO then selects a set of producers that can collectively satisfy the overall demand at the lowest cost, subject to their supply constraints
[
5
,
9
,
2
]
.

A key design choice in such procurement auctions is the pricing mechanism:
how much producers are paid for the energy that they produce?
Two mechanisms have been mainly considered, Pay-as-Bid (PB)
[
25
]
and Pay-as-Clear (PC)
[
5
]
. In PB, the selected producers are paid according to their bid. In PC, they are paid a uniform price per kWh that is equal to the highest accepted bid. The choice between these two pricing mechanisms has been a large debate in energy economics and market design. Today, PC has vastly dominated over PB in both US and EU energy markets with the basic argument being the fact that PC promotes truthful bidding. For example, quoting from the main website of the European Commission on electricity market design
[
1
]
:
“This model (pay-as-clear) provides efficiency, transparency and incentives to keep costs as low as possible. […] The alternative would not provide cheaper prices. In the pay-as-bid model, producers (including cheap renewables) would simply bid at the price they expect the market to clear, not at zero or at their generation costs. Overall, it is better for consumers to have a transparent model that reveals the true costs of energy and provides incentives for individuals to become active in generating their own electricity.”

Despite the aforementioned debate and several papers (see Section
1.2
) discussing aspects of mechanisms PB and PC, there are very few related game-theoretic results. Our work aims to provide a game-theretic study, first motivated by the following natural questions:

Question 1

Does PC really lead to truthful bidding? Do PB and PC actually lead to similar energy prices?

Since producers repeatedly adjust their bids based on the past bids of their competitors in energy markets, we use the notion of
Nash equilibrium
(NE) to capture their long-run behavior when interacting with pricing mechanisms
[
9
]
.

1.1
Our Contribution

Taking a step away from PB or PC, the minimum property that a pricing mechanism
\mathcal{M}
should satisfy is that it should not be
strongly dominated
by any other mechanism. This means that there should not be any other mechanism leading to lower energy prices than
\mathcal{M}
in all energy markets. Our first main contribution is that
neither PC, nor PB, nor the well-known truthful Vickrey-Clarke-Groves (VCG) mechanism can be strongly dominated
. This is formally stated as Theorem
3.1
. On the positive side, Theorem
3.1
establishes that there is no mechanism strongly dominating PC, which is an important property for the most widely deployed mechanism in practice. Interestingly, Theorem
3.1
establishes the same property for PB and VCG as well. Furthermore, our proof indicates that there always exist mixed NE of PB and PC that yield higher or lower energy prices. This raises the following key question:

Question 2

Are there any formal advantages of Pay-as-Clear over Pay-as-Bid or vice versa?

Despite the fact that PC is the mechanism that is actually used in practice, we provide an affirmative answer on the above question in favor of PB. In particular, our second main finding is that
the worst-case NE of PB always leads to smaller energy prices than the worst-case NE of PC
. This is formally stated as Theorem
3.2
and is the most interesting contribution of the paper (both technically and conceptually). Informally, when comparing PB and PC with respect to their worst-case outcome, PB is always the best choice.

Interestingly, the above is not true once comparing PC vs VCG. In Theorem
3.3
, we provide instances of energy markets with
n
producers where VCG yields an average energy price that is
\Theta(\log n)
times higher than the worst-case energy price of PC. Additionally, en route to establishing our results, we provide a detailed analysis of both PB and PC, revealing properties such as the non-truthfulness of PC and PB, the existence of pure NE for PC, the non-existence of pure NE for PB, and more.

In Section
7
, we experimentally compare the two mechanisms PB and PC when the energy producers repeatedly use online learning to select their bids. Our experimental results suggest that PB consistently leads to significantly lower average energy prices. This behavior also appears in energy markets where the best-case mixed NE of PC is lower than the worst-case mixed NE of PB.

2
Preliminaries

An
energy market
consists of
n
energy
producers
(or
agents
). Each producer
i\in[n]
can supply up to
s_{i}\in(0,1]
units of energy and has an integer
marginal cost
of
c_{i}\in\{0,1,...,M\}
per unit of energy produced. We use
[M]
as an abbreviation for the set of integers
\{0,1,...,M\}
. The supply of each producer is
publicly known
while the marginal cost is
private information
of the producer.

A mechanism
\mathcal{M}
takes as input a bid
b_{i}
submitted from each producer
i\in[n]
, interpreted as her marginal cost. The mechanism
\mathcal{M}
takes as input the
bid profile

\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},...,b_{n})
and decides the amount of energy
x_{i}(\mathbf{b})\in[0,1]
that will be bought from producer
i\in[n]
as well as the corresponding price per unit
p_{i}(\mathbf{b})\in[M]
. The mechanism
\mathcal{M}
ensures that

•

the total amount of energy that will be bought from all producers is equal to
1
, i.e.,
\sum_{i\in[n]}{x_{i}(\mathbf{b})}=1
, so that the amount to be bought from producer
i\in[n]
does not exceed her maximum supply, i.e.,
0\leq x_{i}(\mathbf{b})\leq s_{i}
(
feasibility constraints
), and

•

for every agent
i\in[n]
who sells a non-zero amount of energy, the corresponding price is not lower than the agent’s reported marginal cost, i.e.,
p_{i}(\mathbf{b})\geq b_{i}
(
individual rationality
).

We define the
average unit price
(or, simply, unit price) of mechanism
\mathcal{M}
as
p^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{unit}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{b}):=\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}(\mathbf{b})\cdot p_{i}(\mathbf{b})
; this is equal to the total amount of money spent to cover the whole energy demand.

We use the term
allocation
to refer to the vector
\mathbf{x}(\mathbf{b})=(x_{1}(\mathbf{b}),...,x_{n}(\mathbf{b}))
. We consider mechanisms that compute
cost-minimizing
allocations. For their definition, we use the order relation
i\succ_{\mathbf{b}}j
to denote that either
b_{i}<b_{j}
or
b_{i}=b_{j}
and
i<j
.

Definition 1
(market clearing price and cost-minimizing allocation)

Given a bidding profile
\mathbf{b}
, the
pivotal agent

\tau(\mathbf{b})
is the first agent in the ascending order
\prec_{\mathbf{b}}
covering the total energy demand, i.e.,
s_{\tau(\mathbf{b})}+\sum_{j\prec_{\mathbf{b}}\tau(\mathbf{b})}{s_{j}}\geq 1
and
s_{i}+\sum_{j\prec_{\mathbf{b}}i}{s_{j}}<1
for every
i\prec_{\mathbf{b}}\tau(\mathbf{b})
. The
market clearing price

q(\mathbf{b}):=b_{\tau(\mathbf{b})}
is the bid of the pivotal agent. The
cost-minimizing allocation

\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(\cdot)
is then defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{x}_{i}(\mathbf{b})=\begin{cases}s_{i},&i\prec_{\mathbf{b}}\tau(\mathbf{b})\\
1-\sum_{j\prec_{\mathbf{b}}\tau(\mathbf{b})}s_{j},&\tau(\mathbf{b})=i\\
0,&\tau(\mathbf{b})\prec_{\mathbf{b}}i\end{cases}.

Mechanisms Pay-as-Bid (PB), Pay-as-Clear (PC), and Vickrey-Clark-Groves (VCG) use the cost-minimizing allocation
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{b})
but different pricing rules
\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{b})
. PB uses the simplest pricing mechanism where the per unit price paid to producer
i\in[n]
equals her bid, i.e.,
p^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(PB)}}_{i}(\mathbf{b})=b_{i}
. PC pays to every agent the market clearing price
q(\mathbf{b})
per unit of energy, i.e.,
p^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(PC)}}_{i}(\mathbf{b})=q(\mathbf{b})
. For VCG, the pricing function for each producer
i\in[n]
is defined as

\displaystyle p^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{(VCG)}}_{i}(\mathbf{b})=\begin{cases}\frac{\sum_{j\in[n]/\{i\}}\widehat{x}_{j}(\mathbf{b}_{-i})\cdot b_{j}-\sum_{j\in[n]/\{i\}}\widehat{x}_{j}(\mathbf{b})\cdot b_{j}}{\widehat{x}_{i}(\mathbf{b})},&\text{if }\widehat{x}_{i}(\mathbf{b})\neq 0,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Hence, VCG pays each agent an amount equal to the decrease in social cost caused by the presence of the agent. Excellent introductions to VCG can be found in
[
22
]
and
[
16
]
.

Example 1

Consider the energy market with
n=4
producers with supplies
s_{1}=1/3,s_{2}=1/2,s_{3}=1/4,s_{4}=2/3
and marginal costs
c_{1}=0,c_{2}=1,c_{3}=2,c_{4}=3
. If every producer bids her marginal cost, then
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(\cdot)
will first buy
1/3
from agent
1
and
1/2
from agent
2
(matching their maximum supplies), and the remaining
1/6
from agent
3
. In this case, PB pays
0
per unit to agent
1
,
1
per unit to agent
2
,
2
per unit to agent
3
, and
3
per unit to agent
4
. PC pays every agent the market clearing price, which is
2
per unit. And VCG pays
11/4
per unit to agent
1
, since the cost to other agents is
1\cdot 1/2+2\cdot 1/6
when agent
1
stays in the market and increases to
1\cdot 1/2+2\cdot 1/4+3\cdot 1/4
when agent
1
leaves. By similar calculations, the price per energy unit is
17/6
,
1
, and
0
to agent
2
,
3
, and
4
, respectively.

2.1
From Mechanism to Games

Any mechanism
\mathcal{M}
induces a
finite-action game
, where each producer
i\in[n]
behaves strategically and selects her bid
b_{i}
among the bid values in
[M]
so as to maximize her
individual revenue

U_{i}^{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{b}):=(p_{i}(\mathbf{b})-c_{i})\cdot x_{i}(\mathbf{b})
.

Definition 2
(mixed Nash equilibrium)

Given a mechanism
\mathcal{M}
, a mixed Nash equilibrium is a collection of independent probability distributions
\mathbf{\sigma}:=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n})
over the possible bid values in
[M]
, such that for each agent
i\in[n]
,

\displaystyle\Ex_{\mathbf{b}\sim\sigma}[U_{i}^{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{b})]\geq\max_{b^{\prime}_{i}\in[M]}\Ex_{\mathbf{b}_{-i}\sim\sigma_{-i}}[U_{i}^{\mathcal{M}}(b^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{b}_{-i})].

Given an instance
\mathcal{I}:=\{(s_{i},c_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}
of an energy market, we denote by
\mathrm{MNE}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{I})
the set of all mixed Nash equilibria of mechanism
\mathcal{M}
for instance
\mathcal{I}
.

Since we consider finite games, any mechanism
\mathcal{M}
admits at least one mixed Nash equilibrium.

Definition 3
(pure Nash equilibrium)

Given a mechanism
\mathcal{M}
, a pure Nash equilibrium is a bidding profile
\mathbf{b}
such that for each agent
i\in[n]
,

\displaystyle U_{i}^{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{b})\geq\max_{b^{\prime}_{i}\in[M]}U_{i}^{\mathcal{M}}(b^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{b}_{-i}).

It is well-known that VCG has the truthful bid profile
(c_{1},c_{2},...,c_{n})
as pure Nash equilibrium.

We conclude this section by defining two different ways of comparing mechanisms in terms of the unit price at their mixed Nash equilibria.

Definition 4
(dominance relations between mechanisms)

A mechanism
\mathcal{M}

strongly dominates
mechanism
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
if and only if

\max_{\sigma\in\mathrm{MNE}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{I})}p^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{unit}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\sigma)\leq\min_{\sigma\in\mathrm{MNE}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime},\mathcal{I})}p^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{unit}}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}(\sigma)~~\text{for every instance }\mathcal{I}.

A mechanism
\mathcal{M}

weakly dominates
mechanism
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
if and only if

\max_{\sigma\in\mathrm{MNE}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{I})}p^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{unit}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\sigma)\leq\max_{\sigma\in\mathrm{MNE}(\mathcal{M}^{\prime},\mathcal{I})}p^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{unit}}_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}(\sigma)~~\text{for every instance }\mathcal{I}.

Less formally, when mechanism
\mathcal{M}
strongly dominates mechanism
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, then in every instance, the unit price at any mixed NE of
\mathcal{M}
is at most as high as the unit price of any mixed NE of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
. In case of weak dominance, there exists a mixed NE of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
that has at least as high unit price as any mixed NE of
\mathcal{M}
.

3
Overview of Technical Results

Our first main result establishes that PC is not strictly dominated by any other mechanism. However, we show that this also holds for PB and VCG.

Theorem 3.1

There is no mechanism strictly dominating Pay-as-Clear, Pay-as-Bid, or VCG.

Despite the fact that none among PB, PC, and VCG can be strictly dominated by any other mechanism, there are still interesting comparisons among them. Our second main result establishes that PB weakly dominates PC.

Theorem 3.2

Pay-as-Bid weakly dominates Pay-as-Clear. At the same time, Pay-as-Clear does not weakly dominate Pay-as-Bid.

Theorem
3.2
establishes that in every energy market, the unit price of any mixed NE of PB is always lower than the unit price of the worst-case mixed NE of PC. At the same time, Theorem
3.2
excludes the opposite direction.

Remark 1

We remark that in all non-degenerate energy markets, the unit price of the worst-case NE of PC is actually
strictly smaller
than the unit price the worst-case NE of PB.

Theorem
3.2
is directly implied by Theorems
3.4
and
3.5
, which characterize the unit prices of mixed NE for PC and PB, respectively. Both Theorem
3.4
and Theorem
3.5
are based on instance-dependent parameters and are presented in the next section.

Interestingly enough, VCG does not weakly dominate PC and can in fact lead to a considerably higher unit price. This is formally established in Theorem
3.3
.

Theorem 3.3

VCG does not weakly dominate Pay-as-Clear. There is an instance with
n
agents where the unit price of VCG is
\Theta(\log n)
times the unit price of the worst mixed NE of Pay-as-Clear.

3.1
Pay-as-Clear vs Pay-as-Bid

We now introduce some necessary notation for the comparison of PC and PB.

Definition 5

The best response of producer
i\in[n]
in Pay-as-Clear, with respect to the bidding profile
b_{-i}
, is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{BR}(b_{-i}):=\begin{cases}\argmax_{b_{i}\in[M]}U_{i}(b_{i},b_{-i})&\text{if }\max_{b_{i}\in[M]}U_{i}(b_{i},b_{-i})>0\\
c_{i}&\text{if }\max_{b_{i}\in[M]}U_{i}(b_{i},b_{-i})=0\end{cases}

We also denote by
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i}
the highest bid among the best responses of agent
i\in[n]
in case any other agent
j\neq i
bids either
c_{j}
or
c_{j}+1
,

\displaystyle b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i}:=\max_{\mathbf{d}_{-i}\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}}\max\{b_{i}\in[M]~:~b_{i}\in\mathcal{BR}(\mathbf{c}_{-i}+\mathbf{d}_{-i})\}.

Finally, we denote by
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i}:=\lceil c_{i}+\max_{b^{\prime}_{i}\in[M]}U_{i}(b^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{c}_{-i})/s_{i}\rceil
the smallest possible price giving agent
i\in[n]
at least utility
\max_{b^{\prime}_{i}\in[M]}U_{i}(b^{\prime}_{i},\mathbf{c}_{-i})
when she sells her maximum of
s_{i}
energy units.

Before proceeding, we provide an example clarifying the crucial notions of
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i}
and
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i}
.

Example 1

Consider an energy market with
M=6
and
n=3
producers with supplies
s_{1}=3/4,s_{2}=3/4,s_{3}=1/10
and costs
c_{1}=0,c_{2}=1,c_{3}=4
. Notice that the best response always locates on the cost of some other agent or
M
. When other agents bid truthfully, agent
1
’s best response is
4
since bidding
4
provides a utility of
1
, which is higher than the utility from bidding
1
, which is
0.75
, or bidding
6
, which is
0.9
. And the utility given by bidding
4
while other agents bid truthfully gives
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{1}=\lceil 0+1/0.75\rceil=2
. To reveal the final
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{1}
, we need to consider further if agent
2
, agent
3
, or both, bid their cost plus one. When agent
2
ever bids
c_{2}+1=2
, the best response of agent
1
is
2
. And if only agent
3
bids
c_{3}+1=5
, the best response is
5
. We conclude
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{1}=5
by taking the maximum among all the above best responses. We can also perform similar calculations for agent
2
, obtaining
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{2}=6
and
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{2}=2
. For agent
3
, it always yields a utility of
0
when other agents bid their cost or the cost plus one. Thus,
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{3}=4
by definition, and
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{3}=4
.

The quantities
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i}
and
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i}
can be used to establish lower and upper bounds on the unit price of mixed NE of PC and PB. Theorem
3.4
establishes such a lower bound for PC.

Theorem 3.4

For any energy market
\mathcal{I}
, Pas-as-Clear admits at least one pure NE with unit price at least
\max_{i\preceq_{\mathbf{c}}\tau(\mathbf{c})}b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i}
. Moreover, the unit price of any mixed NE of Pay-as-Clear is at least
\max_{i\preceq_{\mathbf{c}}\tau(\mathbf{c})}b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i}
.

Theorem
3.5
establishes a unit price upper bound at the worst-case mixed Nash equilibrium for PB.

Theorem 3.5

Any mixed NE of Pay-as-Bid is supported in
[\max_{i\preceq_{\mathbf{c}}\tau(\mathbf{c})}b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i}-1,\max_{i\preceq_{\mathbf{c}}\tau(\mathbf{c})}b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i}]
.

It should now be clear how Theorem
3.2
is directly implied by Theorem
3.4
and Theorem
3.5
. Specifically, Theorem
3.5
establishes that the support of any mixed NE of PB lies between the unit price of the best and worst mixed NE of PC. In Theorem
3.6
, we establish that under some mild assumption excluding degenerate instances, the worst-case NE of PB is strictly better than the worst-case NE of PC.

Theorem 3.6

The unit price given by the worst-case mixed NE of Pay-as-Bid is strictly smaller than
\max_{i\preceq_{\mathbf{c}}\tau(\mathbf{c})}b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i}
in a large family of instances.

8
Conclusion

The central result of this work is that the worst-case Nash equilibrium under the Pay-as-Bid mechanism always yields lower energy prices than the worst-case NE under the Pay-as-Clear mechanism. While this does not preclude the existence of specific mixed NEs under PC with lower prices than certain mixed NEs under PB, it nonetheless provides the strongest possible worst-case guarantee. Specifically, we show that PC, like PB and the VCG mechanism, cannot be strongly dominated by any alternative mechanism.

Our experimental evaluations support our theoretical findings, indicating that online learning dynamics consistently lead to lower average unit prices under PB compared to PC. An intriguing direction for future research is to derive formal price guarantees for both PB and PC under the assumption that all producers employ no-regret online learning algorithms to determine their bids.

6
) and (
7
) together, we get

\displaystyle U(t,\mathbf{c}_{-i}+\mathbf{d}^{\star}_{-i})=\max_{b_{i}\in[M]}U_{i}(b_{i},\mathbf{c}_{-i}+\mathbf{d}^{\star}_{-i}).

Thus, by definition,

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle\leq\max\{b_{i}\in[M]\text{ such that }b_{i}\in\mathcal{BR}(\mathbf{c}_{-i}+\mathbf{d}^{\star}_{-i})\}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\mathbf{d}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\max\{b_{i}\in[M]\text{ such that }b_{i}\in\mathcal{BR}(\mathbf{c}_{-i}+\mathbf{d}_{-i})\}=b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i}.

Finally, we consider what if
\max_{b_{i}\in[M]}U_{i}(b_{i},\mathbf{c}_{-i})=0
. In this case, we have
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i}=c_{i}+1
. Assume
t>c_{i}+1
, agent
j
with
c_{j}=c_{i}
will bid in
[c_{i}+1,t-1]
with probability of
1
since
\sum_{k\preceq_{\mathbf{c}}\tau(\mathbf{c})}s_{k}\geq 1
. And when agent
i
bids
t
, it receives a conditional expected utility of
0
since
\sum_{k\neq i,c_{k}\leq c_{i}}s_{k}\geq 1
. It contradicts that
\sigma
is a mixed NE since agent
i
can move all the probability mass of bidding
t
to bid
c_{i}+1
to improve the utility.

In all cases, the theorem follows.

Proof

Let
i^{\star}:=\argmax_{i\preceq_{\mathbf{c}}\tau(c)}b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i}
. Assume that if
s_{i^{\star}}<1
,
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}+1<b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}
, and
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{j}\leq b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}-2
for every agent
j\neq i^{\star}
. We will show that the unit price given by the worst-case mixed NE of PB is at most
p:=(1-s_{i^{\star}})\cdot\left(b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}+1+\gamma\cdot\sum_{\ell=b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}+2}^{b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}}{1}/{(\ell-c_{i^{\star}}-1)}\right)+s_{i^{\star}}\cdot b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}
where
\gamma=\max_{b^{\prime}_{i^{\star}}\in[M]}U_{i^{\star}}(b^{\prime}_{i^{\star}},\mathbf{c}_{-i^{\star}})/s_{i^{\star}}+1

p<b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}\leq\max_{i\preceq_{\mathbf{c}}\tau(\mathbf{c})}b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i}
.

We remark that
s_{i^{\star}}<1
is a natural assumption since, most likely, no single producer
i\in[n]
will be able to cover the whole energy demand. The assumption
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}+1<b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}
, is also mild since
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}+1\geq b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}
only happens when

sells exactly
s_{i^{\star}}
energy as a pivotal agent. This only happens if
\sum_{j:c_{j}\leq b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}}s_{j}
is equal to the total energy demand of
1
. This case will occur with
0
probability assuming a small random perturbation on the supplies
s_{j}
of the agents. Similarly, assuming in case of sufficiently small discretization and a random permutation on the supplies
s_{j}
, the assumption
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{j}\leq b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}-2
is satisfied with probability
1
.

We start by tightening the support of the mixed NE.

Lemma 5

Let
i^{\star}:=\argmax_{i\preceq_{\mathbf{c}}\tau(c)}b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i}
. In case that every agent
j\neq i^{\star}
has
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{j}\leq b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}-2
. Then the support of any mixed NE is a subset of
[b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}-1,b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}]
.

Proof

The lower bound follows directly from Theorem
6.1
, so we only need to prove the upper bound. We first establish that in case agent
j\in[n]
sells its supply
s_{j}\in[0,1]
at price
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{j}+1
, then its utility is higher than the
best possible utility
once bidding
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{j}
. By the definition of
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{j}
we have that,

\displaystyle b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{j}=\left\lceil c_{j}+\frac{\max_{b_{j}\in[M]}U_{j}(b_{j},\mathbf{c}_{-j})}{s_{j}}\right\rceil\geq c_{j}+\frac{\max_{b_{j}\in[M]}U_{j}(b_{j},\mathbf{c}_{-j})}{s_{j}}.

(8)

Then we consider the maximum utility achieved by becoming the pivotal agent, we have that

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbf{d}_{-j}\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}}\max_{b_{j}\in[M]}U_{j}(b_{j},\mathbf{c}_{-j}+\mathbf{d}_{-j})

\displaystyle\leq\max_{b_{j}\in[M]}U_{j}(b_{j},\mathbf{c}_{-j}+1)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{b_{j}\in[M]}U_{j}(b_{j}-1,\mathbf{c}_{-j})+(b_{j}-(b_{j}-1))\cdot s_{j}

\displaystyle=\max_{b_{j}\in[M]}U_{j}(b_{j},\mathbf{c}_{-j})+s_{j}.

(9)

The first inequality follows since the price under the same allocation is maximized if every other agent bids one step to the left. The second inequality follows since the allocation is the same if every agent shifts its bid one step to the left. The equality is simply by algebra.

Plug Inequality (
9
) into (
8
), we get

\displaystyle b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{j}+1\geq c_{j}+\frac{\max_{\mathbf{d}_{-j}\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}}\max_{b_{j}\in[M]}U_{j}(b_{j},\mathbf{c}_{-j}+\mathbf{d}_{-j})}{s_{j}}.

Notice that by Theorem
6.1
, the support of any mixed NE is greater than
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}-1
meaning that if agent
j
bids
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}-2\geq b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{j}+1
then agent
j\in[n]
sells its whole energy
s_{j}
at price at least
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{j}+1
, meaning that the utility of bidding
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}-2
is at least
U_{j}(b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{j},\mathbf{c}_{-j}+\mathbf{d}_{-j})
.

Thus, the agent
j
with
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{j}\leq b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{j}-2
will not have a positive probability to bidding any value to make it become the pivotal agent surely since the utility is always better if it can sell all the supply with price
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{j}+1
. And by a similar argument as in the Theorem
6.1
, agent
i^{\star}
bids at least
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}
with probability of
1
. Thus, agent
j
is ensured to sell everything by bidding
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}-1
. Recall we assume that
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{j}\leq b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}-2
. So any agent
j\neq i
prefers to bid
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}-1
than becoming the pivotal agent, in other words, it cannot be the one who bids the highest possible bid. The lemma follows since we can repeat the proof of Theorem
6.2
with the knowledge that agent
i
bids the highest.
∎

Now, consider a mixed NE
\sigma
. Assume that
t=\max_{\sigma_{i^{\star}}(b_{i^{\star}})>0}b_{i^{\star}}
. By Lemma
5
, we have
\sigma_{i^{\star}}(b_{i^{\star}})>0
only if
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}-1\leq b_{i^{\star}}\leq b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}
. Assume without loss of generality that
\sigma_{i^{\star}}(b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}})>0
; otherwise, the unit price is always lower than the bound we present. Due to the equilibrium, agent
i^{\star}
’s expected utility conditioned on bidding
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}
is at least the expected utility conditioned on bidding any other
b_{i^{\star}}\in[M]
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Ex_{\mathbf{b}_{-i^{\star}}\sim\sigma_{-i^{\star}}}[U_{i^{\star}}(b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}},\mathbf{b}_{-i^{\star}})]

\displaystyle\geq\Ex_{\mathbf{b}_{-i^{\star}}\sim\sigma_{-i^{\star}}}[U_{i^{\star}}(b_{i^{\star}},\mathbf{b}_{-i^{\star}})]\geq s_{i^{\star}}\cdot(b_{i^{\star}}-c_{i^{\star}})\cdot\Pr_{\mathbf{b}_{-i\star}\sim\sigma_{-i}}[x_{i^{\star}}(b_{i^{\star}},\mathbf{b}_{-i^{\star}})=s_{i^{\star}}]

(10)

The second inequality follows since we ignore any utility given by the case
x_{i^{\star}}(b_{i^{\star}},\mathbf{b}_{-i^{\star}})\neq s_{i^{\star}}
, in other words, agent
i^{\star}
sells all its supply while bidding
b_{i^{\star}}
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\Ex_{\mathbf{b}_{-i}\sim\sigma_{-i}}[U_{i^{\star}}(b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}},\mathbf{b}_{-i^{\star}})]\leq\max_{\mathbf{d}_{-{i^{\star}}}\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}}\max_{b_{i^{\star}}\in[M]}U_{i^{\star}}(b_{i},\mathbf{c}_{-i^{\star}}+\mathbf{d}_{-i^{\star}})\leq\max_{b_{i^{\star}}\in[M]}U_{i^{\star}}(b_{i^{\star}},\mathbf{c}_{-i^{\star}})+s_{i^{\star}}.

(11)

The first inequality follows since
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}
is either
c_{j}
or
c_{j}+1
for some agent
j
. The second inequality follows by Inequality (
9
).
Puttng Inequality (
10
) and (
11
) together, we have

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathbf{b}_{-i}\sim\sigma_{-i}}[x_{i^{\star}}(b_{i^{\star}},\mathbf{b}_{-i^{\star}})=s_{i^{\star}}]\leq\frac{\max_{b^{\prime}_{i^{\star}}\in[M]}U_{i^{\star}}(b^{\prime}_{i^{\star}},\mathbf{c}_{-i^{\star}})+s_{i^{\star}}}{s_{i^{\star}}\cdot(b_{i^{\star}}-c_{i^{\star}})}=\frac{\gamma}{b_{i^{\star}}-c_{i^{\star}}}.

Notice that in case
x_{i^{\star}}(b_{i^{\star}},\mathbf{b}_{-i^{\star}})<s_{i^{\star}}
then at least
1-s_{i^{\star}}
energy is bought at price at most
b_{i^{\star}}
. Denote the random variable
X_{\leq\alpha}
as the amount of energy that is bought at a price at most
\alpha
. The randomness comes from which bidding profile is drawn from the distribution. We have

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathbf{b}\sim\sigma}[X_{\leq\alpha}\geq 1-s_{i^{\star}}]\geq 1-\Pr_{\mathbf{b}_{-i}\sim\sigma_{-i}}[x_{i^{\star}}(b_{i^{\star}}=\alpha,\mathbf{b}_{-i^{\star}})=s_{i^{\star}}]\geq 1-\frac{\gamma}{\alpha-c_{i^{\star}}}.

(12)

We can then upperbound the unit price by minimizing the energy sold at a low price. Specifically, when
X_{\leq\alpha}\geq 1-s_{i^{\star}}
but

\displaystyle\Ex_{\mathbf{b}\sim\sigma}[p^{unit}(\mathbf{b})]\leq\sum_{\alpha=b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}-1}^{b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}}f(\alpha)\cdot(s_{i^{\star}}\cdot b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}+(1-s_{i^{\star}})\cdot\alpha),

(13)

where we denote

Notice that
\Pr_{\mathbf{b}\sim\sigma}[X_{\leq\alpha}\geq 1-s_{i^{\star}}]=0
for any
\alpha<b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}-1
since every agent bids at least
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}
. And
\Pr_{\mathbf{b}\sim\sigma}[X_{\leq\alpha}\geq 1-s_{i^{\star}}]=1
for any
\alpha\geq b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}
since every agent bids at most
b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}

\displaystyle f(\alpha)=\begin{cases}0,&\alpha=b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}-1\text{ or }b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}},\\

\gamma\cdot(\frac{1}{\alpha-c_{i^{\star}}-1}-\frac{1}{\alpha-c_{i^{\star}}}),&b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}+2\leq\alpha\leq b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}-1\\
\gamma\cdot\frac{1}{\alpha-c_{i^{\star}}-1},&\alpha=b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}\\
\end{cases}.

(14)

This is because the assignment presenting in Equation (

Plugging Equality (

\displaystyle\Ex_{\mathbf{b}\sim\sigma}[p^{unit}(\mathbf{b})]

\displaystyle\leq(1-s_{i^{\star}})\cdot\left((1-\frac{\gamma}{b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}-c_{i^{\star}}+1})\cdot(b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}+1)+\gamma\cdot\sum_{\alpha=b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}+2}^{b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}-1}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-c_{i^{\star}}-1}-\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-c_{i^{\star}}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\left(s_{i^{\star}}+(1-s_{i^{\star}})\cdot\gamma\cdot\frac{1}{b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}-c_{i^{\star}}-1}\right)\cdot b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}

\displaystyle=(1-s_{i^{\star}})\cdot\left(b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}+1-\gamma\cdot\frac{b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}-1}{b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}-c_{i^{\star}}-1}+\gamma\cdot\sum_{\alpha=b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{L}}_{i^{\star}}+2}^{b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}-1}\frac{1}{\alpha-c_{i^{\star}}-1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\left(s_{i^{\star}}+(1-s_{i^{\star}})\cdot\gamma\cdot\frac{1}{b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}-c_{i^{\star}}-1}\right)\cdot b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i^{\star}}

Example
2
demonstrates an energy market where the unit price given by VCG can be arbitrarily bad compared to the unit price given by PC or PB.

Example 2

Let
\delta>2
be a constant integer. Consider an energy market with
n=2k-1
producers and
M=\delta\cdot k-2
, where each producer’s supply and cost can be described as follows

\displaystyle s_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{k},&i\leq k,\\
\frac{1}{2k},&i=k+1,\\
\frac{1}{k(i-k)(i-k+1)},&k+2\leq i\leq 2k-1,\end{cases}

\displaystyle c_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&i\leq k,\\
\delta,&i=k+1,\\
\delta\cdot(i-k)-2,&k+2\leq i\leq 2k-1.\end{cases}

Recall that VCG is a truthful mechanism, and the externality determines the payment each agent receives their presence imposes on the total cost of the market, i.e., what the market would have to cost if that particular agent had not participated. Thus, the payment to each agent
i\in[k]
in the equilibrium where everyone bids truthfully is

\displaystyle p_{i}(\mathbf{c})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=k+1}^{2k-1}c_{j}\cdot s_{j}+M\cdot\left(1-\left(\sum_{j=1}^{2k-1}s_{j}-s_{i}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\delta\cdot\frac{1}{2k}+\sum_{j=2}^{k-1}\frac{\delta\cdot j-2}{k\cdot j\cdot(j+1)}+\frac{\delta\cdot k-2}{k^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{k}\cdot\mathcal{H}_{k}-\frac{1}{k},

where
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is the
k
-th harmonic number and the equalities hold by simple algebra. So, the worst-case unit price given by NE in VCG is at least
\Omega(\log n)
.

Next, we consider the worst-case unit price given by PB and PC. Due to Theorem
3.5
, the unit price in worst-case mixed NE in PB is bounded by
\max_{i\preceq_{\mathbf{c}}\tau(\mathbf{c})}b^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{H}}_{i}
. Since we have
(\delta\cdot j-1)\cdot\frac{1}{k}\cdot(1-\sum_{\ell=2}^{j}\frac{1}{\ell\cdot(\ell-1)})=(\delta-\frac{\delta}{j})\cdot\frac{1}{k}<\delta\cdot\frac{1}{k}

\delta

\delta+1
