Identifying a program’s “core components” using a co-design approach: a methodological worked example from a youth mental health and addictions navigation service
Authors: Elena Sheldrake, Kristen Yee Joshi, Susana Rivas, Naomi Algate, Anthony J. Levitt, Roula Markoulakis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSuccessful implementation of innovations requires early, foundational steps that establish the groundwork for long-term sustainability. A key step in this process is defining the core components of an innovation: the indispensable, non-negotiable elements that, if removed, would fundamentally alter the innovation. While the importance of identifying core components has been recognized, practical guidance on how to develop them is limited. As an illustrative example, the Family Navigation Project (FNP), a youth and family mental health and addictions (MHA) service, developed a process for defining their core components. Clearly articulating these components is vital for broader implementation of FNP across diverse contexts, ensuring fidelity, maintaining foundational integrity, and enabling scalability of similar innovations.MethodsGuided by Implementation Science frameworks, a five-phase methodology was used to co-develop the core components of FNP: (1) identification and extraction of preliminary component sources, (2) knowledge generation and interest-holder input, (3) conceptual analysis and core component identification, (4) member checking, and (5) finalization. All phases engaged FNP interest-holders, including FNP leadership, operational staff, research staff, frontline staff, and youth and family advisors, through an innovative co-design approach.ResultsEight core components were identified: (1) Rooted in the Lived Experience of Youth and Families, (2) Low Barrier, (3) Personalized Care Pathways, (4) Expert Guidance of the Mental Health and Addictions Systems, (5) Compassionate Persistence (6), Championing Equity, Diversity, Inclusion, and Anti-Racism, (7) Interdisciplinary Collaboration, and (8) Relationship-Based Care.ConclusionsClearly identifying the core components of FNP supports successful and sustainable implementation in new contexts. This co-design approach offers a replicable model for other services and innovations to maintain fidelity while adapting and implementing in diverse settings. By grounding implementation in lived experience and collaboration, it positions FNP and similar services to expand access to other environments or settings that would benefit from youth and family MHA navigation programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40900-026-00876-7.

Background

Effective implementation of innovations requires more than simply transferring models from one setting to another. Implementation science, the study of methods to promote the systematic uptake of evidence-based practices, provides structured approaches to support adaptation, sustainability, and scalability [1, 2]. A core tenet of this discipline is the recognition that context matters: contextual factors can either facilitate or hinder success in real-world settings, such that innovations that are effective in one environment may fail in another if contextual barriers are not addressed [1, 3, 4]. However, translating and maintaining these in practice can be complicated, involving the skillful management of influential factors inherent in healthcare, such as client complexity, technology infrastructure, policies and procedures, and inter-organizational relationships [2, 5, 6].

The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) highlights domains and constructs critical to successful implementation of an innovation [4, 7] including Innovation Adaptability, defined as the degree to which an innovation can be modified to fit local conditions without compromising its essential functions. One approach to doing so is identifying the foundational elements, or “core components,” of an innovation [8]. Before implementation can occur, it is crucial to clearly understand and operationalize what makes an innovation useful in practice [9, 10]. According to the National Implementation Research Network (NIRN) an innovation must meet four key criteria to be usable, including: (1) a clear description of the program, encompassing the underlying philosophy, values, and principles, (2) essential functions, or “core components” that define the program, (3) operational definitions of essential functions, and (4) a structured approach to assessing performance and fidelity [10].

Core components represent the fundamental, indispensable elements of an innovation, determined by considering the key aspects, active ingredients, and practical applications of an innovation [10]. They provide a framework for practitioner roles, guide program activities, and ensure the innovation is implemented as intended to achieve its desired outcomes [9, 11]. Identifying core components early in implementation is crucial, no matter the setting or context, as they provide a basis for consistent application and reliable outcomes [8, 12, 13]. Understanding these principles allows for flexibility in how the innovation is operationalized without sacrificing function. A well-defined set of core components can also enhance implementation speed and efficiency [10], promote cost-effectiveness and support decision-making on adaptations across different contexts [10, 13]. Furthermore, they provide a basis for evaluating an innovation’s effectiveness and sustainability [13].

NIRN emphasizes the importance of pre-implementation planning, including core component development, highlighting the need for deep reflection and consultation with key interest-holders to meet this criterion [8]. However, there is no existing universal approach to core component identification or development. Co-design approaches hold a great deal of promise in their applicability to developing the core components of an intervention. Though terms like “co-production” and “co-creation” are oftentimes used interchangeably alongside co-design under the broader umbrella of co-approaches, the project team adopted co-design to describe the methodological process consistently [14, 15]. Defined as a collaborative method where individuals from various backgrounds and disciplines share and impart knowledge to inform the design of a solution, co-design emphasizes participatory, real-world input to generate innovations grounded in users’ needs [14, 16]. This research approach ensures that the work amplifies and highlights the voices of all parties involved, which enhances relevance and fosters knowledge-user and interest-holder buy-in. Co-producers of evidence are typically knowledge-users and interest-holders: individuals that are actively involved in the knowledge production process of a study or affected by the research [17]. This broadens research to include not only researchers, but those who may influence, administer, or be an active user of the research, including decision-makers, people who will receive care based on findings, and healthcare providers.

This methodological guidance paper provides the detailed process to developing the core components of an innovation using co-design, with the Family Navigation Project (FNP) serving as a practical example to apply the methodology. Established in 2013 in the Greater Toronto Area, FNP helps youth and families find and gain access to mental health and addictions (MHA) supports and resources, such as service providers and treatment centres, and offering ongoing support as needed [18, 19]. For youth with MHA concerns, navigation has been shown to increase access to care, improve patient satisfaction, ease care transitions, support emotional safety and advocacy, and reduce caregiver burden [20–22]. Despite these benefits, navigation programs remain scarce and largely unavailable across many regions. Given their demonstrated impacts, there is a pressing need to make navigation models more widely accessible, supported by thoughtful, scalable implementation strategies. Defining core components is a critical first step in ensuring that programs such as FNP can be adapted to local contexts, while maintaining fidelity and effectiveness.

Drawing upon FNP as a worked example, this paper introduces the Core Component Co-Design (CCCD) process, a newly developed methodological approach involving structured co-design that engages and empowers interest-holders to identify the core components of an innovation, ensuring they are rigorous and adaptable.

Methods

The FNP CCCD followed a structured, five-phase process to ensure an evidence-based approach that integrated co-design with interest-holders: (1) Identification and extraction of preliminary component sources, (2) Knowledge integration and interest-holder input, (3) Conceptual analysis and core component identification, (4) Member checking, and (5) Finalization. Each phase is detailed below, and summarized in Table 1, which also displays the integration of specific frameworks, models, and literature that guided the CCCD process. This process took approximately eight months (September 2024 – April 2025) to complete, but timeframes for new adopters may vary depending on contextual factors, such as the number of interest-holder groups, program history, and volume of data. To ensure adherence to principles of public and patient involvement, the project team completed the Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public (GRIPP2) Reporting Checklist – Short Form. Existing co-design frameworks and techniques informed the structuring of the CCCD process phases. First, the Generative Co-Design Framework for Healthcare Innovation provided an overarching structure with its three stages: Pre-Design (understanding the problem and gathering evidence), Co-Design (collaborating and generating ideas) and Post-Design (analysis and finalization) [27]. In parallel, the five steps of the Design Thinking framework offered a complementary scaffold that aligned well with this structure: (1) Empathize and (2) Define supported knowledge generation and problem identification, (3) Ideate aligned with generating ideas through interest-holder input, (4) Prototype corresponded to the creation of the core components first draft using conceptual analysis, and (5) Test aligned with soliciting feedback via member checking and iterative refinement [28].

Although these frameworks had not previously been applied to the development of core components, they served as guiding models for the approach used. Traditional co-design literature also informed the process, ensuring adherence to key principles such as person-centredness, having a desired end state and goal, focusing on practical, real-world solutions, and incorporating diverse perspectives [31]. This literature further supported clarity around project members’ roles within the co-design process [32]. Additionally, a systematic review of co-design processes indicated that most co-designs consist of four or five phases, helping the team determine an appropriate number of phases for their own process [14].

Identification and extraction of preliminary component sources

The project team, comprising a scientist, post-doctoral fellow, research coordinator, a project manager, and two frontline staff who work at FNP, established a foundational structure for how to first compose, identify, and define core components. This phase included reviewing literature on core component processes [11] and examples from similar initiatives [12] to gather inspiration and create a vision for the intended final product. Literature on co-design processes and guiding design model frameworks (Design Thinking [28], Generative Co-Design Framework [27]) were consulted to ensure adherence to foundational co-design values and to inform the methodological structure. They also examined their own work at FNP, including published literature [23, 24], program logic models [25, 26], the FNP website, and other program artefacts (e.g., pamphlets, strategic plans, mission/vision/value statements) to gather relevant program information, and to identify guiding principles. Two front-line FNP team members (NA, SR) provided insights into the theoretical and practical aspects of FNP services. Guided by these sources, the project team collaboratively generated ideas about FNP’s core components on a virtual Mapping Board to document and organize emerging ideas. The project team also began a detailed documentation process starting at this stage, to maintain dependability and accountability. These initial steps laid the groundwork for core component development.

Knowledge generation and interest-holder input

Knowledge generation and interest-holder input integrated the perspectives of key FNP members, ensuring the core components were relevant to and had input from those directly involved in the program. This phase included in-depth consultation with every group of key FNP interest-holders, including FNP leadership, frontline staff, research staff, operations staff, and lived experience advisory groups (FNP’s Family Advisory Council (FAC) and Youth Advisory Council (YAC)). To address potential power imbalances among these groups, each completed the subsequent phases independently, rather than in mixed settings. This separation was intentional to mitigate the influence of power dynamics (e.g., leadership unintentionally shaping perspectives of staff, or staff overshadowing the voices of lived experience). By creating parallel consultation processes, each group was provided with a safe, equitable environment with peers to contribute openly.

In each consultation session, interest-holders were first educated on the concept of core components by the project team. A “cookie analogy” was used to simplify this abstract idea: just as a cookie has essential ingredients (like flour, sugar, eggs, and butter) that define what it is, a program has core components that are fundamental to its identity. While some elements like chocolate chips or nuts can be changed without altering the essence, modifying key ingredients, such as using too much flour, might result in a cake instead, ultimately changing the outcome. This analogy helped convey that while adaptations are acceptable, preserving the core components is critical to maintaining the integrity of an innovation. The project team also showed interest-holders the published core components of a similar organization to provide a contextual example [33].

Interest-holders were informed of the work completed to date, project status, and introduced to a facilitated activity to answer the question: ‘What are the core components of FNP?’ To do so, interest-holders divided into small groups and were provided with either sticky notes (if completed in-person), or access to a digital whiteboard (if completed virtually). They were asked to independently list any ideas that came to mind regarding FNP’s core components, using one sticky note per idea or each idea posted on the virtual board. Next, members of each small group shared their individual core component ideas with one another, and collectively prioritized their top five core components. Each group then shared their five core components with the whole group, followed by a general group conversation to discuss overall impressions, reflections, and points of concordance and discordance. These groups discussions created space for candid dialogue, allowing commonalities, discrepancies, and diverse perspectives to surface and further stimulate idea generation. Throughout these conversations, the project team observed and documented emerging patterns, which later influenced their analytical process. All notes were collected and translated onto the project team’s Mapping Board (a virtual whiteboard created on Canva). This process was repeated for each FNP interest-holder group (leadership, operational staff, research staff, frontline staff, and lived experience councils).

Conceptual analysis and core component identification

Using the Mapping Board, the project team conducted a conceptual analysis by visually organizing and interpreting the data collected from interest-holders. Each contribution (represented as a digital ‘sticky note’) was transcribed onto the Mapping Board. Digital sticky notes were then systematically sorted into broader concepts/themes This process involved placing similar ideas together, identifying recurring language, and clustering overlapping conceptual groupings to reveal patterns across sessions. Through collaborative and iterative discussion, the project team examined the frequency, nuance, and context of these patterns to understand the significance related to FNP. This analytic stance prioritized inclusivism (i.e., findings reflect dominant and recurring patterns) over anecdotalism (i.e., unique and selective rarities) [34]. To capture the essence of the grouped contributions, emerging clusters were labelled with preliminary core component names (see Table 2 for subset of data from Mapping Board). The Mapping Board served as a dynamic space for analysis, where the project team could iteratively refine and synthesize data in real-time, physically rearranging, merging, or splitting groups as deeper insights emerged.

Once preliminary core component names were created, each was accompanied by a clear definition that encapsulated its purpose within FNP. To create the definitions, each ‘sticky note’ was subsequently colour-coded to sub-organize the high volume of information, and distinguish what information was relevant to defining the core components, rather than describing their enactment in practice. Information was colour-coded as red to denote a ‘definition’ (essential functions that define FNP), blue to denote a ‘practical description’ (describe the core components that allow an innovation to be teachable, learnable, or doable in practice) or green to denote ‘both’ (a combination of definition and practical description). Definitions were created primarily using the notes colour-coded as a ‘definition’, with supporting information from the ‘both’ group. Practical descriptions were reserved for future stages beyond the scope of this paper (i.e., creation of Practice Profiles [11]). The initial draft went through several rounds of internal revisions within the project team, and ultimately provided a preliminary working document for further refinement.

Member checking

To validate and refine the preliminary core components, member checking was conducted [29, 30]. The project team re-engaged each group to review and validate the draft to ensure the resulting core components accurately reflected shared understandings and perspectives. Each group received a refresher on the purpose of core components, an update on progress thus far, and meeting objectives. Each of the originally consulted groups were provided with a copy of the ‘FNP core components’ draft, which outlined the preliminary core components and definitions. Depending on the format of the meeting, materials were distributed as either print (in-person sessions) or digital (virtual sessions) documents.

In alignment with ‘knowledge generation and interest-holder input’ (phase 2), each group was asked to first individually read and annotate the draft core components, focusing on three objectives: (1) identify which core components were essential, (2) identify which were not essential, and (3) identify any potentially missing components. Following individual reflection and annotation, participants formed small groups to discuss their insights and collectively rank core components. Each group was then asked to provide both positive feedback and constructive suggestions for two core components (i.e., identifying unnecessary or missing core components, or proposing revisions). One core component was assigned by the project team to ensure all components would be addressed, and one core component was selected by each group, so that they could share feedback that came to mind.

Groups shared their rankings and core component feedback in a collective discussion where open dialogue and reflection was encouraged. All materials from this session were collected by the project team, adding them to the Mapping Board for review and integration into the finalized core components document. These materials included the annotated documents, along with feedback captured during the discussion. A member of the project team took detailed notes, paying particular attention to moments when feedback from interest-holder groups was heavily debated in the larger discussion.

Finalization

The last phase, finalization, integrated feedback obtained in the member checking phase to produce a finalized set of core components. The definitions of each component were refined to ensure conceptual clarity, practical applicability, and alignment with both interest-holder perspectives and the overarching goals of FNP. The final version of FNP core components went through iterative discussions amongst the FNP project team, to ensure that the preliminary component sources identified could ultimately provide guidance to a new instance of FNP or similar navigation service, beyond the existing FNP service in Toronto. During this phase, the project team engaged in reflexive discussions that explicitly considered team members’ clinical, research, leadership, operational, and lived experience roles, as well as how the project team’s own considerations of their positionality, assumptions, biases, and associated power dynamics could influence interpretation and decision-making. Further, the analytic stance of inclusivism [34] supported the resolution of discrepancies in feedback across interest-holder groups. When revisions were required, descriptions were expanded to accommodate multiple viewpoints rather than privileging a single perspective. Decisions were made through open discussion, negotiation of differing perspectives, and collective agreement to reach consensus. These decision-making processes contributed to how definitions evolved and reached their final form. Once consensus was reached among all members of the project team, the document was circulated to each interest-holder group to solicit their final feedback on the draft and to communicate anticipated next steps.

Results

The FNP CCCD process led to the identification of eight core components that define the essential elements of FNP. The eight core components identified were: (1) Rooted in the Lived Experiences of Youth and Families, (2) Low Barrier, (3) Personalized Care Pathways, (4) Expert Guidance of the Mental Health and Addictions Systems, (5) Compassionate Persistence, (6) Championing Equity, Diversity, Inclusion, and Anti-Racism, (7) Interdisciplinary Collaboration, (8) Relationship-Based Care.

Most interest-holder groups contributed ideas that were reflected across all core components, meaning each group offered input that aligned with or fit every core component. The only area with comparatively less contribution from lived experience advisory councils was the core components of Interdisciplinary Collaboration. This core component may have been emphasized more heavily by leadership, operational, frontline, and research staff because of their closer connection to operational infrastructure. Importantly, this absence of lived experience emphasis was not interpreted as divergence as the advisory councils did not provide any information during Phase 2 that contradicted or challenged the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration, nor contested its inclusion during Phase 4.

Below, nuances behind developing each core component through the CCCD process are presented, followed by details of the definitions of each core component and their intended purpose within FNP. Italicized phrases below represent samples of exact contributions (‘sticky notes’) generated by interest-holders throughout the co-design process. Summary of core components

Rooted in the lived experiences of youth and families

Interest-holder groups played a significant role in shaping the wording of the definition, especially ‘family of choice’, emphasizing the need to explicitly recognize and respect the diverse family structures and dynamics experienced by FNP clients. They also guided language that better reflects the depth of engagement with lived experience members, intentionally using ‘relationships’ to highlight the strong integration of lived experience members that are embedded within FNP. Additionally, the phrase ‘enriched by’ was removed, noting that it unintentionally implied that lived experience is an optional enhancement rather than a foundational principle.

Rooted in the Lived Experiences of Youth and Families emphasized the importance of grounding FNP practices, policies, and service delivery in the authentic voices and insights of those directly impacted. Interest-holders consistently identified lived experience as integral to informing best practices, shaping both design and delivery of care. There was strong emphasis that lived experiences of the local community ensure that FNP remains relevant, responsive, and attuned to community-specific needs. Moreover, interest-holders highlighted the importance of youth and family engagement, advocating for approaches where individuals with lived experience are not peripheral contributors, but active partners in co-creating solutions and making decisions that guide the program. The combination of clinical staff and lived experience through the Youth Advisory Council, Family Advisory Council, and clients alongside staff, at all levels of the organization (i.e., from the level of direct client support to organizational leadership) was essential to fostering well-rounded understanding of needs and improving care pathways. By embedding lived experience engagement throughout all stages of program development and delivery, interest-holders recognized that the voice of lived experience guides decision-making and is critical to ethical, effective, and person-centred practice.

Low barrier

The wording around Low Barrier emerged from deep discussions about what truly makes FNP accessible, inclusive, responsive, and adaptable. Each term was carefully selected to reflect the concrete practices (e.g., no waitlists, minimal eligibility criteria, no cost), which ensure families can benefit from FNP without unnecessary hurdles. Interest-holders also debated the title itself, considering whether “accessibility” might better capture the intent behind the criteria, but ultimately agreed that Low Barrier best captured the range of practices and principles that allow families to engage with ease.

Interest-holders described FNP as modelling an open-door policy, which strives to not turn people away and is not time-limited. FNP provides low-barrier services through no waitlists, no [provincial health insurance plan] number or address needed, no doctor referral required, and option of phone or email, striving to provide a program that is accessible for all clients and families regardless of circumstances. Interest-holders underscored the importance of flexible criteria and the practice of ensuring that families are called back no matter what. The program was described as fast and responsive, with smooth transitions and easily accessible entry points that minimize wait times and complexity. Further, low barrier reflected an aspirational drive for accessibility at FNP that actively removes barriers to care. Interest-holders reflected that FNP is always trying to improve and expand access to mitigate barriers to care across the entire MHA care system.

Personalized care pathways

Personalized Care Pathways prompted extensive discussion about merging it with Relationship-Based Care due to the natural overlap between the two. Interest-holders ultimately agreed that the two were distinct and combining them risked losing important features unique to each. The term “holistic” was replaced with “all aspects of wellness”, to better capture the comprehensive scope of support. To preserve the uniqueness of this component, interest-holders language such as “individual” and “unique”, which reinforced the personalized nature of FNP.

Personalized Care Pathways reflected interest-holders’ strong emphasis on individualized and customized navigation approaches that are youth- and family-centred and responsive to changing needs, successes, and hurdles to accessing MHA services. Interest-holders emphasized that adopting a whole-person approach and strengths-based mindset is crucial to recognizing each youth and/or family’s unique context, goals, and readiness. They highlighted the importance of adjusting supports aligned with the biopsychosocial model. In doing so, it ensures that personalized navigation plans are carefully curated and matched to individual needs and focused on finding the right services. Such an approach moves beyond a one-size-fits-all design, instead promoting flexibility, adaptability, and empowerment throughout the navigation journey. Interest-holders described non-judgmental interactions, ranging from intake and needs assessment through to ongoing follow-up, as essential to personalized care. These interactions honour the client’s voice and choice, and move at the pace of the client’s needs. Recommendations and supports were most effective when they respect the narrative of each family, are client-driven, and actively promote collaboration and validation.

Expert guidance of mental health and addictions systems

The wording of Expert Guidance of Mental Health and Addictions Systems originally felt too technologically focused, prompting the need to refine and rebalance the language. Interest-holders emphasized that this core component must clearly reflect the role of advocacy for clients, not just navigation expertise. They also highlighted the importance of clarifying the phrase “real-time systems awareness”, which was revised to “real-time awareness of the mental health and addictions systems” to convey this concept.

Expert Guidance of Mental Health and Addictions Systems reflected interest-holders’ recognition of the importance of solutions-focused and coordinated care to help youth and families navigate the complexities of MHA systems. Interest-holders emphasized the critical role of FNP in providing informed and guided care to youth and families by drawing upon evidence-based information, staff’s comprehensive knowledge, and input from specialists. Such guidance was essential in overcoming barriers to access and connecting families with appropriate resources that consider cultural, language, and cost-related needs while also being provided in a trauma-informed, private, and confidential manner. Interest-holders emphasized the importance of advocacy to ensure that all youth and families receive considerate support, acknowledging and appreciating systemic barriers that often hinder equitable access to quality care.

Interest-holders highlighted that the provision of education and resources was a key strategy to empower families, creating optimal care pathways that navigate within and across systems. This core component also illuminated the importance of supporting communication between systems, through engagement with other healthcare providers. Interest-holders noted that FNP is connected with other organizations in the community and is familiar with supports in the area, which fosters awareness of ideal sequencing of care.

Compassionate persistence

Interest-holders felt the initial phrasing, “unwavering and continuous commitment” was too intense, and was revised to “demonstrated perseverance”, which they saw as more realistic, and aligned with how they envisioned Compassionate Persistence. They also emphasized conveying longevity, in that families can stay engaged for as long as they need, and re-engage at any time, reflecting the flexible and caring approach that defines FNP.

Compassionate Persistence captured interest-holders’ emphasis on the importance of sustained, empathetic engagement with youth and families throughout their care journey. They described the need for long-term, ongoing support that prioritizes continuity of care and extends beyond initial contact, demonstrating commitment. Interest-holders underscored the value of follow-up and longitudinal approaches, wherein navigators remain supportive and dedicated to finding the best match for each youth and/or family’s unique needs. Modeled by a commitment to never give up, FNP engages in multiple attempts to engage with families, providing care even if the youth is not ready to engage in supports, and offers revisits as needed. Navigators create a welcoming environment grounded in kindness, consistently going above and beyond for their clients. Compassionate Persistence embodies a steady and reliable presence that offers hope, kindness, reassurance, and continuous engagement along the way.

Championing Equity, Diversity, Inclusion, and Anti-Racism

Championing Equity, Diversity, Inclusion, and Anti-Racism was originally titled “Commitment to Equity”, but interest-holders immediately felt this wording was too light and ambiguous. Through deep discussion, they emphasized the need for a stronger title that was more assertive and conveyed a clear sense of responsibility and leadership. They also noted that the initial definition felt passive. The revised version is intentionally action-oriented, emphasizing the need to actively foster inclusivity, address systemic barriers, and embed equity, diversity, and inclusion into all aspects of FNP.

Championing Equity, Diversity, Inclusion, and Anti-Racism reflected participants’ shared aspiration to create environments that are not only inclusive but also actively embrace and celebrate diversity. Central to this core component was the emphasis on fostering trust and safety, ensuring that individuals from all backgrounds feel welcomed and respected. Interest-holders underscored the importance of allyship, cultural humility, and maintaining unbiased perspectives and attitudes, recognizing these are foundational to cultivating spaces that support diverse identities and experiences. Expressions of empathy, understanding, and a commitment to curiosity signaled a broader recognition that equity and inclusion require ongoing reflection and learning.

Interest-holders specified that continued education and support around race, inclusion, and diversity, supported by formal training and applying what is learned are integral to this core component. Discussions also highlighted the critical role of anti-racist and anti-oppressive learning and practice in dismantling systemic barriers and addressing MHA stigma, with a call for both individual and organizational accountability. Further, interest-holders emphasized the need for ethical and professional community engagement, ensuring that staff remain responsive and engaged with communities in ways that are welcoming, respectful of diversity and inclusive. Staff can respect diversity by recognizing each client’s unique story and continue to hire and support staff that have a strong desire to make a positive impact on the lives of others. Collectively, these insights illustrate a deep-rooted commitment to advancing principles of equity, diversity, inclusion and anti-racist approaches as active, everyday practices embedded within workplace culture, daily interactions, and community engagement.

Interdisciplinary collaboration

Interdisciplinary Collaboration was originally titled “Multidisciplinary Collaboration” but was shifted to “interdisciplinary”. Interest-holders felt “multidisciplinary” referred to people from different disciplinary backgrounds working in parallel, rather than together, and therefore did not accurately reflect the true integration of disciplines and depth of connections within FNP. Interest-holders also felt the original definition focused too heavily on disciplines within FNP staff and overlooked external partners. They stressed that meaningful collaboration beyond organizational boundaries was a core strength of the interdisciplinary approach that contributed knowledge and resources integral to FNP.

Interdisciplinary Collaboration highlighted interest-holders’ value of bringing together diverse perspectives to strengthen FNP program delivery and enhance outcomes. Central to Interdisciplinary Collaboration was the importance of an interdisciplinary team combining clinical and lived experience, with partnership and teamwork contributing to a holistic understanding of client needs. Accordingly, interest-holders envisioned interprofessional approaches, where different types of professionals engage as valued partners, not tied to any specific organization but united in a shared purpose. This core component was described as dynamic, involving multilevel, layered support that welcomes open communication, information sharing, and ongoing learning. Ideas emerged around a collective mind, highlighting the importance of creative, out-of-the-box problem solving. Interest-holders emphasized that effective interdisciplinary collaboration requires environments to be (aptly put) collaborative, in that professional, clinical, and experiential contributions are all integral to best practices and responsive care.

Relationship-based care

Relationship-Based Care was, as previously noted, debated as a standalone component because of its overlap with Personalized Care Pathways, and some interest-holders felt it could be embedded within that core component. Ultimately, it remained distinct because of the central role of relationship-building to FNP, both within the team and with external partners. Interest-holders noted that the original definition underplayed the importance of external relationships, and suggested edits to emphasize that rapport and strong collaboration with community service providers are critical to FNP’s success.

Relationship-Based Care is an approach that places relationships with community members, internal FNP members, youth and families, and service providers at the heart of the program. Interest-holders consistently underscored that FNP must foster meaningful and trusted relationships with clients and service providers to ensure the program is responsive to the needs of youth and families. A relationship-based model was one that provides wraparound support, engaging whole care and caregiver systems. Interest-holders also emphasized the value of collaborations with community and families, ensuring care remains youth- and family-centred, as well as community-informed and involved. Community outreach with local partners was described by interest-holders as essential, promoting team-based ideals not only among professionals, but also across community networks, reinforcing the value of internal and external connections.

Discussion

Co-design alongside key interest-holders with diverse perspectives led to the development of the CCCD process, a collaborative and value-driven approach to guide the identification of the core components, exemplified by FNP. Through conceptual analysis, eight distinct yet interconnected core components were identified that form the foundation of FNP: (1) Rooted in the Lived Experience of Youth and Families, (2) Low Barrier, (3) Personalized Care Pathways, (4) Expert Guidance of the Mental Health and Addictions Systems, (5) Compassionate Persistence, (6) Championing Equity, Diversity, Inclusion and Anti-Racism, (7) Interdisciplinary Collaboration, and (8) Relationship-Based Care. Collectively, these core components represent the essence of FNP that is deeply person-centred, equitable, and attuned to the complexities of working within and across fragmented MHA care systems. Importantly, the core components do not exist in isolation; to some degree, certain characteristics are embedded across multiple core components, reinforcing that FNP is the result of deeply interconnected and holistic principles. Additionally, no component is valued more greatly than another; all work in unison to portray FNP authentically.

The CCCD process reflects the combination of voices across clinical experts, organizational leadership, operational staff, research staff, and individuals with lived experience, highlighting the value of co-design and meaningful engagement in developing an organization’s core components. Importantly, the CCCD process operationalizes the balance between fidelity and adaptation through structured, iterative feedback loops with interest-holders, which serve to validate and refine the innovation. This approach aligns with best practices in implementation science, which stress iterative feedback and collaborative development with co-producers of evidence to refine and strengthen innovations [14].

Collectively, the CCCD process reflects key implementation principles from NIRN and CFIR. NIRN emphasizes that the early stages of implementation require explicit identification of an innovation’s core components [10, 11]. Establishing these core components is foundational to effective implementation and serves to prevent program shifts by clarifying what elements are non-negotiable across settings. CFIR emphasizes the importance of understanding how and where adaptation is appropriate in implementation [7]. Core components are conceptualized as the anchoring elements of an innovation, distinguishing essential functions from the adaptable periphery that may be modified in response to local context. Together, these frameworks inform how core components can be identified and preserved while allowing for context-specific adaptation.

Furthermore, a systematic review of barriers and facilitators in healthcare innovation implementation [35] identified key elements that support the CCCD process: (1) research and information sharing and (2) intentional implementation planning. The review emphasizes that gathering evidence should begin early in the implementation process, and that successful implementation relies on intentional and well-articulated implementation plans [35]. This reinforces the idea that a foundational process, like defining an innovation’s core components, forms an effective and advantageous strategy for early implementation preparations.

Interest-holder engagement was fundamental to refining the core components of an innovation and ensuring effectiveness in addressing youth MHA challenges. Interest-holders provide diverse perspectives, identify potential barriers and concerns, and contribute to a collaborative approach in developing meaningful solutions. In the co-creation of core components, a variety of interest-holders were included that would be impacted by the research findings and direct experience [17], including formal leadership, operations staff, research staff, frontline staff, family advisory council and youth advisory council.

Organizational leadership representatives are essential to include in core component development, as they contribute essential strategic perspectives of an innovation [9]. In this case, FNP’s leadership team provided feedback that aligned core components with organizational values and culture. Further, innovation staff offer irreplaceable insights into the daily nuances and real-world experiences that occur [9], especially when scaling up an already-existing program. During the CCCD process, frontline staff grounded their feedback in practical realities, offering critical insight into barriers, unintended consequences, client priorities, and systemic concerns.

By contributing to the co-design phase and not just validating outcomes, interest-holders helped co-create components that are context-sensitive, culturally relevant, and more likely to achieve buy-in [9]. Youth and family advisory councils, with their lived and living experience of MHA challenges, were another integral group. Their insights brought authenticity, challenged traditional power dynamics, and amplified community voices often underrepresented in service design, which is a well-documented gap in existing research [36, 37]. Their engagement ensured the core components reflected real experiences with FNP, enriching the model with perspectives that cannot be replicated or replaced by other interest-holder roles. Co-design with those directly impacted can shape the foundational integrity of an innovation. Co-design advances methodological practice by embedding experience-informed processes into core component identification. Without a commitment to co-design, core components would otherwise be developed by expert or professional consensus without embedding the expertise of those with lived experience. These principles contribute to the limited literature on core components, emphasizing the importance of those with lived experience as meaningful collaborators.

A key element of this methodological approach was member checking, traditionally recommended as a means to enhance rigour in qualitative research. Member checking is a way to ensure accuracy by allowing participants to correct errors, validate transcription, and eliminate unwanted information [29, 38, 39]. While historical practice of member checking asked participants to validate transcripts from qualitative interviews, formats have expanded to validate analyzed results, which allow members to provide input before the final product is completed. This ensures the intended message is conveyed accurately and seeks true input to strengthen the findings, rather than merely correcting surface-level transcription errors [30]. Validating findings with respondents reinforces the power of collaboration by transforming this aspect of research design into meaningful, collaborative work that amplifies the voices of participants [39]. This version of member checking asked interest-holders to engage with and provide feedback on the preliminary core component draft that was produced following conceptual analysis. This process strengthened the final core components by incorporating high-quality feedback, enhancing the trustworthiness of the findings, and increasing the authors’ confidence in communicating the findings following validation by interest-holders.

When the CCCD process unfolded, it benefitted greatly from the strong relationships that already existed within interest-holder groups, and as a result encountered very little need for conflict resolution. However, this may not always be the case in every organizational context. When conflicting information emerges or major disagreements occur, it becomes essential to return to the core principles of co-design [40]. These include intentionally identifying a diverse group of co-design members to ensure broad representation and perspectives, while remaining mindful of membership choices can introduce risk, harm, or power differentials for certain populations [41, 42]. From the outset, dedicating time to building trust, developing relationships, and creating safe spaces is imperative [43]. Clearly articulating key objectives of the co-design process, along with establishing a collaboratively developed working agreement, are important strategies for ensuring smooth collaboration. These practices help set shared expectations early on and can reduce the likelihood of conflict as work progresses.

The core components of FNP serve as the building blocks of a navigation program, preserving the integrity of the original program while creating a blueprint for an adaptable, scalable, and sustainable program across diverse settings. These principles are foundational in implementation science and are supported by frameworks such as the CFIR, which emphasizes adaptability and sustainability as key outcomes to consider before implementation [7, 44]. Equally important to scalability and sustainability, program context is a key consideration in any implementation. This CCCD process took place in a metropolitan area within a program that includes employees and lived experience advocates with diverse educational backgrounds, cultures, ages, and gender identities. These contextual conditions shaped how co-design occurred and will inevitably differ from those of other organizations, where factors such as organizational setting, political environment, employee composition, population demographics, and the nature of community participation may lead to different processes. Context is therefore inherent to implementation and must always be acknowledged and intentionally addressed.

At the same time, programs adopting this approach must remain attentive to collaborator capacity, compensation, and time. Meaningful engagement requires thoughtful planning to avoid overburdening partners, especially those contributing lived experience. The CCCD process illustrates balancing collaboration with resource limitations: although the project team carried most of the analytic workload due to its time demands, they intentionally created opportunities for collaboration that ensured all interest-holder perspectives shaped interpretation without exceeding anyone’s time and capacity. This example demonstrates how programs can adapt processes and uphold collaborative principles while remaining realistic about resource constraints.

Core components clarify what must be preserved in the implementation of an innovation, while also distinguishing the adaptable periphery that can be modified to suit local context, as highlighted by CFIR’s Innovation Adaptability construct. Successful implementation has the potential to influence entire systems, improving care delivery and patient outcomes. Clear articulation of core components provides a structured foundation that supports adoption, particularly when local resources, leadership, and community engagement are aligned [45]. To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first paper to publish core component development principles using an innovative co-design methodology, offering a foundation for other organizations to adopt collaborative practices.

Strengths and limitations

Structured methodology and rigorous engagement of interest-holders across multiple phases was a strength of the CCCD process. Member checking enhanced credibility and rigour of findings, while creating additional opportunities for authentic collaboration [30]. Another strength of the model is the person-centred approach, which recognizes the complexity of MHA needs, avoiding one-size-fits-all solutions in favour of individualized care pathways. The co-design process fostered creativity and innovation, sparking new ideas through open, inclusive dialogue [46]. Including family and youth advisory committees added richness to the core components by grounding content in lived experience [46]. Further, the intentional inclusion of those most connected to the work, who are passionate, engaged, and caring about the innovation at hand was deeply meaningful. Involving individuals deeply invested in FNP ensured that those with firsthand knowledge had a meaningful voice in the process, strengthening the quality and depth of the work. This also likely benefitted the process as everyone was on the same page, and therefore had minimal conflicts to resolve between interest-holders.

Throughout the CCCD process, the project team upheld qualitative rigour and trustworthiness by integrating multiple strategies across all phases of this work [47]. Confirmability was strengthened through member checking and peer debriefing (engaging with colleagues to review interpretations and findings), to iteratively refine the core components, examine assumptions, subjectivity, and interpretive decisions that support transparency [47]. Credibility was reinforced through triangulation, by combining existing evidence with interest-holder contributions (e.g., sticky notes, discussion, annotated materials) and through prolonged engagement to deeply understand and accurately represent diverse perspectives [47]. Dependability was exemplified by maintaining a detailed audit trail that documented analytic decisions, draft iterations, and feedback. Finally, transferability was supported through sampling strategies designed to capture a wide range of perspectives, enhancing relevance and applicability to the findings [34, 47].

However, the process was not without its challenges. Gathering input from multiple interest-holder groups across various sessions introduced complexities into synthesizing feedback. Divergent opinions sometimes made it difficult to reconcile input in a way that honoured all perspectives. Iterative and collaborative conceptual analyses allowed the project team to revise the core components to include as much feedback as possible. Instructions provided to participants also needed refining. For instance, unclear guidance/instructions regarding the member checking process to the first interest-holder group led to off-target feedback and general confusion. At first, staff were focused on editing wording, rather than focusing on whether the description and content of each core component was accurately captured. This confusion prompted the project team to adjust instructions mid-session and then refine the process for clarity in preparation for subsequent group member checking sessions.

Another important limitation to any co-design project is understanding how power dynamics can influence input [48]. Although interest-holder groups were intentionally separated to minimize the impact of power imbalances and create safe spaces, these dynamics cannot be avoided entirely. Therefore, it was important to facilitate inclusive discussions that respected all voices, regardless of communication style. On balance, there may have also been a missed opportunity for different depth of discussion, generative ideas, and negotiations had interest-holder groups been mixed, introducing a tradeoff between collaborative processes and the management of power dynamics. Future studies would benefit from creating opportunities for mutual agreements that promote open and non-judgmental interactions.

And while a strength of this work was the existing familiarity and depth of knowledge that all members had to FNP, the project team acknowledges that their close relationship to FNP may inherently introduce bias, as they were the primary members responsible for analysis and subsequent organization of core component themes. Moreover, although interest-holders were invited to provide final comments and approval, the project team were the primary members involved in the ‘Finalization’ phase. The content of the final document was grounded in what interest-holders had previously communicated, and they were given a final opportunity to review and confirm that the core components accurately reflected their perspectives. As such, their input meaningfully informed the final product, but future adopters may wish to integrate all team members more directly (e.g., via participatory action research) into this and all phases to further strengthen collaboration.

Finally, co-design may benefit some participants more than others. For example, someone who is not as comfortable speaking in large settings might not share their ideas compared to someone who prefers public speaking, and requires thoughtful facilitation to ensure equitable methods of participation. Each interest-holder group will bring different perspectives and representation on the topic, shaped by different experiences and interests [48]. Mitigating strategies included opportunities for independent reflection and annotation in addition to group discussions. However, future work may consider building upon the CCCD approaches described here by offering opportunities for both synchronous and asynchronous feedback and participation.

Future directions

Future work will follow the NIRN framework’s trajectory by organizing and integrating these co-designed core components into comprehensive practice profiles [11]. These profiles will serve as structured, actionable guides that define key functions, activities, and competencies required for effective implementation of the FNP model. By articulating how core components are operationalized in practice, the profiles will support consistency, fidelity, and adaptability in various settings. The CCCD process described here can be adapted by programs in the preparatory stages of implementation to guide the development of such profiles.

While the CCCD process benefitted from existing resources, such as pre-established advisory councils, research capacity, and technological infrastructure, it is not dependent on such resources and can be adjusted to fit the organization’s capacity. For instance, input from lived experience does not need to come solely from advisory councils, and can be included through active contributions from clients or individuals familiar with the organization instead.

Ultimately, the CCCD process will enable the implementation team to refine supports, training, and performance assessment strategies in alignment with the evolving needs of their communities and service providers. Once finalized, these implementation supports will lay the groundwork for scaling and adapting the model to other settings and contexts, promoting broader system integration and equitable access to navigation services.

Conclusions

Implementation is deeply influenced by context, comprising an interplay of diverse factors such as available resources, organizational culture and infrastructure, interest-holder support, and sociopolitical climate that can help or hinder success. For that reason, innovation structures must be stable to preserve program integrity across changing environments. Accordingly, the core components of FNP capture its essence, providing a solid foundation for implementation for future scale and spread efforts. This collaborative and context-sensitive approach offers a replicable model for other navigation programs seeking to expand their reach while maintaining fidelity and integrity.
