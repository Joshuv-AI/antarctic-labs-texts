Jordan homomorphisms of triangular algebras over noncommutative algebras
Oksana Bezushchak

Abstract
. D. Benkovič described Jordan homomorphisms of algebras of triangular matrices over a commutative unital ring without additive 2 2 -torsion. We extend this result to the case of noncommutative rings and remove the assumption of additive torsion. Let R R be an associative unital algebra over a commutative unital ring Φ \Phi . Consider the algebra T n ​ ( R ) T_{n}(R) of triangular n × n n\times n matrices over R R , and its subalgebra T n 0 ​ ( R ) T_{n}^{0}(R) consisting of matrices whose main diagonal entries lie in Φ \Phi . We prove that for any Jordan homomorphism of T n ​ ( R ) T_{n}(R) , its restriction to T n 0 ​ ( R ) T_{n}^{0}(R) is standard.

1.
Introduction

Let
A
and
B
be associative (not necessarily commutative) algebras over an associative commutative ring. An additive map
\varphi:A\to B
is called a
Jordan homomorphism
if it satisfies the following identities:

(i)

\varphi(x^{2})=\varphi(x)^{2}
for all
x\in A
;

(ii)

\varphi(xyx)=\varphi(x)\varphi(y)\varphi(x)
for all
x,y\in A
.

If the algebra
B
does not have additive
2
-torsion, then the identity (ii) follows from (i). For elements
x,y\in A,
denote
x\circ y=xy+yx.
The assumption (i) implies

\varphi(x\circ y)=\varphi(x)\circ\varphi(y).

Let us recall that an additive map
\psi:A\to B
is called an
antihomomorphism
if it satisfies the identity:
\psi(ab)=\psi(b)\psi(a)
for any elements
a,b\in A.

Every algebra homomorphism or antihomomorphism is a Jordan homomorphism, but not vice versa.

A deep and well-studied topic in ring theory and Jordan structure theory concerns the natural conditions under which Jordan homomorphisms can be represented as combinations of homomorphisms and antihomomorphisms of associative algebras. L.K. Hua
[
12
]
showed that a Jordan automorphism of a division ring is an automorphism or an antiautomorphism. I. Herstein
[
11
]
proved that a Jordan homomorphism onto a prime ring without additive
2
-torsion is a homomorphism or an antihomomorphism. M. Brešar
[
5
,
6
]
extended it to the description of Jordan homomorphisms onto semiprime rings. On the other hand, W.S. Martindale
[
14
]
and N. Jacobson
[
9
]
(see also K. McCrimmon
[
15
]
) described all Jordan homomorphisms of rings with at least three pairwise orthogonal full idempotents. See
[
7
]
for further recent results on Jordan homomorphisms.

A bit more precisely: let
A^{\text{op}}
be the opposite algebra of an algebra
A
; that is,
A
and
A^{\text{op}}
have the same additive structure, but multiplication in

is defined by

for all

for all

, is a Jordan homomorphism. We denote by

the subalgebra of

, generated by all elements of the form

, where
a\in A
.

A Jordan homomorphism

In this paper, we explore Jordan homomorphisms of triangular algebras over noncommutative algebras.

Let
\Phi
be an associative commutative ring with

\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&R\end{pmatrix}.

Consider the ideal

S_{n}(R)=\begin{pmatrix}0&R&\cdots&R\\

\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\

\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\

Theorem 1
.

Let
R
be an arbitrary associative
\Phi
-algebra with identity, and let

Suppose that
\varphi:T_{n}(R)\to B

\varphi

Theorem
1
establishes universality of the Jordan homomorphism

T_{n}^{0}(R)\longrightarrow S\langle T_{n}(R)\oplus T_{n}(R)^{\text{op}}\rangle,

but it leaves open the question about the structure of

In the next theorem, we prove the decomposition

S\langle T_{n}^{0}(R)\oplus T_{n}^{0}(R)^{\text{op}}\rangle\cong\operatorname{Diag}(\Phi)\ltimes(S_{n}(R)\oplus S_{n}(R)^{\text{op}}),

which leads to a description of Jordan homomorphisms.

Theorem 2
.

Let
{\varphi:T_{n}(R)\to B}
be a Jordan homomorphism. Then there exist a unique homomorphism
\psi_{1}:T_{n}^{0}(R)\to B
and a unique antihomomorphism

(i)

\varphi(a)=\psi_{1}(a)=\psi_{2}(a)

(ii)

\psi_{1}(a)\psi_{2}(b)=\psi_{2}(b)\psi_{1}(a)=0

(iii)

\varphi(a)=\psi_{1}(a)+\psi_{2}(a)
for all

The homomorphism

Let

The elements

The

Lemma 1
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta
, with
\alpha+\beta\not\in\Delta\cup(0)

u(A_{\alpha})u(A_{\beta})=(0).

Proof.

Let
x,y\in A
and suppose
xy=yx=0.

Then
u(x)\circ u(y)=u(x\circ y)=0.
Hence,
u(x)u(y)=-u(y)u(x).

Futhermore, for any element

we have

u(xya+ayx)=u(x)u(y)u(a)+u(a)u(y)u(x)=u(x)u(y)u(a)-u(a)u(x)u(y)=0.

Therefore, the element
u(x)u(y)
commutes with the element

Since

generates the algebra
U(A)

lies in the center of

For any element

\text{ad}(e):x\to[e,x],

Since
\text{ad}(e)^{2}(x)=[e,[e,x]]=e^{2}x+xe^{2}-2exe,
it follows that

\text{ad}(u(e))^{2}(u(x))=u(\text{ad}(e)^{2}(x)).

Let
\alpha=\omega_{i}-\omega_{j},

\beta=\omega_{i}-\omega_{k},

Let

Hence,

\operatorname{ad}(u(e_{j}))^{2}\big(u(a)\big)=u(a),\quad\operatorname{ad}(u(e_{j}))^{2}\big(u(b)\big)=0.

This yields:

\operatorname{ad}(u(e_{j}))^{2}\big(u(a)u(b)\big)=\Big(\operatorname{ad}(u(e_{j}))^{2}\big(u(a)\big)\Big)u(b)+\\

The case

A^{0}\oplus(A^{0})^{\text{op}}

generated by elements
a+a^{\text{op}}
,
a\in A^{0}
.

For
1\leq i<j\leq n
and
r\in R
, let
e_{ij}(r)
denote the matrix
having
r
in the
(i,j)
-entry and zeros elsewhere. Then

(e_{i}+e_{i}^{\text{op}})(e_{ij}(r)+e_{ij}(r)^{\text{op}})=e_{ij}(r).

Hence

e_{ij}(r)\in S\langle A^{0}\oplus(A^{0})^{\text{op}}\rangle,\qquad S_{n}(R)\subseteq S\langle A^{0}\oplus(A^{0})^{\text{op}}\rangle.

Similarly,

S_{n}(R)^{\text{op}}\subseteq S\langle A^{0}\oplus(A^{0})^{\text{op}}\rangle.

Let
\varphi:A\to B
be a Jordan homomorphism.
By Theorems
1
, there exists a homomorphism

\chi:\{\,a+a^{\text{op}}\mid a\in\operatorname{Diag}(\Phi)\,\}+S_{n}(R)+S_{n}(R)^{\text{op}}\;\longrightarrow B

such that
\varphi(a)=\chi(a+a^{\text{op}})
for all
a\in A^{0}
. The subalgebra

(2)

\{\,a+a^{\text{op}}\mid a\in\operatorname{Diag}(\Phi)\,\}+S_{n}(R)

is isomorphic to
S_{n}(R)
, while the subalgebra

(3)

\{\,a+a^{\text{op}}\mid a\in\operatorname{Diag}(\Phi)\,\}+S_{n}(R)^{\text{op}}

is isomorphic to
S_{n}(R)^{\text{op}}
. Let
\psi_{1}
be the restriction of
\chi
to subalgebra (
2
), and let
\psi_{2}
be the restriction of
\chi
to subalgebra (
3
). The homomorphism
\psi_{2}
may be regarded as an antihomomorphism from
A^{0}
to
B
. Then

\varphi(a)=\psi_{1}(a+a^{\text{op}})=\psi_{2}(a+a^{\text{op}}),\quad\text{for all }a\in\mathrm{Diag}(\Phi);

\varphi(a)=\psi_{1}(a)+\psi_{2}(a),\quad\text{for all }a\in S_{n}(R);

\psi_{1}(a)\psi_{2}(b)=\psi_{2}(b)\psi_{1}(a)=0,\quad\text{for all }a,b\in S_{n}(R).

It remains to prove the uniqueness of a homomorphism
\psi_{1}
and an antihomomorphism
\psi_{2}
. The pair
(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})
gives rise to a homomorphism
\psi:S\langle A^{0}\oplus(A^{0})^{\text{op}}\rangle\to B
such that the corresponding diagram

\textstyle{A^{0}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{S\langle A^{0}\oplus(A^{0})^{\text{op}}\rangle\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\psi}
\textstyle{B}

is commutative. Since the algebra
S\langle A^{0}\oplus(A^{0})^{\text{op}}\rangle
is generated by elements
a+a^{\text{op}},

a\in A^{0},
and
\psi(a+a^{\text{op}})=\varphi(a),
the homomorphism
\psi
is unique. This implies the uniqueness of
\psi_{1}^{\prime}
and
\psi_{2}^{\prime}
, and completes the proof of Theorem
2
. ∎
