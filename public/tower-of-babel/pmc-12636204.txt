A mixed-methods study on awareness of sexual and reproductive rights among women in Chandigarh, India
Authors: Zawata Afnan, Madhu Gupta, Kapil Goel, Yukti Bhandari, Immanuel Joshua
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSexual and reproductive rights (SRRs) include the ability to make informed choices about family planning and reproductive health, free from discrimination, coercion, or violence. This study aimed to assess awareness and identify factors influencing perceptions of reproductive rights among women aged 15–35 years in Chandigarh.MethodsA concurrent mixed-method study was conducted among 116 women in Chandigarh using a pretested, semi-structured tool for quantitative analysis. A grounded theory approach was used to explore perceptions among 37 women through six focus group discussions (FGD), guided by a pretested FGD guide. Quantitative data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Trial Version 26.0, while thematic analysis of qualitative data was conducted manually. Data integration was performed at the interpretation stage.ResultsUrban residents had significantly higher awareness of SRRs and legal abortion rights [Adjusted odds ratio (AOR) = 5.2, 95% CI = 1.5–17.5; AOR = 3.5, 95% CI = 1.28–9.55]. Unmarried women were more aware of abortion rights (AOR = 4.68, 95% CI = 1.83–11.06). Higher education was associated with greater awareness of family planning (AOR = 16.06, 95% CI = 2.8–91.6). Individual factors included differing priorities, hesitancy to discuss, and fear of abortion and contraceptive side effects. Family-related factors involved decision-making roles (in-laws and husbands). Societal factors included low autonomy and religious beliefs. The stigma surrounding reproductive health and sex education hindered women from exercising their rights.ConclusionSignificant gaps exist in SRR awareness, particularly among married, less educated, and slum-dwelling women. Family control, religious beliefs, and stigma hinder reproductive autonomy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40834-025-00398-9.

Introduction

In 1994, the concept of reproductive health received due attention. As a signatory to the International Conference on Population and Development, India has committed itself to ethical and professional standards in family planning services, including the right to personal reproductive autonomy and collective gender equality [1]. Reproductive rights were established as a subset of human rights. Couples have a basic human right to determine freely and responsibly the number and the spacing of their children. Reproductive rights are vigorously contested, regardless of the population’s socioeconomic level, religion, or culture [2]. This aligns with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 3 and 5, which aim to ensure health and well-being for all, and to achieve gender equality and empower all women and girls, respectively. Realizing sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHRs) is a critical component in achieving these global targets, as it directly contributes to reducing maternal mortality, promoting universal access to reproductive healthcare, and ending harmful practices against women and girls [3]. Due to uneven access to information, care, and basic health services, early marriage age (mean age 17.2 years), deep-seated health beliefs, prevailing social and cultural structures, low literacy rate (42.0%), unmet need for family planning (24.6%), unsafe abortion and births attended by untrained personnel (80.0%) all contribute to unsatisfactory quality of life of females in economically poor countries [4]. Reproductive Rights embrace certain human rights that are already recognized in national laws and international laws, as well as in international human rights and other consensus documents [5]. The rights rest on the recognition of the basic rights of all couples and individuals to decide freely and responsibly the number, spacing, and timing of their children and to have the information and means to do so. It also entails the right to attain the highest standard of sexual and reproductive health, and the right to make decisions concerning reproduction free of discrimination, coercion, and violence, as expressed in human rights documents [6]. Availing reproductive rights can help prevent maternal fatalities and improve women’s status in society [4].

Women of reproductive age (15–49 years) are commonly exposed to numerous harmful practices and violations, including forced marriage, widowhood, female genital mutilation, trafficking, and sexual abuse. Considering this, everyone above the age of 15 years has the right to have reproductive and sexual health of the highest standard. Lack of knowledge of Sexual and Reproductive Rights (SRRs) may lead to a negative perception of and failure to exercise SRRs among women. It has the potential to disempower women, creating a potential barrier to claiming these rights. Even if there is awareness of legitimate rights, contextual social, cultural, and structural factors (e.g. patriarchal family setting, religious beliefs, family dysfunctionality, poverty, location, etc.) may contribute to women not realizing their rights [7].

Millions of women in India and globally lack access to comprehensive reproductive health services, despite a strong need and desire for them. There is a need for women to understand the critical role these SSRs can play in their lives and in achieving their desires. Which will ultimately empower them to make a case for themselves [8].

In India, very few studies have been conducted to assess knowledge of SSRs, and no study has been conducted in Chandigarh to date. The existing studies related to SSRs knowledge assessment include mostly married women and college students, and none have included the teenage population. Therefore, this study was planned to find out the awareness and perception of SSRs among women in Chandigarh Union Territory.

Methodology

We conducted a concurrent mixed-methods study incorporating both quantitative and qualitative components. It followed a partially mixed design, with greater emphasis placed on the qualitative component. The study was conducted in Chandigarh, the health and wellness center (HWC), sector 49, i.e., the field practice area of the Department of Community Medicine and School of Public Health, PGIMER from June 2023 to October 2023. Women who were severely ill and/or had compromised mental health were excluded. A written informed consent was obtained from all participants. We had obtained assent from the minors and consent from their parents/guardian.

Z = 1.96 for a confidence level (α) of 95%, proportion of awareness of sexual and reproductive rights taken at 52% [10] error = 10%, and non-response rate of 20%. A systematic random sampling technique was used to include 116 study participants by house-to-house visits. A list of all the women of age group 15–35 years of age was obtained from the auxiliary nurse midwives posted in Ayushman Bharat Health and Wellness Centre, Sector-49 (HWC-49). Skip interval (k) was calculated and then the first person was recruited randomly using the kth value (i.e. 73) as skip interval, members of the population at a regular interval were selected until the desired population size was achieved.

Qualitative assessment

Using the grounded theory approach, the awareness and factors influencing the perceptions of reproductive rights among the women of the age group between 15 and 35 years were explored. A total of 37 participants were purposively recruited for the Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) maintaining cultural heterogeneity. Women were divided into 3 age groups i.e. 15 to 19 years (selected from Government Model High School, Sector-49D), 20 to 25 years, and 26 to 35 years (selected from economically weaker society and urban society in Sector-49, Chandigarh). A minimum of two FGDs in each of these groups (total 6 FGDs) were conducted. FGDs were conducted among 6–7 homogenous women in each age group. The recordings from the FGDs were transcribed and then translated. Subsequently from the verbatims, memos were developed, codes were generated, and sub-themes and major themes were identified through a systematic analysis of the transcripts.

Data collection and analysis

Quantitative assessment

Data were collected using a self-designed and semi-structured questionnaire in English and Hindi language which included socio-demographic details and awareness regarding awareness of SSRs and its varied dimensions. Awareness was ‘self-reported awareness’ by the participants. Hence, the difference in those “aware” versus “not aware” was based on participants’ responses to questions as ‘yes/no’. Knowledge, however, was not assessed using a specific scoring system. Face and content validity of the questionnaire was assessed by four experts including two public health, specialists, a obstetrician and gynaecologist and a paediatrician. Socioeconomic status (SES) was assessed using the modified BG Prasad classification [10]. However, as the number of participants were less, so we have recategorized the SES in three classes (upper, middle and lower). Data analysed using Microsoft Excel (version 2021) and IBM Statistical Package for Social Sciences trial version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Results were expressed in frequency and percentages for categorical data, and mean and standard deviation for continuous variables. Pearson chi-square test was applied to test the level of significance for categorical variables. Logistics regression was applied to calculate the odds ratio and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Qualitative assessment

FGDs were conducted to explore the awareness and factors affecting the perceptions of women regarding reproductive rights and factors influencing these perceptions focusing on the right to family planning services and information on contraceptive methods, the right to safe and legal abortion, and the right to information on sex and sex education. These FGDs were conducted in neutral settings like the field area of Sector-49 and HWC-49, after taking prior appointments from the study participants. Data was collected using a predesigned and pretested FGD guide with probes. The moderator introduced themself, and using the FGD guide with pre-determined questions, encouraged discussions among members by enquiring if they had heard about reproductive rights, if these rights are important and benefiting society or bringing changes in women’s life, and if not, what could be the factors influencing the perception on reproductive rights. All the sessions were audio recorded after obtaining consent for audio recording and, significant non-verbal communication cues were manually recorded. Textual data was first transcribed into the recorded language and then translated into English. Thematic analysis was done manually using the grounded theory approach as shown in Fig. 1. Data integration was done manually correlating quantitative values with qualitative findings.

Quantitative assessment

Around 92.2% of the participants belonged to Hindu religion and 7.8% belonged to other religion. Out of 116 women, only 36.6% had heard of sexual and reproductive rights. Among those participants who had heard of SRRs, 81% of the participants knew about the right to family planning and information on contraceptive methods, 37.1% were aware of the right to safe and legal abortion and 50.9% of the study participants were aware of the right to information on sex and sex education. 50% of those in the urban areas and 55.5% of those studies till intermediate level/diploma/graduate level had received the formal structured sexual education. The adjusted odds of awareness of sexual and reproductive rights among women residing in urban areas were significantly higher than those residing in slum areas [AOR = 5.2, (95%CI = 1.5–17.5)]. The adjusted odds of awareness level among married women were significantly higher among those who had more than one child than those who had one child compared to those with no child [AOR = 22.8, (95%CI = 2.03–256)]. Qualitative assessment

The major themes identified were the right to family planning and information on contraceptive methods, the right to have a safe and legal abortion, and the right to have information on sex or sex education. The background characteristics showed that 37.8% of participants were between the age group of 15–19 years, 32.4% lay in the age group of 20–25, 27.02% between 31 and 35 years followed by 13.5% of these participants were between 26 and 30 years. Participants residing in urban areas were 51.3% and in slums were 48.3%. Discussion

This mixed methods study highlights low awareness levels and multiple factors affecting the perception of SRRs among women aged 15–35 in Chandigarh. Four key SRRs were assessed i.e. awareness of overall reproductive rights, awareness of the right to family planning services and information on contraceptive methods, right to safe and legal abortion, and right to information on sex and sex education. While, participants were aware of human rights, fundamental rights, and the right to choose one’s religion but were unfamiliar with the term sexual and reproductive rights. The qualitative aspect provided insights on barriers: low priority/low perceived need or interest in knowing about these rights, as these rights don’t affect health directly, limited decision-making autonomy regarding which contraceptive methods to use, family planning and abortion services, dependency on the family members (husband/mother in law) to seek consent and take decisions, religious beliefs against abortions, shyness to discuss reproductive rights/sex/sex education openly, etc. Only 36.6% of the participants heard about sexual and reproductive rights and 56.4% of these participants could not list down any rights related to reproductive health when asked about the same. It was further explored that the major source of information on sexual and reproductive rights was from social media (33.3%) which is due to the increasing popularity of social media/internet usage among the participants, followed by the school (30.7%) and health facilities. Marital status was found to be a major obstacle in availing the right to safe and legal abortion which on probing during focus group discussion revealed that the women’s autonomy still lies in the decision maker or the family, 67.8% of the participants think that the husband consent is necessary for abortion. Furthermore, a majority of the population did not feel the relevance of knowing about SRRs, as a lack of awareness about these rights did not lead to immediate health issues. When asked about reproductive rights participants appeared unfamiliar as if they were hearing it for the first time. Their understanding of family planning and information on contraceptive methods were not quite adequate as most of them interpreted family planning is expanding the family and taking care of husband and child. Participants only knew about the right to body integrity, the right to family planning, and the right to safe and legal abortion which means other rights remain largely unrecognized and need to be highlighted more. A study conducted in the medical schools across North India by Gupta et al. (2024), highlighted that despite trained faculty, rights-based reproductive services were only partially implemented. Key barriers included provider bias, infrastructural limitations, high workload, and low awareness or autonomy among women. These challenges underscore the gap between policy and practice in delivering equitable reproductive healthcare [11]. Similar, low awareness levels have been reported globally. Studies from Nigeria, Nepal, India, and Ethiopia found SRRs awareness ranging from almost no awareness to 45.2%, with many participants struggling to define or list down the rights [6, 12–14]. A study by Makind et al. reported that out of 45.2% who heard of SRRs, 58.4% couldn’t list correctly any SRRs and only 23.4% could list one. 81.8% had poor knowledge of SRRs overall. Among those who listed SRRs, the right to family planning was the most commonly known (37.2%), followed by the right to marriage (10.4). In the FGD sessions, almost all the participants didn’t understand the meaning of SRRs as they kept asking for clarification [12]. Also, a study by Rashad et al. [15] showed that 71.25% of the study sample did not know the definition of reproductive health and rights. 70% of the study sample were not aware of the concept of reproductive rights.

We observed that the right to family planning and information on contraceptive methods were known by 81% of the participants, but there existed religious, and educational inequalities among the women in accessing their reproductive rights AOR = 16.06 (95%CI = 2.8–91.6), and AOR = 17.2 (95%CI = 1.4–208) respectively P value < 0.05. This is similar to the findings of Gupta et al., where authors reported geographical inequities regarding awareness about contraception and health services were significantly less among rural (12.7% and 1.1%, respectively) adolescents as compared to slum (17.9% and 4.6%, respectively) and urban adolescents (33.5% and 7.8%, respectively) (P < 0.05) [9]. Another study by Gupta et al. highlighted that one of the barriers to availing right right-based reproductive services lower awareness and decision-making power [13]. Yuya et al. [14] reported that more than 50% of the population were aware of reproductive rights and the major predictors of knowledge were, the father’s higher education, the socio-economic status of the students, and participants who participated in reproductive health club was significantly associated with the knowledge of reproductive rights. On the other hand, the study by Manoj Kaphe [6] highlighted the awareness of respondents towards sex education (59.4%), women’s rights (10.1%), and abortion rights (11.6%). Whereas, we reported that the awareness of respondents towards sex education and the right to safe and legal abortion was 70.2% and 37.1% respectively. Three-fourths of them knew about the right to family planning and having information on contraceptive methods. The factors affecting the perception of reproductive rights were found to be majorly seen as individual, family, and societal influences. Another study in Ethiopia by Mohammed et al. [16] assessed husbands’ knowledge and involvement in women’s reproductive rights and their associated factors, The levels of husbands’ knowledge and involvement were 48.3% and 40.1%, respectively. Whereas, consent of husbands for abortion in our study was 81% in the slum population and was significantly higher with those residing in urban areas at 64.9% [AOR = 3.5 (95%CI = 1.28–9.55)] p-value < 0.05. Focus group discussions revealed that the husband’s/ head of the family’s consent is crucial for abortion, leaving women with very little say in whether to keep the child or abort. Women often defer to their husbands’ choices on contraceptives as they are preoccupied with household responsibilities and a lack of involvement in such decisions. This finding highlights the complex interplay between socio-cultural norms and reproductive autonomy, while access to abortion is a fundamental human right- integral to dignity, equality, and full participation—actual autonomy may be constrained by the requirement for spousal consent, particularly in marginalized settings [17]. Moreover, unmarried women who had conceived are often pressured to abort to protect their family reputation. A similar study by Tardale et al. [18] aimed to determine the factors influencing the women’s decision-making power regarding reproductive health and rights in southwest Ethiopia and it was found that 41.5% of the women had a greater decision-making power regarding reproductive health and rights. The major factors responsible for the negative perception of availing reproductive rights were personal understanding, shyness, autonomy, willingness to learn, attitude, decision maker of the family, generational perspective, religious belief, family type, stigma related to the topic, and government policies.

Strength

A mixed-methods study design was used, and a systematic random sampling technique was employed. We included the adolescent age group (15–18 years) as these are the group who will better utilize their rights in the future if they have a positive perception and better knowledge of these rights.

Limitations

There could be social desirability and interviewer bias in the quantitative study and data saturation bias in the qualitative study. Temporality or a causal relationship is difficult to establish in the cross-sectional study. The small sample size was a limitation of the quantitative study, as we used logistic regression that needed large samples for significant confidence intervals. The generalizability of the study is limited to the study population. Though both married and unmarried females were included but we didn’t interview friends and family members for their role in awareness, as a part of this study.

Public health implications

Bridging the geographical inequities by imparting more awareness among the slums will provide greater access to safe and legal abortion, family planning, delayed marriage, and teenage pregnancy. Thereby, improving maternal mortality ratio and neonate mortality rates. Participant caste and marital status being one of the major hindrances in availing right to safe and legal abortion can be bridged by better assurance and awareness within the community.

Conclusion

Women living in urban areas, those who were unmarried, had higher education, or belonged to the Hindu religion were more likely to be aware of their sexual and reproductive rights, especially regarding the right to family planning and safe abortion. However, many women still struggled with low awareness, due to personal hesitations, a belief that these rights were not immediately important to their health, and the strong influence of decision-makers. Deep-rooted societal stigma and religious beliefs further limited their ability to exercise their SRRs as indicated by qualitative study. These findings highlight the urgent need for structured sex education, supportive policies, and community efforts to help women better understand and claim their reproductive rights. SRRs are one of the subsets of human rights, women can avail these rights to live in a society with dignity and have autonomy over their body. The government sector can use social media to spread awareness as increased uptake of information is seen through this channel. Socio-economic and geographical inequities regarding awareness could be bridged by organizing awareness campaigns. Marital status should not become a hindrance in availing any rights, Women should know their autonomy. Therefore, knowledge dissemination should be done at the school level by integrating with school health and wellness program and at the community level by AWW/ANMs/ASHA’s under the reproductive health programs.
