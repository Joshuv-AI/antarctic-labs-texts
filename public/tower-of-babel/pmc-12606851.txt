Development and initial validation of Healthcare Worker Occupational Fatigue Scale: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Lanhui Tan, Ying Zhang, Yifan Liao, Shi Liu, Ruyi Zhang, Xiaoyan Chen, Wenbin He, Fei Fang, Ying Wang, Li Liu, Bilong Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealthcare workers are at high risk of occupational fatigue. However, the lack of a specific assessment tool hinders understanding of the prevalence of fatigue and the effectiveness of interventions, posing a significant threat to physical and mental well-being.ObjectiveThis study aimed to develop and preliminarily validate a scale specifically designed to assess occupational fatigue among healthcare workers, thereby providing a practical tool for self-assessment and occupational health management.MethodsAn initial item pool was generated through a comprehensive review of literature related to this topic and existing fatigue scales. Content and face validity were assessed via a two-round Delphi expert consultation and a pilot survey, resulting in a preliminary scale version. Psychometric properties were evaluated using data from 475 healthcare workers recruited through convenience sampling from a tertiary Grade A general hospital in Wuhan, Hubei Province. The structural validity was examined using exploratory factor analysis and confirmatory factor analysis. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and Guttman Split-Half reliability. Data analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 and AMOS 23.0.ResultsThe final Healthcare Worker Occupational Fatigue Scale (HWOFS) comprises 35 items across five dimensions: physical, cognitive and emotional, social, sleep-related, and auditory fatigue. The scale demonstrates excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.973, Guttman Split-Half reliability coefficient = 0.924). Content validity was strong, with item-level content validity indices ranging from 0.867 to 1.000 and a scale-level content validity index of 0.925. The confirmatory factor analysis supported the five-factor structure, showing good model fit. All standardized factor loadings were ≥ 0.532 (all p < 0.01).ConclusionThis study demonstrated the robust reliability and validity of the newly developed HWOFS. These initial findings support the potential of HWOFS as a tool for healthcare institutions to establish fatigue warning systems, optimize human resource allocation, and develop occupational health protection policies. Background

Fatigue is broadly defined as a sensation or condition of weariness, tiredness, or diminished energy [1]. Occupational fatigue is the exhaustion that arises during, after, or in association with work [2]. Fatigue among healthcare workers (HCWs) has long been a concern, as it may lead to reduced concentration, delayed reaction times, and impaired clinical decision-making [3–5], thus posing significant risks to patient safety and care quality [5, 6]. Empirical evidence demonstrates that fatigue increases the likelihood of medical errors by 22% compared to well-rested surgeons [7]. Similarly, 83% of nurses reported committing medical errors attributable to fatigue in a previous study [8].

Occupational fatigue among HCWs is notably high, with reported prevalence rates ranging from 21.6% to 91.9% [9]. This significant variation in the prevalence can be attributed to various factors, including inconsistent definitions of fatigue, diverse assessment tools, varied sample sizes, and divergent social environments [9]. Fundamentally, the lack of a validated, specific fatigue assessment tool has perpetuated inconsistent methodologies across healthcare settings, thus impeding accurate quantification of fatigue and the development of universally applicable prevention and management strategies.

A previous systematic review [10] has identified several instruments commonly used in HCWs, including the Occupational Fatigue Exertion and Recovery scale, Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory, Need for Recovery Scale, and the Swedish Occupational Fatigue Inventory. Although these tools offer valuable approaches to assessing fatigue, their applicability to HCWs remains limited. First, a key limitation is that the target population was not clearly defined during their development, and contextual factors relevant to healthcare were often overlooked. For example, in the Chinese medical system, which is characterized by high work intensity, distinctive cultural values, and hierarchical environments [11, 12], the applicability of existing tools may be particularly constrained. Second, there is a lack of high-quality and consistent evidence to support the reliability and validity of these tools in assessing fatigue among HCWs. Consequently, the application of these tools may neglect key determinants and manifestations of fatigue among HCWs, limiting both accuracy and cross-study comparability.

Therefore, in the present study, the Healthcare Worker Occupational Fatigue Scale (HWOFS) was developed and validated in this study following DeVellis’ guidelines [13]. The primary objective was to establish an instrument specifically grounded in healthcare occupational contexts, thereby addressing limitations of contextual applicability and incomplete dimensional coverage observed in existing generic fatigue measures. The theoretical foundation is informed by the Conceptual Framework of Occupational Fatigue established by Watterson et al. [2], which was derived from the Systems Engineering Initiative for Patient Safety model. The final HWOFS comprises five dimensions: physical, cognitive and emotional, social, sleep-related, and auditory fatigue, forming a theory-driven and contextually embedded assessment tool with comprehensive dimensional coverage.

Methods

The HWOFS was developed in two main steps: 1) instrument development and 2) preliminary validation (Fig. 1).

Process of development and preliminary validation of the HWOFS

Instrument development

Item generation

Guided by the Conceptual Framework of Occupational Fatigue [2], a comprehensive literature search was conducted across four databases: PubMed, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Wanfang Database. Search terms included “fatigue,” “health personnel,” “medical staff,” and related phrases. Two researchers (LH T and Y Z) independently reviewed the relevant articles to identify recurrent themes associated with occupational fatigue among HCWs. This was followed by an analysis of items from existing psychometrically validated instruments, identifying five themes related to occupational fatigue: physical, cognitive and emotional, social, sleep-related, and auditory fatigue. An initial item pool comprising 45 items was subsequently developed. Among them, 25 items were adjusted by referring the following scales: the Swedish Occupational Fatigue Inventory [14], Fatigue Scale-14 [15], Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory [16], Occupational Fatigue Exhaustion Recovery Scale [17], Self-regulated fatigue scale [18], Maslach Burnout Inventory [19], and Intensive Care Unit Nurses’ Alarm Fatigue Questionnaire [20]. Items adapted from existing English-language scales underwent a rigorous forward- and back-translation process conducted by two bilingual researchers (LH T and YF L) to ensure conceptual equivalence. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third researcher (Y W). To ensure discriminability and simplicity, all items were rated using a five-point Likert scale (1 = never happened, 5 = always like this).

Content and face validity test

Initially, the content validity of the 45 items was assessed using the Delphi method to achieve expert consensus. The experts were selected based on their qualifications and research experience related to hospital or nursing management and instrument development. Ultimately, a total of 15 experts from Hubei Province, Beijing City, or Sichuan Province in China were invited to participate in the consultations. All of them had at least 10 years of work experience, with seven holding master’s degree or higher. Each expert used a four-point Likert scale (1 = unrelated to 4 = very relevant) to evaluate the representativeness, comprehensibility, ambiguity, and clarity of each item. They also provided recommendations for the addition, reduction, or modification of items. The item-level content validity index (I-CVI) and scale-level content validity index (S-CVI/Ave) were calculated. The I-CVI score for each item was determined by dividing the number of experts who rated the item as 3 or 4 by the total number of experts [21]. Items scoring below 0.80 were revised or removed based on expert feedback. The S-CVI/Ave was determined by averaging the I-CVI of all items. A score of 0.90 or higher is considered acceptable [22]. The mean importance score and coefficient of variation (CV) were used to assess the level of concentration and coordination among expert opinions. Items were considered for deletion if the mean score was less than 3.00 or if the CV value exceeded 0.25 [23].

Following two rounds of content validity testing, the preliminary scale underwent face validity testing with a convenience sample of 20 HCWs from a tertiary Grade A general hospital in Wuhan, Hubei Province, China. The inclusion criteria were: 1) doctors, nurses, pharmacists, laboratory or examination personnel, technicians, administrative staff, and manual workers; 2) active employment during the investigation period; and 3) provision of informed consent and voluntary participation. The exclusion criteria were: 1) individuals studying or undergoing further training elsewhere during the investigation period; 2) those who withdrew from the study midway; 3) individuals who experienced significant life changes in the past month, such as bereavement, severe physical illness (e.g., recent surgery, postural or musculoskeletal disorders, neurological disorders), or sudden mental illness; and 4) participants currently receiving psychological treatment or counseling. Participants were first asked to complete the draft scale and provide feedback on the relevance and suitability of the items.

Preliminary validation of the HWOFS

Validation of the HWOFS was based on a cross-sectional survey. Item, factor, and reliability analyses were conducted sequentially.

Participants

Participants were recruited from a tertiary Grade A general hospital in Hubei Province, China. The inclusion criteria were consistent with those used for the face validity assessment. The sample size was determined based on the requirements of factor analysis. An exploratory factor analysis (EFA) typically requires a sample size that is five to 10 times the number of items on a scale. The draft scale, following content and face validity evaluations, consisted of 40 items, necessitating a sample size between 200 and 400. For confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), the sample size should exceed 200 and be larger than that used for EFA [24]. Considering an anticipated 15% rate of invalid responses, the final estimated sample size was at least 471 participants.

Data collection

Electronic questionnaire (Supplementary Material) was distributed via Wenjuanxing platform through the official internal OA system of the hospital between April 10 and April 25, 2025. The questionnaire included demographic information, professional details, and the final draft of the HWOFS. Participants accessed the questionnaire by scanning a QR code and provided informed consent before proceeding. They were asked to reflect on their experiences of occupational fatigue over the past month, a period chosen to balance recall accuracy and symptom range. A total of 507 questionnaires were collected. After excluding 32 invalid questionnaires based on completion times of less than 5 min and patterns of uniform responses, 475 valid questionnaires were retained for analysis, representing an effective response rate of 93.69%. These 475 datasets were sequentially numbered based on the submission time and subsequently categorized into two subsamples using a random number method. These subsamples were utilized for EFA (dateset A, n = 225) and CFA (dateset B, n = 250).

Analysis

Collected data were imported into Excel software, reviewed by two independent researchers (LH T and Y Z), and subsequently analyzed using IBM SPSS version 26.0 and AMOS version 23.0. Descriptive statistics were used to calculate the demographic characteristics of the participants, including frequencies (%), means, and standard deviations. Corrected item-total correlations were used to assess the relationships between the items and the instrument. Items with a correlation coefficient (r) below 0.40 were removed [21]. Exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses were conducted using EFA and CFA, respectively. First, the appropriateness of conducting factor analysis was evaluated using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) index and Bartlett’s test of sphericity before the EFA. Principal component analysis with varimax rotation was conducted to examine the latent factor structure of the scale. The criterion for appropriate factor extraction was an eigenvalue of 1.00 or more, with communalities and factor loadings of 0.50 or more applied, respectively [21]. Subsequently, the maximum likelihood method was used to estimate the model parameters and fit indices for CFA. Model fit was assessed using the following indices: goodness of fit index (GFI), root mean square residual (RMR), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), comparative fit index (CFI), incremental fit index (IFI), and the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI). A GFI, CFI, IFI, and TLI values greater than 0.90 indicate a good fit, while values greater than 0.80 denote a reasonable fit [25]. Conversely, RMR and RMSEA values less than 0.05 indicate a good fit, and values between 0.05 and 0.08 suggest a reasonable fit [25]. Finally, the internal consistency reliability of the HWOFS was evaluated by calculating Cronbach’s alpha, with a cutoff value of 0.70 or higher considered acceptable for the overall scale [22].

Results

Content and face validity

The results from the first round of expert evaluation showed that the I-CVI ranged from 0.800 to 1.000, with an S-CVI/Ave of 0.887. Despite achieving I-CVI values exceeding 0.800, experts perceived certain items as ambiguous or confusing. Consequently, five items were removed following deliberation, and two items were revised. In the second round of evaluation, the I-CVI ranged from 0.867 to 1.000, and the S-CVI/Ave was 0.925, suggesting that the content validity of the HWOFS is robust.

The assessment of face validity involved 20 pilot participants, comprising nine males and 11 females, all of whom reported that the content of this scale was clear, comprehensible, and did not necessitate further revisions. The time required to complete the questionnaire varied from 4.83 to 12.20 min, with an average of 8.49 ± 2.06 min. No items were altered or removed at this stage. The final draft of the HWOFS includes five subscales with 40 items.

Preliminary validation

Sample characteristics

Most of the 475 participants were female (n = 401, 84.42%) and predominantly aged between 31 and 40 years (n = 232, 48.84%). Most participants had a bachelor’s degree (n = 287, 60.42%) and were in marriage (n = 348, 73.26%). The average duration of hospital employment was 10.64 ± 8.19 years. Item analysis

The corrected item-total correlation coefficients for the 40 items ranged from 0.228 to 0.825 (all p < 0.01). Among them, items “I am always energetic at work,” “Even when feeling tired, I still need to maintain an active state at work,” and “I take a nap for more than 20 min” had correlation coefficients with the total score of less than 0.400, warranting their exclusion. Finally, 37 items were retained.

Structural analysis

The KMO and Bartlett’s test of sphericity indicated that the data were appropriate for an EFA (KMO = 0.965, χ2 = 8175.252, p < 0.001). The result of EFA identified five factors with eigenvalues equal to or greater than 1.000. Each item’s factor loading was at least 0.516, and the cumulative variance contribution rate was 72.248%. The item “I feel anxious and irritable and easily lose patience during work” was not consistent with the anticipated “cognitive and emotional fatigue” dimension. Additionally, the items “I am reluctant to discuss work-related topics with colleagues or family,” “Due to the excessive workload, I occasionally delay responding to alarms,” and “Long-term exposure to noise has made me habitually ignore non-emergency alarms” exhibited dual loadings. These items were retained based on three criteria established through research group consensus: 1) high content validity, as indicated by an I-CVI > 0.90; 2) clinical significance, as confirmed via expert panel evaluation; and 3) representation of a unique aspect within their respective theoretical dimensions. Table 2 presents the factor loadings. The five extracted common factors were designated as physical fatigue (14 items), cognitive and emotional fatigue (eight items), social fatigue (four items), sleep-related fatigue (five items), and auditory fatigue (six items). The 37-item and five-factor structures found with the EFA were tested using CFA. The initial results of CFA indicated that the standardized path coefficients for the items “I tend to forget critical steps or information within the workflow” and “I snore during sleep” were below 0.500, and the model fit indices were suboptimal. Consequently, these two items were removed, and the CFA was conducted again. The subsequent analysis revealed that the standardized path coefficients for all items in the scale exceeded 0.500 (all p < 0.001; Fig. 2), and the model fit indices were more closely aligned with the ideal values compared to the initial analysis. Adjustments were iteratively made based on the model modification indices. The goodness of fit for the final five-factor model was as follows: χ2/df = 1.914, RMSEA = 0.061, RMR = 0.053, CFI = 0.920, IFI = 0.921, GFI = 0 0.792.

Reliability tests

The overall Cronbach’s alpha for the HWOFS was 0.973, with each subscale exhibiting a Cronbach’s alpha exceeding 0.800. Furthermore, split-half reliability analysis revealed that the Guttman Split-half coefficients for both the entire scale and subscales were greater than 0.800. Collectively, these findings suggest that the scale has robust internal consistency and reliability (Table 3).

Discussion

HCWs face an elevated risk of irreversible cumulative chronic fatigue owing to the inherent vulnerability of their patient population, the continuous nature of their work schedules, and consistently high job demands [26]. This persistent fatigue not only significantly impairs their health but also increases the likelihood of medical errors, posing substantial patient safety risks [26, 27]. The HWOFS developed in this study comprises 35 items encompassing 5 dimensions: physical, cognitive and emotional, social, sleep-related, and auditory fatigue. This tool provides a standardized and professional measure for assessing occupational fatigue among HCWs. The HWOFS reduces heterogeneity across studies and establishes a unified basis for both the design and evaluation of interventions. It can be utilized as a benchmark tool for preliminary screening, longitudinal monitoring, and assessment of the effectiveness of interventions. For organizational management practices, baseline surveys are recommended to establish reference fatigue levels. For example, an increase in total fatigue scores exceeding 20% from baseline, either at the individual or group level, or persistently elevated scores in specific dimensions (e.g., scores consistently above 4 on a 5-point Likert scale), should be considered indicative thresholds for initiating further assessment or targeted interventions.

In the present study, the development and validation of the HWOFS adhere to rigorous quality and quantity standards to ensure scientific objectivity and methodological robustness. An initial item pool was generated through a comprehensive literature review and evaluation of relevant measurement tools. Two rounds of structured expert consultation demonstrated excellent content validity and reliable expert consensus. EFA extracted five factors, accounting for a cumulative variance of 72.248%, indicating that the scale adequately captures the construct of occupational fatigue among HCWs [21]. CFA supported the hypothesized scale structure, with key fit indices (including the CFI, IFI, RMR, and RMSEA) meeting accepted standards, thus indicating satisfactory model fit. Although the GFI fell below conventional thresholds, this is not uncommon in initial versions of multidimensional scales, often owing to model complexity (e.g., 5 factors and 35 items) [28, 29]. This finding suggests that future studies are required to validate the stability of the model within larger samples or develop a short-form version to improve fit performance. The overall Cronbach’s α and split-half reliability indicated exceptionally high internal consistency, exceeding that of some widely used general fatigue scales, which typically have Cronbach’s α values ranging from 0.53 to 0.93 [10]. This reliability level may be attributed to the increased item homogeneity and domain-specific focus of the HWOFS on HCWs. However, a very high Cronbach’s α value may indicate a high degree of project redundancy [30]. Therefore, future research should explore the use of more rigorous statistical methods, such as evaluating the discrimination of items based on a larger sample size of data in combination with item response theory. Such studies could help identify and eliminate redundant items with relatively low information contribution while maintaining their validity and reliability.

The HWOFS demonstrates convergence with established instruments [15, 16, 31], in its comprehensive evaluation of core fatigue domains, including physical and cognitive-emotional dimensions. The first dimension of the HWOFS, physical fatigue, encompasses 14 items that reflect manifestations of physical exhaustion resulting from manual labor or repetitive tasks, including limb weakness and muscle soreness following work. Empirical evidence indicates that occupational demands significantly elevate the prevalence of physical fatigue in HCWs compared to the general population [32]. Moreover, prolonged standing, patient transfers, and sustained surgical procedures substantially increase the musculoskeletal strain [33]. These findings further validate the practical sensitivity and applicability of the physical fatigue dimension of HWOFS.

The second dimension, cognitive and emotional fatigue, includes seven items and captures HWCs fatigue states across the cognitive, emotional, and motivational dimensions. HWCs routinely confront life-or-death responsibilities, physician–patient conflicts, and resource scarcity chronic stressors that elicit prolonged stress responses [26, 34]. Sustained exposure to such occupational conditions frequently manifests as anxiety, irritability, attentional deficits, and diminished perception of work-related achievements and values [35]. Empirical evidence indicates that emergency department physicians who manage critically ill patients frequently exhibit a 30% higher prevalence of attentional impairment and emotional blunting than their counterparts in other departments [36]. Given the high prevalence of physical, cognitive, and emotional fatigue among HCWs, these two dimensions constitute essential components within fatigue assessment instruments.

Notably, the distinctive strength of the HWOFS, compared with existing fatigue instruments, lies in its high degree of contextual alignment with healthcare work environments. Specifically, the scale incorporates three critical yet previously underemphasized dimensions that are particularly salient to clinical settings: social, sleep-related, and auditory fatigue. The third dimension, social fatigue, comprises four items and arises from frequent emotional labor and role transitions. In the workplace, HCWs routinely engage with colleagues, patients, and caregivers while navigating diverse communicative contexts [37, 38]. This necessitates considerable social skills and emotional regulation, which demand significant psychological resources. The items “Suppressing negative emotions (e.g., anxiety, irritability) during communication with patients and their caregivers fatigues me” and “After work, I tend to avoid social engagements, including gatherings with relatives and friends.” align directly with Hochschild’s [39] emotional labor theory. HCWs must continuously regulate their emotional expressions across different scenarios. Surface acting, which is characterized by suppressed emotions and social withdrawal after work, accelerates psychological resource depletion, ultimately leading to social fatigue [38].

The fourth dimension, sleep fatigue, comprises four items assessing sleep quality and perceived insufficient of recovery. The unique demands of healthcare work, characterized by shift work, night shifts, and the need to respond to emergencies, frequently compromise the duration and quality of sleep among HCWs [40]. Accumulating evidence suggests that irregular sleep schedules disrupt circadian rhythms and impair sleep quality, contributing to sleep fatigue [41]. This resultant fatigue adversely affects not only daily work performance but may also have long-term detrimental effects on physical health, such as an elevated risk of cardiovascular disease and metabolic disorders [42]. In contrast to general fatigue scales such as the Fatigue Scale-14 [15], which treats sleep disturbances merely as concomitant symptoms of fatigue, the HWOFS establishes “sleep fatigue” as an independent dimension. This design underscores that, for HCWs, sleep impairments are often not secondary consequences of fatigue but rather a core issue directly attributable to shift work or the round-the-clock operational demands.

The fifth dimension, auditory fatigue, comprises six items that evaluate the sensory fatigue induced by prolonged exposure to noise within healthcare environments, such as medical equipment alarms, human voices, and telephone rings [43]. This dimension reflects a frequently overlooked occupational stressor: chronic sensory pollution. It encompasses both noise-related symptoms, such as reduced auditory sensitivity, anxiety, and exhaustion, and associated cognitive and behavioral adaptations, including attentional diversion and delayed responses to alarms [43, 44]. Notably, this manifestation has rarely been addressed in prior assessments of occupational fatigue. Even specialized instruments, such as the ICU Nurses’ Alarm Fatigue Questionnaire [20], predominantly focus predominantly on alarm responsiveness and psychological outcomes, thereby failing to comprehensively capture the multifaceted effects of auditory load, somatic symptoms, and behavioral adaptations in noisy clinical settings. To the best of our knowledge, the HWOFS represents the first attempt to systematically incorporate “auditory fatigue” as an independent dimension, underscoring the sensitivity of the scale to the specificities of the healthcare work environment. This approach also provides an empirical basis for enhancing occupational health practices through improved acoustic environment management.

Limitations

Although the scale demonstrates reasonable reliability and validity, it has some limitations. First, the sample was recruited exclusively from a single tertiary hospital in China. This may have introduced a bias toward nursing-specific fatigue experiences in both the structure and items of the scale, potentially limiting its generalizability across diverse healthcare settings, regions, and health systems. Further validation in broader samples is recommended to establish cross-institutional, cross-professional, and cross-cultural applicability. Second, test–retest reliability was not assessed because of the inherent situational dependence and temporal volatility of the constructor. Occupational fatigue among HCWs fluctuates markedly with shift cycles (e.g., night shifts or day rotations), emergent events (e.g., mass casualties and outbreaks), and seasonal demands (e.g., influenza surges). Environmental variability between assessments can systematically underestimate reliability by violating the assumption of temporal stability. Therefore, future studies should incorporate repeated measurements under controlled work context conditions. Finally, the absence of reverse-scored items precluded the assessment of acquiescence response bias. Therefore, subsequent revisions should consider incorporating reverse-coded items to mitigate this potential bias.

Conclusions

The present study developed and initially validated a fatigue assessment scale specifically designed for HCWs, comprising 35 items across 5 dimensions. Preliminary evaluation demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity, providing a useful tool for self-assessment of fatigue levels among HCWs. However, as this represents the initial validation study, further multi-center and cross-cultural validation is required to confirm the applicability of the scale across diverse healthcare settings and cultural contexts before widespread adoption. This clarification more accurately represents the current stage of the development and validation of the scale.
