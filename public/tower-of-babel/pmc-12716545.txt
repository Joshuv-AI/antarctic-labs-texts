Ensuring equitable engagement in global health education: Rwandan perspectives and observations about learner exchange with high-income countries
Authors: Sirey Zhang, Micaela Dickinson, Christian Umuhoza, Florence Masaisa, Emmanual Rusingiza, Emmanuel Munyaneza, Lisa V. Adams
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGlobal health originated from colonial and tropical medicine, which served the interests of colonial powers. Despite efforts to address this legacy, the field still faces challenges in unequal partnerships between high-income (HICs) and low-income and middle-income (LMICs) countries. Activists push for paradigm shifts emphasising health equity, decolonisation and partnerships based on solidarity and justice. The Geisel School of Medicine at Dartmouth aligns with these movements, focusing its global health programmes on equity.MethodsThis study, employing ethnographic methods to centre the perspectives of learners at one of its partner institutions, the University Teaching Hospital of Kigali, identified key themes to enhance equity in global health medical education.ResultsThese themes yielded concrete actions at the student, curricular and institutional levels that should be undertaken to promote equity and justice in academic global health partnerships.ConclusionThe findings offer a tailored framework for fostering equitable and justice-oriented practices in future exchanges with potential applications to academic global health exchanges between HICs and LMICs more broadly.

Introduction

Global health originated in colonial and tropical medicine, aimed at protecting colonial powers while advancing the interests of the global north. Today, it spans politics, research, academia and clinical practice, considering historical and socio-political contexts to address health problems. Yet colonial legacies persist, seen in the prioritisation of global health security, limited funding for local priorities and focus on infectious diseases over rising chronic conditions.16 The implications of global health’s legacies still play out in the inequitable partnerships between institutions from high-income countries (HICs) and low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs) in developing healthcare systems and producing research.7 Within these collaborations is a known trend of LMIC researchers conducting fieldwork and data collection, while foreign researchers analyse data and claim authorship of papers.8

Recent years have seen calls in academic global health to recenter health equity and decolonise partnerships, addressing enduring power imbalances between HIC and LMIC institutions. These inequities persist in research funding, which often flows through HIC institutions, limiting LMIC researchers’ resources and opportunities. Global health journals and editorial boards remain largely dominated by male voices from Europe and North America.4913

As a part of this trend, Naidu has coined the term ‘Northern ventriloquism’ to describe visitors from HICs travelling to LMICs for traction in research and scholarship back home.14 LMIC insiders often serve as information sources for HIC researchers, whose work validates findings through Western frameworks that overlook local knowledge. Even when LMIC scholars lead, ‘ventriloquism’ persists as global health continues to mirror HIC institutions, reinforcing their dominance. As she reflects, “as a woman of color from a middle-income country, I am skeptical of the resolve to bring the so-called promised land closer. Will there be anything left of ourselves when it comes?”14

Medical students from HICs are also common perpetrators of ‘Northern ventriloquism’, as seen in the disproportionate global health experiences available to HIC trainees that more often than not benefit the HIC visitors.15 Eichbaum et al note that ‘global health is an artificial construct created by HICs to describe healthcare in LMICs’, with HICs often setting the metrics for success in global health research.16 As such, many LMIC global health academics are calling for more two-way exchanges, investment in mentoring learners from LMICs, prioritising authorship from LMIC researchers and greater funding of these efforts.17 Anand et al propose a ‘glocal health’ framework for training HIC students that addresses issues such as saviourism in current models, suggesting that experiential learning and research in marginalised HIC communities can foster justice-oriented thinkers ready to tackle global and local health equity challenges.18 Banerjee et al suggest combating saviourism by building a culture of sharing and giving up power when necessary and by asking partners about their goals and how students’ work can address local community needs, as LMIC hosts emphasise cultural awareness and respectful conduct as essential competencies for visiting trainees, with none viewing them as a solution for local care gaps.19 20

Since 2012, Dartmouth’s Geisel School of Medicine, the University of Rwanda and the Rwandan Ministry of Health have collaborated in the Rwanda-led Human Resources for Health (HRH) programme to strengthen medical training, with Rwanda retaining control over funding and design—reversing the typical HIC-dominated model in global health.2124 In its execution, HRH aimed to shift the paradigm of dependency and disempowerment and gradually reduce reliance on foreign support while simultaneously building its new healthcare leadership.25 As part of the HRH programme, Dartmouth faculty undertook long-term teaching roles in Rwanda and partnered with local physicians to strengthen medical education. Dartmouth also hosted Rwandan learners and faculty for clinical rotations in New Hampshire. A medical student exchange programme launched in 2015 was paused during COVID-19 and resumed in 2023 with renewed focus on reciprocity. In early 2024, the University of Rwanda hosted the first postpandemic Dartmouth students for electives at the University Teaching Hospital of Kigali (CHUK).26 27 As early participants in this exchange, we examined Rwandan students’ and faculty experiences to contribute to LMIC-centred global health literature and offer insights for strengthening similar partnerships.28

Methods

This study employed ethnographic methods, including semistructured interviews and participant observation, with guidance from an independent senior medical anthropologist. The interviewers (MD and SZ) completed the Boston University Practitioner’s Guide to Global Health modules on cultural competency, and one (SZ) had prior qualitative research experience and a bachelor’s degree in anthropology.

15 University of Rwanda medical students were interviewed in person using a guide designed to explore their experiences collaborating with visiting students and their goals for an exchange rotation at an HIC institution. The guide, developed from a literature review on global health exchanges, included open-ended and follow-up questions and was piloted and revised with colleagues from Dartmouth and CHUK to ensure clarity and cultural appropriateness. Participants were peers of the researchers from their internal medicine and paediatrics rotations, representing the broader pool of Rwandan clinical students. Although visiting HIC students were not formally interviewed, informal discussions with them informed our observations. Verbal informed consent was obtained after explaining the project’s goals, confidentiality and potential impact. Interviews, conducted in English in private CHUK conference rooms, were held individually and in focus groups. Manual note-taking was used instead of recordings to foster open dialogue and reduce power imbalances; all quotes were anonymised and linked only to general topics.

In addition to interviews, the two interviewers (Dickinson and Zhang) conducted observations across multiple CHUK departments while participating in a 6-week clinical elective as student members of care teams. Daily notes—recorded in real time and summarised each evening—captured setting-specific dynamics, emotions and interactions between local and visiting trainees, primarily on inpatient wards. The interviewees were the same students the researchers worked alongside during this elective occurring January-February 2024.

Interview and observation data were transcribed, deidentified and thematically coded using a structured grounded theory approach. Observation notes and interview transcripts were first coded independently, then grouped into broader themes through iterative analysis. Illustrative quotes were extracted to support key findings and ensure consistency across reviewers.29 Themes identified in both independent analyses were included in the results. Unique themes were jointly reviewed and either integrated or excluded through discussion. Final themes were refined collaboratively by team members from CHUK and Dartmouth, supported by representative quotes and observation notes to ensure grounding in the data. Data were stored and analysed using Microsoft Excel.

Results

15 Rwandan medical students were interviewed (5 men and 10 women) and 340 hours were spent in the clinical setting by the two lead researchers (Dickinson and Zhang). The medical students were all in their final year of study.

The data from participant observation and interviews fell into four themes: the interpersonal interactions between learners from HICs rotating at CHUK, experiences and perceptions of Rwandan students regarding visiting learners, goals for Rwandan students hoping to complete a rotation at an HIC institution, and their perceived barriers to completing a visiting rotation at an HIC institution. Discussion

This study draws on the lived experiences of Rwandan medical providers and learners in exchanges with HIC institutions at CHUK. Integrated with literature on global health partnerships, the resulting themes provide insights for creating more equitable future exchanges.

Absence of reciprocity

A key finding is that US learners relied heavily on Rwandan students to navigate cultural, linguistic and hospital system contexts, placing an unreciprocated burden on the latter. While this mediation benefits HIC learners, it raises concerns about power dynamics, equity and reinforcement of hierarchical structures. Observations showed Rwandan students often paused their own tasks to interpret, explain hospital logistics and guide visiting students—offsetting much of any help the visitors provided to the healthcare team.

(Observation notes) As visiting students, we have relied on the Rwandan medical students for interpretation during patient interactions, to fill out forms in Kinyarwanda for patients, and to explain the hospital systems, e.g. what medicines and tests are available.

The Rwandan students understood this as a necessary component of exchange with visiting institutions, and they largely did not mind the imposition on them if the visiting HIC students were quick to adapt and integrate into their learning environment, both socially and as peers.

(Rwandan medical student) I don’t like when they are not organized, when they don’t know where to go when and you can’t rely on them. When they are chill and you can just joke and have fun then it’s good, because you can learn from them, and they can learn from you.

Due to imbalanced medical student exchanges, Rwandan medical students disproportionately serve as hosts and miss out on the benefits of being guests elsewhere, which is the norm in global academic medicine.30 Since visiting HIC students do not have the opportunity to host Rwandan students at their home institutions as often or at all, this highlights a significant imbalance in the exchange structure. The issue is compounded when visiting students take learning opportunities away from local students, particularly in surgical subspecialties.

(Observation notes) In a discussion about the presence of learners from HIC institutions potentially impacting the learning of Rwandan medical students, one visiting student commented how there were so many visiting students in surgery rotations to the extent that many Rwandan medical students were unable to participate in the operating room.

From our perspective, prioritising operating room access for visiting HIC students can inadvertently limit essential opportunities for Rwandan students, whose clinical education depends on these experiences.

Motivations of HIC learners

The motivations of US and European learners for participating in these exchanges were multifaceted. Most visiting students expressed a genuine desire to contribute to CHUK’s medical education and to broaden their understanding of cross-cultural healthcare, aiming to improve both local and global health contexts.

(Observation notes) When the Rwandan residents were unavailable on the wards because of a required exam, visiting residents took the medical students, both Rwandan and visiting, on rounds and provided informal teaching.

(Observation notes) When asked why they came to Rwanda, several visiting medical students and residents from HICs referenced wanting to see what healthcare is like in a “low-resourced setting,” while others talked about wanting to teach students and support the clinical faculty.

While the desire to experience healthcare in a low-resourced country is not inherently harmful, it reflects the ability of HIC visitors to enter an LMIC, take what they can and leave without contributing reciprocal, positive change. Naidu describes this as a form of voyeurism, with these self-serving intentions being both conscious and unconscious.14 The phenomenon that Naidu describes is made possible by the conditions that HIC learners may find themselves in, as one student commented on their ability to act clinically beyond what they were able to in their home institution.

(Observation notes) One visiting learner commented how if a medical student wanted to, they could get away with doing a lot of clinical care independently such as procedures, writing orders, and making decisions about patients’ care, whereas visiting LMIC students at their institution were there for ‘observership’ and could not even access the medical record system.

Some visiting learners appeared to act with a level of independence and overconfidence in their skill level that, while presumably well intentioned, could reflect a misalignment between local norms and expectations and be seen as self-serving. These dynamics may reflect enduring structural legacies, including historical inequities in medical education and global engagement, which often place global health in a charitable rather than a human rights framework and continue to influence behaviour and expectations today. Even with conscious effort to stay within one’s scope, a subconscious mindset that disregards the rules of an HIC institution in the LMIC context can still cause harm.

A notable example shared by an attending physician involved a visiting student using patient information photographs for clinical presentations at their home institution without obtaining consent from the patient, their family or clinical supervisors, despite no patient identifiers being shared. Another instance of a perceived double standard occurred when a visiting learner, facing a language barrier with patients, felt empowered to act in ways that would otherwise be ethically unacceptable in their home context.

(Observation notes) One visiting learner used a family member of one patient who was able to speak some English as an interpreter for other patients because they did not feel that patient privacy was as much of a concern in Rwanda compared to [their home country].

While the learner may have been trying to provide the best care possible for their Rwandan patients, this clearly reflects a feeling that the legal and ethical standards of their home country do not apply in LMICs such as Rwanda, and therefore side-stepping the rules and professional norms is allowable.

In addition to assessing our peers, it’s crucial to critically examine our own motivations for this project to ensure our global health efforts are driven by a genuine commitment to collaboration, learning and mutual benefit, rather than by voyeuristic, self-serving impulses or overconfidence. A common question in the clinical setting from students, faculty and staff was why Rwanda was chosen for our rotation and study. Logistically, CHUK was an ideal location due to its long-standing collaboration with Dartmouth and faculty involvement in the HRH programme.

(Observation notes) Other foreign medical students have specifically cited wanting to be here to be ‘helpful’ or to see what it is like doing healthcare in a ‘low resource’ setting… Firstly, should there be a different term that acknowledges the cause of this, i.e. environments of inequity? Secondly, this feels voyeuristic, self-serving, and overconfident in one’s own abilities as a medical student… but why do I want to be here? Is it to contribute to the learning environment? Is it to be a part of a ‘bigger,’ more ‘global’ community? Is it to learn about healthcare delivery in a different context alongside people from that context? Is it to exercise my critical examination of systems and how they manifest in the healthcare provided and the medical learning environment? Are these reasons good enough given the unequal exchange, the burden I could be, and the privilege I have?

Our experiences with visiting students, along with our personal reflections, underscore the challenge of navigating these dynamics. While it was not anyone’s explicit intent to behave voyeuristically or reproduce a saviour narrative at CHUK, our observations and interviews revealed that most visiting students approached their experience with self-benefiting intentions. Though not intentionally harmful, these actions often recapitulate historical patterns of HIC actors benefiting at the expense of LMIC people and places.

Contextual humility

Our analysis underscores the importance of contextual humility in navigating cultural differences and misunderstandings. Global health exchanges expose learners to diverse cultural norms, making it crucial to engage in self-reflection, recognise biases and approach interactions with openness and respect. Our data suggest that more preparation is needed for visiting learners to understand the contexts they will encounter. Supervising physicians at CHUK excelled in teaching us how our presence should never interfere with optimal care in the local context.

(Observation note) One attending physician emphasized the importance of using Kinyarwanda during medical rounds if the patient’s family is around or if the patient is able to understand what is going on, since it is more important to involve those impacted the most by the care plan rather than accommodate the visiting students by communicating in English. They stated that historically, more has been disproportionately done to accommodate visiting learners at the expense of patient care, and that this is overall a legacy of colonialism.

Embedding country-specific and site-specific preparation in the HIC global health curriculum—such as learning history, engaging with antiracist and anticolonial literature, and simulating resource imbalances—can prevent HIC learners’ actions from conflicting with the realities of the local setting. For instance, Rwandan students were bothered by visiting learners ordering extra tests without considering financial implications, showing a lack of awareness of local resources. Local students also expressed frustration with CHUK being labelled ‘low resourced’ and ‘poor resourced’,” feeling it was treated as inferior to HIC healthcare settings.

(Rwandan medical student) When they don’t try to connect with us or learn about our way of things and when they keep on emphasizing how much better their education is and how good they have it, such as calling our place a poor-resourced setting …then we do not have a good experience with these visiting students.

The repeated use of terms such as ‘low resourced’ or ‘poor resourced’ to describe CHUK was perceived by some local learners as diminishing, underscoring the importance of framing such settings within their historical and systemic context. These terms also imply there’s is something inherently wrong with the local context rather than emphasise the history that led to the current status quo.

Respecting our Rwandan peers’ feelings, we propose ‘environment of inequity’ as an alternative term. In these environments, visiting HIC learners should enter as peers, embodying a ‘collaborative model’,’ rather than adopting a ‘deficit model’ that assumes superiority in addressing resource gaps beyond their training. It is important that visiting HIC learners approach exchanges with humility, recognising that training systems differ across contexts and that each setting offers valuable knowledge and expertise. Moreover, many LMIC clinical faculty were trained in the same HIC institutions as visiting learners, often through equitable exchanges.

Our observations revealed frequent questioning of Rwandan students and residents about the tests, treatments and interventions available at CHUK and in Rwanda. While well intentioned, this often highlighted the resource imbalance between the local environment and those of HIC visitors. Ideally, this knowledge gap would be addressed before the HIC learner’s rotation begins in their home country. The need for prerotation preparation should be applied to tangible aspects such as the medications and labs that are not available in Rwanda due to the aforementioned ‘environment of inequity’.

(Rwandan medical student) Sometimes the visiting residents and attendings order many tests for patients and they don’t realize the cost to the patient, then they don’t even care about the results of the test.

(Rwandan medical student) When they come open to learning and working together, that’s good. Sometimes they don’t try to understand the context of our patients, especially the cost of certain tests.

Lastly, there were moments of cultural differences in patient care, particularly in end-of-life care, that were challenging to navigate as a visiting learner. Despite preparation to be respectful and understanding, we acknowledge that contextual humility is difficult, even with awareness and effort. It requires practice, ongoing reinforcement from mentors and administrators and can improve over time.

Negotiating systemic barriers

Our data also shed light on many systemic barriers where HIC institutions can take direct action to mitigate inequities and promote fair global medical education exchanges. Quotes from Rwandan students regarding their aspirations to rotate in the USA shed light on what they feel would contribute the most to their learning as well as the greatest challenges anticipated by learners from LMICs in accessing international educational opportunities.

(Rwandan medical student) It’s so easy for you to come here, but it’s so hard for us to go to the U.S.…The problem is the visa. Since the U.S. is a racist country, it is very hard for people from Rwanda to obtain one. We have to provide extensive proof that we will not overstay. Also, it is so expensive to apply for the visa and purchase the flights.

Citing opportunities to engage in research not yet done in Rwanda, exposure to procedures and technologies, and garnering new ideas in patient care to bring home, the students from Rwanda strongly believe in the benefit of an opportunity to rotate at an HIC institution:

(Rwandan medical student) If I had the chance to do a rotation in the U.S., I would be able to observe and participate in a lot of research that is not done here in Rwanda.

(Rwandan medical student) I would like to learn about technology that you have in the U.S., like ultrasound…It would be a good experience to go somewhere new and see what it’s like there.

Many Rwandan students express a desire for greater access to rotations and educational experiences abroad; however, the exchange opportunities for students from Rwanda to HIC institutions are currently small compared with exchanges in the opposite direction, as demonstrated by the sheer number of learners from various HIC institutions at CHUK.

(Observation notes) So many universities all over the world are sending residents and students here, yet when I ask Rwandan students and residents where possible options of places [for them] to go, they don’t know of any concrete, established ones.

Even if opportunities arise and become established, these quotes from Rwandan students illustrate their perceived challenges related to rotating in an HIC: financial constraints and obtaining visas, including perceptions of discrimination and bias in the visa application process. Even after arrival, the systemic barriers that exist in HICs and their institutions prevent visiting students from acting with similar autonomy in their clinical learning as students from HICs are awarded at CHUK, an obstacle that must be ameliorated.31

Conclusion

This study captures Rwandan medical learners’ perspectives on exchanges between HIC institutions and CHUK. While specific to Dartmouth and CHUK, the findings may inform other HIC–LMIC collaborations. Effective global health practice requires collaboration, resource redistribution and addressing historical inequities. Electives should not serve as opportunities for HIC learners to ‘observe’ low-resource settings or gain disproportionate benefit, while LMIC learners bear the exchange’s burden. Prior research highlights the value of bidirectional partnerships and student pairing to promote mutual learning, cultural humility and sustained collaboration.4 9 11 30 Our findings reinforce this by highlighting LMIC learners’ desire for more equitable exchanges, with clearly defined roles, shared objectives and support for their own outbound opportunities. Moving in this direction not only improves the educational quality of global health electives but also aligns with broader calls for decolonising global health and centring equity and solidarity in cross-national collaborations.

To ensure equitable participation of students from HICs in global health endeavours, it is imperative to extend the standardised benchmarks employed in academic global health partnerships to the student level. This necessitates a commitment to reciprocal exchanges, resource redistribution, HIC abdication of privilege, collaborative engagement and longitudinal interaction with partners.17 32 33 Our ethnographic data show that these commitments need to exist on three levels: the student level, the global health curricular level, and the HIC institutional level. Effective preparation for students from HIC institutions embarking on global health experiences should encompass both mandatory and recommended components. First, students should engage in extensive predeparture learning, which includes gaining an understanding of their destination’s history, medical system structure, basics of the local language and relevant resource availability related to the local ‘environment of inequity,’ as discussed above. This foundational knowledge is crucial for navigating the complexities of global health settings and fostering respectful interactions with local communities.

HIC global health curricula should include teachings on colonial and neoliberal economic history, as well as antiracism and anticolonialism to deepen students’ understanding of health equity. Predeparture self-reflection sessions on privileges, identities and motivations can help students understand their impact, as well as develop greater sensitivity to the nuances of their language, behaviours and potential unintended consequences.34 Applying the ‘glocal’ model, we propose a predeparture requirement that students participate in a local or domestic health equity experience before pursuing travel abroad, ensuring that they understand the root causes of health disparities both globally and within their own communities.18 We advocate for ‘glocal’ rotations that immerse medical students in health equity work within their own national or regional contexts before participating in international experiences, as the principles of global health and health equity should be grounded in all geographic and national settings. We believe such early rotations enrich their education and help ensure that students’ motivations are rooted in a genuine commitment to health equity rather than a conscious or unconscious primary interest in travel.

Finally, it is proposed that clinical rotations in LMICs be reserved for students in their final year, preferably after completing a sub-internship and core rotations in all specialties. This will increase the likelihood that students have acquired the necessary clinical skills and knowledge to fully benefit from the experience, thereby minimising the burden to the local clinical team. To avoid potential harm to patients, students should be aware of what it means to practise within their scope.

We propose pairing first-year medical students interested in global health with peers from partnering institutions to cocreate research of mutual interest, with the student from the HIC using their resources. These pairs would also support each other during rotations at their respective institutions, promoting mutual learning. We acknowledge that, as the two main Dartmouth researchers, we did not have this partnering experience, which may have influenced the research process and results.

Institutions funding global health experiences must prioritise equity and reciprocity by supporting students from partner institutions, including covering travel, housing, research expenses and visa support. All visiting medical learners to Dartmouth are provided visa support and appropriate letters from our visa and immigration office, and all their costs are covered, including airfare, ground travel, lodging and stipends for meals and incidentals, as well as health insurance and medical malpractice insurance coverage. A balanced partnership requires fairness and collaboration, ideally with at least a one-to-one support ratio, if not more favourably toward LMIC institutions.

To apply these research findings to other institutional partnerships, it is crucial to explore these topics with relevant stakeholders, especially with input from curricular leaders from LMIC institutions to drive the specific policies and interventions. While these findings reveal themes applicable to many global health exchanges, generalisability could be enhanced through studies involving multiple institutions. We acknowledge that while these large-scale changes are not immediately feasible, we believe it remains an important ideal that institutions should actively strive toward. Given the historical and ongoing inequities in global health education, aiming for balanced, reciprocal exchanges—where LMIC learners have equitable access to opportunities—is a necessary corrective direction. Equity does not mean identical inputs from HIC and LMIC stakeholders, but rather a commitment to fair outcomes that address current and past imbalances and injustices, and HIC institutions, given their relative resource advantages, are well positioned to invest in more just and reciprocal partnerships—an important step in addressing historical imbalances.
