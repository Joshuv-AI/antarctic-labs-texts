Operative workflow characterization of the Hugo robotic-assisted surgery and da Vinci Multiport systems in left-sided colorectal surgery: a single-institution pilot analysis
Authors: Jae Hyun Park, Youngjin Kim, Min Jung Kim, Ji Won Park, Seung-Bum Ryoo, Kyu Joo Park, Seung-Yong Jeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe modular four-cart design of the Hugo robotic-assisted surgery (RAS) system raises a practical concern: does it impose a greater operative workflow burden than established platforms? We characterized operative workflow across the Hugo RAS and da Vinci Multiport systems, incorporating a concurrent mature-adoption Multiport cohort as an institutional maturity reference.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed 15 consecutive qualifying cases per cohort: Hugo RAS, initial da Vinci Multiport (de novo adoption), and recent da Vinci Multiport (mature adoption, concurrent with Hugo RAS). Primary endpoints were workflow time intervals across five phases derived from prospectively recorded timestamps. Secondary endpoints included 30-day complications, TME completeness, lymph node yield, and resection margin status.ResultsHugo RAS access phase (27 minutes; IQR, 20–55 minutes) was equivalent to both the initial (p > 0.999) and recent Multiport cohorts (p = 0.693). Nonsurgical time was equivalent between Hugo RAS and recent Multiport cohorts (45 vs. 40 minutes; p = 0.461), operated by the same team at identical institutional maturity. Workflow differences between Hugo RAS and initial Multiport were mirrored by equivalent differences between initial and recent Multiport cohorts—implicating institutional maturity rather than platform effects. TME completeness was 100% in all evaluable cases, and lymph node yield was equivalent across all cohorts (all pairwise p > 0.999).ConclusionThe Hugo RAS four-cart architecture did not impose a greater workflow burden than the da Vinci Multiport system under equivalent institutional conditions. Remaining differences reflected case-mix heterogeneity and asymmetric adoption maturity, supporting continued multi-platform robotic colorectal program development.

INTRODUCTION

Robotic-assisted colorectal surgery has evolved from an experimental technique to an established standard [1], yet for nearly two decades, it remained the domain of a single platform. The recent introduction of the Hugo robotic-assisted surgery (RAS) system (Medtronic) and the da Vinci Single-Port (SP) system (Intuitive Surgical) marks the first genuine diversification of this market, offering institutions architecturally distinct alternatives to the da Vinci Multiport system, which has long defined the field [2].

For institutions considering the Hugo RAS, its most distinctive feature is its modular four-cart design [3]. Unlike the da Vinci Multiport systems (Intuitive Surgical), in which all arms are attached to a single overhead boom during a single docking maneuver, the Hugo RAS requires four independent carts to be individually positioned, angulated, and locked around the patient before surgery can begin [4]. Early adopters have been candid about this challenge: the independent configuration of the carts increases the risk of external collisions if cart spacing is not strictly maintained, and the physical footprint requires a larger operating room (OR) layout compared with integrated platforms [5]. A recent comparative study reported longer nonsurgical, patient-independent time with the Hugo RAS compared to the da Vinci Xi system, potentially affecting cost-effectiveness [6]. Whether this finding is generalizable across institutions with varying levels of robotic experience remains an unanswered question.

Answering this question requires head-to-head workflow data from a single institution operating all three platforms—data that remain a critical gap in the robotic surgery literature. The few published Hugo RAS colorectal series are primarily single-platform reports focusing on initial feasibility [7]. Furthermore, any multi-platform institutional comparison faces inherent confounding, as the surgical team has already undergone the implementation processes of previous robotic platforms. This asymmetric institutional maturity systematically advantages later-adopted platforms in any efficiency comparison, yet it remains largely unaddressed in the colorectal literature. By analyzing granular operative phases across three cohorts—the Hugo RAS, initial da Vinci Multiport (representing de novo robotic program initiation), and recent da Vinci Multiport (representing mature institutional adoption, concurrent with Hugo RAS program initiation)—this study aims to address this gap.

We report the initial operative workflow experience of a single high-volume colorectal center with all three platforms, with perioperative safety and oncologic quality evaluated as secondary endpoints. The central research question is whether the Hugo RAS four-cart architecture imposes a greater OR setup burden than the established Multiport system in practice.

METHODS

Study design

This single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted at Seoul National University Hospital, a high-volume tertiary referral center for colorectal surgery. Three robotic platforms were sequentially adopted at this institution: the da Vinci Multiport system was introduced as the institution’s de novo robotic program, whereas the da Vinci SP and Hugo RAS were subsequently adopted into an already robotics-competent environment. The primary analysis compared three cohorts: Hugo RAS, initial da Vinci Multiport (reflecting de novo adoption), and recent da Vinci Multiport (reflecting mature institutional workflow, concurrent with Hugo RAS program initiation). A supplementary analysis comparing the Hugo RAS and da Vinci SP systems is presented in Supplementary Table 1. For each cohort, the first 15 consecutive qualifying cases were included. These cases comprised elective robotic left-sided colorectal resections, including anterior resection, low anterior resection, ultralow anterior resection, or intersphincteric resection. The initial da Vinci Multiport cohort underwent surgery between April 2015 and November 2018; the recent da Vinci Multiport and Hugo RAS cohorts underwent surgery concurrently between September 2025 and March 2026, representing the current level of institutional workflow maturity. Cases involving hybrid laparoscopic-robotic techniques or combined organ resections were excluded. Concomitant lateral pelvic lymph node dissection was retained because it is performed within the docked robotic interval and does not alter the platform-specific docking configuration.

Primary and secondary endpoints

The primary endpoint was operative workflow, characterized through five platform-specific time intervals derived from prospectively recorded institutional OR management timestamps: the pre-procedural phase (anesthesia start to surgical incision), access phase (incision to robotic docking), active robotic phase (docking to de-docking), closure phase (de-docking to wound closure), and post-procedural phase (wound closure to anesthesia end). Total operative time (incision to wound closure), total room time (anesthesia start to end), and nonsurgical time (pre-procedural plus post-procedural phases) were also reported. Secondary endpoints were 30-day perioperative safety outcomes and oncologic quality, the latter comprising total mesorectal excision (TME) specimen completeness, harvested lymph node count, proximal and distal resection margins, and circumferential resection margin (CRM) status.

An important technical note concerns the active robotic phase. All initial da Vinci Multiport cases required intraoperative re-docking to transition from the abdominal to the pelvic dissection phase. Because individual re-docking timestamps were not captured in the institutional OR management system, the active robotic phase for this cohort includes an undocumented setup interruption and is therefore not directly comparable to the uninterrupted single-dock intervals of the Hugo RAS and da Vinci SP cohorts.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and compared across the three primary cohorts using the Kruskal-Wallis test, with post hoc pairwise comparisons performed using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction. Categorical variables were compared pairwise using the Fisher exact test with Bonferroni correction (raw p-value multiplied by 3 and capped at >0.999). For the supplementary Hugo RAS vs. da Vinci SP analysis, continuous variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U-test and categorical variables using the Fisher exact test, without Bonferroni correction given the single pairwise comparison.

Baseline characteristics were compared using standardized mean differences (SMDs) instead of formal hypothesis testing, as the cohorts represented sequential adoption groups rather than randomly allocated comparators. Cohen’s d, calculated using the pooled standard deviation, was applied to continuous variables; the pooled proportion method was applied to binary variables; and Cohen’s w (based on the chi-square statistic) was applied to the nominal procedure-type variable. An SMD ≥0.2 was considered to indicate a potentially meaningful between-group imbalance. All analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A two-sided p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

A total of 45 patients were included across three cohorts of 15 consecutive cases each: Hugo RAS, initial da Vinci Multiport (initial dV MP), and recent da Vinci Multiport (recent dV MP). Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The three cohorts differed substantially in disease-related factors, reflecting the sequential, indication-driven nature of platform adoption rather than random allocation. Neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy was administered in 93% of initial dV MP cases compared with 47% in the Hugo RAS cohort and 27% in the recent dV MP cohort (SMDs 1.018 and 1.361, respectively), reflecting the initial Multiport program’s focus on locally advanced low rectal cancer. Tumor location mirrored this pattern: the median anal verge distance was 6 cm in the initial dV MP cohort vs. 12 cm in the Hugo RAS cohort and 17 cm in the recent dV MP cohort (SMDs 0.925 and 1.605 for Hugo vs. initial and initial vs. recent, respectively). Procedure-type distribution differed substantially across all three pairwise comparisons (Cohen’s w: Hugo vs. initial, 0.402; Hugo vs. recent, 0.432; initial vs. recent, 0.583). Notably, the initial dV MP cohort comprised exclusively low anterior resections and ultralow anterior resections or intersphincteric resections (LAR 87%, ULAR/ISR 13%), whereas the recent dV MP cohort included a substantial proportion of anterior resections (47%). Demographic variables, including age, sex, and body mass index, were broadly comparable across cohorts, with SMDs below 0.5 for most variables.

Operative workflow

Operative time intervals are summarized in Table 2 and illustrated in Fig 1. The primary interpretive principle is that these three cohorts represent fundamentally different institutional adoption contexts: the initial dV MP cohort was operated on during the institution’s de novo robotic program initiation, whereas the Hugo RAS and recent dV MP cohorts were introduced into an already robotics-competent environment and were operated on concurrently. All between-group comparisons must be interpreted considering both this the asymmetric maturity and the substantial case-mix heterogeneity described above. The access phase—the interval from surgical incision to robotic docking, reflecting the platform-specific docking maneuver—was equivalent across all three cohorts: Hugo RAS, 27 minutes (IQR, 20–55 minutes); initial dV MP, 25 minutes (IQR, 18–42 minutes); and recent dV MP, 25 minutes (IQR, 20–28 minutes) (Hugo vs. initial, p > 0.999; Hugo vs. recent, p = 0.693; initial vs. recent, p > 0.999). This equivalence between the Hugo RAS and both Multiport cohorts, despite the former requiring four independent cart-positioning steps compared with the latter’s single-boom docking maneuver, represents the central platform-specific finding of this study. The wide IQR of the Hugo RAS access phase (20–55 minutes), compared with that of the recent dV MP cohort (20–28 minutes), reflects ongoing case-to-case variability in cart configuration during early adoption—a team-level learning phenomenon distinct from the surgeon’s individual console learning curve. The pre-procedural phase was significantly longer in the initial dV MP cohort (54 minutes; IQR, 45–58 minutes) than in both the Hugo RAS cohort (40 minutes; IQR, 33–46 minutes; p = 0.095) and the recent dV MP cohort (35 minutes; IQR, 25–40 minutes; p < 0.001), whereas the Hugo RAS and recent dV MP cohorts did not differ significantly (p = 0.230). Nonsurgical time—the sum of the pre- and post-procedural phases and the metric most directly reflecting institutional OR management efficiency—followed the same pattern: the initial dV MP cohort (60 minutes; IQR, 55–68 minutes) was significantly longer than the Hugo RAS cohort (45 minutes; IQR, 38–52 minutes; p = 0.040) and the recent dV MP cohort (40 minutes; IQR, 30–45 minutes; p < 0.001), whereas the Hugo RAS and recent dV MP cohorts were equivalent (p = 0.461). The equivalence of Hugo RAS and recent dV MP nonsurgical time—two platforms operated by the same institutional team at the same level of maturity—provides the most direct evidence that the Hugo RAS four-cart architecture does not impose a materially greater OR management burden than a conventional Multiport system under equivalent conditions. Total room time and total operative time were both significantly shorter in the recent dV MP cohort than in the initial dV MP cohort (p < 0.001 and p = 0.008, respectively), whereas total room time was significantly longer in the Hugo RAS cohort than in the recent dV MP cohort (p = 0.033). The active robotic phase must be interpreted with caution as a platform comparison metric, as it is the interval most sensitive to procedural complexity. The initial dV MP cohort’s exclusively low and ultralow rectal case composition involved substantially more demanding pelvic dissection than the anterior resection–predominant recent dV MP cohort, thereby confounding any platform-level attribution. The active robotic phase did not differ significantly between the Hugo RAS and initial dV MP cohorts (p = 0.545) or between the Hugo RAS and recent dV MP cohorts (p = 0.655). However, it was significantly shorter in the recent dV MP cohort than in the initial dV MP cohort (120 minutes [IQR, 83–130 minutes] vs. 165 minutes [IQR, 125–187 minutes], p = 0.031), likely reflecting this case-mix difference. The post-procedural phase was significantly longer in the initial dV MP cohort than in both the Hugo RAS cohort (p = 0.003) and the recent dV MP cohort (p = 0.019), consistent with an institutional maturity effect.

Perioperative safety and oncologic outcomes

Perioperative and oncologic outcomes are summarized in Table 3. Given the substantial case-mix heterogeneity across cohorts, these findings are presented descriptively and should be interpreted with caution. Estimated blood loss (EBL) and hospital stay were significantly lower in the Hugo RAS and recent dV MP cohorts compared to the initial dV MP cohort (both p < 0.001), consistent with the more complex case composition of the latter. No significant differences were observed between Hugo RAS and recent dV MP in any perioperative outcome. TME completeness was 100% in all Hugo RAS and recent dV MP evaluable cases; lymph node yield was equivalent across all cohorts (all pairwise p > 0.999). CRM positivity rates were 23%, 29%, and 50% for Hugo RAS, initial dV MP, and recent dV MP, respectively (all pairwise p > 0.999), though the small recent dV MP evaluable denominator (n = 8) limits interpretation of this finding.

DISCUSSION

This study was motivated by a practical question: does the Hugo RAS four-cart modular architecture impose a meaningful OR workflow burden compared with established robotic platforms? The primary finding was that it did not. The Hugo RAS access phase was statistically equivalent to both the initial and recent da Vinci Multiport cohorts (p > 0.999 and p = 0.693, respectively), despite requiring four independent cart-positioning steps compared with the Multiport system’s single-boom docking maneuver. Notably, nonsurgical time was equivalent between the Hugo RAS and the concurrent recent dV MP cohort (p = 0.461). As both platforms were operated by the same institutional team during the same period, this comparison partially mitigates the institutional maturity confound inherent to sequential cohort designs, though nonsurgical time includes anesthesia-related intervals that may vary with patient characteristics independent of the robotic platform. The access phase, as a more architecture-specific interval, is discussed separately below. These findings directly address concerns raised by early Hugo RAS adopters regarding the practical workflow implications of the modular design [5,6] and extend the feasibility signals established by earlier single-platform series to the colorectal setting [7–10]. Before attributing any between-group differences to platform characteristics, two caveats must be considered. First, the three cohorts were profoundly heterogeneous in clinical composition. The initial dV MP cohort comprised exclusively low anterior resections and ultralow anterior resections, with near-universal neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (93%) and a median anal verge distance of 6 cm. The Hugo RAS cohort had the broadest procedure mix, including 27% ultralow anterior or intersphincteric resections. The recent dV MP cohort predominantly comprised anterior resections (47%), with a median anal verge distance of 17 cm and a low neoadjuvant therapy rate (27%). These differences reflect the sequential, indication-driven nature of platform adoption rather than random allocation and confound any direct between-group comparison. Second, the initial dV MP cohort was adopted during the institution’s de novo robotic program initiation—when OR teams, anesthesiologists, and nursing staff were simultaneously developing foundational robotic workflow competence—whereas the Hugo RAS and recent dV MP cohorts were introduced into an already robotics-competent environment [11,12]. This asymmetric institutional maturity introduced a directional bias that systematically disadvantaged the initial dV MP cohort across all efficiency metrics. The inclusion of a concurrent recent dV MP cohort represents the key methodological contribution of this study’s design. By operating Hugo RAS and recent dV MP cases during the same institutional period and by the same surgical team, the recent dV MP cohort serves as a contemporaneous mature-adoption reference, directly controlling for institutional maturity in a way that retrospective historical comparisons cannot. Prior multi-platform comparisons in the robotic colorectal literature have largely relied on sequential cohorts without explicitly accounting for this maturity effect [13,14]. The equivalence between the Hugo RAS and recent dV MP cohorts across the access phase and nonsurgical time is consistent with the interpretation that workflow differences between Hugo RAS and the initial dV MP cohort reflect institutional maturity rather than platform effects. EBL and hospital stay showed similar patterns, though these metrics are substantially influenced by case-mix composition and should not be interpreted as independent evidence of institutional maturity equivalence. The significant improvement in nearly all workflow metrics between the initial and recent dV MP cohorts—despite identical platform architecture—directly quantifies this maturity effect. The access phase warrants specific discussion as the interval most sensitive to architectural design. A docking learning curve for Hugo RAS has been documented in colorectal surgery, with cumulative sum analysis demonstrating a significant negative trend in docking times with increasing experience [9]. The wide IQR of the Hugo RAS access phase (20–55 minutes), compared with that of the recent dV MP cohort (20–28 minutes), reflects ongoing case-to-case variability in four-cart configuration during early adoption. This represents a team-level phenomenon—positioning four independent carts requires OR nursing staff to internalize procedure-specific tilt angles, inter-cart spacing, and collision-avoidance protocols through repetition [5,6]—and is therefore distinct from the surgeon’s console learning curve [11]. Published colorectal series report median Hugo RAS docking times of 10–14 minutes after a short standardization period [7,14], suggesting that the variability observed in our series represents a transient adoption-phase phenomenon rather than an inherent platform limitation. Institutions planning Hugo RAS adoption should therefore structure implementation programs to explicitly address team-level cart configuration training alongside surgeon simulation. It should be acknowledged that the access phase as defined in this study includes the interval from surgical incision to robotic docking and therefore incorporates trocar placement time in addition to the platform-specific docking maneuver. Trocar placement duration may vary according to patient characteristics, body habitus, and adhesion extent, and represents a source of within-phase variability that cannot be attributed to platform architecture. This is a recognized limitation of timestamp-based phase analysis and should be considered when interpreting access phase comparisons. The active robotic phase must be interpreted with particular caution as a platform comparison metric, as it is the interval most sensitive to procedural complexity. Low anterior resection and intersphincteric resection involve substantially more demanding pelvic dissection than anterior resection, thereby extending console time independently of platform mechanics [15]. The significantly shorter active robotic phase in the recent dV MP cohort than in the initial dV MP cohort (120 minutes vs. 165 minutes, p = 0.031)—despite identical platform architecture—is best explained by case-mix differences, underscoring the importance of procedure-matched comparison in robotic platform evaluation. Regarding secondary endpoints, oncologic quality was satisfactory and consistent across platforms, with 100% TME completeness in Hugo RAS and recent dV MP evaluable cases, equivalent lymph node yields, and CRM positivity rates comparable across cohorts—consistent with meta-analytic evidence that robotic TME achieves oncologic quality comparable to conventional laparoscopy [16]. Perioperative outcome differences between the initial and mature-adoption cohorts are primarily explicable by case-mix composition rather than platform-specific effects [17].

This study has several limitations. The asymmetric institutional adoption context introduced irreducible directional confounding, thereby precluding causal attribution of efficiency differences to platform characteristics alone. The profound case-mix heterogeneity limits direct outcome comparisons, particularly for the active robotic phase and total operative time. The da Vinci Multiport model (Si vs. Xi) was not consistently documented; therefore, cases were grouped as a single cohort based on architectural similarity. Intraoperative re-docking required in all initial dV MP rectal cases was not individually timestamped, introducing an unmeasured interruption into that cohort’s active robotic phase. The small CRM-evaluable denominator in the recent dV MP cohort (n = 8) limits interpretation of the 50% CRM positivity rate. Finally, the sample size of 15 cases per cohort is appropriate for adoption-phase characterization but insufficient for definitive safety or efficacy conclusions. Despite these limitations, this study provides the first single-institution comparative workflow analysis incorporating a concurrent mature-adoption Multiport reference cohort alongside a novel platform—a design that helps mitigate the institutional maturity confound that has limited the interpretability of prior multi-platform comparisons.

The Hugo RAS four-cart architecture did not impose a greater operative setup burden than the da Vinci Multiport system during initial adoption. TME completeness was 100%, and lymph node yield was equivalent across all three platforms, confirming that oncologic quality was maintained throughout. The remaining workflow differences are likely related to case-mix heterogeneity and asymmetric institutional maturity rather than platform-specific effects, though causal attribution is not possible given the non-randomized study design. Team-level cart configuration standardization represents the primary modifiable target for Hugo RAS workflow optimization. These pilot data support the continued development of multi-platform robotic colorectal programs.
