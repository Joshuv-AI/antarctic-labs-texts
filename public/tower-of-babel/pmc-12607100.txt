The effect of functional fatigue on lower-limb kinematics, balance, and proprioception in individuals with ACL-reconstruction and dynamic knee valgus
Authors: Mostafa Jalili Bafrouei, Seyed Hossein Mirkarimpour, Mohammadreza Seyedi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIndividuals with a history of anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) often experience altered biomechanics, such as dynamic knee valgus (DKV), which increases the risk of re-injury. Functional fatigue, resulting from prolonged physical exertion, can further compromise neuromuscular control, balance, and proprioception, exacerbating biomechanical risk factors. This study investigates the impact of functional fatigue on kinematics, balance, and proprioception in individuals with ACLR and DKV.MethodsThis experimental study used a mixed-model design with a between-group factor (fatigue vs. no-fatigue) and a within-subject factor (pre- vs. post-fatigue). Forty-four male participants with ACLR and DKV underwent a Shortened Cycle Fatigue Protocol. Assessments included lower-limb kinematics (LESS), dynamic balance (Y-Balance Test), and knee proprioception (joint repositioning error at 30° and 60°).ResultsFunctional fatigue led to significant worsening in lower-limb kinematics, dynamic balance, and knee proprioception in the experimental group. Specifically, post-fatigue assessments showed higher LESS scores (p < 0.001), decreased Y-Balance performance (p < 0.001), and increased joint repositioning error at 30° (p < 0.001) and 60° (p < 0.001) compared to their baseline. In contrast, the control group maintained stable performance across all measures, highlighting the impact of functional fatigue on biomechanical risk factors.ConclusionFunctional fatigue significantly impairs proprioception, alters lower extremity kinematics, and decreases dynamic balance in individuals with ACLR and DKV. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating fatigue management strategies into rehabilitation and training protocols to mitigate the risk of re-injury. Clinicians should consider incorporating proprioception training, dynamic balance exercises, and muscular endurance conditioning to mitigate fatigue-induced biomechanical impairments and promote safe return-to-sport practices.Keywords: ACL reconstruction, Dynamic knee valgus, Functional fatigue, Proprioception, Biomechanics

Introduction

The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) plays a critical role in providing stability to the knee joint, particularly during dynamic movements such as running, jumping, and pivoting [1–3]. ACL injuries are common in sports involving sudden direction changes or high-impact activities [4]. When the ACL is torn or ruptured, surgical intervention is often required to restore knee function, most commonly through ACL reconstruction (ACLR) [5]. The ACLR procedure aims to restore knee stability and function, allowing athletes to return to pre-injury activity levels [6]. However, many individuals experience long-term challenges, including decreased proprioception, balance deficits, anterior knee pain, and increased biomechanical risk factors [7], which may predispose them to secondary injuries, particularly involving the ACLR or surrounding structures [8].

Following ACL reconstruction, individuals often exhibit altered biomechanical patterns during functional movements [9]. These alterations include reduced knee flexion, increased knee valgus, and altered joint alignment, especially in dynamic activities such as landing from jumps or cutting maneuvers [6]. Dynamic knee valgus (DKV) is defined as an excessive medial angulation of the knee during dynamic tasks. DKV itself acts as an ACL injury risk factor [10]. DKV alters the moment arm of ground reaction forces, increasing knee abduction moments, internal rotation torques, and anterior tibial shear forces [11]. These stresses, particularly concentrated in the medial compartment and posterior tibial plateau, heighten ACL strain and explain the strong association between persistent DKV and re-injury risk [12].

All participants exhibited this characteristic before surgery. Postoperatively, it may potentially worsen due to muscular weakness and neuromuscular deficits, which could influence the study outcomes [13]. Additionally, proprioceptive deficits, which are commonly observed post-ACLR, further impair motor control and increase the likelihood of improper joint positioning and movement patterns [14]. Addressing these biomechanical risk factors is essential for reducing the risk of re-injury and optimizing functional outcomes in ACLR patients [15]. In athletes, the risk of subsequent injuries is potentially exacerbated by several interrelated factors, including kinesiophobia, deficits in neuromuscular activation, incomplete adherence to rehabilitation protocols, and early resumption of athletic activities [16]. These elements can synergistically impede optimal recovery and increase susceptibility to recurrent ACL injuries in this demographic [17]. Given the role of DKV in increasing ACL strain, factors that further compromise lower-limb neuromuscular control, such as muscle fatigue, may exacerbate biomechanical risks.

Functional fatigue, a state of diminished muscle performance due to prolonged physical exertion, has significant implications for biomechanical function [18]. As muscles become fatigued, their ability to generate force decreases, which may lead to compromised stability, altered movement patterns, and a higher risk of injury [19]. Fatigue can affect not only muscle strength and endurance but also neuromuscular control, leading to delayed reaction times, impaired proprioception, and increased joint instability [20]. Previous research has shown that fatigue may exacerbate biomechanical risk factors, such as knee valgus and altered landing mechanics [21], which are particularly concerning in individuals with a history of ACLR. Secondary ACL (SACL) injuries following reconstruction are significantly influenced by biomechanical impairments, dynamic instability, and proprioceptive deficits in the knee joint [22]. Damage to proprioceptive receptors and low-level fatigue tolerance can disrupt normal movement patterns during athletic activities [23], thereby increasing the likelihood of subsequent injuries; therefore, considering functional fatigue is a key role in secondary prevention of ACL injuries.

The primary aim of this research is to investigate the effects of functional fatigue on biomechanical risk factors, including kinematics patterns, balance, and proprioception, in individuals with a history of ACLR and dynamic knee valgus. While previous studies have explored the impact of fatigue and biomechanical risk factors in various sports injuries, few have specifically addressed the combined influence of functional fatigue on ACLR patients, particularly individuals with DKV, providing more information about the relationship between DKV and SACLR. Additionally, there is a lack of research examining how these factors contribute to secondary injuries or impairments in movement mechanics during rehabilitation, such as jumping, hopping, and running. By addressing this gap, this study aims to provide valuable insights into the management of functional fatigue and recovery strategies, to reduce the risk of re-injury in individuals post-ACLR, particularly those exhibiting dynamic knee valgus.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a mixed-model design, including a between-group factor (experimental group who underwent the fatigue protocol vs. control group who did not undergo the fatigue protocol) and a within-subject factor (pre-fatigue vs. post-fatigue assessments). Male participants with ACLR and DKV were recruited for both groups.

Participants

A total of 44 males ACLR with DKV participants, divided into two groups, the experimental group (N = 22) and the control group (N = 22), based on G-power with effect size f 0.60, α error 0.05, power 0.80, degree of freedom 6, and covariate 1, aged 25–35 years to ensure a homogeneous sample and minimize age-related confounding factors, who had undergone ACLR with autograft hamstring were the standard procedure performed by the clinic and surgeon involved in this study, and were active in sports both before and following surgery, were selected for this study. Female participants were not included due to availability at the study setting. Participants were purposively sampled based on their suitability for the study, with strict adherence to inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria: having DKV, male athletes aged 25–35 years, history of ACLR with hamstring autograft, at least 6 months post-surgery, no history of other knee injuries, normal BMI (18–25), and voluntary participation and informed consent. Exclusion criteria: previous surgeries on the lower extremities (excluding ACLR), injury occurring during the study, failure to complete one of the tests, and multi-ligament injuries or osteochondral damage.

Experimental protocol

To induce fatigue, the experimental group underwent a Short-Stretching Cycle Fatigue Protocol [24, 25] during the testing session. The protocol involved 10 sets of 10 repetitions of forward and backward jumps, with a 30-second rest interval between sets. The participant stood in a neutral position with feet shoulder-width apart, with hands placed on the iliac crest opposite the metal bar attached to the wall, which was positioned 10 cm away. A tape measure was placed beside the bar to ensure accuracy in recording maximum and reduced jump height. Our exercise regimen is tailored to elicit peripheral functional fatigue, a specific type of fatigue that contrasts with central and mental forms. Participants were verbally encouraged to exert maximum effort throughout the protocol.

Before starting the fatigue protocol, each participant’s maximum jump height was recorded by marking the highest point reached on the metal bar, considered as the participant’s peak jump (100% of their jump capacity). A sub-maximal jump was defined as 70% of their maximum jump height. The fatigue protocol was continued until the participant could no longer perform a jump beyond the sub-maximal threshold, signaling that the fatigue level had been reached, at which point the protocol was discontinued [24]. Following the fatigue protocol, the post-test assessments were immediately repeated to evaluate. The control group comprised ACLR participants with DKV who were assessed at the same time points as the experimental group, but did not perform the fatigue protocol.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome measures assessed lower-limb kinematics, jump-landing mechanics evaluated with LESS [26]. Dynamic balance, quantified through the Y-Balance test [27], was performed unilaterally on the involved limb. This test required maximal reach in anterior, posteromedial, and posterolateral directions while maintaining a single-leg stance. Proprioceptive acuity was measured via active joint repositioning tasks at 30° and 60° of knee flexion, with angular discrepancies (°) between target and replicated positions serving as the joint repositioning error [28].

Procedure

The DKV was initially assessed using the single-leg squat test [29]. To assess DKV, anatomical landmarks including the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS), midpoint of the patella, and midpoint between the medial and lateral malleoli were marked on the frontal plane. The DKV angle was measured by drawing two lines: one from the ASIS to the patella center and another from the patella center to the intermalleolar midpoint. Participants were positioned with arms crossed and trunk upright, then asked to flex their knees to 60°, guided by a goniometer (Model 12–1027, USA) placed laterally along the femoral-tibial axis. This procedure was repeated for the surgically treated limb, with the contralateral limb flexed to approximately 60° without contact or rotation. Frontal plane photographs (Canon camera, Japan) captured knee alignment during flexion. Each trial was performed three times, with the mean angle recorded as the final DKV angle. Participants with mean DKV angle values between 16.3° and 32.4° were included in the study [30].

Participants were then assessed for baseline lower-limb kinematics by LESS [26]. Moreover, dynamic balance and knee proprioception were assessed by the Y-balance test and knee repositioning error. Following group allocation, a pre-test assessment of the LESS was conducted for both groups. The LESS was administered and scored according to the standardized criteria proposed by Padua et al. [31], with higher scores reflecting poorer landing technique. The mean score of three jumps was recorded as the final score for each participant. Participants performed a jump from a 30 cm platform, landing approximately 50% of their height away from the platform, followed by an immediate vertical jump. During training, emphasis was placed on maximizing upward movement post-landing. No feedback was provided during testing unless the participant performed the jump incorrectly.

After the instruction, participants were allowed two practice jumps to familiarize themselves with the protocol, followed by three recorded jumps. Any jump not meeting the specified horizontal distance or failing to achieve maximum vertical jump post-landing was repeated. Two tripod-mounted cameras (Canon MD 255, Japan) captured frontal and sagittal views from distances of 4.8 and 4 m, respectively. After completing the baseline assessments, the functional fatigue protocol was applied, involving the jumping exercise to induce fatigue. Post-fatigue assessments were conducted immediately afterward to evaluate any changes in kinematics, dynamic balance, and proprioception. The order of the post-fatigue assessments was consistent for all participants: kinematics were assessed first, followed by dynamic balance and then proprioception tasks. This order was chosen to standardize testing, and assessments were performed immediately after the fatigue protocol to minimize the potential effects of recovery.

Data analysis

In the statistical analysis, the normality of the data (mean and standard deviation) was initially assessed using skewness and kurtosis tests. Subsequently, for inferential statistics, a one-way analysis of variance (repeated measure) and the Bonferroni post hoc test were employed. All analyses were conducted using SPSS statistical software version 27, with a significance level set at p ≤ 0.05.

Discussion

This study investigated the effects of functional fatigue on lower extremity biomechanics, including kinematics, balance, and proprioception, in individuals with a history of ACLR and DKV. The results revealed significant alterations in biomechanical risk factors, specifically lower-limb kinematics, balance, and knee proprioception following functional fatigue. Given the high risk of re-injury in individuals with ACL reconstruction and dynamic knee valgus, these findings are of particular concern. Functional fatigue exacerbated key risk factors associated with re-injury, including impaired proprioception and deteriorated dynamic control, as evidenced by LESS and Y-Balance performance. The experimental group showed a marked decline in joint position sense and neuromuscular control, both of which are essential for injury prevention in this high-risk population.

The observed decline in knee proprioception is particularly concerning for individuals with a history of ACL reconstruction (ACLR). It is well-documented that ACLR patients often experience deficits in afferent signaling and alterations in mechanoreceptor function due to surgical trauma and ligament injury. These neurophysiological changes may lead to an inherently diminished capacity to perceive joint position, even before fatigue begins to set in. This reduction in joint position sense highlights the crucial role of proprioception in maintaining joint stability, particularly during dynamic activities. As fatigue further compromises proprioceptive acuity, the risk of improper loading patterns and joint misalignment increases, which, in turn, raises the likelihood of ACL re-injury [32]. Fatigue affects the activation patterns of the quadriceps and hamstrings, leading to impaired dynamic knee joint stability [33].

Additionally, fatigue may alter muscle stiffness and reduce force production, diminishing the muscle’s capacity to act as an effective energy absorber and dynamic stabilizer [34], which directly contributes to the observed kinematics errors (higher LESS scores). This can result in greater knee valgus joint angles and increased internal joint moments, predisposing individuals to knee injuries. The results of this study align with previous research indicating that fatigue increases the likelihood of joint misalignment, such as DKV, which has been associated with ACL injury [35]. Furthermore, the observed reductions in dynamic balance scores post-fatigue underline the importance of preserving neuromuscular control during high-intensity tasks, as fatigue disrupts the body’s ability to stabilize the knee joint, decreases hamstrings/quadriceps co-contraction, and uses a visual-motor strategy rather than a sensory-motor strategy [36].

In the present study, functional fatigue significantly increased LESS scores in the experimental group, indicating poorer landing mechanics and greater kinematic errors. This finding highlights that fatigue compromises the ability of the lower extremity to maintain proper alignment and shock absorption during high-demand tasks [37]. Our results are consistent with previous studies reporting that fatigue exacerbates dynamic knee valgus and increases the likelihood of unsafe loading patterns associated with ACL injury risk [38, 39]. Significantly, while the control group maintained stable performance, the experimental group showed a clear deterioration post-fatigue, underscoring the critical role of fatigue management in preserving landing mechanics in individuals with ACLR and DKV.

Dynamic balance, as assessed by the Y-Balance Test, also declined significantly after fatigue in the experimental group, whereas the control group remained stable. This deterioration suggests that fatigue disrupts neuromuscular control strategies required for maintaining single-leg stance stability [3]. Similar studies have shown that fatigue reduces the efficiency of lower-limb muscle co-contraction and shifts reliance toward visual feedback rather than proprioceptive input, leading to impaired balance performance [40]. Given that balance deficits are strongly associated with recurrent injuries, the current findings emphasize that ACLR individuals with DKV may be especially vulnerable to fatigue-related balance impairments during sport-specific activities.

The decline in knee joint position sense observed in this study further reinforces the adverse impact of fatigue on proprioceptive acuity. Errors in joint repositioning at both 30° and 60° of flexion suggest that fatigue compromises the sensorimotor system’s ability to provide accurate afferent feedback, which is crucial for dynamic stability [18]. These results align with prior evidence indicating that ACLR individuals have baseline proprioceptive deficits due to altered mechanoreceptor function, and that fatigue magnifies these impairments [33]. By combining proprioceptive deficits with impaired landing mechanics and reduced dynamic balance, fatigue appears to create a cumulative risk profile that may place the ACL graft under substantial mechanical stress.

The present study emphasizes the need for incorporating fatigue management strategies in rehabilitation and athletic training programs. Given the significant changes in lower-limb kinematics, knee proprioception, and dynamic balance following functional fatigue, it is crucial to develop interventions aimed at reducing these adverse effects. Training programs that emphasize muscular endurance, proprioceptive training, and the restoration of joint stability are essential to mitigating fatigue-related biomechanical risks, particularly in athletes with a history of ACLR or DKV.

Limitations of the study and clinical implications

While this study provides valuable insights into the impact of functional fatigue on lower-limb biomechanics in individuals with ACLR and DKV, there are some limitations that should be considered. First, the study only included male participants, which limits the generalizability of the findings to female athletes, who may experience different fatigue responses and biomechanical alterations. Future studies should aim to include both male and female participants to explore sex-specific differences in fatigue-induced biomechanical changes. Second, the fatigue protocol applied in this study may not fully replicate the fatigue experienced during sport-specific activities. Although the protocol was designed to induce significant fatigue, it may not have captured the complexities of fatigue encountered in real-world conditions. Further research should aim to develop more ecologically valid fatigue protocols that mimic the demands of sports and activities most relevant to ACLR and DKV populations. Third, electromyography (EMG) was not utilized to directly measure neuromuscular changes. Including EMG could have provided more direct insights into muscle activation patterns and neuromuscular control, thereby strengthening the interpretation of the biomechanical findings. Additionally, while this study focused on a single session of fatigue, it remains unclear how long the biomechanical impairments last or whether these changes accumulate over time. Longitudinal studies are needed to investigate the long-term effects of functional fatigue on joint health and injury risk, especially in athletes undergoing rehabilitation.

The results of this study have important clinical implications for the rehabilitation and training of athletes with ACLR and DKV. Given the significant impact of functional fatigue on joint proprioception, kinematics, and dynamic balance, clinicians and coaches should consider incorporating fatigue management strategies into training and rehabilitation protocols. This may include: proprioception training, muscular endurance training, dynamic balance training, and fatigue monitoring. These strategies may help reduce the biomechanical risks associated with functional fatigue, facilitating a safer return to sport and minimizing the likelihood of re-injury in this vulnerable population.

Conclusion

This study highlights the significant effects of functional fatigue on lower extremity biomechanics in individuals with a history of ACLR and DKV. Functional fatigue was found to impair knee proprioception, alter lower-limb movement patterns, and decrease dynamic balance, all of which increase the biomechanical risk for ACL re-injury. These findings underscore the importance of integrating fatigue management strategies in rehabilitation and training programs to mitigate the adverse effects of fatigue and promote safer movement patterns. Further research is needed to explore the long-term consequences of fatigue and to develop more comprehensive training protocols for this population.
