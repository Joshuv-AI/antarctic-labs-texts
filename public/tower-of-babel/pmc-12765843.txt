A triple-action PROTAC for wild-type p53 cancer therapy
Authors: Gregory H. Bird, Utsarga Adhikary, Michael J. Schmidt, Marina Godes, Bethany Tesar, Christina M. Camara, Joao A. Paulo, Julia F. Vidlak, Thomas M. DeAngelo, Marilyn Marquez, Prafulla Gokhale, Ruitong Li, Shannan J. Ho Sui, Michael D. Cameron, Steven P. Gygi, Loren D. Walensky
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryDespite the central role of p53 suppression in cancer pathogenesis, the promise of therapeutic p53 reactivation remains unrealized, with targeted and combination chemotherapies limited by efficacy, toxicity, and delivery. To overcome these challenges, we introduce a triple-action proteolysis targeting chimera (TAPTAC) that simultaneously targets three oncogenic mechanisms to reactivate apoptosis. TAPTAC1 diverts HDM2 from degrading p53 to eliminating oncogenic targets such as BET proteins, while also blocking HDMX-mediated sequestration, thereby maximizing p53 reactivation in concert with cancer protein degradation. TAPTAC1 outperforms combination treatments and PROTACs that target HDM2 and BET proteins, but not HDMX, and is broadly effective in wild-type (WT) p53 cancers, including mouse models of osteosarcoma and leukemia. Importantly, TAPTAC1 leverages cancer dependency on HDM2 to enhance selectivity and mitigate toxicity. With WT p53 retained in 90% of pediatric and 50% of adult cancers, TAPTACs provide a therapeutic platform for addressing key limitations of prior anti-cancer strategies.

Introduction

The landmark discovery that a proteolysis targeting chimera (PROTAC) could be used to recruit an endogenous E3 ubiquitin ligase to degrade a pathologic target via the ubiquitin-proteasomal pathway (UPS) ushered in a new era of drug development based on eliminating rather than inhibiting disease-causing proteins.1 This mechanism circumvents traditional challenges associated with blocking protein function, particularly for proteins deemed “undruggable” by conventional small molecules. Over the past two decades, the field has witnessed exponential growth, with a diverse array of PROTACs being developed to target a wide spectrum of proteins implicated in various diseases, notably cancer.2 PROTAC-DB, an online database that tracks PROTAC development, lists 6,111 PROTACs for well over 950 protein targets.3 More than three dozen PROTACs have either reached the investigational new drug (IND) phase or advanced to phase 1, 2, and 3 of clinical testing, with companies large and small joining the fray to tackle disease areas ranging from autoimmunity to cancer.4 The potential clinical impact is profound, which is fueling investment and driving an explosion of the PROTAC field.

Despite the promise of PROTACs, this drug modality embodies a series of drawbacks. The size of the chimeric molecules typically violates Lipinski’s rule-of-five5 and can limit cellular penetrance and thus drug delivery.6 Among the challenges in design are orienting the two components, which are typically joined by a linker of variable length and composition, in a manner that not only forms the desired ternary complex but also enables target ubiquitination. Dosing the PROTACs too high can result in the hook effect, whereby unproductive binary rather than ternary complexes are formed, thus limiting the range of dosing that can be applied to achieve therapeutic activity.7 Another key consideration, sometimes overlooked, is whether degrading the target actually provides an advantage over inhibiting the target, both with respect to safety and efficacy. Finally, the clinical impact of degrading a single target (or class of targets) may be insufficient as monotherapy, particularly in the context of cancer where multiagent regimens are the norm.

Here, we aimed to create an engine for PROTAC development based on harnessing our stapled peptide (SP) technology that restores bioactive alpha-helical structure to isolated peptides, confers striking proteolytic resistance, and when appropriately designed, enables cellular uptake to achieve on-mechanism pharmacologic activity in vitro and in vivo.8,9,10,11 We chose a potent and cell-permeable stapled p53 peptide as our PROTAC subcomponent owing to its capacity to target HDM2 and HDMX, which suppress p53 by degradation or sequestration, respectively.10,12 For the second component, we chose the small molecule JQ1 due its foundational role in the PROTAC field, especially in the context of the JQ1-thalidomide conjugate dBET1,13 and the regulatory interplay between the BET and p53 pathways in the nucleus.14,15 The resultant TAPTAC, designed to deliver a 3-in-1 mechanism of action by blocking HDM2 and HDMX to maximally reactivate p53-mediated tumor suppression and simultaneously degrading a master regulator of oncogenic signaling (Figure 1A), achieved striking on-mechanism anti-cancer efficacy.Figure 1Design and in vitro activity of TAPTAC1(A) A chimera composed of a stapled p53 peptide and a small molecule BET protein inhibitor was designed to achieve triple-action targeting of HDM2, HDMX, and BET proteins to maximally restore p53 while hijacking HDM2 to degrade an oncogenic driver (BET proteins) instead of a tumor suppressor (p53).(B) Chemical composition of TAPTAC1, which links the BET inhibitor JQ1 to the stapled p53 peptide via a lysine-βAla moiety installed at position 25 of the p53 transactivation helix.(C) MD simulations demonstrate the assembly of a ternary complex between TAPTAC1 and the respective JQ1- and p53-binding domains of BRD4 and HDM2.(D) TAPTAC1 effectively generated ternary complexes (green) between the BD1 domain of BRD4 and HDM2 (left) and HDMX (right). Results

Design and biochemical characterization of TAPTAC1

We designed a prototype TAPTAC by combining an optimized analog of the clinical-grade stapled p53 peptide ALRN-6924,16 bearing an E21A mutation for enhanced potency, and a Q25K mutation for insertion of a β-alanine residue that links to the small molecule BET inhibitor JQ117 (Figure 1B). Molecular dynamics simulations of TAPTAC1 revealed the alpha-helical nature of the p53 peptide component, stabilized by the i, i+7 all-hydrocarbon staple, and the outward projection of the JQ1-bearing linker on the opposite face of the peptide’s HDM2/HDMX interaction surface; the simulations also demonstrate the ability of TAPTAC1 to form a ternary complex with the BD1 domain of BRD4 (BRD4BD1) and the p53-binding region of HDM2 through a series of key contacts (Figures 1C and S1; Video S1). Size exclusion chromatography (SEC) revealed TAPTAC1-mediated ternary complex formation, evidenced by a shifted elution profile of BRD4BD1 with HDM2 or HDMX upon co-incubation (Figure 1D). Whereas HDM2 effectively polyubiquitinates p53, but not BRD4BD1-BD2, the addition of TAPTAC1 switched the targeting specificity of HDM2, resulting in polyubiquitylation of BRD4BD1-BD2 rather than p53 (Figure 1E). These data demonstrate that TAPTAC1 not only nucleates the desired ternary complexes but also transforms HDM2 from an oncoprotein that targets p53 to a tumor suppressor that instead polyubiquitylates a BET oncoprotein.

TAPTAC1 reactivates the p53 pathway and induces BET protein degradation in cells

To evaluate cellular activity, we compared the effect of TAPTAC1 (3-in-1 activity), its F19A point mutant that abrogates interaction with HDM2/HDMX, and A1874,18 a small-molecule PROTAC that links the selective HDM2 inhibitor RG7388 to JQ1 (2-in-1 activity) (Figure 2A). In SJSA-1 osteosarcoma cells that overexpress HDM2, TAPTAC1 has only modestly enhanced activity compared to A1874, with F19A mutagenesis weakening TAPTAC1 by two orders of magnitude, reflective of residual BET-inhibitor function (Figure 2B). In SJSA-X cells that overexpress HDM2 and HDMX, TAPTAC1 outperforms A1874 by more than an order of magnitude, with F19A mutagenesis again eliminating HDM2/HDMX targeting (Figure 2C). To link TAPTAC1’s effect on viability to p53 and BET pathway modulation, SJSA-X cells were treated with 100-nM TAPTAC1, and BRD4, p53, p21, HDM2, and HDMX levels were monitored over time by western blot. We observed that (1) BRD4 becomes undetectable by 8 h after treatment; (2) p53 levels increase over time, peak at 12 h, and normalize by 24 h (consistent with its negative feedback loop); (3) p53 transcriptional targets p21 and HDM2 surge by 24 h; and (4) HDMX levels remain elevated and increase by 24 h (Figure 2E). These protein dynamics are consistent with the anticipated mechanism of action (Figure 1A). We confirmed that (1) TAPTAC1’s degrader activity extends to other BET proteins including BRD2 and BRD3 (Figure S2A), (2) TAPTAC1-induced BET degradation is blocked by proteasome inhibition (Figure S2B), and (3) TAPTAC1 targeting appears to disrupt HDM2/HDMX complex formation (Figure S2C), which could contribute to drug-induced elevation of HDMX in SJSA-X cells (like p53, HDMX is a substrate of HDM2 E3-ligase activity19). Analogous dynamics were observed in JEG-3 choriocarcinoma cells (Figure S2D). To expand the analysis to the proteome, we performed quantitative proteomic analyses on lysates from SJSA-X cells treated with 1 μM TAPTAC1 for 24 h. BET proteins BRD2–4 were among the most downregulated, and p53 pathway proteins, including p53, p21, HDM2, and HDMX, were among the most upregulated, as compared to vehicle-treated cells (Figure 2F; Tables S1A and S2). The top hits from GO pathway analyses included p53 pathway signatures (Figure S2E), and hallmark analyses highlighted p53 pathways as among the most upregulated pathways and G2M checkpoint and Myc targets the most downregulated (Figures S2F–S2H).

We next examined whether the chimera provides a benefit beyond combination treatment. Whereas A1874 is not more effective than treatment with its parts, RG7388 and JQ1 (Figure 3A), TAPTAC1 is 30-fold more potent than the SP/JQ1 combination (Figure 3B). TAPTAC1 is also 10- to >100-fold more potent than a cereblon (CRBN)-based BET-protein degrader, dBET6,20 and the individual JQ1 and SP components of TAPTAC1, respectively (Figure 3C). TAPTAC1’s p53 dependence is likewise evident in comparing treatment responses in isogenic WT and p53−/− HCT116 cells (Figure 3D). Broad spectrum anti-cancer activity of TAPTAC1 in WT p53 cells

We performed Profiling Relative Inhibition Simultaneously in Mixtures (PRISM) analysis21 to expand TAPTAC1 testing across >900 genetically characterized cancer lines. TAPTAC1 exhibited striking potency (Figure 4A; Table S3), with melanoma, skin, kidney, soft tissue, hematopoietic, and ovarian cancers among the most susceptible (Figures 4B and S3A). Correlation of gene expression with TAPTAC1 sensitivity revealed strong enrichment for p53 transcriptional targets that mediate cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis, including EDA2R, FDXR, CDKN1A (p21), BAX, and BBC3 (PUMA), as well as HDM2 itself (Figure 4C). Conversely, decreased expression of CDKN2A and CDKN2B also correlated with TAPTAC1 sensitivity, consistent with loss of upstream restraints on HDM2 through direct inhibition by ARF (CDKN2A) and indirect RB-E2F-HDM2 signaling (CDKN2B). These findings indicate that TAPTAC1 activity converges on a transcriptional program essential for p53-driven cell death. CRISPR-based gene dependency analyses supported this mechanism. Sensitivity to TAPTAC1 was most dependent on HDM2, HDM4, PPM1D, USP7, and DDX31, factors that normally antagonize or restrain p53 activity through ubiquitination, degradation, or checkpoint dampening, highlighting that TAPTAC1 susceptibility hinges on the removal of these brakes (Figure 4D). Conversely, resistance emerged upon loss of TP53 itself or key mediators that otherwise stabilize and propagate its apoptotic and checkpoint functions, including TP53BP1, USP28, CDKN1A (p21), and CHEK2, further reinforcing that TAPTAC1 requires a functional p53 signaling axis to drive cancer cell death (Figure 4D). The far-and-away best genetic predictor of cancer cell susceptibility to TAPTAC1 was p53 status (Figures 4E; Table S4A), with cancer cells that retain WT p53 demonstrating an average area under the (viability) curve (AUC) of 0.66, whereas those with mutant or deleted p53 having an average AUC of 0.90 (Figure 4F and Table S4B). To validate the PRISM findings, we tested p53 WT ovarian (OVTOKO), choriocarcinoma (JEG-3), and leukemia (EOL-1) cell lines and observed nanomolar susceptibility that eclipsed the activity of A1874 (Figures 4G–4I), suggesting that maximal p53 reactivation by dual HDM2/HDMX targeting is an important attribute of TAPTAC1. Indeed, TAPTAC1 sensitivity strongly correlated with gene dependency scores for both HDM2 and HDMX for these cell lines (Table S1B). We extended our analyses to primary leukemia cells from newly diagnosed pediatric patients with WT p53 T-ALL and again observed greater activity for TAPTAC1 compared to A1874 (Figures 4J–4L).

To interrogate the mechanistic basis for TAPTAC1 superiority over A1874, we performed PRISM analysis of A1874 (Figure 5A; Table S5) and comparative bioinformatics analyses using PRISM AUC and DepMap 24Q4 datasets, including dBET6 as a further comparator. In p53 WT cell lines, TAPTAC1 sensitivity correlates with genetic dependencies on both HDM2 and HDMX, A1874 with HDM2 alone, and dBET6 with neither, confirming the drugs’ distinct targeting profiles (Figure 5B). The differential dependencies are lost in p53 mutant lines, underscoring their reliance on WT p53 (Figure 5C). Using the PRISM outputs and ATLANTIS conditional-inference forest models, we compared biomarker importance scores across the three compounds and ranked features by variability (standard deviation) of their importance values to identify markers distinguishing TAPTAC1 sensitivity. The top 20 biomarkers with the highest variability in feature importance across the three compound screens highlight the p53 regulatory network, including TP53 mutations, HDM2/HDMX/PPM1D dependencies, and expression of p53 target genes, including CDKN1A, EDA2R, FDXR, and BAX (Table S1C). Collectively, these results designate the p53 pathway, including HDMX, as the defining biomarker axis that explains TAPTAC1’s superior efficacy profile. Selectivity of action of TAPTAC1 for cancer vs. non-transformed cells

Given the toxicity observed for HDM2 and BET inhibitors in vivo,22,23 we examined the selectivity of TAPTAC1. We found that TAPTAC1 has comparatively little effect on the viability of non-transformed human cells, such as RPTEC and HUVEC (Figure 5E). To further probe TAPTAC1’s cancer-selective activity, we treated C6 (glioma) and DI-TNC1 (astrocyte) rat cells and monitored cell viability, again observing that the cancer cell is uniquely susceptible to TAPTAC1 (Figure 5F). Quantitative proteomic analysis revealed that of the 9,375 proteins queried, 22 were significantly differentially abundant at a threshold of -log10 p value > 2 and |log2FC| > 2 upon TAPTAC1 treatment of C6 cells, with HDM2 scoring as the third most upregulated protein and BRD2–4 proteins among the top four most downregulated proteins; using the same threshold, no proteins were significantly differentially abundant upon TAPTAC1 treatment of DI-TNC1 cells (Figures 5G and 5H; Tables S6 and S7). TAPTAC1 had little effect on DI-TNC1 protein dynamics even at 10× the C6 dose, whereas C6 cells exhibited BET downregulation (including E2F and Myc targets), p53 pathway reactivation, and cell death (Figures 5F–5H and S3B–S3D; Table S1D), consistent with TAPTAC1 exploiting cancer dependencies to achieve selective activity.

In vivo efficacy of TAPTAC1 in mouse models of solid and liquid tumors

In preparation for in vivo testing, we tested TAPTAC1 in plasma stability, plasma protein binding, and pharmacokinetic analyses. TAPTAC1 was remarkably stable in both mouse and human plasma, with no evidence of degradation even after 4 h (Figure 6A). Mouse plasma protein binding measured after a 6-h incubation was 97.3% bound and 2.7% free (Table S8A). We initially selected 10 mg/kg IV daily dosing for in vivo testing, since the Cmax was 1,000-fold above the SJSA-X IC50. SJSA-X tumors (∼400 mm3; n = 5/arm) grew steadily in vehicle-treated mice but regressed throughout the 7-day TAPTAC1 treatment period (Figure 6D). On day 7, excised tumors showed a marked size difference between vehicle and TAPTAC1 groups (Figure 6E). Quantitative proteomic analysis revealed a dramatic shift in the protein expression landscape upon TAPTAC1 treatment. The most downregulated proteins included BRD4, CXCR4, HGF, LDLRAD4, APOM, and other oncogenic, angiogenic, and proliferative regulators, together with metabolic enzymes such as CES1 and APBA2 (Figure 6F; Tables S1E and S9). Among the most upregulated proteins was MDM4, in addition to structural muscle genes (MYBPC1, MYOT, MYOM3, ACTN2, and CSRP3), metabolic enzymes (ALDOB, GPD1, and BHMT), myelin- or nerve-associated genes (MPZ, PRX, and ADGRL2), and stress response regulators (HSPB7 and MT1L) (Figure 6F; Tables S1E and S9). This composite signature indicates that residual tissue after 7 days of TAPTAC1 treatment is largely devoid of tumor and instead dominated by host stroma, muscle, and peripheral nerve, reflecting the collapse of osteosarcoma-specific programs and replacement by host expression patterns.

To assess dose responsiveness, tolerance, and durability, we repeated the SJSA-X study using 0.3, 1, and 3 mg/kg daily IV dosing (n = 5/arm) and extended treatment beyond 7 days. Over 20 days, TAPTAC1 showed dose-dependent tumor suppression and corresponding survival benefit, with all vehicle and 0.3 mg/kg-treated animals expiring by day 12 and all 1 mg/kg-treated animals by day 17 (Figure 6G). Notably, the effective doses are 10–100× lower than those reported for JQ1 or stapled p53 peptide monotherapy in comparable models.12,17 We next evaluated whether less frequent TAPTAC1 dosing could sustain efficacy, administering 3 mg/kg twice weekly (Mondays and Thursdays). Even with this reduced schedule, TAPTAC1 produced marked tumor suppression in a 37-day study, with animals maintaining weight and exhibiting a clear survival advantage over vehicle controls (Figures 6H–6J).

We next extended our in vivo studies to models of hematologic malignancy, starting with EOL-1 acute myeloid leukemia,24 identified by PRISM as a WT p53-bearing, HDM2/HDMX/BET-expressing, TAPTAC1-sensitive line. NSG mice (n = 4/arm) received tail vein injections of EOL-1 cells (2 × 106) and began daily IV treatment with vehicle or 2 mg/kg TAPTAC1 on day 7. Whereas vehicle-treated mice developed marked leukocytosis (>100K white blood cells [WBCs]) and profound anemia, TAPTAC1 treatment maintained WBCs below 100K, reduced percent circulating blasts, and preserved percent neutrophil counts (Figures S4A–S4C; Table S8C). All vehicle-treated animals died between days 4 and 6, while all TAPTAC1-treated mice survived the 7-day course (Figure S4D). To compare equivalent endpoints, a second study (n = 6/arm) initiated treatment earlier (day 4) and again showed lower WBCs, reduced percent blasts, and higher percent neutrophils in TAPTAC1-treated mice (Figures S4E and S4F).

Finally, we compared the in vivo activity of TAPTAC1, a peptide-scrambled TAPTAC1 mutant (Figures S4G and S4H), and A1874 in a murine leukemia model that enabled non-invasive, longitudinal monitoring of leukemic burden by bioluminescence imaging (BLI), while avoiding species-specific biases in drug tolerance that can arise when targeting human cancers in mice. We employed luciferized p185+ Arf−/− murine B-ALL cells, which retain WT p53 but lack the Arf tumor suppressor, thereby releasing the HDM2 brake and stabilizing HDMX to create a stringent p53-suppression context well suited for comparative assessment of TAPTAC1.25,26,27 Twice-weekly IV dosing led to marked suppression of leukemia progression by TAPTAC1, with A1874 and the mutant TAPTAC1 having little to no effect, essentially tracking with vehicle-treated mice (Figures 7A–7C). TAPTAC1-treated animals maintained their weight relative to the other treatment arms and achieved a survival advantage (Figures 7D and 7E). The same TAPTAC1 dosing regimen applied to immunocompetent mice showed little to no effect on complete blood counts or serum chemistries (Tables S8D and S8E). Discussion

p53 suppression is a common mechanism by which cancer resists treatment. Although p53 mutagenesis and deletion are well-known cancer adaptations for enforcing cellular immortality,28 particularly in the face of chemotherapy and radiation, p53 suppression through protein interaction, predominantly by HDM2 and HDMX, is far more common.29,30,31,32 Indeed, the overwhelming majority of anti-cancer regimens directly rely on, or could benefit from, the activation of p53-mediated apoptosis to achieve therapeutic efficacy.33 Nevertheless, the promise of targeted, pharmacologic reactivation of p53 has yet to be realized. Decades-long efforts to develop p53-reactivating therapeutics have faced significant challenges, such as the hematopoietic toxicity22 and HDMX-mediated resistance,10,34 which have thwarted small molecule inhibitors of HDM2 in clinical trials.

The development of all-hydrocarbon stapling to fortify the bioactive structure of protein interaction motifs,9,35 such as the p53 transactivation helix that mediates p53 binding to HDM236 and HDMX,37 found application in the design and testing of all-hydrocarbon stapled p53 peptides as p53-reactivating agents.10,38 ALRN-6924 (sulanemadlin), a dual HDM2/HDMX targeting stapled p53 peptide that advanced to clinical testing in adults and children, demonstrated safety and preliminary efficacy in a phase 1 trial, with striking responses in select patients.39 Although ALRN-6924 was not advanced further due to generally modest activity in limited clinical trials and a failed pivot from treating WT p53 cancers to chemoprotection in p53-null cancers,16 we sought to more effectively harness dual HDM2/HDMX targeting for its original intent—restoring p53-mediated apoptosis in treatment-resistant cancer. In marrying stapled p53 peptide and PROTAC technologies, we envisioned a synergistic benefit from (1) maximal p53 reactivation through dual HDM2/HDMX targeting and (2) simultaneous recruitment of HDM2, an oncogenic E3 ligase, to degrade collaborating oncogenic drivers, such as BET proteins, in WT p53 cancers. Here, we find that our prototype triple-action PROTAC, TAPTAC1, can restore the p53 pathway and pharmacologically transform HDM2 from an oncogene into a tumor suppressor by degrading BET proteins instead of p53. As a stapled peptide-small molecule chimera, TAPTAC1 is at least an order of magnitude more potent than its individual drug components or a small molecule PROTAC that shares the HDM2 and BET targeting features of TAPTAC1 but is unable to engage HDMX. Notably, TAPTAC1 is toxic to cancer cells but spares non-transformed cells. This selectivity may stem not only from normal cells being less dependent on aberrant p53 suppression and BET signaling for survival but also from the advantage of engaging an overexpressed, oncogenic E3 ligase rather than a homeostatic one for oncoprotein degradation. The on-mechanism activity of TAPTAC1 translates to the in vivo context, demonstrating efficacy in mouse models of both solid and liquid cancers bearing WT p53.

A cardinal feature of relapsed and treatment-resistant cancers is a combination of deregulated signal transduction that drives cancer cell proliferation and blocks cancer cell death.40 Thus, multiagent treatment that addresses multiple cancer-causing pathways is required to achieve remission and cures. However, advancing new anti-cancer drug candidates in combination is especially challenging. First, concerns about unanticipated toxicities have long led regulators to require clinical testing of experimental therapeutics as single agents. Second, identifying effective combinations and then brokering clinical testing agreements that bring companies and their proprietary drugs together remains a formidable, logistical barrier. Finally, individual drugs have distinct pharmacologic properties that from a practical standpoint can thwart the delivery of both agents to the same cancer cell at the same time. By combining stapled p53 peptide and PROTAC technologies, the TAPTAC platform creates a single agent capable of simultaneously targeting three key cancer-causing mechanisms, synergizing p53 reactivation with tailored degradation of tumor-specific protein drivers. This 3-in-1 approach has the potential to overcome major barriers to clinical translation and therapeutic efficacy, paving the way for improved treatments of recalcitrant human cancers.

Limitations of the study

This study establishes proof of concept for a triple-action PROTAC that co-opts HDM2 to degrade oncogenic BET proteins while maximally reactivating p53 through dual HDM2/HDMX blockade, but several limitations merit consideration. Although molecular dynamics simulations, biochemical assays, and cellular assays support formation of the desired ternary complex among TAPTAC1, HDM2/HDMX, and BET proteins, definitive structural elucidation will be required to more fully define the architecture, dynamics, and ubiquitination geometry of this assembly. Such studies will clarify how linker composition and placement, staple positioning, and binding cooperativity influence complex stability and target specificity. Mechanistic and phenotypic analyses in this work focus on BET proteins as prototypical substrates to benchmark TAPTAC1 activity. However, BET proteins are among the most tractable and readily degraded targets in the field. Extending the platform to additional oncogenic drivers, particularly those less amenable to proteasomal degradation, will be important to establish the generalizability and modular potential of stapled-peptide-based PROTACs that combine oncoprotein degradation with robust p53 reactivation.

Short-term pharmacokinetic and toxicologic analyses revealed favorable plasma stability and tolerability based on tracking animal weight, blood counts, and serum chemistries. Nonetheless, comprehensive toxicologic evaluation under prolonged dosing and across species remains to be performed. Experience with HDM2-selective inhibitors and BET inhibitors/degraders has revealed hematopoietic, hepatic, and other liabilities, underscoring the importance of confirming that TAPTAC1’s 3-in-1 mechanism and substantially reduced dosing requirements confer both a therapeutic advantage and broadened safety window. Importantly, TAPTAC1 exploits HDM2, an oncogenic E3 ligase pathologically overexpressed in and required by cancer cells, rather than a constitutively expressed ligase equally capable of degrading essential proteins in malignant and normal tissues. This design distinction may underlie the striking selectivity observed for TAPTAC1 upon treating transformed versus non-transformed cells. Moreover, TAPTAC1’s activity and tolerability in a fully murine leukemia model indicates that its efficacy spans homologous targets across species and that toxicity was not masked by human-specific target engagement. The mechanisms by which resistance may emerge, whether through p53 mutation or pathway disruption, altered HDM2 expression or activity, or impaired ternary complex formation, also remain to be elucidated. Moving forward, systematic exploration of TAPTAC structure-function relationships, chronic dosing safety, mechanisms of resistance, and combinatorial designs that synergize with p53 reactivation will be key to advancing this platform from proof of concept to a broadly applicable therapeutic strategy for patients with WT p53 cancers.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

Cultured cell lines were purchased from and authenticated by ATCC. SJSA-X cells were provided by the Wahl lab,41 p185+ Arf−/− murine B-ALL cells from the Opferman lab,25 and HCT116 p53−/− cells from the Vogelstein lab. cells from the Vogelstein lab. The following media were employed for cell line cultures: RPMI: EOL-1, OVTOKO, HUVEC, DI-TNC1, p185+ Arf−/− B-ALL; DMEM: SJSA-1, SJSA-X, HCT116, HCT 116 p53−/−; MEM: RPTEC; EMEM: JEG-3; McCoy: Saos-2; F-12K: C6. In each case, the media were supplemented with penicillin/streptomycin and 10% FBS. The cells were verified as mycoplasma-negative using the MycoAlert mycoplasma detection kit (Lonza Biologics).

Animal models

For animal experiments, 7–10 week old, female NSG (Jackson Labs, 005557) or C57BL/6 (Jackson Labs, 000664) mice were used and housed in groups of 4–5 in a pathogen-free animal facility at 22°C with a 12-h light/dark cycle and fed normal chow ad libitum. All mouse experiments, including euthanasia, were performed in accordance with Dana-Farber Cancer Institute Animal Care and Use Committee approved protocol #06-004 and #04-111.

Method details

Peptide synthesis

The stapled p53 peptide component of TAPTAC1, ALRN-6924 E21A and its F19A and AYWA (aa 21–24) to AWAY mutants, were synthesized by sequential amino acid addition to Rink Amide AM resin (EMD Biosciences) using Fmoc chemistry, as described.10 After acetylation of the N-terminus, an orthogonally protected lysine (Dde) was deprotected with 2% hydrazine to liberate a primary amine, which was acylated with Fmoc-β-Ala, followed by stapling with Grubbs 1st generation catalyst, as described.10 The Fmoc was then removed and JQ113 was coupled to complete the synthesis. The peptide was cleaved from the resin, purified by reverse phase high performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), and quantified by amino acid analysis, performed as reported.42 Lyophilized peptides were reconstituted in 100% DMSO and diluted into aqueous buffers for experimentation.

Molecular dynamics simulations

All-atom molecular dynamics simulations were used to map explicit molecular interactions between HDM2, BRD4, and TAPTAC1. Simulations of the HDM2-TAPTAC1 complex were initiated based on the structure of the compound bound to humanized zebrafish HMDX (PDB: 8EBK). The binding mode was verified by use of the coarse-grained CABS docking methodology43 and resulting structure rotated so that its principal axes lie along the orthorhombic cell vectors (thus minimizing cell volume). The system was equilibrated in a solvent box for 200 ns to relax the system and sample its conformational space. An identical simulation procedure was performed for the JQ1-bound structure of the N-terminal bromodomain (BD1) of human BRD4 (PDB: 3MXF). These simulations were sampled to assemble an HDM2-TAPTAC1-BD1 complex by aligning the JQ1 ligand from an extended conformer of HDM2-TAPTAC1 with an equilibrated snapshot from the BD1-JQ1 trajectory. This final assembly equilibrated for 300 ns. Throughout these all-atom simulations, protein dynamics are described using the CHARMM36 protein force field. Systems were solvated using TIP3P water (15 Å padding) and supplemented with 0.15 M K+/Cl− to ensure electrical neutrality.

For docking, we used coarse-grained (CG) simulations to validate the TAPTAC1 binding orientation on HDM2. Placement was accomplished by using the CABS methodology to fold a fully-flexible peptide model in the presence of the folded target protein.43,44 A series of ten CABS trajectories were calculated and 1000 models were sampled from each. The resulting ensemble of 104 candidates was filtered to remove unbound systems and clustered using the k-medoids method. This delivered ten plausible TAPTAC1-HDM2 complexes. We reconstructed the atomic details for these CG alpha-carbon traces using the MODELLER package.45

All-atom molecular dynamics simulations were driven by NAMD 2.14 code, which uses a fully periodic BBK-type integrator with velocity rescaling. We employed rigid bond constraints to achieve numerical stability at a timestep of δt = 2 fs. Our NVT calculations applied a Langevin thermostat to heavy atoms for temperature control (T = 300.0 K; damping γ = 1.0 ps-1), while NPT simulations controlled pressure with a Langevin piston (target P = 101.325 kPa; period = 100.0 fs; decay time = 50.0 fs). Anisotropic cell fluctuations were allowed and multiple time-stepping was used for nonbonded interactions, with short-range interactions evaluated every 2 fs and full electrostatics every 4 fs. Short-range interactions were cut off at 1.2 nm and smoothed with sigmoidal rescaling for atoms separated by more than 1.0 nm. Smoothed particle mesh Ewald (PME) was used for long-range electrostatics. Equilibration began with a 2000 step conjugate gradient (CG) minimization followed by 1.0 ns of NVT equilibration with all protein atoms fixed. Next, a harmonic constraint potential (kprot = 5 kcal mol-1 Å-2) was applied to hold protein atoms near their initial positions, and 2000 steps of CG minimization and 2.0 ns of NPT equilibration was used to relax the protein. Finally, all constraints were removed, and production calculations were executed in NPT ensemble for the indicated durations with samples captured every 5 ps.

Recombinant protein production

Recombinant HDM2 (residues 17–125) bearing an N-terminal hexahistidine tag and a thrombin cleavage site was cloned into the pET28a vector, expressed in BL21(DE3) E. coli, and purified using sequential Ni-affinity and size-exclusion chromatography (SEC). Protein expression was induced with 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG; Gold Biotechnology) for 4 h at 30°C. Bacterial pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris, pH 8, 500 mM NaCl, 2 protease inhibitor tablets [Roche]) and lysed by two passes through a microfluidizer (M-110L, Microfluidics) chilled to 4°C. Insoluble debris was removed by centrifugation (20,000 rpm, 45 min, 4°C). The supernatant was applied to a Ni-NTA (Qiagen) column pre-equilibrated with lysis buffer. The column was washed with lysis buffer and wash buffer (20 mM Tris, pH 8, 300 mM NaCl), and proteins were eluted using an imidazole gradient (5–300 mM) in wash buffer. Eluted protein fractions were dialyzed, concentrated, and further purified on a Superdex S-75 (GE Healthcare) gel filtration column equilibrated with FPLC buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.2, 300 mM NaCl). Protein purity and identity were confirmed by Coomassie staining and Western blotting analyses.

Humanized zebrafish HDMX (zHDMX) was cloned, generated, and purified as previously described.12 Briefly, zHDMX (residues 15–106, L46V/V95L double mutation) bearing an N-terminal hexahistidine tag and enterokinase recognition sequence (pET15b vector, Novagen) was expressed in BL21(DE3) E. coli using 0.2 mM IPTG for 4 h at 30°C. The protein was purified by sequential Ni-affinity chromatography and SEC. Bacterial pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris, pH 8, 500 mM NaCl, 2 protease inhibitor tablets [Roche]) and lysed by two passes through a microfluidizer (M-110L, Microfluidics) chilled to 4°C. Insoluble debris was removed by centrifugation (20,000 rpm, 45 min, 4°C). The supernatant was applied to a Ni-NTA (Qiagen) column pre-equilibrated with lysis buffer. The column was washed with lysis buffer and wash buffer (20 mM Tris, pH 8, 300 mM NaCl), and proteins were eluted using an imidazole gradient (5–300 mM) in wash buffer. Eluted protein fractions were dialyzed, concentrated, and further purified on a Superdex S-75 (GE Healthcare) gel filtration column equilibrated with FPLC buffer (50 mM NaPO4, pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM TCEP). Protein purity and identity were confirmed by Coomassie staining and Western blotting analysis.

The human BRD4 BD1 domain (residues 48–168) in the pNIC28Bsa4 vector (Addgene) was expressed in BL21(DE3) E. coli in LB medium containing 50 mg/mL kanamycin. Cells were grown at 37°C to an OD of 0.8, induced with 0.5 mM IPTG, incubated overnight at 17°C, collected by centrifugation, and stored at −80°C. Cell pellets were lysed using a microfluidizer in buffer A1 (25 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 20 mM imidazole, 7 mM BME) supplemented with 0.1% IGEPAL and 1 mM PMSF. The lysate was centrifuged at 20,000xg for 40 min. Ni-NTA beads (Qiagen) were mixed with the lysate supernatant for 2 h at 4°C and washed with 10 column volumes of buffer A1, followed by 5 column volumes of buffer A2 (25 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 2000 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 20 mM imidazole). Protein was eluted with an imidazole gradient (20–400 mM) in buffer A2. Further purification was performed by SEC in buffer A3 (25 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 200 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 0.5 mM TCEP, 1 mM DTT) using a Superdex-200 10/300 column (GE Healthcare).

Complex formation analysis by size exclusion chromatography

Recombinant HDM2, zHDMX, and BRD4 BD1 proteins, described above, were diluted to 20 μM in 500 μL of assay buffer (300 mM NaCl, 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5). Protein samples were incubated individually, in combination, or in combination with added TAPTAC1 for 1 h at 30°C. The proteins and their complexes were evaluated by SEC using a Superdex S-75 column (GE Healthcare). Min-max normalization of the data was performed with RStudio (version 4.1.2) and visualized using ggplot2 (version 3.4.4). SDS-PAGE analysis was conducted on peak-containing fractions to further confirm the presence of the indicated proteins and complexes.

In vitro ubiquitylation assay

An in vitro ubiquitylation system was established using ubiquitylation assay buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP) and the following components (R&D Systems): 1 μM Human GST-HDM2 (E3-202-050), 100 nM UBE1 (E−305-025), 1 μM UbcH5c/UBE2D3 (E2-627-100), 10 mM MgATP (B-20), and 100 μM human ubiquitin protein (U-100H-10M). Recombinant FLAG-p53 (20 μM, BPS Bioscience, 100412) and recombinant 6xHis-BRD4 bearing BD1 and BD2 domains (20 μM, BPS Bioscience, 31045) were added as substrates for the ubiquitylation assay, in the absence or presence of TAPTAC1 (60 μM), and incubated for 0, 1, 2, or 4 h. Reactions were stopped with LDS buffer (Invitrogen) and boiled at 95°C for 5 min. Samples were run on a 4–12% Bis-Tris gel in 1× MES buffer (Invitrogen) and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane, which was blocked for 1 h at room temperature in 3% BSA. Rabbit His-Tag Antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #2365S, RRID: AB_2115720) was incubated overnight at 4°C to detect 6xHis-BRD4, and Rabbit FLAG Tag Antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #2368S, AB_2217020) was used to detect FLAG-p53. Membranes were washed in PBST and incubated at room temperature with goat anti-rabbit IgG-poly-HRP (Invitrogen, #32260, RRID: AB_1965959). After washing with PBST, membranes were imaged by chemiluminescence for the presence of ubiquitin-based protein laddering.

Cell viability assays

Cells (5 × 103/well) were seeded in 384-well format in 5% FBS/DMEM. A 3-fold serial dilution of the indicated compounds was added and cultures incubated for 72 h. Cell viability was assayed by addition of CellTiter-Glo chemiluminescence reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Promega) and luminescence was measured using a SpectraMax M5 microplate reader (Molecular Devices). Luminescence data were analyzed and plotted using Prism software (GraphPad).

Primary peripheral blood samples from pediatric T-ALL patients were collected for experimental use under Dana-Farber Cancer Institute Institutional Review Board protocol #06–078. The leukemia specimens were determined to be p53 wild-type by rapid heme panel testing. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from patients 06078-743 and 06078-834 were viably frozen before assay and those from patient 06078-882 were assayed upon isolation. PBMCs were purified using Ficoll-Paque PREMIUM (Fisher Scientific, 45-001-751). Approximately 2 mL of anticoagulated blood from EDTA tubes was mixed with an equal volume of RPMI-1640 (Gibco, #21780-076), layered onto 3 mL Ficoll in a 15 mL Falcon tube, and centrifuged in a Sorvall Legend RT+ (Thermo) at 400xg for 35 min at 18°C, with the break shut off during deceleration. The upper plasma- and platelet-containing layer was removed with a sterile pipette and the PBMCs were collected at the interphase above the Ficoll (granulocytes and red blood cells were separated from the PBMCs by centrifugation and located below the Ficoll). PBMCs were transferred to a sterile 15 mL centrifuge tube and diluted 1:6 with RPMI-1640, followed by gentle mixing and centrifugation at 300xg for 10 min, with the break set on low during deceleration. Isolated PBMCs were resuspended in RPMI-1640 with 10% FBS (Gemini Bio), counted using Trypan blue (Gibco) on a TC10 Automated Cell Counter (BioRad), and used immediately, with remaining cells viably frozen in 90% FBS and 10% DMSO (Sigma D2650-100ML). Viably frozen PBMCs were quick-thawed at 37°C for 2 min and diluted in drop-wise fashion with 9 mL warmed medium containing RPMI-1640 and 10% FBS. Cells were gently mixed and then centrifuged at 1300 rpm for 10 min. PBMCs were resuspended in 5 mL medium containing RPMI-1640 and 10% FBS at a concentration of 2-4x106 cells/mL, followed by counting using Trypan Blue, and incubation at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 1.5 h. Post-incubation, PBMCs were centrifuged at 1300 rpm for 10 min, and resuspended in RPMI-1640, 5% FBS, and 1% pen-strep (Gibco), and counted using Trypan Blue. For the viability assay, 50,000 viable PBMCs were seeded per well in a 96-well format, using opaque plates (Costar #3917). PBMCs were cultured overnight (19 h) with a 10 μM top dose, 7-point, 2-fold dilution curve of TAPTAC1 or A1874 (Selleckchem, #S8785), or 0.1% DMSO vehicle (Sigma D2650-100ML) control in a total volume of 100 μL. Viability was assessed by CellTiter-Glo assay (Promega #G7571) following the manufacturer’s instructions and data was acquired on a Spectramax M5 plate reader (Molecular Devices). Luminescence data were analyzed and plotted using Prism software (GraphPad).

Caspase 3/7 activation

SJSA-X and Saos-2 cells (5,000 cells/well) were treated with TAPTAC1 (0, 40, 200, 1000 nM dosing) and caspase 3/7 activation was monitored over time (6, 18, 24, 48 h) by Caspase-Glo 3/7 Assay (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Luminescence was measured on an EnVision plate reader (PerkinElmer).

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase activity

SJSA-X and Saos-2 cells (5,000 cells/well) were treated with TAPTAC1 (0, 40, 200, 1000 nM dosing) and senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity was monitored at days 3 and 6 using the Senescence β-Galactosidase Activity Assay Kit (fluorescence-based; Cell Signaling Technology, #23833) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescence was measured on a SpectraMax M5 plate reader (Molecular Devices).

Protein dynamics

SJSA-X cells were treated with 100 nM TAPTAC1 for 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h in DMEM supplemented with 5% FBS, 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin, and 2 mM glutamine. JEG-3 cells were treated with 500 nM TAPTAC1 for 0, 8, and 24 h in EMEM supplemented with 5% FBS, 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin, and 2 mM glutamine. To prepare samples for Western blotting analysis, cells were rinsed with PBS and subsequently lysed on ice for 20 min using RIPA lysis buffer (Sigma #20–188) supplemented with cOmplete, EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche). Insoluble material was separated by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 10 min in a table-top centrifuge and protein concentration of the isolated supernatant was measured by BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #23227). SJSA-X and JEG-3 protein samples (10 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE using 4–12% Bis-Tris (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 3–8% Tris-Acetate (Invitrogen) protein gels, respectively, and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes using the iBlot 2 Dry Blotting System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For the proteasome inhibition experiment, SJSA-X cells were cultured, treated with TAPTAC1, and processed as above except that bortezomib (50 nM) was added and assessment time points were at 4, 8, and 24 h and 3–8% Tris-Acetate SDS-PAGE gels (Invitrogen) were used for electrophoresis. The membranes were blocked in a solution of 5% milk prepared in PBS-T (Phosphate Buffered Saline, 0.01% Tween) and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, 1:1000 in 3% BSA (p53 [Millipore, #OP43-100UG, RRID: AB_10682938], p21 [Cell Signaling Technology, #2947, RRID: AB_823586], HDM2 [Millipore, #OP46-100UG, RRID: AB_10681293], HDMX [Fortis Life Sciences, #A300-287A, RRID: AB_263407], actin [Sigma, #A1978, RRID: AB_476692]) or 5% milk prepared in PBS-T (BRD4 [Cell Signaling Technology, #13440, RRID: AB_2687578]; BRD3 [Cell Signaling Technology, #94032]; BRD2 [Cell Signaling Technology, #5848, RRID: AB_10835146]). The membranes were then washed with PBS-T and treated with secondary HRP-conjugated antibodies 1:2000 in 3% BSA (p53, HDM2 membranes: anti-mouse IgG, Bio-Rad, #1706516, RRID:AB_2921252; p21, HDMX membranes: anti-rabbit IgG, Cell Signaling Technology, #7074, RRID:AB_2099233) or in 5% milk prepared in PBS-T (BRD2-4 membranes; anti-rabbit IgG, Cell Signaling Technology, #7074, RRID:AB_2099233) for 90 min at room temperature. After three additional 5 min washes with PBS-T, protein detection was carried out using Cytiva Amersham ECL Prime Western Blotting Detection Reagent (Fisher Scientific, #45-002-401) and an AI600 Chemiluminescent Imager.

HDM2/HDMX Co-Immunoprecipitation

SJSA-X cells were treated with vehicle or 100 nM TAPTAC1 for 24 h. Cells were harvested, washed in ice-cold PBS, and lysed using a non-denaturing lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 1 mM EDTA, protease and phosphatase inhibitors). Whole-cell lysate (250 μg) was incubated with HDMX antibody-coated magnetic epoxy beads (Invitrogen, #14301) for 24 h at 4°C on an orbital rotator. The beads were then collected and washed 3 times with lysis buffer. Bound proteins were eluted by addition of 2× SDS sample buffer and heating at 95°C for 10 min. Eluted proteins were separated by 4–12% Bis-Tris gel electrophoresis, transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, blocked with 5% non-fat milk in PBS-T, and probed with HDM2 antibody (Millipore, #OP46-100UG, RRID: AB_564803), followed by anti-mouse IgG-HRP secondary (Bio-Rad, #1706516, RRID: AB_2921252).

Quantitative proteomics

Sample Preparation. Tandem mass tag (TMT) proteomics was performed as described.46 Briefly, cells were treated with TAPTAC1 for 24 h (SJSA-X and DI-TNC1, 1 μM; C6, 100 nM) and then whole cell extracts were prepared by lysis in 1× RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Lysate protein was quantified by Bradford assay (Pierce). For quantitative proteomics of tumor specimens, whole tumors (n = 3) were resected en bloc from mice treated with either vehicle or TAPTAC1 (10 mg/kg/d) for 7 days and immediately flash frozen. Each frozen tumor was homogenized into a fine powder using a Retsch Cryomill and then suspended in lysis buffer (8 M urea, 200 mM EPPS pH 8.5, 1× Roche Protease Inhibitors, 1× Roche PhosSTOP phosphatase inhibitors). The homogenate was centrifuged at 21,000 × g for 5 min, and the resulting supernatant was transferred to a fresh tube. Protein concentrations were measured using the Bradford assay (Pierce). For each sample, 100 μg of protein was aliquoted for reduction and alkylation steps. Proteins underwent disulfide bond reduction using 5 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine for 30 min at room temperature, followed by alkylation with 10 mM iodoacetamide for 30 min in the dark at room temperature. To quench excess iodoacetamide, 10 mM dithiothreitol was added, and the mixture was incubated for 15 min in the dark at room temperature. Proteins were precipitated using a chloroform-methanol method before digestion. Specifically, 4 volumes of pure methanol were added to the sample and vortexed, followed by the addition of 1 volume of chloroform and vortexing, and then 3 volumes of water with vortexing. The mixture was centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 15 min at room temperature, washed twice with 100% methanol, and dried by vacuum centrifugation.

Trypsin and LysC digestion. The dried samples were resuspended in 200 mM EPPS, pH 8.5, and digested for 3 h at room temperature using Lys-C protease at a 100:1 protein-to-protease ratio. Trypsin was subsequently added at the same ratio, and the reaction was incubated for 6 h at 37°C.

Tandem Mass Tag (TMT) Labeling. TMTpro 18-plex reagents (0.8 mg) were dissolved in anhydrous acetonitrile (40 μL) of which 5 μL was added to the peptides (50 μg) with 15 μL of acetonitrile to achieve a final concentration of ∼30% (v/v). Following incubation at room temperature for 1 h, the reaction was quenched with hydroxylamine to a final concentration of 0.3% (v/v). TMT-labeled samples were pooled at a 1:1 ratio across all samples. For each experiment, the pooled sample was vacuum centrifuged to near dryness and subjected to C18 solid-phase extraction (SPE) (Sep-Pak, Waters).

Offline basic pH reversed-phase (BPRP) fractionation (whole proteome). The pooled TMT-labeled peptide (specifically, the unbound and wash from the phosphopeptide enrichment) was fractionated by BPRP HPLC using an Agilent 1260 pump. Peptides were subjected to a 56 min linear gradient from 5% to 35% acetonitrile in 10 mM ammonium bicarbonate pH 8 at a flow rate of 0.25 mL/min over an Agilent 300Extend C18 column (3.5 μm particles, 2.1 mm ID and 25 cm long). The peptide mixture was fractionated into a total of 96 fractions, which were consolidated into 24 super-fractions and subjected to FAIMS-MS/MS. These fractions were subsequently acidified with 1% formic acid and vacuum centrifuged to near dryness. Each fraction was also desalted via StageTip, dried via vacuum centrifugation, and reconstituted in 5% acetonitrile, 5% formic acid for LC-MS/MS.

Mass spectrometric data collection (SJSA-X cellular lysates). Mass spectrometry data were collected using an Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA) coupled to a Neo Vanquish liquid chromatograph. We used the Orbitrap detector of MS2 analysis. Peptides were separated on a 110 cm uPAC C18 column (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For each analysis, ∼0.5 μg was loaded onto the column. Peptides were separated using a 60 min gradient of 5–29% acetonitrile in 0.125% formic acid with a flow rate of 300 nL/min. The scan sequence began with an MS1 spectrum (Orbitrap analysis, resolution 60,000, 350–1350 Th, AGC target set to 100%, maximum injection time set to “auto”). Data were acquired for 90 min per fraction. The hrMS2 stage consisted of fragmentation by higher energy collisional dissociation (HCD, normalized collision energy 35%) and analysis using the Orbitrap (AGC 200%, maximum injection time 86 ms, isolation window 0.5 Th, resolution 45,000). Data were acquired using the FAIMSpro interface with the dispersion voltage (DV) set to 5,000V and the compensation voltages (CVs) set at −30V, −50V, and −70V or −40V, −60V, and −80V. The TopSpeed parameter was set at 1 s per CV.

Mass spectrometric data collection (tumor specimens). Mass spectrometric data were collected on an Orbitrap Eclipse mass spectrometer coupled to a Vanquish Neo UHPLC. Approximately 1 μg of peptide was separated at a flow rate of 450 nL/min on a 100 μm capillary column that was packed with 35 cm of Accucore 150 resin (2.6 μm, 150 Å; ThermoFisher Scientific). The scan sequence began with an MS1 spectrum (Orbitrap analysis, resolution 60,000, 350–1350 Th, automatic gain control [AGC] target set to 100%, maximum injection time set to “auto”). Data were acquired for 90 min per fraction. The hrMS2 stage consisted of fragmentation by higher energy collisional dissociation (HCD, normalized collision energy 36%) and analysis using the Orbitrap (AGC 200%, maximum injection time 120ms, isolation window 0.6 Th, resolution 30,000 Turbo TMT). Data were acquired using the FAIMSpro interface with the dispersion voltage (DV) set to 5,000V and the compensation voltages (CVs) set at −30V, −50V, and −70V or −40V, −60V, and −80V. The TopSpeed parameter was set at 1 s per CV.

Mass spectrometric data analysis. Spectra were converted to mzXML using MSconvert.47 Database searching included all entries from the human UniProt reference database. The database was concatenated with one composed of all protein sequences for that database in the reversed order. Searches were performed using a 50-ppm precursor ion tolerance for total protein level profiling. The product ion tolerance was set to 0.03 Da. These wide mass tolerance windows were chosen to maximize sensitivity in conjunction with Comet searches and linear discriminant analysis.48,49 TMTpro labels on lysine residues and peptide N-termini (+304.207 Da), as well as carbamidomethylation of cysteine residues (+57.021 Da) were set as static modifications, while oxidation of methionine residues (+15.995 Da) was set as a variable modification. Peptide-spectrum matches (PSMs) were adjusted to a 1% false discovery rate (FDR).50,51 PSM filtering was performed using a linear discriminant analysis, as described previously49 and then assembled further to a final protein-level FDR of 1%.51 Proteins were quantified by summing reporter ion counts across all matching PSMs, as described previously.52 Reporter ion intensities were adjusted to correct for the isotopic impurities of the different TMTpro reagents according to manufacturer specifications. The signal-to-noise (S/N) measurements of peptides assigned to each protein were summed and these values were normalized so that the sum of the signal for all proteins in each channel was equivalent to account for equal protein loading. Finally, each protein abundance measurement was scaled, such that the summed signal-to-noise for that protein across all channels equals 100, thereby generating a relative abundance (RA) measurement.

PRISM analysis

TAPTAC1 and A1874 was subjected to PRISM analysis21,53 (Broad Institute), which involved treating over 900 genetically-diverse cancer cell lines spanning 45 lineages in culture with a 3-fold serial dilution of compound from 5 μM to 2 nM dosing. For the TAPTAC1 and A1874 PRISM assays, 806 and 843 cancer cell lines passed the quality control testing. Independent cell line susceptibilities, lineage susceptibilities, gene expression correlations, and gene dependencies were assessed using PRISM’s online analysis portal tools (https://www.theprismlab.org/knowledge-base/#prism-assay).

Bioinformatics analyses

Pathway and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis and Classification. To investigate biological processes and pathways associated with differential protein abundance, Over-Representation Analysis (ORA) was performed using the clusterProfiler R package enricher function54 together with gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) accessed via msigdbr.55 Analyses were performed on all proteins identified as differentially abundant using the limma R package (treatment vs. control, triplicates)56,57 with multiple testing correction (false discovery rate [FDR]< 0.05). Differentially expressed proteins were visualized with volcano plots generated using ggplot2 (version 3.5.2), with significant thresholds of FDR < 0.01 and |log2FC| > 2. To increase resolution, enrichment was assessed both on the full set of significant proteins and separately on subsets of up-regulated and down-regulated proteins. Enrichment analyses were conducted independently for human SJSA-X osteosarcoma cells and rat C6 glioma cells, allowing the identification of shared as well as context-specific hallmark gene signatures. For pathway/signature panels and bucket assignment, the top 20 upregulated and top 20 downregulated proteins (by log2FC magnitude) were evaluated for their involvement in p53 and BET signaling changes upon TAPTAC1 treatment. Human gene sets were retrieved through the msigdbr interface (version 25.1.1). Collections from Hallmark, Reactome, and KEGG were used to define “BRD/BET-relevant buckets” (e.g., NF-κB signaling, DNA damage/repair, cell cycle/metabolism, TGF-β/EMT/fibrosis) by matching gene set names via regular expressions; the union of genes within each set was used for annotation. The KEGG “p53 signaling pathway” gene set was applied to define the p53 panel.

Integration of PRISM Drug Sensitivity with DepMap Molecular Features. To investigate determinants of TAPTAC1 activity in relation to HDM2/HDMX biology and BET protein dependency, we integrated PRISM drug sensitivity profiles with multi-omics data from the DepMap Public 24Q4 (https://depmap.org/portal/data_page/?tab=allData) release, using the following resources: CRISPRGeneDependency.csv: gene dependency scores for HDM2 (MDM2), HDMX (MDM4), and BET family proteins (BRD2, BRD3, BRD4, BRDT); OmicsExpressionProteinCodingGenesTPMLogp1BatchCorrected.csv: batch-corrected log-transformed TPM expression values for the same genes; Model.csv: mapping of CCLEName to ModelID to unify cell line identifiers; and OmicsSomaticMutations.csv: somatic mutation calls, from which TP53 mutation status was extracted. Analyses were performed using R scripts. PRISM area-under-the-curve (AUC) sensitivity matrices for TAPTAC1, A1874, and dBET6 were processed and log-transformed (log2[AUC+1]) before integration.

Cell Line Alignment and Annotation. Cell line identifiers across datasets were harmonized using Model.csv. TP53 status was defined using DepMap mutation calls and complemented with curated annotations from our own work (Table S4B). Mutant vs. wild-type assignments were then overlaid onto the PRISM sensitivity data.

Expression and Dependency Features. Baseline expression and gene dependency scores for HDM2, HDMX, and BET proteins were extracted from the corresponding DepMap files and subset to cell lines present in the PRISM datasets. These were then combined with drug sensitivity values to generate an intermediate integrated dataset.

Correlation analysis. Associations between TAPTAC1 sensitivity and gene expression or dependency features were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation (ρ), which evaluates monotonic relationships and is robust to outliers and non-normal distributions. This framework enabled evaluation of whether TAPTAC1 sensitivity is linked to HDM2/HDMX expression and/or BET dependency, and whether such associations differed between TP53 wild-type and TP53 mutant backgrounds.

Plasma stability

TAPTAC1 stability was tested in mouse and human plasma collected in lithium heparin tubes. TAPTAC1-plasma incubations were set up with 500 μL of plasma spiked with 10 μM of TAPTAC1. Samples were gently shaken in an orbital shaker at 37°C and 20 μL aliquots were removed at 0, 5, 15, 30, 60, 120 and 200 min and added to 150 μL of a mixture containing 50% water:50% acetonitrile to stop any further degradation. The samples were allowed to sit on ice for the duration of the assay and then transferred to a MultiScreen Solvinert 0.45 μm low-binding hydrophilic PTFE plate (Millipore). The filtrate was directly analyzed by LC/MS/MS using a Thermo BetaSil column, 2.1 × 50 mm, 5 μm. TAPTAC1 was detected using a Sciex 6500 mass spectrometer with multiple reaction monitoring, following the mass transition of 776.1 to 427.7 Da corresponding to the triple charged ion. The percentage of remaining TAPTAC1 over time was determined by any decrease in the chromatographic peak area and then log transformed to calculate half-life.

Plasma protein binding

Plasma protein binding was determined using equilibrium dialysis. All samples were tested in duplicate using the RED Rapid Equilibrium Dialysis Device (Thermo Scientific). The initial drug concentration in the plasma chamber was 2 μM, and phosphate buffered saline was added to the receiver chamber. The plate was covered and allowed to shake in a 37°C incubator for 6 h. 25 μL was sampled from the plasma and PBS chambers, which were then diluted with either blank PBS or plasma to achieve a 1:1 ratio of plasma:PBS for all samples. The concentration of the drug in the plasma and PBS chambers was determined by LC-MS/MS. The fraction bound was calculated as ([plasma] – [PBS])/[plasma].

Mouse pharmacokinetics

Pharmacokinetics were tested in male C57BL/6J mice, 7–9 weeks of age, with body weights between 21 and 26 g. Doses were free base equivalent, factoring out the contribution of the TFA salt. TAPTAC1 was formulated in 5% DMSO/95% water at final concentrations of 0.3 mg/mL and injected at a volume of 10 μL/g body weight for a 3 mg/kg IV and IP dose. Higher doses of TAPTAC1 were formulated in 5% DMSO, 5% PEG400, and 0.1% Tween-80, diluted with 0.4% w:v methylcellulose at a final concentration of 1 and 3 mg/mL for 10 and 30 mg/kg doses, respectively. Triplicate mice were dosed via the lateral tail vein or via intraperitoneal injection. Repeated blood collection of approximately 20 μL was obtained at 5, 15, 30, 60, 120, 240, 360, 480, and 1440 min using a micro-sampling technique where blood was collected in capillary hematocrit tubes to reduce the total blood taken from the mouse. Plasma was generated using a microcentrifuge with a hematocrit rotor, resulting in approximately 10 μL of plasma, which was immediately frozen. Plasma samples were thawed, and 5 μL of plasma was deprotonated/extracted with the addition of 75 μL of acetonitrile, containing carbamazepine as an internal standard. Standards were prepared in blank mouse plasma and processed in the same analytical run. Samples were centrifuged through a Millipore Multiscreen Solvinter 0.45 micron low binding PTFE hydrophilic filter plate and analyzed using a Sciex 6500 mass spectrometer with multiple reaction monitoring, following the mass transition of 776.1 to 427.7 Da corresponding to the triple charged ion. Pharmacokinetic parameters were calculated using a non-compartmental model (Phoenix WinNonlin, Pharsight Inc.). All procedures are approved by the University of Florida Scripps Institute IACUC, protocol #15-022-03.

In vivo studies

SJSA-X xenografts were established by injecting 2 × 106 cells subcutaneously with matrigel into the flanks of 7–8 week old female NSG mice (Jackson Labs, 005557). Mice (n = 5 per arm) were treated with vehicle (5% DMSO, 5% PEG400, 0.4% methylcellulose solution, 0.1% Tween-80) or TAPTAC1 at 10, 3, 1, or 0.3 mg/kg in vehicle, once daily, by intravenous injection. For the 10 mg/kg dosing study (starting tumor volumes of ∼400 mm3), animals were euthanized after 7 days of treatment and tumors from 3 vehicle and 3 TAPTAC1-treated mice were isolated and processed for quantitative proteomics. For the dose-responsive study (starting tumor volumes of ∼200 mm3), treatments were extended beyond 7 treatment days to monitor for extended tolerance and efficacy. For the multi-week dosing and comparative drug studies (starting tumor volumes of ∼80 mm3), treatments were administered twice weekly (equimolar dosing at 1.3 μmol/kg, which is equivalent to 3 mg/kg for TAPTAC1) and tumor size measured with calipers daily to calculate and track tumor volume, along with animal weight and survival monitoring.

EOL-1 xenografts were established by injecting 2 × 106 cells intravenously into 8 week old female NSG mice (n = 4 per arm) on day 0. Starting on day 7 post-injection, animals were treated with either vehicle (as above) or 2 mg/kg TAPTAC1 IV daily. Complete blood counts (CBC) with differential were drawn prior to EOL-1 cell injection, at time of death, or upon termination of the experiment after 7 days of treatment, and samples processed at the Beth Israel/Deaconess Preclinical Murine Pharmacogenetics Core. The study was repeated in 10 week old female NSG mice (n = 6 per arm) with treatment initiated at day 4 post cellular injection and repeat CBCs drawn within 24 h after 7 days of treatment, upon termination of the experiment. Tumor volume (SJSA-X) and CBC (EOL-1) data were analyzed and plotted using PRISM software (GraphPad). For leukemic monitoring by in vivo bioluminescence imaging (BLI), p185+ Arf−/− murine B-ALL cells (5 × 105) were injected by tail vein into NSG mice (n = 8 per arm) and, 48 h later, twice weekly dosing was initiated with vehicle, TAPTAC1, TAPTAC1 mutant, or A1874 (equimolar dosing at 1.3 μmol/kg). Leukemic burden was measured by BLI twice weekly with animals in the prone and supine positions, along with animal weight and survival monitoring. For assessment of CBCs and serum chemistries upon TAPTAC1 treatment of non-immunocompromised mice, C57BL/6 mice (n = 4 per arm) were treated with twice weekly dosing of TAPTAC1 at 3 mg/kg or vehicle (as above) and blood withdrawn after 2 or 3 weeks for assessment of CBCs and serum chemistries (Na, K, CO2, Cl, BUN, Cr, AST, ALT, TBili), as performed at the Beth Israel/Deaconess Preclinical Murine Pharmacogenetics Core.

All animal studies were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute under approved protocols #06-004 and #04-111.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SD or ±SEM. Exact numbers of biological and technical replicates for each experiment, including the statistical analyses applied, are reported in the Figure Legends. p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Data were analyzed using Prism Software 10.0 (GraphPad).
