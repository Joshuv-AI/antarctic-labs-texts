Factors influencing the supportive care needs of female patients with genital cancer in South Korea
Authors: Yu-Jung Son, Keum-Hee Nam, JaeLan Shim
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Genital cancers are particularly important compared to other cancers because of the psychological impact they have on the individual. This study investigated the complexity in illness and quality of life among female genital cancer patients and determined the effects of these factors on supportive care needs to provide evidential data for the development of nursing intervention strategies to reduce supportive care needs in female genital cancer patients. This cross-sectional study collected data from July 22 to August 17, 2021. The study subjects were 103 female outpatients and inpatients aged 19 years or older who were treated for cervical cancer, endometrial cancer, ovarian cancer, or other female genital cancers such as vulvar cancer and vaginal cancer in a university hospital in Korea. The data were analyzed with t-tests and Scheffé’s test using SPSS 26.0. The factors affecting supportive care needs were examined using hierarchical regression. The average age of the subjects was 56.41 (±9.91) years. Cervical cancer was the most common diagnosis at 42.7%, followed by ovarian cancer at 34.0%, and endometrial cancer at 21.4%. The factors affecting supportive care needs included a middle school education or below (β = 0.21, P = .028), unemployment (β = 0.23, P = .018), complexity in illness (β = 0.32, P < .001), and quality of life (β = −0.68, P < .001). Developing a strategy for managing the complexity in illness and quality of life caused by various variables including disease stage and type of treatment is necessary to reduce the supportive care needs of female genital cancer patients. Improving their quality of life through effective communication with healthcare providers is essential.

1. Introduction

1.1. Significance of the study

According to data from the American Cancer Society, approximately 1.9 million individuals were diagnosed with cancer in 2021. This estimate includes 376,970 patients with genital cancer, among which 116,760 are newly diagnosed women, forming 31% of all genital cancers.[1] Cervical cancer is the most common type of genital cancer in women and the 8th most common cancer in the world according to the 2020 Global Classification, with an incidence of 3.1% (604,127 new cases).[1,2]

Coordinated efforts to optimize care for gynecological cancers are urgently needed considering the prevalence of and significant burden these diseases pose for women worldwide.[1,3] The incidence of female genital cancer is continuously increasing, since early detection is possible due to the development of noninvasive tests, such as tumor markers and early screening.[4] The survival rate of this cancer is continuously increasing as well.[5,6]

Recently, interest in the supportive care needs of patients with cancer has been increasing with a view to implementing various quality-of-life management strategies beyond the primary purpose of preventing cancer recurrence and metastasis.[7] Supportive care needs include not only needs associated with physical and symptom support but also those associated with care and means, information, and emotional support so that cancer survivors can adapt well to the disease experience.[8] Female patients with genital cancer experience more physical side effects of treatment, such as lymphedema, dysuria, sexual dysfunction, fatigue, vomiting, and anorexia[9,10]; higher negative stress caused by high recurrence rates; and higher psychological demands stemming from the unexpected loss of femininity caused by treatment than patients with other cancers.[7] Therefore, studies report high supportive care needs for cancer.[6,9]

The problems experienced by female patients with genital cancer are related to supportive care needs.[6,11] The more the physical, social, and psychological problems experienced by them, the higher the demand for supportive care and the problems’ negative impact on psychosocial coping. This enhances patients’ dissatisfaction with treatment and medical costs.[10,12]

In many studies conducted abroad, the needs for supportive nursing care of female patients with genital cancer were particularly high,[10,13] whereas Korean female patients with genital cancer showed higher psychosocial and disease-related information needs than physical needs.[7,14] When demands for disease-related information remain unmet, patients experience uncertainty about the disease.[15] In particular, a patient subjective cognitive state, which arises when the outcome is unexpected in various stressful situations during cancer survival, enables them to accept complex uncertainties.[16] This is an important variable that affects disease recovery[16] and is reported to not only aggravate physical symptoms but also exacerbate negative emotions, such as depression, anxiety, and stress,[17,18] and obstruct disease recovery.[19] Further, patients with cancer who accurately perceive complexity in their communications with medical staff or institutions have high demands for psychosocial support and care; ultimately, this increases their need for supportive care.[17,20,21] In particular, female patients with genital cancer who experience personal and complex problems, such as sexual problems, are limited in obtaining various disease-related information from medical staff or institutions.[17,19]

The quality of life is an important consideration of the treatment process of patients with cancer, and it is known to act as a prognostic factor.[22] Female patients with genital cancer undergo complex treatments, such as surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy, depending on cancer type and stage[23] and experience, during their survival period, various physical and psychosocial problems arising from treatment-related side effects or complications that often interfere with their daily personal and social activities and result in a lower quality of life than patients with other cancers.[24,25]

Patients with cancer having a low quality of life were found to have a high need for supportive care,[24,26] and identifying the quality of life of female patients with genital cancer is an important part of clarifying their need for supportive care.[10] However, to date, most of the studies on the quality of life of female patients with genital cancer verify it as the final dependent variable[10,27] and consider it unimportant to identify whether the quality of life affects important variables related to female patients with genital cancer. In addition, few studies have analyzed the subdomains of supportive care needs of the female patients and examined the fragmentary relationships of some variables, such as the quality of life[24,25] and resilience.[28,29] Moreover, only very few studies have examined the effect of disease complexity and quality of life on the supportive care needs of female patients with genital diseases. Therefore, this study provides evidence for the development of nursing intervention strategies to reduce the need for supportive care in women with genital cancer by confirming their disease complexity and quality of life level and identifying these aspects’ impact on supportive care needs.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Research design

This descriptive research study examined the effect of disease complexity and quality of life on the supportive care needs of female patients with genital cancer in South Korea.

2.2. Study population

The subjects of this study were outpatients and inpatients receiving treatment for various cancers, such as cervical, endometrial, ovarian, vulvar, and vaginal cancers, at a university hospital in South Korea. The criteria for subject selection were as follows: Subjects must be 1) women aged 19 years or older, 2) capable of communication and participation in surveys, and 3) able to understand this study purpose and methods and provide consent to participate. Women who did not know whether they had female genital cancers, those who had difficulty in responding to the questionnaire due to their inability to communicate, and adolescents under 19 years were excluded from the study.

The number of samples in this study was 88 when calculated using the G-power 3.1.9.7 program with a significance level of 0.05 for multiple regression analysis, an effect size (fz) of 0.15, a median size, a power (1-β) of 0.90, and 15 predictive factors. However, considering a dropout rate of 20%, the questionnaire was distributed among 106 people and, after excluding 3 individuals with insufficient responses, the data of 103 individuals were finally analyzed.

2.3. Data collection

Data collection was conducted from July 22 to August 17, 2021. It was performed after the researcher explained the study purpose in a face-to-face session with the study subjects in the obstetrics and gynecology outpatient department and ward and obtained the participants’ written consent to participate in the study. The patients completed a self-report questionnaire in approximately 20 minutes. Subsequently, a small gift was given to the participants as a token of appreciation.

2.4. Ethical considerations

This study was conducted after receiving approval from the Institutional Review Board (approval number: KU IRB 2021-0037, 2023-0003) of K University in Korea. The researcher directly distributed the questionnaire to study subjects; explained to them the study purpose and method, expected benefits and potential risks, and so on; and only those who provided informed consent to participate in the research were asked to complete the questionnaire. Subsequently, the researcher collected the completed questionnaires. It was specified that the subjects could withdraw from the study at any time if they did not wish to participate further and that there were no disadvantages to their decision. Additionally, the consent form for research participation included the researcher contact information, which enabled the research subjects to ask questions whenever they needed to. The consent form for research participation included statements upholding the subject anonymity and confidentiality and clarifying informed consent and refusal to participate in the research. The data collected by the questionnaire were used only for research purposes, and all subject-related information was encrypted and stored. The subjects were notified that the information would be discarded 3 years after research completion.

2.5. Research tools

This study used a structured questionnaire, whose contents comprised a total of 106 items, including 34 items for supportive care needs, 26 for quality of life, 7 for disease complexity, and 13 for general characteristics. Each measurement tool was distributed after obtaining permission from the tool developer.

2.5.1. Supportive care needs.

For supportive care needs, items corresponding to the Supportive Care Needs Survey—short form 34 (SCNS-SF34) were used among the SCNS tools[30] adapted by Ham Yoon-hee[31] to use the SCNS-SF34 developed by Boyes et al[8] The sub-factors consisted of 34 items in 5 subdomains: 5, 10, 3, 5, and 11 items for physical/daily living needs, psychological needs, sexual needs, patient care and support needs, and health system and information needs, respectively, with each question rated on a 5-point Likert scale. The sum of the scores was calculated out of 100, and the higher the score, the higher the need for supportive care. The reliability at the time of development was indicated by a Cronbach α of 0.96, and the reliability for this study was clarified by a Cronbach α value of 0.93.

2.5.2. Complexity.

For complexity, the Mishel Uncertainty in Illness Scale (MUIC), a tool developed by Mishel[16] to measure the uncertainty about diseases, translated and modified by Chung et al[32] considered 7 of a total of 33 questions. The tool used a 5-point scale, with points ranging from 1 for “not at all” to 5 for “very much” and positive questions were reverse-scored by calculating the score in reverse, with higher scores indicating higher levels of uncertainty. Regarding reliability, the Cronbach α value at the time of development was 0.91 to 0.93; this study Cronbach α value was 0.78, and the subdomain complexity had a Cronbach α value of 0.62.

2.5.3. Quality of life.

For quality of life, the Korean version of the Functional assessment of Cancer Therapy—General Version 4 developed by Cella et al[33] was used. The tool comprises 4 subdomains: physical well-being, social well-being, emotional well-being, and functional well-being. Further, it consists of 26 questions. Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 for “not at all” to 4 for “very much,” with high scores indicating a high quality of life. At the time of development, the reliability of the tool was a Cronbach α value of 0.92 and that of this study was a Cronbach α value of 0.90.

The diagnoses were cervical cancer for 42.7%, ovarian cancer for 34.0%, and endometrial cancer for 21.4% of all subjects. The average time elapsed after cancer diagnosis was 3.97 ± 3.67 years, with 33.0% of the subjects having been diagnosed 5 years previously. 3.2. Differences among disease complexity, the quality of life, and supportive care needs according to subjects’ general characteristics

Table 1 depicts differences among disease complexity, quality of life, and supportive care needs by subjects’ general characteristics. Complexity differed significantly by age (F = 9.22, P < .001), education level (F = 5.11, P = .008), and time elapsed since diagnosis (F = 3.12, P = .029). A post hoc analysis revealed that complexity was higher for those who graduated middle school (or lower) than those having a college degree (or higher).

Further, those with private insurance had significantly lower disease complexity (t = −2.02, P = .046).

The quality of life was higher in employed subjects compared to unemployed ones (t = 2.72, P = .008), and those with private health insurance had a higher quality of life than those without (t = 2.74, P = .007). Supportive care needs were higher in the group of subjects with low education than in the group with a high level of education (F = 3.91, P = .023); they were significantly low among employed subjects (t = −2.86, P = .005), as well.

3.5. Factors influencing supportive care needs

To test the assumption of linear regression analysis, the normality and linearity of all variables were identified; since the Durbin-Watson statistic was 1.412, which was close to the standard value of 2, there was no problem with autocorrelation. The absolute value of the correlation coefficient between independent variables was 0.07 to 0.28, which confirmed that all the variables were independent. The variance inflation factor was 1.005, and the values of variables included in all models were <10, which confirmed the absence of any problem with multicollinearity. The results of hierarchical regression analysis revealed that the first regression model with variables showing remarkable differences in subjects’ general characteristics was statistically significant (F = 6.708, P = .002), and the explanatory power of Model 1 was 10.1%. Statistically significant variables were education level and occupational status, and supportive nursing needs were found to increase in middle school graduates or lower (β = 0.21, P = .028) and unemployed subjects (β = 0.23, P = .018). Model 2 included complexity and quality of life, which are research variables. The regression model was statistically significant (F = 33.248, P < .001), and the explanatory power of Model 2 was 55.8%. The factors influencing supportive care needs were complexity (β = .32, P < .001) and quality of life (β = −0.68, P < .001) (Table 4).

4. Discussion

This study identified the relationship between disease complexity, quality of life, and supportive care needs in female patients with genital cancer in South Korea and clarified the effects of these variables on the patients’ supportive care needs. The level of supportive care needs was found to be 47.44 of 100 points, which is higher than the 34.25 points calculated in a study on patients with gastric cancer in South Korea[34] using an abbreviated version of the tool used in this study. Direct comparison is difficult since there is no earlier study that examined the same disease. However, it was found that the level of supportive care needs required by female patients with genital cancer is relatively higher than that required by patients with other cancers. In addition to facing the physical challenges of cancer treatment, female patients with genital cancer face various other challenges, such as edema, urinary problems, and sexual dysfunction, which make their daily life difficult.[9] This leads to complex issues, including feelings of loss as a woman and conflicts among couples[13]; this suggests a high demand for disease- and treatment-related information.

Disease complexity was 4.43 of 5 points, which was higher than the complexity level identified in the study by Choi and Kang.[35] This difference is attributed to a higher awareness of the disease complexity, including the need to consider the severity of the disease and the sexual aspect, in contrast to the study by Choi and Kang,[35] which included patients with breast cancer and those with thyroid cancer with high survival rates. Among the female genital cancer patients considered in this study, some had ovarian cancer with relatively high severity and high recurrence rates and often received complex treatments, such as surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy.

Results revealed that although the disease complexity did not appear significant in the post hoc analysis, the degree of complexity increased with age. These results were similar to how women aged 40 years or older showed higher health-related unmet care needs than those below 40 years of age in a study on patients with breast cancer.[36] In the current study, 72.8% of the subjects were women aged 50 years or older; they not only lacked opportunities to obtain information related to the latest treatment method and medical environment but also faced difficulties in matching the information that they possess with their conditions, unlike younger patients.[37]

In addition, this study revealed a significant difference in disease complexity according to educational background; middle school graduates and younger subjects were more aware of disease complexity than high school graduates. A study on patients with prostate cancer[38] reports that the lower the patient education level, the higher the disease uncertainty level. In other words, people with high education levels are more likely to acquire and differentiate high-quality health information.[39] Acquiring high-quality health information may reduce the stress related to information and potentially lower perceived disease complexity. In particular, female patients with genital cancer often ask whether they can solve physical and emotional problems or will experience difficulties in communicating with medical staff after undergoing treatment for female genital cancer.[40,41] Hence, it is necessary to motivate experts to develop a customized education program to provide comprehensive psychological, physical, social, and existential information to reduce the complexity of the disease of female patients with genital cancer.

The quality of life was 2.76 of 4 points, which is similar to the results of Oh and Hwang[42] study on patients with breast cancer using other tools. Female patients with breast cancer and genital cancer experience similar physical symptoms during treatment, such as fatigue, pain, and lymphedema, as well as psychological symptoms, such as depression arising from the loss of femininity and fear caused by high recurrence rates. This suggests that the quality of life of both groups of patients is similar.[43] In this study, female patients with genital cancer who had passed 5 years since diagnosis accounted for 34.3% of the total number of patients, suggesting that they had adapted to some extent to the physical and psychological issues caused by the disease and, hence, provided such responses.[44]

Nevertheless, female patients with genital cancer experience physical, psychological, and economic difficulties even after being diagnosed. Depending on their survival stage, such difficulties can affect their daily life and self-management and cause a decrease in quality of life at any time.[45,46] Therefore, in the future, longitudinal studies should measure changes in the patients’ quality of life at different periods of illness.

The quality of life was high for female patients with genital cancer having a job and private insurance. The United Kingdom National Cancer Survivorship Initiative emphasized the necessity of changing the approach to focusing on returning to work, and so on, from focusing on acute cancer treatment for the successful adaptation of cancer survivors.[47] Therefore, it is necessary to clarify the multidimensional needs of female genital cancer survivors based on their survival stages and provide effective social support to manage their quality of life.

This study observed that quality of life has the greatest impact on the supportive care needs of female patients with genital cancer. It was evident that supportive care needs vary significantly according to the quality of life. Although there is a lack of earlier research on the quality of life impact on supportive care needs, similar findings were observed in studies conducted on female patients with genital cancer,[24] patients with brain cancer[48] and patients with breast cancer.[43] These studies reveal that supportive care needs tend to increase with a decrease in the quality of life. The comprehensive quality of life identification and management should be prioritized to reduce the need for supportive care in female patients with genital cancer. However, most of the earlier studies did not distinguish the survival stage when examining the quality of life of female patients with genital cancer; alternatively, the studies were limited to a specific period, defined by the type and stage of treatment. Therefore, there is a need to expand the assessment of the patients’ quality of life according to the disease stages and obtain a comprehensive understanding of their supportive care needs.

As a result of this study, there was no significant difference in supportive care needs according to sociodemographic characteristics. On the contrary, in previous studies, sociodemographic and clinical factors (e.g., age, gender, marital status, income level, and clinical factors such as cancer location, disease stage, and tumor size) were related to the perceived and expressed symptoms of cancer patients.[49] In addition to influencing the pattern of supportive care needs, occupation, type of hospital, marital status, and sexual activity were also identified as influencing factors. Therefore, research is needed to identify the influencing factors of supportive care needs that take into account various individual perspectives.[50]

Meanwhile, in this study, the complexity of the disease was identified as a major influencing factor for supportive care needs. Most patients with cancer experience high disease complexity since they have limited disease-related information, lack awareness of treatment-related healthcare system issues, and fear unknown treatment outcomes. The inference that higher disease complexity leads to a greater demand for information supports the findings of this study.[17]

Recent developments in medical technologies, such as targeted therapy and immunotherapy, have caused patients to experience numerous difficulties in adapting to and acquiring information on various treatment methods.[51] Sajjadi et al[52] said that Iranian patients with cancer experience uncertainty related to the complexity of cancer treatment and complex and fluctuating symptoms and want to constantly search for information and acquire knowledge to overcome uncertainty. However, this study indicates a duality in some patients’ information-seeking approach. They prefer to receive positive news through information exploration but may be reluctant to receive difficult information or a poor prognosis due to fear.[51]

Therefore, prioritizing strategies to identify specific patient needs before information provision is essential. Subsequently, to reduce the complexity arising from information insufficiency, it is crucial to obtain detailed information on disease stage–specific treatment options, treatments’ side effects, the use of medications, and health management from specialized healthcare professionals in a systematic manner. In addition, it is necessary to consider developing and applying educational and counseling programs using mobile devices to provide the information required by patients anytime and anywhere to enable continuous management. Such a support system can contribute to the improvement of the quality of life of female patients with genital cancer and have a positive effect on reducing supportive care needs by reducing complexity.

This study has some limitations. Study subjects were conveniently sampled from a university hospital in a specific region in Korea. Hence, the extracted sample may not accurately represent the entire population. Therefore, it is recommended to conduct large-scale randomized studies in the future. Furthermore, this study used a cross-sectional correlational design to identify the factors influencing the supportive care needs of the participants. As a result, causal relationships between variables were not established. Therefore, in the future, longitudinal studies should be conducted to examine the causal relationships among variables. In addition, since the reliability of the tool to measure disease complexity is low, it is necessary to develop a tool that can better reflect the complexity of female patients with genital cancer.

Nevertheless, this study is significant since it provides foundational data on the factors influencing supportive care needs among female patients with genital cancer, who may have limited accessibility. This study offers a basis for potential interventions to reduce supportive care needs by analyzing the factors influencing supportive care needs.

5. Conclusions

This study investigated the complexity in illness and quality of life among female genital cancer patients and determined the effects of these factors on supportive care needs to provide evidential data for the development of nursing intervention strategies to reduce supportive care needs in female genital cancer patients.

The factors affecting supportive care needs included a middle school education or below, unemployment, complexity in illness, and quality of life. The findings show that the complexity of the disease and the quality of life of female patients with genital cancer were the main influencing factors on supportive care needs. Therefore, to reduce the supportive care needs of female patients with genital cancer, it is necessary to develop a strategy to manage the complexity of various variables associated with each stage of disease or type of treatment by communicating smoothly with medical staff and, thereby, improve the quality of the patients’ life.
