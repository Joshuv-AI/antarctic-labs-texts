Fermions and Supersymmetry in Neural Network Field Theories
Samuel Franki i frank.sam@northeastern.edu, James Halversonii ii j.halverson@northeastern.edu, Anindita Maitiiii iii amaiti@perimeterinstitute.ca, Fabian Ruehleiv iv f.ruehle@northeastern.edu

Abstract
We introduce fermionic neural network field theories via Grassmann-valued neural networks. Free theories are obtained by a generalization of the Central Limit Theorem to Grassmann variables. This enables the realization of the free Dirac spinor at infinite width and a four fermion interaction at finite width. Yukawa couplings are introduced by breaking the statistical independence of the output weights for the fermionic and bosonic fields. A large class of interacting supersymmetric quantum mechanics and field theory models are introduced by super-affine transformations on the input that realize a superspace formalism.

In this paper we introduce fermionic neural network field theories via Grassmann-valued neural networks. Such Grassmann-valued fields are the standard way to incorporate the anticommuting statistics of fermions in the path integral, and therefore we would like to have a similar construction in the neural network setting in order to extend the NN-FT correspondence.

In analogy to the scalar NN-FT case, an essential question is whether a Gaussian limit exists for Grassmann NN-FTs, which we answer in the affirmative by a generalization of the Central Limit Theorem; see Section
2
.
If a Grassmann network architecture is comprised of output weights
a_{ij}
and post-activations
\rho_{j}
(which themselves could be complicated deep neural networks),

\Psi_{i}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{ij}\rho_{j}(x),

(1.3)

N\to\infty
limit; see Section
3

4

5
, new classes of interacting supersymmetric theories are realized. In Section
6
we conclude and summarize our results; the interested reader might skip there immediately for a detailed summary.

While finalizing this manuscript, another paper on fermionic NN-FT appeared
[
23
]
. Their approach differs significantly from ours in that their neural network is not an anticommuting object but rather complex matrix-valued, such that fermionic behavior only arises in free correlators at infinite width. In contrast, our approach leverages Grassmann-valued neural networks to obtain fermionic behavior, including interactions, at all widths.

2
Grassmann Variables and the Central Limit Theorem

This section introduces Grassmann-valued random variables and their statistics in certain
asymptotic limits. Among various asymptotic limits, we are interested in those where sums
over independently and identically drawn Grassmann random variables become draws from
Gaussian distributions, in the sense that all higher-order connected moments vanish. The origin of non-Gaussianities in distributions over Grassmann
random variables can then be studied as parametric deviations from these Gaussian limits.

To facilitate this direction, we derive a Central Limit Theorem for both discrete and continuous sets of Grassmann variables. We will prove in Section
2.1
:

Grassmann CLT:
a sum over
N
i.i.d. Grassmann random variables is Gaussian distributed as
N\to\infty
.

As in the real-valued case in
[
48
,
8
]
, we will demonstrate parametric control of Grassmann non-Gaussianities by violating assumptions of the CLT, especially independence and
N\to\infty
.

2.1
Discrete Case

Let us start our discussion with a scaled sum over
N
randomly drawn discrete Grassmann-valued vectors
{X_{i}}=(X_{i1},\cdots,X_{id})
, where
i=1,\cdots,N
labels the statistical axis (i.e., the one summed over in the Central Limit Theorem), and each vector lives in a
d
-dimensional internal space. The weighted sum is taken along the statistical axis,

\displaystyle\psi_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{ij},\quad j=1,\cdots,d\,.

(2.1)

Here,
\psi_{j}
is the
j
-th element of a
d
-dimensional discrete Grassmann-valued vector.
1
1

1

Throughout, by a Grassmann-valued vector
\psi_{i}
, we implicitly mean
\psi_{i}=a_{i}\odot\xi_{i}
where the Hadamard product keeps track of the fact that we have a coefficient
a_{i}
for each Grassmann variable, which takes values in some field, and the Grassmann variable
\xi_{i}
gives anti-commuting statistics.

Random variables
X_{ij}
are sampled from mean-zero distributions i.i.d. across the statistical axis:

\displaystyle P(X)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}P(X_{i1},\dots,X_{id})\;,

(2.2)

\displaystyle P(X_{11},\dots,X_{1d})=\ldots=P(X_{N1},\dots,X_{Nd})\;,\qquad\mathbb{E}[X_{ij}]=0~\forall~i,j\;,

(2.3)

yet statistically correlated along the internal axis,

\displaystyle P(X_{i1},\cdots,X_{id})\neq\prod_{j=1}^{d}P(X_{ij})\;.

(2.4)

Such correlations are necessary to ensure a nontrivial two-point function.
The Grassmann nature is manifest in elementwise anticommutation relations,

\displaystyle\{X_{ij},X_{kl}\}=0~~\forall~i,j,k,l\;.

(2.5)

With this, one obtains
\{\psi_{j},\psi_{k}\}=0\,\,\forall j,k
.

Next, we wish to study the distribution of
{\psi}:=(\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{d})
, with an emphasis on asymptotic limits for Gaussianity. To that end, we construct the cumulant generating function (CGF) of
{\psi}
. This approach will be extrapolated to the continuous Grassmann random variable case in Section
2.2
.

Introduce a
d
-dimensional vector
\vec{J}=(J_{1},\cdots,J_{d})
as a Grassmann-valued source that anticommutes with
{\psi}
elementwise as
\{\psi_{i},J_{j}\}=0~\forall~i,j
. Then, let us define the cumulant generating function
W_{\psi}[J]
as the logarithm of the partition function,

\displaystyle W_{{\psi}}[{J}]

\displaystyle=\log\mathbb{E}_{P({\psi})}\big[e^{J_{j}\psi_{j}}\big]=\log\mathbb{E}_{P(X)}\big[e^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i}J_{j}X_{ij}}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\mathbb{E}_{P(X_{i1},\cdots,\,X_{id})}\big[e^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}J_{j}X_{ij}}\big]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{X_{i}}\bigg[\frac{{J}}{\sqrt{N}}\bigg].

(2.6)

We have used (
A.1
) and (
A.2
) to obtain the last equality.
The CGF can also be expanded as a sum of connected moments (cumulants):

\displaystyle W_{\vec{\psi}}[{J}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{d}(-1)^{r(r-1)/2}\,\frac{J_{j_{1}}\cdots J_{j_{r}}}{r!}\kappa^{{\psi}}_{j_{1}\cdots j_{r}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{X_{i}}\bigg[\frac{{J}}{\sqrt{N}}\bigg]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{r=1}^{d}(-1)^{r(r-1)/2}\,\frac{J_{j_{1}}\cdots J_{j_{r}}}{r!N^{r/2}}\kappa^{X_{ij}}_{j_{1}\cdots j_{r}},

(2.7)

where

\displaystyle\kappa^{{\psi}}_{j_{1}\cdots j_{r}}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}^{(c)}_{P({\psi})}\big[\psi_{j_{1}}\cdots\psi_{j_{r}}\big],

\displaystyle\kappa^{X_{ij}}_{j_{1}\cdots j_{r}}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}^{(c)}_{P(X_{i1},\cdots,\,X_{id})}\big[X_{ij_{1}}\cdots X_{ij_{r}}\big]

(2.8)

denote elements
2
2

2

\kappa^{X_{ij}}_{j_{1}\cdots j_{a}\cdots j_{b}\cdots j_{c}\cdots j_{r}}={\sign(\pi)}\,\kappa^{X_{ij}}_{j_{1}\cdots j_{c}\cdots j_{b}\cdots j_{a}\cdots j_{r}}
for all
j
due to antisymmetry, where
\pi
is the permutation on the indices and
\sign(\pi)
is
+1
for even permutations and
-1
for odd.
in the rank-

connected moments, with
\mathbb{E}^{(c)}
labeling the connected parts of statistical moments.
3
3

3

Unlike the cumulant expansion of the CGF for commuting variables, the Taylor series over

in (

) truncates at

because
J_{j_{1}}\cdots J_{j_{r}}=0
for
r>d
.

Equation (

) implies a relation between the connected correlators of
\psi
and the connected correlators of
X_{ij}
:

\displaystyle\kappa^{{\psi}}_{j_{1}\cdots j_{r}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\kappa^{X_{ij}}_{j_{1}\cdots j_{r}}}{N^{r/2}}\;,

(2.9)

which can be simplified via the identical-ness condition in (
2.3
):

\displaystyle\kappa^{{\psi}}_{j_{1}\cdots j_{r}}=\frac{\kappa^{X_{ij}}_{j_{1}\cdots j_{r}}}{N^{r/2-1}}\;,

(2.10)

demonstrating that the connected moments of
\psi
of order

vanish when
N\to\infty
.

We further need to show that the first and second connected correlators of
{\psi}
are finite. The first connected correlator vanishes due to mean-free assumptions in (
2.3
), and while the diagonal part of the second connected correlator vanishes,
\kappa^{{\psi}}_{jj}=0
, the off-diagonal part (which requires
d\geq 2
) is

\displaystyle\kappa^{{\psi}}_{j_{1}j_{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{(c)}_{P(X_{i1},\cdots,\,X_{id})}[X_{ij_{1}}X_{ij_{2}}],

(2.11)

and is generally non-zero. If it is finite, which is case-dependent, then the vanishing of higher connected moments shows that
{\psi}
is Gaussian in the infinite width limit.

Non-Gaussianity from Statistical Independence Breaking

We wish to systematically introduce non-Gaussianities in the distribution of
{\psi}
. To do so, one may choose a finite value for the width
N
so that non-Gaussianities are parametrically suppressed as
\mathcal{O}(N^{1-r/2})
in the
r
-th connected moment.

Alternatively, one may violate statistical independence of the variables
X_{ij}
along the
i
index by defining a joint distribution through a hyperparameter
{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}^{q}
such that

\displaystyle P(X;{\alpha}={0})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}P(X_{i1},\cdots,X_{id})\;,

(2.12)

and any
\vec{\alpha}\neq 0
turns on statistical correlations. One can then expand
P(X;{\alpha})
as a Taylor series:

\displaystyle P(X;{\alpha})=P(X;{\alpha}={0})+\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\sum_{s_{1},\cdots,s_{r}=1}^{q}\frac{\alpha_{s_{1}}\cdots\alpha_{s_{r}}}{r!}\partial_{\alpha_{s_{1}}}\cdots\partial_{\alpha_{s_{r}}}P(X;{\alpha})\Big|_{{\alpha}={0}}\;,

(2.13)

where, for simplicity, we assume that
\alpha
-dependence enters only through Grassmann-even terms, so the Taylor coefficients in (
2.13
) are
c
-valued.
4
4

4

By “
c
-number” or “
c
-valued number” we mean a commuting object; e.g., a real number or a product of an even number of anticommuting variables.
With these assumptions, the cumulant generating function inherits correlations among the random variables
X
analogously to the
c
-valued case (see
[
8
]
).

Nonetheless, we will still review the derivation with Grassmann variables inserted appropriately. We begin by introducing a popular trick below:

\displaystyle P\partial_{\alpha}\log P=\partial_{\alpha}P~~~~~~\partial_{\alpha}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{O}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{O}\partial_{\alpha}\log P],

(2.14)

for any
\alpha
-independent operator
\mathcal{O}
. Then, one can define an operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{r,\{s_{1},\cdots,s_{r}\}}:=\frac{1}{P({X};{\alpha})}\partial_{\alpha_{s_{1}}}\cdots\partial_{\alpha_{s_{r}}}P({X};{\alpha})

(2.15)

that satisfies the following recursion relation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{r+1,\{s_{1},\cdots,s_{r+1}\}}=\frac{1}{r+1}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{r+1}(\mathcal{P}_{1,s_{\gamma}}+\partial_{\alpha_{s_{\gamma}}})\mathcal{P}_{r,\{s_{1},\cdots,s_{r+1}\}\backslash s_{\gamma}}.

(2.16)

With this notation, the CGF of
{\psi}
can be expanded as:

\displaystyle W_{{\psi}}[{J}]=\log\Bigg[e^{W_{{\psi},{\alpha}={0}}[{J}]}+\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\sum_{s_{1},\cdots,s_{r}=1}^{q}\frac{\alpha_{s_{1}}\cdots\alpha_{s_{r}}}{r!}\mathbb{E}_{P({X};\,\alpha=0)}\Big[e^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i}{J}\cdot{X}_{i}}\mathcal{P}_{r,\{s_{1},\cdots,s_{r}\}}\big|_{{\alpha}={0}}\Big]\Bigg],

(2.17)

where the free CGF at
\alpha=0
is given in (
2.7
).
When all
|\alpha_{i}|
are sufficiently small such that the
r=1
term dominates, one can use the Taylor expansion
\log(1+x)\approx x+\mathcal{O}(x^{2})
to show that

\displaystyle W_{{\psi}}[{J}]=W_{{\psi},{\alpha}={0}}[{J}]+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\frac{\alpha_{j}}{e^{W_{{\psi},{\alpha}={0}}[{J}]}}\mathbb{E}_{P({X};\,\alpha=0)}\Big[e^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i}{J}\cdot{X}_{i}}\mathcal{P}_{1,j}\big|_{{\alpha}={0}}\Big]+\mathcal{O}({\alpha}^{2}),

(2.18)

at leading order. Thus, in general higher-order connected moments will receive
{\alpha}
-dependent non-vanishing contributions even as
N\to\infty
.

When the hyperparameter
{\alpha}
is chosen as a Grassmann-valued vector instead, with
\{\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j}\}=0
, the CGF becomes

\displaystyle W_{{\psi}}[{J}]=\log\Bigg[e^{W_{{\psi},{\alpha}={0}}[{J}]}+\sum_{r=1}^{q}\sum_{s_{1}\neq\cdots\neq s_{r}=1}^{q}\frac{\alpha_{s_{1}}\cdots\alpha_{s_{r}}}{r!}\mathbb{E}_{P({X};\,\alpha=0)}\Big[e^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i}{J}\cdot{X}_{i}}\mathcal{P}_{r,\{s_{1},\cdots,s_{r}\}}\big|_{{\alpha}={0}}\Big]\Bigg],

(2.19)

where the Grassmann nature of
\alpha_{i}
facilitates the truncation of the sum over
r
at
q
. The leading order corrections, i.e. at
\mathcal{O}({\alpha})
, are the same as in (
2.18
).

2.2
Continuous Case

Having defined the Central Limit Theorem for discrete random Grassmann variables, let us address continuous Grassmann random variables.

We start with a scaled sum over
N
randomly drawn continuous Grassmann-valued functions
X_{i}(x)
of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
i=1,\cdots,N
:

\displaystyle\psi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}(x)\;.

(2.20)

By imposing the anticommutation relation
\{X_{i}(x),X_{j}(y)\}=0~\forall~x,y,i,j
, we obtain

\displaystyle\{\psi(x),\psi(y)\}=0~\forall~x,y\;.

(2.21)

Let us assume that all
X_{i}(x)
are sampled independently from identical mean-zero distributions.

Having introduced this setup, we are interested in studying the distribution over
{\psi}(x)

via the cumulant generating functional (CGF) approach. Let us introduce a Grassmann-valued continuous source current
J(x)
that anticommutes with
\psi(x)
as
\{\psi(x),J(y)\}=0~\forall~x,y
. Then, the CGF of
\psi(x)
is

\displaystyle W_{\vec{\psi}}[{J}]

\displaystyle=\log\mathbb{E}_{P({\psi})}\big[e^{\int d^{d}x\,J(x)\psi(x)}\big]=\log\mathbb{E}_{P(X)}\big[e^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int d^{d}x\,J(x)X_{i}(x)}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\mathbb{E}_{P(X_{i})}\big[e^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\int d^{d}x\,J(x)X_{i}(x)}\big]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{X_{i}}\bigg[\frac{{J}}{\sqrt{N}}\bigg],

(2.22)

using (
A.3
) and (
A.4
) in the second to last equality. The connected correlators of
\psi(x)
are therefore related to the connected correlators of
X_{i}(x)\,\forall\,x
as

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{r(r-1)/2}\int d^{d}x_{1}\cdots d^{d}x_{r}\,\frac{J(x_{1})\cdots J(x_{r})}{r!}\kappa^{(r)}_{{\psi}}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{r})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{r(r-1)/2}\int d^{d}x_{1}\cdots d^{d}x_{r}\,\frac{J(x_{1})\cdots J(x_{r})}{r!\,N^{r/2}}\kappa^{(r)}_{X_{i}}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{r}),

(2.23)

where

\displaystyle\kappa^{(r)}_{{\psi}}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{r})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}^{(c)}_{P({\psi})}\big[\psi(x_{1})\cdots\psi(x_{r})\big],

\displaystyle\kappa^{(r)}_{X_{i}}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{r})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}^{(c)}_{P(X_{i})}\big[X_{i}(x_{1})\cdots X_{i}(x_{r})\big].

(2.24)

Then, just as in the discrete case, the relation between the connected correlators of
\psi
and the connected correlators of
X_{i}
is found to be:

\displaystyle\kappa^{(r)}_{{\psi}}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{r})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\kappa^{(r)}_{X_{i}}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{r})}{N^{r/2}}=\frac{\kappa^{(r)}_{X_{i}}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{r})}{N^{r/2-1}}\;,

(2.25)

where we once again see that the connected correlators of order
r>2
vanish when
N\to\infty
. We can also prove that the first and second connected correlators are finite:
\kappa^{(1)}_{\psi}=0
due to the mean-free assumption, and by the identical-ness assumption, the second connected correlator is:

\displaystyle\kappa^{(2)}_{\psi}(x,y)=\mathbb{E}_{P(X_{1})}[X_{1}(x)X_{1}(y)]\;,

(2.26)

which can be chosen finite with appropriate
P(X_{1})
.

Non-Gaussianity from Statistical Independence Breaking in the Continuous Case

We aim to introduce non-Gaussianities into the distribution of
{\psi}(x)
. Similar to the discrete case, we employ a hyperparameter
{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}^{q}
such that the joint distribution is defined as

P\big(X(x);\,{\alpha}=0\big)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}P\big(X_{i}(x)\big),

analogous to (
2.12
). Then, we can write the cumulant generating function (
2.2
) in terms of

W_{{\psi}}[{J}]=\log\Big[\int D[X(x)]\,P\big(X(x);\,{\alpha}\big)\;e^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int d^{d}xJ(x)X_{i}(x)}\Big]\;,

(2.27)

where

D[X(x)]:=\prod_{i=1}^{N}D[{X}_{i}(x)]

is a functional integration measure over each

.

Then, using the same trick from the policy gradient theorem that was used in the discrete case, (
2.14
)-(

), the CGF (

) can be written as

\displaystyle W_{{\psi}}[{J}]

\displaystyle=W_{{\psi},{\alpha}=0}[{J}]+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\frac{\alpha_{j}}{e^{W_{{\psi},{\alpha}=0}[J]}}\mathbb{E}_{P({X};\,\alpha=0)}\Big[e^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int d^{d}xJ(x)X_{i}(x)}\mathcal{P}_{1,j}\big|_{{\alpha}=0}\Big]+\mathcal{O}({\alpha}^{2}),

(2.28)

which is analogous to (
2.18

{\alpha}
would be an extrapolation of (

3
Grassmann Neural Network-Field Theories

We may utilize Grassmann-valued variables in neural networks to obtain Grassmann NN-FTs.
We will begin in the continuum, writing a Grassmann-valued neural network as

\displaystyle\Psi_{i}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi_{ij}(x)\;;

(3.1)

a normalized sum of Grassmann-valued neurons
\psi_{ij}
that can induce Gaussianity according to the results of Section

Though a variety of functional forms could be chosen for

\rho

\psi_{ij}(x)=a_{ij}\rho_{j}(x),

(3.2)

where the post-activations
\rho_{j}
depend on parameters and could be non-trivial functions appearing in a deep neural network. In this case, the network is

\Psi_{i}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{ij}\rho_{j}(x)\;.

(3.3)

A Grassmann-valued
\Psi
may be obtained in two simple cases: either the
a_{ij}
are Grassmann-valued and the
\rho_{j}
are
c
-valued, or the
a_{ij}
are

-valued and the
\rho_{j}
are Grassmann-valued.

3.1
Grassmann NN-FT from Grassmann Weights

We wish to study the case that the output weights
a_{ij}
are Grassmann-valued and the post-activations
\rho
are
c
-valued. To allow for the possibility of Gaussianity from the CLT, we will work in the case of statistical independence on the axis
j

where the large-

\displaystyle P(a)=\prod_{j=1}^{N}P(a_{1j},\cdots,a_{Dj})\;,

(3.4)

and also identical-ness; i.e., all of the distributions in the product are the same. We also assume that the
a_{ij}
have mean zero and that
\mathbb{E}[a_{ij}a_{kj}]\neq 0~\forall i\neq k
, which is necessary to obtain non-trivial two-point functions.
5
5

5

Factorization of
P

All other network parameters, implicit in the post-activations
\rho
, are labeled collectively as
\theta

P(a)

Let us consider a vector of
D

4
Applications in Fermionic Field Theories

Having developed some essential formalism for Grassmann NN-FTs and Gaussian limits thereof, we now turn to applications in conventional fermionic field theories: the free Dirac spinor and Dirac spinor interactions.

5.4
Supersymmetry Breaking

Let us briefly address how supersymmetry may be broken.

In field theory, one type of symmetry breaking occurs when the theory is deformed in a way such that the symmetry is no longer manifest in the associated ensemble of fields, as defined by a partition function. This is known as
explicit
symmetry breaking. Alternatively, it may also be the case that the ensemble does have the symmetry, but the (potentially metastable) vacuum state of the system does not realize the symmetry. This is
spontaneous
symmetry breaking. Consider the example of a Gaussian random variable
x
,

P(x)\propto e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}}

(5.38)

which has an
x\leftrightarrow-x
symmetry. The deformations of this density to

\qquad P(x)\propto e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}-gx^{3}-\lambda x^{4}}\qquad\qquad P(x)\propto e^{+\frac{1}{2}x^{2}-\lambda x^{4}}

(5.39)

explicitly breaks the symmetry in the first case and spontaneously breaks it in the second case. The spontaneous breaking arises because the “wrong signed” quadratic term yields two modes (analogs of vacua) away from
x=0
that are exchanged under
x\leftrightarrow-x
.

Similarly, explicit supersymmetry breaking happens when parameter densities are deformed in such a way that our superspace mechanism on input parameters is not operative. On the other hand, if the parameter density does allow for the SUSY mechanism in the ensemble, it could be that networks that locally maximize probability in function space (the analog of metastable vacua) nevertheless break supersymmetry.

To illustrate spontaneous SUSY breaking, consider a simple SUSY QM case

\phi=\cos(W\tau+\xi\theta+\bar{\xi}\bar{\theta}+b)

(5.40)

where SUSY arises via the mechanism of Section
5.1
; via uniform
P(b)
,
P(\xi)
, and
P(\bar{\xi})
. If the conditions are violated, the ensemble violates the symmetry, SUSY is explicitly broken.

Let us consider spontaneous breaking of supersymmetry, expanding the parameters around values
W_{*}
,
b_{*}
,
\xi_{*}
,
\bar{\xi}_{*}
which are either local maxima of the densities or lying on a flat direction, e.g. in the case of uniform densities. By the “value” of the Grassmann variables, we mean the value that has been implicit throughout, as in Footnote
1
.
Expanding in fluctuations around these values, e.g. as
W=W_{*}+\delta W
and similarly for the other variables, we have

\phi=\cos\left((W_{*}+\delta W)\tau+(\xi_{*}+\delta\xi)\theta+(\bar{\xi}_{*}+\delta\bar{\xi})\bar{\theta}+(b_{*}+\delta b)\right).

(5.41)

Ignoring the fluctuation, the mode is

\phi=\cos(W_{*}\tau+\xi_{*}\theta+\bar{\xi}_{*}\bar{\theta}+b_{*}),

(5.42)

which is not translation invariant in
\tau
,
\theta
, or
\bar{\theta}
; SUSY is spontaneously broken, and so is ordinary translation invariance in
\tau
. This should be contrasted to the case of
P(W)
where
W_{*}=0
, in which restricting to that mode in
W
yields

\phi=\cos(\xi_{*}\theta+\bar{\xi}_{*}\bar{\theta}+b_{*}),

(5.43)

which is invariant under
\tau

\xi_{*}=\bar{\xi}_{*}=0
we have

\phi=\cos(b_{*})

(5.44)

and SUSY is neither explicitly nor spontaneously broken. The former arises from the densities, whereas the later arises from the mode we expand around.

Admittedly, this formulation of SUSY breaking is presented in a way that naturally utilizes the NN-FT description, rather than via conventional means, such as the expansion into component fields and associated expectation values of associated F-terms and D-terms. We would like to have a more systematic understanding of SUSY breaking in NN-FT, including via conventional descriptions. This is as an important direction for future work.

6
Conclusions

In this paper we have introduced fermions into the NN-FT correspondence.

We began with the development of Grassmann-valued neural networks. First we provided a generalization of the central limit theorem to Grassmann variables in Section
2
, beginning with the discrete case and proceeding to the continuous case. In Section
3
we then defined two different types of Grassmann-valued NN-FTs, according to whether the output weights or the last-layer post-activations are Grassmann-valued. This amounts to the question of whether the Grassmann parameters exist “down the network” or at the output layer. We developed some essential formalism in each of the cases.

With the foundation laid for Grassmann-valued NN-FTs, we turned to applications, including classic results and supersymmetric theories. There are three results in Section
4
. First, we demonstrated an architecture that in the large-
N
limit realized the free Euclidean Dirac spinor in four dimensions. This demonstrates the ability of NN-FT to realize classic fermionic theories. Second, we demonstrated that the Dirac spinor may be coupled to scalars via Yukawa couplings by independence breaking in the parameter densities associated to the fermionic and scalar neural networks. Third, we computed the leading four fermion interaction arising at finite-width by computing the connected
4
-point function. Via a Grassmann-valued analog of the Edgeworth expansion, one can likely compute the four-point coupling associated to this theory; see
[
32
,
8
]
for the analogous computation in the scalar case.

Finally, in Section
5
we introduced constructions of supersymmetric theories. The essential idea of the mechanism, carried out in both 1d and 4d, is to consider supersymmetry as a spacetime symmetry. Since
\{\bar{Q},Q\}
acts as a translation, it is natural to try to engineer SUSY in a manner similar to translation invariance. Translation invariance was achieved in
[
16
]
for a wide class of interacting NN-FTs, building on the seminal idea of random Fourier features
[
39
]
from the ML literature. Our mechanism for SUSY amounts to an extension of the input to superspace, so that the input layer is an affine transformation of superspace, and invariance properties of the associated parameter densities ensure SUSY of the input-layer post-activations. Any network with independent parameters acting on this layer gives a SUSY theory. In general these deep SUSY NN-FTs are interacting, but we also demonstrated the existence of a large-
N
limit in which the theory is a super-Gaussian process. The network is generally a superfield, but constraints can be imposed that turn it into a chiral superfield. Superfield correlators are computed in some of the examples. We ended with a simple example exhibiting spontaneous SUSY breaking, but a more systematic understanding is left as an important direction for future work.

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial(\xi^{\prime},\bar{\xi}^{\prime})}{\partial(\xi,\bar{\xi})}=\mathbbm{1}_{2}\;.
