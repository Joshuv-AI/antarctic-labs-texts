Abstract
Low redshift fast radio bursts (FRBs) enable robust measurements of the host galaxy contribution to the dispersion measure (DM), offering valuable constraints on the circumgalactic medium (CGM) of FRB hosts. We curate a sample of 20 nearby FRBs with low scattering timescales and face-on host galaxies with stellar masses ranging from 10 9 < M ∗ / M ⊙ < 10 11 10^{9}<M^{*}/M_{\odot}<10^{11} . We fit the distribution of the host galaxy DM to a quadratic model as a function of stellar mass with a mass-independent scatter and find that the more massive the host, the lower its host DM. We report that this relation has a negative slope of m = − 97 ± 44 m=-97\pm 44 pc/cm 3 /dex in stellar mass. We compare this measurement to similar fits to three sub-grid models implemented in the CAMELS suite of simulations from Astrid, IllustrisTNG, and SIMBA and find that fine-tuning of the host ISM contribution as a function of stellar mass is required in order to reconcile the observational data with the predictions of the fiducial CAMELS-Astrid model. More generally, models which attribute a positive correlation between stellar mass and host dispersion measure ( m > 0 m>0 ) to the CGM are in tension with our measurement. We show that this conclusion is robust to a wide range of assumptions, such as the offset distribution of FRBs from their hosts and the statistics of the cosmic contribution to the DM budget along each sightline. Our results indirectly imply a lower limit on the strength of baryonic feedback in the Local Universe ( z < 0.2 ) (z<0.2) in isolated ∼ L ∗ \sim L^{*} halos, complementing results from weak lensing surveys and kSZ observations which target higher halo mass and redshift ranges.



I
Introduction

Understanding the ionized gas in and around galaxies is a major missing puzzle piece for galaxy formation and cosmology. It plays a crucial role in the baryon cycle and is a major contaminant for nascent weak lensing surveys including the Vera Rubin Observatory
(
LSST Science Collaboration et al. 2009
)
, Euclid
(
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2025
)
, and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope
(
Spergel et al. 2015
)
. The constraining power of those surveys hinges on an accurate and precise understanding of the impact of baryonic feedback on the matter power spectrum
(
White 2004
;
Zhan & Knox 2004
;
Rudd et al. 2008
, for example)
.

The dispersion measure, or DM, of fast radio bursts
(
Petroff et al. 2019
;
Cordes & Chatterjee 2019
;
Petroff et al. 2022
)
can precisely measure the column density of electrons between the observer and the FRB source
(
Lorimer & Kramer 2004
)
.
This makes FRBs excellent probes of extragalactic ionized gas, sensitive to both the unbound baryons in the large scale structure
(
Masui & Sigurdson 2015
;
Madhavacheril et al. 2019
)
and the collapsed gas overdensities on the outskirts of galaxies and their dark matter halos
(
McQuinn 2014
;
Prochaska & Zheng 2019
)
.

Several classes of methodologies have emerged which use FRBs to probe extragalactic baryons.
One pioneered by
Macquart et al. 2020
aims to model the relationship between the redshift of the FRB host and the total extragalactic DM. The tight relationship between dispersion measure and redshift is known as the Macquart relation
\langle{\rm DM}_{\rm cosmic}(z)\rangle
and now extends to
z>1

(
Connor et al. 2024
)
. It implies that other contributions, such as those from the host galaxy or individual groups and clusters, are subdominant to
\langle{\rm DM}_{\rm cosmic}(z)\rangle
beyond
z\gtrsim 0.5
.

Other analyses aim to isolate particular contributions to the FRB DM, which is the sum of contributions from the Milky Way, overdensities and underdensities in the large scale structure, intervening halos, the host galaxy of the FRB, and the circumburst environment.

For instance, angular cross-correlations with galaxy surveys
(
Shirasaki et al. 2017
;
Madhavacheril et al. 2019
;
Rafiei-Ravandi et al. 2021
;
Shirasaki et al. 2022
;
Hsu et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025a
)
and weak lensing
(
Nicola et al. 2022
;
Reischke et al. 2023
)
are sensitive to baryons in the large-scale structure. Spectroscopic mapping of FRB foregrounds
(
Lee et al. 2022
;
Lee et al. 2023
;
Simha et al. 2020
;
Simha et al. 2021
;
Khrykin et al. 2024b
;
Huang et al. 2025
)
can infer the contributions from individual galactic halos
(
Prochaska & Zheng 2019
)
, groups, and clusters
(
Connor et al. 2023
)
along FRB sightlines to the DM budget to separate the bound and the diffuse baryons along the line of sight. Edge-on FRB hosts probe the interstellar medium (ISM) of the host galaxy
(
Cassanelli et al. 2024
)
, whereas all-sky FRB surveys statistically place upper limits on the Milky Way’s halo
(
Cook et al. 2023a
)
.

Schematically, the DM budget can be modeled as

{\rm DM}_{\textrm{FRB}}={\rm DM}_{\textrm{MW}}+\langle{\rm DM}_{\rm cosmic}(z)\rangle+\delta\rm DM_{LSS}(z)+\dfrac{{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}}{1+z}.

(1)

where
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{MW}}={\rm DM}_{\textrm{MW,ISM}}+{\rm DM}_{\textrm{MW,CGM}}
consists of contributions from the interstellar medium (ISM) and circumgalactic medium (CGM) of the Milky Way.
\langle{\rm DM}_{\rm cosmic}(z)\rangle
is the average cosmic contribution to the DM budget out to redshift
z
, specified by the Macquart relation. Underdensities and overdensities due to large-scale structure along the line of sight, groups and clusters, and intervening galaxies contribute to
\delta\rm DM_{LSS}
.

The last term is considered to be the “host contribution” to the DM budget.
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}
can be further decomposed in the rest frame of the host:

{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}={\rm DM}_{\textrm{h,CGM}}+{\rm DM}_{\textrm{h,ISM}}+{\rm DM}_{\textrm{CBM}}.

(2)

The three terms here correspond to the CGM and the ISM of the host galaxy and contributions from the stellar “circum-burst medium” (CBM) of the FRB
(
Tendulkar et al. 2017
;
Niu et al. 2021
;
Ocker et al. 2022
;
Caleb et al. 2023
, see e.g.)
.

The “Macquart” estimate of the host DM is given by solving Eq.
1
for
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}
, and using models for the Milky Way ISM
(
Cordes & Lazio 2002
)
and CGM
(
Yamasaki & Totani 2020
)
and the Macquart relation to estimate the Galactic and cosmic contributions respectively.

\dfrac{{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}^{\textrm{Macquart}}}{1+z}\approx{\rm DM}_{\textrm{FRB}}-\rm DM_{\mathrm{NE2001}}-\rm DM_{\mathrm{YT20}}-\langle{\rm DM}_{\rm cosmic}(z)\rangle.

(3)

Because of the stochastic large-scale structure contribution and uncertainties in Galactic electron models, individual estimates of
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}^{\textrm{Macquart}}
are noisy: they are either positive or negative. However, measurements of
\langle{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}^{\textrm{Macquart}}\rangle
tend to scatter around a positive mean
(
Shannon et al. 2018
;
Macquart et al. 2020
;
James et al. 2022
;
Shin et al. 2023
;
Bernales-Cortes et al. 2025
)
.

In this paper, we use statistics of
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}^{\textrm{Macquart}}
to constrain the average circumgalactic contribution of the host galaxy and its scatter as a function of stellar mass
M^{*}
.
In §
II
, we consider a sample of
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}^{\textrm{Macquart}}
measurements selected to minimize contributions to
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}^{\textrm{Macquart}}
other than
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{h,CGM}}
, by using bursts away from the Galactic plane with low scattering timescales and whose hosts are nearby, do not belong to a galaxy cluster, and are viewed in a face-on geometry. Then we characterize the
M^{*}-{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}
relation for our sample of FRB host galaxies, in analogy to “cluster scaling relations” which correlate baryonic observables in more massive halos
(
Battaglia et al. 2012
;
Giodini et al. 2013
)
.

In §
III
, we compare our measurements to predictions for
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{h,CGM}}
, the circumgalactic contribution to the host DM, from three sub-grid models implemented in CAMELS: a suite of small-volume (25 Mpc/h)
3
hydrodynamic simulations implementing several distinct sub-grid “prescriptions” found in the Astrid, IllustrisTNG, and SIMBA simulations
(
Villaescusa-Navarro et al. 2021
;
Villaescusa-Navarro et al. 2023
;
Ni et al. 2023a
)
.
These prescriptions aim to accurately capture the large-scale impact of the unresolved small-scale physics that regulate galaxy formation and evolution.
These include prescriptions for star formation, gas cooling, and feedback from stars and active galactic nuclei (AGN).
One major goal of the CAMELS project is to produce theory predictions for gas probes which can independently constrain the suppression of the matter power spectrum induced by baryonic feedback relative to a dark-matter only simulation, as measured in current and next-generation weak lensing surveys such as the Dark Energy Survey
(
Chen et al. 2023
;
Aricò et al. 2023
)
and LSST/Rubin. In §
IV
, we compare our observations to simulations, and find that in order to reconcile the Astrid CGM prediction with the data, a negative correlation between the host ISM contribution and host stellar mass is needed. On the basis of this being potentially unphysical, we consider in §
V
the implications of the fiducial CAMELS-Astrid model being disfavored in the context of baryonic feedback in the Local Universe.

\sigma_{i}^{2}={\sigma}_{\textrm{cosmic}}(z_{i})^{2}+(0.2\rm DM_{\mathrm{i,NE2001}})^{2},

(4)

{\sigma}_{\textrm{cosmic}}
(z) measured from snapshots taken from large-volume hydrodynamic simulations that more accurately model large-scale structure not captured in the small CAMELS volumes.
The choice of
{\sigma}_{\textrm{cosmic}}

We linearly interpolate the IllustrisTNG values provided in Table 4 of

III
Predictions from CAMELS

We now aim to compare the data to theory predictions, built from a representative sample of sightlines from CAMELS.
We make our predictions by extending the ray tracing procedure of
Medlock et al. 2024
.
We use both halo catalogs and simulation snapshots from the
1P
set of simulations in CAMELS. For simplicity, in this work we only consider the fiducial feedback prescription
(A_{SN1}=A_{SN2}=A_{AGN1}=A_{AGN2}=1)
at
z=0.1
, the snapshot closest to the median redshift of our sample.
The halo catalogs are used to assign halo masses, stellar masses, and stellar radii to each halo in the snapshot.
Next, to minimize contamination from the host ISM, which is poorly resolved at the resolution of CAMELS, gas particles within
0.1R_{\mathrm{200,crit}}
of each halo in the halo catalog are removed from each snapshot.
The snapshots are then processed using
yt

(
Turk et al. 2011
)
to interpolate the electron density field onto a regular grid.
The electron density is then integrated along one of the grid axes over the 25 Mpc/h extent of the snapshot, and divided by two to account for the fact that observed FRB sightlines skewer halfway through their host halos on average.
This integral over a 25 Mpc/h sightline within a snapshot defines
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{h,CGM}}
for this paper.
We argue that for comparisons between simulated and observed FRB sightlines, this definition is better-suited than definitions which fix a boundary between the CGM and IGM as a function of halo mass, which is hard to accurately infer.
One potential drawback is that our definition incurs a bias due to the mean gas density within the
25
Mpc/h snapshot, but this on the order of
\lesssim 5
pc cm
-3

(
Medlock et al. 2024
)
. This bias can be subtracted, and with a
1/\sqrt{N}
scaling of the uncertainties used in this work (Eq.
4
), it is subdominant for fewer than
N\lesssim 200
data points.
After removing sightlines intercepting more than one halo within its
R_{\mathrm{200,crit}}
, like in criteria 2 in §
II
, each sightline can be unambiguously associated with the index of the nearest halo in the halo catalog.
Then, within a range of normalized impact parameters
\rho_{\rm min}<\rho<\rho_{\rm max}
, where
\rho=r_{\perp}/R_{\mathrm{200,crit}}
, one sightline per halo is then selected at random.
This corrects for a preferential sampling of massive halos proportional to their larger two-dimensional cross-section within the snapshot.

In Fig.
2
we plot the
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{h,CGM}}
values for our simulated sightlines within a fiducial range of impact parameters (
\rho_{\rm min}=0.0<\rho<0.05=\rho_{\rm max}
) as a function of stellar mass and feedback model. These predictions are slightly sensitive to the host offset distribution of FRBs: FRB sightlines highly offset from their gas halos may exclude a small portion of
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{h,CGM}}
from their sightlines
(
Medlock et al. 2024
, see Fig. 5 of)
. Though highly-offset FRB sources at impact parameters of
\sim 5R_{\mathrm{eff}}
have been associated with their host galaxies
(
Kirsten et al. 2022
;
Shah et al. 2024
;
Eftekhari et al. 2024
)
, these sources have been cut from our observational sample (criteria 3 in §
II
); most FRBs have offsets of less than
\approx 2R_{\mathrm{eff}}

(
Shannon et al. 2024
, see Fig. 6 in)
. Given that the half-light radius
R_{\mathrm{eff}}
is
\sim 0.015R_{\mathrm{200,crit}}
(
Kravtsov 2013
)
, we may conclude that
\rho\lesssim 0.03R_{\mathrm{200,crit}}
for FRB sightlines in Tab.

, even though the second component of the host offsets of some sources is not well-constrained by their highly elongated localization ellipses
(
CHIME/FRB Collaboration et al. 2025a
)
.

IV
Results

We characterize the
M^{*}-{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}
relation by fitting both the observed data and the simulation data to a quadratic in
x=\log_{10}(M^{*}/(10^{10}M_{\odot}))
.
We choose a quadratic functional form for the mean of the relation because a linear model was found to be insufficient to fit the simulation predictions (see Table
2
, which shows evidence for non-zero curvature in most simulation suites).
We parameterize the mean of the relation using an intercept parameter
b
, a slope parameter
m
, and a quadratic term
a
.
To allow for scatter around this mean, we allow the distribution of
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}
to be scattered around its mean value. We parameterize the statistical distribution of
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}
as a Gaussian distribution. The mean of the distribution is a quadratic in stellar mass and the variance of the distribution is a
\sigma^{2}
, taken as a constant over stellar mass for simplicity.

In the case of the simulation data, our model predicts only the mean and scatter of the CGM contribution to the DM:

{\rm DM}_{\textrm{h,CGM}}(M^{*})\sim\mathcal{N}(ax^{2}+mx+b,\sigma^{2})

(5)

In the case of the observational data, which contain all four components of Eq.
2
, we fit

{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}^{\textrm{Macquart}}(M^{*})\sim\mathcal{N}(ax^{2}+mx+b,\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{i}^{2}).

(6)

When fitting the observational data,
\sigma_{i}
accounts for
\sigma_{\mathrm{cosmic}}
and the uncertainty in the Milky Way contribution to the DM, as defined in Eq.
4
. Therefore,
\sigma^{2}
in the data fit characterizes not only the scatter in the host CGM contribution, but also the scatter in the host ISM and circum-burst contributions as well.

For both observed and simulated data, we fit the model using maximum likelihood estimation with a Gaussian likelihood. Abbreviating the observational measurements of
\rm DM^{\mathrm{Macquart}}_{\mathrm{host,i}}
as
y_{\mathrm{i}}
and the model prediction from Eq.
6
as
\hat{y}_{\mathrm{i}}
, the likelihood is

-\log\mathcal{L}=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\dfrac{(y_{\mathrm{i}}-\hat{y}_{i})^{2}}{(\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{i}^{2})}-\log(2\pi(\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{i}^{2})).

(7)

We use the same likelihood with
\sigma_{i}=0
for the simulation fits with
\rm DM_{\mathrm{h,CGM,i}}
as
y_{\mathrm{data,i}}
.

The Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampler
emcee

(
Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013
)
is used to sample the posterior distribution of the model parameters. We adopt uninformative priors on the parameters, with
\pi(a)\sim\mathcal{U}(-1000,1000)
pc/cm
3
/dex
2
;
\pi(m)\sim\mathcal{U}(-300,300)
pc/cm
3
/dex;
\pi(b)\sim\mathcal{U}(-150,250)
pc/cm
3
, and
\pi(\sigma)=1/\sigma
over the range
[1,200]
pc/cm
3
.

The posterior for the 20 observed FRB sightlines, as well as the parameters best fitting the simulations, are shown in Fig.
3
. We tabulate best-fit values fitted parameters in Tab.
2
. In Fig.
2
, the model prediction corresponding to the best-fit parameter values for the fiducial observational sample and fiducial simulation parameters are plotted as a set of three lines. In addition, 50 randomly-selected parameter draws from the posterior are shaded in translucent bands respectively.

. This is consistent with observational data including contributions to the scatter from the host ISM and CBM, which are not included in the simulation predictions.

b_{obs}>b_{sim}

(

Finally, we see that
m_{obs}\neq m_{\mathrm{Astrid}}

This discrepancy could potentially be explained by the different interpretation of
m_{obs}

m_{sim}

m_{obs}

However, the discrepancy means that a large negative contribution to

and
M^{*}

IV.1
Robustness of the results

Our fiducial analysis provides two lines of evidence against the Astrid model.
One piece of evidence comes from the intercept parameter
b
. We observe that
b_{obs}\approx b_{\mathrm{Astrid}}
.
However, due to the presence of the host ISM in the data and its deliberate omission from our simulation predictions, we would expect
b_{obs}>b_{sim}
. Another piece of evidence comes from the slope parameter
m
. We observe that
m_{obs}\neq m_{\mathrm{Astrid}}
at the
4.2\sigma
level, and that a strong negative correlation between
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{h,ISM}}
and
M^{*}
would be needed to reconcile our data with the Astrid prediction for
m
.

We now vary our fiducial observational analysis and simulation analysis to explore how these two conclusions change, and explore whether the simulations can be reconciled with the data under different analysis choices.

The tension in the intercept parameter
b
may be alleviated with several sources of systematics. Negatively-biased values of
{\rm DM}_{\rm cosmic}
, negatively-biased values of
\langle{\rm DM}_{\textrm{h,ISM}}+{\rm DM}_{\textrm{CBM}}\rangle
, or values of
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{MW,CGM}}
higher than predicted by
Yamasaki & Totani 2020
could all alleviate the tension. In our “
\langle{\rm DM}_{\rm cosmic}\rangle-30\%
” analysis, we decrease the
{\rm DM}_{\rm cosmic}
values used in our
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}^{\textrm{Macquart}}
estimates. Table
2
shows that this leaves some room for
\langle{\rm DM}_{\textrm{h,ISM}}+{\rm DM}_{\textrm{CBM}}\rangle=b_{obs}-b_{Astrid}\approx 14
pc cm
-3
.

Decreasing the Milky Way halo contribution may also alleviate the tension in
b
, since the Milky Way contributes to all sightlines. Averaged over all sightlines in our sample,
\langle{\rm DM}_{\textrm{MW,CGM}}\rangle=37
pc cm
-3

(
Yamasaki & Totani 2020
)
, but extreme feedback scenarios could pull it lower than 30 pc cm
-3

(
Keating & Pen 2020
)
, alleviating the tension by tens of units. If the Milky Way halo model also includes the gas beyond its virial radius as probed directly by the lowest FRB dispersion measures, a Milky Way contribution in the range 52-111 pc cm
-3

(
Cook et al. 2023a
)
would worsen the tension in
b
.

The tension in the slope parameter
m
is more difficult to alleviate, since most systematics which may affect our measurements are correlated with either the stellar mass of the host or the DM of the FRB, but not both. Splitting the sample by redshift in half (“
z<0.1
” and “
0.1<z<0.2
” variants) suggests that the
z<0.1
half has a less steep slope, but the difference between the two halves is not statistically significant, and the reduction in the tension in
m
can be somewhat attributed to halving the sample size: both halves have central parameter estimates of
m<0
.

One possibility is that optical selection effects preferentially associate FRBs with higher mass galaxies, though it is not obvious how this would affect the slope measurement. Nevertheless we check that tightening the association criteria used by the FRB association framework PATH
(
Aggarwal et al. 2021
, Probabilistically Associating Transients with their Hosts)
does not impact our conclusions. In the PATH formalism,
P(O|x)
refers to the posterior probability that the FRB is associated with its a putative host; dropping observational data points with
P(O|x)<0.95
does not change our conclusions (see “P(O|x) > 0.95” variant in Table
2
).

We also test our sensitivity to
{\sigma}_{\textrm{cosmic}}
. This is important because our conclusions about feedback in galaxies depend on our parameter estimates, which in turn may depend on feedback circularly via
{\sigma}_{\textrm{cosmic}}
. For example, IllustrisTNG implements a feedback model stronger than Astrid and weaker than SIMBA, resulting in
\sigma_{\textrm{cosmic}}\approx 60
pc cm
-3
at
z=0
and 105 pc cm
-3
at
z=0.2

(
Walker et al. 2024
)
, whereas the original Illustris simulation finds
\approx 15\%
lower values for
{\sigma}_{\textrm{cosmic}}
due to its more extreme feedback
Jaroszynski 2019
, see Fig. 2 of
. Ideally,
{\sigma}_{\textrm{cosmic}}
(z) could be measured from a large simulation volume at the same mass resolution employing the same sub-grid model in which the model predictions were generated. However, the small-volume CAMELS run of Astrid has a different mass resolution from the large-volume production run of Astrid and puts a direct calculation of
{\sigma}_{\textrm{cosmic}}
out of the scope of this work. We test our sensitivity to
{\sigma}_{\textrm{cosmic}}
by setting it to a constant independent of redshift (“
{\sigma}_{\textrm{cosmic}}
= 80 pc cm
-3
” variant), finding that our parameter estimates are not sensitive to second-order effects of feedback through
{\sigma}_{\textrm{cosmic}}
.

As an extreme test of robustness, we exclude the data point which most drives the slope measurement, FRB 20210807D. The statistical tension in
m
values is reduced to
2.9\sigma
, but the slope (
m_{obs}
) remains negative. We regard this as an upper bound on the effect of single outliers in our sample, whose small size could be enlarged by upcoming FRB surveys
(
CHIME/FRB Collaboration et al. 2025b
;
Wang et al. 2025b
;
Hallinan et al. 2019
;
Vanderlinde et al. 2019
)
.

V
Discussion

Of the three fiducial simulations considered, our observations are most in tension with CAMELS-Astrid, while CAMELS-IllustrisTNG and CAMELS-SIMBA impose more modest requirements on the host ISM and CBM contributions to the slope
m
.
Unless the DM of the circum-burst medium is highly sensitive to global properties of the FRB host (e.g. its stellar mass), the discrepancy between the slope parameters measured in observations and simulations must be explained by the host ISM.
To resolve the discrepancy, our observations require that the host galaxy ISM can produce an anti-correlation between
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{h,ISM}}
and
M^{*}
. Astrid requires
m\approx 180
pc/cm
-3
/dex, whereas the stronger feedback feedback models implemented in IllustrisTNG and SIMBA require a more modest, but still negative, host ISM contribution of
m\approx-100
pc/cm
-3
/dex.

Interestingly, a large contribution from the host ISM at low stellar mass
M^{*}\lesssim 10^{9}
would be qualitatively consistent with the large
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}^{\textrm{Macquart}}
values measured in low-mass dwarf FRB hosts. These large values had previously been attributed at least in part to an extreme circum-burst medium on the basis of scattering timescales
(
Caleb et al. 2023
)
, persistent radio emission
(
Marcote et al. 2020
;
Niu et al. 2021
;
Bhandari et al. 2023b
)
, and extreme rotation measures
(
Michilli et al. 2018
)
.

However, a strong negative correlation between stellar mass and
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{h,ISM}}
would be at odds with the positive correlation between
M^{*}
and non-FRB based tracers of the ISM. For instance,
Bernales-Cortes et al. 2025
report a positive correlation between stellar mass and the component of
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{h,ISM}}
traced by H
\alpha
emission measures in a sample of eleven FRB hosts with VLT/MUSE observations, suggesting a slope contribution of
m\approx 31
pc/cm
3
/dex (albeit over a slightly different range of host stellar masses). In addition, the mass of neutral hydrogen gas is known to be positively correlated with increasing stellar mass in HI-selected galaxies
(
Huang et al. 2012
;
Maddox et al. 2015
;
Parkash et al. 2018
)
.
The existence of positive correlations between ISM gas and stellar mass provide empirical evidence which suggest that the fiducial CAMELS-Astrid simulations should be disfavored. CAMELS-IllustrisTNG and CAMELS-SIMBA are more consistent with the data at the 2
\sigma
level, but still require a host ISM contribution in contradiction with
Bernales-Cortes et al. 2025
.

A detailed synthesis of these various correlations in terms of inflows and outflows between the ISM and CGM as a function of sub-grid model
(
Wright et al. 2024
)
is out of the scope of this work.
Replicating the
M^{*}-{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}
relation measured in this paper and correlations such as those characterized by
Bernales-Cortes et al. 2025
in high-resolution simulations including realistic radiative transfer
(
Orr et al. 2024
, such as extensions of)
will be crucial to simultaneously understanding the impact of the host ISM and CGM on the observed DM of FRBs.

Furthermore, it is an open question whether stellar or AGN feedback is principally responsible for feedback effects in isolated galaxies. In this case, further analysis of the CAMELS suite of simulations could explore the divergence between observations and simulations in the slope of the
M^{*}-{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}
relation. Hydrodynamic simulations of stellar and AGN feedback in isolated galaxies
(
Newton & Kay 2013
)
suggest that stellar feedback dominates in isolated galaxies in the absence of galaxy merger activity. This leads to the notion that in this mass range, we are primarily sensitive to stellar feedback.
The negative slope exhibited by our data may be interpreted in terms of the cold flow paradigm of galaxy formation, which suggests that cool gas flows in sufficiently-massive halos
\gtrsim 10^{12}M_{\odot}

(
Dekel & Birnboim 2006
, see e.g.,)
are shock-heated and rarefied, making them vulnerable to expulsion by
either
stellar or AGN feedback effects.

On the other hand, as pointed out by
Ni et al. 2023b
, the principal difference that sets apart Astrid from IllustrisTNG and SIMBA is the kinetic AGN feedback in ASTRID, which is activated in a more stringent way and with a lower maximum efficiency than in the IllustrisTNG model. In addition, it is known that prescriptions for black hole seeding and dynamics differ and are potentially less realistic in the lower resolution Astrid runs in CAMELS (referred to in
(
Ni et al. 2023b
)
as CAMELS-Astrid) than in the production run of Astrid. SIMBA, which predicts the lowest
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{h,CGM}}
, notably features anisotropic AGN feedback
(
Anglés-Alcázar et al. 2017
;
Davé et al. 2019
;
Khrykin et al. 2024a
)
in which collimated jets expel gas from their halos, and it is thought that anisotropic feedback more effectively expels gas to larger radii than isotropic feedback.

It is possible that both stellar
and
AGN feedback play a role in this mass range, since the reservoir of ionized gas in the CGM couples to both stellar and AGN feedback processes.
This causes a complex interplay between star formation and AGN activity
(
Medlock et al. 2025b
)
.
For instance, at low black hole masses and high redshift, stellar feedback regulates the growth of supermassive black holes by limiting their accretion rate
(
Anglés-Alcázar et al. 2017
)
.
In addition,
Booth & Schaye 2013
suggests, contrary to
Newton & Kay 2013
, that stellar and AGN feedback have opposing influences on the star formation rate: it is plausible that similar competing effects may shape the
M^{*}-{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}
relation.
Interestingly, the data point with the lowest value of
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}^{\textrm{Macquart}}
and highest stellar mass (FRB 20210807A) corresponds to a galaxy which exhibits both a high star formation rate and AGN activity
(
Gordon et al. 2023
)
.
Omitting that data point from the fit (“No FRB 20210807D” variant in Fig.
4
) somewhat alleviates the statistical tension in
m
values between observations and simulations to
\approx 3
sigma, but does not change our conclusion that
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{h,ISM}}
contribution needs to have a negative slope with stellar mass to reconcile any of the three CAMELS simulations with the data.

As pointed out by
Medlock et al. 2025a
, our conclusion about the strength of feedback can be compared with other measurements of baryonic feedback from weak lensing, albeit indirectly.
We quantify the strength of feedback using the matter power spectrum suppression factor.
In simulations like CAMELS, this is defined as
S(k)\equiv P_{\rm hydro}/P_{\rm nbody}
calculated from the same snapshots used in §
III
for our
M^{*}-{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}
measurements. The suppression factors measured in the same CAMELS snapshots are plotted in Fig.
5
for the three models considered here, where
S(k)=1
corresponds to no feedback (i.e. gas traces dark matter exactly), and
S(k)\to 0
corresponds to strong feedback which causes a suppression of the matter power spectrum.
The suppression factor inferred from Dark Energy Survey
(
Chen et al. 2023
)
and Subaru Hyper Suprime Cam
(
Terasawa et al. 2025
)
power spectra are also plotted for comparison in Fig.
5
.

While our conclusions are difficult to compare directly with other gas probes, our conclusion is qualitatively consistent with findings from
Hadzhiyska et al. 2024
, which finds that the feedback implementation in IllustrisTNG is too weak to explain observations of the kinetic Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect. We can provide a comparison to other measurements of baryonic feedback subject to the important caveat that the small volume of CAMELS snapshots considered in this work does not sample the redshift range or the range of the halo mass function to which cosmic shear measurements are most sensitive
(
Tröster et al. 2022
;
Aricò et al. 2023
;
Terasawa et al. 2025
)
. Our comparison probes feedback from galaxies in the range
10^{9}<M/M_{\odot}<10^{11}
which correspond roughly to halos at
M_{h}\sim 10^{11-13}M_{\odot}
halos at
z\sim 0
. In contrast, cosmic shear analyses target halo masses of
M_{h}\sim 10^{12-14}
at earlier times (
z\sim 0.5
for the Kilo-Degree Survey and DES and
z\sim 0.9
for Subaru-HSC). Since CAMELS does not capture structure on scales larger than the box size of 25 Mpc/h (
k>0.03
Mpc
-1
), it does not sample the full range of the halo mass function, or include contributions to the smoothing of the density field on large scales from group and cluster-scale halos.
We thus consider our results as a lower limit on the impact of feedback: in other words, an indirect upper limit on
S(k,z=0)
. We note that other approaches, short of direct angular cross-correlations
(
Wang et al. 2025a
)
, also quantify feedback through metrics other than
S(k,z)
. Those focus on quantities like the DM-redshift distribution
(
Macquart et al. 2020
;
James et al. 2021
;
Sharma et al. 2025
)
, the baryon spread
(
Medlock et al. 2025a
)
and IGM/CGM baryon fractions
(
Khrykin et al. 2024b
;
Khrykin et al. 2024a
;
Connor et al. 2024
;
Hussaini et al. 2025
, see e.g.,)
, and like the work presented here, rely on simulation snapshots to connect their inferences of feedback to
S(k,z)
.

VI
Conclusions and Future Work

In this paper, we have pointed out that the relationship between the stellar mass of a galaxy and its
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}^{\textrm{Macquart}}
is a sensitive probe of baryonic feedback in isolated galaxies. Our findings are as follows:

1.

We have used simulations and a sample of FRB data to explore the
M^{*}-{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}
relation for the first time. We have characterized it in terms of its slope, intercept, curvature, and its scatter. In models of weak feedback like the fiducial Astrid model in CAMELS, as
M^{*}
increases, so does
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{h,CGM}}
.

2.

The observations show – contrary to weak feedback models – that
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}^{\textrm{Macquart}}
tends to be weakly
anti-
correlated with stellar mass in FRB host galaxies in the mass range
10^{9-11}M_{\odot}
at
z\sim 0.1
.

3.

In particular, both the slope and intercept measurements from Astrid are hard to explain without invoking a negative correlation between stellar mass and
{\rm DM}_{\textrm{h,ISM}}
. Self-consistent, high-resolution modeling of the host ISM and CGM are needed to rigorously assess whether this is plausible, but observations indicate that some ISM observables (the HI mass and the H
\alpha
-inferred DM) are positively correlated with stellar mass, providing some empirical evidence against this possibility.

4.

Our measurements at present are in conflict with all three feedback models considered, with the predictions of Astrid being the most discrepant. They constrain the gas environments of FRB host galaxies at
z=0
and
10^{9}<M^{*}/M_{\odot}<10^{11}
, and imply an indirect lower limit on the strength of baryonic feedback via the suppression factor of the matter power spectrum
S(k,z=0)
due to
M_{h}\lesssim 10^{13}M_{\odot}
halos.

This analysis could readily be extended in several ways. Going to larger volumes (higher redshifts) better samples the halo mass function using FRB hosts living in groups and clusters like those presented by
Connor et al. 2023
and
CHIME/FRB Collaboration et al. 2025a
.
Well-matched samples as a function of redshift could be used to study the redshift evolution of the gas environments of galaxies over cosmic time.
However, the foreground posed by the IGM will demand a larger number of sources, since
{\sigma}_{\textrm{cosmic}}
increases with increasing redshift.
In the opposite direction, widefield telescopes like CHORD
(
Vanderlinde et al. 2019
)
and coherent all-sky monitors like BURSTT
(
Lin et al. 2022
;
Connor & Ravi 2022
)
may soon deliver an order-of-magnitude increase in FRBs closer than
<
400 Mpc.
Doing so will extend the
M^{*}-{\rm DM}_{\textrm{host}}
relation downwards in stellar and halo mass, which can allow baryonic feedback to be disentangled from dark matter effects in dark-matter dominated systems like dwarf galaxies. In the long run this could open up the new sub-field of “near-field FRB cosmology”.
