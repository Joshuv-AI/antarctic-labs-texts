Treatment outcomes and associated factors among persons with pulmonary tuberculosis in Morocco: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Omayma Benbrik, Oumaima Bounar, Imad Chakri, Laila Lahlou, Hind Serhane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTuberculosis (TB) remains a major public health challenge. Treatment success rate (TSR) is a key indicator for TB control, yet national level evidence on treatment outcomes in Morocco is fragmented. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate pooled TB treatment outcomes and summarize factors influencing success and unsuccessful outcomes among persons with pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB).MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, ScienceDirect, and Web of Science for studies published up to January 2025. The research question was structured using the PICO framework: participants (adults with PTB), intervention (anti-tuberculosis treatment), and outcomes (TB treatment outcomes). Eligible studies were conducted in Morocco, published in English or French, and reported TB treatment outcomes; non-original articles, studies without outcome data, and those outside Morocco were excluded. Two reviewers independently performed study selection, data extraction, and quality assessment. Meta-analysis was conducted in R software, calculating pooled TSR, unsuccessful treatment outcomes, failure, loss to follow-up, and death rates using a random-effects model. Subgroup analyses were performed by drug-susceptibility status and treatment history. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, and risk of bias was evaluated using Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklists of prevalence and case control studies.ResultsThirty-three studies were included. Pooled estimates showed a TSR of 79.00% (95%CI:72.85–84.07, I²=99%), defined as the proportion of persons with tuberculosis who were cured or completed treatment among all treated cases. The pooled proportion of unsuccessful treatment outcomes was 10.03% (95%CI:7.17–13.85,I²=99%), with separate estimates for treatment failure (1.86%, 95%CI:1.08–3.19), loss to follow-up (5.49%,95%CI:3.47–8.59) and death rate (2.00%, 95%CI:1.20–3.30). Subgroup analysis showed lower TSR and higher unsuccessful outcomes among drug-resistant and retreatment cases. Associated factors for higher TSR included younger age, new and smear-negative cases, and enrollment in integrative health programs. Factors linked to unsuccessful outcomes included older age, male gender, rural residence, and smoking.ConclusionTSR among persons with TB in Morocco remains below the WHO target (≥ 90%), with higher unsuccessful outcomes observed among drug-resistant and retreatment cases. Considerable variability across studies highlights the need for targeted interventions. These findings may inform program strategies to improve TB treatment outcomes.Trial registrationClinical trial number: Not applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13586-y.

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major global public health concern. In 2023, 10.8 million people developed TB, and 1.25 million died, including 161,000 people with HIV. A quarter of the world’s population is infected with TB bacteria, with 5–10% progressing to active disease. Ending the TB epidemic by 2030 is a key target of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals [1]. Over 80% of TB cases and deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries. In the Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMRO), which includes Morocco, TB accounted for 8.1% of global cases and nearly 8% of the global multidrug-resistant TB burden in 2023. Despite interventions, reductions in incidence (4.9%) and mortality (6.2%) between 2015 and 2020 fell short of the End TB Strategy targets, indicating persistent challenges in regional TB control [2].

In Morocco, TB continues to exert a substantial burden, particularly among adults with pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB), despite the implementation of national control programs. The treatment success rate (TSR) is a key indicator used to monitor the effectiveness of TB control strategies and inform programmatic decisions [3]. Published studies from Morocco have reported varying TB treatment outcomes, including TSR, failure, loss to follow-up, and death, as well as factors associated with these outcomes. However, these findings remain fragmented, and no comprehensive synthesis has been conducted to generate pooled estimates or summarize determinants of treatment outcomes across the country. While the Moroccan National Tuberculosis Control Program (NTCP) provides routinely reported aggregate treatment outcomes, these data do not capture variations across regions, study populations, or patient subgroups, limiting the ability to fully understand patterns of treatment success and failure.

A systematic review and meta-analysis of published studies can address this gap by integrating evidence from diverse study designs, which capture contextual and patient-level factors not fully reflected in routine surveillance. By pooling results, such a review provides more precise estimates of treatment outcomes, assesses variability across studies, and identifies populations at higher risk of unsuccessful outcomes. It also allows exploration of subgroup differences, including drug susceptibility status, treatment history, and demographic characteristics, offering insights into determinants of treatment success and failure. This synthesis complements national surveillance by consolidating published evidence, identifying gaps in the literature, and informing research priorities. Furthermore, the findings may guide targeted programmatic interventions and support strategies to improve TB control in Morocco.

In this context, this systematic review aimed to estimate pooled TB treatment outcomes and summarize factors influencing success and unsuccessful outcomes among persons with pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) in Morocco.

Methods

Protocol registration

This study protocol was in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis) checklist, and was registered at PROSPERO (ID = CRD42024628599).

Eligibility criteria

We considered the eligibility of the studied based on: (1) Participants: adults ≥ 18 years old, new and retreated PTB patients, drug susceptible (DS) and drug resistant (DR) cases, (2) Intervention: 6 months regimen (2RHZE/4RH), 8 months regimen for retreatment cases (2SHRZE /1RHZE/5RHE) and treatment regimen for DR cases with rifampicin (R), isoniazid (H), pyrazinamide (Z), ethambutol (E) and streptomycin (S). (3) Outcome: clearly reported treatment outcomes: TSR, unsuccessful treatment outcomes, failure, loss to follow-up, and death rates. (4) Study designs: cross-sectional studies, case-control, prospective and retrospective cohorts, and case series (n ≥ 50).

We excluded studies according to -participants: patients with extrapulmonary tuberculosis or latent TB infection, -outcome: unclearly reported treatment outcomes - study designs: systematic reviews and meta-analyses, case series (n < 50), case reports, commentaries/editorials and unpublished reports. We also excluded studies conducted outside of Morocco, and without accessible full text.

Information sources and search strategy

Comprehensive search was conducted on PubMed, Scopus, Science Direct and Web of Science for articles published in Morocco. The thesis register Toubkal was also searched to look for relevant theses. A search strategy was pretested to determine the appropriate strategy to retrieve relevant articles and accordingly add suitable modifications. A combination of MeSH (medical subjects heading), free-text words and boolean operators (AND, OR and NOT) was used to narrow the search. English terms were used for the databases: PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science, and French ones for Sciencedirect database (Supplementary file 1). No filters were used to have an exhaustive search. The last search was completed on January 2025.

Selection process

Two authors (O.B. and O.B.) independently led the selection process. Initially, the screening of titles and abstracts was conducted using Rayyan (http://rayyan.qcri.org), an AI-powered review management platform. Duplicates were detected and deleted, then relevant articles were identified for further evaluation. Full text reading was proceeded to select the eligible studies. We manualy searched the references of each eligible study for additional including. Selected articles were downloaded and imported into Zotero, a bibliographic management software.

Data collection process and data items

A table compiling the eligible studies was developed. The following data were extracted: author’s name, year of publication, location, study design, sample size, mean age, type of patients with TB (DS or DR-TB), TB category (new or retreatment cases), PTB prevalence (%), HIV serostatus (%), TB treatment regimen and treatment outcomes. When group comparisons were performed, we considered each group as a single study and data were extracted accordinly.

We used the WHO 2021 updated treatement outcome definitions for tuberculosis (Table 1) [4] :

Unsuccessful TB treatment outcome (UTO) was also defined by the combination of death, treatment failure and loss to follow-up.

Information on factors associated with TB treatment outcomes reported in the included studies was systematically extracted and compiled. These factors, including demographic, clinical, and treatment-related variables, were summarized using a narrative synthesis, based on the associations reported in the original studies with treatment success or unfavorable outcomes.

Study risk of bias assessment

We assessed the quality of eligible studies. Two independent authors (O.B. and O.B.) conducted the quality assessment using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools checklists of prevalence and case control studies. This appraisal aimed to assess each study’s methodological quality and to determine how risk of bias in the design, conduct, and analysis were addressed. A third author (H.S.) was consulted if consensus could not be reached. We contacted authors via email when outcome data was missing from the studies. Scores considered were: 6–8 as high quality, 5 − 4 as medium quality and 3 − 0 as low quality.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was carried out with R software, using random effect model to calculate effect sizes and confidence intervals (CI). The pooled TSR was calculated and the overall proportion of unsuccessful treatment outcome was reported, while its components (treatment failure, loss to follow-up, and death rates) were pooled separately to provide precise estimates for each category. We assessed the heterogeneity between studies with Cochrane Q test and I² statistic. Funnel plot and Egger’s test were performed to assess publication bias, using the treatment success rate (TSR) as the primary outcome, additionnaly to the trim-and-fill method that was used to evaluate and adjust pooled effect estimates. We performed a subgroup analysis based on region, drug resistance, new or retreatment category and the period before or after the COVID-19 pandemic. All estimated effect sizes and proportions with 95% confidence intervals have been reported.

Results

Study selection

A total of 1799 articles were identified. After duplicates removal, 1495 articles subjected to further title and abstract screening. A total of 73 articles were retained to full-text review. Fifty five were excluded with reasons: 48 with an unclear outcome, four had a wrong population, two had a wrong study design, and one was a wrong publication type. We manualy added fifteen articles from manual search. Thirty-three articles were eligible and included in this study (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

The majority of the eligible studies were cross-sectional (n = 28 (84.8%)), while four (10.2%) were cohort studies, and only one was a case-control study (9.1%).

Concerning TB categories, thirty-one (93.9%) studies included new cases, while two (6.0%) consisted of retreatment ones. Twenty-two (66.6%) studies addressed DS-TB, seven (21.2%) addressed DR-TB, while one treated (3.0%) the co-infection TB/HIV. Most of the studies relied on 3 years data [1–17]. As for the geographical regions, eligible studies were carried out at : Rabat Sale Kenitra (n = 11), Souss Massa (n = 5), Marrakech Safi (n = 3), Tangier Tetouan Al Hoceima (n = 3), Fes Meknes (n = 3), Casablanca Settat (n = 2), Laayoune Sakia El Hamra (n = 1), Guelmim Oued Noun (n = 1), Beni Mellal Khenifra (n = 1), and Oriental (n = 1). All studies combined a sample size of 69 473 participants. Most of studies (n = 32) were conducted in a health facility setting, and only one was conducted in a penitentiary facility. Risk of bias in studies

We performed a critical appraisal of selected studies, to determine the methodological quality and to identify potential design bias. For each answer, we assigned or not an asterisk, considering the presence or absence of the item in the design. Each asterisk represented a point or level of quality. The final score was determined by the total of asterisks. Results of the studies’ quality were: thirteen (34.2%) articles were high quality (score of 6–8), nineteen (50.0%) were medium quality (5 − 4), and six studies (15.8%) were low quality (3 − 0).

Low-quality studies were mainly characterized by limitations in sample representativeness (inappropriate or unclear sample frame), inadequate sample size, and insufficient reporting or management of response rates. Additionally, some studies lacked standardized and reliable measurement of the condition and/or appropriate statistical analysis, which may introduce selection bias. The detailed risk of bias assessment for each included study is presented in Supplementary file 2.

Study outcomes

TB treatment outcomes

This review underscored that TSR varied from 32.95% to 93.2%. The lowest rate was reported in Sidi Kacem, while the highest rate was reported in Sale, both are situated in Rabat Sale Kenitra region, central Morocco. The TB treatment failure rate varied from 0% to 82% in PTB patients, with a highest rate in Guelmim Oued Noun region. Loss to follow up rates varied from 0% to 34.6%. The highest rate was documented among drug resistant TB patients in Rabat Sale Kenitra region. Unsuccessful TB treatment outcome (UTO) was attributed to the sum of death, treatment failure and loss to follow-up. The UTO ranges from to 0.67% to 46.4%, with highest rate among TB-DR patients (Table 3).

Factors associated with TB treatment outcomes

This systematic review compiled and summarized the factors reported in a subset of the included studies that were associated with TB treatment outcomes. TSR was higher in younger patients (under 14 years old), new and smear negative PTB patients [6]. It was also higher in patients using MEMS (Medication Event Monitoring System) such as smart pill boxes which are an innovative health technology for medication storage with real-time informations to a telemedicine platform in order to track the drug adherence of patients [22]. Additionnaly, being enrolled in an integrative health approach, increases the TB treatment success [19]. It was also noted to be higher among relapse cases in comparison with failure and defaulted cases [37].

Related factors to an unsuccessful treatment oucomes were: rural residency, ages from 25 to 34 years old and over 64 years old [7] and male gender [23]. Smoking was also underlined as a risk factor of treatment failure [7, 24]. Regarding the loss of follow-up of TB patients, pulmonary TB cases [13] and male gender [22] were its associated factors. Meta-analysis

The overall prevalence of PTB among our included studies was 61.96% (95%CI: 47.38–74.66). The pooled prevalence of HIV among TB patients in the reporting studies was 1.89% (95%CI: 0.34–9.89%) (Fig. 3).

The pooled TB treatment outcomes were: the TSR was 79.00% (95%CI: 72.85–84.07). The pooled unsuccusseful treatment outcome (UTO) was 10.03% (95%CI: 7.17–13.85), calculated independently from its components. Separate pooled estimates were obtained for the failure rate: 1.86% (95%CI: 1.08–3.19), the loss to follow up rate: 5.49% (95%CI: 3.47–8.59), the death rate: 2.00% (95%CI: 1.20–3.30). The I² index revealed high heterogeneity, ranging from 98% to 100% (Fig. 4).

Subgroup analysis showed that the highest TSR was observed in studies from Fez Meknes region (87.54%, 95%CI: 70.91–95.29) and the lowest TSR was reported in Rabat Sale Kenitra region (67.18%, 95%CI: 47.94–81.99). The Casablanca Settat region exhibited higher pooled rates of unsuccessful treatment outcome (UTO) (21.967%, 95%CI: 19.81–24.27) compared with other regions.

When stratified by drug-susceptibility status, DR-TB cases had a lower TSR (52.06%, 95%CI: 30.24–73.12 vs. 83.64, 95%CI: 79.35–87.19, p < 0.01) and a higher pooled proportion of UTO (19.87%, 95%CI: 8.54–39.73 vs. 8.07 95%CI: 5.89–10.97, p < 0.01). Similarly, retreatment cases demonstrated lower TSR (70.96%, 95%CI: 70.22–71.68 vs. 79.33%, 95% CI: 72.88–84.58, p < 0.01) and higher UTO rates (25.36%, 95% CI: 19.18–32.73 vs. 9.43, 95% CI: 6.67–13.17, p < 0.01). Subgroup analysis by COVID-19 period did not show statistically significant differences in TSR or UTO rates between pre- and post-pandemic periods (Fig. 5).

Publication bias was assessed using the funnel plot (Fig. 6), for the primary outcome (treatment success rate, TSR), including all 33 studies. Small-study effects were assessed using Egger’s regression test. Egger’s test showed no statistical evidence of small-study effects (p = 0.7894). The trim-and-fill procedure did not identify potentially missing studies. These results suggest no statistical evidence of publication bias.

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis excluding low-quality studies was conducted to assess the robustness of the pooled estimates. Before exclusion, the pooled TSR was 79.00% (95%CI: 72.85–84.07) and the pooled proportion of UTO was 10.03% (95%CI: 7.17–13.85). After excluding low-quality studies, the TSR remained similar at 78.32% (95%CI: 71.38–83.96) and UTO at 10.71% (95%CI: 7.34–15.37), indicating minimal impact of study quality on the overall estimates. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to estimate the treatment outcomes of tuberculosis and their associated factors in Morocco. It retrieved data from 33 studies to determine the pooled TSR and unsuccessful treatment. Rates within subgroups were also analyzed, in DS and DS-TB, new and retreatment cases and at pre- and post-COVID-19 period.

The current review revealed an overall TSR of 79.00% (95% CI: 72.85–84.07). This aligns with findings from other studies in in low- and middle-income countries. This result is identical to the 79.0% pooled success rate reported in a meta-analysis of the African continent [38]. While Morocco’s performance is slightly higher than the 76.2% success rate observed among adult pulmonary TB patients across sub-Saharan Africa [39], it remains lower than the 82.6% rate recently reported in Egypt [40]. This variance may reflect differences in regional healthcare infrastructure, the prevalence of HIV co-infection, and the national surveillance systems. Despite these differences, the consistent gap between the identified 79% rate and the WHO’s 85% benchmark suggests that Morocco faces systemic challenges similar to those of its regional peers [41]. These findings underscore that while the current success rate is representative of the broader regional average, further intervention is required to address the predictors of successful outcomes and meet international elimination targets.

Subgroup analysis reaveled variation in TSR across categories of patients in this review. Notably, DR and retreatment cases showed lower TSR.This discrepancy aligns with existing literature [41–45]. A study found that among retreatment cases, TSR was low in TB/HIV co-infection and with a high load of MTB in sputum smear [43]. In DR-TB patients, TSR was found to be less likely achieved among female patients, younger ones and in bilateral lung involvement, while high TSR was associated with monoresistance [44]. Higher rates of comorbidities was also a predictor of low TSR among DR-TB patients [42], especially undernutrition and severe anemia [46]. DR-TB patients are a vulnerable sub-population. Psychological depression was found to be occuring during their extended treatment and financial constraints as the primary trigger. Treatment adherence of DR-TB patients was also low if the patient was not enrolled in an income generating activity or didn’t receive any psychosocial support [45].

In our review, the pooled prevalence of HIV among TB patients in Morocco was 1.89% (95%CI: 0.34–9.89%), highlighting a relatively low but important high-risk population. Globally, the WHO reported a treatment success rate of 79% among people living with HIV (PLHIV) in 2022 [41]. Evidence from Africa suggests that TB/HIV co-infected patients are twice as likely to experience unsuccessful treatment compared with HIV-negative patients, with predictors including site of infection, immune status, and use of antiretroviral therapy or INH prophylaxis [47]. Only one included study in Morocco reported outcomes among PLHIV, limiting subgroup analysis and the ability to assess the impact of HIV on treatment outcomes. Nonetheless, these findings underscore the need for individualized treatment approaches and closer monitoring, particularly for high-risk groups such as drug-resistant and retreatment cases, to improve TB treatment success.

This review also summarized, from a subset of studies, several factors positively associated with higher treatment success. It was higher among younger patients, new and smear negative PTB patients, being enrolled in an integrative health approach and among patients using MEMS. Effectiveness of digital interventions was a consistent finding across previous studies. Improvements were identified in self-care behaviors, TB knowledge, self-efficacy, social support, and satisfaction with health education [48]. TB treatment supervision was also improved using digital adherence technology especially in resource limited settings [49]. These findings suggest that digital and eHealth solutions can enhance treatment monitoring and patient engagement and substantially improve the TSR.

On an other hand, the overall rate of unsuccessful treatment outcome (10.03%, 95% CI: 7.17–13.85) was within the reported range in other studies. Studies from Africa have similarly reported unsuccessful rates ranging from 9% to 48%, largely driven by loss to follow-up and mortality [38, 50]. These outcomes were frequently associated with several factors. This review reported as related ones: male gender, smoking, rural residency, older age and PTB cases. Unsuccessful treatment outcomes have been consistently associated with structural and social vulnerabilities. Notably, several studies have reported that migrant and homless populations were at increased risk of unfavorable TB outcomes, often due to challenges such as limited access to healthcare, unstable housing, language barriers, and lack of legal protections [51, 52]. This highlights the need for targeted strategies to improve access and continuity of care for mobile and undocumented populations. In line with these findings, a qualitative study identified a range of co-occurring barriers to treatment adherence among TB patients, including food insecurity, transportation costs, lack of family support and income instability [53]. Addressing socioeconomic barriers, stigma, and patient education are critical to reduce unsuccessful TB treatement outcomes.

This study provides a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of published data in Morocco on PTB treatment outcomes and their associated factors, over an 18-year period. It synthesizes evidence from recent reviews and primary studies to identify key trends, highlight existing gaps, and ensure thorough coverage of the literature. The inclusion of multiple study designs broadens the scope of the review, allowing for a more comprehensive synthesis of the evidence. Reported treatment outcomes were analyzed according to the NTCP guidelines of Morocco, adopted from WHO, which enhances comparability with other studies and aligns with established reporting standards.

Some limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting this review’s findings. Most included studies were observational, which limits the causal inference. Furthermore, some primary studies had inadequate adjustment for confounding variables. The substantial statistical heterogeneity observed (I² = 98–100%) warrants careful interpretation. Although a random-effects model accounts for variability, it does not explain its sources. Regional inequalities in healthcare infrastructure, particularly in southern and rural provinces where access to diagnostic tools, trained personnel, and consistent drug supply remains limited, may directly influence TSR [6]. Temporal heterogeneity must also be considered, as the studies were conducted across different NTCP phases with evolving treatment guidelines and monitoring approaches. These temporal variations may, in turn, introduce methodological differences in case definitions, outcome classifications, data collection periods, and surveillance quality. Some studies did not adequately disaggregate outcomes by key subgroups, such as HIV status, DR patterns, and socioeconomic status, limiting comprehensive subgroup analyses, thereby limiting the detection of additional sources of between-study variation. Additionally, associated factors were derived from only a subset of studies, restricting generalizability, and potential publication bias arising from reliance on published data should be considered.

Future research would benefit from high-quality, prospective studies investigating context-specific determinants especially the prevalence and impact of HIV coinfection on TB treatment outcomes among patients in Morocco.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides a comprehensive synthesis of current evidence on PTB treatment outcomes. Overall, TSR appear variable across different populations and settings, with several studies reporting outcomes below the WHO End TB Strategy target of 90%. Poorer outcomes were particularly noted among DR-TB and retreatment cases, though subgroup data were limited and heterogeneous. These findings suggest that current TB treatment strategies may be insufficient for high-risk groups. Context-specific interventions, strengthened health system support, and targeted policies for DR and retreatment cases may help improve treatment outcomes. Future research should aim to standardize outcome reporting and explore factors influencing poor outcomes in order to strengthen TB control efforts and support progress toward global treatment targets.
