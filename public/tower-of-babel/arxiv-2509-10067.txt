Weakening assumptions in the evaluation of treatment effects in longitudinal randomized trials with truncation by death or other intercurrent events
Georgi Baklicharov, Kelly Van Lancker, Stijn Vansteelandt

Abstract
Intercurrent events, such as treatment switching, rescue medication, or truncation by death, can complicate the interpretation of intention-to-treat (ITT) analyses in randomized clinical trials. Recent advances in causal inference address these challenges by targeting alternative estimands, such as hypothetical estimands or principal stratum estimands (e.g., survivor average causal effects). However, such approaches often require strong, unverifiable assumptions, partly due to limited data on time-varying confounders and the difficulty of adjusting for them. Additionally, strict trial protocols frequently lead to (near) violations of the positivity assumption, resulting in limited information for identifying these estimands. In this paper, we propose a novel approach that sidesteps these difficulties by focusing on testing the null hypothesis of no treatment effect in the presence of arbitrary intercurrent events, including truncation by death, using longitudinal trial data. Our key idea is to compare treated and untreated individuals, matched on baseline covariates, at the most recent time point before either experiences an intercurrent event. We refer to such contrasts as Pairwise Last Observation Time (PLOT) estimands. These estimands can be identified in randomized clinical trials without requiring additional structural assumptions, and even in the presence of the aforementioned positivity violations. However, they may still be susceptible to a form of residual selection bias. We show that this bias vanishes under the conditions typically required by alternative methods, and find it to be more generally small in extensive simulation studies. Building on this, we develop asymptotically efficient, model-free tests using data-adaptive estimation of nuisance parameters. We evaluate the method’s performance via simulation studies.



1
Introduction

Many randomized trials are affected by intercurrent events (ICEs), such as treatment switching, rescue medication, truncation by death, etc., that occur after treatment initiation but before measurement of the outcome of interest. These events often complicate an intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis. For instance, an ineffective treatment may appear beneficial in an ITT analysis when patients cannot tolerate it well and therefore switch to a treatment external to the trial. Likewise, a beneficial treatment may appear harmful when patients on the control arm deteriorate and are therefore more often switched to a successful rescue medication. ITT analyses become especially subtle when study outcomes are truncated due to death. In that case, standard analyses, e.g., analyses within survivors, are prone to selection bias and may in particular suggest treatment effects on the study outcome, even when treatment only impacts survival
(
Rosenbaum 1984
)
.

Over the past decades, a rich literature has emerged to address ICEs in clinical trials; the ICH E9(R1) addendum on estimands and sensitivity analysis
(
ICH 2019
)
in particular provides a structured framework for handling ICEs in trials. Much of this work focuses on hypothetical estimands which quantify what the ITT effect would have been had there been no ICEs (of a certain type)
(
Michiels et al. 2021
;
Olarte Parra et al. 2023
;
Morgan et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. This is difficult to defend in the context of truncation by death
(
Chaix et al. 2012
)
, where the central focus has instead been on principal stratum (PS) estimands
(
Frangakis and Rubin 2004
)
, such as the survivor average causal effect (SACE)
(
Robins 1986
;
Hayden et al. 2005
;
Tchetgen Tchetgen 2014
;
Wang et al. 2017
)
. This expresses the average treatment effect in the subgroup of individuals who would have survived until the end of study, regardless of their treatment assignment. These analyses go some way in addressing the above concerns, but also have major limitations. First, hypothetical estimands are difficult or even impossible to identify when, as often, there are (near) positivity violations that essentially prohibit people from not switching treatment; such violations are not uncommon as a result of strict protocol guidelines (e.g., on when rescue treatment is indicated) that make ICEs deterministic
(
Olarte Parra et al. 2023
)
. Survivor average causal effects are likewise difficult to learn from data because the subgroup of individuals who would have survived until the end of study is unobserved. Strong identification conditions are therefore required, such as that all common causes of outcome and death have been measured at baseline; this does not align with the fact that many of these risk factors are time-varying, and themselves affected by treatment
(
Vansteelandt and Van Lancker 2025
)
.

In light of these complications, an alternative line of work has focused on composite estimands designed to avoid extrapolation beyond the observed data
(
Mallinckrodt et al. 2019
)
. In the context of recurrent events, for instance, it is natural to contrast the expected number of events accumulated up to the time of death (or another intercurrent event), or the end of study, whichever occurs first, since no further events can occur thereafter. Such contrasts align with standard competing risk analyses and last observation carried forward (LOCF) approaches. However, hypothesis tests based on these estimands fail to distinguish whether potential treatment effects operate through the outcome process, through the ICE process (i.e., time to ICE), or both. To address this, while-alive or while-on-treatment estimands
(
Schmidli et al. 2023
)
are often employed. These estimands compare the cumulative outcome accrued up to the last time point prior to death (or another ICE), normalized by the time elapsed since baseline. The resulting ratio is intended to adjust for the longer observation time that may arise in one treatment arm due to improved survival (or lower ICE rates). Nonetheless, this adjustment lacks a principled justification and may not adequately disentangle the underlying mechanisms of treatment effect
(
Janvin et al. 2024
)
.

We sidestep this issue by instead comparing treated and untreated individuals – matched on baseline covariates – at the last time point before either individual in the pair dies (or more generally, experiences an ICE). Unlike LOCF, while-alive or while-on-treatment estimands, this approach ensures that comparisons are made at the same time point, mitigating bias due to unequal survival times (or unequal times to ICEs). This leads to better interpretable treatment comparisons, but may nonetheless introduce a form of residual selection bias. We give insight into the nature of this bias, but find it to be small in extensive simulation studies, and show that it vanishes under the conditions typically assumed by existing approaches. Building on this insight, we construct asymptotically efficient, model-free tests of the null hypothesis of no treatment effect, using data-adaptive estimation of nuisance parameters. Our framework accommodates arbitrary (uncensored) outcomes measured in longitudinal clinical trials and arbitrary intercurrent events (including dropout and truncation by death).

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce and motivate our estimands. Section
3
describes identification and estimation strategies. Section
4
reports simulation results to evaluate the finite-sample performance of the proposed methods in comparison with existing approaches. In Section
5
, we discuss assumptions under which valid hypothesis testing is possible. Section
6
concludes with a discussion of the main findings, limitations and directions for future work. Technical proofs and supplementary derivations are deferred to the Appendices.

2
Estimand

Suppose that data are available for a random sample of subjects
i=1,...,n
on a baseline covariate vector
L_{i}
, randomized treatment
A_{i}
and outcome
Y_{i}(t)
, which can be categorical or continuous and is planned to be measured at fixed visit times
t=0,1,\ldots,\tau
(with 0 denoting the baseline visit). Furthermore, let
T_{i}\in\{0,1,\ldots,\tau\}
be the last time that the subject is observed prior to dropout, death or any other ICE. We will informally refer to
T_{i}
as the time to ICE. We denote the vector of observed data for individual
i
by
O_{i}
and we assume mutual independence of
\{O_{i}:i=1,...,n\}
. We let
Y^{a}(t)
and
T^{a}
denote the counterfactual outcome at time
t
and counterfactual time to ICE that would have been observed under assignment to treatment
a=0,1
, respectively. Note that
Y^{a}(t)
is undefined under truncation by death when a subject dies prior to time
t
. Importantly, the estimands introduced below are constructed so that they never depend on such undefined potential outcomes.

In view of the presence of ICEs, the aforementioned LOCF estimand contrasts outcomes just prior to the ICE:

E\left\{Y^{1}(\min(T^{1},t))-Y^{0}(\min(T^{0},t))\right\}.

Because
T^{1}
may differ from
T^{0}
and the outcome may change over time, this contrast may differ from zero even when treatment does not affect the outcome process.
We therefore propose to contrast the outcomes of treated individuals with the outcomes of untreated individuals at the same time, namely the last time both were observed without ICE. In particular, consider drawing two random, independent individuals and assigning treatment to one (for whom we observe
(T^{1},Y^{1}(t),L)
) but not to the other (for whom we observe
(T^{*0},Y^{*0}(t),L^{*})
). Then we will consider the estimands

E\left\{Y^{1}(\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t))-Y^{*0}(\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t))\right\}

(1)

and

\frac{E\left\{Y^{1}(\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t))\right\}}{E\left\{Y^{*0}(\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t))\right\}}.

(2)

We refer to these as Pairwise Last Observation Time (PLOT) estimands.

Alternatively, one may consider drawing 2 random, independent individuals with the same baseline covariates
L=L^{*}
, and assigning treatment to one but not to the other. Then we consider

E\left[E\left\{Y^{1}(\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t))-Y^{*0}(\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t))|L=L^{*}\right\}\right]

(3)

or

\frac{E\left[E\left\{Y^{1}(\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t))|L=L^{*}\right\}\right]}{E\left[E\left\{Y^{*0}(\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t))|L=L^{*}\right\}\right]}.

(4)

We refer to these as Conditional Pairwise Last Observation Time (CPLOT) estimands. They will generally differ from the PLOT estimands suggesting that they are not collapsible. The reason is that when individuals with the same covariates are being considered, then the time at which both are ICE-free will tend to be larger, closer to
t
. CPLOT estimands thus tend to contrast outcomes at later time points than PLOT estimands, except when
L=\emptyset
or
L\mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}T
, in which case they coincide. We generally view them as preferable by ‘truncating’ fewer measurements.

The PLOT and CPLOT estimands, by contrasting outcomes between pairs of treated and untreated individuals, bear some resemblance to the (likewise non-collapsible) win ratio
(
Pocock et al. 2012
)
, which assesses whether a randomly selected treated individual has a more favorable outcome than a randomly selected control. However, in our framework, the purpose of pairing is not to rank outcomes but to ensure that both individuals are evaluated at a common, pre-intercurrent-event time point. In this sense, our estimands can be viewed as balanced variants of the LOCF, competing risk and while-alive approaches, now carrying forward the last available outcome measurement from the final time at which
both
individuals remained free of intercurrent events. This balancing facilitates a more formal separation of the underlying mechanisms of treatment effect than is possible with the while-alive estimand. However, it remains to be investigated whether the proposed estimands reduce to zero when treatment has no effect on the outcome process. We defer this analysis to Section
5
.

3
Identification and estimation

The PLOT and CPLOT estimands are identifiable from the observed data without necessitating assumptions besides consistency and randomization. We provide identification and data-adaptive estimation strategies below. Throughout, unless otherwise stated, we will assume that treatment
A
is randomized, independently of baseline covariates
L
.

3.1
Pairwise Last Observation Time estimand: simple estimator

Under the randomization assumption that
A\mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}(Y^{a}(t),T^{a})
, for all
t
and
a=0,1
, and consistency, we have that

\displaystyle E\left\{Y^{1}(\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t))\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{t}E\left\{Y^{1}(s)|\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t)=s\right\}P\left\{\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t)=s\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{t}E\left\{Y(s)|A=1,A^{*}=0,\min(T,T^{*},t)=s\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\times P\left\{\min(T,T^{*},t)=s|A=1,A^{*}=0\right\}.

The unknown expectations and probabilities in this expression can be readily estimated by averaging across all pairs of treated and untreated individuals, as in

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{t}\frac{1}{\sum_{i,j}A_{i}(1-A_{j})}\sum_{i,j}Y_{i}(s)A_{i}(1-A_{j})I\left\{\min(T_{i},T_{j},t)=s\right\}.

(5)

Similarly,
E\left\{Y^{*0}(\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t))\right\}
can be estimated as (
5
) upon replacing
Y_{i}(s)
by
Y_{j}(s)
, so that an additive effect estimate is obtained as

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{t}\frac{1}{\sum_{i,j}A_{i}(1-A_{j})}\sum_{i,j}\left\{Y_{i}(s)-Y_{j}(s)\right\}A_{i}(1-A_{j})I\left\{\min(T_{i},T_{j},t)=s\right\}.

(6)

The standard error can be computed via the bootstrap. We provide analytical standard errors in the next section, as part of a semi-parametric efficient estimation strategy that allows adjustment for baseline covariates.

3.2
Pairwise Last Observation Time estimand: efficient estimator

Denote
p_{a,s}(L)=P(T>s|A=a,L)
for
a=0,1
and
s\in\{-1,0,\ldots,\tau\}
, and
\mu_{a,s,u}(L)=E\{Y(s)|A=a,L,T>u\}
for
a=0,1
and
(s,u)\in\mathcal{I}=\{(s,u):s\in\{0,\ldots,\tau\},\ u\in\{s-1,s\}\}
.

Proposition 1
.

Under (conditional) randomization (
A\mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}(Y^{a}(t),T^{a})|L
, for
a=0,1
) and the consistency assumption,
E\left\{Y^{1}(\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t))\right\}
can be identified as

\sum_{s=0}^{t}E\{p_{0,s-1}(L)\}E\left\{p_{1,s-1}(L)\mu_{1,s,s-1}(L)\right\}-I(t>s)E\{p_{0,s}(L)\}E\left\{p_{1,s}(L)\mu_{1,s,s}(L)\right\}.

(7)

A proof is given in Appendix A. Define
\eta_{a,s}=E\{p_{a,s}(L)\}
and
\gamma_{a,s,u}=E\{p_{a,u}(L)\mu_{a,s,u}(L)\}
, and denote the observed data vector of one individual by
O
. Then, by the chain rule, we have that the efficient influence function (EIF) of (
7
) under the nonparametric model
(
Pfanzagl 1990
;
Bickel et al. 1993
)
equals

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{t}EIF_{\eta_{0,s-1}}\gamma_{1,s,s-1}+\eta_{0,s-1}EIF_{\gamma_{1,s,s-1}}-I(t>s)\left(EIF_{\eta_{0,s}}\gamma_{1,s,s}+\eta_{0,s}EIF_{\gamma_{1,s,s}}\right),

(8)

where
EIF_{\eta_{a,s}}
and
EIF_{\gamma_{a,s,u}}
are the EIFs of
\eta_{a,s}
and
\gamma_{a,s,u}
, respectively, with

\displaystyle EIF_{\eta_{a,s}}

\displaystyle=p_{a,s}(L)+\frac{I(A=a)}{P(A=a|L)}\left\{I(T>s)-p_{a,s}(L)\right\}-\eta_{a,s},

\displaystyle EIF_{\gamma_{a,s,u}}

\displaystyle=p_{a,u}(L)\mu_{a,s,u}(L)+\frac{I(A=a)}{P(A=a|L)}\left\{Y(s)I(T>u)-p_{a,u}(L)\mu_{a,s,u}(L)\right\}-\gamma_{a,s,u}.

We further write
\varphi_{\eta_{a,s}}(O)=EIF_{\eta_{a,s}}+\eta_{a,s}
and
\varphi_{\gamma_{a,s,u}}(O)=EIF_{\gamma_{a,s,u}}+\gamma_{a,s,u}
to denote the respective uncentered influence functions.
From this we can immediately derive the EIF of
\Psi_{t}=E\left\{Y^{1}(\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t))-Y^{*0}(\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t))\right\}
under the nonparametric model.

Proposition 2
.

The EIF of
\Psi_{t}
under the nonparametric model is

\sum_{s=0}^{t}\xi_{s,s-1}(O)-I(t>s)\xi_{s,s}(O)-2\Psi_{t},

where

\xi_{s,u}(O)=\varphi_{\eta_{0,u}}(O)\gamma_{1,s,u}+\eta_{0,u}\varphi_{\gamma_{1,s,u}}(O)-\varphi_{\eta_{1,u}}(O)\gamma_{0,s,u}-\eta_{1,u}\varphi_{\gamma_{0,s,u}}(O).

It follows from Proposition
2
that a debiased machine learning
(
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
)
estimator for
\Psi_{t}
is given by

\hat{\Psi}_{t}=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{s=0}^{t}\hat{\xi}_{s,s-1}(O_{i})-I(t>s)\hat{\xi}_{s,s}(O_{i}),

(9)

where
\hat{\xi}_{s,u}(O)=\hat{\varphi}_{\eta_{0,u}}(O)\hat{\gamma}_{1,s,u}+\hat{\eta}_{0,u}\hat{\varphi}_{\gamma_{1,s,u}}(O)-\hat{\varphi}_{\eta_{1,u}}(O)\hat{\gamma}_{0,s,u}-\hat{\eta}_{1,u}\hat{\varphi}_{\gamma_{0,s,u}}(O)
. Here,
\hat{\eta}_{a,s}
and
\hat{\gamma}_{a,s,u}
are debiased machine learning (AIPW) estimators for
\eta_{a,s}
and
\gamma_{a,s,u}
respectively, obtained as the sample averages of

\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}_{\eta_{a,s}}(O_{i})

\displaystyle=\hat{p}_{a,s}(L_{i})+\frac{I(A_{i}=a)}{\hat{P}(A_{i}=a|L_{i})}\left\{I(T_{i}>s)-\hat{p}_{a,s}(L_{i})\right\},

\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}_{\gamma_{a,s,u}}(O_{i})

\displaystyle=\hat{p}_{a,u}(L_{i})\hat{\mu}_{a,s,u}(L_{i})+\frac{I(A_{i}=a)}{\hat{P}(A_{i}=a|L_{i})}\left\{Y_{i}(s)I(T_{i}>u)-\hat{p}_{a,u}(L_{i})\hat{\mu}_{a,s,u}(L_{i})\right\},

where all nuisance parameters are substituted by consistent estimators, indicated with a hat.
The variance of this estimator can be consistently estimated as 1 over
n
times the sample variance of
\sum_{s=0}^{t}\hat{\xi}_{s,s-1}(O_{i})-I(t>s)\hat{\xi}_{s,s}(O_{i})
.

To estimate the nuisance functions
p_{a,u}(L_{i})
and
\mu_{a,s,u}(L_{i})
, we employ cross-fitting with two complementary strategies tailored to survival and longitudinal outcomes. First, conditional survival probabilities
p_{a,u}(L)=P(T>u|A=a,L)
are estimated using survival random forests
(
Ishwaran et al. 2008
)
, fitted separately within training folds and used to obtain out-of-fold predictions under treatment and control regimes (
a=1,0
). Second, for the conditional mean outcome regressions
\mu_{a,s,s}(L)=E\{Y(s)|A=a,T>s,L\}
and
\mu_{a,s,s-1}(L)=E\{Y(s)|A=a,T>s-1,L\}
, we utilize Super Learner
(
Van der Laan et al. 2007
)
, applied to appropriately constructed long-format data sets indexed by time
s
. Specifically, at each fold we stack observations across time points, restricting to those at risk at the relevant horizon, and fit ensemble regressions separately for the treated and control arms. In our implementation, the Super Learner library consists of main effects generalized linear models (with and without elastic-net regularization), random forests, and generalized additive models. Out-of-fold predictions are then generated at each
s\leq t
, with baseline values carried forward when
Y_{0}
is observed directly. This cross-fitted procedure ensures valid nuisance estimates while mitigating overfitting.

Under simple randomization (
A\mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}L
),
\eta_{a,s}
and
\gamma_{a,s,u}
as well as their uncentered influence functions simplify. In that case,
P(A=a|L)=P(A=a)
,
\eta_{a,s}=E\{P(T>s|A=a,L)\}=P(T>s|A=a)
and
\gamma_{a,s,u}=E[E\{Y(s)I(T>u)|A=a,L\}]=E\{Y(s)I(T>u)|A=a\}
. Let
\varphi^{*}_{\eta_{a,s}}
and
\varphi^{*}_{\gamma_{a,s,u}}
denote the uncentered influence functions where we replace
P(A=a|L)
by
P(A=a)
. Define
\xi^{*}_{s,u}
as
\xi_{s,u}
but with
\varphi^{*}_{\eta_{a,s}}
and
\varphi^{*}_{\gamma_{a,s,u}}
. As shown in Appendix B,

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{t}\xi^{*}_{s,s-1}(O)-I(t>s)\xi^{*}_{s,s}(O)-2\Psi_{t},

(10)

then corresponds to the EIF of
\Psi_{t}
under a semiparametric model with the single restriction that
P(A=a|L)=P(A=a)
, for
a=0,1
. It is readily seen that (
10
) is obtained from (
8
) by substituting
P(A=a|L)
by
P(A=a)
. Consequently, the estimator

\hat{\Psi}^{*}_{t}=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{s=0}^{t}\hat{\xi}^{*}_{s,s-1}(O_{i})-I(t>s)\hat{\xi}^{*}_{s,s}(O_{i}),

(11)

is asymptotically efficient within this restricted semiparametric model, and thus in particular at least as efficient as the estimator from the previous section when both
P(T>u|A=a,L)
and
E\{Y(s)|A=a,L,T>u\}
are consistently estimated. Moreover, this estimator exhibits model double robustness (proof in Appendix C) in the sense that it is asymptotically unbiased when either
P(T>u|A=a,L)
and
E\{Y(s)|A=a,L,T>u\}
, or the propensity score, are consistently estimated. As the latter can be guaranteed when
A\mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}L
(in which case the propensity score can be consistently estimated as a sample proportion), (
11
) is an asymptotically unbiased estimator for
\Psi_{t}
, regardless of whether
P(T>u|A=a,L)
and
E\{Y(s)|A=a,L,T>u\}
are consistently estimated.

The resulting estimation strategy is of interest, even when choosing
L=\emptyset
. In this case,
\hat{\Psi}_{t}
reduces to

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{s=0}^{t}Y_{i}(s)I(T_{i}>s-1)\left\{\frac{A_{i}}{\hat{P}(A=1)}\hat{p}_{0,s-1}-\frac{1-A_{i}}{\hat{P}(A=0)}\hat{p}_{1,s-1}\right\}\\
&\qquad\qquad-I(t>s)Y_{i}(s)I(T_{i}>s)\left\{\frac{A_{i}}{\hat{P}(A=1)}\hat{p}_{0,s}-\frac{1-A_{i}}{\hat{P}(A=0)}\hat{p}_{1,s}\right\},\end{split}

(12)

where
\hat{p}_{a,s}=\hat{P}(T>s|A=a)
.
This estimator is algebraically equivalent to (
6
), but can be computed in
O(n)
time rather than
O(n^{2})
. Its variance can be estimated using the same influence function-based approach, but with
L=\emptyset
.

To formalize the asymptotic properties of
\hat{\Psi}^{*}_{t}
, we introduce some additional notation. Let
\mathbb{P}_{n}
denote the empirical measure (i.e., the sample average). For a function
f(O)
of the data
O
, we write
\mathbb{P}\{f(O)\}=\int f(O)\mathbb{P}(O)dO
, where
\mathbb{P}(O)
is the data density. Importantly,
\mathbb{P}\{\hat{f}(O)\}
averages over
O
, rather than over the estimator
\hat{f}
. When the propensity score is consistently estimated at parametric rate (e.g., as a sample proportion), we have the following asymptotic result (with proof given in Appendix D).

Theorem 1
.

For a randomized treatment
A
,
\sqrt{n}\left(\hat{\Psi}^{*}_{t}-\Psi_{t}\right)
converges to a mean zero normal distribution with variance equal to the variance of (
10
), when the following conditions are satisfied:

1.

The propensity score is consistently estimated at
\sqrt{n}
-rate (e.g., as a sample proportion)
and all other nuisance parameters are estimated on a sample independent of the one on which
\hat{\Psi}^{*}_{\tau}
is evaluated (e.g., using cross-fitting), and

2.

the following convergence conditions hold for
a=0,1
:

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{t}\mathbb{P}\left\{p_{a,s}(L)-\hat{p}_{a,s}(L)\right\}=o_{p}(1),

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{t}\mathbb{P}\{p_{a,s}(L)\mu_{a,s,s}(L)-\hat{p}_{a,s}(L)\hat{\mu}_{a,s,s}(L)\}=o_{p}(1),

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{t}\mathbb{P}\{p_{a,s-1}(L)\mu_{a,s,s-1}(L)-\hat{p}_{a,s-1}(L)\hat{\mu}_{a,s,s-1}(L)\}=o_{p}(1).

This theorem suggests that the proposed estimator allows for using flexible, data-adaptive estimation strategies for the nuisance parameters
P(T>u|A=a,L)
and
E\{Y(s)|A=a,L,T>u\}
, including variable selection, splines, and other forms of regularization, as well as modern machine learning methods or hybrid approaches that combine statistical and algorithmic learning. While such flexible techniques usually induce non-negligible regularization bias and may deliver estimators with slow convergence rates
(
van der Laan and Rose 2011
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
)
, the theorem merely demands consistent estimators, regardless of the rate of convergence. This is likewise the case for standard AIPW estimators of the ATE in simple randomized trials, where consistent estimation of the conditional outcome means suffices in order to achieve root-
n
consistency and asymptotic linearity (and thus normality).
Consequently, we can construct a
(1-\alpha)100\%
confidence interval for
\Psi_{t}
as
\hat{\Psi}^{*}_{t}\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\hat{v}^{1/2}
, with
z_{1-\alpha/2}
the
(1-\alpha/2)
-quantile of the standard normal distribution and
\hat{v}
given by 1 over root-
n
times the sample standard deviation of the EIF of
{\Psi}^{*}_{t}
, with all unknowns substituted by consistent estimators.

Notably, if the known randomization probability is used rather than estimated, condition 2 of Theorem
1
vanishes entirely. We nonetheless recommend estimating the propensity scores as this improves efficiency and reduces variance when other nuisance parameters are not consistently estimated
(
Rotnitzky et al. 2010
)
, and may more generally give finite-sample benefits. A drawback, however, is that the aforementioned confidence interval may fail to cover at the nominal rate when inconsistent nuisance parameter estimators are used. This can be remedied by additionally accounting for the uncertainty in estimating the propensity score (as in
Van Lancker et al. 2024
).

In observational studies where
L
is sufficient to adjust for confounding of the effect of
A
on both the outcome and the time to ICE, the nonparametrically efficient estimator (
9
) remains valid, though this requires stronger conditions on the convergence rates of the nuisance parameters (see Appendix D).

3.3
Conditional Pairwise Last Observation Time estimand

While the PLOT estimand can be readily estimated as (
6
) by comparing all pairs of treated and untreated individuals, the curse of dimensionality prohibits a similar strategy for the CPLOT estimand, as it is generally impossible to find treated and untreated individuals with exactly the same baseline covariates. This inherently makes identification and estimation more challenging and, as we show later, necessitates stronger rate conditions for the estimator presented below.

For the estimand

\Phi_{t}=E\left[E\left\{Y^{1}(\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t))-Y^{*0}(\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t))|L=L^{*}\right\}\right],

we have the following identification result (with proof in Appendix A).

Proposition 3
.

Under (conditional) randomization (or
A\mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}(Y^{a}(t),T^{a})|L
for
a=0,1
) and the consistency assumption,

5
Assumptions for valid treatment effect testing

While we have argued that the PLOT and CPLOT estimands facilitate a more formal separation
of the underlying mechanisms of treatment effect than is possible with the LOCF and while-alive estimands, they may nonetheless deviate from zero even in the absence of a treatment effect on the outcome. This can be seen upon rewriting (
1
) equivalently as

E\left\{Y^{1}(\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t))-Y^{0}(\min(T^{*1},T^{0},t))\right\}.

From this, we see that, when comparing an individual with itself, outcomes under treatment and control are compared at time points
\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t)
and
\min(T^{*1},T^{0},t)
that are the same in distribution, though not necessarily identical.

To illustrate this phenomenon, we present a simple pedagogical example with a continuous outcome obeying the following data-generating model

Y(t)=\alpha(t)+\beta_{0}L+\beta_{1}Lt+\gamma_{0}U+\gamma_{1}Ut+\epsilon_{t},

(17)

where
L
and
U
are baseline covariates and
\epsilon_{t}
is an independent mean zero random error term. Clearly, there is no treatment effect on the outcome. Under this model,
\Psi_{t}
reduces to (proofs are provided in Appendix F)

\beta_{1}E\left\{(L-L^{*})\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t)\right\}+\gamma_{1}E\left\{(U-U^{*})\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t)\right\}

while
\Phi_{t}
(conditioning on only
L
) reduces to

\gamma_{1}E\left[E\left\{(U-U^{*})\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t)|L=L^{*}\right\}\right].

The PLOT estimand is zero when either treatment does not affect the time to ICE,
L
and
U
are independent of the time to ICE, or
\beta_{1}=\gamma_{1}=0
, i.e., when the additive effect of time on the outcome is not modified by any baseline covariates that also influence
T
. The CPLOT estimand is zero under the weaker conditions that either treatment does not affect the time to ICE,
U
is independent of the time to ICE conditional on
L
, or
\gamma_{1}=0
, i.e., when all such effect modifiers are measured at baseline.
Otherwise, the estimands may deviate from zero under the null. To give insight into the extent of such deviations, Table

\exp(2.5+0.8A+L+0.2(2A-1)U)
.

T
is not problematic, unless it modifies the additive time effect on the outcome. If all such time effect modifiers are measured at baseline (i.e., if

To formally examine the conditions under which the proposed estimands preserve the null hypothesis of no treatment effect, we define

Y^{a}(t)
(with a slight abuse of notation) to be the outcome at time

t
under treatment assignment

a
in the hypothetical situation that no ICEs occur. Under this setup, the null hypothesis corresponds to
Y^{1}(t)=Y^{0}(t),\forall t
. Although such hypothetical reasoning may feel less natural in the presence of truncation by death, it remains a common starting point for formulating assumptions, even in the truncation by death literature
(
Hayden et al. 2005
;
Ding et al. 2011
)
. Moreover, note that the proposed estimands do not rely on outcome measurements obtained after ICEs, and that we will avoid such hypothetical reasoning later in this section.

Under the suggested null hypothesis, we can now write

\displaystyle E\left\{Y^{1}(\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t))-Y^{*0}(\min(T^{1},T^{*0},t))\right\}

\displaystyle=\iiint yf_{Y^{1}(\min(v,v^{*},t))|T^{1}}(y|v)f_{T^{1}}(v)f_{T^{*0}}(v^{*})\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}v^{*}

\displaystyle\qquad-\iiint yf_{Y^{0}(\min(v,v^{*},t))|T^{0}}(y|v)f_{T^{0}}(v)f_{T^{*1}}(v^{*})\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}v^{*}

\displaystyle=\iiint yf_{Y^{1}(\min(v,v^{*},t))}(y)f_{T^{1}}(v)c_{1}(F_{Y^{1}(\min(v,v^{*},t))}(y),F_{T^{1}}(v))f_{T^{0}}(v^{*})\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}v^{*}

\displaystyle\qquad-\iiint yf_{Y^{0}(\min(v,v^{*},t))}(y)f_{T^{0}}(v)c_{0}(F_{Y^{0}(\min(v,v^{*},t))}(y),F_{T^{0}}(v))f_{T^{1}}(v^{*})\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}v^{*},

where
c_{1}(F_{Y^{1}(\min(v,v^{*},t))}(y),F_{T^{1}}(v))
and
c_{0}(F_{Y^{0}(\min(v,v^{*},t))}(y),F_{T^{0}}(v))
are the copula densities corresponding to the copulas of
(Y^{1}(t),T^{1})
and
(Y^{0}(t),T^{0})
, respectively. From this we observe that the PLOT estimand may differ from zero, except when treatment does not affect outcome, and moreover the dependence between
Y^{1}(t)
and
T^{1}
is the same as between
Y^{0}(t)
and
T^{0}
in the sense that
the copulas of
(Y^{1}(t),T^{1})
and
(Y^{0}(t),T^{0})
are equal for all
t
. This copula condition is sufficient but not necessary. For the CPLOT estimand, a similar assumption is sufficient, except now involving conditional copulas, given
L
.
While we deem these assumptions to be relatively weak, especially when conditioning on baseline covariates
L
, they are nonetheless difficult to judge.
In Appendix F, we therefore show that the CPLOT estimand reduces to zero under the null hypothesis when one of the following assumptions holds:

Assumption 1
.

T^{1}=T^{0}
almost surely;

Assumption 2
.

\forall t:\,\underline{Y^{a}(t)}\mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}(T^{a}>t)|L
, for
a=0,1
;

Assumption 3
.

\forall t:\,Y^{a}(t)-Y(0)\mbox{$\perp\!\!\!\perp$}T^{a}|L
, for
a=0,1
,

where
\underline{Y^{a}(t)}
is the vector consisting of
Y^{a}(t)
and all future values of
Y^{a}(s),s>t
. Assumption
1
states that the treatment does not affect ICE times. If it holds, both estimands are trivially zero under the null. Assumption
2
is essentially the same as that invoked by IPW estimators of hypothetical estimands (which, unlike our proposal, invoke an additional positivity assumption).
Assumption
3
, which is relevant for additive contrasts (and thus not for estimand (
4
)) is satisfied when there are no unmeasured common causes of outcome and
T
that modify the additive time effect on the outcome. For the PLOT estimands to return zero under the null, these same assumptions with
L=\emptyset
are sufficient. That these assumptions are much stronger, motivates our preference for CPLOT estimands, despite their stronger rate requirements.

When hypothetical reasoning ‘in the absence of ICEs’ is artificial, we may instead define the null as

6
Discussion

In this article, we have proposed new methodology for analyzing longitudinal clinical trial data in the presence of intercurrent events. Causal analyses targeting hypothetical or principal stratum estimands are attractive in such settings, but they face major challenges: absence of data on time-varying confounders or lack of flexible methodology to handle them (for PS approaches), frequent (near-)positivity violations, and reliance on strong assumptions about the joint distribution of counterfactuals (for PS approaches). These issues raise concerns about robustness. Motivated by this, we deliberately set a less ambitious target: avoiding hypothetical reasoning and instead restricting attention to tests of the null hypothesis of no treatment effect. Our approach is based on novel (C)PLOT estimands, which compare outcomes of pairs of individuals at their last common ICE-free time. This possibility of comparing outcomes at earlier times was briefly mentioned in the discussion of
Buyse 2010
for the handling of missing data in the estimation of the win ratio. It more generally avoids extrapolation to unobserved post-ICE outcomes while still targeting the full study population. Importantly, the (C)PLOT estimands can be identified without assumptions beyond randomization and consistency.

The nature of (C)PLOT estimands, however, introduces additional subtleties. We showed that even under the null, non-zero values may arise because of the selective timing of pairwise comparisons. This phenomenon is intrinsic to our strategy and does not occur with competing approaches such as IPCW or PS, though those methods typically rely on stronger identification assumptions. In principle, it could be avoided by redefining the estimand to compare each individual with itself under treatment and no treatment. Yet such a formulation would demand strong assumptions about unobserved counterfactual outcomes, thereby forfeiting the robustness that motivates our approach. Thus, while (C)PLOT estimands involve a subtle trade-off, they achieve a balance between interpretability and robustness that is not attainable with alternative formulations.

In particular, we have demonstrated that our proposal remains valid for treatment effect testing under essentially the same or even weaker assumptions than IPCW or PS. For instance, for additive contrasts of continuous outcomes, we allow unmeasured common causes of outcome and time to ICE provided they do not modify the additive effect of time on the outcome. To formalize such conditions, we introduced a copula-based representation of the joint dependence between potential outcomes and time to ICE. This formulation yields general sufficient conditions under which the proposed estimands equal zero under the null. It also provides a foundation for future sensitivity analyses, by specifying copula families and varying their parameters to assess the impact of assumption violations.

Monte Carlo simulations showed that our method performs well in moderate samples, including scenarios where competing approaches fail. When deviations from zero are expected, they are relatively small and typically smaller than those from alternative methods. Across all settings considered, our estimators were also clearly more efficient, yielding consistently lower variance than IPCW or PS estimators.
By avoiding hypothetical reasoning, our approach seeks to make maximal use of observed data, thereby enabling weaker assumptions and improved precision. The trade-off is reduced interpretability of the estimand. Future work will therefore explore variants with enhanced interpretability, such as the probability that a treated individual has a more favorable outcome than an untreated individual in a random (matched) pair at their last common ICE-free time.
Although our main focus has been on randomized treatments, the methodology extends naturally to observational studies, provided that baseline covariates
L
suffice to adjust for confounding of the effects of treatment on both the outcome and time to ICE. The estimators remain essentially unchanged, but the final condition in Theorems
1
and
2
becomes stronger, as it must also include rate conditions on the propensity score estimator, similar to those for standard AIPW estimators (see Appendix D).

Appendix E: Simulation study details

More details about the alternative methods

We applied the SACE estimator as proposed by
Hayden et al. 2005
and implemented in
von Felten et al. 2025
. Survival probabilities were estimated using logistic regression models with main effects for all baseline covariates, including the baseline outcome. The IPCW estimator gives an estimate for the hypothetical estimand for the treatment effect had there been no intercurrent events. It was obtained as the treatment coefficient from a generalized linear model including the same covariates, fitted among survivors and weighted by the inverse of the estimated survival probabilities. These probabilities were derived from a logistic regression with main effects of all baseline covariates. The LOCF method compared the last observed outcomes of all individuals using an unpaired t-test. The naive survivors method restricted this comparison to outcomes among survivors only.
