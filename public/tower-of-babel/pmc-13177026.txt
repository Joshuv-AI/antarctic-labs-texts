Patient Navigation With Hospital Addiction Consultation and Patient-Directed Discharge
Authors: Jan Gryczynski, Courtney D. Nordeck, Christopher Welsh, Robert P. Schwartz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This secondary analysis of a randomized clinical trial investigates the association of a bedside patient navigation intervention with odds of patient-directed discharge among hospitalized patients receiving addiction consultation services.

Introduction

Hospital patients with comorbid substance use disorder (SUD) are at elevated risk of poor outcomes, including patient-directed discharge (PDD).1 Hospitals are increasingly establishing specialty addiction consultation services (ACS) to improve care for this challenging population.2,3 We examine the association of a bedside patient navigation (PN) intervention, as well as patient characteristics, with PDD in the context of hospital-based ACS.

Methods

This is a nonprespecified secondary analysis from the Navigation Services to Avoid Rehospitalization (NavSTAR) trial (NCT02599818), a parallel randomized clinical trial conducted 2016 to 2019 at a large urban academic hospital with 400 medical or surgical inpatients with comorbid SUD for opioids (78.5%), cocaine (53.5%), alcohol (35.3%), or a combination of these (see trial protocol in Supplement 1). Participants were recruited by research staff through ACS team referral and assigned 1:1 to treatment as usual (TAU; hospital-based ACS) vs TAU plus NavSTAR PN. The trial found that NavSTAR was effective in reducing hospital readmissions.4 The original study was approved by the University of Maryland institutional review board, with approval extending to this study given that participants consented to share their health records. Participants provided informed consent. Reporting followed the CONSORT reporting guideline.

Delivered by master’s degree–level social workers, NavSTAR addressed barriers to recommended SUD and medical treatment, self-care, and basic needs using motivational support and proactive barrier resolution. NavSTAR was initiated at the bedside with a focus on rapport building and patient-centered discharge planning. Services continued in the community for 3 months after discharge.

In this study, we focused on the association of NavSTAR’s in-hospital component with PDD from the initial hospitalization. Data came from baseline interviews and electronic health records. The outcome of interest was PDD. The primary estimate of intervention associations was from unadjusted logistic regression. Secondarily, we fit a multivariable model to improve precision and characterize associations with PDD. Explanatory variables included age (years) and dichotomous factors for female sex, membership in a racial minority group, unhoused status, and infection-related admission (the most common admission category). An initial model included SUD type, dropped due to nonsignificance and stability of other parameters. Data were analyzed December 2025 to January 2026 using Stata MP version 19 (StataCorp). Associations were tested with a significance level of .05 (2 sided).

Results

Among 400 participants (43.0% female and 57.0% male; 55.5% Black, 1.5% Hispanic, 0.3% American Indian or Alaska Native, 0.5% Asian or Pacific Islander, and 42.3% White; median [IQR] age, 47 [35-54] years), the proportion of PDD was twice as high for 200 participants in TAU (12.0%) than for 200 participants in NavSTAR (6.0%), a significant difference (odds ratio [OR], 0.47 [95% CI, 0.23-0.96]; P = .04) (Table). The adjusted model showed a similar outcome. Age was negatively associated with PDD (OR per 1-year increase, 0.95 [95% CI, 0.91-0.98]; P = .004). Participants were more likely to initiate PDD if they lacked stable housing (OR, 2.61 [95% CI, 1.11-6.11]; P = .03) or were hospitalized for infection (OR, 2.83 [95% CI, 1.20-6.72]; P = .02).

Discussion

Leaving an inpatient admission before being medically cleared can undermine progress and increase risk of poor outcomes. PDD in this secondary analysis of a randomized clinical trial was considerably higher than the general population rate of 1% to 2%,5 although samples with SUD tend to have higher rates.1,6 People with SUD may leave the hospital prematurely for myriad reasons, including inadequately managed withdrawal, compromised decision-making, competing responsibilities, perceived stigma, and distrust of the medical system.

Prior research suggests that receipt of ACS is associated with reduced PDD among hospital patients with SUD.6 This study found that even among patients seen by a well-established ACS, a bedside visit from a patient navigator with the promise of postdischarge assistance was associated with reduced risk of PDD. Patient navigators can thoughtfully advocate for patients to remain engaged in their treatment plan without leaving the hospital prematurely.

This study has limitations. It was conducted at a single hospital, and PDD was not a prespecified outcome. NavSTAR is most intensive after discharge, although the in-hospital portion is critical for establishing a relationship and planning. Unmeasured variables likely contributed to PDD. Recent changes in the drug supply may complicate withdrawal management and potentially influence PDD. Future research should optimize strategies to reduce PDD among hospital patients with SUD.
