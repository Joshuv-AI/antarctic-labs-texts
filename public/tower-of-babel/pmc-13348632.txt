Phytohormones producing endophytic fungi Paecilomyceslilacinus modulated metabolic, enzymatic, and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems of Zea mays L. under heavy metal stress
Authors: Muhammad Musa, Rabia Khan, Imdad Ullah, Niaz Ali, Muhammad Fawad Khan, Misbah Bint Riaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe continuous growth of the global population is intensifying the challenge of sustaining future food production. Among the major constraints to agricultural productivity, heavy metal (HM) contamination of soils has emerged as a serious environmental problem that adversely affects crop growth and yield. Environmentally friendly strategies are therefore needed to mitigate HM stress in plants. Endophytic fungi have gained attention for their potential to enhance plant tolerance to abiotic stresses. In this study, the endophytic fungus Paecilomyces lilacinus was evaluated for its ability to produce phytohormones and alleviate lead (Pb) and cobalt (Co) stress in maize (Zea mays L.).ResultsFirstly, culture filtrate of P. lilacinus was analyzed for phytohormone production under Pb and Co stress conditions. The fungus produced significant amounts of gibberellic acid (43.01 µg mL⁻¹), salicylic acid (2192.1 µg mL⁻¹), and abscisic acid (35.4 µg mL⁻¹), along with measurable protein content (170.06 µg mL⁻¹) in cobalt contaminated filtrate. Secondly, pot experiments were conducted to evaluate the effect of P. lilacinus inoculation on maize plants grown under different concentrations of Pb and Co. Inoculated plants showed increased endogenous levels of GA₃, SA, and ABA, along with significantly higher chlorophyll content compared to non-inoculated controls. The fungal association also enhanced antioxidant capacity, as indicated by increased 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) inhibition activity, which reached 90.9% under Pb (90 mg) and 89.1% under Co (90 mg). Similarly, the highest ABTS inhibition activity (96%) was recorded under Pb (90 mg). Moreover, P. lilacinus inoculation significantly increased the activities of different antioxidant enzymes, including catalase, ascorbate acid oxidase, and peroxidase. Enhanced uptake of Pb and Co from the soil was also observed in inoculated maize plants compared with control plants.ConclusionThe findings demonstrate that Paecilomyces lilacinus mitigates heavy metal toxicity in maize by enhancing phytohormone production and strengthening antioxidant defense mechanisms in a symbiotic association with the host plant. This endophytic interaction improves plant tolerance to Pb and Co stress and promotes metal uptake, highlighting the potential of P. lilacinus as a sustainable biological approach for reducing heavy metal toxicity and improving crop productivity in contaminated soils.Keywords: Fungal endophyte, Phytohormones, Metabolites, HM stress, Antioxidant system, Zea mays L. Response to HM stress

Introduction

Heavy metals (HMs) pollution has affected living organisms throughout the world, industrialization and urbanization of cities are widely recognized as the major sources of heavy metal pollution [1, 2]. The current levels of HM in soil, air and water are much higher particularly in urban centers [3] Further, due to rapid increase in human population, the demands for fertilizers, pesticides, industrial products, metal plating, sewage irrigation, tanning and other chemicals have increased tremendously and this has paved the way for excessive HM accumulation [4]. Along with natural sources, there are certain anthropogenic sources like industrial practices that contribute to the contamination of natural environment by the addition of different HMs e.g. Pb and Co [5].The negative impacts of cobalt contamination on plants such as reduction of phytohormones production and stunting are widely recognized. Additionally, it also inhibits the active transport in plants, leaf abscission and leaf stunting. Moreover, Co is involved in production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), hydroxyl radicals (OH) formation, production of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) radicals, enhanced synthesis of Malondialdehyde, proline, and changes in the levels of antioxidant enzymes [6]. Similarly, Pb is a non-essential HM [7] and Pb in higher concentration could adversely affect physiological, biochemical, and molecular pathways of plants [8, 9]. Higher Pb concentrations have been reported to affect cell membrane permeability, hormonal imbalances, suppressing different sulfhydryl-containing enzymes, mineral content as well as and water balance which cause production of ROS [10, 11].

To combat soil contaminated with HM, various physical, chemical and biological treatments have been adopted. Given their higher cost, duration and technical complexities [12], the use of microorganisms or their enzymes to neutralize deleterious HMs in contaminated environment has proven to be the most promising approach [13].The application of microbes to contaminated soil for remediation is an environmentally friendly, cost-effective and practical approach for dealing with HMs pollutants [14]. Plant-associated endophytes can reduce the HM restraints, which can help plants to assemble greater percentage of metals without increasing phytotoxicity [15].

A number of symbiotic endophytic fungi having significant tolerance against HMs as well as plant growth promoting potential have been reported [16].These endosymbionts play the role of bio-stimulants and enhance the host plant development and growth [17, 18]. For example, endophytic fungi in their symbiotic association promote the production of phytohormones affecting key metabolic and physiologic pathways, enabling host defense against stressful conditions [19, 20]. Similarly, in stressful environments endophytes could produce phytohormones that positively affect metabolism, reproduction, and the overall growth of host plant species [21]. Symbiotic fungal endophytes also induce intrinsic secondary metabolites that enhance plant growth and improve signaling and interlinkages with the host plant. Likewise, different beneficial enzymes are released by fungal endophytes that play a vital role in mobilizing nutrients and resources that would otherwise be unavailable to plants [22].

Owing to its global outputs, maize is one of the major cereal crop in the agriculture sector and a conduit towards sustainable agriculture [23]. Although, maize is generally heavymetal tolerant, having the ability to accumulate high levels of HMs in its foliar parts with adequate bioaccumulation factor. Still, recent studies have indicated that HMs stress could affect both the dry matter and yield potential in maize [24]. Given these attributes, maize has the potential of phytoextraction of metals from contaminated soils by translocating them from roots into the shoots [25, 26]. Since both Co and Pb could negatively affect maize crops and its yield potential, and excessive accumulation of both HMs is driven by the increasing anthropogenic demands. Notably, our recent understanding of the mitigation potential using P. lilacinus as biofertilizer against Co and Pb stress in maize plants is very limited and needs enhancements. Therefore, the current study specifically aims to explore the potential of P. lilacinus to promote the production of phytohormones and enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidants by mitigating the harmful impacts of lead and cobalt contamination. Our findings may facilitate understanding the role of endophytic fungi in ameliorating HMs stress tolerance and enhancement of maize crops productivity, a step towards “zero hunger society”.

Methodology

Acquisition of the HM (Pb and Co)-resistant fungal isolate

The previously isolated HM-resistant fungal isolate P. lilacinus (Accession number PP824803) [12] from Justicia adathoda (collected from the HMs contaminated area of Marble Industrial Estate Nowshera) was obtained from the Plant Microbe Interactions Lab, Department of Botany, Abdul Wali Khan University Mardan. Plants of J. adathoda can grow on soils contaminated with HMs. Similarly, cadmium, chromium, copper, nickel, lead, manganese, cobalt and zinc are the most common HMs reported from the collection area [27].

Estimation of phytohormones in HM-supplemented culture filtrates

To determine gibberellins (GA3) in the culture filtrate, healthy Triticum aestivum L. seeds were stepwise de-embryonized. Healthy and mature seeds were soaked in sterile water to soften the seed coat. Before removing the seed coat, a small incision was made using a sterile scalpel and endosperm was separated from the embryo using forceps. Then the grains were washed and treated with 70% ethanol for 30 s and washed thoroughly with distilled water to remove ethanol. Sterilized seeds were kept in Petri dishes containing 10 mL of buffer solution and 2 mL of the fungal filtrate and these plates were incubated for 48 h at 25 ℃. The solution of each plate was filtered and mixed with 0.2 mL Benedict´s solution. The solution was evaporated for 30 min [28], and the optical density (OD) was checked at 254 nm.

Salicylic acid in the culture filtrate was determined following a previously described procedure [29]. Freshly prepared FeCl3 (0.1%; 0.1 mL) and 1 mL supernatant of the culture were mixed. The final volume of the solution was 3 mL. The solution appeared violet and OD was measured at 540 nm using spectrophotometry. Blank samples contained FeCl3 (0.1%) only.

Abscisic acid (ABA) was estimated following [30] with slight changes. A total of 1 mL CF and 60 mL of methanol: chloroform:2 N ammonium hydroxide (12:5:3 v/v/v) was mixed and kept at − 20 °C for use in further steps. The mixture (60 mL) was then mixed with 25 mL of deionized water (dH2O) and then the chloroform (upper phase) was extracted, whereas the lower phase was heated to evaporate the water-methanolic phase. The pH value of the water phase was maintained, and ethyl acetate (15 mL) was mixed at each of the 3 steps and kept in anincubator at 70 °C for 1 h for incubation. The ethyl acetate was evaporated at 45 °C after incubation, and ABA was isolated using thin-layer chromatography (TLC) from the glass plaques and then mixed with methanol (2 mL) for filtration and separation. Whereas, the absorbance was read at 263 nm.

Quantification of the total protein content of the culture filtrates

Total proteins were estimated by the procedure of [31]. Initially, 0.5 mL of copper sulfate (1%) and the same amount of sodium potassium tartrate (2%) were mixed to prepare Biuret Reagent. Then 50 mL of Na2CO3 (2%) in 0.1 N of sodium hydroxide was also mixed. Fungal supernatant 0.1 mL was diluted with distilled water to make a 1 mL solution; same volume of Biuret Reagent was added and thoroughly mixed then 0.1 mL of diluted Folin-Ciocalteau reagent (1:1) and incubated at room temperature for half an hour and OD was measured at 650 nm while Folin-Ciocalteau reagent was used as blank.

Microscopy of P. lilacinuscolonies

For microscopy of P. lilacinusa small piece of the colony was collected, and a tiny drop of water was placed on a microscope slide and covered with a coverslip. A gentle pressure was applied to the coverslip to flatten the specimen. The mycelia were visualized at magnifications of 40× and 100× under a light microscope (Binocular NSL-CX23 Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Using a previously described method, a Lactophenol cotton blue reagent was used as a staining and mounting medium to examine fungal colonies [32].

Determination of phytohormones in maize

GA3 and ABA were determined following the method described by [30]. Maize leaves of 0.5 g were ground and mixed with 60 mL of methanol: chloroform:2 N ammonium hydroxide (12:5:3 v/v/v), and pH was maintainedto 2.5, followed by the extraction procedure involving the addition of 15 mL of ethyl acetate and 25 mL of distilled water to each sample; two transparent phases were formed, and the upper phase (chloroform phase) was removed. The remaining mixture was incubated for 60 min at 70 °C to isolate GA3 and ABA. Later on, the sample was evaporated at 45 °C and methanol (2 mL) was also mixed. The OD of the samples was measured at 254 and 263 nm for GA3 and ABA, respectively.

Similarly, salicylic acid was quantified using previously described method of [29]. In 0.1 mL of freshly prepared maize extract, a total of 2.99 mL chilled freshly prepared ferric chloride (0.1%) was mixed, and the volume raised to 3 mL. The color of the mixture appeared violet. This change in color to violet was measured spectrophotometrically at 540 nm and ferric blank (0.1% ferric chloride solution)was used.

Assessment of chlorophyll and carotenoids of maize plants inoculated with and without P. lilacinus

Chlorophyll and carotenoids in maize were also estimated [34]. Freshly harvested leaves of maize were mixed with 2 mL of acetone (80%), and the final volume raised to 7 mL. The OD of Chl a, Chl b, and carotenoids were checked spectrophotometrically at 480, 645, and 663 nm respectively.

Determination of primary and secondary metabolites in maize plants

For tannin contents the previously described method of [35] was followed. Maize leaves (200 mg) were finely grinded in 70% acetone and kept for six hours in a shaking incubator. Finally, 0.5 mL of Folin and 2.5 mL of NaCO3(20%) were added, and the mixture was homogenized and OD of the mixture was measured at 725 nm.

Estimation of total lipid content was carried out following the standard protocol of [36].Total 0.2 g of maize leaves were grinded in chloroform: methanol (2:1 v/v), and the mixture were shifted to tubes and shaken vigorously, 0.8 ml of NaCl (0.73%) was added and kept at room temperature. Three different layers appeared and the lower layer containing lipids was collected using separating funnel. After separation 0.1 ml of sulfuric acid was added to the lipid layer, and the mixture was mixed thoroughly. The sample was heated at 100◦C for 10 min, followed by cooling and adding 2.4 ml of vanillin reagent, the optical density was recorded at 490 nm upon the appearance of pinkishcolor against and pure canola oil was used as blank.

For electrolyte leakage (EL) determination, maize leaves were washed with autoclaved water to remove the surface-adhered electrolytes and the leaf blade was cut into discs of equal size. All the discs were shifted to capped vials containing dH2O (10 mL) and kept in the shaking incubator at 120 rpm for 24 h, with the temperature adjusted to 25 °C. The conductivity of the initial phase (L1) was measured, and the discs were then autoclaved at 120 °C for 20 min. Then, the electrical conductivity of the second phase (L2) was measured. the percent EL was calculated using following formula [37]:

Malondialdehyde (MDA) was quantified following [38]. Sample extract of 0.1 mL was added to 2 mL of hiobarbituric acid and 2 mL of trichloracetic acid. Then 1.9 mL of dH2O and heated at 100 °C for 10 min. After cooling the sample was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 20 min and optical density of the supernatant was taken at 532 nm.

Antioxidant assays of maizeplants

DPPH activity

The DPPH free radical-scavengingactivity of maize leaves was estimated following the method of [39]. Initially reaction mixture containing 2 mL extract of maize leaves and the same amount of freshly prepared DPPH solution (0.04 mg/mL) was prepared. The reaction mixture was incubated for 30 minutesin the dark, and the OD was read at 517 nm; methanol was used as a blank.

ABTS activity

The ABTS assay based on estimation of the degree of [2,2-azobis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonate)] was performed [40]. ABTS + was generated by reacting ABTS aqueous solution (7 mM) with potassium persulfate (2.45 mM) in the dark for 12–16 h. Initially, stock solution was prepared by adding 1 mL of ABTS to 50 mL of ethanol for absorbance 0.7 ± 0.02 at λ = 734 nm. The test sample was consisting 3 mL stock solution and 20 µL of the plant extract. The absorbance was read at 734 nm the ethanol was used as blank.

Determination of enzymatic antioxidant activities (catalase, Ascorbic acid oxidase, and peroxidase) of maize plants

Catalase was determined in maize plants using the previously described method by [41] with minor modifications. The AAO activity was measured following [42]. Additionally, standard protocol was used to evaluate the peroxidase activity [43]with slight modification.

Quantification of HMs by atomic absorption spectrophotometer

To determine Pb and Co in maize plants using atomic absorption spectrophotometry, initially 0.5 g of oven dried maize seedlings were subjected to acid digestion.A total 1 mL of perchloric acid (HClO4) and 4 mL of nitric acid (HNO3)were used. The sample was cooled at room temperature andthe mixture was filtered through Whatman 42 filter paper. Final volume of the mixture was adjusted with distilled water to 25 mL.Same procedure was followed for control seedlings [44].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26. One-way ANOVA was used to compare treatment means, and post-hoc comparisons were performed using Duncan Multiple Range Test (DMRT) to identify the least significant differences (LSD) among treatments at p = 0.05. All graphical representations of the results were prepared using GraphPad Prism version 9.

Results

In the current study, the isolate i.e., P. lilacinus was first assessed for the release of various phytohormones from culture filtrate supplemented with various concentrations of Pb and Co followed by a pot experiment to assess HM stress alleviation. Similarly, the production of different phytohormones, non-enzymatic antioxidants, and enzymatic antioxidant potential in the host maize plant were evaluated.

The microscopic analysis of the colony of P. lilacinus was carried out. A clear microscopic image is presented in Fig. 1 for understanding the external features of spores.

The production of three different phytohormones was analyzed in the culture filtrate spiked with lead and cobalt at different concentrations to check the resistance of the isolate in heavy metal contaminated medium (Fig. 4). Similarly, a steady decrease was noted at higher concentrations of the HMs. The highest amount of GA3 (42.43 µg/mL) was recorded at Pb 400 µg/mL followed by Co 200 µg/mL (39.33 µg/mL) (Fig. 2a). SA production was concentration-dependent in Czapek broth and the highest production of SA was measured under 800 µg/mL of both Pb and Co (Fig. 2b). Similarly, the production of SA and ABA was improved with elevated levels of Pb and Co in the Czapek broth media, in which the maximum production of SA (1922.63 µg/mL) and highest ABA (49.33 µg/mL) was noted at Co 400 µg/mL concentration of the HMs (Fig. 2c).

Effects of CF of P. lilacinus, Pb, and Co on radicle and plumule lengths of maize seedlings

Preliminary screening for the alleviation of Pb and Co stress was carried out to check the growth promotion potential ofP. lilacinus in maize seedlings. The radicle and plumule lengths in maize seedlings inoculated with and without P. lilacinus were recorded. In our study, the radicle and plumule lengths decreased in a concentration-dependent manner. Plants grown under Co stress revealed an apparent decrease in the radical (1.6 ± 0.14) and plumule lengths (2.3 ± 0.15) at a concentration of 400 ppm, while the plants grown with the fungal inoculum increased the lengths of the radical (1.97 ± 0.15) and plumule (2.9 ± 0.21) compared to the Co positive control [radicle (1.2 ± 0.12)and plumule (2.2 ± 0.10)]. Similarly, the seedlings grown with the fungal inoculum under Pb 400 ppm also decreased the radical (1.63 ± 0.15) and plumule (2.4 ± 0.13) lengths (Table 2).

Discussion

Sustainable agriculture is a high-priority for both developing as well as the developed countries. More recently, environmental hazards in association with HMs stress have continued to limit both crop yield potential as well as the quality thereby, making crops unsuitable for both food and feed purposes [45, 46]. Researchers have reported the harmful effects of HMs which pave the way to food insecurity however, the exact mechanism how these HMs enter into plant tissues is still unclear [47]). In symbiotic association, endophytic microbes release phytohormones in the host plant tissues which may reduce the harmful effects of HMs in host plants [48]. Microbes residing within the plant tissues have adopted various mechanisms to withstand HMs stress through evolutionary adaptation to severe environments [49]Among these microbes, endophytic fungi exhibit high tolerance to HMs [50].Here, the phytohormone-producing endophytic fungus P. lilacinus resistant to HMs was evaluated for the mitigation of Pb and Co stress and improvement of phytohormone, enzymaticantioxidant, and non-enzymatic systems in maize plants.

The P. lilacinus strain could produce hormones (GA3, SA, and ABA) in the culture filtrates supplemented up to 800 µg/mL of Pb and Co in the Czapek broth media. The hormonal production was dose-dependent and associated strongly with P. lilacinus. Maximum amount of GA3 (42.43 µg/mL) was released in Pb 400 µg/mL while the highest production of SA was measured under 800 µg/mL of both Pb and Co i.e. 1922.63 µg/mL and 2115.2 µg/mL respectively (Fig. 2a and b. The ABA content was also gradually influenced up to 600 µg/mL of lead and cobalt stress 42.43 µg/mL and 49.33 µg/mL respectively (Fig. 2c). Literature reveals that microbes produce hormones in the culture filtrate that are absorbed by plants in symbiotic association [33] included. Additionally, hormones produced by endophytic fungi under stress can influence various secondary metabolites, such as flavonoids released by endophytic fungi thatallow stress resistance in host plants [51]. Furthermore, P. lilacinus produced significantly higher amounts of proteins in the culture filtratescontaminated with various concentrations of Pb and Co in comparison to the control treatments.The highest protein content was measured under the highest concentration (400 µg m/L) of Co (170.06 µg m/L) and Pb (132.06 µg m/L) (Fig. 4).Meanwhile, proteins are the primary molecules that play an essential role in plants defense mechanism therefore, this increase was likely. Endophytes boost plant immunity with the production of proteins and phytohormones to overcome stress. Further, microbes could induce production of proteins like surfactin and mycosubtilin etc. which activates the plant innate immune response to stresses [52].

Similarly, phytohormones regulate the responses of plants to withstand HM stress [31] by transmitting signaling molecules in plants [53]. In symbiotic associations, endophytic fungi trigger phytohormone production in host plants and produce phytohormone-based signaling variations. Such a unique exchange of cross-kingdom phytohormones formed between thehost plantsand endophytes protects plants against HMs stress [54].Among these phytohormones, GA3counteracts the inhibitory effects of environmental stress, such as HMs pollution, by causing DELLA degeneration [55]. Additionally, gibberellins positively boost plant agronomic features, whereas SA is a defensive hormone and endophytic fungi release a very potent hormone producing signals [46, 56].The ABA, is a stress hormone produced by fungal endophytes in symbiotic associations with host plants and promotes ABA-deficient plants to synthesize more ABA to mitigate HM stress [57]. Additionally, ABA controls the stress-responsive transcription factor expression such as ABA-responsive element (ABRE)-binding protein/ABRE-binding factor (AREB/ABF), APETALA2/ethylene responsive factor (AP2/ERF), NAM-no apical meristem, Petunia+ATAF1–2-Arabidopsis thaliana activating factor+CUC2-cup-shaped cotyledon,Arabidopsis (NAC; NAM, ATAF, and CUC), basic leucine zipper (bZIP), myelocytomatosis oncogenes (MYC), and myeloblastosis (MYB). Consequently, these transcription factors can regulate genes responsible for stress other than HMs. It regulates the expression of antioxidant enzymes, molecular chaperones, ion channel proteins, and osmo-protectants [58]. Maize plants exposed to Pb and Co stress and inoculated with P. lilacinus showed increased phytohormone production compared to non-inoculated controls. Gibberellic acid (GA₃) concentrations reached 74.72 µg/g under Pb stress and 70.53 µg/g under Co stress at 60 mg/kg. This indicates that P. lilacinus association can enhance hormonal regulation in maize under heavymetal stress, supporting previous findings on fungal-mediated stress tolerance in plants (Fig. 5a). Similarly, the amount of SA in maize plants was also improved in the P. lilacinus inoculated plants in initial 2 concentrations then a clear decline was observed (Fig. 5b). The ABA was also enhanced in the maize plants inoculated with P. lilacinus under Pb 90 µg/g (56.46 µg/g) (Fig. 5c). Our findings are aligned with previous studies [4]where, the authors have found higher phytohormones (GA3, SA, and ABA) concentrations in S. lycopersicum plants inoculated with the fungal isolate P. spadiceum while, the control plants produced much lower amounts of these phytohormones.

Chlorophyll a, b and carotenoids are the primary photosynthetic pigments. Chlorophyll a and b play critical roles in oxygen production and absorption of blue light.Whereas, carotenoids regulate the growth and developmental processes of plants and plays a key role in the interactions of plants with the surrounding environment [59]. These photosynthetic pigments are highly sensitive to HMs stress [60]. Furthermore, HMs significantly decrease chlorophyll content by inhibiting enzymes that are mainly responsible for their biosynthesis, disturbing the absorption of Mg2+ and other elements (such as Fe and Mn) and increasing chlorophyllase activity [61]. In a recent study [62], the authors have revealed that photosynthetic pigments were significantly enhanced by endophytic fungi (Piriformospora indica). Here too, Chl a, b as well as carotenoid contents were significantly enhanced in symbiotic maizeplants grown under Pb and Co stress, followed by minor decline due to the high level of Pb and Co stress. Highest amount of chlorophyll a (7.40 mg/g) was measured under 30 mg Pb/100 (6a), whereas the highest amount of chlorophyll b (6 mg/g) was measured in plants inoculated with P. lilacinus(Fig. 6b). Similarly, the carotenoids were gradually decreased in the increasing concentrations of Pb and Co stressed plants inoculated with P. lilacinus (Fig. 6c). These results are comparable to previous studies where higher chlorophyll and carotenoid contents of wheat plants strongly associated with symbiotic endophytic fungi [63].

Metabolites, which act as non-enzymatic antioxidants play a vital role in plants natural defense under HMs contamination as well as other abiotic stressors [64, 65]. Endophytic fungi secrete these metabolites within the host plants in symbiotic associations, relieve HMs and secondary oxidative stress by improving the antioxidant and metabolic systems [66, 67].Tannins are secondary metabolites sequestered in vacuoles and act as natural plant defenders against pathogens, pests, and also play a role in regulation of seed permeability and dormancy [68]. Furthermore, tanninsact as free radical scavengers under HMs stress conditions [69]. Here, tannins increased significantly (195.33 µg/g)in maize plants associated with P. lilacinus and supplemented with Co 90 mg/100 (Fig. 7a).Similar results with enhanced tannins have been also been reportedearlier [70]. Furthermore, lipid (primary metabolite) production was also improved in the symbiotic plants with elevated Pb and Co concentrations, whereas plants inoculated but without HM stress showed enhanced lipid production. These results are comparable to those of [71], where chromate stress induced higher lipid production in Stemphylium lycopersiciand Stemphylium solani associated soybean plants.

Electrolyte leakage is a phenomenon that occurs as a result of disruption of the biological membranes. Upon exposure to the selected concentration of the metals, the host plants show a dose dependent increase in electrolyte leakage (Fig. 8a), that are in line with recent studies [72]. Moreover, Malondialdehyde (MDA) perform an essential role in plant adaptation and survival under HMs stress [73]. MDA is a lipid breakdown product and a marker for oxidative damage [74]. It was noted that here, that the level of MDAenhancedin maize plants that wereexposed to different concentrations of Pb and Co stress. The enhancements in MDA may cause higher ROS production and lipid membrane damages. However, when maize plants were inoculated with P. lilacinus reduction in lipid peroxidation wasrecorded. In contrast, the lipid content of the plants increased under Pb and Co stress compared to the control plants (Fig. 8b).Thisdecrease in lipid peroxidation may lower cellular membrane damages in maize plants under HMs.

ROS plays important roles in plants physiology, i.e. it increasethedamages caused by oxidative stress and function as a signaling molecule [75]. Under HM stress, plants produce large amounts of ROS because of their ability to alter the permeability of mitochondrial membranes and decrease the production of ROS-clearing enzymes in the antioxidant system. The binding of ROS to cellular membranes and HMs induced direct and indirect interactions with macromolecules cause the accumulation of HMs and reduce oxidative stress in plants [65].Non-enzymatic antioxidants (DPPH and ABTS) scavenged ROS in maize plants. In DPPH assays, percent inhibition was highest in P. lilacinus-inoculated plants under Pb 90 mg/100. Similar trends were observed for ABTS activity, with the highest inhibition in Pb 90 mg/100. The similar findings were obtained by [3], their DPPH level was also enhanced in endophytes inoculated plants. Enzymatic antioxidants like catalase, ascorbate acid oxidase, peroxidase can scavenge stored ROS in plants to enhance their resistance [76, 77]. Among these antioxidant enzymes, catalase transforms H2O2 into water and oxygen, while ascorbate peroxidases convert H2O2 into H2O and use it as an electron donor. POD oxidizes aromatic electron donors, such as guaiacol and pyrogallol, at the expense of H2O2. Therefore, in the present study, P. lilacinusassisted maize plants in synthesizing large quantities of these enzymes that mitigated the negative impacts of these HMs. Similar trends were also found and that inducing free amino acid metabolism in LHL06-inoculated roots increased production of stress-related proteins [glutathione S-transferase L3, isoflavone reductase-like, chalcone isomerase A, NAD(P)H dehydrogenase (quinone), FQR1-like 1 isoform X2, and Peroxidase 3] to mitigate phytotoxicity [78].

Conclusion

The endophytic fungal isolate, P. lilacinus produces significant amounts of phytohormones in the culture filtrate spiked with different concentrationsof Pb and Co. Similarly, this isolate was able to mobilize different metabolites, enabling the host maize plants in symbiotic association to withstand Pb and Co stress. We argue that P. lilacinusin symbiotic association enhanced both the primary and secondary metabolites of host plants and augmented antioxidant systems (enzymatic and non-enzymatic) during Pb and Co stress. It is desirable that the isolate may be further tested in field trials to ensure positive attributes of the endophytic fungus are maintained, before application of P. lilacinusas a phytohormones producing biostimulant at a commercial scale for maize cultivation on HMs contaminated soils. We believe these findings will be helpful in understanding the role of endophytic fungi in ameliorating HMs stress, enhancing crop productivity through phytohormone production and addressing the concerns related to sustainable food security.
