Non-pharmacological interventions for improving sleep quality in adults aged 50 years and older: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Junyu Wang, Yuan Yuan, Ying Bai, Bopeng Qiu, Yiqi Li, Tao Wang, Fuhong Wang, Zhongli Wang, Zhiyu Xiong, Yong Yang, Jie Zhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSleep disturbances are highly prevalent among adults aged 50 years and older and are associated with substantial public health burdens, including impaired cognitive function, reduced quality of life, and increased healthcare utilization. Although non-pharmacological treatments (NPTs) are increasingly recommended as first-line strategies, their comparative effectiveness and optimal implementation characteristics remain unclear. This study aimed to systematically compare the efficacy and acceptability of NPTs for improving sleep quality in adults aged 50 years and older.MethodsA systematic review and Bayesian network meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials were conducted using PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, and Web of Science from inception to April 1, 2025. Eligible trials enrolled adults aged 50 years and older with sleep disturbances (Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index ≥ 5) and evaluated one of 25 predefined NPTs. Global sleep quality was analyzed using standardized mean differences, and all-cause dropout was assessed using risk ratios under random-effects models. A minimum clinically important difference was defined as a standardized mean difference of -0.89. Restricted cubic spline meta-regression was used to examine nonlinear associations with baseline sleep severity and intervention frequency.ResultsA total of 132 randomized controlled trials including 10,872 participants were included. Nineteen NPTs significantly improved sleep quality compared with control conditions. Combined aerobic and resistance training demonstrated the largest effect (standardized mean difference -1.56; 95% credible interval -2.08 to -1.03). Fourteen interventions met or exceeded the minimum clinically important difference; only combined aerobic and resistance training, acupressure, and aerobic exercise alone significantly surpassed this threshold. Meta-regression indicated maximal benefit at a baseline Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index score of approximately 16 and with daily intervention delivery. Only usual care was associated with a lower dropout rate compared with control.ConclusionCombined aerobic and resistance training, acupressure, and aerobic exercise showed the strongest benefits and most consistently exceeded the minimum clinically important difference in adults aged 50 years and older. Other NPTs also improved sleep quality and may be clinically useful in appropriate settings. Prioritizing individuals with moderate-to-severe sleep disturbance and implementing daily interventions may optimize population-level sleep outcomes.Trial registrationPROSPERO CRD420251046892.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-026-01892-5.

Introduction

Sleep is a fundamental determinant of health and functional capacity in adults aged 50 years and older. As individuals age, physiological changes in circadian regulation, sleep architecture, and homeostatic drive often contribute to reduced sleep duration, fragmented sleep, and diminished sleep quality [1]. These disturbances are not merely age-related inconveniences but are closely associated with increased risks of cognitive decline, depression, cardiovascular disease, metabolic disorders, and impaired immune function. Poor sleep quality in adults aged 50 years and older has also been strongly linked to decreased quality of life, reduced independence, and heightened vulnerability to institutionalization and mortality [2–4]. Globally, sleep disturbances are highly prevalent among older adults, with estimates suggesting that up to 50% of individuals over the age of 65 experience clinically significant sleep problems [5]. Insufficient or non-restorative sleep among this population imposes substantial burdens—not only on individuals and caregivers but also on health systems—through increased healthcare utilization, loss of productivity, and elevated costs associated with managing sleep-related comorbidities.

Conventional treatment options for insomnia and related sleep disturbances in later adulthood typically include pharmacological agents such as benzodiazepines, non-benzodiazepine hypnotics, melatonin receptor agonists, and antidepressants. While effective in the short term, these medications carry well-documented risks for adults aged 50 years and older, including sedation, falls, cognitive impairment, dependence, and adverse drug interactions—particularly in the context of polypharmacy [6, 7]. These limitations underscore the growing need for safer, sustainable, and evidence-based alternatives. Current clinical guidelines increasingly recommend non-pharmacological treatments (NPTs)—such as cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), physical activity, and mindfulness-based interventions—as first-line therapy for chronic insomnia in adults, consistent with recommendations from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and European Sleep Research Society.

A growing body of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses has examined the effects of NPTs on sleep outcomes in adults aged 50 years and older, revealing varying degrees of effectiveness across modalities. Prior reviews have primarily focused on individual interventions or direct comparisons, limiting their capacity to inform clinical decision-making when multiple options exist [8–10]. Moreover, existing evidence syntheses often suffer from methodological heterogeneity, narrow inclusion criteria, and insufficient analysis of treatment-specific effect modifiers such as baseline sleep quality, intervention frequency, and treatment duration [11]. As a result, clinicians lack comprehensive, comparative evidence to guide personalized selection of NPTs for sleep improvement in adults aged 50 years and older.

To address these gaps, this study conducted a comprehensive network meta-analysis to compare the efficacy and adherence of diverse NPTs in improving sleep quality among adults aged 50 years and older. By integrating direct and indirect evidence across randomized controlled trials, this study aims to generate a hierarchized synthesis of available interventions, provide guidance for individualized and public health-level treatment planning, and identify optimal strategies for enhancing sleep in adults aged 50 years and older.

Methods

This prospectively registered systematic review and network meta-analysis (PROSPERO reference number: CRD420251046892) followed reporting standards specified in the PRISMA framework [12]. Methodological quality was evaluated through the AMSTAR 2 instrument [13], with standardized implementation across all assessment domains to ensure uniformity and rigor in evaluation.

Searches

A systematic literature search was executed across five biomedical databases (PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, Web of Science) spanning database inception through 1 April 2025. The complete search syntax incorporating controlled vocabulary terms, temporal parameters, and Boolean operators was documented in Supplementary 1. To ensure comprehensive coverage, citation chain analysis was conducted through manual screening of bibliographies from eligible studies and existing reviews. Dual-independent screening protocols were implemented for both preliminary title/abstract evaluation and subsequent full-text appraisal. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through constructive discussion; if needed, a senior investigator adjudicated.

Study inclusion and exclusion criteria

Two reviewers independently conducted an initial screening of all identified abstracts. The inclusion criteria were based on the PICOS (participants, interventions, comparators, outcomes, and study design) approach [14]. Participants: adults aged 50 years and older diagnosed with sleep disorders (PSQI ≥ 5), aged 50 years or older. The NPTs were divided into 25 types according to their content. The control group (CON) included no intervention, maintenance of daily activities, or a wait-list condition (Supplementary 2). For head-to-head studies, the comparator could be any of the 25 NPTs. Studies were required to report the outcome measures of interest: global sleep quality and all-cause dropout. In the study design, published RCTs (individual design, cluster design, or crossover trials using first-period data only) were included. For crossover trials, only outcomes from the first period (before crossover) were extracted to reduce potential carryover effects. First-period data were taken from period-specific results or baseline-to-end-of-period-1 outcomes. Studies evaluating the acute effects of a single session were excluded. In addition, studies that did not clearly describe the types of NPTs and the dose of treatment (e.g., treatment period, weekly frequency, or duration of a single treatment session) were also excluded.

Data extraction

Following study identification and collation using EndNote X9 reference management software, a dual-independent extraction workflow was instituted for eligible records. Bibliographic descriptors (first author, publication year, journal), sample size parameters, population characteristics (age distribution, gender stratification), therapeutic interventions, and efficacy endpoints were systematically recorded (Supplementary 3). Disagreements were resolved through discussion; a third reviewer adjudicated when necessary. For continuous outcome measures, dispersion metrics were standardized through computational transformations: Where primary studies reported standard errors, standard deviations were derived using the equation SD = SE × √n. In cases of incomplete variability reporting, established imputation protocols from the Cochrane Handbook (Version 6.3, Sect. 7.7.3) were applied to estimate SDs from confidence intervals, interquartile ranges, or probability values. When essential data were missing, corresponding authors were contacted up to four times within a six-week period via email and institutional portals.

Risk of bias and quality of the evidence

Two authors independently evaluated the risk of bias for each included study using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (RoB 2) at the study level [15], carefully assessing five key domains to ensure a comprehensive evaluation. For each study, the overall risk of bias was determined based on the ratings across these domains. Studies with all domains rated as low risk were classified as having an overall low risk of bias, while those with at least one domain raising some concerns but no high-risk ratings were categorized as having some concerns. Conversely, studies with one or more domains rated as high risk were deemed to have a high overall risk of bias. Any disagreements in domain-level or overall assessments were resolved through discussion, and if necessary, a third reviewer was consulted to reach a consensus. The detailed justifications for each domain-level assessment and the overall bias rating are provided in Supplementary File 4 to ensure transparency and reproducibility. Confidence in the evidence was evaluated using the CINeMA (Confidence of Network Meta-Analysis) web application, which allows the certainty of the results to be graded as high, moderate, low, or very low [16].

Data synthesis and additional analyses

The primary analysis was a Bayesian network meta-analysis conducted using the R package gemtc (R version 4.4.1), interfacing with Just Another Gibbs Sampler (JAGS v4.3.0) to fit hierarchical random-effects models via Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulation [17, 18]. Four independent chains were run for each outcome (sleep quality and dropout), with 20,000 iterations per chain and the first 5,000 iterations discarded as adaptation. Model convergence was evaluated using trace plots (Supplementary 7). Effect estimates were expressed as risk ratios (RRs) for dichotomous outcomes and standardized mean differences (SMDs) for continuous outcomes, with 95% credible intervals (CrIs). Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic (Supplementary 5). Global inconsistency was examined using the design-by-treatment interaction test implemented in the R package netmeta [19], and local inconsistency was assessed using the separation of indirect from direct evidence (SIDE) approach within gemtc [20]. Publication bias was evaluated, when applicable, using comparison-adjusted funnel plots [21] in which study points were color-coded according to the specific treatment comparison contributing each estimate, and Egger’s test, with p < 0.05 considered suggestive of small-study effects (funnel-plot asymmetry), which may reflect publication bias or other biases (Supplementary 6).

Secondary analyses focused on clinical interpretability and potential effect modifiers. The minimum clinically important difference (MCID) for PSQI was estimated using a distribution-based approach [22, 23]. MCID values of 1.46 points (0.4 SD) and 1.82 points (0.5 SD) were derived. This 0.4–0.5 SD framework is commonly used when anchor-based MCIDs are unavailable or inconsistent across settings and is responsive to baseline variability across populations. A pooled effect size was then calculated for the subset of studies (27 studies) achieving at least the estimated pooled MCID, and treatments most likely to reach the MCID threshold were identified.

Potential sources of heterogeneity in sleep quality were explored using network meta-regression in gemtc (Supplementary 9). Covariates included sample size, percentage female, mean age, baseline PSQI, treatment frequency, treatment period, single-session duration, and weekly treatment time. Linear associations between these covariates and treatment effects were examined.

Exploratory analyses evaluated within-arm change and dose-related patterns. An exploratory dose-related analysis was performed using within-arm standardized mean changes. Sham and control arms were not included in these within-arm models because the primary network meta-analysis indicated comparable effects between sham and control conditions, without evidence of differential benefit. This exclusion applied only to the within-arm dose-related analyses and did not affect the main network comparisons, in which sham and control were retained as comparators. Within-subject standardized mean differences (SMDws) were calculated using published arm-level summary data rather than individual participant data, defined as the standardized change from baseline to post-intervention: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C \left(\frac{{M}_{post}-{M}_{pre}}{S{d}_{pre}}\right);{C}_{j}=1- \frac{3}{4\left({n}_{j}- \right)-1}$$\end{document}. When change-score standard deviations were reported, they were used directly; otherwise, the baseline standard deviation was used, consistent with the standardized mean change approach. For reporting consistency, SMDws estimates were expressed as Hedges’g by applying a small-sample correction factor; thus, Hedges’g values in the spline models reflect bias-corrected within-arm standardized mean changes.

Given the hierarchical structure of effect sizes (multiple estimates nested within groups and studies), multilevel mixed-effects meta-analyses were conducted [24]. Cluster-robust estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated, and studies were weighted by the inverse of the sampling variance to account for within-study and between-study variability (τ2). Restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation was used to estimate variance components [25]. To evaluate potential nonlinear associations between candidate moderators and SMDws, exploratory multivariate meta-regression was conducted using restricted cubic splines with three knots at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles. Analyses were performed using the R package metafor (Supplementary 10), and nonlinear relationships were visualized using ggplot2.

Results

Overall, 3129 records were identified through the initial electronic searches. After removing duplicates, 1822 records were screened for titles and abstracts and 180 full-text articles were screened for eligibility. Characteristics of included studies

The characteristics of the included studies are shown in Supplementary 3. The year of publication ranged from 1997 to 2024. The sample size of the included studies ranged from 12 to 333. For all-cause dropout

One hundred thirty two studies involving 10,872 participants assessed all-cause dropout. All 132 included trials provided attrition data and were therefore included in the dropout network. Figure 2 presents the direct comparisons and sample size distribution among NPTs types for all-cause dropout. Compared with CON, only Usual care significantly reduced the all-cause dropout rate (Fig. 4: RR = 0.51, 95% CrI: 0.30 to 0.87). The global heterogeneity of all-cause dropout was I2 = 0. The results of the design-by-treatment test showed that the global inconsistency was not significant (p = 0.2927), the results of SIDE test showed that only Internet CBT vs. Usual care was statistically significant difference between direct and indirect comparisons of the two comparisons (p = 0.006, Supplementary 5). Network and multivariate meta-regression

We attempted to verify whether the potential factors had a simple linear relationship with the efficacy of sleep quality through network meta-regression. However, our results showed that a large number of potential factors did not significantly affect the efficacy of sleep quality (Supplementary 9). In addition, the results of multivariate meta-regression showed that baseline sleep quality and intervention frequency had a nonlinear relationship with the efficacy of sleep quality (Supplementary 10). Our results showed that the efficacy of non-drug treatments increased nonlinearly with the severity of sleep disorders. In the exploratory spline models, larger improvements were observed around a baseline PSQI of 16 (Hedges' g = −1.43, 95%CI: −1.64 to −1.22), with smaller improvements at higher baseline scores. The effect of intervention frequency and sleep quality presented an inverted U-shape, with larger improvements observed at approximately 7 sessions per week (Hedges' g = −1.32, 95%CI: −1.67 to −0.96) ((Supplementary 10, Figure S10).

Risk of bias and quality of the evidence

Of the 132 trials, for overall bias, 46 studies were assessed as low risk of bias, 72 as some concerns, and 14 as high. In the randomization process, 105 trials were low risk, 27trials were some concerns; for deviations from intended interventions, 98 trials were at low risk, 23 trials were some concerns, and 11 as high; in the missing outcome data, 99 trials were at low risk, 30 trials were some concerns, and 3 as high; in the measurement of the outcome, 104 trials were at low risk, 28 trials were some concerns; and the selection of the reported result, 119 trials were low risk, 13 trials were some concerns (Supplementary 4, Figure S4). Regarding the grade of evidence for the global sleep quality, our results showed that AE + RT, Acupressure, AE, Aromatherapy, Mindfulness, RT, Internet CBT, and CBT were rated moderate or high, while the others were rated low to very low. For all-cause dropout, only Usual care and Sleep Hygiene were rated moderate, while the others were rated low to very low (Figs. 3 and 4, and Supplementary 11).

Discussion

This network meta-analysis synthesized data from 132 trials involving 10,872 adults aged 50 years and older, comparing 25 NPTs for sleep quality and adherence. The analysis identified 19 interventions that significantly improved global sleep quality compared to control conditions. Among these, AE + RT showed the largest effect, followed by Acupressure, AE, Aromatherapy, Acupuncture, Mindfulness, Resistance Training, Internet CBT, and CBT. AE + RT, Acupressure, and AE ranked highest, with 14 treatments meeting or exceeding the MCID threshold for sleep improvement. These findings underscore the clinical relevance of AE + RT, Acupressure, and AE, which demonstrated the greatest effect sizes above the MCID threshold. Additionally, multivariate analyses revealed a nonlinear relationship between treatment efficacy and both baseline sleep severity and intervention frequency, with the largest improvements observed at baseline PSQI scores of 16 and approximately seven sessions per week.

Improving sleep quality in older adults is clinically critical due to the widespread prevalence of sleep disturbances and their impact on cognitive, psychological, and physical health. This study provides comparative evidence on NPT efficacy, with AE + RT ranking highest. This finding aligns with previous literature supporting the benefits of physical activity and cognitive-behavioral approaches in enhancing sleep, but extends the knowledge by providing direct comparisons across multiple interventions. Prior meta-analyses have shown moderate efficacy for treatments like CBT-I, mindfulness, and aerobic exercise, but lacked a statistical framework to directly compare these interventions or detect the superior effect of combined modalities like AE + RT [26, 27]. These results reinforce the consensus that NPTs are viable and often preferable first-line strategies for managing sleep disturbances in later adulthood.

The mechanisms underlying these benefits are multifactorial. Behavioral therapies correct maladaptive sleep beliefs, while mindfulness and relaxation strategies help reduce arousal and improve emotional regulation. Exercise-based interventions confer additional physiological benefits, such as enhanced slow-wave sleep, circadian rhythm entrainment, and reduced inflammatory cytokine levels, which are particularly relevant in older adults experiencing age-related sleep changes [28]. The superior performance of AE + RT in this network meta-analysis may be attributed to the synergistic effects of aerobic and resistance training, which together improve muscular strength, cardiopulmonary endurance, metabolic flexibility, and neurotrophic signaling, contributing to better sleep initiation, maintenance, and perceived restorativeness. Moreover, the structured and routine nature of AE + RT may help reinforce sleep–wake cycle regularity, which is particularly beneficial for older adults with fragmented circadian rhythms. While AE + RT (SUCRA = 0.89) is supported by studies with "some concerns" regarding bias, Acupressure (SUCRA = 0.84) and AE (SUCRA = 0.83) are largely supported by studies rated as low risk of bias. This variability in risk of bias highlights that while AE + RT is the most effective treatment, its evidence base requires cautious interpretation, especially given its association with studies rated as having “some concerns.” These nuances in risk of bias clarify the certainty of evidence behind these top-ranked interventions and provide a more comprehensive understanding of their applicability in clinical settings.

Beyond statistical significance, the clinical relevance of sleep interventions is crucial for real-world decision-making, especially for older adults. The MCID serves as a benchmark to determine whether treatment benefits are perceived as meaningful by patients. Our analysis found 14 interventions that met or exceeded the MCID threshold for improvement in PSQI scores, with AE + RT, Acupressure, and AE showing effect sizes significantly exceeding the MCID threshold. This highlights the clinical robustness of these interventions and their superior potential to deliver clinically meaningful outcomes. Although previous meta-analyses have demonstrated the benefits of exercise and behavioral therapies, few have systematically assessed effect sizes in relation to MCID benchmarks. Existing literature often focused on between-group differences without evaluating the practical benefits of those improvements for patients. Importantly, MCID exceedance should be viewed as supportive evidence for clinical interpretability rather than a strict cutoff, and interventions not exceeding the MCID may still be useful depending on patient goals and feasibility.

The shared features of AE + RT, Acupressure, and AE—such as low cognitive demands—may help explain their ability to surpass the MCID threshold. Both AE + RT and aerobic exercise improve sleep through multiple physiological pathways, including enhancement of slow-wave sleep, stabilization of circadian rhythms, and regulation of the autonomic nervous system [29]. Acupressure improves sleep by stimulating acupoints to influence central arousal regulation, affecting autonomic balance and stress-related neuroendocrine activity. While the mechanistic evidence for acupressure remains less well established in Western settings, it provides a promising, low-cost intervention for improving sleep. These three interventions are also scalable and can be delivered in home-based or community programs, making them accessible to older adults. In contrast, interventions like CBT and mindfulness, while effective, may require intensive psychological engagement, which could limit their accessibility for certain elderly populations [30]. These differences underscore the importance of tailoring interventions to both physiological mechanisms and patient capabilities.

Optimizing the clinical effectiveness of sleep interventions in adults aged 50 years and older requires careful consideration of baseline characteristics and intervention-related factors, including treatment frequency. Our analysis revealed that the efficacy of NPTs was influenced by nonlinear relationships with baseline sleep quality and intervention frequency. Larger improvements were observed around baseline PSQI scores of 16, and at approximately seven sessions per week. However, these associations should be interpreted as observed relationships rather than definitive therapeutic thresholds. The nonlinear pattern identified in this analysis suggests that individuals with moderate sleep disturbances may benefit most from treatment, while those with more severe disturbances may require different approaches.

The observed peak at a baseline PSQI of approximately 16 may indicate a level of sleep disruption substantial enough to allow measurable improvement, while still being responsive to behavioral and activity-based interventions [2]. Similarly, the optimal intervention frequency of seven sessions per week aligns with evidence suggesting that consistent routines stabilize circadian rhythms, promote behavioral changes, and facilitate the neuromuscular and cardiovascular adaptations critical for sleep improvement [31]. These findings underscore the necessity for clinicians to adopt personalized intervention strategies that balance baseline patient characteristics and intervention intensity to maximize therapeutic efficacy in older adults.

The findings of this study have significant implications for clinical practice and healthcare policy regarding sleep management in adults aged 50 years and older. By systematically evaluating and ranking NPTs, this study provides evidence-based guidance for clinicians to prioritize interventions like AE + RT, Acupressure, and AE, which consistently deliver clinically meaningful improvements. Moreover, the nonlinear relationships observed for baseline sleep severity and intervention frequency may inform future trial designs and public health program planning, including stratification by baseline PSQI severity and hypothesis-driven evaluation of intervention dosing schedules. At a policy level, these findings support the integration of structured physical activity programs and accessible behavioral interventions into standard care, potentially reducing reliance on pharmacotherapy and improving long-term sleep outcomes in aging populations.

This network meta-analysis has notable methodological strengths. By using rigorous Bayesian network meta-analysis techniques, it enables simultaneous direct and indirect comparisons among a wide array of NPTs, offering a more robust hierarchy of therapeutic options. The incorporation of clinically meaningful thresholds, such as the MCID, enhances the analysis, facilitating practical interpretation of statistical outcomes. Nonetheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. Substantial heterogeneity across studies, stemming from differences in intervention protocols, adherence, and outcome measurements, could affect efficacy estimates. Although interventions were grouped using prespecified node definitions (Table 1), within node variation in dose and delivery may persist, particularly for exercise and CBT related nodes, and rankings should be interpreted accordingly. Additionally, the lack of long-term follow-up data restricts our ability to assess sustained treatment effects. Most exercise trials reported intensity qualitatively rather than with harmonizable metrics, which limited our ability to conduct intensity restricted sensitivity analyses and may have contributed to residual heterogeneity within exercise nodes. Finally, we did not conduct additional analyses using alternative MCID thresholds because these were not prespecified and would require further analytic assumptions; therefore, MCID-based interpretation should be viewed alongside statistical significance and implementation considerations. Future research should address these gaps by using standardized protocols, longer follow-up duration, and more complete reporting of intervention dose and delivery characteristics to refine clinical guidelines and improve patient outcomes.

Conclusion

This network meta-analysis of 132 randomized controlled trials provides robust evidence that multiple NPTs effectively enhance sleep quality in adults aged 50 years and older. AE + RT, acupressure, and aerobic exercise alone demonstrated the strongest clinical benefits, surpassing the MCID threshold. Other NPTs also improved sleep quality and may still be clinically useful depending on individual needs and implementation context. In exploratory analyses, larger improvements were observed around baseline PSQI scores of 16 and at an intervention frequency of seven sessions per week, suggesting potential considerations for individualized planning and hypothesis-driven research. Clinicians and policymakers should prioritize AE + RT and similarly effective interventions to reduce sleep disturbances and associated adverse health outcomes in adults aged 50 years and older. Future research is needed to clarify long-term efficacy, adherence patterns, and the influence of comorbidities and socioeconomic factors on treatment outcomes.
