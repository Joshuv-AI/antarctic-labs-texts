Longitudinal analysis of sleep duration trajectories and sarcopenia risk in middle-aged and older Chinese adults: evidence from China health and retirement longitudinal study
Authors: Ruoyu Wang, Wangyixing He, Binbin Chang, Ming Wang, Zhang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesSarcopenia has emerged as a significant public health issue that threatens the health of older adults. The association between sleep duration and sarcopenia is receiving increasing attention. Based on data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), this study investigates the dynamic characteristics of sleep duration trajectories and their relationship with the onset of sarcopenia.DesignA retrospective cohort study.SettingThe study used data from the CHARLS across various regions of China.ParticipantsData were drawn from the 2011, 2013 and 2015 waves of CHARLS, including 2521 participants aged 45 years or older at baseline who had complete 3-year records of sarcopenia diagnosis. Sarcopenia was defined according to the 2019 criteria established by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia and was assessed across three dimensions: grip strength, estimated appendicular skeletal muscle mass and physical function.Outcome measuresParticipants were categorised by changes in sleep duration, and linear mixed-effects models were used to identify three trajectories of sleep duration change (decrease–increase, decrease–no recovery and continuous increase). Multivariate logistic regression was conducted to examine the association between these trajectories and the risk of sarcopenia, adjusting for confounders, such as education level, body mass index (BMI) and lifestyle factors.ResultsThe median age of the cohort was 57.00 years, 61.40% of participants were female and the median BMI was 23.44. During the 5-year follow-up, the prevalence of sarcopenia was 14.09%. Compared with the non-sarcopenia group, individuals with sarcopenia had a higher median age (60.00 vs 56.00 years, p<0.001), lower BMI (23.06 vs 23.44, p=0.020), a higher proportion of unmarried/divorced individuals (16.34% vs 11.5%, p=0.010) and lower education levels (p<0.001). After adjusting for gender, age, BMI, residential area, education level, marital status, health insurance status, smoking and alcohol consumption, both increased and decreased sleep durations were associated with higher odds of sarcopenia relative to unchanged sleep duration, though the associations were not statistically significant (increased sleep duration: OR=1.081, 95% CI 0.807 to 1.449, p=0.601 and decreased sleep duration: OR=1.225, 95% CI 0.928 to 1.618, p=0.153). These results remained non-significant after further multivariate adjustment. Analysis of the three sleep duration trajectories identified via the linear mixed-effects model showed that, after multivariate adjustment and in comparison with trajectory 1 (sleep duration decreased and then increased significantly, exceeding baseline levels), both trajectory 2 (sleep duration decreased and then slightly increased but remained below baseline) and trajectory 3 (a consistent increase in sleep duration) were associated with a potential reduction in sarcopenia risk. However, these associations were not statistically significant (trajectory 2: OR=0.833, 95% CI 0.620 to 1.120, p=0.226 and trajectory 3: OR=0.844, 95% CI 0.647 to 1.101, p=0.210).ConclusionsThis study developed a novel sleep trajectory model using longitudinal data, revealing the non-linear, multidimensional nature of the relationship between sleep duration and sarcopenia risk. Although no independent association was identified between 5-year sleep duration trajectories and sarcopenia in Chinese middle-aged and older adults, the study provides a valuable methodological framework for exploring multifactorial interactions in the ageing process and establishes a foundation for future research.Keywords: Aging, Sleep medicine, GERIATRIC MEDICINE, Aged

Introduction

Sarcopenia is a progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass, strength and function, primarily affecting older adults.1 In 2010, the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People published the first consensus statement on sarcopenia, defining it as an age-related syndrome characterised by reduced muscle mass, decline in muscle strength and/or impairment in physical function.2 In October 2016, sarcopenia was officially included in the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10), with the disease code ICD-10-CM (M62.84). With the increasing global ageing population, sarcopenia has become an important public health issue threatening the health of older adults.

Epidemiological data show that the prevalence of sarcopenia in individuals aged 60 and above worldwide ranges from 10% to 27%, and the prevalence increases significantly with age. It is estimated that, by 2050, the global number of people with sarcopenia will reach 500 million.3 4 In China, the prevalence of sarcopenia in older adults aged 60 and above is 5.7%–23.9%, showing a significant age-related trend, with the prevalence reaching as high as 67.1% in those aged 80 and above. Moreover, the prevalence is generally higher in men than in women and lower in the eastern regions than in the western regions. The prevalence is higher in hospitals, nursing homes and rural populations than in urban and community populations.5

The harm of sarcopenia is multifaceted: it increases the risk of falls (which is 2–3 times higher) and can subsequently result in fractures, disability or even death.6 7 Studies have shown a strong association between sarcopenia and all-cause mortality.8 From a socioeconomic perspective, sarcopenia significantly increases medical costs, with inpatient care costs being higher for older adults with sarcopenia compared with those without.9

In recent years, the relationship between sleep duration and sarcopenia has received growing attention. Several cross-sectional studies have indicated that this association follows a U-shaped curve: both insufficient sleep (<6 hours/day) and excessive sleep (>8 hours/day) are associated with an increased risk of sarcopenia, whereas 7–8 hours of sleep appears to confer a protective effect.10 Mechanistic studies suggest that sleep disturbances may impact muscle metabolism through several biological pathways11 12: (1) suppression of actin-related proteins, such as myostatin, thereby reducing muscle synthesis capacity; (2) a reduction in brain-derived neurotrophic factor, leading to impaired neuromuscular regulation and (3) elevated levels of chronic inflammatory markers, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumour necrosis factor-α, which accelerate muscle degradation. However, most existing research has relied on sleep duration measured at a single time point and lacks longitudinal data on dynamic sleep trajectories (such as persistent short sleep or fluctuating sleep patterns), making it difficult to capture the cumulative impact of sleep pattern changes on sarcopenia progression.10

Recent studies have begun to explore the longitudinal relationship between sleep duration and sarcopenia. For instance, a study by Lv et al13 investigated the association between sleep duration and sarcopenia in a cohort of older adults and found that short sleep duration was associated with a higher risk of sarcopenia. While this study provided valuable insights into the relationship between sleep and sarcopenia, our study differs in several key aspects. First, our study focuses specifically on the dynamic changes in sleep duration over time and their longitudinal association with sarcopenia among Chinese older adults. This allows us to capture the cumulative effects of sleep pattern changes, which were not addressed in the study by Lv et al. Second, we use data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) cohort, which provides a unique opportunity to examine these associations in a large, nationally representative sample of Chinese older adults.

This study, using data from the CHARLS cohort, aims to investigate the dynamic characteristics of sleep duration trajectories and their association with the development of sarcopenia. The objective is to provide empirical evidence to inform the formulation of sleep-based interventions for sarcopenia prevention and management strategies.

Materials and methods

Study population

Our study is based on the data from the CHARLS, which is a prospective cohort study. However, we used data from the 2011, 2013 and 2015 follow-up waves; thus, our study design can be considered a retrospective cohort design, as we focused on data collected during this specific period. CHARLS is a nationwide longitudinal study designed to collect high-quality data on various dimensions, including demographic characteristics, health status and functioning, family structure, employment and retirement, lifestyle behaviours, healthcare and insurance for non-institutionalised middle-aged and older adults in China. All participants were permanent residents of China. Participants were contacted by the CHARLS research team through household visits. These visits were conducted as a part of the longitudinal data collection process, with follow-ups every 2–3 years. During these visits, informed consent was obtained from each participant before they were included in the study.

This study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of Peking University (IRB0000151052-11015).

Inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) baseline age ≥45 years and (2) complete 3-year sarcopenia diagnosis data. Exclusion criteria were: (1) pre-existing sarcopenia at baseline and (2) death, loss to follow-up or incomplete data. Participants with cognitive decline were not excluded from the study. In the CHARLS dataset, cognitive function was assessed, but those with cognitive impairment were still included in the analyses.

A detailed flowchart of the inclusion and exclusion process for the study participants is shown in figure 1. Among the 13 974 baseline CHARLS participants, we excluded participants who were lost to follow-up or had died before 2015 (n=1143) or had missing data on ≥1 key analytic variables (sleep duration, grip strength, gait speed, appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) or covariates) (n=7757). Finally, we removed participants<45 years (n=210) and participants (n=2343) with prevalent sarcopenia at baseline.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Definition of sarcopenia

This study defines sarcopenia based on the 2019 standards of the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS),14 using three dimensions: muscle strength, muscle mass and physical function.

Muscle strength: Muscle strength was measured using hand grip strength, which is a standard, reliable method for assessing muscle strength. The following details describe the procedure. (1) We used a calibrated electronic dynamometer (eg, T.K.K. 5401 or similar devices meeting international standards). (2) Participants were asked to sit with their feet flat on the floor, their back straight and their arms relaxed. (3) Each hand was measured three times with at least a 30-s rest between measurements. (4) The highest grip strength value from the dominant hand was taken as the individual’s muscle strength representative value. A decline in muscle strength is defined as a grip strength of <28 kg for men and <18 kg for women.

In this formula, male participants are assigned a value of 0, and female participants are assigned a value of 1. The ASM is also adjusted for height using the formula to assess muscle mass. After estimating ASM values, the skeletal muscle mass index (SMI) was calculated as ASM divided by the square of height (metres) (SMI=ASM/height2). Muscle mass decline is defined as <7.0 kg/m² for men and <5.4 kg/m² for women. These thresholds were chosen because they are consistent with the current AWGS 2019 recommendations and have been validated in extensive studies. These cutpoints are reliable for identifying sarcopenia in both clinical and research settings.

3. Physical function: Physical function was assessed using the 2.5-m walking test and the five-time sit-to-stand test. Participants with a walking speed <1 m/s, a five-time sit-to-stand test time >12 s or those unable to complete the test were considered to have physical function decline.

According to the aforementioned criteria, sarcopenia is diagnosed when a decline in muscle mass is accompanied by either impaired physical function or reduced muscle strength. Participants exhibiting none of these three conditions are considered free of sarcopenia.

The explanatory variables in this study include general demographic characteristics (eg, age and gender), place of residence (urban or rural), income, smoking and drinking habits, education level and sleep duration.

Assessment of sleep duration

Total sleep duration in this study encompasses both nighttime sleep and daytime napping, primarily based on participants’ self-reported data. The questionnaire asked, ‘On average, how many hours did you sleep each night over the past month?’ (noting that this may be shorter than the total time spent in bed). Based on the participants’ responses, changes in sleep duration relative to baseline were categorised into three groups: unchanged sleep duration (change≤1 hour), increased sleep duration (increase>1 hour) and decreased sleep duration (decrease>1 hour). Sleep duration was measured at three time points (waves 2011, 2013 and 2015) separately.

Grouping of the study cohort

To assess changes in sleep duration over time, we classified participants according to the change in their reported sleep duration relative to their baseline value (2011). The participants were divided into three categories: unchanged (change ≤1 hour), increased (change >1 hour) and decreased (change >1 hour). This approach enabled us to examine the dynamic changes in sleep duration between the baseline year (2011) and the follow-up years (2013 and 2015).

Data collection of covariates

Covariates were obtained from baseline data in the CHARLS cohort, which included structured interviews and physical examinations conducted by trained personnel. Variables were selected based on their known associations with both sleep duration and sarcopenia in older adults, including age, gender, place of residence (urban or rural), marital status, health insurance coverage and education level (illiterate or educated). Health-related variables included body mass index (BMI), smoking status and alcohol consumption. BMI was calculated using the formula: BMI=weight (kg)/height² (m²). Assessment of sarcopenia and related covariates was obtained at three time points (waves 2011, 2013 and 2015) separately.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses in this study were conducted using R V.4.4.3, using various R packages, including ‘haven’, ‘dplyr’, ‘skimr’, ‘visdat’, ‘lcmm’ and ‘ggplot2’. The ‘haven’ package’s ‘read_dta’ function was used to import datasets from multiple time points, encompassing several variables. The ‘dplyr’ package was used for data preprocessing, selecting variables relevant to the study and removing rows with missing values according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure data integrity and accuracy.

The ‘lcmm’ package was used to construct linear mixed-effects models to analyse longitudinal changes in the outcome variables over time. The model incorporated both time-related fixed effects and individual-level random effects to account for individual heterogeneity. The Bayesian information criterion (BIC) was used to compare models with different groupings, selecting the model with the lowest BIC as the best-fit model. The ‘ggplot2’ package was used to visualise the results of the model fitting, including plotting individual trajectories, group-level average trajectories and smoothed curves to visually display differences and trends between groups for easier interpretation and presentation.

For continuous variables with a normal distribution, means±SD were used, and t-tests were conducted for comparisons. Medians and IQRs were presented for continuous variables with skewed distributions, and the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis H test was used for comparisons. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, and χ2 tests were used for group comparisons.

Sarcopenia outcomes were examined using binary logistic regression models. We first performed univariate analyses, followed by multivariate analyses that adjusted for sociodemographic covariates (age, sex, residence, education, marital status and health insurance) and health-related covariates (BMI, smoking, alcohol consumption and a history of chronic disease).

Dose–response relationships were evaluated using restricted cubic splines to explore potential non-linear associations between independent risk factors and sarcopenia outcomes.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted, including (a) excluding variables with high missingness, (b) adding additional health indicators where available and (c) stratification by sex and residence.

A two-tailed p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant in this study.

Results

Demographic characteristics of the study cohort

A total of 2521 participants were included in the study cohort, with a median age of 57.00 years. The female proportion was 61.40%, and the median BMI was 23.44 (table 1). During the 5-year follow-up period, the prevalence of sarcopenia in the study population was 14.08% (355/2521). Significant differences were observed between the sarcopenia and non-sarcopenia groups across several sociodemographic variables. Compared with the non-sarcopenia group, the sarcopenia group had a higher median age (60.00 years vs 56.00 years, p<0.001) and a lower BMI (23.06 vs 23.45, p=0.020). Regarding education level, the illiteracy rate was significantly higher in the sarcopenia group (30.42% vs 21.79%, χ²=12.828, p<0.001). The proportion of unmarried/divorced individuals was also higher in the sarcopenia group (16.34% vs 11.50%, p=0.010). In terms of lifestyle factors, drinking was not significantly associated with sarcopenia risk (p=0.064), whereas smoking showed no significant association with sarcopenia risk (p=0.984).

Association between sleep duration change and sarcopenia risk

Table 3 and figure 2A–B display the association between changes in sleep duration and sarcopenia risk. Figure 2A shows that both increased and decreased sleep durations were associated with a potential increase in the risk of sarcopenia compared with unchanged sleep duration, although no statistically significant differences were observed (increased group: OR=1.144, 95% CI 0.862 to 1.520, p=0.351 and decreased group: OR=1.218, 95% CI 0.931 to 1.594, p=0.150). Association between sleep duration evolution trajectories and sarcopenia risk

Table 5 and figure 4A–B illustrate the association between sleep duration evolution trajectories and sarcopenia risk. figure 4A shows that, compared with trajectory 1, both trajectories 2 and 3 may be associated with a reduced risk of sarcopenia, though no statistically significant differences were observed (Trajectory 2: OR=0.859, 95% CI 0.644 to 1.145, p=0.300 and Trajectory 3: OR=0.834, 95% CI 0.644 to 1.080, p=0.168). figure 4B demonstrates that after adjusting for gender, age, BMI, place of residence, education level, marital status, health insurance, smoking and drinking, the risk differences between sleep trajectory groups remained non-significant (Trajectory 2: OR=0.833, 95% CI 0.620 to 1.120, p=0.226 and Trajectory 3: OR=0.844, 95% CI 0.647 to 1.101, p=0.210). This suggests that the independent effect of sleep duration evolution trajectories on sarcopenia risk is limited.

Discussion

This study used CHARLS data from 2011 to 2015 and applied trajectory analysis to examine the impact of dynamic changes in sleep duration on the risk of sarcopenia. Our primary findings indicate that, while changes in sleep duration were associated with an increased risk of sarcopenia, no significant independent effect of sleep trajectory evolution on sarcopenia risk was observed over the 5-year observation period.

A strong correlation between sarcopenia and age has been widely reported in the literature.13 17 For example, a study of older adults found that the prevalence of sarcopenia among those aged 60 and above was 49.8%, with the average age of the sarcopenia group being higher than that of the non-sarcopenia group.18 This was consistent with the higher median age observed in the sleep increase group of our study, where sarcopenia risk was higher. Low BMI is often an indicator of malnutrition in older adults. Inadequate nutritional intake and unhealthy lifestyles in older adults increase the risk of sarcopenia.18 Research from the UK community of older adults found a significant correlation between low BMI and probable sarcopenia, with a risk ratio of 2.25 after multivariate adjustment.19 This suggests that older adults with low BMI should not only undergo malnutrition screening but also sarcopenia screening, with appropriate interventions as necessary. Clinically, these findings highlight the importance of regular health assessments for older adults, particularly those with low BMI, to identify and manage sarcopenia early.

In our study, 61.82% of the sarcopenia group were female, consistent with gender-specific findings in the existing literature.20 Women inherently have lower muscle mass than men. After menopause, oestrogen levels decline, reducing muscle synthesis metabolism and leading to muscle mass loss.21 Additionally, testosterone levels in postmenopausal women decrease, and testosterone plays a critical role in maintaining muscle mass and strength.22 A study of sarcopenia prevalence differences between older men and women in rural Eastern China found that the prevalence of sarcopenia in women was 21.7%, compared with 12.9% in men. After adjusting for age, education level, number of diseases, income level, smoking, alcohol consumption and dietary habits, the sarcopenia prevalence in women was found to be 1.49 times that of men.23 However, it is important to note that other studies have reported different findings. For instance, Shafiee et al24,24 found that the prevalence of sarcopenia was 10% in both men and women (95% CI 8% to 12% vs 8% to 13%), which may be due to differences in gender prevalence rates between Asian and non-Asian populations. Papadopoulou et al25 concluded that the prevalence of sarcopenia was higher in men than in women, with a prevalence rate of 15.2% in men compared with 10.3% in women. This study was conducted in a community-dwelling population in Greece, highlighting the importance of considering regional and demographic differences in sarcopenia prevalence. Ramírez-García et al26 even found that the prevalence of sarcopenia was slightly higher in men than in women, with 6.9% in women and 7.2% in men. This finding may be attributed to the specific population studied (older Mexican adults with favourable health conditions). This variability across studies underscores that sex differences in sarcopenia prevalence may be influenced by genetic, hormonal, lifestyle and regional factors, highlighting the need for population-specific investigations.

The study suggests that changes in sleep duration (increasing or decreasing) may increase the risk of sarcopenia (OR 1.14 vs 1.21), although the results did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the relatively short follow-up period (p>0.05). This finding was highly consistent with those of a longitudinal study from China.27 This Chinese study found that, compared with the group with 6–8 hours of sleep, the adjusted HRs for those sleeping <6 hours and >8 hours were 2.74 and 1.84, respectively, suggesting a U-shaped relationship. Possible mechanisms include the following. (1) Short sleep: it may increase cortisol levels by activating the Hypothalamus-Pituitary-Adrenal axis (HPA), inhibiting protein synthesis and promoting breakdown. (2) Long sleep: it may be associated with increased inflammatory markers (such as C reactive protein) and reduced physical activity, indirectly leading to decreased muscle mass. A systematic review and meta-analysis also showed that the risk of sarcopenia in adults with short sleep duration was increased by 1.2 times, while the risk in those with long sleep duration was increased by 1.53 times,28 findings consistent with this study. These findings further emphasise the importance of maintaining an appropriate sleep duration to prevent sarcopenia. Public health interventions targeting sleep hygiene in older adults could help mitigate the risk of sarcopenia.

This study identified three distinct sleep duration evolution trajectories over a 5-year observation period: a decrease followed by a significant increase, a decrease followed by a mild increase and a continuous increase. However, none of these trajectories demonstrated an independent association with sarcopenia risk. In terms of magnitude, changes in sleep duration across the groups were relatively limited, clustering around 6.8 hours–7.1 hours. Compared with previous studies,29 30 this cohort did not show substantial proportions of individuals with extended (≥9 hours) or shortened (≤6 hours) sleep durations—variations that have been more strongly linked to sarcopenia risk.

The potential mechanisms underlying this association may involve the following factors. (1) Physiological compensatory effects: changes in sleep duration may trigger adaptive metabolic responses. For instance, increases in proinflammatory markers, such as IL-6, associated with short sleep durations may be partially offset by compensatory behaviours, such as afternoon napping.31 This physiological adaptation could mitigate the potential adverse effects of altered sleep on muscle health. (2) Confounding factors masking effects: physical activity may attenuate the negative impact of prolonged sleep through mechanisms, such as activating the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signalling pathway. Notably, 92.11% of participants in this study resided in rural areas, where physical labour is typically intense. This elevated physical activity level may have buffered the detrimental effects of insufficient or excessive sleep on skeletal muscle, potentially obscuring any direct associations. Single-point measurements may overestimate the effect of sleep duration, whereas the actual risk is more likely driven by cumulative sleep disturbances leading to metabolic dysregulation. The molecular clock of skeletal muscle (such as Bmal1 and Clock genes) supports this hypothesis.32 Using trajectory models to capture the non-linear evolution of sleep patterns (eg, the ‘decrease-then-significant increase’ pattern in Trajectory 1) is more reflective of the complexity of the real world compared with the simple dose–response models in the meta-analysis.33

This study has several limitations. First, as an observational study, it is inherently limited in its ability to establish causal relationships. Although multiple confounders were adjusted for, reverse causality cannot be ruled out entirely. For instance, individuals in the early stages of sarcopenia may experience sleep rhythm disturbances—such as difficulty falling asleep or fragmented sleep—due to muscle pain, reduced mobility or metabolic dysfunction, rather than sleep changes being the causal factor in sarcopenia development. Future studies should employ Mendelian randomisation using genetic instrumental variables or conduct prospective interventional trials to clarify the directionality of these associations. Second, the assessment of sleep was limited in scope. Sleep duration was the sole exposure variable considered, and other important dimensions of sleep health—such as sleep quality, circadian rhythm alignment and sleep-disordered breathing—were not included. In future research, integrating objective sleep measures, such as polysomnography and actigraphy, will enable a more comprehensive characterisation of sleep phenotypes. Third, the generalisability of the findings is constrained by the cohort’s demographic composition. The study population was predominantly rural, which limits the applicability of the conclusions to urban settings. Future investigations should adopt multicentre cohort designs that include both urban and rural populations across different regions to enhance representativeness and validate the broader applicability of the results.

Conclusion

This study contributes an important methodological framework for investigating the interactions among multiple ageing-related factors. It highlights the need to examine the dynamic interplay among diverse biological, behavioural and environmental influences in the study of older adults’ health. Future research should expand on these findings by exploring the long-term effects of sleep pattern alterations, focusing on sleep habits, duration and their dynamic changes, incorporating additional dimensions, such as sleep quality, and identifying other modifiable risk factors. Such efforts will contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying sarcopenia and inform more effective prevention and intervention strategies for ageing populations.
