Knowledge, attitudes, and practices of dental residents in managing traumatic dental injuries
Authors: Jian Yu, Jingjing Yu, Chang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTraumatic dental injuries (TDIs) are common emergencies, particularly in children and adolescents, requiring prompt and effective management to ensure favorable outcomes. However, dental residents' preparedness in managing TDIs remains understudied, especially in the context of educational and clinical practice gaps. This study aimed to evaluate the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) of dental residents regarding TDI management, with a focus on identifying training deficiencies and informing curricular improvements.MethodsA survey was conducted among 256 dental residents from the School of Stomatology, Wuhan University, China. A validated electronic questionnaire was distributed to assess demographic characteristics, clinical exposure, knowledge competency, professional attitudes, and practice patterns related to TDIs. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and chi-square tests.ResultsWhile 88.7% of residents encountered TDIs, only 67.2% felt confident in management protocols, with significant disparities across specialties (P= 0.038). Traditional classroom teaching was the primary learning method (87.7%), whereas online resources were less frequently used (9.7% in Oral Surgery). Only 28.1% expressed satisfaction with traditional teaching methods. Although 94.5% acknowledged the importance of pulp preservation, 6.9% of General Dentistry residents held uncertain attitudes. Clinical independence varied, with 63.8% of General Dentistry residents occasionally managing cases independently, compared to 77.4% of Oral Surgery residents who never did.ConclusionsDental residents recognize the clinical importance of TDI management, but gaps in knowledge translation, uneven specialty training, and reliance on outdated pedagogies hinder clinical readiness. Reforms incorporating blended learning, simulation-based training, and competency-based assessments are urgently needed to optimize residents' education and patient care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07382-4.

Background

Traumatic dental injuries (TDIs) are among the most frequent emergencies encountered in dental and emergency settings, particularly affecting children and adolescents whose permanent teeth are still developing [1]. They encompass acute injuries to dental hard tissues, periodontal tissues, or dental pulp caused by external forces, including concussion, fracture, displacement, and avulsion [2]. These injuries are highly prevalent in children and adolescents, especially aged 7–9 years old children [3]. Their young permanent teeth during this period are particularly vulnerable due to their eruptive status with incomplete root formation and immature periodontal/pulpal structures as well as their active lifestyle [4]. Dentists’ ability to recognize and manage TDIs correctly is critical because initial treatment decisions directly lead to long-term outcomes and significantly affect children’s quality of life and psychological well-being [5, 6].

Over the past decades, health authorities introduced the Standardized Residency Training (SRT) program, which provided structured clinical training. As the future frontline providers in dental care and therapy, residents received essential skills in TDIs management [7–9]. International Association of Dental Traumatology (IADT) guidelines, which have been translated into various languages, provided evidence-based recommendations and detailed protocols for treating various types of dental trauma, particularly in immature permanent teeth [2]. However, the previous study indicated that the integration and implementation of these guidelines within the SRT framework remain inadequate, resulting in a significant gap between theoretical knowledge and practical training [10], therefore, reformation of the TDI training program was required.

The majority of previous studies on TDI training has focused on practicing dentists [11], dental educators [12], parental awareness [13], or only focused on the knowledge of dental students [14, 15], neglecting residents with different specialties. Deficits in emergency skills among dental healthcare providers can delay critical interventions during time-sensitive emergencies, worsening patient prognoses by allowing conditions to progress before effective treatment [16]. The level of expertise in TDI management, which can be enhanced through both improved preclinical education and rigorous postgraduate training, directly correlates with improved patient prognosis. Ensuring that dental professionals are well-trained in both their undergraduate curriculum and through a standardized postgraduate residency is essential for the successful management of TDIs. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) of dental residents with different specialties regarding TDIs (including but not limited to avulsion), with a focus on educational influences, to inform targeted curricular reforms.

Methods

Ethical approval

This observation study was conducted at the School of Stomatology, Wuhan University, from May to June 2024. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the School & Hospital of Stomatology, Wuhan University (Approval No.: [WDKQ2024] B42).

Participants

Dental residents across all three training years during the 2021–2024 academic year were enrolled. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) currently enrolled in the SRT program; (2) active participation in clinical or academic training during the study period. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) graduated, withdrawn, or suspended residents; (2) leave of absence during the survey period.

Sample size determination and sampling methodology

The sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula for infinite populations [17, 18] (e = 0.05, p = 0.5, Z = 1.96), yielding an initial estimate of 384 participants. Subsequent finite population correction (n = 465) reduced the minimum required sample size to 211. Although probability sampling would be methodologically ideal, practical constraints necessitated the adoption of a census-style convenience sampling approach to maximize participation. To enhance representativeness and minimize potential sampling bias, the questionnaire was distributed to all 465 eligible participants (1st-, 2nd-, and 3rd-year residents actively enrolled in the standardized training program at the School of Stomatology, Wuhan University during the study period). In total, 256 residents were currently enrolled in the SRT program.

Study design

A structured electronic questionnaire assessing TDIs (Supplemental Information File 1) was developed based on the IADT guidelines from 2020, and its content validity was confirmed through a Delphi expert consultation process [2]. All experts over 10 years of clinical and research experience in the field completed three evaluation rounds. They rated each item on a 5-point Likert scale for relevance and provided qualitative feedback. As the questionnaire is a heterogeneous knowledge test covering multiple domains of dental trauma, a high degree of internal consistency is not theoretically expected or desired, as it would indicate item redundancy. Prior to survey administration, all participants received standardized instructions regarding the study objectives and questionnaire content, followed by a pilot survey to confirm comprehension. The survey was implemented electronically using two validated platforms: (1) the SoJump online survey system (Ranxing, Changsha, China) and (2) the WeChat messaging application (Tencent, Shenzhen, China). All participants provided digital informed consent before questionnaire access. To optimize response rates, automated reminders were distributed seven days after the initial questionnaire deployment. Data collection maintained strict anonymity, with voluntary participation emphasized throughout the study protocol.

Questionnaire structure and content

The questionnaire comprised demographics and four distinct sections assessing various dimensions of TDI management. Demographics involved characteristics, specialty and sex. Section I evaluated TDI clinical exposure and educational experiences through three items. Section II assessed TDI knowledge competency using five clinically relevant items. Section III examined TDI professional attitudes via six Likert-scale items. Section IV investigated TDI clinical practice patterns through four items. Both single-response and multiple-choice question formats were used to optimize data collection for different content domains.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (IBM, Armonk, USA; Version 26.0). Descriptive statistics were presented as frequency distributions with percentages for categorical variables. Between-group comparisons were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square (χ²) test for contingency tables. Either Z-tests with Bonferroni correction or adjusted residual analysis was conducted to identify specific group differences. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

TDIs experience and learning situation

In terms of TDIs experience and learning situation (Table 2), 172 (67.2%) of residents rated themselves “familiar’’ with TDI management protocols. This familiarity varied by specialty (P = 0.038): it was highest in General Dentistry (77.6% felt familiar) and Oral Medicine (72.3%), and lower in Oral Surgery (61.3%) and Others (54.8%). Traditional classroom teaching was the most commonly used method (90.6%), followed by internet resources, which had the lowest uptake among Oral Surgery residents (9.7%) (P = 0.002). Problem-based learning (PBL) was infrequently utilized (around 25–32% across groups). The majority of the residents preferred to learn about “Emergency Treatment’’ (92.6%), “Therapy’’ (93.0%), and “Prognosis’’ (84.4%). Knowledge competency related to TDIs of participants (N = 256)

Regarding the timing of replantation for an avulsed tooth, 58.2% of residents correctly responded that replantation should be done “immediately’’, while another 38.3% answered “within 30 minutes’’. Taken together, 96.5% appreciated the urgency of replantation within a narrow timeframe, although responses were split between immediate vs. 30 min, indicating some ambiguity in the understanding of the optimal window. There was no significant difference among specialties on this question (P = 0.668).

When asked about cleaning a contaminated avulsed tooth, the majority of the residents (93.4%) preferred using “saline’’, while “milk’’ was rarely chosen, and “tap water’’ and “alcohol’’ were mostly avoided. The “gauze or paper’’ and “cold water” were totally rejected.

When asked about the best method to stabilize a replanted immature permanent tooth, “Flexible splinting with stainless steel wire’’ was the most common choice (47.7%). These preferences did not differ significantly by specialty (P = 0.352), although a trend was observed where Oral Surgery and Others residents were more evenly split between wire splints and resin-bonded fixation compared to General Dentistry and Oral Medicine residents, who leaned more towards wire splinting. “Fixation to adjacent teeth with resin’’ was also a popular option, especially among Oral Medicine and Oral Surgery residents. Importantly, there was a pronounced knowledge gap in the understanding of how long to splint an avulsed immature permanent tooth. The correct guideline-based answer (approximately 2 weeks of flexible splinting) was selected by only 29.3% of residents overall.

Attitudes on TDIs management

Professional attitudes on TDIs were shown in Table 4, most participants (94.5%) agreed that preserving the vital pulp in immature permanent teeth is crucial for root development, with significant differences across specialties (P = 0.029). It is worth noting that 6.9% of General Dentistry residents disagreed and 6.8% of Others residents were unclear. All residents almost unanimously recognized the need for proper evaluation and follow-up after dental trauma: 92.6% disagreed with the statement that “no examination is needed if there is no obvious pain or fracture after TDIs’’ with only 6.3% agreeing with that improper approach. A large majority of the residents (95.7%) agreed that mastering TDI management is critical for future clinical practice, with no significant specialty-based differences (P = 0.317). Consistent with the reliance on traditional instruction, about 28.1% of residents reported being satisfied with their current TDI education (72 of 256), with no significant differences between specialties (P = 0.834). Meanwhile, 96.9% agreed that additional training is needed to improve their TDI management skills. Discussion

The previous study revealed significant knowledge and practice gaps in the management of TDIs among dental students [10], and this study analyzed these gaps differ by specialty in ways that reflect both predoctoral education shortcomings and inconsistencies in China’s SRT system. Overall, the residents demonstrated favorable understanding of certain core principles. Nearly all knew the ideal storage media for avulsed teeth and recognized the importance of pulp vitality but showed confusion or lack of knowledge in other critical areas like the optimal replantation timeframe and splinting duration. Moreover, self-reported confidence was low, and practical involvement varied greatly by specialty, suggesting that current training (both in Dental School and residency) is not adequately translating knowledge into clinical competence.

Specialty disparities found in this survey highlight that while SRT in China provides important training opportunities, they currently shows variability in competency outcomes for TDI management, indicating specific areas for further enhancement. Despite 88.7% of residents having encountered TDIs cases, only 67.2% were familiar with relevant knowledge. 80.6% of Oral Surgery residents never managed a TDI on their own, versus only 5.1% in General Dentistry residents, who typically rotate through multiple departments, reported higher familiarity with TDI protocols and more hands-on experience managing trauma cases independently, whereas Oral Surgery residents, despite being advanced trainees, were far less likely to have independently treated such cases. This discrepancy may stem from the structure of residency programs: Oral Surgery departments might handle TDIs in a more attending-driven or surgically focused manner, limiting resident autonomy, while General Dentistry residents (and those in Oral Medicine) possibly see a broader range of cases including TDIs in emergency clinics. The management of TDIs requires multidisciplinary collaboration involving Pediatric Dentistry, Endodontics, Periodontics, Orthodontics, and Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery to develop comprehensive treatment plans. Ensuring that all residents, regardless of specialty, receive adequate supervised experience in emergency TDI care could improve confidence and competence. Indeed, confidence levels were lowest in those specialties with fewer independent cases. TDI education remains fragmented across these disciplines so that every resident attains a baseline proficiency in managing acute dental injuries, which are likely to present in any dental practice. Therefore, it is essential to incorporate interdisciplinary integration to foster professional complementarity when designing teaching programs [19].

About 95.7% of residents affirmed the clinical value of TDI management, but the low satisfaction with traditional teaching methods (28.1%) reflects the deep-seated crisis in dental education systems, necessitating blended teaching to bridge the knowledge-practice gap. This dissatisfaction was echoed by the finding that virtually all residents (96.9%) desire more training to feel competent. In this study, the dominance of traditional classroom teaching and low utilization of online resources indicate a disconnection between current teaching models and clinical skill development needs. Recent studies confirm that blended teaching can increase the interest and engagement of TDIs [20]. The PBL-based TDI case discussion and interdisciplinary case discussions not only reinforcing TDI knowledge, but also improving the ability of problem-solving [21, 22]. The 3D printed dental models designed using CAD/CAM technology can be used to manufacture removable splints, thereby reducing the iatrogenic injury often encountered in the production process of traditional models [23]. As early as 2001, British scientists developed a knowledge-based computer model that integrates the examination, diagnosis and treatment of TDIs to assist clinical decision-making [24]. Up to now, there have been many artificial intelligence chatbots that can provide the public with knowledge and information about TDIs [25–27]. It is believed that the integration of high-fidelity simulation, interdisciplinary case discussions, and learning portfolios can bridge this gap by providing deliberate practice and reflective assessment. Therefore, it is recommended to integrate active learning strategies into teaching reforms enhancing clinical decision-making for acute dental trauma [28].

The findings indicate that preclinical dental education gaps persist into residency. For instance, only 29.3% of residents could identify the correct 2-week splinting duration for avulsed immature teeth. This is a fundamental guideline taught by IADT, yet the majority of residents chose 4 weeks or longer, a misconception that suggests inadequate emphasis or retention of this knowledge. Under traditional curricula, TDI management is often covered briefly in Pediatric Dentistry or Endodontics courses, potentially without hands-on simulation. The fact that so many residents shared the same misunderstanding implies a systemic educational gap rather than an individual lapse. To address this, both undergraduate and residency training should be reformed to reinforce guideline-based trauma management. For example, integrating case-based learning and simulations of TDI scenarios during Dental School could ensure that students not only hear the guidelines but also practice applying them.

Notably, the near-universal recognition of ideal storage media (milk or physiological media) and the importance of pulp vitality indicate some success in knowledge dissemination, probably due to instruction in Dental School. 94.5% of residents acknowledged the importance of preserving the vital pulp for root development, reflecting the awareness of contemporary regenerative endodontic therapy (RET) concepts. However, General Dentistry (6.9%) and Others (6.8%) residents held opposing or uncertain attitudes, possibly due to a lack of hands-on experience with regenerative endodontic therapy and limited understanding of the long-term outcome of vital pulp therapy. If a resident has not seen an immature avulsed tooth saved via pulp revascularization (for example, via apexification or revascularization procedures taught in Endodontics), they might doubt its importance or feasibility. In an analysis of the medical records of the emergency department of a municipal dental clinic, it was found that in 67.6% of the cases of permanent tooth trauma, emergency assistance was inadequate and pulp inactivation or pulpectomy under local anesthesia was performed in a complex crown fracture that needed to preserve pulp vitality [29]. Such findings underlined that merely teaching the theory of pulp preservation is not enough. Incorporating clinical cases of long-term trauma outcomes into TDI courses (both preclinical and during residency) could convince more trainees of the value of preserving pulp vitality for prognosis. In short, gaps in translating knowledge into practice may be bridged by exposure to real-world evidence during training [10].

It is observed that Oral Surgery residents not only had lower exposure to managing TDIs but also showed markedly less inclination toward preventive practices. Oral Surgery residents were significantly less likely to independently manage TDIs than General Dentistry residents, while the latter occasionally managed cases independently. No data have been reported on the ability of dentists or dental students to manage TDIs independently, but according to Brundin et al. [30], it was shown that dental students were the least confident in managing acute TDIs (average 6.1–6.2). From another point of view, it proved that their ability to manage TDIs independently was limited [31]. Additionally, significant disparities existed in patient education, with overall low oral health education rates especially in oral surgery, revealing cognitive biases toward Preventive Dentistry roles, consistent with the low proportion of students inclined to learn oral preventive measures in the results. Low oral health education rates may impact long-term patient outcomes. To improve this, curriculum reform should incorporate modules on communication skills and preventive counseling specifically tailored to traumatic injuries. For example, simulation of patient education scenarios or requiring residents to conduct community outreach on TDIs could build these skills [32]. Emphasizing prevention in every specialty is important; the results of this study show that currently, certain specialties like Oral Surgery may harbor a “curative’’ or intervention-focused mindset at the expense of Preventive Dentistry [31]. In the course of TDIs, teachers could create opportunities for students to participate in oral health education activities [33]. By doing so, future dentists regardless of specialty will be more prepared not only to treat injuries but also to encourage practices that reduce the incidence or severity of TDIs.

Collectively, the findings of the present study reveal one of the areas for improvement in both the Dental School curriculum and the residency training system in China. Encouragingly, residents recognize these shortcomings, which was evidenced by the overwhelming agreement that more training is necessary. In terms of theoretical instruction, the curriculum can adopt a blended learning approach, incorporating knowledge graph-based Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs) and a flipped classroom model to accommodate the varied schedules of residents from different departments [34, 35]. During the pre-clinical training phase, problem-based learning (PBL) [28, 36], case-based learning (CBL), and team-based learning (TBL) methods can be utilized to discuss typical dental trauma cases. These approaches help cultivate teamwork skills, holistic clinical thinking, and clarify the roles of various specialties within the diagnostic and treatment process. Virtual simulation technology can be fully leveraged for repeated practice of clinical procedures to reinforce theoretical knowledge [37–39], while high-fidelity manikins or 3D-printed models [40] can be employed for hands-on skills training. Furthermore, multidisciplinary collaborative training can enhance trainees’ confidence and clinical competence in managing dental trauma [28, 41]. For the evaluation of teaching effectiveness, competency-based assessment methods such as Direct Observation of Procedural Skills (DOPS) [42] and Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCE) [43] focused on dental trauma should be implemented. A blended evaluation strategy combining formative assessments—including online participation and offline discussion performance—with summative evaluations based on theoretical examinations and clinical skills assessments is recommended. Another important reform is strengthening mentorship and cross-specialty rotations. If Oral Surgery residents rotate more time through Pediatric Dentistry or emergency dental units, they would likely gain more exposure to trauma cases and appreciate the full spectrum of care including prevention and follow-up. Likewise, general practice residents could benefit from shadowing specialists to learn advanced trauma management techniques, fostering a two-way learning street. Standardized clinical training should not mean uniformity in a narrow sense, but rather comprehensive uniformity [44]. Every resident should graduate with both the specialized skills of their field and the general competencies to manage common emergencies like TDIs.

There are some limitations to consider in interpreting the above results. This study was conducted at a single institution (School of Stomatology, Wuhan University), and the training experiences there may not fully represent all regions of China. The systematic differences in curriculum design, clinical resources, faculty expertise, and assessment standards among different Dental Schools all affect dental residents’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding dental trauma. For instance, the structure of clinical rotations and departmental emphasis could shape residents’ exposure to TDIs. However, when compared to studies in other countries [6, 11, 45]), similar gaps in confidence and preparedness have been reported, suggesting this is a global concern rather than an isolated issue. This study used census-style convenience sampling method, which may be subject to recall bias due to self-reported data. Although we achieved a response rate of 55.1%, non-response bias remains a possibility if those who chose not to participate systematically differed in their knowledge or attitudes toward TDIs. To control for potential biases, several measures were implemented, such as sending questionnaire reminders and excluding ineligible students—including those who had dropped out, retaken courses, or were on leave during the study period. Furthermore, regional training differences limit the generalizability of results. Most importantly, the reliance on self-reported data means that reported confidence and practices may not fully align with actual clinical performance, a discrepancy known as the “know-do” gap. Future research should expand to multiple training centers and possibly incorporate objective assessments (such as knowledge tests or skill examinations) to validate and build upon these findings.

Conclusion

Dental residents in this study recognized the importance of TDI management but exhibited considerable variation in knowledge and confidence, suggesting that these disparities may be linked to both their undergraduate education and residency specialty training. The observed gaps highlight potential areas for improvement in educational strategies. Modernizing teaching methods and ensuring that all residents acquire key trauma management skills could better prepare them for clinical practice and contribute to improved patient outcomes for TDIs. Given that traumatic dental injuries can present across all fields of dentistry, TDIs management training represents an essential competency for every dentist. The findings support calls for educational innovation to better prepare a new generation of dentists in managing dental emergencies.
