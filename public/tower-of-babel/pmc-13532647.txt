Comparative Prognostic Value of Baseline CRP, NLR, and MLR for 12-Month Outcomes After Ischaemic Stroke: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Yu Zhao, Yutong Wang, Shengyuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveC-reactive protein (CRP), the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and the monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR) are routinely available inflammatory markers of uncertain relative prognostic value after stroke. This study aimed to investigate the associations of baseline CRP, NLR, and MLR with 12-month mortality and recurrence in patients with stroke.MethodsThis single-center prospective cohort study enrolled hospitalized patients with stroke. Univariable analyses were performed to compare baseline characteristics according to 12-month mortality and recurrence status. Multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to evaluate the independent associations of CRP, NLR, and MLR with 12-month mortality and recurrence after adjustment for clinically relevant covariates.ResultsA total of 2937 patients were included in the baseline analysis. Among them, 2166 were included in the 12-month mortality analysis, and 237 died during follow-up. In the multivariable logistic regression model, higher CRP was independently associated with an increased risk of 12-month mortality (OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1.01–1.02, P < 0.001). NLR showed a borderline association with mortality (OR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.00–1.13, P = 0.064), whereas MLR was not independently associated with mortality. A total of 2018 patients were included in the 12-month recurrence analysis, among whom 614 experienced recurrent events. In the multivariable model, higher CRP was independently associated with an increased risk of recurrence (OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1.00–1.02, P = 0.025), whereas NLR and MLR was not independently associated with recurrence.ConclusionBaseline CRP was independently associated with 12-month mortality and, more weakly, with recurrence. The mortality association was substantially stronger, and CRP should be regarded primarily as a marker of long-term mortality risk. NLR and MLR showed no independent association with either endpoint. CRP, combined with key clinical indicators, may assist long-term risk stratification after ischaemic stroke.Keywords: stroke, C-reactive protein, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio, prognosis

Introduction

Stroke remains one of the leading causes of death and long-term disability worldwide and continues to impose a substantial burden on patients, families, and healthcare systems.1,2 In China, stroke also remains a major public health challenge. Among adults aged 40 years and older, the estimated prevalence of stroke in 2020 was 2.6%, highlighting its major contribution to recurrent vascular events, functional dependence, and healthcare expenditure.3 These data underscore the urgent need to improve long-term risk stratification and prognostic assessment after stroke.

Inflammation is increasingly recognized as a central mechanism in the pathophysiology of stroke and its subsequent progression. In addition to its established role in atherosclerotic plaque formation, destabilization, and thrombosis, the post-stroke inflammatory response may further aggravate brain injury through blood-brain barrier disruption, leukocyte infiltration, oxidative stress, and secondary neuronal damage.4 These inflammatory processes may influence not only early neurological deterioration but also long-term clinical outcomes, including mortality and recurrent vascular events.

Against this background, peripheral inflammatory biomarkers have attracted growing attention as practical tools for prognostic evaluation after stroke. CRP is a well-established acute-phase reactant reflecting systemic inflammatory activation, whereas NLR and MLR are inexpensive composite indices derived from routine blood counts and may better capture the balance between innate and adaptive immune responses. Previous studies have shown that elevated CRP is associated with higher mortality, poorer functional outcomes, and an increased risk of recurrent vascular events after stroke.5–12 Likewise, increasing evidence indicates that elevated NLR and altered monocyte-lymphocyte balance may be related to unfavorable prognosis in patients with stroke.13–17

These three markers are not biologically interchangeable, and there are theoretical reasons to expect that they behave differently over a prolonged follow-up period. CRP is a hepatically synthesised acute-phase reactant whose production is driven principally by interleukin-6 and which therefore integrates the intensity of systemic inflammatory signalling over a relatively sustained time frame; a single measurement may consequently provide a reasonable index of a patient’s underlying inflammatory burden. By contrast, NLR and MLR are ratios of circulating leukocyte subpopulations whose absolute counts are strongly and rapidly modulated by transient physiological influences, including catecholamine- and cortisol-mediated demargination and lymphopenia in the acute stress response to cerebral infarction, intercurrent infection, corticosteroid exposure and diurnal variation. Values obtained at a single admission time point may therefore reflect the magnitude of the acute stress response rather than a stable inflammatory phenotype, and may regress substantially towards baseline within days to weeks. Whether such indices retain independent prognostic information over a 12-month horizon, once established clinical determinants and a direct acute-phase reactant are taken into account, has not been established, and is a central question of the present analysis.

However, several important uncertainties remain in this field. First, much of the existing literature has focused on short-term outcomes, such as in-hospital events or 3-month functional status, rather than longer-term prognosis.5,6,13,16 Second, prior studies have often evaluated only one inflammatory marker or one clinical endpoint, making it difficult to compare the relative prognostic value of CRP, NLR, and MLR within the same cohort. Third, the evidence regarding long-term recurrence remains inconsistent, particularly after adjustment for age, stroke severity, and vascular risk factors.9–12 Finally, because leukocyte-based indices are influenced by acute stress responses and intercurrent conditions, their independent prognostic significance beyond conventional clinical indicators remains uncertain.13–17

Therefore, in this single-center prospective cohort study, we investigated the associations of baseline CRP, NLR, and MLR measured at admission with 12-month outcomes after stroke, with a particular focus on all-cause mortality and stroke recurrence. By evaluating these inflammatory markers in parallel and in conjunction with established clinical measures, we aimed to clarify their potential value for long-term risk stratification after stroke. A previous report from our group, based on the same prospective stroke registry, examined the triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index as a metabolic predictor of one-year mortality in the non-diabetic subgroup of this cohort.18 The present study differs in both exposure and population: it focuses on inflammatory rather than metabolic markers, directly compares three routinely available inflammatory indices (CRP, NLR, and MLR) within a single analytic framework, and evaluates the entire stroke cohort rather than the non-diabetic subset.

Methods

Study Design and Reporting Standard

Patients with stroke who were consecutively hospitalized in the Department of Neurology of Shenzhen Third People’s Hospital between January 2021 and July 2023 were screened for eligibility and followed for 12 months after enrollment. This study was designed as a single-center prospective cohort study. The manuscript was prepared in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) recommendations for cohort studies.19

Study Population

Only patients with acute ischaemic stroke were enrolled; patients with primary intracerebral haemorrhage or subarachnoid haemorrhage were not eligible and are not represented in the cohort. Adult patients were eligible if they met all of the following criteria: (1) a diagnosis of stroke established on the basis of clinical manifestations and confirmed by brain CT or MRI;20 (2) age ≥60 years; (3) availability of baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory data, including CRP and complete blood cell counts for calculation of NLR and MLR; and (4) completion of follow-up or availability of sufficient follow-up information for endpoint ascertainment. Patients were excluded if they had any of the following: (1) active malignant disease; (2) severe infection at admission or other major inflammatory conditions that could substantially affect inflammatory biomarker levels; (3) autoimmune disease; (4) recent use of systemic corticosteroids or immunosuppressive agents; (5) missing key baseline exposure, covariate, or outcome data; or (6) inability to complete follow-up.

The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Third People’s Hospital (KY2025-082-01) and adhered to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Baseline Data Collection

Baseline data were collected at admission by trained clinicians using standardized case report forms. The following information was recorded: age, sex, vascular risk factors, lifestyle factors, and stroke-related clinical characteristics. Vascular risk factors included hypertension, atrial fibrillation, cerebral artery stenosis, hyperlipidemia, and hyperhomocysteinemia, as available in the dataset. Lifestyle-related variables included smoking, alcohol use, and sedentary lifestyle. Neurological impairment and functional status at admission were assessed using the NIHSS, mRS, and Barthel Index.21–23 In the present study, these scales were used to characterize baseline stroke severity, global disability, and activities of daily living, respectively.

Potential confounding variables were selected on the basis of clinical relevance and the available dataset. These included age, admission NIHSS score, atrial fibrillation, smoking, alcohol use, sedentary lifestyle, fasting glucose, triglycerides, total cholesterol, LDL-C, and other variables that showed clinically meaningful imbalance in univariable analyses. Baseline stroke severity was categorised from the admission NIHSS score using conventional cut-points as mild (NIHSS 0–4), moderate (NIHSS 5–15) and severe (NIHSS ≥ 16).21 Fasting blood glucose was measured upon admission as part of routine clinical assessment. Although the stress hyperglycemic index (eg, the ratio of glucose to glycated hemoglobin) provides a more precise reflection of acute glucose response, glycated hemoglobin is not routinely tested in all patients during acute hospitalization; therefore, this study employed baseline fasting blood glucose levels at admission as a practical alternative indicator. Because the purpose of the study was to assess the independent association of baseline CRP, NLR, and MLR with long-term outcomes after stroke, established prognostic indicators such as age and stroke severity were retained in multivariable models regardless of their univariable significance.24,25

Measurement of Inflammatory Biomarkers

Peripheral venous blood samples were obtained at admission as part of routine clinical evaluation. Serum CRP concentration was measured by the hospital clinical laboratory using standard methods. Absolute neutrophil count, absolute lymphocyte count, and absolute monocyte count were obtained from the complete blood count. NLR was calculated as the absolute neutrophil count divided by the absolute lymphocyte count, and MLR was calculated as the absolute monocyte count divided by the absolute lymphocyte count. In the main analyses, CRP, NLR, and MLR were treated as continuous variables.

Outcome Definition and Follow-Up

The primary outcomes were 12-month all-cause mortality and 12-month stroke recurrence. All-cause mortality was defined as death from any cause during follow-up. Stroke recurrence is defined as any new, clinically manifest cerebrovascular event occurring more than 24 hours after the initial stroke and during the 12-month follow-up period, including new ischemic stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, or transient ischemic attack; such events must be confirmed by the attending neurologist and radiologically verified during imaging studies. The determination of recurrent events does not consider whether hospitalization was required, and each patient is counted only once for the first recurrence event, in line with contemporary secondary prevention–oriented stroke follow-up principles.26 Patients were followed for 12 months from the date of enrolment. Follow-up was conducted using a combination of four complementary modalities: structured telephone interview by trained investigators using a standardised questionnaire; review of outpatient clinic records at scheduled neurology follow-up visits; review of inpatient records for any subsequent hospital admission; and linkage to the regional health record system of Shenzhen municipality, which was additionally used to ascertain vital status. For participants with more than one available follow-up source, outcome information was adjudicated using the most complete and objective available record, with neuroimaging-confirmed hospital documentation taking precedence over self-report.

In the recurrence analysis, all patients with complete 12-month follow-up data were included regardless of their survival status. Recurrent stroke was defined as an independent endpoint; patients who died from recurrent stroke during follow-up remained in the recurrence cohort. The mortality analysis (n = 2166) and the recurrence analysis (n = 2018) were two parallel analyses conducted using distinct endpoint definitions, with partial overlap between the patient populations but without mutual subset inclusion. The difference in sample sizes reflects variations in available follow-up data for each endpoint: all 2166 patients had sufficient mortality assessment data, whereas 2018 patients possessed complete recurrence evaluation data.

Data Quality Control and Bias Control

To improve data reliability, all baseline variables were extracted using predefined coding rules and entered into a structured database. The dataset was checked for missing values, implausible entries, and internal inconsistencies before formal analysis. Continuous variables were reviewed for distributional plausibility, and categorical variables were cross-checked against original records when necessary. To ensure data quality, all continuous variables underwent outlier screening. Specifically, extreme values exceeding clinically acceptable ranges were verified against original records and traced for data sources; those identified as result of input or transcription errors were excluded, and summary statistics were recalculated accordingly. To reduce selection bias, consecutive eligible patients were enrolled. To reduce measurement bias, laboratory data were obtained from the same hospital laboratory system and clinical scales were assessed according to routine standardized neurological evaluation.21–23 To reduce confounding bias, multivariable regression models were used to adjust for major demographic, clinical, and lifestyle-related factors.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or, when appropriate, median with interquartile range. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Baseline characteristics were first summarized for the overall cohort and by sex. Thereafter, participants were compared according to 12-month mortality status (survival vs death) and, among survivors, according to 12-month recurrence status (no recurrence vs recurrence).

For between-group comparisons, Student’s t test or the Mann–Whitney U-test was used for continuous variables, as appropriate according to data distribution, and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables. Variables considered clinically relevant and/or associated with the outcome in univariable analysis were entered into multivariable logistic regression models to estimate adjusted ORs and 95% CIs for 12-month mortality and 12-month recurrence.19 CRP, NLR, and MLR were entered as continuous exposure variables in the final models together with selected covariates. Multicollinearity among candidate predictors was assessed before model fitting. Multicollinearity among candidate predictors was assessed before model fitting using variance inflation factors (VIF) and tolerance, with VIF > 10 or tolerance < 0.10 taken to indicate problematic collinearity. In the mortality model the maximum VIF was 6.88 (severe disease severity; tolerance 0.161) and all remaining VIF values were below 7.0; in the recurrence model the maximum VIF was 6.03 (admission NIHSS score; tolerance 0.166). No variable met the prespecified threshold for problematic collinearity, and all covariates were therefore retained. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 2937 patients were included in the baseline analysis. The flow of patient inclusion and outcome analyses is shown in Figure 1. Among these patients, 2166 were included in the 12-month mortality analysis, of whom 1929 survived and 237 died during follow-up. In addition, 2018 patients were included in the 12-month recurrence analysis, including 1404 without recurrence and 614 with recurrence.

Flowchart of participant selection and follow-up in the study. Univariate Analysis Results of 12-Month Mortality Rate (Survival vs Death)

Compared with survivors, patients who died were older (74.35 ± 7.89 vs 70.23 ± 7.15 years, P < 0.001) and had more severe disease at admission (P < 0.001). They also had worse neurological and functional status, as reflected by a higher NIHSS score (14.89 ± 8.78 vs 6.20 ± 5.58, P < 0.001), a lower Barthel Index (28.86 ± 29.49 vs 61.87 ± 28.54, P < 0.001), and a higher mRS score (4.05 ± 1.13 vs 2.82 ± 1.35, P < 0.001). Atrial fibrillation was more common in the death group than in the survival group (21.9% vs 9.3%, P < 0.001). Sedentary lifestyle was also more frequent among patients who died (12.2% vs 8.0%, P = 0.026).

In terms of laboratory findings, the death group had higher fasting blood glucose levels (7.56 ± 3.03 vs 6.53 ± 2.66 mmol/L, P < 0.001), lower triglyceride levels (1.31 ± 0.87 vs 1.47 ± 0.97 mmol/L, P = 0.019), markedly higher CRP levels (34.81 ± 53.78 vs 9.37 ± 22.47 mg/L, P < 0.001), higher absolute neutrophil counts (7.49 ± 5.37 vs 5.37 ± 3.99 ×109/L, P < 0.001), and higher NLR values (7.10 ± 5.84 vs 4.32 ± 6.90, P < 0.001). DBP was also slightly higher in patients who died (85.82 ± 14.85 vs 83.60 ± 13.36 mmHg, P = 0.045). No significant differences were observed for sex, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cerebral blood supply artery stenosis, hyperlipidemia, hyperhomocysteinemia, total cholesterol, HDL-C, LDL-C, HCY, absolute lymphocyte count, absolute monocyte count, MLR, or SBP (Table 2).

Patients with recurrence were older than those without recurrence (71.74 ± 7.31 vs 69.79 ± 7.06 years, P < 0.001) and had worse baseline neurological and functional status, including a higher NIHSS score (7.89 ± 6.66 vs 5.96 ± 5.51, P < 0.001), a lower Barthel Index (54.17 ± 30.54 vs 63.38 ± 28.25, P < 0.001), and a higher mRS score (3.11 ± 1.33 vs 2.76 ± 1.36, P < 0.001). Discussion

In this single-center prospective cohort study, we evaluated the associations of baseline CRP, NLR, and MLR with 12-month mortality and recurrence after stroke. Three main findings emerged. First, higher baseline CRP was independently associated with an increased risk of 12-month all-cause mortality after adjustment for major demographic, clinical, and lifestyle-related factors. Second, CRP showed a independently association with 12-month recurrence, whereas age and sedentary lifestyle were independently associated with recurrent stroke. Third, although NLR and MLR were significantly elevated in patients with adverse outcomes in univariable analyses, neither marker remained independently associated with mortality or recurrence after multivariable adjustment. Taken together, these findings suggest that among the inflammatory markers examined in this study, CRP may have the most stable value for long-term prognostic stratification after stroke.

The 12-month recurrence rate observed in this cohort was 30.4%, higher than the typically reported range of 10–15% in population-based studies. This may be attributed to several factors: First, this study was a real-world single-center cohort with a high proportion of elderly patients (mean age 70.8 years) and a significant burden of vascular comorbidities, which may confer greater biological susceptibility to recurrence. Second, our follow-up strategy combined telephone interviews, outpatient visits, hospital record reviews, and regional health record linkage, potentially enabling more comprehensive capture of recurrence events compared to studies relying on a single follow-up method.

The finding that baseline CRP was independently associated with 12-month mortality is consistent with a substantial body of previous literature. Earlier cohort studies showed that elevated CRP in the acute phase of ischemic stroke was associated with greater stroke severity, poor outcome, and increased mortality.5,6 More recently, a meta-analysis confirmed that higher circulating CRP levels were significantly associated with increased all-cause mortality in patients with acute ischemic stroke.27 Another systematic review and meta-analysis focusing on hs-CRP also reported that elevated hs-CRP was associated with higher mortality risk in stroke populations.28 Our findings extend this evidence by showing that even after adjustment for age, baseline NIHSS score, fasting glucose, lifestyle factors, and leukocyte-derived inflammatory ratios, CRP remained independently associated with 12-month mortality. This suggests that CRP may capture a dimension of systemic inflammatory burden that is not fully reflected by conventional clinical indicators alone.

Several biological mechanisms may explain the observed association between CRP and mortality after stroke. CRP is a sensitive marker of systemic inflammation and may reflect a combination of pre-existing atherosclerotic instability, acute tissue injury, endothelial dysfunction, activation of the innate immune system, and susceptibility to post-stroke complications. Persistent or excessive inflammatory activation after stroke may aggravate secondary brain injury, promote thrombosis, impair vascular repair, and increase the risk of infection or multi-organ dysfunction, all of which may contribute to long-term death risk.4 Therefore, the association identified in our study is biologically plausible and supports the concept that systemic inflammation is not merely a bystander phenomenon after stroke, but may be closely linked to subsequent prognosis.

In contrast to the clear association observed for mortality, the association between CRP and recurrence, although statistically independent, was considerably weaker in magnitude. The adjusted odds ratio of 1.008 per 1 mg/L corresponds to a modest increase in recurrence risk, and the lower limit of the confidence interval lies close to unity, indicating that the effect estimate is compatible with only a small underlying association. This result is also broadly in line with the existing literature, in which the association between inflammatory markers and recurrent vascular events has been less consistent than that with mortality or short-term functional outcome. Some studies reported that elevated CRP predicted recurrent ischemic events after transient ischemic attack or ischemic stroke,9,10 and a meta-analysis found that CRP was associated with recurrent stroke and major vascular events after ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack.12 However, other studies suggested that the independent contribution of inflammatory markers to recurrent event prediction may be attenuated after accounting for conventional vascular risk factors, stroke subtype, and competing clinical events.17

Several factors may help explain why CRP did not emerge as a statistically robust independent predictor of recurrence in the present study. First, recurrent stroke is a multifactorial outcome that depends not only on biological vulnerability but also on post-discharge treatment adherence, control of vascular risk factors, rehabilitation participation, and secondary prevention strategies. Second, recurrence mechanisms may vary across stroke subtypes, whereas a single baseline CRP measurement may not fully capture ongoing inflammatory activity during follow-up. Third, patients who died were excluded from the recurrence analysis, which may have reduced statistical power and introduced a degree of survivor selection. Accordingly, our results support a cautious interpretation: CRP may have potential value in recurrence risk assessment, but its independent contribution appears weaker and should be confirmed in larger multicenter cohorts with more detailed follow-up information.

An additional important finding of this study is that NLR and MLR were associated with adverse outcomes in univariable analyses but lost significance after multivariable adjustment. This pattern deserves attention. Previous studies and meta-analyses have suggested that elevated NLR is associated with stroke severity, poor functional outcome, and mortality,13,16 while altered lymphocyte-monocyte balance has also been linked to early neurological severity and prognosis in acute ischemic stroke.15 However, much of this evidence has focused on short-term outcomes, and the reported effect sizes are often influenced by baseline severity, infection status, and stroke subtype. In our study, after adjustment for age, baseline NIHSS score, CRP, and other relevant covariates, neither NLR nor MLR independently predicted 12-month mortality or recurrence. This suggests that these leukocyte-derived ratios may be more reflective of acute stress response or concurrent inflammatory activation at presentation than of long-term independent prognostic risk.

From a clinical perspective, the present findings indicate that CRP may be a more robust and pragmatic inflammatory marker than NLR or MLR for long-term outcome stratification after stroke. CRP is inexpensive, widely available, and routinely measured in clinical practice. In contrast, although NLR and MLR are also easily calculated, their independent long-term prognostic utility may be limited when established clinical variables are taken into account. Thus, our findings support the incorporation of CRP into a broader prognostic framework alongside age and admission NIHSS, rather than relying on leukocyte-derived ratios alone.

Beyond inflammatory biomarkers, our multivariable analyses confirmed that age and baseline NIHSS were independently associated with 12-month mortality. This is highly consistent with prior evidence showing that older age and greater neurological deficit at admission are among the strongest determinants of poor stroke prognosis.24,25 In the recurrence model, age remained independently associated with recurrent stroke, further emphasizing the sustained vulnerability of older patients during long-term follow-up. These results reinforce the validity of the current dataset and indicate that the prognostic models in this study behaved in a clinically plausible manner.

Another noteworthy finding is that sedentary lifestyle was independently associated with 12-month recurrence. This result is clinically meaningful because sedentary behavior is potentially modifiable. Previous studies have shown that reduced physical activity or prolonged sedentary time after stroke is associated with a higher risk of recurrent events and worse recovery.29,30 Our findings add to this literature by suggesting that even after adjustment for age, vascular risk factors, lipid variables, and inflammatory markers, sedentary lifestyle remained a significant predictor of recurrent stroke. This highlights the importance of integrating long-term behavioral intervention, physical activity counseling, and post-stroke rehabilitation into secondary prevention strategies.

The present study has several strengths. First, it prospectively evaluated long-term outcomes after stroke in a relatively large real-world clinical cohort. Second, three commonly available inflammatory markers were assessed simultaneously, allowing direct comparison of their relative prognostic relevance. Third, both mortality and recurrence were examined, providing a broader picture of long-term outcome after stroke. Fourth, the multivariable models included key demographic, clinical, metabolic, and lifestyle-related variables, which improved the robustness of the findings.

This study also has limitations. First, as a single-center cohort study, its generalizability may be limited. Second, inflammatory markers were measured only once at baseline, and dynamic changes during follow-up were not captured. Third, residual confounding cannot be excluded, particularly with regard to stroke subtype, infarct volume, acute treatment details, infection during hospitalization, medication adherence, and post-discharge rehabilitation. Fourth, some patients were lost to follow-up or had incomplete outcome data, which may have introduced selection bias. Furthermore, we employed absolute fasting blood glucose levels at admission, which cannot distinguish between acute stress-induced hyperglycemia and pre-existing chronic hyperglycemia, and exhibit only weak correlation with clinical outcomes in our calibration model. Relative hyperglycemia indicators (particularly the glycated hemoglobin-to-blood-glucose ratio) provide a more accurate reflection of acute glucose response and have been demonstrated to independently predict mortality and poor functional outcomes in ischemic stroke beyond absolute glucose concentrations. Merlino et al demonstrated that this ratio is independently associated with adverse outcomes in patients with acute ischemic stroke and demonstrates superior predictive power compared to using admission blood glucose values alone.31 Since this registry study did not systematically measure glycated hemoglobin levels throughout the follow-up period, we were unable to calculate this parameter. The prognostic value of relative hyperglycemia and its interactions with the inflammatory markers measured in this study require further evaluation in future prospective cohort studies that routinely measure glycated hemoglobin at admission. Finally, because the recurrence analysis excluded patients who died during follow-up, the recurrence findings should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

In this single-center prospective cohort study, Baseline CRP was independently associated with 12-month all-cause mortality and, more weakly, with 12-month stroke recurrence after ischaemic stroke. The association with mortality was substantially stronger than that with recurrence, and CRP should therefore be regarded primarily as a marker of long-term mortality risk, with only a modest additional contribution to recurrence risk assessment. In contrast, NLR and MLR did not demonstrate similarly robust independent associations after multivariable adjustment. In addition to CRP, age, disease severity, atrial fibrillation, DBP, and sedentary lifestyle were important determinants of adverse long-term outcomes. These findings suggest that combining CRP with key clinical indicators may improve long-term risk stratification after stroke and help identify patients who may benefit from closer monitoring and more intensive secondary prevention.
