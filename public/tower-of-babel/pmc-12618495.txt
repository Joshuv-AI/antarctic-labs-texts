Dynamics of a novel fractional-order model for autism spectrum disorder perspective propagation with optimal control
Authors: Mehmet Gumus, Shewafera Wondimagegnhu Teklu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study addresses the propagation of negative societal perspectives toward Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) through a novel fractional-order mathematical model that captures the critical memory effect in individuals’ attitude formation, where historical experiences continuously shape current perceptions. By analyzing intervention strategies combining media awareness campaigns and therapeutic programs, we demonstrate that fractional calculus reveals significantly slower attitude transitions (30–50 percent reduction in rate) compared to classical models, with therapeutic interventions proving three times more effective than media campaigns alone in rehabilitating negative perspectives of individuals. Our optimal control problem identifies a strategy that achieves a 75 percent reduction in negative perspectives, while cost-effectiveness analysis confirms the economic viability of the proposed combined approaches. These findings suggest that integrated interventions utilizing both media and therapy control measures yield the most efficient path to mitigating negative perspectives towards Autism Spectrum Disorder, with fractional-order modeling providing essential insights into the persistent nature of social attitudes that traditional integer-order models cannot capture.Keywords: Autism spectrum disorder, Negative perspective, Fractional order model, Media effect, Optimal control theorySubject terms: Mathematics and computing, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Mathematical modeling has long been a cornerstone for understanding the dynamics of complex systems, most notably in epidemiology, where it has been used to study the spread of diseases like HIV, Tuberculosis, and COVID-191,2. The principles of compartmental modeling, famously established by Kermack and McKendrick3, provide a powerful framework for analyzing how states or conditions propagate through a population. Increasingly, these methodologies are being adapted to investigate social phenomena, such as the spread of behaviors, information, and public perspectives1. In this context, the formation and propagation of negative perspectives towards individuals with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) can be conceptualized and studied as a form of social contagion, where ideas and attitudes are transmitted through social interactions.

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a complex neurodevelopmental condition characterized by challenges in social communication and interaction, alongside restricted and repetitive patterns of behavior or interests4,5. Public perception of ASD significantly impacts the social integration, mental well-being, and quality of life of affected individuals and their families. Negative perspectives, often rooted in misinformation or stigma, can lead to social exclusion and discrimination. To better understand and counteract these dynamics, researchers have turned to mathematical modeling to explore the factors influencing public opinion and the efficacy of potential interventions6–9.

Among the various strategies to foster positive social change, media and public awareness campaigns are paramount. The media serves as a primary channel for disseminating information, shaping public discourse, and challenging stereotypes about ASD10. By promoting accurate knowledge and highlighting the strengths and contributions of individuals with ASD, awareness campaigns can reduce the susceptibility of the general population to negative viewpoints and encourage empathy and acceptance. In addition to broad-based media efforts, targeted therapeutic or educational interventions aimed at rehabilitiating negative attitudes represent another critical control strategy.

Although traditional models using ordinary differential equations (ODEs) have provided valuable insights3, they often fail to capture the “memory effect” inherent in social systems. An individual’s perspective is not formed instantaneously but is shaped by a cumulative history of information, personal experiences, and social conditioning. Fractional-order differential equations have emerged as a superior tool for modeling such phenomena, as their non-local nature inherently accounts for the memory and hereditary properties of the system2,11–13. The inclusion of a fractional-order derivative enables a more realistic representation of how past attitudes and information shape the current and future state of public perception.

Recent advancements in applied mathematics have demonstrated the efficacy of fractional-order calculus for modeling complex biological systems with inherent memory effects, particularly in oncology. Studies by14,15 developed sophisticated fractional-order models to optimize combination therapies for lung and colorectal cancers, establishing a robust methodological framework for capturing the non-Markovian, historical dynamics of disease progression and treatment response. A significant novelty in our work, relative to these studies, lies in applying this powerful mathematical formalism to a socio-behavioral health issue—the propagation of negative perspectives towards Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)—thereby addressing a critical gap in modeling psychosocial “contagion.” Furthermore, while researchers like16,17 have successfully integrated optimal control theory to design therapeutic protocols minimizing physiological side effects, our study fills a parallel gap by employing these techniques to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of media and educational interventions aimed at rehabilitating negative attitudes. Expanding beyond clinical settings,18,19 have pioneered hybrid approaches that merge mechanistic models with data-driven machine learning for improved prediction in heart disease and glaucoma. Our research aligns with this integrative spirit but focuses specifically on the dynamical interplay between intervention strategies and public perception, a domain less explored than clinical outcomes. Finally, the focus on a purely population-level dynamical analysis of a behavioral trait, as opposed to the clinical or physiological endpoints studied in20, underscores the unique contribution of our work in extending fractional-order optimal control to public health psychology and stigma reduction.

To move beyond descriptive analysis and towards developing actionable public health policies, this study integrates optimal control theory. The goal is to identify the most effective and efficient strategies to minimize the prevalence of negative perspectives towards ASD. We introduce control functions representing the intensity of media awareness campaigns and the application of rehabilitation therapies. Furthermore, since public health initiatives operate under finite resources, a cost-benefit analysis is essential. This analysis will allow us to assess the economic viability of different control strategies, identifying the most cost-effective approach for implementation.

This study, therefore, proposes and analyzes a novel fractional-order compartmental model to describe the dynamics of negative perspectives towards individuals with ASD. To the best of our knowledge, this work is a pioneering attempt to integrate a fractional-order framework with optimal control and cost-benefit analysis to address the social dynamics of ASD perception. Limitations and model scope

It is crucial to acknowledge that this model is a purposeful simplification designed to capture the core dynamic interplay between social influence and intervention strategies. The “social contagion” assumption, while powerful for generating insights into system-level dynamics, inherently abstracts away a multitude of complex, individual-level factors. These include cultural norms, educational background, socioeconomic status, and personal experiences, which undoubtedly modulate an individual’s susceptibility to and adoption of negative perspectives. The model’s focus is on the aggregate, population-level behavior that emerges from the simplified rules of transmission and recovery, providing a foundational understanding upon which more complex, heterogeneous models can be built. The sections on optimal control and cost-effectiveness analysis should be interpreted within this scope, offering strategic insights rather than exhaustive sociological explanations.

Interpreting the sociological significance of the memory index . A central contribution of this work is the incorporation of the fractional order , or “memory index,” to model the persistence of social attitudes. The parameter () is not intended to map to a single sociopsychological process but rather quantifies the aggregate strength of the system’s historical dependence. A value of represents a Markovian process with no memory, where current perspectives depend only on immediate present influences. In contrast, introduces a long-tailed memory, meaning past experiences and exposures continuously shape an individual’s current viewpoint. The sociological interpretation of different values is thus tied to the rate of decay of past influences. A value of suggests a society where attitudes are somewhat fluid but still influenced by recent history, potentially reflecting a population with access to diverse, evolving information. A lower value like signifies a society with strong cultural inertia, where attitudes are highly persistent and resistant to change due to deep-seated norms, structural factors, or limited information flow. This is evidenced in our simulations by the significantly slower dynamics and delayed peak in negative perspectives for lower values. Therefore, serves as a crucial phenomenological parameter that bridges the mathematical model and societal context, allowing us to simulate how the same intervention strategy might yield slower results in a traditional community () compared to a more dynamic one ().

A critical consideration in fractional-order modeling is the preservation of dimensional homogeneity. The Caputo derivative has dimensions of , while the right-hand side of System (3) consists of terms with dimensions of . A common and rigorous approach to resolve this discrepancy is to introduce a fractional time constant (where has dimensions of time) to rescale the system and ensure dimensional consistency21. However, for the phenomenological model presented here, the primary objective is to capture the qualitative memory-dependent dynamics of a social system rather than to describe a physical law with strict dimensional units. The parameters , and are thus interpreted as effective rates whose influence is non-locally modulated by the fractional operator of order . Their numerical values are held constant from the integer-order case to enable a direct comparison of the system’s behavior under different memory kernels (i.e., different values) against the classical baseline (). This approach, which focuses on the comparative effect of the memory index itself, is well-established in the modeling of biological and social systems using fractional calculus22,23. The fractional derivative fundamentally alters the temporal scaling of the dynamic response, meaning that a parameter value representing a “rate” in the integer-order model embodies a different, often slower, effective process in the fractional-order context due to the memory effect, even though its numerical value remains unchanged.

Optimal control problem and analysis

Control problem formulation

Optimal control theory provides a mathematical framework for determining strategies to steer a dynamical system towards a desired outcome while minimizing associated costs.
