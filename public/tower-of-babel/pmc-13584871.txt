Two-step engineering of porous MoS2 nanofibers for enhanced charge separation, photocatalytic rhodamine B degradation and photoelectrochemical performance
Authors: Phuong Thi Nguyen, Quan Manh Ly, Duy Van Lai, Quang Hung Vu, San Duc Nguyen, Canh Tuan Nguyen, Minh Thanh Vu, Hoai Phuong Nguyen Thi, Phuong Minh Nguyen, Chinh Van Tran, Duong Duc La
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Engineering MoS2 architectures that simultaneously provide efficient light harvesting, charge separation, interfacial transport, and accessible reactive sites remains challenging for photocatalytic and photoelectrochemical applications. Herein, porous mixed-phase 1T/2H MoS2 nanofibers were fabricated through a two-step strategy combining hydrothermal synthesis with electrospinning-assisted fiber construction. The resulting interconnected architecture integrates semiconducting 2H-MoS2 with conductive 1T-rich domains and an accessible porous interface, providing favorable pathways for photogenerated carrier transport and surface reactions. The MoS2 nanofibers exhibited broad visible-light absorption with an optical band gap of approximately 1.56 eV and achieved 89.60% rhodamine B (RhB) degradation within 120 min under simulated sunlight, with an apparent pseudo-first-order rate constant of 1.27 × 10−2 min−1. Approximately 96.5% of the initial photocatalytic activity was retained after three consecutive cycles. Reactive-species trapping identified photogenerated holes (h+) and superoxide radicals (˙O2−) as the dominant oxidative species, consistent with the estimated band energetics. When directly integrated onto FTO, the porous nanofiber electrode displayed reproducible photoresponse with approximately 94% retention after eight light–dark cycles. Illumination decreased the charge-transfer resistance from 52.3 kΩ to 44.6 kΩ and the interfacial relaxation time from 1.38 to 1.14 s, indicating accelerated interfacial carrier transfer. Mott–Schottky analysis further confirmed predominantly n-type behavior. These results establish a direct relationship among mixed-phase electronic structure, porous fibrous architecture, and photogenerated charge utilization, demonstrating a viable phase-and-morphology engineering strategy for developing MoS2-based materials for solar-driven photocatalytic and photoelectrochemical applications.

Introduction

Escalating fossil-fuel consumption has intensified the global energy crisis and environmental deterioration, creating an urgent need for sustainable technologies.1,2 Semiconductor photocatalysis is particularly attractive because it can couple solar-energy conversion with pollution remediation. It can harness solar irradiation to generate hydrogen through water splitting and simultaneously promote the oxidative degradation of persistent organic contaminants.3,4 Rhodamine B, commonly released from dye-intensive industries, is an important example of such pollutants. Its chemical persistence, poor biodegradability, and potentially carcinogenic and mutagenic effects raise serious ecological and public-health concerns.5–7 Conventional treatments, including adsorption, biological processes, and chemical oxidation, often suffer from pollutant transfer, incomplete mineralization, high energy consumption, or secondary waste generation. These limitations highlight the need for more advanced, environmentally friendly, and efficient wastewater treatment technologies.

Photocatalysis provides an environmentally compatible route for harnessing solar energy to drive both contaminant degradation and solar-fuel production, including water splitting, H2 evolution, and CO2 reduction.8 Upon photoexcitation, semiconductor photocatalysts generate electron–hole pairs that can participate directly in surface redox reactions or produce reactive oxygen species capable of decomposing persistent organic molecules under mild operating conditions.1,9 Although conventional oxides such as TiO2 and ZnO have been extensively investigated, their wide band gaps, restricted visible-light utilization, and rapid charge-carrier recombination continue to limit solar-conversion efficiency and practical environmental remediation.9,10 These limitations motivate the development of visible-light-responsive photocatalysts that combine efficient photon harvesting, rapid charge separation, abundant surface reaction sites, and sufficient redox capability for both organic-pollutant mineralization and photoelectrochemical water splitting.11,12

Recent advances demonstrate the versatility of MoS2 photocatalysts in visible-light-driven dye degradation and H2O2 production, with interlayer and heterointerface engineering improving active-site exposure and charge separation.13–16 MoS2 performance strongly depends on morphology, porosity, layer thickness, edge density, and phase composition.17,18 he semiconducting 2H phase is photoactive but poorly conductive, whereas the 1T phase improves charge transport and active-site availability.19 Therefore, porous MoS2 nanofibers were designed to combine efficient charge transport, accessible surfaces, and abundant active sites for RhB degradation and photoelectrochemical applications.1,20 Beyond semiconductor photocatalysis, supported Mo species have demonstrated broad catalytic versatility; for example, polyaniline-supported Mo efficiently promotes oxidative deoximation using H2O2 in ethanol under mild conditions, highlighting how coordination with a polymeric support can regulate Mo-centered reactivity and enable more sustainable oxidation processes.21 Photocatalytic activity depends on both semiconductor composition and exposed crystal facets. Bi et al. showed that Ag3PO4 rhombic dodecahedra with high-energy {110} facets exhibited enhanced visible-light photooxidation due to their higher surface energy and redox activity.22 Similarly, shape-controlled Cu2O octahedra exposing {110} facets outperformed {110}-terminated cubes, confirming that facet-selective growth can regulate pollutant adsorption, surface activation, and photocatalytic reaction kinetics.23 Studies of anatase TiO2 and ZnO further showed that the relative exposure of high-energy or polar facets can be adjusted through growth modifiers and hydrothermal conditions, thereby altering surface chemistry, adsorption configurations, and the density of reactive sites.24,25

Morphology affects photocatalytic performance through more than surface area alone. Thin nanosheets and hierarchical nanoflowers expose more edge sites and shorten carrier-diffusion distances. Their open channels also facilitate reactant transport. Three-dimensional structures can further enhance light scattering and optical absorption.9,20 Facet-dependent charge dynamics are also important. BiVO4 crystals with controlled {010}/{011} facets showed directional carrier migration and enhanced water oxidation. Different facets also exhibited distinct hole-trapping, recombination, and surface-reaction kinetics.26,27 Morphology can additionally influence vacancies, edge states, phase composition, and interfacial charge transfer.28,29 Thus, effective photocatalyst design requires coordinated control of surface structure, porosity, defects, and charge-transport pathways.

These morphology-dependent principles are particularly relevant to MoS2 because its catalytic and photoelectrochemical properties are intrinsically anisotropic. The semiconducting basal plane of thermodynamically stable 2H-MoS2 is comparatively inert and poorly conductive, whereas unsaturated Mo and S sites located at sheet edges are substantially more reactive; incorporation of the metallic 1T phase can further improve electrical conductivity and facilitate interfacial carrier transport.19,30 Consequently, MoS2 nanoflowers assembled from ultrathin sheets can provide large accessible areas and high edge-site densities, but compact aggregation and restacking may obstruct mass transport, reduce light penetration, conceal active surfaces, and introduce discontinuous pathways for long-range charge migration.1,20 Although extensive efforts have focused on MoS2 nanosheets, nanoflowers, nanoparticles, and phase-engineered hybrids, comparatively little attention has been directed toward integrating edge-rich MoS2 building blocks into continuous porous nanofibers that simultaneously support light harvesting, reactant diffusion, active-site exposure, and directional charge transport.

Despite substantial progress in MoS2-based photocatalysts and photoelectrodes, simultaneously regulating light harvesting, charge separation, interfacial transport, and reactant accessibility within a single architecture remains challenging. In particular, the coupled roles of fiber-scale morphology, nanosheet organization, accessible porosity, and metallic 1T/semiconducting 2H phase coexistence have not been systematically clarified. Most reported MoS2 systems have been investigated separately for photocatalytic pollutant removal or photoelectrochemical applications, leaving the relationship between structural engineering, charge-transfer dynamics, and dual functional performance insufficiently understood. Developing a continuous porous architecture that preserves active MoS2 sites while providing efficient carrier-transport pathways and electrolyte-accessible interfaces therefore represents an important strategy for overcoming these limitations.

Herein, we report a two-step engineering strategy combining hydrothermal synthesis and electrospinning-assisted fiber construction to fabricate porous mixed-phase 1T/2H MoS2 nanofibers. Hydrothermally synthesized MoS2 nanoflowers are incorporated into a cellulose-based fibrous precursor, followed by structural conversion to establish an interconnected porous MoS2 network. This design integrates the light-harvesting capability and abundant accessible sites of nanostructured MoS2 with continuous one-dimensional transport pathways and the enhanced electrical conductivity associated with 1T-rich domains. The resulting nanofibers exhibit a band gap of approximately 1.56 eV and achieve 89.6% RhB degradation within 120 min under simulated sunlight, with reactive-species trapping identifying h+ and ˙O2− as the dominant oxidative species. When directly integrated onto FTO, the porous nanofiber electrode displays reproducible photoresponse, retains approximately 94% of its initial photocurrent after eight light–dark cycles, and exhibits a light-induced decrease in charge-transfer resistance from 52.3 to 44.6 kΩ. Combined optical, electrochemical, impedance, and scavenger analyses establish a direct connection between mixed-phase electronic structure, porous fibrous architecture, interfacial charge transfer, and photocatalytic/photoelectrochemical behavior. These findings provide a structure–function framework for designing MoS2 architectures in which phase and morphology engineering cooperatively regulate photogenerated carrier utilization.

Experimental section

Chemicals and reagents

Ammonium heptamolybdate tetrahydrate ((NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O, 99%), thiourea (CH4N2S, 99%), hydroxylamine hydrochloride (NH2OH·HCl, >98.5%), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 35–37%), and sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥97%) were used as received. N,N-Dimethylformamide (DMF, ≥99.5%, analytical-reagent grade; Xilong Scientific; CAS no. 68-12-2) was used as the electrospinning solvent. Cellulose acetate (CA; acetyl group content, 39.5 wt%; hydroxyl content, 4.0 wt%; Mw ≈ 60 000 g mol−1; Macklin, Shanghai, China) served as the polymeric fiber-forming precursor. Nafion solution (5 wt%) was used as the ionomeric binder for photoelectrode fabrication. Absolute ethanol (≥99.8%, analytical-reagent grade) was obtained from Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Unless otherwise specified, all chemicals were of analytical grade and used without further purification. Ultrapure deionized water was used to prepare all aqueous solutions and throughout the experimental procedures.

Fabrication of MoS2/cellulose nanofibers

MoS2/cellulose nanofibers were fabricated by electrospinning followed by alkaline deacetylation, using a modified procedure adapted from previous reports on cellulose acetate (CA)-based nanofibers.31,32 Briefly, 2.6 g of CA was dissolved in an acetone/DMF mixture (1 : 1, v/v) under magnetic stirring at 60 °C for 2 h to obtain a homogeneous solution. Separately, 1.5 g of the hydrothermally synthesized MoS2 nanoflowers was dispersed in 10 mL of DMF by ultrasonication for 1 h. Subsequently, 3 mL of the MoS2 dispersion was added dropwise to the CA solution, and the resulting mixture was further stirred at 60 °C for 1 h to ensure uniform dispersion of MoS2 within the polymer solution.

The homogeneous MoS2/CA suspension was electrospun using a NanoSpinner24 Advanced system. The suspension was loaded into a 10 mL plastic syringe equipped with a stainless-steel needle and delivered at a constant flow rate of 0.75 mL h−1. An applied voltage of 12 kV and a tip-to-collector distance of 19 cm were used. Precleaned FTO substrates were mounted on the grounded rotating collector, enabling direct deposition of the MoS2/CA nanofibers onto the conductive surface. Electrospinning was conducted for 15 min, followed by vacuum drying for 2 h.

To convert CA into cellulose while preserving the fibrous framework, the as-spun MoS2/CA films were immersed in 0.5 M NaOH in ethanol for 12 h to induce alkaline deacetylation. The treated films were subsequently vacuum-dried for 2 h, yielding MoS2/cellulose nanofibers directly supported on FTO. These samples are hereafter denoted as MoS2/cellulose nanofibers and were used for subsequent characterization and performance evaluation (Fig. 1).

Schematic illustration of the electrospinning-assisted fabrication of porous MoS2 nanofibers directly on an FTO substrate.

Materials characterization

The surface morphology and fibrous architecture of the synthesized MoS2 samples were examined by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, JEOL JSM-7600F). Elemental composition and spatial elemental distribution were analyzed using an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) detector integrated with the FE-SEM system. The crystalline phase and structural characteristics were investigated by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker D8 Advance) using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54056 Å).

The optical absorption characteristics were investigated by UV-visible diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV-vis DRS, Jasco V-750) equipped with an integrating sphere. The optical band-gap energies were estimated from the diffuse reflectance data using the Kubelka–Munk transformation and Tauc analysis.

Photocatalytic degradation experiments and kinetic analysis

The photocatalytic performance of the porous MoS2 nanofibers was evaluated by monitoring the degradation of rhodamine B (RhB) under simulated solar irradiation. In a typical experiment, 20 mg of the photocatalyst was dispersed in 30 mL of an aqueous RhB solution with an initial concentration of 10 mg L−1 in a 250 mL quartz reactor. The suspension was ultrasonicated for 30 min to achieve uniform catalyst dispersion and subsequently stirred in the dark for 60 min to establish adsorption–desorption equilibrium between RhB molecules and the catalyst surface.

Following equilibration, the suspension was irradiated using a 350 W xenon arc lamp (AHD350, China) equipped with an AM 1.5 filter. The distance between the light source and the surface of the reaction suspension was fixed at 10 cm, and the incident light intensity at the sample position was maintained at approximately 100 mW cm−2 (1 sun). Continuous magnetic stirring was applied throughout the irradiation period to maintain a homogeneous catalyst suspension and facilitate effective contact between the photocatalyst and RhB molecules. At predetermined irradiation intervals, 3 mL aliquots were withdrawn and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 1 min to remove the suspended catalyst particles. The residual RhB concentration in the supernatant was determined using UV-vis absorption spectroscopy by monitoring the characteristic absorption maximum at approximately 554 nm. Isopropanol (IPA), ammonium oxalate (AO), and p-benzoquinone (BQ) were separately introduced into the reaction suspension immediately before irradiation as scavengers for hydroxyl radicals (˙OH), photogenerated holes (h+), and superoxide radicals (˙O2−), respectively, each at a concentration of 0.01 M. The residual RhB concentration was monitored as described above, and the degradation efficiency obtained after 120 min in the presence of each scavenger was compared with that measured in the absence of any scavenger.33–35

Photoelectrochemical measurements

Photoelectrochemical (PEC) measurements were performed using an Autolab PGSTAT302N potentiostat/galvanostat in a conventional three-electrode configuration. All experiments were conducted in a sealed reaction cell isolated from the ambient environment (Fig. S1, SI). The MoS2/cellulose nanofiber-based photoelectrode directly supported on a fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) substrate, a Pt mesh, and an Ag/AgCl electrode (3 M KCl) were used as the working, counter, and reference electrodes, respectively. A 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline solution (PBS, pH 7.0) was used as the electrolyte.

PEC measurements were carried out under simulated solar irradiation supplied by a xenon lamp at an incident intensity of 100 mW cm−2 (1 sun). Before each measurement, the electrolyte was purged with high-purity N2 for 1 h to minimize interference from dissolved oxygen. Transient photocurrent responses were recorded at a constant applied potential of 0.63 V versus Ag/AgCl under periodically interrupted illumination, with alternating light-on and light-off intervals of 20 s. These measurements were used to assess the separation, transport, and recombination behavior of photogenerated charge carriers in the MoS2 photoelectrodes.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed at an applied bias of 0.25 V versus Ag/AgCl using a sinusoidal perturbation amplitude of 10 mV over a frequency range of 100 kHz to 0.1 Hz. The resulting Nyquist plots were analyzed to evaluate the interfacial charge-transfer resistance and carrier-transport kinetics of the photoelectrodes. Unless otherwise specified, all measurements were conducted under identical experimental conditions to enable a reliable comparison among the investigated samples.

Results and discussion

Materials characterization

Fig. 2a and b shows the morphology and microstructure of the electrospun MoS2/cellulose nanofibers after alkaline treatment. The low-magnification FESEM image (Fig. 2a) reveals a continuous, randomly interconnected fibrous network without obvious bead formation or severe fiber collapse, indicating that the electrospun architecture is largely preserved during the post-treatment. At higher magnification (Fig. 2b), the individual fibers exhibit relatively smooth and uniform surfaces, with diameters predominantly in the range of approximately 50–120 nm. The as-spun MoS2/cellulose acetate (MoS2/CA) fibers were immersed in 0.05 M NaOH in 96% ethanol for 12 h to promote alkaline deacetylation of CA into regenerated cellulose. Alkaline hydrolysis using dilute NaOH/ethanol is an established route for removing acetyl groups from electrospun CA while retaining its fibrous framework.31,32 Importantly, preservation of the interconnected nanofibrous network provides a structurally continuous precursor for the subsequent thermal conversion step, during which removal of the cellulose phase can facilitate the development of the porous MoS2 nanofiber architecture.

FESEM images of the MoS2/cellulose nanofibers obtained after alkaline treatment of the electrospun MoS2/CA fibers in 0.5 M NaOH/96% ethanol for 12 h followed by vacuum drying: (a) low- and (b) high-magnification images.

Fig. 3a shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of the MoS2/cellulose nanofibers after electrospinning and alkaline deacetylation. The characteristic reflections observed at 2θ ≈ 13.9°, 33.4°, 39.9°, and 49.8° can be indexed to the (002), (101), (103), and (105) planes, respectively, of hexagonal 2H-MoS2, in good agreement with JCPDS no. 37-1492. The low-angle (002) reflection is characteristic of the layered S–Mo–S stacking along the c-axis. In addition, the broad diffraction contribution centered at approximately 22.4° suggests limited long-range order in the cellulose component. This feature is qualitatively consistent with the amorphous cellulose reference (ICDD PDF 00-060-1501) reported by Fawcett et al.;36 however, residual crystalline contributions cannot be excluded. The features near 12–13° and 34° are insufficient to unambiguously identify a cellulose polymorph, particularly given the proximity of MoS2 reflections. Overall, the pattern supports the presence of crystalline 2H-MoS2 within a cellulose-containing matrix after processing, without establishing a quantitative cellulose crystallinity.

Structural characterization of the MoS2/cellulose nanofibers: (a) X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern and (b) Raman spectrum.

The Raman spectrum of the MoS2/cellulose nanofibers further confirms the formation of the characteristic layered MoS2 structure (Fig. 3b). Two prominent bands centered at approximately 381 and 408 cm−1 are assigned to the E12g and A1g vibrational modes of MoS2, respectively, in good agreement with previously reported values for 2H-MoS2.37,38 The E12g mode originates from the in-plane vibration of Mo and S atoms, whereas the A1g mode is associated with the out-of-plane vibration of S atoms.39 The frequency separation between these two modes (Δω ≈ 27 cm−1) is relatively large, suggesting a multilayer/bulk-like MoS2 structure rather than isolated mono- or few-layer sheets.40,41 These results demonstrate that the characteristic MoS2 lattice vibrations are retained within the cellulose-based fibrous precursor.

Notably, two additional weak features at approximately 219 and 335 cm−1 can be associated with the J2 and J3-type Raman modes arising from lattice distortion of metallic 1T/1T′-MoS2, suggesting the presence of a minor 1T-derived component coexisting with the dominant 2H phase.42,43 Thus, the Raman results support a mixed-phase MoS2 structure dominated by the semiconducting 2H phase with a small contribution from the metallic 1T-derived phase, while retaining the characteristic MoS2 lattice within the cellulose-based fibrous precursor.

Fig. 4a compares the UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectra of cellulose, MoS2 nanoflowers, and MoS2 nanofibers. Cellulose exhibits a pronounced ultraviolet response that decreases toward the visible region, with no distinct absorption band at 450–700 nm. This behavior is qualitatively consistent with the cellulose membrane reported by Matsudo et al.44 In contrast, both MoS2 samples exhibit a broad visible-light response. The nanoflowers show the highest apparent absorbance, whereas the nanofibers display an intermediate response with a broad, relatively featureless profile over approximately 400–600 nm. The enhanced visible-light response of the nanofibers relative to cellulose is consistent with the optical contribution of MoS2.20

Photodegradation activity

The photocatalytic activity of pristine MoS2 nanofibers was evaluated by monitoring RhB degradation under simulated sunlight. The characteristic absorption band of RhB at 553 nm gradually decreased with irradiation time (Fig. 5a), corresponding to a removal efficiency of 89.60% after 120 min (Fig. 5b). The linear dependence of ln(C0/Ct) on irradiation time (R2 > 0.95; Fig. 5c) indicates good agreement with pseudo-first-order kinetics, yielding an apparent rate constant (k) of 1.27 × 10−2 min−1. These results demonstrate the effective photocatalytic removal of RhB by the MoS2 nanofibers under simulated sunlight.

As summarized in Table 1, the porous MoS2 nanofibers achieved 89.60% RhB degradation within 120 min under simulated sunlight, with an apparent pseudo-first-order rate constant of 1.27 × 10−2 min−1. This rate constant is approximately 1.9 times that reported for MoS2/rGO 6.52 × 10−3 min−1,49 while the degradation efficiency is also substantially higher than that of pristine MoS2 nanoflowers under concentrated sunlight (67.4% after 120 min).50 Although higher kapp values have been reported for MoS2 microspheres51,52 and TiO2/MoS2 heterostructures,53 the present material exhibits competitive RhB degradation without coupling MoS2 to an additional semiconductor or conductive carbon phase. Its favorable activity is attributed to the interconnected porous nanofibrous architecture and mixed 1T/2H phase composition, which collectively improve active-site accessibility and photogenerated-charge transport. Nevertheless, because photocatalytic kinetics depend strongly on the initial dye concentration, catalyst loading, irradiation spectrum and intensity, and reactor configuration, the data in Table 1 should be regarded as a contextual rather than strictly normalized comparison.

The photochemical stability and reusability of the pristine MoS2 nanofibers were evaluated over three consecutive RhB degradation cycles (Fig. 6 and S2, SI). After each cycle, the photocatalyst was recovered by centrifugation, thoroughly washed with deionized water and ethanol, dried at 70 °C, and reused without further treatment. The RhB degradation efficiency decreased only slightly from 89.60% in the first cycle to 86.42% after the third cycle, corresponding to retention of approximately 96.5% of the initial activity. This modest decrease may primarily arise from unavoidable catalyst loss during recovery and washing, together with partial surface accumulation of reaction intermediates. These results demonstrate the good photochemical stability and recyclability of the pristine MoS2 nanofibers, supporting their potential as a sustainable photocatalyst for solar-driven wastewater treatment.

Recycling performance of pristine MoS2 nanofibers during three consecutive cycles of RhB photodegradation under simulated sunlight irradiation.

The slight decrease in RhB degradation efficiency during recycling can be attributed to two distinct effects. Incomplete recovery during centrifugation, washing, and transfer progressively reduces the amount of catalyst available for subsequent cycles. Independently, residual RhB and partially oxidized intermediates may remain adsorbed within the porous nanofiber network, partially covering reactive sites and restricting photon absorption and mass transport. Similar surface-fouling effects have been reported for reused photocatalysts, for which washing can restore activity by removing adsorbed species.20 Nevertheless, because neither the recovered catalyst mass nor the activity after controlled regeneration was independently measured, the individual contributions of catalyst loss and surface coverage cannot be quantitatively resolved. The relatively small overall decline therefore indicates reasonable recyclability, but should not be interpreted as evidence of complete structural and chemical stability.

The effect of initial solution pH on the photocatalytic degradation of RhB over MoS2 nanofibers was evaluated over the pH range of 3–11. The time-dependent UV-vis absorption spectra are presented in Fig. S3a–e (SI), while the corresponding C/C0 profiles and degradation efficiencies after 120 min are summarized in Fig. 7a and b. The degradation efficiency increased slightly from 86.11% at pH 3 to 88.10% at pH 5 and reached a maximum of 89.60% at pH 7. Further increasing the pH to 9 and 11 reduced the efficiency to 78.80 and 74.70%, respectively, indicating that near-neutral conditions were most favorable for RhB degradation.

Effect of initial solution pH on the photocatalytic degradation of RhB over MoS2 nanofibers under simulated sunlight: (a) temporal evolution of the relative RhB concentration (C/C0) and (b) RhB degradation efficiencies after 120 min of irradiation at pH 3, 5, 7, 9, and 11.

The highest degradation efficiency observed at pH 7 suggests that near-neutral conditions provide a favorable balance among RhB adsorption, photogenerated charge transfer, and surface oxidation reactions over MoS2. In contrast, the decrease in activity at pH 9–11 may be associated with changes in RhB ionization and aggregation, competition between OH− and RhB for surface adsorption sites, and altered kinetics of reactive oxygen species generation and consumption.54,55 Overall, the MoS2 nanofibers exhibited optimal activity under mildly acidic to near-neutral conditions while maintaining an RhB degradation efficiency above 74% across the pH range of 3–11, demonstrating their relatively robust performance under varying aqueous pH conditions.

Photoelectrochemical performance

Photoelectrochemical measurements were performed using the two-step-engineered porous MoS2 nanofiber film electrospun directly onto FTO, without a binder or conductive additive. Measurements were conducted in a three-electrode configuration using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.0) under simulated AM 1.5G irradiation (100 mW cm−2, 1 sun). All potentials were converted from Ag/AgCl to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) scale according to ERHE = EAg/AgCl + 0.0591 pH + E°, corresponding to an offset of +0.610 V at pH 7.0. The neutral electrolyte was selected to provide consistency with the RhB photodegradation experiments while minimizing the corrosive conditions associated with strongly acidic or alkaline media.

As shown in Fig. 8a, the electrode exhibited rapid and reproducible photocurrent switching during eight consecutive light–dark cycles. The current density alternated between approximately 5 and 17 µA cm−2, giving a light-to-dark current ratio of approximately 3.4. After eight cycles, the electrode retained approximately 94% of its initial light-induced current amplitude, demonstrating good short-term photoresponse stability. The sharp current transitions and stable illuminated plateaus indicate efficient photocarrier extraction through the interconnected nanofiber network. Such electrospun architectures combine high porosity and interfacial area with continuous one-dimensional transport pathways and direct contact with the current collector, thereby reducing the electrical discontinuities commonly associated with particulate electrodes.56 A weak anodic overshoot appeared immediately after illumination, followed by rapid establishment of a steady-state current. Because photocurrent transients reflect the dynamic competition among surface-charge accumulation, interfacial transfer, and recombination, their amplitude cannot by itself provide a quantitative measure of surface recombination.57 Nevertheless, the small transient and stable plateau indicate that pronounced charge accumulation did not occur under the applied conditions.

Photoelectrochemical characteristics of the two-step-engineered porous MoS2 nanofiber film deposited directly on FTO: (a) chopped-light chronoamperometric response over eight light–dark cycles; (b) linear sweep voltammograms recorded in the dark and under AM 1.5G illumination (100 mW cm−2); and (c) Nyquist plots measured under dark and illuminated conditions, together with the Rs(Rct‖CPE) equivalent circuits and fitted parameters.

The linear sweep voltammograms in Fig. 8b further demonstrate the light-induced electrochemical response of the porous MoS2 electrode. The illuminated and dark current densities begin to diverge at approximately 0.8 V vs. RHE. The differential photocurrent density, defined as ΔJ = Jlight − Jdark, reaches approximately 4.7 µA cm−2 at 1.23 V vs. RHE and increases to a maximum of approximately 7 µA cm−2 near 1.7 V vs. RHE. The use of ΔJ separates the light-dependent response from the potential-dependent dark background and therefore provides a conservative representation of the net photoresponse. Above approximately 1.8 V vs. RHE, the dark and illuminated curves progressively converge because of the rapidly increasing dark oxidation current. Accordingly, the current measured in this high-potential region should not be attributed exclusively to photoinduced interfacial reactions. More generally, photocurrent onset and magnitude are governed jointly by charge separation, surface-state occupation, and interfacial reaction kinetics rather than by light absorption alone.58

The Nyquist plots recorded under dark and illuminated conditions were fitted using an Rs(Rct‖CPE) equivalent circuit (Fig. 8c). The CPE exponents were approximately 0.91 under both conditions, indicating near-capacitive interfacial behavior, with the remaining deviation from unity attributable to electrode heterogeneity, surface roughness, porosity, and a distribution of relaxation times. The series resistance remained essentially unchanged upon illumination (77.4 Ω in the dark and 77.0 Ω under illumination), confirming that the ohmic contribution of the electrolyte–substrate–electrode configuration was unaffected by light exposure. This result also indicates stable electrical contact between the directly deposited MoS2 nanofiber film and the FTO substrate.

Illumination decreased Rct from 52.3 to 44.6 kΩ, corresponding to a reduction of approximately 14.7%, while the effective interfacial relaxation time decreased from 1.38 to 1.14 s, or approximately 17.4%. Because a CPE does not represent a true capacitance when n < 1, the effective capacitance must be derived from the fitted CPE parameters using an appropriate conversion relationship before calculating τ = RctCeff.59 The simultaneous decreases in Rct and τ indicate faster relaxation and interfacial transfer of photogenerated charge carriers, consistent with the stable chopped-light response. These observations are also qualitatively compatible with the enhanced RhB photodegradation kinetics because both processes depend on photocarrier generation, separation, transport, and interfacial consumption. However, the PEC and photocatalytic results should not be interpreted as directly equivalent because the two measurements involve different solution compositions, electrochemical driving forces, and surface-reaction pathways.

The Mott–Schottky plot of the porous MoS2 nanofiber electrode exhibits a positive slope between +0.2 and +1.0 V vs. RHE (R2 = 0.957), confirming its predominantly n-type character (Fig. 9a). Linear extrapolation yields an apparent flat-band potential of approximately −0.06 V vs. RHE, or −0.09 V vs. RHE after applying the kBT/e correction. The non-linear response below +0.2 V may arise from surface-state charging and additional interfacial capacitances, which commonly complicate flat-band determination for porous and nanostructured semiconductor electrodes.60 Therefore, Efb should be regarded as an approximate energetic descriptor rather than being assigned directly to the conduction-band edge.

Electrochemical characterization of the two-step-engineered porous MoS2 nanofiber electrode: (a) Mott–Schottky plot and linear fit used to estimate the flat-band potential and apparent donor density; (b) cyclic voltammograms recorded within the selected non-faradaic potential window at scan rates of 20–100 mV s−1; and (c) linear dependence of ΔJ/2 on scan rate used to determine the double-layer capacitance, Cdl.

Using the conventional Mott–Schottky relationship, a relative permittivity of 12.8 and a geometric electrode area of 1.5 cm2, the fitted slope gives an apparent donor density of 4.8 × 1020 cm−3. This high value is qualitatively compatible with defect-modulated electronic behavior in MoS2, as sulfur vacancies can substantially alter its Fermi-level position and doping characteristics.61 Nevertheless, the extracted value should not be interpreted as an absolute bulk carrier concentration because the classical model assumes a planar, uniformly doped semiconductor, whereas the present porous electrode contains a large and geometrically non-uniform interface. Contributions from surface states, Helmholtz capacitance, porosity and the difference between geometric and electrochemically active areas may therefore affect the fitted slope.

Fig. 9b and c show cyclic voltammograms recorded within a selected non-faradaic potential window at scan rates of 20–100 mV s−1. The approximately rectangular profiles and linear dependence of ΔJ/2 on scan rate (R2 = 0.999) indicate reproducible interfacial charging and yield a double-layer capacitance (Cdl) of 10.2 µF cm−2.62 This measurable capacitance confirms that a substantial fraction of the porous nanofiber surface is accessible to the electrolyte. However, quantitative conversion of Cdl into electrochemically active surface area requires an independently validated surface-specific capacitance for a chemically comparable MoS2 surface under identical electrolyte and potential conditions.63 Accordingly, the Mott–Schottky and capacitance results collectively support n-type electronic behavior and an electrolyte-accessible porous interface, while the extracted Efb, ND, and surface area remain model-dependent quantities.

Photocatalytic mechanism of RhB degradation over porous 1T/2H MoS2 nanofibers

The photocatalytic mechanism of RhB degradation over the porous MoS2 nanofibers was elucidated by correlating the band structure, mixed 1T/2H phase composition, photoelectrochemical response, and reactive-species trapping results. The optical band gap derived from the Tauc plot is approximately 1.82 eV, while Mott–Schottky analysis gives a flat-band potential of approximately −0.06 V vs. RHE. For an n-type semiconductor, the flat-band potential can be used as an approximation of the conduction-band (CB) edge, yielding ECB ≈ −0.06 V and EVB ≈ +1.76 V vs. RHE at pH 7 (approximately −0.47 and +1.35 V vs. NHE, respectively). This band configuration is favorable for the generation of superoxide radicals because the CB potential is more negative than the O2/˙O2− redox potential (−0.33 V vs. NHE). In contrast, the VB position is insufficiently positive for direct oxidation of H2O/OH− to ˙OH, suggesting that hydroxyl radicals, if formed, originate predominantly from secondary oxygen-reduction reactions.33

This energetic picture is consistent with the reactive-species trapping experiments (Fig. 10). After 120 min of simulated solar irradiation, the RhB degradation efficiency decreased from 89.60% without scavengers to 80.97%, 37.71%, and 35.43% in the presence of IPA, AO, and BQ, respectively. The pronounced suppression caused by AO and BQ indicates that photogenerated holes (h+) and superoxide radicals (˙O2−) are the principal species involved in RhB degradation, whereas the comparatively weak effect of IPA suggests a limited contribution from ˙OH. Although scavenger experiments do not provide a quantitative measure of the contribution of individual reactive species, the observed inhibition trend strongly supports the dominant involvement of h+ and ˙O2−.34

Reactive-species trapping experiments for RhB degradation over the porous 1T/2H MoS2 nanofibers under simulated solar irradiation: (a) UV-vis absorption spectra of the RhB solution before irradiation (RhB; 0 min) and after 120 min of irradiation without scavenger and in the presence of IPA, AO or BQ; (b) corresponding RhB degradation efficiencies after 120 min.

The efficient utilization of photogenerated carriers can be associated with the coexistence of semiconducting 2H and metallic 1T domains within the porous nanofibers. Upon illumination, the 2H-MoS2 domains generate electron–hole pairs, while the conductive 1T-rich regions can facilitate electron extraction and transport, thereby suppressing electron–hole recombination. Electrons reaching surface-accessible sites reduce dissolved O2 to ˙O2−, whereas holes remaining in the 2H domains participate directly in the oxidation of adsorbed RhB molecules. The porous fibrous architecture further provides accessible surface sites and short carrier-transport distances while facilitating the diffusion of RhB and dissolved O2 through the interconnected structure. This interpretation is supported by the reproducible transient photocurrent response and the decrease in charge-transfer resistance from 52.3 kΩ in the dark to 44.6 kΩ under illumination, accompanied by a reduction in the interfacial relaxation time from 1.38 to 1.14 s.

Accordingly, the main photocatalytic pathways can be expressed as:35,6412H-MoS2 + hν → e− + h+2e2H− → e1T−3O2 + e− → ˙O2−4RhB + h+ → oxidation products5RhB + ˙O2− → degradation intermediatesfollowed by further oxidation of the intermediates.

Thus, the enhanced photocatalytic performance originates from the combined effects of the mixed 1T/2H electronic structure and the porous nanofibrous morphology. The former promotes charge separation and electron transport, whereas the latter increases the accessible reaction interface and shortens carrier- and mass-transport pathways. The resulting ˙O2−- and h+-dominated pathway is consistent with both the band-edge energetics and the scavenger experiments (Fig. 10).

Photoelectrochemical charge-transfer behavior of porous MoS2 nanofiber photoanodes

The photoelectrochemical behavior of the porous MoS2 nanofiber electrode provides further evidence for the beneficial effect of the two-step-engineered architecture on photogenerated carrier separation and interfacial charge transfer. The positive slope of the Mott–Schottky plot confirms the n-type character of the MoS2 nanofibers, with a flat-band potential of approximately −0.06 V vs. RHE. Under illumination and anodic polarization, photogenerated holes migrate toward the semiconductor/electrolyte interface, whereas electrons are transported through the nanofiber network toward the FTO substrate (Fig. 11).

Proposed charge-transfer and reactive-species pathways for photocatalytic RhB degradation over the porous 1T/2H MoS2 nanofibers under simulated solar irradiation.

The coexistence of metallic 1T and semiconducting 2H domains is particularly favorable for this process. The 2H domains act as the primary photoactive component, whereas the more conductive 1T-rich regions provide additional pathways for electron transport and may reduce carrier recombination. At the morphological level, the porous nanofiber network provides a large semiconductor/electrolyte contact area and short diffusion distances for minority carriers. Direct integration of the nanofibers with the conductive substrate also favors charge collection without requiring polymeric binders or additional conductive additives.20,65

These structural advantages are consistent with the electrochemical impedance and transient photocurrent measurements. Upon illumination, the charge-transfer resistance decreases from 52.3 to 44.6 kΩ and the corresponding relaxation time decreases from 1.38 to 1.14 s, demonstrating accelerated interfacial charge-transfer kinetics. The rapid and reproducible photocurrent switching further indicates effective photoresponse and reasonable electrical connectivity within the nanofiber electrode. Approximately 94% of the initial photocurrent is retained after eight light on/off cycles, suggesting good short-term photoresponse stability.

Nevertheless, the photocurrent remains in the microampere range, reaching approximately 4.7 µA cm−2 at 1.23 V vs. RHE and approximately 7 µA cm−2 near 1.7 V vs. RHE. Moreover, a detectable anodic photocurrent appears only at approximately 0.8 V vs. RHE. These observations indicate that, despite favorable light absorption and carrier transport through the porous 1T/2H network, interfacial hole transfer and surface recombination remain important kinetic limitations. The relatively high illuminated Rct further supports this interpretation.

The proposed charge-transfer process can therefore be summarized as:66,6762H-MoS2 + hν → e− + h+7e2H− → e1T− → FTO8hbulk+ → hsurface+where the extracted electrons contribute to the measured external photocurrent, while surface-reaching holes participate in anodic interfacial reactions.

The PEC results also provide insight into the charge-carrier-mediated degradation of RhB. Under simulated sunlight, semiconducting 2H-MoS2 generates electron–hole pairs, while the conductive 1T-rich domains facilitate electron extraction and suppress their premature recombination. Electrons reaching the catalyst surface can be transferred to adsorbed O2 to form superoxide radicals (O2˙−), whereas photogenerated holes can directly oxidize adsorbed RhB or react with surface H2O/OH− to produce hydroxyl radicals (˙OH), provided that the corresponding band-edge potentials are thermodynamically sufficient. These charge carriers and reactive oxygen species subsequently promote chromophore cleavage, aromatic-ring opening, and progressive oxidation of RhB into smaller intermediates and, ultimately, mineralized products. Thus, the porous 1T/2H heterophase network enhances RhB degradation by combining efficient light-induced charge generation in 2H-MoS2 with rapid electron transport through the conductive 1T-rich regions.20,35,65

It should be emphasized that the anodic photocurrent alone does not constitute direct evidence of overall water splitting or oxygen evolution. For sulfide-based photoelectrodes, competitive surface oxidation or photocorrosion may also contribute to the measured current. Quantitative O2/H2 analysis, faradaic-efficiency measurements, and post-reaction surface characterization would therefore be required to establish water-splitting activity unambiguously. Within the scope of the present results, the PEC measurements demonstrate that the porous mixed-phase architecture improves photogenerated charge separation and interfacial charge-transfer behavior, complementing the enhanced photocatalytic RhB degradation performance.

Conclusions

In summary, porous mixed-phase MoS2 nanofibers were successfully developed through a two-step strategy integrating hydrothermal synthesis with electrospinning-assisted fiber construction. The resulting architecture combines semiconducting 2H-MoS2 with conductive 1T-rich domains within an interconnected porous fibrous network, providing accessible reaction interfaces and continuous pathways for photogenerated charge transport. Optical analysis revealed broad visible-light absorption with an estimated band gap of approximately 1.56 eV. The engineered MoS2 nanofibers exhibited efficient photocatalytic degradation of RhB, achieving 89.60% removal within 120 min under simulated sunlight with an apparent pseudo-first-order rate constant of 1.27 × 10−2 min−1. The catalyst retained approximately 96.5% of its initial activity after three consecutive cycles and maintained substantial activity over a broad pH range. Reactive-species trapping experiments identified h+ and ˙O2− as the dominant species, whereas ˙OH played a comparatively minor role. These observations are consistent with the experimentally estimated band energetics and support a charge–transfer pathway promoted by the coexistence of 1T and 2H domains. When directly integrated onto FTO, the porous MoS2 nanofiber electrode exhibited reproducible photoresponse and retained approximately 94% of its initial photocurrent after eight light–dark cycles. Illumination decreased the charge-transfer resistance from 52.3 to 44.6 kΩ and the interfacial relaxation time from 1.38 to 1.14 s, indicating accelerated interfacial carrier transfer. Mott–Schottky analysis further confirmed predominantly n-type behavior, while the measured double-layer capacitance supported an electrolyte-accessible porous interface. Collectively, these results demonstrate that phase engineering and porous nanofiber architecture act cooperatively to promote light utilization, carrier separation and transport, and interfacial reactions. This work therefore provides a practical structure–property–function framework for the rational design of MoS2-based nanofibrous materials for solar-driven photocatalytic and photoelectrochemical applications.
