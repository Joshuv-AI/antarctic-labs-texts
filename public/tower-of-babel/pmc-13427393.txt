Collisions on ski slopes pose a high risk of injury – It’s time to take action
Authors: Gerhard Ruedl, Martin Burtscher
Published: 2026

Collisions with another person on ski slopes pose a serious risk of injury. Snow-poor winters related to climate change may contribute to an increasing need for artificial snow on slopes, at least in the ski areas of the Alps. As a result, the number of available downhill slopes may be limited, which causes higher traffic on available ski terrain. In addition, artificial snow is often hard and icy, promoting high downhill speeds and is associated with long braking distances. It is therefore not surprising that the number of reported collisions involving skiers and/or snowboarders is increasing.1 The high kinetic energy of the fast-moving skier or snowboarder is what explains the serious consequences of such collisions. This development is of particular concern to families with young children and older people, who may increasingly avoid the ski slopes and the health-promoting exercise they offer in the fresh air. Therefore, it is imperative that measures are taken to reduce the risk of collision.

The risk

Around 10–15 years ago, collisions with another person on ski slopes in Alpine regions accounted for around 10% of all skiing and snowboarding injuries.2 A 13-year analysis (2005–2018) of the Austrian national registry revealed a total of 30,503 collisions involving 52,430 skiers (85.9%) and 8576 snowboarders (14.1%).3 Notably, according to the evaluation office for ski accidents of the German Ski Association, the risk of a collision injury may have increased to almost 20% in recent years due to the changing conditions referred to above.1 This increase in collision accidents is also evident in other Alpine countries such as Italy, Switzerland, and France, where the urgent need for preventive measures has been emphasized.4

Who is the causer and who is the victim?

Behavior on the ski slope is regulated by the International Ski and Snowboard Federation (FIS) Code of Conduct for Skiers and Snowboarders. According to FIS Rule 2, skiers and snowboarders must adjust their speed and skiing technique according to their ability and the terrain. This requirement is particularly important when jumping over terrain edges. Rules 3 and 4 of the FIS state that, when overtaking, the faster skier/snowboarder must leave enough space to avoid endangering the slower skier in front. In particular, young skiers and snowboarders exhibit risky behavior on the slopes by skiing too close to each other, thereby increasing the risk of collision.5 Skiers and snowboarders traveling at high speed often collide with slower-moving skiers and seated people (e.g., after a fall).3 Children and older skiers are particularly at risk of becoming victims. As media reports show, those causing the collision often flee the scene. Géraut describes those skiers as individuals with inadequate skiing technique and a lack of knowledge of FIS rules.4 It may be their inexperience, underestimating the risk related to a crash with high speed and overconfidence that cause them to act in a reckless manner.4 It was found that beginners, young skiers and non-local residents often lacked the necessary knowledge of FIS rules,6 making them a primary target group for preventive measures.

Mechanisms

The kinetic energy of an object/subject is directly proportional to the square of its speed. This means that the kinetic energy of a skier traveling at 10 km/h increases 25-fold when skiing downhill at 50 km/h, which is close to the average speed of skiers measured on ski slopes.7 In the case of a collision, skiers/snowboarders transfer kinetic energy to each other, which causes injury depending on the amount of kinetic energy and the body’s ability to resist. Consequently, the risk of injury increases sharply with skiing speed, and vulnerable persons such as children, women or older people are at greater risk of severe injury.

Resulting injuries

Injuries resulting from a collision with another skier or snowboarder are usually more severe than those resulting from a fall without third-party involvement.8 It is the victims who are injured more often than those who caused the collision (93% vs. 25%).9 Notably, every second child involved in a collision suffered a head injury.9 These findings have recently been confirmed, with reports of severe, multi-site injuries, especially among children.10 Also fatal accidents are most commonly caused by collisions with objects or other skiers/snowboarders.11 Older skiers are another group that is vulnerable. When geriatric individuals are injured in a skiing accident, unique injury patterns are observed, i.e., higher acuity, increased rates of facility care at discharge, and higher mortality compared with non-geriatric skiers.12

Prevention

To reduce the number of accidents caused by collisions, a combination of organizational measures by ski resort operators and broad educational initiatives must be implemented. Géraut suggested that ski resort managers must take the first step of raising awareness of these accidents, considering the progress in the regulation of slopes in terms of safety, as well as the training and information of skiers in regard to their responsibilities.4 We agree, but would also like to increase accountability by involving parents, school teachers, ski instructors, and role models, such as famous skiers and snowboarders, in this process. For example, the use of instructional videos on behavior and injuries on ski slopes could offer helpful support. The effectiveness of such video information on preventing ski injuries was demonstrated in a randomized prospective intervention study over 25 years ago.13 Compared to those who did not watch the video, there was a 50% decrease in collision frequency. Such instructional information can easily be made available online, on television and in schools, as well as in various locations within ski resorts. These measures may be particularly effective if endorsed, or even presented, by renowned ski stars. Although educational strategies for preventing collisions are preferable, additional measures taken by ski resort operators besides installing FIS rule panels and warning signs at particularly dangerous locations may be helpful. However, research has shown that e.g., “slow” warning signs and even the presence of ski patrollers have little effect on reducing skiing speed.14 Therefore, it may be worth considering whether particularly sensitive areas, such as bottlenecks and intersections, should be subject to video surveillance (in compliance with modern data protection regulations) to support the allocation of blame if necessary. The mere knowledge of such surveillance could contribute to more considerate behavior and also give families with children and elderly people more security. Any aids that help to prevent collisions contribute to both the safety and enjoyment of skiing.
