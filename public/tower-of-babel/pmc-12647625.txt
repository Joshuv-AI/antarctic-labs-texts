Chemometric mapping reveals chemical, phenolic, and antioxidant profiles in Tanacetum polycephalum essential oils from Southwest Iran
Authors: Elham Vahedi, Keramatollah Saeidi, Zahra Lorigooini, Mahmoud Kiani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemometric analysis of essential oils (EOs) and antioxidant capacity across ten Tanacetum polycephalum ecotypes revealed significant intraspecific phytochemical diversity and distinct chemotypes in this medicinal asteraceous species. EOs were examined for their chemical composition using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry. Significant differences were observed in EO yield (0.5–2.3%), total phenol content (3.2–8.7 mg GAE/g DW), and antioxidant activity (DPPH IC₅₀: 2.69–4.13 µg/ml; FRAP: 321.741–555.142 µM Fe²⁺/g) across ecotypes. β-thujone emerged as the predominant compound in most ecotypes (57.3–90.95%), while other major constituents included 1,8-cineole, borneol, terpinen-4-ol, camphor, carvotanacetone, and chrysanthenone. Principal component analysis revealed four distinct chemotypes: β-thujone-dominant, borneol/1,8-cineole, terpinen-4-ol/1,8-cineole, and intermediate β-thujone chemotypes, with clear correlations to elevational gradients. Hierarchical clustering confirmed the chemotype classification, supporting the taxonomic significance of phytochemical markers in population studies. The observed chemical diversity reflects adaptive responses to environmental pressures and genetic differentiation among populations. These findings demonstrate that T. polycephalum represents a chemically diverse species complex with potential for developing specialized cultivars for pharmaceutical, industrial, and agricultural applications based on specific chemotype selection.Keywords: β-thujone, Chemotype, Ecotype, Essential oils, Tanacetum polycephalumSubject terms: Plant ecology, Secondary metabolism

Introduction

The unsustainable overexploitation of wild medicinal plant populations has necessitated the exploration of alternative solutions. The cultivation of these wild taxa in agricultural systems through breeding and domestication practices is a widely accepted approach to mitigate the rapid depletion of natural resources while ensuring a reliable supply of medicinally active plants. Additionally, it could provide economic and social benefits to local communities in a sustainable manner1. Effective breeding practices are instrumental for the successful cultivation of medicinal plants. However, compared to conventional crops, medicinal plant breeding is a relatively young field with immense potential due to the high levels of natural variability present in wild populations2. To capitalize on this potential and cultivate improved varieties, a thorough understanding of the genetic diversity within wild populations is essential. This knowledge will be instrumental in breeding new cultivars with enhanced traits, ultimately contributing to a more diverse and robust cultivated gene pool3,4.

The Asteraceae (Compositae) family is one of the largest and most cosmopolitan groups of flowering plants, with significant economic importance due to its diverse food crops, ornamental species, and medicinally valuable compounds5–7. Within this family, the genus Tanacetum L. represents a taxonomically diverse group of flowering plants, primarily distributed across the Northern Hemisphere, including extensive populations in southwest Iran. The genus exhibits considerable morphological and ecological variation, ranging from herbaceous perennials to, less frequently, shrub-like forms. Among these, T. polycephalum (L.) Schultz-Bip., a perennial herb native to the temperate regions of western Asia, is particularly notable, with a distribution spanning Turkey, Iran, and neighboring countries8. Within Iran, T. polycephalum is predominantly found in the western and southwestern mountainous regions.

T. polycephalum has a well-documented history of traditional medicinal use in Persian and regional ethnobotanical practices, particularly in southwest Iran, for treating a variety of ailments, including headaches, migraines, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes9. Recent investigations have corroborated its antimicrobial10,11, insecticidal12, and cytotoxic effects against cancer cell lines9. These diverse bioactivities suggest T. polycephalum possesses a rich array of secondary metabolites, contributing to its pharmacological potential. Tanacetum species are characterized by a complex phytochemical profile, including essential oils (EOs), sesquiterpene lactones, flavonoids, and phenolic acids. The EOs are particularly prominent, predominantly composed of oxygenated monoterpenes such as camphor, 1,8-cineole, thujones, and borneol. However, notable variability is observed, resulting in distinct chemotypes and species-specific compositions enriched with compounds such as carvone (in T. balsamita L.), artemisia alcohol (in T. hololeucum (Bornm.) Podlech), or (E)-sesquilavandulol (in T. abrotanifolium (L.) Druce)13,14.

Among the species of this genus, T. polycephalum serves as a specific case study, exhibiting EOs similarly enriched with oxygenated monoterpenes. Research across its subspecies has identified recurring constituents such as camphor, 1,8-cineole, borneol, bornyl acetate, α-pinene, and camphene. Nonetheless, considerable phytochemical variation is evident within the species; for example, subsp. argyrophyllum (Boiss.) Podlech is characterized by elevated levels of pinocarvone, subsp. farsicum Podlech by cis- and trans-chrysanthenyl acetate and chrysanthenol isomers, and subsp. duderanum (Boiss.) Podlech by artemisia alcohol and yomogi alcohol. In addition to volatile constituents, extracts of T. polycephalum have been found to contain bioactive sesquiterpene lactones, such as 8β-hydroxy-4β,15-dihydrozaluzanin C.

Despite scattered reports on the chemical composition of T. polycephalum across northern Iran, the phytochemical landscape of populations in the Zagros Mountains remains largely unexplored15. This region encompasses diverse ecological gradients that are known to influence terpene biosynthesis and may underlie the emergence of unique chemotypes. The limited coverage of Zagros populations represents a significant gap in current knowledge. The central hypothesis of this work is that substantial intraspecific phytochemical variation exists among T. polycephalum populations across the Zagros Mountains of southwest Iran, and that this variation is sufficient to delineate discrete chemotypes with potential biological relevance. This hypothesis is tested through chemometric analysis of EO composition, designed to uncover underlying patterns that correspond to these putative chemotypes. Accordingly, the specific aims of this study were (i) to characterize the chemical composition, and evaluate the antioxidant capacity of T. polycephalum EOs from ecotypes across the Zagros region, and (ii) to apply chemometric approaches to delineate distinct chemotypes and assess how ecological variation shapes intraspecific chemodiversity. This integrated framework provides a comprehensive basis for understanding intraspecific variation in T. polycephalum, with direct implications for conservation, selective breeding, and industrial utilization.

Materials and methods

This study investigated T. polycephalum in southwestern Iran, focusing on Kohgiluyeh & Boyer Ahmad and Chaharmahal & Bakhtiari provinces, in 2022. Ten sampling locations were strategically selected within these provinces, guided by distribution data from Flora Iranica and previous reports on T. polycephalum prevalence in southwestern Iran. Geographic coordinates for each sampling site were recorded using a Garmin 78 s GPS device (Table 1). Herbarium vouchers are kept at Research Center of Agriculture and Natural Resources, Shahrekord, Iran under the voucher 1934–11357-1408-374-1655-1665. Samples identification was made by two expert botanists (Shirmardi, Hamzeh Ali PhD., Research Center of Agriculture and Natural Resources, P.O.Box 415, Shahrekord, Iran and Shahrokhi, Asghar; Education Organization, Chaharmahal and Bakhtiari Province, Shahrekord, Iran).

EO isolation

The entire aerial parts of T. polycephalum, collected at the full flowering stage, were shade-dried and pulverized. A 100 g sample of the ground material was subjected to hydro-distillation for 3 h using a Clevenger-type apparatus. The resulting EO was separated from the aqueous phase and dehydrated with anhydrous sodium sulfate. The oil was stored at 4 °C in sealed amber vials until analysis. The essential oil yield was determined gravimetrically and expressed as a weight-by-weight% (w/w).

EO profiling and quantification

Volatile constituents of T. polycephalum EO were separated and identified using gas chromatography (GC) and gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS). A Thermoquest gas chromatograph with flame ionization detector (FID) was employed, utilizing a DB-5 capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). Helium carrier gas flowed at 1.1 mL/min with a 100:1 split injection ratio. The oven temperature program initiated at 60 °C, ramped to 250 °C at 5 °C/min, and held at 250 °C for 10 min.

GC-MS analysis was performed using a Thermoquest-Finnigan mass spectrometer with an ion trap system, coupled to the GC unit. The same DB-5 column and helium carrier gas flow were used. The instrument operated in electron ionization (EI) mode (70 eV), with ionization source temperatures of 200 °C and 250 °C, scanning 40–460 m/z. The oven temperature program was the same as that used in the GC analysis.

Identification of individual EO compounds was accomplished through a multi-faceted approach. The mass spectral fragmentation patterns of unknown compounds were compared to those in the Wiley GC-MS library to identify characteristic ion peaks and molecular fragments, ensuring reliable matches with reference spectra. Where available, these patterns were further validated against authentic standards, confirming the presence of key ions and their relative intensities for compounds such as β-thujone, 1,8-cineole, and camphor. Additionally, mass spectra and retention indices (RIs) were cross-referenced with published literature to enhance identification accuracy. Linear retention indices (LRI) were calculated relative to a homologous series of n-alkanes (C8–C24) under identical chromatographic conditions and compared with literature values to corroborate compound identities. Relative percentages of analytes were determined from GC peak areas without the application of correction factors.

Chemometric analysis

For the chemometric analysis, the dataset containing relative percentages of identified EO compounds was used to investigate intraspecific diversity. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed using MVSP (MultiVariate Statistical Package) version 3.2. Prior to PCA, data was auto-scaled (mean-centered and scaled to unit variance) to prevent compounds with high concentrations from dominating the analysis. The resulting PCA scores plot visualized ecotype clustering, while loadings analysis identified compounds with the highest contributions to ecotype separation along each component. Model robustness was assessed using eigenvalues and the proportion of variance explained by each component. Hierarchical clustering was performed in MVSP v3.2 using Ward’s linkage method with squared Euclidean distance on the auto-scaled chemical composition dataset to evaluate ecotype relationships.

Total phenol content (TPC)

TPC of T. polycephalum EO was quantified using the Folin-Ciocalteu colorimetric assay, adapted for volatile matrices. A 10 µL aliquot of EO was dissolved in 1 mL of pure methanol (99.8%) to prepare a stock solution. Aliquots of 200 µL were mixed with 1 mL of 10% (v/v) Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and incubated at ambient temperature for 5 min to initiate the reaction. Subsequently, 800 µL of 7.5% (w/v) sodium carbonate solution was added, and the mixture was incubated in darkness at ambient temperature for 60 min to minimize volatile loss. A control solution, comprising 200 µL of 60% methanol, 1 mL of 10% Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, and 800 µL of 7.5% sodium carbonate, was prepared concurrently. Absorbance was measured at 760 nm using a spectrophotometer. A standard curve was established simultaneously using gallic acid solutions (10–100 µg/mL), processed identically. The TPC was expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents (mg GAE) per gram of EO 17.

Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant activity of T. polycephalum EO was quantified using the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) free radical scavenging assay18,19 and the ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay20. For DPPH assay, a 200 µL aliquot of 0.1 mM DPPH solution in methanol was combined with 20 µL of EO in a 96-well microplate. The mixture was incubated in darkness at 25 ± 2 °C for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a microplate reader (STAT FAX 2100, Awareness Technology, Inc.). A control solution, consisting 200 µL of 0.1 mM DPPH and 20 µL of methanol (99.8%), and a blank containing only methanol were prepared concurrently. The ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay was performed using a freshly prepared FRAP reagent. This reagent was formulated by mixing 300 mM sodium acetate trihydrate buffer (pH 3.6, prepared in glacial acetic acid), 10 mM 2,4,6-tripyridyl-s-triazine (TPTZ) in 40 mM hydrochloric acid, and 20 mM iron(III) chloride hexahydrate in distilled water in a volumetric ratio of 10:1:1 (v/v/v). For analysis, 3 mL of FRAP reagent was combined with 100 µL of EO solution (1 mg/mL in methanol) in a test tube and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 593 nm using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (LABINDIA UV 3000) against a blank consisting of 3 mL of FRAP reagent and 100 µL of methanol (99.8%). A standard curve was generated using ferrous sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO4·7H2O) solutions. The FRAP values were expressed as micromoles of Fe2+ equivalents per gram of EO (µM Fe2+/g EO).

Statistical analysis

All collected data, including EO yield, TPC, antioxidant activity (DPPH IC50 and FRAP), and chemical composition profiles, were analyzed using SPSS software (version 25.0, IBM Corp.). A completely randomized design with three replicates per ecotype was employed, and analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to determine significant differences among ecotypes (p < 0.05). Duncan’s multiple range test was used for post-hoc comparisons where significant differences were found. Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed to assess relationships among variables, with significance tested at p < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Results and discussion

EO profiling

Our findings revealed statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) among ecotypes for TPC, antioxidant activity, and EO yield (Table 1). The EO content of ten T. polycephalum ecotypes was analyzed (Table 2), revealing significant variation among the different populations. The content ranged from 0.7% to 1.29%, with TP10 (from Tamer-Kooh ecotype) exhibiting the highest content (1.29 ± 0.21%) and TP5 (from Farsan ecotype) the lowest (0.7 ± 0.02%). Statistical analysis indicated that TP10’s oil content was significantly higher than most other ecotypes, but not statistically different from TP6 (1.13 ± 0.14%), TP8 (1.18 ± 0.06%), and TP7 (0.98 ± 0.25%). Ecotypes TP5 (0.7 ± 0.02) and TP3 (0.77 ± 0.04%) demonstrated the lowest EO yields, significantly lower than the high-yielding ecotypes. The remaining ecotypes (TP1, TP2, TP4, and TP9) showed intermediate oil contents, ranging from 0.86% to 0.97%, and were not significantly different from each other. Notably, Rezaee et al. (2012)21 reported EO yields of 0.04 to 1% for T. polycephalum. This variability in EO content could have important implications for the selection of high-yielding ecotypes for potential commercial cultivation or phytochemical studies22.

A comprehensive analysis of the literature reveals a diverse array of constituents identified within T. polycephalum EO across various populations and subspecies. Common major components include camphor, 1,8-cineole, borneol, and bornyl acetate8,11,15,21,32–37. These are joined by other frequently identified compounds like camphene, α-pinene, p-cymene, chrysanthenone, pinocarvone, and thujones8,15,21,32–36. Notably, some studies report the presence of less common major constituents such as nootkatin, trans-sabinene hydrate, dihydro-eudesmol21, artemisia alcohol, yomogi alcohol34 and even 4,5-epoxy−1-isoprophyl-4-methyl-1 38. The recurring identification of certain compounds across multiple studies suggests a core chemical signature for the species, while the presence of unique constituents in specific populations indicates the potential for novel chemotypes with distinct bioactive properties39.

The analysis of the EO compositions from ten ecotypes of T. polycephalum (Table 4) reveals a diverse phytochemical profile with variations in the number of compounds identified, their relative abundances, and the contributions of different compound classes. Additionally, the presence of potential signature or chemo-marker compounds was observed across the ecotypes. Chemical characterization revealed the presence of 31 distinct compounds, among which β-thujone, 1,8-cineole, borneol, and camphor emerged as the main constituents.

Among the analyzed ecotypes, TP4 exhibited the greatest number of identified compounds (31), followed by TP1 and TP3 (30 each), TP9 and TP10 (29 each), TP5 (28), TP2 and TP8 (27 each), TP7 (23), and TP6 (22). The relative contributions of various compound classes also differed significantly between the ecotypes. Oxygenated monoterpenes dominated the EO profiles, ranging from 86.99% in TP2 to 96.55% in TP1. Monoterpene hydrocarbons constituted a smaller fraction, varying from 1.22% in TP7 to 11.74% in TP2. Sesquiterpene hydrocarbons and oxygenated sesquiterpenes were present in comparatively minor quantities, with the highest contributions observed in TP8 and TP6 (0.4%) and TP5 (4.27%), respectively. Such variations may reflect genetic diversity within the species, and/or adaptations to specific environmental conditions among ecotypes.

Certain compounds have emerged as potential chemo-markers or signature compounds for the T. polycephalum or specific chemotypes of the species. β-thujone emerges as the predominant compound, ranging from 1.1% (TP5) to 90.95% (TP7), with notably high concentrations (> 80%) in ecotypes TP1, TP3, TP4, TP7, TP8, and TP10. The consistent abundance of this bicyclic monoterpene ketone suggests its potential ecological significance and could be a defining characteristic of T. polycephalum oil.

1,8-cineole (eucalyptol), a ubiquitous cyclic monoterpene ether in aromatic plants, exhibits a consistent presence across all ten examined ecotypes of T. polycephalum. The compound’s concentration exhibits significant variability, ranging from 0.5% to 20% among the ecotypes. Specifically, TP9 shows the highest concentration of 1,8-cineole at 20%, closely followed by TP5 with 19.28%, and also TP6 at 5%. In contrast, TP7 contains the lowest reported concentration at 0.5%, followed by TP8 at 2.01%. Other ecotypes display intermediate concentrations: TP1 at 2.5%, TP2 at 3.5%, TP3 and TP10 at 2.4%, TP4 at 2.6%.

Borneol, a bicyclic monoterpene alcohol, displayed substantial variation in its concentration across the analyzed T. polycephalum ecotypes. This compound exhibited one of the widest ranges observed, with levels ranging from 0.3% to a remarkable 30.26%. Ecotype TP5 distinguished itself with the highest borneol concentration (30.26%), significantly exceeding all other ecotypes. TP2 and TP6 displayed the next highest concentrations at 6% and 4%, respectively. A cluster of ecotypes, including TP8 (3.42%), TP1 (3%), TP4 (2.90%), TP3 (1.80%), TP10 (1.72%), and TP7 (1.02%), exhibited moderate borneol levels. Notably, TP9 displayed the lowest concentration at only 0.30%.

The concentration of camphor, a cyclic monoterpene ketone, also displayed significant variation across the T. polycephalum ecotypes, with concentrations ranging from 0.35% to 9.25%. Ecotype TP9 exhibits the highest camphor content at 9.25%, significantly exceeding the levels observed in other ecotypes. TP2 and TP5 show the next highest concentrations at 4.73% and 4.7%, respectively, indicating a similar camphor profile in these two ecotypes. A cluster of ecotypes exhibited moderate concentrations: TP1 and TP4 (1.3%), TP8 (2.23%), and TP6 (2.00%). Ecotypes TP10, TP3, and TP7 displayed the lowest camphor levels at 0.83%, 0.72%, and 0.35%, respectively.

The chemical composition of T. polycephalum EOs is further characterized by the presence of numerous minor compounds across the ecotypes. These minor constituents, typically present at concentrations below 1%, collectively contributing to the complex phytochemical profile of the species, further highlighting the remarkable chemotypic diversity within the species. Among these are monoterpene hydrocarbons (e.g., α-pinene, camphene, sabinene, and β-pinene), oxygenated monoterpenes (e.g., cis-sabinene hydrate, linalool, trans-pinocarveol, terpinen-4-ol, α-terpineol, myrtenal, and myrtenol), sesquiterpenes (e.g., germacrene D), and oxygenated sesquiterpenes (e.g., spathulenol, caryophyllene oxide, γ-eudesmol, and β-eudesmol). Additionally, non-terpene compounds like methyl jasmonate are also detected in trace amounts. While individually present in small quantities, these minor constituents may play significant roles in the plant’s ecological interactions and potentially contribute to synergistic effects in the oil’s bioactivities40.

The analysis also revealed substantial chemodiversity, evident not only in the abundance of major constituents but also in the enrichment of specific minor compounds within certain ecotypes. This phenomenon is particularly evident in ecotypes TP5 and TP9, which demonstrate unique chemical profiles. TP5 is characterized by elevated levels of monoterpenes (α-pinene 1.21%, sabinene 1.06%), oxygenated monoterpenes (cis-sabinene hydrate 1.1%, cis-chrysanthenol 4.5%, myrtenal 2.28%, myrtenol 4.75%, cis-crysanthenyl acetate 4.36%, p-cymen-7-ol 9.93%), oxygenated sesquiterpenes (caryophyllene oxide 2.11%), and nonterpene compounds (methyl jasmonate 1.71%). TP9 similarly shows high concentrations of monoterpenes (α-pinene 5%, camphene 3%, β-pinene 1%) and oxygenated monoterpenes (chrysanthenone 2.5%, trans-pinocarveol 2.11%, cis-chrysanthenol 3%, terpinen-4-ol 34.5%, α-terpineol 2.1%, bornyl acetate 7%). Interestingly, other ecotypes also exhibit distinctive profiles: TP6 is notable for high levels of oxygenated monoterpenes (chrysanthenone 8.3%, cis-chrysanthenol 3%, carvotanacetone 12.4%). TP2 shows elevated concentrations of the monoterpene α-phellandrene (9%) and the oxygenated monoterpene myrtenol (2.77%). TP8 contains chrysanthenone at 1.06%, while TP7 has the sesquiterpene spathulenol at 1%.

The unique distribution patterns of minor compounds observed in this study are predominantly associated with ecotypes TP5 and TP9, which exhibit a notable deviation from the β-thujone dominance characteristic of other T. polycephalum ecotypes. This observation suggests a potential inverse relationship between β-thujone content and the enrichment of these minor compounds in TP5 and TP9. The principle of ecological stoichiometry offers a plausible framework for interpreting this association. This concept posits that limitations in a major resource (in this instance, β-thujone) may induce the upregulation of alternative biosynthetic pathways, culminating in the observed accumulation of distinct minor compounds41. Consistent with this hypothesis, TP5 and TP9 demonstrated the most distinctive chemotypic profiles, characterized by negligible β-thujone contributions and showcasing a wide array of minor compounds, reflecting the overall diversity observed.

The observed chemotypic diversity among T. polycephalum ecotypes presents significant potential for targeted breeding and cultivation programs. Such initiatives could aim to develop tailored EO profiles with specific compound concentrations, addressing various industrial, pharmaceutical, or agricultural applications40. The industrial utilization of T. polycephalum EOs relies on their diverse chemical composition. However, this composition is not a fixed characteristic, but rather a dynamic interplay between intrinsic and extrinsic factors40,42. Genetic makeup of plants play a significant role, as evidenced by distinct chemotypes identified in T. polycephalum subsp. duderanum34, subsp. farsicum39, subsp. argyrophyllum (K.Koch) Podlech33, and subsp. heterophyllum8. Supporting this notion, Pirbalouti analyzed the EOs of four Tanacetum species, including T. polycephalum, and revealed chemical diversity within the genus15. Further adding to this complexity, plant part and developmental stage also influence the EO profile. Najafi et al. 33 observed variations in T. polycephalum subsp. argyrophyllum depending on whether the aerial parts were collected before flowering or at full bloom. Notably, 1,8-cineole was solely detected in the oil from fully flowered plants. These findings highlight the influence of plant developmental stage on the composition of EOs. Similarly, Nori-Sharghi (1999) compared the EO composition of flowers and leaves from T. polycephalum subsp. heterophyllum8. Pirbalouti (2019) further highlighted the importance of plant part selection by using inflorescences for T. kotschyi (Boiss.) Grierson and T. persicum (Boiss.) Mozaff., while employing aerial parts for T. chiliophyllum (Fisch. & C.A.Mey. ex DC.) Sch.Bip. and T. polycephalum15. Geographic location, climatic condition, and ecological specifications emerges as another key determinant. Studies by Alamholo (2022), Mojarrad et al. (2019), Mahdavi et al. (2015), Rezazadeh et al. (2014), Rezaee et al. (2012), and Semnani (2011) report significant regional variations within Iran, with different locations favoring distinct dominant compounds like camphor, β-thujone, 1,8-cineole, or borneol. Even the extraction methodology can impact the final product11,21,32,35,37,38. Eblaghi et al. (2016) observed distinct variations in the concentration of bornyl acetate between traditional hydro-distillation and microwave-assisted hydro-distillation. This inherent diversity presents challenges for standardization but also opens opportunities for future research efforts focused on authentication, targeted breeding programs, and exploring applications in various industries36.

TPC and antioxidant properties

An analysis of TPC in EOs extracted from ten T. polycephalum ecotypes revealed significant variations among the populations (Table 2). The phenol content, expressed as gallic acid equivalent (GAE) in mg/g of EO, ranged from 28.86 to 56.14 mg GAE/g. Ecotype TP9 exhibited the highest phenol content (56.14 ± 1.66 mg GAE/g), which was significantly higher than all other ecotypes. This was followed closely by TP5 (54.93 ± 0.41 mg GAE/g) and TP1 (53.68 ± 0.02 mg GAE/g), both of which also showed significantly higher phenolic contents compared to the remaining ecotypes. In contrast, TP3 displayed the lowest phenol content (28.86 ± 0.08 mg GAE/g), significantly lower than all other ecotypes. The remaining ecotypes (TP2, TP4, TP6, TP7, TP8, and TP10) showed no statistically significant differences among themselves, with phenol contents ranging from 40.6 to 41.62 mg GAE/g.

The antioxidant capacity of T. polycephalum ecotypes was evaluated using complementary DPPH and FRAP assays (Table 2), revealing a spectrum of activities across the samples. In the DPPH assay, lower IC50 values indicate stronger antioxidant activity, as they represent the concentration of the EO required to scavenge 50% of the free radicals23. Conversely, higher FRAP values indicate a greater ability of the sample to reduce ferric iron (Fe3+) to ferrous iron (Fe2+), which is linked to antioxidant activity24.

The results of the DPPH radical scavenging activity, expressed as IC50 values in µg/ml, ranged from 2.69 to 4.13 µg/ml, indicating diverse antioxidant potentials among the ecotypes. Ecotype TP1 exhibited the most potent antioxidant activity with the lowest IC50 value (2.69 ± 0.22 µg/ml), followed closely by TP5 (2.80 ± 0.2 µg/ml) and TP9 (2.86 ± 0.5 µg/ml). These three ecotypes showed statistically similar antioxidant activities, significantly higher than all other ecotypes. This similarity suggests a potential commonality in their bioactive constituents. Conversely, TP3 displayed the weakest DPPH radical scavenging activity (IC50 = 4.13 ± 0.14 µg/ml), although not significantly different from TP10 (3.92 ± 0.37 µg/ml), TP7 (3.66 ± 0.05 µg/ml), and TP4 (3.62 ± 0.58 µg/ml). The remaining ecotypes (TP2, TP6, and TP8) demonstrated intermediate antioxidant activities, with IC50 values ranging from 3.35 to 3.57 µg/ml.

The FRAP values, expressed as µM Fe2+/g of EO, ranged from 321.741 to 555.142 µM Fe2+/g, indicating diverse antioxidant potentials among the ecotypes. Ecotype TP1 demonstrated the highest reducing power (555.142 µM Fe2+/g), closely followed by TP5 (549.875 µM Fe2+/g) and TP9 (548.156 µM Fe2+/g). These three ecotypes exhibited markedly higher FRAP values compared to the others, suggesting superior antioxidant capacities. In contrast, TP3 showed the lowest FRAP value (321.741 µM Fe2+/g), indicating the weakest reducing power among all tested ecotypes. The remaining ecotypes (TP2, TP4, TP6, TP7, TP8, and TP10) displayed intermediate FRAP values, ranging from 450.213 to 462.256 µM Fe2+/g, with relatively minor variations among them. This pattern of antioxidant activity broadly aligns with the results observed in DPPH radical scavenging, reinforcing the consistent variability in antioxidant potential among T. polycephalum ecotypes.

The results from both assays demonstrated a high degree of concordance, providing robust evidence for the diverse antioxidant potentials among the ecotypes. This consistent pattern across different antioxidant mechanisms (radical scavenging and reducing power) highlights the reliability of the observed variations in antioxidant potential among the T. polycephalum ecotypes and highlights the importance of considering multiple antioxidant assays when evaluating the potential health benefits of plant-derived compounds.

Correlation analysis (Table 3) revealed a strong negative correlation between total reducing capacity (measured as TPC) and DPPH IC50 (r = −0.938, p < 0.01) and a strong positive correlation with FRAP (r = 0.999, p < 0.01), highlighting the pivotal role of reducing compounds in antioxidant efficacy. The Folin-Ciocalteu assay responds to various electron-donating functionalities, including phenolic monoterpenoids like p-cymen-7-ol (0.05–9.93% across ecotypes), and hydroxylated terpenes (borneol, terpinen-4-ol). These compounds contribute to antioxidant activity through hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) or single electron transfer (SET) mechanisms, with phenolic components additionally forming resonance-stabilized phenoxyl radicals for enhanced reactive oxygen species (ROS) neutralization27.

Weak, non-significant correlations were observed between EO yield and TPC (r = −0.239, p = 0.506), DPPH IC50 (r = 0.292, p = 0.413), or FRAP (r = −0.240, p = 0.503), indicating decoupled biosynthetic pathways. EO production primarily occurs via the mevalonate (MVA) and methylerythritol phosphate (MEP) pathways in plastids and cytosol, yielding monoterpenes like β-thujone28, whereas phenolics derive from the shikimate and phenylpropanoid pathways, producing aromatic precursors like phenylalanine for flavonoids and phenolic acids29,30. These pathways respond independently to stimuli, explaining the variation; for instance, TP10’s high EO yield (1.29%) pairs with moderate phenolics (40.75 mg GAE/g), suggesting resource allocation trade-offs under environmental constraints31.

Elevation showed a moderate, non-significant positive correlation with EO yield (r = 0.509, p = 0.132), hinting at altitude’s influence on terpene accumulation, possibly via enhanced MEP pathway flux under parameters like increased UV or cooler temperatures (3). However, weak correlations with TPC (r = −0.113, p = 0.752) and IC50 (r = 0.095, p = 0.793) suggest minimal altitudinal impact on phenolic biosynthesis, which may be more responsive to other environmenta factors such as soil nutrients or precipitation not measured here.

Correlations were also computed between major EO constituents and antioxidant metrics (Table 3). 1,8-cineole exhibited a significant negative correlation with IC50 (r = −0.639, p = 0.047) and a positive trend with FRAP (r = 0.584, p = 0.076), indicating enhanced antioxidant efficacy in high-content ecotypes (e.g., TP5: 19.28%, IC50 2.80 µg/mL, FRAP 549.875 µM Fe2+/g; TP9: 20%, IC50 2.86 µg/mL, FRAP 548.156 µM Fe2+/g). Mechanistically, 1,8-cineole’s oxygen-containing ring structure facilitates metal coordination, potentially inhibiting Fenton reactions and hydroxyl radical formation, while activating Nrf2 pathways to upregulate antioxidant enzymes including glutathione peroxidase and superoxide dismutase for enhanced ROS detoxification43. This supports its role in membrane stabilization against oxidative stress in plant-pathogen interactions44. Conversely, β-thujone correlated positively with DPPH IC50 (r = 0.653, p = 0.040) and negatively with FRAP (r = −0.594, p = 0.070), suggesting reduced direct antioxidant capacity in dominant chemotypes (e.g., TP7: 90.95%, IC50 3.66 ± 0.05 µg/mL, FRAP 459.123 µM Fe2+/g). The compound’s high prevalence at elevated sites (r = 0.604, p = 0.064) likely results from e.g., increased UV intensity and cooler temperatures promoting thujone synthase activity. However, β-thujone’s α,β-unsaturated ketone structure limits HAT/SET efficiency compared to hydroxyl-containing compounds45,46. Borneol showed non-significant correlations with DPPH IC50 (r = −0.429, p = 0.216) and FRAP (r = 0.380, p = 0.278). Despite the lack of statistical significance, borneol’s hydroxyl group theoretically enables HAT mechanisms to quench peroxyl radicals, preventing lipid peroxidation. This potential is exemplified in TP5, which combines high borneol content (30.26%) with strong antioxidant activity (DPPH IC50 2.80 ± 0.2 µg/mL, FRAP 549.875 µM Fe²⁺/g), though other compounds in this ecotype may also contribute to the observed activity.

47. A strong negative correlation between 1,8-cineole and β-thujone (r = −0.887, p < 0.01) reflects biosynthetic trade-offs, where cyclase enzymes favor one pathway over another45.

Supporting environmental modulation, elevation positively correlated with β-thujone (r = 0.604, p = 0.064) and negatively with 1,8-cineole (r = −0.448, p = 0.194), aligning with altitudinal gradients: high-elevation ecotypes (e.g., TP1 at 3220 m, β-thujone 84.4%) versus low-elevation (e.g., TP5 at 2022 m, 1.1%). Altitude-driven UV exposure and temperature drops may upregulate thujone synthase, favoring ketone accumulation over alcohols/ethers via altered dehydrogenase kinetics45. Weak correlations with borneol (r = −0.230, p = 0.522) suggest additional factors like soil moisture influence its expression.

These mechanistic interdependencies propose targeted therapeutic applications where 1,8-cineole and borneol-enriched chemotypes show promise for direct ROS scavenging in antimicrobial therapeutics, while β-thujone-dominant chemotypes may prove valuable for synergistic applications in diabetes or neuroprotection through indirect oxidative stress mitigation. Future investigations incorporating comprehensive soil and climatic datasets alongside gene expression analysis of key enzymes such as terpene synthases will validate these proposed mechanisms and optimize chemotype selection for specific biomedical applications.

Multivariate analysis: PCA and hierarchical clustering

PCA was employed to elucidate the underlying patterns in the chemical composition of T. polycephalum EOs across ten ecotypes. PCA reduces the dimensionality of complex datasets by identifying uncorrelated variables (principal components) that capture the majority of variance in the original data48. This approach is particularly valuable for essential oil chemometric analysis, where numerous chemical constituents create high-dimensional datasets that are difficult to interpret directly. Unlike hierarchical clustering, which assigns samples to discrete groups, PCA provides a continuous visualization of chemical relationships, revealing gradients of compositional similarity and identifying samples with intermediate characteristics that may represent transitional chemotypes49. This capability is essential for understanding the continuous nature of phytochemical variation in plant populations, where rigid classifications may obscure important biological relationships.

The eigenvalues of the correlation matrix reveal that the first three principal components (PCs) collectively account for 94.22% of the total variance in the dataset, with PC1, PC2, and PC3 explaining 55.74%, 25.3%, and 13.18% of the variance, respectively (Table 5). This high cumulative percentage indicates that these three components effectively capture the majority of the variability in chemical composition among the ecotypes. Examination of the eigenvectors reveals the chemical constituents driving the differentiation among ecotypes (Table 6). PC1 is primarily characterized by a strong positive loading for β-thujone (0.32) and negative loading for 1,8-cineole (−0.31). This suggests that PC1 distinguishes ecotypes based on their β-thujone content relative to other oxygenated monoterpenes. PC2 is defined by positive loadings for terpinen-4-ol (0.39), α-pinene (0.36), and bornyl acetate (0.35), contrasted with negative loadings for borneol (−0.31) and myrtenal (−0.30). This component appears to differentiate ecotypes based on their relative concentrations of these specific monoterpenes. PC3 is notably influenced by strong positive loadings for carvotanacetone (0.66) and chrysanthenone (0.63), likely capturing variations in these compounds across the ecotypes.

The clear delineation of TP5 and TP9 as outliers emphasized their distinctive chemical compositions, which may confer unique properties or biological activities. These findings provide valuable insights into the phytochemical diversity of T. polycephalum, with potential implications for selective breeding, chemotype identification, and the optimization of EO production for specific applications.

Following PCA results, we used hierarchical clustering analysis to further investigate the chemodiversity of the EO components (Fig. 2). Unlike PCA, clustering aims to group data points with similar characteristics into distinct clusters. This is achieved by defining a similarity measure, such as Euclidean distance, to quantify how clos data points are based on their features. In the context of our T. polycephalum EO data, clustering analysis can be employed to group ecotypes with similar EO profiles. This approach can reveal distinct chemotypes, potentially reflecting underlying genetic or environmental influences38. Although a two-group (β-thujone vs. non-β-thujone) dichotomy is biologically intuitive and partly supported by β-thujone concentration differences, our multi-metric clustering approach revealed finer substructure. This partitioning is driven by variation in other constituents that are themselves biologically and chemically meaningful. The dendrogram shows well-defined branch points, with the four clusters separating at distinct dissimilarity thresholds, supporting their interpretation as independent chemotypes. Thus, while the two-cluster simplification provides a broad categorization, it masks chemically coherent differences relevant to chemotaxonomy, ecological adaptation, and potential bioactivity. The analysis therefore highlights four distinct clusters (Fig. 2), each separated at clear dissimilarity thresholds, underscoring the presence of chemically coherent and biologically relevant chemotypes within T. polycephalum: Cluster I, the largest cluster, encompassing six ecotypes (TP3, TP10, TP7, TP1, TP4, and TP8), suggests considerable chemical similarity in their major constituents. Notably, this cluster groups ecotypes from diverse geographical backgrounds within the high-altitude regions of the Kohgiluyeh & Boyer Ahmad province, all characterized by elevated β-thujone content. This finding suggests a potential influence of environmental factors, such as altitude, on the dominant biosynthetic pathways within these ecotypes.

Cluster II, comprising ecotypes TP2 and TP6, exhibits a distinct chemical profile from Cluster I. TP6 is distinguished by high levels of oxygenated monoterpenes, particularly chrysanthenone (8.3%) and cis-chrysanthenol (3%), alongside a remarkable concentration of carvotanacetone (12.4%). TP2, on the other hand, shows elevated concentrations of the monoterpene α-phellandrene (9%) and the oxygenated monoterpene myrtenol (2.77%). This intra-cluster homogeneity suggests a shared biosynthetic pathway for these specific compounds within TP2 and TP6.

Clusters III and IV each contain a single ecotype (TP5 and TP9, respectively), indicating unique chemical profiles. TP5 is distinguished by notably lower β-thujone levels. TP9 exhibits a unique profile characterized by the highest levels of terpinen-4-ol and 1,8-cineole among all ecotypes. This aligns perfectly with the previous discussion on the distinct compositions of TP5 and TP9 as revealed by GC-MS analysis.

TP5, originating from the lowest studied altitude (2022 m), stands out within Cluster III. Its low β-thujone levels suggest a potential altitude-dependent variation in phytochemical composition. Notably, TP5 is characterized by elevated levels of various compound classes, including monoterpenes (α-pinene, sabinene), oxygenated monoterpenes (cis-sabinene hydrate, cis-chrysanthenol, myrtenal, myrtenol, cis-crysanthenyl acetate, p-cymen-7-ol), sesquiterpenes (caryophyllene oxide), and even the inclusion of methyl jasmonate (1.71%). This unique profile further emphasizes the chemotypic diversity within T. polycephalum and warrants further investigation into the underlying factors influencing these variations.

TP9 (Sudejan) exhibits a unique profile, falling into its own cluster (Cluster IV). Similar to TP5, TP9 shows high concentrations of monoterpenes (α-pinene, camphene, β-pinene) and oxygenated monoterpenes (chrysanthenone, trans-pinocarveol, cis-chrysanthenol, α-terpineol, bornyl acetate). However, TP9 is distinguished by its remarkable dominance of terpinen-4-ol (34.5%). This further underscores the remarkable variation in chemotypes observed across T. polycephalum ecotypes.

This study provides evidence supporting the hypothesis that substantial intraspecific phytochemical variation exists among T. polycephalum populations in the Zagros Mountains, sufficient to define discrete chemotypes. Comprehensive chemical profiling of ten ecotypes revealed a diverse phytochemical landscape, with marked differences in the relative abundance of key constituents, including β-thujone, 1,8-cineole, borneol, and terpinen-4-ol. Analyses of TPC and antioxidant activity further demonstrated significant intraspecific variation, with certain ecotypes exhibiting notably higher antioxidant capacities. Chemometric approaches, particularly PCA and hierarchical clustering, provided definitive evidence for this variation. The PCA biplot revealed distinct clusters corresponding to unique chemical profiles, identifying four discrete chemotypes: a predominant β-thujone chemotype and three divergent chemotypes characterized by borneol/1,8-cineole, terpinen-4-ol/1,8-cineole, and an intermediate profile. These findings confirm that the phytochemical diversity within T. polycephalum is sufficiently pronounced to establish stable, well-defined groups.

Overall, this work confirms significant intraspecific chemodiversity within T. polycephalum populations in the Zagros region, addressing a critical knowledge gap in the literature. This chemical variability, likely influenced by both genetic factors and local environmental conditions, has direct implications for targeted cultivation strategies. Specifically, it may inform breeding programs aimed at producing chemotypes with optimized profiles for pharmaceutical, agricultural, or industrial applications.

Conclusion

Our analysis of T. polycephalum EO composition aligns well with existing research, highlighting the presence of key components commonly reported for this species, such as thujone. However, a deeper dive reveals a fascinating interplay. This interplay contributes to the remarkable chemodiversity observed within T. polycephalum EOs. These findings suggest significant chemical variability among ecotypes, with β-thujone potentially playing a unifying role while other major compounds like borneol exhibit ecotype-specific dominance. The application of GC-MS and multivariate analyses (PCA, clustering) provided a robust framework to identify distinct chemotypes, offering a valuable foundation for targeted breeding and industrial applications. While this study provides valuable insights into the phytochemical diversity of T. polycephalum within the studied region, it also highlights opportunities for further research. Future studies could expand the sampling efforts to encompass a wider geographical range within the Zagros Mountains and beyond, to capture a more comprehensive view of the species’ phytochemical diversity. Additionally, while our study suggests the potential influence of genetic and environmental factors on the observed chemodiversity, further research incorporating genomic analyses could provide a more detailed understanding of the specific contributions of these factors.
