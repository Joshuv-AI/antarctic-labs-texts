Hypothesis Selection: A High Probability Conundrum
Anders Aamand, Maryam Aliakbarpour, Justin Y. Chen, Sandeep Silwal

Abstract
In the hypothesis selection problem, we are given a finite set of candidate distributions (hypotheses), ℋ = { H 1 , … , H n } \mathcal{H}=\{H_{1},\ldots,H_{n}\} , and samples from an unknown distribution P P . Our goal is to find a hypothesis H i H_{i} whose total variation distance to P P is comparable to that of the nearest hypothesis in ℋ \mathcal{H} . Specifically, if the minimum distance is 𝖮𝖯𝖳 \mathsf{OPT} , we aim to output an H i H_{i} such that, with probability at least 1 − δ 1-\delta , its total variation distance to P P is at most C ⋅ 𝖮𝖯𝖳 + ε C\cdot\mathsf{OPT}+\varepsilon . Despite decades of work, key aspects of this problem remain unresolved, including the optimal running time for algorithms that achieve the optimal sample complexity and best possible approximation factor of C = 3 C=3 . The previous state-of-the-art result [Aliakbarpour, Bun, Smith, NeurIPS 2024] provided a nearly linear in n n time algorithm but with a sub-optimal dependence on the other parameters, running in O ~ ​ ( n / ( δ 3 ​ ε 3 ) ) \tilde{O}(n/(\delta^{3}\varepsilon^{3})) time. We improve this time complexity to O ~ ​ ( n / ( δ ​ ε 2 ) ) \tilde{O}(n/(\delta\varepsilon^{2})) , significantly reducing the dependence on the confidence and error parameters. Our work makes progress resolving a central open problem: finding the optimal tradeoffs between sample complexity, running time, and the approximation factor in hypothesis selection. Furthermore, we study hypothesis selection in three alternative settings, resolving or making progress on several open questions from prior works: 1. Bounding the expected distance of the output hypothesis, rather than its high-probability performance: We present an upper bound of C = 3 − 2 / n C=3-2/n within the information theoretically optimal sample complexity of O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n / ε 2 ) O(\log n/\varepsilon^{2}) and show that no algorithm can do better even with an unbounded number of samples. This answers a question raised in [Mahalanabis, Stefankovic, COLT 2008] and [Bousquet, Kane, Moran, COLT 2019]. 2. Assuming the numerical value of 𝖮𝖯𝖳 \mathsf{OPT} is known in advance: We present an algorithm obtaining C = 3 C=3 and runtime O ~ ​ ( n / ε 2 ) \tilde{O}(n/\varepsilon^{2}) with the optimal sample complexity and succeeding with high probability in n n . 3. Allowing a preprocessing step on the hypothesis class ℋ \mathcal{H} before observing samples: We present an algorithm with polynomial preprocessing time, C = 3 C=3 , inverse polynomial failure probability, an n n dependence in the query time of n 2 − Ω ⁡ ( ε ) n^{2-\Omega(\varepsilon)} .

Introduction

Hypothesis selection, also known as density estimation, models a fundamental task in statistical estimation: identify the best explanation of noisy observations among a set of known hypothesis. This problem is central to statistical learning and has widespread applications to agnostic learning of a parametric class of distributions (e.g., mixtures of Gaussians
[
SOAJ14
,
DK14
,
ABDM18
,
ABDH+20
]
, Poisson binomials
[
DDS12
]
, and junta distributions
[
ABR16
]
). Formally, we are given
n
distributions
\mathcal{H}=(H_{1},\ldots,H_{n})
(a set of known hypotheses) over a possibly infinite domain
\mathcal{X}
as well as sample access to an unknown distribution
P
(noisy observations) also over
\mathcal{X}
. The goal is to output a distribution
H_{i}\in\mathcal{H}
which is competitive with the
best possible
explanation: denoting
i^{*}=\argmin_{i\in[n]}d_{\textit{TV}}(P,H_{i})
and the optimal value
\mathsf{OPT}=d_{\textit{TV}}(P,H_{i^{*}})
, we desire our reported
H_{i}
to satisfy

d_{\textit{TV}}(P,H_{i})\leq C\cdot\mathsf{OPT}+\varepsilon

(1)

with probability
1-\delta
, for some approximation factor
C\geq 1
and failure probability
\delta\in(0,1)
.
d_{\textit{TV}}
denotes the total variation distance, also known as the statistical distance. We focus on the proper, agnostic setting where the algorithm returns a hypothesis in
\mathcal{H}
but the unknown distribution
P
need not be in
\mathcal{H}
.

There are several competing factors at play, which leads to a rich space of algorithm design: the number of samples we take from
P
(
sample complexity
), the overall running time of the algorithm (
computational complexity
), and the
approximation factor

C
.
1
1

1

We define the running time more precisely in
Section

1.3
, but as a summary it is measured in terms of the “accesses” we make to the known hypothesis
H_{i}
, as well as the time required to compute on the samples from
P
.
This problem and the interplay between these algorithmic quantities have been a significant area of investigation throughout the past several decades
[
Yat85
,
DL96
,
DL97
,
DL01
,
MS08
,
CDSS14
,
AJOS14
,
ADLS17
,
AFJ+18
,
BKM19
,
BKSW19
,
GKK+20
,
BBK+22
,
AAC+23
,
ABS23
,
AAC+24
,
ABS24
,
ASSW25
]
. Despite this extensive line of work the following question remains unanswered in full generality:

Meta-question:
What are the fundamental tradeoffs between sample complexity, running time, and the approximation factor in hypothesis selection, and can a single algorithm achieve optimality in all three aspects simultaneously?

Our paper is guided by this meta-question. We identify fundamental gaps in the literature relating to the interplay of these three parameters, and provide results that fully or partially resolve them. We believe our results can be viewed as intermediate stepping stones towards a complete answer to the meta-question raised above. We discuss the concrete problems that we study in the following section as well as the unique challenges of combining these aforementioned desiderata for the hypothesis selection problem.

1.1
Our Contributions

1.1.1
The Moderate Probability Regime

Early studies of hypothesis selection established that a sample complexity of
s=O(\log(n/\delta)/\epsilon^{2})
is sufficient to achieve a constant approximation factor of
C=3

[
Yat85
,
DL96
,
DL97
,
DL01
]
. Surprisingly, this sample complexity is
independent of the domain size

|\mathcal{X}|
, a sharp contrast to the
\Omega(|\mathcal{X}|)
samples required to learn a distribution
P
directly in total variation distance. It turns out that indeed, this domain-independent sample complexity requires an approximation factor of
C\geq 3
, which illustrates a sharp threshold: a factor of
C<3
is impossible to guarantee, even for
n=2
, unless the sample complexity depends polynomially on the domain size
\left\lvert\mathcal{X}\right\rvert
.
2
2

2

This result was initially proved in
[
BKM19
]
. We identify a subtle but critical error with the analysis of this lower bound in
[
BKM19
]
. We alter the construction and analysis in this work and generalize the hard instance to an arbitrary number of hypotheses
n
(see
Remark

3.8
).

The major drawback of the original algorithms for hypothesis selection is that their
computational complexity
has at least quadratic dependence in
n
. A significant body of recent work has focused on reducing the running time to nearly-linear in
n

[
MS08
,
DK14
,
AJOS14
,
AFJ+18
,
AAC+23
,
ABS23
]
. These attempts, however, often came at the cost of a worse approximation factor (
C\geq 5
) or required special assumptions. Recently,
[
ABS24
]
broke this barrier, presenting an algorithm with the optimal approximation factor
C=3
, sample complexity of
O(\log(n/\delta)/\epsilon^{2})
, and a nearly-linear total running time of
\tilde{O}\left(\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{3}\delta^{3}}\right)
.
3
3

3

\tilde{O}(\cdot)
hides polylogarithmic factors in
n
,
1/\delta
, and
1/\varepsilon
.

Despite being linear in
n
, this complexity is sub-optimal with respect to
\delta
and
\epsilon
.
These results therefore only yield improvements over quadratic time in the constant or
moderate
success probability regime where
1/\delta
is significantly sublinear in
n
.
Interestingly, with the same number of samples, the dependence on
\delta
and
\epsilon
improves if we relax either the approximation factor
C
or the linear-time requirement. On the one hand, the algorithm of
[
MS08
]
achieves the optimal
C=3
, but has a quadratic running time of
O(n^{2}\log(n/\delta)/\epsilon^{2})
. On the other hand, near-linear time algorithms can achieve a running time of
\tilde{O}(n\log(1/\delta)/\epsilon^{2})
if they aim for a larger approximation factor, such as
C\geq 4
(e.g.,
[
AAC+23
,
ABS24
]
).

The polynomial dependence on the failure probability
\delta
is particularly unusual, as typical statistical tasks exhibit a much milder
\log(1/\delta)
dependency. This issue was also present in several other algorithms that achieved
C<9
in near-linear time
[
ABS23
,
ASSW25
]
. This sub-optimality stems from the failure of standard amplification techniques; for instance, repeating a process
O(\log(1/\delta))
times that individually guarantees
C=3
with constant probability and taking the “best option” only yields a combined guarantee of
C=9
as the true best option can only be approximated up to a factor of
3
. Such amplification methods are ineffective for algorithms achieving the optimal approximation factor.

This phenomenon prompts a key question about the optimal dependence on
\delta
and
\epsilon
for the
C=3
case, a question previously posed as an open direction in
[
ABS24
,
ASSW25
]
:

Is there an algorithm for hypothesis selection with the optimal factor
C=3
, optimal sample complexity
s=O(\log n/\varepsilon^{2})
, but with an improved dependence on
\varepsilon
and
\delta
in the running time, while maintaining the nearly linear in
n
dependence?

We answer this question affirmatively by providing an algorithm that significantly improves the running time of
[
ABS24
]
by a factor of
\tilde{O}(1/(\delta^{2}\epsilon))
. Our main result is summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
\delta>1/n
, there exists an algorithm for hypothesis selection that uses
O(\log(n/\delta)/\epsilon^{2})
samples, achieves the optimal approximation factor of
C=3
, and runs in time
\tilde{O}\left\lparen\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}\delta}\right\rparen
.

This result allows for sub-quadratic time hypothesis selection with optimal sample complexity and approximation factor for a much larger range of failure probabilities
\delta
than prior work.
It remains an extremely intriguing open question whether it is possible to get rid of the polynomial dependence on
\delta
in the runtime, namely if there exists an algorithm with runtime
\tilde{O}\left\lparen\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right\rparen
which succeeds with high probability in
n
.

1.1.2
An Alternative Perspective: Expected Value Guarantee

A curious observation from
[
MS08
]
considers a different perspective on the guarantee of hypothesis selection: instead of finding a hypothesis with a high-probability distance guarantee, the focus is on the expected distance of the (randomized) output hypothesis
H_{i}
. That is, the algorithm outputs a distribution over
\mathcal{H}
and aims for a guarantee of the form:

\mathbf{E}\!\left[d_{\textit{TV}}(P,H_{i})\right]\leq C\cdot\mathsf{OPT}+\varepsilon\,.

One might naturally ask: does this weaker requirement on the average performance of the algorithm lead to substantial improvement? Mahalanabis et al.
[
MS08
]
show that for a hypothesis class of size
n=2
,
O(\log(n)/\varepsilon^{2})=O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
samples are sufficient to guarantee
C=2
.
(Note that the output is still in
\mathcal{H}
and competing with the best hypothesis in
\mathcal{H}
.)
This circumvents the required lower bound of
C=3
when a single hypothesis must be output. This finding raises an open question, which was also posed in
[
MS08
,
BKM19
]
:

For an arbitrarily large finite class
\mathcal{H}
, can we get an expected approximation factor
C<3
?

We resolve this open question and provide a complete understanding of the optimal approximation factor
C
in the expected distance setting of hypothesis selection. We prove that with the natural
O((\log n)/\varepsilon^{2})
sample complexity, there exists a randomized algorithm that obtains an approximation factor of
C=3-\frac{2}{n}
in expectation.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists an algorithm that takes
s=O(\log(n)/\varepsilon^{2})
samples from
P
, runs in
\tilde{O}(n^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
time, and, with high probability in
n
, outputs an explicit distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\mathcal{H}
such that:

\mathbf{E}_{H\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\left[d_{\textit{TV}}(H,P)\right]\leq\left(3-\frac{2}{n}\right)\mathsf{OPT}+\varepsilon.

We complement this with a lower bound showing that any algorithm obtaining a better approximation factor than
C=3-\frac{2}{n}
must use a number of samples that is polynomial in the domain size.
Along the way, we identify and correct a subtle error in an existing proof of this fact for the special case of
n=2
due to
[
BKM19
]
.

Theorem 1.3

(Informal version of
Corollary

3.5
)
.

Consider a sample size
s
which is a function of
n,\varepsilon,\delta
.
There exists a sufficiently large, finite domain size
|\mathcal{X}|
such that no randomized algorithm with
s
samples can solve the hypothesis selection problem with an expected approximation factor less than
3-\frac{2}{n}-o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
.

We also remark that
\Omega(\log(n)/\varepsilon^{2})
samples are necessary to obtain any constant factor expected value approximation; see
Theorem

B.2
.

1.1.3
Impact of Preprocessing

An important extension to the standard hypothesis selection problem is the
pre-processing setting
, which is motivated by applications where the selection task is performed repeatedly for a fixed hypothesis set
\mathcal{H}
but against different unknown distributions
P
. In this model, an algorithm first analyzes
\mathcal{H}
before receiving any samples and can spend polynomial time to
preprocess

\mathcal{H}
. This approach has been studied in several works
[
MS08
,
AAC+23
,
AAC+24
]
.

First, note that a
|\mathcal{X}|^{O(\log n)}
time preprocessing allows us to easily determine the right answer (with factor of
C=3
) in
\text{poly}(\log n)
time: just compute the output under all possible
O(\log n)
samples that can be drawn.
[
MS08
]
showed that
2^{O(n)}
time preprocessing can be used to pre-compute all possible computational paths for linear in
n
query time. The exponential time complexity of these preprocessing procedures makes them prohibitively expensive. A natural question, first raised in
[
MS08
]
, is to what extent a polynomial-time preprocessing step can improve overall performance. In particular, we ask:

How fast can we obtain
C=3
using
s=O(\log n/\varepsilon^{2})
samples if we are only allowed
polynomial
preprocessing time?

We show that preprocessing can indeed be useful, allowing us to break the quadratic-time barrier in
n
even in the high-probability regime where
\delta
is an arbitrarily small inverse polynomial in
n
. We have the following theorem.

Theorem 1.4
.

For every
\ \varepsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists an algorithm which uses
\textup{poly}(|\mathcal{X}|,n)
preprocessing time (without knowledge of
P
),
O(\log(n)/\varepsilon^{2})
samples from
P
, and outputs a hypothesis
H_{i}
satisfying

d_{\textit{TV}}(P,H_{i})\leq(3+\alpha)\cdot\mathsf{OPT}+O(\varepsilon)

with probability
1-1/\textup{poly}(n)
in time
\tilde{O}\left(\frac{|\mathcal{X}|\cdot n^{2-\Omega(\alpha)}}{\varepsilon^{3}}\right)
.

In contrast to our other results, our preprocessing result only applies when
\mathcal{X}
is finite.
We remark that partial progress was made in
[
AAC+23
,
AAC+24
]
, which showed that it is possible to obtain
sublinear
runtime in
n
if we further restrict the hypotheses to be discrete distributions and require that
P\in\mathcal{H}
(so that
\mathsf{OPT}
is 0).

1.1.4
Extra Information: Knowing
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{OPT}}

Another relaxation of hypothesis selection studied in literature involves fixing the
quantity

\mathsf{OPT}
. Interestingly, knowing an approximation to this numerical value, but not the underlying hypothesis, allows for faster algorithm or algorithms with better approximation factors
[
DK14
,
BKSW19
,
ABS23
]
.
The primary advantage of such algorithms is that knowing
\mathsf{OPT}
enables us to better filter the sub-optimal hypotheses. If we find evidence that a hypothesis is significantly farther from the true distribution than
\mathsf{OPT}
, we can remove it from consideration, a step that is generally not possible for standard algorithms.
In particular,

[
ABS23
]
gave an algorithm that achieves approximation factor
C=3
in
\tilde{O}(n/(\delta\epsilon^{2}))
time.
Naturally, we ask

Is there an algorithm which knows the value of
\mathsf{OPT}
and achieves
C=3
using
s=O(\log(n/\delta)/\varepsilon^{2})
samples and in runtime
n\cdot\text{polylog}\left(n,1/\delta\right)/\varepsilon^{2}
?

We answer this question affirmatively and provide an algorithm that is
essentially optimal with respect to all three algorithmic aspects of this problem
. In particular, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 1.5
.

When the value of
\mathsf{OPT}
is part of the input, there exists an algorithm with sample complexity
O\left\lparen\frac{\log(n/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right\rparen
and runtime
O\left\lparen\frac{n\log^{3}(n/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right\rparen
, which with probability at least
1-\delta
returns a hypothesis
H_{i}
such that
d_{\textit{TV}}(P,H_{i})\leq 3\cdot\mathsf{OPT}+\varepsilon
.

1.2
Other Related Work

Other frameworks of hypothesis selection:

Hypothesis selection has also been studied in the
improper setting
, where the algorithm can output a density that does not necessarily belong to
\mathcal{H}
. In this setting, an approximation factor of
C=2
is known to be achievable and tight
[
BKM19
,
BBK+22
]
.
This problem has been also studied under the constraints of differential privacy
[
BKSW19
,
GKK+20
,
PAA24
,
ASSW25
]
. We note that all of our algorithms are proper and return a hypothesis in
\mathcal{H}
.

Broader Applications of Hypothesis Selection

While our paper is focused on the fundamental hypothesis selection problem itself, we briefly remark that progress on our questions can potentially have many downstream applications, since hypothesis selection is used as a standard “meta tool” in many learning problems. For example, a classic approach to learning a structured distribution is to choose a net or cover for the class (a finite set of representatives that are ‘close by’ to all possible distributions in the class). The cover is viewed as the set of hypothesis
H_{i}
. Examples of where this strategy has been successful
include Poisson binomial distributions
[
DDS12
]
, mixtures of Gaussians
[
KMV12
,
DK14
,
SOAJ14
,
KSS18
,
ABDM18
,
ABDH+20
]
, distributions with piecewise polynomial density functions
[
ADLS17
]
, and
histograms
[
CDSS14
,
CDKL22
]
; we refer to the survey
[
Dia16
]
for more details.

The significance of the optimal constant factor,
C=3
, also becomes more apparent when using the standard cover methods. If we use a
\gamma
-cover of a parametric distribution class, running a hypothesis selection algorithm on this cover implies that
\mathsf{OPT}\approx\gamma
. To achieve the same final accuracy, an algorithm with a suboptimal approximation factor requires a much denser cover (a smaller
\gamma
) than one with the optimal factor. In some cases, such as for mixtures of
k
-Gaussians, this decrease in
\gamma
can cause the size of the cover to grow exponentially. Therefore, using an algorithm with the best possible approximation factor is crucial for maintaining computational feasibility. A more detailed discussion is provided in
[
ABS24
]
.

1.3
Preliminaries

The key toolkit for designing algorithms with constant approximation factors in
s=O\left\lparen\frac{\log(n/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right\rparen
samples are “Scheffé sets” and “semi-distances” used extensively in prior works on this problem (e.g.,
[
DL01
,
DK14
,
ABS24
]
).
We present the basic definitions and facts about these objects which will be used throughout this work.

Definition 1.6

(Scheffé set
[
DL01
]
)
.

The
Scheffé set
for any pair
i\neq j\in[n]
is

S_{i\rightarrow j}=\begin{cases}\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}:H_{i}(x)<H_{j}(x)\right\}&\qquad\text{ if }i<j\\
\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}:H_{i}(x)\leq H_{j}(x)\right\}&\qquad\text{ if }i>j\end{cases}.

(2)

It is straightforward to see that
S_{i\rightarrow j}
is the set that witnesses the total variation distance of distributions
H_{i}
and
H_{j}
:

H_{j}(S_{i\rightarrow j})-H_{i}(S_{i\rightarrow j})=\sup_{S\subseteq\mathcal{X}}H_{j}(S)-H_{i}(S)=d_{\textit{TV}}(H_{j},H_{i}).

(3)

Computational Model

Our computational model is the standard one used for hypothesis selection
[
DL01
]
. We assume that each of the following operations take
O(1)
time:

1.

We can request a sample from
P
,

2.

Given any domain element
x\in\mathcal{X}
and any
i\neq j
, we can ask if
H_{i}(x)>H_{j}(x)
,

3.

For any
k\in[n]
, in
O(1)
time we can compute
H_{k}(S_{i\rightarrow j})
, i.e. the probability mass that
H_{k}
places on the Scheffé set
S_{i\rightarrow j}
.

The second condition can be relaxed to only output
H_{k}(S_{i\rightarrow j})
up to an additive error of
\varepsilon
but we ignore this detail for simplicity.
This is the standard computational model used in prior work: if these operations take time
T
, then ours and prior bounds often are simply multiplicatively scaled by
T
.
The third operation can be implemented given sampling and query access to the hypotheses
[
CR14
]
or in a one-time preprocessing of the hypotheses .

Definition 1.7

(Semi-distances
[
DL01
]
)
.

For any pair
i\neq j\in[n]
, we define the
semi-distance

w_{i\rightarrow j}\in[0,1]
as

w_{i\rightarrow j}=\left\lvert H_{j}(S_{i\rightarrow j})-P(S_{i\rightarrow j})\right\rvert.

We will let
w_{i\rightarrow i}=0
.
We define the
max semi-distance

W(H_{j})
as

W(H_{j})=\max_{i\in[n]}w_{i\rightarrow j}.

Intuitively,
w_{i\rightarrow j}
is a guess of the total variation distance between
H_{j}
and
P
via the Scheffé set differentiating
H_{i}
and
H_{j}
.
Note that
w_{i\rightarrow j}
and
w_{j\rightarrow i}
use the essentially the same Scheffé set as
S_{i\rightarrow j}=\overline{S_{j\rightarrow i}}
but are measuring the distance between
P
and two different hypotheses
H_{j}
and
H_{i}
, respectively.
We use the following key properties of semi-distances also utilized in prior works
[
DL01
,
DK14
,
ABS24
]
.

Proposition 1.8

(Underestimation)
.

For all
i\neq j\in[n]
,

w_{i\rightarrow j}\leq d_{\textit{TV}}(P,H_{j})

Proof.

w_{i\rightarrow j}
is equal to the absolute difference in mass assigned by
H_{j}
and
P
on some subset of the domain. The total variation distance is defined to be the maximum over such quantities.
∎

Proposition 1.9
.

For any
i\neq j\in[n]
,

d_{\textit{TV}}(P,H_{j})\leq d_{\textit{TV}}(P,H_{i})+w_{i\rightarrow j}+w_{j\rightarrow i}.

In particular, for any
j\in[n]
, as
w_{j\rightarrow i^{*}}\leq\mathsf{OPT}
,

d_{\textit{TV}}(P,H_{j})\leq 2\mathsf{OPT}+w_{i^{*}\rightarrow j}.

Proof.

The first statement can be derived from the triangle inequality.

\displaystyle d_{\textit{TV}}(P,H_{j})

\displaystyle\leq d_{\textit{TV}}(P,H_{i})+d_{\textit{TV}}(H_{j},H_{i})

(Triangle inequality)

\displaystyle=d_{\textit{TV}}(P,H_{i})+\left\lvert H_{j}(S_{i\rightarrow j})-H_{i}(S_{i\rightarrow j})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq d_{\textit{TV}}(P,H_{i})+\left\lvert H_{j}(S_{i\rightarrow j})-P(S_{i\rightarrow j})\right\rvert+\left\lvert H_{i}(S_{i\rightarrow j})-P(S_{i\rightarrow j})\right\rvert

(Triangle inequality)

\displaystyle=d_{\textit{TV}}(P,H_{i})+w_{i\rightarrow j}+w_{j\rightarrow i}\,.

The second statement is derived by setting
i
equal to
i^{*}
, and noting:

w_{j\rightarrow i^{*}}\leq d_{\textit{TV}}(P,H_{i^{*}})\leq\mathsf{OPT}\,.\qed

This is the source of the
3
-approximation in prior work. If we can find a hypothesis
H_{j}
with
W(H_{j})\leq\mathsf{OPT}
, then it must be a
3
-approximation as
W(H_{j})\geq w_{i\rightarrow j}
. We do not know
\mathsf{OPT}
but simply picking the hypothesis with smallest
W(H_{j})
will suffice as
W(H_{j})\leq W(H_{i^{*}})\leq\mathsf{OPT}
using
Proposition

1.8
.

Proposition 1.10
.

With
s=\Theta\left\lparen\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right\rparen
samples and time, a semi-distance
w_{i\rightarrow j}
can be estimated by
\hat{w}_{i\rightarrow j}
such that

|w_{i\rightarrow j}-\hat{w}_{i\rightarrow j}|\leq\varepsilon

with probability
1-\delta
. Similarly, a total of
O\left(\frac{\log(n/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)

samples and
O\left(\frac{n^{2}\log(n/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)
time are required to accurately estimate all
\binom{n}{2}
pairs of hypothesis with an overall success probability of at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

Let
X
be a random variable for the number of sampled elements which land in
S_{i\rightarrow j}
, so that
\hat{w}_{i\rightarrow j}=\left\lvert H_{j}(S_{i\rightarrow j})-X/s\right\rvert
.
Note that
\mathbf{E}\!\left[X\right]=sP(S_{i\rightarrow j})
, so
\left\lvert w_{i\rightarrow j}-\hat{w}_{i\rightarrow j}\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert X-\mathbf{E}\!\left[X\right]\right\rvert/s
.
The result follows from a standard Hoeffding bound on the deviation of the sum of i.i.d. indicator random variables.
The time bound follows from our computational model where sampling, querying membership in
S_{i\rightarrow j}
, and computing
H_{k}(S_{i\rightarrow j})
all take
O(1)
time. By repeating this procedure for all pairs of distributions and reusing samples, each with a failure probability of
\delta/n^{2}
, a union bound guarantees an overall success probability of at least
1-\delta
.
∎

1.4
Technical Overview

1.4.1
Fast Algorithms for Moderate Success Probability

Perhaps the biggest technical challenge of our work is to obtain an algorithm for hypothesis selection with optimal approximation factor
C=3
in time roughly
O\left\lparen\frac{n\cdot\text{polylog}(n)\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}\delta}\right\rparen
time and with optimal sample complexity.
5
5

5

It is a very interesting open question if there exists an algorithm that completely avoids any polynomial dependence on
\delta
.
We here sketch the main ideas behind this new algorithm. In what follows,
\tilde{O}
hides logarithmic factors in
n
. For the discussion, let us forget about
\varepsilon
and assume that we have oracle access to exact semi-distances
w_{i\rightarrow j}
for any
i,j
— if we can understand this case, our result then follows rather easily from the fact that we can approximate any
w_{i\rightarrow j}
within
\varepsilon
in time and sample complexity
O(\log(n)/\varepsilon^{2})
with high probability in
n
via
Proposition

1.10
.
Our main contribution is an algorithm solving what we dub the
Semi-Distance Threshold Problem
. This problem has input parameters
b,\beta\in[0,1]
as well as the hypotheses set
\mathcal{H}
and unknown distribution

. Consider the directed graph
G_{b}=([n],E)
having a directed edge
(i,j)
if and only if
w_{i\rightarrow j}>b
. The goal in this problem is to output either

(1)

\perp
if all nodes in
G_{b}
have an incoming edge, or

(2)

j
for any

such that there is no edge from

to

.

If we output
\perp

indeed satisfies the property in (1) with probability one, whereas if we output some

, then the property in (2) holds with with probability

for a parameter
\beta
.
We here sketch why such an algorithm suffices to get our result and the main ideas behind designing the algorithm and why it can be made to run in
\tilde{O}(n/\beta)
time. The details of this part can be found in
Section

.

How to apply an algorithm for the Semi-Distance Threshold Problem
: Suppose we have an algorithm for the Semi-Distance Threshold Problem. We will employ it
\log(1/\varepsilon)
times with
\beta=\delta/\log(1/\varepsilon)
performing a binary search on
b
to find a
b_{0}
such that with input
b_{0}
, the algorithm outputs
\perp
whereas with input
b_{0}+\varepsilon
it outputs some
j_{0}
. Note that with input
b=0
,
G_{0}
is the complete directed graph and the algorithm will output
\perp
, whereas for
b=1
,
G_{1}
is empty and the algorithm successfully outputs some
j
with probability
1-\beta
. Curiously, these two boundary cases ensures that the binary search can be performed: Indeed, think of an array of size
O(1/\varepsilon)
where we want to find a cell containing a
0
(corresponding to outputting
\perp
) which is immediately to the left of a cell containing a
1
(corresponding to outputting some
j
). Without any assumptions on the entries, this would require us to look at
\Omega(1/\varepsilon)
entries, but assuming the leftmost cell contains a zero and the rightmost cell contains a one, we can do it looking at just
O(\log 1/\varepsilon)
entries. This is achieved by checking the middle cell and recursively updating the search interval to ensure its left boundary is always a zero and its right boundary is a one.

Our algorithm outputs
H_{j_{0}}
as the final result. Assuming all runs of the Semi-Distance Threshold Problem algorithm were correct, it holds that (1)
\mathsf{OPT}\geq b_{0}
and (2)
w_{i^{*}\rightarrow j_{0}}\leq b_{0}+\varepsilon\leq\mathsf{OPT}+\varepsilon
.
Indeed, since the algorithm with input
b_{0}
outputs

must have some incoming edge
(j,i^{*})
in

b_{0}\leq w_{j\rightarrow i^{*}}\leq\mathsf{OPT}

. Moreover, since the algorithm on input

w_{i^{*}\rightarrow j_{0}}<b_{0}+\varepsilon\leq\mathsf{OPT}+\varepsilon
.
By
Proposition

1.9
, returning

thus suffices.

An algorithm for the Semi-Distance Threshold Problem
:
For
V\subseteq[n]
and
i\in n
, we say that
i
is
\alpha
-prompting
(with respect to
V
) if
\{j\in V\mid(i,j)\in E(G_{b})\}
constitutes at least a
\alpha
-fraction of
V
. To solve the Semi-Distance Threshold Problem, we use a subroutine, which for any set
V\subseteq[n]
can either identify an
i\in[n]
which is at least
\beta
-prompting or a
j\in V
satisfying that there is no edge from
i^{*}
to
j
in
G_{b}
with probability
1-\tilde{O}(\beta)
.

To do so, consider first the case where the average “promptingness”
\hat{d}
of
i\in[n]
is
\gg\beta
, that is to say for a random
i\in V
,
\overline{d}:=\mathbb{E}[|\{j\in V\mid(i,j)\in E(G_{b})\}|]/|V|=\frac{|E(G_{b})|}{n\cdot|V|}\gg\beta
. Sampling
\tilde{O}(1/\beta)
random pairs
(i,j)\in U\times V
, and checking if there is an edge, we can detect if this is the case, and if so, estimate
\overline{d}
within a constant factor. Now in this case, for some positive integer
k=O(\log n)
, there must be at least
n/(2^{k}/\log n)
values
i\in[n]
that are
\beta 2^{k}
-prompting. To check if an
i\in[n]
is
\beta 2^{k}
-prompting, it suffices to sample
\tilde{O}(1/(\beta 2^{k}))
values
j\in V
and check if there is an edge
(i,j)
. Since for the "correct" value of
k
, there are
n/(2^{k}/\log n)
many
\beta 2^{k}
-prompting hypothesis, finding one by random sampling from
[n]
requires
\tilde{O}(2^{k}/\log n)
samples and checking if it is
\beta 2^{k}
-prompting, takes
\tilde{O}(1/(\beta 2^{k}))
samples from
V
resulting in a runtime of
\tilde{O}(1/\beta)
. Guessing the correct value of
k
has overhead
O(\log n)
. Further details can be found in
Section

2.4
.

On the other hand, in the case
\overline{d}=O(\beta)
, we proceed differently. In this case, by Markov’s inequality at least a
9/10
-fraction of
i\in[n]
are
O(\beta)
-prompting and by a double counting argument, at least a
9/10
-fraction of

|\{i\in[n]\mid(i,j)\in E(G_{b})\}|/n=O(\beta)
. In this case, we first sample
O(\log n)
values
j\in V
, let
T_{j}=\{i\in[n]\mid(i,j)\in E(G_{b})\}
, and add
j
into a set
S
if
|T_{j}|\leq O(\beta n)
. By a Chernoff bound, this happens with high probability for at least a
(4/5)
-fraction of the sampled
j
’s. The process takes
\tilde{O}(n)
time. Now for each
j\in S
, we check if
any
element in
T_{j}
is
\beta
-prompting. Checking if a single element
i\in T_{j}
is prompting can be done in
\tilde{O}(1/\beta)
time, so this check takes
\tilde{O}(n)
times in total due to the bounded size of
T_{j}
. If we find a
\beta
-prompting
i
, our subroutine has succeeded.

Finally, in the case where no element in any
T_{j}
is
\beta
-prompting, we repeat the above sampling process
again
, constructing
S^{\prime}\subset V
, but this time
resisting the temptation
to ever return a
\beta
-prompting hypothesis even if we find one and instead commit to returning a
v_{j}
which hopefully has no edge from
i^{*}
. To do so, we search for a hypothesis that has low degree
O(\beta n)
, and
no

\beta
-prompting neighbors. Since the previous sampling step failed to produce a
\beta
-prompting hypothesis even though we sampled
\gg\log n
values
j
, we know with very high probability that at least roughly half the element of
V
have low degree
O(\beta n)
and no
\beta
-prompting neighbors. Since we also sample
\gg\log n
values
j\in V
in the second sample, with very high probability we find such a

, and when we do, we return it as the final output.
The point is that if
i^{*}
is
\gg\beta
-prompting then
(i^{*},j)
cannot be an edge since,

has no
\beta
prompting neighbors. Thus in this case, the success probability is much larger than
1-\beta
. On the other hand, if
i^{*}
is only
O(\beta)
prompting, then the probability that the randomly sampled set of size
O(\log n)
will have any edge to
i^{*}
is
O(\log n\cdot\beta)=\tilde{O}(\beta)
. In particular, with probability

, the

returned by the algorithm has no edge from
i^{*}
. We provide a more detailed discussion in
Section

2.5
.

To apply the subroutine, start with
V=[n]
and search for a
\beta
-prompting
i
. Then remove all

such that
(i,j)\in E(G_{b})
from

and repeat. Since we always remove a
\beta
fraction, we can at most perform this step
\tilde{O}(1/\beta)
times, so the total runtime becomes
\tilde{O}(n/\beta)
. From the analysis above, we know that even over the many runs of the subroutine, if we
ever

, then with probability

and our Semi-Distance Threshold algorithm succeeded. The point is that from the analysis above, we know that unless some very low probability error event happened, then the first time the algorithm committed to outputting a

that

has no edge from
i^{*}
. So, we do not have to union bound over all
\tilde{O}(1/\beta)
searches for a
\beta
-prompting hypothesis.

The other case is that eventually

, and then the algorithm returns

has an incoming edge and the output is correct.

A more comprehensive version of this argument is presented in

1.4.2
Expected Approximation Factor

We show matching upper and lower bounds on the expected approximation factor for hypothesis selection with a small number of samples.
We develop a randomized algorithm which, given access to
s=O\left\lparen\frac{\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right\rparen
samples, has an output distribution
\mathcal{D}
over hypotheses such that:

\mathbf{E}_{H\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\left[d_{\textit{TV}}(H,P)\right]\leq\left\lparen 3-\frac{2}{n}\right\rparen\mathsf{OPT}+\varepsilon,

where the expectation is over the randomness of the algorithm.
This is essentially the best possible.
Given
s=o\left\lparen\frac{\sqrt{d}}{n^{1.1}}\right\rparen
samples, any proper, randomized algorithm must have an output distribution
\mathcal{D}
such that:

\mathbf{E}_{H\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\left[d_{\textit{TV}}(H,P)\right]\geq\left\lparen 3-\frac{2}{n}-o\left\lparen\frac{1}{n}\right\rparen\right\rparen\mathsf{OPT}.

In

The construction is heavily based on this prior work.
The overall goal is to construct

such that the following hold:

•

(Approximation Factor) For any

is the closest distribution to

and the rest of

•

(Indistinguishability) Consider a guessing game where the algorithm is given samples from a

for

.
If the number of samples is too small (e.g., dimension-independent), then no algorithm can correctly guess with probability greater than
\frac{1}{n}\left\lparen 1+o(1)\right\rparen
.

A hard instance satisfying these properties implies that no algorithm can distinguish between a single hypothesis with distance
\mathsf{OPT}
and

for any

.
In particular, the expected approximation factor goes to

as

.

To construct the indistinguishable families of distributions, the underlying domain is split into
2n

T_{1},\ldots,T_{2n}
.
For any
i\in[n]
, the hypothesis
H_{i}
and every

are uniform over all pieces other then
T_{2i-1}
and
T_{2i}
.
On elements in
T_{2i-1}

has probability mass
\frac{1+\beta}{d}
slightly more than uniform, and on elements in
T_{2i}
,
H_{i}
has probability mass
\frac{1-\beta}{d}
slightly less then uniform.

The true distribution
P_{i}
is very close to
H_{i}
but introduces some elements in
T_{2i-1}
and
T_{2i}
with more extreme heavy/light probability masses in order to “balance out” the distribution towards the uniform distribution.
We further splits the pieces

and

into intervals of length

.
On each piece in
T_{2i-1}

, the same as

.
The other element is set to have mass zero, so that the total mass of the interval is

, the same as it would be under the uniform distribution.
Similarly, for each piece in
T_{2i}
, all but one element of each interval is set to

with one element set to

.
The distribution

is uniform over all such distributions, in other words, sampling

involves picking a random element in each of these intervals to be the extreme element.

The distance between

and

does not have exactly

The distance between

and

for some
j\neq i
is equal to the cost of moving the extreme elements to the uniform distribution plus the cost of moving the slightly more/less than uniform pieces
T_{2i-1}
,
T_{2i}
,
T_{2j-1}
, and
T_{2j}
to the uniform distribution.
It is a straightforward calculation to verify that the latter distance is roughly three times the former.

It remains to show indistinguishability.
Le Cam’s two point method is a classic result relating TV distance and indistinguishability in guessing games between two distributions: for any algorithm, the advantage in correctly guessing beyond
1/2
probability is bounded by half the TV distance.
This can be shown by taking a coupling view of TV distance: if the TV distance is small, then there is a coupling between the two distributions where they are equal except with probability equal to the distance.
We show a simple lemma generalizing this result to
n
distributions given a
multi-dimensional coupling
where all
n
distributions take on the same value simultaneously except with a small probability
\gamma
.
We call the subset of the domain where the distributions all take on the same value a
coincident subset
.

In our setting, the distributions of interest are
\mathcal{D}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{D}_{n}
where
\mathcal{D}_{i}
is obtained by sampling a random
P_{i}\sim\mathcal{S}_{i}
and then taking
s
i.i.d. samples from
P_{i}
.
We design a multi-dimensional coupling with a coincident subset
\mathcal{B}_{\text{unique}}
which is all possible samples of size
s
which take at most one sample per length
\ell
interval.
The key property of our construction is that, for all
i
,
\mathcal{D}_{i}
assigns uniform probability mass
1/d^{s}
to all samples in
\mathcal{B}_{\text{unique}}
essentially because (a)
P_{i}
has mass
\ell/d
on every interval and (b)
\mathcal{S}_{i}
randomly permutes the probability masses within each interval.
Finally, we show that if the number of samples
s\ll\sqrt{d/n\ell}
, then it is highly likely that a sample from any
\mathcal{D}_{i}
will land in
\mathcal{B}_{\text{unique}}
.
Conditioned on this event, it is impossible to determine any information about the index
i
.
Therefore, if the sample size does not grow with
d
(e.g., any finite sample size for real-valued distributions), then it is impossible to correctly guess the index with probability significantly more than
1/n
.

1.4.3
Subquadratic Time with Preprocessing

The fastest known algorithm for hypothesis selection achieving
1/\text{poly}(n)
failure probability and the optimal approximation factor of

is due to
[
MS08
]
and runs in quadratic time
O(n^{2}\log(n)/\varepsilon^{2})
. This runtime is a natural barrier for this algorithm, and in general, for algorithms which compute high quality estimates of all quadratically many semi-distances
w_{i\rightarrow j}
, which can be done up to accuracy
\pm\varepsilon
with probability
1-1/\text{poly}(n)
using

.

While our (linear in
n
) upper bound of Section

obtains an improved dependence on the failure probability
\delta
, our algorithm does not yield improvements over this prior quadratic-time algorithm in the setting where
\delta
is an arbitrarily small inverse polynomial in
n
. This motives us to ask if a polynomial time

step can help bridge this gap. Indeed,
[
MS08
]
explicitly raised this question
7
7

7

see Question
6
in
[
MS08
]
and asked if it is possible to obtain a linear runtime in
n
, inverse polynomial failure probability, with polynomial time preprocessing, and still achieve the optimal factor of

We make partial progress towards this question and give an algorithm which uses polynomial preprocessing, uses the optimal number of samples, and with high-probability outputs a
3+\alpha
approximation in

time
|\mathcal{X}|\cdot n^{2-\Omega(\alpha)}
. This shows that (if preprocessing is allowed), we can go beyond computing all semi-distances to obtain an algorithm with a high-probability of success. We briefly note that the problem is not interesting with exponential time preprocessing, since we can enumerate all possible
O(\log(n)/\varepsilon^{2})
sized samples that can be observed and store the optimal answer for those samples.
8
8

8

[
MS08
]
give another exponential time preprocessing algorithm with linear-time queries where the preprocessing time does not depend on the underlying domain size.
This would even allow for
sublinear

It turns out that the algorithm of
[
MS08
]
can be abstracted to the following problem which is sufficient to solve hypothesis selection:

Definition 1.13
.

We are given a weighted, undirected complete graph on n vertices. Assume that the edge-weights are distinct. We preprocess the weighted graph and then play the following game with an adversary until only one vertex remains: we report the edge with the largest weight and the adversary chooses one of the endpoints of the edge and removes it from the graph (together with all the adjacent edges).

The hope is to use polynomial time pre-processing to simulate the tournament revelation problem. However, as stated, the problem is far too general. But in the case of hypothesis selection, we can use the additional available

structure: the known hypothesis
H_{i}
can be viewed as vectors in

, where recall
\mathcal{X}
is the domain. This additional structure allows us to simulate the tournament revelation problem in overall subquadratic time.

In more detail, the geometric viewpoint means it suffices to quickly find the diameter of a dynamic point set under arbitrary deletions. To obtain an overall subquadratic time bound, each operation must operate in sublinear in
n
time. This is not possible to do exactly with known techniques in computational geometry, but it is possible to obtain

One minor point is that the

1.4.4
Near Linear Time with Known OPT

In the case where
\mathsf{OPT}
is revealed to us as part of the input, we show that it is possible to further improve the runtime of the algorithm, getting the optimal approximation factor
C=3
while avoiding the polynomial dependence on
\delta
in the runtime. The algorithm is a variant of an idea from
[
ABS24
]
which, without the assumption that
\mathsf{OPT}
is known, obtains
C=4
in a similar runtime. Let us for the discussion again assume that we have oracle to the exact semi-distances
w_{i\rightarrow j}
.

Consider the directed graph
G=(V,E)
on
V=[n]
that has a directed edge
(i,j)
if and only if
w_{i\rightarrow j}>OPT
. From the discussion in the preliminaries section, it suffices to find a
j
such that there is no edge going from
i^{*}
to
j
and return
H_{j}
. Also from that section, we know the additional piece of information that
i^{*}
has no incoming edges. We first search for a
j_{1}
such that
j_{1}
has less than
n/2
incoming edges. This can be done by iterating over all
j\in[n]
and for each such
j
sampling
O(\log n)
nodes
i\in[n]
and checking whether
i
has an edge to
j
. Note that this search always succeeds since
i^{*}
has the desired property. Inductively, assuming we have found a
j_{k}
with at most
n/2^{k}
incoming edges, we define
S_{k}=\{i\mid(i,j_{k})\in G\}
which has size
\leq n/2^{k}
and search for an element
j_{k+1}
in
S_{k}
that has less than
n/2^{k+1}
incoming edges. To test whether a single
j\in S_{k}
for this property, we must sample
O(2^{k+1}\log n)
random
i\in[n]
and check if they have an edge to
j_{k}
. However, the fact that
S_{k}
only has size
n/2^{k}
ensures that the total runtime of this step still becomes
O(n\log n)
. Now two things can happen. Either this process continues for
O(\log n)
steps culminating in

eventually. But in this case

has no incoming edges and we are happy to return

. Alternatively, we eventually don’t succeed in finding

, but in this case

cannot lie in

— if it did, the search would find it if nothing else. But by the definition of

, this means that
j_{k}
doesn’t have and edge to

and so we can return
i^{*}
. The total runtime for the

assuming oracle access to

in constant, so the final runtime becomes
O(n\log^{3}n/\varepsilon^{2})
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For two given parameters
\beta
and
\gamma
, there is an algorithm that uses

many queries to semi-distances and provide an estimate

for the average degree such that the following holds with probability

•

, then

is a constant approximation of

\hat{d}\in[\overline{d}/2,2\,\overline{d}]

•

If we have

then

Similarly, one can estimate the out-degree of a vertex

is a constant approximation to

\hat{d}\in[\mathrm{deg}^{-}(u_{i})/2,2\,\mathrm{deg}^{-}(u_{i})]
.

•

If
\mathrm{deg}^{-}(u_{i})<\gamma/2
, then
\hat{d}<\gamma
.

The identical statement also holds for in-degree estimation.

Proof.

Let
T\geq C\log(1/\beta)/\gamma
for a sufficiently large constant

chosen later. Let

be the random variable where each

In the first case where

\mathbf{Pr}\!\left[|\hat{d}-\overline{d}|\geq\overline{d}/2\right]\leq 2e^{-\frac{T\overline{d}}{12}},

which implies that if as long as

with probability

. Since

In the second case, the upper tail of the Chernoff bound gives

\mathbf{Pr}\!\left[\hat{d}\geq\gamma\right]\leq\mathbf{Pr}\!\left[\hat{d}\geq\left(1+\left(\frac{\gamma}{\overline{d}}-1\right)\right)\cdot\overline{d}\right]\leq e^{-\Theta(\overline{d}\cdot T\cdot\frac{\gamma}{\overline{d}})\leq e^{-\Theta(T\cdot\gamma)}},

so again our choice of

2.4
Finding a hypothesis with average degree promptingness

The goal of this section is to
find
a vertex
u_{i}\in U
with
\mathrm{deg}^{-}(u_{i})\geq\Omega(\overline{d})
in roughly
\tilde{O}(1/\overline{d})
semi-distance queries (see
Lemma

2.6
for the precise bound). To do so, we first partition the degrees in
U
in geometrically decreasing values based on their fractional degrees. This is similar in spirit to "level sets" arguments commonly used in sublinear algorithms, e.g. see the description of the "work investment strategy" of
[
BRY14
]
.

More formally, recall that
\overline{d}
is the average fractional out-degree of the vertices in
U
. Note the prior subsection helps us test the value of
\overline{d}
, which will be crucially used in the final algorithm of the next subsection.

Now consider a partition of the interval
[0,1]
into
k\coloneqq\left\lceil{\log_{2}\left(1/\overline{d}\right)}\right\rceil+2

intervals
[0,1]=\bigcup_{r=1}^{k}\{I_{r}\}
as follows:

\displaystyle I_{r}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\left(2^{-r},\,2^{-r+1}\right]&\quad\quad r\in\{1,\dots,k-1\}\,,\\
\left[0,\,2^{-r+1}\right]&\quad\quad r=k.\end{cases}

The intervals naturally partition the vertices as
U=\bigcup_{r=1}^{k}\{U_{r}\}
, via

U_{r}\coloneqq\left\{u_{i}\in U\mid\mathrm{deg}^{-}(u_{i})\in I_{r}\right\}\,.

Note we may not explicitly know this partition, but all we require is that such a partition exists. The first lemma states that some partition has relatively large fraction of all the vertices.

Lemma 2.5
.

There exists an
r^{*}\in\{1,\cdots,k-1\}
satisfying

\frac{|U_{r^{*}}|}{|U|}\geq\frac{2^{r^{*}}\cdot\overline{d}}{4\cdot\log_{2}(1/\overline{d})}.

Proof.

We can write the average degree
\overline{d}
, based on the contribution of the vertices in each of these subsets
U_{r}
as follows:

\displaystyle\left\lvert U\right\rvert\cdot\overline{d}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u_{i}\in U}\mathrm{deg}^{-}(u_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{k}\sum_{u_{i}\in U_{r}}\mathrm{deg}^{-}(u_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r=1}^{k}\left\lvert U_{r}\right\rvert\cdot 2^{-r+1}

\displaystyle\leq|U|\cdot 2^{-(k-1)}+\sum_{r=1}^{k-1}\left\lvert U_{r}\right\rvert\cdot 2^{-r+1}\,.

We can upper bound the first term by
|U|\cdot\overline{d}/2
using the definition of
k
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\overline{d}}\leq 2^{\left\lceil{\log_{2}\left(1/\overline{d}\right)}\right\rceil}\leq 2^{k-2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\left\lvert U\right\rvert\cdot 2^{-(k-1)}\leq\frac{\left\lvert U\right\rvert\cdot\overline{d}}{2}.

Combining the previous two equations, we have:

\frac{\left\lvert U\right\rvert\cdot\overline{d}}{2}\leq\sum_{r=1}^{k-1}\left\lvert U_{r}\right\rvert\cdot 2^{-r+1}\leq(k-1)\cdot\max_{1\leq r\leq k-1}|U_{r}|\cdot 2^{-r+1}.

2.5
Finding prompting hypothesis via neighbor set

In this section, we propose another approach for finding a prompting hypothesis. More precisely, we find a vertex with out-degree
\mathrm{deg}^{-}(u_{i})
at least
\tilde{O}(\beta)
with probability
1-\beta
. The main difference between this approach and the one we describe in the previous section is that the quality of the vertex we find remains the same regardless of the average degree, however its query complexity varies depending on the average degree of the underlying graph. The query complexity of this approach is
\tilde{O}(n+n\overline{d}/\beta)
, which makes this approach suitable for scenarios where the average degree is low, i.e.,
\bar{d}\ll\beta\approx\delta
. When the average degree is this small, attempting to approximate the degree of all vertices in
U
by querying semi-distances would be too costly.

At a high-level, the algorithm samples a vertex
v_{j}
from the bottom set,
{V}

U
, to find all of its neighbors, which we denote as
\text{Nei}(v_{j})

t=O(\log(1/\beta))
times to make sure that the one with the minimum-sized neighbor set,
v_{j_{\min}}
, certainly has a small set of neighbors.

Once we have the neighbor set, two possibilities arise. First, there might be a
\tilde{O}(\beta)
-prompting hypothesis among the neighbors. Given the small size of the neighbor set, we can efficiently test each neighbor to see if it is a prompting one (i.e., if its estimated degree
\hat{d}_{u_{i}}
exceeds a threshold
\tilde{O}(\beta)
). If such a vertex is found, the algorithm returns it and halts.

Second, it’s possible that no prompting hypothesis exists in the neighbor set. In this case, while the immediate search for a prompting hypothesis has failed, the sampled vertex
v_{j}
has a useful property: it is unlikely to have an edge from
u_{i^{*}}
, meaning it cannot be prompted past our threshold,

H_{i^{*}}

Gaining Intuition via Small

Solution for

For

Then, assuming

(which is an arbitrary choice since all possible

\displaystyle p_{1}\left\lparen 1+\frac{W(H_{1})}{W(H_{3})}\right\rparen

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{E}\!\left[d_{\textit{TV}}(P,H)\right]}{\mathsf{OPT}}

6W(H_{1})W(H_{2})W(H_{3})\leq\sum_{i\neq j}W(H_{i})^{2}W(H_{j}).

Therefore,

\frac{\mathbf{E}\!\left[\mathbf{r}\right]{d_{\textit{TV}}(P,H)}}{\mathsf{OPT}}\leq 1+\frac{4}{3}.

The problem with

Now solving the

Thus, to summarize

, two challenges remain to generalize the approach to general

1.

Can we get a handle on the solution to the system given in

Generalizing to Arbitrary

We handle challenge (1) first. We present an explicit solution to the system given in
Equation

4
, which will be an explicit formula for the probabilities
p_{1},\cdots,p_{n}
in terms of the max-semi distances of
Definition

1.7
. Initially, some

can be negative and later we will deal with challenge (2) to round these values to be a proper probability distribution. Again, we remark that this section can be safely skipped as it is just for intuition, and the full proof details are given in

.

Let
A
be the matrix of
Equation

4
defined as
A_{ij}=1+\frac{W(H_{j})}{W(H_{i})}
for
i\neq j
and
0
for
i=j
. Our goal is to find probabilities
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
such that
Ap
is a constant vector with
\sum_{i}p_{i}=1
. Note that without loss of generality, we can solve for
Ap=\mathbf{1}
and then normalize the
p_{i}
’s.

We prove the following lemma, giving an explicit solution to the system
Equation

4
.

See
1.12

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{W}
to be the vector

\mathbf{W}=(1/W(H_{1}),\ldots,1/W(H_{n})).

Note that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}=1
by construction so we just have to check that
p_{i}
solves the system of
Equation

4
. We have

(A\mathbf{1})_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}\left(1+\frac{W(H_{j})}{W(H_{i})}\right)=(n-1)+\frac{1}{W(H_{i})}\left(D-W(H_{i})\right)=n-2+\frac{D}{W(H_{i})},

so

A\mathbf{1}=(n-2)\mathbf{1}+D\mathbf{W}.

(7)

We also have

(A\mathbf{W})_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}\left(1+\frac{W(H_{j})}{W(H_{i})}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{W(H_{j})}=\sum_{j\neq i}\left(\frac{1}{W(H_{i})}+\frac{1}{W(H_{j})}\right)=\frac{n-2}{W(H_{i})}+C,

meaning

A\mathbf{W}=(n-2)\mathbf{W}+C\mathbf{1}.

(8)

Combining equations (
7
) and (
8
) gives us

A(D\mathbf{W}-(n-2)\mathbf{1})=(DC-(n-2)^{2})\mathbf{1}.

Since
DC\geq n^{2}>(n-2)^{2}
by Cauchy-Schwarz, we know that
p=D\mathbf{W}-(n-2)\mathbf{1}
is a valid solution to
Ap=\mathbf{1}
. Since

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{D}{W(H_{i})}-(n-2)\right)=DC-n(n-2),

as desired.
∎

Note that it is entirely possible for some of the

to be negative, as in the

3.1.2
Proof of the Main
Theorem

1.2

We are now ready to prove our main theorem,
Theorem

1.2
. There are three steps to the proof.

•

Step 1: We show that
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, ignoring the fact that it is not a valid distribution, is able to give a
3-\frac{2}{n}
approximation factor.

•

Step 2: We show how to round
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
to a a valid distribution
\{q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
which maintains the same approximation factor.

•

Step 3: Lastly, we show how to we can form
\{q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
using the approximated max-semi distances instead, introducing an additive
+\varepsilon
factor in the bound of
Theorem

1.2
.

We begin with the first step.

Lemma 3.1

(Step 1)
.

Define
p_{i}
as in Equation
5
. We have

1+\sum_{i\neq i^{*}}p_{i}\left\lparen 1+\frac{W(H_{i})}{W(H_{i^{*}})}\right\rparen\leq 3-\frac{2}{n}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, suppose
i^{*}=n
. Then,

p_{i}\left(1+\frac{W(H_{i})}{W(H_{n})}\right)=\frac{\left(W(H_{i})+W(H_{n})\right)\left(\frac{D}{W(H_{i})}-(n-2)\right)}{W(H_{n})\left(DC-n(n-2)\right)},

which gives

\sum_{i\neq n}p_{i}\left(1+\frac{W(H_{i})}{W(H_{n})}\right)=\frac{1}{W(H_{n})\left(DC-n(n-2)\right)}\cdot\sum_{i\neq n}\left(W(H_{i})+W(H_{n})\right)\left(\frac{D}{W(H_{i})}-(n-2)\right).

Let’s calculate the numerator. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\neq n}\left(W(H_{i})+W(H_{n})\right)\left(\frac{D}{W(H_{i})}-(n-2)\right)

\displaystyle=D(n-1)+DW(H_{n})\left(C-\frac{1}{W(H_{n})}\right)-(n-2)(D-W(H_{n}))-(n-2)(n-1)W(H_{n})

\displaystyle=W(H_{n})(DC-(n-2)^{2}).

Plugging back in, the sum we want has value

\frac{DC-(n-2)^{2}}{DC-n(n-2)}=1+\frac{2(n-2)}{DC-n(n-2)}\leq 1+\frac{n-2}{n},

where the last inequality follows from

DC\geq n^{2}\implies DC-n(n-2)\geq 2n\implies\frac{2(n-2)}{DC-n(n-2)}\leq\frac{n-2}{n}.

Thus,

1+\sum_{i\neq n}p_{i}\left(1+\frac{W(H_{i})}{W(H_{n})}\right)\leq 3-\frac{2}{n},

as desired.
∎

Again, The main issue with the setting of the probabilities given in
Equation

5
is that some
p_{i}
values can be negative. We show how to find a set of non-negative probabilities that also give an approximation ratio at most
3-2/n
. First, let’s recall key formulas:

p_{i}(n)=\frac{D/W(H_{i})-(n-2)}{DC-n(n-2)},\quad C=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{W(H_{i})},\quad D=\sum_{i=1}^{n}W(H_{i}).

Suppose without loss of generality that

W(H_{n})\geq W(H_{n-1})\geq\ldots\geq W(H_{1}).

We call an index
k\in[n]
good if it satisfies

\forall j\in[k],\quad(k-3)W(H_{j})\leq\sum_{i\in[k]\setminus\{j\}}W(H_{i}),

and otherwise, bad. Note that

•

k\leq 3
is always good.

•

If
k
is bad then
k^{\prime}
is also bad for all
k^{\prime}>k
. This is true since
k
being bad means there exists a
j\in[k]
such that

\displaystyle(k-3)W(H_{j})>\sum_{i\in[k]\setminus\{j\}}W(H_{i})\implies(k-2)W(H_{j})>\sum_{i=1}^{k}W(H_{i})\implies(k-2)W(H_{k+1})>\sum_{i=1}^{k}W(H_{i}).

Now given the values
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
of Equation
5
, we consider the following rounding procedure procedure:

1.

If
n
is good then all the probabilities are non-negative so simply set
q_{i}=p_{i}(n)
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.

2.

Otherwise, consider the largest good
k
. Let
q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}
be the values
p_{1}(k),\ldots,p_{k}(k)
(probabilities for the
k
case) and set
q_{k+1}=\ldots=q_{n}=0
. Note that we only use the formula for the
k
case but plug in the fixed
W(H_{i})
values from the
n
case.

Figure 1:
The rounding procedure takes values
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and outputs a valid distribution
\{q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

Since
k
is good, we know all
q_{i}\geq 0
. The goal is to show that our desired approximation ratio under
q

3-2/n
.

Lemma 3.2

(Step 2)
.

Define
q_{i}
as above. We have

1+\sum_{i\neq i^{*}}q_{i}\left\lparen 1+\frac{W(H_{i})}{W(H_{i^{*}})}\right\rparen\leq 3-\frac{2}{n}.

Proof.

We consider two cases. If the optimum hypothesis is among the first
k
, then our distribution already achieves approximation ratio
3-2/k\leq 3-2/n
from Theorem
3.1
. So suppose the optimum index is some
i^{*}>k
. We wish to bound

1+\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}\left(1+\frac{W(H_{i})}{W(H_{i^{*}})}\right).

Since
k+1
is bad, we know that there exists a
j\in[k+1]
satisfying

(k-2)W(H_{j})>\sum_{i\in[k+1]\setminus\{j\}}W(H_{i})\implies(k-1)W(H_{k+1})\geq(k-1)W(H_{j})>\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}W(H_{i})

and thus

(k-2)W(H_{i^{*}})\geq(k-2)W(H_{k+1})>\sum_{i=1}^{k}W(H_{i})\implies W(H_{i^{*}})>\frac{1}{k-2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}W(H_{i}).

Define
D^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}W(H_{i})
and similarly
C^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}1/W(H_{i})
. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}\left(1+\frac{W(H_{i})}{W(H_{i^{*}})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}\left(1+\frac{(k-2)W(H_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}W(H_{i})}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{\frac{D^{\prime}}{W(H_{i})}-(k-2)}{D^{\prime}C^{\prime}-k(k-2)}\right)\left(1+\frac{(k-2)W(H_{i})}{D^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D^{\prime}(D^{\prime}C^{\prime}-k(k-2))}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{D^{\prime}}{W(H_{i})}-(k-2)\right)(D^{\prime}+(k-2)W(H_{i}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D^{\prime}(D^{\prime}C^{\prime}-k(k-2))}\cdot\left((D^{\prime})^{2}C^{\prime}-(k-2)^{2}D^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{D^{\prime}C^{\prime}-(k-2)^{2}}{D^{\prime}C^{\prime}-k(k-2)}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{2(k-2)}{D^{\prime}C^{\prime}-k(k-2)}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{k-2}{k}.

where the last inequality follows as in Theorem
3.1
since
D^{\prime}C^{\prime}\geq k^{2}
. The lemma follows.
∎

Finally, the last step shows that it suffices to compute the distribution
\{q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
using noisy semi-distances. Towards that end, consider the values
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
defined in
Equation

5

\{\tilde{p}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be the corresponding values where we use the same formula, but instead use approximated max-semi distances satisfying
\forall i,W(H_{i})\leq\hat{W}(H_{i})\leq W(H_{i})+\varepsilon
. Now run the same rounding procedure as in
Figure

1
on

to produce
\{\tilde{q}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Note that this is a valid operation since the rounding procedure described in

only depends on the semi-distances. So we can still define a good index

as done there, but with respect to the approximate max semi-distances, and round

1+\sum_{i\neq i^{*}}\hat{q}_{i}\left\lparen 1+\frac{\hat{W}(H_{i})}{\hat{W}(H_{i^{*}})}\right\rparen\leq 3-\frac{2}{n}.

(9)

are also a ‘valid’ set of hypothesis to use for the

semi-distances (which determines the actual expected value approximation factor). Thus, the equation in

Lemma 3.3

(Step 3)
.

Consider the distribution
\{\tilde{q}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
defined above. We have

\sum_{i\neq i^{*}}\hat{q}_{i}\left\lparen W(H_{i^{*}})+W(H_{i})\right\rparen\leq\left(2-\frac{2}{n}\right)\cdot W(H_{i^{*}})+2\varepsilon.

Proof.

Note that all values
\{\tilde{q}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are non-negative by construction. By subtracting
1
,

9
implies

\sum_{i\neq i^{*}}\hat{q}_{i}\left\lparen\hat{W}(H_{i^{*}})+\hat{W}(H_{i})\right\rparen\leq\left(2-\frac{2}{n}\right)\cdot\hat{W}(H_{i^{*}}).

Since we can assume (by shifting and adjusting

) that

\forall i,W(H_{i})\leq\hat{W}(H_{i})\leq W(H_{i})+\varepsilon,

we have

\sum_{i\neq i^{*}}\hat{q}_{i}\left\lparen W(H_{i^{*}})+W(H_{i})\right\rparen\leq\sum_{i\neq i^{*}}\hat{q}_{i}\left\lparen\hat{W}(H_{i^{*}})+\hat{W}(H_{i})\right\rparen

.

We use the probabilities

3.2
Lower Bound

In this section, we show that the algorithm described above is optimal in the following senses.
Any proper algorithm which takes a number of samples which do not depend on the underlying domain size (this is
the key characteristic
of hypothesis selection), must incur an expected approximation factor of
3-2/n-o(1/n)
.
This is formalized in
Theorem

3.4
and
Corollary

3.5
. We additionally show in Theorem
B.2
that
\Omega(\log(n)/\varepsilon^{2})
samples are necessary for an algorithm achieving
any constant
multiplicative factor expected value guarantee.

We begin with our lower bound against the expected approximation factor of algorithms with domain-independent sample complexity.
In fact, we prove a slightly stronger statement: there exist a set of hypotheses and a family of true distributions such that (a) all but one hypothesis
H_{i^{*}}
incurs an approximation factor which is nearly
3
and (b) for a large enough domain size and a uniformly random true distribution from the family, it is impossible guess the optimal index
i^{*}
from samples with probability better than
1/n
.
Our construction is a generalization of that of
[
BKM19
]
from
2
hypotheses to general
n
and is heavily inspired by this prior work.
However, we change the construction and analysis in a subtle but key way. At the end of this section in
Remark

3.8
, we describe the issue with the prior proof.

Theorem 3.4
.

Fix any
\alpha,C>0
.
Then, over a domain of size
d\gg n/\alpha
, there exists hypotheses
H_{1},\ldots,H_{n}
, and distributions-over-distributions
\mathcal{S}_{1},\ldots\mathcal{S}_{n}
with the following properties.
For every
i\neq j\in[n]
and true distribution
P_{i}\sim\mathcal{S}_{i}
,

\frac{d_{\textit{TV}}\left\lparen P_{i},H_{j}\right\rparen}{d_{\textit{TV}}\left\lparen P_{i},H_{i}\right\rparen}\geq 3-\alpha.

Furthermore, given fewer than
o\left\lparen\sqrt{\frac{d}{n/\alpha}}\right\rparen
samples from
P_{i^{*}}\sim\mathcal{S}_{i^{*}}
for
i^{*}\sim\mathrm{Unif}([n])
, no randomized algorithm can correctly guess the index
i^{*}
with probability more than
\frac{1}{n}\left\lparen 1+o(1)\right\rparen
.

Given this theorem, the in-expectation lower bound follows directly.

Corollary 3.5
.

Given
o\left\lparen\frac{\sqrt{d}}{n^{1.1}}\right\rparen
samples, no randomized algorithm can solve the hypothesis selection problem with expected approximation factor less than
3-\frac{2}{n}-o\left\lparen\frac{1}{n}\right\rparen
.

Proof.

For the sake of contradiction, assume that there exists such an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
which uses
o\left\lparen\frac{\sqrt{d}}{n^{1.1}}\right\rparen
samples and has expected approximation factor at most
3-\frac{2}{n}-\frac{C}{n}
for some constant
C>0
.
Consider the hard setting of
Theorem

3.4
with
\alpha=1/n^{1.2}
.
The theorem implies that no algorithm with
o\left\lparen\frac{\sqrt{d}}{n^{1.1}}\right\rparen
samples can guess the index of the true distribution
i^{*}
with probability greater than
\frac{1}{n}\left\lparen 1+o(1)\right\rparen
.

Let
i^{*}
be the index of the true distribution.
If
\mathcal{A}
selects
H_{i^{*}}
, the approximation ratio is
1
.
Otherwise, the approximation ratio is at least
3-1/n^{1.1}
.
Let
p
be the probability that
\mathcal{A}
selects
H_{i^{*}}
.
By the expected performance of
\mathcal{A}
:

\displaystyle p+(1-p)\left\lparen 3-\frac{1}{n^{1.1}}\right\rparen\leq 3-\frac{2+C}{n}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle p\left\lparen-2+\frac{1}{n^{1.1}}\right\rparen\leq-\frac{2+C}{n}+\frac{1}{n^{1.1}}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle p\geq\frac{\frac{2+C}{n}-\frac{1}{n^{1.1}}}{2-\frac{1}{n^{1.1}}}=\frac{1}{n}\left\lparen\frac{2+C-\frac{1}{n^{0.1}}}{2-\frac{1}{n^{1.1}}}\right\rparen\geq\frac{1}{n}\left\lparen 1+\frac{C}{2}-\frac{1}{n^{0.1}}\right\rparen.

This violates
Theorem

3.4
, so we have reached a contradiction.
∎

We will make use of the following lemma about indistinguishability and multi-dimensional couplings.
This is an extension of the classic relation between TV distance, couplings, and indistinguishability to
n
rather than
2
distributions.

Definition 3.6

(Multi-dimensional Couplings and Coincident Subsets)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{D}_{n}
be distributions over the same domain
\mathcal{X}
.
A distribution
\mathcal{Q}
over
\mathcal{X}^{n}
is a
multi-dimensional coupling
of
\mathcal{D}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{D}_{n}
if, for all
i\in[n]
and for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\mathbf{Pr}_{A\sim\mathcal{Q}}\!\left[A_{i}=x\right]=\mathbf{Pr}_{X\sim\mathcal{D}_{i}}\!\left[X=x\right].

Given a multi-dimensional coupling
\mathcal{Q}
, a subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is a
coincident subset
if, for all
x\in S
and for all
i\in[n]
,

\mathbf{Pr}_{A\sim Q}\!\left[A_{i}=x\right]=\mathbf{Pr}_{A\sim Q}\!\left[\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}A_{j}=x\right].

(10)

Lemma 3.7

(Multi-dimensional Coupling Indistinguishability)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{D}_{n}
be distributions over
\mathcal{X}
, let
\mathcal{Q}
be a multi-dimensional coupling of these distributions, and let
S\subseteq\mathcal{X}
be a coincident subset.
Let
\gamma=\mathbf{Pr}_{A\sim Q}\!\left[\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\notin S\right]
be the total mass of elements not in
S
.
Consider any (randomized) algorithm
\mathcal{A}(x;r)

Proof.

By the definition of a multi-dimensional coupling and coincident subset, for any

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}_{i^{*}\sim\mathrm{Unif}\left\lparen[n]\right\rparen,X\sim\mathcal{D}_{i},r}\!\left[\mathcal{A}(X;r)=i^{*}|X\in S\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Pr}_{i^{*}\sim\mathrm{Unif}\left\lparen[n]\right\rparen,Y\sim\mathcal{E},r}\!\left[\mathcal{A}(Y;r)=i^{*}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{Pr}_{i^{*}\sim\mathrm{Unif}\left\lparen[n]\right\rparen,Y\sim\mathcal{E},r}\!\left[\left\lparen\mathcal{A}(Y;r)=i\right\rparen\cap\left\lparen i^{*}=i\right\rparen\right]

Remark 3.8
.

This theorem is a generalization of the lower bound in
[
BKM19
]
for
n=2
.
We now present the issue with that analysis and how our result avoids it.

Consider our construction also with
n=2
.
The key difference is that in the prior construction, the extreme elements for each distribution
P_{i}
are chosen uniformly at random within the meta-interval
T_{i}
, and there are no sub-intervals
T_{i}^{j}
.
In our construction, there is a single extreme element chosen within each sub-interval.
The analysis in
[
BKM19
]
argues that, the distribution
\mathcal{D}_{i}^{s}
of
s
samples from a randomly chosen distribution
P_{i}
conditioned on sampling no duplicate elements is the same as the distribution of
s
samples from the uniform distribution
U
on
[d]
, also conditioned on sampling no duplicate elements.
We argue that, for our construction, this holds among samples which contain no duplicate sub-intervals (no two sampled elements belong to the same sub-interval).

While both claims are true when
s=1
, for larger samples, the former claim is untrue.
Note that in their setting, every distribution
P_{i}
in the hard family of distributions has a fixed number
z
of domain element where its probability mass is zero. In particular, within all but some single meta-interval
T_{k}
, the distribution will have nonzero mass on every domain element. On
T_{k}
, there will be a fixed nonzero number of domain elements which have probability mass zero. Consider the extreme case where the sample size is
s=d-z
.
Consider the distribution of counts of elements in each meta-interval from a sample from
\mathcal{D}_{i}^{s}
conditioned on no collisions.
This count distribution will be supported only on a single element: namely, the count vector which has full count on every meta-interval other than
T_{k}
.
On the other hand, the count distribution of the uniform distribution conditioned on no collisions is supported on many different count vectors as
s<d
.
Therefore, the distributions
\mathcal{D}_{i}^{s}
and a uniform sample of
s
elements, both conditioned on no collisions, are not equivalent.

As an alternative counter-argument, one can consider the case where
s=2
and directly compute the probabilities conditional on no collisions of both samples lying in
T_{2i-1}
(where some extreme elements have probability
0
) as opposed to lying in
T_{2i}
(where some extreme elements have probability
2/d
), the latter of these probabilities being slightly larger due to sampling without replacement. By symmetry, this is not the case for the uniform distribution.

The error occurs in the first two sentences of Claim 26 in
[
BKM19
]
which extends the claim from
s=1
to general
s
.

The introduction of sub-intervals containing a single extreme element in our construction alleviates this problem by introducing independence.
Each sub-interval has probability exactly
\ell/d
, so conditioning on no collisions, the collection of sub-intervals that we sample from is a uniformly random size
s
subset of all sub-intervals. Moreover, the random choices of the functions
f^{j},g^{j}
for
j=1,\dots,k
ensure that elements sampled within the intervals are independent and uniformly random within these intervals. Sampling from the uniform distribution, conditioned on no duplicate sub-intervals, can be simulated by first sampling
s
sub-intervals without replacement, and then independently sampling a uniformly random element within each of these: this exact same probabilistic process generates a sample from
D_{i}^{s}
conditioned on no sub-interval collisions.

S_{k}=\{i\in[n]\setminus\{j\}\mid\hat{w}_{i\rightarrow j}>\mathsf{OPT}+\varepsilon_{0}\}
and further define

. In this case, if

. Otherwise, if no such

, and our algorithm returns

as the final output. We will show shortly that with high probability, if the algorithm does not halt, then
S_{k_{0}}=\emptyset
for some for

.

Define

\lambda_{j}^{\prime}=\frac{|\{i\in[n]\setminus\{j\}\mid\hat{w}_{i\rightarrow j}>\mathsf{OPT}+\varepsilon_{0}\}|}{n-1}.

We state and prove two claims bounding the probability of two error events over the randomness of the algorithm.

Claim 4.1
.

With probability at least
1-\delta_{0}
, for all
1\leq k\leq\lceil\lg n\rceil
, such that
S_{k}
exists, we have that
|S_{k}|<n/2^{k}
.

Proof of Claim.

Suppose
j
with
H_{j}\in S_{k-1}
is such that
\lambda_{j}^{\prime}>1/2^{k}
. Then, a Chernoff bound gives that with probability at least
1-\delta_{0}/n^{2}
, it holds that
\lambda_{j}>1/2^{k+1}
as long as
\alpha
is sufficiently large, and thus such a

is not used to define
S_{k}
. In particular, union bounding over all such

, we obtain that if

exists, then

with probability

. Crudely union bounding over all

gives the result.
∎

Claim 4.2
.

With probability at least
1-\delta_{0}
, there exists no
k\leq\lceil\lg n\rceil
such that for some
H_{j}\in S_{k-1}
, it holds that
\lambda_{j}^{\prime}\leq 1/2^{k+2}
but where the algorithm halts before constructing
S_{k}
.

Proof of Claim.

If there exists a

H_{j}\in S_{k}
such that
\lambda_{j}^{\prime}\leq 1/2^{k+2}

(if

gives the result.
∎

Let
E_{0}
denote the event that

for all distinct

. Let

.

Case 1: The algorithm does not halt.

Since

for some

.
Now by definition,

and thus

Case 2: The algorithm halts.

Note first that the algorithm does not halt when constructing

. Indeed,
H_{i^{*}}\in S_{0}
and

, so for

. The algorithm then returns

which is well-defined since

.
Since

did not occur, for all

\lambda_{j}^{\prime}>1/2^{k+2}

\lambda_{i^{*}}=0

S_{k-1}=\{i\in[n]\setminus\{j_{k-2}\}\mid\hat{w}_{i\rightarrow j_{k-2}}>\mathsf{OPT}+\varepsilon_{0}\}

and the result now follows from

.

Running time.

We next argue about the runtime of the algorithm. Estimating a single semi-distance based on the samples takes time
O(\frac{\log(n/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}})
, so estimating a single

takes time
O(\frac{2^{k}\log^{2}(n/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}})
, and the time to estimate

for all

is thus

assuming that

which thus takes time

, but this is of lower order than the previous bound. Summing over all

.
∎

Appendix B
Any Constant Approximation Lower Bound

We briefly remark that
\Omega(\log(n)/\varepsilon^{2})
samples are necessary to achieve any multiplicative factor expected value guarantee. This follows from standard lower bounds from learning discrete distributions and has already been noted to imply lower bounds for hypothesis selection, e.g. see
[
CDSS14
]
. Nevertheless, it hasn’t been stated explicitly for the expected value case to the best of our knowledge, so we present the full details below for completeness.

First let’s define the following ‘hard’ set
\mathcal{H}_{k}=\{h_{1},\cdots,h_{2^{k}}\}
of hypotheses. We set
k
to be the domain size (suppose it is even for simplicity) and let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be sufficiently small. Split the domain into
k/2
pairs of size
2
of consecutive elements. For every pair
\{i,i+1\}
,
\mathcal{H}_{k}
consists of all distributions that either have probability mass
\left(\frac{1+\varepsilon}{k},\frac{1-\varepsilon}{k}\right)
or
\left(\frac{1-\varepsilon}{k},\frac{1+\varepsilon}{k}\right)
on
i,i+1
respectively. Thus
|\mathcal{H}_{k}|=2^{k/2}
.

The following is a folklore result.

Theorem B.1
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be an algorithm that draws
m
samples from a discrete distribution
P\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
and with probability at least
9/10
outputs a
distribution
\hat{P}
at total variation distance at most
\varepsilon
from
P
. Then
m=\Omega(k/\varepsilon^{2})
.

Theorem B.2
.

Let
C>0
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be sufficiently small. Let
\mathcal{A}
be an algorithm that draws
m
samples from a discrete distribution
P\in H_{k}
and with probability at least
9/10
outputs a set of mixture weights
\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{|\mathcal{H}_{k}|}
such that

d_{\textit{TV}}\left(P,\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}h_{i}\right)\leq C\cdot\min_{i}d_{\textit{TV}}(P,h_{i})+\varepsilon.

Then
m=\Omega(k/\varepsilon^{2})=\Omega(\log(|\mathcal{H}_{k}|)/\varepsilon^{2})
.

Proof.

We note that
\min_{i}d_{\textit{TV}}(P,h_{i})=0
since
P\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
. Thus, if
\alpha_{1},\cdots\alpha_{|\mathcal{H}_{k}|}
are the mixture weights outputted by
\mathcal{A}
, we have
\hat{P}:=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}h_{i}
is a valid probability distribution which satisfies

d_{\textit{TV}}(P,\hat{P})\leq C\cdot\min_{i}d_{\textit{TV}}(P,h_{i})+\varepsilon=\varepsilon.

The lower bound now follows from
Theorem

B.1
.
∎
