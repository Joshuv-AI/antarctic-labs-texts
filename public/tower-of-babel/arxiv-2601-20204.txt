Mode-Wise Spectral Criteria for Coupled Mass Transport in Hybrid PDE–ODE Tumor Microenvironments
Jiguang Yu, Louis Shuo Wang, Zonghao Liu, Jingfeng Liu

Abstract
We study coupled mass transport in a tumor–microenvironment setting with two motile densities ( S , R ) (S,R) and non-motile state switching ( P , A ) (P,A) . The populations diffuse and undergo chemotactic drift; ( P , A ) (P,A) follow pointwise ODE switching. A decoupled inhibitory field D D satisfies a damped Neumann heat equation, giving maximum-principle bounds and exponential decay. Together with the pointwise invariant P + A P+A , these identities yield global existence, positivity, and long-time reduction to limiting ( S , R ) (S,R) kinetics with a unique globally attracting coexistence state. Neumann eigenmode reduction gives closed dispersion relations. The base ( S , R ) (S,R) reaction–diffusion block remains stable for all nonconstant modes for any d S , d R > 0 d_{S},d_{R}>0 , excluding classical Turing destabilization. Chemotaxis is posed via a diffusive cue c c , since ∇ A \nabla A is undefined for non-diffusive A A . In one-way damped coupling, the linearized mode matrix is block triangular and leaves the ( S , R ) (S,R) spectrum unchanged. Two-way coupling adds a feedback rank-one mobility correction, induces effective cross-diffusion, and admits mode growth. We give explicit trace/determinant criteria for unstable Laplacian modes and the resulting instability thresholds.

Despite extensive progress on diffusion-driven (Turing) mechanisms and on Keller–Segel-type chemotactic drift
(
42
;

)
, a unified mode-wise stability theory that combines reaction–diffusion dispersion relations with chemotaxis-induced transport within hybrid PDE–ODE frameworks featuring non-diffusive microenvironmental state transitions remains limited.
In particular, when motile populations are coupled to non-motile microenvironmental states through fast diffusive signals,
it is not a priori clear how classical Turing criteria interact with directional transport and with the presence (or absence) of feedback. In addition, recent hybrid PDE–ABM studies establish well-posedness and derive continuum limits for multiscale feedback models
(
67
)
, a unified mode-wise stability theory that transfers reaction–diffusion (Turing) criteria to chemotaxis-driven drift in hybrid PDE–ODE settings remains less developed.

We address this gap by introducing a diffusive chemoattractant
c(x,t)
(mathematically necessary to ensure
\nabla c
is well-defined),
generated by microenvironmental activation and, in feedback settings, by the populations, and by organizing the analysis according to a directionality dichotomy: one-way damped coupling, in which
c
evolves independently of the populations, versus two-way coupling, in which feedback from
(S,R)
to
c
induces effective cross-diffusion (in an appropriate fast-relaxation closure)
and can restore Turing-type instabilities.

Throughout, pattern formation refers to linear, mode-wise instability of a spatially homogeneous equilibrium under homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions (a Turing-type mechanism).
We explicitly distinguish this notion from chemotaxis-driven aggregation and blow-up phenomena in Keller–Segel systems, which are not the focus of the present work. Figure
1
compares diffusion-driven instability and chemotaxis-driven aggregation.

Our analysis connects a hybrid PDE–ODE reaction–diffusion core model with chemotaxis-driven transport through a directionality-based stability framework (Figure
2
). The main results are:

(C1)

Well-posedness and positivity.

We prove invariance of the positive cone and global well-posedness of mild solutions for the hybrid PDE–ODE system under standard Neumann compatibility assumptions.

(C2)

Long-time reduction and global convergence of kinetics.

Using exponential decay of the inhibitory signal and pointwise conservation in the non-motile subsystem, we derive a long-time reduction to the limiting
(S,R)
kinetics and establish global attractivity of the unique coexistence equilibrium.

(C3)

No diffusion-driven instability in the base reaction–diffusion subsystem.

We show that the coexistence equilibrium remains linearly asymptotically stable with respect to all nonconstant Laplacian modes for arbitrary positive diffusion coefficients; hence no classical Turing instability occurs in the base reaction–diffusion dynamics.

(C4)

One-way damped chemotaxis suppresses patterns.

Introducing a diffusive chemoattractant
c(x,t)
to formulate drift rigorously, we prove that unidirectional, damped coupling (no feedback from the populations to
c
) yields a block-triangular linearization and therefore cannot destabilize the population spectrum; diffusion/chemotaxis-driven patterns are suppressed.

(C5)

Two-way feedback recovers instability via explicit mode-wise criteria.

For bidirectional coupling, we derive explicit trace/determinant conditions characterizing when feedback generates effective cross-diffusion (in a fast-relaxation closure) and restores Turing-type instabilities for some nonconstant Laplacian mode.

Structural identities

We record two elementary structural properties of (

Lemma 2.2

(Pointwise conservation in the non-diffusive
(P,A)
subsystem)
.

Let

Then for a.e.

In particular, if

, and

\sup_{t\geq 0}\bigl(\|P(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}+\|A(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}\bigr)\leq\|P_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}+\|A_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}.

(14)

Proof.

Fix

2.4
Chemotaxis extensions with a diffusive signal
c

Chemotactic drift terms of Keller–Segel type require the spatial gradient of a signal field.
In the hybrid core model (
8
), however, the activated microenvironmental state
A(x,t)
is non-diffusive
and evolves by a pointwise ODE; in general one only has
A(\cdot,t)\in L^{\infty}(U)
, so
\nabla A
is not well-defined.
To formulate directed migration rigorously while preserving the interpretation of
A
as a non-motile state variable, we
introduce a diffusive chemoattractant
c(x,t)
and write chemotaxis in the form

-\chi_{S}\nabla\cdot(S\nabla c),\qquad-\chi_{R}\nabla\cdot(R\nabla c),

with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions
\partial_{\mathrm{n}}c=0
on
\partial U
.
The signal
c
may be generated by the microenvironmental activation
A
(one-way coupling) and, in feedback settings, may also
depend on the population densities (two-way coupling). The resulting formulations allow a clean separation between
directional coupling regimes and their mode-wise stability consequences.

2.4.1
Template I: one-way damped coupling (no feedback from
(S,R)
to
c
).

A prototypical unidirectional chemotaxis extension of (
8
) takes the form

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}S=d_{S}\Delta S-\chi_{S}\nabla\cdot(S\nabla c)+f_{S}(S,R),\\[4.0pt]
\partial_{t}R=d_{R}\Delta R-\chi_{R}\nabla\cdot(R\nabla c)+f_{R}(S,R),\\[4.0pt]
\partial_{t}c=d_{c}\Delta c+\kappa A-\rho c,\end{cases}\qquad\partial_{\mathrm{n}}S=\partial_{\mathrm{n}}R=\partial_{\mathrm{n}}D=\partial_{\mathrm{n}}c=0,

(23)

where
(D,P,A)
evolve as in (
8
) and
(f_{S},f_{R})
denote the corresponding reaction terms in the
S
- and

R
-equations (e.g. the right-hand sides in (
8
)).
Here
c
is produced by
A
and decays at rate
\rho>0
, so perturbations in
c
are damped and do not receive feedback from

(S,R)
. For robustness, we also consider the abstract damped signal model

\partial_{t}c=d_{c}\Delta c+\mathcal{Q}(c),\qquad\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(c^{*})<0,

which isolates the role of one-way damping in the linearization.

2.4.2
Template II: two-way coupling (feedback from
(S,R)
to
c
).

To capture positive feedback loops characteristic of chemotaxis-driven pattern formation, we also study
bidirectional couplings in which the signal depends on the populations:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}S=d_{S}\Delta S-\chi_{S}\nabla\cdot(S\nabla c)+f_{S}(S,R),\\[4.0pt]
\partial_{t}R=d_{R}\Delta R-\chi_{R}\nabla\cdot(R\nabla c)+f_{R}(S,R),\\[4.0pt]
\partial_{t}c=d_{c}\Delta c+q\,c+h(S,R),\qquad q<0,\end{cases}\qquad\partial_{\mathrm{n}}S=\partial_{\mathrm{n}}R=\partial_{\mathrm{n}}c=0,

(24)

where
h(S,R)
encodes signal production (or activation) by the populations and
q<0
enforces linear damping of
c
.

2.4.3
Fast-relaxation closure used in the mode-wise criteria.

For the purpose of explicit mode-wise instability criteria, it is convenient to consider a fast-relaxation regime for
the signal, e.g.
\varepsilon\,\partial_{t}c=d_{c}\Delta c+q\,c+h(S,R)
with
\varepsilon\ll 1
.
Formally, as
\varepsilon\to 0
one obtains a quasi-steady closure

c\approx\mathcal{L}^{-1}\!\bigl(-h(S,R)\bigr),\qquad\mathcal{L}\coloneqq d_{c}\Delta+q,

which, under local approximations (or parameter limits), yields an effective reduced dependence
c\approx g(S,R)
.
This fast-relaxation closure is justified in Appendix
B
.
In Section
3.4
we use this reduced closure to derive explicit trace/determinant conditions for the instability
of individual Neumann Laplacian modes; the one-way case is treated separately and leads to a block-triangular linearization
that suppresses pattern formation.

3.3
Absence of diffusion-driven (Turing) instability in the base reaction–diffusion subsystem

We show that the coexistence equilibrium of the limiting kinetics (

in the base reaction–diffusion subsystem.
In contrast to classical activator–inhibitor kinetics, the Jacobian at the coexistence equilibrium has strictly negative
diagonal entries, so diffusion only increases damping of nonconstant modes.

Theorem 3.8

(No diffusion-driven (Turing) instability at the coexistence equilibrium)
.

Assume
K>0
and
\alpha+\xi>0
so that (
27
) admits the coexistence equilibrium

\mathbb{K}^{*}=(S^{*},R^{*})
given by (
28

Consider the reaction–diffusion system

\partial_{t}S=d_{S}\Delta S+f_{S}(S,R),\qquad\partial_{t}R=d_{R}\Delta R+f_{R}(S,R),

Then, for any diffusion coefficients

(S(x,t),R(x,t))\equiv(S^{*},R^{*})

is linearly asymptotically stable with respect to all nonconstant Neumann Laplacian modes (i.e. all modes

with

). In particular, no diffusion-driven (Turing) instability occurs at
\mathbb{K}^{*}
.

Remark 3.9

(Sign mechanism in the dispersion relation)
.

In classical two-species Turing systems, diffusion can destabilize a linearly stable equilibrium because mixed signs in the Jacobian
interact with unequal diffusion and can force the constant term in the dispersion polynomial to become negative for some
\lambda_{k}>0

Here, the strict negativity of the diagonal derivatives (

the
\lambda_{k}
-coefficient in

is nonnegative and the quadratic (

) remains positive for all
\lambda_{k}>0
.

3.4
Directionality principle for chemotaxis with a diffusive signal

We now incorporate chemotaxis via the diffusive chemoattractant
c(x,t)
introduced in Section
2.4
.
Our main message is a directionality principle: in the one-way damped regime, chemotactic coupling does not alter the
population spectrum and patterns are suppressed; in contrast, two-way feedback can induce effective cross-diffusion and recover
Turing-type instabilities.
Our mode-wise stability analysis follows the classical spectral reduction under Neumann eigenmodes
and yields explicit algebraic criteria (trace/determinant or Routh–Hurwitz type).
For complementary developments on explicit algebraic–spectral thresholds and their robustness under
time discretization, see
(
69

.
Throughout we work under homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions. Figure

distinguishes the dispersion curves under the unidirectional and bidirectional feedback loop. The bidirectional feedback contains the self-reinforcing ingredients required for pattern formation, manifesting as positive eigenvalues in the dispersion curve.

3.4.1

Consider the one-way damped chemotaxis model

(One-way damped chemotaxis suppresses mode-wise pattern formation)

3.4.2
Two-way feedback: recovery of instability via explicit mode-wise criteria

To capture feedback loops characteristic of chemotaxis-driven patterning, we consider the reduced (fast-relaxation) closure
in which the diffusive signal satisfies

locally, yielding the two-species cross-diffusion system

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}S=d_{S}\Delta S-\chi_{S}\nabla\cdot\bigl(S\nabla g(S,R)\bigr)+f_{S}(S,R),\\[3.0pt]

(Section

via fast relaxation; a mode-wise elimination for the full PDE system is placed Appendix

Theorem 3.11

(Two-way feedback recovers Turing-type instability: trace/determinant criteria)
.

Assume (
33
) admits a spatially homogeneous interior equilibrium
\mathbf{u}^{*}=(S^{*},R^{*})
.
Let

\mathcal{J}_{F}\coloneqq D(f_{S},f_{R})(S^{*},R^{*})=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix},\qquad g_{S}^{*}\coloneqq\partial_{S}g(S^{*},R^{*}),\quad g_{R}^{*}\coloneqq\partial_{R}g(S^{*},R^{*}),

\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{J}_{F})<0,\qquad\det(\mathcal{J}_{F})>0.

For each Neumann mode
k\geq 2
with eigenvalue

\mathcal{A}(\lambda_{k})=\mathcal{J}_{F}-\lambda_{k}(\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{H}),\qquad\mathcal{D}\coloneqq\mathrm{diag}(d_{S},d_{R}),

\mathcal{H}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{S}g_{S}^{*}S^{*}&\chi_{S}g_{R}^{*}S^{*}\\[4.0pt]

\mathbf{u}^{*}
is unstable for some nonconstant mode (i.e. a Turing-type instability occurs) if and only if

(a)

Trace condition:

\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{A}(\lambda_{k}))=\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{J}_{F})-\lambda_{k}(d_{S}+d_{R})+\lambda_{k}\bigl(\chi_{S}g_{S}^{*}S^{*}+\chi_{R}g_{R}^{*}R^{*}\bigr)>0.

(b)

\det(\mathcal{A}(\lambda))=A_{1}\lambda^{2}+A_{2}\lambda+\det(\mathcal{J}_{F}),\qquad\lambda\geq 0,

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=d_{S}d_{R}-d_{S}\chi_{R}g_{R}^{*}R^{*}-d_{R}\chi_{S}g_{S}^{*}S^{*},

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=-ad_{R}-dd_{S}+a\chi_{R}g_{R}^{*}R^{*}+d\chi_{S}g_{S}^{*}S^{*}-b\chi_{R}g_{S}^{*}R^{*}-c\chi_{S}g_{R}^{*}S^{*},

(i)

(ii)

A_{1}=0

A_{2}<0

\det(\mathcal{A}(\lambda))=A_{2}\lambda+\det(\mathcal{J}_{F})
becomes negative for sufficiently large
\lambda

(iii)

A_{1}>0

A_{2}<0
, and
A_{2}^{2}-4A_{1}\det(\mathcal{J}_{F})>0

(Simple sufficient parameter regimes for determinant-driven instability)

Maintain the notation of Theorem

Any one of the following conditions is sufficient to guarantee the existence of a nonconstant mode

(S1)

Single-species strong feedback:

(S2)

(S3)

Degenerate (affine) route:

and
A_{2}<0

(S4)

Since
\mathbf{u}
is classical on
[0,T]
, we have

M_{T}\coloneqq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\mathbf{u}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}<\infty.

Write the
S
-equation in the form

\partial_{t}S-d_{S}\Delta S=f_{S}(S,R,D,P,A),

where

f_{S}(S,R,D,P,A)=\lambda_{S}S\Bigl(1-\tfrac{S+R}{K}\Bigr)-\alpha S-\delta(D)S+\xi\bigl[1-\phi(D)\bigr]R.

For a.e.
(x,t)
, define the negative part
S_{-}(x,t)\coloneqq\max\{-S(x,t),0\}
.
Testing the
S
-equation with
S_{-}
and integrating over
U
yields, for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|S_{-}(t)\|_{L^{2}(U)}^{2}

\displaystyle=-d_{S}\int_{U}|\nabla S_{-}(x,t)|^{2}\,dx-\int_{U}f_{S}(S,R,D,P,A)\,S_{-}(x,t)\,dx.

(The diffusion term is handled by integration by parts; the boundary term vanishes due to
\partial_{\mathrm{n}}S=0
.)

Since
f_{S}
is locally Lipschitz in
S
on bounded sets by Lemma
3.1
, there exists
L_{T}>0

(depending only on
M_{T}
and parameters) such that

|f_{S}(S,R,D,P,A)-f_{S}(0,R,D,P,A)|\leq L_{T}|S|\qquad\text{whenever }|S|\leq M_{T},\ 0\leq R,D,P,A\leq M_{T}.

On the set
\{x\in U:\ S(x,t)<0\}
we have
|S|=S_{-}
, hence

f_{S}(S,R,D,P,A)\geq f_{S}(0,R,D,P,A)-L_{T}S_{-}.

Moreover, for all
(R,D,P,A)\geq 0
we have
f_{S}(0,R,D,P,A)=\xi[1-\phi(D)]R\geq 0
.
Therefore,

f_{S}(S,R,D,P,A)\geq-L_{T}S_{-}\qquad\text{a.e.\ on the set }\{S<0\}.

Consequently,

-\int_{U}f_{S}(S,R,D,P,A)\,S_{-}\,dx=-\int_{\{S<0\}}f_{S}(S,R,D,P,A)\,S_{-}\,dx\leq L_{T}\int_{U}S_{-}^{2}\,dx=L_{T}\|S_{-}(t)\|_{L^{2}(U)}^{2},

and therefore

\frac{d}{dt}\|S_{-}(t)\|_{L^{2}(U)}^{2}\leq 2L_{T}\,\|S_{-}(t)\|_{L^{2}(U)}^{2}\qquad\text{for a.e.\ }t\in(0,T).

Since
S_{-}(0)=0
by
S_{0}\geq 0
, Grönwall’s inequality gives
\|S_{-}(t)\|_{L^{2}(U)}^{2}\equiv 0
on
[0,T]
.
Thus
S(x,t)\geq 0
for all
(x,t)\in\overline{U}\times[0,T]
.
As
T<T_{\max}
was arbitrary, we conclude
S\geq 0
on
\overline{U}\times[0,T_{\max})
.

Applying the same argument to
R
gives
R\geq 0
.
For
D
we have
\partial_{t}D-d_{D}\Delta D=-\gamma_{d}D
, and the same test with
D_{-}
yields
D\geq 0
.

Fix
x\in U
. The variables
(P(x,t),A(x,t))
satisfy the linear (time-dependent) system

\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}P(x,t)\\[4.0pt]
A(x,t)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-\theta\phi(D(x,t))&\beta\bigl[1-\phi(D(x,t))\bigr]\\[4.0pt]
\theta\phi(D(x,t))&-\beta\bigl[1-\phi(D(x,t))\bigr]\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}P(x,t)\\[4.0pt]
A(x,t)\end{pmatrix}.

The off-diagonal entries are nonnegative for
D\geq 0
, hence the nonnegative quadrant is forward invariant:
if
P(x,t_{0})=0
and
A(x,t_{0})\geq 0
, then

P_{t}(x,t_{0})=\beta[1-\phi(D(x,t_{0}))]A(x,t_{0})\geq 0
; similarly, if
A(x,t_{0})=0
and
P(x,t_{0})\geq 0
, then

A_{t}(x,t_{0})=\theta\phi(D(x,t_{0}))P(x,t_{0})\geq 0
.
Therefore
P(x,t),A(x,t)\geq 0
for all
t\in[0,T_{\max})
.

This completes the proof of Theorem
3.2
.
∎

Proof.

4.3
Proof of the long-time reduction and attractivity of the limiting kinetics

We prove Theorems
3.5
and
3.6
.
Throughout,
\mathbf{u}=(S,R,D,P,A)
denotes the global mild solution of (
8
)
given by Theorem
3.3

\mathbf{u}_{0}\in\mathbf{X}_{+}

Proof of Theorem
3.5

Proof.

By Proposition
2.3
we have the energy decay

\|D(t)\|_{L^{2}(U)}\leq e^{-\gamma_{d}t}\|D_{0}\|_{L^{2}(U)}\qquad\forall t\geq 0.

Moreover, by the maximum principle (again Proposition
2.3
),
0\leq D(x,t)\leq\|D_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}
for all
t\geq 0
.
Since
\phi
is globally Lipschitz on
[0,\infty)
with
\phi(0)=0
and
\delta
is Lipschitz on
[0,\|D_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}]
with
\delta(0)=0
,
we obtain

\|\phi(D(t))\|_{L^{2}(U)}\leq L_{\phi}\|D(t)\|_{L^{2}(U)}\to 0,\qquad\|\delta(D(t))\|_{L^{2}(U)}\leq L_{\delta}\|D(t)\|_{L^{2}(U)}\to 0,

as
t\to\infty
, where
L_{\phi}
is the (global) Lipschitz constant of
\phi
and
L_{\delta}
is a Lipschitz constant of
\delta

on
[0,\|D_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}]
.

Fix
T>0
. For
t\geq T
, using the uniform-in-time bounds on
S,R
over compact time intervals
(Theorem
3.3
) and the uniform bound on
A
from (
14
), we estimate

\displaystyle\|\delta(D(t))S(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}

\displaystyle\leq\|\delta(D(t))\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}\,\|S(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(U)},

\displaystyle\|\phi(D(t))A(t)R(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}

\displaystyle\leq\|\phi(D(t))\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}\,\|A(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}\,\|R(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}.

Since
0\leq D(\cdot,t)\leq\|D_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}
and
D(\cdot,t)\to 0
in
L^{2}(U)
, we have (after extracting a subsequence if needed)

D(\cdot,t)\to 0
a.e. as
t\to\infty
, and by continuity of
\phi
and
\delta
at
0
,

\phi(D(\cdot,t))\to 0
and
\delta(D(\cdot,t))\to 0
a.e.
Combined with boundedness and dominated convergence, this yields

\|\phi(D(t))\|_{L^{p}(U)}\to 0,\qquad\|\delta(D(t))\|_{L^{p}(U)}\to 0\quad\text{for every finite }p\geq 1.

In particular, along any sequence
t_{n}\to\infty
for which

a.e., we have

\|\phi(D(t_{n}))\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}\to 0
and
\|\delta(D(t_{n}))\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}\to 0
provided

uniformly.
(Uniform decay holds, for example, when

by standard parabolic smoothing; see Appendix
A
.)
Hence all
D
-mediated couplings in the
(S,R)
-equations vanish asymptotically, and the limiting kinetics are given by (
27
).

This proves Theorem
3.5
.
∎

Remark 4.1

(On uniform decay of

Proof of Theorem
3.6

Proof.

Let
(S(t),R(t))
solve (
27
) with
S(0),R(0)\geq 0
and
S(0)+R(0)>0
.
Positively invariance of the nonnegative quadrant follows since
\dot{S}=\xi R\geq 0
on
\{S=0\}
and
\dot{R}=\alpha S\geq 0
on
\{R=0\}
.

Let
N(t)\coloneqq S(t)+R(t)
. Summing (
27
) gives

\dot{N}=\Bigl(1-\frac{N}{K}\Bigr)\bigl(\lambda_{S}S+\lambda_{R}R\bigr).

Since
\lambda_{S}S+\lambda_{R}R\geq 0
and
N(t)>0
, we have
\dot{N}>0
for
0<N<K
and
\dot{N}<0
for
N>K
.
Hence
N(t)
is monotone toward
K
and bounded, so

N(t)\to K\qquad\text{as }t\to\infty.

(45)

Let
S^{*}=\frac{\xi K}{\alpha+\xi}
and define
E(t)\coloneqq S(t)-S^{*}
.
Using
R(t)=N(t)-S(t)
and
(\alpha+\xi)S^{*}=\xi K
, we compute

\displaystyle\dot{E}

\displaystyle=\dot{S}=\lambda_{S}S\Bigl(1-\frac{N}{K}\Bigr)-\alpha S+\xi(N-S)

\displaystyle=-(\alpha+\xi)(S-S^{*})+\lambda_{S}S\Bigl(1-\frac{N}{K}\Bigr)+\xi(N-K)

\displaystyle=-(\alpha+\xi)E+\underbrace{\lambda_{S}S\Bigl(1-\frac{N}{K}\Bigr)+\xi(N-K)}_{=:F(t)}.

(46)

By (
45
) and boundedness of
S(t)
, we have
F(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.
Applying the variation-of-constants formula to (
46
) yields

E(t)=e^{-(\alpha+\xi)t}E(0)+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(\alpha+\xi)(t-s)}F(s)\,ds,\qquad t\geq 0.

Since
F(t)\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty)
,

(i)

E(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.

(ii)

If there exist
T\geq 0
and
\varepsilon>0
with
|F(t)|\leq\varepsilon
for all
t\geq T
, then for
t\geq T

|E(t)|\leq e^{-\lambda t}|E(0)|+e^{-\lambda t}\int_{0}^{T}e^{\lambda s}|F(s)|\,ds+\frac{\varepsilon}{\lambda},

hence
\limsup_{t\to\infty}|E(t)|\leq\varepsilon/\lambda
.

(iii)

If
|F(t)|\leq Ce^{-\beta t}
for constants
C,\beta>0
, then
E(t)=O(e^{-\gamma t})
with
\gamma=\min\{\lambda,\beta\}
(explicit formulas above).

Consequently,
E(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, i.e.
S(t)\to S^{*}
.
Finally, (
45
) and

imply

.
This proves global attractivity of
\mathbb{K}^{*}=(S^{*},R^{*})
.

4.4
Mode-wise stability proofs

We collect the mode-wise stability arguments underlying Theorems
3.8
,
3.10
,
and
3.11
. Throughout, let
\{(\lambda_{k},\omega_{k})\}_{k\geq 1}
denote the Neumann Laplacian eigenpairs on
U
:

-\Delta\omega_{k}=\lambda_{k}\omega_{k}\ \text{in }U,\qquad\partial_{\mathrm{n}}\omega_{k}=0\ \text{on }\partial U,\qquad 0=\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots,\ \lambda_{k}\to\infty,

with
\{\omega_{k}\}
orthonormal in
L^{2}(U)
.

Proof of Theorem
3.8
(no diffusion-driven instability)

Proof.

Let
F(S,R)\coloneqq(f_{S}(S,R),f_{R}(S,R))^{\mathsf{T}}
and denote by

\mathcal{J}\coloneqq DF(\mathbb{K}^{*})=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}

the Jacobian of
F
at
\mathbb{K}^{*}
.
From (
27
) one computes

\displaystyle\partial_{S}f_{S}(S,R)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{S}\Bigl(1-\frac{2S+R}{K}\Bigr)-\alpha,\qquad\partial_{R}f_{S}(S,R)=-\frac{\lambda_{S}}{K}S+\xi,

\displaystyle\partial_{S}f_{R}(S,R)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda_{R}}{K}R+\alpha,\qquad\partial_{R}f_{R}(S,R)=\lambda_{R}\Bigl(1-\frac{S+2R}{K}\Bigr)-\xi.

(47)

At
\mathbb{K}^{*}
we have
S^{*}=\dfrac{\xi K}{\alpha+\xi}
and
R^{*}=\dfrac{\alpha K}{\alpha+\xi}
, hence

a=\partial_{S}f_{S}(\mathbb{K}^{*})=-\frac{\xi\lambda_{S}}{\alpha+\xi}-\alpha<0,\qquad d=\partial_{R}f_{R}(\mathbb{K}^{*})=-\frac{\alpha\lambda_{R}}{\alpha+\xi}-\xi<0.

(48)

Moreover,

\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{J})=a+d<0,\qquad\det(\mathcal{J})=\xi\lambda_{S}+\alpha\lambda_{R}>0,

(49)

so the homogeneous equilibrium is linearly stable.

Seeking normal modes
(s,r)=(\hat{s},\hat{r})\omega_{k}(x)e^{\lambda t}
in the linearization of (
29
) at
\mathbb{K}^{*}

yields, for each
k\geq 1
, the mode matrix

\mathcal{A}(\lambda_{k})\coloneqq\mathcal{J}-\lambda_{k}D_{SR},\qquad D_{SR}\coloneqq\mathrm{diag}(d_{S},d_{R}),

and the dispersion relation
\det(\lambda I-\mathcal{A}(\lambda_{k}))=0
.
Equivalently,
\lambda
satisfies the quadratic

\lambda^{2}+a_{1}(\lambda_{k})\lambda+a_{0}(\lambda_{k})=0,

(50)

where

\displaystyle a_{1}(\lambda_{k})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}(d_{S}+d_{R})-\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{J}),

\displaystyle a_{0}(\lambda_{k})

\displaystyle=d_{S}d_{R}\lambda_{k}^{2}-\bigl(d_{S}d+d_{R}a\bigr)\lambda_{k}+\det(\mathcal{J}).

(51)

For any nonconstant mode
k\geq 2
we have
\lambda_{k}>0
, hence

a_{1}(\lambda_{k})=\lambda_{k}(d_{S}+d_{R})-\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{J})>\lambda_{k}(d_{S}+d_{R})\geq 0,

so
a_{1}(\lambda_{k})>0
.
Using
a<0
and
d<0
from (
48
), we also have
-(d_{S}d+d_{R}a)\lambda_{k}>0
for
\lambda_{k}>0
, and therefore

a_{0}(\lambda_{k})\geq d_{S}d_{R}\lambda_{k}^{2}+\det(\mathcal{J})>0\qquad\text{for all }k\geq 2.

By the Routh–Hurwitz criterion for quadratic polynomials,
a_{1}(\lambda_{k})>0
and
a_{0}(\lambda_{k})>0
imply

\Re(\lambda)<0
for both roots of (
50
). Hence every nonconstant mode is exponentially damped.

Finally, the constant mode
k=1
corresponds to
\lambda_{1}=0
and is stable by (
49
).
Therefore
(S^{*},R^{*})
is linearly asymptotically stable with respect to all Neumann modes, and no diffusion-driven
(Turing) instability can occur.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.10
(one-way damped chemotaxis)

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{u}^{*}=(S^{*},R^{*},c^{*})
be a homogeneous equilibrium of the one-way system (

Write
\mathcal{J}_{F}=D(f_{S},f_{R})(S^{*},R^{*})

(i.e.

Proof of Theorem
3.11
(two-way feedback criteria)

Proof.

Consider the reduced two-way closure (
33
) and let
\mathbf{u}^{*}=(S^{*},R^{*})
be a homogeneous interior equilibrium.
Linearizing at
\mathbf{u}^{*}

\partial_{t}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}=(\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{H})\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{u}}+\mathcal{J}_{F}\tilde{\mathbf{u}},\qquad\tilde{\mathbf{u}}=(S-S^{*},R-R^{*})^{\mathsf{T}},

\mathcal{J}_{F}=DF(\mathbf{u}^{*})

Projecting onto the Neumann eigenmode

\frac{d}{dt}\hat{\mathbf{u}}=\mathcal{A}(\lambda_{k})\hat{\mathbf{u}},\qquad\mathcal{A}(\lambda_{k})=\mathcal{J}_{F}-\lambda_{k}(\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{H}).

For each fixed
k\geq 2

\mathcal{A}(\lambda_{k})
is a real
2\times 2
matrix.
By the Routh–Hurwitz criterion for
2\times 2
systems, the mode

is unstable if and only if

\operatorname{tr}\mathcal{A}(\lambda_{k})>0\qquad\text{or}\qquad\det\mathcal{A}(\lambda_{k})<0,

(53)

with equalities corresponding to neutral stability boundaries.

A direct computation gives the trace formula (
35
), proving the trace criterion.
For the determinant, expanding
\det(\mathcal{A}(\lambda))
as a polynomial in
\lambda
yields (
36
)
with coefficients (
(b)
). Since
\det(\mathcal{J}_{F})>0
by (
34
), the constant term is positive.
Thus

for some

(i)

If
A_{1}<0
, then
\det(\mathcal{A}(\lambda))\to-\infty
as

, hence some large mode destabilizes.

(ii)

If

\lambda>0

(iii)

If

5
Discussion

We developed a hybrid PDE–ODE framework for two competing populations coupled to a non-motile microenvironmental switch and a
diffusive inhibitory signal, and we quantified how chemotaxis-driven transport depends on coupling directionality once the
chemotactic cue is modeled as a diffusive field
c(x,t)
.
Our analysis is intentionally mode-wise and linear: throughout, “pattern formation” refers to linear instability of a spatially
homogeneous equilibrium under homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions, and we distinguish this from Keller–Segel blow-up or aggregation
phenomena, which require different techniques and are not pursued here.

5.1
Mechanistic interpretation of the directionality principle

The core mathematical mechanism can be read directly from the modal linearizations.

5.1.1
One-way damped signaling suppresses patterns.

In the one-way regime (Section
3.4
), the chemoattractant
c
is generated independently of
(S,R)
and is
linearly damped (either autonomously or through a microenvironment-driven source such as
A
with clearance).
After projection onto Neumann modes, the linearized mode matrix is block upper-triangular, so its spectrum is the union of the
(S,R)
diffusion block and the scalar
c
-block.
Damping ensures
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(c^{*})-\lambda_{k}d_{c}<0
for every
k\geq 2
, while the
(S,R)
block retains the same eigenvalues as the base
reaction–diffusion subsystem. Hence chemotaxis does not create new unstable eigenvalues: in this directionality class, the signal
decays without receiving feedback, and the population spectrum is unaffected. Biologically, this corresponds to cells moving along a cue
that is exogenous or microenvironmentally prescribed and cleared; such a cue can bias motion but, in the linear regime, it cannot close a
positive feedback loop that amplifies spatial heterogeneity.

5.1.2
Two-way feedback can recover instability via an effective mobility rewrite.

In the two-way regime, the signal depends on
(S,R)
(directly or through fast relaxation), and the linearization generates effective
cross-diffusion. At the mode level, diffusion is no longer governed by
\mathcal{D}
alone but by
\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{H}
, where

\mathcal{H}
is a feedback-induced matrix determined by chemotactic sensitivities and derivatives of the closure
c\approx g(S,R)
.
This modifies both the trace and determinant of the mode matrix
\mathcal{A}(\lambda_{k})
and allows destabilization of some nonconstant
mode even when the kinetics are stable. The explicit criteria in Theorem
3.11
quantify precisely when feedback
dominates diffusion through either a trace sign change (
35
) or determinant negativity driven by the quadratic
coefficients
A_{1},A_{2}
(
(b)
). Biologically, this corresponds to cue production or activation that is locally amplified by
the populations (directly or indirectly), closing a positive loop that can convert directed migration into emergent heterogeneity.

5.2
Limitations and modeling assumptions

Our two-way instability criteria are stated for the reduced closure
c\approx g(S,R)
.
This is a natural approximation when the chemoattractant equilibrates on a faster time scale than cell movement, but it is not universal.
For the full PDE signal model
\partial_{t}c=d_{c}\Delta c+q\,c+h(S,R)
, eliminating
c
yields an effective
2\times 2
mode matrix whose
coefficients depend on
\lambda_{k}
through the resolvent
(d_{c}\lambda_{k}-q)^{-1}
, so the reduced “local” closure is best interpreted as
an asymptotic or short-scale approximation. A systematic comparison of reduced and full PDE criteria is therefore needed when the signal
time scale is comparable to population dynamics.

Our analysis is deterministic and focuses on mode-wise linear stability under Neumann boundary conditions.
In applications, however, intrinsic fluctuations stemming from finite population sizes and stochastic phenotype flips may be non-negligible.
Complementary stochastic differential equation-based analysis frameworks for
pure demographic noise establish global well-posedness,
and show that demographic noise alone does not induce population extinction; see
(
1
;
68
)
.
In the present hybrid PDE–ODE context, incorporating demographic noise (or hybrid jump perturbations of the local
switching kinetics) is a natural next step, and a key question is how such intrinsic noise interacts with diffusion-driven
mode selection and feedback-induced effective cross-diffusion.

The hybrid PDE–ODE structure introduces parameters governing switching (e.g.
\theta,\beta
), clearance/decay (e.g.
\gamma_{d}
),
and chemotactic sensitivity (e.g.
\chi_{S},\chi_{R}
), in addition to baseline growth and conversion rates.
Identifying these parameters from experimental data remains nontrivial, especially when only partial observations of microenvironmental
states are available. Practical calibration will likely require combining time-resolved spatial data with reduced-order summaries (e.g. dominant Fourier modes) informed by the explicit mode-wise criteria.

5.3
Outlook and extensions

Several directions follow naturally from the present analysis.

A first extension is to derive instability criteria directly for the PDE–PDE–PDE system with
c
governed by

\partial_{t}c=d_{c}\Delta c+q\,c+h(S,R)
, using mode-wise elimination of
c
(Schur complements) to obtain sharp conditions in terms of

(d_{c},q)
and the Laplacian spectrum. This would clarify precisely when the reduced closure
c\approx g(S,R)
is accurate and how nonlocal
signal effects shift thresholds.

While linear instability is an entry point for pattern formation, the observed long-time patterns depend on nonlinear saturation,
possible amplitude equations near onset, and secondary instabilities. Extending the analysis to weakly nonlinear regimes (e.g. via
center-manifold reductions or normal forms) would connect the trace/determinant thresholds to pattern selection and robustness.

Our results hold for
N\in\{1,2,3\}
at the level of mode-wise criteria, but geometry enters through the Neumann spectrum and can strongly
influence which modes are realized. Systematic exploration on three-dimensional domains with realistic boundary geometry is therefore
important for tumor microenvironment applications, where confinement, interfaces, and anisotropy can bias pattern selection.

The present microenvironment is a two-state switch; richer switching networks (multiple phenotypes, irreversible transitions, or
history-dependent states)
(
33
;
20
)
can be incorporated while preserving the hybrid PDE–ODE structure. On the transport side, nonlinear
sensitivities and density-dependent motility
(
70
;
26
;
40
;
14
;
46
)
can be introduced, with the expectation that directionality (presence/absence of feedback)
will remain the organizing principle, while the explicit thresholds will be modified.

In summary, the analysis identifies directionality as a structural determinant of chemotaxis-driven patterning in hybrid PDE–ODE
models: without feedback, damping in the diffusive cue prevents spectral destabilization, whereas with feedback the effective mobility is
rewritten and explicit mode-wise criteria predict when diffusion/chemotaxis-driven instabilities can emerge. This provides a tractable
bridge between reaction–diffusion and chemotaxis paradigms in a microenvironmentally structured setting.

Appendix A
Spectral preliminaries

This appendix collects standard spectral facts for the Neumann Laplacian that are used in the main text, in particular for
(i) mode-wise stability arguments under homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions, and
(ii) the spectral representation and smoothing of the damped heat equation for
D
.
For completeness we record statements in the form needed for reproducibility; detailed proofs can be found in standard references.

A.1
Neumann eigenpairs and basic properties

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
(
N\in\{1,2,3\}
) be a bounded domain with smooth boundary
\partial U
.
Consider the Neumann eigenvalue problem

\begin{cases}-\Delta\omega_{k}=\lambda_{k}\omega_{k}&\text{in }U,\\[2.0pt]
\partial_{\mathrm{n}}\omega_{k}=0&\text{on }\partial U,\end{cases}

(54)

where
\partial_{\mathrm{n}}
denotes the outward normal derivative.
Classical spectral theory
(
19
)
yields a complete orthonormal basis
\{\omega_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
of
L^{2}(U)
consisting of smooth eigenfunctions
and a nondecreasing sequence of eigenvalues

0=\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}\leq\cdots,\qquad\lambda_{k}\to\infty\ \text{as }k\to\infty.

(55)

The first eigenfunction
\omega_{1}
is constant (up to normalization), and modes
k\geq 2
correspond to nonconstant spatial perturbations.

A.2
Weyl-type eigenvalue bounds

We use a quantitative form of Weyl’s law to control summability of eigenfunction expansions.

Lemma A.1

(Weyl-type bounds)
.

(
55
)

There exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
and an integer
k_{0}\geq 2
such that

C_{1}\,k^{2/N}\leq\lambda_{k}\leq C_{2}\,k^{2/N},\qquad\forall k\geq k_{0}.

(56)

Proof.

This follows from Weyl asymptotics for the Neumann Laplacian on smooth bounded domains.
∎

A.3
Sup-norm derivative bounds for eigenfunctions

The following estimate is a convenient sufficient condition for termwise differentiation of eigenfunction expansions.

Lemma A.2

(Sup-norm derivative bound)
.

Let
(\lambda_{k},\omega_{k})
satisfy (
54
) and assume
\|\omega_{k}\|_{L^{2}(U)}=1
.
For every multi-index
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{N}
there exists
C=C(\alpha,U)>0
such that

\|\partial^{\alpha}\omega_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}\leq C(\alpha,U)\,\lambda_{k}^{|\alpha|/2+1},\qquad k\geq 1,

(57)

where
|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{N}
.
Consequently, for each integer
m\geq 1
there exists
C=C(m,U)>0
such that

\|\omega_{k}\|_{H^{m}(U)}\leq C(m,U)\,(1+\lambda_{k})^{m/2+1}.

(58)

Proof.

The estimate follows from elliptic regularity applied iteratively to
-\Delta\omega_{k}=\lambda_{k}\omega_{k}
together with Sobolev embeddings

H^{m}(U)\hookrightarrow C^{m-2}(\overline{U})
for
N\leq 3
.
∎

A.4
Spectral representation for the damped heat equation

For completeness we state the spectral formula used for the damped signal
D
.
Consider

\partial_{t}D=d_{D}\Delta D-\gamma_{d}D\quad\text{in }U\times(0,\infty),\qquad\partial_{\mathrm{n}}D=0\ \text{on }\partial U\times(0,\infty),\qquad D(\cdot,0)=D_{0}.

(59)

Proposition A.3

(Spectral solution formula and smoothing)
.

Assume
D_{0}\in L^{2}(U)
and let
(\lambda_{k},\omega_{k})
be the Neumann eigenpairs from (
54
).
Define
c_{k}\coloneqq(D_{0},\omega_{k})_{L^{2}(U)}
.
Then the unique solution of (
59
) admits the spectral representation

D(x,t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{k}\,e^{-(d_{D}\lambda_{k}+\gamma_{d})t}\,\omega_{k}(x),\qquad t\geq 0,

(60)

with convergence in
L^{2}(U)
uniformly for
t\geq 0
.
Moreover, for every
\varepsilon>0
and every multi-index
\alpha
, the differentiated series

\partial^{\alpha}D(x,t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{k}\,e^{-(d_{D}\lambda_{k}+\gamma_{d})t}\,\partial^{\alpha}\omega_{k}(x)

converges uniformly on
\overline{U}\times[\varepsilon,\infty)
, hence
D(\cdot,t)\in C^{\infty}(\overline{U})
for all
t>0
.

Proof.

The expansion (
60
) follows by separation of variables in the
\{\omega_{k}\}
basis
(
7
)
.
Uniform convergence of derivatives on
[\varepsilon,\infty)
follows from Lemma
A.2
and Weyl bounds
(Lemma
A.1
), since
e^{-d_{D}\lambda_{k}\varepsilon}
dominates any polynomial growth in
\lambda_{k}
.
∎

Appendix B
From the diffusive signal equation to an effective closure

This appendix formalizes how a diffusive chemoattractant equation can be eliminated mode-by-mode, yielding a reduced
closure that modifies the effective mobility of
(S,R)
and leads to the cross-diffusion structure used in the
mode-wise instability criteria.

B.1
A linearized two-way PDE–PDE system around a homogeneous equilibrium

Consider the two-way chemotaxis extension with a diffusive signal

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}S=d_{S}\Delta S-\chi_{S}\nabla\!\cdot(S\nabla c)+f_{S}(S,R),\\[2.0pt]
\partial_{t}R=d_{R}\Delta R-\chi_{R}\nabla\!\cdot(R\nabla c)+f_{R}(S,R),\\[2.0pt]
\partial_{t}c=d_{c}\Delta c+q\,c+h(S,R),\qquad q<0,\end{cases}

(61)

posed on
U
with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions for
S,R,c
.
Let
(S^{*},R^{*},c^{*})
be a spatially homogeneous equilibrium satisfying

f_{S}(S^{*},R^{*})=0,\qquad f_{R}(S^{*},R^{*})=0,\qquad q\,c^{*}+h(S^{*},R^{*})=0.

Write perturbations
\tilde{S}=S-S^{*}
,
\tilde{R}=R-R^{*}
,
\tilde{c}=c-c^{*}
and denote the Jacobians

\mathcal{J}_{F}\coloneqq DF(S^{*},R^{*})=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathcal{J}_{h}\coloneqq\nabla h(S^{*},R^{*})=(h_{S}^{*},h_{R}^{*}).

Since
\nabla c^{*}=0
, linearization of the chemotaxis terms yields

-\chi_{S}\nabla\cdot(S\nabla c)\approx-\chi_{S}\nabla\cdot(S^{*}\nabla\tilde{c})=-\chi_{S}S^{*}\Delta\tilde{c},\qquad-\chi_{R}\nabla\cdot(R\nabla c)\approx-\chi_{R}R^{*}\Delta\tilde{c}.

Hence the linearized system is

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\tilde{S}=d_{S}\Delta\tilde{S}-\chi_{S}S^{*}\Delta\tilde{c}+a\,\tilde{S}+b\,\tilde{R},\\[2.0pt]
\partial_{t}\tilde{R}=d_{R}\Delta\tilde{R}-\chi_{R}R^{*}\Delta\tilde{c}+c\,\tilde{S}+d\,\tilde{R},\\[2.0pt]
\partial_{t}\tilde{c}=d_{c}\Delta\tilde{c}+q\,\tilde{c}+h_{S}^{*}\tilde{S}+h_{R}^{*}\tilde{R}.\end{cases}

(62)

B.2
Mode-wise decomposition and Schur complement elimination

Let
\{(\lambda_{k},\omega_{k})\}_{k\geq 1}
be the Neumann Laplacian eigenpairs on
U
:

-\Delta\omega_{k}=\lambda_{k}\omega_{k},\qquad\partial_{\mathrm{n}}\omega_{k}=0\ \text{on }\partial U,\qquad 0=\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots,\quad\lambda_{k}\to\infty.

Project (
62
) onto a fixed mode
k\geq 2
and write

\tilde{S}(x,t)=\hat{s}_{k}(t)\,\omega_{k}(x),\quad\tilde{R}(x,t)=\hat{r}_{k}(t)\,\omega_{k}(x),\quad\tilde{c}(x,t)=\hat{c}_{k}(t)\,\omega_{k}(x).

Using
\Delta\omega_{k}=-\lambda_{k}\omega_{k}
, we obtain the
3\times 3
linear ODE system

\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{s}_{k}\\[4.0pt]
\hat{r}_{k}\\[4.0pt]
\hat{c}_{k}\end{pmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}a-d_{S}\lambda_{k}&b&\chi_{S}S^{*}\lambda_{k}\\[4.0pt]
c&d-d_{R}\lambda_{k}&\chi_{R}R^{*}\lambda_{k}\\[4.0pt]
h_{S}^{*}&h_{R}^{*}&q-d_{c}\lambda_{k}\end{pmatrix}}_{\eqqcolon\,\mathcal{M}(\lambda_{k})}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{s}_{k}\\[4.0pt]
\hat{r}_{k}\\[4.0pt]
\hat{c}_{k}\end{pmatrix}.

(63)

For spectral analysis, one may eliminate
\hat{c}_{k}
by a Schur complement argument.
Let
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
be a candidate growth rate for mode
k
and consider the eigenvalue problem

\mu\mathbf{z}=\mathcal{M}(\lambda_{k})\mathbf{z},\qquad\mathbf{z}=(\hat{s}_{k},\hat{r}_{k},\hat{c}_{k})^{\mathsf{T}}.

Assuming
\mu\neq q-d_{c}\lambda_{k}
(the signal eigenvalue of the uncoupled
c
-block), the third row gives

(\mu-(q-d_{c}\lambda_{k}))\,\hat{c}_{k}=h_{S}^{*}\,\hat{s}_{k}+h_{R}^{*}\,\hat{r}_{k},\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\hat{c}_{k}=\frac{h_{S}^{*}\,\hat{s}_{k}+h_{R}^{*}\,\hat{r}_{k}}{\mu-(q-d_{c}\lambda_{k})}.

(64)

Substituting (
64
) into the first two rows yields an equivalent
2\times 2
eigenvalue problem

\mu\begin{pmatrix}\hat{s}_{k}\\
\hat{r}_{k}\end{pmatrix}=\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mu;\lambda_{k})\begin{pmatrix}\hat{s}_{k}\\
\hat{r}_{k}\end{pmatrix},

(65)

where the effective mode matrix is

\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mu;\lambda_{k})\coloneqq\Bigl(\mathcal{J}_{F}-\lambda_{k}\mathcal{D}\Bigr)+\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\mu-(q-d_{c}\lambda_{k})}\,\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{S}S^{*}\\
\chi_{R}R^{*}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}h_{S}^{*}&h_{R}^{*}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathcal{D}\coloneqq\mathrm{diag}(d_{S},d_{R}).

(66)

Equivalently, the characteristic equation of
\mathcal{M}(\lambda_{k})
factors as

\det(\mu I-\mathcal{M}(\lambda_{k}))=\bigl(\mu-(q-d_{c}\lambda_{k})\bigr)\,\det\!\Bigl(\mu I-\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mu;\lambda_{k})\Bigr).

(67)

Thus, for each fixed
\lambda_{k}>0
, spectral stability reduces to understanding the nonlinear (in
\mu
)
2\times 2
determinant
in (
67
). The next subsection explains when (
66
) simplifies to the reduced closure
used in the main text.

B.3
Fast-relaxation regime and reduced closure

A standard fast-relaxation scaling introduces
\varepsilon\ll 1
in the signal equation:

\varepsilon\,\partial_{t}c=d_{c}\Delta c+q\,c+h(S,R),\qquad q<0.

(68)

Linearizing (
68
) replaces the third diagonal entry
q-d_{c}\lambda_{k}
in (
63
) by

\varepsilon^{-1}(q-d_{c}\lambda_{k})
, hence (
64
) becomes

\hat{c}_{k}=\frac{h_{S}^{*}\,\hat{s}_{k}+h_{R}^{*}\,\hat{r}_{k}}{\varepsilon\mu-(q-d_{c}\lambda_{k})}.

(69)

If
\varepsilon|\mu|\ll|q-d_{c}\lambda_{k}|
(signal relaxes faster than the growth/decay rate of the
(S,R)
-mode),
then

\hat{c}_{k}\;=\;-\frac{h_{S}^{*}\,\hat{s}_{k}+h_{R}^{*}\,\hat{r}_{k}}{q-d_{c}\lambda_{k}}\;+\;\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon),\qquad\varepsilon\to 0,

(70)

and the effective matrix (
66
) admits the approximation

\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mu;\lambda_{k})=\Bigl(\mathcal{J}_{F}-\lambda_{k}\mathcal{D}\Bigr)-\frac{\lambda_{k}}{q-d_{c}\lambda_{k}}\,\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{S}S^{*}\\
\chi_{R}R^{*}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}h_{S}^{*}&h_{R}^{*}\end{pmatrix}\;+\;\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon).

(71)

In particular, for each fixed mode
\lambda_{k}>0
, the quasi-steady elimination yields a linear
2\times 2
mode matrix
(with no dependence on
\mu
at leading order). This provides a rigorous modal interpretation of the reduced closure.

B.3.1
Connection to a local closure
c\approx g(S,R)
.

The quasi-steady relation in physical space reads

0=d_{c}\Delta c+q\,c+h(S,R)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad c=-\,(d_{c}\Delta+q)^{-1}h(S,R),

(72)

with Neumann boundary conditions. This is a nonlocal closure in general.
However, in regimes where either
(i)
d_{c}
is small compared to
|q|
(reaction-dominated signal), or
(ii) one focuses on sufficiently short spatial scales for which
(q-d_{c}\lambda_{k})^{-1}
is approximately constant across the
unstable band, or
(iii) one adopts a model reduction retaining only a finite set of dominant modes,
(
72
) can be approximated by a local closure
c\approx g(S,R)
near equilibrium, namely

c\approx g(S,R)\quad\text{with}\quad\nabla g(S^{*},R^{*})=-\frac{1}{q}\,\nabla h(S^{*},R^{*})\;=\;-\frac{1}{q}\,(h_{S}^{*},h_{R}^{*}).

(73)

Under (
73
), linearization of the reduced chemotaxis term

-\chi\nabla\cdot(S\nabla g(S,R))
produces precisely the rank-one correction to the diffusion matrix encoded by the outer product

\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{S}S^{*}\\
\chi_{R}R^{*}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}g_{S}^{*}&g_{R}^{*}\end{pmatrix},\qquad(g_{S}^{*},g_{R}^{*})=\nabla g(S^{*},R^{*}),

which is the effective cross-diffusion structure used in Theorem
3.11
.

Remark B.1

(When the reduced cross-diffusion form is accurate)
.

The reduction to a local closure
c\approx g(S,R)
is most accurate when the signal equation is strongly damped (
|q|
large)
and the feedback
h(S,R)
varies on spatial scales not significantly affected by the resolvent
(d_{c}\Delta+q)^{-1}
, or when the
analysis targets an instability band where
(q-d_{c}\lambda_{k})^{-1}
may be treated as approximately constant.
If one wishes to avoid any locality assumption, the mode-wise elimination (
67
)–(
71
)
already provides a fully rigorous route: for each
\lambda_{k}
the
(S,R)
-mode matrix is modified by the scalar factor

-(q-d_{c}\lambda_{k})^{-1}
multiplying the rank-one feedback operator.
