The Strong, Weak and Benign Goodhart’s law. An independence-free and paradigm-agnostic formalisation
Adrien Majka, El-Mahdi El-Mhamdi

Abstract
Goodhart’s law is a famous adage in policy-making that states that “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure”. As machine learning models and the optimisation capacity to train them grow, growing empirical evidence reinforced the belief in the validity of this law without however being formalised. Recently, a few attempts were made to formalise Goodhart’s law, either by categorising variants of it, or by looking at how optimising a proxy metric affects the optimisation of an intended goal . In this work, we alleviate the simplifying independence assumption, made in previous works, and the assumption on the learning paradigm made in most of them, to study the effect of the coupling between the proxy metric and the intended goal on Goodhart’s law. Our results show that in the case of light tailed goal and light tailed discrepancy, dependence does not change the nature of Goodhart’s effect. However, in the light tailed goal and heavy tailed discrepancy case, we exhibit an example where over-optimisation occurs at a rate inversely proportional to the heavy tailedness of the discrepancy between the goal and the metric.

4.2
Heavy tailed goal and light tailed discrepancy

Here, we consider the general case where the goal is heavy tailed and the discrepancy is light tailed, without specifying any dependence structure nor narrowing the case on particular distributions.

In this case, we are able to derive a lower bound on the expected value of the true goal
G
after optimisation, as shown in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that G is heavy tailed, ie denoting
S_{G}
the survival function of
G
we have :
S(G)=\frac{l(t)}{t^{1/\gamma}}
where
l
is a slowly varying function (ie
\forall a>0,\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\lim}\frac{l(at)}{l(t)}=1
) and
\xi
is a right and left light tailed random variable, ie
\exists s\in\mathbb{R}/\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\xi>t\right)}{e^{-st}}\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\rightarrow}0\text{ and }\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\xi<t\right)}{e^{-s|t|}}\underset{t\rightarrow-\infty}{\rightarrow}0
. Define
M:=G+\xi
. Then :

\mathbb{E}\left[G\middle|M>m\right]\underset{m\rightarrow\infty}{\geq}m+o(m)

Proof sketch.

The proof relies on two main ideas.

1.

First, for any couple of variables
\begin{pmatrix}X\\
Y\end{pmatrix}
where
X
is light tailed to the right and to the left, the conditional probability of
X
given
Y
will almost surely tend to
0
with a speed almost equivalent to the unconditional probability, that is for any

\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(X>t\middle|Y\right)}{e^{-ct}}\overset{a.s}{\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\rightarrow}}0

2.

Second, using the first idea, we can operate an informed double-cut of the integral

\int_{\mathbb{R}}gp_{G}(g)\int_{x\geq m-g}p_{\xi|G}(x)dxdg
.

(a)

We first cut at 0 the integral, to handle separately the positive and negative case

(b)

We then make a cut at
m+t_{m}
, where
t_{m}=\frac{2log(m)}{\gamma s}-\frac{log(l(m))}{s}
. This allows a good balance of coverage over the support of
\xi
and a decay of
\mathbb{P}\left(G>m+t_{m}\right)
of the order
1/m^{1/\gamma}
.

∎

This theorem shows that weak and strong Goodhart are impossible in this case.
Indeed, the lower bound show that the expected value of
G
after optimisation will go to infinity.
This result is fully independent from the dependence structure between the true goal
G
and the discrepancy
\xi

This is why we need both left and right tail of

Otherwise we can construct a dependence structure such that the left tail of the discrepancy (where it’s negative) completely compensate for the positive value of the true goal

This emerges in the proof through the trade-off between the coverage of the support of

If this result prevents weak and strong Goodhart, it does not tell anything about the proxy informativeness with respect to the true goal

4.3
Light tailed goal and light tailed discrepancy: the Gaussian case

In this section, we study the double light-tailed situation where both
G
and
\xi

G
and
\xi
as a Gaussian random vector as follows.

\left(\begin{matrix}G\\
\xi\end{matrix}\right)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)

\Sigma=\left(\begin{matrix}a&c\\

and
\xi

\operatorname{Var}\left(G\right)=a

\operatorname{Var}\left(\xi\right)=b

\xi

\operatorname{Var}\left(G\right)>\operatorname{Var}\left(\xi\right)
), the goal
G
goes to infinity while the correlation between the goal
G
and the proxy metric
M
goes to 0. We call this situation the
benign Goodhart’s law
, which is a special case of the “weak Goodhart” case introduced in
[
9
]
. In this case, “benign“ reflects the fact that while the correlation between the goal and the proxy is going to zero it does not prevent the goal from going to infinity.

Lemma 4.1
.

With
(G,\xi)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
,
M=G+\xi
and
\operatorname{Var}\left(G\right)>\operatorname{Var}\left(\xi\right)
the optimisation of the proxy metric also leads to the optimisation of the true goal.

\mathbb{E}\left[G\middle|M>m\right]\underset{m\rightarrow\infty}{\sim}\frac{a+c}{(a+b+2c)}m.

The full proof of Lemma
4.1

Proof sketch.

The proof contains two parts. The first part computes an equivalent for
\mathbb{P}\left(M>m\right)
, which is done using the equivalent for Gaussian tail :

\int_{x}^{\infty}e^{-u^{2}}du=\frac{e^{-x^{2}}}{2x}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(-1)^{n}\frac{(2n-1)!!}{2^{n}x^{2n}}+O\left(\frac{x^{-2N-1}}{\exp(x^{2})}\right).

The second part computes the unormalised expected value from the formula of its definition, i.e.,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(-\sigma_{2}x^{2})\int_{t\geq\mu-x\theta}t\exp\left(-\sigma_{1}t^{2}\right)dtdx.

∎

The coefficient in front of the optimisation threshold
m
is most of the time
<1

G
and the discrepancy
\xi
is close to its minimum value. With greater positive covariance, the discrepancy
\xi
will account for a greater portion of the proxy, thus decreasing the expected value of the goal
G
. On the contrary, with negative covariance between the goal
G
and
\xi

\mathbb{E}_{G}\left[\xi\right]=\frac{cG}{a}

We coin the term “benign” to describe the situation as despite the goal
G
going to infinity, the correlation between the proxy
M
and the goal
G
goes to 0

.

4.4
Exponential goal and heavy tailed discrepancy

In this section, we consider the case where the goal
G
is exponential of parameter
1
. The discrepancy is then drawn conditionally to the goal
G
by a truncated at
1
exponential law of parameter
G
. This imply that
\xi
follow a Pareto law of shape
2
and scale
1
. The same variable elevated to the power of
1/(b-1)
and multiplied by
\eta
will be such that it follow a pareto law of shape
b
and scale
\eta
.

\xi
then has the conditionnal density :
f_{\xi\mid G}(x)=G\exp(-G((\frac{x}{\eta})^{b-1}-1))\frac{x^{b-2}}{\eta^{b-1}}(b-1)\mathds{1}\{x>\eta\}
.
This case is an example of strong Goodhart’s law. The optimisation the goal
G
tends to 0 while making the discrepancy tend to infinity.

Lemma 4.2
.

When
G\sim\mathcal{E}(1)
and
f_{\xi\mid G}(x)=G\exp(-G((\frac{x}{\eta})^{b-1}-1))\frac{x^{b-2}}{\eta^{b-1}}(b-1)\mathds{1}\{x>\eta\}
, the maximisation of the proxy metric
M
also leads to the maximisation of the discrepancy
\xi
, as

\mathbb{E}\left[\xi\middle|M>m\right]\underset{m\rightarrow\infty}{\sim}m\frac{b-1}{b-2}.

The full proof can be found in Appendix
6.15

Proof sketch.

The idea of the proof is inspired by Gaussian tail development. Using the fact that for any polynomial
Q
, we have
\frac{\mathrm{d}\exp{Q(x)}}{\mathrm{d}x}=Q^{\prime}(x)\exp(Q(x))
, this means that any integral of the form

\int_{0}^{m-\eta}P(x)exp(Q(m,x))dx
can be iteratively integrated by part to obtain as follows.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{m-\eta}P(x)exp(Q(m,x))dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left[\frac{f_{Q^{\prime}}^{n}(P(g))}{Q^{\prime}(g)}\exp(Q(g))\right]_{0}^{\frac{m}{b+1}}}_{I_{1}}+(-1)^{N+1}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{m/b+1}f_{Q^{\prime}}^{N+1}(P)(g)\exp(Q(g))dg}_{I_{2}},

where we denote
f_{Q^{\prime}}
the operation consisting in dividing by
Q^{\prime}
and then differentiating,
f_{Q^{\prime}}^{n}
consisting in applying
f_{Q^{\prime}}
n times. Using the fact that
f_{Q^{\prime}}^{N+1}(P)
is bounded (as it is a rational fraction with degree
<0
) by a quantity decreasing to zero with a speed depending on
N+1
,
I_{2}
is
o(I_{1})
(using the Bachmann–Landau notations to reflect the fact that
I_{2}
is of inferior order to
I_{1}
).
The expected values are then an application of this equivalent after a some work on the original integral.

∎

First important thing to notice is that the discrepancy
\xi
is actually what’s being optimised for here. The lighter tail of the goal
G
makes it much less likely than the discrepancy
\xi
to produce extreme realisation. This is true whatever the shape
b
of the discrepancy
\xi
as its tail is proportional to
1/x^{b}
, while the tail of the exponential goal
G
is proportional to
exp(-x)
. This means that, if we know that
M
has a very high realisation, it will be much more likely to be due to a large discrepancy
\xi
than a high
G
.

Moreover, as the discrepancy is drawn approximately following an exponential distribution of parameter
G
(the goal), this means that high realisation of the discrepancy
\xi
are associated with small realisation of the goal
G
. As such, the optimisation procedure - by increasing the likelihood of higher discrepancy
\xi
- will also make instances of very small
G
much more likely. This leads to following result.

Lemma 4.3
.

When
G\sim\mathcal{E}(1)
and

5
Discussion

Whether Goodhart’s law occurs or not has been recently shown to depend on the tail thickness of both the intended goal and the discrepancy between that goal and the actual proxy metric. This has been done
[
9
,
18
]
while assuming the two aforementioned quantities to be independent. Our results depict a complex image of the relationship between tail thickness and dependence structure when it comes to Goodhart’s law as follows.

We prove that the heavy tailed goal - light tailed discrepancy weak and strong Goodhart are not possible. However, the two other cases we analyzed, - namely, the Gaussian case and the exponential goal - heavy tailed discrepancy,- show the importance of dependence structure when the relative tail thickness are not favorable or of the same kind. Indeed, in the heavy tailed discrepancy and exponential goal case, the dependence structure transforms a weak Goodhart case (in the independent case) into a strong Goodhart. Moreover, this strong Goodhart case gets worse as the the tail of the discrepancy gets lighter. This, with our precedent result, is to our sense a hint to the fact that tail thickness is not the
trigger
, but rather something that will
enable
the presence of Goodhart’s law. An unbalanced tail thickness of the true goal and the discrepancy enables some dependence structure to trigger a Goodhart’s law situation when optimizing.

Given our results, we consider that a particular attention should be taken in situations where the discrepancy and the true goal might not be independent. As an example, many industrially deployed algorithms suffer
from their evaluation on historic data, while this data is itself biased by the actual policy used to produce it and resulting in a feedback loop and distribution shift
[

,
19
]
.

We see several natural continuations to our work, and list some of the in the following paragraphs.

Empirical evaluation.

Our theoretical work highlights the prominent role played by the tail thickness in deciding different outcomes in Goodhart’s law and provides a nuanced picture of these outcomes. Empirical follow-ups of our work could bring even more nuance by performing fine-grained studies on such outcomes.

Notably, several experimental designs are possible in settings where we have access to the true goal and thus can evaluate the discrepancy

•

In supervised learning, with voluntarily biased dataset (by flipping a certain number of label depending on certain characteristics for example), to assess how the bias influence the metric’s tail and dependence structure with the goal (possibly making an bridge with fairness research).

•

In reinforcement learning, assessing the presence of heavy tailed reward “in the wild”
[
4
]
might be a good indicator of bad behavior possibility.

•

Testing the tail behavior of learned reward model with respect to gold standard (in a similar fashion as in
[
13
]
) is also to our sense an interesting direction to empirically assess the soundness of this research.

Access to the proxy metric’s tail.

As proxy metrics’ tail seems to determine the absence, presence and strength of Goodhart’s law, empirical study on metrics’ tail thickness within real world applications would be key to assess the importance of Goodhart’s law. Devising empirically grounded categories of tasks that are subject to heavy tail losses could offer a needed roadmap for practitioners to avoid useless or harmful metrics optimization or at least be aware of potential risky situations.

Aggregation of metrics.

In real world settings, we have access to neither the goal nor the discrepancy. However, we might have access to several proxy metrics
M_{1},M_{2},\dots
representative of the same overall goal
G
. Using the multiplicity of proxy metrics at disposal might be key to alleviate Goodhart’s law, notably by devising aggregation rules that would make an aggregated proxy metrics
\tilde{M}
more robust through aggregation and natural variance reduction. This would be to our sense key to alleviate alignment problem in concrete AI implementation.

Goodhart’s law and evasion attacks.

[
14
]
study Goodhart’s law within evasion attack settings
3
3

3

Often called adversarial attacks, we prefer the more specific term
evasion attacks
to distinguish them from other adversarial attacks
[
29
]
such as poisoning attacks and data-extraction/privacy attacks.
. So far we only considered settings where no malicious or adversarial player were present. In practical settings, AI faces adversarial behaviour
[
21
,
10
]
that must be anticipated to avoid catastrophic failure. Creating concrete threat models as well as defense mechanisms represent a large enough scope for development well beyond this paper. But doing so would benefit from the first step that was done in our work, in separating different the possible outcomes in the absence of an adversary, before adding more complexity and studying the interaction between an adversary and the inherent issues related to Goodhart’s law.

Auditing.

Auditing black box models is hard
[
11
]
. Peculiarly, developing robust and non-hackable metrics is of prime interest when auditing ML models. As such, understanding Goodhart’s law can inform and strengthen research on auditing ML models. Our work offers a starting point in formally categorizing what auditors should look for, namely, the different strengths of Goodhart’s law and the different statistical features leading to them.

Theoretical guarantees.

Theoretical guarantees on the possibility of harmful behaviour by AI prior to or at test time are key to mitigate the global risk of AI. Moreover, probabilistic framing of such guarantees is the subject of repeated calls to inform AI safety research
[
2
]
.

While providing such desired probabilistic formulations, our paper highlights the collapse of any prospect for alignment in the presence of heavy-tails and does so with much more nuance than has been previously done with additional assumptions. As such, this work contributes to a better theoretical understanding of AI misalignment, and can serve as the basis for a Goodhart-centered research agenda on AI alignment based on the list of possible continuations discussed in this section.

We will proceed by induction. It’s to be noted that for

, many of the terms in the sum will be null, but we still denote them as a derivative of a certain order of
Q
or
P

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}:"f_{Q}^{n}(P)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\sum_{i_{0}+...+i_{k}=n-k}\frac{P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+1)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{k}+1)}(x)}{Q(x)^{n+k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<n_{1}\leq i_{0}+1\\
\dots\\
n_{k-1}<n_{k}<n\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}(n_{j}+j)".

If
n=1
:

\displaystyle f(P)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\frac{P}{Q}}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=\frac{P^{\prime}}{Q}-\frac{PQ^{\prime}}{Q^{2}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{0}\frac{P^{(1)}}{Q}+(-1)^{1}\frac{PQ^{(1)}}{Q^{2}}.

We have the first step. Suppose we have
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is true. Let’s prove that
\mathcal{P}_{n+1}
is also true.

\displaystyle f^{n+1}(P)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(f^{n}(P))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\sum_{i_{0}+...+i_{k}=n-k}\frac{P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+1)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{k}+1)}(x)}{Q(x)^{n+k+1}}\sum_{0<n_{1}\leq i_{0}+1,\dots,n_{k-1}<n_{k}\leq k+\sum_{j=0}^{k}i_{j}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}(n_{j}+j)\right)^{\prime}

by hypothesis

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\sum_{i_{0}+...+i_{k}=n-k}\left(\frac{P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+1)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{k}+1)}(x)}{Q(x)^{n+k+1}}\right)^{\prime}\sum_{0<n_{1}\leq i_{0}+1,\dots,n_{k-1}<n_{k}\leq k+\sum_{j=0}^{k}i_{j}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}(n_{j}+j).

But we have :

\displaystyle\left(\frac{P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+1)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{k}+1)}(x)}{Q(x)^{n+k+1}}\right)^{\prime}=

\displaystyle\frac{(P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+1)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{k}+1)}(x))^{\prime}Q(x)^{(n+k+1)}}{Q(x)^{2(n+k+1)}}

\displaystyle-\frac{(n+k+1)Q^{\prime}Q^{n+k}(P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+1)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{k}+1)}(x))}{Q(x)^{2(n+k+1)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+1)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{k}+1)}(x))^{\prime}}{Q(x)^{n+k+1}}

\displaystyle-\frac{(n+k+1)Q^{\prime}(x)(P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+1)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{k}+1)}(x)}{Q(x)^{n+k+2}}).

Moreover, focusing on
(P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+1)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{k}+1)}(x))^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle(P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+1)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{k}+1)}(x))^{\prime}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k}P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+1)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{j}+2)}\dots Q^{(i_{k}+1)}(x).

So we have :

\displaystyle\left(\frac{P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+1)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{k}+1)}(x)}{Q(x)^{n+k+1}}\right)^{\prime}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+1)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{j}+2)}\dots Q^{(i_{k}+1)}(x)}{Q(x)^{n+k+1}}

\displaystyle-\frac{(n+k+1)Q^{\prime}(x)(P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+1)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{k}+1)}(x)}{Q(x)^{n+k+2}}).

Which with the entire sum gives :

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{0}+...+i_{k}=n-k}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left(\frac{P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+1)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{j}+2)}\dots Q^{(i_{k}+1)}(x)}{Q(x)^{n+k+1}}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{(n+k+1)Q^{\prime}(x)(P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+1)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{k}+1)}(x)}{Q(x)^{n+k+2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{0}+...+i_{k}=n+1-k}\frac{P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+1)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{k}+1)}(x)}{Q(x)^{n+k+1}}

\displaystyle-\sum_{i_{0}+...+i_{k}=n-k}\frac{(n+k+1)Q^{\prime}(x)(P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+1)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{k}+1)}(x)}{Q(x)^{n+k+2}}.

Plugging the second sum into the whole expression we get :

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k+1}\sum_{i_{0}+...+i_{k}=n-k}\frac{Q^{\prime}(x)(P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+1)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{k}+1)}(x))}{Q(x)^{n+k+2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<n_{1}\leq i_{0}+1\\
\dots\\
n_{k-1}<n_{k}<n\end{subarray}}(n+k+1)\prod_{j=1}^{k}(n_{j}+j).

Taking
k^{\prime}=k+1
:

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{n+1}(-1)^{k^{\prime}}\sum_{i_{0}+...+i_{k^{\prime}-1}=n+1-k^{\prime}}\frac{Q^{\prime}(x)(P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+1)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{k^{\prime}-1}+1)}(x))}{Q(x)^{n+k^{\prime}+1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<n_{1}\leq i_{0}+1\\
\dots\\
n_{k^{\prime}-2}<n_{k^{\prime}-1}<n\end{subarray}}(n+k^{\prime})\prod_{j=1}^{k^{\prime}-1}(n_{j}+j).

The first sum being :

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\sum_{i_{0}+...+i_{k}=n+1-k}\frac{P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+1)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{k}+1)}(x)}{Q(x)^{n+k+1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<n_{1}\leq i_{0}+1\\
\dots\\
n_{k-1}<n_{k}<n\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}(n_{j}+j).

The second one exactly complete it to top
n+1
. Indeed, the last terms of the second sum is the terms needed to complete at the rank
n+1
the formula. Moreover, each term of the second sum complete the first sum for the case when
n_{k}=n
. So we have :

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}(-1)^{k}\sum_{i_{0}+...+i_{k}=n+1-k}\frac{P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+1)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{k}+1)}(x)}{Q(x)^{n+k+1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<n_{1}\leq i_{0}+1\\
\dots\\
n_{k-1}<n_{k}<n+1\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}(n_{j}+j).

∎

We need, before going for the second big lemma, to calculate the derivative to any order of
Q
:

Lemma 6.7
.

If we denote
Q(x):=-g\left(\frac{m-g}{\eta}\right)^{b-1}
with
b>2
and
Q^{(n)}(x)
the derivative of
Q
to the n-th order we have :

Q^{(n)}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(b-i)_{+}(-1)^{n}\frac{(m-g)^{(b-n-1)_{+}}}{\eta^{b-1}}(nm-bg)^{\mathds{1}\{b\neq n\}}b^{\mathds{1}\{b=n\}}.

Proof.

Let’s proceed by induction. The induction hypothesis is that for all
n\in\left\llbracket 2\,;b\right\rrbracket
the following property is true :

P_{n}:"Q^{(n)}(x)=(-1)^{n}\frac{(m-x)^{(b-n-1)_{+}}}{\eta^{b-1}}(nm-bx)^{\mathds{1}\{b\neq n\}}b^{\mathds{1}\{b=n\}}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(b-i)_{+}".

We have :

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{(m-x)^{(b-2)}}{\eta^{b-1}}(bx-m).

For
n=2
,
n<b
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial Q^{\prime}}{\partial x}(x)

\displaystyle=b\frac{(m-g)^{b-2}}{\eta^{b-1}}-(b-2)\frac{(m-g)^{b-3}}{\eta^{b-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(m-g)^{b-3}}{\eta^{b-1}}(2bm+bg-b^{2}g-2m)

\displaystyle=\frac{(m-g)^{b-3}}{\eta^{b-1}}(2m-bg)(b-1).

For
n=2
,
n=b
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial Q^{\prime}}{\partial x}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{b}{\eta^{b-1}}.

So the property holds for
n=2
.

Now suppose we have some
n\in\mathrm{N}
such that the induction hypothesis holds. Then we have for
n+1
,
n+1<b
:

\displaystyle Q^{(n+1)}(x)

\displaystyle=\partial((-1)^{n}\frac{(m-x)^{b-n-1}}{\eta^{b-1}}(nm-bx)\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(b-i))/\partial x,

by induction hypothesis

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n+1}\frac{(m-x)^{b-n-2}}{\eta^{b-1}}(bm-bx+(b-n-1)(nm-bg))\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(b-i)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n+1}\frac{(m-x)^{b-n-2}}{\eta^{b-1}}((b-n-1)nm-(b-n)bx+bm)\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(b-i)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n+1}\frac{(m-x)^{b-n-2}}{\eta^{b-1}}([bn-n^{2}-n+b]m-(b-n)bx)\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(b-i)

with
(n+1)(b-n)=bn-n^{2}+b-n

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n+1}\frac{(m-x)^{b-n-2}}{\eta^{b-1}}((n+1)m-bg)\prod_{i=1}^{n}(b-i).

If
n+1=b
:

\displaystyle Q^{(n+1)}(x)

\displaystyle=\partial((-1)^{n}\frac{(nm-bx)}{\eta^{b-1}}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(b-i)_{+})/\partial x

by induction hypothesis

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n+1}\frac{\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(b-i)}{\eta^{b-1}}

as
b=n+1
,
b-n=1
:

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n+1}\frac{\prod_{i=0}^{n}(b-i)}{\eta^{b-1}}.

The part
n>b
is trivial.
∎

Lemma 6.8
.

For
l,k\in\mathbb{N}
, for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, if we denote
Q(g):=-g\left(\frac{m-g}{\eta}\right)^{b-1}
and
P_{l}(g):=\frac{g^{l}}{(m-g)^{s}}
,
b\in(1,\infty)
and
\eta\in(0,\infty)
we have :

\int_{0}^{m-\eta}\frac{g^{l}}{(m-g)^{s}}\exp(-g\left(\frac{m-g}{\eta}\right)^{b-1})dg\underset{m\rightarrow\infty}{=}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left[\frac{f_{Q^{\prime}}^{n}(P_{l}(g))}{Q^{\prime}(g)}\exp(Q(g))\right]_{0}^{\frac{m}{b+1}}+o(\frac{1}{m^{(N+1)b-l+s-1}}).

Proof.

First we have to cut it in half :

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{m-\eta}\frac{g^{l}}{(m-g)^{s}}\exp(-g\left(\frac{m-g}{\eta}\right)^{b-1})dg=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\int_{0}^{m/(b+1)}\frac{g^{l}}{(m-g)^{s}}\exp(-g\left(\frac{m-g}{\eta}\right)^{b-1})dg}_{I_{1}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\int_{m/(b+1)}^{m-\eta}\frac{g^{l}}{(m-g)^{s}}\exp(-g\left(\frac{m-g}{\eta}\right)^{b-1})dg}_{I_{2}}.

I_{2}
can be roughly bounded as it will be negligible :

\displaystyle\int_{m/(b+1)}^{m-\eta}\frac{g^{l}}{(m-g)^{s}}\exp(-g\left(\frac{m-g}{\eta}\right)^{b-1})dg\leq\frac{(m-\eta)^{l+1}}{\eta^{s}}\exp(-\frac{m}{b+1}).

For the first one, we note
Q(x)=-g\left(\frac{m-g}{\eta}\right)^{b-1}
. The derivative of
Q
is different from
0
on the whole interval
[0,\frac{m}{b+1}]
as it is equal to
Q^{\prime}(x)=\frac{(m-g)^{b-2}}{\eta^{b-1}}(bg-m)
. If we denote
P_{l}(g)=\frac{g^{l}}{(m-g)^{s}}
. With integration by part we have :

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{m/(b+1)}g^{l}\exp(-g\left(\frac{m-g}{\eta}\right)^{b-1})dg

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{P_{l}(g)}{Q^{\prime}(g)}\exp(Q(g))\right]_{0}^{\frac{m}{b+1}}-\int_{0}^{m/(b+1)}\frac{\partial\frac{P}{Q^{\prime}}}{\partial x}(g)\exp(Q(g))dg

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{P_{l}(g)}{Q^{\prime}(g)}\exp(Q(g))\right]_{0}^{\frac{m}{b+1}}-\int_{0}^{m/(b+1)}f_{Q^{\prime}}(P_{l})(g)\exp(Q(g))dg,

iterating
N+1
times we get (with
f^{0}
as the identity):

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left[\frac{f_{Q^{\prime}}^{n}(P_{l}(g))}{Q^{\prime}(g)}\exp(Q(g))\right]_{0}^{\frac{m}{b+1}}}_{S}+(-1)^{N+1}\int_{0}^{m/(b+1)}f_{Q^{\prime}}^{N+1}(P_{l})(g)\exp(Q(g))dg.

We need to show that
\int_{0}^{m/(b+1)}f_{Q^{\prime}}^{N+1}(P_{l})(g)\exp(Q(g))dg
is negligible in front of
S
. Using
Lemma

6.6
, we know that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

\displaystyle f_{Q^{\prime}}^{n}(P_{l})(x)\exp(Q(x))=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{0}+...+i_{k}=n-k\\
i_{0}\leq l\\
i_{1}\leq b-2\\
\vdots\\
i_{k}\leq b-2\end{subarray}}\frac{P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+2)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{k}+2)}(x)}{Q^{\prime}(x)^{n+k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<n_{1}\leq i_{0}+1\\
\dots\\
n_{k-1}<n_{k}<n\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}(n_{j}+j).

Using
Lemma

6.7
, we have for any
i<b

\displaystyle Q^{(i+2)}(x)=

\displaystyle\prod_{s=1}^{i-1}(b-s)_{+}(-1)^{i+2}\frac{(m-x)^{b-i-3}}{\eta^{b-1}}((i+2)m-bg).

And using Leibniz rule we get for
P
:

\displaystyle P^{(i_{0})}=\sum_{k=0}^{i_{0}}\binom{i_{0}}{k}\prod_{j=0}^{k}(l-j)\prod_{i=0}^{n-k}(s-j)g^{l-k}(m-g)^{-s-i_{0}+k}.

We can already bound the derivative of
P^{i_{0}}
by the fact that the integration is within
[0,m/(b+1)]
range :

\displaystyle P^{(i_{0})}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{i_{0}}\binom{i_{0}}{k}\prod_{j=0}^{k}(l-j)\prod_{i=0}^{i_{0}-k}(s-j)m^{l-s-i_{0}}(b+1)^{s+i_{0}-l}.

Then for a given set of
\mathcal{I}=\{i_{0},i_{1},...,i_{k}\}
with
\forall j\in[0,k],i_{j}>0
and
i_{0}+i_{1}+...+i_{k}=n-k
:

\displaystyle\frac{P^{(i_{0})}(x)Q^{(i_{1}+2)}(x)\dots Q^{(i_{k}+2)}(x)}{Q^{\prime}(x)^{n+k}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{q=0}^{i_{0}}\binom{i_{0}}{q}\frac{(b+1)^{s+i_{0}-l}l!s!(b-1)!^{k}(m-x)^{kb+i_{0}-n-2k}\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}((i+2)m-bx)}{(l-q)!(s-q)!\eta^{k(b-1)}(b-i_{1}-2)!\dots(b-i_{k}-2)!}\times

\displaystyle\frac{m^{l-s-i_{0}}\eta^{n+k(b-1)}}{(m-x)^{(n+k)(b-2)}(m-bx)^{n+k}},

using the fact that as
x\in[0,m/b+1]
we have
\frac{(i+2)m-bx}{m-bx}\leq(b+1)(i+1)+1\leq(b+1)(n+1)+1
and simplifying :

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{q=0}^{i_{0}}\binom{i_{0}}{q}\frac{(b+1)^{s+i_{0}-l}l!s!(b-1)!^{k}((b+1)(n+1)+1)^{k}}{(l-q)!(s-q)!(b-i_{1}-2)!\dots(b-i_{k}-2)!}\times\frac{m^{l-s-i_{0}}\eta^{n}}{(m-x)^{n(b-1)-i_{0}}(m-bx)^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{q=0}^{i_{0}}\binom{i_{0}}{q}\frac{(b+1)^{s+i_{0}-l}l!(b-1)!^{k}((b+1)(n+1)+1)^{k}}{(l-i_{0})!(b-i_{1}-2)!\dots(b-i_{k}-2)!}\times\frac{\eta^{n}(b+1)^{nb-i_{0}}}{m^{nb-l+s}b^{n(b-1)-i_{0}}}.

It’s to be noted that we can extend with no difficulty the bound to the case where one or several of the
i_{j}
are superior or equal to
b
: if it’s strictly superior the bound is trivial as the quantity is
0
, and if it’s
b
we simply have a constant. This allow us to extend the result to the case where
b=2
.

Using the fact that the preceding majoration is for any combination
\{i_{0},i_{1},...,i_{k}\}
with
\forall j\in[0,k],i_{j}>0
and
i_{0}+i_{1}+...+i_{k}=N+1-k
, we have for a
C
sufficiently big that does not depend on
m
:

\displaystyle m^{(N+1)b-l+s-1}\left|\int_{0}^{m/(b+1)}f_{Q^{\prime}}^{N+1}(P_{l})(g)\exp(Q(g))dg\right|\underset{\phantom{m\rightarrow\infty}}{\leq}

\displaystyle C\frac{m^{(N+1)b-l+s-1}}{m^{(N+1)b-l+s}}\times\left|\int_{0}^{m/(b+1)}\exp(Q(g))dg\right|

\displaystyle\underset{\phantom{m\rightarrow\infty}}{\leq}

\displaystyle C\frac{1}{m}\times\left|\int_{0}^{m/(b+1)}\exp(-g)dg\right|

\displaystyle\underset{\phantom{m\rightarrow\infty}}{\leq}

\displaystyle C\frac{1}{m}\times(1-\exp(-m/(b+1)))

\displaystyle\underset{m\rightarrow\infty}{\rightarrow}

\displaystyle 0.

This concludes the proof.

∎

Lemma 6.15
.

If

and the conditionnal density of

p_{\xi|G}(x):=G\exp(-G((\frac{x}{\eta})^{b-1}-1))\frac{x^{b-2}}{\eta^{b-1}}(b-1)\mathds{1}\{x>\eta\}
, then we have :

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(G+\xi>m\right)\underset{m\rightarrow\infty}{=}

\displaystyle\frac{\eta^{b-1}}{m^{b-1}}+\frac{2\eta^{2b-2}(b-1)}{m^{2b-1}}+\frac{3\eta^{3b-3}(b-1)(3b-2)}{m^{3b-1}}+\frac{8\eta^{4b-4}(b-1)(2b-1)(4b-3)}{m^{4b-1}}

\displaystyle+o\left(\frac{1}{m^{1-4b}}\right).

Proof.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(G+\xi>m\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(-g)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(-g(\left(\frac{x}{\eta}\right)^{b-1}-1)x^{b-2}\frac{g}{\eta^{b-1}}(b-1)\mathds{1}\{x>\eta,g>0,g+x>m\}dxdg

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-g)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(-g(\left(\frac{x}{\eta}\right)^{b-1}-1)x^{b-2}\frac{g}{\eta^{b-1}}(b-1)\mathds{1}\{x>\eta,x>m-g\}dxdg

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-g)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(-g(\left(\frac{x}{\eta}\right)^{b-1}-1)x^{b-2}\frac{g}{\eta^{b-1}}(b-1)\mathds{1}\{x>\max(\eta,m-g)\}dxdg,

as
g>m-\eta\implies\eta>m-g

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{m-\eta}\exp(-g)\underbrace{\int_{m-g}^{\infty}\exp(-g(\left(\frac{x}{\eta}\right)^{b-1}-1)x^{b-2}\frac{g}{\eta^{b-1}}(b-1)dx}_{A_{1}}dg+S_{G}(m-\eta).

The survival function of

Heavy tailed goal and light tailed discrepancy :

Lemma 6.16
.

Consider any random vector
\begin{pmatrix}X\\
Y\end{pmatrix}
, where
X
is light tailed. Then we have for some
s>0
:

\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(X>t\middle|Y\right)}{e^{-st}}\overset{a.s}{\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\rightarrow}}0.

Proof.

First, by definition of the light tailedness of
X
, we now that there exist
s>0
such that :

\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(X>t\right)}{e^{-st}}\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\rightarrow}0

We have by the tower property of the conditional expectation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{st}\mathbb{P}\left(X>t\middle|Y\right)\right]\underset{\phantom{t\rightarrow\infty}}{=}

\displaystyle e^{st}\mathbb{P}\left(X>t\right)

\displaystyle\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\rightarrow}

\displaystyle 0

First, let’s consider the discrete sequence of random variables :
\forall n\in\mathbb{N},Z_{n}=\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{n}>n\middle|Y\right)
, with
\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{n}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right)

Let
0<c<s
,
\varepsilon>0
. By markov inequality we have :

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(e^{cn}Z_{n}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{n}\right]e^{cn}}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right)e^{cn}}{\varepsilon}

So considering the sum we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{P}\left(e^{cn}Z_{n}\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{n}\right]e^{cn}}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right)e^{cn}}{\varepsilon}

However we know that
\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right)=o(e^{sn})
, for
s>c
. As such we know that :

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(X>n\right)e^{cn}}{\varepsilon}<\infty

This is true for any
\varepsilon>0
, which mean that we have by the Borel-Cantelli lemma :

e^{cn}Z_{n}\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\overset{a.s}{\rightarrow}}0

We now consider the continuous version of
Z_{n}
:
\forall t\in\mathbb{R}^{+},Z_{t}=\mathbb{P}\left(X>t\middle|Y\right)
.

Let
\omega\in\Omega
.
Z_{t}(\omega)
is monotonously decreasing as it’s a probability, as such we have :

\forall t\in\mathbb{R},Z_{t}(\omega)\leq Z_{\lfloor t\rfloor}(\omega)

Moreover :

\displaystyle\forall t\in\mathbb{R}^{+},\exists x\in[0,1)/\exp(ct)=\exp(c(\lfloor t\rfloor+x))

\displaystyle\implies\forall t\in\mathbb{R}^{+},\exp(ct)\leq\exp(c\lfloor t\rfloor)\exp(c)

Combining bot we get

\displaystyle Z_{t}(\omega)e^{ct}\underset{\phantom{t\rightarrow\infty}}{\leq}

\displaystyle e^{c}e^{c\lfloor t\rfloor}Z_{\lfloor t\rfloor}(\omega)

\displaystyle\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\rightarrow}

\displaystyle 0

This happen on some event
\Omega_{0}
with
\mathbb{P}\left(\Omega_{0}\right)=1
as we have
e^{cn}Z_{n}\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\overset{a.s}{\rightarrow}}0

We can conclude :

Z_{t}e^{ct}\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\overset{a.s}{\rightarrow}}0

∎
