The Utility of Intranasal Corticosteroids in Association With Dupilumab for Chronic Rhinosinusitis With Nasal Polyposis: A Real‐Life Multicenter Study by a Tuscany Group
Authors: Pietro Orlando, Giuseppe Licci, Marella Reale, Veronica Seccia, Alessia Giorli, Paolo Iannicelli, Dario Zaccari, Valentina Rossi, Jacopo Cambi, Stefano Berrettini, Marco Mandalà, Gianluca Leopardi, Antonio Sarno, Luca Muscatello, Simone Bocuzzi, Franco Trabalzini, Giandomenico Maggiore, Oreste Gallo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveChronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) is a persistent inflammatory condition often associated with other type‐2 diseases. Dupilumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting IL‐4Rα, has revolutionized the treatment of severe CRSwNP. Traditionally, intranasal corticosteroids (INCS) are used in association with Dupilumab, but their necessity remains uncertain.MethodsThis real‐life, multicentric observational study was conducted across seven centers in Tuscany, Italy, involving 123 patients with severe, uncontrolled type‐2 CRSwNP treated with Dupilumab (300 mg/2 weeks) from January 2021 to December 2022. Parameters assessed included Sinonasal Outcome Test‐22 (SNOT‐22), Nasal Polyps Score (NPS), Sniffin' Sticks identification test (SSIT‐16), and Lund‐Mackay scores (LMS), along with blood eosinophil counts and adherence to INCS. Statistical analyses were performed to compare outcomes between INCS adherent and non‐adherent groups.ResultsA total of 123 patients, 53 men and 70 women with a mean age of 56.6 years were included. Significant improvements were observed in SNOT‐22, NPS, SSIT, and LMS over 12 months. INCS adherence was incomplete in 23.8% of patients. Initially, non‐adherent patients demonstrated a faster SNOT‐22 reduction, while adherent patients showed better NPS and SSIT improvements early on. By 12 months, differences between groups were not statistically significant. No demographic or clinical factors predicted INCS adherence.ConclusionDupilumab is effective in managing type‐2 CRSwNP, potentially without INCS. While INCS may hasten initial improvements, long‐term outcomes are comparable with or without their use. Future randomized controlled trials are necessary to further clarify INCS's role in combination with Dupilumab.

Introduction

Chronic rhinosinusitis with Nasal Polyps (CRSwNP) is a complex pathology characterized by perpetual inflammation of nasal and paranasal cavities driven by type‐2 cytokines such as IL‐4, IL‐5, and IL‐13 [1]. Therefore, it may be often associated with other pathologies that share the same underlying process, such as allergic rhinitis (AR), asthma, nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs‐exacerbated respiratory disease (N‐ERD), and atopic dermatitis (AD) [2, 3]. CRSwNP has been historically treated with intranasal corticosteroids (INCS) and endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS) in case of unsatisfactory disease control with medical treatment. However, its severe symptoms (nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, and olfactory impairment) scarcely regress with the standard medical therapy and nasal polyps have a high recurrence rate even after appropriate ESS [4]. For all the reasons mentioned above, the recent introduction of biological therapies has deeply revolutionized the management of patients affected by severe and recalcitrant type‐2 CRSwNP. Particularly, Dupilumab is a fully‐humanized monoclonal antibody that acts by inhibiting the α subunit of the IL‐4 receptor (IL‐4Rα), thus blocking IL‐4 and IL‐13, two out of three key drivers of type‐2 inflammation [5]. Dupilumab was initially approved as an add‐on therapy with the standard‐of‐care INCS [6, 7]. The safety and efficacy of the association INCS‐dupilumab have been well documented in phase 3 clinical trials and real‐life phase 4 studies [6, 8]. Nonetheless, the real role of INCS in CRSwNP control in patients receiving dupilumab still remains unknown as only a few studies explored patients' adherence to topical therapy.

Our multicentric study first aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of Dupilumab in treating patients affected by CRSwNP in seven different centers from the Tuscany region (Italy). Secondary endpoints were to assess adherence to INCS in this specific population and to ascertain whether adherence to INCS may have an impact on outcomes.

Materials and Methods

This real‐life, multicentric, observational study involved seven different hospital centers throughout the Tuscany region in Italy. All the consecutive patients affected by severe and uncontrolled type‐2 CRSwNP and treated with dupilumab from January 1, 2021 to December 31, 2022 were enrolled in the present study. For type‐2 CRSwNP diagnostic purposes, we considered blood eosinophilic count > 250/μL or total IgE serum level ≥ 100/KU/L or ≥ 10 eosinophils/HPF at the histopathological exam of polypoid tissue biopsy according to EPOS2020 guidelines [1]. All of the patients received subcutaneous Dupilumab 300 mg/2 weeks as an add‐on therapy to INCS (1 puff per nostril twice a day). They were evaluated at baseline (t0), and 3 (t1), 6 (t2), and 12 months (t3) after starting Dupilumab. Each center followed the Italian Agency of Drugs (AIFA) criteria for Dupilumab prescription: age ≥ 18 years old; diagnosis of diffuse CRSwNP at an endoscopic exam and CT scan; severe disease, defined as per NPS ≥ 5 and SNOT‐22 ≥ 50; failure or intolerance to previous medical (≥ 2 cycles of systemic corticosteroids, SCS) or surgical treatment [7]. At the first inpatient visit, demographic and anamnestic characteristics were collected: sex; age; blood eosinophils count; comorbidities, such as AR, asthma, N‐ERD, and AD; history of previous ESS. Blood eosinophil count, patients' adherence to treatment, and disease response were evaluated at 3, 6, and 12 months after Dupilumab's first injection. Adherence to INCS was evaluated at each follow‐up scheduled visit by asking patients about their ease of taking prescribed medications. Study participants were defined as non‐adherent in case of INCS usage < 5 days/week at any time point during the observational period.

Patients fulfilling at least one out of the following five criteria were considered as “responders”, as per EPOS2020 guidelines: improved quality of life, enhanced sense of smell, nasal polyps size decrease, reduced need for SCS, reduced impact of comorbidities [1]. The SinoNasal Outcome Test‐22 (SNOT‐22) questionnaire was used to evaluate patients' quality of life [9]. Sense of smell was examined through a 16‐odors Sniffin’ Sticks identification test (SSIT‐16) [10]. Finally, each patient obtained a videoendoscopic exam of the sinonasal fossae to evaluate nasal polyps score (NPS) according to the Meltzer staging system, and the CT scan was evaluated at baseline and after 12 months with the Lund‐Mackay score (LMS) [11, 12]. Possible side effects were investigated and promptly treated. Further sub‐analysis was performed to establish Dupilumab effectiveness according to sex, type‐2 comorbidities, previous ESS, and adherence to INCS. Further analysis was performed on the non‐adherent subpopulation. Notably, we sought demographic and clinical factors that may predict INCS adherence as well as INCS impact on disease control.

Descriptive and mean values were calculated with Microsoft Excel 2019. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to check for the normality of data. The unpaired two‐tailed Student's t‐test and the Mann–Whitney test (for normally and non‐normally distributed variables, respectively) were used to compare the mean scores for each item between subgroups. The paired t‐test and the Wilcoxon signed ranks test were used to compare the scores between the first and the follow‐up visit. The statistical analyses were performed with SPSS software (IBM Corp. Released 2020. IBM SPSS Statistics for Macintosh, Version 27.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). All statistical tests were two‐sided and a p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

An overall number of 123 patients were included in the present study. There were 53 men (43.1%) and 70 (56.9%) women, with a mean age of 56.6 years. With regard to comorbidities, 87 (70.7%) patients had asthma, 69 (56.1%) had AR, 32 (26.0%) had N‐ERD, and 7 (5.7%) had AD. Regarding previous sinonasal surgery, 114 (92.7%) patients had a history of at least 1 previous ESS, while 9 (7.3%) patients were surgically naive (Table 1). Mean SNOT‐22, SSIT‐16, and NPS at baseline were 60.9, 4.3, and 5.9 respectively. The mean blood eosinophils count was 456.8/mm3 before starting dupilumab.

The mean blood eosinophils count showed a progressive increase at t1 and t2 but the value tended to normalize at t3. Curiously, patients affected by AR had a significantly greater SNOT‐22 reduction at t1 compared to non‐AR ones, but results tended to be similar at 6 and 12 months. In addition, AR patients achieved a significantly higher gain of sense of smell at 12 months. On the contrary, asthmatic patients had a significantly lower NPS reduction at 3 months, even though the difference disappeared in the follow‐up period. On the other hand, no differences were found comparing patients with or without N‐ERD and AD. Patients with a history of previous ESS showed a lower mean reduction of SNOT‐22 after 1 year compared to surgically naive patients.

Regarding INCS adherence, 23.8% of patients voluntarily stopped INCS against medical advice throughout the study period. Specifically, 14.8% of patients declared to have interrupted INCS within 3 months (t1) from the first anti‐IL4R injection, and 9.0% in the interval t1 − t2. No patients reported to have interrupted ICNS usage in the interval t2 − t3. No demographic or clinical factors were identified to be associated with an incomplete adherence to INCS. We found that non‐adherent patients achieved a significantly greater reduction in SNOT‐22 at t2, however, differences were not statistically significant after 1 year. Conversely, adherent patients showed a significantly higher improvement in mean NPS and SSIT at 3 months, but the gain in sense of smell tended to gradually reverse after 12 months, albeit not statistically significant. Considering the subpopulation of non‐adherent patients alone, no differences in terms of dupilumab efficacy according to comorbidities (AR, asthma, or N‐ERD) and history of previous ESS were found. Of 109 patients who completed the follow‐up period, 3 (2.4%) had to discontinue Dupilumab: 2 out of 3 were switched to another biologic because of severe hypereosinophilia arising in the interval t1 − t2 not recovered even after high dose SCS, while the third one required rescue surgery because of poor disease control as per EPOS2020 guidelines. By applying EPOS/EUFOREA2023 criteria, 5.6% and 92.5% of patients were respectively considered as “good” and “excellent” responders at t2, while “good” and “excellent” responders were respectively 2.8% and 96.2% at t3.

Discussion

The management of patients affected by severe and recalcitrant type‐2 CRSwNP has drastically changed after the approval of Dupilumab. Dupilumab acts by blocking IL‐4 and IL‐13 and was demonstrated to be safe and effective in reducing the clinical burden of type‐2 CRSwNP in phase 3 clinical trials and phase 4 real‐life studies. Our results were in line with SINUS‐24 and ‐52 trials and phase 4 studies previously published [6, 8]. We demonstrated a rapid and enduring improvement in quality of life, sense of smell, nasal polyps size, and sinonasal cavities opacity at CT scans in patients under Dupilumab. The mean blood eosinophil count reached the summit at 6 months, with only 2 patients (1.8%) having to discontinue Dupilumab because of severe hypereosinophilia (absolute eosinophil count ≥ 1500/mm3) that did not recover even after a short‐term cycle of SCS [13, 14]. The improvement was irrespective of sex, age, and smoking habits. Similarly, no dissimilarities were found according to asthma and N‐ERD, in agreement with the existing literature. In contrast, patients suffering from AR reported a higher gain in SSIT after 1 year of treatment compared to non‐AR ones, probably due to the synergic effects of the anti‐IL4R on both pathologies. Moreover, surgically naive patients reported a greater mean reduction in SNOT‐22 after 12 months compared to post‐ESS patients, while no differences were observed regarding NPS and smell function, in contrast with previously published studies [8, 15, 16, 17]. However, the small number of surgically naive patients (7.3%) did not permit us to draw firm conclusions. Finally, some differences were highlighted when considering patients' adherence to INCS. Local steroids have always been considered the first‐line strategy for patients suffering from CRSwNP, chronic rhinosinusitis without nasal polyposis, and AR due to their anti‐inflammatory activity [18]. Nonetheless, patients' adherence has historically been documented to be suboptimal in several previous studies [19, 20]. Many factors have been identified to negatively influence INCS adherence among CRS and AR patients: male sex; age 30–60 years old; smoking habits; absence of type‐2 comorbidities; no history of previous ESS; lower SNOT‐22 at baseline; fear of side effects; cost‐related issues [21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. Consequently, Dupilumab was approved as an add‐on therapy to the standard‐of‐care INCS [6, 7]. Still, some real‐life studies reported incomplete adherence even in patients receiving the anti‐IL4R [15, 19, 27, 28]. Pecorari et al. documented the worst adherence rate (61.5%) but no differences in terms of SNOT‐22, NPS, and smell function were observed after 6 months [27]. Similarly, Campion et al. did not find any significant difference among subpopulations concerning INCS or SCS administration [28]. Finally, De Corso et al. reported an adherence rate of 85.0%, and no dissimilarities were observed except for a faster NPS reduction among adherent patients [15]. Our findings slightly diverged from previously published data. We found a significantly faster NPS reduction (−1.7 vs. −1.3) and SSIT improvement (+5.3 vs. +1.3) in the adherent group. On the other hand, we found a significantly faster SNOT‐22 decrease in the non‐adherent population (−29.4 vs. −36.4) that was maintained even after 6 months (−36.6 vs. −47.4). However, results tended to align after 1 year and no statistically significant differences were evident at the end of the study period. Our findings did not elucidate the real contribution of INCS in controlling type‐2 CRSwNP when associated with Dupilumab. In particular, we noticed a discrepancy in the first 6 months of the study period between objective (NPS and SSIT) and subjective (SNOT‐22) scores, while results tended to align and be comparable between adherent and non‐adherent patients after 12 months. We supposed that INCS administration was voluntarily stopped within the first 3 months only by those patients who immediately felt excellent disease control and full faith in the brand‐new therapeutic option. However, non‐adherent patients kept on reporting a significantly lower SNOT‐22 even after 6 months and the score was similar to those of adherent patients after 12 months. Furthermore, no disadvantages were reported by non‐adherent patients but a slower NPS and smell function improvement compared to adherent patients. Finally, no differences in outcomes were found when analyzing the non‐adherent subpopulation according to comorbidities, revealing that there are no specific subgroups that may benefit more from INCS than others. Unfortunately, the low number of surgically naive patients did not permit us to draw firm conclusions about possible differences in INCS effectiveness according to the history of previous ESS. We may speculate that patients' well‐being, the heavy price of INCS (that in Italy has to be paid by patients), and the possible local side effects of INCS may lead to the withdrawal of the local therapy [22, 24, 25, 26, 29]. However, clinicians should educate patients on medical adherence to INCS. Although there was relative reassurance that the abandonment of INCS did not imply a detrimental impact on disease control in the short‐term period, the long‐term effects on nasal polyps control remained unknown.

The retrospective nature of the analysis, the small number of patients, and the short follow‐up period represented the major limitations of this study, as well as the low number of surgically naive patients. In addition, patients' adherence may be challenging to investigate, as well as the real impact of INCS on disease control. Firstly, INCS usage may be evaluated only by indirect methods, thus by asking patients if they took INCS twice daily as prescribed by healthcare providers. Questioning the patient is a relatively simple method to assess adherence, however, patients may misrepresent the real facts and overestimate their compliance. Talking nonjudgmentally to patients may help to make patients feel comfortable and tell the truth [30]. Secondly, we only established three different checkpoints to measure adherence, however, we recognize patients may have interrupted INCS at any moment during the follow‐up period. Finally, we only considered non‐adherent those patients taking ICNS < 5 days/week, although a generally accepted cut‐off has not been identified. Probably, dose simplification (e.g., 2 puffs per nostril once a day) and frequent inpatient visits may help to maximize patients's adherence [30, 31].

Further studies with longer follow‐up periods, a more detailed smell assessment including a threshold and a discrimination test, and larger matched‐pair, double‐blinded, randomized cohorts are mandatory to comprehensively understand the real contribution of the INCS in CRSwNP control in patients under Dupilumab and hopefully identify subpopulations that may benefit more from INCS than others toward a therapeutic regimen as tailored as possible.

Conclusion

The anti‐IL4R Dupilumab was originally approved as an add‐on therapy to the standard‐of‐care INCS for the treatment of type‐2 CRSwNP. In light of our results, we may speculate that dupilumab may be effective in the treatment of patients affected by type‐2 CRSwNP even as a single therapy. Although INCS play a well‐known role in reducing local sinonasal inflammation as demonstrated by the faster improvement of NPS and SSIT in the adherent group, our data demonstrated a faster and long‐lasting SNOT‐22 decrease by non‐adherent patients. Nevertheless, results aligned after 1 year, and no differences were found in the non‐adherent subpopulation when considering comorbidities and prior ESS. In addition, non‐adherent patients saved money formerly used for INCS, avoided local discomfort or even side effects provoked by INCS, and enjoyed a higher quality of life as demonstrated by the rapid and enduring improvement of SNOT‐22. In conclusion, our findings may not allow us to draw firm conclusions about the actual impact of INCS on CRSwNP, even if we observed that the discontinuation of INCS did not have such a harmful effect on disease control. A randomized double‐blinded phase Ⅲ trial would be desirable to understand the real effect of INCS on type‐2 CRSwNP.
