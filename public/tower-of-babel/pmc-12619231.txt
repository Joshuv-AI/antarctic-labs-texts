“We’ve already been doing this, we just didn’t call it implementation science”: a call for shared language in global health
Authors: Amelia E. Van Pelt, Kylie Dougherty, Ucheoma Nwaozuru, Chisom Obiezu-Umeh
Published: 2025

Implementation science has increased in low- and middle-income countries. However, when proposing implementation science approaches to colleagues in these settings, they often remark that they have already conducted similar research. This comment amplifies this observation and the need for a shared implementation science language to strengthen global health efforts.

Background

Advancing global health requires prioritizing the implementation of evidence-based innovations (EBIs). Literature has suggested that it takes approximately 17 years to integrate EBIs into routine care [1], which presents a challenge for improving the health of populations. Multifaceted factors in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) exacerbate this research-to-practice gap and result in poor rates of EBI adoption in these settings [2]. Given the burden of disease in LMICs, this gap is problematic. Fortunately, implementation science, a discipline that has gained popularity in the past two decades, focuses on strategies that can overcome these challenges, especially in LMICs [3]. Implementation science refers to the scientific study of methods to increase the uptake of EBIs [4]. Methodologies involve the identification of factors that affect the integration of EBIs, the design of strategies to address such factors, and the evaluation of the success of implementation strategies.

Over the past several years, the application of implementation science in LMICs has increased [5, 6]. Implementation scientists have proposed these methodologies to address diverse global health problems, such as increasing the use of HIV prevention. However, partnership with research colleagues in LMICs often results in comments such as, “We’ve already been doing this; We just didn’t call it implementation science.” Although the field of implementation science is relatively new, partners often express that they have long applied some of its principles, concepts, and methods without the use of formal terminology during the implementation of interventions. Efforts to understand implementation processes have, in fact, preceded more systematic approaches to integrating EBIs into practice settings, thus minimizing the novelty of implementation science approaches. Acknowledgement of these discussions and pursuit of implementation science-related research with consistent labeling is paramount for advancing the field.

In this comment, we highlight the overlap between implementation science and global health to advocate for using shared terminology to describe implementation research in LMICs.

Conceptual overlap between implementation science and related disciplines

Various reasons may contribute to the reactions that colleagues in LMICs are already conducting research similar to implementation science, including the origin of the field, overlap with other disciplines, and global health practices. As a field that gained momentum over the past 20 years, implementation science acknowledges that it draws upon other disciplines. For example, implementation science borrows theoretical insights from public health, sociology, system science, behavioral science, and organizational theory [7]. Many of the tools in implementation science integrate knowledge from other fields, such as the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research, a well-known implementation determinants framework, that synthesized theories from other frameworks. Thus, implementation science is a multidisciplinary field rather than a pure discipline. This derivation from different disciplines may contribute to the familiarity of approaches and commentary about the lack of novelty of implementation science methods among non-implementation scientists conducting global health research.

Further, the conceptual overlap between implementation science and related disciplines reinforces the belief that many researchers are already employing implementation science approaches in LMICs. For example, disciplines such as quality improvement, program evaluation, and health systems research share commonalities and differences with implementation science [8]. Articulating these distinctions and similarities is critical for enhancing conceptual clarity and fostering effective communication and collaboration across these research fields. First, quality improvement focuses on adapting care processes for a specific context through small-scale, iterative changes, whereas implementation science employs methods to understand and address general gaps in care that apply beyond the local setting to influence the uptake of EBIs at scale [8]. Second, program evaluation focuses on assessing the impact of specific programs on health, whereas implementation science investigates the impact of strategies on EBI use [9]. Third, health systems research explores the operations and improvements of systems, whereas implementation science focuses on translating the use of EBIs within those systems [10].

In addition, many global health researchers and practitioners have employed methods that align with implementation science. For instance, engaging stakeholders, adapting interventions to local contexts, and addressing system-level barriers are central to global health work. However, researchers often describe these approaches using different terminology, such as “program delivery,” “scale-up,” and “operational research.” This alignment in methods reflects the notion that researchers in LMICs have already been doing implementation research without using the formal terminology of the discipline. Recognizing this alignment validates the expertise and contributions of researchers in LMICs while providing opportunities for improving communication and collaboration.

Use of shared implementation science language for global health

Given the similarities in approaches but differences in terminology, we propose that adopting a shared language offers promise for improving global health. We do not suggest that colleagues in LMICs must reframe or duplicate efforts to fit within an implementation science model. Rather, we posit that implementation science can provide a useful structure, repository of tools, and shared vocabulary that can support learning and improve global health.

Utilizing shared language would improve the replicability of research. By adopting standardized terminology and frameworks, colleagues in LMICs can improve the clarity and consistency of the dissemination of research related to EBI implementation. For example, implementation science offers a thorough repository of theories, models, and frameworks, such as the Health Equity Implementation Framework to identify implementation determinants (i.e., factors that affect EBI implementation), the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change taxonomy for designing implementation strategies (i.e., actions taken to increase EBI uptake), and RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance) for evaluating implementation outcomes (i.e., measures of the process of EBI implementation). Standardized definitions would facilitate cross-context reciprocal learning, as researchers would better understand, adapt, and build upon work from other teams. Researchers in LMICs are conducting work to address complex health problems, and implementation science offers a framework to amplify their reach by using non-discipline-specific, generalizable language understood by multidisciplinary audiences. Increasing the ability to learn from successes in one context and replicate or scale up such efforts in other settings through shared language would contribute to the improvement of health for all.

Further, adopting implementation science language would improve the rigor and speed of data synthesis to create more generalizable knowledge. Currently, literature often reports projects using discipline-specific language, which can silo findings and limit opportunities for other contexts. Through the use of a shared language, researchers can systematically categorize and compare findings across diverse studies. Such comparison would facilitate meta-analyses to identify patterns or mechanisms of successful implementation that transcend individual settings. For instance, researchers could identify cross-cutting factors that may affect implementation and integrate such insights into the design of their implementation research. In LMICs with substantial contextual variation, this generalizable knowledge could serve as a valuable starting point for implementation efforts to expedite the research process. Further, building upon such work would contribute to the global evidence base to create more evidence-based policies and practices for global health contexts.

Moreover, the integration of implementation science language into global health research would enhance collaboration. Effective collaboration between multidisciplinary teams requires clear communication. Utilizing shared language from implementation science would facilitate alignment in research goals, methodological approaches, and evaluation metrics. Such clarity would improve understanding among team members, foster stronger partnerships, and support the co-creation of implementation research. Co-creation, in turn, has the potential to promote greater equity in global health by ensuring that scholars in high-income countries are not predominantly leading implementation research. Further, it enables co-production of knowledge in the field of implementation science allowing for reciprocal learnings across settings. The adoption of shared language helps balance power differentials. Ultimately, transparent and collaborative communication would build more equitable partnerships and drive stronger, more sustainable global health research.

In addition to the implications for research, utilizing shared implementation science language in global health would increase opportunities for colleagues in LMICs. Multiple funding mechanisms have elicited proposals for global implementation science specifically. By using methodologies, terminology, and frameworks from the discipline to respond to such calls, researchers can acquire the funding necessary to continue the work that they are already conducting. In addition, various capacity-building initiatives have emerged to train individuals from LMICs in implementation science, e.g., the HIV, Infectious Disease, and Global Health Implementation Research Institute (HIGH-IRI) at Washington University (https://sites.wustl.edu/highiri/); Global Alliance for Chronic Diseases (GACD) which operates across multiple countries (https://www.gacd.org/). Such training equips fellows with the skills to apply an implementation science lens to their work, as well as provides a network of colleagues who understand the same language to serve as collaborators. Thus, training not only facilitates the use of shared language but also supports the potential for more equitable partnership and co-creation of knowledge.

Conclusions

Implementation science is increasingly applied in LMICs, yet conceptual overlap with related disciplines obscures its distinct contributions to global health. The recurring comment on the similarity of approaches is not a dismissal of implementation science, but rather an affirmation of the relevance of the field and a call for shared language. Utilizing common implementation science terminology can improve replicability, produce generalizable knowledge, enhance collaboration, and increase opportunities. Global health colleagues can draw upon the structure and insights from implementation science to support their goals, facilitate greater knowledge exchange, and increase effective implementation across contexts to improve global health.
