Adsorption of methylene blue onto clay: experiments and statistical physics interpretation
Authors: Hedi Jedli, Souhail Mohammed Bouzgarrou, Rym Hassani, Ehab Sabi, Khalifa Slimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study explored the adsorption of methylene blue (MB) from aqueous solutions using natural clay as an adsorbent. The effects of contact time, initial dye concentration, adsorbent dosage, and pH were systematically evaluated. Analysis of correlation coefficients (R2) for the kinetic and isotherm models indicated that the adsorption kinetics follow a pseudo-first-order model, while the Langmuir isotherm provided the best fit to the experimental data at 303, 313, and 323 K. To further elucidate the adsorption mechanism, a generalized model based on statistical physics was applied. Steric analysis examined the number of dye molecules bound per active site, the estimated number of layers formed, receptor site density, and overall adsorption capacity. This model also yielded two adsorption energies associated with MB-clay and MB-MB interactions, ranging from 29.845 to 31.191 kJ/mol and from 21.981 to 24.692 kJ/mol, respectively. Thermodynamic parameters confirmed that MB adsorption is spontaneous under all studied conditions. Overall, these results highlight natural clay as a cost-effective and efficient adsorbent for removing dyes from aqueous solutions.

Introduction

Industrial activities have significantly impacted freshwater quality1. Industries such as textiles, plastics, and food processing discharge wastewater containing large amounts of strongly colored organic matter2. In particular, the textile industry generates effluents rich in synthetic dyes, including methylene blue, which is widely used for coloring materials such as paper, cotton, wood, leather, and silk3,4. These dyes are complex aromatic compounds that can be carcinogenic, non-biodegradable, and toxic to both human health and aquatic organisms5,6. The discharge of dye-containing wastewater into natural water bodies can reduce light penetration, disrupt photosynthesis in aquatic ecosystems, and lead to serious environmental and ecological problems7. Consequently, the effective treatment of dye-contaminated wastewater has become a major environmental challenge and an important research focus in the field of water purification. Methylene blue can cause harmful effects in humans, including tissue necrosis, increased heart rate, shock, and vomiting7. Therefore, the removal of such pollutants from wastewater is essential to protect both human health and the environment. Various treatment methods have been developed for dye removal from wastewater, including adsorption8, photocatalysis9, and biological treatment10. Among these techniques, adsorption is the most widely applied due to its cost-effectiveness, simple operation, and high removal efficiency11. The performance of the adsorption process largely depends on the properties of the adsorbent, such as its surface area, toxicity, regenerability, availability, and cost12. Common adsorbents used for methylene blue removal include activated carbon, zeolite, kaolinite, bentonite and clay minerals, which have shown promising adsorption capacities in wastewater treatment applications13–15. Tunisian clays are natural, abundant, and geologically diverse materials primarily composed of clay minerals such as kaolinite, illite, and smectite. These clays are distinguished by their specific mineralogical composition, particle size distribution, plasticity, and cation exchange capacity, which strongly influence their suitability for various industrial applications, including ceramics, construction materials, and catalysis. In addition to these traditional uses, Tunisian clays have recently attracted increasing attention for environmental applications, particularly as low-cost and efficient adsorbents for wastewater treatment16,17. Their high surface area, layered structure, and ion-exchange properties make them promising materials for the removal of organic pollutants, including synthetic dyes, from aqueous solutions. Recently, various types of clay, including kaolinitic clays18, Tunisian smectite19, and hectorite clay–alginate composites20, have been investigated for the removal of dyes from wastewater. The effectiveness of clay as an adsorbent is mainly attributed to its high ion-exchange capacity, chemical stability, natural abundance, and large surface area21. These properties make clay-based materials promising candidates for wastewater treatment applications. Consequently, numerous studies have focused on the adsorption of methylene blue onto clay materials as an effective method for treating dye-contaminated wastewater22–27. Several studies have demonstrated that Tunisian clays can effectively adsorb cationic dyes such as methylene blue. The adsorption process is influenced by several factors, including pH, contact time, and the initial dye concentration. The underlying mechanisms mainly involve electrostatic interactions, ion exchange, and hydrogen bonding between the dye molecules and the surface of the clay minerals28–30.

The present study aimed to evaluate the potential of Tunisian raw clay for the adsorption of MB. The effects of contact time, initial dye concentration, and adsorbent dose were investigated. Both classical isotherm models and advanced statistical physics models were applied to determine physicochemical parameters, including the number of MB molecules per active site (n), receptor site density (Nm), adsorption capacity at saturation (Qm), and adsorption energy at different temperatures. The findings highlight a practical approach to enhancing the value of accessible, low-cost materials for effective MB removal.

Methodology section

Tunisian clay was chosen to study its ability to adsorb methylene blue (MB). The natural clay were collected from the BORMA oil field reservoir in southern Tunisia22. The clay was used in its natural state, with no additional treatment except for drying at 100 °C for 24 h to remove moisture, and then stored in a desiccator. Its main composition consists of SiO₂ (22.18%), CaO (18.91%), Fe₂O₃ (13.09%), and Al₂O₃ (11.96%), along with small amounts of K₂O and other trace oxides. Methylene blue (MB) with a molecular weight of 319.86 g/mol, 99% purity, and λmax = 664 nm was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich.

Adsorption experiments

Adsorption experiments were conducted using 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 100 mL of MB solution at concentrations of 5–40 mg/L. Clay dosages ranging from 0.025 to 0.2 g were added, and the effect of pH (2–12) was investigated. After adsorption, the clay was filtered, and the remaining MB concentration was determined using UV–Vis spectroscopy.

Results and discussion

Effect of contact time and initial dye concentration

The adsorption of Methylene Blue (MB) onto clay was found to depend strongly on both contact time and the initial dye concentration (Fig. 1). A rapid uptake occurred during the first 5 min, after which the adsorption gradually slowed, reaching near equilibrium at approximately 40 min. This fast initial phase is attributed to the large number of available active sites on the clay surface and the electrostatic attraction between the negatively charged clay and the positively charged MB molecules33.Fig. 1Effect of contact time and initial concentration of MB on the adsorption.

Effect of contact time and initial concentration of MB on the adsorption.

When the initial MB concentration was increased from 5 to 40 mg/L, the adsorption capacity increased from 3.8 mg/g to 25 mg/g. This trend can be explained by the enhanced driving force for mass transfer at higher concentrations, which allows more MB molecules to access and occupy the adsorption sites. At lower concentrations, some sites remain unoccupied, whereas higher concentrations improve the likelihood of dye–surface interactions, resulting in greater coverage of the clay surface34.

Effect of adsorbent dose

Figure 2 illustrates the effect of clay dosage on the removal of Methylene Blue (MB) at different initial dye concentrations. Increasing the amount of clay significantly improved MB removal due to the greater number of adsorption sites and the larger surface area available at higher adsorbent doses. More MB molecules can bind to the clay surface, enhancing overall removal efficiency. Beyond a certain dosage, however, the adsorption rate plateaued, indicating that most dye molecules in solution had already been captured and additional adsorbent offered limited benefit. Under the experimental conditions, the maximum removal reached approximately 97%, demonstrating the high efficiency of clay as an adsorbent34. This behavior indicates the presence of an optimal adsorbent dose, beyond which further increases do not substantially improve removal and may be economically unnecessary. The results also show that the required adsorbent dose increases with higher initial dye concentrations, reflecting the balance between available surface sites and the total number of MB molecules in solution35.Fig. 2Effect of the adsorbent dose.

Effect of the adsorbent dose.

Effect of pH

The initial pH of the dye solution plays a crucial role in adsorption, as it influences the ionization state of the dye, the dissociation of functional groups on the adsorbent, and the surface charge of the clay. For cationic dyes such as Methylene Blue (MB), adsorption is generally favored under higher pH conditions, where the clay surface is more negatively charged, promoting stronger electrostatic attraction between the dye and the adsorbent. Conversely, at low pH, excess H⁺ ions compete with MB molecules for adsorption sites, reducing removal efficiency34.

Figure 3 illustrates the effect of pH on MB adsorption over a range of 2 to 12. The results show that adsorption efficiency increased with pH and reached a maximum at pH 5, achieving approximately 97% removal. Beyond this point, further increases in pH did not significantly enhance adsorption, suggesting that the surface charge of clay and the availability of active sites were already optimized at near-neutral conditions35.Fig. 3Effect of pH solution on the adsorbed quantity.

This implies that the adsorption mechanism likely involves electron sharing or exchange between MB molecules and active sites on the clay surface, rather than simple physical adsorption35.

The strong agreement with the pseudo-second-order model also supports the interpretation that surface interactions, such as covalent bonding or strong electrostatic attraction, dominate the adsorption process, and that diffusion or mass transfer limitations play a secondary role in controlling the overall kinetics35.

The adsorption of MB on the two adsorbents is proposed to occur in a series of steps: first, the dye molecules move from the bulk solution through the boundary layer to reach the external surface of the adsorbent (film diffusion), and then they are transferred from the surface into the interlayer active sites of the adsorbent. At sufficiently high agitation rates, the resistance due to external mass transfer is considered negligible13.

If interlayer diffusion were the rate-limiting step, a plot of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${q}_{t}$$\end{document} versus \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sqrt{t}$$\end{document} would yield a single straight line34. The first linear section corresponds to the rapid adsorption controlled by external diffusion, while the second linear section reflects slower diffusion of MB molecules into the interlayer spaces of the adsorbent particles. This behavior indicates that the adsorption process is controlled by a combination of film diffusion and intraparticle diffusion, with the relative contribution of each depending on the MB concentration and available adsorption sites33,34.

Proposed mechanism for MB adsorption on Clay

Methylene blue (MB) adsorption onto natural clay occurs through a multi-step process involving diffusion, surface binding, and multilayer formation. MB molecules first interact with clay active sites via electrostatic attraction between the positively charged MB and negatively charged clay sites and hydrogen bonding between functional groups (such as -OH and -COO⁻) on the clay and the dye, forming a monolayer. Additional MB molecules then accumulate through MB-MB interactions, resulting in multilayer adsorption. Steric effects influence site occupancy and adsorption capacity, while adsorption energies below 40 kJ/mol indicate physical adsorption. Thermodynamic analysis confirms the process is spontaneous, highlighting natural clay as an effective and low-cost adsorbent for dye removal. Conclusion

This study aims to investigate the adsorption of methylene blue (MB) onto clay. The effects of contact time, initial dye concentration, adsorbent dosage, and pH were evaluated. Based on R2 analysis, the adsorption process follows a pseudo-order kinetic model, with the Langmuir isotherm providing the best fit at different temperatures. A comprehensive investigation using a statistical physics–based model further elucidated the adsorption mechanism. The results showed that MB adsorption is best represented by a multilayer model with saturation, where molecules adopt a parallel orientation on the clay surface, indicating multiple interaction mechanisms. Steric analysis revealed the number of dye molecules per active site, the number of layers formed, and the density of receptor sites. Adsorption energies were all below 40 kJ/mol, consistent with endothermic physical adsorption, while electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding may also contribute. Thermodynamic simulations demonstrated that MB adsorption is spontaneous under all conditions. Overall, these findings suggest that natural clay is an effective, low-cost adsorbent for removing dyes from aqueous solutions.
