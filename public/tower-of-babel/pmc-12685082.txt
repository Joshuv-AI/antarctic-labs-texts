China’s post-zero-COVID Omicron wave: A Bayesian analysis
Authors: Jun Cai, Yanpeng Wu, Hengcong Liu, Zhu Deng, Lan Yi, Liuhe Lai, Anna Funk, Marco Ajelli, Hongjie Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceChina’s exit from its “dynamic zero-COVID” policy in late 2022 triggered a major Omicron wave. Our analysis of national surveillance data suggests that approximately 88% of the population was infected by mid-February 2023. Following the policy shift, transmission dynamics varied substantially over time, correlating strongly with changes in population behavior driven by the perceived risk of infection. These findings offer critical insights into epidemic spread following abrupt policy shifts and underscore the need to integrate dynamic behavioral responses into epidemiological models.

Beginning November 11, 2022, the Chinese government started relaxing its “dynamic zero-COVID” strategy, moving away from the strict containment policy maintained for over 2 y to a disease burden mitigation approach (1). At that time, the “20 Measures” policy was introduced, which eased rules on contact tracing, quarantine durations, and mass testing. This shift was accelerated on December 7, 2022, with the “10 New Measures,” which further reduced testing scope and allowed home quarantine for milder cases, signaling a comprehensive exit from a containment phase toward a mitigation phase (1). Shortly after these policy changes were implemented, China experienced a substantial nationwide wave of COVID-19, primarily caused by the Omicron BA.5 and BF.7 subvariants (2). On January 8, 2023, a few weeks following the peak of this wave, the official management of COVID-19 was reclassified. It was downgraded from Category A—infectious diseases requiring the most stringent controls like mandatory quarantine and isolation (e.g., plague, cholera)—to Category B, namely infectious diseases such as seasonal influenza that are managed through standard public health measures (1).

Quantifying SARS-CoV-2 transmission dynamics during this transition, from strict containment to mitigation, in China has been the subject of extensive research. Data from various sources, including daily reported case counts (3–5), online infection surveys (3–8), sentinel community-based infection surveillance (9, 10), inpatient and critical illness data from hospitals (7, 11), SARS-CoV-2 virological surveillance (12), serum samples (13, 14), wastewater surveillance (15), and obituary data (16, 17) have been used to analyze the impact of this shift in policy. However, partially due to the scattered nature and varying level of reliability of these data sources, key characteristics of this major Omicron wave remain elusive, including the time-varying transmission levels, cumulative infection attack rate (IAR), and role played by human behavior.

To address these knowledge gaps, this study uses a Bayesian framework to fit a transmission model using two key data sources: daily reported case counts from the final stage of the “zero-COVID” policy and weekly SARS-CoV-2 virological surveillance data [from the China National Influenza Surveillance Information System (CNISIS)] obtained after the policy relaxation. Our primary goal is to provide a detailed reconstruction of the nationwide Omicron transmission dynamics in China between November 2022 and February 2023, including characterizing its time-varying transmission intensity and human behavior response. This study contributes to deepening our understanding of the health consequences of the policy shift and informs data-driven strategies for responding to future epidemics or pandemics.

Results

Epidemic Dynamics.

The transmission model was fitted to daily reported case counts (prepolicy change) and weekly SARS-CoV-2-related ILI data (postpolicy change) (Fig. 1 A and B). The inferred temporal dynamics of the Omicron wave in China (November 2022–February 2023) showed a single sharp epidemic peak on December 22, 2022 (Fig. 1C). Prior to the major policy shifts, despite an increase in reported cases (October 28–November 11), the estimated infection prevalence remained extremely low (<0.003%). During the initial relaxation phase (“20 Measures,” November 12–December 7), prevalence began exponential growth. Following the full exit from “zero-COVID” (“10 Measures,” December 7), infection prevalence surged dramatically, peaking at an estimated 21.5% (95% CrI: 17.7 to 24.7%) on December 22, 2022. Prevalence subsequently declined rapidly, and by the time COVID-19 management was officially downgraded to Category B (January 8, 2023), the estimated prevalence had fallen to 3.1% (95% CrI: 2.5 to 3.8%), indicating the primary wave had largely concluded, consistent with China CDC reports (18). Finally, prevalence reached 0.11% (95% CrI: 0.08 to 0.17%) by February 12, 2023 (Fig. 1C).

Our model estimated an initial latent infection count of 5,759 (95% CrI: 4,671 to 7,132) individuals at the start of the simulation period (October 22, 2022) (SI Appendix, Table S2). By December 7, 2022, coinciding with the full relaxation of control measures, the estimated number of new daily infections nationwide reached 3.96 million (95% CrI: 2.95 to 5.26). The cumulative IAR subsequently surged from 1.1% (95% CrI: 0.8 to 1.4%) on December 7 to 85.2% (95% CrI: 73.7 to 90.5%) by January 8, 2023. This suggests that most of the population—84.1%, corresponding to 1.19 billion people—became infected within 1 mo after the major policy shift. The cumulative IAR continued to increase, albeit more gradually, reaching 87.8% (95% CrI: 75.9 to 93.3%) by February 12, 2023 (Fig. 2A).

Estimated Transmission Dynamics.

We estimated that the transmission rate, β(t), increased from 0.54 d−1 (95% CrI: 0.50 to 0.59) during the late “zero-COVID” period (October 22–November 11, 2022) to 0.69 d−1 (95% CrI: 0.62 to 0.76) immediately following the initial policy relaxation (November 12 to 27) (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Table S2). Starting from November 28, the estimated transmission rate steadily increased from 0.76 d−1 (95% CrI: 0.57 to 0.96), peaking at 1.40 d−1 (95% CrI: 1.13 to 1.69) on December 9. Subsequently, possibly reflecting increased public awareness of infection risk, the transmission rate declined to 0.36 d−1 (95% CrI: 0.09 to 0.66) by January 1. On January 7, 2023, corresponding to the beginning of the Spring Festival travel rush (Chunyun), the transmission rate resurged, reaching a second peak of 1.42 d−1 (95% CrI: 0.43 to 2.64) on February 11, 2023 (Fig. 2B). Detailed posterior distributions and convergence diagnostic metrics are provided in SI Appendix, Figs. S2 and S3 and Table S2.

The corresponding estimated effective reproduction number, Ret, was 2.27 (95% CrI: 2.08 to 2.46) and 2.88 (95% CrI: 2.61 to 3.18) during the first and second periods, respectively (Fig. 2C). Following the full policy exit, Ret rapidly increased from 3.20 (95% CrI: 2.38 to 4.02) on November 28 to a peak of 5.69 (95% CrI: 4.56 to 6.85) on December 8, 2022. Subsequently, Ret declined sharply, falling below the epidemic threshold of 1 by December 21, 2022. Although Ret showed a resurgence coinciding with the increased transmission during the Chunyun travel period, it remained largely around the epidemic threshold throughout January and February 2023 (Fig. 2C). This temporary increase in Ret during Chunyun did not ignite a secondary wave, primarily because the high level of population immunity incurred during the primary wave (estimated >80% infected by early January, Fig. 2A) prevented sustained transmission.

The main analysis assumed a SARS-CoV-2 detection and reporting proportion (ρC) of 90% prior to the policy change on November 11, 2022. To evaluate the effect of this assumption, we performed sensitivity analyses using ρC values of 100% and 50%. In both scenarios, the model provided a good fit to the empirical data (SI Appendix, Figs. S4 and S6), and the core inferred epidemic trajectory and transmission dynamics remained robust compared to the main analysis. Key epidemiological outcomes were largely unaffected by the choice of ρC: the epidemic peak consistently occurred on December 22, 2022, with nearly identical estimated peak prevalence values (21.5% [95% CrI: 17.7 to 24.6%] for ρC=100%; 21.5% [95% CrI: 17.9 to 24.7%] for ρC=50%). Similarly, the estimated cumulative IAR by February 12, 2023, showed minimal variation, calculated at 87.7% (95% CrI: 75.7 to 93.1%) for ρC=100% and 88.0% (95% CrI: 76.7 to 93.4%) for ρC=50% (SI Appendix, Figs. S5A and S7A).

While the overall model dynamics were robust, the assumed ρC value primarily impacted estimates related to the initial epidemic size and the transmission increase immediately following the first policy shift. Specifically, assuming a lower reporting rate (ρC=50%) led to a substantially higher estimate for the initial number of exposed individuals (L0=10,551 [95% CrI: 8,807 to 12,918]) and a markedly lower estimate for the increase in transmission rate between the first and second periods (Δβ21=0.05 [95% CrI: 0.00 to 0.13]) (SI Appendix, Table S3). Notably, the 95% CrI for Δβ21 included zero in the ρC=50% scenario, suggesting the increase may not be significant under that assumption. Consequently, estimates for the transmission rate during the second period (β2=0.59 [95% CrI: 0.54 to 0.65]) and the start of the third period (βILI,1=0.68 [95% CrI: 0.48 to 0.88]), along with the corresponding effective reproduction numbers (e.g., Ret=2.25 [95% CrI: 2.08 to 2.40] during the second period), were slightly lower when ρC=50%. Conversely, assuming perfect reporting (ρC=100%) yielded parameter estimates nearly identical to the main analysis (SI Appendix, Table S3).

Relations between Transmission Rate, Mobility, and Behavioral Response.

To explore factors influencing the variations observed in estimated transmission rates βILI,t between November 28, 2022, and February 12, 2023 (third period, Fig. 3A), we investigated their correlations with population mobility and behavioral response. Population mobility was proxied by the smoothed, population-weighted national average congestion index (Fig. 3D), while population behavioral responses were captured using model-derived coefficients for long-term and short-term behavioral responses (lower coefficients correspond to stronger influence of behavior on transmission) (Fig. 3 B and C).

We found a strong positive correlation between the estimated transmission rate, βILI,t, and long-term behavioral response coefficient (Pearson correlation coefficient ρ = 0.92, P < 0.001), suggesting that transmission tended to decrease as the cumulative proportion of the population affected increased (Fig. 3 A and B). Concurrently, the smoothed traffic congestion index showed a strong positive correlation with short-term behavioral response coefficient (ρ = 0.87, P < 0.001); specifically, congestion (our proxy for mobility) reached its lowest points when infection prevalence was highest (i.e., when short-term behavioral response coefficient was lowest, Fig. 3 C and D). However, we did not find these correlations during the Chunyun period (beginning January 7, 2023). This decoupling can likely be attributed to several converging factors: The official reclassification of COVID-19 to Category B management (January 8) and the widely held perception that the Omicron wave had already likely peaked, likely diminishing the influence of infection risk awareness on behavior. Additionally, mobility patterns during Chunyun were driven by holiday travel dynamics—initial urban activity, followed by mass departures reducing urban congestion before the Lunar New Year, and subsequent return travel—rather than primarily by current infection levels. Despite these complex mobility trends and potentially reduced risk perception, the estimated transmission rate (βILI,t) increased during Chunyun (Fig. 3A), possibly due to the increased frequency of social/family gatherings characteristic of the holiday season.

Our model provides estimates of long-term and short-term behavioral response coefficients, which represent how population behavioral influences on transmission changed over the wave. To validate whether these model-inferred dynamics reflect real-world behavioral shifts, we compared them against empirical data from Baidu, China’s largest search engine. We used keyword search indices as real-world proxies for these two distinct drivers of behavior. We used searches for “SARS-CoV-2” as a proxy for individuals’ perceived immediate risk of infection. This index correlated strongly with our model’s short-term behavioral response coefficient (ρ = −0.84, P < 0.001 for the search index; ρ = −0.71, P < 0.001 for the media index) (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S9B). We used searches for “epidemic prevention and control” as a proxy for public attention to official policies. This index aligned closely with our model’s long-term behavioral response coefficient (ρ = 0.89, P < 0.001 for the search index; ρ = 0.52, P < 0.001 for the media index) (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S9A).

Discussion

We reconstructed the transmission dynamics of the SARS-CoV-2 Omicron wave in China from November 2022 to February 2023 using a Bayesian inference framework informed by robust daily reported cases and weekly virological surveillance data. Our analysis showed a rapid growth of the cumulative IAR reaching ~84% (~1.19 billion people) within 1 mo after the policy shift. The transmission potential (i.e., effective reproduction number) was estimated to peak at 5.7 in early December. While the subsequent Spring Festival travel period coincided with an increase in transmission—a dynamic our approach was able to uniquely quantify through estimating time-varying rates—the extensive population immunity acquired in the primary wave prevented a secondary wave. Furthermore, our analysis identified complex correlations suggesting behavioral influences on transmission prior to Chunyun: Estimated transmission rates were strongly linked to a coefficient for long-term population behavioral response, whereas mobility patterns closely correlated with short-term population behavioral response coefficient. These apparent behavioral links seemed less influential during the Chunyun holiday period itself.

Our modeling approach adapts methodological elements from ref. 3, but it extends the study by simultaneously leveraging multiple, previously unavailable, data streams and by addressing a different research question. As compared to the previous literature (3–5), which relied on online polls of self-reported positivity rates for model calibration, our work is based on nationwide weekly ILI data combined with SARS-CoV-2 positivity rate data collected by the surveillance system. Moreover, our analysis quantifies the effect of the spontaneous behavioral response of the population, a question not addressed for this wave, and validates model findings against empirical search and media indices data from Baidu. In terms of methodology, we estimated time-varying transmission rates using a random-walk function, which allowed us to avoid imposing a functional form (5) or using mobility-based assumptions (4). In terms of obtained results, our estimated cumulative IAR of approximately 88% by the end of January to mid-February, 2023, is lower than the 97% (95% CrI: 95 to 99%) reported by Goldberg et al. (3) for late December 2022, but highly consistent with other independent studies that used a variety of methodologies (5–7, 13, 15). For example, Zhou et al. (5) estimated an 84.5% IAR by mid-January 2023 using reported cases and online survey data, while Fu et al. (6) reported 82.4% by February 7, 2023, based on self-reporting online survey data. Moreover, subnational estimates also corroborate our findings: Shanxi province reported an IAR of ~88% (7), and model-based estimates relying on early January hospital serum samples in Guangzhou yielded an IAR of 81% (95% CrI: 72.2 to 86.8%) within 1 mo following the policy shift (13). Other regional analyses, including wastewater surveillance and population serosurveys, paint a similar picture for local infection dynamics during the same period (14, 15, 20).

Our estimates of the effective reproduction number, Ret, align reasonably well with independent estimates, while differences highlight key methodological considerations. Bai et al. (11) reported a pooled initial Re of 4.74 (95% CI: 4.41 to 5.07) across Chinese provinces around the start of the wave, which is comparable to our estimated Ret of 5.64 (95% CrI: 4.50 to 6.83) on December 7, 2022 (the date the “zero-COVID” policy was fully lifted). Our Ret estimates are higher than those reported by Goldberg et al. (3) which analyzed the exponential growth of the epidemic (Rexp = 3.1); this difference likely stems from our use of a longer intrinsic generation time for Omicron (6.8 d), arguably more appropriate for early community transmission, compared to their assumption of a shorter generation time (3.3 d), which is typical of household settings (21). The markedly higher R0 estimate of 9.66 reported by Ma et al. (7) for Shanxi province likely reflects different modeling assumptions, particularly their use of a predefined exponential decay function for transmission rates, contrasting with the more flexible time-varying trend inferred in our study. Given the low population immunity in early December 2022, our estimated peak Ret of 5.69 (95% CrI: 4.56 to 6.85) around December 8 may approximate the intrinsic transmissibility of the Omicron variant in this population, suggesting an approximate 130% increase in transmission as compared to the ancestral SARS-CoV-2 lineages in China [~2.5 (22, 23)]. Furthermore, the subsequent dynamics of Ret, which rebounded during the Chunyun travel period but did not remain sustainably above the epidemic threshold of 1, are consistent with the observed absence of a secondary wave during that time (18).

A strength of our analysis was the estimation of transmission rates that varied over time following the policy adjustments. Rather than assuming a fixed rate (3), we allowed transmission intensity to change, revealing the insight that these inferred transmission variations were closely linked to changes in population behavior driven by perceived infection risk, particularly before the Chunyun period. Moreover, our analysis showed that the short-term and long-term behavioral response coefficients inferred by the model strongly correlated with Baidu search indices for relevant keywords. This statistically significant alignment between our model’s outputs and observed online behavior suggests our proxies are robust, providing further support that spontaneous changes in population behavior were a critical factor in shaping the trajectory of this unprecedented wave. Finally, we observed complex relationships between transmission, behavior, and population mobility (proxied by traffic congestion). While the population significantly reduced mobility in response to perceived risk of infection, this did not always translate to a proportional drop in transmission, especially approaching the holiday period. This finding highlights the critical importance of incorporating dynamic behavioral responses into transmission models, as the interplay between mobility and infection risk is not static. Accounting for these complex dynamics is essential for accurately capturing epidemic trajectories during periods of rapid change.

Our study has several limitations. First, due to data constraints and model structure, our estimates did not account for age-specific dynamics or geographical heterogeneity. External reports suggest largely synchronous infection waves across different regions and similar epidemic curves across age groups (7, 9, 12, 18), although some studies found small variations by age, such as lower cumulative IAR among older adults (6, 13). Second, our model focused solely on infection transmission and did not incorporate clinical outcomes such as hospitalizations or deaths. Modeling such outcomes accurately is complicated by data limitations and documented changes in official reporting definitions during the epidemic (24); this makes disease burden estimation and cross-regional comparisons challenging. Alternative approaches, such as analyzing excess mortality using obituary data, have been used elsewhere (16, 17). Third, our proxy for population mobility relied on an urban traffic congestion index. This simplification does not capture rural mobility patterns, which may limit our interpretation of transmission dynamics during periods with significant urban–rural travel, such as Chunyun. Thus, our behavioral proxies, while informative, can only be considered as indirect measures. Fourth, the study is subject to limitations inherent in many compartmental modeling approaches. The Susceptible-Latent-Infectious-Removed (SLIR) model assumes homogeneous mixing across the population as well as lasting immunity conferred by infection within the study period and does not explicitly differentiate between symptomatic and asymptomatic infections. Additionally, the model relies on fixed mean values for epidemiological parameters, such as the generation time, derived from the literature. However, in the context of the extremely rapid saturation observed in this specific wave (over 80% infected within 1 mo, peak Re(t) > 5.5), the impact of these particular model assumptions on the primary findings, regarding the overall magnitude and speed of the epidemic, is likely limited relative to the statistical uncertainty reflected in the credible intervals.

In conclusion, our Bayesian analysis of China’s Omicron wave following the “zero-COVID” policy exit showed exceptionally rapid transmission through the largely susceptible population, with an estimated 84.1% infected within 1 mo after the policy shift. We found that transmission dynamics following this shift were not constant but varied substantially over time, correlating strongly with changes in population behavior linked to perceived infection risk. These findings are crucial for accurately describing the epidemiology of this unprecedented wave, understanding the resulting landscape of population immunity in China, and informing preparedness for potential future epidemics. Furthermore, the Bayesian inference framework used in this study provides a flexible approach for synthesizing diverse surveillance data streams (including case counts, ILI, seroprevalence, and wastewater data) across different time periods and resolutions. This methodological approach holds significant potential for ongoing tracking and analysis of the transmission dynamics of respiratory pathogens in the postpandemic era.

Materials and Methods

Data.

Official daily reported cases before November 11, 2022.

The National Health Commission (NHC) of China released daily reports on the COVID-19 epidemic from January 10, 2020, to December 23, 2022 (25). We obtained the data from ref. 3, which made the cleaned daily case time-series data from the NHC publicly available. As described in ref. 3, official case counts were considered to be reliable before the relaxation of the “zero-COVID” policy on November 11, 2022, due to mandatory and frequent testing. For our analysis, we used the daily total number of cases within the 15-d period from October 28 to November 11, 2022, by adding the daily reported symptomatic and asymptomatic cases (Fig. 1A).

Weekly ILI and virological surveillance data after November 11, 2022.

From December 1, 2022, to February 12, 2023 (dates of sample collection), 402 national influenza surveillance network laboratories submitted their real-time reverse transcription PCR (rRT-PCR) testing results for SARS-CoV-2 and influenza to CNISIS. Details of the CNISIS SARS-CoV-2 surveillance are reported in ref. 12. Briefly, the data included the weekly number of ILI cases reported by sentinel hospitals as well as weekly SARS-CoV-2 positivity rates reported by the network laboratories for a period of 11 wk, from the week ending on December 4, 2022 (week 48 of 2022) through the week ending on February 12, 2023 (week 6 of 2023). We further calculated the weekly number of ILI cases associated with SARS-CoV-2 infection by multiplying the weekly number of ILI cases by the corresponding SARS-CoV-2 positivity rate (Fig. 1B).

Congestion index from Gaode Maps.

Amap (Gaode Maps) provides hourly and daily indices of urban traffic congestion for 100 Chinese cities, including Hong Kong (26). Based on users’ navigation and travel data, Amap calculates the city traffic congestion index as the ratio of the actual travel time to free-flow (noncongestion) travel time within urban areas. Thus, the congestion index is always greater than or equal to one, with larger values indicating increased traffic congestion delays. We retrieved the daily urban traffic congestion delay indices for 98 cities across mainland China from Amap, covering 53% of the national population. Along with the population data from these cities, we further estimated the population-weighted national average congestion index between November 28, 2022, and February 12, 2023 (Fig. 3D). This national average congestion index was smoothed using Friedman’s super smoother to reduce weekday-weekend fluctuations (19), and then used as a proxy for population mobility.

Keyword search and media indices.

Baidu, the largest search engine in China, hosts the Baidu Index, a data-sharing platform derived from large-scale user behavior data (27). The Baidu Index provides search and media indices for specific keywords across different regions and time periods. The search index quantifies the level and temporal dynamics of internet users’ attention to a keyword, whereas the media index reflects the extent and temporal dynamics of online media coverage and reporting on that keyword. As proxies for public risk perception and risk communication, we used the keywords “SARS-CoV-2” (“新冠”) and “epidemic prevention and control” (“疫情防控”), respectively. The data were extracted from Baidu Index for the period from October 22, 2022, to February 12, 2023, and consist of national average search and media indices for the two keywords (Fig. 3 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S9).

SARS-CoV-2 Transmission Model.

We developed a deterministic SLIR model to simulate Omicron transmission in China between October 2022 and February 2023. The model is described by the following system of ordinary differential equations:[1]dStdt=-βtItNStdLtdt=βtItNSt-γLLtdItdt=γLLt-γIItdRtdt=γIIt,

where N is the population size of China;St, Lt, It, and Rt are the number of susceptible, latent, infectious, and recovered individuals at time t, respectively. Susceptible individuals are infected through contacts with infectious individuals at a time-varying transmission rate βt. The transmission rate, βt, incorporates both the average number of contacts per day and the probability of transmission per contact into a single parameter. For example, a transmission rate of 1.5 d−1 could correspond to an individual having 15 contacts per day, each with a 10% probability of transmission. After infection, latent individuals are infected but still unable to transmit the pathogen for an average of 1/γL days (i.e., the latent period). Infectious individuals can transmit the pathogen for an average duration of 1/γI days (i.e., the infectious period) before moving to compartment R.

We separated the model into three time periods based on the changes in the deployed control measures and the availability of observation data. Specifically,[2]βt=β1,2022/10/22≤t≤2022/11/11β2,2022/11/12≤t≤2022/11/27βILI,t,2022/11/28≤t≤2023/02/12.

Following the method described in ref. 3, we assumed that β1 was constant during the first period (up to November 11, 2022) when the “zero-COVID” policy was replaced by the “20 Measures” policy. During the second period after relaxing interventions (November 12 to 27, 2022), we assumed a constant transmission rate larger than in the first period (i.e., β2>β1). Note that β2 can be rewritten as β1+Δβ21 with Δβ21>0. For the third period, the time-varying transmission rate, βILI,t, was estimated using the number of SARS-CoV-2-related ILI cases. Notably, the second period corresponds to a transitional period between the two different notification systems (i.e., from the number of reported COVID-19 cases to SARS-CoV-2-related ILI cases).

We used the well-known relationship between the transmission rate and reproduction number, R0t=βt/γI (28), to introduce the concept of time-varying reproduction number R0t. As such, we have[3]R0t=R1,2022/10/22≤t≤2022/11/11R2,2022/11/12≤t≤2022/11/27RILI,t,2022/11/28≤t≤2023/02/12.

Additionally, we estimated the effective reproduction number, Ret, at time t by multiplying R0t by the proportion of susceptible individuals within the Chinese population, defined as Ret=R0tStN. While the time-varying reproduction number considers only changes directly affecting pathogen transmissibility (e.g., human behavior, public health interventions), the effective reproduction number also accounts for changes in population immunity.

Parameter Values and Prior Distributions.

Several parameters of the transmission model were taken from the literature (SI Appendix, Table S1). We used a mean latent period of 2.65 d (30) and a mean intrinsic generation time of 6.84 d for Omicron (31), resulting in a mean infectious period of 4.19 d given that the generation time corresponds to the sum of the latent and infectious period in the classic SLIR model (32). To approximate the delay between infection and ascertainment at a sentinel hospital, we used the sum of the incubation period and the delay from symptom onset to specimen collection. Specifically, for Omicron BA.5 we employed a mean incubation period of 3.27 (SD ± 1.05) d (33), and we used a mean delay of 1.5 d from symptom onset to specimen collection. Justification for the latter assumption was that, according to the National Influenza Surveillance Technical Guidelines (34), specimens needed to be collected from ILI cases within 3 d of symptoms onset. Therefore, we assumed that the delay between the time of infection and ascertainment, fd, followed a gamma distribution with a mean of 4.77 d and a SD of 1.05 d. Given that testing was mandatory and frequent under China’s “zero-COVID” policy, we assumed that the case reporting rate, ρC, before November 11, 2022, was 90%. We also did sensitivity analyses where ρC was set to be either 100% or 50%.

As in other studies (3, 4, 13), we assumed minimal preexisting immunity to Omicron at the beginning of the 2022-2023 wave. Thus, we initialized the transmission model with a fully susceptible population.

We used a Bayesian framework to estimate the posterior distributions of the remaining parameters of the transmission model: i) the initial number of latent individuals L0 on October 22, 2022, 1 wk before the beginning of the study period; ii) the transmission rate between October 22 and November 11, 2022, β1; iii) the increased transmission rate between November 12 and 27, 2022, Δβ21; iv) the daily transmission rates between November 28, 2022, and February 12, 2023, βILI,t for t=1,…,77; v) the proportion of ascertained SARS-CoV-2-related ILI cases, ρILI; and vi) the overdispersion parameter of the negative binomial distribution, ϕ. Note that to estimate βILI,t, we first estimated RILI,t as a random walk function with a fixed SD of 0.5 and then used the relationship between βILI,t and RILI,t to estimate βILI,t. We used the same prior distributions for the parameters L0 and β1 as in ref. 3. Specifically, the prior on L0 was assumed to be uniform between 1 and 10 times the number of reported cases on the first day of the daily case count data; the prior on β1 was assumed to be a normal distribution with mean 3 and SD 2. The prior for Δβ21 was assumed to be an exponential distribution of mean 1; the prior for ρILI was assumed to be a beta distribution, Beta(1, 5); and the prior for ϕ was assumed to be a normal distribution with mean 50 and SD 10. These correspond to weak informative priors. SI Appendix, Table S2 provides a summary of all free model parameters together with their prior distributions.

Implementation and Diagnostics.

The model was fitted in a Bayesian framework using the No-U-Turn sampler via the RStan package (35). We sampled four parallel chains of 10,000 iterations each, of which the first 5,000 iterations were considered as a burn-in period. We assessed convergence using the Gelman–Rubin convergence diagnostic R^ and by visually inspecting the trace plots. The 95% and 50% credible intervals (CrIs) of the estimated parameters were based on 20,000 posterior samples.

Coefficients for Population Behavioral Response.

To represent potential behavioral changes, such as social distancing and other protective measures like staying home, avoiding crowded places, and frequent hand washing, driven by awareness of infection risk, we adapted two coefficients from ref. 36: “long-term behavioral response” and “short-term behavioral response.” The long-term behavioral response coefficient reflects behavioral shifts proportional to the cumulative affected population (infected + recovered) up until that time-point, while the short-term behavioral response coefficient reflects shifts proportional to the current infection prevalence. As in ref. 36, these coefficients for behavioral response are defined based on our model outputs as 1-It+RtN and 1-ItN for the long- and short-term behavioral response proxies, respectively. Notably, smaller coefficient values indicate a stronger influence of behavioral responses on transmission rates.
