Median of Forests for Robust Density Estimation
Hongwei Wen, University of Twente, Annika Betken, University of Twente, Tao Huang, LINK-TO

Abstract
Robust density estimation refers to the consistent estimation of the density function even when the data is contaminated by outliers. We find that existing forest density estimation at a certain point is inherently resistant to the outliers outside the cells containing the point, which we call non-local outliers , but not resistant to the rest local outliers . To achieve robustness against all outliers, we propose an ensemble learning algorithm called medians of forests for robust density estimation ( MFRDE ), which adopts a pointwise median operation on forest density estimators fitted on subsampled datasets. Compared to exsiting robust kernel-based methods, MFRDE enables us to choose larger subsampling sizes, sacrificing less accuracy for density estimation while achieving robustness. On the theoretical side, we introduce the local outlier exponent to quantify the number of local outliers. Under this exponent, we show that even if the number of outliers reaches a certain polynomial order in the sample size, MFRDE is able to achieve almost the same convergence rate as the same algorithm on uncontaminated data, whereas robust kernel-based methods fail. On the practical side, real data experiments show that MFRDE outperforms existing robust kernel-based methods. Moreover, we apply MFRDE to anomaly detection to showcase a further application. Keywords: density estimation; robust statistics; random forest; median of means



1
Introduction

Robust density estimation has drawn attention across various fields in statistics and machine learning due to its wide range of applications:

Lin (2010)
presents a robust mixture modeling framework using the multivariate skewed
t
-distributions for unsupervised clustering.

Hill (2015)
uses robust density estimation to model financial data that exhibit heavy tails and outliers.
More recently,
Yoo et al. (2022)
base the identification of adversarial examples in text and image classification datasets on robust density estimation.
Not least,
Humbert et al. (2022)
apply robust density estimation to anomaly detection problems.

A robust density estimator refers to a density estimator that remains consistent when the training data is contaminated by outliers.
In order to construct a robust density estimator,
Diggle (1975)
proposes some compound estimators for the spatial patterns, which exhibit improved robustness compared with the simple estimators. Moreover,
Jain and Orlitsky (2021)
reduces the robust density estimation problem to robust learning of the probability of all subsets of size at most
k
of a larger discrete domain, but their study mainly focuses on the approximately piecewise polynomial distributions.

Among other robust density estimators, it is remarkable that many studies focus on modifications of KDE
Hang et al. (2018)
, which is probably the most popular, commonly used and certainly mathematically the most studied density estimation method.
For instance, by combining KDE with M-estimation,
Kim and Scott (2012)
establishes a robust kernel density estimation (RKDE) method that is insensitive to contamination of the training sample.
Moreover, a different, non-parametric construction for a robust kernel density estimator, the scaled and projected KDE (SPKDE), can be found in
Vandermeulen and Scott (2014)
. Last but not least,
Humbert et al. (2022)
introduces a robust non-parametric density estimator combining KDE and the Median-of-Means principle (MoM-KDE). However, in high-dimensional datasets, samples are sparsely distributed, and the distances between them grow. This phenomenon, known as the “curse of dimensionality”, poses challenges for KDE-based methods. Additionally, selecting an appropriate kernel and bandwidth in high dimensions becomes more intricate. Choosing a bandwidth that is too small may induce sensitivity to noise, while opting for a bandwidth that is too large may yield excessively smoothed estimations, leading to a loss of detail.

In contrast to KDE-based methods, partition-based density estimation methods demonstrate superior adaptability to the curse of dimensionality. For example, density estimation trees
Ram and Gray (2011)
and density forests
Criminisi et al. (2012)
;
Wen and Hang (2022)
, partition the feature space into non-overlapping cells, and count the number of samples dropping in each cell to attain the density function estimation. Consequently, the forest density estimation at any specific point depends only on the number of samples in the cells containing the point. Therefore, the consistency of the forest density estimation at a certain point is not influenced by the outliers that are outside the cells containing the point, which we call
non-local outliers
. However, unfortunately, these methods are not commonly robust since they are sensitive to the outliers within the cells that contains the considered point, which we term
local outliers
.

Against this background, we propose
to combine random forests for density estimation with the Median-of-Means principle resulting in
a locally adaptive, robust density estimator called
medians of forests for robust density estimation
(
MFRDE
).
More precisely, the value of a corresponding density estimator in a point
x
corresponds to the (standardized) median of
S
random forest based density estimators evaluated in
x
.
Given a dataset of size
n
,
random forest based density estimators are each fitted on a subset of size
n/S
resulting from subampling from the dataset without replacement.
Accordingly, a small subsampling size
n/S
results in a large number of subsets
S
. Since the number of local outliers is independent of our choice of
S
, based on the pigeonhole principle, a sufficiently large number of subsets ensures that more than half of all
S
subsets do not contain any local outliers.
As a result, more than half of the
S
SFDEs are consistent estimators.
Among these consistent SFDEs, there must exist one higher and another lower than the median of
S
SFDEs. Since these two consistent SFDEs can be seen as the upper and lower bound of the median of SFDEs, by the sandwich theorem, the median of the SFDEs is also consistent. Therefore, our MFRDE, being the pointwise median of
S
SFDEs after proper standardization, emerges asa robust density estimator.

The contributions of this paper are summarized as follows.

(i)
We introduce the concept of local outliers and non-local outliers. We find that the existing forest density estimation methods can only resist to non-local outliers. To achieve robustness against all outliers, we develop a new forest-based algorithm called MFRDE, which is able to rule out the effects of outliers by properly choosing the subsampling size and applying a pointwise median operation to forest estimators.

(ii)
Theoretically, we introduce the outlier proportion exponent to quantify the number of local outliers. Under this exponent, we establish the consistency and convergence rates of MFRDE under proper parameters. Benefited from the forest method’s inherent resistence to non-local outliers, MFRDE can achieve almost identical convergence rates as the same algorithm applied to uncontaminated data, even if the number of outliers reaches a certain polynomial order in the sample size.

(iii)

By means of parameter analysis on synthetic datasets, we conduct real data experiments to verify the superiority of our MFRDE over robust kernel-based methods. Moreover, we evaluate the robustness of our algorithm by the anomaly detection task on real-world datasets.

2
Methodology

This section
establishes MFRDE (medians of forests for robust density estimation), a novel, robust density estimator resulting from
a combination of the median-of-means principle and random forest density estimation.
For this, we assume a given set
of observations
D:=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
composed of an inlier set
X_{\mathcal{I}}:=\{X_{i}:i\in\mathcal{I}\}
and an outlier set
X_{\mathcal{O}}:=\{X_{i}:i\in\mathcal{O}\}
with the two index subsets
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{O}
satisfying
\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{O}=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{O}=[n]
.
Following the framework established in
Lecué and Lerasle (2020)
, all inliers
X_{\mathcal{I}}
are assumed to be independent, identically distributed from an unknown probability distribution
P
, which admits a density function
f
with respect to the Lebesgue measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}
satisfying
\|f\|_{\infty}<\infty
.
At the same time, no assumption is made with respect to the outliers
X_{\mathcal{O}}
. In other words, the outlying samples can be dependent or adversarial, which can be considered a realistic setting in practice.
Against this background, both methods, the median-of-means principle and forest density estimation, contribute to robust density estimation by addressing outliers in different ways.
The following two sections, Sections
2.1
and

2.2
, provide detailed descriptions of both procedures while elaborating for each its contribution to establishing robustness to outliers when aiming at density estimation. A summary of the overall procedure is given by Algorithm
1
.

2.1
The Medians-of-Means Principle

For the purpose of density estimation, the median-of-means principle
chooses the pointwise median
of density estimators fitted on subsets of the dataset
D
as an
approximation to the true density value.
More precisely,
index subsets
\{\mathcal{B}_{s}\}_{s=1}^{S}
of equal size
m=n/S
are resampled without replacement from the index set
[n]
, such that
for any two indices
i\neq j
,
\mathcal{B}_{i}\cap\mathcal{B}_{j}=\emptyset
.
The corresponding subsets of observations are denoted by
D_{s}:=\{X_{i}:i\in\mathcal{B}_{s}\}
,
s=1,\ldots,S
.
An illustration of the
resampling procedure is provided by Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
Resampling index subsets
(\mathcal{B}_{s})_{s=1}^{S}
of size

from the index set
[n]
.
\sigma([n])
corresponds to a permutation of the index set
[n]
.

To each of the
S
subsamples a density estimator (here a subsampled forest density estimator (SFDE)) is fitted, resulting in the
S
estimators
f_{D_{s},E}
,
s=1,\ldots,S
.
Pointwise median computation of these
S
estimators finally results in
median of forests for robust density estimation
(
MFRDE
) by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(x):=\mathrm{Median}(f_{\mathrm{D}_{1},{\mathrm{E}}}(x),\ldots,f_{\mathrm{D}_{S},{\mathrm{E}}}(x)).

(1)

Due to application of the pointwise median operation, the function
\mathcal{M}(x)
does not necessarily integrate to 1. For density estimation,
\mathcal{M}(x)
therefore has to be standardized, finally resulting in the cestimator

\displaystyle f_{\mathcal{M}}(x):=\frac{\mathcal{M}(x)}{\int_{B_{r}}\mathcal{M}(z)\,dz}.

(2)

For consistent density estimation through application of the pointwise median operation to
S
estimated densities, more than half of these estimators are required to be consistent.
This can be realized by choosing a sufficiently large
S
ensuring that among
S
blocks, more than half of these do not contain any outliers.
According to the resampling procedure in Figure
1
,
each sample point appears exactly once in one of these blocks.
Therefore, if
S\geq 2|\mathcal{O}_{x}|+1
, then, according to the pigeonhole principle, there are at most
|\mathcal{O}_{x}|
blocks containing outliers, i.e. there are at least
S-|\mathcal{O}_{x}|\geq|\mathcal{O}_{x}|+1
blocks not containing any outlier. This means that more than half of all density estimators are not affected by outliers.
Among these, there exists at least one taking higher values and another one taking lower values than the median of all
S
density estimators.
Since the corresponding two values can be seen as upper and lower bound of
\mathcal{M}(x)
consistently estimating the true density function, by the sandwich lemma, the standardized median of the density estimators
f_{\mathcal{M}}
can be shown to be a consistent estimator of
f
, as well.

2.2
Random Forest Density Estimation

In general, random forest density estimation corresponds to an
ensemble method that
aggregates a number of density estimation trees through averaging their outputs.
In particular, the subsampled forest density estimator (SFDE)
fitted to the subset

D_{s}
is defined by

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{D}_{s},{\mathrm{E}}}(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{\mathrm{D}_{s},t}(x),

(3)

where each

f_{\mathrm{D}_{s},t}
,
t=1,\ldots,T
,
corresponds to a different density estimation tree fitted to the subset
D_{s}
.
A density estimation tree
is based on a
partition of the support of the data-generating random variables into smaller regions and
estimation of their density locally by the proportion of observations within the respective region.
Accordingly, random tree density estimation requires the specification of a rule for partitioning the sample space.
This article bases density estimation on the random tree partition rule proposed in
Breiman (2004)
and considered in
Biau (2012)
and
Wen and Hang (2022)
.
Subject to this rule,
we consider the rectangular cell
B_{r}=[-r,r]^{d}
with
r
large enough such that

\mathcal{X}\subseteq B_{r}
.

B_{r}
is then partitioned recursively as follows:

1.

For each rectangular cell, one of its
d
sides is selected randomly, i.e. each side is selected with probability
1/d
.

2.

The cell is split at the midpoint of the chosen side.

Figure 2:
Random tree partitions.

The above splitting procedure is repeated
p
times, where
p\in\mathbb{N}
is fixed beforehand and possibly depends on the sample size
n
.
After
p
recursive steps, we obtain a partition
\pi_{p}:=\{A_{p,j},\,1\leq j\leq 2^{p}\}
of
B_{r}
, i.e.
A_{p,i}\cap A_{p,j}=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
and
\bigcup_{j=1}^{2^{p}}A_{p,j}=B_{r}
.
We call
\pi_{p}
a
random tree partition
with depth
p
. Let
\mathrm{P}_{Z}
denote the probability distribution of the random coordinate selection in the partition
\pi_{p}
, i.e.
\mathrm{P}_{Z}
denotes the uniform distribution on
\{1,\ldots,d\}
.
Denote the cell
A_{p,j}\in\pi_{p}
containing the point
x
as
A_{p}(x)
. For fixed
T\in\mathbb{N}
, we independently generate
T
random tree partitions
(\pi_{p}^{t})_{t=1}^{T}
from the distribution
\mathrm{P}_{Z}
, where
\pi_{p}^{t}:=(A_{p,j}^{t})_{j=1}^{2^{p}}
and
A_{p}^{t}(x)
is the cell containing the point
x
in the
t
-th tree.
The subsampled density estimation tree (STDE) corresponding to the partition
\pi_{p}^{t}
is then defined by

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{D}_{s},t}(x):=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{s}}\boldsymbol{1}\{X_{i}\in A_{p}^{t}(x)\}}{m\mu(A_{p}^{t}(x))}.

(4)

By construction a density estimation tree in a specific point
x
is only affected by outliers within the local environment of
x
, i.e. by outliers in
A_{p}^{t}(x)
.
It is thereby robust to all other, non-local outliers.
As average of a number of density estimation trees, this robustness translates to SFDEs.
For further elaboration on the robustness of SFDEs, consider the following decomposition of the SFDE fitted to the subsample
D_{s}
into
inlier
and
outlier terms
:

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{D}_{s},{\mathrm{E}}}(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{\mathrm{D}_{s},t}(x)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{s}}\boldsymbol{1}\{X_{i}\in A_{p}^{t}(x)\}}{m\mu(A_{p}^{t}(x))}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{p}}{Tm(2r)^{d}}\biggl(\underbrace{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{s}\cap\mathcal{I}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\boldsymbol{1}\{X_{i}\in A_{p}^{t}(x)\}}_{\displaystyle\text{inlier term}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\underbrace{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{s}\cap\mathcal{O}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\boldsymbol{1}\{X_{i}\in A_{p}^{t}(x)\}}_{\displaystyle\text{outlier term}}\biggr),

(5)

where we used
\mathcal{B}_{s}=(\mathcal{B}_{s}\cap\mathcal{I})\cup(\mathcal{B}_{s}\cap\mathcal{O})
, since
\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{O}=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{O}=[n]
.
According to the above decomposition an SFDE may only be affected by outliers in the local environment
\cup_{t=1}^{T}A_{p}^{t}(x)
.
The following definition establishes these as so-called
local outliers
.

Definition 2.1

(
Local Outlier
)
.

For
p\in\mathbb{N}
and a fixed
x\in\mathcal{X}
, let

A_{p}^{t}(x)\in\pi_{p}^{t}
be the cell in the
t
-th tree partition containing
x
.
An outlier
X_{i}\in X_{\mathcal{O}}
is called a
local outlier
of
x
if
X_{i}\in\bigcup_{t\in[T]}A_{p}^{t}(x)
. The collection of the indices of all local outliers of
x
is denoted as

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{x}:=\bigg\{i\in\mathcal{O}:\sum_{t=1}^{T}\boldsymbol{1}\{X_{i}\in A_{p}^{t}(x)\}>0\bigg\}.

(6)

3
Theoretical Results

In this section, we present the main theoretical results for our robust density estimator MFRDE.
To quantify the number of local outliers, we introduce the
outlier proportion exponent
.
Then, by conducting a new learning theory analysis, we establish consistency and convergence rates of MFRDE with respect to the
L_{\infty}
-norm.

As discussed in Section
2
,
when estimating the density
f
at a given point
x
, the partition-based approach MFRDE
f_{\mathcal{M}}(x)
is not influenced by all outliers, but only by local outliers as specified by Definition
2.1
.
In order to characterize the number of local outliers, we introduce the
outlier proportion exponent
as follows.

Assumption 3.1

(
Outlier Proportion Exponent
)
.

Let
\mathcal{O}
be the outlier index set.
Assume that there exists a real number
\beta\in[0,1]
and some constant
c_{U}\geq 1
such that for any set
A\subset B_{r}
with
\mu(A)>0
, there holds

\displaystyle\frac{|X_{\mathcal{O}}\cap A|}{|X_{\mathcal{O}}|}\leq c_{U}\biggl(\biggl(\frac{\mu(A)}{\mu(B_{r})}\biggr)^{\beta}\vee\frac{\log n}{|\mathcal{O}|}\biggr).

(7)

with probability at least
1-1/n
. The number
\beta
is called the
outlier proportion exponent
of the outlier set
X_{\mathcal{O}}
.

Note that for any outlier set
X_{\mathcal{O}}
and any set
A
with
\mu(A)>0
, there holds

\frac{|X_{\mathcal{O}}\cap A|}{|X_{\mathcal{O}}|}\leq 1\leq c_{U}\leq c_{U}\left(\left(\frac{\mu(A)}{\mu(B_{r})}\right)^{0}\vee\frac{\log n}{|\mathcal{O}|}\right),

and thus (
7
) is always satisfied for
\beta=0
. Assumption
3.1
considers the number of outliers in local regions, where a smaller exponent
\beta
indicates a higher concentration of outliers in some areas.
It is worth mentioning that in contrast to
Vandermeulen and Scott (2014)
which assumes a uniform distribution for outliers, Assumption
3.1
does not specify the outlier distribution. In other words, each
\beta
corresponds to various distributions and thus our exponent assumption is a more generalized assumption. Moreover, the outliers in Assumption
3.1
are not necessarily independent and identically distributed. Therefore, our assumptions can cover more realistic scenarios than distributional assumptions.

With the help of the outlier proportion exponent, we are able to characterize the number of local outliers as follows:
For a certain point
x
, with
A:=\bigcup_{t\in[T]}A_{p}^{t}(x)
in (
7
), we have
|X_{\mathcal{O}}\cap A|=|X_{\mathcal{O}_{x}}|=|\mathcal{O}_{x}|
and

\displaystyle|\mathcal{O}_{x}|\leq c_{U}\biggl(\biggl(\frac{\mu\bigl(\bigcup_{t\in[T]}A_{p}^{t}(x)\bigr)}{\mu(B_{r})}\biggr)^{\beta}|\mathcal{O}|\vee\log n\biggr).

(8)

To illustrate the different values of
\beta
, we provide the following three examples.

Example 3.2

(Bounded Continuous Distribution)
.

If the outliers are i.i.d. from a continuous distribution
Q
that has a bounded density
q
with support on
B_{r}
, e.g. the uniform distribution on
B_{r}
, then the outlier proportion exponent
\beta=1
.

To see this, let
\xi_{i}:=\boldsymbol{1}\{X_{i}\in A\}
for
i\in\mathcal{O}
. Then we have
\|\xi_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
,
\mathbb{E}_{Q}\xi_{i}=0
and

\text{Var}\xi_{i}\leq\mathbb{E}_{Q}\xi_{i}^{2}=Q(A)(1-Q(A))\leq Q(A)
. Applying Bernstein’s inequality in Theorem 6.12 of
Steinwart and Christmann (2008)
, we obtain that for any
\tau^{\prime}>0
, there holds

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{O}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{O}}\boldsymbol{1}\{X_{i}\in A\}-Q(A)\leq\sqrt{2Q(A)\tau^{\prime}/|\mathcal{O}|}+2\tau^{\prime}/(3|\mathcal{O}|)\leq Q(A)+2\tau^{\prime}/|\mathcal{O}|

(9)

with probability at least
1-e^{-\tau^{\prime}}
, where the last inequality follows from the inequality
\sqrt{2ab}\leq a+b
for
a,b\geq 0
.
By taking
\tau^{\prime}:=\log n
in (
9
) and using
Q(A)=\int_{A}q(x)d\mu(x)\leq\|q\|_{\infty}\mu(A)=(\|q\|_{\infty}\mu(B_{r}))\frac{\mu(A)}{\mu(B_{r})}
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{O}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{O}}\boldsymbol{1}\{X_{i}\in A\}\leq 2Q(A)+2\log n/|\mathcal{O}|\leq 2\|q\|_{\infty}\mu(B_{r})\frac{\mu(A)}{\mu(B_{r})}+2\log n/|\mathcal{O}|

(10)

with probability at least
1-1/n
, where
c_{U}:=2\|q\|_{\infty}\mu(B_{r})
.
By choosing
c_{U}=2\|q\|_{\infty}\mu(B_{r})
, inequality (
7
) holds with
\beta=1
.

Example 3.3

(Unbounded Continuous Distribution)
.

Let
\theta\in(0,1)
. If the outliers are i.i.d. from a continuous distribution
Q
that has the density function
q(x)=(1-\theta)(x+1/2)^{-\theta}
on
(-1/2,1/2)
and
q(x)=0
else, we have
\beta=1-\theta\in(0,1)
.

To see this, note that for any
A\subset[-1/2,1/2]=:B_{r}
with

\displaystyle Q(A)=

\displaystyle\int_{A}q(x)d\mu(x)\leq\int_{-1/2}^{-1/2+\mu(A)}q(x)d\mu(x)=(x+1/2)^{1-\theta}|_{-1/2}^{-1/2+\mu(A)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu(A)^{1-\theta}=\bigg(\frac{\mu(A)}{\mu(B_{r})}\bigg)^{1-\theta}

with probability at least
1-e^{-\tau^{\prime}}
since
q
is monotonically decreasing.
By choosing
\tau^{\prime}:=\log n
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{O}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{O}}\boldsymbol{1}\{X_{i}\in A\}\leq 2Q(A)+2\log n/|\mathcal{O}|\leq 2\bigg(\frac{\mu(A)}{\mu(B_{r})}\bigg)^{1-\theta}+2\log n/|\mathcal{O}|

(11)

with probability at least
1-1/n
.
By choosing
c_{U}=2
, inequality (
7
) holds with
\beta=1-\theta
.

Example 3.4

(Discrete Distribution)
.

If outliers are generated by a Markov chain with a transition matrix on discrete states, e.g. the states space is

\begin{pmatrix}1/2&1/2\\

then the outlier proportion exponent
\beta=0
.

To see this, note that for any
A\subset[-1,1]=:B_{r}
with

, there holds

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{O}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{O}}\boldsymbol{1}\{X_{i}\in A\}\leq 1=\bigg(\frac{\mu(A)}{\mu(B_{r})}\bigg)^{0}\leq\bigg(\frac{\mu(A)}{\mu(B_{r})}\bigg)^{0}\vee\frac{\log n}{|\mathcal{O}|}.

(12)

By choosing
c_{U}=1
, inequality (
7
) holds with
\beta=0
.

Before presenting the theoretical results for MFRDE, we establish an assumption on
the smoothness of the density function, which is frequently employed in studies for density estimation, see e.g.,
Tsybakov (2008)
;
Jiang (2017)
;
Wang et al. (2019)
.

Assumption 3.5

(
Hölder Continuity
)
.

Assume that the density
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous
,
\alpha\in(0,1]
, i.e., there exists a constant
c_{L}\in(0,\infty)
such that

|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})|\leq c_{L}\|x-x^{\prime}\|^{\alpha}
holds for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The set of all
\alpha
-Hölder continuous functions is denoted by
C^{\alpha}
.

Under Assumptions
3.1
and
3.5
, we establish consistency of MFRDE and we derive convergence rates for MFRDE reported in Theorems
3.6
and
3.7
, respectively.

Theorem 3.6

(
Consistency
)
.

Let Assumptions
3.1
and
3.5
hold, and let
f_{\mathcal{M}}
be the MFRDE estimator defined in (
2
) with subsampling size
m\leq n
.
Furthermore, let the outlier set
X_{\mathcal{O}}
satisfy

|\mathcal{O}|/n\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
Moreover, we denote

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}:=\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{d+2\alpha^{\prime}}\,\text{ and }\,\gamma_{2}:=\frac{d+2\alpha^{\prime}}{(1-\beta)d+2(1+\beta)\alpha^{\prime}},

(13)

where
\alpha^{\prime}:=(1-2^{-\alpha})/\log 2
and

d
is the data dimension.
Then, by choosing

\displaystyle m\lesssim(n/|\mathcal{O}|)^{\gamma_{2}},\,p=((1-2\gamma_{1})/\log 2)\log m,\,T\asymp m^{2\gamma_{1}},

(14)

we have

\displaystyle\|f_{\mathcal{M}}-f\|_{\infty}\to 0\qquad\text{ as }\quad n,m\to\infty,

with probability at least
1-2/n
, i.e.,
f_{\mathcal{M}}
is a consistent estimator of
f
.

Theorem
3.6
shows that the condition of MFRDE being a robust density estimator contains two aspects: firstly, the outlier proportion
|\mathcal{O}|/n
needs to converge to zero as
n\to\infty
. Secondly, the subsampling size
m
should go to infinity as
n\to\infty
and be smaller than the threshold
(n/|\mathcal{O}|)^{\gamma_{2}}
in (
14
). For
d>1
, from (
13
) we can see that
\gamma_{2}
increases with the outlier proportion exponent
\beta
and thus the threshold
(n/|\mathcal{O}|)^{\gamma_{2}}
becomes larger as
\beta
increases. This implies that the more uniformly distributed the outliers, the more relaxed the conditions on
m
needed for the consistency of MFRDE.

Theorem 3.7

(
Convergence Rates
)
.

Let Assumptions
3.1
and
3.5
hold, and let
f_{\mathcal{M}}
be the MFRDE estimator as in (
2
).
Furthermore, let the outlier set
X_{\mathcal{O}}
satisfy
|\mathcal{O}|\leq n/2
.
Moreover, let
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
be as in (
13
).
Then, by taking

\displaystyle m\asymp n\wedge(n/|\mathcal{O}|)^{\gamma_{2}},

(15)

and letting
p
,
T
satisfy (
14
),
we obtain

\displaystyle\|f_{\mathcal{M}}-f\|_{\infty}\lesssim(\log n)^{3/2}\bigl((1/n)^{\gamma_{1}}+(|\mathcal{O}|/n)^{\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}\bigr)

(16)

with probability at least
1-2/n
.

From (
16
) it follows that the error bound becomes smaller as
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
increase. Since
\gamma_{2}
increases with
\beta
for
d>1
, a more uniform outlier distribution results in a smaller density estimation error for MFRDE. In addition, the upper bound in (
16
) depends on the outlier proportion
|\mathcal{O}|/n
. If the data is not contaminated, i.e. if
|\mathcal{O}|=0
, MFRDE achieves the convergence rate
(\log n)^{3/2}n^{-\gamma_{1}}
.
On the other hand, suppose that the number of outliers satisfies
|\mathcal{O}|\lesssim n^{1-1/\gamma_{2}}
,
d>1
and
\beta>0
. In this case,
(|\mathcal{O}|/n)^{\gamma_{2}}\lesssim n^{-1}
and thus the resulting convergence rate of MFRDE in (
16
) turns out to be
(\log n)^{3/2}n^{-\gamma_{1}}
, i.e. MFRDE fitted on contaminated data is able to achieve the same convergence rate as MFRDE fitted on uncontaminated data.

Moreover, when there are no outliers, i.e.
|\mathcal{O}|=0
, choosing
m=n
, MFRDE reduces to the random forest density estimators in
Wen and Hang (2022)
.
In this case, Theorem
3.7
shows that up to a logarithmic factor, forest density estimators converge at the rate
n^{-\gamma_{1}}
with respect to the
L_{\infty}
-norm and in the sense of “with high probability”, which is both faster and stronger than the result that holds with respect to the
L_{2}
-norm “in expectation” as in Theorem 1 in
Wen and Hang (2022)
.

4
Error Analysis

To establish the consistency and convergence rates of MFRDE presented as Theorems
3.6
and
3.7
in Section
3
, we derive the density estimation error bounds for MFRDE in the presence of outliers, as presented in Proposition
4.9
. To prove this, in Section
4.2
, we demonstrate that the error of MFRDE is determined by the errors of the SFDEs fitted on subsets not contaminated by outliers. The error bounds for SFDE are established in Section
4.1
through a novel error decomposition, which introduces new notations and bounds the sample error, sampling error, and approximation error in Sections
4.1.1
,
4.1.2
and
4.1.3
, respectively.

4.1
Error Analysis for SFDE without outliers

In this section, we analyze the distance between SFDE in (
5
) and the true density
f
with respect to
L_{\infty}
-norm.
For this, recall that the cell containing
x
in
\pi_{t}
is denoted as
A_{p}^{t}(x)
.
The population version of the STDE estimator
f_{\mathrm{D}_{s},t}
in (
4
) is given by

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{P},t}(x)=\frac{\mathrm{P}(X_{1}\in A_{p}^{t}(x))}{\mu(A_{p}^{t}(x))}.

(17)

The corresponding population version of SFDE in (
5
) is given by

\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{P},{\mathrm{E}}}(x):=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{\mathrm{P},t}(x),

(18)

For our analysis, we consider the following error
decomposition for
\|f_{\mathrm{D}_{s},\mathrm{E}}-f\|_{\infty}
:

\displaystyle\|f_{\mathrm{D}_{s},\mathrm{E}}-f\|_{\infty}\leq\|f_{\mathrm{D}_{s},{\mathrm{E}}}-f_{\mathrm{P},{\mathrm{E}}}\|_{\infty}+\|f_{\mathrm{P},{\mathrm{E}}}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{P}_{Z}}f_{\mathrm{P},{\mathrm{E}}}\|_{\infty}+\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{P}_{Z}}f_{\mathrm{P},{\mathrm{E}}}-f\|_{\infty},

(19)

where
\mathrm{P}_{Z}
denotes the probability distribution of the random partition
\pi_{p}
.
The error terms on the right-hand side of (
19
) are called
sample error
,
sampling error
,
approximation error
, respectively.

4.1.1
Bounding the Sample Error Term

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f_{\mathrm{D}_{s},{\mathrm{E}}}
and
f_{\mathrm{P},{\mathrm{E}}}
be defined as in (
5
) and (
18
), respectively. Then for any
\tau>0
, there holds

\displaystyle\|f_{\mathrm{D}_{s},{\mathrm{E}}}-f_{\mathrm{P},{\mathrm{E}}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2(2r)^{-d}2^{p}\|f\|_{\infty}(\tau+(4d+3)\log m)/m}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+2(2r)^{-d}2^{p}(\tau+(4d+9)\log m)/(3m)

with probability at least
1-e^{-\tau}
.

4.1.2
Bounding the Sampling Error Term

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
f_{\mathrm{P},{\mathrm{E}}}
be defined as in (
18
). Then for any
\tau>0
, there holds

\displaystyle\|f_{\mathrm{P},{\mathrm{E}}}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{P}_{Z}}f_{\mathrm{P},{\mathrm{E}}}\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{2c_{1}^{2}(\tau+\log 2^{pd})/T}+2(\tau+\log 2^{pd})/(3T)

with probability at least
1-e^{-\tau}
, where
c_{1}
is a constant only depending on
d
and
\|f\|_{\infty}
.

4.1.3
Bounding the Approximation Error Term

Lemma 4.3
.

Let the density function
f
satisfy Assumption
3.5
and
f_{\mathrm{P},\mathrm{E}}
be defined as in (
18
). Then we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{P}_{Z}}f_{\mathrm{P},\mathrm{E}}-f\|_{\infty}\leq c_{L}(2r)^{\alpha}d\exp\bigl((2^{-\alpha}-1)p/d\bigr).

4.1.4
Convergence Rates of SFDE

The following proposition presents the convergence rates of SFDE in terms of the
L_{\infty}
-norm under the assumption that no outliers are contained in the dataset.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
f_{\mathrm{D}_{s},\mathrm{E}}
be the SFDE defined in (
5
). Suppose
f\in C^{\alpha}
with support
\mathcal{X}\subset B_{r}
.
Let
\gamma_{1}
be as in (
13
).
Then, for any subset
D_{s}
satisfying
D_{s}\cap X_{\mathcal{O}}=\emptyset
, by choosing

\displaystyle p\asymp((1-2\gamma_{1})/\log 2)\cdot m,\,T\gtrsim m^{2\gamma_{1}},

there holds

\displaystyle\|f_{\mathrm{D}_{s},\mathrm{E}}-f\|_{\infty}\lesssim\sqrt{\log n}\cdot m^{-\gamma_{1}}

with probability at least
1-1/m
.

Proof of Proposition
4.4
.

Combining (
19
), Lemma
4.1
,
4.2
and
4.3
, we obtain that with probability
\mathrm{P}^{n}
at least
1-2e^{-\tau}
, there holds

\displaystyle\|f_{\mathrm{D}_{s},\mathrm{E}}-f\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2(2r)^{-d}2^{p}\|f\|_{\infty}(\tau+(4d+3)\log m)/m}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+2(2r)^{-d}2^{p}(\tau+(4d+9)\log m)/(3m)+\sqrt{2c_{1}^{2}(\tau+\log 2^{pd})/T}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+2(\tau+\log 2^{pd})/(3T)+c_{L}(2r)^{\alpha}d\exp\bigl((2^{-\alpha}-1)p/d\bigr).

By taking
\tau=\log(2n)
,
p\asymp(1-2\gamma_{1})\log m/\log 2
and
T\gtrsim m^{2\gamma_{1}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|f_{\mathrm{D},\mathrm{E}}-f\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\bigl(\sqrt{2^{p}\log n/m}+\sqrt{\log n+\log(2^{pd})/T}+\exp\bigl((2^{-\alpha}-1)p/d\bigr)\bigr),

\displaystyle\leq(3C_{1}+d)\sqrt{\log n}\cdot m^{-\gamma_{1}}\lesssim\sqrt{\log n}\cdot m^{-\gamma_{1}}

with probability
\mathrm{P}^{n}
at least
1-n^{-1}
, where

\displaystyle C_{1}:=\sqrt{8(d+1)(2r)^{-d}\|f\|_{\infty}}+8(d+3)(2r)^{-d}/3+2(c_{1}+1)+c_{L}(2r)^{\alpha}d.

Thus, we finish the proof.
∎

It is worth mentioning that
by conducting a new learning theory analysis,
Proposition
4.4
establishes
the convergence rates of SFDE
with respect to the
L_{\infty}
-norm and in the sense of
“with high probability”.
In particular, when there are no outliers in the whole dataset
D
, we take
D_{s}=D
such that SFDE reduces to RFDE. By Proposition
4.4
, RFDE achieves the convergence rate
(\log n/n)^{\gamma_{1}}
, which is both faster and stronger than the result that holds with respect to the
L_{2}
-norm “in expectation” as established in Theorem 1 in
Wen and Hang (2022)
. This enables further analysis of MFRDE under the outlier setting.

4.2
Error Analysis for MFRDE with outliers

In this section, we conduct the error analysis for MFRDE in the presence of outliers in terms of the
L_{\infty}
-norm if the true density
f\in C^{\alpha}
.
To this end, we firstly give the following notations. Given the index set of outliers
\mathcal{O}
in the data and the subsampled data sets
D_{s}
,
s\in[S]
,

\displaystyle D^{\prime}_{s}:=D_{s}\setminus X_{\mathcal{O}}

(20)

denotes the subsampled data set that only contains inliers of
D_{s}
.
Moreover, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{s}:=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:X_{\mathcal{O}_{x}}\cap D_{s}=\emptyset\}

(21)

denote
the set of
x
for which

D_{s}
does not contain any local outlier of
x
.

The following lemma shows that with appropriate parameter choice, we are able to reduce the error analysis of MFRDE into the error analysis of SFDE.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
3.6
hold. Let
f_{\mathcal{M}}
and
\mathcal{I}_{s}
be defined as in (
2
) and (
21
), respectively. Then for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
, by choosing the same values for parameters
m
,
p
,
T
and
S
as in Theorem
3.6
, we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\big(\|f_{\mathcal{M}}-f\|_{\infty}\leq 2(1+\|f\|_{\infty})\varepsilon\big)

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{P}\Bigl(\max_{s\in[S]}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{I}_{s}}|f_{\mathrm{D}_{s},\mathrm{E}}(x)-f(x)|\leq\varepsilon\Bigr).

Lemma
4.6
shows that the error bound of SFDE depends on the proportion of inliers in
D_{s}
and the density estimation error of
f_{\mathrm{D}^{\prime}_{s},\mathrm{E}}
fitted on the subsampled clean data
D^{\prime}_{s}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
f_{\mathrm{D}_{s},\mathrm{E}}
and
D^{\prime}_{s}
be defined as in (
5
) and (
20
), respectively. Then we have

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{I}_{s}}|f_{\mathrm{D}_{s},\mathrm{E}}(x)-f(x)|\leq\bigl||D^{\prime}_{s}|/m-1\bigr|\cdot\|f_{\mathrm{D}^{\prime}_{s},\mathrm{E}}\|_{\infty}+\sup_{x\in\mathcal{I}_{s}}|f_{\mathrm{D}^{\prime}_{s},\mathrm{E}}(x)-f(x)|.

(22)

In order to analyze the term
\big||D^{\prime}_{s}|/m-1\big|
on the right-hand side of (
22
), Lemma
4.7
provides an estimate of the number of inliers in the subset
D_{s}
, i.e.
|D^{\prime}_{s}|
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
D^{\prime}_{s}
be defined as in (
20
) and let
\mathrm{P}_{U}
denote the distribution of the resampling procedure. Then there holds that

\displaystyle m(1-|\mathcal{O}|/n)-\sqrt{m\log(4nS)}\leq\bigwedge_{s\in[S]}|D^{\prime}_{s}|\leq m

with probability at least
1-1/(2n)
.

Next, we establish the pointwise convergence rates of
f_{\mathrm{D}^{\prime}_{s},\mathrm{E}}
w.r.t. the
L_{\infty}
-norm for any
x\in\mathcal{I}_{s}
by the similar proof as in Proposition
4.4
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
3.6
hold. Let
D^{\prime}_{s}
be defined as in (
20
) and
f_{\mathrm{D}^{\prime}_{s},\mathrm{E}}
be the random forest estimator fitted on
D^{\prime}_{s}
. In addition, let
\gamma_{1}
be as in (
13
). Then by choosing the same values for parameters
m
,
p
,
T
and
S
as in Theorem
4.9
, we get

\displaystyle\|f_{\mathrm{D}^{\prime}_{s},\mathrm{E}}\|_{\infty}\lesssim m^{-\gamma_{1}}\log n+\|f\|_{\infty},\ \sup_{x\in\mathcal{I}_{s}}|f_{\mathrm{D}^{\prime}_{s},\mathrm{E}}(x)-f(x)|\lesssim m^{-\gamma_{1}}\log n.

with probability at least
1-1/n
.

With these preparations in Lemmas
4.5
-
4.8
, we are able to derive the error bound of MFRDE under the outlier setting.
The following proposition establishes the error bound for MFRDE with respect to
L_{\infty}
-norm, which is essential for establishing the consistency and convergence rates for MFRDE.

Proposition 4.9

(
Density Estimation Error Bound
)
.

Let Assumptions
3.1
and
3.5
hold, and

f_{\mathcal{M}}
be the MFRDE estimator as in (
2
).
Furthermore, let the outlier set
X_{\mathcal{O}}
satisfy
|\mathcal{O}|\leq n/2
. Moreover, let
m
,
p
,
T
satisfy (
14
).
with probability at least
1-1/n
,
there holds

\displaystyle\|f_{\mathcal{M}}-f\|_{\infty}\lesssim(\log n)^{3/2}m^{-\gamma_{1}},

(23)

where
m
denotes the size of the considered subsamples.

Proof of Proposition
4.9
.

By using Lemma
4.7
, we obtain

\displaystyle\max_{s\in[S]}\bigl||D^{\prime}_{s}|/m-1\bigr|\leq|\mathcal{O}|/n+\sqrt{\log(4nS)/m}

(24)

with probability
\mathrm{P}_{U}
at least
1-1/(2n)
.
This, together with Lemma
4.6
and Lemma
4.8
yields that for some constant
c_{2}

\displaystyle\max_{s\in[S]}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{I}_{s}}|f_{\mathrm{D}_{s},\mathrm{E}}(x)-f(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{s\in[S]}\bigl||D^{\prime}_{s}|/m-1\bigr|\cdot\|f_{\mathrm{D}^{\prime}_{s},\mathrm{E}}\|_{\infty}+\max_{s\in[S]}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{I}_{s}}|f_{\mathrm{D}^{\prime}_{s},\mathrm{E}}(x)-f(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{s\in[S]}\bigl||D^{\prime}_{s}|/m-1\bigr|\cdot\max_{s\in[S]}\|f_{\mathrm{D}^{\prime}_{s},\mathrm{E}}\|_{\infty}+\max_{s\in[S]}\|f_{\mathrm{D}^{\prime}_{s},\mathrm{E}}-f\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\bigl(|\mathcal{O}|/n+\sqrt{\log(4nS)/m}\bigr)\cdot(\|f\|_{\infty}+c_{2}m^{-\gamma_{1}}\log n)+c_{2}m^{-\gamma_{1}}\log n

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigl(|\mathcal{O}|/n+\sqrt{2\log(2n)/m}\bigr)\cdot m^{-\gamma_{1}}\log n+m^{-\gamma_{1}}\log n

\displaystyle\lesssim m^{-\gamma_{1}}(\log n)^{3/2}

holds with probability
\mathrm{P}^{n}\otimes\mathrm{P}_{Z}^{T}\otimes\mathrm{P}_{U}
at least
1-1/n
, where
c_{3}:=c_{2}+10\|f\|_{\infty}
.
This together with Lemma
4.5
yields

\displaystyle\|f_{\mathcal{M}}-f\|_{\infty}\lesssim 2c_{3}(1+\|f\|_{\infty})m^{-\gamma_{1}}(\log n)^{3/2}\lesssim m^{-\gamma_{1}}(\log n)^{3/2}

with probability
\mathrm{P}^{n}\otimes\mathrm{P}_{Z}^{T}\otimes\mathrm{P}_{U}
at least
1-1/n
. This finishes the proof.
∎

We are able to prove Theorem
3.6
as a direct consequence of Proposition
4.9
.

Proof of Theorem
3.6
.

Since
|\mathcal{O}|/n\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, the threshold of
m
in (
14
), i.e.
n\wedge(n/|\mathcal{O}|)^{\gamma_{2}}
, goes to infinity.
If we let
m\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, by applying Proposition
4.9
, we obtain the consistency, i.e.
\|f_{\mathcal{M}}-f\|_{\infty}\to 0
. This finishes the proof.
∎

The upper bound (
23
) indicates that the convergence rate of MFRDE becomes faster as
m
increases. Therefore, its fastest convergence rate can be attained by taking
m
equal to the threshold
n\wedge(n/|\mathcal{O}|)^{\gamma_{2}}
, as provided in the following proof.

Proof of Theorem
3.7
.

By applying Proposition
4.9
to
m\asymp n\wedge(n/|\mathcal{O}|)^{\gamma_{2}}
,
p=((1-2\gamma_{1})/\log 2)\cdot\log m
, and
T\asymp m^{2\gamma_{1}}
, we obtain the convergence rates. This finishes the proof.
∎

Simulations and data analysis

5.1
Simulation studies

In this section, we conduct parameter analysis on the parameters of MFRDE, including the subsampling size
m
and two parameters associated with SFDE which are the number of trees
T
and the depth
p
. Moreover, we compare the performance of MFRDE with existing robust density estimation methods under different outlier proportions and outlier types.

Computation for the integration in (
2
).
In experiments, the integral in (
2
) is approximated in the following way. In detail, let
\mathcal{Z}:=\{(-r+(2r)\cdot i/99,-r+(2r)\cdot j/99):i,j\in\{0,\ldots,99\}\}
be
10000
grid points from
B_{r}
.
Then we compute
\mathcal{M}(z_{i})
for each
z_{i}\in\mathcal{Z}
. Subsequently, the integration
\int_{B_{r}}\mathcal{M}(x)dx
can be approximated by

\frac{\mu(B_{r})}{10000}\sum_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathcal{M}(z_{i})
.

Evaluation Metrics.
Following
Cui et al. (2021)
, we employ the mean absolute error (MAE) as the criterion to evaluate the accuracy of the density estimator. To be specific, MAE is defined by
\text{MAE}(\widehat{f}):=\frac{1}{10000}\sum_{z_{i}\in\mathcal{Z}}|\widehat{f}(z_{i})-f(z_{i})|
. It is used in synthetic data experiments where the true density function
f
is known. A lower
MAE
indicates a better estimation of the true density.

Distributions of Inliers and Outliers.
Following
Cui et al. (2021)
, we assume that the first dimension of two-dimensional inliers is drawn from the exponential distribution with density
f_{1}(x):=\frac{1}{2}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}x)\boldsymbol{1}_{(0,\infty)}(x)
and the second dimension of inliers is from the uniform distribution
f_{2}(x):=\frac{1}{5}\boldsymbol{1}_{[0,5]}(x)
.
We consider the following three schemes of outlier distributions:

•

Uniform
: Each dimension of the outliers is drawn independently from the uniform distribution
U[0,5]
.

•

Beta
: Each dimension of the outliers is drawn independently from the shifted and scaled beta distribution with density
f(x)=\frac{1}{10}(1-x/5)^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{1}_{x\in[0,5)}
.

•

Discrete
: We generate outliers by a Markov chain with discrete state space which contains
30
two-dimension points drawn from
U[0,5]\times U[2.5,5]
and the transition matrix
\boldsymbol{1}_{30}\boldsymbol{1}_{30}^{T}/30
.
The initial state is randomly chosen from the discrete state space and the first
|\mathcal{O}|
states in the Markov chain are the outliers we adopt.

It is worth mentioning that in the
Discrete
case, outliers are generated by a Markov chain and thus the outliers are dependent.
The above three outlier cases correspond to the different exponents
\beta=1,0.5,0

-

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose an ensemble learning algorithm called
medians of forests for robust density estimation
(
MFRDE
), which achieves robustness through a pointwise median operation on forest density estimators fitted on subsampled datasets. The local property of MFRDE allows for larger subsampling sizes, sacrificing less accuracy while achieving robustness. Theoretical analysis shows that even if the number of outliers reaches a certain polynomial order in the sample size, MFRDE achieves almost the same convergence rate as the same algorithm fitted on uncontaminated data, making it superior to robust kernel-based methods. The practical application of MFRDE to anomaly detection further showcases its superior performance. Overall, MFRDE presents a promising approach to robust density estimation in various fields, particularly in the presence of outliers.

Definition A.1

(VC dimension)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a class of subsets of
\mathcal{X}
and
A\subset\mathcal{X}
be a finite set. The trace of
\mathcal{B}
on

is defined by

. We say that
\mathcal{B}

\Delta^{\mathcal{B}}(A)=2^{\#(A)}
, that is, if for every

such that
A^{\prime}=B\cap A
. For

, let

\displaystyle m^{\mathcal{B}}(n):=\sup_{A\subset\mathcal{X},\,\#(A)=n}\Delta^{\mathcal{B}}(A).

(25)

Then, the set
\mathcal{B}
is a Vapnik-Chervonenkis class if there exists

such that

and the minimal of such

is called the VC dimension of

, and abbreviate as

Since an arbitrary set of

\mathcal{B}

picks out

if this can be formed as a set of the form

for a

. The collection
\mathcal{B}

shatters

\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
if each of its

subsets can be picked out in this manner.
From Definition
A.1
we see that the VC dimension of the class
\mathcal{B}
is the smallest

for which no set of size
n
is shattered by
\mathcal{B}
, that is,

\displaystyle\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{B})=\inf\Bigl\{n:\max_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}}\Delta^{\mathcal{B}}(\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\})\leq 2^{n}\Bigr\},

where
\Delta^{\mathcal{B}}(\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\})=\#\{B\cap\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}:B\in\mathcal{B}\}
.
Clearly, the more refined
\mathcal{B}
is, the larger is its index.
