The Lyman- 𝜶 \alpha Forest from LBGs: First 3D Correlation Measurement with DESI and Prospects for Cosmology
H. Zou

Abstract
The Lyman- α \alpha (Ly α \alpha ) forest is a key tracer of large-scale structure at redshifts z > 2 z>2 , traditionally studied using the spectra of luminous but relatively rare quasars. In this work, we explore the viability of using the fainter yet significantly more abundant Lyman Break Galaxies (LBGs) as alternative background sources for Ly α \alpha forest studies. We analyze 4,151 Ly α \alpha forest skewers extracted from LBG spectra obtained in the DESI pilot surveys conducted in the COSMOS and XMM-LSS fields. From this dataset, we present the first measurement of the Ly α \alpha forest auto-correlation function derived exclusively from LBG spectra, probing comoving separations up to 48 h − 1 ​ Mpc h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc} at an effective redshift of z eff = 2.70 z_{\rm eff}=2.70 . The measured LBG Ly α \alpha forest auto-correlation is consistent with that derived from DESI DR2 quasar Ly α \alpha forest spectra at a comparable redshift, validating the use of LBGs as reliable background sources for Ly α \alpha forest analyses. In addition, we measure the cross-correlation between the LBG Ly α \alpha forest and the positions of 13,362 galaxies, demonstrating that this observable serves as a sensitive diagnostic for assessing the precision and accuracy of galaxy redshift estimates, and for identifying and correcting systematic offsets. Finally, using both synthetic LBG spectra and Fisher matrix forecasts, we show that a future wide-area survey covering ∼ \sim 5,000 deg 2 \deg^{2} , targeting 1,000 LBGs per square degree at signal-to-noise levels comparable to our sample, could enable LBG-based Ly α \alpha forest baryon acoustic oscillation (BAO) measurements with expected uncertainties of σ α ISO = 0.4 % \sigma_{\alpha_{\rm ISO}}=0.4\% (isotropic) and σ α AP = 1.3 % \sigma_{\alpha_{\rm AP}}=1.3\% (Alcock–Paczynski). This performance is further enhanced when combining the BAO analysis with a Ly α \alpha forest Full Shape (FS) approach, yielding a predicted uncertainty of σ α AP FS = 0.6 % \sigma^{\mathrm{FS}}_{\alpha_{\rm AP}}=0.6\% . These results open a new avenue for precision cosmology at high redshift using the Ly α \alpha forest in dense LBG samples.

Introduction

The large-scale statistical properties of the matter distribution encode a wealth of information on our Universe, from its expansion history to the primordial fluctuations and non-Gaussianities, as well as the gravitational make-up of the cosmic web and the related formation of galaxies. For that reason, a number of surveys such as DESI
[
1
,
2
]
, EUCLID
[
3
]
, PFS
[
4
]
, and WEAVE
[
5
]
are ongoing or planned to measure these cosmological structures over a wide range of scales and redshifts. At redshifts
2<z\lesssim 4
, a remarkably powerful probe is the Lyman-
\alpha
(Ly
\alpha
) forest. The Ly
\alpha
forest is a set of absorption features imprinted onto the optical spectra of background sources. These arise from resonant Lyman-
\alpha
absorption and scattering due to the presence of neutral hydrogen along the line-of-sight. Given that most of this Ly
\alpha
forest signal comes from regions located in the dilute intergalactic medium (IGM)
[
6
]
, these spectroscopic features encode information on the physical properties of this medium
[
7
]
.

At the same time, the Ly
\alpha
forest can be considered as a biased tracer of the large-scale matter distribution. The 3D correlations between absorption features from different lines-of-sight, first measured in
[
8
]
, can be interpreted using a linear clustering model, valid on large, linear scales, and incorporating relevant astrophysical and instrumental contaminants. As a consequence, the baryon acoustic oscillation (BAO) feature at a typical redshift of
z\sim 2.3
could be measured from the large Ly
\alpha
forest samples collected by the BOSS and eBOSS surveys
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
and more recently by the DESI survey
[
12
,
13
]
. The precision achieved on the isotropic BAO distance scale from its latest result (
[
13
]
, hereafter
13
) is at the 0.7 % level. It should be noted that these BAO measurements are actually from a combination of two correlation signals: on the one hand, the auto-correlation signal from the Ly
\alpha
forest, and on the other hand the cross-correlation between those Ly
\alpha
forests and the 3D sky position of the background sources, such as pioneered in
[
14
]
.

Until now, most of the background sources used to carry out Ly
\alpha
forest observations were quasars (also denoted as QSOs). Indeed quasars are among the brightest optical sources in the Universe, with a strong continuum flux in the rest-frame UV wavelength range. Therefore spectra with a good signal-to-noise ratio can be measured from those sources up to high redshift, in relatively short exposure times with existing telescopes. However quasars are rare objects in our Universe, especially when restricting to those with redshift
z>2.1
, which have their Ly
\alpha
forest in the optical observed wavelength range. The quasar luminosity function was measured over a wide range of redshift and magnitude from SDSS spectroscopic observations in
[
15
,
16
]
: predictions from models fitted in
[
16
]
indicate that, in the redshift range
2<z<3
, there are only
\sim 140
quasars per deg
2
with magnitude
r<24
, and down to the deeper limit of
g<25
the expected number count is
\sim 230
per deg
2
. In practice, the DESI survey obtained spectra for 60 high-redshift (
z>2.1
) quasars per deg
2
, using a photometric selection
[
17
]
limited to
r<23
. Such a sky density, while unprecedented over a wide footprint, remains a limiting factor for several scientific applications. For instance, it restricts measurements of line-of-sight absorption cross-correlations between close quasar pairs
[
18
,
19
]
, and also affects the detection of the BAO feature, which remains in the shot-noise-limited regime for DESI
[
20
]
.

In order to increase significantly the sky density of Ly
\alpha
forest samples, other background sources than quasars are needed. Lyman Break Galaxies (LBGs) are prime candidates, as these star-forming galaxies are among the most readily-detected galaxies at high redshifts. Their distinctive break at the location of the Lyman limit at 912 Å makes it possible to select them from broadband photometry
[
21
]
. On the other hand, they are typically fainter than quasars, which means that extracting the Ly
\alpha
forest signal from their spectra requires longer exposure times, larger telescopes, or both. Despite these challenges, the Ly
\alpha
forest in LBG spectra has already been exploited in surveys covering relatively small areas, such as CLAMATO
[
22
,
23
]
, and LATIS
[
24
]
. These efforts aim to reconstruct the 3D cosmic web down to
\sim
Mpc scales, enabling, for example, the identification of high-redshift protoclusters.

In this article we pioneer the study of the Ly
\alpha
forest in LBG spectra in the context of cosmological surveys spanning a much larger footprint. We use DESI observations in order to demonstrate that future surveys, with the DESI instrument or other telescopes, have the potential to use the Ly
\alpha
forest in LBG spectra to improve cosmological measurements with respect to those based on quasars alone. The sample we present here is a set of optical spectra from high-redshift sources, collected by the DESI instrument over two fields, COSMOS and XMM-LSS. We present these samples in
Section

2
. In
Sections

3
and
4
, we extract their Ly
\alpha
forest signals, and measure the correlations between Ly
\alpha
forest from separate lines-of-sight, obtaining the first 3D Ly
\alpha
forest correlation measurement from LBG spectra over scales ranging from 1 to 48
h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
. We show that these correlations can be fit with the same model that is used to interpret the DESI Ly
\alpha
forest correlations from quasar spectra. In
Section

5
, we present forecasts for high-redshift BAO measurements with future large LBG surveys.

2
Dataset

DESI is a fully robotic, highly multiplexed spectroscopic instrument installed on the 4‑meter Mayall Telescope at Kitt Peak National Observatory
[
25
]
. It delivers simultaneous spectra for up to 5,000 targets over a 3.2
∘
diameter field of view
[
2
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
. The majority of DESI’s dark‑time observing program
[
29
]
is dedicated to its main survey, which aims to acquire spectra for roughly 50 million galaxies, including several million quasars at
z\gtrsim 1.6
. In addition, DESI has conducted dedicated observing campaigns in the COSMOS and XMM–LSS fields during both the survey validation and main-survey phases
[
30
]
, including targeted pilot programs to test and refine observation strategies for high-redshift galaxies such as Lyman-break galaxies (LBGs) and Lyman-
\alpha

In the following section, we describe the target selection strategies and the automated redshift estimation pipeline used to construct the LBG and LAE samples for this work. Preliminary analyses of the Ly
\alpha\times
LBG and Ly
\alpha\times
LAE cross-correlations revealed systematic redshift offsets of
\Delta v_{\rm LBG}=-169\pm 22\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}
for LBGs and
\Delta v_{\rm LAE}=-241\pm 20\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}
for LAEs. To mitigate potential biases in the clustering signal, such as a shift in the cross-correlation along the line-of-sight due to these offsets, we apply a correction to the redshift estimates in all subsequent measurements. Details of these corrections are provided in Appendix
A
.

2.1
Target Selection

LBG spectra exhibit a pronounced flux “break” just blueward of the 912 Å Lyman limit in the rest frame, primarily caused by absorption from neutral hydrogen in stellar atmospheres and the interstellar medium (ISM). At high redshifts (
z>2
), additional Ly
\alpha
forest absorption caused by the intergalactic medium, further suppresses the flux blueward of 1216 Å. These characteristic features enable efficient identification of Lyman-break galaxies (LBGs) at
z\sim 2.3
via broad-band photometry, particularly through dropout selection in the
u
-band.

Our target selection employs the
u
-dropout technique, based on deep imaging from the Hyper Suprime-Cam survey
[
31
, HSC;]
, complemented by
u
-band imaging from the Canada-France-Hawaii Telescope’s CFHT Large Area U-band Deep Survey
[
32
, CLAUDS;]
, obtained with MegaCam.

We adopt two complementary
u
-dropout selection strategies. The first, described in
[
21
]
(hereafter
21
), applies color cuts in the
u-g
vs
g-r
plane, alongside a magnitude threshold in the
r
band. The second approach, presented in
[
33
]
, utilizes a random forest algorithm trained on HSC+CLAUDS
ugriz
photometry artificially degraded to the depth of the Ultraviolet Near-Infrared Optical Northern Survey
[
34
, UNIONS;]
, forecasting selection performance for upcoming surveys such as DESI-II.

A smaller subset of our sample is selected using medium-band photometry. In this case, Lyman-alpha emitters (LAEs) are identified by flux excesses in narrow-band filters, while LBGs are selected based on flux decrements between adjacent bands. We emphasize that throughout this work, the term “LAE”
refers exclusively to the photometric selection technique
, rather than to the strength of the Ly
\alpha
emission.

All LBG and LAE targets presented here were observed in DESI pilot programs conducted between 2021 and 2024 in the COSMOS and XMM fields, with exposure times ranging from 2 to 5 hours. In our Ly
\alpha
forest analysis (see
Section

3
), only the LBGs serve as background sources. The median
r
-band magnitude of the LBG sample is
r=23.94
.

2.2
Automated Redshift Measurement

Spectra of the LBG and LAE targets were obtained with the DESI 3-arm spectrographs, which cover the wavelength range 3,600–9,800 Å with a spectral resolution between 2,000 and 5,000
[
35
]
. Given the redshift range of these sources, most of the key spectral features fall within the blue arm (3,600–5,930 Å), where the resolution varies from 2,000 to 3,000. Raw spectra were processed using the DESI spectroscopic pipeline
[
36
]
, and multiple exposures of the same object were coadded with weights proportional to their signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).

Redshift estimation proceeds in two stages. First, a convolutional neural network (CNN) identifies whether the spectrum corresponds to an LBG or LAE and assigns a preliminary redshift. Second, a template-fitting algorithm refines the redshift determination.

The CNN is a modified version of
QuasarNET

[
37
,
38
]
, which DESI uses for quasar classification. For LBG classification, we retrained the CNN using 14 interstellar absorption lines and 2 emission lines (see Table 3 in
21
). Each of the 16 lines are assigned a CNN confidence level (
\mathrm{CL}
) and are sorted in descending order. A spectrum is classified as an LBG or LAE if its fifth-highest
\mathrm{CL}
exceeds a chosen threshold. Additional details on the CNN algorithm and performance can be found in Section 4.1 of
21
.

To refine spectral classification and the redshift estimate, the redshift of the line with the highest CNN confidence is used as a prior for the
redrock
software

1
1

1

https://github.com/desihub/redrock

[
39
]
, DESI’s standard classifier and redshift-fitting tool. We employ a customized version of
redrock
that includes four LBG templates derived from visually confirmed spectra in
21
: one template without Ly
\alpha
emission and three with increasing Ly
\alpha
emission strength.

For our baseline analysis, we adopt a confidence threshold of
\mathrm{CL}>0.995
to enhance sample purity while preserving statistical power. We also consider thresholds in the range
0.97<\mathrm{CL}<0.999
for robustness checks.

Data Analysis

One of the main goals of this work is to present the first measurement of the Ly
\alpha
forest auto‐correlation (Ly
\alpha\times
Ly
\alpha
) extracted solely from LBG spectra, together with the corresponding cross‐correlations between Ly
\alpha
absorption and LBG (Ly
\alpha\times
LBG) and LAE (Ly
\alpha\times
LAE) positions. Our analysis builds on the
13
framework for quasar‐forest studies, implemented via the public
picca
package

2
2

2

https://github.com/igmhub/picca

[
40
]
.

Below, we outline the steps of our correlation‐function measurement pipeline, highlighting the minor differences with respect to the quasar-based pipeline. For further methodological details, we refer the reader to the
13

3.1
Continuum Fitting and Extraction of Transmitted Flux Field

The first step in our analysis is to preprocess the spectra. Following
13
, this normally comprises a series of operations but we do not apply all of them here. We mask bad pixels identically to
13
, removing regions affected by galactic absorption, residual sky-line emission, and CCD defects flagged by the DESI reduction pipeline
[
36
]
(e.g., cosmic ray contamination or hardware-related issues). On the other hand, we do not recalibrate the fluxes as the redshift range (
2.0<z<3.5
) of our LBG sample is not wide enough to completely cover a calibration region (typically C III; see Table 3 of
[
41
]
) in the 3,600–5,423 Å observed frame wavelength range that we have adopted for our analysis.

Unlike quasar forest analyses, we also do not mask Damped Ly
\alpha
Absorbers (DLAs) or Broad Absorption Line (BAL) features. BALs are quasar-specific phenomena and are not expected to appear in LBG spectra. DLAs, however, are present in LBG spectra and are expected to impact our measurements. Unfortunately, no dedicated DLA catalog exists for our LBG sample. Existing DLA detection algorithms, developed for quasar spectra, cannot be straightforwardly applied here due to the differing SNR characteristics and different spectral shapes LBGs with respect to quasars. Furthermore, DLAs in low-SNR quasar spectra (SNR
<3
) are typically not masked in quasar forest analyses (e.g.,
13
), and most of our LBG spectra fall below this SNR threshold. Adapting any of the existing quasar‐based DLA finders
[
42
,
43
,
44
, e.g.,]
is beyond the scope of this work.

After spectral preprocessing, we extract the transmitted‐flux field,

\delta_{g}(\lambda)=\frac{f_{g}(\lambda)}{\overline{F}(\lambda)C_{g}(\lambda)}-1,

(3.1)

where
g
indexes each LBG,
f_{g}(\lambda)
is the observed flux,
\overline{F}(\lambda)
is the mean transmission, and
C_{g}(\lambda)
is the unabsorbed LBG rest-frame spectrum, hereafter referred to as the continuum. We limit our analysis to the
1040-1205
Å rest-frame wavelength interval and the
3600<\lambda_{\rm obs}<5423
Å observed wavelength range, which corresponds to a redshift range of
1.98<z<3.5
.

As in quasar analyses, we model
\overline{F}(\lambda)C_{g}(\lambda)
by a universal rest‐frame continuum,
\overline{C}_{\rm LBG}(\lambda_{\rm RF})
, modified by a first-degree polynomial in
\Lambda=\log\lambda
with two free parameters,
a_{g}
and
b_{g}
,

\overline{F}(\lambda)\ C_{g}(\lambda)=\overline{C}_{\rm LBG}(\lambda_{\rm RF})\left(a_{g}+b_{g}\ \frac{\Lambda-\Lambda_{\rm min}}{\Lambda_{\rm max}-\Lambda_{\rm min}}\right).

(3.2)

We determine
\overline{C}_{\rm LBG}
,
a_{g}
, and
b_{g}
through an iterative likelihood maximization process, simultaneously fitting the pixel‐variance,

\sigma_{g}^{2}=\eta(\lambda)\ \sigma^{2}_{{\rm pipe},g}(\lambda)+\sigma^{2}_{\rm LSS}(\lambda)\left[\overline{F}(\lambda)C_{g}(\lambda)\right]^{2},

(3.3)

where
\sigma^{2}_{{\rm pipe},g}(\lambda)
is the reported variance from the data processing pipeline
[
36
]
,
\eta(\lambda)
corrects for small inaccuracies in the noise estimate,
3
3

3

The
13
pipeline treats
\eta(\lambda)
as a free parameter and found values close to unity with small fluctuations. Given the limited size of our dataset, we decided to fix
\eta(\lambda)=1
.
and
\sigma^{2}_{\rm LSS}(\lambda)
is a free parameter that captures the intrinsic Ly
\alpha
flux variance.

Additionally, we apply quality cuts to remove forest skewers with (i) negative fitted continuum, (ii) negative mean SNR in the forest region, or (iii) fewer than 150 valid pixels. This yields 3,027 accepted forests in COSMOS and 1,124 in XMM. The median SNR of spectra in the forest region is 0.35 Å
-1
, which is about 4.5 times lower than the median SNR of 1.57 Å
-1
found in the quasar Ly
\alpha
forests sample used in
13
. The distribution of SNR as a function of

.

3.2
Correlation Function Measurement

We estimate both the Ly
\alpha
auto-correlation of the transmitted‐flux field
\delta_{g}(\lambda)
(described in
Section

3.1
) and its cross-correlation with the LBG/LAE tracer catalogs introduced in
Section

2
. Pair separations, either between Ly
\alpha
pixels in the auto-correlation or between Ly
\alpha
pixels and galaxies in the cross-correlation, are binned in the line-of-sight (
r_{\parallel}
) and transverse (
r_{\perp}
) directions. To compute these separations, angular and redshift differences are converted into comoving distances using the fiducial cosmology of
[
47
]
.

For both correlation functions, we adopt
\Delta R=2\ h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
bins spanning the range
0<r_{\parallel},r_{\perp}<48\ h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
for the auto-correlation, and
0<|r_{\parallel}|,r_{\perp}<48\ h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
for the cross-correlations. In the latter case, negative
r_{\parallel}
values correspond to configurations where the tracer galaxies are located behind the Ly
\alpha
pixels along the line of sight.

The auto‐correlation in a
M\equiv(r_{\parallel},r_{\perp})
bin, is defined as

\xi^{\alpha\times\alpha}_{M}=\frac{\sum_{i,j\in M}w^{\alpha}_{i}\ w^{\alpha}_{j}\ \delta_{i}\ \delta_{j}}{\sum_{i,j\in M}w^{\alpha}_{i}\ w^{\alpha}_{j}},

(3.4)

where
\delta_{i}\equiv\delta_{g}(\lambda_{i})
, and each Ly
\alpha
pixel weight,
w_{i}^{\alpha}
, is

w^{\alpha}_{i}\equiv w(\lambda_{i})=\left(\frac{1+z_{i}}{1+2.25}\right)^{\gamma_{\alpha}-1}\left[\eta(\lambda_{i})\ \tilde{\sigma}^{2}_{{\rm pipe},g}(\lambda_{i})+\eta_{\rm LSS}\ \sigma^{2}_{\rm LSS}(\lambda_{i})\right]^{-1},

(3.5)

where
\tilde{\sigma}^{2}_{{\rm pipe},g}(\lambda)\equiv\sigma^{2}_{{\rm pipe},g}(\lambda)/[\overline{F}(\lambda)C_{g}(\lambda)]^{2}
,
\gamma_{\alpha}=2.9

[
48
]
,
\eta(\lambda)=1
, and
\eta_{\rm LSS}
modulates the contribution of the intrinsic Ly
\alpha
forest flux variance.
4
4

4

In
13
,
\eta_{\rm LSS}
was set to 7.5 based on results from the DESI Early Data Release (EDR) Ly
\alpha
analysis to minimize the quasar Ly
\alpha
forest auto-correlation covariance
[
41
,
49
]
. Here, we fix
\eta_{\rm LSS}=1
as determining the optimal value for LBG forests requires further study.

Similarly, the Ly
\alpha
cross‐correlation with the galaxy tracer
X
is

\xi^{\rm\alpha\times X}_{M}=\frac{\sum_{i,j\in M}w^{\alpha}_{i}\ w^{\rm X}_{j}\ \delta_{i}}{\sum_{i,j\in M}w^{\alpha}_{i}\ w^{\rm X}_{j}},

(3.6)

with galaxy tracer weights,
w^{\rm X}_{i}
, given by

w^{\rm X}_{i}=\left(\frac{1+z_{i}}{1+2.25}\right)^{\gamma_{\rm X}-1}.

(3.7)

In this analysis, we adopt
\gamma{\rm X}=1.44
, following the convention commonly used for quasars
[
50
]
.
While more detailed models exist for the redshift and magnitude dependence evolution of dropout-selected galaxies (e.g., Equation 2.7 in
[
51
]
) we opt here for this simplified prescription. Incorporating a more refined evolution model is left for future work.

We estimate the covariance matrix of our correlation measurements by dividing the survey footprint into HEALPix pixels with
N_{\rm side}=64

[
52
]
, each covering approximately
0.84\ \deg^{2}
, which corresponds to a comoving scale of
\sim 70\ h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
at
z=2.7
. We compute the correlation function independently within each HEALPix region and derive the covariance matrix using a bootstrap resampling approach, drawing 10,000 realizations from the set of HEALPix pixel subsamples. The estimated covariance is then smoothed following the same recipe as described in Section 3.3 of
[
53
]
.

Due to the relatively small footprint of our analysis (and hence the limited number of independent samples) the estimated covariance may be underestimated. This limitation could be mitigated in future work by analyzing larger sky areas or by extracting the covariance from several realizations of synthetic LBG spectra datasets.

3.3
Model of the Correlation Functions

Deriving tight model constraints is not the primary objective of this study. Nevertheless, we include a model of the correlation functions, analogous to that used by
13
, for completeness. Our analysis employs an anisotropic cross-power spectrum model,

P^{\rm\alpha\times Y}(\mathbf{k},z)=b_{\alpha}b_{\rm Y}\left(1+\beta_{\alpha}\mu_{k}^{2}\right)\left(1+\beta_{\rm Y}\mu_{k}^{2}\right)P_{L}(k,z)G(\mathbf{k})F_{\rm NL}(\mathbf{k}),

(3.8)

where
Y
denotes either Ly
\alpha
or a galaxy tracer, and
\mathbf{k}\equiv(k_{\parallel},k_{\perp})
is the wave-number vector with magnitude
k=\sqrt{k_{\parallel}^{2}+k_{\perp}^{2}}
and
\mu_{k}=k_{\parallel}/k
representing the cosine of the angle between
\mathbf{k}
and the line-of-sight. In this model,
P_{L}(k,z)
is the linear matter power spectrum computed using
CAMB
[
54
]
under the fiducial cosmology of
[
47
]
,
G(\mathbf{k})=\sinc\left(k_{\parallel}\Delta R/2\right)\sinc\left(k_{\perp}\Delta R/2\right)
accounts for the finite binning
\Delta R=2\ h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
of the correlation functions, and
F_{\rm NL}(\mathbf{k})
captures non-linear corrections at small scales.

In the auto-correlation,
F_{\rm NL}
accounts for non-linear structure growth, peculiar velocities, and IGM thermal effects (e.g., broadening and pressure) which are incorporated as specified in Equation 3.6 of
[
55
]
, with parameters
q_{1}=0.8424
,
q_{2}=0
,
k_{v}=0.69516\ h\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}
,
a_{v}=0.4015
,
b_{v}=1.711
, and
k_{p}=21.13\ h\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}
obtained by interpolating the best-fit model from the L160R25 ACCEL2 hydrodynamical simulation at
z\sim 2.7
(see Table A3 of
[
56
]
). For the cross-correlation, the combined effects of galaxy velocity dispersion and statistical redshift estimation errors are modeled by a Lorentzian profile:

F^{\rm cross}_{\rm NL}(\mathbf{k})=\left[1+k^{2}_{\parallel}\sigma^{2}_{z,\rm X}\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}},

(3.9)

where
\sigma_{z,\rm X}
is a free parameter representing the velocity dispersion of the tracer due to both peculiar velocities and redshift errors. In addition, to account for systematic redshift offsets that shift the cross-correlation along the line-of-sight, we introduce a free parameter,
\Delta r_{\parallel,\rm X}
, which quantifies this shift.

We model the redshift evolution of the linear biases for Ly
\alpha
and galaxies using a power law,
b_{\rm Y}(z)=b_{\rm Y}(z_{\rm eff})\left[(1+z)/(1+z_{\rm eff})\right]^{\gamma_{\rm Y}}
, with
\gamma_{\alpha}=2.9
for Ly
\alpha
and
\gamma_{\rm X}=1.44
for galaxy tracers, as in Sect.
3.2
. For galaxy tracers, the RSD parameter is derived from the relation
\beta_{\rm X}=f(z)/b_{\rm X}(z)
, where
f(z)\sim\Omega_{m}^{0.55}(z)
is the linear growth rate. In our analysis,
f(z)
is fixed to its value at
z\sim 2.7
(i.e,
f\sim 0.98
). Note that this relation is not applied to the Ly
\alpha
forest, for which
\beta_{\alpha}
is treated as independent, though we approximate it as constant in redshift for simplicity, despite evidence of redshift dependence
[
55
,
56
]
.

Additionally, we account for contributions to the correlation functions from contaminants to the Ly
\alpha
forest such as unmasked High Column Density systems (HCDs), absorption from other atomic transition lines blended within the Ly
\alpha
forest (hereafter referred to as metals), and correlated noise. These contributions, if not properly modeled, could bias the Ly
\alpha
forest parameter measurements. The procedures used to incorporate these effects are described in
Section

3.3.1
.

The final power spectrum model is decomposed into multipoles up to
\ell=6
and transformed into correlation function multipoles via a Hankel transform. The resulting model is then interpolated onto an
(r_{\parallel},r_{\perp})
grid to yield the following forms for the correlation functions:

\displaystyle\xi^{\alpha\times\alpha}_{\rm Total}

\displaystyle=\xi^{\alpha\times\alpha}+\sum_{m}\xi^{\alpha\times m}+\xi^{\rm noise},

(3.10)

\displaystyle\xi^{\rm\alpha\times X}_{\rm Total}

\displaystyle=\xi^{\rm\alpha\times X}+\sum_{m}\xi^{{\rm X}\times m},

(3.11)

where each
m
labels a distinct metal transition. Finally, distortions in the correlation functions arising from the continuum fitting procedure are incorporated via a distortion matrix, following the approach described in Section III.A of
13
and in
[
57
]
.

3.3.1
Contaminants Model

High Column Density systems.

HCDs, including Lyman‑limit systems (LLS) and DLAs, are biased tracers of the underlying matter density field. When blended into the Ly
\alpha
forest, their presence introduces a scale‑dependent excess of large‑scale power into the 3D Ly
\alpha
forest correlations
[
58
,
59
,
60
]
, boosting the inferred Ly
\alpha
linear bias and suppressing the RSD parameter
[
61
]
. To account for these effects, we model the Ly
\alpha
bias and RSD as “effective” parameters that include a scale‑dependent HCD contribution:

\displaystyle b^{\prime}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=b_{\alpha}+b_{\rm HCD}F_{\rm HCD}(k_{\parallel}),

(3.12)

\displaystyle b^{\prime}_{\alpha}\beta^{\prime}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=b_{\alpha}\beta_{\alpha}+b_{\rm HCD}\beta_{\rm HCD}F_{\rm HCD}(k_{\parallel}),

(3.13)

where
b_{\rm HCD}
and
\beta_{\rm HCD}
are the linear bias and RSD parameter associated with the HCD contribution to the transmitted-flux fraction. Note that
b_{\rm HCD}
is negative and small in this context, as it describes absorption in the transmitted-flux field, rather than the clustering bias of the HCD systems themselves, which is typically
\sim 2

[
62
,
63
]
. The term
F_{\rm HCD}(k_{\parallel})=\exp(-L_{\rm HCD} k_\parallel)
describes the scale-dependent contribution of HCDs to the correlation function, with
L_{\rm HCD}
representing their typical length scale.

Other atomic transitions (metals).

Our pipeline assumes that every absorption in the analysis region arises from Ly
\alpha
but in reality metal absorption blended into the forest introduce spurious correlations most noticeable along the line-of-sight. .
We include this contamination via metal cross-terms,
\xi^{\alpha\times m}
in the auto‐correlation and
\xi^{{\rm X}\times m}
in the cross‐correlation. Given the scales relevant to our analysis, we model only the contribution from the Si III(1207) transition, treating its linear bias,
b_{\rm Si\thinspace{}III(1207)}
, as a free parameter and fixing its RSD to
\beta_{\rm Si\thinspace{}III(1207)}=0.5
. We neglect metal auto‐correlations and cross-correlation between distinct metals as they are subdominant, although strong transitions such as C IV, Si IV, and Mg II can in principle significantly contribute through their auto-correlation
[
64
]
. However preliminary analyses did not show a robust signal detection.

Correlated noise.

The DESI data reduction pipeline introduces correlated noise between fibers on the same focal‐plane petal
[
36
,
64
]
, which contaminates the Ly
\alpha
forest auto‐correlation. We model this contribution as an additive term,

\xi^{\rm noise}=a_{\rm noise}\delta^{K}(r_{\parallel})f(r_{\perp}),

(3.14)

where
a_{\rm noise}
is a free parameter that sets the amplitude of the noise contribution,
\delta^{K}
is the Kronecker delta in
r_{\parallel}
, and
f(r_{\perp})
is a decreasing function of
r_{\perp}
, normalized such that
f(0)=1
, proportional to the probability that two pixels originate from the same petal.

4.2
Model parameter constraints

As noted in
Section

3.3
, deriving tight model constraints is beyond the primary scope of this work. This is primarily because the statistical power of our measurements is insufficient, and also because we adopt a simplified model that does not fully capture the underlying physics on the small scales considered here, potentially biasing the results. Nevertheless, we perform model fitting for completeness and emphasize that
the following results should be interpreted with caution
.

We adopt a Gaussian likelihood and use the
Vega
package

5
5

5

https://github.com/andreicuceu/vega
to model the correlation functions. Best-fit parameter values and statistics are estimated using the

[

75
]

[
76

77

, both integrated within the

to allow for possible redshift evolution of

We carry out auto+cross-correlation joint fits, also accounting for their cross-covariance. We do not present auto or cross‐only fits, as their statistical power is significantly lower than that of the combined fit. We also attempted additional fits based on the Ly

Prospects for Cosmology

Our detection of the Ly
\alpha
forest auto and cross-correlations using a relatively small sample of LBG spectra demonstrates the feasibility of using the Ly
\alpha
forest in LBGs as a large-scale structure tracer. To assess the potential of LBG-based measurements for cosmological applications, we present forecasts for the precision achievable on Baryon Acoustic Oscillation (BAO) and Full Shape (FS) measurements of the correlation function. We consider a future survey covering 5,000
\deg^{2}
with a surface density of 1,000 LBGs per
\deg^{2}
, assuming target selection similar to that of our COSMOS sample. We explore the expected performance using two complementary methods: a forecast based on synthetic LBG spectra (
Section

5.1
), and a Fisher matrix approach (
Section

5.2
).

We define the parallel and transverse BAO dilation parameters as

\displaystyle\alpha_{\parallel}

\displaystyle=\frac{D_{H}(z_{\rm eff})/r_{d}}{\bigl[D_{H}(z_{\rm eff})/r_{d}\bigr]_{\rm fid}},

(5.1)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\perp}

\displaystyle=\frac{D_{M}(z_{\rm eff})/r_{d}}{\bigl[D_{M}(z_{\rm eff})/r_{d}\bigr]_{\rm fid}},

(5.2)

where
D_{H}
is the Hubble distance,
D_{M}
the comoving angular diameter distance, and
r_{d}
the sound horizon at drag epoch. The subscript “fid” denotes the values given by the Planck 2018 fiducial cosmology
[
47
]
. Alternatively, we define the isotropic dilation parameter,
\alpha_{\rm ISO}

[
53
]
and the Alcock-Paczynski distortion parameter,
\alpha_{\rm AP}
, as

\displaystyle\alpha_{\rm ISO}

\displaystyle=\frac{D^{0.55}_{H}(z_{\rm eff})D^{0.45}_{M}(z_{\rm eff})/r_{d}^{2}}{\bigl[D^{0.55}_{H}(z_{\rm eff})D^{0.45}_{M}(z_{\rm eff})/r_{d}^{2}\bigr]_{\rm fid}}=\alpha_{\parallel}^{0.55}\alpha_{\perp}^{0.45}\,

(5.3)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\rm AP}

\displaystyle=\frac{D_{M}(z_{\rm eff})/D_{H}(z_{\rm eff})}{\bigl[D_{M}(z_{\rm eff})/D_{H}(z_{\rm eff})\bigr]_{\rm fid}}=\frac{\alpha_{\perp}}{\alpha_{\parallel}},

(5.4)

where the exponent weights in
\alpha_{\rm ISO}
correspond to the optimal combination found in the
13
analysis. While these coefficients are appropriate for quasar Ly
\alpha
forest analyses, the optimal values for LBG Ly
\alpha
forest may differ. This will be clarified once BAO measurements from the LBG forest become available.

As in
Section

4
, we adopt the notation Ly
\alpha
(LBG) and Ly
\alpha
(QSO) throughout this section to explicitly differentiate between the Ly
\alpha
forest signal extracted from LBG spectra and that from quasars.

5.2
Fisher Forecast

To complement our mock-based BAO forecast (
Section

5.1
), we carry out a Fisher matrix forecast under identical survey conditions. This analysis follows the formalism developed by
[
88
]
and subsequently applied in
[
20
,
89
]
. The forecast is implemented using the publicly available
lyaforecast
code

11
11

11

https://github.com/igmhub/lyaforecast
. Unlike the mock-based forecast, however, we do not include Full Shape constraints derived from the Fisher formalism, as this functionality is not currently included in
lyaforecast
.

The Fisher matrix
F_{ij}
is related to the covariance of our dataset by the relation
[
90
]
:

F_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left(C^{-1}\frac{\delta C}{\delta\theta_{i}}C^{-1}\frac{\delta C}{\delta\theta_{j}}\right),

(5.5)

where
\theta
are a set of parameters, and
C(\theta)=\langle\delta_{i}\delta_{j}\rangle
, for a Gaussian distributed data vector
\delta
with
\langle\delta\rangle=0
. Then, the best possible unbiased estimates on
\theta
, given by the Cramer-Rao lower bound
[
91
]
, are related to the Fisher matrix via:

\sigma_{i}\geq(F^{-1})_{ii},

(5.6)

where
\sigma_{i}
is the variance on the estimate of parameter
\theta_{i}
.

Following
[
88
]
, we construct our covariance matrix in Fourier space as:

\left\langle\tilde{\delta}(\mathbf{k})\tilde{\delta}^{*}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\right\rangle=(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{D}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\left(P_{F}(\mathbf{k})+P_{w}^{\perp}P^{\mathrm{1D}}_{F}(k_{\parallel})+P^{\mathrm{eff}}_{N}\right),

(5.7)

where
P_{F}(\mathbf{k})
is the 3-dimensional Ly
\alpha
power spectrum (P3D),
P^{\mathrm{1D}}_{F}(k_{\parallel})
is the 1-dimensional Ly
\alpha
power spectrum (P1D).
P_{w}^{\perp}
is the power spectrum of the weight overdensity (
\delta_{w}(\mathbf{x}_{\perp})=w(\mathbf{x}_{\perp})/\overline{w}-1
), where
w(\mathbf{x}_{\perp})
is the weight assigned to each line of sight. The weight field is treated as white noise in the transverse direction, reflecting the assumption that weights are uncorrelated between different forests. In contrast, within each line of sight, the weights are assumed to be perfectly correlated across pixels. Finally,
P^{\mathrm{eff}}_{N}
is the weighted noise power, dependent on the target density and spectral noise. A detailed description on the estimation of each of the terms in
Equation

5.7
can be found in
[
88
]
, here we will just give a brief summary.

In general,
P_{w}^{\perp}
depends on the distribution of galaxies with magnitude and redshift, the survey area, target density and forest length. The weighting also depends on pixel variance
\sigma_{N}
, which is also a function of magnitude, redshift and observed wavelength (position within the forest)
12
12

12

Since we are assuming spectral noise is the same everywhere within a forest, we evaluate relevant terms at a mean forest wavelength, rather than iterating across all pixels.
. The noise term
P_{N}^{\mathrm{eff}}
also depends on all of the parameters mentioned above, but features
\sigma_{N}
explicitly rather than just having dependence in the weights (
P_{N}^{\mathrm{eff}}\propto\langle\sigma_{N}^{2}\rangle)
.

We adopt a SNR of 0.33 Å
-1
for the Ly
\alpha
(LBG)
\times

(LBG), Ly
\alpha
(LBG)
\times
LBG, and Ly

(LBG)

\alpha
(LBG)
\times
LBG correlation functions estimates. This corresponds to the median SNR value found in the mock LBG spectra used in our mock-based forecast. We note that this approximation is used because we lack noise estimates as a function of redshift, magnitude, and observed wavelength.

6
Summary and Conclusions

Measurements of the Ly
\alpha
forest from quasar spectra have delivered groundbreaking cosmological constraints at
z>2
, including a sub-percent precision BAO measurement at
z_{\rm eff}=2.33
from
13
. However, such studies remain limited by the relatively low number density of quasars. In this work, we present the first 3D Ly
\alpha
forest correlation analysis based exclusively on Lyman-break galaxy (LBG) spectra, using a pioneering DESI pilot sample in the COSMOS and XMM fields selected via broadband imaging
[
21
,
33
]
. By adapting the DESI Ly
\alpha
forest analysis pipeline (e.g., as used in
13
) we demonstrate that it performs robustly even on the lower signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) LBG spectra.

We measure the Ly
\alpha
forest auto-correlation function on a
0<r_{\parallel},r_{\perp}<48\ h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
grid at an effective redshift
z_{\rm eff}=2.70
, based on 4,151 forests extracted from LBG spectra. Remarkably, the results show excellent agreement with those derived from the significantly higher SNR quasar sample in
13
, firmly establishing LBGs as viable tracers of the intergalactic medium across cosmological volumes.

In addition to the auto-correlation, we analyze the Ly
\alpha
-galaxy cross-correlation using the positions of 4,931 LBGs and 8,431 Lyman-alpha emitters (LAEs). The Ly
\alpha
–galaxy cross-correlation provides a powerful tool for calibrating galaxy redshifts, allowing us to correct systematic redshift offsets of
\Delta v_{\rm LBG}=-169\pm 22\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}
for LBGs and
\Delta v_{\rm LAE}=-241\pm 20\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}
for LAEs.

These results highlight the potential of Ly
\alpha
forests extracted from LBG spectra as a complementary cosmological probe to quasar-based analyses, and thus establishes a promising route to mapping large-scale structure and constraining the nature of the Universe at redshifts
z\gtrsim 2
.

Looking ahead, we forecast the BAO measurement capabilities of a future spectroscopic survey targeting 1,000 LBGs per square degree over a 5,000 deg
2
footprint, assuming a median SNR of
\sim 0.33
Å
-1
. Using both Fisher matrix and synthetic LBG spectra-based forecasts independently, we find that the combination of the Ly
\alpha
forest in LBG auto-correlation and the Ly
\alpha\times
LBG cross-correlation can deliver competitive constraints at
z>2.6
on the radial and transverse BAO parameters, with estimated uncertainties of
\sigma_{\alpha_{\parallel}}=0.8\%
and
\sigma_{\alpha_{\perp}}=0.7\%
. These correspond to isotropic and Alcock–Paczynski (AP) parameter uncertainties of
\sigma_{\alpha_{\rm ISO}}=0.4\%
and
\sigma_{\alpha_{\rm AP}}=1.3\%
, respectively.

These results show that BAO measurements combining the Ly
\alpha
auto-correlation and the Ly
\alpha\times
LBG cross-correlation offer a promising probe of the expansion history of the high-redshift Universe, particularly through their sensitivity to the Alcock–Paczynski (AP) effect. This constraining power is further enhanced when the BAO analysis is complemented by a Full Shape analysis of the Ly
\alpha
forest, yielding a predicted uncertainty of
\sigma_{\alpha_{\rm AP}}=0.6\%
on the AP parameter.

This is especially relevant in light of recent DESI results that suggest a preference for alternative dark energy models
[
92
,
93
]
. Differentiating between such models at high redshift requires percent-level precision measurements, something achievable with large LBG samples. This level of performance is within reach of next-generation spectroscopic programs (e.g., DESI-II
[
73
]
, MUST
[
94
]
, SPEC-S5
[
95
]
, WST
[
96
]
), especially when combined with wide-area imaging from DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys
[
97
]
, UNIONS
[
34
]
, Euclid
[
98
]
, and LSST
[
99
]
. As such, the Ly
\alpha
forest from LBGs represents a critical component for future surveys aiming to test the nature of the Universe in the high-redshift regime.

Appendix A
Correction for the Bias in Redshift Estimate

The automatic redshift estimates for our LBG and LAE samples were calibrated against visually inspected spectra, which primarily rely on strong absorption features from low-ionization lines (Si II(1260), O I(1302) + Si II(1304), C II(1335)) and high-ionization lines (the Si IV(1394), Si IV(1403) doublet). As shown by
[
45
]
, these interstellar absorption lines are systematically blueshifted relative to the systemic redshift of the galaxy, leading to an underestimation of the true redshift in visual inspections.

In our correlation-function framework (see
Section

3
), a systematic error in the galaxy redshift translates into a shift of the cross-correlation along the line-of-sight. By measuring the Ly
\alpha\times
LBG cross-correlation using automatic redshift estimates we find a
\Delta r_{\rm\parallel,LBG}=-1.54\pm 0.20\ h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
offset. Similarly, for Ly
\alpha\times
LAE we measure an offset of
\Delta r_{\rm\parallel,LAE}=-2.20\pm 0.18\ h^{-1}\,\mathrm{Mpc}
. These displacements correspond to a velocity offsets of
\Delta v_{\rm LBG}=-169\pm 22\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}
and
\Delta v_{\rm LAE}=-241\pm 20\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}
at our effective redshift, respectively.

The LBG velocity offset is consistent with the blueshifts of strong interstellar absorption lines reported in
[
45
, Table 1]
, particularly the C II(1334) line and the Si IV doublet, which exhibit typical offsets of
-150\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}
and
-180\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}
relative to systemic redshifts, respectively.

, averaged over two line-of-sight separation bins:

We correct for these redshift offsets by applying a shift to each galaxy redshift, using the transformation

, where

We begin by examining metal and noise contamination. In our baseline fit, which adopts

. A plausible explanation for this discrepancy lies in differences in sample purity: as shown in

, both

Higher CL thresholds (i.e., higher purity) appear to suppress these effects and may help bring the LBG-derived values into closer agreement with those from the quasar-based analysis. For correlated noise, an additional contributing factor may be the lower SNR of the LBG spectra at lower CL thresholds, which can amplify the apparent noise signature in the auto-correlation.

Next, we examine the LBG, Ly
\alpha
, and HCD parameters. As discussed in
section

4.2
, we find that the inferred LBG bias increases with CL threshold, while the LBG redshift error parameters remain relatively stable. This behavior suggests a significant fraction of interlopers in the LBG sample at lower CLs, which biases the inferred clustering amplitude. A similar effect is expected for the Ly
\alpha

. For
\beta_{\rm HCD}
, we recover values consistent with the Gaussian prior set to

, while we are not able to robustly constrain
L_{\rm HCD}
. We expect this limitation to be alleviated as the LBG sample size increases and additional constraints (e.g., masking of DLAs) are applied.

Table

3
, we also examined the trend in
b_{\alpha}
as a function of CL threshold while fixing
\beta_{\alpha}
and the HCD parameters to the values derived from
13

b_{\alpha}

b_{\alpha}=-0.1276\pm 0.0033
for

for
\mathrm{CL}>0.99

-0.1304\pm 0.0035
for
\mathrm{CL}>0.995
, and
-0.1339\pm 0.0039
for
