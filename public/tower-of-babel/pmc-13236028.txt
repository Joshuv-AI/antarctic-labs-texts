A literature review of the pharmacokinetic profiles of 20 commonly prescribed antihypertensive medications for use in chemical adherence testing
Authors: Randah Alghamdi, Hanad Osman, Patrick J Highton, Muhammad Fahad, Farah Salim, Dan Lane, Dennis Bernieh, Sian Jenkins, Beatrice Pieri, Olivia M Smith, Mahee Gupta, Pranav Ramesh, Maciej Tomaszewski, Nilesh J Samani, Prashanth Patel, Kamlesh Khunti, Hussain Mulla, Donald JL Jones, Iain Squire, Pankaj Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNonadherence to antihypertensive medications is a major concern, with significant implications for healthcare systems and patient outcomes. Chemical adherence testing (CAT) is a recommended method to assess nonadherence objectively by detecting drugs or their metabolites in blood or urine samples. Interpretation of CAT results require relevant pharmacokinetic parameters. However, pharmacokinetic data in established sources are not graded for quality and not compiled into a single resource.We systematically collated and graded the literature on pharmacokinetic parameters for 20 commonly prescribed antihypertensive agents.A total of 53 suitable manuscripts were included. Pharmacokinetic data, including half-life and maximum concentration, were collated. Also, minimum concentration at regular dosing intervals was calculated.This resource allows clinicians and researchers to interpret CAT results with more confidence and accuracy and will help facilitate the interpretation of CAT. It also shows that there are significant gaps in available literature, and further research is needed.Keywords: antihypertensive medications, chemical adherence testing, half-life, hypertension, minimum concentration, nonadherence, peak concentration, pharmacokinetics

INTRODUCTION

Hypertension is the leading cause of disease burden globally [1]. Nonadherence in cardiovascular diseases, including hypertension management, has been linked to increased rates of hospitalizations and mortality. Adherence is defined as ‘the extent to which a person's behaviour, taking medication, following a diet, and/or executing lifestyle changes, corresponds with agreed recommendations from a healthcare provider’ [2]. The evidence collected using objective direct methods of assessment suggest that as many as 20–50% of patients are at least partially nonadherent to prescribed antihypertensive medications [3–6]. One study has estimated a 10–40% increased chance of hospitalization related to cardiovascular diseases and a 50–80% increased probability of mortality [7].

Although there are a variety of methods that can be used to assess adherence to antihypertensive medications, chemical adherence testing (CAT) has been highlighted by the European Society of Hypertension as a useful direct and objective tool to assess for medication adherence [8–10]. CAT provides an objective measure that detects drugs or their metabolites in biomatrices like urine or plasma. CAT provides a ‘snapshot’ of adherence, and recent guidelines recommend that for interpretation of results, the relevant pharmacokinetic parameters should be provided alongside the laboratory results [11]. Pharmacokinetics describes the movement of drugs within the body and consists of the study of a drug's absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion from the body [12].

Although there are multiple sources of PK data from the literature, the information in these sources has not been graded for quality or collated and compiled into a single-access reference resource to aid the interpretation of results. Furthermore, not all researchers and clinicians have access to these sources. We present here systematically collated and graded data of pharmacokinetic parameters for the 20 most commonly prescribed antihypertensive medications obtained from well established sources.

Search for relevant studies

Data on pharmacokinetic parameters were obtained from published manuscripts quoted in the following sources: Clarke's: Analysis of Drugs and Poisons 4th Edition[13]; Goodman's & Gilman's: The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics, 13th Edition[14]; Martindale: The Complete Drug Reference 39th Edition[15] and the Micromedex: Medication database[16]. These sources were selected as they are well established sources of information for pharmacokinetics.

The pharmacokinetic parameters for data collected were: clearance, volume of distribution, bioavailability, time to peak concentration (Tmax), peak concentration, half-life and fraction excreted in the urine. The minimum concentration (Cmin) in blood was calculated using the following pharmacokinetic equation: Cmin = Cmax × e-kτ, where Cmax is the maximum concentration, k is the rate constant and τ is the dosing interval [17]. The dosing interval was set at 24 or 12 h, dependent on whether the medication is usually administered once or twice daily, respectively.

Eligibility for including studies

Manuscripts were included in this analysis if: participants were healthy individuals aged 18–50 years; manuscripts contained data on steady-state concentration of the medication; the sample size used to study pharmacokinetics was at least 6; and the method of analysis was high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LCMS), gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), gas chromatography (GC), gas–liquid chromatography (GLC) or gas chromatography-electron capture detection (GC-ECD).

If the above criteria revealed no manuscripts for a particular medication, then manuscripts containing nonsteady-state, participants older than 50 years of age and participants who were not defined as healthy (but had normal renal and liver functions) were included.

Pharmacokinetic data: exclusion criteria

Manuscripts were excluded if: the medication studied was not administered orally; there was no full text available; the manuscript was not in English; the study did not involve humans; the manuscript was a case report or a systematic review (except in the absence of other data); nonclinical dosages of medications were used in the pharmacokinetic studies (except in the absence of manuscripts that used clinical doses).

Pharmacokinetic data: grading and scoring of the quality of evidence

In order to evaluate the quality of the evidence, each manuscript was graded 1 (very low) to 4 (very high), and a total score assigned, based on the following arbitrarily chosen criteria that were considered to be important for evaluating pharmacokinetic data (Table 2). Medications that were in a steady-state were scored higher than those that were single-dose pharmacokinetic studies. This is because clinically, patients are expected to be regularly taking their medications and therefore steady state is more applicable clinically than single-dose pharmacokinetic studies. The size of the study – with larger studies being scored higher, age of participants (adults aged ≤50 years were scored higher as they were considered to have pharmacokinetic parameter estimates more representative of the hypertension population), whether the participants were healthy or not and analysis method with the most sensitive method being scored the highest. A total score was assigned to each manuscript, and manuscripts were ranked according to the score.

RESULTS

Details of included studies

The total number of manuscripts eligible for inclusion in the current analysis was 243 (Figs. 1 and 2). After filtering out duplicates, 202 manuscripts remained (Supplementary Table 1). After applying the exclusion criteria, 105 published manuscripts remained, of which 27 completely matched the study criteria and were included in our analysis (Fig. 1).

The remaining 78 (31 literature reviews, 47 other manuscripts) were also reviewed to extract the data in case of a lack of available pharmacokinetic data in the included studies as explained before (Fig. 2). A further five manuscripts were identified from the reference list of the 31 literature reviews after removing any duplicated manuscripts and using the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Collated pharmacokinetic data

Table 3 is a summary of the collected pharmacokinetic parameters and the complete data are presented in Supplementary Table 5. The plasma concentration data and estimated pharmacokinetic parameters varied widely across the medications. The range of peak plasma concentrations for the 20 medications varied from 2 ng/ml for Moxonidine to 4573 ng/ml for Valsartan. The time to peak concentration ranged from 0.5 h for Nifedipine to 9.6 h for Atenolol. There was also a broad range in the half-lives among the medications studied. Diltiazem had the shortest half-life with 0.3 h, and Amlodipine had the longest at 59.6 h. Diltiazem was the medication with the maximum excretion of 96.5% in urine, whereas Irbesartan was the lowest excreted medication in urine at 0.4%. Diltiazem's distribution volume was the highest at 12.1 l/kg, whereas Furosemide and Valsartan had the lowest at 0.1 l/kg. The plasma clearance rate range was from 1.1 ml/min for Furosemide to 2966 ml/min for Diltiazem. Ramipril had the lowest bioavailability at 12.2%, while Bisoprolol and Indapamide had the highest bioavailability at 90%. The calculated Cmin at the regular dosing interval ranged from 0.01 ng/ml for Losartan to 1262.2 ng/ml for Irbesartan. The calculated Cmin per milligram of dose at the dosing interval ranged from 0.0002 mg/ml per mg for Losartan to 80.5 ng/ml per mg for Amlodipine (Table 3).

As detailed in Supplementary Table 5, most of the studies had a small sample size. Many of the reviewed studies did not present all the pharmacokinetic parameters of interest. This ranged from four studies that did not report peak concentration to 55 studies that did not report distribution volume. Further, pharmacokinetic parameters were not available for all clinically used doses, for example, pharmacokinetic data was only available for the 10 mg strength of Bisoprolol and 80 mg strength of Valsartan. Furthermore, none of the reported studies included data on minimum concentrations at steady state.

DISCUSSION

We have compiled all pharmacokinetic parameters of the 20 most commonly prescribed antihypertensive medications in the UK to provide a reference resource to aid the interpretation of the results of CAT. This information is important, as although CAT can provide the concentration of the medication detected in plasma at a given time, without the contextual information regarding the typical rates at which these medications are absorbed, distributed and eliminated, it is difficult to interpret the findings.

Recent CAT guidelines suggest that medication pharmacokinetic parameters, in particular half-lives, should be presented and discussed alongside routine medication and blood results in clinical practice in order to support more informed discussion and treatment [11]. These guidelines also provide data on interpretation of results. As a rule of thumb medications can be detected in blood for a period of four to six half-lives – thus approximately 8–12 h for Furosemide (half-life of around 2 h) and around 240–360 h for Amlodipine (half-life of around 60 h) [22,23].

Presenting the Cmax and Cmin as per milligram of dose offers a more detailed analysis of drug concentrations relative to dosing, which is particularly useful in comparing these pharmacokinetic parameters in different doses of the same drug. By adjusting for dose, clinicians can more accurately determine the concentration of the drug in the body at specific doses, facilitating more precise determination of adherence.

Patients can have intermittent adherence which refers to scenarios where patients may not take their medication consistently and regularly miss doses of their medication. Cmin can be a useful parameter in assessing intermittent adherence. If the patient takes their medications regularly, then the expected concentration of the medication would be between the Cmin and Cmax range. The Cmin data provides insightful information to clinicians and helps assess medication adherence more objectively. For example, the calculated Cmin for Amlodipine is 6.1 ng/ml. If the concentration of Amlodipine in a sample is below this value, it would indicate to clinicians that the patient may not be taking this medication regularly.

The Cmax can also provide an indication of white coat adherence or the toothbrush effect. White coat adherence or the toothbrush effect refer to patients taking their medication shortly before their clinic visit and therefore may seem to be adhering to their medications [24]. If the medication level present in the sample is above Cmax and the time of ingestion is less than the Tmax of that particular medication, it may indicate white coat adherence or the toothbrush effect, that is, the patient has taken the medication just before the clinic visit.

Another area to highlight is that the information collated will help laboratories set their quantitation method to ensure their limit of quantification (LOQ) is below the Cmin. If the LOQ is greater than the Cmin for a particular medication, the medication may not be detected even if the patient is adherent. For example, if the Cmin for a particular medication is 5 ng/ml and the LOQ for the medication is 10 ng/ml, this medication will not be detected at steady-state concentrations between the Cmin and the LOQ and therefore the patient may be deemed to be nonadherent incorrectly. It will also help laboratories determine the range of their assay (calibration range), which should be at least up to the Cmax for a medication.

This study has several strengths, it is the first comprehensive structured review of the literature on pharmacokinetic data that collated primary literature from multiple well established sources. It also provides clinically useful information for the interpretation of CAT results in blood and will allow centres that use CAT to optimize their method such that they are suitable.

It is important to note that there are significant limitations. The sample size in most studies was small, the sources were heterogenous, and information on some pharmacokinetic parameters such as urine excretion was missing. In addition, the data was available for only certain doses of medications, which in some cases such as for Spironolactone is not commonly used for treatment of hypertension. Also, the impact of drug–drug interactions and the effects of patient genotypes were not considered when extracting the pharmacokinetic data from the included studies. Also, as the majority of the pharmacokinetic data collated were from studies that mainly recruited Caucasian males, this may not be useful tool to aid for CAT interpretation for females that are not often included in pharmacokinetic studies along with other minority ethnic groups. In addition to this, different formulations (e.g. tablets, capsules and liquids and also immediate-release formulations vs. modified/controlled release formulations) will also have an impact on pharmacokinetic parameters such as Cmax and half-life. Furthermore, as the list of the 20 commonly prescribed antihypertensive medications were obtained based on the number of requests for CAT received between January 2015 and December 2019, this may not be representative of the most commonly prescribed antihypertensives in the UK currently. This study also collated plasma pharmacokinetic data as urine pharmacokinetic data is inconsistently reported in many pharmacokinetic studies. This could, therefore, have an impact on the interpretation of CAT results, which currently mainly uses urine samples. Finally, the wider implementation of CAT may also depend on the availability of specialized laboratory infrastructure and analytical expertise, which may vary across healthcare centres.

Despite these limitations, we view the data presented as the first iteration and further iterations with more data will be required to provide a complete and more robust picture. There are other sources that can be referred to obtain pharmacokinetic data on medications used for hypertension including the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Label Database, for example, however, as demonstrated in Supplementary Table 6, these resources also have the same limitation of missing pharmacokinetic data, especially Cmin and therefore would not help clinicians in interpreting CAT results accurately. Also, there are differences between the pharmacokinetic data using our method compared with the FDA Label Database. For example, the elimination half-life of Amlodipine in the FDA Label Database is between 30 and 50 h [25] compared with 59.6 h in our data. As we have systematically obtained pharmacokinetic data by grading pharmacokinetic papers, this would, therefore, make our data more reliable than the data used in FDA Label Database. We would hope that centres in the future undertake pharmacokinetic studies to develop their own Cmin and Cmax data to further strengthen the data collated and presented in this manuscript.

To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first review to collate the summary of standards and criteria for collecting and grading the data of pharmacokinetic parameters from published literature for the most commonly prescribed antihypertensive medications in the UK. The data presented herein will be a useful reference resource to aid in the interpretation of CAT results of antihypertensive medications.
