Effect of immersive virtual reality on fear, anxiety, and pain in pregnant women during vaginal examination in clinical training: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Fatemeh Abdi, Sareh Dashti, Nasibeh Roozbeh, Mohammad H. Mahrooz, Mohammadmehdi Heidari, Shayesteh Jahanfar, Tahereh Fathi Najafi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vaginal examination is an essential component of maternal assessment during pregnancy, performed to evaluate cervical status and monitor the progress of labor. Despite its clinical importance, this procedure may provoke fear, anxiety, and pain in pregnant women. Immersive Virtual Reality has emerged as a safe, non-pharmacological intervention that enables patients to interact with a device that delivers a simulated environment during medical procedures. By creating an engaging, immersive experience, VR can divert pregnant women’s attention away from the immediate clinical setting of the examination, thereby alleviating psychological distress and discomfort. This study aimed to investigate the effect of immersive VR on fear, anxiety, and pain among pregnant women during clinical training of vaginal examination. This randomized controlled trial was performed in a teaching hospital in Mashhad, Iran. (IRCT20220704055367N7). The participants of this trial were 70 pregnant women who were in the latent or active phase of labor and required vaginal examination as part of routine intrapartum care. The participants were randomly assigned to either an immersive VR intervention group (n = 35) or a standard-care control group (n = 35). The participants who were assigned to the VR group used a head-mounted display with a relaxing virtual environment during the entire vaginal examination conducted by medical trainees. The state of anxiety was assessed by using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) before and immediately after the vaginal examination. Pain was assessed by using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), McGill Pain Questionnaire (MPQ), and the fear of pelvic examination checklist immediately after the vaginal examination. A total of 70 pregnant women were randomized into VR and control groups. Post-intervention analysis revealed significantly lower state anxiety in the VR group compared to controls (34.31 ± 9.07 vs. 45.66 ± 13.03; p < 0.001). Pain intensity measured by VAS was also significantly reduced in the VR group (3.43 ± 2.13 vs. 6.29 ± 2.18; p < 0.001). The McGill Pain Questionnaire showed significantly lower scores across all pain dimensions in the VR group (p < 0.05). Furthermore, fear during all stages of the vaginal examination was significantly lower in the VR group than in the control group. (p < 0.001). Immersive VR serves as an effective non-drug treatment that delivers safe patient results while maintaining high user satisfaction because it decreases fear and anxiety and pain levels during vaginal examinations conducted by trainees on pregnant women. Background

Vaginal examination is a common clinical procedure used to assess the female reproductive system for the diagnosis and management of sexually transmitted infections, gynecological cancers, and as part of routine prenatal care1. In pregnancy, vaginal examination constitutes an essential and sensitive component of maternal assessment, performed to evaluate cervical status, monitor the progress of labor, and detect potential complications. Despite its clinical importance, this procedure is often associated with fear, anxiety, and pain among pregnant women2 Research indicates that many women experience vaginal examination as distressing, invasive, embarrassing, humiliating, and uncomfortable3.

Pregnant women are particularly vulnerable to anxiety due to the hormonal and emotional changes characteristic of this period4. Stress-induced muscular tension, particularly contraction of the pelvic floor muscles, may compromise the accuracy of assessing cervical dilation, fetal head position, and labor progression5. Maternal anxiety during vaginal examination may interfere with the accuracy of the procedure, potentially leading to incomplete assessment and clinical misjudgment6.

In recent years, immersive Virtual Reality (VR) has emerged as a safe, non-pharmacological technique for managing pain and anxiety during medical procedures7. VR simulates an environment that immerses users to the point that they experience a genuine sense of “being there.” A growing body of evidence suggests that VR use across various clinical contexts such as minor surgical procedures, sensitive examinations, and clinical skills training—can lead to significant reductions in anxiety, perceived pain intensity, distress, and even fear and phobic reactions in patients8.

In parallel, clinical training in vaginal examination represents a fundamental and indispensable component of medical education. Without structured, healthcare would be extremely difficult, if not impossible, to find. However, evidence shows that high levels of anxiety during clinical training may negatively affect students’ learning, clinical performance, and overall competence9. Therefore, immersive VR, particularly in sensitive procedures such as vaginal examination, appears to offer a valuable opportunity not only to enhance pregnant women’s experiences but also to improve the quality and emotional safety of clinical education7.

Despite this growing evidence, the emotional burden associated with vaginal examination—particularly during labor—remains insufficiently addressed in clinical practice. Existing literature often acknowledges the discomfort associated with the procedure but provides limited guidance on targeted, evidence‑based strategies to reduce fear and anxiety in real time10,11.

Furthermore, although VR has been explored in obstetric settings such as labor pain management and prenatal anxiety reduction, its application during vaginal examination, a uniquely intimate and anxiety‑provoking procedure, has received little direct investigation. This represents a critical gap in the literature.

Clinical training adds another layer of complexity. Vaginal examination performed by students is essential for skill acquisition, yet it may heighten maternal anxiety and discomfort12. Interventions that simultaneously support patient comfort and facilitate high‑quality clinical education are therefore urgently needed13.

To address these gaps, the present study investigates whether immersive VR can effectively reduce fear, anxiety, and pain among pregnant women undergoing vaginal examination during clinical training. By focusing on this specific and sensitive context, the study aims to generate targeted evidence that can inform both intrapartum care and medical education.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was a randomized controlled trial conducted in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT 2010) guidelines. The trial registration number with the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials is IRCT20220704055367N719 2025-07-19 (https://www.irct.ir/index.php). Also, the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Iran University of Medical Sciences (ethical approval code: IR.IUMS.REC.1404.416). Written informed consent was also obtained from the participants. The research setting in Mashhad, Iran.

Participants (eligibility criteria)

The research group consisted of pregnant women who had been sent to the triage unit for a vaginal examination. The women were considered for the study if they met the following criteria: they were 18–50 years old, had at least a primary education level, gave informed consent to the vaginal examination, agreed to use a VR headset, and were capable of seeing images and hearing sounds. Women suffering from acute pelvic pain, perineal injury or wounds, or in labor emergencies were among those considered for exclusion. Moreover, those taking tranquilizers, having been diagnosed with psychotic or psychiatric disorders, vaginismus, sufferers of sexual abuse, victims of traumatic pelvic examinations, and those with any chronic illness were also excluded.

Randomization, allocation concealment, and blinding

Eligible women who signed informed consent were randomly allocated to the intervention (VR) group or the comparison group using a table of random numbers in a 1:1 ratio. For allocation concealment, participants were asked to pick one from a set of sealed, opaque envelopes that had been prepared in advance. Inside each envelope, there was a label with either “1” (VR group) or “2” (comparison group) written on it. Since the VR intervention was interactive, it was impossible to blind the participants and clinicians to group allocation. To reduce the effect of bias that may have been introduced due to a lack of blinding, the remaining procedures and data collection were carried out in the most standardized manner possible.

Study interventions

Virtual Reality (VR) Group: Before the vaginal examination, the participants in this group were instructed on the use of the VR equipment and were given five minutes to try the headset. One minute before the vaginal examination, the researcher put a VR headset on the participant’s face. Each participant chose the relaxing environment they liked most from a selection of 360 nature videos (e.g., waterfalls, seas, rivers, beaches, rain, flowers, forests) with accompanying calming sounds of nature, such as birdsong. This selection based on previous studies and on the most common preferences for relaxing in natural environments. The VR headset was the Meta Quest 3, a wireless device weighing only 520 g, with high-fidelity stereo headphones and adjustable, flexible straps. The VR experience was allowed for up to 10 min (one minute before and up to nine minutes during the examination). The maximum examination time was decided through the simulations carried out by the researcher.

Comparison Group: Participants in the comparison group also had a vaginal examination procedure, as did those in the intervention group. However, they did not use the VR headset. Instead, the same nature images (waterfall, sea, river, beach, rain, flowers, forest) were displayed for them on a monitor located in the examination room. The examination environment was kept quiet and dimly lit for both groups.

Participants were asked to turn off their mobile phones. For the sake of privacy and comfort, participants’ body clothing, which was put on a hanger, and a drape were used to cover their lower body. A drape was used to cover their lower bodies. The researcher helped each participant get into the lithotomy position on the examination bed. The researcher did the examination, and a midwifery student did the examination. The researcher supported the participant throughout the procedure and ensured that ethical standards were followed. To make the clinical teaching process more uniform and to avoid information bias, direct supervision of the student by the faculty member and pre-study training for the midwifery students were carried out. Participants in both groups -test and control- completed the post-intervention questionnaires immediately after the examination.

Outcome measures

The effect of the intervention on several outcomes was evaluated. These were anxiety, pain intensity, quality, and fear of pelvic examination. Before the intervention, a baseline demographic and obstetric characteristic questionnaire, designed by the researcher, was administered to both groups.

A demographic and obstetric characteristics checklist, developed by the researcher, was completed at baseline. The checklist requested information on the individual’s personal data (age, education, occupation, place of residence), socioeconomic status (income, insurance coverage), obstetric history (gravidity, parity, history of miscarriage, current gestational age, underlying diseases, current pregnancy complications, medication use), previous vaginal examination experience (number of prior examinations, and pain, fear, and anxiety experienced during those examinations), and knowledge of VR.

The level of previous anxiety referred to the participant’s self -reported anxiety experienced during a prior vaginal examination. This variable was assessed brief verbal interview and categorized as mild, moderate, or severe. To minimize variation in interpretation, standardized verbal anchors were provided: mild (slight worry not interfering with the procedure), moderate (noticeable discomfort but the examination was completed), and severe (marked distress leading to avoidance or interruption of the examination). The categorization was entirely based on participants’ self-reports with interviewer guidance.

using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI). It is a 40-item questionnaire that measures state anxiety in the range of items 1 to 20 and trait anxiety in the range of items 21–40. State anxiety items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale with 1 signifying “not at all” and 4 signifying “very much so”. In contrast, trait anxiety items are rated on a 4-point scale with 1 signifying “never” or " rarely” and 4 signifying “almost always”. Each subscale’s score can be between 20 and 80, with higher scores indicating a greater level of anxiety. The Persian version of the STAI has shown good validity and reliability. Cronbach’s alpha for state anxiety was 0.92 and for trait anxiety 0.9014. The STAI was conducted before and immediately after the intervention. Both groups completed the STAI twice toto determine the changes: First, before the treatment (baseline), and second, immediately after the treatment.

Pain severity was gauged through a 10 cm Visual Analog Scale (VAS), where 0 was “no pain,” and 10 was “the worst imaginable pain. " The pain was considered mild, moderate, and severe if the scores were 13, 47, and 810, respectively15. The VAS was given immediately after the treatment. Pain’s multifaceted character was evaluated from using the short-form McGill Pain Questionnaire (MPQ). This interview consists of 15 descriptive words (11 sensory and 4 affective), which are graded on a 4-point intensity scale (0 = none, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, 3 = severe), resulting in sensory, affective, and total pain rating indices (overall score range: 045). It also includes a 6-point verbal rating scale for current pain intensity (0 = no pain, 5 = unbearable). The Persian version of the MPQ has been validated and found reliable (Cronbach’s alpha = 0. 76)15,16. The MPQ was given immediately after the treatment. Pain measures were administered immediately after treatment in both groups. The STAI, VAS, and McGill Pain questionnaire were administered in a single session. The completion of these assessments took an average of 30 min. To ensure data quality and participant comfort, each questionnaire was completed individually in a calm environment, with sufficient time rushing.

Fear of pelvic examination was assessed using a checklist developed by the researchers. This checklist was initially based on the Fear of Pelvic Examination Scale (FPEX)17. One item was removed, and two were modified to align with the present study’s aim and target population. The final instrument included six items measuring feelings of fear, stress, discomfort, intrusive thoughts, and the desire to avoid the examination. The responses were recorded on a dichotomous scale for each item. To assess face validity, the checklist was initially reviewed by several pregnant women to evaluate the clarity, comprehensibility, and simplicity of the items, and necessary modifications were applied. Seven PhD in reproductive health and midwifery reviewed the checklist to determine whether the items appropriately represented the dimensions, is a measure of content validity. The checklist’s consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which was 0.91; thus, the checklist was considered to have good internal consistency. This checklist was completed immediately after the intervention for both groups.

Sample size calculation

The calculation of the sample size was based on the work by Kurt et al.1, in which the STAI score was utilized as the primary outcome. Using an effect size of 11. 02 and a standard deviation of 12.8 from that experiment, and, considering 80% power and a 5% type I error rate, the sample size for the initial experiment was estimated at 22 participants per group. However, to be on the safe side of a possible 20% attrition, the sample size for the experiment was finally increased to 35 participants per group (70 participants in total).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 27). Descriptive statistics were calculated: categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means and standard deviations. Baseline characteristics and outcome measures were compared between the two groups using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The effects of the VR intervention on anxiety and pain levels are summarized in Table 2. Regarding anxiety, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups at baseline for either state anxiety (P=0.170) or trait anxiety (P=0.089). However, post-intervention analysis using the Mann-Whitney U test revealed a significant reduction in anxiety levels in the VR group. The mean state anxiety score was significantly lower in the VR group (34.31± 9.07) compared to the control group (45.66± 13.03) (Z=-3.86,P<0.001). Similarly, trait anxiety was significantly lower in the intervention group post-procedure (P<0.001). Regarding pain assessment, participants in the VR group reported significantly lower pain intensity on the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) than controls (3.43±2.13 vs. 6.29±2.18; =−4.71, P<0.001). Consistent results were observed with the McGill Pain Questionnaire. The VR group demonstrated significantly lower scores across all pain dimensions—affective, sensory-continuous, sensory-intermittent, and sensory-neuropathic—as well as the total pain score (P<0.05 for all subscales), indicating a significant analgesic effect of the VR intervention.

As shown in Table 3, the frequency of reported negative feelings (including fear, stress, discomfort, intrusive thoughts, and the desire to avoid the examination) was significantly lower in the VR group compared to the control group across all stages of the vaginal examination (P<0.001).In the control group, the prevalence of negative feelings was extremely high, ranging from 91.4% (during light preparation) to 97.1% (during insertion and internal touch). In contrast, the VR intervention significantly reduced these rates. For instance, during the internal examination (touching the cervix), only 34.3% of the VR group reported negative feelings compared to 97.1% in the control group. Similarly, during the labia preparation stage, 71.4% of women in the VR group reported no negative sensations, whereas only 8.6% of women in the control group did. Most participants reported being highly satisfied with the VR device.

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial examined the effect of immersive VR on fear, anxiety, and pain during vaginal examination performed for clinical training. The findings demonstrate that VR significantly reduced state anxiety, pain intensity, and fear across all stages of the examination compared to standard care. These results align with previous studies showing that VR can effectively distract patients, reduce perceived pain, and alleviate anxiety during various medical procedures.

These findings are strongly consistent with a growing body of research evaluating VR in analogous gynecologic and obstetric procedures. In this line, a randomized controlled trial conducted specifically on VR during pelvic examinations and reported clear reductions in both anxiety and pain in the intervention group, mirroring the pattern observed here, even when examinations were performed by trainees rather than experienced clinicians1. Similarly, another study documented lowered state-trait anxiety and more stable vital signs when VR was used during non-stress testing in pregnant women, confirming the technology’s suitability for this emotionally and physiologically vulnerable population4. Extending these results, a recent study on VR headsets during invasive prenatal diagnostic procedures (chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis) found significantly lower anxiety and pain scores, with universal recommendation of the intervention and no adverse effects reported18.

The benefits of VR appear to extend well beyond brief examinations into the intrapartum period. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis of 12 randomized trials (N = 1095) concluded that VR significantly relieved labor pain, reduced anxiety, and improved maternal satisfaction with delivery, without adverse effects on labor duration19. A more recent 2025 systematic review synthesizing 17 studies across obstetrics and gynecology settings reported that 78% of investigations examining pain and 71% of those assessing anxiety documented statistically significant reductions with VR use; labor and various procedural contexts were the most frequently studied applications. Individual trials have reinforced these aggregate findings20. Meanwhile, a randomized study of immersive VR during the first and second stages of unmedicated labor showed effective pain and anxiety reduction with high feasibility and no major side effects21. A previous study examining the use of VR for early labor access to this technology, and 95% of patients indicated they would use VR again for future deliveries22. Qualitative data collected on participants’ experiences in early labor confirmed the general perception that VR is beneficial for pain and anxiety relief, the value of using VR for distraction and relaxation, and a collective recommendation for its use in labor23.

Despite the generally positive findings in the current literature, some studies yielded less robust or non-significant outcomes, underscoring the importance of reporting implementation factors. A Belgian pilot matched case-control study observed no statistically significant pre–post change in pain scores or reduction in epidural analgesia use during labor, possibly because of relatively short VR exposure times or less immersive content in some sessions24. The present trial’s protocol—continuous, fully immersive, calming multisensory stimulation maintained throughout the entire examination—likely maximized attentional capture and accounts for the uniformly strong effects observed. Recognizing these nuances helps situate the present findings within a more balanced evidence landscape and avoids overstating the generalizability of the results.

While the findings strongly support the effectiveness of immersive VR, they should be interpreted with appropriate caution. The magnitude of improvement observed in this study may partly reflect the controlled research environment, the novelty of VR technology, and the high level of researcher support provided during the examination. These contextual factors may not fully replicate typical clinical conditions, where staffing, time constraints, and workflow pressures differ.

Future research should explore whether the benefits of VR persist across repeated examinations, different stages of labor, and diverse clinical environments. Additionally, evaluating the cost‑effectiveness, feasibility of routine implementation, and patient preferences informing broader adoption.

Strengths and limitations

Despite some limitations, it is a research methodology. Its strengths significantly enhance the validity and usefulness of the study’s results. Notably, one of the most important strengths was the introduction of an innovative, non-pharmacological intervention—virtual reality immersion—during a potentially distressing clinical procedure. However, this study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the trial was conducted at a single tertiary hospital, which may limit generalizability to other settings with different patient populations or resource levels. Second, blinding of participants and clinicians was not possible due to the nature of the intervention, which may have introduced performance or expectancy bias. Third, although the sample size was adequate for detecting differences in primary outcomes, it was relatively small and lacked diversity in socioeconomic and cultural backgrounds. Fourth, outcomes were measured immediately after the examination, preventing assessment of longer‑term psychological effects or changes in attitudes toward future examinations. Fifth, the VR content consisted exclusively of nature‑based environments; other VR modalities, such as interactive or cognitive‑behavioral programs, were not evaluated and may produce different outcomes. Finally, the presence of a researcher during the procedure may have influenced participants’ comfort levels, potentially amplifying the observed effects.

Conclusion

Immersive VR appears to be a promising non-pharmacological intervention for reducing fear, anxiety, and pain during vaginal examination in pregnant women. The findings of this randomized controlled trial demonstrate meaningful improvements across multiple dimensions of patient experience, suggesting that VR may enhance both intrapartum care and clinical training environments.

However, these results should be interpreted as preliminary rather than definitive. The study’s single-site design, limited sample size, and short-term outcome assessment restrict the extent to which the findings can be generalized. Broader implementation will require further research across diverse populations, clinical settings, and VR modalities, as well as evaluation of feasibility, cost-effectiveness, and long-term outcomes.

Nonetheless, the present study provides an important foundation for integrating user-friendly technologies into obstetric care in a way that prioritizes patient comfort, emotional safety, and high-quality clinical education.
