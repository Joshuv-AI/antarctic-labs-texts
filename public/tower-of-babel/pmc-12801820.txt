Intracoronary infusion of autologous bone marrow mononuclear cells in patients with chronic total occlusions and left ventricular dysfunction: the BMMC/CTO trial
Authors: Luis Carlos Maestre-Luque, Rafael Gonzalez-Manzanares, Francisco Hidalgo, Javier Suárez de Lezo, Miguel Romero, Alejandro Gutiérrez-Barrios, Ignacio Gallo, Concepción Herrera, Simona Espejo-Perez, Olga Fernández-López, Rosario Mata Alcázar-Caballero, Soledad Ojeda, Manuel Pan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere is controversy about the benefits of bone marrow mononuclear cells (BMMC) to improve left ventricular dysfunction (LVD) in patients with coronary artery disease. We sought to evaluate the safety, feasibility and efficacy of the intracoronary infusion of BMMC to improve left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) in patients with chronic total coronary artery occlusions (CTO).MethodsProspective, randomized, open-label and controlled study. Patients with a percutaneous revascularization of CTO (CTO-PCI) three months before and persistent LVD (LVEF < 45%) were included and randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive intracoronary infusion of autologous BMMC plus conventional medical treatment or conventional treatment alone (control group). The primary efficacy endpoint was the absolute change in LVEF by cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) at 6-month follow-up. The functional capacity status by NYHA class, the overall mortality and the adverse cardiac events rate were also evaluated in the 24-month follow-up period.ResultsTwenty-eight patients were included, 13 received intracoronary infusion of BMMC and 15 received standard care. No major procedural complications occurred during BMMC infusion, and the rates of overall mortality and adverse cardiac events in follow-up were similar. At 6-month follow-up, the absolute change in LVEF was greater in BMMC group, without reaching statistical significance [∆ LVEF 0% ( − 5 to 0) in the control group vs. 2.5% ( − 2.7 to 4) in the BMMC group, median between-groups difference 2.5% ( − 2 to 4.5), p = 0.118)]. The change in NYHA class did not differ between treatment arms at any stage of follow-up.ConclusionsThe intracoronary infusion of BMMC in patients with previous CTO-PCI and persistent LVD seemed to be safe and feasible. There was a numerical but not statistically significant difference in terms of LVEF recovery favouring the BMMC group. The study offers a novel design for future larger trials to clarify the impact of BMMC therapy in patients with CTO.Trial registration The trial was registered in the European Union Drug Regulating Authorities Clinical Trials Database (EudraCT number: 2013-000915-26).

Background

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is the main cause of mortality and loss of quality of life worldwide [1]. A frequent condition in patients with obstructive CAD is the development of left ventricular dysfunction (LVD), and chronic total coronary artery occlusions (CTO) can reach a prevalence of up to 45% in this setting, conferring a worse prognosis [2]. There is current controversy about the benefit of percutaneous coronary intervention of CTO (CTO-PCI) in patients with LVD, since some randomized controlled trials (RCTs) showed no effect on left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) recovery [3].

Bone marrow mononuclear cells (BMMC) comprise a heterogeneous population of single nucleus cells including monocytes, lymphocytes and hematopoietic stem cells. BMMC can migrate across the coronary endothelium, and their homing to ischemic myocardium is largely driven by the C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4)/ stromal cell derived factor 1 alpha (SDF-1α) axis [4, 5]. Once delivered into the damaged tissue, BMMC may exert paracrine effects that promote angiogenesis, modulate inflammation, and support myocardial repair [6].

The intracoronary infusion of BMMC has been validated in observational studies and RCTs carried out in patients with acute and chronic CAD, reporting a good safety profile and a potential for LVEF recovery [7, 8]. In the subgroup of patients with CTO, some RCTs showed improvement of LVEF with direct intramyocardial injection of circulating blood derived progenitor cells [9, 10]. However, there are no RCTs evaluating the intracoronary delivery of BMMC in this population.

This study sought to evaluate the safety, feasibility and efficacy of the intracoronary infusion of autologous BMMC in patients with a previous revascularized CTO and persistent LVD.

Methods

Trial design and oversight

The BMMC/CTO trial is a prospective, randomized, open-label and controlled study conducted at Reina Sofía University Hospital (Córdoba, Spain) and Puerta del Mar University Hospital (Cádiz, Spain). Patient enrolment started in November 2013 and finalized in July 2021. An independent data and safety monitoring committee, designated by the Promotor of the study, the Andalusian Network for the design and translation of Advanced Therapies, oversaw the trial. An interim analysis was pre-specified once approximately half of the planned sample size had been reached, in order to monitor safety and efficacy signals. Although no safety signal was found and no formal stopping criteria were defined, the independent Data and Safety Monitoring Board recommended discontinuation of the trial due to the markedly slow recruitment rate, since a more prolonged recruitment period could introduce variability related to evolving clinical practices and therapeutic standards. Data was anonymized, and confidentiality was preserved, in accordance with the European Union Regulation 2016/679—Protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation).

Patient population

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) written informed consent to participate in the study; (ii) patient aged 18–80 years; (iii) presence of at least one completely recanalized CTO; (iv) LVEF < 45% determined by cardiac ventriculography (including left and right projections) during the revascularization procedure; (v) persistent LVEF < 45% determined by cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) three months after CTO-PCI; (vi) serum creatinine values under 2.5 mg/dL; (vii) normal hemogram and coagulation profile, defined as leukocytes ≥ 3000 cells/μl, neutrophils ≥ 1500 cells/μl, platelets ≥ 100,000 cells/μl and haemoglobin ≥ 10 gr/dL; (viii) negative pregnancy test for women in fertile age; (ix) patients’ acceptance to use safe contraceptive methods during the follow-up period (both men and women). The presence of myocardial viability was not considered an inclusion criterion. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) presence of at least one incomplete revascularized CTO; (ii) ventricular function improvement resulting in LVEF ≥ 45% three months after CTO-PCI; (iii) positive serology for Human Immunodeficiency Virus, Hepatitis B virus or Hepatitis C virus; (iv) contraindications to dual antiplatelet therapy after CTO-PCI; (v) hematologic disorders; (vi) malignant tumours; (vii) presence of devices that are not compatible with CMR; (viii) pregnancy or refuse to use safe contraceptive methods (both men and women).

Randomization

After enrolment, patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive intracoronary infusion of BMMC plus conventional medical treatment (BMMC group) or conventional medical treatment alone (control group). Randomization was performed using a centralized, computer-generated sequence via a secure web-based system, ensuring allocation concealment. The Promotor was responsible for the randomization procedure.

Procedures and medications

Bone marrow cells preparation

Four hours before infusion, approximately 100 to 300 ml of autologous bone marrow were aspired from posterior iliac crest using standard techniques. The aspiration product was processed with a closed, automated cell processing system (SEPAX, Biosafe SA, Geneva, Switzerland). BMMC were isolated by density gradient centrifugation using a Ficoll-Paque media adjusted to a density of 1077 g/ml. After that, BMMC were washed twice in a solution of human serum albumin 4% to remove the Ficoll media. The final product obtained was a 20 ml Lactate Ringer solution (2.5% of glucose and 1% of human serum albumin) containing a dose of 500 to 1000 × 106 BMMC/ml. In order to ensure that a minimum amount of 500 × 106 BMMC/ml was reached, samples for cellular recounting were harvested from every cellular concentrate before the infusion. In addition, cellular composition (by flow cytometry), microbiologic analysis and viability testing were performed.

Bone marrow cells infusion

Once the final product was ready, it was transferred to the catheterization laboratory and immediately administered to the patient via intracoronary infusion through the previously revascularized CTO artery, using a “stop-slow” procedure. Heparin (100 units/Kg) was initially given intravenous, and the dose was subsequently adjusted as necessary to maintain the activated clotting time at 200–250 s. Radial access preferentially, or femoral access, were used to introduce a 6-French diagnostic catheter. First, a coronary angiogram was performed to check the persistence of good results in the recanalized artery and to evaluate progression of coronary disease in other locations. After that, the diagnostic catheter was exchanged for a 6-French therapeutic catheter, and a coronary guidewire was passed through the target coronary artery. An over-the-wire balloon dilatation catheter was used to inflate a semi-compliant balloon in the proximal region of the artery, remove the coronary guidewire and dispense the BMMC concentrate with a velocity of 0.5–1 ml/min.

Conventional medical treatment

The standard of care for LVD consisted of beta-blockers (BB), mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRA), renin angiotensin system inhibitors (RASi) and the angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI) Sacubitril/Valsartan. Diuretics were used in case of congestive symptoms. To prevent in-stent thrombosis, patients received dual antiplatelet therapy for 6 to 12 months after CTO-PCI and continued with indefinite maintenance of aspirin. Lipid lowering drugs were used to maintain low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels below 70 mg/dL. BB, calcium-channel blockers and nitrates were employed as anti-anginal drugs if necessary.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging

The CMR was performed at baseline (three months after CTO-PCI) and six months after BMMC administration using a 1.5-T scanner (Aera Magnetom; Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). The standard protocol included left ventricular long-axis, short-axis and three-chamber cine sequences, T1 and T2 mapping, and late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). Measurement of left ventricular volume and calculation of LVEF was based on previously validated techniques [11]. All image data were analysed with the post-processing tool Syngo.via (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany).

Definitions

CTO was defined as angiographically proven anterograde flow obstruction of a coronary artery, known, or suspected to have lasted > 3 months [with Thrombolysis In Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) flow = 0] [12], based on the patient’s history. Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) was defined following the universal definition endorsed by the European Society of Cardiology [13]. LVD was defined as a LVEF < 45%, irrespective of the imaging modality used (biplane ventriculography at the time of CTO-PCI or cardiac magnetic resonance imaging at the 3-month evaluation). This threshold holds prognostic relevance and is in accordance with international guidelines on heart failure (HF) and LVD [14, 15]. Clinically relevant improvement of left ventricular function after BMMC infusion was defined as an increase in at least 5% of LVEF in absolute terms. Worsening HF was defined as the need for increasing diuretic dose or hospitalization for intravenous therapy.

Endpoints and follow up

The primary endpoint was absolute change in LVEF. The secondary endpoints were change in New York Heart Association (NYHA) class, overall mortality and adverse cardiac events (AMI, unplanned revascularization, atrial fibrillation, ventricular ectopic beats, worsening HF). The absolute change in LVEF and the change in NYHA class were considered efficacy endpoints. Change in LVEF was evaluated by CMR at baseline and 6 months after BMMC infusion. This timeframe has been identified as sufficient to detect meaningful improvement after stem cell’s therapy [8, 16]. Change in NYHA class was evaluated at baseline and at 6, 12, 18 and 24 months after BMMC infusion. Overall mortality and adverse cardiac events were considered safety endpoints and were evaluated during the entire follow-up period of 24 months.

Statistical analysis

The sample size calculation was based on the results of a previous observational study carried on at Reina Sofía University Hospital (Córdoba), in which the infusion of BMMC to 19 patients with chronic anterior myocardial infarction and severely depressed ventricular function was safe and feasible, and yielded an average net gain of 5% in LVEF [7]. A sample size of 30 patients per group was estimated to provide 80% power to detect a significant treatment effect, assuming a between-group difference of 5% in LVEF in absolute terms and a two-sided alpha level of 0.05, with a 10% loss to follow-up. It was assumed that control group would not improve LVEF during follow-up period, because the theorical effect of revascularization on LVEF would be expected to be captured prior to inclusion through a protocol-mandated CMR performed three months after CTO-PCI.

Data were expressed as absolute and percentage in the case of qualitative variables. Quantitative variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median and interquartile range, depending on the distribution. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q–Q plots. Between-group comparisons were performed using the Student’s t-test or its nonparametric equivalent, the Mann–Whitney U-test, for continuous variables, and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. All tests were two tailed and were considered significant when P < 0.05. The between-group median difference in the absolute change in LVEF was estimated using the Hodges-Lehmann method. We conducted an intention-to-treat analysis, whereby all randomized patients were analysed according to their originally assigned treatment arm, regardless of protocol adherence, protocol deviations or treatment received. Statistical analyses were performed using performed using R (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Safety endpoints

No major procedural complications occurred and no significant ST-segment changes or ventricular arrythmias were detected during BMMC delivery. Transient chest discomfort was reported in 5 patients but in all cases, symptoms resolved immediately after briefly pausing the infusion. Two patients in the control group died in the follow-up period, one of them suffered an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest 374 days after inclusion, and the other died from terminal heart failure 409 days after inclusion. There were no statistical differences in mortality between groups (p = 0.481). In relation to adverse cardiac events, 2 (15.4%) patients in the BMMC group and 3 patients (20%) in the control group had an episode of worsening HF (P = 0.505), 1 (6.6%) patient in the control group and none in BMMC group had a MI (p > 0.999), and 2 (13.2%) patients in the control group and none in BMMC group underwent unplanned revascularization due to persistent angina (p = 0.497). None of the reported adverse cardiac events were related to BMMC infusion.

Discussion

In this randomized controlled study, intracoronary infusion of BMMC in patients with persistent LVD following successful CTO-PCI seemed to be feasible and safe. A trend towards greater improvement in LVEF was observed in the BMMC group at six months, without reaching statistical significance. Given the premature discontinuation of the study and the reduced sample size, these findings should be taken cautiously.

Our safety results are consistent with previous RCTs evaluating intracoronary BMMC administration. Similar to the findings from Wollert et al. [8], in patients with recently revascularized AMI, and Hu et al. [17], in individuals with chronic ischemic LVD, we observed no procedure-related complications and comparable rates of major adverse cardiovascular events between treatment arms. In our study, this favourable safety profile was maintained throughout 24 months of follow-up.

Concerning efficacy, the limited sample size might be a primary contributor to the lack of statistical significance. However, other factors should be analysed, including the enrichment strategy targeting patients with persistent LVD, the inherent biological constraints of BMMC, and the absence of synergy with concurrent coronary revascularization.

The enrolment criteria prioritized the established prognostic thresholds of LVD (LVEF < 45%) [14, 15], rather than the magnitude of functional recovery after CTO-PCI. While this strategy ensured inclusion of a clinically high-risk population with theoretical larger potential for benefit, the presence of mixed myocardial viability patterns may have attenuated the magnitude of the treatment effect, since non-viable tissue is less amenable to functional recovery following cell’s therapy. As reported in other RCTs in patients with cardiovascular disease, enrichment criteria to select high-risk patients can either enhance the chance of improvement [18–20] or, conversely, limit the effect size of the therapy [21].

Additionally, the specific cell phenotype employed in this study might have influenced the LVEF recovery. Although BMMC can migrate into the ischemic myocardium and promote tissue repair, they do not differentiate into cardiomyocytes. Mesenchymal stromal cells, conversely, have demonstrated transdifferentiating potential [22], what could involve superior regenerative performance. In the MSC-HF trial, the intramyocardial injection of bone-marrow derived mesenchymal stromal cells improved both LVEF and scar burden in patients with ischemic LVD [23]. Moreover, only 23.4% of BMMC in our study showed specific migration to SDF-1α, which may have limited cell homing and myocardial retention. Some RCTs with higher migratory potential have yielded more favourable outcomes [9], suggesting that future research should focus on improving cell selection and enhancing homing properties.

It is also notable that several positive RCTs reporting LVEF recovery combined BMMC therapy with simultaneous coronary revascularization [24–26], complicating the attribution of benefit to the cellular product alone. Our trial was specifically designed to isolate the independent effect of BMMC therapy by setting a 3-month interval between CTO-PCI and the baseline CMR that was used to determine eligibility based on LVEF. However, this may have reduced the likelihood of exploiting potential synergistic effects between myocardial reperfusion and cell-mediated repair.

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the intended sample size was not achieved, resulting in reduced statistical power for the detection of an efficacy signal. This was mainly driven by the slower than anticipated recruitment rate, which was compounded by the unexpectedly high proportion of patients who experienced improvement in LVEF after CTO-PCI. Second, the open label design and the absence of a sham control procedure could have introduced potential bias. However, the use of an objective and reproducible efficacy endpoint (LVEF by CMR) might have attenuated this concern. Third, the lack of pre-PCI CMR imaging is a major limitation for the comprehensive evaluation of myocardial viability and the comparison of functional changes over time, including quantitative wall thickness evaluation and advanced segmental function analysis. Nonetheless, LVEF was consistently measured using standardized CMR protocols before and after BMMC infusion, ensuring consistency in global left ventricular function measurement, which holds stronger prognostic relevance than segmental indices. Lastly, the restrictive enrichment design may limit the generalizability of our findings to broader CTO populations.

Conclusion

In this RCT the intracoronary infusion of BMMC in patients with persistent LVD after CTO-PCI appeared to be feasible and safe. There was a numerical but not statistically significant difference in LVEF improvement favouring BMMC group, with the small sample size posing a major barrier on detecting an efficacy signal. The study offers a novel design for future larger trials to clarify the impact of BMMC therapy in patients with CTO.
