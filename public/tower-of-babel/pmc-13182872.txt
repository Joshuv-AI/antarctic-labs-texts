Identifying Donor Immune Cells in the Intestinal Mucosa of Chimeric Recipient Mice by Immunofluorescence
Authors: Vinicius Canale, Samuel Asiedu, Fuad Malki, Gang Chen, Meera G Nair, Declan F McCole
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMurine competitive congenic bone marrow transplantation is an important method used to identify genetic contributions to immune cell abundance, activation, differentiation, and mobilization, either in the bone marrow or in the peripheral organs of the host. This enables analysis of interactions between cells from both donors in relation to each other and the host itself. Flow cytometry is the predominant approach for experimental designs such as these for its powerful quantitative capacity and ability to identify rare and complex immune cells using cell surface markers. However, information regarding immune cell localization and interaction in peripheral organs is usually unexplored due to technical difficulties in identifying immune cells through immunofluorescence, as tissue fixation results in protein cross‐linking and inadvertent masking of protein epitopes. In addition, commercially available antibodies validated for flow cytometry are generally designed to interact with integral epitopes expressed on the outer cell membrane. This feature renders conventional immunofluorescence using formalin‐fixed tissues impractical because antigen retrieval techniques result in breakage of cross‐linked proteins by the denaturation process. Here, we have adapted and improved routinely used immunofluorescence protocols to enable identification of CD45+ immune cells and their specific isoforms in the intestinal tissue of recipient mice, permitting tracking of the immune cell origins and localization within peripheral organs. This is achieved by immediately freezing intestinal specimens in OCT and performing methanol fixation only after tissue sectioning, as opposed to formalin‐based fixation immediately post–tissue harvest as in conventional immunofluorescence protocols, allowing us to identify immune cell markers using antibodies validated for flow cytometry. This technique adds a new layer of information that allows characterization of how immune cells interact with one another in situ in a mouse model of competitive congenic bone marrow transplant. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor type C, encoded by the Ptprc gene, also known as cluster of differentiation 45 (CD45), is generally expressed on the cell surface of all hematopoietic cells, except for erythrocytes and platelets (Jafri et al., 2017). Allelic variants of CD45 have been identified in C57BL/6 mice, with the common isoform CD45.2 expressed by the Ptprcb allele. An additional allelic variant, Ptprca , which translates into the CD45.1 isoform, was identified in SJL mice. Since the Ptprca allele was successfully backcrossed onto the BL/6 background, it has been extensively used as a tool for competitive congenic bone marrow transplant (BMT), enabling tracking and quantification of hematopoietic cells from the donors in the recipient mouse (Ryan et al., 2024). The engrafted hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) can be tracked at different time points in the peripheral blood, bone marrow, and/or peripheral organs of the recipient mouse. Flow cytometry analysis is the gold standard for cell quantification examining the contribution of the donor, competitor, and recipient cells in multiple organs of a single organism, thereby enabling assessment of dynamic genetic contributions to immune cell differentiation, activation, and mobilization.

The CD45.1 and CD45.2 isoforms differ by only five amino acids within the extracellular domain of the leukocyte common antigen (LCA), which is expressed on the cell surface of immune cells. This is sufficient to permit specific recognition of each isoform by monoclonal antibodies (Zebedee et al., 1991). Generally, antibodies designed for flow cytometry analysis rely on recognition of integral epitopes expressed on the outer cell membrane of single cells, as opposed to antibodies designed for western blotting and immunohistochemistry that detect linear epitopes in cell lysates under denaturing conditions. In fact, most murine chimeric studies extensively use flow cytometry analysis as the main, or sole, technique in characterizing cell population proportions in the host. Thus, there is a gap in our understanding of how immune cells from different donors can interact with each other and, importantly where they localize in the peripheral organs of the host. This is due to technical difficulties regarding availability of commercially available antibodies that detect the CD45 isoforms under denaturing conditions, for example, for immunohistochemistry.

Given that the goal of competitive congenic BMT studies is to identify the genetic contribution to immune cell responses, we designed an experimental setup where the Ptpn2 gene was deleted in myeloid cells by crossing transgenic mice, in which critical exons in the Ptpn2 gene locus were flanked by loxP sites, with another mouse line expressing the Cre recombinase enzyme under the Lyz2 (LyzMCre) promoter, as described elsewhere (Spalinger et al., 2020). Briefly, PTPN2 loss‐of‐function mutations have been found in association with several chronic inflammatory conditions, including both subtypes of inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis), type 1 diabetes, and rheumatoid arthritis (Hsieh et al., 2020; Song et al., 2022; Thompson et al., 2010; Yilmaz et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2014). Hence, the investigation of PTPN2 gene function in myeloid cells is clinically relevant and should be further explored. Finally, these mice were further crossed so that each genotype expresses a specific CD45 isoform, thus enabling identification and tracking of the cells’ origins/genotype.

We successfully tested and validated the flow cytometry monoclonal antibodies anti‐CD45.1 and anti‐CD45.2 for immunofluorescence to detect these isoforms in the spleen and intestine of recipient mice after successful competitive congenic BMT, enabling investigation of the genetic contribution of each subject (i.e., donor 1 vs. donor 2 vs. recipient) in peripheral organs. Therefore, coupled with flow cytometry analysis, we were able to examine immune cell proportions from each donor and host and how these different populations of cells interact with each other in situ, uncovering another layer of information. Basic Protocol 2 describes how to perform image analysis of RGB (red, green, and blue) photo format when the resulting experimental images contain four colors, as is the case in this experimental design, detecting yellow/orange for immune cells positive for both the CD45.1 and CD45.2 isoforms. This set of protocols outlines common laboratory procedures, including adapted tissue harvesting, adjusted immunofluorescence analysis, and imaging processing for identification of each CD45 isoform in individual images after channels/colors are split. However, the specific methodologies for the microscopy imaging acquisition, flow cytometry used for validation, and, most importantly, the animal husbandry design for the generation of chimeric mice expressing Ptpn2 fl/fl‐CD45.1 (WT donor) vs. Ptpn2 ΔMϕ‐CD45.1/2 (KO donor) vs. Ptpn2 ΔMϕ‐CD45.2 (KO recipient), in addition to the BMT protocol, are not detailed here. The primary objective of this set of protocols is to enable other researchers to evaluate the localization and interaction of immune cells from the donors in the host through immunofluorescence imaging.

STRATEGIC PLANNING

To ensure accuracy, all images intended for analysis must have comparable attributes, such as dimensions, light intensity, magnification, and settings within the acquisition mode on the microscope/confocal software (e.g., exposure, gain, power). These attributes do not need to be the same for each channel/color (intended for a specific antibody) but must be consistent between samples. Additionally, the images must contain a scale bar for calibration purposes if distance measurements are needed. Lastly, a limitation for a study such as this is the image format in which the pictures are saved. Generally, the manufacturer of the microscope and acquisition system develops their own software and image format that are compatible with their system. Although Fiji software (ImageJ) accepts a multitude of image formats, images are typically exported and saved in tagged image file format (TIFF) for accessibility in popular photo software while retaining the quality and detailed metadata of each individual image. However, the limitation in TIFF image analysis with several channels or colors is the color format into which the TIFF files are translated: red, green, and blue (RGB) or cyan, magenta, yellow, and key (black) (CMYK). Hence, an attempt to split an image with secondary colors in a TIFF file, such as orange in this case, will generate two individual channels with a spillover signal, with orange colors shown in both the red and blue channels; this makes it impossible to analyze each color separately. Therefore, the fluorophore choices and/or image format for downstream analysis must be carefully considered. The approach we took to circumvent this problem was using the colocalization tool in Fiji software. The plugin highlights the colocalized points of two 8‐bit images (or stacks). The two images (or stacks) will be affected by the red and green channels of an RGB image. The colocalized points will appear white by default (display value = 255). The plugin initially generates an 8‐bit image with only the colocalized points, and then it combines the three 8‐bit images in an RGB image. Two points are considered as colocalized if their respective intensities are strictly higher than the thresholds of their channels [which are 50 by default: threshold channel 1 (0‐255)] and if their ratio (of intensity) is strictly higher than the ratio setting value [which is 50% by default: ratio (0%–100%)].

For the work described here, all animal experiments were performed according to, and approved by, the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of California Riverside, under protocol #48. Mouse colonies were held in a specific pathogen–free facility and were given free access to food and water. For the Ptpn2 ΔMϕ mouse line, lacking Ptpn2 expression in myeloid cells, mice with Ptpn2 flanked by loxP sites (Ptpn2 fl/fl) were bred with heterozygous mice expressing the recombinase enzyme Cre under the Lysozyme2 promoter (LysMCre) to generate Ptpn2 fl/fl controls and Ptpn2/LysMCre mice (Ptpn2 ΔMϕ, for simplification), as previously described (Spalinger et al., 2022). Mice expressing the CD45.1 isoform on a C57BL/6 background were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. CD45.1 and CD45.2 Ptpn2 ΔMϕ were bred in‐house to generate CD45.1/2‐Ptpn2 ΔMϕ. Figure 2A shows a simplified schematic of how chimeric mice were generated, and detailed protocols have been published elsewhere (Aparicio‐Vergara et al., 2010; Mercier et al., 2016; Montecino‐Rodriguez & Dorshkind, 2020). CD45.2 recipient mice were treated with 125 mg/kg busulfan intraperitoneally for five consecutive days to deplete the bone marrow myeloid stem cells in preparation for BMT (Montecino‐Rodriguez & Dorshkind, 2020). Briefly, busulfan is a chemotherapy drug used in the treatment of chronic myeloid leukemia that acts by inhibiting DNA replication, resulting in severe myelosuppression. Busulfan administration prepares the recipient mouse to receive a transplant of HSCs from the donor mice. An equal mix of bone marrow cells from CD45.1‐Ptpn2 fl/fl mice (WT donor) and CD45.1/2‐Ptpn2 ΔMϕ mice (KO donor) was injected into recipient mice for the BMT. Six weeks after BMT was performed, single‐cell suspensions were isolated from the bone marrow and large intestine of recipient mice and stained for flow cytometry to validate the efficiency of BMT. To assess cell viability, cells were stained with Zombie Aqua (BioLegend, cat. #423102) and incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Fc receptors were then blocked by incubating the cell suspension with anti‐CD16/32 (BioLegend, cat. #101302, 1 µg per 106 cells) for 5 min on ice. Following the Fc block, cells were stained with the surface antibodies APC‐CD45.1 (clone A20) and BV711‐CD45.2 (clone 104) in staining buffer [phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) containing 2% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS)] for 25 min at 4°C in the dark. Cells were washed twice with staining buffer and analyzed using the Novocyte Quanteon flow cytometer. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo v10 (Tree Star). The gating strategy is demonstrated in Figure 2B, showing clear, distinct populations of recipient immune cells (CD45.2), KO donor cells (CD45.1/2), and WT donor cells (CD45.1).

IMMUNOFLUORESCENCE OF CD45 ISOFORMS IN FRESH‐FROZEN COLON TISSUE USING FLOW CYTOMETRY–VALIDATED ANTIBODIES

To ensure that commercially available antibodies originally designed for flow cytometry can specifically bind to CD45.1 and CD45.2 in fresh tissue, we first performed immunofluorescence in the spleen of mice expressing CD45.1 or CD45.2, showing that the epitopes of CD45 isoforms retain antigenicity in situ (Figure 3A). Additionally, the same antibodies were able to identify an abundant number of immune cells in the intestinal mucosa of the same mice (Figures 3B and 3C). Then, using a CD45‐heterozygous mouse where immune cells express both the CD45.1 and CD45.2 isoforms, we were able to visualize nearly 100% coverage of both isoforms, CD45.1 (green), and CD45.2 (red), resulting in a broad orange hue (CD45.1 + CD45.2) in the merged image (Figure 3D). Figure 3E shows the specificity of each antibody because there was no detection of immune cells in the intestine of CD45.1 mice when stained for CD45.2, and vice versa.

PROCESSING RGB IMAGES TO SPECIFICALLY DETECT EACH CELL TYPE FOR DOWNSTREAM ANALYSIS USING Fiji SOFTWARE

The immunofluorescence experimental design used here (Basic Protocol 1) results in four distinct colors: blue for cell nucleus (DAPI), green for CD45.1+ cells (WT donor), red for CD45.2+ cells (recipient cells), and orange/yellow for CD45.1/2+ cells (KO donor cells). Given that RGB files are color limiting, when attempting to analyze more than three colors individually, the following protocol provides an efficient strategy to circumvent this obstacle.

COMMENTARY

Critical Parameters

Note that both primary antibodies used in this work (Basic Protocol 1) are fluorophore conjugated and raised in mice, simplifying the identification of protein targets in a single‐step labeling approach (no secondary antibody needed). Because our animal model for this experimental design is the mouse, using a two‐step identification approach is strongly discouraged; relying on secondary antibodies that bind to murine IgG molecules results in elevated background staining and loss of specificity toward the intended target. Additionally, most commercially available antibodies designed for flow cytometry experiments are raised in mice; hence, careful experimental design is recommended to utilize the correct animal models, appropriate available antibodies, and appropriate strategy for labeling the protein targets of interest.

Understanding Results

Tissue processing is a crucial step for immunohistochemistry. To preserve the morphological architecture of various tissues, adequate fixation is essential. Formaldehyde is the most popular fixative, although there is a wide range of fixatives that can be used to robustly prevent tissue decay and keep cells and tissues very close to their natural state during the specimen processing. Formaldehyde‐based fixatives accomplish tissue preservation by creating chemical bonds between proteins, anchoring soluble proteins to the cytoskeleton, and increasing the rigidity of the tissue (McFadden et al., 2019). The common complication of fixation methods is precisely the protein cross‐linking that occurs, masking protein epitopes and preventing antibody detection (Howat & Wilson, 2014). Antigen retrieval techniques can circumvent this problem by breaking the cross‐links and exposing the protein epitopes to restore immunoreactivity (Janardhan et al., 2018; Shi et al., 2001). However, most antigen retrieval protocols induce protein denaturation, which is a positive feature when using conventional primary antibodies designed to interact with linear epitopes, such as for western blotting, as opposed to using antibodies designed to detect integral proteins expressed on the outer surface of the cell membrane, as for flow cytometry experiments. Thus, to ensure protein‐antibody immunoreactivity, working with unfixed frozen tissue is an alternative to avoid suboptimal morphology.

Both anti‐CD45 antibodies used in this work (Basic Protocol 1) have been previously reported to work for immunohistochemistry in fresh tissues using cryosections. However, they were not used simultaneously, which would generate information on their localization and interaction in situ. Additionally, in both prior studies, the sections were fixed with acetone prior to staining (Chen et al., 2005; Suzuki et al., 2000), but in our hands, this method did not yield satisfactory results (data not shown). Here, we showed that post‐sectioning fixation with methanol was able to preserve the tissue architecture without affecting the antigenicity of the epitopes, enabling identification of CD45 isoforms according to their source of origin after BMT. An elegant alternative approach for this study design would be to use reporter mice expressing CD45 isoforms with a fluorescent tag, such as GFP or YFP, facilitating the identification of the CD45 isoforms for both flow cytometry and immunofluorescence techniques. However, such an approach would require extensive and careful husbandry design, complicated genotyping, and consideration of the lengthy protocol required to properly establish expression for both reporter cells and the genetic contribution of interest when working transgenic mice, such as in this study. Nevertheless, the approach described here is a feasible alternative when not employing fluorescent‐tagged CD45 isotypes that successfully demonstrates translocation of donor immune cells into the peripheral tissue of the recipient mouse to unequivocally show that all three isoforms are present in the intestinal mucosa.

It is important to note that we still observe a large proportion of the recipient mouse immune cells (CD45.2 in red; Figure 4), as expected, given that busulfan administration is intended to eliminate the HSCs in the bone marrow, but not immune cells present in peripheral tissues of the recipient mouse. This is in agreement with our flow cytometry data investigating the role of Ptpn2 gene activity regarding differentiation and localization of myeloid cells (in press).

Time Considerations

Basic Protocol 1

For experienced researchers, this approach is quite straightforward. Tissue harvest, tissue sectioning, and tissue staining can be accomplished in three consecutive days if so desired. However, fresh‐frozen tissues as well as the slides with tissue sections can be safely stored in a −80°C freezer for several months, permitting for staggering of sample harvest and sectioning prior to tissue staining and image acquisition.

Basic Protocol 2

The duration of this protocol depends on the number of images acquired on the microscope and the number of experimental conditions. It could take a couple of weeks until all images are appropriately processed, labeled, and saved prior to cell or signal quantification, which could extend for a few more days.
