Symmetric Monoidal Bicategories and Biextensions
Ettore Aldrovandi, Milind Gunjal, Department of Mathematics, Florida State University, Tallahassee, FL 32306-4510

Abstract
We study monoidal 2-categories and bicategories in terms of categorical extensions and the cohomological data they determine in appropriate cohomology theories with coefficients in Picard groupoids. In particular, we analyze the hierarchy of possible commutativity conditions in terms of progressive stabilization of these data. We also show that monoidal structures on bicategories give rise to biextensions of a pair of (abelian) groups by a Picard groupoid, and that the progressive vanishing of obstructions determined by the tower of commutative structures corresponds to appropriate symmetry conditions on these biextensions. In the fully symmetric case, which leads us fully into the stable range, we show how our computations can be expressed in terms of the cubical Q Q -construction underlying MacLane (co)homology.

Introduction

It is well known (see, e.g.
[
Bre99
]
,
[
BC97
]
) that monoidal categories can be analyzed using categorical extensions. If
{E}
is a monoidal category, in fact a monoidal group-like groupoid, we denote by
B=\pi_{0}({E})
and
A=\pi_{1}({E})
(or simply

and

if

is clear from the context) its group of isomorphism classes of objects and the automorphism group of the unit object, respectively. Then

can be written as an extension of
B
group by the Picard groupoid associated to the suspension
\Sigma A=[A\to 0]
. The resulting invariant is a class in
H^{2}(B,\Sigma A)=H^{3}(B,A)=H^{3}(K(B,1),A)
. If
{E}
is braided (resp. symmetric), so that
B
is necessarily abelian and has a trivial
A
-module structure, it is also well known that the corresponding invariant lives the higher Eilenberg-MacLane cohomology groups
H^{4}(K(B,2),A)
(resp.
H^{5}(K(B,3),A)
). In the latter—symmetric—case, we refer to the invariant as the
k
-invariant of the extension.

Breen put forward an alternative approach to studying monoidal categories by considering their commutator
[
Bre99
]
. If
{E}
is as above, with
A
and
B
abelian and
B
acting trivially on
A
, its commutator is an
A
-torsor

over
B\times B
whose fiber

over

is the set of all arrows
YX\to XY
, where
X,Y
are objects of
{E}
representing the isomorphism classes

and
y
, respectively. Remarkably,

carries two compatible partial composition laws that give it the structure of a (weak) biextension. This is, in a somewhat imprecise but suggestive manner, an extension of
B
by
A
with respect to each of the two variables while keeping the other fixed
[
Gro72
,
Mum68
]
. To give an idea, if

,
f^{\prime}\colon YX^{\prime}\to X^{\prime}Y
, and
g\colon Y^{\prime}X\to XY^{\prime}
, we define
f+_{1}f^{\prime}
and
f+_{2}g
as the composites:

\displaystyle Y(XX^{\prime})\longrightarrow(YX)X^{\prime}\xrightarrow{Xf}(XY)X^{\prime}\longrightarrow X(YX^{\prime})\xrightarrow{Xf^{\prime}}X(X^{\prime}Y)\longrightarrow(XX^{\prime})Y

and

\displaystyle(YY^{\prime})X\longrightarrow Y(Y^{\prime}X)\xrightarrow{Yg}Y(XY^{\prime})\longrightarrow(YX)Y^{\prime}\xrightarrow{fY^{\prime}}(XY)Y^{\prime}\longrightarrow X(YY^{\prime}),

where the unnamed arrows are the associator isomorphisms. The “weak” attribute refers to the fact that, for a general monoidal category, the composition laws may not be commutative, even though
A
and
B
are, analogously to what happens in ordinary central extensions. The two composition laws are compatible by way of an “interchange law” that we will full explain below, as it plays a crucial role in our work, but for now let us note that, if

, we have the identity

(f+_{1}f^{\prime})+_{2}(g+_{1}g^{\prime})=(f+_{2}g)+_{1}(f^{\prime}+_{2}g^{\prime})

expressing the compatibility between the two partial composition laws.

Analyzing the biextension associated to a monoidal category
{E}
allows to directly characterize the conditions under which it admits a symmetric structure. To briefly explain this point, let us recall that a biextension
E\to B\times B
is
anti-symmetric
if the symmetric biextension
E\wedge\sigma^{*}E
is trivial as a symmetric biextension, where
\sigma\colon B\times B\to B\times B
is the permutation map. If
\Delta\colon B\to B\times B
is the diagonal, consider the
A
-torsor
\Delta^{*}E
, which is an extension of
B
by
A
. Its square
(\Delta^{*}E)^{2}
is canonically split if
E
is anti-symmetric, and we further say that
E
is
alternating
if
\Delta^{*}E
itself is split, in a manner compatible with the canonical splitting of
(\Delta^{*}E)^{2}
.

One of the fundamental results in
[
Bre99
]
is that the monoidal structure on
{E}
is symmetric precisely when the associated biextension given by the commutator map is alternating
and
the underlying biextension admits a trivialization compatible with the anti-symmetric structure. Biextensions are amenable to descriptions in terms of cocycles, and in the particular case of that associated to a symmetric monoidal category there is a tight relation with the symmetry data. Note that if
{E}
is symmetric monoidal, the associated biextension, by its very definition, is trivialized by the symmetry isomorphism
c_{X,Y}\colon YX\to XY\in E_{x,y}
, and the extent to which it is a trivialization of the full alternating structure gives a direct access to the
k
-invariant. While the main motivation in loc. cit. is to provide a geometric characterization for certain components of
H^{3}(B,A)
(and more generally
H^{n}(B,A)
), analyzing the biextension gives an alternative approach to the decomposition of the extension
0\to\Sigma A\to{E}\to B\to 0
that proves useful when generalized to a higher categorical setting.

Our goal in this paper is to apply these ideas to analyze monoidal bicategories. To do so, we need to appropriately upgrade the notions of extensions and biextensions to their categorical analogs by considering extensions and biextensions of groups by a Picard category.

It is known that the Q complex gives stable cohomology of Eilenberg-MacLane space, so we work out the cocycle conditions explicitly in case of full symmetry and verify the results that we found using biextensions. The main theorems of this paper are Theorem
3.9
and Theorem
6.1
where we describe the cocycles of
H^{3}(\pi_{0}{\mathbb{E}},\cAut_{\mathbb{E}}(I))
and invariants of a biextension, for a monoidal bi-category
\mathbb{E}
, explicitly.

3
Categorical Extensions

It is well-known that one can analyze a monoidal category by considering a categorical extension of its
\pi_{0}
group by the Picard category
\Sigma(\pi_{1})
and analyzing the K-invariant, i.e., some cohomology groups of the extension. We recall the relevant details quickly and develop the theory for monoidal bi-categories.

Definition 3.1
.

1.

Let
G
be a group,
{A}
be a Picard groupoid. A monoidal category

is called a categorical extension of

{A}
if there exists an exact sequence of functors, i.e., concatenation of two functors is homotopically zero, as follows:

where

is viewed as a discrete category.

2.

G
acts canonically on
{A}
. If this action is trivial, we call the extension a central extension.

In our case, the action of
G
will always be trivial on
{A}
.

Example 3.2
.

Given a functor from a Picard groupoid
{A}
to a monoidal category
{E}
, we can consider a group
G=\pi_{0}({E})/\pi_{0}({A})
, where

is the group generated by the isomorphism classes of the objects of the category. Then this creates a categorical extension of

.

3.1
Categorical Extensions and Monoidal categories

Starting with a monoidal category
{E}
, we can consider the Picard groupoid
{A}=\Sigma(\pi_{1}({E}))
, where
\pi_{1}({E})
is the automorphism group of the monoidal unit of

. So we can consider a categorical extension as in example

now becomes

0\to\Sigma(\pi_{1}({E}))\to{E}\to\pi_{0}({E})\to 0.

So this corresponds with a cocycle in

, which is isomorphic to

. One can explicitly construct the cocycle as shown in
[

]
. We recall the cocycles for the ease of readers.

Cohomology

Consider an arbitrary section
s\colon\pi_{0}({E})\to{E}
of
p\colon{E}\to\pi_{0}({E})
. Due to the monoidal structure of
{E}
, for each

there exists an isomorphism

. Since the association has to satisfy the pentagon axiom,

[f]\in H^{3}(\pi_{0}({E}),\pi_{1}({E}))
. This is the cocycle that corresponds to the monoidal category
{E}
we started with.

4
Biextensions

We use extensions to analyze the associativity of a monoidal (bi-)category, but to analyze the braiding and symmetry, we need a structure that takes two objects into account. Breen used this idea in
[
Bre99
]
to analyze the symmetry of monoidal categories using biextensions. With this, we also recover the conditions for associativity when we restrict ourselves to one group. Let us first recall the definition of biextensions in the case of groups, then we quickly recall Breen’s work on monoidal categories that leads to our work on monoidal bi-categories.

Definition 4.1
.

(See
[
Mum68
]
,
[
Gro72
]
)
Let

be an abelian group,
G,H
be groups. A biextension
E
, of
G\times H
by
A
is an
A
-torsor over
G\times H
, endowed with a pair of partial composition laws whose restriction to appropriate fibers may be depicted as a morphism of
A
-torsors.

+_{1}\colon E_{x,y}\wedge^{A}E_{x^{\prime},y}\to E_{xx^{\prime},y}

+_{2}\colon E_{x,y}\wedge^{A}E_{x,y^{\prime}}\to E_{x,yy^{\prime}}.

Here
E_{x,y}
denotes the fiber above a point
(x,y)\in G\times H
. These two composition laws are required to be

1.

associative

2.

compatible with each other, i.e.

(X_{x,y}+_{1}X_{x^{\prime},y})+_{2}(X_{x,y^{\prime}}+_{1}X_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}})=(X_{x,y}+_{1}X_{x,y^{\prime}})+_{2}(X_{x^{\prime},y}+_{1}X_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}).

.

A biextension

4.2

For a monoidal category

,

can be proved to be an abelian group using the Eckmann-Hilton argument. Moreover, for any

(h,f)\mapsto f\circ\eta_{X}(h)
. Similarly, one can also define a right action.

So we consider a biextension of
\pi_{0}({E})\times\pi_{0}({E})
by

, endowed with the following pair of partial composition laws as shown in
[

]

+_{1}\colon{E}_{x,y}\wedge^{\pi_{1}{E}}{E}_{x^{\prime},y}\to{E}_{xx^{\prime},y}

+_{2}\colon{E}_{x,y}\wedge^{\pi_{1}{E}}{E}_{x,y^{\prime}}\to{E}_{x,yy^{\prime}}.

Here
{E}_{x,y}=\text{Hom}_{{E}}(YX,XY)
denotes a fiber above
(x,y)\in\pi_{0}({E})\times\pi_{0}({E})
with chosen

that represent the classes

, respectively. The partial composition laws are set maps defined as follows. For

It can be easily checked that these are well-defined under the action of

. These partial composition laws must satisfy conditions like associativity, commutativity, and compatibility with each other, and that gives us corresponding cocycles as shown in
[

-complex for a symmetric monoidal category

For a given symmetric monoidal category
{E}
, we can consider the extension
0\to\Sigma(\pi_{1}({E}))\to{E}\to\pi_{0}({E})\to 0
as earlier.
Let

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces z}

denote an element in
Q_{1}(\pi_{0}{E})
, i.e.,
y=x+z
. Note that, since
{E}
is braided,
x+z=z+x
for every
x,z\in\pi_{0}\mathbb{E}
. Similarly, let

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces z}
an element in
Q_{1}({A})
if there is an isomorphism
x+z\xrightarrow{\cong}y
.
Now let us consider a section
s\colon\pi_{0}{E}\to{E}
. Due to the monoidal structure of
{E}
, for each
x,y\in\pi_{0}{E}
and the fact that
s(-)
is an
\pi_{1}
-torsor, we have a morphism of
\pi_{1}
-torsors,
\lambda_{x,y}\colon s(x)\wedge^{\pi_{1}}s(y)\xrightarrow{\cong}s(x+y)
. This gives us elements of the form
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s(x)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s(x+y)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s(y)}
in
Q_{1}({A})
. Moreover, these must satisfy the following cocycle conditions, i.e., the element mentioned below must lie in
Q_{2}({A})
.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s(x)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{s(x+y)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s(y)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{s(x+z)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bullet}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{s(y+t)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s(z)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{s(z+t)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s(t)}

In other words, the existence of this cube implies that
s(x+y)+s(z+t)\cong s(x+z)+s(y+t)
. Notice that this single condition represents associativity as well as commutativity. If we make
z=0
, the diagram says
s(x+y)+s(t)\cong s(x)+s(y+t)
, similarly, making
x,t=0
, we get
s(y)+s(z)=s(z)+s(y)
.

6.3.2
Q
-complex for a symmetric monoidal bi-category

In the case of a symmetric monoidal bi-category
\mathbb{E}
, in addition to the previous structure, for each
x,y\in\pi_{0}\mathbb{E}
, we have
\lambda_{x,y}\colon s(x)+s(y)\xrightarrow{\cong}s(x+y)+c(x,y)
for a
c(x,y)\in\text{Ob}(\pi_{1}\mathbb{E})
. Hence, in this case, we have a non-trivial element of
Q_{2}({A})

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s(x)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{s(x+y)+c(x,y)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s(y)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{s(x+z)+c(x,z)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s(x+y+z+t)+\theta\begin{pmatrix}x&&y\\
z&&t\end{pmatrix}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{s(y+t)+c(y,t)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s(z)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{s(z+t)+c(z,t)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s(t)}

Here
\theta

\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\
z&t\end{pmatrix}\colon

c(x+y,z+t)+c(x,y)+c(z,t)\to c(x+z,y+t)+c(x,z)+c(y,t)
is a morphism in
{A}
representing the pentagon for associator in
\mathbb{E}
. This must satisfy the following (4,4)-coherence axiom for extensions.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces((s(x)+s(y))+(s(z)+s(t)))+((s(a)+s(b))+(s(c)+s(d)))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces((s(x)+s(y))+(s(a)+s(b)))+((s(z)+s(t))+(s(c)+s(d)))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces((s(x)+s(z))+(s(y)+s(t)))+((s(a)+s(c))+(s(b)+s(d)))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces((s(x)+s(a))+(s(y)+s(b)))+((s(z)+s(c))+(s(t)+s(d)))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces((s(x)+s(z))+(s(a)+s(c)))+((s(y)+s(t))+(s(b)+s(d)))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces((s(x)+s(a))+(s(z)+s(c)))+((s(y)+s(b))+(s(t)+s(d)))}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces ca}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces ca+ca}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces ca+ca}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces ca}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces ca}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces ca+ca}

After expanding this diagram using the monoidal product, we get the following condition for
\theta
.

\begin{multlined}\theta\begin{pmatrix}x+y&z+t\\
a+b&c+d\end{pmatrix}\cdot\left(\theta\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\
a&b\end{pmatrix}+\theta\begin{pmatrix}z&t\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\right)\cdot\theta\begin{pmatrix}x+a&y+b\\
z+c&t+d\end{pmatrix}=\\
\left(\theta\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\
z&t\end{pmatrix}+\theta\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\right)\cdot\theta\begin{pmatrix}x+z&y+t\\
a+c&b+d\end{pmatrix}\cdot\left(\theta\begin{pmatrix}x&z\\
a&c\end{pmatrix}+\theta\begin{pmatrix}y&t\\
b&d\end{pmatrix}\right)\end{multlined}

To analyze the commutativity, we need to consider the compatibility with 16 variables, instead of just the 8 variables. But this becomes too difficult to compute. So we instead use the same technique with biextensions. We consider a biextension for a given monoidal bi-category and put the
Q
-complex structure over
{A},\mathbb{E}
, and
\pi_{0}\mathbb{E}\times\pi_{0}\mathbb{E}
, we get the following two partial composition laws as earlier.

X_{x,y}+_{1}X_{x^{\prime},y}\xrightarrow{\cong}X_{x+x^{\prime},y}+f(x,x^{\prime};y)

X_{x,y}+_{2}X_{x,y^{\prime}}\xrightarrow{\cong}X_{x,y+y^{\prime}}+g(x;y,y^{\prime})

Here
X_{-,-}\colon\pi_{0}\mathbb{E}\times\pi_{0}\mathbb{E}\to\mathbb{E}
is a section, and
f(-,-;-),g(-;-,-)\in\text{Ob}({A})
. Let us denote the maps
ca_{1}
and
ca_{2}
that arise by commutativity and associativity on
+_{1}
and
+_{2}
individually as follows.

ca_{1}\colon(X_{x,y}+_{1}X_{x^{\prime},y})+_{1}(X_{x^{\prime\prime},y}+_{1}X_{x^{\prime\prime\prime},y})\to(X_{x,y}+_{1}X_{x^{\prime\prime},y})+_{1}(X_{x^{\prime},y}+_{1}X_{x^{\prime\prime\prime},y})

ca_{2}\colon(X_{x,y}+_{2}X_{x,y^{\prime}})+_{2}(X_{x,y^{\prime\prime}}+_{2}X_{x,y^{\prime\prime\prime}})\to(X_{x,y}+_{2}X_{x,y^{\prime\prime}})+_{2}(X_{x,y^{\prime}}+_{2}X_{x,y^{\prime\prime\prime}})

Similarly, we denote the morphism due to the compatibility of
+_{1}
and
+_{2}
by
ca_{12}
as follows.

ca_{12}\colon(X_{x,y}+_{1}X_{x^{\prime},y})+_{2}(X_{x,y^{\prime}}+_{1}X_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}})\to(X_{x,y}+_{2}X_{x,y^{\prime}})+_{1}(X_{x^{\prime},y}+_{2}X_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}})

Now we can break the problem of considering 16 variables into two parts—the (4,2)-coherence axiom and the (2,4)-coherence axiom. The maps
ca_{1},ca_{12}
must satisfy the following (4,2)-coherence axiom (diagram
2
). After expanding this diagram using the partial composition laws we get the following cocycle conditions.

\begin{multlined}\chi\begin{pmatrix}x+x^{\prime}&x^{\prime\prime}+x^{\prime\prime\prime}\\
y&y^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\left(\chi\begin{pmatrix}x&x^{\prime}\\
y&y^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}+\chi\begin{pmatrix}x^{\prime\prime}&x^{\prime\prime\prime}\\
y&y^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\right)\cdot\theta\left(\begin{pmatrix}x&x^{\prime}\\

\left(\theta\left(\begin{pmatrix}x&x^{\prime}\\

y&y^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\right)\end{multlined}

Here
\theta
and

are defined as follows.

\begin{multlined}\chi\begin{pmatrix}x&x^{\prime}\\

whereas

\begin{split}f(x+x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}+x^{\prime\prime\prime};y)+f(x,x^{\prime};y)&+f(x^{\prime\prime},x^{\prime\prime\prime};y)\longrightarrow\\
f(x+x^{\prime\prime},x^{\prime}+x^{\prime\prime\prime};y)&+f(x,x^{\prime\prime};y)+f(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime\prime};y).\end{split}

Similarly, we also need the (2,4)-coherence axiom for

where we consider

and

6.3.1

Q
-complex capture all the information in the case of full symmetry.
