A pH-responsive composite hydrogel based on konjac glucomannan and its oxidized derivatives: synthesis, characterization and swelling behavior
Authors: Chongyang Zhu, Cunhuan Xu, Xueqing Zhu, Xiang Ma, Hongmin Geng, Cong Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA novel pH-responsive hydrogel composed of oxidized konjac glucomannan (OKGM) and konjac glucomannan (KGM) was fabricated using sodium trimetaphosphate as a crosslinker for intestinal-targeted delivery applications. A series of OKGM samples with carboxyl contents ranging from 1.35% to 5.59% and degree of oxidation (DO) values from 0.49 to 2.01 was successfully prepared. The OKGM and the OKGM-KGM hydrogels were characterized by Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy, thermogravimetric analysis, and small angle X-ray scattering. Congo red assays revealed that OKGM adopted a random-coil conformation under alkaline conditions. Rheological analysis showed that OKKH-1 exhibited the highest yield stress (948.8 Pa), indicating the strongest gel network, whereas increasing DO reduced structural strength and enhanced flexibility. The hydrogels showed pronounced pH responsiveness, with equilibrium swelling ratios of 5.12-12.27 g/g at pH 2.0 and 13.96-21.91 g/g at pH 7.2. Furthermore, in vitro release studies revealed that the release kinetics conformed well to the Korsmeyer-Peppas model with release exponent values greater than 0.89, implying that the release process was governed by a super case-II transport mechanism. These results demonstrate that the OKGM-KGM hydrogels possess tunable structure and performance, showing promise as biocompatible carriers for controlled intestinal delivery in food and pharmaceutical applications.Keywords: Oxidized konjac glucomannan, Hydrogel, pH-responsive, Controlled release

Introduction

The three-dimensional network of hydrogels, which are formed from hydrophilic polymers, provides a remarkable swelling capacity that allows them to retain substantial amounts of water. Recently, considerable attention has been devoted to designing “intelligent” hydrogels that can respond to external environmental factors such as pH, temperature, or light (Yang et al., 2022). Among these, pH-responsive hydrogels have attracted particular attention as intelligent drug delivery systems, offering controlled and site-specific release while protecting unstable drugs from degradation (Madineh et al., 2025). Nevertheless, synthetic materials generally suffer from limited biodegradability and biocompatibility, which underscores the importance of developing natural polysaccharide-based hydrogels (Zhang et al., 2022).

Konjac glucomannan (KGM), a neutral polysaccharide derived from the tuber of Amorphophallus konjac C., has a backbone of β-(1 → 4) linked D-mannose and D-glucose in a molar ratio of approximately 1.6:1 or 1.4:1 (Kapoor et al., 2024). Occasional β-1,3 glycosidic branches may occur at mannose units, while acetyl groups randomly attached to the C-6 position of the main chain result in an acetylation degree between 5% and 10% (Zhang et al., 2025). The unique gel-forming ability of KGM make it an ideal candidate for developing biocompatible hydrogels used in controlled drug delivery systems, food packaging, and tissue engineering (Wang et al., 2025a). Nevertheless, as an uncharged neutral polysaccharide, it is insensitive to the pH variations along the digestive tract, which restricts its use in responsive delivery systems (Wang et al., 2019). Recent progress has shown that chemical tailoring (such as oxidation, carboxymethylation, and deacetylation) and composite design appear particularly effective for improving the functional adaptability of KGM-based hydrogels (Wang et al., 2024a; Wang et al., 2025b). In particular, the 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl (TEMPO)-mediated oxidation selectively converts primary hydroxyl groups at the C-6 position of sugar residues into carboxyl groups, enabling precise adjustment of negative charge density (Shi et al., 2017). Oxidized deacetylated KGM can form soft gels and is being explored for texture-modified food applications such as dysphagia diets (Wang et al., 2024b). In our previous research (Zhu et al., 2023), we developed a carboxymethylated KGM hydrogel crosslinked with sodium trimetaphosphate (STMP), a food-grade crosslinker widely used for natural polymers (Alavarse et al., 2022). The STMP-polysaccharide crosslinking mechanism has been previously described (Lack et al., 2007). Briefly, under alkaline conditions, the hydroxyl groups of polysaccharides are converted into alkoxide ions, which subsequently undergo nucleophilic attack on STMP, leading to the formation of crosslinked polysaccharide chains via esterification reactions. Based on this, we propose that oxidized KGM (OKGM) and/or KGM can be crosslinked through STMP, yielding a composite hydrogel in which the introduced carboxyl groups confer pH responsiveness. The resulting OKGM-KGM hydrogel is expected to possess tunable swelling and release characteristics suitable for intestinal functional components delivery applications.

In this study, OKGM with various carboxyl contents was prepared and characterized. Subsequently, the OKGM-KGM composite hydrogels were prepared and evaluated in terms of their physicochemical, microstructure, rheological, and swelling properties. Nicotinamide (NTM) is known for its neuroprotective effects, including the inhibition of apoptosis, maintenance of genomic stability, and promotion of remyelination after stroke. However, its oral use is limited by deamidation (Braidy et al., 2019), making controlled release essential to preserve its activity. Therefore, the release behavior of NTM from the developed hydrogel system was investigated using an in vitro simulated gastrointestinal model.

Materials and methods

Materials

KGM powder (YZ-J-40H, batch number: 20241111) with a purity of 91.8% (dry basis) and viscosity of 40.3 Pa s was purchased from Hubei Yizhi Konjac Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Yichang, Hubei, China). NaClO (6-14% active chlorine basis, batch number: C16863233, CAS: 7681-52-9) and TEMPO (purity: 98%, batch number: C15698345, CAS: 2564-83-2) were supplied by Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All other reagents were of analytical grade and supplied by Tianjin Kemiou Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China).

Preparation of OKGM

OKGM was prepared according to previously described methods (Shi et al., 2017;Shi et al., 2023) with minor adjustments. KGM (10 g) was dissolved in 1 L of NaOH solution (pH 10) containing 0.01 g TEMPO and 0.1 g NaBr for 1 h at ambient temperature with constant mild stirring. The oxidation was initiated by adding NaClO, and the mixture was stirred for another hour while the pH was maintained at 10 using 1 M NaOH. The volume of NaClO and oxidation temperature used are listed in Table 1. The reaction scheme for KGM oxidation is illustrated in Fig. 1. After completion, the product was precipitated with anhydrous ethanol, filtered, and washed sequentially with 70% and 100% ethanol until no chloride ions were detected using AgNO3 solution. The purified OKGM was then dried using a blast oven at 60 °C. The carboxyl content (%) was analyzed by acid-base titration according to (Shi et al., 2023), and the degree of oxidation (DO) was determined following (Qu et al., 2021).

Preparation of crosslinked OKGM-KGM hydrogels

To prepare the composite hydrogels, 0.5 g of OKGM (samples OKGM-1∼OKGM-5, as listed in Table 1) and 1.5 g of KGM were uniformly dispersed in 50 mL of deionized water and stirred for 2 h to obtain homogeneous solutions at ambient temperature. The blends were labeled as OKKH-1 to OKKH-5, corresponding to their respective OKGM components. Subsequently, 1.0 g of STMP was added to each OKGM-KGM solution under gentle stirring until completely dissolved. Finally, the resulting mixtures were sealed with laminating film and left undisturbed for 12 h at ambient temperature to allow complete gel formation.

Molecular weight determination

The weight-average (Mw) and number-average (Mn) molecular weights of the KGM and OKGM were determined using size exclusion chromatography coupled with multi-angle laser light scattering and refractive index detection (DAWN HELEOS-II laser photometer, Wyatt Technology, USA) (Zhang et al., 2023). Samples (1 g/L) were filtered through 0.22 μm membranes before injection (20 μL), the flow rate was 0.3 mL/min.

Zeta-potential measurement

Zeta-potential values of KGM and OKGM were determined using a Zetasizer Nano ZEN3700 (Malvern Instruments, UK). The samples (1 mg/L) were prepared and analyzed at 25 °C.

Congo red conformation analysis

The Congo red conformation analysis was performed as described by (Zhu et al., 2023). Two milliliters of a polysaccharide solution (2 g/L) was mixed with 2 mL of a 0.1 mM Congo red solution. NaOH was then added to adjust the final concentration range from 0 to 0.50 M (0.05 M intervals). After 10 min, absorbance spectra were recorded between 400 and 700 nm using a Varioskan LUX spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA).

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR)

The chemical structures of OKGM and OKKH were analyzed by FT-IR spectroscopy (TENSOR II, Bruker, Germany). The dried samples were ground with KBr, pressed into pellets, and subsequently scanned over the 4000-400 cm−1 range using a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 16 scans.

Small angle X-ray scattering (SAXS)

SAXS experiments were conducted on a SAXSpace system (Anton Paar, Austria) using Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 0.1541 nm) at 25 °C. KGM and OKGM were dissolved in deionized water (1% m/v). The KGM, OKGM, and OKKH samples were loaded into capillary tubes and equilibrated for 2 h before measurement. The fractal dimension was determined from the slope (α) of the log-log SAXS curve in the low-q region, i.e., I(q) ∼ q−α, where Dm = α (0 < α < 3) for mass fractals and Ds = 6 - α (3 < Df < 4) for surface fractals (Geng et al., 2023).

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Thermal stability was evaluated using a TG-A Q50 analyzer (TA Instruments, USA) by heating the samples from 40 to 600 °C at a rate of 20 °C/min under a nitrogen flow of 10 mL/min for protection.

Scanning electron microscope

The freeze-dried OKKH hydrogels were observed using scanning electron microscope (SEM, Quanta 250FEG, Czechia). The samples were sputter-coated with gold and then observed under SEM at an accelerating voltage of 3 kV. The open-source ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA) was used to extract the pore structure information from the SEM images.

Rheological measurements

Rheological analysis was performed using a HAAKE MARS60 hybrid rheometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) equipped with a parallel-plate setup (35 mm diameter, 1000 μm gap). Prior to rheological testing, the samples were equilibrated on the lower plate for a duration of 5 min. Strain sweep tests (0.01-100%, 1 Hz, 25 °C) were used to identify the linear viscoelastic region (LVR), followed by frequency sweep tests (0.1-100 Hz, 1% strain) to determine the viscoelastic parameters, i.e., storage (G′) and loss (G″) moduli (Qu et al., 2021). Release profile of NTM

NTM was selected as a model drug to study release behavior using an in vitro gastrointestinal simulation. HCl (pH 2.0) and PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.2) were used to simulate gastric fluid (SGF) and simulate intestinal fluid (SIF) (Mohammadi et al., 2021). All experiments were conducted in triplicate. Data plotting and statistical analysis were performed using Origin 2021 (OriginLab, USA) and SPSS 20.0 (IBM, USA), respectively, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Characterization of OKGM

The carboxyl content, DO, Mw, Mn, and zeta-potential of KGM and the prepared OKGM samples are summarized in Table 1. The carboxyl content of OKGM increased progressively from 1.35% to 5.59%, while the DO increased correspondingly from 0.49 to 2.01 as the NaClO dosage or reaction temperature rose. In contrast, all oxidized samples exhibited lower molecular weights than KGM, and Mw declined with increasing carboxyl substitution. This decrease can be attributed to partial cleavage of glycosidic linkages during oxidation, resulting in shorter polysaccharide chains, which agrees with previous findings (Li et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023a;Wang et al., 2024b). The polydispersity index (PDI, Mw/Mn) of KGM was 1.175, while the OKGM-1∼OKGM-5 samples exhibited higher PDI values ranging from 2.510 to 7.641, indicating a broader molecular weight distribution after oxidation. This broadening can be attributed to random chain scission of the KGM backbone during oxidation (Li et al., 2023a). Regarding surface charge, the zeta potential of the OKGM-1∼OKGM-5 samples ranged from −20.89 to −24.39 mV, while that of KGM was −3.22 mV. The increase in charge densities of the derivatives corresponds to the higher carboxyl content, suggesting that the charge density of OKGM is closely related to the number of introduced carboxyl groups.

Congo red is commonly used to detect triple-helical configurations in polysaccharides, as it forms complexes that exhibit a redshift in the maximum absorption wavelength (λmax) (Yuan et al., 2020). Upon increasing NaOH concentration, triple-helical structures generally unwind into single-coil conformations, causing a reduction in λmax (Zhang et al., 2016). As shown in Fig. 2a, no obvious decrease in λmax occurred in the Congo red-KGM complex until NaOH reached 0.25 M, while a bathochromic shift from 495 to 480 nm (Congo red alone) to 513–504 nm was observed for all tested conditions. This suggests that KGM adopts a random-coil conformation under alkaline conditions, possibly due to the deacetylation alkaline treatment. Similarly, OKGM-1 to OKGM-5 samples did not exhibit a clear descending trend in λmax, indicating a random-coil conformation of the Congo red-OKGM complexes under the same conditions. This conformational change is consistent with the marked reduction in Mw and the broader PDI observed after oxidation.

Chemical structure of OKGM and OKKH by FT-IR

Fig. 2b shows the FT-IR spectra of KGM, OKGM, and the synthesized OKKH hydrogels. Regarding KGM, the broad absorption band at 3500-3300 cm−1 corresponds to O-H stretching, while peaks at 2925, 1380, and 1020 cm−1 arise from -CH2- vibrations (Guan et al., 2022). The characteristic acetyl group band at 1734 cm−1 disappeared in OKGM, indicating deacetylation during alkaline oxidation (Wang et al., 2024b). Moreover, peaks at 872 and 808 cm−1, typical of mannose residues (Hu et al., 2024), were observed in KGM. In OKGM spectra, the disappearance of the 1636 cm−1 band suggests disruption of intra-molecular hydrogen bonding, while new absorption peaks at 1607 and 1419 cm−1 correspond to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching of carboxyl groups, respectively (Liu et al., 2020a), confirming successful oxidation. In OKKH hydrogels, a new band near 1309 cm−1 was detected and assigned to P=O stretching, while an additional peak at 1103 cm−1 corresponded to P-O-C vibrations of phosphate ester linkages (Zhu et al., 2021, 2023). The emergence of these signals verifies the formation of phosphate ester crosslinks between the hydroxyl groups of KGM/OKGM and STMP.

SAXS analysis

SAXS profiles of KGM, OKGM-1∼OKGM-5, and OKKH-1∼OKKH-5 are presented in Fig. 3a and b. SAXS spectra generally contain two q-regions: the low-q region, which reflects larger-scale structures, and the high-q region, which represents the interfacial features of the scatterers (Alba et al., 2018; Song et al., 2022). In the colloidal state, KGM macromolecular backbones tend to stretch, forming very small aggregates even without external treatment (Song et al., 2022). In the low-q region, the scattered intensity of OKGM-1∼OKGM-5 decreased with increasing carboxyl content, suggesting a reduction in electron density contrast. In contrast, an increase in the scattered intensity in the low-q region has been documented in the lignan-rice starch system (Guo et al., 2022), the agar/KGM/κ-carrageenan ternary system (Qiao et al., 2023), and the soy protein-polysaccharide fibre system (Garina et al., 2024). This phenomenon has been ascribed to enhanced molecular aggregation, which disrupts the structural homogeneity of the respective matrix structures. In the case of OKGM, however, the observed behavior can be attributed to the introduction of carboxyl groups, which disrupt the hydrogen bonding network of KGM and promote aggregate dissociation (Ren et al., 2026), thereby leading to a more homogeneous molecular dispersion. At high-q values, the curves for all samples converged, indicating that the local, small-scale structures were less affected by oxidation.

SAXS patterns of (a) KGM and OKGM-1∼OKGM-5, and (b) OKKH-1∼OKKH-5. OKGM-1∼OKGM-5: oxidized KGM with different carboxyl contents (1.35%, 2.22%, 3.90%, 4.62%, and 5.59%); OKKH-1∼OKKH-5: hydrogels prepared by combining OKGM-1∼OKGM-5 with KGM, respectively.

For the OKKH samples, the upturn observed in the low-q region indicated the presence of structural inhomogeneities within the cross-linked gel networks, likely arising from the formation of cross-linking aggregates (Yang et al., 2016). The introduced carboxyl groups on OKGM carry negative charges. Increasing the carboxyl content enhances the electrostatic repulsion between the polysaccharide chains. This repulsion is postulated to hinder uniform chain association and interfere with the STMP-mediated crosslinking process, which primarily reacts with hydroxyl groups. Consequently, the crosslinking reaction is likely driven to occur in a more localized and heterogeneous manner. Higher carboxyl content thus enhances network heterogeneity, leading to increased electron density contrast between dense clusters and dilute regions. This effect is reflected in the progressive increase in SAXS intensity from OKKH-1 to OKKH-5.

As for the fractal structure, the slope (α) was fitted using the SAXS curve of 0.2 - 0.5 nm−1. The fitted α values for KGM, OKGM-1∼OKGM-5 and OKKH-1∼OKKH-5 were 0.36, 0.42, 0.41, 0.21, 0.24, 0.19, 1.40, 1.21, 0.64, 0.81 and 0.66, respectively. All samples exhibited mass fractal characteristics (Dm = α), which positively correlate with the compactness of the scatterers (Shilova, 2020). With increasing DO, the slope magnitude slightly decreased for OKGM, indicating a marginal improvement in structural uniformity. This trend is consistent with the mechanism by which oxidation disrupts the native hydrogen-bonding network and enhances molecular chain dispersion. In contrast, the hydrogels displayed higher slope magnitudes than the corresponding sols, confirming the presence of large-scale structural heterogeneities characteristic of fractal networks formed during crosslinking (Zheng et al., 2023). As the DO increased, the slope magnitude generally decreased, indicating the formation of looser, lower-dimensional gel networks. This structural change is attributed to diffusion-limited crosslinking aggregation caused by electrostatic repulsion.

Thermal stability

The TG and derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) curves of KGM, OKGM-1∼OKGM-5, and OKKH-1∼OKKH-5 are presented in Fig. 4. All samples exhibited three main stages of weight loss. The first stage below 200 °C corresponds to moisture loss. The second major degradation step, between 200 and 350 °C, is associated with cleavage of glycosidic linkages, release of bound water, and partial decomposition of macromolecular chains (Wang et al., 2024b). The final stage (350-600 °C) reflects further thermal breakdown of the polymer backbone. The onset temperature of the second stage for OKGM was approximately 210 °C—around 50 °C lower than that of native KGM—indicating reduced thermal stability after oxidation. Total weight losses for KGM and OKGM-1∼OKGM-5 were 71.19%, 70.02%, 68.82%, 68.17%, 70.55% and 65.84%, respectively. The slightly lower thermal resistance of OKGM can be attributed to the substitution of carboxyl groups and reduced molecular integrity, consistent with observations in carboxymethylated waxy starch (Liu et al., 2020b). For the OKKH samples, total weight losses for OKKH-1∼OKKH-5 were 24.89%, 26.83%, 31.02%, 29.28% and 29.86%, respectively. All hydrogel samples retained more weight at high temperatures compared with OKGM, demonstrating enhanced thermal stability. Moreover, hydrogels derived from OKGM with lower carboxyl content showed greater residual mass. These results indicate that STMP-mediated crosslinking significantly reinforces the structural and thermal stability of the composite hydrogels.

Morphology observation of OKKH

Photographs and SEM micrographs of the OKKH-1∼OKKH-5 samples are presented in Fig. 5. All samples successfully formed cohesive hydrogels. OKKH-1 exhibited a hard and firm texture, whereas the others became progressively softer and more pliable as the carboxyl content of OKGM increased. SEM images revealed that all hydrogels possessed highly interconnected irregular polygonal pores with a certain thickness, exhibiting a lamellar morphology. The porosity values of OKKH-1∼OKKH-5 were 52.76%, 53.75%, 53.58%, 54.85% and 57.40%, respectively. The lamellae of OKKH-1 appeared more compact and well-organized, while those of the other hydrogels were looser and more porous. Furthermore, the mean pore sizes for the OKKH samples were 85.25 ± 7.93 μm, 90.29 ± 8.74 μm, 90.54 ± 9.33 μm, 97.88 ± 10.05 μm, and 110.20 ± 12.01 μm, respectively, indicating that a greater DO led to larger pores. The variation in pore morphology and gel texture can be attributed to the reduction in molecular weight and enhanced electrostatic repulsion introduced by carboxyl substitution, both of which weaken network compaction and modify gelation behavior.

Rheological properties

Strain sweep

The strain sweep profiles of the OKKH-1∼OKKH-5 samples are depicted in Fig. 6a–b, and the LVR was determined from these measurements. Within the LVR, the G′ consistently exceeded the G″ for all OKKH samples, indicating that the hydrogels possessed stable, interconnected networks exhibiting dominant elastic behavior (Mousavi et al., 2020). Beyond this range, G′ decreased with increasing strain, implying network disruption and the onset of non-linear deformation. The yield point (τy) is defined as the stress at the limit of LVR, representing the minimum stress required to perturb the internal microstructure and induce irreversible deformation (Wang et al., 2026). Upon exceeding the LVR, G′ continues to decrease; when the curves of G′ and G″ intersect, macroscopic flow commences, thereby demarcating the flow point (τf) (Wang et al., 2024a). In the intermediate regime between the τy and the τf, G′ remains greater than G″ for all the OKKH samples, signifying a progressive attenuation of initial structural integrity while the material retains its gel-like character.

As presented in Table S1, OKKH-1 exhibited the highest τy value of 948.8 Pa, while the τy values of OKKH-2∼OKKH-5 declined significantly as the DO of OKGM increased. This trend is likely attributable to the lower carboxyl content, which leaves a greater number of hydroxyl groups available to participate in crosslinking reactions, thereby favoring stronger crosslinking and the formation of a denser gel network relative to the more highly oxidized counterparts. Furthermore, the higher DO of OKGM was accompanied by a reduction in chain length (i.e., lower Mw), which also contributed substantially to the structural integrity of the hydrogel network. Similar results have been reported for ozone-oxidized KGM (Wang et al., 2024b). OKKH-5 exhibited the highest flow transition index (τf/τy) value of 0.71, indicating the greatest flowability among the hydrogels, followed by OKKH-4. Consequently, the balance between mechanical strength and flexibility of the OKGM-KGM composite hydrogel can be effectively tailored by modulating the DO, thereby meeting the requirements of customized applications in the food and pharmaceutical fields.

Frequency sweep

The frequency dependence of G′ and G″ of the OKKH-1∼OKKH-5 samples is illustrated in Fig. 6c–d. For OKKH-1 and OKKH-2, both moduli remained nearly constant below 10 Hz, indicating frequency-independent and stable gel structures. In contrast, hydrogels with higher DO exhibited more pronounced frequency dependence across the full range. In all cases, G′ exceeded G″, confirming elastic-dominated viscoelastic behavior in the absence of a crossover point. As DO increased, both G′ and G″ declined, reflecting reduced structural integrity and weaker network formation. These rheological observations are consistent with the morphological changes observed in the SEM images.

The viscoelastic behavior of the samples was further analyzed in the low-frequency region (0.1-10 Hz) and the high-frequency region (10-100 Hz) using a power-law model (Fan et al., 2022), and the corresponding fitting parameters are listed in Table S2. As shown in Table S2, the power-law model provided a good fit for all samples in the low-frequency region (R2 > 0.9), except for OKKH-1. The relatively weak frequency dependence exhibited by OKKH-1 in the low-frequency region suggests a dominant solid-like elastic character, whereas the other samples displayed a predominantly viscoelastic response (Song et al., 2024). This indicates that OKKH-1 may be well-suited for applications requiring structural resilience under dynamic mechanical conditions. The value of b' (b'') increased across the frequency range for all OKKH samples, with a clear tendency for higher b' (b'') values to accompany a greater DO of OKGM, indicating enhanced relaxation of internal entanglements at elevated frequencies (Wang et al., 2026). This behavior may be attributed to the localized segmental motions of polymer chains occurring between crosslink junctions, or to the relaxation of transient physical entanglements superimposed upon the covalent network under high-frequency oscillatory deformation (Chen et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2023). The higher DO of OKGM led to a reduction in chain length and denser electrostatic interactions, thereby accentuating this behavior within the composite hydrogel system.

Swelling behavior

The swelling ratios of OKKH-1 to OKKH-5 in SGF and SIF are shown in Fig. 7a–b. The experimentally observed equilibrium swelling ratios ranged from 5.12 to 12.27 g/g in SGF and 13.96-21.91 g/g in SIF, demonstrating significantly greater swelling in alkaline conditions. Such pH-dependent swelling is advantageous for controlled release systems that remain compact in the stomach but expand and release drugs in the intestine (Sagar et al., 2021). Table 2 summarizes the swelling kinetic parameters of OKKH-1 to OKKH-5. All hydrogels exhibited excellent correlation coefficients (R2 > 0.99). Both the r0 and qe values were markedly higher at pH 7.2, which was consistent with the swelling trends shown in Fig. 7. It should be noted that the fitted qe values were slightly different from the experimentally observed equilibrium swelling ratios because the former were obtained from kinetic model fitting, whereas the latter were determined directly from the swelling experiments.

Overall, the swelling behavior of the OKKH samples was governed by the combined effects of electrostatic repulsion, hydrophilicity, and network constraints. Under acidic conditions, most carboxyl groups remained protonated, which reduced charge repulsion and allowed hydrogen bonding to restrict chain mobility and network expansion (Li et al., 2023b). In contrast, under intestinal pH, the carboxyl groups were ionized to –COO-, thereby increasing the negative charge density of the hydrogel network. This ionization enhanced electrostatic repulsion between polymer chains and promoted water penetration, resulting in markedly higher swelling ratios in SIF than in SGF. However, the effect of increasing carboxyl content on swelling was not purely linear. On the one hand, a higher carboxyl content increased hydrophilicity and osmotic driving force, both of which favored water uptake (Jing et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2015). On the other hand, oxidation also altered the crosslinked network structure. As indicated by the SAXS and rheological results, increasing DO led to a looser and more heterogeneous gel architecture, together with reduced structural integrity. This change may be associated with the reduction in Mw and the lower availability of hydroxyl groups for effective STMP-mediated crosslinking, which weakened the elastic retractive force of the network.

Therefore, the observed swelling ratios reflected a balance between swelling-promoting factors, mainly carboxyl ionization and increased hydrophilicity, and swelling-restricting factors, mainly network integrity and crosslinking constraints. This competitive mechanism explains why the swelling ratio generally increased with carboxyl content but did not follow a strictly monotonic trend. In particular, although OKKH-5 had the highest carboxyl content, its equilibrium swelling ratio in SIF was slightly lower than that of OKKH-2, suggesting that excessive oxidation weakened the gel matrix to such an extent that further increases in charge density no longer translated into higher water uptake. These results indicate that a moderate oxidation level may provide a more favorable balance between pH responsiveness and structural stability.

In vitro NTM release

The cumulative NTM release curves of OKKH-1 to OKKH-5 in simulated gastrointestinal fluids are shown in Fig. 8. After 300 min, release curves approached a plateau. The cumulative NTM release in SGF (120 min) was 48.61%, 45.90%, 48.95%, 49.84%, and 55.61% for OKKH-1 through OKKH-5, respectively, while in SIF (300 min) the values were 90.90%, 90.73%, 91.57%, 91.91%, and 93.02%. These results clearly demonstrate pH-dependent release behavior, confirming the suitability of OKKH samples for intestine-targeted delivery applications. Drug release kinetics describe the time-dependent transport of encapsulated molecules and provide insights into the mechanisms controlling release (Orlandi et al., 2020). Three kinetic equations—Zero-order, Higuchi, and Korsmeyer-Peppas—were applied to fit the data (Table 3). The Korsmeyer-Peppas model yielded the best correlation (highest R2) for all formulations. In cylindrical systems, when the release exponent (n) equals 0.45, diffusion is Fickian (case-I); for 0.45 < n < 0.89, transport is anomalous (non-Fickian); n = 0.89 corresponds to case-II transport; and n > 0.89 indicates super case-II transport (Nazaruk et al., 2014). The n values for all OKKH samples ranged between 1.1090 and 1.8678, indicating that NTM release was governed by super case-II transport and dominated by polymer relaxation/dissolution (Jian et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2020a).

Furthermore, it should be noted that the simulated gastrointestinal fluids used in this study lacked digestive enzymes (e.g., pepsin and pancreatin), and thus the in vitro model remains a simplified representation of the gastrointestinal environment. Meanwhile, KGM and OKGM are polysaccharide-based dietary fibers whose main chains are generally not hydrolyzed by human digestive enzymes in the upper gastrointestinal tract (Kapoor et al., 2024). Therefore, although the absence of digestive enzymes may not markedly affect the direct degradation of the hydrogel matrix, it may still limit the physiological relevance of the release system. Accordingly, caution is needed when interpreting the present in vitro results in relation to in vivo performance.

Conclusion

In this study, OKGM with tunable oxidation levels was successfully prepared, with carboxyl content increasing from 1.35% to 5.59% and DO rising from 0.49 to 2.01. Oxidation significantly reduced the Mw of KGM and altered its molecular conformation and dispersion behavior. SAXS analysis further demonstrated that oxidation enhanced chain dispersion and homogeneity in sols, while promoting structural heterogeneity and looser fractal networks in the corresponding crosslinked hydrogels due to electrostatic repulsion. A novel pH-responsive OKGM–KGM composite hydrogel was successfully fabricated through STMP-mediated crosslinking, and its formation was verified by FT-IR, TGA, SEM, and rheological analyses. The results confirmed that the carboxyl content of OKGM played a key role in regulating the microstructure, viscoelasticity, swelling, and release behavior of the hydrogels. Among the tested formulations, OKKH-1 exhibited the strongest gel network, with the highest yield stress (948.8 Pa), the most compact lamellar structure, and the smallest mean pore size (85.25 ± 7.93 μm), indicating superior structural stability and mechanical robustness. However, its swelling capacity under intestinal pH was lower than that of some other formulations, which may limit its responsiveness in applications requiring rapid expansion and release. In contrast, OKKH-2 showed the most balanced overall performance for intestinal-targeted delivery, with the highest experimentally observed equilibrium swelling ratio at pH 7.2 (21.91 g/g), relatively low cumulative release in SGF (45.90% at 120 min), and high cumulative release in SIF (90.73% at 300 min). These results suggest that OKKH-2 provides a more favorable balance between gastric protection and intestinal release efficiency.

Overall, formulations with lower DO favor gel strength and structural integrity, whereas those with moderate oxidation provide a better compromise between mechanical stability and pH-responsive delivery behavior. Therefore, OKKH-1 may be more suitable for applications requiring enhanced mechanical robustness, while OKKH-2 appears to be a more promising candidate for intestine-targeted controlled-release systems in food and pharmaceutical fields.
