The Role of Thyroid Hormones in Neurodegenerative Disorders: Opportunities and Challenges
Authors: Yi Zhang, Miao Hao, Yuchuan Li, Kewen Cheng, Chunhai Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThyroid hormone (TH) is crucial to maintaining nervous system’s normal function and is essential for formation of dendrites, axons, and myelin sheaths of neurons, which form the basis for transmitting messages between neurons, and the deficiency of TH affects their development, which in turn affects nervous system’s normal operation. Recent articles have found that TH deficiency or abnormal levels not only affect mood and cognitive function but are also closely related to the development of a series of neurodegenerative disorders, like Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s and Huntington’s diseases, along with multiple sclerosis. The pathologic mechanisms of such diseases are complex, and it is difficult to confirm clinical diagnosis, and there exists a shortage of valid therapies, which can greatly reduce patients’ life quality once they develop. Therefore, a deep comprehension of TH mechanism in neurodegenerative disorders and exploring factors affecting its relationship with neurodegenerative disorders are of great clinical and research value. This article reviews the research progress in this field so far, analyzes the functional changes of TH in neurological pathological states, and explores its potential in clinical applications, aiming to provide new orientation for neurodegenerative disorders’ future diagnosis and therapy, and thus for developing more valid therapeutic strategies. Keywords: Thyroid hormone, Neurodegenerative disorders, Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, Multiple sclerosis

Introduction

Thyroid hormone (TH) is an important class of biologically active substances secreted by the thyroid gland. It mainly consists of thyroxine (thyroxine, T4) and more biologically active triiodothyronine (3,5,3′-triiodothyronine, T3). T3 is primarily derived from T4 through deiodination in peripheral tissues (approximately 80%), and a smaller portion is synthesized in the thyroid gland (approximately 20%). TH performs diverse physiological functions in the body, especially in metabolism, development, growth, and the functional regulation of the nervous system [1]. The mechanisms of action of different forms of thyroid hormones on the nervous system exhibit significant differences. As the active form of thyroid hormone, T3 can directly regulate target gene transcription by binding to thyroid hormone receptors (TRα and TRβ) within the cell nucleus, thus affecting neural cell proliferation, differentiation, myelination, and synaptic plasticity. This role is mainly accomplished through genomic effects mediated by nuclear receptors [2]. On the other hand, T3 can produce biological effects through non-genomic pathways independent of nuclear receptors, which include rapid signaling pathways activated in subcellular structures such as the cell membrane, cytoplasm, or mitochondria [3]. For instance, T3 can activate the PINK1-parkin signaling pathway to enhance mitochondrial autophagy, thereby promoting the recovery of mitochondrial function and alleviating neuroinflammation, which contributes to its neuroprotective effects [4]. Furthermore, T3 improves neuronal metabolism following cerebral ischemia by promoting γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and Ca2+ influx and accelerating presynaptic release [5]. Additionally, T3 can reduce cerebral edema and neural damage by inhibiting the expression of aquaporin-4 (AQP4) in astrocytes surrounding microvasculature [6]. As a precursor form of thyroid hormone, T4 possesses relatively low biological activity and usually requires being transformed into T3 to exert its biological effects. However, under certain circumstances, T4 may also exert biological effects through non-genomic mechanisms [2]. For instance, mice genetically engineered to lack the deiodinase enzyme required for T4 to T3 exhibit only minor physiological deficits and virtually no neurological abnormalities [7]. This suggests that T4 may exert regulatory functions even in the absence of T3. Furthermore, T4 can modulate voltage-gated sodium channels in neurons via non-genomic effects, affecting neuronal excitability [8]. In mouse animal models and human fetal development studies, TH was found to be mainly involved in late neurodevelopment events, such as neuronal migration and neuron-glia differentiation; among them, T4 is the main participant [9]. Moreover, studies on transgenic mouse models have demonstrated that T4, as an important neuromodulator, is essential for regulating neuronal differentiation, synaptic development, and myelin formation [10]. Other studies have shown that disturbed TH levels may lead to abnormal neurological functions and even trigger a range of nervous system diseases, including various neurodegenerative disorders as well as mental disturbances like schizophrenia, affective disturbance, anxiety neurosis, and melancholia [11]. Thus, abnormal levels of TH may be strongly pertaining to varied neurodegenerative disorders’ onset and progression.

Neurodegenerative disorders mean a group of illnesses characterized by neurons’ progressive loss, mainly including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), Huntington’s disease (HD), etc. These diseases usually cause cognitive, motor, and behavioral dysfunctions, imposing a heavy burden on patients and their families [12]. With the increasing trend of global aging, the incidence of neurodegenerative disorders is increasing annually, which significantly affects soundness in body and mind along with the life expectancy of the aged population. According to a report by the World Health Organization, neurodegenerative disorders will overtake cancer as the second underlying cause of death, only following cardiovascular illnesses, in the next 15–20 years [13].

In recent years, extensive studies have concentrated on TH’s role in neurodegenerative disorders. It has been shown that abnormal thyroid function may become an important risk factor for neurodegenerative disorders. For example, epidemiological studies have demonstrated that patients with hypothyroidism [14] often experience cognitive decline; the occurrence of hypothyroidism in adults has been found to cause cognitive deficits in the form of adverse working memory and incapability to focus on complicated mental tasks in several clinical controlled studies [15, 16]. In addition, population-based cohort and case-control studies have indicated that higher free TH levels are not only pertaining to higher dementia risk but also lead to poor performance in cognition and executive function tests in PD patients [17, 18]. Currently, academia has conducted a range of studies on the correlation between TH and neurodegenerative disorders; the research on the correlation between TH and neurodegenerative disorders is shown in Table 1, which refers to research on the correlation between TH and neurodegenerative disorders. Therefore, in-depth exploration of the relationship between TH and neurodegenerative disorders not only helps to reveal its underlying pathological mechanisms but also provides new therapeutic ideas and intervention strategies for clinical practice.

TH’s Impact Upon Nervous System in Physiological or Pathological Conditions

Biological Role of TH

Synthesis and Metabolism of TH

TH mainly includes thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3). And its synthesis process involves multiple steps such as iodine uptake, thyroglobulin synthesis, iodination, and tyrosine binding. TH synthesis mainly occurs in the thyroid gland, and iodine uptake is accomplished through iodine transporters on the surface of thyroid cells. TH synthesis and storage take place in thyroglobulin [52]. After being proteolyzed by proteases, the protein is released into the blood circulation. Then, it is transported by TH-binding proteins to various tissues; once there, it binds to the corresponding receptors and exerts its biological functions. In addition, TH metabolism in the body is mainly carried out by the liver and kidneys. The action of the deiodinase converts T4 to more biologically active T3 [53] binding to TH nuclear receptor and recruit transcription activators to induce target gene expression. The metabolic process of TH is regulated by multiple factors, besides fine adjustment of hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis, including nutritional status, hormone levels, diseases, and the influence of the external environment. These factors together affect the homeostasis of TH in the body.

The Effects of Deiodinase and Thyroid Hormone Transporters on TH

The availability of TH within the brain is jointly regulated by thyroid hormone transporters (such as MCT8 and OATP1C1) and deiodinases (such as DIO2 and DIO3). DIO is a group of redox enzymes that can activate or inactivate TH, and the balance of their activity is a key determinant of TH availability within cells. DIO includes Type I (DIO1), Type II (DIO2), and Type III (DIO3). Among these, DIO2 primarily catalyzes the outer ring deiodination of T4 to form T3, serving as the core enzyme for TH activation in the brain, while DIO3 catalyzes the inner ring deiodination of T3 and T4 to form rT3 and T2, participating in TH inactivation [54]. Elevated DIO2 activity promotes the conversion of T4 to T3 and enhances TH activity; conversely, increased DIO3 activity accelerates the inactivation of T3 and T4, reducing active TH levels [55]. Additionally, DIO2 is highly expressed in glial cells in the brain, and its activity is regulated by TH negative feedback. For example, during hypothyroidism, DIO2 expression is upregulated to promote T3 production and maintain metabolic demands [56].

MCT8 (monocarboxylate transporter 8) is the primary transporter of TH in the brain, responsible for transporting T3 and T4 into neuronal cells, serving as the key pathway for TH entry into brain tissue [57]. When the MCT8 gene is mutated, elevated T3 levels, decreased T4 levels, and normal or mildly elevated TSH levels may occur, indicating abnormal TH metabolism [58]. OATP1C1 (organic anion transport polypeptide 1C1) is highly expressed in vascular endothelial cells and glial cells in the brain and participates in TH metabolism by transporting T4 and rT3 [59].

Deiodinase and transport proteins can exert synergistic effects. When TH levels elevate, DIO3 activity increases, promoting the inactivation of T3 and T4. Concurrently, MCT8 and OATP1C1 mediate the clearance of TH, maintaining TH homeostasis in the brain. In hypothyroidism, DIO2 activity is upregulated, promoting the conversion of T4 to T3, while MCT8 and OATP1C1 enhance the absorption of TH to meet basal metabolic requirements [60].

TH’s Role in Neural Development

TH is vital to neurodevelopment, especially during fetal and infancy. Sabatino et al. confirmed that TH has a remarkable influence on the proliferation, differentiation, and synaptic formation of neurons through the analysis of the single carboxylic acid transporter 8 and organic anion transporter polypeptide double knockout (Mct8/Oatp1c1 DKO) rat model and the related research on human neuroblastoma cell models [61]. Specifically, TH adjusts gene’s transcription via binding to its receptor and then affects neurons’ maturation and function. For example, Li et al. found that T3 induced neurogenesis, neuroblast development, myelination, and neuronal migration during brain growth in a male SD rat model and a neuroblastoma cells’ in vitro hypoxic model study [62]. When the thyroid function is reduced, it will directly or indirectly affect several neurons’ and glial cells’ proliferation, apoptosis, migration, and differentiation in the postnatal brain growth process [63, 64]. For example, TH deficiency delays the switch between immature and mature tau transcripts. Moreover, experiments on the rat pheochromocytoma cell line PC12 model have demonstrated that tau protein expression is required during neuronal growth. Furthermore, the rat model also found that hypothyroidism is associated with increased glial proliferation (i.e., astrocyte proliferation) in the cerebellum and a significant decrease in synaptic density, all of which indicates that TH has a direct and indirect effect on several steps of neuronal and glial cell maturation. In addition, studies on in vitro models of human neural precursor cells have shown that a lack of T3 in the fetal and postnatal development process may cause delayed brain development, mental retardation, and under varied conditions, neurological dysfunction [65].

The Influence of TH on Dendrites, Axons, and Myelin Formation

The current study shows that TH deficiency has significant effects on the development of dendrites, axons, and myelin sheaths, which involve multiple molecular mechanisms and signaling pathways. First, TH promotes dendritic growth by activating neurotrophic factors (such as NGF and BDNF) and transcription factors (such as Myrf). TH deficiency can result in decreased levels of these molecules, thereby affecting dendritic branching and synapse formation [66]. In addition, TH directly regulates dendritic spine formation; the lack of TH greatly decreases the number of dendritic spines and the integrity of the postsynaptic membrane and consequently affects information integration [67]. Furthermore, neurotransmitters, such as glutamate and acetylcholine, can modulate the growth of dendrites and synaptic plasticity by activating the MAPK and PI3K/Akt pathways, and the deficit of TH may inhibit these pathways [68]. An animal study showed that iodine deficiency (ID)-induced TH insufficiency attenuates dendritic spine development by interfering with the functional effects of the Rac1 signaling pathway on the cytoskeleton. Rac1 is a member of the Rho GTPase family that exerts broad influences on the development of neurons, such as regulating neuronal migration, dendritic branching, axonal elongation and guidance, synapse formation, and cell polarization [69].

Deficiency of TH mainly affects the axon formation as follows: firstly, TH can regulate the expression of Bcl-2 (an anti-apoptosis protein) and Bax (a pro-apoptosis protein) to regulate neuronal apoptosis. TH deficiency will cause Bcl-2 expression to decrease and Bax expression to increase, and axon growth is limited [70]. Secondly, TH promotes the axon regeneration via STAT3 and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways [68]. When TH is deficient, the axon regeneration is seriously inhibited. In addition, TH promotes oligodendrocyte differentiation via TGF/β and Hedgehog signaling pathways, and the expression of TH is probably involved in activating these signaling pathways, and the deficiency of TH may impair these signaling pathways and cause the disorder of myelin formation [71].

TH deficiency can affect the structure and function of myelin sheath by influencing the synthesis of related proteins. For instance, TH directly regulates the expression of MBP (myelin base protein), PLP (protein-lipid protein), and MAG (myelin glycoprotein). TH deficiency can lead to a reduction in the synthesis of these proteins [72]. Additionally, TH regulates the proliferation and differentiation of oligodendrocytes by activating the STAT3 and PI3K/Akt pathways. For example, TH can repair demyelination damage through the PI3K/Akt pathway, while TH deficiency can inhibit this process [73]. Furthermore, TH coordinates key steps in myelin formation by integrating the MAPK, PI3K/Akt, and JAK-STAT pathways. TH deficiency leads to reduced activity in these pathways, potentially affecting myelin structure and function by inhibiting cell migration and proliferation [60]. In summary, TH is an essential regulator indispensable for nervous system development.

The Relationship Between TH and Neurodegeneration

Disorders in TH levels leading to neurodegeneration have been reviewed in previous sections. However, the neurodegenerative process itself also exerts significant effects on the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis. First, the degeneration of TRH neurons in the hypothalamus reduces stimulation of the anterior pituitary, resulting in decreased TSH secretion. The inflammatory response triggered by neurodegeneration can also inhibit TRH neuron activity, reduce TSH secretion, and cause fluctuations in TH levels [74]. For example, TRH and TSH levels in AD patients are significantly lower than in healthy individuals. A case-control study showed that reduced TRH levels in AD patients are associated with cognitive impairment, and TSH level disorders are linked to reduced cerebral blood flow and metabolic abnormalities [75]. Additionally, Aβ deposits in AD patients may interfere with TH metabolism, leading to reduced TSH and TRH secretion, forming a bidirectional interaction between neurodegeneration and endocrine dysfunction. Furthermore, inflammatory factors such as IL-6 and TNF-α released during neurodegeneration can inhibit deiodinase activity, leading to reduced T3 production, while increasing rT3 levels, further impairing HPT axis function [76].

In summary, neurodegeneration significantly affects the function of the HPT axis by directly damaging TH metabolism and interfering with the feedback regulation of the HPT axis. Clinically, thyroid function testing and cognitive assessment should be combined to identify at-risk populations at an early stage, and intervention measures such as hormone replacement and anti-inflammatory treatment should be adopted to improve prognosis.

TH Participates in the Pathogenic Process of Neurodegenerative Diseases

Neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and toxic damage are commonly considered pathogenic mechanisms in the process of neurodegenerative diseases, and the effect of TH cannot be neglected. On the one hand, TH is involved in neurodegenerative diseases by affecting the balance between the production and clearance of ROS. Hyperthyroid patients exhibit an enhanced basal metabolism, which will promote the production of ROS. Meanwhile, the enhanced lipid peroxidation further induces neuronal damage [77]. In contrast, patients with hypothyroidism present an impaired ability of removing ROS, resulting in the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). For example, the levels of TT3 are decreased in AD patients with severe cognitive impairment may be associated with increased oxidative stress [78]. Thyroid hormone receptor β(Thrb) is involved in neurodegenerative diseases via modulating microglial inflammation and apoptosis. The expression of Thrb could suppress Aβ42-induced apoptosis and attenuate the induced inflammatory response probably by upregulating the Sirt3/FOXO3a pathway [79]. Moreover, increased hippocampal neuronal apoptosis is induced in hypothyroid patients, possibly related to cellular metabolic abnormalities induced by low T3/T4 levels [80]. In contrast, TH indirectly participates in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases by modulating vascular metabolism and coagulation function. It has been reported that hypothyroidism patients are prone to carotid atherosclerosis; high cholesterol and high homocysteine levels induced by hypothyroidism may cause occlusion of small cerebral arteries, resulting in chronic hypoperfusion and further aggravating neurodegenerative diseases [81]. TH can also regulate constriction and endothelial function to influence cerebral blood flow. For example, impaired vascular endothelial dilation function in patients with hypothyroidism may exacerbate the degree of cerebral ischemia [82]. Moreover, the hypercoagulable state in patients with hypothyroidism can promote thrombus formation, leading to cerebral vascular occlusion. TH replacement therapy can reduce blood viscosity and improve cerebral circulation [83].

In summary, TH participates in the pathological process of neurodegenerative diseases by directly regulating oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and mitochondrial function, as well as indirectly affecting vascular metabolism and cell apoptosis.

Role of TH in Neuroprotection

TH not only has a significant impact on development but also shows remarkable biological effects in neuroprotection in adulthood. Research has shown that TH can protect neurons from impairment in multiple ways, including antioxidant effects, promoting the expression of nerve growth factors, and regulating apoptosis pathways. For example, Chang et al. found in a subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) mouse model that T3 can exert its neuroprotective effect by reducing ROS release, inhibiting microglial cell aggregation and activation, reducing neuroinflammation, and suppressing neuronal apoptosis [4]. In addition, TH has been shown to improve the symptoms of neurodegenerative disorders. For example, animal experiments designed by Fu et al. indicated that levothyroxine treatment significantly improved the learning ability of AD model mice in spatial learning and memory tasks and can help neurons resist oxidative stress damage and reduce neuronal apoptosis [84]. Some metabolomics-based studies have also proven that TH treatment can restore dopaminergic neurons damaged by neurotoxins [85].

TH also exerts its neuroprotective effect by regulating other proteins. For example, as a carrier protein for thyroxine and retinol in plasma and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), transthyretin (TTR) has been confirmed in animal models to combine with amyloid and thus avoid amyloid deposition [86]. In vivo experiments in an AD transgenic mouse model have demonstrated the neuroprotective effect of TTR [87]. Research by Sousa et al. shows that a lack of TTR can accelerate deterioration in cognitive abilities, which is usually related to aging [88]. Moreover, a new TH regulatory gene in neuronal precursors, the multifunctional protein Seladin-1 encoded by the DHCR24 gene, has also been discovered to have certain neuroprotective effects. It has antioxidant and anti-apoptotic effects and is regarded as a neuroprotective agent [89]. Studies have shown that in AD transgenic mouse models’ brain regions, the extent of downregulation of seladin-1 expression parallels the increase in the number of hyperphosphorylated tau proteins [90]. In the rat pheochromocytoma PC12 model, seladin-1 overexpression in vitro can protect against Aβ-mediated neurotoxicity and oxidative stress damage [91]. In a model of fetal human neural epithelial and human mesenchymal stem cells, Benvenuti et al. confirmed that Seladin-1 may be an additional medium for the TH function in the developing brain [92]. Benvenuti et al. also found that TH, especially T3, can facilitate Seladin-1 expression in neuronal precursor cells (like human mesenchymal stem and fetal neuroepithelial cells), thereby protecting neuronal precursor cells from programmed cell death [93]. In summary, TH is an essential element in nervous system growth and is crucial to neuronal differentiation, synaptic development, and myelination, as shown in Fig. 1. These findings emphasize the importance of TH in maintaining normal nervous system function and provide new methods for future neuroprotective treatments.

TH and Alzheimer’s Disease

Mechanism of TH in the Pathological Progression of AD

AD is a complicated neurodegenerative disorder, and its pathological mechanism involves multiple factors., including amyloid deposition, neuronal tangles, neuroinflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction, as shown in Fig. 2. AD is the most representative form of dementia, and its main manifestations are progressive cognitive dysfunction and behavioral impairment. AD’s iconic pathological features are amyloid β protein (Aβ) deposition and its extracellular plaques’ accumulation in the AD brain, as well as neurofibrillary tangles’ formation via hyperphosphorylated tau protein inside the cells [94]. Furthermore, neuroinflammation also plays a vital role in the development and progression of AD. In the AD mouse model, it was found that the activation of microglial cells and the release of inflammatory factors further aggravated neuronal damage [95]. Mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress are considered to be another important mechanism of AD. Studies have shown that mitochondrial damage leads to energy metabolic disorders, causing impaired metabolism and increased production of reactive oxygen species [96], which in turn causes neuronal death. Oxidative stress aggravates the pathogenic process of AD by facilitating the deposition of Aβ, the hyperphosphorylation of tau protein, and the loss of synapses and neurons [97].

The pathophysiological mechanism of AD

Emerging evidence suggests that TH plays a modulatory role in AD through several mechanisms. Preclinical studies have shown that TH dysregulation influences key AD-related pathways [26]. For instance, in male Wistar rats and mouse neuroblastoma models, disrupted TH signaling enhanced Aβ production through transcriptional upregulation of the APP gene and enhanced tau phosphorylation [98, 99]. Hypothyroidism in rodents resulted in increased Aβ deposition in the hippocampus, disturbed tau phosphorylation, and upregulated neuroinflammation [27, 99]. In contrast, T3 suppression downregulated APP expression and reduced Aβ accumulation in SD rats and human neuroblastoma cells [28]. Additionally, hypothyroid rats demonstrated spatial memory impairment along with AD-like neuropathological changes, such as tau hyperphosphorylation and altered synaptic plasticity markers [100].

At the molecular level, it has been proposed that thyroid hormone may regulate AD pathology via ERK1/2 and p38 MAPK signaling [101]. Recently, it has been reported that the human postmortem brains of AD patients exhibited upregulated expression of neuroserpin, which is an inhibitor of tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) and blocks Aβ clearance. Upregulation of the expression of neuroserpin was accompanied by increased thyroid hormone receptor levels, suggesting that the upregulated expression of neuroserpin might be a potential compensatory mechanism or a disturbed thyroid hormone signaling in AD patients [102, 103].

Although much of the mechanistic evidence derives from animal and cellular models, recent clinical studies have begun to correlate TH levels with AD biomarkers in humans. For example, a prospective MRI-based cohort study found that low serum free thyroxine (fT4) was associated with greater cerebral Aβ deposition [29]. A cross-sectional study further reported a positive correlation between serum thyrotropin (TSH) and Aβ burden, and a negative correlation with fT4, even within euthyroid individuals [104]. Elevated serum tau levels have also been observed in hyperthyroid patients [105]. Inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP)—elevated in both hypothyroidism and AD—may serve as a link between thyroid dysfunction and neuroinflammation [106]. Advanced imaging techniques, including Aβ-PET with 11C-PIB or 18F-FBB, have enabled more precise correlation between TH levels and amyloid pathology in vivo [107].

Correlation Between Disorder of TH Levels and AD

TH has been confirmed to play a vital role in the development and function of the central nervous system, and changes in its level may be closely associated with the development of AD. Epidemiological studies have found that THs imbalances may cause significant brain dysfunction, including memory disorders and cognitive impairments [108]. An important marker of thyroid dysfunction is thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) [109]. A clinical cohort study consists of 194 patients with AD and subclinical hypothyroidism contrasted with 122 control subjects showed that TSH levels were ascended in patients with AD [19]. In a clinical study of AD patients, low serum FT4 and high serum TSH levels appeared to be related to discrepancy in AD-specific brain lesions, even in the condition of normal thyroid function [20]. Therefore, Ganguli et al. concluded from an original cohort study that subclinical hypothyroidism is a dangerous factor for the onset and progression of AD [20]. In several other cohort studies, subclinical hyperthyroidism was also related to an increased risk of dementia [21–24, 110]. Furthermore, there is a prominent relevance between thyroid dysfunction and cognitive impairment [111]. Chaalal et al. have found that hypothyroidism is a potential risk factor for the onset and development of AD by a study on the model of male Wistar rats [25], and severe hypothyroidism can damage multifarious cognitive functions, including thought, verbal ability, mind, mentality, perception, psychomotor function, and execution [112]. In addition, cohort studies of people with AD have shown that hyperthyroidism is associated with cognitive impairments and an increased risk of dementia in adults [23]. The serum total tau levels of participants with hyperthyroidism were significantly higher than those of participants with normal thyroid function. This suggests that hyperthyroid people may be undergoing AD-like pathological processes, characterized by tau protein aggregation and hyperphosphorylation [30].

In addition, an analysis of community populations using serological and neuropsychological assessments found that thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) can regulate the synthesis and release of acetylcholine and that a decrease in TRH levels caused by negative feedback from elevated TH secretion may lead to disturbances in brain acetylcholine metabolism, thereby contributing to the development of dementia [113]. Similarly, after analyzing the serological and psychological assessments of community populations, it was found that the exacerbation of cholinergic deficiency and relevant cognitive impairment observed in patients with AD has also been attributed to TH-induced acetylcholine depletion [114]. In vitro neuronal cultures revealed that TRH depletion significantly enhances tau phosphorylation, potentially through increased p38-MAPK activation and reduced PP2B protein expression [115]. These factors often interact and influence each other, thus working together in the progression of AD.

Progress of TH as a Potential AD Therapeutic Target

In recent years, the thyroid gland has attracted extensive attention as a completely new direction in the treatment of Alzheimer’s disease. Some experimental studies have explored the use of TH replacement therapy in AD patients. In male Wistar rats, TH supplementation may improve cognitive function and slow disease progression [31]. In an animal model study of AD in mice, it was found that treatment with T3 can delay the progression of AD, mainly by inducing downregulation of GSK3β kinase expression and thereby alleviating mitochondrial dysfunction in AD [32]. GSK3β plays a key role in the regulation of mitochondrial function and mediates the abnormal phosphorylation of tau protein, which leads to the formation of neurofibrillary tangles [116]. Evidence suggests that TH can reduce oxidative stress and improve mitochondrial function in human fibroblast cell lines [117]. Several studies in rat models have shown that TH has antioxidant and cytoprotective effects under conditions of oxidative stress [118–120]. It has been reported that TH also upregulates the expression of the apolipoprotein E (APOE) gene in an AD mouse model, thereby participating in neuroprotection. APOE is a plasma lipoprotein that plays an important role in Aβ clearance [121], and it has been linked to a number of neurodegenerative disorders, which include AD [122]. In addition, cohort studies suggest that TH has a significant impact on neurodevelopment and neuroprotection and may also influence the pathological process of AD by improving metabolism and reducing inflammation [33]. Studies have also found that regulating the synthesis and metabolism of TH may provide new treatment strategies for AD, such as the use of TH analogs or drugs that regulate their signaling pathways [123]. Runfola et al. designed a TH derivative called SG2, which was found to have therapeutic potential in the treatment of AD due to protective effects against neuronal plasticity damage in mouse models of AD [124]. Additionally, 3-iodothyronamine (T1AM), as an endogenous thyroid hormone derivative, has been found in animal models to alleviate behavioral and memory deficits caused by Aβ deposition [125], demonstrating certain neuroprotective effects. Despite the encouraging results of the current study, further extensive clinical tests are needed to validate the efficacy and safety of TH in therapy.

TH and Parkinson’s Disease

Mechanism of TH in the Pathological Progression of Parkinson’s Disease

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a common neurodegenerative disease with a complex pathogenesis. Multiple factors are mainly involved in dopaminergic neuronal death, alpha-synuclein aggregation, and neuroinflammation [126], as shown in Fig. 3. Inflammation has been shown to have a significant impact on the pathogenesis of PD through studies of mouse models; particularly, the inflammatory response in the CNS may contribute to neuronal damage and death [127]. A range of inflammatory mediators as well as microglia activation is involved in the progression of inflammation during the course of PD [128]. Interestingly, some of the inflammatory responses and cytokines produced by microglia and other phagocytes also have neuroprotective effects. Therefore, inflammation has a dual role in the molecular process of Parkinson’s disease, including neuroprotective and neurodegenerative effects [129]. Studies on animal and cellular models of PD have indicated that the aggregation of alpha-synuclein to form Lewy vesicles is regarded as a key pathological feature of PD at the molecular level. Its aggregation not only affects the function of neurons but may also trigger toxic reactions in cells and promote mitochondrial oxidative stress, thereby further aggravating neurodegeneration [130]. Therefore, understanding the pathogenesis of PD is crucial for the development of new treatment strategies.

The pathophysiological mechanism of PD

TH exerts an important regulation on mitochondrial metabolism and oxidative stress responses, two major pathways involved in PD pathogenesis [131]. Both hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism have been reported to affect oxidative stress levels, with implications for the vulnerability of dopaminergic neurons [132]; while the former enhances ROS production, the latter decreases the antioxidant capacity. T3, but not T4, affects the expression of the antioxidant proteins uncoupling protein (UCP) and superoxide dismutase [133]. Furthermore, elevated TSH directly promotes oxidative stress; higher levels of lipid peroxidation products are observed in clinical and subclinical hypothyroidism [134]. These bioactive lipids can facilitate α-synuclein-membrane binding and insoluble aggregate formation, a finding corroborated by recent human studies showing associations between thyroid dysfunction and accelerated α-synuclein pathology in PD patients [135].

Autoimmunity is another interesting convergence of PD and thyroid dysfunction. Autoantibodies directed against dopaminergic neurons may promote or trigger PD through the engagement of microglia and interference with dopamine receptors [136]. Thyroid autoimmune disease, like Hashimoto’s thyroiditis and Graves’ disease, is also associated with an increased risk for PD and suggests a possible cross-connection of autoimmune pathways [137].

Genetic studies further support the link between TH and PD. For example, LRRK2 mutations, which are frequently found in PD as well as autoimmune thyroid diseases, induce oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and α-synuclein accumulation [138, 139]. RASD2, another TH-regulated gene, encodes striatal protein Rhes. Its deletion in hypothyroid states results in neuroinflammation, activation of microglia and astrocytes, and the occurrence of PD [140].

Effect of TH Level Disturbance on PD

Several studies have indicated that TH may play a protective role in Parkinson’s disease through mechanisms that regulate neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and neuronal survival. For example, Lee et al. used an SD rat model and found that TH can induce dopamine nerve differentiation and promote its repair after injury, confirming the protective effect of TH on dopaminergic neurons [41]. Peng et al. analyzed 106 cognitively normal and 94 cognitively impaired patients in China and found a significant association between TH levels and the degree of cognitive impairment in PD patients [34]. Both hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism have been linked to PD [141, 142]. In the Tshrhyt mouse model, it was found that hypothyroidism usually leads to the loss of dopamine neurons [35], which is strongly relevant to oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and other factors. In addition, a case-control study found that hypothyroidism is similar to PD symptoms, often accompanied by bradykinesia and cognitive dysfunction, while hyperthyroidism can aggravate PD symptoms by increasing tremor and/or levodopa-induced dyskinesia [36]. In a cross-sectional study, low serum FT3 levels and normal or reduced serum TSH and FT4 levels were observed in 34.4% of PD patients, and these patients tended to have more severe motor impairment, weight loss, and cognitive decline [37]. Another cross-sectional study has found the relevance between cognitive function and thyroid status in early PD patients with normal thyroid function. Higher levels of fT4 were related to poorer executive function performance [18]. Li et al. reported that follow-up of patients with Graves’ disease/hyperthyroidism showed an increased risk of subsequent PD in these patients [38]. Rugbjerg et al. came to a similar conclusion [39]. The above studies all indicate that abnormal levels of TH may be closely related to the development of PD.

The Potential of TH Alternative Therapy for the Treatment of PD

Several experimental and clinical studies have been conducted in recent years to investigate the potential of TH replacement therapy in the treatment of PD. For example, Salama et al. found that levothyroxine injection can induce neuroprotection in striatal regions by increasing the number of microglia, as studied in an animal model of SD rats, thereby improving the motor function of the animal model of PD [40]. Lee et al. designed and synthesized several TH derivatives and found experimentally that TH and its derivatives successfully promoted the regeneration of dopamine neurons after neurotoxic injury in SD rats [138]. It has also been reported that the tremor symptoms of a male PD patient with hyperthyroidism improved after treatment with TH [42]. Polini et al. found that 3-iodothyronine amine (T1AM) as an endogenous TH derivative can modulate the inflammatory response of brain nerves in an in vitro model of microglia and has the therapeutic potential to prevent neurodegeneration [43]. Zheng et al. administered the TRH analog taltirelin to a PD rat model and found that taltirelin effectively improved motor function without inducing motor impairment [143]. These studies suggest that appropriate TH replacement therapy may help improve clinical symptoms and quality of life of PD patients, especially those with combined hypothyroidism [144]. However, the current studies still have some restrictions, with a small sample capacity and lack of long-term follow-up data, so further randomized controlled trials are demanded to validate the effectiveness and safety of TH alternative therapies [145]. Overall, TH alternative therapy shows some promise in the treatment of PD, and future research will help verify its practical effects in the treatment of Parkinson’s disease.

TH and Huntington’s Disease

Pathogenesis of Huntington’s Disease

Huntington’s disease (HD) is a hereditary neurodegenerative disease mainly characterized by dyskinesia, cognitive impairment, and psychiatric symptoms [146]. The pathogenesis of HD is complex and involves multiple biological processes, including gene mutations, protein aggregation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuroinflammation, as shown in Fig. 4. Studies on the HD mouse model have found that mutations in the HTT gene can lead to the production of abnormal huntingtin (mutant huntingtin, mHtt). This protein aggregates in nerve cells to form cytotoxic aggregates, which in turn trigger apoptosis [147]. Moreover, it has been indicated that mHtt negatively affects mitochondrial function, causing energy metabolism disorder and further aggravating neuronal damage [148]. In neuroinflammation, HD is concerned with elevated inflammatory mediators in the central and peripheral nervous system [149]. The expression of mHtt also activates microglia, which triggers an inflammatory response and damages the survival environment of peripheral neurons [150].

The pathophysiological mechanism of HD

Currently, the pathological association between TH and HD remains unclear. Studies have found that genetic polymorphisms in the gene encoding the activated deiodinase (Thr92Ala-D2) are relatively common in patients with hypothyroidism. The resulting transcriptional changes include upregulation of genes involved in mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and apoptosis, which may be linked to neurodegenerative diseases such as HD [151]. Furthermore, μ-crystallin (Crym), a TH-binding protein, may serve as a key regulator of T3 transport between the cytoplasm and nucleus. Studies indicate that Crym overexpression reduces the sensitivity of striatal neurons to mutant mHtt toxicity, thereby diminishing mHtt fragment-induced cell death. This protective effect may be linked to altered TH levels; however, the precise role of TH in this process remains unclear [152].

In addition, the reciprocal action of genetic and environmental factors has an important impact on the pathogenesis of Huntington’s disease. Although HD is an autosomal dominant disorder, recent research results from transgenic mouse models verify the effect of environmental factors on the occurrence and progression of diseases [153], such as diet and lifestyle [154]. In summary, the pathogenesis of Huntington’s disease is a complicated network of intertwined multiple factors, and further studies are required to uncover its specific mechanisms and potential treatment targets.

Role of TH in HD

Patients with Huntington’s disease often have abnormalities in thyroid function, so recently, increasing research has focused upon TH’s role in HD. Among them, there have been many reports of the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis participating in metabolic and neuroendocrine regulation. Case-control studies have found that the total T4, T3, fT4, and TSH levels of HD patients are similar to those of healthy controls [155, 156]. Other studies have found that the TRH-stimulated response to TSH is impaired in HD patients [157]. In another case-control study, Saleh et al. found that although there were no significant disparities in the early stages of disease onset compared to controls, TSH levels in HD patients decreased as the disease progressed [44], suggesting that the loss of hypothalamic neurons may occur as the disease progresses. A case study conducted by Aziz et al. confirmed that patients with HD had slightly increased TSH levels, significantly higher T3 levels, and higher T4 levels compared to controls. This finding suggests a disturbance of the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis in patients with early HD [45]. There are relatively few studies on the direct correlation between TH levels and HD, and no clear conclusions have been drawn yet, probably due to the lack of large-sample experimental studies.

Therapeutic Potential of TH in HD

The treatment of Huntington’s disease still faces challenges, and the therapeutic potential of TH is gradually being recognized. Some studies have found that a lack of TH may exacerbate the pathological process of HD and that appropriate supplementation may help improve the symptoms and quality of life of patients. For example, Badini et al. used 3-nitropropionic acid (3-NP) to induce motor dysfunction in rats similar to HD, including impaired motor coordination, muscle weakness, movement retardation, and stiffness. Treatment with levothyroxine (L-T4) was then administered, and it was found that L-T4 treatment significantly decreased 3-NP-induced motor deficits, oxidative stress, and histological variations in the striatal region [46], thus demonstrating the therapeutic potential of L-T4 for HD-like behaviors. Other studies have shown a potential relation between TH and the expression of neurotrophic factors in brain development [158]. Several studies in a mouse animal model have found that thyroxine increases the level of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) in developing neurons [159, 160], while elevated BDNF levels have been found to be crucial for reversing motor and cognitive deficits in neurodegenerative disorders in a Wistar rat model [161]. Badini et al. also found that L-T4 treatment increased striatal BDNF levels in HD rats, which may be related to behavioral improvement [46]. Although more clinical trials are needed to verify the effectiveness and safety of TH in Huntington’s disease patients, the preliminary results already point the way forward for future research. Therefore, TH as a potential therapeutic agent is worth further exploration, with the aim of providing new treatment options and strategies to improve quality of life for patients with Huntington’s disease.

TH and Multiple Sclerosis

Pathophysiological Mechanisms of Multiple Sclerosis

Multiple sclerosis (MS) means an autoimmune disease of the central nervous system with a complex pathophysiological mechanism, mainly manifested as nerve myelin loss and damage to nerve fibers [162]. The pathogenesis of MS involves genetic and environmental factors along with abnormalities of the immune system. Studies have shown that T cells are crucial to MS pathogenesis, especially the imbalance of Th17 cells and regulatory T cells (Treg). Additionally, the breakdown of the blood-brain barrier and the occurrence of neuroinflammation are also considered to be important factors in disease progression [163]. As the disease progresses, patients may experience a variety of clinical manifestations, including cognitive impairment, fatigue, and motor dysfunction. These symptoms dramatically affect patients’ quality of life while increasing the burden of healthcare. Therefore, an in-depth understanding of MS pathophysiologic mechanisms is of great importance for the formulation of effective therapeutic strategies.

TH is Involved in MS Occurrence and Development by Modulating Immune Response

TH has an important impact on modulating the immune response, and studies have suggested that it affects immune cell function through multiple mechanisms. In the case of hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism, it can have an effect on multiple immune functions, like chemotaxis, phagocytosis, reactive oxygen species production, and cytokine synthesis and release [164]. In a mouse lymphoma model, it was found that TH can also promote T cell proliferation and differentiation, adjust the balance of Th1 and Th2 cells, and thus influence autoimmune diseases’ occurrence and development [165].

Significant TH deficiency reduced myelin-associated glycoprotein mRNA expression and delayed aggregation in the cerebral cortex, which impairs myelin formation [166]. Furthermore, the abnormal TH levels caused by MCT8 gene mutations likely result from the impaired role of TH in oligodendrocyte differentiation and myelin gene expression. This impaired TH function likely explains the hypomyelination observed in MCT8-deficient patients [167]. Therefore, for MS patients, abnormal thyroid function may affect the condition. For example, oligodendrocyte proliferation is required for remyelination [168], and TH exerts an important influence in oligodendrocyte maturation. Therefore, TH often affects the severity and prognosis of MS. In addition, appropriate TH levels help maintain immune homeostasis and may reduce the symptoms of MS by suppressing the inflammatory response [47]. Therefore, the adjustment of TH levels may offer new insights for MS treatment.

TH in the Treatment of MS

Recently, increasing researches have focused upon TH’s potential application in the treatment of MS. Wong et al. believe that during the critical period of development, hypothyroidism can lead to abnormalities in the central nervous system, like incomplete maturation of neurons and glial cells, reduced synaptic density, and myelin defects [48], which may be pertaining to the onset of MS. In neurodegenerative disorders in which demyelination is the main event, T3 and T4 are viewed as promising therapeutic interventions due to their role in propelling myelin formation [169]. Hypodermic injection of T4 or T3 has been confirmed to ameliorate disease-related neurological impairments after onset in MS models [49, 170]. Therefore, oligodendrocyte protection and myelin regeneration may be achieved by TH injection as a therapeutic means of repairing the central nervous system [171, 172]. Hung et al. observed that treatment with T4 can significantly alleviate delayed myelination and oligodendrocyte death in the presence of ischemic and hypoxic injury [50]. In another study, mice were induced to achieve deep demyelination, and then, T3 was injected intraperitoneally daily. After a period of time, myelin regeneration in the corpus callosum and cerebellum was ameliorated, myelin loss and axonal degeneration were decreased, and the number of myelinated axons nearly reached control levels [51]. All of the above studies reflect that TH might have some therapeutic potential in MS treatment.

However, there exist no data to illustrate to what extent TH treatment can repair axonal damage, oligodendrocyte, and myelin loss [173]. Therefore, a combination of therapies ought to be the ultimate target for valid MS therapy [49]. However, the effectiveness of TH as a potential therapeutic drug for multiple sclerosis has been verified to a certain extent in various animal experimental models. Therefore, more clinical researches are needed in the future to explore how TH can improve demyelination and axonal damage in MS and exert neuroprotective effects.

Opportunities and Challenges

Since most neurodegenerative disorders are incurable, clinical treatment is still focused on preventing or delaying the progression of the disease. However, a single treatment method cannot effectively improve motor dysfunction, cognitive impairment, neuropsychiatric disorders, etc., caused by neuronal degeneration or degeneration. However, the thyroid gland is a significant endocrine organ in the body, and it is regarded as a potential therapeutic target. Starting with hormone levels is not a new treatment strategy.

We herein discuss TH’s important role in neurodegenerative disorders and its potential clinical applications in our article. Studies have illustrated that TH is crucial to normal physiological processes and also has a significant impact on the development and progression of neurodegenerative disorders (like AD, PD), as shown in Fig. 5.

TH’s role in neurodegenerative disorders: a summary

In summary, TH participates in the occurrence and development of neurodegenerative diseases through multiple pathways mentioned above and is widely involved in regulating neuronal metabolism, promoting neuroprotection, and influencing neuronal growth and regeneration. Therefore, it is expected to become a new target for the treatment of these diseases in the future.

Although current research outcomes are encouraging, it is undeniable that the specific pathophysiological mechanism linking TH and neurodegenerative disorders is not yet fully understood, and there is even considerable controversy in some areas. For example, some studies have indicated that TH levels (such as TT3 and FT3) in AD patients are positively correlated with cognitive function scores. However, other studies have shown that hyperthyroidism may also increase the risk of AD [174]. In addition, there is controversy regarding the relationship between TH levels and PD risk: some studies have found no significant association between abnormal TH levels and PD, while others have suggested that hypothyroidism may increase PD risk by affecting the metabolism of dopaminergic neurons. Concurrently, conclusions drawn from different research methodologies vary. For instance, Ganguli and Choi et al. suggest that elevated serum TSH levels in individuals with clinically normal thyroid function correlate with AD-specific brain changes and dementia risk [19, 20], whereas Chaalal et al. propose that hypothyroidism itself may be a potential risk factor for AD progression [25]. Neurodegenerative diseases like AD often involve multiple risk factors. Beyond the common influence of age, dietary habits and metabolic factors also play crucial roles in AD risk and progression. For instance, insulin resistance associated with diabetes and related metabolic conditions contributes to increased AD risk [175]. Furthermore, the impact of TH levels on neurodegenerative diseases varies significantly depending on the stage of the disease. For example, in the early stages of AD, TH levels may exert a neuroprotective effect by clearing Aβ, while in the late stages, oxidative stress may exacerbate neuronal damage. In PD, TH may delay neuronal degeneration through its antioxidant effects in the early stages of the disease, but in the late stages, metabolic imbalance may accelerate neuronal death.

Analogs or derivatives of TH, such as SG2 and T1AM, and TRH derivatives, such as taltirelin, have shown therapeutic efficacy in animal models to prevent or ameliorate neurodegeneration and are considered promising therapeutic agents for neurodegenerative diseases [43, 124, 125, 137, 143]. In addition, Lisa et al. reported that the thyroid hormone analog sobetirome enhanced the survival of oligodendrocytes and induced myelin regeneration in mouse animal models [176]. Duncan et al. demonstrated that the TH receptor agonist LL-341070 holds potential to promote myelin regeneration and recovery of neural function. Administration in demyelinating animal models resulted in extensive myelin regeneration in the brain, spinal cord, and other parts of the nervous system [177]. TH receptor agonists may become a novel target to promote myelin regeneration and improve clinical outcomes in demyelinating diseases.

However, most of the research on TH therapy for neurodegenerative diseases is still based on preclinical models, and a limited number of clinical trials are in progress. For example, the Phase 1b clinical trial has shown that oral levothyroxine is well-tolerated and efficacious in MS patients [178]. In addition, the therapeutic effects of TH treatment were investigated in MS-like animals and patients with relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS). This clinical trial is currently in the Phase I tolerability clinical trial stage, and no definitive results on efficacy have been obtained [179]. These clinical trials have several common shortcomings, such as a small number of participants and lack of long-term efficacy and safety data. Therefore, the clinical application of TH and its analogs still confront many challenges.

However, the risks and potential adverse effects of long-term TH therapy are still unknown. For example, long-term use of T3 leads to arrhythmia and electrolyte disturbances, so they should be closely monitored. Clinical use in elderly patients or patients with multiple comorbidities should be especially cautious [180]. In addition, excess thyroid hormone circulation can cause osteoporosis, myocardial and skeletal muscle disorders, heart rate and body temperature, and adverse mood and mental disorders. It is also a challenge to obtain the desired effects of thyroid hormone while avoiding the off-target effects [181]. Given this situation, future research should focus on alternative TH therapies, exploring how to optimize medication regimens and develop personalized treatment strategies to enhance patients’ quality of life. Given this current situation, future research should focus on alternative therapies for TH, exploring how to optimize its treatment protocols and develop personalized therapeutic strategies to enhance patients’ quality of life. Simultaneously, in-depth investigation into the specific mechanisms of TH in neuroprotection will help elucidate its potential role in neurodegenerative diseases, thereby advancing the development of more effective treatment strategies.

There are several important research questions that deserve further exploration in this process. First, are there disparities in the impacts of various types of TH on the nervous system? Second, how can the effectiveness of TH treatment be assessed in different patient groups? Finally, are there potential side effects of TH use, and how can these risks be balanced against the benefits of treatment in clinical practice? The answers to these questions will provide a more solid foundation for applying TH in treating neurodegenerative disorders.

Conclusions

The etiological factors of neurodegenerative diseases are often diverse, with neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and toxic substance damage all playing significant roles in their onset and progression. Among these, TH is a key player that cannot be overlooked. While the efficacy of TH therapy has been partially validated in animal models, particularly showing significant potential in the treatment of AD and PD, the feasibility of TH therapy in clinical trials has yet to be fully validated. Additionally, there is no comprehensive and convincing explanation for how TH exerts its neuroprotective effects or at which stage of the disease process it acts. Based on this, future therapeutic strategies may focus on early intervention before widespread neurodegeneration occurs, or on selecting appropriate treatment windows for application, rather than solely relying on symptomatic treatment after disease onset. Furthermore, the long-term safety and safe dosage of TH require further testing, necessitating large-scale clinical trials to provide supporting data. Undeniably, TH has emerged as a potential therapeutic target for neurodegenerative diseases. Strategies such as developing precision delivery systems, combining with other therapeutic approaches, and formulating personalized treatment plans hold promise for overcoming current challenges and enhancing therapeutic efficacy. Simultaneously, deepening research into the interactions between TH and other neuroactive substances, developing novel TH analogs, and evaluating long-term efficacy and safety will provide new insights and methodologies for treating neurodegenerative diseases.
